Functional characterization of orthodontic bands coated with chitosan-zinc oxide nanocomposite: antibacterial and electrochemical characterization
Authors: Syeda Ridaa Fatima, Nida Zehra Bano, Khalil Ahmad, Muhammad Kaleem, Muhammad Atiq-Ur-Rehman, Muhammad Amber Fareed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAfter orthodontic treatment, dental caries develops in many patients, especially in posterior teeth beneath or around the molar bands. The objective of this study was to deposit a Nanocomposite coating composed of chitosan and zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) on orthodontic bands via electrophoretic deposition (EPD) and to characterize the Nanocomposite coating using various characterization techniques.Materials and methodsZnO NPs were synthesized in the laboratory and were incorporated in the chitosan based Nanocomposite coating deposited on commercially available orthodontic band. A Taguchi design of experiment (DoE) approach was used to optimize the EPD parameters, and it was concluded that an applied potential of 15 V with a deposition time of 5 min was the optimum parameter for uniform Nanocomposite coating on an orthodontic band. SEM image of Nanocomposite coating with a thickness of 5.11 μm exhibited uniform morphology. ZnO NPs were uniformly distributed over the chitosan matrix of the Nanocomposite coating.ResultsThe hydrophilic nature of Nanocomposite coating showed a potential antibacterial effect against Streptococcus mutans at all-time points (p ≤ 0.05) which was confirmed by turbidity test. The highest antibacterial activity coincided with zinc ion release on day six. Potentiodynamic polarization showed a shift in corrosion potential from (-0.6966 V) to (-0.5949) towards the noble direction also by reducing the corrosion current density from (7.81 µA/cm2) to (4.04 µA/cm2) after depositing the Nanocomposite coating on orthodontic band indicating the improved corrosion resistance ability.ConclusionThis study concludes that Zinc oxide chitosan Nanocomposite coating on orthodontic molar bands can be done using Electrophoretic Deposition. This Nanocomposite coating has the potential to impart antibacterial activity to the orthodontic bands. These coated orthodontic bands possess antibacterial properties against Streptococcus mutans and are corrosion-resistant as proven by the electrochemical analysis.Keywords: Antibacterial, Nanocomposite coating, Orthodontics, Streptococcus mutans, Zinc oxide nanoparticles

Introduction

Orthodontic treatment is recommended for the alignment of aesthetically and functionally compromised teeth with fixed orthodontic appliances as its mainstay. A significant disadvantage of this treatment modality is that certain retentive sites for bacterial proliferation are created, preventing effective brushing and saliva’s flushing action, causing the overall bacterial count, specifically, Streptococcus mutans (S. mutans) in the oral cavity to rise within the first week of orthodontic treatment [1]. As S. mutans is the initiator of dental caries, a high incidence of dental caries is observed during and after orthodontic treatment, specifically at the site of molars fitted with orthodontic bands used to provide anchorage to the orthodontic wire [2]. The location, geometry, and lack of antibacterial activity contribute to excessive plaque formation [3]. Development of dental caries at the site of these molar bands can lead to increased treatment time, additional restorative costs for the orthodontic patient, and long-term enamel damage and compromised results.

Antibacterial coatings on a material’s surface can impart antibacterial properties while maintaining its mechanical strength. Such coatings are important in dentistry, where the inherent properties of a material are of great importance and cannot be altered or lost. Orthodontic brackets and wires have been coated with a variety of antibacterial agents, mostly nanoparticles of metallic oxides [4, 5]. Although several materials are coated on stainless steel flat surfaces, the unique geometry of the orthodontic band presents different challenges and cannot be compared to a flat surface. Limited literature is available regarding antibacterial coatings on orthodontic bands.

Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) are multifunctional nanoparticles with a range of desirable properties that make them valuable across diverse industries, including biomedical applications [6]. The ease and low cost of synthesizing these nanoparticles by the chemical precipitation method facilitate large-scale production. ZnO NPs of various morphologies exhibit antibacterial action against a wide range of bacterial species. With the ability to generate Reactive Oxygen species (ROS), these particles have a higher antimicrobial activity than organic polymers [6].

Chitin is a major biopolymer extracted from shrimp, crabs, and crustaceans. When chitin is deacetylated up to 50%, it becomes soluble in aqueous media, called chitosan. Chitosan is biocompatible with unique antibacterial and antifungal properties and can be used as an antibacterial coating on biomaterial substrates [7].

Zinc oxide nanoparticles have been deposited on titanium implants as an effective antibacterial agent against both gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and gram-negative bacteria Escherichia Coli (E. coli). Recently, Zinc oxide nanoparticles have been coated on orthodontic bands. Missier et al., in 2023, found that the bacterial colonies cultured from oral swabs of the rats were significantly reduced when their teeth were fitted with ZnO NPs coated orthodontic bands [8]. Bahrami et al., in 2024, found similar results that coating orthodontic bands with ZnO NPs shows antibacterial activity [9]. Chitosan coatings have been deposited on different metallic implants in combination with various biocompatible, bioactive particles such as bioactive glass, hydroxyapatite and ZnO NPs to deposit antibacterial, bioactive composite coatings [10, 11]. Incorporating ZnO NPs within Chitosan allows for superior antibacterial activity by combining the generation of ROS with the disruption of bacterial cell membrane resulting in a synergistic dual action effect. The combination of organic and inorganic components in a nanocomposite coating has been known to optimize structural integrity and efficacy while also lowering chances of corrosion. Such coatings have found application as coating agent in textile industry as well as food packaging and in wound healing [12]. This synergistic effect renders Chitosan as the most suitable organic medium for preparing suspension of ZnO NPs required for coating technique. In this study, chitosan polymer solution was used to develop a stable Chitosan-ZnO NPs coating solution.

Electrophoretic deposition (EPD) is a versatile coating technique that requires less energy and produces fine-quality polymer, ceramics, and composite coatings on metallic substrates. This technique requires a stable suspension with an organic liquid to coat a substrate. EPD cannot be employed with water as a suspension, as voltage applications cause oxygen and hydrogen gases to be released, which can have a deleterious effect on the coating [13]. Stable suspension is prepared in which charged particles are suspended, and when subjected to EPD, these charged particles are forced to move and deposit on oppositely charged electrodes under the influence of an electric field. There are two types of EPD because of the applied current, direct current cathodic and direct current anodic. In both techniques, the charged particles are deposited on oppositely charged electrodes.

Adjusting certain parameters (applied voltage or deposition time) of EPD gives better control over the thickness of the coating deposited. Another study demonstrated that a bioactive glass coating was deposited on Ti-Nb meshes via EPD to facilitate bone regeneration [14]. Bioactive glass coating was also deposited on Co-Cr-Mo alloy for biomedical purposes using EPD. One of the advantages of EPD lies in its ability to coat substrates with complex geometry [15]. This research aims to synthesize the ZnO NPs and then incorporate these nanoparticles with Chitosan to synthesize a Nanocomposite coating to deposit on the orthodontic band via EPD to impart antibacterial activity to the band. This Nanocomposite coating has not yet been attempted on orthodontic bands, and to the knowledge of this author, EPD has also not been employed to coat orthodontic bands. The objective of this research is to impart antibacterial properties without compromising their electrochemical and mechanical properties to orthodontic bands.

Materials and methods

Materials

Materials used in this research work were distilled water, zinc nitrate hexahydrate, reagent grade 98%, sodium hydroxide, 98–100% pellets, medium molecular weight chitosan with 95% deacetylation degree, ethanol absolute with purity of ≥ 99.8%, and acetic acid with purity of ≥ 99.5% which were purchased from Sigma Aldrich, Germany. Artificial saliva was purchased from Nigheban Pharmacy, Islamabad, Pakistan, while the Bacterial strain of Streptococcus mutans (Clarke ATCC 25175) from Manassas, USA.

Synthesis of ZnO NPs

Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) were synthesized from the chemical precipitation method [16]. A 0.1 molar solution of zinc nitrate hexahydrate was prepared by dissolving 18.9 g of it in 1000 ml of distilled water. A 0.5 molar solution of sodium hydroxide was also prepared by dissolving 20 g of sodium hydroxide pellets in 1000 ml of distilled water. Zinc nitrate solution was added dropwise into sodium hydroxide solution which was stirred at a speed of 700 rpm on the magnetic stirring plate (Velp-Scientifica, made in the USA) at room temperature. A milky solution was obtained after one hour of magnetic stirring and white precipitates were observed settling down at the bottom. The solution was decanted leaving behind the precipitates. Precipitates were centrifuged thrice in 15 ml distilled water and twice in ethanol at 9000 rpm for 10 min using a centrifuge (BIOBASE BKC-TH16, Germany). The particles were dried overnight at 100 °C in an incubator (JP Selecta thermostat WT 303–0 S, Spain). ZnO NPs were also subjected to calcination at 350 °C for 3 h. After the synthesis of ZnO NPs, these particles were characterized by SEM and FTIR and then integrated into the Nanocomposite coating deposited on an orthodontic band.

Chitosan suspension preparation

The chitosan solution was prepared by dissolving 0.5 g/L of chitosan in an acidic solution prepared by dissolving 1 vol% of acetic acid in 20 vol% of distilled water. The solution was magnetically stirred for 30 min, and then 79 vol% of ethanol was added to the chitosan-based solution and left for 24 h[17]. 50 ml of chitosan was taken, and 3 g/L of ZnO NPs were added to it and stirred for 30 min at 350 rpm at room temperature [18]. The suspension was subjected to ultrasonication for 30 min and then again stirred for 10 min on a magnetic stirring plate. The pH value of the suspension was adjusted from 3.5 to 4 by adding acetic acid dropwise [19].

Sample preparation

The commercial stainless steel orthodontic bands (AISI 304, ASTM A240), size 36, were purchased from Dent aurum (Ispringen, Germany). These were cleaned in an ultrasonication bath in a solution of ethanol and acetone of (1:1) concentration for 15 min before coating. The suspended orthodontic bands were used as the cathode while a medical-grade stainless steel strip (AISI 316 L, ASTM A240) of dimensions (2.0 × 1.5) cm was used as an anode. The interelectrode distance was maintained at 10 mm.

EPD of nanocomposite coating

Stable suspension of ZnO NPs in chitosan was subjected to EPD to deposit a Nanocomposite coating on orthodontic bands. EPD parameters like applied potential (V) and deposition time (min) were optimized by a Taguchi design of experiment (DoE) establishing values of two variables that impact the final Nanocomposite coating. Two control factors (voltage and deposition time) were used to build an orthogonal Taguchi (DoE) array (L9-type), where L stands for Latin square 9 and represents the number of experiments. Table 1lists the control factors and level variables used to optimize the EPD parameters for Nanocomposite coating on orthodontic bands. Both applied voltage and deposition time have three levels. The effect of varying the control factors (applied voltage and time of deposition) in accordance with specific levels on the deposition yield of Nanocomposite coating deposited on the orthodontic band and percentage yield was calculated. Since there is evidence of non-uniform, cracked coatings deposited as a result of using high voltages, the voltage parameter in this study was limited to 15 V[20].

Following optimization of coating parameters, the orthodontic band and stainless steel strip were submerged in the stable Nanocomposite coating suspension. Existing literature demonstrated that at low pH ~ 3.5 to 4, the amine groups of chitosan were protonated. These protonated (positively charged) macromolecules of chitosan were adsorbed onto the surface of ZnO NPs imparting them a positive net charge. As the optimized voltage for a specific time of deposition was applied through the EPD assembly, the positively charged ZnO NPs with adsorbed chitosan macromolecules were forced to move towards the cathode (orthodontic band) and were deposited on it[21]. Fig. 1 shows the schematic representation of Nanocomposite coating deposited on an orthodontic band via EPD at optimized parameters.

Multiple samples of orthodontic bands were Nanocomposite-coated and subjected to different characterization techniques required for orthodontic band applications. For comparison uncoated orthodontic band was taken as the control. Coatings were allowed to dry under ambient conditions before testing.

Characterization of the nanocomposite coating

Morphological analysis of nanocomposite coating

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) model (FESEM, LEO 435VP, Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) analysis of the Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic band was conducted to analyze the morphology and porosity of the Nanocomposite coating. Furthermore, the thickness of the Nanocomposite coating was also determined by SEM. Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic bands were sputtered by (Q150/S, Quorum Technologies) with gold and palladium nanoparticles up to a thickness of ~ 5–7 nm to make them conductive to avoid charging artefacts during analysis.

Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy

Energy dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) was also conducted to understand the elemental composition of the Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic bands. The electron beam from SEM was directed at the sample to produce X-rays. Each X-ray produced corresponded to a unique element and was collected by a silicon drift detector. The signal was measured and interpreted by an EDX detector. Furthermore, the elemental mapping of the Nanocomposite coating at cross-section was also performed to visualize the spatial distribution of ZnO NPs in the chitosan matrix.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy

Functional groups present in Nanocomposite coating were identified by the Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) model (Nicolet 6700, Summit lite, Thermo Scientific™). The spectrum was recorded in the range of wavenumbers 4000 to 500 cm− 1 at 40 scans per spectrum with a resolution rate of 4 cm− 1.

Contact angle

The wettability of the Nanocomposite coating was evaluated by contact angle test (sessile drop method). Artificial saliva (Nigheban Pharmacy, Karachi) was used for contact angle analysis. The orthodontic band was extended to expose a flat surface. A droplet of 5 µl of saliva was dispensed on the surface of the control and Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic band, and images were taken. The images were analyzed using Image J software to determine the contact angle of the coating. The contact angle was measured three times for the control and Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic band [22].

Adhesion strength

The adhesion strength of the Nanocomposite coating was evaluated by the crosshatch test (adhesion tester) according to ASTM D3359. A crosshatch cutter was used to make a series of parallel cuts on the extended flat surface of the Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic band, to create grid patterns. Adhesive tape was applied to the Nanocomposite coating and pulled off with a forceful jerk. The adhesion strength of the Nanocomposite coating was assessed by the amount that remained intact with the orthodontic band. The results were compared with the ISO standard 2409 scoring of cross-cut test results [23].

Antibacterial analysis

The antibacterial activity of the Nanocomposite coating deposited on the orthodontic band was analyzed by the turbidity test. Microdiluted broth containing S. mutans was prepared and poured into a 24-well plate and standardized using a UV/VIS spectrophotometer (BIOBASE, Germany). The wavelength of the spectrophotometer was adjusted to 600 nm (OD600) with absorbance at 0.015. The transmittance of the broth was set at 100%. Whole control samples and Nanocomposite- coated orthodontic bands (n = 3) were completely immersed in the microdiluted broth and placed in an incubator at 35 °C. Upon removal at predetermined intervals of 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, and 28 days, the broth containing the samples was visually examined for turbidity. To quantify the visual result, the optical density of each sample’s broth was determined by comparing it with the optical density of blank broth. The presence of bacteria was indicated by a higher value of optical density. The greater the bacterial proliferation in the broth, the higher the OD value, thus lower the antibacterial property of the sample [24].

pH and degradation analysis

pH and degradation analysis were conducted using artificial saliva as a storage medium. The initial pH of artificial saliva was checked through a pH/ORP Meter (HANNA Instruments HI 2211, Italy). The control samples (orthodontic bands) and Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic bands (n = 3) were placed in 15 ml of artificial saliva in falcon tubes, which were then placed in a shaking incubator (BIOBASE, Germany) at 37 °C and 80 rpm to mimic the dynamic oral environment. After each 24 h, the artificial saliva was refreshed to imitate the flushing action of saliva in the oral cavity. The pH of artificial saliva was checked at predetermined intervals of 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, and 28 days. The percentage mass loss (Mass loss%) at each interval was also calculated by the formula in Eq. (1).

Zinc ions release

Zinc ions leaching behavior from the Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic band (n = 3) was assessed through Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES Spectrometer Thermoscientific CAP 6000, USA). Many samples of 1 ml were collected from the artificial saliva used in pH and degradation analysis at the intervals of 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, and 28 days. Collected samples were checked for the presence of zinc ions at a concentration of µg/litre.

Electrochemical analysis

The barrier properties of the Nanocomposite coating and control (orthodontic band) were analyzed by a Potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) test conducted by a potentiostat/galvanostatic model (GAMRY Reference 600, USA) in simulated body fluid (SBF). In this analysis, a three-electrode system was used in which the Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic band and the control sample were taken as the working electrode, while the graphite and Ag/AgCl electrodes acted as counter and reference electrodes, respectively. Barrier properties of the control and Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic band were evaluated, and a comparison between their extracted values was drawn. The samples were polarized from − 1000 mV to −200 mV with a scan rate of 1 mV/sec. Both curves were analyzed in corrosion view (Corr View) software to extract the valuable parameters like corrosion potential, corrosion current density, and corrosion rate of each sample by auto-fit Tafel mode.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was done by using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS), IBM Version 21. Means were calculated. Comparison of variables between Nanocomposite-coated and uncoated (orthodontic band) groups was carried out by independent t-test and Independent One-way ANOVA. The p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Kinetics and hamaker’s model

By comprehending the kinetics of deposition, it is possible to manipulate the deposits’ thickness and shape to suit the EPD process’s intended use. Prior studies have shown that the relationship between applied deposition voltage and deposition yield should ideally be linear. In the EPD process, surface area (cm2), applied deposition voltage (V), deposition time (min), and suspension concentration, all have a direct impact on the deposition rate [25]. Here, different deposition voltages and deposition times for Nanocomposite coating were used to examine the kinetics of the EPD process for its coating. Figure 2 shows the effect of applied potential and deposition time on deposition yield (%) of Nanocomposite coating deposited on orthodontic bands at varying parameters of EPD. Previous literature has suggested that use of high voltage for EPD results in non-uniform, cracked coatings [20]. Normally, as the applied potential or deposition time is increased, the deposition yield increases linearly up to 7 min, but as the applied potential or deposition time is increased from 5 min, this linear relationship between deposition yield and EPD parameters vanishes and deposition yield changes abruptly [26]. Fig. 2A shows three curves, A, B, and C, between the applied potential (V) and deposition yield (%). Curve A is associated with the applied potential of 5 V, 10 V, and 15 V, with a constant deposition time of 3 min. Reduced deposition of the Nanocomposite coating was observed for these applied voltages. Curves B and C of Fig. 2A showed almost similar behavior, i.e. as the potential was increased from 5 V to 15 V, the deposition yields of both curves also increased while keeping the deposition time, 5 min and 7 min, constant. More depositions occurred at 15 V with a deposition time of 7 min. Furthermore, in Fig. 2B, curve A associated with varying the deposition time from 3 min to 7 min while keeping the applied potential 5 V constant, shows the deposition to be low because the applied voltage was not enough to attract the charged particles of ZnO NPs to deposit on the cathode. Curves B and C of Fig. 2B showed almost similar behavior as the deposition time increased, the deposition yield (%) also increased. Because at higher applied potentials, 10 V to 15 V, the positively charged particles of ZnO NPs were forced to move toward the negatively charged cathode (orthodontic band) with a higher time of deposition.

A shows the effect of applied potential on deposition yield (%) at constant time of deposition while (B) shows the effect of deposition time on the deposition yield (%) keeping potential constant

Morphological analysis

Figure 3 shows the SEM images of Nanocomposite coating deposited on the orthodontic band at optimized parameters (15 V with 5 min deposition time). Figure 3A revealed that the Nanocomposite coating exhibited almost uniform morphology at a magnification of 10 KX. The flower-like structure of ZnO NPs was uniformly distributed over the entire surface of the Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic band. Porosity of the coating was qualitatively analysed from SEM images. Localized areas of reduced density of ZnO NPs were observed. Despite these variations, the Nanocomposite coating effectively covered the entire surface of the orthodontic band. Figure 3B shows the morphology of the Nanocomposite coating at high magnification of 50 KX, which revealed that ZnO NPs present in the Nanocomposite coating exhibited a flower-like structure. Figure 3C showed that the Nanocomposite coating exhibited a thickness of ~ 5.11 μm, which was in line with previously conducted studies [27].

A and B shows the morphological analysis of the Nanocomposite coating while (C) shows the thickness of the Nanocomposite coating deposited on the orthodontic band. D shows the EDX Spectrum of the Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic band

EDX analysis of the Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic band was carried out to determine the elemental composition. EDX spectrum of the Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic band revealed the presence of Zinc (Zn) and Iron (Fe) with small amounts of chromium, oxygen, carbon, and aluminum [27]. The quantitative analysis revealed the concentration of zinc and iron to be 43.83 wt% and 37.14 wt% respectively as illustrated in the inset Table in the EDX spectrum in Fig. 3D. The presence of chitosan in the Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic band was indicated by peaks of carbon and oxygen in the EDX spectrum [28]. Nitrogen, a constituent element of Chitosan was not distinctly detected. This is in line with previous studies where low nitrogen content and its limited sensitivity to EDX makes detection difficult [29].

Although, EDX confirmed the successful incorporation of ZnO NPs as demonstrated by the EDX spectrum, the elemental mapping of the Nanocomposite coating was also conducted which also confirmed the presence of ZnO NPs in the Nanocomposite coating. The EDX mapping conducted on the thickness of Nanocomposite coating also provided a deeper understanding of elemental composition and spatial arrangement of key elements of Nanocomposite coating deposited on an orthodontic band via EPD. Functional group identification of ZnO NPs

FTIR analysis of Nanocomposite coating represented the fingerprint region of ZnO NPs ranging from 1000 to 500 cm− 1 as shown in Fig. 5. The peak at 3260 cm− 1 indicated the presence of a hydroxyl group. FTIR spectrum also showed peaks that indicated the presence of alcohol. The presence of a hydroxyl group in ZnO NPs was reported in the existing literature as a result of atmospheric moisture and the use of water during its synthesis [30]. The presence of alcohol is also consistent with previously conducted studies on ZnO NPs [31]. FTIR analysis confirmed the elemental composition of ZnO NPs. In addition, a characteristic peak corresponding to the amide group of chitosan was observed in the range of 1470 to 1390 cm− 1 indicating the presence of chitosan in the Nanocomposite coating [32].

Antibacterial analysis

Figure 8 shows the results of the turbidity test of the Nanocomposite coating and a comparison between the values of Optical Density (OD) of the control and Nanocomposite coating. As observed in the d, e, and f wells of 24-well plates in Fig. 8A, containing orthodontic bands (control sample), show high turbidity, indicating the presence and growth of S. mutans, while in the a, b, and c wells of 24 24-well plate containing Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic bands, there is no turbidity. These results show that the Nanocomposite coating deposited on the orthodontic band exhibits strong antibacterial efficacy and resists the growth of S. mutans. In this study, a statistically significant difference (p-value ≤ 0.05) between the antibacterial activity of experimental and control was observed. The optical density of the Nanocomposite coating was less than that of the control at each point in time (1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, and 28 days) as shown in Fig. 8B.

Discussion

In this study, the Nanocomposite coating composed of chitosan and ZnO NPs was coated on commercially available stainless steel orthodontic bands via EPD. The use of optimized parameters i.e. 15 V and 5 min deposition time allow a uniform deposition of the Nanocomposite coating. The nanoflower like structure observed at 50kx magnification as shown in Fig. 3B is acquired through the clumping of nanorods [33]. The optimal coating thickness for a single coating ranges from 2 to 10 μm[27]. The ZnO-Chitosan coating deposited by EPD in this study is 5.11 μm thick. Mechanical properties of the substrates have been proven to be refined by a coating thickness of 5 μm[34]. Antibacterial coatings of titanium oxide and titanium nitride have been deposited with a thickness of 5 μm[35].

Figure 4 shows an elemental map of Nanocomposite coating superimposed on secondary electrons of the cross-section of the Nanocomposite coating. Elemental maps of each individual element that was present in the Nanocomposite coating were displayed as Fe, C, Zn, and O which demonstrated their presence in the Nanocomposite coating. Furthermore, the presence of carbon and zinc confirmed the successful incorporation of chitosan and zinc nanoparticles in the Nanocomposite coating.

Chitosan is hydrophilic due to amine and hydroxyl groups which are polar and have an affinity to form hydrogen bonding with molecules of water. Furthermore, ZnO NPs are also hydrophilic in nature because Zn atoms form zinc hydroxide by adsorbing the hydroxyl group. Both chitosan and ZnO NPs were responsible for decreasing the contact angle of the control sample (orthodontic band) after depositing the Nanocomposite coating [36]. The lower value of the contact angle of the Nanocomposite coating suggests a hydrophilic nature indicating greater wettability. In addition, it has been established that greater hydrophilicity of ZnO NPs improves its antimicrobial function [37]. Chitosan is also considered a hydrophilic antimicrobial polymer that acts as a barrier resisting the attachment of bacteria. The hydrophilic nature of the prepared Nanocomposite coating is attributed to the amine group of chitosan and oxygen present in ZnO NPs. Many studies have shown the effect a hydrophilic surface has on bacterial adhesion and subsequent bacterial activity. The increased hydrophilicity is useful for dental applications since bacterial adhesion is considerably lower for a hydrophilic surface rather than a hydrophobic surface [38]. A study conducted in 2020 has shown that the coating of hydrophilic nanoparticles can lead to improved antimicrobial properties [38].

The visual result of cross-hatch adhesion test, in which the coating appears to flake along the edges of the square patterns as shown in Fig. 7, indicates the moderate delamination of Nanocomposite coating which was rated as 3B according to ISO class results (ISO class 2/ASTM class:3B). Heise et al.. reported similar results in their research involving silicon oxide and chitosan coating on stainless steel substrate [39].

In this study, a turbidity test was attempted to evaluate the antibacterial activity of the experimental samples. The optical density of the experimental group was lesser than the control group at each point in time. Hammad in 2020 and Zeidan in 2022, deposited the ceramic coating of ZnO NPs on orthodontic wires and brackets, respectively, and reported that the ceramic coating imparted the antibacterial activity [39, 40]. It is suggested that this antibacterial activity may be because of the loosely bonded ZnO NPs on the surface of the Nanocomposite coatings. This finding is similar to another study conducted in 2017 in which a higher rate of zinc ions release was observed initially [41]. The increase in the antibacterial activity corresponded with the high release of zinc ions, also observed on day 6. The pH study demonstrated a statistically significant increase on day 6. It has been observed that zinc ions released at a higher pH are much more stable and sustained [42]. It is suggested that, as the zinc ions from the surface of the coating were utilized, their release rate decreased, leading to a decrease in antibacterial activity. It can be concluded that the Nanocomposite coating exhibited an antibacterial effect against S. mutans. Despite the decrease observed in the antibacterial efficacy, the bacterial count for the Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic band remained significantly lower as compared to that of the control orthodontic band. The results of the Nanocomposite coating in this study are similar to other researches that proves ZnO NPs to be an effective antibacterial coating agent [43, 44].

Artificial saliva in experimental group showed higher pH than the saliva in control group at all-time intervals. It has been reported that zinc ion release is stable and sustained at a higher pH as compared to its rapid release at lower pH. [42] The higher pH was also beneficial as it promoted greater antimicrobial activity of zinc ions and greater corrosion resistance of the orthodontic band [45]. The sustained physiological pH proves that the Nanocomposite-coated orthodontic bands did not cause an abnormal change in pH that may be harmful to the oral environment.

It was indicated in this study that the degradation of the Nanocomposite coating was lower than the control because of the increased corrosion resistance provided by the oxide layer that resisted the loss of ions [46]. This result demonstrated that the Nanocomposite coating deposited on the orthodontic band was a corrosion-resistant coating which showed less degradation, as assessed in terms of mass loss percent, than the control [47]. The high initial zinc ions release was due to the loosely bonded zinc atoms at the surface. The decrease in the zinc ions suggested that the strongly bonded zinc ions were released at a later stage. An increase in zinc release was also observed from day 3 to day 6. It suggested that as the pH increased, there was a higher release of zinc ions and higher antibacterial activity.

The barrier properties of the orthodontic band were improved because of the Nanocomposite coating. Chitosan in itself has the ability to form a barrier film on coated substrate protecting its surface from corrosion [48]. The anodic slope difference demonstrated that the rate of dissolution of the control was decreased by Nanocomposite coating. The higher cathodic slope indicated that the tendency of reduction reactions was reduced by Nanocomposite coating. PDP results were consistent with previous results reported in the literature while demonstrating an improvement in barrier properties illustrated in terms of corrosion parameters due to Nanocomposite coating [49, 50]. As a limitation of this study, considerable resources were required to optimize the coating methodology and parameters, which limited the number of specimens tested. Additionally, time constraints prevented the evaluation of the coating’s long-term performance.

The present study demonstrated the synthesis of ZnO NPs and their incorporation in chitosan based Nanocomposite coating, which was deposited on orthodontic bands via EPD for potential antibacterial properties.
