Lesionectomy as management for super-refractory status epilepticus due to focal cortical dysplasia: illustrative case

Status epilepticus (SE) is a neurological emergency characterized by high morbidity and mortality rates.1 It is defined as a condition resulting either from the failure of the mechanisms responsible for seizure termination or from the initiation of mechanisms, leading to abnormally prolonged seizures (after time point 1). It is a condition that can have long-term consequences (after time point 2), including neuronal death, neuronal injury, and alteration of neuronal networks, depending on the type and duration of seizure.2

When seizure activity persists despite the administration of at least two appropriately selected and adequately dosed intravenous medications, including benzodiazepines, it is classified as refractory SE (RSE).3 In addition to RSE, the condition known as super-refractory SE (SRSE) is defined by seizure activity persisting for at least 24 hours after the initiation of anesthesia; this may occur without interruption despite appropriate anesthetic treatment, may recur while under adequate anesthetic therapy, or may relapse following the withdrawal of anesthesia, requiring its reintroduction.3

Currently, no standardized protocols exist for treating SRSE, making this an important focus for future research. Management of SRSE typically occurs in intensive care units (ICUs) equipped with mechanical ventilation, comprehensive cardiovascular monitoring, and continuous video electroencephalography (EEG). Initial treatment involves using anesthetic agents and antiseizure medications (ASMs). In refractory cases, alternative interventions may include resective surgery, neuromodulation devices, immunomodulation, a ketogenic diet, pyridoxine infusion, ketamine, and magnesium infusion.4 However, evidence remains poor for most of these therapeutic options (level U with class IV studies). There is only one randomized controlled trial for clinical hypothermia (HYBERNATUS trial),5 which showed no functional difference at 3 months.6 This situation presents a challenge for both neurologists and intensive care specialists in treating SRSE.

Focal cortical dysplasia (FCD) is a common cause of focal epilepsy. Approximately 76% of these cases are drug resistant, often requiring surgical intervention to achieve seizure control.7,8 The most commonly performed surgical procedure in such cases is focal resection targeting the identified lesion.9,10 Surgical treatment in SRSE is increasingly reported, particularly in patients with identifiable focal lesions such as FCD.11 However, the timing of surgical intervention remains a matter of controversy. While early resection has been associated with improved outcomes in some series, real-world clinical practice often involves delays. As reported previously, in-hospital and system-related factors can lead to delays of several years from diagnosis to surgery, and even years between confirmed drug resistance and surgical treatment.12,13

We present a rare case of SRSE secondary to FCD, managed successfully with resective surgery. This case highlights an uncommon but effective therapeutic strategy for SRSE and provides a brief review of similar reports in the literature. Additionally, we reflect on the potential consequences of surgical delay, particularly in patients with known epileptogenic lesions.

Illustrative Case

A 25-year-old Latin American female with a history of focal epilepsy diagnosed at the age of 3 years was admitted to the emergency department due to worsening seizures. Her seizures (usually occurring during sleep) were characterized by a focal onset with vocalization, followed by impaired awareness, head and ocular deviation to the right, followed by bilateral manual automatisms such as adjusting her clothing, ultimately progressing to bilateral tonic-clonic movements. All the aforementioned manifestations lasted approximately 1 minute, followed by somnolence and disorientation that lasted for 10 minutes. A few months prior to admission, she had an increase in seizure frequency characterized by seizure clusters consisting of 5–10 seizures a day, 4–5 times per week, not related to either medication withdrawal or catamenial pattern. At that time, the patient was on levetiracetam (LEV) 1.5 g twice daily (BID) and lacosamide (LCM) 100 mg BID. No prior history of SE was reported. Additionally, comorbidity with major depressive disorder was managed with escitalopram 10 mg every night at bedtime and alprazolam 0.5 mg BID.

Regarding the primary complaint, she experienced 20 seizures within 24 hours, requiring emergent care. On admission to the emergency department, she was administered 10 mg of intramuscular midazolam and a 1-g loading dose of intravenous phenytoin. Her ASM regimen was adjusted to LEV 2 g BID and LCM 200 mg BID. Despite initial management, she kept having seizures progressing to RSE, requiring third-line treatment with midazolam infusion, propofol, and mechanical ventilation. She was immediately admitted to the ICU.

The patient was monitored with continuous scalp EEG that demonstrated persistent nonconvulsive SE with bilateral frontocentral epileptiform activity, predominantly right-sided (Fig. 1). Her previous interictal EEG studies demonstrated epileptiform activity consistent with the findings of the continuous monitoring. Worsening in the epileptiform activity led to an increase in sedation and the introduction of sevoflurane. Despite multiple attempts to reduce sedation during observed periods in which the patient’s recordings did not show abnormalities consistent with nonconvulsive SE, while weaning sedation, a recurrence of the nonconvulsive SE on continuous EEG was observed. This made sedation withdrawal impossible, leading to the classification of the patient as having prolonged SRSE.

The case was presented at a multidisciplinary epilepsy surgery meeting, where the medical team reviewed the patient’s prior management, clinical history, previous evaluations, and diagnostic studies to determine the most suitable therapeutic approach. Additionally, former neuropsychological evaluations revealed an anatomical correlation of abnormalities involving the right temporal cortex and right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. Following the session, fourth-line therapy was proposed, recommending resective surgery (lesionectomy) as a therapeutic intervention.

A right frontal craniotomy measuring 4 × 4 cm was performed, with the bone flap centered 1 cm posterior and 4 cm anterior to the coronal suture and extending 3.5 cm laterally. After removal of the bone flap, a durotomy was performed with the base oriented toward the superior sagittal sinus. A 16-channel electrocorticography (ECoG) grid was placed over the cortical surface, demonstrating persistent epileptiform activity corresponding to the topography of FCD previously identified on neuroimaging. Intraoperative ECoG confirmed that this activity localized to the rostral third of the right F1 gyrus. Microsurgical resection was initiated, and an en bloc lesionectomy of the rostral one-third segment of the F1 gyrus was performed, including adjacent subcortical white matter (Fig. 2D and E). Hemostasis of the surgical bed was verified, and Surgicel was applied. Reconstruction of the surgical approach was then completed in standard fashion. At the end of the resection, ECoG demonstrated the absence of epileptiform discharges along the resection margins, with no residual activity in the surrounding right frontal cortex.

Histopathological examination confirmed FCD type IIB, with evidence of dysmorphic neurons, balloon cells scattered along the bottom of the sulcus, and deep white matter (Figs. 3 and 4).

Postoperatively, the patient was monitored in the ICU under sedation for 24 hours, followed by gradual awakening and continuous EEG surveillance. She was successfully weaned off mechanical ventilation and remained seizure free during hospitalization. Following clinical recovery, her ASM regimen was optimized, changing LEV to brivaracetam and continuing LCM. At follow-up evaluations, she remained neurologically stable with significant seizure improvement, classified as International League Against Epilepsy class 1 or Engel class Ib at the 1-year follow-up, demonstrating functional recovery and no recurrence of SE.

Discussion

Herein, we report the case of a 25-year-old female with SRSE due to FCD, which remarkably resolved after lesionectomy. Following the surgical procedure, the resected tissue was sent to the neuropathology department for histopathological analysis, confirming the presence of FCD.

FCD is a common cortical developmental malformation due to abnormal postmigrational development, resulting in dysmorphic neurons that are highly epileptogenic and a major cause of drug-resistant epilepsy.14 It is characterized by irregular dysmorphic neurons and microscopically evident architectural disorganization in the neocortex. Histologically, the Palmini classification initially subdivided FCD into two subtypes.15 However, in 2011, Blümcke et al. updated the FCD classification, distinguishing three subtypes: type I consists of FCD with abnormal radial cortical lamination (type Ia), FCD with abnormal tangential cortical lamination (type Ib), or FCD with both abnormal radial and tangential cortical lamination (type Ic). Type II includes FCD with dysmorphic neurons (type IIa) and FCD with dysmorphic neurons and balloon cells (type IIb). Type III refers to architectural distortion of cortical layers associated with various conditions: architectural distortion in the temporal lobe with hippocampal atrophy (type IIIa), architectural distortion adjacent to glial or glioneuronal tumors (type IIIb), architectural distortion adjacent to vascular malformations (type IIIc), and architectural distortion adjacent to other acquired lesions in early childhood, such as trauma, ischemic events, or encephalitis (type IIId).16 In this case, histological examination confirmed type IIb FCD, distinguished by dysmorphic neurons and balloon cells, primarily located at the base of the sulcus and extending into the deep white matter. These abnormalities confer particularly high epileptogenic potential and significantly increase the likelihood of earlier seizure onset.8,16

The patient’s seizures were consistent with focal seizures with a frontal lobe onset that progressed to bilateral tonic-clonic. A study by Fauser et al. reported variations in seizure semiology, noting that generalized tonic-clonic seizures were the most common, followed by tonic and hypermotor seizures.8 Similarly, Kuzniecky et al. highlighted a predominance of unilateral or bilateral tonic motor seizures, often accompanied by secondary generalized tonic-clonic seizures preceded by focal symptoms.17 Likewise, in some pediatric cohorts with FCD, tonic seizures, clonic seizures, and spasms have been described as the most frequently presented.18 Rarely, gelastic seizures have also been reported.19 The presentation of seizures varies depending on the patient’s age and the location of the epileptic focus.16

SRSE poses one of the most challenging scenarios in epilepsy management, due to its association with prolonged ICU stays, systemic complications, and permanent excitotoxic neuronal injury. Current treatment protocols emphasize a staged escalation: benzodiazepines as first-line therapy, followed by intravenous ASMs such as phenytoin, phenobarbital, or valproate, and finally induction of general anesthesia titrated to EEG burst suppression when seizures persist. Despite this structured approach, a subset of patients continue to seize, leading to SRSE. In such cases, alternative strategies include immunotherapy, a ketogenic diet, hypothermia, neuromodulation, or emergency resective surgery.4

Although FCD is a well-recognized cause of lesional epilepsy, several reported cases have progressed to SE or SRSE, yet only a few instances have been successfully managed with surgery, primarily involving pediatric patients.9,10 Reported surgical treatments include lesionectomy, hemispherotomy, lobectomy, corticectomy, and 1 case requiring both callosotomy and vagus nerve stimulator implantation (Table 1).20 In this context, our case is an adult patient with SRSE successfully treated with a single lesionectomy guided solely by ECoG. Supporting this, a meta-analysis by Niazi et al. demonstrated that resective surgery in patients with RSE or SRSE was associated with higher SE cessation rates and improved long-term seizure control. Conversely, prolonged SE can promote the development of widespread epileptic networks, leading to poorer outcomes. In cases in which resection is not feasible, disconnective procedures such as corpus callosotomy and multiple subpial transection have proven to be effective.21

Consistent with these observations, Jha et al. reported 157 epilepsy surgeries in patients with SE-identified FCD as the second common etiology.22 This finding underscores that, despite being a well-recognized cause of lesional epilepsy, FCD can still progress to SRSE, mainly due to delays in diagnosis and referral for epilepsy surgery.13 Also, a previously reported series from our group reported in-hospital and system-related factors that can lead to delays of several years from diagnosis to surgery, and even years between confirmed drug resistance and surgical treatment, and also a delay regarding the waiting time between the first epilepsy consultation and epilepsy surgery.12

Notably, in this case, no prior intracranial EEG monitoring was performed; intraoperative ECoG was the key tool guiding resection. While ECoG is useful for intraoperative localization, its limitations must be recognized when used without prior intracranial EEG, including its focus on monitoring predominantly interictal discharges, restricted time and cortical coverage available during surgery, and high susceptibility to anesthetic effects. Moreover, experts disagree on the significance of epileptiform patterns and the prognostic value of residual discharges on postresection ECoG.23

Observations

This case underscores the importance of early surgical consideration in SRSE to minimize complications from prolonged seizures and delayed resection, remembering that “time is brain” also applies to epilepsy surgery. Intraoperative ECoG-guided lesionectomy achieved sustained seizure freedom at 1 year in an adult patient with SRSE secondary to FCD. These findings support the feasibility of tailored surgical approaches for SRSE, even in resource-limited settings where access to advanced monitoring is limited.24 More broadly, it illustrates the persistent challenge of delayed referral for epilepsy surgery and the need to prioritize timely surgical evaluation in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy, including those with lesional etiologies.25,26

Lessons

Currently, although no consensus or clinical guidelines provide specific indications for considering epilepsy surgery, in this case, lesionectomy combined with intraoperative ECoG proved to be a viable treatment strategy for SRSE or SE secondary to FCD in an adult patient. This approach may be effective in selected cases in which other treatments have failed. The case highlights the potential role of surgical intervention, particularly lesionectomy, in managing SRSE or SE and underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in optimizing patient outcomes.

Epilepsy surgery delay is an important barrier for the treatment of patients with drug-resistant epilepsy, especially in low- and middle-income countries. Improving the time to surgical treatment could avoid catastrophic complications, such as SRSE in patients with resectable lesions.
