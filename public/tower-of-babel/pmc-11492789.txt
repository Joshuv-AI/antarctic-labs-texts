Is domestic violence during COVID-19 in lactating women related to infantile colic? An important question
Authors: Masoumeh Simbar, Zahra Kiani, Negin Shaterian, Mohammadamin Jandaghian Bidgoli, Negar Shaterian, Ghasem Bayani, Farzaneh Rashidi, Maliheh Nasiri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsychosocial factors such as maternal stress in lactating women were shown to be related to an increased risk of infantile colic because the infants can be affected through breast milk. Therefore, domestic violence against lactating mothers can be effective on the infants’ health. It is also demonstrated that domestic violence against women has increased following the COVID-19 pandemic and quarantine. Therefore, this study aims to assess the relationship between domestic violence against breastfeeding women during COVID-19 and the incidence of infantile colic.MethodsThis was a case-control study that was conducted on 356 lactating mothers who attended Bentolhoda Hospital in Bojnoord-Iran from November 2021 to August 2022. The participants were exclusively breastfeeding mothers who were under 6 months infants. Mothers with and without infantile colic infants were considered the case and control groups, respectively. Two questionnaires were used for data collection including a demographic questionnaire, and a questionnaire to assess domestic violence against women during the quarantine period of Corona pandemic. The data was analyzed using SPSS-V22 and Pearson Spearman correlation and linear regression tests.ResultsThe results showed that women in the case group had a significantly higher score for “sexual violence and restraint” than women in the control group [0.59 ± 0.34 versus 0.62 ± 0.25 (Mean ± SD), respectively] (P < 0.05). The chance of infantile colic was higher among the infants of mothers who experienced domestic violence, but it was not statistically significant (OR = 1.236, P = 0.573). Significant relationships were found between the infantile colic and the history of abortion (OR = 1.430, P = 0.028) and cesarean section (OR = 1.723, P = 0.044).ConclusionDomestic violence during pregnancy can potentially increase infant colic through breast milk. Although our findings need more investigation, it can be recommended to plan the direction of screening for domestic violence and providing the necessary care and counseling to the parents in perinatal care services to prevent infantile colic and improve the infant’s health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20377-0.

Background

Infantile colic is a common complication among infants [1]. The most common definition for infantile colic is based on Wessel’s “rule of threes” including unexplained episodes of paroxysmal crying for longer than three hours per day, for three days per week, for at least three weeks [2]. In a more recent definition, digestive functional disorders are also included in the definition of infantile colic (Rome IV diagnostic criteria), which includes irritability and restlessness for at least one week in an infant who does not have any problems in her/his development [3]. The prevalence of infantile colic is reported to be 17–25% [4]. In Iran, the prevalence of infant colic is 20% [5].

Infantile colic is associated with maternal depression [6]. Besides, there are significant relationships between the infant’s crying related to infantile colic: the cessation of breastfeeding, weak parent-child relationship, and child abuse [7]. Increased risk of some other consequences of infantile colic among children was also suggested to be emotional vulnerability, sleep disorders, hyperactivity, Low IQ, asthma, and other atopic disorders [8]. Besides, infantile colic can be related to parents’ relationships as well as parent-child relationships [1].

Domestic violence is a global concern and it is affecting parents’ health and relationships [9]. Besides, COVID-19 as a rapidly spreading disease in Iran and worldwide created a major public health crisis [10]. Following the COVID-19 pandemic, countries turned to the “Stay at home” policy to prevent infection that caused significant disruption in people’s daily lives [11]. An increased rate of violence against women has been demonstrated since the beginning of the quarantine due to the COVID-19 pandemic [12].

According to the World Health Organization, about one-third of all women who were in a relationship experienced physical or sexual violence by their intimate partner, and in some areas, 38% of women were abused [13]. In a cross-sectional study conducted on 203 Iranian women, the rate of domestic violence against women was 34.9% during the COVID-19 pandemic. Also, the rates of physical, emotional, and sexual violence were 26.6%, 26.1%, and 21.2%, respectively [14]. In a cross-sectional study conducted in Iran in 2023 on 933 women of reproductive age, the average rate of domestic violence was 44% [15]. In a systematic review and meta-analysis, which represent a sample size of 15,514 women, the prevalence of domestic violence in Iran was estimated at 66% [16].

Several factors are suggested to be involved in increasing the rate and severity of domestic violence during the COVID-19 pandemic including [1] increasing the duration of the relationship between the victim and the perpetrator of violence [2], increasing the duration of quarantine, and decreasing social mobility, and so limiting the ways for helping women who are victims of violence [3], increasing situational stressors related to domestic violence (such as financial stress and lack of job security) [4], loss of self-control in perpetrators of violence, due to the conditions of situational factors and using violence and abuse as a tool for creating a sense of control and [5], increasing alcohol consumption among perpetrators of domestic violence [17].

A study showed that malnutrition, fever, diarrhea, and cough are more common among children of mothers who experience violence from their partners [18]. In addition, Another study also indicated that the prevalence of infantile colic is related to the lack of proper parental relationships [19]. In another descriptive study on 154 babies between 3 and 6 months, findings showed a significant relationship between domestic violence during the mother’s pregnancy and infant colic [20]. However, in a cohort study on 1086 infants to investigate the factors of infantile colic, no relationship was observed between the type of partner’s relationship with colic [21]. In a study on 68 women who experienced domestic violence and 134 women without domestic violence during pregnancy, the results showed that the prevalence of colic in 2-month-old infant is higher in the group who experienced domestic violence during pregnancy, but this difference between the two groups was not significant [22]. Information on the relationship between domestic violence and infantile colic is scarce and controversial.

A systematic review in 2021, findings showed that pregnant women who experienced domestic violence during pregnancy had higher levels of depression and the likelihood that infants would develop colic through breast milk [23]. Breastfeeding is one of the most important ways of mother-infant communication that creates positive emotions and a strong bond [24, 25]. Breast milk is the most valuable food source for improving maternal and infant health as well as the maternal-child emotional relationship [26]. Breastfeeding improves maternal-infant bonding and maternal mental health which can be effective on an infant’s health [20].

Infantile colic diagnosis is important because its continuation can cause anxiety and fatigue for families. Considering the maternal-infant relationship through breast milk, domestic violence against breastfeeding women can affect infants’ health. There are a few studies to investigate the relationship between domestic violence against breastfeeding mothers and the incidence of infantile colic before the COVID-19 pandemic [18, 19, 21]. Therefore, this study aims to assess the relationship between domestic violence in breastfeeding women with infantile colic during the COVID-19 pandemic and the quarantine.

Method

This was a case-control study that was conducted on 356 breastfeeding mothers who attended Bentolhoda Hospital in Bojnoord-Iran from November 2021 to August 2022. The participants had infants under 6 months. Mothers with and without infantile colic infants were considered the case and control groups, respectively.

Inclusion criteria for both case and control groups

The participants were exclusively breastfeeding mothers who were under 6 months infants with a birth weight over 2500 g and 38 to 42 weeks at birth. They had a permanent marriage. The participants had no history of medical or mental illness or affected by COVID-19, or experienced grief in the last 6 months. The infants also had no underlying disease. Unwillingness to participate in the study was the exclusion criterion.

The infantile diagnosis criteria were based on Wessel’s “rule of threes”: unexplained episodes of paroxysmal crying for longer than three hours per day, for three days per week, for at least three weeks [8], and the physician’s approval. The only difference between the case and the control groups was whether or not the infant had colic.

A total of 356 mothers and their infants (between 2 and 6 months) in two case (n = 198) and control (n = 158) groups were recruited for the study. It was a 1:1 ratio between mother and infant. The participants of both, the control and the case groups were selected from the same hospital to reduce the possibility of selection bias, using the convenience sampling method.

Tools for data collection

Women with the eligibility criteria and willing to participate in the study completed two questionnaires including a demographic-obstetrics questionnaire and a questionnaire to assess domestic violence against women during the quarantine period of the pandemic. The questionnaires were completed electronically using Google Forms by the mothers, and the link to the questionnaires was sent to the participants via WhatsApp, Telegram, Instagram, and email. The questionnaires were completed by the mothers and all the participants who were invited completed the questionnaires.

Demographic and obstetric questionnaire

This questionnaire was developed based on an extensive literature review [27–30]. This questionnaire contained questions to collect information about the participants’ age, duration of the marriage, education, income, economic status, occupation, number of pregnancies, type of delivery, number of abortions, number of children, history of infantile colic in children, number of marriage, history of depression and sedative use during pregnancy and postpartum, and history of a medical condition (Appendix 1). The qualitative and quantitative content validity [31], of the questionnaire was assessed and confirmed by 10 faculty members of the Department of Midwifery and Reproductive Health affiliated with Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences. The content validity indices of the demographic and Obstetrics sections were 0.94 and 0.92 respectively.

Questionnaire of domestic violence against women during the quarantine period

this questionnaire was developed and its psychometric properties were assessed by Ghanei et al. in 2021 [32] (Appendix 1). The questionnaire contained 17 questions in three dimensions: physical violence (6 items), psychological violence (7 items), and sexual violence and restraint (4 items). The responses were scored 0 for never, 1 for once to twice; 2 for 3 to 5 times; 3 for 6 to 10 times, and 4 for more than 10 times. The total score of domestic violence was obtained from the sum of the physical, psychological, and sexual aspects of the questionnaire. The total scores range from 0 to 68. This questionnaire had an acceptable face, content, and construct validity as well as appropriate reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.9 and McDonald’s omega = 0.84) [32]. The reliability of the questionnaire was confirmed by Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87 and McDonald’s omega = 0.84 in our study.

Sample size

Considering a 95% confidence interval, 90% power, and the calculated effect size which was obtained from a pilot study (The difference in the average score of violence between the two groups (, the sample size was calculated at 131 samples for each group, and therefore, 262 samples were considered for two groups of case and control.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22 and by descriptive statistics such as frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation. The normal distribution of the data was checked with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and then the independent t-test, Mann-Whitney, Fisher’s exact test, Chi-square, and logistic regression were used. 18 independent variables (violence, rank of children, number of children, number of deliveries, history of infantile colic, history of breastfeeding, neonate sex, history of abortion, type of delivery, socioeconomic status, husband’s Job, mother’s Job, husband’s, mother’s education, neonatal age, marital age, father’s age, and mother’s age) were assigned to predict the infantile colic as the dependent variable. The variables with correlation coefficient of at least 0. 3 were included in the regression Model. A stepwise forward logistic regression analysis was performed. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

We tried to control these differences with the regression method. The groups were not significantly different regarding their neonatal age, husband’s job, husband’s education, parity, socioeconomic status, and neonate’s Sex (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

Discussion

This was the first study to assess the relationship between infantile colic with domestic violence against breastfeeding women during COVID-19. The results showed that the average scores of different dimensions of domestic violence among mothers with infantile colic infants are higher than the control group, but the results were not significant except in the sexual violence dimension. Avcı et al. suggested that (2022) violence can raise the mother’s cortisol hormone which can be transferred to the infant through breast milk and cause infantile colic [33]. Sung (2018) also stated that infantile colic is a common and self-limiting disorder that is significantly related to some factors such as maternal depression and psychological disorders, that can affect the infant through breast milk [34]. Colic may be the natural release of accumulated pre- and peri-natal trauma [35].

The logistic regression test demonstrated that the type of delivery is related to infantile colic and also infants born by cesarean section have a higher chance of infantile colic than infants born by normal vaginal delivery. The type of delivery, anesthetic, and delivery processes can be effective on the infants’ digestive system and intestine health [36]. The microbiome is suggested to be an important factor in the mechanism of infantile colic because it is affected by the method of delivery [37]. A microbiome is a collection of microorganisms (such as fungi, bacteria, and viruses) that usually live on certain parts of the body, such as the digestive tract or skin [38]. Since birth, the microbiome has 3 main functions: protective (as a microorganism prevents the entry of pathogens), metabolic (digestion and metabolism of colostrum, breast milk, other foods, vitamin synthesis, detoxification), and trophic (epithelial cell growth and the homeostatic maintenance of the immune system including tolerance to food antigens) [39]. Mothers who gave birth by normal vaginal delivery usually initiate breastfeeding immediately after birth, which seems to be an effective factor in reducing the chance of infantile colic [40, 41]. Pregnancy in early life is the golden time for the establishment of the infant microbiota, which is affected by environmental factors [42].

Consistent with our results, Karacı et al. (2019) indicated that cesarean delivery is a risk factor for infant colic [41]. But, Talachian et al. (2008) presented that the type of delivery is not related to infantile colic [5]. It should be notable that in Talachian’s study, the infants’ age was less than 3 months, while the present study was performed on infants aged up to six months old. Furthermore, the sample size in their study was 65 and 256 in the colic and non-colic groups respectively, while in our study the samples were 198 and 158 in the case and the control groups, respectively. Therefore, there is still no strong evidence to support the difference in the prevalence of infantile colic among infants born by normal vaginal delivery and cesarean section, and due to the multifactorial nature of infantile colic, it seems further correlational studies with a larger sample size are necessary. However, in Iranian society, the cesarean delivery rate is higher than vaginal delivery, and this issue requires more attention. A significant rise was observed in the rates of C-sections during the COVID-19 compared to before the COVID-19 (52.9% vs. 50.8%; p = 0.001) in Iran. Since, cesarean delivery has adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, special attention should be paid to it especially during pandemics such as COVID-19, because we may face similar pandemics in the future [43].

Our findings revealed a significantly higher rate of infantile colic among infants of mothers with a history of abortion than mothers with no history of abortion. Mothers with a history of abortion usually feel more concerned about infants’ health and fear losing them after birth [44]. Infants with infantile colic generally have trouble regulating their emotions, which can reflect the lack of parental relationship regulation. Mothers with a history of abortion may experience some degrees of anxiety and depression during and after pregnancy, which can lead to hormonal changes and transfer to the infant through breast milk and cause restlessness and infantile colic [45]. A systematic review study by Yat et al. about mental health in pregnant and postpartum women showed that the prevalence of mental health problems such as anxiety, depression, and psychological distress has increased in COVID-19 [46]. During the COVID-19, pregnant women were experience more stress, anxiety and depression about their fetus and infant than before COVID-19, because the information about the effect of coronavirus on them was unknown [47], and the fear of repeated abortion was higher in them than women who had no history of abortion [48].

The present study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic which caused many changes in people’s lifestyles. It was shown that women spent more time at home and were exposed to more violence in quarantine which leads to emotional disorders among women [49]. Quarantine conditions at home cause depression and anxiety in pregnant women, and on the other hand, there is a greater tendency for psychological damage during pregnancy, which can aggravate these factors in the context of pandemics, and in low- and middle-income countries due to problems Financial, social and environmental risks are more [50]. Pregnant women were more exposed to domestic violence during the outbreak of COVID-19 which decreased their quality of life [51]. Additionally, there is a physiologic fetal-maternal connection by the placenta during pregnancy. This connection is maintained through breast milk after birth. Therefore, the experience of domestic violence by mothers and the related hormonal changes, such as epinephrine and norepinephrine alterations [52], can be transferred to the infant through the placenta or breast milk [33]. Also, anxiety and depression can be related to the development of colic through transmission of cortisol and microbiome through breast milk [53]. Although there is little information at this time and there is no violation in the studies, some studies such as Saavedra et al. in 2003 did not observe this relationship [21].

The results showed that the average scores of domestic violence against mothers were not significantly different in the case and control groups during the COVID-19 Pandemic. Of course, our findings in this study are influenced by sample size, variation in the measurement of violence; untruthfulness in accountability, recall bias, or underreporting of violence due to stigma or fear, especially during the specific social conditions during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, only in the dimension of “sexual violence and restraint”, the women in the case group had a significantly higher score than women in the control group. Sexual violence is one of the most common types of violence against women, which can occur following the changes in family status and vulnerability of women after childbirth [54] because women are usually concerned about initiating sexual relationships after childbirth and infant care [55]. Specifically, during the first months after childbirth, women are 22 times more likely to develop a mental disorder than at any other time in their lives [56]. Several studies disclosed that sexual violence increases in crises and causes forced intimacy [57–59]. Higher sexual violence scores through some potential physiological pathways such as cortisol transfer through breast milk can affect infants and relate with infantile colic [60, 61]. The results of a systematic review of 2023 findings showed that prenatal maternal stress, characterized by exposure to stress, anxiety, depression, or intimate partner violence is linked to biological alterations in infants, including disruptions to their intestinal microbiota, which have long-term implications for children’s developmental outcomes [53]. During the Covid-19 pandemic, women were worried about the transmission of Corona through sexual intercourse and the negative effects on the fetus and infant [62–64]. These stressors could increase cortisol and transfer of cortisol to infant through breast milk, which then cause infantile colic.

Limitations of the study

One of the main limitations of a case-control study is its limitation to assess the cause-and-effect relationship. These studies are only descriptive and the results are prelude can only make the scientific hypotheses. Given the multifactorial nature of infantile colic, the absence of longitudinal data (to track colic onset or changes in maternal stress over time) weakens the ability to draw causal inferences. In addition, there is a possibility of bias in case-control studies. For instance, selection bias could occur if mothers who experienced higher levels of violence were less likely to participate, potentially affecting the results. Another limitation of the study is the numerical bias related to the fact that cases are more than controls. This imbalance may affect statistical power and the reliability of the findings. There were some controversies in the results with some other studies that were attributed to the different inclusion criteria such as the infants’ age, place of the study, conducting the study during the COVID-19 pandemic, and the sample size. Therefore, there were no possibilities for exact comparing the results of the studies. Besides, due to the lack of a similar study in the COVID-19 pandemic condition, the comparison between studies is not possible, and further investigation seems to be necessary. A potential bias of this study was the recall bias due to self-reporting. We tried to reduce this bias by explaining the goals of the research to women, helping pregnant women under violence and babies with colic, and the value of this information for community health Using platforms like WhatsApp and Instagram for data collection may introduce selection bias, as participants need internet access and familiarity with these platforms. Given the small mean differences in scores, the study may lack the statistical power to detect meaningful differences in some dimensions (e.g., physical and psychological violence).

Strengths of the study

This study was performed for the first time, and with an adequate sample size to investigate the relationship between domestic violence against breastfeeding women during COVID-19 and infantile colic. While the study finds a relationship between domestic violence and infantile colic, it is unclear whether this is truly a causal relationship or a correlation influenced by other factors. More rigorous statistical methods (such as controlling for confounding variables) would be necessary to support any causal claims.

Conclusion

Despite the mentioned limitations, our study was one of the first studies on the relationship between domestic violence and infant colic, and the findings showed that sexual violence, cesarean delivery, and a history of abortion are related to infant colic. These factors are mainly related to infant colic through anxiety, stress, and depression and the effect on the microbiome. There are physiological mechanisms (e.g., stress-induced changes in breastfeeding behavior) or psychological factors (e.g., increased anxiety in the mother) that mediate this relationship. Considering the results, environmental factors (maternal and infant factors like pre-existing maternal mental health issues, infant temperament, or breastfeeding difficulties) can be effective in the incidence of infantile colic. These could all influence the rate of infantile colic, confounding the results. Therefore, reducing mothers’ anxiety, stress, and depression by using screening and simple educational interventions is suggested, especially during pandemics to prevent the complications in mothers and infants.

It is necessary to pay attention to the role of environmental factors in the policies and maternal-child care programs and try to control environmental factors so that the environmental and family problems be evaluated and improve the situation to some extent.

Therefore, this relationship should be considered in future health policies and maternal-child plans, and screening of domestic violence and providing the necessary care and counseling to the parents are suggested in perinatal care to prevent infantile colic and improve the infant’s health.
