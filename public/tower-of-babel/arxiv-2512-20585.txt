Integrable perturbation theory for dark solitons of the defocusing nonlinear Schrödinger equation
Nicholas J. Ossi, Barbara Prinari, Jianke Yang

Abstract
The goal of this work is to revisit the squared-eigenfunctions-based perturbation theory of the scalar defocusing nonlinear Schrödinger equation on a nonzero background, and develop it so that it can correctly predict the slow-time evolution of the dark soliton parameters, as well as the radiation shelf emerging on the sides of the soliton. Proof of the completeness of the squared eigenfunctions is provided. Our closure relation accounts for the singularities of the scattering data at the branch points of the continuous spectrum, which leads to the correct discrete eigenfunctions of the closure relation. Using the one-soliton closure relation and its discrete eigenmodes, the slow-time evolution equations of the soliton parameters are determined. Moreover, the first-order correction integral to the dark soliton is shown to contain a pole due to singularities of the scattering data at the branch points. Analysis of this first-order correction integral leads to predictions for the height, velocity and phase gradient of the shelves, as well as a formula for the slow time evolution of the soliton’s phase, which in turn allows one to determine the slow-time dependence of the soliton center. All the results are corroborated by direct numerical simulations, and compared with earlier results.

Introduction

The mathematical modeling of physical phenomena often leads to a certain class of nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs) known as integrable systems. Distinguished features of integrable systems are that: (i) they admit soliton solutions, and (ii) their initial-value problem can be effectively linearized via the Inverse Scattering Transform (IST). One of the prototypical integrable systems is the nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation:

iq_{t}+q_{xx}-2\sigma|q|^{2}q=0,\qquad\sigma=\pm 1,

where
q(x,t)
is a complex function of
x,t\in\mathbb{R}
, subscripts denote partial differentiation throughout, and
\sigma
distinguishes between anomalous and normal dispersion, with
\sigma=-1
corresponding to the “focusing” NLS, and
\sigma=1
to the “defocusing” NLS.
The NLS equations appear as universal models for weakly dispersive nonlinear wave trains, and have been derived in such diverse fields as deep water waves, plasma physics, nonlinear optical fibers, low-temperature physics and Bose-Einstein condensates, magneto-static spin waves, and more
[
1
,

,

,

,

,

,

,

]
.

Unlike its focusing counterpart, the defocusing NLS does not admit localized bright solitons, exponentially decaying as
|x|\to\infty
, but it possesses dark soliton solutions, which appear as localized dips of intensity over a nonzero background
[
9
]
. We consider the defocusing NLS equation in the form:

iq_{t}+q_{xx}-2(|q|^{2}-q_{0}^{2})q=0,

(1.1)

with the constant nonzero boundary conditions

q(x,t)\rightarrow q_{\pm}\equiv q_{0}e^{i\theta_{\pm}}\qquad\text{as }x\rightarrow\pm\infty,

(1.2)

where
q_{0}>0
is the (symmetric) amplitude of the background and
\theta_{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}
are the asymptotic phases; the additional linear term proportional to
q
in (
1.1
) is introduced via a gauge transformation
q(x,t)\mapsto e^{-2iq_{0}^{2}t}q(x,t)
in order to allow for time-independent boundary conditions. The exact single dark soliton solution of (
1.1
) is given by:

q(x,t)=e^{i\sigma_{1}}\left\{k_{1}+i\Lambda_{1}\tanh\left[\Lambda_{1}(x+2k_{1}t-x_{1})\right]\right\},

(1.3)

where
-q_{0}<k_{1}<q_{0}
determines the soliton velocity, while
\Lambda_{1}
fixes its amplitude (i.e., the depth of the soliton below the background
q_{0}
) and
x_{1},\sigma_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
give the soliton center and phase, respectively. Note that the dark soliton amplitude and velocity are not independent, since they are related to each other and to the background amplitude
q_{0}
via:

k_{1}^{2}+\Lambda_{1}^{2}=q_{0}^{2}.

(1.4)

In addition, the phases
\theta_{\pm}
of the background are related to the soliton parameters via:

\theta_{\pm}=\sigma_{1}\pm\arctan(\Lambda_{1}/k_{1}).

(1.5)

One limitation of integrable models is that, in general, the systems considered in physical experiments are non-integrable. On the other hand, the theoretical predictions for the soliton solutions in integrable cases provide an extremely valuable tool for investigating non-integrable solitary waves in regimes that are not too far from the integrable ones.
As such, researchers rely on perturbation-based techniques of related integrable systems, when possible, to study how solitons and their evolution are affected by the inclusion in the mathematical description of terms that account for small dissipation, small linear/nonlinear loss, etc.

While the perturbation theory for solitons that decay rapidly at infinity has been extensively explored since the late 1970s —using various different methods such as multi-scale perturbation analysis, IST-based techniques, perturbations of conserved quantities, and direct numerical simulations

Some early works investigated the perturbation of black (i.e., stationary dark) solitons in lossy fibers numerically

This is because the perturbation produces a moving shelf on both sides of the soliton. The presence of this shelf —confirmed both numerically and analytically— was in fact used in

To date, the most comprehensive analysis of dark-soliton perturbations for the scalar defocusing NLS is that of Ablowitz

Using a multi-scale expansion together with perturbed conservation laws, the authors derived both the magnitude and phase of the shelf, as well as the adiabatic evolution of all soliton parameters, showing the emergence of a moving boundary layer that links the inner soliton core to the outer background. This approach was recently generalized to describe the effect of small perturbation to the dark-bright solitons of the coupled NLS equation, the so-called Manakov system

An alternative approach to soliton perturbation theory in the rapidly decaying case was introduced in

We will refer to this perturbative approach interchangeably as “integrable” perturbation theory, or IST-based perturbation theory, or, to be more specific, squared-eigenfunctions-based perturbation theory.
Since the early 1990s, numerous efforts have been made to extend IST-based perturbation theory to dark solitons. In 1994, Konotop and Vekslerchik derived orthogonality conditions from a set of squared eigenfunctions for the scalar defocusing NLS equation on a constant background, from which all soliton parameters can, in principle, be obtained

[

In
[

, Huang, Chi & Chen used a generalized Marchenko equation and extended the completeness proof of the previous work

to the multi-soliton case. The proof in
[

38

In a subsequent work by Ao & Yan

As a matter of fact, the difference between the two can be traced to the fact that the earlier work used one of the symmetries of the scattering problem to reduce the number of eigenfunctions involved in the closure relation to only the ones that are linearly independent.
It is also worth mentioning that in

In some references, even the number of orthogonality conditions was incorrect.
Moreover, none of the above references attempted to determine the radiation shelf that develops around the dark soliton, or presented comparisons of the theoretical predictions with numerical simulations. The existence of the shelf has long been confirmed by numerical simulations, and, as was shown in

The goal of this work is to revisit the perturbation theory of the scalar defocusing NLS on a nontrivial background based on the squared eigenfunctions, and develop it so that it can correctly predict the slow-time evolution of all the dark soliton parameters, as well as the radiation shelves emerging on the sides of the soliton. First, we prove completeness of the squared eigenfunctions for general potentials. A crucial difference with respect to the earlier works is the need to properly account for the singularities of the scattering data at the points
\pm q_{0}
, which are branch points for the continuous spectrum. Taking contributions from such singularities out of the integral term of the closure relation and leaving the remaining integral as a principal-value integral proves to be critical in this squared-eigenfunctions-based perturbation theory, because this leads to the correct discrete eigenmodes to be used in orthogonality conditions of the perturbation theory. Then we use the 1-soliton closure relation and suitable suppression of secular growth to determine the adiabatic evolution of the soliton parameters
k_{1}(T),\Lambda_{1}(T)
, as well as a condition relating the evolution of
\sigma_{1}(T)
and
x_{1}(T)
under perturbations of order
\epsilon
as functions of a slow time variable
T=\epsilon t
. The latter condition is missing in all the previous works that used squared-eigenfunctions-based perturbation theories.
More importantly, the first-order correction integral to the dark soliton computed via squared eigenfunctions is shown to contain a pole due to singularities of the scattering data at the branch points. Analysis of this first-order correction integral leads to predictions for the height and velocity of the shelves. Moreover, this same analysis provides the spatial phase gradient on each side of the shelf, as well as a formula for the slow time evolution of the core soliton’s phase, which in turn allows one to determine the dependence of the soliton center on the slow time.
All the results are corroborated by direct numerical simulations, and compared with the results of the direct perturbation theory in
[
30
]
, and with the earlier works using integrable perturbation theory. In particular, the estimates we obtain for all soliton parameters agree with the ones in
[
30
]
to order
\epsilon
, the only difference being for the soliton center, which, unlike the other parameters, obtained from perturbed conserved quantities up to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, is determined in
[
30
]
in terms of differential equations obtained from the Hamiltonian at
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
. We will also explain the shortcomings of the predictions for the adiabatic evolution of the soliton parameters in the earlier works within the framework of the squared-eigenfunctions-based perturbation theory.

The plan of the paper is the following. In Sec 2 we give an overview of the IST in order to set the notations and present the properties of eigenfunctions and scattering data that are necessary for the following sections.
In Sec 3 we derive the closure relation of squared eigenfunctions for general potentials by calculating the variations in the IST generalized to the case of a nonzero background. In Sec 4 we discuss the linearization operator and obtain the 1-soliton closure relation. The multiple scale perturbation theory is developed in Sec 5. Sec 6 investigates the first order correction, the shelf and evolution of the phase. The comparison with direct numerical simulations and earlier results is provided in Sec 7, with explicit applications to linear and nonlinear damping, dissipation and self-steepening. Finally, Sec 8 is devoted to some concluding remarks and open problems, and more technical calculations are collected in the appendices.

2
Overview of the IST

In this section we give a succinct overview of the IST for the defocusing NLS with nonzero boundary conditions, to set the notations and review the properties of eigenfunctions and scattering data which are required for the following sections. Additional details can be found, for instance, in
[
42
,
43
]
.

We start by considering the (unperturbed) defocusing NLS in the form
(
1.1
) with the constant symmetric nonzero boundary conditions

q(x,t)\rightarrow q_{\pm}\equiv q_{0}e^{\pm i\theta}\qquad\text{as }x\rightarrow\pm\infty,\qquad q_{0}>0,\ \theta\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.1)

The asymptotic phases can be chosen as
\pm\theta
without loss of generality on account of the invariance of the PDE upon multiplication by an arbitrary phase. [Note that arbitrary asymptotic phases
\theta_{\pm}
as
x\to\pm\infty
can be simply accounted for by replacing
\theta=(\theta_{+}-\theta_{-})/2
throughout.] The Lax pair of the defocusing NLS is given by

\displaystyle v_{x}=Uv,\qquad

\displaystyle U=-ik\sigma_{3}+Q,\qquad\sigma_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad Q=\begin{bmatrix}0&q\\
q^{*}&0\end{bmatrix},

(2.2a)

\displaystyle v_{t}=Vv,\qquad

\displaystyle V=i\sigma_{3}(-2k^{2}+Q_{x}-Q^{2}+q_{0}^{2})+2kQ.

(2.2b)

The asymptotic eigenvalues
\lambda
of (
2.2a
) as
x\to\pm\infty
are related to the spectral parameter
k
by
\lambda^{2}=k^{2}-q_{0}^{2}
. Thus,
\lambda=\lambda(k)
is a multivalued function with branch points at
k=\pm q_{0}
, and a two-sheeted Riemann surface cut along
(-\infty,-q_{0})\cup(q_{0},+\infty)
can be defined such that
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\lambda\geq 0
on one sheet, and
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\lambda\leq 0
on the other. A uniformization variable
z
can be introduced in the following way

z=k+\lambda\,,\qquad k=\frac{1}{2}(z+q_{0}^{2}z^{-1})\,,\qquad\lambda=\frac{1}{2}(z-q_{0}^{2}z^{-1}),

(2.3)

such that both sheets of the Riemann surface on which
k
is defined are mapped into a single complex
z
-plane. The matrix Jost solutions that satisfy both parts of the Lax pair (
2.2
) are defined by the boundary conditions

\displaystyle\Phi(x,t,z)=\begin{bmatrix}\phi(x,t,z)&\bar{\phi}(x,t,z)\end{bmatrix}\sim E_{-}(z)e^{-i\Omega(x,t,z)\sigma_{3}}\qquad\text{as }x\rightarrow-\infty,

(2.4a)

\displaystyle\Psi(x,t,z)=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\psi}(x,t,z)&\psi(x,t,z)\end{bmatrix}\sim E_{+}(z)e^{-i\Omega(x,t,z)\sigma_{3}}\qquad\text{as }x\rightarrow+\infty,

(2.4b)

where

\displaystyle E_{\pm}(z)=I-iz^{-1}\sigma_{3}Q_{\pm}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}1&-iq_{\pm}/z\\
iq^{*}_{\pm}/z&1\end{bmatrix},\quad Q_{\pm}=\begin{bmatrix}0&q_{\pm}\\
q^{*}_{\pm}&0\end{bmatrix},

(2.5a)

\displaystyle\Omega(x,t,z)=\lambda(z)(x+2k(z)t).

(2.5b)

Here and in the following,
I
denotes the
2\times 2
identity matrix. It can be shown that if
q\to q_{\pm}
sufficiently rapidly as
x\to\pm\infty
, the eigenfunctions
\phi
and
\psi
are analytic for
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits z>0
and continuous for
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits z\geq 0
, while
\bar{\phi}
and
\bar{\psi}
are analytic for
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits z<0
and continuous for
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits z\leq 0
, in both cases including at the images of the branch points
z=\pm q_{0}
. Furthermore,
\Phi
and
\Psi
have a singularity at
z=0
(cf. (
2.5a
)), and

\det\Phi(x,t,z)=\det\Psi(x,t,z)=\gamma(z)\,,\qquad\gamma(z)=1-q_{0}^{2}z^{-2},

(2.6)

which is nonzero except at
z=\pm q_{0}
.
For
z\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{\pm q_{0}\}
, the Jost solutions can be related via:

\Phi(x,t,z)=\Psi(x,t,z)S(z)\,,\qquad S(z)=\begin{bmatrix}a(z)&\bar{b}(z)\\
b(z)&\bar{a}(z)\end{bmatrix}\,,\qquad\det S(z)=1.

(2.7)

The scattering coefficients
a(z)
and
\bar{a}(z)
can be analytically continued into the upper and lower half planes, respectively, while
b(z)
and
\bar{b}(z)
are only defined on the real axis. Importantly, since the eigenfunctions are defined as simultaneous solutions of the Lax pair, the scattering coefficients are time-independent. Specifically, they can be written as:

\displaystyle a(z)=\frac{W(\phi(x,t,z),\psi(x,t,z))}{\gamma(z)},\qquad\bar{a}(z)=\frac{W(\bar{\psi}(x,t,z),\bar{\phi}(x,t,z))}{\gamma(z)},

(2.8a)

\displaystyle b(z)=\frac{W(\bar{\psi}(x,t,z),\phi(x,t,z))}{\gamma(z)},\qquad\bar{b}(z)=\frac{W(\bar{\phi}(x,t,z),\psi(x,t,z))}{\gamma(z)},

(2.8b)

where
W
denotes the Wronskian determinant, showing that generically all scattering coefficients are singular as
z\to\pm q_{0}
, unless the Jost functions become linearly dependent at either
\pm q_{0}
, in which case
\pm q_{0}
is called a “virtual level”, and the scattering coefficients are
\mathcal{O}(1)
as
z
approaches
\pm q_{0}
. Importantly, for any reflectionless/pure soliton potential, both
\pm q_{0}
are virtual levels, and all scattering coefficients are finite as
z\to\pm q_{0}
.

The eigenfunctions satisfy the following symmetries corresponding to the involution
z\mapsto z^{*}
:

\Phi(x,t,z)=\sigma_{1}\Phi^{*}(x,t,z^{*})\sigma_{1},\qquad\Psi(x,t,z)=\sigma_{1}\Psi^{*}(x,t,z^{*})\sigma_{1},

(2.9)

which in turn imply the following symmetries for the scattering coefficients:

a(z)=\bar{a}^{*}(z^{*})\quad\text{for }\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits z\geq 0,\qquad b(z)=\bar{b}^{*}(z)\quad\text{for }z\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.10)

Due to a second involution in the Lax pair, namely
z\mapsto q_{0}^{2}/z
, we also have the symmetries:

\Phi(x,t,z)=-\frac{1}{iz}\Phi(x,t,q_{0}^{2}/z)\sigma_{3}Q_{-}\,,\qquad\Psi(x,t,z)=\frac{1}{iz}\Psi(x,t,q_{0}^{2}/z)\sigma_{3}Q_{+}\,,

(2.11)

and the scattering coefficients satisfy:

a(q_{0}^{2}/z)=e^{2i\theta}\bar{a}(z)\quad\text{for }\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits z\geq 0,\qquad b(q_{0}^{2}/z)=-\bar{b}(z)\quad\text{for }z\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.12)

Eq. (
2.7
) can be written as:

\displaystyle\frac{\phi(x,t,z)}{a(z)}=\bar{\phi}(x,t,z)+\rho(z)\psi(x,t,z),\qquad\frac{\bar{\phi}(x,t,z)}{\bar{a}(z)}={\psi}(x,t,z)+\bar{\rho}(z)\bar{\psi}(x,t,z),

(2.13)

where the reflection coefficients are give by:

\rho(z)=\frac{b(z)}{a(z)},\qquad\bar{\rho}(z)=\frac{\bar{b}(z)}{\bar{a}(z)}.

(2.14)

Generically, it is known that
a(z)
has a finite number of zeros that lie on the circle
|z|=q_{0}
(minus the branch points
\pm q_{0}
), and due to the symmetry (
2.10
), if
a(\zeta_{j})=0
then
\bar{a}(\zeta_{j}^{*})=0
. So, the discrete eigenvalues come in pairs

\{\zeta_{j},\zeta_{j}^{*}\}_{j=1}^{J},\qquad\zeta_{j}=q_{0}e^{i\alpha_{j}},\qquad 0<\alpha_{j}<\pi.

(2.15)

At each discrete eigenvalue, (
2.8
) implies that the Jost eigenfunctions become proportional:

\phi(x,t,\zeta_{j})=b_{j}\psi(x,t,\zeta_{j}),\qquad\bar{\phi}(x,t,\zeta_{j}^{*})={b}^{*}_{j}\bar{\psi}(x,t,\zeta_{j}^{*}),

(2.16)

for some constant
b_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
satisfying the symmetry
b_{j}^{*}=-b_{j}
. With this in mind, the residue contributions from each pair of discrete eigenvalues are

\mathop{\rm Res}\limits_{z=\zeta_{j}}\frac{\phi(x,t,z)}{a(z)}=C_{j}\psi(x,t,\zeta_{j}),\qquad\mathop{\rm Res}\limits_{z=\zeta_{j}^{*}}\frac{\bar{\phi}(x,t,z)}{\bar{a}(z)}=C_{j}^{*}\bar{\psi}(x,t,\zeta^{*}_{j}),

(2.17)

where
C_{j}=b_{j}/a^{\prime}(\zeta_{j})
is the norming constant associated with the discrete eigenvalue (hereafter,
′
denotes differentiation with respect to the scattering parameter
z
). Moreover, the first symmetry in (
2.12
) gives:

\bar{a}^{\prime}(z)=-e^{-2i\theta}\frac{q_{0}^{2}}{z^{2}}a^{\prime}(q_{0}^{2}/z),

and therefore the norming constants are such that

C_{j}^{*}=e^{2i(\theta-\alpha_{j})}C_{j}.

(2.18)

Note that, like the scattering coefficients and the reflection coefficients, the norming constants are time-independent because the Jost eigenfunctions have been defined as simultaneous solutions of the Lax pair.

The inverse scattering problem is then solved by formulating (
2.13
) as a RHP across the real
z
axis. After applying Cauchy projectors to (
2.13
) and accounting for the residues, the RHP for the Jost eigenfunctions
\psi(x,t,z)
and
\bar{\psi}(x,t,z)
is converted into the following linear system of algebraic-integral equations:

\displaystyle\psi(x,t,z)e^{-i\Omega(x,t,z)}=\begin{bmatrix}-iq_{+}/z\\
1\end{bmatrix}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{e^{-i\Omega(x,t,\zeta_{j}^{*})}\bar{\psi}(x,t,\zeta_{j}^{*})C_{j}^{*}}{z-\zeta_{j}^{*}}-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-i\Omega(x,t,\zeta)}\bar{\psi}(x,t,\zeta)\rho^{*}(\zeta)}{\zeta-(z+i0)}d\zeta,

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}(x,t,z)e^{i\Omega(x,t,z)}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
iq_{+}^{*}/z\end{bmatrix}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{e^{i\Omega(x,t,\zeta_{j})}\psi(x,t,\zeta_{j})C_{j}}{z-\zeta_{j}}+\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{i\Omega(x,t,\zeta)}\psi(x,t,\zeta)\rho(\zeta)}{\zeta-(z-i0)}d\zeta.

(2.19)

Finally, once the eigenfunctions are known
the potential (i.e., the solution
q(x,t)
to (
1.1
)) can be recovered
from the large-
z
asymptotic behavior of the eigenfunctions.

3
Completeness of squared eigenfunctions for general potentials

The main purpose of this section is to prove completeness of the squared eigenfunctions for general potentials.
We first show how to calculate variations of the scatting data in terms of a variation of the potential, and vice versa, which in turn will yield the adjoint squared eigenfunctions and the squared eigenfunctions. Then, we use the variations to prove the completeness result. The methodology follows
[
29
]
, while also accounting for the nonzero boundary conditions. Throughout this section, we suppress explicit dependence on
t
.

3.1
Variation in the scattering data from a variation in the potential

Since the eigenfunction
\Phi
defined in (
2.4a
) satisfies (
2.2a
),
a variation
\delta\Phi
in the eigenfunction corresponding to a variation
\delta Q
in the potential must satisfy

\delta\Phi_{x}=-ik\sigma_{3}\delta\Phi+Q\delta\Phi+(\delta Q)\Phi\,,\qquad\lim_{x\rightarrow-\infty}\delta\Phi=0.

(3.1)

One can check that the solution of (
3.1
) is given by

\delta\Phi(x,z)=\Phi(x,z)\int_{-\infty}^{x}\Phi^{-1}(y,z)\delta Q(y)\Phi(y,z)dy.

(3.2)

Taking the limit as
x\rightarrow+\infty
, we have
\Phi=\Psi S\rightarrow E_{+}e^{-i\Omega\sigma_{3}}S
, so that (
3.2
) becomes

\delta S(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi^{-1}(y,z)\delta Q(y)\Phi(y,z)dy.

(3.3)

If we write the eigenfunctions as

\Phi=\begin{bmatrix}\phi&\bar{\phi}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{1}&\bar{\phi}_{1}\\
\phi_{2}&\bar{\phi}_{2}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\Psi=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\psi}&\psi\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\psi}_{1}&\psi_{1}\\
\bar{\psi}_{2}&\psi_{2}\end{bmatrix},

(3.4)

then their inverses are

\Phi^{-1}=\frac{1}{\gamma(z)}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\bar{\phi}}\\
\hat{\phi}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{\gamma(z)}\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\phi}_{2}&-\bar{\phi}_{1}\\
-\phi_{2}&\phi_{1}\end{bmatrix}\,,\qquad\Psi^{-1}=\frac{1}{\gamma(z)}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\psi}\\
\hat{\bar{\psi}}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{\gamma(z)}\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{2}&-\psi_{1}\\
-\bar{\psi}_{2}&\bar{\psi}_{1}\end{bmatrix},

(3.5)

where we recall that
\gamma(z)=\det\Phi=\det\Psi=1-q_{0}^{2}/z^{2}
(cf (
2.6
)).

With these definitions, (
3.3
) gives entrywise:

\displaystyle\delta a(z)=\frac{1}{\gamma(z)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\psi}(y,z)\delta Q(y)\phi(y,z)dy,\quad\delta b(z)=\frac{1}{\gamma(z)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\bar{\psi}}(y,z)\delta Q(y)\phi(y,z)dy,

(3.6a)

\displaystyle\delta\bar{a}(z)=\frac{1}{\gamma(z)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\bar{\psi}}(y,z)\delta Q(y)\bar{\phi}(y,z)dy,\quad\delta\bar{b}(z)=\frac{1}{\gamma(z)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\psi}(y,z)\delta Q(y)\bar{\phi}(y,z)dy.

(3.6b)

The variations in the reflection coefficients defined by
\rho=b/a
and
\bar{\rho}=\bar{b}/\bar{a}
(cf. (
2.14
)) are found to be

\displaystyle\delta\rho(z)=\frac{1}{\gamma(z)a(z)^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\phi}(y,z)\delta Q(y)\phi(y,z)dy,\quad\delta\bar{\rho}(z)=\frac{1}{\gamma(z)\bar{a}(z)^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\bar{\phi}}(y,z)\delta Q(y)\bar{\phi}(y,z)dy,

(3.7)

where (
3.6
) as well as
\hat{\phi}=a\hat{\bar{\psi}}-b\hat{\psi}
and
\hat{\bar{\phi}}=\bar{a}\hat{\psi}-\bar{b}\hat{\bar{\psi}}
have been used. If we define the inner product
1
1

1

It is customary to define the inner product this way, without explicit complex conjugation, with the implication that the left argument is a member of an appropriate dual space. The adjoint
M^{A}
of an operator
M
is defined such that
\langle M^{A}f,g\rangle=\langle f,Mg\rangle
.

\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(y)^{T}g(y)dy,

(3.8)

then (
3.7
) can be written as

\displaystyle\delta\rho(z)=\frac{1}{\gamma(z)a(z)^{2}}\left\langle\chi,\begin{bmatrix}\delta q\\
\delta q^{*}\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle,\qquad\delta\bar{\rho}(z)=\frac{1}{\gamma(z)\bar{a}(z)^{2}}\left\langle\bar{\chi},\begin{bmatrix}\delta q\\
\delta q^{*}\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle,

(3.9)

where the so-called
adjoint squared eigenfunctions
are defined as:

\chi(x,z)=\begin{bmatrix}-\phi_{2}(x,z)^{2}\\
\phi_{1}(x,z)^{2}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\bar{\chi}(x,z)=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\phi}_{2}(x,z)^{2}\\
-\bar{\phi}_{1}(x,z)^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(3.10)

Note that on account of the symmetries (
2.9
)-(
2.10
), we have that the two equations in (
3.9
) are equivalent.

Remark 1
.

Since
\gamma(\pm q_{0})=0
, two possible scenarios can occur: (i) If the unperturbed reflection coefficient is nonzero, then
a(z)
has simple poles at
z=\pm q_{0}
, so we have
\delta\rho(\pm q_{0})=0
and the property
|\rho(\pm q_{0})|=1
is preserved. For the present discussion, we assume this to be the case. (ii) If the unperturbed reflection coefficient is zero, then
a(\pm q_{0})
is finite, meaning that
\delta\rho
becomes singular as
z\rightarrow\pm q_{0}
. Notably, this happens in the case of pure solitons, to be discussed later. The implication is that for infinitesimally small variations in the potential
\delta q
, the resulting variation in the reflection coefficient
\delta\rho
is finite. This causes the generation of a radiation shelf, a phenomenon that is also observed in the perturbation theory associated with the KdV equation, due to a similar singularity that occurs as the spectral parameter approaches zero (see for example
[
11
,
13
]
).

3.2
Variation in the potential from a variation in the scattering data

In matrix form, the relations between the Jost functions given in (
2.13
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}=\mu G,\qquad G=\begin{bmatrix}1&e^{-2i\Omega(x,z)}\bar{\rho}(z)\\
-e^{2i\Omega(x,z)}\rho(z)&1-\rho(z)\bar{\rho}(z)\end{bmatrix},\qquad z\in\mathbb{R},

(3.11a)

\displaystyle\mu=\begin{bmatrix}e^{i\Omega(x,z)}\displaystyle{\frac{\phi(x,z)}{a(z)}}\ &e^{-i\Omega(x,z)}\psi(x,z)\end{bmatrix},\quad\bar{\mu}=\begin{bmatrix}e^{i\Omega(x,z)}\bar{\psi}(x,z)\ &e^{-i\Omega(x,z)}\displaystyle{\frac{\bar{\phi}(x,z)}{\bar{a}(z)}}\end{bmatrix},

(3.11b)

which is a RHP across the real
z
-axis. To start with, we assume that
a(z)
and
\bar{a}(z)
have no zeros, i.e.,
\mu
and
\bar{\mu}
are analytic functions of
z
in the upper and lower half planes, respectively. A variation of (
3.11
) reads:

\delta\bar{\mu}=(\delta\mu)\,G+\mu\,\delta G\,,\qquad\delta G=\begin{bmatrix}0&e^{-2i\Omega(x,z)}\delta\bar{\rho}(z)\\
-e^{2i\Omega(x,z)}\delta\rho(z)\ \ &-\rho(z)\delta\bar{\rho}(z)-\bar{\rho}(z)\delta\rho(z)\end{bmatrix}.

(3.12)

If we write
G=\mu^{-1}\bar{\mu}
, and introduce the definitions

\nu=(\delta\mu)\mu^{-1},\qquad\bar{\nu}=(\delta\bar{\mu})\bar{\mu}^{-1},\qquad F=-\mu(\delta G)\bar{\mu}^{-1},

(3.13)

then we have the RHP:

\nu-\bar{\nu}=F.

(3.14)

After simplification,
F
is given by

F=\Psi\begin{bmatrix}0&-\delta\bar{\rho}(z)\\
\delta\rho(z)&0\end{bmatrix}\Psi^{-1}.

(3.15)

Using the large-
z
asymptotic behavior of the Jost eigenfunctions (which can be found, for example, in
[
43
]
) in their respective half-planes we have that

\nu,\bar{\nu}\sim\begin{bmatrix}\mathcal{O}(1/z)&-i\delta q/z\\
i\delta q^{*}/z&\mathcal{O}(1/z)\end{bmatrix},\qquad|z|\rightarrow\infty.

(3.16)

Since
\nu,\bar{\nu}\rightarrow 0
as
|z|\rightarrow\infty
, the solution on (
3.14
) can be written down in terms of Cauchy projectors as

\displaystyle\nu(x,z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{F(x,\zeta)}{\zeta-(z+i0)}d\zeta,\qquad\bar{\nu}(x,z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{F(x,\zeta)}{\zeta-(z-i0)}d\zeta.

(3.17)

Comparing the large-
z
asymptotic behavior of (
3.17
) with (
3.16
)
it can be deduced that

\begin{bmatrix}\delta q(x)\\
\delta q^{*}(x)\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma(\zeta)}\Big\{\eta(x,\zeta)\delta\rho(\zeta)+\bar{\eta}(x,\zeta)\delta\bar{\rho}(\zeta)\Big\}d\zeta,

(3.18)

where the
squared eigenfunctions
are defined as:

\eta(x,z)=\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{1}(x,z)^{2}\\
\psi_{2}(x,z)^{2}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\bar{\eta}(x,z)=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\psi}_{1}(x,z)^{2}\\
\bar{\psi}_{2}(x,z)^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

(3.19)

Now, due to the symmetries (
2.11
) and (
2.12
), the squared eigenfunctions and reflection coefficients satisfy

\bar{\eta}(x,z)=-\frac{q_{0}^{2}}{z^{2}}e^{-2i\theta}\eta(x,q_{0}^{2}/z),\qquad\delta\bar{\rho}(z)=-e^{2i\theta}\delta\rho(q_{0}^{2}/z),

(3.20)

which allows one to combine the two terms in (
3.18
) into one by
making a change of variables
\zeta^{\prime}=q_{0}^{2}/\zeta
in the second integral:

\begin{bmatrix}\delta q(x)\\
\delta q^{*}(x)\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma(\zeta)}\eta(x,\zeta)\delta\rho(\zeta)\left(1-\frac{q_{0}^{2}}{\zeta^{2}}\right)d\zeta=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\eta(x,\zeta)\delta\rho(\zeta)d\zeta.

(3.21)

This reduction is in contrast to the case of zero boundary conditions, where both squared eigenfunctions need to be retained since there is no symmetry analogous to (
3.20
).

3.3
Completeness relation for general potentials

As shown in the last section, a variation in the potential induced by a variation in the reflection coefficient can be expressed in terms of the squared eigenfunction
\eta(x,z)
defined in (
3.19
) via Eq. (
3.21
). Conversely, a variation in the reflection coefficient induced by a variation in the potential is expressed in terms of the adjoint squared eigenfunction
\chi(x,z)
in (
3.10
) via (
3.9
).
Inserting (
3.21
) into (
3.9
), we have

\delta\rho(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi\gamma(z)a(z)^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big\langle\chi(z),\eta(z)\big\rangle\delta\rho(\zeta)d\zeta,

(3.22)

from which we obtain the inner product between the squared eigenfunction
\eta
and its adjoint
\chi
:

\big\langle\chi(z),\eta(\zeta)\big\rangle=2\pi\gamma(z)a(z)^{2}\delta(z-\zeta).

(3.23)

On the other hand, substituting (
3.9
) into (
3.21
) and using the definition of the inner product (
3.8
) gives

\begin{bmatrix}\delta q(x)\\
\delta q^{*}(x)\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma(\zeta)a(\zeta)^{2}}\eta(x,\zeta)\chi(y,\zeta)^{T}d\zeta\begin{bmatrix}\delta q(y)\\
\delta q^{*}(y)\end{bmatrix}dy,

(3.24)

which leads to the closure/completeness relation:

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma(\zeta)a(\zeta)^{2}}\eta(x,\zeta)\chi(y,\zeta)^{T}d\zeta=2\pi\delta(x-y)I.

(3.25)

Recall that both (
3.23
) and (
3.25
) have been derived under the assumption that
a(z)
has no zeros and that the unperturbed reflection coefficient
\rho(z)
is nonzero. If this is the case, the integrand has no poles, the set
\{\eta(x,z):z\in\mathbb{R}\}
is complete, and (
3.25
) is the closure relation. Generically,
a(z)
has a finite number of simple zeros
\{z=\zeta_{j},\;\text{Im}\,\zeta_{j}>0\}_{j=1}^{J}
that lie on the circle
|z|=q_{0}
. Furthermore, in any reflectionless case, poles at
z=\pm q_{0}
coming from the fact that
\gamma(\pm q_{0})=0
also must be accounted for. Therefore, a more general closure relation is required. The general closure relation can be obtained by simply replacing the integral in (
3.25
) with one taken over a contour in the upper-half
z
-plane from
-\infty+i0
to
+\infty+i0
that passes above the circle
|z|=q_{0}
. Furthermore, when discrete eigenvalues are present (i.e., when
a(z)
has zeros) the orthogonality of the continuous eigenfunctions as given in (
3.23
) still holds for
z,\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
, and will be supplemented with additional orthogonality relations between the discrete squared eigenfunctions that arise due to the residues at the complex zeros of
a(z)
.

The most compact form of the general closure relation is:

\int_{\Gamma}\frac{1}{\gamma(\zeta)a(\zeta)^{2}}\eta(x,\zeta)\chi(y,\zeta)^{T}d\zeta=2\pi\delta(x-y)I,

(3.26)

where
\Gamma
is a contour from
-\infty+i0
to
+\infty+i0
that passes above the circle of radius
q_{0}
in the upper half plane. A justification of the generalization of (
3.25
) to the contour integral (
3.26
) is provided in Appendix A.
Explicitly accounting for the residue contributions from the poles at the discrete eigenvalues
\zeta_{j}
(given in Appendix A), the closure relation can be written as

2\pi\delta(x-y)I=\int_{C}\frac{1}{\gamma(\zeta)a(\zeta)^{2}}\eta(x,\zeta)\chi(y,\zeta)^{T}d\zeta-2\pi i\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{\gamma(\zeta_{j}){a}^{\prime}(\zeta_{j})^{2}}\Big[\eta(x,\zeta_{j})\Theta(y,\zeta_{j})^{T}+\eta^{\prime}(x,\zeta_{j})\chi(y,\zeta_{j})^{T}\Big],

(3.27)

where
C
is a contour along the real axis indented in the upper-half plane to avoid
\pm q_{0}
, and we have defined

\Theta(x,\zeta_{j})=\chi^{\prime}(x,\zeta_{j})+\frac{\gamma(\zeta_{j}){a}^{\prime\prime}(\zeta_{j})-\gamma^{\prime}(\zeta_{j}){a}^{\prime}(\zeta_{j})}{\gamma(\zeta_{j}){a}^{\prime}(\zeta_{j})}\chi(x,\zeta_{j}).

(3.28)

As before, prime denotes differentiation with respect to
z
. Note that in the case of zero boundary conditions, the closure relation contains two continuous and four discrete squared eigenfunctions. In the present case, the number of independent eigenfunctions is halved by accounting for the symmetry
z\mapsto q_{0}^{2}/z
.

Finally, we can account for the possibility of simple poles on the real axis at
z=\pm q_{0}
(which are due to
\gamma(\pm q_{0})=0
, and are present whenever
a(\pm q_{0})
is finite, which occurs for reflectionless potentials) by replacing the integral over
C
with a principal value integral over the real axis, while accounting for the residues

\mathop{\rm Res}\limits_{z=\pm q_{0}}\frac{\eta(x,z)\chi(y,z)^{T}}{\gamma(z)a(z)^{2}}=\frac{1}{\gamma^{\prime}(\pm q_{0})a(\pm q_{0})^{2}}\eta(x,\pm q_{0})\chi(y,\pm q_{0})^{T},

(3.29)

with a factor of
\pi i
. Again, we stress that (
3.29
) is only nonzero in the reflectionless case, for which
a(\pm q_{0})
is finite. With this, the
full closure relation
is:

\displaystyle 2\pi\delta(x-y)I=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma(\zeta)a(\zeta)^{2}}\eta(x,\zeta)\chi(y,\zeta)^{T}d\zeta-\pi i\sum_{\pm}\frac{1}{\gamma^{\prime}(\pm q_{0})a(\pm q_{0})^{2}}\eta(x,\pm q_{0})\chi(y,\pm q_{0})^{T}

\displaystyle-\,2\pi i\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{1}{\gamma(\zeta_{j}){a}^{\prime}(\zeta_{j})^{2}}\Big[\eta(x,\zeta_{j})\Theta(y,\zeta_{j})^{T}+\eta^{\prime}(x,\zeta_{j})\chi(y,\zeta_{j})^{T}\Big].

(3.30)

Here and throughout the paper,
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int
denotes the Cauchy principal value integral. It is worth noting at this point that while several previous works (including
[
33
,
34
]
) derived essentially equivalent completeness relations, the half residues due to the poles at
\pm q_{0}
were not explicitly accounted for. The inclusion of these contributions plays an important role in the perturbation theory, on which we will elaborate in Sec 5.

4
Linearization operator and completeness relation for a single soliton

In this section, we discuss the linearization operator of the NLS equation with nonzero boundary conditions and its relation to the squared eigenfunctions, and we derive the explicit expression of the completeness relation for a 1-soliton solution.

4.1
The linearization operator

Upon directly taking a variation of the NLS equation (
1.1
), we get

i\delta q_{t}(x,t)+\delta q_{xx}(x,t)-2(2|q(x,t)|^{2}-q_{0}^{2})\delta q(x,t)-2q(x,t)^{2}\delta q^{*}(x,t)=0.

(4.1)

Combining (
4.1
) with its complex conjugate, a variation in the potential should satisfy

\mathcal{L}\begin{bmatrix}\delta q(x,t)\\
\delta q^{*}(x,t)\end{bmatrix}=0,

(4.2)

where the linearization operator is

\mathcal{L}=\begin{bmatrix}i\partial_{t}+\partial_{xx}-2(2|q(x,t)|^{2}-q_{0}^{2})&-2q(x,t)^{2}\\
2q^{*}(x,t)^{2}&i\partial_{t}-\partial_{xx}+2(2|q(x,t)|^{2}-q_{0}^{2})\end{bmatrix}.

(4.3)

Inserting (
3.21
) into (
4.2
) shows that the squared eigenfunction
\eta
is an eigenfunction of the linearization operator with zero eigenvalue, namely

\mathcal{L}\eta(x,t,z)=0.

(4.4)

Moreover, the formal adjoint of
\mathcal{L}
is

\mathcal{L}^{A}=\begin{bmatrix}-i\partial_{t}+\partial_{xx}-2(2|q(x,t)|^{2}-q_{0}^{2})&2q^{*}(x,t)^{2}\\
-2q(x,t)^{2}&-i\partial_{t}-\partial_{xx}+2(2|q(x,t)|^{2}-q_{0}^{2})\end{bmatrix},

(4.5)

and from

\langle\chi(z),\mathcal{L}\eta(\zeta)\rangle=\langle\mathcal{L}^{A}\chi(z),\eta(\zeta)\rangle=0,

(4.6)

we deduce that the adjoint squared eigenfunction
\chi
satisfies

\mathcal{L}^{A}\chi(x,t,z)=0.

(4.7)

Apart from an arbitrary phase, the exact single dark soliton solution of (
1.1
) is

q(x,t)=u(\xi)=k_{1}+i\Lambda_{1}\tanh\xi,\qquad\xi\equiv\Lambda_{1}(x+2k_{1}t-x_{1}),

(4.8)

where
\zeta_{1}=k_{1}+i\Lambda_{1}=q_{0}e^{i\theta}
. Thus, when performing a linearization around the 1-soliton solution, it proves convenient to express the linearization operator and its adjoint in terms of the traveling coordinate
\xi
rather that
x
(while allowing for potential explicit dependence on
t
). In this case, we have
\partial_{t}\rightarrow\partial_{t}+2\Lambda_{1}k_{1}\partial_{\xi}
,
\partial_{x}\rightarrow\Lambda_{1}\partial_{\xi}
and

\mathcal{L}=i\partial_{t}+L,\qquad\mathcal{L}^{A}=-i\partial_{t}+L^{A},

(4.9)

where

\displaystyle L=\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{1}^{2}\partial_{\xi}^{2}+2i\Lambda_{1}k_{1}\partial_{\xi}-2(2|u(\xi)|^{2}-q_{0}^{2})&-2u(\xi)^{2}\\
2u^{*}(\xi)^{2}&-\Lambda_{1}^{2}\partial_{\xi}^{2}+2i\Lambda_{1}k_{1}\partial_{\xi}+2(2|u(\xi)|^{2}-q_{0}^{2})\end{bmatrix},

(4.10a)

\displaystyle L^{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{1}^{2}\partial_{\xi}^{2}-2i\Lambda_{1}k_{1}\partial_{\xi}-2(2|u(\xi)|^{2}-q_{0}^{2})&2u(\xi)^{*2}\\
-2u(\xi)^{2}&-\Lambda_{1}^{2}\partial_{\xi}^{2}-2i\Lambda_{1}k_{1}\partial_{\xi}+2(2|u(\xi)|^{2}-q_{0}^{2})\end{bmatrix}.

(4.10b)

In the single soliton case, the squared eigenfunctions can be computed explicitly by solving the linear system (
2.19
)
for the Jost eigenfunctions
\psi(x,t,z)
and
\bar{\psi}(x,t,z)
with
J=1
,
\rho(z)\equiv 0
, and discrete eigenvalue and norming constant given by:

\zeta_{1}=k_{1}+i\Lambda_{1},\qquad C_{1}=-2\Lambda_{1}e^{2\Lambda_{1}x_{1}},

(4.11)

and
\phi(x,t,z)
can be recovered from the above via:

\phi(x,t,z)=a(z)\bar{\psi}(x,t,z),

(4.12)

(cf. (
2.13
) with
\rho(z)\equiv 0
) as well as the explicit expression for
a(z)
in the 1-soliton case, namely:

a(z)=\frac{z-\zeta_{1}}{z-\zeta_{1}^{*}}.

(4.13)

Eqs. (
3.19
) and (

) then give the following explicit expression for the squared eigenfunctions:

\displaystyle\eta(x,t,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}[iq_{+}z^{-1}(z-\zeta_{1}^{*})+\Lambda_{1}e^{-\xi}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits\xi]^{2}\\
[(z-\zeta_{1}^{*})-i\Lambda_{1}e^{-\xi}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits\xi]^{2}\end{bmatrix}\frac{e^{2i\Omega(x,t,z)}}{(z-\zeta_{1}^{*})^{2}},

(4.14a)

\displaystyle\chi(x,t,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}-[iq_{-}z^{-1}(z-\zeta_{1})-\Lambda_{1}e^{-\xi}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits\xi]^{2}\\

(4.14b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\xi,z)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\xi,z)

\displaystyle\Upsilon(\xi,z)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}-[iq_{-}z^{-1}(z-\zeta_{1})-\Lambda_{1}e^{-\xi}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits\xi]^{2}\\

(4.16b)

From (

(4.17)

Substituting (

\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle L\mathcal{Z}(\xi,z)=4\lambda(k-k_{1})\mathcal{Z}(\xi,z),\qquad L^{A}\Upsilon(\xi,z)=4\lambda(k-k_{1})\Upsilon(\xi,z).

(4.18)

The discrete eigenfunctions associated with the soliton eigenvalue
\zeta_{1}
can be obtained by directly evaluating (

, from which we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{1}(\xi):=\mathcal{Z}(\xi,\zeta_{1})=\Lambda_{1}^{2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\

1\end{bmatrix}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi.

(4.19)

Furthermore, setting

in (

L\mathcal{Z}_{1}(\xi)=L^{A}\Upsilon_{1}(\xi)=0.

(4.20)

Differentiating (

, the generalized discrete eigenfunctions are found to be

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}_{1}(\xi):=\left.\partial_{z}\mathcal{Z}(\xi,z)\right|_{z=\zeta_{1}}=-2i\Lambda_{1}\left\{\begin{bmatrix}q_{0}^{2}\zeta_{1}^{-2}\\
-1\end{bmatrix}e^{-\xi}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits\xi-k_{1}\zeta_{1}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-1\end{bmatrix}\xi\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi\right\},

(4.21a)

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{\prime}_{1}(\xi):=\left.\partial_{z}\Upsilon(\xi,z)\right|_{z=\zeta_{1}}=2i\Lambda_{1}\left\{\begin{bmatrix}q_{0}^{2}\zeta_{1}^{-2}\\
1\end{bmatrix}e^{\xi}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits\xi+k_{1}\zeta_{1}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix}\xi\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi\right\},

(4.21b)

and differentiating (

(4.22)

4.2
1-soliton completeness relation

Here we specialize the completeness relation to the 1-soliton case. Returning to the generic completeness relation (

) as well as (

) for

\int_{\Gamma}\frac{1}{\gamma(\zeta)|\zeta-\zeta_{1}|^{4}}\mathcal{Z}(\xi,\zeta)\Upsilon(\xi^{\prime},\zeta)^{T}d\zeta=2\pi\delta(\xi-\xi^{\prime})I.

After calculating the residue due to

where, as before,

\pm q_{0}

\Xi_{1}(\xi)=\Upsilon_{1}^{\prime}(\xi)+\frac{2ik_{1}}{\Lambda_{1}\zeta_{1}}\Upsilon_{1}(\xi).

(4.25)

One can verify directly that

\langle\Xi_{1}(\xi),\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}_{1}(\xi)\rangle=0

Now, we account for the residues at the branch points. Setting

A straightforward calculation shows that the squared eigenfunctions evaluated at
\pm q_{0}

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{0}^{\pm}(\xi)=2(k_{1}\mp q_{0})\begin{bmatrix}-u(\xi)\\
u^{*}(\xi)\end{bmatrix}-\mathcal{Z}_{1}(\xi),\qquad\Upsilon_{0}^{\pm}(\xi)=2(k_{1}\mp q_{0})\begin{bmatrix}u^{*}(\xi)\\

Pulling out the residue contribution to the integral in (

\pm q_{0}

\displaystyle 2\pi\delta(\xi-\xi^{\prime})I=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma(\zeta)|\zeta-\zeta_{1}|^{4}}\mathcal{Z}(\xi,\zeta)\Upsilon(\xi^{\prime},\zeta)^{T}d\zeta-{\pi i}\sum_{\pm}\frac{\pm 1}{{8q_{0}}(k_{1}\mp q_{0})^{2}}\mathcal{Z}_{0}^{\pm}(\xi)\Upsilon_{0}^{\pm}(\xi^{\prime})^{T}

(4.28)

Although it may appear that the contributions from

4.27
), we find that

\sum_{\pm}\frac{\pm 1}{{8q_{0}}(k_{1}\mp q_{0})^{2}}\mathcal{Z}_{0}^{\pm}(\xi)\Upsilon_{0}^{\pm}(\xi^{\prime})^{T}=\frac{1}{2\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\left\{\begin{bmatrix}-u(\xi)\\

The last term in (

\frac{\pi\zeta_{1}}{4\Lambda_{1}^{3}}\mathcal{Z}_{1}\Xi_{1}^{T}-\frac{\pi k_{1}}{2\Lambda_{1}^{4}}\mathcal{Z}_{1}\Upsilon_{1}^{T}=\frac{\pi\zeta_{1}}{4\Lambda_{1}^{3}}\mathcal{Z}_{1}\Upsilon_{1}^{\prime\,T},

\displaystyle 2\pi\delta(\xi-\xi^{\prime})I=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma(\zeta)|\zeta-\zeta_{1}|^{4}}\mathcal{Z}(\xi,\zeta)\Upsilon(\xi^{\prime},\zeta)^{T}d\zeta

\displaystyle-\frac{\pi i}{2\Lambda_{1}^{2}}\left\{\begin{bmatrix}-u(\xi)\\

\mathcal{Z}_{1}\Upsilon_{1}^{\prime\,T}

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}(\xi)=\mathcal{Z}_{1}^{\prime}(\xi)-\frac{2i\Lambda}{\zeta_{1}}\begin{bmatrix}-u(\xi)\\

(4.32)

then the final expression for the

is:

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma(\zeta)|\zeta-\zeta_{1}|^{4}}\mathcal{Z}(\xi,\zeta)\Upsilon(\xi^{\prime},\zeta)^{T}d\zeta+\frac{\pi\zeta_{1}}{4\Lambda_{1}^{3}}\Big[\mathcal{Z}_{1}(\xi)\tilde{\Upsilon}_{1}(\xi^{\prime})^{T}+\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}(\xi)\Upsilon_{1}(\xi^{\prime})^{T}\Big]=2\pi\delta(\xi-\xi^{\prime})I.

(4.33)

Note that the discrete eigenfunctions satisfy the following orthogonality conditions (which can either be read off from the closure relation or verified directly):

\langle\Upsilon_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{1}\rangle=\langle\tilde{\Upsilon}_{1},\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\rangle=0,\qquad\langle\tilde{\Upsilon}_{1},\mathcal{Z}_{1}\rangle=\langle\Upsilon_{1},\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}\rangle=8\Lambda_{1}^{3}\zeta_{1}^{-1}.

(4.34)

5
Perturbation theory

Consider the defocusing NLS equation with an additional small forcing pertubation:

iq_{t}(x,t)+q_{xx}(x,t)-2(|q(x,t)|^{2}-q_{0}^{2})q(x,t)=\epsilon F[q(x,t)],\qquad 0<\epsilon\ll 1,

(5.1)

and introduce the slow time scale
T=\epsilon t
so that
\partial_{t}\rightarrow\partial_{t}+\epsilon\partial_{T}
.

Remark 2
.

An exact dark soliton solution of (
5.1
) with
\epsilon=0
has the asymptotic behavior
q(x,t)\rightarrow q_{0}e^{i\theta_{\pm}}
as
x\rightarrow\pm\infty
with generic phases
\theta_{\pm}
. Following the technique of
[
30
,
31
]
, substituting this into (
5.1
) in the limit
x\rightarrow\pm\infty
while allowing
q_{0}
and
\theta_{\pm}
to depend on
T
yields

q_{0T}=\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\left\{e^{-i\theta_{\pm}}F[q_{0}e^{i\theta_{\pm}}]\right\},\qquad\theta_{\pm T}=\mp\frac{1}{q_{0}}\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\left\{e^{-i\theta_{\pm}}F[q_{0}e^{i\theta_{\pm}}]\right\}.

(5.2)

If the perturbation is such that
F[q_{0}e^{i\theta_{\pm}}]=F[q_{0}]e^{i\theta_{\pm}}
, then we have

q_{0T}=\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits F[q_{0}],\qquad(\Delta\theta)_{T}=0,

(5.3)

where
\Delta\theta=\theta_{+}-\theta_{-}
is the asymptotic phase difference. The first formula in (
5.3
) can be used to determine the evolution of the background amplitude of a perturbed dark soliton
a priori
. In the method outlined below, one instead determines the parameters
k_{1}
and
\Lambda_{1}
as in (
4.8
) and recovers the background through
q_{0}=\sqrt{k_{1}^{2}+\Lambda_{1}^{2}}
, which necessarily will give the same result as (
5.3
).

When
\epsilon=0
, the exact dark soliton solution satisfying the boundary conditions in (
2.1
) is
q(x,t)=u(\xi)
given by (
4.8
). Now, suppose that the soliton parameters depend on the slow time scale:

k_{1}=k_{1}(T),\qquad\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda_{1}(T),\qquad x_{1}=x_{1}(T).

(5.4)

In this case, we redefine the traveling coordinate
\xi
as

\xi=\Lambda_{1}(T)\left(x+2\int_{0}^{t}k_{1}\,ds-x_{1}(T)\right).

(5.5)

Note that since the slow-time-dependent velocity must be integrated, it is actually the case that the dependence of
k_{1}
on an even slower time scale
\epsilon^{2}t
could contribute nontrivially to the soliton center. Thus, to ensure a correct prediction for the soliton center, one would need to include another time scale in the perturbation theory. This is discussed in the context of a specific example in Remark 3 of Section 7.3, but is beyond the scope of this work.

Furthermore, to account for slow evolution of the overall phase, we introduce another parameter

\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{1}(T),\qquad\sigma_{1}(0)=0,

(5.6)

and let

q(x,t)\approx e^{i\sigma_{1}}\big[u(\xi)+\epsilon\tilde{q}(\xi,t)\big],

(5.7)

where
\tilde{q}
is the first order correction and explicit dependence on
T
is omitted for brevity. We will later show that the slowly evolving parameter
\sigma_{1}(T)
corresponds to inevitable secular growth in the first order correction, which can be absorbed into the phase at least for small times.
Substituting (
5.7
) into (
5.1
) leads at
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
to the following equation:

\displaystyle(i\partial_{t}+L)A(\xi,t)=W(\xi),\qquad A=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{q}\\
\tilde{q}^{\,*}\end{bmatrix},\qquad W=\begin{bmatrix}w[u]\\
-w[u]^{*}\end{bmatrix},

(5.8a)

\displaystyle w[u]=e^{-i\sigma_{1}}F[ue^{i\sigma_{1}}]-ie^{-i\sigma_{1}}\partial_{T}(ue^{i\sigma_{1}}),

(5.8b)

where the linearization operator
L
is the same as in (
4.10
). Note that

\displaystyle e^{-i\sigma_{1}}\partial_{T}(ue^{i\sigma_{1}})=k_{1T}-\sigma_{1T}\Lambda_{1}\tanh\xi+i\Lambda_{1T}(\tanh\xi+\xi\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi)-ix_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{2}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi+i\sigma_{1T}k_{1}.

(5.9)

Throughout, the subscript
T
denotes the partial derivative with respect to
T=\epsilon t
. According to the fully reduced 1-soliton closure relation (
4.33
), we can expand
A
and
W
in terms of the complete set of squared eigenfunctions
\{\mathcal{Z}(\xi,\zeta),\mathcal{Z}_{1}(\xi),\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}(\xi)\}
with respective adjoint eigenfunctions
\{\Upsilon(\xi,\zeta),\Upsilon_{1}(\xi),\tilde{\Upsilon}_{1}(\xi)\}
as follows:

W(\xi)=\frac{\zeta_{1}}{8\Lambda_{1}^{3}}\left[\langle\tilde{\Upsilon}_{1},W\rangle\mathcal{Z}_{1}(\xi)+\langle\Upsilon_{1},W\rangle\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}(\xi)\right]+\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\langle\Upsilon(\zeta),W\rangle}{\gamma(\zeta)|\zeta-\zeta_{1}|^{4}}\mathcal{Z}(\xi,\zeta)d\zeta,

(5.10a)

A(\xi,t)=\frac{\zeta_{1}}{8\Lambda_{1}^{3}}\left[\langle\tilde{\Upsilon}_{1},A(t)\rangle\mathcal{Z}_{1}(\xi)+\langle\Upsilon_{1},A(t)\rangle\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}_{1}(\xi)\right]+\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\langle\Upsilon(\zeta),A(t)\rangle}{\gamma(\zeta)|\zeta-\zeta_{1}|^{4}}\mathcal{Z}(\xi,\zeta)d\zeta.

(5.10b)

Substituting (
5.10
) into (
5.8a
) and equating coefficients of the discrete eigenfunctions gives

\displaystyle i\partial_{t}\langle\Upsilon_{1},A(t)\rangle=\langle\Upsilon_{1},W\rangle,\qquad i\partial_{t}\langle\tilde{\Upsilon}_{1},A(t)\rangle-4\Lambda_{1}^{2}\zeta_{1}^{-1}\langle\Upsilon_{1},A(t)\rangle=\langle\tilde{\Upsilon}_{1},W\rangle.

(5.11)

To suppress secular growth in (
5.11
), we need to enforce the orthogonality conditions

\langle\Upsilon_{1},W\rangle=\langle\tilde{\Upsilon}_{1},W\rangle=0.

(5.12)

The first orthogonality condition above yields the evolution of the soliton velocity explicitly in terms of the perturbation:

k_{1T}=\frac{1}{2}\,\text{Im}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F_{0}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi\,d\xi,\qquad F_{0}=e^{-i\sigma_{1}}F[ue^{i\sigma_{1}}].

(5.13)

As we will show with several examples in Sec 7, once the perturbation is specified, the second orthogonality condition in (
5.12
) yields two separate conditions on the soliton parameters (one corresponding to eliminating the divergent integrals). One of these conditions can be used to fully determine the dependence of
\Lambda_{1}
on the slow time
T
, and the other relates the evolution of the soliton phase and center,
\sigma_{1}
and
x_{1}
.

Moreover, equating coefficients of the continuous eigenfunctions gives

i\partial_{t}\langle\Upsilon(\zeta),A(t)\rangle+4\lambda(k-k_{1})\langle\Upsilon(\zeta),A(t)\rangle=\langle\Upsilon(\zeta),W\rangle.

(5.14)

The solution of (
5.14
) with zero initial condition is

\langle\Upsilon(\zeta),A(t)\rangle=\frac{\langle\Upsilon(\zeta),W\rangle}{4\lambda(k-k_{1})}\left[1-e^{4i\lambda(k-k_{1})t}\right].

(5.15)

Using the above equation in (
5.10b
) yields for the first order correction:

A(\xi,t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\gamma(\zeta)|\zeta-\zeta_{1}|^{4}}\frac{1-e^{4i\lambda(\zeta)(k(\zeta)-k_{1})t}}{4\lambda(\zeta)\big(k(\zeta)-k_{1}\big)}\langle\Upsilon(\zeta),W\rangle\mathcal{Z}(\xi,\zeta)d\zeta.

(5.16)

Note that
\gamma(\zeta)=2\zeta^{-1}\lambda(\zeta)
, so this could be written as

A(\xi,t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta}{|\zeta-\zeta_{1}|^{4}}\frac{1-e^{4i\lambda(\zeta)(k(\zeta)-k_{1})t}}{8\lambda(\zeta)^{2}\big(k(\zeta)-k_{1}\big)}\langle\Upsilon(\zeta),W\rangle\mathcal{Z}(\xi,\zeta)d\zeta,

(5.17)

which, up to an inessential difference in the normalization of the squared eigenfunctions, has the same form as the one given in
[
34
]
, with the important caveat that the integral has to be considered in the principal value sense, due to the singularities at
\zeta=\pm q_{0}
which arise from
\lambda(\zeta)
. We stress that due to the fact that the integrand has poles at
\pm q_{0}
, it is crucial for this integral to be considered in the principal value sense in order for it to be well-defined. This also explains why one has to explicitly single out the contributions at
\pm q_{0}
from the closure relation.

It is worth highlighting that the issue of the poles at
\pm q_{0}
was explicitly acknowledged in
[
33
]
. Our present approach to account for these poles is to modify the adjoint discrete squared eigenfunctions appropriately (i.e., enforce orthogonality with
\tilde{\Upsilon}_{1}
rather than with
\Upsilon^{\prime}_{1}
) such that the first order correction is reduced to a principal value integral. On the other hand, the approach of
[
33
]
was to enforce the additional orthogonality condition
\langle\Upsilon_{0}^{\pm},W\rangle=0
. However, this condition is too strong. In fact, we will later show that the inner product
\langle\Upsilon_{0}^{\pm},W\rangle
is generically nonzero, a fact that is crucial to understanding the generation of the shelf.

6
First order correction, shelf, and evolution of the phase

As we will show in this section, the first order correction can be effectively used to describe the shelf that develops on each side of the soliton, which in fact results from the singularities
of the scattering data at the points
\pm q_{0}
, and to obtain estimates for the height and velocity of the shelf. Furthermore, the asymptotic behavior of the first order correction provides the spatial phase gradient on each side of the shelf, as well as a formula for the slow time evolution
of the core soliton phase, which in turn allows one to determine the dependence of the soliton center on the slow time
T
.

Taking the first component of (
5.17
), on account of (
5.7
) and (
5.8a
) the first order correction
\tilde{q}
is given by:

\tilde{q}(\xi,t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta}{|\zeta-\zeta_{1}|^{4}}\frac{1-e^{4i\lambda(\zeta)(k(\zeta)-k_{1})t}}{8\lambda(\zeta)^{2}\big(k(\zeta)-k_{1}\big)}\langle\Upsilon(\zeta),W\rangle\Big[i(\zeta_{1}-q_{0}^{2}\zeta^{-1})+\Lambda_{1}e^{-\xi}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits\xi\Big]^{2}e^{2i\frac{\lambda(\zeta)}{\Lambda_{1}}\xi}d\zeta.

(6.1)

First, we study the first order correction on the right side of the shelf, i.e., the asymptotic limit
\xi\rightarrow+\infty
:

\tilde{q}^{+}\sim-\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta}{|\zeta-\zeta_{1}|^{4}}\frac{1-e^{4i\lambda(\zeta)(k(\zeta)-k_{1})t}}{8\lambda(\zeta)^{2}\big(k(\zeta)-k_{1}\big)}\langle\Upsilon(\zeta),W\rangle\big(\zeta_{1}-q_{0}^{2}\zeta^{-1}\big)^{2}e^{2i\frac{\lambda(\zeta)}{\Lambda_{1}}\xi}d\zeta.

(6.2)

For large
\xi
, the
\xi
-dependent exponential in (
6.2
) is rapidly oscillating, so the primary contributions to the principal value integral come from neighborhoods around the simple poles
\zeta=\pm q_{0}
(recall
\lambda(\pm q_{0})=0
). It will be convenient to define

\mathcal{F}(\zeta)=-\frac{\zeta(\zeta_{1}-q_{0}^{2}\zeta^{-1})^{2}}{16\pi|\zeta-\zeta_{1}|^{4}\big(k(\zeta)-k_{1}\big)}\langle\Upsilon(\zeta),W\rangle,

(6.3)

and split the integral into two parts

\tilde{q}^{+}\sim\mathcal{F}(-q_{0})\underbrace{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{1-e^{4i\lambda(\zeta)(k(\zeta)-k_{1})t}}{\lambda(\zeta)^{2}}e^{2i\frac{\lambda(\zeta)}{\Lambda_{1}}\xi}d\zeta}_{I_{-}}+\mathcal{F}(q_{0})\underbrace{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-e^{4i\lambda(\zeta)(k(\zeta)-k_{1})t}}{\lambda(\zeta)^{2}}e^{2i\frac{\lambda(\zeta)}{\Lambda_{1}}\xi}d\zeta}_{I_{+}}.

(6.4)

After simplification, including using (
4.27
), we have

\mathcal{F}(\pm q_{0})=\pm\frac{i\alpha e^{i\theta}}{16\pi(k_{1}\mp q_{0})},

(6.5)

where
\alpha
is defined by

\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}u^{*}\\
u\end{bmatrix},W\right\rangle=:i\alpha,\qquad\alpha\in\mathbb{R}.

(6.6)

Note that due to the form of
W
, the inner product in (
6.6
) is purely imaginary regardless of the choice of perturbation.
The integrals
I_{\pm}
in (
6.4
) are computed explicitly through lengthy but straightforward calculations detailed in Appendix C. This calculation also naturally reveals the right boundary of the shelf to be
\xi\sim 2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})t
. In particular, putting everything together into (
6.4
), we find that
\tilde{q}^{+}\sim 0
for
\xi\gg 2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})t
, and inside the shelf region
1\ll\xi\ll 2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})t
we have

\tilde{q}^{+}\sim\frac{\alpha e^{i\theta}}{8q_{0}(k_{1}+q_{0})}+\frac{\alpha e^{i\theta}}{4\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})}i\xi+\frac{\alpha e^{i\theta}}{2}it.

(6.7)

This suggests that there is inevitable growth in
\xi
and
t
in the first order correction resulting from the singularities at
\pm q_{0}
. Now, according to our original ansatz (
5.7
), the full approximate solution in the right shelf region should be expressed as

q^{+}\sim e^{i\sigma_{1}}\left[q_{0}e^{i\theta}+\epsilon\tilde{q}^{+}\right].

(6.8)

Substituting the expression for
\tilde{q}^{+}
, (
6.8
) is equivalent up to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
to

q^{+}\sim e^{i\theta+i\sigma_{1}}\left[q_{0}+\epsilon\frac{\alpha}{8q_{0}(k_{1}+q_{0})}\right]\left[1+\frac{\alpha}{4q_{0}\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})}i\epsilon\xi+\frac{\alpha}{2q_{{0}}}i\epsilon t\right].

(6.9)

We can account for the terms that grow with
T=\epsilon t
and
\epsilon\xi
by moving them into the exponent, i.e., by approximating the above as

q^{+}\sim\left[q_{0}+\frac{\alpha}{8q_{0}(k_{1}+q_{0})}\right]\exp\left\{i\theta+i\sigma_{1}+\frac{\alpha}{4q_{0}\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})}i\epsilon\xi+\frac{\alpha}{2q_{{0}}}i\epsilon t\right\}.

(6.10)

The purpose of the introduction of the
T
-dependent phase parameter
\sigma_{1}
is now evident. Namely, we can choose the slow time evolution of the soliton phase to be

\sigma_{1T}=-\frac{\alpha}{2q_{0}},

(6.11)

such that the secular growth in time resulting from the inevitable time dependence of the first order correction integral is suppressed.
Therefore, we construct the full approximate solution in the right shelf region as

q^{+}\sim(q_{0}+\epsilon h^{+})e^{i\theta+i\epsilon\varphi^{+}},

(6.12)

where the height of the shelf and the spatial phase gradient are respectively given by

h^{+}=\frac{\alpha}{8q_{0}(k_{1}+q_{0})},\qquad\varphi_{\xi}^{+}=\frac{\alpha}{4q_{0}\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})}.

(6.13)

We later empirically demonstrate for several examples that this approximation of the spatial and temporal growth of the first order correction as a complex exponential does accurately reflect the dynamics, at least for a moderate time interval. That said, we hypothesize that the breakdown of this approximation could be a reason why our predictions for the soliton phase, which coincide with the ones in
[
30
]
, do not necessarily retain their validity up to
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon)
, as we will discuss in more details in Sec 7 and in the conclusions.

Note that (
6.11
) serves as a generic formula for the evolution of the soliton phase parameter, which depends (through
\alpha
, see (
6.6
)) on the form of the perturbation. Recall that the orthogonality conditions from Sec 5 provide three differential equations for four parameters. When supplemented with this formula, the soliton parameters are completely determined.
Also, observe from the above that the formation of the shelf is directly connected to the evolution of
\sigma_{1}
. Namely, if for a given perturbation we have
\alpha=0
, then no shelf develops and
\sigma_{1}
is constant (see for example the self-steepening perturbation discussed in Sec 7.4).

Repeating these calculations on the left side of the shelf, i.e., in the asymptotic limit
\xi\rightarrow-\infty
, we find that the left boundary of the shelf is
\xi\sim 2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}-q_{0})t
, with
\tilde{q}^{-}\sim 0
for
\xi\ll 2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}-q_{0})t
, and in the left shelf region
2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}-q_{0})t\ll\xi\ll-1
:

\tilde{q}^{-}\sim-\frac{\alpha e^{-i\theta}}{8q_{0}(k_{1}-q_{0})}+\frac{\alpha e^{-i\theta}}{4\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}-q_{0})}i\xi+\frac{\alpha e^{-i\theta}}{2}it.

(6.14)

Similarly, we construct the full approximate solution in the shelf region as

q^{-}\sim(q_{0}+\epsilon h^{-})e^{i\theta+i\epsilon\varphi^{-}},

(6.15)

where

h^{-}=-\frac{\alpha}{8q_{0}(k_{1}-q_{0})},\qquad\varphi_{\xi}^{-}=\frac{\alpha}{4q_{0}\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}-q_{0})}.

(6.16)

From these formulae it can be seen that, regardless of the perturbation, we have

\Lambda_{1}\varphi_{\xi}^{\pm}=\pm 2h^{\pm},

(6.17)

which is in agreement with the results in
[
30
]
.

7
Applications

In this section, we apply our results to the perturbed NLS equation (
5.1
) with various small forcing perturbations: linear damping, nonlinear damping (also referred to as two-photon absorption, or TPA, in nonlinear optics, which is the terminology used in
[
30
]
), dissipation, and self-steepening. All the results are corroborated by direct numerical simulations, and compared with the results of the direct perturbation theory, and with the earlier works using perturbation theory based on the squared eigenfunctions.

7.1
Linear damping

Consider Eq. (
5.1
) with a linear damping perturbation:

F_{0}=-iu=-ik_{1}+\Lambda_{1}\tanh\xi.

(7.1)

In this case, (
5.13
) implies that

k_{1T}=-k_{1}.

(7.2)

We now demonstrate that two distinct constraints on the soliton parameters arise from the second orthogonality condition. To this end, a straightforward calculation shows that

\langle\Upsilon_{1}^{\prime},W\rangle=-\frac{4q_{0}}{\zeta_{1}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Big\{\left[\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(e^{-i\theta}F_{0})-\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(e^{-i\theta}D)\right]e^{\xi}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits\xi+\frac{k_{1}}{q_{0}}\left[\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(F_{0})-\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(D)\right]\xi\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi\Big\}d\xi,

(7.3)

where
D=e^{-i\sigma_{1}}\partial_{T}(ue^{i\sigma_{1}})
, which is given explicitly in (
5.9
).
Note that

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(D)=k_{1T}-\sigma_{1T}\Lambda_{1}\tanh\xi,

(7.4a)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(e^{-i\theta}D)=\frac{1}{q_{0}}\Big(k_{1}k_{1T}-\sigma_{1T}\Lambda_{1}k_{1}(\tanh\xi-1)+\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{1T}(\tanh\xi+\xi\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi)-x_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{3}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi\Big),

(7.4b)

where we have used
\cos\theta=k_{1}/q_{0}
and
\sin\theta=\Lambda_{1}/q_{0}
, and in the linear damping case we have

\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(F_{0})=-k_{1},\qquad\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(e^{-i\theta}F_{0})=-\frac{1}{q_{0}}\Big(k_{1}^{2}+\Lambda_{1}^{2}\tanh\xi\Big).

(7.4c)

Substituting (
7.4
) into (
7.3
) and computing all convergent integrals gives

\langle{\Upsilon}^{\prime}_{1},W\rangle=4\Lambda_{1}\zeta_{1}^{-1}\left\{(\Lambda_{1T}+\Lambda_{1})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\xi}\tanh\xi\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits\xi\,d\xi+\sigma_{1T}k_{1}+\Lambda_{1T}-2x_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{2}\right\}.

(7.5)

The integral that remains is (logarithmically) divergent. Next, we find

\langle(u^{*},u)^{T},W\rangle=-\frac{2i}{\Lambda_{1}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Big\{|u|^{2}+\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(u^{*}D)\Big\}d\xi,

(7.6)

with

\displaystyle|u|^{2}=k_{1}^{2}+\Lambda_{1}^{2}\tanh^{2}\xi,

(7.7a)

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(u^{*}D)=k_{1}k_{1T}+\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{1T}\tanh\xi(\tanh\xi+\xi\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi)-x_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{3}\tanh\xi\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi.

(7.7b)

Substituting these and computing all convergent integrals gives

\langle(u^{*},u)^{T},W\rangle=-2i\left\{(\Lambda_{1T}+\Lambda_{1})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tanh^{2}\xi\,d\xi+\Lambda_{1T}\right\}.

(7.8)

The integral that remains is (linearly) divergent. To eliminate the divergence from both inner products above, it suffices to enforce:

\Lambda_{1T}=-\Lambda_{1}.

(7.9)

With
\Lambda_{1}
now fully determined, (
7.5
) and (
7.8
) reduce to

\displaystyle\langle{\Upsilon}^{\prime}_{1},W\rangle=4\Lambda_{1}\zeta_{1}^{-1}(\sigma_{1T}k_{1}-\Lambda_{1}-2x_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{2}),

(7.10a)

\displaystyle\langle(u^{*},u)^{T},W\rangle=2i\Lambda_{1}.

(7.10b)

We note that the same linear damping perturbation was studied in
[
34
]
. In that work, there were two important differences in the approach compared to ours: (i) the possibility of slow evolution of the phase independent of the other soliton parameters, which we account for with
\sigma_{1T}
, was not considered; (ii) the discrete squared eigenfunctions were not properly modified to account for the contributions from
\pm q_{0}
, that is, the orthogonality condition being enforced was
\langle\Upsilon^{\prime}_{1},W\rangle=0
. For these reasons, the conclusion in
[
34
]
is that the evolution of the soliton center is given by
x_{1T}=-1/2\Lambda_{1}
. This prediction is not consistent with physical intuition (or numerical simulations), as it is independent of the direction that the soliton is traveling.
Putting (
7.10a
) and (
7.10b
) together as in (
4.32
), we find the proper orthogonality condition to be

\langle\tilde{\Upsilon}_{1},W\rangle=4\Lambda_{1}\zeta_{1}^{-1}(\sigma_{1T}k_{1}-2x_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{2})=0,

(7.11)

which provides another condition on the soliton parameters, namely,

\sigma_{1T}k_{1}=2x_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{2}.

(7.12)

Finally, upon supplementing this condition with the formula for
\sigma_{1T}
obtained in (
6.11
), all four soliton parameters are uniquely determined. In particular, using (
7.10b
), we find

\sigma_{1T}=-\frac{\Lambda_{1}}{q_{0}}.

(7.13)

The corresponding evolution of the soliton center is

x_{1T}=-\frac{k_{1}}{2q_{0}\Lambda_{1}}.

(7.14)

Solving for the explicit time evolution of all soliton parameters:

\displaystyle q_{0}(T)=q_{0}(0)e^{-T},\qquad k_{1}(T)=k_{1}(0)e^{-T},\qquad\Lambda_{1}(T)=\Lambda_{1}(0)e^{-T},

(7.15a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(T)=-\frac{\Lambda_{1}(0)}{q_{0}(0)}T+\sigma_{1}(0),\qquad x_{1}(T)=\frac{k_{1}(0)}{2q_{0}(0)\Lambda_{1}(0)}(1-e^{T})+x_{1}(0).

(7.15b)

All of these formulae agree exactly with those found using a different method in
[
30
]
, after the appropriate notational changes, with the exception of the soliton center, which was determined in
[
30
]
by employing information from
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
.

7.2
Nonlinear damping

Consider Eq. (
5.1
) with a nonlinear damping perturbation

F_{0}=-i|u|^{2}u=(q_{0}^{2}-\Lambda_{1}^{2}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi)(-ik_{1}+\Lambda_{1}\tanh\xi).

(7.18)

In this case, (
5.13
) implies that

k_{1T}=-\left(\frac{2}{3}k_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{3}q_{0}^{2}\right)k_{1}.

(7.19)

Following similar steps as in Sec 7.1, we substitute

\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(e^{-i\theta}F_{0})=-\frac{1}{q_{0}}(q_{0}^{2}-\Lambda_{1}^{2}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi)(k_{1}^{2}+\Lambda_{1}^{2}\tanh\xi),

(7.20)

into (
7.3
), and after simplification we find

\displaystyle\langle\Upsilon_{1}^{\prime},W\rangle=\frac{4\Lambda_{1}}{\zeta_{1}}\left\{\left(\Lambda_{1}q_{0}^{2}+\frac{2}{3}\Lambda_{1}k_{1}^{2}+\Lambda_{1T}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\xi}\tanh\xi\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits\xi\,d\xi+\Lambda_{1T}-\frac{2}{3}\Lambda_{1}q_{0}^{2}+\sigma_{1T}k_{1}-2x_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{2}\right\}.

(7.21)

Furthermore, it can be found that

\langle(u^{*},u)^{T},W\rangle=-2i\left\{\left(\Lambda_{1}q_{0}^{2}+\frac{2}{3}\Lambda_{1}k_{1}^{2}+\Lambda_{1T}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}1d\xi-4q_{0}^{2}\Lambda_{1}+\frac{4}{3}\Lambda_{1}^{3}-\Lambda_{1T}\right\}.

(7.22)

To eliminate the divergence in both (
7.21
) and (
7.22
), set

\Lambda_{1T}=-\left(\frac{2}{3}k_{1}^{2}+q_{0}^{2}\right)\Lambda_{1}.

(7.23)

Note that combining the evolution of
k_{1}
and
\Lambda_{1}
, we get the evolution of the background

q_{0T}=-q_{0}^{3}.

(7.24)

With
\Lambda_{1}
determined, (
7.21
) and (
7.22
) become

\displaystyle\langle\Upsilon_{1}^{\prime},W\rangle=\frac{4\Lambda_{1}}{\zeta_{1}}\left\{-\left(\frac{2}{3}k_{1}^{2}+\frac{5}{3}q_{0}^{2}\right)\Lambda_{1}+\sigma_{1T}k_{1}-2x_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{2}\right\},

(7.25a)

\displaystyle\langle(u^{*},u)^{T},W\rangle=2i\left(\frac{2}{3}k_{1}^{2}+\frac{5}{3}q_{0}^{2}\right)\Lambda_{1}.

(7.25b)

Combining, we find

\langle\tilde{\Upsilon}_{1},W\rangle=4\Lambda_{1}\zeta_{1}^{-1}(\sigma_{1T}k_{1}-2x_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{2}),

(7.26)

which is the same as in the previous example. So, we must again enforce the condition

\sigma_{1T}k_{1}=2x_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{2}.

(7.27)

From (
6.11
), using (
7.25b
) we find the evolution of the phase to be

\sigma_{1T}=-\left(\frac{2}{3}k_{1}^{2}+\frac{5}{3}q_{0}^{2}\right)\frac{\Lambda_{1}}{q_{0}},

(7.28)

which in turn determines the evolution of the soliton center to be

x_{1T}=-\left(\frac{2}{3}k_{1}^{2}+\frac{5}{3}q_{0}^{2}\right)\frac{k_{1}}{2q_{0}\Lambda_{1}}.

(7.29)

The evolution equations for the velocity, amplitude, and phase coincide with the ones obtained from the method of
[
30
]
(note that no prediction for the soliton center is given in
[
30
]
).

7.3
Dissipation

Consider Eq. (
5.1
) with a dissipative perturbation

F_{0}=iu_{xx}=2\Lambda_{1}^{3}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi\tanh\xi.

(7.31)

In this case, since
F_{0}
is purely real, (
5.13
) implies that

k_{1T}=0.

(7.32)

Returning to (
7.3
) and putting in the new expression for
F_{0}
gives

\displaystyle\langle\Upsilon_{1}^{\prime},W\rangle=\frac{4}{\zeta_{1}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\{2\Lambda_{1}^{4}e^{\xi}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{3}\xi\tanh\xi+\Lambda_{1}\Lambda_{1T}(e^{\xi}\tanh\xi\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits\xi+\xi e^{\xi}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{3}\xi)\right.

(7.33)

\displaystyle\left.-x_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{3}e^{\xi}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{3}\xi-\sigma_{1T}\Lambda_{1}k_{1}(\tanh\xi-1)e^{\xi}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits\xi-k_{1}\sigma_{1T}\Lambda_{1}\xi\tanh\xi\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi\right\}d\xi.

Computing the convergent integrals, this reduces to

\langle\Upsilon_{1}^{\prime},W\rangle=4\Lambda_{1}\zeta_{1}^{-1}\left\{\Lambda_{1T}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{\xi}\tanh\xi\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits\xi\,d\xi+\frac{4}{3}\Lambda_{1}^{3}+\sigma_{1T}k_{1}+\Lambda_{1T}-2x_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{2}\right\}.

(7.34)

Next, we find

\langle(u^{*},u)^{T},W\rangle=-2i\left\{\Lambda_{1T}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tanh^{2}\xi\,d\xi+\frac{4}{3}\Lambda_{1}^{3}+\Lambda_{1T}\right\}.

(7.35)

The only way to remove the divergence from both (
7.34
) and (
7.35
) is to set

\Lambda_{1T}=0.

(7.36)

With this, we have

\displaystyle\langle\dot{\Upsilon}(\zeta_{1}),W\rangle=4\Lambda_{1}\zeta_{1}^{-1}\left(\frac{4}{3}\Lambda_{1}^{3}+\sigma_{1T}k_{1}-2x_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{2}\right),

(7.37a)

\displaystyle\langle(u^{*},u)^{T},W\rangle=-\frac{8}{3}i\Lambda_{1}^{3}.

(7.37b)

Putting these together, we find

\langle\tilde{\Upsilon},W\rangle=4\Lambda_{1}\zeta_{1}^{-1}(\sigma_{1T}k_{1}-2x_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{2}),

(7.38)

which leads to the same condition on the center and phase that was found in both the previous examples,

\sigma_{1T}k_{1}=2x_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{2}.

(7.39)

Substituting (
7.37b
) into (
6.11
) gives

\sigma_{1T}=\frac{4\Lambda_{1}^{3}}{3q_{0}},

(7.40)

and the corresponding evolution of the soliton center is

x_{1T}=\frac{2\Lambda_{1}k_{1}}{3q_{0}}.

(7.41)

Solving explicitly for all soliton parameters:

\displaystyle q_{0}(T)=q_{0}(0),\;\;\;k_{1}(T)=k_{1}(0),\;\;\;\Lambda_{1}(T)=\Lambda_{1}(0),

(7.42a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(T)=\frac{4\Lambda_{1}(0)^{3}}{3q_{0}(0)}T+\sigma_{1}(0),\;\;\;x_{1}(T)=\frac{2\Lambda_{1}(0)k_{1}(0)}{3q_{0}(0)}T+x_{1}(0).

(7.42b)

Remark 3
.

In
[
30
]
,
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
information is used to obtain the following formula (after notational changes) for the soliton center, which is plotted in blue in the right panel of Figure
10
:

x_{1}(T)=x_{1}(0)+\frac{2\Lambda_{1}(0)k_{1}(0)}{3q_{0}(0)}T+\frac{8\Lambda_{1}(0)^{3}k_{1}(0)}{9q_{0}(0)}T^{2}.

(7.43)

A possible explanation as to why the present theory is unable to capture the quadratic term in (
7.43
) is that this term could arise from a dependence of the velocity parameter
k_{1}
on the slower time scale
\hat{T}=\epsilon T=\epsilon^{2}t
. In particular, since

\frac{8\Lambda_{1}(0)^{3}k_{1}(0)}{9q_{0}(0)}T^{2}=2\int_{0}^{T}\frac{8\Lambda_{1}(0)^{3}k_{1}(0)}{9q_{0}(0)}\hat{s}\,d\hat{s},

an identical correction to the location of the soliton center can be obtained by taking the evolution of the velocity parameter to be

k_{1}(\hat{T})=k_{1}(0)-\frac{8\Lambda_{1}(0)^{3}k_{1}(0)}{9q_{0}(0)}\hat{T}.

(7.44)

Recall that the parameter
k_{1}
controls not only the velocity but also the trough amplitude of the dark soliton. As such, if the correction found in
[
30
]
for the center is to be interpreted instead as a correction to
k_{1}
as in (
7.44
), then there should be an observable slow decrease in the trough amplitude over longer time scales. Numerical evidence suggests that this is the case, and that (
7.44

7.4
Self-steepening

We note that while the condition
\sigma_{1T}k_{1}=2x_{1T}\Lambda_{1}^{2}
has appeared in all three previous examples, it is not universal. For example, in the case of a self-steepening perturbation

F_{0}=-i(|u|^{2}u)_{x}=-\Lambda_{1}^{2}\big(2ik_{1}\Lambda_{1}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi\tanh\xi-k_{1}^{2}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi-3\Lambda_{1}\mathop{\rm sech}\nolimits^{2}\xi\tanh^{2}\xi\big),

(7.46)

one finds after applying our method that
k_{1T}=0
,
\Lambda_{1T}=0
,
\sigma_{1T}=0
and no prominent shelf is formed. The absence of the shelf is tied to the fact that for this perturbation, we have that

\langle\Upsilon_{0}^{\pm},W\rangle=0.

(7.47)

Thus, the singularity in the first order correction integral (
6.1
) is fully removed, and in turn there is no secular growth. For the same reason, any perturbation satisfying (
7.47
) will not generate a shelf.
In the self-steepening case, the only soliton parameter affected is the center, whose evolution is obtained directly from the orthogonality condition
\langle\tilde{\Upsilon}_{1},W\rangle=0
and is given by

x_{1T}=2k_{1}^{2}+\Lambda_{1}^{2}.

(7.48)

So, the center is pushed forward linearly by the perturbation, which is similar to the situation for bright solitons under a self-steepening effect (see
[
29
]
). We note that our predictions here are significantly different than those given in
[
35
]
for the same perturbation. In that work, the amplitude and velocity of the dark soliton are predicted to evolve as a result of the perturbation. However, this is not supported by the numerics, which confirm that these parameters remain constant under the perturbation. Figure

demonstrates that our prediction for the soliton center (

8
Conclusions

In this work, we revisited the IST-based integrable perturbation theory of the scalar defocusing NLS on a nontrivial background, addressing the conjecture posed in
[
30
]
that, because of the existence of an expanding shelf that develops on each side of the soliton due to the perturbation, the squared eigenfunctions associated with the soliton are an insufficient basis, and questioning the existence of a closure relation for this problem, and the ability of integrable perturbation theory to accurately describe the radiation shelf.

We provided a proof of the completeness of the squared eigenfunctions, a crucial difference with respect to the earlier works lying in properly accounting for the singularities of the scattering data at the points
\pm q_{0}
, which are branch points for the continuous spectrum.
Moreover, we showed that the first order correction can in fact be used to describe the soliton shelf, which results from the singularities of the scattering data at the points
\pm q_{0}
, and we obtained estimates for the magnitude, velocity and phase gradient on each side of the shelf.
Using the 1-soliton closure relation, suitable suppression of secular growth, and the asymptotic behavior of the first order correction we determined the adiabatic evolution of the soliton parameters as functions of a slow time variable
T=\epsilon t
.

All the results were corroborated by direct numerical simulations, and compared with the results of the direct perturbation theory in
[
30
]
, and with the earlier works using perturbation theory based on the squared eigenfunctions. In particular, we showed that the estimates we obtained for all soliton parameters agree with the ones in
[
30
]
to order
\epsilon
, the only difference being for the soliton center, which, unlike the other parameters, obtained from perturbed conserved quantities up to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
, is determined in
[
30
]
in terms of differential equations obtained from the Hamiltonian at
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
. We also explained the shortcomings of the predictions for the adiabatic evolution of the soliton parameters in the earlier works within the framework of the integrable perturbation theory.

For some perturbations, our predictions for the soliton center and phase do not match the numerical data on the time scale of
T=\epsilon t=\mathcal{O}(1)
. A similar problem for the phase can also be seen in some of the examples considered in
[
30
]
.
The reason for this discrepancy has been discussed in Remark 3, and it lies in the fact that since the slow-time-dependent velocity must be integrated in time when passing to the co-moving frame (cf Eq. (
5.5
)), it is actually the case the dependence of
k_{1}
on an even slower time scale
\epsilon^{2}t
could contribute nontrivially to the soliton center. Thus, to ensure a correct prediction for the soliton center, one would need to include another time scale in the perturbation theory. A similar argument would apply to the soliton phase
\sigma_{1}
, on account of the integration in time of the slow-varying background amplitude
q_{0}
in Eq. (
7.16
).
This indicates that in order to achieve
correct estimates up to
t=\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon)
for the soliton center and phase, one would need to compute the second order correction terms. It is important to stress that this is an intrinsic feature of the problem, and not of the perturbative approach, as both our estimates, based on the integrable perturbation theory, and the ones in
[
30
]
, based on direct perturbation theory, with multiple scale expansions and perturbed conservation laws, exhibit the same behavior. In fact, one could argue that similar considerations should be applied even in the bright soliton perturbation theory, where the same transformation to the co-moving frame is routinely applied.

We believe the present work may open up a new vein of research on integrable perturbation theory for integrable systems on a nonzero background, and the methodology can also be generalized to discrete and coupled integrable systems.
Another natural follow-up is to explore ways to improve the accuracy of the estimates for the adiabatic evolution of the soliton parameters for times up to
\mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon)
. This could be done by introducing an additional time scale
\epsilon^{2}t
and a second order correction to the potential, which will require handling quadratic contributions in the first order correction appearing in the orthogonality conditions. Alternatively, one could generalize the approach used to determine the slow-time evolution of the soliton center in
[
30
]
, which makes use of the Hamiltonian at
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
, and consider also the other perturbed conserved quantities to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
in order to get additional corrections to the soliton phase, amplitude and velocity.

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ip\vartheta}}{p}dp=i\pi\mathop{\rm sgn}\nolimits\vartheta.

(C.7)

To this end,
J_{1}
can be split again into

J_{1}\sim\frac{1}{q_{0}}\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{1}{\zeta+q_{0}}e^{\frac{2i}{\Lambda_{1}}(\zeta+q_{0})\xi}d\zeta-\frac{1}{q_{0}}\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{1}{\zeta+q_{0}}e^{\frac{2i}{\Lambda_{1}}(\zeta+q_{0})\left[\xi-2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})t\right]}d\zeta.

(C.8)

Applying the formula (
C.7
) gives

J_{1}\sim\frac{\pi i}{q_{0}}\left\{1-\mathop{\rm sgn}\nolimits\big[\xi-2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})t\big]\right\}.

(C.9)

If
\xi\gg 2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})t
, then
J_{1}\sim 0
. In the right shelf region
1\ll\xi\ll 2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})t
,
J_{1}\sim 2\pi i/q_{0}
. Following similar steps for
J_{2}
, we obtain

J_{2}\sim\frac{2\pi i}{\Lambda_{1}}\left\{1-\mathop{\rm sgn}\nolimits\big[\xi-2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})t\big]\right\}.

(C.10)

which gives in the shelf region
J_{2}=4\pi i/\Lambda_{1}
. Lastly, noting that
\lambda^{\prime}(\zeta)\approx 1
and
k^{\prime}(\zeta)\approx 0
near the pole, we get

J_{3}\sim 4\pi i(k_{1}+q_{0})\,\mathop{\rm sgn}\nolimits\big[\xi-2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})t\big],

(C.11)

which shows that

J_{3}\sim\begin{cases}4\pi i(k_{1}+q_{0}),\quad&\xi\gg 2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})t\\
-4\pi i(k_{1}+q_{0}),\quad&1\ll\xi\ll 2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})t\end{cases}.

(C.12)

We now move on to evaluate the integral
I_{+}
, given by

I_{+}=4\,\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(\zeta-q_{0})^{2}}\frac{1-e^{4i\lambda(\zeta)(k(\zeta)-k_{1})t}}{(1+q_{0}\zeta^{-1})^{2}}e^{2i\frac{\lambda(\zeta)}{\Lambda_{1}}\xi}d\zeta,

(C.13)

which has a pole at
\zeta=q_{0}
.
Similarly to the previous calculations, applying integration by parts splits the integral into

I_{+}=H_{1}+i\xi H_{2}+itH_{3},

(C.14)

where

\displaystyle H_{1}=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\zeta-q_{0}}\frac{8q_{0}\zeta^{-2}}{(1+q_{0}\zeta^{-1})^{3}}\big[1-e^{4i\lambda(\zeta)(k(\zeta)-k_{1})t}\big]e^{2i\frac{\lambda(\zeta)}{\Lambda_{1}}\xi}d\zeta

(C.15a)

\displaystyle H_{2}=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\zeta-q_{0}}\frac{1}{(1+q_{0}\zeta^{-1})^{2}}\frac{8\lambda^{\prime}(\zeta)}{\Lambda_{1}}\big[1-e^{4i\lambda(\zeta)(k(\zeta)-k_{1})t}\big]e^{2i\frac{\lambda(\zeta)}{\Lambda_{1}}\xi}d\zeta

(C.15b)

\displaystyle H_{3}=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.50687pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.86179pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.26085pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.09116pt}}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\zeta-q_{0}}(-16)\frac{\lambda^{\prime}(\zeta)(k(\zeta)-k_{1})+\lambda(\zeta){k}^{\prime}(\zeta)}{(1+q_{0}\zeta^{-1})^{2}}e^{4i\lambda(\zeta)(k(\zeta)-k_{1})t}e^{2i\frac{\lambda(\zeta)}{\Lambda_{1}}\xi}d\zeta.

(C.15c)

Using the formula (
C.7
), we find that first two integrals are

\displaystyle H_{1}\sim\frac{\pi i}{q_{0}}\left\{1-\mathop{\rm sgn}\nolimits\big[\xi-2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}-q_{0})t\big]\right\},

(C.16)

\displaystyle H_{2}\sim\frac{2\pi i}{\Lambda_{1}}\left\{1-\mathop{\rm sgn}\nolimits\big[\xi-2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}-q_{0})t\big]\right\}.

(C.17)

Since we are studying the large positive
\xi
limit, and
k_{1}<q_{0}
, we have that
H_{1}\sim 0
and
H_{2}\sim 0
. Thus, the contributions to the height and phase gradient of the right side of the shelf only come from the pole
\zeta=-q_{0}
. The third integral is found to be

H_{3}\sim 4\pi i(k_{1}-q_{0})\mathop{\rm sgn}\nolimits\big[\xi-2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}-q_{0})t\big],

(C.18)

which is limit gives
H_{3}\sim 4\pi i(k_{1}-q_{0})
. To summarize, putting everything into (
6.4
), we have that if
\xi\gg 2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})t
,

\tilde{q}^{+}\sim-\mathcal{F}(-q_{0})4\pi(k_{1}+q_{0})t-\mathcal{F}(q_{0})4\pi(k_{1}-q_{0})t=0,

(C.19)

as expected, and in the right shelf region
1\ll\xi\ll 2\Lambda_{1}(k_{1}+q_{0})t
,

q^{+}\sim\mathcal{F}(-q_{0})\left[\frac{2\pi i}{q_{0}}-\frac{4\pi}{\Lambda_{1}}\xi+4\pi(k_{1}+q_{0})t\right]-\mathcal{F}(q_{0})4\pi(k_{1}-q_{0})t.

(C.20)

Putting in (
6.5
), this reduces to the expression given in Sec 6. The calculation of the left side of the shelf from the large negative
\xi
limit proceeds in an analogous way. In that case, the contributions to the height and phase gradient of the shelf come only from the pole
\zeta=q_{0}
.
