Isolated trigeminal neuropathy as an initial manifestation of chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy: A case report
Authors: Anaal El-khasawneh, Shaher Abdullah Mitib AL-Etan, Noon Elimam, Bayan Awni Khasawneh, Mahmoud Abdallah Harb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) is an immune-mediated disorder affecting the peripheral nerves and nerve roots. It typically presents with symmetrical weakness of both proximal and distal muscles. Although cranial nerve involvement is rare, it can occasionally occur before the typical peripheral neuropathy develops. We report an unusual case of a 37-year-old woman in whom cranial neuropathy preceded peripheral demyelinating neuropathy. She initially presented with painful vision loss caused by compressive optic neuropathy due to trigeminal nerve hypertrophy. She later developed lower limb weakness, and characteristic MRI findings of cranial nerve hypertrophy together with persistent demyelinating changes on nerve conduction studies over a 2-year follow-up confirmed the diagnosis of CIDP. This case highlights the importance of considering CIDP in patients with painful ophthalmoplegia and cranial nerve hypertrophy. Early recognition of these atypical presentations may lead to earlier diagnosis, timely treatment, and better outcomes.

Introduction

Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) is an immune-mediated disorder affecting the peripheral nervous system and nerve roots. Patients typically present with symmetrical weakness involving both proximal and distal muscles persisting for at least 8 weeks [1].

Although most cases of CIDP are idiopathic, preceding infections, particularly respiratory and gastrointestinal, have been reported in some instances, without identification of a specific causative organism. CIDP has also been associated with systemic conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus, HIV, and hepatitis B or C, suggesting a multifactorial immune-mediated process [2].

This is supported by its underlying pathophysiology, which involves both T-cell–mediated and humoral immune responses targeting peripheral nerve myelin, leading to segmental demyelination and, in some cases, secondary axonal damage [3]. Given this pathophysiological complexity, diagnosis relies on a combination of clinical features, electrodiagnostic evidence of demyelination, and supportive findings. Several diagnostic criteria have been proposed, with the European Federation of Neurological Societies and Peripheral Nerve Society (EFNS/PNS) 2021 criteria demonstrating high sensitivity 83% and specificity 94% [1]

Despite its typically peripheral presentation, cranial nerve involvement is uncommon, occurring in approximately 8%-9% of patients. When present, facial nerve palsy is the most frequent manifestation, reported in about 45% of cases, followed by involvement of the trigeminal and oculomotor nerves, which together account for approximately 32% of cases [4].

When cranial neuropathies occur early, the presentation may mimic primary orbital, inflammatory, or vascular disorders, creating significant diagnostic uncertainty. Painful visual loss with ophthalmoplegia and sensory changes in the trigeminal distribution is particularly misleading and may prompt evaluation for orbital pseudotumor, cavernous sinus pathology, or carotid-cavernous fistula [5]. Recognition of such atypical manifestations is essential to avoid delays in diagnosis and treatment.

We report a patient in whom painful unilateral visual loss and orbital signs preceded the development of demyelinating neuropathy, ultimately leading to the diagnosis of CIDP.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old woman with no significant past medical history presented to the ophthalmology department with sudden painful loss of vision in her left eye, preceded by headache, dizziness, and tinnitus in the left ear.

Ophthalmologic examination revealed a left relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD), decreased corneal sensation, limited abduction, and mild proptosis. Visual acuity in the left eye was 0.3, and fundus examination was normal, there was no optic disc swelling, optical coherence tomography (OCT) was normal, visual evoked potentials (VEP) showed normal responses, and visual field testing was within normal limits, consistent with the early compressive nature of the optic nerve involvement.

An emergent orbital magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) study suggested retro-orbital pathology, raising suspicion for orbital pseudotumor or carotid–cavernous fistula. Brain computed tomography angiography (CTA) was unremarkable, while contrast-enhanced MRI demonstrated bilateral enlargement and tortuosity of the trigeminal nerve branches (V1, V3) (Fig. 1A and C). Three days later, the patient developed progressive bilateral lower limb weakness and presented to the neurology department. She was conscious, alert, and oriented. Motor examination revealed 4/5 strength in both lower limbs with absent deep tendon reflexes.

Nerve conduction studies (NCS) demonstrated demyelinating neuropathy. Lumbar puncture showed albuminocytologic dissociation, with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) protein of 160 mg/dL and normal glucose (55 mg/dL). Normal serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) levels. Routine laboratory investigations, including electrolytes, renal and liver function tests, and serum protein levels, were normal. Vascular screening, tumor markers, and pan-computed tomography were unremarkable.

A diagnosis of acute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (AIDP) was considered, and the patient was treated with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), along with thromboprophylaxis and gastric protection according to hospital protocol.

Five days after the initial ophthalmologic presentation, repeat examination showed marked improvement, with resolution of RAPD and proptosis, normal extraocular movements, and improved visual acuity to 0.6 in the left eye; fundus examination remained normal.

At 6-month follow-up, limb strength had returned to normal, with mildly reduced visual acuity in the left eye of 0.7. However, persistent demyelinating features on NCS and trigeminal nerve abnormalities on MRI, along with a chronic course, supported a diagnosis of chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP). Treatment with corticosteroids, azathioprine, and neuropathic pain medications was initiated.

At 2-year follow-up, she remained neurologically stable with full strength 5/5 and mild residual visual impairment, with visual acuity in the left eye of 0.6. Persistent demyelinating findings on NCS and unchanged trigeminal nerve abnormalities on MRI were noted.

Discussion

Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) is an immune-mediated demyelinating neuropathy characterized by progressive or relapsing sensorimotor dysfunction lasting at least 8 weeks, supported by electrophysiologic evidence of peripheral nerve demyelination and findings such as cerebrospinal fluid albuminocytologic dissociation and nerve root or plexus enhancement on magnetic resonance imaging; in selected cases, nerve biopsy may provide additional diagnostic support [6].

In contrast to Guillain–Barré syndrome, involvement of the cranial nerves, respiratory muscles, and autonomic nervous system is less common in CIDP. Cranial nerve involvement occurs in approximately 8%-9% of cases and most frequently affects the facial nerve, followed by the trigeminal and oculomotor nerves [4]. When present, cranial neuropathies may precede limb weakness and create diagnostic uncertainty, particularly when orbital pain, ophthalmoplegia, or trigeminal sensory loss mimic orbital inflammatory or vascular conditions [5].

Neuroimaging may demonstrate hypertrophy and enhancement of cranial nerves, reflecting inflammatory demyelination and remyelination, while electrophysiologic studies typically show reduced nerve conduction velocity, conduction block, and prolonged distal latencies [7,8].

The imaging differential diagnosis of bilateral cranial nerve hypertrophy and enhancement encompasses a spectrum of inflammatory, infiltrative, infectious, and neoplastic disorders. Among neoplastic etiologies, schwannomas may present with cranial nerve enlargement but typically appear as solitary, well-circumscribed masses that displace adjacent structures without direct invasion, most commonly arising from the vestibulocochlear nerve (CN VIII). Larger lesions often demonstrate heterogeneous signal intensity due to cystic degeneration or hemorrhage [9]. Among inflammatory etiologies, neurosarcoidosis is an important imaging mimic, as it may demonstrate enlargement and enhancement of multiple cranial nerves, with or without associated leptomeningeal disease. Although the facial nerve is most commonly affected clinically, MRI most frequently demonstrates optic nerve involvement; however, the trigeminal, oculomotor, and other cranial nerves may also be involved. Additional findings, including leptomeningeal enhancement, hypothalamic–pituitary involvement, orbital inflammatory changes, or perineural spread from sinonasal disease, may support the diagnosis [10]. Lymphoma is another important imaging differential diagnosis. CNS lymphoma comprises 2 major subtypes: primary CNS lymphoma (PCNSL) and secondary CNS involvement by systemic lymphoma. Contrast-enhanced MRI is the imaging modality of choice. In secondary CNS lymphoma, leptomeningeal involvement is the predominant imaging pattern, occurring in approximately two-thirds of cases, with contrast-enhanced MRI demonstrating leptomeningeal, subependymal, dural, or cranial nerve enhancement. In contrast, PCNSL predominantly involves the brain parenchyma, characteristically presenting as solitary or multiple enhancing lesions in the periventricular or superficial cerebral regions; parenchymal involvement also accounts for approximately one-third of secondary CNS lymphoma cases [11].

In contrast, our patient demonstrated isolated bilateral trigeminal and oculomotor nerve hypertrophy and enhancement without associated leptomeningeal, hypothalamic–pituitary, orbital, or sinonasal abnormalities, making neurosarcoidosis less likely. Normal serum ACE levels further reduce the likelihood of sarcoidosis, although they do not exclude the diagnosis. Similarly, the absence of leptomeningeal enhancement and parenchymal lesions argues against CNS lymphoma, while cerebrospinal fluid findings favor an inflammatory rather than malignant process. Furthermore, the absence of orbital inflammatory changes and venous engorgement makes orbital pseudotumor and carotid–cavernous fistula unlikely.

The present case illustrates an atypical presentation in which painful unilateral visual loss with orbital signs preceded the development of demyelinating neuropathy, initially suggesting primary orbital pathology. The early clinical course was consistent with acute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (Guillain–Barré syndrome [GBS]), given the rapid onset of weakness, areflexia, demyelinating features on nerve conduction studies, and albuminocytologic dissociation. However, persistence of electrophysiologic and radiologic abnormalities beyond the expected recovery period, along with a chronic course requiring ongoing immunomodulatory therapy, supported a diagnosis of CIDP [1].

Distinguishing CIDP from GBS is essential, as CIDP typically follows a progressive or relapsing course beyond 8 weeks and requires long-term immunotherapy, whereas GBS is usually monophasic [12]. The marked clinical improvement of ocular findings following IVIG in our patient further supports an immune-mediated demyelinating process affecting cranial nerves rather than primary orbital pathology, as both conditions are known to respond to IVIG, although sustained treatment is often required in CIDP [13]. Despite marked clinical improvement, trigeminal nerve hypertrophy persisted on follow-up MRI 2 years after treatment. The significance of persistent cranial nerve enlargement in CIDP remains unclear, as current evidence supports MRI mainly as a diagnostic adjunct rather than a marker of treatment response [14]. There are no contemporary longitudinal studies specifically assessing its prognostic value after clinical recovery. Persistent enlargement likely reflects chronic structural changes from repeated demyelination and remyelination [15], which may remain despite neurological improvement. Therefore, MRI abnormalities should be interpreted cautiously and correlated with clinical and electrophysiological findings rather than used as standalone indicators of disease activity or prognosis.

Conclusion

This case highlights an unusual presentation of CIDP in which cranial nerve involvement and painful unilateral visual loss preceded limb weakness, mimicking primary orbital and vascular pathology. The diagnosis was supported by evolving clinical features, persistent demyelinating findings on electrophysiology, characteristic MRI abnormalities, and response to immunotherapy. Awareness of such atypical presentations is essential to avoid misdiagnosis, enable early treatment, and improve outcomes.
