Willingness to use fentanyl test strips among street drug users in Puerto Rico: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Liam Reyes Orlando, Héctor M Colón Jordán, Angélica M Rosario Santos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFentanyl contamination in illicit drug markets has significantly increased overdose risk in Puerto Rico and other regions. Fentanyl test strips (FTS) have emerged as a promising harm reduction tool. While several studies in the United States have examined FTS willingness and acceptability, research in Puerto Rico remains scarce. This study offers one of the first large-scale epidemiological assessments of willingness to use FTS among street-recruited drug users on the island.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of 400 street drug users recruited from syringe exchange programs in Puerto Rico between March and June 2024. Participants completed structured interviews assessing sociodemographic characteristics, substance use behaviors, overdose history, and program engagement. Willingness to use FTS was measured through a direct question and coded as a binary variable. Analyses included descriptive statistics, bivariate tests of independence, Bayes factors, logistic regression, and Bayesian Model Averaging (BMA).ResultsOverall willingness to use FTS was high (76.5%). Engagement in substance abuse treatment was strongly associated with willingness (OR = 3.09, 95% CI: 1.81–5.26, p < 0.001; Bayes Factor = 336.17). In contrast, severe substance use disorder (SUD) was negatively associated (OR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.18–0.73, p = 0.005; Bayes Factor = 1.19). Other variables such as age, overdose history, or homelessness showed inconsistent or weak evidence. BMA confirmed treatment engagement (inclusion probability = 100%) and SUD severity (95.4%) as the most robust predictors, while age and other covariates had low inclusion probabilities, suggesting weaker evidence across models.ConclusionsWillingness to use FTS is high among Puerto Rican street drug users, especially among those engaged in substance abuse treatment. Lower willingness among individuals with severe SUD aligns with patterns reported in prior research, suggesting that higher levels of dependence may limit the perceived feasibility or utility of FTS. This underscores the need for low-threshold, tailored harm reduction strategies. These findings highlight both the promise and challenges of implementing drug-checking interventions in underserved, high-risk populations. As FTS are not currently available in Puerto Rico, expanding access through harm reduction and treatment programs should be a public health priority.

Background

Substances sold in unregulated street drug markets are generally of unknown composition and concentration, creating a dangerous disconnection between the consumer and the product [1]. Individuals purchasing illicit drugs often have no way of knowing what substances they are ingesting or their potency, which significantly increases the risks associated with accidental overdose. This uncertainty is compounded by the common practice of adding adulterants—substances used to dilute or enhance the psychoactive effects of drugs—without the consumer’s knowledge or consent [2]. While some adulterants are pharmacologically inert, increasingly potent substances such as fentanyl and xylazine are being used, greatly amplifying the potential for severe and even fatal health outcomes [3–5]. The lack of quality control in these markets means that each use involves an unpredictable mixture of unknown substances, posing an ongoing and serious public health risk [6, 7].

Illicitly manufactured fentanyl has become a key driver behind the alarming increase in fatal overdoses in recent years, exacerbating the opioid crisis in various communities. Fentanyl, a synthetic opioid up to 50 times more potent than heroin, is often mixed with other drugs without users’ knowledge, drastically increasing the risk of overdose. Since 2002, the age-adjusted rate of overdose deaths in the United States involving synthetic opioids other than methadone remained relatively stable (0.4 deaths per 100,000 population in 2002) until 2013 (1.0). However, the rate has risen dramatically, reaching 22.7 deaths per 100,000 population in 2022 [8].

Puerto Rico has also been impacted by increases in fatal overdoses. In 2022 there were 2,140 opioid-related overdoses—an average of six overdoses daily [9]. Most were caused by fentanyl or other opioids. A community-based naloxone distribution program reported fewer than 10 overdoses per quarter in 2019, but by the first quarter of 2023, this number had risen to 110 [10]. Descriptive data from 2020–2022 further illustrate these dynamics: most overdoses occurred among men aged 35–44 years, in large metropolitan areas, and often in open spaces during daytime hours [11]. These findings emphasize that Puerto Rico’s epidemic mirrors some U.S. patterns but also has distinct sociodemographic and geographic features that require tailored harm reduction responses. In addition to surveillance data, qualitative research has underscored how local PWID perceive fentanyl as ubiquitous in the drug supply, often discouraging the use of drug checking [12, 13]. Importantly, Puerto Ricans outside the island also face disproportionate overdose risks. Research on the diaspora in the mainland United States found that island-born Puerto Ricans experience higher overdose mortality rates compared to other Hispanic groups [14], underscoring the broader vulnerability of this population. These dynamics unfold within a context of limited treatment and harm reduction infrastructure, as Puerto Rico has long been described as a treatment and syringe service program desert [15]. This scarcity magnifies the potential value of low-threshold interventions such as FTS, which can be feasibly integrated into community-based outreach efforts.

To mitigate overdose risk, drug checking services (DCS) have been introduced internationally, offering chemical analysis to identify fentanyl and other adulterants. These services may help people who use drugs (PWUD) make informed decisions, stimulate engagement with harm reduction services, and generate valuable public health data [6, 16, 17]. While well established in Europe, adoption in the United States has been more limited and often tied to festivals and nightlife events [18].

A variety of drug-checking methods exist, including gas chromatography, mass spectrometry, and infrared spectroscopy [19]. More recently, fentanyl test strips (FTS)—originally developed as immunoassays for urine—have been used off-label to detect fentanyl in street drug solutions [20, 21]. FTS are low-cost, rapid, and portable, making them a promising overdose prevention tool [22].

Research on the willingness and acceptability of FTS has grown substantially in recent years. Early exploratory work often focused on recreational settings such as music festivals, raves, and nightlife events, documenting high levels of interest among young, primarily white populations in Canada and Europe [23–26]. However, these findings are not fully generalizable to populations at greatest risk of overdose. Since then, a broader body of research in the United States has shifted toward high-risk groups and harm reduction contexts. Studies have documented willingness among young adults in Rhode Island [27, 28], people who inject drugs (PWID) in North Carolina and across syringe service programs in Baltimore, Boston, and Providence [21, 29], individuals receiving medications for opioid use disorder [30, 31], stimulant users [32], and rural PWUD in Kentucky [33]. Post-COVID literature further highlights both enthusiasm and limitations: while many PWID express interest in FTS, uptake may be constrained by fentanyl’s ubiquity and dependence-driven use patterns [34, 35].

These findings underscore that FTS acceptability is not limited to recreational or nightlife populations but extends across diverse harm reduction and clinical settings. Importantly, variation in willingness reflects not only population differences but also temporal shifts in the drug market. As Montero and colleagues [36] note, fentanyl has increasingly displaced heroin and is commonly combined with synthetics such as xylazine and methamphetamine, dramatically altering overdose risks. These changes, accelerated during the COVID-19 pandemic, have heightened the relevance of drug checking interventions.

Emerging evidence also suggests that individual treatment engagement and the severity of substance use disorder (SUD) may shape willingness to adopt harm reduction tools such as FTS. Studies among people receiving medications for opioid use disorder and young adults who use prescription opioids have documented higher willingness to use FTS when participants are engaged in treatment programs, highlighting the potential role of clinical settings in facilitating uptake [30, 31, 37]. Conversely, qualitative findings indicate that individuals with more severe SUD may be less likely to adopt FTS, as high levels of dependence and the perceived ubiquity of fentanyl in local markets often render drug checking less feasible or meaningful [34, 35, 38]. These insights underscore the importance of considering both treatment status and SUD severity when evaluating willingness to use FTS across diverse populations.

Despite this growing U.S. literature, Puerto Rico remains understudied, even though fentanyl has rapidly permeated the local drug supply and overdose deaths have escalated sharply in recent years [9, 10]. Qualitative work has shown that PWID on the island often perceive fentanyl as unavoidable, which may discourage FTS use [12, 13]. Epidemiological data similarly describe alarming overdose trends [11]. Puerto Ricans outside the island also face disproportionate risks, with island-born Puerto Ricans in the mainland United States experiencing higher overdose mortality than other Hispanic groups [14]. These studies situate the island’s epidemic within a broader Puerto Rican overdose crisis, both locally and in the diaspora. By providing quantitative evidence on willingness to use FTS among street-recruited PWUD in Puerto Rico, the present epidemiologic-based study addresses an important gap and contributes to a growing understanding of how harm reduction tools can be tailored to high-risk, under-resourced settings.

Methods

This study recruited 400 drug users between March and June 2024. Two syringe service programs (SSPs)—Intercambios Puerto Rico and Punto en la Montaña—were responsible for participant recruitment and interviews. Each organization recruited 200 participants: 150 opioid users and 50 non-opioid users, ensuring diversity in drug use profiles. Recruitment occurred in municipalities where these programs provide harm reduction and treatment services. Punto en la Montaña recruited in Cayey, Cidra, Aibonito, Comerio, Aguas Buenas, Guayama, and Caguas, while Intercambios Puerto Rico recruited in San Juan, Carolina, Loíza, Canóvanas, Río Grande, Luquillo, Fajardo, Ceiba, Naguabo, Humacao, Las Piedras, Juncos, Gurabo, and Yabucoa. Recruitment sites included drug copping areas, shooting galleries, and other known public hangouts, as well as SSP facilities and mobile units. To reduce participant self-selection and increase sample diversity, researchers and program staff implemented a recruitment plan that varied sites, neighborhoods, and days of the week.

Eligibility criteria required participants to be 21 years or older and to test positive for opioids, cocaine, benzodiazepines, or amphetamines on a saliva test. Trained field staff conducted face-to-face interviews using a standardized protocol, recording responses either on paper or electronically. Participants received modest monetary compensation ($25) for their time and transportation, consistent with ethical guidelines for studies with vulnerable populations. Data was reviewed for missing or inconsistent responses, and a final cleaned dataset was configured for analysis.

The dependent variable, willingness to use fentanyl test strips, was derived from survey responses. Participants were first asked whether they had ever used fentanyl or xylazine test strips. Those who reported not having used them were then asked if they would use such strips if they had access to them. For this analysis, willingness was operationalized using responses to this latter question and coded as a binary variable (Yes/No). This measure captures hypothetical willingness rather than actual prior use.

Independent variables included sociodemographic, behavioral, and treatment-related characteristics hypothesized to influence willingness to use FTS. Sociodemographic indicators included money-earning activities (formal employment or informal paid work, dichotomized yes/no) and age of first drug use (≤ 13 years, 14–18 years, ≥ 19 years). Substance use behaviors were assessed with a dichotomous indicator of recent opioid use, which included prescription and non-prescription opioids such as heroin, fentanyl, buprenorphine, and methadone. Substance use disorder (SUD) severity was measured using the DSM-5 11-symptom checklist, with participants endorsing six or more symptoms classified as meeting criteria for severe dependence. Injection practices were measured through a binary variable indicating any drug injection in the past 30 days and a categorical variable on frequency of injections per day. Based on these responses, participants were classified as not injecting, injecting three or fewer times per day, or injecting more than three times per day. Overdose history was captured by two variables: any lifetime overdose and whether the participant had witnessed overdoses, categorized as none, one to two, or three or more times. Program engagement was measured with two binary indicators: receiving help from preventive community organizations (for housing, treatment, or medical coverage) and current participation in substance abuse treatment, including detoxification, residential programs, or medication-assisted treatment with methadone or buprenorphine.

Although alcohol and crack cocaine use were measured, these variables were not included in the final models. To our knowledge, no existing literature identifies them as significant predictors of willingness to use FTS, and preliminary model testing suggested weak associations. Because this was an exploratory study, we prioritized variables with stronger empirical and theoretical support to reduce unnecessary model complexity and the risk of overfitting. Geography was not included as a covariate in multivariable models. While recruitment spanned both urban and rural municipalities, the analysis assumed homogeneity across participants. This represents an important limitation, as treatment access and drug market dynamics differ across regions of Puerto Rico.

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the sample. Bivariate tests of independence assessed associations between willingness to use FTS and the independent variables. For multivariate analysis, logistic regression was performed using a backward stepwise selection procedure to identify significant predictors. To complement frequentist inference, Bayesian methods were also employed. Bayes factors were calculated using the Bayes Factor R package [39] to evaluate evidence for or against bivariate associations. Bayes factors were interpreted following Jeffreys’ conventional thresholds, as adapted by Lee and Wagenmakers [40].

Bayesian Model Averaging (BMA) was applied to logistic regression models to address model uncertainty and identify predictors with the highest posterior probability of inclusion. The BMA approach averages across all possible models, weighing each by its posterior probability, thereby avoiding overfitting while providing robust inference [41, 42]. The analysis produced posterior mean coefficients (average effect sizes across models) and posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs), reflecting the overall evidence for each variable’s relevance. Variables with higher PIPs were considered stronger predictors of willingness to use FTS. All Bayesian analyses were conducted in R using the BMA package [41], assuming uniform priors and applying the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) for model weighting.

Table 1 presents the sociodemographic profile of the study participants. The sample consisted predominantly of adults aged 30 to 49 years (55.5%), followed by those aged 50 and above (31.8%), and a smaller proportion under 30 years of age (12.8%). Most respondents identified as male (79.5%), while 19.8% were female. Regarding education, more than half of the participants had completed high school (55.3%), 23.8% had not completed high school, and 20.8% reported having education beyond high school. Additionally, 36.3% of participants reported experiencing homelessness at some point during the previous 12 months.

As shown in Table 2, overall willingness to use fentanyl test strips was high, with 76.5% of participants indicating they were willing. When examining bivariate associations between willingness and selected participant characteristics, several significant and marginally significant findings emerged. Age was significantly associated with willingness (p = 0.005). Participants aged 30 to 49 years reported the highest willingness (80.2%), which was significantly greater than that of participants under 30 years (58.8%). Those aged 50 and older also demonstrated relatively high willingness (78.0%). Educational attainment showed a marginal association with willingness (p = 0.051), with participants having education beyond high school more likely to express willingness (86.7%) compared to those with less than a high school education (73.7%). Participants with non-severe substance use disorder (SUD) were more likely to report willingness (84.8%) than those with severe SUD (74.3%) (p = 0.038). The number of overdose events witnessed also showed a marginal association (p = 0.055), with participants who had witnessed three or more overdoses being more willing (80.3%) compared to those who had witnessed none (67.8%). Notably, active engagement in treatment was strongly associated with willingness; 85.3% of participants currently in treatment expressed willingness to use fentanyl test strips compared to 68.9% among those not in treatment (p < 0.001). In contrast, no statistically significant differences in willingness were observed by sex, homelessness in the past 12 months, informal work status, opioid use, history of overdose, or frequency of injection.

Bayesian analysis provided complementary evidence to the frequentist results by estimating the strength of evidence for the associations observed. The Bayes factor for active treatment was 336.17, indicating extreme evidence supporting a strong association with willingness to use fentanyl test strips. Conversely, although the association between SUD severity and willingness was statistically significant, the Bayes factor was 1.19, indicating only weak evidence. Other variables, such as education level (BF = 0.62) and overdose witnessing (BF = 0.44), had Bayes factors less than 1, suggesting weak evidence either supporting or refuting an association. These findings highlight the utility of Bayesian methods in providing nuanced interpretations, especially for marginal or borderline associations.

Table 3 summarizes the results from traditional logistic regression and Bayesian Model Averaging (BMA) analyses examining predictors of willingness to use fentanyl test strips, controlling for multiple covariates. Traditional logistic regression identified two key significant predictors: treatment status and severity of substance use disorder. Participants currently engaged in active treatment had over three times higher odds of willingness compared to those not in treatment (OR = 3.09; 95% CI: 1.81–5.26; p < 0.001). In contrast, participants with severe SUD had significantly lower odds of willingness compared to those with non-severe SUD (OR = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.18–0.73; p = 0.005). Age also remained relevant, with participants aged 30–49 showing significantly increased odds (OR = 2.59; 95% CI: 1.31–5.13; p = 0.006) and those aged 50 and older demonstrating borderline significance (OR = 2.21; 95% CI: 0.99–4.37; p = 0.052). No other covariates showed statistically significant associations in the multivariable model.

The BMA analysis reinforced the importance of active treatment and SUD as the most robust predictors. Active treatment had an inclusion probability of 100%, indicating it was present in all top models, with a positive model coefficient of 1.14, consistent with increased willingness to participate. Similarly, SUD had a high inclusion probability (95.4%) with a negative coefficient (-1.04), reflecting reduced willingness among those with severe SUD. Age, although significant in traditional logistic regression, had a low inclusion probability (6.8%) and was not included in the most probable Bayesian model, suggesting less consistent evidence for its predictive role. Other variables, such as informal help (12.9%), homelessness (11.6%), overdose witnessing (6.3%), and informal work (2.2%), appeared in some Bayesian models but with lower inclusion probabilities, indicating potential but less consistent contributions to explaining willingness. These findings illustrate the added value of Bayesian model averaging in identifying variable importance while accounting for model uncertainty and possible alternative explanatory patterns.

Discussion

This study examined willingness to use fentanyl test strips to check the contents of drugs purchased in illegal street drug markets in Puerto Rico. While much of the earlier literature on FTS focused on recreational drug users in nightlife settings such as parties, raves, or music festivals, our study contributes a large-scale quantitative assessment among PWID and individuals who use street-acquired substances—groups at particularly high risk of fentanyl-related overdose. By focusing on this underserved and marginalized population, the study provides a more grounded understanding of how FTS might be accepted and implemented in real-world, high-risk contexts. A large majority of participants in our study (76.5%) reported willingness to use FTS, indicating strong acceptability of this harm reduction strategy among PWUD in Puerto Rico. Results from multivariable logistic regression and Bayesian Model Averaging (BMA) identified active treatment and severity of substance use disorder (SUD) as key predictors of willingness, with active treatment emerging as the strongest and most consistent factor across models.

Our findings align with prior research in the mainland United States, where willingness to use FTS among PWUD has ranged from 63 to 85%, depending on population characteristics and outreach setting [21, 29]. Moreover, they surpass those reported by Kennedy et al. [43] in a Canadian setting, where only 43% of PWID expressed willingness to use drug checking services. The higher willingness observed in Puerto Rico may reflect growing concerns about fentanyl contamination and the limited availability of institutional harm reduction tools. Taken together, this suggests that drug-checking services, such as FTS distribution, may be well received in high-risk communities, even in settings without supervised injection sites.

At the same time, our findings contrast with those reported by Abadie et al. [12, 13], who found that many PWID in Puerto Rico assume fentanyl is ubiquitous in the drug supply and therefore avoid using FTS. Rather than representing a contradiction, this contrast reflects a difference in methodological scope: Abadie’s ethnographic work documents actual use practices and barriers, while our survey measures hypothetical willingness. This distinction underscores a critical gap between potential uptake and real-world implementation, suggesting that expressed willingness may not always translate into consistent FTS use once practical barriers are considered. Similar concerns have been raised in post-COVID research in the mainland United States. For example, Zibbell et al. [34, 35] found that even when PWID express willingness, structural realities—including fentanyl’s ubiquity and high levels of physical dependence—can render FTS use impractical or irrelevant in daily practice.

A strong and statistically significant association was observed between engagement in substance abuse treatment and willingness to use FTS. Participants currently involved in treatment programs (e.g., detoxification, residential programs, or medication-assisted treatment) were over three times more likely to report willingness compared to those not in treatment (OR = 3.09, 95% CI: 1.81–5.26, p < 0.001). This association was also identified by the BMA analysis, which gave substance abuse treatment a posterior inclusion probability of 100%. These results suggest that engagement with treatment services reflects a broader orientation toward risk reduction and behavioral change. Individuals in treatment may be more likely to acknowledge the dangers of fentanyl contamination and adopt strategies that reduce overdose risk, even if they have not stopped using entirely. These findings highlight the potential of integrating FTS into treatment and community-based programs as a low-barrier harm reduction strategy.

By contrast, severe SUD was negatively associated with willingness to use FTS. Participants classified with severe SUD had significantly lower odds of willingness (OR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.18–0.73, p = 0.005), a result supported by the BMA analysis with an inclusion probability of 95.4%. This suggests that individuals with more chaotic or intense patterns of substance use may be less receptive to adopting harm reduction tools. High levels of dependence may impair planning and risk management capacities [44, 45], while stigma and marginalization may further limit access to services [46]. Tailored interventions, including peer-led outreach and street-based distribution, may be essential for increasing uptake of FTS among this subgroup.

Several prior studies have identified key factors associated with willingness to use FTS, which help contextualize and support our findings. Research on Rhode Island found that individuals who were older, experienced homelessness, had injections, used heroin, or had witnessed an overdose were more likely to use FTS [27, 28]. Similarly, participants in medication-assisted treatment programs demonstrated high uptake and acceptability of FTS up to 85%, highlighting the role of active engagement in structured care [47]. Other studies have shown that positive FTS result often stimulated risk-reducing behavior, such as injecting more slowly or using less of the substance [48, 49]. These findings mirror ours: active treatment engagement likely aligns with increased risk awareness and openness to harm reduction tools, while severe SUD reflecting chaotic use patterns, may impede engagement despite high risk. Together, this body of evidence reinforces our conclusion that access to treatment and SUD severity are salient, pragmatic predictors of FTS willingness, and underscores the need for harm reduction strategies that are both accessible and tailored to individuals with higher levels of disorder severity.

Experiences with overdose did not significantly predict willingness. Participants with a history of overdose showed slightly higher willingness (80.0%) compared to those without (74.1%), but this was not statistically significant (p = 0.167). Similarly, witnessing an overdose showed only a marginal association (p = 0.055), though participants who had witnessed three or more overdoses reported greater willingness (80.3%) than those who had witnessed none (67.8%). These findings are consistent with prior research showing mixed associations between overdose experience and willingness to use FTS [27, 28, 50]. Although not predictive in this study, these groups remain important targets for harm reduction given their elevated risk of future overdose.

Another important contextual factor is the practice of Caballo, documented by Abadie et al. [13], in which small doses of drugs are purchased and shared among partners rather than in larger amounts. When combined with high-frequency injections, this practice presents structural challenges for consistent FTS use. For instance, PWID injected multiple times a day would theoretically require numerous strips daily, which is unlikely to be sustainable. Moreover, if fentanyl is detected repeatedly, individuals may continue using regardless due to economic constraints and immediate dependence needs. Our study did not evaluate these barriers directly,rather, it assessed hypothetical willingness. Nevertheless, acknowledging the Caballo practice highlights the gap between willingness and feasibility, underscoring the need for future implementation studies to address economic and structural limitations.

This study has several limitations. It relied on cross-sectional survey data, limiting causal inference and capturing willingness at a single point in time. Responses were self-reported and may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. While geographically diverse, the sample may not represent all PWUD in Puerto Rico, particularly those disconnected from harm reduction or treatment services. Most importantly, the dependent variable measured hypothetical willingness rather than observed FTS use, and the study did not explore barriers to access or use. These limitations reinforce the need for mixed-methods research combining epidemiological and ethnographic approaches.

Despite these limitations, the study has important implications for public health policy. The high willingness to use FTS, particularly among individuals in treatment, suggests treatment programs may be strategic points of distribution. The lower willingness among those with severe SUD underscores the importance of low-threshold, peer-driven outreach. In Puerto Rico, where overdose deaths are rising and fentanyl is pervasive, introducing FTS into community harm reduction strategies could provide a valuable additional tool for prevention. The Puerto Rican diaspora has also shown elevated overdose risks compared to other Hispanic groups in the mainland United States [14], reinforcing the urgency of developing culturally and contextually appropriate harm reduction approaches. Future research should evaluate real-world implementation, including how practices such as Caballo influence uptake, and how distribution models can be tailored to the structural realities of PWUD on the island.
