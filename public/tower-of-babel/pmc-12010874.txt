Adoption of a novel medium for the industrial (3000 L) production of Serendipita indica employing a nutrient limitation strategy using insoluble carbon and phosphate sources
Authors: Jubair Al Rashid, Md Abuhena, Md Dilshad Karim, Lutfur Rahman, Jingjing Wang, Zhiyong Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract The use of the endophytic fungus Serendipita indica has rapidly increased due to its wide range of host species, ability to foster plant-growth, and ability to confer tolerance to a number of stresses. However, its industrial-scale production is still in its infancy due to its low-biomass yield and prolonged cultivation time. Thus far, Hill–Kafer medium has traditionally been used for S. indica cultivation, resulting in lower yields and excessively long incubation times. Here, we adopted a simple insoluble carbon and phosphate input medium for rapidly generating high biomass. We developed and optimized the SIF1 medium, achieving maximum biomass production (424.5 ± 1.9 g/L), significantly outperforming Hill–Kafer medium. Statistical optimization of SIF1 identified optimal levels (15 g/L oats, 7.5 g/L tricalcium phosphate, 95-hr incubation). Validated results in the laboratory (FUS-10 L: 484.4 ± 4.7), pilot (300 L: 496.5 ± 7 g/L), and industrial (3000L: 492.4 ± 7.1 g/L) bioreactors proved the efficacy of SIF1. Compared to Hill–Kafer (54.8 ± 3.7 g/L), SIF1 showed nine-fold higher biomass productivity and reduced cultivation time by approximately 6 days. Based on our findings, it appears that SF1 will be a highly efficient medium for producing S. indica on an industrial scale and expanding its use.One-Sentence SummaryThis study presents a rapid industrial production strategy for the beneficial fungus Serendipita indica, providing a scalable solution for wider applications and contributing to global food security and environmental sustainability.

Introduction

Together with proper utilization of environmental resources, both nutrition and food safety are essential requirements for people worldwide. Particularly, food insecurity, along with heavy metal toxicity, is one of the most serious threats to the existence of millions of entities living in underdeveloped countries. To meet such huge food requirements, adverse health effects need to be considered, while utilizing various agrochemicals to produce these foods. The focus on producing high-value crops and practicing green farming has changed the use of agrochemical inputs in recent years. Microbial inoculants are being used as an alternative to agrochemicals in an effort to improve plant growth, yields, and nutritional value in an affordable and environmentally safe manner. However, the most major impediments to widespread adoption of microbial inoculants in developing countries are their high manufacturing costs, limited accessibility, and a paucity of suitable microbial inoculants with a broader crop range.

To address the health concerns and undesirable environmental effects of agrochemical use, microbial inoculants like Serendipita indica offer an effective alternative that promotes green farming while reducing adverse ecological consequences. Serendipita indica has been the topic of substantial research for several years and is now recognized as a model fungus with numerous desirable characteristics, including most of the benefits of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF). Like comparable AM fungi, it can infect a wide range of plant species and benefit them in terms of yield, biomass, and growth (Singh et al., 2000). The most significant benefit of S. indica over AMF is its ability to be produced in the laboratory on artificial media without the help of a host (Varma et al., 2012). Similar to other microbial inoculants, it promotes plant growth and output (Varma et al., 1999; Waller et al., 2005; Gill et al., 2016), upsurges drought tolerance in plants, slows or impedes leaf wilting, prolongs the ageing of callus tissue, protects plants from pathogen attacks, and improves plant phosphate uptake (Waller et al., 2005; Zuccaro et al., 2011). Surprisingly, S. indica alleviates the stress on plants driven by water loss, pH imbalance, and heavy metal toxicity (Kumari et al., 2004). Furthermore, the fungus provides enduring resistance to disease by elevating the level of antioxidants such ascorbate and glutathione in the host to combat oxidative stress triggered by infections (Waller et al., 2005; Vadassery et al., 2009).

Another beneficial part of the fungus is its ability to form chlamydospores, which have a longer shelf life in severe conditions (Wall & Lewis, 1980; Lewis & Papavizas, 1983). In culture media, S. indica mycelia easily transform into spores that can thrive in adverse environments and restart the vegetative growth when conditions are favourable. With all of these features, spores of S. indica provide a potential alternative from an application viewpoint. As a result, spore-forming microorganisms are currently the most effective microbial inoculant for stimulating plant growth while preventing phytopathogen spread (Casula & Cutting, 2002).

The commercial production of such microbial inoculants is facing serious challenges because of a scarcity of supplies of raw ingredients and an optimized industrial manufacturing process using low-cost, high biomass-producing media. The physiochemical and nutritional variables are influenced by the fermentation medium, which has a direct impact on process costs and efficiency (Zhang & Greasham, 1999a; Abuhena et al., 2022a). Until recently, several researchers worked on developing a novel medium for increasing S. indica biomass production. However, no suitable medium has not yet been developed that can increase biomass production, shorten culture time, and be used commercially at a low cost (Pham et al., 2008; Kumar et al., 2011, 2012). Until far, the most often used medium has been the Hill–Kafer complex medium (Hill & Kafer, 2001; Kumar et al., 2011). Notably, multiple efforts have been done for the Hill–Kafer medium to boost S. indica biomass production by adding various carbohydrate, nitrogen, and phosphorus sources (Pham et al., 2008; Kumar et al., 2011). Nonetheless, these efforts were only partially successful, while the changes increased complexity significantly (Pham et al., 2008; Kumar et al., 2011).

AM-like fungi possess diverse physiological attributes, making their evolution exceedingly sensitive to the composition of their growth medium, especially carbon sources. Fungal cells notably prefer glucose as their primary carbon source (Boldt et al., 2011). Furthermore, sugars serve a crucial part in the biochemical interactions among plants and fungi, directing root growth and S. indica infection dynamics. Therefore, providing an appropriate carbon source is crucial. Research has shown that different carbon sources impact the biomass production of S. indica (Kumar et al., 2011). Additionally, studies have identified 15 potential hexose transporter genes in S. indica, with the PiHXT5 (high affinity transporter) being controlled in symbiosis and responding differently toward varying glucose levels (Rani et al., 2016). This underscores the importance of carbon availability in the development of hyphae and the production of biomass in S. indica. Earlier research also highlighted that S. indica generates large levels of acid phosphatases, aiding plants in accessing various forms of phosphate in the soil (Singh et al., 2000; Kang et al., 2002; Henri et al., 2008).

Consequently, there is a need to formulate a growth medium that is simple, cost-effective, and composed of readily available ingredients to achieve high biomass yields for S. indica. Therefore, statistical methods such as response surface methodology that embraces the Box–Behnken design (BBD) enable the optimization of multiple influencing parameters simultaneously (Abuhena et al., 2022a). Recent studies have highlighted statistically optimized process designs for microbial production of enzymes and metabolites using these methodologies (Abuhena et al., 2022a; 2022b). However, despite these advancements, industrial-scale production of S. indica remains largely unexplored.

Hence, the goal of the current research is to explore the nutritional requirements for promoting of S. indica’s growth in order to produce it on a large scale through industrial fermentation. Insoluble carbon and insoluble phosphate sources were used as limiting nutrients to induce high biomass production of S. indica. Finally, statistical methodology was used to analyse the interaction of the essential components and find their optimal values in order to optimize production.

Materials and Methods

Fungal Strain and Inoculum Preparation for Bioreactors

The strain of S. indica (ATCC® 204458TM) used in this investigation was obtained through Apex Biotechnology Laboratory from University Boulevard (Manassas, VA, USA). Hill–Kafer medium was used to maintain the stock culture through inoculation on an agar slant cultivated for 10 days at 30°C ± 2°C and then stored at 4°C (Prasad et al., 2005). To prepare preculture and seed culture, S. indica were first cultured in Hill–Kafer medium on a petri dish at 30°C ± 2°C for 10 days. The revived culture was then placed in the liquid medium (potato dextrose broth) by punching out 8 mm of agar discs with a corkborer and incubated for 7 days at 30°C ± 2°C at 250 rpm on a rotary shaker (TOPT-111B, Biobase, China) (Kabir et al., 2023).

Bioreactors

This study utilized three types of bioreactors across different scales. Initially, laboratory-scale bioreactors (FUS-10 L) were employed for SF1-SF5 and BBD trials. Subsequently, an industrial-scale bioreactor (V2:3000 L) and a pilot-scale bioreactor (V1:300 L), purchased from Wenzhou Daysly Technology Co. Ltd., Zhejiang, China, were used for further investigations. The experiments were done at the fermentation floor of Apex Biofertilizers and Biopesticides Limited equipped with essential utilities, including an industrial chiller (25T, KL-LSF30S), an industrial compressor (ZT37, 8.6–50 HZ, 5.5 m3/min, Atlas Copco), an industrial boiler (ST 1300EF), among others. The process was automated with a PLC system (MCGS), regulating variables such as pH, temperature, and dissolved oxygen with integrated sensors (Abuhena et al., 2022a).

Growth Characterization of S. Indica

To characterize the culture with various P and C sources, each formed ball was visually assessed for its size, texture, colour, and shape. The growth of S. indica was quantified in terms of fresh biomass per liter of culture broth. After filtering a specific amount of culture broth via a cloth filter, the biomass was determined. Additionally, fresh biomass was dried for removing all moisture in a vacuum oven at 50°C to determine the dry cell weight (DCW) per liter of culture broth. Five replicates were used to determine fresh biomass and, eventually, DCW. For SF1, the DCW conversion from 1 kg of fresh biomass is 93 ± 1.2 g/L, while for Hill–Kafer, it is 79.1 ± 1.5 g/L.

Growth of S. Indica in Different C and P Sources

It is evident that a range of synthetic media have the potential to support the growth of the AM-like fungus S. indica. According to various investigators (Varma et al., 1999; Pham et al., 2008), the Hill & Kafer (2001) medium has shown a long incubation time and a low growth rate. However, so as to produce high biomass at a maximum rate, S. indica must require perfect growing conditions and nutritional requirements. Previous research revealed that soluble carbon sources, which include sucrose, glucose, jaggery, and glycerol, were not favourable to boosting the growth of S. indica (Kumar et al., 2011; Singhal et al., 2017; Attri & Verma, 2018; Osman et al., 2020). Researchers found that the medium’s supply of glucose was consumed when the exponential phase of growth came to an end (Attri & Verma, 2018). As an outcome of absorbing glucose, the fermentation broth's pH dropped. The study aimed to reduce cultivation time and prevent catabolic repression of soluble carbon and phosphate by evaluating the impact of different carbon and phosphate sources on biomass production. Therefore, it would be sensible to choose carbon sources that can meet the microbes’ needs for carbon without limiting growth or being readily consumed. Nevertheless, S. indica can also solubilize P from insoluble sources and encourage the accumulation of the plant biomass (Swetha & Padmavathi, 2016). However, some investigations have demonstrated that excessive P can limit S. indica growth. At this point, P sources are also a crucial factor to consider while maximizing S. indica growth.

Growth of S. indica with C sources

In this instance, it is crucial to evaluate the carbon sources, which include the insoluble carbon that S. indica needs to break down in order to use it. Hence, S. indica was grown on media enriched with the following C-sources: maize powder, soymeal, oats (OT), and wheat powder in order to evaluate its potential to utilize carbon from insoluble sources and accelerate growth. potato dextrose broth (PDB) was used as the control, and for the other media, we replaced dextrose of PDB with the relevant carbon sources. Each medium had potato infusion 200 g/L and carbon source 20 g/L. The experiments were labelled as MC1 (Maize), MC2 (Soymeal), MC3 (Oats), MC4 (Wheat), and MC (PDB). The following was collected: soybean meal from Loba Chemie, India; oats (QUAKER) from a nearby market; and maize and wheat from the Bangladesh Agricultural Development Corporation centre in Rajshahi, Bangladesh. Prior to use, all items were washed, dried, and kept in a dry area of the lab. Furthermore, a grain flour mill (6FT-28, Kingrunda) was used to grind wheat, rice, maize, and oats to produce 0.08 mesh flour. In these investigations, we utilized 150 mL of medium in 500 mL flasks, with five replications. The medium with a pH of 6.5 was sterilized for 20 min at 15 psi. The medium was then inoculated with 8 mm agar discs and cultured at 30°C ± 2°C for 7 days. The total growth for each treatment was measured on a single day.

Growth of S. indica with P sources

During this phase, S. indica was cultivated on the media containing the following P-sources: Fe–P, Al–P, Ca3(PO4)2, (TCP), and KH2PO4 (KP). This was done to evaluate S. indica’s capacity for P utilization and accelerate growth in submerged culture. To find microbes that have the capability to solubilize phosphate, Pikovskaya (PK) is a frequently utilized medium. The PK medium that we utilized in this instance was modified by adding oats (13 g/L) in place of glucose and eliminating yeast extract. However, the phosphate (3 g/L) sources varied, and each medium contained the same amounts (g/L) of (NH4)2SO4 1.0, KCl 0.2 g, MgSO4.7H2O, MnSO4.H2O 0.001, 0.1NaCl 0.2, and FeSO4.7H2O 0.001. The experiments were designated as MP1 (KP), MP2 (TCP), MP3 (Al-P), and MP4 (Fe-P). All chemicals have been obtained from Merck, India. In these experiments, we employed media quantities of 150 mL in 500 mL flasks with five replications. For 20 min, all of the medium with a pH of 6.5 were autoclaved at 15 psi. After that, the media were inoculated with 8 mm agar discs and cultured at 30°C ± 2°C for 7 days. The growth of the S. indica was measured as fresh biomass. For every treatment, the ultimate total growth was measured on a single day.

Culture Media Adoption for the Growth of S. Indica in Submerged Bioreactors

On a lab scale, PDB, Hill–Kafer, and PK media were utilized for axenic culture; however, large-scale manufacturing required an alternate medium. The goal was to develop a rapid-growing medium that produced a greater quantity of biomass. To compete economically, bioinoculant manufacturers must produce a high biological output in a short cultivation time. To assess the maximum S. indica production in liquid media, Hill–Kafer was set as control. Initially, S. indica was grown on 5 different, specially designed production media, each of which contained the same amounts (g/L) of (NH4)2SO4 1.0, KCl 0.2 g, MgSO4.7H2O, MnSO4.H2O 0.001, 0.1NaCl 0.2, and FeSO4.7H2O 0.001. However, the carbon as well as phosphate sources varied: SIF1 medium contained (g/L) oats powder 13.0, and Ca3(PO4)2 5.0; SIF2 medium contained (g/L) oat powder 13.0, Ca3(PO4)2 2.5 and KH2PO4 2.5; SIF3 medium contained (g/L) oat powder 6.5, soymeal 6.5 and Ca3(PO4)2 5.0; SIF4 medium contained (g/L) oat powder 6.5, maize powder 6.5 and Ca3(PO4)2 5.0; SIF5 medium contained (g/L) oat powder 6.5, wheat powder 6.5, and Ca3(PO4)2 5.0. The media was evaluated in a laboratory-scale (10 L) fermenter, with five replications. The media was inoculated with 2% of newly produced inoculum and incubated at 30°C ± 2°C and 180 rpm. After 24 hr, samples were taken and analysed. After 7 days, all media were removed, and biomass production was assessed.

Validation of Outcomes From BBD Optimization

To verify the predicted model data, laboratory bioreactors were first operated under the expected ideal conditions, using Hill–Kafer as the control. Once the settings were confirmed, they were applied to both 300 L pilot and 3000 L industrial bioreactors. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate to ensure accuracy and reliability of the results.

Statistical Tools

Software Design Expert 13 was used to do BBD analysis. A Tukey’s HSD (Honestly Significant Difference)test was run using an ANOVA (Analysis of Variance)in R to look at significance variation at the P < 0.05 level.

Results and Discussion

Growth of S. Indica in Different C and P Sources

Serendipita indica grows both intercellularly and intracellularly, forming spiral structures akin to the arbuscules of AMF. This creates a symbiotic interface among the plant’s cell wall and the fungus’ hyphae, making S. indica an excellent model for studying symbiosis (Gill et al., 2016; Pérez-Alonso et al., 2020). Furthermore, at this interface, some transporters facilitate both the absorption of nutrients and the reciprocal exchange of substances between the symbiotic participants, mainly involving inorganic phosphate and carbohydrates (Gill et al., 2016; Pérez-Alonso et al., 2020). An instance of this is when the host provides carbon to the fungus, while the fungus provides P to the host plant. The fact that PiHXT5 (high-affinity hexose transporter) in S. indica is controlled upon symbiosis and interacts in different ways with diverse levels of carbon. It implies that S. indica’s hyphal expansion and functional properties are carbon-dependent (Lahrmann et al., 2013; Dias et al., 2020). High levels of carbon (sucrose) and phosphate can negatively impact the development of the fungus and also symbiosis. Additionally, the growth patterns of biotrophic roots indicate that gene expression related to phosphate and carbon utilization is influenced by the availability of these nutrients (Lahrmann et al., 2013).

Growth of S. indica with C sources

The experiment’s outcomes demonstrated that S. indica can utilize carbon sources found in oats, maize, wheat, and soymeal (Fig. 1a). For degrading insoluble carbon, basidiomycetes employ a suite of hydrolytic enzymes, typically including endoglucanase, cellobiohydrolase, and beta-glucosidase (Dias et al., 2020). The genomic data of S. indica revealed that all 121 proteins contain one or more carbohydrate-binding motifs, such as WSC, CBM1, or LysM. The cellulose-binding CBM1 domain is nearly entirely present in fungal enzymes that break down plant cell walls (Zuccaro et al., 2011). It has been reported that extracellular enzymes engaged in carbon metabolism increase activity when carbon supply is limited, while enzymes related to the nitrogen and phosphorus cycles increase activity when carbon is available (Zuccaro et al., 2011; Gill et al., 2016; Dias et al., 2020). Among the four carbon sources used to cultivate S. indica in submerged culture, oats (MC3) yielded the highest amount of fresh biomass (184.2 ± 1.9 g/L). Maize (MC1) and wheat (MC4) produced the second-highest biomass of 119.7 ± 1.8 (g/L) and 116.2 ± 2.3 (g/L), respectively, which were nearly the same, while MC2 (Soymeal) resulted in the lowest biomass (92.7 ± 2.5 g/L). The results of the growth media study using different carbon sources are shown in Fig. 1a.

Growth of S. indica with P sources

Phosphate utilization test by S. indica in the modified PK's broth with four phosphate sources, KP, TCP, FeP, and AlP, yielded positive results with varying biomass amount (Fig. 1b). Malla et al. (2004) reported that S. indica can solubilize P from insoluble phosphate sources such as RP (Rock Phosphate) and TCP, along with KP (Ngwene et al., 2016). According to earlier research on P solubilization, S. indica is able to synthesize acid phosphatases that are needed to solubilize ZnP, TCP, AlP, and RP. As a result, the host plant would have access to complex or insoluble phosphate stores in the soil (Singh et al., 2000; Kang et al., 2002; Henri et al., 2008; Ngwene et al., 2016). The study found that TCP (307.8 ± 1.3 g/L) significantly boosted S. indica growth compared to other P sources (Fig. 1b). In Al–P (111.4 ± 2.2 g/L) and Fe–P (119.2 ± 1.7 g/L), the least amount of fungal growth was observed. Furthermore, compared to KP (136.1 ± 2.1 g/L), S. indica produced a greater amount of fungal biomass in TCP, including much greater quantities of chlamydospore. This finding implies that S. indica grows more quickly at the terminals of its hyphae when there is a limited P supply, exhibiting an exploratory growth behaviour. For instance, filamentous fungi have been reported to employ this fungal expansion strategy in response to reduced supply of nutrients (Ritz 1995; Ritz & Crawford, 1999). Conversely, the exploration speed decreases when the fungus thrives in an adequate or high nutritional condition (Falconer et al., 2005; Van Bruggen et al., 2000).

Culture Media Adoption for the Growth of S. Indica in Submerged Bioreactors

In submerged laboratory bioreactors, SF1 produced the highest amount of biomass (424.5 ± 1.9 g/L) among the five different media used for S. indica cultivation. SF2 had the second-highest yield at 368.9 ± 2.2 g/L; however, it was much lower than SF1, showing a significant yield difference. However, SF3, SF4, and SF5 produced nearly the same biomass ranges of 330–343 g/L, while Hill–Kafer yielded the least biomass (162.4 ± 2.4 g/L). Figure 2 illustrates the outcomes of the growth media evaluation. In this particular instance, S. indica also exploited distinct carbon and P sources to grow since the fungus has the potential to infect and improve the biomass of plant species under varying nutritional conditions (Singh et al., 2000). Based on metabolic pathway analysis, glucose is often the preferred carbon source. However, the media can be optimized by testing other sugars to see if they offer metabolic advantages. Implementing controlled release systems for carbon and P sources can prevent catabolite repression and ensure continuous growth for S. indica. A mix of organic and inorganic nutrient sources can be provided based on the organism’s assimilation capabilities. Subjecting S. indica to gradual changes in media composition can lead to adaptive growth, where the fungus develops improved growth characteristics. Using high-throughput methods to screen various media compositions can identify optimal conditions for growth.

Validation of BBD optimized results

The design-expert software’s point-predicting algorithm was used to model the independent factors’ optimal levels. At optimal oat concentrations of 15 g/L, TCP of 7.5 g/L, and incubation duration of 95 hr in SF1, the highest possible biomass of 483.7 g/L was anticipated. Operating the laboratory-scale bioreactors (FUS-10 L) at the predicted conditions resulted in an average biomass production of 484.4 ± 4.7 g/L in SF1, whereas Hill–Kafer produced 54.8 ± 3.6 g/L (Fig. 3d). In contrast, the pilot bioreactor (300 L) and industrial bioreactor (3000 L) produced an average biomass production of 496.5 ± 7 g/L and 492.4 ± 7.1 g/L, respectively. This outcome also shows a robust association between the lab-to-industrial scale scale-up of optimized variables.

Discussion

Scaling up AM like fungus S. indica manufacturing to an industrial level poses numerous challenges, encompassing simple nutrients medium, high biomass production, less incubation time, formulation, and infectivity assessment. The pivotal steps towards the broader application primarily hinge on industrial-scale production. Successful large-scale manufacturing necessitates the identification of crucial cultural components, which allows for the economically viable creation of top-notch goods with superior performance. The choice of appropriate nutrient sources, such as carbon and phosphorus, has a considerable impact on the uniqueness and sustainability of endophytic S. indica large-scale cultivation. The approach outlined in this study was meticulously designed to meet the requirements for industrial-scale production, emphasizing high biomass, less incubation, and batch uniformity.

While several research efforts have delved into S. indica production across various platforms, from the conical flask to lab-scale fermenter, aiming to generate substantial volumes of S. indica, none have effectively optimized production parameters to consistently attain high biomass of S. indica. Most methods available today yield a limited number of biomasses, rendering them unsuitable for efficient and swift upscale production. However, Osman et al. (2020) employed a vegetable juice-based medium to enhance production, enhancing accessibility for plant use. Kumar et al. (2011) explored the mass production using soymeal in a 14-L bioreactor with a less success of biomass enhancement. Likewise, Attri & Verma (2018) endeavoured to enhance biomass production by employing several sources of jaggery but could not achieve the desired results. Although a number of alternative media have been developed up to this point for the cultivation of S. indica, their unsuitability of scale-up and expensive preparation requirements have led to less successful yields. However, in this case, Hill–Kafer medium, which has more than 20 different ingredients, can be considered, as S. indica often takes longer than 10 days after inoculation to cover a Petri plate with hyphae. The Hill–Kafer medium has been the target of numerous attempts to increase S. indica biomass output with the addition of different sources of carbohydrates, nitrogen, and phosphorus (Pham et al., 2008; Kumar et al., 2011; Arora et al., 2014). However, while the modifications greatly increased cost, these efforts were merely succeeded (Pham et al., 2008; Kumar et al., 2011).

The physiological behaviours of microorganisms are diverse, and as such, the components of the media, especially carbon sources, have a major effect on their growth. The biochemical dynamics of the plant–fungal relationship can be influenced by sugars at this interface. Given the impact of sugar on root growth and S. indica infection, providing a proper carbon source is of paramount importance. Carbon sources have been observed to affect the growth of S. indica, as reported by Kumar et al. (2011). In this study, oats, wheat, maize, and potatoes were chosen as carbon sources as they have been used to grow several microbes, including Trichoderma (Abuhena et al., 2022b). On the other hand, TCP, KP, AlP, and FeP were selected as phosphate sources since they have been utilized in the production of microbes by some researchers (Ngwene et al., 2016; Swetha and Padmavathi, 2016). In this work, our goal was to develop a medium that would be simple to make, using only a few easily obtained low-cost materials, and would yield huge quantities of biomass for the fungus S. indica.

After conducting a number of tests, we found that the oats and TCP medium outperform the conventional PDB and complex media in terms of the rate at which hyphal development and the growth of fungal biomass in liquid culture occur. The rapid growth rate of S. indica that was found was also fluctuating based on the carbon sources that were employed. Several carbon sources (oats, wheat, soymeal, potato infusion, and maize) produced findings that were comparable. Similar results were obtained using different P sources (KP, FeP, AlP, and TCP), implying that the reported fast growth rate of S. indica changed depending on the P sources used. In this study, we have adopted a medium utilizing commercially accessible oats and insoluble TCP as the main ingredients. After statistical (BBD) optimization, optimized media enabled the enhancement of biomass production to 484 g/L in laboratory and 496 g/L in pilot bioreactor, which was much higher than any other S. indica production media until now (Fig. 4). In Hill–Kafer medium, Pham et al. (2008) recorded outputs that were significantly lower than ours, ranging from 36.7 to 23.9 g/L of fresh weight. Attri & Verma (2018) investigated biomass production utilizing several jaggery sources and obtained 16.83 g/L after 9 days. A study by Kumar et al. (2011) on increasing the biomass yield of S. indica in a 14-L bioreactor by utilizing a modified Kaefer medium produced 0.18 g/L of biomass per hour. This is much less than our results using a 3000 L bioreactor, which yielded 492 g/L after 95 hr of culture. Recently, Osman et al. (2020) used a vegetable juice-based medium to enhance production, attaining 21 g/L biomass. In comparison, after only 4 days of incubation, S. indica’s biomass output increased manyfold in the SF1 medium. According to the study’s outcomes, the strategy of mass production is shaped by relatively inexpensive medium formulation, process optimization, and scaling up. An excellent measure of a medium’s economic feasibility is its ability to produce large amounts of biomass quickly and efficiently on an industrial scale.

SF1 (oats and TCP-based) medium resulted in higher biomass production of S. indica in submerged culture, circumventing the growth constraint through readily soluble sugar (sucrose) and phosphate (KH2PO4) sources.

In light of this, our current work demonstrates that mass production of S. indica is feasible using the SF1 medium. The SF1 medium is both environmentally friendly and cost-effective. By avoiding complex, synthetic nutrients, it reduces the environmental footprint associated with production and disposal (Zhang & Greasham, 1999a; Abuhena et al., 2022a). The insoluble nature of TCP ensures slow phosphate release, minimizing nutrient runoff and ecosystem harm (Ngwene et al., 2016). Both oats and TCP are biodegradable, breaking down naturally without harmful residues, making the medium eco-friendly. Additionally, SF1 supports green farming by enabling higher biomass production of S. indica, offering a sustainable alternative to chemical fertilizers and synthetic media, and aligning with the goal of reducing agricultural environmental impact (Kumar et al., 2011).

Conclusion

The novel SF1 medium produced significantly greater fungal biomass than traditional complex media for growing S. indica. We expect that this medium will be the most productive for S. indica when producing on an industrial scale because it performed well in both the pilot and industrial bioreactors. Furthermore, this medium will be preferred since it contains simple nutrients, particularly TCP and OT, which promote the fast growth of fungi. Since TCP and OT have limitations on the availability of P and C, the number of fungal spores generated was incredibly high. In addition to its scientific utility, the low cost of the ingredients suggests that it could form the basis for economically successful large-scale fungal inoculum production.
