Efficacy of zinc oxide nanoparticles in chemical castration of male Wistar rats
Authors: Mehdi Kian, Ali Soleimanzadeh, Shahram Javadi, Gholamreza Najafi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the dose-dependent effects of intratesticular zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) on reproductive toxicity in male Wistar rats, evaluating their potential as a chemical castration agent. Fifty rats, weighing 250–300 g and aged 12–14 weeks, were divided into five groups: control, sham (normal saline), and ZnO NPs at 5 mg/mL, 10 mg/mL, and 25 mg/mL. ZnO NPs (< 100 nm) were dissolved in saline and injected into the caudal testis. After 60 days, assessments included testicular oxidative stress markers (total antioxidant capacity [TAC], catalase [CAT], glutathione peroxidase [GPx], superoxide dismutase [SOD], malondialdehyde [MDA], nitric oxide [NO]), epididymal sperm parameters (count, motility, viability, morphology, DNA integrity, plasma membrane functionality), histopathological and morphometric evaluations (Johnsen/Cosentino scores, seminiferous tubule diameter [STsD], Sertoli cell index [SCI], repopulation index [RI], meiotic index [MI], Leydig cell nuclear diameter [LCND], tubular differentiation index [TDI], spermiogenesis index [SPI]), apoptosis-related gene expression (Caspase-3, Caspase-8, Caspase-9, Fas, Bcl-2 via quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction [qRT-PCR]), and fertility indices post-mating. Results revealed dose-dependent impairments: reduced sperm concentration, motility, viability, and membrane integrity; increased DNA damage and morphological abnormalities; depleted antioxidants with elevated MDA/NO; severe histopathological changes including tubule atrophy and cell loss; upregulation of pro-apoptotic genes and downregulation of Bcl-2; and reduced fertility with higher implantation losses. While ZnO NPs exhibit beneficial effects, such as antioxidant and antimicrobial properties at low concentrations (typically < 5 mg/mL or 1–50 mg/kg in oral/intraperitoneal studies), they become toxic at higher doses (≥ 10 mg/mL in this intratesticular model), primarily through oxidative stress and apoptosis. These findings, despite species-specific and route-dependent variations, underscore ZnO NPs’ detrimental impact on spermatogenesis, emphasizing the need for cautious application to mitigate risks to male fertility.

Introduction

Reproductive health and fertility management are critical issues in human and veterinary medicine, with implications for population dynamics, environmental sustainability, and disease prevention1,2. Castration has evolved over millennia to mitigate risks associated with overpopulation, including zoonotic threats and androgen-driven behavioral disruptions, while also addressing conditions such as trauma, neoplasia, varicocele, hydrocele, orchitis, epididymitis, spermatic cord torsion, and inguinal hernia1,3. The techniques, encompassing hormonal, chemical, and surgical methods, focus on identifying safer and more effective approaches to regulate hormone production and spermatogenesis while maintaining overall health1,3–5.

Conventional surgical methods, while effective, present several disadvantages: high costs, the necessity for anesthesia, the demand for sterile environments and skilled personnel, and the risk of complications such as infection or extended recovery times6–9. Examples of hormonal and immunological treatments include targeting gonadotropins and generating antibodies against luteinizing hormone, both of which often yield short-term effects and unexpected systemic impacts on non-reproductive tissues3,10,11. Chemical castration offers a less invasive alternative that enhances control over androgen-related issues and supports scalable applications, while also reducing procedural stress, bleeding risks, and resource demands4,12.

Various agents, including calcium chloride, clove oil, mannitol, glycerin, atrazine, sodium chloride, deslorelin, ethanol, iron (III) chloride hexahydrate, chlorhexidine gluconate, cetrimide, formalin, cadmium chloride, ferric chloride, ferrous sulfate, zinc gluconate neutralized by arginine, and lactic acid, have been evaluated across multiple models, encompassing rodents, dogs, horses, and cows6,9,11,13–18. The necessity for innovative compounds that achieve specific, irreversible inhibition of spermatogenesis and androgenesis while minimizing side effects is underscored by ongoing issues, including inflammation, pain, reversible tissue damage, and variability in permanence4,15,19.

Reproductive toxicity remains a significant concern in toxicology, particularly with nanomaterials like zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs), which can induce oxidative stress, DNA damage, and apoptosis in reproductive tissues20–23. Recent reviews highlight the impact of nanoparticles on male fertility, including reduced sperm quality and testicular damage, often mediated by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and inflammatory pathways24. Chemotherapeutic agents and heavy metals, such as cadmium, further exacerbate these effects by disrupting spermatogenesis and hormonal balance25,26. These insights underscore the need for targeted studies on nanomaterial-induced toxicity to inform safer applications.

Due to their small size and biocompatibility, nanoparticles, including zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs), can penetrate the body via multiple pathways, such as ingestion, inhalation, dermal absorption, and injection. These substances can interfere with cellular processes and lead to systemic toxicity24,25. Prolonged exposure to ZnO nanoparticles has been associated with various adverse effects in animal models, including hepatotoxicity, renal damage, pulmonary inflammation, and reproductive issues such as altered testicular function and reduced sperm quality9,26,27. Semiconductor metal oxides are extensively utilized in biomedical applications due to their UV-blocking, fungicidal, and photocatalytic properties. These applications include anticancer therapy, bioimaging, and antimicrobial uses, as well as in cosmetics, sunscreens, food packaging, and agriculture28–30. The production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by ZnO nanoparticles (NPs) leads to oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, DNA damage, and apoptosis in mammalian cells, raising concerns regarding their potential health effects25,30,31. In vitro studies indicate cytotoxicity in human cells, such as nasal mucosa and epidermal keratinocytes, even at low dosages, highlighting the need for in vivo assessments31,32. This study aims to evaluate histology, oxidative stress, and various sperm parameters after the administration of intratesticular ZnO NP in male rats to investigate reproductive toxicity, despite prior research exploring multiple exposure routes. We predict that increased concentrations, determined from initial cytotoxicity data, will lead to greater impairments via oxidative stress and apoptosis (Tables 1, 2 and 3).

Results

Computer-assisted semen analysis (Table 4) showed that exposure to ZnO nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) greatly decreased sperm motility and kinematic characteristics. There were no significant differences between the control and normal saline (NS) groups for any of the metrics (p > 0.05). Treatment with 5 mg/mL ZnO NPs led to a substantial reduction in total motility compared to the control and NS groups (78.53 ± 2.91% vs. 82.24 ± 3.72% and 81.90 ± 3.72%, respectively; p = 0.037).

Building on these motility changes, at elevated concentrations, more pronounced dose-dependent reductions were observed. The 10 mg/mL ZnO NP group had much lower levels of epididymal sperm (p = 0.012), total motility (p = 0.008), progressive motility (p = 0.015), and velocity parameters (VAP, p = 0.021; VCL, p = 0.019; VSL, p = 0.025) than the control, NS, and 5 mg/mL ZnO NP groups. The 25 mg/mL ZnO NP group had the most significant deficits, recording the lowest values across all kinetic parameters (VAP, VCL, VSL, ALH, and BCF). All of these groups were very different from each other (p < 0.001 for all comparisons), and the effect sizes (eta-squared) were between 0.45 and 0.62. There were no clear variations between the groups in linearity (LIN) or straightness (STR) (p > 0.05).

Complementing the kinematic data, further testing revealed a dose-dependent decrease in sperm integrity (Table 5). The viability and functionality of sperm plasma membranes were significantly diminished in the 10 mg/mL ZnO NP group (p = 0.028 compared to lower dose groups) and in the 25 mg/mL ZnO NP group (p < 0.001 compared to all others). Conversely, they were significantly elevated in the control, NS, and 5 mg/mL ZnO NP groups, with no differences among these groups (p > 0.05). The control and NS groups exhibited the lowest percentage of sperm with DNA damage (p > 0.05), succeeded by the 5 mg/mL ZnO NP group (p = 0.041 vs. controls) and the 25 mg/mL ZnO NP group (p < 0.001 vs. all others). The 25 mg/mL ZnO NP group had the highest rates of abnormalities and the lowest proportion of normal sperm. This was very different from all the other groups (p < 0.001), and the 95% confidence intervals clearly showed the difference. With higher doses, all types of sperm morphological defects (head, tail, proximal droplet, and distal droplet) rose significantly.

These sperm alterations correlated with increased oxidative stress in testicular tissue (Table 6). A one-way ANOVA [F(3, 36) = 18.72, p < 0.001 for TAC] demonstrated that the activity of key antioxidant enzymes dropped significantly in a dose-dependent way. The control group had a markedly elevated total antioxidant capacity (TAC) compared to all treatment groups (p < 0.001). The ZnO NP levels in the 5 mg/mL group were considerably lower than those in the control and NS groups (p = 0.014). The lowest levels were seen in the 10 mg/mL (p = 0.002) and 25 mg/mL (p < 0.001) groups. The activities of catalase (CAT; F(3, 36) = 16.89, p < 0.001) and glutathione peroxidase (GPx; F(3, 36) = 15.43, p < 0.001) exhibited comparable patterns. The control group demonstrated the highest superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, which progressively decreased throughout treatment groups [F(3, 36) = 21.56, p < 0.001]. High levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), which is an indication of lipid peroxidation, showed that there was an oxidative imbalance. The 25 mg/mL ZnO NP group had levels that were much greater than the other groups (p < 0.001). Nitric oxide (NO) levels significantly increased in the higher-dose groups (10 and 25 mg/mL ZnO NPs) as compared to controls and the 5 mg/mL group (p = 0.009 and p < 0.001, respectively).

Histopathological investigation further confirmed these biochemical changes, revealing significant testicular injury (Table 7; Fig. 1). The control, NS, and 5 mg/mL ZnO NP groups had significantly elevated reproductive organ weights (testis and epididymis) and their body weight ratios compared to the 10 mg/mL (p = 0.018) and 25 mg/mL (p < 0.001) ZnO NP groups, with no significant differences among them (p > 0.05). The Johnsen score, which plummeted from 8.36 ± 0.39 in the controls to 1.29 ± 0.53 in the 25 mg/mL ZnO NP group [F(3, 36) = 32.47, p < 0.001], showed that spermatogenesis was almost completely stopped. The elevated Cosentino scores in the 10 mg/mL and 25 mg/mL groups (p = 0.007 and p < 0.001, respectively) supported this finding. Morphometric analyses indicated significant reductions in seminiferous tubule diameter (STsD), Sertoli cell index (SCI), repopulation index (RI), meiotic index (MI), Leydig cell nuclear diameter (LCND), tubular differentiation index (TDI), and spermiogenesis index (SPI) when comparing the 10 mg/mL and 25 mg/mL ZnO NP groups to the control, NS, and 5 mg/mL groups [F(3, 36) > 14.5, p < 0.001 for each parameter].

Discussion

This study examined the use of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) to induce reproductive toxicity in male rats, in comparison to chemical castration models. The observed reductions may be associated with increased levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Previous studies indicate that chemical agents can disrupt motility, an essential feature of normal spermatozoa, through mechanisms such as calcium (Ca2+) signaling and cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)-dependent protein kinase A (PKA)9. Injecting sodium chloride into the testicles of male cats resulted in decreased sperm motility due to disruption of specific pathways. Mohammad and James33 and Fesseha and Negash34 found that chlorhexidine gluconate and cetrimide reduced bucks’ sperm motility from 60 to 80% to zero within three days. Oliveira et al.35 reported a significant decrease in motility in dogs following zinc gluconate injections. A study indicated significantly decreased sperm motility after the intratesticular injection of silver-doped carbon dots (AgCDs) for chemical castration in rats9. The findings demonstrate that ZnO nanoparticles at higher concentrations (10 mg/mL and 25 mg/mL) significantly reduced sperm motility in rats, indicating testicular toxicity36. Elevated ROS production likely results in lipid peroxidation of the sperm membrane and inhibits intracellular enzymes37.

ZnO NPs harmed sperm count and viability, which are important signs of fertility. This is similar to what was seen in chemical castration models. A study utilizing AgCDs in rats revealed a reduction in sperm concentration associated with oxidative damage. Similar effects have been noted with other substances; for example, the injection of mannitol in rats led to elevated levels of immature sperm and DNA fragmentation, as determined by acridine-orange staining. Previous studies on nanomaterials, including quantum dots, have linked elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) to DNA damage, membrane disruption, and infertility. The direct binding of ZnO nanoparticles or zinc ions to DNA obstructs repair mechanisms, and the reduced expression of fen-1/pol-B exacerbates these impairments. Barkhordari et al.38 exhibited dose- and time-dependent cytotoxicity in human spermatozoa, noting viability reductions at concentrations between 10 and 1000 µg/mL. Likewise, Abbasalipourkabir et al.39 documented oxidative stress and diminished sperm quality in rats receiving ZnO nanoparticles at doses surpassing 50 mg/kg. In mice given daily doses of 5-300 mg/kg BW ZnO nanoparticles for 35 days, Talebi et al.40 observed significant alterations in motility, count, and abnormalities. Furthermore, oxidative stress negatively affects the functionality of Leydig cells and spermatozoa, according to Acharya and Mishra41. In line with the findings of AgCDs9, the present study showed that increasing ZnO NP concentrations resulted in increased DNA damage, decreased sperm count and viability, and increased TZI and the proportion of immature sperm. Both improper functioning of antioxidant enzymes42 and excessive production of ROS43 can lead to these effects. Elevated concentrations also reduced the gonadosomatic index and testis weight in relation to body weight, indicating inhibited gonadal development caused by S-phase cell cycle arrest in spermatocytes44,45, similar to the weight reductions observed in AgCDs-treated rats9.

Testicular tissue is susceptible to oxidative damage induced by ZnO nanoparticles (NPs) due to their capacity to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS) that surpass the limits of antioxidant defense mechanisms. Upon entering cells, ZnO nanoparticles (NPs) typically aggregate in lysosomes, where they disintegrate in the acidic environment, releasing zinc ions (Zn2+)40. These ions generate free radicals, such as superoxide anions and hydroxyl radicals, which result in damage to DNA, proteins, and lipids. This form of damage interacts with proteins, lysosomes, the endoplasmic reticulum, and nucleic acids, inhibiting essential enzymes and ultimately resulting in necrosis or apoptosis37,46. ZnO nanoparticles exacerbate oxidative imbalance and trigger cellular apoptosis by activating endoplasmic reticulum stress pathways, particularly the unfolded protein response45,47,48. This study indicates that rat testes exposed to ZnO nanoparticles experienced notable decreases in antioxidant enzymes, such as catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and total antioxidant capacity (TAC). ZnO nanoparticles function as pro-oxidants in the male reproductive system, where an accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) may hinder sperm production and compromise the integrity of sperm membranes. The elevated levels of polyunsaturated fatty acids in germ cells explain this phenomenon. The result is consistent with studies on rats treated with silver carbon dots, which showed similar patterns: activities of CAT, SOD, GPx, and TAC decreased simultaneously, while levels of MDA and NO increased9. The findings further illustrate chemically induced oxidative alterations. Moustafa et al.12 demonstrated that formalin elevated MDA and NO levels while decreasing glutathione (GSH) levels in canine testicular models. Karami et al.49 presented further evidence concerning the susceptibility of reproductive tissues to agents that interfere with enzymatic antioxidants, indicating that exposure to calcium chloride diminished SOD and GPx activities under comparable conditions.

Histopathological and morphometric assessments validated testicular degeneration, aligning with the results of chemical castration. In the AgCDs study involving rats, metrics such as STsD, SCI, RI, MI, TDI, and SPI diminished concurrently with tubule depletion, vacuolization, atrophy, and giant cell formation9,50. Likewise, Talebi et al.40 documented cellular disorganization, sperm-deficient tubules, hyperemia, and nuclear alterations in rats subsequent to oral/intraperitoneal administration of ZnO nanoparticles at 1–2.5 g/kg. Mozaffari and Parivar51 observed diminished sperm counts and degenerated cells in the epididymis of NMRI mice at a dosage of 250 mg/kg, resulting in the loss of seminiferous tubule cells. Shirvani et al.52 observed interstitial damage, edema, inflammation, and cellular disruption in rats following 25–100 mg/kg intraperitoneal injections. Moridian et al.53 reported reductions in testis weight at 300 mg/kg and modifications in stereological and morphometric parameters at 50–300 mg/kg after 35 days. Han et al.54 demonstrated that ZnO nanoparticles infiltrate Sertoli and Leydig cells in CD1 mice, resulting in dose- and time-dependent cytotoxicity, apoptosis, alterations in epithelial structure, and spermatozoal anomalies through reactive oxygen species-mediated DNA damage and mitochondrial potential depletion. These support our findings of substantial histological and morphometric changes, encompassing disorganization, vacuolization, diminished dimensions, and cellular loss.

Apoptosis serves as a quality control mechanism to eradicate damaged cells during mammalian development, and ZnO NPs enhanced the expression of pro-apoptotic genes while decreasing that of anti-apoptotic ones at the molecular level45. In our study, Bcl-2 was downregulated, while Caspase-3, -8, -9, and Fas were dose-dependently upregulated (highest at 25 mg/mL; elevated at 10 mg/mL vs. lower groups), activating extrinsic (Fas/Caspase-8) and intrinsic (Caspase-9) pathways convergent on Caspase-3. This is comparable to the AgCDs study in rats, where apoptosis was promoted by an increase in P53, Bax, and caspase-3 and a decrease in Bcl-29. Free radicals phosphorylate or alter it as a marker of apoptosis, but Bcl-2 inhibits Bax mitochondrial entry and cytochrome C release, preventing caspase activation42,46,55. Reduced Bcl-2 and increased pro-apoptotic genes imply that ZnO NPs increase radicals, causing downregulation and cell death, which explains decreased sperm viability through unbalanced gene expression46.

Our study’s unique contribution lies in demonstrating the dose-dependent threshold for ZnO NPs’56 transition from potentially beneficial (e.g., antioxidant at low doses) to toxic effects in an intratesticular model, offering a novel comparison to established chemical castrants like AgCDs. Alternative mechanisms, such as direct zinc ion release disrupting ion homeostasis or inflammation-mediated pathways, could also contribute, though ROS appears dominant based on elevated MDA/NO levels. Limitations include the focus on rats, potentially limiting translatability, and the acute intratesticular route, which may not mimic chronic environmental exposures.

ZnO nanoparticles (NPs) have demonstrated effectiveness in inducing reproductive damage akin to AgCDs in rat studies, specifically affecting the testes9,40,46. It is essential to consider the potential impact on the body as a whole. ZnO nanoparticles have the potential to induce inflammation and oxidative damage in certain areas, enter the bloodstream, and accumulate in organs such as the liver, kidneys, testes, and spleen, similar to Ag nanoparticles46. Qin et al.57 reported no significant toxicity in rats following 28 days of oral administration of AgNPs at doses up to 1 mg/kg BW, as assessed through weight, food intake, and histopathological examination. Due to their small size and neutral charge, carbon dots, such as those examined in the AgCDs study, are frequently excreted in urine. Their elimination indicates a reduced impact on the liver and spleen. They may assist in imaging, drug administration, and disease treatment9. The concentrations of ZnO nanoparticles employed were below those associated with significant toxicity in other studies, and our short-term protocol mitigates the potential for long-term adverse effects. Despite the advantages, further investigation is required to determine long-term safety, pharmacokinetics, and systemic effects for a comprehensive risk assessment.

There are many ways that this study could be better. The study was performed solely on Wistar rats, potentially limiting the applicability of the results to other species, including humans, due to variations in reproductive physiology and nanoparticle metabolism among species. The intratesticular injection method allows for direct exposure assessment, but it may not accurately reflect standard environmental or occupational exposure pathways, such as inhalation or dermal absorption, which could lead to an overestimation of toxicity in real-world situations. The study focused solely on the 60 days following the vaccination, which may have overlooked potential long-term or chronic effects, such as the duration of healing or the gradual onset of adverse outcomes over time. The investigation focused exclusively on doses of 5, 10, and 25 mg/mL. This study did not consider smaller quantities that have a more significant environmental impact, nor the variations in nanoparticle size that could influence their absorption efficiency and potential toxicity. Finally, we looked at oxidative stress markers and gene expression. Due to the limited research in omics fields such as proteomics and metabolomics, intricate molecular networks remain difficult to comprehend.

Future research will need to examine the limitations outlined here regarding the effects of ZnO nanoparticles in models such as primates, which are more similar to humans. Furthermore, the exposure scenario should be more realistic (e.g., inhalation), and the study duration should be longer than 60 days to be able to evaluate any chronic effects and reversibility. In addition, different sizes of nanoparticles, lower doses, and the use of advanced omics techniques would not only provide more mechanistic insights but also stimulate more significant breakthroughs. Testing antioxidants (e.g., vitamin E) could also be a way to identify protective strategies. Epidemiological studies on human populations exposed to pollution sources would help bridge the gap between preclinical and clinical studies and inform nanoparticle safety regulations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that intratesticular ZnO NPs induce dose-dependent reproductive toxicity in male Wistar rats, mimicking chemical castration effects comparable to agents like AgCDs. Key findings include reduced sperm motility, count, viability, and membrane integrity; increased DNA damage and abnormalities; oxidative stress with depleted antioxidants and elevated MDA/NO; histopathological degeneration with tubule atrophy and cell loss; apoptosis via upregulated Caspase-3/-8/-9/Fas and downregulated Bcl-2; and impaired fertility with higher implantation losses. These results highlight ZnO NPs’ potential as a chemical castrant at doses ≥ 10 mg/mL, while underscoring risks to male fertility through ROS-mediated mechanisms. The significance lies in advancing safer nanomaterial applications in veterinary medicine, with future prospects including cross-species validation, chronic exposure studies, and protective interventions to balance therapeutic benefits and toxicity.

Methods

Zinc oxide nanoparticles

Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) supplied zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs, < 100 nm particle size, catalog number: 544906). No noticeable agglomeration was observed at the specified concentrations, and the spherical nanoparticles exhibited a zeta potential of around − 20 mV in saltwater. Suspensions for the respective experimental groups were prepared by dissolving the nano powder in standard saline at concentrations of 5, 10, and 25 mg/mL58. These doses were selected based on preliminary cytotoxicity assays and prior literature indicating low toxicity at < 5 mg/mL equivalents and progressive impairment at higher levels in reproductive models40–42.

Animals and experimental design

Fifty Wistar albino rats, aged 12 to 14 weeks and weighing between 250 and 300 gr, were utilized in this study. The animals were sourced from the Animal Resource Center at Urmia University of Medical Sciences, located in Urmia, Iran. The subjects were maintained in a standard laboratory environment characterized by a 12-hour light/dark cycle, adequate air circulation, a temperature of 20 ± 2 °C, and a relative humidity of 50 ± 10%. Throughout the trial, standard mouse feed and tap water were consistently available. The Animal Ethics Committee of Urmia University approved each technique (IR-UU-AEC-3/79), adhering to the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In vivo Experiments) criteria, applicable laws, and institutional regulations9. A volume of 0.1 mL was administered into each testis bilaterally. No adverse incidents or fatalities occurred during the trial. Euthanasia was conducted on the 60th day via an intraperitoneal injection of thiopental sodium at a dosage of 250 mg/kg. Sperm was obtained from the epididymal cauda, along with blood samples9. The samples were centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to facilitate serum separation. The serum was subsequently stored at − 80 °C for future use. The left testis from each animal was extracted and stored at − 80 °C for the assessment of oxidative damage indicators. The right testis was fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for histological examination9.

Epididymal sperm collection

Sperm was obtained from the cauda epididymis. The right epididymis was used for all analyses to ensure consistency. The cauda epididymis was sectioned into small fragments and subsequently placed in a Petri dish containing 1 mL of human tubal fluid medium. This medium comprises potassium chloride, calcium chloride, and glucose, among other substances. The dish was maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 30 min9,59,60.

Semen analysis

The quantity of epididymal sperm

A Neubauer hemocytometer (Marienfeld Superior, Lauda-Königshofen, Germany) was utilized to enumerate the sperm following their rendering nonviable and subsequent dilution at a ratio of 1:5 in distilled water. A digital laboratory scale with a precision of 0.0001 g (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) was utilized to weigh the meticulously collected epididymal tissue fragments. The mean sperm count per gram of epididymal tissue was subsequently determined60.

The motility of sperm in the epididymis

Motility tests were conducted using computer-assisted semen analysis (CASA; Test Sperm 3.2 software, Videotest, St. Petersburg, Russia; equipped with an Olympus BX41 microscope, Tokyo, Japan) at room temperature (Table 1). This system assessed the subsequent parameters: Total motility, progressive motility, amplitude of lateral head displacement (ALH; µm/s), curvilinear velocity (VCL; µm/s), straight-line velocity (VSL; µm/s), average path velocity (VAP; µm/s), straightness (STR; %), linearity (LIN; %), and beat-cross frequency (BCF; Hz) are key parameters in assessing motility characteristics. A small volume of the sperm solution, approximately 10 µL, was placed on a microscope slide and examined using dark field or phase-contrast microscopy. Five microscopic regions contained a minimum of 500 spermatozoa9.

Morphological characteristics and viability of epididymal sperm

The eosin-nigrosin staining method was employed following WHO protocol to assess sperm morphology and vitality61. This involved utilizing a light microscope at 400x magnification (Olympus BX41, Tokyo, Japan) to analyze a mixture comprising one volume of semen and two volumes of 1% eosin. Viable sperm remained colorless, facilitating viability assessment, whereas nonviable sperm were stained red with eosin when mixed with nigrosin in distilled water. Eosin-nigrosin staining provides information regarding the quantity of abnormal sperm present. Sperm exhibiting abnormal pigmentation in the head, neck, or tail are classified as nonviable, whereas sperm containing residual cytoplasm are deemed abnormal. At a magnification of 400x, each sample contained 200 sperm, with results presented as a percentage62,63.

Damage to epididymal sperm DNA

The acridine orange (AO) staining technique was employed to differentiate between denatured, native, and double-stranded DNA in sperm chromatin. The most intense fluorescence was observed in denatured DNA. Following two hours of treatment with a 1:3 ratio of acetic acid and Carnoy’s fixative, the smears were air-dried for five minutes before immersion in a stock solution containing 1 mg of AO in 1000 milliliters of filtered water. The solution was maintained at 4 °C in a dark chamber for five minutes. A fluorescent microscope with 400x magnification (Olympus BX41, Tokyo, Japan) was employed to examine the spermatozoa, categorizing those that appeared red or yellow as damaged or aberrant64.

Sperm plasma membrane functionally (PMF)

Sperm exhibiting PMF were identified using the hypo-osmotic swelling test. For the test, 10 µL of the sperm sample was mixed with 100 µL of a hypo-osmotic solution containing fructose (1.351 g/L) and sodium citrate (0.735 g/L). The mixture was incubated for one hour at 37 °C. A phase contrast microscope with 400× magnification (Olympus, BX41, Tokyo, Japan) was utilized to evaluate the functionality of the sperm plasma membrane. Coiled or swollen tails were identified as the principal indicators of an effective PMF65.

Assessment activities of glutathione peroxidase (GPx), superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and total antioxidant capacity (TAC)

The TAC assay kit (Naxifer; Navand Salamat Company, Urmia, Iran), as directed by the manufacturer, which uses colorimetric measurement based on ferric ion reduction, was used to measure the total antioxidant capacity (TAC) levels in the testis. The findings were expressed as mmol/gr protein66. A GPx kit (NagpixTM; Navand Salamat Company, Urmia, Iran) was used to measure the amount of glutathione peroxidase (GPx) in the testis. The results were expressed as U/mg protein and were obtained by monitoring the oxidation of NADPH in an enzymatic reaction67. Using the Nasdox SOD test kit (Navand Salamat Company, Urmia, Iran), the concentration of superoxide dismutase (SOD) in the testis was measured in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions to measure the inhibition of superoxide radicals. Color development at 405 nm was used to measure the activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD), and the results were reported in nmol/mg protein. In a two-step process, the NatazTM Catalase Activity Assay Kit (Navand Salamat Company, Urmia, Iran) was used to measure the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide to quantify catalase activity. After a 10-minute incubation period at room temperature, the absorption rate at 550 nm was measured to determine the catalytic activity; the results were expressed in U/mg protein66.

Nitric oxide measurement and malondialdehyde (MDA) level determination

Oxidative stress levels were measured using the Lipid Peroxidation Assay Kit (NalondiTM; Navand Salamat Company, Urmia, Iran). A spectrophotometer (BioTek ELx800, Winooski, VT, USA) was used to measure the MDA concentration at 523 nm, which was then expressed as µmol/g protein68. The Griess reaction was used to measure the amount of nitric oxide (NO) in the testicular tissue samples69.

Examination of testicular tissue histopathologically and histomorphometrically

A digital scale (Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) was used to measure the weight of the testes (gr) and epididymis (gr) in each experimental group. Testicular weight as a percentage of body weight was computed.

Before being embedded in paraffin, the rats’ right testes, which had been preserved in a 10% buffered formalin solution, were dehydrated with ethanol. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was applied after thin layers were cut to a width of seven mm using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2255, Nussloch, Germany). A light microscope (Olympus Model BH-2, Tokyo, Japan) was then used to examine the stained slides. To minimize bias and guarantee representativeness, two hundred randomly chosen round or roughly round cross-sections of seminiferous tubules were used to compute seminiferous tubule diameter (STsD); inter-observer agreement was significant (kappa > 0.8)60.

Each cross-section’s two vertical diameters were measured using a light microscopic ocular micrometer (Model CHT, Olympus Optical Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Thereafter, the average values were determined. 60 randomly chosen seminiferous tubules per group were used to measure the Sertoli Cell Index (SCI), Reproductive Index (RI), and Meiotic Index (MI). The Sertoli Cell Index (SCI) is the proportion of germ cells in seminiferous tubules to Sertoli cells, which are distinguished by their unique nuclei and nucleoli. The RI measures the number of tubules containing germ cells that reach at least middle or higher spermatogonial levels. The MI indicates the number of cells lost during cell division and is calculated as the ratio of round spermatids to pachytene primary spermatocytes. The Leydig Cell Nuclear Diameter (LCND) was measured using a calibrated ocular micrometer in accordance with Elias and Hyde’s methodology70. The Tubular Differentiation Index (TDI) refers to the number of seminiferous tubules that contain at least three fully developed germ cells, while the Spermatogenic Index (SPI) refers to the number of seminiferous tubules with sperm cells. Two hundred transverse sections of seminiferous tubules from each animal, one hundred sections per testis, were analyzed to evaluate both indices50.

Using the Johnsen scoring system, which has a range of 1 to 10, the mean testicular biopsy score was evaluated to ascertain the stage of spermatogenesis. A light microscope (CHT model, Olympus Optical Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was used to examine two hundred seminiferous tubules (Table 2). Testicular damage was evaluated using the Cosentino scoring system (44). There are four test-related grades in the scoring system. Grade 1 is regarded as the typical testicular presentation. Densely packed seminiferous tubules with less ordered, non-cohesive germ cells are the hallmark of grade 2. Pyknotic and reduced nuclei, disordered and lost germ cells, and blurred seminiferous tubule borders are the hallmarks of grade 3. Grade 4 indicates coagulative necrosis and a high density of germ cells in the seminiferous tubules50.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), cDNA synthesis, and RNA extraction

The StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) was used in combination with the SinaSyber Blue HF-qPCR Mix (CinnaGen, Tehran, Iran), which is manufactured by CinnaGen in Tehran, Iran. The reaction system was 25 µL, and the relative expression of the genes Fas, caspase-3, caspase-8, caspase-971, and Bcl-272 in relation to the reference gene 18SrRNA73 was assessed using the qRT-PCR method (Table 3). The qRT-PCR thermal cycling parameters included 45 three-step cycles after an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 10 min. A 10-second denaturation phase at 95 °C, a 30-second annealing phase at 60 °C, and a 30-second extension phase at 72°C were all part of each cycle. The following formula was used to determine the samples’ relative gene expression: The formula 2−(SΔct−CΔct) is used to calculate the relative expression. SΔct is obtained by subtracting the reference gene’s cycle threshold (ct) value from the tested gene’s, and CΔct represents the internal control samples’ ct values. To determine the normal distribution of relative expression values, the data was subjected to a logarithmic transformation using base 1074.

Index of fertility

To increase mating opportunities and decrease stress, each male rat was paired with an unexposed female rat in a different cage, allowing for spontaneous mating at a ratio of 1 male to 2 females for up to 72 h. At 24, 48, and 72 h after mating, vaginal swabs were taken. Each female’s vaginal swab contained sperm, which signified the start of pregnancy and verified mating (GD0). Males and females were separated after this time. Prior to euthanasia on GD17, female subjects were anesthetized with a 10% ketamine (80 mg/kg/IP; Alfasan, Netherlands) and 2% xylazine (10 mg/kg/IP; Alfasan, Netherlands) combination9. Following the extraction and analysis of the reproductive systems, fertility indices were computed as follows: pre-implantation loss = (corpora lutea - implants) / corpora lutea × 100; post-implantation loss = (implants - viable fetuses) / implants × 100; pregnancy index = (number of pregnant females / numbers mated) × 100. The study was conducted by Hood and Hood75 and de Almeida Lima et al.76.

Analysis of statistics

SPSS software, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), was used to perform statistical analyses. The mean ± standard deviation (SD) is used to express the data. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to determine the normality of the distribution, and Levene’s test was used to determine the homogeneity of variances; all groups met the parametric assumptions (Shapiro-Wilk p > 0.05 for each parameter; Levene’s p > 0.05). To examine the differences between the experimental groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test was used for post-hoc comparisons after ANOVA was used to identify significant differences (p ≤ 0.05) to identify particular pairwise differences. Every test used a two-tailed methodology, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
