Wastewater and environmental surveillance as an early warning system and public health tool for mpox outbreak detection and management: a scoping review
Authors: Sizwe Nkambule, Hanyani Lebese, Yonela Mkunyana, Edison Mavundza, Mark E Engel, Renée Street
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWastewater and environmental surveillance (WES) has demonstrated the potential to detect signal for the mpox virus (MPXV), thereby supporting public health efforts to mitigate outbreaks. The aim of this scoping review was to document the use of WES as an early warning system and public health tool for mpox outbreak management.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science and Scopus in August 2024, and updated in April 2025, to identify eligible studies using the PCC framework.ResultsThe search yielded 256 records. Title and abstracts were screened for 140 records. Full text screening was done on the remaining 43 records, of which 10 further records were excluded. A total of 33 studies, published between 2022 and 2024, were included for synthesis in this review. Twenty-two studies reported a positive correlation/association between trends of MPXV in wastewater data and the reported cases on mpox. Four studies included in this scoping review reported the detection of MPXV in wastewater before clinical cases. Other studies reported negative associations, as well as false positive and false negative results from WES for mpox. Surveillance was predominantly conducted at wastewater treatment plants (91%), leaving a gap in surveillance of non-sewered settings. The qPCR method was reported by 61% of studies for molecular analysis. However, assessments of the qPCR method are needed to improve sensitivity and accuracy in the quantification of MPXV in wastewater.ConclusionsFurther research is needed to improve the capability of WES to provide early indication of mpox outbreaks and inform the accurate quantification of MPXV in wastewater to serve as an early warning system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04518-9.Keywords: Wastewater and environmental surveillance, MPXV, Mpox, Outbreak management, Public health, Early warning system

Background

Mpox (previously known as monkeypox), a viral disease caused by the monkeypox virus (MPXV), has emerged as a significant public health threat. The MPXV is classified into two genetically distinct clades namely clade i (endemic to Central Africa) and clade ii (endemic to West Africa) [1]. In 23 July 2022, the World Health Organisation (WHO) declared the global outbreak of mpox a public health emergency of international concern (PHEIC) after cases were reported in non-endemic countries without travel links to endemic regions [2]. As of early 2024, over 100,000 cases were reported across 122 countries, including 115 nations where mpox had never been previously recorded [3] thus amplifying the global nature of the threat [1]. Among the challenges that have limited timely clinical testing and reporting with the recent outbreak include stigma associated with the mpox disease and surveillance fatigue, likely resulting from the recent corona virus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic [4]. There is also the possibility of confusing mpox symptoms with other rash-causing infections such as chicken pox, which has led to misdiagnoses and negatively impacted contact tracing [5]. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), diagnostic challenges include limited testing capacity and resources to perform molecular testing and genomic sequencing [5]. Collectively, these challenges related to testing, disease presentation and stigma highlight the need for a novel approach to monitor the spread of mpox to inform response measures [6].

In the recent 2022 mpox outbreak, MPXV was detected in faeces, urine, saliva and semen, supporting to the detection of the MPXV DNA in wastewater samples [4, 7]. Wastewater and environmental surveillance (WES) involves the collection of samples from sewage, wastewater or environmental waters from defined catchments and the subsequent analysis of target pathogens and/or nucleic acids for public health surveillance purposes [1]. This surveillance approach acts as a valuable public health tool, providing early warning and evidence of infectious diseases circulating in the population. Monitoring pathogen presence, concentration trends and identifying variants of concern, enables public health stakeholders to assess infection trends. Apart from biological excretions, the MPXV is also shed through mucosal and skin lesions during activities such as teeth brushing, hand washing and bathing, thereby contributing to its presence in grey water [2]. It is through these various excretion and shedding routes that raise interest in WES being applied to support the public health response.

Leveraging the WES approach, various studies have since reported on the detection of MPXV DNA in wastewater samples [8–10]. Based on these findings, the presence of MPXV DNA in wastewater has been used as an indicator of mpox prevalence and to track the spread of the virus [11, 12]. Through the collection of wastewater samples, the approach enables the screening of large numbers of a population and the use of an anonymous pooled sample [12, 13]. In Chicago for example, the monitoring of MPXV in wastewater was used to supplement the clinical surveillance of mpox and provide further insight on localised mpox outbreaks [14]. A study by Oghuan et al. [15] showed increases in MPXV concentrations in wastewater 1–2 weeks prior to the reporting of a new clinical case in El Paso, Texas. A WES approach can also assist in monitoring outbreaks, identifying hotspots and asymptomatic cases and enhance outbreak management alongside clinical surveillance. The integration of clinical and collaborative surveillance with WES can also be valuable in LMIC settings where there are limited resources and testing capacity.

This review builds on a previous review by Islam et al. [16] that focused on sample collection, processing and public health safety around wastewater-based surveillance of MPXV. This scoping review also seeks to extend the evidence base from the review by Atoui et al. [7] that reviewed studies on the shedding of MPXV and its detection in wastewater, as well as the review by Omatola et al. [17] that reviewed evidence on the correlation between MPXV surveillance data in wastewater and the reported clinical case data. Specifically, this scoping review aims to summarize the evidence on the potential of WES as an early warning system and public health tool to manage mpox outbreaks. In the context of WES and for purposes of this scoping review, early warning system refers to the detection of disease presence or infection trends through wastewater surveillance prior to clinical reporting [18]. The focus of this review is not solely on whether WES can detect MPXV in wastewater or assess correlation in wastewater data and mpox clinical case data. Instead, this review will explore whether WES can potentially act as an early warning system for acute mpox outbreaks and provide early insight to MPXV transmission.

Methods

Review question

What is the existing evidence supporting the use of WES as an early warning system and public health tool for managing mpox outbreaks?

Eligibility criteria

This scoping review used the Population, Concept and, Context (PCC) framework recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute to identify relevant studies and main concepts [19].

Population: Any human population/community at risk of MPXV exposure.

Population: Any human population/community at risk of MPXV exposure.

Concept: Field-based studies aiming to detect MPXV in sewered and non-sewered wastewater, including grey water or human-impacted environmental waters.

Concept: Field-based studies aiming to detect MPXV in sewered and non-sewered wastewater, including grey water or human-impacted environmental waters.

Context: All geographic locations and countries, in both rural and urban settings.

Context: All geographic locations and countries, in both rural and urban settings.

The PCC framework used to identify relevant studies for this scoping review sought to search for studies on how trends of MPXV in wastewater can inform public health responses to mpox outbreaks. As such, studies that focused on human populations are eligible. Field-based studies were eligible as these could demonstrate the real-world application of WES for mpox. Studies conducted across all countries and in rural and urban setting were eligible to accommodate the application of WES in different settings.

Exclusion criteria

The screening of studies in this review was further guided by the following exclusion criteria:

Studies that used wastewater and environmental surveillance of non-human MPXV.

Studies that used wastewater and environmental surveillance of non-human MPXV.

Studies that were solely based on laboratory experiments, using other or similar viruses as a surrogate of MPXV in wastewater samples.

Studies that were solely based on laboratory experiments, using other or similar viruses as a surrogate of MPXV in wastewater samples.

Types of sources

This scoping review considered original research articles and reports that met the inclusion criteria, irrespective of study design. Laboratory-based experimental studies, which refers to studies limited to control laboratory environments and did not include sample collection in a natural setting, were excluded.

Search strategy and identification of studies

A comprehensive search strategy for searching electronic databases was developed (Additional file 1: Table S1). Some of the key words used in these searches included ‘Monkeypox virus’, ‘Mpox virus’, ‘wastewater-based epidemiological monitoring’, ‘wastewater surveillance’, ‘environmental surveillance’, ‘disease outbreaks’ and ‘disease management’. In August 2024, we searched the PubMed, Web of Science and Scopus databases for eligible studies, with no language restrictions and publication date. We also conducted hand searches of the reference lists of included studies and related reviews. We performed an updated search in April 2025. We exported all studies retrieved from the electronic searches into Rayyan for deduplication and screening.

Study selection

Four review authors, in pairs of two (EM and HL) and (SN and YM), independently screened the titles and abstracts of the studies to identify potential eligible studies. We retrieved the full texts of all potentially eligible studies. The same four review authors independently screened the full texts to identify the studies that met the eligibility criteria. Disagreements between the authors were resolved by discussion and consensus during review meetings.

Data extraction

A purpose-designed data extraction form was used to extract, inter alia, the following: study characteristics, sampling strategy, laboratory methods, results from wastewater analysis (Additional file 1: Table S2). Four review authors, in pairs of two (EM and HL) and (SN and YM), independently extracted data from each selected study. One author from each pair extracted the data, while a second author verified the data, with discrepancies resolved through discussions with a third reviewer. The data was captured on a single Excel spreadsheet to facilitate the centralised extraction of data from the different articles. This also allowed us to combine results from the different reviewers and conduct analysis representative of heterogeneous data.

Data analysis

The data were described using a narrative synthesis approach and presented according to the thematic areas outlined in the data extraction tool, highlighting key results. Pivot tables were created to manage and summarize the data extracted from each study. Summary data was also presented in tables and figures.

Search results

The search yielded 256 records (Web of Science = 111, PubMed = 53 and Scopus = 92). After removing 116 duplicates, we screened the title and abstracts of the remaining 140 records. Of these, we excluded 97 records based on the eligibility and exclusion criteria and retrieved the full-text articles of the remaining 43 records. Following the full text screening, 10 records were further excluded for different reasons. A total of 33 studies were therefore included in our review for synthesis. The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Additional file 3: Table S3. WES and clinical case insights

The majority of studies (82%) reported clinical data together with findings from the wastewater data, with results varying. Of these studies (n = 27) that reported clinical case data alongside wastewater data, 82% of studies reported a positive correlation/association between the two data sets (see Additional file 3: Table S3). In terms of WES offering early detection, Oghuan et al. [15] reported an increasing trend in MPXV viral load in wastewater 1–2 weeks before the reporting of mpox cases in the City of El Paso Texas. Similarly, WES conducted by Wolfe et al. [20] in California reported that in some places, the detection of MPXV in wastewater preceded the reporting of the first cases of mpox. However, it must be noted that a lead time between detection in wastewater and reporting of the first case was not provided. In a retrospective study conducted in Chicago, Foulkes et al. [14] detected MPXV in wastewater seven weeks before case-based identification of mpox.

Bagutti et al. [21] found that there was no correlation between positive detection of MPXV in wastewater, and the number of daily cases (OR = 1.23, 95% CI = 0.53–2.90, p = 0.629) or 7-day median (OR = 1.69, 95% CI = 0.21–13.87, p = 0.624). However, there was an association between the positive detection of MPXV in wastewater when comparing it with the number of symptomatic cases (OR = 2.18, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.38–3.43, p = 0.001) and with the 7-day median of symptomatic cases (OR = 1.54, 95% CI = 1.03–2.30, p = 0.035). Other analytical approaches to test association included Wolfe et al. 2022 [20] that reported a significant and positive association between the 5-day average concentration of MPXV in wastewater, and the 7-day average of mpox incidence rate. The association was compared using the episode date which was taken as the symptom onset date (Kendall’s tau = 0.59, p < 0.001, n = 176), and the report date which was taken to be the date of the laboratory result (Kendall’s tau = 0.66, p < 0.001, n = 130).

In other instances, despite MPXV being detected across the majority of WES samples, a study by Sherchan et al. [22] found no correlation between wastewater data and mpox cases. Bowes et al. [4] reported on instances of non-detection of MPXV in wastewater during periods when reported clinical cases were high, and other periods when the inverse occurred. Foulkes et al. [14] was able to use wastewater surveillance to detect the presence of MPXV 7 weeks prior to clinical cases reported in Chicago. The association between wastewater data and mpox cases has also been tested using passive samplers. In their work, Besijn et al. [23] reported a positive detection in wastewater after deploying passive samplers in a section of a street where there was a known person with MPXV.

WES setup and implementation

Studies in China, Spain and Thailand tracked MPXV in wastewater using already established WES networks that were set-up to monitor severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) during the COVID-19 pandemic [8, 24, 25]. With specific reference to studies in the USA, Sherchan et al. [22] noted that although mpox clinical cases were reported from 52 different locations, there were only four cities in the country that conducted WES to support clinical surveillance during the March 2023 outbreak. Collectively, these studies highlight how investment in infrastructure and resources from prior WES networks can be leveraged and adapted to monitor different pathogens. In New Orleans, a unique application of WES was demonstrated in a study by Brighton et al. [25], which monitored MPXV in wastewater before, during and after a music festival, showcasing the use of WES for tracking mass gathering events. Using WES for mass gathering events has also been used to support mass sporting events [26, 27] and monitor gatherings around holiday periods [28].

Study regions and timelines

The studies included in this review were published between 2022 and 2024, with the majority (45%) published in 2024, followed by 39% and 16% in 2023 and 2022, respectively. Incidentally, the publishing of these studies coincided with the mpox outbreaks in May 2022, early 2023 and the recent August 2024, all of which were declared as a PHEIC by the WHO [1]. Geographically, 33% of studies were conducted in the USA (Fig. 3). The majority of studies (76%) were undertaken in high income countries (HICs), while 24% were undertaken in upper middle-income countries namely Brazil, Peru, China and Thailand. Based on WHO region classification (Fig. 3), 55% of studies are part of the Americas region, with 28% belonging to the Europe region and fewer representing the Western Pacific (10%) and South-East Asia (7%) regions. No studies were retrieved in countries from Africa or Eastern Mediterranean WHO regions.

Sample types, locations and methods

For MPXV detection, 91% of wastewater samples were collected from influent wastewater, while a minority (9%) were collected on settled solids or faecal sludge samples (Table 1). Comparisons were made between wastewater liquids and solids in a study by Wolfe et al. [20], which found a significant association (Kendall’s tau = 0.52, n = 28, p < 0.001) in the concentration detected between these two types of wastewater samples. Wolfe et al. [20] also reported significantly higher concentration (Wilcoxon signed rank test, n = 28, p < 0.001) of MPXV in solids than in liquid wastewater. There were no studies reporting on the collection of effluent wastewater samples. Overall, this review found that studies solely focused on collecting sewered samples, with no studies collecting non-sewered samples, or human-impacted environmental waters samples from rivers, lakes or streams. Six percent of studies did not specify the type of samples collected.

The majority of studies (91%) collected wastewater samples at wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs). The population size captured by WWTPs across these studies ranged from 167,000 to 9,667,667 inhabitants, with a median population of 1,013,750 inhabitants. Other locations used as part of the sampling strategy included 6% and 9% of studies collecting wastewater samples from upstream locations, namely manholes and pumpstations, respectively. Interestingly, 6% of studies sampled from wastewater treatment plants that exclusively serve airport precincts namely the Schiphol Airport in Amsterdam, and the Fiumicino Airport in Rome [9, 10], and are indicative of an interest in ports of entry into countries. de Jonge et al. [10] noted that the Schiphol Airport is a major travel hub with a variable population, processing as much as 5.1 million passengers in a month. Although there was interest in sampling from airport WWTPs, there were no studies that collected wastewater samples directly from aircrafts. Nearly a third of studies (31%) conducted their sampling at locations from various defined communities and settings. These included shopping centres, condominiums, office complexes, restaurants and hospitals. With specific reference to hospital wastewater, Calabria de Araujo et al. [29] detected higher frequency in detection of MPXV in hospital wastewater (66.7%) when compared to municipal WWTPs (13.3%). Additionally, Ou et al. [30] reported a significant strong correlation (r = 0.82, p < 0.001) between the peak concentration of MPXV in hospital wastewater and the number of mpox cases, and between the mean concentration of MPXV in the hospital wastewater and the number of mpox cases.

Most studies (70%) utilized composite active sampling methods, typically deploying this instrument over a 24-h period, retrieving a pooled and representative sample [6, 15, 31]. This type of method involves the collection and consolidation of multiple wastewater samples taken throughout a time period and into a single volume [32]. There were 27% of studies that used the grab sampling technique, and six of these studies collected wastewater samples using both the grab and composite sampling methods. Comparisons in the performance between these two methods was reported by Sherchan et al. [22], reporting a higher detection ratio from the grab wastewater samples (8 out of 9 samples), compared to composite wastewater samples (5 out of 9 samples). Notably, one of the studies included in this review used composite passive/trap sampling techniques which included a ‘torpedo’ style passive sampler which was a plastic porous container with an absorption material placed within the casing [26]. A weekly sampling routine was the most common, reported in 61% of studies. Daily sampling was conducted in 19% of studies, while 12% followed an ad hoc approach and 8% collected samples monthly.

Molecular analysis of MPXV in wastewater

In this review, 61% of studies utilized quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and a similar number of studies used sequencing methods to analyse wastewater samples (Table 2). There were 30% of studies that analysed their wastewater samples using the reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) method. Other methods reported in studies include digital polymerase chain reaction (dPCR) (n = 4) and digital drop PCR (ddPCR) (n = 3). Sherchan et al. [22] reported that laboratories used various qPCR assays, with some using CDC established qPCR assays, others using TaqMan qPCR probes and primers and in certain instances designing their own novel qPCR assays. It was further noted that given how these different qPCR assays target different regions of MPXV that are also known to mutate, further research is needed to test specificity, sensitivity and accuracy of different qPCR assays [22].

An assessment was made of MPXV qPCR assays developed by the CDC and compared with an in-house developed assay. Briefly, the study found that the in-house developed assay provided larger qPCR amplicon size which led to more Sanger sequence-confirmed MPXV results when compared to the CDC qPCR assays [6]. Additionally, the study reported lower Ct values of 35.6 for their custom designed assay, where in comparison the CDC assays result where 36.3 and 36.1 for their respective G2R_G and G2R_WA assays, indicating that the custom designed assay was more sensitive in comparison to CDC assays [6]. Another study evaluated the performance of qPCR assays without preamplification and reported high Ct values ranging from 38 to 47 and provided results of non-detection of MPXV in wastewater samples despite Sanger sequencing confirming the presence of MPXV DNA [4].

Of the various MPXV DNA detection assays used to target regions of the MPXV gene, 59% of studies used the generic assay which is common to all MPXV sequences. There were 21% of studies that sought to detect clade ii, with a smaller proportion of studies (7%) that used DNA detection assays for sub-clade iib, while 3% analysed clade i. In their work, Girón-Guzmán et al. [13] found that the assay targeting the clade ii/West African clade yielded slightly higher sensitivity and detections rates and thus recommend the specific targeting of clade ii for WES.

Wastewater data reporting and analysis

With regards to units of measure of MPXV detected in WES samples, the same number of studies (n = 8) reported the viral load in copies/ml and gene copies/L. In their study, Ampuero et al. [33] detected MPXV viral load in wastewater, ranging between 35 and 2231 genome copies/ml, and Wannigama et al. [8] reporting between 10 and 90 copies/ml viral load. During a 1-week daily surveillance period, Zheng et al. [24] reported MPXV viral load that ranged from 372 to 7606 copies/L. There were 21% of studies reporting cycle threshold (Ct) values, and 7% equally using quantification cycle (Cq) values and copies per gram (copies/g). A few studies reported the viral load using Ct ratios and genome copy equivalents per millilitre (GCE/ml). For example, Gazecka et al. [31] estimated the amplitude of MPXV in their study population by calculating MPXV Ct to pepper mild mottle virus (PMMoV) Ct ratio, which ranged between 1.54 and 1.80. One study reported on the viral load using qualitative data and presented data on the absence or presence of MPXV in wastewater. The normalization of wastewater data was reported in 24% of studies, using either PMMoV, CrAssphage, or flow rate as indicators. Gazecka et al. [31] used PMMoV as part of their estimate on the spread of MPXV by calculating the MPXV Ct to PMMoV Ct ratio, with a lower ratio indicative of higher amounts of MPXV DNA in wastewater. Other normalization approaches reported included multiplying the viral load concentration in wastewater by the daily flow rate at the wastewater treatment plant, which was used to calculate the viral load in GCE per day [6, 32]. Novel statistical and analytical methods were used to enhance reporting of trends in wastewater and clinical data. The use of machine learning models was reported by Ou et al. [30] to address low accuracy from single model predictions, with the artificial intelligence (AI) model constructed to infer the number of mpox cases to the viral load of MPXV in wastewater. This was the only study included in this review that employed AI in their data interpretation.

Discussion

Early warning for acute mpox outbreaks

Although there is potential public health value in using WES for monitoring the presence of mpox in a community, there were very few instances where MPXV in wastewater was detected ahead of the reporting of clinical mpox cases. In some instances, the wastewater data reported false positive signals and indicated the presence of mpox in a community despite there being no reported clinical cases. The reasons for the lack of association between wastewater data and clinical cases was potentially due to limited or inaccessible access to facilities where people can get tested [34]. When relying on clinical case data, one has to take into consideration that the information is dependent on people’s health-seeking behaviour, with testing often being done after the onset of symptoms [34]. Furthermore, there’s a need to factor in individuals who may choose not to be tested due to the stigma associated with mpox [4, 21, 22]. The implications of these dynamics around clinical testing means that the WES data can be a valuable indicator of mpox cases in a community, as infected individuals would shed the mpox virus during their early stages of infection, and possibly before they have been tested and diagnosed.

Currently, there is limited understanding regarding the shedding of MPXV into wastewater by presymptomatic and asymptomatic infections [6]. Additionally, findings from Bagutti et al. [21] raise questions around the time lag between virus shedding by infected individuals and the timing when individuals are diagnosed with mpox symptoms. Other challenges with relying on WES to act as an early warning system for acute mpox outbreaks is that the WES approach does not account for the movement and commuting of people across catchment areas, making it difficult to ascertain which segments of the population are shedding and excreting MPXV [14]. Additionally, when mpox cases are extremely low, the positivity rate in the detection of MPXV in wastewater samples can decrease which in turn casts doubt linked to wastewater data providing false negative results [4, 11, 12]. In their assessment, Wong et al. [35] noted that WES for MPXV is more effective at passively monitoring outbreaks and/or resurgences in comparison to relying on clinical case reporting on its own. However, they also emphasise that this effectiveness is only observed in smaller communities with low cases of mpox [35]. Therefore, further research is needed to determine an individual's contribution to the presence of MPXV in wastewater as part of evaluating the potential of WES being used as an early warning system.

In certain instances, existing investment in infrastructure and resources from prior WES networks and early warning systems for SARS-CoV-2 were leveraged to monitor mpox. However, despite the establishment of numerous networks across different countries during the COVID-19 pandemic, there is limited reporting on whether the mpox surveillance was set up as a stand-alone or part of an existing WES early warning system. Determining how many SARS-CoV-2 wastewater surveillance networks were activated for mpox could further inform public health stakeholders on the extent of successful integration and adaptation of WES to monitor different pandemics.

Early insights to MPXV transmission through WES

Enhancements need to be made in molecular methods used to analyse the presence of MPXV in wastewater, given certain challenges that limit sequencing of wastewater such as the complexities and high diversity of the DNA extracts from the wastewater samples, and the technical limitations of MPXV signal detection in the qPCR method [6]. Such challenges thus make it difficult to obtain confirmatory information on qPCR MPXV signals from WES [6]. Some suggestions to address these challenges include using spiked wastewater or raw wastewater in the assessment of the sensitivity and specificity to validate protocols [25]. The use of qPCR in wastewater surveillance is advantageous as it offers rapid detection and can cover a wide range of viral groups and specific strains [36]. However, as reported earlier by [4, 6, 22], further research is needed to overcome challenges in the reliability and sensitivity of some qPCR assays and methods. These challenges can affect the utility of WES for mpox outbreak management by casting doubt on the accuracy of quantification results and will require additional measures to be taken to confirm the presence/absence of MPXV in wastewater. Additionally, a limited number of studies such as [6, 20, 23, 37] explored the different clades which vary in virulence and can provide deeper insights into the virus’s impact on public health. The molecular detection of MPXV in wastewater has largely relied on PCR and sequencing methods, indicating the value of obtaining rapid results from laboratory analysis.

The studies included in this scoping review collected wastewater samples from different locations, with each location revealing different dynamics that can inform mpox outbreak management. All studies included in this review exclusively conducted wastewater surveillance in sewered settings. This results in a gap in surveillance coverage in those communities not connected to sewered network. As such, more research is needed on WES for mpox in non-sewered communities to gain insights on how MPXV is transmitted in these settings. There were unique approaches applying WES at airports to track MPXV at ports of entry; however, these were not without limitations. de Jonge et al. [10] noted that sampling from airports which are small and highly specific catchment areas resulted in intermittent detection of MPXV in wastewater.

In other studies that explored WES in upstream and defined communities such as a hospital, there was a higher frequency in detection of MPXV in wastewater, and strong association between concentrations of MPXV in hospital wastewater and mpox cases. However, in their study, Sharkey et al. [38] noted that it would be difficult to identify which patients would have used the bathroom facilities in the hospital, with specific reference to patients who only visited the clinic or emergency room. When conducting WES for MPXV, particularly at upstream locations and defined settings, ethical considerations must be taken into account especially in communities where the detection of viruses in wastewater can have negative social or cultural impacts. In countries with discriminatory laws against same sex relations, WES for MPXV at certain spatial resolutions may identify mpox infected areas and cause harm to gay, bisexual and other men who have sex with men, who have been predominantly affected by the mpox outbreak.

Limitations of this study

Although this review screened for available research articles of WES for mpox, technical reports were not screened. This study also did not include reporting that was published on public facing dashboards that monitored MPXV in wastewater. Lastly, this scoping review did not conduct an appraisal of the included studies due to the time sensitive nature for obtaining evidence during the recent global mpox outbreaks. However, it must be noted that appraisals are not required for scoping reviews. To gain further insights on the performance of various molecular methods used to analyses MPXV in wastewater, study appraisals would be advisable to determine the methodological rigor of the assessment. This would also enhance the validity of assessments conducted by the different studies on which qPCR assays provide the most reliable results in the quantification of MPXV in wastewater.

Conclusion

This review found that MPXV can be detected in communities through WES. However, there is uncertainty regarding its capabilities to consistently and reliably identify mpox cases. There were instances where MPXV was detected in wastewater prior to clinical cases reporting, as well as having strong associations between the two datasets. Conversely, there were studies that also reported on negative associations between the two datasets, and WES being unable to detect MPXV despite there being high mpox cases. Further research is needed to test the reliability in detection during different stages of mpox outbreaks to better understand WES capabilities to accurately serve as an early warning system. Given that qPCR is the most common method used by laboratories, more research is needed on its sensitivity and accuracy to confidently rely on the quantification of MPXV in wastewater. To cover the gap in its surveillance capabilities, further research is also needed to demonstrate how WES can be implemented in unsewered settings to gain insight in these communities.
