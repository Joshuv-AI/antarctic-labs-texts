CPSVD: Enhancing Large Language Model Compression via Column-Preserving Singular Value Decomposition
Lin Xv, Jingsheng Gao, Xian Gao, Ting Liu, Yuzhuo Fu

Abstract




Abstract

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) faces a critical bottleneck in their immense size, necessitating efficient compression techniques. While Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) is a promising approach, existing SVD-based methods treat the entire parameter matrix uniformly, overlooking that SVD approximation errors vary significantly across different matrix parts, which often leads to suboptimal compression. To address this, we propose
C
olumn-
P
reserving
S
ingular
V
alue
D
ecomposition (CPSVD), a novel method that refines SVD-based LLM compression by intelligently segmenting the parameter matrix. Unlike traditional SVD, CPSVD identifies and directly preserves matrix columns with high decomposition errors, applying SVD only to columns with low decomposition errors, while precisely determining the optimal balance point between these two strategies to minimize error. Furthermore, leveraging the inherent heterogeneity in decomposition errors across different matrices within an LLM, CPSVD adaptively allocates non-uniform compression rates to modules within that layer, while adhering to a target layer-wise compression ratio, thereby further enhancing compression performance. Extensive experiments demonstrate that CPSVD consistently outperforms state-of-the-art SVD-based LLM compression methods, achieving lower perplexity and higher accuracy on zero-shot tasks.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have rapidly emerged as foundational technologies, demonstrating unprecedented performance across diverse tasks ranging from complex text generation and translation to sophisticated reasoning and problem-solving
(
Wan et al. 2024
)
. This remarkable progress is largely attributed to the scaling laws
(
Kaplan et al. 2020
)
, which have driven a relentless increase in model size, with leading LLMs such as GPT
(
Brown et al. 2020
)
and LLaMA
(
Touvron et al. 2023a
)
now comprising tens to hundreds of billions of parameters. While their colossal scale unlocks extraordinary capabilities, the immense computational and memory demands of these models severely restrict their widespread adoption
(
Chavan et al. 2024
)
, creating an urgent need for efficient model compression techniques
(
Zhou et al. 2024
)
.

Mainstream compression methods for LLMs include quantization
(
Frantar et al. 2022
;
Xiao et al. 2023
;
Mozaffari and Dehnavi 2024
)
, pruning
(
Frantar and Alistarh 2023
;
An et al. 2024
;
Fang et al. 2024
)
, knowledge distillation
(
Gu et al. 2024
;
Zhong et al. 2024
)
, and low-rank factorization
(
Hsu et al. 2021
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
. Among these, Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) has gained significant favor within low-rank factorization due to its inherent ability to reveal parameter importance and facilitate straightforward truncation. Methods like ASVD
(
Yuan et al. 2024
)
, SVD-LLM
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
, AdaSVD
(
Li et al. 2025
)
and ResSVD
(
Bai et al. 2025
)
have achieved notable progress.

Despite their specific implementation differences, a common characteristic of current SVD-based methods is that they leverage SVD to perform a global decomposition and approximation of the entire parameter matrix, thereby effectively preserving important information. However, we observe a critical phenomenon: different parts of a parameter matrix exhibit significantly varying sensitivity to SVD decomposition. After decomposition and truncation, some parts of the matrix may incur only minor truncation errors, while others suffer from non-negligible errors. This implies that a uniform decomposition of the entire parameter matrix can be costly and suboptimal. Furthermore, we find that different parameter matrices within an LLM also display considerable variations in their sensitivity to SVD decomposition, yet existing methods often apply the same compression ratio to different parameter matrices within the same Transformer layer, overlooking the optimization potential offered by these inter-matrix differences.

Building upon the aforementioned insights into the limitations of existing SVD-based compression methods, we propose CPSVD, a novel approach that differentiates in its treatment of various parts of the parameter matrix. CPSVD utilizes the ’columns of a parameter matrix’ as its fundamental processing unit, ensuring that the compressed results retain a sufficiently good structure for practical compression benefits. For the SVD decomposition of a matrix
W
,
W=U\Sigma V^{T}+W_{e}
(where
W_{e}
is the truncation error matrix), CPSVD innovatively analyzes the sensitivity of different columns to decomposition based on the
W_{e}
. Specifically, columns highly sensitive to decomposition (i.e., those that would incur larger approximation errors) are directly preserved, while the remaining, less sensitive columns undergo SVD. To determine the optimal balance point between these two processing strategies, we further leverage the monotonicity of truncation errors to efficiently determine the optimal proportion between these two parts, aiming to minimize the overall approximation error. Furthermore, we observe that at the same compression ratio, different parameter matrices within an LLM exhibit significant variations in their compression errors. Based on this finding, CPSVD, while maintaining a consistent overall compression ratio for a given Transformer layer, strategically transfers or compensates portions of the compression burden from matrices with larger intra-layer compression errors to those with smaller errors. This approach further optimizes the overall compression error at the layer level, thereby enhancing model performance.

Our key contributions are summarized as follows:

•

Column-Preserving Strategy
: We propose an innovative matrix compression method that effectively balances compression ratio and performance by directly preserving critical information columns and performing low-rank decomposition on the rest.

•

Module-Wise Compression
: Based on compression loss, we design an intra-layer adaptive scheme for allocating compression ratios, which significantly reduces the overall compression error.

•

Performance Advantage
: Experiments show that our method outperforms existing SVD-based compression techniques on multiple models and datasets.

2
Related Works

2.1
SVD-based Model Compression

SVD-based compression techniques reduce the number of parameters by decomposing a large matrix into three smaller matrices and retaining only the most significant components. Native SVD decomposition only focuses on the parameter matrix, ignoring data information, which leads to large errors. To address this, FWSVD
(
Hsu et al. 2021
)
attempts to leverage an approximate Fisher information matrix to gauge parameter importance, but this method is primarily designed for smaller models and usually requires retraining to recover performance. In the era of Large Language Models, ASVD
(
Yuan et al. 2024
)
was the first to propose an activation-aware, training-free SVD-based compression method. Building upon ASVD, SVD-LLM
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
theoretically provides a method to obtain the minimum error for the product of parameters and activations. AdaSVD
(
Li et al. 2025
)
employs an iterative optimization approach, alternately solving for the two decomposed low-rank matrices. ResSVD
(
Bai et al. 2025
)
, on the other hand, focuses on further decomposing the residual matrix formed by the truncation. While these methods strive to reduce truncation error from different perspectives, they share a common limitation: they are all premised on performing SVD decomposition on the entire parameter matrix, overlooking the crucial characteristic that certain parts of a matrix may not be well-suited for standard SVD decomposition.

2.2
Importance Evaluation

Parameter importance estimation is a widely adopted strategy in LLM pruning methods. In the field of unstructured pruning, SparseGPT
(
Frantar and Alistarh 2023
)
, based on OBS
(
Hassibi, Stork, and Wolff 1993
)
, utilizes the Hessian matrix to evaluate parameter importance for pruning and updating. Wanda
(
Sun et al. 2024
)
, on the other hand, adopts a simpler OBC
(
Le Cun, Denker, and Solla 1989
)
approach, directly defining parameter importance as the product of the parameter value and its corresponding activation. Subsequent work by BESA
(
Xu et al. 2023
)
further introduced a learning-based approach to optimize this evaluation process. For structured pruning, LLM-Pruner
(
Ma, Fang, and Wang 2023
)
evaluates parameter importance using gradient information, while ShortGPT
(
Men et al. 2025
)
treats the parameters of each Transformer layer as a whole, determining importance based on input-output similarity. Distinct from these feature-based methods, ShearedLLaMA
(
Xia et al. 2023
)
directly learns module importance using a mask-training approach. Although our method does not fall under the category of pruning, these parameter evaluation methods offer significant insights for our approach to measuring column importance.

2.3
Low Rank Plus Sparse

Merging low-rank factorization with sparsity is a promising approach. Recent work such as LoSparse
(
Li et al. 2023
)
combines low-rank factorization with a sparse column matrix through iterative training. Another method
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
, by solving constrained equations, decomposes the original matrix into a low-rank matrix and an unstructured sparse matrix; while this approach avoids training, its unstructured sparsity limits practical performance gains. Meanwhile, SLIM
(
Mozaffari and Dehnavi 2024
)
proposes a low-rank and sparse method with a focus on quantization awareness. Unlike these methods, where the low-rank factorization and sparse matrices are complementary, our approach treats them as independent components.

3
Preliminary

We review SVD’s application in low-rank approximation as a prior work
(
Wang et al. 2024
;
Yuan et al. 2024
)
. The problem is typically defined for a parameter matrix
\bm{W}
and input activations
\bm{X}
as finding a rank-
r
approximation matrix
\bm{W}_{r}
that minimizes the output error:

\begin{gathered}\bm{W}_{r}=\operatorname{argmin}_{\hat{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}\|\bm{W}\bm{X}-\hat{\bm{W}}\bm{X}\|_{F}\end{gathered}

SVD decomposes the original matrix
\bm{W}
as
\bm{W}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{T}
, where
\bm{U}
and
\bm{V}
are orthogonal matrices, and
\bm{\Sigma}
is a diagonal matrix containing singular values. According to the Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem
(
Bhatia 1997
)
, the optimal rank-
r
approximation is obtained by retaining the top
r
largest singular values and their corresponding singular vectors. This results in the truncated SVD approximation
\bm{W}_{r}=\bm{U}_{r}\bm{\Sigma}_{r}\bm{V}_{r}^{T}
.

This approximation can be represented as the product of two smaller matrices,
\bm{W}_{r}=\bm{U}^{\prime}_{r}(\bm{V}^{\prime}_{r})^{T}
, where the singular values are distributed between the two matrices for numerical stability. This gives
\bm{W}_{r}=(\bm{U}_{r}\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}_{r}})(\bm{V}_{r}\sqrt{\bm{\Sigma}_{r}})^{T}
.

4
Method

The core workflow of CPSVD is illustrated in Figure
1
. The method first assigns a global compression ratio to each layer. Subsequently, for different modules within a layer, it dynamically allocates a corresponding module pruning rate based on the magnitude of their decomposition error. Based on this, we determine the columns to be retained within each module and perform a low-rank decomposition on these selected columns. The detailed algorithm for the column retention strategy is elaborated in Section
4.2
, while the compression ratio allocation scheme is introduced in Section
4.3
. The pseudocode for the algorithm is presented in Appendix A.

4.1
Motivation

Given
\bm{W}_{r}=\bm{U}^{\prime}_{r}(\bm{V}^{\prime}_{r})^{T}
, the error matrix due to truncation is
\bm{W}_{e}=\bm{W}-\bm{W}_{r}
, and the corresponding truncation loss is
L=\|\bm{W}\bm{X}-\bm{W}_{r}\bm{X}\|_{F}=\|\bm{W}_{e}\bm{X}\|_{F}
. A core insight of our method is that, while
L
provides a global error metric, the approximation error generated by SVD decomposition is highly non-uniform across different columns of the matrix. To quantify this non-uniformity and precisely measure each column’s contribution to the total truncation loss, we naturally refine the truncation loss to a column-wise level. We define the truncation error for the
i
-th column,
L_{i}
, as the projection error of the
i
-th column of the error matrix
\bm{W}_{e}
, denoted
\bm{W}_{e,i}
, with respect to the input
\bm{X}
:

L_{i}=\|\bm{W}_{e,i}\bm{X}_{i,:}\|_{F}

where
\bm{X}_{i,:}
is the subvector of
\bm{X}
corresponding to the
i
-th column of parameters. As illustrated in Figure
2
, the
L_{i}
values indeed exhibit significant variance across different columns, with a minority of columns showing substantially higher truncation errors than the average.

4.2
Column-Preserving Strategy

To address the heterogeneous sensitivity across columns in the weight matrix, we propose a hybrid compression strategy that combines explicit column preservation with low-rank approximation. Let
W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be the original weight matrix. We aim to select a subset of columns to preserve, and approximate the rest via truncated SVD.

Let
S\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
denote the index set of columns to be preserved, with cardinality
c=|S|
, and let
\bar{S}
be its complement, i.e.,
\bar{S}=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\setminus S
. Then we may write
W
as:

W=[W_{S}\;\;W_{\bar{S}}],

where
W_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times c}
consists of the preserved columns, and
W_{\bar{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(n-c)}
contains the columns to be compressed via SVD.

We then construct the compressed matrix
W^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
by defining:

W^{\prime}[:,i]=\begin{cases}W[:,i],&\text{if }i\in S,\\
\left(U\Sigma V^{\top}\right)[:,j],&\text{if }i=\bar{S}_{j},\end{cases}

Formally, the optimization problem can be expressed as:

\begin{split}\arg\min_{c,S}\quad&\left\|\bm{W}\bm{X}-\bm{W}^{\prime}(S,r(c))\bm{X}\right\|_{F}^{2}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad&S\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\},\\
&|S|=c,\\
&mc+(m+n-c)\cdot r(c)\leq B\end{split}

where
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the original weight matrix,

\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
is the input matrix,

S
is the index set of preserved columns with cardinality
c
,

r(c)
is the SVD rank applied to the remaining columns,

\bm{W}^{\prime}(S,r(c))
is the resulting hybrid compressed matrix,
and
B
is the total parameter budget.

Solving the optimization problem exactly requires searching over all possible values of both
c
and
S
. While feasible in principle, such exhaustive enumeration is computationally intractable due to its combinatorial complexity.

To make the problem tractable, we first observe that if the value of
c
is fixed, the remaining challenge is to select the optimal subset
S
of size
c
. However, this is still a combinatorial problem involving
\binom{n}{c}
possible subsets.

To overcome this, we note the purpose of choosing
S
is to preserve the columns that contribute most to the reconstruction loss when compressed. This naturally suggests a greedy strategy: Select the
c
columns with the largest truncation errors. Let the sorted list of errors be denoted by

L_{(1)}\geq L_{(2)}\geq\cdots\geq L_{(n)}.

Then for any given
c
, we define the preserved set as

S_{c}=\{i\mid L_{i}\in\{L_{(1)},\ldots,L_{(c)}\}\}.

This reduces the original bivariate optimization over
(c,S)
to a univariate optimization over
c
alone.

However, directly evaluating all possible values of
c
is still expensive. To enable efficient search, we analyze the structure of the total reconstruction loss as a function of
c
. Let this loss be denoted by:

\mathcal{L}(c)\approx\mathcal{L}_{0}+A(c)-B(c),

where:

•

\mathcal{L}_{0}
is the baseline loss when no columns are preserved;

•

A(c)
represents the additional loss due to reducing the SVD rank to
r(c)
;

•

B(c)=\sum_{i=1}^{c}L_{(i)}
is the cumulative gain from preserving the top-
c
columns.

This decomposition reveals two competing effects:

•

As
c
increases,
B(c)
increases monotonically, but with diminishing marginal gains due to the sorting of
L_{i}
;

•

Meanwhile, since the total number of parameters is fixed, increasing
c
reduces the allowable rank
r
, resulting in more singular values being discarded. Moreover, as the discarded singular values are monotonically increasing, the additional loss
A(c)
increases monotonically with
c
, and its marginal cost also increases.

Based on the above analysis, the loss function
L(c)
falls into one of three categories: monotonically decreasing, monotonically increasing, or first decreasing and then increasing. For such functions with well-behaved monotonicity, ternary search can be applied to efficiently find the optimal
c
.

Although the above solution method involves multiple approximations, these approximations are reasonable within the problem context. Figure
3
shows the relationship between the loss and the number of preserved columns in practice. The observed monotonicity aligns well with our expectations, which validates the rationality of our approximations.

Moreover, from Figure
3
we observe that the benefit of column preservation varies across different matrices. For the O matrix, the gain can exceed 90%, while for the down matrix, the gain is about one third. For other matrices, the gain remains within 10%. Additionally, the optimal number of preserved columns c differs significantly among matrices, reflecting their distinct characteristics.

4.3
Module-Wise compression ratio

Existing research has explored the heterogeneity of compression rate allocation across different Transformer layers
(
Yin et al. 2024
;
Lin et al. 2024
)
. These methods predominantly determine layer-wise compression rates based on intrinsic macroscopic model properties, such as outlier-based approaches
(
Yin et al. 2024
)
or input-output similarity-based methods
(
Lin et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2025
)
. However, these inter-layer allocation strategies are often decoupled from the actual performance of specific compression methods on different modules, thus overlooking the potential for further optimizing compression efficacy. Building upon this, we delve deeper into uncovering the disparities among intra-layer modules, even when a layer’s total parameter budget is already determined.

For the problem of layer-wise compression ratio, we draw inspiration from existing work
(
Lin et al. 2024
)
and define the importance of a layer as
s=1-\cos(\bm{x}_{\text{in}},\bm{x}_{\text{out}})
, where
\bm{x}_{\text{in}}
and
\bm{x}_{\text{out}}
denote the input and output of the layer, respectively. The compression ratio for each layer is then assigned according to
r_{i}=Nr\cdot\text{softmax}(-s_{i}/t)
, where
N
is the number of layers,
r
is the overall target compression ratio, and t is a temperature parameter, with a default value of 0.1.

Within each layer, we observe that constituent modules exhibit significant variations in their relative errors after SVD decomposition. We define the relative error for module
{W}_{i}
as
E_{i}=\|(W_{i}-\hat{W}_{i})X_{i}\|_{F}/\|W_{i}X_{i}\|_{F}
, where
{W}_{i}
is the module’s original parameter matrix,
\hat{W}_{i}
is its approximation, and
X_{i}
is its input. As depicted in Figure
4
, this error discrepancy is clearly evident, highlighting the great promise of distributing differentiated compression rates to various modules.

To minimize the overall intra-layer approximation error while adhering to the layer’s total parameter budget, we propose module-wise compression rate allocation scheme. Assume a transformer layer has a target compression ratio
r
. This layer comprises multiple modules
M_{i}
with original parameter counts
P_{i}
. We first measure each module’s relative error
E_{i}
and derive an error rank
I_{i}
from it.

We define each module’s importance weighting factor
V_{i}
as:

V_{i}=\frac{P_{i}}{e^{I_{i}/\alpha}}

where

. Notably,

remains small at low compression ratios but increases significantly at high compression ratios. This dynamically prevents excessively aggressive pruning of certain modules under high-compression scenarios, thereby maintaining the method’s stability across a wide range of compression rates.

The layer’s total parameters to be removed is
\Delta P_{\text{total}}=r\times\sum_{j}P_{j}
. We allocate this total removal burden among modules based on their
V_{i}
factors. The number of parameters to be compressed from each module
\Delta P_{i}
is calculated as:

\Delta P_{i}=\Delta P_{\text{total}}\times\frac{V_{i}}{\sum_{j}V_{j}}

Here, a larger
V_{i}
indicates that the module requires more parameter compression. This allocation strategy means that modules deemed more important (higher
V_{i}
) will contribute more parameters to the total removed budget, effectively achieving the target compression by reducing parameters from modules that are less critical or more amenable to compression.

Finally, the adaptive compression ratio
r_{i}
for each module is calculated as:

r_{i}=\Delta P_{i}/P_{i}

This definition comprehensively considers both the parameter count and error sensitivity of different modules, resulting in a superior error distribution and enhanced overall model performance while maintaining the overall layer-level compression budget.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a new model compression method called CPSVD, which leverages a differentiated SVD decomposition. The key insight of our approach is the observation that under an input-aware setting, different columns of a matrix exhibit varying SVD truncation errors. Based on this, we introduce a
column-preserving strategy
to further reduce the overall compression error. Furthermore, our method uses the compression error to guide the allocation of
compression ratios for different modules
. Experimental results across multiple models and datasets demonstrate that CPSVD consistently outperforms existing vanilla SVD methods, providing strong evidence that our column-preserving strategy offers a stable and significant improvement for SVD-based model compression.

Appendix D
Theoretical Analysis of Efficiency

To analyze the computational and storage efficiency of our method, consider a linear layer with weight matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and input matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
. A standard dense matrix multiplication
WX
requires
\mathcal{O}(mnd)
floating-point operations (FLOPs). To reduce this cost, a common approach is to apply low-rank approximation by decomposing
W\approx UV^{\top}
, where
U\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
,
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}
, and
r\ll\min(m,n)
. This leads to an efficient computation
WX\approx U(V^{\top}X)
with complexity:

\mathcal{O}(nrd+mrd)=\mathcal{O}((m+n)rd).

Given a target compression ratio
\rho\in(0,1)
, the number of parameters is constrained by
r(m+n)\leq\rho mn
, yielding:

r=\frac{\rho mn}{m+n},

and the total computation becomes
\mathcal{O}(\rho mnd)
, which is linearly reduced with respect to
\rho
.

In contrast, our hybrid strategy divides the columns of
W
into two parts:
- The first
c
columns are retained exactly, forming
W_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times c}
;
- The remaining
n-c
columns are compressed using low-rank SVD, yielding a factorization
U\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times r}
,
V\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-c)\times r}
.

Let
X=\begin{bmatrix}X_{c}\quad X_{r}\end{bmatrix}
, where
X_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{c\times d}
and
X_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-c)\times d}
are the corresponding partitions of the input. Then, the output becomes:

WX\approx W_{c}X_{c}+U(V^{\top}X_{r}).

The computation cost of this hybrid method is given by:

\mathcal{C}(c,r)=d\cdot\left[mc+r(n-c+m)\right],

which includes
mc\cdot d
FLOPs for the exact part and
r(n-c)d+mrd
FLOPs for the low-rank part.

To ensure the total number of parameters remains under the compression budget
\rho mn
, we impose:

mc+r(m+n-c)\leq\rho mn,

which can be rearranged to solve for the admissible rank:

r=\frac{\rho mn-mc}{m+n-c}.

Substituting this expression for
r
into
\mathcal{C}(c,r)
shows that the total computation remains approximately:

\mathcal{C}(c)=d\cdot\left[mc+\frac{\rho mn-mc}{m+n-c}(n-c+m)\right]=\rho mn,

which simplifies back to roughly
\mathcal{O}(\rho mnd)
, consistent with the baseline low-rank method. Thus, under fixed parameter budget
\rho mn
, optimizing over
c
does not change the total computation but adjusts the allocation between exact and low-rank components.

For storage efficiency, the baseline low-rank method requires storing
r(m+n)
parameters. In the hybrid case, the retained part contributes
mc
parameters, and the low-rank part contributes
r(m+n-c)
, leading to:

\text{Total Parameters}=mc+r(m+n-c)\leq\rho mn,

with equality if the budget is fully utilized. This confirms that our method preserves both the computational and storage constraints.

In summary, the standard low-rank method based on SVD can be viewed as a special case of our hybrid strategy with
c=0
, where all columns are compressed via low-rank decomposition. Under a given compression ratio
\rho
, both approaches share the same leading-order computational and storage complexity
\mathcal{O}(\rho mnd)
, but differ in how this budget is utilized. The hybrid method generalizes the low-rank approach by allowing partial column preservation, offering a flexible trade-off: it retains important columns exactly while applying compression only to the remaining less critical ones. This selective treatment may lead to improved empirical performance, especially when certain columns contain disproportionately important information.
