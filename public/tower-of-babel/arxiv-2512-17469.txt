Super-resolution wavefront reconstruction in adaptive-optics with pyramid sensors
Carlos M. Correia, Charlotte Z. Bond, João Aveiro, François Leroux, Cédric Taïssir Heritier, Christophe Vérinaud

Abstract
“Super-resolution” (SR) refers to a combination of optical design and signal processing techniques jointly employed to obtain reconstructed wave-fronts at a higher-resolution from multiple low-resolution samples, overcoming the intrinsic limitations of the latter. After compelling examples have been provided on multi-Shack-Hartman (SH) wave-front sensor (WFS) adaptive optics systems performing atmospheric tomography with laser guide star probes, we broaden the SR concept to pyramid sensors (PyWFS) with a single sensor and a natural guide star. We revisit the analytic PyWFS diffraction model to claim two aspects: i) that we can reconstruct spatial frequencies beyond the natural Shannon-Nyquist frequency imposed by the detector pixel size and/or ii) that the PyWFS can be used to measure amplitude aberrations (at the origin of scintillation). SR offers the possibility to control a higher actuator density deformable mirror from seemingly fewer samples, the quantification of which is one of the goals of this paper. A super-resolved PyWFS is more resilient to mis-registration, lifts alignment requirements and improves performance (against aliasing and other spurious modes AO systems are poorly sensitive to) with only a factor up to 2 increased real-time computational burden.

We elaborate on an argument first alluded to in

Unlike in SH-WFS-based adaptive-optics (AO) systems where multiple

PyWFS, every quadrant applies a different transformation. Historically it has been thought that each and every sample is required
for proper signal reconstruction. As we shall see, under certain
conditions the information
contained in opposite PyWFS quadrants is redundant. We will lift
these conditions and claim more information can be reconstructed out
of the same number of (effectively distinct) samples, provided the system is
optically designed to allow for it.

In the absence of amplitude aberrations (leading ultimately to
scintillation), the far-field, Fraunhofer-propagated EF at the
tip of the PyWFS resulting from the Fourier transform of the input EF
is Hermitian

.Therefore, the PyWFS’ diagonally opposite quadrants measure essentially two identical copies of the diffracted
intensity pattern

Building on this, we can state one of the paper’s key ideas: by offsetting the sampling grids of each quadrant – achieved by tilting the electric field slightly in each quadrant – we generate interleaved sampled frames. This enables the reconstruction of spatial frequencies beyond the native Shannon-Nyquist limit, effectively achieving super-resolution.

In other words, what in the SH-WFS case were four distinct measurement channels correspond, in the PyWFS case, to the four nominal diffraction intensity patterns formed at the respective re-imaged pupil planes. The effect of offsetting the lenslet arrays in SH-WFS designs is, in the PyWFS, achieved by offsetting the sampling pixel coordinates of each quadrant relative to one another, such that their samples, when projected onto the aperture, becomes interleaved by a fraction of a pixel.
A practical means to readily obtain this effect is by adjusting (by design) the PyWFS apex angles. The
same can in principle be achieved by rotating the pyramid optic with
respect to the detector. Sampling at equidistant points should lead to
near optimal performance as shown in
Champagnat et al. 2009
though, and thus we will focus on this setup only.

As in the SH-WFS, it is almost intuitive to advocate an offset by half
a PyWFS detector pixel. With this we can extend the
effective Nyquist-Shannon cut-off frequency to a larger upper limit, with the
following foreseen
advantages:

•

further mitigation of the detrimental effect of measurement aliasing on AO performance

•

relaxation of the alignment constraints: this may lead to potential advantage in probing high-spatial frequency signals,
such as the effects caused by the presence of telescope struts

•

potential use of fewer pixel counts to drive any given DM

We show also that the redundant PyWFS measurements can instead be
used to estimate amplitude errors, with no strict need for offsetting. This is
so because in the presence of amplitude errors, the EF after propagation
looses its Hermitian status. By combining the Hermitian with the
non-Hermitian responses we obtain a full-rank transformation out of
which both phase and amplitude can be estimated.

However, if one is interested in simultaneously estimating both
high-resolution phase and amplitude, a trade-off is necessary for the corresponding linear
system of equations becomes under-determined. The reason is as
follows. Suppose the input field is sampled with
n^{2}_{s}

points. In order to estimate all of its spatial frequency content,
4n^{2}_{s}

PyWFS intensity measurements are required, from which
2n^{2}_{s}
are the
minimal set to estimate the wavefront
aberrations (
n_{s}^{2}
for each of the ’x’ and ’y’ components). The remaining

can be used either to estimate
spatial frequencies above the original Shannon-Nyquist cut-off

amplitude aberrations. Estimating
4n^{2}_{s}

Either of these extended information cases, however, requires processing the diffracted intensity patterns

Beyond the fundamental principles enunciated here, more investigation

2
Super-resolution wavefront reconstruction

We will distinguish two forms of super-resolution:

1.

Optical – where the diffraction limit of imaging systems is
transcended by swapping spatial-frequency bands. The fundamental
diffraction limit still holds though. A practical example is fibre
nulling interferometry
Serabyn et al. 2019

2.

Image processing (reconstruction) – where the resolution of digital imaging
sensors is enhanced by jointly employing
geometric
and

statistical
approaches combined with a well crafted sampling
strategy introducing some form of
diversity
(over time, space –
phase and/or amplitude,
wavelength… or a combination thereof)

In this paper we will focus solely on image processing SR. These refer to a set of common techniques in computational imaging to combine multiple low-resolution (LR) samples to obtain higher-resolution data beyond the Shannon-Nyquist cut-off frequency of the LR samples. SR can therefore
overcome to some extent the resolution limitations of LR systems.

We have argued in
Oberti et al. 2022
that SR is naturally granted by the
geometry of tomographic systems employing several wavefront sensors
(WFS) along as many distinct (i.e. non-collinear) lines-of-sight. When
back-propagating the overlapping sampling grids from the pupil plane
to different ranges, intrinsic offsets between the samples are
generated which is the key element to obtain a SR effect. This purely
geometric effect is in practice combined with a statistical one due to
the use of regularised reconstruction. The latter can be used to
estimate frequencies beyond the cut-off even with a single sampling
grid. The combination of both is perceived and shown to provide the
best performance results one could expect.

2.1
Generalised concept of
diversity

SR operates on fused data from all available lower-resolution WFS measurements provided each contains some form of unique phase information – the basic pre- requisite for super resolution.

We further argue that one such effect can be thought of as a
diversity effect. In classical phase-diversity one introduces a
longitudinal phase along the propagation path
Gerchberg 1974
; the
technique we explore here introduces it transversely with respect to
the propagation. It is this unique (and
a-priori
-known, either by design or calibration) offset phase information from each of the
LR measurements which allows us to go beyond the cut-off sample
frequency of each sample grid taken individually.

In order to introduce a unique phase information in the pupil plane – where classically all WFS grids perfectly overlap –
Oberti et al. 2022
suggest offsetting the SH-WFS lenslet array by fractions of a sub-aperture width.

3.1
Measuring amplitude aberrations

Let’s now look at the case with amplitude aberrations that we take as

A(x,y)=P(x,y)e^{\chi}

(9)

where
\chi
is the so-called log-amplitude aberration term.

In this general case, the Hermitian property of the electric field in
the focal plane is lost since the even/odd symmetry of the
real/imaginary parts is no longer established; as it stands,

E(x,y)=P(x,y)e^{\chi+{i\psi(x,y)}}

(10)

leading to a complex-valued exponent.

A particular and successful application of these properties is the use
of phase aberrations to cancel out amplitude errors in high-contrast,
coronagraphic systems – albeit only on half of the
Fourier plane – in what is the fundamental principle of a technique
called speckle nulling

;
Give’On 2009
;
Martinache et al. 2014
.

The case we are interested in is the PyWFS’s ability to
measure both phase and amplitude errors.
This can now be established since the phase and amplitude errors
manifest respectively as
Hermitian and non-Hermitian components of the Fourier-transformed
electric field.

spatial derivative.

It is a small stretch to develop the ”slope” measurements starting from
Eq. (
25
) as

s(x)=|U^{+}(x)|^{2}-|U^{-}(x)|^{2}

(28)

By noting that
U^{\pm}(x)=D\mp iT
, i.e. the
direct
and Hilbert-
transformed

parts, the squared modulus develops as

|U^{+}(x)|^{2}=|D|^{2}+|T|^{2}+iD^{*}T-iDT^{*}

(29)

and

|U^{-}(x)|^{2}=|D|^{2}+|T|^{2}-iD^{*}T+iDT^{*}

(30)

from which

s(x)=2i\left(D^{*}T-DT^{*}\right)

(31)

since the real-valued quadratic terms all cancel out. The resulting difference
of subtracting a variable from its complex conjugate is that the
even terms vanish yet the odd terms add together. By developing the
complex exponentials in
cos()
and
isin()
and using the linearity
property of the integration we end up with
Vérinaud 2004

s(x)\propto\int\frac{sin(\phi(x^{\prime})-\phi(x))}{x^{\prime}-x}\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}

(32)

Computing the slopes-maps in the traditional way thus means getting rid of the even powers of
the intensity. Therefore, it is commonly assumed that this results in
a more linear operation after cancelling out the even non-linear terms. However, in practice we calibrate the system using small wavefront aberrations well within the linear range of the sensor and so encoded within the AO control matrix is the differential nature of the linear behaviour. Therefore any common terms (i.e. the even intensity powers) are filtered out during the reconstruction process and we do not observe a significant difference in linearity between the slopes-map and intensity methods.

We should note as well that in the absence of amplitude aberrations
the intensity diffraction patterns across the PyWFS diagonals carry
redundant information. The use of a reduced-rank slopes-maps (2x for
the ’x’ and the ’y’) is then justified in the idealised case. To be
able to extract higher frequency content from the PyWFS signals,
full-rank processing shall be required.

Up until this point we have not introduced the discrete nature of the
measurements. We will explore this in the next section.

4.2
Super-resolved PyWFS from its discrete samples

In practice the diffracted PyWFS intensity patterns are sampled onto a
regular pixel digital detector. Historically the pyramid optic is designed in such a
way that the re-imaged pupils have an integer number of pixels in
between their reference coordinates such that pixels in corresponding
re-imaged detector pupils sample the same portion of the aperture. This
equivalent sampling
of each pupil image is required for AO control using the slopes-maps and in practice requires tight tolerances on the PyWFS optics, typically requiring pupil image precision of
\sim 0.1
detector pixels.

Frequencies beyond the natural
Nyquist-Shannon cut-off create aliased
diffraction patterns once sampled, adding sine and cosine functions to the pupil images at the aliased
frequencies. For the equivalent sampling case these spatial frequencies cannot be recovered and instead appear as errors at lower spatial frequencies in the wavefront measurement.

If however the re-imaged pupils are not perfectly separated and indexed on the same
coordinates, corresponding pixels end up sampling different portions of the
aperture. This offset, which was traditionally thought to be
detrimental to PyWFS-based AO performance, is the key element that
provides the PyWFS SR capabilities.

In the equivalent sampling case reduced-rank computation of the slopes does not result in any loss of information due to the factor 2 redundancy (the diagonal PyWFS diffraction patterns carry the same phase information). In the offset case this is no longer true as the diagonal diffraction patterns are sampled at different locations.
The real terms in Eqs. (
29
) and(
30
) no longer cancel out when computing the slopes maps. Further to that, the difference between a term and its complex conjugate is no longer proportional to the sinus of the difference but has even terms in the form of
cos()
functions.
Use of the slopes results in a loss in sensitivity at higher frequencies as measurements at different locations in the pupil are combined. To retain the full phase information and take advantage of the SR effect requires full-rank processing, either through the direct use of the normalised intensities or using two additional linear combinations of intensities
Deo et al. 2018
.

The path forward is to avoid the linearly recombination of the diffraction
intensity patterns with the subsequent, unavoidable loss of information but to treat
them separately reconstructing directly from twice as many
measurements (with respect to the use of slopes).

The rest of the argument is straightforward. Discretisation is represented by the convolution by
the pixel averaging function
\Pi(x/d)
and sample function (a Dirac comb
function
III(x/d)
) we get

\overline{i}_{\pm}=\left(|U^{\pm}(x)|^{2}\star\Pi(x/d)\right)III(x/d)

(33)

If however we sample each side of the diffracted field past the
knife-edge with half a pixel offset, we get the samples

\begin{array}[]{cl}\overline{i}_{+}=&\left(|U^{+}(x)|^{2}\star\Pi(x/d)\right)III(x/d)\\
\overline{i}_{-}=&\left(|U^{-}(x)|^{2}\star\Pi(x/d)\right)III(x/d+d/2)\end{array}

(34)

Much as in the SH-WFS case, the half pixel offset provides a measurement diversity that is exploited via reconstruction to estimate frequencies beyond the pixel-imposed spatial-frequency cut-off
frequency, past which aliasing occurs, allowing for an equivalent cut-off of

\displaystyle f_{max}^{SR}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}=2f_{max}\hskip 20.0pt\text{1-D
case}

(35)

\displaystyle f_{max}^{SR}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{d^{2}/2}}=\sqrt{2}f_{max}\hskip 20.0pt\text{2-D case}

(36)

reaching a larger effective reconstructed wavefront cut-off frequency, i.e.
f_{max}^{SR}>f_{max}
.

At this stage a subtlety of uttermost importance needs to be
highlighted. It is at the origin of Eq. (
35
).
When extending the super-resolution reasoning to the 2D
case, we see that the SH-WFS and the PyWFS cases are fundamentally
different. Whereas in the former case we can double the reconstruction cut-off frequency by offsetting a minimum of four sensors by 1/4 pixel along diagonal stretches, this does not hold for a single PyWFS. This is simply because, after offsetting, we can obtain at most a 2x increase in the number of independent measurements — that is, the two additional, redundant quadrants — falling short of the minimum required for proper in-band reconstruction. In practice, this means that only twice as many modes will be properly reconstructed, as will be shown in the physical-optics simulations in
section §
6
.

Experimental validation: Proposal for a test setup

The PyWFS designs done hitherto do not contemplate the offsets required to perform SR unless for unwanted reasons such as distortions and optical misalignments.

Here we propose a demonstration that can be performed by seemingly any existing PyWFS-based AO system. It consists in binning down the PyWFS signals with a differential offset in between and seek to control
\sqrt{2}
as many modes as those granted by the binned-down PyWFS signals. This is straightforward to implement as linear algebra operations and thus they should hit no bottleneck in the real-time processing, provided it was sized properly for the unbinned case. Figure

We won’t discuss systems with bespoke pipelining features implemented to take advantage of specific detector read modes and to speed up slope computation. Not only we advocate the use of the pixel intensities directly, not a linear combination thereof, using linear algebra methods to compute the slopes results in a much more flexible system that can accommodate several sampling schemes, slopes and full-pixels altogether. The only modification is that the normalisation by the intensity can be done with the value registered in a previous sampling interval to avoid waiting for the full detector to be read before one such calculation can take place, therefore shattering the pipelinability of the scheme.

8
Conclusions

In this paper we showed the following.

1.

The classical 4-sided PyWFS can be used in super-resolution mode, in which
case we can reconstruct spatial frequencies beyond the
Shannon-Nyquist cut-off frequency imposed by the pixel size.

2.

Due to the number of available redundant measurements across
diagonally-opposed quadrants,
adopting a SR configuration by differentially offsetting the re-imaged
pupils, we can reconstruct (up to) twice as many modes
as compared to the non-SR configuration

3.

the nominal 4-sided PyWFS signals can be
decomposed into Hermitian and non-Hermitian parts,
effectively granting the possibility of estimating
simultaneously both phase
and amplitude aberrations as the system of
equations is full-rank. In an astronomical high-contrast
imaging context, amplitude errors are quite prominent. For
example
Guyon 2005
states
In an OPD-only AO
correction, the uncorrected scintillation C1 dominates by
far the achievable PSF contrast within the central 2”

. Applications where a single PyWFS drives two DMs conjugated
to different planes could therefore be envisioned. The limited
linearity range of the PyWFS may limit its use on other
applications; phase and amplitude aberrations are of
importance in medical imaging, microscopy and space situational awareness

4.

Both SR and amplitude aberrations estimation cannot be used jointly, though. This
would lead to an under-determined system of equations, where
one would attempt to estimate twice more unknowns than there
are linearly independent equations. In practice a trade-off
must be found depending on design constraints and performance
requirements.

5.

Either/or SR and amplitude measurement can only be made using
the so-called
intensity-maps processing, which effectively requires a larger
reconstruction matrix for a given PyWFS configuration. This is
compensated fully by its ability to control twice as many modes,
granting effectively a balanced complexity increase.

Candidate systems which could use this technique are Keck’s HAKA
project, where the
HO-DM with 58x58 actuators could be driven by a lower order PyWFS but also systems on the GSMT’s where the
scope is not driving a higher-density DM from fewer measurements but
instead raise robustness and system reliability provided the current
designs and sizings that have been adopted.

From the several questions this work raises, one that could cater to
existing PyWFS AO not supporting super-resolution is whether estimating
jointly phase and amplitude could lead to improved
phase correction. Although a fully-fledged simulation featuring
Fresnel propagation to generate electric-field aberrations in addition
to phase-only aberrations could be used to clarify this question, we
invite the community operating PyWFS-based AO systems to test out this
assumption.
