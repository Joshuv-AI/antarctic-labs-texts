Self-Reference and Atrial Pace Mapping With iPASO Identify Multiple Non-Pulmonary vein Triggers in Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Yuki Kato, Yuichiro Sagawa, Kazuya Murata, Hirofumi Arai, Atsuhito Oda, Junichi Kishaba, Yumi Yasui, Tomoyuki Kouguchi, Hideki Arima, Yasuteru Yamauchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPulmonary vein (PV) isolation is a well-established and effective treatment for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF). However, in some patients, AF recurrence is driven by non-PV triggers. Identifying these triggers is crucial for achieving long-term ablation success, but their localization can be challenging.Case SummaryThis report presents the case of paroxysmal AF in which the combined use of self-reference mapping and atrial pace mapping using Intracardiac Pace Match Scoring (iPASO) enabled successful identification and ablation of multiple non-PV triggers, resulting in a satisfactory outcome.DiscussionTo the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case in which multiple non-PV triggers were successfully eliminated through the combined use of self-reference mapping and iPASO mapping.Take-Home MessageA multimodal approach, combining self-reference mapping and iPASO mapping, may be a useful strategy for identifying and managing non-PV triggers in clinical practice.

History of Presentation

A 79-year-old woman was referred to our hospital in 2024 for repeat catheter ablation of atrial fibrillation (AF). Remote home monitoring demonstrated an increase in AF burden to more than 50% despite treatment with maximally tolerated dosage of antiarrhythmic drugs. She was otherwise hemodynamically stable at presentation.Take-Home Messages•Each mapping method has distinct advantages and limitations.•Self-reference mapping is preferable when immediate recurrence of AF is present as Intracardiac Pace Match Scoring mapping requires pacing during sinus rhythm and cannot be applied otherwise, whereas Intracardiac Pace Match Scoring mapping is advantageous when premature atrial contractions or atrial tachycardia episodes are infrequent.•Combining both approaches facilitates efficient and accurate identification of multiple non-pulmonary vein triggers.

Past Medical History

The patient previously underwent catheter ablation for symptomatic paroxysmal AF at another institution in 2013. The procedure included radiofrequency-guided pulmonary vein (PV) isolation (PVI) and cavotricuspid isthmus ablation. Although PVI was successful, 2 non-PV triggers were suspected to arise from the left atrial posterior wall (LAPW) and right atrial septum. At the time of the initial ablation, high-density mapping catheters were not available, which may have limited the precise localization of non-PV triggers when using only conventional mapping catheters. Sick sinus syndrome subsequently developed, necessitating implantation of a permanent pacemaker. Long-term rhythm control had been maintained with aprindine (40 mg) and bepridil (100 mg).

Differential Diagnosis

The major considerations included recurrence of AF due to PV reconnection, non-PV triggers arising from the LAPW or right atrial septum, and progression of the atrial substrate due to structural remodeling.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 72% and a left atrial diameter of 40 mm. Computed tomography in the prone position performed before the procedure excluded intracardiac thrombus and revealed no structural abnormalities. The left PVs formed a common trunk, and the left atrial volume was measured at 108 mL.

Management (Medical/Interventions)

After obtaining informed consent, catheter ablation with QDOT Micro catheter (Biosense Webster), contact force sensing, irrigation, and radiofrequency catheterization were performed under deep sedation with propofol. All antiarrhythmic drugs were discontinued 1 week before the procedure. Two electrode catheters were positioned in the coronary sinus (CS) and along the tricuspid annulus from the lateral to septal regions. Intracardiac electrical cardioversion was repeatedly attempted using 10-J shocks via a BeeAT catheter (Japan Lifeline) in the CS, and a wide Halo duodecapolar catheter (Japan Lifeline) was placed along the tricuspid annulus. Because the patient remained in AF, direct current cardioversion was attempted. Sinus rhythm was briefly restored, but immediate recurrence of AF (IRAF) occurred (AF2) (Figure 1A). Voltage mapping during AF1 was performed using an electroanatomical mapping system (CARTO 3; Biosense Webster) with a lattice-type, 36-electrode multipolar catheter (OPTRELL; Biosense Webster). Although the left PVs remained isolated, reconnection was observed in the right superior and inferior PVs as well as the right PV carina (Figure 1B). Based on findings from the prior session, non-PV triggers originating from the LAPW and septum were suspected. Positioning OPTRELL at the left atrial septum allowed recording of the earliest activation during AF1 initiation, and self-reference mapping precisely localized the origin (Figure 2A). Self-reference mapping was performed by repeatedly using cardioversion and recording the earliest activation site (EASs). OPTRELL was sequentially positioned at each EAS, marked as a reference, and the process was repeated. A cluster of reference tags was ultimately identified, indicating the origin of the non-PV trigger. The EAS was identified at the anterior right PV carina between the right superior and inferior PVs, and extensive re-PVI was performed. After completion of re-PVI, the arrhythmia terminated and sinus rhythm was restored (Figure 2B). After AF1 termination (IRAF status), a second short-lasting, self-terminating AF (AF2) and premature atrial contractions (PACs) were intermittently observed. The EAS of AF2 was localized to the left atrial inferior wall, with arrhythmia triggered by a P-on-T phenomenon and a coupling interval of 200 ms (Figure 3). Contiguous ablation along the inferior left atrial wall, precisely localized by self-reference mapping, resulted in AF2 termination (Figure 4).Figure 1The First Nonpulmonary Vein Trigger (AF1) and Voltage Map(A) The initial intracardiac electrogram showed atrial fibrillation (AF). PAC firing that originated from the proximal electrodes of the Halo catheter positioned at the right atrial septum was also observed with a coupling interval of 240 ms (Figure 5A). After ablation of the left atrial inferior wall, only PACs remained; sinus rhythm was maintained. Atrial pace mapping using intracardiac pattern matching, known as Intracardiac Pace Match Scoring (iPASO), was performed with dual-chamber electrograms. iPASO mapping was performed using the Intracardiac Pattern Matching (ICPM) module. Dual-chamber intracardiac electrograms (EGMs) from the right atrium and CS during the trigger beat were recorded as reference signals. Pace mapping was then conducted near the earliest reference site. The ICPM software automatically calculated a matching score (0-1.0) between paced and reference EGMs. To visualize this on the 3-dimensional map, the score was converted to a percentage and displayed as the local activation time, with higher scores representing earlier activation. The site with the highest score was defined as the EAS. This novel atrial mapping technique enabled precise identification of the PAC origin. Although PACs occurred infrequently, iPASO mapping was used to localize their origin and estimate the most likely focus. Even when pacing at the same site, the pace-mapping score can vary with output and cycle length. High output may cause far-field capture, whereas low output may fail to capture. Although shorter pacing cycles enable faster mapping, excessively short cycles can induce conduction delay and alter atrial activation, lowering the matching score even at the tachycardia origin. Therefore, we performed iPASO mapping with a pacing cycle length of 400 ms and 5 V output. Consequently, a high iPASO (0.75) was observed just above the fossa ovalis (Figure 5B). This region was considered the likely origin. Activation mapping confirmed the same EAS as that predicted by iPASO mapping. Based on these findings, ablation was performed at this right atrial septal site. After the ablation, both AF and PACs completely disappeared. Although no non-PV triggers were observed from the LAPW in this case, empirical LAPW isolation was performed, considering that this was the second procedure and that LAPW could potentially serve as a substrate for future AF, as well as to prevent roof-dependent atrial tachycardia. Outcome and Follow-Up

Following this ablation session, the previously elevated AF burden, as monitored by remote home monitoring, decreased to 0%. The patient remained free of AF recurrence after discontinuation of all antiarrhythmic drugs (Figure 6B).

Discussion

Although the PVs are the primary origin of AF, non-PV triggers can arise from various locations, including the superior vena cava, LAPW, crista terminalis, CS, inferior vena cava, ligament of Marshall, interatrial septum, and bilateral atrial appendages.1, 2, 3, 4 In this case, information obtained during the previous ablation session and anatomical knowledge of these common foci contributed to the successful identification of the non-PV trigger origins. However, there is no guarantee that the non-PV trigger identified in the present case is identical to the one suspected 11 years ago. Rather, it may reflect the current atrial substrate. The previous finding should therefore be regarded only as a possible indication, not a confirmed one.

The self-reference mapping technique, which was originally reported by Matsunaga et al,5 who used the PentaRay catheter (Biosense Webster), involves repeated direct current cardioversion during AF, tagging the earliest activation on the mapping catheter and advancing the catheter upstream until the converging activation tags reveal the focal origin. This approach is particularly effective for patients with frequent AF triggers and is enhanced by the use of grid-type mapping catheters (Advisor HD Grid Mapping Catheter [Sensor Enabled; Abbott] and OPTRELL). We previously reported the utility of self-reference mapping for identifying non-PV triggers originating from the cavotricuspid isthmus or Eustachian ridge.6,7 Although the shape of the splines of the OctaRay and PentaRay catheters (Biosense Webster) can deform depending on their position, thus making it difficult to identify the upstream site of electrical activation, the lattice structure of grid catheters maintains a constant spatial relationship regardless of placement, thereby enabling identification of the EAS. Therefore, we believe high-density grid catheters are more suitable for mapping non-PV triggers.

The iPASO mapping technique, as reported by Yamashita et al,8 is based on ICPM and can be used to identify the origin of PACs or atrial tachycardia. This method uses unipolar electrograms and right atrial and CS catheters (up to 10 poles) to calculate matching scores, which are then displayed on an activation map. Because iPASOs cannot be directly projected onto a 3-dimensional electroanatomical map, they are converted to surrogate temporal lead values, which are subsequently applied to the activation map. Using this approach, the site with the greatest lead time corresponds to the highest iPASO. In the present case, a site with a score of 0.60 was represented as a lead time of −60 ms and a site with a score of 0.75 was represented as a lead time of −75 ms.8

Yamashita et al8 reported a case in which a separate activation map of PACs demonstrated that the EAS was located on the left atrial roof. This site exactly matched the location with the highest iPASO, indicating that the site with the highest score on the iPASO map corresponded to the true EAS.8 This case suggested that iPASO mapping may enable the identification of focal tachycardia origins, even when PACs are infrequent. Conventionally, activation mapping requires repeated PAC occurrence. However, even a single PAC can be used as a reference to construct a map and estimate the likely origin site using the iPASO technique. In the present case, PACs were infrequent; however, by creating an iPASO map in advance, the origin site could be predicted. Subsequent activation mapping was performed to target only the surrounding region to potentially reduce the overall mapping time.

Although no definitive cutoff values for iPASOs have been established, targeting the site with the highest score is considered optimal. In this case, the site with a score of 0.75 was selected for ablation. However, iPASOs may vary depending on the pacing output and cycle length.8 At our institution, iPASO mapping is performed using pacing at 5 V with a cycle length of 400 ms to minimize variability. Furthermore, iPASO mapping is less suitable for patients with low atrial or CS potentials and for those with IRAF.9 Because iPASO mapping can only be performed during sinus rhythm, it cannot be applied in patients with IRAF who immediately revert to AF. In addition, self-reference mapping, which identifies the earliest activation site at the initiation of AF, is difficult to perform when AF or PACs occur only infrequently. Therefore, each technique has its own inherent limitations. However, for operators familiar with both techniques, as in the present case, mapping of non-PV triggers can be achieved even in the presence of frequent IRAF or, conversely, infrequent AF/PACs by using the OPTRELL catheter and CARTO system. This approach is therefore highly valuable for identifying non-PV triggers under a wide range of clinical conditions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case in which multiple non-PV triggers were successfully eliminated using a combination of self-reference mapping and iPASO mapping. The patient remained arrhythmia-free for more than 1 year following the procedure, highlighting the potential of a multimodal strategy.

Conclusions

The combined use of self-reference mapping and iPASO mapping enabled efficient and accurate identification of multiple non-PV triggers, resulting in successful ablation of refractory AF. This multimodal approach may be a useful strategy for managing non-PV triggers in clinical practice.
