Design, Synthesis, and Evaluation of New 1,4‐Naphthoquinone‐1,2,3‐triazoles as Antimalarial Agents
Authors: Lina Rezainia, Mohammad Hosein Sayahi, Eisa Kaveh Vernousfaderani, Seyedeh Sara Mirfazli, Mohammad Hossein Morshedsolouk, Navid Dastyafteh, Mehdi Nateghpour, Leila Farivar, Mina Saeedi, Haleh Hanifian, Mahdi Mohebali, Seyedeh Mana Shafiee, Gholamreza Hassanpour, Mohammad Mahdavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe increasing resistance to existing antimalarial agents has prompted researchers to develop new therapeutic candidates. To address this, the hybridization of 1,4‐naphthoquinone and 1,2,3‐triazole yielded a new series of compounds evaluated as prospective in vitro antimalarial agents against the chloroquine‐sensitive 3D7 and chloroquine‐resistant K1 strains of Plasmodium falciparum. The target compounds were synthesized efficiently via a multistep route, involving the reaction of 1,4‐naphthoquinone and p‐aminobenzoic acid, followed by reactions with propargylamine and different prepared chlorides. The synthetic route was concluded using a copper(I)‐catalyzed azide‐alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) “click” reaction. Based on structure‐activity relationships (SAR), the compound bearing a 4‐chloroaryl moiety (9g) was the most active against P. falciparum 3D7, exhibiting 60.00% growth inhibition at 25 µM. Furthermore, compound 9k bearing a 2‐methylaryl moiety exhibited the most potent activity against P. falciparum K1, showing 64.52% growth inhibition at 50 µM. In silico simulations of compound 9g on thirteen protein targets in P. falciparum indicated strong interactions with the 1J3 and 1LDG targets. Also, the stability of the ligand‐protein complexes was verified for 3FGO.Keywords: 1,2,3‐triazole; 1,4‐naphthoquinone; antimalarial; click reaction; dynamic studies; Plasmodium falciparum

Introduction

Malaria is a mosquito‐borne infection transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes and represents one of the most prominent health challenges globally. It is regarded as a deadly disease, resulting in close to 500 million cases annually, with children in developing nations representing the overwhelming majority of the victims [1, 2]. As of 2023, the WHO estimated 263 million malaria cases globally, with an incidence of 60.4 per 1000 population at risk. This is higher than the 58.6 per 1000 rate that was recorded in 2022. Global mortality rates in 2023 were estimated at 13.7 deaths per 100 000 individuals, highlighting the urgent need for effective interventions with fewer side effects [2].

Malaria is caused by various species of Plasmodium; however, Plasmodium falciparum causes the most severe form of the disease (malaria tropica). It is the deadliest of the vector‐borne infections in the world, claiming half a million lives annually. The life cycle of the parasites is a complicated process that occurs in a vertebrate host and a mosquito vector through both sexual and asexual reproduction. The complexity hinders the development of drugs and vaccines. Once it enters the human host, the parasite first reproduces in the liver and then enters red blood cells, triggering the clinical signs of the condition [3]. Common symptoms are periodic fever, chills, sweating, headache, malaise, myalgia, anemia, and nausea, whereas severe cases may progress to cerebral malaria, respiratory distress, hypoglycemia, multi‐organ failure, and death [4].

The most common antimalarial drugs are chloroquine phosphate, mefloquine, antifolates, and artemisinin‐based combination therapies (ACTs); nevertheless, currently used medicines for the management of the disease have proven to be neurotoxic, prone to resistance, and thermodynamically unstable. The FDA intends to enhance patient and medical practitioner awareness of the potentially serious, long‐term risks of using mefloquine [5].

There is a pressing need to find new chemotypes with distinct mechanisms of action. Consequently, a large variety of compounds have been reported to possess antimalarial activity. Among the promising structural classes, naphthoquinones are a promising group of biologically active compounds with significant medicinal applications among the promising structural classes. Naphthoquinones undergo potent redox cycling, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and destabilizing cellular redox balance [6]. This property explains their wide range of biological effects. For example, they have been shown to exhibit anticancer activity through topoisomerase inhibition, triggering apoptosis, and modulating signaling pathways [7]. They also display antibacterial [8, 9], antifungal [10], and antiviral [11] properties. Moreover, many studies emphasize trypanocidal [12], antileishmanial [13], anti‐inflammatory [14], and antioxidant [15] potential, demonstrating their flexibility as core structures in drug development. The naphthoquinones have a multifaceted activity profile, making them excellent scaffolds for the design of novel therapeutic agents.

Similarly, 1,2,3‐triazoles have become significant heterocyclic motifs commonly used in the design of bioactive compounds. The introduction of a 1,2,3‐triazole moiety, readily obtained by the copper(I)‐catalyzed addition of azide and alkyne in the azide‐alkyne cycloaddition reaction (CuAAC) is desirable due to its several aspects, including antibacterial [16], antifungal [17], antitubercular [18], antiviral [19], and anticancer [20] effects, often mediated by enzyme inhibition, DNA‐binding interactions, or disruption of structural integrity in pathogens. Also, the antimalarial activity of 1,2,3‐triazoles is well‐documented in the literature [21, 22, 23]. The biological effects of 1,2,3‐triazole and its derivatives are attributed to their excellent metabolic stability, resistance to hydrolysis, and their status as a rigid, aromatic heterocycle capable of forming hydrogen bonding interactions, dipole interactions, and π–π stacking within enzyme active sites. Their nitrogen‐rich structure enhances polarity and aqueous solubility while retaining an appropriate balance of lipophilicity for penetrating parasite membranes. As a bioisostere of traditional amide and ether linkers, yet with superior chemical properties, the 1,2,3‐triazole ring is an ideal structural element for generating stable, synthetically flexible antimalarial hybrids.

The hybridization strategy is a technique for integrating two bioactive pharmacophores into a single molecular backbone to leverage their biological activities. This paper reports the synthesis of new 1,4‐naphthoquinone‐1,2,3‐triazole hybrids, which have not previously been evaluated for their antimalarial activity against the 3D7 and K1 strains of P. falciparum. A structure‐activity relationship (SAR) study was also conducted to identify the best candidates for further investigation.

Results and Discussion

Design

To develop antimalarial agents, a molecular hybridization strategy was employed to synthesize target 1,4‐naphthoquinone‐1,2,3‐triazole hybrids, as shown in Figure 1.

Literature‐reported compounds against malaria and the hybridization strategy to design new hybrids.

Naphthoquinones are known for their broad and versatile biological activities [10]. They have also been identified as potent pharmacophores against malaria; notable examples include lapachol and atovaquone (Figure 1) are important examples. Various mechanisms of action underlie the antimalarial activity of naphthoquinones. Lapachol and its ortho isomer (β‐lapachone) have indicated activity and cytotoxicity against P. falciparum [24]. Atovaquone is a competitive inhibitor of ubiquinol, specifically inhibiting the mitochondrial electron transport chain at the bc1 complex, leading to a loss of mitochondrial function and disruption of metabolic homeostasis [25].

As reported by Paengsri et al., compound A bearing a naphthoquinone core exhibited in vitro antimalarial activity against P. falciparum (the K1 multidrug resistance strain), with an IC50 value of 0.77 µg/mL, relative to dihydroartemisinin (IC50 = 0.00153 µg/mL) and mefloquine (IC50 = 0.034 µg/mL) as reference drugs [26].

Furthermore, 1,2,3‐triazole and its derivatives serve as active pharmacophores exhibiting antimalarial activity [27, 28]. Kushawaha et al. reported that compound B exhibited an IC50 of 0.21 µM against P. falciparum (the K1 multidrug‐resistant strain), relative to chloroquine as the reference drug (IC50 = 0.0064 µM) [29].

According to the literature, 1,4‐naphthoquinone‐1,2,3‐triazoles have emerged as antimalarial compounds. For instance, compounds C and D (Figure 1) exhibited IC50 values of 0.81 and 2.70 µM, respectively, against P. falciparum (the W2 chloroquine‐resistant strain), relative to chloroquine (IC50 = 0.21 µM), demonstrating the effectiveness of the hybridization strategy [30].

Reported hybrids by Agarwal et al. [31] further underscored the effectiveness of the hybridization method in combating malaria. Compounds E and F demonstrated activity against P. falciparum (the FCR3 atovaquone‐resistant strain), with IC50 values of 0.028 and 0.033 µM, respectively, relative to atovaquone as a positive control (IC50 = 1.713 µM).

Amides and simple methylene linkers have been recognized as the most common linkers in bioactive molecules [32] and were employed in this study to connect 1,4‐naphthoquinone and 1,2,3‐triazole moieties. Amides act as both hydrogen bond donors (NH) and acceptors (C═O). They facilitate the formation of intra‐ and intermolecular hydrogen bonding interactions that contribute to chemical stability and target engagement, respectively. Amides introduce a defined geometry through partial double‐bond character, enabling controlled conformational alignment between the pharmacophoric scaffolds and thereby optimizing interactions with biological targets. In the antimalarial hybrid design, the amide linker provides structural stability and enhances molecular orientation toward the protein targets.

In this work, we designed a focused set of 1,4‐naphthoquinone‐1,2,3‐triazole hybrids bearing a range of aryl substituents rather than aliphatic or heterocyclic derivatives. The choice was guided by reported SAR trends indicating that aromatic substituents on the naphthoquinone framework often enhance antiplasmodial activity via π–π stacking and hydrophobic interactions within the enzyme binding pocket. Furthermore, aryl‐based analogs offer predictable electronic and steric modulation of the 1,2,3‐triazole linker, facilitating systematic evaluation of substituent effects. Heterocyclic or aliphatic substituents were not prioritized at this stage because our goal was to first assess the influence of electronic and positional variations in aromatic groups on activity and docking profiles, before expanding to other structural classes in future work.

Chemistry

An efficient synthesis of naphthoquinone‐1,2,3‐triazole derivatives 9a–o (Supporting Information) was accomplished as outlined in Scheme 1. The process began with the reaction of naphthoquinone (1) and 4‐aminobenzoic acid (2) in methanol at room temperature for 24 h, affording 4‐((1,4‐dioxo‐1,4‐dihydronaphthalen‐2‐yl)amino)benzoic acid (3). This compound was then treated with propargylamine in the presence of TEA and TBTU in DMF at room temperature for 24 h, yielding 4‐((1,4‐dioxo‐1,4‐dihydronaphthalen‐2‐yl)amino)‐N‐(prop‐2‐yn‐1‐yl)benzamide (5). Separately, aniline derivatives 6a–o were reacted with 2‐chloroacetyl chloride (7) in DMF at room temperature for 24 h to afford 2‐chloro‐N‐phenylacetamides with various substituents on the phenyl ring 8a–o. Subsequently, these acetamide derivatives were dissolved in DMF containing sodium azide and TEA. The final series of compounds 9a–o, 4‐((1,4‐dioxo‐1,4‐dihydronaphthalen‐2‐yl)amino)‐N‐((1‐(2‐oxo‐2‐(phenylamino)ethyl)‐1H‐1,2,3‐triazol‐4‐yl)methyl)benzamides, was synthesized by adding 4‐((1,4‐dioxo‐1,4‐dihydronaphthalen‐2‐yl)amino)‐N‐(prop‐2‐yn‐1‐yl)benzamides (5), sodium ascorbate, and copper sulfate pentahydrate to the resulting mixture at room temperature for 24 h.

Activity of Compounds 9a–o Against P. falciparum 3D7

Compound 9g, bearing a 4‐chloroaryl moiety, exhibited the most potent activity with a growth inhibition of 60% (parasitemia of 0.75%). Relocating the Cl on the aryl ring reduced growth inhibition; the SAR of chloro‐substituted analogs is complex. Compound 9e, bearing a 2‐chloroaryl group, showed moderate inhibition (38.80%), whereas compound 9f, bearing a 3‐chloroaryl group, was the least active (10.58%) among the chlorinated derivatives. The dichloro analogue 9h displayed moderate inhibition (30.71%), indicating that two chloro groups do not enhance activity compared to the optimal para‐monosubstitution. Substitution of bromine at the para position (9i) led to low activity (23.27% inhibition), proving less effective than 9g, suggesting that a larger halogen does not benefit this scaffold. Comparison of the fluorinated derivatives revealed that compound 9c, bearing a 2‐fluoroaryl group, displayed higher growth inhibition (47.78%) than compound 9d, bearing a 3‐fluoroaryl group (33.33%), suggesting that fluorine substitution is more favorable at the ortho position than at the meta position.

The results also revealed that the incorporation of a benzyl group into the scaffold (compound 9b) gave significant potency, achieving 53.26% growth inhibition and ranking as the second most active compound. This may be attributed to increased flexibility or an extended distance between the aromatic moiety and the 1,2,3‐triazole, which facilitates parasite growth inhibition. Conversely, compounds 9a, bearing the parent phenyl ring, and 9j, bearing the 3‐nitroaryl moiety, were only weakly active, with 12.00% growth inhibition.

The effect of electron‐donating substituents, such as methyl, methoxy, and ethyl, was investigated, revealing generally favorable. In particular, the presence of methyl at the ortho position (compound 9k, 48.15.5%) was significantly more effective than substitution at the para position (compound 9l, 38.79%). Replacing the methyl group with a methoxy group at the same position (compound 9n) resulted in lower potency (34.92%), indicating that the methyl group is more effective than the methoxy group at the ortho position. Notably, increasing the number of methyl groups reduced activity, yielding a growth inhibition of 18.16%. Introduction of an ethyl group also exhibited moderate activity (39.5,0%).

Activity of Compounds 9a–o Against P. falciparum K1

Compounds 9k, bearing a 2‐methylaryl group, and 9b, bearing a benzyl moiety, exhibited the most potent activity against P. falciparum K1, with growth inhibition of 64.52% and 64.12%, respectively. Furthermore, compounds 9o (63.56%), bearing a 4‐ethylaryl group, and the ortho‐ and para‐fluorinated derivatives (9c: 62.90% and 9d: 62.13%) also demonstrated potent antimalarial activity. Compounds 9j (3‐NO2, 59.50%) and 9g (4‐Cl, 50.24%) showed moderate activity.

This profile indicates that minimal steric bulk at the ortho or para position, or a benzylic spacer, enhances activity, likely by maximizing hydrophobic contacts and optimizing ligand orientation, without compromising efficacy against the resistant K1 strain. The relatively good activity of the nitro group (9j) may be attributed to an electronic effect, for example, a change in H‐bonding or redox interaction, that facilitates activity in resistant parasites. Conversely, larger halogens or excessive substitution reduce efficacy: 9h (2,4‐diCl, 10.74%) and 9i (4‐Br, 13.19%) are weak, and the unsubstituted phenyl derivative (9a, 30.8%) is ineffective, underscoring the sensitivity of activity to steric congestion and halogen size. The para‐methyl derivative (9l, 35.85%) is less active than the ortho‐methyl (9k) and para‐methoxy (9n, 42.60%) derivatives, while the dimethyl analog (9m, 43.11%) is only moderately active, suggesting that optimal activity requires a balance of lipophilicity without excessive bulk.

Comparison of compounds 9a–o against P. falciparum strains 3D7 and K1 at concentrations of 25 µM and 50 µM, respectively, revealed distinct substituent effects. Some analogs, specifically 9b (benzyl), 9c (2‐F), 9k (2‐Me), and 9o (4‐Et), were active in both strains, indicating that small lipophilic groups in either the ortho or para positions elicit antimalarial activity. In contrast, large halogen substituents (9i, 4‐Br), multiple substitutions (as in 9h, 2,4‐diCl or 9m, 2,4‐diMe), or the absence of halogen substituents on the phenyl ring were associated with reduced efficacy. It is worth noting that compound 9j (3‐NO2) was selective for the K1‐resistant strain, indicating that electronic effects may influence the chloroquine resistance mechanism. Collectively, these results indicate the need to balance steric and electronic properties to maximize dual‐strain antimalarial activity.

Overall, the SAR analysis highlights the 4‐chloroaryl substitution pattern as the most favorable modification for future design iterations.

Molecular Dynamics Simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed on the ligand–protein complexes 1J3I, 1LDG, 3FGO, 3BPM, and 7SXL to assess the stability of the ligand within the binding site of the target protein. Simulations ranging from 20 to 100 ns were conducted to analyze the dynamic behavior of the complexes. The results are reported in terms of RMSD, RMSF, number of hydrogen bonds, and Rg, as illustrated in Figures 3 and 4.

Root‐mean‐square deviation (RMSD) and root‐mean‐square fluctuation (RMSF) of compound 9g with targeted proteins (1J3I, 1LDG, 3FGO, 3BPM, and 7SXL).

Number of hydrogen bonding interactions (H‐bond) and radius of gyration (Rg) of the compound 9g and targeted proteins (1J3I, 1LDG, 3FGO, 3BPM, and 7SXL) complex.

The RMSD plot for the 1J3I–ligand complex indicated stability during the initial 30 ns; however, subsequent fluctuations suggested significant structural deviations, implying conformational instability. The radius of gyration (Rg) for this complex, ranging from 2.2 to 2.4 nm, corroborated the RMSD observations. For the 1LDG–ligand complex, the RMSD plot revealed that the protein achieved structural stability after approximately 30 ns, with fluctuations between 0.45 and 0.60 nm, indicating stable binding within the pocket. The presence of approximately 3–5 hydrogen bonds facilitated ligand stability, while the corresponding RMSF plot showed that, except for residues 150 and 178, the residues exhibited low fluctuation levels.

Analysis of the 3BPM complex revealed ligand instability. The RMSF plot for 3BPM indicated higher fluctuation levels in specific protein regions, particularly residues 15–20, which led to the ejection of the ligand from the active site. Notably, the RMSD plot for the 3FGO–ligand complex showed minimal fluctuation (range: 0.30–0.45 nm) over the 100 ns simulation. Furthermore, compound 9g exhibited lower fluctuation compared to the control ligand. The RMSF values for 3FGO identified specific protein regions with higher fluctuation, suggesting flexible regions within the protein structure characteristic of the sarcoendoplasmic reticulum (SR) calcium transport ATPase (SERCA). The average Rg values for the 3FGO complex ranged from 2.925 to 2.975 nm, indicating that the structural integrity of the binding site was maintained during the simulation. Finally, the RMSD plot for 7SXL exhibited high fluctuation throughout the 20 ns simulation, indicating a lack of conformational stability.

Conclusion

This study reports a novel hybridization of 1,4‐naphthoquinone and 1,2,3‐triazole moieties, offering a promising strategy for antimalarial drug discovery against both chloroquine‐sensitive (3D7) and chloroquine‐resistant (K1) strains of P. falciparum. Structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis identified the 4‐chloroaryl derivative (compound 9g) as highly effective against the 3D7 strain at a concentration of 25 µM. Furthermore, molecular docking and dynamics simulations corroborated the potential of 9g, revealing stable binding interactions with key parasite protein targets, including 1J3I, 1LDG, and 3FGO.

Materials and Methods

General

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra for 1H and 13C were recorded in DMSO using a Bruker Ultra Shield operating spectrometer at frequencies of 300 MHz (1H) and 75 MHz (13C). Data are reported as chemical shifts, multiplicity, coupling constants, and integration. Melting points were determined on an Electrothermal 9100 apparatus. Elemental analyses for C, H, and N were performed using a Heraeus CHN‐O‐Rapid analyzer. IR spectra were recorded on a Shimadzu IR‐460 spectrometer. Mass spectra were recorded on an Agilent Technology (HP) mass spectrometer operating at an ionization potential of 70 eV.

A General Procedure for the Synthesis of 4‐((1,4‐Dioxo‐1,4‐dihydronaphthalen‐2‐yl)amino)benzoic Acid (3)

4‐((1,4‐Dioxo‐1,4‐dihydronaphthalen‐2‐yl)amino)benzoic acid (3) was synthesized according to a previously described method [33] with slight modifications. To a stirred suspension of 1,4‐naphthoquinone (1, 1 mmol) in methanol (5 mL) was added 4‐aminobenzoic acid (2, 1 mmol). The resulting dark red solution was stirred at 25°C for 24 h. The reaction mixture was poured into cold water, and the resulting brownish‐red suspension was stirred at 25°C for 30 min. The precipitate was collected by filtration, washed with ice‐cold water, and dried to afford the desired compound as a brownish‐red solid.

A General Procedure for the Synthesis of 4‐((1,4‐Dioxo‐1,4‐dihydronaphthalen‐2‐yl)amino)‐N‐(prop‐2‐yn‐1‐yl)benzamide (5)

TBTU (13 mmol) and Et3N (13 mmol) were added to a mixture of 4‐((1,4‐dioxo‐1,4‐dihydronaphthalen‐2‐yl)amino)benzoic acid (3, 10 mmol) and propargylamine (4, 10 mmol) in DMF (50 mL). The reaction mixture was stirred for 24 h at room temperature. Upon completion, water was added, and the precipitate was collected by filtration to obtain 4‐((1,4‐dioxo‐1,4‐dihydronaphthalen‐2‐yl)amino)‐N‐(prop‐2‐yn‐1‐yl)benzamide (5).

A General Procedure for the Synthesis of 2‐Chloro‐N‐Substituted Acetamide Derivatives 8a–o

An aniline derivative (6, 50 mmol) was suspended in DMF (25 mL) and cooled to 0°C. Subsequently, chloroacetyl chloride (9, 65 mmol) was added dropwise. The reaction mixture was then stirred at room temperature for 24 h. Upon completion, the mixture was diluted with water, poured into crushed ice, and the precipitate was collected by filtration. The residue was washed with water to obtain the pure 2‐chloro‐N‐substituted acetamide derivatives 8a–o.

A General Procedure for the Synthesis of Target Compound 9a–o

An acetamide derivative (8, 1.2 mmol), K2CO3 (1.5 mmol), and NaN3 (1.5 mmol) were dissolved in DMF and then stirred at room temperature for 1 h. Then, CuSO4.5H2O (0.15 mmol), sodium ascorbate (0.45 mmol), and compound 5 (1 mmol) were added to the reaction mixture. Upon completion, copper ions were removed by washing with a 0.05 M solution of EDTA. Biological Assay

The P. falciparum 3D7 (chloroquine‐sensitive) and K1 (chloroquine‐resistant) strains were obtained from the Pasteur Institute of Iran. The 3D7 strain was cultured in vitro under stringent, sterile conditions following a previously described method [34]. An in vitro assay was conducted to evaluate the antiplasmodial activity of the synthesized compounds against the 3D7 and K1 strains of P. falciparum. Briefly, stock solutions of the synthesized compounds were prepared in DMSO (Fluka) and diluted with RPMI 1640 medium to achieve final concentrations of 25 and 100 µM. Chloroquine diphosphate (Sigma‐Aldrich, Germany) was used as a reference standard and prepared at concentrations of 1, 5, 25, and 50 µg/mL. Vehicle control wells containing DMSO at the corresponding concentration (0.5%) were prepared in parallel to assess solvent toxicity on erythrocytes.

For the assay, 100 µ L of each test solution was dispensed into designated wells of a sterile 96‐well microplate, along with appropriate controls. The growth control consisted of RPMI medium containing infected erythrocytes (no drug), while the negative control contained RPMI with uninfected erythrocytes. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Subsequently, 20 µ L of P. falciparum‐infected erythrocytes (adjusted to 2%–3% parasitemia) were added to each well. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 h in a candle jar to maintain a low‐oxygen environment. Following incubation, thin blood smears were prepared from each well, fixed with methanol, and stained with 10% Giemsa solution. Parasitemia was determined by counting the number of infected erythrocytes per 10 000 red blood cells under a light microscope. In Silico Studies

Protein Target Selection and Preparation

To predict the potential targets and mechanisms of action of compound 9g, a network pharmacology approach was employed to identify 13 protein targets associated with P. falciparum pathogenesis and drug resistance. These targets were subsequently selected for molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation based on their reported roles in parasite survival, metabolism, and mechanisms of antimalarial action. The 3D crystal structures of these targets were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) (accessed October 17, 2025; https://www.rcsb.org/) [35], comprising the following PDB IDs: 1J3I, 1LDG, 3FGO, 3BPM, 7SXL, 2GHU, 1QNG, 4CQ8, 3QS1, 6UKJ, 1LF3, 1YVB, and 1A3N. Protein structure preprocessing and energy minimization were conducted using Chimera v1.17.1 [36] with the AMBER ff14SB force field, employing 1000 steps of the steepest descent algorithm with a step size of 0.02 Å.

Test Compound Preparation

The 3D coordinates of compound 9g were generated using ChemBioDraw 13.0. Rotatable bonds were identified to enable conformational flexibility during the docking simulations.

Molecular Docking Study

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina‐GPU 2.1 [37] with grid boxes prepared using AutoDock Tools v1.5.6 [38], centered on experimentally known binding sites with dimensions of 25 × 25 × 25 Å. The calculations were executed with the following parameters: seed = 1, energy range = 5.0 kcal/mol, threads = 8000, and GPU acceleration on an NVIDIA RTX 4070 Ti Super GPU. Docking results were visualized using Discovery Studio v4.5 (Biovia, San Diego, CA, USA), and binding affinity heatmaps were generated using Microsoft Excel 2016. The docking protocol was validated by re‐docking the native ligand from the crystal structure, with success defined as recovery of the crystallographic pose within RMSD < 2.0 Å.

Molecular Dynamics

Ligand–receptor complexes were selected for molecular dynamics simulations based on two criteria: (1) predicted binding affinity greater than that of a known reference ligand for the target and (2) binding affinity ≤ −4.0 kcal/mol. Ligand topology and parameter files were generated using the SwissParam web server (https://www.swissparam.ch/; accessed October 22, 2025) [39], employing the MMFF force field for compatibility with the CHARMM36 force in GROMACS [40]. All MD simulations were performed using GROMACS 2024 on an Ubuntu 22.04 LTS workstation equipped with GPU acceleration (NVIDIA RTX 4070 Ti Super, CUDA Driver 12.40). Each ligand–receptor complex was centered in a dodecahedral simulation box, solvated using the TIP3P water model, and neutralized with Na+ and Cl− ions using the genion tool. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm for 50 000 steps to achieve a maximum force (Fmax) below 10 kJ/(mol · nm), utilizing the CHARMM36‐Jul2022 force field.

Subsequent equilibration involved two phases: (1) NVT equilibration, where protein heavy atoms were position‐restrained (force constant = 1,000 kJ/(mol · nm2)) and the system was heated from 0 to 300 K over 200 ps using the V‐rescale thermostat (τt = 0.1 ps); and (2) NPT equilibration at 300 K and 1 bar for 1 ns using semiisotropic pressure coupling and the Parrinello–Rahman barostat (τp = 2 ps), with restraints maintained. Production MD simulations were conducted for 100 ns per complex at 300 K and 1 bar with a 2 fs time step; position restraints were removed during production. Trajectories were analyzed using GROMACS built‐in utilities to calculate the root mean square deviation (RMSD) of C α atoms, root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) per residue, radius of gyration (Rg), and the number of hydrogen bonds, thereby assessing the structural stability and dynamics of the complexes.
