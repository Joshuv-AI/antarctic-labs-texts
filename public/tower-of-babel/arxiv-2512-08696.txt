Multifractal Analysis of equilibrium states of endomorphisms of ℙ k \mathbb{P}^{k}
Nathan Dalaklis, Yan Mary He

Abstract
. Let f f be a holomorphic endomorphism of ℂ ​ ℙ k \mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{k} of algebraic degree at least 2 and let X ⊆ ℂ ​ ℙ k X\subseteq\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{k} be an uniformly expanding set. In this paper, we study multifractal analysis of equilibrium states of Hölder continuous functions for the non-conformal dynamical system f : X → X f\colon X\to X . In lieu of Hausdorff dimensions, we use a new dimension theory (i.e., the volume dimension theory) to define various local dimension multifractal spectra and show that each of these spectra form a Legendre transform pair with the temperature function as in the conformal case. As an application of our main theorems, we also prove a conditional variational principle for such dimension multifractal spectra.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Motivation and background

If
f\colon X\to X
is a dynamical system, many of its important properties can be characterized using
local asymptotic quantities

\varphi_{\infty}(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi_{n}(x)
such as Lyapunov exponents, local dimensions and local entropies. The distribution of these local values across the space
X
often forms a
multifractal structure
of
X
. In particular, one considers level sets
J(\alpha):=\{x\in X:\varphi_{\infty}(x)=\alpha\}
and obtains a multifractal decomposition of
X
.
Multifractal analysis provides a global statistical picture of these local asymptotic quantities and interprets the geometry of their variation on
X
. In particular, one defines a
multifractal spectrum

S(\alpha):={\mathcal{S}}(J(\alpha))
where
{\mathcal{S}}
is a quantity measuring the size of the set
J(\alpha)
such as Hausdorff dimension or topological entropy.

There has been substantial amount of work on multifractal analysis for
conformal
dynamical systems. In
[
9
]
, Besicovitch studied the Hausdorff dimension of sets determined by the frequency of the
digits in dyadic expansions, which is essentially a multifractal analysis of the Birkhoff averages of the indicator functions for the doubling map.
Multifractal analysis of the Birkhoff averages of a continuous potential on various dynamical systems such as a mixing subshift of finite type is studied in
[
3
,
1
,
2
,
14
,
15
,
18
,
25
,
26
]
. Pesin-Weiss
[
19
]
studied a multifractal analysis of equilibrium measures for conformal expanding maps.
Roy-Urbanski
[
23
]
studied the multifractal analysis of the conformal measure associated to a family of weights imposed upon a graph directed Markov system. We refer the reader to the survey paper
[
12
]
for recent development.

On the other hand, the multifractal analysis of
non-conformal
dynamical systems is substantially more difficult and remains largely undeveloped. Conformal maps expand or contract equally in all directions, which allows one to obtain distortion lemmas to control how sets are stretched and to compare scales uniformly. However, non-conformal maps allow arbitrarily large distortion, even on small scales. This lack of conformality complicates the geometric structure of invariant sets and measures, causing classical methods to fail and suggesting that new techniques are needed.
In
[
6
]
, Barreira-Gelfert used
non-additive thermodynamic formalism
to study the multifractal analysis of the level sets of Lyapunov exponents for a class of non-conformal repellers. In
[
16
,
17
]
, the authors studied the local dimension spectra for Bernoulli measures on Bedford-McMullen carpets. More recent work by Cao-Pesin-Zhao
[
11
]
has made progress towards understanding the dimensions of the whole repeller, but they do not preform a multifractal analysis or estimates of the spectra therein. In the case of the plane, Falconer-Fraiser-Lee
[
13
]
and Qiu-Wang-Wang
[
22
]
investigated the
L^{q}
-spectra for planar iterated function systems and planar graph directed systems with restrictions on the contractive behavior of the mappings.

In this paper, we consider the non-conformal multifractal analysis of equilibrium states in the setting of complex dynamics and in particular holomorphic endomorphisms of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{k}
, which are generalizations of rational maps on the Riemann sphere to higher dimensional complex projective spaces. The recent work
[
10
]
of Bianchi and the second author introduces a delicate dimension theory (i.e., the volume dimension) for expanding measures and sets of such maps.
Thanks to the volume dimension, in this paper, we study a multifractal analysis of equilibrium states for a holomorphic endomorphism of
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{k}
on an uniformly expanding set.

1.2.
Statement of results

Let
f
be a holomorphic endomorphism of
\mathbb{P}^{k}=\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{k}
of algebraic degree at least 2.
Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{k}
be an uniformly expanding set; that is
X
is a closed invariant set and there exist
\eta>1
and
C>0
such that

||Df_{x}^{n}(v)||>C\eta^{n}||v||
for every
x\in X
,
v\in T_{x}\mathbb{P}^{k}
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Consider the dynamical system
f\colon X\to X
. Let
g\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
be a Hölder continuous function with pressure
\mathcal{P}(g)=0
. For
(q,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we consider the following 2-parameter family of potentials
\phi_{q,t}\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\phi_{q,t}:=qg-t\log|\Jac f|.

For each fixed
q\in\mathbb{R}
, the pressure function
t\mapsto\mathcal{P}(\phi_{q,t})
is strictly decreasing with
\mathcal{P}(\phi_{q,0})>0
; see Lemma
3.1
(
3
). We define
T(q)
to be the unique number such that
\mathcal{P}(\phi_{q,t})=0
. The function
q\mapsto T(q)
is called the
temperature function
, which is one of the main objects of study in multifractal analysis.

Another main object of study in multifractal analysis is a fine dimension multifractal spectrum. For conformal dynamical systems, it is often the so-called
fine Hausdorff dimension multifractal spectrum
. The idea is to decompose the set
X
as level sets
\{X_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\geq 0}
of some
local dimension
of a fixed measure
\nu
, together with an irregular set consisting of points in
X
at which the local dimension does not exist. The local dimension at
x\in X
is defined as
\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\frac{\log\nu(B(x,\kappa))}{\log\kappa}
, where
B(x,\kappa)
is the ball of radius
\kappa
around
x
. Then the fine Hausdorff dimension multifractal spectrum is the function
\alpha\mapsto{\rm HD}(X_{\alpha})
, where HD denotes the Hausdorff dimension.

In our setting, however, the dynamical system
f\colon X\to X
is
non-conformal
. As a consequence, balls and Hausdorff dimension of sets and measures are no longer intrinsic to the dynamics. Therefore the fine Hausdorff dimension multifractal spectrum may no longer be useful and an appropriate substitute is needed. In
[
10
]
, Bianchi and the second author introduced a
local volume dimension
and
volume dimension
for sets and measures, which depends on the dynamics of
f
and incorporates the absence of an analogue of Koebe’s theorem and the non-conformality of holomorphic endomorphisms of
\mathbb{P}^{k},k\geq 2
. We use such dimensions in lieu of local dimension and Hausdorff dimension in our multifractal analysis.

Let
\nu
be the equilibrium state of
g
. For each
q\in\mathbb{R}
, let
\nu_{q}
be the equilibrium state of
\phi_{q,T(q)}
. We define

\alpha(q):=-\frac{\int_{X}gd\nu_{q}}{\int_{X}\log|\Jac f|d\nu_{q}}>0.

Recall from
[
10
]
that
\delta_{\nu,x}
is the
local volume dimension
of
\nu
at
x\in X
.
For each
q\in\mathbb{R}
, we define

J_{\nu}(\alpha(q)):=\{x\in X:\delta_{\nu,x}=\alpha(q)\}.

Then we obtain a
multifractal decomposition
of
X
, namely,

X=\cup_{q}J_{\nu}(\alpha(q))\cup J_{\nu}^{\prime}

where
J_{\nu}^{\prime}:=\{x\in X:\delta_{\nu,x}\text{ does not exist}.\}
.

Consider the volume dimensions of the sets
J_{\nu}(\alpha(q))
. We first recall from
[
10
]
the
volume dimension

\VD_{f,\mu}(Y)
of the set
Y\subset X
with respect to a measure
\mu
.
We define the
parametrized volume dimension multifractal spectrum

\hat{S}_{\nu}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\hat{S}_{\nu}(\alpha(q)):=\VD_{f,\nu_{q}}(J_{\nu}(\alpha(q))).

We call this volume dimension multifractal spectrum
parametrized
as the volume dimension is taken with respect to the measure
\nu_{q}
which varies with
q\in\mathbb{R}
. We note that if all the Lyapunov exponents of
\nu_{q}
are equal, by
[
10
]
, we recover the Hausdorff dimension of the set, i.e.,
\VD_{f,\nu_{q}}(J_{\nu}(\alpha(q)))=\frac{1}{k}{\rm HD}(J_{\nu}(\alpha(q)))
.

If
Y
is an uniformly expanding set, recall from
[
10
]
(see also Definition
2.7
) that the volume dimension
\VD_{f}(Y)
is defined. It turns out that
J_{\nu}(\alpha(q))
is
f
-invariant (see Remark
4.7
)
and thus it is an uniformly expanding set.
We define the
fine volume dimension multifractal spectrum

S_{\nu}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

S_{\nu}(\alpha(q)):=\VD_{f}(J_{\nu}(\alpha(q)))=\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{+}_{J_{\nu}(\alpha(q))}(f)}\VD_{f,\mu}(J_{\nu}(\alpha(q))).

While it seems that our (parametrized) volume dimension multifractal spectrum (
\hat{S}_{\nu}(\alpha)
or)
S_{\nu}(\alpha)
is only defined for
\alpha
in the range
[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]\subset(0,\infty)
(see Remark
3.8
) of the function
q\mapsto\alpha(q)
, we will see in Theorem
1.3
that this is not a constraint as the set
J_{\nu}(\alpha)
is empty if
\alpha\notin[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]
.

Let
I
be an interval and let
h\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
be a strictly convex
C^{2}
-function, i.e.,
h^{\prime\prime}(x)>0
for all
x\in I
. Recall that the
Legendre transform
of
h
is the differentiable function
g\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

g(p):={\rm max}_{x\in I}(px-h(x)).

Two strictly convex functions
h
and
g
form a
Legendre transform pair
if and only if
g(\alpha)=h(q)+q\alpha
where
\alpha(q)=-h^{\prime}(q)
and
q=g^{\prime}(\alpha)
.

Our first two main theorems concern properties of the functions
q\mapsto\hat{S}_{\nu}(\alpha(q))
(resp.
q\mapsto S_{\nu}(\alpha(q))
) and
q\mapsto T(q)
and state that the functions
\alpha\mapsto-\hat{S}_{\nu}(-\alpha)
(resp.
\alpha\mapsto-S_{\nu}(-\alpha)
) and
q\mapsto T(q)
form a Legendre transform pair. In the conformal setting, an analogue of the theorem has been proved by several authors; see for example
[
6
,
20
,
19
,
21
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer.
Let
f
be a holomorphic endomorphism of
\mathbb{P}^{k}
of algebraic degree at least 2,
X\subseteq P^{k}
an uniformly expanding set and
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{X}^{+}(f)
an equilibrium state of a Hölder continuous function
g\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
. Then the following statements hold.

(1)

The temperature function
T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is real-analytic. We have
T(0)=\VD_{f}(X)
and
T(1)=0
.
The derivative of
T
satisfies
T^{\prime}(q)=-\alpha(q)<0
for all
q\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover,
T(q)
is convex. It is strictly convex if and only if
\nu\neq\nu_{0}
, where
\nu_{0}
is the equilibrium state of the potential function
-\VD_{f}(X)\log|\Jac f|
.

(2)

The function
\alpha\mapsto\hat{S}_{\nu}(\alpha)
is real-analytic and
\hat{S}_{\nu}(\alpha(q))=\VD_{f}(\nu_{q})=T(q)+q\alpha(q)
.

(3)

If
\nu\neq\nu_{0}
, then the functions
\alpha\mapsto-\hat{S}_{\nu}(-\alpha)
and
q\mapsto T(q)
are strictly convex and form a Legendre transform pair.

(4)

If
\nu=\nu_{0}
, then
T(q)
is a straight line and
\alpha(q)=\VD_{f}(X)
for all
q\in\mathbb{R}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
f
and
\nu
be as in Theorem
1.1
. Then Theorem
1.1
(2) and (3) hold for
S_{\nu}(\alpha(q))
.

The above theorems imply that if
\alpha\in[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]
, then the set
J_{\nu}(\alpha)
is non-empty. Our next theorem gives a converse, namely, if
\alpha\notin[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]
, then

J_{\nu}(\alpha)=\emptyset
. In other words, the
S_{\nu}(\alpha)
-spectrum is
complete
. Completeness of the fine Hausdorff dimension spectrum has been proved by Schmeling
[
24
]
; see also
[
21
, Theorem 9.2.5]
and
[
20
, Theorem 21.2]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
f
and
\nu
be as in Theorem
1.1
.
Then the
S_{\nu}(\alpha)
-spectrum (resp.
\hat{S}_{\nu}(\alpha)
-spectrum) is complete; namely, we have

\alpha_{1}=\inf_{x\in X}\underline{\delta}_{\nu,x}\text{~ and ~ }\alpha_{2}=\sup_{x\in X}\overline{\delta}_{\nu,x}.

In particular,
J_{\nu}(\alpha)=\emptyset
if and only if
\alpha\notin[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]
.

Let
(\Sigma_{A}^{+},\sigma)
be the symbolic coding of
f\colon X\to X
. Now we define a
symbolic
multifractal decomposition of
X
and
symbolic
(parametrized) volume dimension spectrum. In the spirit of Barreira-Saussol
[
3
]
,
for each
q\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the symbolic level set
\hat{X}_{q}
using the ratio of Birkhoff sums as

\hat{X}_{q}:=\left\{\omega\in\Sigma_{A}^{+}:\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(\pi(\sigma^{k}(\omega)))}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\log|\Jac f(\pi(\sigma^{k}(\omega)))|^{-1}}=\alpha(q)\right\}.

We define the
symbolic parametrized volume dimension multifractal spectrum

\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\nu}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\nu}(\alpha(q)):=\VD_{f,\nu_{q}}(\pi(\hat{X}_{q})).

We observe that
\pi(\hat{X}_{q})
is an uniformly expanding set.
We define the
symbolic fine volume dimension multifractal spectrum

\mathcal{F}_{\nu}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\alpha(q)):=\VD(\pi(\hat{X}_{q}))=\displaystyle\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{+}_{\pi(\hat{X}_{q})}(f)}\VD_{f,\mu}(\pi(\hat{X}_{q})).

Our next theorem gives the multifractal analysis for
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\nu}(\alpha(q))
and
\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\alpha(q))
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
f
and
\nu
be as in Theorem
1.1
. Then Theorem
1.1
(2) and (3) hold for both
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\nu}(\alpha(q))
and
\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\alpha(q))
.

As a collorary of the above theorems, we obtain the
conditional variational principle
for the dimension multifractal spectra.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
f
and
\nu
be as in Theorem
1.1
. Then the conditional variational principle holds; that is, for any
\alpha\in[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]
, we have

(1.1)

\hat{S}_{\nu}(\alpha)=\sup\{\VD_{f}(\rho)\;|\;\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{J_{\nu}(\alpha(q))}^{+}(f)\}

The conditional variational principle also holds for
S_{\nu}(\alpha)
,
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{\nu}(\alpha)
and
\mathcal{F}_{\nu}(\alpha)
.

It is expected that the volume dimension theory developed in
[
10
]
would work in more general contexts, such as smooth endomorphisms of compact Riemannian manifolds with singularities or invariant measures with positive sum of Lyapunov exponents. Therefore, we expect that our multifractal analysis to work in this generality as well.

1.3.
Strategy of the proofs

Recall that in the conformal case,
the local (Hausdorff) dimension is defined as

\delta_{\nu,x}:=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\log\nu(B(x,r))}{\log r},

where
\nu
is the equilibrium state of a fixed potential
g
. We define the associated Hausdorff dimension spectrum function for this local dimension multifractal
S_{\nu}(\alpha(q)):={\rm HD}(J_{\nu}(\alpha(q)))
.
In this case, the standard approach to the multifractal analysis of
\nu
(see for example
[
19
,
28
]
) requires that one compare the measure of the ball
B(x,r)
and the measure of the projection
\pi([\omega_{1}\dots\omega_{n}])
of a cylinder set of the symbolic coding. The utility of this comparison is that one may then find a
Moran cover
; a cover composed of sets in refinements of the Markov partition that determine a good enough cover of the set for dimension calculations; see for example
[
19
]
. Using tools from thermodynamic formalism, one can then derive the Legendre pair relationship between
S_{\nu}(\alpha(q))
and the temperature function
T(q)
; namely,
S_{\nu}(\alpha(q))=T(q)+q\alpha(q)
.

In our case, showing the existence of a Moran cover is unnecessary since for the local volume dimension, one is only concerned with the
U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,N)
sets. These sets are the images of balls under local inverse branches, exactly the sets whose measure is described by the Gibbs property (see
[
28
, Definition 13.2.1]
). For this reason, it would appear that we have trivialized our non-conformal analysis by choosing the local volume dimension. However, there are a few complications. First, the local volume dimension is a triple limit and so there is some accounting required here. Second, as we see in Proposition
4.5
, the result one would obtain from this standard approach only concludes on
\VD_{f,\nu_{q}}(J_{\nu}(\alpha(q)))
,
the volume dimension of the set with respect to
f
and the measure
\nu_{q}
. Here the measure
\nu_{q}
is dependent on the parameter
q
and so our dimension function is changing with
q
, whereas in the Hausdorff dimension case it remains constant. Therefore, by following the standard approach alone, one does not recover a true multifractal analysis with respect to the volume dimension.

Our goal, however, is to show something stronger.
Let
g
be a fixed potential with equilibrium state
\nu
on
X
, where
X
is an expanding set
of a holomorphic endomorphism
f
of
\mathbb{P}^{k}
algebraic degree at least
2
.
There is a multifractal decomposition of
X
by level sets
J_{\nu}(\alpha(q))
of local volume dimension. We show that the
f
-volume dimension spectrum function
S_{\nu}(\alpha(q)):=\VD_{f}(J_{\nu}(\alpha(q)))
form a Legendre pair with the temperature function; namely,
S_{\nu}(\alpha(q))=T(q)+q\alpha(q)
.

In the introduction to volume dimension (see
[
10
]
), it is not shown that the local volume dimension is an
f
-invariant. Since a priori we lack knowledge of this invariance, we begin our analysis with
\hat{X}_{q}
, a level set in the symbolic space of the ratio of two Birkhoff sums. We show that
\pi(\hat{X}_{q})=J_{\nu}(\alpha(q))
and
\hat{X}_{q}
is invariant. With the invariance of
J_{\nu}(\alpha(q))
established, we then utilize
[
10
, Proposition 4.22]
and the Gibbs property to complete our analogue to the standard local dimension analysis. From here our main tool is
[
10
, Theorem 5.6]
which connects
\VD_{f}(X)
to
\VD_{f}(\mu_{X})
where
\mu_{X}
is a volume conformal measure. With this fact we establish that
\nu_{q}=\mu_{J_{\nu}(\alpha(q))}
(see Lemma
5.1
) and conclude that
\VD_{f,\nu_{q}}(J_{\nu}(\alpha(q)))=\VD_{f}(J_{\nu}(\alpha(q)))
. We also have a volume lemma for volume dimensions from
[
10
, Theorem 1.1]
which allows us to retrieve a conditional variational principal for our local volume dimension spectrum.

2.
Volume dimensions

In this section, we give a brief overview of the local volume dimension and volume dimension for sets and measures as introduced in
[
10
]
.

2.1.
Holomorphic endomorphisms

Let
f\colon\mathbb{P}^{k}\to\mathbb{P}^{k}
be a holomorphic endomorphism of algebraic degree
d\geq 2
.
Recall that
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{k}
is an
uniformly expanding
set if it is closed and
f
-invariant, and there exist
\eta>1
and
C>0
such that

||Df_{x}^{n}(v)||>C\eta^{n}||v||
for every
x\in X
,
v\in T_{x}\mathbb{P}^{k}
, and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{M}_{X}^{+}(f)
the set of
f
-invariant ergodic probability measures supported on
X
. Since
X
is uniformly expanding, we note that any
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{X}^{+}(f)
has strictly positive Lyapunov exponents.

For the rest of this section, we fix a holomorphic endomorphism
f\colon\mathbb{P}^{k}\to\mathbb{P}^{k}
, an uniformly expanding set
X
and
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{X}^{+}(f)
.

2.2.
Local volume dimension

In this section, we recall the definition of the
local volume dimension
(which depends on
f
and
\nu
) introduced by Bianchi and the second author in
[
10
]
.

A result by Berteloot-Dupont-Molino (see
[
8
,
7
]
)
states that there exists an increasing (as
\varepsilon\to 0
) measurable exhaustion
\{Z^{\star}_{\nu}(\varepsilon)\}_{\varepsilon}
of a full-measure subset
Z^{\star}_{\nu}
of the space of orbits for
f
such that
the preimages of sufficiently small balls along orbits in
Z^{\star}_{\nu}(\varepsilon)

are approximately ellipses, and the contraction rate for their volume
is essentially given
by
e^{-nL_{\nu}}
.

Remark 2.1
.

Since
X
is uniformly expanding, we have
Z_{\nu}^{*}(\varepsilon)=Z_{\nu}^{*}
for any
\varepsilon>0
. However, we keep the parameter
\varepsilon
in our analysis for the purpose of future generalization of
X
to be non-uniformly expanding.

Denote by
\pi\colon Z^{\star}_{\nu}\to\mathbb{P}^{k}

the projection associating to any orbit
\hat{z}=\{z_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}

its element
z_{0}
. It follows from further estimates developed in
[
10
]
, that for every small
\varepsilon>0
, there exist some
r(\varepsilon)
and
n(\varepsilon)
such that, for all
x\in\pi(Z^{\star}_{\nu}(\varepsilon))
,
0<\kappa<r(\varepsilon)
, and
N\geq n(\varepsilon)
, the neighbourhood
U=U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,N)
of
x

satisfying

f^{N}(U)=B(f^{N}(x),\kappa\,e^{-NM\varepsilon})

where
e^{M}
is a bound for the expansion of
f
and
f^{N}|_{U}
is injective is well-defined. We set

\delta_{\nu,x}(\varepsilon,\kappa,N):=\frac{\log\nu(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,N))}{\log\sqrt{{\rm Vol}(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,N))}},

where
\rm Vol
denotes the volume
with respect to the Fubini-Study metric.

Remark 2.2
.

The above definition of
\delta_{\nu,x}
is different from the definition of
\delta_{\nu,x}
in
[
10
]
by adding an extra square root in the denominator.

Definition 2.3

(
[
10
]
)
.

(1)

The
lower local volume dimension

\underline{\delta}_{\nu,x}
at
x
is defined as

\underline{\delta}_{\nu,x}:=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\liminf_{\kappa\to 0}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\delta_{\nu,x}(\varepsilon,\kappa,N).

(2)

The
upper local volume dimension

\overline{\delta}_{\nu,x}
at
x
is defined as

\overline{\delta}_{\nu,x}:=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\limsup_{\kappa\to 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\delta_{\nu,x}(\varepsilon,\kappa,N).

(3)

The
local volume dimension

\delta_{\nu,x}
at
x
is defined as

\delta_{\nu,x}:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\delta_{\nu,x}(\varepsilon,\kappa,N)

if the limits exist.

2.3.
Volume dimension of sets

We continue to use the notation from Section
2.2
. In this section, we recall the definition of volume dimension
\VD_{f,\nu}(X)
of a set
X\subseteq\pi(Z_{\nu}^{\star}(\varepsilon))
introduced in
[
10
]
.

For every
X\subseteq\pi(Z^{\star}_{\nu})
and
\varepsilon>0
, setting
X^{\varepsilon}:=X\cap\pi(Z^{\star}_{\nu}(\varepsilon))
, we define the quantity

\VD^{\varepsilon}_{f,\nu}(X^{\varepsilon})
as

\VD^{\varepsilon}_{f,\nu}(X^{\varepsilon}):=\sup\left\{\alpha:\Lambda^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(X^{\varepsilon})=\infty\right\}=\inf\left\{\alpha:\Lambda^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(X^{\varepsilon})=0\right\},

where

\Lambda^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(X^{\varepsilon}):=\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\lim_{N^{\star}\to\infty}\inf_{\{U_{i}\}}\sum_{i\geq 1}{\rm Vol}(U_{i})^{\alpha/2}.

Here the infimum is taken over the covers consisting of sets
U_{i}
of the form
U_{i}=U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,N_{i})
, for some
x\in\pi(Z_{\nu}(\varepsilon))
and
N_{i}\geq N^{\star}
.

Remark 2.4
.

The above definition of
\Lambda^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(X^{\varepsilon})
is different from the definition of
\Lambda^{\varepsilon}_{\alpha}(X^{\varepsilon})
in
[
10
]
by adding an extra square root in
{\rm Vol}(U_{i})
.

Definition 2.5

(
[

]
)
.

For every

, the

\nu
of

is

(2.1)

Definition 2.6

(

]
)
.

The

\VD_{f}(\nu):=\inf\{\VD_{f,\nu}(X)\;:\;X\subseteq\pi(Z_{\nu}^{\star}){\rm~Borel},\nu(X)=1\}

Definition 2.7

(

]
)
.

Let

Theorem 2.8

(

3.
Pressure and temperature

In this section, we define and prove basic properties of the temperature function. Since the temperature function is defined using the pressure of a 2-parameter family of potentials, we first define this 2-parameter family of potentials and prove basic properties of the pressure function in Section
3.1
. We discuss the temperature function in Section
3.2
.
Throughout this section, we fix a holomorphic endomorphism
f\colon\mathbb{P}^{k}\to\mathbb{P}^{k}
of algebraic degree at least 2 and an expanding set
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{k}
.

3.1.
The pressure function

We begin by defining a 2-parameter family of potential functions that we will work with.
Let
g\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
be a Hölder continuous function. For
(q,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we consider the following 2-parameter family of potentials

(3.1)

\phi_{q,t}:=qg-t\log|\Jac f|.

Since
\phi_{q,t}
is Hölder continuous, we denote by
\nu_{q,t}
its unique equilibrium state.

Given a Hölder continuous function
\phi\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
, recall from
[
10
]
the definition of the pressure
\mathcal{P}_{X}^{+}(\phi)
of
\phi

\mathcal{P}_{X}^{+}(\phi):=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{X}^{+}(f)}\left\{h_{\nu}(f)+\int\phi d\nu\right\}

where the sup is taken over all
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{X}^{+}(f)
. By the variational principle, we have
\mathcal{P}_{X}^{+}(\phi)=\mathcal{P}_{X}^{\rm top}(\phi)
, where
\mathcal{P}_{X}^{\rm top}(\phi)
is the
topological pressure
that we recall now from
[
27
, Chapter 11]
.
Consider a sequence
\{\mathcal{V}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of open finite covers of
X
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\diam(\mathcal{V}_{n})=0.

Then the topological pressure is given by

\mathcal{P}_{X}^{\rm top}(\phi):=\lim_{n\to\infty}P(\phi,\mathcal{V}_{n})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\log\inf_{\mathcal{V}_{n}}\left\{\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}_{n}}\exp(\overline{S_{m}}\phi(V))\right\}

where
\overline{S_{m}}\phi(V):=\sup\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\phi\circ f^{k}(x):x\in V\right\}
.

Recall the 2-parameter family of potential functions
\phi_{q,t}
in (
3.1
). Up to replacing
g
by
g-\mathcal{P}_{X}^{+}(g)
, we can assume that
\mathcal{P}_{X}^{+}(g)=0
.
With this assumption, we set

\mathcal{P}(q,t):=\mathcal{P}_{X}^{+}(\phi_{q,t}).

Lemma 3.1
.

The pressure function
(q,t)\mapsto\mathcal{P}(q,t)
satisfies the following properties.

(1)

For any
(q,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have
\mathcal{P}(q,t)<\infty
.

(2)

The function
(q,t)\mapsto\mathcal{P}(q,t)
is real-analytic.

(3)

For each fixed
q\in\mathbb{R}
, the map
t\mapsto\mathcal{P}(q,t)
is strictly decreasing. Moreover, we have
\lim_{t\to-\infty}\mathcal{P}(q,t)=\infty
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{P}(q,t)=-\infty
.

(4)

\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial t}(q,t)=-\int\log|\Jac f|d\nu_{q,t}<0
.

(5)

\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}}{\partial q}(q,t)=\int g\;d\nu_{q,t}
.

(6)

If
g<0
, then we have
\mathcal{P}(q_{1},t_{1})\leq\mathcal{P}(q_{2},t_{2})
if
q_{1}\geq q_{2}
and
t_{1}\geq t_{2}
.

Proof.

To see (
1
), note that
(X,f)
is an expansive topological dynamical system in the sense of Urbanski-Roy-Munday
[
27
]
. By
[
27
, Proposition 11.3.2]
, for each
(q,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have

\mathcal{P}(q,t)\leq h_{top}(f)+\sup_{x\in X}\phi_{q,t}.

As
\phi_{q,t}
is Hölder continuous on
X
, we have
|\sup_{x\in X}\phi_{q,t}|<\infty
. Hence (1) follows.

For (
2
), by
[
28
, Theorem 13.10.3]
which shows that the mapping of a Hölder continuous potential
\phi:X\to\mathbb{R}
with exponent
\beta
to its topological pressure
\mathcal{P}_{X}^{\rm top}(\phi)
,
is real analytic and the fact that
(q,t)\mapsto\phi_{q,t}
is affine, we see that the composition
(q,t)\mapsto\mathcal{P}(q,t)
is real-analytic.

For (
3
), let
q\in\mathbb{R}
be fixed and let
t^{\prime}>t
. Then by the variational principal, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(q,t)

\displaystyle\geq h_{\nu_{q,t^{\prime}}}(f)+q\int g\;d\nu_{q,t^{\prime}}-t\int\log|\Jac f|d\;\nu_{q,t^{\prime}}

(3.2)

\displaystyle>h_{\nu_{q,t^{\prime}}}(f)+q\int g\;d\nu_{q,t^{\prime}}-t^{\prime}\int\log|\Jac f|d\;\nu_{q,t^{\prime}}=\mathcal{P}(q,t^{\prime}).

Therefore
t\mapsto\mathcal{P}(q,t)
is strictly decreasing. To see the last two statements, we write

\mathcal{P}(q,t)=h_{\nu_{q,t}}(f)+q\int gd\nu_{q,t}-t\int\log|\Jac f|d\nu_{q,t}.

Taking limits as
t\to\infty
and
t\to-\infty
then yields the result.

For (
4
), the argument is near identical to
[
28
, Theorem 16.4.10]
. Using the variational principal,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(q,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq h_{\nu_{q,t}}(f)+q\int g\;d\nu_{q,t}-t^{\prime}\int\log|\Jac f|d\;\nu_{q,t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}(q,t)+(t-t^{\prime})\int\log|\Jac f|d\nu_{q,t}.

Therefore,

\mathcal{P}(q,t^{\prime})\geq\mathcal{P}(q,t)+(t-t^{\prime})\int\log|\Jac f|\;d\nu_{q,t}.

If
t^{\prime}<t
, then
t^{\prime}-t<0
and

\frac{\mathcal{P}(q,t^{\prime})-\mathcal{P}(q,t)}{t^{\prime}-t}\leq-\int\log|\Jac f|\;d\nu_{q,t}<0.

Similarly, if
t^{\prime}>t
, then
t^{\prime}-t>0
and

\frac{\mathcal{P}(q,t^{\prime})-\mathcal{P}(q,t)}{t^{\prime}-t}\geq-\int\log|\Jac f|\;d\nu_{q,t}.

Taking limits and noting that
\mathcal{P}(q,t)
is real-analytic (and thus differentiable) finishes the argument. The proof of (
5
) is similar. The reader should note that the computation does not result in a negative sign in front of the integral in this case.

For (
6
), we follow
[
28
, Section 21.1]
. Let
q_{1}\geq q_{2}
and
t_{1}\geq t_{2}
. Then if
g<0
,
\phi_{q_{1},t_{1}}(x)\leq\phi_{q_{2},t_{2}}(x)
for all
x\in X
. Thus, for any open set
V
,
\overline{S_{m}}\phi_{q_{1},t_{1}}(V)\leq\overline{S_{m}}\phi_{q_{2},t_{2}}(V)
. Since
\exp(\cdot)
and
\log(\cdot)
are monotone increasing, and
\inf\{\cdot\}
preserves inequalities, the result follows.
∎

3.2.
The temperature function

In this section, we define the
temperature function
and study its properties. We continue to use notations from Section
3.1
. Since
X
is uniformly expanding, the arguments in this section are standard; see for example
[
6
,
20
,
21
]
.

Definition 3.2
.

The
temperature function

T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as
q\mapsto T(q)
, where
T(q)
is the unique number such that

\mathcal{P}(q,T(q))=0
.

Remark 3.3
.

By Lemma
3.1
(
3
), the above definition is well-posed. Moreover, we have
T(0)=\VD_{f}(X)
by
[
10
, Theorem 5.1]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

The temperature function
T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is real-analytic.

Proof.

Since the function
(q,t)\mapsto\mathcal{P}(q,t)
is real-analytic by Lemma
3.1
(
2
),
the conclusion follows from the Inverse Function Theorem once we establish

\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}(q,t)}{\partial t}\bigg|_{(q_{0},t_{0})}\neq 0\text{ for any }(q_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

By Lemma
3.1
(
4
), we have

\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}(q,t)}{\partial t}\bigg|_{(q_{0},t_{0})}=-\int_{X}\log|\Jac f|d\nu_{q_{0},t_{0}}\neq 0

where
\nu_{q_{0},t_{0}}
is the equilibrium state for
\phi_{q_{0},t_{0}}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Fix
q\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\nu_{q}
be the equilibrium state of
\phi_{q,T(q)}
. We define

(3.3)

\alpha(q):=-\frac{\int_{X}gd\nu_{q}}{\int_{X}\log|\Jac f|d\nu_{q}}>0.

Lemma 3.5
.

The temperature function
T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
T^{\prime}(q)=-\alpha(q)
for all
q\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, it is strictly decreasing.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{P}(\phi_{q,T(q)})=0
for any
q\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\frac{d}{dq}\mathcal{P}(\phi_{q,T(q)})=\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}(q,t)}{\partial q}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}(q,t)}{\partial t}\bigg|_{t=T(q)}T^{\prime}(q)=0.

Therefore, we have

T^{\prime}(q)=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}(q,t)}{\partial q}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}(q,t)}{\partial t}\bigg|_{t=T(q)}\right)^{-1}.

By Lemma
3.1
(
5
), we have

\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}(q,t)}{\partial q}\bigg|_{(q_{0},t_{0})}=\int_{X}gd\nu_{q_{0},t_{0}}\neq 0

where
\nu_{q_{0},t_{0}}
is the equilibrium state for
\phi_{q_{0},t_{0}}
.

Hence, we obtain

T^{\prime}(q_{0})=-\frac{\int_{X}gd\nu_{q_{0},t_{0}}}{-\int_{X}\log|\Jac f|d\nu_{q_{0},t_{0}}}=-\alpha(q_{0}).

The conclusion follows.
∎

Observe that
\nu_{0}
is the unique equilibrium state of the potential
\phi_{0,T(0)}=-2\VD_{f}(X)\log|\Jac f|
.

Lemma 3.6
.

The temperature function
T\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex. Moreover, it is strictly convex if and only if
\nu\neq\nu_{0}
.

Proof.

Differentiating
\mathcal{P}(\phi_{q,T(q)})
with respect to
q
twice, we obtain

T^{\prime\prime}(q)=-\frac{T^{\prime}(q)^{2}\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{P}(q,t)}{\partial t^{2}}+2T^{\prime}(q)\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{P}(q,t)}{\partial q\partial t}+\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{P}(q,t)}{\partial q^{2}}}{\frac{\partial\mathcal{P}(q,t)}{\partial t}}

where the partial derivatives are evaluated at
(q,t)=(q,T(q))
.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\mathcal{P}(-t\log|\Jac f|+qg)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\mathcal{P}(-(t_{0}+\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})\log|\Jac f|+qg)

\displaystyle={\rm Var}_{\phi_{q}}(\log|\Jac f|,\log|\Jac f|).

Here the (asymptotic) variance
{\rm Var}
is given by

{\rm Var}_{h}(h_{1},h_{2}):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{X}h_{1}(h_{2}\circ f^{k})d\mu_{h}-\int_{X}h_{1}d\mu_{h}\int_{X}h_{2}d\mu_{h}\right)

where
\mu_{h}
is the equilibrium state for
h
.

Similarly, we have

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial q^{2}}\mathcal{P}(-t\log|\Jac f|+qg)={\rm Var}_{\phi_{q}}(g,g),\text{ and }

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial q\partial t}\mathcal{P}(-t\log|\Jac f|+qg)={\rm Var}_{\phi_{q}}(g,\log|\Jac f|).

Hence, we obtain

T^{\prime\prime}(q)=\frac{{\rm Var}_{\phi_{q}}(g-T^{\prime}(q)\log|\Jac f|,g-T^{\prime}(q)\log|\Jac f|)}{\int_{X}\log|\Jac f|d\nu_{q}}.

Therefore,
T^{\prime\prime}(q)\geq 0
for any
q\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover,
T^{\prime\prime}(q)=0
if and only if the function
g-T^{\prime}(q)\log|\Jac f|
is cohomologous to a constant, if and only if
g
and
-T^{\prime}(q)\log|\Jac f|
are cohomologous. If
g
and
-T^{\prime}(q)\log|\Jac f|
are cohomologous, then
\mathcal{P}(-T^{\prime}(q)\log|\Jac f|)=\mathcal{P}(g)=0
, which implies
-T^{\prime}(q)=\VD_{f}(X)
and thus
\nu=\nu_{0}
.
∎

Corollary 3.7
.

If
\nu\neq\nu_{0}
, then the function
q\mapsto\alpha(q)
is strictly decreasing. If
\nu=\nu_{0}
, then
\alpha(q)=\VD_{f}(X)
for all
q\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Since
\alpha(q)=-T^{\prime}(q)
by Lemma
3.5
, the conclusion follows from Lemma
3.6
.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

We denote by
[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]\subset(0,\infty)
the range of the function
q\mapsto\alpha(q)
. By Corollary
3.7
, if
\nu=\nu_{0}
, then
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=\VD_{f}(X)>0
. If
\nu\neq\nu_{0}
, then
\alpha_{1}=\alpha(\infty)
and
\alpha_{2}=\alpha(-\infty)
. In this case,
\alpha_{2}=\alpha(-\infty)<\infty
since
\lim_{q\to-\infty}\frac{-\int gd\nu_{q}}{\int\log|\Jac f|d\nu_{q}}\leq\frac{\sup(-g)}{\inf\log|\Jac f|}<\infty
. Moreover,
\alpha_{1}=\alpha(\infty)>0
since
\frac{-\int gd\nu_{q}}{\int\log|\Jac f|d\nu_{q}}\geq|T(q_{0})|/q_{0}>0
for all
q
where
q_{0}>0
is a fixed number with
T(q_{0})<0
; see
[
21
, p. 254]
.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Let
f\colon\mathbb{P}^{k}\to\mathbb{P}^{k}
be a holomorphic endomorphism of algebraic degree at least 2,
X\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{k}
an uniformly expanding set,
g\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
a Hölder continuous function and
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{X}^{+}(f)
the equilibrium state of
g
.
For each
q\in\mathbb{R}
, recall the potential function
\phi_{q}=\phi_{q,T(q)}\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\phi_{q}:=qg-T(q)\log|\Jac f|

and its unique equilibrium state
\nu_{q}\in\mathcal{M}_{X}^{+}(f)
. Recall also
\alpha(q)
as defined in (
3.3
) and

J_{\nu}(\alpha(q))
as defined in (
2.2
).

Definition 4.1
.

The
parametrized volume dimension multifractal spectrum
is the map
\hat{S}_{\nu}\circ\alpha\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

\hat{S}_{\nu}(\alpha(q)):=\VD_{f,\nu_{q}}(J_{\nu}(\alpha(q))).

Remark 4.2
.

We sometimes need to regard
S_{\nu}(\alpha(q))
as a function of
\alpha
. We write
S_{\nu}(\alpha)
for
\alpha\in[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]
which is the range of
\alpha(q),q\in\mathbb{R}
. In this case, provided

,
\hat{S}_{\nu}(\alpha)=\VD_{f,\nu_{q}}(J_{\nu}(\alpha(q)))
where

is the unique real number such that
\alpha(q)=\alpha
. The uniqueness of

is guaranteed by Corollary
3.7
.

4.1.
Symbolic coding

Since
f\colon X\to X
is uniformly expanding, there exists a
Markov partition

\mathcal{R}:=\{R_{1},\ldots,R_{p}\}
of
X
so that the dynamical system
f\colon X\to X
is conjugate to a one-sided subshift of finite type
\sigma\colon\Sigma_{A}^{+}\to\Sigma_{A}^{+}
. We denote by
\pi\colon\Sigma_{A}^{+}\to X
the projection map.

If
\phi\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is a Hölder continuous function, then we denote by
\widetilde{\phi}:=\phi\circ\pi
, and it is also Hölder continuous. Both
\phi
and
\widetilde{\phi}
have a unique equilibrium state, denote by
\nu_{\phi}
and
\widetilde{\nu}_{\widetilde{\phi}}
respectively, and they satisfy
\nu_{\phi}=\pi_{*}\widetilde{\nu}_{\widetilde{\phi}}
.

If
\mu
is the equilibrium state of a Hölder continuous potential
\phi\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
, then
\mu
satisfies the
Gibbs property
(see
[
28
, Section 13.2]
). That is, for every
\omega\in\Sigma_{A}^{+},\varepsilon>0,0<\kappa<r(\varepsilon)
and
m>n(\varepsilon)
, we have

(4.1)

C_{1}\leq\frac{\nu(U(\pi(\omega),\varepsilon,\kappa,m))}{\prod_{k=0}^{m-1}e^{\phi(\pi(\sigma^{k}(\omega)))}}\leq C_{2}

where
C_{1},C_{2}>0
are constants. In particular,
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
may be taken such that
C_{2}>1
and
C_{1}=C_{2}^{-1}
.
Recall
r(\varepsilon),n(\varepsilon)
from Section
2.2
.

Given
\varepsilon>0,\kappa>0,N>0
, let
r=r(\varepsilon,\kappa,N)>0
be a small positive real number depending on
\varepsilon,\kappa
and
N
. For
\omega\in\Sigma_{A}^{+}
, let
m(\omega)=m(\omega,\varepsilon,\kappa,N)
be the unique positive integer such that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{m(\omega)}\left|\Jac f(\pi(\sigma^{k}(\omega)))\right|^{-1}>r\text{ and }

(4.3)

\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{m(\omega)+1}\left|\Jac f(\pi(\sigma^{k}(\omega)))\right|^{-1}\leq r.

In our applications,
r
will be

{\rm Vol}(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,N))^{1/2}
for a fixed
x\in X
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Fix
x\in X
and
\phi\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
a Hölder continuous potential with equilibrium state
\mu
. Further, let
\omega\in\Sigma_{A}^{+}
be such that
\pi(\omega)=x
and for given
\varepsilon>0,0<\kappa<r(\varepsilon),N>n(\varepsilon)
, set
r={\rm Vol}(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,N))^{1/2}
.
Then we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,m(\omega)))}{\log r}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,N))}{\log r}

should either limit exist.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
0<\kappa<r(\varepsilon)
.
Note that by inequality (
4.2
),
[
10
, Corollary 2.4 and Lemma 2.7]
, we have

(4.4)

m(\omega)\geq-\frac{k\log\kappa}{L_{\nu}+k\varepsilon}+N\left(\frac{L_{\nu}-k\varepsilon}{L_{\nu}+k\varepsilon}\right)-1.

For all
N>n(\varepsilon)
sufficiently large, note that

(4.5)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,m(\omega)))}{\log r}\geq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,N))}{\log r}

is immediate as
m(\omega)>N
by (
4.4
) and the
U
sets are nested. Now we show the other direction.
By the Gibbs property of
\mu
(
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle\log

\displaystyle\;\mu(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,m(\omega)))\geq\log C_{1}+\sum_{k=0}^{m(\omega)-1}\phi(\pi(\sigma^{k}(\omega)))

\displaystyle=\log C_{1}-\log C_{2}+\log C_{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{m(\omega)-1}\phi(\pi(\sigma^{k}(\omega)))

\displaystyle\geq\log\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}+\log\mu(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,N))+\sum_{k=N}^{m(\omega)-1}\phi(\pi(\sigma^{k}(\omega)))

\displaystyle\geq\log\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}+\log\mu(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,N))+(m(\omega)-N)\inf\phi.

So for all N large enough, we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle\frac{\log\mu(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,m(\omega)))}{\log r}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log\mu(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,N))}{\log r}+\frac{\log\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}}{\log r}+\left(\frac{m(\omega)-N}{\log r}\right)\inf\phi.

Consider the term
\left(\frac{m(\omega)-N}{\log r}\right)
. By (
4.4
) and
[
10
, Lemma 2.7]
, we obtain

(4.7)

\left(\frac{m(\omega)-N}{\log r}\right)\leq\frac{-\frac{k\log\kappa}{L_{\nu}+k\varepsilon}+N\left(\frac{L_{\nu}-k\varepsilon}{L_{\nu}+k\varepsilon}-1\right)-1}{k\log\kappa-N(L_{\nu}+k(2M+2)\varepsilon)}.

And so we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\frac{m(\omega)-N}{\log r}\right)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{\kappa\to 0}-\left(\frac{\frac{L_{\nu}-k\varepsilon}{L_{\nu}+k\varepsilon}-1}{L_{\nu}+k(2M+2)\varepsilon}\right)=0.

Hence, taking limits
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}
on both sides of (
4.6
) gives

(4.8)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,m(\omega)))}{\log r}\leq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log\mu(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,N))}{\log r}.

The result then follows from (
4.5
) and (
4.8
).
∎

Lemma 4.4

For every

Proof.

Denote by

The conclusion follows.

q\geq 0
,

q\alpha_{1}\leq 4+q\inf_{\rho}\frac{\int_{X}gd\rho}{-\int_{X}\log|\Jac f|d\rho}.

Dividing both sides by
q
and letting
q\to\infty
, we obtain

\alpha_{1}\leq\inf_{\rho}\frac{-\int_{X}gd\rho}{\int_{X}\log|\Jac f|d\rho}.

This completes the proof.
∎

6.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We first show that
\alpha_{1}=\inf_{x\in X}\underline{\delta}_{\nu,x}
. Suppose
\alpha_{1}<\inf_{x\in X}\underline{\delta}_{\nu,x}
. This implies that there exists a large
q_{0}\gg 1
such that
J_{\nu}(\alpha(q_{0}))
is empty, which contradicts Proposition
4.5
. Therefore
\alpha_{1}\geq\inf_{x\in X}\underline{\delta}_{\nu,x}
.

Now suppose
\alpha_{1}>\inf_{x\in X}\underline{\delta}_{\nu,x}
. Then using Lemma
4.6
, there exist
\eta>0,x\in X
and a sequence
\{n_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that for each
k\geq 1
, we have

\frac{-\sum_{j=0}^{n_{k}}g(f^{j}(x))}{\sum_{j=0}^{n_{k}}\log|\Jac f(f^{j}(x))|}\leq\alpha_{1}-\eta.

For each
k\geq 1
, consider the measure
\rho_{k}:=\frac{1}{n_{k}}\sum_{j=0}^{n_{k}}\delta_{f^{j}(x)}
where
\delta_{y}
denotes the Dirac mass at the point
y
. Then we have

\frac{-\int_{X}gd\rho_{k}}{\int_{X}\log|\Jac f|d\rho_{k}}\leq\alpha_{1}-\eta.

Let
\rho
be an accumulation point of the sequence
\{\rho_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
of measures. Then taking the limit
k\to\infty
, we have

\frac{-\int_{X}gd\rho}{\int_{X}\log|\Jac f|d\rho}\leq\alpha_{1}-\eta.

If
\rho
is ergodic, then this contradicts Lemma
6.2
. If
\rho
is not ergodic, then since the set of periodic measures is dense, we approximate
\rho
by a sequence of periodic measures
\tilde{\rho}_{n}
. Then there exists a sufficiently large
n
such that
\frac{-\int_{X}gd\tilde{\rho}_{n}}{\int_{X}\log|\Jac f|d\tilde{\rho}_{n}}\leq\alpha_{1}-\eta/2
, which also contradicts Lemma
6.2
. Therefore we must have
\alpha_{1}=\inf_{x\in X}\underline{\delta}_{\nu,x}
.

The statement
\alpha_{2}=\sup_{x\in X}\overline{\delta}_{\nu,x}
can be proved in a similar way.
∎

9.
Properties of the multifractal decomposition

Recall that we have the following multifractal decomposition of the set
X
, i.e.,

X=J^{\prime}_{\nu}\cup\left(\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb{R}}J_{\nu}(\alpha(q))\right).

Lemma 9.1
.

For every
q\in\mathbb{R}
, the set
J_{\nu}(\alpha(q))
is dense in
X
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.6
, it suffices to show that
\pi(\hat{X}_{q})
is dense in
X
. For any
\omega\in\Sigma^{+}_{A}
, set

\alpha(\omega):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g(\pi(\sigma^{k}(\omega)))}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\log|\Jac f(\pi(\sigma^{k}(\omega)))|^{-1}}.

Now, let
\mathcal{O}
be an open set in
X
. Since the Markov partition weakly partitions
X
, there exists an
n
for which there is an
S\in\bigvee_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathcal{R}
with
S\subseteq\mathcal{O}
. Let
w(S)
be the finite admissible word encoding
S
.

Next let
\omega\in\hat{X}_{q}
. Since
f\colon X\to X
is exact, it is transitive, and thus the associated adjacency matrix
A
for
\Sigma_{A}^{+}
defining the admissibility condition is irreducible, so there exists a finite word
u
such that
w(S)_{|w(S)|}u\omega_{0}
is admissible. Then,

\alpha(\omega)=\alpha(w(S)u\omega)=\alpha(q).

Hence
\pi(w(S)u\omega)\in S\cap\pi(\hat{X}_{q})\subseteq\mathcal{O}
. As
\mathcal{O}
was arbitrary,
\pi(\hat{X}_{q})
is dense in
X
.
∎

Lemma 9.2
.

The set
J_{\nu}^{\prime}
is non-empty.

Proof.

We show that there exists
x\in X
such that
\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{\kappa\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\log\nu(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,N))}{\log{\rm Vol}(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,N))^{1/2}}
does not exist. By Gibbs property (
4.1
),
\log C_{2}+S_{N}g(x)\leq\log\nu(U(x,\varepsilon,\kappa,N))\leq\log C_{1}+S_{N}g(x)
. It suffices to show that there exists
x\in X
such that the limit of Birkhoff averages
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}S_{N}g(x)
does not exists.

Let
\rho_{1},\rho_{2}
be two equilibrium states such that
\int_{X}gd\rho_{1}\neq\int_{X}gd\rho_{2}
. By Birkhoff’s ergodic theorem, there exist points
y_{i}
typical for
\rho_{i}
such that

\frac{1}{N}S_{N}g(y_{i})\to\int_{X}gd\rho_{i}
. In the mixing subshift of finite type
\Sigma_{A}^{+}
, we construct an infinite word
\omega
by concatenating alternatingly long blocks cut from the orbits of
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
, in such a way that the lengths of the blocks satisfy
L_{1}\ll M_{1}\ll L_{2}\ll M_{2}\cdots
. Here
L_{i}
(resp.
M_{i}
) is the length of the
i
th block of orbits of
y_{1}
(resp.
y_{2}
).

Then the partial sums
\frac{1}{m}S_{m}g(\pi(\omega))
will alternating between
\int_{X}gd\rho_{1}
and
\int_{X}gd\rho_{2}
. Therefore the limit
\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}S_{m}g(\pi(\omega))
does not exist. Thus
\pi(\omega)\in J_{\nu}^{\prime}
.
∎
