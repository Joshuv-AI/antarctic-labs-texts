Subclavian artery pseudoaneurysm treated with surgery following endovascular balloon occlusion: a case report
Authors: Jaeik Jang, Jayun Cho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Subclavian vessel injury concomitant with a closed clavicle fracture is rare, and challenges associated with exposure often make proximal control difficult. This paper presents the case of a 26-year-old man who presented to the emergency department after a fall from the sixth floor with multiple injuries, including closed clavicle fractures and a subclavian artery pseudoaneurysm. Proximal control was achieved with endovascular balloon occlusion rather than by performing cervical extension of median sternotomy. Subsequently, exposure was obtained through a subclavian incision, and the injury was treated with a bypass graft.

INTRODUCTION

Key pitfalls in managing subclavian vessel injuries include the difficulty of anticipating exposure for proximal control. Attempting exposure through the deltopectoral groove without first securing proximal control can lead to exsanguination. Proximal control may be facilitated by clavicular bone resection; alternatively, simultaneous supraclavicular and infraclavicular incisions can be used to mitigate the associated morbidity [1]. Given the density of vascular structures at the thoracic outlet and the morbidity associated with thoracic incisions, endovascular techniques for proximal control of subclavian artery injuries, as in this case, may be advantageous.

Vascular injuries associated with closed clavicle fractures from blunt trauma are less common than those arising from penetrating mechanisms [2]. To our knowledge, subclavian artery pseudoaneurysms caused by blunt trauma and accompanied by closed clavicle fractures are extremely rare [3]. Hybrid treatment strategies involving endovascular balloon occlusion followed by open surgical bypass have been reported only sporadically [4].

This report presents the case of a 26-year-old man who arrived at the emergency department after a fall from the sixth floor. A closed clavicle fracture with associated subclavian artery injury was managed with endovascular balloon occlusion for proximal control followed by bypass grafting.

CASE REPORT

A 26-year-old man presented to the emergency department with multiple injuries, including leg pain, after a fall from the sixth floor. He had no significant family history, and aside from alleged bipolar disorder and impulse control disorder, no other medical history was reported. He had severe pain in the left distal thigh. On inspection, a prominent degloving injury was noted on the left thigh, and multiple abrasions were observed on the feet. Minor bruising of the right shoulder and swelling of the right temporal scalp were also observed. The temperature and capillary refill of the limbs were normal; however, the patient appeared confused, and assessment of distal motor and sensory function was not possible.

The patient was immediately evaluated in the emergency department, and arterial blood gas analysis revealed a lactate level of 5.3 mmol/L and a base excess of −5.5 mmol/L. His hemoglobin level was 14.4 g/dL. Subsequent whole-body computed tomography (CT) imaging, including the brain, face, cervical spine, chest, abdomen, and femoral regions, revealed a right temporal bone fracture; pneumocephalus; acute compression fractures of T6–T10; acute comminuted fractures of the right scapula, clavicle, and proximal humerus; and suspected anterior arc fracture of the right third to sixth ribs. Additionally, a large hematoma with a 2-cm right subclavian artery pseudoaneurysm (Fig. 1A, B) was identified, along with a small hemothorax and mild lung contusion in the right lower lobe. Because of a distracting left thigh injury and the patient’s confusion, the trauma team initially overlooked the right subclavian artery pseudoaneurysm, which was recognized the day after presentation. Furthermore, a small amount of hemoperitoneum around the liver and fractures of the left tibia from the proximal to distal segments were noted. The patient’s Injury Severity Score was 17. Angiography performed the following day revealed enlargement of the subclavian pseudoaneurysm to approximately 30 mm (Fig. 1C).

Due to the missed diagnosis of right subclavian artery injury on CT, treatment was not initiated on the day of injury. A trauma surgeon controlled the bleeding from the left thigh degloving injury in the operating room, after which an orthopedic surgeon performed open reduction and internal fixation of the left tibial fracture.

The following day, right subclavian artery injury was confirmed, and treatment was initiated. The treatment modality was decided in consultation between interventional radiologists and trauma surgeons. Initially, endovascular balloon occlusion was performed by a radiologist, followed by subclavian artery repair by a trauma surgeon. The patient underwent ultrasound-guided insertion of a left common femoral artery sheath in the angiography suite. Subsequent arteriography revealed a 3-cm pseudoaneurysm of the right subclavian artery, which was controlled with balloon occlusion using a 6-mm-diameter, 4-cm-long catheter (Fig. 1D). The patient was then promptly transferred to the operating room, where exploration involved retraction of the fractured proximal clavicle and division of the pectoralis minor muscle (Fig. 2A). A trauma surgeon performed thrombectomy with a Fogarty no. 4 catheter (Edwards Lifesciences) and interposition using a 6-mm-diameter, 4-cm-long ringed polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) graft from the subclavian artery to the proximal axillary artery (Fig. 2B, C). Vascular injury to branches of the subclavian vein required ligation, and the subclavian artery was repositioned anteriorly to enable vascular anastomosis above the subclavian vein (Figs. 2B, 3C). Wound closure included reapproximation of the divided pectoralis minor muscle. Concomitant perioperative findings included a suspected injury to the inferior trunk of the brachial plexus and a suspected phrenic nerve injury due to anterior scalene muscle injury. Fortunately, no diaphragm elevation suggestive of phrenic nerve injury was observed on follow-up radiography, and no hoarseness attributable to recurrent laryngeal nerve injury was noted.

Following consultation with the otolaryngology department, conservative management was chosen for the right temporal bone fracture. For pneumocephalus, conservative treatment was selected, and follow-up brain CT was performed the next day. Additionally, conservative management was chosen for acute compression fractures at T6–T10.

On hospital day 5, the orthopedic surgeon performed open reduction and internal fixation with plate and cable wiring for the fragmented, displaced, and comminuted fractures of the clavicular shaft (Fig. 3A, B). Conservative treatment was administered for injuries to the right scapula, humerus, and brachial plexus.

Postoperative follow-up CT angiography showed maintained patency of the subclavian artery (Fig. 3C), and no change was noted in the vertebral artery between preoperative and postoperative imaging (Fig. 3D, E). No abnormalities of the carotid artery were observed (Fig. 3F).

On hospital day 21, the patient was discharged with paresthesia, decreased sensation, and motor impairment of the right arm. Circulation remained intact.

One year later, the patient showed good distal circulation, with residual motor impairment and paresthesia limited to the shoulder. Motor and sensory function in the wrist and elbow had nearly recovered.

DISCUSSION

According to Raviraja et al. [5], clavicle fractures accompanied by subclavian artery injury from blunt trauma are rare. Various treatment modalities have been used, including open surgery, endovascular management, hybrid procedures, vascular closure devices, and endovascular-to-surgical conversion, depending on the characteristics, location, and mechanism of the injury [6,7]. Although covered stents are often favored for their minimal invasiveness, we opted against this strategy for two reasons. First, the injured segment was in the costoclavicular space adjacent to the thoracic outlet, an area subjected to repetitive mechanical stress from shoulder movement [8,9]. Second, while endovascular repair has demonstrated excellent outcomes within the first 5 years after intervention in both atherosclerotic and traumatic subclavian artery lesions [10–12], durability beyond this period remains uncertain, which is especially relevant in young trauma patients with decades of life expectancy. Open surgical repair using ringed PTFE grafts has shown more consistently durable patency in disease-related subclavian stenosis and may be preferable when long-term vessel integrity is paramount [13]. Accordingly, surgical bypass with a ringed PTFE graft was considered the safest and most durable approach in this setting. We fully acknowledge the advantages of endovascular treatment in trauma settings, particularly in minimizing collateral damage in inflamed or anatomically complex areas. However, in this patient, the anatomic configuration and patient-specific factors necessitated a durable repair. The hybrid approach provided secure proximal control through ballooning while enabling definitive repair with graft placement under direct vision.

The choice between open surgery and endovascular management varies depending on the mechanism of injury and anatomical location. Axillary injuries and penetrating mechanisms are often treated with open surgery, whereas subclavian injuries and blunt mechanisms are commonly managed with endovascular treatment due to the challenges associated with surgical access [14]. Selecting open surgery for subclavian artery injuries can complicate proximal control and may require median sternotomy [1]. However, there has been a recent trend toward endovascular management [15]. In 2023, Torres et al. [14] reported 62 cases of subclavian and axillary artery injuries, of which 81% were treated using an endovascular technique. Nevertheless, no consensus has yet been established that favors one treatment over the other regarding long-term patency. In a 2023 retrospective study of 33 patients by Hanif et al. [12], the patency rate after endovascular stenting for subclavian artery injuries was 100% (four of four patients), whereas open repair had a rate of 50% (four of eight patients); however, both results were based on short follow-up durations of up to 24 months, a period insufficient to assess long-term durability. Moreover, endovascular management with a stent may pose disadvantages, such as the risk of stent fracture [8]. In 2019, Vértes et al. [9] reported 43 cases of stent fracture in a follow-up study of 122 subclavian artery stents in 120 patients.

Endovascular stenting for subclavian artery injury carries the risk of stent fracture over time, whereas open surgery alone poses challenges in obtaining proximal control. In contrast, the hybrid approach used in this case offers distinct advantages: bypass grafting eliminates the risk of stent fracture, and endovascular balloon occlusion facilitates secure proximal control. In this case, proximal control was achieved with endovascular balloon occlusion, followed by open surgery with graft placement. This hybrid approach, which combines endovascular and open surgical techniques, has been reported only in a few case studies, with no large-scale studies available [4]. Therefore, this case is clinically significant, suggesting that this strategy may be an option for subclavian artery injury resulting from blunt trauma accompanied by clavicle fracture. Further research is warranted to evaluate the long-term outcomes of this approach.
