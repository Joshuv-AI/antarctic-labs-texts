Early interventions for the prevention of preeclampsia in pregnant women at increased risk: a network meta‐analysis
Authors: Alexandros Sotiriadis, Ismini Tsakiri, Michail Papapanou, Maria Toulaki, Maria Zoi Bourou, Charalampos S Siristatidis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis is a protocol for a Cochrane Review (intervention). The objectives are as follows:To compare and rank the benefits and harms of interventions for the prevention of preeclampsia in pregnant women at increased risk who are up to 16+6 weeks of gestation, using network meta‐analysis.

Background

Description of the condition

Preeclampsia is a complex, multisystem disorder of pregnancy. It is a leading cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality worldwide, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries [1, 2]. The incidence of preeclampsia ranges globally from 2% to 8% of pregnancies and varies by geographic region, socioeconomic conditions, and population characteristics, including ethnicity, differences in underlying risk factors, and access to care [1].

Preeclampsia's pathogenesis is multifactorial and involves maternal and placental components [3]. According to the conventional two‐stage model, which best describes early‐onset disease associated with foetal growth restriction (FGR), the development of preeclampsia occurs in two key stages. The first stage involves abnormal placentation, characterised by inadequate trophoblastic invasion and poor remodelling of the maternal spiral arteries, leading to reduced uteroplacental perfusion and placental ischaemia. The second stage is driven by the release of anti‐angiogenic and pro‐inflammatory factors from the ischaemic placenta into the maternal circulation. These circulating factors cause widespread endothelial dysfunction, resulting in hypertension, proteinuria, and multi‐organ involvement [1, 4]. In simple terms, preeclampsia is a multisystem disorder of pregnancy involving high blood pressure and organ dysfunction, driven largely by placental problems and posing risks to both mother and baby. However, recent research has emphasised the role of pre‐existing maternal cardiovascular and endothelial predisposition in the pathogenesis of placental dysfunction itself, which blurs the boundaries between the maternal and placental elements [5, 6].

Numerous maternal, foetal, and environmental risk factors have been associated with the development of preeclampsia [4]. Established risk factors include a history of preeclampsia in a prior pregnancy, chronic hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, and autoimmune disorders such as systemic lupus erythematosus and antiphospholipid syndrome [4, 7, 8]. First pregnancy, multiple gestation, advanced maternal age, obesity, and assisted reproductive technologies also elevate the risk [4, 8]. Finally, hereditary predisposition and a family history of preeclampsia suggest a genetic component, while environmental and lifestyle factors, including poor nutrition and low socioeconomic status, may further increase susceptibility [4, 9].

Preeclampsia was traditionally defined by the onset of hypertension and substantial proteinuria after 20 gestational weeks [4]. However, its definition has been broadened in recent years. The International Society for the Study of Hypertension in Pregnancy's (ISSHP) definition of preeclampsia is widely used today and is based on the following criteria [10]:

systolic blood pressure of 140 mmHg or higher and/or diastolic blood pressure of 90 mmHg or higher in previously normotensive women, measured at least twice, four hours apart, and accompanied by one or more of the following new onset symptoms after 20 weeks' gestation:proteinuria (≥ 300 mg in a 24‐hour urine collection, protein to creatinine ratio ≥ 0.3, dipstick reading ≥ 2++);signs of maternal organ dysfunction (thrombocytopenia, liver function tests at least two times over normal or severe persistent right upper quadrant or epigastric pain, serum creatinine concentration > 1.1 mg/dL or doubling of creatinine in the absence of other renal disease, pulmonary oedema, or new‐onset cerebral or visual symptoms);signs of uteroplacental dysfunction (foetal growth restriction, abnormal umbilical artery Doppler waveform analysis, or stillbirth).

Although rather simplistic, preeclampsia is clinically classified into early‐onset preeclampsia, occurring before 34 weeks of gestation, and late‐onset preeclampsia, occurring at or after 34 weeks [1]. A similar classification differentiates between preterm and term preeclampsia (< 37 and ≥ 37 weeks, respectively) [1]. These two subtypes differ in pathophysiology, clinical presentation, and outcomes. Early‐onset preeclampsia is primarily associated with abnormal placental development and poor trophoblastic invasion, leading to significant placental insufficiency. It is commonly linked to foetal growth restriction, small‐for‐gestational‐age (SGA) infants, and higher rates of preterm birth and perinatal complications [1]. In contrast, late‐onset preeclampsia is more often related to maternal metabolic and cardiovascular factors, with relatively preserved placental function and better foetal outcomes [2]. However, both forms carry risks of maternal complications, such as eclampsia (a serious complication of pregnancy in which a woman with preeclampsia develops seizures due to the severe effects of high blood pressure‐related disease), HELLP syndrome (the acronym describes its key features: i.e. haemolysis; elevated liver enzymes; low platelet count), and organ dysfunction [1].

Distinguishing between early‐onset and late‐onset preeclampsia is crucial for risk stratification, monitoring, and management to optimise maternal and neonatal health. Screening for preeclampsia aims to identify women at high risk early in pregnancy, so that preventive measures and close monitoring can be initiated. Current guideline‐based approaches, including those from the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), differ in their recommended risk assessment strategies but are both based on maternal clinical history [1, 7, 11]. NICE recommends a risk‐based assessment using predefined maternal clinical risk factors at the first antenatal visit, with reported sensitivity ranging from 22% to 47% and low diagnostic accuracy [2]. In contrast, ACOG incorporates a broader range of moderate‐risk indicators, with somewhat higher detection rates (60% to 65%) but also higher screen‐positive rates. Despite these differences, both approaches are limited by their reliance on clinical history, without the routine incorporation of biophysical or biochemical markers. This has prompted the development of more comprehensive prediction models [12]. The Fetal Medicine Foundation (FMF) has developed a first‐trimester screening model that integrates maternal demographic and clinical characteristics with biophysical and biochemical markers at 11 to 13+6 weeks’ gestation. Its markers include mean arterial pressure (MAP), uterine artery Doppler pulsatility index (UtA‐PI), serum pregnancy‐associated plasma protein‐A, and, if available, placental growth factor (PlGF) [12]. The FMF's model has been reported to detect approximately 70% to 75% of preterm preeclampsia cases at a 10% false‐positive rate [2], suggesting improved predictive performance compared with traditional risk factor‐based screening strategies. In women classified as high risk according to NICE or ACOG criteria but as low risk according to the FMF algorithm, the observed risk of preterm preeclampsia appears to be close to or below baseline levels [13].

Description of the intervention and how it might work

Researchers have investigated a range of pharmacological and nutritional interventions for the prevention of preeclampsia, including low‐dose aspirin, heparins, statins, antioxidant therapies, calcium supplementation, and polyunsaturated fatty acids, each targeting proposed mechanisms involved in disease development.

Low‐dose aspirin is an evidence‐based strategy and has emerged as a safe and effective prophylactic intervention when administered during early pregnancy, particularly among high‐risk populations [14]. Current evidence supports the initiation of low‐dose aspirin (typically 75 mg to 150 mg daily) before 16 weeks of gestation, since it has been associated with a substantial reduction in the incidence of preeclampsia, especially its preterm forms [14]. Low‐dose aspirin is believed to improve placental perfusion through its antiplatelet and anti‐inflammatory effects, as well as through a direct effect on the invading trophoblast [15].

Low molecular weight heparin (LMWH), traditionally used for thromboprophylaxis, has shown potential in reducing the risk of preeclampsia, particularly in women with underlying thrombophilia or a history of placental‐mediated pregnancy complications (PMPCs) [16]. LMWH may improve placental function by enhancing uteroplacental blood flow, modulating trophoblast invasion, and reducing inflammation [16]. Integrating LMWH into preventive care protocols may theoretically act synergistically with strategies such as low‐dose aspirin in high‐risk populations, but the evidence is less consistent and weaker than for low‐dose aspirin [17].

Unfractionated heparin has also been explored for the prevention of preeclampsia, particularly in women with thrombophilia or prior adverse pregnancy outcomes, but evidence on its effectiveness is limited and inconclusive [18]. Unfractionated heparin may exert beneficial effects through its anticoagulant, anti‐inflammatory, and endothelial‐stabilising properties, potentially improving placental perfusion and reducing microthrombi formation in the uteroplacental circulation [19].

Given the role of oxidative stress in the pathogenesis of preeclampsia, antioxidant therapies have been investigated for their potential preventive benefits [20]. Vitamin C, a potent antioxidant, could theoretically improve endothelial function and reduce oxidative damage in the placenta [20]. Increasing evidence suggests that vitamin D plays a role in placental development, immune modulation, and endothelial function – all key factors implicated in the pathogenesis of preeclampsia [9]. Vitamin E, a lipid‐soluble antioxidant, has been proposed as a potential preventive agent due to its ability to reduce oxidative damage and support vascular health [21]. Current evidence suggests that vitamins C and E have not been shown to prevent preeclampsia and are not recommended, while vitamin D may be beneficial in deficient women but is not currently advised specifically for preeclampsia prevention.

Calcium supplementation has been proposed as a preventive strategy due to its role in vascular smooth muscle function and blood pressure regulation [21]. While the World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended high‐dose calcium (≥ 1000 mg/day) for women with low dietary calcium intake, the effectiveness of low‐dose calcium supplementation (< 1000 mg/day) in preventing preeclampsia remains less clear [21]. Authors of a 2023 network meta‐analysis on prophylactic strategies for preeclampsia concluded that even modest calcium supplementation may reduce the risk of preeclampsia, particularly in populations with baseline calcium deficiency [22].

Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), particularly omega‐3 fatty acids, have been assessed for their anti‐inflammatory, vasodilatory, and endothelial‐stabilising properties, which may play a role in mitigating the pathophysiology of preeclampsia. Current data show no consistent or clinically significant reduction in the overall incidence of preeclampsia [23].

Recent interest has focused on the potential role of statins, particularly pravastatin, due to its pleiotropic effects, including anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and endothelial‐stabilising properties. Statins are biologically promising, but have not yet demonstrated consistent clinical benefit for preventing preeclampsia; thus, they are not considered as a standard preventive therapy outside the context of clinical trials [3]. Evidence suggests that pravastatin may improve placental perfusion, reduce anti‐angiogenic factors such as soluble fms‐like tyrosine kinase‐1 (sFlt‐1), and mitigate endothelial injury [3, 24].

While each component may be generally well‐tolerated at low doses, the safety and efficacy of their concurrent use remain to be fully established.

Why it is important to do this review

Preeclampsia is associated with approximately 46,000 maternal deaths and 500,000 foetal or newborn deaths yearly worldwide [25, 26]. It results in a wide range of maternal complications across multiple organ systems, such as eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, acute renal failure, myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke, and other neurologic and ophthalmologic complications [7, 27]. Additionally, preeclampsia is associated with impaired foetal growth, stillbirths, neonatal mortality, and other adverse perinatal outcomes. It can lead to a variety of long‐term health impairments in infants, such as respiratory distress syndrome, cerebral palsy, and other neurodevelopmental disorders [28]. According to a multi‐country study, 25% of stillbirths were attributable to SGA, and the highest risk was observed for premature SGA foetuses [29]. Furthermore, in 2020, half of the 2.4 million neonatal deaths worldwide were attributed to conditions affecting small and vulnerable newborns, with preterm and term SGA infants representing major contributors [30]. Moreover, preeclampsia is significantly associated with higher medical costs due to prolonged length of hospitalisation, caesarean delivery, preterm deliveries, and infant complications [31]. Therefore, preeclampsia and SGA pregnancies are important contributors to maternal and neonatal mortality and morbidity rates, posing considerable clinical and economic challenges.

Several systematic reviews have evaluated individual preventive interventions for preeclampsia and related outcomes. For example, a 2019 Cochrane review examined the use of antiplatelet agents, including low‐dose aspirin and dipyridamole, in women at risk of developing preeclampsia or SGA pregnancies [32]. Another Cochrane review assessed the effects of calcium supplementation during pregnancy for the prevention of preeclampsia [33], and a further review is underway to evaluate the role of statins for this indication [34]. However, the published Cochrane reviews focus on single interventions, and the trial evidence they synthesise is somewhat dated. To the best of our knowledge, there is no up‐to‐date synthesis comparing and ranking multiple preventive interventions for preeclampsia in pregnant women at increased risk. Given the substantial burden of preeclampsia and its associated maternal and perinatal complications worldwide, a network meta‐analysis may help to address this gap by enabling simultaneous comparison and ranking of available interventions.

With this Cochrane network meta‐analysis, we will compare all interventions aimed at preventing preeclampsia in women at increased risk. We aim to identify which interventions offer the highest efficacy and lowest risk, using both direct and indirect evidence, further informing and guiding clinical practice.

Objectives

To compare and rank the benefits and harms of interventions for the prevention of preeclampsia in pregnant women at increased risk who are up to 16+6 weeks of gestation, using network meta‐analysis.

Methods

We will follow guidance in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and Methodological Expectations for Cochrane Intervention Reviews (MECIR) when executing the review [35, 36, 37, 38]. We will ensure the review follows the PRISMA 2020 and PRISMA extension for network meta‐analyses (PRISMA‐NMA) guidelines for reporting [39, 40]. We adhered to guidance in these resources when developing the Background and Methods sections.

Criteria for considering studies for this review

Types of studies

We will include published and unpublished randomised controlled trials (RCTs), cluster‐RCTs, and cross‐over trials, using data from the first phase only, before cross‐over. We will exclude quasi‐randomised and pseudo‐randomised studies. We will use data from conference abstracts and contact authors for additional information, if necessary. We will apply no restrictions regarding the year of dissemination or language of publication [35, 36, 41].

Types of participants

We will include pregnant women identified as being at high risk of preeclampsia (PE), who are up to or at 16+6 weeks’ gestation at the time of intervention initiation. We will accept all definitions of 'high risk' applied by study authors, provided they are explicit and clear. Definitions of high risk may vary. We will accept both risk‐based screening (e.g. ACOG, NICE) and combined screening models [2, 7, 11, 12].

We will include studies with singleton pregnancies only and studies with both singleton and twin pregnancies if the population of twin pregnancies is less than 10% of the study's total population. We will contact authors of studies with mixed populations to request data specific to singleton pregnancies. We will exclude studies in which the cause of preeclampsia in participants has been identified as anti‐phospholipid syndrome (APS).

Types of interventions

We will include the following early (administered before or at 16+6 weeks of gestation) interventions for prevention of preeclampsia in women at high risk: low‐dose aspirin (less than 300 mg per day), low molecular weight heparin (LMWH), unfractionated heparin, vitamin C, vitamin D, vitamin E, calcium, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), pravastatin, and combinations of these treatments. We will apply no restrictions on the doses of heparins, vitamin C, vitamin D, vitamin E, calcium, PUFAs, or pravastatin. Each intervention will be compared to each other, placebo, or no intervention. We will not exclude studies based on the duration of the intervention.

Outcome measures

We selected outcomes to capture maternal hypertensive disease, placental dysfunction and foetal growth, pregnancy course, and perinatal consequences. We prioritised SGA as we anticipated that it may be more consistently reported across trials, whereas foetal growth restriction definitions vary and often require Doppler data, despite being a more specific marker of placental insufficiency.

To detect RCTs, we will combine the PubMed search with the Cochrane highly sensitive search strategy [44]. We will combine the trial filters developed by the Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN) with our Embase search. We will not apply any language or date restrictions. We developed our search strategy based on the guidelines in Chapter 4 of the Cochrane Handbook of Systematic Interventions [44, 45]. We will not involve an information specialist in this review, but we will adhere to the guidance in Chapter 4. The full search strategy is available in Supplementary material 1.

Searching other resources

We will check the reference lists of the retrieved studies, relevant reviews, and conference proceedings from major conferences (e.g. ACOG, Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG), European Board & College of Obstetrics (EBCOG), European Society of Gynaecology (ESG)) for studies that the main search strategy may miss.

We will search relevant grey literature sources for additional studies, such as the Google Scholar database, where we will examine the first 200 records. Furthermore, we will search the Epistemonikos database, a multilingual database of health evidence that aggregates systematic reviews and other types of evidence synthesis from multiple sources. We will consult experts in the field to help us identify ongoing or existing studies.

To safeguard the quality and integrity of included studies, we will check all records in PubMed for retraction notices, expressions of concern, or other post‐publication updates, and will take these into account during study selection and data interpretation.

Data collection and analysis

Selection of studies

We will import all records identified through the electronic searches into EndNote and remove duplicates [46]. We will then upload the deduplicated records to Covidence for title and abstract screening [47]. Working independently, two review authors (of IT, MT, MZB) will screen titles and abstracts to identify records that meet the review's inclusion criteria. We will exclude clearly irrelevant articles. We will attempt to retrieve the full texts of the remaining potentially eligible studies. Working independently, two review authors (of IT, MT, MZB) will screen the full texts of the articles for eligibility. We will exclude ineligible studies and report the reasons for exclusion in the 'Characteristics of excluded studies' table. If we remain uncertain about a study's eligibility, we will contact authors to ask for additional information and clarification. Should any disagreements between the review authors arise, we will consult a third review author (AS, CSS, or MP) to decide on the matter. We will outline the study selection process in a PRISMA flow diagram.

Assessment of study integrity

Working independently, two review authors (IT, MT) will evaluate studies meeting our inclusion criteria for trustworthiness, using the TRACT checklist [48], a screening tool used to flag potentially problematic or unreliable RCTs. TRACT considers domains such as trial governance and registration, authorship, study design and conduct, data integrity and plausibility, and consistency of reporting. We will consider indicators such as prospective registration, plausibility of recruitment and data patterns, completeness of reporting, and consistency between trial documents and published results. We will assess each study as having no concerns, some concerns, or major concerns, with justification based on information from the trial report, protocol, and trial registration (where available) [48]. If we identify major concerns, we will attempt to contact study authors for clarification. If concerns remain unresolved, we will categorise the study as ‘awaiting classification’ and report the reasons for this decision. A third review author (AS, CSS, or MP) will resolve any disagreements.

Data extraction and management

Working independently, two review authors (of IT, MT, MZB) will extract study characteristics and outcome data from the reports of each included study, using a data extraction form pilot‐tested on at least two included studies. A senior review author (AS, CSS, or MP) will resolve any disagreements between junior authors [49, 50]. We will extract data on study characteristics and the above‐mentioned critical and important maternal and neonatal outcomes according to the methodology described in Chapter 5 of the Cochrane Handbook [49]. We will import extracted data into a Microsoft Excel file. We will collate multiple reports of the same study, so that each study (rather than each report) is the unit of interest in the review. We will identify the primary reference for each study and retain secondary references. We will contact study authors if we require further information about study methods or data.

Risk of bias assessment in included studies

Working independently, two review authors (of IT, MT, MZB) will assess the risk of bias in the included studies according to methods described in Chapters 7 and 8 of the Cochrane Handbook [51, 52]. A third review author (AS, CSS, or MP) will resolve any conflicts. We will assess risk of bias using the Cochrane risk of bias tool version 2 (RoB 2) for randomised controlled trials, including the appropriate adaptations for cluster‐randomised and cross‐over trials [53]. The RoB 2 tool assesses bias across five domains: bias arising from the randomisation process; bias due to deviations from intended interventions; bias due to missing outcome data; bias in measurement of the outcome; and bias in selection of the reported result [54].

We will complete the signalling questions for each domain and derive domain‐level judgements of 'low risk of bias', 'some concerns', or 'high risk of bias' according to the RoB 2 algorithm [51, 52, 54]. We will support judgements with information from trial publications, protocols, and trial registrations, where available. We will derive an overall risk of bias judgement for each outcome based on the domain‐level assessments, classifying studies as low risk of bias (all domains low risk), some concerns (at least one domain with some concerns but none at high risk), or high risk of bias (at least one domain at high risk or multiple domains with some concerns), in line with RoB 2 guidance [51, 52, 54]. Any departures from the algorithm judgements will be made by consensus among the review authors and will be fully justified and documented, with reference to the trial report, protocol, and registration data where available. Where information is insufficient to make a judgement, we will contact study authors for clarification.

We will use the RoB 2 Excel tool to store and display our judgements and explanations (available from www.riskofbias.info/). We will publish this as a supplementary file.

For the domain of 'bias due to deviations from intended interventions', we will examine the intention‐to‐treat (ITT) effect, meaning we will evaluate the results based on the originally assigned intervention, regardless of adherence.

We will assess risk of bias using the RoB 2 tool for each included study at the outcome level for all outcomes included in the synthesis (see Outcome measures).

Measures of treatment effect

All outcomes are expected to be dichotomous. We will express treatment effects as risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Where necessary, we will ensure consistency in the direction of effect across studies by appropriate transformation of outcome data. We will present results from direct (pairwise), indirect, and network meta‐analysis comparisons using forest plots [38, 55].

Relative treatment ranking

We will provide intervention hierarchies for each outcome by estimating cumulative ranking probabilities using Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking (SUCRA) values [56]. We will derive these rankings within a Bayesian framework using non‐informative priors, based on 20,000 posterior simulations. We will rank interventions as superior if they have a higher cumulative probability of reducing the investigated outcomes. We will present the SUCRA intervention hierarchies using cumulative rankograms [56].

Unit of analysis issues

Since we will include only singleton pregnancies in the analyses, the unit of analysis will be the individual pregnant woman randomised [35]. Regarding pairwise meta‐analyses, if a single trial involves three or more arms, we will divide the control group into equal parts, assuming equal incidence in these groups. Regarding network meta‐analyses, we will include multi‐arm studies and will account for correlations between comparisons. We will exclude ineligible study arms from the analysis (though we will summarise all study arms in the 'Characteristics of included studies' table).

For cluster‐RCTs, we will extract the ICC from each trial's data. If the ICC is not available, we will extract it from an RCT or an observational study with a similar population. If none of the above is possible, we will conduct a sensitivity analysis for different ranges of ICCs. We will combine the results of cluster‐RCTs with the other included RCTs only if the results are reasonably homogeneous. We will record the number of clusters per group, the total size of clusters per group, and the unit of randomisation.

Dealing with missing data

We will perform all analyses on an intention‐to‐treat (ITT) basis. In case of missing data, we will contact study authors. If we receive no response, we will send a reminder 20 days after the initial communication [35]. If we are unable to obtain missing data, we will address this using an imputation approach for the critical outcome of preeclampsia. We will present two scenarios: a best‐case scenario, in which none of the participants with missing data experienced preeclampsia, and a worst‐case scenario, in which all participants with missing data experienced preeclampsia. For other outcomes, we will analyse the available data with the assumption that no events occurred in the participants lost to follow‐up. If we identify substantial missing data (> 10%) in certain studies, we will conduct sensitivity analyses excluding these studies to assess the impact of data loss on the effect estimates.

Reporting bias assessment

We will evaluate reporting bias by comparing the included studies' planned critical and important outcomes at registration or protocol stage with the outcomes they ultimately report. In instances where results are not available, we will contact study authors to request the unreported results [38, 57].

We will employ comparison‐specific funnel plots to screen for small‐study effects and publication bias, if an adequate number of studies (more than 10) report the same outcome. If visual inspection suggests funnel plot asymmetry, we will consider this as potential evidence of publication bias and incorporate it into our assessment of the certainty of evidence. However, if the number of available studies is small, our ability to detect publication bias will be limited, and we will acknowledge this as a limitation where relevant.

Synthesis methods

We will compare all interventions (i.e. low‐dose aspirin, LMWH, unfractionated heparins, vitamins C, D, and E, calcium, PUFAs, pravastatin, and combinations) with one another and with placebo or no treatment.

Methods for direct treatment (pairwise) comparisons

We will first conduct pairwise meta‐analyses for each comparison only if at least two trials are included and the studies are sufficiently homogeneous in terms of their participants, interventions, and outcomes to provide a clinically meaningful summary, in accordance with guidance in Chapter 10 of the Cochrane Handbook [38, 58]. If this is not the case, we will follow the Synthesis Without Meta‐analysis (SWiM) approach [59].

Given the expected clinical heterogeneity (e.g. different baseline risks for preeclampsia among included populations, different expected definitions of high‐risk populations), we will fit random‐effects models. We will assess clinical heterogeneity by documenting clinical and methodological characteristics of the included studies. We will estimate the between‐study variation using a restricted maximum likelihood (REML) approach and calculate the CI for the summary effect using a Wald‐type CI [55, 60]. We will further assess the presence of statistical heterogeneity by visually inspecting the forest plots and calculating the I2 statistic [58, 61]. We will interpret an I2 value above 50% as indicative of substantial heterogeneity.

We will use Review Manager (RevMan) software for these analyses [62]. We will present the results of the direct comparisons in separate supplementary forest plots.

Methods for indirect and network comparisons

We will visualise the networks and evaluate their connectivity via network diagrams [37, 63]. We will conduct a frequentist random‐effects network meta‐analysis to generate pooled log‐transformed RRs with 95% CIs. The log‐transformed estimations will be back‐transformed to RRs to facilitate the interpretation of results. We will present the results in forest plots [56]. We will perform all analyses using the "network" package in Stata version 15 [64, 65].

Assessment of transitivity across treatment comparisons

We will assess transitivity by recording and comparing the clinical and methodological characteristics of studies across the different treatment comparisons. We will consider the transitivity assumption to hold if (1) the common comparator used across the network is sufficiently similar when it appears in different comparisons, and (2) there are no important differences in the distribution of potential effect modifiers (e.g. study design, gestational age at intervention initiation, and baseline risk profiles) among the different comparisons.

Assessment of inconsistency and heterogeneity

We will evaluate inconsistency using both global and local approaches. We will assess global inconsistency using the design‐by‐treatment interaction model, which can account for discrepancies that arise when studies with different designs (e.g. two‐arm versus three‐arm trials) yield different results. This method also captures any disagreement between direct and indirect sources of evidence. We will compute the global approach for overall inconsistency according to the type of between‐treatment comparison for all cases, and will then use the values to test for global linearity using the Wald test [66, 67]. We will also perform local inconsistency testing using the loop‐specific and node‐splitting approaches. The loop‐specific method examines inconsistency within closed loops of the network, while the node‐splitting method evaluates the agreement between direct and indirect evidence for a specific comparison in the loop [68]. For the latter, we will apply the symmetrical variant proposed by White [69]. We will quantify heterogeneity at the network level using the estimated between‐study variance (Tau2) from the respective random‐effects network meta‐analysis. We will assume a single common heterogeneity parameter for all comparisons across the network. We will use the REML estimator for the Tau2 as above [60].

Investigation of heterogeneity and subgroup analysis

If we identify important heterogeneity, inconsistency, or both, we will conduct subgroup analyses for the review’s critical outcomes (preeclampsia and SGA). For pairwise meta‐analyses, we will evaluate subgroup differences using the formal test provided in RevMan, in accordance with guidance in Chapter 10 of the Cochrane Handbook [58]. If fewer than five studies are available per subgroup, we will interpret the findings cautiously and avoid emphasising them, as they are unlikely to support meaningful conclusions. We will perform subgroup analyses by country income level, using World Bank classifications (high‐ and upper‐middle‐income versus low‐ and lower‐middle‐income countries).

We will examine subgroup differences by comparing the network diagram for each subgroup, performing a network meta‐analysis for each subgroup, and comparing their relative treatment effects and relative treatment ranking.

Equity‐related assessment

As previously mentioned, we will explore whether intervention effects vary by country income level using the World Bank Country Classifications for 2024–2025 [70]. For multi‐country trials, we will assign the study to the income category of the country contributing the largest proportion of participants. Access to antenatal care and early preventive strategies, screening pathways, and referral capacity likely differ by healthcare‐system resources. Therefore, the effects of early preventive methods for preeclampsia may vary across income settings. Moreover, women in low‐ and lower‐middle‐income countries may experience higher preeclampsia‐related morbidity and face greater barriers to timely care [71].

In addition, there may be differences in baseline equity‐relevant characteristics between the high‐risk populations included in the review (e.g. differences related to race/ethnicity or maternal age). In order to address these differences, and in accordance with the PROGRESS‐Plus domains, where applicable, we will also record and report relevant demographic data (e.g. race/ethnicity, maternal age; see Data extraction and management) [72, 73]. Because definitions of race/ethnicity vary across studies, we will extract verbatim categories and explicitly report them in the 'Characteristics of included studies' table. By examining the contexts in which the included studies were undertaken and the characteristics of the populations included, we will determine the extent to which the generalisability of our findings may be affected, and appraise the completeness and applicability of the evidence across diverse populations and settings.

Using GRADEpro software [75], we will develop summary of findings tables for all outcomes included in the network meta‐analyses. We will assess the certainty of the evidence separately for individual pairwise treatment comparisons and for treatment ranking estimates.

Specifically, two review authors (IT and MT) will independently assess the certainty of evidence for each comparison using the GRADE approach, categorising certainty as high, moderate, low, or very low. We will use the definitions for each level of certainty detailed in Chapter 5 of the GRADE Handbook [76]. We will apply the five standard downgrading domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The overall RoB 2 assessment for each result will feed into the GRADE assessment. Any disagreements between authors will be resolved by discussion or, if necessary, by a third author (AS, CSS, or MP).

For indirect and network estimates, we will use the contribution matrix framework according to the Confidence in Network Meta‐Analysis (CINeMA) approach [77]. This approach is more suitable for our review, which we expect to involve many interventions. We will express each network estimate as a weighted sum of the available direct comparisons, with weights reflecting the percentage contribution of each direct comparison. This will allow us to combine the domain‐specific GRADE assessments of the direct comparisons, weighted by their contribution, to inform the certainty of the indirect and network estimates [37].

We will assess indirectness by evaluating how closely the evidence aligns with the review PICO (women at high risk of preeclampsia receiving early interventions) and by examining transitivity—that is, whether differences in populations, intervention characteristics, or study settings could modify treatment effects. We will assess inconsistency by jointly evaluating heterogeneity within direct comparisons and incoherence between direct and indirect evidence, using both local approaches (e.g. node‐splitting) and global approaches (e.g. design‐by‐treatment interaction). We will judge imprecision based on the 95% confidence intervals of the network estimates and, when presenting intervention rankings, by considering the overlap of ranking distributions.

Consumer involvement

Due to restricted resources, we will not involve consumers in this review. We selected outcomes in accordance with the Core Outcome Measures in Effectiveness Trials (COMET) core outcome set for preeclampsia, which were developed with consumer involvement [78].
