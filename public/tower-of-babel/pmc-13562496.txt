Green synthesis of zanthoxylum rhetsa (roxb.) DC. mediated iron oxide nanoparticles with physicochemical characterisations and in vitro biomedical applications
Authors: Sumangal S. Kale, Ajay P. Nikum, Vijay L. Gurav, Ghanasham B. Sathe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A sustainable and eco-friendly approach was developed for the green synthesis of iron oxide nanoparticles (ZR-FeONPs) using the aqueous leaf extract of Zanthoxylum rhetsa (Roxb.) DC., where phytochemicals served as reducing and stabilizing agents. UV-Visible spectroscopy revealed a characteristic absorption peak at 275 nm, while FTIR analysis showed distinct bands (593.57–441.40 cm-1) corresponding to Fe-O vibrations confirming nanoparticle formation. XRD analysis established the crystalline hematite-phase Fe2O3 with an average crystallite size of 17.87 nm. Morphological studies using FEG-SEM and TEM demonstrated quasi-spherical, polydisperse particles with an average size of ~38–40 nm. EDX analysis confirmed elemental composition dominated by Fe (67.7 wt %) and O (29.5 wt %). The nanoparticles exhibited moderate colloidal stability (-24 mV) and superparamagnetic behavior with a saturation magnetization of 47 emu/g. Biological evaluations revealed significant antibacterial (18-22 mm) and moderate antifungal (13–15 mm) activities. The nanoparticles also showed dose-dependent antioxidant (51.81%, IC = 58.19 µg/mL), anti-inflammatory (52.32%, IC50= 95.62 µg/mL) and antidiabetic (56.32%, IC50= 82.34 µg/mL) properties. Additionally, moderate anti-tuberculosis activity (50.23% at 1000 µg/mL) and notable anticancer effects (44.32% inhibition against MCF-7 cells) were observed. Cytotoxicity studies indicated high biocompatibility, with >84% viability in L929 cells. Overall, this study highlights Z. rhetsa (Roxb.) DC. as an effective and underexplored bioresource for synthesizing multifunctional, biocompatible magnetic nanoparticles with promising applications in nanomedicine and therapeutic development.Graphical abstract The graphical abstract illustrates the green synthesis of iron oxide nanoparticles using Zanthoxylum rhetsa (Roxb.) DC. leaf extract, wherein the phytoconstituents function as both reducing and stabilizing agents to convert iron precursor salts into stable nanoparticles. The process of bio-reduction and subsequent capping is represented through cyclic pathways leading to the formation of well-defined iron oxide nanoparticles. Detailed characterization using multiple analytical techniques confirms their structural, morphological, elemental, surface and magnetic properties. Furthermore, the synthesized nanoparticles are evaluated for diverse invitro biomedical applications highlighting their considerable potential in the field of nanomedicine.

Introduction

Nanotechnology has revolutionized the field of materials science through the incorporation of extremely small particles typically ranging from 1 to 100 nanometers which significantly influence material design and properties. This rapidly developing field is multidisciplinary in nature and encompasses a wide range of applications including bioactivity, energy storage, agriculture and environmental remediation [1]. Nanosized particles have attracted significant attention due to their wide range of applications, ease of synthesis, and ability to undergo surface modification. This category includes various transition metals (d-block elements) which exhibit diverse functionalities and are widely applied in magnetic separation, targeted drug delivery, medical imaging and cancer therapy [2, 3].

Iron oxide nanoparticles (FeONPs) are particularly valued for their superparamagnetic properties which make them highly suitable for applications such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), magnetically guided drug delivery and cancer hyperthermia therapy [4]. Their environmentally friendly nature and redox-active properties also make them effective for the removal of pollutants including heavy metals and hazardous organic compounds [5]. However, conventional synthesis methods for FeONPs such as co-precipitation, sol-gel and thermal decomposition [6] often involve toxic chemicals, high energy requirements and the generation of hazardous by-products. These limitations restrict their applicability particularly in biomedical and environmentally sensitive applications[7]. To overcome these limitations, green synthesis has emerged as a safer and more sustainable alternative. This eco-friendly approach utilizes biological systems, including microbes, algae, fungi and plants, for the synthesis of nanoparticles [8, 9]. Plant-based synthesis is particularly advantageous due to its simplicity, scalability and abundance of phytochemicals that facilitate both the reduction and stabilization of metal nanoparticles. Bioactive compounds such as flavonoids, alkaloids and terpenoids play a crucial role in converting metal ions into nanoparticles and stabilizing them, thereby preventing aggregation [10]. Plant extracts are rich in bioactive secondary metabolites, including phenolics, alkaloids, flavonoids, tannins, saponins and proteins. These compounds facilitate the reduction of metal ions into nanoparticles while simultaneously preventing agglomeration resulting in stable and functional nanostructures [11]. Furthermore, water-based (aqueous) extraction methods enhance biocompatibility by avoiding the use of harmful solvents and adhering to the principles of green chemistry. The literature reports the successful biosynthesis of FeONPs using various medicinal plants. Sargassum muticum has been widely studied for its strong reducing properties [12]. Other plants such as Camellia sinensis (green tea) [13] and Lawsonia inermis [14] have also demonstrated similar efficacy in reducing Fe3+ ions to iron oxide nanoparticles, with promising antimicrobial and biomedical applications. Biosynthesized iron oxide nanoparticles (FeONPs) have demonstrated significant antimicrobial activity against a wide range of bacterial and fungal strains [15–17]. In addition, FeONPs exhibit notable antioxidant properties by scavenging free radicals such as DPPH and inhibiting lipid peroxidation, which is beneficial in mitigating oxidative stress related conditions including cancer and neurodegenerative disorders [18]. Owing to their superparamagnetic behavior and biocompatibility, FeONPs also find extensive biomedical applications including targeted drug delivery, tissue regeneration, diagnostic imaging and wound healing where magnetic guidance enhances therapeutic precision [19, 20].

Although Indian Ayurveda encompasses a vast array of medicinal plants many species with significant traditional therapeutic value remain underexplored particularly in the field of nanoscience. Zanthoxylum rhetsa (Roxb.) DC., commonly known as “Indian prickly ash” or “Tirfal,” is widely used in Ayurveda and folk medicine for the treatment of infections, inflammation, toothache, fever and digestive disorders [21, 22]. Its rich phytochemical profile includes bioactive compounds such as berberine, flavonoids, lignans, tannins and essential oils, which are known to exhibit antioxidant, antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory activities [23, 24]. Despite its rich phytochemical composition Zanthoxylum rhetsa (Roxb.) DC. has been scarcely explored for nanoparticle synthesis, with only limited studies reporting the formation of silver [25] and gold nanoparticles [26]. Its potential for the green synthesis of iron-based nanoparticles remains largely untapped, representing a promising research opportunity. The green synthesis mechanism generally involves the phytochemical-mediated reduction of metal ions (e.g., Fe3+→Fe2+) followed by nucleation and subsequent growth of nanoparticles. Bioactive constituents such as polyphenols, proteins and other capping agents play a crucial role in stabilizing the nanoparticles, regulating their size and morphology and preventing aggregation [27–29].

The present study aims to develop an eco-friendly and sustainable approach for the synthesis of iron oxide nanoparticles using phytochemicals derived from Zanthoxylum rhetsa (Roxb.) DC. as reducing and stabilizing agents. It further seeks to address existing research gaps by exploring the potential of this medicinal plant in the green synthesis of iron-based nanoparticles and evaluating their suitability for biomedical applications. By integrating the principles of phytochemistry, nanotechnology and sustainable chemistry this work intends to contribute to the development of biocompatible and environmentally benign nanomaterials.

Materials and methods

Ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3.6H2O) purchased from Sigma Aldrich and used as received, Zanthoxylum rhetsa (Roxb.) DC. leaf extract, Whatman filter paper No. 41 and Distilled water. All cell line were used for invitro studies was purchased from National Centre for Cell Science, Savitribai Phule Pune University Campus Ganeshkhind Road, Pune - 411007. Maharashtra State, India

Collection of plant samples

Fresh plant specimens of Zanthoxylum rhetsa (Roxb.) DC. were collected from Satarda village (15.7586° N, 73.8007° E) in Sawantwadi, Sindhudurg district, Maharashtra, India. The collected material was thoroughly washed with tap water followed by distilled water to remove adhering dust and surface impurities. The plant was taxonomically identified and authenticated by Prof. Dr. U. L. Dethe a recognized botanist, taxonomist and Head of the Department of Botany at S.P.K. Mahavidyalaya, Sawantwadi, Maharashtra, India. A voucher specimen (No. 6-R/682/2025; SSK01) was prepared and deposited for future reference.

Preparation of plant extract

Freshly collected Zanthoxylum rhetsa (Roxb.) DC. leaves were properly washed and dried in the shade at room temperature to preserve their natural components. Then grind into fine powder using an electric grinder. 5 g of Zanthoxylum rhetsa (Roxb.) DC. leaves powder was mixed with 100 cm3 of distilled water and boiled for one hour to make an extract. After cooling, the solution was filtered through Whatman filter paper No. 41. This extract was stored at 04°C in glass bottle for further use.

Green synthesis of ZR-FeONPs

For this green synthesis, 50 mL of Zanthoxylum rhetsa (Roxb.) DC. leaf extract was mixed with 50 mL of 0.1 M ferric chloride hexahydrate solution (FeCl3.6H2O). The mixture was swirled at 2000 rpm for 8 h using a magnetic stirrer (Remi model number 5 ML). Color change to deep black confirmed the formation of ZR-FeONPs. The formed nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation, washed several times with distilled water, then dried in a hot air oven at 80°C. It was calcinated in a muffle furnace at 550°C. The yield of synthesised nanoparticle found to be approximately 5%. Prepared ZR-FeONPs used for further characterisation and various bio-medicinal applications. Characterisation techniques for ZR-FeONPs

The average crystallite size of ZR-FeONPs was evaluated using an XRD-Ultra IV diffractometer (Rigaku Corporation, Japan) and calculated through the Scherrer formula, D = 0.9λ/βcos θ. Optical characteristics were analyzed by UV-Vis spectroscopy over the 200-800 nm wavelength range with a resolution of 1 nm using a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer (Japan). Functional groups responsible for nanoparticle stabilization were identified using FTIR spectroscopy (Bruker Alpha II, USA) within the 4000-400 cm-1 region. Surface morphology and structural attributes were examined through FEG-SEM using a QUANTA 200 instrument (Netherlands). Elemental composition was determined with an integrated EDX detector attached to the same instrument at an accelerating voltage of 20 keV. Particle size was further assessed using a Tecnai 12 G2 TEM operated at 20-120 kV. The surface charge and colloidal stability were measured using a laser Doppler electrophoresis-based SZ-100V2 Zeta Potential analyser (Horiba, Japan). Magnetic properties were investigated at room temperature using a CryoMagTech CMT-60 VSM across a magnetic field range of −10,000 to +10,000 Oe.

In vitro bio medicinal applications of ZR-FeONPs

Antibacterial activity

The assessment of antibacterial efficiency of ZR-FeONPs was done through the agar-well diffusion method. Under this method, a nutrient agar medium was formulated using peptone, NaCl agar and distilled water. The medium was autoclaved to sterilized. These media were transferred in sterile Petri plate sand were left to set. Using sterile L shaped rod, bacterial strains were inoculated on the media which included Gram positive (Bacillus cereus ATCC 14579, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213) and Gram negative (Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, Proteus vulgaris ATCC 8427) rods. Later, four wells were punched with sterile borer and volumes of ZR-FeONPs (100 μL) were added to the plate. The values are taken as means of triplicate sets. Streptomycin antibiotic was used as a standard control for the comparison. Plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 h. The zone of inhibition was then calculated with the help of a millimetre scale [30, 31].

Antifungal activity

The antifungal effectiveness of ZR-FeONPs was investigated against the fungal strains Candida albicans ATCC 10231 and Aspergillus niger ATCC 1015 using a modified Saubroad agar (Hi Media) diffusion technique. To achieve this, Peptone agar medium was created and sterilized in an autoclave. The medium was then transferred into sterile Petri dishes and permitted to solidify. The spore suspension of the fungus was applied to the PDA agar plate. Boreholes were utilized to establish four wells. Amounts of ZR-FeONPs (100 μL) were placed in the wells of the Petri dish and incubated at 27°C for 24 h. The experiment was conducted using three identical sets of values. The area of inhibition surrounding the wells was measured in millimetres [30, 31].

Antioxidant activity

The scavenging activity of synthesized ZR-FeONPs was estimated using 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical by the scavenging method [32]. The reaction mixture included 1 mL of respected samples of different concentrations with 1.5 mL of 0.1% DPPH radical solution prepared in methanol and incubated further for 30 min. in dark. The absorbance of the mixture was recorded at 510 nm using a spectrophotometer (UV-1800, Shimadzu, Japan). Results and discussion

Characterisation of green synthesised ZR-FeONPs

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

XRD pattern of the synthesized ZR-FeONPs reveals the diffraction peaks observed at 2θ values at 24.120, 33.138, 35.599, 40.817, 49.468, 54.085, 62.508 and 64.033 respectively, as indicated by their indices of the (012), (104), (104), (113), (024), (116), (214) and (300) crystal planes respectively shown in Fig. 1. These peaks align with the standard JCPDS card No. 89-8104 [40], confirming the formation of rhombohedral hematite-phase Fe2O3 structure with potential incorporation of Fe ions enhancing stability and altering crystallinity with average crystallite size 17.87 nm. The sharp and intense diffraction peaks suggest a high degree of crystallinity and purity of the nanoparticles. The absence of any additional impurity peaks further validates the successful synthesis via phytochemical constituents in plant extracts, which act as reducing and stabilizing agents. Several studies have confirmed that plant-mediated synthesis of iron oxide nanoparticles (FeONPs) results in nanocrystals with similar structural characteristics. For instance, Platanus orientalis for FeONPs synthesis, reporting comparable peak intensities and lattice planes [41], effective crystalline nature shown by using Fraxinus chinensis [42], Albizia adianthifolia extract also showed reflections matching hematite phases, confirming the role of plant phenolics and flavonoids in nucleating uniform crystals [15] respectively. Peltophorum pterocarpum reporting enhanced crystallinity and magnetic properties similar to what is observed in this study [43]. Overall, the XRD pattern supports the successful green synthesis of ZR-FeONPs with good crystallite nature and phase purity, suitable for various biomedical and environmental applications.Fig. 1XRD peaks of ZR-FeONPs

XRD peaks of ZR-FeONPs

Ultraviolet-Visible Spectroscopy (UV-Vis)

The UV-Visible absorption spectrum of the green-synthesized ZR-FeONPs exhibits a distinct peak at 275 nm, as shown in Fig. 2, which is characteristic of Fe2+-O2- charge transfer transitions and confirms the formation of iron-based nanoparticles. The pronounced absorption in this ultraviolet region may also be attributed to the presence of surface-bound bioactive phytochemicals including phenolics, flavonoids, terpenoids and alkaloids derived from the plant extract which contribute to both stabilization and optical properties of the nanoparticles. Unlike noble metal nanoparticles such as gold and silver, iron oxide nanoparticles do not typically exhibit a distinct Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) band in the visible region due to their non-metallic nature and different electronic structure. In the present study, the absorption peak observed at approximately 275 nm is attributed to Fe2+-O2- charge transfer transitions along with contributions from phytochemical constituents of the plant extract. The absence of any characteristic absorption peak in the visible region (400-800 nm) confirms the lack of SPR behavior, which is consistent with previously reported studies on plant-mediated iron oxide nanoparticles. For instance where plant extracts such as Cistus monspeliensis [44], Laurus nobilis [45], Fraxinus chinensis [42], Glycosmis mauritiana [46], Calotropis procera [47] were employed to synthesize iron oxide nanoparticles with UV-Vis absorbance maxima in the 230-280 nm region confirming bio-assisted nanoparticle formation. The stability and intensity of the absorption peak also support the effective stabilization of nanoparticles results affirm that ZR-FeONPs were successfully synthesized using a plant-mediated eco-friendly route and their UV-Vis profile aligns well with previously reported green-synthesized iron oxide nanomaterials.Fig. 2UV-Visible absorption spectrum of ZR-FeONPs

The FTIR spectrum of the green-synthesized ZR-FeONPs reveals distinct peaks that confirm both the formation of FeONPs and the involvement of plant-based functional groups in their reduction and stabilization. A broad absorption band observed around 3415.24 cm-1 corresponds to O-H stretching vibrations of hydroxyl groups indicating the presence of alcohols and phenolic compounds from the plant extract, which act as natural reducing agents. The peak at 2920.94 cm-1 is attributed to C-H stretching of aliphatic -CH2 groups, typically from organic plant molecules. A peak at 1614.50 cm-1 corresponds to C = C stretching, suggesting the presence of aromatic. The absorption at Peaks observed in the range 600-400 cm-1 particularly near 593.57, 538.49, 471.06 and 441.40 cm-1 are characteristic of Fe-O stretching vibrations, confirming the successful formation of iron oxide nanoparticles as shown in Fig. 3. These spectral features align with prior studies using Glycosmis mauritiana [46], Calotropis procera [47], pine tree needles [48], Azardirachta Indica [49] where FTIR analysis similarly confirmed the participation of polyphenolic and proteinaceous compounds from plant extracts in nanoparticle formation and surface capping. Thus, the FTIR spectrum supports that ZR-FeONPs were synthesized through a green route where plant phytoconstituents acted both as reducing and stabilizing agents ensuring biocompatibility and functional surface chemistry.Fig. 3FTIR analysis of ZR-FeONPs

Field Emission Gun-Scanning Electron Microscopy (FEG-SEM)

FEG-SEM images synthesized of ZR-FeONPs provide valuable insights into their surface morphology and microstructural features. At higher magnification (Fig. 4. i.e. a) and b) which shows 10000× and 4,000×) the nanoparticles appear as aggregated clusters with irregular morphology and a rough surface texture. It indicates partial agglomeration which is commonly reported in green-synthesized nanoparticles due to the presence of bioorganic stabilizing agents derived from plant extracts. These capping agents i.e. secondary metabolites contribute to the particle formation but can also promote partial aggregation during drying and imaging as noted in FeONPs synthesized using Calotropis procera [50] and aloe vera [51] extracts. Lower magnification images (Fig. 4. i.e. c) and d) respectively 1,000× and 500×) show flake-like larger structures intermixed with fine particles suggesting the presence of a heterogeneous mixture of nano and micro-scale fragments possibly arising from plant metabolites or unreacted precursors. Similar morphologies have been observed in FeONPs synthesized from Calotropis procera [47] and Ruellia tuberosa [52] where the nanoparticle surfaces were coated with organic matter from the extract, contributing to bio-functional surface properties. Thorough and porous nature of the particles as evident in the FEG-SEM images is advantageous for catalytic, antimicrobial and environmental applications due to enhanced surface area and reactivity. These morphological features are in line with previous reports where green synthesis led to polydisperse, non-spherical iron oxide nanoparticles with bio-organic coatings, enhancing their stability and biocompatibility [53].Fig. 4FEG-SEM magnification of ZR-FeONPs i.e. a) 10000×, b) 4000×, c) 1000× and d) 500×

Energy dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX)

The Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) analysis of ZR-FeONPs confirms the elemental composition and purity of the nanoparticles. The spectrum and data reveal Fe (67.7 wt%, 38.6 at%) and oxygen (29.5 wt%, 58.9 at%) indicates the formation of iron oxides as show in Fig. 5, predominantly Fe2O3 consistent with prior reports on plant-derived iron oxide nanoparticles [43]. Trace elements like potassium (K), calcium (Ca), sulfur (S), phosphorus (P) and silicon (Si) are also detected, which are typically called as micronutrients in the plant [54]. These trace elements can influence the morphology, stability and biocompatibility of the nanoparticles as previously observed in FeONPs synthesized using Azardirachta Indica [49] studied. The high net intensity values of Fe and O peaks reaffirm the dominance of iron oxide formation and its successful reduction via plant phytochemicals. Ruellia tuberosa [52] and Cicer arietinum [55] respectively reported similar EDS profiles, indicating that phytoconstituents assist not only in reduction but also in elemental stabilization. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM pictures of ZR-FeONPs show polydisperse nanoparticles with quasi-spherical to irregular morphology indicating heterogeneous nucleation and development aided by phytochemicals in the plant extract. The annotated particle sizes range from 16 nm to 71 nm, with the majority grouping around the average size of ~38-40 nm as shown in Fig. 6. The size distribution of iron oxide nanoparticles generated through plant-mediated pathways is directly dependent on secondary metabolites. Some regions in TEM suggests crystalline cores, Fe2O3 enveloped by initial organic shells from plant constituents commonly observed in biosynthesized nanoparticles [56]. Similar morphological features have been reported for FeONPs synthesized using Platanus orientalis [41], Teucrium polium [57] and Artemisia vulgaris [58]. The absence of uniform size favors the green synthesis process in contrast to the monodisperse structures generally generated using chemical or hydrothermal process. The observed particle agglomeration may be due to hydrogen bonding or van der Waals interactions among surface-bound biomolecules akin to findings in studies involving Rosemary extract [59]. In contrast, FEG-SEM analysis shows aggregated, irregular and flake-like clustered structures with rough surface features. This difference arises from surface imaging at comparatively lower resolution and the tendency of biosynthesized nanoparticles to undergo agglomeration during drying. The presence of plant-derived capping agents further promotes interparticle interactions leading to cluster formation. Therefore, the variation is complementary rather than contradictory TEM reflects the true primary nanoparticle morphology whereas FEG-SEM represents the aggregated surface architecture in the solid state.Fig. 6TEM analysis of ZR-FeONPs as in a), b), c) and d)

Zeta potential (ZP)

The zeta potential analysis of ZR-FeONPs reveals a mean zeta potential of -24.0 mV shown in Fig. 7, indicating moderate colloidal stability due to electrostatic repulsion between the charged particles. This value lying close to the ±30 mV threshold, suggests that the nanoparticles possess a sufficient surface charge to remain well-dispersed in suspension reducing the risk of agglomeration. The surface potential arises from the presence of bioorganic molecules such as amine, hydroxyl and carboxyl groups originating from the plant extract used during synthesis, which serve as both reducing and capping agents. Additionally, the electrophoretic mobility value of -0.000162 cm²/Vs may be influenced by pH, ionic strength or measurement conditions though the net zeta potential value still reflects surface capping by plant derived functional groups. These findings are consistent with previous reports on plant-mediated synthesis of iron oxide nanoparticles using species such as Citrus maxima [60], Ceratonia siliqua [61] and Clitoria ternatea [62] where zeta potential values ranged from -40 to +40 mV depending on the phytochemical profile and synthesis conditions. A zeta potential in this range implies that ZR-FeONPs are moderately stable, suitable for applications in drug delivery, water treatment or antimicrobial formulations where stability in suspension is critical.Fig. 7Zeta Potential analysis of ZR-FeONPs

Vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM)

The room-temperature VSM profile of the ZR-FeONPs exhibited a reproducible S-shaped M-H curve with negligible coercivity and almost zero remanence confirming their superparamagnetic nature. The nanoparticles showed a saturation magnetization (Ms) of 47 emu/g, which is sufficiently high for magnetic guidance and separation yet low enough to avoid residual magnetization after field removal as shown in Fig. 8. Similarly, Gundelia tournefortii [63] leaf extract shows ~48.6 emu/g with near zero coercivity indicating superparamagnetic, 53.32 emu/g shows from Juglansregia phytogenic [64] iron oxide nanoparticles with low coercive forces and minimal hysteresis. In another study Saccharum arundinaceum [65] extract mediated iron oxide nanoparticles displayed 80.58 emu/g further confirming the dominance of superparamagnetic in plant capped FeONPs. The observed soft magnetic behavior of ZR-FeONPs is consistent with other photosynthesized iron oxide nanoparticle systems, where nanoscale dimensions and the presence of organic capping agents disrupt long-range magnetic ordering. This results in a reversible field-dependent magnetization profile making such nanoparticles particularly suitable for diverse biomedical and environmental applications.Fig. 8VSM analysis of ZR-FeONPs

Bio-medicinal applications of ZR-FeONPs

Antibacterial activity

The ZR-FeONPs demonstrated pronounced antibacterial activity against a diverse range of pathogenic bacteria producing inhibition zones between 18 and 22 mm at the tested concentration which were slightly lower than those observed for the standard antibiotic streptomycin (27–30 mm). Among the evaluated strains, Proteus vulgaris ATCC 8427 exhibited the highest susceptibility with a zone of inhibition of 22 mm, followed by Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213 (20 mm), Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 (19 mm) and Bacillus cereus ATCC 14579 (18 mm) as illustrated in Fig. 9 and Table 1. The broad-spectrum antibacterial efficacy of ZR-FeONPs can be primarily attributed to their nanoscale dimensions and high surface-to-volume ratio which facilitate enhanced interaction with microbial cells. Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant potential of ZR-FeONPs, evaluated using the DPPH free radical scavenging assay, demonstrated a concentration-dependent response, achieving 51.81% inhibition at 100 µg/mL with an IC50 value of 58.19 µg/mL, while the standard ascorbic acid exhibited higher activity 78.23% inhibition at 100 µg/mL and IC50 = 43.53 µg/mL as shown in Figs. 11 and 12. Despite comparatively lower activity, the nanoparticles showed significant free radical scavenging capability, primarily governed by their surface chemistry and redox behavior. The mechanism involves phytochemical residues from Zanthoxylum rhetsa (Roxb.) DC such as phenolics and flavonoids adsorbed on the nanoparticle surface which act as effective electron or hydrogen donors to stabilize DPPH radicals by converting them into non-reactive forms. Simultaneously, the iron oxide core contributes through reversible Fe2+/Fe3+ redox cycling facilitating electron transfer processes that further neutralize reactive species. The combined effect of surface-bound bioactive molecules and the intrinsic redox properties of the nanoparticles enhance their ability to quench free radicals thereby imparting measurable antioxidant activity. Similar outcomes were reported in previous studies where iron oxide nanoparticles made from Vitis vinifera [74], Myrtus communis [75] and Phoenix dactylifera leaves [76] also showed antioxidant properties. Plumeria obtuse [70] based FeONPs gained antioxidant strength from both surface phytochemicals and the redox activity of iron ions which can donate electrons to stabilize free radicals. Cytotoxicity activity

The MTT assay on L929 fibroblast cells demonstrated that ZR-FeONPs possess excellent biocompatibility with minimal cytotoxic effects, as indicated by consistently high cell viability ranging from 84.88% to 99.09% and low inhibition values between 0.91% and 15.12% across the tested concentration range of 20-100 µg/mL as shown in Fig. 21, whereas the standard exhibited pronounced toxicity reducing viability to 16.85% at 100 µg/mL with an IC50 of 59.68 µg/mL. Notably, ZR-FeONPs did not achieve 50% inhibition at any tested dose and therefore their IC50 is considered not effective (NE) as illustrated in Fig. 22 confirming their non-toxic nature toward normal cells. This high biocompatibility is mechanistically associated with controlled and limited generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) which prevents excessive oxidative stress in healthy cells. In addition, phytochemical capping agents derived from Zanthoxylum rhetsa (Roxb.) DC form a stabilizing biomolecular layer on the nanoparticle surface that reduces nonspecific cellular interactions and helps neutralize excess reactive species such as hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and superoxide anions (O2•⁻). As a result, while ZR-FeONPs can still induce ROS-mediated damage in pathogenic or cancerous cells leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis this moderated oxidative response in normal cells preserves cellular integrity thereby enabling selective activity with a favourable safety profile. Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrates a green, sustainable and efficient approach for the synthesis of iron oxide nanoparticles using Zanthoxylum rhetsa (Roxb.) DC leaf extract (ZR-FeONPs). The UV-Vis absorption band at 275 nm and the FTIR absorption band in the range of 600-400 cm-1 confirm the successful formation of iron oxide nanoparticles. The average crystallite size was found to be 17.87 nm, while the average particle size i.e. ~38–40 nm indicates a quasi-spherical morphology. FEG-SEM data revealed aggregated clusters, while EDX analysis confirmed high purity with dominant Fe and O composition. The nanoparticles exhibited moderate colloidal stability i.e. -24 mV and distinct superparamagnetic behavior with a saturation magnetization of 47 emu/g. Biological investigations highlighted their multifunctional potential demonstrating strong antibacterial activity (18–22 mm) and notable antifungal effects (13–15 mm). Dose-dependent antioxidant (51.81%, IC50 = 58.19 µg/mL), anti-inflammatory (52.32%, IC50 = 95.62 µg/mL) and antidiabetic activities (56.32%, IC50 = 82.34 µg/mL) were observed. Additionally, 50.23% inhibition was observed at 1000 µg/mL for anti-tuberculosis activity, while anticancer activity showed 44.32% inhibition at 100 µg/mL. The nanoparticles also exhibited excellent biocompatibility ( > 84% cell viability), indicating their safety for biomedical applications. Overall, this work establishes Z. rhetsa as a promising and underexplored bioresource for magnetic nanomaterial synthesis. Future studies should focus on in vivo validation, mechanistic insights, toxicity profiling and targeted drug delivery applications to facilitate translation into clinical and industrial use.
