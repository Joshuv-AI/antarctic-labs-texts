Predictive factors for humeral and ulnar stem loosening after total elbow arthroplasty: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Akash Singh, James Satalich, Miguel Fiandeiro, Harrison Fellheimer, Matthew Ramsey, Surena Namdari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere has been a significant increase in the use of total elbow arthroplasty (TEA) in advanced osteoarthritis, post-traumatic arthritis, and complex distal humerus fractures. The purpose of this study is to determine the incidence of total elbow loosening and association with patient demographics, implant specifications, and cement mantle characteristics.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted of patients who underwent primary TEA between 2013 and 2022 at a single institution. Revisions and conversions from prior open reduction and internal fixation were excluded. Pre-operative distal humeral cortical thickness and bone quality were assessed. Variables included anterior humeral flange contact with bone, graft placement behind the flange, heterotopic ossification, humeral stem thickness and length, and cement mantle length. Cement mantle quality was graded as adequate, marginal, or inadequate. Radiographic loosening of the humeral and ulnar components was assessed across 4 zones and categorized as focal (1 to 2 zones) or diffuse (3 to 4 zones). Increased translucency from initial post-operative radiograph, cement fractures, and component migration were assessed.ResultsA total of 166 TEAs with an average follow-up of 24 months were reviewed in 130 females (78.3%), and 36 males (21.7%). Most common diagnoses were fracture (45.2%), rheumatoid arthritis (26.5%), and osteoarthritis (10.8%). Humeral loosening was identified in 68 elbows (41%) and ulnar loosening occurred in 55 elbows (33%). Cement mantle quality and length was significantly associated with humeral and ulnar loosening. Longer cement mantles were associated with a lower risk of ulnar component loosening. For each millimeter increase in ulnar mantle length, the odds of loosening decreased by 13%. Adequate humeral mantle was identified in only 100 patients (61%) and an adequate ulnar mantle in only 116 (70%). Cases without an adequate mantle had significantly higher rates of diffuse and focal loosening. At final follow-up, increased periprosthetic translucency was observed in 16 elbows (9.6%), cement fracture in 24 (14.5%), and component migration in 20 (12.0%). Diagnoses, implant type, flange contact, graft placement under flange, and heterotopic ossification formation were not different between cases with loosening, component migration, or cement fracture.Discussion/ConclusionDiffuse humeral and ulnar component loosening is common after TEA at 21% and 13%, respectively, and poor cement mantle quality and length are significant predictive factors of aseptic humeral and ulnar loosening.

Total elbow arthroplasty (TEA) is indicated for advanced rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, post-traumatic arthritis, and complex distal humerus fractures. While historically rheumatoid arthritis was the most common, the greater efficacy of medical management has shaped traumatic cases to have an increasing indication.15,17 In a large database study of 1,600 patients, Romero et al15 identified fracture as the most common indication at 48% followed by degenerative joint disease at 39% and rheumatoid arthritis at 12%.

With increased utilization, there is concern for poor survivorship.11 Several key survivorship concerns include aseptic loosening and infection. In a meta-analysis of 23 studies including 1,429 TEAs, aseptic loosening was found in 12.9% of cases but has been reported to be as high as 34% in registry studies.2,17 Overall revision rates have been reported ranging from 6.7% to 14.6%.2,11

Intraoperatively, inadequate cement mantle quality has been associated with an increased rate of aseptic loosening.5,9 Placement of bone between the anterior humeral cortex and flange has been hypothesized to improve implant stability and aid in load transfer by protecting the bone cement interface and resisting torsional forces.1,8

The purpose of this study is to determine the incidence of total elbow loosening and the association with demographic factors, implant specifications, and cement mantle characteristics from a single large academic institution.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

A retrospective cohort study was conducted of all adult patients who underwent primary TEA between 2013 and 2022 at a single large tertiary care referral center. Institutional review board approval was obtained prior to study initiation. Patients were excluded if they had (1) a revision TEA, (2) a conversion from prior elbow fracture open reduction and internal fixation, and (3) absence of immediate post-operative radiographs (XRs). While conversion TEAs from prior failed open reduction and internal fixation are at an elevated risk of aseptic loosening,9 this study aimed to reduce confounding variables and focus on primary TEA in patients without prior elbow operations.

Data collection

Patient demographic and clinical variables were obtained through electronic medical record review. Collected variables included age at procedure, sex, body mass index, smoking status, and Charleston Comorbidity Index. Operative indication, operating room time, length of stay, estimated blood loss, intraoperative complications, and implant types were also collected.

Pre-operative radiographic assessment

Pre-operative anterior-posterior (AP) and lateral elbow XRs were assessed for distal humerus cortical thickness. In order to obtain a standardized diaphyseal cortical thickness of the distal humerus, a vertical line 6 cm from the olecranon tip was used to measure the difference between the outer and inner cortical diameter (Fig. 1). When available, pre-operative computed tomography (CT) scans were reviewed to assess distal humeral bone quality using Hounsfield unit measurements. A circular region of interest was placed just proximal to the transepicondylar axis on the axial, sagittal, and coronal views. The circle was drawn so the diameter would just incorporate the cortex on either side of the circle's diameter, similar to techniques in previous shoulder and upper extremity studies.4,10 For patients with both XR and CT available for review, both were analyzed as possible predictive factors of loosening.Figure 1Demonstration of measuring the distal humeral cortical thickness. Post-operative radiographic assessment

Post-operative radiographic evaluation included serial post-operative X-rays at approximately 2 weeks, 6-8 weeks, and most recent follow-up, or immediately prior to revision surgery. Variables included presence or absence of contact between the anterior humeral flange and distal humerus (Fig. 2), presence of graft material behind the anterior flange, heterotopic ossification (HO) formation, humeral stem thickness and length on AP view, and cement mantle length of the humeral and ulnar components on AP and lateral views. Magnification differences were standardized between XRs based measuring the ratio of the length of the humeral stem and the cement mantle. All radiographic measurements were completed by 2 fellowship trained shoulder and elbow surgeons. Mantle length was measured as the distance from the tip of the stem to the distal extend of the cement mantle. If the mantle did not extend to beyond the tip of the prosthesis, a negative value was given, measuring from the distal extent of the mantle to the tip of the prosthesis. Cement mantle quality was graded based on the Morrey classification (Table I, Fig. 3).13 Although mantle quality can be subject to measurement bias, the Morrey classification incorporates quantitative thresholds to differentiate adequate versus inadequate mantle qualities. Assessment of radiographic loosening

Radiographic loosening of humeral and ulnar components was evaluated by a four-zone system on AP and lateral XRs as described by Goldberg et al.7 At each follow-up time point, radiolucent changes of both the humeral and ulnar components were assessed at either the cement-prosthesis or cement-bone interface for each zone. The total number of zones with evidence of radiolucency were counted and categorized as focal (1-2 total zones) or diffuse (3-4 total zones), which was defined as > 1 mm on any radiographic view, similar to Gandhi et al.6 Additional binary (Yes/No) radiographic variables included an increase in periprosthetic translucency, the presence of cement fractures, and the presence of component migration, which was assessed by comparison of serial XR. Any visible migration >2 mm was considered evidence of component migration. Secondary outcomes

Prosthetic joint infection (PJI) was assessed by chart review and defined by International Consensus Meeting (ICM) 2018 criteria

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographic and surgical variables. Continuous variables were compared using Student t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests as appropriate, and categorical variables were compared using chi-square or Fisher exact tests. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of humeral and ulnar component loosening. All statistical analyses were done using R studio (version 4.5.1, Vienna, Austria). Variables with clinical relevance or P < .10 on univariate analysis were included in regression models. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05. Pairwise P value comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction.

Radiographic characteristics

Adequate humeral cement mantle was identified in 100 patients (61%) and an adequate ulnar cement mantle in 116 (70%). At final follow-up, a radiographic increase in periprosthetic translucency was observed in 16 elbows (9.6%), cement fracture in 24 (14.5%), and component migration in 20 (12.0%). Humeral loosening was identified in 41% of cases including 20% focal and 21% diffuse patterns. Ulnar loosening occurred in 33% of cases, including 20% focal and 13% diffuse patterns. Combined humeral and ulnar loosening was present in 36 (25%) elbows.

Humeral component loosening

Patient diagnoses, implant type, humeral stem thickness or length, graft placement under the anterior flange, and HO formation were not statistically significant between cases with loosening, component migration, or cement fracture (Table IV). Cement mantle length was significantly shorter on AP (11.5 mm vs. 27.9 mm, P = .012) and lateral views (13.3 mm vs. 29.0 mm, P = .030) in cases with humeral loosening compared to cases without humeral loosening (Table V). The proportion of inadequate or marginal humeral cement mantle quality was significantly higher in diffuse humeral loosening (P = .04). An inadequate cement mantle was seen in 43% of cases that eventually developed diffuse humeral loosening compared to only 4% of cases that did not develop subsequent loosening. Even in cases without any humeral loosening, only 74% of these were determined to have an adequate cement mantle (Fig. 5, Table VI). Among cases with an increase in humeral translucency, 50% had marginal and 43% had inadequate mantle quality compared to 40% marginal and 11% inadequate in cases without an increase in translucency (Supplementary Appendix B). Multivariable analysis did not demonstrate that humeral mantle quality, length, or flange contact with bone were significant predictive factors of humeral loosening (Supplementary Appendix A). Ulnar component loosening

Focal and diffuse ulnar loosening were significantly higher in patients with inadequate or marginal mantle quality (Fig. 6, Table VII). Cement mantle length was significantly shorter on AP (7.15 mm vs. 19.2 mm, P = .0002) and lateral views (13.3 mm vs. 29 mm, P = .001) in cases with diffuse loosening compared to cases without ulnar loosening. Logistic regression demonstrated increasing cement mantle length associated with reduced odds of ulnar loosening (OR 0.87 [0.80-0.93], P < .001). (Supplementary Appendix A). Cement mantle quality was observed as inadequate in 48% of diffusely loose cases versus 8% of no loosening. Reoperation

Of this cohort, there was a reoperation rate of 21.7% (n = 36/166). Of these reoperations, 64% underwent a single reoperation, 13% underwent 2 reoperations, and 11% underwent 3 reoperations. (median 1; range 1-12 revisions). The average time to initial reoperation was 2.1 years (range 17 days-12.9 years). The most common indications for reoperation were periprosthetic fracture (39%), infection (28%), and aseptic loosening (17%) (Supplementary Appendix D). Twelve cases (7.2%) had evidence of PJI and were not included in the final loosening analysis.

Discussion

The principal finding of this study is that cement mantle characteristics—both qualitative and quantitative—are strongly associated with radiographic loosening after TEA. Shorter cement mantle length and marginal or inadequate mantle quality were consistently associated with humeral and ulnar component loosening, while greater mantle length demonstrated a protective effect in multivariable analysis.

Inadequate cement mantle quality and length have been demonstrated in the literature to be associated with aseptic TEA loosening, which was similar to the results in this study.5,9 Adequate humeral quality was seen in 74% of cases without loosening which is comparable to Hill et al9 who observed 86% adequate mantle quality in cases who did not undergo reoperation compared to 14% adequate in cases who underwent revision TEA.

The importance of cement technique in TEA has been recognized but incompletely quantified. Prior investigations have largely emphasized mantle quality as a categorical variable.5 The present study expands on this work by incorporating objective measurements of humeral and ulnar cement mantle length and quality and their independent association with loosening. While intraoperative cementation can be technically difficult, proper surgical technique as described by Faber et al3 including canal irrigation, drying, canal plugging, and low viscosity cement pressurization should be performed. Smith et al16 also demonstrated that a cement gun with a narrow nozzle produces significantly greater cement penetration ratios and a higher cement index compared with a catheter-tip syringe, even without canal occlusion. While intraoperative cementation can be difficult, these results identify a clear intraoperative target for improving cementation and adequate implant positioning to improve TEA survivorship. Surgeons should also be mindful of the consequences of placing too much cement as this can make revision TEA more difficult, particularly in cases of PJI.

This cohort's diffuse humeral loosening rate of 21% is higher than other cohorts. Davey et al2 reported 12.9% in a systematic review of 1,429 elbows. While this cohort's rate is higher than previous cohorts, it should be noted that the radiographic parameter to consider a loosening zone was >1 mm of translucency between the bone-cement or cement-prosthesis interface on any radiographic view.

The extracortical anterior humeral flange has been hypothesized to improve implant stability and aid in load transfer. In this study, the odds of developing humeral loosening was not significantly different in cases with contact between the flange and either a graft or the humeral cortex (OR 0.96, 95% confidence interval 0.63-3.76, 0.167). Although this study was not powered to detect a difference between flange contact with a bone graft or the humeral cortex, the lack of significance warrants further biomechanical analysis. Herren et al8 performed a finite element analysis that supported the use of an anterior flange in revision TEA with insufficient condylar bone stock.

This cohort's 7.2% infection rate is similar to other cases in the literature. In the Swedish elbow arthroplasty register, Wänström et al18 used International Classification of Diseases and NOMESCO Classification of Surgical Procedures codes to identify a suspected PJI rate of 8%, which was further confirmed to be 3.8% after 2 positive cultures with the same microorganism were found. Wolfe et al19 observed an infection rate of 7.3% in 164 primary TEAs. Periprosthetic fracture was surprisingly the most common indication for reoperation in our cohort, which is different than Hill et al9 who demonstrated aseptic loosening to be the most common indication.

Although HO was only responsible for 1 reoperation in this cohort, it was still observed at final follow-up in 39% of patients. Liu et al12 cited HO presence in 10% of patients and symptomatic HO in 3%. However the incidence varies in the literature. In a cohort of 55 total elbow arthroplasties, Robinson et al14 observed an overall incidence of 84%, with the highest incidence in the trauma subgroup at 96%. Although in our cohort HO formation at final follow-up was not associated with humeral or ulnar loosening, it was present in 11 cases (79%) of cases with an increase in translucency compared to its presence in 57 (45%) of cases without an increase in translucency (P = .03). However, there is still no literature to support the theory that development of HO is a predictive factor of elbow loosening or failure. Clinical correlation and further research should be performed to assess if HO formation can be associated with loosening.

This study is limited in part by its retrospective design and the inability to report clinical outcome measures such as range of motion, satisfaction, and functional scores. There were several demographic variables which have been cited to be predictive of total elbow loosening including age, smoking, and indication.9 Although these did not achieve statistical significance in this study, they are variables which should be studied in larger cohorts. Other limitations include this study's reliance on radiographic endpoints, which do not always correlate with symptoms or eventual reoperation. Radiographic measurements may be subject to observer variability, although 2 reviewers did review all available XRs. Another limitation is that although humeral stem length was quantified, ulnar stem length as well as linkage was not assessed. Hill et al9 demonstrated that shorter ulnar stem length and component linkage were predictive of ulnar loosening. Pre-operative CT-based bone quality assessment was not consistently available. Furthermore, the effect of linked and unlinked components was not assessed. Additionally, although this cohort's radiographic follow-up averaged 26 ± 29.6 months, the range included patients from 14 days to 10.8 years, due to 3 cases being lost to follow-up after total elbow arthroplasties for acute distal humerus fractures. Finally, as a single-center study, these findings are not generalizable to all practices.

Conclusion

Diffuse humeral and ulnar component loosening is common after TEA at 21% and 13%, respectively. Greater mantle length appears protective, while inadequate mantle quality is associated with increased humeral and ulnar loosening. These findings highlight cementation technique as a key modifiable factor in TEA fixation and survivorship.
