Immigrants’ Gendered Remittance‐Sending Patterns and Purposes: The Role of Marital Status
Authors: Heather Dicks, Lisa Kaida, Liam Swiss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTResearch on immigrant remittances and gender is dominated by qualitative studies focusing on specific groups of immigrants; meanwhile, quantitative analysis is scarce due to data limitations. Drawing on the Study on International Money Transfers, a rare survey dataset on remittance behaviours, we compare how immigrant women and men in Canada send money abroad, with a focus on how gender intersects with marital status. We find that married immigrants make some joint decisions regarding remittances. At the same time, there are notable differences in the purpose of remitting across gender and marital status. Married immigrant women appear to use their remittances to express kinship love, more so than married men. Meanwhile, unmarried men remit larger sums than unmarried women, possibly due to cultural pressures. Furthermore, unmarried immigrant women invest more frequently in healthcare expenses, pointing to their potential role as transnational caretakers for the health of families left behind.

Introduction

International migration has reshaped much of our global landscape. While this does not represent a new phenomenon, our increasingly globalized world has expanded the opportunities for continued transnational activity following migration. Indeed, transnational family configurations are now a common occurrence, with families stretching across national borders yet maintaining kinship ties through regular touchpoints and exchanges. A key source of transnational connection often comes in the form of remittances—financial transfers sent from immigrants living abroad to their friends and family. These resources currently represent a veritable global economic force; every year, immigrants send hundreds of billions of dollars to friends and family living in other countries (World Bank 2023). Despite the magnitude of this international resource flow, systematic analysis of who is sending money and why is limited in Canada. In addition, little research explores remittances while also considering the intersecting features of both gender and marital status (Donato et al. 2014).

The extant research on gendered remittances produces varied and sometimes conflicting findings. Some research finds immigrant men to be the main remitters, depicting them as transnational breadwinners (Carrasco and Obućina 2023; Dodson et al. 2008; King et al. 2006; Semyonov and Gorodzeisky 2005; Soltero 2009). Other studies suggest immigrant women are gaining greater economic independence and agency through migration (Åkesson 2009; McCallum 2022; Tagutanazvo and Dzingirai 2022) and point to large‐scale remittance‐sending behaviours among women (Blue 2004). Studies also note gender differences in the reasons for remitting, with men often remitting as an economic investment and women remitting to maintain kinship ties (Van den Bosch and Mavrotas 2022).

Marital status, moreover, is shown to influence economic outcomes of immigrants and corresponding remittance decisions (Donato et al. 2014; Geist and McManus 2012; Holst et al. 2012; Lersch 2016). Accordingly, migration‐related theories, such as the New Economics of Labour Migration (NELM), highlight the importance of understanding migration as a household decision, rather than merely an individual, economically motivated choice (de Haas et al. 2020; Massey et al. 1993; Stark and Levhari 1982).

In this study, we adopt a quantitative approach and analyse data from a nationwide survey on the remittance activities of immigrants in Canada from low‐ and middle‐income countries. We conduct regression analysis on the gender dynamics of remittance‐sending, considering the intersection of both gender and marital status. We address the following three questions: Are immigrant women more likely to remit than their male counterparts?Among remitters, is there a gender difference in the amount remitted and the purposes of remitting?How do the remittance behaviours (propensity of remittance, remittance amount, and remittance purposes) of married immigrant women and men compare with those of unmarried immigrant women and men?

Literature Review

Immigrant Remittances as Transnational Activities

Emerging from early micro‐economic studies in the 1970s and 1980s to gain multidisciplinary and multisectoral interest in both research and practitioner communities, remittances have profoundly captured global scholarly and policy interests (Rapoport and Docquier 2005). Since first emerging as a scholarly field, researchers have expanded the concept of remittances to include other cross‐border exchanges along migration corridors, including non‐financial or social remittances (Entzinger and Scholten 2022; Levitt 1998) or even reverse remittances (Mazzucato 2011). Nevertheless, in reflecting on remittances as a transnational force, it is the sustained financial magnitude of immigrant‐to‐friend/family money transfers that remains a key point of interest. In this regard, remittances can be broadly understood as the money immigrants in a host country send to friends and family living abroad, usually those in their origin country. Over the last several decades, the flow of remittances has grown to be a major economic force; in 2025, global remittances were estimated to have exceeded USD $850 billion (World Bank 2026). By comparison, in 2000, they were USD $122 billion (World Bank 2023).

In recent decades, research on remittances has thrived within migration and development literature, yet gaps remain. One such gap is the limited attention that researchers have invested in understanding the gendered flow of remittances; the majority of remittance literature fails to incorporate a gender lens into this global phenomenon (Kunz 2008; Singh 2019). As described below, the research that does engage in this space is predominantly ethnographic fieldwork, focusing on homogenous groups of immigrants. Although these studies provide rich descriptions of specific cultural nodes in the broader global web of migration, generalizability of their findings to a diverse immigrant population in a host country like Canada is questionable. Furthermore, when we look at these various studies, we see conflicting findings, as reviewed below.

Gender Dynamics in Remittance‐Sending and Remittance Amounts

Much of the literature that considers remittances and gender points to a lower propensity of remittance‐sending among immigrant women compared to immigrant men. This fits within a broader understanding of traditional (heteronormative) gender norms, in which the man is the breadwinner—albeit a transnational breadwinner—while the woman is expected to maintain the household in his absence (Carrasco and Obućina 2023; Dodson et al. 2008; Soltero 2009).

Several researchers point to gender labour market inequality as a contributing factor to immigrant women's lower levels of remittance‐sending; here, women's relatively poorer economic performance in the host country dampens their ability to remit as much as their male counterparts (Carrasco and Obućina 2023; Dodson et al. 2008). Other researchers, however, highlight that gendered labour market participation cannot fully explain the observed difference in remittance‐sending behaviours, suggesting underlying gender‐driven variation (Holst et al. 2012; Semyonov and Gorodzeisky 2005).

Moreover, some research suggests prevalent remittance‐sending activities among immigrant women, especially in the context of the growing proportion of female economic immigrants (UNDESA 2020). Here, scholars have pointed to pressures associated with the perceived selflessness of mothers, which culminates in stronger remittance behaviours of women working abroad (Blue 2004; McCallum 2022).

Although not frequently adopted in quantitative research on immigrant integration, West and Zimmerman's (1987) interactionist concept of doing gender is helpful here. This approach argues that gender is best understood not as an “achieved status,” but instead as the result of recurring everyday interactions or accomplishments (p. 126). Through this lens, remittance‐sending can be considered not only as a reflection of pre‐existing gender differences but as a social practice through which gender identities are actively produced and affirmed. As such, immigrant men and women engage in remittance‐sending in ways that aim to accomplish culturally situated expectations of masculinity and femininity. For men, regular remittances may serve as a means of performing and validating ideals of transnational breadwinning, while for women, remittances may function as a way of enacting expectations of caregiving, altruism, and family responsibility. Consequently, observed gender differences in remittance behaviour may reflect not only gendered disparities in economic resources but also ongoing efforts to “do gender” across transnational family relationships.

Gender, however, does not operate in isolation. Immigrant women's and men's marital status shapes their economic experiences in ways that interact closely with gender (Donato et al. 2014). Indeed, both gender and marital status together appear to play a larger role in labour force participation rates than a person's nativity (whether they were born within the country or immigrated there) (Donato et al. 2014). Doing gender in the context of a marriage shapes the division of labour in the household and in the labour market (West and Zimmerman 1987). Research shows that women are more likely than men to defer their career path for their (male) partner's migration decision, and women who migrate for family reasons experience a significant decline in their earnings after migration (Geist and McManus 2012; Lersch 2016).

Considering the decision, then, to remit and its association with marital status, existing literature suggests that, in general, married immigrants (men and women) tend to remit more money than unmarried immigrants (Holst et al. 2012). These differences clearly highlight the importance of not only considering gender, but also marital status when delineating varied remittance‐sending behaviours.

Do Women and Men Remit for Different Reasons?

Although it is valuable to gain insight into the propensity and amount of remittances immigrants are sending, it is also critical to examine the gendering of remittance purposes. This allows us to elucidate the dynamic transnational roles they possibly play for their families left behind. Studies that explore the reasons for which immigrants choose to send remittances largely highlight the fact that these financial flows are not merely monetary but also indicate transnational care. Looking at immigrants from Ghana (Coe 2011; Wong 2006), Senegal (Hannaford 2016), and the Philippines (Katigbak 2015; McKay 2007), scholars underline the fact that enmeshed within remittance‐sending is affection and love; these resources serve as a form of transnational glue holding kinship ties together.

Research that explores the reasons for remittance‐sending through a gendered lens is limited; however, those studies that do engage with this topic note that men tend to remit for economic reasons, while women are more likely to remit to maintain family ties and, in so doing, engage in a form of transnational mothering (Bosch and Mavrotas 2022; McCallum 2022; Parreñas 2005). These studies suggest there are expectations on women to be generous and demonstrate love and affection through the sending of remittances. This contrasts with narratives around immigrant men, in which their remittances are depicted as fulfilling masculine norms associated with breadwinning.

A doing gender perspective further suggests that the meanings attached to remittances are shaped by social interactions and normative expectations rather than solely by individual preferences (West and Zimmerman 1987). Remittances may therefore represent an important mechanism through which immigrants demonstrate their commitment to gendered family roles or expectations despite geographic separation. Women who remit to support children, parents, or extended kin may be engaging in forms of transnational care that reaffirm conventional expectations of femininity, while men's remittances may be interpreted by both senders and recipients as evidence of their fulfilment of expected provider responsibilities. Examining remittance motives through this lens highlights how financial transfers can simultaneously sustain family or community livelihoods and reproduce gendered social relations transnationally.

Furthermore, research has revealed that married individuals are less likely to make financial decisions independently than unmarried individuals (Treas 1993; Yodanis and Lauer 2007a; Yodanis and Lauer 2007b; Treas and Tsui‐o‐Tai 2012). Research reveals that this joint decision‐making increases with international migration (Eroğlu 2020). Remittances represent a significant financial decision within an immigrant's household. Thus, we can assume that the joint decision‐making tendencies that married couples exhibit in other financial affairs would hold in the case of remittance‐related decisions. This would include the decision to remit, how much is remitted and the reason for remitting.

Moreover, a doing gender framework suggests that the enactment of gender may vary according to marital status, as married and unmarried individuals are accountable to different sets of social expectations and relational obligations. Investigating remittance purposes at the intersection of gender and marital status may therefore reveal important differences in how immigrants negotiate and perform gender within transnational family contexts.

To date, to the best of our knowledge, no published studies have specifically looked at the purpose of remitting disaggregated according to both gender and marital status. Exploring the intersection of these two variables will thus make a valuable contribution to the growing body of remittance literature.

Data and Methods

Dataset

We analyse data from the 2017 Study on International Money Transfers (SIMT), the first nationally‐representative survey in Canada to capture details of the remittance‐sending behaviours of the immigrant population (Dimbuene and Turcotte 2019; MacIsaac 2024). The SIMT draws on a sample of immigrants (permanent and temporary residents) aged 18 years and older in Canada who were born in countries that are eligible to receive official development assistance (most of the world's low‐ and middle‐income countries or so‐called “developing” countries). This includes most countries within Africa, the Middle East, Asia, and Latin America1. The SIMT captures a cross‐sectional snapshot of immigrant remittance‐sending activities for the year 2017 (Statistics Canada 2019).

In gathering this data, Statistics Canada used a stratified sampling methodology, which first considered three strata: temporary residents identified through Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC), immigrants identified through IRCC records, and immigrants identified through the Canadian census. These three strata were then further disaggregated by region of birth (Africa, South Asia, East and Southeast Asia, the Americas, and other countries), after which point a stratified sample of 40,000 was selected. Statistics Canada received roughly 23,000 responses in total, with a response rate of 58 percent. In compiling the data pulled from this survey, Statistics Canada estimated probabilistic weights, which they provided within the dataset for integration into analysis such that estimates could more accurately reflect broader nation‐wide patterns (MacIsaac 2024; Statistics Canada 2019).

As Gibson and McKenzie (2017) point out, there may be serious limitations associated with just using aggregated financial data to study remittances. The use of survey data, however, proves to be a reliable means of investigating this international flow. The SIMT includes a diverse range of questions pertaining to the characteristics of immigrant remitters in Canada, alongside the frequency of their remittances, the amounts remitted, the channels used for sending remittances and the reasons for remitting. The overarching objective of the survey is to understand the global money transfers made by the immigrant population in Canada that go to immigrants’ family and friends living abroad. The SIMT data are intended to be used by the government in determining appropriate policies associated with lowering the cost of sending remittances to help provide more reliable and secure international money transfer options to immigrants.

Measures

As measures of immigrant remittance behaviours, we consider three sets of dependent variables: (1) remitting to any country in 2017 (yes/no); (2) total amount remitted in 2017 (continuous, in Canadian dollars); and (3) the intended purposes behind remittance‐sending. The remittance purposes listed in the SIMT include: living expenses, medical expenses, education, major expenses, major emergencies, insurance, gift, entertainment and/or other. We code each purpose into a dichotomous variable (yes/no).

The independent variable is gender. Regarding the question of gender, respondents in the SIMT were only given the option to select “male” or “female” in response to the question “Are you male or female?” As such, we limit our understanding of gender to a simplified frame. This conflation of sex and gender in the context of migration and remittances dominates the literature, and the dynamics associated with gendered relations are predominantly understood within a heteronormative traditional Western understanding of marriage and family. Given limitations associated with our dataset, we too adopt this narrow frame but acknowledge that more should be done in the future to broaden and deepen our understanding of gender and migration. Thus, throughout this paper, we discuss women/females versus men/males; however, we understand this is an oversimplification associated with sex and gender, which we choose to accept on account of the limitations associated with the dataset.

Another key indicator in this study is marital status. Based on the marital status variable in the SIMT data, we classify those who are legally married and in common‐law relationships as married individuals, whereas the rest (widowed, separated, or divorced, and single or never married) are aggregated as unmarried individuals.

Analytical Techniques

We conduct regression analysis to estimate the three sets of dependent variables discussed earlier: the probability of remitting in 2017 (DV1); the amount of remittance in 2017 (DV2); and the purpose of remitting (DV3). For the dichotomous dependent variables (DV1 and DV3), we utilize linear probability models2. For the continuous measure of the total remittance amount, we turn to Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression. For each of these models, we divide the sample according to those individuals that are either married or in common‐law relationships (considered “married” for the purposes of this study) and those who are separated, divorced, widowed or single (never married) (considered “unmarried” for the purposes of this study).

First, we include the independent variable (gender) only in the regression models to see if statistically significant gender differences in outcomes can be observed in the samples. Next, for those bivariate relationships with significant gender differences in outcomes, we further add the individual‐level and origin country‐level control variables outlined in Table 2 to our models3. We use Statistics Canada survey weights within our analyses to ensure our findings reflect population‐level trends. Considering the clustering of data at immigrants’ origin country‐level, we also estimate cluster‐robust standard errors. All the analyses shown in this paper were conducted in Statistics Canada's secure Research Data Centres using Stata 18.

Results

Decision to Remit and Amount Remitted

Turning first to the decision to remit, our bivariate results (linear probability models controlling only for gender) show no statistically significant gender difference in the probability of remitting for both married and unmarried immigrants (at p < 0.05 level). In other words, there is no significant selection into remitting based on the gender of immigrants from low‐ and middle‐income countries in Canada. In the subsequent regression models estimating remitters’ amount and purposes of sending money, we do not use sample selection methods (e.g., Heckman probit).

The results in Table 3 (OLS regression models controlling for gender only) also reveal that among married immigrants who sent remittances in 2017, there is no statistically significant difference in the remittance amount between women and men (at p < 0.05 level). Meanwhile, we find statistically significant gender differences in remittance amounts among unmarried immigrants. Unmarried women remit less than their male counterparts (by CAD $1130).

Moreover, results on some of the control variables are worth noting. In particular, we note that a larger diaspora leads to greater remittance‐sending within that cultural group. Furthermore, older immigrants, those with higher personal income, and those with more education are among the highest remitters. Finally, considering immigration status, we note that landed immigrants or permanent residents remit significantly more per year than naturalized Canadian citizens.

We further investigate which compositional differences in the individual‐level and origin country characteristics between women and men that are controlled in the multivariate regression models contribute most to the observed difference in the remittance amounts sent by unmarried male versus female immigrants4. We find that personal income contributes the most to the explained portion of the gender difference, followed by immigrant status and education.

To contextualize these results to our hypotheses (H1–H4) stated earlier, H1 and H2 (pertaining to the decision to remit) are rejected, given that no significant gender differences in the probabilities of remitting are found for married and unmarried immigrants. H3 (on remittance amount) is partially supported only for unmarried individuals. Finally, H4 (varied gender differences in remittance amount by marital status) is supported as the gender gap in remittance amounts is indeed larger for unmarried immigrants than married immigrants.

Married Men Remit More for Insurance Than Married Women

For married immigrant remitters, the statistically significant gender difference in the probability of remitting for insurance disappears (at p < 0.05 level) when their individual‐ and origin county‐level characteristics are adjusted. In other words, the observed gender gap in the probability of remitting for insurance is explained by the compositional differences between immigrant men and women in their demographic, socioeconomic, and contextual characteristics.

Turning to the control variables, we see that as the diaspora grows, so too does the probability of remitting for the purpose of paying for insurance. Moreover, those with more education, those who remit larger sums of money each year, and landed immigrants or temporary residents (rather than naturalized Canadian citizens) are most likely to remit for the purpose of insurance. The Oaxaca‐Blinder decomposition (results not shown here but are available upon request) suggests 32 percent of the observed gender difference in the probability of remitting for insurance purposes can be explained by the compositional differences in the control variables between married women and men. Among the control variables, personal income and education (i.e., married men's higher personal income and education than women) make the largest contributions to the explained portion of the gender difference in remittance for insurance purposes.

Married Women Remit More for Gift‐Giving Than Married Men

When we add in our control variables, we find that married women's higher probability of remitting as a gift compared to married men maintains its statistical significance (p < 0.001). Married female remitters have a 3.8 percentage point higher probability of remitting as a gift than their male counterparts.

Results on some of the control variables are worth noting. First, we find that the size of diaspora plays a role in remitting for this purpose. The larger the diaspora, the more likely married immigrants from that cultural group will remit for the purpose of gift giving. Interestingly, we also find that the higher the gender equality index in the immigrant's country of birth, the more likely they will remit as a gift.

Some other statistical differences we note are that those with more education and those in higher personal income brackets are more likely to remit as a gift, although this likelihood peaks at the CAD $50,000–$59,999 bracket and then declines slightly above this income level. Unlike insurance, we find that the more money individuals remit each year, the less likely this is directed toward gifts, although this relationship is quite small.

Our Oaxaca‐Blinder decomposition results tell us that approximately 20 percent of the observed gender difference in the probability of remitting as a gift can be attributed to our gender differences in the distribution of the control variables. Here we find that the gender equality index in the immigrant's origin country contributes most to the difference explained through the control variables (i.e., female immigrants are more frequently coming from countries with a higher gender equality index than male immigrants, which is positively associated with remitting as a form of gift‐giving).

Unmarried Women Remit More for Medical Expenses Than Unmarried Men

Looking then to unmarried individuals and women's higher likelihood of remitting for medical expenses over men's, we again find that our statistically significant relationship holds (p < 0.01) after we introduce our control variables. Unmarried female remitters have a 4.8 percentage point higher probability of remitting to cover medical expenses than unmarried male remitters. Likewise, we find several additional associations as a result of the introduction of the control variables.

First, we find that the size of the diaspora has a significant impact on the decision to remit for medical expenses, albeit this time it is a negative relationship; the larger the diaspora group, the less likely unmarried remitters will send money for the purpose of covering medical expenses. Next, we see that as the total annual amount remitted increases, so does the likelihood that at least some of this money will go toward medical expenses. However, this association is very small. Considering some demographic features, unmarried individuals with children and who are aged 40–59 years have a higher probability of remitting to cover medical expenses than those without children and those in other age brackets. Interestingly, individuals in the highest income brackets are least likely to remit for medical expenses compared to those within lower income brackets. Lastly, we note that temporary or non‐permanent residents have a lower probability of remitting for the purpose of covering medical expenses than Canadian citizens.

Our Oaxaca‐Blinder decomposition results suggest that around 48 percent of the gender difference in remitting for the purpose of covering medical expenses can be explained by the compositional differences in control variables between women and men. Here, we find that age, personal income bracket, and number of children contribute the most to the observed difference (i.e., unmarried female immigrants are older, have lower personal income and are more likely to have children than male immigrants; all of which contribute to a higher probability of remitting for the purpose of covering medical expenses).

Unmarried Men Remit More for Entertainment Than Unmarried Women

The higher probability that unmarried immigrant men will remit for entertainment compared to unmarried immigrant women loses its statistical significance (at p < 0.05 level) when we control for individual‐ and country of origin‐level characteristics. However, we do find other interesting relationships here. Notably, as life expectancy and the gender equality index in the immigrant's origin country increase, so too does their probability of remitting for entertainment purposes. In terms of demographic differences, as remitters age, they are less likely to remit for entertainment. Further, those remitters that are employed part‐time have a higher probability of remitting for entertainment than those employed full‐time.

Turning to our Oaxaca‐Blinder decomposition, we find that 56 percent of the observed gender difference is explained by the individual and country of origin‐level characteristics associated with unmarried male and female remitters. The largest contributing factor is age (i.e., female immigrants are older, and older immigrants in general are less likely to remit for entertainment).

Returning to the three hypotheses on remittance purposes stated earlier, the results indicate H6 is partially supported, applied partly to married immigrants. H7 is also partially supported; gender differences in remittance purposes are larger only for select purposes (medical expenses and entertainment) for unmarried individuals compared to married individuals.

Discussion and Conclusion

Drawing on West and Zimmerman's doing gender as a key theoretical framework (West and Zimmerman 1987), this study aimed to examine gendered patterns in immigrant remittances in Canada. We addressed three questions: whether immigrant women are less likely to remit than men; whether gender differences exist in the amount remitted and the purposes of remitting; and how marital status intersects with gender to influence remittance decisions, amounts, and purposes. Using data from the 2017 SIMT, we conducted regression analyses on immigrants from ODA‐eligible countries.

Main findings from the analysis are threefold. First, we find no significant gender difference in the probability of remitting, either among married or unmarried immigrants. This diverges from expectations from the dominant existing literature, which suggests that immigrant women would be less likely to remit than immigrant men given the persistent gender gaps in labour market participation and income in the host country. However, our finding aligns with a strand of research which suggests the growing share of women among economic migrants has narrowed remittance propensity gaps (UNDESA 2020). Moreover, the decision to remit may reflect household‐level obligations shared across gender lines rather than individual‐level gender variations.

Second, for remitters, the link between gender and remittance amounts varies widely by marital status. Among married immigrants, no significant gender difference is found in the amount remitted. This is consistent with expectations from the New Economics of Labour Migration that married households make joint remittance decisions, mitigating individual‐level gender variations. Among unmarried immigrants, however, a noticeable gender gap emerges; unmarried men remit approximately CAD $960 more per year than unmarried women, even after a series of individual‐ and origin country‐level characteristics are controlled. This is consistent with Semyonov and Gorodzeisky (2005) and Holst et al. (2012), who similarly find persistent gender gaps in remittance amounts after considering gender gaps in labour market participation. By remitting larger amounts, unmarried men may be doing gender via interactions that aim to attain breadwinner norms (West and Zimmerman 1987).

Third, purposes of remitting are somewhat gendered but are intricately linked to marital status as well. Married women are more likely than married men to remit for gift‐giving purposes, even when gender differences in individual‐ and origin country‐level characteristics are considered. This supports the view of remittances as a medium of transnational care; married immigrant women may accomplish feminine norms of affection and maintenance of kinship through targeted, gift‐oriented transfers, which represent an expression of transnational mothering (McCallum 2022; Parreñas 2005). Their continued practice of care‐oriented gender norms despite migrating to a host country like Canada with stronger formal gender equality suggests the persistence of doing gender across cultural contexts (Foner 1997).

Among unmarried immigrants, we find women are more likely than men to remit to cover medical expenses. This is a novel finding with no clear explanation in the existing literature. Immigrant women's feminized remittance networks, whereby women preferentially send money to female recipients who are trusted to allocate funds to household and family needs, may facilitate requests for health‐related transfers that would not as readily flow through male recipients or be made to a married woman navigating joint household finances (Parreñas 2005; Pickbourne 2016; Wong 2006). Moreover, gendered disparities in healthcare access in low‐ and middle‐income countries, in which women are systematically disadvantaged, may create specific demand for medical remittances directed at female relatives, including maternal and child health costs. Unmarried women may be uniquely positioned to address such demands (Azad et al. 2020; Moradhvaj and Saikia 2019). Taken together, these findings reflect how doing gender plays out differently depending on household configuration: married women accomplish gender through affective remittances, while unmarried women do so through a transnational caretaking role focused on health.

Beyond our main findings, we also note a few interesting relationships that emerged among our control variables. First, we find that individuals in higher income brackets are least likely to remit for medical expenses. Since no existing research has explicitly examined the association between immigrants’ incomes in the host country and their remittance for medical expenses, interpretation of this finding is difficult. However, some studies indicate a higher propensity to use remittances for medical purposes among lower‐income families in the origin country due to their limited access to health care (Amuedo‐Dorantes and Pozo 2009; Ponce et al. 2011). It is possible that lower‐income immigrants, who are more likely to come from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, may possess a stronger sense of obligation to pay for their families’ medical expenses than higher‐income immigrants.

Another interesting relationship of note is that a higher life expectancy and a higher gender equality index within an immigrant's birth country are both significantly related to a higher probability of remitting for the purpose of entertainment. Stronger gender equality and longer life spans are both hallmarks of economic growth and development within a given country (Girón and Kazemikhasragh 2022; He and Li 2020). It may be that the remittance recipients living in countries with better metrics in terms of life expectancy and gender equality are among those with relatively higher income levels. As such, these recipients may not require remittances for basic necessities, but can instead spend these resources on goods and services associated with leisure, like entertainment expenditures.

Finally, the size of the diaspora in Canada is found to have a positive impact on the amount remitted. The larger the diaspora, the more money unmarried immigrants from that diaspora group will remit. This trend may point to a culture of remitting that emerges among people from a similar geographic location (Liang, Li and Ma 2013). Moreover, it may point to diaspora groups pooling resources and sending collective remittances for projects back in their country of origin (Galstyan and Ambrosini 2023).

Although this study advances the immigrant remittance literature through a rigorous analysis on untapped remittance‐focused survey data, several limitations warrant note. For one, the SIMT provides a cross‐sectional snapshot of 2017, preventing any assessment of longer‐term trends or how broader shocks, such as COVID‐19, the eruption of global conflicts, and ensuing refugee inflows, may reshape gendered remittance dynamics over time. In addition, the SIMT's binary coding of gender constrains our analysis to a male/female frame that conflates sex and gender, limiting our ability to capture the full spectrum of gendered identities and their remittance implications. Future research using longitudinal data and more inclusive gender measures would meaningfully extend these findings. The interpretations we offer for the medical expense finding, in particular, remain speculative and will benefit from dedicated qualitative investigation.

In conclusion, this study fills an identified gap in a body of literature that has largely not examined the intersection of gender and marital status in immigrant remittance behaviour at the population level. Our results confirm that attending to this intersection is not merely a theoretical refinement but yields substantively different empirical conclusions: absent marital status, key gender differences in remittance amounts and purposes remain invisible. We hope these findings encourage future researchers to take both dimensions seriously and to continue building a more theoretically grounded and empirically diverse understanding of how gender is done, and redone, through transnational financial transfers.
