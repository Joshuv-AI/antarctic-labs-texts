Interleukin-40 and Soluble CD40 Ligand in Multiple Sclerosis: Inflammatory Signals or Clinically Applicable Biomarkers?
Authors: Okan SOKMEN
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

I read with great interest the article entitled “Serum Interleukin-40 and Soluble CD40 Ligand as Complementary Biomarkers for Disease Activity in Multiple Sclerosis Patients”1. The authors should be commended for investigating B-cell-associated inflammatory mediators and highlighting the potential relevance of the CD40-CD40L signaling pathway in multiple sclerosis (MS). As research in MS increasingly focuses on immune mechanisms and biomarker discovery, studies exploring novel cytokine pathways represent an important scientific contribution.

As a neurologist, I would like to evaluate the findings of this study from a clinical perspective. Although the study provides valuable insights into inflammatory mediators, several methodological and translational aspects should be considered before interleukin-40 (IL-40) and soluble CD40 ligand (sCD40L) can be interpreted as clinically meaningful biomarkers.

First, the cross-sectional design of the study limits the interpretation of temporal dynamics. The observation that IL-40 and sCD40L levels are elevated in patients with active MS demonstrates an association with inflammatory activity; however, it does not clarify whether these molecules change over time within the same individual or predict disease activity. A clinically useful monitoring biomarker should ideally exhibit longitudinal variability that parallels or precedes clinical or radiological disease activity. In current MS biomarker research, serum neurofilament light chain (sNfL) has emerged as a key marker because it reflects neuroaxonal injury and demonstrates dynamic responsiveness to disease activity and treatment effects2.

Second, the absence of a significant correlation between levels of IL-40 or sCD40L and clinical parameters, such as Expanded Disability Status Scale scores or disease duration, is noteworthy. Disability progression in MS is primarily associated with cumulative neuroaxonal damage rather than transient inflammatory signaling. Therefore, the lack of correlation with disability measures may suggest that these molecules reflect immune activation rather than structural tissue injury. Within the evolving MS biomarker framework, it may be conceptually useful to distinguish between immune activation markers, such as cytokines, and neuroaxonal injury markers, including sNfL and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP)2.

Third, the diagnostic performance reported in the receiver operating characteristic analysis should be interpreted within a broader clinical context. A comparison was made between MS patients and healthy controls. However, in real-world neurological practice, the differential diagnosis frequently involves other inflammatory demyelinating diseases such as neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease. Without the inclusion of disease control groups, the specificity of IL-40 and sCD40L within the wider spectrum of neuroinflammatory disorders remains uncertain.

Nevertheless, the biological rationale for investigating these molecules is well supported. The CD40-CD40L pathway plays a central role in immune activation through T-cell priming and B-cell co-stimulation and has been implicated in the pathogenesis of both experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis and human MS3. Moreover, recent therapeutic developments highlight the importance of this pathway. Clinical trials evaluating CD40L inhibition with frexalimab have demonstrated promising reductions in inflammatory activity in patients with MS, highlighting the clinical relevance of this immune signaling axis4.

In conclusion, the study by Neamah et al.1 contributes valuable data by expanding the spectrum of candidate inflammatory markers in MS. However, before IL-40 and sCD40L can be considered clinically applicable biomarkers, further research is required. Longitudinal studies, the inclusion of disease-control cohorts, and comparisons with established biomarkers such as sNfL and GFAP will be essential to determine whether these biomarkers provide additional value in clinical practice.
