Medicaid Expansion and Mortality Among Patients With Colorectal Cancer
Authors: Oluwasegun Akinyemi, Oladayo Oyebanji, Babawale Oluborode, Bolarinwa Akinwumi, Fadeke Ogunyankin, Kakra Hughes, Terrence Fullum, Quyen Chu, Surya Nalamati, Marwan Abouljoud, Christine Nembhard, Craig Reickert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat is the association of Medicaid expansion with overall survival in colorectal cancer, as well as with stage at diagnosis, treatment patterns, and survival benefits across racial and ethnic groups and disease stages?FindingsIn this cohort study of 658 593 adults with colorectal cancer, Medicaid expansion was associated with a 6.3% relative reduction in overall mortality hazard. Benefits were largest among Hispanic patients (approximately 22.6% relative reduction) and patients with stage IV disease.MeaningThese findings suggest that Medicaid expansion was associated with improved colorectal cancer survival, but benefits may not have been uniformly distributed, underscoring the need for targeted strategies to address persistent disparities.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer remains a major public health burden in the US and is a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality.1,2 Recent estimates have indicated that more than 150 000 new cases are diagnosed annually, with more than 50 000 deaths each year.3 Although incidence has declined among older adults, largely due to screening, the rising incidence of early-onset disease and persistent disparities in outcomes underscore ongoing challenges in colorectal cancer control.4,5

A substantial body of evidence highlights the role of social determinants of health in shaping colorectal cancer outcomes.6,7 Insurance status, socioeconomic conditions, and access to high-quality care are associated with stage at diagnosis, receipt of treatment, and survival.7 Patients from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds and historically marginalized racial and ethnic groups are more likely to present with advanced disease and experience worse outcomes.6,7,8

The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act (ACA), enacted in 2010, represents one of the most important health policy interventions in recent decades.9,10 A central component of the ACA was the expansion of Medicaid eligibility to adults with low income, with the goal of reducing the rate of uninsured individuals and improving access to preventive services, early diagnosis, and evidence-based treatment.9,11 Since implementation, Medicaid expansion has been associated with substantial increases in insurance coverage and reductions in the uninsured population, as well as in improvements in cancer screening and treatment use.12,13

However, evidence regarding the impact of Medicaid expansion on survival in colorectal cancer remains limited and incomplete. Prior studies have largely focused on intermediate outcomes, such as insurance coverage, stage at diagnosis, and treatment patterns, with fewer studies evaluating long-term survival using rigorous quasi-experimental designs.12,13,14 Moreover, the extent to which survival benefits vary across disease stage and racial and ethnic groups, and the mechanisms underlying these differences, remains not well defined. In particular, it is unclear whether observed survival gains are driven primarily by earlier detection, improved access to definitive treatment, or a combination of both.

To address these gaps, we conducted a large, national, retrospective cohort study using a difference-in-differences (DID) framework to evaluate the association between Medicaid expansion and overall mortality among adults with colorectal cancer. Leveraging extended follow-up across periods before and after ACA, we examined not only overall survival but also heterogeneity by race and ethnicity and disease stage, as well as changes in stage at diagnosis and treatment use. We hypothesized that Medicaid expansion would be associated with improved survival and that these benefits would be mediated, at least in part, through earlier detection and enhanced access to definitive care, with variation across populations.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using the National Cancer Database (NCDB), a nationwide, hospital-based oncology registry jointly sponsored by the American College of Surgeons Commission on Cancer and the American Cancer Society.15 The NCDB captures approximately 70% of newly diagnosed cancers in the US and includes detailed data on patient demographics, tumor characteristics, treatment, and survival outcomes.15 Because the NCDB comprises deidentified data, this study was determined to be exempt from review and the requirement for informed consent by the Howard University Institutional Review Board. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cohort studies.15

Study Population

We identified adults aged 40 to 64 years diagnosed with histologically confirmed colorectal cancer between January 1, 2006, and December 31, 2021. Patients older than 64 years were excluded to minimize confounding related to Medicare eligibility. We excluded individuals with missing data on key variables, including state of residence, insurance status, race and ethnicity, or survival time. Patients with missing time to event or vital status were excluded.

Exposure Definition

The primary exposure was residence in a Medicaid expansion state. States were classified based on ACA Medicaid expansion status using NCDB policy indicators. States that expanded Medicaid between 2010 and 2014 were classified as expansion states, while those that did not expand during the study period were classified as nonexpansion states. To minimize exposure misclassification, states with late or partial expansion were excluded. The specific states in each category are listed in eTable 1 in Supplement 1.

Study Periods and Outcomes

The pre-ACA period was defined as January 1, 2006, through December 31, 2013, and the post-ACA period as January 1, 2014, through December 31, 2021. The primary outcome was overall mortality; the NCDB does not record cause of death. Time to event was defined from the date of diagnosis to death or last follow-up, with censoring at last contact. Secondary outcomes included stage at diagnosis (early vs late) and receipt of cancer-directed treatments (surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and any treatment).

Covariates

Models were adjusted for demographic, clinical, and socioeconomic factors, including age, sex, race and ethnicity, tumor stage, Charlson-Deyo Comorbidity Index, insurance status, facility type, and neighborhood-level income and education. Race and ethnicity were classified using registry-coded fields in the NCDB (derived from medical records rather than self-report) and categorized as Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, and other (including American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, and Pacific Islander) (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). Race and ethnicity were assessed because they are associated with colorectal cancer stage, treatment, and survival and were central to the study’s equity aims. Disease stage was based on American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging recorded in the NCDB, coded according to the AJCC edition in effect at the year of diagnosis (6th edition for 2006 to 2009, 7th edition for 2010 to 2017, and 8th edition for 2018 to 2021).16,17,18 Early stage was defined as AJCC stage I to II and late stage as stage III to IV.

Statistical Analysis

The data analyses were performed between January 18 and March 30, 2026. Baseline characteristics were compared using χ2 tests for categorical variables and analysis of variance for continuous variables. The primary analysis used a DID framework. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models included indicators for expansion status (EXPAND); post-ACA period (ACA); and their interaction (EXPAND × ACA), which represents the policy effect. Adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs are reported, and relative hazard reductions were calculated as (1 − HR) × 100. Because a product interaction within a Cox model estimates the association on the multiplicative hazard scale rather than as an additive DID, we conducted prespecified additive scale sensitivity analyses (linear probability and logistic marginal effects models), as reported in the eTable 3 in Supplement 1.

To evaluate heterogeneity, models incorporated interaction terms for race and ethnicity (EXPAND × ACA × race and ethnicity) and disease stage (EXPAND × ACA × stage). Joint Wald tests were used to assess subgroup differences.

Secondary analyses examined mechanisms underlying survival changes. Logistic and marginal probability models were used to estimate DID effects on early-stage diagnosis and treatment use. Results are expressed as percentage point differences with 95% CIs.

The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using scaled Schoenfeld residuals. The parallel trends assumption was assessed with an event-study analysis of the primary mortality outcome (eFigure in Supplement 1). Prespecified additive scale, expansion timing (restriction to January 1, 2014, expanders), and residual confounding sensitivity analyses are reported in eTables 3 to 6 in Supplement 1.

All statistical tests were 2-sided with a significance threshold of P < .05. Analyses were conducted using Stata, version 17 (StataCorp LLC).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The study cohort included 658 593 adults with colorectal cancer (mean [SD] age, 54.9 [6.3] years; 291 793 female [44.3%] and 366 800 male [55.7%]), with 316 097 patients in the pre-ACA period and 342 496 in the post-ACA period (Table 1). Across both periods, patients in nonexpansion and expansion states were similar in age. Sex distributions were also comparable across pre-ACA nonexpansion and expansion states, although small, but statistically significant differences emerged post-ACA expansion (female, 43.5% vs 44.0%; male, 56.5% vs 56.0%; P = .02).

Substantial differences were observed in racial and ethnic composition. In both periods, expansion states compared with nonexpansion states consistently included a higher proportion of Hispanic patients (pre-ACA, 13.0% compared with 12.1%; post-ACA, 12.1% compared with 11.2%), non-Hispanic White patients (pre-ACA, 69.6% compared with 67.1%; post-ACA, 68.0% compared with 66.1%), and patients of other race and ethnicity (pre-ACA period, 6.6% compared with 2.6%; post-ACA period, 8.9% compared with 4.0%) and a lower proportion of non-Hispanic Black patients (pre-ACA, 10.8% compared with 18.3%; post-ACA period, 11.0% compared with 18.8%) (all P < .001).

Clinically, modest, but statistically significant differences in stage distribution were observed. In the post-ACA period, expansion states had a slightly higher proportion of early-stage disease (stage I, 22.6% vs 20.2%) and a lower proportion of stage IV disease (25.5% vs 27.5%) (P < .001), suggesting more favorable stage at diagnosis. Comorbidity burden was also marginally lower in expansion states, but a greater proportion of patients had a Charlson-Deyo Comorbidity Index of 0 in the expansion states vs the nonexpansion states for both periods (pre-ACA, 80.8% vs 78.6%; post-ACA, 79.7% vs 77.8%) (both P < .001).

Marked differences were evident in insurance coverage. Expansion states had substantially lower uninsured rates compared with nonexpansion states in both periods, with the gap widening post-ACA (2.4% vs 9.7%) alongside a pronounced increase in Medicaid coverage (19.0% vs 9.5%) (P < .001). Socioeconomic gradients were also notable. Expansion states compared with nonexpansion states included fewer patients from the lowest income quartile (12.2% vs 25.6%) and more from the highest income quartile (45.0% vs 24.1%) in the post-ACA period (P < .001). Similar patterns were observed across education strata (Table 1).

Facility type differed meaningfully by expansion status, with patients in expansion states in the post-ACA period more frequently treated at academic centers (39.4% vs 29.5% post-ACA) and less often at comprehensive community programs (34.9% vs 41.2%) (P < .001) (Table 1). Treatment patterns showed minimal, nonsignificant differences for radiotherapy across groups. However, modest differences were observed in systemic therapy use, including slightly lower chemotherapy (57.6% vs 59.4%) and immunotherapy (8.8% vs 10.4%) use in expansion states vs nonexpansion states in the post-ACA period (both P < .001) alongside consistently high rates of any treatment across all groups.

Event-Study Analysis (Medicaid vs Uninsured)

Event-study analyses showed no differential prepolicy trends, supporting the parallel trends assumption (Figure). Prior to expansion, estimated probabilities of Medicaid coverage compared with uninsured status were stable and similar across expansion and nonexpansion states. After ACA implementation, a sharp and sustained divergence emerged. In expansion states, the adjusted probability of Medicaid coverage (vs uninsured) increased from 0.63 (95% CI, 0.62-0.65) to 0.65 (95% CI, 0.62-0.65) to 0.88 (95% CI, 0.88-0.90) to 0.89 (95% CI, 0.88-0.90) in the post-ACA period, while nonexpansion states showed only modest changes from 0.43 (95% CI, 0.42-0.46) to 0.50 (95% CI, 0.49-0.52). The gap widened to more than 35 percentage points and persisted over time, indicating a durable shift from uninsured to Medicaid coverage. These findings confirm the identification strategy and show substantial, sustained reductions in uninsurance following Medicaid expansion. We also tested the parallel trends assumption directly on the primary mortality outcome. In an event-study analysis of 24-month all-cause mortality, a joint test of the 6 preimplementation interaction terms (2007-2012) showed no evidence of differential pre-ACA period trends (F6,1258 = 0.82; P = .55), and expansion-associated reductions emerged only after 2014 (eFigure in Supplement 1). A hazard scale model of the interaction of expansion with a linear pre-ACA period trend was likewise nonsignificant (hazard ratio [HR], 1.00 [95% CI, 0.98-1.01]; P = .74).

Overall Mortality

Among patients with colorectal cancer, Medicaid expansion was associated with a significant reduction in overall mortality compared with nonexpansion states in the post-ACA period. The DID HR was 0.94 (95% CI, 0.93-0.95; P < .001), corresponding to a 6.3% relative reduction in mortality hazard (95% CI, 5.1%-7.5%) (Table 2).

Heterogeneity by Race and Ethnicity

Race and ethnicity–stratified analyses revealed substantial heterogeneity in the association between Medicaid expansion and mortality outcomes (Table 2). The greatest improvement was observed among Hispanic patients (HR, 0.77 [95% CI, 0.75-0.80]), corresponding to a 22.6% relative reduction in mortality hazard (95% CI, 19.6%-25.4%; P < .001). In sensitivity analyses that progressively adjusted for facility mix, including a within-facility comparison, the association attenuated modestly but remained substantial and statistically significant (eTable 6 in Supplement 1). In contrast, more modest, but statistically significant reductions were observed among Black patients (HR, 0.96 [95% CI, 0.93-0.99]; relative reduction, 4.1% [95% CI, 0.7%-7.4%]) (P = .02) and White patients (HR, 0.96 [95% CI, 0.94-0.97]; relative reduction, 4.3% [95% CI, 2.8%-5.7%]) (P < .001). No statistically significant difference was observed among patients categorized as other race and ethnicity (HR, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.89-1.01]; P = .10).

Stage-Stratified Analyses

In stage-stratified analyses, Medicaid expansion was associated with significant reductions in mortality across all disease stages (Table 3). Notably, the largest benefit was observed among patients with stage IV disease (HR, 0.88 [95% CI, 0.83-0.94]), corresponding to an 11.6% relative reduction (95% CI, 6.3%-16.6%; P < .001) and suggesting a meaningful impact even in advanced disease. Significant reductions were also observed among patients with stage I (HR, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.92-0.97]; relative reduction, 5.4% [95% CI, 3.1%-7.7%]) (P < .001), stage II (HR, 0.97 [95% CI, 0.94-0.99]; relative reduction, 3.2% [95% CI, 0.7%-5.7%]) (P = .01), and stage III (HR, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.93-0.97]; relative reduction, 5.2% [95% CI, 2.9%-7.5%]) (P < .001) disease.

Furthermore, Medicaid expansion was associated with significant changes in treatment use (Table 4). Surgical treatment, typically the cornerstone of curative-intent therapy, increased by 1.54 percentage points (95% CI, 1.19-1.90 percentage points; P < .001), and receipt of any treatment increased modestly by 0.68 percentage points (95% CI, 0.46-0.89 percentage points; P < .001). In contrast, the use of chemotherapy decreased by 1.30 percentage points (95% CI, −1.80 to −0.80 percentage points; P < .001) and immunotherapy decreased by 1.62 percentage points (95% CI, −1.84 to −1.40 percentage points; P < .001), while no significant change was observed in radiotherapy (−0.20 percentage points [95% CI, −0.59 to 0.20 percentage points]; P = .33).

To examine whether the stage IV association reflected changes in systemic therapy, we conducted stage-stratified analyses of treatment use. Rather than a uniform reduction in cancer-directed treatment, the changes differed by stage. Among patients with late-stage (III-IV) cancer, expansion was associated with increased surgical treatment (1.62 percentage points [95% CI, 1.03-2.21 percentage points]; P < .001) and no significant change in chemotherapy (−0.35 percentage points [95% CI, −0.81 to 0.11 percentage points]; P = .59) (eTable 5 in Supplement 1). Furthermore, reductions in chemotherapy and immunotherapy use, although not uniform, were observed, with the chemotherapy decline concentrated in early-stage disease and the small immunotherapy decline occurring in late-stage disease.

Discussion

This national cohort study of more than 658 000 adults with colorectal cancer found that Medicaid expansion under the ACA was associated with a significant and clinically meaningful reduction in overall mortality. By using a rigorous DID framework, we observed a 6.3% relative reduction in mortality hazard among patients in expansion states compared with nonexpansion states. These survival gains were accompanied by measurable shifts in stage at diagnosis and surgical treatment use.

A central finding of our study is the heterogeneous impact of Medicaid expansion across racial and ethnic groups.19 While expansion was associated with improved survival across most populations, the magnitude of benefit was substantially greater among Hispanic patients, who experienced an estimated 22.6% relative reduction in mortality hazard.19,20 In contrast, more modest improvements were observed among non-Hispanic Black and White patients, and no statistically significant benefit was observed among patients categorized as other race and ethnicity.21 The pronounced Hispanic association also warrants caution regarding residual confounding because Hispanic patients were more concentrated in expansion states and at academic centers.22,23 In sensitivity analyses that progressively adjusted for facility mix, including a within-facility comparison, the association attenuated modestly but remained substantial and statistically significant (eTable 6 in Supplement 1), suggesting that residual confounding by care setting may not fully explain the finding.24,25

Importantly, stage-stratified analyses revealed that the survival benefits associated with Medicaid expansion were evident across all disease stages, with the largest magnitude observed among patients with stage IV disease.20,26 To examine whether the stage IV association reflected changes in systemic therapy, we conducted stage-stratified analyses of treatment use (eTable 5 in Supplement 1). Rather than a uniform reduction in cancer-directed treatment,20 the changes differed by stage. Among patients with late-stage (III-IV) cancer,20,27 expansion was associated with increased surgical treatment and no significant change in chemotherapy,28 a pattern more consistent with more stage-appropriate treatment than with reduced access.20,27,28 Because the NCDB lacks data on treatment sequence, adherence, regimen detail, and tumor biology,20 the association among patients with advanced disease cannot be attributed to a specific clinical pathway.29,30 Consistent with this interpretation, Medicaid expansion was associated with a significant shift toward earlier stage at diagnosis, with an increase of 2.13 percentage points in the probability of early-stage disease.31,32

In addition to stage migration, we observed meaningful changes in treatment use patterns. Medicaid expansion was associated with increased use of surgical treatment, the cornerstone of curative-intent therapy, and a modest increase in receipt of any treatment, suggesting improved access to definitive care.12,20,27,28 We also observed reductions in chemotherapy and immunotherapy use. In formal stage-stratified DID analyses (eTable 5 in Supplement 1), these declines were not uniform. The chemotherapy decline was concentrated in early-stage disease,33,34 for which systemic therapy is frequently not guideline indicated, whereas the small late-stage immunotherapy decline occurred during a period of rapidly evolving immunotherapy indications in colorectal cancer,34,35 governed by microsatellite-instability status not captured in the NCDB.33,34,35 Taken together, these findings support a multifactorial mechanism in which improved insurance coverage may facilitate earlier diagnosis and enhance access to curative-intent interventions,12,20,27 particularly surgery, thereby contributing to improved survival.8,20,36,37

Taken together, these findings suggest that the observed mortality reductions associated with Medicaid expansion may be partially mediated through earlier stage at diagnosis and increased access to definitive surgical treatment. These intermediate shifts are modest in absolute terms and given the absence of granular clinical data, are hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. They are consistent with, but do not establish, earlier detection and improved access to definitive care as mechanisms.

The policy implications of these findings are substantial. First, they provide robust, contemporary evidence supporting Medicaid expansion as an effective population-level intervention to reduce cancer mortality.20,38 Second, they highlight the importance of moving beyond coverage alone to ensure equitable delivery of high-quality cancer care.14,39 Targeted strategies, such as improving access to high-volume centers,40 strengthening care coordination, and addressing social determinants of health,41,42 could be essential to maximize the benefits of expansion and reduce persistent disparities.20,28,41,42,43

Limitations

This study had several limitations, including the inherent limitations of an observational design. Although rigorous methods were applied, including the use of a quasi-experimental DID design, residual confounding may persist. The NCDB is hospital based and may not capture all care delivered outside Commission on Cancer–accredited facilities, potentially limiting generalizability.44 Additionally, detailed information on the sequence of treatment, adherence to therapy, and tumor biology was not available, precluding more granular mechanistic analyses. In addition, the DID policy association was estimated with a product interaction in a Cox model, which operates on the multiplicative hazards scale rather than as an additive DID45; additive scale sensitivity analyses (linear probability and logistic marginal-effects models) yielded concordant conclusions.45,46 Because the analytic file did not retain a state identifier, standard errors could not be clustered at the state level, and robust standard errors may modestly understate uncertainty.46,47

Another consideration was the restriction of the study population to adults aged 40 to 64 years. While this approach minimizes confounding related to near-universal Medicare eligibility among older adults, it limits generalizability to the broader population of patients with colorectal cancer, in which incidence is highest among individuals aged 65 years or older.3,48,49,50 Furthermore, the study period coincides with a rising incidence of early-onset colorectal cancer among younger adults, particularly in the latter half of the study period.

Variation in state-level implementation and health system characteristics also may have influenced the observed associations. Nevertheless, the study’s large, nationally representative cohort and robust analytic approach provided strong evidence that may not be materially altered by these limitations.

Conclusions

This cohort study found that Medicaid expansion under the ACA was associated with significant reductions in mortality among adults with colorectal cancer, with particularly pronounced benefits among Hispanic patients and patients with advanced disease. Because causal inference is inherently constrained in an observational design, the consistent association between Medicaid expansion and improved colorectal cancer outcomes across multiple analyses is relevant for policy and clinical practice.
