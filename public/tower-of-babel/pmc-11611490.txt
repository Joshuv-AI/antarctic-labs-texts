Awareness on the spread of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales in animals as One Health
Authors: Su Min Kyung, Han Sang Yoo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOne Health is a multisectoral cooperative and collaborative approach aimed at achieving integrated health by recognizing the interconnectedness of people, animals, plants, and the environment. In the modern world, the One Health approach is considered an essential perspective for overcoming various emerging diseases, including antimicrobial resistance (AMR). One of the top priority AMR infections is caused by carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE), which are bacterial strains not susceptible to carbapenems, the last-resort antibiotics for clinical Gram-negative bacterial infections. Although carbapenems are not allowed to use in animals in the global community, various types of CRE are being reported among animals, including the carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE). Therefore, since the dissemination and clinical infection of CPE represent a shared health threat, measures to raise awareness of this issue should be considered from the perspective of the One Health approach.Keywords: One Health, antibiotic resistance, multidrug resistance, carbapenem

One of the most important concepts in the modern veterinary clinical environment is to consider the integrated characteristics of various aspects of global society. In the modern world, 60% of emerging infectious diseases originate from animals, with more than 70% of those identified as originating from wildlife [1]. The ‘One Health approach,’ which addresses the interconnected health aspects of humans, animals, plants, and the environment, is an essential concept for managing various modern clinical threats. One of the top-priority concerns that the One Health approach can address is antimicrobial resistance (AMR), a shared infectious threat to people, animals, and the environment. Based on global reports (Table 1) concerning humans, animals, and the environment, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE)—one of the priority resistant strains highlighted by the World Health Organization—should also be addressed from a One Health perspective [2,3,4].

The spread of antibiotic-resistant bacteria has recently emerged as a new public health problem worldwide even though the discovery of antibiotics has had a revolutionary impact on global human history. Since the discovery of penicillin in 1928, numerous antibiotics have been invented, leading to the unwelcome development of a wide range of AMR. Now, AMR has become a significant concern and threat to public health worldwide. The significance of resistant pathogens in the contemporary world is demonstrated by the high proportion (20.9%) of drug-resistant strains among newly emerging infectious diseases [1]. In modern society, AMR has already become a serious public health threat, resulting in a significant number of deaths. Presently, nearly 3 million cases of AMR infections are occurring, leading to 35,000 people deaths in the USA and up to 25,000 deaths in Europe [2]. From a global standpoint, at least 1.27 million deaths are attributed to AMR infections, and more than 5 million deaths might be potentially associated with AMR [2,3].

Carbapenems are recognized for their broad spectrum of antibacterial activity and reliability against most β-lactamase producers because of higher stability to β-lactamases. Due to their broad spectrum of action and resistance against β-lactamase, carbapenems have been widely utilized in clinical settings for the treatment of Gram-negative bacterial infections. After the first approval of imipenem as the first carbapenem in 1985, meropenem, ertapenem and doripenem were sequentially discovered, approved and clinically used with their characteristic scopes of application [5]. However, after the first emergence of carbapenemase-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae, carbapenem resistance has spread around the world and the types of resistance have also become diverse. To date, various types of CRE strains have been reported worldwide and the infection rate also increased rapidly, contributing to significant losses and economic costs. In 2017, CRE in the USA were reported to be associated with 13,100 cases and 1,100 deaths, resulting in reported healthcare costs of 130 million dollars [2]. Therefore, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) listed the threat of CRE as one of the urgent AMR threats in the USA [2]. Although the use of carbapenems in animals, whether as pets or in livestock production, is not licensed globally, animals have nonetheless become reservoirs for CRE through unidentified routes in the modern world [4,6,7].

The threat of CRE cannot be regarded as a concern limited to human society. In modern society, economic development has led to a significant increase in both the interest in and adoption of companion animals. As a result of this social phenomenon, companion animals are now considered important members of human society. A growing concern in this relationship between humans and companion animals is the potential exchange of zoonotic pathogens, including AMR strains such as CREs [4,8,9]. Recently, reports on the occurrence of CREs from companion animals have been increasing globally regardless of their approval. Several recent studies have described risk factors associated with the sharing of AMR strains between humans and animals. For example, companion animals hospitalized in veterinary hospital settings may contribute to spread AMR bacterial strains among the owners [7]. Or the sharing of AMR strains may occur within co-dwelling household [10]. Therefore, AMR bacterial strains such as CREs can be transmitted between companion animals and people. Considering the range of clinical services offered by modern veterinary medicine, the emergence of CREs from animals raises significant concerns, not only in the context of veterinary medicine but also in public health.

The urgent need for control measures to address the concern of CRE arises not only from the current spread of the bacteria. In early days, carbapenem resistance was attributed to several different mechanisms; mutations in the D2 porin protein located in the cell wall, and the modification of penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs), such as amino acid sequence substitutions or acquisition of a novel PBP [5]. Nowadays, an increasing spectrum of mechanisms of resistance are emerging as global threat [3]. Above all, the most effective and menacing resistance development depends on the acquisition of carbapenemase, which is developing and spreading globally in modern society [4,6,8]. About 30% of CREs are known to carry carbapenemase-producing mobile gene elements, which can confer resistance to neighboring strains [2]. Enterobacterales strains such as K. pneumoniae and Escherichia coli can become CRE by acquiring mobile genes that confer the ability to produce carbapenemase, which can also be referred to as carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE) [2]. CPEs are well known for readily spreading resistance to nearby bacterial strains [2]. Therefore, urgent and effective management strategies to prevent and control the global spread of carbapenemases should be discussed and established.

There are additional contributing factors for why the CDC have evaluated CREs as an “urgent threat” among the major AMR strains [2]. Carbapenems had been considered the "last-resort" antibiotics until the emergence of CRE. However, based on the current global situation of CRE, the rising concern is that the usage of other last-resort drugs may result in the emergence of another resistant strain in the clinical environment [11]. After the era between 1949 and 1980, known as the “antibiotic discovery void”, major antibiotic development has been stagnated [3]. Nowadays, the development of novel drugs to address this crisis is significantly difficult because of the extremely low returns on investment for new drug inventions [12]. In other words, even if a new antibiotic is invented, resistance to the drug will eventually emerge [3]. Therefore, in order to combat the emergence of antibiotic resistance, including CRE, it is necessary to in-depth explore alternative ways through a One Health approach along with continuous efforts of new drug development when considering the circulation of antibiotic resistance in human, animals and environments. In addition, to achieve common goals in addressing this global threat, multi-sectoral cooperation, communication, and collaboration are necessary, involving not only the academic field but also the clinical and industrial sectors.

To address the rapid spread of carbapenemase-producing organisms, various interventions are being undertaken. However, the development of novel drugs to address this crisis is significantly difficult, as the returns on investment for new drug inventions may not align with the amount of money invested, primarily due to a significantly low success rate [12]. Nowdays, novel approaches are needed to overcome the rapid spread of carbapenemase producers, including β-lactam-β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, such as ceftazidime-avibactam, a combination drug consisting of a β-lactamase inhibitor and ceftazidime [13]. In recent years, bacteriophages have again been receiving a lot of attention as one of the alternative ways to deal with antibiotic-resistant bacteria. Therefore, approaches to use bacteriophages as replacement therapeutic options for antibiotics are also under development. Phages, known as natural enemies of bacteria, attach to bacterial cells and insert genetic material into them, ultimately leading to bacterial cell lysis and killing the organism [14]. Advantages of phage therapy include its ability to selectively target pathogens, self-amplification, biofilm penetration, and preservation of commensal bacteria [15].

There is an urgent need to find out ways to effectively deal with carbapenem antibiotic resistance, which is currently spreading rapidly. Therefore, in order to effectively control the spread of CRE, it is essential to apply One Health approach to block the opportunities for the spread of the resistant bacteria among human, animals, and environment in addition to the continuous development of new antibiotics and antibiotic alternatives and rational use of antibiotics (Fig. 1). Considering the global system of companion animal raising and management, it is necessary to establish a comprehensive management system based on One Health in order to suppress the spread of CRE, especially in companion animals.
