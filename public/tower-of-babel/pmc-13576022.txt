Adolescent-Centered Cannabis Messaging in Clinical Settings: Preliminary Qualitative Insights From Adolescents for Clinicians
Authors: Pravesh Sharma, Hannah Kolarik, Payton Jorgenson, Christina Smith, Stephan Arndt, Nicholas L Bormann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYouth-directed cannabis messaging is often generic, adult-centered, and does not reflect contemporary patterns of cannabis use. This study examined adolescents’ perceptions of cannabis-related messaging and identified messaging strategies they find relevant.MethodsThe authors conducted qualitative interviews with adolescents aged 14–17 years with past 30-day cannabis use. Interviews were analyzed using thematic analysis with constant comparison. Data collection continued until no new themes were identified (N = 7).ResultsAdolescents reported that fear-based and abstinence-only messaging were ineffective. They viewed nonjudgmental, autonomy-supportive, and youth-centered messaging as more credible. Participants emphasized that clinicians should acknowledge the perceived benefits of cannabis to build trust and credibility. Messages that focused on immediate consequences of cannabis use, such as brain fog and memory impairment, were more effective than generalized long-term harms. Adolescents expressed greater trust in harm reduction messaging and communication strategies that integrate real-world adolescent experiences with clinician expertise.ConclusionsThese preliminary findings suggest that clinicians should consider framing cessation messaging around cognition and memory. Although the long-term goal is cannabis cessation, clinicians should remain ready to transition toward harm reduction guidance, based on adolescents’ receptiveness and their response to initial abstinence-focused conversation, reflecting an adaptive framework that shifts in intensity and orientation with the patient’s response rather than following a static treatment algorithm. Although these strategies may enhance engagement and mitigate treatment dropout, larger-scale studies are needed before these strategies can be broadly recommended for clinical practice. Ethical incorporation of peer-lived experiences to support harm reduction and cessation should be considered.

Introduction

Cannabis is among the most used substances in adolescence and young adulthood. 1,2 Recent national data indicate that in 2024, the past 12-month cannabis use was 7.2% in 8th graders, 15.9% in 10th graders, and 25.8% in 12th graders. 2 Over the last decade, cannabis products have evolved substantially, and adolescents now have varied products to choose from, ranging from combustible forms to edibles and high-potency concentrates. 3 The high-potency products are often used through dabbing or vaping and are also prevalent among adolescents. For example, in 2024, 5.6% of 8th graders, 11.6% of 10th graders, and 17.6% of 12th graders reported cannabis vaping in the prior year. 2 The same survey noted that delta-8-tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ8-THC), a psychoactive cannabinoid different from the commonly known cannabinoid delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ9-THC), was used by 2.9% of 8th graders and 7.9% of 10th graders. These data highlight the widespread availability and use of cannabis and its products by adolescents. 3

Adolescence and young adulthood constitute a critical period of neural development. 4 Cannabis use during this vulnerable period may disrupt typical brain development. Consequently, this puts adolescents who use cannabis at a higher likelihood of developing cannabis use disorder, psychiatric diagnoses (eg, anxiety, psychosis), use of other substances, and broader psychosocial challenges, such as impaired academic performance and disrupted social development. 5 Therefore, clinicians who treat adolescents are in a unique position to engage adolescents in open and nonjudgmental conversation about cannabis use and its harms. 6

Compared with adults, adolescents often have greater reluctance to discuss substance use with clinicians. 7 This reluctance may reflect limited trust in health care practitioners and discomfort disclosing sensitive information to authority figures. 7,8 Clinicians, in turn, frequently report hesitancy to initiate conversations about substance use out of concern that it may strain rapport or make adolescents uncomfortable. 9 These bidirectional dynamics create missed opportunities for early identification and intervention, allowing cannabis use to go unaddressed in this critical period of development. 10

In clinical settings, cannabis messaging is inconsistent and infrequent, mostly delivered within the single premise of discontinuing ongoing use. 11 However, this approach often leads to adolescent defensiveness and resistance to behavioral change. 12,13 Clinicians across primary care and mental health settings frequently report limited guidance on how to communicate with adolescents in ways that are acceptable, credible, and collaborative. 14 As a result, even well-intentioned clinicians are left with no clear youth-informed framework for cannabis conversations that may deter future use. 1

Moreover, current cannabis-related messaging from health care practitioners is often generic, adult-centric, and poorly aligned with the realities of contemporary adolescent cannabis use. 14,15 This mismatch between what clinicians offer and adolescents find helpful may erode rapport, undermine trust, and reduce the potential relevance of clinical guidance. Tailoring communication to adolescents’ perspectives and information needs is essential, yet there is limited research that draws directly on adolescents’ perspectives to guide how clinicians talk about cannabis. 16,17

In this study, the authors used in-depth qualitative interviews with adolescent cannabis users to examine how they perceive cannabis-related health messages and to identify the communication approaches they find credible, relevant, and persuasive. The authors examined their views on message content, delivery style, messenger credibility, and preferred communication modalities. Additionally, the authors sought to understand how contextual factors, such as social normalization, illegality, availability, and stress, shape the way youth interpret and respond to cannabis messaging. By providing direct input from adolescent cannabis users, this study seeks to provide preliminary guidance on what communication feels credible, respectful, and meaningful in routine primary care and mental health settings.

Methods

SETTING

This study was conducted within the Mayo Clinic Health System and was approved by the Mayo Clinic institutional review board, which determined that the study involved no greater than minimal risk and approved a waiver of signed consent and parental permission. Adolescents aged 14–17 years provided assent. The study did not involve the collection of protected health information, and Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) authorization was not required.

PARTICIPANTS

The authors aimed to recruit 8–10 English-speaking adolescents (ages 14–17) between September 1, 2025, and December 1, 2025, who self-reported current cannabis use within the past 30 days and had received care from a Mayo Clinic Health System primary care or mental health practitioner within the past year. Exclusion criteria included lack of English language fluency; inability to provide assent in English; presence of a DSM-5 diagnosis of current moderate-to-severe substance use disorder (other than cannabis or nicotine); a history or current diagnosis of a psychotic disorder or episode (excluding cannabis-induced psychosis); and a neurodevelopmental disorder or intellectual disability. Participation was voluntary and did not require parental consent. No remuneration was provided.

INTERVIEW

A semistructured interview guide was developed by study team members and pretested to ensure that length, flow, and content were appropriate (Supplementary Material 1). Each interview was conducted via Zoom audio only by 2 fourth year medical students on the authors’ team (HK and PMJ) trained by the principal investigator, who has expertise in qualitative interview methodology. Training included mock interviews conducted by the principal investigator prior to data collection. The interviews lasted 30–45 minutes. Although structured questions were used, the interviews were flexible, driven in part by participants’ responses, with probes and prompts used for elaboration and clarification. Audio recordings were stored in an encrypted Mayo Clinic desktop in a password-protected folder, further protected by a Mayo Clinic firewall. A unique participant ID # was linked to each participant’s audio recording. The study staff uploaded the recordings to the Mayo Clinic–approved transcription vendor. The transcript contained no patient identifier but a uniquely assigned pseudonym.

DATA SATURATION

Data collection concluded at N = 7 participants, following consultation with the study’s data analyst, who determined that additional recruitment was unlikely to yield new thematic insights, indicating data saturation. 18 Data collection and analysis occurred concurrently. After each interview, transcripts were compared to prior data using constant comparison. Saturation was assessed at the level of code and meaning development, defined as the point at which no new codes, themes, or dimensions of existing themes emerged.

QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS

Data analysis employed an inductive thematic analysis approach, using iterative coding and constant comparison to identify patterns across interviews. All transcripts were read in full to gain familiarity with participant narratives. Initial coding was conducted at the semantic level, capturing explicit statements related to cannabis perceptions, motivations for use, cognitive and physical effects, and attitudes toward prevention messaging. Codes were then organized into conceptual categories and refined through repeated review. 19

Although qualitative analysis prioritized thematic depth and meaning, the authors also documented the distribution of themes across participants to enhance transparency. Frequency descriptors (eg, “most,” “several”) and occasional counts were used to indicate how widely themes were represented, without implying statistical generalizability.

To ensure rigor and trustworthiness, themes were validated through multiple strategies. 18–21

Results

Across all interviews, adolescents offered clear and consistent guidance on which cannabis-related messages resonate with them, which they dismiss, and which communication elements shape their likelihood of reducing or avoiding future cannabis use. Findings clustered into 5 overarching domains aligned with the study aim.

PERCEPTIONS OF MESSAGE EFFECTIVENESS

Fear-Only Messages Are Ineffective

Throughout the interviews, adolescents described fear-based messaging as ineffective and disengaging. As one participant explained:

“The fear factor . . . makes teens wanna try it more.”

Acknowledge Perceived Benefits, Then Emphasize Functional Consequences and Trade-Offs

Adolescents emphasized that effective communication must begin with clinicians acknowledging perceived benefits of cannabis (such as improved sleep, increased appetite, stress reduction, or emotional numbing) adolescents experienced (or the reason they initiated using in the first place) before shifting to harms. They noted that when these perceived benefits were not acknowledged, health messages were readily dismissed and felt “out of touch.”

“I think it can be helpful . . . people [who use cannabis] are really stressed and use it as a stress reliever.”

After acknowledging perceived benefits, adolescents stated that clinicians should explore adolescents’ own experiences with near-term functional consequences that adolescents already recognize, such as memory problems, next-day brain fog, missed assignments, mood changes, dependence, and financial strain to support reduction in future use, rather than relying on general statements that “drugs are bad.”

“I still feel like I get brain fog . . . starting at 15. . . . I don’t want my brain stuck at that level.”

“If you smoked last night, you wake up and you might have brain fog. . . . If you’re at a job and you can’t remember anything, you’re probably not gonna keep that job very long.”

“It’s really stressful . . . trying to figure out how to get money for it. . . . You don’t wanna run your pockets dry.”

Delivery of the Message

Beyond content, adolescents emphasized the importance of open conversations rather than “lectures.” They valued clinicians who asked reflective, autonomy-supportive questions aligned with how adolescents evaluate their cannabis use. Such questions allow clinicians to align with the adolescent while further exploring dependence and the functional impact of cannabis. Adolescents also suggested self-reflective questions that peers could use to evaluate their own readiness and context before using cannabis:

“Ask yourself: ‘Do I need this or want this?’ ‘Am I in an okay environment?’ ‘Am I okay in my head right now?’”

Messaging should also feel relatable rather than didactics:

“Open conversation. . . be relatable . . . not just listing statistics.”

MESSAGE CREDIBILITY

Adolescents emphasized that the credibility of cannabis-related messages depends heavily on who delivers the information and how authentic, grounded, and personally relevant it feels. Three credibility subthemes emerged.

Credibility Depends on the Messenger: Adolescents Trust Lived Experience Plus Clinician Expertise

Participants consistently described dual authority as central to credibility: Real peers who had experienced harms and successfully quit cannabis paired with clinicians who can explain those experiences within evidence-based guidance. Adolescents distrusted adults who had not “been through it” or relied on moral judgment.

“I think I’d use someone my age [rather] than a higher-up. . . . Making it into like a short-form video (TikTok style), like how most people are addicted to watching TikTok.”

Honesty and Transparency Are Core Credibility Signals

Youth described honesty, transparency, and willingness to acknowledge uncertainty as strong credibility markers. Oversimplification or exaggeration of harm was seen as eroding trust and reinforced skepticism toward clinical guidance. Adolescents responded more favorably to clinicians who communicated nuances and admitted the limits of available evidence rather than delivering categorical warnings.

“The real stories are just really impactful. . . . I don’t talk about it because I know that’s exactly what I am gonna be told.”

Evidence Feels Credible When Linked to Something to Compare

Adolescents interpreted cannabis risks through comparison. Cannabis feels “normal” and “less dangerous” than other substances due to social normalization. As a result, adolescents reported that health messages are most effective when they highlight short-term, functional costs that adolescents can readily recognize in their daily lives rather than long-term harms that feel distant.

“Weed is a slow burn. . . . Compared to fentanyl or alcohol, the impacts seep into your brain.”

“Compared to Molly and Coke, it’s not as addictive. . . . I can control myself and still be me.”

PREFERENCE FOR HARM REDUCTION–ORIENTED COMMUNICATION OVER ABSTINENCE-ONLY MESSAGING

Adolescents expressed a preference for harm reduction–oriented communication rather than abstinence-only messaging from clinicians.

“I personally don’t think it’s extremely harmful. But I don’t think that somebody should be dependent on it to be happy [suggesting that reducing dependence, rather than necessarily complete cessation, was seen as an acceptable goal].”

“Don’t use fear. . . . Educate on outcomes and remind people they can be happy without dependence [emphasizing freedom from dependence as the primary goal while not explicitly requiring complete abstinence].”

Participants emphasized that effective clinical guidance should focus on healthy functioning and autonomy rather than directives to just quit.

WHAT CAN DETER CANNABIS USE AMONG ADOLESCENTS

Immediate Cognitive and Functional Harms

Messages that connected cannabis use to memory problems, next-day brain fog, impaired focus, and reduced productivity were viewed as the most believable and effective in motivating reduced use. Adolescents repeatedly linked these effects to missed assignments, tardiness, difficulty focusing, lower productivity, interpersonal friction, negative self-perception (feeling “fried,” “not in it”), and impaired daily functioning. Although some participants noted perceived short-term focus benefits at low doses, they generally acknowledged that longer-term use led to more substantial cognitive and functional decline.

“When I did smoke, I didn’t have the best grades. . . . [I was] constantly forgetting things. . . . Just not in it.”

“I still feel like I get brain fog . . . starting at 15. . . . I don’t want my brain stuck at that level.”

“If you smoked last night… you wake up and you might have brain fog. . . . If you’re at a job and can’t remember anything, you’re probably not gonna keep that job very long.”

Mental Health vs Physical Harms and Impacts

Adolescents emphasized that mental health effects were among the most serious and believable harms, often more compelling than distant physical risks. Many described short-term numbing or detachment followed by a return to or worsening of underlying problems. Several participants reported improved mental health after quitting. Adolescents indicated that mental health impacts felt more personally relevant and compelling than physical harms, suggesting clinicians should prioritize mental health consequences when discussing cannabis risks.

“Daily use . . . I was more anxious . . . more paranoid. . . . It’s a short-term fix . . . it always comes back.”

“With psychoactive effects, it can fry your brain.”

Lung and oral harms were commonly accepted by adolescents as part of cannabis use, although this acceptance did not always translate into concern.

“Doctor told me . . . you have asthma. . . . ‘I wouldn’t smoke; do edibles instead.’”

“I’ve known one person who had to go to the hospital because of her lungs . . . more nicotine vaping.”

Cardiovascular risk was inconsistently understood, although its importance became more pronounced when personally experienced (eg, heart racing).

“I got really high . . . heart was beating, I started sweating . . . then I threw up . . . too much weed could be bad.”

Cardiovascular effects were often described to be perceived as dose-dependent or unlikely to affect the individual personally. Some participants were dismissive of cardiovascular risks and stroke, remarking:

“I know smoking anything isn’t good for your heart . . . but I don’t think it’ll happen to me.”

“It could have those chances (heart/stroke) . . . but that’s only if you stack it with other drugs.”

Dependence, Escalation, and Withdrawal-Like Experiences

Several adolescents described experiencing psychological dependence (“dependence is real”), tolerance, escalating use, and difficulty stopping, such as insomnia and irritability. These experiences often prompted them to reevaluate or reduce their cannabis use.

“When I try to stop . . . can’t sleep, emotions out of control.”

“It was (first) only at night . . . then night and morning . . . then throughout the day.”

Drawing on these experiences, adolescents offered specific recommendations for how clinicians should address withdrawal-like symptoms. They emphasized that clinicians should explain that early discomfort after quitting is typical and provide support, such as sleep strategies, help managing emotions, and gradual tapering plans. Adolescents also noted that relapse is a common part of the process and that clinicians should frame unsuccessful quit attempts as opportunities to learn and try again rather than as failures.

“I had to take a tea (tolerance) break . . . 8 months. . . . Relapse is part of recovery. . . . Learn from it.”

Product Safety and Lacing Concerns

In unregulated markets, adolescents expressed concern about laced and counterfeit products.

“In our state . . . you’re getting fake things. You’re getting laced. Nothing is real.”

“You have no idea what you’re actually inhaling.”

“Show what it’s grown in . . . . You’d have no idea if it’s laced or not [participant expressing that without visibility into growing conditions and sourcing, adolescents have no way to assess contamination risk].”

These concerns introduced a sense of unpredictability and vulnerability that made messaging about purity, contamination, and route-of-use safety highly persuasive. Adolescents indicated that clinicians who acknowledged product safety concerns in unregulated markets felt more credible and relevant to their real-world experiences.

Linking Harms to Goals and Identity

Messages were more believable and more likely to influence intentions when they connected cannabis use to school performance, employment, sports, financial stability, and self-image.

“If you’re at a job and you can’t remember anything, you’re probably not gonna keep that job very long.”

“If you have important things to do and you’re using . . . you could get in trouble.”

CONTEXTUAL FACTORS SHAPING HOW ADOLESCENTS RECEIVE CANNABIS-RELATED MESSAGES

Adolescents’ interpretations of cannabis-related messages were strongly influenced by their broader environments, including illegality, access, family dynamics, stress, and social norms. They considered these contextual factors highly relevant when receiving cannabis-related guidance from clinicians. Clinicians who demonstrated awareness of these contextual factors and incorporated them into their messaging were viewed as more credible, relatable, and trustworthy by adolescents.

Illegality and Market Conditions Shape Risk Perception

In regions without legal cannabis markets, adolescents described navigating higher-risk, unregulated supply chains, which influenced both their concerns and their expectations of clinicians. Adolescents explained that illegality creates uncertainty, misinformation, and reliance on peers or dealers for product knowledge. Adolescents felt clinicians who acknowledged the realities of unregulated markets and product uncertainty were seen as more informed and relevant to their experiences.

“People here don’t talk about strains or doses. . . . It’s just whatever the guy has that day.”

“When it’s illegal, you’re getting information from other kids, not from someone who actually knows.”

Social Normalization Reduces Perceived Risk

Cannabis use was described as common, which often made adolescents dismiss general warnings or abstinence-only messages. However, this same environment heightened the relevance of functioning-based and emotional health messaging. Adolescents noted that messaging felt more credible when clinicians acknowledged the social normalization of cannabis in their communities rather than dismissing it.

“To me, it really doesn’t seem like a big deal. I was raised around it.”

“People around me doing it . . . my friends.”

Home Environment, Stress, and Emotional Context Influence Message Reception

Adolescents experiencing family and environmental stress described cannabis as a coping tool, altering their expectations of clinicians and the messages they found meaningful. They wanted clinicians to recognize the role of stress and home environment in their cannabis use, noting that messages felt more compelling when these pressures were acknowledged.

“I think that is really helpful in terms of dealing with things at home—because getting reactive only creates more problems.”

“. . . they [adolescents] have bad day, they’ll go home and get high. . . . It started like that, now it’s almost a need.”

Varied and Informal Sources of Cannabis Information

Many adolescents described reliance on personal experiences rather than authoritative sources for cannabis use–related health information. Adolescents viewed clinicians as one of the few trusted sources of accurate cannabis information, given their reliance on informal and experiential sources.

“School, family, and friends . . . social media . . . kind of everywhere, honestly [regarding source of cannabis information].”

“I have not, like, done a whole lot learning about it, just a whole lotta [lot of] doing it.”

Discussion

This qualitative study examined how adolescents in clinical settings perceive cannabis-related communication and identified preliminary insights to inform future research and clinical practice. In the following sections, the authors contextualize these findings.

FEAR-BASED, HARM-ONLY MESSAGING UNDERMINES THERAPEUTIC COMMUNICATION WITH ADOLESCENTS

Adolescents consistently rejected fear-based and didactic messaging as mechanisms for behavior change. This finding stands in contrast to portions of the empirical literature, which remains divided on the effectiveness of fear appeals. For instance, a large meta-analysis encompassing approximately 27,000 participants reported a modest but positive average effect of fear appeals among high school–aged youth across diverse behavioral domains (d ≈ 0.29–0.35). 22 Importantly, however, these effects were moderated by adolescents’ perceived efficacy; that is, their belief in their ability to manage or respond to the threatened outcome. 23

In naturalistic contexts, adolescents in the authors’ study described fear-based messages as disengaging rather than motivating, particularly when such messages failed to acknowledge their lived experiences or perceived benefits of cannabis use. This pattern is consistent with psychological reactance theory, which posits that messages perceived as coercive or autonomy-threatening can provoke defensive responses, including resistance or avoidance. 24 This response is often conceptualized as defensive avoidance or a “backfire” effect, wherein individuals disengage from messages they perceive as excessively threatening and insufficiently empowering. 23–25 These divergent findings may be partially explained by methodological differences: meta-analytic evidence is largely derived from controlled experimental studies with high internal validity, which may not fully capture the relational, developmental, and contextual dynamics that inform adolescents’ responses to health messaging in real-world settings. 22

ACKNOWLEDGING PERCEIVED BENEFITS: CLINICAL BOUNDARIES AND ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

Participants emphasized that effective messages should begin by acknowledging the perceived benefits of cannabis use, such as stress relief and improved sleep, before transitioning to discussions of potential harms. When these benefits were not recognized, adolescents frequently dismissed messages as uninformed or out of touch. Given that adolescence is a developmental period characterized by identity formation, autonomy-seeking, and self-exploration, messaging that invalidates these processes may be experienced as dismissive or demeaning, thereby undermining engagement. 26–28 This approach aligns closely with the principles of motivational interviewing, which emphasize acknowledging individuals’ experiences and motivations, exploring ambivalence, developing discrepancy, and eliciting intrinsic motivation rather than prescribing behavior change. 29 Acknowledging the perceived positive aspects of cannabis use, without endorsing their clinical validity, appears to reduce resistance and facilitate a shift from defensive dismissal toward reflective, internally motivated engagement. 13,29

Although adolescents in this study reported perceiving benefits from cannabis use, such as improved sleep, reduced anxiety, and stress relief, the existing evidence does not consistently support these effects at a population level. Some individuals may experience short-term symptomatic relief, particularly at low doses; however, tolerance develops with continued use, often leading to escalating consumption and blunting of any initial perceived benefits. 30,31 Clinicians may acknowledge what the adolescent believes cannabis does for them, while being transparent about the lack of evidence supporting these perceived benefits. Because adolescents may selectively interpret empathy as clinical agreement, clinicians should employ a structured, motivational interview–informed approach in which acknowledgment of the motive for use is immediately followed by evidence-based discrepancy, with a brief collaborative reflection serving as a check against this misinterpretation. For example, “I hear that cannabis feels calming to you. . . . It’s clear you’re working hard to manage something genuinely difficult. At the same time, the evidence shows that over time, cannabis can worsen the very symptoms it initially seemed to help. I’m curious what you think about that?” This approach may acknowledge the adolescent’s experience while preserving clinical accuracy and avoiding inadvertent endorsement.

Clinicians should recognize that the nature of the perceived benefit may itself guide the direction of clinical inquiry; for example, an adolescent reporting emotional numbing as a reason for use due to contextual factors (some noted in the authors’ study) may open a conversation about underlying emotional experiences, while an adolescent reporting sleep or stress relief may lead the inquiry in a different direction. Neither should be dismissed, and each warrants a different clinical conversation. Although acknowledging adolescents’ perceived benefits of cannabis use may enhance engagement, clinicians retain an ethical obligation to provide accurate, evidence-based guidance and to recommend abstinence when clinically indicated. This should always occur within the boundaries of the clinician’s duty to promote adolescent health and prevent harm and should be individualized to the patient’s developmental stage, clinical context, and readiness to change.

IMMEDIATE FUNCTIONAL CONSEQUENCES AND PRODUCT SAFETY AS KEY DETERRENTS FOR ADOLESCENT CANNABIS USE

In this study, the information adolescents found most persuasive focused on immediate, functional consequences they could readily recognize. Examples included next-day brain fog, impaired concentration, memory problems, worsening anxiety or mood, and disruptions at school or work. Linking cannabis use directly to these outcomes and the adolescent’s own goals (eg, grades, employment, relationships) was viewed as more substantial than distant warnings about chronic disease. This pattern is consistent with delayed discounting, in which near-term outcomes carry more motivational weight than abstract future risks, particularly among people who use substances. 32 These findings align with protection motivation theory, which posits that individuals are motivated to change behavior when they perceive a personally valued domain to be under threat. 33 When clinicians explicitly connect cannabis use to risks involving cognitive functioning, adolescents may be more likely to view these risks as personally relevant and adjust their behavior accordingly. 34 Furthermore, prospect theory suggests that individuals assign greater weight to potential losses than to equivalent gains, making loss-framed messages (eg, declines in memory or academic performance) particularly compelling. 35,36

Product safety also emerged as a major deterrent. Adolescents recognized that unregulated products may be contaminated or mislabeled; however, given their strong dislike of fear-based messaging, clinicians should be cautious about how this message is delivered. Clinicians should avoid using catastrophic warnings (eg, “you might die,” “you will get sick”) when informing youths. The challenge for clinicians is to maintain a clear distinction between educating and lecturing, which is best achieved through an inquiry-based approach. Adolescents interpret directive or prescriptive statements as authoritative rather than supportive, which can hinder open communication. Before providing information, clinicians should invite adolescents to share their own knowledge and experiences; for example, asking “Do you know anyone who has experienced contaminated products?” This opens a collaborative conversation, allows adolescents to reflect on risks within their own environment, and positions the clinician as a partner rather than an authority figure delivering warnings. Messaging that preserves autonomy while providing concrete, functional information may more effectively support reductions in cannabis use. 37 Consistent with this, adolescents expressed a preference for adults who listen first, ask open-ended questions, and invite their perspectives.

ADOLESCENTS PREFER HARM REDUCTION OVER ABSTINENCE-ONLY CANNABIS MESSAGING

Adolescents in this study expressed a preference for harm reduction guidance over abstinence-only messaging. Although this preference has been documented across youth-focused research, which shows that abstinence-only models may fail to engage adolescents, 38 the current evidence base is limited. These findings should be interpreted with caution and within the broader clinical and ethical framework of pediatric care, in which practitioners are responsible not only for engaging adolescents but also for promoting health, preventing harm, and when appropriate, recommending abstinence. 39,40 However, defaulting directly to harm reduction may eliminate a critical clinical conversation. Clinicians should also be aware that an adolescent’s preference for harm reduction over abstinence may itself reflect fear- or avoidance-based responding, a pattern that warrants careful clinical attention and should not be accepted at face value without further exploration. Clinicians should consider abstinence as the entry point, as the adolescent’s response itself provides valuable clinical information about dependence and functional impact. Where resistance to cessation is encountered, this serves as a natural inflection point at which harm reduction strategies may be introduced, creating an adaptive clinical structure responsive to the adolescent’s readiness and motivation to change.

Although concerns have been raised that harm reduction approaches may inadvertently normalize substance use among adolescents, available evidence does not consistently support this concern, particularly within structured clinical frameworks, although the evidence base remains limited and context-dependent. 38,41,42 The authors’ study contributes foundational data for future harm reduction–related investigations.

Overall, these findings highlight the importance of a balanced, developmentally informed approach that allows clinicians to acknowledge adolescents’ lived experiences and provide pragmatic, evidence-informed guidance, while clearly communicating the benefits of reducing and abstaining from cannabis use.

HOW ADOLESCENTS CONTEXTUALIZE RISK: IMPLICATIONS FOR CLINICIAN CREDIBILITY

Adolescents in this study interpreted cannabis risks through comparison with other substances, often perceiving cannabis as less dangerous due to social normalization. Although this reflects a common adolescent risk framing, clinicians should not validate or reinforce substance-to-substance comparisons, as this may inadvertently minimize cannabis-specific risks. Rather, awareness of how adolescents contextualize risk can help clinicians address these perceptions directly and accurately, redirecting the conversation toward cannabis-specific functional consequences that are personally relevant to the adolescent.

ADOLESCENT PREFERENCE FOR HYBRID CLINICIAN–PEER DIGITAL INFORMATION

Adolescents in this study recommended informational content that combines clinician expertise with peer experiences in accessible media, including short-form videos. An emerging body of research indicates that adolescents demonstrate a clear preference for digital, short-form informational media, particularly when such content is designed to be accessible, visually engaging, and relevant to their lived experiences. 43 These formats reduce stigma and increase treatment-seeking behaviors. 44 Existing studies tend to examine these components separately, with some focusing on clinician-generated educational videos or peer-driven narratives rather than hybrid models. This represents a clear gap in the literature and highlights an important direction for future research, particularly for developing and empirically testing co-delivered or co-narrated clinician–peer short-form digital interventions.

ETHICAL IMPLEMENTATION CONSIDERATIONS FOR PEER-BASED MESSAGING

Although adolescents in this study expressed a clear preference for messaging that incorporated peer experiences, translating this approach into clinical or educational practice requires careful consideration. Key implementation challenges include selection of appropriate peer narrators, ensuring that shared experiences are representative, developmentally appropriate, and do not inadvertently glamorize substance use. Additionally, the use of peer narratives raises important ethical and logistical considerations, including whether and how peer contributors are compensated, how their involvement is supervised, and how to ensure the accuracy and consistency of information when integrating lived experiences with clinical guidance.

Issues of confidentiality and privacy are particularly notable when involving adolescents as peer messengers, especially in clinical contexts where disclosure of substance use may carry stigma or legal implications. Safeguards such as anonymization, structured content development, and clinician oversight may be necessary to mitigate these risks. Furthermore, peer-informed messaging may be most appropriate when integrated into structured, clinician-guided formats (eg, curated digital content, co-developed message frameworks), rather than relying on unsupervised peer narratives.

Overall, listening directly to adolescents’ voices can equip clinicians with communication strategies that resonate with youth and may ultimately enhance the effectiveness of clinical conversations aimed at reducing or preventing cannabis use among adolescents.

Proposed Adolescent Cannabis Messaging Model. This model illustrates how message design, contextual factors, and messenger credibility interact to inform adolescents’ responses to cannabis-related clinical communication. Effective message design encompasses 3 domains: content that acknowledges adolescents’ perceived reasons for cannabis use, emphasizes near-term functional harms, and transitions to harm reduction when resistance to cessation is encountered; credibility derived from trusted peer and clinician voices, evidence-based information, and honest and transparent communication; and delivery that is nonjudgmental, autonomy-supportive, incorporates adolescent peer messaging, and uses accessible formats such as short-form video. These message design elements operate within a broader context shaped by adolescents’ existing beliefs about cannabis, social normalization, legal status, and psychosocial stress, all of which influence how youth interpret and respond to clinical guidance. When these elements are aligned, adolescents demonstrate increased perceived relevance, reduced defensiveness, greater openness to information, and higher motivation to reduce use. This model is grounded in qualitative data from adolescent cannabis users in outpatient clinical settings and is intended as a conceptual, youth-informed framework to guide clinical conversations rather than a validated intervention model.

STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS

There are several strengths and limitations to the present study. The relatively small sample size (N = 7) may be viewed as a major limitation; however, qualitative rigor is determined by depth, richness, and saturation of data rather than sample size alone. Given the focused study aim and relatively homogeneous clinical population (adolescents actively using cannabis in care settings), thematic saturation was achieved, although findings may not represent the full diversity of adolescent experiences. As such, findings should be understood as analytically saturated within this specific clinical context and are intended to inform future research directions and clinical thinking rather than to prescribe or support broad clinical adoption.

The sample was drawn from a single health system and limited to English-speaking adolescents engaged in clinical care; it may not represent the broader population of adolescent cannabis users. In particular, this sample may exclude perspective from adolescents who are uninsured, are less connected to health care systems, from rural or underserved communities, from non–English-speaking backgrounds, or who may have different experiences, risk perceptions, and informational needs related to cannabis use. Additionally, the study excluded adolescents with moderate-to-severe substance use disorders, psychotic disorders, and neurodevelopmental disabilities. As a result, the findings may not extend to higher-risk populations with more complex psychiatric or developmental profiles, who may have different patterns of cannabis use, perceptions of risk, and communication needs, and who may require more tailored or intensive communication strategies. However, these exclusion criteria were intentionally applied to reduce clinic heterogeneity and to better isolate adolescents’ perspectives on cannabis-related messaging within routine outpatient care contexts, allowing for deeper exploration of communication preferences among a more comparable group of participants.

Interview participants may have been subject to recall bias or social desirability bias, potentially providing responses they perceived as more acceptable to themselves or to interviewers. To mitigate this risk, interviews were conducted by study staff who were not clinicians and therefore less likely to evoke clinician-related response biases.

Importantly, the sample was intentionally drawn from adolescents receiving care, as the study aimed to generate preliminary practical insights for clinician–adolescent communication within health care settings. This focus prioritizes relevance to clinical practice over broad population representation and is best understood as informing communication approaches for adolescents in typical outpatient care settings rather than those with more severe or complex clinical presentations. A notable strength is that the study draws directly from the perspectives of adolescents actively using cannabis (ages 14–17), a population that is infrequently included in qualitative research on cannabis-related communication.

Taken together, these limitations call for future studies that include adolescents from nonclinical settings, diverse geographic regions, non-English–speaking populations, and those with co-occurring substance use disorders or severe mental illness to examine how cannabis-related communication strategies can be adapted across different social and clinical contexts. Larger-scale clinical studies are required before these preliminary findings can be recommended for broad clinical adoption. A planned follow-up quantitative study will build on the themes generated here to further examine the relationship between clinician communication strategies and treatment-seeking behavior among adolescents who use cannabis.

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence that clinicians should avoid fear-based or authoritative cannabis messaging. Clinicians should assess each patient holistically, including their readiness and motivation to change. Although the ideal goal remains complete cannabis cessation, clinicians should be ready to transition to harm reduction strategies to establish rapport and prevent treatment dropout, while supporting and motivating patients toward complete cessation. Given the preliminary nature of these findings, larger-scale studies are required before broader clinical adoption. Relatable, story-based examples also help build trust and keep adolescents engaged; clinicians should therefore assess ethical implications before bringing these insights into the conversation.
