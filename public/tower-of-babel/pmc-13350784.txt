Peptides as integrative modulators for clinical prognosis and targeted therapy in pancreatic cancer
Authors: Bhawana Yadav, Shiva Prasad Kollur, Amena Ali, Payas Arora, Aanika Gupta, P Saranraj, Vijay Jagdish Upadhye, Pallavi Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic cancer remains a formidable global health challenge, ranking as the twelfth most common malignancy yet claiming an outsized toll with dismal 5-year survival rates 13.3%, largely due to late-stage diagnosis and limited therapeutic options. This comprehensive review delves into the transformative potential of peptides as innovative biomarkers and prognostic indicators, addressing the critical gaps in early detection and personalized management of this aggressive disease. Drawing from cutting-edge research in molecular oncology, proteomics, and bioinformatics, this paper highlights key genetic drivers such as KRAS, TP53, CDKN2A, and SMAD4 mutations that fuel Pancreatic cancer progression and stromal desmoplasia. Diverse peptide sources, including tumor-derived neoantigens, stromal remodeling fragments, and immune-modulating signals and their release mechanisms via ectodomain shedding and exosomes, were explored. Emphasizing advanced discovery pipelines, from mass spectrometry-based proteomics to immunoassays and machine learning-driven validation, current work spotlights promising candidates like PF4, PRO-C11-511, and CXCL7, which enhance diagnostic accuracy (AUC up to 0.961) and predict outcomes when integrated with established markers like CA19-9. By overcoming limitations of current biomarkers, such as low specificity and stage-dependency. This review underscores peptides’ role in revolutionizing precision oncology, paving the way for non-invasive screening, targeted therapies, and improved survival in pancreatic cancer patients.Keywords: Early detection, CA19-9, Chemotherapy response, KRAS mutations, Machine learning, Molecular profiling, Pancreatic cancer, Peptide biomarkers, Tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Globally, pancreatic cancer is the twelfth most prevalent cancer in diagnosis frequency, while in the United States, it contributes roughly 3% to all cancer diagnoses and approximately 8% to all cancer-related fatalities [1]. The GLOBOCAN 2022 report published by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has recorded that India reported 14,586 new cases of pancreatic cancer, which was around 1.0 per cent of all new cases of cancer in the country. There is also a relatively small predominance of the disease toward men, with male cases being 8198 as compared to female cases being 6,388. The mortality rates also confirm the virulent character of this malignancy; in 2022 alone, 13,878 deaths caused by pancreatic cancer were registered in India, which corresponds to a high mortality-to-incidence ratio that is similar to the rest of the world. The age-standardised mortality rate (ASR) is still alarming, with a figure of 0.96 per 100,000, and it is a significant reason to introduce improved methods of early testing and specific treatment approaches in the Indian healthcare system [2].

This aggressive cancer allows just under 13.3% of patients to survive 5-years primarily because this malignancy commonly goes undetected until reaching more severe stages, often with metastasis to other organs [3]. A major obstacle in detecting pancreatic cancer early is that patients typically experience no symptoms in its initial stages, making it difficult to identify the disease before it progresses [4]. Symptomatic cases present with vague indicators like nausea, jaundice, weight loss, and abdominal pain, all common to many digestive disorders, complicating identification [5]. Dense desmoplasia, characterized by the presence of substantial proportion of extracellular matrix components like collagen and fibronectin, along with various cell types such as fibroblasts and immune cells is pathognomonic for ductal adenocarcinoma of the pancreas [6]. Pancreatic cancer is relatively uncommon, even a highly accurate biomarker test would generate a substantial number of false positives, leading to many individuals undergoing unnecessary and potentially invasive tests [7]. Although individuals with inherited genetic mutations or precursor lesions like cysts have a higher risk of developing this cancer, they represent only a fraction of all patients diagnosed with this disease. A major challenge lies in identifying those at high risk who do not have these identifiable risk factors- the so-called “sporadic” cases. The absence of convenient, accurate screening methods for asymptomatic high-risk patients creates major diagnostic obstacles for pancreatic cancer. Currently, there are no blood-based biomarkers that have been fully validated and are ready for widespread use to identify this disease in its early stages [8].

Cancer biomarkers are measurable indicators that can assess cancer risk, diagnose the disease, or provide insights into patient outcomes [9]. These biomarkers, typically in the form of specific molecules, can be found within tumor cells themselves or in bodily fluids like blood and urine. Their source might be the carcinoma directly or the adjacent normal cells responding to the pathology. Essentially, biomarkers are cellular components that become overexpressed due to the disease process [10]. Precision oncology employs comprehensive molecular characterization of cancer samples or circulating biomarkers to identify disease-specific signatures. This approach enhances diagnostic accuracy, outcome prediction, targeted treatment selection, drug sensitivity testing, and recurrence monitoring [11].

Pancreatic cancer pathophysiology and the search for biomarkers

Molecular basis of pancreatic cancer

Malignancies of the pancreas are genetically characterized by a few recurring mutations in genes partaking in suppression of tumors as well as in oncogenesis, which are linked to disease progression. Notable among these are four common mutations: KRAS, TP53, CDKN2A, and SMAD4 [12]. KRAS, a pivotal GTPase family oncogene produces a 21 kDa protein that toggles between functional states. The active conformation depends on GTP loading via GEFs, contrasting with the inactive GDP-bound form assisted by GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs). It bridges the receptor for growth factors on the cell membrane and the intracellular signaling pathways [12]. Mutations in Ras either hinder GAP binding or disrupt the GTPase activity of Ras. These alterations cause a significant shift in the equilibrium, making Ras proteins predominantly exist in the GTP-bound state, effectively disconnecting them from upstream receptor signals and driving continuous activation of downstream effector pathways. Ras mutations are commonly selected in cancers because they activate multiple effector pathways that promote malignancy development. Affected molecular circuits include mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) signaling, the PI3K-AKT-mTOR axis, Rho GTPases, and phospholipase C activity [13]. While G12, G13, and Q61 mutations commonly hyperactivate KRAS, K117 and A146 are increasingly recognized mutation sites. A study of 136 pancreatic adenocarcinoma patients found that 71 (52%) carried mutations in G12 and Q61 [14]. The presence of KRAS mutations correlates with dramatically decreased survival in pancreatic cancer, with differential effects across mutation classes. Circulating tumor DNA analysis reveals KRAS mutations with enhanced prognostic value compared to tissue testing. Interestingly, while predictive for Korean patients as demonstrated by a study, KRAS status lacked prognostic significance in European/North American cohorts [15]. The expression of CDK8 is stimulated by mutated KRAS, possibly through the regulation of HIF-1α. Experimental evidence demonstrates that the proteins CDK8 and genetically altered KRAS both function to stimulate cellular multiplication while simultaneously inhibiting programmed cell death under laboratory conditions. Furthermore, investigations indicate these molecular players facilitate the aggressive spread of pancreatic tumor cells by activating the Wnt signaling cascade involving β-catenin. This activation subsequently elevates expression levels of the transcription factors Snail1 and ZEB1, known to be pivotal regulators orchestrating the transition from epithelial to mesenchymal cellular states [16].

The 53 kDa phosphorylated protein, p53 is encoded by the tumor suppressor gene receiving the greatest research attention in oncology [12]. In up to 70% of Pancreatic Ductal Adenocarcinoma (PDAC), mutations in p53 can lead to oncogenic gain-of-function effects. These mutations usually appear after KRAS mutations and are closely associated with metastatic disease behaviours, with PDGFRβ induction identified as a key contributor to these phenotypes [17]. When amino acids are substituted, p53 DNA-binding ability is impaired, which eliminates its transcription modulation capacity. This results in the inability of mutant p53 to induce genes that regulate proliferative halting or self-destruction mechanisms, in response to DNA damage or cellular stress. Increasing evidence suggests that mutations in TP53 gene could contribute to the metastatic properties of PDAC, indicating a gain-of-function mechanism for mutant protein p53. One study revealed that the p53R175H variant binds to p73 and prevents it from inhibiting the expression of PDGFRβ. This leads to increased PDGFRβ levels, which activate an autocrine signaling loop that drives tumor cell migration, invasiveness, and metastasis in animal models. Other research has shown that the inactivation of p53 in different cancers also fosters metastasis by disabling the transcription factor’s role in inducing genes that block cell migration, epithelial-mesenchymal transition and stemness. Thus, p53 mutations in PDAC could drive metastasis through mechanisms that do not involve PDGFRβ [18]. The oncoprotein KRAS in its mutated form boosts CDK8 production, likely by affecting HIF-1α. Both components have been proven to enhance growth rates while preventing cell death in vitro. Moreover, their combined action promotes aggressive behaviour in pancreatic cancer cells via Wnt/β-catenin pathway stimulation. This, in turn, upregulates Snail1 and ZEB1, which are key drivers of epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition [19]. Mutant p53 modulates RNA processing in a sequence-dependent manner, resulting in enhanced KRAS function. It achieves this by promoting the expression of the splicing regulator hnRNPK, which causes the integration of exons with a high cytosine content in GTPase-activating proteins, activators of GTP hydrolysis (convert RAS-GTP to RAS-GDP), thereby inactivating RAS. This modification of GAP isoforms leads to their detachment from the cell membrane, ultimately increasing KRAS activity [20]. ARF6 and AMAP1 overexpression influence integrin and E-cadherin dynamics, promoting metastasis. KRAS boosts ARF6 and AMAP1 mRNA translation via eIF4A and eIF4E, while TP53 activates ARF6 through PDGF signaling. This pathway is crucial for PD-L1 recycling, promoting immune evasion and correlating with poor patient outcomes [12]. The CDKN2A genetic locus on chromosome 9 gives rise to a pair of tumor-inhibiting proteins: p14ARF and p16INK4, through alternative splicing. p16INK4 suppresses the activity of CDK4/CDK6-Cyclin D1 complexes, causing G1 phase arrest by preventing progression into S phase. p14ARF, on the other hand, inhibits MDM2, which regulates the p53 pathway, thereby regulating cellular division processes. This indicates that the two proteins encoded by CDKN2A regulate the cell cycle through two distinct mechanisms [12]. Representing a prime site for genetic changes in neoplasm, CDKN2A has a high mutation rate in sporadic pancreatic cancer, with 98% of cases showing inactivation. Homozygous deletions, allelic loss, and epigenetic silencing represent mechanisms disrupting this gene’s function. Half of patients (16/32), showed CDKN2A deletions when analyzed by FISH techniques, and these patients had a significantly shorter overall survival [21]. Inherited CDKN2A mutations are implicated in increased risk of pancreatic malignancies. Mutations in this gene, such as deletion, mutation, or promoter methylation causing loss of function, have been detected in PDAC, cancers of colon and lung and melanoma. CDKN2A mutations, together with those in KRAS, TP53, and SMAD4, are key factors in pancreatic cancer progression. Various germline mutations in CDKN2A found in PDAC patients include nonsense and missense mutations, frameshift deletions, insertions, and splicing variants, each with distinct effects. In an analysis of 638 PDAC patients from FPC families, 16 individuals were identified with pathogenic germline mutations in CDKN2A, including three frameshift-inducing deletions, twelve missense variants and a single nonsense mutation [22]. Improved prognosis is linked with increased CDKN2A expression. The correlation between increased CDKN2A expression and immune cell activation highlights its dual role in tumor immunity - functioning as both a prognostic biomarker and potential treatment target. Notably, individuals with CDKN2A mutations or large deletions demonstrated worse therapeutic outcomes and decreased survival rates [23].

SMAD4, also known as “deleted in pancreatic carcinoma 4” (DPC4), resides on chromosome 18q21.1. Functioning as a SMAD family transcription factor, this protein facilitates TGF-β signal transduction to the nucleus. TGF-β activation induces receptor binding and subsequent phosphorylation of SMAD2/3, promoting formation of a SMAD2/3/4 transcriptional regulatory complex. This pathway exerts tumor suppressor activity by inducing G1 phase cell cycle arrest and apoptosis. The gene, TIEG, modulated by TGF-/SMAD4, has been demonstrated to cause death in cell lines derived from pancreas [23]. The loss or inactivation of SMAD4 is common across various cancers, with pancreatic cancer having the highest incidence (50%), which leads to protein activation loss. In pancreatic cancer, approximately 30% of mutations in SMAD4 are due to homozygous deletions. Mutations or inactivation of SMAD4 could potentially serve as a prognostic marker. A study of 17 out of 90 pancreatic cancer patients revealed a strong correlation (P = 0.006) between SMAD4 deleterious mutations and survival outcomes. Modifications to the MH2 domain were detected in 12% of pancreatic cancer cases, with SMAD4 inactivation occurring due to homozygous deletions in 32% and 12% of the patients [20]. Patients retaining functional SMAD4 showed superior clinical outcomes (19.2 months median survival) compared to those with SMAD4 loss (14.7 months). In a genomic study of 22 PDAC models, 32% exhibited DPC4 homozygous deletions, always concurrent with KRAS, p53 and p16 aberrations, positioning DPC4 inactivation as a terminal molecular event. SMAD4 deficiency also links to immunosuppressive features, including reduced lymphocytic infiltration, suppressed T-cell markers, and attenuated cytotoxic T-cell activity [22]. The TGF-β signaling activates PI3K/Akt/mTOR, promoting protein production, motility, and cell invasion via the SMAD4-independent route. Increased mTORC2 activity induces mesenchymal changes and motility, but inhibiting mTORC2 reduces cancer spread by blocking EMT. However, tumors with active SMAD4-dependent TGF-β signaling display poorer differentiation and more EMT. The SMAD3/SMAD4 complex induces snail gene expression, repressing E-cadherin, while ZEB transcription factors further promote EMT. A recent study revealed that the TGF-β/SMAD4 pathway can activate N-cadherin to trigger invasive behaviour in pancreatic cells [24].

The microenvironment surrounding a tumor presents significant intricacy, displaying numerous components [25]. For instance, cells that perform functions of both inherent and acquired immune responses, alongside signaling proteins and diverse regulatory substances within the immune system, the surrounding structural framework, and connective tissue cells, all participate. This collective interaction results in the creation of a tumor that often suppresses the body’s defense mechanisms, contains low oxygen levels, and shows extensive structural alteration, which leads to resistance against diverse medical interventions, including chemical treatments, treatments aimed at particular cellular targets, and treatments designed to activate the immune system [26]. The microenvironment of pancreatic tumor comprises both malignant and stromal cells, in addition to ECM components. Cancer progression is supported by various stromal elements such as tumor-infiltrating macrophages, immunosuppressive Tregs, activated pancreatic stellate cells, and MDSCs. These cells release matrix metalloproteinases, TGFβ, components of the ECM, along with growth factors, maintaining the pancreatic cancer microenvironment [27].

Cancer of the pancreas is defined by two key microenvironmental features: significant desmoplasia and extensive immunosuppression. Desmoplasia, characterized by substantial fibrosis at the primary tumor location, is pathologically evident in PDAC. Clinically, this presents as increased ECM protein levels and a large-scale conversion of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts. Desmoplasia is linked to a negative prognosis, as it fosters pancreatic cancer advancement and reduces chemotherapy effectiveness [27]. Pancreatic cancer often avoids immune detection through several suppressive pathways. Reduced MHC-I presentation hinders T-cell activation, and checkpoint molecules on CD8 + T cells lead to their functional decline, despite neoantigen presence [28]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), a significant immune cell component, promote angiogenesis and suppress immunity. Cytokines recruit TAMs, which release growth factors and IL-10, thus blocking dendritic cell-driven anti-tumor responses and inhibiting CD8+ TILs [29].

Chronic inflammation drives PDAC tumorigenesis via inflammatory mediators that remodel the TME and induce desmoplastic stroma. Cancer-associated fibroblasts secrete these mediators, growth factors, and enzymes, altering stroma, immunity, and metabolism. Cytokines, chemokines, and ROS are key mediators. Cytokines, with pleiotropic functions, exhibit context-dependent pro- or anti-inflammatory effects and are elevated in PDAC. Elevated levels of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α create a tumor-promoting microenvironment in pancreatic cancer. IL-6-STAT3 enhances invasion, and IL-1 activates oncogenic pathways. Mutant KRAS/MYC suppress interferon, enabling NK cell evasion [30]. Using a conditional Cre-loxP approach, adult mice were engineered with pancreatic-specific expression of oncogenic KRAS (G12V). While the Ras mutation alone didn’t initiate PanIN development, co-occurring chronic pancreatitis served as a critical promoter of premalignant lesions and invasive adenocarcinoma, demonstrating that inflammation within a mature pancreas can override the body’s natural defenses against carcinogenesis [31].

In a healthy pancreas, pancreatic stellate cells, remain inactive. However, in pathological states, particularly during inflammation or cancer, these cells activate. Upon activation, these cells secrete substantial amounts of IL-6, driving pancreatic cancer cell proliferation via Nrf2-dependent metabolic alterations. Additionally, the IL-6/JAK2/STAT3 signaling axis facilitates pancreatic tumor development and advancement. M2 macrophages drive PDAC advancement. They raise TLR4 expression, leading to IL-10 elevation, which induces EMT and increases invasive capacity. p65, a NF-κB component, is present in all mammalian cells. Its persistent activation links to cancerous cell transformation. Elevated NF-κB activity facilitates aggressive tumor behavior in pancreatic neoplasms, enhancing tumor growth and spread in malignancies of pancreas [32]. TNFR1 is implicated in inflammatory signaling via the induction of NF-κB, JNK, and p38-MAPK. Upon ligand binding, TNFR1 recruits’ death-inducing signaling complexes that trigger apoptosis via both Bcl-2 family-mediated mitochondrial permeabilization and oxidative stress pathways. IL-8 can function as VEGF, signal through VEGFR2, and drive angiogenesis [33]. IL-1-α induces upregulated expression of MMP1 and MMP3, concurrent with downregulated expression of MMP2, TIMP2, and TIMP3. Given TIMP3 inhibitory role on MMP1 and MMP3, its reduced expression potentiates their proteolytic activity, thereby remodeling the tumor microenvironment. Experimental models demonstrate that forced IL-1α expression confers pro-metastatic and pro-invasive properties to pancreatic carcinoma cells. IL-4 induces the expression of anti-apoptotic factors, contributing to enhanced cell survival, and reduces cell adhesion molecule expression, which facilitates invasiveness. STAT3 activation via IL-4/type II receptor interactions in parenchymal cells drives oncogenic transformation by regulating networks involved in cell survival, death resistance, motility, and microenvironmental immune modulation. COX-2, an enzyme central to inflammation, is found to have elevated expression in pancreatic cancer. High COX-2 levels indicate poor survival [34].

Biological processes leading to the release of peptides

Ectodomain shedding involves the proteolytic cleavage and release of a membrane protein’s extracellular domain, which often modulates the protein’s biological activity. The cleavage mechanism operates dually—either through plasma membrane-localized shedding that liberates ectodomains directly, or via intra-organellar processing in secretory pathway compartments that are topologically extracellular. Sheddases, a class of proteases, mediate this cleavage near the transmembrane domain, generating a soluble ectodomain and a membrane-bound fragment. Certain sheddases, termed secretases, are specifically involved in the release of secreted ectodomains [35]. For example, ADAM-17 has been identified as the sheddase that detaches the soluble, active TNF-α from the cell membrane, contributing to cachexia, a significant issue in PDAC patients. Additionally, ADAM-17 functions as a major sheddase for numerous cell surface molecules, most notably EGFR ligands [36]. A range of proteins undergo shedding, including those involved in cell adhesion, precursors and receptors for growth factors and cytokines, enzymes located on the cell surface, and numerous others. It’s been proposed that every membrane protein could experience some degree of shedding. Growth factor signaling, crucial for cancer and various diseases, is influenced by ectodomain shedding. The breakdown of cell-surface adhesion molecules, via proteolysis, shows growing links to tumor advancement and spread. Ectodomain levels in plasma can serve as prognostic tools [37].

Peptide release can also occur via exosomes, a process documented in the context of Alzheimer’s disease. Extracellular plaque formation needs Aβ peptide export. A point of interest is the mechanism by which these internally made, non-polar peptides are transported outward. It’s observed that β-cleavage takes place in particular endosomes, and a part of Aβ is present in multivesicular bodies. These bodies, when merged with the cell’s outer layer, expel the vesicles they hold as exosomes into the surrounding area [38].

Specifically, exosomes originating from tumors contain tumor antigens that play a role in dendritic cell activation, leading to immune recognition and the induction of cytotoxic effects against tumors. Exosomes from apoptotic cells display the common exosomal marker CD63, along with LAMP1 and HSP70, induced by cellular stress associated with apoptosis. Biological fluids, like urine, breast milk, and plasma, among others, all contain exosomes [39]. Isolation of exosomes from these, aids in finding biomolecules related to disease, which include proteins and nucleic acids. Research has proven that exosomes originating from aged cells carry molecules that are biologically active. Target cell growth and migration during coculture may be affected by these exosomes. Accordingly, exosomes coming from senescent cells could prove to be biomarkers for vascular diseases tied to aging [40].

Peptides as emerging biomarkers

Peptide biomarkers have been identified through diverse techniques like phage display, combinatorial chemistry, and peptidomic profiling. Progress in molecular profiling technologies has facilitated identification of novel biomarkers across diverse specimen types. Through these studies, researchers have gained essential biological insights that substantiate using peptide markers for neoplasm diagnosis and therapeutic approaches [41]. Tumor formation frequently initiates via chromosomal translocations that lead to chimeric fusion proteins. These distinctive hybrid peptides, arising from tumor-specific gene fusions and exclusive to certain malignancies, are being explored as diagnostic markers (Table 1). Gene fusion-derived proteins unique to certain neoplasms exhibit high specificity and a qualitative, all-or-none nature (present or absent). These characteristics make these molecules appealing candidates for cancer detection [42].

Peptides, when bound by stable blood proteins like albumin, gain protection from elimination. This binding protects them from being cleared, enhancing their suitability as biomarkers [47]. Techniques like mass spectrometry can detect peptides with high sensitivity, even at low concentrations [47]. Some peptides are derived from tissue-specific proteins, meaning their presence or altered levels can indicate pathological changes in particular tissues or organs. Changes in the peptide profile of biological fluids can reflect disease-specific alterations in protein processing or degradation, providing valuable diagnostic information [48].

Peptides are generated by action of proteinases. Tissue processes, including cellular interactions, immune presentation, and cell death, produce peptides, which enter the bloodstream (Fig. 1). The majority of cancer-specific peptide fragments in patient serum arise from enzymatic breakdown at known sites after blood collection. These low molecular weight biomarkers aren’t directly from diseased tissue. Instead, they result from proteinase-driven cleavage during coagulation and complement activation. These fragments, derived from blood proteins, act as substrates for disease-specific proteinases originating from tumors or their surroundings. The specific substrates targeted are clotting cascade by-products. The detected cancer-specific signatures in the serum peptidome reflect enzyme activity of tumor cells, acting as indirect markers for cancer detection and classification [47]. The adaptive immune system’s class I MHC-CD8+ T-cell surveillance involves peptide presentation on cell surfaces. Cellular proteins, especially those containing translational errors, are promptly cleaved by proteasomes into small 8–11 residue peptides. ER-targeted transport via TAP permits peptide-MHC class I assembly and eventual plasma membrane localization. The polymorphic genes encoding class I heavy chains (HLA-A, -B, -C) lead to diverse peptide presentation. Investigating plasma sHLA-associated peptide repertoires enables better comprehension of illness development, offering advantages over traditional tissue proteomics. Elevated sHLAs during inflammation help stabilize otherwise unstable peptides, which are prone to degradation [49].

C-peptide and pancreatic polypeptide (PP) are released via classical vesicle-mediated exocytosis, with C-peptide co-secreted alongside insulin and entering systemic circulation. Neurotensin (NT) and gastrin-releasing peptide (GRP) are secreted through vesicular pathways and act in a paracrine manner on receptor-positive neighboring tumor cells to promote proliferation and growth. Growth/Differentiation Factor 15 (GDF15) utilizes both conventional and unconventional mechanisms, reflecting its complex role in tumor progression. Existing biomarkers and their limitations

In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), CA19-9 serves as the primary biomarker for evaluating therapeutic response [50]. Derived from the SW1116 colorectal carcinoma model (1979) and targeted by antibody 1116-NS-19-9, CA19-9 emerged as a mucin-associated glycoprotein detectable in pancreatic and colorectal cancer patients (1981). Its glycosylated protein structure aligns with secretory mucins, and while constitutively expressed in gastrointestinal and reproductive tissues, aberrant levels occur in cancer and inflammatory states due to metabolic shifts [51]. Three main steps describe CA19-9 biosynthesis. Precursor chains undergo galactosylation via B3GALTs as the primary step. Second, S3TOs add a sialic residue. Finally, FUT3 adds fucose. These enzymes, therefore, directly affect CA19-9 synthesis. However, its creation is complex, involving multiple glycosylation enzymes directly and indirectly. For instance, FUT2 and α 2,6-sialyltransferases can reduce CA19-9 synthesis indirectly. Substrate amounts, enzyme actions, and locations also impact CA19-9 production. The tumor marker CA19-9 owes its diagnostic value to the distinctive glycosylation anomalies seen in pancreatic malignancies [52]. Three mouse xenograft models of pancreatic cancer were employed to investigate CA19-9 dynamics during L19-IL-2 treatment. The immunocytokine treatment group exhibited substantial CA19-9 reduction, contrasting with rising levels in untreated animals and no change in single-agent groups. These findings support CA19-9 as both a progression marker and treatment response indicator [53].

CA19-9 is found within developing tissues and typical gastrointestinal epithelial cells. Healthy people might display elevated CA19-9 after drinking black tea, or with significant tea intake. Multiple benign conditions can elevate CA19-9 levels, ranging from hepatobiliary diseases (cholestasis, hepatitis), pancreatic inflammation, pulmonary disorders, endocrine abnormalities, to gynecological and gastrointestinal pathologies. The marker’s lack of cancer-type specificity is evidenced by its elevation in diverse malignancies including gastrointestinal, pulmonary, mammary, and hepatobiliary tumors, alongside pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasia [52]. Its diagnostic utility in pancreatic cancer exhibits stage-dependence, with only 50% sensitivity for sub-3 cm lesions. Studies utilizing the conventional 37 kU/L cutoff reported aggregate sensitivity of 81% and specificity of 90% for PDAC identification, with higher thresholds improving specificity at sensitivity’s expense [51].

Non-coding RNA, called miRNAs, regulate gene expression after transcription. Profiling miRNA expression is becoming important for early cancer detection [54]. In order to compare miRNA expression, pancreatic cancer samples, benign tissues, normal pancreas, pancreatitis tissues, and cell lines were used. Real-time PCR profiling identified alterations in levels of miRNA analysis revealed differential expression patterns of various regulatory microRNAs in neoplastic versus normal tissue [55]. Certain cancer-enriched miRNAs were specifically identified within malignant cell populations [55]. These circulating microRNAs represent promising non-invasive biomarkers for pancreatic cancer applications such as early detection, molecular subtyping, and predicting therapeutic response [56]. MIC-1, a TGF-β superfamily member, is overexpressed in diverse tumor types [57]. Serum MIC-1 levels showed strong diagnostic performance. Pooled sensitivity reached 86.7%, and specificity was 85.9%. False-positive rates were 14.1%. Positive and negative likelihood ratios were 6.17 and 0.15, respectively. The diagnostic odds ratio of 39.92 indicated high efficacy for MIC-1 as a diagnostic biomarker [58]. Serum samples from both pancreatic adenocarcinoma patients and healthy volunteers were analyzed for ADH, MIC-1, and CA19-9 content using ELISA. The cancer patient cohort displayed significantly greater concentrations for each of these biological markers (p-value less than 0.001). MIC-1 exhibited the highest sensitivity (90%) at early stages [59]. CEA, a glycoprotein typically produced in small amounts after birth, as it is primarily a fetal marker, has been implicated in several malignancies. A number of published studies have focused on determining CEA usefulness for detecting pancreatic malignancies [60]. Use of a CEA-based biomarker panel for pancreatic cancer diagnosis showed substantially enhanced capability compared to single-marker tests. Quantitative analysis revealed the following characteristics: true positive identification rate of 45% (confidence range 41–50%), true negative determination of 89% (86–91% confidence interval), and probability ratios of 5.39 (3.16–9.18) for positive tests versus 0.55 (0.41–0.72) for negative results. The overall discriminative power was excellent, with a receiver operating characteristic score of 0.90 and a diagnostic effectiveness measure of 0.84. Elevated CEA levels also correlated significantly with lower overall survival [61]. A carbohydrate antigen with sialic acid, CA242, has been found to correlate highly with CA19-9 in individuals diagnosed with malignancies of the pancreas [60]. The pancreatic cancer cohort demonstrated substantially elevated detection rates for CA242, CA199, CA125, and CEA biomarkers both pre- and post-intervention when compared to healthy controls. Notably, individuals with larger neoplasms (> 4 cm diameter) or advanced disease (Stage III/IV) presented with markedly increased circulating levels of these markers relative to patients with smaller tumors (≤ 4 cm) or earlier stage (I/II) malignancies [62]. By combining CEA and CA242 detection, another research notably increased diagnostic specificity to 92%. Therefore, the use of multiple indicators alongside CA242 provides better cancer prediction [63].

Types of peptides as potential biomarkers

Tumour-derived peptides

Peptide-based biomarkers have gathered massive global attention for the development of efficient early diagnostic tools and personalized vaccine therapy in pancreatic carcinoma. Among the peptide biomarkers, tumour-derived peptides, which emerge during tumorigenesis, have showcased considerable potential owing to their high specificity and immunogenicity towards immunotherapeutic applications.

The primary source of these peptides is neoantigens or tumour specific antigens which are the novel variants of membrane bound proteins expressed specifically on tumour cells thereby, exhibiting elevated immunogenic potential and suitable targets for T cell-based cancer immunotherapy [64–66]. These are derived either from viral manifestation including Epstein Barr virus inducing Burkitt’s lymphoma, nasopharyngeal cancer and gastric cancer, Human Papilloma virus inducing oropharyngeal cancer, anal cancer and cervical cancer and Merkel Cell polyomavirus inducing Merkel cell carcinoma [67–69] or from genetic mutations including frameshift mutations, single nucleotide mutation, gene fusion and structural alterations or from pots translational modifications and alternate splicing mechanisms [65, 66]. Neoantigens in 80–85% of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cases predominantly emerge from the missense mutations thriving in the exon 12 of the KRAS oncogene, resulting in the production of G12R, G12D and G12V variants [70]. The KRAS protein functions as a membrane-bound GTPase essential for modulating MAPK/ERK and PAM signalling pathways involved in apoptosis, cell proliferation and differentiation [71, 72]. The mutation frequency analysis performed by utilising the integrated data from four pancreatic cancer cohorts in the cBioPortal database revealed the following 17%, 32.5% and 39.2% mutation prevalence rates of KRAS G12R, G12V and G12D variants, respectively [73–78]. Additionally, higher occurrence of the G12D mutation has been associated with reduced patient survival rates, and when accompanied by the downregulation of p53 protein, it substantially accelerates the progression of metastasis, thereby acting as a potent biomarker of pancreatic cancer [79–82]. Mutated KRAS protein establishes an immunosuppressive environment by stimulating the release of those chemokines and cytokine molecules which trigger immortalization of cells and immune evasion by preventing the migration of natural killer cells, cytotoxic and helper T lymphocytes [83]. Moreover, G12D and G12V variants are further associated with the stimulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells within the tumours to evade immune response through the elevated expression of granulocyte macrophage colony -stimulating factor and MIP-2β [84]. Approximately 1.2–2.0% pancreatic cancer incidences worldwide demonstrate the prevalence of neoantigens arising from gene fusion events, with nearly 98% cases possessing wild-type KRAS allele lacking oncogenic mutations [85]. The gene fusion events were accompanied by co-occurring mutations within the principal tumour suppressor genes encompassing SMAD4, CDKN2A, CDKN2B and TP53. Among the fusion genes, BRAF, FGFR2, RET and ALK were primarily involved, accounting for 33.7%, 17.4%, 12.7% and 10.1% cases, respectively, with ALK::EML4 and BRAF::SND1 representing the most observed fusion combination. In the ALK::EML4 gene fusion event, 20th to 29th exons of the ALK gene residing at 2p21 position fuse with 1 st to 6th exons of the EML4 gene residing at 2p23 position, while in the BRAF::SND1 gene fusion, 1 st to 10th exons of SND1 residing at 7q32 position fuse with 9th to 18th exons of BRAF residing at 7q34 position. Owing to the elevated frequency of ALK::EML4 gene fusion, especially in pancreatic carcinoma and non-small cell lung carcinoma, it has been assessed as a potent therapeutic target [86]. Additionally, a clinical study encompassing 59 patients administered with personalized fusion gene-directed therapies targeting fusion gene products including ALK, BRAF, FGFR2, MET, RET, NRG1, NTRK1/2/3, ROS1 and RAF1 genes demonstrated therapeutic efficacy predominantly in individuals with chimeric protein emerging from fusion events involving FGFR2, NTRK and RET [85]. Likewise, a remarkable clinical case fusion gene targeted therapy involved a 68-year-old patient diagnosed with late-stage pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma possessing the FGFR2::TACC2 gene fusion exhibited measurable improvements to erdafitinib, an inhibitor of FGFR2, within 2 months of administration [87]. Pancreatic cancers often exhibit gene fusion at a rate of 0.3% and 0.5% in NTRK and NRG1 genes, respectively [88, 89]. An FDA-approved drug, Larotrectinib, targeting chimeric proteins generated by NTRK genes, received approval in 2018, exhibiting a response rate of 75% in clinical trials [90].

Tumor-derived peptides can also originate from tumor-associated proteins, which are encoded by non-mutated genes but exhibit disproportionately elevated levels of expression in neoplastic cells [91]. Such peptides are responsible for inducing immune tolerance either from central thymic deletion or peripheral anergy mechanisms or both; therefore, immunotherapeutic approaches directed against them are often associated with autoimmune adverse effects [92, 93]. Ca125 is one of the promising peptide biomarkers that has been utilised for tracking metastatic progression and tumour growth in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, as it is extensively expressed in 50–70% of the cases [94–96]. Ca125 emerges after undergoing cleavage from a transmembrane glycoprotein, Mucin 16 (MUC16); therefore, several clinical studies have been conducted to demonstrate the linkage between the advancement of pancreatic metastasis and expression of MUC16/Ca125 [96, 97]. Furthermore, certain reports suggested that upregulation of MUC16/Ca125 signified a marked reduction in life expectancy of diseased individuals by triggering the invasion of tumorous cells [98, 99]. Another tumor- associated antigen, which can be beneficial for therapy and prognosis in pancreatic cancer due to its substantial expression in over 80% cases, is mesothelin (MSLN) [100–102]. MSLN, earlier referred to as cell-surface antigen K1 (CAK1), is a 71 kDa glycoprotein that mediates the differentiation of tumour cells [103]. MSLN usually undergoes cleavage to produce a 41 kDa membrane-associated protein and release a 32 kDa soluble protein megakaryocyte potentiating factor (MPF) within the blood plasma [104, 105]. Furthermore, studies revealed that overexpression of MSLN in tumorous cells significantly upregulated IL-6 to enhance hasten cell proliferation and confer resilience to apoptosis by stimulating Akt/PI3K/NF-κB signalling pathway [101, 106, 107]. Moreover, several clinical studies have reported highly disproportionate expression of MSLN between control pancreatic ductal epithelial cells and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells, thereby rendering MSLN a potential peptide biomarker [108–110]. Owing to its upregulation in a majority of 30–60% of pancreatic cancer cases, Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), a heavy glycoprotein, emerges as a viable tumor-associated antigen that can be exploited as a robust biomarker [111, 61]. A retrospective cohort analysis revealed that diseased individuals with extensively elevated expression of CEA exhibited significantly reduced survival rates [112]. Moreover, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that the incorporation of protein biomarkers encompassing CA125, CA19-9 and CA50 alongside CEA significantly enhanced the sensitivity of pancreatic cancer diagnosis, thereby enabling more effective differentiation between malignant cases and healthy subjects [113–115]. MUC1/CA15-3, a transmembrane glycoprotein widely exploited for the early diagnosis of pancreatic cancer due to its overexpression within the nucleus and mitochondria of tumorous cells [116–118]. The upregulation of MUC1 impairs the adhesive interactions prevailing between the tumorous cells and extracellular matrix, thereby contributing towards their dissociation and initiating metastasis [119]. Furthermore, the elevated concentration of MUC1 protein is frequently associated with overstimulation of PIK3/Akt, Wnt and MAPK signal transduction pathways which contributes towards immortalization of mutated cells [130]. Due to the high prognostic value of MUC1, several immunotherapies and vaccines have been developed to effectively reduce metastatic progression in MUC1-positive pancreatic cancer, including TAB004, Pemtumomab and PankoMab [120–123]. Stathmin 1/STMN1 involved in the destabilization of microtubules, is referred to as oncoprotein 18 due to its recurrent upregulation in diverse types of carcinomas encompassing pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, ovarian cancer, colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma [124–128]. Research has established that overexpression of STMN1 is associated with metastatic progression and undifferentiation in intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [124, 129, 130]. Therefore, STMN1 can be explored as a potential peptide biomarker to track pancreatic cancer progression.

Certain proteins modulating epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) are often responsible for the origination of tumor-associated peptides during intercellular matrix remodelling [131–133]. EMT is a pivotal pathway that facilitates loss of polarity and adhesive potential marked by the denaturation of gap junctions, bridging granules and tight junctions in epithelial cells, enabling them to gain migratory traits of mesenchymal cells, thereby driving cancer metastasis and contributing towards drug resistance, especially in pancreatic ductal carcinoma [134, 135]. The major drivers of EMT include the expression of ZEB1/2, Prrx1 and Snail1/2 transcription factors, stimulation of WNT, hypoxia and TGF-β signal transduction pathways and the upregulation of miR-103/107 and miR-9 miRNAs [136]. Ample evidence in the scientific literature supports that MUC1 overexpression is strongly associated with upregulation of STAT3, Vimentin, Slug and Snail, which hastens the progression of EMT [137–139]. Likewise, Claudins (CLDN), membrane-associated proteins constituting an integral component of tight junctions, drive pancreatic metastatic progression [140]. CLDN 18 exhibits high levels of interaction with the EGFR signal transduction pathway to facilitate EMT and migration and proliferation tumors cells, and the variant CLDN 18.2 is typically overexpressed in pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia, intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasia and mucinous cystic neoplasia during pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [141–143]. Therefore, the scientific evidence suggests the extensive exploitation of these proteins to study the metastatic progression in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma.

Peptides related to stromal response

The predominant characteristic of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma is the substantial accumulation of extracellular matrix proteins, including adapter and structural peptides, pancreatic stellate cells, immune cells and cancer-associated fibroblasts, which facilitate a fibrotic reaction responsible for triggering desmoplasia in tumorous cells [144, 145]. During desmoplasia, cancerous cells undergo alterations or proteolytic cleavage of their extracellular matrix proteins to synthesize structural, signalling or regulatory peptides for the tumor microenvironment, which can also be utilised as potentially robust biomarkers [146]. The primary proteins providing structural strength to the extracellular matrix include type I, III and IV collagen, out of which type IV has been widely exploited as a biomarker in postoperative patients [147, 148]. Likewise, disproportionately high expression of type I collagen had been linked with a significantly reduced overall survival duration of 6.4 months among the patients [149]. Overexpression of collagen proteins actively contributes towards the metastatic progression and proliferation of tumorous cells, which further enhances the survival of these tumours [150]. Additionally, a cohort analysis revealed that levels of N-terminal propeptide of type III collagen (PRO-C3) substantially elevated with the advancement of pancreatic cancer, thereby establishing its application in diagnosing the disease earlier by differentiating between benign and malignant stages as it hastens the growth of tumorous cells and provides chemoresistance [151]. Scientific evidence suggests that PRO-C3 also induces dormancy in tumours [152]. Furthermore, there have been diverse studies indicating that type I, III and IV collagen proteins have a substantial increase in their levels, especially in benign tumours and metastatic lesions, and subsequently lowering the survival and enhancing tumorigenesis in patients [153–156]. Thereby, exhibiting the underlying application in the timely prognosis of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. Another extracellular matrix protein that represents a potential biomarker is fibronectin (FN1) due to its pivotal role as a structural scaffold that mediates interactions between cell and matrix [157]. Moreover, contributes towards the regulation of tissue homeostasis, wound repair and developmental mechanisms. It has been reported that interactions among membrane-associated integrin proteins and FN1 drive vital processes to pancreatic tumours encompassing angiogenesis, therapeutic resistance, cell survival, metastasis and cell invasion [158]. Research conducted on 34 patients revealed a significant reduction in survival that corresponded to extensively high FN1 expression, thereby indicating its diagnostic ability [159]. Likewise, retrospective studies involving 138 and 95 subjects, respectively, demonstrated disproportionately elevated expressions of FN1 within the tumour microenvironment of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [160, 161].

In addition to extracellular matrix, the inflammation associated with desmoplastic response also serves as a rich repository of secretory biomarker proteins for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. Due to the frequent co-occurrence of chronic pancreatitis in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, the tumorous tissues get heavily infiltered with stromal and immune cellar components such as tumour-associated macrophages, pancreatic stellate cells, regulatory T cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts and myeloid-derived suppressor cells [162–164]. These cells produce a diverse array of chemokine molecules encompassing vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) and interleukin 1β (IL-1β), IL-6, IL-10, IL-17 and IL-18, which significantly modulate the tumour microenvironment [165, 166]. TGF-β has been recognised as the key determinant in the oncogenic progression of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, where it exhibits dual functions of either suppressing or promoting tumour based on the advancement of the disease and the tumour microenvironment [167]. It is known to facilitate EMT by upregulating the production of Snail and Zeb1/2, and tumorigenesis as the disease worsens, whereas during the initial phase of the disease, it mediates apoptosis to modulate the cell cycle, and enhances the development of stroma by stimulating pancreatic stellate cells [168, 169]. A previous investigation demonstrated that marked upregulation of TGF-β1 expression corresponded with poor survival outcomes and independently predicted the prognosis as confirmed by multivariate analysis [170]. Similarly, another research revealed the expression of TGF-β1 as a robust and reliable predictor of metastasis by effectively differentiating among diverse stages of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [171]. Additionally, several S100 proteins, a family of calcium-binding proteins, have been identified for their potential role in driving malignancy in pancreatic cancer [172–176]. For instance, disproportionately elevated levels of S100A2 were associated with pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia and invasive ductal carcinoma [177]. Moreover, the involvement in adenocarcinoma differentiation, this protein has been rendered as a beneficial biomarker of metastatic progression in pancreatic carcinoma [177, 178]. Likewise, S100A2 is significantly upregulated in metastatic tumours instead of benign ones, which has led to its identification as an indicator of tumour metastasis and is linked with high mortality rates among pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma patients [179]. Similarly, upregulation of S100A6 protein had been determined in pancreatic carcinoma in numerous scientific literature [180–183]. This protein is specifically upregulated during carcinogenesis and can be exploited for the detection of elevated risk lesions [184]. The application of S100A6 in differentiating invasive ductal carcinoma, intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm and chronic pancreatitis has been suggested due to its variable expression among all [180, 184]. Furthermore, this protein is known to drive EMT, metastasis and cell proliferation in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [185]. A proteomic analysis of over 400 proteins, revealed that S100A8 and S100A9, primarily secreted by myeloid cells, are predominant proteins that facilitate the development of pancreatic tumour initiating cells in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, hence can be used in the early diagnosis [186]. The elevated release of S100A11 in the tumour microenvironment of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma facilitates the nearby fibroblasts via RAGE/TPL2/COX2 signalling cascade to mediate the production of prostaglandin E2 to enhance the metastatic progression [187]. Additionally, S100A10 also contributes towards the pathogenesis of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma as its significant upregulation triggers the development of tumorous cells in in-vivo and stimulates metastatic progression via cell adhesion, proliferation and migration by the expression of JNK/LAMB3-LAMC2 pathway in in-vitro scenario [188].

Peptides as markers of treatment response and resistance

Chemotherapeutic treatment has always occupied the central stage for treating pancreatic carcinoma, especially pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, because of its relatively higher resistance to such therapies due to its aggressive nature. The efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents is often reduced due to the prevalence of dense fibrotic stroma of tumorous cells, which hampers the delivery of drugs [189–191]. The predominantly utilised therapies involve adjuvant chemotherapy, which is regarded as the treatment administered immediately following the surgery to substantially deplete remaining tumorous cells to lower the disease recurrence [192], and neoadjuvant chemotherapy, which is regarded as the treatment provided prior to surgery to significantly reduce the size of tumours [193]. At present, the widely adopted guidelines to preoperatively treat pancreatic carcinoma highlight the administration of gemcitabine, FOLFIRINOX, mFOLFIRINOX, and gemcitabine in combination with nanoparticle albumin-bound (nab)- paclitaxel [193]. Gemcitabine had been actively utilised in the first-line monotherapy regimen from 1997 to 2011 for treating the advanced stage of pancreatic carcinoma [194]. But following the year 2011, several research studies exhibited improved overall survival outcomes of 11.1 months accompanied with increased response rate in patients administered with FOLFIRINOX [195]. Eventually, another study underscored the similar enhancement in the survival outcomes with a median overall survival equivalent to 8.5 months among the patients treated with gemcitabine in combination with nab-paclitaxel [196, 197]. It has been established that during the administration of these chemotherapeutic agents, substantial alterations occur in the expression levels of various proteins, which can be utilised to monitor treatment response and potential adaptive drug resistance in pancreatic carcinoma (Fig. 2).

Molecular mechanisms by which biomarker overexpression affects the efficacy of common chemotherapeutic agents. The figure illustrates both response biomarkers and resistance biomarkers within a cancerous cell. Overexpression of enzymes and transporters like hENT1 and CES2 enhances drug uptake for Gemcitabine and Irinotecan (FOLFIRINOX), correlating with improved sensitivity and survival. Conversely, overexpression of enzymes like RRM1, DPD, and TS results in the inactivation or breakdown of drugs like 5-Fluorouracil and Gemcitabine, leading to reduced efficacy, drug resistance, and poor patient prognosis

For instance, human equilibrative nucleoside transporter 1 (hENT1) characterised as a protein that is actively involved in the transmembrane transport of nucleoside-based therapeutic agents, is also known to play a pivotal role in gemcitabine uptake [198, 199]. Consequently, hENT1 is extensively studied as a marker peptide for evaluating the response to gemcitabine in in-vivo and in-vitro pancreatic carcinoma models. Furthermore, there have been diverse clinical investigations involving pancreatic carcinoma afflicted patients administered with gemcitabine, which revealed disproportionately higher levels of hENT1 were associated with significantly improved overall survival rates, thereby establishing the effectiveness of gemcitabine [200–202]. Likewise, findings from the retrospective analysis of phase Ⅲ trials RTOG-9704 and ESPAC-1/3 indicated that hENT1 can be exploited as potent biomarker to study the effectiveness of gemcitabine adjuvant chemotherapy, as the individual with significantly higher levels of hENT1 depicted prolonged survival in contrast with those exhibiting lower levels [202, 203]. Expression levels of microtubule associated protein-2 (MAP2) which predominantly contribute towards the stabilization of microtubules, and neuronal morphological development and plasticity, had been explored as a potential biomarker for studying resistance to gemcitabine in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [204]. A comprehensive proteomic analysis revealed substantial upregulation and elevated phosphorylation of MAP2 in gemcitabine resistant PANC1 cells [205]. Additionally, elevated MAP2 expression was clinically associated with reduced overall survival in patients administered with gemcitabine as established from two cohorts of patients. Ribonucleotide reductase catalytic subunit M1 (RRM1), pivotal in the synthesis and repair of DNA, has been closely identified as an agent actively contributing towards the development of resistance to gemcitabine in pancreatic carcinoma [206]. Research involving in-vitro experiments and immunohistochemical staining of pancreatic carcinoma tumorous cells revealed that upregulation of cytoplasmic RRM1 was particularly associated with significantly reduced overall survival in patients treated with gemcitabine, thereby, conferring resistance towards the drug and reducing its overall efficacy [207]. Likewise, a clinical analysis of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma patients undergoing curative resection followed by gemcitabine adjuvant chemotherapy, revealed a marked difference in the 3- and 5-year overall survival rates [208]. Those individual with upregulation of RRM1 had significantly worse survival rates of 10.5% and 5.1% in contrast to individuals with downregulation of RRM1 with 25.7% and 21.5% survival rates. Another similar study involving the evaluation of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma patients undergoing resection followed by gemcitabine adjuvant chemotherapy demonstrated improved long term survival rates with overexpression of hENT1 and low expression of RRM1, thereby serving as robust treatment-based protein biomarkers [209]. FOLFIRINOX is a combination chemotherapy regimen comprising of 5-flurouracil, leucovorin, irinotecan and oxaliplatin. Among these, 5-flurouracil undergoes catabolism predominantly by dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase and exerts its effects through inhibition of thymidylate synthase [210]. In research involving 15 diverse human pancreatic carcinoma cell lines, a notable association between the upregulation of dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase and thymidylate synthase and reduced sensitivity towards 5-flurouracil, establishing that overexpression of these enzymes imparts significant resistance towards the drug [211]. A multidisciplinary study involving genomic databases, tissue microarrays, proteomics and in-vivo and in-vitro approaches for evaluating the primary function of carboxylesterase-2 (CES2), the enzyme involved in the catabolism of irinotecan into SN-38, demonstrated that upregulation of CES2 substantially improved the overall survival in individuals administered with FOLFIRINOX neoadjuvant chemotherapy for resectable or borderline resectable pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [212].

Methodologies for peptide discovery and validation

Ultrasound is frequently employed as a first-line imaging technique for individuals experiencing gastrointestinal region pain or cholestasis manifestations. This cost-effective and non-invasive method is often preferred for the initial evaluation of suspected pancreatic tumors. Pancreatic cancer typically presents on ultrasound as a mass with diminished echogenicity and an irregular internal appearance. Additionally, there may be evidence of pancreatic enlargement, ductal changes (such as dilation or narrowing), and effects on nearby blood vessels [213]. Computed Tomography (CT) scans offer high sensitivity (89–97%) for pancreatic cancer detection, typically revealing low-density masses due to reduced blood flow in solid tumors. Multi-phase spiral CT technology has significantly enhanced diagnostic capabilities, improving contrast and visualization of small tumors and peripancreatic vessels. Furthermore, multi-slice CT perfusion imaging leverages vascular differences between tumors and normal tissue, allowing characterization based on blood flow and capillary permeability, aiding in tumor differentiation and assessment of pathophysiological changes [214]. Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is a non-invasive imaging method that uses fluids present in the bile and pancreatic ducts to create contrast. This technique takes advantage of the unique composition of normal pancreatic tissue, which is rich in water, proteins, and manganese ions. By utilizing specialized Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) sequences, particularly those with fat suppression and dynamic contrast enhancement, MRCP can effectively distinguish cancerous tissue from the normal pancreas and adjacent structures. This approach is particularly valuable for identifying tumors with limited blood supply and may surpass spiral CT in sensitivity to identify pancreatic malignancies at initial stages [215]. When evaluating pancreatic tumors, diagnostic procedures like Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) and Percutaneous Transhepatic Cholangiography (PTC) offer valuable multi-angle imaging capabilities. Similarly, combining 3D MRI techniques with MRCP can enhance visualization and preoperative assessment, providing information comparable to CT scans in determining if a tumor can be surgically removed [216]. Endoscopic Ultrasound (EUS) provides detailed visualization of pancreatic tumors by inserting an ultrasound probe into the digestive tract, offering superior clarity compared to external ultrasound. Endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA), with its high resolution and ability to avoid interference, excels in detecting small pancreatic lesions and assessing cancer spread, surpassing CT and external ultrasound. This technique, particularly fine needle aspiration, achieves near-perfect diagnostic accuracy, enabling differentiation between benign and malignant tumors through pathological examination [217]. Positron Emission Tomography (PET) imaging, using radioactive glucose (fluorodeoxyglucose), detects tumors by their increased glucose uptake. The system captures gamma rays emitted by the tracer, creating 3D images of its concentration. Combining PET with CT significantly improves diagnostic precision. High-resolution PET can identify small pancreatic tumors (as little as 7 mm), potentially aiding in early detection and treatment of operable cancers [218].

The deep location of the pancreas and interference from surrounding organs, particularly gas in the gastrointestinal tract, hinder accurate imaging, making early diagnosis of small pancreatic tumors (< 2 cm) challenging, especially with ultrasound. While CT is widely used, subtle changes in pancreatic shape caused by small tumors can be easily overlooked, particularly with basic CT protocols. EUS-FNA, while offering high accuracy, carries risks such as bleeding, pancreatic leakage, and the potential for needle-track seeding of cancer cells. ERCP, due to its invasive nature, can lead to complications like pancreatitis and cholangitis. Furthermore, it may not effectively differentiate various causes of pancreatic duct changes and has limitations in visualizing the pancreatic parenchyma. Consequently, less invasive techniques like ultrasound, CT, MRCP, and EUS are often preferred. PET scans, while promising, can be influenced by local variations in sugar metabolism, potentially leading to inaccurate diagnoses. Careful interpretation is crucial to avoid misidentification of benign conditions as cancerous [216] (Fig. 3).

Diagnostic imaging techniques for pancreatic cancer detection: highlighting MRCP for duct visualization, CT scan as the standard initial imaging, PET/PET-CT for metastatic assessment, EUS-FNA for early tumor detection, and Transabdominal ultrasound as a first-line tool

Carbohydrate Antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) testing represents the standard serological approach for detecting pancreatic neoplasms and predicting patient outcomes in current practice. The test demonstrates 79–81% sensitivity and 82–90% specificity when detecting PDAC [219]. Studies have demonstrated that serum levels of CA19-9 can rise substantially 2 years before physicians confirm PDAC. At a specificity of 95%, CA19-9 (employing the > 37 U/mL criterion) captures 68% of PDAC patients at the 1-year pre-diagnostic window, and this sensitivity remains at 53% even 2 years prior to diagnosis [220]. The sensitivity of CA19-9 is limited in early-stage disease. Additionally, individuals lacking a fucosyltransferase enzyme may produce false-negative results. Bile duct obstruction can cause false positive results [221].

Sample collection and preparation

Sources of samples (blood, pancreatic juice, tissue biopsies)

Blood is typically drawn by venipuncture into tubes containing an anticoagulant, followed by rapid processing in order to preserve labile analytes. Evaluation of PDAC among South African patients involved the collection of blood samples from a total of 46 individuals, divided into 6 healthy volunteers, 6 patients of chronic pancreatitis (CP), and 34 patients of PDAC [further divided into 22 patients with resectable (RPC), 8 with locally advanced (LAPC), and 4 with metastatic (MPC) conditions] [222]. Pancreatic cancer detection has been carried out using microRNA biomarkers analysed from whole blood of PDAC and CP patients, with the microRNA expression in whole blood providing two diagnostic panels to distinguish healthy individuals from those afflicted with pancreatic cancer [223]. As an example, Majumder et al. collected venous blood in K₂EDTA vacutainer tubes and processed each sample within 4 h. Tubes are typically centrifuged to separate plasma from cells (double-spin protocols are common) and aliquots frozen at − 80 °C to reduce multiple freeze-thaw cycles. Silica-coated binding beads and lysis buffer containing isopropyl alcohol can be added to the blood samples for DNA capture as well as precipitation [224]. Streck Cell-Free DNA blood collection tubes (cfDNA BCTs) have potential benefits over normal K2EDTA tubes but have mostly been tested in healthy subjects. Blood was obtained from cancer patients (n = 53) with colorectal (n = 21), pancreatic (n = 11), and non-small-cell lung cancer (n = 21) using both cfDNA BCT tubes and K2EDTA tubes, processed either immediately, after 3 days (BCTs), or 6 h (K2EDTA) at room temperature. The results of the study supported the use of cfDNA BCT tubes for the reliable evaluation of cfDNA and mutational load in clinical oncology specimens [225]. In addition to serum/plasma, pancreatic juice has been extensively utilized for proteomic analysis pertaining to the detection of pancreatic cancer. The proteome of pancreatic juice has been subjected to characterization in patients suffering from PDAC, pre-cancer, and benign pancreatic diseases, and in individuals without any apparent pathological condition. The pancreatic juice provides a vast source of proteins associated with PDAC that can be used for distinguishing such patients from healthy individuals [226]. Analysis and comparison of somatic mutations between primary tumors and pancreatic juice obtained from PDAC patients who had undergone Whipple’s operation was performed using amplicon-based targeted deep sequencing. Pancreatic juice was found to be rich in KRAS mutations not commonly found in the primary tumors, thus necessitating the inclusion of TP53 mutation detection and additional markers in the use of pancreatic juice in clinical settings [227].

Sample processing techniques (enrichment, fractionation)

Different methods of sample processing techniques can be effectively applied for the study of the multi-faceted development and tumor biology of pancreatic cancer. Fine needle aspiration (FNA), particularly in combination with rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE), has proved to be the standard for the purpose of sampling pancreatic solid masses by means of endoscopic ultrasound (EUS), although this method has some limitations. As a result, EUS fine needle biopsy (FNB), using a new generation of needles, can be employed to minimize the limitations of FNA [228]. Following sampling, processing of the samples presents a plethora of options. For instance, circulating tumor cells (CTCs) from peripheral blood samples collected from 24 pancreatic cancer patients were enriched and cultivated using a new size-based separation MetaCell® technology which involved culturing the CTCs on a membrane in fetal bovine serum (FBS) enriched RPMI media [229]. Enrichment of tumor fraction in scenarios of low content of tumor cells in the neoplastic tissue can also be performed using the laser capture microdissection (LCM) technique. The technique enables small tissue isolation from a heterogenous population by means of direct visualization through a microscope [230] (Fig. 4).

Integrated Multi-Stage Strategy for Clinical Peptide Biomarker Translation. Five-step pipeline for translating peptide and protein biomarkers from initial discovery to clinical validation. Stage 1 (Sample Collection) emphasizes the necessity of proper handling (e.g., storage at − 80 °C) for diverse sample types, including biofluids and tissue from venipuncture or surgical biopsy. Stage 2 (Sample Processing) involves techniques like MetaCell enrichment to isolate low-abundance analytes, such as Circulating Tumor Cells (CTCs). The core discovery is conducted in Stage 3 (Mass Spectrometry Analysis), leveraging high-resolution mass spectrometers for quantitative proteomic analysis, including both label-free and label-based methods. Stage 4 (Immunoassay Validation) addresses limitations of Mass Spectrometry (e.g., large sample volumes) by using highly sensitive platforms like Digital ELISA for verification. The final Stage 5 (Data Analysis and Validation) involves rigorous statistical and machine learning approaches (e.g., PCA, SVM) to classify and stratify patients, underscoring the critical need for validation in large, independent, and multi-center clinical cohorts

Mass spectrometry-based proteomics

Overview of different mass spectrometry techniques (LC-MS/MS, MALDI-TOF)

Mass spectrometry (MS) has been used to identify T-cell epitopes in PDAC-associated antigens or neoantigens, following the isolation of HLA class I (HLA-I) and HLA class II (HLA-II)-restricted peptides from human PDAC tissues. The identified epitopes bound non-matched HLA molecules, along with the induction of T-cell response in peripheral T-cells from both HLA-type matched as well as non-matched patients. Polyfunctional cytokine responses were induced in peripheral T-cells by peptides containing both HLA class I and II epitopes [231]. While MALDI-mass spectrometry imaging (MALDI-MSI) has been applied in the characterization of the proteomic composition of primary PDAC and distant metastasis for the training and validation of machine learning models, the technique was directly applied to analyse 93 PDAC and 126 pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor (pNET) tissues assembled in tissue microarrays. The prepared training and validation sets were subjected to a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) model, with the model prepared based on 46 peptide ions. The model correctly classified 95% PDAC patients and 100% pNET from the validation set formed from the heterogeneous dataset (PDAC + pNET) [232, 233].

Label-free vs. label-based quantification methods

Label-based quantification methods facilitate the comparison of two or more samples pertaining to the same mass spectrum with a considerable level of accuracy and reproducibility. Further, it also avoids the missing value problem associated with label-free approaches. However, label-based methods also have some limitations; these include substantial costs, additional steps involved in appropriate sample preparation, a limited number of samples analysed in the span of a single experiment, and the use of the MS1 spectrum for quantification. On the other hand, label-free approaches enable cost minimization and reduction in the number of sample preparation steps, thus providing a high preference for biomarker research. This approach, however, is accompanied by low accuracy and poor reproducibility [234]. An unlimited number of proteomes have been compared by label-free quantification using direct Label-free quantification (LFQ), a ratio-based approach meant for sample normalization and the determination of protein intensities. This method scales linearly with the samples, allowing the analytical procedures to finish in a matter of minutes, thus sharply cutting time investment in otherwise lengthy procedures [235].

Data processing and peptide identification

Upon analysis of serum specimens obtained from 176 PDAC patients and 158 healthy control patients using MALDI-TOF-MS, the serum peptide profiles obtained were differentiated into the two respective groups using a classification model based on 13 peptides. For patients with early-stage PDAC, the model produced significant sensitivity (100%) and specificity (96.7%) using 13 peptides in the classification process [236].

Immunoassays

ELISA, antibody arrays, and other immuno-based methods

Novel methods using biomarker detection arrays have evolved for the accurate detection of pancreatic cancer. One such method is the reverse phase protein array (RPPA). The RPPA produced considerable comparability to patient samples analysed using conventional enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) with respect to the biomarker CA19-9. Further, the sample reproducibility of CA19-9 was significant. When normalized to albumin, RPPA samples demonstrated increased sensitivity in comparison to ELISA, with the added advantage of appropriately distinguishing CA19-9 levels between cancer and non-cancerous samples [237]. For the identification of suitable blood-based biomarker candidates for the timely detection of PDAC, approximately 3000 proteins were measured through the use of conventional immunoassays (ELISA) and Olink multiplex technology. Machine learning further combined the biomarker candidates to form 4- to 6-plex signatures, which were found to produce 84% sensitivity at 95% specificity, vastly outperforming CA19-9 (53% sensitivity). A total of 41 biomarker candidates distributed across multiple signatures were identified in the study [238]. Indirect and sandwich enzyme immunoassays (EIA) were applied with the aim of comparing the reactivity of PAM4 (clivatuzumab, an antibody with high specificity for PDAC) with numerous anti-mucin antibodies. Upon analysis, MUC5AC was found to be the specific mucin species to which PAM4 holds reactivity. Thus, the study proposed the use of anti-MUC5AC monoclonal antibodies as probes in the sandwich EIA to improve the detection of early-stage PDAC [239].

Advantages and limitations

Immunoassays (for instance, ELISA and antibody arrays) represent key components of proteomics in terms of the detection and quantification of specific proteins. Their wide usage comes from the very sensitive, specific, and amendable analysis potential they have, as well as their sensitivity, which continuously improves (for example, digital ELISA can be sensitive to the femtogram level). This is a significant advantage of using ELISA as it can help with early discovery of diseases. Digital immunoassays present a three-point advantageous front: sensitivity, multiplexing, and clinical use [240]. The ability to examine multi-analytes at the same time with antibody arrays presents the opportunity to examine biological or clinical samples comprehensively, which in turn can assist in biomarker identification and discovery, combined with benefits like cost-effectiveness, speed, ease of use, and technical simplicity [241]. Despite all the aforementioned advantages, a number of limitations are associated with these immunoassays. ELISA, one of the most conventional immunoassays, has some significant drawbacks. These include tedious procedures, the requirement of a high sample volume, limited options for multiplexing, and necessity of centralized laboratory equipment [242].

Bioinformatics and data analysis

Statistical analysis for biomarker discovery

Statistical methods for biomarker discovery using metabolomics data include supervised as well as unsupervised models for multivariate analysis (MVA), including principal component analysis (PCA), support vector machine (SVM), Partial Least Square Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA), Random Forest (RF), and variational autoencoders. Some of the tools available for statistical analysis also include MetaboAnalyst, MZmine 3, MetaboLyzer, PhenoMeNal, SECIMTools (SouthEast Center for Integrated Metabolomics), SIMCA®, and R packages [243]. Statistical analysis associated with accurate biomarker discovery should be focused on evaluating sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive value, ROC curve, calibration, and discrimination, as highlighted by a study discussing statistical considerations for biomarker discovery and validation [244].

Peptide database searches

A study highlighted the Pancreatic Expression database (http://www.pancreasexpression.org/) aimed at enabling greater comprehensive research on pancreatic cancer by the global scientific community. The database, which holds 32 datasets consisting of 7,636 gene expression measurements, can be utilized for the purpose of target site discovery, mining information pertaining to cancer ontology, analysis of cancer gene promoters, identification and analysis of genes linked to cancer pathogenesis, cross-platform meta-analysis, and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) selection for conduct of pancreatic cancer association studies [245]. Accurate and rapid peptide identification has been highlighted using PEAKS DB, a tool that combines de novo sequencing results with database search procedures for the improvement of filtration and the scoring function, thus enhancing both sensitivity and accuracy [246]. Further, JUMP provides another hybrid database search program, generating amino acid tags and ranking peptide spectrum matches (PSMs) by means of an integrated score from the tags and pattern matching. Using this approach, JUMP can achieve sensitivity and specificity better than other search engines used for peptide identification, such as SEQUEST, Mascot, InsPecT, and PEAKS DB [247].

Machine learning approaches for biomarker prediction

Although the prediction of biomarkers and clinical outcomes in PDAC through the use of imaging-based machine learning models in rapidly increasing with appreciable quality scores, improvement of methodological quality has been highlighted as a potential requirement [248] (Table 2). A study employed machine learning and deep learning-based hybrid approaches for the prediction of Hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection that results in chronic liver damage. Among the tested methods (Random Forest, Bagging Classifier, Cat Boost, Gaussian NB, Bernoulli NB, Multinomial NB, ANN, Linear Discriminant Analysis, SGD Classifier, and MLP), Random Forest produced the highest value of accuracy (99.53%) in the diagnostic attempt [249]. An artificial neural network, PDAC-ANN, was used to classify PDAC samples based on training performed using five best-performing genes (AHNAK2, KRT19, LAMB3, LAMC2, and S100P) among 40 identified core genes (CG). The PDAC-ANN performed classification with an F1-score of 0.83 for the normal samples, while producing a score of 0.88 for the PDAC samples. The average score was 0.86. The model presented a sensitivity of 87.6% and a specificity of 83.1% [250].

Validation studies

Importance of independent cohorts

Creation of validation cohorts, both prospective and retrospective, hold several advantages with respect to the overall outcomes a study design. Prospective cohorts enable the optimal measurement of predictors as well as outcomes, along with a reduction in risk associated with recall bias, and ease of establishment of causality. Retrospective cohorts, on the other hand, lead to lower costs and facilitation of the study of rare diseases [251]. Independent discovery and validation cohorts were designated for the prediction of survival among patients after resection in PDAC. A total of 224 and 191 patients were included in the discovery (single institution) and validation (multi-institutional) cohorts, respectively. In both the cohorts, shorter overall survival (OS) was found to be associated with S100A4, Ca-125 and mesothelin expression, while multivariable analysis revealed that expression of at least one of these three biomarkers held an independent association with reduced OS [252]. A retrospective cohort study was performed using resected PDAC, including cases recorded between 2008 and 2015. Out of the two cohorts used, the discovery cohort consisted of 160 PDAC cases pertaining to 154 patients (148 primary and 12 metastatic), while the replication cohort consisted of 95 primary PDAC cases. From the identification of five predominant mutational subtypes, the PDAC cases were clustered into four major subtypes: age-related, double-strand break repair, mismatch repair, and 1 with unidentified etiology. Double-strand break repair and mismatch repair subtypes presented an association with increased antitumor immunity expression and overexpression of regulatory molecules [253].

Multi-centre validation studies

Multi-centre studies, widely utilized in clinical and public health research, hold advantages such as quicker recruitment, enhanced generalizability, and diversity in population coverage. However, shortcomings associated with them, including statistical, methodological, and implementation challenges, require careful and strategic minimization strategies. These include a sound study design, assured standardization, use of high-quality data, appropriate consideration of statistical measures and requirements, and a uniform implementation methodology [254]. With respect to surgical factors influencing survival after distal pancreatectomy (DP) for PDAC, a study conducted a large multi-centre analysis involving 1200 patients from 34 centres (11 European countries and the U.S.A.). Gerota’s fascia resection, R0 resection, and decreased lymph node ratio were found to be associated with an improved overall survival among patients. Adjuvant chemotherapy found association with improved survival, while extended resection was found to be associated with worse overall survival [255].

Assessing sensitivity and specificity

A systematic review and meta-analysis, involving a timeline of January 2004 to June 2015 and a total of 5,399 patients (3,567 patients with PDAC), analysed the sensitivity and specificity of PDAC diagnosis using MRI, CT, PET and PET/CT, EUS, and transabdominal ultrasound (TAUS). Upon evaluation, all methods of detection except PET presented sensitivity and specificity within 95% confidence intervals for the diagnosis of PDAC [256]. A study evaluated the sensitivity and specificity of MRI and CT scans with respect to vascular invasion of pathologically confirmed PDAC. With an assessment of 65 and 474 vessels with and without vascular invasion in 107 patients, MRI and CT scans showed comparable sensitivities (71%/74%) and specificities (97% each) [257].

Challenges and limitations of peptide biomarkers

Developing effective pancreatic cancer biomarkers is challenging due to inconsistent study methodologies, complex statistical analyses with limited data, and hidden biases. Technical limitations in assays and difficulties in translating research to clinical use further hinder progress [270]. A major hurdle in pancreatic cancer biomarker development is obtaining suitable tumor tissue for analysis. Early diagnosis often relies on fine needle aspiration, which may yield limited tumor cells. Additionally, pre-treatment biopsies frequently contain insufficient tumor content for accurate assessment. Biomarker discovery efforts are hindered by two key issues: scarce optimal samples and suboptimal subject matching. Standardized procedures for sample collection, storage, and analysis are also crucial but often lacking. Furthermore, antibody-based technologies, while common, face limitations in robustness, throughput, and cost-effectiveness, requiring extensive validation and large sample volumes [271]. The intricate genetic and molecular variability in pancreatic malignancies creates difficulties for identifying diagnostic markers. A single biomarker may not adequately capture the heterogeneity of the disease, making it difficult to find markers that reliably distinguish different stages or subtypes [272]. Additionally, many biomarkers lack specificity, appearing in other cancers or even benign conditions, which complicates their use for accurate diagnosis. For example, CA19-9, a commonly used marker, can be elevated in various gastrointestinal cancers and non-cancerous disorders, limiting its effectiveness for early detection. Furthermore, a subset of individuals cannot produce CA19-9 due to an enzyme deficiency, further restricting its applicability [273].

Peptide biomarkers, including phage-query-separated peptide antigens of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and peptidomic peptide fragments, like PF4 or CXCL7, are subject to unique challenges; in particular, high levels of cardiac renders and subsequent decay by the circulating matrix metalloproteinases dramatically reduce both circulating levels and the longevity of the concentration, which reduces the viability of serial measurement [274, 275]. Lack of specificity of analysis is seen in some part because most putative pancreatic autoantigens overlap with those found in chronic pancreatitis, or in fibrotic processes, such as collagen-PRO-C11-511 is disproportionately elevated in stroma-dominated lesions. This problem is further intensified by the desmoplastic reaction of PDAC that is intense in nature, as the tumor cells comprise less than 20–40% of the total mass of tumour and thus dilute the plasma peptide signals [275–277]. Validation issues have been persistent due to small cohort size, assay variability as seen by dissimilarity between MALDI -TOF and Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) systems, and unfavorable pharmacokinetics of unmodified peptides, which have short half-lives, which are immunogenic [278]. It’s comparatively low rate of incidence, even in peptides with 95 per cent specificity, leads to high levels of false positive rates, which require Phase 3 trials of over 1000 patients to be adequately validated. In addition, retroperitoneal position limits the availability of core biopsy, which biases the studies to advance cohorts [277, 279]. The heterogeneity of the tumour microenvironment silences the process of peptide release through exosome sequestration and shedding, whilst ethnic differences, such as the lower reactivity of CA19-9 in Indian populations, inhibit the generalisation of the generated results. The suggested solutions to these limitations include incorporating peptide candidates into full multi-omics panels, such as with ctDNA -KRAS/methylation assays using Epi-sPNA, which has demonstrated 93% sensitivity; recognition of peptide patterns in low-volume serum is done by artificial intelligence and machine-learning techniques; cyclization, incorporation of D-amino acids and PEGylation to increase half-life beyond 24 h; standardization is done complying to Minimum Information for Publication of Quantitative Real-Time PCR Experiments (MIQE) standards, and National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) reference materials are used in a way to ensure that results can be replicated [280, 281].

Future directions

In addition to the already available panels (PF4/CXCL7 + CA19-9) to use together with CA19-9, future studies should consider breaking ground in hybrid peptidomics platforms that combine peptide fragments with isomiRs—including miR-21-5p forms—and glycopeptides to learn new stroma-specific signals that cannot be detected in the analysis of bulk tumor tissue. This can overcome tumour microenvironment dilution, enabling increased detection of low-abundance signals due to cancer-associated fibroblasts, and could potentially achieve over 95% specificity in stage I disease in diabetes correlated cohorts, a gap in Ca19-9 validation literature currently [282]. The future of the wearable biosensors that cover the interstitial fluid peptidome and the identification of the transient increases in the peptide products of PRO-C11-511 before the development of signs and symptoms will be possible through the development of wearable biosensors that have peptide-functionalised graphene electrodes. In combination with the federated artificial-intelligence learning of a population of over ten thousand wearables across the world, this approach will help predictive alerts on high-risk patients in India, thus avoiding the constraints of invasive-biopsies and creating a paradigm shift in traditional statistical liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) assays [283]. Directed evolution of de novo peptides in synthetic PDAC-on-chip systems provides a promising way to integrate unnatural amino acids like fluorinated residues in order to provide dual diagnostic and prognostic capabilities. These engineered and engineered to specific forms of mutation allow a highly rigorous comparison between basal-like subtypes, which are not vulnerable to the action of KRAS, and thus seal a significant gap in sections of current translational research that require specific mutations, thereby bridging the gap to a significant degree [284]. The addition of patient-specific peptide reactions, electronic health record-based risk measures (including Indian GLOBOCAN demographic data), and quantum-enhanced simulations into PDAC digital twins will facilitate the prediction of therapeutic outcomes and prioritise the use of the stromal modulators like PEGPH20 before peptides are used to guide immunotherapies. The virtual trial platform will also reduce the ethical load of biopsy in the process of conducting Phase 3 trials, and the long-term objective is to attain a 20% enhancement in the overall survival rate by 2030 [284].

Conclusion

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains a deadly challenge with a 5-year survival rate of 13.3%, underscoring the need for innovative approaches like peptide biomarkers. These peptides, from neoantigens to immune modulators, enhance early detection, differential diagnosis, and prognosis, while combinations like THBS2 with CA19-9 boost diagnostic power (AUC 0.95). Despite their promise, challenges such as sensitivity/specificity issues and reproducibility hurdles persist, necessitating multi-center validation and standardized assays. Peptides hold transformative potential for precision oncology, enabling non-invasive screening and tailored therapies. Future efforts should focus on prospective trials and multi-omics integration to overcome barriers. This approach could shift PDAC management, improving survival and quality of life.
