Spermatozoa lacking TEX51 display hypofertility and defects in morphology
Authors: Sophie Dupuis, Morgane Le Beulze, Tyler D R Vance, Rémi Pierre, Jeffrey E Lee, Ahmed Ziyyat, Sandrine Barbaux
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFertilization is essential for all sexually reproducing species, yet the complete molecular mechanism remains unclear. Here, we revisit a structure-based search of the AlphaFold human proteome to identify new members of the IST superfamily, a key family of proteins associated with fertilization. We discover that TEX51, predicted to form a 4-helix bundle, a ß-hinge, a pair of CXXC motifs and a transmembrane domain, shares structural similarities with IST superfamily proteins (IZUMO1, SPACA6, and TMEM95). Given the testis-specific expression of TEX51 and conservation across mammals, we hypothesize that TEX51 plays a role in fertilization. To investigate this, we create a Tex51-knockout (KO) mouse model that removes the complete Tex51 open reading frame and incidentally a part of the nearby lncRNA Gm44577. These KO males are hypofertile as shown by in vivo and in vitro analyses, despite showing normal sperm motility. Morphological abnormalities in the KO sperm include disjunction between the flagellum midpiece and principal piece, and an increased presence of cytoplasmic remnants. Electron microscopy further reveals defects in the anchoring of the acrosome to the nucleus. These findings expand the roles of IST superfamily proteins beyond functions in gamete interaction to broader aspects of reproduction such as sperm formation during spermiogenesis.Subject terms: Genetics, Structural biology, Spermatogenesis

Introduction

Fertilization culminates with the adhesion and fusion of sperm and egg membranes to create a zygote. Although the list of molecules essential for this process has rapidly grown during the past decade, it remains incomplete1. On the egg side, CD9, which is a molecular complex organizer belonging to the tetraspanin family, and JUNO, which is a GPI-anchored folate receptor-like protein, are the only known indispensable players2–4. On the sperm side, key molecules include IZUMO1, TMEM95, SPACA6, SOF1, FIMP, TMEM81, DCST1, and DCST25–11. Disruption of the genes encoding any of these proteins in mice leads to the same phenotype: sperm can reach the perivitelline space and adhere to the oolemma but fail to fuse and accumulate. The precise mechanisms by which these molecules participate in the adhesion and fusion process are largely unknown. Only IZUMO1 and its receptor JUNO have been demonstrated to interact, and this interaction allows their host gametes to adhere. Very recently, an interaction between three of the sperm proteins (IZUMO1, SPACA6, and TMEM81) was experimentally demonstrated in zebrafish and predicted in mammals using AlphaFold6. Using the same software, another team predicted the existence of the same complex and also its interaction with oocyte CD9 and JUNO12.

Several of the critical fertilization factors adopt similar three-dimensional structures. The structure of the monomeric human IZUMO1 protein was the first to be determined. IZUMO1 features an anti-parallel four-helix bundle (4HB) domain in its N-terminal region and a C-terminal immunoglobulin-like (Ig-like) domain separated by a two-stranded β-hinge13–15. A pair of CXXC motifs forms disulfide bonds to link the 4HB to the β-hinge. Interestingly, subsequent studies analyzing the structures of human SPACA616 and TMEM9517 revealed similarities to the monomeric human IZUMO1 protein. SPACA6 has the same organization (i.e., 4HB, β-hinge, Ig-like domains) and contains the pair of CXXC disulfide linkages, whereas TMEM95 contains a similar 4HB, β-hinge, and CXXC motifs, but lacks the Ig-like domain. Based on these structural and functional similarities, a new protein superfamily was proposed, named IST after IZUMO1-SPACA6-TMEM9516. It was suggested that the IST-superfamily represents a new conserved structural family of proteins involved in gamete adhesion, fusion or fertilization.

Breakthroughs in deep learning have now enabled the accurate prediction of structural models for proteins in the human proteome, even where no prior structural information existed. This comprehensive coverage of the human proteome allows for 3D protein structure comparisons at the proteome level, enabling the identification of uncharacterized structures by relating them to known ones. We hypothesized that other proteins with similar folds might also function in fertilization. In this study, our analysis of the AlphaFold human structural proteome identified Testis-expressed 51 (TEX51) as a new member of the IST superfamily. TEX51 is predicted to be a transmembrane protein with a 4HB domain, a hinge region, and a pair of CXXC motifs, and it shares structural similarities with IZUMO1, SPACA6 and TMEM95. Given its testis-specific expression, specifically from the spermatid stage, we hypothesized that TEX51 plays a role in fertilization similar as do other IST superfamily proteins. A knockout (KO) mouse model lacking Tex51 expression, and incidentally disrupting expression of the nearby lncRNA Gm44577, exhibited both in vitro and in vivo hypofertility and structural defects to the sperm flagella. These findings underscore the importance of IST superfamily members in reproductive biology.

Results

Human proteome structural searches reveal TEX51 is an IST superfamily member

Proteins in the IST superfamily all share an anti-parallel 4HB, two CXXC motifs, a β-hinge and/or an Ig-like domain (Fig. 1). The Ig-like domain in IZUMO1 and SPACA6 adopts a rare 2 + 5 or 2 + 4 stranded topology, with β-sheet I containing strands b and e, and β-sheet II containing strands A, G, F, C and C’13. A search of the AlphaFold database using the Dali server18 and SPACA6 as a search model identified a long list of potential candidate proteins (Supplementary Data 1). Many of these are Ig-containing proteins due to the Ig-like domain in SPACA6 search model. While some of these Ig-containing proteins, such as TMEM81, have a β-hinge and IZUMO1 Ig-like 2 + 5 or 2 + 4 β-stranded topology and are essential for fertilization, most are annotated as being involved in other cellular processes unrelated to fertilization, reflecting the widespread presence of immunoglobulin folds in the human proteome. Given that AlphaFold predictions may not always accurately predict the β-stranded topology, we focused on identifying proteins with an anti-parallel 4HB, β-hinge, and CXXC motifs. Manual inspection of the AlphaFold database identified that TEX51 is structurally similar to TMEM95, and to parts of IZUMO1 and SPACA6. We propose TEX51 is the newest member of the IST superfamily (Fig. 1).

TEX51 adopts a structure similar to TMEM95

Tex51 is located on human chromosome 2 and its canonical sequence in Uniprot is annotated as a 166 amino acid type I membrane protein. TEX51 is expressed specifically in the testis, particularly in spermatids, as revealed by transcriptomic and proteomic studies19,20 (Supplementary Fig. 1). The structure of TEX51 predicted by AlphaFold 321 has a central two-helix bundle flanked by two perpendicular helices, two-stranded β-hinge, and transmembrane anchor (Fig. 1). However, the two perpendicular helices are not predicted with high confidence. Uniprot lists nine computationally mapped potential isoforms encoded by Tex51 ranging from 52 to 203 amino acids in length (Supplementary Table 1). The three isoforms with at least 190 residues have an intact signal peptide, a pair of CXXC motifs, a transmembrane domain, and a 4HB with anti-parallel helices (Supplementary Fig. 2). The structure of TEX51 is similar to TMEM95, IZUMO2, IZUMO3 and IZUMO4 with its 4HB, β-hinge and two CXXC motifs, but it lacks the Ig-like domain as seen in IZUMO1 and SPACA6, thus clearly situating TEX51 in the IST superfamily of fertilization factors (Table 1).

Disruption of Tex51 and Gm44577 lncRNA expression results in hypofertile male mice

A CRISPR/Cas9 approach was designed to disrupt the Tex51 gene in mice (detailed in Supplementary Text). Guide RNAs were designed to target regions flanking the gene’s open reading frame; specifically, two guide RNAs targeted sequences upstream of exon 1 and two targeted sequences downstream of exon 8 (Fig. 2a). Two mouse lines were characterized. Genotyping and sequencing identified deletions of 4465 and 4513 base pairs at the Tex51 locus in the two KO lines (Fig. 2b). A long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), termed Gm44577, is located close to the Tex51 gene in the opposite direction. The structure of this gene remains ambiguous, as different databases describe differing isoforms (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Notably, the longest isoforms appear to originate from a promoter likely disrupted by the Tex51 deletions. To assess the impact of these deletions, we examined Gm44577 expression in the KO models. The long isoforms originating near Tex51 and present in WT testis were absent in KO testis cDNA. In contrast, the more distal isoform, likely transcribed from an alternative promoter, remained in both testis samples from KO lines and WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Gm44577 is a mouse-specific lncRNA, predominantly expressed in testis but also in muscle, spleen, and nervous tissue (www.ebi.ac.uk/gxa; www.informatics.jax.org). While Tex51 likely plays a key role in the observed phenotype, we cannot exclude a potential contribution from partial disruption of Gm44577. Therefore, we refer to our model as Tex51-Gm44577 KO, hereafter abbreviated as Tex51/Gm-KO. The lines were maintained through crosses, and as the results were consistent between the two lines, data were pooled. Due to the lack of commercially available antibodies against TEX51, the absence of expression was verified by RT-PCR in homozygotes. No amplification was detected in cDNA from testis samples from KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Female Tex51/Gm-KO mice were normally fertile and Tex51/Gm-KO males exhibited normal sexual behavior. However, when WT females were fertilized by Tex51/Gm-KO males, fewer offspring were produced compared to mating with WT male littermates (4.1 ± 0.9, n = 16 vs 7.2 ± 0.4, n = 13; p < 0.05) (Fig. 3a). Additionally, some males exhibited both a complete lack of offspring, and a reduced or an almost normal offspring, despite the presence of a positive vaginal plug after each mating. This indicates that the region containing the Tex51 and Gm44577 genes is not essential for fertilization, but that it does affect a fertility-related process. To determine how TEX51 influences fertilization, we performed overnight matings between superovulated WT females and Tex51/Gm-KO or control WT males and collected oocytes the next morning. The fertilization rate was lower with Tex51/Gm-KO males than with WT littermates (76.1% ± 0.04%, n = 130 vs 88.3% ± 0.03%, n = 111; p < 0.05) (Fig. 3b). This confirmed that loss of TEX51 and disruption of Gm44577 expression cause a defect prior to or during fertilization.

Tex51/Gm-KO males are hypofertile in vitro

In vitro fertilization (IVF) assays were performed to evaluate the fertilization capacity of Tex51/Gm-KO sperm. In cumulus-intact IVF, Tex51/Gm-KO sperm had a fertilization rate (i.e., the number of fertilized oocytes among all oocytes) of 8.4 ± 0.02% (n = 154), considerably lower than the 61.8 ± 0.05% (n = 110) fertilization rate with WT sperm (p < 0.0001, Fig. 3c). In zona-free IVF assays, the fertilization index (i.e., mean number of sperm heads per oocyte) was 0.49 ± 0.03 (n = 300) for Tex51/Gm-KO sperm and 1.1 ± 0.04 (n = 290) for WT sperm (p < 0.0001, Fig. 3d). These results demonstrate that although fertilization can occur without TEX51 and Gm44577, it is significantly less efficient both in vitro and in vivo.

Sperm from Tex51/Gm-KO males have abnormal flagellar parameters and altered ultrastructure

In order to understand why Tex51/Gm-KO sperm did not efficiently fertilize eggs, we examined the integrity of sperm and testicular tissue. Testicular histology revealed no differences between WT and Tex51/Gm-KO tissues (Fig. 4a). Epididymal sperm counts were also similar between the WT and Tex51/Gm-KO groups (Fig. 4b). Computer-aided sperm analysis (CASA) showed that sperm motility, along with other CASA parameters, in Tex51/Gm-KO males was indistinguishable from that of sperm from WT males (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Table 2). However, Papanicolaou coloration, which is used to evaluate sperm morphology, revealed differences between Tex51/Gm-KO and WT sperm. Some flagella in Tex51/Gm-KO sperm showed small disjunctions at the interface between the midpiece and the principal piece, with a small portion appearing “naked” (Fig. 5a). Additionally, many Tex51/Gm-KO sperm retained some cytoplasmic remnants, detected as droplets on straight or hairpin flagella (Fig. 5a). These structural anomalies, which appeared more frequent in Tex51/Gm-KO sperm than WT sperm (Fig. 5b), at least for abnormal flagellum, could explain the observed decreases in fertilization rates.

The annulus is a small ring-shaped structure present between the midpiece and the principal piece of the flagella. We stained WT and Tex51/Gm-KO sperm with an anti-SEPTIN4 antibody to determine if the annulus was affected by the observed disjunctions in the Tex51/Gm-KO sperm. SEPTIN4, which is constitutively expressed in the annulus22, was present in both WT and Tex51/Gm-KO sperm (Fig. 5c). This result suggests that the structure of the annulus is not affected Tex51/Gm-KO sperm.

To further explore the abnormalities in Tex51/Gm-KO sperm cells, we examined sperm samples by negative-stain transmission electron microscopy. We found that 25.9% of Tex51/Gm-KO sperm (n = 659) exhibited abnormal sperm heads, compared to only 2.8% in WT controls (n = 211). The abnormal heads featured acrosomes that were loosely attached and asymmetrically positioned at the tip of the nucleus (Fig. 6a (blue arrows) and 6b left). Additionally, cytoplasmic remnants were observed at the junction between the midpiece and the principal piece in 90.6% of Tex51/Gm-KO sperm (n = 107), whereas 49% of WT sperm (n = 49) displayed this feature (Fig. 6a (red arrows) and 6b (right)). We further investigated IZUMO1 localization in epididymal sperm from both WT and Tex51/Gm-KO mice using direct immunofluorescence staining. In WT sperm, 86% appeared acrosome intact, while 14% were spontaneously acrosome reacted. In contrast, about 73% of Tex51/Gm-KO sperm were already acrosome reacted in capacitated epididymal samples (Fig. 6c, d (left) and Supplementary Fig. 5 (left)). In addition, in Tex51/Gm-KO sperm, 30% displayed incomplete or abnormal IZUMO1 localization, indicating abnormalities in acrosome shape or stability (Fig. 6c, d (right) and Supplementary Fig. 5 (right)). These findings suggest that the absence of TEX51 leads to defects in sperm morphology, which may contribute to inefficient fertilization.

Discussion

Despite the success of human IVF, the molecular mechanisms of fertilization remain poorly defined. Identification of all molecular players involved in fertilization is necessary to ascertain the etiologies of infertility and to guide development of novel strategies for non-hormonal contraception. So far, only eight molecular players have been identified on the sperm side that are essential for human sperm-egg attachment or fusion. Among these, IZUMO1, SPACA6, and TMEM95 are structurally similar, leading to their classification as founding members of the IST superfamily of fertilization-related proteins. Members of the IST superfamily are defined by a 4HB, a pair of CXXC motifs, a β-hinge and/or an Ig-like domain. The exact roles of these domains in fertilization remain unclear, but several features of the IST superfamily may be linked to membrane fusion and cell entry, which would be key processes in fertilization. The 4HB and β-hinge domains share structural similarities with SPECT1 and TRAP14, which are Plasmodium proteins involved in host cell traversal and host cell invasion23,24. CXXC motifs are known redox sensors and have been implicated in viral-host cell membrane fusion25. Based on its predicted structure, TEX51 clearly has a 4HB domain, β-hinge, and pair of CXXC motifs, and is most similar to other IST superfamily members, such as TMEM95, IZUMO2, IZUMO3, and IZUMO4. TEX51 is expressed specifically in the testes from the spermatid stage, suggesting a role in reproduction. TEX51 is conserved in mammals as well as lizards, snakes and turtles (Fig. 7), and the mouse and human protein sequences share ~59% sequence identity. Notably, a high level of conservation was observed for surface residues on the 4HB domain (Supplementary Fig. 6), suggesting that this part of the protein plays an important role that is conserved across species. We believe that this firmly places TEX51 as a new member of the IST superfamily.

Due to the proximity of the Tex51 and Gm44577 genes, the deletion of Tex51 affected the expression of its neighbor lncRNA, leading to the loss of certain Gm44577 isoforms. While we cannot rule out that the absence of these isoforms may impair the function of Gm44577, disrupt its downstream targets or contribute to the phenotype, several factors suggest a predominant role for Tex51. Notably, Gm44577 is expressed in the testis but is also detected in other tissues, and it is a mouse-specific gene. In contrast, Tex51 is highly conserved across species (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 6). Taken together, the evidence from two independent KO lines strongly supports that the observed phenotype is primarily driven by the loss of Tex51.

Tex51/Gm-KO male mice are hypofertile, highlighting a crucial role of this region in reproduction. Analysis of in vivo fertilization and the presence of live births revealed that the observed defect in Tex51/Gm-KO mice stemmed from a fertilization issue, rather than problems with embryo development. The Tex51/Gm-KO phenotype ranges from almost normal litters to complete lack of gestation, likely influenced by spermatogenesis waves and the cumulative effects of various sperm defects. In vitro fertilization assays, using both cumulus-intact and zona-free oocytes, further demonstrated that Tex51/Gm-KO sperm had lower fertilization rates compared to WT sperm. The difficulties Tex51/Gm-KO sperm face in penetrating cumulus cells and the zona pellucida suggest potential defects in sperm motility, hyperactivation, or overall sperm integrity.

To investigate why Tex51/Gm-KO sperm were less efficient at fertilizing eggs compared to WT sperm, we examined the integrity of sperm and testicular tissue. Histological assessments of testis tissue, along with analyses of sperm counts and motility parameters, revealed no significant differences between Tex51/Gm-KO sperm and those from control WT littermates. However, light and electron microscopy revealed abnormalities in the sperm acrosome, with some acrosomes asymmetrically positioned at the tip of the nucleus and loosely attached. Capacitated epididymal sperm heads from Tex51/Gm-KO males appeared prematurely acrosome-reacted compared to WT samples and often displayed abnormal IZUMO1 distribution, confirming acrosome detachment, fragility or malformation. Additionally, cytoplasmic remnants containing lipid droplets were frequently observed in Tex51/Gm-KO sperm. This phenotype is also seen in sperm from Spermatogenesis Associated Protein 3 (Spata3)-deleted mice. While the function of SPATA3 is not fully understood, it is not thought to affect gamete interaction26. These abnormalities may impair sperm maturation and the sperm beating mode necessary for fertilization27.

Additional abnormalities were observed in the flagella of Tex51/Gm-KO sperm. These flagella often exhibited a hairpin conformation, which, while not inherently abnormal, was associated with an excess of cytoplasmic material. In Tex51/Gm-KO sperm with straight flagella, we frequently observed a disjunction between the midpiece and principal piece, leading to exposed cytoplasmic droplets and a small segment of the axoneme that appeared naked. Transmission electron microscopy confirmed that many Tex51/Gm-KO sperm have cytoplasmic droplets near the junction of the midpiece and principal piece. Notably, these flagellar defects were not linked to the annulus structure, as SEPTIN4, a key component of the annulus, was normally localized in Tex51/Gm-KO sperm. Moreover, there was no evidence of asthenozoospermia, which is typically associated with mutations in annulus proteins such as TAT1 and septins28,29.

TEX51 appears to be essential for producing normally shaped and differentiated sperm cells, rather than for proper gamete interactions or fusion, as observed with other IST superfamily members. Sperm with mutations in genes essential for the gamete interaction often appear normal but fail to fuse with eggs. In contrast, Tex51/Gm-KO sperm, despite their structural defects, can still fertilize eggs, albeit at a significantly reduced rate. This finding underscores that structural similarity does not always imply functional conservation. For instance, IZUMO2, IZUMO3 and IZUMO4 are also IST superfamily members, but do not participate in gamete interaction (Table 1)30. The function of TEX51 might be similar to IZUMO3, as disruption of the gene encoding IZUMO3 also leads to male hypofertility and acrosome defects31. It is also possible that TEX51 has a role similar to proteins encoded by other Tex gene family members, which have testis-specific expression and links to fertility-related functions32. Further research is needed to fully elucidate the precise role of TEX51.

Our study broadens our functional understanding of IST superfamily proteins. Initially recognized for their roles in gamete interaction and fertilization, it is now clear that IST superfamily members may have a wider involvement in reproduction (Table 1). The IST superfamily members are widely distributed across the Animalia kingdom, including mammals, marsupials, reptiles, amphibians, fish, birds, and sharks (Fig. 7). All identified members of this superfamily have highly enriched RNA expression in early and/or late spermatids. Thus, the evolutionarily conserved IST superfamily proteins likely playing crucial roles in reproduction. Our list of IST superfamily members is likely incomplete, and we expect that additional proteins with similar structural features will be discovered. With the advancement in AlphaFold predictions, we can now expand the search for additional IST superfamily members, enhancing our understanding of the significance of this protein superfamily in reproduction.

Methods

AlphaFold human proteome searches

Using the experimentally determined SPACA6 structure (PDB: 7TA2) as the prototype model for the IST superfamily16, we performed a structural search of the models in the AlphaFold human structural proteome using the Dali server with a hierarchical AF-DB search18. Hits were visually inspected for structural similarity. Since most hits contained an immunoglobulin and were annotated with cellular functions unrelated to fertilization, we focused on identifying proteins with an anti-parallel 4HB, β-hinge and two CXXC motifs − key features of the IST superfamily.

AlphaFold 3 structural predictions

TEX51 canonical (A0A1B0GUA7) and isoform sequences (A0A9L9PYI6, A0A9L9PXS2, A0A1B0GUP2, A0A2R8Y5A7) were obtained from Uniprot (https://www.uniprot.org). Structural predictions were generated with the AlphaFold server (Google/DeepMind) powered by AlphaFold 321. Model coordinates were visualized, and figures generated in PyMOL (Schrödinger, LLC).

Animals and ethics approval

Animal experimentation was conducted in agreement with French national guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. The use of laboratory animals for the current project was approved by the local ethics committee (C2EA-34, Comité d’éthique en matière d’expérimentation animale Paris Descartes) and governmental ethical review committees via the APAFiS Application (Autorisation de projet utilisant des animaux à des fins scientifiques), under registration reference APAFIS #14124-2017072510448522 v26, A. Ziyyat (10/10/2018-10/10/2023). All relevant ethical regulations for animal use were therefore strictly followed.

C57BL/6 WT 7–15 weeks old female mice (Strain name C57BL/6JRj) were purchased from Janvier Labs (Le Genest-Saint-Isle, France) to obtain oocytes. All animals were maintained at the animal facility of the Cochin Institute (Paris) at a stable temperature (21–23 °C) and 14 h light/10 h dark periods, with free access to food and water.

Generation of transgenic mice

A CRISPR/Cas9 approach was designed and performed by the MouseT’IC core facility of the Cochin Institute (Paris, France). Guide RNAs targeting sequences upstream and downstream of the Tex51 open reading frame were produced (Supplementary Table 3), preincubated with Cas9 and electroporated into fertilized C57BL/6 J oocytes. After a successful division, embryos were transferred into pseudopregnant females. Identified founders were mated with WT animals and offspring were crossed to obtain homozygous Tex51/Gm-KO mice. Further details can be found in Supplementary Text.

Genotyping and RT-PCR

DNA was extracted from biopsies using the NucleoSpin Tissue kit (Macherey-Nagel). RNA was extracted from testis using a Trizol protocol (ThermoFisher Scientific) and reverse transcribed using the MMLV enzyme (ThermoFisher Scientific) after DNase I (Promega) treatment. PCR reactions were performed under standard conditions with the GoTaq Flexi DNA polymerase kit (Promega) using primers listed in Supplementary Table 3. Sanger sequencing (Eurofins Genomics) was used to identify the limits of the gene deletions.

In vivo and in vitro fertility assessment

Sexually mature mice (7–15 weeks old) were housed in triple occupancy cages: one male and two females. WT females were mated overnight with either KO or WT littermate males. Vaginal plugs were checked the next morning and litter sizes were counted after 3 weeks.

WT female mice between 5 and 8 weeks old were superovulated with two injections, first with 5 IU pregnant mare serum gonadotrophin (Intervet) and then 48 h later with 5 IU human chorionic gonadotrophin (Intervet). Some of these females were mated with KO or their WT littermate males overnight, and females showing a vaginal plug the next morning were sacrificed by cervical dislocation to collect oocytes and evaluate in vivo fertilization.

For the cumulus-intact IVF assay, superovulated females were sacrificed by cervical dislocation 14 h after the last injection. Cumuli oophori were collected and maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2 in Ferticult medium (FertiPro) supplemented with 3% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) under mineral oil (Sigma Aldrich).

For the zona-free IVF assay, oocytes were incubated 3–5 min at 37 °C with hyaluronidase (Sigma Aldrich) in M2 medium (Sigma Aldrich) to release cumulus cells, rinsed, and kept in Ferticult medium with 3% (w/v) BSA at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere under mineral oil. The zona pellucida was then dissolved with acidic Tyrode’s solution, pH 2.5 (Sigma Aldrich). Zona-free eggs were rapidly rinsed in Ferticult medium supplemented with 3% (w/v) BSA and kept at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 2–3 h to recover.

Cauda epididymides dissected from Tex51/Gm-KO or their WT littermate male mice aged 8–13 weeks were mechanically pressured to release mouse sperm. Spermatozoa were capacitated at 37 °C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 90 min in a 500-μl drop of Ferticult medium supplemented with 3% (w/v) BSA, under mineral oil.

Cumulus-intact or zona-free oocytes in a 100 μl drop of Ferticult medium, 3% (w/v) BSA were mixed in vitro with capacitated spermatozoa at a final concentration of 1 × 106/ml or 1 × 105/ml, respectively, for 3 h. Oocytes were then washed and mounted in Vectashield, including DAPI (Vector Laboratories) for observation under UV light (Zeiss Axioskop 20 microscope). Oocytes were considered fertilized when at least one fluorescent decondensed sperm head was observed in their cytoplasm.

Histology, staining and immunostaining of sperm and testes

Testes from adult WT and KO males were dissected and collected, fixed overnight in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde in PBS, dehydrated and embedded in paraffin. Serial 5-μm-thick sections were cut and stored at room temperature. Testis sections were rehydrated before hematoxylin-eosin staining.

Sperm were retrieved from dissected cauda epididymis by mechanical pressure in Ferticult medium and spread onto a Superfrost Plus slide (ThermoFisher Scientific). Sperm cells were fixed using 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min. For the Papanicolaou protocol, slides were washed in 95% ethanol and inserted in Harris hematoxylin (VWR) for 3 min for nucleus counterstaining, then washed, and stained with OG-6 dye (RAL diagnostics 05.12013) and with EA-50 (RAL diagnostics 05.12019) for 5 min. Slides were finally dehydrated (95% ethanol, absolute ethanol, and xylene) and mounted with a permanent mounting medium. For immunofluorescence staining, sperm suspensions (or smears) were permeabilized for 10 min with 0.02% (v/v) Triton X-100 in 1× PBS at room temperature, and were then saturated for 30 min in PBS, 3% (w/v) BSA, and 10% (v/v) goat serum. Afterward, samples were incubated for 1 h with either a rabbit polyclonal anti-SEPTIN4 antibody (HPA021587, Sigma Aldrich, 1/50 dilution) or a rat monoclonal anti-IZUMO1 antibody (clone 125, AB211626, Abcam, 1/100 dilution). The samples were washed three times in PBS, 1% (w/v) BSA. Samples were then incubated with secondary antibodies− anti-rabbit IgG-Alexa Fluor 488 or goat anti-rat IgG-Alexa Fluor 594 (ThermoFisher Scientific)—at a concentration of 10 µg/mL for 1 h at room temperature. After three additional washes, a drop of sperm suspension was smeared on a slide, air-dried and mounted with Vectashield containing DAPI (Eurobio Scientific). The characterization of the labeling phenotypes was performed thanks to a double-blind analysis of images.

Sperm motility analysis

Sperm parameters were assessed using the CEROS II apparatus (Hamilton Thorne). Briefly, mouse spermatozoa obtained by mechanical pressure from the cauda epididymis of Tex51/Gm-KO and their WT littermate male mice (8–10 weeks old) were capacitated at 37 °C under 5% CO2 atmosphere for 90 min in a 500 μl drop of Ferticult medium supplemented with 3% (w/v) BSA under mineral oil. The movements of at least 1000 sperm cells per sample were analyzed in 20-μm chambers (Leja Products B.V.) with a Zeiss AX10 Lab A1 microscope (10× objective) using standard settings. Images were analyzed using the HT CASAII software (Hamilton Thorne).

Transmission electron microscopy

Spermatozoa from WT and Tex51/Gm-KO males were prepared as described above and fixed by incubation in PBS supplemented with 2% (v/v) glutaraldehyde (Grade I, Sigma) for 2 h at room temperature. Sperm pellets were washed twice in PBS and post-fixed by incubation with 1% (w/v) osmium tetroxide (Electron Microscopy Sciences), then dehydrated by immersion in a graded series of alcohol solutions and embedded in Epon resin (Polysciences Inc). Ultra-thin sections (90 nm) were cut with a Reichert Ultracut S ultramicrotome (Reichert-Jung AG) and then stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Sections were analyzed with a JEOL 1011 transmission electron microscope, and digital images were acquired with a Gatan Erlangshen CCD camera and Digital Micrograph software. The integrity of sperm organelles was visually evaluated.

Statistics and reproducibility

Results are expressed as mean ± SEM of at least three independent experiments. For statistical analyses, two-tailed t-tests with or without Welch’s correction or with correction for multiple testing using the Bonferroni-Dunn method or Mann-Whitney tests were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.00 for Windows (GraphPad Software). Differences were considered statistically significant when the p-value was <0.05. All animal experiments were performed at least twice using independent biological samples. The data shown in the figures represent biological replicates.
