Efficacy and safety of low-molecular-weight heparin or rivaroxaban combined with immunotherapy and chemotherapy in the treatment of advanced nonsmall cell lung cancer: a prospective, randomized, controlled clinical study

Published: 2025

Introduction

Among cancers, lung cancer has the highest mortality rate worldwide. In 2021, the number of new lung cancer cases worldwide was approximately 2.2 million. Asia has the highest incidence rate of lung cancer, with 1.2 million cases [1]. China is a country with high incidence and mortality rates of lung cancer. In 2020, approximately 710,000 people died from lung cancer in China [2–4]. Lung cancer is classified into nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer on the basis of histological types and molecular features; among them, NSCLC accounts for approximately 80–85% of lung cancer cases [5]. Although traditional surgery combined with radiotherapy and chemotherapy can improve the survival of patients with lung cancer, the overall prognosis remains unsatisfactory, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 20% [6]. Thrombosis is the primary cause of death in outpatients with cancer undergoing chemotherapy, second only to the cancer itself [7]. The risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) is particularly high among patients with lung cancer [8]. Therefore, improving treatment regimens and preventing thrombosis in patients with NSCLC can reduce their risk of death.

The rapid development of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) has opened a new era of tumor immunotherapy. A previous study has shown that PD-1/PD-L1 monoclonal antibody ICIs improve the survival rate of patients with lung cancer [9]. According to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, PD-1/PD-L1 ICIs (e.g., pembrolizumab, atezolizumab, nivolumab, and cemiplimab) combined with platinum-based dual-agent chemotherapy are recommended for the first-line treatment of advanced NSCLC [10]. In addition, many PD-1/PD-L1 ICIs developed in China, including toripalimab, sintilimab, camrelizumab, tislelizumab, and sugemalimab, have been proved to improve patient prognosis and recommended as class I therapeutic drugs by the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology guidelines [11]. Currently, ICIs combined with chemotherapy are the standard first-line treatment for advanced NSCLC patients with driver gene negativity and are widely used in clinical practice. Some patients may experience persistent clinical responses from combination therapy. However, there are still some patients who do not benefit from ICI treatment.

Immune escape in the tumor microenvironment (TME) is considered an important obstacle to immunotherapy. According to recent studies, coagulation factors are involved in tumor immune escape and may promote the development of ICI resistance [12, 13]. Aside from prolonging their survival time through thrombin generation, tumor cells can also activate protease-activated receptors (PARs), especially protease‑activated receptor 2 (PAR2), which directly promotes tumor progression through tissue factor and coagulation factor VIIa or coagulation factor Xa (FXa) expressed by cancer cells [14, 15]. These findings have also been confirmed in preclinical studies, where FXa synthesized by myeloid cells inhibits antitumor immunity in the TME by activating PAR2, thereby directly promoting tumor progression [13]. Graf et al. found that anticoagulation and FXa inhibitors (FXa-i) may restore the efficacy of ICIs [13], and the potential mechanism may be that thrombin inhibitors restore antitumor immunity by blocking the release of transforming growth factor beta, increasing the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy [12]. In mouse tumor models, rivaroxaban monotherapy is comparable to ICIs in reducing tumor growth, and combination therapy has a synergistic effect on tumor inhibition. The synergistic effect of rivaroxaban and ICIs can increase antigen-activated CD137 + cytotoxic CD8 + T cells [13]. Currently, malignant tumors are prone to develop hypercoagulability and even thrombosis. The guidelines of the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) recommend that prophylactic anticoagulant therapy may be considered for all hospitalized patients in the department of internal medicine who are diagnosed with active tumors, especially those receiving chemotherapy [16]. According to a retrospective real-world cohort study, the clinical application of FXa-i can enhance the efficacy of ICI therapy by restoring antitumor immunity without increasing the risk of bleeding [17]. This finding was consistent with preclinical research findings. Research has shown that immunotherapy and chemotherapy can alter the TME, enhance immune function, and affect the distribution of T lymphocyte subsets in peripheral blood [18]. However, another retrospective study found that immunotherapy combined with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) treatment had no significant effect on overall survival (OS) in NSCLC. By contrast, the use of anticoagulants led to a trend toward shorter progression-free survival (PFS) [19].

Currently, studies evaluating the impact of anticoagulant drugs on the efficacy of immunotherapy are retrospective studies, and the results are inconsistent. In theory, anticoagulant therapy may improve the efficacy of immunotherapy. However, different anticoagulant drugs may have inconsistent effects on immunotherapy. Moreover, patients often do not receive a single immunotherapy in clinical practice. ICIs combined with chemotherapy are the first-line standard treatment for NSCLC, and the impact of different anticoagulant drugs on the current standard treatment regimen remains unclear. Therefore, the impact of different anticoagulant drugs on the antitumor efficacy of immunotherapy + chemotherapy regimens needs to be evaluated. The present study aimed to evaluate the antitumor effects of LMWH or rivaroxaban combined with immunotherapy and chemotherapy and their impact on patients’ immune function. The research results will provide new ideas for the clinical treatment of NSCLC and clinical basis for selecting appropriate anticoagulant drugs for patients with NSCLC.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

In this prospective, randomized, single-blind, controlled, single-center clinical trial, patients with advanced NSCLC who received treatment at Dianjiang County People’s Hospital in Chongqing from April 2022 to November 2024 were included. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Dianjiang County People’s Hospital in Chongqing (20220912). All patients signed informed consent forms. The study was registered on the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP) (ID: ChiCTR2500106653).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) All patients diagnosed with advanced NSCLC on the basis of the “Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Primary Lung Cancer (2018 Edition)” [20]; (2) Advanced NSCLC with TNM stage IIIB–IV confirmed by cell and histological analysis, without surgical indications; (3) There is imaging evidence to confirm that the patient has clear measurable or assessable lesions; (4) No other treatments were received prior to accepting the research protocol; (5) Individuals without severe center of gravity, liver, and kidney dysfunction; (6) Negative driver gene (EGFR, ALK, ROS1) test; and (7) 0–1 performance status score. Meanwhile, the exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with bone marrow hematopoietic dysfunction; (2) patients with allergic constitution and allergies to multiple drugs; (3) patients with poor medication adherence that affects efficacy evaluation; (4) patients who experience serious complications during the treatment process and are unable to continue receiving treatment; (5) patients with a history of thrombosis; (6) patients with central nervous system intracerebral hemorrhage, high-risk intracranial or spinal cord bleeding lesions, active bleeding, clinically significant chronic bleeding, or bleeding tendency; (7) patients with disseminated intravascular coagulation; (8) patients with acute venous thrombosis and those found to have thrombosis during admission examinations; and (9) patients who have been taking aspirin and clopidogrel orally for a long time because of underlying diseases.

Randomization and masking

Random numbers were generated using PEMS3.1 for Windows, employing the “completely randomized (two or more groups) design program” (with a total of 150 patients, three treatment groups, and equal number of cases in each group). All subjects who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were randomly divided into the control group, LMWH group, and rivaroxaban group at a ratio of 1:1:1. As this study had a single-blind design, the evaluators did not know what treatment the patients received. Because patients were treated with drugs with different administration routes and schedules, the patients and clinical doctors could not be blinded.

Procedures

All patients in the control group were treated with a combination of tislelizumab (200 mg/time, drip on the 1st day, with 21 days as one cycle) and chemotherapy. Lung adenocarcinoma was treated with pemetrexed (500 mg/m2, drip on the 1st day, with 21 days as one cycle) + carboplatin (drip dosage calculated based on AUC = 6; with 21 days as one cycle and medication administered on the first day of each cycle) chemotherapy regimen. Lung squamous cell carcinoma was treated with albumin paclitaxel (260 mg/m2, drip on the first day, with 21 days as one cycle) + carboplatin chemotherapy regimen. Lung squamous cell carcinoma was treated with a combination of albumin paclitaxel (260 mg/m2 infusion, 21 days as one course of treatment, administered on the first day of each course) and carboplatin chemotherapy regimen. The medication regimen for carboplatin was the same as that for lung adenocarcinoma. The patients in the LMWH group received 4000 IU/day of enoxaparin sodium injection in addition to the control treatment regimen. Subcutaneous injection was administered upon admission until discharge. Meanwhile, the patients in the rivaroxaban group were given rivaroxaban at a dose of 10 mg/day in addition to the control treatment regimen. Oral administration was started upon admission and continued until discharge.

Efficacy evaluation and data collection

Patients underwent complete physical examination, routine blood tests, blood biochemistry tests, coagulation function tests, tumor marker tests, and electrocardiogram one day before enrollment. Then, routine blood and blood biochemistry tests were rechecked weekly.

The efficacy was evaluated every two cycles by computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging according to the RECIST 1.1 criteria until disease progression (PD) or the subject withdraws from the study.

Adverse events (AEs) were assessed in accordance with the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events Version 3.0.

Immunocyte assay was performed as follows: Before treatment and after two cycles of treatment, flow cytometry (FACSCanto II, BD, USB) was used to detect CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, regulatory T (Treg) cells, and CD4+/CD8+ T cell ratio in whole blood. The antibodies used in flow cytometry in this study include: Anti-CD4 antibody (FlTC antihuman CD4, Clone: SK3, Catalog No.: 980802, BioLegend, San Diego, USA); Anti-CD8 antibody (PerCP antihuman CD8, Clone: SK1, Catalog No.: 980916, BioLegend, San Diego, USA); Anti-CD25 antibody (APC antihuman CD25, Clone: M-A251, Catalog No.: 985810, BioLegend, San Diego, USA); Anti-FoxP3 antibody (PE antihuman FOXP3 Antibody, Clone: 206D, Catalog No.: 320108, BioLegend, San Diego, USA).

Endpoint and definition

The primary endpoint was PFS, which was defined as the time from randomization to PD or death from any cause. The secondary endpoints included the objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), OS, safety, and quality of life. OS was defined as the time from randomization to death from any cause. ORR was defined as the percentage of subjects achieving complete response (CR) + partial response (PR) in each treatment group. DCR was defined as the percentage of subjects achieving CR + PR + stable disease (SD) in each treatment group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism and SPSS version 22.0. Two-tailed tests were used, and statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05. Confidence intervals (CI) were set at 95%. In the analysis of baseline data in each group, measurement data that conformed to normal distribution were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s). Independent samples t-test was used for between-group comparisons, whereas paired t-test was used for within-group comparisons. For nonnormally distributed data, nonparametric tests were used for analysis. Categorical variables were analyzed using χ2 tests or rank sum tests. PFS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and survival curves were compared between the two treatment groups. The hazard ratio (HR) and its confidence interval (CI) were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression.

Kaplan–Meier curve analysis of PFS

The median PFS was 8.5 months in the control group (95% CI: 7.6–9.4), 8.6 months (95% CI: 8.1–9.1) in the LMWH group, and 11.2 months (95% CI: 9.4–13.0) in the rivaroxaban group. Kaplan–Meier curve analysis showed no significant difference in PFS between the LMWH and control groups (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.041, 95% CI: 0.676–1.604; P = 0.852). The rivaroxaban group had significantly higher PFS than the control group (HR = 0.766, 95% CI: 0.623–0.967; P = 0.021) and LMWH group (HR = 0.582, 95% CI: 0.376–0.901; P = 0.013).

Comparison of therapeutic effects among the three groups

The ORR was 58.69% in the control group (CR = 2.17%, PR = 56.52%), 62.50% in the LMWH group (CR = 0%, PR = 62.50%), and 79.59% in the rivaroxaban group (CR = 4.08%, PR = 75.51%). The DCR was 73.91% in the control group, 79.17% in the LMWH group, and 87.75% in the rivaroxaban group. Meanwhile, the SD was 15.22% in the control group, 16.67% in the LMWH group, and 8.16% in the rivaroxaban group. No significant differences were observed in the ORR, CR, PR, DCR, or SD among the three groups (all P > 0.05). Discussion

In recent years, research on the correlation between the TME and tumor prognosis as well as immunotherapy has been a hot topic. Shengshan Xu et al. found that several genes are not only associated with the prognosis of cancer patients but also can reflect the immune characteristics of the TME, and can predict the response of tumor patients to immunotherapy or serve as targets for immunotherapy [21–23]. ICIs are used for the immunotherapy of patients with NSCLC. ICIs combined with chemotherapy have become the first-line standard treatment for advanced NSCLC patients with negative driver genes. However, the efficacy is still unsatisfactory in some patients [10]. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to explore the efficacy and safety of rivaroxaban or LMWH combined with ICI and chemotherapy in Chinese patients with NSCLC, and to evaluate the changes in peripheral blood immune function cells after two cycles of treatment. The results showed that the PFS was substantially better in the rivaroxaban group than in the LMWH and control groups. In addition, in the rivaroxaban group, the proportion of CD4 + T cells and Treg/CD4 + ratio was significantly improved after treatment compared with those before treatment. These results suggest that rivaroxaban combined with chemotherapy and immunotherapy improves PFS in patients with NSCLC, possibly because of the improvement of their immune function. Further studies are needed to explore the underlying mechanisms.

Patients with NSCLC are a high-risk population for tumor-associated thrombosis. Thrombosis not only affects patients’ quality of life, but may also promote tumor progression and metastasis by activating the coagulation cascade [24]. Coagulation factors may contribute to immune escape, thus promoting ICI resistance, which could be one of the potential causes for the poor efficacy [12, 13]. The NCCN and American Society of Clinical Oncology recommend that all adult inpatients with cancer receive anticoagulant therapy without contraindications [16]. Although the purpose of this recommendation is to prevent thrombosis, several studies have confirmed that anticoagulant therapy or FXa-i may restore the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) and enhance the antitumor immune activity of ICI [12, 13]. According to a previous study, FXa-activated PAR2 promotes anoikis resistance and immune escape in cancer, indicating that the combined use of coagulation inhibitors and PD-1/PD-L1 ICI may hold promise for enhancing the therapeutic efficacy in cancer [25]. In the present study, the proportion of CD4 + T cells in the rivaroxaban group was significantly higher after treatment than before treatment. Moreover, the Treg/CD4 + ratio was significantly lower after treatment than before treatment, suggesting that rivaroxaban may enhance the antitumor immune response through multiple mechanisms. The following are the potential reasons that explain why rivaroxaban can enhance the antitumor immune effect: First, rivaroxaban effectively prevents and treats tumor-related thrombosis by inhibiting factor Xa, thereby improving the overall health of patients and enhancing the tolerance and responsiveness to ICIs combined with chemotherapy [26]. Second, rivaroxaban enhances antitumor immunity by targeting macrophage FXa–PAR2 signaling, inhibiting thrombin-mediated inflammatory response to improve the TME, and enhancing immune cell infiltration and activity [27]. Third, rivaroxaban may further enhance the antitumor immune response of ICI by regulating immune cell function [27]. A previous study found that ICI therapy can unleash the immunosuppressive role of Treg cells [28]. Rivaroxaban can enhance antitumor immunity by decreasing the abundance of Treg cells and improving the TME [13]. This finding is consistent with the results of this study. Rivaroxaban may enhance the antitumor efficacy of ICIs combined with chemotherapy by regulating immune function. However, its specific mechanisms may be related to pretreatment immune function status or dynamic changes during treatment. Further studies are needed to explore and validate the potential mechanisms by which rivaroxaban combined with ICI + chemotherapy improves the antitumor therapeutic effect.

LMWH is a commonly used anticoagulant for patients with NSCLC. However, its efficacy may be limited by various factors, including the need for subcutaneous injection, short half-life, and large individual differences. In the present study, although the LMWH group had better PFS and ORR compared with the control group, the differences were not statistically significant. This finding may be related to the weak anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects of LMWH [29]. The rivaroxaban group had significantly better PFS than the other two groups. A retrospective real-world cohort study found that FXa-i combined with ICIs can enhance the efficacy of ICI treatment, significantly improve ORR, and prolong PFS and OS in patients with cancer [17]. Song et al. found that compared with LMWH, rivaroxaban can not only reduce the risk of composite VTE or major bleeding, but also bring survival benefits to patients with lung cancer, significantly reducing the all-cause mortality rate [30]. In the present study, although no statistically significant difference in ORR was observed in the rivaroxaban group compared with the other two groups, the numerical value was significantly higher. This finding indicates that the combination of rivaroxaban with ICI + chemotherapy has better benefits in improving the treatment effect and prolonging the PFS of patients with NSCLC.

In this study, the proportion of CD4 + T cells in the LMWH and rivaroxaban groups was significantly increased after treatment compared with that before treatment. Moreover, the Treg/CD4+ ratio significantly decreased after treatment compared with that before treatment. However, no significant differences were observed in the proportion of CD8 + cells and CD4+/CD8+ ratio before and after treatment. CD4 + T cells are helper T cells that enhance the antitumor immune response by secreting cytokines and coordinating immune reactions [31]. Treg cells expressing the transcription factor Foxp3 play an important role in maintaining immune homeostasis and preventing autoimmunity [32]. A previous study found that Treg cells are highly suppressive, can upregulate several immune checkpoints, and express certain specific marker molecules on the cell surface, which is associated with poor prognosis in patients with cancer [33]. Studies have shown that CD4 + T cells can mediate antitumor immunity by directly killing tumor cells, indirectly activating innate immune cells, or inhibiting tumor angiogenesis [34]. CD4 + T cells serve as central regulators of the immune response. On the one hand, the differentiation of CD4 + T subsets leads to the activation of the innate immune response, induction and proliferation of effector CD8 + T cells, and promotion of antibody production by B cells. On the other hand, skewing of CD4 + T cell differentiation toward incorrect subsets (e.g., suppressive Tregs) can severely affect the resolution of infections or cancer treatment [35]. In the rivaroxaban group, the proportion of CD4 + T cells was significantly higher after treatment than before treatment. This finding suggests that rivaroxaban may promote antitumor immunity by increasing the number of CD4 + T cells. However, in this study, there was no significant change in CD8 + cells before and after treatment in either the LMWH group or the Rivaroxaban group, which is inconsistent with previous studies. A study found that treatment with rivaroxaban can increase the number of tumor-killing CD8 + T cells, whereas treatment with heparin has no such effect [36]. The potential cause of this result may be related to chemotherapeutic drugs. A study has shown that during the proliferative burst mediated by PD-1 blockers, the concurrent use of chemotherapeutic drugs may inhibit the accelerated differentiation and self-renewal of stem-like T cells, ultimately leading to impairments in both the number and overall effector functions of CD8 + T cells [37]. In our study, similar changes in these immune cell counts were observed in the LMWH-treated group, yet their PFS did not significantly differ from that of the control group, unlike the rivaroxaban group. The potential reason may be related to the mechanism of action of LMWH. The main effects of LMWH are anticoagulation and improvement of microcirculation. Secondly, LMWH lacks the function of directly inhibiting tumor cell proliferation/metastasis. But FXa inhibitors can enhance the antitumor immune activity of ICIs and inhibit tumor cell growth [13, 17]. In addition, the small sample size of this study may also be one of the potential factors contributing to this result.

In terms of safety, no significant differences were observed among the rivaroxaban, LMWH, and control groups. This finding indicates that adding rivaroxaban to ICI combined with chemotherapy does not increase the risk of AEs.

In general, adding rivaroxaban to immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy for patients with NSCLC can significantly prolong the median PFS and improve the immune function of patients. In clinical practice, the use of rivaroxaban can not only reduce thrombosis risk in patients with NSCLC, but also improve the therapeutic effect of ICIs combined with chemotherapy, providing a new treatment option for these patients.

This study has some limitations. First, it is a single-center study with a small sample size, which may lead to selection bias. Second, although changes in immune functional cells in the peripheral blood of patients were detected, the relationship between these changes and immune cell infiltration in the TME was not further explored. Third, this study lacks information on tumor PD-L1 expression in patients. Although this study was a randomized trial, its relatively small sample size made it difficult to rule out the possibility of an imbalance in tumor PD-L1 status, which might have affected the analysis results. Lastly, since the dynamic changes and total count of immune cells in peripheral blood are not necessarily correlated with the status of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME), there are limitations in this study that relies solely on peripheral blood data to explain the antitumor effect and immune response. In the future, large-scale, multicenter, and randomized controlled studies need to be conducted to verify the results of the present study and explore the specific mechanisms by which rivaroxaban regulates immune functions. Future studies can focus on the following directions: further exploring the effects of rivaroxaban on immune cell infiltration and functions in the TME, performing biomarker-based research to screen out the population of patients with NSCLC who are most sensitive to rivaroxaban combined with ICI and chemotherapy, and exploring the efficacy and safety of the combined application of rivaroxaban and other immunotherapeutic drugs (e.g., CTLA-4 and PD-L1 inhibitors).

Conclusion

Rivaroxaban combined with ICI + chemotherapy has potential advantages in NSCLC treatment. It may enhance the antitumor efficacy by regulating immune functions, thereby prolonging the PFS of patients. Further studies are still needed to investigate the changes in immune cells in the TME before and after treatment, verify its mechanism of action, and explore the optimal application regimen.
