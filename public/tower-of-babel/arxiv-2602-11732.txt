Achieving EF1 and Epistemic EFX Guarantees Simultaneously
Hannaneh Akrami, Ryoga Mahara, Kurt Mehlhorn, Nidhi Rathi

Abstract
We study the fundamental problem of fairly dividing a set of indivisible goods among agents with additive valuations. Here, envy-freeness up to any good (EFX) is a central fairness notion and resolving its existence is regarded as one of the most important open problems in this area of research. Two prominent relaxations of EFX are envy-freeness up to one good (EF1) [ 21 ] and epistemic EFX (EEFX) [ 22 ] . While allocations satisfying each of these notions individually are known to exist even for general monotone valuations [ 41 , 7 ] , whether both can be satisfied simultaneously remains open for all instances in which the EFX problem is itself unresolved. In this work, we show that there always exists an allocation that is both EF1 (in fact, the stronger notion EFL) and EEFX for additive valuations, thereby resolving the primary open question raised in [ 7 ] and bringing us one step closer to resolving the elusive EFX problem. We introduce a new share-based fairness notion, termed strong EEFX share , which may be of independent interest and which implies EEFX feasibility of bundles. We show that this notion is compatible with EF1, leading to the desired existence result.



1
Introduction

Fair division is a central problem lying at the intersection of computer science, economics, and social choice theory, which has gained significant attention since the seminal work of Steinhaus, Banach, and Knaster
[
45
]
. Fairness considerations are central to the design of many social institutions and arise naturally in diverse allocation settings, including inheritance disputes, spectrum allocation, air traffic management, and public housing
[
43
]
.

In this work, we study the classic problem of
fairly
allocating a set
M
of
m
indivisible items among a set
N=\{1,\ldots,n\}
of
n
agents with
additive valuations

\{v_{i}\}_{i\in N}
over these items. An allocation is a partition
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
of
M
into
n
bundles, where agent
i
receives bundle
X_{i}
. Fairness notions broadly fall into two categories:
envy-based
and
share-based
. Under the envy-based view, an agent considers an allocation
fair
if no other agent receives a
significantly
better bundle than her. In contrast, under the share-based view, an agent considers an allocation
fair
if the value they receive is at least what they regard as a
fair share
, typically defined via a thought experiment that does not rely on comparisons with other agents.

1.1
Envy-Based Notions

In the case of allocating a divisible resource (the classic “
cake-cutting
” setting),
envy-freeness
is the canonical envy-based fairness notion. An agent
i
envies another agent
j
if
i
values
j
’s bundle strictly more than their own. An allocation is said to be
envy-free
(EF) if no agent envies any other agent. While EF allocations are guaranteed to exist for divisible resources
[
46
,
47
]
, even the simplest instance with two agents and a single indivisible item that both agents value positively rules out the existence of EF allocations—or of any cardinal approximation thereof—when resources are indivisible.

Envy-freeness up to any item (EFX)

[
23
]
is a natural and widely studied relaxation of envy-freeness for indivisible goods. We say that agent
i

strongly envies
agent
j
if there exists an item
g\in X_{j}
such that the envy of
i
toward
j
persists even after removing
g
from
X_{j}
. An allocation is
EFX
if no agent strongly envies another agent. Equivalently, for every pair of agents
i
and
j
, any envy that
i
has toward
j
disappears upon the removal of
any
single item from
j
’s bundle. The existence of EFX allocations is known only in very restricted settings; e.g., for a limited number of agents
[
44
,
25
,
20
,
2
]
, limited number of items
[
12
,
42
]
, and for special valuation functions
[
44
,
35
,
10
]
. Whether EFX allocations exist in general remains a major open problem in fair division theory. While resolving this question appears highly challenging, the existence of allocations satisfying weaker fairness guarantees has been established.

One such notion is
envy-freeness up to one item (EF1)

[
21
]
, which requires that whenever agent
i
envies agent
j
, removing some single item from
X_{j}
eliminates the envy. When agents have additive valuations, a simple round-robin algorithm produces an EF1 allocation
[
23
]
. Moreover, Lipton et al.
[
41
]
showed that when agents have monotone valuations, EF1 allocations always exist and can be computed in polynomial time.

Envy-freeness up to one less-preferred good (EFL)

[
17
]
is strictly more demanding fairness notion than EF1. It requires that for any pair
i
and
j
of agents, either
|X_{j}|\leq 1
or there is some
g\in X_{j}
, whose value for
i
is no more than the value of
X_{i}
such that
i
’s envy towards
j
disappears after the removal of
g
from
X_{j}
. It was introduced by Barman et al.
[
17
]
, who proved
the existence of EFL allocations when the agents’ valuations over resources are additive, which can be naturally generalized to monotone valuations.

Inspired by the
epistemic envy-freeness
notion introduced by Aziz et al.
[
15
]
, Caragiannis et al.
[
22
]
introduced the fairness notion of
epistemic EFX
(EEFX). Intuitively, EEFX requires that each agent’s bundle could be part of an EFX allocation from her own perspective. Given an allocation, agent
i
is said to be
EFX-satisfied
if they do not strongly envy any other agent. A bundle
B
is
EEFX feasible
for agent
i
if there exists an allocation
X
such that
X_{i}=B
and agent
i
is EFX-satisfied in
X
. Finally, an allocation
X
is
EEFX
if, for every agent
i
, the bundle
X_{i}
is EEFX feasible for
i
. Equivalently, for every agent
i
, there exists a redistribution of the items outside
X_{i}
under which agent
i
is EFX-satisfied. Caragiannis et al.
[
22
]
proved that EEFX allocations exist and can be computed in polynomial time for additive valuations. This result was complemented by Akrami and Rathi
[
7
]
, who established the existence of EEFX allocations for monotone valuations and showed that finding one is PLS-hard already for submodular valuations.

While the existence of allocations satisfying EF1 or EEFX individually is known even for general monotone valuations
[
41
,
7
]
, the existence of an allocation satisfying both notions simultaneously remains open for all settings in which the existence of EFX is itself open. As suggested by Akrami and Rathi
[
7
]
, the question

“Even for instances with additive valuations, does there always exist an allocation that is simultaneously EEFX and EF1?”

is therefore natural and intriguing, as it brings us one step closer to resolving the elusive EFX problem. At the same time, it probes the structural compatibility of two prominent relaxations of EFX. Observe that the existence of EFX allocations would immediately answer the above question in the affirmative, whereas a counterexample demonstrating the incompatibility of EF1 and EEFX would imply that EFX allocations do not always exist. In this work, we resolve this question affirmatively, even when EF1 is strengthened to the strictly stronger notion of EFL.

Theorem 1.1
.

Given a fair division instance
\mathcal{I}=(N,M,\{v_{i}\}_{i\in N})
with additive valuation functions, there exists an allocation
X
that is simultaneously EFL and EEFX.

We emphasize that our result is existential and can be translated into an exponential-time algorithm. Designing a polynomial-time algorithm for finding such allocations remains open even for four agents with additive valuations.

1.2
Share-Based Notions

Analogous to envy-freeness among envy-based notions,
proportionality
is the canonical share-based fairness notion for divisible resources. As with envy-freeness, proportionality can be guaranteed in the classic cake-cutting setting but fails to hold in general when resources are indivisible. Consequently, several relaxations have been proposed for indivisible goods, the most prominent of which is the
maximin share (MMS)

[
21
]
. The maximin share of an agent
i
denoted by
\mu_{i}
is the maximum value agent
i
can obtain by partitioning the set of items into
n
bundles and receiving the worst bundle. Formally, let
\Pi
be the set of all partitions of
M
into
n
bundles. Then,

\text{MMS}_{i}=\max_{P\in\Pi}\min_{j\in[n]}v_{i}(P_{j}).

For
\alpha\geq 0
, an allocation is called
\alpha
-MMS if every agent values their bundle at least
\alpha
times their MMS value. When
\alpha=1
, the allocation is simply called an MMS allocation. While MMS allocations are not always guaranteed to exist
[
40
]
, establishing the existence of approximate MMS allocations has been an active area of research (see
Section

1.5
). Caragiannis et al.
[
22
]
showed that MMS implies EEFX. Hence, EEFX can be viewed as a relaxation of both the purely share-based notion MMS and the purely envy-based notion EFX.

Another share-based notion that is highly related to the notion of EEFX is the
minimum EFX share
(MXS) introduced by Caragiannis et al.
[
22
]
. The MXS value of an agent
i
is the minimum value of an EEFX feasible bundle for her, and an allocation is said to be MXS if all agents value their bundle at least at their MXS value. Clearly, the existence of EEFX allocations implies the existence of MXS allocations.

Both envy-based and share-based approaches to fairness have inherent shortcomings. For instance, consider three agents and three pairs of shoes, where each agent receives a non-matching pair. In this allocation, no agent envies another, yet from a share-based perspective, the outcome is clearly unsatisfactory. Conversely, receiving a fair share does not preclude the possibility that another agent obtains a significantly more desirable bundle. It is therefore desirable to identify allocations that perform well with respect to both envy-based and share-based fairness criteria.

Initiated by Amanatidis et al.
[
12
]
, subsequent work has studied the compatibility of various share-based notions with different envy-based notions
[
27
,
6
]
. Particularly relevant to our work are results on the existence of allocations that are simultaneously EFL and MXS under additive valuations
[
14
,
31
]
.

1
illustrates the relationships among the fairness notions introduced so far for additive valuations.

1.3
Lone Divider Approach

When the goal is to achieve a share-based notion, typically an approximation of maximin share (
\alpha
-MMS), a celebrated technique is that of
lone divider

[
1
,
7
,
10
,
38
,
5
]
. For an agent
i\in N
, let us write
\alpha_{i}
to denote her fair share value. This algorithm runs in rounds, and in each round a subset of agents gets allocated one bundle each that they find fair according to their own fair share. These agents leave the process, and if there are still some agents remaining, the algorithm proceeds to satisfy them. Let
n^{\prime}
be the number of remaining agents at the beginning of some round of this algorithm. Let
i
be one such remaining agent. Then
i
divides the set of remaining items
M^{\prime}
into
n^{\prime}
bundles
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n^{\prime}}
each of value
\alpha_{i}
for
i
; we say that agent
i
has the role of the
lone divider
in this round. Then, a bipartite graph is constructed with one part corresponding to these
n^{\prime}
bundles and the other part corresponding to the remaining agents. There is an edge between an agent
j
and a bundle
B_{k}
, iff
v_{j}(B_{k})\geq\alpha_{j}
. A matching in this graph is called
envy-free
if there is no edge between unmatched agents and matched bundles, i.e., all the unmatched agents value all the matched bundles below their fair share. It can be proven that an envy-free matching always exists. Then, under one such matching, the matched agents will be allocated their matched bundles and leave the algorithm, and the algorithm proceeds to the next round. See
Algorithm

1
.

In order to prove the correctness of this algorithm, one needs to show that any remaining agent
i
can play the role of the lone divider. Equivalently, one needs to show that
i
can divide the set of remaining items into
n^{\prime}
many bundles of value at least
\alpha_{i}
(to agent
i
), assuming that
n-n^{\prime}
many bundles of value strictly less than
\alpha_{i}
have been removed in the previous rounds. Clearly, whether this holds or not depends on
\alpha_{i}
.

Akrami and Rathi
[
6
]
extended this technique to simultaneously accommodate for both envy-based and share-based guarantees. In particular, they prove the existence of partial allocations that are EFX and
2/3
-MMS which can be extended to complete allocations that are EF1 and
2/3
-MMS. In order to guarantee EFX, they introduce extra steps in each round of the algorithm. Namely, after
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n^{\prime}}
bundles are created by agent
i
, bundles
B^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,B^{\prime}_{n^{\prime}}
are created in a way that
B^{\prime}_{j}\subseteq B_{j}
is a minimal subset such that
B^{\prime}_{j}
has at least one adjacent edge, i.e., there exists an agent who would be happy upon receiving
B^{\prime}_{j}
. So far, the algorithm proceeded with respect to the remaining
n^{\prime}
-many agents. Now, the agents who had received a bundle in previous rounds are allowed to claim a bundle from
B^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,B^{\prime}_{n^{\prime}}
if they strongly envy one. If not, the algorithm proceeds as before. Otherwise, there exists an agent
\ell
who was previously allocated a bundle and now strongly envies some bundle
B^{\prime}_{j}
. Let
B^{\prime\prime}_{j}\subsetneq B^{\prime}_{j}
be minimal such that
B^{\prime\prime}_{j}
is envied by some agent
i^{*}
who was assigned
A_{i^{*}}
previously. This round of the algorithm takes
A_{i^{*}}
away from
i^{*}

and allocates
B^{\prime\prime}_{j}
to
i^{*}
. Then, we proceed to the next round with the set of remaining agents being the same, but the set of remaining items has lost
B^{\prime\prime}_{j}
and gained
A_{i^{*}}
. See
Algorithm

2
.

Feige
[
31
]
formalized this argument more generally and provided necessary and sufficient conditions for this algorithm to work. In particular, he defined the
residual maximin share

\text{RMMS}(M,v_{i},n)
of an agent
i
as the maximum value for which agent
i
can act as the lone divider (see Section
2
for a formal definition). We will write
\text{RMMS}_{i}
to denote
\text{RMMS}(M,v_{i},n)
when the context is clear. Note that, we have
\text{MXS}_{i}\leq\text{RMMS}_{i}\leq\text{MMS}_{i}
, by definition. An allocation is RMMS if all agents value their bundle at least as much as their RMMS value. Building on this, the extension suggested by Akrami and Rathi
[
6
]
proves the existence of partial allocations that are EFX and RMMS and complete allocations that are EF1 and RMMS.
Finally, note that Feige also observed that EF1 can be strengthened to EFL, yielding a slightly stronger guarantee.

1.4
Technical Contribution

At first glance, the lone divider technique might not seem helpful in proving EF1 and EEFX allocations, since EEFX is not a share-based notion. It seems not so clear how exactly one should categorize EEFX.
Unlike standard envy-based notions, EEFX feasibility of a bundle is independent of
how
the remaining items are allocated, a feature typically associated with share-based concepts. On the other hand, unlike classical share-based notions, EEFX feasibility is not determined solely by the
value
of a bundle. In particular, there may exist bundles
A
and
B
such that agent
i
values
A
more than
B
, yet
B
is EEFX feasible for
i
while
A
is not (see
Example

2.10
). This shows that while MXS can be guaranteed by the lone divider approach
[
31
]
and is compatible with EF1, it does not imply that the final allocation is EEFX.

To prove
Theorem

1.1
, we introduce a new share-based notion, termed as
strong EEFX share
, which may be of independent interest. While EEFX itself is not a share-based notion, the strong EEFX share captures the threshold beyond which EEFX feasibility becomes
monotone
. For an agent
i
, we define her
strong EEFX share
, denoted by
\theta_{i}
, as the minimum threshold value such that every bundle with value at least this threshold is EEFX feasible for
i
. Since MMS implies EEFX feasibility
[
22
]
, the strong EEFX share is upper-bounded by MMS value. By definition of MXS, we also know that strong EEFX share is lower-bounded by MXS value. Crucially, we show that the strong EEFX share has the necessary conditions for the lone divider approach to work, i.e., the strong EEFX share is at most the RMMS value for an agent (see Theorem
3.1
). Overall, for an agent
i
, we establish the following relation between her strong EEFX share and other share-based fair guarantees,

\text{MXS}_{i}\leq\theta_{i}\leq\text{RMMS}_{i}\leq\text{MMS}_{i}.

Using Theorem
3.1
, we obtain the existence of a partial EFX allocation, which can be extended to a complete EFL allocation, wherein every agent receives a bundle whose value is at least their strong EEFX share. By definition, this implies that the allocation is EEFX, thereby establishing the existence of allocations that are simultaneously EEFX and EFL (and hence, EF1) for additive valuations.

Furthermore,
Example

2.10
shows that the strong EEFX share is strictly stronger than the MXS share, thereby strengthening the result of
[
14
]
, who established the existence of EFL allocations guaranteeing every agent their MXS share.

1.5
Further Related Work

After
[
40
]
showed that MMS allocations are not guaranteed to exist, a sequence of works established the existence of
\alpha
-MMS allocations for additive valuations
[
11
,
33
,
19
,
32
,
30
,
3
,
4
,
36
]
, culminating in the current state of the art of
\alpha=7/9

[
37
]
.

Beyond MMS, several share-based fairness notions have been studied in discrete fair division. Proportionality up to one item (PROP1)
[
28
]
can be guaranteed simultaneously with Pareto optimality
[
18
]
, whereas proportionality up to any item (PROPx) is known not to be guaranteed to exist in the goods setting
[
16
]
.

Analogous to
\alpha
-MMS, one can define
\alpha
-EFX, which requires that

v_{i}(X_{i})\geq\alpha\cdot v_{i}(X_{j}\setminus{g})
for all agents
i,j
and all items
g\in X_{j}
. The existence of
\alpha
-EFX allocations has been established in a number of works
[
27
,
12
,
24
,
29
]
. While the existence of EFX allocations for four agents with additive valuations remains open,
[
39
]
proved the existence of EFX allocations for three classes of valuations. Furthermore, EF2X—a relaxation of EFX requiring that the removal of two items from an envied bundle eliminates any envy—is guaranteed to exist for restricted additive valuations
[
8
]
and for four agents with cancelable valuations, a class that strictly generalizes additive valuations
[
13
]
.

For a comprehensive discussion of these results and related literature, we refer the reader to the survey by
[
9
]
. A closely related and extensively studied line of work concerns the allocation of
chores
rather than goods; see
[
34
]
for a comprehensive overview.

2
Preliminaries

For any positive integer
k
, let
[k]
denote the set
\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
A
fair division instance
is given by
\mathcal{I}=(N,M,\{v_{i}\}_{i\in N})
, where
N=[n]
is a set of
n
agents,
M
is a set of
m
indivisible items, and for any agent
i\in N
,
v_{i}:2^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
denotes their valuation function over the set of items.
For all
i\in N
, we assume
v_{i}
is
normalized
,
i.e.,
v_{i}(\emptyset)=0
, and
monotone
, i.e., for all
i\in N
,
g\in M
and
S\subseteq M
,

v_{i}(S\cup\{g\})\geq v_{i}(S)
.
Moreover, we say a valuation function
v
is
additive
if for all subsets
S\subseteq M
, we have
v(S)=\sum_{g\in S}v(\{g\})
.
Unless stated otherwise, we assume that valuations are additive.

An
allocation

X=(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})
of the items among agents is a partition of items into
n
bundles
such that bundle
X_{i}
is allocated to agent
i
. That is, we have
X_{i}\cap X_{j}=\emptyset
for all

i,j\in N
with
i\neq j
and
\bigcup_{i\in[n]}X_{i}=M
. In case
\bigcup_{i\in[n]}X_{i}\subset M
, we call the allocation as
partial
. Whenever not specified, the allocation is
complete
; i.e.,
\bigcup_{i\in[n]}X_{i}=M
.

We introduce standard fairness notions such as EF1, EFL and EFX.

Definition 2.1

(EF1 (Envy-freeness up to one good)
[
21
]
)
.

An allocation
X
is
envy-free up to one good (EF1)
if for all agents
i,j\in N
,
either
v_{i}(X_{i})\geq v_{i}(X_{j})
, or there exists an item
g\in X_{j}
such that
v_{i}(X_{i})\ \geq\ v_{i}(X_{j}\setminus\{g\}).

Definition 2.2

(EFL (Envy-freeness up to one less-preferred good)
[
17
]
)
.

An allocation
X
is
envy-free up to one less-preferred good (EFL)
if for all agents
i,j\in N
,
at least one of the following conditions holds:

1.

|\{g\in X_{j}\mid v_{i}(\{g\})>0\}|\leq 1
,

2.

there exists an item
g\in X_{j}
such that

v_{i}(X_{i})\geq v_{i}(X_{j}\setminus\{g\})
and

v_{i}(X_{i})\ \geq\ v_{i}(\{g\})
.

Definition 2.3

(EFX (Envy-freeness up to any good)
[
23
]
)
.

An allocation
X
is
envy-free up to any good (EFX)
if for all agents
i,j\in N
,
for any item
g\in X_{j}
, we have
v_{i}(X_{i})\ \geq\ v_{i}(X_{j}\setminus\{g\}).

Note that under additive valuations, EFX implies EFL, and EFL implies EF1.

Given an agent
i
with bundle
B
, we say
i

strongly envies
a bundle
B^{\prime}
if there exists a good
g\in B^{\prime}
such that
v_{i}(B)<v_{i}(B^{\prime}\setminus\{g\})
. Equivalent to
Definition

2.3
, an allocation is EFX, if and only if there exists no strong envy from any agent towards a bundle of another agent.

Definition 2.4

(EFX Partition)
.

A partition
(P_{1},\ldots,P_{k})
of
M
into
k
bundles is EFX for agent
i
if
v_{i}(P_{j})\geq v_{i}(P_{j^{\prime}}\setminus\{g\})
for all
j,j^{\prime}\in[k]
and
g\in P_{j^{\prime}}
.

Plaut and Roughgarden
[
44
]
proved that when agents have identical monotone valuations, an EFX allocation exists.
Equivalently, for any agent
i
, there exists an EFX partition of
M
into
n
bundles for agent
i
.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
44
]
)
.

For an agent
i
with monotone valuation, there exists an EFX partition of
M
into
n
bundles for agent
i
.

Next, we introduce fairness concepts related to epistemic EFX (EEFX), another important relaxation of EFX.

Definition 2.6

(EEFX feasibility)
.

A bundle
B\subseteq M
is
EEFX feasible
for an agent
i
iff there exists a partition of
M\setminus B
into
n-1
bundles
P_{1},\ldots,P_{n-1}
such that
v_{i}(B)\geq v_{i}(P_{j}\setminus\{g\})
for all
j\in[n-1]
and
g\in P_{j}
. If
B
is EEFX feasible for agent
i
, such a partition
P=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n-1})
is called as an
EEFX certificate
for
B
. Similarly,
B
is
EEFX infeasible
for agent
i
iff no such partition exists.

Definition 2.7

(EEFX (Epistemic envy-freeness up to any good)
[
22
]
)
.

An allocation
X
is
epistemic EFX (EEFX)
if for every agent
i\in N
, the bundle
X_{i}
is EEFX feasible for
i
.

Definition 2.8

(MXS (Minimum EFX share)
[
22
]
)
.

For any agent
i\in N
, the minimum EFX share of
i
is

\text{MXS}_{i}=\min\{v_{i}(S)\mid S\text{ is EEFX feasible for $i$}\}.

The
strong EEFX share
is a key new concept in this paper, that we define next.

Definition 2.9

(Strong EEFX Share)
.

For any agent
i\in N
, the
strong EEFX share
of
i
is

\theta_{i}=\min\{v_{i}(S)\mid\text{ any set $T$ with $v_{i}(T)\geq v_{i}(S)$ is EEFX feasible for $i$}\}.

Note that since
M
is EEFX feasible for any agent
i
, the strong EEFX share is well-defined. Moreover, it is easy to observe that
\theta_{i}\geq\text{MXS}_{i}
for any agent
i
.

Example 2.10
.

We present an example to highlight the fact that EEFX feasibility is not monotone in bundle-value, and hence, motivating the definition of the strong EEFX share. We further show that
\theta_{i}
can be strictly higher than
\text{MXS}_{i}
, making the former a stronger fairness guarantee than the latter.

:

2.10

1
, using the same color coding. This partition shows that
X_{1}=\{g_{1},g_{2}\}

Consider an instance
\mathcal{I}=(N,M,\{v_{i}\}_{i\in N})
with
N=[3]
,
M=\{g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{10}\}
. Let

]

If an allocation satisfying both EF1 and EEFX exists for all non-degenerate instances, then such an allocation exists for all instances.

Proof.

Let
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
be a set of additive valuation functions, possibly degenerate, and let

be the set of items. Without loss of generality, we may assume

4
Future Directions

While we establish the existence of allocations that are simultaneously EF1 and EEFX for agents with additive valuations, many related problems remain open. We conclude by proposing several new open questions in this direction.

Monotone valuations.

While the existence of EF1 and EEFX allocations is known individually for monotone valuations, establishing the existence of allocations that simultaneously satisfy both fairness notions for any valuation class more general than additive remains an interesting open problem.

Chores.

The same problem can be asked for the setting consisting of chores, where agents have non-positive utility over the set of items.

Complexity of finding EF1 + EEFX allocations.

Our proof is existential and can be translated into an exponential-time algorithm for finding such allocations. While EF1 and EEFX allocations can each be computed in polynomial time for additive valuations, determining the computational complexity of finding allocations that satisfy both fairness guarantees simultaneously remains an intriguing open direction.

Strong EEFX share vs. MMS

It would be interesting to establish any meaningful relations between the strong EEFX share and the well-known share-based guarantee of MMS. Since MMS implies EEFX feasiblity
[
22
]
, the strong EEFX share is clearly upper-bounded by MMS. Can we lower-bound the strong EEFX share by
\alpha
times MMS for some
0<\alpha<1
?
