Convergence and turnpike properties of linear-quadratic mean field control problems with common noise
Erhan Bayraktar Jiamin Jian

Abstract
We investigate convergence and turnpike properties for linear-quadratic mean field control problems with common noise. Within a unified framework, we analyze a finite-horizon social optimization problem, its mean field control limit, and the corresponding ergodic mean field control problem. The finite-horizon problems are characterized by coupled Riccati differential equations, whereas the ergodic problem is addressed via a Bellman equation on the Wasserstein space, which reduces to a system of stabilizing algebraic Riccati equations. By deriving estimates for these Riccati systems, we establish a turnpike property for the finite-horizon mean field control problem and obtain quantitative convergence results from the social optimization problem to its mean field limit and the associated ergodic control problem.



1
Introduction

Large-population stochastic systems arise in a wide range of applications where a large number of interacting agents are exposed to both idiosyncratic sources of uncertainty and common external shocks. When the agents act cooperatively, a natural modeling paradigm is social optimization: a central planner coordinates the controls of all agents so as to minimize a global performance criterion.

In this paper we study linear-quadratic (LQ) social optimization problems with common noise, together with their mean field limits and long-time behavior. The presence of common noise, representing external shocks shared by all agents, induces conditional dependence across the whole population in the large-population limit and leads to control problems driven by conditional McKean–Vlasov dynamics. Even in the LQ setting, this feature produces coupled Riccati systems whose solvability, stability, and asymptotic properties require careful analysis.

We investigate three closely related control problems within a unified framework: (i) a finite-horizon social optimization problem for an
N
-agent system; (ii) the associated finite-horizon mean field control problem, obtained as the large-population limit; and (iii) an ergodic mean field control problem, which captures the long-time behavior of socially optimal policies. The main objectives of this work are twofold. First, under a common set of assumptions, we establish well-posedness and provide explicit characterizations of the optimal controls for all three control problems. Second, we quantify the connections between them by studying both large-population convergence and turnpike phenomena, namely the convergence of finite-horizon mean field control problems toward their ergodic counterparts as the time horizon becomes large.

Although each of these problems has been studied individually in the literature, a unified analysis linking social optimization, mean field control, and ergodic control—particularly in the presence of multiplicative common noise—has not yet been fully developed. Our present work aims to address this gap.

1.1
Relevant works

1.1.1
Social optimization problems

Social optimization problems, in which a population of interacting agents cooperates to optimize a collective objective, arise in diverse applications such as communication networks
[
27
]
and social welfare systems
[
39
]
. In large-population settings with weak interactions, such problems naturally lead to mean field approximations.

In the linear-quadratic framework, early work by Huang, Caines, and Malhamé
[
29
]
established asymptotic team-optimal solutions for mean field social optimization problems under additive noise. Subsequent extensions addressed non-uniform agents
[
16
]
, indefinite state weights and cost functionals
[
30
,
51
]
, and additional sources of randomness
[
52
]
. In particular, Huang and Yang
[
30
]
analyzed asymptotic solvability of finite-horizon LQ mean field social optimization problems, while Wang and Zhang
[
51
]
studied infinite-horizon variants. Despite these developments, most existing results focus either on finite-horizon or infinite-horizon settings separately and typically treat common noise in a restricted form. A unified analysis connecting finite-population social optimization, its mean field limit, and the long-time behavior under general common noise remains relatively limited.

1.1.2
Mean field optimal control problems

Mean field control problems arise as infinite-population limits of social optimization problems and provide a tractable alternative to high-dimensional centralized control. Foundational contributions include the works of Nourian, Caines, Malhamé, and Huang
[
41
]
, as well as the monograph by Bensoussan, Frehse, and Yam
[
10
]
, which established the basic formulation and analytical framework of mean field control.

Subsequent theoretical developments have employed both the stochastic maximum principle
[
1
,
11
]
and dynamic programming approaches
[
42
]
. Infinite-horizon mean field control has also been investigated, for instance in
[
9
]
, via the solvability of the associated infinite-horizon McKean–Vlasov forward-backward stochastic differential equations (SDEs). In the linear-quadratic setting, Yong
[
55
]
derived explicit solutions through forward-backward SDEs and Riccati equations, with further extensions addressing non-homogeneous terms
[
48
,
35
]
, random coefficients
[
53
]
, and infinite-horizon control problems
[
28
]
.

In the presence of common noise, the limiting state dynamics become conditional McKean–Vlasov processes. Pham and Wei
[
42
]
established a general dynamic programming principle for McKean–Vlasov control problems and illustrated its application to the LQ setting. Moreover,
[
34
]
proved global well-posedness of the associated Hamilton–Jacobi equation for an LQ mean field control problem with an additive common noise, where the dependence on the mean field terms can be non-convex. For the more general framework considering the optimal control of McKean–Vlasov dynamics, we refer to works
[
33
,
22
,
4
,
43
]
and references therein.

1.1.3
Turnpike property

The turnpike property refers to the phenomenon that, for optimal control problems over long time horizons, optimal controls and state trajectories remain close to those of a corresponding stationary or ergodic problem for most of the time interval, except near the temporal boundaries. This concept originates in economic theory, dating back to von Neumann
[
40
]
and later developments by Dorfman, Samuelson, and Solow
[
23
]
, and has become a fundamental principle in optimal control.

In deterministic control, turnpike properties have been extensively studied for both finite- and infinite-dimensional systems, encompassing both discrete-time and continuous-time formulations; see, for instance
[
44
,
20
,
49
,
26
]
and the references therein. These works establish turnpike behavior under various dissipativity and stability conditions. A comprehensive and up-to-date survey on turnpike theory and its various applications is presented in
[
50
]
.

In contrast, turnpike properties in stochastic optimal control remained largely unexplored until the recent breakthrough by Sun, Wang, and Yong
[
45
]
, who established the first rigorous turnpike results for linear-quadratic stochastic control problems. This work initiated a systematic study of stochastic turnpike phenomena, leading to further developments in settings with periodic coefficients
[
47
]
, and more general stochastic dynamics
[
19
,
38
,
37
]
.
In addition, by introducing the so-called probability cell problem, the work
[
32
]
establishes a connection between the cell problem and the ergodic control problem, and reveals turnpike properties of LQ stochastic optimal control problems from multiple perspectives.

More recently, attention has turned to turnpike behavior in mean field control problems. In particular, Sun and Yong
[
46
]
extended their earlier work
[
45
]
to mean field linear stochastic systems, establishing a turnpike property under the stabilizability assumption on the homogeneous state equation. In a related development,
[
8
]
derived the turnpike property for finite-horizon linear-quadratic mean field control problems via the analysis of the associated ergodic control problem. On the other hand, driven by the growing interest in understanding long-term equilibrium behavior in large interacting particle systems, the study of turnpike properties in multi-player differential games (see
[
18
]
) and mean field games
[
12
,
13
,
17
]
has emerged as a rapidly advancing field.

Despite these advances, turnpike results for mean field control problems with common noise, particularly when the common noise enters the dynamics multiplicatively, remain scarce. The conditional nature of the limiting dynamics and the resulting coupled Riccati systems pose additional analytical challenges.

1.1.4
Convergence of mean field control problem

A fundamental question in mean field control theory concerns the quantitative convergence of finite-population social optimization problems toward their mean field limits. Such results provide a rigorous justification for using mean field controls as approximations of centralized policies in large-population systems. Convergence of controlled large-population systems to their mean field limits has been studied under various regularity and convexity assumptions, using probabilistic, PDE-based, and variational techniques; see, for example
[
33
,
15
,
25
,
2
,
14
,
34
]
. Recently, Daudin, Delarue, and Jackson
[
21
]
derived two optimal convergence rates for value functions from
N
-particle systems to the corresponding mean field control problems, identifying regimes corresponding to central limit scaling and dimension-dependent rates. Moreover, Bayraktar, Ekren, and Zhang
[
7
]
established a convergence rate for particle approximations of a class of second-order PDEs on the Wasserstein space, which arise in mean field control when the control of the common noise is state-independent and in stochastic control with partial observation.

While most of the literature treats only the finite-horizon control problems, fewer works address convergence in regimes where both the population size and the time horizon are large. The present paper contributes to this direction by establishing convergence results that jointly capture the dependence on the population size and the time horizon.

1.2
Contributions and organization of the paper

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

•

Unified framework and solvability results
. We study linear-quadratic social optimization problems for large-population stochastic systems with common noise, and analyze the finite-horizon social optimization problem, its mean field control limit, and the associated ergodic mean field control problem within a single framework. Our model accommodates multiplicative common noise and a general coupling structure that is not covered by existing works. Under a common set of assumptions, we establish solvability for all three control problems; see Proposition
3.6
, Proposition
4.1
, and Theorem
5.7
. In particular, the finite-horizon social optimization and mean field control problems are characterized via coupled Riccati systems, while the ergodic mean field control problem is solved through a Bellman equation on the Wasserstein space and a system of stabilizing algebraic Riccati equations.

•

Ergodic analysis under common noise
.
We introduce appropriate stabilizability conditions for the ergodic mean field control problem with multiplicative common noise and prove a verification theorem via the associated Bellman equation on the Wasserstein space, see Theorem
5.7
. The analysis relies on moment estimates in Lemma
5.5
and the identification of invariant distribution in Lemma
5.6
for the optimal state process.

•

Turnpike properties
. We derive quantitative estimates comparing finite-horizon and ergodic mean field control problems and establish a turnpike property in Theorem
6.3
showing that, away from the temporal boundaries, the optimal pair of the finite-horizon mean field control problem remains close to its ergodic counterpart. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first turnpike result for linear-quadratic mean field control with multiplicative common noise. Moreover, a turnpike property in terms of value functions also presented in our work, see Corollary
6.4
.

•

Large-population convergence
. We prove quantitative convergence results from the social optimization problem to the mean field control problem, both at the level of optimal pairs and value functions, see Theorem
7.2
and Proposition
7.4
, respectively. Moreover, we obtain convergence estimates that explicitly capture the joint dependence on the population size and the time horizon in Corollary
7.3
and Proposition
7.5
, thereby linking finite-horizon and
ergodic regimes.

2
Problem setup

In this section, we present the three control problems central to our analysis: the finite-horizon control problem for large-population systems, its finite-horizon mean field limit, and the associated ergodic mean field control problem describing the long-time regime.

2.1
Finite-horizon control problem for large population systems

Let
(\widetilde{\Omega},\widetilde{{\cal F}},\widetilde{\mathbb{P}})
be a complete filtered probability space satisfying the usual conditions, and let
\{W^{i}(\cdot):i=0,1,\dots,N\}
be
N+1
independent one-dimensional standard Brownian motions defined on it. For each
i\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}
, let
\{{\cal F}^{i}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
denote the augmented filtration generated by

\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}
as the complete, right-continuous augmentation of the filtration
\{\vee_{i=1}^{N}{\cal F}^{i}_{t}\vee{\cal F}^{0}_{t}\vee\widetilde{{\cal F}}_{0}\}_{t\geqslant 0}

\widetilde{{\cal F}}_{0}
is the
\sigma
-algebra containing all the Borel set on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Assume that
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
is atomless on
\widetilde{{\cal F}}_{0}
. The expectation
\mathbb{E}^{\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}}
on the space
(\widetilde{\Omega},\widetilde{{\cal F}},\widetilde{\mathbb{P}})
is denoted by
\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}
for simplicity.

For each
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}

\xi^{i}\in L^{4}_{\widetilde{{\cal F}}_{0}}

N

\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\hskip 57.81621pt+\big\{CX^{i,N}(t)+\bar{C}X^{(N)}(t)+Du^{i,N}(t)+\sigma\big\}dW^{i}(t)\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\hskip 57.81621pt+\big\{\Gamma X^{i,N}(t)+\bar{\Gamma}X^{(N)}(t)+\gamma\big\}dW^{0}(t),\quad t\geqslant 0,\\
X^{i,N}(0)=\xi^{i},\end{cases}

In the
N
-agent system (
2.1

W^{i}(\cdot)

W^{0}(\cdot)
is the Brownian motion representing a common noise shared by all agents.

T>0
denote a large time horizon and

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{c}[0,T]:=\Big\{u(\cdot):[0,T]\times\widetilde{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\Bigm|u(\cdot)\hbox{ is $\widetilde{\mathbb{F}}$-progressively measurable, and}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}|u(t)|^{2}dt\Big]<\infty\Big\}.

We refer to

as the centralized admissible control set for the social optimization problem.

Let

be constant matrices, and let

be constant vectors. Given the state dynamics (

), we introduce the following quadratic cost functional for the

J_{T}^{i}(\bm{\xi};{\bm{u}}(\cdot)):=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}f\big(X^{i,N}(t),X^{(N)}(t),u^{i,N}(t)\big)dt\Big],

(2.2)

where
\bm{\xi}=(\xi^{1},\dots,\xi^{N})
collects the initial states,
{\bm{u}}(\cdot)=(u^{1,N}(\cdot),\dots,u^{N,N}(\cdot))
is the collection of control from all agents, and
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is the running cost function defined as following

\displaystyle f(x,\bar{x},u)

\displaystyle:=\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}Q&S^{\top}\\
S&R\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}x\\
u\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle+2\left\langle\begin{bmatrix}q\\

(2.3)

The objective of the

2.1
) and the cost functional (
2.2
) for each agent. Define the social cost by

J^{\text{soc}}_{T}(\bm{\xi};{\bm{u}}(\cdot)):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}J^{i}_{T}(\bm{\xi};{\bm{u}}(\cdot)).

The finite-horizon stochastic optimal control problem for the
N
-agent system is then formulated as follows.

Problem (SO-N)
T
.
For a given collection of initial states
\bm{\xi}=(\xi^{1},\dots,\xi^{N})
with
\xi^{i}\in L^{4}_{\widetilde{{\cal F}}_{0}}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}

{\bm{u}}_{T}(\cdot)=(u_{T}^{1,N}(\cdot),\dots,u_{T}^{N,N}(\cdot))

V^{\text{soc}}_{T}(\bm{\xi}):=J_{T}^{\text{soc}}(\bm{\xi};{\bm{u}}_{T}(\cdot))=\inf_{\bm{u}(\cdot)\in(\mathscr{U}_{c}[0,T])^{N}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}J_{T}^{i}(\bm{\xi};{\bm{u}}(\cdot)),

(2.4)

where
(\mathscr{U}_{c}[0,T])^{N}:=\mathscr{U}[0,T]\times\dots\times\mathscr{U}[0,T]
.

If there exists a control
{\bm{u}}_{T}(\cdot)=(u_{T}^{1,N}(\cdot),\dots,u_{T}^{N,N}(\cdot))\in(\mathscr{U}_{c}[0,T])^{N}
attaining the infimum in (
2.4
), then
Problem (SO-N)
T
is said to be open-loop solvable, and
{\bm{u}}_{T}(\cdot)
is referred to as an open-loop optimal control for social optima. The corresponding open-loop optimal state process is denoted by
X_{T}^{i,N}(\cdot):=X^{i,N}(\cdot\,;\bm{\xi},{\bm{u}}_{T}(\cdot))

2.2
Finite-horizon mean field control problem

In this subsection, we introduce the corresponding mean field control problem under finite-horizon. Let
(\Omega,{\cal F},\mathbb{P})
be a complete filtered probability space which can support two independent one-dimensional standard Brownian motions
W(\cdot)
and
W^{0}(\cdot)
. Here,
W(\cdot)
represents the idiosyncratic noise and
W^{0}(\cdot)
represents the common noise. Let
\{\mathcal{F}^{W}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
and
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}^{0}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
be the augmented filtrations generated by
W(\cdot)
and
W^{0}(\cdot)
, respectively. We define
\mathbb{F}
as the complete, right-continuous augmentation of the filtration
\{{\cal F}^{W}_{t}\vee{\cal F}^{0}_{t}\vee{\cal F}_{0}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
, where
{\cal F}_{0}
is the
\sigma
-algebra containing all the Borel set on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and we assume that
\mathbb{P}
is atomless on
\mathcal{F}_{0}
. The expectation on the space
(\Omega,{\cal F},\mathbb{P})
is denoted by
\mathbb{E}
.

It is well-known that as the population size
N
grows to infinity, the dynamics (
2.1
) are formally approximated by the following linear SDE with conditional mean field term

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle dX(t)=\big\{AX(t)+\bar{A}\mathbb{E}\big[X(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]+Bu(t)+b\big\}dt\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\hskip 57.81621pt+\big\{CX(t)+\bar{C}\mathbb{E}\big[X(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]+Du(t)+\sigma\big\}dW(t)\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\hskip 57.81621pt+\big\{\Gamma X(t)+\bar{\Gamma}\mathbb{E}\big[X(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]+\gamma\big\}dW^{0}(t),\quad t\geqslant 0,\\
X(0)=\xi\sim\mu_{0},\end{cases}

(2.5)

where
\xi\in L^{4}_{{\cal F}_{0}}
has law
\mu_{0}\in{\cal P}_{4}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and is independent of
W(\cdot)
and
W^{0}(\cdot)
. The corresponding cost functional for the mean field control problem when
N\to\infty
is

J_{T}(\mu_{0};u(\cdot)):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}f\big(X(t),\mathbb{E}\big[X(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big],u(t)\big)dt\Big].

(2.6)

Define the admissible control set on
[0,T]
by

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}[0,T]:=\Big\{u(\cdot):[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}\Bigm|u(\cdot)\hbox{ is $\mathbb{F}$-progressively measurable, and}

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}|u(t)|^{2}dt\Big]<\infty\Big\}.

We can now formulate the corresponding finite-horizon mean field control problem as following:

Problem (MFC)
T
.
For a given random variable
\xi\in L^{4}_{{\cal F}_{0}}
with
\xi\sim\mu_{0}\in{\cal P}_{4}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, under the dynamic process (
2.5
) and the cost functional (
2.6
), find an open-loop optimal control

such that

U_{T}(\mu_{0}):=J_{T}\big(\mu_{0};u_{T}(\cdot)\big)=\inf_{u(\cdot)\in\mathscr{U}[0,T]}J_{T}(\mu_{0};u(\cdot)).

(2.8)

Similarly, if there exists a control
u_{T}(\cdot)\in\mathscr{U}[0,T]
that satisfies (
2.8
), we say that
Problem (MFC)
T
is open-loop solvable, and
u_{T}(\cdot)
is called an open-loop optimal control. The corresponding open-loop optimal state process is denoted by
X_{T}(\cdot):=X(\cdot\,;\xi,u_{T}(\cdot))
.
The mapping
U_{T}(\cdot):{\cal P}_{4}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as the value function of
Problem (MFC)
T
, and
(X_{T}(\cdot),u_{T}(\cdot))
is referred to as an open-loop optimal pair.

2.3
Ergodic mean field control problem

On the other hand, when the time horizon
T
is large, it is natural to compare the finite-horizon mean field control problem with its ergodic counterpart, which is commonly used as a proxy for the long-time regime. It is proved that the optimal pair for the finite-horizon control problem satisfies a turnpike property, which demonstrates that solutions to the finite-horizon control problem remain exponentially close to those of the ergodic control problem except near the temporal
boundaries.

We now introduce the ergodic mean field control problem:

Problem (EMFC).
For a given random variable
\xi\in L^{4}_{{\cal F}_{0}}
with
\xi\sim\mu_{0}\in{\cal P}_{4}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, under the dynamic process (
2.5
), determine a pair
(U(\cdot),c)
and
\bar{u}(\cdot)\in\mathscr{U}
such that

\displaystyle c:=\inf_{u(\cdot)\in\mathscr{U}}\mathop{\overline{\rm lim}}_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\big[f\big(X(t),\mathbb{E}\big[X(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big],u(t)\big)\big]dt,

(2.9)

\displaystyle U(\mu_{0}):=J_{\infty}(\mu_{0};\bar{u}(\cdot))=\inf_{u(\cdot)\in\mathscr{U}}J_{\infty}(\mu_{0};u(\cdot)),

where

J_{\infty}(\mu_{0};u(\cdot)):=\mathop{\overline{\rm lim}}_{T\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\big[f\big(X(t),\mathbb{E}\big[X(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big],u(t)\big)-c\big]dt,

and

\mathscr{U}
is a proper class of admissible control to be defined later (see Definition
5.4
).

If
\bar{u}(\cdot)\in\mathscr{U}
exists, it is called an optimal control, and the associated state process
\bar{X}(\cdot):=X(\cdot\,;\xi,\bar{u}(\cdot))
is called an optimal state. The mapping
U(\cdot)
is referred to as the relative value function of
Problem (EMFC)
, the constant
c
is called the optimal ergodic cost, and
(\bar{X}(\cdot),\bar{u}(\cdot))
is termed an optimal pair. A solution to
Problem (EMFC)
is defined as a 4-tuple
\{U(\cdot),c,\bar{X}(\cdot),\bar{u}(\cdot)\}
.

3
Finite-horizon control problem for large population systems

In this section, we establish the solvability of
Problem (SO-N)
T
in (
2.4
) through the corresponding system of Riccati equations by using an analytical approach.

For the simplicity of notations, we denote that

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{X}}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}X^{1,N}(t)\\
\vdots\\
X^{N,N}(t)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn},\quad{\bm{u}}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}u^{1,N}(t)\\
\vdots\\
u^{N,N}(t)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nm},

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{A}}=\text{diag}[A,\dots,A]\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nn},\quad\bar{{\bm{A}}}=\bm{1}_{N\times N}\otimes\frac{\bar{A}}{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nn}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{B}}^{i}=e_{i}^{N}\otimes B\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times m},\quad{\bm{b}}=\bm{1}_{N\times 1}\otimes b\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn},

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{C}}^{i}=e_{i}^{N}\otimes(e_{i}^{N})^{\top}\otimes C\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nn},\quad\bar{{\bm{C}}}^{i}=e_{i}^{N}\otimes\frac{\bm{1}_{N\times 1}^{\top}}{N}\otimes\bar{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nn},

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{D}}^{i}=e_{i}^{N}\otimes D\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times m},\quad\bm{\sigma}^{i}=e_{i}^{N}\otimes\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn},\quad\bm{\gamma}=\bm{1}_{N\times 1}\otimes\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn},

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{\Gamma}}=\text{diag}[\Gamma,\dots,\Gamma]\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nn},\quad\bar{{\bm{\Gamma}}}=\bm{1}_{N\times N}\otimes\frac{\bar{\Gamma}}{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nn}.

Here,
\text{diag}[A,\dots,A]
is the block-diagonal matrix whose diagonal blocks are
A,\dots,A
.

With the above notation, the system of dynamics (
2.1
) for
N
agents can be written in compact form as

\begin{cases}\displaystyle d{\bm{X}}(t)=\Big\{({\bm{A}}+\bar{{\bm{A}}}){\bm{X}}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\bm{B}}^{i}u^{i}(t)+{\bm{b}}\Big\}dt\\
\displaystyle\hskip 43.36243pt+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(({\bm{C}}^{i}+\bar{{\bm{C}}}^{i}){\bm{X}}(t)+{\bm{D}}^{i}u^{i}(t)+\bm{\sigma}^{i}\big)dW^{i}(t)\\
\hskip 43.36243pt+\big\{({\bm{\Gamma}}+\bar{{\bm{\Gamma}}}){\bm{X}}(t)+\bm{\gamma}\big\}dW^{0}(t),\quad t\geqslant 0,\\
{\bm{X}}(0)=\bm{\xi}.\end{cases}

(3.1)

Similarly, we can rewrite the social cost as following:

\displaystyle J_{T}^{\text{soc}}(\bm{\xi};{\bm{u}}(\cdot))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}J^{i}_{T}(\bm{\xi};{\bm{u}}(\cdot))=\widetilde{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\int_{0}^{T}F({\bm{X}}(t),{\bm{u}}(t))dt\Big],

where
F:\mathbb{R}^{Nn}\times\mathbb{R}^{Nm}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

F({\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}):={\bm{x}}^{\top}({\bm{Q}}+\bar{{\bm{Q}}}){\bm{x}}+2{\bm{u}}^{\top}{\bm{S}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{u}}^{\top}{\bm{R}}{\bm{u}}+2{\bm{q}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}+2{\bm{r}}^{\top}{\bm{u}},

and

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{Q}}=\frac{1}{N}\text{diag}[Q,\dots,Q]\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nn},\quad\bar{{\bm{Q}}}=\bm{1}_{N\times N}\otimes\frac{\bar{Q}}{N^{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nn},

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{S}}=\frac{1}{N}\text{diag}[S,\dots,S]\in\mathbb{R}^{Nm\times Nn},\quad{\bm{R}}=\frac{1}{N}\text{diag}[R,\dots,R]\in\mathbb{R}^{Nm\times Nm},

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{q}}=\frac{\bm{1}_{N\times 1}}{N}\otimes q\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn},\quad{\bm{r}}=\frac{\bm{1}_{N\times 1}}{N}\otimes r\in\mathbb{R}^{Nm}.

3.1
The derivation of the system of Riccati equations and its solvability

Let
V(t,\bm{x})
denote the value function of
Problem (SO-N)
T
with the initial condition
\bm{X}(t)=\bm{x}:=(x^{1},\dots,x^{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn}
at time
t
. Let
{\bm{u}}=(u^{1},\dots,u^{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{Nm}
. The Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation for
V(t,\bm{x})
is

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\partial_{t}V+\min_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nm}}\Big\{(D_{\bm{x}}V)^{\top}\Big(({\bm{A}}+\bar{{\bm{A}}}){\bm{x}}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\bm{B}}^{i}u^{i}+{\bm{b}}\Big)\\
\displaystyle\hskip 28.90755pt+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(({\bm{C}}^{i}+\bar{{\bm{C}}}^{i}){\bm{x}}+{\bm{D}}^{i}u^{i}+\bm{\sigma}^{i}\big)^{\top}D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V\big(({\bm{C}}^{i}+\bar{{\bm{C}}}^{i}){\bm{x}}+{\bm{D}}^{i}u^{i}+\bm{\sigma}^{i}\big)\\
\displaystyle\hskip 28.90755pt+\frac{1}{2}\big(({\bm{\Gamma}}+\bar{{\bm{\Gamma}}}){\bm{x}}+\bm{\gamma}\big)^{\top}D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V\big(({\bm{\Gamma}}+\bar{{\bm{\Gamma}}}){\bm{x}}+\bm{\gamma}\big)+F({\bm{x}},{\bm{u}})\Big\}=0,\\
V(T,{\bm{x}})=0.\end{cases}

To obtain the desired system of Riccati equations, we introduce additional notation. Set

{\bm{B}}=({\bm{B}}^{1},\dots,{\bm{B}}^{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nm},\quad\text{and}\quad{\bm{e}}^{i}=(e_{i}^{N}\otimes I_{m})^{\top}=(0,\dots,I_{m},\dots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times Nm}.

Moreover, for
{\bm{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nn}
, we define the mappings

\displaystyle\cal{\displaystyle M}_{1}({\bm{Z}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\bm{e}}^{i})^{\top}({\bm{D}}^{i})^{\top}{\bm{Z}}{\bm{D}}^{i}{\bm{e}}^{i},\hskip 9.24994pt{\cal M}_{2}({\bm{Z}})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\bm{C}}^{i}+\bar{{\bm{C}}}^{i})^{\top}{\bm{Z}}{\bm{D}}^{i}{\bm{e}}^{i},

\displaystyle\cal{\displaystyle M}_{3}({\bm{Z}})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\bm{\sigma}^{i})^{\top}{\bm{Z}}{\bm{D}}^{i}{\bm{e}}^{i},\hskip 9.24994pt{\cal M}_{4}({\bm{Z}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\bm{C}}^{i}+\bar{{\bm{C}}}^{i})^{\top}{\bm{Z}}({\bm{C}}^{i}+\bar{{\bm{C}}}^{i})+\frac{1}{2}({\bm{\Gamma}}+\bar{{\bm{\Gamma}}})^{\top}{\bm{Z}}({\bm{\Gamma}}+\bar{{\bm{\Gamma}}}),

\displaystyle\cal{\displaystyle M}_{5}({\bm{Z}})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\bm{\sigma}^{i})^{\top}{\bm{Z}}({\bm{C}}^{i}+\bar{{\bm{C}}}^{i})+\bm{\gamma}^{\top}{\bm{Z}}({\bm{\Gamma}}+\bar{{\bm{\Gamma}}}),\hskip 9.24994pt{\cal M}_{6}({\bm{Z}})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\bm{\sigma}^{i})^{\top}{\bm{Z}}\bm{\sigma}^{i}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\gamma}^{\top}{\bm{Z}}\bm{\gamma}.

Then, the above HJB equation can be reduced to

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\partial_{t}V+\min_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nm}}\big\{{\bm{u}}^{\top}\big({\bm{R}}+{\cal M}_{1}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)\big){\bm{u}}+\big((D_{{\bm{x}}}V)^{\top}{\bm{B}}+\bm{x}^{\top}{\cal M}_{2}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)+{\cal M}_{3}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\hskip 28.90755pt+2{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{S}}^{\top}+2{\bm{r}}^{\top}\big){\bm{u}}\big\}+(D_{{\bm{x}}}V)^{\top}\big(({\bm{A}}+\bar{{\bm{A}}}){\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}\big)+\bm{x}^{\top}{\cal M}_{4}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)\bm{x}\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\hskip 28.90755pt+{\cal M}_{5}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)\bm{x}+{\bm{x}}^{\top}({\bm{Q}}+\bar{{\bm{Q}}}){\bm{x}}+2{\bm{q}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}+{\cal M}_{6}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)=0,\\
V(T,{\bm{x}})=0.\end{cases}

(3.2)

Suppose
{\bm{R}}+{\cal M}_{1}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)
is positive definite, the minimizer of (
3.2
) is given by

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}(t,{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\big({\bm{R}}+{\cal M}_{1}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)\big)^{-1}\big((D_{{\bm{x}}}V)^{\top}{\bm{B}}+\bm{x}^{\top}{\cal M}_{2}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)

(3.3)

\displaystyle+{\cal M}_{3}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)+2{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{S}}^{\top}+2{\bm{r}}^{\top}\big)^{\top}.

Substituting the minimizer (
3.3
) into (
3.2
) yields the reduced HJB equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}V-\frac{1}{4}\big[(D_{{\bm{x}}}V)^{\top}{\bm{B}}+\bm{x}^{\top}{\cal M}_{2}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)+{\cal M}_{3}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)+2{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{S}}^{\top}+2{\bm{r}}^{\top}\big]\big({\bm{R}}+{\cal M}_{1}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)\big)^{-1}

\displaystyle\big[(D_{{\bm{x}}}V)^{\top}{\bm{B}}+\bm{x}^{\top}{\cal M}_{2}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)+{\cal M}_{3}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)+2{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{S}}^{\top}+2{\bm{r}}^{\top}\big]^{\top}

\displaystyle+(D_{{\bm{x}}}V)^{\top}\big(({\bm{A}}+\bar{{\bm{A}}}){\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}\big)+\bm{x}^{\top}{\cal M}_{4}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)\bm{x}+{\cal M}_{5}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)\bm{x}+{\bm{x}}^{\top}({\bm{Q}}+\bar{{\bm{Q}}}){\bm{x}}

\displaystyle+2{\bm{q}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}+{\cal M}_{6}(D^{2}_{\bm{x}\bm{x}}V)=0.

Motivated by the linear-quadratic structure, we consider the solution to the HJB equation (
3.2
) takes the following form

V(t,{\bm{x}})={\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{P}}_{T}(t){\bm{x}}+2{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{p}}_{T}(t)+{\bm{k}}_{T}(t),

(3.4)

where
{\bm{P}}_{T}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn\times Nn}
is symmetric,
{\bm{p}}_{T}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn}
, and
{\bm{k}}_{T}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Applying standard techniques from linear-quadratic optimal control problems, where we plug the ansatz (
3.4
) in the reduced HJB equation and separately compare polynomial terms of orders 0, 1, and 2, we derive the following system of ODEs for the unknowns
\{{\bm{P}}_{T}(t),{\bm{p}}_{T}(t),{\bm{k}}_{T}(t):t\in[0,T]\}
:

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\dot{{\bm{P}}}_{T}(t)-\big({\bm{P}}_{T}(t){\bm{B}}+{\cal M}_{2}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))+{\bm{S}}^{\top}\big)\big({\bm{R}}+2{\cal M}_{1}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))\big)^{-1}\big({\bm{P}}_{T}(t){\bm{B}}+{\cal M}_{2}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))+{\bm{S}}^{\top}\big)^{\top}\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\hskip 36.135pt+{\bm{P}}_{T}(t)({\bm{A}}+\bar{{\bm{A}}})+({\bm{A}}+\bar{{\bm{A}}})^{\top}{\bm{P}}_{T}(t)+2{\cal M}_{4}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))+{\bm{Q}}+\bar{{\bm{Q}}}=0,\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\dot{{\bm{p}}}_{T}(t)-\big({\bm{P}}_{T}(t){\bm{B}}+{\cal M}_{2}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))+{\bm{S}}^{\top}\big)\big({\bm{R}}+2{\cal M}_{1}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))\big)^{-1}\big(({\bm{p}}_{T}(t))^{\top}{\bm{B}}+{\cal M}_{3}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))+{\bm{r}}^{\top}\big)^{\top}\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\hskip 36.135pt+{\bm{P}}_{T}(t){\bm{b}}+({\bm{A}}+\bar{{\bm{A}}})^{\top}{\bm{p}}_{T}(t)+\big({\cal M}_{5}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))\big)^{\top}+{\bm{q}}=0,\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\dot{{\bm{k}}}_{T}(t)-\big(({\bm{p}}_{T}(t))^{\top}{\bm{B}}+{\cal M}_{3}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))+{\bm{r}}^{\top}\big)\big({\bm{R}}+2{\cal M}_{1}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))\big)^{-1}\big(({\bm{p}}_{T}(t))^{\top}{\bm{B}}+{\cal M}_{3}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))+{\bm{r}}^{\top}\big)^{\top}\\
\hskip 36.135pt+2({\bm{p}}_{T}(t))^{\top}{\bm{b}}+2{\cal M}_{6}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))=0\end{cases}

(3.5)

with the terminal conditions

{\bm{P}}_{T}(T)=0\in\mathbb{S}^{Nn},\quad{\bm{p}}_{T}(T)=0\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn},\quad{\bm{k}}_{T}(T)=0\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.6)

Next, to ensure the well-posedness of the system (
3.5
) and the solvability of
Problem (SO-N)
T
, we impose the following assumption.

(H1)

The matrices
Q,\bar{Q}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
R\in\mathbb{S}^{m}_{++}
satisfy that

Q-S^{\top}R^{-1}S\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++},\quad\text{and}\quad Q+\bar{Q}-S^{\top}R^{-1}S\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}.

Note that in Assumption
(H1)
, we don’t require that
\bar{Q}
to be positive semi-definite. In particular, it may be an indefinite matrix.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(H1)
hold. Then the system of differential equations (
3.5
) admits a unique solution
\{{\bm{P}}_{T}(\cdot),{\bm{p}}_{T}(\cdot),{\bm{k}}_{T}(\cdot)\}
such that
{\bm{P}}_{T}(t)\geqslant 0
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Moreover, there exists a constant
\alpha>0
such that

{\bm{R}}+2{\cal M}_{1}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))\geqslant\alpha I_{Nm},\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

Proof.

From assumption
(H1)
, since
R\in\mathbb{S}^{m}_{++}
and
Q-S^{\top}R^{-1}S\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
, we have
{\bm{R}}\in\mathbb{S}^{Nm}_{++}
and
Q>S^{\top}R^{-1}S>0
, i.e.,
Q\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
. Then, by the definition of
{\bm{Q}}
,
{\bm{S}}
, and
{\bm{R}}
, it is clear that

{\bm{Q}}-{\bm{S}}^{\top}{\bm{R}}^{-1}{\bm{S}}=\frac{1}{N}\text{diag}\big[Q-S^{\top}R^{-1}S,\dots,Q-S^{\top}R^{-1}S\big]\in\mathbb{S}^{Nn}_{++}.

Let
\widetilde{{\bm{Q}}}:=\bar{{\bm{Q}}}+{\bm{Q}}-{\bm{S}}^{\top}{\bm{R}}^{-1}{\bm{S}}
. We define
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
as an orthogonal matrix such that its first column is
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bm{1}_{N\times 1}
, and the remaining columns form an orthonormal basis of the subspace
\{(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}=0\}
.
Consider the congruence transform

\widetilde{{\bm{Q}}}_{2}=({\bm{U}}\otimes I_{n})^{\top}\widetilde{{\bm{Q}}}({\bm{U}}\otimes I_{n}).

Then, by calculation, we obtain

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\bm{Q}}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}(I_{N}\otimes(Q-S^{\top}R^{-1}S))-\frac{1}{N^{2}}({\bm{U}}^{\top}\bm{1}_{N\times N}{\bm{U}}\otimes\bar{Q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}(I_{N}\otimes(Q-S^{\top}R^{-1}S))-\frac{1}{N^{2}}(\text{diag}[N,0,\dots,0]\otimes\bar{Q}).

Since
Q+\bar{Q}-S^{\top}R^{-1}S\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
and
Q-S^{\top}R^{-1}S\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{++}
, we derive that
\widetilde{{\bm{Q}}}_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{Nn}_{++}
, which implies that
\widetilde{{\bm{Q}}}\in\mathbb{S}^{Nn}_{++}
. Thus, Conditions (L1), (L2), and (4.23) in Chapter 6 of
[
54
]
are satisfied. Therefore, by Theorem 7.2 in Chapter 6 of
[
54
]
,
there exists a unique solution
{\bm{P}}_{T}(\cdot)\in C([0,T];\mathbb{S}^{Nn}_{+})
to the first equation in the system of equations (
3.5
) such that
{\bm{R}}+2{\cal M}_{1}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))\geqslant\alpha I_{Nm}
for all
t\in[0,T]
for some
\alpha>0
.
Upon substituting
{\bm{P}}_{T}(\cdot)
into the second equation of (
3.5
), the resulting equation for
{\bm{p}}_{T}(\cdot)
becomes a linear ordinary differential equation, which admits a unique solution on
[0,T]
. Subsequently, once
{\bm{P}}_{T}(\cdot)
and
{\bm{p}}_{T}(\cdot)
are determined, the equation for
{\bm{k}}_{T}(\cdot)
in (
3.5
) also reduces to a linear ODE, which likewise admits a unique solution on
[0,T]
.

The following lemma provides an explicit characterization of the optimal control and the value function for
Problem (SO-N)
T
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose Assumption
(H1)
holds. Let
\{{\bm{P}}_{T}(\cdot),{\bm{p}}_{T}(\cdot),{\bm{k}}_{T}(\cdot)\}
be the unique solution to the system of equation (

is open-loop solvable, and the social optimal control is given in feedback form by

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}(t,{\bm{X}}(t))

\displaystyle=-\big({\bm{R}}+{\cal M}_{1}(2{\bm{P}}_{T}(t))\big)^{-1}\big[\big({\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{P}}_{T}(t)+({\cal M}_{2}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t)))^{\top}+{\bm{S}}\big){\bm{X}}(t)

\displaystyle+{\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{p}}_{T}(t)+({\cal M}_{3}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t)))^{\top}+{\bm{r}}\big],

Proof.

The conclusion is an immediate consequence of Lemma

3.2
Solvability of Problem (SO-N)
T

Note that the optimal control provided in Lemma

depends on the solution

and
{\bm{p}}_{T}(\cdot)
to the system (
3.5

3.5

to

T

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\dot{{\bm{P}}}_{T}(t)-\big({\bm{P}}_{T}(t){\bm{B}}+{\cal M}_{2}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))+{\bm{S}}^{\top}\big)\big({\bm{R}}+2{\cal M}_{1}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))\big)^{-1}\big({\bm{P}}_{T}(t){\bm{B}}+{\cal M}_{2}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))+{\bm{S}}^{\top}\big)^{\top}\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\hskip 36.135pt+{\bm{P}}_{T}(t)({\bm{A}}+\bar{{\bm{A}}})+({\bm{A}}+\bar{{\bm{A}}})^{\top}{\bm{P}}_{T}(t)+2{\cal M}_{4}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))+{\bm{Q}}+\bar{{\bm{Q}}}=0,\\

Lemma 3.3
.

Let

\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\

for all

, where both

and

[0,T]

Similarly, for the equation satisfied by

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\dot{{\bm{p}}}_{T}(t)-\big({\bm{P}}_{T}(t){\bm{B}}+{\cal M}_{2}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))+{\bm{S}}^{\top}\big)\big({\bm{R}}+2{\cal M}_{1}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))\big)^{-1}\big(({\bm{p}}_{T}(t))^{\top}{\bm{B}}+{\cal M}_{3}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))+{\bm{r}}^{\top}\big)^{\top}\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\hskip 36.135pt+{\bm{P}}_{T}(t){\bm{b}}+({\bm{A}}+\bar{{\bm{A}}})^{\top}{\bm{p}}_{T}(t)+\big({\cal M}_{5}({\bm{P}}_{T}(t))\big)^{\top}+{\bm{q}}=0,\\
{\bm{p}}_{T}(T)=0\in\mathbb{R}^{Nn},\end{cases}

(3.8)

we provide an analogous structural characterization of its solution in the following lemma. The proof is provided in Appendix
A.2
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
(H1)
holds, and let
{\bm{P}}_{T}(\cdot)
be the solution to the Riccati equation (
3.7
). Then, the solution
{\bm{p}}_{T}(\cdot)
to the differential equation (
3.8
) has the following representation:

{\bm{p}}_{T}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}(p_{T}^{1,N}(t))^{\top},(p_{T}^{1,N}(t))^{\top},\dots,(p_{T}^{1,N}(t))^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

where
p_{T}^{1,N}(\cdot)
is a vector-valued function on
[0,T]
with
p_{T}^{1,N}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Intuitively, the solution
V(t,{\bm{x}})
to the HJB equation (
3.2
) is expected to be of the order
O(1)
. If we fix
x_{i}=x
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, then by using the results in Lemma
3.3
and
3.4
, we obtain

\displaystyle V(t,{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle={\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{P}}_{T}(t){\bm{x}}+2{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{p}}_{T}(t)+{\bm{k}}_{T}(t)

\displaystyle=Nx^{\top}P_{T}^{1,N}(t)x+(N^{2}-N)x^{\top}P_{T}^{2,N}(t)x+2Nx^{\top}p_{T}^{1,N}(t)+{\bm{k}}_{T}(t),

which implies that we should have

\|P_{T}^{1,N}(t)\|=O(N^{-1}),\quad\|P_{T}^{2,N}(t)\|=O(N^{-2}),\quad|p_{T}^{1,N}(t)|=O(N^{-1}),\quad\text{and}\quad|{\bm{k}}_{T}(t)|=O(1)

for all
t\in[0,T]
. Motivated by the above heuristic reasoning on the order of magnitude of
\|P_{T}^{1,N}(t)\|
,

In the previous section, we introduced the finite-horizon mean field control problem and established its solvability in Proposition

5.2
Verification theorem to the ergodic mean field control problem

Recall that
Problem (MFC)
T
in (
2.8
) can be regarded as a probabilistic interpretation of infinite-dimensional Hamilton-Jacobi equation (
4.4
). In this subsection, we establish an analogous link for the ergodic setting: we show that
Problem (EMFC)
in (
2.9
) provides a probabilistic interpretation of the Bellman equation (
5.6
). More precisely, we relate the Bellman equation (
5.6
) to
Problem (EMFC)
through a verification argument in the context of control theory.

In a standard stochastic control problem, one seeks an optimal pair
(\bar{X}(\cdot),\bar{u}(\cdot))
that minimizes the cost functional and thereby determines the value function
U(\cdot)
. In contrast,
Problem (EMFC)
in (
2.9
) involves two intertwined objectives: finding a pair
(\bar{X}(\cdot),\bar{u}(\cdot))
that simultaneously minimizes the long-term average cost and the associated relative (residual) cost.

A further subtlety in
Problem (EMFC)
is that the choice of
u(\cdot)
does not necessarily ensure the existence of
\lim_{T\to{\infty}}J_{T}(\mu_{0};u(\cdot))
. In particular, the control space
\mathscr{U}
is not necessarily a subset of
\mathscr{U}_{ad}[0,\infty)
(as defined in (
5.5
)). To address this, we introduce a refined control space
\mathscr{U}
.

Definition 5.4
.

A control process
u(\cdot)
is called an admissible control in the ergodic mean field control problem (
2.9
) if:

•

It is
\mathbb{F}
-progressively measurable;

•

For any initial state
\xi\in L^{4}_{{\cal F}_{0}}
and
t\geqslant 0
, the state process
X(\cdot):=X(\cdot\,;\xi,u(\cdot))
of the solution to (
2.5
) satisfying

\mathbb{E}\big[|X(t)|^{4}+|u(t)|^{4}\big]<\infty;

•

The joint law of
(X(t),\mathbb{E}[X(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}],u(t))
converges to some distribution
\nu_{\infty}\in{\cal P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m})
in the
2
-Wasserstein metric
{\cal W}_{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
, i.e.,

\lim_{t\to\infty}{\cal W}_{2}\big({\cal L}(X(t),\mathbb{E}[X(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}],u(t)),\nu_{\infty}\big)=0.

In what follows, we provide a complete characterization of
Problem (EMFC)
in the linear-quadratic setting, building on the solvability of the Bellman equation (

) established in Proposition

. We first introduce the following SDE, whose solution, denoted by

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle d\bar{X}(t)=\big\{(A+B\Theta^{*})\bar{X}(t)+\big[\bar{A}+B(\bar{\Theta}^{*}-\Theta^{*})\big]\mathbb{E}\big[\bar{X}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]+B\theta^{*}+b\big\}dt\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\hskip 43.36243pt+\big\{(C+D\Theta^{*})\bar{X}(t)+\big[\bar{C}+D(\bar{\Theta}^{*}-\Theta^{*})\big]\mathbb{E}\big[\bar{X}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]+D\theta^{*}+\sigma\big\}dW(t)\\
\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\hskip 43.36243pt+\big\{\Gamma\bar{X}(t)+\bar{\Gamma}\mathbb{E}\big[\bar{X}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]+\gamma\big\}dW^{0}(t),\\

(5.12)

For simplicity of notation, we denote

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\langle P_{1}\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{X}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big],\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{X}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]\big\rangle\big]

\displaystyle K_{1}\Big(-\frac{1}{2}+Ke^{-\lambda(T-t)}\Big)\mathbb{E}\big[\big\langle P_{1}\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{X}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big],\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{X}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]\big\rangle\big]+Ke^{-\lambda(T-t)},

\mathbb{E}\big[\big\langle P_{1}\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{X}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big],\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{X}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]\big\rangle\big]\leqslant\int_{0}^{t}e^{\int_{s}^{t}K_{1}(-\frac{1}{2}+Ke^{-\lambda(T-r)})dr}Ke^{-\lambda(T-s)}ds\leqslant Ke^{-\lambda(T-t)}

\mathbb{E}\big[\big|\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{X}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]\big|^{2}\big]\leqslant Ke^{-\lambda(T-t)},\quad\forall t\in[0,T],

for some positive constants

\displaystyle P(A+B\Theta^{*}_{T}(t))+(A+B\Theta_{T}^{*}(t))^{\top}P+(C+D\Theta_{T}^{*}(t))^{\top}P(C+D\Theta_{T}^{*}(t))

\displaystyle P(A+B\Theta^{*})+(A+B\Theta^{*})^{\top}P+(C+D\Theta^{*})^{\top}P(C+D\Theta^{*})+H_{3}(t),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\big[\langle P\widehat{X}(t),\widehat{X}(t)\rangle\big]

\displaystyle+K\big|\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat{X}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]\big|^{2}\Big]

for all

. By the inequality (

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\big[\langle P\widehat{X}(t),\widehat{X}(t)\rangle\big]\leqslant K_{2}\Big(-\frac{\widehat{\beta}}{2}+Ke^{-\lambda(T-t)}\Big)\mathbb{E}\big[\langle P\widehat{X}(t),\widehat{X}(t)\rangle\big]+Ke^{-\lambda(T-t)}.

Next, from the above estimation and the moment boundedness of

(\xi^{1},\dots,\xi^{N})

\mathbb{R}^{n}

Theorem 7.2
.

Assume that
(H1)-(H2)-(H3)
hold. For each

\sup_{i\in\{1,\dots,N\}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{T}^{i,N}(t)-X_{T}^{i}(t)|^{2}+|u_{T}^{i,N}(t)-u_{T}^{i}(t)|^{2}\big]\leqslant\frac{K}{N}e^{KT},

Proof.

By Proposition

\displaystyle dX_{T}^{i,N}(t)

\displaystyle=\Big\{\Big(A+B\Theta_{T}^{N}(t)+\frac{1}{N}B\bar{\theta}_{T}^{N}(t)\Big)X_{T}^{i,N}(t)+\big(\bar{A}+B(\bar{\Theta}_{T}^{N}(t)-\Theta_{T}^{N}(t))\big)X_{T}^{(N)}(t)

\displaystyle+B\theta_{T}^{N}(t)+b\Big\}dt+\Big\{\Big(C+D\Theta_{T}^{N}(t)+\frac{1}{N}D\bar{\theta}_{T}^{N}(t)\Big)X_{T}^{i,N}(t)

\displaystyle+\big(\bar{C}+D(\bar{\Theta}_{T}^{N}(t)-\Theta_{T}^{N}(t))\big)X_{T}^{(N)}(t)+D\theta_{T}^{N}(t)+\sigma\Big\}dW^{i}(t)

First, we give the estimate for

\mathbb{E}[|X_{T}^{(N)}(t)|^{2}]

for all

Next, we consider the estimate for

\displaystyle d\big(X_{T}^{(N)}(t)-\mathbb{E}\big[X_{T}^{i}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]\big)

\displaystyle\Big\{\big(A+\bar{A}+B\bar{\Theta}_{T}^{*}(t)\big)\big(X_{T}^{(N)}(t)-\mathbb{E}\big[X_{T}^{i}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]\big)+B\big(\bar{\Theta}_{T}^{N}(t)-\bar{\Theta}_{T}^{*}(t)\big)X_{T}^{(N)}(t)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}B\bar{\theta}_{T}^{N}(t)X_{T}^{(N)}(t)+B\big(\theta_{T}^{N}(t)-\theta_{T}^{*}(t)\big)\Big\}dt

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|X_{T}^{(N)}(t)-\mathbb{E}\big[X_{T}^{i}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]\big|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle K\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|X_{T}^{(N)}(t)-\mathbb{E}\big[X_{T}^{i}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]\big|^{2}\Big]+K\Big\{\big\|\bar{\Theta}_{T}^{N}(t)-\bar{\Theta}_{T}^{*}(t)\big\|^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{T}^{(N)}(t)|^{2}\big]

\displaystyle K\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|X_{T}^{(N)}(t)-\mathbb{E}\big[X_{T}^{i}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]\big|^{2}\Big]+\frac{K}{N}e^{Kt}e^{2L_{1}(T-t)}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big|X_{T}^{(N)}(t)-\mathbb{E}\big[X_{T}^{i}(t)|{\cal F}_{t}^{0}\big]\big|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle e^{Kt}\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi^{i}-\mathbb{E}[\xi^{i}]\Big|^{2}\Big]+e^{Kt}\frac{K}{N}\int_{0}^{t}e^{2L_{1}(T-s)}ds\leqslant\frac{K}{N}e^{KT}

for all

is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables in

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}L_{1}\big(\|P_{T}^{N^{\prime}}(s)-P_{T}(s)\|+\|\Pi_{T}^{N^{\prime}}(s)-\Pi_{T}(s)\|\big)ds+\frac{L_{2}}{N^{\prime}}(T-t).

By Grönwall’s inequality, we have for all
t\in[t_{0}^{N^{\prime}},T]
,

\|P_{T}^{N^{\prime}}(t)-P_{T}(t)\|+\|\Pi_{T}^{N^{\prime}}(t)-\Pi_{T}(t)\|\leqslant\frac{L_{2}T}{N^{\prime}}e^{L_{1}T}\leqslant\frac{\eta_{0}}{2},

which contradicts the definition of
t_{0}^{N^{\prime}}
. This proves that there exists a
N^{*}>0
such that for all
N\geqslant N^{*}
,

\|P_{T}^{N}(t)-P_{T}(t)\|+\|\Pi_{T}^{N}(t)-\Pi_{T}(t)\|\leqslant\eta_{0}
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
In particular, there exists
K>0
, independent of
T
and
N
, such that

\sup_{N\geqslant N^{*}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big(\|P_{T}^{N}(t)\|+\|\Pi_{T}^{N}(t)\|\big)\leqslant K.

(A.4)

Step 4.
For
N\geqslant N^{*}
, the estimate (
A.4
) implies that both trajectories of
P_{T}^{N}(\cdot)
and
\Pi_{T}^{N}(\cdot)
on
[0,T]
remain in a bounded set, and
therefore
Step 1
yields

\displaystyle\big\|\Psi_{1}(P_{T}(t))-\Psi_{1}(P_{T}^{N}(t))\big\|+\big\|\Psi_{2}(P_{T}(t),\Pi_{T}(t))-\Psi_{2}(P_{T}^{N}(t),\Pi_{T}^{N}(t))\big\|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle L_{1}\big(\big\|P_{T}(t)-P_{T}^{N}(t)\big\|+\big\|\Pi_{T}(t)-\Pi_{T}^{N}(t)\big\|\big)

for all
t\in[0,T]
, where
L_{1}
is a positive constant, independent of
T
and
N
. Moreover, there exists a constant
L_{2}>0
, independent of
T
and
N
, such that

\big\|g_{1}(N,P_{T}^{N}(t),\Pi_{T}^{N}(t))\big\|+\big\|g_{2}(N,P_{T}^{N}(t),\Pi_{T}^{N}(t))\big\|\leqslant\frac{L_{2}}{N}

for all
t\in[0,T]
. Subtracting the ODEs for
P_{T}^{N}(\cdot)
and
P_{T}(\cdot)
gives

\big\|\dot{P}_{T}^{N}(t)-\dot{P}_{T}(t)\big\|\leqslant L_{1}\big\|P_{T}(t)-P_{T}^{N}(t)\big\|+\frac{L_{2}}{N},\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

By the definition of Frobenius norm, for all
t\in[0,T]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{d}{dt}\|P_{T}^{N}(t)-P_{T}(t)\|\Big|

\displaystyle=\frac{\big|\text{trace}\big((P_{T}^{N}(t)-P_{T}(t))^{\top}(\dot{P}_{T}^{N}(t)-\dot{P}_{T}(t))\big)\big|}{\|P_{T}^{N}(t)-P_{T}(t)\|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\big\|\dot{P}_{T}^{N}(t)-\dot{P}_{T}(t)\big\|

from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. It follows that

-\frac{d}{dt}\big\|P_{T}^{N}(t)-P_{T}(t)\big\|\leqslant L_{1}\big\|P_{T}(t)-P_{T}^{N}(t)\big\|+\frac{L_{2}}{N},\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

By using the terminal condition
P_{T}^{N}(T)-P_{T}(T)=0\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
, we derive the following estimate

\big\|P_{T}^{N}(t)-P_{T}(t)\big\|\leqslant\frac{L_{2}}{NL_{1}}\big(e^{L_{1}(T-t)}-1\big)\leqslant\frac{L(t,T)}{N},\quad\forall t\in[0,T],

where
L(t,T):=\frac{L_{2}}{L_{1}}e^{L_{1}(T-t)}
is a constant that is independent of
N
. Next, from the ODEs satisfied by
\Pi_{T}^{N}(\cdot)
and
\Pi_{T}(\cdot)
, we have

\begin{cases}\vskip 4.0pt\displaystyle\dot{\Pi}_{T}^{N}(t)-\dot{\Pi}_{T}(t)+\Psi_{2}(P_{T}^{N}(t),\Pi_{T}^{N}(t))-\Psi_{2}(P_{T}(t),\Pi_{T}(t))+g_{2}(N,P_{T}^{N}(t),\Pi_{T}^{N}(t))=0,\\
\Pi_{T}^{N}(T)-\Pi_{T}(T)=0\in\mathbb{S}^{n}.\end{cases}

The same argument yields that

\big\|\Pi_{T}^{N}(t)-\Pi_{T}(t)\big\|\leqslant\frac{L(t,T)}{N},\quad\forall t\in[0,T]

for some possible different
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
(and hence a different
L(t,T)
). Finally, the mappings
\Psi_{3}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
and
\Psi_{4}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)
are locally Lipschitz by the same reasoning as in
Step 1
, and using the
already established bounds for
P_{T}^{N}(\cdot)-P_{T}(\cdot)
and
\Pi_{T}^{N}(\cdot)-\Pi_{T}(\cdot)
, we conclude that there exists some possible different
N^{*}>0
such that, for all
N>N^{*}
,

\big|p_{T}^{N}(t)-p_{T}(t)\big|+\big|\kappa_{T}^{N}(t)-\kappa_{T}(t)\big|\leqslant\frac{L(t,T)}{N},\quad\forall t\in[0,T].
