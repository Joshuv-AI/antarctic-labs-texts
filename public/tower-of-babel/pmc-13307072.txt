Designing Thermomechanically Stable Nanocrystalline Alloys for Low Friction and Wear: A Perspective
Authors: Frank W. DelRio, Rachel A. Knapp, Nicolas Argibay, Michael Chandross, Tomas F. Babuska, John F. Curry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this Perspective, we discuss recent work and future trends on the usage of thermomechanically stable and self-lubricating nanocrystalline (NC) alloys for low friction and wear applications. The theoretical underpinnings and mechanistic framework for attaining and maintaining low friction and wear in metal contacts are presented. Initial work on stress, time, and temperature-dependent changes in friction and wear in NC alloys validates the models, demonstrating lower friction from decreased interfacial strength, contact pressure, sliding speed, and temperature and increased roughness and material hardness. Subsequent work reveals that NC Pt–Au, metal nitrides, complex-concentrated, and high-entropy alloys come with additional stabilization factors such as low-friction carbon tribofilms and oxide/amorphous–crystalline layers but are still susceptible to destabilization factors including irradiation and adverse environmental conditions.

Introduction

Historically, microstructural and thermal stability in metals is most explicitly required and best understood in nanoscale integrated circuit (IC) interconnects, microelectromechanical system (MEMS) contacts, and high-current metallization in power/automotive applications. For example, in Al/Al–Cu and Cu-based back-end-of-line interconnects, electromigration (EM) reliability has been framed as a grain-boundary-dominated transport challenge, with elongated, plate-like grains, controlled textures, and robust cap/liner interfaces as explicit targets. − Similar microstructure-centric constraints appear in direct and hybrid Cu–Cu joints and micro/nanobumps, where the bonded interface, intermetallic compound morphology, and β-Sn grain orientation must remain stable at elevated temperatures and currents to prevent diffusive migration that leads to Kirkendall porosity and resistance drift. − At larger length scales, Al/Al–Cu and Cu-based conductive channels for power, radio frequency, and automotive electronics demand microstructural and thermal stability against thermoelectromechanical cycling, bond-wire forces, and coefficient of thermal expansion mismatch stresses, , whereas thin Au-based electrodes for bonding and MEMS must resist recrystallization and Kirkendall porosity at high temperatures. Within these systems, practical “stable” operating regimes are reasonably mapped: Cu maintains usable microstructures over multiyear lifetimes up to 150–300 °C and 106–107 A/cm2, Cu–Cu and SnAgCu-based solder/microjoints show acceptable resistance and mechanical robustness up to 125–150 °C, and Au resists recrystallization and pore formation up to 500–700 °C. To extend the limits, a number of different thermal, chemical, mechanical, tribological, and electrical methods have been utilized. Thermally, it was found that postbond annealing of (111) nanotwinned-Cu microbumps drove recrystallization/abnormal grain growth to remove the original bonding interface, shifting cracks into tougher bulk zigzag grain boundaries with essentially no increase in resistance over 1000 thermal cycles. Chemically, graphene-doped twinned-Cu redistributed layers formed thinner oxides and reduced voiding; time to 20% resistance rise improved from 117 to 291 h at 120 °C and 106 A/cm2. Mechanically, higher bonding strength suppressed interfacial fracture under electromigration in Cu–Cu bumps by maintaining adhesion so transport occurred without early open circuits; lifetimes exceeded 5000 h at 200 °C and 106 A/cm2. Tribologically, coupled shear/heat/current in AgCu10 sliding contacts under current created subsurface lamellae, 9R phase, and local amorphization at Ag/Cu interfaces that seeded microcracks and delamination, stressing the need for phase-compatible architectures to avoid strain mismatch. Electrically, Co caps with Ru (and Co-doped Ru) liners were found to reduce dominant Cu/cap/liner interface diffusion; Co-doped Ru mitigated Co depletion and boosted performance in advanced nodes. The various tactics point to a unifying next step: modeling and engineering grain-boundary (GB) structure and kinetics in nanocrystalline (NC) alloys to extend their microstructural stability.

Early foundational GB structural models and excess-volume/glasslike interpretations laid the groundwork for understanding the microstructural and thermal stability. For example, atomistic studies established that many NC GBs, especially the high-energy, high-angle GBs, are structurally disordered, excess-volume-rich regions that can resemble confined amorphous or even liquid-like layers at high temperatures. , Quantitative descriptors, such as GB-specific volume (excess volume) and potential energy, mapped against misorientation in NC Al, reinforced differences between low-angle and high-angle GBs and linked low-density/high-energy GBs to enhanced sliding propensity. More recent work expanded the models: GB thickness and compliance were extracted in NC Cu (based on a soft intergranular phase view), Voronoi/topological analyses and order parameters were used to identify lattice distortions and liquid-like GB signatures in NC Al, and preparation-route comparisons demonstrated strong path-dependence of GB excess volume and energy, properties with direct implications for segregation energetics and grain-growth kinetics. In parallel, robust protocols emerged for building fully dense NC networks suitable for mechanism studies. Voronoi tessellation and seed-solidification methods (followed by relaxation and annealing) became foundational for creating periodic, equiaxed, dislocation-poor NC metals. Further refinements emphasized density control, removal of atomic overlaps, and deliberate GB relaxation to tune nonequilibrium free volume and defect content (important because the GB state influences subsequent diffusion and deformation). Alternative constructions (e.g., random-close-packed seed tessellations for monodisperse grains) enabled cleaner control of GB volume fraction and facilitated extraction of effective GB properties. The methods have been generalized across chemistries and crystal classes, including segregation-bearing alloys (e.g., Ni–Mo) and ceramics (e.g., UN) built from multigrain polycrystals with connected networks. , With realistic networks, molecular dynamics (MD) demonstrated GB-diffusion-controlled creep and clarified coupling to GB sliding and microstructural evolution. Early simulations established the feasibility of GB diffusion creep in atomistic NC-like structures, with canonical NC Pd studies showing Coble-type scaling and later revealing how concurrent grain growth modifies creep behavior and transport pathways. , Mechanistic work tied creep to the GB structural state, arguing that disordered and high-energy boundaries exhibit liquid-like diffusion that strongly enhances diffusional creep and that GB sliding vs diffusion is a cooperative, sliding-accommodated diffusional mechanism. Additional cross-material studies extracted GB diffusivities, activation energies, and effective diffusional widths from trajectories, providing parametrizations that can be fed into continuum creep models. These structural and kinetic foundations motivated creep-focused treatments, showing that GB-segregated solutes retard GB-mediated creep by modifying GB diffusivity/mobility and, more generally, facilitated several different segregation-based stabilization frameworks.

In more detail, one of these foundational GB models sought to establish a thermodynamics-grounded context for engineering intrinsically thermally stable NC alloys, centered on regular NC solution models that explicitly incorporated GBs as thermodynamic entities. Core work by Schuh et al. − formulated two-region free-energy models where grains and GBs followed regular-solution thermodynamics, with GB volume fraction scaling as (d – t)/d, where d is the grain diameter and t is the GB thickness. The model enforced chemical-potential equality between regions, recovered classical segregation isotherms and bulk phase diagrams in appropriate limits, and introduced a criterion for drawing nanostructure stability maps in terms of a bulk mixing enthalpy ΔH mix and GB segregation enthalpy ΔH seg. Parametric studies subsequently classified regimes with no GB-stabilized nanocrystals, metastable segregated states, and fully stable alloys that were predicted to be free from grain growth and phase separation approaching their melting temperature. A large Miedema-based database of ΔH seg (≈2500 binaries) enabled high-throughput screening for candidate compositions. Monte Carlo and kinetic Monte Carlo simulations extended the ideas, resolving configurational entropy, local segregation, and GB complexions. − The simulations confirmed equilibrium grain sizes, negative GB formation energies in closed systems, duplex states where segregation and nanoprecipitates coexisted, and stabilized polycrystals where GB free energy approached zero. , Parallel work treated GBs as phase-like complexions, yielding complexion diagrams that mirror bulk regular-solution phase diagrams, − and more recent work introduced segregation spectra and phase/defect diagrams that accounted for distributions of segregation energies across GB character space. , The NC free-energy schemes were also embedded in CALPHAD-style phase-equilibria frameworks, using complete binary diagrams (with intermetallics) to ensure consistency of NC states with bulk equilibria. − This has been applied particularly to W-based systems, where models predicted that W–Ti, W–Al, and W–Sc form thermodynamically stable NC alloys, while W–Ag and others suffer destabilizing segregation; experiments on powders, films, and multilayers validated predictions up to 1600 K. ,,,− Similar methods were used to benchmark Fe-based and other transition-metal binaries. ,,

In this Perspective, we examine recent work and potential future directions on the usage of thermomechanically stable and self-lubricating (e.g., Pt–Au) NC alloys for low friction and wear applications. The work is divided into five sections. In Section , we present several mechanistic frameworks for understanding tribological properties in metallic contacts and leverage the models to determine the important parameters for attaining and maintaining low friction and wear under different operating conditions. In Section , we use the established models to explain the stress-, time-, and temperature-dependent changes in friction in NC alloys and reveal how microstructural and thermal stability in NC Pt–Au alloys lengthens the low-friction and wear regimes. NC Pt–Au is useful as a model system for understanding the effects of solute segregation and as a benchmark for developing other low-friction/wear coatings, but likely not as a low-cost option for widespread deployment. In Section , we examine other methods for reducing friction and wear, first through self-lubricating, carbon-based tribofilms on catalytically active NC Pt–Au alloys and metal nitrides and then through oxide and amorphous–crystalline layers on complex-concentrated alloys (CCAs) and high-entropy alloys (HEAs). Although the definitions are fluid and constantly evolving in the field, HEAs are often thought of as a subset of CCAs, with the former consisting of a single-phase material with 5 (or 5+) elements and high configurational entropy and the latter consisting of a single/multiphase with 3+ elements and a less-rigorous entropy condition. In Section , we highlight some potential destabilization factors, namely, exposure to radiation, water, and oxygen. In Section , we discuss open questions and future directions based on combinatorial synthesis, high-throughput testing, and machine learning (ML) for faster advancements in mechanistic views of friction and wear. In all, the Perspective offers a forum for defining the theoretical underpinnings of friction and wear, comparing and contrasting properties and mechanisms across different NC material types (e.g., metals, nitrides, CCAs/HEAs) without and with tribofilm formation and predesigned heterostructures (when applicable), and proposing future work on framework limitations, electrical properties, microstructural control, environmental response, and lower-cost options.

Mechanisms of Friction and Wear

This section focuses on two themes: the theoretical underpinnings for attaining low friction and wear and a mechanistic framework for maintaining low friction and wear in metallic contacts. On attaining low friction/wear, Greenwood–Williamson-type (GW) theory framed rough contact as a statistical population of asperities: given a nominal load, only the tallest asperities engage, producing a distribution of microcontact areas and pressures, and a real contact area that scales with load. Mechanistically, the model is critical in assessing friction and wear through its ability to evaluate the junction-size distribution at the contact interface, i.e., the theory predicts how many contacts exist and how large they are under given roughness and loading. Modern extensions preserved the approach while also removing some of the idealizations: replaced single-scale asperities with self-affine roughness, calculated contact areas directly using boundary-element or continuum solvers, included elastic interactions between adjacent contacts, and added elastoplasticity so that contact radii and stored energy reflected plastic flattening and residual stresses rather than just purely elastic Hertzian assumptions. − These developments shifted the emphasis from a single average asperity toward a full distribution and morphology of contact points that can potentially cause friction and enable wear debris. Rabinowicz’s compatibility charts mapped material pairs into compatible (stable sliding, low wear) and incompatible (transfer, galling, seizure) outcomes. In other words, compatibility is an assessment of whether, under realistic roughness and load, the evolving ensemble of contact junctions tend to remain small and weak enough to shear/plastically accommodate sliding, or grow into large, strongly adhered junctions that fail catastrophically or weld and spread. In that sense, Rabinowicz-style maps implicitly combined contact statistics (how often large junctions arose), adhesion/affinity (how strongly junctions resisted shear separation), and material resistance to fracture or plastic flow. − Atomistic simulations by Aghababaei et al. , made this implicit threshold explicit via a critical junction size d*. For junctions smaller than d*, sliding proceeded via plastic flow or atom-by-atom transfer with little wear; larger junctions accumulated enough elastic/plastic energy to nucleate cracks and emit particles whose size scaled with the junction (the transition in behavior is shown in the map in Figure ). Once d* is known, GW-type rough-contact models provide the distribution of junction areas such that debris formation is then controlled by whether contact area exceeds a critical value. It is important to note that the distribution of junction areas is not only based on the properties of the metal surface but also the counterface (e.g., sapphire, alumina, steel), given that both ultimately dictate the adhesion, junction formation, elastic/plastic mismatch, and debris generation. Multicontact coalescence and elastic interactions were also found to promote larger junctions, explaining mild-to-severe transitions as contact density increases, even when isolated contacts might be subcritical. , MD simulations of various tip–surface combinations revealed the significance of adhesive contact formation and interfacial processes on contact area, friction, and wear; in particular, the energetics and dynamics of jump-to-contact, elastic-plastic-yield, neck formation, wetting, reconstruction, stick–slip, and transfer were examined. Taken together, the approach yielded a mechanistic route to predict macroscopic wear, where the Archard coefficient can now be reinterpreted as a probability rather than an empirical constant. , The framework showed that designing contacts for low friction/wear means: (1) chemically, moderating interfacial shear strength so that stress was below shear strength; (2) geometrically, engineering roughness to reduce real contact area and capillary adhesion; (3) microstructurally, attaining high hardness to get distributed plasticity over junction fracture; and (4) operationally, keeping pressures at ranges where only a small fraction of junctions are critical. On maintaining low friction and wear, it was noted that pure metals and alloys exhibit an apparent bimodal stability in their friction behavior, where contact conditions associated with low friction converge to a single coefficient of friction and those associated with high friction diverge to different coefficients of friction. Argibay et al. developed a mechanistic framework to explain this bimodal stability, linking friction coefficients in unlubricated metals to stress- and temperature-dependent evolution of a near-surface grain structure, using both macro-scale experiments and atomistic simulations. Sliding tests on self-mated high-purity Cu and Au with a linear contact force ramp were combined with MD simulations of self-mated pure Ag and self-mated Ag–Cu alloys (initial grain size ≈ 5 nm) to show that metal friction naturally segregates into two stable regimes, each tied to a distinct steady-state surface grain size and deformation mode. In the low-friction regime (with friction coefficients μ < 0.5), the sliding interface formed an ultrananocrystalline (UNC) surface film with grains on the order of 10–20 nm. Plastic deformation was accommodated by the shear-activated formation of amorphous shear bands and GB-mediated mechanisms, possibly from GB sliding, rotation, and dislocation emission/absorption. This ultrarefined surface layer was different from the as-processed structure and was dynamically formed through the competing mechanically driven mechanisms of grain refinement and coarsening until a stress- and temperature-dependent equilibrium grain size was reached, as shown in Figure a. In the high-friction regime (μ > 1.0), surface grains evolve to be larger than the equilibrium scale, and deformation is dislocation-dominated, with intragranular glide dictating plasticity. The transition between the regimes was predicted by a quantitative model adapted from Yamakov-type grain-evolution kinetics and tied time-dependent grain size to stress and temperature, as shown in Figure b. Without empirical fitting, the model predicted an equilibrium grain size and whether the interface stabilized in the low- or high-friction regime for both the self-mated Cu and Au contacts, using only material properties and loading conditions as shown in Figure c. In most metals (e.g., Au, Cu, Ni), boundaries between the regimes were described by the defined Beilby and Hall–Petch stability limits. However, in metals with relatively high stacking fault energies (e.g., Al) and flow stresses (i.e., hardness), the authors identified a third critical stress, below which it was possible to maintain low friction. It was deduced that lower friction was maintained through reduced stress σ̃, time τ̃, and temperature T, as further discussed in Chandross and Argibay. Eder et al. created similar deformation mechanism maps for nanocrystalline Cu–Ni alloys subjected to sliding using MD computational micrographs; the resulting maps showed how alloying and thus stacking fault energy led to different microstructural responses via grain refinement, twinning, stress build-up and release, and shear layer formation, which ultimately controlled the friction/wear evolution. Altogether, the work suggested that maintaining low friction and wear adds two requirements to the four above: (5) temporally, attaining lower speeds to get slower surface grain size evolution and (6) thermally, keeping lower temperatures to suppress diffusion-driven coarsening (Table ).

Baseline Stability: Binary and Higher-Order Alloys

Several binary NC alloys have been shown to satisfy the requirements above. In particular, early work on the microstructural stability of NC alloys focused on requirements (2)–(4), acquiring a fundamental understanding of the effects of roughness, hardness, and pressure on friction. Rupert and Schuh provided the first indirect evidence of mechanically driven evolution toward an equilibrium grain size via sliding wear tests on Ni–W alloys. In more detail, electrodeposited Ni–W films with initial grain sizes of 3–47 nm developed, under pin-on-disk sliding, a few hundred nanometer thick layer in which the finest grains coarsen from 3 to 20 nm and GBs relax, as shown in Figure . The hardness of the evolved layer matched that of thermally annealed bulk material, but with a thickness that coincided with the plastic zone rather than a thermal diffusion length, implying that coarsening and relaxation were driven by a local strain field, not frictional heating. Panzarino and Rupert made the convergence to an equilibrium grain size explicit by comparing wear and microstructure in as-deposited (3 nm) and annealed (45 nm) films. Under large Hertzian stresses, both films evolved toward a common subsurface microstructure with a saturated surface grain size of ≈10–20 nm and a grain-size gradient with depth. The authors found a crossover at this size between the GB-dominated mechanism at smaller grains and dislocation-dominated plasticity at larger grains, in agreement with the expected transition from low-to-high friction. Argibay et al. directly operationalized the framework on similar Ni–W films with different Hertzian stresses. The authors measured a stress-dependent steady-state surface grain size (again, 10–20 nm) and hardness and found a critical stress where friction transitions between regimes. Although not on NC alloys, parallel work on the effects of nanoscale surface roughness on adhesive friction showed that the relationship breaks away from classical macroscopic intuition such that friction decreased as roughness increased due to a reduction of real contact area and suppression of capillary forces. The time-dependent friction and microstructural changes reported for NC Ni and Ni–Fe in Prasad et al. and Padilla et al. can also be explained as the system evolving toward an equilibrium grain size under cyclic sliding, thus addressing requirement (5). The results indicated that at low contact pressures and/or sliding speeds, the friction was low and the wear surface exhibited an UNC microstructure, while at high contact pressures and/or sliding speeds, the friction was high and the wear surface did not show substantial changes in microstructure. Thus, low friction was achieved at small strain rates, possibly due to the deformation mechanism and strain-rate sensitivity. Finally, temperature-dependent changes in friction were examined by Chandross et al. with Cu contacts at cryogenic temperatures. The authors observed shear-induced softening of NC metals at low temperatures, which appeared paradoxical if these temperatures simply hardened material. However, it was found that cryogenic conditions suppressed diffusion-driven coarsening toward a stronger, dislocation-dominated state and instead trapped the interface in an UNC, GB-dominated state, thus providing an operational window for requirement (6). The shear stress restructured GBs toward lower-shear-strength configurations, leading to softer and lower-friction interfaces. In total, the work on Ni and Cu alloys established that binary NC alloys are a viable route for meeting the low friction and wear criteria above. However, as several of these factors are often prescribed rather than chosen in most engineering applications (e.g., σ̃, τ̃, and T), it has been necessary to discover new NC alloys that further extend the operational windows.

NC Pt–Au alloys have emerged as model systems for extending these regimes through their segregation-stabilized microstructure and exceptional thermomechanical and tribological stability in sliding contacts. Curry et al. demonstrated that sputtered 2-μm thick Pt–Au films (10 at. % Au) exhibit extraordinary mechanical and thermal stability and ultralow wear behavior under reciprocating sliding in air at Hertzian contact pressures up to ≈1.1 GPa. Annealing up to 500 °C for 1 week caused no measurable grain-size changes, and postsliding microscopy revealed negligible microstructural evolution in wear tracks. The coefficient of friction in air was ≈0.2–0.3, specific wear rates were 3 × 10–9 mm3N–1m–1, and delamination was suppressed, as shown in Figure , indicating a transition to atomic-scale attrition from a mechanically and thermally stable microstructure. The stability arose from Au segregation to GBs and surfaces, which reduced GB energy and mobility, producing solute drag that repressed grain growth. Barr et al. quantitatively linked segregation to thermal stability, showing GB-character-dependent segregation energies and grain-growth behavior in Pt–Au films. It was also found that not all GBs were equally stabilized; GB population and connectivity thus controlled overall stability and grain-growth kinetics. In particular, large-scale atomistic simulations showed that segregation to triple junctions was the dominant mechanism in maintaining the microstructure, effectively locking the GB network and hindering its evolution. A recent combinatorial study of Pt–Au films showed that surface roughness varied from ≈5 to 10 nm, producing asperities with heights well below d*, as suggested by requirement (2). Mechanical studies on freestanding sputtered Pt–Au films (5–10 at. % Au) further explained trade-offs between stability and toughness. Heckman et al. showed that Au segregation stabilizes grain size but embrittles GBs, leading to intergranular fracture and reduced ductility, while also suggesting compositional crack arrest via heterogeneous segregation and nanocrack network formation as a toughening mechanism. Follow-on work demonstrated that solute-segregated, NC Pt–Au exhibited better high-cycle fatigue resistance, linking segregation-stabilized microstructures to cyclic mechanical reliability. Jain et al. further elucidated the strengthening mechanisms through a systematic study on the effects of grain size, solute content, texture, density, and dislocation content in five Pt–Au films and deduced that the highest hardness film benefited from nanoscale composition modulations, further extending the regime defined by requirements (3)–(4). Jain et al. also investigated very-high-cycle fatigue in Pt–Au films using a silicon MEMS resonator device and demonstrated no fatigue damage after 1010 cycles at stress amplitudes above 1 GPa, addressing the temporal response in requirement (5). At the atomistic level, MD simulations provided mechanistic insight. Cheng and Chandross showed that alloying in NC metals suppresses shear-induced grain growth, promotes GB-mediated sliding, and lowers friction compared to pure metals, where sliding drives coarsening and slip-dominated plasticity.

An interesting question arises when comparing the microstructural evolution and resulting tribological behavior of NC Pt–Au alloys to that of other NC alloys (e.g., Ni–W): how does Pt–Au retain average grain sizes of ≈40 nm and still maintain low friction and wear, whereas Ni–W shows refinement to grain sizes of ≈10–20 nm and then changes to subsequent surface properties? One potential explanation stems from the source of the microstructural evolution. Surface evolution is the direct result of a competition between defect injection through deformation (which drives grain refinement through the formation of GBs via coalescence of dislocations) and stress-driven grain growth. When in the NC regime, intracrystalline dislocation nucleation (e.g., Frank–Read source) becomes energetically unfavorable, shifting nucleation to GBs. Interestingly, in NC Pt–Au alloys, Au significantly segregates to GBs and leads to a reduction in GB energy below zero. Asaro et al. derived a theoretical model for dislocation emission in NC FCC metals. It was found that the stress required to emit a dislocation from a GB in NC FCC metals like Pt–Au was a function of the GB energy. Thus, it is conceivable that stable NC Pt–Au sliding against a smooth ceramic surface results in an optimal contact regime where stresses were below the yield strength of the metal, thus mitigating abrasive wear and bulk flow, but also below the activation stress to drive dislocation nucleation from GBs. In this regime, the alloy moderates both deformation-driven grain refinement (no dislocation emission) and stress-driven grain growth, since thermodynamic stability implies no thermodynamic (free-energy reduction) drive for grain growth. Stochastic events like fatigue-driven GB cleavage, such as can occur when defects like voids are present at GBs, and abrasive wear events, such as via localized stress increases associated with third-particle embedding in the contact, are likely the sources of any detected wear (which is extremely low) and microstructural evolution. Moreover, since Pt and Au are both noble metals, NC Pt–Au alloys are not susceptible to oxidation, removing a likely destabilization mechanism that might contribute to microstructural evolution. On the contrary, it has been found via atom probe tomography and atomistic simulations that NC Ni–W alloys (with average grain sizes of 3, 10, and 20 nm) exhibit only a mild amount of GB solute segregation, which could explain the lower barrier to microstructural evolution during sliding. Furthermore, given that the constituent materials (i.e., Ni and W) both oxidize, NC Ni–W alloys have that as an added means for destabilization. Altogether, the differences in behavior can be attributed to extent of solute segregation and susceptibility to oxidation.

In contrast to the as-deposited and relatively homogeneous films discussed above, parallel work has focused on gradient nanograined (GNG) and aperiodic microstructures. In binary alloys, GNG microstructures are exemplified by Cu-based systems. In these studies, a thick subsurface gradient (nanograins transitioning to coarse grains through the film thickness) enables persistent low friction under dry sliding by stabilizing the near-surface deformation mode. Rather than forming a brittle, delaminating tribolayer (often seen in uniformly nanograined or coarse-grained metals), the GNG architecture distributed plastic strain over depth and maintained a continuous, nanoscale shear zone that accommodated repeated sliding without roughening or spallation. , A key mechanistic point is that friction was governed less by hardness alone and more by whether the gradient structure prevented strain localization and microstructural instability; when the gradient degraded (e.g., via wear-off, grain coarsening, subsurface cracking), a low-to-high friction transition occurred. Thus, sustained low friction was linked to kinetic persistence of a stable nanograined shear layer supported by the deeper gradient buffer. For higher-order alloys, analogous ideas appear in surface-nanocrystallized and gradient-structured steels and stainless steels. A recent review paper on surface nanocrystallization methods in steels reported consistent wear-resistance improvements and attributed the benefits to increased hardness, compressive residual stress, and modified tribolayer/tribo-oxide formation, while also emphasizing gaps in the understanding of nanograin stability on sliding and elevated temperatures. Gradient nanograined austenitic stainless steels (e.g., 316L and 304) showed improved repeated-sliding performance, commonly interpreted as arising from the coupled effects of high hardness and strain accommodation of the graded surface layer (reducing running-in damage and delaying subsurface cracking/delamination). , A related multicomponent instance was a nanocrystalline Al-based alloy with a hard, multiphase nanostructure; its wear resistance and reduced friction were linked to high hardness and formation/compaction of protective debris-based layers during sliding. Additional details on reducing friction and wear via predesigned or tribo-induced heterostructures can be found elsewhere. In aperiodic microstructures, work on nanocrystalline Al–Cu–Fe quasicrystalline and approximant thin films, and sputtered Al–Cu–Fe films processed to develop quasicrystalline structure, targeted tribological behavior via phase constitution and nanoscale film microstructure. While details differ across processing and post-treatment methods, the friction-lowering mechanisms were typically associated with reduced adhesive junction formation at the contact and a tendency to form stable surface films/transfer layers during sliding aided by the hard, intermetallic/quasicrystalline coating. − As such, both GNG and aperiodic approaches address requirements (1)–(3) by maintaining a persistent near-surface steady state on sliding but achieve the improvements via different means (i.e., subsurface stability for GNG metals vs interface adhesion/film formation for quasicrystalline coatings).

Stabilization Strategies: Tribolayers and Entropy

Interestingly, it has recently been established that NC Pt–Au alloys come with an additional stabilization mechanismthe catalytic nature of Pt enables in-operando growth of self-lubricating amorphous carbon films that significantly reduce the interfacial shear strength, thus providing an added route for also addressing requirement (1). Argibay et al. first established that catalytically active NC alloys like Pt–Au mechanochemically convert environmental hydrocarbons into surface-bound amorphous carbon “tribofilms” on sliding. The self-generated, low-shear tribofilms formed under sufficient contact stress were continually renewed by mechanochemical, also termed tribocatalytic for sliding contacts, reactions that yield steady-state low friction and wear without predeposited coatings, as shown in Figure in different environments. The work established the basic phenomenology for tribofilm formation on Pt–Au: nucleation and growth were coupled to mechanical work at the interface and the catalytic character of the alloy. Jones et al. later extended the research on Pt–Au films in dry N2 with trace organics. The authors observed pressure-induced nucleation and growth of carbonaceous films, 50–200 nm thick, with embedded Pt–Au nanoparticles. Steady-state friction coefficients were found to be as low as μ ≈ 0.015 and inversely proportional to pressure, showing non-Amontonian behavior. A transition from net film growth to removal near 1.2 GPa demonstrated that steady-state friction was governed by a balance between cyclical tribofilm formation and erosion through wear, rather than by further evolution of the Pt–Au coating microstructure. DelRio et al. then systematically varied Pt–Au composition (0–10 at. % Au) and showed that increasing Au reduces average grain size and enhances GB solute segregation, leading to higher hardness and lower modulus. Higher Au content suppressed substrate wear and favored stable carbon-based tribofilm growth with near-edge X-ray absorption fine structure spectra showing similarities to those of hydrogenated amorphous carbon. Steady-state friction and wear decreased with Au, demonstrating composition-based tunability of tribofilm behavior, which was linked to the underlying Pt–Au microstructure. Edwards et al. further explored four Pt–Au alloys under pressure-dependent sliding in dry N2 with trace organics. Multitechnique characterization revealed that tribofilm chemistry, structure, and mechanics arise from interplay between catalytic and mechanochemical pathways, controlled jointly by the Pt–Au electronic structure via composition and contact pressure. The authors provided design rules for tailoring mechanocatalytic activity via stoichiometry and mechanical properties. Huynh et al. synthesized these and other studies into a general tribocatalysis framework, emphasizing how composition, environment, and stress govern whether and how low-shear carbon films form, the bonding character (e.g., amorphous vs graphitic), and the resulting friction. In all, the results can be interpreted using an extended form of the Argibay et al. mechanistic framework, where the microstructure is a metal–carbon composite tribolayer, with the carbon tribofilm accommodating shear and the NC alloy providing stiffness and catalytic functionality. Moreover, it was established that NC Pt–Au alloys provide an effective pathway for extending all six operational requirements described above for low friction and wear.

Another class of materials that exhibit low friction and wear behavior are nanocrystalline and nanocomposite transition-metal nitride coatings intentionally triboactivated with catalytically active metals to create thin, low-shear tribofilms under sliding. Several studies examined Cu-based nitride nanocomposites (e.g., MoN-Cu, VN-Cu, MoVN-Cu) in neat liquid alkanes (e.g., decane, dodecane, hexadecane), showing that sliding can trigger tribocatalytic hydrocarbon decomposition and in situ growth of amorphous carbon tribofilms that substantially reduce friction and wear relative to steel benchmarks, with stability depending on contact pressure, temperature, and alkane chain length. Follow-on work extended this concept to fuel-lubricated conditions, where MoVN-Cu maintains low friction/wear across operating variables; TEM/EDS and Raman linked nanoscale Cu clusters with C-rich regions in the wear track, supporting a “self-replenishing” mechanism in which film removal re-exposes catalytic Cu sites that renucleate C growth. A related high-throughput, composition-gradient MoN-Cu study indicated environment-composition coupling, with higher Cu content worsening wear in decane but improving wear in ethanol, consistent with solvent chemistry altering carbon-film formation kinetics and/or bonding. Beyond fuels, a MoN-Cu study explicitly anchored the microstructural scale: at an optimal N2 flow, the coating contains ≈6.4 nm Mo x N grains plus a Cu phase and reported ultralow friction (≈0.003) in harsh lubricated testing (engine oil with MoDTC additive and PAO), attributing performance to tribofilm formation from nitride-Cu architecture and lubricant/additive interactions. Ag-containing nitrides established environment-switchable tribochemistry: V–Ag–N (with Ag as a solute) yielded low friction under dry sliding via silver vanadate formation and under oil lubrication via in situ onion-like carbon formation from base oil, whereas other work on MoN-Ag and TiN-Cu suggested analogous C-based tribofilm pathways. , Finally, CrAlN-derived “triboactive” coatings incorporating Mo/Cu emphasized that tribofilm formation on the coating is crucial to protect steel counterfaces in additivated PAO, reinforcing the practical role of dopants in enabling lubricant-derived reaction layers. ,

Despite the successes with binary NC and nitride systems, more recent work has focused on the advent of metals with more alloying elements for stabilization (e.g., ternary alloys, CCAs, and HEAs). In TiNbZr-Ag, Liu et al. showed that sliding generated a multilayer tribologically affected zone topped by an amorphous–crystalline nanocomposite (i.e., nanocrystals embedded in an amorphous, O- and Fe-rich matrix with Ag enrichment). The surface layer had a compressive yield strength of ≈2.4 GPa and deformed homogeneously to ≈20% strain, measured via nanopillar compression, reducing wear rates by about an order of magnitude. Its combination of high strength and substantial ductility allowed it to act as a robust, deformable layer that accommodated shear through in situ formation of a strong and deformable oxide, thus further addressing requirements (1) and (3). In ultrafine-grained (UFG) TiMoNb, Zhu et al. illustrated how GB segregation and nanoprecipitates can be used to stabilize a UFG matrix and support protective oxide glazes at high temperatures. At room temperature, it was found that the TiMoNb alloys exhibited wear resistance comparable to alumina, as demonstrated in the friction and wear characteristics of the TiMoNb surfaces and alumina balls. Ti-enriched GB films and coherent B2 nanoprecipitates impeded grain growth up to 600 °C; during sliding at temperature, a dense NC metallic-oxide glaze formed at the surface, preserving low friction and wear rates (≈10–6 mm3N–1m–1). Similarly, Luo et al. showed that TaMoNb alloys evolved on sliding into amorphous–crystalline surface nanocomposite layers and a gradient nanostructure, which both yielded enhanced wear resistance. In Ni­(AlNbTiV) and Ni3(AlNbTiV) alloys, Geng et al. demonstrated that predesigned nanocoupled grains, nanoprecipitates, and composition undulations suppressed grain growth and enabled temperature-dependent tribolayers. At room temperature, extremely thin NC/UFG layers formed, while from 600 to 800 °C, in situ tribo-oxidation produced a dense NC glaze with alumina nanoparticles in an amorphous oxide matrix. The hardness and modulus of the glaze exceeded those of the substrate and provided ultralow wear from room temperature to 800 °C, thereby extending the operational temperature window in requirement (6). Jones et al. illustrated that sliding can generate UNC and dynamically amorphized metallic surface layers with low friction and wear in the absence of oxides via ultrahigh vacuum tests on CoCrFeMnNi; the low-friction case resulted in grains smaller than 10 nm, as shown by the representative cross-sectional TEM images of wear tracks (Figure ). As such, the work established amorphous-UNC oxide tribolayers as a general route to low-shear NC alloys. Other CCAs/HEAs followed similar patterns. CoCrNiCu developed Cu-rich nanolayers and compacted oxide glazes on sliding, improving wear despite reduced hardness. CoFeNi2 and associated alloys formed tribo-induced nanocrystallites with amorphous GBs or self-organized tribolayers that enhance wear resistance. , HEA composites with solid-lubricant phases used controlled oxide and second-phase films to achieve stable performance over wide temperature ranges. Altogether, the work established oxide glazes and amorphous-UNC nanocomposites as potential engineered tribolayers in CCAs/HEAs, which can be used to enhance microstructural and thermal stability via improvements to the operational windows defined in requirements (1)–(6). The chemical complexity (often discussed in the context of configurational entropy) stabilized the interface by reducing microstructural mobility; segregation/solute drag and precipitate/architecture effects inhibited shear or heat-assisted grain growth and localization, thus keeping a strong load-bearing subsurface. It also enabled adaptive, mechanically compatible tribolayers that sustained cyclic shear without cracking/spalling and regenerated continuously during sliding. Destabilization Factors: Irradiation and Environment

It is important to note that GB stabilization in NC alloys does not improve resistance to all external stimuli. For instance, alloying in NC systems produces mixed outcomes under irradiation: some designs clearly enhance radiation tolerance, while others mainly expand microstructural and thermal stability but are vulnerable to irradiation-driven evolution. Schoell et al. studied both NC Pt and Pt–Au thin films under ion irradiation. As before, the NC Pt–Au was processed to produce a solute-stabilized GB network that resists thermal grain growth, while the NC Pt served as a chemically simpler baseline. Under ion irradiation (20 MeV Au4+ at room temperature and at 500 °C), both NC Pt and Pt–Au underwent substantial irradiation-induced grain growth, indicating that neither microstructure is radiation-stable. As such, Pt–Au did not show a clear suppression of irradiation-driven coarsening relative to NC Pt. Mechanistically, this implied that the Pt–Au GB stabilization that works against curvature- and diffusion-driven thermal coarsening was overridden by cascade-induced GB motion and radiation-enhanced diffusion/defect fluxes (i.e., alloying Pt with Au improved thermal but not radiation stability of the NC structure). In NC Cu, in situ TEM experiments with single-beam (1 MeV Kr2+) and dual-beam (1 MeV Kr2+, 12 keV He+) irradiations showed extensive GB migration and grain coarsening in films with initial grain sizes of 10s of nm. Additional NC Cu studies (with He implantation at 450 °C and heavy-ion irradiations) similarly reported strong GB sink behavior but with significant irradiation-induced grain growth and defect clustering. , For NC Cu–Zr films, ball-milled for 10 h and heat-treated to create either ordered GBs or amorphous intergranular films (AIFs), quantitative TEM image analysis showed that AIF-containing films had lower defect-cluster number/area densities and much smaller irradiation-induced grain growth than the ordered-GB state at the same doses. The positive alloying mechanism was explicit: Zr-induced AIFs provided excess free volume and high defect mobility at GBs, thus both reducing retained damage and suppressing GB-driven coarsening relative to NC Cu, as shown in Figure . For NC Ni, pulsed-laser-deposited films irradiated with 3 MeV Ni self-ions and 10 keV He implantation, followed by TEM annealing, developed cavities that preferentially formed and grew on GBs; subsequent annealing produced cavities comparable to the grain size and even spanning the film thickness. This result reflected limited radiation tolerance and strong GB-controlled cavity evolution. In contrast, NC Ni–W with an initial grain diameter of 6 nm, irradiated with Ni+ self-ions to doses up to 100 dpa, showed grain growth to 32 nm by 10 dpa and then grain growth stagnation from 10 to 100 dpa. Orientation and boundary analysis linked stagnation to the development of a biaxial texture and an increased fraction of low-energy GBs. Consequently, W alloying had mixed effects: it did not prevent early irradiation-induced coarsening, but eventually drove the W-doped network to low-energy, low-mobility GBs, yielding a radiation-stabilized microstructure that pure NC Ni did not achieve. The implications of the finding in the context of friction and wear behavior are clearin application areas where NC alloys may be subjected to both irradiation and shear, the extent of stabilization is alloy-dependent, with some seeing benefits and others seeing none.

External stimuli like radiation can not only result in destabilization of the NC alloy but also in the destabilization of the self-lubricating tribofilms. For example, on NC Pt–Au alloys, environmental species largely determine whether carbon-based tribofilms can form and persist. In dry, oxygen-free nitrogen with only trace hydrocarbons, Pt–Au supported the growth of thick (≈50–200 nm), amorphous carbon tribofilms that yielded ultralow friction (μ ≈ 0.015) and exhibited a well-defined transition from net growth to wear with increasing contact pressure. , In that case, tribofilm evolution was governed by mechanocatalytic kinetics and stress, not by oxidation. However, when exposed to water and/or oxygen, it was found that tribofilm stability was largely tied to the exact makeup of the environment. In environments with IPA and water, friction coefficients remained elevated at μ ≈0.2, similar to values for a-C:H films under contact conditions at similar humidity. In high and low concentrations of anhydrous hexanes, μ ranged from 0.05 to 0.2 (friction decreased as the hexane concentration decreased) and decreased further after the hexanes were fully replaced with nitrogen. The results in different environments implied that the presence of water alone did not appear to inhibit the tribochemical formation of a-C:H, but did compete for adsorption at Pt sites and modulate the balance between nucleation, passivation, and oxidation of carbon. Perhaps more importantly, it was found that a combination of oxygen and water completely inhibited film formation and the subsequent low friction and wear behavior. The formation of a well-adhered and highly cross-linked film depends on chain scission and polymerization as an intermediate step, where ambient hydrocarbons must exhibit bond scission followed by a high probability of carbon–carbon bond formation, which gradually leads to the formation of amorphous sp3 carbon networks exhibiting extremely high wear resistance. One possible mechanism that can curtail this formation is an inhibition of polymerization through an increase in the probability of oxidation/hydroxylation of free carbon bonds after chain scission. Another possible mechanism is the effect that the same environmental species can have on the passivation of the Pt surface, which is likely a significant factor in the reduction of the activation barrier for hydrocarbon chain scission. In a similar way, too high of a concentration of environmental species is also known to prohibit the transition to a stable, low friction, wear-resistant tribofilm by flooding the contact with hydrocarbons. In this limiting case, the ability of hydrocarbons to undergo polymerization and cross-linking is inhibited by reducing their surface residence time. For other carbon-based tribofilms, especially those formed tribochemically on steels and other alloys, oxygen similarly worked as a destabilization factor. Zhang et al. showed that carbonaceous films readily formed and resulted in low friction under shear in O2-free N2 and H2, but were then rapidly removed such that friction returned to air-like levels in dry air (Figure ). The gas-switching and in situ Raman experiments indicated that such films were mechanically weak and needed to be continuously replenished; O2 diverted mechanochemically generated hydrocarbon radicals into oxygenated intermediates and ultimately CO/CO2, blocking net film growth. Several reviews on tribocatalysis , generalized this picture to Pt-group and other catalytic metals: reducing or inert atmospheres enabled the in situ formation of carbon (e.g., a-C, graphite, graphene, nanotubes) and oxidizing, humid, high-temperature environments favored oxidative thinning and film failure. It was surmised that temperature could be used to further tune the process by accelerating both tribocatalytic formation and oxidative etching; on Pt-containing surfaces, this was expected to produce sharp transitions between the formation-dominated and oxidation-dominated regimes as T and O2/H2O ratios are varied. In contrast, predeposited carbon films were less formation-limited but still suffered oxidation and humidity-induced changes in friction; their greater thickness and stronger interfacial adhesion made them more tolerant to oxygen, water, and temperature than thinner, replenishment-controlled tribofilms on catalytic metals.

Open Questions and Future Directions

Across the recent studies, several important research gaps and open questions remain about how nanocrystalline/ultrafine-grained metals respond to tribological loading. First, the generality and limits of the “steady-state grain size-friction regime” framework are unclear. Ni–W and Cu/Au/Ag clearly evolved toward a stress-dependent surface grain size of order 10 to 20 nm, with corresponding transitions between GB-mediated and dislocation-mediated plasticity and friction regimes. − However, Pt–Au maintained ≈40 nm grains, with negligible microstructural evolution even after extensive sliding while still showing low friction and ultralow wear. Similarly, high-temperature alloys (e.g., Ni­(AlNbTiV), CoCrNi) formed oxide-metal nanocomposite glazes whose grain size was multicomponent and not simply comparable to metallic nanograins. , Whether a unified equilibrium length scale exists for metallic, oxide, and mixed tribolayersand how it depends on stress, temperature, and chemistryremains open. Second, studies on electrical loading are largely missing. The main papers on friction/wear in NC alloys were purely mechanical, , despite explicit motivation from sliding electrical contacts. As such, the data provided no insight into how current, Joule heating, and electromigration interact with GB networks and solute segregation and thus friction and wear. This limits relevance to real connectors and switches, where local current densities and temperature spikes can drive GB diffusion and voiding. Interestingly, Argibay et al. leveraged cross-sectional TEM images of tribofilms on Pt–Au alloys to reveal their constituent components, namely, Pt and Au nanoparticles in an amorphous carbon matrix. The concentration of metallic nanoparticles in tribofilms will likely dictate their conductivity, suggesting that studies combining friction and wear experiments, controlled current and contact-resistance measurements, and post-mortem TEM of wear tracks will be essential for co-optimization of properties. Similar work will be required to determine irradiation-friction and temperature-friction linkages. Third, there are several open questions on how to systematically design, control, and generalize predesigned and tribo-induced heterostructures to attain specific friction and wear responses. There is still a demand for quantitative design rules that map alloy chemistry, contact stress, temperature, and environment onto a specific optimal architecture (e.g., gradient, lamellar, nanohierarchical, oxide nanocomposite); current successes with gradient nanograined Cu − and nanohierarchical CCAs/HEAs , remain largely case-by-case. It is also unclear how predesigned heterostructures (e.g., grain-size gradients, precipitate networks, segregation patterns) actually steer tribo-induced evolution; e.g., what conditions lead to film survival and what conditions result in microstructural coarsening. Similar studies will be required on Pt–Au films to elucidate how the current trends with columnar microstructures might change with equiaxed films. Fourth, the environments in which Pt–Au and other NC alloys have been tested are largely limited to ambient conditions, dry N2, and hydrocarbons. In the work on Pt–Au in dry N2 and hydrocarbons, gas analysis was utilized to measure the hydrocarbon species and concentrations. However, a study where the species and concentrations are systematically varied across numerous contact switch environments is still missing. Furthermore, it will be important to complement these systematic friction and wear experiments with post-mortem atomic force microscopy (AFM) and near-edge X-ray absorption fine structure spectroscopy to determine the properties and structure of the resulting tribofilms. Fifth, it will be imperative to explore lower-cost and easier-to-manufacture alternatives for several of these NC alloys. On costs related to Pt–Au, for instance, a recent cost-benefit analysis by Desai et al. observed that the price of pure Au was roughly twice that of pure Pt per ounce, perhaps making Pt–Au too costly depending on the application and setting the stage for lower-cost options.

The three areas likely to see continued development in the near term to answer these open questions are combinatorial thin-film synthesis tools, high-throughput mechanical and tribological testing platforms, and ML-based decision engines in closed loops. On combinatorial synthesis tools, some platforms for thin-film metal alloysespecially binary alloys, CCAs, and HEAsare already in place or in development, with thermal coevaporation and magnetron cosputtering as the archetypes. For binary and ternary alloys, typical material types include Al–Fe, Al–Gd, Ag–Pd, Ni/Cu/Ag–W, Pt–Au–Pd, and Pt–Au, all deposited as one-dimensional strips or two-dimensional spreads on glass/borosilicate slides or silicon wafers − and some exhibiting NC features. − For CCAs and HEAs, multicathode magnetron sputtering from 3 to 5 targets on 3 to 4 in wafers produced continuous composition spreads or discretized pad arrays. − On high-throughput characterization, notable advancements are being made in AFM, nanoindentation, and tribometry. In AFM, it is possible to isolate single-asperity contacts in controlled idealized geometries (i.e., known tip radius, load, and environment), thereby providing the basis for validating adhesion and interfacial shear laws and modeling more complicated interfaces that occur in practical electrical contact applications; , examples of the methods include amplitude modulation-frequency modulation, contact resonance force microscopy, and high-speed friction measurements. − In nanoindentation, several groups have created high-speed mapping tools, resolving microstructural heterogeneity − and enabling property tomograms. In tribometry, Bassett et al. and later Babuska et al. demonstrated a custom-built high-throughput ball-on-flat bidirectional linear reciprocating tribometer capable of simultaneously testing six samples at a time. On ML-based decision engines, there are several cases of closed-loop, multiobjective, multifidelity frameworks for the mechanical properties of binary alloys, CCAs, and HEAs. Desai et al. used combinatorial synthesis and high-throughput tools with feedforward neural networks to enable high-dimensional process-structure–property relations in Pt–Au alloys, yielding NC alloys with high hardness and low resistivity for electrical contact switch applications. Bouri et al. demonstrated uncertainty-aware ML and Bayesian optimization for large-scale in-silico screening of HEA compositions with high Vickers hardness. Huang et al. used density functional theory ML-guided design to discover a single-phase, body-centered cubic Nb4Ta4V3Ti alloy that exhibited castability, excellent room-temperature yield strength (1 GPa) and ductility (20% uniform strain), and excellent high-temperature strength (500 MPa at 1000 °C). Altogether, the advancements in the three core areas are starting to yield significant developments in low-friction and wear NC alloys. Brown et al. recently used multimodal variations of regression and variational autoencoder (VAE) models to predict the evolution of cycle-dependent friction for Pt–Au alloys. The models leveraged encoder and decoder networks to translate between diverse data sourcesincluding chemical information from X-ray fluorescence, mechanical data from nanoindentation, and simulated atomistic parameters from SimTraand the dynamics of friction evolution over time. The work showed that VAE significantly outperformed the regression model in high-dimensional scenarios, and that the first 10% of friction coefficients proved to be particularly useful, as it captured the initial trends that are critical for predicting long-term friction. To enable even faster materials discovery in this field, it will become necessary to fully combine and automate the synthesis, characterization, and ML tools onto single platforms to create self-driving laboratories. Inorganics-focused reviews have formalized the required triad of modular robotics, automated interpretation, and active learning in experimental design, distinguishing closed-loop autonomy from high-throughput screening. , The next step will be the inclusion of these components into a tribology-centric framework.
