LRIG2 suppresses NK cell-induced GSDME-mediated pyroptosis via the LAMP1-STAT3 pathway in glioma
Authors: Xiaoshuang Hou, Lan Lin, Po Zhang, Youwei Li, Minhai Dong, Sui Chen, Xiaohu Oyang, Jingbo Wang, Fangling Cheng, Xiaopeng Li, Min Zhao, Feng Mao, Xingjiang Yu, Desheng Hu, Baofeng Wang, Dongsheng Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGliomas are resistant to current therapies, and natural killer (NK) cell-based immunotherapy shows limited efficacy. Gasdermin E (GSDME)-mediated pyroptosis contributes to NK-induced tumor killing, but NK cell dysfunction in the glioma microenvironment remains poorly understood. This study investigates whether leucine-rich repeats and immunoglobulin-like domains 2 (LRIG2) enable glioma cells to evade NK-induced pyroptosis and elucidates the underlying mechanism.MethodsWe used in vitro co-culture systems with glioma cell lines (LN229, HS683, FU) and primary NK cells or NK92-MI cells to assess cytotoxicity, pyroptosis (lactate dehydrogenase release, morphology, GSDME cleavage by western blot), and cytokine release (Cytometric Bead Array). In vivo, orthotopic glioma models in C57BL/6 and RAG1-KO mice received intracranial stimulator of interferon genes agonist diABZI. Mechanistic dissection involved biochemical approaches (co-immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry and membrane protein extraction), cellular imaging (immunofluorescence), and genetic perturbation via CRISPR-mediated knockdown.ResultsNK cells induced GSDME-dependent pyroptosis in glioma cells in vitro, but this was impaired in vivo due to suppressed NK cytotoxicity. LRIG2 was highly expressed in gliomas; its overexpression inhibited GSDME cleavage, pyroptosis, and glioma cell death, whereas LRIG2 knockdown enhanced these effects. Mechanistically, soluble LRIG2 shed from glioma cells bound to lysosomal-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1) on NK cells, upregulating phosphorylated-signal transducer and activator of transcription (p-STAT3) via Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) and reducing granzyme B and perforin release. Disrupting the LRIG2–LAMP1–STAT3 axis by LRIG2 knockdown or STAT3 knockout restored NK cytotoxicity and GSDME cleavage. Combining LRIG2 knockdown with diABZI significantly enhanced NK granzyme B expression and prolonged mouse survival.ConclusionsLRIG2 enables glioma immune evasion by suppressing NK-induced GSDME-mediated pyroptosis via the LAMP1–STAT3 pathway. Targeting this axis represents a promising strategy to enhance NK cell-based immunotherapy for gliomas, positioning LRIG2 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

We discovered that although NK cells can kill glioma cells via gasdermin E (GSDME)-dependent pyroptosis rather than gasdermin D or gasdermin B in vitro, glioma cells evade this killing through leucine-rich repeats and immunoglobulin-like domains 2 (LRIG2). LRIG2 binds to lysosomal-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP1) on NK cells, upregulates phosphorylated-signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT3), and suppresses the release of granzyme B and perforin, thereby impairing NK cell antitumor activity and conferring resistance to pyroptosis.

Background

Gliomas, particularly glioblastoma (GBM), the most lethal glioma subtype, pose formidable challenges due to their aggressive behavior, resistance to therapy, and inexorable recurrence. Although significant advances have been made in delineating the genetic drivers, cellular heterogeneity, and progression mechanisms of gliomas,1–4 these insights have yet to translate into effective treatments, and the median survival for patients with isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)-wild-type glioblastoma is about 8–21 months, depending on the study.5

Recent advancements in the field of immunotherapy—which uses strategies to reenergize or alter the immune system to target cancer—have shown striking results in patients with many types of malignancy, but have again been proven relatively ineffective in treating GBM.6 Single-cell profiling has revealed a deeply immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) in glioma, where the immune infiltrate is predominantly composed of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)/microglia and immunosuppressive myeloid cells like myeloid-derived suppressor cells, with only a paucity of effector T lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells.7 GBM is considered immunologically to be a cold tumor.8

A promising avenue for the treatment of glioma lies in pyroptosis, a form of programmed cell death that triggers antitumor immunity by releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines, augmenting macrophage phagocytosis, and activating cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and NK cells, which transforms “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors, thereby enhancing the antitumor effects of various treatments.9 As the principal executive protein of pyroptosis, the gasdermin family plays a pivotal role in influencing pyroptosis-associated outcomes in tumors and can serve as a regulatory target.10 Notably, granzyme A released from CTLs and NK cells cleaves gasdermin B (GSDMB), while granzyme B (GzmB) targets gasdermin E (GSDME), thereby triggering the pyroptotic cascade in cancer cells.10 11 Therefore, harnessing NK cell-induced pyroptosis emerges as a feasible approach for glioma treatment.

Our research team has focused on the leucine-rich repeats and immunoglobulin-like domains 2 (LRIG) gene family (LRIG1, LRIG2, and LRIG3). LRIG1 and LRIG3 predominantly act as tumor suppressors, whereas LRIG2 functions as an oncogenic driver.12–15 We recently demonstrated that LRIG proteins contribute to shaping the immunosuppressive glioma microenvironment.16 17 In particular, LRIG2 promotes the polarization of TAMs towards an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype and concurrently inhibits their phagocytic capacity.16 In this study, we further explored whether LRIG2 engages additional mechanisms to sculpt the immunosuppressive TME. We examined the impact of modulating LRIG2 expression in glioma cells on various immune populations and observed that LRIG2 suppresses the cytotoxic activity of infiltrating NK cells. Prompted by this observation, we investigated the underlying mechanisms and found that LRIG2 inhibits NK cell-induced pyroptosis in glioma cells through the prevention of GSDME cleavage. Mechanistically, LRIG2 binds to LAMP1 on the surface of NK cells; this interaction upregulates phosphorylated-signal transducer and activator of transcription (p-STAT3) expression and suppresses the release of GzmB and perforin, thereby impairing NK cell antitumor function. These findings establish that LRIG2-mediated inhibition of GSDME cleavage confers resistance to NK cell-induced pyroptosis in gliomas. Consequently, therapeutic strategies aimed at disrupting the LRIG2–LAMP1–STAT3 axis in NK cells to restore GSDME-mediated pyroptosis may represent a viable approach for treating gliomas with high GSDME expression.

Methods

Experimental mouse models

5–6 weeks old male mice were used in our study. To investigate the infiltration of NK cells into glioma and the functional changes, 2×105 wild-type (WT) or mutated GL261 cells, genetically modified knockdown LRIG2 by short hairpin RNA, suspended in 20 µL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were transplanted into the frontal lobes of brains by stereotaxic intracranial injection as previously described.17 In order to observe the role of sufficient amounts of NK in glioma, we injected a diABZI stimulator of interferon genes (STING) agonist (Selleck, Cat# S8796) or its dilution into the glioma tumor to trigger NK cell enrichment in the tumor. To confirm the effect of LRIG2 on NK cell function, we used Rag1 knockout mice with stereotactic intracranial injections of WT or LRIG2 overexpressing HS683. Mice were immediately euthanized on reaching predetermined clinical endpoints, such as a weight loss of less than 20% of their original body weight or the onset of neurological symptoms like claudication or drowsiness, except during survival experiments. Subsequently, the blood, tumor in the brain, and deep cervical lymph nodes of mice were isolated to prepare single-cell suspension for flow cytometry to analyze the function of immune cells.

diABZI-induced NK cell recruitment assay

C57BL/6 mice were intraperitoneally injected with 1 mL of sterile 3% thioglycollate to induce sterile peritonitis and recruit inflammatory cells to the peritoneal cavity. For the diABZI treatment group, mice received a co-injection of thioglycollate with diABZI at a dose of 1.25 mg/kg via the intraperitoneal route. Control mice received thioglycollate alone (vehicle control). At 72 hours post-injection, mice were euthanized and peritoneal cells were harvested by lavage with 5–10 mL of ice-cold PBS containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The lavage fluid was collected into sterile tubes on ice. Cells were centrifuged (300×g, 5 min, 4°C), and the cell pellet was resuspended in fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer for flow cytometry. The percentage of NK cells was calculated as the proportion of NK cells among total CD45+ immune cells.

In vivo GSDME cleavage assay

Human glioma HS683 cells were cultured under standard conditions and harvested for intracranial implantation into RAG1 knockout mice. Tumor-bearing mice were randomly assigned to receive an intracranial injection of the STING agonist diABZI at either a low dose (1.5 mg/kg) or a high dose (3 mg/kg), with control mice receiving an equal volume of normal saline (online supplemental figure S2G). At day 15 post-implantation, tumor tissues were harvested, and GSDME cleavage was assessed by western blotting using standard protocols.

Single-cell isolation from tissues and flow cytometry

For human tissue single-cell suspensions, Fc receptor blocking was performed on the total cell suspension using Human TruStain FcX (Fc Receptor Blocking Solution, BioLegend, Cat# 422301) followed by surface staining using the universal flow cytometry staining protocol. In general, after counting the cell suspension, it would be divided into three equal parts and stained according to flow cytometry antibody color scheme, the cells that are only stained on the surface would be directly analyzed by flow cytometry, if intracellular staining was required, the cells would be fixed and then intracellularly stained and then analyzed by flow cytometry. For mouse tissues, cells were counted and divided into equal numbers according to experimental needs, and then flow cytometry analysis was performed. When analyzing NK cell function, we employ specific flow cytometry schemes tailored for human and mouse samples, as illustrated in online supplemental figure S1A and S2A, respectively. The antibodies and reagents used are: CD45-APC Cy7 (BioLegend Cat# 368516, RRID: AB_2566376), CD3-FITC (BioLegend Cat# 300306, RRID:AB_314042), CD56-Cy7 (BioLegend Cat# 362512, RRID:AB_2564085), P2Y12-PE (BioLegend Cat# S16001E, RRID:AB_3674097), CD8-PercP Cy5.5 (BioLegend Cat# 344710, RRID:AB_2044010), GzmB-APC (BioLegend Cat# 372204, RRID:AB_2687028), Perforin-BV421 (BioLegend Cat# 308122, RRID:AB_2566204), CD45-FITC (BioLegend Cat# 157607, RRID:AB_283-2553), NK1.1-PE Cy7 (BD Biosciences Cat# 552878, RRID:AB_394507), CD3-APC Cy7 (BioLegend Cat# 100222, RRID:AB_2242784), CD8-PercP Cy5.5 (BD Biosciences Cat# 551162, RRID:AB_394081), CD107a-BV421 (BD Biosciences Cat# 564347, RRID:AB_2738760), Zombie B55 Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend, Cat# 423122).

Cell culture

The human glioma cell line LN229 (RRID:CVCL_0393), HS683 (RRID:CV-CL_0844), human NK cell line NK92-MI (RRID:CVCL_3755) and mouse glioma cell line GL261 (NCI-DTP Cat# Glioma 261, RRID:CVCL_Y003) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The primary glioma cell lines, FU (GWH04)18 were constructed and preserved by our laboratory. The HS683, GL261, FU and LN229 cells were cultured with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine serum in a humidified 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator. NK92-MI were cultured with α-mem containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 10% HI Horse Serum (Thermo Fisher, Cat# 26050088).

Isolation of primary NK cells

For human NK cell isolation, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were first isolated by density gradient centrifugation using Ficoll (Tian Jin Hao Yang Biological Manufacture, Cat# LDS1075). NK cells were then purified from the PBMCs by negative selection using a magnetic-activated cell sorting kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat# 130–092-657). The purified NK cells were maintained in complete Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (RPMI 1640) medium supplemented with interleukin (IL)-15 (5 ng/ mL) and IL-2 (100 U/mL).

Establishment of cell lines

The glioma cells with LRIG1/2/3 overexpression and LRIG2 knockdown were established as described previously.16 17 19 The CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing technology was applied to establish STAT3 knockout NK92-MI or IL-6 knockout HS683 cell lines. The designed target sgRNA sequences (STAT3 Forward: CACCGAGCTACAGCAGCTTGACACA, STAT3 Reverse: AAACTGTGTCAAGCTGCTGTAGCTC; IL-6 Forward: CACCGATTCGTTCTGAAGAGGTGAG, IL-6 Reverse AAACCTCACCTCTTCAGAACGAATC) were cloned into the Lenti-CRISPRv2 plasmid backbone (RRID:Addgene_98290) and transfected into cells. Stable cell lines were screened with puromycin, and monoclonal cells were selected to obtain STAT3 or IL-6 knockout cell lines.

To establish LAMP1-knockdown and control models, freshly purified NK cells were transfected with either siLAMP1 or a non-targeting control siRNA (siNC) using the Human Natural Killer Cell Nucleofector Kit (Lonza, Cat# VPA-1005).

Co-culture of NK cells and glioma cells

Glioma cell lines HS683, FU, and LN229 were seeded into culture plates. Once the cells adhered, the medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM. NK92-MI cells or primary NK cells were centrifuged and washed two times with serum-free DMEM. NK cells were then added to the glioma cells at an efficacy-to-target ratio of 2:1 (NK Cells: glioma cells), and the co-culture was maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 2 hours.

Assessment of NK killing activity

There were three main ways to evaluate the effect of NK killing activity in this study: (1) FVD (Zombie B55 Fixable Viability Kit, BioLegend, Cat# 423122) was used to label dead cells, and CD45 or carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE) was used to label NK cells or tumor cells, and the killing efficiency of NK cells was determined by flow cytometry detection of the proportion of tumor cell death. (2) The proportion of target cells pyroptosis was determined by western blot (WB) detection of the proportion of GSDME cleavage indicating the pyroptosis of tumor cells. (3) Cell viability efficiency was measured by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release in the supernatant. The assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit, Beyotime, Cat# C0016). Cells were cultured in 96-well plates and divided into the following groups: medium (background control), glioma (sample control), glioma for subsequent lysis (maximum enzyme activity control), NK92-MI with glioma, and NK92-MI supernatant with glioma. 1 hour before the end of cell culture, add 20 µL of the LDH release reagent to the maximum enzyme activity control group. After incubating the cells for 2 hours, 120 µL of the cell supernatant was collected, followed by the addition of 60 µL of LDH assay working solution. The mixture was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. The absorbance of each well was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader to calculate cytotoxicity.

Detecting the release of cytotoxic cytokines by NK cells

According to the instructions of the LEGENDplex Human CD8/NK Panel kit (BioLegend, Cat# 741186), we measured the release of cytokines from NK cell supernatants, including GzmB, perforin, interferon (IFN)-γ, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α.

Enrichment of LRIG2 secreted in tumor culture supernatant

Glioma cells HS683, FU, and LN229 were seeded into culture plates. After cell adhesion, the medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM, and the cells were incubated for 12 hours. The glioma supernatant was collected into centrifuge tubes and concentrated by centrifugation at 4,000 revolutions per minute (RPM) for 10–15 min using Ultra Centrifugal Filters (Amicon, Cat# UFC9050) to concentrate the proteins in the glioma supernatant.

Purification of GST-sLRIG2 fusion protein

Purification of GST-sLRIG2 fusion protein was performed as described.16

Identification of LRIG2 interacting protein

For co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), cells were lysed with cell lysis buffer for western and immunoprecipitation (IP) (Beyotime, Cat# P0013) to obtain proteins. The cell lysates were incubated with BeyoMag Anti-Flag Magnetic Beads (Beyotime, Cat# P2115) overnight at 4°C. The magnetic beads were washed using cell lysis buffer and then sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) sample loading buffer was added for WB. Or, the cell lysates were incubated with LRIG2 antibody overnight at 4°C. After further incubation with Immunoprecipitation Kit with Protein A+G Magnetic Beads (Beyotime, Cat# P2179) for 3 hours at 4°C, the beads were washed and SDS-PAGE sample loading buffer was added for WB. For identification of LRIG2 interacting membrane protein, the same Co-IP protocol was used after the extraction of the membrane protein.

For Co-IP-mass spectrometry (MS), the aforementioned samples were subsequently analyzed by BGI; Shenzhen; China (RRID:SCR_011114).

Membrane protein extraction

The assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Membrane and Cytosol Protein Extraction Kit, Beyotime, Cat# P0033). After co-culturing NK cells and glioma tumor cells, membrane protein extraction reagent A was added to the cells and incubated on ice for 10–15 min. The cells were repeatedly frozen and thawed under liquid nitrogen and room temperature. Observe the extent of cell lysis under a microscope; the extent of cell lysis needs to be greater than 70%. Centrifuge the cell lysate at 700 g for 10 min at 4°C and collect the supernatant. Then, centrifuge the collected supernatant at 14,000 g for 30 min at 4°C to precipitate the cell membrane fragments. After removing the supernatant, perform another centrifugation at 14,000 g for about 10 s, carefully removing the supernatant while retaining the pellet. Add the cell lysis buffer to obtain the membrane proteins.

Western blot

For WB, cells were lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) Lysis Buffer (Servicebio, Cat# G2002) and SDS-PAGE Sample Loading Buffer to extract protein. For glioma samples, small scissors were used to cut the samples into small pieces. Then, cell lysis buffer was added and the samples were placed on ice. Ceramic beads for tissue homogenization were put into the samples, and a tissue homogenizer (Servicebio, Cat# KZ-II) was used for lysing cells. The protein was separated by SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to the 0.45 µM polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. The PVDF membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk and then incubated with primary antibodies. The membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit antibodies (Beyotime Cat# A0208, RRID: AB_2892644) or HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse antibodies (Beyotime Cat# A0216, RRID: AB_2860575). Results

NK cells induce pyroptosis in glioma cells in vitro by cleaving GSDME

To verify the cytotoxic potential of NK cells against gliomas, we co-cultured the glioma cell lines LN229 or HS683 with NK92-MI cells or primary NK cells. We first observed that NK92-MI cells approached and established contact with LN229 cells, with some even fully invading glioma cells, a phenomenon known as “in-cell killing,” which may be more effective than conventional “kiss-killing” from the outside22 (figure 2A). These observations confirm that NK cells efficiently kill glioma cells in our co-culture system. We then further performed flow cytometry and observed a significant increase in dead glioma cells (fixable viability dye (FVD)+) after co-culture with primary NK cells (figure 2B, C); this finding was corroborated by a marked increase in LDH release into the supernatant (figure 2B. C). Morphologically, co-cultured glioma cells exhibited swelling and bubble-like protrusions (figure 2D), characteristic of pyroptosis.23 Examination of gasdermin protein cleavage revealed that GSDME (also known as DFNA5), but not GSDMB or GSDMD, was cleaved in glioma cells following NK92-MI cell co-culture (figure 2E). Importantly, incubation of glioma cells with NK92-MI cell supernatant alone failed to induce GSDME cleavage (figure 2F), nor did it result in significant LDH release (figure 2G, B and C). In contrast, analysis of patient data revealed that GSDME cleavage was barely detectable in clinical samples (figure 2H). To determine whether GSDME can be cleaved in vivo, we designed the following experiment. Human glioma HS683 cells were intracranially implanted into RAG1 knockout mice, and the mice subsequently received an intracranial injection of the STING agonist diABZI at either a low dose (1.5 mg/kg) or a high dose (3 mg/kg) (online supplemental figure S2G), a treatment expected to enhance NK cell infiltration into gliomas and potentiate their cytotoxic capacity.24 25 At day 15 post-implantation, tumor tissues were harvested and subjected to western blotting to assess GSDME cleavage. The results showed that GSDME cleavage was barely detectable in tumors from control mice without diABZI treatment, whereas cleaved GSDME was readily observed in diABZI-treated mice (online supplemental figure S2G). Collectively, these findings provide direct in vivo evidence that enhanced NK cell infiltration—facilitated by STING agonism—can induce GSDME-dependent pyroptosis in glioma cells. These results indicate that NK cells induce pyroptosis by cleaving GSDME, and therefore effectively kill glioma cells in our in vitro co-culture system.

GSDME is highly expressed in patients with glioma without loss-of-function mutations

Having established that NK cells can induce GSDME-mediated pyroptosis in vitro, we next examined the clinical relevance of GSDME in gliomas. Analysis of patient data revealed that GSDME expression was significantly upregulated in both low-grade gliomas and GBM compared with adjacent normal tissues (figure 2H, I). However, high GSDME expression did not correlate with improved overall survival (figure 2J). As loss-of-function mutations could explain this disconnect,10 we analyzed the mutation status of GSDME. Only approximately 1% of patients with glioma harbored GSDME mutations, none of which occurred at the critical cleavage sites (figure 2K, L), indicating that GSDME is functionally intact in most gliomas. Notably, similar to GSDME, GSDMB, which can also be cleaved by granzymes, did not show a similar increase in expression in gliomas (figure 2). In summary, only 1% of glioma patients harbor GSDME mutations, and none of these mutations are located at critical cleavage sites. Therefore, we conclude that GSDME is highly expressed in patients with glioma and does not possess loss-of-function mutations.

NK cells infiltrate gliomas but lose their antitumor function in vivo

To assess the role of NK cells in the glioma TME in vivo, we first analyzed immune cell infiltration. Single-cell RNA sequencing data (GSE233304)26 from patients with glioma confirmed the presence of NK cells within the tumor immune landscape (figure 3A), a finding validated by immunofluorescence staining on clinical glioma sections (figure 3B). The proportion of NK cells among total immune cells was relatively low across the three patient samples (figure 3C). Flow cytometry analysis of glioma tissues showed that NK cells and CD8+ T cells were present within tumors but at low levels. T lymphocytes (CD45+CD3+) constituted a small fraction of CD45+ immune cells, with increased infiltration in tumor tissue versus adjacent normal tissue (figure 3A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway analysis of differentially expressed genes in NK cells isolated from patient peripheral blood versus glioma tissue revealed significant enrichment of NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity pathways (figure 3E), suggesting that NK cell function is suppressed on tumor infiltration. Consistent with this, the expression of GzmB and perforin in CD8+ T cells and NK cells was highest in patient blood and significantly reduced in adjacent normal tissues and tumor tissues (figure 3B). Similar results were observed in glioma-bearing mice, with GzmB expression in NK cells being highest in the peripheral blood and low in the tumor and deep cervical lymph nodes (figure 3G). To evaluate whether increasing the influx of activated NK cells into the glioma microenvironment can enhance their antitumor effects by increasing GzmB expression, we used the STING agonist diABZI. diABZI is a potent, non-nucleotide small-molecule agonist of the STING pathway.27 STING activation by diABZI exerts its antitumor effects through multiple pathways that are relevant to NK cell function: (1) Enhanced NK cell recruitment and infiltration. Activation of the cGAS-STING pathway drives the secretion of chemokines such as IL-2 and CXCL9, which enhance NK cell recruitment to tumors and promote their cytotoxic activity.25 (2) Direct enhancement of NK cell effector function. STING activation drives the expansion of diverse immune cell populations, including NK cells.28 29 To investigate whether the STING agonist diABZI promotes NK cell recruitment in vivo, we used the well-established thioglycollate-induced peritonitis model in C57BL/6 mice, which is known to induce robust accumulation of macrophages in the peritoneal cavity.30 Mice were divided into two groups: the control group received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 3% thioglycollate alone, and the treatment group received i.p. injection of 3% thioglycollate together with diABZI. 3 days post-injection, peritoneal lavage fluid was collected and subjected to flow cytometry to determine the proportion of NK cells (CD45+NK1.1+CD3−) among total immune cells. The results demonstrated that diABZI co-administration markedly increased the percentage of NK cells in the peritoneal cavity compared with thioglycollate alone, suggesting that STING activation by diABZI effectively drives NK cell infiltration to the site of inflammation (online supplemental figure S2H). Similarly, we tested whether intracranial injection of diABZI would recruit NK cells. Following intracranial injection of diABZI, it was able to recruit the NK cells into the mouse brain (figure 3H–J). DiABZI also increased the number of NK cells and the expression of GzmB in deep cervical lymph nodes and blood of mice, regardless of whether they were tumor-bearing mice (figure 3K and online supplemental figure S2B–D). Additionally, the numbers of brain-infiltrating CD8+ T cells were determined in both diABZI-treated and tumor-bearing control mice (online supplemental figure S2E). These data collectively indicate that while NK cells can infiltrate gliomas, their cytotoxic function is severely suppressed within the TME. This suggests that, given the high expression of GSDME in gliomas, NK cells could be used to effectively treat gliomas.

LRIG2 inhibits NK cell-mediated pyroptosis in glioma cells by promoting GSDME cleavage

To investigate the mechanisms underlying NK cell dysfunction in the glioma microenvironment and to advance NK cell-based immunotherapy for glioma, we leveraged our group’s longstanding interest in the LRIG protein family and examined whether LRIG family members negatively regulate NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity. We simultaneously overexpressed LRIG1, LRIG2, and LRIG3 in HS683 cells, and subsequently co-cultured these cells with NK92-MI cells to measure the levels of granzyme B and perforin in the supernatant. Our results showed that overexpression of LRIG1 or LRIG3 did not affect the levels of GzmB and perforin in the supernatant (online supplemental figure S2F), whereas overexpression of LRIG2 inhibited the release of GzmB and perforin from NK92-MI cells (figure 1D). Furthermore, our previous studies have demonstrated that LRIG1 and LRIG3 exert diametrically opposite effects compared with LRIG2 in glioma: LRIG1/3 function as tumor suppressors that inhibit glioma cell growth and promote the polarization of TAMs toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype.16 17 19 31 32 These effects are contrary to the direction we intended to investigate—namely, factors that suppress NK cell antitumor activity. Prompted by these observations, we investigated whether LRIG2 affects other immune cell populations in vivo, particularly NK cells. An analysis of LRIG2’s effect on immune cell infiltration in glioma revealed that LRIG2 knockdown did not alter the infiltration of T cells (CD4+ or CD8+) or NK cells (figure 4A, B and online supplemental figure S3A) but significantly increased the expression of the degranulation marker CD107a (a marker of CTL/NK cell degranulation and cytotoxic function)33 on tumor-infiltrating NK and CD8+ T cells (figure 4C and online supplemental figure S3B), suggesting a role for LRIG2 in modulating lymphocyte cytotoxic function.

LRIG2 was significantly overexpressed in gliomas compared with normal adjacent tissue (figure 4D and online supplemental figure S3C), and high LRIG2 expression correlated with shorter survival time (online supplemental figure S3E). Knockdown of LRIG2 in mouse glioma models improved survival (online supplemental figure S3D). Since NK cell killing requires stable immunological synapse formation,34 we investigated the adhesion of NK cells to glioma cells.35 LRIG2 was either overexpressed (with Flag tags) or knocked down in LN229, HS683, and FU glioma cell lines, respectively. When NK92-MI cells were co-cultured with a monolayer of glioma cells, their firm adherence to the substratum was essential, as non-adherent NK cells would otherwise be lost during medium changes. Quantification of adherent NK cells revealed that glioma cells overexpressing LRIG2 bound significantly fewer NK cells, suggesting that LRIG2 expression impairs the capacity of NK cells to establish stable contacts with glioma cells, likely by hindering immunological synapse formation (figure 4E). These findings imply that LRIG2 may inhibit NK cell-mediated killing of glioma cells in vitro. To test this, glioma cells with modulated LRIG2 expression were co-cultured with primary NK cells, and dead tumor cells were labeled using FVD. Results showed that the death of glioma cells overexpressing LRIG2 was significantly reduced, whereas the death of LRIG2-knockdown glioma cells was markedly increased (figure 4F). Collectively, these results indicate that high LRIG2 expression in gliomas suppresses NK cell-mediated killing of glioma cells. We hypothesized that LRIG2 counteracts NK cell cytotoxicity by impairing GSDME-mediated pyroptosis. Indeed, LRIG2 overexpression reduced the proportion of glioma cells exhibiting pyroptotic morphology (figure 4G) and diminished LDH release on co-culture with primary NK cells, whereas LRIG2 knockdown exerted the opposite effects (figure 4H and online supplemental figure S3G). At the molecular level, LRIG2 overexpression attenuated NK92-MI cell-induced GSDME cleavage, whereas LRIG2 knockdown enhanced this cleavage (figure 4I, J and online supplemental figure S3G). In line with these observations, we further examined Caspase3 activation on co-culture with NK92-MI cells: overexpression of Flag-LRIG2 substantially suppressed Caspase3 cleavage, while knockdown of LRIG2 markedly increased it (online supplemental figure S3H), a pattern fully consistent with that of GSDME processing. Moreover, LRIG2 regulated the expression of apoptosis-related proteins, including BAX, BCL2, and PARP (figure 4J and online supplemental figure S3G), indicating that LRIG2 suppresses not only NK cell-mediated pyroptosis but also concomitant apoptosis in glioma cells. These results demonstrate that LRIG2 inhibits NK cell-mediated pyroptosis in glioma cells through GSDME cleavage.

LRIG2/soluble LRIG2 binds to the NK cell membrane

To elucidate the mechanism by which LRIG2 inhibits NK cell function, we first measured cytotoxicity-related cytokine release using a Cytometric Bead Array. Co-culture with LRIG2-overexpressing glioma cells reduced the secretion of GzmB and perforin by NK cells (figure 4K). The release of IFN-γ and TNF-β by NK cells varied among glioma cell lines, likely due to intrinsic differences among the glioma cell lines.

Given that the sLRIG2 can be shed and exert paracrine effects and retains functions similar to those of the full-length protein,16 we investigated a similar mechanism affecting NK cells. Western blotting was performed to assess the presence of sLRIG2 protein in the culture supernatant of glioma cell lines, with Coomassie blue staining serving as a loading control. The results demonstrated that sLRIG2 was indeed present in the supernatant, and its levels were markedly increased in the supernatant of cells overexpressing LRIG2 compared with the control group (figure 1A). When NK92-MI cells were incubated with this supernatant, flow cytometric analysis revealed binding of Flag-tagged LRIG2 to the NK cell surface, as evidenced by both the increased mean fluorescence intensity of Flag-LRIG2 and the elevated percentage of Flag-LRIG2-positive NK92-MI cells (figure 1B and online supplemental figure S5B). Direct co-culture of NK cells with glioma cells also showed LRIG2 association with the NK cell membrane (figure 1C). This sLRIG2-containing supernatant, as well as purified GST-sLRIG2 recombinant protein (online supplemental figure S5C), inhibited GzmB and perforin release from primary NK cells (figure 1D and online supplemental figures S4C,D and S5D). To investigate the receptors or proteins through which sLRIG2 exerts its effects by interacting with NK92-MI cells, glioma cells were co-cultured with NK cells and the cells were then divided into two groups for the extraction of whole cell proteins and membrane proteins, respectively. Flag antibody was used for Co-IP of Flag-LRIG2 with its interacting proteins, and Co-IP-MS was used to identify the co-precipitated proteins (figure 1E). After comparing the protein abundance of the membrane protein group and the total protein group that interacted with Flag-LRIG2, it was found that the membrane proteins with higher interaction rankings included MYO1G, DSC1, ATP8A1, MAN1B1, LYN, and LAMP1 (figure 1F; online supplemental table S1). Notably, LAMP1 ranked among the top hits in the mass spectrometry data, suggesting a strong possibility that LRIG2 binds to LAMP1. On reviewing the literature, we found that the other identified membrane proteins have not been reported to be associated with NK cell functions or to serve as receptors. In contrast, LAMP1 is widely recognized as a canonical marker of NK-cell degranulation, as it is translocated to the cell surface on lysosome-plasma membrane fusion.36 Moreover, LAMP1 has been reported to function as a potential receptor in other contexts, such as in macrophages where it acts as a receptor for the adipokine CTRP3 to regulate inflammatory signaling.37 Given that our study focuses on the mechanism by which LRIG2 modulates NK-cell cytotoxicity, LAMP1 emerged as the most functionally relevant candidate from our membrane protein screening, as it directly reflects the degranulation process critical for NK-cell killing. Other identified membrane proteins, while potentially interesting, lacked this direct mechanistic link to the cytotoxic function being investigated. Therefore, we prioritized LAMP1 for detailed interaction validation. To investigate whether LRIG2 interacts with LAMP1 on the NK cell membrane, we performed Co-IP after co-culturing glioma cells with NK cells. The results showed that LRIG2 bound to LAMP1 (figure 1G). When GST-sLRIG2 recombinant protein was incubated with NK cells followed by Co-IP, LRIG2 was also found to bind to LAMP1 (figure 1H). Consistent findings were obtained from glioma specimens, confirming the interaction between LRIG2/sLRIG2 and LAMP1 in gliomas (figure 1). Immunofluorescence staining further supported this conclusion: when the two cell types were co-cultured, LRIG2 and LAMP1 were observed to interact at the immunological synapses (figure 1J). Moreover, LRIG2 appeared to be shed into the extracellular space by glioma cells and subsequently bound to LAMP1 on the NK cell membrane. This was validated by incubating NK cells with sLRIG2-containing supernatant from glioma cells, which resulted in significant co-localization of LRIG2 and LAMP1 on the NK cell membrane (figure 1K). Additionally, LRIG2 was found to downregulate LAMP1 expression in NK cells, as demonstrated by co-culturing NK cells with LRIG2-overexpressing cells or with purified GST-sLRIG2 protein (figure 1G, H).

Based on these findings, we propose that following cleavage, the extracellular domain of LRIG2 on the membrane of glioma cells is released into the extracellular space, where it binds to LAMP1 on the surface of NK cells and subsequently downregulates LAMP1 expression in these cells. This mechanism may contribute to the observed reduction in NK cell cytotoxic function, including the decreased release of granule-associated molecules such as granzymes and perforin.

The LRIG2–LAMP1 axis is sufficient to activate p-STAT3 in NK cells

To elucidate the mechanism and potential signaling pathways by which LRIG2 interacts with NK cells through LAMP1-mediated binding and impairs their antitumor function, we first reviewed the literature on LAMP1 and found that LAMP1 also functions as a receptor.38 When bound to its ligand, it induces alterations in the STAT signaling pathway.37 STAT plays significant roles in immune regulation. For NK cells, different members of the STAT family influenced the survival, cell identity, clonal expansion, and cytotoxicity of resting, effector, and adaptive NK cells by regulating the expression of various downstream target genes.39 40 Therefore, we hypothesize that LRIG2/sLRIG2 binding to LAMP1 on NK cells may exert its effects by modulating the STAT signaling pathway. Co-culture of glioma cells with NK cells, followed by assessment of the impact of LRIG2 on the expression levels of multiple STAT family members in NK cells revealed that LRIG2 did not alter the expression levels of STAT1/3 or p-STAT1/4/6; however, p-STAT3 expression increased in the presence of overexpressed LRIG2 and decreased on knockdown (figure 5A–C). LRIG2 expression also positively correlated with STAT3 in glioma datasets (online supplemental figure S5E). Treatment of NK cells with either sLRIG2-containing supernatant or purified GST-sLRIG2 protein similarly upregulated p-STAT3 expression levels (figure 5E, F). As STAT3 activation in NK cells is known to transcriptionally repress GzmB and perforin,41 this pathway likely mediates the effect of LRIG2. Immunofluorescence confirmed the presence of p-STAT3+ NK cells in patient gliomas (figure 5G), where p-STAT3 levels were generally elevated (figure 5D). To determine whether the upregulation of p-STAT3 in NK cells induced by LRIG2 is mediated through LAMP1, we knocked down LAMP1 expression in NK cells using siRNA and then treated the cells with the GST-sLRIG2 recombinant protein. We found that LAMP1 knockdown reversed the LRIG2-induced increase in p-STAT3 levels (figure 5H). Furthermore, to explore how the LRIG2–LAMP1 interaction activates STAT3, we first surveyed the JAK family kinases known to phosphorylate STAT3—namely JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and TYK242—and then performed a bioinformatics analysis to assess the correlation between each of these kinases and LRIG2, LAMP1, or STAT3. Among these kinases, JAK1 showed the strongest correlation with LRIG2, LAMP1, and STAT3 (online supplemental figure S5F). Based on this finding, we performed western blot analyses under two conditions: co-culture of LRIG2-overexpressing HS683 cells with NK92-MI cells, and (2) incubation of NK92-MI cells with conditioned media from LRIG2-overexpressing HS683 cells. We then examined the expression changes of JAK1/2/3 and TYK2 in NK92-MI cells. Our results demonstrated that either LRIG2-overexpressing cells or their conditioned media led to upregulation of JAK1, but not JAK2, JAK3, or TYK2, in NK92-MI cells (online supplemental figure S5G). These findings suggest that LRIG2 binding to LAMP1 on the NK cell membrane may activate STAT3 phosphorylation via JAK1.

Glioma cells can express IL-6,43 while the IL-6/STAT3 pathway plays a crucial role in driving tumor cell growth and shaping the immunosuppressive microenvironment.44 For instance, in lung cancer and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, tumor cells secrete IL-6, which activates the STAT3 pathway in NK cells, thereby downregulating the expression of activating receptors on NK cells and resulting in impaired NK cell function.45 46 To investigate whether the inhibitory effect of LRIG2 on NK cell cytotoxicity is mediated by IL-6 secreted from glioma cells, IL-6 was knocked out in glioma cells (figure 5I). Subsequently, when glioma cells were co-cultured with NK cells, knockout of IL-6 in glioma cells did not affect the inhibitory effect of LRIG2 overexpression on the release of GzmB and perforin from NK cells (figure 5J), nor did it influence the effect of LRIG2 on GSDME cleavage (figure 5K). Meanwhile, when NK cells were incubated with the supernatant from IL-6 knockout glioma cells, the supernatant also did not affect the regulatory effect of LRIG2 overexpression on p-STAT3 levels in NK cells (figure 5L). Therefore, the LRIG2–LAMP1 axis is sufficient to activate p-STAT3 in NK cells in a tumor-cell-autonomous and IL-6-independent manner, which in turn directly inhibits the release of granzyme B and perforin from NK cells.

Blocking the LRIG2-LAMP1-STAT3 axis in NK cells represents a potential therapeutic target for glioma

As previously noted, on entering the glioma microenvironment, NK cells undergo significant functional suppression, leading to reduced release of GzmB and perforin. To further validate the direct suppressive effect of LRIG2 on NK cells in vivo, we used Rag1 knockout mice, which lack mature T and B cells but retain a normal NK cell compartment, thereby minimizing potential interference from other immune cells47 (figure 6B). Human glioma HS683 cells with or without LRIG2 knockdown were implanted intracranially into Rag1 KO mice (figure 6A). compared with WT mice, Rag1 KO mice exhibited a higher proportion of tumor-infiltrating NK cells, although LRIG2 expression did not affect the overall number of infiltrating NK cells (figure 6C). Notably, LRIG2 knockdown significantly enhanced GzmB expression in tumor-infiltrating NK cells (figure 6D), suggesting that LRIG2 suppresses NK cell GzmB release in vivo. Therefore, modulating the expression of LRIG2 in gliomas or enhancing the cytotoxic function of NK cells represents a potential therapeutic approach. We therefore believe that diABZI could synergize with LRIG2 inhibition to enhance antitumor efficacy. As mentioned above, diABZI was selected as a tool to achieve this because: it is a well-characterized, potent STING agonist with demonstrated in vivo efficacy; STING activation is known to promote NK cell recruitment and activation; and the STING pathway represents a key innate immune surveillance mechanism that can be therapeutically exploited to “heat up” immunologically cold tumors. When LRIG2 expression was knocked down in gliomas and combined with intracranial diABZI injection, the expression of GzmB in NK cells within gliomas and in the deep cervical lymph nodes of mice was the highest (figure 6E), surpassing that of mice treated with diABZI alone. This indicated that the combination enhanced the ability of NK cells to kill glioma cells, and the mice of the combination treatment group had the longest survival (figure 6F). Thus, the combination of knock-down LRIG2 and diABZI administration to improve NK cell cytotoxicity can effectively treat gliomas. Additionally, another strategy involved modulating the expression of STAT3 within NK cells to enhance their cytotoxic capacity. After knocking out the expression of STAT3 in NK cells (figure 6G), the secretion of GzmB and perforin was significantly increased (figure 6H). Furthermore, the inhibitory effect of LRIG2 on NK cell cytotoxicity was eliminated. The effect of overexpressing LRIG2 on the release of GzmB and perforin by NK cells, as well as the cleavage of GSDME, disappeared after knocking out the expression of STAT3 in NK cells (figure 6I and J). Collectively, these findings underscore the critical regulatory role of the LRIG2–STAT3 axis in NK cell cytotoxicity and identify the LRIG2–LAMP1–STAT3 cascade as a promising therapeutic target for augmenting NK cell function in glioma, with potential implications for strategies targeting LRIG2, STAT3 or GSDME.

Discussion

Pyroptosis, a gasdermin-mediated form of lytic programmed cell death, holds significant promise for cancer immunotherapy due to its ability to release pro-inflammatory signals and potentially convert immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” microenvironments.48 Among the gasdermin family members, GSDME exhibits variable expression across different malignancies.49 Here, we revealed that GSDME is highly expressed in gliomas, with minimal mutational frequency. However, elevated GSDME levels did not correlate with improved patient survival. We propose that this functional disconnect results from sparse NK cell infiltration within the glioma microenvironment, coupled with their reduced capacity to deliver sufficient GzmB and perforin in this immunosuppressive niche, thereby hindering GSDME cleavage and subsequent pyroptosis. Importantly, our data confirmed that glioma cells remain intrinsically susceptible to pyroptosis when exposed to functionally competent NK cells, reinforcing GSDME as a viable therapeutic target. Nevertheless, a major challenge persists: NK cells, including those engineered chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-NK cells,50 rapidly lose their cytotoxic function within the hostile glioma TME.51 Tumors are adept at evading NK cell surveillance through multiple mechanisms, such as downregulating NKG2D ligands,52 secreting immunosuppressive factors like transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β),53 and inducing STAT3 acetylation.54 This pervasive immunosuppression underscores the urgent need for strategies to restore NK cell cytotoxicity, enhance cytokine production, and improve persistence in the glioma TME.55 Addressing these limitations requires elucidating the precise mechanisms underlying NK cell suppression and developing effective counterstrategies—an effort that represents a critical direction for future research aimed at harnessing pyroptosis for therapeutic benefit.

Our findings identified LRIG2 as a key molecular mediator that suppresses NK cell-mediated pyroptosis in gliomas. Highly expressed in gliomas, LRIG2 impeded this process by suppressing GSDME cleavage, resulting in attenuated pyroptotic cell death and prolonged survival in murine glioma models. Furthermore, we showed that LRIG2 potently suppresses the secretion of GzmB and perforin by NK cells, molecules essential for GSDME cleavage. We also demonstrated that the extracellular domain of LRIG2 can be cleaved by ADAM1716 and released into the extracellular space as soluble LRIG2. Functionally, sLRIG2 mirrors the activity of full-length LRIG2 by binding to LAMP1 on the NK cell membrane, which in turn increases STAT3 phosphorylation and transcriptionally represses the release of cytotoxic effector molecules.39 While our findings establish that LRIG2 binding to LAMP1 activates STAT3 phosphorylation via JAK1, we recognize that the precise molecular events bridging LAMP1 engagement to JAK1 activation warrant further investigation. Moreover, the downstream target genes mediating the JAK1–STAT3 axis-dependent suppression of NK cell function remain to be identified in future studies. However, it is well established in the literature that STAT3 activation in NK cells can downregulate the expression of activating receptors, thereby impairing NK cell function. For instance, tumor-derived IL-6 and IL-8 have been reported to activate the STAT3 pathway in NK cells, leading to decreased surface expression of activating receptors such as NKp30 and NKG2D.46 Additionally, STAT3 has been shown to negatively regulate DNAM-1 expression on NK cells, and STAT3 activation by tumor-derived cytokines impairs human NK-cell function in a STAT3-dependent manner.56 These reports support the notion that STAT3 activation—regardless of the upstream trigger—can compromise NK cell cytotoxicity through modulation of activating receptor expression. Additionally, although we demonstrated that the LRIG2–LAMP1 axis is sufficient to activate p-STAT3 in NK cells in a tumor-cell-autonomous and IL-6-independent manner using IL-6-deficient glioma cells, we recognize that the in vivo TME is considerably more complex. TAMs and other stromal or immune cells are major sources of IL-6, which may also contribute to STAT3 phosphorylation in infiltrating NK cells. Therefore, our findings do not exclude the possibility that microenvironmental IL-6 converges on STAT3 activation in NK cells. Instead, we propose that the LRIG2–LAMP1 axis and the IL-6/IL-6R pathway may cooperatively or sequentially regulate STAT3 activity in NK cells within the glioma microenvironment. We acknowledge that several limitations exist in the current study concerning the role of LRIG2 in NK cell regulation. One such limitation is the use of the RAG1 knockout mouse model, which completely lacks T cells and thus fails to fully recapitulate the complex immune microenvironment of human patients with glioma. Nevertheless, this model was deliberately chosen to isolate NK cell function as a single variable, facilitating a focused mechanistic investigation of the LRIG2–LAMP1 axis in NK cell-mediated antitumor immunity. We fully recognize, however, that findings obtained from this model may not directly translate to immunocompetent settings. Accordingly, further validation in more physiologically relevant systems, such as orthotopic syngeneic glioma models or humanized immune system mice, is warranted to strengthen the translational implications of our observations.

Standard therapies for gliomas yield only modest survival benefits.57 Consequently, emerging immunotherapies, including immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) and adoptive cell therapies aim to modulate immune responses within the TME. However, these strategies face substantial hurdles in gliomas.58 The profoundly immunosuppressive nature of the glioma TME, which starkly contrasts with the more favorable (though still challenging) TME of intracranial metastases that often harbor greater T and NK cell infiltration and show enhanced ICB responses,59 60 is likely a major underlying cause for these limitations. These disparities underscore the critical need to develop strategies that can effectively augment cytotoxic lymphocyte infiltration and sustain their function within gliomas. Current CAR-NK strategies encounter hurdles including downregulation of activating ligands and upregulation of immune checkpoints.61 Therefore, combinatorial approaches, including synergy with ICB,62 are being explored to enhance precision and sustain effector cell activity.

Building on our discovery of the LRIG2-LAMP1-STAT3 axis in suppressing NK cell-induced pyroptosis via GSDME blockade, we propose a dual-pronged therapeutic strategy. This approach involves downregulating LRIG2 to alleviate its suppression of NK cell cytotoxicity—specifically GzmB and perforin release—combined with intracranial administration of STING agonists (eg, diABZI) to bolster NK cell recruitment and infiltration into the glioma TME. This combination is designed to synergize directly with the latent pyroptotic potential conferred by GSDME expression in glioma cells, thereby creating a more favorable environment for NK cell-mediated tumor killing. Furthermore, our experimental data indicate that modulating STAT3 expression in NK cells enhances their antitumor capacity, suggesting that future strategies could integrate STAT3 modulation into LRIG2-targeted CAR-NK therapies for glioma. Collectively, these findings provide a mechanistic framework for rationally designed combination immunotherapies aimed at overcoming the profound immunosuppression that limits current treatment efficacy in gliomas.
