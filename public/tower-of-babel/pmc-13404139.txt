Visceral adipose tissue differentially affects tau and Aβ pathology in 3xTg-AD mice
Authors: Laura Trujillo-Estrada, Miriam Bettinetti-Luque, Juana Andreo-Lopez, Celia da Cunha, Mario Morales-Cabello, Cynthia Campos-Moreno, Elisabeth Sanchez-Mejias, Stefania Forner, Alessandra C. Martini, Raquel Sanchez-Varo, Antonia Gutierrez, Frank M. LaFerla, David Baglietto-Vargas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is a complex disorder involving multiple cellular and molecular mechanisms. Recent evidence suggests that metabolic alterations play a crucial role in AD progression. Likewise, diabetes and obesity—two major metabolic diseases—are well-established risk factors for AD. These conditions are associated with a significant expansion of white adipose tissue (WAT). Here, we hypothesize that visceral WAT may act as a key mediator between peripheral metabolic dysfunction and brain illnesses.MethodsImmunohistochemistry and biochemical approaches were used to evaluate the WAT from WT and db/db mice. Similar techniques were applied to examine the brain tissue of 3xTg-AD mice that received white epididymal fat pads from WT or db/db donors and determine the impact of adipose tissue transplantation on tau and Aβ pathology.ResultsOur study revealed that recipient 3xTg-AD mice that received db/db fat pads developed profound changes in tau pathology due to increased expression of the cyclin-dependent kinase 5 activator p25 compared with 3xTg-AD mice that received fad pads from WT or sham mice. This increment in p25/cdk5 was associated with a prominent inflammatory response induced by the WAT transplant. Moreover, the opposite effect on Aβ pathology was found. The reduction in Aβ levels was correlated with an increase in microglial phagocytic capacity.ConclusionsOverall, our study demonstrated a novel crosstalk between AD and metabolic disorders through white adipose signaling resulting in differential effects on tau and Aβ pathology mediated by an activated immune response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02964-3.

Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common form of dementia and has a significant impact on the elderly population [1]. The incidence of AD has risen significantly posing a major challenge to the healthcare system, with an annual estimated cost of a trillion US dollars and affecting more than 50 million people worldwide [2]. Thus, AD is clearly one of the most urgent medical and socioeconomic problems faced by our society, and more in-depth research into the development of novel and effective disease-modifying therapies is crucial. The situation is further compounded by the fact that elderly AD patients, on average, suffer from two to eight comorbidities, which play an important role in the onset and progression of the disease [3]. Extensive clinical evidence highlights obesity and diabetes as two of the most important comorbidities affecting global health. Likewise, novel epidemiological and animal model studies indicate that diabetes and midlife obesity are significant risk factors for AD [4–12]. Indeed, these metabolic disorders are associated with lower cognitive performance, reduced brain white matter, decreased blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity, brain atrophy, and increased risk for late-onset AD [13–16]. Both conditions pose significant public health challenges, with the World Health Organization (WHO) reporting a global prevalence of 1.9 billion overweight individuals and more than 176 million people suffering from diabetes [17]. Unfortunately, these numbers are projected to rise exponentially in the upcoming decades due to lifestyle factors, potentially exacerbating the progression of various brain disorders, including neurodegenerative and neuropsychiatric conditions. Interestingly, both diabetes and obesity are associated with a substantial increase in adipose tissue (AT), specifically white adipose tissue (WAT) [18, 19]. WAT is recognized as a relevant organ involved in the maintenance of metabolic processes, reproduction, protection, and insulation [20–23]. Notably, emerging findings highlight an important crosstalk between the WAT and the brain, regulating a wide range of cerebral functions such as synaptic plasticity, memory consolidation, neurogenesis, neuroinflammatory processes and BBB homeostasis [18, 24, 25]. Likewise, compelling evidence suggests that not all types of AT have the same impact on metabolism and peripheral inflammation, as visceral white adipose tissue (VAT) accumulation may increase immune infiltration and reduce the expression of certain adipokines such as leptin or adiponectin, whereas subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) appears to be less harmful [26, 27]. Conversely, brown adipose tissue (BAT) is associated with improved glucose metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and lipid metabolism mediated by the secretion of batokines [28, 29]. Therefore, these different adipose deposits may have different role in this crosstalk and despite recent advances in understanding the crucial role of WAT in central nervous system (CNS) function [18, 24, 25], the differential contribution of these AT subtypes to the development of age-related diseases, such as AD, remains largely unclear.

Obesity and diabetes are linked to a state of chronic low-grade inflammation, a key driver of peripheral metabolic diseases [30–34]. This process is mediated mainly by macrophages, which infiltrate the expanding AT, reaching up to 40–50% of the cells in this tissue. In fact, adipose tissue macrophages (ATMs) play a pivotal role in the inflammatory environment associated with obesity, diabetes and metabolic dysfunction. Under normal conditions, ATMs primarily exhibit a homeostatic profile, contributing to AT remodeling, lipid metabolism, and immune surveillance [35, 36]. However, metabolic distress triggers a shift towards a pro-inflammatory phenotype, characterized by an increased secretion of different inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin 6 (IL-6), and interleukin 1beta (IL-1β) [37–39]. This inflammatory response is not restricted to AT but can have a far-reaching effect on distant organs, including the brain [23, 31, 40, 41]. Interestingly, increasing evidence suggests that ATM-derived inflammatory cytokines can compromise BBB integrity, promote microglial activation and contribute to sustained neuroinflammation by recruiting additional peripheral immune cells to the CNS [23, 42]. Despite these associations, the concrete mechanism linking metabolic inflammation and AD progression remains poorly understood.

Microglia, the brain’s primary resident immune cells, are involved in the clearance of toxic proteins and cellular debris, synaptic remodeling, promotion of myelinogenesis, neuronal homeostasis, BBB integrity, and modulation of neuroinflammatory responses [43–46]. However, in the context of metabolic-induced chronic inflammation, sustained activation of microglia by cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β can lead to exacerbated neuroinflammation and the development of several proteinopathies, synaptic dysfunction and neuronal loss [47–50]. Thus, persistent exposure to a pro-inflammatory environment can induce a primed state, making these cells more reactive to subsequent stimuli, modulating AD progression. The interplay between peripheral inflammation and microglial dysfunction highlights a crucial but poorly understood link between systemic metabolic disorders and neurodegeneration, emphasizing the need for a more in-depth exploration of these communications that may contribute to the development of relevant therapeutic approaches for AD and metabolic disorders.

Here, we investigated the impact of AT transplantation from db/db mice (a well-established model of diabetes and obesity) into abdominal cavity of the 3xTg-AD mouse model on the progression of AD-like pathology. Specifically, we observed that epididymal fat pads from db/db mice showed an increase in cell size, change in adipokine secretion, and a pro-inflammatory profile. After the epididymal fat pads were transplanted into the 3xTg-AD mice, we found an activated inflammatory response that drove an increment of tau hyperphosphorylation by modulating the cyclin-dependent kinase 5 and its activator p25 (CDK5/p25). Moreover, an important phagocytic response was also identified around the amyloid plaques, which reduced the number of these aggregates. Our findings provide new insights into the relationship between metabolic dysfunction and AD pathology, with potential implications for understanding the impact of obesity and diabetes on neurodegenerative diseases.

Materials and methods

Transgenic mice and surgical procedure

Female homozygous 12-month-old 3xTg-AD mice were used in the current study. The generation and characterization of the 3xTg-AD mice have been described previously [51]. This model displayed early metabolic changes at the age of 6-7-month-old, including impairments in glucose tolerance and fasting plasma insulin [52, 53].

Female homozygous 3-month-old leptin receptor mutant mice (db/db) [54] and wild-type (WT) mice were used to collect the epididymal fat pads that were transplanted into the 3xTg-AD mice. After euthanasia, bilateral incisions were made through the dermis and muscle superficial to the epididymal fat pads of WT and db/db mice. The excised fat was trimmed into 500 mg slices and maintained in sterile saline for < 5 min before insertion into the peritoneal cavity of the recipient mouse. For the transplantation experiments, 3xTg-AD mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and bilateral incisions were made as described for WT and db/db mice. The transplant was secured to the peritoneal muscle with a dissolvable suture before closure and postoperative recovery [55, 56]. Three groups of 3xTg-AD mice were established: 3x SHAM, which received bilateral incisions without fat tissue transplantation; 3x WT, which received fat tissue from WT mice; and 3x db/db, which received fat tissue from db/db mice (Supplemental Fig. 1). The fat tissue was maintained for two weeks in the abdominal area of recipient mice as described in previous studies [56, 57].

All animal experiments were carried out in accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and with the Spanish and the European Union regulations (RD53/2013 and 2010/63/EU) approved by the local Animal Research Committees from the University of California (USA), and University of Malaga (Spain). Experiments and procedures with animals were designed to minimize animal suffering and reduce the number of animals used.

Tissue preparation

After euthanasia, the animals were perfused transcardially with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). Next, hemibrains were used to collect soluble and insoluble fractions of the hippocampus for electrochemiluminescence-linked immunoassay (ELISA) and western blot (WB) analysis or fixed in paraformaldehyde (PFA) for immunohistological staining. For soluble fraction extraction, the hippocampus was homogenized (using a Dounce homogenizer) in T-PER (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) containing complete protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Sigma-Aldrich, San Luis, MO, USA). After homogenization, the soluble fraction was isolated by one centrifugation (44000 rpm, 1 h at 4 °C). Then, the pellet was resuspended in 70% formic acid, centrifugated (44000 rpm, 1 h at 4 °C) and considered as the insoluble fraction. The protein content of soluble and insoluble fractions was determined using the Micro BCA assay (ThermoFisher Scientific).

Epididymal fat pads from WT and db/db mice were fixed in PFA for paraffin sectioning and histological analysis. The fat was also collected to measure cytokine levels by ELISA.

For insoluble protein extraction and analysis, acetone/TCA precipitation protocol was used as previously reported [58]. Briefly, insoluble proteins were homogenized with 70% formic acid (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by centrifugation at 100,000 ×g for 1 h. The supernatant was collected and the formic acid of the supernatant was neutralized using the following ratio (1 volume of sample, 1 volume of neutralization buffer and 1.8 volumes 10 N of NaOH). Then, each volume of sample was mixed with 10 volumes of cold 10% TCA (Trichloroacetic acid; Thermo Fisher Scientific) in acetone (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at − 20 °C overnight. Next, samples were centrifuged at 15,000 ×g for 10 min and the supernatant was removed. Then, cold acetone was added and incubated at − 20 °C for 10 min. After that, samples were centrifuged at 15,000 ×g for 15 min. Supernatant was removed and the pellet was air-dried. The pellet was resuspended in T-per (Thermo Fisher Scientific) extraction buffer (150 mg/ml), complemented with a protease inhibitor (Complete Mini Protease Inhibitor Tablets, Roche Diagnostics GmbH) and phosphatase inhibitor (5 mmol/l, Sigma-Aldrich).

Immunoblotting

Equal amounts of protein (5 µg) were separated on 10% Bis-Tris gels (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Then, membranes were blocked for 1 h in a 5% (w/v) suspension of bovine serum albumin (BSA; Gemini Bio-Products, West Sacramento, CA, USA) in 0.2% Tween 20 Tris-buffered saline (TBS) (pH 7.4. After blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with one of the following primary antibodies: MHCI (1:1000; Novus Biologicals, Madrid, Spain, NB120-6405), MHCII (1:1000; Novus Biologicals NB100-65541), ADAM17 (1:1000; Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom, ab2051), IDE (1:1000; Abcam ab32216), anti-NEP (1:500; Abcam ab79423), APP C-term (1:1000, Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA, 171610), ADAM10 (1:1000; Abcam 124695), BACE (1:1000; Millipore MAB5308), HT7 (1:1000; ThermoFisher Scientific MN1000), AT8 (1:1000; ThermoFisher Scientific MN1020), AT180 (1:1000; ThermoFisher Scientific MN1040), GSK3β (1:1000; Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA, 9315), p-GSK3β (Ser9; 1:1000; Cell Signaling 9336), JNK (1:1000; Cell Signaling 9252), p-JNK (1:1000; Cell Signaling 9251), p38MAPK (1:1000; Cell Signaling 8690), p-p38MAPK (1:1000; Cell Signaling 4511), CDK5 (1:1000; Millipore), p35/25 (1:1000; Cell Signaling 2680), rabbit polyclonal anti-human-tau (1:3000; Dako A0024) and GAPDH (1:2000; Santa Cruz Biotech, Dallas, TX, USA, sc25778). The membranes were washed in tween-TBS for 20 min and incubated at 20 °C with the specific secondary antibody at a dilution of 1:10000 (Pierce Biotechnology) for 1 h. The blots were developed using Super Signal (ThermoFisher Scientific).

Histological staining

For thioflavin-S (Thio-S) staining, the sections were incubated in 0.02% thioflavin-S (Sigma-Aldrich T1892) in 50% ethanol for 10 min, followed by 2 washes in 50% ethanol and 2 washes with TBS. Then, the sections were mounted and coverslipped with Fluoromount-G (Southern Biotech, Birmingham, AL, USA).

For hematoxylin-eosin staining, paraffin-embedded WAT was hydrated in graded ethanol and stained with hematoxylin for 1 min. After that, the tissue was incubated in 96% ethanol plus acetic acid, washed and stained with eosin. Then, sections were dehydrated, cleared with xylene and coverslipped with DPX (VWR) mounting medium.

Immunohistochemistry

Coronal free-floating Sect. (40 μm thick) were pretreated with 3% H2O2/3% methanol in TBS to block endogenous peroxidase activity. After washing, sections were incubated with 6E10 monoclonal antibody (1:500; BioLegend SIG-39320), AT8 monoclonal antibody (1:500; Thermo Scientific MN1020), anti-CD45 (1:500; Bio-Rad clon IL-3/16 MCA1388), anti-CD68 (1:500; Abcam ab152512), CD163 (1:200, Abcam ab182422), anti-CD3 (1:100; Abcam ab135372), GFAP (1:2000; Abcam ab7260) or Iba1 (1:500; Abcam ab5076) antibodies overnight at room temperature. Sections were then incubated with the corresponding biotinylated secondary: anti-mouse, anti-rabbit, or anti-rat, 1:500 dilution for 1 h at 20 °C, followed by Vector ABC Kit and DAB reagents (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) to visualize staining.

For fluorescence staining, sections were incubated in secondary donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1000, Invitrogen A-21206) for CD68 and GFAP antibodies, donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 555 (1:1000, Invitrogen A-31570) for 6E10 antibody and donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 647 for Iba1 antibody (1:1000, Invitrogen A-31571) during 1 h. Sections were then mounted and coverslipped with Fluoromount-G (Southern Biothech).

Quantitative analyses

The biochemical data were quantitatively analyzed using Image J 1.52q software. For Thio-S plaque loading quantification, fluorescent sections were imaged with a Leica DM 2500 laser scanning confocal microscope with identical laser and detection settings. Images (5 sections per animal, n = 6) were analyzed using Bitplane Imaris software.

Microglia and astroglia were modeled using Bitplane Imaris software and changes in these cells, such as cell body area, process length, and number of microglial branches, were analyzed (5 sections per animal, n = 6). Colocalization study for 6E10, Iba1 and CD68 was performed using Bitplane Imaris software (3 plaques per section were analyzed, with 2 sections per animal, n = 4).

CD45 and AT8 markers loadings were quantified in the hippocampus of the mice (3 sections per animal, n = 4–5 per experimental group) by using ImageJ software.

To analyze the change in adipose cell size, the sections were imaged using an Axioskop microscope and an Axiocam camera (Zeiss Germany) microscope. Grayscale images (2 sections per animal, n = 5) were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Electrochemiluminescence-linked immunoassay

V-Plex pro-inflammatory panel 1 (mouse) kit for IFNγ, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, I-L6, KC/GRO, IL-10, IL-12p70 and TNF-α (Meso Scale Discovery, MSD) were used in the hippocampi of 3x SHAM, 3x WT and 3x db/db mice as well as in the fat tissue of WT and db/db mice. Soluble fractions from the hippocampus or fat were diluted with diluent 41 (2-fold dilution), and 50 µl per well of prepared sample, calibrator and control were added to a plate and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. Then, samples were washed and further incubated with the detection antibody solution for 2 h at room temperature. After washings, 150 µl of Read buffer was added to each well, and the plate was read on the MSD instrument. The data obtained were normalized using the protein content of each sample.

To evaluate Aβ accumulation in the soluble and insoluble fractions, one V-Plex plus Aβ peptide panel 1 (6E10) kit for Aβ38, Aβ40 and Aβ42 was used, and the data were normalized with using the protein concentration of each sample in 3x SHAM, 3x WT and 3x db/db mice.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Milliplex mouse Adipocyte Magnetic Bead Panel kit for Adiponectin, leptin and resistin (Millipore; Catalog # MADCYMAG-72 K) were used in the hippocampi of 3x SHAM, 3x WT and 3x db/db mice as well as in the fat tissue of WT and db/db mice. Soluble fractions from the hippocampus or fat were diluted with T-PER buffer (5-fold dilution for leptin and resistin and 5000-fold dilution for Adiponectin), and 10 µl per well of prepared sample, calibrator and control were added to a plate, as well as 25 µl of beads. After incubation at room temperature for 2 h, samples were washed and further incubated with the detection antibody solution for 30 min at room temperature. After that, 50 µl of Streptavidin-Phycoerythrin was added per well and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After washings, 100 µl of Sheath Fluid was added per well, and the plate was read on the Luminex instrument. Adiponectin, leptin, and resistin concentrations in every sample were calculated with reference to the standard curve and expressed as picograms per milliliter.

FGF21 levels were measured by using the ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (ThermoScientific; Catalog #EEL084). Briefly, standards, hippocampi of 3x SHAM, 3x WT and 3x db/db mice as well as fat tissue of WT and db/db mice were prepared and loaded onto the 96-well plate and incubated for 90 min (5-fold dilution was prepared for each sample for a total volume of 100 µl per well). Then, they were washed and proceeded to the binding of the biotin, followed by the streptavidin conjugated to the horseradish peroxidase and then the stabilized chromogen. Washes were included in every indicated step. Finally, the stop solution was added, and the absorbance was read at 450 nm in a spectrophotometer. FGF21 concentration in every sample was calculated with reference to the standard curve and expressed as picograms per milliliter.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism (10.4.1 version) software. All values are expressed as means ± SEM, with p ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant. Data sets were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. When normal distribution was confirmed, data sets were assessed using an Unpaired t test (with Welch’s correction when both groups did not have the same standard deviation or SD) for comparing two groups, and 1- or 2- way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test for analyzing more than two groups. When the data sets did not follow a normal distribution, Mann-Whitney test was performed to analyze 2 groups, and Kruskal-Wallis test was performed to analyze more than 2 groups, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test. Asterisks were assigned as follows: * p ≤ 0.05, ** p ≤ 0.01, and *** p ≤ 0.001, and the number of biological replicates is expressed as “n”.

Results

White visceral fat tissue from db/db mice shows hypertrophied adipocytes, alterations in adipokine secretion, and a proinflammatory profile

Under different metabolic disturbances, such as diabetes and obesity, AT experiences important morphological and functional changes, including proliferation and hypertrophy of adipocytes, infiltration of immune cells and production of proinflammatory molecules [23]. Here, we verified that the individual fat cells of epididymal WAT from db/db mice experienced an increase in the area compared with those from WT mice (+ 59.57 ± 17.71%; Unpaired t test with Welch’s correction test, p = 0.0006), suggesting a hypertrophy of adipocytes (Fig. 1A and B). In addition, more connective tissue was observed in AT from db/db mice than WT mice, which may be associated with the expansion of adipocytes (Fig. 1A1-A2). Likewise, adipokine secretion is also altered in the epididymal WAT from db/db mice compared to WT mice, showing reduced levels of Adiponectin (-38.27 ± 11.71%; Unpaired t test with equal SD, p = 0.06), Resistin (-72.42 ± 5.72%; Unpaired t test with equal SD, p < 0.0001) and FGF21 (-76.73 ± 3.97%; Unpaired t test with equal SD, p = 0.02) in db/db mice compared to WT mice (Fig. 1C), suggesting that morphological and functional alterations occur in the epididymal WAT from db/db mice compared to WT mice.

Under obesity and diabetes conditions, the number of ATMs in the WAT could represent up to 40–50% of the total cell population and markedly triggers the secretion of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines [59, 60]. Indeed, greater expression of MHC class II was detected in the epididymal WAT of db/db mice compared to the one in WT mice (+ 106.46 ± 108.58%; Unpaired t test with equal SD, p = 0.0256), suggesting an increase in the number of ATMs with a pro-inflammatory profile (Fig. 1D, E and Supplemental Fig. 5). Moreover, we performed an ELISA on protein extracts from the epididymal fat pad and we observed a significant increase in multiple cytokines, such as IL-10 (+ 275.19 ± 73.40%; Unpaired t test with Welch’s correction, p = 0.0312), IL12p70 (+ 65.84 ± 8.86%; Mann Whitney test, p = 0.0667), IL-1β (+ 174.00 ± 52.79%; Mann Whitney test, p = 0.0190), IL-4 (100.03 ± 27.80%; Unpaired t test with equal SD, p = 0.0257), IL-6 (+ 297.74 ± 117.40%; Unpaired t test with Welch’s correction, p = 0.0838), KC/GRO (+ 704.93 ± 131.35%; Mann Whitney test, p = 0.0095) and TNF-α (+ 229.78 ± 50.75%; Mann Whitney test, p = 0.0095) (Fig. 1F) in db/db mouse fat pads compared with age-matched WT, indicating a significant pro-inflammatory environment in the WAT of these mice.

White visceral adipose tissue transplantation from db/db mice exacerbates the microglial response but not adipokine levels in 3xTg-AD mice

Since the db/db epididymal fat pad shows an important pro-inflammatory profile, we hypothesized that the fat transplanted from these mice may affect the brain and worsen the neuroinflammatory response in the hippocampus of the 3xTg-AD mice. Therefore, we evaluated the morphology and number of microglial cells in the hippocampal area of 3x SHAM, 3x WT and 3x db/db groups (Fig. 2A and B). The morphometric quantification of confocal images revealed an increase in the processes area (+ 56.10 ± 16.88% and + 41.81 ± 15.33%; one-way ANOVA, Tukey test, p(3x SHAM − 3x db/db) = 0.0176, p(3x WT − 3x db/db) = 0.0463) (Fig. 2B2), microglia branching (+ 105.35 ± 33.73% and 78.71 ± 23.92%; one-way ANOVA, Tukey test, P(3x SHAM − 3x db/db) = 0.0263, p(3x WT − 3x db/db) = 0.0619) (Fig. 2B3) and total processes length (+ 55.85 ± 18.56% and + 49.66 ± 17.50%; one-way ANOVA, Tukey test, p(3x SHAM − 3x db/db) = 0.0208, p(3x WT − 3x db/db) = 0.0311) (Fig. 2B4) in 3x db/db compared to 3x SHAM and 3x WT, respectively. However, these morphological modifications were not associated with changes in the number of microglial cells (Fig. 2B1). These results suggest that transplantation of fat pads from db/db mice into 3xTg-AD mice induced a more branched morphology in homeostatic microglial cells instead of proliferation. We also analyzed microglial activation by immunohistochemistry using the cluster of differentiation 45 (CD45) antibody (Fig. 2C and D), which is a highly conserved receptor protein tyrosine phosphatase that modulates microglial activation [61]. The quantification of CD45-positive cells in the subiculum area, which contain significant amount of amyloid plaques in 3xTg-AD at this age, revealed greater immunoreactivity loading of this marker in 3x db/db mice compared to 3x WT (Fig. 2C and D), indicating that more activated microglial cells are found in 3xTg-AD mice that received fat pads from db/db mice (+ 62.20 ± 9.27%; Unpaired t test with equal SD, p = 0.0259). Next, we analyzed the cytokine expression profile in the hippocampus of each group by ELISA (Fig. 2E). Our data showed that the 3x db/db mice displayed a significant increase in IL-1β level compared to 3x SHAM and 3x WT mice, respectively (+ 26.88 ± 18.28% and + 81.52 ± 32.96%; one-way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction, p(3x SHAM − 3x db/db) = 0.0235, p(3x WT − 3x db/db) = 0.0158), suggesting that this pro-inflammatory cytokine plays a main role mediating the activation of microglial cells in 3x db/db mice.

A recent study has shown that peripheral macrophages can migrate into the brain parenchyma in human AD samples [62]. Thus, we used the marker CD163 to determine whether these modifications in myeloid cells were specifically associated with microglia. CD163-positive cells were found only within the perivascular niche of the three experimental groups and no infiltration into the brain tissue was observed (Supplemental Fig. 2A). We also used the CD3 marker to detect lymphocyte cells in the brain parenchyma and an equal loading of CD3-positive cells was found between the different groups (Supplemental Fig. 2B). Therefore, these changes did not involve the recruitment of peripheral macrophages or resulted in an increase in lymphocytes. Finally, we analyzed the number and morphology of the astrocytes, and no differences were detected (one-way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis test) (Supplemental Fig. 3).

Furthermore, the expression levels of Adiponectin, leptin, resistin and FGF21 were measured in the brain of 3xTg-AD mice to determine if the alterations of adipokine levels in the fat tissue may be reproduced in the brain, however, no differences were detected (one-way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis test) (Supplemental Fig. 4). Overall, these data suggest that fat pad transplantation initiates and activates a neuroimmune response that mainly influences microglia without infiltration from perivascular macrophages in the 3xTg-AD mice.

White visceral adipose tissue transplantation from db/db mice accelerates tau pathology in 3xTg-AD mice

Hyperphosphorylation of the microtubule-associated protein tau is one of the major pathological events in AD [63]. Here, we investigated the effect of epididymal WAT on tau pathology in the 3xTg-AD mice (Fig. 3). WB analysis revealed a significant increase in tau phosphorylation at residues Ser202/Thr205 (AT8) in 3xTg-AD mice that received fat tissue from db/db mice compared with 3x SHAM and 3x WT mice, respectively (+ 106.47 ± 24.80% and + 121.72 ± 26.63%; one-way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction, p(3x SHAM − 3x db/db) = 0.0257, p(3x WT − 3x db/db) = 0.0254) (Fig. 3A, B Supplemental Fig. 6). In addition, neither steady-state (HT7) nor phospho-tau species recognized by Thr231(AT180) were altered by WAT transplantation (one-way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction) (Fig. 3A, B and Supplemental Fig. 6). Immunohistochemical analysis corroborated these previous results, and showed substantial accumulation of hyperphosphorylated tau recognized by pSer202/Thr205 antibody in the somato-dendritic area of the pyramidal layer neurons in 3x db/db (Fig. 3C3 and D) mice compared with that in 3x SHAM (Fig. 3C1 and D) and 3x WT (Fig. 3C2 and D) mice (+ 244.05 ± 58.02% and + 17.52 ± 35.74%; one-way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction, p(3x SHAM − 3x db/db) = 0.0333, p(3x WT − 3x db/db) = 0.0655). In addition, the levels of insoluble tau were also increased in 3x db/db mice compared with 3x SHAM and 3x WT mice, respectively (+ 119 ± 16.59% and + 159.28 ± 50.45%; one-way ANOVA, Tukey test, p(3x SHAM − 3x db/db) = 0.0159, p(3x WT − 3x db/db) = 0.0704) (Fig. 3E, F and Supplemental Fig. 6). Multiple different kinases and phosphatases modulate the level of phosphorylation of tau [63]. Here, we sought to identify the kinases involved in the accumulation of pSer202/Thr205-positive tau aggregates. Thus, the levels of total and activated forms of specific enzymes such as glycogen synthase kinase-3-beta (GSK3β), c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38MAPK), cyclin-dependent kinase 5 (CDK5), p35 and p25 were measured in the brains of 3xTg-AD mice under these three experimental conditions [63]. Notably, the results showed an specific increase in the steady-state levels of p25 in the 3x db/db group compared with 3x SHAM and 3x WT groups (+ 61.35 ± 8.83% and + 101.57 ± 11.03%; one-way ANOVA, Tukey test, p(3x SHAM − 3x db/db) = 0.0063, p(3x WT − 3x db/db) = 0.0010, respectively) (Fig. 3G, H and Supplemental Fig. 6), suggesting that the epididymal WAT led to tau hyperphosphorylation at the Ser202/Thr205 epitope by modulating the CDK5 pathway through the activator p25 [64].

Visceral fat pads transplanted from db/db mice reduce Aβ pathology in 3xTg-AD mice

In addition to the formation of neurofibrillary tangles, the AD brain is characterized by the accumulation of Aβ aggregates [65]. Here, we sought to determine whether white epididymal adipose tissue affects Aβ pathology. To this aim, we analyzed soluble and insoluble Aβ levels (Aβ38, Aβ40 and Aβ42) using the MSD platform (Fig. 4A and B). While soluble Aβ remained unaltered (Fig. 4A), insoluble Aβ levels showed a significant reduction in 3xTg-AD mice that received fat tissue from db/db mice compared to those transplanted with fat tissue from WT mice (Fig. 4B). Specifically, the 3x db/db group showed a decrease of -32.49 ± 7.28% in Aβ38 levels compared with the 3x WT group (one-way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction, p(3x WT − 3x db/db) = 0.1389), a decrease of -36.12 ± 5.90% in Aβ40 levels compared with 3x SHAM group, and a decrease of -37.41 ± 5.78% in Aβ42 levels compared with the 3x WT group (one-way ANOVA, Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction, p(3x SHAM − 3x db/db) = 0.7762, p(3x WT − 3x db/db) = 0.1673). Next, we corroborated our biochemical analysis with a histological approach by measuring the plaque load positive for Thio-S staining (Fig. 4C and D). Our data revealed a reduction in amyloid aggregates in the 3x db/db mice compared with both 3x SHAM and 3x WT groups. We further examined whether the decrease in Aβ was associated with any changes in either the production or clearance of the Aβ peptide. Thus, steady-state levels of the amyloid precursor protein (APP) holoprotein and constitutive proteases, including α-secretases (ADAM 10 and 17) and β-secretase (BACE1), were unaffected between 3x SHAM, 3x WT and 3x db/db mice (one-way ANOVA, Tukey test) (Fig. 4E, F and Supplemental Fig. 5). Additionally, we evaluated the effects of fat transplantation on the major Aβ-degrading enzymes [66, 67], such as neprilysin and insulin degrading enzyme (IDE), and found no differences between groups. Our data indicate that WAT from db/db mice reduces Aβ plaque formation in 3xTg-AD mice without altering Aβ processing or degradation (one-way ANOVA, Tukey test) (Fig. 4E, F and Supplemental Fig. 5).

Another important mechanism of amyloid catabolism occurs within cells that engulf the Aβ peptide. Microglia and astrocytes have been reported to play a central role as moderators of Aβ clearance and degradation [66, 67]. Given that we have observed changes in microglial morphology and activation state, we investigated whether fat tissue transplanted from db/db mice may activate a microglial response that determines the reduction in amyloid burden in 3x db/db mice. Thus, we performed a triple immunostaining for microglial cells (Iba1), Aβ deposits (6E10), and phagolysosomes (CD68) (Fig. 5A). Cluster of differentiation 68 (CD68) is a microglial lysosomal protein expressed at high levels by macrophages and activated microglia and is a well-known indicator of phagocytic activity [68, 69]. Triple immunostaining and quantification revealed greater colocalization of these markers in 3x db/db mice than in 3x WT mice (white arrows, Fig. 5A2 and B) (+ 44.88 ± 8.94%; Unpaired t test with equal SD, p = 0.0186). Overall, our study suggests that fat pads from db/db mice trigger an activated and phagocytic phenotype in microglial cells in the hippocampus of 3xTg-AD mice compared with 3xTg-AD mice treated with fat pads from WT mice, and resulting in a reduction in the number of plaques.

Discussion

In this study, we provide compelling evidence of the differential regulation on tau and Aβ pathologies mediated by the white visceral adipose tissue. Specifically, our data demonstrate that transplantation of epididymal fat pads from a model of diabetes and obesity (db/db mice) into the abdominal cavity of 3xTg-AD mice (3x db/db group) exacerbates the neuroimmune response and cerebral tau pathology through modulation of the CDK5/p25 pathway. Furthermore, the 3x db/db group also presented a decrease in amyloid aggregates associated with an enhanced microglial phagocytic function. Together, these findings highlight the connection between peripheral metabolic organs, including the AT, and the brain. More importantly, the dysregulation of these metabolic organs in different diseases such as obesity or diabetes modulates the progression of AD pathology.

In this context, obesity and diabetes are associated with a state of chronic low-grade inflammation [30–34], in which the infiltration of immune cells within the AT is a key contributor to this inflammatory state [30–34]. In our study, we found that the white visceral adipose tissue of db/db mice displayed an enlargement of adipocytes along with an increment in the interstitial spaces compared to WT section, suggesting the accumulation of infiltrated cells. To support this, WB analysis revealed an increase in the steady-state level of the immune cell marker MHC class II in the WAT of db/db mice compared with the WT control mice. In addition, ELISA assay showed a high increase of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL12p79, KC/GRO and TNF-α, in visceral WAT from db/db mice compared to WT, confirming the inflammatory state of this tissue. Hence, previous and pioneering studies using a mouse model of diabetes and obesity have demonstrated a significant increase in ATMs [37–39, 56, 57]. Notably, these works revealed that most of the ATMs within WAT release multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12 and TNF-α, and express high levels of different surface markers, for instance, MHC class II, F4/80, CD68, CD11b or CD11c [37–39, 70]. Thus, our current findings support these previous studies showing an increment of ATMs polarized to a pro-inflammatory profile. Moreover, recent single cell RNA sequencing studies revealed that the macrophage population in the AT is quite heterogeneous and clustered into several subpopulations that exhibit distinct functional states [71–73]. In addition, multiple different immune cells such as mast cells, neutrophils, eosinophils, natural killers, B and T lymphocytes also contribute to the immune milieu of AT and influence the inflammatory tone in complex ways [71–73]. Taken together, these studies support a complex model in which lean AT maintains an anti-inflammatory environment, whereas obese and diabetic AT adopts a pro-inflammatory phenotype in which multiple immune cells are involved.

This change not only alters local tissue homeostasis but can also exert systemic and pleiotropic effects that influence the brain and may contribute to the development and progression of neurodegenerative diseases such as AD [18, 23, 31, 36, 40]. Notably, the findings of the current study indicate that the pro-inflammatory state in the AT is translated to the brain and exacerbates the neuroinflammatory response in 3xTg-AD mice and modulates tau and Aβ pathology. Specifically, we observed an important change in microglia cells towards and activated state in the 3xTg-AD mice that received fat pads from db/db mice compared with those in the control groups. This response seems to be specific to microglia since no morphological changes were found in astroglial cells. Recent studies have shown that epididymal fat pads from db/db mice cause significant synaptic and cognitive impairments in WT mice through an activated microglial response [56, 57]. Notably, these reports demonstrated that IL-1β emerged as a key mediator that correlates hippocampal dysfunction with obesity showing high levels in both tissues, the brain and AT. Moreover, using an IL-1 receptor antagonist (IL1Ra) approach or blocking IL-1 signaling specifically in microglia cells mitigates the synaptic and cognitive deficits induced by the AT [56, 57]. Interestingly and in accordance with this data, our study showed an increase in IL-1β in both the WAT from donor db/db mice and the brain of receptor 3x db/db mice. Taken together, our study and previous work suggest that IL-1 signaling is a key mechanism to link these peripheral changes with the CNS. However, a key question that remains to be addressed in this study and others is to determine the main cell type responsible for the increase of IL-1β in the epididymal fat pads, since both immune cells and adipocytes may contribute to the high levels of this cytokine in the WAT [74–77].

Although the brain has been considered an immune-privileged organ, under certain conditions such as infection, obesity or inflammatory-related neurological diseases, it could be compromised by the disruption of the BBB and the infiltration of peripheral immune cells [31, 50, 62, 78–82]. Here, we demonstrated that the AT transplant in 3xTg-AD mice did not induce the infiltration of peripheral macrophages (CD163+ cells) and had no major effect on lymphocyte migration (CD3+ cells) into the hippocampal parenchyma. Nevertheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that these cells may exert a paracrine effect, modulating the activated neuroinflammatory response observed in the brains of 3x db/db mice.

The formation of tau aggregates is an important hallmark present in postmortem AD samples [63, 83–85]. The tau protein undergoes multiple aberrant conformational modifications that lead to its aggregation [86–89]. One of these modifications is the increase in phosphorylation, which reduces tau affinity for microtubules at the synaptic compartment and facilitates its aggregation into neurofibrillary tangles in the neuronal soma [90–93]. The phosphorylation of tau protein is carried out through different kinases [63], being CDK5 one of the most relevant enzymes for this process. CDK5 is activated by p35 in post-mitotic neurons and CDK5/p35 is thought to play an important role in brain development and synaptic activity [94–97]. However, in brain diseases such as AD, CDK5 is proposed to be abnormally activated [98–100] or hyperactivated by p25. Importantly, several studies have highlighted the role of CDK5/p25 in abnormal tau phosphorylation at different sites, including Ser202 and Thr205 [64, 101–103]. These previous evidence support our findings by showing that 3x db/db group has an increase in tau phosphorylation in these residues compared with the 3x WT and 3x SHAM groups. This growth in tau hyperphosphorylation was associated with an increase in the steady state level of the p25 activators, suggesting that fat pad transplant from db/db mice exacerbates the tau pathology by modulating CDK5/p25.

Inflammation is a key mechanistic driver of AD pathogenesis. Interestingly, it has been recently reported that microglial activation anatomically correlates with tau pathology in AD brains [104, 105] and probably plays an important role in regulating the tauopathy [106]. For instance, microglial activation induces tau phosphorylation and aggregation and promotes tau propagation in the brain [107–111]. Here, we demonstrated that visceral fat pad transplantation from db/db mice accelerates the tau pathology in 3xTg-AD mice that is associated with an exacerbation of the microglial response. Similarly, several in vivo and in vitro studies have shown that inflammation induced by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or IL-1β overexpression in transgenic mice increases tau hyperphosphorylation through the modulation of several putative tau kinases such as GSK3β, p38MAPK and CDK5 [112–115]. In our study, we detected an increase in IL-1β in the hippocampus of 3x db/db mice that could be responsible for the tau hyperphosphorylation through the activation of CDK5 by increased formation of the p25 fragment. Interestingly, the inhibition of IL-1 signaling reduces the activity of several tau kinases in the brain, including CDK5/p25, and also decreases phosphorylated tau levels in 3xTg-AD mice [116]. Thus, our work suggests that an activated microglial response mediated by elevated levels of IL-1β may exacerbate tau pathology through the CDK5/p25 pathway.

In the present study, we also determined the impact of fat pad transplantation on Aβ pathology. Here, we demonstrated a reduction in the insoluble Aβ levels and amyloid aggregates in 3xTg-AD mice after the fat transplantation from obese mice. These changes were not associated with either altering the APP processing or the level of the primordial Aβ clearance enzymes. However, this decreased burden was due to an increase in microglial Aβ uptake as shown by the higher colocalization of Aβ, Iba1 and the marker of microglial phagocytic activity CD68. Several studies have reported that Aβ plaque clearance through phagocytic activation of microglia induce the expression and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines by these cells, including IL-1β [117–119]. Actually, IL-1β is released by activated microglia after stimulation with Aβ in vitro, and higher expression of IL-1β has also been detected in microglial cells surrounding Aβ plaques in both AD patients and animal models [120, 121]. In addition, overexpression of IL-1β in amyloidogenic transgenic mice showed a decrease in Aβ pathology mediated by an activated and phagocytic microglia response with no change in the expression or level of APP, BACE and APP-related products [115, 119, 122]. Accordingly, our data also demonstrates a similar response and an increase in IL-1β levels in 3x db/db mice, however, whether the increase in IL-1β levels in the hippocampus of 3xTg-AD mice is produced by the activated resident microglia, or other brain or peripheral immune cells remains to be determined. With this regard, several studies have shown that the peripheral IL-1β can reach the CNS through saturated transporters through the BBB [123, 124] and initiate a local amplification in the resident microglia cells [125, 126]. Interestingly, a recent study demonstrated that visceral fat pads initiate an IL-1R1 activation in brain-resident immune cells, which leads to the development of synaptic and cognitive impairments in the CNS [57], and therefore, reinforcing this idea.

Overall, our study indicates that the fat transplant from db/db mice triggers a pro-inflammatory response that differentially modulates Aβ and tau pathology in 3xTg-AD mice. However, the adipocytes can express a high variety of different molecules including hormones, growth factors, cytokines, reactive lipid metabolites and lipokines that may reach the brain and influence AD progression [18, 23, 127–129]. Our study showed an important morphological and functional alteration in the epidydimal fat that resulted in a reduction in the expression of adiponectin, resistin and FGF21 levels. Here, we additionally studied the level of these adipokines in the brain of 3xTg-AD mice, since several studies have shown that changes in these molecules impact on Aβ and tau pathology through modulation of stress response kinases, mitochondrial activity, lysosomal pathways and inflammatory processes in AD models [18, 130–134]. No major differences were found in the brain of 3xTg-AD for these molecules, although a tendency to change the levels of resistin and FGF21 were observed in the brain samples of 3x db/db mice compared to the other two groups, suggesting that it could be possible that the levels of these adipokines and the inflammatory state in the epididymal fat may play together a central role as the driver of the changes observed in Aβ and tau pathology in this model.

In this context, it has been described that FGF21 can cross the BBB by diffusion [135], bind its receptors expressed in the brain [136, 137] and play an important neuroprotective role in both Alzheimer´s and Parkinson´s disease [138–141]. In AD, several studies have shown that FGF21 is able to reduce the level of hyperphosphorylated tau and reduce the formation of neurofibrillary tangles [139, 142, 143]. From a mechanistic point of view, these studies proposed that FGF21 could mitigate the tau pathology by modulating the inflammatory response and reducing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β [144–146]. Thus, the reduction of FGF21 and the increase of resistin in 3x db/db mice could contribute to the pro-inflammatory milieu in the brain of 3xTg-AD mice and exacerbate tau pathology.

As a limitation of the present work, it will be necessary to determine in future studies if the epidydimal fat transplant affects the function of several peripheral metabolic organs in the 3xTg-AD, including endogenous adipose tissue, liver and pancreas. It is possible that the transplant could exert a direct effect on the brain but also an indirect connection through modulating these metabolic organs. With this regard, an important communication exists between these organs and a dysregulation of any of them may imply important consequences to the development or progression of AD pathology [30, 76, 147–150]. Finally, given the important heterogeneity of the adipose fat pads, with differences in lipolytic and lipogenic activities, adipokines secretion and infiltration rate [23], new studies addressing their differential impacts on Aβ and tau, might shed light about their implication concerning neurodegenerative disorders such as AD.

Conclusions

In summary, our study suggests that the pro-inflammatory environment associated with the WAT in diverse chronic metabolic disorders such as obesity and diabetes can have a major impact on the progression of multiple neurological disorders, including AD. Moreover, this study highlights the important connection between the periphery and the brain, and how these metabolic alterations may affect brain function. A more profound understanding of these mechanisms would reveal novel insights and provide new opportunities to discover potential therapeutic strategies to treat both metabolic and neurodegenerative diseases.
