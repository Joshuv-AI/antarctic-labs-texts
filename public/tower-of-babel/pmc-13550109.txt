A Retrospective Chart Review of PTSD Symptoms in Military Servicemembers Participating in an Individually Guided Neuromodulation (Alpha-rTMS) Wellness Program
Authors: Alexander Balbir, Kayleigh Prowse, Julie Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Military servicemembers, particularly those who have been exposed to combat, face heightened risks of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD, estimated to range from 4–17%),1,2 which can significantly impair their overall quality of life.1 In addition, PTSD has high comorbidity with depression,3 sleep disruptions,4 and traumatic brain injuries (TBI).5 PTSD is reported to affect approximately 25% of veterans who served in Iraq and Afghanistan,6 with incidences as high as 32% in civilian first responders.7 As many as 11 million adults suffer from PTSD in a given year.8 There is an urgent need for effective non-pharmacological treatment with minimal side effects. Since standard of care interventions yield suboptimal outcomes or are limited by side effects and patient non-compliance.9,10

Current FDA-approved pharmaceutical options, including Sertraline and Paroxetine, have reported only up to 13% improvement in PTSD severity rates for patients with PTSD.11 Non-pharmacological standard of care interventions, such as cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) and cognitive processing therapy (CPT), report modest reduction in PCL scores.12 Noninvasive neuromodulation in the form of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) has been used in the treatment of military veterans suffering from depression and comorbid PTSD. Therapy parameters for rTMS are expansive, as modifications to pulse frequency, stimulation location, stimulation intensity and other components of an rTMS session are all potentially adjustable.13 Of these parameters, the frequency and stimulation targets are potentially important for optimal therapeutic response. Contemporary applications of rTMS target the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (LDLPFC) due to its potential impact on the subgenual cingulate as involved in depressive pathology;14 however, it has been theorized that a separate circuit may be more closely associated with PTSD pathology – the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and default mode network.15,16

The default mode network (DMN) comprised the mPFC and lateral parietal lobules17 and involved in self-referential thought, memory and directed attention, and has been implicated as disrupted in PTSD, the dysregulation of which potentially impacts emotional recall and executive function.2 Additionally, dysregulation of the DMN has been linked to disruption in the electroencephalographic (EEG) 8–13 Hz resting-state alpha rhythm, with alpha power attenuation reported in key DMN hubs including the precuneus and the mPFC in PTSD.16 It has been shown that in normal healthy controls that transcranial stimulation of alpha oscillations may upregulate the DMN.18 Stimulation frequency proximity to individual alpha frequency (IAF) of patient EEG has been linked to treatment response in depression, where more proximal stimulation has garnered more positive response.19,20 TMS where stimulation is set to resting-state individualized alpha activity (α-rTMS), using the magnetic EEG/EKG resonant therapy (MeRT) algorithm, has been supported by several positive preliminary reports in the treatment of PTSD symptoms,21–23 with 42% reduction in PTSD symptom severity for military veterans and 37.1% reduction in active-duty special operators, and has since been cleared by the FDA (2026) for treatment of PTSD.

In an effort to support the large number of veterans suffering from symptoms of PTSD and service-related disabilities, the Wounded Warrior Project (WWP), which supports over 200,000 injured military veterans, funded the Operation Synchrony, a novel neurorehabilitation program to treat veterans of any military branch with α-rTMS while monitoring clinical symptoms. We used the PTSD Symptom Checklist (PCL-5), a standard of care for assessing PTSD, as a self-report measure of symptom severity. This chart review examines and reports on the charts of patients who participated in the program and received α-rTMS in a clinical environment to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of the intervention on symptom severity reflected in change of the PCL-5 scores.

Methods

Participants

Patient charts from three outpatient Southern California medical clinics participating in the program were obtained and reviewed from self-referred retired and active military members. Charts, including clinical scales and notes, were labeled with randomized IDs assigned at the registration of the patients to the program. Data were extracted from charts into data tables which were then used for analysis. The participants had to have served on or after September 11, 2001 and self-reported clinical symptoms associated with PTSD. Program participants were also required to commit to receiving five days of α-rTMS treatment per week, for 4 weeks (20 treatments in total). Participants were assessed for eligibility based on their service history and for having any contraindications to receiving rTMS (including pacemakers, defibrillators, metal in the head, history of seizures). Participants agreed to abstain from use of drugs of abuse and alcohol throughout the course of the program and were breathalyzed before each visit. Evidence of abusing drugs and alcohol was grounds for dismissal from the treatment program and therefore was not included in this chart review. Further, the charts did not include information on concomitant medications or other treatments; however, this was not an exclusion criterion. Participants were asked to keep medication/therapy the same prior and during the treatment. For each scale, only those participants who provided complete data at baseline (prior to treatment), at two weeks into treatment (week-2), and at four weeks (end of treatment, week-4) were included for analysis.

Intake and personal history forms, clinical scales, therapy notes, treatment notes, and EEG data from those in the program between January 1, 2021, and December 31, 2023, were reviewed following IRB approval, and written informed consent for treatment was obtained. This chart review protocol was approved under an exemption by WCG (Western IRB and Copernicus Group) IRB Study #1-1763934-1. All identifying information was removed and de-identified prior to analysis and during data aggregation. Charts were examined for data on clinical scales, reported side effects, daily therapy notes taken, data pertaining to intake and screening, history of injury, and demographics.

Operation Synchrony Procedures

α-rTMS Treatment

Participants underwent a 10-minute electroencephalogram (EEG) recording with eyes closed, using the 19 standardized scalp locations of the International 10–20 Electrode Placement System at baseline, week-2, and week-4. The EEG data served as input to the determination of each participant’s TMS stimulation pulse rate.22,23

The magnetic pulse frequency was set to their derived pulse rate (IAF), and stimulation intensity was targeted at 80% of motor threshold. α-rTMS, commercially known as Magnetic EEG-EKG Resonant Therapy (MeRT), was delivered in 5 s trains, with 45 s intertrain intervals, for a total of 36 trains, or approximately 30 minutes.

Participants were assigned a randomized patient ID at the beginning of the program, which was used as the identifier for their record and session notes. Participants received daily α-rTMS sessions delivered 5 days a week, 30 minutes each, for a total of four weeks (a total of 20 sessions). At each session, clinical staff recorded any side effects related to α-rTMS in the participant’s chart.

Clinical Scales

Participants completed clinical scales and self-report questionnaires at three time points: baseline, week-2, and week-4. The scales included (1) the Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) which is a 20-item self-report measure to assess symptoms and severity of PTSD, ranging from 0 to 80,24 where a PCL-5 cutoff score between 31 and 33 is considered indicative of probable clinical evidence of PTSD.25,26 A PCL-5 score of ≥33 was used to identify a subgroup with probable PTSD; (2) the Rivermead Post Concussion Syndrome Questionnaire which is a 16-item self-report measure evaluating post-concussive symptoms, with a final sum score ranging from 0 to 64;27 (3) the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) which is a 9-item scale used to assess depression severity, with a final sum score ranging from 0 to 27. A PHQ-9 score of ≥15 was used to identify a subgroup with probable “moderately-severe” levels of depression;28 and (4) the Brief Resilience Scale (BRS) which is a 6-item measure assessing the individual’s ability to bounce back from stress.29 For all scores except the BRS, larger scores reflect higher levels of impairment, whereas lower BRS scores reflect lower levels of resiliency.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics at each time point were computed using Pingouin v0.5.4 in Python v3.9.12. The group average percent change was calculated from baseline to week-4 to reflect the total percent change across the course of the program for each scale. Percent change from baseline to week-2 and from week-2 to week-4 was then calculated to reflect the portion of total change that occurred in the first two and second two weeks.

For each scale, a one-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was estimated to model the scores across three time points. Paired-sample t-tests were then used to test the significance of change in scores between each time point, and 95% confidence intervals for the mean difference were obtained. Three post-hoc statistical tests were performed for each scale; therefore, a Bonferroni correction was applied to correct for multiple comparisons within each scale. Thus, statistical significance was set using .05 for corrected p-values. Cohen’s d effect sizes were also calculated for each comparison to facilitate interpretation of the effects.

Separate thresholds were applied to baseline PCL-5 (≥33) and PHQ-9 (≥15) scales to identify subgroups, which were used to further examine how participants with severe levels of impairment progress through the program. In addition, data on the incidence of side effects including headache, fatigue, nausea/dizziness, serious adverse events (SAEs), as normally reported in TMS procedures, were extracted from treatment notes and presented based on their incidence across the treatment period.

Results

Participants

Charts from a total of N=94 participants who completed the program were eligible for data analysis. The mean age of participants was 39.3 years (range: 21–76 years), and the sample comprised 77 males and 17 females. See Table 1 for demographic details. It is noted that there were n=10 participants not included in the analyses, as they did not fully complete the program, 2 who withdrew before receiving any treatment and 8 who discontinued prior to completion of the program. The majority stopped treatment for personal reasons (moving, scheduling conflicts, etc). Critically, this small percentage of participants that did not complete the program were considered to be random occurrences and did not significantly differ from the completers on age (p=0.23), gender (p=0.52), or baseline scales (PCL-5 p=0.80; PHQ-9 p=0.61; RPQ-16 p=0.46; BRS p=0.19).

Treatment Protocol Information

All participants received stimulation at their α-rTMS frequency (M=10.91 Hz, SD=0.84 Hz) corresponding to the Fpz location on a standard 10–20 system EEG cap.

Clinical Scales

From the full sample of N=94, all timepoints were not available for PCL-5 in 3 participants (3.2%), PHQ-9 in 5 participants (5.3%), RPQ-16 in 6 participants (6.4%), and BRS in 7 participants (7.4%). Despite this small amount of missing data, imputation was not performed as this would have artificially reduced the standard errors in an already large sample. Hence, any statistical benefit would have been negligible at the risk of distorting the underlying distributions of the available data. Therefore, charts with missing data on a given scale were excluded from the analyses for that scale.

Average PCL-5 scores decreased by a total of 48% from baseline (M=35.9, SE=2.02) to week-4 (M=18.8, SE=1.58). Of the 48% total change, 31% of the decrease occurred by week-2, and an additional 17% decrease occurred from week-2 to week-4. A significant effect of time was identified from the ANOVA on PCL-5 scores (F=58.5, p=2.7e−20). Paired-sample t-tests indicated that the change from baseline to week-4 was highly significant (t=9.0, p=1.1e−13, 95% CI=[13.3, 20.9]), as was the change from baseline to week-2 (t=6.6, p=9.6e−9, 95% CI=[7.8, 14.6]), and week-2 to week-4 (t=5.4, p=1.9e−6, 95% CI=[3.7, 8.1]). Cohen’s d effect sizes ranged from large (d=0.99) for baseline to week-4, medium (d=0.61) for baseline to week-2, and small (d=0.37) for week-2 to week-4.

A threshold was applied to identify a subgroup whose baseline PCL-5 scores supported a probable clinical PTSD symptomatology. There were 48 participants who met threshold on the PCL-5 at baseline (PCL-5≥33), representing 53% (48/91) of the population. During the program, 48% (23/48) of this subgroup achieved sub-threshold PCL-5 scores at week-2. An additional 13 participants achieved sub-threshold PCL-5 scores between week-2 and week-4 for a total of 75% (36/48) of this subgroup by end of the program.

A similar trend was observed for the other scales, such that a highly significant effect of time was observed (see Table 2). Generally, the most significant differences were seen between baseline and week-4 (all pcorr.<.05; see Table 2 for details). Likewise, significant changes were also observed from baseline to week-2 for all scales, and significant improvements continued from week-2 to week-4 for all scales except BRS.

Side Effects

No serious adverse events related to the treatments were reported in charts. Side effects were mild and were reported transiently throughout the program. 23 participants (24%) reported fatigue, 12 participants (13%) reported headaches, and 5 participants (5%) reported nausea/dizziness more than once during the entirety of the program. The majority of the side effects were reported within the first two weeks and all resolved by the end of treatment.

Discussion

This study retrospectively extracted clinical outcomes from the medical charts of participants in a wellness program (Operation Synchrony) using α-rTMS treatment in active duty and veteran military servicemembers with symptoms of PTSD, depression, and traumatic brain injury. Chart analyses demonstrated substantial improvements across all domains, with highly significant reductions by the end of the four-week treatment period, with the largest changes seen between baseline and week-2 of the wellness program. Importantly, strong reductions were noted among those whose baseline symptoms were above clinical thresholds. Results showed that 75% of the participants who met baseline threshold for PTSD on the PCL-5 and 78% of those who met baseline threshold for moderately severe depression on the PHQ-9 had achieved sub-threshold scores by the end of treatment. These findings provide real-world data in support of the potential effectiveness of α-rTMS treatment in the PTSD population. Recent FDA clearance (K260402) of MeRT for personalized treatment of PTSD patients demonstrated the effectiveness in a blinded, sham-controlled RCT.

The reductions in PCL-5, PHQ-9, and RPQ-16 scores reflect clinically meaningful symptom relief, as evidenced by large effect sizes and the large percentage of patients who fell below thresholds after 4 weeks of α-rTMS treatment. Notably, these results are within the range or better than those reported in existing literature for traditional pharmacological treatments.9,10 These improvements likely stem from the individualized EEG-guided nature of α-rTMS (MeRT), which targets cortical dysregulation commonly associated with PTSD. The significant improvements in resilience, as indicated by the BRS improvement, further suggest that MeRT may help foster adaptive coping mechanisms, which are crucial for long-term recovery and quality of life.

While the program lasted four weeks, it is noted that the majority of the improvements were observed from baseline to week-2. Those effects can be seen for both percent change and the proportion of the sample who achieved sub-threshold scores. While we do not minimize the continued improvements that were observed from week-2 to week-4, these findings suggest that α-rTMS may be associated with a rapid recovery within the first two weeks.

α-rTMS was well-tolerated by participants, with mild and transient side effects reported in a minority of cases. Temporary fatigue and headache were the most common complaints, consistent with side effects reported in other rTMS-based interventions. Importantly, no serious adverse events were recorded. These findings emphasize the safety profile of α-rTMS, likely contributed to by using an 80% motor threshold, which is well below that of conventional TMS treatment outcome studies.

While the results are promising and add important information in regards to the real-world application of MeRT for PTSD symptoms in the military population, several limitations are acknowledged. The absence of a control group limits the ability to assess the impact of regression to the mean, placebo effects, or the natural course of the condition on the reported improvements with α-rTMS treatment. Since concomitant treatments were not reported, between-group differences could reflect, at least in part, differences due to concomitant treatments received during the study period and could present limitations to the interpretation of these findings. Future studies should include well-characterized patients with a formal PTSD diagnosis in randomized controlled trials, including a sham-controlled condition, to establish causality and also assess long-term persistence of the treatment effectiveness (eg, 3- and 6-month follow-up). Such studies are currently underway. Additionally, further research should explore the effectiveness of α-rTMS across diverse settings and among different demographic groups suffering from PTSD, including female veterans, who were underrepresented in this sample, and investigation of those who were excluded due to missing clinical scales.

This study contributes to a growing body of evidence supporting α-rTMS as an innovative, individualized treatment for PTSD, depression, and TBI in veterans and active-duty military servicemembers. The significant improvements in psychological functioning observed in this study underscore the potential for α-rTMS to address the multifaceted needs of this population.
