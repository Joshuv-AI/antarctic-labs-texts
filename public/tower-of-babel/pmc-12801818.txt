Sweet orange essential oil and (+)-limonene prevent oxidative stress, reduce inflammation, and apoptosis in differentiated SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma/BV-2 microglia co-culture neurodegeneration models
Authors: Edina Pandur, Loretta Heilmann, Margita Szilágyi-Utczás, Tibor Rák, Katalin Sipos, Adrienne Csutak, Györgyi Horváth
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParkinson’s disease (PD) is becoming increasingly prevalent worldwide. The pathophysiology of this condition is characterized by oxidative stress, inflammation, iron accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and protein aggregation, all of which contribute to cell death and neurodegeneration. Microglia, the innate immune cells of the brain, play a significant role in the development and progression of PD by releasing inflammatory cytokines upon activation. Essential oils (EOs) are emerging as potential therapeutic agents because of their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties.MethodsIn our study, we investigated the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects of sweet orange [Citrus sinensis (L.) Osbeck (Rutaceae)] EO and its main compound (+)-limonene on monocultures of all-trans retinoic acid-differentiated and 6-hydroxydopamine-induced (6-OHDA) SH-SY5Y cells and bilaminar co-cultures containing differentiated and 6-hydroxydopamine-induced SH-SY5Y cells and BV-2 microglia.ResultsSweet orange EO and (+)-limonene significantly decreased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and increased oxidative stress defense by increasing the total antioxidant capacity and glutathione peroxidase and superoxide dismutase activities in human neuroblastoma (SH-SY5Y) cells. Additionally, both EO and its main compound mitigated inflammation by downregulating the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines. They also decreased the cytochrome c levels and caspase-3 activity. Furthermore, sweet orange EO and (+)-limonene attenuated microglia-mediated inflammation in co-cultures, suggesting their potential application as modulators of microglial activity.ConclusionsBased on these results, sweet orange EO and its main component, (+)-limonene, can be considered as neuroprotective agents and are promising candidates for complementary therapy in Parkinson’s disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05215-z.

Background

Essential oils (EOs) are complex mixtures of volatile compounds isolated by distillation or pressing from different plants. They can be extracted from various plant parts, including seeds, flowers, tree blossoms, leaves, bark, fruits, grasses, roots, gums, and bulbs [1]. Their main components are terpenes (monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes) and phenylpropanoids [2]. In recent decades, EOs have garnered significant research attention due to their numerous medicinal properties [3]Their beneficial effects include antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antitumor, and antioxidant activities [3–7]. They are also promising therapies for brain disorders, such as depression, anxiety, stress, sleep disorders, mental fatigue, and neurodegenerative diseases, such as Parkinson’s disease (PD), Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and glaucoma [8–14].

Sweet orange [Citrus sinensis (L.) Osbeck (Rutaceae)] is a small evergreen tree originally domesticated in subtropical Asia. It is currently cultivated in warm climates worldwide. Sweet orange EO (SO EO) exhibits a significant antioxidant effect due to its high content of phenolic and monoterpene compounds [15–17]. It can prevent the formation of free radicals by inhibiting several oxidizing enzymes, such as cyclooxygenase, NADPH-oxidase, lipoxygenase, and nitric oxide synthase [17, 18]. SO EO can be used in the food industry because of its antimicrobial properties [19].

The main components of SO EO are limonene, specifically (+)-limonene (83.9–95.9%), myrcene, and α-pinene (0.6–1.0%) [20–23]. (+)-Limonene is widely used in the food and cosmetics industries, as well as in aromatherapy and medicine [24]. It is considered safe for human use because it exhibits low toxicity and does not have teratogenic, carcinogenic, or mutagenic effects [24]Limonene possesses pharmacological properties, including antibacterial, anticancer, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective [25–28]. (+)-Limonene exhibits analgesic action by inhibiting the transient receptor potential (TRP) vanilloid 1 (V1)/ankyrin 1 (A1) receptors [29]. (+)-Limonene may also be a promising treatment for glaucoma, which is characterized by progressive optic neuropathy [30]. Myrcene exerts neuroprotective effects in a mouse model of AD by decreasing neuroinflammation and reducing β-amyloid plaque formation [31]. α-Pinene suppresses TNF-α production and decreases the activity of the NF-κB signaling pathway in an Alzheimer’s disease rat model, providing neuroprotection [32, 33].

Based on the main effects of the primary compounds of SO EO, it may be a promising treatment for neurodegenerative diseases in which oxidative stress and inflammation contribute to neuronal apoptosis, such as AD, PD, and even glaucoma [30, 34].

PD is one of the most prevalent neurodegenerative diseases, causing motor and non-motor dysfunctions [35]. PD affects dopaminergic neurons, leading to decreased dopamine production and deterioration of the central nervous system [36]. However, the background of this disease remains unclear. It can be described by oxidative stress and inflammation, which can be affected by iron accumulation, leading to ferroptosis [37–39]. Conversely, α-synuclein accumulation in the dopaminergic neurons may also contribute to oxidative damage and mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to apoptosis [40, 41]. Dopaminergic amacrine cells in the human retina and ganglion cells are negatively affected by iron accumulation and ferroptosis. PD shares common genetic and molecular pathomechanisms with primary open-angle glaucoma, independent of intraocular pressure [42, 43].

The primary goal of Parkinson’s therapies is to restore dopamine levels, which are downregulated by damage to dopaminergic neurons, and to alleviate the deterioration of motor symptoms [44]. One way to achieve this is to use levodopa, which acts as a precursor and is metabolized to dopamine in the brain, effectively compensating for neurotransmitter deficiency [45, 46]. Selective monoamine oxidase B (MAO-B) inhibitors such as selegiline and rasagiline prevent the breakdown of dopamine and possess antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [46, 47]. Another possibility is the utilisation of dopamine agonists, which can produce effects similar to those of dopamine by binding to dopamine receptors [48]. In addition to the drugs currently in use, invasive interventions may be necessary to slow disease progression. Deep brain stimulation (DBS) and, less commonly, focused ultrasound ablation or Duopa™ therapy, primarily targeting the subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus internus, are the most widely used neurosurgical treatments for Parkinson’s disease, especially in patients with motor complications unresponsive to medication [49]. The main goals of DBS and other advanced therapies, such as levodopa-carbidopa intestinal gel infusion (Duopa™ therapy), are to reduce motor fluctuations, improve the quality of life, and decrease the need for dopaminergic medications [49, 50]. Given the need for new and effective therapies, the development of potent herbal compounds offers a promising alternative that may help patients avoid invasive interventions in the future.

In the present study, we examined the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects of SO EO and (+)-limonene, the main compound. An in vitro model was established using retinoic acid-differentiated SH-SY5Y cells, in which oxidative stress was induced by treatment with 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA). Monocultures of differentiated SH-SY5Y cells and co-cultures of differentiated SH-SY5Y and BV-2 microglial cells were used for the examinations. The cultures were treated with SO EO or (+)-limonene, and rasagiline, an MAO-B inhibitor, was used as a positive control.

These findings indicate that SO EO and (+)-limonene effectively reduced oxidative stress and enhanced antioxidant defense mechanisms in differentiated SH-SY5Y cells. These compounds also attenuated inflammation by downregulating the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Furthermore, SO EO and (+)-limonene promoted ATP synthesis while reducing cytochrome c levels and caspase-3 activity, suggesting their anti-apoptotic properties. Consequently, SO EO and (+)-limonene have emerged as promising candidates for alternative therapeutic interventions in PD.

Materials and methods

Gas chromatography (GC) analysis of sweet orange essential oil

The solvent (dichloromethane; DCM), n-alkane homologue line (C7-C30; Supelco #49451-U; Merck Life Sciences Kft, Budapest, Hungary), and samples at 1:1000 dilution were analyzed on an Agilent 7890B gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) coupled to an Agilent 7010B triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) in the MS1 scan and electron ionization (EI) mode. An HP-5ms (5% diphenyl + 95% dimethyl polysiloxane) 30 m × 0.25 mm ID × 0.25 μm column (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used for separation. The gas chromatograph was equipped with a split/splitless injector at a temperature of 280 °C. He (purity 5.0) was used as the carrier gas at a constant linear velocity of 30.0 cm/sec. The interface was maintained at 200 °C, and the ion source temperature was 250 °C. Separation was achieved using a linear gradient from an initial oven temperature of 50 °C at 3 °C/min to a final temperature of 350 °C, which was maintained for 5 min. The mass range was tested at m/z = 40 and 550, respectively. The injection volume was 0.5 µL.

To calculate the linear retention index (LRI), the C7-C30 n-alkane lines were applied. The compounds were identified using the FFNSC 4.0 GC-MS library (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The MS spectral similarity criterion was > 90%, and the LRI window was ± 5 units.

Chiral analysis was carried out on the same GC-MS instrument, applying a Supelco β‑DEX™ 120 (30 m × 0.25 mm ID, 0.25 μm) column (Merck Life Sciences Kft, Budapest, Hungary) using the following gradient: an initial oven temperature of 50 °C was maintained for 3 min, followed by a ramp at 10 °C/min to 90 °C maintained for 8 min, then reached at 20 °C/min at a final temperature of 220 °C, which was maintained for 5 min. All other parameters were the same as those used in non-chiral analysis.

Cell cultures and treatments

SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells (ATCC-CRL-2266) were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Capricorn Scientific GmbH, Ebsdorfergrund, Germany) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Capricorn Scientific GmbH, Ebsdorfergrund, Germany), 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA; Capricorn Scientific GmbH, Ebsdorfergrund, Germany), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S; Capricorn Scientific GmbH, Ebsdorfergrund, Germany). BV-2 murine microglial cells (a gift from Prof. Dr. László Tretter, Semmelweis University, Budapest, Hungary) were maintained in high-glucose DMEM (Capricorn Scientific GmbH, Ebsdorfergrund, Germany) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S in poly L-ornithine-coated flasks (Biologix Europe, Hallbergmoos, Germany). SY-SY5Y cells (passage number ≤ four) were differentiated by adding 1 µM all-trans retinoic acid for 5 days using serum-depleted (1% FBS) complete culture medium. Oxidative stress was induced in differentiated SH-SY5Y cells by treatment with 150 µM 6-OHDA (Merck Life Sciences Kft, Budapest, Hungary) for 24 h. The differentiated SH-SY5Y cells were then treated with 5000-fold dilutions (0,018% final concentration) of SO EO ((+)-limonene concentration in Eo 1530.3 ng/mL) or (+)-limonene (1680.26 ng/mL) for 24 h. Rasagiline (Merck Life Sciences Kft, Budapest, Hungary), an MAO-B inhibitor, was used as a positive control at a concentration of 1 µM for 24 h. For co-culture experiments, SH-SY5Y cells were differentiated and treated with 6-OHDA for 24 h. BV-2 cells were seeded on sterile plastic coverslips (22 mm; Sarstedt AG & Co. KG, Nümbrecht, Germany) coated with poly-L-ornithine. Coverslips were added to the differentiated 6-OHDA-treated SH-SY5Y cells (one coverslip/well), with the BV-2 cells facing the neurons in a bilaminar co-culture. Co-cultures were treated with SO EO (obtained from Sicily, Italy, by Herby’s Ltd., Pécs, Hungary), (+)-limonene (Merck Life Sciences Kft, Budapest, Hungary), or rasagiline for 24 h. Control cells were treated with the same amount of DMSO used in the diluted EO and (+)-limonene (less than 1%) in both mono- and co-cultures. Ferric ammonium citrate (FAC; 100 µM; Merck Life Sciences Kft, Budapest, Hungary) pretreatment (24 h) was also performed to simulate iron accumulation associated with PD. Viability measurements

For the viability measurements, SH-SY5Y cells were cultured on a 96-well plate at 104 cell density. Stock solutions of SO EO and (+)-limonene were emulsified by adding 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Merck Life Sciences Kft, Budapest, Hungary; 100%) to a mixture of 90% EO/(+)-limonene (90 µL of EO/(+)-limonene mixed with 10 µL of DMSO). The stock solutions were diluted in a complete cell growth medium to concentrations of 200x, 500x, 1000x, 2000x, and 5000x. Ten microliters of each dilution was added to 90 µL of growth medium to treat SH-SY5Y cells for 24 h. Therefore, the final dilutions were 2000x, 5000x, 10000x, 20000x, and 50000x. BV-2 cells were seeded in poly L-ornithine-coated 96-well plates at a density of 104 cells. The cells were then treated in the same manner as the SH-SY5Y cells.

Cell viability was determined using a TOX8 kit (Merck Life Sciences Kft, Budapest, Hungary) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 10 µL of TOX8 resazurin reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h. The optical density was measured using a MultiSkan GO spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) at a wavelength of 600 nm. The optical density (OD) of DMSO-treated cells (control) was considered 100%. The viability of the treated cells was compared to that of the control cells and expressed as a percentage of the control.

The optimal 6-OHDA concentration was determined by treating SH-SY5Y cells with 50, 100, 150, 200, and 300 µM 6-OHDA for 24 h. The optimal concentration of rasagiline was determined using a viability assay at rasagiline concentrations of 0.5, 1, 2, and 5 µM for 24 h. Iron accumulation in SH-SY5Y cells was induced by supplementation with FAC at 50, 100, 200, and 500 µM. The viability measurements of 6-OHDA-, rasagiline-, and FAC-treated differentiated SH-SY5Y cells are shown in Additional File 1.

Determination of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production

For the ROS measurements, SH-SY5Y cells were cultured on a 96-well plate at 104 cell density. The cells were then treated according to the experimental setup for monocultures described in Section "Cell cultures and treatments". For co-cultures, SH-SY5Y cells were seeded onto 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well. BV-2 cells were cultured on poly-L-ornithine-coated coverslips (13 mm; Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) for 24 h, and the coverslips were then added to the differentiated SH-SY5Y cells (one coverslip/well) at the end of the 6-OHDA or FAC + 6-OHDA treatments. The amount of ROS was determined in SH-SY5Y cells, both in mono- and co-cultures, using a Fluorometric Intracellular ROS Kit (Merck Life Technologies Kft, Budapest, Hungary). The assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After adding the ROS detection reagent, cells were incubated for 30 min in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Fluorescence intensity was measured at an excitation wavelength of 640 nm and an emission wavelength of 675 nm using an EnSpire Multimode microplate reader (PerkinElmer, Rodgau, Germany). The ROS level was compared to that of DMSO-treated control cells and expressed as a percentage of the control. ROS production in control cells was considered 100%.

Total antioxidant capacity (TAC) measurements

The total antioxidant capacity was determined using the Antioxidant Assay Kit (Merck Life Science Kft, Budapest, Hungary) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Differentiated SH-SY5Y cells were cultured and treated in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well (Biologix Europe, Hallbergmoos, Germany). SH-SY5Y cell pellets from mono- and co-cultures were washed once with ice-cold 1x phosphate-buffered saline (Capricorn Scientific GmbH, Ebsdorfergrund, Germany). The cell pellets were lysed in cold PBS, and 100 µL of each sample was used for the measurements. The optical density was measured at 570 nm using a MultiSkan GO spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Antioxidant concentrations were expressed as Trolox equivalents in micromoles (µM).

Glutathione peroxidase activity measurements

Glutathione peroxidase activity was determined using a Glutathione Peroxidase Assay Kit (Merck Life Science Kft, Budapest, Hungary) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, differentiated SH-SY5Y cells were cultured and treated in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well (Biologix Europe, Hallbergmoos, Germany), as described previously. SH-SY5Y cell pellets from mono- and co-cultures were washed once with ice-cold 1x PBS (Capricorn Scientific GmbH, Ebsdorfergrund, Germany). The cells were lysed in 200 µL of 1x PBS by sonication (2 × 1 min) and then centrifuged for 10 min at 14,000 × g. To measure the glutathione peroxidase activity, 10 µL of the supernatant was used from each sample. The OD was measured at 340 nm using a Multiskan GO spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). GPx activity was expressed as units per liter (U/L).

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity determinations

SOD activity was determined using a Superoxide Dismutase Assay Kit (Merck Life Science Kft, Budapest, Hungary), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, differentiated SH-SY5Y cells were cultured and treated in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well (Biologix Europe, Hallbergmoos, Germany). SH-SY5Y cell pellets from mono- and co-cultures were washed once with ice-cold 1x PBS (Capricorn Scientific GmbH, Ebsdorfergrund, Germany). The cells were lysed in 500 µL of 1x Lysis Buffer on ice for 10 min and collected by centrifugation for 5 min at 12,000 × g. To measure SOD activity, 20 µL of supernatant was used from each sample. The supernatants were mixed with 160 µL of the Working Reagent and 20 µL of diluted xanthine oxidase enzyme. The OD was measured at 440 nm using a Multiskan GO spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). SOD activity was calculated according to the manufacturer’s instructions and was expressed as U/mL.

Total intracellular iron content determinations

Intracellular iron content was determined using a colorimetric ferrozine-based assay. Differentiated SH-SY5Y cells were cultured and treated in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/well (Biologix Europe, Hallbergmoos, Germany), as previously described. SH-SY5Y cell pellets from mono- and co-cultures were washed once with ice-cold 1x PBS (Capricorn Scientific GmbH, Ebsdorfergrund, Germany). Cell pellets were lysed with 200 µL of 50 mM NaOH at room temperature for 2 h. After incubation, 100 µL of the samples was mixed with 100 µL of iron-releasing reagent (1.4 M HCl, 4.5% w/v) and incubated for 2 h at 60 °C. After the incubation, the samples were cooled to room temperature. Then, 30 µL of iron detection reagent (6.5 mM ferrozine, 6.5 mM neocuproine, 2.5 M ammonium acetate, and 1 M ascorbic acid) was added and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured in 96-well plates at 550 nm using a Multiskan GO spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). A standard curve of FeCl3 was used to determine its concentration, and it was treated in the same manner as the samples. For normalization of iron content, the protein concentration was determined for each sample using a DC Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad Inc., Hercules, CA, USA). The intracellular iron content was expressed as micromoles of iron per milligram of protein (µM/mg).

ATP measurements

The intracellular ATP concentrations of differentiated and differently treated mono- and co-cultured SH-SY5Y cells were measured using an ATP Determination Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Differentiated SH-SY5Y cells were cultured and treated in 6-well plates at 5 × 105 cells/well (Biologix Europe, Hallbergmoos, Germany). To determine the activity, the SH-SY5Y cell pellets were lysed in 1x Reaction Buffer. Next, 10 µL of lysate was added to 1x Reaction Buffer in a total volume of 100 µL. Luminescence was measured using an EnSpire Multimode microplate reader (PerkinElmer, Rodgau, Germany). The values are expressed in nM.

Cytochrome c assay

Cytochrome c levels were determined using a Human Cytochrome c ELISA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Differentiated SH-SY5Y cells were cultured and treated in 6-well plates at 5 × 105 cells/well (Biologix Europe, Hallbergmoos, Germany). The SH-SY5Y cell pellets were washed once with ice-cold 1x PBS (Capricorn Scientific GmbH, Ebsdorfergrund, Germany). The cells were lysed in the lysis buffer provided by the kit for 1 h at room temperature with gentle shaking and then centrifuged at 200 x g for 15 min. The supernatants were diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and 100 µL of each supernatant was used for the ELISA. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multiskan GO spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Concentrations were calculated using the SkanIt software (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The results were expressed in nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL).

Caspase-3 activity assay

The caspase-3 activity was determined using the Caspase-3 Assay Kit (Merck Life Science Kft, Budapest, Hungary) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Differentiated SH-SY5Y cells were cultured and treated in 6-well plates at 5 × 105 cells/well (Biologix Europe, Hallbergmoos, Germany). The cell pellets were washed once with ice-cold 1x PBS (Capricorn Scientific GmbH, Ebsdorfergrund, Germany). The cells were lysed in 300 µL of lysis buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.2, 100 mM NaCl, 0,5% (v/v) Triton X-100) with shaking for 30 min on ice. The cell lysates were centrifuged at 2500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. To measure the caspase-3 activity, 50 µL of each supernatant was used. Supernatants were incubated with the working solution for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. The fluorescent intensity (FI) was determined at λEx 400 nm/λEm 490 nm using an EnSpire Multimode Microplate Reader (PerkinElmer, Rodgau, Germany). FI was normalized to the absolute control (non-treated) values and expressed as ∆FI.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) measurements

After each treatment of SH-SY5Y cells and the co-cultures of SH-SY5Y and BV-2 cells, the culture media from the control and treated cultures were collected and stored at − 80 °C until measurements were taken. The fractalkine secretion of the differentiated SH-SY5Y cells was determined using the Human Fractalkine ELISA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). IL-6 and TNF-α production in differentiated SH-SY5Y cells was measured using Human IL-6 and TNF-α ELISA kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The IL-6 and TNF-α secretions of BV-2 cells in the co-cultures were determined using Mouse IL-6 and Mouse TNF-α ELISA Kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). All the ELISA kits were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The levels of secreted proteins were expressed as ng/mL.

Statistical analysis

The experiments were repeated three times, and five technical replicates were used for the viability and ROS measurements. There were three technical replicates for TAC, GPx, and SOD measurements, iron content determination, ATP, cytochrome c, caspase-3 activity, and cytokine ELISA measurements. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 24.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s post-hoc test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were considered statistically significant if the p-value was lower than 0.05.

Results

GC analysis of the sweet orange essential oil

A total of 32 components were detected in the tested essential oil based on three parallel measurements. The compounds and their corresponding literature and experimental LRI values, retention times, and sub-peak areas, calculated without correction and expressed as percentages, are presented in Table 1. The main component of SO EO is limonene, which accounts for nearly 91% of the essential oil. Chiral analysis confirmed the overwhelming predominance of the (+)-enantiomer and revealed an enantiomeric purity of 99.59%. The other two main components were myrcene (3.23%) and α-pinene (1.82%). Four compounds, found in smaller amounts, could not be precisely identified and were classified as unknown oxygenated monoterpenes. The results obtained are consistent with those reported in the literature [20, 21].

Discussion

Neurodegenerative diseases, such as Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and glaucoma, are becoming increasingly prevalent worldwide and are largely driven by global population aging and exacerbated by modern lifestyle-related risk factors, including environmental stressors and metabolic disorders [51].

The global incidence of Parkinson’s disease has doubled over the past 25 years, a trend that aligns with the findings of the Global Burden of Disease Study, which reported that between 1990 and 2015, the prevalence, disability, and mortality associated with the disease more than doubled. Projections estimate that the number of people living with Parkinson’s disease will reach 14.2 million by 2040 [52].

PD is characterized by the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons, resulting in the impairment of both motor and non-motor functions [35]. In the affected dopaminergic neurons, oxidative stress is triggered, leading to the peroxidation of proteins and lipids, as well as mitochondrial and DNA damage, which ultimately results in cell death [37, 38, 41].

As patients age, their medical conditions progressively deteriorate. Current pharmacological interventions primarily aim to alleviate symptoms or slow down disease progression [44]. The major treatments are the restoration of dopamine production, the inhibition of dopamine degradation, and the activation of dopaminergic receptors with agonists [46, 48]. Therefore, complementary therapies with minimal or no side effects, such as the use of natural compounds, are being promoted to improve the quality of life of patients. These therapies can slow the progression of PD, thereby reducing the need for patients to take allopathic drugs or undergo surgical management.

Microglia, which are immune cells of the central nervous system, play an important role in the development and progression of PD [53]. Microglia are activated by damage to dopaminergic neurons, phagocytose damaged neurons, produce inflammatory cytokines, and participate in α-synuclein clearance. However, increased microglial activation exacerbates neurodegeneration [54–56].

The role of essential oils in neurodegenerative diseases has been discussed in several studies [12, 57, 58]. Essential oils may offer potential therapeutic benefits in the management of PD, owing to their anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, which can mitigate the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress [59, 60].

Sweet orange essential oil (SO EO), as well as its main compounds (+)-limonene, myrcene, and α-pinene, also exhibit antioxidant properties [15, 17, 25], making them a promising therapy for neurodegenerative disorders. According to the literature, SO EO aromatherapy has been shown to reduce pain and anxiety associated with certain clinical conditions [61, 62]. SO EO has also been shown to reduce anxiety in PD patients [63].

In the present study, the antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, and anti-inflammatory effects of SO EO and (+)-limonene, the main compound, were investigated. Our in vitro models resemble oxidative stress in PD through the application of 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) to retinoic acid-differentiated SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells in monoculture, as well as differentiated SH-SY5Y cells and BV-2 microglia in a bilaminar co-culture. In the latter configuration, BV-2 cells on plastic slides were introduced into differentiated SH-SY5Y cells following a 24 h exposure to 6-OHDA. Additionally, we induced iron accumulation in PD neurons using ferric ammonium citrate (FAC) in combination with 6-OHDA in both mono- and co-cultures to assess whether SO EO and (+)-limonene mitigated iron-mediated oxidative stress.

Oxidative stress, mediated by the overproduction of free radicals, is one of the underlying causes of PD development [37]. The accumulation of these reactive molecules can cause severe damage to membranes, proteins, and DNA, leading to apoptosis [64].

According to these findings, both SO EO and (+)-limonene effectively mitigated oxidative stress by reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and enhancing total antioxidant capacity (TAC), which includes both small-molecule and enzyme-based antioxidants. In the monocultures, the two treatment agents exhibited no significant differences in ROS levels. However, in the presence of BV-2 microglia, (+)-limonene demonstrated significantly greater potency, even in the presence of FAC, than SO EO. Interestingly, in monocultures, FAC pretreatment alone reduced ROS levels. A possible explanation for this result is that both the differentiation of SH-SY5Y cells and the treatments were carried out in serum-depleted culture medium containing a low amount of available iron. Low iron availability may reduce intracellular iron content. The FAC treatment increased the iron content and may have restored the synthesis of iron- and heme-containing molecules. This process may contribute to elevated ATP levels and increased catalase activity, a heme-containing antioxidant enzyme that reduces ROS levels [65]. According to the results, GPx activity was elevated following FAC treatment, which could contribute to the reduced ROS levels.

The ROS-reducing activity by various essential oils and their main components has been proven. Rose essential oil demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction in ROS production in a 6-OHDA-induced Parkinson’s model of C. elegans [66]. β-caryophyllene was shown to mitigate oxidative stress in dopaminergic neurons within a rotenone-induced rat model of Parkinson’s disease [67]. Both linalool and geraniol have been validated as effective in diminishing ROS in in vitro neuronal damage models induced by rotenone and 6-OHDA, even in scenarios involving iron accumulation [68]. Additionally, Citrus japonica essential oil, with limonene as its principal compound, was effective in reducing oxidative stress in the paraquat rat model of Parkinson’s disease [69].

Regarding TAC, 6-OHDA treatment resulted in decreased TAC levels in differentiated SH-SY5Y cells across both culture types. In contrast, SO EO and (+)-limonene significantly increased TAC levels, with no discernible difference in the magnitude of their effects. Notably, the presence of microglia did not influence the TAC values. However, the presence of iron diminishes the efficacy of the essential oils. This may be due to the reduced direct antioxidant activity of SO EO in the presence of iron, which can accelerate oxidation [3].

Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) family and superoxide dismutase (SOD) family are antioxidant enzymes, that can protect the cells from oxidative damage [70]. GPx enzymes have peroxidase activity, catalyzing the reduction of hydrogen peroxide and organic hydroperoxides and inhibiting the peroxidation of macromolecules such as lipids, proteins, and DNA [71]. SOD enzymes also contribute to the antioxidant defense system by converting superoxide anions into hydrogen peroxide and oxygen [72]. In this aspect, SOD enzymes protect dopaminergic neurons from oxidative damage and contribute to neuroprotection [73].

In the case of GPx, both treatments (SO EO and (+)-limonene) significantly elevated the GPx levels. However, no significant difference was observed between the actions of SO EO and (+)-limonene, neither in mono- nor in co-cultures, and iron supplementation did not affect the GPx levels. Similar effects were observed in SOD activity measurements. These observations support the highly potent antioxidant effects of SO EO and the (+)-limonene main compound in 6-OHDA-induced differentiated SH-SY5Y cells. Similar to SO EO and (+)-limonene, β-caryophyllene significantly enhanced the activity of SOD and catalase, thereby augmenting the total antioxidant capacity of dopaminergic neurons in a rotenone-induced rat model [67]. Additionally, the main compounds linalool and geraniol were effective in increasing protection against oxidative stress by modulating small molecules and enzyme-related TAC [68].

Elevated levels of labile iron pool induce oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in cells. The reactive ferrous iron ions present in the labile iron pool facilitate the Fenton reaction, resulting in the production of hydroxyl radicals [74]. This iron-mediated oxidative stress contributes to lipid peroxidation and may initiate iron-dependent cell death, known as ferroptosis [38]. The iron-chelation potency of EOs can ameliorate iron accumulation in dopaminergic neurons in PD. Aloysia citrodora leaf EO (main compounds limonene, 1,8-cineole and geranial) has demonstrated dose-dependent iron-chelating capability [75]. It appears that iron supplementation in conjunction with 6-OHDA treatment led to increased iron accumulation in differentiated SH-SY5Y cells, both in mono- and co-cultures. However, in the absence of FAC pretreatment in co-cultured SH-SY5Y cells, elevated iron levels were observed, indicating that BV-2 microglia actively contribute to iron accumulation in neurons [38]. The intracellular iron content of differentiated SH-SY5Y cells was reduced solely by (+)-limonene, but not by SO EO, compared to that in 6-OHDA-induced cells. These findings suggest that other components of SO EO may interfere with the effects of (+)-limonene on iron metabolism. Ademosun et al. demonstrated the inhibitory effects of EOs derived from sweet orange peel and seeds on lipid peroxidation in vitro [76]. Our findings suggest that SO EO and (+)-limonene may inhibit iron-induced lipid peroxidation by enhancing the total antioxidant capacity (TAC) rather than altering the intracellular iron content.

Oxidative stress also affects mitochondrial functions, such as energy production and heme and iron-sulfur cluster synthesis. The reactive species target the mitochondrial membrane, decreasing ATP production and leading to the release of cytochrome c, which together are involved in the activation of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway [77, 78]. Treatment of differentiated SH-SY5Y cells with 6-OHDA significantly decreased ATP levels, but SO EO, as well as (+)-limonene, successfully compensated for and increased ATP synthesis in both mono- and co-cultures. It should be noted that the ATP levels were lower in the co-cultured differentiated SH-SY5Y cells compared to the monocultures, suggesting that the presence of microglia also influences the energy production of the neuron via glutamate and neuron–astrocyte–microglia interaction [79, 80]. Similar to SO EO and (+)-limonene, geraniol increased the ATP levels of 6-OHDA-treated differentiated SH-SY5Y cells [68].

To elucidate the apoptotic inhibitory effects of SO EO and (+)-limonene, the levels of cytochrome c and the activity of the caspase-3 effector caspase were assessed. Both SO EO and (+)-limonene significantly reduced intracellular cytochrome c levels, with (+)-limonene demonstrating greater efficacy than SO EO. These findings indicate that the enhanced antioxidant protection of SH-SY5Y cells facilitated by SO EO and (+)-limonene attenuates mitochondrial damage and inhibits the release of cytochrome c. However, SO EO treatments were more effective in lowering caspase-3 activity than (+)-limonene in both monocultures and FAC-pretreated co-cultures, suggesting that SO EO may also modulate other apoptosis-related pathways. The neuroprotective effects of limonene have been observed by Eddin et al. in a rotenone-induced rat model [81].

Microglia, the resident immune cells in the brain and retina, actively contribute to the development and progression of PD through the release of inflammatory factors such as IL-6, IL-1, and TNF-α, which are neurotoxic at a certain level [82]. Neuroinflammation mediated by glial cells is related to oxidative stress occurring in PD and even glaucoma [83, 84]. Therefore, the cytokine-dependent neurotoxicity and oxidative damage are mainly responsible for the neurodegeneration of the dopaminergic neurons in PD [85]. Inhibition of TNF-α secretion can prevent caspase-8 and caspase-3 activation and enhance apoptosis [86].

Microglial activity is modulated by fractalkine/CX3CR1 signalling. Membrane-bound fractalkine, a unique chemokine, interacts with its receptor CX3CR1, which is expressed by microglia, thereby maintaining the resting state of these cells [87]. In PD, membrane-bound fractalkine is released from dopaminergic neurons, forming a soluble variant that acts as a modulator of microglia, contributing to their excessive activation and subsequent neurodegeneration [88]. Elevated levels of soluble fractalkine affect the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in microglia [89]. Nonetheless, it is hypothesized that in the early stages of the disease, soluble fractalkine confers neuroprotection by activating microglia [90].

In our model, the fractalkine levels were significantly increased after 6-OHDA treatment. Simultaneously, SO EO and (+)-limonene significantly reduced the secretion of soluble fractalkine by differentiated SH-SY5Y cells in both mono- and co-cultures. However, (+)-limonene decreased fractalkine levels more than SO EO did. Interestingly, in the presence of microglia, fractalkine levels were lower than those in the monocultures of differentiated SH-SY5Y cells, and iron supplementation enhanced the effect of (+)-limonene on fractalkine release. In a separate study, geraniol was found to effectively reduce fractalkine secretion in SH-SY5Y cells treated with 6-OHDA [68], although its efficacy appears to be less pronounced compared to SO EO and (+)-limonene. Concurrently, limonin was observed to attenuate microglial activation in rats injected with 6-OHDA, albeit with a lower potency than limonene [91].

SO EO and (+)-limonene were less effective in decreasing IL-6 pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion from differentiated SH-SY5Y cells, but still significantly reduced IL-6 release compared to 6-OHDA treatment. SO EO and its main compound were much more effective in reducing the levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α, with (+)-limonene mostly diminishing its release below the detection limit. These observations support the anti-inflammatory effects of SO EO and (+)-limonene on differentiated SH-SY5Y cells in our neurodegenerative model.

The SH-SY5Y/BV-2 co-cultures serve as effective models for investigating neuroinflammatory processes [92, 93]. These co-cultures allow for the distinct observation of pro-inflammatory cytokine release from microglia, separate from SH-SY5Y cells, owing to the murine origin of the BV-2 cells. Furthermore, they can respond to each other’s stimuli [94, 95]. Consequently, the secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α, pro-inflammatory cytokines, by BV-2 cells was also determined in the co-cultures. Notably, SO EO and (+)-limonene demonstrated significant efficacy in reducing the secretion of both pro-inflammatory cytokines, even in the presence of iron. Furthermore, the main compound, (+)-limonene, exhibited a higher anti-inflammatory potency. Research has demonstrated that limonene effectively mitigates neuroinflammation in a rotenone-induced model of Parkinson’s disease [81]. Similarly, the anti-inflammatory properties of linalool and geraniol have been documented in SH-SY5Y cells treated with 6-OHDA [68]. Furthermore, treatment with β-caryophyllene has been shown to reduce microglial activation and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines in a rotenone-induced rat model of Parkinson’s disease [67].

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic properties of SO EO and its main compound, (+)-limonene. Upon comparing their activities, it was found that (+)-limonene exhibited greater efficacy than SO EO. However, it should be noted that the amount of essential oil used contained 9.79% less (+)-limonene main compound, compared to the (+)-limonene treatment. Considering this, (+)-limonene was shown to be more potent than the positive control rasagiline, an MAO-B inhibitor, in enhancing the total antioxidant capacity and ATP levels, inhibiting apoptosis, and reducing the production of inflammatory factors in differentiated SH-SY5Y cells. Furthermore, SO EO and (+)-limonene attenuated inflammation mediated by BV-2 cells in co-cultures, indicating the potential application of these agents as modulators of microglial activity.

Although our 6-OHDA-induced neurodegeneration models effectively represent the oxidative stress and iron accumulation observed in PD, which contribute to ROS production, oxidative damage, and inflammation, they do not encompass the complexity of PD. Nevertheless, it can be concluded that SO EO and its major compound, (+)-limonene, are promising candidates for anti-Parkinson’s treatment. Further experimental validation is required following the nanotechnological formulation of these agents to establish their in vivo applicability.
