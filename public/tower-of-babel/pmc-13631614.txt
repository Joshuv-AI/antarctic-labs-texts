Biomimetic nanoparticles camouflaged with M2 macrophage membrane for osteoporosis therapy: A triple-action strategy that upregulates anti-inflammation and osteogenic responses while suppressing MMP-9
Authors: Chengbai Dai, Cheng Chen, Jun Zhu, Minghao Chao, Hanrong Yan, Pengjin Wei, Ming Guan, Bin Pan, Fenglei Gao, Rongze Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteoporosis is a systemic skeletal disorder characterized by bone loss and microstructural deterioration, and its pathogenesis is closely associated with dysregulation of the bone marrow immune microenvironment. In this study, bioinformatic analysis first revealed significant oxidative stress and mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction in macrophages within the osteoporotic microenvironment. Based on this, we constructed a biomimetic nanoplatform (ZCM2) composed of ZIF-8@CaCO3 core–shell nanoparticles camouflaged with M2 macrophage membranes, designed to achieve comprehensive osteoporosis therapy through a triple synergistic mechanism: the ZIF-8 core scavenges reactive oxygen species to exert antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, inhibits MMP-9 secretion to reduce pathological bone resorption, and the CaCO3 shell releases calcium ions to promote osteogenic differentiation. ZCM2 exhibited favorable biosafety, high cellular uptake efficiency, and excellent bone-targeting capability. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that ZCM2 upregulates the expression of Nrf2 and HO-1, downregulates the expression of NF-κB p65, and significantly reduces the secretion of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) by macrophages, thereby attenuating bone resorption. Meanwhile, calcium ions released from the CaCO3 shell activate the RUNX2/OSX/OPN signaling axis, promoting osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells. In an ovariectomized mouse model of osteoporosis, ZCM2 markedly improved bone microarchitecture, promoted new bone formation, and effectively inhibited bone resorption activity. Collectively, this biomimetic nanoplatform integrates anti-inflammatory, anti-resorptive, and pro-osteogenic functions, offering a multifaceted synergistic therapeutic strategy for osteoporosis.Keywords: Biomimetic nanoparticles, Osteoporosis, Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, Calcium delivery

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a systemic skeletal disorder characterized by reduced bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture [1], which significantly increases the risk of fragility fractures [2]. Its development is closely associated with aging, hormonal alterations, and metabolic imbalance [3], making it a major health challenge in aging societies [4]. However, currently available anti-osteoporotic therapies have limited ability to correct the dysregulated bone marrow immune microenvironment, particularly the pathological activation of macrophages [5]. The ovariectomized (OVX) mouse model was used to recapitulate estrogen deficiency-associated postmenopausal osteoporosis, providing a clinically relevant setting to investigate the potential contribution of senescence-associated macrophage dysfunction to bone loss.

Macrophages contribute to skeletal homeostasis [6], while aging progressively induces their dysfunction and senescence [7]. In the osteoporotic microenvironment, these dysfunctional macrophages are exposed to persistent oxidative stress, accompanied by significant alterations in pentose phosphate pathway metabolism [8]. This metabolic reprogramming is closely associated with impaired cellular antioxidant capacity [9]. Under oxidative stress, excessive reactive oxygen species modify critical cysteine residues of Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1), resulting in conformational changes and dissociation of the Keap1–nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) complex [10]. The released Nrf2 becomes stabilized and translocates into the nucleus, where it forms heterodimers with Maf proteins [10] and binds to antioxidant response elements (AREs) [11], thereby initiating the transcription of downstream antioxidant genes [12]. Among them, heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) is a key antioxidant enzyme regulated by Nrf2 that catalyzes the degradation of heme [13]. The resulting metabolites can directly scavenge free radicals [14], suppress lipid peroxidation [15], and exert potent anti-inflammatory effects through the bilirubin system [16]. Notably, HO-1-derived metabolites can effectively inhibit the nuclear translocation of nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), a central transcription factor governing inflammatory responses [17]. Under oxidative stress stimulation, IκB undergoes phosphorylation-dependent degradation [18], releasing the p50/p65 heterodimer that translocates into the nucleus and triggers the transcription of numerous pro-inflammatory mediators [19] and matrix-degrading enzymes [20]. Among these, matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), a major proteolytic enzyme secreted by activated macrophages, plays a critical role in collagen degradation and bone resorption [21]. Our previous transcriptomic analysis further revealed that redox-regulation-related genes are globally downregulated in aged macrophages, suggesting impaired antioxidant defense [22]. Therefore, activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant pathway, thereby suppressing NF-κB signaling and reducing MMP-9 secretion, represents a promising strategy to restore bone immune homeostasis and prevent pathological bone resorption.

In addition to excessive bone resorption, impaired osteogenesis is another key contributor to the progression of osteoporosis [23]. Insufficient calcium ions within the bone marrow microenvironment are considered an important factor limiting osteogenic differentiation [24]. Beyond serving as the essential mineral component for bone deposition, calcium ions also function as critical signaling molecules that regulate the osteogenic fate of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) [25]. The influx of extracellular Ca2+ activates calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) [26], thereby initiating downstream osteogenic signaling cascades. The master transcription factor runt-related transcription factor 2 (RUNX2) plays a central role in osteogenic differentiation [27]. Upon activation, RUNX2 translocates into the nucleus and binds to RUNX2-responsive elements within the promoters of osteogenic genes [28]. For example, the promoter region of osteopontin (OPN) contains specific RUNX2 binding sites, and RUNX2 binding markedly enhances OPN transcriptional activity. OPN, a major non-collagenous protein of the bone matrix, participates in regulating matrix mineralization and cell adhesion [29]. Meanwhile, RUNX2 also induces the expression of osterix (OSX) [30], another key downstream osteogenic transcription factor that further activates the transcription of osteogenic genes such as type I collagen and osteocalcin [31], collectively driving MSCs toward osteogenic lineage commitment [32]. These findings indicate that calcium ions not only provide the material basis for bone mineralization but also act as instructive signals that promote osteogenic differentiation through activation of the RUNX2/OSX/OPN signaling axis [33]. Therefore, the introduction of calcium ions into therapeutic strategies may simultaneously suppress bone resorption while stimulating bone formation, thereby achieving bidirectional regulation of bone metabolism.

Despite these advances, effectively integrating antioxidant therapy with calcium ion delivery while overcoming the limitations of conventional drugs—such as short circulation time [34], poor bone targeting [35], and rapid immune clearance [36]-remains a critical challenge for translational application. In recent years, cell membrane–based biomimetic nanotechnology has emerged as a promising strategy for efficient drug delivery [37]. By coating synthetic nanocores with natural cell membranes, nanoparticles can inherit the biological functions of their source cells [22]. Among various membrane sources, M2 macrophage membranes have attracted considerable attention due to their unique advantages. First, membrane proteins such as CD47 provide “self” signals that enable nanoparticles to evade recognition and clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system [38], thereby prolonging systemic circulation. Second, adhesion molecules and chemokine receptors retained on the membrane confer intrinsic targeting capability toward inflammatory microenvironments [39], facilitating efficient accumulation in diseased bone tissue. Third, M2 macrophage membranes intrinsically possess anti-inflammatory phenotypic characteristics and can modulate immune responses through interactions with immune cells [40]. Recent studies have demonstrated that M2 macrophage membrane–coated biomimetic nanoplatforms exhibit excellent immune evasion capability [41], enhanced tissue retention, and improved therapeutic efficacy in inflammatory diseases such as arthritis [42]. Therefore, integrating functional nanocores with M2 macrophage membrane coating may provide a multifunctional platform capable of simultaneously delivering antioxidant therapy, calcium ions, and immunomodulatory effects for the treatment of osteoporosis.

Based on this rationale, we designed and constructed an M2 macrophage membrane–biomimetic nanoparticle system (ZCM2), consisting of a ZIF-8 metal–organic framework core coated with a calcium carbonate shell. This nanoplatform is intended to exert therapeutic effects through three complementary mechanisms: suppressing inflammation and oxidative stress, inhibiting MMP-9 secretion to reduce pathological bone resorption, and promoting osteogenic differentiation. Through these integrated functions, ZCM2 provides a promising strategy for the comprehensive treatment of osteoporosis.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

2-methylimidazole, Zn(NO3)2·6H2O, polyacrylic acid, CaCl2 and Na2CO3 were all purchased from Aladdin Reagent, DMEM culture medium and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Gibco (Shanghai, China). CCK-8 assay kit and dexamethasone were procured from TargetMol (USA). 2,7-dichloro-fluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). Live/Dead Cell Double Staining Kit, JC-1 Apoptosis Detection Kit, and Apoptosis Detection Kit were purchased from Key-GEN (Nanjing, China), and other reagents are reagents grade and purchased from Beijing chemical factors. All of reagents were used as received without further purification.

Cell macrophage membrane were collected using Optima XPN-100 centrifugation. The morphology and structure of ZCM2 were characterized using transmission electron microscopy imaging (FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit Twin, Holland). Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) and elemental mapping images were obtained to the elemental composition. The crystal structure of the sample was determined by X-ray diffractometer (Bruker D8 Advance) with Cu Kα radiation. UV-vis absorption spectra of different samples were recorded by an Evolution 220 UV-Visible Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All fluorescence measurements were performed on a Hitachi FL4600 fluorescence spectrophotometer (Kyoto, Japan). Fluorescent images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica STELLARlS 5, Germany). DLS and Zeta potential analysis were performed at room temperature on a Zetasizer (Nano-Z, 60 Malvern, UK). Flow cytometric analysis was performed on a FACS Calibur flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson, USA). CCK-8 assay was performed using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 450 nm. In vivo fluorescence imaging was recorded on LB983 Night OWL II* Imaging System. (Berthold, Germany). The photothermal images were recorded by a FLUKE infrared (IR) thermal camera. The 660 nm laser was emitted from an LWIRL660-10W-F fiber-coupled laser.

D-galactose-induced senescent macrophage model

RAW264.7 murine macrophages were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Upon reaching 70%-80% confluence, the cells were washed with PBS and subsequently incubated with fresh medium containing 100 mM D-galactose for 48 h to establish the cellular senescence model.

Synthesis of ZCM2 nanoparticles

ZCM2 nanoparticles were synthesized via a three-step procedure. First, ZIF-8 nanoparticles were prepared by slowly dropping an ethanolic solution of 2-methylimidazole (660 mg in 15 mL ethanol) into another ethanolic solution of Zn(NO3)2·6H2O (297.49 mg in 15 mL ethanol) under stirring at 600 rpm for 1 h at room temperature under a nitrogen flow. The product was collected by centrifugation (6000 rpm, 15 min) and washed three times with ethanol.

Subsequently, the obtained ZIF-8 (50 mg) was redispersed in an aqueous polyacrylic acid (PAA) solution (0.5 mg/mL). Under stirring, CaCl2 solution and then Na2CO3 solution were added dropwise. After 30 min of reaction, the product, denoted as ZC (ZIF-8@CaCO3), was collected by centrifugation (6000 rpm, 15 min) and washed with water.

Finally, RAW264.7 cells were polarized to an M2 phenotype by stimulation with 40 ng/mL IL-4 for 24 h prior to membrane extraction. M2 macrophage membranes were harvested using an ultrasonication method. The ZC nanoparticles were then slowly added to the M2 membrane solution and stirred overnight at 600 rpm to obtain the final ZCM2.

Assessment of H2O2 scavenging capacity (total antioxidant activity) via a two-step DTNB assay

The assay was performed using a two-step sequential protocol. Test materials (ZIF-8, ZC, or ZCM2) were incubated with 0.5 mL of H2O2 (10 mM) in 0.5 mL of Na2HPO4-citric acid buffer (pH 5.0) at 37°C for 30 min in the dark. Then, 2 mL of GSH (5 mM) was added and the mixture was incubated for another 10 min. Subsequently, DTNB (40 μM) was introduced and absorbance at 412 nm was recorded. The remaining GSH level, inversely related to the residual H2O2, reflects the H2O2-scavenging capacity of the materials.

Cell viability assay

BMSCs and RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well. After adhesion, cells were treated with varying concentrations (10-100 μg/mL) of ZCM2 for 12 h. Subsequently, 10% CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37°C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader to assess cell viability.

Live/dead cell staining

BMSCs and RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates (1 × 106 cells/well) and treated with different concentrations of ZCM2 for 12 h. Cells were then stained with Calcein-AM/PI working solution for 30 min at 37°C in the dark. After washing with PBS, fluorescent images were immediately captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope, with live cells exhibiting green fluorescence and dead cells showing red fluorescence.

Cellular uptake study

ZCM2 was labeled with FITC by stirring the mixture in the dark for 24 h. RAW264.7 cells were cultured in confocal dishes and incubated with FITC-labeled ZCM2 for various time points. At each endpoint, cells were washed with cold PBS, fixed, and stained with DAPI for 15 min. Intracellular fluorescence was visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica STELLARIS 5, Germany). For quantitative analysis, parallel samples were trypsinized, filtered, and analyzed by flow cytometry to determine the FITC-positive rate and mean fluorescence intensity.

Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well. After attachment, cells were stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) to mimic an inflammatory environment and treated with different nanomaterials for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were stained with JC-1 dye for 30 min at 37°C in the dark. Changes in mitochondrial membrane potential were then analyzed using a confocal microscope.

Micro-CT scanning and analysis

Starting from the first month post-modeling, the femurs of mice were scanned every two weeks using a micro-CT scanner for small animals. The scanning parameters were set as follows: voltage of 70 kV and an exposure time of 300 ms. Acquired raw data were imported into 3Dslicer 5.2.1 software for three-dimensional reconstruction and subsequent quantitative analysis of bone microarchitecture parameters.

Ex vivo fluorescence imaging

ZIF-8, ZC, and ZCM2 were labeled with the Cy3 fluorescent dye. At 0, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h before euthanasia, mice were intravenously injected with 100 μL of Cy3-labeled nanoparticle solution (70 μg/mL) via the tail vein. At the predetermined time points, bilateral femurs and major organs were collected. Cy3 fluorescence signals were detected using an in vivo imaging system to analyze the biodistribution of the nanoparticles.

Statistical analysis

All quantitative findings are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical evaluations were performed using Graphpad Prism 8.0. For comparisons involving multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post-test was utilized unless noted otherwise. The comparison of two groups was executed using a two-tailed paired Student's t-test. Significance levels were indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Senescent macrophages exhibit oxidative stress and metabolic reprogramming of the pentose phosphate pathway

To investigate the immune microenvironment alterations associated with age-related osteoporosis, a senescent RAW264.7 macrophage model was established using D-galactose treatment, followed by RNA-seq analysis to characterize transcriptomic changes. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation between senescent and control groups in principal component space (Fig. 1A, S1), indicating successful induction of senescence-related transcriptional remodeling by D-galactose. Differential expression analysis (|log2 FC| > 1, adjusted P < 0.05) identified numerous significantly altered genes, with pronounced changes in genes related to redox regulation and metabolism (e.g., Nnt, Ptp4a1, Commd1b) (Fig. 1B), suggesting perturbations in intracellular redox homeostasis and metabolic status. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Fig. 1C) showed that differentially expressed genes were primarily enriched in biological processes such as signal transduction, cell differentiation, and transmembrane transport. Additionally, terms related to transcriptional regulation, G protein-coupled receptor signaling pathways, and response to xenobiotic stimulus were significantly enriched, indicating extensive remodeling of macrophage functions in signal perception, gene expression regulation, and immune responses upon senescence induction.

Transcriptomic validation of senescent macrophages. A) Principal component analysis (PCA) of control and senescent macrophages; B) Volcano plot showing differentially expressed genes between control and senescent cells; C) GO enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes; D) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes; E) GSEA enrichment plot of the pentose phosphate pathway; F) GSEA enrichment plot of genes associated with proton motive force driven mitochondrial ATP synthesis.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis further mapped the differentially expressed genes to specific functional pathways (Fig. 1D), revealing significant enrichment in immune-related pathways (e.g., cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction) and metabolic pathways (e.g., pentose phosphate pathway) (Fig. 1E). Meanwhile, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis showed an overall downregulation of genes associated with oxidoreductase activity (Fig. 1C), indicating impaired redox regulatory capacity in senescent macrophages. Further gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed significant enrichment changes in genes associated with proton motive force-driven mitochondrial ATP synthesis (Fig. 1F–S2), suggesting disruption of mitochondrial energy metabolism and ATP production.

Collectively, these results indicate that senescence-induced dysregulation of redox function may serve as a critical link connecting metabolic reprogramming and immune functional alterations in macrophages. This transcriptomic profiling provides a foundational rationale for understanding immune microenvironment disturbances in age-related osteoporosis and supports the development of nanotherapeutics targeting redox regulation.

Synthesis and characterization of ZCM2 nanomaterials

As illustrated in Scheme 1, ZIF-8 nanocores with antioxidant activity were first synthesized via an aqueous-phase method at room temperature. Subsequently, a dense calcium carbonate (CaCO3) shell was coated onto the ZIF-8 surface through polyacrylic acid (PAA)-mediated in situ mineralization to enhance stability and provide a calcium source essential for bone regeneration. Finally, interleukin-4 (IL-4)-induced M2 macrophage membranes (LipaM2) were electrostatically adsorbed onto the surface of ZC to obtain ZCM2 nanoparticles.

M2 macrophage membrane–biomimetic nanoparticles (ZCM2) for osteoporosis therapy. Schematic illustration of the synthesis of ZCM2. ZCM2 activates the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant pathway in macrophages, thereby inhibiting NF-κB activation and MMP-9 secretion, which suppresses pathological bone resorption. Meanwhile, ZCM2 stimulates the RUNX2/OSX/OPN signaling cascade in mesenchymal stem cells to promote osteogenic differentiation. Collectively, ZCM2 achieves synergistic anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, and pro-osteogenic regulation for osteoporosis treatment.

The morphology of the intermediates and final product was characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Fig. 2A and B). Pristine ZIF-8 exhibited a typical rhombohedral dodecahedral structure with uniform size and well-defined edges. After CaCO3 coating, ZC particles displayed a rougher surface and a morphological transition from dodecahedral to irregular polyhedral shapes with increased edge contrast. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis revealed that, in addition to the characteristic Zn 2p peaks of ZIF-8, ZC showed distinct Ca 2p and C–O peaks (Fig. 2C and D), confirming the formation of the CaCO3 shell. TEM imaging and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping of ZCM2 (Fig. 2E) revealed a well-defined core–shell structure: a high-contrast ZC core surrounded by a continuous low-density amorphous halo. Elemental mapping demonstrated that Zn and Ca were predominantly localized in the core, C was broadly distributed, and P (a membrane-specific element) was enriched in the halo region, indicating successful coating of M2 membranes onto ZC. To further verify the presence and preservation of M2 macrophage membrane components on ZCM2, Western blot analysis was performed using isolated M2 macrophage membranes and ZCM2. The membrane-associated protein Na+/K+-ATPase, together with macrophage-related proteins CD68, CD14, and Cav-1 and the M2 phenotype-associated protein Arg-1, was detected in both the isolated M2 macrophage membranes and ZCM2. These findings further confirmed that M2 macrophage membrane proteins were successfully retained on ZCM2, providing additional evidence for the successful membrane coating of ZC (Fig. 2F).

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements (Fig. 2G) showed that the hydrodynamic diameter of ZIF-8 was 77.47 ± 2.57 nm. After CaCO3 coating, it increased to 86.03 ± 2.39 nm, corresponding to a shell thickness of approximately 8.5 nm. The final ZCM2 nanoparticles exhibited a hydrodynamic diameter of 97.63 ± 2.30 nm, with an M2 membrane layer thickness of about 11.6 nm. Zeta potential analysis (Fig. 2G) revealed a gradual shift in surface charge: from +26.79 ± 1.50 mV for ZIF-8 to +7.93 ± 0.48 mV after CaCO3 deposition, and further to −20.39 ± 1.15 mV upon M2 membrane coating, confirming the stepwise modification of surface properties.

Antioxidant and ROS scavenging capacity of ZCM2

The H2O2-scavenging capacity of the nanomaterials was evaluated using a two-step sequential DTNB assay based on the residual GSH level. Following pre-incubation with H2O2, the addition of GSH resulted in a marked decrease in the characteristic DTNB-derived absorbance at 412 nm in the H2O2-only group (Fig. 2H), indicating substantial H2O2-induced GSH consumption. In contrast, pre-treatment with ZIF-8, ZC, or ZCM2 significantly increased the residual GSH level compared with H2O2 alone, indicating that these nanomaterials reduced H2O2-mediated GSH depletion by scavenging H2O2. Among them, ZCM2 exhibited the highest H2O2-scavenging capacity. The peroxidase-like (POD-like) activity of the nanomaterials was assessed using the TMB colorimetric method. In Fig. 2I, the absorbance at 652 nm was low in the H2O2-only group, while it increased markedly upon addition of the nanomaterials, confirming their intrinsic POD-like activity. Among them, ZCM2 displayed the strongest catalytic capability. The regulatory effect on oxidative stress was further evaluated using methylene blue (MB) as an indicator of hydroxyl radical (•OH) levels. As shown in Fig. 2J, the degradation of MB induced by H2O2 was significantly retarded in the presence of ZIF-8, ZC, or ZCM2, demonstrating their ability to scavenge •OH. Notably, ZCM2 exhibited the most potent antioxidant activity, highlighting its potential to mitigate oxidative stress.

Biosafety evaluation and cellular uptake of ZCM2

To evaluate the biosafety of ZCM2 and determine the appropriate dosage for subsequent experiments, cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay in RAW264.7 macrophages and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) (Fig. S4), two representative cell types in the bone marrow microenvironment. As shown in Fig. 3A, ZCM2 not only exhibited no cytotoxicity but also promoted the proliferation of both cell types at concentrations of 10–20 μg/mL. Cell viability remained above 90% at concentrations below 40 μg/mL. However, at 70 μg/mL, BMSC viability decreased to 44.8%, and at 90 μg/mL, RAW264.7 cell viability was 50.85%. The higher sensitivity of BMSCs to ZCM2 may be attributed to the natural macrophage compatibility conferred by the macrophage membrane coating. Live/dead staining further confirmed these findings (Fig. 3B): only a few BMSC deaths were observed at 30 μg/mL, the viability was 57.76% at 60 μg/mL (Fig. 3C), consistent with the CCK-8 results, and substantial cell death occurred at 90 μg/mL. Considering the complexity of the bone marrow microenvironment, a concentration of 20 μg/mL was selected for subsequent experiments.

To investigate the cellular uptake behavior of ZCM2, FITC-labeled ZCM2 was incubated with BMSCs, and uptake was observed by fluorescence microscopy at different time points (0, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min) (Fig. 3D). ZCM2 rapidly entered the cells within a short time, and the intracellular fluorescence intensity gradually increased over time, peaking at 120 min. Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed this trend (Fig. 3E), showing a time-dependent increase in mean fluorescence intensity. These results demonstrate that ZCM2 can be efficiently internalized by BMSCs, indicating excellent cellular uptake efficiency.

ZCM2 attenuates oxidative stress and inflammation in macrophages via activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway

An oxidative stress model was established in RAW264.7 macrophages by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation, and the protective effects of ZC and ZCM2 were evaluated. JC-1 staining revealed a marked loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (increased green fluorescence) in LPS-treated cells, which was partially restored by ZC and more prominently by ZCM2 co-treatment (Fig. 4A).

Immunofluorescence analysis showed that LPS treatment markedly decreased cellular Nrf2 fluorescence while substantially increasing NF-κB fluorescence in the nucleus (Fig. 4B). In contrast, co-treatment with ZC or ZCM2 significantly inhibited LPS-induced NF-κB nuclear translocation and restored Nrf2 fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4B). Further analysis by nuclear–cytoplasmic fractionation followed by Western blotting showed that LPS treatment significantly increased the nuclear level of NF-κB, accompanied by an elevated cytoplasmic p-IκBα/IκBα ratio (Fig. 4C–E), indicating activation of NF-κB signaling.

Under ZC or ZCM2 treatment, nuclear Nrf2 levels were significantly higher and its downstream antioxidant enzyme HO-1 in the cytoplasm was markedly upregulated (Fig. 4C and D), with ZCM2 exhibiting the strongest effect. Next, cells were pretreated with the Nrf2-specific inhibitor ML385 and HO-1 levels were assessed by Western blot (Fig. S5). The results indicated that inhibition of Nrf2 significantly attenuated the ZCM2-induced upregulation of HO-1. Together, these findings suggest that ZCM2 alleviates LPS-induced oxidative stress and inflammatory responses by upregulating the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway and inhibiting NF-κB nuclear translocation.

ZCM2 attenuates bone loss by inhibiting MMP-9 secretion

Transcriptomic analysis revealed significant alterations in inflammatory responses and extracellular matrix remodeling in senescent macrophages. Combined with our previous findings on the anti-inflammatory effects of ZCM2, we hypothesized that ZCM2 may influence bone metabolism by regulating matrix-degrading enzymes secreted by macrophages. As a major matrix-degrading enzyme secreted by activated macrophages, MMP-9 plays a critical role in bone collagen degradation. Immunofluorescence staining showed that LPS stimulation markedly upregulated MMP-9 fluorescence signal in RAW264.7 cells, which was effectively suppressed by ZCM2 intervention (Fig. 5A). Flow cytometry further confirmed these results, demonstrating that both the proportion of MMP-9-positive cells and the mean fluorescence intensity were significantly lower in the ZCM2-treated group compared to the LPS model group (Fig. 5B). These findings indicate that ZCM2 effectively inhibits MMP-9 secretion from activated macrophages, which may represent one of the mechanisms underlying its protective effect against osteoporotic bone resorption.

To further identify the major cellular source of MMP-9, we measured MMP-9 levels in the culture supernatants of LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells and BMSCs by ELISA. LPS stimulation induced MMP-9 secretion in both cell types, but RAW264.7 cells released substantially higher levels of MMP-9 than BMSCs (Fig. S6). These findings indicate that macrophages are a major source of MMP-9 under inflammatory conditions, supporting our observation that ZCM2 suppresses macrophage-associated MMP-9 production.

ZCM2 promotes osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs by providing calcium ions

The pathogenesis of osteoporosis involves not only accelerated bone matrix resorption but also stem cell senescence and impaired osteogenesis due to calcium deficiency in the bone marrow microenvironment. Calcium ions serve not only as essential trace elements for osteogenic differentiation but also as direct inducers of osteogenic commitment. This study investigated the calcium release properties of different nanoparticles and their effects on osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs. The calcium ion release profiles of ZCM2 were evaluated in PBS at pH 5.5, 6.5, and 7.4 using a calcium colorimetric assay, and the results are shown in Fig. S2. At pH 5.5, Ca2+ release was rapid, with a marked increase within the first 6 h followed by a plateau. At pH 6.5, the release rate was slower than at pH 5.5 but remained considerably faster than under neutral conditions. In contrast, at physiological pH 7.4, Ca2+ release was minimal and reached a plateau after 8 h, with cumulative release significantly lower than that observed under acidic conditions.

Western blot analysis revealed that while ZIF-8 treatment showed a trend toward increased expression of osteogenic markers OPN and OSX, the changes were not statistically significant. In contrast, ZC and ZCM2, which contain a calcium carbonate layer, significantly upregulated OPN and OSX expression, with ZCM2 showing the most pronounced effect (approximately 4-fold increase compared to the control group) (Fig. 5C and D). Concurrently, expression of the key osteogenic transcription factor RUNX2 was markedly enhanced in ZC and ZCM2 groups. Immunofluorescence staining corroborated these findings: control BMSCs exhibited low OPN and RUNX2 expression, while ZC and particularly ZCM2 treatment resulted in markedly enhanced cytoplasmic OPN green fluorescence and nuclear RUNX2 purple fluorescence accumulation (Fig. 5E). These results demonstrate that ZCM2 effectively promotes osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs through calcium ion delivery.

In vivo biosafety and bone-targeting evaluation of ZCM2

To evaluate the biosafety of ZCM2 and determine the appropriate dosage for subsequent in vivo experiments, an in vitro hemolysis assay was performed (Fig. 6B,S7). Hemolysis rates remained below 5% at concentrations below 100 μg/mL, but increased markedly at 80, 90, and 100 μg/mL compared to lower concentrations. Based on these results, a dose of 70 μg/mL was selected for tail vein injection, as illustrated in Fig. 6A.

After 4 weeks of treatment, mice were euthanized and major metabolic organs were collected for histopathological analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed no significant morphological abnormalities in the liver, kidneys, spleen, lungs, or heart, indicating favorable in vivo biocompatibility of ZCM2 (Fig. 6C). Serum biochemical analysis showed that liver function markers (ALT, AST) and kidney function markers (BUN, CR) were all within normal ranges, further confirming the absence of hepatorenal toxicity (Fig. 6D). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ZCM2 exhibits good biosafety in vivo.

To assess the bone-targeting capability of ZCM2, Cy3-labeled ZIF-8, ZC, and ZCM2 were administered via tail vein injection. Mice were euthanized at 0, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post-injection, and femurs were collected for in vivo imaging analysis (Fig. 6E). A time-dependent increase in fluorescence intensity was observed in all groups. ZIF-8 showed relatively slow accumulation and a low peak intensity in the bone marrow cavity. ZC exhibited a similar accumulation rate but slightly higher fluorescence intensity, likely attributable to the protective effect of the calcium carbonate layer.

Notably, ZCM2 demonstrated the most rapid and pronounced bone marrow accumulation, with fluorescence intensity reaching the peak levels of ZIF-8 and ZC by 24 h and remaining elevated at 48 h post-injection. To further investigate the biodistribution, major organs were harvested at 12 h post-injection of Cy3-labeled ZCM2 for ex vivo imaging (Fig. 6F–S8). ZCM2 was predominantly distributed in the liver and kidneys, with only minimal residual signal detected in the lungs, indicating hepatorenal metabolism and limited pulmonary retention.

ZCM2 ameliorates osteoporosis by promoting bone regeneration

To investigate the therapeutic effects and underlying mechanisms of ZCM2 on osteoporosis, femoral samples from mice after 4 weeks of treatment were systematically evaluated using histological staining, Micro CT analysis, and immunohistochemistry. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining (Fig. 7A) revealed that the OVX group exhibited sparse trabecular bone and thinning of cortical bone, while all treatment groups showed increased trabecular number, with the ZCM2 group displaying a trabecular structure comparable to that of the control group. Masson staining (Fig. 7B) demonstrated that newly formed bone (stained blue) was almost absent in the OVX group, indicating markedly suppressed bone formation. In contrast, all treatment groups presented varying degrees of new bone formation, with the ZC and ZCM2 groups showing the most pronounced effect, suggesting that the calcium carbonate layer promotes bone regeneration. TRAP staining (Fig. 7C) was performed to assess osteoclast activity. The OVX group showed a significant increase in TRAP-positive areas (red) in the metaphyseal region, whereas all nanoparticle-treated groups exhibited reduced positive areas. Notably, the ZC and ZCM2 groups displayed almost no TRAP-positive staining, similar to the control group, indicating effective inhibition of osteoclast function by the nanoparticles.

Micro CT three-dimensional reconstruction and quantitative analysis of bone microstructural parameters (Fig. 7D–I) showed that compared with the control group, the OVX group had significantly lower bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular number (Tb.N), along with increased trabecular separation (Tb.Sp). All treatment groups exhibited improvements in these parameters, with the ZCM2 group demonstrating the most robust recovery: BMD, BV/TV, Tb.Th, and Tb.N approached control levels, and Tb.Sp was even lower than that of the control group.

ZCM2 ameliorates osteoporosis by reducing MMP-9

To evaluate the effect of nanoparticles on MMP-9 expression in the bone marrow microenvironment, immunohistochemical staining for MMP-9 was performed (Fig. 7J). The OVX group displayed extensive MMP-9-positive staining (light brown) throughout the bone marrow cavity, while all treatment groups showed markedly reduced positive areas, with the ZCM2 group exhibiting the most pronounced reduction. Additionally, ELISA of bone marrow supernatants revealed a significant reduction in MMP-9 protein levels, a finding consistent with the immunohistochemical results (Fig. S10). To further verify the regulation of MMP-9 at the transcriptional level, quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis was performed on bone marrow samples from the different treatment groups. Consistent with the protein-level findings, ZCM2 significantly reduced Mmp9 mRNA expression compared with the OVX group (Fig. S9), further supporting the inhibitory effect of ZCM2 on MMP-9 expression in the bone marrow microenvironment.

Flow cytometric analysis of bone marrow cells revealed that ZCM2 treatment markedly increased the proportion of anti-inflammatory reparative immune cells (Fig. 8A–C). Although the overall frequency of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages showed only modest changes (Fig. 8B–D), the M1/M2 ratio was substantially reduced compared to the model group (Fig. 8E). This indicates that ZCM2 attenuates inflammation in the bone marrow microenvironment and promotes the polarization of macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory reparative phenotype. Furthermore, although the percentage of MMP-9+ cells in the ZCM2 group did not return to normal levels, it was significantly lower than that in the model group (Fig. 8F and G), further supporting the anti-inflammatory effects of ZCM2. These results indicate that ZCM2 effectively decreases MMP-9 levels in the bone marrow microenvironment. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ZCM2 alleviates OVX-induced osteoporosis by promoting bone regeneration, improving bone microarchitecture, and inhibiting osteoclast activity and MMP-9 expression.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully constructed a biomimetic nanoparticle system (ZCM2) by coating M2 macrophage membranes onto ZIF-8@CaCO3 core–shell nanoparticles for the comprehensive treatment of osteoporosis. The ZCM2 nanoparticles exhibited excellent biosafety, efficient cellular uptake, and remarkable bone-targeting capability. Mechanistically, ZCM2 enhanced the Nrf2/HO-1 antioxidant pathway in macrophages, thereby inhibiting NF-κB-mediated inflammation and reducing the secretion of MMP-9, a key enzyme responsible for pathological bone resorption. Concurrently, the calcium ions released from the CaCO3 shell promoted osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells via activation of the RUNX2/OSX/OPN signaling axis. In vivo experiments using an ovariectomized osteoporosis mouse model demonstrated that ZCM2 significantly ameliorated bone loss, improved bone microarchitecture, promoted new bone formation, and suppressed osteoclast activity and MMP-9 expression in the bone marrow microenvironment. Collectively, this triple-action biomimetic nanoplatform integrates anti-inflammatory, anti-resorptive, and pro-osteogenic functions, offering a promising and multifaceted therapeutic strategy for osteoporosis. Our findings highlight the potential of cell membrane-camouflaged nanomedicines in modulating the bone immune microenvironment and restoring bone homeostasis, paving the way for future clinical translation in the management of age-related bone diseases.
