A case of sitosterolaemia-caused systemic large-vessel stenosis mimicking Takayasu arteritis in which FDG-PET provided a clue for the differential diagnosis

FDG-PET is useful for distinguishing between large-vessel vasculitis and primary dyslipidaemia.

Dear Editor, Sitosterolaemia is a primary dyslipidaemia involving the abnormal accumulation of sitosterol due to a loss-of-function mutation in the ABCG5 gene and causes early-onset severe arteriosclerosis [1]. Sitosterolaemia causes severe juvenile arteriosclerosis, subcutaneous xanthomas and Achilles tendonitis; however, certain cases are underdiagnosed, as they sometimes have normal lipid profiles for triglycerides and cholesterol. It is extremely rare for rheumatologists to encounter cases of sitosterolaemia, as it has a prevalence of 1 in ≈200 000 individuals [1]. We experienced a case of sitosterolaemia mimicking Takayasu arteritis. [18F]-2-fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose (FDG)-PET showed utility for making a clear differential diagnosis between sitosterolaemia and Takayasu arteritis.

A 52-year-old man presented to the rheumatology department with hypertension and carotid vascular bruits. The patient was healthy and had no other systemic symptoms except for multiple soft nodules near the hand joints. Regarding his medical history, he had undergone surgery in childhood to remove lumps from his elbows and buttocks, but further details were unavailable. The laboratory data from our hospital showed no organ damage, but a continuous slightly increased CRP value (0.57 mg/dl). His glucose levels and lipid profiles were normal (total cholesterol 156 mg/dl, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol 51 mg/dl, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol 94 mg/dl and triglyceride 79 mg/dl). US of the carotid arteries showed all-around wall thickening with an irregular surface, suggesting large-vessel vasculitis (LVV; Supplementary Fig. S1, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online). Plain MRI of the neck showed severe stenosis of the bilateral carotid artery (Supplementary Fig. S2, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online). Contrast-enhanced CT revealed multiple stenoses in the bilateral internal carotid, common iliac and origin of the celiac and superior mesenteric arteries and abdominal aorta with mild wall thickening (Supplementary Fig. S3, available at Rheumatology Advances in Practice online), findings that were consistent with Takayasu arteritis.

Based on the findings from these images, we suspected LVV rather than arteriosclerosis. However, we were not fully confident that the slight inflammation was solely a manifestation of LVV without complication by any infection or malignant tumours. In addition, more information was required to determine the requisite intensity of immunosuppressant therapy. Therefore, to further exclude malignancy-related vasculitis and assess vasculitis activity, FDG-PET was performed.

Unexpectedly, no FDG accumulated in the arteries mentioned above; however, a marked accumulation was noted in the bilateral tendons in the forearm, patellar and bilateral Achilles tendons (Fig. 1). This finding rejected the possibility of Takayasu arteritis, and hyperlipidaemia, especially severe primary dyslipidaemia, was highly suspected instead.

FDG accumulation (red arrows) in tendons of the forearm (upper arrows), patellar (middle arrows) and Achilles tendons (lower arrows)

According to a detailed family history, he had a second-degree consanguineous marriage, his father had died from a heart attack and his older brother had died suddenly from an unknown reason. An examination of the lipids revealed very high sterol levels (14 mg/dl; >1 mg/dl). Other primary hyperlipidaemias, including familial hypercholesterolaemia and cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis, were excluded based on the lipid profile. A gene analysis showed the patient was homozygous for a loss-of-function mutation in the ABCG5 gene, confirming the diagnosis of autosomal recessive sitosterolaemia.

The patient started a low-plant-sterol diet with ezetimibe, an inhibitor of the NPC1L1 receptor through which sitosterol is taken up, to prevent the further accumulation of sitosterol. Approximately 1 year later, the sitosterol blood level had improved to 10 mg/dl.

There are no reports of primary hyperlipidaemia showing such a wide range of stenosis, which was one of the reasons we nearly misdiagnosed this case as Takayasu arteritis. Imaging-based differentiation approaches to distinguish LVV, including Takayasu arteritis, from other diseases have not yet been established. In the latest EULAR recommendations concerning the use of imaging in LVV, MRI is the first choice, followed by CT, FDG-PET and US for imaging of Takayasu arteritis [2]. The specific US findings of LVV are based on the presence of a ‘halo sign’ and a hypoechoic ring around the vessel wall. Takayasu arteritis in particular is characterized by a ‘macaroni sign’, as the wall of the common carotid artery is circumferentially thickened and the lumen is narrowed [3, 4]. However, moderate–severe arteriosclerosis can reportedly also present with these halo signs, making the differential diagnosis difficult between LVV and arteriosclerosis complications [3]. MRI is the preferred imaging modality for diagnosing Takayasu arteritis because of the lack of radiation and the ability to scan the aorta. However, there are no reports on how to distinguish between arteriosclerosis and LVV using MRI.

FDG-PET is widely used to assess LVV inflammation. The PET Vascular Activity Score has been reported to be useful in comparing the activity of Takayasu arteritis and giant cell arteritis with that in other groups, including patients with hyperlipidaemia and healthy subjects [5]. In Takayasu arteritis, both M1 and M2 macrophages contribute to inflammation in large vessels [4], and activated macrophages and lymphocytes within the arterial wall overexpress glucose transporters (Glut-1 and Glut-5) [6]. FDG is readily taken up by these cells.

Clinical FDG-PET images of primary dyslipidaemia are rare and, to our knowledge, there are no reports of sitosterolaemia. Only one report has described the detection of tendon xanthomas by FDG-PET in a case of familial hypercholesterolaemia, possibly due to the presence of foamy macrophages with high metabolic rates in the tendons [7]. In addition, there have been reports of cases of sitosterolaemia combined with Takayasu arteritis [8]; if Takayasu arteritis is active, the FDG accumulation in the large vessels can be confirmed by FDG-PET. Although the frequency of primary dyslipidaemia is much lower than that of LVV, each therapy is different, and misdiagnosis can be life-threatening. The use of FDG-PET in this case was powerful in rejecting arteritis, which was confusing in contrast-enhanced CT findings and unexpectedly useful in detecting tendon xanthoma due to sitosterolaemia.

In conclusion, we encountered a rare case of primary dyslipidaemia—sitosterolaemia showing a wide range of arteriosclerosis and mimicking Takayasu vasculitis. There have been no reports of cases requiring differentiation between LVV and sitosterolaemia. This case unintentionally revealed that FDG-PET is useful for distinguishing vascular inflammation in LVV, such as Takayasu arteritis, from severe arteriosclerosis.
