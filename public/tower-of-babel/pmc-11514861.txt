Office-based risk equation of Globorisk for prediction of ten-years cardiovascular risk among Iranian population: findings from Fasa PERSIAN cohort study
Authors: Amir Baseri, Azizallah Dehghan, Rozhan Khezri, Zahra Montaseri, Dagfinn Aune, Fatemeh Rezaei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGloborisk is one of the prediction tools for 10-year risk assessment of cardiovascular disease, featuring an office-based (non-laboratory-based) version. This version does not require laboratory tests for determining the CVD risk. The present study aims to determine the 10-year CVD risk using the office-based Globorisk model and factors associated with the 10-year CVD risk.MethodsIn this study, baseline data from 6810 individuals participating in the Fasa cohort study, with no history of CVD or stroke, were utilized. The risk equation of the office-based Globorisk model incorporates age, sex, systolic blood pressure (SBP), body mass index (BMI), and smoking status. The Globorisk model categorizes the risk into three groups: low risk (< 10%), moderate risk (10% to < 20%), and high risk (≥ 20%). To identify factors associated with the 10-year CVD risk, the predicted risk was categorized into two groups: <10% and ≥ 10%. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed to determine factors associated with an increased CVD risk.ResultsAccording to the 10-year CVD risk categorization, 78.3%, 16.4%, and 5.3% of men were in the low, moderate, and high risk groups, respectively, while 85.8%, 10.0%, and 4.2%, of women were in the respective risk groups. Multivariable logistic regression results indicated that in men, the 10-year CVD risk decreases with being an opium user, and increases with being illiterate, having abdominal obesity, and low or moderate physical activity compared to high physical activity. In women, being married, and higher fiber consumption decrease the 10-year CVD risk, while being illiterate, low or moderate physical activity compared to high physical activity, having abdominal obesity, opium use, and being in wealth quintiles 1 to 4 compared to quintile 5 increase the risk.ConclusionsConsidering the factors associated with increased CVD risk, there is a need to enhance awareness and modify lifestyle to mitigate and reduce the risk of CVD. Additionally, early identification of individuals at moderate to high risk is essential for preventing disease progression. The use of the office-based Globorisk model can be beneficial in settings where resources are limited for determining the 10-year CVD risk.Keywords: Cardiovascular diseases, Office-based, Globorisk, Risk assessment

Background

Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) are chronic conditions that arise from a combination of genetic, physiological, environmental, and behavioral factors [1]. Of all NCD deaths, 77% occurs in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is among the main types of NCDs [1], and CVD is the leading cause of hospital admissions and mortality globally [2, 3]. Approximately 80–86% of CVD-related deaths occur in LMICs [4, 5]. The burden of mortality due to CVD is on the rise in LMICs [2]. It is expected that this upward trend in CVD-related mortality will persist in LMICs [6, 7].

Over the past 40 years in Iran, the most common causes of death have shifted from communicable to noncommunicable diseases, especially CVD [8]. CVD tends to occur at younger ages in LMICs like Iran [9], and more than half of CVD-related deaths occur in asymptomatic individuals without past history [10]. Therefore, by measuring the predicted risk of CVD occurrence, individuals at risk can be identified [11]. Early detection of healthy individuals with high predicted risk of CVD can provide a basis for therapeutic interventions and lifestyle modifications, aligning with the World Health Organization’s (WHO) goal of reducing premature mortality from CVD [12].

Today, various models are employed to determine the 10-year risk score for CVD as a means of early identification of healthy individuals at risk [13–15]. These models serve as crucial tools in reducing the burden of CVD, including lowering mortality, morbidity, disability, and healthcare costs [16].

Globorisk calculates the 10-year CVD risk score separately for each country and calculates the CVD risk for healthy individuals using laboratory-based and office-based models [17]. In the laboratory-based model, cholesterol and diabetes are considered in addition to other risk factors, while the office-based model uses BMI instead of blood cholesterol and diabetes status [17]. Substantial agreement between office-based and laboratory-based Globorisk models has been reported previously [18]. Also, Globorisk office-based models have a good agreement with the WHO model [19]. This tool is helpful in healthcare, public health, and decision-making to identify individuals at risk of CVD for timely interventions. In recent years, this tool has been validated and calibrated using data from multiple countries [20].

In Iran, lacking tools for predicting CVD risk, they use tools from other populations or international sources, On the other, Iran, like most LMICs, faces challenges due to resource constraints, including limited laboratory facilities. Therefore, there may be limitations in utilizing laboratory-based models for calculating the 10-year CVD risk. Given that a significant burden of CVD is associated with LMICs [4, 5], and considering the importance of WHO recommendations for implementing cost-effective public health programs and clinical interventions in LMICs to reduce disability and premature deaths from CVD [21], The office-based model can help identify those at risk of CVD in resource-limited areas with limited lab access.

Numerous studies have reported good agreement between laboratory-based and non-laboratory-based models [11, 18, 19, 22, 23], recommending the use of non-laboratory models when lab testing isn’t feasible. While, as of now, no study has been conducted to identify the factors influencing the risk score of the non-laboratory version of the Globorisk model, the present study aims to determine the factors affecting the risk score of the office-based Globorisk model in a large Iranian population.

Methods

This study was conducted using the baseline data of the Fasa cohort study, which is a subset of the PERSIAN cohort study. The PERSIAN study covered 18 distinct areas in Iran, with an approximate population of 180,000 [24]. The Fasa cohort study specifically focused on residents of the Sheshdeh and Qarah Bolagh district in the southern part of Fars province. Baseline data were collected from 2015 to 2016, encompassing a population of 10,138 individuals. The study targeted individuals aged 35 years and older. Detailed aspects of the Fasa cohort study have been elucidated by Farjam et al. and Homayounfar et al. [25, 26]. Following the study’s approval, the Fasa cohort center was established, issuing a call for public collaboration to participate in the study. Despite the inclusion of small towns and villages in the mentioned region, the level of community participation was relatively high. Moreover, indigenous individuals were employed at the Fasa cohort center for data collection, contributing to further increased community participation and collaboration.

Data collection involved surveys, medical examinations, anthropometric measurements, blood indices, and laboratory tests. Given that the Globorisk risk equations were developed for individuals aged 40 to 80, this study excluded participants under 40 and over 80 years old. Additionally, as the 10-year CVD risk prediction applies to individuals without a history of CVD, those with a history of CVD and stroke were excluded. A total of 6810 individuals aged 40 to 80 with no prior history of CVD or stroke were included in this study. In Fig. 1, the flowchart detailing the sample selection process is presented.

Participant inclusion flow chart

To measure the 10-year risk of CVD, the non-laboratory-based version of the Globorisk risk prediction model was utilized. Globorisk has well-validated internally integral part model development; calibrated for countries that can be applied with routinely available data [27]. Globorisk has risk charts for 182 countries [20]. Also, Globorisk employs a Cox proportional hazard model to determine the 10-year risk of fatal and non-fatal cardiovascular outcomes. In this study we used Cox proportional hazard model to determine the 10-year risk of fatal and non-fatal cardiovascular outcomes. The risk equations within Globorisk are developed for individuals aged 40 to 80. The 10-year CVD risk score can be categorized into three risk groups: low-risk (< 10%), moderate-risk (10–20%), and high-risk (≥ 20).

In the equation of the office-based Globorisk CVD risk model for measuring cardiovascular disease risk, factors such as age (in years), gender (men and women), SBP (mmHg), smoking status (yes and no), and BMI are utilized. Additionally, the country (Iran) must be specified. Individuals’ ages were determined through national identification cards. To measure SBP, individuals were instructed to rest at the cohort center, and their blood pressure was then measured twice with a time interval of 15 min and the average was recorded. Individuals were queried about their smoking status. For BMI measurement, individuals’ height and weight were taken using standard instruments. Subsequently, weight was divided by the square of height in meters, and BMI was calculated.

In this study, factors influencing CVD risk were identified. These factors include education level (Illiterate, 1–5 year, and ≥ 6 year), diabetes (yes and no), abdominal obesity (yes and no) which is considered if men’s waist circumference is greater than or equal to 102 cm and women’s is greater than or equal to 88 cm, opium use (yes and no), marital status (married and others), with “others” encompassing divorced and widowed individuals, ethnicity (Fars and others), where “others” includes Arab, Turk, and other ethnicities, daily fiber intake germs ( = < 25 and > 25), and number of daily sleep hours (≤ 6, 6–8, and ≥ 9). Physical activity was calculated based on the Metabolic Equivalent of Task (MET) and then divided into three groups: low, moderate, and high. The wealth score index was determined using multiple correspondence analyses (MCA) on family assets and then categorized into five groups from quintile 1 to quintile 5.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis was conducted for both quantitative and qualitative variables. For quantitative variables, mean (standard deviation) was calculated, while for qualitative variables, number (percentages) were computed. Initially, the 10-year CVD risk for the office-based model was determined. Subsequently, the risks were categorized into low-, moderate-, and high-risk groups. Risk categorization was further stratified by sex, and the percentages or means of factors that may contribute to an increased risk of CVD in men and women were identified.

Subsequently, the calculated risk was divided into two groups: less than 10% (low risk) and 10% or more (moderate and high risk). Initially, univariable logistic regression was performed to examine the association between the 10-year CVD risk and education, diabetes, abdominal obesity, physical activity, wealth score index, opium use, marital status, ethnicity, fiber intake, and hours of daily sleep. Variables with a significance level less than 0.20 were then included in the multivariable logistic regression, identifying factors that may impact the 10-year risk. Data analysis was conducted using STATA 14 software, considering a significance level of 0.05 in this study.

Ethical considerations

This study is the outcome of a research proposal approved by the Ethics Committee of Jahrom University of Medical Sciences (IR.JUMS.REC.1401.160). Individuals participated in the Fasa PERSIAN cohort study voluntarily, providing their informed consent, and the questionnaire used was anonymous. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

The education levels of women and men significantly varied across different risk groups, with individuals in the low-risk group having higher educational attainment. Discussion

In this study, we identified factors influencing the 10-year CVD risk score in the office-based Globorisk model within a large Iranian population. Our findings revealed that, based on the categorization of the 10-year CVD risk using the office-based Globorisk model, 78.3% of men were in the low-risk group, 16.4% in the moderate-risk group, and 5.3% in the high-risk group. Similarly, in women, 85.8% were in the low-risk group, 10.0% in the moderate-risk group, and 4.2% in the high-risk group. For men, multivariable logistic regression results indicated a positive association between the 10-year CVD risk ≥ 10% in the office-based Globorisk model and low years of education, low level of physical activity, and having abdominal obesity and inverse association with using opium. For women, positive associations were observed with being illiterate, low level of physical activity, having abdominal obesity, low level of wealth index, and using opium and inverse association was observed with being married and higher fiber intake.

In the current study, the categorization of the 10-year CVD risk in the office-based Globorisk model revealed that 5.3% of men and 4.2% of women fall into the high-risk group. The results of the study conducted by Hajifathalian et al., using data from 8 cohorts in various countries, also demonstrated variations in the classification of 10-year CVD risk across different nations [17]. It is important to note that the Globorisk model calculates the 10-year CVD risk based on each country’s coefficients, so differences in risk categorization among individuals in different countries are expected. Additionally, the prevalence of CVD risk factors that may influence risk categorization varies among different societies.

The study results revealed a significant inverse dose-response association between years of education and the 10-year CVD risk ≥ 10% in both men and women. Our findings align with numerous studies that have observed a significant inverse association between education level and the 10-year CVD risk [28–30]. The inverse association between education and the 10-year CVD risk may be attributed to the influence of education on individuals’ lifestyles; those with higher education levels tend to lead healthier lives, face fewer risk factors, and have a longer healthy life expectancy [31]. Our study also indicated a significant positive association between opium use and the 10-year CVD risk ≥ 10% in women. However, in men, this association was inverse. There is evidence indicating that CVD is notably more common in opium users including men and women compared to non-users, demonstrating a dose-response association [32]. Also, opium use is associated with an increased 10-year risk of CVD, including coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, and other adverse cardiovascular outcomes [32, 33]. Therefore, the results among men in the current study should be interpreted cautiously, as there may be unknown confounders influencing the outcomes. To attain more robust findings, longitudinal studies may be better placed to investigate this association further.

The present study revealed a significant association between physical activity and the 10-year CVD risk ≥ 10% in both men and women. The 10-year CVD risk in men with low and moderate physical activity was 2.4 and 1.9 times higher, respectively, than in men with high physical activity. Similarly, the CVD risk in women with low physical activity was 2.3 times higher, and in women with moderate physical activity, it was 5.0 times higher compared to women with high physical activity. These findings align with studies conducted in Iran and Norway [28, 34]. Physical activity can impact risk factors for non-communicable chronic diseases such as CVD, type 2 diabetes, and obesity [35]. Therefore, it is recommended to establish practical platforms for implementing physical activity programs, accompanied by education and the promotion of a healthy lifestyle for individuals across communities. Implementing these strategies should be seriously considered, given the high burden of CVD and financial challenges in LMICs.

We observed a significant association between the 10-year CVD risk ≥ 10% and abdominal obesity in both men and women. The present study demonstrated that the CVD risk in men with abdominal obesity is 3.1 times higher than in men without abdominal obesity. Similarly, in women, a significant association was observed, with the CVD risk being 2.3 times higher in those with abdominal obesity compared to those without. Abdominal obesity is indeed a significant risk factor for CVD, as indicated in other studies as well [36, 37]. Although a direct association between obesity and CVD risk factors, including dyslipidemia, type 2 diabetes, and hypertension has been reported. It should be considered that various forms of obesity, whether defined by body mass index or abdominal obesity using waist circumference, as well as visceral adiposity, are associated with an increased risk of CVD [36].

The results indicated that the 10-year CVD risk ≥ 10% is lower in married women compared to others (single, divorced, and widowed). However, a significant association between marital status and CVD risk was not observed in men. The findings of a meta-analysis involving over 2 million participants showed that unmarried individuals are at a 42% higher risk of CVD, 16% higher risk of coronary heart disease (CHD), 43% higher risk of CHD death, and 55% higher risk of stroke death [38]. Nevertheless, sex differences have been observed in the association between marital status and CVD [39]. When differences in the association between marital status and CVD are observed in gender subgroups, the role of factors such as psychosocial and socioeconomic factors, as well as other acute stressors, becomes more apparent [40]. Unmarried women experienced notably more stressful life events than unmarried men [41]. They may even experience an increased occurrence of sub-clinical symptoms of depression, anxiety, and major mental disorders [42]. Additionally, the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and obesity indicates a higher risk for CVD in women compared to men [43]. Moreover, risk factors of CVD such as smoking, hypertriglyceridemia, and low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels have a more significant impact on women than on men [43].Therefore unmarried women may have a different lifestyle than unmarried men. They may engage in less physical activity, most of them are overweight and obese and have an unhealthy diet, and the prevalence of diabetes, high blood pressure, and blood lipid disorders is higher in them than in single men. Further research is needed to explore the biological, behavioral, and social mechanisms underlying these sex differences.

The present study revealed a significant association between the wealth score index and the 10-year CVD risk ≥ 10% in women. The CVD risk was 4.3, 5.7, 3.7, and 3.9 times higher in women in quintiles 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively, compared to those in quintile 5. Numerous studies have demonstrated the association between socioeconomic status (SES) and CVD [44, 45], indicating that a greater burden of CVD is often related to individuals with lower SES [44]. Moreover, it has been reported that a substantial portion of the CVD burden is attributed to LMICs [2]. The wealth index likely influences the 10-year CVD risk by impacting individuals’ exposure to CVD risk factors, lifestyle choices, and timely access to diagnostic and therapeutic services. However, we did not observe a significant association between the wealth index and the 10-year CVD risk ≥ 10% in men.

Furthermore, the current study demonstrated a significant association between the 10-year CVD risk ≥ 10% and fiber intake in women. The benefits of fiber consumption in reducing CVD risk align with the findings of Zhang et al.‘s study conducted on a population of 14,947 individuals [46]. It appears that an education-based approach promoting a healthy diet, emphasizing increased consumption of whole grains, fruits, and fiber-rich vegetables, could be beneficial in preventing CVD [47–49].

The first risk prediction tool utilized in North America to assess CVD risk and determine the need for initiation of statin therapy for primary prevention was the Framingham risk score. The Pooled Cohort Equations were created recently by combining five cohorts from various regions of the United States due to concerns that the FRS might overestimate risk and not be applicable to the current U.S. population [50, 51]. These prediction models for 10-year CVD risk are developed for American populations, which may limit their applicability in other countries, the Globorisk model addresses this challenge by incorporating country-specific coefficients in calculating individuals’ 10-year CVD risk [17]. This model proves to be a key solution, particularly in LMICs. In our study, we identified factors influencing the 10-year CVD risk using the office-based Globorisk model. This model is advantageous as it simplifies the determination of the 10-year CVD risk compared to laboratory-based models, avoiding the need for extensive laboratory tests and facilities. Given that a significant burden of CVD occurs in LMICs like Iran [5], identifying factors that may impact CVD risk can contribute to reducing CVD risk in high-risk individuals, especially in LMICs.

Study strengths and limitations

One of the strengths of the current study is the meticulous collection of data from a population-based cohort study. Additionally, the use of the office-based Globorisk model for predicting the 10-year CVD risk, which requires no laboratory tests and calculates the 10-year CVD risk separately for each country, adds to the study strengths. However, a notable weakness is that the validity of the office-based Globorisk model has not been examined in the Iranian population. Therefore, it is recommended that cohort studies with a large sample size and appropriate follow-up duration be conducted to assess the validity of the office-based Globorisk model in the Iranian population. It should be noted; this study is part of PERSIAN cohort study. Therefore, caution should be taken in generalizing the results to the whole of Iran.

Conclusions

Approximately 22% of men and 14% of women were at moderate to high risk of CVD over the next 10 years. Education, physical activity, abdominal obesity were risk predictors for CVD in both men and women, marital status, wealth score, and fiber intake were predictors only in women, and opium use was a risk predictor for CVD only in men. Increasing awareness, improving lifestyle, and targeted interventions may help control and modify CVD risk factors, ultimately leading to a reduction in the burden of CVD in LMICs such as Iran. Additionally, timely identification of individuals at high risk of CVD is crucial for prompt therapeutic and preventive interventions. To assess CVD risk in LMICs, the utilization of office-based models, including the Globorisk model, which are cost-effective and readily available, can be instrumental.
