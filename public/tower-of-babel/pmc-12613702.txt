Time from symptom recognition to cancer diagnosis and factors linked to delayed diagnosis of hematological childhood cancers at a tertiary hospital in uganda: a mixed methods study
Authors: Joan Nakabiri, Brian Agaba, Ronald Naitala, Betty Najjuuko, Catherine Asiimwe, Hellen Alamo, Brian Mafabi, Marilyn Hockenberry, John M. Ssenkusu, Victoria Nankabirwa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLate presentation of childhood cancers is prevalent across several Sub-Saharan African countries, including Uganda, and is largely driven by delays in diagnosis. These delays often result in disease progression and poorer clinical outcomes. However, data on diagnostic timelines and the underlying factors contributing to these delays remain limited in Uganda. This study aimed to estimate the median duration from initial symptom recognition to confirmed diagnosis and to explore the determinants of diagnostic delays among pediatric cancer patients at Mulago National Referral Hospital in Uganda.MethodsThis mixed-methods study combined quantitative and qualitative data collection approaches. The quantitative component involved reviewing records of children diagnosed with leukemia or lymphoma at the hospital’s Pediatric Hematology Oncology department between February 2019 and June 2023. In the qualitative component, six key informant interviews and four focus group discussions were conducted with health workers and caregivers of children diagnosed with cancer. Quantitative data were collected using a tool in Open Data Kit, then analyzed using descriptive statistics, and Kaplan Meier curves. Qualitative data were analyzed through thematic analysis.ResultsWe analyzed records of 387 pediatric cancer patients, of whom 65.4% (253/387) were male. The median age at diagnosis was 8 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 4.7–11.7 years). The overall median duration from symptom onset to diagnosis was 47 days. Among specific cancer types, the median time to diagnosis was 31 days (IQR: 16–85 days) for leukemia and 68 days (IQR: 32.3–175.8 days) for lymphoma. Qualitative data identified several factors contributing to diagnostic delays, including caregiver-related challenges—such as limited knowledge, financial constraints, suboptimal health-seeking behaviors, and cultural beliefs—and health system barriers, including insufficient provider expertise, misdiagnoses, limited diagnostic infrastructure, and weak referral pathways.ConclusionThe median time to diagnosis among pediatric cancer patients was prolonged, largely attributable to suboptimal caregiver health-seeking behaviors, limited awareness among both caregivers and healthcare providers, inadequate diagnostic capacity, and inefficient referral systems. Targeted interventions to address these barriers are essential to enhance timely diagnosis and initiation of treatment, thereby improving clinical outcomes for children with cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15025-8.

Background

The global burden of childhood cancer is on the rise. While often considered rare, data from the World Health Organization (WHO) reveals that childhood cancers are, in fact, the leading cause of death from non-communicable diseases among children worldwide. Every year, approximately 400,000 children and adolescents aged 0–19 years are diagnosed with cancer globally, with the majority residing in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. Important to note, about 44% of children with cancer die before they are even diagnosed [2, 3].

The survival rate for children with cancer is 80% in high-income countries, but less than 20% in LMICs. Late presentation, a key factor in poor outcomes, is particularly pronounced in Sub Saharan Africa and other LMICs [2, 3]. These delays are primarily due to both patient/caregiver and healthcare system-related factors.

Certain cancers like Burkitt’s lymphoma, if detected early, have a high likelihood of cure when treated adequately [4]. Early diagnosis, i.e. identifying the disease before it spreads, can significantly improve survival rates and reduce morbidity [5, 6]. Studies have consistently shown that children diagnosed at earlier stages have better survival outcomes compared to those diagnosed later [6, 7]. As a result, early detection remains a key goal in pediatric oncology, as it enables timely intervention when the disease burden is still manageable, thereby improving survival rates and treatment success [8].

Like other LMICs, childhood cancer presents a significant public health challenge in Uganda [9]. However, the true extent of the childhood cancer burden is unclear due to lack of comprehensive population-level data, making it difficult to plan effectively for cancer management and control. One study conducted by Stoeter and colleagues analyzed childhood cancer trends in Kyadondo County, Kampala, Uganda, using data from the Kampala Cancer Registry, and reported an incidence rate of 146.6 cases per one million children [10].

Despite efforts by the government, particularly through the Uganda Cancer Institute and its community cancer awareness programs [11], a significant proportion of pediatric cancers in Uganda are diagnosed at advanced stages. This delay in diagnosis often results in poor prognoses and high mortality rates [9, 12].

Various studies conducted in LMICs have highlighted several factors contributing to the delayed diagnosis of childhood cancers including financial constraints; caregiver knowledge and perception towards cancer – cultural beliefs and seeking alternative medicine; misdiagnosis and inadequate health provider expertise; unavailability of diagnostic tools at lower-level facilities [12–18]. However, data on the time between symptom recognition and cancer diagnosis in Uganda is scarce, and the factors contributing to diagnostic delays remain poorly documented. This has implications on increased government healthcare expenditures for pediatric cancer treatment, higher morbidity and mortality rates, reduced quality of life for affected children, and significant psychosocial and financial burdens on families.

This study sought to determine the median time from symptom recognition to diagnostic confirmation and explored the factors influencing diagnostic delays in children with cancer at Mulago National Referral Hospital (MNRH), Uganda. The study focused on lymphomas and leukemias, which contribute nearly 60% of the childhood cancer cases and share related symptomatology [19].

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a mixed methods study in the Pediatric Hematology and Oncology department of Mulago National Referral Hospital, located 4 km from Kampala central business district. It receives approximately 250 new cancer patients per year with about 51% having leukemia or lymphoma. At this facility, leukemias and lymphoma diagnosis confirmations are made through flow cytometry of peripheral blood, bone marrow aspirate or fine needle aspirates. CT scans are also part of the diagnostic investigations.

Study population

The study group was children (aged 0–18), their caregivers, and health workers at Mulago National Referral Hospital’s Pediatric Hematology and Oncology department, involved in care of pediatric patients with leukemia or lymphoma from February 2019 to June 2023. It included records of all confirmed diagnoses within that period but excluded records lacking essential data or cases discovered incidentally without prior symptoms.

Sample size and sampling procedure

All complete children’s records with a confirmed diagnosis of leukemia or lymphoma between February, 2019 and June, 2023 were reviewed for this study. For the qualitative part, six key informant interviews (KIIs) and four focus group discussions (FGDs) were held with selected health workers and caregivers of children with blood cancers respectively. Participants were purposively chosen, and interviews continued until data saturation was achieved.

Study variables

The dependent variable was time in days between symptom recognition and diagnosis among children 0–18 years diagnosed with lymphoma or leukemia at the Pediatric Hematology Oncology department of MNRH. The start time referred to the time when the initial symptoms related to cancer were recognized by the caregiver, The endpoint " childhood cancer diagnosis confirmation” referred to the pediatric oncologist’s formal diagnosis of either lymphomas or leukemia which had been concluded from either histology or flow cytometry of peripheral blood, bone marrow aspirate, and fine needle aspirate.

Independent variables included demographic and household factors like the child’s age, sex, residence, caregiver relationship, and parents’ employment. The qualitative component examined caregiver and health system factors affecting diagnosis delays, along with caregivers’ experiences, perceptions, and challenges during the diagnostic process.

Data collection tools and procedure

A questionnaire (used as a data abstraction tool), along with key informant interview and focus group discussion guides (provided as supplementary files 1, 2 and 3 respectively), were developed specifically for this study to collect information on factors contributing to delays in diagnosis. A pretested data abstraction tool was used to abstract data from patient records. The key informant interviews were conducted in English among health workers who had worked in the Pediatric Hematology Oncology department of MNRH for at least three years. The focus group discussions were conducted with caregivers of pediatric cancer patients whose children had been diagnosed with cancer from May to July 2023.

Data analysis

Quantitative data was entered into Excel, checked for completeness, and analyzed using STATA 17. Descriptive statistics like the medians with their interquartile ranges and proportions were used to summarize the participants’ socio-demographic characteristics. Kaplan Meier curves were drawn to illustrate the median time from symptom recognition to diagnosis confirmation. Due to non-normality of time to diagnosis, non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis) were used at bivariable analysis. Variables with p ≤ 0.25 in bivariable analysis were included in a Cox regression model to identify factors influencing time to diagnosis. The Cox-proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using the estat phtest in STATA, and was met (p value > 0.05).

Thematic analysis method using inductive coding was used to analyze qualitative data. Exploration of data and synthesis of codes, subthemes and themes was done in Atlas.ti Version 22. A master sheet that aligned with study objectives summarized themes, subthemes, frequencies and key issues. Relevant verbatim quotations were selected as evidence to support the generated themes and sub themes.

Results

A total of 387 records of children diagnosed with leukemia or lymphoma at Mulago National Referral Hospital were included in the study (Fig. 1). Additionally, six key informant interviews were conducted with 6 health workers (3 doctors and 3 nurses), and 29 caregivers participated in four focus group discussions.

Quantitative findings

Sociodemographic characteristics of the children

The majority of the children (65.4%) were male. The median age was 8 years (Interquartile Range [IQR]: 4.7–11.7 years). Over half of the children (58.7%) resided in the central region, and most (60.7%) lived in rural areas (Table 1). Leukemia was the most common diagnosis, accounting for 60.7% (235) of cases while those with lymphoma were 152 (39.3%).

Time from symptom recognition to diagnosis

The overall median time from initial symptom recognition to a confirmed cancer diagnosis was 47 days (IQR: 21.0–107.0). Children with leukemia were diagnosed earlier a [median time in days (IQR): 31.0 (16.0–85.0)] than children with lymphoma [68.0 (32.3–175.8)] (Fig. 2).

Kaplan-Meier curves of time from symptom onset to diagnosis in pediatric leukemia and lymphoma cases at Mulago National Referral Hospital, February 2019 to June 2023

Since all included records attained the outcome of interest (cancer diagnosis), there was no censoring. The time to diagnosis of children with leukemia was different from that of children with lymphoma; and this difference was statistically significant as seen with the log rank p < 0.001.

At bivariable analysis, children from rural areas experienced significantly longer delays (median: 53.0 days) than those from urban settings (median: 33.0 days), p = 0.018. Similarly, patients who underwent flow cytometry had a shorter median diagnostic time (43.0 days) compared to those who did not (74.5 days), p = 0.012 (Table 1).

However, at multivariable analysis, no factors were found to be significantly associated with the time to diagnosis in the multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis as seen in Table 2; this could have been due to the influence of confounding factors or collinearity with other variables, which were accounted for in the multivariable model.

Qualitative findings

Qualitative data from the key informant interviews and focus group discussions identified caregiver-related and health system-related barriers contributing to delays in cancer diagnosis. Thematic analysis yielded key insights summarized below:

Care giver related factors

The participants cited various care giver related factors that contribute to delay in diagnosis of childhood cancers. These were grouped into the following sub- themes: poor caregiver health-seeking behavior and cultural beliefs; limited knowledge about childhood cancers; and financial access barriers.

Poor caregiver health-seeking behaviors and cultural beliefs.

Study participants mentioned that caregivers often delayed seeking formal healthcare, relying instead on home remedies, traditional healers, or spiritual practices. Some did not take early symptoms seriously.

“…they still seek native doctors or churches, hoping for healing. By the time they bring the child to the hospital, the symptoms have worsened.” – Female, KI 6.

“…some caregivers just give the child Panadol or when the child lacks blood, they just boil local herbs or go to witch doctors and spend time yet the disease is spreading, after all those ways failing, that is when they think of going to the hospital.” ─Female, FGD 4, R1.

Many participants highlighted lack of prior awareness that children can also develop cancer, leading to delays in recognizing symptoms as serious.

Financial access barriers.

Poverty was frequently mentioned by the participants as a barrier to timely diagnosis due to costs associated with transport and healthcare.

“…poverty affects access. Even when healthcare workers suspect cancer, families may not afford to travel to the right facility.” – Male, KI 4.

“…sometimes patients have to pay a fee to see a medical practitioner hence those families that are poor may find it hard in terms of both transport to the health care but also fees where such services are charged.” ─Male, KI 5.

Health System-Related factors

Regarding the healthcare system, participants identified multiple barriers which hinder early accurate diagnosis of childhood cancers. These barriers were grouped into sub themes including; lack of provider expertise, frequent misdiagnosis, limited diagnostic capacity, ineffective referral systems and lack of integrated services. These are discussed in the subsequent sections;

Discussion

This study findings revealed that the time from initial symptom recognition to diagnosis confirmation for childhood cancer patients at Mulago National Referral Hospital was considerably long. Both caregiver-related factors (such as poor health-seeking behaviors, knowledge gaps, and financial barriers) and health system-related factors (including lack of provider expertise, frequent misdiagnoses, limited diagnostic capacity, ineffective referral mechanisms, and a lack of integrated services) were cited as contributors to this delay.

In this study, the time from symptom recognition to diagnosis confirmation for childhood cancers is markedly longer than the times reported in high income countries like Canada, South Africa, and Brazil [18, 20, 21]. Prolonged diagnostic delays have been linked to poorer survival outcomes in children with cancer, particularly in LMICs [22]. Uganda, which experiences poor outcomes for childhood cancers [9], may have these extended diagnostic times as a contributing factor. In contrast, the findings of this study showed shorter diagnostic delays compared to studies conducted in Turkey [23] and Kenya [14]. These differences could be attributed to varying delays in health systems, caregiver-related factors, and challenges specific to diagnosing childhood cancers.

Additionally, our study found that leukemia patients had a significantly shorter diagnostic time compared to lymphoma patients. These findings are consistent with those reported in Turkey [24]. Lymphomas present more ambiguous symptoms that can be mistaken for less severe conditions, potentially delaying specific cancer investigations. Similar challenges across various cancer types have been documented in studies by Black, Boswell [25] and [26]. Factors such as the nature of symptom presentation, the availability of specific diagnostic tests, and the expertise required for accurate diagnosis likely contribute to these variations [27]. These results emphasize the need for tailored diagnostic strategies based on cancer type. Furthermore, the findings underscore the necessity for targeted educational programs to enhance healthcare providers’ capacity to identify and manage childhood cancers promptly [28–30].

Good health-seeking behavior is critical for the early identification of childhood cancers [31]. Unlike adults, children may not communicate their symptoms effectively and often rely on their caregivers to seek medical attention [8, 13]. Our study highlighted poor health-seeking behavior among caregivers as a significant contributor to diagnostic delays. Caregivers often turned to home remedies or traditional healers, which delayed their decision to seek medical care. This is likely influenced by cultural beliefs favoring alternative medicine [14, 31]. Additionally, the nonspecific nature of cancer-related symptoms may cause caregivers to underestimate the severity of the condition. For instance, fever is commonly associated with childhood illnesses like infections, may be a symptom of childhood cancer but is often not perceived as such [32]. These findings highlight the need for community education programs aimed at promoting good health-seeking behaviors and encouraging timely care at medical facilities. These programs have been implemented in several countries with significant success [33–35].

Limited caregiver knowledge about childhood cancers was also found to influence the time to diagnosis in our study. Many caregivers held misconceptions, such as believing that children do not get cancer, that cancer is incurable, or that it is caused by witchcraft and should only be treated with traditional medicine [12, 13]. Lower parental education levels were also found to contribute to delays in seeking care [13]. Therefore, improving parental education and public awareness about childhood cancer symptoms and presentation is crucial to facilitate timely care-seeking behavior [22, 30].

As with many studies, financial constraints were identified as a major barrier to timely diagnosis in our study. These constraints manifest as lack of transportation to healthcare facilities and insufficient funds for sustaining hospital stays and medical expenses, leading to delays in seeking care. Caregivers often fail to travel to referral hospitals due to the high cost of transportation [12, 14]. Furthermore, children from higher socioeconomic families tended to experience shorter diagnostic times, likely because these families can afford care at private healthcare facilities that offer more expertise and faster diagnostic services [13, 36]. These findings suggest the need for policies aimed at reducing the financial burden of cancer diagnosis and treatment, such as implementing community insurance schemes to subsidize healthcare costs [13, 14].

The study also found that lack of provider expertise hindered the health system’s ability to timely diagnose childhood cancers. Similar to previous studies, we identified barriers within the healthcare system, including competing healthcare burdens, inadequate training on childhood cancers, and weak primary healthcare capacity to detect cancer, resulting in misdiagnoses or diagnosis delays [15, 37, 38]. Variations in the capacity of primary care providers to recognize severe symptoms suggestive of childhood cancer are a significant challenge. The scarcity of specialized pediatric oncology training among primary healthcare providers worsens this issue, as seen in studies from Egypt and South Africa, where 39.5% and 58% of patients, respectively, were misdiagnosed [16, 18]. Some symptoms, such as fever and headache, are vague and can easily be mistaken for common childhood illnesses like malaria [39]. Therefore, enhancing the training of primary healthcare providers and general pediatricians about cancer symptoms in children is essential for improving diagnostic accuracy and ensuring timely referrals.

The unavailability of diagnostic tools, especially at the primary care level, was another significant barrier to timely diagnosis. These challenges are part of a broader issue of resource allocation within healthcare systems [40], where advanced diagnostic tools are often concentrated in higher-level health facilities, leaving primary care settings under-equipped [41]. Previous studies have similarly documented difficulties accessing diagnostic tools, which hinder timely cancer diagnosis [15, 17]. This emphasizes the need to prioritize the decentralization of diagnostic services and ensure the availability of essential diagnostic tools at all levels of care.

Additionally, ineffective referral systems were found to delay the diagnosis of childhood cancers. This reflects a lack of integrated care pathways and communication between various levels of the healthcare system [42, 43]. Systemic inefficiencies, such as fragmented healthcare services, poor communication channels, substandard care at referral facilities, and inadequate financial resources, all contribute to delayed diagnoses [44]. Similar challenges have been observed in other LMICs, highlighting the need for improved healthcare networks and referral systems to facilitate timely diagnosis of childhood cancers [4, 45–47].

Limitations

Firstly, 33.7% of pediatric cancer records were excluded due to missing data, which may have introduced systematic bias, particularly selection bias. Additionally, for the secondary data, we used medical records which were based on the caregivers’ own account of past events and experiences and this could have led to recall bias; and several key variables necessary for a thorough quantitative assessment of factors associated with delayed diagnosis were unavailable. However, it is likely that the missing data occurred at random, suggesting that the impact on the validity of the study findings is minimal. To mitigate these limitations, a mixed-methods study design was employed, allowing for qualitative insights to compliment and enrich the quantitative findings.

Conclusion

The median duration from initial symptom recognition to diagnostic confirmation among pediatric cancer patients was quite prolonged. Factors identified as key contributors to this delay included suboptimal caregiver health-seeking behaviors, limited awareness among caregivers and healthcare providers, constrained diagnostic capacity, and inefficient referral mechanisms. Targeted efforts to address these barriers are essential to facilitate timely diagnosis and treatment, thereby improving clinical outcomes for children with cancer in Uganda.
