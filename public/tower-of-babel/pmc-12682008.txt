Pickering emulsions stabilized by moringa seed residue protein/tannic acid particles for curcumin loading: Enhanced retention and bioaccessibility
Authors: Ying Zou, Hong Hu, Zhilian Huang, Haolan Dong, Shaodan Peng, Ruyi Li, Tiaokun Fu, Jianping Rao, Long Zheng, Xiaobing Huang, Jihua Li, Wei Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pickering emulsions stabilized by moringa seed residue protein (MSRP) or MSRP-tannic acid (MSRP-TA) composites were employed to address the issues of curcumin's high environmental sensitivity and low bioaccessibility. By enhancing MSRP'S amphiphilicity and reducing its particle size, the MSRP-TA forms a dense interfacial layer, thereby improving emulsion stability and reducing curcumin photodegradation, synergized by the inherent antioxidant properties of TA. Specifically, the emulsions stabilized by MSRP-TA maintained a curcumin retention rate of over 90 % after 7 d of UV exposure. During in vitro digestion, the presence of TA facilitated the release of fatty acids and improved curcumin bioaccessibility, with the highest bioaccessibility reaching 72.60 ± 1.33 %. However, high proportions of TA could negatively affect curcumin bioaccessibility. The MSRP/TA ratio of 1:0.3 was optimal, balancing retention and bioaccessibility. Overall, this study provides a novel strategy for utilizing the by-product to stabilize Pickering emulsions, thereby enhancing the stability and bioaccessibility of curcumin

Introduction

Curcumin, a natural polyphenolic compound found in the rhizomes of Curcuma longa, possesses a wide range of biological activities, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties (Araiza-Calahorra, Akhtar, & Sarkar, 2018). However, its practical application is limited by its poor water solubility, low bioaccessibility, and susceptibility to degradation under environmental stresses such as light and pH variations (Pan-On, Dilokthornsakul, & Tiyaboonchai, 2022). To overcome these limitations, effective delivery carriers are needed to enhance its stability and bioaccessibility.

Pickering emulsions, stabilized by solid particles, are favored for their resistance to coalescence and biocompatibility (Xiao, Li, & Huang, 2016). Moreover, Pickering emulsions can be tailored to meet specific needs through the selection and design of stabilizing particles (Hu, Wang, Zhao, & Lu, 2025). Recent research has shifted toward replacing synthetic inorganic particles (e.g., silica) with organic particles derived from biopolymers such as starch, cellulose, and proteins in response to the demand for clean-label and sustainable products (Liang et al., 2025). Proteins are particularly attractive due to their unique physicochemical properties and excellent emulsifying abilities (Xu et al., 2025). However, their interfacial performance is highly susceptible to environmental conditions such as pH, ionic strength, and temperature. To overcome this, proteins are often complexed with polyphenols to enhance emulsion stability and bioactive protection (Ma, Yang, Huo, & Li, 2025). Moreover, these complexes can significantly alter the gastrointestinal fate of emulsions, influencing lipolysis kinetics, bioactive release, and ultimately, nutrient bioaccessibility. For instance, complexes involving proteins such as soy (Hui et al., 2024), pea (Huang et al., 2024), or zein (Chen et al., 2024) with various polyphenols have been shown to retard lipid digestion and sustain the release of bioactives.

Moringa seed residue, a by-product of moringa seed oil extraction, is an underutilized source of plant protein (Yang et al., 2025), containing over 35 % protein. In our previous research, we found that moringa seed residue protein (MSRP) can be used to stabilize Pickering emulsions, although these systems exhibited limited long-term stability (Huang et al., 2021). We subsequently addressed this issue by complexing MSRP with tannic acid (TA), which significantly improved emulsion stability (Huang et al., 2022). Building on this, the present study seeks to explore a critical and less understood aspect: how TA modification, specifically at varying MSRP:TA ratios, influences the delivery performance of MSRP-based Pickering emulsions, particularly regarding the stability and bioaccessibility of encapsulated curcumin.

While protein-polyphenol stabilizers are known, the novelty of this work lies in using TA-modified MSRP to enhance the bioaccessibility and protection of curcumin. Unlike other plant proteins (e.g., soy, pea, zein), MSRP is derived from an abundant processing by-product, aligning with circular economy principles, and has a distinct amino acid profile that may yield unique interfacial and digestive behaviors. In this study, we fabricated curcumin-loaded Pickering emulsions stabilized by MSRP and MSRP-TA composite particles at varying mass ratios (1,0, 1,0.1, 1,0.3, and 1,0.5) to elucidate how the MSRP/TA ratio modulates interfacial properties, curcumin protection, and digestion behavior. The research explored the storage stability of these curcumin-loaded Pickering emulsions, the protective effect of the emulsions against curcumin degradation, and the in vitro digestive characteristics of curcumin-loaded Pickering emulsions, and bioaccessibility of curcumin. The findings of this study can (1) advance the valorization of moringa seed residue, contributing to sustainable food production; (2) expand the repertoire of plant protein-based Pickering stabilizers; and (3) provide fundamental insights into designing MSRP-TA emulsions for enhanced stability and bioaccessibility of lipophilic bioactives like curcumin.

Materials and methods

Materials

Moringa seed residues were provided by Henan Gold Moringa Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Henan, China). Tannic acid (purity ≥98 %), porcine gastric mucin (M2378, purity ≥98 %), pepsin (P7000, ≥ 250 U/mg), pancreatic enzyme (P7545, ≥ 4000 U/g), and bile salts (B8631) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich, Inc. (St. Louis, MO, USA). Curcumin (purity ≥98 %) was purchased from Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The other chemical reagents were of analytical grade.

MSRP extraction

The extraction of MSRP was conducted according to the method reported by (Du et al., 2012) with slight modifications. Defatted powder of Moringa oleifera seed residue was mixed with 0.5 mol/L ammonia sulfate at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:10 (w/v), stirred at room temperature for 2 h, and then centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 15 min. The supernatant was collected by filtration. Ammonia sulfate was added to the supernatant to reach 80 % saturation, which was then kept at 4 °C for 2 h and centrifuged to obtain the precipitate. The precipitate was dialyzed in pure water at 4 °C for 5 d. The sample was then freeze-dried to obtain the MSRP. The yield and purity of the MSRP prepared by this method have been calculated in our previous study, with the yield being 4.24 % and the purity reaching 88.20 % (Huang et al., 2021).

Preparation of MSRP-TA composite particles

MSRP-TA composite particles were prepared according to the previously reported method (Huang et al., 2022). MSRP was dissolved in PBS (prepared from NaH2PO4 and Na2HPO4, pH 7.0, 0.01 M) and hydrated overnight to prepare a 2 % (w/v) MSRP stock solution. TA was added to PBS and stirred for 1 h until completely dissolved, yielding a 4 % (w/v) TA stock solution. The pH of the TA solution was adjusted to 7.0 using NaOH. The MSRP stock solution and the TA stock solution were then combined under continuous stirring to form MSRP-TA composite particles with varying ratios. The concentration of MSRP was maintained at 1 %, while the concentrations of TA were set at 0, 0.1 %, 0.3 %, and 0.5 %, respectively. These ratios were selected based on our previous research (Huang et al., 2022), which investigated the impact of various MSRP/TA ratios on emulsion stability but did not explore their effects on emulsion digestibility and the protection and delivery capabilities of active substances. Consequently, we chose several MSRP/TA ratios that exhibited significant differences in properties such as zeta potential and contact angle for further study.

Preparation of curcumin-loaded pickering emulsion

Curcumin-loaded Pickering emulsion was prepared based on the previously reported method with some modifications (Shah, Zhang, Li, & Li, 2016). Curcumin was added to MCT at a concentration of 0.6 % (w/w), heated to 120 °C and maintained for 30 s to dissolve curcumin. Given that curcumin's solubility is temperature-dependent yet prolonged heat treatment can induce its degradation (Araiza-Calahorra et al., 2018), we employed this high-temperature, short-duration method to enhance solubility while minimizing degradation. After cooling to room temperature, it was subsequently mixed with soybean oil at a mass ratio of 1:9, stirred at 300 rpm for 4 h, and centrifuged to obtain a clear oil phase enriched with curcumin. The aqueous phase consisted of PB solutions with different ratios of MSRP-TA composites (MSRP: 1 %, TA: 0, 0.1 %, 0.3 %, and 0.5 %). The oil phase and the aqueous phase were mixed at a ratio of 1:1 (w/w) and homogenized using an IKA Ultra-Turrax T25 homogenizer (IKA, Germany) at 14,000 rpm for 3 min to produce curcumin-loaded Pickering emulsions. All the aforementioned procedures were conducted under light-protected conditions to minimize the potential impact of light exposure on curcumin.

Evaluation of storage stability

The prepared Pickering emulsions were contained within glass tubes and incubated at 25 °C for 30 d to analyze their long-term physical stability. The particle size, microstructure, and visual appearance of the emulsions were measured as specific evaluation indexes (0, 7, 14, and 30 d).

Charge and size measurements

Emulsion samples were collected from each stage of the storage and in vitro digestion experiments. To avoid multiple scattering effects, samples were diluted 100-fold with PBS (adjusted to the same pH as the sample) before analysis. The zeta-potential of the emulsions was measured by a laser nanometer particle size analyzer (Zetasizer Nano ZSP, Malvern, U.K.) at 25 °C. The mean particle diameter (d4,3) and particle size distribution of the emulsions were determined by laser diffraction (Mastersizer 2000, Malvern, UK). Each test was conducted in triplicate, with each replicate consisting of 12 measurements.

Microstructure observation

Emulsion samples (10 μL) were taken from each stage of the storage and in vitro digestion experiments. After diluting with deionized water by a factor of 10, a 5-μL droplet was placed onto a glass slide. An optical microscope (OM, Leica DMI6000B, Germany) was employed to characterize the emulsion microstructure under a 40× objective lens. The microscopic images of the emulsions were captured using the digital camera attached to the microscope.

Determination of curcumin retention rate

The curcumin retention rate was determined based on a reported method with some modifications (Xu et al., 2024). Specifically, 10 mL sample of curcumin-rich emulsion was placed in a glass tube and sealed. The sealed test tube was stored at room temperature in the dark for 30 d, and the curcumin content was measured at 0, 0.5, 1, 3, 5, 7, 14, and 30 d, respectively. Samples were shaken for 30 s before all analyses to ensure uniformity. Emulsion sample (0.3 mL) was vortexed with 5.7 mL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to mix evenly. Then, 1 mL of n-hexane was added, and the mixture was vortexed again to dissolve curcumin in the organic solvent. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 500 ×g for 10 min, and the lower layer containing curcumin was collected. The extraction process was repeated three times. The combined curcumin solutions were dried under nitrogen gas and then dissolved in 1 mL DMSO for curcumin concentration determination. The absorbance of the sample was measured at 450 nm using a spectrophotometer. The content of curcumin was calculated from the curcumin standard curve (ŷ = 156.43×-0.0014, R2 = 0.9999). The retention rate (Rt) for curcumin is expressed as:(1)Rt=100×Ct/C0where C0 indicates the initial concentration of curcumin and Ct indicates the concentration of curcumin after storage for t d.

Effect of ultraviolet (UV) light on curcumin degradation

The ability of the emulsion to protect curcumin from UV-induced degradation was determined based on previously reported methods with some modifications (Cao et al., 2024). Curcumin-rich emulsion (10 mL) was placed in a glass tube and sealed. The sealed test tube was then positioned under a clean bench (equipped with 30 W UV light) for 7 d. The curcumin content and color were measured at 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 5, and 7 d, respectively.

In vitro simulated digestion

A three-stage simulated gastrointestinal tract (GIT) model (mouth, stomach, and intestine) was employed to evaluate the gastrointestinal fate of the curcumin-loaded emulsions according to the previously reported standardized in vitro methods (Wu et al., 2025). The changes in particle size, zeta potential, and microstructure of the emulsions during in vitro digestion were determined using the methods described in 2.5.1, 2.5.2, respectively.

Mouth phase: 30 mL of simulated saliva fluid (containing 3 mg/mL mucin and various minerals, the specific formulations are detailed in Table S1) were mixed with 30 mL of curcumin-loaded emulsion (diluted to an oil content of 2 %). The resulting mixture was then adjusted to pH 6.8 and incubated on a shaker at 37 °C for 5 min at 100 rpm to simulate agitation in the mouth.

Stomach phase: 35 mL of the sample from the mouth phase was mixed with 35 mL of pre-warmed (37 °C) simulated gastric fluid containing 3.2 mg/mL pepsin, the simulated gastric fluid was prepared by dissolving 2 g of NaCl and 7 mL of HCl in deionized water and making up to 1 L. The mixture was then adjusted to pH 2.5 and placed in a shaking incubator (100 rpm) for 2 h at 37 °C to replicate the stomach environment.

Intestine phase: 40 mL of the sample from the stomach phase was adjusted to pH 7.0, followed by adding 1.5 mL of simulated intestinal fluid (prepared by dissolving 5.5 g of CaCl₂·2H₂O and 32.87 g of NaCl in 150 mL of deionized water) and 3.5 mL of bile salt solution (0.1875 g of bile salts in 3.5 mL 5 mM pH 7.0 PBS) with constant stirring. The resulting mixture was then adjusted back to pH 7.00, and then 2.5 mL of pancreatic enzyme solution (24.0 mg/mL) was added. An automatic titration unit with a stirring propeller (Metrohm, USA Inc.) was used to monitor the pH and maintain it at pH 7.0 by titrating 0.1 mol/L NaOH solution into the reaction vessel for 2 h at 37 °C. The free fatty acids (FFAs) release rate was calculated from the titration curves using the following equation as a previous study (H. Hu, Wang, & Lu, 2024).(2)FFArelease rate%=100×VNaOHMNaOHMoil2Woilwhere Woil: weight of oil phase in the intestinal phase, VNaOH: volume of NaOH added, MNaOH: molar concentration of NaOH, Moil: molecular weight of oil phase.

Curcumin bioaccessibility

The bioaccessibility of the curcumin was measured using a previously reported method with some modifications (Wei, Zhou, Liu, & Li, 2025). Briefly, after completing the small intestine stage of digestion, the raw digesta was collected. Part of the sample was analyzed as is, while another part of the sample was centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 30 min), and the clear supernatant was considered the mixed micelle fraction. The curcumin concentrations in both the digesta and micelle fractions were measured spectrophotometrically as described earlier (section 2.5.3). The bioaccessibility (%) of the curcumin was calculated using the following expression:(3)Bioaccessibility%=CM/CD×100where, CM is the curcumin concentration in the mixed micelle phase collected from the digesta after centrifugation, and CD is the total curcumin concentration in the small intestine phase after digestion.

Statistical analysis

Each sample was prepared independently three times for testing. The data are presented as mean values ± standard deviation. Differences among the experimental results were analyzed using SPSS 17.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to assess significance at p < 0.05 by Tukey's HSD test.

Results and discussions

Storage stability of curcumin-loaded MSRP-TA Pickering emulsion

Droplet size variation

As shown in Fig. 1A, the average particle size of curcumin-loaded MSRP and MSRP-TA Pickering emulsions increased gradually during 30 d of storage. The average particle size of the emulsions decreased with higher TA concentrations in the system, indicating that TA addition improves the emulsifying capacity of biological particles and enhances emulsion stability (Wang et al., 2024). After 30 d of storage, the emulsion remained multimodal, but the peak width was narrowed and the particle size was relatively more uniform (Fig. 1B and C). A noteworthy phenomenon is that the peak of particle size distribution above 1000 μm in the MSRP-stabilized emulsion disappeared after 30 d of storage. This occurrence may be because the large droplets that were not effectively stabilized by MSRP particles further coalesced during storage to form a separate oil phase layer (observable in Fig. 3A), which was not recorded in the particle size measurements taken after 30 d. At the same time, the average particle sizes of curcumin-loaded MSRP-TA Pickering emulsions were 30.8, 27.3, and 16 μm, smaller than those of unloaded emulsions in our prior study (51.2, 34.8, and 18.2 μm for MSRP/TA ratios of 1:0.1, 1:0.3, and 1:0.5) (Z. Huang et al., 2022). Visual appearance

The visual appearance of MSRP and MSRP-TA Pickering emulsions loaded with curcumin before and after storage for 30 d was recorded (Fig. 3A). The MSRP emulsion initially appeared slightly layered, while the MSRP-TA Pickering emulsions were homogeneous. After 30 d of storage, the MSRP Pickering emulsion loaded with curcumin showed obvious delamination, with slight oil precipitation at the top observed, which was consistent with the microstructural performance. The MSRP/TA = 1:0.1 Pickering emulsion loaded with curcumin also exhibited slight stratification after 30 d. In contrast, the MSRP-TA Pickering emulsion with other TA concentrations did not show significant changes in appearance, indicating that the interface structure of the emulsion with relatively high TA concentration was more stable. In addition, after 30 d of storage, the emulsions can remain on the top of the sample bottle after inversion, showing a gel-like state.Fig. 3The visual appearance (A) and curcumin retention (B) of curcumin-loaded MSRP or MSRP-TA Pickering emulsions.Fig. 3

Curcumin retention capacity of MSRP-TA pickering emulsion

Light-shielding environment retention capacity

The change in curcumin content during storage in the dark can reflect the embedding effect of the emulsion (Zheng, Zhang, Chen, Luo, & McClements, 2017). Fig. 3B illustrates the retention of curcumin in MSRP and MSRP-TA Pickering emulsions. The retention rate of curcumin in the MSRP emulsion gradually decreased over time, reaching 70.38 % after 30 d of storage. The curcumin retention rate in the MSRP-TA Pickering emulsion also declined with longer storage time, with higher TA concentrations correlating with higher curcumin retention rates. Han et al. reported that the retention rate of curcumin in Pickering emulsions stabilized by chitosan/Arabic gum nanoparticles was lower than 30 % after 210 h of storage (Han, Chen, Gao, Zhang, & Tang, 2020). Shah et al. used Pickering emulsions made from tripolyphosphate and chitosan composite particles to encapsulate curcumin, achieving a retention rate of about 50 % after 5 d (Shah et al., 2016). These results indicate that the interface of the Pickering emulsion prepared with the MSRP-TA complex is relatively tight, effectively preventing the leakage of curcumin.

UV exposure retention capability

UV irradiation is a straightforward and effective non-thermal sterilization method used in food and beverage processing. However, many beneficial bioactive compounds in food, such as curcumin and carotene, are unstable under UV exposure (Wang et al., 2024). Therefore, the ability of both MSRP and MSRP-TA Pickering emulsions to protect curcumin from photodegradation was investigated (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Impact of UV-light on curcumin degradation in MSRP or MSRP-TA Pickering emulsions stored at ambient temperature, curcumin retention (A), L* color ratio (B), a* color ratio (C), and b* color ratio (D).Fig. 4

Impact of UV-light on curcumin degradation in MSRP or MSRP-TA Pickering emulsions stored at ambient temperature, curcumin retention (A), L* color ratio (B), a* color ratio (C), and b* color ratio (D).

Fig. 4A shows that the retention of curcumin in both MSRP and MSRP-TA emulsions decreased after UV exposure. It can be observed that the rate of this decrease was slower at higher TA concentrations. After 7 d of exposure, curcumin retention was 76.98 % in the MSRP emulsion, 86.38 % in the MSRP-TA (1:0.1) emulsion, 94.23 % in the MSRP-TA (1:0.3) emulsion, and 95.04 % in the MSRP-TA (1:0.5) emulsion, respectively. Thus, the protective ability of the Pickering emulsions against UV degradation of curcumin is ranked as follows: MSRP/TA = 1:0.5 > MSRP/TA = 1:0.3 > MSRP/TA = 1:0.1 > MSRP. Compared with some previously reported emulsion systems, the protective effect of MSRP/TA emulsion on curcumin is superior. For instance, the retention rate of curcumin in cellulose nanocrystal-stabilized Pickering emulsions is less than 80 % after 7 days of UV exposure (Aw et al., 2022). In addition, the retention rate of curcumin in emulsions stabilized by common surfactants, such as Tween 80 and Span 80, is less than 40 % after 7 days of exposure to light (Li et al., 2021).

This protective effect can be mechanistically deconstructed into two primary factors. Firstly, the relatively thin interface layer of the MSRP emulsion, which may have gaps between the particles (Sarkar, Kamaruddin, Bentley, & Wang, 2016), leaves curcumin molecules more exposed to UV radiation and accelerates their degradation compared to the other emulsions. For MSRP-TA composite particles, our previous work revealed that as the TA ratio increases, the particle size of MSRP-TA gradually decreases (Huang et al., 2022). Smaller particle sizes typically result in stronger interfacial adsorption and a thicker interfacial layer (Li et al., 2024). As the TA concentration increases, the interfacial layer of MSRP-TA emulsions becomes thicker, thereby reducing the exposure of curcumin to UV light. Previous studies have shown that the zein-pectin complex also forms dense interfacial network structures around emulsion droplets that can protect curcumin from UV-induced degradation (Zhou et al., 2018). Secondly, TA may function as a natural antioxidant, further delaying the degradation of curcumin (Serrano, Puupponen-Pimiä, Dauer, Aura, & Saura-Calixto, 2009). Under UV stress, reactive oxygen species (ROS) are often generated, which can accelerate the oxidative degradation of curcumin. Polyphenols, including TA can effectively scavenge these free radicals and ROS in the interfacial region, thereby quenching the degradation pathways before they can damage curcumin. This chemical protection works in concert with the physical barrier effect (Dias, Pinto, Freitas, Santos, & Silva, 2020).

Color significantly impacts the appearance of the product in food processing, and a stable color can enhance consumers' confidence in product quality. Therefore, we determined the color of the curcumin-loaded emulsion during UV light exposure (Fig. 4B). The L* value (luminosity) of curcumin-loaded MSRP-TA emulsions was lower than that of MSRP emulsions, indicating decreased brightness with higher TA concentrations. This may be due to the denser, more colored interfacial layer formed by MSRP-TA complexes, which affects light scattering and absorption (Dai et al., 2020). The a* value of the MSRP emulsion remained lower than that of the MSRP-TA emulsion, likely due to the reddish-brown appearance of the TA solution. The b* value of the MSRP emulsion initially increased and then decreased, indicating that curcumin was exposed and partially degraded. However, the b* value of the MSRP-TA emulsion decreased slightly, with higher TA concentrations yielding more stable yellow hues. This enhanced stability is attributed to TA's antioxidant properties and improved encapsulation efficiency, as evidenced by higher curcumin retention.

In vitro digestion of curcumin-loaded MSRP-TA pickering emulsion

Droplet size change in digestion

As illustrated in Fig. 5A, the particle size distribution of the emulsion exhibited a broader peak after oral digestion when compared to the original emulsion. A significant increase in the number of smaller-sized particles was observed, particularly in emulsions formulated with MSRP and MSR/TA = 1:0.1. This observation may be ascribed to the adsorption of negatively charged mucosal proteins in the oral cavity onto the interfacial layer of the droplets during simulated chewing. This adsorption could potentially enhance or modify the coverage of the interfacial layer. The interaction between the positively charged emulsion droplets and the negatively charged mucosal proteins likely contributed to the reduction in emulsion droplet sizes (Sarkar, Goh, & Singh, 2009).Fig. 5Particle size (A), ζ-potential (B), particle size distribution (C, D, E, and F) of curcumin-loaded MSRP emulsions or MSRP-TA emulsions at various digestion stages.Fig. 5

Particle size (A), ζ-potential (B), particle size distribution (C, D, E, and F) of curcumin-loaded MSRP emulsions or MSRP-TA emulsions at various digestion stages.

After gastric digestion, the average droplet size of the emulsion increased, with a significant rightward shift in the particle size distribution for emulsions prepared with MSRP and MSRP/TA = 1:0.1, indicating droplet aggregation. This aggregation may be attributed to several factors: (i) pepsin may have hydrolyzed a portion or all of the proteins covering the droplet interface; (ii) the high ionic strength of the gastric fluid could shield the electrostatic repulsion between droplets; and (iii) the low pH of approximately 2.5 during gastric digestion may alter the electrostatic interactions between droplets (Li et al., 2019).

At the beginning of intestinal digestion, the average particle size of the emulsion decreased (Fig. 5A), likely due to the increased electrostatic repulsion between droplets caused by the higher pH. Notably, the average particle sizes of emulsions composed of MSRP and MSRP/TA ratios of 1:0.1 decreased from 167 μm and 26.6 μm to 109 μm and 21.5 μm, respectively. This indicates the degradation and/or decomposition of oil droplets and proteins by trypsin and proteases during digestion. In contrast, the average particle sizes of the MSRP/TA ratios of 1:0.3 and 1:0.5 increased from 20.8 μm and 18.3 μm to 35.6 μm and 31.6 μm, respectively. This suggests that high TA concentrations may lead to the formation of larger colloidal particles due to the aggregation of insoluble calcium salts or mixed micelles. The phenomenon of increased emulsion droplet size after small intestinal digestion has also been reported in previous studies (Xu et al., 2024).

The droplet size distribution of the four emulsions at different digestion stages is shown in Figs. 5C–F. After gastric digestion, the MSRP and MSRP/TA = 1:0.1 emulsions exhibited a new particle size distribution peak that was much larger than that of the original emulsions, indicating poor stability of these two emulsions in the gastric environment. In contrast, the main particle size distribution peaks of the MSRP/TA = 1:0.3 and MSRP/TA = 1:0.5 emulsions remained close to those of the original emulsions after oral and gastric digestion, with only a broadening of the peaks observed after small intestinal digestion. This suggests that the MSRP/TA = 1:0.3 and MSRP/TA = 1:0.5 emulsions have a certain degree of stability in the gastric environment and exhibit the ability for intestinal targeted release.

Zeta-potential change

The variation in zeta-potential of the emulsions during digestion is depicted in Fig. 5B. Following oral digestion, the absolute charge of emulsions containing MSRP and MSRP/TA = 1:0.1 increased, transitioning from an initial positive charge to a negative charge. This suggests that the negatively charged mucin proteins interacted with the positively charged droplets, facilitating gradual electrostatic binding (Xu, Kan, et al., 2024). Conversely, the negative charges of emulsions with MSRP/TA = 1:0.3 and MSRP/TA = 1:0.5 decreased after oral digestion. This reduction can be attributed to the electrostatic shielding effect between the negatively charged interface of the MSRP-TA complexes and the mucin proteins, leading to a decrease in the absolute value of the negative charge (Wang et al., 2025). In a study investigating the digestive characteristics of soy protein-soy polysaccharide-EGCG complex stabilized emulsions, it was also found that the absolute value of the zeta potential of soy protein complex-stabilized emulsions was significantly lower than that of emulsions stabilized by soy protein alone after gastric digestion (Ke, Yang, & Li, 2025).

Following gastric digestion, the zeta-potential of all emulsions decreased, indicating that the low pH environment altered the electrostatic interactions between the droplets. Additionally, the high ionic strength of gastric juice may have further shielded the electrostatic repulsion between the droplets. In the early stages of simulated intestinal digestion, the negative charge of the emulsions increased, primarily due to the rise in pH. After intestinal digestion, all samples exhibited higher negative charges, likely attributable to the presence of anionic groups, such as mucin proteins, TA, enzymes, bile salts, and free fatty acids (Zhang, Zhang, Zhang, Decker, & McClements, 2015). Moreover, the MSRP/TA = 1:0.1 emulsion exhibited a higher negative charge compared to other emulsions. This may be attributed to the lack of cross-linking coverage among the groups within the system, which prevents mutual shielding. This finding is consistent with the results regarding droplet size.

Microstructure and visual appearance

Fig. 6 illustrates the changes in the microstructure and appearance of the emulsions during in vitro digestion. Following oral digestion, the structure of all emulsions remained relatively intact, with uniform droplet sizes observed in the three MSRP-TA emulsions. However, in the MSRP emulsion, some droplets floated to the surface due to gravitational separation. Following gastric digestion, the droplets in all emulsions increased in size, with the MSRP emulsions showing significant aggregation and structural breakdown. This is consistent with the results of droplet size distribution measurements (Fig. 5C). At the onset of intestinal digestion, the pH increased to 7, leading to the redispersion of emulsion droplets, which can be attributed to enhanced electrostatic repulsion. Interestingly, some droplets in the MSRP emulsion became smaller, but the disruption of droplet structure was irreversible, likely linked to the denaturation of MSRP that occurs during rapid pH changes. After small intestinal digestion, optical microscope images revealed that some oil droplets in the MSRP emulsion were not fully digested and contained irregular particles. This phenomenon may be due to the larger particle size and reduced specific surface area of the MSRP emulsion, which limits the contact area between trypsin and the fat droplets. Consequently, larger droplets require more time to be hydrolyzed by lipase (Ye et al., 2024). The microstructure of the three MSRP-TA emulsions after small intestinal digestion exhibited a small number of residual regular droplets and a large number of irregular particles. The appearance of all emulsions was a uniformly turbid liquid, further indicating the presence of irregular particles post-digestion. These irregular particles may include micelles, digestive enzymes, calcium saponins, crystals, and undigested oil droplets (Li et al., 2019).Fig. 6The optical microscope images and visual appearance of curcumin-loaded MSRP or MSRP–TA emulsions at various digestion stages. Release of FFAs

The FFA release from curcumin-loaded MSRP emulsions and MSRP-TA emulsions was assessed using a pH-stat method during the intestinal digestion phase, with the results presented in Figs. 7A-C. During intestinal digestion, the FFA release from all emulsions increased rapidly within a short period (Fig. 7A). However, the digestion profiles of the different emulsion systems exhibited slight variations. Fig. 7B illustrates the specific FFA release at the onset of intestinal digestion over 10 min: the FFA release for MSRP and MSRP/TA = 1:0.1, 1:0.3, and 1:0.5 emulsions was 34.39 ± 2.37 %, 57.16 ± 2.13 %, 66.44 ± 2.55 %, and 72.63 ± 0.87 %, respectively. These results demonstrate a trend of increasing FFA release rate with the increasing TA ratio. This trend is likely due to the fact that the increased TA ratio significantly reduces the droplet size of MSRP-TA stabilized emulsions. Smaller droplet sizes provide a larger specific surface area for lipase to contact the oil droplets, which is a well-established factor promoting faster lipid digestion. Although TA has been reported to have some ability to inhibit lipase activity (Li et al., 2023), droplet size is the primary factor influencing emulsion digestion. A similar phenomenon was also observed in previous research on TA-starch composite particles stabilizing Pickering emulsions (Hu, Wei, Liu, Bai, & Zeng, 2024).Fig. 7FFA released from curcumin-loaded MSRP or MSRP-TA Pickering emulsion at intestinal digestion (A), FFA released at the beginning of digestion (10 min, B), FFA released at the end of digestion (120 min, C), and bioaccessibility (D).Fig. 7

FFA released from curcumin-loaded MSRP or MSRP-TA Pickering emulsion at intestinal digestion (A), FFA released at the beginning of digestion (10 min, B), FFA released at the end of digestion (120 min, C), and bioaccessibility (D).

As the digestion time increased, the FFA release from the MSRP emulsion accelerated, while that from the MSRP-TA emulsion became progressively more moderate. This may be attributed to the fact that the lipid digestion in MSRP-TA emulsions has already approached saturation. In contrast, the MSRP particles in MSRP emulsions are partially hydrolyzed by trypsin, thereby exposing more oil droplet surfaces and accelerating the lipid digestion in MSRP emulsions during the later stages (Hu, Wang, & Lu, 2024). After 120 min of digestion, the lipids in all emulsions were nearly completely digested. The FFA release for MSRP and MSRP/TA = 1:0.1, 1:0.3, and 1:0.5 emulsions was 98.88 ± 3.77 %, 97.65 ± 3.72 %, 95.65 ± 2.82 %, and 96.50 ± 2.03 %, respectively, with no significant differences observed among them (Fig. 7C).

Bioaccessibility of curcumin

The bioaccessibility of curcumin stabilized by MSRP emulsion and MSRP-TA emulsion is depicted in Fig. 7D. The bioaccessibility values for MSRP and MSRP/TA = 1:0.1, 1:0.3, and 1:0.5 emulsions were 50.65 ± 2.85 %, 72.60 ± 1.33 %, 63.81 ± 2.51 %, and 56.44 ± 1.59 %, respectively. The order from highest to lowest was MSRP/TA = 1:0.1 > MSRP/TA = 1:0.3 > MSRP/TA = 1:0.5 > MSRP. In most cases, there is a positive correlation between the bioaccessibility of active substances loaded in emulsions and the release rate of free FFA during small intestinal digestion (Araiza-Calahorra et al., 2018). However, in this study, the three emulsions with MSRP/TA ratios of 1:0.1, 1:0.3, and 1:0.5 showed no significant differences in FFA release rates. Instead, their bioaccessibility decreased with increasing MSRP/TA ratios. This non-monotonic trend can be mechanistically explained by the interplay between two competing processes: the liberation of curcumin from the lipid phase and its subsequent degradation/precipitation in the aqueous environment. While the final extent of lipolysis was similar across all MSRP/TA emulsions, the kinetics of the initial digestion phase were accelerated at higher TA ratios (Fig. 7B). A faster initial digestion rate leads to the rapid collapse of the emulsion droplet structure, causing a large burst of curcumin to be released into the intestinal fluid in a short time. This surge exceeds the instantaneous solubilization capacity of the mixed micelles, leaving a significant fraction of curcumin exposed to the harsh intestinal environment (pH, oxygen, enzyme, etc.). This promotes its chemical degradation. In addition, some curcumin may form complexes with MSRP or other proteins, leading to aggregation and precipitation (Molet-Rodríguez, Martín-Belloso, & Salvia-Trujillo, 2025). As a result, the amount of curcumin retained in the micellar layer at the end of digestion decreased. In summary, the decline at higher TA ratios is attributed to this kinetic mismatch, where rapid digestion outstrips the system's ability to protect and solubilize the released curcumin.

Compared with other Pickering stabilizers, the ability of MSRP-TA to significantly enhance bioaccessibility (up to 72.6 %) is noteworthy. Many Pickering stabilizers, such as cellulose, exhibit poor bioaccessibility due to their low lipolysis degree in the emulsions they stabilize, which is attributed to their difficulty in being displaced by bile salts (Li et al., 2022). Although some modified Pickering emulsion stabilizers (like modified starch) can highly improve bioaccessibility (Choi, Shin, & Kim, 2025), they often lack additional functional advantages. The MSRP-TA system seems to achieve a good balance: it provides sufficient interfacial stability to protect curcumin, promotes efficient lipid digestion to form micelles, and offers antioxidant protection, all of which collectively enhance bioaccessibility.

Additionally, considering the previous observations that the emulsion with an MSRP/TA ratio of 1:0.3 already had a protective ability against curcumin degradation under dark and UV exposure conditions close to that of the emulsion with an MSRP/TA ratio of 1:0.5, and that it also had a certain resistance to gastric acid, the MSRP/TA ratio of 1:0.3 may be an excellent choice that can both enhance the anti-degradation ability and improve the bioaccessibility of curcumin.

Although this study has elucidated the effects of different ratios of TA and MSPR on the stability, digestion characteristics, etc., of curcumin-loaded TA-MSPR emulsions, there are some limitations in this study that can be addressed in future research. For instance, future studies could employ more advanced interfacial characterization techniques to investigate the role of the MSPR-TA interfacial layer in these effects. The presence of multiple substances in simulated intestinal fluid means that the current study is unable to accurately explore the specific mechanisms by which the TA ratio affects the bioaccessibility of active compounds loaded in MSPR-TA emulsions. This area also presents a promising direction for future research. Moreover, in vitro digestion experiments always have differences compared with the in vivo environment. Future work should include in vivo experiments, such as pharmacokinetic studies, to verify the effect of MSRP/TA emulsions on the bioaccessibility of curcumin.

Conclusion

In summary, composite particles were prepared by combining protein extracted from moringa seed residue with tannic acid at varying ratios to stabilize Pickering emulsions and enhance curcumin's stability and bioaccessibility. The results indicated that the Pickering emulsions stabilized by MSRP-TA complexes demonstrated excellent storage stability, primarily attributed to enhanced interfacial adsorption and the formation of smaller droplet sizes. Moreover, the MSRP-TA complex provided excellent protection against curcumin degradation, with a retention rate of over 90 % after 7 d of UV exposure at MSRP-TA ratios of 1:0.3 and 1:0.5, likely due to the thicker interfacial layer and the inherent antioxidant activity of TA. In vitro digestion studies revealed that the emulsions stabilized by the MSRP-TA complex displayed gastric stability and targeted intestinal release. Although a higher TA ratio (1:0.5) accelerated initial lipid digestion, the emulsion at the MSRP/TA ratio of 1:0.3 achieved a good balance between rapid digestion and degradation protection, resulting in moderate bioaccessibility. Overall, the MSRP/TA ratio of 1:0.3 was found to be optimal, combining good storage stability, UV protection, and moderate bioaccessibility. These findings offer a novel application for moringa seed residue, highlighting the dual role of TA in enhancing emulsion stability and antioxidant capacity, as well as the potential of MSRP-TA-stabilized Pickering emulsions as delivery systems for lipophilic bioactive compounds.
