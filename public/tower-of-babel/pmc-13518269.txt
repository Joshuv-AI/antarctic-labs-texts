Qihuang Needle Therapy for Motor Symptoms in Parkinson's Disease: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Wanqing Peng, Renhui Zhao, Ziting Huang, Jingpei Zhou, Yanning Liu, Zhijuan Liu, Xinyu Li, Jingjing Deng, Xubo Hong, Yanfang Chen, Nanbu Wang, Zhenhu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMotor symptoms in Parkinson's disease (PD) often respond incompletely to medication, highlighting the need for effective adjunctive therapies. Acupuncture may offer benefits, but high‐quality evidence for specialized techniques such as Qihuang needle therapy is lacking.ObjectiveOur goal was to evaluate the efficacy of Qihuang needle therapy for improving motor symptoms in PD patients.MethodsThis single‐center, randomized, single‐blind, sham‐controlled trial, enrolled 140 patients with idiopathic PD, who were assigned to either Qihuang needle therapy or sham acupuncture for nine sessions over 6 weeks, in addition to their stable medications. The primary outcome was the change in the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale Part III (UPDRS‐III) score from baseline to week 6. Secondary outcomes included changes in the Non‐Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS) and the 39‐item Parkinson's Disease Questionnaire (PDQ‐39).ResultsOverall, 129 participants (92.1%) completed the trial. The Qihuang needle group showed significantly greater improvement in UPDRS‐III at week 6 (between‐group difference: 8.45 points; 95% confidence interval [CI], 5.49–11.42; P < 0.001), sustained at 10‐week follow‐up (10.88 points; 95% CI, 7.89–13.87; P < 0.001). Significant improvements were also noted in non‐motor symptoms (NMSS) and quality of life (PDQ‐39) at both time points (all P < 0.001). Greatest efficacy was observed in postural instability and gait disorder subtypes.ConclusionsQihuang needle therapy significantly improved motor and non‐motor symptoms and quality of life in PD patients compared with that via sham acupuncture, with benefits sustained for ≥1 month. These findings support Qihuang needle therapy as a safe and effective adjunctive treatment for PD. © 2026 The Author(s). Parkinson's disease (PD) is characterized by progressive motor symptoms, including bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor, and postural instability, 1 and represents a substantial and growing public health challenge worldwide. 2 Levodopa‐based therapy remains the pharmacological cornerstone, but long‐term use is limited by motor and non‐motor complications, and neither medical nor surgical therapy modifies disease progression, 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 highlighting the need for effective adjunctive treatments to improve symptom control and reduce medication burden. 7

Acupuncture has garnered increasing attention for its potential to alleviate motor and non‐motor symptoms of PD. 8 , 9 , 10 Nevertheless, existing evidence is heterogeneous, and the effects of standardized acupuncture protocols on core motor deficits require further rigorous validation. 9 , 10

Qihuang needle (QHN) therapy is a contemporary acupuncture system featuring a hollow, oval‐tipped needle that enhances stiffness while minimizing trauma. Guided by the classical theory from the Huangdi Neijing, QHN uses a diagnostic–therapeutic trinity: meridian differentiation, precise point selection, and targeted needling techniques. Treatments are brief, non‐retained, and typically target 3 to 5 points per session, aiming to achieve strong Deqi through deep mechanical stimulation. This approach contrasts sharply with conventional multi‐point, adjuvant electricity, and retained acupuncture. Preliminary reports suggest that QHN therapy improves motor function in PD, 11 however, robust evidence from high‐quality randomized controlled trials is lacking. Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of QHN therapy for motor symptoms in PD using a rigorously designed, sham‐controlled trial and to provide robust evidence for integrating QHN into PD rehabilitation programs.

Methods

Study Design

This single‐center, prospective, randomized, single‐blind, sham‐controlled trial was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine from July 12, 2023 to April 12, 2025. The trial protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the hospital (approval no. K‐2023‐036) before participant enrollment. The study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and is registered (ChiCTR2300072690). The trial was reported according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. 12 All participants provided written informed consent. The full study protocol is provided in Supporting information S1.

Participants

Participants were recruited from the study center's outpatient clinics, online platforms and community advertisements. The inclusion criteria were (1) idiopathic PD diagnosis according to the Movement Disorder Society clinical diagnostic criteria 13 ; (2) 60–80 years old; (3) disease duration of 1 year or more, with a stable regimen of anti‐Parkinson's medication for ≥2 months before to enrollment; (4) Hoehn and Yahr stages 1 to 4; (5) conscious with stable vital signs, no significant hearing impairment, and no significant cognitive impairment (Mini‐Mental State Examination score ≥24); and (6) signed written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) secondary parkinsonism or Parkinson‐plus syndromes (eg, multiple system atrophy and progressive supranuclear palsy); (2) severe comorbid conditions affecting the cardiac, hepatic, renal, or hematological systems, or any active malignancy; (3) participation in any other interventional clinical trial within the preceding 3 months; and (4) a history of severe psychiatric disorders or any other condition that would present a significant communication barrier.

Randomization and Blinding

Eligible participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the treatment or control group. The randomization sequence was computer‐generated using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Crop, Armonk, NY, USA) by an independent statistician not involved in participant recruitment or intervention. The sequence was placed in sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes to ensure allocation concealment. Participants, outcome assessors responsible for administering the clinical rating scales, and the data statistician were all blinded to group assignments throughout the trial. The acupuncturists administering the treatments could not be blinded because of the nature of the interventions, however, they had no role in outcome assessment or data analysis. The treatments for the two groups were administered in separate rooms, and interaction between participants from different groups was discouraged to minimize potential bias. After the final intervention session, participants were asked to guess their group assignment (“real acupuncture,” “sham acupuncture,” or “unsure”) to assess the success of blinding, which was subsequently analyzed using a χ2 test.

Intervention

Participants maintained a stable regimen of anti‐Parkinson medication throughout the trial. Any medication change was recorded as a protocol deviation. The levodopa equivalent daily dose was calculated at baseline and any adjustments were meticulously recorded in the case report forms.

Participants in both groups received a total of nine treatment sessions over 6 weeks, adhering to the following schedule: twice per week for the initial 3 weeks, and once per week for the subsequent 3 weeks. All treatments were administered by licensed acupuncturists with 5 or more years of clinical experience, who had undergone standardized training in the trial‐specific protocols to ensure consistency.

Treatment Group

Participants in the treatment group received QHN therapy in addition to their usual medication. The QHN is a single‐use, sterile acupuncture needle (Chongqing Medical Device Approval: 20202200072; 0.5 × 50 mm) (Fig. S1 in Supporting information S1). Its unique design, featuring a hollow body, a transparent handle, and an oval tip, is intended to enhance mechanical strength and facilitate the elicitation of a pronounced Deqi sensation.

Three acupoint formulas of bilateral acupoints were alternated: formula 1: Shousanli (LI10), Yangchi (TE4), Qihaishu (BL24), Xiyangguan (GB33), Feiyang (BL58); formula 2: Xiaochan (Extra Point), Daling (PC7), Ciliao (BL32), Weizhong (BL40), Chengshan (BL57); and formula 3: Sidu (TE9), Yangxi (LI5), Pishu (BL20), Juliao (GB29), Yanglingquan (GB34). Acupoints were located according to the World Health Organization Western Pacific Region standards, 14 Xiaochan (Extra Point) is an empirical extra point used in our clinical practice. The alternation helps comprehensively regulate different meridians and avoid local adaptation to repeated stimulation at the same points. For the QHN procedure, following sterile skin preparation, needles were inserted perpendicularly to a depth of approximately 0.5 to 1.5 B‐cun. A standardized manual stimulation technique was applied to achieve Deqi. Subsequently, the unique “Hegu” needling technique—involving angling the needle approximately 30° superiorly and inferiorly from the original insertion point—was performed before needle withdrawal. Detailed descriptions of acupoint locations and manipulations are provided in Table S1 in Supporting information S1 and Figure S2 in Supporting information S2.

Control Group

Control group participants received sham acupuncture alongside their usual medication. The same type of needle was used to ensure participant blinding. However, the needles were inserted shallowly at non‐acupoint sites located 1 to 3 cm laterally from the true acupoints used in the treatment group. Crucially, no manual stimulation was performed to elicit Deqi, and needles were retained for only approximately 5 seconds before withdrawal. This method was designed to mimic the tactile sensation of real acupuncture while minimizing any specific physiological effects. The locations of all sham points and manipulations are specified in Table S2 in Supporting information S1 and Figure S2 in Supporting information S2. On completion of the 10‐week follow‐up assessment, all participants in the control group were offered a complimentary course of nine authentic QHN therapy sessions.

Clinical Assessments

All clinical scale assessments were conducted in the afternoon during the patient's “on” medication state. A single team of trained and blinded neurologists performed all assessments; and the same rater evaluated the same participant at all time points.

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome was the change in Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale part III (UPDRS‐III) 15 scores from baseline to week 6 (post‐treatment) and week 10 (follow‐up). The UPDRS‐III contains 14 items evaluating key motor aspects, such as facial expression, tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, postural instability, and gait. Each item is scored from 0 to 4, with total scores ranging from 0 to 56. Higher scores indicate more severe motor symptoms.

Secondary Outcomes

Secondary outcomes were measured using the Non‐Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS) 16 and the 39‐item Parkinson's Disease Questionnaire (PDQ‐39) 17 at baseline, week 6, and week 10.

The NMSS assesses nine domains of non‐motor symptoms (eg, cardiovascular, sleep/fatigue, and mood/cognition) through 30 items. Each item is rated by severity (0–3) and frequency (1–4), with a total score ranging from 0 to 120, and higher scores indicate greater non‐motor symptom burden.

The PDQ‐39 evaluates health‐related quality of life across eight dimensions (eg, mobility, activities of daily living, emotional well‐being, and social support) via 39 items scored on a 5‐point frequency scale (0–4). Total scores range from 0 to 156, with higher scores indicating poorer quality of life.

Exploratory Outcomes

Exploratory biomechanical assessments were performed at baseline and week 6 in a prespecified subset of 26 participants per group. Patients at Hoehn and Yahr stage 4 were excluded from this subset owing to walking aid use.

Spatiotemporal gait parameters and balance were assessed using the Footscan plantar pressure analysis system (RSscan International). Key measures included plantar force distribution, center of pressure (CoP) trajectory, and temporal–spatial gait parameters. Improved gait performance was defined as increased stride length and gait speed, and reduced gait cycle time. Enhanced postural stability was reflected by reduced CoP sway area.

Muscle stiffness was objectively assessed using shear‐wave elastography (SWE) (Aplio i800, Canon Medical Systems) by measuring the mean longitudinal Young's modulus of the bilateral biceps brachii, brachioradialis, and lateral gastrocnemius muscles. Higher values suggested increased tissue hardness and muscle tone. For each muscle, the average Young's modulus of both sides was analyzed.

Others

Adverse events (AEs), such as needle fainting, hematoma, or infection, were monitored and recorded throughout the trial. All AEs were assessed for severity and relationship with the intervention. Participants were withdrawn if continuing the trial was deemed to pose a significant risk.

Statistical Analysis

The sample size was determined a priori using PASS software (version 15.0.5; NCSS). Based on a pilot study, we anticipated a mean change in UPDRS‐III scores of 11.3 (standard deviation [SD], 12.33) and 4.25 (SD, 11.3) in the treatment and control groups, respectively. Overall, 116 participants (58 per group) were required to achieve 90% power at a two‐sided significance level of α = 0.05, allowing for a 20% dropout rate.

Data were analyzed using R software (version 4.5.1; R Foundation). The significance level was α = 0.05 (two‐sided). Baseline comparisons used independent t tests for normally distributed continuous variables, Mann–Whitney U tests for non‐normally distributed continuous variables, and χ2 tests or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables. The primary analysis was performed according to the intention‐to‐treat principle, including all randomized participants. Linear mixed‐effects models were fitted to five multiply imputed datasets that had been generated under the missing‐at‐random assumption to handle missing data, with baseline values and prognostic variables included as covariates. The models included fixed effects for group, time (baseline, week 6, week 10), and their interaction with a random subject intercept. The models estimated the least‐squares mean changes from baseline within each group and the mean differences between groups at each time point, along with their 95% confidence intervals (CI). A per‐protocol set analysis was also conducted, comprising participants who completed at least seven treatment sessions and the week 6 assessment. For exploratory biomechanical outcomes assessed only at baseline and week 6, an analysis of covariance was performed, adjusting for baseline values. Supporting information S1 provides details on sample size calculation and statistical analysis. Subgroup analysis was performed according to motor subtypes defined by the Jankovic classification method, 18 adapted for UPDRS‐III. A treatment‐by‐subtype interaction term was included in the linear mixed‐effects model. Detailed calculations are in Supporting information S2 (Methods S1).

Results

Participants

Altogether, 205 PD patients were assessed for eligibility, 140 participants (68.3%) met the inclusion criteria and were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the treatment (n = 70) or the control group (n = 70). During the study period, 11 participants (7.9%) discontinued treatment, with one withdrawal in the treatment group and 10 in the control group. Figure 1 provides the participant flow.

Study flowchart.

Overall, 129 participants (92.1%) completed the intervention and follow‐up assessments. Participants' age ranged from 60 to 79 years, with a mean (SD) of 67.9 (5.4) years, and 78 (55.7%) were male. The median disease duration was 5.3 years. Baseline characteristics were well‐balanced between the two groups (Table 1). Baseline characteristics of the exploratory biomechanical subset (n = 52) and three subtype groups were also balanced (Tables S1 and S2 in Supporting information S2).

Primary Outcome

The change in the UPDRS‐III score from baseline to week 6 was −13.78 (95% CI, −15.83 to −11.73) in the treatment group, compared with −5.32 (95% CI, −7.46 to −3.19) in the control group. The between‐group difference was 8.45 points (95% CI, 5.49–11.42; P < 0.001), favoring the QHN therapy (Table 2, Fig. 2A). This treatment benefit was maintained and increased at the 10‐week follow‐up. The between‐group difference at week 10 was 10.88 points (95% CI, 7.89–13.87; P < 0.001), reflecting further improvement in the treatment group and a reduction in improvement in the control group (Table 2, Fig. 2D). Per‐protocol analysis yielded consistent results (Table S3 in Supporting information S2).

Secondary Outcomes

Significant improvements in favor of QHN were also observed across all secondary outcomes. For health‐related quality of life measured using the PDQ‐39, the between‐group difference was 12.02 points at week 6 (95% CI, 7.53–16.51; P < 0.001) and increased to 19.36 points at week 10 (95% CI, 14.89–23.83; P < 0.001) (Table 2, Fig. 2C,F). Similarly, for non‐motor symptoms assessed using the NMSS, the treatment group demonstrated substantially greater improvement, with a between‐group difference of 14.63 points at week 6 (95% CI, 8.08–21.18; P < 0.001) and 20.93 points at week 10 (95% CI, 14.31–27.56; P < 0.001) (Table 2, Fig. 2B,E). Results from the per‐protocol analysis were congruent (Table S3 in Supporting information S2).

Subgroup Analysis

A subgroup analysis based on PD motor subtypes revealed a significant treatment‐by‐subtype interaction for both the UPDRS‐III (P < 0.001) and NMSS (P < 0.001), indicating a differential treatment effect. Patients with postural instability and gait disorder dominant (PIGD‐D) subtype showed the greatest motor improvement (between‐group difference in UPDRS‐III: −12.10; 95% CI, −18.64 to −5.55; P = 0.01), followed by mixed (mixed: −7.53; 95% CI, −13.00 to −2.06; P = 0.02) and tremor‐dominant (T‐D: −4.00; 95% CI, −8.20 to 0.21; P = 0.06) (Fig. 3). A similar pattern was observed for non‐motor symptoms (NMSS), with the largest effect in PIGD (between‐group difference: −21.54; 95% CI, −35.32 to −7.75; P = 0.01). The quality of life (PDQ‐39) improved significantly in all subtypes except mixed, with no significant interaction (P = 0.221). Detailed results are in Table S4 (Supporting information S2).

Exploratory Outcomes

In the biomechanical subset (n = 26 per group), no significant between‐group differences were observed in gait parameters or most muscle stiffness measures. Objective assessment of muscle stiffness via SWE revealed a significant reduction in biceps brachii stiffness within the treatment group, with a mean change of −16.45 kPa (95% CI, −21.68 to −11.22; P < 0.001) and a between‐group difference of −8.50 kPa (95% CI, −15.00 to −2.01; P = 0.011). No significant between‐group differences were observed in other muscle groups or balance parameters (Table S5 in Supporting information S2).

Blinding Credibility Assessment

The success of participant blinding was assessed after the intervention was completed. The distribution of participant guesses regarding their group assignment did not differ significantly between the QHN and sham acupuncture groups (χ2 = 3.1, P = 0.21). A high and comparable proportion of participants in both groups believed they had received true acupuncture (66.7% in the treatment group vs. 51.7% in the control group), indicating that the sham procedure was perceptually credible and that blinding was adequately maintained (Table S6 in Supporting information S2).

AEs

Safety monitoring throughout the trial recorded 33 AEs among 19 participants (13.6%), with a higher incidence in the treatment group (69.7% vs. 30.3%). The vast majority of events were mild and transient, consisting primarily of bleeding or minor hematoma at the needle insertion site. No serious AEs were reported in either group, and no participant withdrew from the study owing to an AE (Table S7 in Supporting information S2).

Discussion

This randomized clinical trial provides robust evidence that QHN therapy, as an adjunct to conventional medication, significantly improved motor function, non‐motor symptoms, and quality of life in PD patients. The between‐group difference in the UPDRS‐III score of 8.45 points at the primary endpoint was not only was statistically significant, but also surpassed the established minimal clinically important difference. 19 Subgroup analysis revealed greater efficacy in patients with PIGD‐D and mixed subtypes than in those with T‐D.

The discrepancy between significant improvements in UPDRS‐III scores and the lack of change in most objective gait parameters warrants careful interpretation. UPDRS‐III is a multidimensional scale that encompasses the primary targets of QHN therapy. Clinical observations and video analysis suggested improvements in axial symptoms (eg, turning and posture) that may not be fully captured by planar gait analysis, suggesting that QHN therapy's benefits may be more pronounced in improving motor quality (eg, tone, speed, and coordination) rather than specific gait metrics. Future studies should integrate more comprehensive motion capture systems to better quantify these multidimensional benefits.

The control group exhibited a discernible short‐term improvement at week 6, which attenuated during the follow‐up period. This pattern is characteristic of the potent contextual effects inherent in acupuncture trials, encompassing patient expectations and the ritual of therapy. 20 A study demonstrated that non‐penetrating sham needles themselves produce a “specific effect” that can account for approximately 18.9% of the total treatment effect of acupuncture, potentially leading to an underestimation of verum acupuncture's efficacy. 21 Within this framework, the improvement in our control group is likely a composite of a classic placebo response and this insert‐specific physiological effect. Consequently, the significant and sustained superiority of QHN therapy indicates a genuine, specific treatment effect that persists beyond the dissipation of non‐specific contextual factors. Our results likely represent a conservative estimate of the true efficacy of QHN therapy.

Acupuncture may inhibit neuroinflammation and gut dysbiosis, protect dopaminergic neurons from oxidative damage, and modulate aberrant neural circuitry. 22 , 23 , 24 Our exploratory biomechanical and subgroup analyses suggests that QHN therapy is particularly effective against rigidity and gait disturbance. The reduction in biceps brachii stiffness measured by SWE in the treatment group provides tangible evidence of the capacity of QHN to alleviate muscular rigidity. This effect is consistent with the superior clinical efficacy observed in patients with PIGD‐D subtypes. We hypothesize that the deep, peritendinous stimulation of QHN may predominantly modulates the dysfunctional nigrostriatal and basal ganglia output circuits that underpin bradykinesia and postural instability, 25 by enhancing GABAergic inhibition in the substantia nigra pars reticulata, thereby disinhibiting thalamocortical motor pathways and improving voluntary movement execution. 26 , 27 The relative lack of efficacy in the T‐D subtype aligns with the predominant role of cerebello‐thalamo‐cortical circuits in tremor, 28 which may be less susceptible to this intervention. Therefore, these findings form a plausible hypothesis that links peripheral biomechanical improvement to the selective central neuromodulation of specific motor pathways.

Our findings align with those of previous reports on the benefits of on acupuncture in PD, 29 , 30 but extend the evidence by highlighting the superior effects of a perimuscular needling technique. Unlike conventional acupuncture, QHN requires less operation time with deeper and more focused stimulation, which may explain its stronger effects on rigidity and gait impairment compared with those of broader needling approaches. 8 This positions QHN as a distinct and potentially more potent therapeutic modality for specific motor deficits in PD and not merely a form of acupuncture.

This study has several strengths. First, the study was designed and reported in strict adherence to the CONSORT and Standards for Reporting Interventions in Clinical Trials of Acupuncture (STRICTA) guidelines for non‐pharmacologic trials, ensuring a high standard of methodological rigor and transparency. 31 , 32 The successful implementation and validation of participant blinding is also a pivotal strength. Penetrating sham needles are now recognized to have specific effects. The use of these needles was crucial for maintaining the perceptual credibility of the intervention. The high and comparable proportion of participants in both groups believed they had received real acupuncture confirms the potency of the contextual effects elicited by the procedure and validates the sham control as an active comparator. 33 , 34 Furthermore, our comprehensive assessment strategy, which integrated well‐validated clinical scales with exploratory objective biomechanical measures, provides a multi‐faceted evaluation of treatment efficacy. Motor subtype analysis provide valuable insights for potential personalized application of QHN therapy.

This study has some limitations. First, the single‐center design, although ensuring standardization of the complex QHN intervention, may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader PD populations and different healthcare settings. 35 Second, the 10‐week follow‐up period is insufficient to determine the long term benefits or optimal frequency of maintenance sessions. Third, although we have proposed plausible neurobiological mechanisms based on extant literature, the absence of direct measures means our mechanistic explanations remain hypothetical and require direct experimental confirmation. Fourth, objective biomechanical assessments were conducted in a prespecified subset owing to resource constraints, and this limited the statistical power to detect more modest effects on gait and balance, affecting the generalizability of these specific outcomes. Fifth, the higher dropout rate in the control group could have slightly inflated the estimated treatment effect. Finally, as is inherent in most acupuncture trials, the acupuncturists could not be blinded, introducing potential performance bias. However, blinding of outcome assessors and statisticians combined with a validated sham procedure, mitigates this concern. That over 50% of participants believed they received real acupuncture suggests that the sham procedure was perceptually credible and potent in inducing placebo responses. 36 A further consideration arises from the evolving understanding of sham acupuncture controls. The inserted sham needle we used is now recognized to have its insert‐specific effect. 21 This insight reframes the significant between‐group difference we observed and likely represents a conservative estimate of the true specific effect of QHN.

Future studies should include multicenter trials with larger samples and longer follow‐up, explore alternative sham methods for QHN, and incorporate neuroimaging and biomarker analyses to elucidate central mechanisms. Investigating the synergistic effects of QHN and pharmacotherapy or rehabilitation will also be of significant interest.

In conclusion, this randomized clinical trial demonstrates that QHN therapy significantly improved motor function, reduced non‐motor symptoms, and enhances quality of life in PD patients compared with that via sham acupuncture. These clinical‐scale improvements are promising; however, the discrepancy with objective gait measures calls for further investigation. These findings suggest that QHN therapy may warrants consideration as part of a comprehensive management strategy for PD, particularly for patients with PIGD‐D subtypes.
