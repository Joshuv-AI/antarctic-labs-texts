T‐Cell Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia in a Young Patient With Andersen–Tawil Syndrome Successfully and Safely Treated With Intensive Chemotherapy Including Potential Precipitating Drugs: A Case Report After 3.5 Years of Follow‐up
Authors: Ramy Rahmé, Paule Moussounda Bamba, Thi Thuy Duong Do, Anthony Behin, Estelle Gandjbakhch, Claude Gardin, Thorsten Braun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAndersen–Tawil syndrome (ATS) is a rare, hereditary channelopathy characterized by periodic paralysis, cardiac arrhythmias, and sometimes developmental anomalies. No association with hematologic malignancies has previously been reported. We describe the case of a 27‐year‐old man with genetically confirmed Type 1 ATS who developed T‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia. He was treated according to the GRAALL‐2014 protocol and achieved sustained complete molecular remission without allogeneic transplantation after 3.5 years of follow‐up. Management required careful adaptation to mitigate ATS‐related risks. Specifically, QT‐prolonging and neurotoxic agents were avoided or substituted, glucose infusions were minimized, and acetazolamide was introduced early. Despite exposure to high‐dose corticosteroids and anthracyclines, only moderate, self‐limited paralytic episodes occurred during intensive phases. During maintenance, ventricular ectopy and QT prolongation prompted chemotherapy dose adjustments and beta‐blocker therapy, leading to rapid normalization. This case highlights the feasibility of delivering intensive chemotherapy in ATS with tailored supportive care. Although likely coincidental, this unprecedented association raises questions about potential links between ion channel dysfunction and leukemogenesis.

Andersen–Tawil syndrome (ATS) is a rare orphan genetic channelopathy. Its prevalence is estimated to be 1 in 1 million people worldwide. Loss‐of‐function mutations in the KCNJ2 gene are responsible for 60% of cases and define Type 1 ATS. The KCNJ2 gene encodes for an inward‐rectifier potassium channel known as Kir2.1 that participates in establishing action potential waveform and excitability of neuronal, skeletal muscle, and cardiac muscle tissues [1]. Clinically, this syndrome is characterized by a triad of manifestations: (1) periodic paralysis; (2) cardiac electrical changes (prolonged QT interval, ventricular arrhythmias); and (3) various developmental abnormalities. The signs and symptoms of ATS vary widely, and can be different even among affected members of the same family. Affected individuals present in the first or second decade of life with either cardiac symptoms or muscle weakness [1].

Several factors have been identified as responsible for the occurrence of paralytic attacks with significant cardiac risk [2]. Rest following strenuous physical activity, carbohydrate ingestion, emotional stress, and exposure to cold are notable precipitating triggers. In addition, treatment with corticosteroids greatly increases the risk with some life‐threatening complications, such as quadriplegia and respiratory distress requiring intubation [3, 4]. Medications known to prolong QT intervals must be avoided. Clinical manifestations can be observed regardless of serum potassium levels. Preventive management of ATS includes: control of the contributing factors (i.e., lifestyle or dietary); administration of oral potassium if hypo‐ or normokalemia is documented during attacks; mild exercise, which may shorten or reduce the severity of the attack; prevention of primary and secondary cardiac complications (i.e., proper antiarrhythmic drugs, implantable cardioverter‐defibrillator); specific drugs (i.e., acetazolamide, dichlorphenamide) [2].

Here, we report the case of a 27‐year‐old man with the sole history of Type 1 ATS genetically diagnosed at the age of 14, who was treated in our department for T‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T‐ALL) between July 2022 and August 2025. The case report complies with the ethical and regulatory standards in force in France, including the obtainment of informed consent and the protection of patient confidentiality. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported case of acute leukemia occurring in a patient with ATS. A literature review identified no prior reports of hematologic malignancies in association with ATS, and KCNJ2 is not recognized among germline cancer predisposition genes associated with ALL [5]. Hereafter, we describe the treatment course leading to a successful long‐term remission and present the mitigating measures that we employed to manage the potential precipitating factors of ATS. We also discuss whether this association is fortuitous or suggests a potential biological relationship warranting further investigation.

On the neurological level, our patient presented a history of periodic paralysis with well‐identified triggers: prolonged rest; rest after exertion (such as a soccer game, cycling, etc.) resulting in paraplegia for several days and falls; and a carbohydrate‐rich diet. Besides, the use of oral potassium paradoxically exacerbated the neuromuscular symptoms on some occasions. His symptoms started at the age of seven. By the age of 15, he changed his lifestyle and diet, leading to a significant decrease in the frequency of these attacks, but mild muscle weakness eventually became permanent. No cardiac involvement was previously documented.

On the hematological level, this T‐ALL case presented with mediastinal involvement, mild leukocytosis at 31 × 109/L and massive medullary infiltration with lymphoblasts. Immunophenotyping was consistent with cortical T‐ALL according to the EGIL classification, as blasts expressed TdT, CD2, CD5, CD7, and CD1a without expression of surface CD3. Molecular testing using NGS revealed two genetic mutations in the NOTCH1 gene (L1596H, VAF 13%; L1678P, VAF 22%) without any mutation in the N‐RAS, K‐RAS, or PTEN genes. Therefore, this case was classified as favorable‐risk T‐ALL according to the French Group for Research in Adult Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (GRAALL) oncogenetic risk classifier of adult T‐ALL [6]. The diagnostic workup ruled out blastic infiltration in cerebrospinal fluid; electrocardiogram (ECG) and echocardiography were normal, corrected QT (QTc) interval was within normal range at 417 ms. Sperm cryopreservation was performed prior to chemotherapy. The patient was treated according to the GRAALL 2014 protocol that encompasses different phases: prephase, induction, Consolidations 1 and 2, delayed intensification, Consolidation 3, and the first and second year of maintenance therapy (Figure 1). We faced multiple challenges during treatment that lasted for almost 3 years, some of which were specific to each phase. The patient achieved complete molecular remission (CMR) after induction, which was assessed by IG/TCR gene rearrangement measurement, and remained in CMR after 3 years of follow‐up. Accordingly, allogeneic stem cell transplantation was not performed in first remission [7].

Initially, the patient was admitted into a high‐efficiency particulate air (HEPA)‐filtered room with a total surface area of 8 m2 to undergo the five‐drug induction phase. We identified several risk factors during this period. Daily physical therapy sessions were essential to counterbalance movement limitation in this limited space. We avoided, whenever possible, the use of glucose infusion fluid as a vehicle for intravenous administration of chemotherapy drugs and antibiotics. Prophylaxis for tumor lysis syndrome included intravenous hydration with normal saline solution, along with rasburicase and allopurinol: no electrolyte abnormalities were observed during the prephase or subsequently. We prioritized the use of the atypical antipsychotic drug olanzapine for the prevention of chemotherapy‐induced nausea and vomiting, as other routinely used drugs were not recommended. The use of triazole antifungal agents was contraindicated in ATS patients; hence, echinocandins were the preferred option for antifungal prophylaxis. Moreover, atovaquone was used as prophylaxis for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, as both trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and pentamidine were also contraindicated because of QT prolongation risk. To alleviate the risk of vincristine‐induced peripheral neuropathy, we opted for treatment substitution with the pharmacologically similar, but possibly less neurotoxic, vindesine [8]. In parallel, acetazolamide was introduced straight away as a preventive treatment. As planned in the GRAALL protocol, the patient was able to receive the 21‐day full course of corticosteroids, that is, prednisone, at the high dose of 60 mg/m2 during the induction phase. As a matter of fact, glucocorticoids are a mainstay of ALL treatment, and a lack of early response is predictive of overall disease prognosis. Given the vital position of glucocorticoids, their use could not be avoided or limited in this case, at least in the intensive phases of the treatment [9]. During this induction phase, our patient experienced a few moderate and short‐lived paralytic episodes that were self‐managed with physical activity and the sporadic increase of saline fluid infusion. After hematopoietic recovery, he reported a slight worsening in his permanent muscle weakness. Finally, anthracyclines, which are central to ALL treatment protocols, carry well‐established arrhythmogenic properties [10]. In patients with ATS who have baseline vulnerability to ventricular arrhythmias due to impaired IK1 current and abnormal ventricular repolarization, the addition of anthracycline‐induced cardiotoxicity presented a compounded risk [1]. Using close ECG monitoring, no cardiac incident occurred during induction. These same protective measures were applied during the late intensification phase.

During consolidation cycles, the patient experienced worsening of his muscle weakness that lasted for several days after hospital discharge and caused moderate impact on his daily activities, but he reported no significant paralytic attacks. He noticed that saline fluid administration given during cytarabine and high‐dose methotrexate courses improved his symptoms. Alongside the management of T‐ALL, Holter monitoring was performed quarterly. During the maintenance phase, corticosteroids were removed from the 12 reinduction cycles in order to limit the risk of paralytic episodes [3, 4]. Asymptomatic premature ventricular complexes (PVCs), not previously observed in this patient, were detected during follow‐up: up to 508 PVCs per day, mainly isolated with only one doublet. QTc interval was superior to 450 ms for more than 60% of the monitoring time. These cardiac anomalies prompted immediate modifications in the oral chemotherapy schedule, that is, dose adaptation of 6‐mercaptopurine and spacing out methotrexate administrations, as well as the introduction of a beta‐blocker. Three months later, Holter monitoring showed significantly fewer PVCs (i.e., 22 isolated) with a few premature atrial complexes (i.e., 19 isolated); QTc interval was normal. The patient was able to finish all the maintenance cycles and is still in CMR 3.5 years after diagnosis without experiencing any significant cardiac or muscular complications. Acetazolamide was maintained during all these phases.

Regardless of whether the association is causal, this case highlights important clinical challenges in managing ALL chemotherapy in a patient with a cardiac channelopathy. We were able to successfully and safely manage this case with little therapeutic adaptations owing to a thorough knowledge of the pathophysiology of ATS. On another note, while KCNJ2 is not recognized as a cancer predisposition gene, emerging evidence suggests that ion channels, including potassium channels, play important roles in cancer biology. Kir2.1/KCNJ2 has been implicated in several malignancies through somatic alterations. Upregulation of KCNJ2 has been shown to modulate multidrug resistance in small‐cell lung cancer through regulation of the Ras/MAPK pathway, and Kir2.1 promotes invasion and metastasis in gastric cancer [11, 12]. More broadly, ion channels play roles in leukemia cell proliferation, survival, and chemoresistance [13, 14]. Nonetheless, a critical distinction must be made between these findings and the present case. The cancer‐associated effects described in the literature involve somatic overexpression or gain‐of‐function alterations of Kir2.1 in tumor cells, whereas Type 1 ATS results from germline loss‐of‐function mutations causing KCNJ2 haploinsufficiency. Whether germline KCNJ2 deficiency could theoretically influence leukemogenesis through effects on the bone marrow microenvironment, hematopoietic stem cell function, or lymphocyte development, remains entirely speculative and unsupported by current evidence.
