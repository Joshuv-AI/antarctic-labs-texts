Incidental cystic pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasm and differential diagnosis
Authors: Daniel Martin, Eric R Taylor, Enas Abdallah, Mintallah Haider, Keith Ferguson, Daniel Jeong, Sebastian Feuerlein, James R Costello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms (panNENs) are tumors derived from pancreatic precursor cells with neuroendocrine differentiation. They display characteristic biologic, histopathologic, and functional features which define their classification along a spectrum ranging from well-differentiated pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (panNETs) to poorly differentiated pancreatic neuroendocrine carcinomas (panNECs). We report on an interesting case of a well-differentiated panNET with a large central area of cystic change. This example showcases the diagnostic utility of many widely available imaging modalities. We discuss how CT, MRI/MRCP, PET-CT, and endoscopic ultrasound can help diagnose these neoplasms with greater specificity and provide improved insight for initial staging. We will also discuss some imaging mimics to PanNENs that may be considered while refining our differential.Keywords: PET-CT, Endoscopic ultrasound, MRI/MRCP abdomen, Pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms

Introduction

Pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms (panNENs) are masses arising from pancreatic precursor cells with heterogeneous neuroendocrine differentiation. Overall, panNENs account for close to 2% of all clinically detected pancreatic masses and 10% of all pancreatic masses identified on autopsy. These neoplasms display multiple phenotypes with varying biologic, histologic, and functional behaviors with characteristic clinical presentations. Based on histopathologic features, they can be classified along a spectrum ranging from poorly differentiated pancreatic neuroendocrine carcinomas (panNECS) to well-differentiated pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (panNETs). Generally, these tumors are nonfunctioning, but when patients present symptomatically with or without early lab abnormalities, these functional tumors can be diagnosed much earlier. Although the majority of panNENs arise sporadically, they can develop in association with hereditary syndromes such as tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC), von Hippel-Lindau (VHL), multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1), and neurofibromatosis 1 (NF1). With expanding applications in localization, treatment planning, and surveillance, imaging occupies a central role in the diagnosis and management of these tumors [1].

In recent years there has been some refinement within the grading and classification system [1]. Within this new system, classification is based on both the cellular differentiation and the cellular proliferation (mitotic indices and Ki-67 indices). With this information in hand, panNENs are categorized into 3 grades (G1-G3). Well differentiated panNETs include G1 and G2. With this revision, G3 tumors can now either be a poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinoma (panNEC or panNEC-G3) or a new subtype of well differentiated panNETs with high mitotic and Ki-67 indices (panNET-G3). PanNETs are well-differentiated with high synaptophysin or chromogranin A positivity and little to no cellular atypia. Conversely, PanNECs (including both small and large cell types) are poorly differentiated with high atypia, associated necrosis, and little expression of neuroendocrine markers [1].

NCCN guidelines recommend a combination of anatomic and functional imaging for the evaluation of panNENs [2]. Cross sectional imaging with computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography (PET)-CT complements histopathologic classification with information regarding local invasion and/or metastatic spread. CT is the most widely available imaging modality with improved spatial resolution and reduced acquisition time, incorporating multiphase contrast enhanced protocols to augment detection. Given the hypervascular imaging appearance of panNETs, arterial phase imaging (SN of 83%-88%) helps to differentiate from more hypovascular pancreatic tumors (e.g., pancreatic ductal and acinar cell carcinomas) [3].

Devoid of damaging ionizing radiation, contrast enhanced MRI demonstrates exceptional soft tissue contrast and is equipped with powerful functional imaging and quantitative imaging biomarkers (QIB) to diagnose panNETs (SN of 93% and SP of 88%) [3]. Recent advances in diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) have significantly augmented the SN of MRI for detecting small panNENS of less than 1.5 cm in size. Relative to precontrast signal intensity, panNENs typically show robust arterial enhancement that may be isointense to hyperintense to background pancreas with associated restricted diffusion.

Nuclear medicine imaging techniques evaluate biochemical characteristics and perform functional imaging for both the initial detection and subsequent follow-up of panNETs. PET/CT incorporates somatostatin analog radiotracers (68Gallium-DOTATATE or 64Cu Dotatate) to accurately interrogate for the somatostatin receptor (SN of 93% and a SP of 95%). Both 68Gallium DOTATATE and 64Cu DOTATATE demonstrate much higher SN than traditional 111In-pentetreotide SPECT/CT imaging (OctreoScan; Mallinckrodt Pharmaceuticals, St Louis, Mo) with the added benefit of higher spatial resolution with PET/CT. The 64Cu DOTATATE somatostatin analog has a much longer half-life than 68Gallium-DOTATATE (12.7 hours vs 68 minutes), allowing for its distribution from a centralized pharmacy and overall facilitating its general availability. 18F-FDG-PET demonstrates advantages in detecting poorly differentiated panNECs with high Ki-67 indices and with low somatostatin receptor expression. High-grade (G3) and poorly differentiated tumors (panNEC-G3) show much higher tracer uptake with 18F-FDG-PET vs a somatostatin analog tracer [3].

Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) reports a mean detection rate for panNETs of 90% with a diagnostic accuracy of 90.1% [3]. This modality provides for a more definitive tissue diagnosis via US-guided tissue biopsy, reporting high-yield tissue sampling with greater than 85% adequacy. For small imaging occult panNENs, EUS is the modality of choice. While advanced imaging provides significant insight, correlation with clinical and pathologic features remains critically important to confirm the diagnosis [3].

Case report

This is a case of a woman in her 50s with a past medical history of breast lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS) and papillary thyroid carcinoma. On a screening breast MRI, she was incidentally noted to have a T2 hyperintense mass within the liver. She presented with no reported symptoms and with no suspicious laboratory abnormalities. Further evaluation with contrast enhanced CT of the abdomen confirmed a cyst in the liver, but it also revealed a 1 cm enhancing cystic pancreatic head mass (Fig. 1). The patient was referred to gastroenterology who ordered an MRI of the abdomen with and without contrast (gadoteridol) with magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) to better assess the hepatic cysts and the pancreatic lesion. On MRI, the hepatic cysts were confirmed on T2w, single-shot, fast spin echo and MRCP imaging. The pancreatic head mass demonstrated a circular rind of tissue with both robust arterial enhancement and diffusion restriction (Fig. 2). Enhancement was most conspicuous on arterial phase imaging. There was no loss of signal on echo train imaging (in and out-of-phase) or fat saturation techniques (both T1w or T2w) to suggest internal lipid. The center of the lesion was T2 hyperintense and demonstrated no internal enhancement. Alternatively, T2 shine-through was seen on diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps, consistent with central cystic change (Fig. 3). There was no pancreatic ductal dilation, pancreatic ductal communication, or metastatic disease.

Direct visualization and tissue sampling via EUS were recommended. EUS demonstrated a centrally anechoic mass with a peripheral rim of hypoechoic tissue, and a fine needle biopsy was performed (Fig. 4). Pathology revealed intact fragments of benign pancreatic parenchyma with surrounding glands. Pancreatic sequencing showed high expression of chromogranin A and was concluded to be most consistent with a pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor. There were no copy number alterations or other high-risk mutations reported. A Ki-67 score was not reported. The case was discussed at a multidisciplinary tumor board, and based on the MR imaging features, a cystic neuroendocrine tumor was concluded as the primary diagnostic consideration. 64Cu-DOTATATE PET/CT was performed and highlighted robust tracer uptake along the periphery or “outer rind” of the pancreatic mass with a Krenning score of 4. Like the MRI, a well differentiated panNET was concluded as the primary diagnostic consideration (Fig. 5). For low-grade pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors which are nonfunctional, continued observation with serial imaging at a 6-month interval was recommended. The patient concurred with this plan. Subsequent imaging has been performed up to 1 year following initial presentation with continued stability and with no reported clinical signs of a functional presentation.

Discussion

This case details an example of a cystic, well differentiated panNET. PanNENs, particularly the well differentiated variety (panNETs), most commonly present as solid neoplasms. The cystic appearance of these neoplasms is less common and represents only 2%-8% of resected pancreatic neoplasms [4]. Cystic panNETs can mimic other cystic neoplasms of the pancreas, leading to frequent mischaracterization. Since cystic panNETs often present as nonfunctioning tumors, their initial diagnosis can be very challenging. There are several explanations for the confounding cystic change of these panNETs. The primary mechanism centers on development of a fibrous capsule with infarction and evolution of cystic change [4]. Frequently, the cystic change can result in tumors of increasing size [4]. Generally, these cystic panNETs have a more indolent presentation with little to no rates of metastasis [4]. The cystic panNETs tend to occur in older patients (greater than 50) and show a higher association for MEN1. Our case highlights a smaller and well-circumscribed mass, measuring 1.3 cm in diameter.

PanNETs typically present as avidly enhancing masses with robust diffusion restriction [5]. The characteristic hypervascularity may mimic other diagnostic considerations such as renal cell carcinoma metastases, melanoma metastases, and intrapancreatic splenules. Additional characterization with MRI and nuclear medicine studies can refine the differential. Metastasis to the pancreas is rare with clinical history providing much needed direction. Clear cell renal carcinoma arises with the highest incidence [6]. In the case of intrapancreatic splenules, identical signal and enhancing characteristics to splenic tissue are identified on multiphase, cross-sectional imaging in addition to increased tracer uptake on Tc-99m heat-damaged red blood cell imaging [7]. Other considerations may include peripancreatic gastrointestinal tumors which can be distinguished by their anatomic localization to the submucosal layer of the adjacent bowel wall and retroperitoneal paragangliomas which are generally located in the para-aortic region [8].

Additional diagnostic considerations of solid pancreatic lesions include masses that demonstrate delayed enhancement such as pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) and solid pseudopapillary neoplasms (SPPNs). PDACs represent over 90% of all pancreatic tumors and are hypoenhancing relative to the background pancreatic tissue [9]. They are mostly found in the pancreatic head or the uncinate process, but they can be found anywhere along the course of the pancreatic duct. Frequently, the tail masses present at an advanced stage with a large invasive appearance which is often associated with metastatic disease [10]. Their surrounding rind of stromal desmoplasia contributes to the emergence of ductal dilation and can occur with small masses that are nearly inconspicuous by imaging. In the case of panNETs, pancreatic ductal dilation only emerges in the case of very large and often nonfunctioning tumors [11]. Ductal dilation with panNETs can be observed through the secretion of serotonin metabolites which induce a dense fibrotic response which can result in ductal distension [12]. Solid pseudopapillary tumors typically manifest in young women in their second and third decades of life. They will classically demonstrate solid and cystic components and may contain regions of internal hemorrhage. Additionally, they display soft tissue with a T2w hypointense appearance that demonstrates delayed enhancement with progressive filling on portal venous and 3-minute delayed phase imaging [11].

Mimics of cystic pancreatic neoplasms include intraductal pancreatic mucinous neoplasms (IPMN) and pseudocysts in addition to mucinous and serous cystic neoplasms. These entities represent more than 90% of pancreatic cystic lesions and may display overlapping morphologic and imaging features. Pancreatic pseudocysts are the most common cystic masses and are seen most frequently in patients with a history of prior pancreatitis or trauma. They will appear rounded and unilocular with a surrounding and delayed-enhancing fibrous capsule (best visualized on interstitial, 3-minute delayed phase imaging) and internal fluid that frequently shows a layered T1w hyperintense appearance [13]. They will typically fluctuate in size and show communication with the pancreatic duct [14]. When forming a differential for cystic neoplasms, demographic factors are often helpful. IPMN and serous cystic neoplasms most commonly manifest in the sixth decade with serous cystic neoplasms showing a strong female predilection, while mucinous cystic neoplasms occurring almost exclusively in middle aged women at the level of the body and the tail of the pancreas [15]. Mucin producing lesions such as IPMNs and the macrocystic and more unilocular appearing mucinous cystic neoplasms have greater malignant potential, while serous cystadenomas are largely considered benign [13]. Serous cystadenomas display a microcystic appearance resembling a “bunch of grapes” with multiple small (<1 cm) cysts oriented around an epicenter and with no pancreatic ductal communication [13]. Serous cystadenomas are most commonly located in the pancreatic head and are frequently associated with a central scar which can show calcification which is best seen on CT [13]. IPMNs originate from the epithelial lining of the ductal system and are classified as either main duct, branch-type, or mixed-type IPMNs. MRCP and/or ERCP interrogation best identifies their communication with the pancreatic duct [16]. Mucinous cystadenomas most commonly occur in the pancreatic body and tail with internal septa of variable thickness along with internal heterogeneity and possible nodularity [13]. A higher risk of malignant transformation can be associated with septations and nodularity with variable enhancement along with or without coarse and irregular calcifications along the periphery.

Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) with biopsy can be an invaluable diagnostic tool, allowing for direct visualization and analysis of the histological, biochemical, and immunocytologic characteristics of the tissue sample acquired by fine needle biopsy as well as analysis of any cyst fluid obtained by fine needle aspiration. Analysis of cyst aspirate can differentiate from other diagnoses such as mucinous neoplasms with their high viscosity fluid containing extracellular mucin with elevated amylase, signifying possible communication with the pancreatic duct [13].

Sporadic panNETs are associated with mutations in MEN1, ATRX/DAXX, and mTOR pathways, while panNECs can exhibit alterations in RB and TP53 pathways (frequencies of 74% and 95%, respectively) in addition KRAS and SMAD4 mutations. KRAS alterations are more frequently associated with PDACs, IPMNs, and mucinous neoplasms [9]. Cytologic markers for chromogranin A and synaptophysin can help to better identify neuroendocrine tumors [9]. These neuroendocrine markers demonstrate highest positivity in well differentiated panNETS with their high expression of somatostatin receptors (SSTRs). Most panNETs will express SSTR-2 with SSTR-1 and SSTR-5 expressed to a lesser extent. In the case of high grade and poorly differentiated tumors such as panNEC, the tumors dedifferentiate and lose expression of SSTR. This biology underscores the selection of somatostatin analog tracers (68Gallium DOTATATE and 64Cu DOTATATE) when evaluating for well differentiated G1 and G2 panNETs and the use of 18F-FDG when detecting high-grade and poorly differentiated panNEC-G3. At times, PET/CT imaging is performed with dual agents (18F-FDG and a somatostatin tracer analogue). This can help to differentiate a panNEC-G3 tumor which shows high uptake on 18F-FDG PET/CT (and little to no DOTATATE uptake) from a well-differentiated panNET with high mitotic and Ki-67 indices (panNET-G3) which shows increased uptake with both agents [17,18].

Lastly, this case highlights the importance of maintaining heightened attention for additional synchronous and metachronous tumors at the time of staging and restaging. In this patient’s case, the cystic panNET was identified while further characterizing an indeterminate hepatic mass seen on a follow up breast MRI for prior breast cancer. When faced with these findings, it is important to differentiate between signs of metastatic disease vs detection of a second primary malignancy. While reviewing our many daily follow up imaging scans, this is always important information to keep in mind [19,20].

In conclusion, this paper discusses a case of a cystic neuroendocrine tumor of the pancreas and its unique diagnostic challenges. Awareness of this consideration in addition to a multidisciplinary discussion incorporating various imaging modalities as well as histologic, cytologic, and immunohistochemical analysis can help to better establish the diagnosis.

Learning points

Pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms are a heterogeneous group of tumors which display variable biologic, histologic, and morphologic features. Various imaging modalities play a vital role in detection, diagnosis, and management.

•Biologic activity allows for classification of panNENs into well differentiated panNETs vs poorly differentiated panNECs. Broadly, characteristics such as vascularity and somatostatin expressivity correlate with features seen on imaging.
