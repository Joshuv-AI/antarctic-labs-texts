High-risk features in adult patients with B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia: redefining risk in the era of targeted immunotherapy
Authors: Dong Won Baek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The treatment landscape of adult B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) has changed substantially with the introduction of targeted immunotherapeutic agents. Historically, high-risk disease was defined by clinical and cytogenetic features such as older age, elevated leukocyte count, and Philadelphia chromosome (Ph)-positive status. Advances in genomic profiling, together with the use of next-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors, bispecific T-cell engagers, antibody–drug conjugates, and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapies, have altered both treatment outcomes and risk stratification. Outcomes for patients with Ph-positive B-ALL have improved markedly, particularly with ponatinib- and blinatumomab-based regimens, but several molecularly defined subgroups continue to have poor prognoses. These include IKZF1plus Ph-positive B-ALL, TP53-mutated and hypodiploid B-ALL, Ph-like B-ALL, and KMT2A-rearranged B-ALL. Emerging evidence indicates that introducing immunotherapeutic agents early in treatment can improve response quality and rates of measurable residual disease negativity, including in patients with high-risk disease. Relapse nevertheless remains a major challenge, and the optimal integration of novel agents, allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation, and cellular immunotherapy remains uncertain. This review summarizes the biological and clinical features of the major high-risk molecular subgroups of adult B-ALL and discusses how recent therapeutic advances are redefining risk assessment and treatment strategies in the era of targeted immunotherapy.

Introduction

B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL) is an aggressive hematologic malignancy marked by uncontrolled proliferation of immature B-cell precursors [1]. Despite substantial improvements in treatment over recent decades, adults with B-ALL continue to have poorer survival than pediatric patients, and relapse remains a major cause of treatment failure [2, 3]. Historically, prognostic stratification in adult acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) incorporated both patient- and disease-related factors, including age, presenting white blood cell count, and treatment response [4]. Landmark studies subsequently established the prognostic importance of genetic and cytogenetic abnormalities. In the Medical Research Council United Kingdom Acute Lymphoblastic Leukaemia (UKALL) XII/Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) E2993 trial, age, leukocyte count at diagnosis, and cytogenetic abnormalities were key determinants of treatment outcome [5]. Further cytogenetic analyses from the same study confirmed the adverse prognostic effect of Philadelphia chromosome (Ph)-positive disease and the importance of chromosomal abnormalities for risk stratification [6, 7].

Genomic profiling has revealed substantial biological heterogeneity within B-ALL and has identified several molecularly defined high-risk subtypes [8]. Contemporary classification systems and clinical guidelines recognize high-risk genetic abnormalities such as hypodiploidy, TP53 alterations, KMT2A rearrangements, and Ph-like ALL. These subtypes have distinct genetic mechanisms and are associated with relative resistance to conventional chemotherapy and an increased risk of relapse [9–12]. At the same time, treatment of adult B-ALL has changed considerably. The introduction of next-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), bispecific T-cell engagers, antibody–drug conjugates, and cellular immunotherapies has improved outcomes in selected patient populations [13–16]. Ph-positive ALL is a notable example. Once considered among the poorest-risk subgroups, Ph-positive ALL now has substantially better survival with potent TKI-based therapy, particularly ponatinib-containing regimens combined with immunotherapeutic agents such as blinatumomab [17, 18]. Accordingly, some traditional high-risk features may carry less prognostic weight in the current treatment era.

The definition of high-risk adult B-ALL is therefore changing. Therapeutic advances may have reduced the prognostic importance of some historical risk factors, whereas other features continue to identify patients with persistently poor outcomes who may benefit from treatment intensification, novel agents, or allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (allo-HCT) [19–22]. Reassessment of high-risk features within contemporary treatment paradigms is therefore increasingly important. This review discusses the evolving concept of high-risk B-ALL in adults treated in the era of targeted immunotherapy. The discussion centers on molecularly defined high-risk subgroups, particularly IKZF1plus Ph-positive ALL, TP53-mutated and hypodiploid ALL, Ph-like ALL, and KMT2A-rearranged ALL. We also examine how recent therapeutic advances have reshaped risk stratification and the role of allo-HCT in contemporary clinical practice.

Philadelphia chromosome-positive acute lymphoblastic leukemia and the IKZF1plus genotype

Historically, Ph-positive ALL was considered one of the poorest-risk subtypes of adult ALL. Before TKIs became available, long-term survival was largely limited to patients who were eligible for allo-HCT [23]. Incorporating TKIs into frontline therapy has since improved outcomes substantially, and chemotherapy-free regimens that combine TKIs with blinatumomab have further changed the treatment paradigm [24, 25]. The D-ALBA study reported durable remissions after dasatinib followed by blinatumomab; the 4-year disease-free survival (DFS), overall survival (OS), and event-free survival (EFS) rates were 75.8%, 80.7%, and 74.6%, respectively [17]. Despite these improvements with modern TKI-based approaches, Ph-positive ALL remains biologically heterogeneous, and specific genomic alterations such as IKZF1plus identify patients at increased risk of relapse and treatment failure [26]. IKZF1plus is generally defined as an IKZF1 deletion accompanied by additional deletions involving CDKN2A/B and/or PAX5 [19]. In the long-term D-ALBA analysis, patients with IKZF1plus had substantially lower 4-year DFS than patients without IKZF1 deletion or with IKZF1 deletion alone (45.5% versus 82.3% and 74.0%, respectively) [17]. The adverse prognostic effect of IKZF1plus has also been reported with intensive TKI-based regimens. In a genomic analysis of adults with Ph-positive ALL treated with hyperfractionated cyclophosphamide, vincristine, adriamycin/doxorubicin, and dexamethasone (hyper-CVAD) plus dasatinib or ponatinib, IKZF1 deletion was identified in 60% of patients and IKZF1plus in 46%. IKZF1plus was associated with a higher risk of relapse and inferior OS and remained an independent predictor of poor OS in multivariable analysis [26]. Thus, although BCR::ABL1-directed therapy has reduced the historical adverse effect of Ph positivity, IKZF1plus continues to define a biologically high-risk subset.

The combination of ponatinib and blinatumomab is therefore a rational and clinically feasible strategy for IKZF1plus Ph-positive ALL. Ponatinib potently inhibits BCR::ABL1, including T315I-mutated clones, whereas blinatumomab can deepen molecular responses through CD19-directed T-cell engagement. In a phase 2 study of ponatinib plus blinatumomab, newly diagnosed patients with Ph-positive ALL achieved complete molecular response rates above 85%, with estimated 3-year EFS and OS rates of approximately 77% and 91%, respectively [27]. Updated genomic analyses further indicate that IKZF1plus is no longer significantly associated with increased relapse risk in patients treated with this regimen, unlike earlier experience with dasatinib–blinatumomab or ponatinib-based chemotherapy, in which IKZF1plus remained a strong adverse prognostic factor [26, 28]. Although longer follow-up and prospective validation remain necessary, these findings support ponatinib plus blinatumomab as a promising frontline approach that may partly offset the adverse prognostic effect of IKZF1plus and reduce reliance on intensive chemotherapy or routine allo-HCT in selected patients. Hypodiploidy and TP53-mutated acute lymphoblastic leukemia

Hypodiploidy is a well-established adverse cytogenetic abnormality in B-ALL and is generally defined by a modal chromosome number below 44. It is further classified as near-haploid ALL (24–31 chromosomes) or low-hypodiploid ALL (32–39 chromosomes). Although both subgroups are associated with poor outcomes, their biological features differ. Near-haploid ALL commonly involves alterations in Ras signaling and receptor tyrosine kinase pathways, whereas low-hypodiploid ALL is strongly associated with TP53 alterations and genomic instability [29]. Recent genomic studies have clarified the biology of low-hypodiploid ALL. Comprehensive molecular analyses indicate that biallelic TP53 inactivation is the defining genetic hallmark of this subtype and is present in more than 90% of adult cases. Adult low-hypodiploid ALL also appears to arise from age-related TP53-mutant clonal hematopoiesis, suggesting a biological link between aging and leukemogenesis [30, 31]. Unlike pediatric low-hypodiploid ALL, in which germline TP53 alterations are relatively common, TP53 mutations in adults are predominantly somatic and often precede leukemic transformation. These observations suggest that TP53 dysfunction may be a central driver of disease initiation and progression rather than merely a secondary event in low-hypodiploid ALL [29–31].

TP53 alterations also occur outside hypodiploid disease. Large-scale genomic analyses have shown that TP53 mutations span multiple genetic subtypes of adult B-ALL and frequently coexist with other adverse-risk abnormalities [32]. In a contemporary multicenter study of more than 800 adults with ALL, TP53-mutated disease was associated with significantly inferior OS compared with TP53-wild-type disease, independently of other prognostic variables. Patients with biallelic TP53 alterations had particularly poor outcomes compared with those with monoallelic alterations, further supporting the adverse prognostic significance of TP53 dysfunction [32].

Targeted immunotherapies may improve outcomes in TP53-mutated ALL and partly mitigate its historically poor prognosis. Retrospective analyses suggest that patients treated with blinatumomab, inotuzumab ozogamicin, or cluster of differentiation 19 (CD19)-directed chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy can achieve clinically meaningful responses despite TP53 alterations. Durable disease control remains difficult, but reported outcomes appear better than those historically achieved with conventional chemotherapy alone. A possible biological explanation is that immune-based therapies act independently of p53-mediated apoptotic pathways and may therefore bypass an important mechanism of chemoresistance in TP53-mutated leukemia [32–34].

Philadelphia chromosome-like acute lymphoblastic leukemia

Ph-like ALL, also termed BCR::ABL1-like ALL, is a biologically heterogeneous subtype of B-ALL with a gene-expression profile resembling that of Ph-positive ALL despite the absence of the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene [35]. Since its initial description in pediatric ALL cohorts, Ph-like ALL has been recognized as a clinically important high-risk subtype across age groups. Its incidence increases with age and is approximately 20%–30% among adults with B-ALL [11, 35, 36]. Multiple studies have consistently reported poorer outcomes than for non–Ph-like B-ALL, including lower rates of measurable residual disease (MRD) clearance, higher relapse rates, and inferior OS [37, 38].

The marked genomic heterogeneity of Ph-like ALL complicates both diagnosis and treatment. Unlike Ph-positive ALL, Ph-like ALL includes a broad range of kinase-activating alterations that converge on cytokine-receptor and tyrosine-kinase signaling pathways [35, 36]. Approximately 50%–60% of cases harbor CRLF2 rearrangements, often with accompanying JAK–STAT pathway mutations, whereas 10%–20% contain ABL-class fusions involving ABL1, ABL2, CSF1R, or PDGFRB. Alterations involving EPOR, JAK2, FLT3, NTRK, and other kinase-signaling pathways have also been described [10, 11, 35–38]. This heterogeneity contributes directly to diagnostic complexity and therapeutic uncertainty. Routine diagnosis remains difficult despite increasing recognition of the biological importance of Ph-like ALL. Gene-expression profiling is the reference standard but is largely restricted to specialized centers. Many institutions therefore use stepwise diagnostic strategies that combine flow-cytometric assessment of CRLF2 expression, fluorescence in situ hybridization, multiplex fusion assays, and next-generation sequencing. The lack of a universally accepted diagnostic algorithm may contribute to under-recognition of this high-risk entity [10, 11, 35, 36, 39].

The therapeutic implications of Ph-like ALL also remain under investigation. Kinase-targeted approaches, including TKIs for ABL-class fusions and JAK inhibitors for CRLF2/JAK pathway–altered disease, have shown promising preclinical activity, but clinical evidence is limited and prospective studies have not established a standard targeted strategy [10]. By contrast, immunotherapeutic approaches have shown encouraging clinical activity. In a post hoc analysis of the phase III TOWER study, patients with relapsed or refractory Ph-like ALL who received blinatumomab had higher remission rates and longer survival than those treated with conventional chemotherapy, suggesting that CD19-directed immunotherapy may partly offset the adverse prognosis of this subtype [40–42]. Long-term outcomes nevertheless remain suboptimal, and many patients still require allo-HCT for durable disease control [43].

KMT2A-rearranged acute lymphoblastic leukemia

KMT2A-rearranged ALL is among the most biologically aggressive B-ALL subtypes and remains an important high-risk feature in adults. KMT2A rearrangements occur in approximately 5%–10% of adult B-ALL cases, with t(4;11)(q21;q23)/KMT2A::AFF1 as the most common fusion. Clinically, this subtype commonly presents with hyperleukocytosis, an immature immunophenotype, and a high risk of early relapse. Although intensive induction therapy produces high complete response (CR) rates, long-term survival remains inferior to that of other molecular B-ALL subtypes [9, 44].

Poor outcomes persist in KMT2A-rearranged ALL even after MRD-negative remission. In a large international study of adults with KMT2A-rearranged ALL, patients harboring KMT2A::AFF1 had significantly inferior OS despite favorable initial response rates, supporting an intrinsically high-risk disease biology rather than chemotherapy resistance alone [9, 45]. Recent studies have further characterized biological heterogeneity within KMT2A-rearranged ALL. Myeloid antigen co-expression and persistent MRD after induction have emerged as important prognostic factors [46]. Patients with high MRD levels at the end of induction appear to benefit more from intensified consolidation, including acute myeloid leukemia (AML)-like regimens followed by allo-HCT. Risk stratification in KMT2A-rearranged ALL may therefore need to integrate both genomic and MRD-based parameters [45–47].

Immunotherapeutic agents can induce meaningful responses in relapsed or refractory KMT2A-rearranged ALL, although overall prognosis remains poor. Blinatumomab and inotuzumab ozogamicin have shown clinical activity and can serve as bridges to transplantation in selected patients. Relapse after immunotherapy remains common, however, underscoring the need for more effective molecularly targeted strategies [48, 49].

Measurable residual disease-guided dynamic risk stratification

Cytogenetic and molecular abnormalities define baseline biological risk in B-ALL, whereas MRD provides a dynamic measure of treatment sensitivity and residual relapse risk. MRD after induction and consolidation is among the strongest predictors of relapse and survival in adult ALL, independent of baseline clinical and genetic factors [50, 51]. Persistent or recurrent MRD can therefore reclassify patients without adverse genetic features as functionally high risk [50, 51].

MRD can be measured using multiparameter flow cytometry, quantitative polymerase chain reaction, or next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based assays. Flow cytometry generally reaches a sensitivity of approximately 10–4, whereas molecular and NGS-based assays can detect leukemic clones at levels of 10–5 to 10–6 [50–53]. The meaning of MRD negativity therefore depends on the assay, specimen quality, detection limit, and timing and should not be equated with absolute eradication of leukemia. The European LeukemiaNet recommends that treatment decisions based on MRD negativity rely on testing in an experienced laboratory with a sensitivity of at least 0.01% [51]. Serial monitoring is also important because persistent MRD or reappearance of a previously undetectable clone may signal treatment resistance or molecular relapse before overt hematologic recurrence [50, 51].

In Ph-negative B-ALL, persistent MRD may prompt MRD-directed blinatumomab therapy and consideration of allo-HCT [50, 51]. The Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group-American College of Radiology Imaging Network (ECOG-ACRIN) E1910 trial, however, showed that MRD carries prognostic information beyond a simple positive–negative classification. Among adults with newly diagnosed Ph-negative B-ALL who achieved MRD-negative remission by flow cytometry, adding blinatumomab to consolidation chemotherapy increased the 3-year OS rate from 68 to 85% and relapse-free survival from 64 to 80% [14]. Conventional MRD negativity therefore does not establish eradication of all clinically relevant disease, and patients who are MRD-negative may still benefit from immunotherapy. MRD is better viewed as a continuous, treatment-dependent measure of residual risk than as a binary marker used only to select patients for treatment intensification [14, 50].

High-sensitivity NGS-based MRD (NGS-MRD) may further refine risk assessment around allo-HCT. Detectable pretransplant NGS-MRD, including levels below 10–4, is associated with increased post-transplant relapse risk, whereas any detectable post-transplant MRD strongly predicts recurrence regardless of pretransplant status [52, 53]. NGS-MRD may therefore identify clinically relevant residual disease in patients classified as MRD-negative by flow cytometry and may inform pretransplant treatment and post-transplant surveillance [52, 53]. Whether delaying allo-HCT to eradicate very low-level NGS-MRD improves survival remains uncertain, however, and prospective evidence for specific MRD-triggered interventions after transplantation is limited [50, 53].

MRD has progressed from a post-treatment prognostic marker to a central component of dynamic risk stratification. Interpretation should account for baseline molecular features, assay sensitivity, response kinetics, treatment exposure, and transplant status. MRD can update risk throughout treatment, but it should inform rather than independently determine therapeutic decisions within a continuously revised, biologically informed framework [50, 51].

Novel strategies for patients with high-risk features

Targeted immunotherapy is increasingly being incorporated into frontline treatment for adult B-ALL, particularly in patients with high-risk features. In a recent multicenter study of adults with TP53-mutated ALL, patients who received frontline blinatumomab-based regimens achieved higher-quality responses than those treated with conventional chemotherapy alone. Among newly diagnosed patients, CR rates exceeded 90%, and approximately 80%–90% of those treated with blinatumomab-based therapy achieved MRD-negative remission. Frontline inotuzumab ozogamicin–based regimens likewise produced high remission rates and deep molecular responses. These findings suggest that immune-based therapies may partly overcome TP53-associated chemotherapy resistance and support early use of blinatumomab or inotuzumab ozogamicin in frontline treatment of TP53-mutated B-ALL [32].

A phase 2 study of sequential hyper-CVAD and blinatumomab in newly diagnosed Ph-negative ALL further supports the potential benefit of frontline targeted immunotherapy. Of 38 enrolled patients, 21 (55%) had at least one high-risk feature, including TP53 alterations, KMT2A rearrangements, hypodiploidy, a complex karyotype, or persistent MRD positivity. Despite these adverse disease features, outcomes were encouraging: the estimated 3-year relapse-free survival (RFS) and OS rates were 73% and 81%, respectively. The 3-year OS rate among patients with high-risk features was approximately 76%, slightly lower than that among standard-risk patients [34]. Inotuzumab ozogamicin has also been incorporated successfully into frontline therapy for newly diagnosed B-ALL. In a phase 2 study of a mini-hyperfractionated regimen of cyclophosphamide, vincristine, and dexamethasone (mini-hyper-CVD) plus inotuzumab ozogamicin, followed by blinatumomab, in older patients with newly diagnosed Ph-negative ALL, approximately 40% had TP53 mutations, 14% had CRLF2 abnormalities, and nearly 30% had high-risk cytogenetic features. Despite these baseline risks, the regimen achieved a 99% rate of CR or complete remission with incomplete hematologic recovery (CRi) and a 94% MRD-negativity rate, with estimated 5-year progression-free survival (PFS) and OS rates of 44% and 46%, respectively [54]. The increasing use of targeted immunotherapies has also revealed distinct mechanisms of treatment failure and relapse. Under selective pressure from blinatumomab or CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy, leukemic cells may evade treatment through loss or reduction of CD19 expression caused by genetic alterations, alternative splicing, or expansion of preexisting CD19-negative clones; inotuzumab ozogamicin may similarly select subclones with dim or absent CD22 expression [58–61]. Lineage switch is another form of antigen escape, particularly in KMT2A-rearranged ALL, in which leukemic clones may acquire a myeloid phenotype while retaining the founding genetic alteration and thereby evade B-cell–directed therapy [59, 62]. Extramedullary disease, including central nervous system (CNS) involvement, may also respond later or differently than bone marrow disease and may become a site of relapse despite systemic remission [63, 64]. These failure patterns support serial assessment of target-antigen expression, molecular MRD monitoring, and continued surveillance for extramedullary disease during and after targeted immunotherapy.

Role of allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation in adults with high-risk B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia

Allo-HCT has long been a key post-remission strategy for adults with high-risk B-ALL, largely because of its graft-versus-leukemia effect [6]. Before targeted therapies became available, allo-HCT during first complete remission (CR1) was routinely recommended for patients with adverse cytogenetic or molecular features, including Ph-positive ALL, hypodiploidy, KMT2A rearrangements, and persistent MRD [6, 22, 65]. Its role is now changing as modern targeted therapies and immunotherapies are incorporated earlier in treatment.

This shift is especially apparent in Ph-positive ALL. Ph-positive disease was traditionally considered an indication for allo-HCT when transplantation was feasible [65]. Contemporary frontline regimens combining TKIs, particularly ponatinib, with blinatumomab now produce high molecular response rates and favorable long-term survival [66]. As a result, routine allo-HCT for every patient with Ph-positive ALL is increasingly being reconsidered. Some genomic subgroups, particularly patients with IKZF1plus alterations, remain at increased relapse risk and may still benefit from transplantation when optimal molecular responses are not achieved [67].

Allo-HCT nevertheless remains important for several molecularly defined high-risk subtypes. TP53-mutated ALL continues to be associated with poor outcomes despite recent therapeutic advances. Immune-based therapies such as blinatumomab, inotuzumab ozogamicin, and CD19-directed CAR T cells can induce meaningful remissions, but durable disease control remains difficult. Retrospective analyses indicate that patients who achieve remission with these therapies have better leukemia-free survival after allo-HCT than patients who do not undergo transplantation. Allo-HCT therefore remains a reasonable consolidation strategy for eligible patients with TP53-mutated disease [32, 33]. KMT2A-rearranged ALL also carries a high relapse risk despite high initial CR rates. Studies have reported inferior long-term survival even after MRD negativity, supporting the view that KMT2A rearrangement reflects intrinsically aggressive disease biology [10, 45]. Allo-HCT in CR1 therefore continues to be recommended for transplant-eligible patients with KMT2A-rearranged ALL. Ph-like ALL is similarly associated with high relapse rates and poor survival across age groups [12, 35]. Although targeted and immunotherapeutic approaches are promising, long-term evidence remains limited, and allo-HCT continues to be an important treatment option for many patients with this subtype.

The value of allo-HCT as consolidation after CAR T-cell therapy remains uncertain. Although some patients have durable remissions after CAR T-cell therapy alone, early loss of B-cell aplasia or limited CAR T-cell persistence may signal waning immune surveillance and a higher risk of CD19-positive relapse [68–70]. Consolidative allo-HCT may therefore be considered for transplant-eligible patients who achieve CR after CAR T-cell therapy but show early B-cell recovery, loss of detectable CAR T cells, persistent or re-emerging MRD, or high-risk genomic features associated with poor response durability [70–72]. Retrospective and real-world studies suggest that allo-HCT performed during MRD-negative remission after CAR T-cell therapy may reduce relapse and improve EFS or PFS [70–72]. Decisions about post–CAR T-cell allo-HCT should therefore be individualized according to disease risk, response durability, and transplant eligibility.

Discussion

Targeted immunotherapies have substantially changed the treatment landscape of adult B-ALL over the past decade and, in turn, the definition of high-risk disease. Historically, adverse prognosis was linked to clinical and cytogenetic factors such as older age, elevated leukocyte count, and Ph positivity [4–7]. Modern therapy, particularly ponatinib-based regimens, has markedly improved outcomes in Ph-positive ALL and challenged its classification as uniformly high risk [17, 18]. Several molecular subgroups nevertheless continue to have poor outcomes. IKZF1plus alterations in Ph-positive ALL, TP53-mutated or hypodiploid ALL, Ph-like ALL, and KMT2A-rearranged ALL remain associated with higher relapse rates and poor survival [2]. These patterns indicate that high-risk classification in the modern era should increasingly reflect biological drivers of treatment resistance rather than traditional cytogenetic categories alone. Risk should also be updated according to treatment response instead of remaining fixed at diagnosis. Serial MRD assessment can reclassify patients during treatment, although interpretation must account for assay sensitivity, response kinetics, and baseline molecular features [14, 50–53].

Recent studies increasingly support introducing targeted immunotherapy early in high-risk B-ALL rather than reserving these agents for relapsed or refractory disease. Frontline blinatumomab and inotuzumab ozogamicin have produced high rates of MRD-negative remission and encouraging long-term outcomes even in patients with adverse molecular features. These findings support using intensified immunotherapy from diagnosis in biologically high-risk disease to reduce relapse rather than relying on these agents only for salvage [32, 34, 54]. The growing availability of blinatumomab and inotuzumab ozogamicin has also moved standard-risk B-ALL toward more individualized, response-adapted treatment by increasing remission depth and MRD-negativity rates. Some patients with sustained MRD-negative remission may therefore achieve durable disease control without allo-HCT. Current evidence nevertheless suggests that allo-HCT may continue to benefit patients with high-risk features, particularly TP53-mutated, KMT2A-rearranged, or Ph-like ALL. Transplant decisions should therefore consider molecular risk, MRD status, treatment response, and access to novel immunotherapies. Although allo-HCT may no longer be necessary for every patient historically classified as high risk, it remains an important option for many patients with newly defined high-risk B-ALL features [32, 33, 45].

Relapsed or refractory high-risk B-ALL remains difficult to manage despite encouraging results with frontline blinatumomab and inotuzumab ozogamicin. Patients with TP53-mutated, KMT2A-rearranged, or Ph-like ALL continue to have high relapse rates and poor long-term survival even after immune-based therapy. Blinatumomab and inotuzumab ozogamicin can induce clinically meaningful salvage remissions, but responses often lack durability in patients with adverse biological features [73]. This limited durability may partly reflect treatment-specific failure mechanisms, including CD19 or CD22 antigen escape, KMT2A-associated lineage switch, and extramedullary relapse [58–64]. CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy offers another strategy for inducing deep remissions in heavily pretreated patients [68, 69]. In selected transplant-eligible patients, particularly those with early loss of B-cell aplasia, limited CAR T-cell persistence, recurrent MRD, or adverse genomic features, allo-HCT may be used as consolidation after CAR T-cell-induced remission [70–72]. Further prospective studies are needed to determine how best to integrate CAR T-cell therapy into treatment of high-risk B-ALL and to define which patients should receive consolidative allo-HCT and when it should be performed.

The concept of high-risk B-ALL has evolved with the advent of targeted immunotherapy. Genomic profiling, serial MRD assessment, and novel therapies have made disease biology and treatment response increasingly important in prognostic assessment and treatment selection. Future strategies are likely to rely on individualized risk assessment that integrates molecular features, response to therapy, and the use of immunotherapy. Prospective studies are still needed to determine how these approaches can best improve outcomes in biologically high-risk disease.
