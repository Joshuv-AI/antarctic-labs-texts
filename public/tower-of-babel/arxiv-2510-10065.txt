Calibration of answer probabilities in verbal autopsies: Working Paper
Nathan Higgins, James Liley, Eilidh Cowan

Abstract
In most of the world, causes of death are not recorded. Verbal autopsies are structured interviews with people close to the deceased, which are used to estimate the likelihood of various causes of death. Such estimates typically make use of a table of marginal probabilities, called a ‘probbase’, describing the frequency of answers to each interview question conditional on each cause of death. Assembling probbase tables is challenging, since data labelled with verified causes-of-death are not typically available, and is generally done on the basis of expert opinion. We propose a method to verify or partially learn a probbase table given only a set of verbal autopsy questionnaires (i.e., unlabelled data). Essentially, we assess how well a probbase can be used to impute answers. Our method requires a mild conditional independence assumption on the joint distribution of questionnaire data and causes of death. More generally, our method serves as a means to assess verbal autopsy algorithms and parameters without the need for external cause-of-death labelling. We offer theoretical arguments to support our method, and some brief evaluations on data simulated to resemble realistic verbal autopsy questionnaires. We find moderate promise for the approach in this context, in that we may differentiate probbase values which are too high or too low with around 75% correctness using 1500 verbal autopsy questionnaires. This paper serves as an introduction to our approach and a statement of intent, in the spirit of preregistration. We identify a range of theoretical and practical open problems and describe a planned outline of work to evaluate the method. We invite comments and suggestions on our approach and open questions. We stress that our method has not yet been thoroughly tested and we do not endorse its use in a real-world setting at this stage.



1
Introduction

Verbal autopsy (VA) is a method to infer a cause of death in a recently-deceased individual using a standardised post-mortem interview with the family of the deceased
(
Byass, 2010
;
Byass et al., 2019
)
.
The interview consists of a series of yes/no questions, not all of which will be answered.
Given the answers to an interview, a probability is estimated for a set of potential causes of death (CoD), using one of a set of methods we will collectively call ‘VA algorithms’.
The method is widely used in global health and has been extended in various ways
(
D’Ambruoso et al., 2021
;
Byass, 2010
)

In some methods for estimation, the probabilities are interpretable as a (multinomial) posterior distribution over causes of death
(
Li et al., 2014
;
Byass et al., 2003
;
Byass et al., 2019
;
Miasnikof et al., 2015
)
.

In general, VA algorithms use as a parameter a matrix of probabilities of answering a question a particular way given causes of death.
This may be interpreted as a marginal likelihood
(
Byass et al., 2019
;
Miasnikof et al., 2015
)
, an approximation of true probabilities correct up to ordering
(
McCormick et al., 2016
)
, or an informative prior on the true probabilities
(
Li et al., 2019
)
.
This matrix is generally estimated from expert opinion from medical practitioners, and is often called a ‘probbase’: we will use that term here.
An estimate of cause of death frequencies in the population is also generally specified as an input to the estimation procedure
(
Fottrell et al., 2011
)
, which may be interpreted as a prior over cause-of-death probabilities
(
Byass et al., 2019
;
Miasnikof et al., 2015
)
and which is often called a ‘cause-specific mortality fraction’ (CSMF).
Missingness in interview answers is generally assumed to be non-informative
(
Byass et al., 2019
;
Li et al., 2019
)
.

The correct probbase tends to differ across populations
(
Clark et al., 2018
)
, making it particularly laborious to estimate.
In addition, in many settings where VA is used, training data labelled with causes-of-death is unavailable, meaning that there is no way to directly verify the accuracy of a VA algorithm (or its parameters).
Evaluation of probbase entries is consequently a problem of interest, with various approaches.
Some training data is available for specific geographic settings
(
Groenewald et al., 2023
)
in which physicians review VA answers and assign likely causes of death, enabling a probbase to be learned directly.
Some modern approaches to verbal autopsy analysis reduce reliance on the probbase, by treating it as a prior estimate of a probability to be estimated
(
Li et al., 2019
;
Li et al., 2024
)
or by allowing an open interpretation of the questionnaire
(
Chu, 2025
)
.
A potentially appealing approach is to consider the probbase as a parameter, and maximise the likelihood of an observed VA dataset (or use the likelihood to develop a Bayesian model
(
Li et al., 2019
)
), but VA methods do not always lead to tractable likelihoods, particularly in open approaches
(
Chu, 2025
)
.

VA questionnaires are regularly updated. In order to ensure reliable cause-of-death coding continues across these updates, VA algorithms must be updated to match current questionnaire standards
(
Byass et al., 2003
;
Byass et al., 2019
)
, which correspondingly require updating of probbases.
Previous revision has generally relied on physician coded VAs (as above) to validate a new probbase.
In 2022 the WHO made considerable changes to the global standard verbal autopsy questionnaire
(
World Health Organization et al., 2022
)
but the updating of the algorithm has been severely constrained.
Although an algorithm is now available, it remains unvalidated due to lack of physician coded VAs using the 2022 questionnaire.

In this work, we propose a simple machine-learning-like approach to jointly assess a VA algorithm and a probbase, making use of only a set of interview answers from a given population, with an assumption that answers can be partitioned into blocks which are mutually conditionally independent given causes of death.
In particular, we propose assessing a candidate probbase and VA algorithm via its ability to impute individual interview answers.
We propose artificially leaving out all answers to a set of VA questions, and using the remaining answers to establish a distribution over likely causes of death.
We then propose using this distribution to reconstruct the ‘missing’ answers, and assessing the probbase by the accuracy of this reconstruction.

We consider that our method may have application in flagging elements of probbase for review: in particular, if imputation accuracy would be markedly improved by a small change in some probabilities, then these can be prioritised for checking by physicians.
This could potentially reduce the burden of probbase re-estimation.
With sufficient data and an effective VA algorithm it may be possible to ‘learn’ a probbase to a greater extent, reducing reliance on physician-coded estimates. Correspondingly, if we are reasonably confident in the calibration of probbase, we may be able to use this method to evaluate VA algorithms, since algorithms which closely estimate true posterior distributions will generally lead to better imputation.

This paper is intended as a means to introduce our method and invite comment and suggestion. Although we provide some examples showing that the method is promising, we stress that extensive further work is needed to establish its scope and usefulness.
In this preliminary proposal, we organise our sections as follows.
We begin by describing our proposed method heuristically.
We then review recent work in this area, and related methods, with particular focus on Bayesian methods to learn the joint distribution of interview answers and causes of death.
We then introduce our notation and assumptions, establish some supporting theoretical results, and perform some short evaluations of our method.
We then describe a series of open questions and planned regimes for evaluation of the usefulness of the method.

Our code is publicly available at
https://github.com/jamesliley/VA_imputation
. We intend to develop a package for the Comprehensive R Archive Network (CRAN) implementing our methods, using similar syntax to the
openVA
package for verbal autopsy analysis.

1.1
Proposed Procedure

We firstly describe our proposed method heuristically. We aim to estimate what we call the ‘imputational ability’ of a probbase, given a set of completed verbal autopsy questionnaires.

We require an understanding of the covariance structure of interview answers.
In particular, we need to be able to identify ‘blocks’ of interview questions for which answers are conditionally independent given a cause of death.
That is, for a verbal autopsy for a patient who died of a given cause, the chance that they answered a question as ‘yes’ in a given block does not depend on their answers to questions in other blocks.

Start with:

•

A set of completed verbal autopsy interviews,

•

A set of question blocks

•

A probbase, and

•
1

A prior;

for

each block:

do

2

Start with the original set of interview answers;

3

For the block we have chosen, consider every question in that block which was answered (e.g. not missing);

4

For these questions, set the answers to ‘missing’, to get a new dataset;

5

Using this dataset with answers set to ‘missing’, evaluate the (posterior) probability of each cause of death for each individual using a verbal autopsy method, making use of the probbase and prior under investigation;

6

Using the cause-of-death probabilities from the previous step, evaluate the probability of seeing a ‘yes’ answer or a ‘no’ answer for each question which was left out;

7

Compare these probabilities with the actual answers to the left-out questions in the original set of interview answers;

8

Summarise this comparison numerically, and remember the result.
end for

9

Take the average value of this comparison across questions;

10

Return this average value.

1.3
Related work

Early verbal autopsy approaches used physician-coded analysis of questionnaires; that is, a physician reviewed the questionnaire and assigned a set of likely causes of death. This was recognised as being susceptible to inter-physician variability, and probabilistic methods were developed
(
Byass et al., 2003
)
.

The first statistical VA algorithm aiming to establish a Bayesian posterior distribution over cause of death (COD) given a set of symptoms was developed in
(
Byass et al., 2003
)
and extended in
(
Byass et al., 2019
)
. The development of automated VA algorithms was motivated by a need to reduce workload on physicians and standardise data assessment. Given a questionnaire
a\in\{0,1,NA\}^{s}
for which
a_{k}=1
for
k\in\mathcal{K}
, the InterVA4 algorithm assigns a verbal autopsy score
V_{j}(a,q)
as

P(D=d_{j}|A=a)\approx V_{j}(a,q)=\frac{\pi_{j}\prod_{k\in\mathcal{K}}q_{jk}}{\sum_{r}\pi_{r}\prod_{k\in\mathcal{K}}q_{rk}}

(3)

These methods (InterVA4 and InterVA5) are notable in that the posterior distribution is not directly estimated, in that absent symptoms (those
k
for which
a_{k}\in\{0,NA\}
) are not considered in calculations. An extension in which absent symptoms are included in the posterior calculation was developed in
(
Miasnikof et al., 2015
)
: if we define
\mathcal{K}=\{k:a_{k}\in\{0,1\}\}
then the associated VA score
V_{j}(a,q)
is defined as:

P(D=d_{j}|A=a)\approx V_{j}(a,q)=\frac{\pi_{j}\prod_{k\in\mathcal{K}}q_{jk}^{a_{k}}(1-q_{jk})^{1-a_{k}}}{\sum_{r}\pi_{r}\prod_{k\in\mathcal{K}}q_{rk}^{a_{k}}(1-q_{rk})^{1-a_{k}}}

(4)

This essentially uses a ‘Naive Bayes classifier’
(
James et al., 2013
)
: if, for any
k_{1},k_{2},j
we have that
P(A_{k_{1}}=a_{k_{1}},A_{k_{2}}=a_{k_{2}}|D=d_{j})=P(A_{k_{1}}=a_{k_{1}}|D=d_{j})P(A_{k_{2}}=a_{k_{2}}|D=d_{j})
then we have:

\displaystyle V_{j}(a,q)

\displaystyle=\frac{P(D=d_{j})\prod_{k\in\mathcal{K}}P(A_{k}=1|D=d_{j})^{a_{k}}P(A_{k}=0|D=d_{j})^{1-a_{k}}}{\sum_{r}P(D=d_{r})\prod_{k\in\mathcal{K}}P(A_{k}=1|D=d_{r})^{a_{k}}P(A_{k}=0|D=d_{r})^{1-a_{k}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{P(D=d_{j})P(A_{\mathcal{K}}=a_{\mathcal{K}}|D=d_{j})}{\sum_{r}P(D=d_{r})P(A_{\mathcal{K}}=a_{\mathcal{K}}|D=d_{r})}

\displaystyle=P(D=d_{j}|A_{\mathcal{K}}=a_{\mathcal{K}})

The mechanism for developing a probbase matrix in InterVA4 and InterVA5 is to consult a panel of expert physicians to estimate each probability, which is encoded qualitatively as one of
\{A,A+,\dots,F\}
. In InterVA4 and InterVA5, these are replaced with numeric values according to a fixed lookup table
(
Byass et al., 2003
;
Byass et al., 2019
)
. It is also possible to learn a probbase directly from labelled training data
(
Miasnikof et al., 2015
)
.

A method called InSilicoVA
(
McCormick et al., 2016
)
proposes a hierarchical Bayesian model which includes an explicit recalibration of the probbase matrix, in which a mapping is learned from letter codes to probabilities. The specification enables incorporation of additional information into the VA method, such as physician-assigned causes of death. A major aim of the InSilicoVA algorithm is to learn about the cause-specific mortality fraction at the population level, and the COD distributions at the individual level
(
McCormick et al., 2016
)
.

There has been significant attention on comparison of verbal autopsy algorithms, and the degree to which parameters (prior, probbase) matter to CoD assignments
(
Clark et al., 2018
)
. Further work on verbal autopsy analysis has extended the general Bayesian model
(
Li et al., 2019
;
Zhu and Li, 2024
;
Zhu and Li, 2025
)
, explored of sensitivity to the prior
(
Fottrell et al., 2011
)
, and expanded the scope of the method to include circumstances of mortality
(
D’Ambruoso et al., 2021
)
.
Other verbal autopsy methods include the ‘Tariff’ method
(
Serina et al., 2015
)
and a recently-developed method using large language models
(
Chu, 2025
)
.

A general Bayesian model is proposed in
(
Li et al., 2019
)
in which the joint distribution of causes of death and symptoms is modelled allowing for dependence between symptoms, using a spike-and-slab prior on the covariance to incentivise sparsity.
Posterior estimates of cause of death can then be attained by sampling from the posterior on distributional parameters.
This entails learning a probbase in a sense; or rather, starting with an estimate and modifying it according to the data in order to make a cause-of-death assignment.

1.4
Relation to our method

Our method is similar in spirit to
(
Li et al., 2019
)
in that we use the joint distribution of answers to infer the conditional distribution of causes of death. Indeed,
Li et al., 2019
is adaptable to learning distributional parameters - including the probbase - from a marginal distribution of only
A
. However, a Bayesian model for the distribution of
A,D
essentially requires a specification of the form of the likelihood
P(A=a|D=d_{j})
.
Our method, technically, aims to evaluate the accuracy of a VA algorithm using only unlabelled data without making any specifications on the form of the likelihood function - only the posterior. Our approach of attempting to learn a probbase from observed VA data is, of course, not original; both the InSilicoVA and Bayesian approach learn probbase elements to varying degrees
(
McCormick et al., 2016
;
Li et al., 2019
)
.

In summary, our approach, which is somewhat machine-learning inspired, jointly evaluates a VA method and a probbase, without the need for labelling of samples, or explicitly specifying a form for the likelihood function. We do require an explicit form for the posterior: but this is a VA method, which we consider to be an object under investigation. We consider an arbitrary VA method, and make no assumptions on the joint symptom-cause-of-death distribution other than a block-diagonal covariance structure.

2.3
Assumptions

We now state our main assumptions. The first concerns the joint distribution
(A,D)
.

Assumption 1
.

We presume that
A_{k_{1}},A_{k_{2}}
are conditionally independent given
D
if
k_{1}\subseteq B_{\ell_{1}}
,
k_{2}\subseteq B_{\ell_{2}}
with
\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}
. That is, we presume that, for
a_{1},a_{2}\in\{0,1,NA\}

P(A_{k_{1}}=a_{1},A_{k_{2}}=a_{2}|D=d_{j})=P(A_{k_{1}}=a_{1}|D=d_{j})P(A_{k_{2}}=a_{2}|D=d_{j})

(8)

This assumption guarantees that the joint distribution of
(A|D)
has a block-diagonal covariance structure. Practically, we assume that if we consider people who died of a particular CoD, and two questions in different blocks, those who answered ‘Yes’ to one question are no more or less likely to answer ‘Yes’ to the other.

We describe a possible approach to a mild violation of this assumption in Appendix
A2
. In particular, we consider the situation in which some questions (for instance, demographic variables) may be correlated with all other questions.

Our theoretical results, and our open conjecture, essentially state that:

1.

If our VA algorithm works well, then the imputation accuracy is best when the estimation of the probbase is correct (but may also be optimised for other incorrect probbases)

2.

If our probbase is ‘partly’ correct, then the imputation accuracy is best
only
if the rest of the probbase is correct; that is, no incorrect probbase can do as well.

3.

Conjecture: in general, as long as question answers are not too correlated with each other, the imputation accuracy is best only when the whole probbase is correct.

These results motivate searching for a probbase that makes the imputation accuracy as good as possible.

Suppose values

\left[I_{\mathcal{A}}(\hat{q})-I(\hat{q})\right]^{2}\leq c/n+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}\right)

We next show that, for a well-calibrated VA algorithm imputational ability is minimised (possibly non-uniquely) when the probbase is correct.

Theorem 1
.

Under assumption
1
, suppose we also have that

V_{0}(\alpha_{-{\ell}},\hat{q})_{j}=P(D=d_{j}|A_{-\ell}=\alpha_{-\ell})\text{ for all }\alpha\in\{0,1,NA\}^{s}:P(A_{-\ell}=\alpha_{-\ell})>0

The assumption in this theorem can be interpreted as ‘the VA algorithm is mostly-correctly calibrated’. A stronger-than-necessary version of this assumption can be stated as ‘the VA algorithm
V_{0}
is completely calibrated’, according to the following:

Assumption 2

(Complete calibration)
.

For a given sequence
\alpha\in\{0,1,NA\}^{s}
, let
s_{1}=\{k:\alpha_{k}\in\{0,1\}\}
. Then a VA algorithm
V_{0}
is ‘completely calibrated’ if for all

V_{0}(\alpha,q)_{j}=P(D=d_{j}|A_{s_{1}}=\alpha_{s_{1}})

that is, ‘given a VA questionnaire, the VA algorithm returns the correct posterior probability over
D
given the non-NA answers if the probbase is correct’. While VA algorithms generally aim to satisfy this assumption, it is often violated: in particular, the InterVA4 and InterVA5 algorithms do not satisfy it. Robustness of our method to this assumption is an important ongoing question.

The preceding theorem and lemma are the key supporting theoretical results for our method. In particular, it shows that in terms of imputation accuracy, with appropriate assumptions, we
cannot do better
than the correct probbase: if we find some other probbase for which the imputational ability improves, then the original probbase was not correct (though we cannot necessarily assert that the new one is better).

An important ongoing question is whether this minimum is achieved
only
for the correct probbase. For instance, with only the previous result, it could be that many potential probbases exist which minimise the imputational ability, only one of which is actually correct. In other words, we may find ourselves with a probbase for which we cannot seem to improve on the imputational ability; but can we be sure that this probbase is now correct?

Our next theoretical result is to say that in many cases we can indeed be sure of this. We require that the probbase is
partly
correct, and that the VA algorithm is well-calibrated if
and only if
the probbase is correct.

Theorem 2
.

Under assumption
1
, suppose that for any
\ell
we have:

V(\alpha_{-{\ell}},\hat{q})_{j}=P(D=d_{j}|A_{-\ell}=\alpha_{-\ell})\text{ for all }\alpha\in\{0,1,NA\}^{s}

if and only if
\hat{q}_{jk}=q_{jk}
for all
k\notin B_{\ell}
. Suppose also that for some
\iota
with
|B_{\iota}|\geq r
we have
\hat{q}_{jk}=q_{jk}
for
k\in B_{\iota}
. Then, almost surely, we have
I(\hat{q})>I(q)
for all
\hat{q}\neq q
, where by ‘almost surely’ we mean in the sense of considering the discrete distribution of
(A,D)
to be defined by a point in a continuous space.

The slightly unusual condition of requiring
q_{jk}=\hat{q}_{jk}
for all
k\notin B_{\ell}
can be understood by recognising that the values
\alpha_{-\ell}
give information only about answers not in
B_{\ell}
, so we would expect a VA algorithm to make use of probbase entries
\hat{q}_{jk}
with
k\notin B_{\ell}
.

We require an ‘if and only if’ condition, in contrast to Theorem
1
; that is, we can compute ‘correct’ cause-of-death probabilities only if we have a (mostly) correct probbase. This condition is violated if, for instance, the VA algorithm ignores the values
\hat{q}
entirely but can be correctly calibrated nonetheless. We may expect this to occur in the infinite data limit when
\hat{q}
is used as a prior (as is done in
(
Li et al., 2019
)
), since the reliance of the VA algorithm on
\hat{q}
will be small.

This motivates our search for:

q^{*}_{\mathcal{A}}:=\argmin I_{\mathcal{A}}(\hat{q})\approx\argmin I(\hat{q})\approx q

(11)

Proofs of Theorems 1 and 2 and Lemma 1 are given in Appendix
A1
.

We propose searching for a minimum of
I_{\mathcal{A}}(\hat{q})
over candidate values
\hat{q}
by gradient descent, although other minimisation methods may also be usable. If
V(\alpha,\hat{q})
is differentiable in
\hat{q}
, then so is
I_{\mathcal{A}}(\hat{q})
, motivating our search. However, the function
I_{\mathcal{A}}(\hat{q})
is likely to be highly nonconvex, given the dimensionality of
\hat{q}
. For this reason, we generally assume the probbase is ‘almost’ right, in the hope that the nearest local minimum is the global minimum.

3
Simulation

In order to evaluate our method, we need a dataset conforming to a known probabilistic model. We simulated VA data to resemble a realistic VA dataset as closely as possible (the WHO2012
RandomPhysician
dataset from the
openVA
package
(
Li et al., 2023
;
Li et al., 2025
)
).
This dataset consists of a set of VA questionnaires roughly annotated with causes of death.
In this section, we describe our approach to simulation of VA data and our evaluation of a potential probbase using the simulated data.

3.1
Practical Assumptions

We simulated data in the WHO2012 format
(
World Health Organization et al., 2012
)
. This was largely due to data availability and correspondence to the available probbase matrix. More recent VA data formats include 2016 and 2020 updates of the WHO format, and an alternative format from the Population Health Metrics Research Consortium
(
Murray et al., 2011
)
.

As above, a key assumption for imputing VA probabilities is that VA questions are split into conditionally independent blocks. We simulated VA data under this assumption. We sampled latent causes of death
D
according to a multinomial distribution parametrised by prior
\pi
.

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\sim\text{Multinomial}(\pi)

Following
(
Li et al., 2024
)
, for answers
A_{\ell}=\{A_{k}\}_{k\in b_{\ell}}
, we began with a latent variable
z_{\ell}=\{z_{k}\}_{k\in b_{\ell}}
simulated as:

\displaystyle(z_{\ell}|D=d_{j})

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{j\ell})

finally simulating non-demographic answers
A_{k}
from
z_{k}
using our probbase
q=\{q_{jk}\}
as

\displaystyle(A_{k}|D=d_{k})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}\left(z_{k}\leq\Phi^{-1}(q_{jk})\right)

where
\Phi^{-1}
is the inverse Gaussian cumulative density function. For our probbase, we used
probbase3
from the
openVA
package, converted to numeric values using the lookup table in the
InterVA
function in
openVA
. We used the standard prior
\pi
from this probbase, which is the same as that used by InterVA4.

We learned the block structure
\{b_{\ell}\}
and covariance matrices
\Sigma_{j\ell}
from the
RandomPhysician
dataset, by first computing the global covariance matrix across all answers for individuals allocated a particular cause of death, then hierarchically clustering results, and using sample covariance matrices for each resultant cluster.

We simulated age and sex data randomly according to frequencies in
RandomPhysician
, but did not use these. The integration of this data is a planned avenue for future work; see Appendix
A2
. This meant that our simulated dataset contained occasional paradoxical elements, such as pregnancy in the elderly.

Code for our simulation is available in the pipeline
imputation_test.R
in our GitHub repository at
https://github.com/jamesliley/VA_imputation

3.2
Methodology

Given our simulations, we then applied our method. We used the
interVA
algorithm
(
Li et al., 2014
)
, due to its simplicity and rapidity of calculation.

We considered two simulated datasets of VA values, one with 1500 entries, and one with 10000 entries. We firstly evaluated the imputation accuracy
I_{\mathcal{A}}(\hat{q})
for a variety of inputs
\hat{q}
:

•

q
: The correct probbase,
probbase3
,

•

\hat{q}^{1}
: A ‘chaotic’ probbase for which
\hat{q}_{jk}\sim U([0,1])
independently

•

\hat{q}^{2}
: A ‘very rough’ probbase derived from randomly ‘shuffling’ values of
q

•

\hat{q}^{3}
: A ‘globally perturbed’ probbase derived from perturbing each element of
q
independently by a value sampled from
\mathcal{N}(0,1/20)
,

•

\hat{q}^{4}
: A ‘slightly-perturbed’ probbase derived from perturbing 20 randomly-chosen elements of
q
independently by a value sampled from
\mathcal{N}(0,1/20)

We then attempted to evaluate the degree to which we could identify perturbed elements of a probbase. We simulated datasets as above with the true probbase given by
probbase3
. We considered elements of
probbase3
which exceeded 0.2 (to give us room to perturb them), of which there were about 750. We were beginning with a modified probbase
\tilde{q}
for which 250 entries had been increased by
0.2
, 250 entries had been decreased by 0.2, and (about) 250 had been left unchanged. We put ourselves in the position of a user who had only access to the ‘perturbed’ probbase, and no knowledge of which elements were increased or decreased, and established whether we could work this out.

We essentially did this by starting with the perturbed probbase, considering each element one-by-one, and seeing if imputation accuracy improved when we slightly increased or decreased that element. If we could substantially improve the imputation accuracy by changing that element to be higher or lower, then we concluded that that element was probably perturbed.

Technically, for each modified entry
\tilde{q}_{jk}
, we denoted
\tilde{q}^{+}
as
\tilde{q}
with
\tilde{q}_{jk}
replaced by
\tilde{q}_{jk}+\epsilon
and
\tilde{q}^{-}
as
\tilde{q}
with
\tilde{q}_{jk}
replaced by
\tilde{q}_{jk}-\epsilon
, using
\epsilon=1/100
. We then approximated:

\frac{\partial}{\partial\tilde{q}_{jk}}I_{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{q})\approx\frac{I_{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{q}^{+})-I_{\mathcal{A}}(\tilde{q}^{-})}{2\epsilon}:=\gamma_{jk}

(12)

and assessed whether
\gamma_{jk}
could predict
y_{jk}:=\text{sign}(\tilde{q}_{jk}-q_{jk})
.

4
Open questions and plans

Our theoretical results and brief numerical exploration are suggestive that this method could be useful in practice. However, we consider that the resolution (or exploration) of various open questions would be of benefit before this method enters use.

We anticipate that we will not be able to address all open questions in only this manuscript. Our current priorities are determining the extent to which the method is robust to covariance misspecification, extending simulations to closer to real data settings, and establishing use cases.
We also intend to try and further address theoretical questions, although these are not necessary for the method to be usable.

4.1
Theoretical questions

In general, it appears to be true that
I(\hat{q})
almost surely achieves its minimum only for
\hat{q}=q
, but this is difficult to prove. We state this loosely as:

Conjecture 1
.

For reasonable conditions on the block-covariance structure of
(A|D)
(as per assumption
1
) we have that

I(\hat{q})>I(q)

(14)

for all
\hat{q}\neq q
, almost surely.

We give some arguments for this in Appendix
A3
. Other theoretical questions include

1.

In various limits, can we disregard the block covariance structure? For example, we may have a
small
covariance between almost all questions, conditional on a CoD. As the number of questions becomes large, the deviation from a block-covariance or naive-Bayes assumption may have vanishing effect.

2.

Are the marginal values (probbase elements) identifiable if we have make no assumptions on conditional independence? Essentially, we consider whether a general method can exist for this problem without making any use of such an assumption. A counterexample would be two distinct distributions of CoDs and answers which led to the same ‘imputation scores’ (that is,
P(A_{k}=1|A_{-k}=a_{-k})
) but with different VA probabilities (that is,
P(D=d_{j}|A=a)
). We suspect that such counterexamples exist but have not yet explicitly constructed any.

4.2
Practical and computational questions

Our main practical aim is to establish the extent to which the method may be usable in real-world applications, beyond our simulated setting. This is likely to involve the use of labelled datasets.

1.

Can we apply this in a real-world setting? We immediately run into the very difficulty which motivated this work: that in order to assess performance of any VA method on real data, we need to have a ‘gold standard’ against which to compare it. We intend to consider a given probbase to be ‘accurate’ (even if it is not perfectly calibrated) with respect to a given set of VA data, then perturb it, and assess the performance of the perturbed probbase against the original. We may also be able to make direct use of labelled VA datasets
(
Byass et al., 2003
;
Clark et al., 2018
)
.

2.

Can this method be used to differentiate performance of different VA algorithms in a population? We have not yet used our method for this purpose, but in theory, with a correct probbase, we could compare different VA models, or different parametrisations of VA models (for instance, different covariance structures).

3.

Our method is philosophically similar to the approach in
Li et al., 2019
in which a Bayesian model is fitted to observed data, although the objective is somewhat different. It would be worthwhile to consider how these these methods may be best used together.

4.

How best should we manage minor violations of the conditional independence assumption? In particular, how robust is the method to minor violations? We cannot necessarily guarantee that blocks of the type in Assumption
1
will exist, or that we will correctly specify them. We intend to investigate robustness to violations of this assumption.

5.

Our ability to detect incorrect values of probbase will vary across elements of probbase: we would expect those that are used ‘more often’ to calculate posterior probabilities will be those for which errors are easier to detect. For instance, probbase entries concerning very rare causes of death may be only learnable very slowly, and associated partial derivatives of imputation accuracy may be low. We may be better to use an element-specific criterion for whether we consider an element to be perturbed, rather than a universal criterion on partial derivatives as in our simulations above.

6.

In the large-data limit, we expect that a probbase can be ‘learned’ from scratch, although without cause-of-death labellings. It is tempting to proceed in a wholly unsupervised way to find patterns of similar answers, potentially identifying epidemiologically important groupings or subtypes of the standard CoDs in the probbase. We may look at whether our method can be used towards this; the approach in
Li et al., 2019
is very promising in this regard.

5
Discussion

We propose a method to calibrate ‘probbase’ matrices for use in verbal autopsy analysis, using only completed verbal autopsy questionnaires without the need for labelling with causes of death. Our method is applicable with any verbal autopsy method, and requires only an assumption on the covariance structure of answers conditional on a given cause of death. Our method has only been superficially tested at this stage and we are not yet able to recommend its use in practical settings.

Our method is somewhat similar to a maximum-likelihood estimator, in that we consider a continuous objective depending on our object of inference (the probbase). We cannot directly use a true maximum-likelihood estimator, however, as the likelihood function - the probability of observing a set of answers given a cause of death - is not directly tractable in general, particularly if the verbal autopsy algorithm is complex (e.g., as in
(
Chu, 2025
)
, which makes use of large language models). We are in an unusual setting in which the likelihood is unspecified, but we have a given form for the posterior (the probability of a given cause of death given a set of answers), given by the VA algorithm in question.

Learning probbase matrices is difficult, but is a reasonably important task for VA operation
Clark et al., 2018
. The two dominant non-Bayesian methods - asking physicians to manually generate a probbase, or to assign CoDs to a training corpus of VA samples - are both susceptible to inter-clinician variation, or systematic errors in medical practice. Moreover, answer probabilities change across populations
Clark et al., 2018
, potentially substantially, meaning the process of training a probbase must either be repeated often, or a degree of miscalibration accepted. Even within a population, probbases may change with circumstances of mortality
(
D’Ambruoso et al., 2021
)
, with time, or across demographics, as we discuss in Appendix
apx:independence_violation
. Our method may give a quick option for doing this, or at least for abbreviating the process, by prioritising elements of probbases to review. This is a relatively unambitious aim. Our method more generally gives a method for assessing the overall combination of a probbase and VA algorithm, and could potentially be used more widely, particularly with a large corpus of unlabelled VA data, to learn parameters of a VA algorithm.

The main assumption in our method is of a block structure to the covariance of answer probabilities conditional on causes of death. This assumption may not hold in practice, and an important component of ongoing work is assessing robustness to this. Our hope is that these conditional independence blocks may be conserved across different populations, whilst marginal probabilities (probbases) may change. We recognise that some questions (in particular, demographic questions such as age and sex) violate this assumption by being essentially correlated with all answers: this violation can at least be managed in theory, as we describe in Appendix
A2
.

We can also guarantee that imputation ability is minimised only if a VA algorithm is well-calibrated, which many VA algorithms are not. Our method seems at least partly robust to this, as our simulations suggest a usable algorithm in the InterVA4 context. We also make an (effective) assumption that VA algorithms should treat missing values as though they are missing-at-random, and ‘marginalise them out’. VA algorithms taking advantage of non-random missingness will violate this assumption, and some extension may be needed to our method to account for this case.

In our simulations so far, we have exclusively used the InterVA4 algorithm
Byass et al., 2003
because it can be evaluated very quickly and because data are readily available. With more complex VA algorithms, computational time may become substantial. The method is readily parallelisable: in particular, across different VA questions. We have simply considered gradient of imputation accuracy as a marker for whether a probbase entry is perturbed, but other options are available; we could, for instance, make use of the second derivative.

We consider that our method may find application for unusual, hard-to-reach populations, for which existing probbases are likely to be miscalibrated and for which CoD- labelled data is likely to be unavailable. Our method is adaptable to a range of VA algorithms and models. We consider that the assessment of VA algorithms and probbases in the absence of labelled data is an important problem, and our approach contributes to a set of methods for doing this.
