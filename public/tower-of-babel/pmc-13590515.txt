A cross-national study of demographic variation and childhood predictors of traumatic distress
Authors: Meekang Sung, Seth A. Mattson, Richard G. Cowden, Renae Wilkinson, R. Noah Padgett, Christy Denckla, Koichiro Shiba, Byron R. Johnson, Tyler J. VanderWeele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraumatic distress is a major global public health concern requiring cross-cultural understanding.MethodsUsing nationally representative data from the Global Flourishing Study (N = 202,898; 22 countries), we estimated country-specific prevalence of traumatic distress and examined 9 sociodemographic and 13 childhood predictors. Random-effects meta-analyses assessed cross-country variation and associations.ResultsHere we show that the prevalence of traumatic distress ranges from 16% (Poland) to 53% (Egypt). Higher prevalence of distress is observed among younger individuals, gender-diverse groups, those separated from partners, unemployed, less educated, those born outside their country and those who attend religious services regularly. Several childhood experiences at age 12 are associated with a higher risk of traumatic distress, including abuse, family financial insecurity, feeling like an outsider, parental divorce, and poor health. Childhood abuse is a significant predictor in 21 countries.ConclusionsTraumatic distress prevalence and predictors vary across countries and are shaped by both current sociodemographic and early-life factors.

Introduction

Exposure to traumatic events, a shocking, fearful, or dangerous experience that can affect someone emotionally and physically1, is a pervasive global phenomenon with well-documented consequences for mental, emotional, social, and physical well-being across the lifespan2–5. The profound consequences of trauma make understanding, treating, and preventing trauma a public health priority6–8. The prevalence of trauma and its at times severe and impairing psychological sequelae, namely post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)9, have been well characterized. Further research is needed to quantify sociodemographic and early-life predictors that shape vulnerability to Traumatic distress (TD), which we capture broadly in this study as an individual’s experience of being bothered by prior traumatic exposure. Understanding these factors is essential for advancing trauma-informed public health strategies and for identifying high-risk populations across cultural and national contexts.

Levels of TD likely differ across different national contexts, shaped by the interplay of multiple individual, environmental, and societal factors. Such variation may reflect not only population-level differences in levels of exposure to traumatic events (e.g., violence, geopolitical conflict and consequences of climate change), but also the influence of broader sociocultural and structural factors that shape the experience and expression of trauma10.

Traumatic distress is a conceptual umbrella term meant to capture the broad range of responses to the subjectively experienced distress that results from experiencing a traumatic event. It has been argued that from a public health perspective, with the broader aim of preventing posttraumatic distress in as many people as possible, a broader understanding and definition of trauma is needed as to not be limited by DSM-5-TR narrow clinical definitions which require rather specific types of criterion A traumatic events as well as the clinical syndrome of PTSD11. Others have argued, in light of recent literature supporting greater heterogeneity within the PTSD construct, for strengths in a dimensional understanding of trauma which takes into account both the subtler and subjectively internalized forms of distress as a result of trauma, as well as the potential for resilience and growth in the face of recovery from a traumatic event12. PTSD therefore falls under the conceptual umbrella of traumatic distress as a severe form of distress from specific sub-types of traumatic events. Given that the literature in public health settings on this broader spectrum of traumatic distress is relatively underdeveloped, we draw upon extant literature relative to risk factors for PTSD as suggestive of the types of sociodemographic and childhood risk factors relevant to this study.

Globally, the World Mental Health Survey sampled both trauma exposure and subsequent PTSD across countries, including non-Western contexts. Among 71,083 respondents in 24 countries, 70% of the population reported exposure to a traumatic event, ranging from 29% in Romania to 83% in Peru5. PTSD was prevalent in 4% the general population, varying highly across countries based on income level9.

Many studies have studied the risk factors of PTSD. Identified risk factors of PTSD and subsequent distress include less social supports, minority race/ethnicity, immigration, single parenthood, female gender, family history of PTSD, economic and occupational instability, lower education, and childhood adversity (especially prior abuse)7,13–19, Women are consistently more likely to develop PTSD after exposure to a potentially traumatic event than men15,20–22, while gender non-binary and transgender persons experience significantly higher rates of trauma23. Although findings on age are mixed, younger people often report more trauma exposure24–26. Being married is also found to be a consistent protective demographic factor against PTSD5,27.

Regarding the impact of the level of religious and spiritual (R/S) involvement on trauma, higher levels of R/S involvement are generally associated with lower prevalence of PTSD, as demonstrated in nationally representative samples of US military veterans28 and a nationally representative sample of African Americans29,30. Other studies suggest that the effect varies drastically depending on type of R/S involvement, with negative forms of R/S coping associated with more severe PTSD symptomatology31,32.

There is increasing recognition of the long-term impact of early-life experiences on later mental health outcomes33,34. However, few studies have systematically linked these formative experiences to adult trauma using cross-national data. The World Mental Health Survey found four childhood adversities (physical and sexual abuse, neglect, and parental psychopathology) as the the only consistent predictors of developing PTSD35. Similarly, a meta-analysis of studies of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and later PTSD found that sexual abuse and experience of violence were the most predictive34. Regarding R/S factors, while there is some research on individual forms of R/S coping and trauma36,37, no research to date has explored the association of childhood religious service attendance with PTSD or TD in adulthood.

Although some large-scale studies have examined trauma in cross-cultural samples, none have conducted cross-national, population-level assessments of such a broad range of past and present factors linked to TD. The Global Flourishing Study (GFS), with nationally representative samples from 22 geographically diverse countries, provides opportunity to assess population-level trauma in different geographic and cultural contexts.

Leveraging Wave 1 data from the GFS, the present preregistered study addresses three primary aims: (1) to examine cross-national variation in TD prevalence; (2) to evaluate associations between sociodemographic characteristics and TD, including whether these associations vary across countries; and (3) to assess associations between childhood factors and TD, and the extent to which these relationships differ cross-nationally.

Across the 22 countries, we find that the prevalence of traumatic distress varies substantially, ranging from 16% in Poland to 53% in Egypt. Younger age, gender-diverse identity, separation from a partner, unemployment, lower education, regular religious service attendance, and being born outside one’s country of residence are associated with higher traumatic distress. Childhood adversities, including abuse, family financial insecurity, feeling like an outsider, parental divorce, and poor health, are linked to elevated adult traumatic distress in the majority of countries, with childhood abuse emerging as a significant predictor in 21 of the 22 countries. Together, these findings show the broad relevance of childhood experiences for later mental health, while highlighting cross-national heterogeneity that motivates context-sensitive approaches to prevention and intervention.

Methods

Methodological details described below have been adapted from a published paper on GFS38. Further methodological information is available elsewhere39–45. The research objectives, methodological details, and data analysis plan for this study were preregistered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/s7naf; https://osf.io/94s2n).

The GFS was ruled exempt from full human-subjects review requirements by the Baylor University Institutional Review Board (IRB) (#1841317-2) under 45 CFR 46.104(d)(2) because it met the criteria for exemption according to Baylor’s IRB guidelines (e.g., minimal risk to participants, adherence to specific federal IRB regulations). Ethical approval for all data collection activities was also obtained from the IRB at Gallup Inc. Data collection activities were performed in accordance with relevant ethical regulations, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. All personally identifiable information was removed from the data used in the present study by Gallup Inc.

Study sample

The GFS survey centers on salient aspects of well-being46,47, such as happiness, health, meaning, character, relationships, and financial stability48, along with other sociodemographic, social, economic, political, religious, personality, childhood, and community variables.

The first wave 1 of GFS data that were released in 2024 included nationally representative samples from 22 geographically and culturally diverse countries: Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Egypt, Germany, Hong Kong, India, Indonesia, Israel, Japan, Kenya, Mexico, Nigeria, the Philippines, Poland, South Africa, Spain, Sweden, Tanzania, Turkey, the United Kingdom, and the United States (N = 202,898). The countries were selected to (1) maximize coverage of the world’s population, (2) ensure geographic, cultural, and religious diversity, and (3) prioritize feasibility in line with existing data collection infrastructure. The GFS used three recruitment approaches: probability-based sampling, non-probability sampling, or a combination of both. In most countries, panel members were recruited through probability-based face-to-face or telephone methods and subsequently invited to complete the annual survey by phone or online42,49.

Data collection was conducted by Gallup Inc. Gallup translated the GFS survey into multiple languages39 following the TRAPD (translation, review, adjudication, pretesting, and documentation) model for cross-cultural survey research42,50. Data for Wave 1 was collected primarily during 2023, although some countries began data collection in 2022 and exact dates of data collection varied to some extent by country51.

Outcome

Past 30-day symptoms of distress related to trauma exposure, which we have captured for the purposes of this large epidemiologic study with the label traumatic distress (TD), was assessed using an adapted version of the single-item post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) screener which was developed to screen for distress arising from exposure to a traumatic event52. This item has been utilized in screening for probable risk of meeting criteria for PTSD in primary care settings. When dichotomized as “bothered” or “not bothered” by exposure to a traumatic event, this single item screener was found to validly serve as a proxy screener for those at relative low vs. high risk of having PTSD based on the PTSD checklist (PCL)51. We similarly dichotomized this variable as a proxy indicator of those who are more and less likely to have severe distress following exposure to a lifetime traumatic event. In asking participants to think about events which threatened their life directly or were witnessed, this definition of a traumatic event generally follows the language of criterion A events in the Revised Version of Fifth Edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM-5-TR)53 as exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or threatened violence. Cognitive testing was performed with this item during the survey development process for the GFS to strengthen cross-cultural equivalence54.

The screener item administered in the GFS Wave 1 survey was, “Think about the biggest threat to life you’ve ever witnessed or experienced first-hand during your life. In the past month, how much have you been bothered by this experience?” Response options included “A lot”, “some”, “not very much”, and “not at all”. In the primary analyses, the responses were recoded into a dichotomous variable of experienced TD (i.e., “a lot”, “some”) vs. did not experience TD (i.e., “not very much”, and “not at all”). This approach was consistent with the preregistered analytic plan and other studies using GFS data with a similar response scale55,56. While the GFS does not include a clinical diagnostic measure, construct validity of the TD measure was supported by meaningful polychoric correlations with PHQ-4 derived depression (r = 0.41) and anxiety (r = 0.44) symptom composites, indicating that the measure captures clinically relevant negative affect.

Variables

Sociodemographic characteristics of TD

Participants self-reported their sociodemographic characteristics, including: gender (male, female, other), marital status (single/never married, married, separated, divorced, widowed, or domestic partner), employment status (employed, self-employed, retired, student, homemaker, unemployed and searching, or other), educational attainment (up to 8 years, 9-15 years, or 16+ years), religious service attendance ( > 1x/week, 1x/week, 1-3x/month, a few times a year, or never), and immigration status (born in this country, born in another country). Although the gender item asked “What is your gender?”, the response options were female and male rather than woman and man; accordingly, this paper will use female and male throughout. Birth year/current age was assessed using participant reports of their current age (in years), and birth year was calculated based on the year of data collection minus their current age. We created the following age groups: 18-24, 25-29, 30-39, 40-49, 50-59, 60-69, 70-79, and 80 or older (see tables for birth year ranges). Religious affiliation in adulthood was also assessed, and response options included 15 major religions [e.g., Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Judaism, etc.], “some other religion,” or “no religion/atheist/agnostic”50. Race and ethnicity were assessed in most but not all countries (Germany, Japan, Spain, and Sweden had restrictions on collecting such data), and response categories were specific to each country. In the childhood predictor analyses, race and ethnicity were dichotomized as racial/ethnic plurality (the category with the largest proportion) and minority (collapsing other categories) in each country to reduce data sparsity.

Additional details about the measurement of the sociodemographic variables can be found in the GFS Codebook (https://osf.io/cg76b).

Childhood predictors of TD

Relationships with parents

Participants were asked to assess the quality of their relationships with parents using separate questions about their mother and father: “Please think about your relationship with your [mother/father] when you were growing up. In general, would you say that relationship was very good, somewhat good, somewhat bad, or very bad?” Responses were dichotomized to “very/somewhat good” versus “very/somewhat bad” to reduce collinearity in regression models. “Does not apply” was treated as a dichotomous control variable for respondents who did not have a mother or father due to death or absence.

Parents’ marital status

Marital status of parents during childhood was assessed with the question: “Were your parents married to each other when you were around 12 years old?” Response options included “married,” “divorced,” “never married,” and “one or both of them had died.”

Subjective financial status growing up

Participants reported their subjective assessment of the financial status of their family while growing up with the question: “Which one of these phrases comes closest to your own feelings about your family’s household income when you were growing up, such as when YOU were around 12 years old?” Response options included “lived comfortably,” “got by,” “found it difficult,” and “found it very difficult.”

Experienced abuse

Abuse during childhood was assessed with the question: “Were you ever physically or sexually abused when you were growing up?” Response options were “yes” and “no.” This variable was not measured in Israel.

Outsider in family growing up

Participants were asked about their feelings of family belonging during childhood using the question: “When you were growing up, did you feel like an outsider in your family?” Response options were “yes” and “no.”

Childhood health

Self-rated health during childhood was assessed using responses to the question: “In general, how was your health when you were growing up? Was it excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor?”

Childhood religious service attendance

The frequency of religious attendance during childhood was assessed using the question: “How often did YOU attend religious services or worship at a temple, mosque, shrine, church, or other religious building when you were around 12 years old?” Response options included >1x/week, 1x/week, 1-3x/month, a few times a year, or never.

Childhood religious affiliation

Childhood religious tradition/affiliation was assessed using the question: “What was your religion when you were 12 years old?” To reduce data sparsity, in regression models the response categories of religious affiliation with a prevalence <3% were collapsed. The “no religion/atheist/agnostic” group was used as the reference group when at least 3% of the observed sample within the country endorsed this category; otherwise, the most prominent religious group was used as the reference category.

The childhood measures included in the GFS were each anchored to age 12, consistent with precedent established in prior research that used religious service attendance at age 12 as a standard reference point57. This approach ensured consistency across related retrospective items and reduced the cognitive burden associated with recalling multiple distinct life stages. Further details regarding questionnaire development have been reported elsewhere.⁴⁰

Missing data

Missing data on all variables were imputed using multivariate imputation by chained equations, with five imputed datasets produced58,59. To account for variation in the assessment of certain variables across countries (e.g., religious affiliation, racial/ethnic identity), the imputation process was conducted separately in each country. This within-country imputation approach ensured that the imputation models accurately reflected country-specific contexts and assessment methods. Sampling weights were incorporated as predictors in the imputation models to account for differential probabilities of selection and post-stratification adjustments, under the assumption that item-level missingness may be associated with factors related to survey inclusion and response49. Response rates of the survey ranged from 24.0% in South Africa to 100.0% in Japan42.

Statistical analysis

All analyses accounted for the complex survey design components by including weights, primary sampling units, and strata.

Cross-national prevalence of TD

To examine cross-national variation in TD prevalence, nationally representative proportions for TD, along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were estimated separately for each country and ordered from highest to lowest.

Cross-national associations between sociodemographic characteristics and TD

Descriptive statistics for the full sample were estimated for each sociodemographic characteristic, weighted to approximate national representativeness within each country based on standard demographic characteristics derived from country-specific census data or high-quality external population benchmarks. Weights were calibrated to the adult population of each country, with trimming applied to control for variance inflation. Random effects meta-analysis, along with 95% CIs, standard errors, lower and upper limits of a 95% prediction interval across countries, heterogeneity (τ), and I2, were used for estimating variation within a particular sociodemographic variable across countries60. Within each country, a global test of variation of TD, across levels of each sociodemographic characteristic was conducted, and a pooled global p-value61 was reported across countries to evaluate if a given association for the sociodemographic characteristic with TD holds within at least one country. Bonferroni correction was applied by dividing the significance level (α = 0.05) by the number of sociodemographic characteristics examined to adjust for multiple comparisons62,63.

Cross-national associations between childhood factors and TD

Similar analyses were conducted for childhood variables. Childhood religious affiliation and race and ethnicity were used in the childhood predictor analyses, when available, as control variables within countries, but were not included in the meta-analysis because their response options varied by country. Descriptive statistics for the full sample, weighted to be nationally representative within each country, were estimated for each childhood variable. In each country, a weighted modified Poisson regression model with complex survey-adjusted standard errors was used to regress TD on all candidate childhood factors simultaneously, mutually adjusting for one another. Random effects meta-analysis was used for estimating association between TD and candidate childhood predictors across countries. A global test of association between TD and each candidate childhood predictor was conducted, and Bonferroni correction was applied by dividing the significance level (α = 0.05) by the number of predictors to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Robustness and sensitivity analyses

For each childhood variable, in both the country-specific analyses and meta-analyses, E-values were calculated to evaluate the sensitivity of the results to potential unmeasured confounding. An E-value is the minimum strength of the association an unmeasured confounder must have with both the outcome and the predictor, above and beyond all measured covariates, for an unmeasured confounder to explain away the observed association64. As a supplementary analysis, population weighted meta-analyses were performed to pool the regression coefficients from the country-specific analyses.

Younger participants (ages 18–24) were most likely to report TD (0.37, 95% CI: 0.33–0.41) and then decreased steadily across older age groups to its lowest among those aged 80 or older (0.23, 95% CI: 0.11–0.43). Females had a higher prevalence of TD (0.38, 95% CI: 0.33–0.42) compared with males (0.34, 95% CI: 0.29–0.38), and identifying as “other” gender had the highest prevalence of TD (0.45, 95% CI: 0.15–0.79). By marital status, separated participants reported the highest prevalence (0.45, 95% CI: 0.40–0.50), whereas married participants reported the lowest (0.34, 95% CI: 0.30–0.39). Employment status also showed variation, with those in the “none of these/other” category (0.42, 95% CI: 0.38–0.47) and the unemployed seeking work (0.41, 95% CI: 0.37–0.44) reporting the highest prevalence, while retirees reported the lowest (0.33, 95% CI: 0.29–0.38). Lower educational attainment was associated with a greater prevalence of TD, with a rate of 0.40 (95% CI: 0.35–0.42) observed among those with ≤8 years of schooling compared to 0.32 (95% CI: 0.28–0.36) among those with 16 or more years. For religious service attendance, prevalence of TD was higher among those attending services 1–3 times per month (0.40, 95% CI: 0.35–0.45) or more compared to those who never attended (0.34, 95% CI: 0.29–0.38).

In Argentina and Brazil, non-overlapping CIs indicated significantly higher TD among females compared to males (Table S1b, Table S3b). Consistent with the pooled meta-analytic results, in 18 countries married participants reported the lower TD prevalence compared to divorced individuals. In the United States and Nigeria, separated participants reported substantially higher TD (proportion difference >0.2) relative to married participants, and widowed participants in Israel showed notably elevated levels compared to partnered participants (0.55,95% CI: 0.46, 0.63) (Table S20b, Table S12b).

Employment patterns and educational gradients were consistent with the meta-analysis across most countries. In 17 of 22 countries, individuals with 16 or more years of education had lower TD than those with eight years of education or less. An exception was Nigeria (Table S12b), where those with ≤8 years of education reported lower TD (0.38, 95% CI: 0.30–0.47) than those with 16+ years (0.51, 95% CI: 0.42–0.60). Results for age and religious service attendance largely mirrored the pooled findings, although Kenya displayed an inverse age gradient (Table S10b), with younger participants reporting lower TD than older groups. Also contrary to pooled results, weekly religious service attendance compared to never attendance was associated with lower TD in Israel and Nigeria (Table S8b, Table S12b).

Cross-national associations between childhood factors and TD

Table 5 presents results from random-effects meta-analyses examining childhood predictors of adult TD. All pooled global p-values were below the Bonferroni-corrected threshold, indicating that each childhood factor was associated with TD in at least one of the 22 countries.

Childhood abuse showed the strongest association, with a 33% higher risk of TD in adulthood (RR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.26–1.40). Feeling like an outsider in one’s family while growing up had the third strongest association (RR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.11–1.24), behind only childhood abuse and self-rated health as “poor” growing up. Parental marital disruption was linked to higher TD compared with having married parents, particularly for those whose parents were divorced (RR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.03–1.16). Subjective financial status during childhood demonstrated a dose-dependent association: higher status (lived comfortably vs. got by) was protective (RR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.94–0.99), while lower status increased risk (found it difficult vs. got by: RR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.02–1.10; found it very difficult vs. got by: RR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.06–1.13). A similar pattern was observed for self-rated childhood health, with better health associated with reduced risk (excellent vs. good: RR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.83–0.95; very good vs. good: RR = 0.92, 95% CI: 0.89–0.96) and poorer health linked to increased risk (fair vs. good: RR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.03–1.14; poor vs. good: RR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.11–1.37). Religious service attendance in childhood was positively associated with adult TD, with a higher risk among those attending weekly (RR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.01–1.12) or 1–3 times per month (RR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.04-1.21), compared with never attending. Female respondents were more likely than males to report TD in adulthood (RR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.05–1.14).

Country-specific results for childhood abuse and feeling like an outsider in one’s family were largely consistent with the meta-analysis. Childhood abuse was significantly associated with a greater risk of adult TD in all countries except Turkey (Table S18c), where the association was in the same direction, but the 95% CI crossed the null (RR = 0.15, 95% CI: –0.01, 0.32). Feeling like an outsider in one’s family showed an elevated risk of TD in 20 countries, while the strongest association was observed in Hong Kong (RR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.30–1.68) (Table S22c). Weekly religious service attendance at childhood was associated with higher TD in Hong Kong, Japan, Mexico, Spain, and Sweden, but with lower TD in Poland (Table S14c).

Robustness Analyses

Table S23 shows that the associations between childhood factors and adult TD were moderately robust to unmeasured confounding. For example, the E-value for childhood abuse (RR = 1.33) was 1.99 (1.83 for the CI limit), indicating that an unmeasured confounder would need to increase the risk of both abuse and TD by nearly twofold, beyond measured covariates, to fully explain away the observed association.

Discussion

There are several findings of this study. First, there is substantial cross-national differences in TD prevalence, with the highest levels in Egypt (0.53), Turkey (0.52), and Brazil (0.51), and the lowest in Sweden (0.25), Indonesia (0.22), and Poland (0.16). Second, meta-analysis results revealed consistent sociodemographic patterns across countries: TD prevalence was lower at older ages, while higher prevalence was observed among those who were separated or divorced, unemployed, less educated, foreign-born, attended religious services regularly, and female. While most country-specific results mirrored these meta-analytic patterns, several exceptions stood out: a reversed age gradient in Kenya, a protective association of weekly religious service attendance in Poland, and higher TD risk among native-born participants relative to foreign-born participants in Brazil. Third, several childhood factors were associated with increased TD risk in adulthood in the meta-analysis: childhood abuse, poor self-rated health, feeling like an outsider in one’s family, financial insecurity, parental divorce, and regular religious service attendance. Childhood abuse showed significant associations with TD risk in 21 countries.

Differences in distribution of TD across countries likely reflect complex interactions among cultural norms, political conditions, community resilience factors, and actual exposure to trauma. For example, cultural norms and community resilience may impact appraisal of how bothersome an event it; in an adjacent analysis on suffering in this same data set, certain countries such as Germany show relatively low prevalence of TD but high prevalence of suffering55. Regarding country-specific differences, recent population-level research in Poland demonstrates disproportionally higher levels of individual and inter-generational trauma65,66. Reasons for the discrepancy may point to limitations in single-item screening for TD, particularly when subjective experience combined with community resilience may complicate the reliability of capturing impairment from PTSD symptoms. Conversely, Egypt demonstrated the highest prevalence of TD, concurrent with recent country-specific research suggesting high levels of trauma exposure across various domains, including significant gender-based and political violence67.

The sociodemographic variables consistent across countries replicate previous studies, including higher TD among females, unemployed, and those with less education. Marital stability conferred a protective effect68,69, while individuals experiencing marital disruption had higher levels of TD, with separation conferring particularly high prevalence. Also similar to previous findings, prevalence of TD tended to be lower among older age groups25,26. Yet, because Wave 1 data are cross-sectional, it is unclear whether this difference reflects reduced exposure to trauma or the possibility that aging fosters more resilient and adaptive interpretations of past experiences70.

In pooled cross-country meta-analytic results involving childhood predictors of adult TD, childhood adversity emerged as a consistent and powerful predictor, reinforcing prior evidence that early-life deprivation and relational instability can have enduring psychological consequences71,72. The high prevalence and strong negative impact of childhood abuse (a 33% increased risk of TD, highest of all childhood factors) underscores the need for continued intervention as early as possible for at-risk children. In adults, special attention should be paid to screening for and treating often under-recognized consequences of these childhood adversities, such as Complex-PTSD and Moral Injury73.

The associations with religious service attendance were more complex. Contrary to our hypothesis and prior work suggesting protective effects of religious involvement, both childhood and current regular attendance were linked with elevated TD risk in most countries. While religious service attendance was associated with benefit for most outcomes in the GFS38, pain and suffering were also exceptions. With the demographic analyses, this may partially reflect the possibility that individuals struggling with TD are more likely to seek communal religious involvement to cope. Religious experience may in part potentially make one more aware of or sensitive to various forms of distress. The exception was Poland, where weekly attendance was associated with lower TD. These divergent findings underscore the need to consider cultural and contextual moderators when evaluating the role of communal forms of religion in trauma.

Taken together, a combination of risk and protective factors during childhood and adulthood suggest several potential priorities for public health. Early-life factors such as childhood abuse, poverty, and family instability appear particularly influential, representing targets for prevention efforts. In mitigating risk factors, screening might be considered for children who report outsider status, poor health, or family disruption, as these early signals could indicate heightened vulnerability. Supporting material and marital stability in parents may confer protection to children. Relatedly, in adulthood, periods of geographic, relational or economic disruption, such as migration, separation/divorce and unemployment, may mark points of increased risk where supportive interventions could prove valuable.

This study points to several directions for future research. Further work is needed to understand the mechanisms by which experiences like family alienation and outsider status confer lasting vulnerability. Cross-national variations may provide valuable opportunities to refine theories of trauma and resilience in diverse cultural contexts. Finally, how communal religious experience might facilitate both positive and negative ways of coping with trauma is yet unclear.

There are several limitations. First, TD was assessed with a single item, which may not capture the full conceptual scope of the construct. Some of the differences with prior literature may be attributable to measurement, such as differences in whether TD or PTSD was assessed. For example, factors such as the frequency, type, and recency of prior traumatic events are key factors in quantifying the nature and severity of distress as well as risk factors relevant to future recovery or impairment. More exhaustive and specific measures of PTSD needed to quantify these factors were beyond the feasible scope of this current study. Relatedly, the GFS did not include an embedded validation subsample administered a multi-item PTSD instrument, which would be needed to estimate the positive predictive value of the single-item measure or to impute predicted probabilities of PTSD across the larger sample. Second, there may be cultural variation in how individuals interpret the TD question and response options, and potential seasonal effects. Third, childhood predictors were reported retrospectively and may be subject to recall bias; however, such bias would need to be at least as strong as the observed associations themselves to fully account for the findings74. Fourth, the childhood predictor models may be affected by overadjustment, as some predictors could plausibly lie on the causal pathway of others, making the true associations of some childhood factors stronger than estimated. Fifth, although data were drawn from 22 countries across different regions, many cultural contexts remain unrepresented, limiting the generalizability of findings beyond the included countries. Sixth, the sociodemographic characteristics are measured cross-sectionally and it is therefore difficult to establish a causal relationship.

In conclusion, this multi-country study highlights consistent sociodemographic patterns in traumatic distress: older individuals reported lower traumatic distress prevalence, whereas prevalence was higher among those who were separated or divorced, unemployed, or had completed less education. Early-life adversities—including childhood abuse, poor parental relationships, family alienation, parental marital disruption, financial hardship, and suboptimal childhood health—were also linked to elevated traumatic distress risk in adulthood, as was regular religious service attendance. These findings emphasize the importance of developing public health strategies that address the long-term impact of childhood adversity and prioritize support for socially and economically vulnerable populations.
