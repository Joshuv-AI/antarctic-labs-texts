Surface Composition and Temperature Interact to Shape Viral Survival and Growth
Authors: Ketty Kabengele, Anmol Seth, Thomas Johnson, C Brandon Ogbunugafor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEnvironmental factors play a critical role in viral persistence, yet the combined effects of temperature and surface chemistry on viral fitness are not fully understood. In this study, we examined bacteriophages T4 and φX174 across copper, stainless steel, and polypropylene plastic surfaces at 4°C and 37°C to assess how these variables influence survival and growth. Both phages exhibited marked differences in survival across surfaces: Copper at 37°C caused rapid inactivation and cooler conditions promoted persistence. Growth varied independently of survival, indicating that viral stability and growth are partially decoupled traits shaped by environmental context. Linear modelling and correlation analyses confirmed significant, non‐additive interactions between surface type and temperature. These findings suggest that abiotic factors can act synergistically to determine viral fitness outside the host. More broadly, they refine our understanding of the mechanisms that govern the environmental ecology of viruses.

Introduction

The threat of epidemics and pandemics has led to increased interest in the disease life history. Pathogens employ various routes to transmit between hosts, including airborne, asymptomatic, sexual, vector‐borne and others (Meena et al. 2019; Antonovics et al. 2017). Indirect transmission has received relatively little attention by comparison, where pathogens are transmitted through an environmental intermediate such as a physical surface, sometimes summarized as transmission via ‘fomites’ (Gwaltney et al. 1978; Pancic et al. 1980; Hall et al. 1980; Keswick et al. 1983; Ansari et al. 1988; Abad et al. 1994; Walther and Ewald 2004; Zhao et al. 2012).

The free‐living survival of viruses on surfaces has been the focus of study in recent years. For example, studies of influenza (Bean et al. 1982; Ewald 1991; Boone and Gerba 2005; Mubareka et al. 2009; Killingley and Nguyen‐Van‐Tam 2013; Asadi et al. 2020), noroviruses (Ho et al. 1989; Barker et al. 2004; Wu et al. 2005; Jones et al. 2007; Marshall and Bruggink 2011; Fallahi and Mattison 2011; Repp and Keene 2012; Tuladhar et al. 2013; Xiao et al. 2017; Overbey et al. 2021), and coronaviruses (Doremalen et al. 2020; Xie et al. 2020; Fedorenko et al. 2020; Todt et al. 2021; Ratnesar‐Shumate et al. 2020; Kwon et al. 2021) have demonstrated that viruses differ in their ability to survive on surfaces, which may influence outbreak dynamics and public health interventions. Metals such as copper are known to exhibit strong antimicrobial activity (Faúndez et al. 2004; Wilks et al. 2005; Noyce et al. 2007; Mehtar et al. 2008; Grass et al. 2011; Bleichert et al. 2014; Rani et al. 2021; Nakano et al. 2025). Copper, for example, generates reactive ions that damage viral capsids and nucleic acids, while surfaces such as polypropylene plastic and stainless steel tend to preserve structural integrity. These material‐specific properties can therefore modulate both viral persistence and replicative capacity. In addition to the study of surface survival, previous work has identified several abiotic factors that influence viral survival, including temperature, humidity and pH (Fedorenko et al. 2020; Noyce et al. 2007; Feng et al. 2003; McDevitt et al. 2010; Noti et al. 2013; Guo et al. 2021). Similarly, cooler temperatures tend to extend viral persistence relative to warmer ones (McDevitt et al. 2010; Páez et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2025). A larger literature on thermal adaptation in viruses has revealed that temperature adaptation can be a highly evolvable trait across different virus types (Gloria‐Soria et al. 2020; Alto et al. 2013; McBride et al. 2008). Together, these findings highlight the importance of abiotic stressors in shaping viral persistence. However, most studies have examined abiotic factors in isolation. Less well understood are the interactive effects of multiple stressors operating in tandem.

An additional dimension often overlooked in studies of viral persistence is growth. In the context of viral ecology, survival refers to the ability of free viral particles to remain infectious outside a host cell, while reproduction (or growth) refers to the capacity of those particles, once they encounter a suitable host, to initiate infection and produce new progeny. These two components of fitness are conceptually and mechanistically distinct, yet both contribute to the overall success of a virus in a given environment. Scientists have often framed these traits in the framework of life history theory (Stearns 1976; Kochin et al. 2010; Keen 2014; Wang 2017; De Paepe and Taddei 2006; Goldhill and Turner 2014). Furthermore, experimental and theoretical studies have proposed that survival and reproductive potential can have a variety of relationships, depending on the characteristics of virus‐host interactions (De Paepe and Taddei 2006; Kabengele et al. 2024; Bonhoeffer et al. 1996; Brandon Ogbunugafor et al. 2013). This raises fundamental questions about how survival and growth relate in different environments and whether the relationship between these traits is consistent, context‐dependent or idiosyncratic.

Understanding how specific environmental conditions influence both survival and reproductive potential can help refine public health strategies, improve risk assessment and inform theoretical frameworks that guide constraints on pathogen evolution. To address outstanding questions about how environmental factors shape the relationship between viral free‐living survival and growth, we investigated how temperature and surface composition jointly influence these traits using two well‐characterized bacteriophages, φX174 (Sanger et al. 1978) and T4 (Miller et al. 2003), as model systems. These phages were tested on three surfaces (copper, polypropylene plastic and stainless steel) at two temperatures (4°C and 37°C). This work builds upon previous studies by quantifying viral survival rates across multiple surface types and temperatures, measuring the growth rates of surviving viruses and exploring the relationship between survival and growth in these specific environments. In this study, survival is defined as the number of viable phage particles remaining after each treatment, while growth is the number of progeny viruses produced by phages that survive treatment. Although T4 and φX174 are bacteriophages, they provide useful models for general principles of viral ecology: they are genetically distinct, differ in morphology and life history strategies and have been widely used in laboratory studies of stability and evolution. Moreover, their life history constraints can inform our understanding of other viruses, including those transmitted via fomite‐mediated pathways.

The surface materials and temperatures chosen for this study are ecologically and practically relevant. Stainless steel and polypropylene are ubiquitous in medical, domestic and industrial settings, while copper is increasingly used for its antimicrobial properties, though efficacy may depend on temperature. The temperatures used (4°C and 37°C) bracket conditions commonly encountered in natural and clinical environments. By quantifying decline rates, growth rates and correlations between survival and growth across surface‐temperature environments, our study provides insight into how abiotic interactions shape viral fitness. A central objective of this work was to determine whether survival and growth are correlated across environmental contexts. Previous research has demonstrated that viruses can face trade‐offs between persistence and growth (De Paepe and Taddei 2006), but whether such trade‐offs are consistent or context‐dependent remains an open question. A question that our experimental design was specifically constructed to address. More broadly, this work demonstrates how combinations of environmental contexts can drive non‐additive outcomes in viral ecology, with implications for understanding the persistence of human and animal pathogens, informing epidemiological models of fomite‐mediated transmission and informing theories of microbial ecology and epidemiology.

Materials and Methods

Bacteriophage Propagation and Host Growth

Both φX174 (ATCC 13706‐B1) and T4 (11303‐B4) phages were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and grown in their recommended host bacteria, E. coli. φX174 was grown in the host bacteria E. coli strain C (ATCC BAA‐13706) following the ATCC guidelines, and T4 was grown in MG1665 derived from E. coli K‐12 (Baba et al. 2006).

Both hosts were grown overnight at 37°C with shaking at 160 rpm in their respective media (MG1655 in Luria Bertani liquid media, φX174 host in nutrient broth ATCC Medium 129). Viral propagation was performed in liquid media according to protocols recommended and published on the ATCC website. Viral dilutions were prepared in suitable liquid media, and a range of concentrations was evaluated to identify the most appropriate stock concentrations for experimental use: 10−5 PFU/mL for φX174 and 10−7 PFU/mL for T4. The optimal dilutions were selected based on plaque visibility and countability. All dilutions were stored at 4°C as recommended by ATCC. All material surfaces were purchased online from Amazon. High‐density polypropylene plastic (USA Sealing bulk‐PS‐PP‐323, 1/8 in. in height, 16 in. in width, 16 in. in length) was purchased and cut into smaller squares (2 in. long and 2 in. wide). Stainless steel discs (2 in. in diameter, 15 gauge thickness circular plate 304) and circular copper metal discs (24.26 mm in diameter, 99.9% pure). All materials were thoroughly sanitized, washed and soaked in alcohol prior to the experiments (Figure 1).

Results

To investigate how temperature and surface type influence viral stability, we employed two well‐characterized bacteriophages, T4 and φX174. Each was exposed to copper, stainless steel and polypropylene plastic at 4°C and 37°C. The two viruses were exposed to surfaces at 4°C and 37°C. For each phage, surface and temperature combination, three biological replicates were conducted. Controls were maintained in Eppendorf tubes at 4°C. A volume of 2000 μL of phage lysate was added to each surface at the beginning of the experiment, with 100 μL samples collected every 2 h up to 8 h for viral quantification by plaque assay (Figure 1a). At the final time point (8 h), samples were taken from each surface, and growth was measured by infecting bacteria with the sampled phages (Figure 1b). To measure growth, we added 100 μL of host bacteria grown overnight OD600 of 0.4−0.5 and 100 μL of phage lysate, and growth was measured as the increase in phage titre every 10 min starting at 30 min post‐infection until 60 min post infection. Viral quantification for both growth and survival was performed using the plaque assay method. 100 μL of appropriate host bacteria were mixed with 100 μL of phages: 10−5 PFU/mL for φX174 and 10−7 PFU/mL for T4 and 3 mL of 50°C top agar (25 g of LB broth, 7.5 g agar and 1 L of DI water), then poured onto nutrient agar plates for φX174 and LB agar plates for T4 and incubated overnight at 37°C. Plaques were counted the next day and virus titers were calculated.

Effects of Surfaces and Temperature on Survival and Growth

Our results demonstrate that surface type can markedly influence the rate at which phages lose stability, particularly on copper surfaces (Figure 2a, Figure 3, Figure S1, Tables S1 and S2). In contrast, both stainless steel and polypropylene plastic supported prolonged viral stability, with minimal temperature effects at 4°C. A Kruskal–Wallis test revealed a significant temperature effect for phages exposed to copper (p=0.0021). For viruses on stainless steel, the identity of phage, rather than temperature, was the significant factor (p=0.0039). On plastic surfaces, neither phage type nor temperature produced a significant effect. Control experiments confirmed that temperature alone influenced stability (p=0.0039), with phages at 4°C exhibiting higher survival than those at 37°C. Table S1 summarizes these results.

Survival on Surfaces

We used linear models to estimate the rate of loss of viability for T4 and φX174 across surfaces. On stainless steel and plastic, φX174 displayed slightly slower decline rates than T4 at equivalent temperatures Figure 2b, and Table S3, suggesting differences in environmental persistence between the two viruses. Both phages exposed to copper at 37°C exhibited pronounced reductions in half‐life (Figure 2c and Figure 3, and Table S3). φX174 had a half‐life of approximately 0.54 h, while T4 showed near‐complete inactivation, indicating a strong thermal effect on copper surfaces. Both φX174 and T4 showed high survival rates on control surfaces, regardless of temperature (φX174 = 93.3% and T4 = 95.7%). In contrast, exposure to copper significantly reduced survival, decreasing φX174 to 39.4% and T4 to 2.5% at 4°C and completely inactivating both viruses at 37°C. However, on plastic and stainless steel, φX174 showed greater persistence than T4. Taken together, these results indicate that temperature effects on these phages are surface‐dependent, revealing a pattern of phage‐by‐surface interaction. Furthermore, these findings suggest that phage inactivation cannot be predicted solely by surface factors, but rather by statistical interactions among them Figure 3 and Table S2.

Since our linear models showed that both phage and temperature were significant, we further tested the interaction effects using estimated marginal means. We found that in control experiments at 4°C, φX174, and T4 had comparable survival rates (93.3% vs. 95.7%). However, when copper was introduced, a striking effect was observed. φX174 survived at only 39.4%, while T4 was completely inactivated at 2.5%. Notably, φX174 outperformed T4 on both plastic (47.9% vs. 34.6%) and Steel (56.0% vs. 41.5%) at 4°C, which shows a significant phage × surface interaction. At 37°C, the two phages differed in the control treatments, with φX174 reaching 68.3% survival and T4 at 40.3%. However, at 4°C, they were similar, supporting a significant phage × temperature interaction. Further, the temperature effect varied by surface. The controls showed a substantial 41% drop between 4°C and 37°C, while plastic and steel showed only 5–6‐point drops. Strikingly, copper inactivated T4 more effectively than φX174 at 4°C. However, both phages were completely deactivated at 37°C Table S4.

Effects and Interactions Between Environmental Variables on Survival Rates

To formally test for interactions among experimental variables, we fit a full factorial linear model and performed a Type III ANOVA on survival rates at the final time point (Table 1). The three‐way interaction among phage type, surface and temperature was highly significant (p<0.001), indicating that differences in survival between φX174 and T4 depended jointly on surface material and temperature. All two‐way interactions: Phage × surface, phage × temperature and surface × temperature were also significant (p<0.001), reinforcing the context‐dependent nature of viral persistence. Both surface (p<0.001) and temperature (p<0.001) exerted strong main effects on survival. In contrast, the main effect of phage type alone was not significant (p=0.523), indicating that overall survival did not differ systematically between the two phages when averaged across all environments. Rather, the performance of each phage was contingent on the specific surface‐temperature combination, a hallmark of genotype‐by‐environment interaction.

Growth Following Surface Exposure

After examining the free‐living survival of the phages, we observed how the surviving phages grew in response to various surface and temperature conditions. We allowed the surviving phages to grow in fresh media for 60 min. Growth titers were initially analysed using raw titers across all surface and temperature combinations. The titers were normalized by dividing each final titre by the starting titre at time zero. This was to express growth as a fold change, resulting in both absolute differences in viral growth dynamics and relative changes over time. This helped minimize any initial advantage a phage might have started with. Both T4 and φX174 grew and had significant increases in their populations, as shown in Figure 4 and Figure S2. Analysis of normalized growth dynamics revealed that T4 had higher fold changes than φX174. On surfaces where T4 initial titers were below the limit of detection, that is, control, copper and plastic at 4°C and copper at 37°C, T4 continued to grow and reached comparable final titers. The limit of detection was set at 1 PFU/mL, and samples below this limit were assigned a value of 0.5 PFU/mL, calculated and plotted as LOD/2 to enable visualization on a log scale and prevent undefined values for growth titers only (Figure S3). These findings indicate that substantial growth occurred from initially undetectable T4 populations, regardless of the surface type Figure S3. Shifting temperatures from 4°C to 37°C also affected viral growth, although this effect was different across surfaces (Figure 4). Additionally, we calculated final virus titre ratios, growth‐phase ratios and temperature effects within 1 h of growth to quantify the observed patterns between the two phages (Table 2). Compared to φX174, T4 generally reached high titers. However, on copper at 37°C, all phages completely lost viability, consistent with the strong inactivating effect of this environment.

Growth Rates and Temperature Sensitivity

Temperature effects on phage growth were not uniform across phages. When surface temperature increased from 4°C to 37°C, φX174 showed modest declines in growth, with ratios near or below 1, and the largest reduction was observed on stainless steel (approximately 0.77). In contrast, T4 exhibited greater variability across surfaces, including a marked increase in growth on stainless steel (approximately 1.75) (Tables S5 and S6).

Relationship Between Survival and Growth

We next examined the relationship between phage survival and growth across all phage × surface × temperature combinations using Spearman's rank correlation. First, we wanted to test whether an overall association existed between surface survival and growth capacity, and whether this association remained consistent across all experimental conditions. We performed per‐condition Spearman rank correlation between the condition‐average survival titre at 8 h and the mean growth titre at 60 min, pooling data across all 16 phage × surface × temperature combinations. We found that across all phage × surface × temperature conditions, mean growth was positively correlated with mean survival (Spearman's ρ=0.88,p−value<0.001). However, since φX174 and T4 differ biologically, and how they respond to ecological stress, an analysis of correlations within each phage revealed that the relationship was substantially stronger for T4 than for φX174 (φX174 Spearman's ρ=0.13,p−value=0.76, T4 Spearman's ρ=0.93,p−value=0.0008). Taken together, conditions that favoured survival also favoured growth at these phage × surface × temperature conditions, but most of this association was stronger for T4 than φX174. Testing Spearman's correlations within each condition and phage revealed heterogeneous results. φX174 showed strong positive relationships on copper (Spearman's ρ=1.00,p−value<0.001) and control (Spearman's ρ=0.90,p−value<0.05) at 4°C, suggesting that under these conditions, stability and growth were linked. However, significant negative correlations on plastic at 37°C were observed (Spearman's ρ=0.90,p−value<0.05), indicating that conditions favouring survival may not favour growth under these conditions Figure 5, Table 3, Table S7. On the other hand, T4 showed weaker, non‐significant correlations across all conditions Figure 5 and Table 3 and Table S7. In other contexts, relationships were weak or non‐significant, suggesting that the connection between phage persistence and growth potential is highly context‐dependent.

Discussion

To investigate how abiotic stressors (surfaces) shape viral persistence and growth, we challenged bacteriophages T4 and φX174 across copper, stainless steel and polypropylene plastic at 4°C and 37°C. Viral survival was strongly influenced by both temperature and surface composition, and these effects were frequently non‐additive. Although the effects of these stressors on growth were comparatively uniform, the relationship between survival and growth did not reduce to simple patterns but instead reflected interactions among surface composition, thermal context and virus type. Temperature exerted a pronounced effect on viral survival for both phages. Viruses incubated at 37°C experienced greater reductions in viability than those exposed to 4°C on the same surfaces (Figure 2, Figure S1, Table S5). Notably, we observed that viral populations could recover reproductive capacity even after becoming undetectable in survival assays (Figure 4, Figures S2 and S3). The relationship between survival and growth varied across environments. For example, φX174 exhibited a strong positive correlation between survival and growth on copper surfaces, but a negative association between these same traits on polypropylene plastic (Figure 5).

When comparing T4 with φX174, one could ask the simple question: ‘Which has higher free‐living survival’? Our results suggest that it depends. In our results, φX174 has a higher survival across most temperatures and surface compositions. But the complete answer is context dependent: φX174 has a higher free‐living survival in most surface‐temperature combinations, as dictated by several metrics (decline rate, half‐life). But at 37°C on copper, it actually has a lower survival rate than T4. The varying survival dynamics across different surface temperatures suggest the existence of a phenomenon we can label as a ‘virus type by environment interaction’, which can also be considered a kind of genotype‐by‐environment interaction (G × E). And our study shows that growth is much more resistant to surface‐temperature combinations, with T4 exhibiting a higher mean growth rate across all environments tested. This can also be framed in terms of environmental robustness, which is a measure of the invariance of trait performance across environmental contexts (Masel and Siegal 2009; Ogbunugafor et al. 2010). Here, robustness refers to the degree to which a phage maintains its capacity to survive and grow across a range of environmental conditions, a measure of phenotypic invariance to environmental perturbation. Both viruses demonstrate environmental robustness with respect to growth, with T4 maintaining a higher average growth rate across conditions.

Looking across surface compositions and temperatures, several consistent patterns emerge: Every virus type has its lowest survival on copper surfaces. In particular, warmer temperatures accelerated viral decay relative to cooler conditions. These results extend previous demonstrations of copper's antimicrobial activity to varying temperatures and align with direct evidence that copper and copper alloys rapidly inactivate viruses on contact, whereas stainless steel often facilitates longer persistence (Noyce et al. 2007; Warnes and Keevil 2013; Warnes et al. 2015; Li and Dennehy 2011). These results also align with a broader literature documenting the role of thermal stress in modulating viral performance.

Implications for Infectious Disease Ecology

Beyond free‐living survival, our experiments show that post‐exposure growth is not a simple function of residual viability. The correlations between survival and growth varied in sign and magnitude across environments, suggesting that stability and growth output represent partially decoupled axes of fitness shaped by the environment. For instance, a strong positive correlation between survival and growth among these phages indicated no consistent tradeoff, suggesting that the fitness cost tradeoff is context‐dependent (Kabengele et al. 2024; Rafaluk‐Mohr 2019; Turner et al. 2021). The per‐condition heterogeneity observed in correlations, for example, strong positive on copper and control at 4°C for φX174, strong negative on plastic at 37°C, and uniformly weaker for T4 may reflect a material‐specific dynamic rooted in phage life‐history biology. Plastic can promote desiccation, which may compromise capsid integrity in ways that differentially affect persistence versus replicative capacity. By contrast, phage decay on copper and stainless steel surfaces is likely driven by chemical processes that can compromise capsid structure more uniformly. When damage is severe enough to eliminate viability entirely, a positive correlation between survival and growth naturally emerges and only particles that escape damage retain both traits. A critical implication is that a trade‐off observed in one environmental context does not necessarily hold in another.

This is a significant outcome when we consider the larger literature on infectious disease ecology and life history theory that has long examined the constraints governing relationships between traits such as virulence and transmission, survival, and growth (Stearns 1976, 1998; De Paepe and Taddei 2006; Bonhoeffer et al. 1996; Brandon Ogbunugafor et al. 2013; Heineman and Brown 2012). Our findings demonstrate that survival and growth are largely decoupled and that their relationship is specific to certain surface‐temperature combinations. This supports modern notions that trait relationships in pathogen‐host systems are idiosyncratic and context‐dependent. More broadly, the correlations between viral persistence and growth that we observed reflect fundamental life‐history constraints on viral evolution. These trade‐offs have implications for how selection acts on the balance between environmental durability and replicative capacity, and how such trade‐offs may, in turn, influence other traits, including virulence and transmission in viruses, where fomite‐mediated pathways link survival directly to reproductive opportunity.

Implications for Biomedicine and Epidemiology

Although our study uses model bacteriophages, the findings have implications for fomite‐mediated transmission and for applications such as phage therapy for medical devices and implants. Questions about how pathogens survive on materials underlie efforts to reduce surface contamination through the use of antimicrobial copper materials (Casey et al. 2010).

The findings also resonate with evolutionary responses to metal stress in microbes, as was demonstrated in a recent study on long‐term copper exposure in E. coli (Boyd‐Vorsah et al. 2025). This invites future work asking whether repeated exposure to surfaces can drive viral adaptations in persistence or replication, and whether such adaptations reshape the coupling between survival and growth. With regard to epidemiology, our findings add nuance to prevailing conceptions of the forces shaping fomite‐mediated transmission. Models of fomite‐mediated transmission often include a decay rate parameter to describe how pathogen populations decline (Ogbunugafor et al. 2020; Miller‐Dickson et al. 2019). Our findings suggest that models of pathogen decay, whether conceptual or computational, must account for interactions among abiotic factors in shaping this parameter, enabling more accurate analyses and targeted interventions. Furthermore, public health strategies that aim to intervene in environmental transmission, such as the use of copper surfaces in clinical settings, must consider how these effects are mediated by temperature.

Limitations

The study has several limitations. For one, we utilized bacteriophages, which offer valuable mechanistic insights, but differ from human and animal viruses in many ways that undermine a neat application of our findings to the diseases of plants and animals. In addition, our experiments were limited to 8 h, which may not capture longer‐term persistence dynamics. Similarly, although we normalized growth titers prior to analysis to mitigate initial advantages among populations retrieved from surfaces, we acknowledge that measuring growth based solely on surviving populations may introduce confounding variables. Lastly, we measured survival and growth sequentially rather than simultaneously, which may obscure finer‐scale temporal dynamics. Given the limited sample size, the within‐condition correlations may be prone to spurious associations and should be interpreted as descriptive rather than as evidence for condition‐specific biological relationships. The robust biological signal is represented by the across‐condition correlation, which aggregates data across all conditions (Spearman's ρ=0.88,p−value<0.001). Future work should extend these experiments to longer durations, incorporate a broader range of temperatures and humidity conditions, and test additional viral systems, particularly enveloped and RNA viruses of public health importance.

Conclusion

The relationship between environmental persistence and reproductive capacity lies at the core of viral ecology, because viral fitness depends jointly on the ability to survive outside a host and to replicate efficiently once inside one. Trade‐offs between these components of fitness are therefore central to understanding how viruses evolve across heterogeneous environments.

Our findings demonstrate that viral fitness in the context of free‐living survival and transmission can be shaped by complex, context‐dependent interactions between environmental factors. By disentangling how temperature and surface type jointly influence these traits, we provide a framework for resolving the complexity of the myriad forces that shape viral transmission.
