Abstract
This paper focuses on distributed node-specific signal estimation in topology-unconstrained wireless acoustic sensor networks (WASNs) where sensor nodes only transmit fused versions of their local sensor signals. For this task, the topology-independent (TI) distributed adaptive node-specific signal estimation (DANSE) algorithm (TI-DANSE) has previously been proposed. It converges towards the centralized signal estimation solution in non-fully connected and time-varying network topologies. However, the applicability of TI-DANSE in real-world scenarios is limited due to its slow convergence. The latter results from the fact that, in TI-DANSE, nodes only have access to the in-network sum of all fused signals in the WASN. We address this low convergence speed issue by introducing an improved TI-DANSE algorithm, referred to as TI-DANSE + . The TI-DANSE + algorithm outperforms TI-DANSE in terms of convergence speed by letting the updating node use each partial in-network sum of fused signals (coming from its neighbors) separately, when updating its estimation parameters. In this way, the number of available degrees of freedom in the optimization problem at the updating node is increased, leading to faster convergence. This separate use of incoming partial in-network sums is further exploited by combining TI-DANSE + with a tree-pruning strategy that maximizes the number of neighbors at the updating node. In fully connected WASNs, it is observed that TI-DANSE + converges as fast as the original DANSE algorithm (the latter only defined for fully connected WASNs) while using peer-to-peer data transmission instead of broadcasting and thus saving communication bandwidth. If link failures occur, the convergence of TI-DANSE + towards the centralized solution is preserved without any change in its formulation. Altogether, the proposed TI-DANSE + algorithm can be viewed as an all-round alternative to DANSE and TI-DANSE which (i) merges the advantages of both, (ii) reconciliates their differences into a single formulation, and (iii) shows advantages of its own in terms of communication bandwidth usage. The convergence properties and signal estimation performance of TI-DANSE + are demonstrated through speech enhancement experiments in simulated topology-unconstrained WASNs.



I
Introduction

The ever-increasing ubiquity of devices equipped of acoustic sensors (microphones) in our environment enables the creation of
wireless acoustic sensor networks (WASNs)
composed of multiple wirelessly interconnected devices (also called multi-sensor “nodes”), e.g., smartphones, laptops, or hearing aids
[
4
,
1
]
. Such
WASNs
can be used as distributed systems, as opposed to centralized systems in which processing is exclusively performed at a fusion center where all audio signals are aggregated. Each node in a
WASN
can receive, locally process, and transmit signals to other nodes. By wirelessly exchanging signals, devices can access information about sound sources of interest even when these are far from them.
The real-world deployment of distributed systems remains a challenging task as several practical requirements must be fulfilled such as, e.g., low communication bandwidth usage to avoid network congestion, robustness to dynamic network topologies as nodes may drop in and out of the
WASN
, and quick adaptivity
[
25
]
. These challenges are best addressed early on, i.e., at the application layer in the design of distributed algorithms, which is the focus of this paper.

I-A

State-of-the-art and Related Works

Research works in the field of distributed algorithms have so far largely focused on parameter estimation, i.e., estimating a single, static or slowly varying parameter vector (e.g., the dimensions of a room, the location of sound sources, or features of a speech signal) of interest to all nodes in the
WASN

[
35
,
30
,
29
]
.

This has traditionally be done using, e.g., consensus-based techniques
[
36
,
23
,
16
,
15
]
.
However, in many applications, nodes need to estimate entire signals, i.e., samples of an underlying incoming nonstationary process such as a speech signal
[
37
,
13
]
, which may also be node-specific.

Although consensus-based strategies may be used in cases where all nodes aim to estimate the same signal, node-specific signal estimation problems can be efficiently tackled via spatial filtering-based solutions that exploit the correlation between the signals recorded at different nodes. In spatial filtering-based methods, the target signal of any given node is estimated by performing a linear combination of all signals available at that node, where the weights of that combination are found as solution to an optimization problem involving the signals recorded by all nodes in the network.

To achieve the same performance as a centralized system
through distributed spatial filtering, a naive solution may be to allow all nodes to broadcast all of their sensor signals
to all other nodes in the
WASN
. This would, however, result in intolerably high communication bandwidth usage, especially when many nodes are involved.
This motivated the development of the
distributed adaptive node-specific signal estimation (DANSE)
algorithm
[
2
]
, in which nodes first combine their local sensor signals into low-dimensional (i.e., fused) signals before transmitting them, thereby reducing bandwidth usage. It was shown that the
DANSE
algorithm converges towards the centralized solution when the target signals of all nodes in the
WASN
emerge from a common set of latent sound sources.
Using the
DANSE
algorithm in an
fully connected (FC)

WASN
, the same signal estimation performance as an equivalent centralized system can be obtained iteratively, without relying on a fusion center.

The
DANSE
algorithm is designed to function solely in
FC

WASNs
, i.e., networks where all nodes can directly transmit data to all other nodes. This motivated the development of alternative formulations, first for
WASNs
with static tree-topologies
[
5
]
and static mixed-topologies
[
31
]
, then for any, possibly time-varying topologies via the topology-independent (TI)
DANSE
algorithm (
TI-DANSE
)
[
32
]
.
This algorithm achieves convergence in any topology-unconstrained
WASN
by pruning the network to a tree topology at each algorithmic iteration and building the in-network sum of all fused signals, which is then used for node-specific signal estimation.

Even though
TI-DANSE
enables distributed signal estimation in any
WASN
, it converges slowly towards the optimum
[
32
,
12
]
. This slow convergence is an inherent characteristic of
TI-DANSE
since, at each iteration, the updating node only has access to the global in-network sum of all fused signals to update its filters.
The number of degrees of freedom (
degree of freedoms (DoFs)
) in the local
TI-DANSE
optimization problem is indeed much smaller than for
DANSE
in an equivalent
FC

WASN
, where nodes have access to all individual fused signals from all other nodes. Letting
K
represent the number of nodes in the
WASN
and
Q
the number of fused signals computed at and exchanged between each node, an updating node in
DANSE
has access to
Q(K-1)
signals transmitted by other nodes while an updating node in
TI-DANSE
has access to only
Q
signals transmitted by other nodes. The resulting issue is particularly noticeable when deploying
TI-DANSE
in an
FC
, or nearly
FC

WASN
, where convergence to the centralized solution is then significantly slower than that of the
DANSE
algorithm. This drawback of
TI-DANSE
severely hinders its applicability in real-world scenarios that require fast adaptivity.

I-B

Contributions

In this paper, a distributed algorithm termed
TI-DANSE
+
is proposed to resolve the slow convergence issue of

. The
TI-DANSE
+
algorithm allows all nodes in any unconstrained and possibly time-varying

topology to reach the same signal estimation performance as if they were part of a centralized system, while only exchanging low-dimensional fused versions of their local sensor signals.
The
TI-DANSE
+
algorithm achieves this without compromising on convergence speed when the network is
FC
, i.e., reaching the same convergence speed as
DANSE
in
FC

WASNs
.

Letting

denote the number of direct neighbors of the updating node, the key enabling principle of

+
lies in the fact that the updating node uses
B
partial in-network sums of fused signals coming from each of its neighbors
separately
to update its estimation parameters.
This is a major difference with respect to
TI-DANSE
, where the partial sums are summed into a global sum, which is then used to perform signal estimation.
An updating node in
TI-DANSE
+
thus has access to
QB
fused signals, against
Q
for an updating node in
TI-DANSE
.
Since
B=K{-}1
in a
FC

WASN
,
TI-DANSE
+
converges as fast as
DANSE
while requiring less communication bandwidth at every iteration since

+
relies on peer-to-peer data transmission from all nodes to the updating node and back, while

relies on broadcasting from every node to every other node and back.

The use of partial in-network sums of fused signals coming from different branches in a tree-topology
WASN
has been explored in
[
24
]
for a general signal fusion problem. However, the solution proposed there requires each non-updating node to solve an additional optimization problem
which, to be solved, requires the estimation at each iteration of one additional set of signal statistics per non-updating node. This greatly limits the practical applicability of
[
24
]
.

Since the number of
DoFs
in the local optimization problem at an updating node in
TI-DANSE
+
depends on the number
B
of direct neighbors of the updating node, the choice of
WASN
tree-pruning strategy is crucial and impacts the convergence speed of the algorithm.
In this paper, a tree-pruning strategy that maximizes the number of
DoFs
at the updating node is proposed.
Using this strategy, simulated experiments show that
TI-DANSE
+
converges as fast as
DANSE
in
FC

WASNs
and faster than
TI-DANSE
in any non-
FC

WASN
topology. In general, it is observed that the more connections exist between nodes of the original non-pruned
WASN
, the faster
TI-DANSE
+
can converge.

The
TI-DANSE
+
algorithm can be viewed as an all-round alternative to
DANSE
and
TI-DANSE
which (i) merges the advantages of both, (ii) reconciliates their differences into a single formulation, and (iii) shows advantages of its own in terms of communication bandwidth in
FC

WASNs
.

In
[
10
]
, a brief high-level description of the
TI-DANSE
+
algorithm was introduced. In this paper, we provide more details, including a communication complexity analysis,
a computational complexity analysis, the addressing of several implementation aspects, and more extensive simulation results including dynamic topologies,
generalized eigenvalue decomposition (GEVD)
-based filter updates, and simulations in a realistic speech enhancement setting where signal statistics are estimated from the available microphone recordings. Importantly, a convergence proof is provided, formally demonstrating that
TI-DANSE
+
converges towards the centralized solution in any topology-unconstrained
WASN
.

(11)

Eventually, the root node

|\mathcal{U}_{k}^{i}|

. It then constructs the so-called

\boxed{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{i}\triangleq\Big[\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\>\underbrace{(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{{l_{1}}\to{k}}^{i})^{\mathrm{T}}\>\dots\>(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{{l_{B}}\to{k}}^{i})^{\mathrm{T}}}_{({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\to{k}}^{i})^{\mathrm{T}}}\Big]^{\mathrm{T}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{\widetilde{M}_{k}^{i}}\times{1}}}

(12)

where
\widetilde{M}_{k}^{i}\triangleq M_{k}+Q|\mathcal{U}_{k}^{i}|
and
\{l_{1},\dots,l_{B}\}=\mathcal{U}_{k}^{i}
. The partial in-network sums

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\>|\>(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{U}_{k}^{i}}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{{l}\to{k}}^{i})^{\mathrm{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\>|\>(\sum_{q\neq k}{\mathbf{z}}_{{q}}^{i})^{\mathrm{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}.

(13)

The number of

M_{k}+Q|\mathcal{U}_{k}^{i}|
(cf. (
12

(cf. (

III-B
2
Filter Update

After the
TI-DANSE
+

\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{k}^{i+1}\triangleq\underset{\mathbf{W}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{\widetilde{M}_{k}^{i}}\times{Q}}}{\mathrm{arg\,min}}\>\mathbb{E}\left\{{\left\|\mathbf{d}_{k}-\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{H}}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{i}\right\|^{2}_{2}}\right\}.

(14)

This filter can be partitioned as:

\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{k}^{i+1}=\Big[({\mathbf{W}}_{{k}{k}}^{i+1})^{\mathrm{T}}\>\underbrace{({\mathbf{G}}_{{l_{1}}\to{k}}^{i+1})^{\mathrm{T}}\>\dots\>({\mathbf{G}}_{{l_{B}}\to{k}}^{i+1})^{\mathrm{T}}}_{({\mathbf{G}}_{\to{k}}^{i+1})^{\mathrm{T}}}\Big]^{\mathrm{T}},

(15)

with
{\mathbf{W}}_{{k}{k}}^{i+1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{M_{k}}\times{Q}}

\mathbf{y}_{k}
and
{\mathbf{G}}_{\to{k}}^{i+1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{Q|\mathcal{U}_{k}^{i}|}\times{Q}}
the filter applied to the partial in-network sums
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\to{k}}^{i}
. The
{\mathbf{G}}_{\to{k}}^{i+1}

|\mathcal{U}_{k}^{i}|
submatrices
{\mathbf{G}}_{{l}\to{k}}^{i+1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{Q}\times{Q}},\>\forall\>l\in\mathcal{U}_{k}^{i}
, where
{\mathbf{G}}_{{l}\to{k}}^{i+1}
is applied to
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{{l}\to{k}}^{i}
.
The solution to (
14
) is an
MWF
as:

\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{k}^{i+1}=\left({\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}}^{i}\right)^{-1}{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\mathbf{d}_{k}}^{i},

(16)

with
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}}^{i}\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left\{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{i}(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{i})^{\mathrm{H}}\right\}
and
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\mathbf{d}_{k}}^{i}\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left\{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{i}\mathbf{d}_{k}^{\mathrm{H}}\right\}

As in the centralized case, the
SCM

{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\mathbf{d}_{k}}^{i}
can be estimated from the available signals, e.g., exploiting on-off characteristics of the desired speech signal via a

\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{i}

{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\mathbf{d}_{k}}^{i}=\left({\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}}^{i}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}}^{i}\right)\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{i},

(17)

with the node-specific noise

and the selection matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{i}\triangleq[\mathbf{E}_{{k}{k}}^{\mathrm{T}}\>|\>\mathbf{0}_{Q|\mathcal{U}_{k}^{i}|\times Q}^{\mathrm{T}}]^{\mathrm{T}}
. The estimation of

and
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}}^{i}
is discussed in detail in Section

The target signal estimate at node

\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{i+1}\triangleq(\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{k}^{i+1})^{\mathrm{H}}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}^{i}.

(18)

Note that all nodes in the

have their own filter

and use it to compute their own fusion matrix

(19)

At the end of the filter update, the root node

Intuition for proof of Theorem 2:

The proof of Theorem 2 is divided into several steps. In a first step, it is shown that the trajectory of
TI-DANSE
+
across iterations is not affected when, at a given iteration
i
(and only at
i
), the updating node estimates the target signal of another node instead of its own. This fact can be intuitively understood by realizing that any linear transformation applied to
\mathbf{d}_{k}
at iteration
i
in the optimization problem solved by the root node (i.e., (
14
)) will be effectively applied to all fused signals at the next iteration
i+1
(and hence to all partial in-network sums). This transformation of the partial in-network sums can be compensated for by the next updating node (at
i+1
), when it solves the
LMMSE
problem aiming at its own target signal. Combining this with the fact that all target signals in the network are linearly related to each other (cf. (
3
)), it can be concluded that letting node
k
estimate
\mathbf{d}_{q}
(with
q\neq k
) during one iteration does not affect the trajectory of
TI-DANSE
+
.

In a second step, this conclusion is extended to a modified version of
TI-DANSE
+
where all nodes in a full update cycle (i.e.,
K
consecutive iterations) aim at estimating the target signal of one fixed node
\nu\in\mathcal{K}
. This allows to prove convergence of
TI-DANSE
+
to a lower bound. In a third and final step, the optimality of this bound is proven by showing that this modified
TI-DANSE
+
, that does not affect the trajectory of the unmodified
TI-DANSE
+
, corresponds to a block coordinate descent algorithm which is known to converge to the global optimum for convex problems (such as (
14
))
[
6
]
. Indeed, through one full update cycle where the target of all nodes is
\mathbf{d}_{\nu}
, the nodes optimize their own filter one at a time while keeping filters of non-updating nodes fixed, which is indeed equivalent to block coordinate descent.

III-D

Tree-Pruning Strategy

The formulation of the
TI-DANSE
+
algorithm does not depend on a particular tree topology, such that the tree-pruning strategy may be freely chosen.
The reader is reminded that an undirected
WASN
is assumed here, i.e., signals can be exchanged in both ways between any two connected nodes. The following discussion may be extended to directed graphs by considering edge directions during the pruning process, although this is not explicitly treated here.
A typical choice is
MSTs
, as they minimize the amount of energy required to transfer data through the tree by minimizing the total distance between connected nodes.
Although this is generally a popular option
[
32
]
, pruning the
WASN
such that
|\mathcal{U}_{k}^{i}|
is maximized at any iteration
i
shows advantages in the context of
TI-DANSE
+
. Indeed, it was shown in
[
32
]
that the convergence speed of
DANSE
-like algorithms increases with the number of available
DoFs
that an updating node possesses when solving its
LMMSE
problem. This explains the slow convergence speed of
TI-DANSE
, where the updating node only possesses
M_{k}+Q

DoFs
compared to
M_{k}+Q(K-1)
in
DANSE
for
FC

WASNs
. In constract, the size of the
TI-DANSE
+
observation vector at the root node (
12
) is
\widetilde{M}_{k}^{i}=M_{k}+Q|\mathcal{U}_{k}^{i}|
. This implies that maximizing
|\mathcal{U}_{k}^{i}|
at each iteration maximizes the number of
DoFs
available at the root node which, in turns, maximizes the potential for a fast
TI-DANSE
+
convergence.

We propose a strategy to prune a topology-unconstrained
WASN
to a tree with a maximum
|\mathcal{U}_{k}^{i}|,\>\forall\>i
, and with a minimum total edge cost, which may be defined, e.g., as the Euclidean distance between nodes. This is essentially a constrained version of the Kruskal’s
MST
-finding algorithm
[
22
]
.
The proposed tree-pruning strategy is termed
multiple max-
|\mathcal{U}_{k}|
trees (MMUT)
and is explicitly defined in Algorithm
1
. It considers the fact that the maximum number of connections that root node
k
can have after pruning is the number of neighbors that
k
has in the original non-pruned
WASN
.
Note that applying
MMUT
pruning to an
FC

WASN
will result in a star topology where all non-root nodes are directly connected to the root.

Add

\mathcal{T}(k)
.

Remark:
Since step

[

]
, i.e.,
\mathcal{O}(E\log E)
with

the number of edges in the non-pruned

. This complexity is negligible compared to the
TI-DANSE
+
algorithm itself, which is dominated by the inversion of the
\widetilde{M}_{k}^{i}\times\widetilde{M}_{k}^{i}
matrix in (

. Furthermore, as long as the non-pruned

III-E

Computational Complexity Analysis

The most computationally costly step at any given iteration
i
of the
TI-DANSE
+
algorithm is the inversion of the
\widetilde{M}_{k}^{i}\times\widetilde{M}_{k}^{i}

SCM

{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}}^{i}
at the updating (root) node
k
to compute
\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{k}^{i+1}
via (
16
). Each non-updating node also must invert a
Q\times Q
matrix to compute its target signal estimate using (
22
).
It follows that the total asymptotic computational complexity of the
TI-DANSE
+
algorithm at iteration
i
is:

\displaystyle\mathrm{CC}_{\text{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{acronym}{dansep}{{{}}TI-DANSE${}^{+}$} }}

\displaystyle\triangleq O\left(\left(M_{k}+Q|\mathcal{U}_{k}^{i}|\right)^{3}+(K-1)Q^{3}\right).

(29)

This complexity is greater than that of
TI-DANSE
,
\mathrm{CC}_{\text{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{acronym}{tidanse}{{{}}TI-DANSE} }}\triangleq O((M_{k}+Q)^{3})

[
32
]
and may be significantly lower than that of
DANSE
in
FC

WASNs

\mathrm{CC}_{\text{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{acronym}{danse}{{{}}DANSE} }}\triangleq O\left(\left(M_{k}+Q(K-1)\right)^{3}\right)

[
32
]
, as the latter scales cubically with
K
. The
MMUT
pruning strategy proposed in Section
III-D
allows to maximize
|\mathcal{U}_{k}^{i}|
at each iteration
i
, which, although beneficial for convergence speed, increases the computational complexity of
TI-DANSE
+
. This underlines a trade-off to consider when defining the tree-pruning strategy.

Both
TI-DANSE
+
and the solution proposed in
[
24
]
use partial in-network sums in a tree-pruned
WASN
.
The solution from
[
24
]
requires every non-updating node to solve its own optimization problem at each iteration, which involves the inversion of a
Q\times Q
matrix in the case of
LMMSE
-based estimation. This leads to the same total asymptotic computational cost as
TI-DANSE
+
, i.e.,
\mathrm{CC}_{\text{\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{musluoglu_distributed_2023}{}{}]}}}=\mathrm{CC}_{\text{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{acronym}{dansep}{{{}}TI-DANSE${}^{+}$} }}
. However, the accurate estimation of the
SCMs
necessary to solve the optimization problems at non-updating nodes in
[
24
]
may require a large number of signal observations, and can only be performed once the target signal at the root node is available. This limits the practical convergence speed of that solution. In contrast, non-updating nodes in
TI-DANSE
+
can readily compute their target signal estimate via (
22
) once an update has been carried out at the root node.

III-F

Communication Complexity Analysis

During the
TI-DANSE
+
fusion flow at any iteration
i
(as defined in Section
III-B
), the total number of signals exchanged through the
WASN
is
Q(K-1)
, i.e., the dimension of the in-network sums multiplied by the number of communication links after tree-pruning.
During the diffusion flow, the root node
k
floods
\widehat{\mathbf{d}}_{k}^{i+1}
through the
WASN
such that the number of signals exchanged is also
Q(K-1)
. In total, one iteration of the
TI-DANSE
+
algorithm corresponds to the exchange of
2Q(K-1)
signals, which is typically much fewer than in a centralized system where
M=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{K}}M_{q}
signals must be sent to a fusion center.

In
TI-DANSE
too,
2Q(K-1)
signals are exchanged at each iteration
[
32
]
. In topology-unconstrained
WASNs
,
TI-DANSE
+
is thus nearly equivalent to
TI-DANSE
in terms of communication bandwidth usage, since the exchange of the matrices
{\mathbf{G}}_{{l}\to{k}}^{i+1},\>\forall\>l\in\mathcal{U}_{k}^{i}
(
Q^{2}
data points per matrix) is negligible in comparison to the necessary transmission of batches of signal observations to estimate the
SCMs
in (
16
).

However, in
FC

WASNs
, the
DANSE
algorithm requires the exchange of fused signals between every node at each iteration
[
2
]
, leading to
KQ(K-1)
exchanged signals per iteration, as it requires every node to build the observation vector in (
23
). If
K>2
, which is often the case in
WASNs
,
TI-DANSE
+
is clearly preferable to
DANSE
when aiming at limiting the number of exchanged signals and saving communication bandwidth.

III-G

Implementation Aspects

III-G
1
SCM Estimation

Nodes involved in
TI-DANSE
+
need the
SCMs

{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}}^{i}
and
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\mathbf{d}_{k}}^{i}
to compute the
MWF
(
16
). As established in Section
II
, if the target
\mathbf{d}_{k}
and the noise-only contribution
\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}^{i}
to the observation vector are uncorrelated, the matrix
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\mathbf{d}_{k}}^{i}
can be estimated as
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}}^{i}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}}^{i}
, where the two latter
SCMs
can be estimated based on observed data.

Although an exponential averaging strategy to estimate
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}}^{i}
and
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}}^{i}
can reasonably be used in the centralized case in (
9
), it is generally unsuitable for
DANSE
-like algorithms (see, e.g.,
[
2
, pp.9]
),
TI-DANSE
+
included. Indeed, since
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}}^{i}
and
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}}^{i}
are iteration-dependent, using (
9
) across iterations would result in an averaging of inconsistent signal statistics and would produce inaccurate
SCMs
.

In order to retain statistical consistency in the
SCMs
at play it may be advisable for all versions of
DANSE
,
TI-DANSE
+
included, to estimate the required
SCMs
by averaging over frames of signals captured within a single iteration and to
restart
the
SCM
estimation from scratch at the beginning of the next iteration.
In practical scenarios, however, the
VAD
of the desired source may only seldomly switch state. In that case, if the number of time-frames
L
between two iterations is too small,
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}}^{i}
or
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}}^{i}
may not be properly estimated between two iterations, resulting in an inaccurate filter update. When resetting the
SCMs
at every new iteration, it is instead advisable to allow
L
to vary depending on the changes in
VAD
state. One may, e.g., opt for a criterion such that
i\leftarrow i+1
only when both
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}}^{i}
and
{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}}^{i}
have been updated a sufficient number of times.

III-G
2
GEVD-Based Estimation

The signal estimation performance of
MWF
-based algorithms such as
TI-DANSE
+
can be improved when an upper bound to the number of latent target sources
S
in (
2
) can be estimated.
Indeed, the matrix
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{ss}}
in (
8
) has rank
S
, as can be seen based on (
2
) since:

\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{ss}}=\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{H}}\right\}=\overline{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{lat}}(\mathbf{s}^{\mathrm{lat}})^{\mathrm{H}}\right\}\overline{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^{\mathrm{H}},

(30)

where
\overline{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{1}^{\mathrm{T}}\>\dots\>\overline{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{K}^{\mathrm{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{M}\times{S}}
.
However, the estimation of
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{ss}}
as
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{yy}}-\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{nn}}
, as suggested in Section
II
, in practice mostly results in a matrix with a rank greater than
S
(and sometimes even not positive semidefinite) due to approximation errors. Constraining the rank of
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{ss}}
to a value
R\leq S
can be achieved using a
GEVD
applied to the matrix pencil
\{\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{yy}},\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{nn}}\}
and yields more robust performance in scenarios where accurate estimation of
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{ss}}
is challenging
[
14
]
.

GEVD
-based estimation has been applied to
DANSE
and
TI-DANSE
, resulting in
GEVD-DANSE

[
20
]
and
TI-GEVD-DANSE

[
12
]
, respectively. It can be shown that applying such a
GEVD
-based rank constraint to
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{ss}}
allows the corresponding unconstrained algorithm to converge even when
Q<S

[
20
]
. This is a non-negligible advantage over
DANSE
-like algorithms estimating
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{ss}}
as
\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{yy}}-\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{nn}}
, where precise a priori knowledge of the value of
S
is required to exactly set
Q=S
and ensure convergence
[
2
]
.
Extension to
TI-DANSE
+
naturally follows, leading to the
TI-GEVD-DANSE
+

algorithm. The latter is here merely proposed as a possible extension of
TI-DANSE
+
for improved performance and functioning when
Q\leq S
(recall that
Q=S
is an assumption of Theorems 1 and 2 in Section
III-C
). A formal convergence proof is not available, but simulation results are provided in Section
IV
to demonstrate the performance of
TI-GEVD-DANSE
+
.

A
GEVD
on the pencil
\{{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}}^{i},{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}}^{i}\}
yields:

{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}}^{i}=\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}^{i}{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}^{i}(\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}^{i})^{\mathrm{H}}\>\>\>\text{and}\>\>\>{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}}^{i}=\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}^{i}(\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}^{i})^{\mathrm{H}},

(31)

where the
\widetilde{M}_{k}^{i}
generalized eigenvalues are ordered from largest to smallest in
{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{k}^{i}=\mathrm{diag}\{{\sigma}_{k1}^{i},...,{\sigma}_{k\widetilde{M}_{k}^{i}}^{i}\}
and the corresponding generalized eigenvectors are the columns of
(\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}^{i})^{-\mathrm{H}}
.
The rank of

{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k}}^{i}\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left\{\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k}^{i}(\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k}^{i})^{\mathrm{H}}\right\}
can be constrained to a value
R
by setting the
\widetilde{M}_{k}^{i}-R
smallest generalized eigenvalues to 0, obtaining:

{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{s}}_{k}}^{i}={\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}_{k}}^{i}-{\mathbf{R}}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}\widetilde{\mathbf{n}}_{k}}^{i}=\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}^{i}\mathbf{\Delta}_{k}^{i}(\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}^{i})^{\mathrm{H}},

(32)

where
\mathbf{\Delta}_{k}^{i}=\mathrm{diag}\{{\sigma}_{k1}^{i}-1,\dots,{\sigma}_{kR}^{i}-1,0,\dots,0\}
.
This allows to rewrite the update equation at root node
k
for
TI-GEVD-DANSE
+

by substituting (
32
) into (
16
) via (
17
):

\widetilde{\mathbf{W}}_{k}^{i+1}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}^{i})^{-\mathrm{H}}\tilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k}^{i}(\widetilde{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}^{i})^{\mathrm{H}}\widetilde{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{i},

(33)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{k}^{i}=\mathrm{diag}\{1-1/{\sigma}_{k1}^{i},\dots,1-1/{\sigma}_{kR}^{i},0,\dots,0\}
.

To implement
TI-GEVD-DANSE
+
it is sufficient to replace (
16
) by (
33
) in
TI-DANSE
+
. The other algorithmic steps remain unchanged.

V
Conclusion

We proposed
TI-DANSE
+
, a distributed
LMMSE
-based algorithm for node-specific signal estimation in topology-unconstrained
WASNs
. The algorithm achieves rapid convergence toward the centralized
MWF
estimate without requiring a fully connected (
FC
) topology. To increase convergence speed, a tree-pruning strategy was introduced that maximizes the number of neighbors at the updating node.

The results demonstrate that
TI-DANSE
+
serves as an effective single-algorithm replacement for the combined use of
DANSE
and
TI-DANSE
. It consistently outperforms
TI-DANSE
in non-
FC
networks and matches the convergence speed of
DANSE
in
FC
topologies, while reducing the amount of data exchanged between nodes in that case. The choice of tree-pruning strategy is shown to be a key factor in achieving these improvements.

However, challenges remain, particularly with respect to real-time online operation.

In particular, the fast and accurate estimation of signal statistics (i.e.,
SCMs
) from limited data at each iteration, although paramount to compute the
MWFs
involved in
TI-DANSE
+
(as well as
DANSE
and
TI-DANSE
), is still difficult in practice, especially in dynamic acoustic scenarios. Potential solutions include the use of advanced
SCM
estimation techniques, possibly based on deep learning, which could be explored in future work.

Step 2:

Consider now a second modified version of
DANSE
where all updating nodes

\mathbf{d}_{\nu}
(where
\nu

\nu

j

+

Once again going back to the unmodified

+

\nu

\mathbf{d}_{\nu}

J_{\nu}\left({\mathbf{W}}_{\nu}^{j+K}\right)\leq J_{\nu}\left({\mathbf{W}}_{\nu}^{j+K-1}\right)\leq\dots\leq J_{\nu}\left({\mathbf{W}}_{\nu}^{j}\right).

(70)

q

(J_{q}({\mathbf{W}}_{q}^{j}))_{j\in\mathbb{N}},\>\forall\>q\in\mathcal{K}

Step 3:

A full update cycle of the modified
TI-DANSE
+

\mathbf{d}_{\nu}

\nu

[
6

LMMSE

\mathbf{d}_{\nu}

k

k

TI-DANSE
+

TI-DANSE
+

LMMSE
