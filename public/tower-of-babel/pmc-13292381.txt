Structured observation to explore systems awareness and patient safety learning in undergraduate nursing education: A mixed-methods educational study
Authors: María José Ferreira Díaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreparing nursing students for contemporary practice requires more than technical skill acquisition. It demands the ability to interpret complex organisational environments, recognise system-level risks and engage with patient safety as a shared professional responsibility. However, during clinical placements, learning often remains task-focused, with limited pedagogical support to foster systems awareness and clinical management understanding.AimTo examine the perceived educational utility of a structured observation strategy in relation to systems awareness, clinical management understanding, and patient safety competencies among undergraduate nursing students during clinical placements.DesignA descriptive, cross-sectional mixed-methods educational study.MethodsUndergraduate nursing students undertaking clinical placements in three hospitals and nine primary care centres completed the Clinical Management Observation Tool (CMOT), comprising ten Likert-scale items addressing key domains of clinical management and patient safety culture, and one open-ended reflective question. Quantitative data were analysed descriptively and comparatively across settings, while qualitative responses were examined using inductive thematic analysis. Although descriptive in design, the study was conceived as an exploratory evaluation of a pedagogical strategy rather than a causal effectiveness study.ResultsA total of 125 students participated. Overall scores indicated positive observations of organisational functioning and patient safety culture across settings. Primary care settings were rated significantly higher than hospital settings in patient and family information and participation. Qualitative findings revealed students’ capacity to identify organisational strengths, system pressures and realistic improvement opportunities related to teamwork, communication, workflow and leadership.ConclusionsStructured observation functions as a low-cost educational intervention that supports reflective, systems-oriented learning in complex clinical environments. By guiding students to critically interpret organisational processes rather than focus solely on task performance, structured observation may contribute to the development of professional judgement, patient safety awareness and readiness for contemporary nursing practice.

Introduction

Developing competencies in clinical management, teamwork, communication, and patient safety is a central expectation of contemporary nursing education worldwide. International frameworks highlight that future nurses must not only master technical skills but also understand, interpret, and navigate complex healthcare systems (Frenk et al., 2010; ICN, 2020). Clinical placements provide an essential environment for such learning, offering students exposure to real organisational structures, workflow dynamics, interprofessional collaboration, and safety practices. However, research consistently shows that these experiences may remain fragmented, unstructured, and overly task-oriented unless educators provide explicit learning strategies to guide students’ observations (Erlam et al., 2022; Grealish et al., 2020). As a result, students may have limited opportunities to systematically recognise how organisational processes and patient-safety practices operate within clinical environments, which represents an important educational gap in nursing training.

Global patient-safety scholarship underscores the importance of organisational culture, communication quality, team's role clarity, and visible leadership as determinants of safe and effective care (Sammer et al., 2021; WHO, 2021). Instruments such as the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (HSOPS), the Medical Office Survey on Patient Safety Culture (MOSPS) and the Safety Attitudes Questionnaire (SAQ) have demonstrated variability in these domains across healthcare settings and countries, with primary care often showing more consistent communication and relational continuity than hospital environments (Sorra et al., 2019). While these instruments are widely used to measure organisational safety culture, they are not designed to guide students’ structured observation of clinical management processes during placements. This limitation motivated the development and application of the Clinical Management Observation Tool (CMOT) in the present study. Understanding these contextual differences is pedagogically relevant, as it allows students to recognise system-level factors that shape risk, safety and quality. These frameworks also informed the conceptual orientation of the Clinical Management Observation Tool (CMOT), which guides students’ structured observation of organisational and patient-safety processes during clinical placements.

Educational research emphasises the value of reflective, active and experiential learning approaches in helping students make sense of practice complexity (Chan et al., 2023; Phillips et al., 2020). Structured observation tools can serve as scaffolds that orient students toward organisational processes—such as leadership behaviour, teamwork, documentation quality, resource management, or workflow coordination—helping them integrate theoretical concepts with lived clinical experience (Manninen et al., 2020). Moreover, such tools align with international calls for transformative learning, enabling students to progress from “doing tasks” to recognising themselves as participants in a broader system of care (Frenk et al., 2010).

Despite this recognition, few studies have examined structured observation as a strategy to enhance nursing students’ understanding of clinical management and patient-safety culture across diverse practice settings. Comparing hospital and primary care environments is particularly relevant in nursing education, as these settings differ substantially in organisational structure, patient flow, and communication dynamics, offering students distinct opportunities to observe clinical management and safety practices. This study addresses this gap by applying the Clinical Management Observation Tool (CMOT), adapted from validated patient-safety instruments, to explore students’ observations in three hospitals and nine primary care centres. The research was conducted within the Spanish public healthcare system, where undergraduate nursing students complete clinical placements across both hospital and primary care settings as part of their professional training. The aims were: (1) to describe students’ perceptions of organisational and safety-culture features during clinical placements, and (2) to analyse the educational value of structured observation for enhancing critical thinking and systems awareness.

Educational strategies that support this form of systems awareness remain underdeveloped and underreported, particularly within routine clinical placements. In particular, little is known about how undergraduate nursing students systematically observe and interpret organisational and patient-safety processes during routine clinical placements. Understanding how students perceive and interpret organisational processes across different clinical settings—such as hospitals and primary care—may help educators design more effective learning strategies that promote systems awareness and patient-safety competencies. This study contributes empirical evidence on how structured observation can support students’ interpretation of clinical management and patient-safety processes during placements.

Methods

Design

A descriptive, cross-sectional educational study with a mixed-methods approach was conducted to explore nursing students’ observations of clinical management and patient-safety culture during clinical placements. The design aligns with experiential and reflective learning models commonly used in nursing education research (Ribeiro et al., 2022). Although descriptive in design, this study was conceived as an exploratory evaluation of a pedagogical strategy rather than as a causal effectiveness study. The study followed a convergent (parallel) mixed-methods design, in which quantitative and qualitative data were collected concurrently and integrated during interpretation, rather than sequentially.

Setting

The study was carried out across three public hospitals (tertiary and secondary care) and nine primary care centers within the same regional health system in Spain. These institutions are part of the public healthcare network that provides clinical training for undergraduate nursing students enrolled in the regional university nursing program. These settings provided heterogeneous organizational contexts in which students could observe variations in teamwork, communication, leadership, resource management, and safety practices.

Clinical placements were part of the mandatory practicum module in the third year of the Bachelor of Nursing program, requiring rotation through medical–surgical wards, emergency units, specialty services, and primary care teams.

Participants

Participants were undergraduate nursing students enrolled in the third-year clinical placement course during the 2025–2026 academic year. A convenience sampling strategy was used, as all students completing placements during the study period were invited to participate.

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (a) enrolment in the practicum module, and

(b) rotation through at least one clinical setting.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion criteria included only questionnaires with substantial missing data or incomplete contextual information. After applying these criteria, a final sample of 125 fully completed responses was obtained. Participation was entirely voluntary and anonymous, and students were informed that their decision to participate—or not—had no impact on their academic evaluation.

Instrument

Data were collected using the Clinical Management Observation Tool (CMOT), an educational instrument specifically adapted to guide reflective observation during clinical placements. The tool was developed from two validated frameworks:

(1) the Medical Office Survey on Patient Safety Culture (MOSPS), used internationally to assess teamwork, communication and safety climate in primary care (Ministerio de Sanidad, 2011); and (2) the G_Clinic Questionnaire, designed for evaluating clinical management processes in hospital settings (Gálvez-González et al., 2014). The full CMOT questionnaire is provided as Supplementary Material 1.

The CMOT comprises ten Likert-type items evaluating key domains of clinical management and patient-safety culture, as well as one open-ended reflective question. Content validity was established by a panel of three experts in nursing management, patient safety, and educational methodology. The panel included professionals with expertise in nursing management, patient safety, and undergraduate nursing education, selected according to their experience in clinical training, organisational processes, and educational methodology. Experts reviewed the relevance, clarity, educational appropriateness, and contextual applicability of each item across both hospital and primary care settings. Minor wording adjustments were made to improve conceptual clarity and suitability for undergraduate nursing students. Although a formal scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) was not calculated, item revisions were discussed collectively and refined until consensus was reached among the panel members. The CMOT was therefore considered suitable for exploratory educational use, although further psychometric validation would be necessary before considering the tool as a standardized measurement instrument.

The tool was administered electronically via Google Forms to facilitate accessibility and maintain anonymity.

Internal consistency of the 10-item scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which indicated acceptable reliability (α = 0.75).

The CMOT domains were conceptually informed by established patient-safety culture frameworks, particularly the Hospital Survey on Patient Safety Culture (HSOPS) and the Medical Office Survey on Patient Safety Culture (MOSPS), which emphasise teamwork, communication, leadership visibility and organisational learning.

Procedure

At the beginning of the practicum, students received a brief orientation session explaining the purpose of the observation tool and its role as a reflective learning strategy. They were instructed to apply the tool during a specific clinical shift of their choice, documenting real observations rather than perceptions of their own performance.

Data were collected using an online questionnaire administered through Google Forms. The survey included both the 10-item CMOT Likert-scale instrument (quantitative component) and one open-ended reflective question through which students provided written qualitative responses regarding their observations during clinical placements.

Data collection occurred over a period of six weeks, with students completing the questionnaire at the end of their rotations. No identifying data of professionals or patients were collected.

To ensure compliance with ethical and institutional requirements, permissions were obtained from the educational supervisors and clinical practice coordinators at all participating centers.

Coding was conducted independently by two researchers and discrepancies were resolved through consensus to enhance credibility and confirmability of the analysis. Reflexive discussions were conducted during the analytic process to consider potential researcher assumptions and minimise interpretive bias during coding and theme development. Brief analytic notes were maintained during theme development to support transparency and consistency of interpretation.

The qualitative responses were intended to complement the quantitative observations by providing illustrative examples of students’ reflections. Given the exploratory educational purpose of the study, qualitative data were not intended to generate an in-depth interpretive theory but rather to provide contextualized insight into how students perceived and interpreted organisational and patient-safety processes during placements. Consequently, thematic analysis focused on identifying recurrent reflective patterns that complemented the quantitative findings. Formal data saturation was therefore not considered applicable within the scope and design of this mixed-methods educational study.

Thematic analysis followed the six-phase approach described by Braun and Clarke (2006). Reflexive discussions were incorporated throughout the analytic process to consider potential researcher assumptions and enhance interpretive transparency, consistent with later reflexive developments in thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2019). Students’ original responses were written in Spanish and translated into English for reporting purposes. Translation accuracy was verified through bilingual review by the research team to preserve semantic meaning. Trustworthiness was supported through independent coding, consensus discussions, and transparent reporting of analytic procedures, enhancing credibility and confirmability. These procedures also contributed to the dependability of the analysis, while transferability was considered through the detailed description of the study context.

Mixed-methods integration

Quantitative and qualitative findings were triangulated to provide a comprehensive understanding of students' observations and learning experiences.

Quantitative findings

Overall scores on clinical management and safety culture

Across the whole sample, mean scores for the 10 items ranged between 3.86 and 4.46 on a 1–5 scale, indicating generally positive perceptions of clinical management and patient-safety culture. The highest mean values were observed for clarity of team organization and task distribution (Item 1; M = 4.46, SD = 0.63), global assessment of the care environment (Item 10; M = 4.46, SD = 0.60), and completeness and correctness of clinical documentation (Item 8; M = 4.41, SD = 0.67). In contrast, the lowest mean score was observed for time and workflow management (Item 5; M = 3.86, SD = 0.80), suggesting that students perceived greater room for improvement in the management of delays, duplications, and waiting times.

The overall mean CMOT score per student (average of the 10 items) was 4.24 (SD = 0.41; range 2.8–5.0), reflecting a generally favourable perception of the organizational and safety climate in the practice settings. These results suggest that students generally perceived organisational structures and documentation practices positively, while time and workflow management were more frequently identified as areas with greater room for improvement.

Hospital versus primary care settings

When comparing hospital and primary care settings, mean scores were high in both contexts, with relatively small differences in the overall CMOT index. The mean overall CMOT score was 4.24 (SD = 0.43) in hospital settings and 4.28 (SD = 0.32) in primary care settings.

No statistically significant difference was found in the global mean score between hospital and primary care (t-test, p = 0.64; Mann–Whitney U, p = 0.78).

At item level, most domains showed similar scores across settings. However, a statistically significant difference was observed for Item 6 (patient/family information and participation), with higher scores in primary care (M = 4.67, SD = 0.58) compared with hospital settings (M = 4.27, SD = 0.81; p = 0.004).

This suggests that students perceived stronger patient/family information and involvement in decision-making in primary care compared with hospital services. Differences in the remaining items (team organization, resources, communication, protocols, time management, safety practices, documentation, leadership and global assessment) did not reach statistical significance (all p > 0.05).

Given the small and uneven sample sizes across specific hospital units, no formal comparative analysis by individual service (e.g., emergency department, medical wards, operating theatre) was conducted. Descriptive inspection, however, indicated variability between highly acute units and more stable wards, particularly in time management and perceived workload. Accordingly, comparisons between hospital and primary care settings should be interpreted as exploratory and descriptive rather than confirmatory.

Teamwork and communication as strengths

Many students highlighted well-coordinated teamwork and clear internal communication as the most positive aspects observed. They frequently described how nurses and other professionals divided tasks, organized the shift, and maintained shared situational awareness during handovers and daily routines. Examples included structured shift reports, explicit prioritization of patients, and supportive collaboration within the team. One student noted that “communication between professionals was fluid and tasks were clearly organised throughout the shift” (Student, hospital placement), illustrating how students associated coordinated teamwork with safer care environments.

Students perceived these behaviours as directly linked to quality of care and safety, noting that clear role distribution and effective communication helped prevent errors and ensured continuity of care.

Organizational tensions and resource constraints

Alongside these strengths, students identified several areas for improvement in clinical management. Integrated interpretation

To strengthen interpretation of the findings, quantitative and qualitative results were integrated to identify converging patterns in students’ observations across clinical settings. The integration of quantitative and qualitative findings indicates that the Clinical Management Observation Tool (CMOT) enabled students to systematically assess key dimensions of clinical management and patient-safety culture during their placements. High scores across most items, combined with rich reflective narratives, suggest that the tool supported structured, critical observation rather than purely task-focused participation. The significantly higher rating for patient and family involvement in primary care reinforces the potential of this setting as a reference environment for communication and shared decision-making learning.

These converging findings suggest that structured observation enabled students to connect observable organisational features with underlying safety and management processes. Differences observed between hospital and primary care settings were also reflected in qualitative accounts, particularly regarding communication practices and patient involvement, reinforcing the educational relevance of contextual variation. Qualitative reflections also helped explain why some domains received higher quantitative ratings, particularly in primary care settings, where students frequently described stronger continuity of communication, clearer patient interaction, and greater opportunities for relational participation. In contrast, descriptions of workflow pressure and organisational complexity in hospital settings provided contextual insight into lower ratings related to time management and coordination.

Discussion

This study contributes to the growing body of evidence on the role of structured observation in supporting students’ interpretation of organisational processes and patient-safety culture during clinical placements. Consistent with international literature, students were able to identify organisational strengths—particularly teamwork, documentation quality, and clear role distribution—while also recognising vulnerabilities related to workload, communication gaps, and resource limitations (Sammer et al., 2021; Aiken et al., 2012). These findings reinforce the value of structured pedagogical supports that help students move beyond procedural tasks and engage with system-level characteristics of care delivery. These patterns may reflect the influence of organisational structures, supervision practices, and established safety routines that shape how students interpret clinical environments during placements.

The present study does not measure objective learning gains but rather explores students’ perceptions and reflections generated through a structured observation process. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of perceived educational value and systems awareness development rather than direct measurement of learning effectiveness.

Structured observation as a formative educational intervention

This study was not designed to establish causal relationships or measure the effectiveness of an educational intervention in experimental terms. Instead, it sought to explore how structured observation functions as a formative learning strategy within real clinical environments. By providing students with a guided framework to observe organisational processes, communication patterns and safety practices, the CMOT supported a shift from task-oriented participation toward systems-oriented understanding.

The findings suggest that structured observation facilitates the development of professional judgement by enabling students to recognise how organisational factors shape care quality and patient safety. This capacity to “see the system” is increasingly recognised as a core competency for contemporary nursing practice, particularly in complex and resource-constrained healthcare settings.

High organisational scores and their alignment with global evidence

Students’ generally positive ratings (overall mean 4.24/5) reflect the presence of established safety practices and coordinated workflows in many clinical environments, echoing findings from studies conducted in Europe, North America and Asia (Lee and Dahinten, 2021; Feng et al., 2022). However, previous studies have also highlighted considerable variability in clinical learning environments and patient-safety cultures, with some settings characterised by inconsistent supervision, communication breakdowns, or resource constraints that may negatively influence students’ learning experiences.This pattern may also reflect the structured supervision models commonly used in clinical placements, where students observe established team routines and safety practices that shape their interpretation of organisational functioning.Team organisation and documentation quality were particularly highly rated—domains repeatedly identified as markers of a mature safety culture and effective clinical governance (Pronovost et al., 2006; WHO, 2021). These findings may also be influenced by the structured organisation of clinical placements, where supervision practices, clearly defined team roles, and established documentation routines provide students with visible models of coordinated and safe care delivery.

Variation in management domains and system pressures

Time and workflow management received the lowest scores, a pattern reported internationally. These observations may reflect broader structural pressures within healthcare systems, including increasing service demand, complex patient care trajectories, and organisational constraints that shape workflow efficiency and communication processes in clinical environments.Studies show that hospitals often exhibit higher task intensity, variable staffing ratios, and workflow fragmentation, which contribute to communication failures and missed care (Aiken et al., 2012; Grealish et al., 2020). Students demonstrated the capacity to recognise these pressures, signalling emerging systems-thinking skills that are essential for safe practice in complex environments.

Primary care as a setting that enhances communication and participation

The significantly higher score in patient/family information and participation in primary care aligns with global research showing that longitudinal relationships, clearer appointment structures and lower technological intensity favour relational communication (Chan et al., 2023; Ministerio de Sanidad, 2011). These findings highlight primary care as a pedagogically valuable environment for developing person-centred communication and shared decision-making competencies—skills emphasised in international education frameworks (ICN, 2020; WHO, 2021).

Reflective depth and system awareness in qualitative findings

The qualitative themes reveal sophisticated reflective engagement. Students noticed alignment between team coordination, handover quality and safety—relationships widely documented in patient-safety literature (Lee and Dahinten, 2021; Pronovost et al., 2006). Importantly, students’ reflections extended beyond isolated observations and frequently connected organisational factors with perceived implications for patient safety, workflow efficiency, and quality of care. Qualitative responses therefore provided contextual insight into how students interpreted system-level processes within clinical environments, supporting the view that structured observation promoted emerging systems awareness rather than simple descriptive reporting.

They also identified actionable opportunities for improvement—briefings, stock management, clearer delegation, interprofessional meetings—which mirror interventions used internationally to strengthen safety culture.

This supports the argument that structured observation promotes higher-order cognitive processes, enabling students to articulate how organisational factors shape care quality. Recent systematic reviews confirm that reflective, scaffolded learning approaches are essential for helping students understand complexity in clinical systems (Phillips et al., 2020; Erlam et al., 2022).

Strengths and limitations

Key strengths include the multi-site design (three hospitals and nine primary care centres), the use of a validated conceptual foundation, and a mixed-methods approach that enhances interpretative richness through the integration of quantitative scores and qualitative reflections. These characteristics align with methodological recommendations for educational research (Chan et al., 2023).

Limitations include sample imbalance across settings, limited unit-level comparisons, and the need for further psychometric validation of the CMOT. In particular, the absence of a formal quantitative content validity index should be acknowledged as a methodological limitation. Nevertheless, the consistency of findings with international literature supports the tool’s utility. The unequal distribution of observations between hospital and primary care settings reflects the structure of clinical placements in the program and may have reduced statistical power for between-setting comparisons. Consequently, non-significant differences should be interpreted cautiously due to the potential risk of Type II error.

In addition, the qualitative component was based on a single open-ended reflective question and was designed to complement quantitative findings rather than to achieve deep interpretive qualitative analysis. Consequently, qualitative findings should be interpreted as exploratory and contextual rather than as a comprehensive qualitative account of students’ experiences.

Overall significance

In a global context where nursing education increasingly emphasises safety, leadership and system understanding, this study provides empirical evidence that structured observation is a feasible, low-cost and pedagogically powerful strategy. By enabling students to critically interpret the clinical environment, the CMOT aligns with international standards for preparing nurses capable of contributing to safe, effective and system-aware care.

Implications for nursing education and workforce preparation

The findings of this study highlight the educational value of incorporating structured observation tools into undergraduate nursing curricula. The CMOT enabled students to move beyond task-oriented participation and to engage in systematic, critical observation of organizational processes, leadership behaviors, communication patterns and safety practices. This approach can strengthen key competencies related to clinical governance, teamwork and system awareness—competencies increasingly emphasized in competency-based nursing education frameworks.

Structured observation fosters reflective learning by helping students articulate what they see, interpret how clinical environments function and identify potential areas for improvement. This aligns with contemporary calls for transformative learning in health professions education, which emphasize the need to prepare graduates capable of understanding and contributing to complex healthcare systems. Integrating similar tools into practicum courses could support:•the development of students' ability to recognize organizational strengths and vulnerabilities;•early engagement with quality and safety principles;•improved understanding of interdisciplinary collaboration;•and enhanced capacity to link theory with real-world practice.

Primary care placements, in particular, may serve as valuable environments for learning communication and patient-participation skills, as suggested by the higher scores in patient/family involvement. Educators may consider using structured observation to intentionally guide students' attention to these domains across diverse settings. Educators may consider incorporating structured observation as a recurring formative activity supported by guided debriefings, reflective writing and supervisor feedback.

Beyond its educational value, structured observation may contribute to workforce preparation by supporting early development of systems awareness, safety culture understanding and professional accountability. Embedding similar strategies within clinical placements could help bridge the gap between academic learning and the realities of contemporary nursing practice, facilitating a smoother transition from student to registered nurse.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that a structured Clinical Management Observation Tool (CMOT) may support nursing students’ learning during clinical placements by guiding them to critically observe management processes and patient-safety culture. Students identified key strengths in teamwork, communication and documentation, while also noting areas for improvement such as time management, material availability and interprofessional coordination. Primary care settings showed particularly strong performance in patient and family involvement, underscoring their potential as pedagogical environments for developing person-centered communication skills.

The CMOT appears to promote reflective, system-focused learning consistent with contemporary educational priorities in nursing. As health systems evolve and clinical environments grow more complex, tools that help students understand organizational dynamics and their impact on care quality may play an essential role in preparing future nurses for active participation in safety culture and clinical governance. Further research should explore the psychometric properties of the CMOT and examine its applicability in different educational and clinical contexts.
