Correction to “Local Information Privacy and Its Applications to Data Aggregation”
Bo Jiang, Ming Li, and Ravi Tandon

Abstract
In our previous works [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] , we defined ( ϵ , δ ) (\epsilon,\delta) -Local Information Privacy (LIP) as a context-aware privacy notion and presented the corresponding privacy-preserving mechanism. Then we claim that the mechanism satisfies ( ϵ , 0 ) (\epsilon,0) -LIP for any ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 for arbitrary P X P_{X} . However, this claim is not completely correct. In this document, we provide a correction to the valid range of privacy parameters of our previously proposed LIP mechanism. Further, we propose efficient algorithms to expand the range of valid privacy parameters. Finally, we discuss the impact of updated results on our original paper’s experiments, the rationale of the proposed correction and corrected results.

II
Discussion

1.

Impact of updated results on our original paper’s experiments:
The analysis presented in this document focuses solely on the valid range of
\epsilon
and does not alter any of the mechanism’s parameters. Consequently, the experimental results from the original paper remain valid, with the only change being an updated
\epsilon
range recalculated based on the analysis in this correction.

2.

The choice of
\epsilon,\delta
for local mechanisms in the wild:
The real-world implementation of local privatization models usually adopts large
\epsilon
s to provide good utility. For example, Apple picks
\epsilon=8
in their LDP-based Count Mean Sketch protocol for domain collection, and
\epsilon=4
for emoji collection
[
4
]
. This does not significantly degrade the privacy guarantee, as they use an Encryption - Shuffling - Analysis (ESA) architecture. In this architecture, the shuffler, positioned in the middle, prevents the server from tracking the original data source and provides privacy amplification by a factor of
1/\sqrt{N}
, where
N
denotes the number of users. We have theoretically shown that
2\epsilon
-LIP implies
\epsilon
-LDP in proposition 1 of
[
1
]
, which means LIP can also benefit from shuffling. Even though the original work on shuffled DP does not include an analysis for the relationship between
(\epsilon,\delta)
-LDP and CDP, the idea of amplifying the privacy guarantee through shuffling remains the same. Additionally, the tightness of this benefit for LIP remains an interesting area for future research.

3.

The rationale of collision in Algorithm 1:
A natural limitation of the mechanism of general randomized response (GRR)
[
5
]
is the suboptimal utility-privacy tradeoff for high-dimensional data, corresponding to a small
P_{\min}
in context-aware models. In LDP research, GRR is usually combined with hashing techniques that reduce input data dimension at the cost of slight data input collision. This technique has been proven to achieve a better utility-privacy tradeoff than GRR for high-dimensional data. A natural extension of this idea to LIP is to design functions that combine inputs with small priors until
P_{\min}
is greater than
1/(e^{\epsilon}+1)
for a given
\epsilon
. This operation guarantees privacy and utility concurrently.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem 1.

In this section, we proof Theorem 1, i.e., the context-aware RR mechanism achieves
(\epsilon,0)
- LIP, where

\epsilon\geq\log\left(\frac{1}{P_{\min}}-1\right).

We first introduce the following lemma which is helpful in the proof.

Lemma 1
.

With the context-aware RR, the marginal distribution of
Y
is equivalent to the marginal distribution of
X
, i.e.
P_{X}(x)=P_{Y}(x)
, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

\displaystyle P_{Y}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{Y|X}(y|y)P_{X}(y)+\sum_{x\neq y}P_{Y|X}(y|x)P_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{\epsilon}-1+P_{X}(y)}{e^{\epsilon}}P_{X}(y)+\sum_{x\neq y}\frac{P_{X}(y)P_{X}(x)}{e^{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{\epsilon}-1+P_{X}(y)}{e^{\epsilon}}P_{X}(y)+\frac{P_{X}(y)(1-P_{X}(y))}{e^{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{\epsilon}P_{X}(y)-P_{X}(y)+P_{X}(y)P_{X}(y)}{e^{\epsilon}}+\frac{P_{X}(y)-P_{X}(y)P_{X}(y)}{e^{\epsilon}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{X}(y).

∎

For
(\epsilon,0)
-LIP, Definition 1 can be further simplified to, for all
y,x\in\mathcal{X}
:

e^{-\epsilon}\leq\frac{P_{Y}(y)}{P_{Y|X}(y|x)}\leq e^{\epsilon}.

Case 1:
When
x\neq y
:
the privacy metric
\frac{P_{Y}(y)}{P_{Y|X}(y|x)}
becomes:

\frac{P_{X}(y)}{P_{Y|X}(y|x)}=\frac{P_{X}(y)}{P_{X}(y)e^{-\epsilon}}\overset{\Delta}{=}e^{\epsilon},

which is guaranteed to be bounded by
[e^{-\epsilon},e^{\epsilon}]
.

Case 2:
When
x=y
:
the privacy metric
\frac{P_{Y}(y)}{P_{Y|X}(y|x)}
becomes:

\frac{P_{X}(y)}{P_{Y|X}(y|x)}=\frac{P_{X}(y)}{1-\frac{(1-P_{X}(y))}{e^{\epsilon}}}=\frac{P_{X}(y)e^{\epsilon}}{e^{\epsilon}-1+P_{X}(y)}.

Next, we compare with the upper and lower bound respectively.

Comparing with the upper bound:

\displaystyle e^{\epsilon}-\frac{P_{X}(y)e^{\epsilon}}{e^{\epsilon}-1+P_{X}(y)}=

\displaystyle\frac{[e^{\epsilon}-1+P_{X}(y)-P_{X}(y)]e^{\epsilon}}{e^{\epsilon}-1+P_{X}(y)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(e^{\epsilon}-1)e^{\epsilon}}{e^{\epsilon}-1+P_{X}(y)}\geq 0.

Comparing with the lower bound:

\displaystyle\frac{P_{X}(y)e^{\epsilon}}{e^{\epsilon}-1+P_{X}(y)}-e^{-\epsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{X}(y)e^{\epsilon}-e^{-\epsilon}(e^{\epsilon}-1+P_{X}(y))}{e^{\epsilon}-1+P_{X}(y)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{X}(y)e^{\epsilon}-1+e^{-\epsilon}-P_{X}(y)e^{-\epsilon}}{e^{\epsilon}-1+P_{X}(y)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{X}(y)e^{\epsilon}(1-e^{-2\epsilon})-(1-e^{-\epsilon})}{e^{\epsilon}-1+P_{X}(y)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{X}(y)e^{\epsilon}(1-e^{-\epsilon})(1+e^{-\epsilon})-(1-e^{-\epsilon})}{e^{\epsilon}-1+P_{X}(y)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(1-e^{-\epsilon})[P_{X}(y)e^{\epsilon}(1+e^{-\epsilon})-1]}{e^{\epsilon}-1+P_{X}(y)}.

Note that
(1-e^{-\epsilon})\geq 0
,
e^{\epsilon}-1+P_{X}(y)\geq 0
, therefore, the lower bound holds when:

P_{X}(y)e^{\epsilon}(1+e^{-\epsilon})-1\geq 0,\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{or}\penalty\ \penalty\ 1-e^{-\epsilon}=0

which implies for all
y\in\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle P_{X}(y)\geq\frac{1}{e^{\epsilon}+1},\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{or}\penalty\ \penalty\ \epsilon=0.

This further implies that the mechanism achieves
\epsilon
-LIP if for the
\min_{y\in\mathcal{X}}P_{X}(y)
, denoted as
P_{\min}
in the following:

P_{\min}\geq\frac{1}{e^{\epsilon}+1}.

Therefore, the mechanism achieves
\epsilon
-LIP, when

\epsilon\geq\log\left(\frac{1}{P_{\min}}-1\right).
