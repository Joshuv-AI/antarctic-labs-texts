Critical speed of a binary superfuid of light
Pierre-Élie Larré, Claire Michel, Nicolas Cherroret

Abstract
We theoretically study the critical speed for superfluid flow of a two-dimensional (2D) binary superfluid of light past a polarization-sensitive optical obstacle. This speed corresponds to the maximum mean flow velocity below which dissipation is absent. In the weak-obstacle regime, linear-response theory shows that the critical speed is set by Landau’s criterion applied to the density and spin Bogoliubov modes, whose relative ordering can be inverted due to saturation of the optical nonlinearity. For obstacles of arbitrary strength and large spatial extent, we determine the critical speed from the conditions for strong ellipticity of the stationary hydrodynamic equations within the hydraulic and incompressible approximations. Numerical simulations in this regime reveal that the breakdown of superfluidity is initiated by the nucleation of vortex-antivortex pairs for an impenetrable obstacle, and of Jones-Roberts soliton-type structures for a penetrable obstacle. Beyond superfluids of light, our results provide a general framework for the critical speed of 2D binary nonlinear Schrödinger superflows, including Bose-Bose quantum mixtures.

Model equations

In this section, we first describe the optical setup of interest, followed by the introduction of the wave equation that governs the dynamics of the binary superfluid of light. We then derive a hydrodynamic formulation for it and, finally, establish the model describing the flow incident on the obstacle.

2.1
Binary superfluid

We consider a polarized, monochromatic laser beam propagating along the

\smash{n_{2}=n_{2,++}=n_{2,--}}

The laser amplitudes

(3)

where

\smash{U_{\pm}(\mathbf{r})=-kn_{1,\pm}(\mathbf{r})/n_{0}}
,

\smash{\rho=\rho_{+}+\rho_{-}}

\smash{\pm}

\smash{\rho_{\pm}}

\smash{\mathbf{v}_{\pm}=\nabla\phi_{\pm}/k}

\smash{x{-}y}
plane. The potential
\smash{U_{\pm}(\mathbf{r})}
describes a localized, static obstacle affecting the flow of the
\smash{\pm}
component. This potential is centered at
\smash{r=0}

\smash{r\gg w}

\smash{U_{\pm}>0}

\smash{U_{-}(\mathbf{r})}

(5)

(6)

\smash{g}

\smash{\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}}

where the coefficient

\alpha=\frac{g_{+-}}{g}\in(0,1)

\smash{\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}\neq\rho_{\mathrm{sat}}}

\smash{\beta\ll 1}

2.2
Hydrodynamic description

The wave equation (
7

\smash{\psi_{+}}
and
\smash{\psi_{-}}
(see, e.g., Refs.

\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{+}\\
\psi_{-}\end{bmatrix}=\sqrt{\rho}e^{i\Phi/2}\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\theta/2)e^{i\phi/2}e^{-i\mu_{+}z}\\
\sin(\theta/2)e^{-i\phi/2}e^{-i\mu_{-}z}\end{bmatrix}.

This representation is equivalent to parametrizing the

(11)

\smash{\pm}

3.2
Drag force

Concluding Sec.
3
, we have identified two mean-velocity thresholds for excitation of the two-component superfluid. Within linear-response theory, these are the speeds
\smash{c_{\mathrm{d}}}
and
\smash{c_{\mathrm{s}}}
of Bogoliubov phonons in the density and spin modes. Below the lowest of these two speeds, the flow is superfluid with zero drag. Wave resistance, following a terminology established in the context of capillary-gravity waves
26
, is abruptly activated above this critical speed, resulting in significant drag due to emission of density and spin Bogoliubov waves. However, linear-response theory does not predict how the critical speed depends on the characteristic parameters of the obstacle potential, such as its amplitudes for instance. To explore this dependence, we must employ a theoretical approach that extends beyond. This is addressed in the subsequent section.

4
Nonlinear response

Hydrodynamically, superfluid motion is characterized by a
stationary
flow that
monotonically flattens
far from the obstacle. In the present section, we derive the conditions for the existence of such a flow in the presence of a repulsive obstacle potential with
arbitrary
total and relative amplitudes
\smash{U>0}
and
\smash{-U<u<U}
[see Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
)]. In doing so, we go beyond the linear-response regime (
\smash{U,|u|\ll\mu_{0}}
) investigated in the previous section.
We show that these conditions reduce to requiring that the mean asymptotic velocity
\smash{V_{0}}
be bounded by a critical value
\smash{V_{\mathrm{c}}}
, which depends on
\smash{U}
and
\smash{u}
, and can naturally be identified as the critical speed for superfluidity.

To make the problem analytically tractable, we restrict our attention to an obstacle of radius
\smash{w\gg 1}
, i.e., in proper units, larger than the characteristic healing length
\smash{\xi=1/(kg\rho_{0})^{1/2}}
introduced in Sec.
2
. In this regime, the hydrodynamic fields vary in the
\smash{x{-}y}
plane on length scales of the order of
\smash{w}
, so that all the dispersive, gradient-type terms in Eqs. (
13
) and (
15
) can be neglected. This dispersionless approach, sometimes referred to as the
hydraulic
approximation
86
;
7
, may be regarded as a generalization of the Thomas-Fermi approximation
52
to nonzero flow velocity, and is relevant for experiments
17
;
2
.

As previously mentioned, we focus on a stationary (
\smash{z}
-independent) flow. Correspondingly, the two continuity equations (
16
) read

\nabla\cdot\bigg(\!\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\sigma\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{V}+\begin{bmatrix}\sigma\\
\rho\end{bmatrix}\frac{\mathbf{v}}{2}\bigg)=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix},

(31)

and the two Bernoulli equations (
13
) and (
15
) become, in the hydraulic approximation,

\displaystyle\frac{(1+\alpha)\rho}{1+\beta\rho}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}-U\Theta(w-r)-(V^{2}-V_{0}{}^{2})-\frac{v^{2}}{4},

(32)

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\alpha)\sigma}{1+\beta\rho}

\displaystyle=-u\Theta(w-r)-\mathbf{V}\cdot\mathbf{v}.

(33)

The first two equations (
31
) constitute the differential part of the problem, while the subsequent two equations (
32
) and (
33
) provide algebraic relations between the densities
\smash{\rho}
,
\smash{\sigma}
and the velocities
\smash{\mathbf{V}}
,
\smash{\mathbf{v}}
of the binary superfluid. Importantly, we restrict ourselves to hydrodynamic solutions
\smash{[\rho(\mathbf{r}),\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{r}),\sigma(\mathbf{r}),\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{r})]}
that approach the uniform configuration
\smash{(1,V_{0}\hat{\mathbf{x}},0,0)}
at a large distance
\smash{r\gg w}
from the obstacle, as specified in Eq. (
17

\smash{V_{0}}
for given obstacle (
\smash{U}
,
\smash{u}
) and fluid (
\smash{\alpha}
,
\smash{\beta}
) parameters.

4.1
Elliptic flow

Fundamentally, such superfluid solutions exist provided the two coupled partial differential equations in (
31

strongly elliptic

\smash{dx/dy}
of the characteristic curves
\smash{x=x(y)}

\smash{\mathcal{B}}
, and
\smash{\mathcal{C}}
are expressed in terms of
\smash{\sigma/\rho}
,
\smash{A}
,
\smash{B}
,
\smash{C}
, and
\smash{D}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\rho^{2}}>0,

(46)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle=A+B-\frac{\sigma}{\rho}(C+D),

(47)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=AB-CD.

(48)

Solving the quartic equation (
45
) provides the characteristic velocities
\smash{dx/dy}
of Eq. (
40
):

\bigg(\frac{dx}{dy}\bigg)^{2}=\frac{-\mathcal{B}\pm\sqrt{\mathcal{B}^{2}-4\mathcal{A}\mathcal{C}}}{2\mathcal{A}}=\frac{2\mathcal{C}}{-\mathcal{B}\pm\sqrt{\mathcal{B}^{2}-4\mathcal{A}\mathcal{C}}}.

(49)

As explained above, Eq. (
40
) is strongly elliptic—and the flow is therefore superfluid
16
;
15
—when
\smash{dx/dy\in i\mathbb{R}}
. This condition is satisfied if and only if all three of the following inequalities hold:

\mathcal{B}>0,\quad\mathcal{C}>0,\quad\mathcal{B}^{2}-4\mathcal{A}\mathcal{C}>0.

(50)

Although these local conditions may seem obscure in their raw mathematical form, they are, in fact, fully equivalent to a local-density generalization of the Landau criterion, which holds true in the hydraulic approximation (see, e.g., Refs.
92
;
86
;
46
;
47
for single-component superfluids). This equivalence will be illustrated in Sec.
4.3
in the case of an impenetrable obstacle potential.

A close inspection of Eqs. (
32
), (
33
), (
39
), (
41
)–(
44
), and (
46
)–(
48
) shows that inequalities (
50
) involve only the mean and relative velocities in the regions where superfluidity breaks down [at
\smash{(x,y)=(0,\pm w^{\pm})}
or for
\smash{r<w}
]. To recast these conditions into a single constraint on the incident velocity
\smash{V_{0}}
, thereby identifying the critical speed
\smash{V_{\mathrm{c}}}
, we must first determine how these local velocities are related to
\smash{V_{0}}
. This is the purpose of the next section.

4.2
Incompressible flow

To determine the mean and relative velocity fields
\smash{\mathbf{V}}
and
\smash{\mathbf{v}}
, we treat the flow as
incompressible
. This involves assuming that the total and relative densities
\smash{\rho}
and
\smash{\sigma}
are independent of velocity, which is equivalent to neglecting the kinetic terms in the Bernoulli equations (
32
) and (
33
). In this limit,
\smash{\rho}
and
\smash{\sigma}
become piecewise-constant functions of the radial coordinate
\smash{r}
:

\displaystyle\frac{(1+\alpha)\rho}{1+\beta\rho}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0}-U\Theta(w-r),

(51)

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\alpha)\sigma}{1+\beta\rho}

\displaystyle=-u\Theta(w-r),

(52)

and the continuity equations (
31
) reduce to a simple Laplace problem for the flow potentials
\smash{\Phi}
and
\smash{\phi}
:

\nabla^{2}\!\begin{bmatrix}\Phi\\
\phi\end{bmatrix}\underset{r\gtrless w}{=}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix},\quad\begin{bmatrix}\Phi\\
\phi\end{bmatrix}\underset{r\gg w}{\simeq}\begin{bmatrix}2V_{0}r\cos\varphi\\
0\end{bmatrix},

(53)

where
\smash{\varphi}
denotes the angular coordinate of
\smash{\mathbf{r}=r(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\cos\varphi+\hat{\mathbf{y}}\sin\varphi)}
. Note that this incompressible approach, first introduced in Ref.
84
, was later extended by Rica
81
using a Janzen-Rayleigh expansion
60
;
53
, a standard technique for capturing compressibility effects. Formally, this involves an expansion of the velocity potentials in the compressibility
\smash{1/(kc^{2})=1/(g\rho_{0})}
relative to
\smash{1/(kV_{0}{}^{2})}
. Although the superfluid is not strictly incompressible, recent findings
46
;
47
indicate that the leading-order, incompressible approximation is sufficient to capture the essential features of the critical speed across a wide range of obstacle strengths. Given its demonstrated accuracy in the hydraulic regime, we adopt this simpler incompressible framework here.

From Eqs. (
51
) and (
52
), we now express the densities
\smash{\rho}
and
\smash{\sigma}
as functions of the obstacle amplitudes
\smash{U}
and
\smash{u}
. This step, essential for determining the velocity fields, also allows us to establish the domain of validity of the incompressible approximation in terms of
\smash{U}
and
\smash{u}
. Outside the obstacle, in the region
\smash{r>w}
of the plane,
\smash{\rho}
and
\smash{\sigma}
are simply [use Eq. (
18
) for
\smash{\mu_{0}}
]

\rho_{\mathrm{out}}=1,\quad\sigma_{\mathrm{out}}=0,

(54)

while inside, for
\smash{r<w}
, they take the form

\rho_{\mathrm{in}}=\frac{1-U/\mu_{0}}{1+\beta U/\mu_{0}},\quad\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}=-\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}\frac{u/\mu_{0}}{1+\beta U/\mu_{0}}.

(55)

For the first identity in (
55
) to be physically meaningful, i.e.,
\smash{\rho_{\mathrm{in}}>0}
, the total potential amplitude
\smash{U}
must satisfy
\smash{U/\mu_{0}<1}
. Together with the condition in Eqs. (
5
) that the obstacle be repulsive, we thus obtain

0<\frac{U}{\mu_{0}}<1.

(56)

We deduce that for
\smash{U/\mu_{0}>1}
,
\smash{\rho_{\mathrm{in}}}
must identically vanish since it cannot be physically negative. This imposes
\smash{\rho_{+,\mathrm{in}}=\rho_{-,\mathrm{in}}=0}
, and thus
\smash{\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}}
must also vanish. In this regime, the superfluid is completely excluded from the interior of the obstacle potential, which can therefore be described as
impenetrable
. In the
penetrable
regime (
56
), the condition
\smash{|\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}|/\rho_{\mathrm{in}}=|\!\cos\theta_{\mathrm{in}}|<1}
must additionally be satisfied for the second identity in (
55
) to be well defined. This further constrains the relative potential amplitude
\smash{u}
to
\smash{|u|/\mu_{0}<[(1-\alpha)/(1+\alpha)](1-U/\mu_{0})}
. Given the repulsiveness condition in Eqs. (
6
),
\smash{u}
must therefore lie within the range

\frac{|u|}{\mu_{0}}<\begin{cases}\frac{U}{\mu_{0}}&\text{if}\quad\frac{U}{\mu_{0}}<\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\\
\frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha}\bigg(1-\frac{U}{\mu_{0}}\bigg)&\text{if}\quad\frac{U}{\mu_{0}}>\frac{1-\alpha}{2}\end{cases}.

(57)

In summary, two distinct regimes emerge within the incompressible approximation for the velocity fields. When
\smash{U/\mu_{0}>1}
, the obstacle potential is impenetrable, excluding the superfluid from its interior, so that
\smash{\rho_{\mathrm{in}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}=0}
. Conversely, in the penetrable regime (
56
), the superfluid penetrates the obstacle and the interior densities
\smash{\rho_{\mathrm{in}}}
and
\smash{\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}}
are given by Eqs. (
55
). Within this regime, the current theory is limited to the
\smash{U{-}u}
domain specified by (
57
).

With this established, we now solve the Laplace problem (
53
), which in the presence of the obstacle must be supplemented with the following continuity relations at the
\smash{r=w}
boundary:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Phi\\
\phi\end{bmatrix}_{r=w^{+}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi\\
\phi\end{bmatrix}_{r=w^{-}},

(58)

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{r}\Phi\\
\partial_{r}\phi\end{bmatrix}_{r=w^{+}}

\displaystyle=\rho_{\mathrm{in}}\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{r}\Phi\\
\partial_{r}\phi\end{bmatrix}_{r=w^{-}}+\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{r}\phi\\
\partial_{r}\Phi\end{bmatrix}_{r=w^{-}}.

(59)

While Eqs. (
58
) ensure the continuity of the velocity potentials
\smash{\Phi}
and
\smash{\phi}
, Eqs. (
59

\smash{\rho\mathbf{V}+\sigma\mathbf{v}/2}
and
\smash{\sigma\mathbf{V}+\rho\mathbf{v}/2}
[we have used Eqs. (
54
) for the exterior densities], which is a consequence of (

) and holds in the hydraulic limit
\smash{w\gg 1}

;

\smash{a_{0}+b_{0}\ln r+\sum_{n\geqslant 1}r_{n}(r)\varphi_{n}(\varphi)}

\smash{r_{n}(r)=a_{n}r^{n}+b_{n}r^{-n}}
,
\smash{\varphi_{n}(\varphi)=c_{n}\cos(n\varphi)+d_{n}\sin(n\varphi)}
, and the coefficients
\smash{a_{0},\dots,d_{n}}
are determined from the asymptotic and boundary conditions in Eqs. (

), (

), and (

).

The velocity fields

As indicated in Sec.

4.3
Critical speed

4.3.1
Impenetrable obstacle

When the obstacle potential is impenetrable, i.e.,
\smash{U/\mu_{0}>1}
, superfluidity breaks down at the obstacle’s poles
\smash{(r,\varphi)=(w^{+},\pm\pi/2)}
, as will be shown numerically in Sec.
4.4
. At these points, the relative velocity
\smash{v}
given in Eqs. (
62
) and (
63
) is zero, because
\smash{\lambda\propto\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}=0}
. As a result, the relative density
\smash{\sigma}
deduced from Eq. (
33
) at
\smash{r=w^{+}}
also vanishes:

\sigma=0,\quad v=0.

(64)

Equations (
41
)–(
44
) therefore reduce to
\smash{A=1-V^{2}/\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{d}}{}^{2}}
,
\smash{B=1-V^{2}/\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{s}}{}^{2}}
, and
\smash{C=D=0}
, and Eqs. (
46
)–(
48
) become
\smash{\mathcal{A}=1}
,
\smash{\mathcal{B}=A+B}
, and
\smash{\mathcal{C}=AB}
. This being so, the last superfluid constraint in (
50
) provides no further information, as it is automatically satisfied:
\smash{\mathcal{B}^{2}-4\mathcal{A}\mathcal{C}=(A-B)^{2}}
is indeed always positive. The remaining constraints are
\smash{A+B>0}
and
\smash{AB>0}
, which yields the two inequalities

V<\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{d}},\quad V<\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{s}}.

(65)

These conditions represent a local formulation of the Landau criterion (
24
): for the flow to remain superfluid, the local mean velocity must be smaller than both the local density and spin speeds of sound. While these conditions are derived at the obstacle’s poles following Refs.
16
;
15
, Chaplygin’s method
74
;
51
ensures their validity across the entire plane. Crucially, it can be shown from Eqs. (
32
), (
39
), and (
60
) that
\smash{V(\mathbf{r})}
is maximized at the poles, whereas
\smash{\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{d}}(\mathbf{r})}
and
\smash{\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{s}}(\mathbf{r})}
are simultaneously minimized there. Consequently, satisfying the criterion at these specific points is both necessary and sufficient to ensure superfluidity globally, providing a rigorous justification for the polar analysis pioneered in Refs.
16
;
15
. This local Landau criterion is also physically consistent with the hydraulic approximation
92
;
86
;
46
;
47
, which assumes smoothly varying densities and thus supports a local-density description.

In inequalities (
65
), the mean velocity
\smash{V}
is replaced with
\smash{2V_{0}}
using Eqs. (
62
) and (
63
) with
\smash{\Lambda=1}
(which follows from
\smash{\rho_{\mathrm{in}}=\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}=0}
). The total density
\smash{\rho}
in the local speeds of sound
\smash{\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{d}}}
and
\smash{\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{s}}}
, on the other hand, is deduced from Eq. (
32
) at
\smash{r=w^{+}}
, setting
\smash{V=2V_{0}}
and
\smash{v=0}
according to (
64
):

\rho=\frac{1-3V_{0}{}^{2}/\mu_{0}}{1+3\beta V_{0}{}^{2}/\mu_{0}},\quad V=2V_{0}.

(66)

In turn, superfluid flow, encapsulated in inequalities (
65
), is ensured as long as

V_{0}<V_{\mathrm{c}}=\min\{V_{1},V_{2}\},

(67)

where the upper bounds
\smash{V_{1}}
[obtained from the first inequality in (
65
)] and
\smash{V_{2}}
(second inequality) are expressed in units of the Landau speeds of sound
\smash{c_{\mathrm{d}}}
and
\smash{c_{\mathrm{s}}}
, respectively, as

\displaystyle\frac{V_{1}}{c_{\mathrm{d}}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{(1+\beta)[\sqrt{(1+\beta)(121+25\beta)}-(11+5\beta)]}{9\beta}},

(68)

\displaystyle\frac{V_{2}}{c_{\mathrm{s}}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2(1+\alpha)}{11+5\alpha}}.

(69)

Condition (
67
) defines
\smash{V_{\mathrm{c}}}
as the critical speed for superfluidity in the impenetrable-obstacle regime, within the hydraulic and incompressible approximations. Based on the mathematical decoupling of the ellipticity conditions (
50
) into the mode-specific constraints
\smash{V<\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{d}}}
and
\smash{V<\tilde{c}_{\mathrm{s}}}
[arising from the vanishing of the relative fields in Eqs. (
64
)], one might naively associate
\smash{V_{1}}
and
\smash{V_{2}}
with the critical speeds in the density and spin sectors, respectively. However, as will be shown in Sec.
4.4
, the physical excitations generated above
\smash{V_{\mathrm{c}}=\min\{V_{1},V_{2}\}}
appear indistinguishably in both the density and spin maps. Indeed, in the impenetrable regime, the obstacle naturally engenders a strongly nonlinear response that typically results in significant mode hybridization. This physical complexity motivates our use of the
\smash{V_{1(2)}}
notation, purposely avoiding a more reductive labeling such as
\smash{V_{\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{s})}}
.

4.3.2
Penetrable obstacle

For a repulsive and penetrable obstacle potential [condition (

\smash{\beta}

5
Conclusion and outlook

We have studied the critical speed for dissipationless motion of a 2D binary superfluid of light flowing past a repulsive obstacle with polarization-dependent amplitudes. We have considered an optical fluid with saturable intra- and intercomponent interactions, focusing on the simplest case of a balanced mixture in the miscible regime.

The critical speed
\smash{V_{\mathrm{c}}}
corresponds to the lowest mean velocity below which the fluid’s collective modes—of density and spin type in the linear regime—are not excited. We have derived this speed analytically for both weak and arbitrary obstacle strengths. In the weak-obstacle regime, we have employed linear-response theory and showed that
\smash{V_{\mathrm{c}}=\min\{c_{\mathrm{d}},c_{\mathrm{s}}\}}
, in agreement with Landau’s criterion, where
\smash{c_{\mathrm{d}}}
and
\smash{c_{\mathrm{s}}}
are the density and spin Bogoliubov sound velocities. Their relative ordering depends on the degree of interaction saturation. These results are consistent with the behavior of the drag force experienced by the obstacle, which vanishes below
\smash{V_{\mathrm{c}}}
and rises sharply above
\smash{c_{\mathrm{d}}}
and
\smash{c_{\mathrm{s}}}
, signaling energy dissipation via the emission of density and spin Bogoliubov waves.

For arbitrary obstacle strengths, we have simplified the problem using the hydraulic approximation, assuming smooth density profiles, together with an incompressible approximation, where densities are taken to be velocity independent for the purpose of computing the flow. In this framework, the critical speed
\smash{V_{\mathrm{c}}}
is determined by the conditions for strong ellipticity of the two stationary continuity equations. It is lower than the Landau sound velocities and depends on the total and relative obstacle amplitudes
\smash{U}
and
\smash{u}
. We have calculated
\smash{V_{\mathrm{c}}}
for both penetrable and impenetrable obstacles. In the penetrable regime, it is a highly nontrivial decreasing function of
\smash{U}
and
\smash{u}
, while it saturates to a constant value in the impenetrable limit. We have confirmed the analytical estimates for
\smash{V_{\mathrm{c}}}
by real-time numerical simulations of the two coupled nonlinear Schrödinger equations. The simulations further show that superfluidity breaks down through the emission of vortex-antivortex pairs or Jones-Roberts solitons, of both density and spin character, generated respectively at the poles or inside the obstacle for the impenetrable and penetrable cases.

The theoretical results presented in this paper naturally call for further investigation. First, for a penetrable obstacle, we have been able to derive the critical speed only within a region of obstacle amplitudes defined by conditions (
57
). Outside this region, in the white triangle in Fig.
5
, it seems natural to assume, by continuity, that the ratio
\smash{|\sigma_{\mathrm{in}}|/\rho_{\mathrm{in}}}
saturates at its maximum value of unity. However, this assumption leads to the counterintuitive prediction of critical speeds exceeding the Landau sound velocities. This issue could be clarified by incorporating dispersive effects into the theoretical framework
14
;
36
;
5
, and by treating the superfluid compressibility more rigorously through Janzen-Rayleigh expansions, a task we leave for future work. Second, a dedicated analytical study of the magnetic, spin-type vortices and solitons observed in our numerical simulations is warranted. These still poorly studied nonlinear excitations are likely central to a complete understanding of dissipation mechanisms in 2D binary superfluids.

Appendix B
Numerical methods

A standard split-step Fourier method
33
is used to solve the coupled equations (
7
). In this method, the complex field
\smash{\psi_{\pm}(\mathbf{r},z)}
is obtained through the following incremental relation:

\psi_{\pm}(\mathbf{r},z+\delta z)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\Big\{e^{-i\frac{K^{2}}{2}\delta z}\mathcal{F}\Big[e^{-i\hat{P}_{\pm}\delta z}\psi_{\pm}(\mathbf{r},z)\Big]\Big\},

(82)

where
\smash{\mathcal{F}}
denotes the space Fourier transform,
\smash{K}
is the magnitude of the transverse wave vector
\smash{\mathbf{K}=K_{x}\hat{\mathbf{x}}+K_{y}\hat{\mathbf{y}}}
, and
\smash{\hat{P}_{\pm}=U_{\pm}(\mathbf{r})+(\rho_{\pm}+\alpha\rho_{\mp})/(1+\beta\rho)}
describes the obstacle and interaction potentials for the
\smash{\pm}
wave. In the simulations, we use a smoothed version of the two-amplitude sharp disk (
4
), of the form

U_{\pm}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{U_{\pm}}{2}\bigg(1+\tanh\frac{w-r}{\delta w}\bigg),

(83)

which is more amenable to our numerical scheme. We consider a radius
\smash{w=10}
, which is large enough to match the hydraulic regime studied theoretically, and a shoulder
\smash{\delta w=1}
to avoid numerical difficulties at short length scales. The initial light spots are top-hat-shaped with equal intensities,
\smash{\rho_{\pm,0}=1/2}
, and equal angles with respect to the
\smash{z}
axis,
\smash{\mathbf{v}_{\pm,0}=V_{0}\hat{\mathbf{x}}}
:

\psi_{\pm}(\mathbf{r},z=0)=\frac{e^{iV_{0}x}}{\sqrt{2}}e^{-r^{6}/R^{6}}.

(84)

The super-Gaussian weight
\smash{\exp(-r^{6}/R^{6})}
defines the radial extension of the two initial beams. In the simulations, we take
\smash{R=140}
. To minimize transient nonequilibrium features arising from the abrupt introduction of the obstacle and interaction potentials at the entrance,
\smash{z=0}
interface
54
;
85

\smash{1/\mu=1}
, and the propagation is computed until the system reaches a nearly stationary state.
