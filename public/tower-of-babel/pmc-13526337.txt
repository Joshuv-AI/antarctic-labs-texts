Downregulation of UNC5B-AS1 in Preterm Infants with Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia and Its Protective Mechanism via the miR-134-5p/IGF1 Axis
Authors: Jiandong Chen, Wenjuan Zheng, Lingying Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeBronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is a major chronic lung disease in preterm infants. This study was designed to elucidate the functional significance and molecular mechanism of the long non-coding RNA UNC5B-AS1 in BPD pathogenesis.Clinical Samples and Cell AssaysThis study included 50 preterm infants with BPD and 42 healthy newborns. The levels of UNC5B-AS1, miR-134-5p, IGF1, Bax, and Bcl-2 were detected by RT-qPCR. In in vitro experiments, A549 and MLE-12 cells were used to establish a hyperoxia-induced BPD model. A series of assays was performed to evaluate the cellular phenotypes, including CCK-8 for viability, flow cytometry for apoptosis, ELISA for inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), and SOD activity, and DCFH-DA probing for ROS levels. IGF1 protein expression was examined by Western blot. Dual-luciferase reporter assay and RIP experiments were performed to validate the targeting relationships between UNC5B-AS1 and miR-134-5p, and between miR-134-5p and IGF1.ResultsSerum levels of UNC5B-AS1 were significantly downregulated, while miR-134-5p expression was significantly upregulated in BPD infants, both showing good diagnostic value. UNC5B-AS1 directly binds to miR-134-5p. In the cellular model, the protective effect of UNC5B-AS1 overexpression against hyperoxia-induced injury was reversed by miR-134-5p overexpression. Furthermore, IGF1 was confirmed as a direct target of miR‑134‑5p, and knockdown of IGF1 abolished the protective effects achieved by miR‑134‑5p inhibition.ConclusionSerum UNC5B‑AS1 is downregulated and miR‑134‑5p is upregulated in preterm BPD infants. Mechanistically, UNC5B‑AS1 may sponges miR‑134‑5p to upregulate IGF1, protecting against hyperoxia‑induced alveolar injury.Keywords: bronchopulmonary dysplasia, UNC5B-AS1, miR-134-5p, IGF1, newborn

Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is a primary chronic respiratory concern for the preterm infant population, particularly affecting those of very low birth weight.1 A hallmark of BPD is the arrested development of pulmonary blood vessels and alveoli.2,3 BPD can have profound multisystem consequences for preterm infants.4 Regarding the respiratory system, it leads to persistent lung function impairment from infancy through young adulthood.5 In terms of the neurological system, it is closely associated with lower average intelligence quotient (IQ), cognitive deficits, and language impairments.6 Despite continuous advances in perinatal care, the incidence of BPD remains high,7 imposing a heavy burden on affected children, their families, and society.8 Therefore, identifying effective biomarkers for early diagnosis and intervention targets holds significant clinical importance.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are non-coding RNA molecules longer than 200 nt.9 As crucial epigenetic regulators, lncRNAs play key roles in various lung diseases.10,11 Dysregulation of lncRNAs has been functionally linked to key pathological features of BPD. Among these, H19 participates in regulating lung injury in newborn mice with hyperoxia-induced BPD.12 Metastasis-associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1 (MALAT1) can modulate arachidonate 5-lipoxygenase (ALOX5) activity by binding to miR-188-3p, thereby mediating inflammatory responses in the lungs.13 Currently, research on UNC5B antisense RNA 1 (UNC5B-AS1) is very limited. However, recent evidence has shown that UNC5B-AS1 can suppress the inflammatory phenotypic transition in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells via lactylation modification in the context of pulmonary hypertension,14 suggesting its potential as a regulator of inflammation and apoptosis. The only relevant finding in BPD is its downregulation in exosomes from the umbilical cord blood of preterm infants with BPD.15 The specific mechanisms by which UNC5B-AS1 influences the pathogenesis and development of BPD remain entirely unexplored.

The competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) hypothesis provides a theoretical framework for understanding how lncRNAs can regulate downstream target gene expression by “sponging” microRNAs (miRNAs).16 Specifically, lncRNAs can function as ceRNAs by binding to miRNAs through response elements, thereby acting as “molecular sponges” that relieve the inhibitory effect of miRNAs on their target messenger RNAs (mRNAs). This regulatory layer is biologically significant because it allows lncRNAs to indirectly modulate the expression of proteins critical for cellular function, including those involved in inflammation, apoptosis, and tissue development. In the context of BPD, where oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation drive disease progression, ceRNA networks have emerged as key players. For example, a study by Zhong et al demonstrated that the lncRNA TUG1 mitigates inflammation by binding to miR-29a-3p, thereby protecting against hyperoxia-induced lung injury.17 Based on this theory, miRNAs are key effector molecules in this regulatory network. Notably, in experimental BPD models, miR-134-5p confers protection by suppressing ferroptosis.18 However, its expression levels in BPD patients and its upstream regulatory mechanisms remain to be elucidated. Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF1) is a key growth factor essential for lung development and repair, and its signaling is dynamically regulated in hyperoxia-induced neonatal lung injury.19 Given that miRNAs typically exert their effects by binding to the 3′-untranslated region (3’UTR) of target mRNAs, we investigated whether IGF1 is a direct downstream target of miR-134-5p.

Based on the above background, we hypothesized that UNC5B-AS1 might act as a ceRNA involved in BPD via miR-134-5p/IGF1 axis. To test this hypothesis, we combined clinical sample analysis with experiments using a hyperoxia-induced cell model, aiming to elucidate the role of this axis in alveolar epithelial injury and to identify new diagnostic and therapeutic targets for BPD.

Materials and Methods

Study Subjects

This study enrolled 92 neonates from Longyan First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University between January 2023 and June 2025, including 50 infants diagnosed with BPD and 42 healthy infants as controls. BPD diagnosis was confirmed by pediatricians according to the criteria established by the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD). Infants requiring oxygen supplementation at a concentration of ≥30% or continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP)/mechanical ventilation were classified as having severe BPD. Infants with inherited metabolic disorders, neonatal sepsis, major congenital malformations, perinatal asphyxia, or incomplete clinical data were excluded. At 36 weeks’ postmenstrual age (PMA), blood samples were collected from 50 BPD infants and 42 normal control infants after obtaining informed consent from their parents. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of Longyan First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University and strictly adhered to the ethical principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell Culture and Treatment

Human lung epithelium A549 cells were purchased Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China, Cat# SCSP-503). The mouse pulmonary epithelial cell line MLE-12 was purchased from Cellverse (Shanghai, China, Cat# iCell-m036). Cell cultures were maintained in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, Cat# 11965092), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Cat#10099141) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, Gibco; Cat# 15140122), at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. To simulate a hyperoxic environment, cells in good growth condition were randomly divided into two groups for intervention: Normoxia control group: cultured under normal oxygen conditions (21% O2, 5% CO2, balance N2). Hyperoxia treatment group, as in previous studies: exposed to a pre-equilibrated high-oxygen environment (85% O2, 5% CO2, balance N2) for 24 hours.20,21

Cell Transfection

The following plasmids and oligonucleotides were provided by GenePharma Co. Ltd: pcDNA3.1 empty vector (used as a negative control, NC), pcDNA3.1-UNC5B-AS1 overexpression plasmid, si-IGF1, miR-134-5p mimic, and inhibitor. Seed cells in a 24-well plate. Add 100 ng of plasmid or 50 nM of oligonucleotide (miR mimic, inhibitor, or siRNA) to each well, and transfect the cells using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# 11668019) for 48 hours.

Total RNA Isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from blood samples and cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# 15596026). RNA concentration and purity were quantified using a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# ND-2000). Subsequently, cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara Bio, Cat# RR037A). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was carried out on an ABI 7300 system (Applied Biosystems, USA) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Cat# 4309155). GAPDH served as the internal reference for UNC5B-AS1, IGF1, Bax, and Bcl-2 mRNA, while U6 was used as the internal reference for miR-134-5p. The relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

CCK-8 Assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK‑8; Dojindo Laboratories, Cat# CK04). After respective treatments, cells were seeded into 96-well plates. After 24, 48, and 72 hours of culture, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37°C for 2 hours. The absorbance of each well was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA).

Flow Cytometry for Apoptosis

Apoptosis was assessed using the FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences, Cat# 556547). After transfection, cells were treated under either hyperoxic or normoxic conditions, harvested by trypsinization, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and resuspended in 1× binding buffer at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Next, 5 μL of FITC-annexin V and 5 μL of propidium iodide (PI) were added to 100 μL of the cell suspension. The mixture was gently vortexed and incubated at room temperature (in the dark) for 15 minutes. Within 1 hour, the stained cells were analyzed using a FACS Calibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay

The potential targeting relationships between miR-134-5p and UNC5B-AS1 or IGF1 were predicted using the LncBook and miRWalk online databases. To validate these interactions in cells, fragments of UNC5B-AS1 and IGF1 containing either the wild-type or mutant miR-134-5p binding sequences were cloned into the pGL3 luciferase reporter plasmid (Promega, Cat# E1761) to construct WT and MUT reporter vectors. These recombinant vectors were then co-transfected with the miR-134-5p mimic or inhibitor into cells. After 48 hours of transfection, luciferase activity was quantified using a dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (Promega, Cat# E1910).

RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP) Assay

RIP assays were performed using a Magna RIP™ RNA‑Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore, Cat# 17‑700). Briefly, cells were lysed in RIP lysis buffer, and the resulting supernatants were incubated with magnetic beads conjugated with anti-Ago2 or anti-IgG antibodies for 4 hours. Subsequently, the enriched RNA complexes were isolated, and the levels of UNC5B-AS1, miR-134-5p and IGF1 were quantified via RT-qPCR.

ELISA

Cell culture supernatants were collected, centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C, and stored at −80°C until analysis. Concentrations of tumor necrosis factor‑alpha (TNF‑α), interleukin‑1 beta (IL‑1β), interleukin‑6 (IL‑6), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) were measured using commercial ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ protocols:

Human TNF‑α ELISA Kit (R&D Systems cat# HSTA00E), Human IL‑1β ELISA Kit (R&D Systems; Cat# DY201), Human IL‑6 ELISA Kit (R&D Systems, Cat# HS600B), Mouse TNF‑α, IL‑1β and IL‑6 ELISA kits (all from R&D Systems; Cat# MTA00B, MLB00C, M6000B, respectively), SOD ELISA Kit (Abcam, Cat# ab171738).

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Analysis

To detect intracellular ROS levels in individual cells, the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH‑DA; Sigma‑Aldrich, Cat# D6883) was used. Treated cells were stained with DCFH-DA in the dark for 30 minutes, then washed with 1× PBS to remove excess dye. Fluorescence intensity was immediately measured using a FACS Aria II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA) via the FITC channel.

Western Blot Analysis

Cellular proteins were extracted and then resolved by SDS‑polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‑PAGE) using a 10% separating gel. Following electrophoresis, the resolved proteins were electroblotted onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, Cat# IPVH00010). The membrane was subsequently probed with specific primary antibodies targeting IGF1 and GAPDH (Abin, Cat# HY095021 and Cat# HY030035, respectively), followed by incubation with the corresponding horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‑conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam, Cat# ab6712). Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate (Pierce™ ECL Western Blot Substrate, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 32106). Finally, the membranes were imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system (Tano, Cat# Tano‑4600), and the band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 software and GraphPad Prism 7. Comparisons between two groups were performed using a Student’s t-test (two-tailed). For comparisons among multiple groups (three or more), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Comparisons between two groups were made using Student’s t-test. The diagnostic value was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

UNC5B-AS1 and miR-134-5p are Dysregulated in BPD Infants

Clinical samples were collected from BPD infants and healthy controls; their baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that the UNC5B-AS1 levels was notably downregulated in the serum of BPD infants (P < 0.001, Figure 1A), with even lower expression observed in severe BPD cases (P < 0.001, Figure 1B). Conversely, the miR-134-5p levels was considerably upregulated in BPD infants (P < 0.001, Figure 1C) and showed a further increase in severe BPD (P < 0.001, Figure 1D). These expression patterns suggest a potential regulatory relationship between UNC5B‑AS1 and miR‑134‑5p in BPD pathology.

Diagnostic Value of UNC5B-AS1 and miR-134-5p for BPD

ROC curve analysis was performed to assess the diagnostic potential of UNC5B‑AS1 and miR‑134‑5p for BPD. UNC5B-AS1 levels effectively distinguished BPD infants from healthy controls (AUC = 0.891, sensitivity = 92.00%, specificity = 76.19%, Figure 2A), and also differentiated severe BPD from non‑severe cases (AUC = 0.842, sensitivity = 83.33%, specificity = 68.75%, Figure 2B). Similarly, miR-134-5p exhibited good diagnostic efficacy for BPD (AUC = 0.916, sensitivity = 80.00%, specificity = 92.86%, Figure 2C) and severe BPD (AUC = 0.814, sensitivity = 72.22%, specificity = 81.25%, Figure 2D).

UNC5B-AS1 Binds to miR-134-5p

To elucidate the functional mechanism of UNC5B-AS1, we first determined its primary subcellular localization. Nuclear‑cytoplasmic fractionation assays showed that UNC5B‑AS1 was predominantly localized in the cytoplasm (Figure 3A), suggesting its potential role as a ceRNA. Bioinformatics analysis predicted a potential binding site between UNC5B-AS1 and miR-134-5p (Figure 3B). Subsequently, DLR assays confirmed that UNC5B-AS1 directly binds to miR-134-5p (P < 0.001, Figure 3C). RIP assays further validated this direct interaction (P < 0.001, Figure 3D). Correlation analysis of clinical samples showed a statistical negative correlation between UNC5B‑AS1 and miR‑134‑5p expression (r = −0.639, P < 0.001, Figure 3E). Functionally, knockdown of UNC5B-AS1 significantly upregulated miR-134-5p expression, whereas overexpression of UNC5B-AS1 produced the opposite effect (P < 0.01, Figure 3F). These data demonstrate that UNC5B-AS1 acts as a molecular sponge for miR-134-5p, thereby negatively regulating its expression.

UNC5B-AS1 Alleviates Hyperoxia-Induced Alveolar Injury by Sponging miR-134-5p

A hyperoxia‑induced alveolar epithelial cell injury model was used to simulate the in vitro pathological process of BPD. Hyperoxia induction resulted in the downregulation of UNC5B-AS1 and upregulation of miR-134-5p. Transfection with oe-UNC5B-AS1 reversed these changes, while subsequent transfection with miR-134-5p mimic restored miR‑134‑5p levels (P < 0.01, Figure 4A and B). Functionally, overexpression of UNC5B-AS1 significantly improved cell viability (P < 0.001, Figure 4C), suppressed apoptosis (P < 0.001, Figure 4D), and modulated the expression of apoptosis-related proteins Bax and Bcl-2 (P < 0.001, Figure 4E). Importantly, co-overexpression of miR-134-5p partially reversed these protective effects of UNC5B‑AS1 (P < 0.05, Figure 4C–E). Additionally, oe-UNC5B-AS1 alleviated the release of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and oxidative stress levels, manifested as reduced ROS and increased SOD (P < 0.001, Figure 4F–H). These beneficial effects were antagonized by miR‑134‑5p overexpression (P < 0.05, Figure 4F–H).

miR-134-5p Directly Targets IGF1

To further elucidate the downstream mechanism, we confirmed that IGF1 is a direct target of miR-134-5p. Bioinformatics tools predicted a binding site between them (Figure 5A). Both DLR assays (Figure 5B) and RIP assays (Figure 5C) verified that miR-134-5p directly binds to and inhibits IGF1. In clinical BPD samples, IGF1 expression was decreased (P < 0.001, Figure 5D) and negatively correlated with miR-134-5p levels (r = −0.555, P < 0.001, Figure 5E). Furthermore, miR-134-5p negatively regulated IGF1 expression (P < 0.01, Figure 5F). These findings collectively delineate the regulatory axis from UNC5B-AS1 to miR-134-5p to IGF1.

IGF1 Reverses the Promotive Effects of miR-134-5p on Hyperoxia-Induced Alveolar Epithelial Cell Injury

Rescue experiments confirmed that IGF1 is a key downstream effector in this pathway. In the BPD cell model, IGF1 expression was downregulated; transfection with miR-134-5p inhibitor increased IGF1 levels, and this increase was attenuated in the miR-134-5p inhibitor + si-IGF1 group (P < 0.01, Figure 6A and B). Functionally, miR‑134‑5p inhibition significantly alleviated the decrease in cell viability, whereas concomitant knockdown of IGF1 blocked this protective effect (P < 0.05, Figure 6C). Consistently, the miR-134-5p inhibitor effectively reduced cell apoptosis, but this anti-apoptotic effect was significantly weakened when IGF1 was knocked down (P < 0.01, Figure 6D and E). Furthermore, inhibition of miR‑134‑5p significantly reduced the release of pro‑inflammatory factors (P < 0.001) and improved oxidative stress indicators (P < 0.001); however, all these beneficial effects were reversed by the simultaneous knockdown of IGF1 (P < 0.01, Figure 6F–H).

The UNC5B-AS1/miR-134-5p/IGF1 Axis Is Involved in Hyperoxia-Induced Damage to MLE-12 Cells

To overcome the limitations of using A549 cells, we repeated the core functional experiments in MLE-12 mouse alveolar epithelial cells. Dual-luciferase reporter assays and RIP experiments confirmed that the direct binding relationships between UNC5B-AS1 and miR-134-5p, as well as between miR-134-5p and the 3’UTR of IGF1, were identical in MLE-12 cells (P < 0.001, Figure S1A–D). Furthermore, as shown i, hyperoxia significantly downregulated UNC5B-AS1 expression and upregulated miR-134-5p expression in MLE-12 cells, consistent with observations in A549 cells (P < 0.001). Overexpression of UNC5B-AS1 reversed these changes, significantly increased cell viability (P < 0.001, Figure S2C), reduced apoptosis (P < 0.001, Figure S2D and E), and attenuated the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (P < 0.001, Figure S2F), and reduced ROS levels, while increasing SOD activity (P < 0.001, Figure S2G and H). All of these protective effects were partially abolished upon co-transfection with a miR-134-5p mimic. Furthermore, rescue experiments using a miR-134-5p inhibitor and si-IGF1 yielded identical functional results (P < 0.01, Figure S3A–G).

Discussion

Oxygen therapy is indispensable in the care of preterm infants, and oxygen itself is a crucial regulator of lung development.22 However, prolonged hyperoxia can cause pathological injury to the immature lungs, potentially leading to irreversible sequelae such as BPD.23 The pathophysiology of BPD is complex, involving multiple processes including inflammatory injury, and arrest of alveolarization and microvascular development.24,25 Although the use of pulmonary surfactant and various protective ventilation strategies has remarkably improved the survival rate of extremely preterm infants, the incidence of BPD has not correspondingly declined,26 making it a major ongoing challenge in neonatology. Against this background, this study focused on the lncRNA UNC5B-AS1, revealing for the first time its clinical significance along with its target, miR-134-5p, in BPD. We discovered that UNC5B-AS1 participates in the disease process by acting as a molecular sponge to regulate the miR-134-5p/IGF1 axis, providing new theoretical insights and potential therapeutic directions for understanding BPD pathogenesis.

Dysregulation of non-coding RNAs is i increasingly recognized as a contributor to lung diseases.27 Our finding of downregulated UNC5B-AS1 in BPD patients is consistent with initial discoveries and extends the observation of its reduction in BPD infant serum. Conversely, miR-134-5p was upregulated in BPD. More importantly, we established the diagnostic potential of both molecules, which can not only distinguish BPD patients from healthy controls but also differentiate severe from non-severe cases. These results position UNC5B-AS1 and miR-134-5p as potential non-invasive biomarkers, aligning with the current research trend of seeking early, objective biomarkers for BPD. For instance, similar to this study, lncRNAs such as MALAT1 have also been proposed as potential markers for BPD.28 Our findings add significant new members to this field.

lncRNAs and miRNAs are important ncRNA molecules that form complex regulatory networks at the post-transcriptional and epigenetic levels.29,30 The ceRNA mechanism is gradually being elucidated in the pathology of BPD. Studies have confirmed that lncRNA SNHG6 can exacerbate hyperoxia-induced alveolar epithelial cell injury by sponging miR-335;31 similarly, lncRNA CASC2 has been shown to participate in BPD-associated inflammatory response and apoptosis by regulating the miR-194-5p axis.32 In addition to lncRNAs, recent studies have shown that miRNAs in circulating exosomes can predict and prevent severe BPD in extremely preterm infants,33 while extracellular vesicles derived from mesenchymal stem cells carrying miR-203a-3p can alleviate BPD-associated apoptosis and inflammation.34 Collectively, these findings suggest that ceRNA networks, as well as individual microRNAs, significantly influence BPD-related pathological processes, including alveolar arrest, inflammation, oxidative stress, and vascular dysregulation. Our data demonstrate that UNC5B-AS1 sponges miR-134-5p, consistent with the established ceRNA paradigm. The protective effect of UNC5B-AS1 overexpression in our in vitro model is particularly noteworthy. Hyperoxia-induced alveolar epithelial cell injury is a fundamental model for studying BPD.35 We demonstrated that UNC5B-AS1 ameliorates hyperoxia-induced cytotoxicity, inflammation, and oxidative stress, directly linking its function to key pathological features of BPD. miR-134-5p is a nodal molecule playing important roles in various chronic lung diseases; for example, it has been reported to participate in the pathology of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) by regulating airway mucus hypersecretion.36,37 Crucially, restoring miR-134-5p expression effectively reversed the protective effects conferred by UNC5B-AS1 overexpression, a finding consistent with the report by Lan et al that a miR-134-5p inhibitor ameliorates BPD.18 This result provides compelling functional evidence for the specificity of the proposed ceRNA regulatory axis.

Furthermore, we delineated the downstream effector of this axis by identifying IGF1 as a direct target of miR-134-5p. IGF1 is a well-characterized growth factor essential for lung development and cellular repair.19,38 Deficiency in IGF1 signaling has been reported as a significant factor contributing to impaired alveolar development in humans and disrupted lung structure in animal models.39 Our research found that downregulation of UNC5B-AS1 in BPD leads to accumulation of miR-134-5p, which in turn suppresses IGF1 expression, ultimately resulting in cellular injury. In rescue experiments, the benefits of miR-134-5p inhibition were abolished by IGF1 knockdown, definitively positioning IGF1 as the critical functional endpoint of this pathway. We demonstrate that UNC5B-AS1 attenuates hyperoxia-induced cytotoxicity, inflammation, and oxidative stress, thereby directly linking its function to key pathological features of BPD. Mechanistically, downregulation of UNC5B-AS1 leads to elevated levels of miR-134-5p, which in turn inhibits IGF1. IGF1 is essential for the proliferation, differentiation, and survival of alveolar epithelial cells;40 its absence impairs alveolar septation and microvascular maturation,41 ultimately resulting in alveolar simplification and interstitial remodeling (ie, physical scarring), which are characteristic features of the BPD lung. Therefore, this molecular cascade identified in our in vitro model provides a plausible explanation for the transition from cellular dysfunction to the histopathological features of BPD in pediatric patients.

This study has certain limitations, and there are corresponding strategies that can be employed to address these issues in future research. First, regarding the experimental model, our in vitro experiments primarily relied on A549 cells (an adult-derived cancer cell line that cannot fully replicate the characteristics of neonatal alveolar epithelial cells). Although we performed cross-validation in MLE-12 cells (a normal mouse alveolar epithelial cell line), the lack of in vivo validation remains a significant limitation. To address this issue, we plan to establish a neonatal mouse hyperoxia model in the future to directly confirm the physiological relevance of the UNC5B-AS1/miR-134-5p/IGF1 axis by evaluating pulmonary histopathology and molecular changes. Second, regarding clinical samples, we used serum rather than lung-specific samples (eg, tracheal aspirates or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid), and all samples were obtained from a single center, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Future multicenter studies incorporating tracheal aspirates from intubated preterm infants are needed to validate the expression of this axis in the lung and establish its diagnostic reliability across different populations. Third, our functional analysis was limited to alveolar epithelial cells and did not explore the role of this axis in other lung cell types (such as fibroblasts and macrophages). Future studies should employ co-culture systems or cell-type-specific knockdown methods to extend the scope of investigation to these cell types, thereby providing a comprehensive understanding of the cell-specific roles of this ceRNA network in the pathogenesis of BPD.

Conclusion

In summary, this study found that in the serum of preterm infants with BPD, the expression of UNC5B-AS1 was significantly reduced, while that of miR-134-5p was significantly increased, both showing promising diagnostic potential (AUC of 0.891 and 0.916, respectively), which warrants further validation in larger cohorts. Mechanistically, UNC5B-AS1 exerts its effects by binding to miR-134-5p, thereby lifting the suppression of IGF1 and preventing hyperoxia-induced alveolar epithelial cell damage. While IGF1 has previously been implicated in BPD, our study is the first to elucidate the upstream UNC5B-AS1/miR-134-5p regulatory axis governing IGF1 expression in this context. These findings provide new mechanistic insights into potential serum-based biomarkers (particularly miR-134-5p) and offer new avenues for the early diagnosis and targeted treatment of BPD.
