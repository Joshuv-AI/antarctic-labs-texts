Temporal trends in weight-based disparities in physical fitness of children: 30-years of continuous surveillance through Slovenian national system
Authors: Antonio Martinko, Rok Blagus, Gregor Jurak, Gregor Starc, Maroje Sorić
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity in childhood has deleterious consequences for health while improving physical fitness can significantly reduce health risks related to high body mass index. We aimed to examine the evolution of disparities in physical fitness based on weight status among 7–15-year-olds in Slovenia between 1989 and 2019 and compare these trends across sex, age and socioeconomic status.MethodsWe used annual data collected within the SLOfit monitoring system in the period between 1989 and 2019, totalling 4,256,930 participants (about 137 000 per year). We examined cardiorespiratory fitness (600-m run test), muscular fitness (60-s sit-ups, bent arm hang, and standing broad jump test) and skill-related fitness (backwards obstacle course, 60-m dash, arm plate tapping). We grouped children according to the IOTF cut-offs for BMI to those living with normal weight or excess weight and estimated changes in physical fitness over time by fitting quantile regression models separately by sex and age group, and then using segmented regression to identify the patterns of trends over time.ResultsWeight-based disparities in physical fitness were large in 1989 and have further increased by 2019. The increase in disparities was generally around 5 percentiles larger in boys, and 10–15-year-olds compared to younger children. It was particularly pronounced for body core strength and speed in boys (up to 15 and 19 percentiles, respectively) and upper body strength and speed among girls (up to 13 percentiles). Most of the increase in disparities in health-related fitness accumulated during the 2010s, when the fitness of children generally improved, but much less so in children living with excess weight.ConclusionsDespite recent improvements in population fitness levels, children with excess weight seem to be left behind, which adds to existing health inequalities. Our results should encourage policymakers to redesign policies aimed at promoting physical activity and enhancing physical fitness to make them more equitable and ultimately lead to reducing inequalities in fitness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20372-5.Keywords: Health inequalities, Secular changes, Obesity, Socioeconomic status

Background

The occurrence of childhood obesity has escalated on a global scale, with more than an eightfold increase in prevalence from 1975 to 2016 and estimates that there are more than 330 million children and adolescents aged 5–19 with either overweight or obesity in 2016 [1]. Childhood obesity is linked to serious lifelong health consequences, affecting multiple systems and increasing the risk of many non-communicable diseases [2]. It also has psychological and social impacts, such as stigmatization, discrimination, increased financial burdens on families, diversion of community resources, and higher healthcare expenses [3].

Physical fitness has been identified as the leading non-invasive indicator of health in children [4]. High levels of fitness during childhood and adolescence have been linked to future cardiovascular and metabolic health benefits [5, 6]. Children who maintain high levels of physical fitness are less likely to develop obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease [4, 7, 8], symptoms of anxiety and depression [9, 10] while maintaining higher academic performance and better cognitive function [11, 12].

Childhood obesity is often linked to lower fitness levels, yet some children with obesity exhibit high fitness. Research on the “fat but fit” phenomenon indicates that improved fitness can mitigate certain adverse effects of childhood obesity [13–16], including better cardiometabolic health [13, 15, 16] and overall well-being [17]. Moreover, long-term increases in cardiorespiratory fitness may significantly reduce obesity-related mortality risk, related to high BMI [18], supporting a weight-neutral approach to obesity management [19].

Global trends in the fitness of children are well known and data show a decline in fitness up until the 2010s, after which stagnation was observed in most fitness components [20]. Still, the evidence is typically based on studies that had low data granularity, have assessed one or very few physical fitness components, and were mostly based in high-income countries [20–23]. On the other hand, there is very little data on fitness trends in children living with excess weight especially data that would allow examining the evolution of weight-based disparities over a long period [24–28]. Generally, across several available studies, children and adolescents with overweight and obesity have experienced larger decrements in fitness in the decades either before or after the 2000s when compared with their peers with normal weight [24, 29–33], with some conflicting results reported [26]. That said, fitness trends of children and adolescents from only several high-income countries were reported recently [25–28, 34, 35]. At the same time, both fitness levels and adiposity can vary significantly with socioeconomic position both across and within countries [34, 35], with less favourable trends in fitness noted in children of lower socioeconomic status (SES) [36, 37]. The only study that has examined physical fitness trends of children considering both weight status and SES [24] has reported improvements in the fitness of children with excess weight in undeveloped areas and a simultaneous decline in areas with high urbanisation over 30 years in China [24].

Systematic fitness surveillance in childhood can identify trends and support early interventions to prevent adverse health outcomes [38]. While currently, only a few countries benefit from such systems [39], Slovenia has been conducting near-census level fitness monitoring (including around 95% of all primary-school children – ISCED 1 and 2) since the late 1980s [40]. At the same time, Slovenia ranks among the top 5 European countries concerning children’s physical fitness [41]. Robust physical fitness of Slovenian children largely stems from a strong emphasis on physical education in the school system. The national curriculum prioritizes physical literacy, allocating three weekly hours to the subject in general. While generalist teachers instruct younger students, specialized PE teachers with advanced 5-year university education take over in upper grades. Smaller, sex-segregated classes (up to 20 students) in these later years allow for tailored instruction. This has positioned Slovenia among the countries with the highest grades for overall physical activity and physical fitness of children and adolescents globally [39].

Nonetheless, Slovenia too has been grappling with the challenges of high-income nations in addressing detrimental lifestyle changes of its population [42]. During the last three decades, Slovenia transitioned from a socialist to a market economy, joined the European Union and witnessed rapid economic development. Similar to other countries in transition, a dramatic increase in overweight and obesity among Slovenian children was recorded, but since 2010 until the COVID-19 pandemic, the prevalence of obesity has been steadily declining [43]. At the same time, initial declines in cardiorespiratory fitness also recently reversed, muscular fitness showed mostly negative trends, while skill-related physical fitness showed varying trends among Slovenian children and adolescents [42, 44]. Increasing heterogeneity in fitness levels across the last 30 years was also noted, suggesting a growing disparity in terms of physical fitness [42, 45].

This study addresses the paucity of research on disparities in childhood physical fitness by weight status, a critical gap identified in international research priorities [38]. Using near-census data from the SLOfit system, we examined temporal trends in weight-based disparities in physical fitness among 7–15-year-olds from 1989 to 2019. Our analysis identified changepoints in these trends and assessed their magnitude, further exploring regional disparities predicated on area-based SES.

Methods

Study design, participants, and measurements

In this study, we used data on physical fitness collected within the SLOfit monitoring system (detailed description is in the Additional file 6, under Physical fitness test description subsection) [40]. The fitness test battery, including the three anthropometric measurements and eight fitness tests, has not changed since the system’s introduction in the school year 1986/87. To reduce the potential seasonal bias, all children are tested every year in April. Out of the employed battery of tests within SLOfit, we address health-related physical fitness, through measurements of body composition including BMI and triceps skinfold thickness, as well as cardiorespiratory fitness using the 600-m run test, and muscular fitness using the 60-s sit-ups, bent arm hang, and standing broad jump tests. We assessed skill-related fitness based on the results of the backwards obstacle course, 60-m dash, and arm plate tapping tests to evaluate gross motor coordination, speed, and neuromuscular coordination, respectively. More details on the individual tests and the overall methodology of testing are available in Additional file 6, under SLOfit test battery subsection. We based our analyses on data collected yearly in the period between 1989 and 2019 totalling 4,256,930 participants aged 7–15 years. This amounts to more than 135,000 individuals per year over 31 years. The exact number of observations divided by sex, age, weight status and SES for all the years included in the study period is presented in Additional file 1 under Counts and trends (Table S1.1 and Table S1.2). Children living with normal and excess weight were grouped according to the extended, age and sex-specific, International Obesity Task Force cut-off points [46] defining excess weight as living with either overweight or obesity. Biologically implausible values for children’s height, weight, and BMI were addressed using The AnthroPlus package [47] to flag and exclude values based on criteria: weight-for-age z-scores < -6.0 or > 5.0, height-for-age z-scores > 6.0, and BMI-for-age z-scores > 6.0. As a measure of area-based SES, we used an aggregated index of development, i.e., the municipal development index (MDI) [48], calculated yearly by the Slovenian Statistical Office, and averaged over a period between 2009 and 2019 because no data were available prior to 2009. MDI takes into account economic factors (gross domestic product, income tax), social indicators (ageing, unemployment, etc.), as well as cultural aspects (cultural monuments), and features relating to nature [48]. MDI is a relative number, a ratio between the value of the arithmetic means of the standardised values of the indicators in the municipality and the value of the arithmetic mean of the standardised values of the indicators in the country (i.e., the coefficient of the average Slovenian MDI of 1.00). Higher values correspond to a more developed municipality. Individual average MDI values per region and municipality can be seen in Additional file 3 under Socioeconomic status – Tables S3.1, S3.2 and Figure S3.1 As heterogeneity in MDI was low, we opted to contrast the most developed (i.e., Osrednjeslovenska - average MDI = 1.19) and the least developed region (i.e., Pomurska - average MDI = 0.80). In 2019, about 28.1% and 5.3% of the participants lived in Osrednjeslovenska and Pomurska region, respectively. The number of observations across all regions stratified by year is shown in Additional file 1 under Counts and trends (Table S1.3 and Table S1.4). To test the robustness of the conclusions related to area-based development, we also conducted sensitivity analyses by contrasting the two most developed regions (i.e., Osrednjeslovenska and Gorenjska) to the two least developed regions (Pomurska and Podravska).

Statistical analysis

We used previously calculated percentile values for each fitness test [42]. First, we fitted quantile regression models separately by sex and age group (3 age groups: 7–9 years – middle childhood in both sexes; 10–12 years – transition into puberty and mid-puberty for most girls and transition into puberty for most boys; and 13–15 years – later adolescence for most girls and the interval of the growth spurt for most boys). This way of defining age groups represents various developmental stages commonly used in studies of school children and adolescents [49]. Quantile regression is a statistical method that extends traditional linear regression by estimating the relationships between variables across different quantiles (percentiles) of the response variable distribution, rather than just the mean. This allowed us to explore how changes in time affect the distribution of the physical fitness test scores across their entire range. When fitting the quantile regression models to estimate the joinpoints, we modelled the median from each test as a dependent variable, while we included the year as a continuous independent variable, but also weight status, regional MDI and the interaction between year and weight status as independent variables. We then fed the results of these models to segmented regression to identify the joinpoints, i.e., the moment of the change in the trend of physical fitness over time. We express the magnitude of the increase or decrease by estimating the annual percent change (APC) during a specific period. In addition, we report the average annual percent change (AAPC) over the whole period. AAPC is based on the segmented analysis and denotes a quantitative summary measure of trends that may not be constant over a certain preselected period, and it does so by connecting several different line segments at joinpoints. To calculate it, the weighted average of the APCs from the joinpoint model is computed, using weights equal to the length of the APC interval [50]. Joinpoints and AAPCs are related in a sense that joinpoints inform about a year where a statistically significant change in trends occurred, each of these trends is described by an APC and related APCs indicate the accompanied magnitude of change. We identified the segmented model with the optimal number of joinpoints (chosen between the models with 0, 1, 2, and 3 joinpoints) based on the Akaike information criterion (AIC). To compare the trends in fitness of children living with excess weight to those of children living with normal weight we report changes in percentile points for a given fitness component and respective 95% confidence interval (CI) in each of the groups. Percentile points are age- and sex-specific and derived from national standards, constructed based on measurements of 95% of Slovenian children in the 1989–2019 period [42]. This metric enables direct comparison between different fitness components, and weight, sex, and age groups. A change of 1 percentile point for a given group, and a given fitness component, indicates that the rank in the standard population changed by 1% point. Positive values of estimated APCs and AAPCs imply improvements, while negative values indicate worsening. Finally, we examined the differences in trends between the most developed and least developed regions. Here, we fitted the quantile regression models similarly to the main analyses while including only children living in the most and least developed regions and stratified the analyses by region in the segmented regression models.

We performed all statistical analyses using R version 4.3.0 [51], with data processing and visualizations created within tidyverse [52], and by using the patchwork package [53]. We used packages quantreg [54] and segmented [55] for fitting quantile regression and segmented regression models, respectively, as well as for calculating APC and AAPC.

Health-related physical fitness

In 1989, weight-based disparities (i.e., the difference between children with normal weight and excess weight) were large for all health-related fitness components across age and sex groups. The gap was particularly prominent for upper body strength and cardiorespiratory fitness, with differences totalling around 30–35 and 20–25 percentile points, respectively. At the same time, for body core strength, the gap amounted to only 8–14 percentile points depending on the age and sex group.

From 1989 to 2019, the difference in BMI between children with normal weight and children with excess weight widened in almost all age and sex groups, but only trivially (by around 1 percentile point in most groups). The opposite holds for TSF, and the largest decrease in the gap was seen in the 7–9-year-old boys and 10–12-year-old girls with TSF increasing by around 4 percentile points more in children with normal weight over 30 years (Fig. 1).

Averaged over the entire study period cardiorespiratory fitness decreased in all age, sex, and weight groups, except for 13–15-year-old boys with normal weight (AAPC=-0.02; 95%CI=-0.04 to 0.01). Weight-based disparities widened in all age and sex groups, as the decline in cardiorespiratory fitness over 30 years was 2–5 times larger for children with excess weight when compared to their peers with normal weight (Fig. 1). The increase in disparities was twice as large in boys compared to girls and amounted to around 10 percentiles vs. around 5 percentiles, respectively. The increase in disparities was also slightly larger in the oldest age group, especially among girls.

Trends in muscular fitness from 1989 to 2019 diverged depending on the body part assessed. While lower body strength (measured via SBJ) dropped sharply in all groups, body core strength (i.e., the number of sit-ups in 60s) increased. Upper body strength decreased in all children with excess weight as well as in 7–12-year-old boys with normal weight and increased in all girls and 13–15-year-old boys with normal weight. Weight-based disparities increased in all groups for all dimensions of muscular fitness, with no notable sex differences except for lower body strength where the gap between weight groups increased much more for boys than for girls (Fig. 1). The rise in disparities was generally the largest for body core strength, with the improvement over 30 years being 5–15 percentile points larger in children with normal weight across sex and age groups.

Skill-related fitness

In 1989, weight-based disparities were large for gross motor coordination and speed but trivial for neuromuscular coordination (Fig. 1). The gap between weight-based groups in gross motor coordination was similar across age groups, but more prominent in boys than in girls (around 25 percentile points vs. around 20 percentile points). At the same time, the gap in speed did not differ between sexes and amounted to around 20–25 percentile points.

Weight-based disparities increased for all fitness components in all age and sex groups, although the increase was trivial for neuromuscular coordination, especially among girls (Fig. 1). The increase in the gap between weight groups was the largest for speed, being consistently larger in boys than girls and peaking at 19 percentile point increase among 13–15-year-old boys. On the other hand, for gross motor coordination weight-based differences in trends were similar in both sexes and amounted to 8–11 percentile points increase in the gap over 30 years.

Health-related physical fitness

BMI increased during the 1990s and 2000s in all groups, and then decreased in the 2010s in children with normal weight, while not changing much in children with excess weight. Most increase in TSF occurred during the 2000s and was much more prominent in children with normal weight, which contributed strongly to the overall decrease of weight-based disparities (Fig. 2).

Cardiorespiratory fitness decreased over the first 10 years, remained stable during the 2000s and then improved from around 2010 to 2019. This improvement in the last 10 years was typically lower or absent in children with excess weight compared to their peers. Consequently, the period between 2005 and 2010 and 2019 contributed the most to the increasing inequalities in cardiorespiratory fitness in both sexes and in all age groups.

Body core strength improved continuously up to 2014/2015 in most groups, with the largest increases being noted during the first 5–10 years, and with the consistently larger increase in children with normal weight leading to the widening of the gap between weight groups from 1989 to 2015. During the last time interval (4–5 years for most groups) body core strength decreased in most groups and the weight-based gap increased further in 10–12-year-old boys and girls and 7–9-year-old girls, but contracted among 13–15-year-old boys. Upper body strength decreased over the whole period in children with excess weight, but only between 1997 and 2008 in children with normal weight. The increase seen in children with normal weight after around 2008, together with a simultaneous decrease in children with excess weight was the strongest contributor to increasing weight-based disparities in upper body strength. Finally, for lower body strength a very brief increase over the first 2–3 years was seen in some groups, followed by about 25 years of continued decrease in all groups. The decrease slowed down or stopped during the last 5–10 years in most groups, while an increase was noted in 13–15-year-old girls with both excess and normal weight. Most of the increase in disparities was generated from 1989 to 2000 among 7–12-year-old boys and around the period 1995–2010 in their older peers. In girls, the increase in disparities was uniform across the studied period.

Skill-related fitness

Gross motor coordination showed continuous improvement in the youngest age groups, a sharp improvement over the first 5–6 years, followed by a slight but continuous deterioration in older age groups in boys, and a sharp improvement over the first 5–6 years, followed by a slight deterioration in the next 15 years and another period of improvement in the last 10 years in older age groups in girls. Weight-based disparities increased the most during the initial period, with notably smaller improvement in children with excess weight, and continued increasing in the last period, although to a lesser extent, with a slightly smaller improvement and lack of improvement in girls and boys with excess weight, respectively.

Speed decreased during the first 5 years in the youngest children and then increased over the next 25 years. In the older children speed remained fairly stable in children with excess weight throughout the 30 years and improved continuously after 1995 in their peers with normal weight. Weight-based disparities mostly increased in the period from the early 2000s to mid-2010s due to a notable improvement in children with normal weight and concomitant stagnation in children with excess weight.

Pattern of trends in neuromuscular coordination was complex, but uniform across age and sex groups. Initial improvement over the first 5–6 years was followed by a decrease in the next 5 years and another period of improvement during the last 20 years. Weight-based disparities were not evident in 1989, but they emerged over the first half of the 1990s and remained fairly stable by 2019.

Effect of area-based socioeconomic status on patterns of weight-based disparities in physical fitness

Body composition and cardiorespiratory fitness showed less favourable trends in low SES region, while the opposite was true for body core and lower body strength. Weight-based disparities in most fitness components increased more in the less developed region for at least some of the age and sex groups. This was most consistent for body core strength (7–12-year-old boys and girls), lower body strength (7–9-year-old boys and girls and 13–15-year-old girls), speed (7–12-year-old boys and girls) and neuromuscular coordination (10–12-year-old boys and 10–15-year-old girls) and was mostly due to less favourable trends in children with excess weight in these areas during the 2010s. Disparities in body composition and upper body strength increased to a similar extent in both high and low development region (Fig. 5). Lastly, disparities in cardiorespiratory fitness among 10–12-year-old boys and 13–15-year-old girls increased only in more developed region, while not changing much in less developed region. This increase in disparities in more developed areas predominantly happened during the 2010s when the improvement of cardiorespiratory fitness seen only in developed areas was more pronounced in children with normal weight.

Discussion

We examined the trends in physical fitness of children living with excess weight in Slovenia across more than 3 decades of systematic surveillance and found that weight-based disparities in physical fitness have mostly increased. The increase in disparities was generally larger in boys than in girls, and older age groups compared to the youngest children. The pattern of change in weight-based disparities was dependent on the fitness component, but most of the increase in disparities in health-related fitness accumulated during the 2010s, while the 1990s were predominantly responsible for the increase in disparities for skill-related fitness. During these periods fitness improved overall, but much less, or not at all, in children living with excess weight. Finally, we found that weight-based disparities in muscular and skill-related fitness grew more in the least developed region, while the opposite was true for cardiorespiratory fitness.

Observed trends in our study align with global and European trends over the last three decades, an initial decline followed by a mild to moderate reversal during the last decade [20–22]. At the same time, we observed growing disparities in fitness between children with excess weight and their peers with normal weight. Available data from previous studies also point to a growing divide between fit and unfit children as well as between those with normal weight and those with excess weight [24–26, 28]. Notably, there is limited data available regarding fitness trends among children with excess weight, but most existing evidence points towards larger decrements in health-related fitness components, particularly cardiorespiratory fitness, among children and adolescents with excess weight compared to those with normal weight [24, 29–33]. However, studies from Switzerland and the UK reported a decrease in disparities in the 2010s, indicating the need for further investigation and systematic data collection in this area [26, 28]. Growing disparities in skill-related fitness noted in our study are in line with existing evidence [56] and might also have clinical relevance as these fitness components have been shown to promote physical activity participation in children [57]. It has been demonstrated that exercise and physical activity interventions can assist children with excess weight to enhance their motor competence compared to those with normal weight [58]. Hence, future physical activity policies should focus on children with excess weight, particularly those who are not part of a sports club, to improve their motor skills and encourage regular physical activity [56].

In terms of trends in overall physical fitness, relatively small sex and age-specific differences are reported in the literature, with everything from positive, and negative to neutral results across different age and sex groups [20–22]. Our investigation into the disparities in fitness trends of various age and sex groups revealed a complex scenario, with notable differences across fitness components. Although changes in disparities in body composition and neuromuscular coordination showed little sex and age differences disparities for most other fitness components generally increased more in boys and adolescents compared to girls and younger children. The larger increase in disparities among boys and adolescents could be attributed to several factors. Boys, particularly during adolescence, often experience more rapid physical development and greater demands for physical performance, which may exacerbate the impact of excess weight on fitness outcomes. Additionally, societal expectations and engagement in physical activities may differ by sex, with boys potentially facing more pressure to excel in physically demanding tasks, thus magnifying the effects of weight-based disparities. Age-related changes in body composition may be associated with the stage of maturation with sex differences in the timing and magnitude of these changes. The more significant increase in disparities among adolescents may also reflect the cumulative impact of unhealthy lifestyle behaviours that become more entrenched as children age.

The widening of weight-based disparities in physical fitness was most pronounced during periods of population-wide improvements, particularly among boys and adolescents. Related to the patterns of trends over the last 3 decades, our study revealed that there was an initial increase in BMI during the 1990s and 2000s, followed by a decrease in the 2010s among children with normal weight. Cardiorespiratory fitness initially decreased, stabilized, and then improved after 2010, predominantly in children with normal weight, widening the gap between weight groups. Skill-related fitness showed initial improvements, intermittent declines, and subsequent improvements, with disparities emerging in the early 1990s and remaining stable or slightly decreasing by 2019. These patterns reflect a consistent trend: when overall fitness improved, children with excess weight saw much smaller or negligible gains, exacerbating disparities. During the 1990s Slovenia encountered challenges similar to other transitional economies, particularly in addressing lifestyle changes that have emerged over recent decades. Slovenia declared its independence in June 1991 after a long process of working towards economic and political independence. The country had to restructure its economy and trade relationships to integrate with Western Europe after breaking away from its former partners in the Balkans and Eastern Europe. Slovenia was successful in achieving economic recovery and has become an integrated part of the Western European market [59]. Notably, the quantity of calorie-dense foods consumed doubled between the 1980s and 1990s by 2004. This coincided with an increase in sedentary behaviour, mostly because of the widespread adoption of screen technologies. Confirming this is the fact that the proportion of households with cable or Internet Protocol television rose from 11% in the late 1980s to 90% by 2008, and Internet access expanded from 1% of households in 1995 to over 70% by 2011. Concurrently, the ownership of bicycles within households decreased from 68.5% in 1990 to 60.4% by 2000. These unfavourable lifestyle trends paralleled a rise in overweight and obesity rates among Slovene children, a problem that went largely unaddressed until the late 2000s. It was only after the introduction of several nationwide initiatives around 2010, focused on enhancing physical activity and nutrition within the educational system that a decline in overweight and obesity among Slovene children was observed. One of the pivotal policies was an effective school-based national physical activity programme “Healthy Lifestyle” [60], accompanied by an aggressive media campaign about proper diet and media consumption that might have contributed to the improvements in fitness witnessed during the 2010s in this study and earlier studies from Slovenia [42, 61]. Still, this study showed that the benefits of these policies have not been equally evident in children with excess weight, especially for cardiorespiratory fitness, upper body strength, and speed. This urges for modified physical activity policies that should be designed in a way to decrease inequalities in fitness.

It is generally reported that children and adolescents from high SES families tend to have higher mean fitness values and are less likely to have poor fitness [62]. Furthermore, individuals with lower SES tended to have reduced motivation to engage in healthy behaviours and a higher likelihood of experiencing negative health outcomes related to metabolic and cardiovascular disease risk factors when compared to those with higher SES [62]. To that end, we have seen that more developed regions had better health-related physical fitness outcomes, although children from the least developed region generally performed better in muscular fitness and skill-related fitness. Since the fitness level of children seems to be determined partly by SES, it is important to note that inequalities of income in Slovenia, assessed by the Gini index, have been decreasing during the study period [63]. This probably resulted in similar trends of fitness changes between the most and the least socioeconomic developed regions. Still, for at least some of the age and sex groups, we have found that weight-based disparities in most fitness components increased more in the less developed region. According to a recent global study, the benefits of living in cities for growth and development have decreased in the 21st century in many parts of the world, including Central and Eastern Europe [64]. These findings collectively underscore the need for policies that strive to reduce socioeconomic inequalities and point to a need for a stronger focus on special physical activity programmes in least developed regions. Still, given all the limitations of SES-based analyses in our study, further work that will include SES indicators at the individual level is needed to obtain more robust estimates of the effect of SES on trends in weight-based disparities in children’s physical fitness.

We would like to note several strengths of our study. Firstly, through SLOfit, fitness was systematically assessed yearly over more than 3 decades by trained personnel, i.e., physical education teachers, while data were checked and cleaned at several time points providing trust in the collected data and adding to the robustness of the conclusions. Furthermore, this enabled a near-census sample of more than 135,000 individuals per year from all regions of Slovenia. Thirdly, along with examining children of different ages and sex, we also considered different weight status groups and explored trends across communities characterised by different levels of economic development, features that were previously rarely studied.

Our study was not without limitations. First, we did not have information on various factors that might have influenced disparities in physical fitness based on weight, such as changes in physical activity, diet, sleep, or sedentary behaviour. Second, we didn’t manage to fully examine the individual’s pubertal status and maturation tempo which could have had an impact on the outcomes. Still, we separately assessed 7–9-year-olds in our study where pubertal status shouldn’t have impacted the results, lessening the impact of this limitation. Third, we examined trends in average fitness across groups and did not attempt to quantify trends in the variability of physical fitness which could provide additional insights into the inequalities between the fit and unfit children. Future research should at least include some measure of asymmetry (such as skewness) or variability (such as standard deviations). Fourth, we relied on normative standards and not raw values to estimate the disparities to be able to compare the magnitude of disparities between age and sex groups, making it difficult to judge the clinical relevance of our findings. Fifth, when relating trends in disparities to SES, we relied on an area-based measure of SES across regions, while we had no individual-level data. In addition, we included only two regions with the highest and lowest developmental index while excluding most of the population. We considered only two regions because the heterogeneity of the developmental index was very low, but these two regions stood out at each end, thereby increasing the resolution. Sixth, we chose to restrict the analyses to the period until 2019, the year when in December an outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic started and caused unprecedented and abrupt changes in children’s fitness levels. Therefore, our study ignores trends after 2019 although presently those results could be of particular importance. Still, we reported these trends in a recent analysis [65] and felt it would be inappropriate to include them here. Finally, in terms of comparability of our results with other studies, it is important to note that differences in trends between children included in studies conducted in Slovenia and those from other studies might be related to the specifics of the tests used, which varied between countries and were not always consistent between boys and girls [22].

Conclusion

Our findings add the most recent evidence on trends in weight-based disparities in physical fitness of children living with excess or normal weight. We observed a widening gap in physical fitness between children with excess weight compared to those with normal weight which was more pronounced in boys and children older than 10 years. Moreover, disparities in most fitness components have increased more in less developed communities. Consequently, despite recent improvement in population fitness levels, children with excess weight seem to be left behind which adds to health inequalities and could translate to even larger health inequities when these children grow up. Reducing inequalities in physical fitness should therefore be given a higher priority in the public health arena. Our results should encourage policymakers to develop and implement initiatives aimed at enhancing physical fitness, literacy, activity, and health status overall, but especially in children living with excess weight.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Until 1996, SLOfit Students measurements were mandatory for all children and youth, after which the Personal Data Protection Act was passed, informed parental consent was introduced and in 1999 students’ written consent was incorporated. Although now voluntary, participation rates remained high, over 95% and 75% in the primary (6-to 14-year-olds; ISCED 1 and 2) and upper secondary school population (15-to 19-year-olds; ISCED 3), respectively. All Slovenian primary and upper secondary schools are required by law to monitor the students’ somatic and motor development. The option to have their data centrally analysed and included in the national database must be offered to all students. Parents or students aged 15 and above can revoke consent for personal data in SLOfit at any time via the app or SLOfit school administrator, after which the data will be anonymized within 15 days. They have continuous access to their data, and they can even grant access to their fitness reports to everybody via the special My SLOfit application. Data stored in the My SLOfit application is permanently stored, while other data are automatically pseudonymised a year after the individual ends their education. Pseudonymised data is used for research per the Commission on Ethical Issues in Sport agreement No. 6-2019-539. The National Medical Ethics Committee of the Republic of Slovenia (ID 102/03/15) reviewed and approved this study that involved human participants, following the Declaration of Helsinki. As previously stated, the legal guardian/next of kin provided written informed consent for children to participate in the SLOfit assessment. This means that the present analyses are secondary analyses of only a part of the data already collected through the SLOfit assessments. Hence, we followed all relevant guidelines and regulations while more details on how all the data were treated can be found elsewhere [47].
