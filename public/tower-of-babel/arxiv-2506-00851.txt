Dynamical Properties of Dense Associative Memory
Kazushi Mimura, Jun’ichi Takeuchi, Yuto Sumikawa, Yoshiyuki Kabashima, Anthony C. C. Coolen

Abstract
The dense associative memory is one of the basic modern Hopfield networks and can store large numbers of memory patterns. While the stationary state storage capacity has been investigated so far, its dynamical properties have not been discussed. In this paper, we analyze the dynamics by means of an exact approach based on generating functional analysis. It allows us to investigate convergence properties as well as the size of the attraction basins. We also analyze the stationary state of the updating rule.



1
Introduction

1.1
Background

Dense associative memory
[
1
]
, a model for storing binary patterns, was proposed and shown to significantly improve the storage capacity of the traditional Hopfield model
[
2
]
. While it can be regarded as a rediscovery of the many-body Hopfield model
[
3
,
4
]
, it exhibits slightly different properties. On the other hand, extensions of dense associative memory, such as the Hopfield layer, have been actively developed to enable dense associative memory to store real-valued patterns
[
5
,
6
]
. Hopfield models with such large memory capacities, including these variants, are referred to as modern Hopfield networks, which have gained increasing attention and have even inspired Transformer architectures
[
7
]
.

The equilibrium properties of the Hopfield layer have been analytically studied, including evaluations of its storage capacity
[
8
]
. Since the Hopfield layer can reach a near-equilibrium state in almost a single update step, its dynamical properties have not been considered a significant issue. In contrast, dense associative memory, like the traditional Hopfield model, requires some iterative updates to reach a stationary state. However, its dynamical behavior has not been investigated so far. As a result, fundamental aspects such as the attraction basin, namely, how far from a stored pattern can the initial state be for the system to still successfully recall it, still remain unclear.

While the dynamical properties of dense associative memory have not been investigated, those of the traditional Hopfield model have been extensively analyzed. In this paper, we analyze the dynamical properties of dense associative memory using generating functional analysis, an asymptotic theory in the large-system limit, which has been widely used in those studies.

1.2
Contributions

Until now, studies on dense associative memory have focused only on its equilibrium properties. In this paper, we analyze its dynamical properties using the asymptotic analytical method known as generating functional analysis (GFA). GFA provides exact solutions in the large-system limit even if the system exhibits complex dependence on past states. Although the result of the dynamics obtained by GFA can treat such a complex dependence, computationally expensive numerical analysis is generally required. We show some important properties such as the storage capacity and the attraction basin from the update rule, which had not been fully understood before. In this paper, instead of numerically solving the exact GFA equations, we apply an approximation to the exact solution and evaluate the resulting approximate solution to obtain the storage capacity and the attraction basin. Under some mild assumptions, we also investigate the stationary state.

1.3
Related Works

Gardner and Abbott independently introduced a Hopfield model with many-body interactions, which is essentially equivalent to dense associative memory, and analyzed its equilibrium properties using the replica method to evaluate its storage capacity. The difference between their models and the dense associative memory proposed by Krotov and Hopfield lies in the presence or absence of self-coupling terms. While this difference does not affect the order of the storage capacity, it does influence the constant factor. Additionally, Lucibello and Mézard analyzed the equilibrium properties of the Hopfield layer using the replica method and obtained its storage capacity
[
8
]
. So far, no analysis of the dynamical behavior of the modern Hopfield model has been reported. On the other hand, there has been extensive research on the dynamics of the traditional Hopfield models. For example, the papers
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
and our previous papers provide exact analysis based on GFA.

2
Preliminalies

2.1
Dense Associative Memory

The dense associative memory is one of the recurrent neural network models to store and recall a large number of patterns as fixed points of the dynamics. The energy of dense associative memory is given by

H=-\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}F\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}^{\mu}h_{i}\biggr),

(1)

where
h_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
denotes the state of the
i
-th unit, and
\xi_{i}^{\mu}
denotes the
i
-th element of the
\mu
-th pattern. Each
\xi_{i}^{\mu}
independently takes the value
\pm 1
with equal probability
1/2
. Introducing a nonlinear function
F
, such as a power function, makes memory patterns become deeper minima in the energy landscape and reduces interference between different memory patterns. This is because the nonlinearity suppresses weaker overlaps during the recall process. In this paper, we restrict ourselves to the case

F(x)=\frac{x^{n}}{2N^{n-1}}.

(2)

It should be noted that since the coefficient
1/(2N^{n-1})
does not affect the performance, it is equivalent to setting
F(x)=x^{n}
. The update rule is defined by the difference of two energies. before and after state transitions. We keep only the leading term in the argument of the sgn function in the update rule, which gives

\displaystyle h_{i}^{(t+1)}=

\displaystyle\,\mbox{\rm sgn}\,\biggl[\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}F\biggl(+\xi_{i}^{\mu}+\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\xi_{j}^{\mu}h_{j}\biggr)-\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}F\biggl(-\xi_{i}^{\mu}+\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\xi_{j}^{\mu}h_{j}\biggr)\biggr]

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mbox{\rm sgn}\,\biggl[\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\xi_{i}^{\mu}n\biggl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\xi_{j}^{\mu}h_{j}\biggr)^{n-1}+\mbox{(small order terms)}\;\biggr],

(4)

where
\,\mbox{\rm sgn}\,(x)
denotes the sign function that takes
1
if
x\geq 0
, and
-1
otherwise. In the case of
n=2
the network reduces to the parallel dynamics version of traditional Hopfield model, i.e.,
h_{i}^{(t+1)}=\,\mbox{\rm sgn}\,(u_{i}^{(t)})
with
u_{i}^{(t)}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}J_{ij}h_{j}^{(t)}
and
J_{ij}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\xi_{i}^{\mu}\xi_{j}^{\mu}
.

2.2
Outline of Generating Functional Analysis

The generating functional analysis (GFA) has been applied to the model which is described using realizations of random variables, following De Dominicis
[
9
]
. GFA allows us to analyze the asymptotic dynamical behavior in the infinitely large system using the generating functional which is the dynamical equivalent of the characteristic function is statistics.

Let vectors
\bm{h}^{(t)}=(h_{i}^{(t)})

\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}
be the states of the model at time
t
and let the initial state be
\bm{h}^{(0)}
. Let the updating rule be

\displaystyle\bm{h}^{(t+1)}=\,\mbox{\rm sgn}\,(\bm{u}^{(t)};\{\bm{\xi}_{i}\}_{i\in[1:M]})

(5)

for
t\in\{0,\cdots,T-1\}
, where
[m:n]=\{i\in\mathbb{Z}:m\leq i\leq n\}
. If we know the joint probability of the states
p[\bm{h}^{(0)},\cdots,\bm{h}^{(T)}]
, we can use it to calculate various expectation values. This joint probability is referred to as the
path probability
. The way to evaluate expected values of interest, we introduce the
generating functional
, defined as

\displaystyle Z[\bm{\psi}]=\biggl\langle\exp\biggl[-\mathrm{i}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{i}^{(t)}h_{i}^{(t)}\biggr]\biggr\rangle,

(6)

where the bracket
\langle\cdots\rangle
denotes the average over the path probability
p[\bm{h}^{(0)},\cdots,\bm{h}^{(T)}]
, and we have introduced the dummy variables
\bm{\psi}^{(t)}=(\psi_{i}^{(t)})

\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and we write
\bm{\psi}=(\bm{\psi}^{(0)},\cdots,\bm{\psi}^{(T)})
for shorthand.

In GFA, the expectation values of interest are calculated from derivatives with respect to some elements of the dummy variables. We assume that the generating functional is concentrated to its average over the memory patterns
\bar{Z}[\bm{\psi}]
in the large-system limit, where the overline
\overline{(\cdots)}
denotes the average over memory patterns, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}^{1},\cdots,\bm{\xi}^{M}}[(\cdots)]
. The average over the memory patterns cause a coupling between distinct times. Averaging over the random variables, we will move to a saddle-point problem
[
14
]
in the limit
N\to\infty
. Taking derivatives with respect to the dummy variables allow us to examine some averages, e.g.,

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\lim_{\bm{\psi}\to\bm{0}}\frac{\partial\bar{Z}[\bm{\psi}]}{\partial\psi_{i}^{(t)}}=-i\langle h_{i}^{(t)}\rangle,~~\lim_{\bm{\psi}\to\bm{0}}\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{Z}[\bm{\psi}]}{\partial\psi_{i}^{(t)}\partial\psi_{i}^{(t^{\prime})}}=-\langle h_{i}^{(t)}h_{i^{\prime}}^{(t^{\prime})}\rangle,~~\lim_{\bm{\psi}\to\bm{0}}\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{Z}[\bm{\psi}]}{\partial\psi_{i}^{(t)}\partial\theta_{i}^{(t^{\prime})}}=-i\frac{\partial\langle h_{i}^{(t)}\rangle}{\partial\theta_{i^{\prime}}^{(t^{\prime})}}.

(7)

It should be noted that the normalization relation
Z[\bm{0}]=1
plays an important role in the elimination of spurious solutions to the saddle-point equations. The terms in the averaged generating functional can be split into three related parts. The first one is a signal part. The second one is a static noise part due to the random variables within the model. The last one is retarded self-interaction due to the influence of the state at the previous stage, which may be able to affect the present state. The GFA allows us to treat the last part. After the analysis, it turns out that the system can be described in terms of the following three quantities:

\displaystyle m^{(t)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}^{1}\overline{\langle h_{i}^{(t)}\rangle},~~~~C^{(t,t^{\prime})}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{\langle h_{i}^{(t)}h_{i}^{(t^{\prime})}\rangle},~~~~G^{(t,t^{\prime})}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\overline{\langle h_{i}^{(t)}\rangle}}{\partial\theta_{i}^{(t^{\prime})}},

(8)

where these are referred to as the
overlap
, the
correlation function
, and the
response function
, respectively.

3
Analysis

In this paper, we consider parallel dynamics, in which the states of all units are updated simultaneously. The updating rule of the dense associative memory for the variable
\bm{h}^{(t+1)}
at time
t
can be given by the following probability distribution:

p[\bm{h}^{(t+1)}|\bm{h}^{(t)}]=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\delta[h_{i}^{(t+1)};\,\mbox{\rm sgn}\,(u_{i}^{(t)})]
,
where
\delta[m;n]
denotes the Kronecker’s delta that takes
1
if
m=n
and
0
otherwise. Here, introducing the threshold
\theta_{i}^{(t)}
, so called
external field
, into the argument of the sign function in the update rule, we put
u_{i}^{(t)}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\xi_{i}^{\mu}n(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\xi_{j}^{\mu}h_{j})^{n-1}+\theta_{i}^{(t)}
which is called the
local field
. The external field
\theta_{i}^{(t)}
is set to zero after calculation. This dynamics represents Markovian dynamics. The path probability
p[\bm{h}^{(0)},\cdots,\bm{h}^{(T)}]
is given as the products of the probability distribution of the updating rule:

\displaystyle p[\bm{h}^{(0)},\cdots,\bm{h}^{(t)}]=p[\bm{h}^{(0)}]\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\rho[\bm{h}^{(t+1)}|\bm{h}^{(t)}],

(9)

where
p[\bm{h}^{(0)}]=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p[h_{i}^{(0)}]
denotes the initial state distribution.

Definition 1
.

The generating functional
Z[\bm{\psi}]
is defined as

\displaystyle Z[\bm{\psi}]=\sum_{\bm{h}}p[\bm{h}^{(0)},\cdots,\bm{h}^{(t)}]\exp\biggl(-i\sum_{s=0}^{t}\bm{h}^{(t)}\cdot\bm{\psi}^{(t)}\biggr),

(10)

where
\sum_{\bm{h}}
denotes
\sum_{\bm{h}^{(0)},\cdots,\bm{h}^{(T)}\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}}
.

We here assume that the generating functional is self-averaging, namely, in the large-system limit, the generating functional is concentrated on its average over the memory patterns
\{\xi^{\mu}\}_{\mu\in[1:M]}
and the typical behavior of the model only depends on the statistical properties of the memory patterns. We therefore evaluate the averaged generating functional. Introducing the definition of the local field using the Dirac delta function, the generating functional averaged over the memory patterns is as follows:

\displaystyle\bar{Z}[\bm{\psi}]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}^{\geq 1}}\sum_{\bm{h}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{T}}d\bm{u}~p[\bm{h}^{(0)}]\biggl(\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\delta[h_{i}^{(t+1)};\mathrm{sgn}(u_{i}^{(t)})]\biggr)e^{-\sum_{t=0}^{T}\bm{h}^{(t)}\cdot\bm{\psi}^{(t)}}

\displaystyle\times\biggl(\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\delta\biggl(u_{i}^{(t)}-\sum_{\mu=1}^{M}\xi_{i}^{\mu}\biggl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\xi_{j}^{\mu}h_{j}^{(t)}\biggr)^{n-1}-\theta_{i}^{(t)}\biggr)\biggr)

(11)

Assuming that the pattern
\bm{\xi}^{1}
is being recalled, we separate the local field in the generating functional into a signal term including the recalling pattern and a noise term including other patterns
\{\bm{\xi}^{\mu}\}_{\mu\in[2:M]}
. Using the Fourier integral form of Dirac delta function, the generating functional becomes

\displaystyle\bar{Z}[\bm{\psi}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{h}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{T}}d\bm{u}\delta\hat{\bm{u}}~p[\bm{h}^{(0)}]\biggl(\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\delta[h_{i}^{(t+1)};\mathrm{sgn}(u_{i}^{(t)})]\biggr)e^{-\sum_{t=0}^{T}\bm{h}^{(t)}\cdot\bm{\psi}^{(t)}}

\displaystyle\times\exp\biggl[-\sum_{t=0}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{u}_{i}^{(t)}u_{i}^{(t)}\biggr]\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}^{1}}\exp\biggl[-i\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{u}_{i}^{(t)}\xi_{i}^{1}~n\biggl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\xi_{j}^{1}h_{j}^{(t)}\biggr)^{n-1}\biggr]\;\mathcal{N},

(12)

where
\mathcal{N}
denotes a noise term that includes all non-recalled patterns
\bm{\xi}^{2},\cdots,\bm{\xi}^{M}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}=

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}^{>1}}\exp\biggl[-i\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{\mu=2}^{M}\hat{u}_{i}^{(t)}\xi_{i}^{\mu}~n\biggl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}\xi_{j}^{\mu}h_{j}^{(t)}\biggr)^{n-1}\biggr]

(13)

The integrand in the generating functional (
12
) scales as
e^{O(N)}
. In order to obtain a nontrivial storage capacity, the order of
\mathcal{N}
must be
e^{O(N)}
. From this, the scaling of
M
can be determined analytically, which is equivalent to the discussion of the absolute capacity by Krotov and Hopfield. The main part of our analysis is the calculation of
\mathcal{N}
.

Lemma 1
.

The expectation value of the noise term of all non-recalled patterns is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}=

\displaystyle\exp\biggl[-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{n^{2}M}{N^{n-2}}~\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\sum_{t^{\prime}=0}^{T-1}\biggl\{

\displaystyle(n-1)^{2}\biggl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i}^{(t^{\prime})}\hat{u}_{i}^{(t)}\biggr)\biggl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{N}h_{i^{\prime}}^{(t)}\hat{u}_{i^{\prime}}^{(t^{\prime})}\biggr)\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}A(n-2,k)\biggl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}h_{j}^{(t)}h_{j}^{(t^{\prime})}\biggr)^{k}

\displaystyle+\biggl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{u}_{i}^{(t)}\hat{u}_{i}^{(t^{\prime})}\biggr)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A(n-1,k)\biggl(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}h_{j}^{(t)}h_{j}^{(t^{\prime})}\biggr)^{k}+O(N^{-1})\biggr\}\biggr],

(14)

where

A(\ell,k)=\bigl(\!\!\begin{array}[]{c}\ell\\
k\\
\end{array}\!\!\bigr)^{2}\;k!\;B(\ell-k)^{2}
, and

B(m)=\mathbb{I}(m\mbox{ : even})\;(2m-1)!!.

It can be obtained by evaluating the leading terms after taking the expectation, using combinatorial arguments. Here,
\mathbb{I}(P)
denotes the indicator function that takes
1
if the proposition
P
is true, and
0
otherwise. From Lemma
1
, the number of memory patterns
M
must scale as
M=O(N^{n-1})
for non-trivial analysis, because
\mathcal{N}=e^{O(N)}
when
M=O(N^{n-1})
. Therefore, we put

\displaystyle M=\alpha_{n}N^{n-1}.

(15)

The signal term that includes the recalling pattern
\bm{\xi}^{1}
can be rearranged as
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}^{1}}

\exp[

-

i

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}

\sum_{i=1}^{N}

\hat{u}_{i}^{(t)}

\xi_{i}^{1}~

n(\frac{1}{N}

\sum_{j\neq i}^{N}

\xi_{j}^{1}

h_{j}^{(t)}

)^{n-1}]

=

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}^{1}}

\exp[

-

iN

\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}

(\frac{1}{N}

\sum_{i=1}^{N}

\hat{u}_{i}^{(t)}

\xi_{i}^{1})~

n(\frac{1}{N}

\sum_{j=1}^{N}

\xi_{j}^{1}

h_{j}^{(t)}

+

O(N^{-1})

)^{n-1}]
. Since the expectation over the non-recalled patterns has already been taken, and only the recalling pattern remains in the expression, we write the recalling pattern as
\bm{\xi}
from here on. The averaged generating functional is described in terms of five kinds of average values. We introduce the following macroscopic parameters:

\displaystyle m^{(t)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}h_{i}^{(t)},~~k^{(t)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\xi_{i}\hat{u}_{i}^{(t)},

\displaystyle q^{(t,t^{\prime})}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i}^{(t)}h_{i}^{(t^{\prime})},~~Q^{(t,t^{\prime})}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i}^{(t^{\prime})}\hat{u}_{i}^{(t)},~~K^{(t,t^{\prime})}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i}^{(t^{\prime})}\hat{u}_{i}^{(t)},

(16)

where the functions
m^{(t)}
is referred to as the
overlap
. Using the Fourier integral form of the Dirac delta function again, the averaged generating functional can be given by

\displaystyle\bar{Z}[\bm{\psi}]=\int d\bm{m}d\hat{\bm{m}}d\bm{k}d\hat{\bm{k}}d\bm{q}d\hat{\bm{q}}d\bm{Q}d\hat{\bm{Q}}d\bm{K}d\hat{\bm{K}}~e^{N(\Psi+\Phi+\Omega)+O(\log N)},

(17)

where

\displaystyle\Psi=

\displaystyle\textstyle i\sum_{t}\{\hat{m}^{(t)}m^{(t)}+\hat{k}^{(t)}k^{(t)}-k^{(t)}~n(m^{(t)})^{n-1}\}

The averaged generating functional is dominated by a saddle-point in the large-system limit. The saddle point condition gives values of the macroscopic parameters. Hereafter, we choose the factorised distribution

denotes an initial overlap. The factorised initial overlap allows the averaged generating functional to decompose into independent single-unit contributions.

4
Main results

4.1
Exact dynamics

The behavior of this model differs significantly between the case
n=2
and the case
n\geq 3
. Since the case
n=2
has already been extensively studied, we focus only on the case
n\geq 3
in this paper. GFA provides an exact solution as an asymptotic analysis in the large-system limit
N\to\infty
. Using the saddle point method to evaluate the integral in the averaged generating functional, one can obtain the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

For a given initial state distribution
p[h^{(0)}]
and
n\geq 3
,
the overlap
m^{(t)}
, the correlation function
C^{(t,t^{\prime})}
,
and the response function
G^{(t,t^{\prime})}
are given by

\displaystyle m^{(t)}=\langle\kern-2.29996pt\langle\xi h^{(t)}\rangle\kern-2.29996pt\rangle,~~~~C^{(t,t^{\prime})}=\langle\kern-2.29996pt\langle h^{(t)}h^{(t^{\prime})}\rangle\kern-2.29996pt\rangle,~~~~G^{(t,t^{\prime})}=\mathbb{I}(t>t^{\prime})\;\frac{\partial\langle\kern-2.29996pt\langle h^{(t)}\rangle\kern-2.29996pt\rangle}{\partial\theta^{(t^{\prime})}},

(21)

where
\langle\kern-2.29996pt\langle f(\bm{h})\rangle\kern-2.29996pt\rangle
denotes the average defined as

\displaystyle\langle\kern-2.29996pt\langle f(\bm{h})\rangle\kern-2.29996pt\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\int\mathcal{D}\bm{v}\sum_{\bm{h}}f(\bm{h})p[h^{(0)}]\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\delta\biggl[h^{(t+1)};\,\mbox{\rm sgn}\,\biggl(\xi n(m^{(t)})^{n-1}+(\Gamma\bm{h})^{(t)}+v^{(t)}+\theta^{(t)}\biggr)\biggr],

(22)

which is referred to as the effective path measure.
The random vector
\bm{v}
follows a multivariate normal distribution
with mean
\bm{0}
and covariance matrix
R=(R^{(t,t^{\prime})})
, where the
(t,t^{\prime})
-element is

\displaystyle R^{(t,t^{\prime})}=

\displaystyle n^{2}\alpha_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}A(n-1,k)(C^{(t,t^{\prime})})^{k}.

(23)

The matrix
\Gamma
is given by
\Gamma=D\circ G
.
The
(t,t^{\prime})
-elements of
D
and
G
are
D^{(t,t^{\prime})}
and
G^{(t,t^{\prime})}
, respectively.
Each element of the matrix
D=(D^{(t,t^{\prime})})
is defined as

\displaystyle D^{(t,t^{\prime})}=

\displaystyle n^{2}(n-1)^{2}\alpha_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}A(n-2,k)(C^{(t,t^{\prime})})^{k}.

(24)

The operator
\circ
denotes the Hadamard (elementwise) product.

The term
(\Gamma\bm{h})^{(t)}
in the effective path measure represents a
retarded self-interaction
. Due to this retarded self-interaction, the state at the next time step depends in a complex way on the past states. On the other hand, unlike in the traditional Hopfield model, i.e., the case of
n=2
, the noise variance does not depend on the overlap, and it does not increase even when the overlap becomes large. As a result, it is considered that the phenomenon, in which the system begins to recall correctly but eventually fails to complete it, becomes less likely to occur.

4.2
Approximated dynamics

From the exact solution obtained via the generating functional analysis, we obtain the following approximated result when self-coupling is neglected.

Corollary 1
.

Neglecting the retarded self-interaction term as an approximation, i.e., setting
\Gamma=O
, we obtain

\displaystyle m^{(t+1)}=\,\mbox{\rm erf}\,\biggl(\frac{(m^{(t)})^{n-1}}{\sqrt{(2n-3)!!~2\alpha_{n}}}\biggr),

(25)

where
\,\mbox{\rm erf}\,(x):=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-z^{2}}dz

denotes the error function, and
m^{(0)}
is the initial overlap.

In this approximation, the equilibrium state of the dynamics can be simply obtained by setting
m^{(t)}=m
, and the resulting fixed-point equation corresponds to the equilibrium analysis by
n
-body Hopfield model. Although the coefficients differ, this is due to the fact that the energy function is not the same as that in Krotov’s model.

4.3
Stationary State

We also extract the stationary state of the updating rule, under some assumptions. We can set the recalling pattern to
\bm{\xi}=\bm{1}
without loss of generality. We recognize that the representation of the effective path measure given by (
22
) is fully equivalent to the measure corresponding to the following non-Markovian
single-unit process
:

\displaystyle h^{(t+1)}=\,\mbox{\rm sgn}\,(n(m^{(t)})^{n-1}+(\Gamma\bm{h})^{(t)}+v^{(t)}+\theta^{(t)}),

(26)

where
\bm{v}=(v^{(t)})
can be regarded as a random variable that obeys normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{R})
, e.g.,

We make the following assumptions to evaluate the stationary state.

Assumption 1

(Weak long-term memory)

Assumption 2

(Finite integrated response)

Since the response function represents the memory, i.e. what happened to the system in the past, assuming weak long-term memory implies that the system responds to its past in an averaged way. The details of what takes place during finite stages tend to be washed away. Therefore, under Assumption
1

6
Conclusion

We performed an asymptotically exact analysis of the dynamical behavior of dense associative memory using generating functional analysis (GFA) in the large-system limit. The analysis revealed the presence of a retarded self-coupling term, indicating that the next state of the system depends in a complex manner on all past states. We also confirmed that this property cannot be captured by a method based on the signal-to-noise analysis. In the traditional Hopfield model, i.e.,
n=2
, it was found that the system exhibits a noise variance that depends intricately on non-recalled patterns. In contrast, for
n\geq 3
, the noise variance due to non-recalled patterns does not depend on the overlap with the recalled pattern. As a result, the phenomenon observed in the traditional Hopfield model classical model, which is successful recall leads to increased noise variance, does not occur. As a result, the recall process becomes simpler.

Assuming the existence of a stationary state, we derived from the GFA equations a macroscopic fixed-point equation that describes it. Due to the presence of the self-coupling term, this result differs from that of existing equilibrium analysis. This difference comes from the fact that, for models with
n\geq 3
, the system does not satisfy the detailed balance condition. However, when the Maxwell construction is applied, the result becomes consistent with the result obtained from equilibrium analysis. A more detailed numerical investigation remains a topic for future work.
