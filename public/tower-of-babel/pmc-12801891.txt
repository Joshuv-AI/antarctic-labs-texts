Magnetic resonance-guided online adaptive stereotactic body radiotherapy for advanced inoperable pancreatic cancer: a prospective study
Authors: Chen Wang, Zhiqun Wang, Lang Yu, Qiu Guan, Tingting Dong, Xiaorong Hou, Haoran Xu, Yue Zhang, Fei Jiang, Xin Lian, Ke Hu, Jie Qiu, Fuquan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo assess the local control, safety, and symptom relief of magnetic resonance-guided online adaptive stereotactic body radiation therapy (MRg-oART-SBRT) combined with systemic therapy in advanced unresectable pancreatic cancer.MethodsThis single-center, open-label, prospective trial enrolled ≥ 18-year-old patients with pathologically confirmed pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, including locally advanced, post-recurrent, and oligometastatic cases deemed unresectable via multidisciplinary evaluation. ECOG 0–2 patients with/without prior chemotherapy received 40–50 Gy/5 fractions via 1.5T MR-linear accelerator with daily online adaptive radiotherapy. Primary endpoint was 6-month local control rate; secondary endpoints included 3-month pain relief, ≥G3 gastrointestinal toxicity within 90 days, and 6-month distant progression-free survival.ResultsFrom November 2023 to December 2024, 34 patients completed MRg-oART-SBRT. With median follow-up of 12.3 months (IQR 9.0–14.4) after radiotherapy, the 6-month and 1-year local control rate were both 80.2%. Median distant progression-free survival was 9.7 months after radiotherapy. 97% reported pain relief at 3 months after radiotherapy. Acute probably radiation-related toxicity was mild (G1–2 diarrhea 18%). Thrombocytopenia (G2–3: 18%) and palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia (G1–2: 12%) were most common treatment-related adverse reactions.ConclusionMRg-oART-SBRT demonstrates encouraging short-term control, manageable toxicity, and survival signals in advanced pancreatic cancer. Larger multicenter trials with extended follow-up are warranted to validate long-term efficacy and clinical utility.Trial registrationThe study was registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, with the registration number ChiCTR2500096492.The retrospective registration date was January 24, 2025.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains a critical unmet medical need, ranking as the fourth leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide with a dismal 5-year survival rate less than 10% among solid tumors [1]. Despite advances in systemic therapy, only 15–20% of patients qualify for curative resection at the initial diagnosis due to early vascular invasion or metastatic spread [2]. While neoadjuvant therapy may convert some initially unresectable cases to operable status, more than 70% of the majority of patients with locally advanced or metastatic PDAC are deemed inoperable at presentation or develop recurrence post-surgery [3]. For these individuals, systemic chemotherapy remains the backbone of care, yet it is often associated with cumulative toxicities and quality of life (QOL) decline, particularly in elderly or frail populations. Notably, approximate 50% of patients present with distant metastases at diagnosis, generally with a poor prognosis [4]. Among these, a subset with oligometastatic disease (defined as a low tumor burden and metastatic lesions confined to a limited number of organs) exhibits distinct biological characteristics [5]. Emerging evidence suggests that this subgroup may derive survival benefits from local therapy when combined with systemic therapy, as compared to systemic therapy alone [6].

While systemic therapy dominates advanced PDAC management, local treatment modalities like radiotherapy (RT) offer distinct advantages for select subgroups. Conventional RT with non-ablative doses has demonstrated modest benefits in pain palliation and local control (LC) for local advanced PDAC but has not shown survival superiority over chemotherapy alone [7, 8]. In contrary, stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT)—a high-precision, hypofractionated RT technique—has emerged as a promising strategy to deliver ablative doses to tumors while minimizing toxicity [9]. Preclinical and early clinical data in other oligometastatic malignancies (e.g., lung, liver) have shown that SBRT improves local control and survival [10, 11]. In borderline resectable pancreatic cancer, neoadjuvant SBRT with sparing of dose-limiting structures (e.g., gastrointestinal tract) may similarly enhance local control and potentially survival [12–16].

However, delivering SBRT to the pancreas is technically challenging due to its anatomical proximity to radiosensitive organs and inherent motion from respiration and peristalsis. Traditional CT-based planning often struggles to distinguish subtle tumor margins from adjacent normal tissues, necessitating conservative dose escalation [17]. Magnetic resonance-guided online adaptive radiotherapy (MRg-oART) addresses these limitations by leveraging high-resolution MRI for real-time tumor localization, inter-fractional anatomy tracking, and adaptive plan optimization. This technology enables online dose painting to account for target volume changes and organ motion, potentially allowing safer delivery of ablative doses. While MRg-oART has been validated in thoracic and pelvic malignancies [18–20], its application in advanced inoperable PDAC—particularly in oligometastatic or post-recurrent subsets—remains underexplored.

Here, we report the results of a single-center study evaluating MRg-oART-SBRT in advanced PDAC patients, including those with local advanced unresectable disease, postoperative recurrence, or oligometastasis. The primary objective of this study is to evaluate the safety, feasibility, and preliminary efficacy of delivering 40–50 Gy in 5 fractions using MRg-oART-SBRT, with particular emphasis on reducing radiation exposure to adjacent gastrointestinal organs. We specifically investigate the potential for safe dose escalation, characterize toxicity profiles, and analyze clinical outcomes in a patient population historically considered high-risk for radiotherapy-related complications.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This single-center, open-label, single-arm, prospective trial obtained ethical approval from Ethics Committee of Institutional Review Board of Peking Union Medical College Hospital. The study was registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry, with the registration number ChiCTR2500096492. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Eligible patients were adults (aged ≥ 18 years) with pathologically confirmed PDAC via cytological or histological examination. Enrollment required multidisciplinary team (MDT) confirmation of ineligibility for surgical resection and inclusion in one of three subgroups: local advanced unresectable PDAC; postoperative recurrence ≥ 6 months after last chemotherapy course; oligometastatic disease (metastases in ≤ 3 organs and with ≤ 5 total metastatic lesions). All patients had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–2. Patients had no contraindications to MRI and were able to maintain a supine position for ≥ 40 min. As assessed by the physician, patients were expected to complete the MR-guided linear accelerator radiotherapy protocol. Tumor size was not an exclusion criterion. Exclusion criteria included the development of widespread distant metastases after induction chemotherapy. No specific requirements were imposed on the systemic treatment regimens administered prior to radiotherapy.

Magnetic resonance-guided online adaptice stereotactic body radiotherapy (MRg-oART-SBRT)

Pre-scan MRI was performed for localization images. Abdominal compression with a pressure plate was applied to restrict tumor motion to ≤ 5 mm. All patients were treated on a MR-guided radiation delivery system (Elekta Inc., Unity®) using x-rays (MR-linac). Daily image guidance was performed using 1.5 T MR scan. The gross tumor volume (GTV) includes the radiographically visible pancreatic cancer lesion and the tumor-vessel interface (TVI) within 5 mm. The planning target volume (PTV) was generated by expanding the GTV by 3–5 mm in three dimensions. A total dose of 40–50 Gy in 5 fractions (biologically effective dose BED10 = 72–100 Gy) was prescribed to the PTV. The treatment plan evaluation required that PTV D95% was >80% of the prescribed dose and GTV D95% was >95% of the prescribed dose. Dose constraints for the gastrointestinal organs at risks (OAR) including duodenum, stomach, and small bowel, were set with a hard maximum limit of D0.5cc < 35 Gy. The planning objectives were to achieve D0.5cc < 33 Gy and V30Gy < 5 cc for each of these structures during optimization. Final plan approval was contingent upon meeting both quantitative criteria and passing a qualitative review, in which senior radiation oncologists and physicists verified the absence of concerning geographic overlap of high-dose regions on the OAR, especially duodenum, across all treatment fractions. The BED for each fraction was calculated based on the actual dose delivered to the region of interest in each treatment session, and the cumulative BED was obtained by summing these fractional BED values [21]. Intravenous chemotherapy was not administered during the radiotherapy course.

Therapy after MRg-oART-SBRT

Post-radiotherapy plans was determined by MDT discussion involving surgeons, medical oncologists, radiation oncologists, radiologists, pathologists, nutrition specialists, and palliative care physicians. Contrast-enhanced CT or MRI and hematological assessments were conducted 3 months after radiotherapy to evaluate eligibility for surgery or chemotherapy. No restrictions were placed on systemic therapy.

Patient assessments

Patients underwent follow-up evaluations every 3 months post-radiotherapy, including clinical symptom and sign assessments, imaging and hematological examinations, Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) pain scoring with Brief Pain Inventory (BPI), and QOL questionnaire surveys with EORTC QLQ-C30. Adverse events (AEs) were evaluated using a combination of Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) criteria for radiation-specific toxicity and Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE v5.0) for systemic toxicity. Acute AEs were defined as those occurring within 90 days of radiotherapy, while chronic AEs arising ≥ 90 days post-radiotherapy. Given the multi-modality treatment received by most patients, the correlation of AEs with radiotherapy was assessed via cross-blind evaluation by two senior physicians within the MDT, categorized as definitely, probably, or possibly related to radiation.

Statistical analysis

The primary endpoint, the LC rate at 6 months after MRg-oART-SBRT, was defined as the proportion of patients without progression of pancreatic lesions by 6 months post-treatment or until the last follow-up. Secondary endpoints included: (1) pain relief rate at 3 months after radiotherapy, defined as a ≥ 2-point reduction in NRS score; (2) incidence of grade ≥ 3 gastrointestinal toxicity within 90 days after radiotherapy, graded according to the RTOG criteria; (3) distant progression-free survival (DPFS) at 6 months post-radiotherapy. For patients with non-metastatic disease at diagnosis, DPFS was measured from treatment initiation to the first occurrence of distant metastasis; for those with metastatic disease, it was defined as time to new distant lesions. The exploratory endpoint, 1-year overall survival (OS) rate after diagnosis, was defined as the proportion of patients alive 12 months from the date of diagnosis. Survival outcomes (LC, DPFS, OS) were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with median survival times and time-specific rates calculated accordingly. 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for survival estimates were derived via normal approximation. All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.13.1, Python Software Foundation) with key scientific libraries, including Pandas (v2.2.3) and Matplotlib (v3.9.2) for data manipulation, SciPy (v1.14.1) for statistical modeling, and Seaborn (v0.13.2) for data visualization.

Results

From November 2023 to December 2024, a total of 34 eligible patients were consecutively enrolled in our center. The patient and tumor characteristics at baseline were summarized in Table 1. The median patient age was 66 years (range: 38–81). Diagnoses were predominantly locally advanced disease (n = 24, 71%), followed by oligometastatic disease (n = 7, 21%) and postoperative recurrence (n = 3, 9%). Pancreatic lesions were mostly located in the head or neck of the pancreas (n = 11, 32%). The median CA199 level was 120 U/mL at the time of diagnosis and 45 U/mL prior to SBRT.

Patient and tumor characteristics(N = 34)

Two patients did not receive pre-SBRT chemotherapy, both of whom were postoperative recurrence cases. The median time of pre-SBRT chemotherapy duration for the remaining patients was 5.4 months, with the AG regimen (gemcitabine + nab-paclitaxel) being the primary chemotherapy protocol (n = 19, 56%). All patients were treated on 1.5T MR-linac. Due to positional variations of adjacent gastrointestinal tract and the dosimetric constraints, adaptive treatment plans were actually employed for all irradiations. The projected doses to target volumes and OARs, as well as the actual doses received in each treatment, are presented in Fig. 1A-E. In actual treatment, the median volume of the PTV and GTV were 63.32 cc (IQR 46.31-83.18 cc) and 35.84 cc (IQR 23.42-53.86 cc), respectively. The actual treatment plan met the criteria that the D95% of the PTV was > 80% of the prescribed dose and the D95% of the GTV was > 95% of the prescribed dose. The maximum dose within the target volume reached 125% of the prescribed dose. Based on favorable OAR dose metrics observed in the initial five patients, the prescription dose was escalated from 40 Gy (BED₁₀ = 72 Gy) to 50 Gy (BED₁₀ = 100 Gy) starting from the sixth patient. Subsequent treatment plans were individualized using a 50 Gy pre-plan for each patient. The 50 Gy prescription was approved only if all OAR constraints (particularly duodenum) were met; otherwise, the dose was de-escalated to 40 Gy. The specific average doses to the target volumes and organs at risk are shown in Table 2. The target dose in the actual treatment ART plan achieved the pre-planned dose with a deviation of ≤ 10%, and the dose to OARs was comparable to the pre-planned dose (Fig. 1F&G). In all patients, the cumulative duodenal D0.5 cc was maintained under 35 Gy.

The median follow-up time was 12.3 months (IQR 9.0–14.4) since radiotherapy and 20.1 (IQR 16.7–21.9) months since diagnosis. The 6-month and 1-year local control rates from radiotherapy were 80.2% and 80.2%, respectively. The 1-year and 2-year local control rates from diagnosis were 87.2% and 77.1%, respectively. The median DPFS was 9.7 months from radiotherapy and 18.2 months from diagnosis. The 6-month and 1-year OS rates from radiotherapy were 89.9% and 85.1%, respectively, and the 1-year and 2-year OS rates from diagnosis were 100.0% and 81.3%, respectively (Fig. 3). When analyzing the 24 patients with locally advanced disease separately, the LC at 6 months and 1 year after RT was 82.0% for both time points; the DPFS was 77.9% and 65.4% at 6 months and 1 year, respectively; and the OS was 90.9% and 90.9% at 6 months and 1 year.

At the 3-month post-radiotherapy evaluation, pain relief was observed in 97% of patients compared with baseline, and quality of life improvement was reported in 82%. The most common probable radiotherapy-related AEs were diarrhea, decreased appetite, and abdominal distension. Acute gastrointestinal toxicity included diarrhea (G1-G2 18%) and decreased appetite (G1-G2 9%, G3 3%); chronic toxicity comprised diarrhea (G1-G2 15%) and decreased appetite (G1-G2 9%, G3 3%). Only 1 probably radiotherapy-associated acute and chronic grade G3 AEs were recorded, both as decreased appetite. Common treatment-related AEs included thrombocytopenia (G2–3: 18%) and Palmar-plantar erythrodysesthesia (G1–2: 12%). A summary of all adverse events is provided in Table 3.

Discussion

We conducted a prospective study on MRg-oART-SBRT for patients with advanced inoperable pancreatic cancer and performed a systematic analysis of the treatment dosimetry and clinical outcomes. Before each treatment, MRg-oART employed MRI images for image guidance, monitoring of anatomical structures and positional changes of the target volume and organs at risk in current fraction, and provided clear differentiation between them. For each fraction, the treatment plans were adapted and optimized based on MRI images to ensure precise delivery of high-dose radiation. In this study, with a median follow-up of 12.3 months after radiotherapy, we have demonstrated durable local control and favorable survival outcomes of MRg-oART-SBRT in patients with advanced inoperable pancreatic cancer.

Pancreatic cancer is relatively insensitive to radiotherapy, and the clinical benefits of conventional radiotherapy are limited [7]. Previous retrospective studies have suggested that increasing the dose of conventional radiotherapy may yield favorable local effects and improve the prognosis of patients with borderline resectable or locally advanced unresectable pancreatic cancer [13]. However, the pancreas has a unique anatomical structure, closely adjacent to gastrointestinal tissues. These organs at risk exhibit significant movement during treatment, leading to positional uncertainty. Besides, the hollow mucosal tissues are sensitive to radiotherapy, leading to a high risk of adverse reactions. Although SBRT with non-ablative doses ensures safety, its efficacy is limited [22]. In contrast, traditional CT-guided high-dose radiotherapy has failed, precisely because of severe gastrointestinal toxicity, despite efforts to push dose escalation [23, 24]. MR-guided adaptive radiotherapy, through real-time image guidance and adaptive planning for each treatment session, overcomes the disadvantages of soft-tissue imaging and local dose limitations of traditional radiotherapy, and can thus achieve precise delivery of high-dose SBRT. In early - stage resectable pancreatic cancer, alternative adaptive SBRT based on an MR-guided linear accelerator has achieved favorable safety and efficacy outcomes [25]. In the SMART study, for patients with borderline resectable and locally advanced pancreatic cancer, a hypofractionated regimen of 50 Gy/5f has achieved promising outcomes with 1-year LC rate 82.9%, while avoiding severe gastrointestinal toxicity [15, 26]. While the SMART study used the MRIdian system, our study employed the 1.5T Unity system with higher magnetic field strength, enabling superior image quality for more accurate pancreatic lesion and normal tissue differentiation and contouring. Comparatively, our cohort consisted of patients with more advanced disease stages, encompassing those with locally advanced, postoperatively recurrent, and oligometastatic unresectable pancreatic cancer; nonetheless, the 1-year clinical outcomes were comparable (1-yr LC: 80.2%, DPFS: 48.7%). Furthermore, our prospective findings align with those of retrospective study by Tringale et al. [27], which also utilized the Unity system and included advanced patients, demonstrating concordant clinical outcomes between the two studies (1-year LC: 80.7%, DPFS: 39.7%). These findings strongly suggest that MRg-oART-SBRT can confer significant therapeutic benefits in patients with advanced pancreatic cancer.

Even after undergoing radical surgery and perioperative chemotherapy, a subset of patients experiences local recurrence in the primary tumor region. Due to anatomical disarray in the previous surgical field, adjacent vascular invasion, and the elevated risk of complications, repeat surgery is only recommended for highly selected patients. Consequently, radiotherapy, as a non-invasive local treatment, assumes a crucial role in the management of these patients. Previous meta-analyses have indicated that the median OS for patients with recurrent disease treated with SBRT ranges from 9 to 16 months, while combination of chemotherapy and radiotherapy between 10 and 19 months [28] In a study employing SBRT on CyberKnife with pre-implanted gold fiducial markers, the prescribed radiation dose was 35–40 Gy in 5 fractions, with a biologically effective dose at 10 Gy per fraction (BED10) of 64.38 Gy [29]. When combined with gemcitabine, this treatment regimen achieved 1-year OS of 56.5%, median PFS of 5.4 months, 1-year PFS of 8.2%, and a grade ≥ 3 gastrointestinal (GI) toxicity incidence of 2%. In our study, the 1yOS rates were 89.9%, demonstrating comparable efficacy. Moreover, the actual delivered radiation dose of GTV D95% reached 47.37 Gy (BED10 = 92.34 Gy), and no grade ≥ G3 GI probably radiotherapy-related toxicity occurred. These results suggest that MRg-oART-SBRT can achieve more precise irradiation under non-invasive conditions, providing enhanced therapeutic outcomes for patients with recurrent pancreatic cancer.

As an intermediate state between localized disease and widespread metastases, oligometastasis exhibits unique tumor biological behavior. Local treatments targeting both the primary tumor and metastatic lesions may confer additional survival benefits when combined with systemic therapy [6, 30]. However, currently, oligometastatic pancreatic cancer is predominantly managed with the same multi-drug chemotherapy regimens as other metastatic pancreatic cancers, resulting in suboptimal overall prognosis. Although the indication for surgical resection is reassessed after chemotherapy, this process may lead to disease progression in oligometastatic patients who are insensitive to the initial regimen, thereby causing them to miss the optimal window for local treatment. In the EXTEND basket trial focusing on radical radiotherapy for oligometastatic lesions, preliminary results demonstrated that the radical radiotherapy plus drug treatment group achieved a significantly prolonged PFS compared to the drug-only treatment group (10.3 months vs. 2.5 months), without any radiation-related adverse events of grade 3 or above. These findings highlight the potential role of radiotherapy in oligometastatic pancreatic cancer. In our cohort, all seven oligometastatic patients maintained a good quality of life, indicating that combining high-dose pancreatic SBRT with systemic therapy is a promising strategy for advanced cases. Nevertheless, the integration of SBRT into treatment paradigms for oligometastatic pancreatic cancer warrants further dedicated investigation.

In terms of safety, our study utilized daily MRg-imaging, which eliminates the radiation exposure associated with cone-beam CT-based guidance. Other real-time tracking technologies like CyberKnife require invasive fiducial marker implantation in advance, however can only track specific points within the tumor and cannot capture overall tumor movement, regression, or assess mobility and dose exposure of OARs. By contrast, MRg-oART provides continuous monitoring of all structures within the scanning field, with no additional implantation procedure, thereby significantly expanding its eligible patient population. Beyond pre-treatment imaging, MRg-oART enables dose adjustment for each treatment fraction. We summed the maximum dose to the daily-contoured OAR across all fractions. This approach is conservative, as it overestimates the true cumulative dose to any specific point. Since SBRT constraints focus on maximum dose, not volumetric averages, this provides a worst-case safety check. The fact that even these overestimated values remained within limits robustly ensures OAR safety. Furthermore, this approach provides valuable flexibility: as long as the total cumulative dose is acceptable, temporary, limited exceedances of the average limit in individual fractions are permissible, thus preventing unnecessary target dose compromise. This dual achievement highlights the precision and safety advantages of MRg-oART, optimizing the therapeutic ratio by maximizing tumor control while minimizing potential toxicity to surrounding normal tissues.

The patients enrolled in this study had relatively advanced overall staging and had undergone multiple lines of chemotherapy or surgical interventions, with poorer performance status compared to early-stage patients. Even though, they demonstrated good treatment tolerance, and all completed the full course of treatment smoothly. The study showed that acute and chronic adverse events were manageable, with only one case of possibly radiation-related grade G3 adverse event (decreasd appetite), which could not be excluded as being associated with systemic therapy. At three months after treatment, 97% of patients achieved local pain relief, and 82% maintained or improved their quality of life, comparable to those in relatively early-stage patient cohorts [26]. These findings highlight the significant clinical value of MRg-oART-SBRT technology in palliative tumor therapy and symptom relief for advanced refractory pancreatic cancer patients.

Additionally, we noted that a subset of patients achieving partial response or stable disease after radiotherapy opted not to proceed with further chemotherapy due to personal treatment preferences and chemotherapy intolerance. The prognosis of this subgroup and long-term outcomes remains to be determined. Currently, the optimal chemotherapy regimen and cycle number for patients with unresectable pancreatic cancer are not well-defined. Prolonged chemotherapy may have implications for quality of life, mental health, and healthcare economic burden. Whether selected patients could reduce chemotherapy cycles or even be exempt from chemotherapy after receiving ablative-dose radiotherapy warrants further investigation.

The primary limitations of this study are the small sample size and the relatively short follow-up duration. However, considering the rapid progression of pancreatic cancer and the short overall survival time of patients, it is still meaningful for new technologies to demonstrate safety and efficacy in refractory pancreatic cancer patients with limited treatment options. We are currently continuing to follow up with these patients to obtain long-term outcomes. Furthermore, while the heterogeneity of the included patient population provides valuable real-world data, it may also introduce statistical confounding. Additionally, as a single-center, single-arm study, our findings require further validation. Future multi-center collaborations with expanded cohorts are warranted to strengthen the validity and generalizability of our conclusions.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that MRg-oART-SBRT delivered in 5 fractions of 40–50 Gy is a promising novel therapeutic approach for patients with advanced unresectable pancreatic cancer. The regimen demonstrated a favorable safety profile while achieving encouraging short-term local control and survival outcomes. Clinically, this treatment strategy effectively alleviated disease-related symptoms and improved patients’ quality of life. Future multi-center randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate these findings and establish this technique as a new standard of care for this challenging patient population.
