A functionally complete logic gate in a soft photoresponsive hydrogel
Authors: Fariha Mahmood, Victor V. Yashin, Anna C. Balazs, Kalaichelvi Saravanamuttu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Materials that compute—or process stimuli to generate a result output—are important in applications ranging from soft robotics to therapeutics. Here, we report a NAND gate based on the interactions of three self-trapped beams in a photoresponsive hydrogel. The beams self-trap by triggering localised contraction and corresponding refractive index changes (Δn) and communicate with each other through the interconnected hydrogel network. Light-induced Δn in one region suppresses contraction (and Δn) elsewhere. This inhibits self-trapping and reduces the power of the central beam—which competes with two equidistant neighbours—compared to either peripheral beam, which competes with just one neighbour. The NAND gate exploits this geometry-dependent inhibition: the central beam’s peak power—the output—exceeds a threshold value unless both neighbours—inputs—are on, i.e., an output = 0 is retrieved only with input [1, 1]. We then demonstrate two and, separately, twelve sequentially chained NAND operations, and propose a route to multiple, simultaneously linked operations in a single, internally mediated step. Here, the output from one operation is spontaneously directed to subsequent operations. Our findings open pathways to soft materials with autonomous computational functionality.

Introduction

The field of “materials that compute” is focused on designing systems where the materials’ innate properties and inherently occurring physicochemical interactions allow it to perform computation. In effect, the material and computer constitute the same entity1,2. While current examples of “computing materials” can accomplish functions such as pattern recognition1, it remains a challenge to devise materials that perform the full range of Boolean operations possible with electronic logic gates. The latter gates take binary inputs (1 and 0) from electrical currents and produce an output that corresponds to a given logic operation. For example, the AND operation can be performed by a logic gate that returns a value of 1 if both inputs are 1, while the operation OR returns a value of 1 if either input is 13,4. When chained together, these gates represent complex functions such as half-adders or multiplexers, and serve as the building blocks of modern computing.

Physically implementing the Boolean framework in chemical or soft materials systems could enable the material itself to perform the full complement of logic operations and open routes to confer modern computing-inspired functionality to easily processable polymer or hydrogel systems1,5. This is only possible with a “functionally complete” set of logic gates, such as [AND, NOT], and [NAND], which can be combined to represent all other logic gates6. For example, [NAND] takes two inputs and returns an output of 0 only when both inputs are 1, and is distinctive since this single gate can be used to construct any other basic logic gate. An OR gate, for instance, can be constructed from three sequentially chained NAND gates.

Here, we realize such a “computing material” in an all-optical system, taking advantage of inherent interactions between light and photoresponsive hydrogels. As a light beam propagates through a photoresponsive medium, it induces refractive index changes (Δn) and sets up a dynamic competition between Δn and the natural divergence of light. The result is a self-trapped beam, which retains its spatial intensity profile even as it propagates over long distances7,8. As demonstrated herein, self-trapped beams and their unique interactions yield a foundational example of “materials that compute”. This approach can facilitate the development of artificial systems that operate independently—without relying on sequences of commands from external networks of electronic devices. Equally importantly, we note that these systems do not seek to compete with the speed and capacity of current electronics- or photonics-based computing processors. Instead, the objective is to develop advanced materials technologies, which can independently process and compute with external stimuli. Such systems aim to mimic the sophisticated stimuli-responsiveness of biological systems and can serve as powerful platforms in applications ranging from soft robotics9,10 to therapeutics that self-guide drug release to areas of greatest benefit11.

To intrinsically perform logic operations, materials systems must also be capable of internally mediated chaining of logic operations. Here, the flow of output must be converted to the input of the next operation without external intervention and interruption. This internal chaining could be achieved in systems where (1) inputs and outputs consist of the same physicochemical signal, (2) outputs of logic operations are autonomously routed to subsequent gates, and (3) elicit the desired response at those gates—i.e., behave as inputs. In existing systems12,13, the flow of operations is typically governed externally; outputs are converted to subsequent inputs through interpretation, additional instrumentation, or a sequence of different experiments instead of occurring autonomously within the system itself.

Self-trapped light beams8 display several characteristics that are advantageous for the development of soft material platforms for complex, internally mediated logic operations. Namely, self-trapped beams can influence each other7, leading to interactions such as attraction14,24, repulsion14–16, or spiraling17–20. These behaviors are mediated by mutually-induced Δn and influenced by parameters such as relative phase, intensity, or separation distance7,8. Hence, self-trapped beams could be subjected to designed sequences of interactions as they propagate through a medium, permitting a series of operations to be performed without external intervention21.

Other forms of stimuli-responsive chemical and soft materials systems have been designed to carry out logic-based, preprogrammed actions. Examples include photo-switches that mimic binary arithmetic12, sugar-based computers that play tic-tac-toe13, stimuli-responsive molecules22, DNA sequences23, and soft robotic devices9,10 that process environmental stimuli through logic-based action sequences. Elegant examples of combinational logic circuits based on AND, NOT and OR gates25, and discrete NAND, NOR and NOT operations have been assembled from spiropyran-based molecular switches26. Stimuli-responsive polymers have been combined to assemble grippers that closed with AND logic (when two stimuli were present) or OR logic (when one or both stimuli were present)10. DNA origami nanocapsules kinetically trapped by two types of surface-bound antibodies11 burst only in the presence of both corresponding antigens to release a viral payload—a programmed AND logic response11.

The above-described systems do not possess the full functionality and capability of Boolean logic because they typically cannot enable internally mediated chaining and/or cannot be assembled into functionally complete gates. We show here that both could be achieved with self-trapped beams: we experimentally demonstrate the functionally complete logic gate—NAND—assembled from three collinear, self-trapped beams of visible light in a photoresponsive hydrogel. This NAND gate is based on the interactions of three self-trapped beams elicited through an opto-chemo-mechanical process in a merocyanine-functionalized poly(acrylic acid-co-acrylamide) hydrogel27. We also demonstrate an AND gate and XOR gates by performing sequentially chained NAND operations, and propose a pathway for simultaneously performing multiple chained NAND operations. This experimental demonstration of a functionally complete and chainable logic gate opens routes to design materials that begin to encompass the autonomous functionality of natural, complex soft systems including biological soft tissue1,5.

Results

Self-trapping and interactions of three collinear beams: a mechanism to develop a NAND logic gate

We studied the self-trapping and interactions of three collinear and mutually coherent beams. Then we designed and experimentally demonstrated the NAND logic gate based on an understanding of these dynamics. In a typical experiment, a linearly polarized, visible laser beam (λ = 532 nm) was split into three equal-intensity, mutually coherent beams with a diffractive optical element (DOE) (see “Optical experiments”). Beams were focused onto the entrance face (z = 0 mm) of a sample cell containing the merocyanine-substituted hydrogel (see “Sample preparation”). The focused beams each had diameters of 20 μm and center-to-center separation distances of ~95 μm (~five times the beam diameter).

In a control experiment, we imaged the natural divergence of the three beams in air along the propagation axis (z); corresponding intensity profiles are presented in the Supplementary Information (Fig. S1). At z = 0 mm (corresponding to the sample entrance face), the beams appeared as three well-defined, bright spots but diverged and suffered interference as they propagated such that at z = 5 mm (corresponding to the sample exit face) only periodic interference fringes were visible. By contrast, when launched into the hydrogel, the same three beams self-trapped and propagated with reduced divergence and interference.

Optical self-trapping in the hydrogel takes place through an opto-chemo-mechanical process, which was elucidated in previous work (Fig. 1)27. Briefly, visible light-induced isomerization of merocyanine to spiropyran increases the local hydrophobicity of the hydrogel, expelling water from regions of high optical intensity (where the extent of isomerization is greatest). The resulting contraction of the hydrogel creates a localized increase in refractive index—the Δn required for self-trapping. This light-induced Δn is erased when spiropyran relaxes back to merocyanine in the dark, allowing the network to reswell to its original form. We previously reported self-trapping of one and two beams in the same hydrogel employed in the current study27; these results were replicated and presented for comparison in Figs. Figure 2B shows that the beams initially diverge (t = 0 s) and suffer interference; the corresponding intensity pattern at the exit face is complex. It consists of both primary spots, which appear at positions corresponding to the three distinct spots visible at z = 0 mm, and secondary spots, which originate from self-trapping of the interference fringes (vide infra). Over time, divergence and therefore interference are suppressed, and the beams appear as three distinct spots (t = 313 s), signifying that self-trapping has occurred. Temporal plots in Fig. 2C provide additional insight into the self-trapping dynamics of the beams, which are labeled 1 to 3 from left to right and represented by blue, orange, and green traces, respectively. The beams showed an initial rapid rise in peak power lasting 100 s to 150 s (Fig. 2C(i)), followed by a plateau or slow decay for the remainder of the experiment (up to 300 s) (Fig. 2C(ii)). This trend was reproduced in all repeat experiments (n = 9) (Fig. S5). In all nine experiments, Beams 1 and 3 consistently reached their maxima at ~100 s, Beam 2 maximized at ~100 s in the majority (n = 6) and at ~50 s in the rest (n = 3). Significantly, Beams 1 and 3 were brighter than the central Beam 2 in all experiments: Beam 1 achieved 1.5 to 2.2 times the maximum peak power of Beam 2, while Beam 3 maxima were 1.2 to 1.8 times greater relative to Beam 2.

Experiments with four collinear, mutually coherent beams showed comparable results (Figs. S2 and S6 and Movie S2). In eight of nine experiments, the two central beams were consistently darker than the two edge beams, with central beams typically (in seven of nine experiments) achieving comparable powers.

Most importantly for the design of the NAND gate, both the 2D intensity profiles (Fig. 2B) and temporal plots (Fig. 2C) showed that Beam 2—the central beam—was always the darkest despite all three beams being identical when initially launched into the sample. This evidence of long-range interactions—or communication—between the beams (which are separated by ~5-fold their diameter) is consistent with previous observations of nonlocal inhibition between a pair of self-trapped beams in the same hydrogel27. This mutually inhibitive behavior is unique to merocyanine-functionalised hydrogels and originates from the mechanism underlying Δn: light-induced isomerisation of merocyanine to spiropyran triggers a cascade of changes within the poly(acrylic acid-co-acrylamide) gel, ultimately resulting in the local contraction of the polymer network27. Due to the interconnected, highly-crosslinked nature of the medium, a contraction in one region pushes solvent away into another region, causing that latter region to swell. Hence, the first contraction renders the network less capable of contraction elsewhere—a molecular tug-of-war with the overall effect of reducing the maximum achievable Δn, and thereby the extent of self-trapping27.

We reason that the consistently smaller power of Beam 2 originates from the effect described above; Beam 2 suffers competition from two nearest neighbors (i.e., experiences the “tugs” associated with the contraction from these two neighboring beams) whereas Beam 1 and Beam 3 both have only a single primary competitor in Beam 2 (Fig. 2A). (The same effect leads to the depression in peak power of Beams 2 and 3 in the four beam experiments, as shown in Figs. S2 and S6). Although Beams 1 and 3 are in principle equivalent in three-beam experiments, Beam 1 consistently achieved a greater maximum in peak power than Beam 3. We attribute this asymmetry to the adventitious low-intensity, high-order diffraction spot at 200 μm from Beam 3 in the three-beam experiment. This peripheral spot introduces additional inhibition of self-trapping for Beam 3, which is not present for Beam 1.

Self-trapping dynamics of interference fringes

Finer details of self-trapping dynamics were discerned in the 2D and 1D intensity profiles (Fig. 3), particularly with respect to the evolution of interference fringes. These profiles reveal a rich interplay between the beams due to their mutual coherence. If Δn is sufficiently large to suppress divergence, overlap between self-trapped beams is negligible, interference does not occur, and the fringes disappear. By contrast, if Δn is small or develops slowly in time, the fringes persist and may themselves induce waveguides24,28. We distinguish between the behavior of primary spots—those corresponding to the three beams at the input face—and secondary spots, which originate from self-trapping of interference fringes. Early in the experiments, self-trapping of primary spots was characterized by a decrease in 1/e2 beam diameter coupled with an increase in peak power. Over the nine repeat experiments, Beams 1, 2, and 3 all achieved an average minimum diameter of 16 μm ± 4 μm. Parallel to this, we observed self-trapping of secondary spots. Initially, we observed faint interference fringes with a periodicity of approximately 4 μm. As Δn increased, the fringes became more prominent and themselves self-trapped, forming secondary spots in between primary spots (see Fig. 2B from t = 0 s, t = 20 s or Fig. 3 from t = 8 s to 20 s). These spots showed a rapid rise in intensity, typically exceeding the brightness of the primary spots in the initial stages of the experiment. This dominance is short-lived, with the self-trapping of primary spots overtaking secondary spots within 60 s. Insets and 1D intensity traces depict the migration of peak power towards a mutual center point. In all experiments, the remaining 200 s were characterized by a plateauing or slow decay in peak power. In this final regime, the relationships between spots stabilized, and secondary spots grew less prominent. In all experiments, interactions and competition between bright areas resulted in a final intensity profile consisting of the three self-trapped primary spots with two weaker secondary spots between them.

Long-range inhibition between self-trapped beams

To confirm that the consistent depression of Beam 2 in the three-beam experiments is due to the long-range inhibition specific to the merocyanine-functionalised hydrogel system, we repeated the experiment in a photoresponsive organosiloxane polymer. The refractive index increase in the latter originates from light-induced free radical polymerization of its methacrylate substituents (Fig. 5A)29. Long-range inhibition has not been observed in extensive studies of self-trapping and related phenomena in this material2,16,20,29–39. Two representative experiments are presented in Figs. 5 and 6, which contrast 2D and 1D intensity profiles (Fig. 5B, C), and temporal peak power plots (Fig. 6A, B) for the merocyanine-functionalised hydrogel and the organosiloxane, respectively. Both systems showed the same overall behavior in their temporal plots: a rapid rise in peak power within the first 50 s, followed by a plateau or slow decay for all beams. Notably, unlike the hydrogel, all three beams in the organosiloxane system developed at the same pace so that the plots of peak power were effectively superimposed (Fig. 6B). Other distinctions between the hydrogel and organosiloxane systems may be attributed to the large and rapid increase in Δn in the organosiloxane material relative to the hydrogel27,29. In the organosiloxane, rapid self-trapping of the primary spots resulted in the disappearance of interference fringes early in the experiment. While faintly visible at early times, by 50 s (Fig. 5C), no fringes were visible in either the 2D or 1D intensity profiles. A beam that is fully self-trapped maintains its entrance face profile throughout its path length without narrowing or diverging. In this case, the input profile consisted of three bright spots without any evidence of interference (see Fig. S1). Thus, a rapid increase in Δn and consequently, a greater extent of self-trapping eliminates the interference fringes early in the experiment, suppressing the formation of secondary spots. Additionally, self-trapped beams in the organosiloxane exhibited large periodic fluctuations in peak power, which originate from the excitation of high-order modes29.

This striking difference in behavior of self-trapped beams in the hydrogel and organosiloxane is clearly visualized in the temporal True/False plots presented in Fig. 6A, B. These trace the result of whether the peak power of Beam 2 was larger than the mean of the corresponding values of Beams 1 and 3 at any given point in time. These measurements were taken in the final 200 s of the experiment, where self-trapping had stabilized for all beams. For every experimental timepoint when Beam 2’s peak power was smaller than the mean of its neighbors, a value of False or “F” was recorded (red region). Conversely, if the peak power was greater, a value of True or “T” was recorded (green region). In the case of the hydrogel, a consistent F result was obtained. By sharp contrast, there was no clear pattern in the corresponding plot for the organosiloxane, which showed values of both True and False. In the organosiloxane, factors such as noise and asynchronicity in the oscillation of modes may momentarily affect the power of Beam 2 relative to that of its neighbors, causing fluctuations in the T/F result. Consistent depression of Beam 2’s peak power was observed only in the hydrogel. This comparison was repeated for all organosiloxane experiments; results are presented in Fig. S7.

The NAND logic gate

We built a NAND gate (representing the operation NOT AND) by exploiting the long-range, mutually inhibitive interactions of the three self-trapped beams in the hydrogel, which result in the consistent reduction in the peak power of Beam 2 (Fig. 5). Most basic logic gates take two binary inputs and return one binary output. In the case of NAND specifically, all inputs except [1, 1] return a value of 1, or True.

The scheme in Fig. 7 depicts the proposed working principles of the NAND gate, which were demonstrated in the corresponding experimental results (Fig. 8 and Table 1). A bit with a value of 1 represents a Boolean value of True, and is physically represented by a beam above a calculated threshold value (a beam that is “on”). (For discussion of how this threshold is calculated, see below.) Correspondingly, a 0 bit represents the Boolean value of False, and is physically represented by a beam below threshold (or a beam that is “off”). Desired inputs are launched into the hydrogel in the form of a beam pattern consisting of up to three collinear beams of equal intensity. The beams are allowed to self-trap and stabilize before readings are taken. As shown in Figs. 6A and S7, readings may be taken at any point in the 200 s stable period following self-trapping. In this work, the single timepoint closest to t = 200 s was chosen. The central beam (Beam 2) represents the output signal and is always launched. It represents the result of “A NAND B”, which is assessed after 200 s have elapsed. The result is assigned a value of 1 (or True) if the central beam’s maximum peak power, Msignal, is above a threshold value, Minput. Otherwise, it is assigned a value of 0, or False. We note that in this system, the absolute value of Msignal is only assessed relative to the two inputs. As long as the central beam achieves a lower maximum value than its neighbors (which is observed reproducibly in Table S1), it will be assigned a value of False. This ensures that the results are not dependent on statistical variations between multiple operations, which is critical for the chaining of sequential operations.

To perform “0 NAND 0”, only the signal beam is launched, which self-traps without inhibition. As the threshold Minput is 0, Msignal > Minput and a value of 1 is obtained. “1 NAND 0” and “0 NAND 1” are both represented by experiments with two beams: the signal beam, and one input beam. Although the beams exhibit some mutual inhibition, the signal beam is not notably darker than the input beam. Thus, Msignal > Minput and a value of 1 is obtained. Finally, for “1 NAND 1”, a signal beam and two input beams are launched. The signal beam is inhibited to a greater degree than its neighbors, resulting in Msignal < Minput and a result of 0. Thus, these results correspond to the behavior of a NAND gate. Discussion

The role of hydrogel properties in design of the NAND gate

Previously, we found that laser light propagating through a photoresponsive hydrogel produced self-trapped beams that underwent long-range interactions27. Specifically, these interactions mutually inhibited self-trapping within the material and thus diminished the intensity of light observed at the output of the system. Here, using three collinear beams, we showed that the relative geometric arrangement of these beams affected the degree of light inhibition. We demonstrated that beams with more than one nearest neighbor experience increased inhibition compared to those with only one. Taking advantage of this ability to correlate the optical input and output, we developed an all-optical NAND gate, which is essential for realizing all possible logic gates in the material.

The photochemical nature of this medium and the inherent interactions between light and this photoresponsive hydrogel are critical aspects of its functionality: it is the contraction of the soft, crosslinked polymer network that allows self-trapping to occur, and for the presence or absence of neighboring beams to communicate over distances much greater than the beam diameter. It is through this material-specific and uniquely photochemical interaction that the logic gate functionality was achieved. Although other materials may return the correct results for the [0, 0], [1, 0] and [0, 1] inputs in the NAND gate, long-range inhibition is necessary to obtain a result of False for an input of [1, 1], as can be seen by comparing the hydrogel and organosiloxane media (Fig. 6A, B).

Chaining of operations

Our findings provide a path to achieve the goal of sequentially chaining operations in the hydrogel medium. Experiments can be performed consecutively to represent multiple NAND logic gates, with the result of one operation being used to determine the inputs of subsequent operations within the same or different samples. For example, the AND operation can be represented by two NAND gates: A NAND B generates a result, C, which becomes the input of the second operation, C NAND C (Fig. 9A). We demonstrated 1 AND 1 experimentally, which can be seen in Fig. 9B. In the first experiment, three beams are launched to represent A = 1 and B = 1, and a result of 0 is obtained. This result, i.e., C = 0, is fed into the second experiment, where only a single beam is launched, yielding the final result of 1. A more complex operation, (1 XOR 1) XOR (0 XOR 0) based on 12 NAND experiments, was also performed. In prior work, we quantitatively demonstrated that self-trapping experiments in the hydrogels are reproducible, and that once the laser beam is removed, the hydrogel relaxes within seconds to its original, equilibrium state. This in turn allows the system to support repeat experiments ( > 45 limited only by the number of repeat experiments performed) without significant loss of intensity or damage to the material27. In our current study, we observed comparable reproducibility both within the same hydrogel sample and between different hydrogel samples. Consistent behavior was observed in the generation of a single self-trapped beam (Fig. S3) as well as the generation and interactions of two (Fig. S4), three (Figs. S5 and S7) and four (Fig. S6) self-trapped beams. It is this degree of reproducibility that enables results from the entire 27 experiments summarized in Table S1 to function as NAND gates—i.e., they all exhibit the expected relative changes in intensity described in Fig. 7. This indicates that consecutively chained operations—where the system relaxes back to its original state in the same sample27 or in different samples—would not suffer from significant cumulative loss of signal.

In the above-described sequential or consecutive chaining approach, the output of each operation must be relaunched as the input of the subsequent operation. In other words, n logic gates would require n consecutive experiments to be conducted within the same or separate gels. Importantly, this system also has the potential for simultaneous chaining of operations. Here, multiple operations occur simultaneously within a single experiment. This is because the outputs take the form of self-trapped beams of specific intensities; these beams inhibit neighboring beams to an extent determined by that intensity. To utilize this, the output beams of earlier operations must be geometrically arranged so that they serve as input beams for subsequent operations. This would therefore involve the simultaneous interactions of five or more beams all within the same hydrogel. As an example, Fig. 9C depicts three NAND gates (consisting of up to seven beams) arranged to form an AND gate, with outputs from earlier NAND gates (denoted M1 and M2) placed such that they function as inputs acting upon a final output, M3. The inhibitory interaction between beams must be tuned such that an output beam representing 0—i.e., a beam that has had its peak value lowered below threshold by interaction with inputs—is unable to inhibit (switch off) subsequent outputs (by bringing them below threshold). For the AND gate in Fig. 9C, when A = 0 and B = 0, neither intermediate output is attenuated, allowing the final output to be fully diminished below threshold (Fig. 9D(i)). This gives a result of 0. Some attenuation occurs when A = 1 or B = 1, but this is not sufficient to bring the intermediate outputs below threshold, so the final output is still diminished below threshold (Fig. 9D(ii)). When both A = 1 and B = 1, the intermediate outputs are attenuated, allowing the final output to remain above threshold, giving the desired reading of 1 (Fig. 9D(iii)).

Our observations of the dynamics of self-trapping showed that beam interactions and extent of inhibition can be tuned through careful choice of parameters, such as beam separation, geometry, polarization and initial input intensity. There is also some degree of freedom in defining the threshold reading that corresponds to a value of 1. In addition to varying beam power or beam-to-beam distances, the material’s composition is a key variable in the tuning of the degree of inhibition. The ability to modify interactions through parameters such as crosslinker density or chromophore content is a particular advantage of polymer-based photochemical media. Tuning of these parameters will be crucial to expand the possibilities of this system, and in particular, to combine multiple NAND gates simultaneously within the same experiment. Although self-trapping has been observed in other classes of media, many of these materials do not display the same degree of tunability or processability as polymers. More broadly, working in the nonlinear regime of photochemical, polymer-based media may present other advantages and opportunities to create light-responsive materials that modulate and process their own inputs, moving beyond simple on-off operations to complex functionality, and thereby true computational ability.

Methods

Materials

Acrylamide, N,N’-methylenebisacrylamide, ammonium persulfate, tetramethylethylenediamine, acryloyl chloride, and acrylic acid were used as obtained from Sigma Aldrich. 1’,3’,3’-Trimethyl-6-hydroxyspiro(2H-1-benzopyran-2,2’-indoline) was used as purchased from Acros Organics.

Sample preparation

A previously-described procedure was adapted to perform acrylation of the spiropyran chromophore using acryloyl chloride and 1’,3’,3’-trimethyl-6-hydroxyspiro(2H-1-benzopyran-2,2’-indoline)27. To prepare hydrogels, acrylamide (0.30 g), acrylic acid (0.30 g), N,N’-methylenebisacrylamide (0.03 g) and spiropyran acrylate (0.0076 g) were dissolved in 1.2 mL DMSO and heated to 55 °C. Ammonium persulfate (0.03 g) was dissolved in 0.3 mL deionised water. The monomer solution was removed from heat and the ammonium persulfate solution was added, followed by 30 μL of tetramethylethylenediamine. The combined solution was dispensed into 3D-printed molds and allowed to cure over 2 days, after which gels were removed and placed in 1 mM HCl for 1 week to remove residual monomer. Hydrogels were immersed in deionised water for at least 2 days prior to optical experiments.

Hydrogels for self-trapping experiments were rectangular prisms with XYZ dimensions of 10 mm × 10 mm × 4 mm. Prior to an experiment, each gel was placed in a 3D-printed holder with pathlength z = 6 mm and immersed in deionised water. As the hydrogels became swollen in water, their refractive index was estimated to be approximately 1.33, resulting in an optical path length of 5.32 mm.

The organosiloxane was prepared as previously described20,29. The sol was injected into cylindrical sample cells (z = 6 mm, diameter = 16 mm) and irradiated with diffuse white light for 5 min to increase initial viscosity prior to optical experiments with the laser.

Optical experiments

Optical experiments were conducted using the assembly shown in Fig. S9, where laser light from a solid-state Nd:YAG laser source (λ = 532 nm, Verdi V2, Coherent Inc.) is attenuated, passed through a polymer sample (denoted S), and captured using imaging lenses and a charge-coupled device camera (WinCamD, Dataray Inc.). Single beam and mutually incoherent double beam experiments were performed as previously described27. For mutually coherent three and four beam experiments, a beam stop was placed at Position I (Fig. S9), thus blocking the second arm of the beam from the remainder of the optical assembly. A diffractive optical element was placed at Position II, which split the single input beam (Arm 1) into three or four beams as needed.

To generate two mutually coherent beams, we selectively blocked the rightmost beam in a three-spot array. A beam stop and DOE were placed at Position I and II respectively to generate a three-spot pattern. An aperture was placed at Position III, flush with the sample entrance face. Its horizontal position was adjusted to selectively block only the rightmost beam, or Beam 3, in the spot pattern.

For all experiments in the hydrogel, optical powers were chosen such that each beam would have an incident power of 6.0 mW—e.g., an experiment with four beams had a total input power of 24.0 mW. For experiments with the organosiloxane, each beam had an incident power of 100 nW.

Data analysis

Optical experiments produced 2040 by 1530 arrays of analog-to-digital converter (ADC) values. These values are linearly correlated with the power incident on a given pixel of the charged-coupled device chip. Each 300 s experiment typically resulted in approximately 140 images. To track the maximum ADC values, we selected 80 μm by 80 μm regions of interest (ROI) centered around each beam position. The maximum reading within each ROI was recorded for the length of the experiment.

Horizontal profiles were obtained by plotting the ADC readings along the line that bisected the maximum ADC reading for the leftmost and rightmost beams. Vertical profiles were obtained by plotting the ADC readings along the line that bisected the maximum ADC reading for the beam under analysis. Beam diameters were measured from this data by performing a Gaussian fit and measuring the width using a 1/e2 threshold.
