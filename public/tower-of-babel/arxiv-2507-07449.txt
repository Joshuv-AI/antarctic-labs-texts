A sequence of compact metric spaces and an isometric embedding into the Gromov-Hausdorff space
Takuma Byakuno

Abstract
For a convergent series with positive terms, we prove that the ℓ ∞ \ell^{\infty} product space of bounded subspaces of the Gromov-Hausdorff space can be isometrically embedded into the Gromov-Hausdorff space, where each subspace consists of compact metric spaces with the diameter less than or equal to the term.

Mathematics Subject Classification
: Primary 30L05; Secondary 54E35, 53C23.

Key words and phrases
: Gromov-Hausdorff space, Isometric embeddings.

1
Introduction

The
Gromov-Hausdorff space
is a metric space consisting of a certain set
\mathcal{M}
and a distance function
d_{\mathrm{GH}}
.
All compact metric spaces belong essentially to the set
\mathcal{M}
, and
d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,Y)
is determined by the difference between the shapes of two compact metric spaces
X,Y
.

The distance function
d_{\mathrm{GH}}
is called the
Gromov-Hausdorff distance
, and we can easily calculate the Gromov-Hausdorff distance in several cases.
We can define a compact metric space
tX\in\mathcal{M}
as a scale transformation of
X\in\mathcal{M}
by
t>0
:
Let
d_{X}
be a distance function on
X
.
Then
tX
and its distance function
d_{tX}
are defined by

tX=X
, and
d_{tX}(x,y)=td_{X}(x,y)
respectively.
It is straightforward to see

(1)

d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,\mathbbm{1})=2^{-1}\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits X
for
X\in\mathcal{M}
,

(2)

d_{\mathrm{GH}}(tX,tY)=td_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,Y)
for
X,Y\in\mathcal{M}
and
t>0
,

(3)

d_{\mathrm{GH}}(tX,sX)=2^{-1}|t-s|\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X)
for
X\in\mathcal{M}
and
s,t>0
,

where
\mathbbm{1}
is a one-point metric space.

Now, we can consider the diameter as a function
\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
, and we can show this function is a 2-lipschitz function by (1):

|\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X)-\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(Y)|\leq 2d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,Y)\,.

We can also consider the Gromov-Hausdorff space as a cone with the vertex at
\mathbbm{1}
,
and the diameter function
\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
expresses the depth of this cone:
If we define the subset
\mathcal{B}_{s}
,
\mathcal{B}_{\leq s}
by

\mathcal{B}_{s}=\left\{X\in\mathcal{M}\,\middle|\,\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X)=s\right\}\ ,\quad\mathcal{B}_{\leq s}=\left\{X\in\mathcal{M}\,\middle|\,\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X)\leq s\right\}\,,

then
\mathcal{B}_{\leq s}
is a subcone over
\mathcal{B}_{s}
, See Figure
1
.
The isometry group of the Gromov-Hausdorff space is trivial by
[
3
]
.

Figure 1:
The Gromov-Hausdorff space.

It is known that the Gromov-Hausdorff space
\mathcal{M}
has a dense countable set, and
\mathcal{M}
is therefore separable.
In addition, we can show that
\mathcal{M}
is complete, but
\mathcal{M}
is not isometric to the Urysohn universal space.
See
[
1
, Section 3]
and
[
3
]
.
Recently, Nakajima, Yamauchi, and Zava studied the topological dimension of
\mathcal{M}
in
[
4
]
and calculated the topological dimension of
\mathcal{M}_{\leq n}
, which is a subspace consisting of compact metric spaces with at most
n
points.

In 2017, Iliadis, Ivanov, and Tuzhilin
[
1
]
showed that all finite metric spaces can be isometrically embedded into the Gromov-Hausdorff space, which means this space is universal for a class of all finite metric spaces.

According to
[
1
]
, there are many open problems related to the geometrical properties of
\mathcal{M}
.
Among them, in this paper, we focus on

Problem 1.1
.

Can we isometrically embed all compact metric spaces into the Gromov-Hausdorff space
\mathcal{M}
?

Iliadis, Ivanov, and Tuzhilin’s result can be considered as a partial answer to this problem, and this partial answer can be generalized to the following as an easy application of
[
1
, Theorem 4.1]
.

Proposition 1.2
.

All bounded metric subspaces of an
\ell^{\infty}
normed space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be isometrically embedded into the Gromov-Hausdorff space.

For topological embedding, Ishiki showed that the Hilbert cube can be topologically embedded into
\mathcal{M}
in
[
2
]
.

In this paper, we will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
c=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}r_{n}
be a convergent series with
r_{n}>0
.
Then the direct product set
\mathcal{B}_{\leq r}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{B}_{\leq r_{n}}
equipped with
\ell^{\infty}
(or supremum) distance can be embedded into
\mathcal{B}_{36c-3r_{1}}
.
Namely, we can construct a map
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}:\mathcal{B}_{\leq r}\to\mathcal{B}_{36c-3r_{1}}
satisfying

d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(Y))=\max_{n=1,2,\ldots}d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X_{n},Y_{n})

for all
X=(X_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty},Y=(Y_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\mathcal{B}_{\leq r}
.

This theorem implies

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
c=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}r_{n}
be a convergent series with
r_{n}>0
.
Then a subspace
\left\{(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\,\middle|\,0\leq x_{n}\leq r_{n}\right\}
of the space of bounded sequences
\ell^{\infty}
can be isometrically embedded into the Gromov-Hausdorff space.

2
Preliminaries

Let
X,Y
be sets, and
R
a subset of
X\times Y
.
For
A\subset X
, we define
R[A]
as an
image
of
A
:

R[A]=\left\{y\in Y\,\middle|\,\mathrm{there\ exists}\ a\in A\ \mathrm{such\ that}\ (a,y)\in R\right\},

and we define
R^{-1}
as an
inverse
of
R
:

R^{-1}=\left\{(y,x)\,\middle|\,(x,y)\in R\right\}\subset Y\times X.

Similarly, an
inverse image

R^{-1}[B]
is also defined.

Using this notation, we can define
\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(X,Y)
by
a set of all
R\subset X\times Y
satisfying

R[\{x\}]\neq\emptyset
and
R^{-1}[\{y\}]\neq\emptyset
for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
.
For
R\in\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(X,Y)
, we have
R^{-1}\in\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(X,Y)
.
The
distortion
of
R\in\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(X,Y)
is a real or infinite value

\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)=\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(X,Y;R)=\sup\left\{|d_{X}(x,\xi)-d_{Y}(y,\eta)|\,\middle|\,(x,y),(\xi,\eta)\in R\right\},

where
d_{X},d_{Y}
are the distance functions on
X,Y
, respectively.

The Gromov-Hausdorff distance is typically defined by using the “Hausdorff distance”, but we can alternatively define the
Gromov-Hausdorff distance
between two metric spaces
X,Y
by

d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\inf_{R\in\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(X,Y)}\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)\,.

We write the
diameter
of a metric space
(X,d)
as
\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X)
:

\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X)=\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X,d)=\sup_{x_{0},x_{1}\in X}d(x_{0},x_{1}).

Then the Gromov-Hausdorff distance satisfies several distance axioms:

(1)

0\leq d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,Y)\leq\max\{\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X),\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(Y)\}
for all metric spaces
X,Y
,

(2)

d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,Y)=d_{\mathrm{GH}}(Y,X)
for all metric spaces
X,Y
,

(3)

d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,Y)\leq d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,Z)+d_{\mathrm{GH}}(Z,Y)
for all metric spaces
X,Y,Z
,

(4)

For all compact metric spaces
X,Y
,

d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X,Y)=0
if and only if
X
is isometric to
Y
.

Therefore,
d_{\mathrm{GH}}
is a distance function on a set
\mathcal{M}
satisfying

(M1)

For every
X\in\mathcal{M}
,
X
is a (nonempty) compact metric space,

(M2)

For an arbitrary compact metric space
X
,
we can find a suitable
Y\in\mathcal{M}
such that
Y
is isometric to
X
,

(M3)

For every
X,Y\in\mathcal{M}
, we obtain
X=Y
if
X
is isometric to
Y
.

There exists such a set
\mathcal{M}
since an arbitrary compact metric space can be isometrically embedded into the space of bounded sequences
\ell^{\infty}
.
The metric space
(\mathcal{M},d_{\mathrm{GH}})
is called the
Gromov-Hausdorff space
.
By the definition of
\mathcal{M}
, for a compact metric space
X
, there exists a unique
Y\in\mathcal{M}
such that
X
is isometric to
Y
.
Then we may consider
[X]=Y
.

For simplicity, we write
\omega
for the ordinal number of the well-ordered set
\{0,1,\ldots\}
(the set of all non-negative integers):

0\leq 1\leq 2\leq\cdots\leq n\leq\cdots\leq\omega\leq\omega+1\leq\cdots\,.

Let
\{r_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
(
\Lambda
is a set) be a family of points such that
r_{\lambda}
is a non-negative real number for each
\lambda\in\Lambda
.
Then we can also define a series of

\{r_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
by

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}r_{\lambda}=\sup\left\{\sum_{\lambda\in P}r_{\lambda}\,\middle|\,P\ \mathrm{is\ a\ finite\ subset\ of}\ \Lambda\right\}\,.

This paper uses this notation only if
\Lambda
consists of ordinal numbers.
For instance, if the sequence
(r_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
has the convergent series, we have

\sum_{0\leq\lambda<\omega+1}=r_{0}+\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}r_{n}\right)+r_{\omega}\,.

Proof of Theorem
1.3

Firstly, we shall prove Theorem
1.3
and remark on an isometric embedding of a subspace consisting of a compact metric space with constant Hausdorff dimension.
Finally, we shall prove Corollary
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
c=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}r_{n}
be a convergent series with
r_{n}>0
.
Then we set
r^{\prime}_{n}=r_{n}+r_{n+1}
, and
we define
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X)\in\mathcal{M}
for
X=(X_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\mathcal{B}_{\leq r}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{B}_{\leq r_{n}}
: Firstly, we write
X_{0},X_{\omega},X_{\omega+1}
for one-point metric spaces, where
X_{0},X_{\omega},X_{\omega+1}
have one points
p_{0},p_{\omega},p_{\omega+1}
and distance functions
d_{0},d_{\omega},d_{\omega+1}
, respectively.
We also write a distance function on
X_{n}
as
d_{n}
.
Then we put
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X)=\bigsqcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}X_{\lambda}
, and take a unique distance function
d_{X}
on
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X)
such that
d_{X}
is an extension of
d_{\lambda}
for all
0\leq\lambda\leq\omega+1
and satisfies

d_{X}(x_{\alpha},x_{\beta})=3\sum_{\alpha\leq\lambda<\beta}r^{\prime}_{\lambda}

for
\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda
with
\alpha<\beta
,
x_{\alpha}\in X_{\alpha}
and
x_{\beta}\in X_{\beta}
, where
r^{\prime}_{0}=2c
and
r^{\prime}_{\omega}=8c
.
This metric space
(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X),d_{X})
looks like Figure
2
.

Figure 2:
The definition of
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X)
.

The metric space is compact, thus we can consider
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X)=[\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X)]\in\mathcal{M}
.
We also have
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X)\in\mathcal{B}_{36c-3r_{1}}
:

\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X))=d_{X}(p_{0},p_{\omega+1})=6c+6c-3r_{1}+24c=36c-3r_{1}\,.

We shall prove that
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}:\mathcal{B}_{\leq r}\to\mathcal{B}_{36c-3r_{1}}
is an isometric embedding, that is,
d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(Y))=\sup_{n=1,2,\ldots}d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X_{n},Y_{n})
for
X=(X_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty},Y=(Y_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\mathcal{B}_{\leq r}
.
We put
S=\sup_{n=1,2,\ldots}d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X_{n},Y_{n})
, and note that
X_{0}=Y_{0}=\{p_{0}\}
,
X_{\omega}=Y_{\omega}=\{p_{0}\}
and
X_{\omega+1}=Y_{\omega+1}=\{p_{0}\}
.

For
R_{\lambda}\in\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(X_{\lambda},Y_{\lambda})
(
0\leq\lambda\leq\omega+1
), we have
R=\bigcup_{0\leq\lambda\leq\omega+1}R_{\lambda}\in\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(Y))
and
\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)\leq\sup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R_{\lambda})=\sup_{n=1,2,\ldots}\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R_{n})
,
since
\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R_{0})=\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R_{\omega})=\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R_{\omega+1})=0
.
Thus
d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(Y))\leq S
.

Now, we may assume
S\neq 0
.
Since
\max\{\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X_{n}),\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(Y_{n})\}\leq r_{n}
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{n}=0
, There exists a number
N
such that
d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X_{n},Y_{n})<S
for
n\geq N
.
Therefore, we can take a number
K=1,2,\ldots
such that
S=d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X_{K},Y_{K})
.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(Y))<S
.
Then there exists
R\in\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(Y))
such that
\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)<2S
.

For
y_{0}\in R[X_{0}]
and
y_{\omega+1}\in R[X_{\omega+1}]
, we shall prove
y_{0}\in Y_{0}
and
y_{\omega+1}\in Y_{\omega+1}
.
Since
\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)<2S\leq 2c
,

d_{Y}(y_{0},y_{\omega+1})\geq d_{X}(p_{0},p_{\omega+1})-\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)>(36c-3r_{1})-2c=34c-3r_{1}.

If there exists an ordinal number
\lambda
such that
y_{0},y_{\omega+1}\in Y_{\lambda}
, we have

34c-3r_{1}<d_{Y}(y_{0},y_{\omega+1})=d_{\lambda}(y_{0},y_{\omega+1})\leq\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(Y_{\lambda})\leq c\,,

which is a contradiction. Thus, there are ordinal numbers
\alpha,\beta
such that
y_{0}\in Y_{\alpha}
and
y_{\omega+1}\in Y_{\beta}
, but
\alpha\neq\beta
. If
\max\{\alpha,\beta\}<\omega+1
, then we have

34c-3r_{1}<d_{Y}(y_{0},y_{\omega+1})=3\sum_{\min\{\alpha,\beta\}\leq\lambda<\max\{\alpha,\beta\}}r^{\prime}_{\lambda}\leq 3\sum_{0\leq\lambda<\omega}r^{\prime}_{\lambda}=12c-3r_{1}\,,

which is a contradiction to
c>0
. Therefore
\max\{\alpha,\beta\}=\omega+1
.
In addition, if
0<\min\{\alpha,\beta\}
, then

34c-3r_{1}<d_{Y}(y_{0},y_{\omega+1})=3\sum_{\min\{\alpha,\beta\}\leq\lambda<\omega+1}r^{\prime}_{\lambda}\leq 3\sum_{0<\lambda\leq\omega+1}r^{\prime}_{\lambda}=30c-3r_{1}\,,

which is also a contradiction to
c>0
. As a consequence, we have
\max\{\alpha,\beta\}=\omega+1
and
\min\{\alpha,\beta\}=0
.

Suppose
\alpha=\omega+1
and
\beta=0
, that is,
y_{0}\in Y_{\omega+1}
and
y_{\omega+1}\in Y_{0}
.
Taking
y_{\omega}\in R[X_{\omega}]
, we have

d_{Y}(y_{\omega},y_{\omega+1})\geq d_{X}(p_{\omega},p_{\omega+1})-\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)>24c-2c=22c\,.

If
y_{\omega}\in Y_{0}
, we have
c<d_{Y}(y_{\omega},y_{\omega+1})\leq\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(Y_{0})=0
, which is a contradiction.
In addition, if
y_{0}\in Y_{\delta}
(
0<\delta<\omega+1
), we can also obtain a contradiction:

22c<d_{Y}(y_{\omega},y_{\omega+1})=3\sum_{0\leq\lambda<\delta}r^{\prime}_{\lambda}<3\sum_{0\leq\lambda<\omega}r^{\prime}_{\lambda}=12c-3r_{1}\,.

Therefore
\alpha=0
and
\beta=\omega+1
since
\min\{\alpha,\beta\}=0
and
\max\{\alpha,\beta\}=\omega+1
.
It means
y_{0}\in Y_{0}
and
y_{\omega+1}\in Y_{\omega+1}
.

For
x_{K}\in X_{K}
and
y_{K}\in R[\{x_{K}\}]
, we shall prove
y_{K}\in Y_{K}
.
There exists an ordinal number
\delta
such that
y_{K}\in Y_{\delta}
.
Since
y_{0}\in Y_{0}
and

d_{Y}(y_{0},y_{K})\geq d_{X}(p_{0},x_{K})-\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(R)>3\sum_{0\leq\lambda<K}r^{\prime}_{\lambda}-2c>6c-2c=4c>0\,,

we have
\delta>0
. Therefore, if
\delta\neq K
, we have
3r_{K}\leq\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)<2S
:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)\geq

\displaystyle|d_{Y}(y_{0},y_{K})-d_{X}(p_{0},x_{K})|=\left|3\sum_{0\leq\lambda<\delta}r^{\prime}_{\lambda}-3\sum_{0\leq\lambda<K}r^{\prime}_{\lambda}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\displaystyle 3\sum_{K\leq\lambda<\delta}r^{\prime}_{\lambda}&\mathrm{if:}K<\delta\\
\displaystyle 3\sum_{\delta\leq\lambda<K}r^{\prime}_{\lambda}&\mathrm{if:}\delta<K\end{cases}\geq\begin{cases}\displaystyle 3r^{\prime}_{K}&\mathrm{if:}K<\delta\\
\displaystyle 3r^{\prime}_{K-1}&\mathrm{if:}\delta<K\end{cases}\geq 3r_{K}\,.

However, Since we put
S=d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X_{K},Y_{K})
,

2S=2d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X_{K},Y_{K})\leq 2\max\{\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X_{K}),\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(Y_{K})\}\leq 2r_{K}<3r_{K}<2S\,.

Thus, we have
\delta=K
, that is,
y_{K}\in Y_{K}
.

Consequently, we have
R[X_{K}]\subset Y_{K}
.
In addition, we can obtain
R^{-1}[Y_{K}]\subset X_{K}
by
\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R^{-1})=\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)
similarly.
Therefore we can define
R_{K}\in\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(X_{K},Y_{K})
by
R_{K}=R\cap(X_{K}\times Y_{K})\in\mathop{\mathrm{GHC}}\nolimits(X_{K},Y_{K})
.
Since
R_{K}\subset R
,

S=d_{\mathrm{GH}}(X_{K},Y_{K})\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R_{K})\leq\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{dis}}\nolimits(R)<S\,,

which is a contradiction. Hence
d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X),\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(Y))=S
.
∎

Remark
.

The metric space
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X)
is the disjoint union of
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a subset of
\mathcal{M}
such that
\bigsqcup_{0\leq\lambda\leq\omega+1}X_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{M}
for
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots\in\mathcal{X}
and
X_{0}=X_{\omega}=X_{\omega+1}=\mathbbm{1}
.
Then, putting
\mathcal{X}_{r}=\left\{X\in\mathcal{X}\,\middle|\,\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(X)\leq r\right\}
, we can isometrically embed
\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{X}_{r_{n}}
into
\mathcal{X}
.
For example,

\mathcal{W}_{a}=\left\{X\in\mathcal{M}\,\middle|\,\mathrm{the\ Hausdorff\ dimension\ of}\ X\ \mathrm{is}\ a\right\}\,,\quad a\geq 0\,.

Now, we define
\dim_{H}(X)
as the Hausdorff dimension of
X
.
By the countable stability of the Hausdorff dimension, we have

\dim_{H}(\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X))=\sup_{0\leq\lambda\leq\omega+1}\dim_{H}(X_{\lambda})=\sup_{n=1,2,\ldots}\dim_{H}(X_{n})\,,

that is
\mathop{\mathfrak{S}}\nolimits_{r}(X)\in\mathcal{W}_{a}
for
X=(X_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(\mathcal{W}_{a})_{r_{n}}
.
Therefore,
\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(\mathcal{W}_{a})_{r_{n}}
can be isometrically embedded into
\mathcal{W}_{a}
.

Proof of Corollary
1.4
.

Let
\mathbbm{2}=\{-1,1\}
be a subspace of the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}
.
Then, for
s,t>0
,

d_{\mathrm{GH}}(s\mathbbm{2},t\mathbbm{2})=|s-t|\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\mathrm{diam}}\nolimits(\mathbbm{2})=|s-t|\frac{|1-(-1)|}{2}=|s-t|.

Hence, we can isometrically embed
\left\{(x_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\,\middle|\,0\leq x_{n}\leq r_{n}\right\}
into
\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{B}_{\leq r_{n}}
, and into
\mathcal{B}_{36c-3r_{1}}\subset\mathcal{M}
by Theorem
1.3
.
∎
