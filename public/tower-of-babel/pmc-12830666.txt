Development for soft actuator with multilayer structure and its application
Authors: Gyeongji Kang, Kahye Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMost actuators require considerable degrees of freedom to perform tasks effectively, which often leads to larger size and reduced portability. As an alternative, soft actuators have been introduced, offering compact form, high maneuverability, and multiple modes of motion. In this study, a Hydraulically Amplified Self-Healing Electrostatics (HASEL) actuator was configured in a layered structure to achieve practical usability and efficient movement. The actuator operates through hydraulic pressure and volumetric expansion generated under high voltage. It was fabricated in a simple rectangular design, and a new type of soft actuator was realized by stacking single units to amplify angular displacement and output force. Precise control is not required, since the gripper’s performance can be tuned by adjusting the number and arrangement of layers. The system can also be adapted for different applications by producing and combining various supporting frames. This paper presents two types of soft grippers based on the layered HASEL actuator, which are expected to be useful in fields such as smart farming systems that demand both adaptability and mobility.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

Actuators are fundamental components in robotic systems, crucial for generating motion and force1. While rigid actuators have historically been the cornerstone of robotics due to robust performance and precise control2,3, their inherent stiffness and limited degrees of freedom pose challenges for delicate or adaptive manipulation4–6. This limitation is particularly evident in scenarios involving irregularly shaped or fragile objects, where precise force control is critical to avoid damage.

In response to these challenges, soft actuators have emerged as a promising alternative to traditional rigid designs7–9. Constructed from compliant and flexible materials, they can offer superior adaptability, enabling them to conform to and securely interact with objects of various shapes and sizes10–12. This flexibility makes soft actusuators particularly useful for handling delicate or fragile items. However, many existing soft actuators, especially those based on hydraulic or pneumatic principles, suffer from limited mobility, noise, and bulky drive hardware, which constrains use in compact or mobile systems13,14.

Electrohydraulic HASEL actuators have recently been proposed to address some of these limitations15–20. By combining electrostatic forces with dielectric fluids, HASEL actuators can achieve a high payload-to-weight ratio, rapid response, and mechanical flexibility due to their lightweight construction. In addition, their straightforward manufacturing process allows easy customization of shape and size to meet specific application needs, and their self-healing behavior under electrical breakdown enables reliable and repeatable gripping without complex control systems17. Compact implementations using lightweight high-voltage components broaden their application potential in mobile and space-constrained platforms21,22.

Most existing HASEL studies, however, have focused on linear stroke and blocking force, and stacked configurations have been used primarily to increase volume change and output20,21. In the literature, reported angular motions are typically in the tens of degrees: approximately 70° for hinge-type or Peano-HASEL joints and around 10° for modular pouch-twisting designs23,24. Fold-based electrohydraulic actuators can achieve ~ 170° in a single unit, but these demonstrations primarily emphasize angle generation rather than load characterization25. Taken together, these reports highlight the need for HASEL-based architectures that explicitly target large, controllable angular displacement under load in a compact form suitable for integration into practical grippers.

To address this gap, we propose a multilayer HASEL architecture that converts constrained volumetric expansion into amplified angular displacement. Earlier layered HASEL assemblies used linearly stacked donut units to amplify blocking force, which is useful for pressing in confined spaces but not designed to produce large rotational motion20. In contrast, the present method constrains each HASEL unit to expand in a prescribed direction and stacks such units so that distributed curvature accumulates over an effective lever arm, providing a volume-driven route to rotation that is distinct from hinge- or fold-based designs.

Traditional single-layer HASEL designs generate electrostatic pressure and produce volume expansion15,17,26. Simply increasing the fluid volume in a single-layer enlarges electrode separation and can reduce the effective electrostatic force. To address this limitation, our design employs a multilayer structure inspired by layered pneumatic actuators and dielectric elastomer actuators, which increase motion and force by stacking individual layers27–37. By distributing the active volume across multiple layers, the proposed multilayer HASEL maintains effective electric fields while amplifying angular motion, and it retains the simple fabrication and adaptability characteristic of HASEL actuators.

In this study, a soft and modular gripper using this actuator is demonstrated, capable of grasping fragile objects without precise control. The actuator is straightforward to fabricate and can be customized in shape and size. Under high voltage, it expands to produce substantial output and passively recovers when deactivated. Its lightweight and flexible nature, combined with its high output-to-weight ratio, make it a promising candidate for practical gripper applications requiring usability and versatility.

Experimental setup and design

In this study, three types of layered structure actuators were fabricated, single-layer, 2-layer, and 4-layer configurations. A voltage of 10 kV was applied using a high-voltage supply (MK3003P, MKPOWER, Korea), inducing electrostatic forces on the electrodes and causing the surfaces coated with carbon tape to adhere to each other. This interaction generated hydraulic force, resulting in the expansion of the non-electrode regions. In all stacked configurations, the electrode layers were electrically connected in parallel and driven by a single high-voltage source. Therefore, all layers in a given stack were actuated simultaneously, and selective activation of a subset of layers was not implemented in the present design.

The performance of actuators depends on various factors, such as the width-to-length ratio, the electrode-to-total-area ratio, and the amount of internal dielectric fluid. To isolate the effects of stacking, this study fixed the actuator size, electrode size, and dielectric fluid quantity, varying only the number of actuator layers. Angular displacement and performance differences were analyzed across these configurations.

Displacement was measured using a laser displacement sensor (IL-100, Keyence, Japan) positioned vertically above an actuator placed horizontally on a flat surface (Fig. 1). Because the dielectric fluid is transparent, a small paper marker was attached to the top layer to ensure reliable detection. The laser tracked a single point near the mid-chamber on the side opposite the electrode (center of the expanding region). This location was chosen because (i) the electrode-adjacent area undergoes zipping without meaningful angular change, (ii) the far tip exhibits large rotation and is difficult to track, and (iii) the mid-chamber provides sufficient expansion while remaining trackable. The voltage-off state served as the baseline. At large rotations, the tracked point follows a curved rather than purely vertical trajectory. As a result, the laser captures only the vertical projection, underestimating the true displacement.

Additionally, to utilize the actuators as grippers, two types of supporting frames were fabricated: a silicone frame with an assembled structure and a 3D-printed frame with a hinge structure. By combining each frame with the layered actuators, two distinct types of soft grippers were developed.

Single/multiple layered actuator fabrication method

The actuator designed for this study features a rectangular configuration with dimensions of 20 mm in width and 40 mm in length. According to Acome et al.20, the ratio of the electrode area to the total actuator area is a critical factor influencing the performance of HASEL actuators20. To determine the optimal configuration, preliminary experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects of actuator size, electrode ratio, and operating frequency on displacement. Both the horizontal and vertical of the actuator showed the difference in the amount of reaction according to the length change, and regardless of the size of the actuator, it showed that the peak was constantly reached according to the cycle of the input waveform. This size was found to provide an optimal balance between generating sufficient electrostatic force and maintaining structural stability. Based on these results, the 20 mm × 40 mm rectangular design was selected as the final test target due to its superior displacement performance and suitability for gripper applications.

The actuator is composed of Biaxially Oriented Polypropylene (BOPP) film, conductive double-sided carbon tape, and dielectric fluid (FR3, Cargill, USA) (Fig. 2a). BOPP was chosen for its high dielectric strength, low leakage current, and mechanical robustness, making it a reliable barrier film for electrohydraulic actuation. The fabrication process begins by bonding two BOPP films together using a heat press machine (HP3805, Xinhong Mechanical & Electrical Co., Ltd., China). A 20 mm × 40 mm mold was employed to create an unsealed gap for the injection of dielectric fluid, ensuring precise dimensional consistency. The gap was then filled with FR3, a soybean-oil-based insulating fluid selected for its favorable dielectric properties (relative permittivity ~ 3.2, breakdown strength ~ 45 kV at 2.5 mm, and viscosity ~ 33 cSt at 40 °C). These characteristics provide strong electrostatic forces and stable fluid mobility for actuation. In addition, FR3 has a high fire point (~ 316 °C), ensuring safe operation under high-voltage conditions. It is also biodegradable, offering a sustainable alternative for long-term use in soft robotic systems.

(a) Schematic representation of a single actuator. (b) Process for fabricating a multilayer actuator. (c) Comparison of the multilayer actuator in its OFF and ON states.

To optimize actuator performance, the volume of the dielectric fluid was calculated based on the assumption that it would expand into a cylindrical shape when the actuator is fully activated, consistent with the methodology described by Acome et al.20. To achieve optimal performance, 1.3 ml of dielectric fluid—representing 50% of its maximum expansion volume—was precisely injected into each actuator using a micropipette. This careful control ensures consistent performance across actuators, preventing overexpansion and preserving functional integrity. Following the fluid injection, a 20 mm × 20 mm section of conductive double-sided carbon tape was applied to the upper surface of both sides of the BOPP film, serving as electrodes. This electrode configuration ensures uniform electric field distribution across the dielectric layer, which is critical for efficient actuation.

For the fabrication of multilayered actuators, individual single-layer actuators were sequentially stacked and adhered using the conductive carbon tape. This modular assembly method facilitates scalability of the actuator system (Fig. 2b). To ensure safety and prevent electrical short circuits, an additional layer of BOPP film was adhered to the outermost electrode surfaces on both ends of the multilayer actuator. This protective layer enhances the reliability and operational safety of the actuator during use. As illustrated in Fig. 2c, the multilayer actuator remains flat in the OFF state, with the dielectric fluid distributed in the pouch region. When a high voltage is applied (ON state), electrostatic attraction between the opposing electrodes causes the electrode regions to contract and displaces the fluid into the non-electrode pouch region, leading to out-of-plane expansion that accumulates across layers and generates bending deformation.

Single/multiple layered actuator

To enable versatile combinations of layered actuators, a modular soft gripper was designed for easy assembly and disassembly. For stable movement and controlled speed during both actuation and recovery phases, the gripper’s frame was fabricated using flexible silicone. The frame features a rectangular parallelepiped design, with its upper section divided into six equal segments separated by 1 mm intervals. Each segment includes holes to accommodate the insertion of layered actuators. The gripper frame was fabricated using Ecoflex 00–30, a soft, elastic silicone with excellent tear resistance and biocompatibility, enabling stable flexion in response to the angular displacement of the inflating actuators and safe interaction with delicate objects. Molds for the frame were created using a 3D printer, ensuring consistent and repeatable fabrication of identical gripper units. The molds were fabricated with a ProJet HD3500 Plus printer (3D Systems, Inc.) using VisiJet M3 Crystal as the build material and VisiJet S300 as the support material. VisiJet M3 Crystal, an acrylic-based photopolymer with high dimensional stability and a tensile strength of ~ 45–50 MPa, provided sufficient rigidity to ensure gripping stability while remaining lightweight.

For the experimental evaluation, actuators with 2-layer and 4-layer stacks were employed. By systematically varying the number and position of the layered actuators, the study investigated optimal configurations to achieve desired angular displacement and effective performance as a gripper. The experiments focused on comparing angular changes and assessing the gripper’s ability to securely grasp objects under different configurations.

Manufacture of a double-bent soft gripper

The double-bent soft gripper was developed to utilize the unique capability of single actuators to bend in multiple directions and the amplified angular deformation achieved through stacking multiple actuators. Based on experimental results, the 4-layer actuator was selected for its optimal balance between angular displacement and force, making it particularly suitable for a two-axis gripper design. The gripper integrates components made from acrylic, silicone, and 3D-printed materials to achieve structural stability and functional flexibility.

The gripper’s assembly began with the fabrication of a rigid acrylic base, designed to provide structural support. Onto this base, the actuators and silicone parts were layered in sequence. Thread was used to secure the connection between the acrylic base and the silicone components, ensuring stability during operation. The silicone parts were engineered with 2 mm intervals at the bending points of the actuator, allowing them to flex naturally in sync with the actuator’s movements. At the distal end of the silicone layer, a hole was created to insert an acrylic plate, which was then fixed in place with thread to support the second actuator. This ensured stable and reliable operation even during complex movements.

Elastic thread was employed to secure the actuators firmly while allowing natural movement as they changed angles during operation. To minimize any restrictions on angular movement, only the electrode portion of the actuator—an area that does not expand—was fixed to the structure. For the second actuator, the electrode section was anchored to the silicone layer. A grooved silicone part, 2 mm thick and designed for gripping, was placed on top of this layer. The upper and lower silicone components were then connected using elastic thread, ensuring flexibility and precise alignment. The final step involved mounting two identical twin-axis actuators onto a hinge-structured frame fabricated with a 3D printer. This modular frame provided the necessary flexibility and structural integrity to accommodate the angular deformation generated by the layered actuators.

For the single actuator, which serves as the baseline, an angular change of 11.7° was observed (Fig. 3a,b). The 2-layer actuator exhibited angular changes of 16.7° and 17.3° in each layer, resulting in a total angular change of 34.0°, approximately 2.9 times greater than that of the single actuator (Fig. 3c,d). The 4-layer actuator demonstrated angular changes of 20.2°, 26.2°, 26.4°, and 20.3° across its layers, culminating in a total angular change of 93.1°, which is approximately 7.9 times the angular change of the single actuator (Fig. 3e,f). These results confirm that stacking actuators produces a more significant amplification of angular change compared to using a single actuator, rather than merely providing a proportional increase in bending.

In terms of displacement, the single actuator showed a change of approximately 1.7 mm. The 2-layer actuator demonstrated a displacement of 4.7 mm, about 2.7 times that of the single actuator, while the 4-layer actuator exhibited a displacement of 8.8 mm, roughly 5 times greater (Fig. 3g). The data demonstrate a clear increase in overall displacement with additional layers, suggesting an effective per-layer amplification.

All three actuators exhibited a similar post-actuation response: a brief oscillation after power-off followed by gradual stabilization. Although all returned to the initial state, the recovery time differed slightly. The single and 2-layer actuators took longer to fully recover, not returning to their pre-actuation state until approximately 6 s after power was cut off. In contrast, the 4-layer actuator recovered more rapidly, returning to its original state within the same timeframe. This faster recovery in the 4-layer actuator is likely due to the increased volume of dielectric fluid, which reduces the electrostatic forces and, combined with the steeper angle of deflection, allows for a smoother descent.

Soft layered actuators

To evaluate the gripper’s performance based on the number and position of layered actuators, we tested various combinations of 2-layer and 4-layer actuators inserted into a silicone frame (Fig. 4). The curvature was measured before and after applying voltage to compare the bending degree, which varies depending on the placement of the actuators. To further assess the gripper’s performance, we simultaneously actuated two opposing pairs of layered actuators mounted in the silicone frame to grasp an object. The test used a strawberry model (≈ 45 mm × 50 mm × 57 mm, 17 g) positioned between the gripper immediately prior to voltage application (Fig. 4i–k). Both stacking orders successfully grasped the model without slippage. However, the 4–2 combination produced greater curvature near the upper contact region holding the object and consequently yielded a more secure grip.

Finally, the results indicated that the curvature differed when testing the actuators alone compared to when gripping an object. This variation is likely due to the silicone’s frictional characteristics, which fix the lower end of the gripper, while the upper end is slightly compressed by the object’s weight, leading to a decrease in curvature. It was confirmed that having more layers near the gripping region enhances both curvature and gripping force, making the gripper more effective.

A double-bent soft gripper

The inherent material properties of the soft actuator provide a high degree of freedom in movement, which complicates precise directional control. To mitigate this limitation, a layered configuration was employed as a double-bent actuator, leveraging its ability to move effectively in a single direction.

Before constructing the gripper, the deformation behavior of a double-bent structure composed of two four-layer stacked actuators was examined under applied voltage (Fig. 5a,b). When oriented vertically, the internal dielectric fluid was displaced downward by gravity, reducing the effective hydraulic pressure compared to horizontal operation. Despite this decrease in overall angular change, which was affected by both gravity and the elastic force of the securing thread, the actuators achieved angular expansions of 24.8° and 58.2° from the base, confirming their effective actuation performance (Fig. 5b).

(a) Double-bent actuator in the OFF state. (b) Double-bent actuator in the ON state. Grasping of (c) a tube of hand cream, (d) a strawberry model, (e) an insulting tape roll with two pairs of double-bent actuators in the ON state.

To adapt the double-bent actuator for gripping applications, two pairs of twin-axis actuators were mounted onto a hinge-structured frame, creating a functional gripper capable of grasping and manipulating objects. In addition, silicone tips were attached to the ends of the frame to increase friction during grasping and to prevent damage to the target objects. This configuration enables lightweight yet stable operation and allows effective gripping of various objects.

The gripper’s performance and applicability were evaluated using several objects with distinct shapes and surface properties. The gripper successfully and securely grasped a hand cream tube (Fig. 5c), a strawberry model (Fig. 5d), and an insulating tape roll (Fig. 5e), demonstrating its adaptability to cylindrical, soft, and rigid objects, respectively. These results qualitatively confirm that the proposed gripper can handle objects with diverse geometries, stiffness, and surface textures without structural modification of the actuator itself. Furthermore, the silicone tips can be easily replaced or redesigned to suit different operational requirements. This modularity enables broader adaptability, extending beyond fragile fruits to a wide range of smart farming and automation environments.

Conclusion

A performance evaluation was conducted to assess the application of a single-shaped rectangular HASEL actuator in a soft gripper through multilayer stacking. The results highlight that the actuator is compact, lightweight, and straightforward to manufacture. Even when multiple layers are stacked, it retains its lightweight characteristics while producing greater displacement and angular change than a single layer. Under the tested conditions, the actuators operated at high voltage and returned to their initial state after power-off.

Experiments with 2- and 4-layer configurations demonstrated significant improvements in angular displacement and corresponding linear displacement compared with a single-layer actuator. These observations suggest strong potential for applications requiring precise motion and controlled force output. The modular soft-gripper design also allows easy replacement of defective actuators by inserting and joining new units, which improves usability and maintenance. Moreover, the ability to modify the gripper frame supports adaptation to diverse tasks and indicates potential use in other soft-actuator systems demanding high efficiency and adaptability.

Despite these promising results, there are limitations to the current approach that merit consideration. The performance of HASEL actuators can be influenced by environmental factors such as temperature, humidity, and exposure to contaminants. High temperatures, for instance, may alter the properties of the dielectric fluid and silicone components, affecting the actuator’s reliability. Incorporating materials with enhanced thermal and environmental resistance could mitigate these challenges. Another potential limitation is the reliance on high voltage for operation, which poses safety concerns and limits scalability, particularly for portable or low-power systems. Developing alternative methods to reduce voltage requirements could significantly broaden their usability. To ensure safe operation under high electric fields, multilayer insulation and encapsulation around the electrode regions can be employed to prevent dielectric breakdown. In addition, the required driving voltage could be substantially reduced by using high-permittivity dielectric materials such as PVDF and by optimizing film thickness.

Furthermore, periodic polarity switching between the positive and negative electrodes could maintain the same effective electric field while reducing the required operating voltage by nearly half. This approach is also expected to accelerate the release response by enabling faster charge–discharge transitions. Such a driving method could improve energy efficiency while mitigating thermal and electrical stress, thereby enhancing the overall safety and practicality of the actuator system. Together with the aforementioned material and insulation strategies, this approach could make HASEL actuators more suitable for real-world applications.

Frequent deformation and recovery cycles may also lead to material wear over time, and durability under repeated actuation is an essential requirement for practical applications. Although long-term cycle testing was not conducted in this study, our goal was to establish a proof-of-concept for angular displacement control and demonstrate the feasibility of the actuator as a gripper. Systematic durability assessments will therefore be an important focus of future work. The present evaluation was also limited to a strawberry model as a representative fragile object. While this served as a meaningful demonstration, further validation with a broader range of fruits and vegetables (e.g., tomatoes, mushrooms, apples) will be necessary to fully demonstrate the versatility for smart farming applications.

Consistent with established principles and prior studies, stacking can be interpreted as increasing the span over which curvature accumulates () and redistributing the through-thickness stiffness, as curvature follows 17,23. Additionally, geometry and electrode layout may influence the local electric field and, through Maxwell stress, the resulting pressure ( E2)15,17,23. This interpretation is proposed at the hypothesis level and is not claimed as experimentally verified in this work. Building on this hypothesis, future designs with separately wired layers could enable selective layer actuation, allowing systematic tuning of angular displacement and stiffness in multilayer stacks.

Future work will focus on extending both the capabilities of the system and its range of applications. Designs could evolve to include multi-axis configurations for more complex, precise motion. Embedded sensing could provide real-time feedback on force, displacement, and environmental conditions for more adaptive control. Furthermore, exploring new materials with improved elasticity, durability, and resistance to environmental factors could enhance performance while ensuring long-term reliability. Efforts to optimize energy usage, such as reducing voltage requirements or integrating renewable energy solutions, could improve portability and energy efficiency. These directions would strengthen the utility of soft grippers and open opportunities in biomedical devices, wearable robotics, and industrial automation, where adaptability, precision, and safety are critical.

Overall, the findings of this study highlight the adaptability and modularity of HASEL actuators, providing a robust foundation for future exploration. Their potential to improve robotic and automated systems is particularly promising in areas requiring flexibility, mobility, and the gentle handling of objects, such as smart farming and other delicate operational environments.
