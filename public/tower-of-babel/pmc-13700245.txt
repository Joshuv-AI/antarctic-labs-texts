Renal tubular injury-repair imbalance in calcium oxalate nephrolithiasis: mechanisms and therapeutic implications
Authors: Shushuai Yang, Yuanjiong Qi, Shichao Zhang, Longchao Zhang, Qiuyu Yu, Chenglong Xu, Yue Chen, Shiyong Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalcium oxalate (CaOx) nephrolithiasis is a globally prevalent disease with complex pathogenesis and limited targeted therapies. The imbalance between renal tubular epithelial cell (RTEC) injury and repair serves as a key driver of stone formation. Under hyperoxaluric or crystal-induced stress, RTECs undergo multiple regulated cell death pathways including apoptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and necroptosis, alongside autophagic dysfunction. These processes trigger the release of damage-associated molecular patterns and inflammatory mediators that amplify local inflammation and enhance crystal adhesion. Importantly, tubular repair after injury is not a uniform process but diverges into three distinct outcomes, including adaptive tubular regeneration, repair failure, and fibrogenic maladaptive repair. Persistent RTEC injury-repair imbalance disrupts tubular barrier integrity, perpetuates self-sustaining inflammatory cascades, and upregulates epithelial crystal adhesion molecules, collectively remodeling the renal microenvironment into a stable pro-stone niche that facilitates CaOx crystal deposition and stone formation. This review systematically summarizes the types of RTEC injury, the compromised repair pathways and their regulatory networks. It also discusses emerging therapeutic strategies aimed at correcting the injury-repair imbalance axis to provide new directions for the prevention and treatment of CaOx nephrolithiasis.Keywords: Calcium oxalate nephrolithiasis, Injury-repair imbalance, Regulated cell death, Maladaptive repair, Therapeutic strategy

Introduction

Kidney stone disease (nephrolithiasis) is a highly prevalent urinary system disorder worldwide, with its prevalence continuing to rise over the past few decades. Both developed and developing countries are facing a significant public health burden imposed by this disease (Williams 2005). Epidemiological data show that the global overall prevalence of kidney stones has reached 15%, and in some regions, due to factors such as dietary structure and climate, the prevalence even exceeds 20% (Pak 1998; Bargagli et al. 2021). Moreover, the recurrence rate of kidney stones in patients is as high as 50%, and in severe cases, it can progress to chronic kidney disease (CKD) and even end-stage renal disease (ESRD), further exacerbating the disease burden (Siener 2021). In terms of stone composition, calcium oxalate (CaOx) stones are the most common, accounting for approximately 80% of all cases, while the remaining types include uric acid stones, struvite stones (infectious stones), and cystine stones (Blay et al. 2020). Among these, the formation mechanism of CaOx stones, which involves interactions between multiple cells and pathways, has become the core focus of current research (Tang et al. 2025; Khan et al. 2021). Despite extensive research efforts, the current therapeutic landscape for CaOx nephrolithiasis remains limited, lacking targeted strategies to address its complex pathogenesis.

The pathogenesis of CaOx stones is highly complex and involves a multitude of biochemical, metabolic, and physiological factors, including disturbances in oxalate and calcium homeostasis, alterations in the gut microbiota, the development of Randall’s plaques, and persistent inflammatory responses (Ticinesi et al. 2018; Evan et al. 2006). Among these contributors, damage to renal tubular epithelial cells (RTECs) stands out as the most critical and closely linked event (Tang et al. 2025; Khan and Alli 2025). RTECs serve as the core functional cells of the kidney, solute transport (e.g., via the oxalate transporter SLC26A6), and maintenance of urinary microenvironment stability (Liu et al. 2025). Beyond their physiological roles, RTECs form a physical barrier between urine and the renal interstitium, a barrier whose integrity is critical for preventing abnormal crystal adhesion and tissue damage (Evan 2010; Alelign and Petros 2018). Under pathological conditions such as hyperoxaluria or CaOx crystal stimulation, RTECs undergo diverse forms of regulated cell death, including apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, alongside dysregulated autophagy (Tang et al. 2025; Khan and Alli 2025). Apoptosis, characterized by cell shrinkage and apoptotic body formation, disrupts the tubular barrier through RTEC detachment and exposes phosphatidylserine, enhancing crystal adhesion (Verkoelen et al. 1998). Necroptosis, mediated by the RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL pathway, triggers robust inflammatory responses via the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) (Dhuriya and Sharma 2018). Pyroptosis, driven by inflammasome activation, leads to Gasdermin D-mediated cell membrane perforation and the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β and IL-18 (Elias et al. 2023). Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent cell death pathway, results from glutathione depletion and lipid peroxide accumulation, further exacerbating RTEC dysfunction (Xie et al. 2022). These distinct yet interconnected injury modes collectively disrupt renal homeostasis, amplifying local inflammation and creating a microenvironment conducive to crystal formation.

Healthy kidneys possess a potent intrinsic repair system that defends against mild injury. Damaged renal tubular epithelial cells undergo dedifferentiation, proliferation, and redifferentiation, and immune cells including M2 macrophages and regulatory T cells mediate anti‑inflammatory responses and tissue regeneration (Roccatello et al. 2024; Ferenbach et al. 2009). However, in CaOx nephrolithiasis, sustained stimulation by oxalate and crystals impairs this repair capacity. Persistent inflammation inhibits RTEC proliferation and induces G2/M cell cycle arrest, while excessive pro-inflammatory cytokines drive abnormal epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), promoting renal interstitial fibrosis (Liu et al. 2022; Lovisa et al. 2015). Metabolic disturbances, such as reduced citrate production, further weaken RTEC antioxidant defenses, establishing a vicious cycle of "injury—inflammation—repair defect" that accelerates crystal deposition and stone formation.

In this review, we first summarize multiple subtypes of regulated cell death triggered by hyperoxaluria and CaOx crystals; we then elaborate on the full spectrum of repair responses following renal tubular injury, including adaptive tubular regeneration, repair failure and fibrogenic maladaptive repair, while deeply dissecting the bidirectional crosstalk between tubular damage and dysregulated intrinsic repair machinery. Finally, we further illustrate how persistent renal tubular epithelial injury-repair imbalance drives the construction of a pro-stone microenvironment to facilitate CaOx stone initiation and progression, and discuss multi-target therapeutic candidates based on this core pathological axis of imbalance. Innovatively, this review constructs a complete pathogenic cascade of “CaOx-induced tubular injury – failed or maladaptive repair – renal injury-repair imbalance– pro-stone niche formation”, which overcomes the drawbacks of prior reviews that discuss cell death and repair as disjointed, isolated modules. This integrated framework delivers fresh mechanistic perspectives and actionable therapeutic clues for the pathogenesis and precise intervention of CaOx nephrolithiasis.

Methods

This review systematically synthesizes the mechanistic, clinical, and translational evidence linking CaOx crystal-induced renal tubular injury and subsequent repair processes to the pathogenesis of nephrolithiasis. A structured literature search was conducted across multiple electronic databases, including Web of Science, PubMed, and Google Scholar. The core search strategy combined the following terms: ("calcium oxalate nephrolithiasis" OR "CaOx kidney stone") AND ("renal tubular epithelial injury" OR "regulated cell death" OR "tubular repair" OR "cellular homeostasis"). Supplementary sub-keywords were additionally applied for each cell death subtype and for key repair-related signaling pathways. No formal language or date restrictions were imposed; however, particular emphasis was placed on studies published within the past 20 years to comprehensively capture recent advances in the field of CaOx nephrolithiasis. Included studies were limited to original in vitro investigations using renal tubular epithelial cell crystal exposure models, in vivo rodent studies employing hyperoxaluria or CaOx crystal injection paradigms, human renal biopsy-based pathological analyses, and high-quality review articles specifically addressing tubular pathological mechanisms in stone disease. Studies were excluded if they were duplicate publications, lacked sufficient mechanistic data, focused exclusively on clinical imaging or epidemiological aspects without mechanistic exploration. The screening process proceeded in two stages: an initial title and abstract screening independently conducted by two authors, followed by a full-text secondary assessment. Mechanistic conclusions were primarily supported by reproducible findings across independent experimental models.

Mechanisms of RTEC injury in CaOx nephrolithiasis

In the pathological process of CaOx nephrolithiasis, the injury of RTECs is a core link driving crystal formation, adhesion, and deposition. Pathogenic factors such as hyperoxaluria and CaOx crystals can trigger a variety of regulated cell death pathways, including apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, by activating upstream events such as oxidative stress, inflammatory response, and mitochondrial dysfunction. Meanwhile, they disrupt autophagic homeostasis, ultimately leading to the loss of RTEC function and disturbance of the renal microenvironment (Xu et al. 2025; Green et al. 2011; Tang et al. 2021). In recent years, numerous in vitro and in vivo studies have confirmed that these cell injury modes do not exist independently but collectively promote stone formation through a complex network of signal interactions, providing a key basis for elucidating the pathogenesis of nephrolithiasis and developing targeted intervention strategies (Fig. 1).

Apoptosis

Apoptosis, a highly ordered form of programmed cell death, plays a crucial regulatory role in the occurrence and development of CaOx nephrolithiasis. Its core characteristics include cell shrinkage, nuclear pyknosis, apoptotic body formation, and no accompanying inflammatory response (Kakarla et al. 2020). However, the functional abnormalities and detachment of RTECs during apoptosis directly damage the renal tubular barrier, creating conditions for crystal adhesion (Verkoelen et al. 1998; Zhao et al. 2019). In the pathological microenvironment of nephrolithiasis, oxalate or CaOx crystals can activate the intrinsic apoptotic pathway of RTECs by inducing oxidative stress (Fig. 2A). Specifically, high concentrations of oxalate first stimulate RTECs to produce a large amount of reactive oxygen species (ROS). ROS oxidize mitochondrial membrane phospholipids, disrupt the mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), and cause the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), thereby releasing cytochrome C (Cyt C) into the cytoplasm (Li et al. 2020; Thamilselvan et al. 2014). Selenoprotein K (SelK) acts as a critical regulator of endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) and apoptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells under hyperoxaluric conditions. By modulating the IRE1-ASK1-JNK signaling axis, SelK controls oxidative stress, ERS activation, and caspase‑dependent apoptotic cell death. Upregulation of SelK effectively mitigates ERS and apoptosis, whereas its deletion exacerbates these pathological processes (Su et al. 2024).

The Bcl-2 protein family is a core regulatory factor of the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway, and its members determine the survival fate of RTECs through the "pro-apoptotic-anti-apoptotic" balance. Studies have shown that Bcl-2 family proteins control apoptosis by regulating the permeability of the mitochondrial outer membrane, a process that involves the activation of pro-apoptotic effector proteins such as BAX and BAK (Bhola and Letai 2016). Furthermore, the dynamic interactions of Bcl-2 family proteins have also revealed the mechanisms underlying apoptotic cell death in both healthy and diseased cells, providing new opportunities for therapeutic intervention (Singh et al. 2019). Under oxalate stimulation, the expression of pro-apoptotic proteins such as Bax is upregulated, and they translocate to the mitochondrial membrane to form pore structures, exacerbating mitochondrial damage. In contrast, the expression of anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl-2 is downregulated, which cannot effectively inhibit the release of mitochondrial contents, ultimately disrupting the apoptotic balance.

Beyond its direct cytotoxic effects, apoptosis actively perpetuates a pro-lithogenic microenvironment through indirect yet equally critical pathways. Upon detachment of apoptotic renal tubular epithelial cells, phosphatidylserine (PS) is externalized on the cell surface, where it functions as a potent "adhesion signal" that binds to calmodulin on CaOx crystals, thereby markedly enhancing crystal anchorage to the tubular basement membrane (Lee et al. 2013; Segawa et al. 2011). Concurrently, the released apoptotic debris serves as a heterogeneous nucleating template, facilitating crystal growth and aggregation, which collectively promote intratubular cast formation and subsequent collecting duct obstruction—a structural prerequisite for macrostone development (Lee et al. 2013; Segawa et al. 2011; Kiss et al. 2006). Thus, rather than representing a terminal event, apoptosis actively feeds forward into a cascade of crystal retention, aggregation, and luminal blockade, critically linking initial cell injury to the physical processes of stone propagation and recurrence.

Necroptosis

Necroptosis is a form of cell death with both "programmed regulation" and "inflammatory effects". Its morphological characteristics include cell membrane rupture and release of cytoplasmic contents, which is different from the "silent death" of apoptosis. Necroptosis can induce a strong inflammatory response by releasing DAMPs, playing a key role in the "injury-inflammation-stone" vicious cycle of CaOx nephrolithiasis (Fig. 2B) (Dhuriya and Sharma 2018; Weinlich et al. 2017). The molecular mechanism of necroptosis depends on the cascade activation of the receptor-interacting protein kinase (RIPK) family and is independent of caspases. Under stimulation by CaOx crystals or hyperoxaluria, the tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNFR1) on the surface of RTECs is activated, recruiting RIPK1 and inducing its phosphorylation. Phosphorylated RIPK1 further binds to and activates RIPK3, forming a "RIPK1-RIPK3 complex". Subsequently, RIPK3 activates mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL) through phosphorylation. Phosphorylated MLKL translocates to the cell membrane and oligomerizes, forming transmembrane pores, which increase cell membrane permeability, disrupt osmotic pressure balance, and ultimately cause cell swelling and rupture (Carney 2016; Mulay et al. 2016).

The promoting effect of necroptosis on nephrolithiasis is mainly reflected in two aspects. For one thing, the cytoplasmic contents (such as ATP and nucleic acids) released by cell membrane rupture can act as chemotactic signals to recruit immune cells such as macrophages and neutrophils to the injury site, exacerbating local inflammation, and the inflammatory microenvironment can further enhance the sensitivity of RTECs to CaOx crystals (Liu et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2024); for another, the renal tubular barrier damage caused by necroptosis is more severe, which not only allows crystals to easily penetrate the basement membrane into the interstitium but also exposes more crystal adhesion sites (such as collagen IV), promoting crystal retention and growth (Mulay et al. 2017; Hou et al. 2022). Collectively, through the concerted actions of inflammatory exacerbation and structural compromise, necroptosis actively propagates crystal retention, interstitial penetration, and the eventual expansion of stone burden.

Pyroptosis

Pyroptosis is a typical pro-inflammatory form of regulated cell death. Its core characteristics are caspase-dependent cell membrane perforation mediated by NLRP3 inflammasome activation and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18, which are at the core of the inflammation-driven mechanism of CaOx nephrolithiasis(Fig. 2C) (Burdette et al. 2021; Tong et al. 2025). Activated NLRP3 recruits apoptosis-associated speck-like protein (ASC) and forms a complex through ASC oligomerization, which then recruits and activates caspase-1 (caspase-4/5 in humans and caspase-11 in mice) (Shi et al. 2015). Activated caspase-1 mediates pyroptosis through two pathways: one is to directly cleave Gasdermin D (GSDMD) protein, producing a GSDMD-N terminal fragment with membrane perforation activity. This fragment forms pores with a diameter of approximately 10–20 nm on the cell membrane, increasing cell membrane permeability, disrupting cytoplasmic osmotic pressure balance, and ultimately causing cell swelling and rupture. The other is to catalyze the hydrolysis of pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 (precursors of IL-1β and IL-18), generating mature pro-inflammatory cytokines that are released extracellularly through GSDMD pores, inducing a strong local inflammatory response (Broz 2015; Wei et al. 2022).

In the pathological process of CaOx nephrolithiasis, CaOx crystals or hyperoxaluria activate inflammatory responses in RTECs through multiple pathways, which involve sophisticated molecular regulatory mechanisms (Mulay and Anders 2017). Studies have demonstrated that CaOx crystals induce the upregulation of cytoplasmic and mitochondrial ROS, thereby promoting the priming and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and stimulating the maturation and activation of IL-18 and IL-1β (Liu et al. 2023). Furthermore, CaOx crystals promote M1 macrophage polarization and the formation of macrophage extracellular traps (METs) via the CREB1/CRTC2‑regulated exosomal miR‑93‑3p signaling axis, thus exacerbating renal interstitial injury (Sun et al. 2025). Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) also play a significant role in the formation of renal calculi. Studies have demonstrated that miRNAs and lncRNAs are involved in multiple aspects of the pathological mechanisms of renal calculi, including calcium metabolism, oxalate metabolism, oxidative stress, cell-crystal adhesion, autophagy, apoptosis, and macrophage polarization (Wang et al. 2024; Cao et al. 2018).

Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is a regulatory form of cell death characterized by iron-dependent massive accumulation of lipid peroxides. Its core mechanism is the impairment of lipid peroxide clearance caused by glutathione (GSH) depletion or inhibition of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) activity (Tonnus et al. 2025). In recent years, it has been confirmed to be an important pathway for RTEC injury induced by CaOx crystals (He et al. 2021). In CaOx nephrolithiasis, CaOx crystals or hyperoxaluria induce ferroptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells through multiple signaling pathways and molecular events (Fig. 2D). Exposure to high concentrations of CaOx crystals or oxalate directly triggers mitochondrial dysfunction and redox imbalance, accompanied by elevated Fe2⁺ accumulation and excessive lipid peroxidation, which are core features of ferroptosis (Patel et al. 2018). CaOx crystals downregulate the expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4, two key negative regulators of ferroptosis, while upregulating ferroptosis-promoting proteins including p53, ACSL4, and transferrin receptor, thereby disrupting intracellular antioxidant systems and accelerating lipid peroxide deposition (He et al. 2021; Ye et al. 2023).

Studies focusing on ferroptosis and oxalate-related mitochondrial damage are still expanding, and the precise crosstalk between iron metabolism, redox imbalance, and crystal‑driven renal injury remains to be fully clarified. Further elucidation of the molecular networks governing CaOx-induced ferroptosis will facilitate the discovery of novel therapeutic targets and deepen our understanding of how oxidative and iron-dependent cell death modulates renal tubular damage and kidney stone progression.

Autophagy

Autophagy is an evolutionarily conserved catabolic process that maintains cellular homeostasis by degrading and recycling damaged organelles, misfolded proteins, and invasive crystals via formation of autophagosomes and subsequent fusion with lysosomes (Tang et al. 2020). In CaOx nephrolithiasis, autophagy exhibits a context-dependent dual role: adaptive autophagy preserves renal tubular epithelial cell survival and reduces crystal deposition, whereas excessive or dysregulated autophagy promotes cell death, oxidative injury, and stone progression (Li et al. 2022). This autophagy deficiency arises from hyperactivated MTOR signaling, which directly suppresses the nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity of TFEB, a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy. Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of MTOR effectively restores autophagic flux, reduces excessive crystal-cell interactions, and alleviates CaOx‑induced renal inflammation and crystal deposition (Unno et al. 2020). Oxidative stress also potentiates ERS in CaOx‑stimulated RTECs, which further upregulates the expression of LC3B, BECN1, GRP78, and CHOP to trigger excessive autophagy and aggravate renal injury (Kang et al. 2020). Gut microbiota-derived metabolites such as trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) can further amplify autophagy via the PERK/ROS pathway under hyperoxaluric conditions, exacerbating cell death and crystal adhesion (Dong et al. 2022).

The functional outcome of autophagy in renal injury is determined by autophagic flux integrity. Moderate and fluent autophagy defends against renal damage through three core mechanisms: clearing dysfunctional mitochondria to reduce mtROS release; alleviating ERS by removing misfolded proteins; and directly degrading intracellular harmful substances to attenuate cytotoxicity (Ang et al. 2020; Yamamoto et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2014; Ma et al. 2020). The renoprotective effect of SGLT2 inhibitors in nephrolithiasis is closely associated with the restoration of autophagic flux. Dapagliflozin attenuates CaOx crystal deposition and renal injury by suppressing mTOR activity, normalizing AMPK signaling, and reversing impaired autophagic flux in non-diabetic stone models, thereby maintaining intracellular homeostasis and reducing crystal-induced cytotoxicity (Liu et al. 2025). Accordingly, excessive or dysregulated autophagy accelerates oxalate‑induced renal damage through activation of the p38 signaling pathway, whereas pharmacological inhibition of autophagy (e.g., chloroquine) or genetic knockdown of Beclin1 alleviates oxidative stress, mitochondrial injury, and subsequent CaOx crystal deposition (Duan et al. 2018).

Other forms of cell death

The study of cell death mechanisms in renal tubular epithelial cells has expanded significantly, with emerging pathways such as cuproptosis and disulfidptosis gaining attention. These novel forms of cell death are distinct from traditional pathways like apoptosis and necrosis, offering new insights into renal pathology and potential therapeutic targets. Cuproptosis, a copper-dependent form of regulated cell death, has been observed in renal tubular epithelial cells under conditions of high oxidative stress. Ginsenoside Ro has been shown to alleviate cuproptosis by inhibiting the zinc transporter ZnT1, thereby reducing oxidative stress and crystal deposition in renal cells (Li et al. 2026). This observation suggests that targeting metal ion transporters could represent a potential strategy for mitigating renal injury, although whether cuproptosis operates as a primary driver of RTEC death in CaOx nephrolithiasis remains to be established. Disulfidptosis, characterized by the formation of abnormal disulfide bonds leading to cytoskeletal collapse, represents another novel cell death pathway involving rapid NADPH depletion and cystine accumulation under metabolic stress conditions (Xiao et al. 2024). While this process is distinct from other forms of programmed cell death, its occurrence and functional significance in crystal-induced tubular injury have not yet been investigated.

Collectively, these emerging pathways offer intriguing mechanistic possibilities, but their roles in CaOx nephrolithiasis remain speculative at present. Future studies are needed to determine whether cuproptosis or disulfidptosis actively contribute to crystal-induced renal injury, or whether they represent epiphenomena of broader metabolic or inflammatory disturbances.

Repair responses following renal tubular epithelial injury

Renal tubular epithelial cells possess robust intrinsic self-renewal capacity. Upon tubular epithelial injury induced by CaOx crystals and hyperoxaluria, renal tissues activate a comprehensive spectrum of endogenous repair cascades. Adaptive tubular regeneration serves as physiological protective repair, which restores intact tubular epithelial architecture without fibrotic lesions under mild and transient insults. Persistent crystal-mediated toxic stress easily precipitates repair failure, characterized by complete loss of proliferative and regenerative capacity in tubular cells. Unresolved tissue injury further drives the transition toward maladaptive repair, accompanied by sustained profibrotic signaling cascades, persistent chronic inflammation, and irreversible extracellular matrix deposition. A series of cellular processes encompassing cell cycle progression, immune cross-talk, epithelial phenotypic plasticity, and multiple signaling cascades together govern the terminal outcome of tubular repair (Fig. 3A). In-depth elucidation of these repair mechanisms will provide important theoretical support and intervention targets for reversing renal tubular injury and preventing the progression of CaOx nephrolithiasis to renal fibrosis.

Adaptive tubular regeneration

Normal cell cycle progression serves as the core prerequisite for adaptive repair. MCM4 upregulates p53/p21 signaling to maintain stable cycle operation, which may potentially preserve tubular regenerative capacity and mitigate injury severity (Huang et al. 2026). Smad7 blocks the TGF‑β/Smad3‑p21 cascade to avoid G1-phase arrest, thereby sustaining RTEC proliferative potential (Fu et al. 2017). The circular RNA circBNC2 encodes ctBNC protein to facilitate CDK1/cyclin B1 complex nuclear translocation, ensuring smooth G2/M transition; sufficient circBNC2 expression presumably safeguards adaptive regeneration and avoids early repair dysfunction (Wang et al. 2022).

Immune homeostasis is indispensable for adaptive regeneration. Macrophage-derived apoptosis inhibitor of macrophage (AIM) protein plays a direct role in tubular epithelial repair: AIM accumulates on intraluminal necrotic debris, binds to kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1) expressed on injured tubular epithelial cells, and enhances the phagocytic clearance of debris by epithelial cells, thereby alleviating tubular obstruction and promoting tissue repair (Arai et al. 2016). Regulatory T cells (Tregs), a subset of CD4 + CD25 + Foxp3 + T lymphocytes, are key regulators of immune homeostasis and play a protective role in tubular epithelial repair after ischemic injury (Kinsey et al. 2009). Tregs infiltrate the injured kidney during the repair phase, and their depletion exacerbates tubular damage, reduces tubular proliferation, and increases pro-inflammatory cytokine production by kidney-infiltrating T cells, leading to impaired renal function and reduced survival (Gandolfo et al. 2009). The protective effect of Tregs is primarily mediated by negative modulation of pro-inflammatory responses in other T cell subsets, as they have minimal impact on the recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages (Gandolfo et al. 2009).

Adaptive tubular regeneration relies on extensive bidirectional communication between renal tubular epithelial cells and resident interstitial cell populations, including immune cells, fibroblasts and endothelial cells (Fig. 3B). Such intercellular communication may be achieved through direct cell–cell contact or paracrine secretion of cytokines, chemokines and signaling mediators, jointly constructing a microenvironment permissive to epithelial restoration. For instance, injured RTECs secrete multiple cytokines including transforming growth factor‑β (TGF‑β) and Wnt ligands, which modulate fibrosis and inflammation by interacting with fibroblasts and immune cells, thereby shaping the renal repair process (Qi and Yang 2018; Tan et al. 2016). Subsequently, immune cells exert essential functions in the repair of RTECs. Studies have documented that lymphocytes and innate immune cells play pivotal roles in AKI progression and recovery. Distinct immune cell subsets, including CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, B cells, and neutrophils, exert divergent effects at different stages of AKI (Lee et al. 2024). Moreover, macrophages display complex functional phenotypes during renal repair, exerting either detrimental or protective effects depending on their subtype and the phase of injury (Kumar 2018; Masola et al. 2018). In addition, crosstalk between RTECs and endothelial cells contributes critically to renal repair. Endothelial cells regulate vascular tone, local tissue perfusion, coagulation, and inflammatory responses. Emerging evidence demonstrates that signaling and interactions between RTECs and endothelial cells are indispensable for renal microvascular function and tissue repair (Verma and Molitoris 2015).

Collectively, orderly cell cycle progression, balanced immune homeostasis, and bidirectional intercellular crosstalk coordinately orchestrate intact adaptive tubular regeneration following mild tubular injury. However, given that the majority of current mechanistic insights into repair processes derive from non-CaOx models, future investigations specifically addressing the CaOx pathological microenvironment are urgently needed to translate these findings into targeted therapeutic strategies that sustain physiological tubular regeneration and avert the progression toward chronic fibrosis and stone recurrence.

Failed repair under persistent CaOx crystal stimulation

When tubular tissues suffer sustained, repeated exposure to high oxalate and CaOx crystals, physiological adaptive regeneration gradually collapses, entering a state of repair failure characterized by partially attenuated proliferative competence of RTECs, which serves as an intermediate transitional stage toward maladaptive fibrogenesis. Cell cycle arrest at G1 or G2/M phase represents the hallmark of repair failure. Upregulated RGC-32 disturbs the G2/M checkpoint and blocks RTEC mitosis (Shen et al. 2016). DNA-PKcs deficiency may potentially aggravate repair failure by impairing DNA damage repair and disrupting cycle progression (Feng et al. 2025). Circular RNA circBNC2, which is highly expressed in normal RTECs and hepatocytes but downregulated after injury, acts as a negative regulator of G2/M arrest (Wang et al. 2022).

Upon persistent stimulation by CaOx crystals, injury of renal tubular epithelial cells triggers a shift in cell cycle distribution from the G0/G1 phase to the S and G2/M phases; this cell cycle remodeling is regarded as one critical contributor to elevated COM crystal adhesion (Khamchun and Thongboonkerd 2018). Moreover, existing evidence demonstrates that adhesion and internalization of COM crystals elicit robust oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, consequently impairing cellular proliferation and tissue repair capacity (Peerapen et al. 2018). Persistent oxidative stress and mitochondrial injury disrupt autophagic homeostasis, as excessive mTOR activation inhibits TFEB nuclear translocation to hinder lysosome biogenesis and autophagosome clearance. These accumulated damaged organelles and crystal fragments continuously amplify cytotoxicity and further suppress RTEC proliferation, ultimately leading to failed cellular repair (Unno et al. 2020). In addition, excessive oxidative stress not only induces direct cellular damage but also disrupts tight junctions via activation of the p38 MAPK signaling cascade, which further exacerbates epithelial injury (Peerapen and Thongboonkerd 2013). In summary, failed repair of renal tubular epithelial cells following persistent COM crystal challenge represents a multifaceted pathological process encompassing cell cycle redistribution, oxidative stress overload, and tight junction disintegration. The crosstalk among these cascades aggravates epithelial damage, and the resultant defective cellular repair ultimately facilitates the initiation and progression of nephrolithiasis.

Maladaptive repair and renal fibrosis

If repair failure cannot be reversed, renal tissue proceeds to irreversible maladaptive repair, mainly manifested as partial epithelial-mesenchymal transition, sustained profibrotic signal activation, chronic inflammatory infiltration and massive extracellular matrix deposition, which ultimately drives renal interstitial fibrosis and provides favorable conditions for persistent crystal retention.

Epithelial Plasticity and Partial EMT in maladaptive tubular repair

Partial EMT constitutes the core pathological event of maladaptive repair. The occurrence of EMT is often accompanied by cell cycle arrest and the exacerbation of fibrosis, a process regulated by various factors. First, research has found that partial EMT programs can cause renal tubular epithelial cells to arrest in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle during fibrotic injury, which limits the cells' ability to repair and regenerate (Lovisa et al. 2015; Huang and Susztak 2016). Snai1-induced partial EMT, mediated through the p53-p21 axis, is closely associated with G2/M phase arrest and the progression of renal fibrosis. This process is further exacerbated by NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses (Qi et al. 2021). The Notch1 signaling pathway also plays an important role in EMT and fibrosis following renal injury. Studies have shown that Notch1 activity is significantly elevated in fibrotic kidneys and is associated with an α-SMA-positive myofibroblast phenotype. The activation of Notch1 promotes EMT and matrix deposition in RTECs, a process dependent on the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling pathway (Hong et al. 2019). Additionally, TGF-β1 is a major pathogenic factor inducing EMT, regulating its occurrence through multiple pathways. Research indicates that miR-141 modulates TGF-β1-induced EMT by inhibiting HIPK2 expression, thereby playing a role in renal tubulointerstitial fibrosis (Huang et al. 2015). Finally, the role of the IL-11 signaling axis in chronic kidney disease (CKD) cannot be overlooked. IL-11 promotes EMT and fibrosis in RTECs through immune crosstalk with macrophages. The application of IL-11 neutralizing antibodies effectively interrupts the IL-11/IL-11Rα1 interaction, reduces pro-fibrotic extracellular matrix deposition, enhances the regenerative capacity of RTECs, and improves renal parenchymal repair (Wang et al. 2025).

Immunometabolic homeostasis dysregulation in maladaptive tubular repair

The repair of tubular epithelial cells is closely intertwined with immunometabolic regulation, where pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) expressed on tubular epithelial cells integrate immune sensing and metabolic rewiring (Miller et al. 2011; Anders and Schaefer 2014; Rosin and Okusa 2011). Tubular epithelial cells rely predominantly on fatty acid oxidation (FAO) for energy production under physiological conditions, but switch to glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) after injury to meet the energy demands of proliferation and antioxidant defense. This metabolic rewiring is regulated by innate immune sensors: activation of TLR2/TLR4 by DAMPs suppresses FAO and enhances glycolysis, while NLRP3 inflammasome activation promotes lipid accumulation and lipotoxicity by inhibiting FAO (Rijt et al. 2022). Conversely, NLRX1, a mitochondrial-localized PRR, regulates mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and FAO to reduce oxidative stress-induced tubular epithelial cell apoptosis, and its deficiency exacerbates ischemic injury (Rijt et al. 2022). Direct evidence from CaOx nephrolithiasis models further corroborates the detrimental role of metabolic derangement in maladaptive repair and fibrosis. Specifically, UHRF1 has been shown to promote lipid accumulation and suppress fatty acid oxidation through inhibition of the AMPK pathway, thereby aggravating CaOx-induced renal fibrosis (Sun et al. 2025).

Integrated regulatory signals governing divergent repair phenotypes

Multiple transcription factors and signaling pathways dynamically switch repair between adaptive regeneration, repair failure and maladaptive fibrosis. The WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway serves multiple functions in kidney injury and repair. It is crucial for morphogenesis and cellular organization during embryonic development. Although typically suppressed in adulthood, it can be reactivated during organ injury and regeneration. Research shows that transient activation of WNT/β-catenin signaling can stimulate tissue regeneration after AKI, whereas sustained (uncontrolled) signaling may promote renal fibrosis, podocyte injury, and proteinuria in chronic kidney disease (Schunk et al. 2021). Furthermore, the transient upregulation of EGR1 has been found to enhance kidney repair by activating SOX9-positive tubular cells. As a pioneer transcription factor, EGR1 is rapidly upregulated after AKI and increases SOX9 expression by directly binding to the Sox9 gene promoter, thereby promoting cell proliferation and activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway (Chen et al. 2022). Dynamic changes in enhancers and super-enhancers also play a significant role in the post-injury repair process. ChIP-seq analysis of kidneys following ischemia–reperfusion injury has identified key transcription factors, including HNF4A, GR, STAT3, and STAT5, which show specific binding at enhancer and super-enhancer sites, revealing the enhancer dynamics and transcriptional changes during renal repair (Wilflingseder et al. 2020). These findings hold potential for identifying targets for therapeutic intervention. Finally, Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-induced IL-22 plays an important role in accelerating kidney regeneration. Studies demonstrate that TLR4 signaling drives tubular regeneration after acute injury by inducing certain interleukins, such as IL-22. IL-22 deficiency or blockade impairs post-ischemic tubular recovery, whereas IL-22 supplementation fully restores the regenerative capacity of the epithelium (Kulkarni et al. 2014). These results indicate that TLR4-activated monocytes accelerate renal tubular regeneration and recovery through the secretion of IL-22. In conclusion, while the coordination of multiple key pathways and transcription factors is indispensable for orchestrating renal repair following tubular injury, the translational potential of these molecular targets for CaOx nephrolithiasis therapy requires direct validation in crystal-specific experimental models.

Renal tubular injury-repair imbalance promotes pro-stone niche generation

CaOx nephrolithiasis develops through a complex pathological process tightly coupled to diverse tubular epithelial injury and tissue repair events. Distinct from previous reviews that independently investigate either cell damage or regenerative signaling as isolated research objects, this work innovatively constructs RTEC injury–repair imbalance as a complete hierarchical mechanistic framework to interpret stone pathogenesis. Under chronic hyperoxaluria and Ca crystal stimulation, RTECs activate unrestricted multi-modal regulated cell death, while the kidney’s endogenous adaptive repair programs are simultaneously suppressed and diverted toward irreversible repair failure or fibrogenic maladaptive repair. This bidirectional imbalance between unconstrained cellular injury and crippled tissue restoration constitutes the core pathogenic axis governing the full post-injury pathological cascade, serving as the indispensable prerequisite for the formation of an intrarenal pro-stone microenvironment. A self-amplifying vicious loop is therefore formed: the imbalance-driven tubular lesions sustain a crystal-permissive niche, and accumulated CaOx deposits reciprocally exacerbate multi-type RTEC death to further deepen the gap between injury and repair. Our group’s original experimental research delivers direct empirical evidence validating this unifying framework: targeted restoration of the equilibrium between GSDMD-mediated pyroptotic injury and CHMP4B-dependent tubular repair markedly reduces renal crystal burden in murine models of CaOx nephrolithiasis (Yang et al. 2025). Cumulative evidence across diverse renal injury models further supports the universal pathogenic relevance of decoupled cell death and regenerative signaling, indirectly reinforcing the central position of RTEC injury–repair imbalance in CaOx stone pathogenesis (Landau et al. 2019; Lu et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2021; Guo et al. 2024).

Diverse regulated cell death pathways disrupt intrinsic renal tubular repair programs

Apoptosis suppresses adaptive regeneration and drives maladaptive repair. Investigations into the functional role of GOLPH3 in CaOx-triggered renal injury and fibrosis have demonstrated that GOLPH3 exerts a pathogenic effect in CaOx-mediated renal damage by exacerbating Golgi stress, inflammatory response, apoptosis, and renal fibrosis (Song et al. 2025). In addition, another study uncovered the renoprotective function of the mitochondrial enzyme HIBADH against CaOx nephrolithiasis, revealing that HIBADH safeguards renal tissues via modulating oxidative stress and apoptotic signaling (Chen et al. 2025). HIBADH overexpression attenuates crystal adhesion and cellular apoptosis, facilitates cell cycle progression, and alleviates mitochondrial-derived oxidative stress. These findings further corroborate that apoptosis inhibition not only boosts adaptive tubular regeneration but also ameliorates renal repair by mitigating inflammatory infiltration and fibrotic lesions.

Necroptosis shifts immune homeostasis toward pro-fibrotic phenotype. Necroptosis is closely associated with inflammatory activation and renal fibrosis, collectively exacerbating the pathological damage of nephrolithiasis. On one hand, activation of the key necroptotic molecules RIPK3 and MLKL can trigger NLRP3 inflammasome‑mediated inflammatory responses, thereby aggravating renal injury (Lawlor et al. 2015). On the other hand, activation of the RIP1‑RIP3‑MLKL axis is closely related to the progression of renal fibrosis, which is mediated by the Wnt3α/β‑catenin/GSK‑3β signaling pathway (Jiang et al. 2022).

Pyroptosis exhibits intimate linkage to inflammasome activation, notably the NLRP3 inflammasome, under CaOx-mediated renal injury (Song et al. 2019). Accumulating evidence has revealed that LINC00339 accelerates pyroptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells by modulating the miR-22-3p/NLRP3 regulatory axis (Song et al. 2019). As a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA), GAS5 regulates Notch signaling and subsequently triggers the NF-κB/NLRP3 inflammasome cascade, which drives M1 macrophage polarization and robust proinflammatory cytokine secretion (Zhang et al. 2026). Persistent inflammatory responses of this nature not only aggravate renal tissue damage but also potentially hinder anti-inflammatory adaptive immune-mediated renal repair.

Ferroptosis represents an iron-dependent regulated cell death modality characterized by lipid peroxidation and plasma membrane rupture. During the progression of CaOx nephrolithiasis, aberrant ferroptotic activation exacerbates renal damage and accelerates fibrotic progression. Previous research has demonstrated that CaOx crystal deposition initiates ferroptosis via the Nrf2/HO-1 and p53/SLC7A11 signaling axes, which blunts the antioxidant defense capacity of renal tubular epithelial cells against oxidative stress, thereby aggravating cellular injury and crystal adhesion (Zhao et al. 2023). Another study revealed that p53 deacetylation alleviates CaOx deposition-elicited renal fibrosis through the suppression of ferroptosis (Ye et al. 2023). In addition, pharmacological inhibition of CXCR4 mitigates crystal-induced renal fibrosis by repressing autophagy and EMT (Ye et al. 2022). Collectively, ferroptosis may exert pleiotropic pathogenic effects in CaOx nephrolithiasis. Beyond directly triggering epithelial damage, ferroptosis potentially crosstalks with multiple metabolic and signaling cascades, which might contribute to maladaptive tubular repair and progressive renal fibrosis.

Autophagic flux is indispensable for intracellular homeostasis and adaptive tubular regeneration. Balanced autophagic flux serves as a prerequisite for adaptive regeneration; meanwhile, it preserves cellular functional integrity via the clearance of damaged mitochondria and intracellular microcrystals. Accumulating evidence indicates that impaired autophagic flux triggers mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby disrupting cellular homeostasis (Song et al. 2026). In CaOx nephrolithiasis, blockade of autophagic processes exacerbates renal tubular epithelial injury and inflammatory responses, ultimately driving maladaptive repair and renal fibrosis (Song et al. 2026). Downregulation of VAMP7 acts as a primary mediator of defective autophagosome–lysosome fusion. Ectopic overexpression of VAMP7 restores autophagic homeostasis and alleviates crystal-induced cytotoxicity, which provides a novel therapeutic target for precise intervention against nephrolithiasis (Song et al. 2026). Additional research has revealed that trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) accelerates CaOx crystal deposition and renal injury by facilitating autophagy-associated cell death (Dong et al. 2022). Collectively, these mechanistic findings further underscore the pivotal role of autophagy in CaOx nephrolithiasis. Modulation of autophagic activity holds therapeutic potential to ameliorate stone-related tissue damage and fibrotic lesions.

Crosstalk among multiple regulated cell death pathways exacerbates defective tubular repair. Upon CaOx stimulation, apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis and ferroptosis do not function in isolation; instead, they form a synergistic cascade that collectively aggravates renal tubular injury–repair imbalance (Tang et al. 2025; Khan and Alli 2025). For instance, lipid peroxides generated during ferroptosis act as priming signals to trigger NLRP3 inflammasome activation and facilitate the secretion of pyroptosis-associated proinflammatory cytokines. Meanwhile, DAMPs released via necroptosis further disrupt mitochondrial function and render epithelial cells more susceptible to ferroptosis. Such synergistic injury mediated by intersecting cell death cascades may suppress adaptive repair machinery, potentially contributing to irreversible defective tissue repair.

Pathological consequences of persistent injury-repair imbalance: construction of pro-crystallization renal microenvironment

The sustained coexistence of unrestrained regulated cell death and dysfunctional adaptive/maladaptive repair mechanisms induces renal tubular injury–repair imbalance, which remodels intratubular and interstitial tissues into a stable pro-stone niche characterized by three mutually reinforcing pathological hallmarks: irreversible tubular barrier disruption, self-amplifying inflammatory cascades, and global upregulation of crystal adhesion molecules. These three processes constitute a self-perpetuating vicious cycle: crystal exposure initiates tubular injury and repair dysfunction, and this imbalance-shaped microenvironment reciprocally promotes crystal nucleation and anchoring, thereby aggravating kidney stone formation and ultimately driving the development of chronic renal fibrosis (Fig. 4).

Renal tubular barrier disruption

The renal tubular epithelium, composed of RTECs connected by tight junctions and anchored to the basement membrane, serves as a critical physical and functional barrier separating the urinary lumen from the renal interstitium, whose integrity is essential for maintaining urinary ion homeostasis and preventing abnormal crystal infiltration. Renal tubular injury–repair imbalance triggers massive RTEC exfoliation, plasma membrane rupture, and sustained secretion of inflammatory proteases, which degrade tight junction proteins and thereby compromise the selective permeability of epithelial monolayers (Kuo et al. 2022; Davidovich et al. 2014). This barrier disruption may have at least two potentially lithogenic consequences. On one hand, it could facilitate the local accumulation of stone-forming ions (Ca2⁺ and oxalate) within the renal interstitium, possibly elevating their concentrations to levels approaching supersaturation and thereby increasing the likelihood of CaOx crystal nucleation, although direct in vivo evidence confirming this ion concentration shift remains limited. On the other hand, when barrier integrity is sufficiently compromised, nascent CaOx crystals may gain access to the basement membrane and infiltrate the renal interstitium, potentially evading clearance by urinary flow and creating a spatial niche favorable for crystal growth and aggregation (Fong-Ngern et al. 2017; Peerapen and Thongboonkerd 2021). It should be noted, however, that whether these mechanisms represent universal consequences of tubular injury in human CaOx nephrolithiasis remains to be further investigated.

Inflammatory response amplification

Unresolved injury coupled with failed adaptive repair establishes a feedforward inflammatory loop. Apoptotic/necroptotic RTECs release DAMPs (HMGB1, histones) to activate TLR4/RAGE signaling on resident immune cells; pyroptosis simultaneously secretes mature IL-1β/IL-18 to recruit circulating neutrophils and M1 macrophages (Xu et al. 2025; Su et al. 2024). Recruited inflammatory cells release massive ROS and MMP-9 proteases: ROS further aggravates RTEC oxidative injury and cell death, while MMP-9 degrades basement collagen IV to expose additional crystal binding sites and activate latent TGF-β1 to worsen maladaptive fibrosis (Wu et al. 2021). The remodeled intrarenal pH, redox potential and ion composition under chronic inflammation further elevate CaOx crystal nucleation efficiency, constructing a cytokine-rich pro-crystallization niche (Khan et al. 2021; Taguchi et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2022).

Increased expression of crystal adhesion molecules

Persistent oxidative stress and chronic inflammation originating from injury–repair imbalance globally upregulate surface adhesion receptors on residual injured RTECs, drastically enhancing CaOx crystal binding capacity (Khamchun and Thongboonkerd 2018; Gan et al. 2016). α-enolase, a well-characterized apical crystal adhesion receptor, is overexpressed after tubular damage to stably anchor CaOx monohydrate crystals on epithelial surfaces (Fong-Ngern and Thongboonkerd 2016). Long non-coding RNAs NEAT1 and H19, whose expression is induced under imbalanced injury-repair status, further amplify crystal adhesion by exacerbating ROS and inflammatory signaling cascades (Yan et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2019). Apoptotic cell debris released during unregulated cell death also acts as heterogeneous nucleation cores to accelerate crystal aggregation and tubular obstruction (Tang et al. 2025; Khan and Alli 2025; Chutipongtanate and Thongboonkerd 2010). Collectively, the three pathological phenotypes driven by RTEC injury–repair imbalance may collectively maintain a persistent crystal-permissive microenvironment, which could serve as a vital intermediate bridge linking tubular injury to the initiation and recurrence of clinical CaOx kidney stones.

Intervention strategies based on injury-repair imbalance

The pathological progression of CaOx nephrolithiasis is essentially a sustained imbalance between renal tubular epithelial cell injury and subsequent repair. Persistent pathogenic stimuli trigger excessive programmed cell death, while disordered repair processes such as cell cycle arrest, maladaptive inflammation, and incomplete autophagy further amplify renal microenvironment disturbance and crystal deposition. Therefore, rationally designed intervention strategies targeting the core nodes of injury-repair imbalance have become a promising approach to prevent renal injury, inhibit crystal formation, and block the transition to chronic kidney disease. These strategies mainly focus on three aspects: directly suppressing RTEC injury pathways, enhancing intrinsic repair capacity, and regulating immune-inflammatory responses, with the ultimate goal of restoring the homeostatic balance between injury and repair (Fig. 5).

Targeting RTEC injury pathways

Targeting various injury pathways of renal tubular epithelial cells is a key direction for intervening in CaOx kidney stones. By inhibiting excessive cell death and regulating autophagic balance, renal injury can be reduced and stone formation can be prevented. Targeted inhibition of pathological injury pathways in RTECs represents a pivotal therapeutic direction for the intervention of CaOx nephrolithiasis. As elaborated above, RTEC injury in CaOx stone disease is orchestrated by diverse forms of programmed cell death and aberrant autophagic activity, which synergistically trigger renal parenchymal damage and drive abnormal crystal nucleation and deposition. Compared with single-targeted interventions that merely focus on one isolated injury mechanism, simultaneously manipulating distinct RTEC injury cascades and restoring autophagic homeostasis is likely to serve as a more potent therapeutic regimen to alleviate renal injury and block stone formation (Table 1).

Potential strategies targeting different programmed cell deaths to treat CaOx nephrolithiasis

The mitochondrial apoptotic pathway acts as a core executor of RTEC apoptosis in CaOx-induced renal injury, and modulation of Bcl-2 family proteins constitutes a classical intervention strategy. This approach mainly encompasses upregulating the expression of anti-apoptotic proteins such as Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL, or suppressing the activity of pro-apoptotic proteins including Bax and BNIP3, thereby stabilizing mitochondrial membrane potential, reducing cytochrome c release, and blocking the downstream caspase cascade reaction (Liu et al. 2018; Ji et al. 2024; Lahoti et al. 2012). Existing studies have verified that selenium/selenoprotein K alleviates RTEC damage and CaOx crystal deposition via inhibiting endoplasmic reticulum stress-related apoptosis (Su et al. 2024); additionally, the development of targeted drugs targeting apoptosis-associated microRNAs is also regarded as a promising therapeutic strategy for CaOx nephrolithiasis (Zhong et al. 2025; Fan et al. 2022).

Targeting the receptor-interacting protein kinase 1/3 (RIPK1/RIPK3)-mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL) signaling axis is indispensable for curbing RTEC necroptosis. Small-molecule inhibitors like GNF-7 and AZD5423 can effectively block the RIPK1/RIPK3-MLKL pathway, thereby suppressing necroptosis and mitigating acute kidney injury (Xu et al. 2022; Qin et al. 2020); more importantly, in preclinical models of CaOx nephrolithiasis, the selective RIPK3 inhibitor Cpd-42 markedly neutralizes RIPK3-mediated necroptosis, thereby reducing renal parenchymal injury, local inflammatory responses, and ectopic crystal deposition (Hou et al. 2022).

In terms of treatment, targeting the pyroptosis pathway may provide novel strategies for the prevention and treatment of CaOx kidney stones. Studies have shown that vitexin, a natural flavonoid, significantly attenuates CaOx crystal-induced pyroptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells, thereby protecting the kidneys. Its mechanism is associated with the inhibition of ROS production, the blocking of NF-κB signaling pathway activation, and the downregulation of NLRP3 inflammasome expression (Ding et al. 2021). Similarly, relaxin-3 (H3 relaxin) has been demonstrated to alleviate crystal-related renal injury, with its protective effects mainly achieved by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and caspase-1 activation, thereby attenuating pyroptosis and inflammatory responses (Liu et al. 2020). The combination of GSDMD inhibitors and CHMP4B overexpression can significantly relieve RTEC injury and crystal deposition (Yang et al. 2025). Furthermore, engineered nanoparticles can effectively inhibit pyroptosis via targeted delivery of NLRP3 inhibitors, thereby alleviating kidney injury and crystal deposition (Ba et al. 2025).

Multiple regulatory molecules have been identified to modulate CaOx‑induced ferroptosis in RTECs. Sirtuin1 (Sirt1) suppresses ferroptosis and alleviates CaOx nephropathy by recruiting PGC‑1α and NRF2 to co‑activate the transcription of GPX4 (Duan et al. 2024). Similarly, luteolin exerts renoprotective effects by targeting Nr4a1 to restore the Slc7a11‑GPX4 axis, thus inhibiting lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial injury during CaOx stimulation (Ye et al. 2025). ANKRD1, a positive regulator of ferroptosis, is upregulated under oxalate stimulation and inhibits the activity of GPX4 by binding to it, exacerbating lipid peroxidation. In the ANKRD1 knockout mouse model, hyperoxaluria-induced renal injury and the amount of CaOx crystal deposition are lower than those in wild-type mice (Zhao et al. 2023). In contrast, caveolin-1 (CAV1) can inhibit ferroptosis by activating autophagy to clear damaged mitochondria (mitophagy), reducing mtROS production, and reducing iron overload (Yang et al. 2022). In addition, p53 can promote ferroptosis by directly inhibiting the expression of SLC7A11, while Nrf2 can inhibit ferroptosis by upregulating the expression of GPX4 and HO-1 (heme oxygenase-1, which promotes iron metabolism). These pathways collectively form a regulatory network for RTEC ferroptosis (Zhao et al. 2023; Yang et al. 2022).

Dysregulated autophagy is a core pathological driver in CaOx nephrolithiasis, yet elevated LC3/BECN1 expression or autophagosome accumulation does not necessarily indicate enhanced autophagic activity, but may rather reflect impaired autophagic flux (Li et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2023). Maintaining the integrity of autophagic flux is therefore essential for sustaining RTEC homeostasis and preventing crystal deposition, representing a key therapeutic node. The SGLT2 inhibitor dapagliflozin can restore impaired flux via mTOR downregulation, reducing crystal deposition and RTEC injury (Ye et al. 2025; Duan et al. 2024); conversely, lysosomal inhibitors such as chloroquine that block autophagic flux should be avoided (Duan et al. 2018). Inhibition of excessive autophagy attenuates oxidative stress and crystal deposition, whereas restoration of autophagic flux mitigates RTEC injury (Liu et al. 2014, 2025; Duan et al. 2018). Mounting evidence supports that therapeutic strategies should center on normalizing autophagic flux—rather than simply enhancing or inhibiting autophagy—in conjunction with antioxidant agents to scavenge ROS and attenuate maladaptive autophagic activation (Li et al. 2022; Duan et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2023). Collectively, targeted modulation of autophagic activity and related regulated cell death pathways can effectively reverse renal microenvironmental disorders, inhibit pathological crystal deposition, and prevent progression to chronic kidney disease.

Enhancing RTEC repair capacity

Another tractable strategy for intervening in CaOx nephrolithiasis is to augment the intrinsic repair capacity of injured RTECs, which can break the vicious “injury–stone formation” cycle by boosting RTEC proliferation and regeneration, fine-tuning repair-associated signaling cascades, and optimizing cellular metabolic homeostasis. This approach holds great potential to reverse established renal microenvironmental disorders and prevent crystal deposition by restoring the phenotypic and functional homeostasis of injured RTECs (Table 2). Modulating cell proliferation and regenerative responses represents a core component of this therapeutic strategy: the colony-stimulating factor 1 (CSF-1)/CSF-1 receptor (CSF-1R) signaling axis not only directly accelerates RTEC proliferation and suppresses pathological apoptosis, but also drives macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype (Menke et al. 2009; Zhang et al. 2012; Perry and Okusa 2015). These reparative M2 macrophages subsequently secrete hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), epidermal growth factor (EGF), and other trophic factors, further potentiating RTEC repair in a paracrine manner (Dong et al. 2019; Du et al. 2021; D'Angelo et al. 2013). Additionally, circBNC2 encodes the functional ctBNC2 protein, which facilitates the assembly and nuclear translocation of the CDK1/cyclin B1 complex, accelerates the G2-M cell cycle transition, alleviates G2/M phase arrest, reduces the secretion of profibrotic mediators, and mitigates renal fibrosis secondary to chronic RTEC injury (Wang et al. 2022; Bonventre 2011). Collectively, these interventions are integral to sustaining adaptive tubular regeneration and preventing the pathological shift toward repair failure and maladaptive fibrosis.

Fine-tuning repair-related signaling pathways is also indispensable for enhancing RTEC regenerative potential. Transient activation of the WNT/β-catenin pathway effectively stimulates tubular tissue regeneration following acute kidney injury (AKI), yet sustained hyperactivation of this cascade must be avoided to prevent maladaptive renal fibrosis (Schunk et al. 2021; Huffstater et al. 2020). As a pivotal pioneer transcription factor, EGR1 directly binds to the promoter region of the Sox9 gene, upregulates SOX9 expression, promotes RTEC proliferative activity, and cooperatively activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway to reinforce renal reparative responses (Aggarwal et al. 2024; Raza et al. 2021; Li et al. 2018, 2025; Trogisch et al. 2024). Moreover, TLR4-activated monocytes secrete interleukin-22 (IL-22), which markedly accelerates tubular epithelial regeneration; exogenous supplementation of IL-22 has been proven to restore the impaired regenerative capacity of injured RTECs (Kulkarni et al. 2014; Xu et al. 2014; Chen et al. 2021). Direct evidence from CaOx nephrolithiasis models has further corroborated the regenerative potential of pharmacological agents: the SGLT2 inhibitor dapagliflozin, by downregulating mTOR activity and restoring impaired autophagic flux, effectively reduced CaOx crystal deposition and alleviated RTEC injury in glyoxylate-induced non-diabetic mouse models (Liu et al. 2025). This finding aligns with clinical observations from a randomized phase 2 trial, wherein empagliflozin significantly reduced urinary supersaturation ratios for calcium phosphate and uric acid in non-diabetic stone formers, supporting the translational relevance of SGLT2 inhibition in modifying the lithogenic urinary milieu (Anderegg et al. 2025).

Optimizing cellular metabolic reprogramming and phenotypic stability constitutes the third pillar of RTEC repair enhancement. Injured RTECs rely on metabolic shifts toward glycolysis and the pentose phosphate pathway to meet the high bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands of proliferation and antioxidant defense. However, sustained SOX9 expression should be suppressed to prevent RTEC transdifferentiation into a progenitor-like state and subsequent secretion of WNT ligands that activate myofibroblasts and drive fibrosis (Aggarwal et al. 2024). Abnormal EMT also compromises RTEC repair, and targeted interventions—such as IL-11 neutralizing antibodies that block the IL-11/IL-11Rα1 interaction—effectively reverse pathological EMT and preserve the regenerative potential of RTECs (Li et al. 2023).

Fostering a reparative immune microenvironment

Another pivotal strategy for mitigating CaOx nephrolithiasis and facilitating renal tubular epithelial cell repair is to orchestrate immune regulation and inflammation control, which reshapes the hostile inflammatory milieu into a reparative niche by modulating immune cell phenotypes, suppressing excessive inflammatory cascades, and restoring immune metabolic homeostasis. Dysregulated immune responses and persistent inflammation act as core drivers of RTEC injury, crystal deposition, and maladaptive repair, making targeted immune modulation a promising adjunctive therapeutic approach for nephrolithiasis. Macrophages exhibit remarkable phenotypic plasticity, with their functional states ranging along a continuum from pro-inflammatory to reparative profiles, rather than existing as discrete M1 and M2 subsets. Importantly, macrophage phenotypes are dynamically shaped by the local microenvironment and evolve across different stages of disease progression (Hammouri et al. 2026; Chu et al. 2025; Anders and Ryu 2011). Under inflammatory conditions, pharmacological agents and natural compounds have been shown to redirect macrophage functional orientation toward a more reparative state, curtailing the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α and IL-1β. However, it is equally important to recognize that persistent macrophage-derived mediators—including TGF-β1 and PDGF—exert dual effects in renal pathology. While these factors may facilitate tissue repair at appropriate levels and in timely resolution, their sustained production can also drive maladaptive fibrosis, myofibroblast activation, and progressive extracellular matrix deposition (Tsai et al. 2021; Chanu et al. 2023). Thus, the therapeutic goal is not to uniformly "promote" these mediators but rather to maintain their spatiotemporal balance within a beneficial range, avoiding both insufficient repair and excessive fibrogenesis. Additionally, the AIM protein interacts with KIM-1 to augment the phagocytic clearance of necrotic cellular debris by injured RTECs, alleviating local inflammation and relieving tubular obstruction (Arai et al. 2016; Arai and Miyazaki 2018).

Enhancing the abundance and suppressive function of Tregs further reinforces the reparative immune microenvironment. Adoptive transfer of exogenous Tregs effectively suppresses the secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators by Th1/Th17 effector cells, stimulates RTEC proliferation, and attenuates tubular damage; pharmacological or immunotherapeutic approaches that expand endogenous Treg populations or enhance their immunosuppressive activity can also resolve the renal inflammatory microenvironment and accelerate tissue repair (Carney 2017; Herrnstadt and Steinmetz 2021; Paust et al. 2012). It is important to note, however, that these Treg-based interventions remain at the preclinical stage, and their efficacy in the context of CaOx crystal-induced tubular injury has not yet been directly validated. Future studies are therefore warranted to determine whether Treg expansion or adoptive transfer can effectively mitigate crystal-associated inflammation, reduce tubular injury, and prevent stone formation in CaOx nephrolithiasis models, before any clinical translation can be reasonably contemplated.

Targeted inhibition of core inflammatory signaling pathways constitutes another critical intervention. Blocking the NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome axes curbs the excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, breaking the cycle of inflammation-driven RTEC injury (Liu et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2020; Rodriguez et al. 2024). Recombinant human IL‑1 receptor antagonist (rhIL‑1Ra), which blocks IL‑1‑mediated inflammatory signaling, alleviates renal fibrosis by suppressing RNF182‑dependent MFN2 degradation and restoring mitochondrial homeostasis in chronic kidney disease (Yang et al. 2025). Immune metabolic reprogramming further optimizes the reparative niche. Activating mitochondrial pattern recognition receptors such as NLRX1 modulates mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation, attenuating oxidative stress-induced RTEC apoptosis (Stokman et al. 2017; Bi et al. 2024); inhibiting aberrant metabolic reprogramming driven by TLR2/TLR4 signaling prevents ectopic lipid accumulation and lipotoxicity, preserving RTEC viability and function (Shen et al. 2020; Allam et al. 2012). Collectively, these immune-modulatory strategies represent a promising avenue for potentially re-establishing immune homeostasis, attenuating chronic inflammation, and supporting RTEC regeneration and fibrosis resolution, pending validation in CaOx-specific models and clinical trials.

Precise targeted therapy and personalized clinical management

Beyond single-pathway interventions that merely restore the homeostatic balance of RTEC injury and repair, clinical prevention and long-term recurrence management of CaOx nephrolithiasis urgently demand a shift toward precise targeted therapy and personalized care. Significant interindividual heterogeneity in stone pathogenesis arises from divergent oxalate metabolism, genetic susceptibility and gut microbiota profiles; generalized monotherapy regimens frequently fail to sustain long-term suppression of crystal formation, underscoring the necessity of individualized intervention frameworks.

First, CaOx stone development is tightly correlated with urinary protein compositions. Cumulative evidence reveals that certain urinary proteins, such as uromodulin and thrombin fragment 1, mitigate stone genesis by inhibiting CaOx crystal aggregation and epithelial adhesion (Webber et al. 2006; Shi et al. 2025). Furthermore, the glycosylation status of urinary proteins correlates with lithogenic risk, whereby hypo-glycosylation may elevate the propensity for crystal precipitation (Webber et al. 2006). Profiling urinary protein signatures accordingly provides a molecular foundation for personalized stone prophylaxis and therapeutic stratification. Then, targeted regimens against oxidative stress and inflammatory cascades represent promising anti-lithogenic approaches, including the administration of antioxidants and inflammasome inhibitors (Boldt and Di Sole 2025; Khan and Canales 2023). Third, specific enteric bacteria possess intrinsic oxalate-degrading capacity, which lowers urinary oxalate concentrations and diminishes stone risk (Kaufman et al. 2008; Pang et al. 2026). Modulation of gut microbiota via probiotic or synbiotic supplementation may therefore serve as an adjuvant strategy for CaOx stone prevention and treatment (Yang et al. 2025; Marzi et al. 2026). Lastly, individual metabolic phenotypes and dietary patterns exert profound impacts on lithogenesis. Diets with excessive acid burden are linked to elevated CaOx stone risk, whereas moderate calcium intake coupled with restricted oxalate consumption effectively reduces crystal formation susceptibility (Haghighatdoost et al. 2021; Vázquez Carral 2025). Personalized dietary modification and lifestyle adjustments tailored to each patient’s metabolic signature constitute an indispensable component of precision nephrolithiasis management.

Notably, all abovementioned biomarker-guided stratification, multi-target combined regimens and personalized intervention frameworks remain predominantly supported by in vitro RTEC experiments and rodent hyperoxaluria/CaOx crystal models. At present, relevant clinical observational studies and prospective human intervention trials are extremely scarce. Most personalized management strategies are derived from pathway-level mechanistic inference rather than verified clinical efficacy in stone patients. The interindividual metabolic variability in human populations may weaken the preclinical therapeutic effects observed in standardized animal models; further large-cohort clinical investigations are urgently required to validate the clinical practicability of precise and personalized anti-stone interventions before wide clinical translation can be realized.

Conclusions

The injury-repair imbalance of renal tubular epithelial cells acts as a pivotal driver in the pathogenesis of CaOx nephrolithiasis, a highly prevalent urinary disorder associated with high recurrence rates and progressive renal dysfunction globally. RTEC injury under hyperoxaluria or crystal stimulation manifests as multifarious regulated cell death modalities, encompassing apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, coupled with dysregulated autophagy, which collectively disrupt the renal tubular barrier, amplify inflammatory cascades, and enhance crystal adhesion. Concurrently, tubular repair after injury is not a uniform process but diverges into three distinct outcomes. Adaptive tubular regeneration restores epithelial integrity through orderly cell cycle progression, balanced immune crosstalk, and intact autophagic homeostasis. Repair failure occurs when persistent crystal toxic stimuli block RTEC proliferative capacity, leading to sustained proliferative arrest and loss of regenerative potential. Fibrogenic maladaptive repair is driven by partial epithelial-mesenchymal transition, sustained profibrotic signaling, and irreversible extracellular matrix deposition. These compromised repair processes, together with dysregulated immune crosstalk and metabolic reprogramming, give rise to a self-reinforcing vicious cycle of injury-inflammation-repair failure that facilitates CaOx crystal nucleation, growth, and retention (Fig. 6).

The fibrogenic niche formed by injured RTECs, inflammatory cells, and extracellular matrix components further perpetuates the injury-repair imbalance and accelerates renal fibrosis. Emerging evidence from molecular and preclinical studies has highlighted that targeting this core imbalance yields promising therapeutic potential, including inhibiting pathological cell death, restoring autophagic flux, enhancing RTEC regenerative capacity, modulating immune cell polarization, and interrupting fibrogenic signaling networks. The application of single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics has refined the characterization of RTEC subpopulations and their crosstalk with immune and stromal cells, deepening the mechanistic understanding of stone-related renal damage.

Nevertheless, the translation of these experimental findings into clinical practice remains hindered by interspecies differences, the complexity of pathological networks, and the limitations of drug delivery systems. A multifaceted and multidisciplinary strategy is therefore imperative to develop precise, multi-targeted interventions that restore the homeostatic balance between RTEC injury and repair, block stone formation, and halt the progression to chronic kidney disease. Further in-depth investigations into the regulatory networks governing the injury-repair imbalance will provide novel theoretical foundations and translational opportunities for the prevention and targeted treatment of CaOx nephrolithiasis, ultimately reducing the global health burden imposed by this prevalent disorder.
