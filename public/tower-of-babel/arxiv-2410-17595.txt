Computing MHD equilibria of stellarators with a flexible coordinate frame
Florian Hindenlang, Gabriel G. Plunk, Omar Maj

Abstract
For the representation of axi-symmetric plasma configurations (tokamaks), it is natural to use cylindrical coordinates ( R , Z , ϕ ) (R,Z,\phi) , where ϕ \phi is an independent coordinate. The same cylindrical coordinates have also been widely used for representing 3D MHD equilibria of non-axisymmetric configurations (stellarators), with cross-sections, defined in R , Z R,Z -planes, that vary over ϕ \phi . Stellarator equilibria have been found, however, for which cylindrical coordinates are not at all a natural choice, for instance certain stellarators obtained using the near-axis expansion (NAE) [ 1 , 2 ] , defined by a magnetic axis curve and its Frenet frame. In this contribution, we propose an alternative approach for representing the boundary in a fixed-boundary 3D MHD equilibrium solver, moving away from cylindrical coordinates. Instead, we use planar cross-sections whose orientation is determined by a general coordinate frame ( G G -frame). This frame is similar to the conventional Frenet frame, but more flexible. As an additional part of the boundary representation, it becomes an input to the equilibrium solve, along with the geometry of the cross-sections. We see two advantages: 1) the capability to easily represent configurations where the magnetic axis is highly non-planar or even knotted. 2) a reduction in the degrees of freedom needed for the boundary surface, and thus the equilibrium solver, enabling progress in optimization of these configurations. We discuss the properties of the G G -frame, starting from the conventional Frenet frame. Then we show two exemplary ways of constructing it, first from a NAE solution and also from a given boundary surface. We present the details of the implementation of the new frame in the 3D MHD equilibrium solver GVEC. Furthermore, we demonstrate for a highly shaped QI-optimized stellarator that far fewer degrees of freedom are necessary to find a high quality equilibrium solution, compared to the solution computed in cylindrical coordinates.

Introduction

The computation of 3D MHD equilibria of stellarators is a challenging problem. We focus on the so-called fixed-boundary equilibrium solver, that starts from a given closed boundary surface, which acts as a closed flux surface of the plasma region. Thus, the geometrical representation of the boundary surface becomes a crucial part of the equilibrium solve. The main objective of this work is to demonstrate an approach to solving the equilibrium with a generalized and flexible surface representation.
For this purpose, we introduce curvilinear coordinates that are constructed from two linearly independent vectors attached to a guiding curve. The tangent of the curve and the two vectors form a frame which we denote ’
G
-frame’ (general coordinate frame).
We claim that the
G
-frame allows us to represent the geometry of stellarators with closed 3D surfaces of basically any shape.
An important requirement for the
G
-frame is that all its basis vectors are
differentiable functions
everywhere along the curve parameter. The requirement of differentiability is not always met by the conventional Frenet frame, as described in section
3
.

The construction of the
G
-frame is flexible and can be adapted to different boundary surface shapes. We provide two examples of its explicit construction. In the first example, we construct it from the Frenet frame of a NAE solution. In the second example, it is constructed from a boundary surface, taken from the publicly available QUASR database
[
3
]
.
Another construction method that naturally meets the requirements of the
G
-frame is the Bishop frame
[
4
,
5
]
, which is also called the parallel transport frame, or rotation-minimizing frame, and which can be easily constructed discretely
[
6
]
from point positions along a 3D curve.

The main motivation of this work is to use the
G
-frame in the context of the 3D MHD equilibrium solution in stellarators. The conventional and widely used cylinder coordinates, with cross-sections in
R,Z
parametrized over the geometrical toroidal angle
\phi
, has strong limitations on the 3D surface shapes it can represent, and thus artificially restricts the space of possible shapes that can be explored in stellarator optimization.

One of the most prominent 3D MHD equilibrium codes, founding modern computational stellarator optimization, is VMEC
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
. A key idea of VMEC is the assumption of closed nested flux surfaces to find 3D MHD equilibria.
Note that the assumption of a continuum of nested flux surfaces does not necessarily hold in three-dimensional magnetic fields and can lead to current singularities at rational surfaces, as discussed in
[
10
]
.
In VMEC, starting from an initial state of closed nested 3D surfaces, expressed in flux-aligned coordinates (or magnetic coordinates)
[
11
]
, the equilibrium solution is found by minimization of the MHD energy iteratively, changing the 3D surface shapes while keeping them nested.
More recently, 3D MHD equilibrium solvers following the same assumption of nested 3D surfaces have been developed, such as DESC
[
12
]
and GVEC
[
13
]
, being different from each other, as well as from VMEC, in their discretization and the way the iterative solution is found.

When designing GVEC, care was taken separating the actual geometry representation from its toroidal topology. In GVEC, the geometry is described by 2D cross-sections in two coordinates
(q^{1},q^{2})
and parametrized by a third periodic coordinate
\zeta
. The mapping from
(q^{1},q^{2},\zeta)
to Cartesian coordinates is not fixed and can be described by any expression. Simple examples are the periodic cylinder (
x=q^{1},y=q^{2},z=\zeta
) and the conventional toroidal representation (
x=q^{1}\cos(\zeta),y=q^{1}\sin(\zeta),z=q^{2}
). It is this feature that allows us to use the
G
-frame in GVEC to solve 3D MHD equilibria.

Stellarator equilibria are traditionally found by numerical optimization of their boundary shapes, to ensure that certain physical properties are satisfied, for example related to plasma confinement and stability. An alternative and elegant approach to finding optimal stellarator shapes is by use of the near axis expansion (NAE) in Boozer coordinates, as introduced by
[

and extended over the last years

{\bm{N}}^{F}
) and bi-normal (
{\bm{B}}^{F}

As the configurations are defined by the magnetic axis, generally an arbitrary closed curve, completely new optimized stellarator shapes can be found for which cylindrical coordinates are not at all a natural choice. For instance, certain quasi-symmetric and quasi-isodynamic stellarators, even knotted configurations, have been obtained (or ‘constructed’) using NAE

,

2
MHD equilibrium solver

2.1
MHD equilibrium with flexible curvilinear coordinates

In GVEC, we adopt essentially the same approach as in VMEC
[
7
]
to compute the MHD equilibrium. First, under the assumption of closed nested flux surfaces, the geometry is defined by an embedding map

f:(\rho,\vartheta,\zeta)\mapsto(x,y,z)\,,

(1)

mapping from flux-aligned coordinates (a radial coordinate proportional to the square root of the toroidal magnetic flux,
\rho\sim\sqrt{\Phi}
and two periodic coordinates
(\vartheta,\zeta
) to Cartesian coordinates
(x,y,z)
. We decompose the full map
f\coloneqq h\circ\tilde{X}
with

\displaystyle\tilde{X}

\displaystyle:(\rho,\vartheta,\zeta)\mapsto(q^{1},q^{2},q^{3})=\big(X^{1}(\rho,\vartheta,\zeta),X^{2}(\rho,\vartheta,\zeta),\zeta\big)

(2)

\displaystyle{h}

\displaystyle:(q^{1},q^{2},q^{3})\mapsto(x,y,z)={h}(q^{1},q^{2},q^{3})

(3)

leaving
X^{1},X^{2}
as functions of
(\rho,\vartheta,\zeta)
to describe the geometry of each cross-section along
\zeta
. The map
{h}
is fixed throughout the equilibrium calculation but can be arbitrarily specified by the user at the beginning. Simple examples of
{h}
are given by the periodic cylinder
(x,y,z)\coloneqq(q^{1},q^{2},\zeta)
and the conventional cylindrical representation
(x,y,z)\coloneqq(q^{1}\cos\zeta,q^{1}\sin\zeta,q^{2})=(R\cos\phi,R\sin\phi,Z)
.

It is always assumed that
(x,y,z)=h(q^{1},q^{2},q^{3})
is an orientation-preserving coordinate transformation, i.e., the Jacobian determinant is strictly positive,
\det(Dh)>0
. (Here and in the following
D
denotes the derivative operator.) For the composition
f=h\circ\tilde{X}
to be defined, the function
\tilde{X}
must take values in the domain of definition of
h
. This is the only constraint that links
h
and
\tilde{X}
. In addition, we require (independently of
h
) that

\det D\tilde{X}=\left(\partial_{\rho}X^{1}\partial_{\vartheta}X^{2}-\partial_{\rho}X^{2}\partial_{\vartheta}X^{1}\right)>0,\quad\text{for $\rho>0$},

which is the Jacobian of the transformation
(\rho,\vartheta)\mapsto(X^{1},X^{2})
.
Hence, for any fixed angle
\zeta
, the map
(q^{1},q^{2})=\big(X^{1}(\rho,\vartheta,\zeta),X^{2}(\rho,\vartheta,\zeta)\big)
is an orientation-preserving diffeomorphism from the unit disk, parametrized by polar coordinates
(\rho,\vartheta)
, into a domain in the
(q^{1},q^{2})
-plane. By the chain rule,
Df=Dh(\tilde{X})D\tilde{X}
, we have that
f
is an orientation-preserving diffeomorphisms away from the polar singularity at
\rho=0
.

The magnetic field is defined by the geometry, an additional angle transformation
\lambda(\rho,\vartheta,\zeta)
and the rotational transform profile
\iota(\rho)=\chi^{\prime}(\rho)/\Phi^{\prime}(\rho)
, with the poloidal magnetic flux
\chi
,

\mathrm{B}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}(b^{\vartheta}e_{\vartheta}+b^{\zeta}e_{\zeta})\,,\quad b^{\vartheta}\coloneqq(\chi^{\prime}-\Phi^{\prime}\partial_{\zeta}\lambda)\,,\quad b^{\zeta}\coloneqq\Phi^{\prime}(1+\partial_{\vartheta}\lambda)\,.

(4)

Finally, the equilibrium, defined by the flux surface geometry (
X^{1},X^{2}
) and
\lambda
, is found via minimization of total MHD energy:

\displaystyle W(X^{1},X^{2},\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\mathrm{B}^{2}+\frac{p(\rho)}{\gamma-1}dV

(5)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(\frac{1}{2\mu_{0}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\left(b^{\vartheta}g_{\vartheta\zeta}b^{\zeta}\right)+\frac{p(\rho)}{\gamma-1}\sqrt{g}\right)\,d\rho\,d\vartheta\,d\zeta\,,

with a given pressure profile
p(\rho)
and total toroidal flux
\Phi(1)
.

As the full map
f
is decomposed into
f=h\circ\tilde{X}
, the metric terms in (
5
) are computed as

\sqrt{g}=\sqrt{G}\left(\frac{\partial X^{1}}{\partial\rho}\frac{\partial X^{2}}{\partial\vartheta}-\frac{\partial X^{2}}{\partial\rho}\frac{\partial X^{1}}{\partial\vartheta}\right)\,,\quad g_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{\partial q^{i}}{\partial\alpha}G_{ij}\frac{\partial q^{j}}{\partial\beta}\,,\quad G_{ij}=\frac{\partial h}{\partial q^{i}}\cdot\frac{\partial h}{\partial q^{j}}\,,

(6)

with
(q^{1},q^{2},q^{3})\coloneqq(X^{1},X^{2},\zeta)
and
i,j=1,2,3
and
\alpha,\beta\in[\rho,\vartheta,\zeta]
.

2.2
Curvilinear coordinates constructed from a G-Frame

We start from an arbitrary parametrized curve
{\bm{X}}_{0}(\zeta)
, with derivative
d{\bm{X}}_{0}(\zeta)/d\zeta=:{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}(\zeta)\not=0
. As we will only represent closed surfaces, the curve must be closed (periodic) and we choose
\zeta\in[0,2\pi]
. We attach two vectors
{\bm{N}}(\zeta),{\bm{B}}(\zeta)
to the curve, such that they form a right-handed frame with the tangent
\bm{T}:={\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}
as the third vector. We refer to the system of
\bm{T},{\bm{N}},{\bm{B}}
as
G
-frame.

In this paper, we define the coordinate map
h
in terms of the
G
-frame as

{h}:(q^{1},q^{2},\zeta)\mapsto(x,y,z)={\bm{X}}_{0}(\zeta)+q^{1}{\bm{N}}(\zeta)+q^{2}{\bm{B}}(\zeta)\,.

(7)

In order to compute the metric terms in (
6
), we need first derivatives of
{\bm{X}}_{0},{\bm{N}},{\bm{B}}

\qquad\frac{\partial{h}}{\partial q^{1}}={\bm{N}}\,,\quad\frac{\partial{h}}{\partial q^{2}}={\bm{B}}\,,\quad\frac{\partial{h}}{\partial q^{3}}=\frac{\partial{h}}{\partial\zeta}={\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}+q^{1}{\bm{N}}^{\prime}+q^{2}{\bm{B}}^{\prime}=:{\tilde{\bm{T}}_{q}}\,.

(8)

Then the metric tensor and determinant of
{h}
amount to

G=\left[{\begin{array}[]{ccc}|{\bm{N}}|^{2}&{\bm{N}}\cdot{\bm{B}}&{\bm{N}}\cdot{\tilde{\bm{T}}_{q}}\\
&|{\bm{B}}|^{2}&{\bm{B}}\cdot{\tilde{\bm{T}}_{q}}\\
\text{\tiny symm.}&&|{\tilde{\bm{T}}_{q}}|^{2}\\
\end{array}}\right]\,,\quad\mathcal{J}_{h}={\tilde{\bm{T}}_{q}}\cdot\left({\bm{N}}\times{\bm{B}}\right)\,.

(9)

When computing variations of the MHD energy with respect to
X^{1},X^{2}
, we also need to compute the derivatives of
\mathcal{J}_{h},G
with respect to
q^{1},q^{2}
, which can be easily obtained.

Note that, similar to the singularity of the
RZ
-frame at
R=0
, this coordinate frame becomes invalid where two consecutive
{\bm{N}},{\bm{B}}
-planes intersect. Since the Jacobian is linear in
q^{1},q^{2}
, this singularity appears as a line, at a distance from the curve at a curvature radius
1/\kappa(\zeta)
.

As this limit depends directly on the given coordinate frame, we can not make a general statement of this restriction. Up to now, we have not yet found a conflicting boundary surface for QI-optimized stellarators, since they tend to align and stretch along the bi-normal direction in regions of high curvature of the axis, thus remaining far away from the singular line. A detailed discussion in found in
A
.

3
The general coordinate frame

3.1
The conventional Frenet frame

In order to motivate why we have introduced a
G
-frame, we first discuss the properties of the conventional Frenet frame.
It is fully defined by an arbitrary parametrized curve
{\bm{X}}_{0}(\zeta)
, with derivative
d{\bm{X}}_{0}(\zeta)/d\zeta={\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}(\zeta)\not=0
. As we will only represent closed surfaces, the axis must be closed (periodic) and we choose
\zeta\in[0,2\pi]
.

Figure 3:
Construction of the local Frenet frame from first and second derivatives of the curve
{\bm{X}}_{0}(\zeta)
. The normal
{\bm{N}}^{F}
scales with the second derivative, so it must be
\neq 0
to yield a valid Frenet frame.

To avoid confusion with the previously introduced notation, we will use the superscript ’
F
’ when referring to the conventional Frenet frame. Note that
\bm{B}
does not refer to the magnetic field.
The Frenet frame satisfies the following ODE along the curve
{\bm{X}}_{0}(\ell)
,

\frac{d}{d\ell}\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{T}}^{F}\\
{\bm{N}}^{F}\\
{\bm{B}}^{F}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\kappa(\ell){\bm{N}}^{F}\\
-\kappa(\ell){\bm{T}}^{F}+\tau(\ell){\bm{B}}^{F}\\
-\tau(\ell){\bm{N}}^{F}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(10)

with arc-length
\ell
, curvature
\kappa
, torsion
\tau
.
The arc-length is a function of the chosen parametrization of the curve

\ell(\zeta)=\int_{0}^{\zeta}|{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}(\tilde{\zeta})|\mathrm{d}\tilde{\zeta},\qquad\ell^{\prime}\,(\zeta)=\frac{d\ell}{d\zeta}=|{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}(\zeta)|.

(11)

As shown in Fig.
3
and (
12
), the Frenet frame is locally computed from the first and second derivatives of the curve

{\bm{T}}^{F}=\frac{d{\bm{X}}_{0}}{d\ell}=\frac{d\zeta}{d\ell}\frac{d{\bm{X}}_{0}(\zeta)}{d\zeta}=\frac{{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}}{|{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}|}\,,\quad{\bm{B}}^{F}=\frac{{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}\times{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{|{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}\times{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime\prime}|}\,,\quad{\bm{N}}^{F}={\bm{B}}^{F}\times{\bm{T}}^{F}\,,

(12)

and the curvature and torsion are

\kappa(\ell)=\frac{\left|{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}\times{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime\prime}\right|}{|{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}|^{3}}\,,\quad\tau(\ell)=\frac{({\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}\times{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime\prime})\cdot{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime\prime\prime}}{|{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}\times{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}\,.

(13)

Equations (
12
) define a smooth orthonormal frame on the considered curve, provided that
{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}(\zeta)\times{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)\not=0
. If this quantity vanishes, the bi-normal
{\bm{B}}^{F}
is indefinite and this can happen if and only if
\kappa(\zeta)=0
. (We recall that
{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}(\zeta)\not=0
per assumption, so that the unit tangent
{\bm{T}}^{F}
is always well-defined.) Let us suppose that the curvature vanishes at isolated points only, which appears to be the case in practice. If
\zeta=\zeta_{0}
is an isolated zero of the vector-valued function
{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}(\zeta)\times{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)
, we must have
{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}(\zeta)\times{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)=(\zeta-\zeta_{0})^{n}\bm{u}_{0}+O\big(|\zeta-\zeta_{0}|^{n+1}\big)
, where
\bm{u}_{0}\not=0
is a constant vector and the integer
n
is the order of the zero. Then, near
\zeta_{0}
, we have

{\bm{B}}^{F}(\zeta)=\frac{{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}(\zeta)\times{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)}{|{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}(\zeta)\times{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)|}=\bigg(\frac{\zeta-\zeta_{0}}{|\zeta-\zeta_{0}|}\bigg)^{n}\frac{\bm{u}_{0}}{|\bm{u}_{0}|}+O\big(|\zeta-\zeta_{0}|\big),

and thus

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}{\bm{B}}^{F}(\zeta_{0}\pm\varepsilon)=(\pm 1)^{n}\frac{\bm{u}_{0}}{|\bm{u}_{0}|}.

We deduce that the standard definition of the bi-normal vector given in equations (
12
) has a discontinuity at an isolated zero of
\kappa
if the integer
n
is odd. It is continuous otherwise.
When the discontinuity occurs, it amounts to a change of sign in the direction of the unit vector
\bm{u}_{0}/|\bm{u}_{0}|
. Simple examples of this behaviour are given by the curves
{\bm{X}}_{0}(\zeta)=(\zeta,\zeta^{3},0)
and
{\bm{X}}_{0}(\zeta)=(\zeta,\zeta^{4},0)
for
\zeta
in a neighborhood of
\zeta_{0}=0
. In both cases
\zeta=\zeta_{0}=0
is an isolated zero of the curvature, with
n=1
and
n=2
, respectively. In the following, we refer to these discontinuities as a "flip" of the bi-normal. Since, under the assumptions, the unit tangent is smooth, and the Frenet system is right-handed by definition, a flip of the bi-normal implies a flip of the normal vector.

The Frenet frame has the advantage of being defined solely by the curve. However, the following disadvantages exist:

1.

The Frenet frame is not defined at points of zero curvature. As stated above, the
bi-normal (and normal) of the frame can flip their direction when crossing such points. This is particularly problematic for stellarator applications since zero curvature points are necessary for quasi-isodynamic
stellarators
[
2
]
, and is depicted in the top-left of Fig.
4
.

2.

Another subtlety is related to the global self-linking number of the Frenet frame. The
self-linking number
is the number of rotations of the frame when following it along one turn. First, it is convenient to have zero self-linking number for the coordinate frame, for example to ensure that the rotational transform can be determined in a straightforward manner. Second, it is possible that, after correcting for the frame flips mentioned above, the resulting frame is not periodic. For example in stellarators with odd field period numbers where there is
1/2
rotation of the frame in each field period, a sign difference remains after a full toroidal transit. An example of a Frenet frame having a self-linking number of
-4
is shown top-right of Fig.
4
.

(N,B)
-frame by an axis-centered rectangle swept along the axis, the surface color marks the directions

.

3.2
Defining properties of a G-Frame

As we have stated above, a
G
-frame is defined by a guiding curve and two basis vectors.
Here we formulate the following
requirements
:

1.

A smooth closed guiding curve
{\bm{X}}_{0}(\zeta)
is provided, that serves as origin of the cross-sections and its tangent
{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}(\zeta)
serves as the first basis vector

2.

The second and third basis vectors
{\bm{N}}(\zeta)
,
{\bm{B}}(\zeta)
are provided and are
required to be differentiable functions
of the parameter of the curve
\zeta
.

3.

Naturally, the vectors
{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}
,
{\bm{N}}
and
{\bm{B}}
must form a right-handed coordinate system
{\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}\cdot({\bm{N}}\times{\bm{B}})>0
, but they
do not need to be orthogonal
.

Hence, the
G
-frame allows us to effectively undo the twist that comes about in cases of non-zero self-linking number. A visualization of the resulting
G
-frame is shown in the bottom of Fig.
4
.

Finally, a boundary surface is described by two coordinates
X_{b}^{1}(\vartheta,\zeta),X_{b}^{2}(\vartheta,\zeta)
and the mapping to 3D Cartesian coordinates, using the basis vectors of the
G
-frame:

\bm{x}(\vartheta,\zeta)={\bm{X}}_{0}(\zeta)+X_{b}^{1}(\vartheta,\zeta){\bm{N}}(\zeta)+X_{b}^{2}(\vartheta,\zeta){\bm{B}}(\zeta)\,.

(14)

3.3
Interface to the
G
-frame

In GVEC, we defined an interface to the
G
-frame, that has the following properties:

1.

It is assumed that
{\bm{X}}_{0}(\zeta),{\bm{N}}(\zeta),{\bm{B}}(\zeta)
are smoothly differentiable along the curve parameter
\zeta
.

2.

The frame is given at a discrete set of points
on the full torus

\zeta_{i}=\frac{i-1/2}{n_{p}}(2\pi)\,,\quad i=1,\dots,n_{p}

(15)

3.

The number of points
n_{p}
must be a multiple of the field periodicity
n_{\textrm{FP}}
of the configuration, in order to be able to exactly represent a single field period.

4.

Cartesian coordinates are used to represent the curve position
{\bm{X}}_{0}(\zeta_{i})
and normal and bi-normal vectors
{\bm{N}}(\zeta_{i}),{\bm{B}}(\zeta_{i})
.

The
G
-frame is not unique and thus there are many ways to construct it. This flexibility in the definition of the
G
-frame is intended to allow users to choose a construction that suits the cases of interest to them. One prominent candidate is the Bishop frame, also called the ’parallel transport frame’
[
4
,
5
]
or ’rotation-minimizing frame’
[
6
]
.

Another possibility to construct a
G
-frame is to start from a known conventional Frenet frame, identify its flips and twists, then define a corresponding rotation angle function
\Gamma(\zeta)
and apply a rigid rotation of the
({\bm{N}}^{F},{\bm{B}}^{F})
plane by

\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{T}}(\zeta)\\
{\bm{N}}(\zeta)\\
{\bm{B}}(\zeta)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&\quad\cos(\Gamma(\zeta))&\sin(\Gamma(\zeta))\\
0&-\sin(\Gamma(\zeta))&\cos(\Gamma(\zeta))\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{T}}^{F}(\zeta)\\
{\bm{N}}^{F}(\zeta)\\
{\bm{B}}^{F}(\zeta)\end{pmatrix}\,.

(16)

4
Constructing the general coordinate frame

4.1
Construction from a NAE solution

The first construction of the
G
-frame starts from the Frenet frame along the magnetic axis of a QI-optimized NAE configuration
[
19
]
henceforth called the
N2-12
configuration. In this case, the Frenet frame was obtained by solving the ODE (
10
) with a given
signed
curvature function,
\kappa^{s}
, as plotted in Figure
5
. As a consequence, the bi-normal (and normal) directions as defined in equations (
12
) flip at two locations, namely at
\zeta=\pi/2
and
\zeta=3\pi/2
, and the
signed
Frenet frame can be defined as
({\bm{T}}^{S},{\bm{N}}^{S},{\bm{B}}^{S})=({\bm{T}}^{F},s_{\kappa}{\bm{N}}^{F},s_{\kappa}{\bm{B}}^{F})
, where
s_{\kappa}=\text{sign}(\kappa^{s})
.

Figure 5:
Signed curvature
\kappa^{s}
and torsion for the magnetic axis of the
N2-12
configuration. The horizontal axis is arc-length, which varies from
0
to
\pi
over the first field period.

Additionally, this signed Frenet frame exhibits a twist of
-\pi
within a field period. Thus, we introduce a constant rotation of the frame from
0
to
-\pi
, within each field period, in order to ’untwist’ the frame. Altogether, by using equation (
16
), with the rotation function

\Gamma^{\text{{N2-12}}}(\zeta)=(s_{\kappa}-1)\pi/2-\pi\left(\frac{n_{\textrm{FP}}}{2\pi}\zeta-\left\lfloor\frac{n_{\textrm{FP}}}{2\pi}\zeta\right\rfloor\right)\,,\quad\zeta\in[0,2\pi]\,,

(17)

we finally obtain a continuous
G
-frame, without net rotation. The operator
\left\lfloor\frac{n_{\textrm{FP}}}{2\pi}\zeta\right\rfloor
yields an integer within each field period.

Furthermore, due to the choice of the point positions (
15
), the discrete point set excludes the points of zero curvature at
\zeta=[1,3,\dots,2n_{\textrm{FP}}-1]\frac{\pi}{n_{\textrm{FP}}}
as well as the points where the rotation function jumps, at
\zeta=[0,2,\dots,2n_{\textrm{FP}}]\frac{\pi}{n_{\textrm{FP}}}
.

In this section, we illustrate an alternative construction of the

1

The boundary surface is shown in Figure

Note that all

The idea of the proposed construction method is to produce a
G
-frame that follows the orientation of the
\zeta
contour lines. Hence, if a surface is parametrized in the
RZ
-frame, the contour lines of
\zeta
remain in the

-frame was constructed in the previous section

, the proposed construction method from its boundary surface would result in the exact same

-frame.
We propose the following steps as a possible construction method from a given boundary surface:

Step 1: Evaluate surface in Cartesian space:

The target surface, given as
\bm{x}(\vartheta,\zeta)
, is sampled on a set of
(\vartheta_{j},\zeta_{i})
positions on the full torus

\zeta_{i}=\frac{i-1/2}{n_{z}}2\pi\,,\quad\vartheta_{j}=\frac{j-1/2}{n_{t}}2\pi\,,\qquad i=1,\dots,n_{z},\quad j=1,\dots,n_{t}\,.

(18)

If the number
n_{z}
is chosen as a multiple of the number of field periods
n_{FP}
, it is possible to reduce the discrete dataset exactly to one field period.

Step 2: Project to surface with elliptical cross-sections:

The lines of constant
\zeta
are Fourier transformed in
\vartheta
, yielding closed curves
{{\bm{x}}|_{\zeta}}(\vartheta)
. The zero Fourier mode describes a curve along parameter
\zeta
. It serves as the guiding curve
{\bm{X}}_{0}
of the
G
-frame.

The first Fourier mode of
{{\bm{x}}|_{\zeta}}(\vartheta)
describes a set of planar ellipse curves, centered at the guiding curve
{\bm{X}}_{0}
, as shown in green in Figure
6
. Note that they are not necessarily orthogonal to the guiding curve tangent vector.

Step 3: Compute the plane of the ellipse cross-sections:

At each position
\zeta_{i}
, the second basis vector
{\bm{N}}
is constructed from
{\bm{X}}_{0}(\zeta_{i})
through
{{\bm{x}}|_{\zeta}}(\vartheta=0)
, with a unit length.
Using the point
{{\bm{x}}|_{\zeta}}(\vartheta=\pi/2)
, the unit normal of the ellipse plane
{\bm{K}}
is computed and yields the third basis vector
{\bm{B}}=({\bm{K}}\times{\bm{N}})
being orthogonal to
{\bm{N}}
and of unit length.

In the third step, the direction of the second basis vector

was fixed to the surface parameter
\vartheta=0
. The resulting

It has a zero self-linking number, but also twists strongly at the end of each field period.

Knowing that in each cross-section, we have a two-dimensional centered rotated ellipse, we can relate its Fourier coefficients to the rotation angle of the ellipse, see details in
B

{\bm{N}}
and
{\bm{B}}

In the final frame, the rotating ellipse is represented by a single poloidal and a single toroidal Fourier mode,

The final step is to cut the surface with the
{\bm{N}},{\bm{B}}
-planes of the
G

\bm{x}(\vartheta_{j},\zeta)

G
-frame.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed a new and flexible approach of representing the geometry of 3D MHD equilibria of strongly shaped stellarators. Motivated by the discovery of new configurations found by means of the near-axis expansion (NAE), we adopt the idea of an axis-following frame with cross-sections normal to the axis, instead of the usual cylindrical coordinates (’
RZ
-frame’).
We show the deficiencies of the conventional Frenet frame, such as abrupt flips of the basis vector directions or unnecessary twists and we introduce a general coordinate frame (
G
-frame),
which overcomes these deficiencies. We describe the steps needed for implementation of the
G
-frame in the 3D MHD equilibrium solver GVEC.

Using the
G
-frame for the particular case of a two-field-periodic QI-configuration, we show that far fewer degrees of freedom are needed for the representation of the boundary, compared to the
RZ
-frame. Consequently, we found that an accurate equilibrium solution, obtained in GVEC with the
G
-frame, also needs less resolution and less iterations to converge, compared to the equilibrium solution with the
RZ
-frame.

We want to stress that the approach is not inherently reliant on a NAE solution. Therefore, we demonstrate that it is also possible to construct the
G
-frame from a boundary surface, without knowledge of the magnetic axis or its Frenet frame.

Computing 3D MHD equilibria in a
RZ
-frame has strong limitations on the 3D shapes it can represent, artificially restricting the space of possible shapes in stellarator optimization. Recently, the first optimization of a Figure-8 shaped stellarator was conducted using GVEC
[
22
]
, which was made possible by using the
G
-frame for the boundary surface representation, based on a quasi-isodynamic NAE solution.

We believe that a frame that follows the shape of the surface, such as the
G
-frame, will allow the representation of stellarators of virtually any shape with minimal number of degrees of freedom, and thereby open paths for progress in stellarator optimization. We show with two examples on how to construct the
G
-frame, and hope to gain more insight on how to optimally represent any boundary surface in the future.

Appendix A
Behavior of the Jacobian of the map
{h}
with the
G
-frame

Given the map
{h}
in (
7
) using the
G
-frame, only first derivatives of
{\bm{X}}_{0},{\bm{N}},{\bm{B}}
, as already used in (
9
), will be necessary.
The Jacobian determinant is defined as

\mathcal{J}_{h}\coloneqq\frac{\partial h}{\partial q^{3}}\cdot\left(\frac{\partial h}{\partial q_{1}}\times\frac{\partial h}{\partial q_{2}}\right)={\tilde{\bm{T}}_{q}}\cdot({\bm{N}}\times{\bm{B}})\quad\text{with}\quad{\tilde{\bm{T}}_{q}}:={\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}+q^{1}{\bm{N}}^{\prime}+q^{2}{\bm{B}}^{\prime}.

(20)

The derivatives of the Jacobian determinant are conveniently computed from (
20
),

\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}_{h}}{\partial q^{1}}={\bm{N}}^{\prime}\cdot({\bm{N}}\times{\bm{B}})\,,\quad\frac{\partial\mathcal{J}_{h}}{\partial q^{2}}={\bm{B}}^{\prime}\cdot({\bm{N}}\times{\bm{B}}).

(21)

Note that these identities can be computed directly, using the
G
-frame interface from section
3.3
.

Figure 16:
Cross-sections in the
-X^{1},X^{2}
coordinates of the
G
-frame, computed from the boundary surface presented in section
4.2
. The region of negative Jacobian is shown in gray, at the minimum distance from the origin
d_{\text{min}}
.

As the Jacobian
\mathcal{J}_{h}
is linear in
q^{1},q^{2}
, we can express it as

\mathcal{J}_{h}(q^{1},q^{2},q^{3})=\mathcal{J}_{0}+q^{1}\mathcal{J}_{1}+q^{2}\mathcal{J}_{2},

(22)

where
\mathcal{J}_{i}
, for
i=0,1,2
, depends only on
q^{3}=\zeta
, with

\mathcal{J}_{0}={\bm{X}}_{0}^{\prime}\cdot({\bm{N}}\times{\bm{B}}),\qquad\mathcal{J}_{1}={\bm{N}}^{\prime}\cdot({\bm{N}}\times{\bm{B}}),\qquad\mathcal{J}_{2}={\bm{B}}^{\prime}\cdot({\bm{N}}\times{\bm{B}}).

(23)

In each cross-section (
\zeta
=const.), the point of minimum distance in
q^{1},q^{2}
where the Jacobian becomes zero is

q^{1}_{\text{min}}=-\frac{\mathcal{J}_{0}\mathcal{J}_{1}}{\mathcal{J}_{1}^{2}+\mathcal{J}_{2}^{2}},\quad q^{2}_{\text{min}}=-\frac{\mathcal{J}_{0}\mathcal{J}_{2}}{\mathcal{J}_{1}^{2}+\mathcal{J}_{2}^{2}}.

(24)

The distance from the origin is then

d_{\text{min}}=\sqrt{(q^{1}_{\text{min}})^{2}+(q^{2}_{\text{min}})^{2}}=\frac{\mathcal{J}_{0}}{\sqrt{\mathcal{J}_{1}^{2}+\mathcal{J}_{2}^{2}}},

(25)

and the line of zero Jacobian is then expressed as

\left(q^{1}_{\text{min}}-q^{2}_{\text{min}}t,\quad q^{2}_{\text{min}}+q^{1}_{\text{min}}t)\right),

(26)

which limits the domain of positive Jacobian in
q^{1},q^{2}
to one side.

Taking the example of the
G
-frame computed from the boundary surface in section
4.2
, one can visualize the cross-sections along with the region of negative Jacobian, as shown in Figure
16
. At
\zeta=0
the distance is the smallest, but still allows for a much larger boundary surface, thus allowing to increase the aspect ratio while keeping the
G
-frame fixed. Also, it can be seen that the major axis of the cross-section tends to align with the line of zero Jacobian.

Appendix B
Rotated ellipse representation in 2D

In this section, we relate the representation of a centered rotated ellipse in two dimensions to its Fourier coefficients.

Figure 17:
Sketch of the centered rotated ellipse in the 2D plane, with coordinates
X^{1},X^{2}
, rotation angle
{\Gamma_{e}}
and offset
\vartheta_{0}

As shown in Figure
17
, the general form of a rotated ellipse centered at the origin of
X^{1},X^{2}
is

\displaystyle X^{1}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\quad\cos({\Gamma_{e}})a\cos(\vartheta+\vartheta_{0})+\sin({\Gamma_{e}})b\sin(\vartheta+\vartheta_{0})\,,

(27)

\displaystyle X^{2}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=-\sin({\Gamma_{e}})a\cos(\vartheta+\vartheta_{0})+\cos({\Gamma_{e}})b\sin(\vartheta+\vartheta_{0})\,,

with minor radius
a
, major radius
b
, rotation angle
{\Gamma_{e}}
and shift
\vartheta_{0}
. Another equivalent form is using four coefficients
x^{1}_{c},x^{1}_{s},x^{2}_{c},x^{2}_{s}
and,

\displaystyle X^{1}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=x^{1}_{c}\cos(\vartheta)+x^{1}_{s}\sin(\vartheta)\,,

(28)

\displaystyle X^{2}(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=x^{2}_{c}\cos(\vartheta)+x^{2}_{s}\sin(\vartheta)\,.

We write the coefficients in terms of
\varepsilon^{+}=(a+b)/2,\varepsilon^{-}=(a-b)/2,{\Gamma_{e}},\vartheta_{0}
as

\displaystyle x^{1}_{c}

\displaystyle=\quad\varepsilon^{+}\cos({\Gamma_{e}}-\vartheta_{0})+\varepsilon^{-}\cos({\Gamma_{e}}+\vartheta_{0})\,,

(29)

\displaystyle x^{1}_{s}

\displaystyle=\quad\varepsilon^{+}\sin({\Gamma_{e}}-\vartheta_{0})-\varepsilon^{-}\sin({\Gamma_{e}}+\vartheta_{0})\,,

\displaystyle x^{2}_{c}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon^{+}\sin({\Gamma_{e}}-\vartheta_{0})-\varepsilon^{-}\sin({\Gamma_{e}}+\vartheta_{0})\,,

\displaystyle x^{2}_{s}

\displaystyle=\quad\varepsilon^{+}\cos({\Gamma_{e}}-\vartheta_{0})-\varepsilon^{-}\cos({\Gamma_{e}}+\vartheta_{0})\,,

and then express the parameters of (
27
) in terms of the Fourier coefficients as

a=\varepsilon^{+}+\varepsilon^{-},\quad b=\varepsilon^{+}-\varepsilon^{-}\,\,,\quad\varepsilon^{(\pm)}=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{(x^{1}_{c}\pm x^{2}_{s})^{2}+(x^{1}_{s}\mp x^{2}_{c})^{2}}\,.

(30)

and

{\Gamma_{e}}\pm\vartheta_{0}=\arctan\left(\frac{x^{1}_{s}\pm x^{2}_{c}}{x^{2}_{s}\mp x^{1}_{c}}\right)\,.

(31)

Note that the rotation function
\Gamma(\zeta)
shown in Figure
7
is computed from
(\vartheta_{0}-{\Gamma_{e}})
.
