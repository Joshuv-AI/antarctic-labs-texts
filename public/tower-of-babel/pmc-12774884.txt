Doughnut-like wrinkled fibrous nano silica architecture as a novel and effective reusable green heterogeneous nanocatalyst for the Hantzsch reaction
Authors: Elham Sohrabifard, Ali Reza Kiasat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In the present study, we engineered a novel amphoteric monodisperse colloid (MOS − KCC-1) featuring a unique doughnut-like wrinkled fibrous nanosilica structure. This research focused on designing a novel internally cross-linked wrinkled fibrous organosilica nanocomposite incorporating organic basic bridges within its framework. To achieve this, a trivalent organosilane bridging agent was synthesized via a facile nucleophilic substitution reaction between cyanuric chloride and (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane. Subsequently, this triazine organosilica (TS) cross-linker was integrated into the pore walls of dendritic fibrous nanostructured silica using a sol–gel process under open-vessel reflux conditions. The resulting heterogeneous hybrid nanocomposite, KCC-Triazine, was characterized by FT-IR, FE-SEM, TEM, XRD, TGA, BET and EDX mapping. The rationale behind this design was that the wrinkled fibrous structure, with its numerous pores and slits, would function as a nanoreactor and serve as an excellent catalyst in organic transformations due to the presence of organic basic units within the pore walls. Given the utility of polyhydroquinoline (PHQ) and polyhydroacridine (PHA) scaffolds as nitrogen-based heterocycles with diverse biological activities, the catalytic performance of the nanocomposite was evaluated in the synthesis of these classes of compounds via the one-pot, four-component Hantzsch condensation reaction between aldehydes, active methylene compounds and NH4OAc under solvent-free conditions. The combination of high catalytic activity and good reusability, along with the convenience of solvent-free conditions, makes this method an attractive option for the facile and efficient synthesis of basic nitrogen-containing heterocycles. Furthermore, the nanocatalyst can be easily separated from the reaction mixture and reused multiple times without a significant loss in activity, offering both economic and environmental advantages.

Introduction

The synthesis of nanoarchitectured mesoporous silica materials is fundamental to the advancement of multifunctional materials science. Their distinctive textural properties and well-defined morphologies enable a broad spectrum of applications, including catalysis, sensing, energy storage, etc.1,2. Among these, catalysis stands out as a key component of sustainable chemistry, fundamental to organic transformations. The growing adoption of heterogeneous catalysis over homogeneous methods in green synthesis, particularly in pharmaceutical applications, reflects this trend3,4. In this regard, over recent years, special attention has been paid to the use of conventional ordered mesoporous silica (OMS) materials, such as FDU, KIT-6, KIT-5, SBA-16, SBA-15, MCM-48, and MCM-41, which are highly valued in catalytic applications. Their exceptional physicochemical properties, including large surface area, environmentally friendly water-based synthesis (sol–gel process), biodegradability, excellent chemical and thermal stability, functionalizable and tunable porous morphology and reusability, make them highly effective in various catalytic processes5–8. However, several challenges have occasionally restricted the applications of OMS materials. These include pore instability under extreme heat and pressure due to thin walls, tubular pore structures and pore-blocking phenomena, as well as the low density of surface silanol groups in silica frameworks, limited guest molecule loading capacity, and poor accessibility of active sites within ordered mesopores9,10.

One of the most recent innovations in mesoporous silica is KCC-1, which has gained prominence as a rising and promising star in catalytic heterogeneous systems11. KCC-1 is an open three-dimensional (3D) nanosilica with a unique bicontinuous concentric lamellar structure, flexible and fibrous morphology, hierarchical porosity, high biocompatibility, well-modified surface properties, extensive surface area, accessible active sites, customizable pore sizes and shapes, selective permeability, tunable nanochannels volume and superior thermal/hydrothermal/mechanical stability12–16. Unlike conventional mesoporous silica materials, the uniqueness of KCC-1 arises from homocentric radially extending fibers that originate from the core of each silica sphere. This structural feature prevents the blocking of active sites, thereby enhancing the catalytic activity of the nanostructure. The fibrous morphology not only provides an exceptionally high surface area but also improves accessibility to active sites. In addition, the presence of homocentric radial cavities and a high density of surface silanol groups facilitates easy surface modification. The highly accessible internal space allows for high loading and diffusion of guest molecules into its channels with minimal restriction, resulting in outstanding catalytic performance and improved recovery efficiencies10,13,17–21. However, the amorphous nature of the concentric fibers of KCC-1, combined with the abundance of surface silanol groups, results in a hydrophilic surface, which can limit its catalytic activity. Several strategies have been explored to enhance its hydrophobicity, including the incorporation of organic framework moieties and silylation of surface silanol groups3,22.

Periodic mesoporous organosilicas (PMOs) are a type of classical mesoporous silica material that features an organic–inorganic hybrid structure with a high surface area, well-ordered mesopores, superior thermal stability and reusability. The role of PMOs in catalysis is a fascinating subject due to the uniform distribution of organic groups within the silica matrix, which is synthesized via simple template sol–gel processes in the presence of a surfactant. This approach allows for the incorporation of a wide range of organic moieties within the MOS channel walls of MOS as bridges between silicon centers through the use of a precursor such as (R′O)₃Si-R–Si(OR′)₃23–28. However, their name can be misleading, as they do not exhibit truly ‘periodic’ structures. These materials are referred to as mesoporous organic silica (MOS). Because the organic groups are part of the pore walls and better prevention of channel blockage, MOSs can achieve the highest organic content, offering unmatched accessibility compared to other mesoporous nanoparticles29–31. The inclusion of organic functionalities in MOSs enhances flexibility, hydrophobicity, mechanical and hydrothermal stability, superior strength, biocompatibility, adjustable pore size organization and reusability, making them an attractive candidate for widespread applications28,32–34.

The Hantzsch reaction is a renowned one-pot, four-component process for the synthesis of polyhydroquinolines (PHQs) and polyhydroacridines (PHAs), nitrogen-based heterocycles with diverse biological activities35,36. Recently, there has been a growing emphasis on developing new synthetic techniques for these compounds due to their remarkable and inspiring variety of pharmacological and therapeutic effects, including antimalarial, calcium channel blocking, neuroprotective, antioxidant, antimicrobial, cardiovascular, hypertension, antifungal, vasodilatory, anticancer, bronchodilatory, platelet aggregation inhibiting, antiatherosclerotic, geroprotective, hepatoprotective, antidiabetic, and blood glucose-lower35,37–39. However, most of the reported methods face numerous barriers, including the need for microwave irradiation, ultrasound, the use of non-recyclable catalyst, high catalyst loading, expensive metal precursors, poisonous and expensive organic solvents, unsatisfactory yields, harsh reaction conditions, and tedious procedures, therefore the exploration of the environmentally friendly methods has emerged as an affordable yet challenging issue35,40–42.

The development of heterogeneous catalysts is a key imperative within green chemistry that offers a multitude of benefits, leading to considerably more efficient and sustainable chemical conversions. Unlike homogeneous catalysts, heterogeneous catalysts are immobilized or solid in nature, which allows for simple separation from the reaction medium by either filtration or centrifugation, and allows easy separations of products that permit multiple recycles of the catalyst to reduce costs and waste.The ability of the catalyst to conduct the conversion under mild conditions typically lowers energy requirements and emissions of greenhouse gases that have direct implications for environmental sustainability. Reduced environmental footprint and improved process safety provide further benefits and promote their utility. These combined benefits elevate heterogeneous catalysts to a position of importance in the transformation towards more sustainable and environmentally benign chemical production43,44. Recent advancements in heterogeneous catalysts for the Hantzsch reaction include: Fe3O4@SiO2@PTS-THEIC-(CH2)3OB(OH)245, Fe3O4@nSiO2@mSiO2/DBU36,[βCD/ Im](OTs)246, Trimesic acid-functionalized chitosan47, Cd-Ag-MOF@ZnO48, UiO‑66‑Pyca‑Ce (III)49, Fe3O4@SiO2@(CH2)3@Gl50, Cu(II)-SB/GPTMS@SiO2@Fe3O441, BPHSBF51 Cu-CYA coordination polymer52, PMO-ICS-PrSO3H28, [Silica-R-NMe2-R'-NMe2][Cl]53, Fe3O4@SiO2PEG/NH239. By having these facts in mind and continuing our research in the field of mesoporous organosilica synthesis and investigating their applications in the synthesis of organic compounds3,13, in the present project, we have engineered a novel MOS − KCC-1 architecture that incorporates N2, N4, N6-tris(3-(triethoxysilyl)propyl)-1,3,5-triazine-2,4,6-triamine (Triazine-bridge organo silica (TS)) cross-linkers within its channels, resulting in a doughnut-like wrinkled fibrous nano silica structure. We then investigated its catalytic activity in the one-pot Hantzsch reaction under solvent-free conditions.

Materials and methods

All chemical materials were purchased from Merck and Fluka chemical companies and used without further purification. The products were characterized by comparison of their physical data with reported results and by FT-IR spectroscopy, recorded using a St-Jean-Baptiste Avenue Bomem 450 FT-IR spectrometer (PerkinElmer, Series 1998) in the range of 400–4000 cm-1 from KBr disks. Melting points of the products were measured by Thermo Scientific LTD IA9200 . The purity determination of the products and reaction monitoring were accomplished by TLC on silica gel PolyGram SILG/UV 254 plates. Ultrasonic treatments were performed using a water bath ultrasonic cleaner (Model SONICA 2200 ETH S3), operating at a nominal power of 305 W and an ultrasonic frequency of approximately 50 kHz. The device is powered by a standard 230/240 V supply at 50 Hz. The instrument is equipped with a Sweep System to ensure homogeneous energy distribution throughout the sonication process. Elemental analyses for Si, C, O, N and the particle size and morphology of the nanocomposite particles were examined by field emission microscope scanning electron microscopy, FE-SEM (MIRA3-LMU). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observations of the nanostructures of the novel NPs were obtained using a LEO-906E-80 kV instrument. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) and Barret–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) methods were used for calculating the specific surface area and pore-size distribution, respectively. Nitrogen adsorption was assessed using a Belsorp Mini II instrument. Solid samples were degassed at 120 °C and purged with N2 overnight, and weighed meticulously. TGA analysis was obtained with the SDT-Q600 instrument. A ramp rate of 10 °C/min in the range of 25–1000 °C was used. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the catalyst were obtained via a Philips X-ray diffractometer (model X’Pert Pro). The diffraction patterns were collected at 25 °C and with a 2θ (Bragg angle) range of 10–80°.

Materials and physical measurements

Preparation of KCC-1

In a typical synthesis, CTAB (2 g, 5 mmol) and urea (2.4 g, 40 mmol) were mixed in DI water (100 mL) at room temperature and stirred in a round-bottom flask for 30 min. TEOS (4.7 g, 23 mmol) in cyclohexane (100 mL) was then added dropwise to the reaction mixture and stirred for 1h at room temperature. To the mixture, the co-surfactant (1-pentanol, 55 mmol) was added dropwise and stirred for 30 min at room temperature. The mixture was then heated at 82 °C in an oil bath and further stirred for 12 h. After cooling, the solid KCC-1 was isolated by centrifugation, washed with distilled water and ethanol (3 times each), and dried at 80 °C for 12 h. KCC-1 was then calcined at 550 °C for 6 h to remove the remaining surfactants and co-surfactants54 (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the synthesis of KCC-1.

Preparation of Triazine-bridge organosilica

In a round-bottom flask, to the mixture of cyanuric chloride (0.922 g, 5 mmol) in dioxane (50 mL), a mixture of (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (3.541 g, 16 mmol ) solubilized in dioxane (50 mL) was added and stirred for 5 min. To the mixture, Na2CO3 (1.059 g, 10 mmol) was added, and then stirred under reflux conditions for 12 h. The reaction was allowed to cool, and the precipitate was separated and washed with ethanol and then dried for 24 h in a vacuum oven at 80 °C55 (Fig. 2a).Fig. 2(a) Schematic Illustration of the Synthesis of Triazine-bridge organo silica as an organo silane cross-linker; (b and c) Stepwise synthesis process of doughnut-like wrinkled fibrous nano silica structure (KCC- Triazine nanocomposite).

Preparation of doughnut-like wrinkled fibrous nano silica (KCC- Triazine)

To the mixture of cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) (1 g, 2.7 mmol) in 30 mL of deionized water, urea (0.6 g, 10 mmol) was added and the mixture stirred at room temperature for 30 min to form a homogeneous solution and labeled as "A". Then, 1.5 mL of 1-pentanol was added to 30 mL of cyclohexane and stirred until a homogeneous oily solution was formed. This oily solution was added to the A solution under vigorous stirring. This mixture was marked with "B".

The mixture of TEOS (2 g, 10 mmol) and Triazine-bridge organosilica (2g, 2.7 mmol) was sonicated for 30 min and added dropwise to the B mixture and vigorously stirred for 60 min at room temperature until a cloudy white emulsion achieved. The reaction mixture was then refluxed for 24 h at 75 °C. The modified KCC, KCC-Triazine architecture that incorporates TS cross-linkers within its channels, was purified by centrifugation, washed with a mixture of deionized water and acetone to eliminate any CTAB, and dried in a vacuum oven at 80 °C for 12 h. It was then dispersed in acidic ethanol and heated at 70 °C for 24 h to extract any remaining residual CTAB (Fig. 2b, 2c).

General procedure for the synthesis of polyhydroquinolines (PHQs) promoted by KCC- Triazine

In a test tube, a mixture of KCC-Triazine (0.01 g), dimedone (1 mmol), ethyl acetoacetate (1mmol), ammonium acetate (1.2 mmol), and aromatic aldehydes (1 mmol) was added. The mixture was stirred under solvent-free conditions in an oil bath at 80 °C for the time as recorded in Table 2. After completion of the reaction (monitored by TLC), hot ethanol (10 mL) was added to the reaction mixture and stirred until all organic compounds were dissolved. Afterwards, the catalyst was separated by filtration, washed several times with aqueous EtOH (50%), and dried for use in the next reaction. To the solution, distilled water (5 mL) was added dropwise, and the precipitate product was assembled by filtration and recrystallized in ethanol/water.

General procedure for the synthesis of polyhydroacridines (PHAs) promoted by KCC- Triazine

A mixture of TS-KCC-1 (0.01 g), dimedone (2 mmol), ammonium acetate (1.2 mmol), and aromatic aldehydes (1 mmol) was stirred under solvent-free conditions in an oil bath at 80 °C. After completion of the reaction (monitored by TLC), hot ethanol (10 mL) was added to the reaction mixture and stirred. After separation of the catalyst by filtration, distilled water (5 mL) was added dropwise to the solution, and the precipitate product was collected by filtration and recrystallized in ethanol/water.

The synthetic routes for the preparation of KCC-1 and doughnut-like wrinkled fibrous silica nanocomposite by triazine-bridges were sketched in Fig. 1 & 2. The organic–inorganic hybrid silica framework (KCC‑Triazine) based on KCC‑1 was prepared in three key steps. In the first step, the silanated triazine units were easily synthesized through nucleophilic substitution reaction between 3‑aminopropyl triethoxysilane and cyanuric chloride in the presence of Na2CO3 under reflux conditions. In the next steps, the triazine‑bridge organosilica was incorporated into the pore walls of dendritic fibrous nanostructure silica through a sol–gel process under open‑vessel reflux conditions. Tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS) and the triazine‑bridge organosilica served as silica sources, cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) as both template and structure‑directing agent, cyclohexane as co‑solvent, 1‑pentanol as co‑surfactant to stabilize micelles and microemulsion droplets, and urea as a controlled hydrolysis agent. The formation of well-structured organosilica in this method can probably be attributed to the fact that upon addition of 1-pentanol-cyclohexane solution to CTAB-urea-water solution, a bicontinuous microemulsion (BμE) consisting of water/cyclohexane/1-pentanol and CTAB is formed. This results in the formation of water- and oil-containing nanochannels separated by flexible surfactant monolayers with nearly zero curvature. These bicontinuous microemulsions act as a template for the layered phases. In the aqueous phase, TEOS undergoes urea-catalyzed hydrolysis to generate silicic acid and silicate molecules. These hydrolyzed precursors then co-condense with the TS linker, directing the assembly of a hybrid silica framework that evolves into the characteristic Doughnut-like wrinkled fibrous nanosilica architecture.

To qualitatively assess the incorporation of Triazine-bridge organo units into the pore walls of KCC-1, the FT-IR spectra of KCC-1, Triazine-bridge organosilicon, and KCC-Triazine were recorded (Fig. 3). In all FTIR spectra, the typical absorption bands were observed in the range of 467–472 cm–1, 698–864 cm–1, 912–959 cm–1, and 1043–1098 cm–1 which shows the bending vibration of Si–O–Si, Si–O stretching vibrations, Si–OH stretching vibrations, and Si–O–Si asymmetric stretching vibrations, respectively. The strong absorption bands at 1128–1209 cm-1 are attributed to the asymmetric bending and stretching vibrations of free silanol groups (Si − OH). The C − H symmetric stretching peak was presented at 2945 cm-1 in Fig. 3b and 2927 cm-1 in Fig. 3c. In the heterocyclic ring, the bands centered at 1542 cm-1 and 1649 cm-1 in Fig. 3b and 1506 cm-1 and 1652 cm-1 in Fig. 3c are related to the vibrational bending frequency of N–H and C = N bonds, respectively. Moreover, the broad absorption band centered at 3100–3500 cm − 1 is assigned to the stretching vibrations of SiO–H, the stretching vibrations of N–H in the triazine ring, and O–H vibrations of surface-adsorbed water molecules. Thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) was investigated to further confirm the successful incorporation of organic sites into channel inorganic sites, as shown in Fig. 8. This analysis was carried out in the temperature range of 25–1000 °C. According to Fig. 8, the TGA curve of KCC-Triazine shows an initial weight loss of approximately 6% below 200 °C, which can be attributed to the removal of physically adsorbed water and hydroxyl groups of the silica surface. A further and more significant weight loss occurs between 200 °C and 770 °C, which is ascribed to the decomposition and removal of the triazine-bridge organic moieties embedded within the silica network. Following the successful characterization of the KCC-Triazine nanocomposite, we investigated its potential as a tunable mesoporous nanocatalyst for the efficient, green, and rapid solvent-free synthesis of polyhydroquinoline (PHQ) and polyhydroacridine (PHA) derivatives via the Hantzsch reaction. To evaluate the catalytic activity of KCC-Triazine and determine the optimized conditions for the synthesis of PHAs, various parameters such as solvent type, temperature, reaction time, and catalyst loading of KCC-Triazine were investigated (Table 2). For this purpose, the four-component condensation of dimedone (2 mmol), benzaldehyde (1 mmol), and NH4OAc (1.2 mmol) was investigated as the model reaction. Initially, in the absence of the KCC-Triazine nanocatalyst under solvent-free conditions, only 15% of the desired PHA was obtained after 180 min (Table 2, entry 1). As shown in Table 2, the influence of various amounts of KCC-Triazine nanocatalyst (5 − 20 mg) under solvent-free conditions in the model reaction was evaluated. It was observed that the yield of the product enhanced with increasing the amount of KCC-Triazine from 5 to 15 mg (Table 2, entries 2–4). Among these, 10 mg of KCC‑Triazine nanocatalyst achieved the highest yield in the short reaction time. In the next step, the impact of different temperatures on the model reaction was investigated. The reaction was carried out at room temperature with 10 mg of nanocatalyst, and 25% yield was achieved after 180 min (Table 2, entry 8). With increasing temperature from 25 °C to 100 °C, a significant increase in the yield was observed (Table 2, entries 5–8). Among the applied solvents such as water, ethanol, acetonitrile, PEG 400, DMF, and n-hexane, the best result was obtained after 5 min under solvent-free conditions (Table 2, entries 9–14). To show the catalytic role of triazine units, a control experiment was carried out using KCC-1 (without TS modification) under optimized reaction conditions. The results demonstrate that KCC-Triazine is more efficient, achieving higher yields in shorter reaction time compared to KCC-1 (Table 2, entry 15). Discussion about plausible mechanism

The suggested mechanism for the synthesis of polyhydroquinoline derivatives in the presence of the KCC-Triazine catalyst is illustrated in Fig. 11. The pore walls including internally triazine bridges in nanocomposite can be act as a reaction vessel.These engineered pores as nanoreactors create an ideal environment that increase possibility of effective collision between the reactants. In the proposed mechanism, the triazine sites abstract the α-proton from dimedone, generating a carbanion intermediate (I). The N–H groups of the triazine units and hydroxyl groups of silanol participate in activating the carbonyl group of the aldehyde substrate through hydrogen-bonding interactions. This synergistic effect enhances the electrophilicity of the carbonyl group of the aldehyde and enhances its electrophilic character. The activated carbonyl functional group of the aldehyde reacts with the enolic form of dimedone and gives the corresponding Knovenagel intermediate (II). On the other hand, the basic organic moieties within the pore walls of the KCC-triazine facilitate the reaction between ammonium acetate and ethyl acetoacetate via hydrogen bonding, leading to the production of the enamine intermediate (III). Finally, the Michael addition reaction occurs between the nucleophilic enamine and the electrophilic intermediate (II), followed by cyclization and elimination of water, leading to the desired polyhydroquinoline derivatives.Fig. 11Plausible Reaction Mechanism.

Investigating the recyclability of the catalyst

The recyclability of the catalyst is vital for industrial applications and green chemistry. To assess this, we investigated the catalytic efficiency of the nanocomposite for polyhydroquinoline synthesis over multiple cycles. After the reaction reached completion, the catalyst was separated by simple filtration, washed with hot ethanol, and dried at 80 °C under vacuum before being reused in the subsequent run. As demonstrated in Fig. 12, the nanocomposite can be recycled at least five times without any significant loss of activity. Comparing the FT-IR spectrum of the reused catalyst for five runs (Fig. 13) with that of the virgin nanocomposite (Fig. 3c) reveals that the characteristic peaks remain consistent, indicating that the catalyst’s structure is preserved under the reaction conditions. Furthermore, after the five recycling cycle, the catalyst was recharacterized using FE-SEM analysis. Conclusion

The KAUST Catalysis Center (KCC-1), with its amorphous concentric fibers and highly accessible internal surface area, exhibits exceptionally high catalytic activity. Meanwhile, the mesoporous channels and large surface area of MOSs make them effective nanoreactors, facilitating the controlled release of reactants into the channels and enhancing reaction rates. Due to the unique features of MOSs and KCC-1, we have developed a novel hybrid architecture of doughnut-like wrinkled fibrous nanostructured silica materials by incorporating triazine-bridge as organic–inorganic cross-linker within the pores. Nanocomposite pore walls with internal triazine-bridge act as nanoreactors. This structure enhances the possibility of effective collisions between starting materials by creating a suitable reactive environment. The Doughnut-like wrinkled fibrous shaped nanocatalyst KCC-Triazine, with its high porosity, accessible porous channels, high organic content, and nanosilica-based basic sites (nitrogen functionalities), showed excellent stability and high yields in the one-pot Hantzsch reaction under solvent-free conditions. Moreover, performing multicomponent reactions (MCRs) under solvent-free conditions is highly advantageous due to their combined benefits. This method offered several significant advantages, including greener strategy, low catalyst loading, the avoidance of toxic transition metals or reactive reagents for catalyst preparation, short reaction times, a solvent-free medium, high to excellent yields, easy product separation and purification, and catalyst reusability.
