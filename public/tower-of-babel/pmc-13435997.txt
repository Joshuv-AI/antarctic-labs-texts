Integrated service delivery to improve adolescent health and human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination in Lao PDR: a quasi-experimental mixed-methods study – study protocol
Authors: Thet Lynn, Vanphanom Sychareun, Kongmany Chaleunvong, Viengnakhone Vongxay, Souksamone Thongmixay, Chandavieng Phimmavong, Chansathith Taikeophithoun, Josien Van Der Kooij
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Lao People’s Democratic Republic (PDR) faces the significant public health challenges of high cervical cancer incidence and suboptimal human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine coverage alongside broader adolescent health concerns such as high adolescent pregnancy rates and inadequate sexual and reproductive health (SRH) literacy. This study protocol outlines an approach to address these interconnected issues comprehensively by evaluating an integrated service delivery model.Methods and analysisThis study employed a quasi-experimental design with mixed methods to assess the impact of an integrated service package comprising HPV vaccination and improved adolescent health services in two districts of Louangnamtha province in Lao PDR. The study population included girls aged 10–13 years for HPV vaccination and boys and girls aged 10–13 years for other adolescent health services. The intervention district received this integrated service package delivered through school-based, facility-based and outreach approaches. The comparison district continued with its standard HPV vaccination and routine adolescent health service delivery, which does not include the additional refresher training, comprehensive sexuality education or dedicated SRH outreach services provided in the intervention district.The intervention period was 7 months, from November 2024 to May 2025. Quantitative data on HPV vaccine uptake, SRH knowledge and attitudes and utilisation of adolescent health services were collected at baseline, midline (at 3 months) and endline (at 7 months). Qualitative data on barriers and facilitators to implementation, provider workload and capacity, and stakeholder perceptions were collected through in-depth interviews and focus group discussions with adolescents, caregivers, healthcare providers, educators, community leaders and policymakers at endline.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval for the study has been received from the University of Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee in Laos (ID: 833/REC, dated 4 September 2024). Informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians. Findings will be disseminated through academic publications, policy briefs, workshops and community engagement activities to inform policies and practices in Lao PDR and contribute to the global knowledge base on integrated service delivery models.Trial registration numberNCT06650956.Keywords: HPV Vaccine, Implementation Science, Health policy

Introduction

The Lao People’s Democratic Republic (PDR) faces significant health challenges, including a high incidence of cervical cancer, which remains the second leading cause of death due to neoplasms among females aged 15–49.1 2 Despite the availability of prophylactic human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccines, national HPV vaccination coverage remains suboptimal, with the 2023 national average at 17.3% for HPV1 coverage.3 This is compounded by broader adolescent health concerns, such as the highest adolescent pregnancy rate in Southeast Asia and inadequate sexual and reproductive health (SRH) literacy.4 5 The WHO advocates for increased HPV vaccination coverage and quality to accelerate the elimination of cervical cancer, particularly in low- and middle-income countries such as Laos where screening programmes are limited.6

The national HPV1 coverage of 17.3% in Lao PDR is comparable to or slightly higher than the rates seen in some neighbouring countries. For instance, in 2023, only 12% of women and girls aged 15–29 in Vietnam had received the HPV vaccine.7 This regional data illustrates a shared public health challenge where national programmes are struggling to achieve high coverage. On an international scale, Lao PDR’s coverage rate is aligned with the average for Gavi-supported countries. In 2023, the HPV vaccine coverage in the 57 lower-income countries supported by Gavi was an average of 16%, a figure to which Lao PDR’s 17.3% coverage is quite similar.8 Despite these relative comparisons, all of these rates fall dramatically short of the ambitious global targets set for cervical cancer elimination. The WHO’s Global Strategy to Accelerate the Elimination of Cervical Cancer sets a key target of achieving 90% HPV vaccination coverage for girls by age 15 by 2030.6 The significant gap between Lao PDR’s current 17.3% coverage and this global target highlights the urgent need for innovative, effective and scalable interventions to improve vaccine uptake and protect the health of adolescent girls across the country.

Rationale for integrated service package

The integration of HPV vaccination with adolescent health services is a strategic and necessary approach to address these complex and interconnected health needs of adolescents in Lao PDR, aligning with national health priorities and international recommendations.9 This multicomponent service package was designed to significantly increase HPV vaccine uptake by offering the vaccine alongside other essential health services, such as SRH and rights (SRHR) education and counselling, thereby reaching a larger number of adolescents, especially in rural areas where healthcare access may be limited. This integrated approach is also expected to improve broader adolescent health outcomes by providing comprehensive SRH services, leading to reductions in unintended pregnancies and sexually transmitted infections (STIs). Furthermore, it aims to address social and cultural factors that contribute to poor adolescent health outcomes by fostering a safe and supportive environment that builds trust and rapport. The design of this integrated intervention was notably influenced by the social ecological model, which recognises the interconnectedness of individual, interpersonal, organisational, community and policy-level factors in shaping health behaviours and outcomes, aiming to maximise reach and efficiency through existing education and healthcare infrastructure. This integrated approach is also a cost-effective way to deliver essential health services, leveraging existing infrastructure and potentially leading to long-term cost savings from cervical cancer prevention.

Contribution to the evidence base

While integrated health service delivery models have shown success in other contexts, this study is specifically needed to assess the effectiveness of such a multicomponent service package in the unique context of Lao PDR. This study protocol aims to provide critical evidence on the overall impact of this integrated intervention, rather than isolating the effect of ‘integration’ alone, acknowledging the multifaceted nature of the intervention package. It will evaluate the effects of the integrated service package on HPV vaccine uptake, SRH knowledge and attitudes (KA), utilisation of adolescent health services, healthcare providers’ perceived workload and capacity, and stakeholder perceptions. The findings will inform policies and practices related to HPV vaccination and adolescent health service delivery in Lao PDR, contributing to the global body of knowledge on integrated service delivery models and potentially influencing similar interventions in other countries with low HPV vaccination coverage and limited access to adolescent health services.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a quasi-experimental design with a convergent parallel mixed-methods approach to assess the impact of an integrated service delivery package comprising HPV vaccination and enhanced adolescent health services in Lao PDR. A quasi-experimental design was appropriate as random assignment of participants to intervention and comparison groups is not feasible in this implementation setting. The intervention model was a parallel assignment, where one group received the integrated service package and the other group receives standard services. To prevent bias, outcome assessors were masked, meaning they did not know which group received the intervention. The mixed-methods approach allowed for an in-depth understanding of the quantitative impact of the intervention on HPV vaccine uptake and other health outcomes, as well as the qualitative aspects of feasibility, acceptability and barriers/facilitators to implementation.

Study settings

The study was conducted in two districts of Louangnamtha province in Lao PDR: one intervention district and one comparison district. The selection of the intervention district was influenced by its lower HPV vaccine coverage within the province, the existing availability of adolescent health services and the willingness of local authorities and healthcare providers to participate. The comparison district was selected based on similar characteristics to ensure comparability. The comparison district continued with its standard HPV vaccination (targeting 10-year-old girls) and routine adolescent health service delivery, which may not include the same level of integration or the full range of SRHR services offered in the intervention district.

As shown in figure 1, HPV1 vaccination coverage among 10–17 years old girls in Laos in 2023 varies by province, with Bolikhamxai leading at 27.6% and Bokeo trailing at 12%. The national average stands at 17.3%. While Louangnamtha’s 12.3% coverage exceeds the lowest rates, it remains below the national average, suggesting a need for targeted efforts to improve vaccine uptake in this region and reach the goal of preventing cervical cancer.3

Cumulatively from 2020 to 2023, nearly 60% of 10–17 years old girls cohorts have completed two doses of HPV vaccine. Starting from 2024, Laos switched to a single-dose HPV vaccination strategy targeting 10–13 years old adolescent girls. It is important to note that the nearly 60% cumulative coverage refers to the proportion of 10–17 years old girls who have completed two doses of the HPV vaccine over the entire period from 2020 to 2023, whereas these provincial figures represent the single HPV1 dose coverage specifically for the year 2023. Both sets of coverage data are derived from the National Immunisation Programme’s (NIP) administrative records.3 10

Louangnamtha province has achieved good progress in reproductive health, with high rates of modern contraceptive use and demand for family planning satisfied. This suggests a positive environment for integrating HPV vaccination with existing reproductive health services. Child nutrition indicators have also improved, particularly in reducing underweight prevalence, indicating a potential opportunity to leverage child health services for HPV vaccine delivery. However, immunisation coverage has slightly declined, and disparities exist in hepatitis B birth dose coverage compared with BCG, highlighting the need for targeted interventions to improve vaccine uptake, especially among specific populations and geographical areas.11

Study population

Service users

HPV vaccination (relevant to SO-1): The primary target population for the HPV vaccine was 10-year-old girls residing in the selected districts. The age range of 10–13 years is set to allow operational flexibility in a context where vital registries are weak and missed opportunities for vaccination are prevalent as indicated in anecdotal reports. This population group was studied to determine the extent to which the integrated service package increased HPV vaccine uptake among those in the intervention district compared with the comparison district over 7 months. The study focused on girls for HPV vaccination as it aligns with the national HPV vaccination programme targeting adolescent girls to prevent cervical cancer. The central justification for this targeted approach is the national HPV immunisation programme’s primary objective: to reduce the incidence and mortality of cervical cancer, noting its high burden and the WHO’s goal to accelerate the elimination of cervical cancer.6 Given that cervical cancer is a disease that affects women, the most direct and efficient pathway to achieving this goal is to vaccinate the at-risk female population. The decision to limit vaccination to girls was a pragmatic trade-off. While the benefits of gender-neutral vaccination—such as preventing HPV-related cancers in men and contributing to herd immunity—are well-documented, a resource-constrained programme must prioritise its most urgent and deadly threat.12 By concentrating the high-cost vaccination resource on the population most directly and severely affected by the target disease, the programme maximises its chances of achieving its primary, policy-level goal in the most efficient manner possible.

Sample size for quantitative approach

The study involved young adolescent girls and boys aged 10–13 years old, both in-school and out of school. The sample size was determined using the formula for proportional studies in a quasi-experimental design, with a design effect of 2 to account for sampling methods other than simple random sampling. The sample size for boys was equal to that of girls.

The formula used is as follows: n=NZ2P(1−P)e2(N−1)+Z2P(1−P)×D

Where: n=number of required samples,

N=known number of study population,

Z=level of confidence at 95% (with a value of 1.96 for alpha 0.05),

e=precision of event measurement (or margin of error),

P=prevalence or proportion of event,

D=design effect.

The prevalence of HPV1 vaccination coverage in Louangnamtha province (P=12.3%) and the total number of girls aged 10–13 years old (N=3364) was used for the calculation.

The estimated sample size for girls is 350, including a 10% buffer. The sample size for boys was equal to that of girls (350). The total sample size for both intervention and comparison sites was 700 girls and boys aged 10–13 years old. This sample size was calculated to be sufficient to detect statistically significant differences in HPV vaccine uptake and SRHR KA between the intervention and comparison districts. Furthermore, this number of participants was deemed adequate to identify changes that are clinically meaningful for improving adolescent health outcomes and to provide robust evidence that can inform policy decisions regarding integrated health service delivery in Lao PDR, aligning with the pragmatic aims of this study to offer actionable findings for health systems planners and funders.

Sampling for quantitative approach

The study involved two districts (Sing and Viengphoukha) in Louangnamtha province, preassigned as intervention and comparison districts. The sampling strategy targeted year 5 primary school and year 1 secondary school students, with 40% of the sample (140 students: 70 girls, 70 boys) from year 5 and 50% (175 students: 88 girls, 87 boys) from year 1. The remaining 10% (35 students: 18 girls, 17 boys) was out-of-school adolescents, potentially missed by school vaccination programmes.

The sampling strategy involved the following steps:

School selection: In the intervention district (Sing), there are a total of 76 primary and 10 secondary schools. From this list, four primary schools and four secondary schools were randomly selected to participate in the study using computer-generated random numbers via Microsoft Excel software. Similarly, in the comparison district (Viengphoukha), there are a total of 46 primary and 10 secondary schools. From this list, four primary and four secondary schools were again randomly selected using computer-generated random numbers via Microsoft Excel software.

School adolescents recruitment: Within each selected school, two lists were created: one for girls and one for boys aged 10–13 years. Each student on these lists was assigned an ID number. A sex-stratified systematic sampling approach, proportional to the size of each list, was employed to select participants. The sampling interval was calculated as the total number of students on the list divided by the desired sample size. The first participant was randomly selected, and subsequent participants were chosen based on the sampling interval until the desired sample size is reached. Informed consent was obtained from selected students and their parents/guardians before conducting structured interviews.

Intervention

The intervention was an integrated service package comprised of HPV vaccination and adolescent health services in the intervention district, delivered through multiple touchpoints from November 2024 to May 2025. The design of this multilevel integrated approach is informed by evidence from successful interventions in other settings, such as Togo’s experience integrating health education with HPV vaccination.9 The integrated service package was delivered through three primary touchpoints—school-based, health facility based and outreach, drawing on evidence from various studies that have shown the effectiveness of multicomponent interventions.13

Qualitative data analysis

Mixed methods integration (joint analysis)

The quantitative and qualitative data will be integrated at the interpretation stage using a convergent parallel design and a triangulation protocol. This approach will use the qualitative findings to explain and provide context for the quantitative results. For example, if the DID analysis (quantitative) shows a statistically significant increase in vaccine uptake in the intervention district, the thematic analysis of FGDs with adolescents and providers (qualitative) will be used to explore the perceived drivers of this change. We will specifically analyse themes related to the convenience of the integrated platform, the impact of the CSE content or the influence of community outreach activities to provide a richer, contextualised explanation of the quantitative findings and explore the ‘how’ and ‘why’ behind the observed effects.

Purpose of the plan

The monitoring plan is designed to ensure the effective collection, management and analysis of data throughout the project. In the journey of integrating HPV vaccination with adolescent health services in Lao PDR, the Monitoring Plan will help track progress, identify challenges and inform decision-making to improve health outcomes for adolescents.

Timely production of quality data

Ensuring timely data production is a multifaceted challenge that extends beyond merely implementing electronic data recording forms and dashboards. One significant lesson from Health Poverty Action’s (HPA) operational research projects in the past is the lack of clear direction on who should use which forms and when. This often leads to confusion among field staff, resulting in inconsistent data collection practices. Additionally, the design of the forms themselves can be problematic. Poorly designed forms that are not user-friendly can hinder efficient data entry, causing delays and errors.

Another critical challenge is the lack of user confidence in using these electronic forms. Many field staff may not be familiar with digital data collection tools, leading to hesitancy and mistakes. This issue is compounded by a general lack of motivation among users, who may not see the immediate benefits of timely and accurate data entry. Without proper incentives and engagement, the enthusiasm for maintaining high data quality can wane.

Moreover, the timeliness and completion of data are sometimes not well-tracked. Optimal levels of motivation and technical aptitude within the central Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) team will be pivotal in mitigating such risk. Without a robust system to oversee the entire data management process, it becomes difficult to ensure that data is submitted on time and is complete. The central M&E team will be mobilised to provide the necessary technical skills to effectively use the interactive dashboard and other data management tools, further addressing the issue.

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients and the public were involved in this study protocol design primarily through community consultations and engagement activities during the initial conceptualisation phase, informing the relevance of the research questions and preferred service delivery methods for adolescents. Their priorities regarding accessible and comprehensive SRH services, particularly in addressing high adolescent pregnancy rates and SRH literacy, were considered when developing the enhanced integrated service package. Adolescents and caregivers will be key informants in exploring their perceptions of the intervention (SO-4). Plans to disseminate the results to study participants and relevant wider communities include sharing findings through community meetings, local workshops and culturally appropriate information, education and communication (IEC) materials such as fact sheets and infographics.

Ethical considerations and dissemination

Ethics review

Ethical approval for the study has been received from University of Health Sciences (UHS) Research Ethics Committee (REC) in Laos with identification number of 833/REC dated 4 September 2024. Informed consent will be obtained from all participants or their legal guardians before any data collection. Confidentiality and privacy will be maintained throughout the study by anonymising data and ensuring secure storage of information. The study will adhere to international ethical guidelines for research involving human subjects. Any potential risks or harms to participants will be minimised, and appropriate measures will be taken to ensure their well-being.

Informed consent

Informed consent for adolescent girls and boys participating in the integrated service implementation research in Lao PDR should consider their age, maturity and understanding of the research. It is important to ensure that the adolescents are adequately informed and empowered to make decisions about their participation. The information provided to adolescent girls should be tailored to their age and level of understanding. Parental or guardian consent is required for participation in research. Adolescents aged 10–13 years are required to provide their own assent process, indicating their willingness to participate in the research. (online supplemental materials S02 and S03: Informed consent forms) This process involves explaining the research to the adolescent in a way that they can understand and seeking their agreement to participate. In addition, the research team will ask permission from schoolteachers as well. It is important to clarify the legal and ethical requirements for consent in Lao PDR and to involve parents or guardians as appropriate.

Risks and benefits

There are no direct physical risks in participation in this study. However, participants may face social risks such as stigma or discrimination related to HPV vaccination, particularly in communities where there is a lack of awareness or misconceptions about the vaccine. The HPV vaccine has a well-established safety profile, with the most common side effects being local adverse events, primarily pain, redness and swelling at the injection site. Systemic reactions like headache and dizziness were also observed but were generally mild and self-limiting. Serious adverse events were rare, and no association was found between HPV vaccination and new-onset chronic diseases or infertility.14

There are no direct monetary benefits in participating in this study, except small non-monetary incentives such as refreshments, hygiene items and monetary compensation of no more than US$5 per participant for their time in attending the surveys and interviews. Other potential benefits for the community and health system include increased access to HPV vaccination, cost-effectiveness and health system strengthening. An indirect benefit for participants is an increase in KA regarding SRHR among adolescent boys and girls. This research provides valuable insights for policymakers, healthcare practitioners and researchers to optimise the integration of HPV vaccination services in Lao PDR and similar settings.

Confidentiality

Confidentiality is a critical aspect of participating in HPV vaccine integrated service implementation research in Lao PDR. Several measures will be in place to ensure confidentiality for participants. Data collected from participants will be anonymised to protect their identities, involving assigning unique identifiers to participants and using these identifiers in place of personal information. Any personal health information collected during the research will be stored securely, with access restricted to authorised personnel only.
