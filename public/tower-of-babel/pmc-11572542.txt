Prevalence of dual use of combustible tobacco and E-cigarettes among pregnant smokers: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ganesh Bushi, Mahalaqua Nazli Khatib, Ashok Kumar Balaraman, Suhas Ballal, Pooja Bansal, Balvir S. Tomar, Ayash Ashraf, M. Ravi Kumar, Aashna Sinha, Pramod Rawat, Abhay M. Gaidhane, Sanjit Sah, Rukshar Syed, Gajendra Sharma, Rachana Mehta, Mona Thangamma A. G., Nagavalli Chilakam, Sakshi Pandey, Manvinder Brar, Muhammed Shabil
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAs e-cigarettes gain popularity as potential tobacco cessation aids, concerns arise about their dual use with traditional cigarettes, especially among pregnant women, potentially subjecting both women and fetuses to heightened risks. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to determine the overall prevalence of dual use of tobacco smoking and e-cigarette use in pregnant women.MethodsA literature search was conducted across databases including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane on October 20, 2023. The included studies reported the number of pregnant women and the count of those who were dual users. Quality assessment was undertaken using the JBI tool. The pooled prevalence of dual use was determined via a random-effects model. All statistical analyses were executed using R software, version 4.3. PROSPERO: CRD42023486020.ResultsEighteen studies were analyzed, encompassing 5,983,363 pregnant women. The meta-analysis indicated an overall prevalence of 4.6% (95% CI: 2.0-10.3) for dual users with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 100%). Subgroup analysis based on the country showed a prevalence of 4.9% (95% CI: 2.0 to 11.6) for USA and 8.1% (95% CI: 0.00 to 1.00) for UK. Meta-regression revealed reduction of prevalence of dual use from 2019 to 2023. A potential publication bias was indicated by the LFK index and the Doi plot.ConclusionThe dual consumption of e-cigarettes and traditional tobacco in pregnant women is a significant health concern, with a notable prevalence. Given the established risks of tobacco smoking during pregnancy and the uncertainties surrounding e-cigarettes, more comprehensive research and public health interventions are urgently needed to address this issue.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20746-9.

Introduction

Electronic nicotine delivery systems, commonly known as e-cigarettes, have seen a surge in their use since first appearing in the US in 2007 [1, 2]. While substantial proof regarding the efficacy of e-cigarettes as a reliable method to quit smoking remains elusive [3–5] they are prominently advertised to smokers as potential cessation aids. Research indicates that smokers, especially pregnant women [6–8] might be receptive to such marketing strategies. Some data even points to a growth in e-cigarette usage among expecting mothers, possibly due to such promotional activities [9]. Reports suggest that around 7% of expectant mothers turn to e-cigarettes during their pregnancy, and a significant 45% of these users believe e-cigarettes present a safer alternative than traditional cigarettes and could aid in either quitting or reducing their smoking habits during pregnancy [10, 11].

E-cigarette use has recently been associated with heightened risks of lung damage in humans, as evidenced by multiple studies [12–14]. Beyond the immediate implications for lung health, there’s also concerning evidence from animal research. These studies reveal that offspring born to mothers exposed to e-cigarettes might face a range of health issues. Specifically, such offspring have been shown to exhibit neurodevelopmental challenges [15], compromised lung growth [16], reductions in crown-rump length and overall foetal weight [17], and elevated levels of inflammation and oxidative stress [16, 18].

Tobacco, infused with an array of over 7000 chemicals, including the notably addictive substance, nicotine, is predominantly consumed by smoking, but non-smoking variants are also in use. Its consumption during pregnancy is alarming due to the severe risks it presents to the health of the infant. The rates of expectant mothers engaging in this habit vary significantly across countries. For instance, a global estimate indicates that 53% of women who are daily smokers persist in the habit during their pregnancy [19]. Additionally, smokeless tobacco products, which are either inhaled nasally or chewed, have a notable presence, particularly among women in regions like South-East Asia [20–22]. And while many women attempt to abandon tobacco once they confirm their pregnancy, a substantial number tend to revert post-delivery. The hazards linked to prenatal tobacco exposure range from complications like dysfunctional placental activities, intrauterine growth issues [23, 24] to a plethora of neonatal health challenges [25–28]. The co-use of traditional cigarettes and e-cigarettes, or dual use, among pregnant women presents a complex challenge. The motivations for dual use among pregnant women often stem from the desire to quit traditional cigarettes, driven by concerns over fetal health and influenced by the perception of e-cigarettes as a safer alternative [29, 30]. This view is reinforced by marketing and social environments that promote e-cigarettes as cessation aids, despite limited evidence of their efficacy and safety [31]. Factors such as the accessibility of e-cigarettes, appealing flavors, and the psychological stress of pregnancy encourage dual use as a coping strategy, amidst ambiguous information about their risks [32]. Health consequences of dual use during pregnancy are concerning and not fully understood. While traditional cigarette risks are well-documented, including developmental issues in infants, the effects of e-cigarettes are less clear. However, dual use might increase overall exposure to harmful substances, potentially exacerbating health risks for both mother and fetus, indicating a pressing need for comprehensive research to guide public health recommendations [32, 33]. Understanding the behavior motivation behind dual use, the factors that influence this choice, and its health implications is crucial. the motivation to quit smoking often intensifies during pregnancy due to concerns for fetal health, yet the conflicting information regarding e-cigarettes’ efficacy as cessation aids leaves many expecting mothers in a quandary.

Numerous studies have investigated the prevalence of smoking and e-cigarette use among pregnant smokers [32, 34–37]. This systematic review and meta-analysis seek to determine the prevalence of dual use of tobacco smoking and e-cigarette use in this population.

Methods

This review was conducted following the PRISMA guidelines (Table S1), which set the standard for systematic reviews and meta-analyses [38]. The protocol has been registered in PROSPERO under the registration number: CRD42023486020. We used Nested Knowledge (Nested-Knowledge, MN, USA) web software for screening and data extraction.

Inclusion criteria

Studies eligible for inclusion are either observational or interventional that report both the total number of pregnant women who were smokers and the number of those who are dual users of traditional tobacco smoking and e-cigarettes. Dual use of tobacco and e-cigarettes refers to the concurrent use of traditional tobacco products, such as cigarettes, cigars, or pipes, and e-cigarettes by pregnant women. This practice means that a person is not exclusively using one product type but is alternating between or simultaneously using both conventional tobacco products and e-cigarettes. Studies focusing solely on e-cigarette use or tobacco use without considering dual use were not included. Studies that did not report the number of dual users or prevalence were excluded. Case reports, commentaries, abstracts, reviews, and animal studies were excluded from this review. Only articles available in English were considered (Table S2).

Literature search and screening

An electronic literature search was performed across databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Embase and Cochrane on 20 October 2023. Keywords and MeSH terms related to e-cigarettes, pregnant women, and dual use were utilized in the search. There were no limitations placed on the year of publication or the type of articles during the search. The detailed search strategy is given in Table S3.

Two independent reviewers carried out the screening of articles. Initial screening was based on reading the title and abstract, followed by a more detailed full-text screening phase. Nested Knowledge software was employed for the screening and de-duplication processes. Any disagreements between the reviewers concerning the eligibility of articles were settled by a third reviewer.

Data extraction

Data extraction was undertaken by two reviewers independently. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. Information such as the author’s name, publication year, study design, total number of pregnant women, and the number of dual users was extracted. The data was extracted using the “tagging” function of Nested Knowledge software, and then transferred to Excel.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed to ensure the reliability and validity of our findings using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal tools [39], which allowed us to examine crucial domains such as the appropriateness of the sample frame and sampling method, the adequacy of the sample size, the detail provided about the study participants and setting, the thoroughness of the data analysis, the validity and reliability of condition identification and measurement, the suitability of the statistical analysis, and the adequacy of the response rate. Each study was independently appraised by two reviewers, with any disagreements resolved through discussion or a third reviewer’s input. This rigorous process helped us categorize studies into three quality levels: high, moderate, and low. High-quality studies were those that adequately addressed all or most of the key domains with a low risk of bias.

Statistical analysis

A meta-analysis was conducted to assess the overall prevalence of dual use among pregnant women. The analysis utilized the number of dual users and the total number of pregnant women. Results from various studies were pooled using a random-effects model [40–43]. The level of heterogeneity among studies was quantified using the I2 and tau-squared metrics [44, 45]. The 95% prediction interval was employed to highlight this inconsistency [46, 47]. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The tau-squared value was computed using the maximum likelihood method. A Doi plot was used to detect potential publication biases [48]. Subgroup analysis was performed on the basis of country. Meta-regression was performed to find out the association of sample size and year of publication with the overall result. The leave-one-out analysis was used to assess the impact of individual studies on the overall result. All tests were two-tailed. All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.3 [49].

Results

Literature search

A total of 720 records were appeared from the database search, of which 101 were duplicates. 619 records were screened by title and abstract, leading to a full-text screening of 204 records. Of these, 187 articles were excluded for various reasons. 18 studies were found eligible. Additionally, from citation searching, 3 studies were identified, of which only 1 was deemed eligible. Ultimately, 18 studies were included in this systematic review. The PRISMA chart showing screening and selection process is depicted in Fig. 1.

Characteristics of included studies

A total of 18 studies were included in the review with 5,983,363 pregnant women. Table 1 provides an overview of the included studies. In examining the characteristics of various studies on dual users, several distinct approaches emerge across different countries. For instance, in the USA, a significant cross-sectional study by Ammar et al. (2023) encompassed a vast 190,707 participants, pinpointing 1,404 as dual users. In a contrasting approach, Ashford et al. (2021) conducted a cohort study in the same country, focusing on a tighter sample of 278 individuals, 91 of whom were identified as dual users. Across the Atlantic in the UK, Bowker et al. undertook two studies. Their 2020 research used a cross-sectional design, assessing 3,360 participants and finding 118 dual users. A subsequent 2021 cohort study from the same team reviewed a sample of 383, with 48 participants identified as dual users. This array of studies showcases the varied methodologies and sample sizes researchers have employed and shown patterns of dual usage across different locations. Studies assessed the smoking status at different time periods of pregnancy, such as between 8 and 24 weeks and during the last trimester of pregnancy. The JBI quality assessment tool revealed that three studies [35, 37, 50] were of high quality, indicating they adequately addressed most or all of the key domains with a low risk of bias. Eleven studies [32, 34, 36, 49, 51–57] were rated as moderate quality, having some methodological concerns or inadequacies in certain domains, which presented a moderate risk of bias. Four studies [58–61] were determined to be of low quality due to methodological flaws across several domains, indicating a high risk of bias. The common issues impacting study quality included inadequate sample size, unclear methods for condition identification, and insufficient detail in describing study subjects and settings. These assessments underscore the importance of rigorous methodological standards in research to ensure the reliability and validity of findings (Table S4).

Characteristics of studies

Prevalence of dual users

Eighteen studies reported the dual use of e-cigarettes and tobacco smoking in pregnant women. The meta-analysis, conducted on a total of 5,983,363 pregnant women, showed a pooled prevalence of 4.6% (95% CI: 2.0-10.3) of dual users with a prediction interval of 0.001 to 0.643 and a heterogeneity of 100%. Subgroup analysis

We performed subgroup analysis based on the country of study. 13 studies were conducted in the USA. A pooled prevalence of 4.9% (95% CI: 2.0-11.6) was found for dual users with a heterogeneity of I2 = 100%. Two studies from the UK revealed a prevalence of 8.1% (95% CI: 0.00–1.0) with 99% heterogeneity. Single studies were available from Ireland, the Netherlands, and Germany with prevalences of 31% (95% CI: 27–35), 0.6% (95% CI: 0.3–1.1), and 0% (95% CI: 0.00-0.7), respectively. Figure 3 shows the forest plot of subgroup analysis based on country.

Meta-regression and sensitivity analysis

We performed a meta-regression based on sample size and year of publication to determine its effect on the overall result. Sample size was not found to be associated with the result (p = 0.67) (Figure S1). However, the year of publication was found to be negatively associated with prevalence of dual use (p = 0.006). The trend showed the prevalence of dual use among pregnant women decreased over the years from 2019 to 2023 (Fig. 4). The leave-one-out analysis was conducted to determine the effect of individual studies on the effect size and heterogeneity. No significant difference was observed in the leave-one-out analysis (Figure S2).

Discussion

The use of tobacco and e-cigarettes among pregnant women presents a pressing public health concern. There is substantial evidence indicating the risks associated with tobacco smoking during pregnancy, ranging from premature births to fetal complications. The emergence of e-cigarettes as a potential alternative has evoked mixed responses. While some view them as a potential tool for harm reduction, others are wary due to the lack of data on long-term effects.

Our systematic review and meta-analysis is the first of its kind to investigate the prevalence of dual use among pregnant women, which is found to be 4.6%. This result, however, was accompanied by high heterogeneity across studies, a factor that requires careful exploration to improve interpretability and application of findings. Despite employing meta-regression and leave-one-out analysis to pinpoint sources of variability, our attempts to identify definitive causes of heterogeneity were inconclusive. Several factors appear to contribute to the observed variability, including country-specific differences, methods of data collection, the timing and definition of smoking behaviors, sample overlap potential, and other methodological discrepancies. Country-related differences emerged as a notable factor, as the majority of studies included in our review originated from the USA, with fewer studies from the UK and other regions. Although the prevalence of dual use appeared similar in both the USA and UK, differences in healthcare guidance, public health messaging, and socioeconomic variables may influence dual use behavior and require further analysis in future research with broader international representation. For instance, country-specific public health campaigns and cultural attitudes towards smoking and e-cigarette use likely impact pregnant women’s choices, contributing to the variability in prevalence across regions.

Another factor contributing to heterogeneity was the variation in methods of data collection. Many studies relied on self-reported smoking behaviors, which may introduce recall or social desirability bias, particularly in pregnant women who may be less likely to disclose dual use due to perceived stigma. Additionally, a number of studies utilized data from the Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS), which raises the possibility of sample overlap and may artificially inflate or deflate prevalence rates when combined in meta-analysis. This reliance on secondary data also limits consistency, as data collection methods and definitions may vary across studies, influencing the comparability of results. Timing and definitions of smoking status presented additional sources of heterogeneity. In the studies we reviewed, smoking behaviors were often defined differently, with some studies reporting smoking status only at specific points in pregnancy (e.g., the last trimester or between 8 and 24 weeks) and others capturing continuous smoking behavior throughout pregnancy. These distinctions are critical, as smoking patterns may fluctuate during different stages of pregnancy, and variations in definitions can impact both prevalence and risk factor assessment. For instance, dual use measured only in the first trimester may miss those who quit smoking or e-cigarette use later in pregnancy, leading to an underestimation of dual use prevalence. Moreover, other methodological inconsistencies across studies contributed to heterogeneity. The sample size varied greatly between studies, from as few as 76 participants to over five million, with some studies designed as cross-sectional surveys, while others used cohort designs.

Smoking during pregnancy is linked to adverse effects on both the mother and the newborn. An previous meta-analysis analysis revealed that consistent tobacco consumption during gestation correlated with increased occurrences of smaller newborn size, shorter stature, and reduced head circumference at birth [62]. This also demonstrated a proportional relationship between the amount of tobacco smoked and the degree of growth impediments. This data hints at the possible long-term implications of in-utero tobacco exposure, potentially affecting early childhood growth and cognitive development. Another systematic review highlighted that when considering only sole e-cigarette use, disregarding concurrent usage with traditional cigarettes, the prevalence dropped to between 0.5% and 1.6% [63]. This underscores the notion that, during pregnancy, e-cigarettes are often used in conjunction with, rather than as a complete replacement for, traditional cigarettes. The review also indicated that e-cigarette users during pregnancy were predominantly younger non-Hispanic White women, often with a lesser educational background and income. Conversely, women who smoked traditional cigarettes or combined them with e-cigarettes generally had a lower socioeconomic status compared to those who solely vaped. While the harmful effects of tobacco smoking on maternal and fetal health are well-documented, nicotine exposure, common to both tobacco and e-cigarettes, can be detrimental [64]. Although e-cigarettes are often marketed as a safer choice, they still contain chemicals whose effects on fetal development remain ambiguous. Dual use could exacerbate these risks. Moreover, the societal stigma tied to smoking during pregnancy might drive women to perceive e-cigarettes as a less harmful option or even as a cessation aid [65]. Such misconceptions can intensify nicotine dependence. The added stress and guilt of dual use can also bear psychological implications, further impacting fetal well-being.

E-cigarettes contain nicotine, a substance known to detrimentally affect fetal development, leading to outcomes such as preterm births and low birth weights. Additionally, e-cigarettes release potentially harmful substances like propylene glycol, glycerin, and flavorings [66]. When heated, these substances can decompose into toxicants such as formaldehyde, acetaldehyde, and acrolein, which are associated with respiratory and cardiovascular diseases and could pose significant health risks to both the mother and fetus. Furthermore, e-cigarettes can emit heavy metals like lead, nickel, and chromium from their heating coils [67]. These metals, when inhaled, may contribute to respiratory, cardiovascular, and neurodevelopmental disorders. It is noteworthy that e-cigarettes do not produce carbon monoxide, unlike traditional cigarettes. However, in dual-use scenarios where traditional cigarettes are also used, the reintroduction of carbon monoxide exposure compounds the risks, exacerbating potential hypoxia in foetuses.

While the risks associated with dual use of e-cigarettes and combustible tobacco during pregnancy are significant, there is an opposing viewpoint that merits consideration. Advocates for e-cigarette use among pregnant women argue that, for those unable to quit smoking, e-cigarettes may present a less harmful alternative. The rationale behind this perspective is that e-cigarette vapor contains many of the same harmful chemicals found in traditional cigarette smoke but at substantially lower levels [68]. Proponents suggest that, although not devoid of risk, the reduced presence of these toxicants in e-cigarettes might make them comparatively less detrimental to both the pregnant woman and her developing baby than continuing to smoke traditional cigarettes alone. This argument hinges on the belief that reducing the harm associated with smoking, even if not eliminating it, could be beneficial when cessation is not achievable [69]. Such a harm reduction approach shows the complexity of smoking behavior during pregnancy and highlights the need for a deeper understanding of both the behavioral motivations and the health impacts of using e-cigarettes as a potential cessation or reduction tool in this vulnerable population.

While the risks associated with dual use of e-cigarettes and combustible tobacco during pregnancy are significant, some advocate that e-cigarettes may serve as a less harmful alternative for pregnant women who are unable to quit smoking entirely. Proponents argue that, although e-cigarette vapor still contains harmful chemicals, the levels of these toxicants are generally lower than in traditional cigarette smoke. Consequently, they suggest that using e-cigarettes instead of conventional cigarettes might pose comparatively lower risks to both the pregnant woman and her developing baby, particularly when full cessation is not feasible. This perspective is based on the idea that even a reduction in harm rather than complete elimination could still be beneficial for those unable to quit smoking entirely. However, this harm reduction approach has met with concern from medical and regulatory authorities. Federal agencies in the United States have issued public warnings about e-cigarette use, as cases of severe lung illness linked to vaping have increased sharply, with over 34 fatalities reported. Similarly, the European Respiratory Society (ERS) has stated that “vaping is a probable potential cause” of these illnesses, although definitive conclusions have yet to be drawn. This concern highlights the complex nature of the harm reduction concept in tobacco control. A harm reduction strategy typically suggests the use of alternative nicotine delivery products, such as e-cigarettes or heated tobacco, to replace conventional cigarettes and thus mitigate harm. Although the approach seems logical and appealing to smokers, healthcare providers, and policymakers, the ERS cautions that the reality is far more nuanced. The ERS argues that the harm reduction approach rests on the incorrect assumption that smokers cannot or do not wish to quit smoking altogether [70]. Research, in fact, indicates that the majority of smokers express a desire to quit and increasingly dislike their nicotine dependence. Moreover, effective, evidence-based treatments for tobacco dependence are widely available, safe, and proven to help individuals achieve full cessation. From a public health perspective, therefore, complete cessation, rather than partial harm reduction through alternative products, remains the most favorable strategy.

It is pertinent to evaluate the implications of nicotine exposure from dual use of e-cigarettes and combustible tobacco during pregnancy by drawing parallels with Nicotine Replacement Therapy (NRT). Studies involving NRT during pregnancy have demonstrated that pregnant women who complement their smoking habit with NRT patches smoke fewer cigarettes, exhale lower levels of CO, yet maintain similar cotinine concentrations, a biomarker for nicotine exposure. This suggests that NRT may help mitigate some risks associated with smoking, without significantly increasing nicotine intake. Analogously, e-cigarettes could hypothetically serve a similar harm reduction role [71]. They might reduce the quantity of cigarettes smoked and exposure to harmful combustion products, while managing nicotine levels effectively. Such potential benefits propose that dual use of e-cigarettes, for those unable to quit smoking, might not necessarily exacerbate health risks but rather serve as a viable harm reduction strategy. However, the long-term outcomes and the balance of potential harm versus benefit require rigorous scientific investigation to ensure that health policy is guided by robust evidence [22].

Our understanding of dual use, especially during pregnancy, requires further investigation. While there’s ample data on tobacco smoking, there’s a significant data gap concerning long-term e-cigarette use during pregnancy. Future research should focus on the physiological changes due to e-cigarette usage and its interplay with traditional smoking. Longitudinal studies can shed light on birth outcomes and children’s health who experienced dual nicotine exposure in utero. To design effective public health strategies, understanding the sociocultural drivers behind dual use is paramount. Technological advancements should also be harnessed for more accurate nicotine exposure measurements. Nevertheless, our study has its limitations. We limited our scope to English-language articles, leading to a noticeable absence of studies from Asia, Africa, and South America. Furthermore, the persistent source of heterogeneity remained unidentified, and the status of dual use and tobacco use was self-reported by participants, which could introduce bias. The estimates were unadjusted and might be subject to residual confounding. There is no information on frequency of use of both combustible and e-cigarettes, occasional use might be of less concern than regular use of both combustible and e-cigarettes. The presence of publication bias is also inevitable and should be taken into consideration while interpreting the results. Additionally, several studies included in our review utilized data from the Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System, prompting concerns about potential sample overlap. This aspect warrants further investigation to ensure the robustness of our findings.

Conclusion

The dual use of tobacco and e-cigarettes among pregnant women poses significant health concerns, with our systematic review revealing a 4.6% prevalence of such behavior with high heterogeneity across studies. The combined risks of both products emphasize the urgency for further research and public health interventions. Addressing misconceptions about e-cigarettes and understanding sociocultural drivers is crucial for effective strategies to protect maternal and fetal health. More studies from different locations are required to better understanding of the dual use among pregnant women.
