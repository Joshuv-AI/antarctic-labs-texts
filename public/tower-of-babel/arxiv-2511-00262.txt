LLM-Driven Cost-Effective Requirements Change Impact Analysis
Romina Etezadi, Sallam Abualhaija, Chetan Arora, Lionel Briand

Abstract
. Requirements are inherently subject to changes throughout the software development lifecycle. Within the limited budget available to requirements engineers, manually identifying the impact of such changes on other requirements is both error-prone and effort-intensive. That might lead to overlooked impacted requirements, which, if not properly managed, can cause serious issues in the downstream tasks. Inspired by the growing potential of large language models (LLMs) across diverse domains, we propose ProReFiCIA , an LLM-driven approach for automatically identifying the impacted requirements when changes occur. We conduct an extensive evaluation of ProReFiCIA using several LLMs and prompts variants tailored to this task. Using the best combination of an LLM and a prompt variant, ProReFiCIA achieves a recall of 93.3% on a benchmark dataset and 95.8% on a newly created industry dataset, demonstrating its strong effectiveness in identifying impacted requirements. Further, the cost of applying ProReFiCIA remains small, as the engineer only needs to review the generated results, which represent between 2.1% and 8.5% of the entire set of requirements.



1.
Introduction

Software systems today are increasingly complex, distributed, and tightly coupled, making even seemingly minor changes potentially risky and costly. In this context, Change Impact Analysis (CIA) plays a critical role in Requirements Engineering (RE) by systematically identifying, evaluating, and managing the effects of proposed modifications
(
Jönsson and Lindvall 2005
)
. The primary objective of CIA is to determine which components—ranging from requirements and design artifacts to source code and documentation—are likely to be affected by a change in requirements. Accurate impact assessment is essential not only for minimizing unintended side effects but also for supporting cost estimation, prioritization, and strategic decision-making in software maintenance and evolution
(
Arora et al. 2015a
)
.
Throughout the software development lifecycle, requirements are often subject to rapid changes due to evolving project needs, stakeholder input, or external factors. When a requirement is modified, it is thus essential to assess the potential impact of this change on other related requirements. This process is known as inter-requirement change impact analysis
(
Pohl 2016
)
. The process of identifying, assessing, and incorporating these changes across the entire requirements specification can be both time-consuming and costly.

Traditionally, CIA has relied on manual inspections, expert judgment, and syntactic dependency analysis. While effective in small systems, these approaches often struggle to scale to large, evolving software landscapes and may fail to capture implicit or semantic dependencies.
To address the challenges of manually identifying the impacted requirements, automated approaches have been proposed
(
Nejati et al. 2016
;
Abualhaija et al. 2024
)
. However, research on the impact analysis of inter-requirement changes has been limited
(
Arora et al. 2015b
)
. Other studies on traceability link recovery (TLR), which focus on predicting trace links between different software artifacts, have proposed techniques that can also be applied to CIA tasks through the use of requirement traceability
(
Hey et al. 2025
;
Vogelsang et al. 2025
;
Mucha et al. 2024
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Abbas et al. 2023
)
. These approaches leverage semantic similarity, dependency graphs, and machine learning to infer relationships between artifacts.
Despite their utility, the primary challenges of CIA cannot be fully addressed through general TLR techniques alone.
One key limitation lies in the nature and variability of change requests. In practice, a change may be triggered either by an updated requirement that modifies existing functionality or by a descriptive explanation, often expressed in natural language (NL), outlining how the change should be implemented across related artifacts
(
Arora et al. 2015a
)
.
General TLR techniques, operating at the requirements level, are typically designed to recover relationships or links between requirements using the same NL style rather than focusing on complex change descriptions.
Consequently, they may fail to capture implicit dependencies, indirect impacts, or nuanced semantic relationships that are critical for accurately predicting change propagation.

The advent of LLM models has opened new avenues for RE tasks
(
Zadenoori et al. 2025
)
. LLM models can capture subtle semantic relationships between NL texts and, more importantly, infer indirect dependencies, which is an essential objective in CIA tasks. Building on the capabilities of LLMs and prompt engineering, we introduce
ProReFiCIA
, an approach that leverages LLMs’ reasoning and contextual understanding to identify potential change propagation. By doing so,
ProReFiCIA
enhances the completeness of detected impacts while maintaining high accuracy in predicting which requirements are truly affected.

Contributions.

This paper makes the following contributions:

•

We design a set of 64 distinct prompt variations and perform an extensive evaluation to analyze how the level of detail in the prompt, as well as its structure, influences the performance of LLM models on the CIA task.

•

We conduct a comprehensive evaluation across five distinct LLMs to assess their relative effectiveness at addressing the CIA task.

•

We present
ProReFiCIA
, an automated approach for CIA that combines refinement and filtering stages with advanced prompt engineering techniques to enhance overall performance and compare it with baseline techniques. Note that
ProReFiCIA
does not require a pre-existing training dataset, making our approach adaptable across domains without extensive data collection or preparation.

•

We evaluate
ProReFiCIA
. On an existing dataset, we show that, on average, it successfully identifies 93.3% of the impacted requirements while retrieving only 8.5% of the total requirements for inspection by the analyst. Furthermore, on a newly created and more complex industry dataset,
ProReFiCIA
identifies 95.8% of the impacted requirements while retrieving only 2.1% of the total requirements. Results therefore show the high cost-effectiveness of
ProReFiCIA
.

2.
Background

In this section, we provide definitions of the key concepts and terms used throughout this paper. The definitions are organized into two categories: 1) Change Impact Analysis (CIA) and 2) Natural Language Processing (NLP).

2.1.
Change Impact Analysis

Change Rationale.

A change rationale is an NL description that specifies a change to be applied to the requirements documents. Furthermore, a change rationale may contain a requirement that has been modified
(
Arora et al. 2015c
)
. The description of a change rationale can belong to three categories
(
Arora et al. 2015c
)
:
1)
Addition:
In this category, the description of a change indicates the addition of a new feature or functionality. For example, consider the
SAT-DLink
system introduced in Section
1
, with the following change rationale: “
The SAT-DLink platform shall support another ancillary payload (AP-1) up to 2.2 kg in weight.
”. This statement indicates that a new payload (AP-1) is to be integrated into the system. Consequently, the addition of this payload may affect other requirements related to the platform’s payload capacity, system interfaces, operational procedures, and resource allocations.
2)
Deletion:
The description indicates that a specific feature or functionality is to be removed from the system. For example, the change rationale “
dual launch configuration is not required
” indicates that the dual launch capability should be eliminated.
3)
Modification:
This category involves both the removal of an existing feature or functionality and the addition of a new one. For example, consider the change rationale: “
Change the reference documents from Ref-D1 to Ref-D2, based on the new project guidelines
”. In this case, any previous reference to the Ref-D1 should be replaced with the new project reference document, i.e., Ref-D2. Such modifications may have cascading effects on related system components.

Impact Set.

When a change rationale is provided, it is necessary to identify and list all requirements that may be affected by the proposed change. The collection of such requirements is referred to as the
impact set
. This impact set represents the full scope of potential changes within the system, capturing both direct and indirect effects on the requirements. Correctly determining the impact set is essential for maintaining system consistency, ensuring traceability, and supporting informed decision-making during the change management process.

2.2.
Natural Language Processing

Large Language Models.
LLMs have revolutionized NLP by leveraging the Transformer
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
architecture, which employs self-attention mechanisms to capture intricate dependencies within data sequences. Subsequent advancements have led to the development of LLMs with tens to hundreds of billions of parameters, trained on vast corpora using self-supervised learning techniques
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
. Therefore, instead of fine-tuning language models (LMs), prompt engineering reformulates downstream tasks as natural-language instructions.
With the right prompts, a single unsupervised LLMs can handle many tasks without additional training. However, the model’s effectiveness depends on the careful design of input prompts
(
Liu et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2024
)
.

LLM Embeddings.

Embeddings derived from LLMs (LLM Embeddings) represent a new generation of contextual representations that leverage the scale and multi-task capabilities of modern LLMs. Unlike embeddings from traditional pretrained language models (PLMs) such as BERT, RoBERTa, or ALBERT, LLM embeddings are derived from models trained on massive corpora using autoregressive or instruction-following objectives. This broader training enables LLM embeddings to capture not only syntactic and semantic properties but also higher-level reasoning, world knowledge, and contextual subtleties
(
Krassovitskiy et al. 2025
)
, making them more suitable for tasks that require nuanced semantic understanding, complex instructions, or domain-specific knowledge.

Cache-Augmented Generation.
Recent advances in LLMs capable of processing longer texts have prompted a shift toward cache-augmented generation (CAG)
(
Chan et al. 2025
)
. This approach addresses the traditional retrieval bottleneck by preloading relevant knowledge directly into the LLM’s extended context window, enabling more efficient access to necessary information
(
Huang and Chang 2023
)
. By incorporating information within the context window, CAG can also enhance response coherence, ensuring that each part of the generated output remains aligned with the provided context rather than deviating into unrelated content
(
Mialon et al. 2023
)
. Despite these benefits, processing longer text sequences introduces challenges, such as the "lost-in-the-middle" problem, where the model may inadvertently overlook or underweigh portions of the prompt
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
.

Natural Language Inference.
Natural Language Inference (NLI)
(
Dagan et al. 2005
)
is a fundamental task in natural language processing (NLP) that involves determining the logical relationship between a premise and a hypothesis. The goal is to classify the relationship into three categories: entailment, contradiction, or neutral. For instance, consider the premise, “
A man is eating a salad
”. From this statement, we can infer the hypothesis, “
A man is having a meal
”. In this case, the premise entails the hypothesis because eating a salad constitutes having a meal. This illustrates how NLI involves reasoning about the logical relationship between two pieces of text.

3.0.1.
Step 1: Prompt Engineering

To identify optimal prompts, we systematically explore different prompt variations using the RICE template
(
Vogelsang and Fischbach 2024
)
. We selectively include or exclude different elements depending on the desired level of detail. The RICE template consists of four components: Role, Instruction (i.e.,
What the model is told to do
), Context or Constraints (i.e.,
The setting and rules for how to do it
), and Examples for a few-shot setting. In this paper, we exclude examples to evaluate the LLM models in a purely zero-shot setting, ensuring that performance reflects their inherent generalization abilities without reliance on task-specific data.
Each component of the RICE template can include varying levels of detail, which can influence the LLM’s outcome.
Accordingly, we design task-relevant details to align with the objectives of the CIA task for each component of the prompt.

Role:
In the context of prompt engineering,
role
typically refers to the persona or perspective the model is instructed to take on during its response. However, this component is considered optional in our approach, whose effectiveness as part of the prompt must be assessed. The text labelled as Detail
1
in Figure
3
illustrates the
role
component by asking the model to have a requirements engineer persona.

Instruction:
In prompt engineering,
instruction
refers to the explicit directive given to the model, telling it what to do. For the CIA task, we propose three distinct directives. Among them, the directive corresponding to the core CIA objective (Detail
2
) is considered mandatory and is assumed to be always included in the prompt. Details
3
and
4
correspond to two optional instructions included in the prompt. Detail
3
is intended to encourage the model to consider commonsense knowledge that may be necessary for understanding the change impact. Detail
4
, on the other hand, guides the model towards identifying semantic relationships between keywords present in both the change rationale and requirements.

Context:
In prompt engineering,
context
refers to the background information or supporting content provided to the model. It helps the model understand the situation, task, or domain better, which can lead to more accurate and relevant responses. For this component, we define three primary types of context. First, Detail
5
provides a brief explanation of the CIA task. Second, Detail
6
offers a description of what a change rationale is, including the types of information it may contain. Third, Detail
7
specifies the domain in which the requirements are written. All of these details within the
context
component are considered optional. All possible prompt variations are generated by systematically including or excluding each optional detail.

3.0.2.
Step 2: Prompt Execution and Selection

Considering the 64 prompt variations and 5 LLM models, we apply the CAG technique described in Section

The robustness of LLM models and the effect of each prompt detail are examined in RQ1 and RQ2, respectively, as discussed in Sections

In addition, given the large number of prompt variations and LLM models, as well as the high cost of executing all combinations, we select the model–prompt pairs for investigation in post-processing. This selection process is the focus of RQ3, elaborated in Section
4.7

3.0.3.
Step 3: Refinement Step

In the refinement step, we address the tendency of LLM models to occasionally overlook certain requirements in the list. Therefore, motivated by the way humans iteratively revisit overlooked information to ensure completeness, we introduce an additional post-processing step to mitigate the lost-in-the-middle problem discussed in Section
2

This step is called the refinement step. The requirements identified in this step are then combined with those from the first execution to form the updated impact set. Below, we present an example illustrating how this step is applied:

3.0.4.
Step 4: Filtering Step

Following the refinement step, we obtain the impact set corresponding to the change rationale, along with justifications for selecting each requirement. At this step, the generated impact set can be presented to a requirements analyst for review.
We further propose the following algorithm to reduce the false positives in the impact set:

Ranking Phase:
In this phase, the LLM is prompted to rank the requirements within the impact set according to the level of confidence it believes they are impacted. To facilitate this, the model is provided with the change rationale, the full text of each requirement, and the LLM-generated reasoning used to select them. Presenting this contextual information encourages reflection on prior decisions and supports a critical reassessment of outputs. This process enables the LLM to reorder the impact set based on a more thorough evaluation of relevance and confidence, ultimately enhancing the prioritization of requirements for further analysis. Below, we present the prompt we utilize to get the rank list:

Prompt used to rank the requirements in the impact set:

You are an analyst in the field of requirements engineering. I will provide a change rationale, its corresponding impact set, and justification for selection. Rank the requirements in the impact set according to the strength of their relationship to the change rationale, based on the content of the requirement texts and the provided justifications for their selection.

Output format: Sorted_List: <req_ids>

Change Rationale: <change_rationale_text>

Impacted Requirements: <req_ids>

Justification: <text>

Selection Phase:
This phase is described in Algorithm
1
, which filters the ranked requirements. It finalizes the impact set by using a natural language inference model described in Section
2
. It takes two textual inputs and determines whether the second input logically entails the first. In this context, the NLI model is used to assess whether a selected requirement, together with its accompanying justification, entails the given change rationale. The output is a binary prediction indicating entailment (labelled as 1) or non-entailment (labelled as 0). As this context requires only two labels (impacted or not impacted), we exclude the third label of the NLI model discussed in Section
2
during fine-tuning.
The details regarding how this fine-tuning process was applied in our experiments are presented in Section
4.8
.
To maintain recall while minimizing false positives, the NLI results are combined with the previously obtained ranked list of requirements. This approach strikes a balance between retaining true positives and filtering out false positives. More precisely, the following algorithm is applied to determine whether a requirement should remain in the final impact set:

•

If the size of the impact set is less than 5, no cutoff is applied—the entire set of requirements is retained, and the process terminates at this step, as the set is sufficiently small for rapid human inspection.

•

If the impact set size exceeds 5, the algorithm proceeds as follows: For each requirement, first, the NLI output is examined. If the prediction is 1, the requirement is retained. If the prediction is 0, then the requirement’s position in the sorted list is reconsidered. If the position falls within the top 50%, the NLI output is ignored and the requirement is retained, as items in the higher-ranked half are more likely to be relevant. Otherwise, the requirement is considered a false positive and is removed.
The decision to retain only the top half was based on our observation that, through preliminary experiments, the impacted requirements mainly appear in the first half of the sorted list.

Empirical Evaluation

This section presents the evaluation of our approach. We begin with a brief description of the baselines and datasets employed in Section

4.1.
Baselines

To enhance the evaluation of our proposed solutions, we compare our solution with three baselines: 1) NARCIA
(
Arora et al. 2015b
)
, an NLP-based approach from the literature specifically developed for requirements change impact analysis. 2) The second baseline is based on a traceability technique that employs Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) technology. 3) As a third baseline, we employ a straightforward semantic similarity algorithm that leverages embeddings generated by LLMs.

NARCIA.

NARCIA facilitates requirements change impact analysis by applying NLP techniques that go beyond basic keyword matching, focusing instead on the phrasal structure of requirements. Their key idea is that when a requirement changes, not all related requirements are equally affected; instead, change propagation depends on specific conditions. An analyst should specify change propagation conditions using Boolean logic over phrases or their synonyms, defining when and how a change should propagate based on the given change rationale. Based on the identified change propagation conditions, requirements are prioritized according to their likelihood of being affected. The assigned score reflects the probability that a given requirement will be impacted by the change tied to the corresponding propagation condition.
Their approach applies text chunking to identify noun phrases (NPs) and verb phrases (VPs) in both the change rationale and the candidate requirement. By detecting changes at the phrase level and categorizing them as added or deleted, the method enables a more context-aware impact analysis. For each candidate requirement, it is checked whether these changed phrases (NPs/VPs) appear in the candidate requirement under specific change propagation conditions (e.g., subject/object overlap, semantic similarity). Each satisfied propagation condition contributes to the impact score. The final likelihood score for a candidate requirement is dependent on the matched propagation conditions defined for the change rationale.
The system generates a ranked list of affected requirements for a given change rationale, reducing manual effort by allowing analysts to focus on top-ranked requirements.

CoT.

As part of our evaluation, we consider the recently proposed requirements traceability technique by Hey et al.
(
Hey et al. 2025
)
. In their approach, they introduced a RAG method, combining a retrieval step with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting. For each source requirement, the RAG retrieves the top-k corresponding target requirements using semantic similarity. Each source–target requirement pair is then provided to the LLM, which, through Chain-of-Thought prompting, determines whether a trace link exists between the given pair. For the retrieval step, they employed OpenAI’s embedding model
text-embedding-3-large
, and for the prompting step, they experimented with various LLMs, among which GPT4o demonstrated the best overall performance. Moreover, they have experimented with two different prompt structures:
KISS
and
CoT
. The KISS prompt is a simple zero-shot prompt that asks the LLM a yes-or-no question about whether the source requirement is related to the target requirement. In contrast, the
CoT
uses chain-of-thought prompting to determine traceability by providing source and target artifact and asking, as in
KISS
, a simple yes-or-no question about whether a link should exist between them. Based on their experiments,
CoT
showed better performance on average. Therefore, in our study, we focus exclusively on GPT4o and the
CoT
prompt for this method.

Semantic Similarity (S).

With the emergence of powerful embedding models derived from LLMs, the need for extensive training or fine-tuning has been significantly reduced. In this work, since no task-specific training or fine-tuning data is available, we leverage LLM embeddings, for the baseline semantic similarity, which provide richer and more informative representations. To select an appropriate model, we utilize the top-ranked embedding model,
gemini-embedding-001
, according to the MTEB (Massive Text Embedding Benchmark) leaderboard
1
1

1

https://huggingface.co/spaces/mteb/leaderboard
. The similarity between each change rationale and all requirements is computed using the cosine similarity of their embeddings. For each change rationale, a list of similarity scores corresponding to all requirements is generated. To determine the impact set, requirements are ranked in descending order by their similarity scores (from highest to lowest), and a cutoff is applied to select the most relevant ones. We employ three different strategies for cutoff point selection to systematically evaluate the effect of the cutoff point choice on the resulting impact sets:

•

T1:
We use a fixed threshold value, and any requirement with a similarity score exceeding this threshold is included in the impact set. For this study, we use the constant value 0.5 because it is the midpoint of the similarity interval.

•

T2:
NARCIA proposes a specific technique for cutoff point selection, which we also adopt in this work. In this approach, the cutoff point in a sorted list of requirements is determined using a delta chart that visualizes the differences between consecutive similarity scores. The cutoff is set at the position beyond which no significant peaks remain, meaning the scores no longer clearly distinguish which requirements are impacted. A peak is considered significant if it exceeds one-third of the highest peak. For example, given similarity scores 0.85, 0.82, 0.80, 0.78, 0.60, 0.58, 0.57, and 0.40, the differences between consecutive scores would be 0.03, 0.02, 0.02, 0.18, 0.02, 0.01, and 0.17. The highest peak is 0.18 (between 0.78 and 0.60), so any peak larger than 0.18 / 3 = 0.06 is considered significant. The second peak at 0.17 (between 0.57 and 0.40) is also significant and is the last. The cutoff is set at the end of the right slope of the last significant peak (0.17 between 0.57 and 0.40), so the cutoff is at the requirement with a similarity score of 0.57.

•

T3:
This cutoff point selection approach, proposed by
(
Etezadi et al. 2025
)
, is similar to
T2
and determines the cutoff by analyzing the differences between consecutive similarity scores in the sorted list. The cutoff point is set to the position of the largest drop. For example, consider the example illustrated in
T2
. The largest difference of 0.18 occurs between 0.78 and 0.60, indicating a sharp decline. Therefore, the cutoff is set at 0.78, marking the boundary between requirements likely to be impacted by the change and those that are not.

dataset is more complex than the WASP dataset, as it contains a larger number of requirements, with less than 18% of them impacted by any of the given change rationales. In contrast, the WASP dataset exhibits a higher proportion, with 31% of its requirements impacted. This disparity indicates that change impact analysis for
SAT-DLink
is inherently more challenging: a much larger pool of requirements must be searched to identify a comparatively smaller fraction that is actually relevant. As a result, the risk of overlooking impacted requirements is higher, and the precision of retrieval methods becomes particularly critical. By utilizing these two datasets, we aim to evaluate the effectiveness and robustness of our solution across different domains and levels of complexity.

4.3.
LLM Model Selection

As part of our proposed approach, which relies on prompt-based techniques, we need to select an appropriate LLM to execute the designed prompts.
Different LLMs offer distinct capabilities, training foundations, and optimization strategies, all of which can influence how effectively they support requirements engineering tasks.
Relying solely on one model should not be considered best practice, as the rapid obsolescence of LLMs can quickly undermine the validity, generalizability, and reproducibility of the results
(
Zadenoori et al. 2025
)
. To address this limitation, evaluating multiple LLMs can mitigate the risk of model-specific bias but also provide a more robust understanding of how findings generalize across different LLM architectures.

To provide a comprehensive evaluation, we therefore selected five widely used and recently impactful models in this domain: GPT
(
Radford et al. 2018
)
, Deepseek
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
, LLaMa
(
Dubey et al. 2024
)
, Mistral
(
Jiang et al. 2023
)
, and Gemini
(
Team et al. 2023
;
Team et al. 2024
)
.
Each of these models brings complementary strengths. GPT, one of the earliest and most established families of LLMs, has been widely adopted for RE-related tasks, making it a natural baseline for comparison. Deepseek represents a newer generation of models optimized for reasoning and efficiency, and it has shown promising results in structured tasks relevant to RE. LLaMa, developed as a scalable open-source alternative, is particularly valuable for reproducibility and community-driven experimentation, enabling fine-tuning in RE-specific contexts. Mistral, known for its lightweight yet high-performing architecture, provides an opportunity to test how smaller, efficiency-oriented models handle RE workloads. Finally, Gemini integrates multimodal reasoning capabilities and cutting-edge advancements in alignment, offering insights into how next-generation LLMs can push the boundaries of requirements analysis and automation. For each model, we selected versions with more parameters, as they often retain more world knowledge and domain-specific patterns from pretraining. Moreover, we also focus on the models that have been extensively tested in the RE domain. The chosen versions of all LLMs are listed in Table
2
.

By including these five models, we aim to investigate how different LLMs perform and generalize when applied to CIA in RE. This comparative perspective allows us to explore not only their individual effectiveness but also the extent to which diverse architectures and training paradigms influence outcomes in RE contexts.

4.4.
Research Questions (RQs)

RQ1. Which large language model demonstrates more robust performance across different prompts and datasets?

The goal is to examine the stability and robustness of LLMs under a wide range of variations. Specifically, we evaluate their performance across 64 distinct prompts and two datasets that differ in domain and complexity.

RQ2. Which prompt details most influence LLM models’ performance across datasets, and do their effects contribute to performance gains or losses?

Our objective is to examine the sensitivity of the robust LLM models selected from RQ1 to optional details in prompts.
By doing so, we aim to understand how prompt design and dataset characteristics, such as the domain or complexity of the requirements, jointly influence model behavior. Moreover, we identify the influence of specific details on the model and examine how their inclusion or exclusion contributes to its improvement.

RQ3. What is the most optimal model–prompt pair across datasets for CIA?

Given the numerous LLM models and prompt variations introduced in the paper, our goal is to identify the model and prompt configuration that achieves both reasonably strong performance and generalization across datasets.

RQ4. How does our proposed post-processing step in ProReFiCIA contribute to performance improvement?

We apply refinement and filtering stages to improve recall and precision. These steps are designed to mitigate limitations of LLMs that cannot be effectively addressed solely through prompt engineering.

RQ5. How does ProReFiCIA fare against a simpler baseline solution?

We evaluate the performance of
ProReFiCIA
against a simpler, more cost-effective baseline. For this analysis, we used a CIA baseline from the literature and applied it to the WASP dataset. Additionally, we implemented simpler techniques, including semantic similarity with pre-trained language models.

Methodology.

A total of 64 prompts, as described in Section
3.0.1
, are constructed and applied to the five models across two datasets, yielding 320 distinct results using Recall (R), Precision (P), and F2 scores. Recall measures the proportion of impacted requirements correctly retrieved by a specific prompt and model, indicating the model’s ability to capture all relevant items. Precision quantifies the proportion of retrieved requirements that are actually relevant, reflecting the reliability of the model’s predictions. The F2 score combines recall and precision, placing greater emphasis on recall, and provides a single metric that balances the need to retrieve as many impacted requirements as possible while maintaining a reasonable level of precision. In the context of the CIA task, the effort required to handle false positives is relatively minor compared to the risk of missing an impacted requirement, which justifies the use of F2 as the preferred evaluation metric.

In RQ1, the primary goal is to investigate the significance of changes in the models’ output across the 64 prompts and two different datasets.
We use the results presented in Tables
3
and Table
4
to assess the robustness of the models, where robustness refers to the stability and consistency of model performance when subjected to variations in prompts and datasets. To investigate the robustness of LLMs, we analyze box plots of the F2 scores for each model across the two datasets. The distribution of scores indicates both the central tendency and variability of model performance. Based on this information, we can identify which models maintain stable outputs under varying conditions and which are more sensitive to prompt or dataset changes.

On the

For further analysis of LLaMa and GPT4o, identified as the most robust models on the WASP and

These observations underscore LLaMa’s comparatively more stable performance across prompts.

Answer to RQ1:
Across prompts and datasets, the GPT4o and LLaMa models exhibit the most robust performance compared to the other three models. Furthermore, LLaMa exhibits greater consistency and stability than GPT4o.

Methodology.

In this RQ, we focus on the robust models identified in RQ1, GPT4o and LLaMa, and investigate which specific details within the prompt lead to substantial differences in the outputs of the LLMs.
To evaluate the sensitivity of models to prompt details, we consider prompt details combinations as a set of six features: details
1
to
7
, excluding the mandatory detail
2
. Each combination is applied to every change rationale in the dataset, and the resulting output changes are assessed using the F2 score, which gauges sensitivity to prompt variation. These results are then analyzed with a gradient boosting model
(
Friedman 2001
)
based on decision trees.
Gradient boosting model builds a strong model by sequentially combining many weak learners (ensemble learning over decision trees).
Each new tree is trained to reduce the errors of the previous ones by following the gradient of the loss function, which in this case is the Mean Squared Error (MSE). This iterative process allows the model to capture complex relationships and interactions between features that a single decision tree would likely miss.
For feature importance, gradient boosting leverages the fact that each decision tree splits the data based on features that reduce prediction error. By aggregating these reductions across all trees, the algorithm estimates the contribution of each detail to model performance. In other words, importance scores are averaged across all decision trees created by gradient boosting and then normalized. The importance score calculation, for detail
d
in a single decision tree, is given below:

\text{Imp}(d)=\frac{1}{N_{\text{root}}}\sum_{j\,\text{splits on }d}\Big(N_{j}I_{j}-N_{j_{L}}I_{j_{L}}-N_{j_{R}}I_{j_{R}}\Big)

\displaystyle N_{j}

\displaystyle\text{ = number of samples at node }j

4.8.
Refinement and Filtering in
ProReFiCIA
(RQ4)

Methodology.

The primary objective of this RQ is to examine the effectiveness of our proposed post-processing steps in
ProReFiCIA
. We employ the optimal model–prompt combinations identified in RQ3,
P30
with LLaMa, to investigate the extent to which the proposed post-processing contributes to improving prompt results.
To this end, we compare performance before and after applying the post-processing steps.
The performance is evaluated based on how well the predicted impact set includes all impacted requirements while minimizing the analyst’s effort to filter out false positives. Therefore, we use two metrics:
Effectiveness
to assess how well the impact set retrieved all impacted requirements and
Cost
which evaluates the effort required for validation. For effectiveness we use the recall, which is described in Equation
1
, and we compute the average proportion of requirements retrieved relative to the total number of requirements in the dataset, which is described in Equation
2
.

(1)

eff=\frac{1}{N_{c}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{c}}\frac{TP_{c_{i}}}{TP_{c_{i}}+FN_{c_{i}}}

(2)

cost=\frac{1}{N_{c}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{c}}\frac{TP_{c_{i}}+FP_{c_{i}}}{N_{req}}

N_{c}
represents the number of change rationales in the dataset,
N_{req}
the total number of requirements in the dataset,
TP_{c_{i}}
and
FP_{c_{i}}
represent the number of true positives and false positives retrieved in the impact set for the change rationale
i
.
A higher effectiveness value indicates a more complete impact set, with the maximum possible value being 1 (or 100%). Conversely, a lower cost corresponds to a more efficient impact set.

The refinement step in post-processing is designed to enhance effectiveness by increasing the number of correctly retrieved impacted requirements. In contrast, the filtering step aims to reduce analysis costs by minimizing the number of requirements that need to be reviewed in the final impact set.

In the filtering step, we fine-tune the NLI model using the outputs from Step 3. The fine-tuning process is carried out using the retrieved requirements from the Refinement step. It employs the leave-one-out technique, where each change rationale, along with its impact set, serves as the test set, and the remaining change rationales and impact sets are the training set. The process is repeated for each dataset separately, matching the number of change rationales in each dataset. This ensures that each change rationale is tested once and assigned a prediction. At each stage of fine-tuning under the leave-one-out approach, the NLI model is reset to its original pre-trained weights, ensuring that each iteration starts from the same baseline conditions. By resetting the model before each run, knowledge from earlier training iterations does not affect later evaluations, and each test instance is excluded from the training process.
For each training set, the ground-truth labels for each change rationale-requirement pair are used. Eventually, by repeating this process k times (k=5 for WASP and k=11 for
SAT-DLink
), the complete set of predicted labels for each change rationale-requirement pair is obtained. In practice, it is worth noting that an analyst can fine-tune the model once using a small labelled sample and then reuse it for new change rationales.

5.
Threats to Validity

Internal Validity.

One internal validity threat in this paper concerns the effect of different prompt combinations on the quality of the impact set. To ensure that the observed differences in model performance are genuinely caused by prompt structure and not by other external factors, the study systematically controls the experimental setup. To ensure consistency and reduce variability, the models’ parameters, such as the temperature, were kept fixed across all runs. Each prompt was tested multiple times for each model, and the final results were computed by averaging the outcomes to provide a more stable, reliable estimate of performance. Additionally, to mitigate concerns about internal validity related to the prompt details, these elements were not chosen arbitrarily. Instead, they were carefully designed and selected based on a systematic analysis of prompt engineering principles.
This process ensured that any differences in results across prompts and LLMs genuinely reflected the influence of prompt details rather than uncontrolled confounding factors.

External Validity.

The most critical aspect of external validity lies in the generalizability of the proposed approach across datasets. In this study, we examine
ProReFiCIA
across two distinct datasets, each representing a different domain and level of complexity.
Moreover, we systematically conducted the experiments reported in RQ1 and RQ2 to identify the model–prompt pairs that demonstrate the highest consistency and thus generalizability across datasets.
However, further experiments on more diverse and realistic datasets are necessary to strengthen the external validity of our study. Such datasets, however, are not publicly available and need to be curated as we did with
SAT-DLink
.

Conclusion Validity.
Another important threat to the validity of this work concerns the reproducibility of the results. To mitigate this threat, we ensure that all implementation details are reported with high precision. Moreover, to facilitate independent verification and future research, we archive all experiments and relevant artifacts, allowing others to replicate our findings under identical conditions.

6.
Discussions

Our evaluation shows that
ProReFiCIA
outperforms all baseline methods across both datasets. Further, supplying all requirements directly within the LLM’s context window yields better performance than iteratively querying the model or using the RAG technique for the CIA task. RAG introduces challenges such as retrieval latency and potential inaccuracies in selecting relevant documents
(
Chan et al. 2025
)
. These issues are particularly critical in the context of CIA, where change descriptions are often expressed according to diverse terminology. Moreover, iteratively querying the model was found to increase the number of false positives. Therefore, for the CIA task, it is recommended to leverage the LLM’s full context window, along with refinement and filtering steps, to improve prediction accuracy, reduce the number of LLM queries, and minimize errors introduced during retrieval in the RAG technique.
In this study, all requirements fit within the context window of the LLMs used. However, in practice, the number of requirements may exceed the model’s context capacity. In such cases, we propose applying
ProReFiCIA
multiple times, each time including as many requirements as possible within the context window, and then aggregating the results. Nonetheless, additional datasets are needed to validate this strategy, which we leave as future work for this paper.

7.
Related Work

CIA was first introduced and studied in 1993 by Arnold and Bohner
(
Arnold 1996
;
Bohner et al. 1996
)
. They indicated that impact analysis is about identifying the possible effects of a change or predicting what needs to be modified to implement it.
The need to forecast and manage the impact of software changes increases as software systems grow larger and more complex. Software Change Impact Analysis (CIA) gathers current data on the software system to identify which components will be affected by the proposed change and how those components interact with each other. Based on Arnold and Bohner
(
Arnold and Bohner 1993
)
, there are two main perspectives for CIA, including software dependency analysis and traceability analysis
(
Jayatilleke et al. 2018a
)
.
Arnold et al. described three main steps to analyze the change impacts in a system
(
Arnold and Bohner 1993
)
:

(1)

Analyse change specification and software artifacts

(2)

Trace potential impacts

(3)

Implement the requested changes

Changes initiated by a form of change request, which is referred to as the process of change specification and classification, which finishes the identification of the change type
(
Jayatilleke and Lai 2013
)
. When addressing a change, many requirements cannot be considered independent of the other requirements in the SRS, as different types of relationships can exist between them. As a result, an action taken on one requirement may unexpectedly affect another. Therefore, there is a need to identify requirement interdependencies
(
Jayatilleke et al. 2018b
)
. So, there is a need to investigate how requirements depend on one another when there is no semantic or syntactic similarity between them. The investigated dependencies between requirements can be used to develop a predictive model to forecast the change impacts.

In the last few decades, considerable research effort has focused on change impact analysis, especially in the field of software maintenance and evolution
(
Jayatilleke and Lai 2018
;
Alkaf et al. 2019
;
Lehnert 2011a
;
Lehnert 2011b
)
. Change impact analysis was applied to source codes
(
Li et al. 2013
)
, to requirements traceability
(
Hey et al. 2025
;
Rusdianto et al. 2024
;
Goknil et al. 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2014
;
Li et al. 2008
)
. Some researchers have reported the effects of requirements changes on data, architecture, and software design
(
Yazdanshenas and Moonen 2012
;
Von Knethen 2002
)
. Few researchers have studied the challenges of change verification and validation
(
Bjarnason et al. 2014
)
and of co-changing artifacts to better understand software artifact evolution
(
Antoniol et al. 2005
)
. Some approaches have attempted to automate the analysis of change impacts. Alkaf et al.
(
Alkaf et al. 2019
)
performed an automated CIA approach for User Requirements Notation models. Arora et al.
(
Arora et al. 2015c
)
proposed an approach based on NLP for analyzing the impact of change in Natural Language requirements. Nejati et al.
(
Nejati et al. 2016
)
proposed an approach to automatically identify the impact of requirements changes on system design when the requirements and design elements are expressed using models. Jayatilleke et al.
(
Jayatilleke et al. 2018b
)
presented a technique for requirements change analysis that relied on changes that arise at higher levels. Bano et al.
(
Bano et al. 2012
)
performed a systematic literature review on the causes of requirement change. The authors identified various causes and their frequency during the software development processes. They categorized the extracted causes of changes in requirements into two major categories: necessary and accidental. Aryani et al.
(
Aryani et al. 2009
)
proposed a methodology for analyzing change propagation in software using the domain-level behavioral model of a system.

In the requirements specification, one solution to assess the effect of the change is to check the precise correspondence between the terms in the change and their potential definitions and expressions in other specifications. Change can progress across semantically related terms that are not exact matches or relevant syntactic variants. In this situation, it is appropriate to apply a relatedness measure that considers phrases
(
Arora et al. 2015a
;
Arora et al. 2015b
)
. Besides, dependencies in requirements play an essential role in the analysis of change propagation
(
Zhang et al. 2014
)
.

To find the relationships between requirements, many dependency or interdependency models have been developed to define and distinguish relationships based on the requirements’ structural and semantic properties
(
Zhang et al. 2014
)
. However, there has been no empirical assessment of any of these dependency forms in terms of usefulness and applicability
(
Zhang et al. 2014
)
. To assess the potential effects of requirement changes on the overall system, Hassine
(
Hassine et al. 2005
)
applied both slicing and dependency analysis at the level of the use case map (rather than across requirements in natural language). Baumer et al
(
Baumer et al. 2010
)
showed that semantic role labelling can be used to improve computational metaphor identification, and it might also be more effective at identifying relationships with semantic import than typed dependency parsing.

Arora et al.
(
Arora et al. 2015a
)
showed that in their approach, there is no need to define the requirements dependencies in the early stage because the propagation condition can determine if there is a correlation between a changed requirement and the other ones. Since all potential conditions cannot be enumerated, constructing an explicit dependency graph is difficult. Rather than utilizing typed dependencies, they used correlation rates to evaluate the impact of changes. Typed dependencies focus on syntactic structure and grammatical relations, while semantic roles emphasize conceptual and semantic structure

Baumer et al. 2010

De Marneffe and Manning 2008

Alsalemi
(
Alsalemi and Yeoh 2017
)
performed a systematic literature review that focused on the prediction of requirements volatility. According to their research, only a few papers have been published on predicting volatility requirements, and most articles have focused on the causes of requirements change and their effects on project performance. Their work underscores the need for more empirical studies to better address the practical aspects of requirements volatility.

Dhamija
(
Dhamija and Sikka 2019
)
presented a systematic study of advancements in change impact analysis techniques. The study found a need for research into hidden dependencies in software requirements that may not be readily apparent. Techniques for identifying such hidden dependencies among software objects, such as specifications, design, and code, need to be proposed.

Rusdianto
(
Rusdianto et al. 2024
)
recently proposed an automated framework for CIA in software projects leveraging LLMs. Their approach integrates the GPT4 model with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to enhance reasoning over software artifacts. The method first performs a semantic search across document chunks and ranks them based on their similarity to the given change request. Subsequently, the LLM analyzes the change using a ranked list of relevant chunks across four key dimensions: technical, functional, business, and stakeholder impact.

Rusdianto
(
Hey et al. 2025
)
proposed an automated system based on LLMs for inter-requirement traceability. They further suggested that the same traceability model could be extended for CIA. Their approach first uses RAG to retrieve semantically related documents for a given requirement. Then the LLM determines whether to establish a trace link between the requirement and the retrieved candidates.

Existing studies have primarily relied on rule-based or traditional NLP techniques, such as dependency parsing or chunking.
More recent approaches have leveraged LLM capabilities; however, they have primarily focused on traceability and have not assessed the effectiveness of these methods for CIA in scenarios where a change request with a description, rather than a modified requirement, is provided. Therefore, in this paper, we present
ProReFiCIA
, which uses the capabilities of recent LLMs while addressing the specific challenges of CIA. Moreover, it does not require a pre-existing training dataset, making our approach adaptable across domains without extensive data collection or preparation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively investigate prompt engineering for this particular problem. Unlike most prior studies, which focus on a single model, our approach evaluates five leading LLMs.

8.
Conclusion

This study introduces
ProReFiCIA
, an automated framework for change impact analysis.
ProReFiCIA
leverages prompt engineering with CAG on large language models, combined with two post-processing steps—refinement and filtering—to enhance the accuracy of impact predictions. Furthermore, the study investigates the performance of various LLMs and their robustness to variations in prompt structures and levels of detail. Experimental results reveal that the LLaMa model demonstrated the highest robustness compared to GPT4o, DeepSeek, Gemini, and Mistral. Using two datasets, we found that adjusting the prompt’s level of detail for the LLaMa model can significantly improve its performance across datasets.
Our results demonstrate that employing the most robust model, LLaMa, along with a prompt that performs well across datasets, yields 93.3% and 95.8% of impacted requirements identified, at costs of 8.5% and 2.1% of requirements inspected for the WASP and
SAT-DLink
datasets, respectively, the latter being an industry set we curated.

For future work, we plan to conduct a more detailed investigation into the types and causes of errors produced by
ProReFiCIA
. Given that identifying the impact of a requirement change often requires deeper contextual and domain knowledge, we aim to enhance the system by incorporating external descriptive documents.
