Reconciliation in the context of communities in crisis
Authors: Malek Batal, Geneviève Mercille, Lynn Barwin, Caralynn Berti, Laurie H.M. Chan, Amy Ing, Tom Kovesi, Ariane Lafortune, Mélanie Lemire, Lili Miller, Milena Nardocci, Alexandrine Roy, Tonio Sadik, Curtis Scurr, Jolian Wong
Published: 2026

Dr. Orr’s recent CMAJ commentary clearly outlined the foundations of reconciliatory research: creating ethical space, building respectful relationships, and centring community priorities.1 We add a critical constraint that recent experience has made undeniable: these conditions cannot be realized when communities are repeatedly pushed into crisis. Our team, conducting the FEHNCY (Food, Environment, Health and Nutrition of First Nations Children and Youth) study in partnership with the Assembly of First Nations, was forced to postpone planned research with Pimicikamak Cree Nation twice, first because of wildfires, then a catastrophic winter power outage that led to widespread infrastructure failure and evacuation.2,3 In both instances, survival needs took precedence. However, these disruptions raise fundamental questions about research equity. These crises are neither accidental nor isolated. They reflect decades of environmental dispossession and chronic underinvestment in water, housing, and energy systems, compounded by climate change and the enduring effects of colonial policies. 4,5 These same conditions also shape food insecurity, environmental exposures, and health outcomes. Paradoxically, these are precisely the communities whose participation in health research is most critical and most systematically undermined. When communities facing the greatest health inequities are systematically unable to participate in research because of cascading crises, the resulting evidence gaps limit the applicability and generalizability of findings to those populations most in need. This absence is not neutral; under pressure from funders and career timelines, researchers often shift to communities with more stable infrastructure, where data quality and retention are higher. The communities most in need of evidence become the least studied. Policy responses lag. The cycle repeats. Reconciliatory research requires more than ethical intent. Reconciliation through research demands reconciliation in infrastructure. Community leaders have been unequivocal: reliable infrastructure is a necessity, not a luxury.1 Basic infrastructure is a precondition for ethical research. Funding must flex. Timelines must adapt. Communities must lead. Without these changes, researchers risk deepening the very inequities they seek to address.
