Phenotypic Evolution in a Boy with MYL11-Related Distal Arthrogryposis Type 1C: A Case Report
Authors: Rajesh K. Maurya, Niladri Das, Shubha R. Phadke, Amita Moirangthem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDistal arthrogryposis (DA) type 1C (DA1C) is characterized by multiple congenital contractures, scoliosis, and short stature. It is caused by biallelic variants in MYL11(MYLPF), which encodes a regulatory myosin light chain in fast-twitch skeletal muscle.Case PresentationWe evaluated a 3-year-old boy from a consanguineous family presenting with features of distal arthrogryposis. Clinical, radiographic, and electrophysiological assessments were performed. He had distal joint contractures, camptodactyly, cutaneous syndactyly of the second and third toes, absent distal interphalangeal creases, left-sided inguinal hernia, facial dysmorphism, and short stature. Exome sequencing failed to detect any candidate variants. On re-analysis after 7 years, we identified a homozygous NC_000016.10:g.30377860G>T; MYL11(NM_013292.5):c.470G>T; p.(Cys157Phe) variant, which causes DA1C. This variant was reported by Chong et al. in 2020 in seven patients with MYL11-related disorders and was classified as pathogenic.ConclusionsThis study extends the spectrum of MYL11-related disorders by identifying a phenotype of distal arthrogryposis with marked facial dysmorphism reminiscent of DA2, along with other findings like absent distal interphalangeal joint creases, hirsutism, inguinal hernia, and syndactyly of the 2nd and 3rd toes. This report also highlights the critical role of periodic re-analysis of previously uninformative exome cases.

Introduction

The distal arthrogryposes (DAs) are a group of monogenic conditions characterized by overlapping phenotypic and genotypic features and a similar pathogenesis. They are characterized by nonprogressive congenital contractures of the limbs, most commonly affecting the hands, wrists, feet, and ankles. Other features include contractures of the face, ocular muscles, webbed neck, pterygia, short stature, and scoliosis [1]. Pathogenic variants are identified in only about 60% of families diagnosed with DA, and the precise genetic etiology remains unknown in the rest [1, 2]. The most common DAs are DA1 (OMIM #108120), DA2A (OMIM #193700), and DA2B (OMIM #601680) [1]. The majority of known pathogenic variants occur in just five genes: TPM2, TNNI2, TNNT3, MYH3, and MYH8 [1, 3]. Genes encoding sarcomere proteins, which are the fundamental contractile structures of muscle, have long been considered priority candidates for DA. MYL11 (previously known as MYLPF) encodes a fast phosphorylated skeletal muscle-specific regulatory myosin light chain, which helps in efficient actin-myosin interaction, maximizing contractile force and stabilizing the myosin lever arm. Variants in MYL11 cause both autosomal dominant (inherited and de novo) (AD) and autosomal recessive (AR) DA [1]. Here, we report the diagnostic journey and phenotypic evolution of a male with a homozygous NC_000016.10:g.30377860G>T; MYL11 (NM_013292.5):c.470G>T; p.(Cys157Phe) variant presenting with features of MYL11-related disorder. We compare his clinical presentation with that described by Chong et al. 2020 to further delineate the phenotype of MYL11-related disorders.

Case Presentation

This case report was prepared in accordance with the CARE reporting guidelines. The completed CARE checklist is provided as online supplementary material (Supplementary File 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/msy/adrag013). The proband, a 3-year-old male, was born to 5th-degree consanguineous healthy parents of North Indian descent. He has one elder brother with cryptorchidism and micropenis, who was diagnosed with a 46, XY disorder of sexual development. There was no family history of contractures or neuromuscular disease (Fig. 1e). Antenatal scans showed polyhydramnios in the 3rd trimester. The child was born at 37 weeks by lower segment caesarean section due to breech presentation, meconium-stained liquor, and fetal distress. He had a birth weight of 3.25 kg (Z score −0.20) and contractures of wrists, ankles, and digits at birth. Developmental milestones in all domains were age-appropriate. He underwent surgery for a left-sided inguinal hernia at 2 years of age. At 3 years, weight was 10.8 kg (Z score −2.37), height was 87 cm (Z score −2.45), and head circumference was 46 cm (Z score −2.44). On examination, the child had hooded upper eyelids, prominent nasolabial folds, bulbous nasal tip, small nostrils, long smooth philtrum, thin upper lip, anteriorly rotated ears, low anterior and posterior hairline with abnormal hair whorls, high arched palate, hirsutism with a hypopigmented macule on the back, and drooping shoulders. He also had restricted mouth opening and a grimacing facial appearance. His hands and feet were small, and flat feet were also noted. Restricted neck mobility, restricted movement of both ankle and wrist joints, flexion deformity of all digits, adducted thumbs with webbing, faint palmar creases and dermatoglyphics, and overriding of the second and third toes of both feet were observed (Fig. 1). Parental account and review of serial photographs determined that these joint contractures were nonprogressive. Movements across the shoulder and pelvic girdles, elbows, and knees were normal. He did not have facial weakness, ptosis, spinal deformities, or oral clefts. However, he had a nasal intonation of voice, frequent nasal block, and noisy breathing. Other systemic examination was within normal limits. On follow-up evaluation at 7 years, 9 years, and 13 years, he continued along the same growth pattern with a height between −2 and −3 Z score (online suppl. Fig. 1). His hand and finger deformities remained static with minimal improvement despite physiotherapy. Deviation of the nasal tip toward the right was observed at 2 years of age and worsened. He performed well at school but had some difficulty with oral recitations due to speech clarity issues. He underwent surgery for first web space correction in both hands with a transposition flap from the radial side of the index finger at 9 years of age. Following this, he could use his hands for wider skills and could write better. Septorhinoplasty for the deviated nasal septum was undertaken at 14 years of age. At the last follow-up at 15 years of age, he was in the ninth standard at school and also played in the football team. He had a height of 153 cm (−2.05 Z score), suggestive of mild short stature, and a weight of 40 kg (−1.31 Z score). He had normal pubertal development for age (Tanner stage 4). His facial features and joint contractures were almost the same (Fig. 1). His hemogram, liver function tests, renal function tests, and two-dimensional echocardiography were unremarkable.

He had a normal male karyotype (46, XY). Whole exome sequencing (WES) of the proband was done in 2017 and failed to detect any candidate variants. On re-analysis of the exome in 2024, we identified a homozygous variant in the MYL11 gene, NC_000016.10:g.30377860G>T; MYL11(NM_013292.5):c.470G>T; p.(Cys157Phe) (Fig. 1i). There were no other candidate variants in genes related to the phenotype. This variant is located in the calcium-binding domain of the MYL11 protein. This variant is present in gnomAD v4.1.0 with a low frequency (0.0046%) and no homozygotes, and is absent in the 1000Genomes database and Indian data of 800 exomes. The variant is predicted to be deleterious by various computational mutation prediction databases like MutationTaster, SIFT, DANN, AlphaMissense (0.9), and Revel (0.7). This variant has been submitted twice to ClinVar as likely pathogenic/pathogenic (SCV001335433 and SCV002499438) and was reported by Chong et al. [1] in 2020 in seven patients with distal arthrogryposis. This variant is classified as pathogenic according to ACMG-AMP criteria (PS4, PS3, PM2, PP3) [4].

Discussion

DA is genetically heterogeneous, and around 13 genes have been established as causative. It is classified into 12 different phenotypic subtypes, DA1 to DA12, and there is significant overlap amongst these, especially between DA1 and DA2. Delineation of the subtype requires molecular diagnosis. DA2A/DA2B are typically more severe disorders with mild to moderate facial contractures and greater overall clinical severity as compared to DA1, which presents with distal contractures and the absence of facial contractures. Moreover, calcaneovalgus deformities appear to be more common in DA2B. The causative genes of DA1 and DA2 include TPM2, MYBPC1, MYH3, TNNI2, TNNT3, and MYL11 [3, 5]. Most of these genes encode sarcomere components of skeletal muscle. The sarcomere has a myosin heavy chain and 2 light chains. MYL11 is the fast type of skeletal muscle regulatory light chain, which primarily stabilizes the myosin heavy chain, whereas MYH3 is the principal motor protein. MYL11-associated DA1C was first described by Chong et al. [1] in 2020 in eight families with congenital contractures, scoliosis, and short stature. No other publications on this disorder are available to date. Short stature and proximal joint contractures involving the elbows, hips, and knees are more common in patients with DA1C as compared to DA1 due to variants in other genes. DA1C can be transmitted as either an AD or AR condition. The phenotypes of AD and AR forms are indistinguishable, and heterozygous carriers of the AR form do not have any clinical manifestations [1].

The reported individuals with DA1C have a very wide age range, from fetus to 69 years. Intrafamilial variability was also observed. The most common reported findings of DA1C are short stature, scoliosis, and distal joint contractures. Other less common findings were cryptorchidism, short neck, adducted thumbs, flexed metacarpophalangeal joints, and blepharophimosis. One of the previously reported cases also had MYL11-related segmental amyoplasia of the foot [1]. As our case had marked craniofacial abnormalities (prominent nasolabial folds, bulbous nasal tip, small nostrils, long smooth philtrum, thin upper lip, restricted mouth opening, and a grimacing facial appearance) with distal arthrogryposis and short stature, he was given a provisional diagnosis of Freeman-Sheldon syndrome, DA2A, before molecular confirmation [3].

We provide his growth trajectory and the evolution of clinical features over time, starting from infancy to 15 years of age. Our case expands the spectrum of MYL11-related disorders presenting with a phenotype that resembles DA2 rather than DA1 as described in earlier cases. Thus, this study reinforces the clinical and molecular overlap between DA1 and DA2 and documents the natural history of DA1C from birth to adolescence in this patient. DA is a nonprogressive disorder despite many causative genes disrupting skeletal muscle function [1]. Therapy consists of multidisciplinary management with physical therapy and surgical procedures for contractures being the mainstay [2]. There are only a few individuals reported worldwide with DA1C, and the available reports suggest considerable variability in disease severity despite involvement of the same gene. Myosin extracted from mylpfa-deficient zebrafish moved actin filaments more slowly than wild-type myosin extracts, and zebrafish mylpfa mutant muscle fibers initially formed normally but subsequently degenerated in all muscles. Muscle fibers in myotomes also become irregularly shaped over time and have reduced trunk contractile force in mylpfa knockouts. These findings suggest a pathogenic mechanism where reduction of lever-arm function may impair force transmission and sarcomere function, rather than alter calcium sensing, thereby contributing to the development of congenital contractures [1, 6]. Recent functional evidence indicates that MYL11 is a dose-sensitive regulator of fast-twitch sarcomere assembly and is needed for sarcomere formation and organization of the contractile apparatus. Impaired MYL11 function can cause DA through defective myosin localization and defective sarcomere formation [6]. Mouse knockouts of Mylpf have no skeletal muscle and die soon after birth due to respiratory failure [1].

MYL11 (NC_000016.10) (NM_013292.5) has seven exons, of which six are coding. The MYL11(NM_013292.5):c.470G>T; p.(Cys157Phe) variant lies in the last exon and was reported previously in three unrelated families, including one from South India. There was no shared haplotype amongst these families, suggesting a hotspot variation. Another amino acid change affecting the same codon (Cys157Arg) in an additional three families also supports this explanation [1, 6]. MYL11 protein consists of an N-terminal region and two EF-hand domains (calcium-binding motifs) near the C-terminus. The variants p.Cys157Phe, p.Cys157Arg, and p.Gly163Ser are located in the second EF-hand domain, and the variant p.Ala33Val is located in the N-terminal region outside the calcium-binding domains. Although these pathogenic variants are located in the second EF-hand (calcium-binding) domain, the disease mechanism is not due to defective calcium binding, as the calcium-binding ability of vertebrate skeletal muscle regulatory light chains is largely lost. In silico structural modeling of the previously reported MYL11 variants predicted potential alterations in protein structure and/or conformation. Substitution of the internal Cys157 residue may alter local protein conformation and stability and may influence affinity between MYL11 and myosin heavy chain (MyHC). The amino acid change p.Ala33Val may disrupt the MYL11–MyHC interface and cause dominant-negative effects and impaired myosin interaction. However, the exact functional effect of these variants has not been experimentally evaluated in zebrafish or other functional models [1]. The variant p.Gly163Ser was unable to rescue the mylpfa mutant in zebrafish models, which shows experimental evidence consistent with haploinsufficiency as the probable underlying molecular mechanism in this dominant form of DA1C [6]. The phenotypic heterogeneity in DA1C may be due to various factors like variants in other modifier genes (sarcomeric or excitation-contraction coupling genes) [1]. The variable expression of developmental and adult myosin light-chain isoforms may lead to varying degrees of functional compensation between individuals. Other factors like epigenetic regulation, environmental influences, physical activity, and muscle remodeling may contribute to intra- and interfamilial variability [6, 7].

This study describes the course of MYL11-related distal arthrogryposis in an Indian male from infancy to adolescence, highlighting the clinical resemblance to DA2A. This report also underscores the critical role of periodic re-analysis of previously uninformative exome cases.
