Career Pathways and Employment Experiences of Certified Nurse‐Midwives Compared With Other Advanced Practice Registered Nurses in the United States
Authors: Paula M. Kett, Kyla F. Woodward, Shahida Shahrir, Selina A. Mohammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDespite recent growth in the certified nurse‐midwife (CNM) workforce and data showing better perinatal outcomes associated with their care, midwives continue to be underused in the United States. To facilitate targeted policies for integrating and sustaining this workforce, information is needed regarding the career pathways and employment experiences of CNMs.MethodsIn this longitudinal study, we used the 2018 and 2022 National Sample Survey of Registered Nurses to examine how CNM demographics, career pathways, and job experiences changed over time and in comparison with other advanced practice registered nurses (APRNs). We used multivariable logistic regression to examine the odds of education, financing, and employment characteristics among CNMs compared with other APRNs, then used regression output to calculate predicted probabilities of each characteristic among CNMs compared with other APRNs.ResultsThe size and racial diversity of the CNM workforce changed over time. CNMs had a 5.4% higher probability of working as a community health worker and a 9.5% higher probability of taking out a loan to finance their education than other APRNs. CNMs’ predicted mean annual salaries were up to $25,000 lower than other APRNs, and CNMs had up to a 6.3% higher probability of $60,000 or more in remaining debt compared with the other APRN groups. Finally, compared with other APRNs, CNMs were up to 15.0% less likely to report practicing to the full extent of their license and their education and training, respectively.DiscussionAlthough there was change in the size and diversity of the workforce, CNMs’ financial and employment situations may contribute to persistent challenges in entering and maintaining practice. Future research should explore additional factors related to CNM turnover and retention. Workforce policies should target barriers to practice and support more competitive salaries for CNMs.

INTRODUCTION

Research has demonstrated an association between midwifery‐led care (ie, care provided by a midwife) and improved birth outcomes, including lower odds of low birth weight (LBW) births and cesarean births among people with low‐risk pregnancy as well as fewer obstetric interventions. 1 , 2 , 3 Midwifery‐led care, when compared with perinatal care provided by others (such as obstetrician‐gynecologists), is also associated with reduced disparities in birth outcomes, including reduced Black‐White disparities in LBW rates and lower odds of preterm and LBW births among people who are pregnant with low socioeconomic status. 4 , 5

Certified nurse‐midwives (CNMs), practitioners who provide prenatal, birth, and postpartum care as well as other reproductive and gynecological services, are one type of midwife, among others, who provides midwifery‐led care. Attention and resources have focused on increasing the size and diversity of this workforce because of the known benefits of midwifery‐led care, with approximately 34 million dollars in federal funding provided to 56 grantees across the United States in 2022. 6 These programs have likely contributed to the increasing number of new CNMs annually along with a high rate (85%) of eligible recertifications each year. 7

Despite the growth of the CNM workforce, there is limited use of midwifery‐led care in the United States. In other high‐income countries, midwifery‐led care is viewed as the standard for perinatal care, with additional specialties providing care based on patient risk. 8 These countries have better maternal, infant, and newborn health outcomes than the United States, including lower rates of maternal mortality, LBW, and infant mortality. 9 In contrast, physicians predominantly provide perinatal care in the United States, with midwives attending only an estimated 11.6% of births. 2 , 8 , 10 Along with the lack of normalization of midwifery‐led care, the limited use of midwives in the United States may be due to workforce barriers such as state‐level restricted scope of practice and high rates of burnout, which, in turn, may decrease the availability of this workforce. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 QUICK POINTS ✦There is low use of midwives in the United States despite recent growth in the certified nurse‐midwife (CNM) workforce.✦CNMs, along with other advanced practice registered nurses, experience similar barriers to practice, such as restrictions to scope of practice, but CNMs seem to experience these at higher rates.✦Policy that targets these barriers for CNMs is needed to better integrate and sustain this workforce as part of assuring access to high‐quality perinatal care.

Although some existing research has examined the impacts of practice barriers on CNMs, more information is needed about CNM workforce entrants and the barriers they face when pursuing this career as well as how features of their career entry may affect ongoing employment experiences. For example, financial barriers may impact workforce entry, with some midwives beginning their careers with up to $180,000 in student loan debt. 17 Others may need to identify additional sources of financial support because of recent changes to federal student loan programs that limit the amount of borrowing necessary to complete a degree. 17 Years of nursing experience prior to entry into the CNM workforce may be another factor because those with less experience may face barriers to practicing at the top of scope or in obtaining a competitive salary. 18

Beyond gaining a more systematic understanding of the entry and employment experiences of the CNM workforce, more data are needed on how career pathways and employment experiences of CNMs compare with other advanced practice registered nurses (APRNs). Along with CNMs, APRNs include nurse practitioners (NPs), clinical nurse specialists, and certified registered nurse anesthetists, each of whom may practice in a range of settings (eg, hospital, outpatient clinic) and specialties (eg, primary care, pediatrics). Research has shown that other APRN occupations face similar barriers to practice as CNMs, including restrictive state regulatory environments, hospital bylaws reducing scope of practice, and issues with provider credentialing or insurance reimbursement, although more CNMs report experiencing barriers such as insurance reimbursement. 19 , 20 Amid similar workforce barriers, it is important to understand whether and how CNMs’ career pathways and employment experiences may differ from other APRNs as part of guiding policy for CNMs and APRNs overall (Table S2).

To address these gaps in knowledge, this study examines the career pathways and job experiences of CNMs using data from the National Sample Survey of Registered Nurses (NSSRN). Specifically, this study explores how CNM sociodemographics, career pathways, and job experiences changed over time and compare with those of other APRNs, including those providing women's health care. Findings provide greater insight into the workforce‐related dynamics shaping the midwifery workforce, including the barriers and resources for entry to practice.

METHODS

Study Design and Data

For this longitudinal study, we analyzed publicly available data from the 2018 and 2022 NSSRN datasets. The NSSRN is a national survey of actively licensed registered nurses (RNs) and APRNs in the United States conducted every 4 years by the Health Resources and Services Administration as part of tracking the US nursing workforce. 21 Survey questions focus on respondents’ demographic characteristics, education, training, and work experiences. During survey sampling, RN licenses were stratified as either advanced practice license (APRNs) or all other RN licenses, with sampling rates set to maximize reliability of estimates across the United States and within each state. More details on the survey can be found elsewhere. 21 There were approximately 50,000 RN and APRN responses in each survey. We used the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist to report on this study (see Supporting Information: Table S1). 22

Variables

The sample included APRNs who were employed as of December 31 in the year prior to the survey. The main independent variable was type of APRN, with APRNs categorized into 3 groups: CNMs, women's health APRNs, and other APRNs. Women's health APRNs were defined as any APRN who had a women's health certification or a specialty in obstetrics, gynecology, or labor and delivery but was not a CNM. We chose to separate out women's health APRNs from other APRNs to identify any potential similarities among groups providing care to women. Because this survey included only nurses, certified midwives, who have the same education, certification, and scope of practice as CNMs, are not included. 23

Outcomes

Career Pathways

Career pathway variables included education, health jobs prior to nursing, financing of the initial nursing degree, and remaining debt. Educational variables include respondents having a postsecondary degree prior to their first nursing degree (yes/no), type of first nursing degree (master's or doctoral degree, bachelor's degree, and less than a bachelor's degree), whether their first nursing program was located in the United States, and whether they obtained a degree in a field other than nursing at any point in their career. We examined the number and type of health‐related jobs prior to a respondent's first nursing degree, including work as a community health worker (CHW), nursing or home health aide, licensed practical or vocational nurse, or other (eg, paramedic, medical assistant, dental assistant).

Financing variables included types of financing for initial nursing education (ie, loans, money borrowed from friends or family with the expectation of paying it back, scholarships, grants, and employer tuition plans). We created a binary variable indicating whether the respondent used any type of loan to finance their first nursing degree versus all other sources. We also included a variable indicating whether a respondent had borrowed any money to complete a nursing degree (yes/no). Among respondents who indicated that they borrowed money, we created a variable indicating the amount of respondents’ remaining debt, using the following categories: no debt, $1 to $40,000, $40,001 to $60,000, $60,001 to $80,000, and $80,001 or more.

Employment Experiences

Employment variables examined included years in nursing since first RN license and years since highest nursing degree (both continuous and categorical [0‐5, 6‐10, 11‐20, 21‐30, and 31+ years]), length of time at current primary position (<1 year, 1‐5 years, >5 years), full‐time or part‐time employment, employment setting (hospital, ambulatory, and other), type of employment (employed through agency, employed through facility/organization, or self‐employed), and types of employer‐provided education benefits (none, loan forgiveness, paid education time, flexible scheduling, tuition reimbursement, or an unspecified other benefit). Pretax annual earnings (continuous) were specified for those employed full‐time.

Additional practice variables included respondent's ability to practice to the full extent of their license (yes/no) or education and training (yes/no) and whether the respondent had hospital admitting privileges (yes/no). For respondents who reported they provided patient care, a binary variable was included reflecting whether physician oversight was required (oversight meaning it is required for the physician to see some or all patients seen by the APRN or that physician sign‐off is required). Several measures available in 2022, such as respondent's ability to practice to the full extent of their license, were not available in 2018 and are therefore not presented in the 2018 analyses.

Covariates

Covariates included demographics and practice location. Demographic variables included sex (available as a binary male or female variable), age (both continuous and categorical [≤29, 30‐39, 40‐49, 50‐64, and ≥65 years]), Hispanic ethnicity (yes/no), and racial identity (Asian, Black, White, and “Other” race). Several racial categories were collapsed (American Indian/Alaskan Native, Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander, multiracial, and an existing “other” race category) into other race because of the low number of observations.

Practice location variables included rural or urban location, practice in a Health Provider Shortage Area (HPSA), and Health and Human Service (HHS) Region, one of 10 regions derived from each respondent's state as defined by the Office of Intergovernmental and External Affairs.

Analysis

Applying individual sample weights provided with the data, descriptive statistics were calculated; t tests and χ 2 analyses were applied when appropriate (significance at P < .05) to examine differences in demographic, career pathway, and employment experience characteristics of CNMs compared with women's health and other APRNs. 21 A series of logistic and linear regression analyses were applied to examine the association between type of APRN and the outcomes of interest. Categorical variables for nursing education and remaining debt were collapsed into binary variables (bachelor's degree or higher vs associate's degree or lower and ≤$60,000 vs ≥$60,001). Outcomes that did not demonstrate notable differences between APRN groups in the descriptive analyses were not included in the regression analyses.

All models were adjusted for age, sex, race, ethnicity, having held a health care–related job prior to becoming a nurse, entry degree into nursing, nursing education location, use of loans, and HHS region, except when a covariate was also the outcome of interest (eg, entry degree into nursing). Replicate weights were included in the regression analyses to account for the complex survey design. We conducted regression analyses separately for 2018 and 2022 because of different variance estimation procedures (jackknife replication in 2018 and successive differences replication in 2022).

Regression model output was then used to calculate predicted probabilities for each binary outcome and predicted means for continuous outcomes by year and type of APRN. We present adjusted odds ratios (aOR) throughout the results relative to outcome topics (ie, career pathways, employment experience) and then describe relevant predicted probabilities at the end to illustrate the magnitude of certain differences. Results from 2022 data analyses are focused on in the written results except when comparison with 2018 is warranted.

RESULTS

The sample consisted of n = 24,747 observations for an estimate of 373,829 working APRNs in 2018 and n = 23,129 observations for an estimated 413,747 in 2022 (weighted estimates used from this point forward). In the full sample representing 2018 and 2022, 2.8% of respondents were CNMs, 5.6% were women's health care APRNs, and the remaining 91.6% were other types of APRNs (data not shown).

Demographics

Table 1 details sociodemographics for each group. In 2022, women made up 98.1% of CNMs compared with 96.3% of women's health care and 82.7% of other APRNs. CNMs’ average age compared with all other types of APRNs was significantly higher at 51.6 years versus 48.6 years (women's health) or 46.8 years (other) in 2022 (P = .01); 2018 was similar, whereas CNMs’ average age was significantly higher than other APRN groups (50.7 years [CNMs], 50.5 years [women's health care], 47.3 years [other APRNs], P = .001). From 2018 to 2022, the average age of CNMs increased by 0.9 years, with more than one‐quarter aged over 65 years in 2022, whereas women's health care and other APRNs’ average age decreased by 1.9 and 0.5 years, respectively. Furthermore, the distribution among age groups shifted, with a 7.0% decrease in CNMs aged over 50 years compared with women's health (13.9% decrease) and other APRNs (3.1% decrease).

Career Pathways

Health‐Related Job

Table 2 depicts each APRN group's prenursing experience, education, and financing. Prior to an initial nursing degree, 64.2% of the 2022 CNM respondents had a health‐related job compared with 68.0% of women's health and 70.4% of other APRNs, although this difference was not significant (P = .38) (Table 2). Overall, compared with other groups, significantly more CNMs worked as a CHW prior to their initial nursing degree (8.8% vs 1.9% of each other group in 2022, P = .02), whereas there was no significant difference in the proportion working as nursing aides (39.6% vs 47.7% women's health and 47.9% other APRNs in 2022, P = .33). Education

In 2022, 68.3% of CNMs reported a bachelor's degree or higher as their initial nursing degree compared with 66.2% of women's health and 64.7% of other APRNs, a nonsignificant difference (68.3% vs 66.2% and 64.7%, respectively; P = .89) (Table 2). Also in 2022, 38.5% of CNMs, 27.7% of women's health, and 31.6% of other APRNs reported earning a nonnursing degree (P = .23). In adjusted models, differences between groups in type of RN entry degree and receipt of a nonnursing academic degree were nonsignificant (Table 3).

Financing

More than half of respondents in 2022 used loans to finance their initial RN education, representing a 14.0 percentage point increase from 2018 for CNMs compared with a 9.6 percentage point increase for women's health and a 4.8 percentage point increase for other APRNs (Table 2). CNMs’ reported rate of borrowing any type of money (including loans, family, and friends) was 75.4% compared with 69.5% of women's health and 70.6% of other APRNs, a nonsignificant difference (P = .38). CNMs more often reported having any remaining debt compared with other groups (2022 only, Table 2; P = .048). Use of loans was significantly different in adjusted models, with CNMs having higher odds of financing their nursing education with loans compared with other APRNs (aOR, 1.57; 95% CI, 1.05‐2.37; Table 3).

Employment Experiences

Years of Experience

Respondents from all 3 groups ranged from early to late career stages (Table 4). In 2022, the CNMs’ average number of years since completing their first RN program was highest at 25.1 years compared with 23.2 years for women's health care APRNs and 20.2 years for other APRNs (P = .002). CNMs also had the greatest average number of years since their highest nursing degree (16.7 years) compared with women's health (12.8 years) or other APRNS (11.1 years) (P < .001). CNMs were less likely (54.5%) than women's health care APRNs (57.3%) and more likely than other APRNS (46.4%) to be in their primary position for more than 5 years and less likely (4.7%) to be in their primary position for less than one year compared with 10.1% of women's health care APRNs and 13.3% of other APRNs (P = .02). There were no significant differences in adjusted models (Table 3).

Job Characteristics

Over 95% of respondents in all 3 APRN categories reported providing patient care as some part of their role in both 2018 and 2022 (Table 3). The proportion of all APRN groups working full‐time increased from 2018 to 2022, with CNMs and other APRNs reporting working full‐time more frequently in 2022 (84.4% and 84.6%, respectively) than women's health APRNs (73.9%) (P < .001). CNMs were more often employed through an organization (91.1% compared with 90.2% of women's health care APRNs and 87.2% of other APRNs, P = .005).

In 2022, mean annual earnings for full‐time CNMs ($98,972) were lower than earnings for women's health ($113,661) or other APRNs ($124,496) (P < .001; Table 3). Regression analyses showed a significant negative association between CNMs and other APRNs (β = −24,385; 95% CI, −38,314 to −10,455). The proportion of CNMs having loan forgiveness available through an employer was not significantly different among CNMs (4.6%) compared with other APRNs (6.6%; P = .52); this difference was also nonsignificant in adjusted models (aOR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.30‐1.22).

The proportion of CNMs reporting being able to practice to the full extent of their license (79.1%) was not significantly different from women's health care (91.5%) and other APRNS (88.5%) (P = .52) (Table 3). The proportion of CNMs reporting the ability to practice to the full extent of their education and training was 75.4% in 2018 and 73.8% in 2022 compared with women's health care (80.3% and 88.3%, respectively) and other APRNs (76.1% and 84.2%, respectively). Differences between groups were not significant in 2018 (P = .53) nor in 2022 (P = .06). A not significantly different percentage of CNMs reported having no required physician oversight of their patients (78.2%) than women's health (72.4%) or other APRNs (71.1%) (P = .24). CNMs more often reported having hospital admission privileges (P < .001); however, the proportion of CNMs reporting such privileges changed from 2018 (54.3%) to 2022 (29.4%). The odds of CNMs having hospital admitting privileges were significantly higher than other APRNs in 2022 regression analyses (aOR, 2.10; 95% CI, 1.55‐2.84), although the aOR was larger in 2018 (aOR, 5.21; 95% CI, 3.48‐7.80; Table 4).

Job Location

Significantly fewer CNMs reported working in rural areas (6.9% compared with 8.0% women's health and 11.4% of other APRNs, P = .01), but the percentage working in HPSAs was more similar across groups (11.7% for CNMs, 11.4% for women's health, and 13.3% for other APRNs, P = .6; Table 3).

Finally, CNMs had differing practice experiences as well. Compared with women's health and other APRNs, CNMs were 13.2% and 11.3% less likely to report practicing to the full extent of their license, 14.7% and 11.9% less likely to report being able to practice to the full extent of their education, and 9.9% and 13.0% more likely to report having hospital admission privileges.

DISCUSSION

Findings demonstrate several ways the CNM workforce is distinct from other APRNs with respect to their sociodemographics, career pathways, educational financing, and job experiences. Although there was change in the estimated size and racial composition of CNM respondents in 2018 compared with 2022, CNMs’ financial and employment situations may contribute to ongoing challenges in entering and maintaining practice.

Pathways Into CNM Careers

The racial composition of the CNM workforce changed over time, with 34.9% identifying as Asian, Black, or other race in 2022, and 7% identifying as such in 2018. These changes may signal some positive effects of federal programs focused on increasing recruitment, support, and retention for CNMs from different backgrounds. 6 CNM entrants were also more likely to be CHWs prior to nursing, which could indicate experience in community‐based perinatal health care, echoing previous research in which CNMs reported working as doulas prior to entering a midwifery career. 24 CHW experience could also be connected to CNMs’ longer tenure in their roles and may provide some insight as to where to focus for recruitment. CHWs potentially have a better understanding of the demands and expectations of midwifery prior to entering the workforce and may be better prepared to balance work and life demands, a frequently cited reason for leaving the CNM profession early. 12 , 25

Job Experiences

Despite a longer interval since their highest nursing degree and longer job tenure as a midwife, 79.1% of CNMs reported being able to practice to the full extent of their education and license compared with 91.5% of women's health care APRNs and 88.5% of other APRNs. The issue of scope of practice restrictions and their impacts is not new; previous research has shown that in states with more restrictive CNM scope of practice (ie, requiring physician supervision or oversight), there may be fewer available CNMs, 5 , 16 , 26 whereas in states with more autonomous scope of practice regulations there are more midwife‐attended births. 27 , 28 CNMs may face further restrictions at the organizational level because hospital bylaws may impose requirements for physician supervision of CNMs regardless of state law. 11 , 13 , 15 , 29 Although practice restrictions are not unique to CNMs, findings here highlight ongoing challenges and the need for targeted solutions that promote autonomy and facilitate retention of CNMs in the workforce. In addition, there is a need for more research related to whether scope of practice restrictions, such as physician supervision requirements or specified physician to CNM ratios, may act as a barrier for entry to the CNM workforce. 30

Financing, Debt, and Salary

Beyond facing scope of practice restrictions, CNMs were also more likely to have financial impacts related to education and to report a lower average salary than other APRNs in the sample and less likely to report loan forgiveness through an employer. These findings aligned with previous research showing a high proportion of CNMs taking out loans to finance their education. 24 There is mixed evidence regarding salary differences between groups. One study found that CNM salaries can range widely, with those who practice at least partly in hospitals typically earning a higher salary, whereas another study found that CNMs’ average salaries tend to be similar to those of NPs, with current Bureau of Labor Statistics data showing slightly lower average pay for CNMs. 31 , 32 , 33 However, findings here show a range of financial barriers that could pose issues for CNM retention.

Recommendations

Increased access to midwifery‐led care would support better birth outcomes in the United States, and the trend toward a larger and potentially more racially representative workforce indicates progress toward this goal. However, challenges with educational financing, equitable salaries, and practicing at full scope contribute to burnout, job dissatisfaction, and early career exit. Research to better understand these and other factors underlying turnover, including reasons for the greater practice restrictions reported among CNMs, would provide a better foundation for future retention initiatives. 14 , 34 For example, existing research suggests that improving opportunities for career advancement and ensuring effective leadership and a supportive practice environment may help retain CNMs. 12 , 35 State and national policy solutions could include reimbursement parity with physicians, measuring service administration to help identify target areas for improvement, and facilitating integration of midwives into the health system. 36 Other research is needed to investigate why CNMs have more educational debt than other APRN groups, beyond the lower salaries identified here. Furthermore, given that our findings show fewer CNMs working in rural areas or HPSAs compared with other APRNs, these challenges raise special concern for their potential impact on known rural perinatal care shortages. 37 Optimizing regulations, such as supporting independent practice and hospital privileges, providing equitable employment benefits (eg, tuition reimbursement) on par with other APRNs, and targeted recruitment of new CNMs to rural and HPSA areas, should be part of ongoing efforts to increase access to midwifery‐led care across the United States.

Limitations

The NSSRN is not focused on specific populations of APRNs but aims to sample a percentage of all APRNs in a state. As such, the number of CNMs who are sampled may not accurately represent the number of CNMs and their characteristics within a state, and so, we have focused our analysis more broadly on type of geography. Furthermore, because the survey is restricted to nurses, other types of midwives are not included. This secondary analysis uses data from individual survey responses, and some variables are grouped prior to public release, so there may be variation due to both reporting and grouping of values such as those in salary and debt. Finally, because of our focus on comparing CNMs with other ARPNs, we chose not to separate out all types of APRNs but do acknowledge each group is likely varied in its background and employment experiences.

CONCLUSION

Our findings on the career pathways and employment experiences of the CNM workforce show a workforce with unique challenges compared with other APRN groups, including higher debt, lower salaries, and more restricted practice. These findings offer targets for policy and investments that address these challenges as part of ensuring a well‐supported CNM workforce is available to meet the needs of people in different geographies and settings in the United States.
