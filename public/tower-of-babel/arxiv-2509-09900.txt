NISQ Security and Complexity via Simple Classical Reasoning
Alexandru Cojocaru, Juan Garay, Qipeng Liu, Fang Song

Abstract
We give novel lifting theorems for security games in the quantum random oracle model (QROM) in Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) settings such as the hybrid query model, the noisy oracle and the bounded-depth models. We provide, for the first time, a hybrid lifting theorem for hybrid algorithms that can perform both quantum and classical queries, as well as a lifting theorem for quantum algorithms with access to noisy oracles or bounded quantum depth. At the core of our results lies a novel measure-and-reprogram framework, called hybrid coherent measure-and-reprogramming , tailored specifically for hybrid algorithms. Equipped with the lifting theorem, we are able to prove directly NISQ security and complexity results by calculating a single combinatorial quantity, relying solely on classical reasoning. As applications, we derive the first direct product theorems in the average case, in the hybrid setting—i.e., an enabling tool to determine the hybrid hardness of solving multi-instance security games. This allows us to derive in a straightforward manner the NISQ hardness of various security games, such as (i) the non-uniform hardness of salted games, (ii) the hardness of specific cryptographic tasks such as the multiple instance version of one-wayness and collision-resistance, and (iii) uniform or non-uniform hardness of many other games.



1
Introduction

Hash functions are a fundamental workhorse in modern cryptography. Efficient constructions such as SHA-2 and SHA-3 are widely used in real-world cryptographic applications. This is in no small part facilitated by the
random oracle model
(ROM) abstraction
[
BR93
]
, which often enables efficient cryptographic constructions and simple analysis.

The development of quantum computing poses significant threats to cryptography.
Quantum random oracle model
(QROM) has since been proposed
to take into account quantum attackers
[
BDF+11
]
.
The various techniques that have been developed for analyzing security in the QROM, however,
are often either
ad-hoc
(for specific scenarios) or too involved to apply. They include approaches such as polynomial methods
[
BBC+01
]
, adversarial methods
[
Amb02
]
, small-range distributions
[
Zha12
]
, and compressed oracles
[
Zha18
]
.

Achieving post-quantum security often comes with the need for larger security parameters, hence causing an efficiency blow-up. For hash functions in particular, this leads to substantially longer output length and increased running time. On the other hand, the study of
Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum
(NISQ) devices
[
Pre18
]

constitutes a crucial intermediate goal.
In the NISQ model, one focuses on: (i)
hybrid
algorithms, where
parties are granted a quota of both classical and quantum resources, resulting in a model which subsumes the purely classical or purely quantum computing models as special cases;
(ii) quantum algorithms with access to
noisy oracles
, where each quantum query is affected by a dephasing noise; or
(iii)
bounded-depth
quantum algorithms.
These models of computation are also practically motivated as a fully quantum algorithm typically requires running a large-scale quantum computer coherently for an extended amount of time, while in the near-to-intermediate term, the available quantum devices are likely to be computationally restricted as well as expensive
[
Pre18
]
.
Understanding the complexity of various cryptographic tasks against adversaries with both classical and quantum capabilities is not only crucial in near-term cryptography, but also a key step towards advancing our knowledge of quantum computational complexity, and efforts have been made to analyze, in this model, pre-image resistance
[
Ros24
,
CGS23
,
HLS24
]
and collision resistance
[
HLS24
]
. However, while these methods establish tight security for these two cryptographic uses of hash functions, they appear too complex and specific to be
ported to other applications.

1.1
Summary of Our Results

In this work we establish novel lifting theorems for search games in the presence of NISQ adversaries,
specifically,
(i) hybrid algorithms that are equipped with both classical and quantum queries, (ii) quantum algorithms with access to noisy oracles, as well as (iii) quantum algorithms with bounded quantum depth. At the heart of this result is a novel measure-and-reprogram technique, which we refer to as

The lifting theorems allow us to prove quantum and NISQ security for various cryptographic applications by calculating a single combinatorial quantity, relying solely on classical reasoning. Using this new framework, we derive the first

Hybrid Lifting Theorem with Classical Reasoning.

A multi-output
k
-search game between a challenger and an adversary is defined as follows. The adversary receives
k

k

k
-search game.
The goal of the

\displaystyle p(R):=\Pr[\exists\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{k}\ |\ (y_{\pi(1)},y_{\pi(2)},...,y_{\pi(k)})\in R:(y_{1},...,y_{k})\xleftarrow{\$}Y^{k}]\,.

Note that

Theorem 1.6

(Hybrid Lifting Theorem with Classical Reasoning – Informal)
.

For any hybrid algorithm
\mathcal{A}
equipped with
q
quantum and
c

-search game as specified by the winning relation

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins \allowbreak multi-output $k$-search game }]\leq\left(O\left(\frac{q^{2}}{k^{2}}+\frac{c}{k}\right)\right)^{k}\cdot p(R)\,.

This lifting theorem translates into the following NISQ (hybrid and noisy) hardness results for our applications in query complexity and cryptography.

1.2
Related Work

The measure-and-reprogram framework was proposed and subsequently generalized and improved with tighter bounds, in the works of
[
DFM20
,
LZ19
,
DFMS22
]
. A main application of the framework has been in the context of the Fiat-Shamir transformation,
with several works establishing its post-quantum security

[
Cha19
,
DFMS19
,
GHHM21
]
. Other cryptographic applications of measure-and-reprogram have been considered in
[
Kat21
,
BKS21
,
ABKK23
,
JMZ23
,
DFHS23
,
KX24
]
. Applications in query complexity of the framework have been developed in
[
CGLQ20
,
YZ21
]
.

The
coherent
measure-and-reprogram framework for the purely quantum setting was recently proposed in
[
CGLS25
]

as an improvement over the original measure-and-reprogram framework, yielding tighter bounds and implying lifting theorems in the
QROM.

In the hybrid algorithm setting, to our knowledge, previous works have only focused
on establishing hardness results for very specific search problems.
Rosmanis studied
the basic unstructured
search problem in the hybrid query model
[
Ros24
]
in order to find the unique solution of the problem. This hardness bound is also shown in
[
HLS24
]
, by a new recording technique tailored to the hybrid query model.
The work of
[
CGS23
]
generalizes the Rosmanis result
by studying the hybrid hardness for search problems where the input is sampled according to
arbitrary distributions.
Finally, the work of
[
HLS24
]
proves the hardness of the collision problem by their
generalized recording technique.

Comparison with
[
CGLS25
]
.

It is worth contrasting the contributions of our work to a prior work in
[
CGLS25
]
.
First off, instead of the coherent measure-and-reprogram approach
[
CGLS25
]
,
our starting point is the
standard
measure-and-reprogram technique
[
DFMS22
]
.
Indeed, our core idea can be used to make both measure-and-reprogram techniques work in the hybrid and NISQ models. While basing on the coherent version gives tighter results,
without it, we can still get lifting lemmas for hybrid and NISQ algorithms based on
[
DFMS22
]
.

Conceptually, we believe our work
changes how NISQ hybrid and noisy models are understood; for example,
the hybrid regime is not simply a convex combination of classical and quantum behaviors—subtle interference effects can arise when quantum queries are combined with classical ones, which our framework captures rigorously. As such, our result offers a clean and provably sound way to account for these effects in the ROM.

From a technical point of view, our new idea that enables the lifting lemmas for NISQ algorithms is in a sense “orthogonal” to that in
[
CGLS25
]
.
Specifically,
while our main theorem relies on a simulator that is adapted from the simulator in
[
CGLS25
]
,
this adaptation can be applied to the simulator in the standard measure-and-reprogram lemma
[
DFMS22
]
,
which enables us to get a similar theorem, albeit with a worse loss compared to our main result.
We choose to base our main result on
[
CGLS25
]
in order to obtain the tightest parameter values, but we emphasize that the underlying technique is independent of the specific measure-and-reprogram variant being used.

From an applications point of view, deriving NISQ security even for standard problems such as pre-image finding and collision-finding has been extremely challenging;
we believe that with our newly proposed framework one can derive in a straightforward, systematic and modular manner several NISQ security and complexity results.

\ket{\phi_{q}^{H}}
is the final state of
\mathcal{A}
.

Without loss of generality, the algorithm will have three registers
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z}
at the end of the computation, where
\mathcal{X}
consists of a list of inputs,
\mathcal{Y}
consists of a list of outputs corresponding to these inputs and some auxiliary information in
\mathcal{Z}
.

Hybrid Algorithms.

We also consider hybrid query algorithms in this work. A hybrid query algorithm can make both quantum queries (as defined above) and classical queries to a random oracle, in an adaptive manner. We use
q
to denote the number of quantum queries and
c
to denote the number of classical queries.

To make a classical query, an algorithm first measures its input register to obtain a classical input
x
. It then forwards
x
to the classical interface of the oracle
H
, receiving the pair
(x,H(x))
in return. For the remainder of this work, we will interpret a classical query in this equivalent manner:

•

Let
\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{x}
be a state in the input register. Instead of measuring and querying it through the classical interface, the algorithm first makes a quantum query on
\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{x}\otimes\ket{0}
to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{x,H(x)}.

•

It prepares an empty register
{\cal H}
(short for “history register”) and CNOT both input and output into the register:

\displaystyle\sum_{x}\alpha_{x}\ket{x,H(x)}\ket{x,H(x)}_{\cal H}.

The algorithm will then never touch
{\cal H}
.

The interpretation above serves as a purification of the classical query, eliminating the need for measurement. By adopting this perspective, we can assume that no measurements are made until the conclusion of the algorithm, ensuring that all internal states (e.g.,
\ket{\phi_{i}^{H}}
) remain pure throughout.

More formally, we will consider a new register — the
history
register — denoted by
\mathcal{H}
. The idea is that each classical query
x
to the oracle
H
will be appended at the end of the history register.
A classical oracle query to an oracle
H
will behave as applying the unitary
O_{H}^{{C}}
:

O_{H}^{{C}}\ket{x}_{\mathcal{X}}\ket{y}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes{\ket{C}_{\mathcal{H}}}\longrightarrow\ket{x}_{\mathcal{X}}\ket{y}_{\mathcal{Y}}\otimes{\ket{C\,||\,(x,H(x))}_{\mathcal{H}}},

Here, “
L\,||\,e
” denotes appending an element
e
to the end of the list
L
.

In the rest of the work, we will assume hybrid algorithms never make classical queries to the same input twice (
no duplicated classical queries
); since it can always store the input to the history register. Whenever it queries the same input classically, it will fetch the output from the history register and make a dummy query.

Reprogramming Oracles.

Definition 2.1

(Reprogrammed Oracle)
.

Reprogram oracle
H
to output
y
on input
x
, results in the new oracle, defined as:

H_{x,y}(z)=\begin{cases}y,&\text{ if }z=x\\
H(z),&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We can similarly define a multi-input reprogram oracle
H_{\vec{x},\vec{y}}
for
\vec{x}\in X^{k}
without duplicate entries and
\vec{y}\in Y^{k}
:

H_{\vec{x},\vec{y}}(z)=\begin{cases}y_{i},&\text{ if }z=x_{i}\\
H(z),&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

2.2
The Quantum Measure-and-Reprogram Experiment

We recall the measure-and-reprogram experiment and the state-of-the-art results here, first proposed by
[
DFM20
]
and later adapted by
[
YZ21
]
.

Definition 2.2

(Measure-and-Reprogram Experiment)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a
q
-quantum query algorithm that outputs
\vec{x}\in X^{k}
and
z\in Z
. For a function
H:X\rightarrow Y
and
\vec{y}=(y_{1},...,y_{k})\in Y^{k}
, define a measure-and-reprogram algorithm
\mathcal{B}[H,\vec{y}]
:

1.

For each
j\in[k]
, uniformly pick
(i_{j},b_{j})\in([q]\times\{0,1\})\cup\{(\perp,\perp)\}
such that there does not exist
j\neq j^{\prime}
such that
i_{j}=i_{j^{\prime}}\neq\perp
;

2.

Run
\mathcal{A}^{O}
where the oracle
O
is initialized to be a quantumly accessible classical oracle that computes
H
and when
\mathcal{A}
makes its
i
-th query, the oracle is simulated as follows:

(a)

If
i=i_{j}
for some
j\in[k]
, measure
\mathcal{A}
’s query register to obtain
x_{j}^{\prime}
and do either of the following:

i.

If
b_{j}=0
, reprogram
O
using
(x_{j}^{\prime},y_{j})
and answer
\mathcal{A}
’s
i_{j}
-th query using the reprogrammed oracle;

ii.

If
b_{j}=1
, answer
\mathcal{A}
’s
i_{j}
-th query using the oracle before reprogramming and then reprogram
O
using
(x_{j}^{\prime},y_{j})
;

(b)

Else, answer
\mathcal{A}
’s
i
-th query by just using the oracle
O
without any measurement or reprogramming;

3.

Let
(\vec{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{k}),z)
be
\mathcal{A}
’s output;

4.

For all
j\in[k]
such that
i_{j}=\perp
, set
x_{j}^{\prime}=x_{j}

5.

Output
\vec{x^{\prime}}:=((x_{1}^{\prime},...,x_{k}^{\prime}),z)

We next state the state-of-the-art quantum measure-and-reprogram result.

Lemma 2.3

(Quantum Measure-and-Reprogram (adaptation from
[
DFM20
,
YZ21
]
))
.

For any
H:X\rightarrow Y
, for any
\vec{x}^{*}=(x_{1}^{*},...,x_{k}^{*})\in X^{k}
without duplicated entries, for all
\vec{y}=(y_{1},...,y_{k})
and any relation
R\subseteq X^{k}\times Y^{k}\times Z
, we have:

\begin{split}\Pr&[\vec{x}^{\prime}=\vec{x}^{*}\wedge(\vec{x}^{\prime},\vec{y},z)\in R\ |\ (\vec{x}^{\prime},z)\leftarrow\mathcal{B}[H,y]]\\
&\geq\frac{1}{(2q+1)^{2k}}\Pr[\vec{x}=\vec{x}^{*}\wedge(\vec{x},\vec{y},z)\in R\ |\ (\vec{x},z)\leftarrow\mathcal{A}^{H_{\vec{x}^{*},\vec{y}}}]\end{split}

where
\mathcal{B}[H,y]
is the measure-and-reprogram experiment and where the probabilities are taken over the randomness of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
,
respectively.

2.3
Predicates and Success Probabilities

Definition 2.4

(Predicate/Verification Projection/Symmetric Predicate)
.

Let
R
be a relation on
X^{k}\times Y^{k}\times Z
. A predicate
V^{H}(\vec{x},\vec{y},z)
parameterized by a random oracle
H
, returns
1
if and only if
(\vec{x},\vec{y},z)\in R
and
H(x_{i})=y_{i}
for every
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
.

Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{Z}
be the registers that store
\vec{x},\vec{y},z
, respectively.
We define
\Pi^{H}_{V}
as the projection corresponding to
V^{H}
:

\Pi^{H}_{V}\ket{\vec{x},\vec{y},z}=\begin{cases}\ket{\vec{x},\vec{y},z}&\text{ if }V^{H}(\vec{x},\vec{y},z)=1;\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}.

Finally, for any predicate
V^{H}
, we are able to establish the success probability using the projection
\Pi^{H}_{V}
.

Definition 2.5

(Success Probability)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
a quantum/hybrid query algorithm. Its success probability of outputting
\vec{x},\vec{y},z
such that

V^{H}(\vec{x},\vec{y},z)=1

is defined as

\Pr\left[\mathcal{A}^{H}\rightarrow(\vec{x},\vec{y},z)\text{ and }V^{H}(\vec{x},\vec{y},z)=1\right]=\left\lVert\Pi^{H}_{V}\ket{\phi_{q}^{H}}\right\rVert^{2},

where the probability is taken over the random oracle
H
and over the randomness of
\mathcal{A}

and where we recall that
\ket{\phi_{q}^{H}}
refers to the final state of
\mathcal{A}
.

Sometimes, we care about the event that
\mathcal{A}
outputs a particular
\vec{x}
and still succeeds.
For any
\vec{x}_{\sf o}
, the following probability denotes that
\mathcal{A}
outputs
\vec{x}\equiv\vec{x}_{\sf o}
and succeeds:

\Pr\left[\mathcal{A}^{H}\rightarrow(\vec{x},\vec{y},z),\ \,\ \vec{x}\equiv\vec{x}_{\sf o}\,\text{ and }V^{H}(\vec{x},\vec{y},{z})=1\right]=\left\lVert G_{\vec{x}_{\sf o}}\Pi^{H}_{V}\ket{\phi_{q}^{H}}\right\rVert^{2},

where
G_{\vec{x}_{\sf o}}
is defined as the projection that checks whether
\mathcal{A}
outputs
\vec{x}\equiv\vec{x}_{\sf o}
.

3
Hybrid Coherent Measure-and-Reprogram

In this section, we extend the
recently proposed framework
[
CGLS25
]
to the hybrid setting, namely,
the
hybrid coherent measure-and-reprogram
framework that handles hybrid algorithms performing both classical and quantum queries. We will assume that the query pattern (i.e., whether each query is classical or quantum) has already been determined; that is, the algorithm cannot adaptively choose whether the next query will be
quantum or classical.
We will call such algorithms
static algorithms
. In the next section, we recall a theorem from the literature showing that both are equivalent (up to a constant factor in the number of queries).

3.1
Main Theorem

Theorem 3.1

(Hybrid Coherent Measure-and-Reprogram)
.

Let
H,G:X\to Y
be two functions.
Let
k
be a positive integer (which can be a computable function in both
n=\log{|X|}
and
m=\log{|Y|}
). Let
V^{H}
be any predicate defined over
X^{k}\times Y^{k}\times Z
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be any static hybrid
q
-quantum and
c
-classical query algorithm to the oracle
H
.
Then there exists a black-box polynomial-time quantum algorithm
{\sf Sim}^{H,G,\mathcal{A}}
, satisfying the properties below.

For any
\vec{x}_{\sf o}\in X^{k}
without duplicate entries and
\vec{y}_{\sf o}=G(\vec{x}_{\sf o})
, we have,

\displaystyle\Pr\left[{\sf Sim}^{H,G,\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow(\vec{x},\vec{y},z)\text{ and }\vec{x}\equiv\vec{x}_{\sf o}\text{ and }V^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}(\vec{x},\vec{y},z)=1\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{2^{2k}\cdot}k\cdot A_{k,q,c}}\cdot\Pr\left[\mathcal{A}^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}\rightarrow(\vec{x},z)\text{ and }\vec{x}\equiv\vec{x}_{\sf o}\text{ and }V^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}(\vec{x},H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}(\vec{x}),z)=1\right],

where:

\displaystyle A_{k,q,c}=\sum_{t=0}^{k}{q\choose t}^{2}\cdot{k\choose t}\cdot{c\choose{k-t}}.

Alternatively, the bound can be simplified as follows:

\displaystyle\Pr\left[{\sf Sim}^{H,G,\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow(\vec{x},\vec{y},z)\text{ and }\vec{x}\equiv\vec{x}_{\sf o}\text{ and }V^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}(\vec{x},\vec{y},z)=1\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left(O\left(\frac{q^{2}}{k^{2}}+\frac{c}{k}\right)\right)^{k}}\cdot\Pr\left[\mathcal{A}^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}\rightarrow(\vec{x},z)\text{ and }\vec{x}\equiv\vec{x}_{\sf o}\text{ and }V^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}(\vec{x},H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}(\vec{x}),z)=1\right],

Furthermore,
{\sf Sim}
makes exactly
k
(quantum+classical) queries to
G
.

Remark 3.2
.

By relying on the result
by Don et al.
[
DFH22
]
, the
theorem can be directly extended to any general hybrid algorithm in which the order of the classical and quantum queries can be adaptive (and can depend on the underlying oracle), at the cost of only a constant factor (specifically,
increasing the number of classical and quantum queries by a factor of 2).

To show Theorem
3.1
, we will construct the simulator
{\sf Sim}^{H,G,\mathcal{A}}
similar to the simulator in the purely quantum coherent measure and reprogram in
[
CGLS25
]
.
There is only one difference:

•

Let
\vec{v}
denote the indices of the queries to be reprogrammed.
Instead of picking them
uniformly at random, we will pick them according to a new distribution
a_{t}
, which we will specify
later. Our choice of
a_{t}
leads to tight bounds for problems like function inversion using hybrid queries.

Next, we formally state our hybrid experiment/simulator.

Definition 3.3

(Hybrid Coherent Measure-and-Reprogram Experiment)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a static hybrid algorithm equipped with

q
quantum queries and
c+k
classical queries that outputs
\vec{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in X^{k},\vec{y}\in Y^{k}
and
z\in Z
.
We assume
\vec{y}
is computed by
H(\vec{x})
, as the last
k
queries.

For a function
H:X\rightarrow Y
and
G:X\rightarrow Y
, define a measure-and-reprogram algorithm
\mathcal{B}[H,G]
as follows:

1.

Let
t
represent the total number of quantum queries that are going to be reprogrammed out of the total
k
number of reprogrammings.
We will sample
t
according to some distribution

(a_{t})_{0\leq t\leq k}
(
\sum_{t=0}^{k}a_{t}=1
), where
a_{t}
denotes the probability of measuring-and-reprogram exactly
t
quantum queries (and
k-t
classical queries).

2.

Pick a subset
\vec{v}
of
[c+q+k]
of length
k
,
uniformly at random, conditioned on having exactly
t
values corresponding to quantum queries
.
We have
1<v_{1}<\cdots<v_{k}\leq c+q+k
.
Pick
\vec{b}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
uniformly at random.

3.

Run
\mathcal{A}
with an additional control register
{\cal R}
, initialized as empty
\ket{\emptyset}
. Define the following operation
U
that updates the control register: for
x
that is not in
L
,

\displaystyle U\ket{x}{\ket{L}}_{\cal R}\rightarrow\ket{x}{\ket{L\cup(x,G(x))}}_{\cal R}.

Here
L
is the set of input and output pairs to be reprogrammed. Since we will only work with basis states
\ket{x}\ket{L}
where
x
is not in
L
,
U
clearly can be implemented by
a unitary (by assuming that the set
L
is initialized as empty).

4.

When
\mathcal{A}
makes its
i
-th query,

(a)

If
i=v_{j}
for some
j\in[k]
and it is a quantum query, do either of the following:

i.

If
b_{j}=0
, update
{\cal R}
using the input register and
G
, and make the
i
-th query to
H
controlled by
{\cal R}
;

ii.

If
b_{j}=1
, make the
i
-th query to
H
controlled by
{\cal R}
and update
{\cal R}
using the input register and
G
.

iii.

In the above procedure, before updating the control register, it checks coherently that the input register is not contained in the control register; otherwise, it aborts. Concretely, this corresponds to performing a projective measurement yielding a single bit of information, aborting if the input is contained, and otherwise proceeding with the rest of the simulation execution.

(b)

Else, answer
\mathcal{A}
’s
i
-th query (either quantum or classical) controlled by
{\cal R}
.

5.

Let
(\vec{x},\vec{y},z)
be
\mathcal{A}
’s output;

6.

Measure
{\cal R}
register to obtain
L=(\vec{x}^{\prime},\vec{y}^{\prime})
.

7.

Output
(\vec{x},\vec{y},z)
if
\vec{x}^{\prime}\equiv\vec{x}
; otherwise, abort.

Remark 3.4
.

We emphasize that the purpose of the simulator is to mimic
\mathcal{A}
. Here we assume that the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, after the first
q
queries, has already prepared the output
(x,z)
. We then force
A
to make
k
additional classical queries in order to generate
y=H(x)
.
This helps simplifying the proof and since we can assume without loss of generality that
c+q\geq k
, this modification only results in at most a larger constant factor.

Remark 3.5
.

The distribution
(a_{t})_{0\leq t\leq q}
of the
t
locations to be reprogrammed
will be established later in the analysis (
Section

3.4
). Intuitively, our objective
will be to determine what is the optimal value of
a_{t}
that will minimize the overall loss (giving us the tightest reprogramming bound).

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

We sketch here the outline of the proof:

1.

We first show how to bound the success probability of any hybrid algorithm
\mathcal{A}
by a linear combination of success probabilities of simulators (as defined in the hybrid coherent measure-and-reprogram experiment) that perform exactly
t
quantum queries (
Section

3.2
).
This is the most technical part and at a high level it involves the following steps:

•

Decompose the final state of
\mathcal{A}
, after performing all the classical and quantum queries, based on when the measurements in the hybrid measure-and-reprogram experiment take place and whether the queries are made before or after the measure-and-reprogram operations;

•

Take into account the final
k
classical queries that will be performed by
\mathcal{A}
in order to generate the final output
(\vec{x},H(\vec{x}))
;

•

Further decompose the final state of
\mathcal{A}
based on how many of the measure-and-reprogram operations have occurred when
\mathcal{A}
was performing a quantum query. This allows upper bounding the norm of the final state by a linear combination of un-normalized states corresponding to performing exactly
t
reprogrammings of
\mathcal{A}
’s quantum queries;

•

Apply the verification projectors on each of the states in the decompositions results in a decomposition where we can fix the inputs appearing in the measure-and-reprogram of the classical and quantum queries;

•

The tightness of the measure-and-reprogram bounds relies on the fact that the states corresponding to different classical queries to be reprogrammed will be orthogonal;

•

Finally, express each term in this decomposition to a behavior of the simulator in the hybrid coherent measure-and-reprogram experiment.

2.

In the second stage, we express the success probability of the simulator as a linear combination of the success probabilities of simulators winning with exactly
t
quantum queries (
Section

3.3
).

3.

Next, we observe that the loss of the hybrid measure-and-reprogram comes from the simulator drawing in a suitable way the distribution for the indices of the queries that will be measured-and-reprogrammed. We show how to instantiate this distribution and then by putting all the pieces together (relating the success of
\mathcal{A}
with the success of the simulator) we can derive the hybrid coherent-and-reprogram
Theorem

3.1

(
Section

3.4
).

4.

Finally, we show that the hybrid coherent measure-and-reprogram is optimal by studying the complexity of the multi-image search problem (
Section

3.5
).

∎

3.2
Bounding the Success of
\mathcal{A}

To prove the central hybrid coherent measure-and-reprogram, we will first need to show the following result:

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be any static hybrid
q
-quantum and
c
-classical query algorithm.
Then Simulator
\mathcal{B}
defined in the hybrid coherent measure-and-reprogram experiment (
Definition

3.3
) satisfies the following property for any
\vec{x}_{\sf o}\in X^{k}
without duplicate entries and
\vec{y}_{\sf o}=G(\vec{x}_{\sf o})
:

\displaystyle\Pr

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{A}^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}\rightarrow(\vec{x},z)\text{ and }\vec{x}\equiv\vec{x}_{\sf o}\text{ and }V^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}(\vec{x},H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}(\vec{x}),z)=1\right]\leq

\displaystyle{2^{k}\cdot}k\cdot\sum_{t=0}^{k}{q\choose t}{k\choose t}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{v},\vec{b}\\
|\vec{v}|_{q}=t\end{subarray}}p_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{v},\vec{b}},

where
|\vec{v}|_{q}=t
represents that the simulator reprograms exactly
t
quantum queries (i.e.,
\vec{v}
has exactly
t
locations corresponding to quantum queries) and where

p_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{v},\vec{b}}
denotes the probability that when the simulator
B
picks
\vec{v},\vec{b}
, it succeeds and outputs
\vec{x}\equiv\vec{x}_{\sf o}
.

Proof.

We will denote the final state of a hybrid algorithm performing
c
classical queries and
q
quantum queries by
\ket{\phi_{c,q}}
.
Let
\vec{x}\in X^{k}
be a fixed vector without duplicates and
\vec{y}
a fixed vector in
Y^{k}
.
We can then describe the state of the hybrid algorithm as follows.

After making its
c
classical and its
q
quantum queries to
H_{\vec{x},\vec{y}}
, the algorithm’s state is

{\left|{\phi_{c,q}^{H_{\vec{x},\vec{y}}}}\right\rangle=O_{H_{\vec{x},\vec{y}}}U_{q+c}\cdots O_{H_{\vec{x},\vec{y}}}U_{1}\ket{0}_{\mathcal{A}}.}

We assume the algorithm begins in the state
\ket{0}
, with a register that includes a classical history register, which we omit from the notation for now. There is no need to differentiate between classical and quantum queries, as the distinction only affects whether the input-output pair is added to the history register. Since the history register is not included in the notation, this distinction becomes relevant only in later analysis.

Next, we decompose this state of the hybrid algorithm
\mathcal{A}
so that each component corresponds to one of the cases in the Simulator (in
Definition

3.3
). The decomposition step follows closely the idea from the purely quantum setting
(proof of Theorem 6 in
[
CGLS25
]
)
since we do not distinguish whether a query is classical or quantum at this step.

The original state
\ket{\phi_{c,q}^{H_{\vec{x},\vec{y}}}}
was decomposed up to the first
q+c
queries (instead of all
q+c+k
queries),
in a summation of sub-normalized states
\phi_{\vec{v},\vec{b}}
parameterized by when the
\ell
measurements happen, given by an ordered vector
\vec{v}
such that:
1\leq v_{1}\leq...\leq v_{\ell}\leq c+q
and whether the queries are made before or after these measure-and-reprogram

, for
\ell\in\{0,...,c+q\}
.

For each
\vec{v}
of length

and

, we define:

\ket{\phi_{\vec{v},\vec{b}}}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{\ell}^{k}}\ket{\phi_{\vec{v},\vec{b},\sigma}},

where
\ket{\phi_{\vec{v},\vec{b},\sigma}}
is the state that is measured-and-reprogrammed according to the vectors
\vec{v}
and
\vec{b}
and the order
\sigma
(
x_{\sigma_{1}}
was first measured, and then
x_{\sigma_{2}}
, so on and so forth).

Adding the extra
k
queries.

Assume algorithm
\mathcal{A}
after making the first
q+c
queries, already prepared output
\vec{x},z
. We then force the algorithm to make its last
k
queries in order to generate
\vec{y}=H(\vec{x})
.

Recall the definitions of
\Pi^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}_{V}
and

in
Definition

2.5
. By setting
\vec{x}=\vec{x}_{\sf o}
and
\vec{y}=\vec{y}_{\sf o}

\displaystyle\Pr

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{A}^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}\rightarrow(\vec{x},z)\text{ and }\vec{x}\equiv\vec{x}_{\sf o}\text{ and }V^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}(\vec{x},H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}(\vec{x}),z)=1\right]

\displaystyle=\left\|G_{\vec{x}_{\sf o}}\Pi_{V}^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}\left|{\phi^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}_{{c+k,q}}}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}.

Since

and

commute (as they both are projections over computational basis), we can assume

is applied to the state first.
Even further, as the computation of
\vec{y}=H(\vec{x})
and the projection

applies to the state right before the last
k

.
Therefore, for every
\ket{\phi_{\vec{v},\vec{b},\sigma}}
, even if

(the length of
\vec{v}
), we can measure-and-(immediately)-reprogram exactly

.
Thus, we have:

\displaystyle\left|{\phi^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}_{{c+k,q}}}\right\rangle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{v},\vec{b}\\

(1)

3.5
Optimality for Multi-Image Search

In this section, we will show that our hybrid reprogramming bound (Theorem
3.1
) is optimal for the multi-image search problem. Concretely, we propose a hybrid algorithm for the multi-image search problem and we show that its success probability matches up to constant factors the hardness bound derived from the hybrid reprogramming theorem.

Corollary 3.9

(Hybrid Hardness of Multi-Image Search)
.

Any hybrid algorithm equipped with
q
quantum queries and
c
classical queries can solve the multi-image search problem with probability at most:

\displaystyle P_{max}=2^{2k}\cdot k\cdot A_{k,q,c}\cdot\frac{k!}{N^{k}}

Proof.

This follows directly by plugging the hybrid reprogramming bound derived in this section (Theorem
3.1
) in the Lifting Theorem (Theorem
5.5
):

\displaystyle p(R)

\displaystyle=\frac{k!}{N^{k}}

\displaystyle P_{max}

\displaystyle=2^{2k}\cdot k\cdot A_{k,q,c}\cdot p(R)=2^{2k}\cdot k\cdot A_{k,q,c}\cdot\frac{k!}{N^{k}}

(10)

∎

Lemma 3.10
.

There exists a hybrid algorithm
\mathcal{A}
equipped with
q
quantum queries and
c
classical queries that can solve the multi-image search problem with probability:

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\cdot k!\cdot\frac{1}{N^{k}}\cdot\left(v+u^{2}\right)^{k}

Proof.

Consider the following hybrid algorithm
\mathcal{A}
equipped with
q
quantum queries and
c
classical queries. For simplicity, we will assume that
q
and
c
are multiples of
k
, namely, that there exist positive integers
u
and
v
such that

q=k\cdot u
and
c=k\cdot v
.

1.

In the first stage of the algorithm,
\mathcal{A}
searches for an
x
that is a preimage for any of the
k
images
Y=(y_{1},...,y_{k})
. In this stage,
\mathcal{A}
spends
u
quantum queries and
v
classical queries;

2.

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
succeeded in the first stage for an image
y_{i}
. Then in the second stage
\mathcal{A}
searches for an
x
that is a preimage for any of the
k-1

. In the second stage,
\mathcal{A}
also spends
u
quantum queries and
v
classical queries.

3.

We continue in the exact same manner for
k

u
quantum queries and
v
classical queries).

First consider the success of a hybrid algorithm equipped with
c

q

, for a search space of size

. For this problem, it is known
[

]
that the optimal success probability is at least
\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{v}{N}+\frac{u^{2}}{N-v}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2N}\left(v+u^{2}\right)

Then, the success probability of

is equal to:

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{2N}\left(v+u^{2}\right)\cdot\frac{k-1}{2N}\left(v+u^{2}\right)\cdot\cdots\cdot\frac{1}{2N}\left(v+u^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\cdot k!\cdot\frac{1}{N^{k}}\cdot\left(v+u^{2}\right)^{k}

(11)

∎

It is not hard to observe that for constant

, hence our reprogramming bound is optimal for the constant

Our goal is to also show that for any general

Lemma 3.11

(Optimality of Hybrid Reprogramming for Multi-Image Search)
.

The hybrid coherent measure-and-reprogram bound (in Theorem
3.1
) is optimal for the multi-image inversion problem. Namely, the hybrid algorithm
\mathcal{A}
(in Lemma
3.10
) matches up to constant factors the hardness bound in Corollary
3.9
.

Proof.

To show the optimality, we need to relate the following:

\displaystyle 2^{2k}\cdot k\cdot A_{k,q,c}\cdot\frac{k!}{N^{k}}

\displaystyle\text{ and }\frac{1}{2^{k}}\cdot k!\cdot\frac{1}{N^{k}}\cdot\left(\frac{c}{k}+\frac{q^{2}}{k^{2}}\right)^{k}\text{ , or equivalently: }

\displaystyle A_{k,q,c}

\displaystyle\text{ and }\frac{1}{k\cdot 2^{3k}}\cdot\left(\frac{c}{k}+\frac{q^{2}}{k^{2}}\right)^{k}

Using the definition of
A_{k,q,c}
, to prove the optimality, we need to show:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{k}{q\choose t}^{2}\cdot{k\choose t}\cdot{c\choose{k-t}}\leq\left(O\left(\frac{c}{k}+\frac{q^{2}}{k^{2}}\right)\right)^{k}

where
1\leq k\leq\min(c,q)
.

Finally, the bound on
A_{k,q,c}
is shown separately in Lemma
3.12
.

∎

Lemma 3.12
.

We have for any
q,c>0
and
k<\min\{q,c\}
, the following upper bound on
A_{k,q,c}
:

\displaystyle A_{k,q,c}:=\sum_{t=0}^{k}\binom{q}{t}^{2}\binom{k}{t}\binom{c}{k-t}\leq\left(O\left(\frac{q^{2}}{k^{2}}+\frac{c}{k}\right)\right)^{k}.

Proof.

First, we start from the simple observation that since
\binom{k}{t}\leq 2^{k}
:

\displaystyle A_{k,q,c}\leq 2^{k}\sum_{t=0}^{k}\binom{q}{t}^{2}\binom{c}{k-t}

Let
t^{*}\in\{0,\cdots,k\}
, be defined as
t^{*}={\sf argmax}_{t}\left\{\binom{q}{t}^{2}\binom{c}{k-t}\right\}

Then, we have:

\displaystyle A_{k,q,c}\leq 2^{k}\sum_{t=0}^{k}\binom{q}{t}^{2}\binom{c}{k-t}\leq 2^{k}\cdot k\cdot\binom{q}{t^{*}}^{2}\binom{c}{k-t^{*}}\leq 2^{2k}\cdot\binom{q}{t^{*}}^{2}\binom{c}{k-t^{*}}.

We now only need to bound
\binom{q}{t}^{2}\binom{c}{k-t}
for any
0\leq t\leq k
. Using the inequality
\binom{n}{k}\leq\left(\frac{en}{k}\right)^{k}
holding for any
k\leq n
, we have:

\displaystyle A_{k,q,c}\leq 2^{2k}\cdot\binom{q}{t^{*}}^{2}\binom{c}{k-t^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{2k}\cdot\left(\frac{eq}{t^{*}}\right)^{2t^{*}}\left(\frac{ec}{k-t^{*}}\right)^{k-t^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{2k}\cdot e^{k+t^{*}}\cdot\left(\frac{q^{2}}{{t^{*}}^{2}}\right)^{t^{*}}\left(\frac{c}{k-t^{*}}\right)^{k-t^{*}}

\displaystyle\leq{(2e)}^{2k}\cdot\left(\frac{q^{2}}{{t^{*}}^{2}}\right)^{t^{*}}\left(\frac{c}{k-t^{*}}\right)^{k-t^{*}}.

Finally, we use AM-GM inequality:
x_{1}\cdot x_{2}\cdot\cdots\cdot x_{n}\leq\left(\frac{x_{1}+\cdots x_{n}}{n}\right)^{n}
.

As a result, by applying AM-GM to the
2k
factors
q/t^{*}
(
2t^{*}
factors) and
\sqrt{c/(k-t^{*})}
(
2k-2t^{*}
factors) we have:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{q}{t^{*}}\right)^{2t^{*}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{c}{k-t^{*}}}\right)^{2(k-t^{*})}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\frac{q}{t^{*}}\cdot 2t^{*}+\sqrt{\frac{c}{k-t^{*}}}\cdot 2(k-t^{*})}{2k}\right)^{2k}

(AM-GM)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{q}{k}+\sqrt{c\cdot\frac{k-t^{*}}{k^{2}}}\right)^{2k}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{q}{k}+\sqrt{\frac{c}{k}}\right)^{2k}.

(as
k-t^{*}\leq k
)

Hence, we have:

\displaystyle A_{k,q,c}\leq{(2e)}^{2k}\cdot\left(\frac{q}{k}+\sqrt{\frac{c}{k}}\right)^{2k}

Finally, using the simple inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
, applied to
q/k
and
\sqrt{c/k}
, we get:

\displaystyle A_{k,q,c}

\displaystyle\leq{(2e)}^{2k}\cdot\left(\left(\frac{q}{k}+\sqrt{\frac{c}{k}}\right)^{2}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq{(2e)}^{2k}\cdot 2^{k}\cdot\left(\frac{q^{2}}{k^{2}}+\frac{c}{k}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\left(8e^{2}\left(\frac{q^{2}}{k^{2}}+\frac{c}{k}\right)\right)^{k}.

∎

4
Lifting Theorems in the Noisy Oracle and Bounded-Depth Models

In this section, we
give lifting theorems
for NISQ algorithms.
We first describe the two NISQ models—noisy oracle and bounded-depth—and then show the main lifting results.

4.1
The Noisy Oracle Setting

Definition 4.1

(Noisy Oracle)
.

For an oracle
O:X\rightarrow Y
, we denote by
O_{p}
the noisy oracle of
O
, parameterized by a probability
p\in[0,1]
such that for an input quantum query
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{x}a_{x}\ket{x}
,
O_{p}
will answer as follows:

•

O_{p}\ket{\psi}=\sum_{x}a_{x}\ket{x}\ket{O(x)}
. We call this a
quantum answer
and happens with probability
1-p
;

•

Measure the input state
\ket{\psi}
in the computational basis. Suppose the measurement outcome is
x
. Then output
O(x)
. We call this a
classical answer
and this happens with probability
p
.

The main challenge in this setting, as opposed to the hybrid setting, is that in the noisy oracle setting the Simulator does not know if the oracle replies with a classical or a quantum answer to the quantum queries of
\mathcal{A}
.

To model the algorithm having oracle access to the noisy oracle
O_{p}
, we will rely on the idea of
purification of noisy queries
, used by Rosmanis in the context of analyzing quantum search in the presence of noise
[
Ros23
]
.
Concretely, any query to noisy oracle
O_{p}
can be thought of as
first applying a quantum query to the (noiseless) oracle,
receiving the answer
\rho
, and then
applying the map:

M_{p}\rho=(1-p)\rho+pM\rho,

where
M
denotes measuring the state in the computational basis.
Hence, for each quantum query of the algorithm, we will first apply the oracle query
O_{H}
followed by applying
M_{p}
. We will denote the composed operation by
O_{H,p}=M_{p}(O_{H}\otimes I)
.

Then, any arbitrary algorithm can be denoted as a sequence of arbitrary unitaries
U_{i}
(independent of the underlying oracle
H
) and operators
O_{H,p}
. As a result, the final state of a
q
-quantum query algorithm querying a noisy oracle
H
will be:
\ket{\psi}=U_{t+1}O_{H,p}...U_{2}O_{H,p}U_{1}\ket{0}
.

4.2
The Bounded-Depth Setting

In this model, we
assume that a
d
-depth quantum algorithm can act as an arbitrary quantum algorithm and then after every
d
depth, the entire system of the algorithm will get measured.

Definition 4.2

(Bounded-Depth Quantum Algorithms)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a quantum algorithm. Then we say that
\mathcal{A}
is
d
-depth bounded if after every
d
queries, the entire system of
\mathcal{A}
gets measured by applying the following dephasing channel: if the state of
\mathcal{A}
after
d
queries is
\rho
, then the state is mapped to:

\rho\rightarrow\sum_{x,p,w}\ket{x,y,z}\!\bra{x,y,z}\rho\ket{x,y,z}\!\bra{x,y,z}

where
x
refers to the input,
y
to the output and
z
to the work register of
\mathcal{A}
.

As shown by Hamoudi
et al.

[
HLS24
]
, the two models above are related:

Lemma 4.3

(
[
HLS24
]
)
.

Consider a
d
-depth quantum algorithm
\mathcal{A}
performing
T
quantum queries. Then there exists an algorithm
\mathcal{B}
performing
2T
queries to a noisy oracle
O_{1/d}
, (with the noise parameter
p=1/d
), such that
\mathcal{B}
outputs the same outcome as
\mathcal{A}
.

4.3
Main Result

The main result we will show in this section is a lifting theorem
for algorithms having oracle access to noisy oracles.
As a direct corollary, we can also immediately derive a similar lifting theorem for bounded-depth quantum algorithms.

Theorem 4.4

(Lifting for Noisy Oracles)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a multi-output
k
-search game. Let
\mathcal{A}
be any
T
-quantum query algorithm having oracle access to the noisy oracle
O_{p}

in the game
\mathcal{G}
(against the
k
-classical query challenger
\mathcal{C}
).
Then there exists a
k
-query adversary
\mathcal{B}
against the game such that:

\Pr[\mathcal{B}^{H}\text{ wins }\mathcal{G}]\geq E(p,T,k)\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{A}^{H}\text{ wins }\mathcal{G}].

Where:

\displaystyle E(p,T,k)=\frac{1}{O\left({T\choose k}\left(((1-p)Tk)^{k}+k\right)\right)}.

and where the probabilities are taken over the randomness of the oracles
H
and
O_{p}
and
over the randomness of the
algorithms
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
.

This will be shown by relying on the coherent measure-and-reprogram for noisy oracles (
Theorem

4.6
) in the next section, following the exact same idea as in the proof of
Lemma

5.3
.

Corollary 4.5

(Lifting for Bounded Depth)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a multi-output
k
-search game. Let
\mathcal{A}
be any
d
-depth bounded algorithm performing
T
quantum queries in total in the game
\mathcal{G}
(against the
k
-classical query challenger
\mathcal{C}
).
Then there exists a
k
-query adversary
\mathcal{B}
against the game such that:

\Pr[\mathcal{B}^{H}\text{ wins }\mathcal{G}]\geq E(1/d,2T,k)\Pr[\mathcal{A}^{H}\text{ wins }\mathcal{G}].

This follows directly from
Lemma

4.3
.

4.4
Coherent Measure-and-Reprogram for Noisy Oracles

Theorem 4.6

(Coherent Measure-and-Reprogram for Noisy Oracles)
.

Let

H,G:X\to Y

be two functions.
Let
k
be a positive integer (can be a computable function in
both
n=\log{|X|}
and
m=\log{|Y|}
).
Let
V^{H}
be any predicate defined over
X^{k}\times Y^{k}\times Z
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be any
T
-quantum query algorithm having oracle access to the noisy oracle
O_{p}
computing the function
H
.
Then there exists a black-box quantum algorithm
\mathcal{B}^{H,G,\mathcal{A}}
, satisfying the properties below.

For any
\vec{x}_{\sf o}\in X^{k}
without duplicate entries and
\vec{y}_{\sf o}=G(\vec{x}_{\sf o})
, we have,

\displaystyle\Pr

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{B}^{H,G,\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow(\vec{x},\vec{y},z)\text{ and }\vec{x}\equiv\vec{x}_{\sf o}\text{ and }V^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}(\vec{x},\vec{y},z)=1\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle E(p,T,k)\cdot\Pr\left[\mathcal{A}^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}\rightarrow(\vec{x},z)\text{ and }\vec{x}\equiv\vec{x}_{\sf o}\text{ and }V^{H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}}(\vec{x},H_{\vec{x}_{\sf o},\vec{y}_{\sf o}}(\vec{x}),z)=1\right],

where:

\displaystyle E(p,T,k)=\frac{1}{O\left({T\choose k}\left(((1-p)Tk)^{k}+k\right)\right)}.

Furthermore,
\mathcal{B}
makes exactly
k
quantum queries to the noisy oracle computing the function
G
and has a running time polynomial in
n,m,k
and the running time of
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

The Simulator in the noisy oracle setting will be exactly the same simulator as in the hybrid coherent reprogramming experiment
Definition

3.3
.

In this approach,
for a
T
query algorithm,
we consider an oracle assignment referring to the types of queries performed to the oracle. This model, in particular, captures the noisy oracle as the oracle assignment is given by the noise distribution, i.e. for each of the
T
queries the type of query is independently assigned to be either quantum with probability
1-p
or classical with probability
p
.
Now, we consider the behaviour of the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and the measure-and-reprogram simulator
\mathcal{B}
under different oracle assignments.

Let
V:=\{v_{1},...,v_{k}\}
be the set of reprogrammed queries (by the measure-and-reprogram experiment).
Then, by the definition of simulator
\mathcal{B}
, we have that the probabilities of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are equal if the oracle assignments only differ in the point to be reprogrammed, i.e., in any of
v_{i}\in V
.

Next, we define the following random variables:

•

{\sf type}
– denotes an oracle assignment (for the
T
queries), i.e. for each of the
T
queries specifying whether the query is classical or quantum.

•

q
– denotes the total number of quantum queries (out of
T
) under a given assignment
{\sf type}
.

•

q_{{\sf rep}}
– represents the number of quantum queries among the
k
reprogrammed queries
V:=\{v_{1},...,v_{k}\}
under a given
{\sf type}
.

•

{\sf type}_{V}
– denotes the oracle assignments only for the queries to be reprogrammed in
V
and
{\sf type}_{\bar{V}}
denotes the oracle assignments for all the points that are not to be reprogrammed (not in
V
).

Then we can write the success probability of the
T
-query algorithm
\mathcal{A}
as:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins}]=\sum_{{\sf type}}\Pr[{\sf type}]\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins}\ |\ {\sf type}]

On the other hand from
Section

3.2
, we know that:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins}\ |\ {\sf type}]\leq k\cdot\sum_{v_{1},...,v_{k}}{q\choose q_{{\sf rep}}}\cdot{k\choose q_{{\sf rep}}}\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins }|\ v_{1},...,v_{k},{\sf type}]

Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins}]

\displaystyle\leq k\cdot\sum_{{\sf type}}\sum_{v_{1},...,v_{k}}\Pr[{\sf type}]\cdot{q\choose q_{{\sf rep}}}\cdot{k\choose q_{{\sf rep}}}\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins }|\ v_{1},...,v_{k},{\sf type}]

\displaystyle\leq k\cdot\sum_{{\sf type}}\sum_{v_{1},...,v_{k}}\Pr[{\sf type}]{T\choose q_{{\sf rep}}}\cdot{k\choose q_{{\sf rep}}}\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins }|\ v_{1},...,v_{k},{\sf type}]

\displaystyle\leq k\cdot\sum_{v_{1},...,v_{k}}\sum_{{\sf type}_{\bar{V}}}\sum_{t=0}^{k}\Pr[q_{{\sf rep}}=t]\cdot\Pr[{\sf type}_{\bar{V}}]\cdot{T\choose q_{{\sf rep}}}\cdot{k\choose q_{{\sf rep}}}\cdot

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \cdot\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins }|\ v_{1},...,v_{k},{\sf type}]

\displaystyle=k\cdot\sum_{v_{1},...,v_{k}}\sum_{{\sf type}_{\bar{V}}}\sum_{t=0}^{k}p^{k-t}\cdot(1-p)^{t}\cdot\Pr[{\sf type}_{\bar{V}}]\cdot{T\choose t}\cdot{k\choose t}\cdot

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \cdot\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins }|\ v_{1},...,v_{k},{\sf type}]

\displaystyle=k\cdot\sum_{t=0}^{k}p^{k-t}\cdot(1-p)^{t}\cdot{T\choose t}\cdot{k\choose t}\cdot\sum_{v_{1},...,v_{k}}\sum_{{\sf type}_{\bar{V}}}\Pr[{\sf type}_{\bar{V}}]\cdot

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \cdot\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins }|\ v_{1},...,v_{k},{\sf type}]

Similarly, we can now express the success probability of the simulator
\mathcal{B}
:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{B}\text{ wins}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{{T\choose k}}\sum_{v_{1},...,v_{k}}\Pr[\mathcal{B}\text{ wins}\ |\ v_{1},...,v_{k}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{T\choose k}}\sum_{v_{1},...,v_{k}}\sum_{{\sf type}}\Pr[{\sf type}]\Pr[\mathcal{B}\text{ wins}\ |\ v_{1},...,v_{k},{\sf type}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{T\choose k}}\sum_{v_{1},...,v_{k}}\sum_{{\sf type}_{\bar{V}}}\sum_{t=0}^{k}p^{k-t}\cdot(1-p)^{t}\cdot\Pr[{\sf type}_{\bar{V}}]\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{B}\text{ wins }|\ v_{1},...,v_{k},{\sf type}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{T\choose k}}\sum_{t=0}^{k}p^{k-t}\cdot(1-p)^{t}\sum_{v_{1},...,v_{k}}\sum_{{\sf type}_{\bar{V}}}\Pr[{\sf type}_{\bar{V}}]\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{B}\text{ wins }|\ v_{1},...,v_{k},{\sf type}]

Now, as explained, using the fact that:

\Pr[\mathcal{B}\text{ wins }|\ v_{1},...,v_{k},{\sf type}]=\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins }|\ v_{1},...,v_{k},{\sf type}]
, we also have that
X:=\sum_{v_{1},...,v_{k}}\sum_{{\sf type}_{\bar{V}}}\Pr[{\sf type}_{\bar{V}}]\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins }|\ v_{1},...,v_{k},{\sf type}]=\sum_{v_{1},...,v_{k}}\sum_{{\sf type}_{\bar{V}}}\Pr[{\sf type}_{\bar{V}}]\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{B}\text{ wins }|\ v_{1},...,v_{k},{\sf type}]
. Therefore, we can connect the probabilities of winning for the algorithm and simulator as follows:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins}]

\displaystyle\leq k\cdot\sum_{t=0}^{k}p^{k-t}\cdot(1-p)^{t}\cdot{T\choose t}\cdot{k\choose t}\cdot X

\displaystyle\leq k\cdot\sum_{t=0}^{k}(1-p)^{t}\cdot{T\choose t}\cdot{k\choose t}\cdot X

\displaystyle\leq(((1-p)Tk)^{k}+k)\cdot X

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{B}\text{ wins}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{{T\choose k}}\sum_{t=0}^{k}p^{k-t}\cdot(1-p)^{t}\cdot X=\begin{cases}&\frac{1}{{T\choose k}}\cdot\frac{p^{k+1}-(1-p)^{k+1}}{2p-1}\cdot X\text{ , if }p\neq\frac{1}{2}\\
&\frac{1}{{T\choose k}}\cdot\frac{k+1}{2^{k}}\cdot X\text{ , if }p=\frac{1}{2}\end{cases}

Therefore, by combining both inequalities, we have:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathcal{A}\text{ wins}]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[\mathcal{B}\text{ wins}]\cdot O\left({T\choose k}\left(((1-p)Tk)^{k}+k\right)\right)

∎

quantum queries against
\mathcal{G}^{\otimes g}_{\sf MIS}
is
\epsilon_{\mathcal{G}_{\sf MIS}^{\otimes g}}(q)
, then the maximum probability of any non-uniform adversary equipped with
q
quantum queries and
S
-length classical advice against the original game
\mathcal{G}
is at most:

\epsilon_{\mathcal{G}}^{C}(q,S)\leq 4\cdot\left[\epsilon_{\mathcal{G}_{\sf MIS}^{\otimes{S}}}(Sq)\right]^{\frac{1}{S}}

Then,
Lemma

A.3
follows directly by combining our (strong) hybrid lifting theorem (in
Lemma

5.5
) with the advice result (Lemma
A.4
).

A.2
Application 2: Salting Against Non-uniform Adversaries

Definition A.5

(Salted Game)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a search game (as defined in Def.
5.1
) specified by a random oracle
H:[M]\rightarrow[N]
, a distribution over challenges
\pi_{H}
and a winning relation
R_{H,ch}
defined over
Y
. Then we define the salted version of
\mathcal{G}
as the game
\mathcal{G}_{s}
with salted space
[K]
defined as follows:

1.

The random oracle function is defined as:
G=(H_{1},...,H_{K})
for
K
random functions
H_{i}:[M]\rightarrow[N]
;

2.

For any such
G
, the challenge
ch:=(i,ch_{i})
is produced by first sampling uniformly at random
i\in[K]
and then sampling
ch_{i}
according to
\pi_{H_{i}}
;

3.

The winning relation is defined as
R_{G,ch}:=R_{H_{i},ch_{i}}
;

We will denote by

\epsilon_{\mathcal{G}_{s}}(q,c)

the maximum probability over all

q
-quantum and
c
-classical hybrid algorithms

\mathcal{A}
of winning the salted game
\mathcal{G}_{s}
.

Lemma A.6

(Hybrid Security of Salted Game against Classical Advice)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a multi-output
k
-search game (as defined in Def.
5.1
), specified by a relation
R
. Let
\mathcal{G}_{s}
be the salted game, with salt space
[K]
. Then we have,

\displaystyle\epsilon^{C}_{\mathcal{G}_{s}}(q,c,S)\leq 4\cdot\frac{S}{K}+\left(O\left(\frac{q^{2}}{k^{2}}+\frac{c}{k}\right)\right)^{k}\cdot p(R).

Proof.

By
Lemma

A.4
, the non-uniform security is related to the multi-instance game
\mathcal{G}_{s,\sf MIS}^{\otimes g}
, with salt space
[K]
. The security of the multi-instance game is closely related to the Direct Product, for salted games, as shown in
[
DLW24
]
(in the proof of Theorem 4.1). More precisely,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathcal{G}_{s,\sf MIS}^{\otimes g}}(gq)^{1/g}\leq\epsilon_{\mathcal{G}_{s}^{\otimes g}}(gq)^{1/g}+\frac{g}{K}.

Intuitively, the only difference between the multi-instance game and the Direct Product is that, the same salt can be sampled with duplication. The extra factor
\frac{g}{K}
captures the fact that the salt can be duplicated.
Combining with
Lemma

5.7
, we have:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathcal{G}_{s}}^{C}(q,S)

\displaystyle\leq 4\left(\epsilon_{\mathcal{G}_{s,\sf MIS}^{\otimes S}}(Sq)\right)^{1/S}

\displaystyle\leq 4\left(\epsilon_{\mathcal{G}_{s}^{\otimes S}}(Sq)^{1/S}+\frac{S}{K}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\cdot\frac{S}{K}+\left(O\left(\frac{q^{2}}{k^{2}}+\frac{c}{k}\right)\right)^{k}.

∎

A.4

Next, we can determine the hybrid hardness of the multi-collision problem, namely finding
k
different inputs that map to the same output of the random oracle.

Lemma A.8

(Hybrid Hardness of Multi-Collision Finding)
.

For any hybrid algorithm
\mathcal{A}
equipped with
q
quantum and
c
classical queries, the success probability of
\mathcal{A}
to solve the
k
-multi-collision problem is at most:

\Pr_{H}[\mathcal{A}^{H}()\rightarrow\vec{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{k})\ :\ H(x_{1})=...=H(x_{k})]\leq\left(O\left(\frac{q^{2}}{k^{2}}+\frac{c}{k}\right)\right)^{k}\cdot\frac{1}{N^{k-1}}

We will show this using our strong quantum lifting theorem for image relations (
Lemma

5.5
).
Define
R:=\{y,...,y\}_{y}
the relation over
[N]^{k}
, where
H:[M]\rightarrow[N]
. Then for each permutation
\pi
, we have
\Pr[(y_{\pi(1)},...,y_{\pi(k)})\in R\ |\ (y_{1},...,y_{k})\leftarrow[N]^{k}]=\frac{1}{N^{k-1}}
. As

is permutation invariant, this implies that:

\Pr_{H}[\mathcal{A}^{H}()\rightarrow\vec{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{k})\ :\ H(x_{1})=...=H(x_{k})]\leq\left(O\left(\frac{q^{2}}{k^{2}}+\frac{c}{k}\right)\right)^{k}\cdot\frac{1}{N^{k-1}}

∎

Next, we consider another search application, namely the task of determining
k
different inputs that all map to
0
under the random oracle. One of the main motivations behind this problem is its relation to the notion of proof-of-work in the blockchain context
[
GKL15
]
.

Lemma A.9

(Hybrid Hardness of Multi-Search)
.

For any hybrid algorithm
\mathcal{A}
equipped with
q
quantum and
c
classical queries whose task is to find different
k
preimages of
0
of a random oracle
H
, the success probability of
\mathcal{A}
is upper bounded by:

\displaystyle\Pr_{H}[\mathcal{A}^{H}()\rightarrow\vec{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{k})\ :\ H(x_{i})=0\ \forall i\in[k]]\leq\left(O\left(\frac{q^{2}}{k^{2}}+\frac{c}{k}\right)\right)^{k}\cdot\frac{1}{N^{k}}

Proof.

We will show this using our strong quantum lifting theorem for image relations (
Lemma

5.5
).
Define
R:=\{0,...,0\}
the relation over
[N]^{k}
, where
H:[M]\rightarrow[N]
. Then for each permutation
\pi
, we have
\Pr[(y_{\pi(1)},...,y_{\pi(k)})\in R\ |\ (y_{1},...,y_{k})\leftarrow[N]^{k}]=\frac{1}{N^{k}}

\displaystyle\Pr_{H}[\mathcal{A}^{H}()\rightarrow\vec{x}=(x_{1},...,x_{k})\ :\ H(x_{i})=0\ \forall i\in[k]]\leq\left(O\left(\frac{q^{2}}{k^{2}}+\frac{c}{k}\right)\right)^{k}\frac{1}{N^{k}}

Finally, we consider the 3SUM computational problem. In this task an algorithm needs to find 3 inputs

As before, to establish the quantum and hybrid complexity of the problem one only needs to compute the classical quantity

Lemma A.10

(Hybrid Hardness of 3SUM)
.

For any hybrid algorithm
\mathcal{A}
equipped with
q
quantum and
c
classical queries, the success probability of
\mathcal{A}
to solve the 3SUM problem is upper bounded by:

\displaystyle\Pr_{H}

\displaystyle[\mathcal{A}^{H}()\rightarrow\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\ :\ H(x_{1})+H(x_{2})+H(x_{3})=0]

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\left(q^{2}+c\right)^{3}\cdot\frac{3N^{2}+3N+1}{(2N+1)^{3}}\right)

Proof.

Denote the set

H:[M]\rightarrow Y

is defined as follows:
R=\{(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\ |\ y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3}=0\}

For each permutation
