Oral PCSK9 inhibitors for elevated LDL-C: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials
Authors: Aditya Karthikeyan, Avrohom Karp, Maisha Maliha, Amrin Kharawala, Sridhar Mangalesh, Priyansh Shah, Vishakha Modak, Adir Alper, Apoorva Doshi, Hamsa Murli, Vikyath Satish, Kuan-Yu Chi, Robert T Faillace
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInjectable proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors are an established strategy to lower low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and reduce atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk. Recently developed oral PCSK9 inhibitors may be a user-friendly alternative.MethodsA systematic search from inception through March 6, 2026, identified randomized controlled trials comparing oral PCSK9 inhibitors with placebo in adults with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or at high risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, receiving background lipid-lowering therapy, or with statin intolerance. The efficacy endpoint was percentage change in lipid levels including LDL-C, apolipoprotein-B, lipoprotein(a), triglycerides, and non-HDL-C. Safety outcomes included any adverse event, serious adverse events, discontinuation due to adverse events, and new-onset or worsening of diabetes attributed to intervention. Random-effects meta-analysis was performed using restricted-maximum-likelihood estimation with Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman adjustment. Heterogeneity was assessed using I² statistic.ResultsAcross five randomized phase 2 and 3 trials (n = 4226), oral PCSK9 inhibitors were associated with a significant reduction in LDL-C versus placebo (mean difference −49.92%; 95% CI −56.41 to −43.43; I² = 86%; PI, -72.30 to -27.54). Significant reductions were also observed in ApoB, Lp(a), triglycerides, and non–HDL-C. Safety outcomes were similar between both groups. In dose-matched analyses of MK-0616 at comparable dosing across trials, reductions in lipid markers remained significant with minimal heterogeneity.ConclusionsOral PCSK9 inhibitors were associated with reductions in LDL-C, ApoB, non–HDL-C, and lipoprotein(a), with safety profile comparable to placebo. Longer-term studies evaluating cardiovascular outcomes and comparisons with injectable PCSK9 inhibitors are required to define their clinical role.Keywords: Oral PCSK9 inhibitor, LDL-C, Placebo

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease is widely recognized to be driven by cumulative exposure to ApoB-containing lipoproteins, making intensive LDL-C reduction a cornerstone of ASCVD prevention. Current guidelines recommend intensifying therapy in patients who remain above LDL-C thresholds despite maximally tolerated statin treatment, typically with ezetimibe and, when greater additional lowering is required, with PCSK9-directed therapy [1].

PCSK9 inhibition has emerged as a potent therapeutic modality, with large outcome trials showing that the addition of injectable agents like evolocumab or alirocumab to intensive statin therapy lowers LDL-C by roughly 50–60% and reduces major adverse cardiovascular events in high-risk populations [2,3].

Despite their potent lipid-lowering efficacy, injectable PCSK9 therapies have seen more limited uptake in routine practice than their trial-based benefit might suggest. Real-world use has been constrained by persistent payor rejection, prior authorization requirements, administrative burden, and patient out-of-pocket costs. These barriers have heightened interest in orally bioavailable PCSK9 inhibitors, which may broaden access to PCSK9 pathway-based lipid-lowering through a simpler route of administration [4].

Oral PCSK9 inhibitors have emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy, supported by recent phase 3 randomized trials, including the CORALreef trials, which showed that MK-0616 (renamed as enlicitide decanoate) produces large placebo-adjusted reductions in LDL-C, together with concordant lowering of non-HDL cholesterol, apolipoprotein B, and lipoprotein(a), and with an adverse event profile comparable to placebo [5,6]. Against this background, we performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials to synthesize the available evidence across oral PCSK9 agents and to quantify their effects on key atherogenic lipid parameters. To our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis to incorporate phase 3 CORALreef trials and to perform dose-aligned analyses across oral PCSK9 inhibitors.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the recommendations of the Cochrane Collaboration Handbook [7] and in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Guidelines (Figure S1) [8]. The protocol for this study was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420261345067).

PubMed, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, and ClinicalTrials.gov were systematically searched from inception through March 6, 2026, to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing oral PCSK9 inhibitors with placebo in patients with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or at high risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, receiving background lipid-lowering therapy, or with documented statin intolerance. As only published trial-level data were used, this study was exempt from Institutional Review Board approval.

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH/Emtree) and free-text terms related to PCSK9 inhibition and oral PCSK9 inhibitors. Key search terms included “PCSK9”, “proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitor”, “MK-0616”, “enlicitide decanoate”, “AZD0780”, “NNC0385–0434”, and “randomized controlled trial.” Boolean operators and database-specific syntax were used to maximize sensitivity. The full search strategy is provided in Table S1.

The search was restricted to randomized trials on human subjects. Reference lists of relevant reviews and eligible manuscripts were manually screened for additional trials. Both published and unpublished (conference abstracts, trial registries) RCTs were considered to minimize publication bias. Risk of bias for included trials was assessed independently by two reviewers, using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 Tool (Figure S2) [9].

Study selection and data extraction

Studies were eligible if they enrolled adults ≥18 years with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or at high risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, who are already on lipid-lowering therapy, including a statin, or have a documented statin intolerance. Patients with homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia, renal or liver impairment, and those previously on PCSK9 inhibitors without sufficient washout were excluded from this study. Two reviewers independently assessed eligibility based on full-text review, and disagreements were resolved by consensus. Any discrepancies between trial enrollment criteria and these eligibility rules were noted and discussed in the limitations section. Observational studies, registries, non-randomized comparisons, and trials lacking relevant clinical endpoints were excluded.

Two reviewers independently extracted trial-level data using a standardized template. Extracted variables included trial name, study design, sample size, analytic population for primary and subgroup analysis, follow-up duration, intervention arms, and number of outcome events per arm. Cross-over between treatment arms was recorded when reported. No individual patient-level data were available.

Outcomes

The primary effectiveness endpoint was the percentage change in LDL cholesterol levels from baseline. Secondary efficacy endpoints included percentage changes in apolipoprotein B, lipoprotein(a), triglycerides, and non-HDL cholesterol. The safety endpoints were the occurrence of one or more adverse events, adverse events leading to medication discontinuation, serious adverse events, and new onset or worsening of diabetes attributed to the intervention.

Statistical analysis

For continuous outcomes, pooled mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using Inverse Variance Methods. Random-effects meta-analysis was performed using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator to estimate between-study variance (τ²). As effect sizes were expressed as percentage point differences, between-study variance (τ²) was reported on the squared percentage scale, with corresponding τ values reflecting between-study standard deviation on the percentage scale. For outcomes demonstrating non-negligible between-study variance (τ²), prediction intervals were reported to characterize the expected range of effects across settings. For outcomes with minimal heterogeneity (τ² = 0), prediction intervals were not presented as they provide no additional information beyond the confidence interval. Given the limited number of studies, the confidence intervals were calculated using the Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman (HKSJ) adjustment for a more reliable estimation.

As for the dichotomous outcomes, pooled risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs were estimated using the Mantel–Haenszel method, considering that the event rates were low. For trials with zero events in one treatment arm, a continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to permit estimation of risk ratios.

For trials evaluating multiple intervention doses, each dose arm was included as a separate comparison, with the placebo group evenly divided across intervention arms in accordance with Cochrane methodological recommendations. This approach allowed preservation of dose-specific effects while avoiding double-counting of the comparator.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic. To address conceptual heterogeneity related to variation in dosing regimens and outcome assessment across trials, prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted based on the dose of the intervention and the method used for LDL cholesterol determination. To assess robustness and address heterogeneity related to early-phase data and agent discontinuation, we performed two sensitivity analyses: one excluding phase 2 trials and another excluding discontinued agents. Given the small number of included studies, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were not performed due to limited stability and interpretability in sparse meta-analyses. Formal assessment of publication bias was not performed as funnel plot asymmetry tests and regression-based approaches are underpowered and unreliable in sparse meta-analyses (k = 5). All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.2) using the “meta” package.

Results

We identified 5 RCTs enrolling 4226 participants [5,6,[10], [11], [12]]. All trials evaluated oral PCSK9 inhibitors in participants aged ≥18 years with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or at high risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, receiving background lipid-lowering therapy, or with documented statin intolerance. Three studies were phase 2 trials with different dose ranges [[10], [11], [12]], while two were phase 3 trials evaluating a fixed dose of the medication [5,6]. Across trials, follow-up ranged from 8 to 52 weeks. (Table S2)

Quality assessment

Risk of bias was estimated with the Cochrane RoB 2.0, and the included studies were judged to have some concerns, with no study classified as having a high risk of bias. Based on these findings, the overall methodological quality of the included studies was considered moderate to high, supporting the robustness of the pooled analysis.

Primary efficacy outcome

Oral PCSK9 inhibitors were found to be associated with a significant reduction in LDL-C compared with placebo (MD, −49.92; 95% CI, −56.41 to −43.43; τ² = 96.72; I2 = 86%; PI, −72.30 to −27.54) (Fig. 2, Table S3). Given substantial heterogeneity, prespecified subgroup analyses based on dose and method of LDL-C deduction were conducted. (Figs. 4, 5, Tables S12, S13)

Secondary efficacy outcomes

In addition to the reduction in LDL-C, oral PCSK9 inhibitors were associated with a significant reduction in ApoB (MD, −40.80; 95% CI, −47.08 to −34.53; τ² = 96.66; I2 = 93.5%; PI, −63.12 to −18.49) (Figure S3, Table S4), Lp(a) (MD, −18.54; 95% CI, −23.58 to −13.50; τ² = 28.90; I2 = 44.8%; PI, −31.29 to −5.79) (Fig. 3, Table S5), triglyceride levels (MD, −9.77; 95% CI, −18.29 to −1.25; τ² = 0; I2 = 73.1%) (Figure S4, Table S6) and non-HDL levels (MD, −45.68; 95% CI, −52.85 to −38.5; τ² = 87.92; I2 = 90.8%; PI −68.07 to −23.29) (Figure S5, Table S7) as compared to placebo. The values for triglyceride levels, indicate that the between-study variance estimate approached the boundary of estimation, thus limiting its precision in estimating heterogeneity despite variability in study-level effects.

Safety outcomes

The pooled risk of any adverse event taking place was similar between the two groups (RR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.98–1.09; τ² = 0; I2 = 0%) (Figure S6, Table S8). Similarly, the risk of serious adverse events (RR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.67–1.01; τ² = 0; I2 = 0%) (Figure S7, Table S9) or any adverse event leading to discontinuation (RR, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.53–1.06; τ² = 0; I2 = 0%) (Figure S8, Table S10) was also comparable between the treatment and placebo groups. Additionally, no significant difference was noted between treatment and placebo group in terms of intervention-associated risk of new onset or worsening of diabetes (RR, 1.04; 95% CI, 0.77–1.41; τ² = 0; I2 = 0%) (Figure S9, Table S11)

Subgroup analyses

Exploratory analyses were performed to explore potential sources of heterogeneity observed in the primary analysis. LDL-C determined through the Friedewald formula (MD, −48.35; 95% CI, −56.55 to −40.14; τ² = 81.29; I2 = 84.6%; PI, −71.19 to −25.50) and Beta quantification (MD, −46.20; 95% CI, −83.44 to −8.96; τ² = 192.77; I2 = 85.8%; PI, −116.60 to 24.20), continued to show a substantial degree of heterogeneity, while the usage of the Martin-Hopkins equation resulted in minimal heterogeneity (MD, −60.39; 95% CI, −85.73 to −35.05; τ² = 0; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 5, Table S12).

Furthermore, a dose-matched analysis was performed with identical doses of MK-0616, with 20 mg taken as the common dose across studies. Oral PCSK9 inhibitors continued to be associated with a significant reduction in the levels of LDL-C (MD, −60.09; 95% CI, −67.63 to −52.56; τ² = 0; I2 = 0%), ApoB (MD, −51.66; 95% CI, −56.42 to −46.89; τ² = 0.75; I2 = 0%), Non-HDL (MD, −55.82; 95% CI, −60.18 to −51.46; τ² = 0; I2 = 0%) and Lp(a) (MD, −22.98; 95% CI, −43.80 to −2.16; τ² = 0; I2 = 0%), but this time with minimal heterogeneity (Fig. 4, Table S13). As there was only one trial each for both AZD0780 and NNC0385–0434, dose-matched analysis could not be performed.

Sensitivity analysis

Restricting the analysis to only phase 3 trials, which were only available for MK-0616, yielded significant LDL-C reduction with minimal heterogeneity (MD, −60.39; 95% CI, −85.73 to −35.05; τ² = 0; I2 = 0%), suggesting variability in earlier-phase dose-ranging studies as a potential contributor to heterogeneity (Figure S10, S11).

Exclusion of discontinued agents did not materially alter the pooled LDL-C estimates, with heterogeneity mostly from combining different medications of varied dosing (MD, −50.90; 95% CI, −58.08 to −43.71; τ² = 87.03; I2 = 86.8%; PI, −73.1896 to −28.6072) (Figure S12, S13).

Discussion

Our meta-analysis examined the effects of oral PCSK9 inhibition in the reduction of core ASCVD risk-associated atherogenic particles, including LDL-C, ApoB, non-HDL cholesterol, and Lp(a). As the first meta-analysis to incorporate phase 3 CORALreef trials into the existing evidence base and use dose-aligned analysis to resolve heterogeneity, our study demonstrates that oral PCSK9 inhibitors, including MK-0616, NNC0385–0434, and AZD0780, produce robust, statistically significant reductions in LDL-C and ApoB, non-HDL-C, and Lp(a), thus reinforcing the potential role of these agents in managing hypercholesterolemia and ASCVD risk [13,14]. Not all lipid fractions track equally with PCSK9 inhibition, with this variability most notable in Lp(a), paralleling the pattern previously reported with injectable PCSK9 inhibitors [2,3]. The observed heterogeneity likely reflects differences in exposure, LDL-C estimation methods, and trial design rather than inconsistent directionality of the effect. Moreover, the pooled estimates are predominantly driven by MK-0616, the only agent with phase 3 trials available. Hence, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than a definitive class-wide effect across all oral PCSK9 inhibitors.

Because ASCVD risk is driven by cumulative exposure to ApoB-containing lipoproteins, we first examined ApoB percent change from baseline. ApoB more directly reflects the number of circulating atherogenic particles than LDL-C alone, particularly when cholesterol content per particle varies, and thus provides a biologically relevant marker of treatment effect for therapies targeting the PCSK9 LDL receptor pathway [15,16]. Our analysis showed a significant reduction in ApoB versus placebo (mean difference −40.80%, 95% CI −47.08% to −34.53%). The effect was directionally consistent across agents, with some variability. However, heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 93.5%), suggesting additional factors beyond the primary exposure may have contributed to the variability across studies. We therefore stratified by dose to better define the source of this heterogeneity.

We anchored the ApoB dose stratified analysis to a comparable higher dose group of MK-0616 (18 to 20 mg), as this was the only oral agent represented across both phase 3 and earlier phase 2 trials. In this aligned subset, ApoB lowering was more consistent, with a pooled mean difference in percent change from baseline of −51.66 percentage points (95% CI, −56.42 to −46.89) and no observed heterogeneity (I² = 0%). This magnitude is broadly concordant with ApoB reductions reported with injectable PCSK9 inhibition, where evolocumab has produced placebo-corrected ApoB reductions in the range of approximately 48% to 52% in pooled analyses [2,3,17].

Mechanistically, PCSK9 binds hepatic LDL receptors and targets them for lysosomal degradation, thereby limiting receptor recycling and reducing hepatocyte surface receptor availability. By inhibiting this interaction, PCSK9 targeted therapy preserves LDL receptor recycling, increases hepatic receptor abundance, and enhances clearance of circulating ApoB-containing lipoproteins [18]. Human tracer studies with alirocumab support this mechanism, demonstrating increased IDL and LDL ApoB fractional catabolic rates together with reduced LDL ApoB production after PCSK9 inhibition. Taken together, these findings suggest that at sufficient exposure, oral PCSK9 inhibition may lower ApoB similar to its injectable counterpart. Given that LDL is the principal circulating ApoB-containing lipoprotein, a parallel reduction in LDL-C would be expected within the same dose-aligned subset [19].

We therefore next examined whether LDL-C tracked with ApoB under these conditions. LDL-C reduction was well aligned with ApoB reduction in the dose-adjusted subset. In the MK-0616 higher dose group (18 to 20 mg), LDL-C closely tracked with ApoB, supporting a shared mechanism of particle clearance through PCSK9 inhibition, consistent with its known effect on LDL receptor recycling [18]. In this subset, LDL-C showed a strong and consistent treatment effect compared to placebo, with a pooled mean difference of −60.09% (95% CI −67.63% to −52.56%) and I² = 0%. In comparison, evolocumab reduced LDL-C by ∼59% and alirocumab by ∼60% in the FOURIER and ODYSSEY OUTCOMES trials, respectively [2,3] Inclisiran was noted to decrease LDL by ∼50% in the ORION trials [20] This substantially exceeded other oral non-statin therapies, as ezetimibe lowers LDL-C by 18–25%, bempedoic acid by 17–21%, and their combination by ∼36–40% [[21], [22], [23], [24]]

We then examined whether differences in LDL-C response could be explained by variations in calculation methods. Stratifying by method didn’t significantly reduce variability, as substantial heterogeneity remained in both the Friedewald subgroup (I² = 84.6%) and the beta quantification subgroup (I² = 85.8%), despite consistent LDL-C reductions in the same direction. The Martin-Hopkins subgroup had I² = 0%, but this group included only two comparisons. Overall, these results suggest that the LDL-C calculation method alone doesn’t account for the heterogeneity between studies, and that differences in exposure and trial context are likely the key factors.

Lp(a), now regarded as an independent and causal risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, is also associated with aortic valve stenosis and can add cardiovascular risk even when LDL-C is otherwise well controlled. In our study, Lp(a) levels showed more modest reductions, with a pooled mean difference of −18.54% (95% CI −23.58% to −13.50%), indicating that while oral PCSK9 inhibitors do lower Lp(a), the response is consistently smaller than for LDL-C and ApoB. Consistent with the more modest Lp(a) response, oral PCSK9 inhibitors showed a flatter dose response across several oral PCSK9 agents. This magnitude is in line with findings from injectable PCSK9 therapies: evolocumab in the FOURIER trial reduced Lp(a) by a median of 26.9% at 48 weeks[2,25], and alirocumab in ODYSSEY OUTCOMES showed a median relative reduction of −23% by 4 months[3,26]. Nonetheless, other agents such as ezetimibe and bempedoic acid have minimal or no effect on Lp(a), making PCSK9 inhibitors the most efficacious commercially available option for Lp(a) lowering [24]

Several mechanisms may underlie this pattern. Unlike LDL, Lp(a) is not cleared predominantly through the LDL receptor[27]. Its levels are driven largely by hepatic apo(a) synthesis and isoform biology, so PCSK9 inhibition, despite markedly increasing LDL receptor recycling, only partially enhances Lp(a) removal [28]. The apo(a) moiety likely also shifts clearance toward non-LDLR pathways that are less responsive to PCSK9 inhibition [27], while any reduction in secretion appears modest. The net effect is a muted class response, with Lp(a) typically lowering around 20 to 30%, well below the reductions seen with LDL C and ApoB [25]. However, despite these comparatively modest effects, PCSK9 inhibitors represent the most efficacious commercially available method for Lp(a) reduction currently in use. Several agents have demonstrated dramatic reductions in Lp(a) levels in clinical trials that may soon be poised for commercial use, but currently they remain under investigation.

Oral PCSK9 inhibitors showed no significant increase in any adverse events (RR 1.03; 95% CI 0.98 to 1.09; I2 = 0%). Moreover, the risk of serious adverse events (RR 0.82; 95% CI 0.67 to 1.01; I2 = 0%) or adverse events leading to medication discontinuation (RR 0.75; 95% CI 0.53 to 1.06; I2 = 0%) was not significant either, all of which support a favorable tolerability profile. However, unlike injectable PCSK9 inhibitors, which are supported by a broad safety literature with follow-up durations extending beyond 5 years[29], safety data for oral agents remained limited, with the longest oral PCSK9 inhibitor trial duration being 52 weeks[5]. Furthermore, we found no significant association between PCSK9 inhibition and diabetes (RR 1.04; 95% CI 0.77 to 1.41; I2 = 0%), despite the weak signal reported in some Mendelian randomization studies[30,31], with the discordance most likely arising from the fact that genetic studies reflect lifetime exposure as compared to short-term pharmacological interventions.

Our study comes with certain key limitations. The current evidence base remains limited to five randomized trials with follow-up durations ranging from 8 to 52 weeks, which restricts statistical power and long-term inference regarding the durability of outcomes. Moreover, while our analysis focused on lipid biomarkers, whether these effects translate into cardiovascular benefit remains to be established, as the current trials were not powered to evaluate cardiovascular outcomes. The ongoing CORALreef Outcomes trial, which is projected for completion in 2029, would serve to provide further evidence in this domain. Furthermore, oral agents achieve similar LDL-C reductions to their injectable counterparts and may potentially offer improved adherence and patient preference due to their once-daily dosing and oral administration. However, this advantage remains theoretical and needs head-to-head comparisons in future trials. Finally, differences in study populations, background lipid-lowering therapy, and trial design may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity across lipid outcomes, further limiting the precision and generalizability of pooled estimates. Of note, NNC0835–0434 was discontinued for non-clinical reasons, which does not diminish its clinical relevance, and hence we included it in our analysis [10]

Conclusion

Oral PCSK9 inhibitors demonstrate consistent lipid-lowering effects across early and late phase trials, with the most robust evidence derived from MK-0616. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than establishing a class-wide effect. Long-term cardiovascular outcome data and head-to-head studies with injectable counterparts will be needed to define their ultimate clinical role. (Fig. 1)
