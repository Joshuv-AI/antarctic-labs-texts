Comparative Evaluation of Antimicrobial Activity between Clove and Ginger Extracts against Streptococcus mutans: An In Vitro Study
Authors: M Rajkumar, Akila Ganesh, KC Vignesh, R Kavi Chudar, M Janani, P Kruthika, P Kennedy Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental caries, primarily caused by Streptococcus mutans, is a prevalent oral health issue. Natural compounds such as clove and ginger exhibit antibacterial properties. The plant-derived compounds release phytochemicals, which aid in controlling the risk of the development of dental caries. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of clove and ginger extracts against S. mutansin vitro, potentially offering new insights into alternative antimicrobial agents for caries prevention.ObjectivesTo compare and assess the antimicrobial activity of the spice extract—clove (Syzygium aromaticum) and ginger (Zingiber officinale) against S. mutans.Materials and methodsApproximately, 10 gm of ginger and clove powder was mixed with 10 mL of 70% ethanol. S. mutans [American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) 25175] were subcultured in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth and incubated at 37°C for 18–24 hours. The antimicrobial activity of ginger and clove extracts was evaluated using the microbroth dilution method in sterile 96-well microtiter plates.ResultsGrowth of S. mutans was observed for two concentrations of clove (3.125 and 1.6%) and one concentration of ginger (1.6%). While the lower median CFU count suggests ginger may have stronger antimicrobial activity, the difference was not statistically significant, indicating comparable efficacy of both extracts at this concentration.ConclusionBoth ginger and clove extracts demonstrated antimicrobial activity against S. mutans. Introduction

Dental caries, a common oral disease, is primarily caused by the bacterium (Streptococcus mutans), which contributes to plaque formation and demineralization of tooth enamel.1 Increasing concern over antibiotic resistance has led to a growing interest in alternative treatments, particularly natural antimicrobial agents derived from plants.1 India, often celebrated as the land of spices, has a deep-rooted tradition in which many ingredients have a dual function.1 It enhances the flavor of food and has medicinal properties at the same time. Cloves and ginger have been central to traditional medicinal practices such as Ayurveda for many generations.2 Their therapeutic benefits are not only based on tradition, but are increasingly supported by modern research, which continues to explore the active ingredients that make these spices indispensable in both cooking and herbal remedies.3 Clove (Syzygium aromaticum), known for its aromatic flower buds, is used globally, especially in the Indian and Chinese systems of medicine.3 The main constituent of clove is eugenol, which is an antiseptic, anesthetic, and antimicrobial agent.3 Traditionally, clove was used to ease toothache, and clove oil remains a popular agent in dentistry for the relief of pain and infections, especially those caused by S. mutans, a key bacterium in the formation of dental plaque and dental caries.4 Ginger, also one of the most common spices, is widely used in Indian homes and traditional medicine for its medicinal properties.4 Due to its strong flavor, it prevents inflammation, enhances blood circulation, and calms nausea.4,5 Concerning oral health issues, the anti-inflammatory impact of ginger is beneficial during the treatment of the abscess, while the antimicrobial components have been found to inhibit S. mutans and the bacterium responsible for dental caries, which thrives in the acidic condition of the mouth.6 This bacterium breaks down sugar and demineralizes the enamel on the teeth. Ginger has an inhibitory effect on this bacterium's growth that prevents dental caries and improves the oral health status.6,7 Cloves and ginger have gained greater significance today because of human preference to seek natural substitutes rather than synthetic medicines.8 Due to growing antibiotic resistance and concerns with side effects from traditionally administered drugs, more individuals are adopting these safe and well-tested treatments.8

Burt and Chaieb et al. reported that clove exhibits inhibitory effect on the S. mutans.9,10 Clove also exhibits antibacterial and antifungal activity against S. mutans and Candida albicans.11 Studies showed that Zingiber officinale used efficaciously as an antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, anticancer, neuroprotective, cardiovascular protective, respiratory protective, antiobesity, antidiabetic, antinausea, and antiemetic agent.5,12Z. officinale is found to be an effective potential therapy to prevent and treat oral diseases including dental caries, aphthous ulcers, oral candidiasis, xerostomia, and gingivitis, by reducing the level of dental biofilms in a dose-dependent manner.13–18 A previous study reported that the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of ginger extract was only 1 mg/mL, with the inhibition activity against oral pathogens such as S. mutans.19 In an in vitro study, it was concluded that natural plant extracts such as clove and ginger effectively inhibited S. mutans vitality and capacity to form biofilms.20

Despite the findings from the literature, the comparative antimicrobial efficacy of ginger and clove extracts against S. mutans in an in vitro environment has been less explored. The increasing prevalence of dental caries coupled with the emergence of antimicrobial resistance emphasizes the need for novel, natural and cost-effective therapeutic agents. While synthetic antimicrobials are effective, their prolonged use is often associated with side effects such as altered oral microbiota and toxicity. Therefore, natural plant extracts such as ginger and clove represent a sustainable and accessible alternative. This study aims to compare and assess the antimicrobial activity of ginger and clove extracts against S. mutans.

Materials and Methods

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (CSP-III/24/JAN/01/04). This study was conducted in the Department of Microbiology and Pharmacognosy.

Preparation of Clove and Ginger Extract

Ginger and clove buds were purchased from the local market and dried in the sunlight, and then the dried ingredients were finely pulverized. These spices were identified and authenticated by the Department of Pharmacognosy. Hundred grams of each finely powdered ingredient was stored in sterile, dry, screw-capped bottles for 1 week prior to aqueous extraction. Ten grams of finely ground ginger and cloves were mixed with 10 mL of 70% ethanol. The mixture was shaken and stirred regularly and then allowed to stand for 48 hours. The resulting mixture was filtered.

Inoculum Preparation of the Test Organism

The strain of S. mutans [American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) 25175] was used in this study. S. mutans was subcultured in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth and incubated at 37°C for 18–24 hours. To test the antimicrobial activity of ginger and clove extracts, a 0.5 McFarland standard S. mutans broth was prepared.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing by Microbroth Dilution Method

The antimicrobial activity of ginger and clove extracts was evaluated using the microbroth dilution method in sterile 96-well microtiter plate. Initially, 200 µL of 100% concentration of the clove and ginger extracts were added to the first well, from the second well to the seventh well 100 µL of BHI broth was taken. Serial dilutions of the ginger and clove extracts in the wells were prepared by transferring 100 µL of extracts from the first well to the next and repeating the process for each subsequent wells, finally discarding 100 µL from the last well, resulted in obtaining the concentration of extracts in wells as 100%, 50%, 25%, 12.5%, 6.25%, 3.125% and 1.6% (1 to 7 wells) (Fig. 1). This step was followed by the addition of a drop of 106 CFU/ml of Streptococcus mutans culture suspension to all the wells. The test was performed in quadruplicate and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours and the inhibition of bacterial growth was assessed visually and spectrophotometrically at 600 nm using a microplate reader. The broth from all the wells 1 to 7 were subcultured into blood agar using calibrated loop to confirm the presence or absence of the growth by quantification of colonies after 24 hours of incubation of the subcultured plates.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was performed with the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS Version 26.0) software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, United States). Mean values, standard deviations, Z, and p values were calculated. A nonparametric paired test (Wilcoxon signed-rank) was used to compare the results between the two groups (p < 0.05).

Results

The antimicrobial activity of ginger and clove extracts against S. mutans was assessed using the microbroth dilution method at seven concentrations (100%, 50%, 25%, 12.5%, 6.25%, 3.125%, and 1.6%). Complete growth inhibition was observed at higher concentrations for both extracts.

Clove extract showed complete inhibition (0 CFU/mL) at concentrations of 100%, 50%, 25%, 12.5%, and 6.25%. Bacterial growth was first observed at 3.125% concentration with a mean CFU count of 7 (based on n = 4 replicates). At the lowest concentration tested (1.6%), growth continued with higher CFU counts.

Ginger extract demonstrated complete inhibition at concentrations of 100%, 50%, 25%, 12.5%, 6.25%, and 3.125%. Growth was first observed only at the lowest concentration of 1.6%.

Since 1.6% was the only concentration at which both extracts allowed comparable bacterial growth, statistical comparison was performed at this concentration. The lower median CFU count for ginger indicates stronger antimicrobial activity compared to clove at this concentration. However, the difference did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05), suggesting that both extracts demonstrate comparable antimicrobial efficacy under the tested conditions.

Discussion

The natural endowment of clove (S. aromaticum) and ginger (Z. officinale) to hinder S. mutans accentuates their importance as a natural substitute for processed medicines that are used to treat cariogenic infections in the oral cavity.21 Bioactive substances have been used to impart bacterial metabolism and cell wall integrity to produce their antimicrobial effects. The metabolic activity of bacteria is impacted by gingerols and shogaols.21 Eugenol has formidable bacterial effects that disrupt the cell membrane. A similar study by Paulraj et al. reported that S. mutans and other oral infections can be suppressed using plant-based components.22 The bacterial membranes and bacterial proteins present in them are deranged by photochemical components such as terpenoids, alkaloids, flavonoids, and tannins.23 By hindering their enzymatic pathways, ginger extracts have been shown in related research to inhibit oral pathogens such as Lactobacillus acidophilus and S. mutans.23,24 The immense antibacterial properties of clove (eugenol) extract are shown after interaction with the bacterial cell wall and changing the permeability.25 Focused their interest on the clove-modified formulations at different concentrations to know their antibacterial efficacy and concluded that significant antibacterial properties of clove oil against cariogenic microorganisms, reinforcing its effectiveness in promoting oral health.25 Also, authors have concluded that clove oil has strong antibacterial qualities against cariogenic microorganisms, confirming its ability to support improved dental health.25,26

Minimal inhibitory concentration was determined using the microbroth dilution method. The extracts in this investigation were diluted successively at 100%, 50%, 25%, 12.5%, 6.25%, 3.125%, and 1.6%. The antibacterial activity of the individual extracts was assessed by the above-mentioned dilution technique. The results of the present study show that both ginger and clove extracts exhibit antimicrobial activity against S. mutans at different concentrations. Although no statistically significant results were reported. Ginger exhibited better results by maintaining complete inhibition of growth up to 3.125% concentration, whereas clove showed bacterial growth starting at 3.125% concentration. At the comparable concentration of 1.6%, ginger demonstrated lower CFU counts (median 11.5) compared to clove (median 24), indicating stronger antimicrobial activity, though this difference was not statistically significant. It indicates that ginger extracts show stronger inhibition at lower concentrations when compared to clove. This was in accordance with the study conducted by Jain et al., where organic solvent-based ginger extracts exhibited a higher inhibition zone than other extracts.27 Despite the favorable results obtained from this study, the authors would like to acknowledge the limitations of the study. The plant extracts were prepared using a single solvent (70% ethanol), which does not capture the spectrum of antimicrobial compounds present in ginger and clove. The use of different extraction solvents, such as methanol or aqueous extraction, could assist in the detection of other bioactive compounds. Bansal et al. concluded that ginger and clove aqueous extract, using a combination of chemicals and solvents.11 As the method of producing solvent may affect the efficacy of bioactive compounds, the methodology is more important to note. The concentration of different mixtures and different solvents may produce different results. Only a particular concentration (3.125–1.6%) was used in this study to calculate the MIC, which aids in the dose–response relationship. This study used strains of S. mutans (ATCC 25175).

The study's merits include the use of a controlled in vitro investigation that enables a direct comparison of two widely used herbal extracts under the same experimental setup. After subculturing, blood agar and CFU counts are used, which offer a reliable and practical evaluation of antimicrobial activity. One concentration of ginger and two concentrations of clove were evaluated in this investigation, which limited the capacity to do dose–response comparisons. The p value suggests that the sample size may have been too small to identify minor but biologically important variations. Salivary flow, pH fluctuation, and biofilm dynamics are examples of in vivo oral circumstances that might not be adequately represented by the in vitro study. Future research should aim to explore a broader range of concentrations, standardize extract preparation methods, and conduct in vivo studies to confirm clinical relevance. In addition, the incorporation of inhibition zone assays could complement CFU analysis to provide a more comprehensive assessment of antimicrobial activity.

Conclusion

Both ginger and clove extracts demonstrated antimicrobial activity against S. mutans in this in vitro study. Ginger extract showed complete growth inhibition at a lower concentration (up to 3.125%) compared to clove extract (up to 6.25%). At the comparable concentration of 1.6%, ginger exhibited a lower median CFU count (11.5) compared to clove (24), suggesting potentially stronger antimicrobial activity. However, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.068), indicating comparable efficacy of the two extracts under the tested conditions. These findings suggest that both natural extracts may serve as viable alternatives for caries prevention, with ginger showing a slightly more promising antimicrobial profile that warrants further investigation at various concentrations and in clinical settings.
