Novel antioxidant peptides from chicken bone hydrolysate: Identification, structure–activity relationship and cytoprotective effects against oxidative stress in HaCaT cells
Authors: Yitong Jin, Juan Zhang, Junjian Miao, Zheng Peng, Yanan Liu, Zhijun Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The objective of this study was to identify antioxidant peptides from chicken bone hydrolysate, elucidate their molecular mechanisms, and investigate their protective effects against oxidative damage in HaCaT cells. Four novel antioxidant peptides—SGQLSGPIRFF, LSGPIRFF, LIVYPW, and IFPVHWQF—were identified using nano-LC–MS/MS. Molecular docking results indicated that these peptides can interact with Keap1, indicating their potential involvement in the Keap1–Nrf2 signaling pathway. LIVYPW exhibits the highest EHOMO (−5.6576 eV) and the smallest HOMO–LUMO energy gap (5.0042 eV), showing superior electron-donating ability compared with the other three peptides. Further analysis suggested that the active site of LIVYPW was potentially located at the O47 position of Trp-6. Additionally, these peptides significantly reduce H₂O₂-induced oxidative damage by reducing intracellular reactive oxygen species levels and enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity (SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px). Our results provide a preliminary scientific basis for the potential valorization of chicken bone-derived peptides.

Introduction

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are the by-products of aerobic metabolism. Their excessive accumulation triggers oxidative stress, which damages biomolecules and increases the risk of arthritis, diabetes and cancer (Sheng et al., 2022; Chen, Chen, et al., 2023). To reduce ROS-induced damage to the body, exogenous antioxidants are introduced to complement endogenous scavenging systems. Antioxidant peptides, a class of low-molecular-weight bioactive peptides, have attracted significant attention owing to their diverse sources, high potency, and ease of absorption (Mardani et al., 2023). They can mitigate oxidative stress through free radical scavenging, metal ion chelation, and inhibition of lipid peroxidation (Xu et al., 2024). Through the regulation of key antioxidant enzymes including glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT), antioxidant peptides reinforce the cellular antioxidant defense system. Moreover, they participate in glutathione metabolic pathways and modulate antioxidant-related gene expression (Mengli et al., 2025). Numerous studies have demonstrated that antioxidant peptides play an important role in reducing oxidative stress. Wang et al. (2023) extracted three bioactive peptides from camel bones, which exhibited remarkable DPPH radical scavenging capacity and shielded H2O2-induced HepG2 cells by efficiently eliminating ROS. Hu, Dou, et al. (2024) identified six novel antioxidant peptides in wine-making by-products that considerably mitigated oxidative stress in HaCaT cells through simultaneously upregulating antioxidant enzyme activity and reducing malondialdehyde (MDA) levels. Furthermore, the walnut meal–derived peptide—YWSPNDEQFR—competes with Keap1 for binding sites and inhibits Keap1-mediated Nrf2 degradation, thereby activating the Keap1/Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, and reducing oxidative damage to cells (Ouyang et al., 2025).

At present, the valorization of animal skeletal materials (e.g., from sheep, fish, donkey and cattle) for antioxidant peptides has been extensively documented(Hu et al., 2023; Lv et al., 2023; Wang, Hou, et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Recent studies have expanded to various chicken-derived products, including chicken bone(Jin et al., 2024), chicken blood(Zheng et al., 2018), chicken skin(Zou et al., 2025), and chicken meat(Babu et al., 2025), demonstrating their potential as sustainable sources of antioxidant peptides. The majority of existing investigations have focused on peptide identification followed by in vitro antioxidant verification. Zheng et al. (2018) identified the peptide AEDKKLIQ from chicken blood and validated its antioxidant capacity using ABTS radical scavenging assay. Zou et al. (2025) reported that the peptide VGPTGPAGPR derived from the enzymatically hydrolyzed chicken skin exhibited hydroxyl, ABTS, and DPPH radical scavenging activities. Chicken bones, a primary by-product of broiler processing, contain 14.7% protein and thus serve as an excellent raw material for preparing antioxidant peptides (Ji et al., 2025). Although preliminary studies have identified sequences such as EPDRY from black-bone silky fowl with strong ABTS radical scavenging activity and reducing power (Liu et al., 2013), their antioxidant mechanisms and structure–activity relationships remain poorly understood.

In the respect of mechanism research, bioinformatics has been growingly widespread and serves as an important method for interpreting structural and functional properties of molecules. Molecular docking is a computational simulation technique for predicting ligand-receptor interactions and identifying potential binding modes (Li, Hua, et al., 2022). Tonolo et al. (2020) investigated the interaction between Keap1 and milk-derived antioxidant peptides using docking analysis. This research showed that these peptides inhibit Nrf2–Keap1 binding by occupying the Nrf2 binding site within the Keap1 protein pocket. Furthermore, quantum chemistry, especially density functional theory (DFT), enables theoretical investigation of chemical properties and structure–activity relationships within antioxidant peptides (Wu et al., 2021). Analyses such as average local ionization energy (ALIE), frontier molecular orbitals, and electrostatic potential (ESP) are commonly employed to predict radical scavenging active sites and assess the electron-donating capacity of a molecule (Chen, Sheng, et al., 2025). Studies have shown that nitrogen and oxygen atoms, which have strong electron-donating properties, as well as their associated hydrogen atoms, are the primary contributors to radical scavenging (Wang, Fu, et al., 2024; Mubango et al., 2025). Mubango et al. (2025) predicted that the guanidinium group was the dominant active site on six peptides through DFT computations. In summary, the integration of these approaches may offer a novel strategy for analyzing the mechanism of action of antioxidant peptides.

Beyond theoretical simulations, cell-based experiments are widely used for evaluating antioxidant activity as they directly reflect the absorption, metabolism, and physiological responses of antioxidants within cells (Huo et al., 2022). Human immortalized keratinocyte (HaCaT) (Hu, Liu, et al., 2024), hepatocellular carcinoma (HepG2) (Hu et al., 2022), and colon cancer cells (Caco-2) (Kong et al., 2023) are commonly used as models to study oxidative damage. H₂O₂ and 2,2′-azobis(2-amidinopropane) dihydrochloride are the traditional inducers (Lin et al., 2024; Sheng et al., 2022). HaCaT cells have been widely used in studies on skin oxidative stress, photoaging, and toxicity mechanisms owing to their stable epidermal keratinocyte characteristics(Hseu et al., 2021). When compared with primary keratinocytes, this cell line sustains the expression of differentiation markers during passages and exhibits high sensitivity to oxidative damage, which makes it specifically suitable for simulating exogenous ROS or ultraviolet-induced oxidative damage (Calabrese et al., 2022).

Accordingly, this study aims to purify, characterize, and synthesize antioxidant peptides from chicken bone. Molecular docking and quantum chemistry were used to identify reactive sites and elucidate the reaction mechanism. The antioxidant properties of these peptides were simultaneously validated via conducting in vitro assays and using HaCaT cells as models. This study provides structural and functional insights for the sustainable utilization of chicken processing by-products.

Materials and methods

Materials

The tibia bones of white-feathered chicken were donated by Shengnong Development Co., Ltd. (Nanping, China). Alkaline protease with an activity of 500,000 U/g was purchased from GlpBio Technology Inc. (Montclair, USA). Human keratinocytes (HaCaT cells) were acquired from iCell Bioscience Inc. (Shanghai, China). ABTS radical scavenging assay kit was purchased from Suzhou Greeth Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). Penicillin–streptomycin, dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM), and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). ROS assay kit was obtained from Solarbio Life Sciences (Beijing, China). Assay kits for bicinchoninic acid, total SOD, GSH-Px, CAT, were sourced from Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from Gibco BRL Life Technologies (Gaithersburg, MD, USA). All other chemical reagents used in the experiment were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent (Shanghai, China).

Preparation and purification of chicken bone hydrolysate (CBH)

The chicken bone hydrolysate (CBH) were prepared using the previously established method (Jin et al., 2024). Briefly, the white-feathered chicken tibias were pulverized into a fine powder (<100 mesh). The resulting powder was then soaked in 10% (v/v) hexane for 12 h to remove the lipids (repeated three times). Subsequently, the defatted chicken bone powder was decalcified by immersion in 0.5 M HCl for 48 h. The decalcified powder was rinsed with distilled water until neutral pH and dispersed in distilled water to obtain a 10% (w/v) substrate suspension. Enzymatic hydrolysis was initiated by adding alkaline protease (502.75 U/g) and maintained at pH 9.5 and 48.5 °C for 1.13 h. The reaction was terminated by heating the mixture at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by centrifugation at 10000 ×g (15 min at 4 °C). The supernatant was permeated through a 3 kDa ultrafiltration membrane and the resulting filtrate (<3 kDa) was lyophilized and designated as CBH. CBH was subjected to purification on a Bio-Rad NGC system (Hercules, CA, USA) equipped with a Sephadex G-25 column (100 × 1.6 cm). A 0.22 μm membrane (GVS, Bologna, Italy) was used to filter the lyophilized CBH after it had been redissolved in ultrapure water at a concentration of 20 mg/mL. Prior to sample injection, the column was equilibrated in advance with ultrapure water for 30 min. The sample (300 μL) was then injected and eluted with ultrapure water at 1.2 mL/min. The effluent was monitored in real time at 214 nm, and the eluates were collected as 1 mL fractions each. Based on their peak profiles, the eluates were pooled into four fractions (A, B, C, and D) and lyophilized for further use. The antioxidant capacities of them were determined and the most active fraction was selected for futher purification. Fraction D, displaying the strongest antioxidant activity after gel filtration chromatography, was subjected to further purification using a reverse-phase C18 column (700 mm × 25 mm, 10 μm) (Hu, Dou, et al., 2024). To prepare for loading, the freeze-dried fraction D was dissolved in ultrapure water to prepare a 10 mg/mL solution for loading (loading volume: 10 mL). The mobile phase system was composed of phase A (0.1% trifluoroacetic acid aqueous solution) and phase B (acetonitrile), with a flow rate of 20 mL/min. Elution was performed using a linear gradient as follows: 0–10 min, 10% B; 10–20 min, 20% B; 20–30 min, 30% B; and 30–40 min, 40% B. At 214 nm, the eluate's absorbance was measured. Five fractions (D1, D2, D3, D4, and D5) were collected according to the elution time, then the antioxidant activities of them were tested.

Antioxidant activity measurements

ABTS radical scavenging activity assay

A commercially available ABTS assay kit was used to measure the ABTS radical scavenging activity. The ABTS working solution was prepared by combining equal volumes of ABTS and oxidant solutions, incubating in the dark at 25 °C for 16 h, and diluting with distilled water to an absorbance of 1.40 ± 0.05 at 405 nm. Subsequently, 7 μL of the sample was added to 280 μL of the working solution and the mixture was kept at 25 °C for 2–6 min. A blank was prepared using 7 μL of distilled water in place of the sample. The absorbance of the mixtures was recorded at 405 nm. A Trolox solution with a concentration range of 0.05–1.5 mM was the standard, and the results were expressed in μM per g peptide.

Assessing the chelating capacity of Fe2+

The chelating capacity of Fe2+ was assessed using a previously described method with slight modifications (Zhang et al., 2018). In brief, 1.0 mL of the sample was mixed with 4.0 mL of FeCl₂ (2 mmol/L) and 3.6 mL of distilled water and incubated at 25 °C for 30 s. Next, 0.1 mL of ferrozine (5 mmol/L) was added to initiate the reaction. The absorbance was measured at 562 nm after reacting at 25 °C for 10 min. The results were calculated based on the standard curve of Fe2+ (0.05–1.5 mM) and were expressed in μM Fe2+ per g peptide.

Hydroxyl radical scavenging activity assay

The assay for hydroxyl radical scavenging was assessed according to a previously published method, incorporating minor modifications (Wang et al., 2023). Briefly, the sample was mixed at an equal volume ratio (1:1:1:1) with 9 mmol/L salicylic acid, 9 mmol/L FeSO₄, and 8.8 mmol/L H₂O₂. The mixture was incubated in a water bath at 37 °C for 30 min, and then its absorbance was measured at 510 nm. In the control group, the sample was replaced with ddH₂O. The salicylic acid solution was replaced with ddH₂O and used as the blank group. The scavenging rate was calculated as follows:(1)Hydroxyl radical scavenging activity%=Ac+Ab−As/Ac×100where Ac, Ab, and As denote the absorbance of the control, blank, and sample groups, respectively.

Determination of reducing power

The sample's reducing power was calculated following the procedure described in an earlier study (Moon & Cho, 2023). 1.0 mL of the sample was incubated together with 1.0 mL of phosphate buffer (0.2 M, pH 6.6) and potassium ferricyanide (1%, w/v). The mixture was incubated at 50 °C for 20 min, after which 1.0 mL of 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid was added to it. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 10 min. 2.0 mL of the supernatant was blended with 2.0 mL of distilled water and 0.8 mL of 0.1% (w/v) ferric chloride in a test tube and then incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. The absorbance of the final reaction solution was measured at 700 nm.

Identification of CBH using nano-LC–MS/MS

The fraction D5 that exhibited the strongest antioxidant activity was identified using nano-LC–MS/MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Separation was performed on a Q Exactive mass spectrometer connected online to an Easy-nLC 1200 UHPLC system fitted with an Acclaim PepMap RSLC C18 column (75 μm × 25 cm, 2 μm, 100 Å; Dionex, USA). The mobile phase was composed of phase A (0.1% formic acid aqueous solution) and phase B (0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile). Linear gradient elution was performed under the following program: 0–20 min, 5–38% B; 20–22 min, 38–100% B; and 22–30 min, 100% B, with a 2 μL injection volume. Capillary temperature and spray voltage were calibrated to 275 °C and 1.9 kV, respectively. A maximum injection time of 100 ms was adopted to acquire full MS scan data in the mass range of 350 to 2000 m/z at a resolution of 70,000. Data-dependent mass spectra were obtained using high-resolution MS scans and MS/MS analysis of the most intense precursors. It was fragmented via higher-energy collisional dissociation. The acquired spectra were processed with PEAKS Online 11 software (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc., Canada), and the peptide was identified against the Gallus gallus UniProt database, with mass tolerances set as 10 ppm for precursor ions and 0.03 Da for fragments. No enzyme specificity was observed while searching. To ensure high confidence in peptide identification and minimize false positives, a significance score threshold of -10log P ≥ 20 was applied.

Molecular docking

Potential binding modes of the antioxidant peptides SF-11, LF-8, LW-6, and IF-8 with the Keap1 protein were identified via molecular docking. The crystal structure of the Keap1 (PDB ID: 2FLU) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/). The Keap1 protein was prepared by removing water molecules, adding hydrogen atoms, and extracting the native ligand in PyMOL 2.6 (San Carlos, CA, USA) according to a previously described method (Guo et al., 2024). The 3D structures of the peptide ligands were constructed using ChemDraw 21.0.0 (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA), and their geometries were optimized using energy minimization. In addition, the key binding motif DEETGE derived from Nrf2 was selected as the reference ligand, and its 3D structure was constructed and optimized under the same conditions. Subsequently, the receptor and ligands were converted to PDBQT format using AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 (San Carlos, CA, USA). To cover the active binding site, the grid box was identified as center_x: 6.3, center_y: 6.5, and center_z: 9.1 with dimensions size_x: 28.9, size_y: 26.4, and size_z: 29.5. Semiflexible docking was conducted, with the binding energy being the key evaluation metric (Yang et al., 2018). The resulting 3D and 2D interaction diagrams were visualized using PyMOL 2.6 and BIOVIA Discovery Studio program (client version 2021, San Diego, CA, USA).

Quantum chemical calculations

All quantum chemical computations were performed on Gaussian 16 W (Gaussian Inc., USA), employing the B3LYP functional in conjunction with Grimme's D3(BJ) dispersion correction. Geometry optimization and frequency computations were carried out at the 6-31G* level. While single-point energy calculations were performed on the larger def2-TZVP basis set. The water solvation effect was simultaneously taken into account using the polarizable continuum model (PCM) (Lu, 2024). Fukui functions were derived using the conceptual DFT framework. Using the orbital-weighted Fukui function, the f+(r), f−(r), and f0(r) isosurfaces were obtained (Chen, Xia, & Liang, 2025). The molecular ESP and ALIE projected onto the van der Waals surface were quantitatively analyzed (Lu & Chen, 2012). All isosurface maps were visualized on VMD (Humphrey et al., 1996).

Peptide synthesis

Four novel antioxidant peptides (SF-11, LF-8, LW-6, and IF-8) were synthesized by China Peptides Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) via the Fmoc solid-phase method. The synthetic peptides were obtained with a purity of >98%. As explained in Section 2.3, the antioxidant activities of synthesized peptides were assessed.

Protective effects of antioxidant peptides against H₂O₂-induced oxidative damage in HaCaT cells

Culture of cells

HaCaT cells were grown at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ in DMEM supplemented with 10% (w/v) FBS and 1% (w/v) penicillin–streptomycin solution.

Establishment of H2O2-induced oxidative stress model

HaCaT cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h in complete medium. An oxidative damage model was established by further culturing the cells in serum-free medium for 24 h, followed by a 2 h exposure to H₂O₂ (500–2500 μM). The success of the model was confirmed by a reduction in cell viability measured by the CCK-8 assay.

Cytoprotective effect assay

The cytotoxicity of synthetic peptides (50, 250, 500, 800, and 1000 μg/mL) was first assessed using the CCK-8. To examine their protective potential, HaCaT cells were pretreated with different concentrations of synthesized peptides for 22 h prior to a 2-h exposure to H₂O₂ (1 mM). Cells without H₂O₂ treatment served as the control group, while those subjected to H₂O₂ injury without peptide pretreatment constituted the model group.

Assessment of HaCaT cells' ROS, SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px levels

By employing the 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescent probe, intracellular ROS levels were determined. Images were acquired using a laser confocal microscope (Olympus FV1200, Japan) at excitation and emission wavelengths of 488 nm and 525 nm, respectively (Ouyang et al., 2025). Commercial assay kits were used to assess ROS, SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px activities in compliance with the manufacturer's protocols (Zhang et al., 2025).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results were provided as mean ± standard deviation. The data were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance using SPSS 22.0, followed by Duncan's multiple range test for post hoc comparisons. Differences with a p-value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Isolation and purification of antioxidant peptides from chicken bone

Gel filtration chromatography

Based on molecular size differences, gel filtration chromatography is a highly effective separation method that is commonly utilized in peptide separation, buffer exchange, and desalting. High-molecular-weight components eluted first in gel filtration chromatography, while smaller components eluted later (Zhang et al., 2019). Herein, CBH was separated using fast protein liquid chromatography on a Sephadex G-25 column (separation range: 1000–5000 Da), which yielded four distinct fractions (A, B, C, and D) (Fig. 1a). Subsequently, the antioxidant activities of fractions A, B, C, and D were measured (Fig. 1b). Fraction D, which had the lowest molecular weight, demonstrated the highest ABTS radical scavenging activity (211.78 ± 4.50 μM TE/g, p < 0.05) and Fe2+ chelating capacity (46.72 ± 1.50 μM/g, p < 0.05). These observations aligned with those reported in previous studies. Wang et al. (2021) separated cottonseed peptides using size exclusion chromatography. The low-molecular-weight fraction (CPH3–2) exhibited the most significant ABTS radical scavenging activity. Reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography

RP-HPLC operates based on the principle of hydrophobic interactions, where components with high hydrophobicity and low molecular weight exhibit longer retention times(Wen et al., 2020). Since the antioxidant activity of peptides is closely associated with their hydrophobic amino acid content, elution time can provide insights into their antioxidant potential (Kong et al., 2023). Herein, RP-HPLC was employed to further separate and purify component D, which separated into five fractions designated D1–D5 (Fig. 1c). Among them, D5 presented the highest antioxidant activity (Fig. 1d), with ABTS radical scavenging capacity and Fe2+ chelating activity of 494.46 ± 6.05 μM TE/g and 86.68 ± 2.00 μM/g, respectively. These values were significantly greater than those of the other four fractions. Compared with its precursor component D, D5 exhibited 133.48% and 95.75% higher ABTS radical scavenging and Fe2+ chelating capacities, respectively. Therefore, component D5 was selected for further sequence identification.

Identification of amino acid sequences in purified peptide fragments

The peptide fragments within D5 were identified using nano-LC–MS/MS to examine the connection between its primary structure and antioxidant activity. Notably, 1215 chicken bone peptides (CBPs) were identified (Table S1), of which about 85% were composed of 6–12 amino acid residues (Fig.S1). The top four identified peptide sequences were SGQLSGPIRFF (SF-11), LSGPIRFF (LF-8), LIVYPW (LW-6), and IFPVHWQF (IF-8). These four peptides accounted for 50.11% of the total peak area and were the major components identified in fraction D5. In addition, no identical sequences were documented in the BIOPEP-UWM database (https://biochemia.uwm.edu.pl/biopep-uwm/). The antioxidant activity of peptides was found to be closely correlated with the composition of their amino acid sequences, molecular weight, ratio of hydrophobic to aromatic amino acids, and the structure of their C-terminal and N-terminal residues (Harnedy et al., 2017). Stronger antioxidant activity was frequently observed in peptides with smaller molecular weights (Xia et al., 2022). Herein, the identified peptides were composed of 6–22 amino acid residues with their molecular weights ranging from 596.30 to 2763.31 Da, in line with the properties of typical antioxidant peptides (Sarmadi & Ismail, 2010). Moreover, hydrophobic amino acids, including Val, Phe, Ala, Met, Trp, Pro, Leu, and Ile, play crucial roles in radical scavenging (Górska-Warsewicz et al., 2018; Li, Wu, et al., 2022). In this study, each of the four peptides contains hydrophobic amino acids, with their content ranging from 45.45% (SGQLSGPIRFF) to 83.33% (LIVYPW). Also, the positive grand average of hydropathy (GRAVY) values confirmed their hydrophobic characteristic. Additionally, N-terminal Leu and Ile could raise the peptide concentration at the water/lipid interface, thereby enhancing radical scavenging activity (Wang et al., 2015). Furthermore, numerous repeating amino acid sequences, such as Ala-Ala (AA), Leu-Leu (LL), Val-Val (VV), and Phe-Phe (FF), were identified within these peptide sequences. These “dipeptides” exhibited superior biological activity when compared with free amino acids and showed rapid absorption (Siow & Gan, 2013). Numerous aromatic amino acids, such as Trp, Tyr, and Phe were also found in this study. Through the resonance effects of their aromatic rings, aromatic amino acids have the ability to stabilize radicals and eliminate free radicals by giving protons to electron-deficient radicals (Sarmadi & Ismail, 2010; Zaky et al., 2022). In addition, the sulfhydryl group present in Cys exhibits antioxidant properties. His-containing peptides could trap lipid peroxyl radicals, and the carboxyl groups in the side chains of Glu and Asp could chelate metal ions. Moreover, the position of amino acid residues and the structure of peptides are significant factors in influencing antioxidant activity (Ajibola et al., 2011). All four of the peptides found in this investigation demonstrated antioxidant qualities, according to the structure–activity relationships of antioxidant peptides that have been published (Fig. S3). Potential reaction sites need to be further elucidated through quantum chemistry computations and molecular docking.

Molecular docking simulation of the four peptides using Keap1

The Keap1–Nrf2 pathway serves as a key regulator of cellular defense mechanisms against oxidative stress. Within this signaling pathway, Nrf2 is a pivotal regulatory factor, which protected cells by activating antioxidant mechanisms. Under normal conditions, Keap1 mediates the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of Nrf2, thereby suppressing its activity(Sinha et al., 2025). The binding of exogenous antioxidant peptides and other activating compounds to Keap1 blocks the interaction between Keap1 and Nrf2. This allows Nrf2 to translocate to the cell nucleus, where it activates the expression of downstream antioxidant and cell-protective genes. This process enhances cellular antioxidant capacity and consequently boosts the overall antioxidant defenses of the body (Boyenle et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2020). The DEETGE motif is recognized as the high-affinity sequence for the Kelch domain of Keap1 (Begnini et al., 2020; Lo et al., 2006). Therefore, this sequence was used as the reference ligand in this molecular docking.

Binding energy is a key parameter for evaluating the binding capacity between Keap1 (receptor) and antioxidant peptides (ligands). A more negative binding energy value indicated stronger interactions between the receptor and the ligand (Wei et al., 2024). Generally, values below −7.0 kcal/mol suggest a strong binding affinity (Chen, et al., 2023). Herein, the docking binding energies of SF-11, LF-8, LW-6, and IF-8 were − 7.9, −7.6, −8.1, and − 7.9 kcal/mol, respectively, all indicating favorable receptor–ligand interactions (Mudgil et al., 2022). In comparison, the binding affinity of DEETGE was −7.4 kcal/ mol. Therefore, these results demonstrated that four peptides could effectively bind to the active pocket of Keap1, suggesting competitive binding capabilities.

As shown in Fig.2a, DEETGE was stably embedded in the active pocket of Keap1 and formed eleven hydrogen bonds with Arg380, Arg415, Gln530, Ser555, Asn414, Leu365, Leu557, Val512 and Ser602. Further analysis revealed that all four peptides were embedded within the active cavity of Keap1 forming stable conformations (Fig. 2b–e). Hydrogen bonds, carbon–hydrogen bonds, and π bonds were the main binding forces between antioxidant peptides and the protein. Among those, hydrogen bonding is the most prevalent. Thirteen hydrogen bonds were formed between SF-11 and the following amino acids in the Keap1 protein: Arg415, Arg483, Arg380, Asn414, Asn382, Ser602, Ile416, Ala510, Asn 414, Leu557 and Gly433. LF-8 formed six hydrogen bonds with the Keap1 protein at Arg415, Ser508, Asn414 and Tyr572. LW-6 formed six hydrogen bonds with Arg415, Arg483, Arg380, Asn414, Ser555 and Ser602. Meanwhile, IF-8 formed ten hydrogen bonds with Arg415, Arg483, Arg380, Asn382, Ser602 and Gln 530. Of these, Arg380 exhibited the highest number of interactions with the four peptides. Specifically, Arg380 formed two hydrogen bonds with SF-11, one hydrogen bond and one π-cation interaction with LW-6, and four hydrogen bonds with IF-8. Arg415 formed two hydrogen bonds with LF-8, one hydrogen bond with SF-11, one hydrogen bond and one hydrophobic interaction with LW-6, and one hydrogen bond with IF-8. Notably, the binding residues of the four peptides were highly consistent with those involved in DEETGE–Keap1 interactions, particularly Arg380, Arg415, Ser602 and Asn414, which was in line with the results published by Wang et al. Quantum chemical analysis of the four peptides

To further investigate the relationship between peptide structure and antioxidant activity, this study employed quantum chemical methods to calculate and analyze the conformations and potential active sites of the relevant peptides.

Front-line analysis orbitals are frequently employed in quantum chemical calculations to evaluate molecular reactivity and stability. The energy of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) is a key indicator, where a higher EHOMO value (−5.6576 eV) suggests a greater tendency to donate electrons (Gui et al., 2023). When compared with the other three peptides, LW-6 exhibited a considerably higher EHOMO value, indicating its superior electron transfer capacity and radical scavenging potential. HOMO orbitals, being the electron-rich centers, exerted antioxidant effects by donating electrons or trapping radicals, thereby forming stable structures (Mubango et al., 2025). Specifically, the HOMO distribution for SF-11 and LF-8 concentrated on the guanidino group of Arg. For LW-6 and IF-8, HOMO was primarily distributed on the indole rings of Trp, indicating the susceptibility of these sites to electron loss while interacting with radicals, which was consistent with the findings reported by Wang et al. (2025).

The HOMO–lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy gap (ΔE) is another crucial indicator for characterizing molecular bioactivity. A larger ΔE typically indicates higher molecular stability and a lower probability of electron excitation from the HOMO to the LUMO, while a smaller ΔE corresponds to higher chemical reactivity (He et al., 2024). The ΔE values for SF-11, LF-8, LW-6, and IF-8 were 5.5711, 5.5948, 5.0042, and 5.0222 eV, respectively, indicating that LW-6 exhibited the highest reactivity, followed by IF-8, SF-11, and LF-8 (Fig. 3a).Fig. 3(a) HOMO, LUMO orbitals and energy gap between HOMO and LUMO for four antioxidant peptides. The Fukui function, ESP, and ALIE are also utilized to assess and analyze the reactivity of molecular regions (Fan et al., 2022). Here, f+(r), f−(r), and f0(r) denote the nucleophilic, electrophilic, and radical attack indices, respectively, with higher f(r) values indicating increased reactivity. Electrophilic reagents and radical attack are most likely to occur in regions with the highest negative ESP and the lowest ALIE (Chen, Xia, et al., 2025). The most negative ESP regions for LF-8 and LW-6 were located at N42 of Arg6 and O47 of Trp6, respectively, which was consistent with the HOMO analysis (Fig. 3b). Concurrently, LF-8 inhibited its maximum f−(r) value at the guanidino group N42 of the Arg terminus (Table S2). These findings indicated that N42 was a potent nucleophilic site. This could arise from the atoms in indole structure possessing small dihedral angles within a coplanar plane, forming a highly delocalized and polarizable π-conjugated system that exerts antioxidant effects by releasing reactive hydrogen (Zheng et al., 2016). Similarly, Qi et al. observed higher f0(r) values for the guanidino group N168–N171 of P1 derived from defatted walnuts, suggesting that the arginine guanidino group was the key active site for P1 radical reactions (Qi et al., 2025). Furthermore, the lowest ALIE values for SF-11 and IF-8 were located on the C atoms of the phenyl group at Phe-10 (0.3172 eV) and the C atoms of the phenyl group at Phe-8 (0.3156 eV), respectively (Fig. 3c). The delocalized π electron system of the phenyl group in Phe was an electron reservoir that could quench radicals via a single-electron transfer mechanism.

Synthesis and antioxidant activity analysis of the four peptides

The molecular docking and quantum chemical calculations indicated that SF-11, LF-8, LW-6, and IF-8 exhibited potent antioxidant capabilities. Herein, the antioxidant properties of the four peptides were assessed in vitro after they were created using solid-phase synthesis. As shown in Fig.4, all peptides exhibited a dose-dependent increase in antioxidant activity within the tested concentration range. Among them, LW-6 displayed the highest ABTS radical scavenging capacity, reducing power and Fe2+ chelating capacity, all of which were significantly higher than those observed for the other three peptides (p < 0.05). In addition, LF-8 exhibited the highest hydroxyl radical scavenging activity, reaching 68.94 ± 0.52% at 10 mg/mL. These findings supported the above-mentioned assumptions by showing that the synthesized peptides had antioxidant qualities in vitro. Protective effects of synthetic peptides against cellular oxidative damage

Conducting cell-based experiments is essential for evaluating antioxidant activities of peptides. Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) is the most prevalent oxidative stress inducer, which is capable of permeating cell membranes and triggering programmed cell death (Wen et al., 2020). We assessed the impact of different doses of H₂O₂ and synthetic peptides on HaCaT cell survival using the CCK-8 test in order to clarify the cellular antioxidant mechanisms of the four peptides. The viability of cells gradually declined as the concentration of H₂O₂ increased (Fig. 5a). At 1000 μM H₂O₂, HaCaT cell viability was 77.02 ± 2.10%, indicating a sub-lethal oxidative stress condition suitable for assessing peptide-mediated cytoprotection (Sun & Chen, 2025). In contrast, a further elevation to 1200 μM rapidly declined cell viability to 28.81 ± 0.90%, reflecting irreversible harm to cells (Ouyang et al., 2025). Therefore, 1000 μM H₂O₂ was chosen as the optimal concentration to induce oxidative stress in the experimental model. As shown in Fig. 5b, treatment with all four synthetic peptides resulted in a dose-dependent increase in cell viability. At 500 μg/mL, the cell viability reached 114.27 ± 3.59%, 111.62 ± 2.99%, 99.62 ± 1.60%, and 98.36 ± 5.17%, respectively. When the synthetic peptides concentration exceeded 1000 μg/mL, cell viability slightly decreased but remained above 80%, suggesting no significant cytotoxicity (Lin et al., 2024). To investigate the protective effects of this pretreatment, the HaCaT cells were pretreated with synthetic peptides before being exposed to H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress. A marked reduction in cell viability was observed in the H₂O₂-treated control group (p < 0.05), indicating cellular damage induced by oxidative stress (Fig. 5c). HaCaT cells pretreated with all four peptides showed a substantial increase in cell viability compared with the model group (77.10 ± 1.08%) (p < 0.05). Effects of synthetic peptides on ROS in H₂O₂-injured HaCaT cells

Intracellular ROS levels were indicative of cellular oxidative stress (Xia et al., 2022). Herein, the ROS probe DCFH-DA was used to assess intracellular oxidative stress levels. Treatment with 1000 μM H₂O₂ significantly increased intracellular ROS levels in HaCaT cells by 1.35-fold compared to the control (Fig. 5d). This indicated that H₂O₂ treatment induced oxidative stress in HaCaT cells and led to ROS accumulation. Following treatment with synthetic peptides, the ROS levels exhibited a significant reduction in comparison to the model group (p < 0.05). The ROS content progressively decreased with increasing synthetic peptide, exhibiting a concentration-dependent manner. LW-6 and IF-8 treatment groups exhibited higher ROS scavenging capacity than SF-11 and LF-8. At 200 μg/mL, fluorescence intensity in the SF-11 and LF-8 treatment groups decreased by 26.10 ± 4.32% and 33.09 ± 1.77%, respectively, compared to the model group. Fluorescence imaging revealed similar findings (Fig. 5e). Cells treated with 200 μg/mL LW-6 and IF-8 demonstrated weaker fluorescence signals when compared with those of SF-11-treated and LF-8-treated groups, indicating superior ROS scavenging capacity. These findings demonstrated that all four synthetic peptides effectively attenuated H₂O₂-induced intracellular ROS production. Similar observations have been reported have reported that peptides derived from deer bone (Chen, Xia, et al., 2023) and dry-cured ham (Xie et al., 2024) protect HaCaT cells from oxidative damage by scavenging ROS. These findings consistently implied that the synthesized peptides may directly scavenge ROS to alleviate cellular oxidative stress caused by H₂O₂.

The main components of endogenous antioxidant enzyme system comprise SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px. By regulating superoxide levels, these enzymes prevent oxidative damage to cells (Leying et al., 2024). To accommodate the sensitivity of different assay systems, this study employed different concentrations of synthetic peptides (50, 250, 500 μg/mL) for measuring antioxidant enzyme activity compared to ROS. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that CBPs possess potent antioxidant activity. We identified four major peptides—SF-11, LF-8, LW-6, and IF-8 via nano-LC–MS/MS. Molecular docking simulations revealed that all four peptides bind to the active pockets within the Keap1 protein. Specifically, hydrogen bonding was identified as a dominant interaction in the peptide–Keap1 complex, with residues Arg415 and Arg380 potentially playing key roles in stabilizing the binding. Quantum chemistry–based structure–activity relationship analysis indicated that LW-6 exhibited higher reactivity and antioxidant potential than the other three peptides, with its active site potentially located at the O47 position of Trp-6. Further validation at in vitro and cellular levels confirmed that LW-6 exhibited the highest ABTS radical scavenging capacity, reducing power, and Fe2+ chelating capacity. All four antioxidant peptides protected HaCaT cells from H₂O₂-induced oxidative damage by reducing intracellular ROS accumulation and enhancing the activities of CAT, SOD, and GSH-Px. As natural antioxidants, CBPs possess a wide range of potential applications in functional foods and health supplements, contributing to enhanced human health and disease prevention. Future research would focus on evaluating their in vivo effects in animal models as well as validating the underlying mechanisms, to better assess their potential applicability in functional foods.
