Impact of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol-lowering therapy on intermediate stenosis in non-culprit vessels of acute coronary syndrome
Authors: Kazuyoshi Kakehi, Masafumi Ueno, Masaru Kayama, Nobuhiro Yamada, Kyohei Onishi, Keishiro Sugimoto, Yohei Funauchi, Takayuki Kawamura, Kosuke Fujita, Hiroki Matsuzoe, Koichiro Matsumura, Gaku Nakazawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAssociations between optimal low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) level and changes in coronary flow and plaque characteristics are unclear. Therefore, we examined the effect of LDL-C lowering changes lipid-rich coronary plaques and flow using near-infrared spectroscopy-intravascular ultrasound (NIRS-IVUS) and quantitative flow ratio (QFR) in non-culprit vessels of patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS).MethodsWe prospectively examined 72 patients with ACS who underwent NIRS-IVUS for intermediate stenosis in non-culprit vessels at baseline and at follow-up. According to patients’ LDL-C levels at follow-up, they were classified into two groups. Patients with LDL-C levels < 55 mg/dL were categorized into the very low LDL-C group (VL group), while those with LDL-C levels ≥ 55 mg/dL were categorized into the low LDL-C group (L group). Changes in the lesion lipid core burden index (LCBI) and maximum 4-mm lipid core burden index (maxLCBI4mm) were assessed using NIRS-IVUS. Coronary flow was assessed using the QFR. The differences (Δ) between each value at follow-up and at baseline were determined.ResultsThe median LDL-C level at follow-up was 48 (42–53) mg/dL in the VL group and 64 (60–68) mg/dL in the L group. There was a strong correlation between reduction of maxLCBI4mm and improvement of QFR (R2 = 0.58). The VL group showed a significant decrease in maxLCBI4mm along with improvement in QFR value compared with the L group (P = 0.003; P = 0.008, respectively). The proportion of plaques with a maxLCBI4mm ≥ 400 (18.1% vs. 53.6%, P = 0.002) and the lesion LCBI (35.5(8.25–74) vs. 88.5(42.8–132), P = 0.03) at follow-up was lower in the VL group than in the L group. Plaques with a maxLCBI4mm ≥ 400 at baseline changed more frequently to a maxLCBI4mm < 400 at follow-up in the VL group than in the L group (36% vs. 4%, P = 0.001).ConclusionAggressive LDL-C-lowering therapy for non-culprit intermediate stenosis in ACS appears to promote plaque stabilization and improve coronary flow.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05395-z.

Introduction

The composition of atherosclerotic plaques greatly influences their progression and the potential to trigger acute coronary events [1]. Previous studies have reported that intermediate stenosis in non-culprit lesions is present in more than 50% of patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS), and non-culprit lesions in ACS are more likely to contain vulnerable plaques than those in chronic coronary syndrome [2]. Such vulnerable plaques can be assessed by near-infrared spectroscopy—intravascular ultrasound (NIRS-IVUS), and the lipid burden assessed by NIRS-IVUS is associated with an increased risk of subsequent adverse cardiovascular events [3]. Additionally, vulnerable plaques identified by IVUS and optical coherence tomography are correlated with disruption of coronary flow [4, 5]. Although this disruption contributes to myocardial ischemia and may independently predict poor clinical outcomes in patients with cardiovascular disease, few reports have examined whether plaque stabilization induced by low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C)–lowering therapy improved coronary flow. Statin therapy has been shown to reduce adverse cardiovascular events in patients with atherosclerotic diseases [6], and lowering LDL-C levels can lead to plaque stabilization in non-culprit vessels in ACS [7]. According to the European society of cardiology (ESC) guideline, the current treatment goal for secondary prevention is to achieve LDL-C levels < 55 mg/dL [8]. In patients with acute coronary syndrome, it is reported that vulnerable plaques frequently exists in multiple lesions which increases the risk of subsequent atherothrombotic events, and these patients derive early clinical benefits from early and effect of LDL-C lowering [2, 9]. Effect of LDL-C lowering is particularly important for patients with acute coronary syndrome and can reduce the risk of recurrence events. However, previous studies have reported that achieving LDL-C levels < 70 mg/dL might be difficult in real-world settings [10], and the clinical significance of achieving LDL-C levels < 55 mg/dL has not been well verified.

This study aimed to clarify whether achieving LDL-C levels < 55 mg/dL has a different impact on plaque characteristics as assessed by NIRS-IVUS and on coronary physiology as assessed by quantitative flow ratio (QFR), compared with LDL-C levels ≥ 55 mg/dL, in non-culprit vessels of patients with ACS.

Methods

Study population and design

In this prospective observational study, we enrolled patients who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for ACS and were found to have intermediate stenosis in non-culprit vessels as evaluated by NIRS-IVUS. Each patient underwent a clinical surveillance and an NIRS-IVUS study at baseline and at 12–18 months of follow-up, and changes of plaque characteristics as well as coronary flow assessed by QFR. The patients underwent treatment with the goal of achieving an LDL-C levels < 70 mg/dL; however, a few patients did not reach this target despite intensive therapy. The patients were further divided into two groups: the very low LDL-C therapy group (VL group, LDL-C levels < 55 mg/dL) and the low LDL-C therapy group (L group, LDL-C levels ≥ 55 mg/dL). The patients were recruited in the Division of Cardiology at Kindai University Hospital. They were enrolled if they met all of the following inclusion criteria: (1) admission for ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, and unstable angina pectoris; (2) intermediate stenosis in non-culprit lesions on coronary angiography (CAG); (3) NIRS-IVUS evaluation for intermediate stenosis after PCI; and (4) each patient underwent a clinical evaluation and follow-up CAG with NIRS-IVUS at 12–18 months later. Patients were excluded if any of the following criteria were encountered: (1) chronic kidney disease as shown by an estimated glomerular filtration rate < 30 ml/min/1.73 m2; (2) presentation with hemodynamic instability; (3) severe valvular heart disease; (4) left main trunk lesions; (5) lesions that could not be evaluated by NIRS-IVUS for any reason (e.g., tortuous vessels, severe stenosis, and calcification that made catheter passage difficult); (6) poor image quality that resulted in an inability to perform an NIRS-IVUS analysis; and (7) lesions that could not be evaluated by follow-up CAG with NIRS-IVUS. A non-culprit vessel was defined as intermediate stenosis with no history of PCI and no PCI until follow-up. Intermediate lesions were defined as those with 25%–75% diameter stenosis on quantitative coronary angiography (QCA). One non-culprit vessel per patient was evaluated. In cases with multiple intermediate lesions, we selected the lesion with the greatest degree of stenosis based on QCA for NIRS-IVUS imaging. CAG was performed via a radial or femoral artery approach using a 6 Fr sheath and catheter. Three major vessels—the left anterior descending artery, left circumflex artery, and right coronary artery—were examined by NIRS-IVUS after treating the culprit lesion in ACS. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kindai University Hospital (Reference No. R03-195). This trial registration number was UMIN000055586 and was listed in https://rctportal.mhlw.go.jp/detail/um?trial_id=UMIN000055586. NIRS imaging

The NIRS system used in this study consisted of a 3.2 F rapid exchange catheter, pullback and rotation device, and a console (Dual pro™; Infraredx, Bedford, MA, USA). Images were automatically pulled back from the most distal site of the target artery at a rate of 1.0 mm/s and 1800 rpm (Dual pro). Areas of the artery with lipid-rich plaques appeared in yellow within the image map (called a chemogram). The chemogram data were analyzed using the Makoto® system (Infraredx) [11]. The lipid core burden index (LCBI) and maximum 4-mm lipid core burden index (maxLCBI4mm) were measured at the target lesions using NIRS-IVUS. The vulnerable plaques were defined as a maxLCBI4mm ≥ 400 [3]. The measurement of maxLCBI4mm at follow-up was performed at the identical anatomical site of the baseline maxLCBI4mm.

The ΔmaxLCBI4mm and ΔLCBI represent the change in maxLCBI4mm and LCBI, respectively, and were measured as the difference between the value at follow-up and at baseline (i.e., ΔmaxLCBI4mm = maxLCBI4mm at follow-up; maxLCBI4mm at baseline, ΔLCBI = LCBI at follow-up–LCBI at baseline).

IVUS analysis was done offline using EchoPlaque software (version 4.0, INDEC Medical Systems, Santa Clara, CA, USA). We performed the following quantitative IVUS measurements: vessel volume, plaque volume, and lumen volume; plaque burden (plaque volume divided by vessel volume); and the minimum luminal area and diameter. Coronary arteries with a calcium arc over 90° in the studied segments were excluded. The minimum lumen area (MLA) and external elastic membrane (EEM) area were measured at the narrowest luminal cross section of the target lesion with the highest maxLCBI4mm [12]. The plaque burden at the narrowest site was calculated as: [100 × (EEM area − MLA)/EEM area]. The area of atheroma was defined as the difference between the lumen area and the EEM area. The total atheroma volume was calculated as the sum of the plaque area [13]. The total atheroma volume refers to the entire vascular segment analyzed by NIRS-IVUS. The range is defined as the same distance from the entrance of the non-culprit vessel at baseline and follow-up.

QFR

QFR and three-dimensional (3D) qualitative comparative analysis were analyzed using validated software (QAngio XA 3D version 1.0.28.4; Medis Medical Imaging Systems, Leiden, the Netherlands). Two angiographic views at least 25° apart were selected to minimize image overlap and distortion. From these views, two end-diastolic frames were chosen using ECG guidance. One or two clear anatomical landmarks (like bifurcations) were used to match positions in both frames. The start and end points of the target vessel were also marked. Vessel borders were automatically detected and corrected manually if needed. A 3D model of the artery (excluding side branches) was then created. The 3D qualitative comparative analysis included: minimum lumen diameter, reference vessel diameter, percent diameter stenosis, lesion length.

The software reconstructed a 3D anatomical vessel model without its side branches for the 3D qualitative comparative analysis and QFR computation. The 3D qualitative comparative analysis included the minimum luminal diameter, reference vessel diameter, percent diameter stenosis, and lesion length. QFR was calculated using this 3D model and a contrast-flow model, without using drugs to induce hyperemia. Instead, blood flow velocity was estimated using the TIMI frame count (TFC) method (which measures how quickly contrast moves through the artery). The projection with the clearest contrast flow was used. Details of the computational method and underlying principle of contrast-flow QFR were previously reported [14]. The ΔQFR representing the change in the QFR was measured as the difference between the QFR at follow-up and at baseline (i.e., ΔQFR = QFR at follow-up–QFR at baseline).

Reproducibility of measurements

To assess intra- and inter-observer reproducibility, two experienced independent cardiologists performed repeated measurements under the same analysis protocol.

Serial measurements of lipid parameters

Blood samples were collected before CAG at baseline and at follow-up. All central biochemical analyses were conducted by Kindai University Hospital, Japan.

The fasting serum levels of LDL-C, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglyceride, apolipoprotein A-1, apolipoprotein B, lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)), and remnant lipoprotein cholesterol were measured. The calculation of non-HDL-C levels at admission was conducted as total cholesterol levels minus HDL-C levels.

Statistical analysis

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to determine whether continuous variables had a normal distribution. Continuous variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation if they had a normal distribution or as the median and interquartile range if the distribution was not normal. Changes in continuous variables from baseline were tested with the paired t-test if they were normally distributed or with non-parametric statistics if non-normally distributed (Wilcoxon signed-rank test). A linear regression analysis was performed to investigate the correlation of ΔmaxLCBI4mm with the relative value of each plaque component evaluated by NIRS-IVUS and laboratory data. The changes in maxLCBI4mm or LCBI values of non-culprit vessel at baseline and follow-up were analyzed using two-way repeated measures ANOVA for each group. Categorical variables involving multiple subgroups (e.g., clinical presentation, lesion location) were analyzed using overall chi-square tests to minimize the risk of type I error. No formal multiplicity correction was applied because of the exploratory nature of this study. All univariate variables with P < 0.05 and those deemed of clinical interest were included in the statistical model. All P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using JMP Pro software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Study population

Between December 2021 and January 2025, 114 patients underwent NIRS-IVUS imaging at baseline. Of these patients, 21 declined follow-up CAG, mainly due to personal or logistical reasons such as hospitalization cost or difficulty taking several days off work; 6 died in a non-cardiovascular event by follow-up, 2 underwent coronary artery bypass grafting by follow-up, 10 underwent PCI for intermediate stenosis by follow-up, and 3 had non-evaluable NIRS data and were excluded. Therefore, 72 patients with a complete data set were included in the study (Supplemental Fig. 1). According to patients’ LDL-C levels at follow-up, 44 and 28 patients were classified as VL group (LDL-C levels < 55 mg/dL) and L group (LDL-C levels ≥ 55 mg/dL). The baseline patients’ characteristics are shown in Table 1. There was no significant difference in the site of intermediate stenosis between the two groups. The median follow-up period in the VL group was similar to that in the L group. All patients in both groups received statin therapy at follow-up. No patients in either group were receiving PCSK9 inhibitors at baseline or follow-up, and no patients in either group were receiving high-intensity statins (atorvastatin 40 mg/day or rosuvastatin 20 mg/day) at baseline. At follow-up, two patients (4.4%) in the VL group were receiving high-intensity statins. The types and doses of statins at baseline and follow-up are shown in Supplemental Table 1. The laboratory data at baseline and follow-up are shown in Table 2. Biochemical parameters were well matched in both groups at baseline. Forty-four (61%) patients had LDL-C levels < 55 mg/dL, 24 (33%) had LDL-C levels ≥ 55 mg/dL and < 70 mg/dL, and 4 (6%) had LDL-C levels ≥ 70 mg/dL at follow-up. At the follow-up, LDL-C and apolipoprotein B levels in the VL group were significantly lower than those in the L group (P < 0.0001; P = 0.01, respectively). In addition, early follow-up laboratory data obtained at outpatient visits after discharge (median 6.1 weeks [IQR 5.0–6.9]) showed a mean LDL-C level of 58.8 ± 17.7 mg/dL, with significantly lower Angiographic characteristics, NIRS-IVUS findings, and physiological measurements

The angiographic characteristics, NIRS-IVUS findings, and physiological measurements of the analyzed vessels are shown in Table 3. There was no significant difference between the two groups in MLA and plaque burden at the site of maxLCBI4mm, or in total atheroma volume, at both baseline and follow-up. Discussion

The present study showed that aggressive LDL-C lowering therapy is associated with plaque stabilization and improved coronary flow in patients with ACS. The main findings of this study were that reducing LDL-C levels to < 55 mg/dL decreased both the number of lesions with a maxLCBI4mm ≥ 400 and the lesion LCBI for non-culprit intermediate stenosis in ACS during the chronic phase, while improving QFR at follow-up. However, these findings were not observed in patients with LDL-C levels between 55–70 mg/dL, suggesting that LDL-C levels < 70 mg/dL is not sufficient to prevent disease progression especially in ACS patients.

LDL-C-lowering therapy and plaque characteristics in ACS

Previous studies have reported that in patients with ACS and multivessel coronary artery disease, more than half of cardiovascular events are attributable to non-culprit vessels [15]. Preventive PCI has been proposed as a potential strategy to reduce plaque vulnerability and adverse cardiovascular events [16]. However, some studies have indicated that PCI alone may not be sufficient to alter the vulnerable plaque status in ACS patients [15]. The incidence of future cardiovascular events after PCI in ACS is more often non-culprit lesions than culprit lesions [17]. Two years after PCI, patients still experience continued progression of non-culprit coronary plaques, even with standard drug therapy such as statins [18]. Another study reported that two-thirds of patients who received enhanced lipid-lowering therapy had LDL-C levels ≥ 70 mg/dL, indicating that achieving LDL-C levels < 70 mg/dL remains challenging in real-world practice [10]. In this study, 40% of patients had vulnerable plaques (maxLCBI4mm ≥ 400) in non-culprit lesion at the acute phase, which improved to 21% at the chronic phase, and LDL-C levels were controlled to 53 (46–63) mg/dL with LDL-C lowering therapy at the chronic phase. Some intracoronary imaging studies reported a positive relationship between LDL-C levels and the lipid content of plaques [19, 20]. Furthermore, a greater LDL-C reduction with statin therapy and PCSK9 inhibitors was associated with a greater reduction in plaque volume [7]. In this previous study, a greater reduction in LDL-C levels was also associated with a reduction in the maxLCBI4mm and a reduction in the lipid content of plaques. Similarly, our study showed that aggressive LDL-C lowering therapy had a significant effect on plaque stabilization because lowering LDL-C levels to < 55 mg/dL changed plaques with a maxLCBI4mm ≥ 400 to < 400 and reduced the lesion LCBI.

This study showed that controlling LDL-C levels at 55–70 mg/dL did not result in plaque stabilization (maxLCBI4mm < 400), and some patients had plaque progression at this level, but this was due to high Lp(a) levels. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that elevated Lp(a) levels is associated with a higher percentage of plaques with a maxLCBI4mm ≥ 400 [21]. Therefore, greater benefit may be achieved by control of LDL-C and Lp(a) levels in patients with non-culprit intermediate stenosis in ACS.

Plaque characteristics and coronary flow

Previous studies have reported that plaque vulnerability is associated with reduced fractional flow reserve ratio (FFR) or fractional flow reserve derived from computed tomography (FFRCT) values compared with stable plaques. Generally, lipid-rich plaques develop as a result of increased exposure to atherogenic inflammatory mediators (such as IL-1β and MMP) and oxidative stress, processes that impair endothelial integrity and vascular function [22]. Previous studies have demonstrated that lesions with a larger necrotic core exhibit greater endothelial dysfunction, supporting this pathophysiological link [23]. Because the endothelium plays a central role in modulating vascular tone [24], plaques associated with endothelial dysfunction may show a blunted vasodilatory response under hyperemic conditions, which a decrease in FFR value [25]. Previous studies reported that wall shear stress, axial plaque stress, and pressure gradient were correlated with ΔFFRCT, and there was a strong correlation between shear stress and the site of plaque rupture [26]. When lipid components are reduced through therapy, inflammation is alleviated, vasodilatory capacity is restored, and blood flow resistance decreases, potentially improving coronary flow as measured by QFR [27]. In addition, as lipid components within plaques decrease, endothelial cell function improves, particularly the production of nitric oxide (NO). As a result, coronary artery reactivity (dilatation capacity) may increase, potentially improving coronary blood flow [28]. These findings support the hypothesis that reduction in plaque lipid content leads to attenuation of vascular inflammation and restoration of endothelial function, thereby improving coronary flow. Moreover, exploratory analysis suggested that the magnitude of LDL-C reduction (ΔLDL-C) was modestly associated with both reductions in maxLCBI4mm and improvements in QFR, indicating that greater LDL-C lowering may further enhance plaque stabilization and coronary physiology. This mechanism is consistent with prior study, such as the YELLOW study [29], which demonstrated parallel improvements in plaque composition and physiological indices. Vulnerable plaques therefore affect coronary flow more than stable plaques, due to higher wall shear stress, axial plaque stress, and the pressure gradient. The PACMAN-AMI study similarly supports the benefits of intensive lipid-lowering therapy targeting non-lesion regions in ACS patients [7]. In this study, the L group had an almost doubling the prevalence of maxLCBI4mm, despite a reduction in LDL-C levels from 107.1 to 66.8 mg/dL from baseline to follow-up. However, the L group included 4 patients with LDL-C levels ≥ 70 mg/dL and 5 patients with Lp(a) ≥ 30 mg/dL, which may have significantly influenced the follow-up maxLCBI4mm values. Excluding these patients, the median maxLCBI4mm at follow-up in the L group decreased to 300. The residual high LDL-C and high Lp(a) levels may have contributed to the unexpected increase in plaque lipid content, partially explaining the difference from large-scale trials of intensive lipid-lowering therapy such as PACMAN-AMI. This study further highlights the limitations of QFR improvement and the moderate LDL-C targets (55–70 mg/dL), providing a new dimension for personalized lipid-lowering therapy. These findings align with contemporary guideline recommendations for very–high-risk patients with ACS. Current ESC guidance advocates an LDL-C target of < 55 mg/dL together with a ≥ 50% reduction from baseline, and recent ACC/AHA expert consensus endorses intensifying lipid-lowering therapy when LDL-C levels remain ≥ 55 mg/dL in very–high-risk patients. In our cohort, achieving < 55 mg/dL was associated with a marked reduction in plaque lipid burden (maxLCBI4mm and lesion LCBI) and improvement in coronary physiology (QFR), whereas patients with 55–70 mg/dL did not show consistent plaque stabilization. These mechanistic data therefore reinforce the rationale for pursuing LDL-C levels < 55 mg/dL in ACS and for addressing residual risk factors such as elevated Lp(a). The YELLOW study showed that vessels with improved maxLCBI4mm also exhibited higher FFR value, and that plaque stabilization improved coronary flow [29]. A difference between the YELLOW study and our study is that the patients in the present study only had ACS, and both groups in the present study had LDL-C levels lowered to < 70 mg/dL, which corresponds to the intensive group in the YELLOW study. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, and inherent limitations due to its exploratory nature and constraints in sample size calculation. Because this was an exploratory study, no a priori power analysis was performed to determine the sample size, the statistical power was limited, and the generalizability of our findings to broader populations should be interpreted with caution. Second, this study did not include ACS patients who did not provide consent. In addition, a proportion of patients declined follow-up angiography for personal or logistical reasons, such as hospitalization cost or difficulty taking several days off work, which may have introduced a modest selection bias toward patients who were clinically stable and able to complete follow-up. In addition, three patients (2.6%) were excluded due to poor NIRS-IVUS image quality caused by technical issues (e.g., motion artifact, incomplete pullback), and these exclusions were unlikely to introduce selection bias. Third, QFR measurements in the acute phase may be influenced by changes in microvascular resistance. However, previous studies have demonstrated good concordance between QFR and other validated physiological indices [30]. Fourth, the follow-up duration of 12–18 months may be relatively short to assess long-term atherosclerotic changes. However, it was consistent with the follow-up durations reported in prior mechanistic imaging studies such as PACMAN-AMI, HUYGENS, and IBIS-4. Longer follow-up might reveal different trajectories of plaque stabilization or recurrence, particularly in patients with residual risk factors such as elevated Lp(a) or diabetes. The present study’s findings are consistent with prior mechanistic studies such as PACMAN-AMI and HUYGENS, which demonstrated plaque regression and stabilization with PCSK9 inhibitor therapy. Our study complements these results by showing that comparable improvements in plaque composition and coronary physiology can occur with intensive statin and ezetimibe therapy alone in real-world practice. Fifth, the lipid-lowering regimens were not fully standardized, and no patients received PCSK9 inhibitors during the study period. Although this may have attenuated the potential benefits of intensive lipid lowering, it also allowed evaluation of the effects of achieving LDL-C < 55 mg/dL under routine oral therapy alone. Finally, this study has confirmed the association between plaque lipid reduction and improved QFR, but there is a lack of clinical hard endpoints (such as non-culprit lesion-related MACE). Although maxLCBI4mm and QFR are surrogate indices, both have been previously associated with adverse cardiovascular events [3, 31], suggesting that improvements in these parameters may reflect a lower residual risk in patients achieving LDL-C levels < 55 mg/dL. Therefore, the current findings need to be validated through long-term follow-up to determine their predictive value for cardiovascular events. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to show that lowering LDL-C levels to < 55 mg/dL has a significant effect on plaque stabilization and coronary flow, even when LDL-C levels in the control group were maintained between 55–70 mg/dL.

Conclusion

Aggressive LDL-C lowering therapy for non-culprit intermediate stenosis in ACS appears to be associated with plaque stabilization and coronary blood flow improvement.
