Enhanced methane from co-digestion of wheat straw and sludge with KMnO4 and zero-valent iron
Authors: Xiaowei Wang, Jinyang Huang, Tingting Zhang, Guangyi Zhang, Zisheng Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study proposed a synergistic treatment approach combining KMnO4 pretreatment and zero-valent iron (ZVI) enhancement, along with co-digestion with waste-activated sludge (WAS), to boost wheat straw (WS) anaerobic digestion. Results show that 0.1 g/g WS of KMnO4 pretreatment effectively destroys the straw’s lignin structure, greatly improving its biodegradability and increasing cumulative methane production by about 238.5% compared to the untreated reactor. The addition of ZVI further improved anaerobic digestion efficiency, leading to an additional 13.8% increase in methane production. The underlying mechanism involves the consumption of H+ through corrosion reactions, which alleviates acid inhibition and maintains the pH within the optimal range. Simultaneously, ZVI promotes the activity of key enzymes (e.g., coenzyme F420) involved in the hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis pathway. PICRUSt2 functional gene analysis reveals a significant increase in the abundance of key genes associated with the CO2-dependent methanogenesis pathway in the ZVI reactor.

Introduction

Crop straw is one of the most abundant and readily accessible renewable energy sources worldwide.1 As a major agricultural country, China generates a colossal amount of crop straw annually, posing severe challenges to its sustainable management. Traditional disposal methods, such as open burning, simple landfilling, and partial utilization for fertilizer and feed, generally suffer from low utilization efficiency, substantial resource wastage, and significant environmental and safety risks.2 Therefore, the development of efficient, environment-friendly straw treatment technologies with a focus on resource recovery is of paramount importance. Anaerobic digestion for methane production exhibits significant potential, as it can effectively convert straw organic matter into biogas, offering a crucial pathway for the energy and resource utilization of agricultural waste.3

However, the efficient anaerobic digestion of straw faces notable challenges. The primary components of straw contain a substantial quantity of recalcitrant polymers, notably lignin (8%–15%), cellulose (35%–40%), and hemicellulose (20%–30%). This structural composition severely inhibits hydrolysis, a critical initial step in anaerobic digestion, resulting in prolonged digestion cycles and reduced biogas production.4 To address this challenge, researchers have employed various pretreatment methods aimed at disrupting the lignocellulosic structure and enhancing biodegradability. Currently, commonly used pretreatment methods include mechanical, physical, chemical, and biological approaches.5 Notably, chemical oxidation pretreatment, as a technique capable of effectively degrading lignin while preserving carbohydrates, demonstrates promising potential in the efficient conversion of lignocellulosic biomass.6 KMnO4, a potent oxidizing agent, has been proven to effectively degrade lignin structures. Studies have shown that KMnO4 can effectively disrupt the structure to enhance the subsequent bioconversion potential when treating lignocellulosic biomass.7 Although KMnO4 pretreatment can significantly enhance the hydrolysis-acidification efficiency of straw, its high carbohydrate content may lead to rapid accumulation of volatile fatty acids (VFAs) (>3,000 mg/L), subsequently lowering the system pH (<6.0) and inhibiting methanogenic activity.8

It is reported that waste-activated sludge (WAS), which contains 12%–18% protein (dry weight basis) and ammonia nitrogen (NH4+-N ≈ 800–1,200 mg/L), can produce alkaline substances (e.g., NH3/NH4+) upon decomposition.9 These substances effectively neutralize H+ generated from straw hydrolysis, thereby maintaining the system pH within an optimal range. For instance, Zhao et al. co-digested wheat straw (WS) with WAS at a 1:1 ratio, resulting in a 26.9% increase in methane production compared to the separate fermentation of equivalent amounts of WS and WAS.10

In addition, zero-valent iron (ZVI), as an inexpensive electron donor, has been extensively studied in the field of anaerobic digestion due to its unique reducing properties and biocompatibility.11 The addition of a biochar-immobilized nanoscale ZVI composite (nZVI/BC-33) enhanced methane production from the anaerobic digestion of ciprofloxacin-containing wastewater, achieving a yield of 143 mL/g COD.12 The study revealed that ZVI, in synergy with persulfate, serves to mediate and promote the anaerobic fermentation of sludge. The Fe-activated persulfate reaction enhances electron transfer and elevates the production of reactive radicals, thereby promoting the enrichment of key microbial communities.13 The introduction of ZVI into the straw-sludge co-digestion system will exert the following effects: (1) ZVI can efficiently consume H+ in the system through corrosion reactions (Fe0 + 2H+→Fe2+ + H2), effectively alleviating acidification inhibition caused by VFAs accumulation.14 Meanwhile, the released H2 can be directly utilized by hydrogenotrophic methanogens, thereby increasing methane production; and (2) the Fe2+ produced by ZVI corrosion serves as a crucial cofactor for various anaerobic enzyme systems (e.g., hydrogenase, coenzyme F420), significantly enhancing the metabolic activity of methanogens.

Despite the individual effectiveness of KMnO4 pretreatment, sludge co-digestion, or ZVI supplementation in enhancing anaerobic digestion, a systematic strategy integrating all three approaches remains unexplored. Previous studies have primarily focused on single pretreatment methods or binary combinations, often overlooking the synergistic potential of sequential physicochemical and biological enhancements. For instance, while KMnO4 effectively degrades lignin, it may induce excessive acidification; similarly, WAS co-digestion can buffer pH but may not fully overcome lignin recalcitrance; ZVI alone may enhance methanogenesis but does not address substrate accessibility.

To bridge these gaps, this study proposes, a ternary synergistic system coupling KMnO4 oxidative pretreatment, WAS co-digestion, and ZVI supplementation for the anaerobic co-digestion of WS and WAS. This integrated approach is designed to concurrently tackle three critical bottlenecks: (1) lignin structural barrier via selective oxidation; (2) acidification inhibition through pH buffering and H+ consumption; and (3) low methanogenic activity via enhanced electron transfer and enzymatic activation. We hypothesize that this sequential strategy (i.e., structural deconstruction, nutrient balancing, and bioactivation) will further improve anaerobic digestion than single or dual pretreatment. The findings are expected to provide a novel, scalable, and economically viable pathway for the valorization of lignocellulosic agricultural wastes.

Results

Effect of pretreatments on WS properties

In this experiment, various physicochemical properties of the straw were characterized after pretreatment. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) characterization (Figure 1A) showed that the surface of the raw straw is relatively smooth with a regular structural distribution. In contrast, the WS surface exhibited increased roughness and developed a porous structure after pretreatment, which indicates that the physical structure of the straw had been disrupted. Lignin forms a dense physical barrier that encapsulates cellulose, hindering the access of hydrolytic enzymes, which significantly reduces the degradation efficiency and methane yield during the anaerobic digestion of WS.15 Similar conclusions have also been confirmed in related studies.16 In this experiment, the contents of lignin, cellulose, hemicellulose, and other components in the straw were determined after pretreatment (Figure 1B). It can be seen that KMnO4 pretreatment could significantly reduce the lignin content in the straw, and the extent of reduction was positively correlated with the dosage of KMnO4. Specifically, the lignin content in the original straw was 22.5%, which decreased to only 16.5% after oxidation with 0.2 KMnO4 g/g WS. The Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra, as shown in Figure 1C, exhibited absorption peaks at 3,401 cm−1, 2,916 cm−1, 1,054 cm−1, and 1,731 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching vibrations of O–H, –CH2, C–O, and C=O, respectively, which are characteristic peaks of lignin and cellulose.6 It can be seen that KMnO4 pretreatment reduced the intensities of these peaks, and the reduction of peak intensities was also positively correlated with the dosage of KMnO4. The variations in the absorption peaks further confirmed the alterations in surface functional reactors of the WS before and after pretreatment. Meanwhile, Figure S1 displays the typical cellulose structure of WS. Its characteristic peak intensity initially increased and subsequently decreased with increasing KMnO4 concentration. This trend may be attributed to the increase in cellulose content and the removal of amorphous components (such as hemicellulose and lignin) induced by the pretreatment.17 To analyze the fluorescence characteristics and the composition of dissolved organic matter after KMnO4 pretreatment, three-dimensional (3D)-excitation-emission matrix (EEM) fluorescence spectroscopy was conducted (Figure 1D). The results demonstrated that the fluorescence intensities of various components in the pretreated reactor increased significantly, particularly in regions III, IV, and V. Moreover, these enhancements were directly proportional to the increment of KMnO4 dosage. According to the studies, region III corresponds to fulvic acid-like substances, region IV corresponds to soluble microbial byproducts, and region V corresponds to humic acid-like organic matter.18 These substances are reported to be the intermediate products formed during the decomposition of WS.19 Therefore, it can be concluded that the pretreatment of WS with KMnO4 could break its physical structure, significantly enhance its solubility, and generate substrates that are beneficial for subsequent anaerobic digestion.Figure 1Effect of KMnO4 pretreatment on wheat strawElectron microscope images (A), lignocellulose content (data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation) (B), FTIR analysis (C), and 3D-EEM analysis of liquid fraction (D) after different doses of KMnO4 pretreatment.

Effect of pretreatment on WS/WAS co-digestion

After pretreating straw, it was mixed with WAS at a 1:1 ratio for anaerobic co-digestion to investigate the impact of KMnO4-pretreated straw on the methane production performance of the co-digestion system. The results revealed that the methane production of the subsequent co-digestion system could be significantly enhanced after straw pretreatment, and the increment of methane production was closely related to the dosage of KMnO4 (Figure 2A). Specifically, the methane production initially increased with the increasing KMnO4 dosage and reached its maximum value (200.16 mL/g the organic fraction of suspended solids [VSS]) in the 0.1 g/g WS reactor, which was 238.5% higher compared to the untreated reactor. However, further increasing the KMnO4 dosage (i.e., 0.2 g/g WS) led to a reduction in methane production. A potential mechanism was that KMnO4 pretreatment promoted the hydrolysis of straw and disrupted the lignin structure, resulting in the formation of simple substances (such as VFAs), which were readily degradable for subsequent anaerobic digestion and thereby increased methane production.8 Compared to other reactors, a higher concentration of KMnO4 (0.2 g/g WS) more effectively promoted the decomposition of lignin and carbohydrates in straw. This led to substantial accumulation of VFAs, resulting in a decline in pH and consequently inhibiting the subsequent methane production phase of anaerobic digestion.20 The pH data, as shown in Figures 2B and S2, exhibited fluctuations that vary in accordance with the accumulation and depletion of VFAs. The removal efficiency of organic matter in the co-digestion system is illustrated in Figure 2C. Effect of ZVI supplementation on pretreated WS/WAS co-digestion

Methane production and pH changes

As revealed by the aforementioned experiment, pretreating straw with 0.1 g/g WS KMnO4 is most conducive to subsequent methane production. Therefore, in this study, ZVI was added to WS pretreated with 0.1 g/g WS KMnO4 to investigate the effect of WS pretreatment coupled with ZVI on enhancing co-digestion of WAS and WS. Results demonstrated that the addition of ZVI could further increase methane yield. As shown in Figure 3A, after 40 days of digestion, the methane production in the ZVI reactor reached 216 mL/g VSS, which was 13.8% higher compared to that in the pretreatment-only reactor. During anaerobic digestion, the initial acidification, induced by the characteristic high polysaccharide content and elevated carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of straw during co-digestion, temporarily inhibited the metabolic activity of methanogens, but this inhibitory effect was gradually alleviated as the co-digestion process progressed (Figures 3B and S3).8,21 The organic matter removal efficiency is illustrated in Figure 3C. It can be observed that ZVI can effectively improve the efficiency of organic matter removal. In the ZVI reactor, the removal efficiencies of the total amount of solid materials in a water sample that cannot pass through a 0.45 μm filter membrane (TSS) and VSS were 36.7% and 67.3%, respectively, which were 14.9% and 13.2% higher than those in pretreatment-only reactor. Figure S4 further supports this conclusion. Similar findings have been reported in previous research. Changes in enzyme activity

To further investigate the mechanism by which ZVI promotes co-digestion of pretreated WS and WAS, the activities of relevant enzymes in the methanogenic stage were characterized (Figure 4). Among these, NADH primarily takes part in the energy conversion processes of cellular respiration, facilitating ATP synthesis by acting as an electron donor.23 Coenzyme F420 acts as a core biomarker for methane generation in anaerobic digestion systems and is closely linked to the activity of hydrogenotrophic methanogens.24 ZVI promoted hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis via coenzyme F420 activation and upregulation of CO2-dependent pathways. ATP serves as a pivotal energy carrier in microbial metabolic pathways, facilitating the conversion of organic substrates into methane and carbon dioxide during anaerobic digestion, thereby providing fundamental support for the efficiency and sustainability of biogas production. HdrA2B2C2 and MvhADG-HdrABC are typical electron bifurcation (EB) enzymes, which has been identified as a novel intracellular electron transfer mechanism.25 It can be seen that ZVI effectively enhanced the activities of enzymes during the co-digestion process, especially NADH and coenzyme F420.Figure 4Effects of pretreatment and ZVI on enzymes related to methanogenesis during co-digestionChanges in the activities of methanogenesis-related enzymes in the control group and the ZVI group. Microbial analysis

To elucidate the effects of ZVI on the abundance of microbes, microbial community structure was analyzed via 16S rRNA sequencing. From Figure 5A, it can be seen that the dominant genera in these two reactors were Thermoclostridium stercorarium, Clostridium thermosuccinogenes, Acetobacterium bakii, Levilinea saccharolytica, Tangfeifania diversioriginum, and Abyssalbus ytuae. These microorganisms are common bacterial genera found in anaerobic digestion systems, primarily involved in the decomposition and conversion of WS and WAS. Notably, these bacteria were significantly enriched in the reactor with the addition of ZVI. Among these, Clostridium thermosuccinogenes could enhance syntrophic metabolism and facilitate hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis by primarily generating formate and maintaining low hydrogen partial pressure under reduced culture redox potentials.26 Moreover, under symbiotic conditions, the Acetobacterium bakii can supply acetate as a substrate for acetate-degrading methanogens.27 Tangfeifania diversioriginum enhances syntrophic metabolism and hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis by fermenting complex organics (e.g., arabinose, xylose, and fructose) to supply key substrates for DIET-driven consortia (a syntrophic metabolism mode in the methanogenic process of anaerobic digestion, in which electron transfer between acidogenic bacteria and methanogenic archaea occurs directly via conductive pili (e-pili) or conductive materials (e.g., granular activated carbon), without relying on intermediate carriers such as hydrogen or formate).28 The corrosion of ZVI generates substantial H2, which shifts the reactor environment toward hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis. In the environment shaped by ZVI, hydrolytic and fermentative bacteria initiate the degradation process, while acetogenic bacteria and syntrophic oxidizers (activated under low H2 partial pressure) work together to efficiently convert complex intermediates into acetate and H2. These products are rapidly consumed by hydrogenotrophic methanogens, whose utilization of H2 further reduces the H2 partial pressure, creating a positive feedback that reinforces the syntrophic oxidation process. Additionally, ZVI facilitates more efficient electron flow toward methanogens. Ultimately, these enriched bacteria collectively form an efficient and stable “metabolic assembly line,” continuously supplying optimized substrates (H2/CO2 and acetate) for hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis.Figure 5Effect of pretreatment and ZVI on the microbial community of the co-digestion systemMicrobial communities of the genus bacteria (A), microbial communities of the genus archaea (B), KEGG analysis of anaerobic digestion (see also Table S2) (C), and the number of OTUs (D). As shown in Figure 5B, the most abundant archaea genera were Methanosarcina, Methanomassiliicoccus, Methanobacterium, Methanothrix, which totally account for 67.3% and 54% of archaea 16S rRNA gene sequences in the control and ZVI reactor, respectively. These microorganisms did not exhibit consistent changes in the two reactors. To clarify the influence of ZVI supplementation on methanogenic metabolic routes, PICRUSt2 was employed to analyze genes encoding pivotal enzymes in the methanogenesis process. Figure 5C displays the genes of two methanogenic pathways (i.e., acetate-dependent and CO2-dependent methanogenesis) along with their precise abundance variations. As shown in Figure 5C, compared to the control reactor, the abundances of all genes involved in both acetate-dependent and CO2-dependent methanogenesis pathways were improved in ZVI reactor, especially the abundance of the genes involved in CO2-dependent methanogenesis. Exactly, the abundance of the genes encoding the enzymes related to the transformation of CO2 to 5-methyl-THM(S)PT was 8,615.5–142,660.18 in the ZVI reactor, which is about 10.7%–42.5% higher than the control (see Table S2 for details). The results demonstrate that ZVI supplementation in co-digestion systems significantly enhances the activities of the hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis process. Various indices were employed to characterize the impact of ZVI on species richness and dominance within the microbial community (Figure 5D, OTU: in microbial molecular ecology (e.g., 16S rRNA gene sequencing), a unit used for clustering analysis of sequences). Specifically, the combined use of the Chao and Ace indices provides a more reliable assessment of changes in species richness. In the pretreatment + ZVI reactor, the Chao, Ace, and Simpson indices were 84, 84.3, and 0.103, respectively, whereas in the control reactor, these values were 79.2, 79.7, and 0.142. Alpha diversity analysis revealed that the pretreatment + ZVI reactor enhanced both the richness and microbial diversity of the community.

Discussion

This study successfully enhanced methane production from the co-digestion of WS and WAS through a synergistic strategy combining KMnO4 pretreatment and ZVI reinforcement. Results demonstrated that KMnO4 pretreatment significantly disrupted the recalcitrant lignocellulosic structure of WS via efficient oxidative lignin degradation, substantially improving substrate biodegradability. 3D-EEM fluorescence spectroscopy revealed markedly enhanced intensities of fulvic acid-like substances, microbial byproducts, and humic acid-like components in the liquid phase, indicating effective breakdown of straw macromolecules into readily biodegradable small-molecule organics. However, excessive concentrations of KMnO4 (e.g., 0.2 g/g WS) could induce VFA accumulation (>3,000 mg/L), triggering system acidification (pH < 6.0) and inhibiting methanogenic activity.

The subsequent introduction of ZVI further increased methane yield by 13.7%, and the potential mechanisms are as follows: (1) ZVI corrosion (Fe0 + 2H+ → Fe2+ + H2) effectively consumed H+, maintaining pH within the optimal range of 6.8–7.5 and alleviating acid toxicity to methanogens; (2) ZVI significantly enhanced system performance by strengthening methanogenic metabolism: enzymatic activity showed marked improvement in coenzyme F420 and NADH activities in ZVI-supplemented reactors, indicating promoted electron transfer and energy conversion. PICRUSt2 functional gene analysis further revealed upregulation of key genes in the CO2-dependent methanogenesis pathway within ZVI reactors, confirming specific promotion of ZVI to hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis.29

The study lies in proposing the “KMnO4 structural deconstruction—WAS co-digestion buffering—ZVI bioactivation” ternary synergistic strategy, systematically addressing three major bottlenecks in straw anaerobic digestion: (1) KMnO4 selectively disrupts lignin barriers; (2) WAS provides nitrogen sources while neutralizing acidic byproducts; and (3) ZVI further promotes methane production by mitigating acid inhibition and activating hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis. Compared to single pretreatment or conventional co-digestion processes, this strategy demonstrates significant advantages in cumulative methane production increase. Moreover, as both KMnO4 and ZVI are low-cost chemicals, the approach exhibits strong potential for engineering application.

Limitations of the study

Future research could focus on systematically evaluating the long-term ecological impacts of residual manganese ions and other potential oxidative by-products (such as low-molecular-weight organic acids and phenolic compounds) on subsequent anaerobic microbial communities.

Experimental model and study participant details

The WS utilized in this experiment was collected from Henan Province, China, and preserved under room temperature drying conditions. Prior to use, it was air-dried, milled, and passed through an 80-mesh sieve. The WAS was collected from the secondary sedimentation tank of the Wulongkou Wastewater Treatment Plant in Zhengzhou, China, and stored at 4°C for subsequent use. The inoculum sludge was collected from the Matougang Sludge Treatment Plant in Zhengzhou, China, and was cultivated in the laboratory following methods described in previous studies before use.10

Method details

Design of experimental conditions

To investigate the effects of KMnO4 dosage during WS pretreatment on the co-digestion of WS and WAS and to examine the effects of ZVI on their co-digestion, the experiment was divided into two phases. The first phase aimed to determine the impact of KMnO4 dosage on the co-digestion of WS and WAS. Based on these findings, a second, complementary experiment was conducted to assess the influence of ZVI on the anaerobic digestion of WS and WAS.

Effect of KMnO4 dosage on anaerobic co-digestion performance of WS/WAS

To investigate the influence of KMnO4 dosage during WS pretreatment on anaerobic digestion performance, WS was first pretreated with KMnO4 solution. During the pretreatment phase, 15 bottles were divided into five reactors, and then 90 mL WS with a concentration of 60 g/L(TSS) was added into each bottle. Different concentrations of KMnO4 were then added to each reactor with final KMnO4 concentrations of 0, 0.025, 0.05, 0.1, and 0.2 g/g WS, respectively.8,19 The pretreatment was conducted at 37°C for 6 h under ambient air conditions after which the pretreated WS were transferred to anaerobic bottles and mixed with an equal volume (90 mL) of WAS with a concentration of 60 g/L (TSS).6 Subsequently, 20 mL inoculum sludge was added into each bottle, the headspace air was purged with nitrogen gas, and the bottles were sealed for anaerobic digestion. Table S1 details the specific parameter settings for each experimental group. All reactors were then incubated in a thermostatic incubator shaker at 37 ± 1 °C with continuous shaking at 140 rpm. To investigate the influence of KMnO4 pretreatment on WS properties, another 15 bottles were divided into five reactors and conducted and pretreated as described above. After pretreatment, SEM, EEM, SCOD, cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin were characterized.

Effect of ZVI on the efficiency of co-anaerobic digestion of WS and WAS

Another batch experiment was designed to evaluate the synergistic effects of ZVI supplementation on anaerobic co-digestion performance. Based on the optimal methane production observed in Section “Effect of KMnO4 Dosage on Anaerobic Co-Digestion Performance of WS/WAS” (where WS pretreated with 0.1 g/g WS KMnO4 demonstrated the highest biogas production), two reactors were conducted as follows: reactor 1: 12g WS pretreated with 0.1 g/g WS KMnO4+ 32g WAS+20 mL inoculum sludge but without ZVI addition, hereafter referred to as the blank control reactor; reactor 2: 12g WS pretreated with 0.1 g/g WS KMnO4+ 32 g WAS+20 mL inoculum sludge + 4 g/L ZVI, hereafter referred to as the ZVI reactor. And then these two reactors were operated as described in section “effect of KMnO4 dosage on anaerobic co-digestion performance of WS/WAS”.

Methods of analysis

To investigate the impact of KMnO4 pretreatment on WS, the cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin contents of the raw material and pretreated residues were measured according to standard analytical procedures established by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL).30 Three-dimensional excitation-emission matrix fluorescence spectroscopy (3D-EEM, F-7000 FL) was used according to a previous study to characterize the biodegradability of organic matter in the water-soluble fraction (WSF) derived from the liquid phase after pretreatment.19 Structural alterations in WS before and after pretreatment were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; German-made ZEISS Sigma 300).18 Changes in the functional reactors of WS resulting from pretreatment were analyzed using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; Japan-made SHIMADZU IRTracer-100).31 The crystallinity of WS samples pre- and post-pretreatment was characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD; German-made Bruker D8 ADVANCE). XRD measurements were performed with a scanning step size of 0.02°/2θ over a range of 2θ = 5°–50°.32

TSS, VSS, COD, polysaccharides, and proteins within the reactor mixtures were quantified using standard methods at both the commencement and conclusion of the digestion experiments.33 During the digestion period, 2 mL aliquots of the mixture were withdrawn from the reactors every two days for analysis of VFA composition (GC, Techcomp, GC-7900, China) and pH (pH probe, Leici PHS-3E, China).34 Biogas production was monitored via gas chromatography (Gas chromatograph, Techcomp, GC-7900, China).35 The biological activity of the enzyme is determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay as described in the instruction manual of the ELISA kit (Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Upon termination of the digestion process, mixture samples were combined with 40% glycerol in a 1:1 ratio, snap-frozen, and stored at −20°C. Finally, the samples were transported to Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. for 16S rRNA high-throughput sequencing. For bacterial community analysis, the first round of amplification targeted the 16S rRNA gene V3-V4 region using the primers 341F (CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG) and 805R (GACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC). Subsequently, a second round of amplification was performed to attach primers compatible with Illumina bridge PCR.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experiments included triplicate biological and technical replicates, with data presented as mean ± SD.
