A proposed clinical framework for intentional tooth replantation based on healing dynamics: case series
Authors: Jianpeng Zhang, Shaorong Li, Su Li, Ruitang Shi, Benxiang Hou, Chen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntentional tooth replantation (ITR) serves as a definitive, tooth-preserving treatment but has limited success rate. Its clinical predictability is often hampered by inconsistent management protocols. Previously, ambiguity regarding the postoperative healing process has led to significant variability in follow-up schedules and assessment criteria. This article presents 4 successful cases of ITR with long-term follow-up, systematically describing the clinical and radiographic findings at postoperative intervals of 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months and beyond 1 year, 2 years or even 5 years. Based on these cases and our cumulative experience with 22 ITR cases. We systematically propose a clinical framework for the management of ITR based on the dynamic healing process, including follow-up timeline, key evaluation criteria, and clinical significance. The aim is to enhance the predictability and success rate of ITR, thereby facilitating the long-term preservation of the natural dentition.Keywords: Intentional tooth replantation, Clinical healing process/Healing assessment, Follow-up, Cone-beam computed tomography, Prognosis

Background

Intentional tooth replantation (ITR) is a tooth-conserving intervention that integrates endodontic and surgical principles [1]. The standardized protocol entails atraumatic extraction of the affected tooth, extraoral assessment of the root surface, targeted root canal system management, and immediate reinsertion into the original alveolus [1–9]. Historically, ITR was considered a salvage procedure for failed conventional root canal treatment (RCT), burdened by suboptimal success rates [10, 11].

Advancements in microsurgical techniques, the advent of bioceramic obturating materials, and improved insights into periodontal ligament (PDL) physiology have transformed ITR into a predictable treatment modality, with reported success rates ranging from 88% to 95% [12–16]. Primary indications include scenarios where non-surgical retreatment is unfeasible, such as canal calcification, instrument separation, or ledge formation, and posterior teeth where apical surgery carries high risks due to anatomical constraints. These include proximity to the inferior alveolar nerve or excessive bone thickness precluding surgical access, a situation particularly common in mandibular second molars [12, 13, 17–19]. Expanded indications in recent years encompass management of complex root canal anatomies, dentition preservation in adolescents to maintain alveolar bone development and occlusal integrity, and as a long-term alternative for patients with contraindications to dental implants (systemic comorbidities or financial limitations) [4, 12, 14, 20].

When RCT fails, the clinical algorithm typically prioritizes non-surgical retreatment, apical microsurgery, ITR, or extraction. While retreatment and apical microsurgery remain first-line options under standard conditions, ITR offers unique advantages in complex cases: compared to extraction and implant placement, it preserves the natural tooth, maintains proprioceptive function, and retains the biologically active PDL, critical for physiological alveolar bone remodeling and long-term esthetic stability [12, 21]. Economic evaluations further confirm that ITR is substantially more cost-effective than implant therapy [4, 22]. Relative to apical microsurgery, both modalities yield comparable medium-term survival rates, but ITR provides unrivaled 360° visualization of the root surface. This facilitates thorough debridement and sealing of isthmuses, lateral canals, and detection of occult root fractures, pathologies often inaccessible via the limited surgical field in apical surgery [12, 23–25]. Thus, ITR confers distinct therapeutic value in cases with challenging access or elevated surgical complication risk.

The viability of the PDL is a key condition for the success of ITR and for the prevention of root resorption. Systematic reviews and clinical investigations have identified key prognostic factors for ITR: an extraoral time < 15 min is universally acknowledged as the most critical determinant of PDL cell viability [12, 14, 15, 22, 26]. Additional risk factors include preoperative acute infection (e.g., sinus tract, abscess) [17, 27], use of non-bioceramic root-end filling materials [14, 18, 28, 29], and iatrogenic root/periodontal tissue injury during extraction [30].

Despite favorable survival rates documented in multiple studies [21, 22, 31], the current evidence base for ITR has notable limitations. First, high-quality randomized controlled trials are scarce, leaving comparisons between ITR and alternative treatments reliant on observational data [23, 32]. Second, detailed characterization of the healing process and long-term dynamic changes post-ITR is insufficient. Clinical data indicate that while early postoperative bone healing (survival rate > 90%) is common, survival rates may decline to ~ 82.8% with extended follow-up, accompanied by complications such as replacement or inflammatory root resorption [33–35]. Furthermore, the distinction between “functional healing” and “pathologic failure” requires clarification through granular follow-up data to delineate their progression and clinical relevance [36–38].

Against this backdrop, this case series presents four ITR procedures involving mandibular second molars, supported by comprehensive documentation extending from the immediate postoperative phase to long-term follow-up. We aim to delineate the diverse healing patterns post-ITR, including optimal PDL healing, functional ankylosis, and potential complications. By synthesizing insights from these four cases with those from 20 additional successful ITR procedures in our clinic, we emphasize the dynamic nature of post-ITR healing. Our goal is to provide evidence-based clinical guidance to refine the implementation and postoperative management of ITR.

Case reports

Common presentation of all cases presented in the case series

All the four cases were diagnosed as chronic apical periodontitis of mandibular second molar. Following detailed communication and informed consent, the teeth were atraumatically extracted using forceps, without elevators to minimize trauma, after which the periapical granulation tissue was precisely removed. The root surface was kept moist with continuous saline irrigation to preserve PDL viability. Under microscope, 3 mm of the root apex was resected with a high-speed handpiece, followed by ultrasonic retrograde preparation and obturation with iRoot BP Plus. Then all the teeth were gently reinserted into the socket, and the patient bit on a saline-moistened gauze for one hour; the entire extraoral time was limited to 15 min, and no additional splinting was applied. All CBCT images were taken by the same machine (New Tom VGi, 110 kV, 4.33 mA, 10 × 10 mm, 3.6 s).

Case 1

A female patient presented with a one-month history of swelling and pain in the lower right posterior region, where the right mandibular second molar (tooth #31) had undergone root canal treatment and crown restoration 10 years ago. Clinical examination revealed that the ceramic crown margin was sealed, and the tooth was tender to percussion with I° mobility, along with mild redness and swelling at the gingival margin and periapical area; a sinus tract was visible and a 4-mm periodontal pocket was detected centrally on the buccal side (Fig. 1a). Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) radiograph showed underfilled root canal obturation and a periapical lesion adjacent to the mandibular canal (Fig. 1b-d).

Case 3

A male patient complained of exacerbated pain upon biting in the lower right posterior region for 3 days. The mandibular right second molar (tooth #31) had undergone root canal treatment followed by crown restoration 2 years ago. Examination revealed a crown on the tooth #31 with well-adapted margins, I° mobility, mild gingival redness and swelling, probing depths of 3–4 mm (Fig. 3a). CBCT showed underfilled root canals, a missed mesiolingual canal, and a periapical lesion. Distal alveolar bone loss extended to the middle third of the root, adjacent to the mandibular canal (Fig. 3b-d).

A female patient reported spontaneous pain in the lower left posterior region for 1 week. Discussion

The primary challenge in managing the mandibular second molars presented in this series was the persistent periapical inflammation despite previous endodontic interventions. While endodontic failure is generally attributed to anatomical complexity or procedural errors [39], our study utilized CBCT to reveal specific obturation defects, such as apical ledges (Case 1), C-shaped canal complexities (Case 2, 4), and missed canals (Case 3), that were undetectable on standard periapical radiograph. These findings underscore that for mandibular second molars, where surgical access is restricted by thick buccal bone and proximity to the mandibular nerve, CBCT is an essential supplementary tool [40]. When these intracanal infections cannot be managed via conventional root canal therapy or apical surgery, ITR provides a predictable pathway by enabling direct extraoral visualization and management of the root pathology [41–46].

Rather than a generalized protocol, our proposed management strategy is grounded in the systematic observation of 22 consecutive cases. A key finding from our cohort is the identification of the 6-month post-operative mark as a reliable “initial outcome evaluation node.” While the definitive determination of ITR success typically requires 36 months of follow-up [19, 41, 47, 48], our data showed that 63.64% of cases exhibited complete radiographic resolution by 6 months, with a total of 95.5% showing significant healing trends (Tables 2 and 3), consistent with previous reports of ~ 90% short-term success rates [49]. This aligns with literature suggesting that significant periapical remodeling occurs within the first 6 months [47]. Furthermore, our observation that sinus tract closure occurs within 2 weeks and mobility improves by 1 month (Table 4) provides clinicians with early, tangible markers of successful PDL reattachment and infection control.

The importance of this “6-month node” is further validated by the analysis of our single failure case. In this instance, the patient’s vague discomfort during mastication persisted beyond three months, likely due to occlusal trauma, a factor exacerbated by tight occlusion. This suggests that while short-term healing was achieved in 21 cases, the mechanical environment is as critical as biological debridement. This case emphasizes that post-operative occlusal adjustment is a vital component of the ITR clinical pathway.

PDL viability remains the cornerstone of ITR success [50–53]. In our series, extraoral time was strictly limited to 7–12 min, which is well within the 15-minute threshold recognized to minimize the risk of replacement resorption. By focusing on mandibular second molars, we demonstrated that ITR effectively overcomes the “anatomical trap” of this specific tooth position. The removal of extruded materials and exploration of missed canals (as seen in Cases 1–4) directly addresses the etiologies of persistent infection. However, it is important to note that prognostic factors such as patient age (> 40 years) or the presence of combined perio-endo lesions can still influence long-term stability [39, 54].

Following ITR, PDL reattachment and revascularization rely heavily on adequate initial stability [55]. In the present cases, all treated teeth were mandibular second molars with probing depths ≤ 6 mm, relatively intact surrounding alveolar bone, favorable crown-to-root ratios, and satisfactory postoperative stability; no adverse healing was observed under non-splinting [34, 56, 57]. However, for teeth with post-replantation mobility greater than II°, short roots, a thin buccal cortical plate in anterior teeth, or insufficient interradicular bony support in posterior teeth, elastic periodontal splinting is generally recommended to maintain stability and facilitate periodontal healing [22, 56, 58, 59]. Periodontal reattachment in the cervical region may develop within 1–3 weeks; accordingly, the European Society of Endodontology recommends a routine splinting period of 2 weeks after ITR [10].

In conclusion, this study advances the field by transitioning from specific case reports to a structured clinical framework centered on the 6-month evaluation milestone. By focusing on the specific healing patterns of 22 consecutive mandibular second molar cases, we provide a more realistic and less biased assessment of ITR performance. This “proposed clinical framework” offers a practical reference for clinicians aiming to maximize natural tooth preservation in anatomically complex scenarios, provided that rigorous case selection and long-term surveillance are maintained.

Conclusion

This series of four ITR case reports primarily elucidates three key points. First, persistent intra-canal infection, from sources such as isthmuses, missed canals, ledges, or extruded filling materials, is a major mechanism underlying endodontic retreatment failure. ITR enables direct, extraoral visualization and management of these factors to achieve short-term healing. Second, the absence of clinical symptoms and a reduction in the radiographic lesion size within the short term (≤ 6 months) can be considered indicators of initial success. However, the determination of definitive healing requires follow-up of ≥ 12–36 months and, when necessary, confirmatory CBCT evaluation. Third, for teeth adjacent to critical anatomical structures or otherwise unsuitable for apical surgery, CBCT can be used following initial root canal obturation to verify the quality of the root filling before proceeding with coronal restoration, thereby potentially avoiding the need for ITR. The proposed timeline for clinical healing process in ITR presented in this report still requires further investigation. In sum, proposed endpoint criteria and follow-up durations are needed to evaluate the long-term success rate and complication of ITR, thereby establishing clearer evidence-based guidance for the timing of definitive restoration, frequency of follow-up, and radiographic assessment.
