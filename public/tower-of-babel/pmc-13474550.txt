Libman sacks endocarditis with multivalvular involvement, hemorrhagic stroke, and concurrent mixed plasmodium malaria in systemic lupus erythematosus: a case report
Authors: Abbas Khan, Okasha Tahir, Alina Batool, Jaweria Farman, Muhammad Hassaan Javaid, Eeman shehzad, Abdul Rahim Soroush, Faisal Hassan, Hakim Ullah Wazir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Libman-Sacks endocarditis (LSE) is a sterile inflammatory condition of the heart valves that occurs in the setting of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and antiphospholipid syndrome (APS). Multivalvular affection is a known entity; however, it is not a common occurrence. The association between multivalvular LSE and hemorrhagic stroke is a very rare entity.Case presentationThe case involves a 29-year-old female with a four-year history of SLE that had been diagnosed on the basis of the classification criteria formulated by EULAR and ACR in 2019. In addition, the patient reported fever that had been persisting over a period of ten days along with a condition of symmetrical polyarthritis that had lasted for three weeks following a pregnancy loss in the first trimester. It was documented that the patient was not compliant with immunosuppressive medications including her discontinuation of hydroxychloroquine, prednisolone, and mycophenolate mofetil. Encephalopathy followed which progressed rapidly enough to result in the development of unresponsiveness (Glasgow Coma Score 7/15). Physical examination showed that there was right hemiplegia along with bilateral extensor plantar response (Babinski sign). In terms of investigations, haemolytic anaemia was found (haemoglobin 6.0 g/dL), thrombocytopenia (platelet nadir 39 × 10³/µL), leukocytosis with neutrophilia (neutrophil count 14.2 × 10³/µL), significantly increased levels of inflammatory markers (CRP 42.2 mg/dL, ESR 120 mm/hr), anti-dsDNA level > 250 IU/mL, complement deficiency (C3 0.38 g/L; C4 0.05 g/L) and high titers of antiphospholipid antibodies (anticardiolipin IgG 72 GPL U/mL; anti-β2-glycoprotein I IgG 58 U/mL; lupus anticoagulant positive). Mixed infection caused by Plasmodium vivax and Plasmodium falciparum was confirmed through peripheral blood smear. The transthoracic echocardiography revealed presence of echogenic masses that were seen oscillating with adhesion to the posterior leaflet of mitral valve and aortic valve, associated with moderate to severe regurgitation from both valves, along with mitral stenosis, hence confirming multivalvular LSE. Cranial computed tomography without contrast revealed a large hemorrhagic infarct lesion (measuring 7 × 5.2 cm) in the left frontoparietal lobe with 15 mm midline shift. Management included administration of methylprednisolone pulses and artesunate for treating malaria, as well as blood products and osmotherapy using intravenous mannitol. Surgery was contraindicated due to presence of resistant thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy. The patient could not be managed despite maximal supportive treatment, and extremely poor prognosis was conveyed to the family.ConclusionMultiple valvular Libman–Sacks endocarditis can lead to a hemorrhagic stroke due to the combination of embolic vegetations, vasculopathy related to lupus, and coagulation problems with thrombocytopenia. This is an example of how it is necessary for all SLE patients to be screened through echocardiography and tested for antiphospholipid antibodies, adherence to the drug regimen, and a multidisciplinary team approach.Keywords: Libman–sacks endocarditis, Systemic lupus erythematosus, Antiphospholipid syndrome, Hemorrhagic stroke, Multivalvular heart disease, Thrombocytopenia, Malaria, Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome

Introduction

Libman-Sacks endocarditis (LSE), alternatively known as verrucous or marantic endocarditis, is the classical form of NBTE in SLE and APS. From a histopathologic standpoint, it is characterized by the formation of a clot composed of fibrin-platelet aggregates on the valve leaflets, mostly mitral and aortic valves, devoid of any infectious agents [1]. The simultaneous infection of multiple valves, although described, is considered a rare condition that considerably raises the incidence of emboli and hemodynamic complications.

LSE is associated with the accumulation of immune complexes, complement activation, and endothelial damage, culminating in platelet and fibrin aggregation in the presence of aPL [2]. Antiphospholipid antibodies are present in around 30–40% of SLE patients and dramatically enhance the likelihood of developing thrombosis and valvular disease; 30–80% of APS patients suffer from valvular heart disease (leaflet sclerosis, vegetation, or significant regurgitation) [3].

Although changes occur primarily in the mitral and aortic valves, other valves may become involved. The combination of lupus and antiphospholipid syndrome results in increased risk, not only of clotting but also of valve problems [1, 2]. While there is often no symptom, complications can develop, such as valve malfunction requiring treatment, formation of emboli affecting organs, as well as secondary infection of the heart lining. Signs of this include tiny nodules on the valves, stiffness of tissues, leakages of blood through valves, and rarely stenosis [3].

Clinical consequences of LSE may consist of hemodynamic regurgitation, systemic embolization, and infective endocarditis. In particular, systemic embolization, especially that affecting cerebral circulation (with an incidence of approximately 20% in patients with vegetations in LSE [4]), is one of the most important clinical problems. Hemorrhagic stroke is an unusual consequence of the disease; however, it can develop due to different mechanisms, such as hemorrhagic conversion of embolic infarcts, immune complex vasculitis involving small vessels, and coagulopathy because of thrombocytopenia and/or lupus anticoagulant [5].

In cases of clinical deterioration, treatment decisions concerning cardiac surgery need an experienced interdisciplinary team-based evaluation similar to the process used in infective endocarditis; such an approach will minimize the number of invasive procedures performed, thereby decreasing the incidence of morbidity [6, 7].

Although the close connection between SLE and LSE is a well-known phenomenon, the incidence of multivalvular disease coupled with debilitating cerebrovascular accidents is extremely rare and difficult to diagnose and treat, especially in younger patients who have an active immune response. The case below describes a patient with multivalvular LSE complicated by hemorrhagic stroke due to SLE flare-ups and malaria infection.

Case presentation

A 29-year-old female patient with a four-year history of SLE based on the 2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria involving the following areas: presence of ANA (titre 1:640, homogeneous type), positivity for anti-dsDNA, lupus nephritis (classes III/IV, as assessed by proteinuria and active urine sediment analysis), hematological manifestations (thrombocytopenia and hemolytic anemia), and inflammatory arthritis, was seen at our clinic due to a persistent fever for ten days and symmetrical polyarthritis primarily involving small joint areas in the hands and feet for three weeks. This presentation followed a miscarriage during the first trimester of pregnancy within one month, possibly a consequence of an obstetric manifestation of APS.

Her prescribed outpatient medications when her last clinic review took place included hydroxychloroquine 400 mg per day, prednisolone 10 mg per day, and mycophenolate mofetil 2 g per day. Her non-compliance status is well documented, and she had stopped taking all three drugs about three months before this admission, as evidenced by her medication refill record and her report at the time of admission. Serositis had never been a problem for her before. At the time of admission, her blood pressure reading was 148/94 mm Hg.

During the second day of his admission to hospital, the patient experienced progressive encephalopathy. The patient went from being disoriented to becoming unresponsive within six hours. Clinical neurological evaluation revealed right hemiplegia and bilateral extensor plantar response (Babinski reflex), indicative of upper motor neuron involvement. The patient’s Glasgow Coma Score was 7/15 (E1V2M4).

The transthoracic echocardiogram (Fig. 1) showed an irregular and pulsatile echogenic mass adhering to the posterior leaflet of the mitral valve, accompanied by diffuse thickening of the mitral valve leaflets with mild mitral valve stenosis and moderate to severe mitral valve insufficiency. Diffuse leaflet thickening with moderate to severe aortic valve insufficiency was seen in the aortic valve (Fig. 2). The left atrium was enlarged, but both ventricles had normal systolic function.

Urgent non-contrast CT imaging of the brain showed a significant hemorrhagic infarct of size 7 × 5.2 cm affecting the left frontal-parietal cortex and the subcortical region with edema, lateral ventricular effacement on the affected side, and midline shift to the extent of approximately 15 mm. The findings suggested that there was a massive hemispheric hemorrhagic stroke, which had developed early signs of transtentorial herniation. The presence of hemorrhage indicated embolic infarct with haemorrhagic transformation in the setting of lupus-related vasculopathy.

Diagnostic assessment

Based on the above results from echocardiography, biochemistry and microbiology, the final diagnosis of multisystem Libman–Sacks endocarditis due to an exacerbation of SLE with Plasmodium malaria was made. Since no microorganisms grew on serial blood cultures, along with the particular localization of the valvular lesions (posterior mitral leaflet and aortic valve) and the background of a serologically active SLE, the nature of endocarditis was not infectious but autoimmune. Due to the clinical picture including the valvular lesion, early pregnancy loss, thrombocytopenia, increased antiphospholipid antibodies (anticardiolipin IgG, 72 GPL U/mL; anti-β2GPI IgG, 58 U/mL; lupus anticoagulant positive), and cerebral ischemic episodes in a patient with SLE, several criteria of antiphospholipid syndrome according to revised Sydney Criteria were met.

Management

The management of this patient was managed by a multidisciplinary team of physicians including rheumatologists, cardiologists, neurologists, neurosurgeons, haematologists, anaesthetists, and intensivists. The rheumatologist was an integral member of this team since the time of admission, managing the SLE and APS in collaboration with the intensive care team, the cardiologists, and the neurologists.

Since the presence of mixed species of Plasmodium malariae infection, that is P. vivax and P. falciparum, had been confirmed, the treatment for severe malaria was started right away with intravenous administration of artesunate 2.4 mg/kg every 12 h for 72 h, followed by once a day thereafter as per the World Health Organization recommendation for the management of severe malaria. The management of the severe flare-up of SLE involved the intravenous administration of methylprednisolone 1 g/day for 3 consecutive days (pulse therapy) and oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day (60 mg/day).

.For brain swelling, administration of mannitol intravenously played an important role in reducing cerebral pressure. It is worth noting that for this patient, intravenous infusion of mannitol 1 g/kg (60 g) was given as a bolus within 20 min, repeated after every six hours, depending on the patient’s condition and based on measurements of serum osmolality (not exceeding 320mOsm/kg) to prevent nephrotoxicity. Consultation by a neurosurgeon revealed the possibility of carrying out a decompressive craniectomy, which, however, was postponed because of refractory thrombocytopenia (lowest platelets 39 × 10³/µL) and coagulopathy, posing the threat of intraoperative bleeding.

At no point during hospitalization was therapeutic anticoagulation carried out, given that active intracranial haemorrhage was present in this patient. Similarly, heparin-based thromboprophylaxis was not carried out because of severe thrombocytopenia. Considering the possibility of catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS), plasma exchange was considered, but the patient’s haemodynamic instability and poor suitability of vascular access precluded implementation of the procedure. Administration of IVIG was not done, since one of the indications of using such medication in this case i.e., thrombocytopenia that does not respond to steroids could not be reliably proven. Broad spectrum antibacterial therapy was started empirically using intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g at an interval of eight.

Haematological support comprised four units of packed red cells and eight units of single-donor platelets over the course of admission. Though treated intensively, consciousness did not return; vital signs stayed unstable. Neurological responses continued minimal despite intervention measures. In view of the clinical trajectory, a thorough and compassionate discussion was conducted with the patient’s family regarding the extremely poor neurological and overall prognosis.

Discussion

This case presents an unusual combination of multivalvular Libman-Sacks endocarditis, hemorrhagic stroke, and coexisting malaria infection in a young adult with an acute flare of SLE and proven antiphospholipid syndrome. There are three main elements to be highlighted in this case: (1) the pathology leading to a hemorrhagic stroke with LSE/APS; (2) the additional pathogenic effects of coexisting Plasmodium infection; and (3) the treatment challenges posed by two conditions requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

Libman-Sacks endocarditis is a sterile form of valvulitis that occurs in SLE/APS and frequently affects the mitral and aortic valves [8]. Indeed, the echocardiography of our patient revealed thickening of the mitral and aortic valves with moderate-severe regurgitation (and mild stenosis), which may be due to Libman-Sacks endocarditis. It should be noted that vegetations are much more frequent in SLE patients who have anti-phospholipid antibodies [9], and the prevalence of valvular heart disease (localization of leaflet thickening and vegetations) is reported to be about 30–80% among APS patients (mainly mitral followed by the aortic valves) [10]. In rare cases, LSE could even be responsible for mitral stenosis and necessitate replacement of the valve [11]. Significantly, embolic phenomena are quite common among the patients with LSE vegetations, such as cerebrovascular accidents observed in ~ 20% of those having vegetations of LS [8, 12].

A haemorrhagic stroke is a rare manifestation of SLE and one that is often encountered in active disease. The risk of intracranial haemorrhage in SLE is 2–3 times higher compared to healthy individuals, while the risk of subarachnoid haemorrhage is 4-fold [8, 13]. It may be related to the involvement of endothelium and vessels in SLE pathogenesis as a result of vasculitis in small vessels and immune complex deposition causing wall fragility [8, 13]. Individual factors, such as severe thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count: 39 × 10³/µL), a high titer of aPL antibodies (IgG anti-cardiolipin 72 GPL U/mL; anti-β2GPI IgG 58 U/mL; lupus anticoagulant), and malarial consumptive coagulopathy, led to increased bleeding.

Presence of high-titer aPL antibodies, presence of valvular disease along with first-trimester pregnancy loss in the current patient, satisfies more than one criterion for APS (revised Sydney Criteria) [17, 15]. Although APS is traditionally a thrombotic disorder, hemorrhagic stroke in this case can be understood through the combination of thrombocytopenic coagulopathy superimposed on vasculitis affecting the endothelium. The presence of all three types of aPL antibodies (lupus anticoagulant positivity, high-titer aCL IgG and high-titer anti-β2 GPI IgG positivity) - called ‘triple positivity’ – carries the greatest risks for thrombosis and catastrophic events among aPL positive individuals [17, 18].

Regarding CAPS: although there was multi-system involvement (CNS, heart, kidneys and haematological manifestations), the criteria of CAPS were not satisfied in this particular case. According to the literature [20], the presence of involvement of at least three different organ systems at the same time or within a week period with evidence of histopathological features of microangiopathy is needed to define CAPS. Due to the lack of histopathological evidence of thrombotic microangiopathy, the diagnosis of CAPS cannot be considered confirmed in this case, and thus, multi-system involvement should be explained by SLE exacerbation, APS, thrombocytopenic coagulopathy and concurrent malaria.

Another distinguishing characteristic of the case was that the patient suffered from an acute mixed malaria (Plasmodium vivax + Plasmodium falciparum) while being immunocompromised due to lupus. Malaria is rather uncommon in patients with lupus since they are usually treated with antimalaria drugs, such as hydroxychloroquine. Some genetic studies even state that there is a direct negative correlation between lupus manifestation and malaria presence. However, malaria infection itself may trigger a lupus flare up. In this particular case, the patient stopped taking hydroxychloroquine on her own three months before admission, losing the protection provided by it against lupus exacerbation and malaria infection.

With regard to the significantly raised CRP (42.2 mg/dL) and neutrophilic leucocytosis, which are not usually present in a flare-up of SLE, but would be expected in an infection, the infectious disease work-up undertaken [16] was appropriate. Infections can never be ruled out as one of the precipitating factors and the patient has been treated accordingly.

For patients diagnosed with or suspected of developing antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) / catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome (CAPS), it is recommended to use a combination of therapy involving therapeutic anticoagulation, high-dose steroids, and plasma exchange or intravenous immunoglobulin [20, 17]; however, both anticoagulation due to active intracranial haemorrhage and plasma exchange due to hemodynamic instability were not possible. Here, we observe the scenario when the treatment of choice for one potentially fatal disease becomes a contraindication due to another disease being present.

Though cardiac surgery could have been thought of to repair the severe regurgitation, the risks involved were prohibitive due to the stroke and thrombocytopenia; generally, surgery is reserved for cases where conservative treatment fails [21]. Neurology and Neurosurgery addressed the stroke and edema (mannitol, sedation, etc.) [14, 19], although the risk of performing a surgery for decompression was too much.

The following lessons can be taken from the presented case: (1) The necessity to undertake echocardiography as part of systematic follow-up of all SLE patients who are positive for aPL because of the very frequent valvular involvement which poses embolic risks; (2) The necessity to do comprehensive aPL paneling (anti-cardiolipin antibodies IgG and IgM, anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibodies and lupus anticoagulant) not only when SLE is diagnosed, but also at other crucial stages; (3) The need for complete adherence to hydroxychloroquine, which provides protection against thrombosis, flares, and LSE progression and which would become totally useless in case of discontinuing the treatment by oneself; (4) the need to coordinate the approach between rheumatology, cardiology, neurology, haematology and critical care in complicated SLE patients; and (5) the assessment of the geographical risks of infections in tropical areas as SLE may be mimicked.

Conclusion

In this instance, we see a very rare scenario of multivalvular Libman-Sacks endocarditis associated with hemorrhagic stroke and mixed Plasmodium malarial infection occurring in an SLE flare-up complicated with antiphospholipid syndrome. The patient developed a hemorrhagic stroke because of embolic vegetations in addition to thrombocytopenia, malarial coagulopathy, and vasculopathy from the lupus, which made the combination of neurosurgery and anti-coagulant therapy impossible. This case highlights the importance of routine surveillance through echocardiography and aPL antibodies among all SLE patients, compliance with the use of hydroxychloroquine and immunosuppressive drugs, and referral to other specialists in the case of multi-organ disease. It becomes crucial for physicians working in malaria-endemic countries to appreciate the dual role of infection in triggering lupus flare-ups and causing a potential conflict among therapies. Prognosis among these cases is invariably poor; early diagnosis with preemptive treatment is the only solution available to us.
