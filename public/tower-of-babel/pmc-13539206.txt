A Systematic Review of Health Literacy in People Living With Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Saleha Mahomed‐Kola, Jennifer Watermeyer, Aline Ferreira‐Correia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimHealth literacy (HL) is important for People with Parkinson's Disease (PwPD), as the complexity of symptom management requires individuals to understand and apply health information across diverse healthcare contexts. This systematic review aims to provide a narrative synthesis of worldwide evidence on HL in PwPD by synthesizing overarching themes that demonstrate the ways in which HL affects disease management.MethodsA narrative, thematic synthesis of the included studies was done. Articles were accessed from 13 online databases and 2 AI search engines using the keywords (“health literacy” OR “ehealth literacy” OR “education intervention” OR “knowledge” OR “training” OR “access to resources”) AND “Parkinson's Disease.” Peer‐reviewed empirical research evaluating HL in PwPD was assessed for evidence strength.ResultsSixteen articles met the inclusion criteria. Four themes were identified: (1) Knowledge and understanding of Parkinson's disease; (2) Resources for building health literacy; (3) Caregiver health literacy; and (4) Access to care. Eleven studies were conducted in the Global North and five in the Global South, confirming the underrepresentation of Global South populations in HL research. Health literacy levels varied and were shaped by regional differences and information sources. Caregivers' HL affected care quality and burden, with educational programs showing potential to improve outcomes. Misconceptions persisted across regions, including beliefs in a cure and misunderstanding of symptoms. Disparities in education and access to care were tied to socioeconomic factors and hindered effective disease management.ConclusionTargeted educational interventions are necessary to correct misconceptions, promote digital health tools for self‐management, and address disparities in care. Improving HL in PwPD is key to improving their quality of life (QoL). Trial Registration: PROSPERO (CRD42024512506).

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive, chronic neurodegenerative disorder characterized by both motor and nonmotor symptoms. Effective management of PD requires a comprehensive understanding of the disease, including the need for consistent medication adherence and symptom monitoring [1].

Health literacy (HL) in the context of PD refers to the capacity to understand and use health information related to PD such as knowledge about symptoms, treatments, and self‐care in order to make appropriate healthcare decisions [2]. It plays an important role in supporting individuals with PD in managing their condition and making informed health decisions [3]. Low HL has been associated with greater disease severity, more frequent hospitalizations, increased caregiver burden, and overall worse health outcomes [2]. For PwPD, strong HL skills are essential to navigate complex medication regimens, recognize symptoms, and access necessary healthcare services, all of which contribute to an improved Quality of Life (QoL) [4].

This review synthesizes worldwide evidence on HL in PwPD, since a global perspective helps identify patterns and gaps [5]. By focusing on HL as the central construct, it is understood that key facets of HL like knowledge and education influence PD management and outcomes [6]. For example, lower education (often linked with lower HL) in PwPD correlates with poorer disease management and adherence [6].

In many low‐ and middle‐income countries, increasing life expectancy has led to a growing burden of chronic conditions such as PD, while health systems remain constrained by limited specialist services, and low public awareness, making HL important for symptom recognition and disease management [7, 8]. Health system constraints such as shortages of neurologists, delayed diagnosis, limited access to pharmacological and rehabilitative services, and reliance on primary care or traditional healers mean that PwPD often depend heavily on their own understanding of the condition to seek care and manage symptoms [5, 8].

Given the unique epidemiological profile of PwPD in Global South populations, it is necessary to explore how HL can be tailored to meet the needs of these communities. There is evidence that PD‐related research (and HL research in general) is disproportionately concentrated in the Global North, with far fewer studies emerging from Global South settings [4, 9, 10]. Thus, interventions proposed on the basis of the available evidence, which derives largely from the Global North, may not be applicable to the Global South.

To the authors' knowledge, this is the first systematic review with a narrative thematic synthesis to position HL as the central construct in PD and to examine disparities between the Global North and the Global South. It is complemented by a subsequent scoping review [11], which mapped the extent of the literature without a regional equity analysis. In consolidating this information, this review can inform healthcare policies and clinical practices aimed at improving HL for PwPD by identifying areas where educational resources and interventions are lacking. Furthermore, this review can support the development of targeted, culturally appropriate materials that meet the specific needs of underrepresented populations.

This review was conducted as a narrative systematic review because the existing body of research on this topic is limited, heterogeneous, and methodologically diverse, rendering a meta‐analysis inappropriate [12]. There is also a lack of a sufficient pool of homogeneous quantitative studies which precludes statistical aggregation. Furthermore, studies operationalize HL inconsistently and make use of nonstandardized instruments which prevents meaningful comparisons of effect sizes across studies [13]. Given this fragmentation, a narrative systematic approach is both methodologically justified and necessary, as it allows for the structured integration of diverse forms of evidence that cannot be captured through quantitative pooling alone [14].

The review organizes findings into themes derived from shared patterns across international contexts, with particular attention to disparities between the Global North and the Global South and their implications for future research and healthcare interventions.

Materials and Methods

The objective of this paper is to provide a narrative synthesis of studies examining HL in PwPD, offering a qualitative synthesis of findings from quantitative, qualitative, and mixed methods studies. Specifically, it aims to identify overarching themes that capture how HL shapes disease management and PwPD experiences across diverse contexts; to identify conceptual, methodological, and geographical gaps in the current evidence; and to inform the development of future HL research and interventions for PwPD globally. To achieve these objectives, the following research questions were examined: 1.What is the global evidence on HL in PwPD?2.What contextual factors across global regions influence HL outcomes?3.What gaps exist in the current global literature on HL in PwPD, particularly with respect to populations in the Global South?

Due to the methodological diversity across studies, heterogeneous outcome measures, and inconsistent operationalization of HL, the data were not suitable for meta‐analysis [15]. In line with established guidance for systematic reviews where statistical pooling is inappropriate, the study characteristics and findings were systematically tabulated, and a narrative synthesis was undertaken using a thematic analysis approach [12].

The narrative synthesis followed the framework proposed by Popay et al. [12], which recommends organizing and interpreting evidence through the identification of recurring patterns, relationships, and themes across studies. Thematic analysis was used as an analytic tool within the narrative synthesis to enable the structured integration of diverse forms of evidence and to capture how HL was conceptualized, expressed, and associated with outcomes in PwPD across different contexts. This approach facilitates transparency, conceptual coherence, and meaningful interpretation of complex and noncomparable data [12].

This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [16, 17].

Literature Search Strategy

Multiple databases as well as artificial intelligence search engines were used to adequately identify all literature related to the topic [18], including: EBSCOHost; JSTOR; MEDLINE; Oxford Journals Online; ProQuest Central; PsycINFO; PubMed; SABINET; SAGE Journals Online; ScienceDirect; SpringerLink; Taylor & Francis Journals; Wiley Online Library; Research Rabbit; and Elicit. Reference lists of the chosen articles were examined for any further qualifying articles. During the article selection process, the following Boolean combination [19] was used: (“Health Literacy” OR “eHealth literacy” OR “Education Intervention” OR “Knowledge” OR “Training” OR “Access to Resources”) AND “Parkinson's Disease.” The study protocol was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024512506) in February 2024. Thereafter, the literature searches were conducted between March 2024 and July 2024.

The following inclusion criteria were adopted: 1.Peer‐reviewed empirical research articles.2.Evidence relating directly to HL domains that involve functional, communicative, and critical HL, as well as disease‐specific knowledge relevant to HL (i.e., a person's understanding of PD including knowledge of core symptoms, disease progression, treatment options, medication use, self‐management strategies, and when and how to access appropriate healthcare services).3.The sample populations across the included studies comprised PwPD sampled alone, PwPD sampled together with their caregivers, and PwPD sampled alongside other population groups.4.Research articles related to the current state of HL including the context of modern healthcare and technology.5.Studies reported in English, that were quantitative, qualitative or had mixed method designs.6.Full‐text articles available in electronic format.

Relevance and Quality Appraisal

To ensure consistency and methodological rigor, a structured consensus process was followed. The first author (S. M.‐K.) extracted and screened all titles and abstracts of the identified studies on Rayyan (software for systematic reviews) [20], in accordance with the inclusion/exclusion criteria. Thereafter, a second reviewer (A. F.‐C.) was invited onto Rayyan to conduct an independent review of the articles. The primary and secondary reviewer first compared their decisions and discussed any discrepancies in article selection, data extraction, or interpretation. When disagreements arose, they engaged in deliberation to clarify reasoning and attempt to reach agreement. If consensus could not be achieved through this process, a third reviewer (J. W.) independently evaluated the conflicting articles. All conflicts were resolved by consensus between all three members of the team.

Included articles were assessed using the criteria proposed in the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) [21] and the Mixed Method Appraisal Tool (MMAT) [22]. Two appraisal tools were used to ensure methodological appropriateness and rigor across the diverse study designs included in this review. The CASP was applied to qualitative and quantitative studies, as it is specifically designed to assess methodological quality, credibility, and risk of bias within single‐method designs. In contrast, the MMAT was used for mixed‐methods studies, as it is uniquely structured to evaluate the quality of qualitative and quantitative components individually, as well as the integration of methods within a single study.

Appraisal items were answered Yes (1 point), Can't Tell (0.5 points). or No (0 points). Numerical scoring, although not required by the CASP guidance, provides a transparent and replicable summary of methodological quality across heterogeneous designs and has precedent in systematic reviews in psychology [23]. Quantitative studies were appraised using the adapted 11‐item CASP for quantitative studies [21, 23], with a prespecified inclusion cut‐off of 4 following Laher and Hassem [23]. Qualitative studies were appraised using the 9‐item CASP qualitative checklist [21]; scores of 8–9 were classified as strong, 4–7 as moderate, and 0–3 as weak, with weak studies excluded. Mixed‐methods studies were appraised using the five MMAT criteria [22], with a cut‐off of 3. These cut‐offs were set deliberately low so as to exclude studies with substantive methodological flaws while retaining the breadth of evidence a narrative synthesis requires. Appraisal scores were used to describe the strength of the evidence, not to weight the synthesis. No study was excluded on quality grounds (Tables 1 and 2). A formal certainty‐of‐evidence assessment (e.g., GRADE) was not undertaken, as the heterogeneity of study designs, outcomes, and operationalizations of HL across the included studies precludes meaningful outcome‐level certainty ratings. The CASP and MMAT scores were instead used to describe the strength of the evidence.

Search Results

The search and selection process is outlined in Figure 1. The search identified 2530 records: 1012 from databases, 1508 from artificial intelligence search engines, and 10 from reference lists. Duplicate records (n = 976) were removed; these comprised records flagged automatically in Rayyan [20] and verified by the first author, together with further duplicates identified manually during title and abstract screening. The full texts of the remaining 1554 records were retrieved, screened, and imported into Zotero (referencing software) [40 ]; 1389 records that did not meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria were excluded. Of the 165 reports examined more closely, a further 118 did not meet the inclusion criteria on full‐text review, leaving 47 reports assessed for eligibility. The second reviewer (A. F.‐C.) excluded 10 articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria as defined above. The remaining 37 articles were reviewed by the first author and both reviewers (A. F.‐C. and J. W.); 21 conflicts were identified and resolved by consensus, resulting in the exclusion of scoping and/or systematic reviews (n = 12) and studies that focused on HL solely in other population groups (n = 9). This left a final total of 16 studies for review, in accordance with PRISMA guidelines [17].

Data Extraction and Analysis

The data extracted from the selected articles were systematically compiled and organized by region. Studies were classified as Global North or Global South according to the UNCTAD classification of developed and developing economies [41], cross‐checked against World Bank income groups [42]. On this criterion, studies from the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, Sweden, Norway, Ireland, Japan, and the Republic of Korea were categorized as Global North, and studies from South Africa, Malaysia, and Thailand as Global South. The terms developed and developing were used as a standard way to describe differences in geographic locations of the research. It was not used to suggest that all countries within each group are the same [43, 44]. To avoid ad hoc assignment, each study was classified according to the UNCTAD taxonomy of developed and developing economies [41], cross‐checked against World Bank income groups [42].

Descriptive data extracted for the 16 studies included the study details (author, year, title, journal, and country); type of study (quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods); sample characteristics (number, age, and educational level of participants); and the critical appraisal score of each selected article. Given the methodological diversity, the data were tabulated and a narrative thematic analysis was undertaken [12].

To ensure transparency and methodological rigor, HL‐relevant data were systematically extracted from the results or findings sections of the included studies, consistent with Thomas and Harden [45]. Repeated immersion in each article informed initial coding based on recurring words, concepts, and ideas (e.g., understanding, education, access, and misconceptions). Codes were grouped into descriptive categories and iteratively refined into higher‐order themes based on shared meaning across studies. In line with guidance on narrative synthesis and thematic analysis [12, 45, 46], theme development prioritized interpretive coherence and conceptual patterns rather than frequency of occurrence. Themes were refined through iterative analysis and peer debriefing with co‐authors, with discrepancies discussed until consensus was reached. To enhance transparency and traceability, illustrative extracts were reproduced verbatim, verified against the published full texts, and presented in Table 5 with their corresponding page numbers, allowing a clear link between the primary study findings and the resulting synthesis [12].

Results

The results reflect aspects and impacts of HL in PwPD. These findings are intentionally presented as expressions or consequences of HL, rather than as constructs separate from it, because HL is operationalized inconsistently across the PD literature and is frequently examined indirectly through related domains such as knowledge, self‐management, understanding of the condition, and access to care. This approach allows for a conceptually coherent synthesis of diverse evidence and ensures that relevant studies are not excluded due to variability in terminology or measurement of HL.

Study Characteristics

The 16 studies comprised 11 conducted in the Global North (Table 1) and 5 in the Global South (Table 2). Global North studies originated from the United States (n = 2), the United Kingdom (n = 1), Germany (n = 1), Sweden (n = 3), Norway (n = 1), Ireland (n = 1), Japan (n = 1), and the Republic of Korea (n = 1). Global South studies originated from South Africa (n = 2), Malaysia (n = 2), and Thailand (n = 1). No studies were identified from Central or South America, and none from low‐income countries. This may partly reflect the restriction to English‐language publication.

Educational attainment varied more within regions than a binary North–South divide implies. In the United States, the mean education was 16.1 years in one study and only 1% of participants in another had no formal education. In Germany, 45% of PwPD had university‐level education, although 22% had little or no formal schooling. In Sweden, 42%–46% of participants had completed university in one study and 52% in another had more than 12 years of education. In Japan, 52.6% of participants held junior college or higher qualifications, whereas the Korean sample averaged 9.04 years of education (SD = 4.30).

Education levels among PwPD in Global South settings varied substantially. In Thailand, 50.9% of participants held a bachelor's degree and a further 14.8% held postgraduate qualifications [38]. In Malaysia, tertiary education ranged from 27.6% to 36.7%, with the majority holding secondary qualifications [36, 39]. South African studies showed the most pronounced disparities: 10% of PwPD had tertiary education in one sample [37], and 76% of PwPD in the private healthcare sector had tertiary education compared with 4.8% in the public sector [35].

Quantitative designs predominated (12 of 16 studies): 10 cross‐sectional [35, 36, 38, 24, 26, 28, 31, 32, 37, 39], one quasi‐experimental case–control [29], and one pre–post intervention design [33]. Three studies were qualitative [27, 30, 34] and one used mixed methods [25]. Qualitative samples were small to medium in size, ranging from 7 to 19 PwPD [27, 30, 34].

Sample sizes ranged from 7 to 346 PwPD in the Global North and from 25 to 211 PwPD in the Global South. Age distributions were skewed toward older adults, with means and medians of 65–75 years in 15 of the 16 studies; the Norwegian mixed‐diagnosis sample averaged 53.3 years [33]. Critical appraisal scores were high: quantitative studies scored 9–11 out of 11 on the adapted CASP, qualitative studies scored 8–8.5 out of 9, and the single mixed‐methods study scored 4 out of 5 on the MMAT (Tables 1 and 2). No study was rated weak.

Definition and operationalization of health literacy across the 16 included studies.

Across the included studies, HL was conceptualized and operationalized heterogeneously, reflecting both explicit and implicit approaches to understanding HL in PwPD. In the Global North studies, HL was defined using formal, skills‐based frameworks, emphasizing an individual's capacity to obtain, process, understand, and apply health information to make appropriate health decisions [25, 28]. These studies commonly operationalized HL using standardized instruments, such as the Rapid Estimate of Adult Literacy in Medicine (REALM) and the Short Test of Functional Health Literacy for Adults (S‐TOFHLA), or validated medication knowledge and adherence measures, thereby primarily capturing functional health literacy and its association with treatment understanding and outcomes [25, 28].

A second group of studies adopted a broader educational and self‐management perspective, conceptualizing HL as disease‐specific knowledge, self‐care capacity, and engagement in health‐promoting behaviors [26, 29, 32]. In these studies, HL was operationalized through structured surveys, questionnaires, or participation in education and self‐management programs, such as national Parkinson education initiatives, which assessed understanding of symptoms, treatments, and daily disease management [29, 32]. These approaches extend beyond basic literacy skills to include applied and communicative aspects of HL, such as interpreting health information and acting on it in everyday contexts.

Other studies conceptualized HL more implicitly, framing it through related constructs such as e‐health literacy, access to information, sense of coherence, beliefs, attitudes, and understanding of disease [27, 30, 33, 34]. These studies typically employed semi‐structured interviews, workshops, co‐design methods, or qualitative surveys to explore how PwPD and their caregivers seek, interpret, and use health information, particularly in relation to digital health, service navigation, and communication with healthcare professionals [27, 30]. Rather than directly measuring HL, these studies captured experiential and contextual dimensions of HL, highlighting how deficits in communication, digital access, or health system responsiveness may contribute to inequalities.

In Global South studies, HL was most frequently operationalized through assessments of disease‐specific knowledge, focusing on recognition of motor and nonmotor symptoms, understanding of diagnosis, treatment options, and disease progression [36, 38, 39]. These studies commonly utilized locally adapted questionnaires or structured interviews, reflecting contextual priorities such as delayed diagnosis, misconceptions about Parkinson's disease, and variable access to health information [35, 37]. In the Global South, structural and systemic influences on HL were also highlighted, including disparities between public and private healthcare sectors, limited internet access, and sociocultural beliefs shaping disease understanding [35, 37].

Overall, the literature conceptualizes HL in PwPD along a continuum, ranging from narrowly defined functional literacy skills to broader, contextually embedded understandings encompassing disease knowledge, self‐management, digital literacy, and access to care. This variability in definition and measurement reflects the absence of a united HL framework within the PD literature.

Given the substantial variability in how HL was defined and operationalized, the synthesis examined how HL was expressed within disease understanding, caregiving, and healthcare access, rather than treating HL as a uniformly measured construct. The first theme captures what PwPD and caregivers know and misunderstand about the condition; the second, the mechanisms through which HL is built (educational interventions and digital health); the third, the relational demands HL places on caregivers; and the fourth, the structural conditions that shape access to information and services. Subthemes denote distinct facets within each theme, not intervention types or study categories. Four themes were identified across the included studies: knowledge and understanding of Parkinson's disease, resources for building health literacy, caregiver health literacy, and access to care (Table 4). The first theme captures both what PwPD and their families know about the condition and the erroneous beliefs and misconceptions that persist alongside that knowledge. The second reflects the mechanisms studies used or proposed to build HL, spanning educational interventions and digital health/eHealth approaches. The third extends HL beyond the patient to caregivers, for whom it intersects with the burden of care. The final theme concerns access to care, with a single subtheme addressing disparities and underrepresented groups. Each theme and its subthemes are presented below with illustrative extracts from the included studies.

Knowledge and Understanding of Parkinson's Disease

This theme captures how PwPD and their caregivers acquire, interpret, and apply information about the condition: their awareness and knowledge of PD, and the erroneous beliefs that persist alongside it [36, 38, 24, 26, 28, 31, 32, 37, 39]. Across studies, knowledge and misconception were not opposites but co‐occurred, often within the same samples [26, 37, 39].

Awareness and Knowledge

Knowledge of PD was incomplete in every study that measured it, and the pattern of gaps was consistent across regions despite marked differences in educational attainment and specialist access. Nonmotor symptoms and treatment specifics were the most poorly understood aspects of the disease wherever they were assessed: all participants in a United States sample held at least one misconception about nonmotor symptoms [26], Thai PwPD showed significant knowledge gaps in diagnosis, therapeutic options, and disease progression [38], and 43% of German PwPD showed deficits in at least one of four medication knowledge domains [28]. In South Africa, only 18% of an isiXhosa‐speaking sample recognized PD from its symptoms, although PwPD and traditional healers were more knowledgeable than the general public [37].

Sociodemographic predictors of knowledge were broadly consistent across settings: lower education, polypharmacy, depression, and cognitive and motor decline predicted poorer medication knowledge in Germany [28], while younger, working‐age PwPD, women, and those with longer disease duration scored higher in Thailand [38]. The direction of the age effect diverged, however: older age predicted greater PD knowledge in South Africa [37] but poorer knowledge in Germany and Thailand [28, 38], suggesting that what age indexes (accumulated exposure to the disease vs. distance from formal education) differs across contexts.

How PwPD acquire information also shaped knowledge. Swedish PwPD, who typically had one neurologist visit per year and only half of whom had regular access to PD‐specific providers, relied primarily on online sources (36%), patient organizations (29%), and healthcare professionals (25%), with internet reliance concentrated among younger participants and women [32]. In Japan, critical HL (the capacity to appraise and apply information), but not functional or communicative HL, was associated with greater physical activity after adjusting for demographic and clinical variables [24]. Together, these findings indicate that higher‐order appraisal skills, rather than basic literacy, translate knowledge into disease‐management behavior.

Erroneous Beliefs and Misconceptions About Parkinson's Disease

Misconceptions were reported in both regions, but their content tracked the local information environment. In South Africa, causal misattributions dominated: participants attributed PD to mental illness, stress, strong emotions, consumption of certain foods or drinks, other diseases, and witchcraft [37]. In Malaysia and Thailand, the most prevalent errors concerned curability and medication safety: 50%–80% of PwPD and caregivers held misconceptions such as the beliefs that PD always involves tremors, is hereditary, results from past actions or can be cured, and roughly half feared that PD medications damage internal organs [36, 39]. In the United States, misconceptions were treatment‐specific: 82% believed levodopa stops working as the disease progresses and 77% believed deep brain stimulation improves balance and reduces falls, while fewer than half could answer questions on levodopa dosing despite more than 75% managing their own medication [26].

Two cross‐study patterns are notable. First, misconceptions were not confined to low‐resource settings or to less educated samples: they were prevalent in the United States sample irrespective of gender, provider type, or education level [26], indicating that access to well‐resourced healthcare does not, by itself, protect against misinformation. Second, misconception co‐occurred with overconfidence where both were measured: 90% of the United States sample rated their own knowledge as sufficient [26], and Malaysian PwPD and caregivers were more knowledgeable than the general public yet still endorsed a cure [36, 39]. Misconceptions therefore warrant direct correction; they do not resolve with healthcare contact or general education alone.

Resources for Building Health Literacy

This theme captures the mechanisms through which HL is developed including structured educational interventions and digital health resources [25, 27, 29, 30, 33].

Educational Interventions

Three structured programs were evaluated, all in the Global North [25, 29, 33]. All three improved outcomes for PwPD. The United States TeleDREAMS program improved HL post‐intervention, particularly when PwPD participated alongside care partners, and its remote format reduced dropout relative to in‐person delivery [25]. Sweden's National Parkinson School improved perceived health status, attitudes, and self‐management skills [29]. Norway's Bodyknowledging Program, delivered over seven sessions in group or individual formats, produced positive changes in sense of coherence, especially among community participants [33].

Care partner benefit was the consistent exception: the Swedish program produced no significant change among care partners [29], and in the United States program, gains were concentrated in PwPD, with joint participation amplifying patient benefit [25]. No educational intervention was evaluated in a Global South setting, despite these settings showing the largest knowledge gaps; an inverse relationship between where interventions are tested and where they are most needed.

Digital Health/eHealth

Two studies examined digital health directly, in the United Kingdom and Sweden [27, 30]. In both, digital tools were valued for self‐management and co‐care: 84% of Swedish respondents saw value in a proposed eHealth service, and 86%–97% rated its six co‐care features (self‐tracking, pre‐visit forms, graphical visualization, clinical decision support, self‐care recommendations, and asynchronous communication) as important [30]. In both studies, benefit was conditional on eHealth literacy, technological acceptance, and organizational factors such as teamwork and administrative burden [27, 30]. Digital health therefore operates as an amplifier as it extends self‐management for PwPD with adequate digital HL and resources, and widens inequities for those without.

Caregiver Health Literacy

Caregivers' capacity to understand and use health information shaped both the care they provided and the burden they carried [29, 31, 34, 36]. Where caregiver HL was limited, caregivers had difficulty recognizing symptoms and managing care, felt overwhelmed, and were often unaware of available support.

Burden of Care

Four studies linked caregiver HL to burden, and the direction of the association was consistent: less knowledge, greater burden. In Korea, caregivers with poorer PD knowledge reported greater burden, particularly when providing intensive daily care; burden was higher among spouse caregivers and caregivers of female PwPD, and better disease understanding reduced strain most clearly among spouses [31]. Irish caregivers reported high emotional and physical demands and were largely unfamiliar with palliative care, but recognized its value once informed [34]. In Malaysia, decision‐making was generally shared between PwPD and caregivers, yet caregivers held the same misconceptions as patients, and only about half reported adequate community support [36]. Read against the intervention findings, in which care partners benefited less than PwPD from patient‐focused programs [25, 29], these results indicate that caregiver HL needs are distinct and are not met by assuming indirect benefit from programs designed for patients.

Access to Care

This theme refers to the ability of PwPD and their caregivers to obtain the medical resources and information needed to manage the disease effectively. Inequalities in healthcare systems created disparities that limited access for some populations [27, 35, 36].

Disparities, Access, and Underrepresented Groups

Access barriers were documented in both regions but differed in kind. In South Africa, the divide was sectoral: PwPD in the private healthcare sector received a wider range of treatments and reported higher QoL across domains than those in the public sector [35]. In the United Kingdom, the barrier was sociotechnical: participants from an ethnic minority group had difficulty accessing healthcare and using technology for remote consultations despite long residence in the United Kingdom, good education, and English proficiency, and assistive technologies that enabled digital access for participants with sight and hearing impairments were costly [27]. In Malaysia, the gap was at the community level: support groups were regarded as important in PD care, but only half of participants reported adequate access to them [36].

Across settings, socioeconomic position determined not only access to treatment but also access to the information environment on which HL depends [27, 35]. Groups already underrepresented in PD research, including ethnic minorities, public‐sector patients, and people with sensory impairments, faced the steepest barriers, compounding the geographic imbalance of the evidence base itself.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes global evidence on HL in PwPD to examine how HL influences disease understanding, management, and engagement with healthcare systems across diverse sociocultural contexts. The findings foreground HL as a multidimensional process through which PwPD and their caregivers apply health‐related information to manage the condition. By positioning HL as the central construct, this review aimed to consolidate fragmented evidence in the literature, and inform contextually relevant interventions for PwPD worldwide. This global perspective is particularly important given evidence that lower educational attainment is often associated with lower HL [6]. This is linked to poorer disease management and treatment adherence among PwPD which affects populations in low‐resource settings.

Educational interventions emerged as a key mechanism for strengthening HL, particularly when they were structured and sustained. Programs such as TeleDREAMS [25] and the National Parkinson School [29] demonstrate that targeted education can improve self‐management, sense of coherence, and active engagement in care. However, the differential benefits observed for PwPD compared to caregivers [25, 29, 34] indicate that HL needs are relational but not uniform, supporting calls in the broader HL literature for caregiver‐specific interventions rather than assuming indirect benefits from patient‐focused programs. Notably, all three evaluated interventions were conducted in the Global North and none has been tested in the settings where knowledge gaps were largest.

Variation in awareness and knowledge across populations further illustrates how HL is shaped by demographic, clinical, and contextual factors [24, 28, 32]. Across studies, gaps in disease recognition, medication knowledge, and understanding of nonmotor symptoms were common, particularly among older individuals, those with lower education, and those with limited access to specialist care. These findings are consistent with existing evidence linking lower HL to poorer disease management, reduced adherence, and lower QoL in PwPD [2]. Notably, evidence that critical HL was associated with greater physical activity reinforces the importance of higher‐order HL skills, such as appraisal and application of information, beyond basic knowledge [24].

Digital health and eHealth resources offered opportunities to support self‐management and co‐care but also revealed substantial inequities [27]. While digital tools facilitated access to information and communication with healthcare providers, their effectiveness was constrained by digital literacy, technological acceptance, and organizational barriers. These findings mirror broader concerns about the digital divide in HL research, suggesting that digital solutions may exacerbate inequities if implemented without attention to accessibility and contextual relevance.

Erroneous beliefs and misconceptions about Parkinson's disease were evident across both high‐income and low‐ and middle‐income settings. Misunderstandings regarding disease causation, prognosis, medication effects, and curability were widespread and, in some contexts, deeply embedded in cultural belief systems [37]. Importantly, misconceptions were not confined to low‐resource settings [26], challenging assumptions that access to healthcare alone mitigates misinformation and highlighting the need for HL interventions that address belief systems alongside factual knowledge.

Caregiver HL emerged as a critical determinant of caregiver burden, with limited HL associated with increased emotional strain, difficulty managing symptoms, and reduced awareness of available supports [25, 29, 34]. These findings align with caregiving literature indicating that HL mediates caregivers' capacity to navigate services and engage in shared decision‐making [47].

At a structural level, HL was closely linked to access to care, with disparities across healthcare sectors, underrepresented groups, and socioeconomic contexts shaping both information access and health outcomes [35]. Together, these findings support conceptualizations of HL that extend beyond individual skills to include structural and systemic influences. There is a need for comprehensive, culturally sensitive educational initiatives that address misinformation, support early diagnosis, and equip PwPD and caregivers to engage actively in their care [24, 28]. Some programs have shown that caregivers' specific needs must also be addressed [25].

Educational disparities between the Global North and the Global South reflect broader socioeconomic inequalities, although the pattern is not uniform: high proportions of tertiary‐educated PwPD were reported in the United States, Germany, Sweden, Japan, and Thailand [24, 25, 28, 29, 32, 38], whereas the Korean sample averaged 9 years of schooling (M = 9.04, SD = 4.30; [31]). The South African samples showed the widest disparities: 76% of PwPD in the private healthcare sector had tertiary education compared with 4.8% in the public sector [35], and in a community‐based Xhosa‐speaking sample, only 10% had college or university education while 52% had no schooling or less than Grade 7 [37]. The starkest divide was within‐country where the private–public split in South Africa points to structural inequities in access to education and care that a country‐level North–South binary does not capture [35]. Only 5 of the 16 included studies were conducted in the Global South, and none in a low‐income country, so the evidence base remains thinnest precisely where health‐system constraints make HL most consequential [5, 7]. While this review was under revision, a scoping review of HL in PD was published [11], reporting similar findings: gaps in functional HL, limited assessment of interactive and critical HL, and no standardized PD‐specific HL interventions. This convergence reinforces the need for validated, multidimensional HL measures and intervention research in underrepresented populations, particularly in the Global South.

Conclusion

This review addresses the gaps identified in the literature by providing the first narrative synthesis that positions HL as a central construct in PD across global contexts. It integrates evidence from diverse settings to identify shared patterns and contextual differences in how HL shapes disease understanding, caregiving, and access to care. The synthesis highlights four interrelated themes: how PD is known and understood, the resources through which HL is built, the HL demands placed on caregivers, and the structural barriers to accessing care. Each of these themes should be addressed through coordinated educational, caregiver‐focused, and system‐level interventions. While progress has been made in improving HL among PwPD through educational interventions, knowledge gaps and misconceptions hinder effective disease management. By refuting common myths, enhancing access to reliable information and improving digital HL, future efforts can ensure better self‐management, improve QoL, and reduce the burden of care.

Limitations

Several factors limit the generalizability and comprehensiveness of these findings. The review included only peer‐reviewed empirical studies published in English. This restriction is a selection bias with direct bearing on the review's conclusions: because English dominates scientific communication, research from non‐Anglophone settings, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries, is less likely to be published, indexed, and retrieved through English‐language databases. The underrepresentation of Global South evidence reported here therefore reflects, at least in part, the language in which science is communicated, and not only where research on HL in PwPD is conducted. Studies older than 10 years were excluded, potentially omitting foundational research on HL in PwPD. Reliance on self‐reported data in many included studies may introduce response bias, particularly for medication adherence and disease knowledge, where participants may overestimate their understanding. Methodological diversity across the included studies limited the comparability of findings and precluded meta‐analysis. The Global North–Global South binary, although anchored here to the UNCTAD classification of economies [41], collapses considerable heterogeneity within regions, and single studies from Thailand and Korea do not represent the wider Asian region (or their income groups). Finally, 11 of the 16 studies were conducted in the Global North, which limits the conclusions that can be drawn about HL in the socioeconomic and cultural contexts where access to care and health information differs most from those represented here; this imbalance should be read in conjunction with the language‐related selection bias noted above.
