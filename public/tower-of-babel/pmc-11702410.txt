The prevalence and outcomes of pre‐admission vitamin D levels in the management of proximal femur fractures
Authors: Aysha Rajeev, Connor Hunter, Saurav Krishnan, Atta Ullah, George Koshy, Gateshead Health Foundation NHS Trust
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe prevalence of low Vitamin D levels (<30 nmol/L) is about 15.7% globally. The aim of this study was to examine the prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in elderly patients with fragility fractures of the hip by estimating 25‐hydroxyvitamin D levels and to assess whether low vitamin D levels at the time of admission affect functional outcomes at 3 months and mortality at 28 days and 1 year.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study of all patients admitted with a fracture of the neck of the femur between January 2018 and March 2022. Data were obtained from the National Hip Fracture Database (NHFD) and Medway software. A total of 1440 patients were included. Patient demographics, including age, sex, fracture pattern, vitamin D levels at the time of admission, functional status at 120 days, and mortality at 1 month and 1 year, were analyzed.ResultsThe average age of the patients was 81.91 years (range 60–108). Of the patients, 1009 (70%) were female and 431 (30%) were male. Vitamin D levels were low in 796 patients (55.3%). Mobility significantly declined in patients with vitamin D deficiency within 3 months after surgery for proximal femur fractures. The 28‐day and one‐year mortality rates were 6.7% and 30.3%, respectively, in patients with low vitamin D levels, compared to 4.7% and 22.3% in those with normal levels. Patients with low vitamin D levels at the time of admission had higher mortality rates at both 28 days and 1 year compared to those with normal levels.ConclusionOur study demonstrates that low vitamin D levels at the time of admission for proximal femur fractures are associated with poorer functional mobility and higher perioperative and 1‐year mortality rates.

INTRODUCTION

Proximal femur fracture risk in the elderly is a function of multiple factors, including bone mineral density (BMD), muscle strength, and balance, all of which have been related to Vitamin D status and function. 1 The commonest mechanism of proximal femur fractures is due to falls, which is directly related to the Vitamin D status. 2 , 3 There is a consistent relationship between neuromuscular function and Vitamin D plasma concentrations. 4 Vitamin D is crucial for intestinal calcium absorption and for maintaining calcium homeostasis and skeletal integrity. 5

Vitamin D [1,25(OH)2D3] is a seco‐steroid that plays a critical role in calcium and phosphate homeostasis. 6 Serum vitamin D levels are positively correlated with BMD values. 7 Vitamin D deficiency leads to a reduction in BMD and increases the risk of bone fractures in elderly people. 8 Hypovitaminosis D is associated with secondary hyperparathyroidism, which increases bone turnover and accelerates bone loss, resulting in an increased risk of fractures. 9 Hypovitaminosis D is also associated with muscle weakness in the lower extremities with consequent poor function and increased risks of falling, further increasing the risk of fracture. 10

Vitamin D deficiency is defined as less than 30 nmol/L of serum 25(OH) vitamin D, and relative insufficiency between 31 and 50 nmol/L, with normal levels greater than 50 nmol/L. 11 The overall pooled prevalence of serum 25(OH)D of less than 30 nmol/L irrespective of age, ethnicity and geographical location is about 15.7%. 12 The physiological maintenance of normal concentrations of Vitamin D is very important in a variety of body functions, such as muscle health and cognitive function, which could affect mobility and risk of falls. 13 Low levels of serum Vitamin D levels in patients with proximal femur fractures are associated with increased morbidity and mortality at 1 year. 14

The aim of the study is to find out the prevalence of Vitamin D levels in proximal femur fractures at the time of admission and whether it affects the outcomes, including mobility at 120 days, 28‐day, and 1‐year mortality.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A retrospective study was conducted on all patients admitted with a fracture of the neck of the femur between January 2018 and March 2022. Data were obtained from the National Hip Fracture Database (NHFD) and Medway software. During this period, 1577 patients were admitted with hip fractures. Patients were excluded from the study if they had metastatic bone tumors, metabolic bone diseases other than osteoporosis, fractures resulting from high‐energy trauma, atypical femoral fractures, or if their records were missing blood test results for 25(OH) vitamin D. After applying these exclusion criteria, 1440 patients were included in the final analysis.

Serum vitamin D levels at the time of admission were collected and analyzed. Patient demographics, including age, sex, fracture pattern, and vitamin D levels at the time of admission, were documented. Pre‐injury mobility was assessed, categorizing patients into those who were independently mobile and those who required a walking aid. These categories were then compared to the patients' mobility status at 120 days post‐surgery. Mortality rates at 1 month and 1 year were also calculated.

All blood samples were taken in the Accident and Emergency department at the time of admission, following the neck of femur pathway protocol. Vitamin D levels were measured by estimating serum 25(OH) vitamin D concentrations, with results reported in nanomoles per liter (nmol/L). In this study, vitamin D levels below 50 nmol/L were classified as low, while levels above 50 nmol/L were considered adequate.

Statistical analysis

The data were first checked for completeness and the distribution of variables was assessed for normality. Continuous variables, such as age and serum vitamin D levels, were summarized using means, standard deviations, and ranges. Vitamin D levels were further categorized into deficient (<30 nmol/L), insufficient (30–50 nmol/L), and normal (>50 nmol/L) for comparative analysis. Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages and compared between groups using Pearson's Chi‐squared test. The association between continuous variables and outcomes, such as mortality at 28 days and 1 year and mobility at 120 days univariable and multivariable logistic regression models were used. Continuous variables were included as both raw and categorized data in regression models to explore potential linear and non‐linear relationships.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 29.0.0), with a p‐value of <0.05 considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 1577 patients were admitted to our unit with fractures of the neck of the femur. Among them, 132 patients (8.4%) did not have their vitamin D levels checked at the time of admission. After applying the exclusion criteria, 1440 patients were included in the study. The average age of the patients was 81.91 years (range 60–108). Of these, 1009 (70%) were female and 431 (30%) were male. The fractures were categorized as 582 extracapsular (40%) and 858 intracapsular (60%).

The recorded pre‐injury mobility status as follows: 525 patients (36.5%) were freely mobile with no aids, 409 patients (28.4%) were mobile outdoors with one aid, 250 patients (17.4%) were mobile outdoors with two aids, 177 patients (12.3%) were mobile indoors but never went outside without help, and 79 patients (5.4%) had no functional mobility (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

Proximal femur fractures are one of the commonest admissions in our hospitals especially with an aging population. 15 Vitamin D deficiency leading to osteomalacia and osteoporosis has been attributed as the principal cause of these difficult fractures. 16 Vitamin D is a fat‐soluble molecule needed for intestinal malabsorption of calcium and phosphates which are need for the normal maintenance of bone metabolism and calcium homeostasis. 17 The National Osteoporosis Association has given clear guidelines regarding the levels of Vitamin D levels in an adult human being. According to them serum 25OHD <30 nmol/L is deficient, serum 25OHD of 30–50 nmol/L may be inadequate, serum 25OHD 50 nmol/L is sufficient for almost the whole population. 11 In our study we have used this guideline to categorize our patients in to those with low and normal Vitamin D levels.

In the Western population the prevalence of Vitamin D deficiency is almost 60% in elderly patients with proximal femur fractures. 18 , 19 , 20 In the Asian population also, various authors have reported high prevalence on Vitamin D deficiency in hip fractures varying from 90.5% in Malay patients,61.1% in Indian patients and 55% in Chinese patients. 21 Other studies from Japan also have reported high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in elderly hip fracture patients, varying between 39% and 74%. 22 , 23 Our study showed an overall prevalence of 55.3% which is similar to the reports from other Western countries.

The incidence of Vitamin D deficiency is quite high in elderly patients. 24 The cutaneous production of Vitamin D decreases with age due to lack of exposure and thinning of the skin due to aging. 25 The other causes of low vitamin levels in the elderly may be due lack of intake and intestinal absorption, decreased production in the liver and kidneys. Guerra et al. in their a case–control study in which serum samples of 25(OH)D were obtained from 110 proximal hip fracture inpatients and 231 control patients without fractures, all over 60 years of age found that patients with proximal hip fracture had significantly decreased serum 25(OH)D levels when compared with the control group. 26 In our study also patients aged above 70 years of age with proximal femur fractures had significantly low Vitamin D levels.

Narula et al. in their study of 196 women aged between 42 and 80 years of age found that 62% had Vitamin D deficiency. 27 But in another study by Simonelli et al. concluded that the mean Vitamin D levels in elderly patients with hip fractures are low, but they were not significantly associated with the age and sex of the patients. 28 In our study there was a difference in the levels of Vitamin D levels between males and females, females having significantly low levels.

The association of Vitamin D levels and the type of proximal femur fractures sustained by elderly patients has been studied in the past. Khan et al. did not find any difference in the 25(OH)D levels between patients with femoral neck and trochanteric fractures. 29 Han et al. also reproduced the same results regarding the type of fracture and serum Vitamin D levels. Our study showed patients with intracapsular fractures had low levels of Vitamin D compared to extracapsular fractures but no statistical difference was found. 30

Hao et al. in their study on 290 hip fracture patients with Vitamin D deficiency found that there is a direct link between the low levels of Vitamin D and mobility at 30 and 60 days post operatively. 31 The authors have also attributed the cause of reduced mobility to poor nutritional status of hip fracture patients both in the pre and post‐operative period. But the studies by Han et al. did not find significance between low Vitamin D levels and ambulatory status. 29 In our study we found that there was a significant deterioration in the mobility status in patients with low Vitamin D levels between pre and post‐operative period especially in patients who were freely mobile with no walking aids.

There is a direct link between the low Vitamin D levels and high mortality rates as reported by various studies. 32 , 33 In patients with fracture neck of femurs, athere are studies which show higher mortality rates. Todly et al. in their study of 199 patients with low Vitamin D levels in fracture neck of femurs has reported an increase in mortality rates varied between 8% and 14%. 34 Fischer et al. in the series of 1820 consecutive fracture neck of femurs found that one of the variables determining the mortality rate was low levels of Vitamin D concentrations. 35 Our study also has showed an in patients with low levels of Vitamin D the perioperative and 1 year mortality was significantly higher compared to those with normal Vitamin D levels at the time of admission.

Limitations of the study

In our study, Body Mass Index (BMI) was not assessed, despite its known association with the prognosis of hip fractures and Vitamin D levels. Additionally, the type of surgery performed and its relationship with Vitamin D levels were not documented. These factors represent important variables that could influence outcomes and should be examined in greater detail in future studies.

CONCLUSIONS

This study highlights the prevalence of Vitamin D deficiency in patients admitted with proximal femur fractures. This finding underscores the potential benefit of adopting a pragmatic approach to offer adequate Vitamin D supplementation to this patient group. Our study also demonstrated that low levels of Vitamin D at the time of admission are associated with poor functional outcomes in the postoperative period, as well as significantly higher perioperative and one‐year mortality rates.

Given that the frequency of falls and fractures significantly increases with age, there is a pressing need for cost‐effective preventive interventions. Vitamin D supplements are both inexpensive and safe, making them a viable candidate for public health interventions and inclusion in clinical practice.
