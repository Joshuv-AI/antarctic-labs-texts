A Class of Axis–Angle Attitude Control Laws for Rotational Systems
Francisco M. F. R. Gonçalves, Ryan M. Bena, Néstor O. Pérez-Arancibia

Abstract
We introduce a new class of attitude control laws for rotational systems, which generalizes the use of the Euler axis–angle representation beyond quaternion-based formulations. Using basic Lyapunov’s stability theory and the notion of extended 𝒦 ∞ \mathcal{K}_{\infty} functions, we developed a method for determining and enforcing the global asymptotic stability of the single fixed point of the resulting closed-loop (CL) scheme. In contrast with traditional quaternion-based methods, the proposed generalized axis–angle approach enables greater flexibility in the design of the control law, which is of great utility when employed in combination with a switching scheme whose transition state depends on the angular velocity of the controlled rotational system. Through simulation and real-time experimental results, we demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach. According to the recorded data, in the execution of high-speed tumble-recovery maneuvers, the new method consistently achieves shorter stabilization times and requires lower control effort relative to those corresponding to the quaternion-based and geometric-control methods used as benchmarks.

For example,

Euler axis with
\sin\frac{\Theta_{\text{e}}}{2}

attitude-error
Euler axis increases. In a similar

geometric-based
attitude control laws implicitly apply torques in the direction of the shorter rotational trajectory required to eliminate the attitude error. As discussed in
[
8
]

[
9
]

In this letter, we introduce a class of attitude control laws applicable to a wide gamut of rotational systems that can be modeled as rigid bodies moving in the
three-dimensional
(
3
D)
space, including
uncrewed aerial vehicles
(UAVs), satellites, and microrobotic swimmers. The proposed method generalizes the use of the Euler
axis–angle
representation in the formulation of the feedback law that stabilizes and drives the rotational motion of the controlled system. It is widely known that global asymptotic stability in the
3
D rotational space cannot be achieved with the use of continuous
time-invariant
feedback controllers. Nonetheless, a common technique used to overcome this topological obstruction is to design discontinuous control laws
[
6
]
. Following this approach, by combining Lyapunov’s stability theory and the notion of extended
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
functions, we developed a method that ensures the existence of a unique CL fixed
attitude-error
quaternion
(AEQ) and the global asymptotic stability of the corresponding equilibrium point, which depends on a
positive-definiteness
condition that the controller gains must satisfy.

A main contribution of the work presented in this letter is the design flexibility enabled by a generalized formulation of the proportional term in the proposed control law; specifically, it allows the user to select a suitable proportional control function based on the platform
and/or
application, with stability guarantees. Furthermore, in contrast to existing
quaternion-based
control methods, the new generalized
axis–angle
attitude control approach ensures a greater proportional action the farther away the system’s state is from the stable CL equilibrium AEQ. This characteristic is especially useful when a scheme of the proposed type is employed in combination with intelligent switching methods capable of taking into account the angular velocity when selecting the direction in which the input torque is applied during operation, as for example done in the case presented in
[
8
]
. We tested the functionality and performance of the introduced approach through simulations and outdoor flight tests. The obtained simulation and experimental data show that compared to two other
high-performance
benchmark controllers—one
quaternion-based
and another geometric—a scheme of the new type is unequivocally superior. Specifically, data obtained through dozens of
high-speed

tumble-recovery
maneuvers show that the new approach consistently achieves shorter stabilization times and requires less control effort, from a statistical standpoint. Due to space constraints, in this letter, we limit the discussion to the attitude control problem; however, a position control scheme of the type presented in
[
18
]
can be straightforwardly integrated with the presented method for attitude control.

III-A

A New Class of Control Laws

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be an extended
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{e}}

\displaystyle=\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}},

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{e}}
is a

. In addition, given a selected interval, there exists a unique

pair representing any geometrically nontrivial attitude error; namely,
\Theta_{\text{e}}\neq 2\pi k

. When the attitude error is trivial, the corresponding rotation axis is not uniquely defined and can take any value in
\mathcal{S}^{2}
. Note, however, that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{e}}
is still well defined at
\Theta_{\text{e}}=0
. For any nontrivial case, the selected domain for

yields a

pair that defines one of two geometrically unique and opposite physical rotations. To deal with this rotational ambiguity, at initial time

is selected to minimize a cost

8
]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{J}\left(k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\text{e}}+k_{\delta}\boldsymbol{\dot{\alpha}}_{\text{e}}+k_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}+\boldsymbol{\dot{\omega}}_{\text{d}}\right)+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{\omega},

in which

,

, and

) into (

&+\left.k_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}+\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\text{d}}\right]+\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{\omega}.\end{split}

;
(iii)
\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{e}}=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}=\boldsymbol{0}
. Also, for (
8a
) to be satisfied, either (ii) or (iii) is true,
\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{e}}=\boldsymbol{0}
, or
\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{e}}\perp\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}
. Since
\boldsymbol{n}_{\text{e}}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}
cannot simultaneously be orthogonal and parallel, the only viable option is either (ii) or (iii). The fulfillment of either (ii) or (iii) requires that the solution to
(
8a
)–(
8c
)
satisfies
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\star}_{\text{e}}=\boldsymbol{0}
. Since
\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}
is a unit vector evolving on
\mathcal{S}^{2}
, it follows that
\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}\perp\boldsymbol{\dot{u}}_{\text{e}}
and, therefore, for (
8c
) to be satisfied,
\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})=0
. Last, recalling that
\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})
is an extended
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
function and, therefore,
\gamma(0)=0
, we conclude that
\Theta_{\text{e}}^{\star}=0
.

It is important to note that for the law specified by (
6
), we selected
\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})
to be an extended
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
function in order to enforce the existence of a unique zero for
\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})
and a unique equilibrium point for the CL dynamics given by
(
7a
)–(
7b
)
, over the selected range of
\Theta_{\text{e}}
. In summary, the CL dynamics resulting from using the control input specified by (
6
) exhibit a unique equilibrium point, given by the
quaternion-vector
pair
\boldsymbol{\mathrlap{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-6.8pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\displaystyle\hskip 1.2pt{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0mu}$}}{\raisebox{-6.8pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\textstyle\hskip 1.2pt{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0mu}$}}{\raisebox{-6.8pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\hskip 1.2pt{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0mu}$}}{\raisebox{-6.8pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\hskip 1.2pt{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0mu}$}}}q}^{\star}_{\text{e}}=\left[1~0~0~0\right]^{T}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{\star}_{\text{e}}=\boldsymbol{0}
. This result does not contradict the topological obstruction discussed in
[
15
]
and references therein, according to which every continuous
time-invariant
CL vector field on
\mathcal{SO}(3)
must exhibit more than one equilibrium point because, for the selected function
\gamma(\cdot)
and range for
\Theta_{\text{e}}
, the term
\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}
is discontinuous at
\Theta_{\text{e}}=2\pi
rad
. As a consequence, the resulting CL dynamics specified by
(
7a
)–(
7b
)
are also discontinuous, which forces the existence of a unique fixed point, similarly to the cases presented in
[
6
]
and references therein. Because of the imposed discontinuity on the CL dynamics, to analyze the system of differential equations given by
(
7a
)–(
7b
)
, we use Carathéodory’s concept of solution as stated in
Definition 2.1
of
[
16
]
.

III-C

Stability Analysis

Theorem 1.

Let the attitude and
angular-velocity
references
,
\boldsymbol{\mathrlap{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-6.8pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\displaystyle\hskip 1.2pt{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0mu}$}}{\raisebox{-6.8pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\textstyle\hskip 1.2pt{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0mu}$}}{\raisebox{-6.8pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\hskip 1.2pt{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0mu}$}}{\raisebox{-6.8pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\hskip 1.2pt{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0mu}$}}}q}_{\text{d}}

and

\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{d}}
,
be smooth and bounded functions of time. Also, let the real controller gains satisfy

k_{\delta}
,
k_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}>0
,
and

k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}>\frac{k_{\delta}k_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}{4}
.
Then, the fixed point

\{\Theta_{\text{e}}^{\star},\;\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}^{\star}\}

of the CL
state-space
rotational dynamics specified by (
7a
) and (
7b
)

is globally asymptotically stable.

Proof.
Let a candidate Lyapunov function be given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}V=&\begin{bmatrix}\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\boldsymbol{u}^{T}_{\text{e}}&\boldsymbol{\omega}^{T}_{\text{e}}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{k_{\delta}^{2}}{2k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}&\dfrac{k_{\delta}}{2k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\\
\dfrac{k_{\delta}}{2k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}&\dfrac{1}{2k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}\\
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}\\
\end{bmatrix}\\
&+\int_{0}^{\Theta_{\text{e}}}\gamma(\phi)d\phi.\end{split}

(9)

It is straightforward to show that the eigenvalues of the matrix in the quadratic term of (
9
) are

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lambda_{1}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{2}=\frac{k_{\delta}^{2}+1}{2k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\end{split}.

(10)

Therefore, the quadratic term in (
9
) is positive semidefinite and
V
is positive definite because the second term is positive definite as long as
\gamma(\cdot)
exists strictly in the first and third quadrants, which is true for any extended
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
function. This is a sufficient but not a necessary condition for positive definiteness. To continue, we expand
V
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}V&=\frac{k_{\delta}^{2}}{2k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\gamma^{2}(\Theta_{\text{e}})+\frac{k_{\delta}}{k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}+\frac{1}{2k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}\\
&\hskip 8.61108pt+\int_{0}^{\Theta_{\text{e}}}\gamma(\phi)d\phi,\end{split}

(11)

and compute its time derivative, which yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{V}&=\frac{k_{\delta}^{2}}{k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial\Theta_{\text{e}}}\dot{\Theta}_{\text{e}}\\
&\hskip 8.61108pt+\frac{k_{\delta}}{k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\left[\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial\Theta_{\text{e}}}\dot{\Theta}_{\text{e}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}+\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\boldsymbol{\dot{u}}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}+\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\dot{\omega}}_{\text{e}}\right]\\
&\hskip 8.61108pt+\frac{1}{k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\dot{\omega}}_{\text{e}}+\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\dot{\Theta}_{\text{e}},\end{split}

(12)

which can be simplified by recalling that
\dot{\Theta}_{\text{e}}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}
, noticing that
\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}=0
because
\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}\in\mathcal{S}^{2}
, and substituting (
7b
) into (
12
). Namely,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{V}&=\frac{k_{\delta}^{2}}{k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial\Theta_{\text{e}}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}\\
&\hskip 8.61108pt+\frac{k_{\delta}}{k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\left[\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial\Theta_{\text{e}}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}+\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\boldsymbol{\dot{u}}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}-k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\gamma^{2}(\Theta_{\text{e}})\right.\\
&\hskip 8.61108pt\left.-k_{\delta}\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial\Theta_{\text{e}}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}})-k_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}\right]\\
&\hskip 8.61108pt+\frac{1}{k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}^{T}\biggl[-k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}-k_{\delta}\biggl(\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\boldsymbol{\dot{u}}_{\text{e}}\biggr.\biggr.\\
&\hskip 8.61108pt\left.\left.+\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial\Theta_{\text{e}}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}})\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}\right)-k_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}\right]+\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}.\end{split}

(13)

Thus, after canceling out the terms with equal magnitudes and opposite signs, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{V}&=-k_{\delta}\gamma^{2}(\Theta_{\text{e}})-\frac{k_{\delta}k_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}{k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}-\frac{k_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}{k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}\\
&=-\begin{bmatrix}\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}^{T}&\boldsymbol{\omega}^{T}_{\text{e}}\\
\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{W}\begin{bmatrix}\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})\boldsymbol{u}_{\text{e}}\\
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}\\
\end{bmatrix},\end{split}

(14)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{W}=\begin{bmatrix}k_{\delta}&\dfrac{k_{\delta}k_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}{2k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\\[4.30554pt]
\dfrac{k_{\delta}k_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}{2k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}&\dfrac{k_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}{k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\end{bmatrix},\end{split}

(15)

which can be made positive definite by selecting

\displaystyle\begin{split}\{k_{\delta},k_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\}>0\quad\text{and}\quad k_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}>\frac{k_{\delta}k_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}{4},\end{split}

(16)

thus also enforcing that
\dot{V}(t)<0
. Last, by noticing that
V
, as specified by (
9
), is radially unbounded, we conclude that the unique fixed point of the CL dynamics given by
(
7a
)–(
7b
)
,
\{\Theta_{\text{e}}^{\star},\boldsymbol{\omega}_{\text{e}}^{\star}\}
, is globally asymptotically stable.
\square

A robustness analysis of the proposed control law is outside the scope of this letter; however, this task can be performed using the
Lyapunov-based
methods presented in
[
11
]
.

III-D

Discussion

There exist infinitely many valid choices of
\gamma(\cdot)
functions; some superior to the rest. For example, a sigmoid function is an extended class
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
function that provides tunable saturation while maintaining linearity near the equilibrium. Namely,

\displaystyle\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})=\Theta_{\text{max}}\frac{1-e^{-\xi\frac{\Theta_{\text{e}}}{\Theta_{\text{max}}}}}{1+e^{-\xi\frac{\Theta_{\text{e}}}{\Theta_{\text{max}}}}},

(17)

where
\Theta_{\text{max}}
and
\xi
are design parameters. In this context,
\Theta_{\text{max}}
is the angle at which the maximum magnitude of the actuation torque occurs and
\xi
determines the rate at which the magnitude of
\gamma(\Theta_{\text{e}})
changes with respect to
\Theta_{\text{e}}
. Taking the partial derivative of (
17
) with respect to
\Theta_{\text{e}}
yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial\Theta_{\text{e}}}=\frac{2\xi e^{-\frac{\Theta_{\text{e}}}{\Theta_{\text{max}}}}}{\left(1+e^{-\xi\frac{\Theta_{\text{e}}}{\Theta_{\text{max}}}}\right)^{2}},\end{split}

(18)

which provides insights and can be used to guide the process of controller synthesis. In particular, the slope at the origin has the form
\frac{\partial\gamma}{\partial\Theta_{\text{e}}}(0)=\frac{\xi}{2}
, in which
\xi
can be tuned taking into consideration, for example, actuator limitations and control objectives.

Although the differences between these values are not substantial, the experimental data provide compelling evidence of the suitability and superior performance of the proposed approach; particularly, under nonideal conditions due to the presence of actuator saturation, model uncertainty, and disturbances. We inferred that these nonideal conditions also explain the discrepancies between the simulated and experimental
\Lambda
values. Last, it is important to mention that the large variability in the experimental data is expected due to the random nature of
tumble-recovery
experiments.

V
Conclusion

We introduced a new class of
axis–angle
attitude control laws that provide substantial design flexibility with guaranteed
closed-loop
stability while ensuring a greater proportional control effort the farther away the system’s state is from the unique stable fixed AEQ of the CL dynamics. This characteristic is particularly useful when the proposed approach is used in combination with intelligent switching schemes. Evidence regarding functionality and high performance was provided in the form of data obtained through simulations and outdoor flight tests, during which we commanded a quadrotor to autonomously execute
tumble-recovery
maneuvers in the air. The new method consistently achieved reduced stabilization times and required less control effort compared to the
quaternion-based
and
geometric-control
methods used as benchmarks.
