Engaging communities: A scoping literature review of community-based participatory research in genetics service delivery settings
Authors: Heather A Zierhut, Pranathi Kandikonda, Emily M Simon, Cameron Donarski, Megan Kocher, Mariana Ramírez, Crystal Y Lumpkins, Elena R Fisher
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetic counseling (GC) services have the potential to improve health outcomes, yet there are documented disparities in the use of GC services among minoritized racial/ethnic populations. Community-based participatory research (CBPR) is an approach to integrate diverse patient perspectives to improve GC services. The objective of this review was to assess the current state of international CBPR within GC and genetics services from 2006 to 2023. Five databases were searched for inclusion criteria: 1) published in 2006 or later; 2) peer-reviewed publication; 3) primary research studies; 4) community-based AND participatory research; 5) focused on GC, clinical genetics, testing, and/or genetic service delivery. Criteria for evaluating CBPR involvement used a synthesis of best processes framework. Screening of 3,824 studies identified 16 papers (n = 12 qualitative; n = 1 quantitative; n = 3 mixed methods). The majority of studies were conducted within the U.S. (n = 12), focused on cancer (n = 5), involved the Asian/Pacific Islander community (n = 8), and centered around genetic education (n = 12). Community partners most frequently were involved in recruitment (n = 12) and results analysis (n = 12) and least involved in research question development (n = 7) and product creation (n = 6). Studies varied substantially in the integration of 9 CBPR gold standard principles with only three studies addressing all nine principles. Few CBPR projects in GC services exist and a lack of guidelines to assess the quality of CBPR methodologies hindered the appraisal of studies. CBPR represents a promising approach to improve GC services and should be explored by genetics service delivery researchers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12687-024-00740-9.Keywords: Community-based participatory research, Genetic counseling, Genetic service delivery, Community-engaged research, Racial/ethnic minority and underrepresented populations, Health equity

Introduction

Genetic counseling (GC) and genetics service delivery, defined as healthcare that provides education and support to individuals who manifest or are at risk for genetic or hereditary conditions, have long been utilized by families for a multitude of reasons (Ormond et al. 2018). Although GC can be useful to families, genetic information has also been used to directly harm and marginalize communities through historic genetic malpractices and ongoing genetic discrimination (Wilson 2003; Garrison 2013; Russo 2018; Iltis et al. 2023). These historical malpractices, along with other factors such as access to care, have contributed to disparities in the uptake of GC services and genetic testing amongst racially/ethnically underrepresented and underserved populations (Cragun et al. 2017; Fisher et al. 2019; Reid et al. 2020; Lin et al. 2021; Chavez-Yenter et al. 2022). GC is an inherently community-based healthcare service, as individuals affected by or at risk for genetic conditions cannot be separated from their biological ties and broader communities. Therefore, it is crucial to assess and implement the preferences of communities negatively impacted by previous genetic malpractices to current GC services.

Community-based participatory research (CBPR) is a partnership approach to research that equitably involves community members, organizational representatives, and academic researchers in all aspects of the research process. This well-documented research practice centers and uplifts the needs and voices of the communities most impacted by the research findings, particularly among underserved populations in medicine and research (Israel et al. 1998). CBPR has been shown across multiple clinical disciplines to increase the equitability and sustainability of research findings, particularly through consistent collaboration with community stakeholders throughout the entire research process (Wallerstein and Duran 2010; Israel et al. 2010; Wallerstein et al. 2020). Consequently, systemic changes can lead to reductions in health disparities by implementing community-informed research findings into medical practice (Hicks et al. 2012; Tapp et al. 2013; Millon Underwood et al. 2013; Shete et al. 2023).

Although community involvement in research is best practice, wide gaps in knowledge exist surrounding the use of CBPR in GC and genetics service delivery settings (Shete et al. 2023). An evaluation of the current landscape of CBPR in genetics service delivery research is needed to identify areas in which additional research should be conducted and build upon the contributions of prior community-involved work to inform study design and practices for future studies. By integrating the communities that have been marginalized from genetics service research into current research endeavors, community-research partnerships have the potential to improve awareness of, access to, and acceptance of culturally informed genetic services.

The objective of this review is to understand the current state of international CBPR GC and genetics service delivery research that involves patient stakeholders and community members from underrepresented and racially/ethnically minoritized communities. We used a scoping literature review approach to gather studies that could inform effective ways to engage diverse communities in CBPR, learn how to structure exemplary CBPR studies, and ultimately improve health disparities in GC and genetics services.

Methods

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search combining the concepts of GC and CBPR was developed by a science librarian (M.K.) in consultation with a subject matter expert (H.A.Z.) and tested on a set of seed articles. The protocol was registered with the Open Science Framework: 10.17605/OSF.IO/2TVDN. The CBPR component of the search was adapted from Harris Nwanyanwu et al. 2017. Searches were conducted in Medline (via Ovid), Embase Classic + Embase (via Ovid), CINAHL Plus (via EbscoHost), Scopus (via Elsevier), and APA PsycInfo (via Ovid) on February 6, 2023. An update was run on the Embase Classic + Embase search on February 22, 2023, to account for a vendor error that resulted in missing records on the original search date. Searches in all databases were date-limited from 2006 to present. The full search strategy for all databases is available in Supplemental Materials 1. Results from all databases were combined and de-duplicated in Covidence systematic review management software, resulting in a total of n = 3,824 articles to be screened for eligibility.

Eligibility criteria and article selection process

Inclusion and exclusion criteria were created by authors H.A.Z. (genetic counselor and researcher), P.K. (undergraduate researcher), and E.M.S. (undergraduate researcher) before title and abstract review. Articles included in the review met the following inclusion criteria: 1) published in 2006 or later; 2) peer-reviewed publication; 3) written in English; 4) primary research studies; 5) community-based AND participatory research (e.g. community members or advisors were involved in the research process); 6) focused on the research topics of GC, clinical genetics, genetic testing, genetic service delivery, or genetic implementation studies. Criteria for exclusion included: 1) published before 2006; 2) not peer-reviewed; 3) secondary or tertiary sources (e.g. reviews, book chapters, protocols, etc.); 4) not community-based AND participatory (e.g. studies conducted within a target community but with no involvement of community members/advisors, or articles that involved healthcare professionals rather than community members); 5) studies on any topics not included above (e.g. biobanks, genetic research, direct-to-consumer genetic testing).

Authors P.K. and E.M.S. were the primary reviewers for the abstract and title review stage. Authors H.A.Z. and E.R.F. served as expert, independent reviewers when a consensus could not be reached by the primary reviewers. A series of meetings over six months took place to review batches of articles and resolve disagreements. During the first meeting, all reviewers discussed the first batch of titles/abstracts and further clarified inclusion criteria such as the definition of CBPR using Kwon et al. (2018) and Sheridan et al. (2017) and, more specifically, the level of community participation necessary for inclusion in this review. Titles/abstracts in which the participatory status of the community was unclear were included for further full-text review rather than excluded in this stage. Independent review of all 3,824 articles by the two primary reviewers led to identification of n = 118 conflicts (3.1% conflict rate).

After the abstract and title review was completed by the primary reviewers (P.K. and E.M.S.), the remaining articles (n = 323) were further screened for eligibility by expert reviewers H.A.Z. and E.R.F due to clarification requested by the more novice reviewers (P.K. and E.M.S.) about whether articles were primary, peer-reviewed research studies or about the type of genetics services delivery (e.g. studies were not focused on a clinical genetics service). Subsequently, the remaining articles (n = 68) were pulled for full-text review. Two authors (C.D. and P.K.) independently assessed each full-text article for eligibility and discussed disagreements (n = 2) with expert reviewers E.R.F. and H.A.Z. Articles excluded in the full-text review stage (n = 52) were primarily not participatory (e.g. did not involve community members/advisors in the research process; n = 39 excluded studies) (Fig. 1). Attempted communication with one publication’s corresponding author was used to try to resolve one of the n = 2 full-text conflicts; however, the corresponding author did not respond and therefore the decision was made by H.A.Z. and E.R.F. to exclude this publication from this review.

Data items and charting process

Once the final group of articles was established (n = 16), the following general study data were abstracted into tables from each publication by authors C.D., H.A.Z., and E.R.F.: summary/goal of the study (typically obtained from the abstract), study design (e.g. qualitative, quantitative, mixed methods, and summary), clinical specialty (e.g. cancer, pharmacogenomics, etc.), country of origin, population of community members/advisors that participated in the research process, population of community members that acted as human subjects of the research (if different from the community research advisors), type of genetics service (e.g. genetic counseling, genetic education, etc.), outcome(s) of the study (e.g. attitudes, beliefs, frameworks, test results, etc.), and outcome(s) of the community-participatory aspect of the study (Table 1).

Synthesis of results

After the data was extracted, reviewed for accuracy, and summarized in tables, authors H.A.Z. and E.R.F. reviewed the data and putative themes with two experts who have experience conducting CBPR and who are members of underrepresented racial/ethnic backgrounds in the U.S. (C.Y.L. and M.R.). The authors discussed how to best present findings, such as how to annotate each article’s reported incorporation of CBPR gold standard principles (Table 3). For example, it was noted that many of the included publications may have incorporated all 9 CBPR gold standard principles, but may not have reported on any of these theoretical concepts within the final publication. Table 3 was correspondingly adjusted to reporting of principles as “No/Not Reported” to account for any discrepancies in reporting of the study versus how the authors and community partnerships may have conducted the study based upon this discussion.

Additionally, the authors concluded that clear reporting guidelines for CBPR would be helpful to better assess the quality of participatory research studies. However, a literature search did not return any widely used CBPR reporting guidelines, and communication with two external CBPR experts regarding the existence of any CBPR reporting guidelines also led to the conclusion that no such guidelines currently exist or are widely used to assess CBPR. Therefore, the authors were unable to utilize any quality reporting guidelines for this review and instead relied on the CBPR gold standard principles described in Israel et al. (1998 and 2010). Authors C.Y.L. and M.R. also reviewed all extracted data tables and the manuscript draft to ensure findings accurately and appropriately addressed the themes discussed with authors H.A.Z. and E.R.F.

Results

Overview

Of the 3,824 titles/abstracts that were screened for inclusion from 2006 to 2023, 16 articles met inclusion criteria (Barlow-Stewart et al. 2006; Boyer et al. 2007; Anie et al. 2016; Avey et al. 2016; Horowitz et al. 2017; Ali et al. 2019; Sanghavi et al. 2019; Blocker et al. 2020; Elsum et al. 2020; Wen et al. 2020; Alvord et al. 2020; Li et al. 2020; Lumpkins et al. 2020; Lemke et al. 2022; Lindberg et al. 2022; Rosas et al. 2020) and were evaluated for data extraction (Table 1), CBPR involvement throughout the study process (Table 2), and adherence/alignment with CBPR gold standard principles (Table 3).

Characteristics of included studies (Table 1)

Most of the studies were conducted within the U.S. (n = 12); the remaining four studies originated from Australia (n = 2), the U.K. (n = 1), and Ghana (n = 1). The most common study design was qualitative (n = 12), followed by mixed-methods (n = 3) and quantitative (n = 1) designs. The majority of studies focused on genetic education for the community and/or community advisors (n = 12). The next most common genetics service delivery areas studied were health screening and management (n = 7), genetic testing (n = 6), and genetic counseling (n = 2).

The range of clinical disease settings/health topics of interest was broad: the most common disease settings were cancer (n = 5), pharmacogenomics (n = 2), and family health history information (n = 2). Other health topics included prenatal/reproductive risks (n = 1), precision medicine/health (n = 1), sickle cell disease (n = 1), obesity and other complex diseases (n = 1), neurological disorders (Machado Joseph disease; n = 1), chronic kidney disease (n = 1), and adult-onset diseases (n = 1).

The communities involved in the research ranged significantly. The most common community engaged in the included studies was Asian/Pacific Islander communities (n = 8), further broken down into Vietnamese (n = 2), Chinese Americans (n = 2), Chinese Australians (n = 1), Hmong (n = 1), Pakistani or British Pakistani (n = 1), or Marshallese (n = 1). Black American/African American (n = 4) and African (n = 1) communities represented the second most common population, followed by the Hispanic/Latino (n = 4), American Indian/Alaskan Native (n = 3), and Native Australian/Aboriginal (n = 1) communities. Apart from racial/ethnic-identified communities, religious and spiritual leaders and communities (n = 2), individuals living in rural communities (n = 1), disease-specific communities (Machado Joseph disease; n = 1), and unspecified English-speaking patient communities (n = 1) were also recognized as community stakeholders in several studies.

Extent of CBPR involvement throughout the study process

Overall, studies were fairly evenly distributed across the range of CBPR study involvement as reported by the authors. Three of the sixteen studies that met inclusion criteria involved their community partners in all seven aspects of the study process, from formation of the research question to creation of a final product. Four studies involved their community partners in six of the seven aspects of the study process. Seven of the studies involved their community partners in four or fewer aspects of the study, and two studies involved their community partners in only one aspect of the study process.

Community partners in the included studies were reported to be most frequently involved in recruiting participants (n = 12), analyzing and interpreting the findings (n = 12), planning study methods and reviewing materials (n = 11), conducting the study/collecting data (n = 11), and sharing findings/planning dissemination of results (n = 9). Only seven studies reported involvement of their community partners in the formation of the research question and definition of relevant outcomes, and only six studies reported that their community partners helped create products or resources.

Alignment with and incorporation of CBPR gold standard principles

The included publications varied substantially in the extent to which each study reported alignment with the nine CBPR gold standard principles. The majority of studies (n = 11) reported incorporating between three and eight of the gold standard CBPR principles. Two publications reported alignment with all nine CBPR principles, and three publications reported alignment with only two of the nine CBPR principles. The two publications that incorporated all nine CBPR principles (Boyer et al. 2007; Li et al. 2020) also involved their community partners in all seven aspects of the study process (Table 2). There was no clear correlation between the studies that reported alignment with fewer CBPR principles and studies that minimally involved their community partners in the study process.

The most common CBPR gold standard principles that were reported to be incorporated within the studies include “CBPR #2: Builds on strengths within the community” (n = 14), “CBPR #4: Integrates action for mutual benefit” (n = 14), “CBPR #1: Recognizes the community as a unit of identity” (n = 13), and “CBPR #7: Addresses health from ecological perspectives” (n = 12). The least commonly reported CBPR gold standard principles included “CBPR #3: Collaborative partnership in all phases of the research” (n = 3) and “CBPR #5: Promotes a co-learning process” (n = 5).

Discussion

In this scoping review, we summarize the wide variation in study designs and techniques used to engage, collaborate, and disseminate community-based research on genetics service delivery among underserved and/or minoritized racial/ethnic populations. As CBPR occurs across a continuum from investigator-driven research with community consultation to community-driven and action-based research (Key et al. 2019), the same spectrum was noted in the sixteen included studies which differed in the ways that researchers collaborated with and integrated community members’ involvement into their research designs. Overall, we advocate for the incorporation of more CBPR principles into the study design and researcher-community relationship as well as accessible guidelines for publication of CBPR research methods to support better assessment of study quality.

The central goal of most of the included studies was genetics education to the community of interest. The fundamental concept of how genes relate to health may be a starting point to connect genetics service delivery research with communities (Millon Underwood et al. 2013; Hillyer et al. 2020; San Miguel-Majors et al. 2020). Awareness of the connection between genetic/heritable risks and one’s health is a critical first step to addressing the downstream implications of genetics health services (e.g. improving health outcomes) which was only researched in two studies (Barlow-Stewart et al. 2006; Blocker et al. 2020). Given the limited number of CBPR studies on genetics services and known disparities in access to genetic testing and genetic counseling for underrepresented racial/ethnic minority communities (Cragun et al. 2017; Chavez-Yenter et al. 2022), additional CBPR studies are needed that focus on not only education and awareness but actual utilization of genetics services. One approach to addressing the need for increased awareness of genetic services is to collaborate with communities to identify ways to engage community members before they are in need of genetic services and provide generalized genetics education. However, beyond education, it is imperative to ensure individuals have access to health screening, genetic testing, and genetic counseling when indicated. This approach connects the education about genetic services and genetic implications for health with the implementation of that knowledge in order to improve the health of the broader community, especially for underserved or marginalized populations who experience known disparities in benefiting from genetics services.

The sixteen studies centered on a varied landscape of genetics-related health topics and predominantly took place in the U.S. Many genetics service studies have historically focused on the three most common clinical specialties of cancer, prenatal/reproductive care, and general genetics (Stoll et al. 2018). However, the studies included in this review addressed many different health topics such as pharmacogenetics, obesity, neurology, and family health history information. It was affirming to witness studies considering a diversity of community interests, and leveraging partnerships with communities in a variety of ways to focus on health topics that were mutually beneficial to research teams.

Areas for improvement in study design and relationships were noted across the studies. Researchers should consider how to involve the community partners from the beginning and throughout the continuum of study implementation. First, researchers may consider the development of community relationships prior to study initiation. Few studies had community involvement in aspects of the initial development of the study, with only seven studies involving community members in the research question formation (Boyer et al. 2007; Horowitz et al. 2017; Sanghavi et al. 2019; Elsum et al. 2020; Li et al. 2020; Lemke et al. 2022; Rosas et al.). Second, CBPR gold standards advise that there is a co-learning, bidirectional, iterative process of research involvement which involves sharing experiences, giving suggestions, integrating ideas, and reporting on the outcomes of suggested changes (Israel et al. 1998, 2010). In many cases, this can lead to the creation of resources, products, and protocols that can be shared with community members and researchers to improve learning on both fronts. Co-learning is particularly important since genetics education was the top focus for many studies, and ensuring that the learning happens directly with the community advisors involved in the research is a crucial step to further promoting genetic education broadly within the community. Researchers must also learn from the community members to shape their studies and make changes in real time. The co-learning process can be time-consuming and may seemingly represent a barrier to the research process, but ultimately with time, the incorporation of learnings leads to better research outcomes for all. For example, in the included study by Anie et al. (2016), the community advisors were first educated on genetics-related topics relevant to the study’s aims, and the researchers were educated by the community advisors on cultural, religious, and psychosocial considerations related to the health topic at hand. This co-learning process led to the creation of culturally sensitive and community-informed guidance on the development of a national sickle cell counselor training program in Ghana. Without the tenet of co-learning, the impact and reach of research findings are expected to be less significant.

The third improvement identified from this review is that community members can and should be more frequently included beyond the active research phase. While it’s unclear if studies did not report long-term community relationships or additional CBPR outcomes beyond publications, this data may help assess not only the outcomes of the research study but also the productivity of the relationship. For example, Boyer et al. (2007) leveraged their community partnerships to tailor dissemination of research results by replacing traditional bar charts with diamond-shaped bars and variables that were renamed with lay terms familiar to the community. In Lemke et al. 2022, informational posters and digital resources about genetic risks for adult-onset diseases were developed by the community partners for display across a hospital campus to promote genetics education in the community. The intersection of community engagement throughout the research process and the application of CBPR principles allowed for culturally tailored dissemination of results that were relevant to the community. Focusing on unique ways to disseminate research findings beyond the publication of manuscripts is particularly crucial to ensure that the outcomes of CBPR can contribute to the long-term goals of the community and researchers. This extended phase of research integration is challenged by the need to find resources to maintain community relationships after the conclusion of a study and to secure funds that are not generally aligned with a research budget. Novel ways to support the continued investment in CBPR relationships are needed beyond active grant status. For example, community relationships and findings from an initial CBPR study (Lumpkins et al. 2023) contributed to the development of the Genetic Services and Health Equity Initiative at the University of Kansas Medical Center, which is a long-lasting service and resource for the community. For CBPR to be successful as a research approach and for trust to continue with community partners, systemic issues such as finding mechanisms to support collaborations that are aligned with community and research values will be necessary.

When assessing CBPR approaches across the continuum, our research team was challenged to identify the specific CBPR methodologies used and whether the researchers had integrated CBPR gold standard principles into their study design and community partnerships. Reporting was not consistent and therefore conclusions about study integration of CBPR principles were not definite, making it impossible to differentiate whether a study had not included a tenet of CBPR or simply did not report it. Attempts to identify an easily accessible and widely used guideline failed. For example, Smith et al. (2010; as listed in the EQUATOR network) attempted to establish best practices for reporting participatory action research yet an analysis by Kato et al. (2020) found that the guideline did not improve the reporting quality of CBPR studies since its publication. Other existing guidelines (Green et al. 1995) support funders, researchers, and evaluators in the appraisal of research proposals that claim to implement a participatory research approach. However, except for the Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public guideline (Staniszewska et al. 2017) which broadly addresses patient- and public-engaged research, there is a paucity of tools that aid researchers in the reporting of CBPR processes making comparisons across publications difficult and potentially too subjective. Developing guidelines that are widely accepted to describe the components of CBPR used in research projects and the way and degree to which they were applied would facilitate both documentation of the CBPR methodology and comparisons between the literature. We propose that an established, agreed-upon guideline for reporting CBPR that is easily accessible would be beneficial to researchers to help ensure better reporting of methodology, and additionally advocate for the integration of community stakeholder perspectives into the development of such a guideline.

These findings should be considered within the context of several important limitations. First, the terminology used to describe community-engaged research may vary significantly between countries. Research conducted in other countries utilizing the same or similar CBPR model may have been missed by this review, primarily due to geographical and historical differences in the terminology used to describe CBPR. For example, terms such as “citizen engagement” (Huttunen et al. 2022), “co-design research” (Cheng et al. 2021), and “community engagement” (Gwynne et al. 2023) used in European and Australian contexts, respectively, were likely not captured by our search strategy. While it was not possible to incorporate all alternative terminologies and concepts of CBPR in this review’s search strategy, the authors acknowledge that important CBPR genomics-related research conducted in international settings may not have been included in this review which could have impacted our overall findings. In addition, the included publications were limited to the English language; therefore, other CBPR studies on genetics services conducted with underserved communities might have been missed if published in another language. It is possible that other eligible English-language studies could also have been missed by our search strategy and review process, particularly due to the authors’ collective difficulty in assessing whether studies were both community-based and community-participatory; this may be in part due to the differences in CBPR terminology described above. Lastly, the authors attempted to provide the most accurate yet holistic assessment of the quality and depth of the researcher and community research partnerships described in each study by assessing publications for reported alignment with CBPR gold standard principles (Israel et al. 1998); however, it is possible that the authors’ assessment of each included study does not encapsulate the nature and quality of the CBPR relationship. We advocate for better CBPR reporting guidelines which could improve both the identification of eligible studies and a more comprehensive assessment of community-researcher relationships.

In summary, this review provides an assessment of the current landscape of CBPR approaches in genetic counseling and genetics service delivery research. Collectively, these sixteen studies illustrate an important foundation for the field to learn from and improve upon by working to infuse a greater number of CBPR principles in genetics service research and to advocate for more comprehensive reporting of CBPR methodologies in order to enhance the rigorous evaluation of future community-research collaborations.
