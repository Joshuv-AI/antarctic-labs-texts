Possible hemodynamic mechanisms in the size change of cerebral aneurysms: an exploratory study
Authors: Zhipeng Xiao, Hao Wan, Jiawei Li, Rong Zou, Yanyang Zeng, Jianbin Hu, Jens Fiehler, Adnan H Siddiqui, Jianping Xiang, Meihua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn asymptomatic small unruptured intracranial aneurysms (IAs), growth is one of the important factors determining treatment to prevent rupture and hemorrhage. Hemodynamic factors play a crucial role in the growth and regression of aneurysms, although the mechanisms involved are not yet clear. The aim of this study was to investigate the hemodynamic mechanisms underlying size changes in small unruptured IAs (<7 mm) during follow-up.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed 63 patients with unruptured IAs (<7 mm) who underwent computed tomography angiography (CTA) imaging follow-up for more than 6 months. Aneurysm hemodynamic and morphological parameters were measured by software AneuFlow. Statistical comparisons were performed between enlarging aneurysms and stable aneurysms.ResultsEnlarging aneurysms (43/63, 68%) exhibited a significantly higher baseline wall shear stress (WSS) (7.98 vs. 5.76 Pa, P=0.04) and a borderline higher WSS gradient (WSSG) (9.83 vs. 5.62 Pa/mm, P=0.05) compared to stable aneurysms. During enlargement, WSSG decreased (P=0.04), while parent vessel diameter increased (P<0.01), accompanied by reduced parent vessel WSS (P<0.01). In 30% of growing cases, parent vessel expansion was correlated with marked WSS and WSSG reduction in the aneurysm, suggesting adaptive remodeling. Low normalized WSS (NWSS) was correlated with decrease in diameter of the parent vessel (Dvessel) (r=0.30, P=0.02) and aneurysm surface area (r=0.36, P<0.01)ConclusionsThis study analyzed the effect of hemodynamics on the size change of aneurysms. High WSS and high WSSG may be inducing factors for aneurysm enlargement, while high WSS may lead to expansion of the parent vessel. In the early stages of aneurysm development, low WSS may contribute to size reduction.Keywords: Intracranial aneurysm (IA), hemodynamics, wall shear stress (WSS), computational fluid dynamics (CFD), spontaneous regression

Introduction

Intracranial aneurysms (IAs), as one of the most destructive pathological changes in the neurovascular system, have a mortality rate of 25% and a disability rate of 66% due to subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) caused by their rupture (1). Although modern imaging technology has significantly improved the detection rate of unruptured aneurysms (about 3–5%), clinical decision-making still faces fundamental challenges: how to accurately predict which aneurysms have a tendency to grow and rupture (2). In asymptomatic small unruptured IAs, growth is one of the important factors determining treatment to prevent rupture and hemorrhage.

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) has emerged as a valuable tool for investigating hemodynamic mechanisms of aneurysm growth. The validity of this approach has been supported by experimental studies; for instance, Raschi et al. demonstrated good agreement between CFD simulations and particle image velocimetry measurements in a growing aneurysm model (3), confirming the reliability of CFD for longitudinal hemodynamic analysis. Previous studies have confirmed that hemodynamics is not only the initiating factor in the formation of aneurysms, but also the core mechanism regulating their growth and remodeling (4). Wall shear stress (WSS), oscillatory shear index (OSI), WSS gradient (WSSG), and gradient oscillatory number (GON) may be indicative hemodynamic parameters for aneurysm growth (5-9). Meanwhile, the spontaneous degeneration of aneurysms is also related to hemodynamics (10). Although rare, a series of case reports suggest a correlation between spontaneous regression of aneurysms and reduced blood flow into aneurysms.

In recent years, with the development of imaging technology and the increasing awareness of disease prevention and treatment, more and more asymptomatic aneurysms have been discovered, providing data for the study of aneurysm growth.

In this study, we establish a cohort of aneurysm cases, analyze the hemodynamic characteristics of aneurysms, and provide a theoretical basis from a hemodynamic perspective for identifying unstable unruptured aneurysms. We present this article in accordance with the STROBEreporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0721/rc).

Methods

Participants

Our retrospective cohort study received approval from the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (No. 2024-692). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study and that most patients were difficult to contact. Before data collection, all patient information underwent anonymization. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

From January 2019 to February 2023, consecutive patients with unruptured IAs were admitted. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) patients diagnosed with unruptured IAs confirmed by computed tomography angiography (CTA); (II) small or medium aneurysm (<7 mm); (III) patients with follow-up CTA imaging for more than 6 months and who did not receive any specific pharmacological treatment aimed at preventing aneurysm growth (e.g., experimental anti-inflammatory or statin therapy) during the follow-up period. Standard medications for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes were not excluded; (IV) availability of complete clinical data and traceable medical history. Exclusion criteria included: (I) patients with SAH history; (II) individuals with dissecting, fusiform, infectious, traumatic, or multiple aneurysms; (III) anterior communicating artery (AcomA) aneurysm; (IV) poor image quality, unable to perform aneurysm or hemodynamic analysis.

Data collection

The CTA examination is performed according to the following steps. Using a Siemens SOMATOM Force dual-source CT, the patient lies in a supine position for a comprehensive scan from the aortic arch to the cranial vertex. Iomeprol (400) 60–80 mL is injected intravenously at the elbow at a flow rate of 4.0–4.5 mL/s. The scanning parameters include a tube voltage of 120 kV, a tube current of 60 mAs/slice, and a slice thickness of 1.25 mm. The images were converted to anonymous Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) files.

DICOM files of initial admission and follow-up images were both imported to AneuFlow, an aneurysm morphology and hemodynamic evaluation software (11,12). AneuFlow undertaken three main tasks in this study, including (I) segmentation, repair, and smoothing of the aneurysm models, (II) measurement of morphology parameters, and (III) CFD simulation and calculation of hemodynamic parameters. According to the grid independence verification in previous research, AneuFlow created ~1 million polyhedral elements with a maximum mesh size of 0.1 mm and three layers of wall prism elements (12). CFD analysis was performed under pulsatile-flow conditions within 3 cardiac cycles, with an assumed inlet flow rate from a published transcranial doppler (TCD) study (13). The time average values within the last cardiac cycle were used for quantification of hemodynamic features. Figure S1 shows the data processing flow of a representative case. The detailed calculation process is presented in Appendix 1 and Appendix 2.

Clinical, morphological, and hemodynamic features

Ten clinical features were recorded, including age, gender, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, alcohol, smoking, drug for hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and hyperglycemia, follow up time, and the location of lesion. Fifteen morphological features were recorded (14,15), including maximum height (Hmax), perpendicular height (H), middle diameter (Dmiddle), diameter of aneurysm neck (Dneck), diameter of the parent vessel (Dvessel), surface area (S), volume (V), inflow angle (θF), inclination angle (θA), vessel angle (θV), aspect ratio (AR), size ratio (SR), undulation index (UI), ellipticity index (EI), nonsphericity index (NSI). Eleven aneurysm hemodynamic features were recorded (5,6,16,17), including WSS, normalized WSS (NWSS), the minimum WSS on the aneurysm (WSSmin), the maximum WSS on the aneurysm (WSSmax), WSS of parent vessel (WSSvessel), the ratio of low shear area where WSS <10% WSSvessel (low shear area, LSA), the ratio of high shear area where WSS > WSSvessel (high shear area, HSA), OSI, and relative residence time (RRT), WSSG, and GON. The detailed definitions of morphology and hemodynamics parameters are presented in Figure S2.

Diagnosis of aneurysm size change (treatment and follow-up)

Patients diagnosed with IAs through CTA at initial admission should be followed up at our hospital and undergo CTA examination at least once. We measured the morphological parameters of the aneurysm based on the images obtained from two examinations. An aneurysm was enlarged if any of the following conditions are met: (I) aneurysm enlarged more than 1mm in at least one direction; (II) aneurysm enlarged more than 0.5 mm in any two directions. Aneurysms were divided into an enlarging group and a stable group according to whether they enlarged. The changes in the morphology of the aneurysm were jointly assessed by two experienced neurosurgeons.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as either median (interquartile range), while categorical data were expressed as number (%). Group differences were explored utilizing the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables and the Chi-squared test for categorical data. The parameter changes of all cases between initial admission and follow-up were analyzed using Wilcoxon signed rank test. Statistical analyses and graphical representations were conducted using SPSS statistical and computing software (Version 22.0.0; IBM).

Results

Characteristics of patients and aneurysms

A total of 130 patients were enrolled initially. Fourteen patients with SAH history, 16 patients with dissecting aneurysms, 14 patients with multiple aneurysms, and 12 patients with aneurysms at AcomA, 11 patients with poor quality images were excluded. Ultimately, 63 patients were reserved. Baseline analysis revealed that 43 (68%) aneurysms have enlarged during the follow-up. Figure S3 is a flow diagram of participants selection. The baseline patients’ characteristic and locations of lesion were included in Table S1. Of the 43 enlarging aneurysms, 29 cases (67.4%) were found at 6 to 12 months’ follow-up, 8 cases (18.6%) at 1 to 2 years’ follow-up, and 6 case (14.0%) at more than 2 years’ follow-up.

In the stable group, the surface area [23.10 (14.00, 32.94) vs. 22.27 (12.30, 30.58), P=0.04] and volume [15.82 (7.11, 21.89) vs. 11.27 (5.24, 19.43), P<0.01] of aneurysms decreased, but NSI [0.093 (0.047, 0.128) vs. 0.096 (0.080, 0.155), P<0.01] increased. The impact of parent vessel enlargement on hemodynamics

In this study, a 0.5-mm increase in the Dvessel was used as the standard to determine whether the parent vessel had increased during follow-up. Among the 43 cases in the enlarging group, 13 (30.23%) cases had enlarging parent vessels. Figure 3 shows the change of WSS and WSSG. In 13 cases with enlarging parent vessel, the WSS and WSSG decreased from 14.13 (8.72, 18.69) and 17.106 (10.59, 23.03) at initial admission to 5.39 (3.42, 14.87) and 7.16 (3.31, 15.59). However, in the other 30 cases with stable parent vessels, WSS and WSSG had no significant changes [6.27 (4.23, 9.10) and 7.80 (5.68, 11.75) at initial admission, 6.43 (4.12, 9.25) and 6.97 (5.48, 9.53) at follow-up].

Correlation between aneurysm size reduction and hemodynamics

We analyzed the correlation between the change of these morphological parameters reflecting the size of aneurysms and hemodynamic parameters at initial admission. The results showed that NWSS was significantly correlated with changes in Dmiddle [r=0.30, 95% confidence interval (CI) (0.06, 0.51), P=0.02] and S [r=0.36, 95% CI (0.19, 0.55), P<0.01] in Figure 6. There were 13 with a reduction in Dmiddle, and 16 in S. From Figure 6, it can be seen that aneurysms with decreasing Dmiddle or S have lower NWSS, concentrated in the range of NWSS <1. The correlation between other hemodynamic parameters and these morphological parameters is not significant (P>0.05).

Discussion

In this study, we found in the follow-up of the unruptured IA cohort that enlarging aneurysms demonstrated significantly higher WSS and a trend towards higher WSSG at baseline, and WSSG decreases as the aneurysm enlarges. In stable aneurysms, low NWSS aneurysms may be beneficial for aneurysm size reduction.

Aneurysm enlargement occurs in high WSS hemodynamic environments

WSS is the tangential frictional force generated by blood flow on the vascular wall, and its strength and direction are influenced by factors such as blood flow velocity, vascular geometry, and blood viscosity. The changes in WSS participate in vascular wall remodeling and the pathological process of aneurysms by affecting endothelial cell (EC) function. In previous studies, inconsistent conclusions have been drawn regarding the impact of WSS due to inconsistent selection rules for aneurysm cases, limited sample sizes, and limitations in vascular imaging techniques. In the reviews by Xiang et al. (4) and Diagbouga et al. (18), a unified theoretical conjecture was proposed for these seemingly contradictory studies. High WSS and WSSG are considered as the initial triggers for the formation of aneurysms. Experimental studies have shown that high WSS at the bifurcation of blood vessels can cause EC dysfunction, leading to damage to the inner elastic layer, loss of smooth muscle cells, and degradation of extracellular matrix, thereby forming early structural defects in aneurysms (19,20).

In our study, the enlarging aneurysms had significantly higher WSS and WSSG. As shown in the exemplary example in Figure 4, aneurysms often enlarge in high WSS areas. Low WSS inhibits the anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative functions of ECs, leading to degenerative changes in the vascular wall, such as inflammatory cell infiltration, smooth muscle cell apoptosis, and matrix metalloproteinase activation, further weakening the aneurysm wall structure. Our finding is consistent with the high-flow impingement pathway described by Karnam et al. (8), wherein sustained high WSS at the neck and inflow zone induces endothelial dysfunction and wall degeneration. While our cohort was dominated by internal carotid artery (ICA) aneurysms (78%) and excluded AcomA aneurysms, the mechanistic principle—that regional high WSS drives localized growth—aligns with their observations.

Our finding contrasts with a matched-pair CFD study by Weiss et al. (21), who reported that lower WSS and larger low shear area were associated with aneurysm growth. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, differences in aneurysm location distribution: Weiss’s cohort included a higher proportion of anterior communicating and middle cerebral artery (MCA) aneurysms, which may follow distinct hemodynamic pathways as described by Karnam et al. (8). Second, differences in growth definition and follow-up duration: our study used a broader size change criterion (≥1 mm in one direction or ≥0.5 mm in two directions) and a shorter median follow-up (11 months vs. longer intervals in their study), potentially capturing earlier stages of growth where high WSS predominates.

Parent artery expansion alters the hemodynamics in aneurysms

High WSS is one of the causes of vascular morphological changes. Under normal physiological conditions, high WSS can promote adaptive outward remodeling of blood vessels (22,23). For example, in arteries with increased blood flow, baseline WSS levels are restored through mechanisms mediated by ECs, such as the nitric oxide pathway (24). In previous studies, chronic WSS elevation induced vessel expansion usually occurs in collateral arteries because of a deficiency in blood supply through a feeding artery, or due to increased metabolic demand in a downstream tissue, thereby signaling a need to increase the caliber of the patent artery (25,26). Usually, in this process, there is an increase in lumen diameter with relatively little change in wall thickness, temporary and minute fragmentation of the internal elastic lamina and proliferation of ECs and smooth muscle cells (24,27).

In this study, we found that among cases of enlarging group, those with expanding parent vessels had higher WSSvessel, and as the parent vessels expanded, the WSS of aneurysms and WSSvessel decreased. At follow-up, WSSvessel decreased to a level comparable to cases without parent artery expansion. This result not only validates the theory of WSS induced vessel expansion, but also leads to new hypotheses. The presence of some aneurysms may be an intermediate process of vascular morphological changes, which occur at weak points in large areas of high WSS on the blood vessels. Subsequently, adjacent blood vessels expand, leading to a decrease in WSS and preventing further enlargement of the aneurysm. This may be a self-regulation mechanism of blood vessels. In this process, due to the complexity of vascular wall morphology, complex hemodynamic features appear, such as eddies and low WSS (28,29), induced inflammatory response, leading to hemodynamic damage (30).

Aneurysm size reduction under low WSS environment

The spontaneous regression of IAs is defined as the reduction or thrombosis confirmed by imaging without surgical/interventional treatment, which is an extremely rare phenomenon with an incidence rate of about 1–3% (10). Existing research has not found any predictable biomarkers or early signs. Anatomical factors, changes in coagulation function, and hemodynamic changes are possible influencing factors. Yeh et al. (31), Cantore et al. (32) and Tsimpas et al. (33) found that degeneration occurs after internal carotid artery-external carotid artery (EC-IC) bypass surgery, ICA blockage and flow diverter deployment at proximal vessel segment, which may be due to reduced perfusion within the aneurysm caused by blood flow redistribution.

In our study, we observed some aneurysms became smaller and found that the degree was related to NWSS. However, due to the lack of digital subtraction angiography (DSA) images of these patients, we are not able to determine whether they were due to aneurysm reduction or thrombosis. But these cases exhibited hemodynamic features similar to those reported in previous cases of low perfusion within aneurysms. Meanwhile, we have also noticed that opposite research conclusions have been proposed. A long-term follow-up study of a single case suggests that area with low WSS and close to the center of the vortical structure on aneurysms may undergo growth (7). This may be related to the different stages of development of the analyzed aneurysm. In the early stages of aneurysm development, vascular wall lesions may be more easily reversed. After the growth of an aneurysm, some patients experience changes in the blood flow status of the blood vessels, leading to reduced blood inflow and inducing degeneration. But in the later stage of aneurysm growth, the low WSS and turbulent blood flow environment can cause the aneurysm wall to degrade, leading to further growth of the aneurysm. In our data, there is also a portion of patients with lower WSS, but aneurysms enlarged.

Difference between growth/spontaneous regression and size change

In this study, we employed broader criteria to identify aneurysms with changing sizes, which differs from the traditional clinical definition of aneurysm growth and spontaneous regression. The cases we included were relatively small in size (<7 mm, with median maximum heights and widths of 2.93 and 3.74 mm), mostly spherical or finger shaped. During the follow-up process, the direction of their size changes was mostly not along the direction of the maximum diameter at the initial examination. In traditional clinical definitions, these cases do not belong to growth or spontaneous regression. In addition, for the 13 cases with reduced Dmiddle, CTA imaging alone cannot confirm whether it is degeneration or thrombus formation in the aneurysm cavity. Therefore, under the definition of this study, there were more aneurysms with size changes than those with growth/spontaneous regression in the conventional dataset.

Limitations

This study has limitations in terms of tools and simulation assumptions. Some aneurysms have necks that extend significantly along the circumference of the blood vessels, making it impossible to divide the vascular model into an aneurysm part and a parent artery part using a single plane. Therefore, when using Aneuflow to measure aneurysm parameters, there is a subjectivity in defining the aneurysm neck, which may result in parameter errors. Moreover, we assumed rigid vessel walls with no-slip boundary conditions. Several large-scale comparative studies have demonstrated that the impact of this assumption on WSS magnitude is bounded and predictable. A patient-specific fluid-structure interaction (FSI) study of 101 IAs reported that while OSI showed substantial variability, time-averaged WSS alterations remained within a limited range for most cases (34). Since our primary analysis compares relative differences between enlarging and stable aneurysms—both groups subjected to identical modeling assumptions—any systematic bias in WSS estimation applies equally to both arms, preserving the validity of between-group comparisons. Therefore, while absolute WSS values should be interpreted cautiously, the observed differences in baseline WSS between groups are unlikely to be artifacts of the rigid-wall assumption. In addition, the inlet boundary conditions were from a flow phantom study, not patient-specific flow measurements. This may affect the accuracy of hemodynamic results. Due to the difficulty in measuring complex hemodynamic parameters such as OSI and RRT through in vitro model experiments, as well as the differences between software solvers and grid partitioning tools and other simulation software, validation studies on the computational accuracy of AneuFlow have not yet been conducted. Although multiple research results using AneuFlow have been published, the validation of this software needs to be carried out in future studies.

There are also some limitations in sample collection in this study. The sample size (n=63) is modest. The study may be underpowered to detect small effect sizes, particularly in subgroup analyses (e.g., parent vessel enlargement, n=13). Our findings should be considered hypothesis-generating. Besides, we excluded AcomA aneurysms for technical reasons: in AneuFlow software, for aneurysms such as AcomA located at complex vascular intersections where the parent artery is difficult to define, automatic or semi-automatic segmentation of the neck plane poses significant difficulties and can easily introduce significant measurement errors. Moreover, there is blood supply from both sides of anterior cerebral artery (ACA), and the flow distribution of blood vessels is more complex, requiring more assumptions. AcomA and MCA aneurysms may follow distinct growth pathways (high-flow impingement vs. slow oscillatory flow) (8). Our cohort is dominated by ICA aneurysms (78%), and our findings primarily apply to this location. The hemodynamic mechanisms of growth and size reduction may differ for AcomA aneurysms and warrant separate investigation.

Thirdly, all patients had only one follow-up CTA. This forces a binary classification (enlarging vs. stable) and obscures potentially complex temporal trajectories, such as intermittent growth, growth followed by stabilization, or size oscillations. For the 13 aneurysms showing reduced Dmiddle, we cannot determine whether this represents sustained true morphological regression, temporary thrombosis without true involution, or measurement variability. Longitudinal studies with multiple, evenly spaced follow-up time points are essential to characterize these dynamics. In the future, we will observe the changes of aneurysms through multiple follow-up visits to further clarify the growth mechanism of aneurysms.

Conclusions

This study analyzed the effect of hemodynamics on the growth of aneurysms and proposed that high WSS and high WSSG may be inducing factors for aneurysm growth, while high WSS may lead to expansion of the parent vessel. In the early stages of aneurysm development, low WSS may contribute to spontaneous regression. Understanding the mechanism by which hemodynamics affects the growth of aneurysms is beneficial for our treatment of aneurysms.
