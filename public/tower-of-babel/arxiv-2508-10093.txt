De Sitter Complexity Grows Linearly in the Static Patch Preprint: APS/123-QED
Vyshnav Mohan ♠ and Watse Sybesma ♡

Abstract
The observable universe has undergone periods of expansion that are well approximated by de Sitter (dS) space. Still lacking is a quantum mechanical description of dS, both globally and when restricted to the static patch. We develop a novel prescription for computing holographic complexity in the dS static patch to determine its microscopic features. Specifically, we propose that the natural candidate for dS complexity is the volume of extremal timelike surfaces restricted to the static patch, anchored to the cosmological horizon or an observer worldline. Our anchoring prescription provides a clear definition of a reference state, overcoming a common ambiguity in prior definitions of de Sitter holographic complexity. The late-time growth of our complexity functional is linear and proportional to the number of degrees of freedom associated to the cosmological horizon, and therefore does not exhibit hyperfast growth. Our results imply the dS static patch is characterized by a quantum mechanical system, with a finite dimensional Hilbert space whose evolution is governed by a chaotic Hamiltonian.



I
Introduction

Profound insights into the quantum nature of Anti-de Sitter (AdS) spacetime are provided through a powerful notion called holography, which in essence allows for mapping an AdS (quantum) gravitational theory to a dual quantum mechanical system
Maldacena 1998
;
Gubser et al. 1998
;
Witten 1998
.
Meanwhile, it is expected that the expansion of our current and early inflationary universe is well-described by de Sitter (dS) spacetime, see e.g.
Baumann 2022
. Realizing holography for dS could provide a window for studying quantum aspects of the universe.

AdS holography has given rise to a fruitful interplay between gravitational physics and quantum information theory, introducing geometric probes in spacetime that have a dual quantum information interpretation.
In particular, proposals of holographic complexity, initially inspired by notions of computational complexity in quantum circuits, have been employed to discern quantum features of spacetime, see e.g.
Baiguera et al. 2025
for a recent review.
The standard computation of black hole holographic complexity using the Complexity = Volume (CV) prescription involves spacelike extremal volume surfaces anchored on timelike surfaces
Susskind 2016
;
Stanford and Susskind 2014
.
From such studies, a picture emerges where the late-time linear growth of holographic complexity is in conjunction with a dual chaotic dynamical evolution (see, for example,
Barbón et al. 2019
).
A tool like complexity could, on the one
hand, reveal quantum aspects of dS, and on the other hand, help working towards understanding dS holography.

The main result we present in this letter is a new CV proposal applicable to dS spacetime that instead involves anchoring
timelike
extremal volumes. Our refinement exhibits late-time linear growth and is confined within the horizons of a single static patch. Our results suggest that the static patch of de Sitter space could be described by a finite-dimensional quantum system with a chaotic Hamiltonian. This is consistent with earlier conjectures surrounding the finiteness of the de Sitter Hilbert space
Fischler and Susskind 1998
;
Parikh and Verlinde 2005
;
Banks 2005
.

Our prescription makes use of three key ingredients, which we illustrate in Figure

. (i) Explicit inclusion of an observer through anchoring, inspired by recent advances
Chandrasekaran et al. 2023
;
Maldacena 2024
, although we alternatively also consider horizon anchoring. In both cases, it holds that a state at a given moment
\tau=t_{0}
can be interpreted as a reference state. (ii) Extremizing the volume
\mathcal{V}
of a
timelike
surface, we
point out that as time evolves, it will converge to an accumulation surface, allowing the late-time behavior to approach a linear growth.
(iii) To define complexity

C:=\frac{\mathcal{V}}{G_{N}\ell_{r}}\,,

(1)

we compare the volume
\mathcal{V}
to a relevant complex-valued reference scale
\ell_{r}
(and Newton’s constant
G_{N}
) that is determined by generalizing a proposal by
Couch et al. 2018
via a min-max procedure.
Owing to the timelike nature of the surface, the volume picks up a complex phase factor of
i
that seems troublesome for interpreting a real complexity. However, our reference scale ensures the complexity to be real valued, by naturally counteracting the complex phase factor
i

Importantly, our results evade the
hyperfast

Susskind 2021
.
In the literature, this behavior has also been ameliorated by introducing a cutoff surface near future infinity, resulting in a linear growth that depends on the specific details of the cutoff surface, which however make the result more difficult to interpret
Jørstad et al. 2022
.
In the work by
Aguilar-Gutierrez et al. 2024
, it was revealed that a family of surfaces exists that bypasses this hyperfast growth, allowing for
various
possible late-time growth behaviors, but not exclusively linear.

) through ‘observer anchoring’.

Complexity Through Observer Anchoring

II.1
Setting up the timelike surface

Now let us extremize the volume functional of these surfaces and compute their growth rate. The general procedure follows the spacelike case of
Carmi et al. 2017

v=t+r^{*}\,,\quad r^{*}(r)=\int_{0}^{r}\frac{\mathrm{d}r^{\prime}}{f(r^{\prime})}\,.

(3)

The volume of the timelike surface can then be written as the following integral

\mathcal{V}=\Omega_{d-1}\int\mathrm{d}\lambda\ r^{d-1}\sqrt{-f(r)\dot{v}^{2}+2\dot{v}\dot{r}}\,,

where we have parametrized the surface as

\mathcal{V}

Since the volume functional does not depend explicitly on

r^{2d-2}\left(-f\dot{v}^{2}+2\dot{v}\dot{r}\right)=-1\,.

(5)

The sign on the right-hand side is negative because we are considering timelike surfaces. The conservation equation and the choice of the affine parameter can then be used to arrive at the following equations of motion

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=r^{2(d-1)}(f(r)\dot{v}-\dot{r})\,,

(6)

\displaystyle r^{2(d-1)}\dot{r}^{2}

\displaystyle=-f(r)+r^{-2(d-1)}p^{2}\,.

(7)

We will analyze these equations of motion in the remainder of this section.

II.2
Extremizing volume
\mathcal{V}

The surface has a turning point, whose location we denote by
r=r_{\min}
. The location of the turning point can be obtained by setting
\dot{r}
to zero:

r_{\min}^{2(d-1)}f(r_{\min})=p^{2}

(8)

We choose
\lambda
to increase in the upward direction in the Penrose diagram in Figure
2
. As a result,
\dot{v}>0
when
r=r_{\min}
. Evaluating (
6
) at the turning point, we find that
p
is positive.

Using the coordinate transformation,

\mathrm{d}\tilde{r}=r^{2d-2}\mathrm{d}r\,,

(9)

we can rewrite (
7
) as a particle scattering off an effective potential

\dot{\tilde{r}}^{2}+V_{\text{eff}}=p^{2}\,,\qquad\text{where}\ V_{\text{eff}}(r)=r^{2d-2}f(r)\,.

The effective potential in the scattering picture

. The location of the accumulation surface is given by

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\left(r^{2d-2}f(r)\right)\right|_{r=r_{a}}=0\,\implies r_{a}=L\sqrt{\frac{d-1}{d}}\,.

(11)

Here, we have ignored the trivial

The accumulation surface plays an important role in our computation, as the extremal surface approaches this constant-

Stanford and Susskind 2014

. Setting
\dot{r}=0

The equations of motion (
6
) and (

. The volume of this surface is identically zero, and the trivial solution of (

) corresponds to the infinite

limit of this class of solutions. We will not consider these solutions or their associated accumulation surface due to their triviality

The energy of the particle,
p^{2}
, is a monotonic function of

(see (

) for the explicit relation; numerical evaluation of the integral can be used to confirm the monotonic behavior). Using the fact that the extremal surface asymptotes to the accumulation surface, we can use (

Using (
5
), we can rewrite the volume integral as follows

\mathcal{V}=2i\Omega_{d-1}\int_{\lambda(r=r_{\min})}^{\lambda(r=0)}\mathrm{d}\lambda=2i\Omega_{d-1}\int_{r_{\min}}^{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\dot{r}}\,.

(12)

We have used the symmetry about the
t=0
surface to simplify the expression. We have also chosen the positive branch for the square root of
-1
for
i
.
The negative branch could also be used, provided the same choice is made when computing the reference scale
\ell_{r}
later in the section.
The order of the integration limits follows from our choice of
\lambda
increasing in the upward direction. Since
\dot{r}
is negative in the region of integration, we can plug in the equation of motion (
7
) to get

\mathcal{V}=2i\Omega_{d-1}\int^{r_{\min}}_{0}\mathrm{d}r\frac{r^{2(d-1)}}{\sqrt{-f(r)r^{2(d-1)}+p^{2}}}\,.

(13)

The equation of motion for
\dot{v}
can be re-expressed as the following integral

\int\mathrm{d}v=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{f(r)}\left[\frac{p}{r^{2(d-1)}\dot{r}}+1\right]\,.

(14)

Choosing appropriate ranges of integration and the associated sign of
\dot{r}
, we get

r^{*}_{\min}+\frac{\tau}{2}=\int^{r_{\min}}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{f(r)}\left[\frac{p}{\sqrt{-f(r)r^{2(d-1)}+p^{2}}}+1\right]\,.

(15)

The above expression implicitly relates the anchoring time
\tau
to
p
since
r_{\min}
depends only on
p
.

To compute the growth rate, we employ a simple trick of rewriting the volume integral in the following form
Carmi et al. 2017

\displaystyle-\frac{\mathcal{V}}{2i\Omega_{d-1}}=\int^{r_{\min}}_{0}\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{f(r)}

\displaystyle\left[\sqrt{-f(r)r^{2(d-1)}+p^{2}}+p\right]

(16)

\displaystyle-p\left(r^{*}_{\min}+\frac{\tau}{2}\right)\,.

Taking a derivative with respect to
\tau
of the equation above, we obtain the simple expression

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{V}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=i\Omega_{d-1}p=i\Omega_{d-1}r_{\min}^{d-1}\sqrt{f(r_{\min})}\,.

(17)

At late times,
r_{\min}
approaches
r_{a}
, and as a result, the growth rate of the timelike extremal surfaces can be approximated by a constant.

II.3
Determining reference scale
\ell_{r}

Now that we have computed the volume
\mathcal{V}
, we need to determine the reference scale to compare it to, in order to evaluate complexity through (
1
).
In the AdS black hole, for example, one typically chooses this scale to be the AdS length scale while in a Schwarzschild black hole, one chooses it to be the horizon radius. In
Couch et al. 2018
, it was suggested that this length scale can be universally defined as the maximum time to fall from the horizon to the accumulation surface. Here, we generalize the prescription to a min-max procedure. We begin by fixing the non-compact directions
r
and
t
, and minimizing the proper time over all infalling curves. We then vary
r
and
t
to extremize the resulting proper time.

We can easily verify that this prescription reproduces all the length scales computed in
Couch et al. 2018
. Now let us use this prescription to compute the reference scale of dS. Since the accumulation surface is timelike, the ‘infalling’ trajectories are spacelike. As a result, the ‘time’ taken to ‘fall’ from the horizon becomes imaginary. For a fixed
r
and
t
profile, the minimum proper length is given by a curve located at fixed angular coordinates. Now varying the
r
and
t
coordinates, we find that the maximal (imaginary) proper time is given by the
t=0
surface. Note that this is similar to what we find in the black hole case, but the crucial difference is that the
t=0
is timelike in the interior of a black hole.

The reference scale
\ell_{r}=\int\sqrt{-g_{\mu\nu}\dot{x}^{\mu}\dot{x}^{\nu}}
is then given by

\displaystyle\ell_{r}=i\int_{r_{a}}^{L}\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\sqrt{f(r)}}=iL\cos^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{d-1}{d}}\,.

(18)

II.4
Putting it all together

We can now determine the growth rate of complexity by using its definition (
1
) and plugging in (
17
) and (
18
):

\frac{\mathrm{d}C}{\mathrm{d}\tau}=\frac{8\pi\left(\frac{d-1}{d}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{\sqrt{d}\cos^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{d-1}{d}}}ST\propto ST\,,

(19)

where
S
and
T
are the entropy and temperature of dS

S=\frac{\Omega_{d-1}L^{d-1}}{4G_{N}}\,,\quad\ \ T=\frac{1}{2\pi L}\,.

(20)

Therefore, timelike complexity grows linearly at late times, with a growth rate proportional to the number of degrees of freedom, as expected for a finite-dimensional chaotic quantum system!

t_{P}=0
and choose

and

. Since the equations of motion are the same, we can proceed as in the previous case to find the same growth rate as in (

III
Complexity Through Horizon Anchoring

Instead of anchoring the extremal surface onto the observer worldline, we can anchor it at the horizon, or in fact anywhere beyond
r_{a}
. To do this, we first define the other Eddington-Finkelstein coordinate
u=t-r^{*}
. The anchor points can now be specified in terms of the Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates
u
and
v
(see Figure
2
). We will choose to anchor the surface at symmetric points by setting
v_{F}=-u_{P}=v/2
.

The equations of motion are once again given by (
6
) and (
7
). The only difference in this setup is that the particle in the scattering picture starts at the horizon and scatters off the potential from the right side, as shown in Figure
3
. The volume integral now takes the form

\mathcal{V}=2i\Omega_{d-1}\int_{r_{\min}}^{L}\mathrm{d}r\frac{r^{2(d-1)}}{\sqrt{-f(r)r^{2(d-1)}+p^{2}}}\,.

(21)

Using the equation of motion for
\dot{v}
, we find that:

\displaystyle\frac{v}{2}-r^{*}_{\min}

\displaystyle=\int_{v(r=r_{\min})}^{v(r=L)}\mathrm{d}v

(22)

\displaystyle=\int_{r_{\min}}^{L}\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{f(r)}\left[\frac{p}{\sqrt{-f(r)r^{2(d-1)}+p^{2}}}+1\right].

This gives us

\displaystyle-\frac{\mathcal{V}}{2i\Omega_{d-1}}=\int^{r_{h}}_{r_{\min}}\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{f(r)}

\displaystyle\left[\sqrt{-f(r)r^{2(d-1)}+p^{2}}+p\right]

(23)

\displaystyle-p\left(\frac{v}{2}-r^{*}_{\min}\right)\,.

Taking a
v
-derivative, we get the integral

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{V}}{\mathrm{d}v}=ip\Omega_{d-1}=i\Omega_{d-1}r_{\min}^{d-1}\sqrt{f(r_{\min})}\,.

(24)

At late times,
r_{\min}
approaches
r_{a}
. This gives us the late-time growth rate

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{V}}{\mathrm{d}v}\simeq i\Omega_{d-1}r^{d-1}_{a}\sqrt{f(r_{a})}\,.

(25)

Using the infalling time we have calculated in the previous section, we find the complexity growth to be given by

\frac{\mathrm{d}C}{\mathrm{d}v}\simeq\frac{i\Omega_{d-1}}{G_{N}\ell_{r}}r^{d-1}_{a}\sqrt{f(r_{a})}\propto ST\,.

(26)

The growth rate matches the final expression we obtained in the previous section, equation (
19
). This suggests that the dS stretched horizon complexity is, in fact, the same as the complexity seen by the observer.

IV
Discussion

In this letter, we have proposed a new definition of holographic complexity in de Sitter spacetime, based on computing the volumes of extremal timelike surfaces within a single static patch.
We find late-time linear behavior for the complexity growth, without any signs of hyperfast growth. We find the same results using both an observer as well as a horizon perspective, both perspectives give us a clear interpretation of the reference state, namely the state at ‘
\tau=t_{0}
’. Our results suggest that the static patch in de Sitter has a dual description in terms of a finite-dimensional quantum chaotic system.

The above picture is further supported by the following observation. If we naively apply the results of
Gautason et al. 2025
to our setup, then the complexity should saturate at a timescale given by the exponential of the area of the accumulation surface. This corresponds to
e^{\mathcal{O}(S)}
, exactly as expected for a chaotic system.

In the future, to find more evidence to support the interpretation of de Sitter as a chaotic system, one could perform shockwave experiments or study the switchback effect to uncover out-of-equilibrium effects. In particular, sending in a shockwave and computing the delay in the onset of linear growth can be used to extract the scrambling time, as discussed in
Anegawa and Iizuka 2023
;
Baiguera et al. 2023
;
Baiguera and Berman 2024
using the standard CV computations in dS.

One may also revisit other holographic complexity proposals, such as Complexity = Action
Brown et al. 2016
and Complexity = Anything
Belin et al. 2022
;
Belin et al. 2023
, as studied in
Jørstad et al. 2022
;
Aguilar-Gutierrez et al. 2024
, by anchoring the Wheeler-DeWitt patch and codimension-one volume surfaces to timelike separated points on the observer’s worldline. We expect these computations to confirm further and strengthen the linear growth found in this work.

Finally, despite carefully defining our results to be only valid beyond two dimensions (
d=1
), we find that the final result allows continuation to two dimensions (
19
), predicting late-time linear growth. Meanwhile, we point out that often two-dimensional models have a higher dimensional pedigree. For example, two-dimensional de Sitter can be obtained through reducing three-dimensional de Sitter or near-extremal limits of four-dimensional de Sitter black holes, see
Sybesma 2021
;
Aalsma and Sybesma 2021
for discussions. One can always ‘dress’ the two-dimensional volume functional with an appropriate power of the dilaton to match the complexity of its higher dimensional parent, see e.g.
Schneiderbauer et al. 2020
;
Anegawa et al. 2023
.

The reason we emphasize the two-dimensional realisation of our results is that it provides an exciting bench mark for matching conjectured dual systems (see for example
Susskind 2021
;
Susskind 2025
;
Susskind 2022
;
Narovlansky and Verlinde 2025
;
Rahman 2022
;
Rahman and Susskind 2023
;
Verlinde 2025
;
Rahman 2022
). These quantum systems can be completely solved using so-called
chord diagrams
, whose intersection numbers map to the lengths of geodesics in the bulk geometry
Blommaert et al. 2025
. Since these are precisely the elements required for a quantum-mechanical definition of holographic complexity, the Krylov spread complexity in these dual descriptions is expected to match our predictions, analogous to the cases studied in
Rabinovici et al. 2023
;
Heller et al. 2024
.
