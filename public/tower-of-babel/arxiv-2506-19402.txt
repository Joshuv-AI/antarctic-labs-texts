Hypercubical manifolds in homotopy type theory
Samuel Mimram, Émile Oleon

Abstract
Homotopy type theory is a logical setting in which one can perform geometric constructions and proofs in a synthetic way. Namely, types can be interpreted as spaces up to homotopy, and proofs as homotopy invariant constructions. In this context, we introduce a type which corresponds to the hypercubical manifold , a space first introduced by Poincaré in 1895. Its importance stems from the fact that it provides an approximation of the group Q Q of quaternionic units, in the sense of being the first step of a cellular resolution of Q Q . In order to ensure the validity of our definition, we show that it satisfies the expected property: it is the homotopy quotient of the sphere S 3 \operatorname{S}^{3} by the expected action of Q Q . This is non-trivial and requires performing subtle combinatorial computations based on the flattening lemma, thus illustrating the effective nature of homotopy type theory. Finally, based on the previous construction, we introduce new higher-dimensional generalizations of this manifold, which correspond to better cellular approximations of Q Q , converging toward a delooping of Q Q .



Introduction

Homology
[
11
]
is a family of abelian groups defined on spaces, such as topological spaces or simplicial sets, which are invariant under homotopy equivalence, i.e. under deformation of such spaces. After their introduction, it was wondered how precise these invariants where and, in particular, whether they would characterize spheres. It turns out that the answer is negative: there are spaces, called
homology spheres
, which have the same homology groups as spheres, but are not homotopy equivalent to them.
Defining such examples has lead Poincaré to study a family of spaces obtained by gluing faces of platonic solids
[
15
]
.

In particular, the
hypercubical manifold

K
consists of

1.

a 3-dimensional cube where each pair of opposite squares have been identified after a quarter-turn rotation.

Nice modern presentations of this space can be found in
[
7
,
17
]
. Its fundamental group can be computed to be the quaternion group

and its universal cover to be the 3-sphere
\operatorname{S}^{3}
. It is well known that we can recover any (nice enough) space

as the quotient of its universal cover under the canonical action of its fundamental group
[
8

: this means that we have an alternative definition of

as

2.

a quotient of the 3-sphere
\operatorname{S}^{3}
under a free action of the quaternion group
Q
.

Defining the hypercubical manifold in homotopy type theory

Over recent years, a variant of dependent type theory, homotopy type theory
[
18
]
, has been introduced. In this setting, types can be interpreted as homotopy types, i.e. spaces up to deformation, thus allowing for performing constructions on spaces in a synthetic way. Advanced versions of this type theory (such as the one implemented in cubical Agda
[
19
]
) feature higher inductive types (HITs) which allow higher constructors in the definition of types, corresponding to attaching cells like in CW-complexes. Our goal in this paper is to define the hypercubical manifold in homotopy type theory and show that it satisfies the expected properties: in particular, we will show the equivalence of the two above definitions within the theory.

The hypercubical manifold can easily be constructed as a HIT
[
9
]
by generalizing the well-known constructions for simple spaces such as the torus
[
19
]
, which essentially consists in translating the standard description
item

1
of the space as a CW-complex.
However, constructing

as a quotient of the 3-sphere by an action of
Q
, following the second definition
item

2
, seems difficult to achieve, because there is no direct way of defining such an action. Namely, we would need to have a
coherent
description of it, that is as a map
\phi:\operatorname{B}Q\to\mathcal{U}
where
\operatorname{B}Q
is a type representing internally the group
Q
(this is called a

of
Q
) and
\mathcal{U}
is the universe of all types, whose elements can be thought here as spaces. Classical definitions of

(as a type of

[
10
]
) have an universal property that only allows eliminating to groupoids. However, there is no groupoid subtype of the universe containing

, because
\operatorname{S}^{3}
is not

Here, we explain how we can still manage to define the coherent action of

on
\operatorname{S}^{3}
in homotopy type theory, from a map

whose fiber is

Higher hypercubical manifolds

This work also suggests useful generalizations of the hypercubical manifold. Namely, there are also free actions of
Q
on spheres
\operatorname{S}^{4n-1}
for any natural number

, giving rise to higher hypercubical manifolds

. We will show that those can also be defined in homotopy type theory, and that they allow constructing a “resolution” of

which behave more and more as equivalences as

(4n-2)
-connected.
Compared to resolutions obtained by other traditional methods (such as those based on rewriting), this resolution is quite “small” and thus more amenable to computations.

We will now show that we can perform the previous constructions in the setting of homotopy type theory, which requires more than a simple direct translation. It is clear that we can use higher inductive types (HITs) to give a direct definition of

as a type consisting of two elements, four identities, three 2-identities and one 3-identity

such that

,

3.7
The fundamental fiber sequence

The previous computation can be summarized by the following fiber sequence.

Theorem 10
.

We have a
fundamental fiber sequence

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{S}^{3}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{B}Q}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}

and thus a coherent action of
Q
on
\operatorname{S}^{3}
whose homotopy quotient is
K
.

This thus justifies considering that our first definition of
K
as a HIT is the hypercubical manifold (up to homotopy).

As a consequence we have the following:

Proposition 11
.

The fundamental group of the hypercubical manifold is
\pi_{1}(K)=Q
.

Proof.

By
[
18
, Theorem 8.4.6]
, the fiber sequence
theorem

10
induces a long exact sequence of homotopy groups

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}(\operatorname{S}^{3})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}(K)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}(\operatorname{B}Q)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{0}(\operatorname{S}^{3})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}\phi}

which simplifies as

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}(K)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Q}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}\phi}

thus showing the desired isomorphism of groups.
∎

As another consequence, we have

Lemma 12
.

The map
\phi
is 2-connected.

Proof.

By
theorem

10
, we have
\|\operatorname{fib}_{\phi}\|_{2}=\|\operatorname{S}^{3}\|_{2}=1
since
\operatorname{S}^{3}
is 2-connected
[
18
, Corollary 8.2.2]
.
∎

3.8
Recovering the Galois fibration

Taking groupoid truncations induces a commuting square

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{B}Q}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\|K\|_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\|\operatorname{B}Q\|_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces|-|_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces|-|_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\|\phi\|_{1}}

where the vertical map on the right is an equivalence because, by definition,
\operatorname{B}Q
is a groupoid. Moreover, since
\phi
is 1-connected (by
lemma

12
), the horizontal map at the bottom is an isomorphism
[
18
, Lemma 7.5.14]
. Thus, up to an automorphism of the codomain, the
\phi:K\to\operatorname{B}Q
coincides with the map
|-|_{1}:K\to\|K\|_{1}
, which is known as the
Galois fibration

[
14
]
:

Lemma 13
.

We have a commuting triangle

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\|K\|_{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{B}Q}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces|-|_{0}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sim}

where the bottom map is an equivalence.

Let us briefly explain the importance of this fibration. Given a pointed connected type
A
, we have that
\|A\|_{1}
is a connected groupoid whose fundamental group is
\pi_{1}(A)
by
[
18
, Theorem 7.3.12]
. The fiber of the truncation map
\|-\|_{1}:A\to\|A\|_{1}
is
\Sigma(x:A).(|\star{}|_{1}=|x|_{1})
which, by
[
18
, Theorem 7.3.12]
again, coincides with the universal cover of
A
which is

\tilde{A}\quad\equiv\quad\Sigma(x:A).\|\star{}=x\|_{0}

along with the first projection
\pi:\tilde{A}\to A
as universal covering map. In other words, we have a fiber sequence

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tilde{A}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{B}\pi_{1}(A)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces|-|_{1}}

which, under the action-fibration duality (
proposition

4
), corresponds to the canonical action of
\pi_{1}(A)
on the universal cover.
The fiber sequence of
theorem

10
is essentially of this form, with
A\equiv K
so we have shown:

Theorem 14
.

The universal cover of the hypercubical manifold is
\operatorname{S}^{3}
.

Note that we have defined the map
\phi:K\to\operatorname{B}Q
in
definition

8
by making an educated guess, but we could have actually computed it from the map
|-|_{0}:K\to\|K\|_{0}
.

4
Higher dimensional hypercubical manifolds

We now construct higher dimensional variants of the hypercubical manifold: for every natural number
n
, we define a type
K^{n}
equipped with a canonical map
\phi^{n}:K^{n}\to\operatorname{B}Q
. In particular, for
n=1
, we recover the previous constructions:
K^{1}\equiv K
and
\phi^{1}\equiv\phi
. Those will be such that there is a fiber sequence

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{S}^{4n-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{B}Q}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi^{n}}

exhibiting the fact that
K^{n}
is a quotient of
\operatorname{S}^{4n-1}
by an action of
Q
, and therefore the map
\phi^{n}
will be
(4n{-}2)
-connected. In this sense, the types
K^{n}
provide better and better approximations of
\operatorname{B}Q
as
n
increases.

In order to define those in type theory, we should draw inspiration from the topological definitions recalled in
section

2.3
. For instance,
K^{2}
is obtained as a quotient of
\operatorname{S}^{7}
by
Q
. Recalling that
\operatorname{S}^{7}
can be constructed as a join
\operatorname{S}^{7}=\operatorname{S}^{3}\ast\operatorname{S}^{3}
of two copies of
\operatorname{S}^{3}
, the action of
Q
on
\operatorname{S}^{7}
can also be decomposed as a form of join of the action of
Q
on
\operatorname{S}^{3}
used to define
K
. This suggests defining
\phi^{2}
as a join
\phi^{2}\equiv\phi\ast\phi
, and similarly for
\phi^{n}
with arbitrary
n
(we will recall below how the join operation translate in type theory).
This construction is reminiscent of the one of real projective spaces
[
5
]
and lens spaces
[
13
]
, which are also obtained as joins of suitable maps to the delooping of their fundamental group.

4.1
The join operation in type theory

Given two types
A
and
B
, their
join

A\ast B
, see
[
18
, Section 6.8]
, is the pushout of the product projections:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A\times B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A\ast B}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\ulcorner}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}

In type theory, the type
A\ast B
can be described as the HIT with
A\to A\ast B
and
B\to A\ast B
as base constructors and
(a,b):A\times B\to A\ast B
as a higher constructor adding paths
a=b
indexed by pairs
(a,b):A\times B
.

The operation
\operatorname{S}^{0}\ast-
is the suspension operation so that
\operatorname{S}^{0}\ast\operatorname{S}^{n}=\operatorname{S}^{n+1}
. The join operation is associative, commutative and admits
0
as neutral element
[
2
, Section 1.8]
, and we thus have the fact that
\operatorname{S}^{n}\ast\operatorname{S}^{m}=\operatorname{S}^{n+m-1}
. It can be shown that taking joins makes types more connected in the following sense
[
3
, Proposition 3]
:

Proposition 15
.

If
A
is
m
-connected and
B
is
n
-connected,
A\ast B
is
(m{+}n{+}2)
-connected.

We can generalize the join construction on pairs of morphisms with common codomain as follows. Given maps
f:A\to C
and
g:B\to C
, their
join

f\ast g
is the universal map obtain from the pushout of the dependent projections
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
from their pullback
A\times_{C}B
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A\times_{C}B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A\ast_{C}B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f\ast g}

The source
A\ast_{C}B
can be thought of as a “dependent join over
C
” in the sense that we have
A\ast_{1}B=A\ast B
: we recover the join operation on types by considering terminal maps. An important result about the join operation is the following
[
16
, Theorem 2.3.15]
:

Proposition 16
.

Given morphism
f:A\to C
and
g:B\to C
, for any
x:C
we have an equivalence

\operatorname{fib}_{f\ast g}x=(\operatorname{fib}_{f}x)\ast(\operatorname{fib}_{g}x)

Note that, as a direct consequence, we have

Proposition 17
.

Given two fiber sequences

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{1}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{2}}

their join

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{1}\ast A_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B_{1}\ast_{C}B_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{1}\ast f_{2}}

is a fiber sequence.

Now consider two maps
\phi_{i}:A_{i}\to\operatorname{B}G
for
i\in\left\{1,2\right\}
and some group
G
: those maps can be understood as actions of
G
on
F_{i}\equiv\operatorname{fib}_{\phi_{i}}
by action-fibration duality. Their join
\phi_{1}\ast\phi_{2}
corresponds to the action of
G
on
F_{1}\ast F_{2}
obtained by letting
G
act as
\phi_{i}
on
F_{i}
seen as a subtype of
F_{1}\ast F_{2}
through the canonical map
F_{i}\to F_{1}\ast F_{2}
(given by the definition as a HIT), and extended “by continuity” on other points.

Given a map
f:A\to B
, we write
f^{n}
for the iterated join of
n
copies of
f
. Combining
propositions

15
and
16
, we have that
f^{n}
is more and more connected as
n
increases.
Taking the inductive limit as
n
goes to infinity, it can be shown that these maps converge toward the inclusion
\operatorname{im}(f)\hookrightarrow B

[
16
, Theorem 4.2.13]
.

4.2
Higher-dimensional hypercubical manifolds in type theory

We define the
n
-th higher hypercubical manifold as the type

K^{n}\equiv K\ast_{\operatorname{B}Q}\ldots\ast_{\operatorname{B}Q}K

as the iterated join of
n
copies of
K
, and

\phi^{n}\equiv\phi\ast\ldots\ast\phi:K^{n}\to\operatorname{B}Q

as the corresponding universal map. By joining the fundamental fiber sequence of
theorem

10
using
proposition

17
,

Proposition 18
.

We have, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a fiber sequence

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{S}^{4n-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{B}Q}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi^{n}}

As a direct consequence of the previous fiber sequence, and the fact that
\operatorname{S}^{n}
is
(n{-}1)
-connected
[
18
, Corollary 8.2.2]
, we have

Proposition 19
.

The canonical map
\phi^{n}:K^{n}\to\operatorname{B}Q
is
(4n{-}2)
-connected.

By taking the inductive limit as explained above, we obtain a delooping
K^{\infty}
of
Q
:

Proposition 20
.

We have a fiber sequence

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K^{\infty}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{B}Q}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi^{\infty}}

and thus
\phi^{\infty}
is an equivalence.

Proof.

The fact that
\phi^{\infty}
is an equivalence follows form the fact that it merely has a point and
\operatorname{B}Q
is connected.
∎

Remark 21
.

Note that the previous construction would have worked starting from any map
\phi:A\to\operatorname{B}Q
(as long as
A
is inhabited), the pointing map
1\to\operatorname{B}Q
for instance.

5
Conclusion

We have defined the hypercubical manifold in homotopy type theory and shown the relevance of our construction by exhibiting the resulting type
K
as a quotient of the sphere
\operatorname{S}^{3}
under the canonical action of the fundamental group
Q
. We have also introduced higher dimensional variants of those spaces.

As a direct application, it would be interesting to use
K^{n}
in order to compute the cohomology groups of
Q
in low dimensions (up to
4n-2
), by applying the construction of
[
4
]
to perform cellular cohomology synthetically in homotopy type theory. With integral coefficients, all those groups are already known, and we should for instance be able to recover
H^{2}(Q)
.

We believe that the methodology introduced here is very general and should be applicable in order to define types corresponding to various well-known spaces, including those considered by Poincaré in
[
15
]
. In particular, we plan to investigate a definition of the homology sphere, which is defined in a similar way although the combinatorics is more involved: it can be described as cellular complex with 5 0-cells, 10 1-cells, 6 2-cells and one 3-cell, its fundamental group has 120 elements and the fiber of the fundamental fibration is a model of
\operatorname{S}^{3}
with 600 0-cells, 1200 1-cells, 720 2-cells and 120 3-cells.
