A qualitative study of healthcare workers’ living and working conditions in rural and urban areas of Senegal in the context of Universal Health Coverage: the ANRS-12399 Soignants Senegal study
Authors: Ousmane Diop, Clément Boutet, Kanuya Coulibaly, El Hadji Ba, Gwenaëlle Maradan, Marion Mora, Bruno Spire, Cheikh Sokhna, Luis Sagaon-Teyssier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealthcare delivery in Senegal is hindered by a limited, unequally distributed healthcare workforce, and by inadequate infrastructure which negatively impacts access to quality care and good health outcomes. Despite efforts to improve healthcare delivery through Universal Health Coverage (UHC), progress remains insufficient. We investigated the living and working conditions of Senegalese healthcare workers (HWs), specifically their representations and experiences of the implementation of UHC programs.MethodsThe ANRS-12399 Soignants Senegal study focused on HWs, including community HWs, working in health posts, health centers, regional and national hospitals, located in urban and rural areas. Individual semi-structured interviews were conducted with doctors and with new (preceding 12 months) arrivals (any HR type). Instead, separate semi-structured focus groups were conducted for non-doctor HWs who had graduated in a chosen technical field (e.g., nurses) and non-graduate HWs (e.g., community HWs).ResultsThe sample comprised 60 HWs; median age was 34 years (IQR [29.5–37.5]). Those with administrative responsibility were more likely to have an understanding of the UHC’s various programs. UHC implementation had led to debt for care structures, poorer working conditions because of a deterioration of the patient–HW relationship through unexpected out-of-pocket costs for supposedly free services, delayed salary payment, and drug stock-outs. Moreover, job tenure was shorter in rural areas and for doctors, indicating the attractiveness of urban workplaces and greater doctor mobility, respectively. Reasons for leaving rural positions included dissatisfaction because of poor recognition, limited opportunities for career development, equipment and training infrastructure shortages, distance from one’s family, and the absence of financial incentives. HWs working in urban settings faced more equipment maintenance issues, but were less likely to leave their position. Different knowledge acquisition desires drove mobility to and from rural areas; seeking stable contracts motivated HWs to work in rural areas.ConclusionHWs working in rural Senegal face greater challenges. Deciding whether to work there entails more than financial considerations. Although UHC is not the origin of HWs’ poor working conditions in the country, it exacerbates them. Addressing the difficulties HWs face in rural Senegal is essential to ensure retention and better implementation of UHC’s various programs.Keywords: UHC, Health workers, Human resources for health, Health worker policies, Working conditions, Senegal, Health worker mobility

Background

The delivery of adequate health services in Africa is hampered by an extremely limited workforce (3% of all healthcare workers (HWs) worldwide) [1]. Furthermore, a lack of medical infrastructure, insufficient national health expenditure (on average 7.2% in sub-Saharan countries’ budgets vs 15% recommended by the Abuja Declaration [2]), and governance issues are important structural barriers to health service access in many countries in this region, including Senegal [2–4].

Senegal is ranked among the 50 countries most vulnerable to public health problems [5]. Its health system faces organizational and financing problems which lead to unequal distribution of material and human resources [5]. Medical density is 0.1 doctors and 0.43 nurses and midwives per 1000 inhabitants, which is lower than the minimum of 4.45 doctors, nurses and midwives combined per 1000 inhabitants recommended by the World Health Organization to ensure adequate coverage in the healthcare workforce [6, 7]. This shortage is partly explained by persistent structural difficulties in deploying sufficient personnel to rural areas, where healthcare workers tend to concentrate in urban centers with greater infrastructure and opportunities. As an illustration, physician density in the semi-urban/rural regions of Fatick and Kedougou stands at 0.04 and 0.06 per 1000 inhabitants, respectively, compared to 0.25 per 1000 in the urban Dakar region [8, 9]. This disparity reflects not only a staffing issue, but also an ongoing retention and mobility challenge, and constitutes a major obstacle to access to quality health services and one of the main barriers to the implementation and success of Universal Health Coverage (UHC) in the country [4, 7, 10, 11]. In rural areas, other factors, such as poverty and socio-cultural issues, make the various UHC programs (i.e., community health insurance schemes and free healthcare policies implemented through the Senegalese Agency for Universal Health Coverage, SENCSU) more difficult to implement as people give lower priority to health than to other day-to-day aspects in their lives (food, clothes, etc.) [3, 10, 12–14]. This results in poor basic health outcomes. For example, the indicators of neonatal and infant mortality in Kedougou (38‰ and 105‰, respectively) and Fatick (26‰ and 59‰) are higher than in Dakar (20‰ and 38‰) [15]. Furthermore, the maternal mortality rate in the Kedougou region (921 per 100 000 births) is higher than in Dakar (342 per 100 000 births) [3]. Similar trends are observed for HIV, the prevalence in Kedougou (0.6%) and in Fatick (0.4%) being higher than in Dakar (0.3%) [16].

In the last 20 years, Senegal has implemented several strategies—including the National Health Financing Strategy—to improve access to healthcare services for vulnerable people [3, 17, 18]. They are part of an endeavor to implement universal health coverage (UHC) in the country. In this context, in 2015, the Senegalese Agency for Universal Health Coverage (Agence de la Couverture Maladie Universelle) was established with the task of implementing UHC nationwide [10, 18]. However, one of the main challenges facing the Senegalese health system is ensuring the efficient management of healthcare human resources by the Ministry of Health, especially in rural areas where shortages of HCWs and HCWs’ mobility are more pronounced. In spite of programs to incentivize healthcare workers (HWs) to work in rural areas—such as Plan Cobra in 2006 which offers short-term contracts and supplementary financial incentives—these areas are still severely understaffed [19, 20]. Previous research found that salary alone is not enough of an incentive to attract HWs to or retain them in rural areas [8]. Several studies on HWs in the African context have highlighted a lack of accommodation and career prospects, a shortage of quality working facilities, poor training opportunities, and distance from one’s family, among the factors that hinder HWs’ willingness to work in rural areas [20–24]. The few studies investigating mobility-related issues in this setting [25, 26] highlighted that creating short-term solutions to recruit HWs in rural areas contributed to high personnel turnover rates linked to poor working conditions (e.g., heavy workload, longer working hours, etc.) and mental health issues. In turn, this led to a deterioration in patient access to care and poorer quality of the health services offered [10, 21, 27]. One possibility to assure greater care and support in rural areas is to engage the help of community HWs (CHWs), a population that plays an essential role in the promotion of and access to primary healthcare, particularly in low-income countries [28–32]. However, while their contribution to health outcomes is well-recognized, CHWs often operate at the fringes of the formal health system and face poor living and working conditions. In the context of UHC implementation, understanding how these workers experience policy changes is essential to ensure the sustainability of community-based health strategies.

In this context, the present qualitative study aimed to understand the living and working conditions of HWs (including CHWs) in rural and urban areas in Senegal and explore their representations and experiences regarding the ongoing implementation of the country’s various UHC programs. Understanding the obstacles and facilitators to working in rural areas could help improve HW retention, thereby ameliorating the management of human resources in the context of UHC in Senegal.

Methods

ANRS 12399 Soignants Senegal is a mixed-methods study aiming to garner a better understanding of the living and working conditions of HWs in rural areas of the country. The study’s qualitative component was conducted in 2021. Besides providing details on the study’s outcomes, the qualitative data were used to develop the quantitative component of the study. The present article presents the qualitative data collected from HWs in Dakar (urban), Fatick (rural) and Kedougou (rural) regions. Documents and procedures were approved by the Senegalese National Ethics Committee for Health Research (CNERS) (No. 00000051/April/9/2021). All participants completed and signed an informed consent form.

Participants

The study was proposed to HWs working at all levels of the health system pyramid: (i) peripheral (health centers and health posts); (ii) intermediate (regional hospitals); and (iii) central (national hospitals). In order to ensure a heterogeneous sample, age, gender and position were taken into consideration when recruiting the study sample. Participants included HWs who had studied and graduated in their particular profession (e.g., doctors, midwives, nurses), and HWs who had not studied specifically for their position (e.g., CHWs, traditional midwives), but who had received official training in providing preventive and curative healthcare services. Interns and CHWs who had no such training, including those whose activities were related exclusively to health awareness promotion (i.e., “Bajenu Gox”, experienced women respected in their community who pass on knowledge), and traditional practitioners were not included.

Data collection and analyses

The qualitative survey was conducted separately for doctors and other HWs in order to reduce any bias induced by hierarchical relationships. Specifically, individual semi-structured interviews (IDIs) were conducted with doctors while semi-structured focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted for graduate HWs who were not doctors (Nd–Gr hereafter) (e.g., nurses) and HWs who were not doctors and had not graduated (Nd–nGr hereafter) (e.g., CHWs). In order to obtain greater insight into aspects related with HW mobility toward rural areas, IDIs were also conducted with HWs who arrived at participating health facilities in the 12 months preceding the survey (new arrivals, NwAr hereafter), irrespective of their position (i.e., new staff members, whether they were doctors or non-doctors). General dissatisfaction due to poor recognition

Doctors, non-doctors and NwAr all perceived a severe lack of recognition by authorities in the Ministry of Health despite the great deal of work they do, and highlighted the absence of substantial remuneration to motivate those who have already taken up rural positions to remain there (BGD6, BI3) (Table 3).

Poor living conditions generally distributed across settings and populations

The Nd–Gr group pointed out inadequate pay while the Nd–nGr group emphasized the lack of contracts as the main factor concerning their poor working conditions (BGC1). HWs working in rural areas also highlighted the low public service salaries and the lack of complementary employment opportunities due to the absence of private health infrastructure in rural areas (NGD8). In urban settings, participants declared that the shortage of HWs was mainly related to inappropriate tasks and difficult relationships with patients (DI1, DI2, DGD2).

The quality of work harmed by a lack of equipment and means

All participant groups complained about the lack of equipment and means to competently perform their work. Complaints varied according to profession. Doctors mentioned that there was insufficient equipment to comprehensively perform their duties, such as adequate machines to make diagnoses (BI2) (Table 4). Non-doctors, especially CHWs, highlighted problems such as power outages/no electricity supply, and transport difficulties which negatively impacted the quality of their work, notably during vaccination campaigns in the rainy season (BGC5). A rural–urban comparison showed that all HWs were confronted with a lack of equipment and qualified personnel for equipment maintenance, although rural HWs placed greater emphasis on the absence of equipment (BI1, BI3) while their urban counterparts placed more emphasis on the lack of equipment maintenance (DI1).

Entanglement of factors associated with HW mobility

Individual and structural factors can lead to urban–rural mobility in both directions

The main individual factors highlighted in terms of job mobility (be it to rural areas or from them) were family and financial factors. Family was the primary cause of this mobility among HWs working in rural areas. More specifically, for some participants, the main reason for leaving their position in rural areas was the distance away from spouses; for others, it was their children’s education (NI1_1, NI1_2, NI3) (Table 6). The search for an optimal economic situation for HWs’ families often resulted in job mobility to rural areas (BN4_1). Indeed, financial incentives were cited as the main reason for relocating from one place to another among NwAr (BN4_2).

Mobility factors

The main structural factors identified as the origin of mobility or an intention to move (irrespective of the HW group), were the type of contract, low wages, the lack of both training and equipment, as well as the absence of financial bonuses linked to the type of zone where they worked. With respect to financial and contractual incentives, doctors and Nd–Gr indicated that the search for relatively better contracts could lead to mobility from urban to rural areas. They cited the Ministry of Health contract, which is considered as stable, and consequently motivates some HWs to agree to go to rural areas rather than stay in urban areas without a contract. In contrast, they also indicated the search for higher wages, which seemed to be an important determinant of mobility from rural to urban areas, especially for doctors (BI1). The civil service salary is the same in Senegal, irrespective of the region. However, HWs can complement their income by working in other private/public structures as well as the one where they have their state contract. Indeed, participants mentioned this point: HWs working in urban areas have more opportunities to increase their income. (DI1).

In terms of career development, doctors and some Nd–Gr emphasized the shortage of infrastructure for developing specialist skills and knowledge. In their opinion, the absence of medical schools, training organizations and stable internet access contributed to the departure of HWs from rural areas (BI3, BGD7). This point of view contrasted with the NwAr group and other Nd–Gr who thought that it was easier to acquire knowledge in rural areas precisely because the shortage of HWs provided the opportunity to practice even more. They felt that it would be much more difficult to have such an opportunity in an urban setting (BN2). This echoed Nd–nGr opinions who highlighted an improvement in their knowledge thanks to on-the-job training (BGC2). The greater involvement in capacity-building training of NGOs in rural areas was also appreciated by CHWs. Finally, Nd–nGr were not very mobile because of their strong local roots. They often took on a second job (e.g., farming, trading) to supplement their income (BGC4).

Factors influencing retention of HWs in rural and urban areas differed substantially

Despite the mismatch between the number and types of tasks performed on the one hand, and poor working conditions on the other, some of the HWs expressed satisfaction with their work. A love for their activities, a sense of commitment and the feeling of doing a noble job were at the core of this satisfaction. This moral dimension motivated HWs to stay in rural areas, particularly the Nd–nGr group but these elements were also cited by Nd–Gr.

HWs working in rural areas declared being enormously satisfied by the fact that they save lives, contribute to the health of the population, and engage in healthcare outreach. For many HWs, particularly Nd–nGr, the main reward for their work was almost divine thanks to the prayers for them from the population. Indeed, some parents named their children after HWs in recognition for their hard work (NGC5P) (Table 7).

Factors harming HW retention in rural and urban areas

There was also a social dimension leading to a strong connection between HWs working in rural areas. This was manifested in the form of mutual aid and social ties between colleagues, sometimes erasing professional hierarchy (BGD8), (NGC6P), although one CHW declared a lack of consideration by HWs in higher professional positions in one rural healthcare center.

Participating doctors highlighted that the key drivers for retaining them in urban areas were the abundance of working opportunities in cities, and the fact that civil servants received equal compensation irrespective of the geographical area (i.e., urban or rural). In addition, doctors and Nd–Gr highlighted recognition of their efforts by patients, as well as ties with family and friends residing in the same area as crucial elements in HW retention (DI1, DGD3). NwAr did not exhibit any significant difference compared to the other groups in terms of retention.

UHC in Senegal: the boomerang effects on supply and demand

Almost all the HWs considered that UHC has had a positive effect on the general population by facilitating access to healthcare. However, almost all also considered that it had had a negative impact on healthcare structures, primarily due to difficulties related to the reimbursement of expenses incurred by structures.

Limited knowledge of HWs about UHC

Irrespective of professional category and work location, HWs demonstrated limited knowledge of UHC concepts in the country, and this topic was addressed only briefly during the interviews. The only aspects that seemed to be known were the existing free programs and their objectives.

Evolution of healthcare structures and ‘poor’ working conditions in the context of UHC

Doctors and Nd–Gr with administrative responsibilities had a better understanding of the impacts of UHC. While all HWs recognized the reduction in financial barriers to access to care, they believed that the UHC programs’ direct influence on working conditions was limited. Doctors perceived that the improved access to healthcare provided by these programs led to improved disease management and early diagnosis (BI2, NI3_1) (Table 8). However, the majority of HWs felt their implementation had negatively affected healthcare structures (NI3), especially periphery structures (i.e., health posts) which lie at the bottom of the Senegal’s healthcare pyramid (NN4, NN3_2). Moreover, they felt that this negative impact was primarily due to poor financial management by the national agency in charge of the UHC implementation. Specifically, they cited the long delays for the reimbursement of costs (services and medicines provided by the structures). These delays resulted not only in drug stock-outs and a lack of materials and equipment, which in turn adversely impacted patient–HW relationships (DI2_1), but also unpaid/delayed wages leading to poor working conditions. Additionally, HWs cited the discrepancies between the official information conveyed about UHC and the actual situation in health facilities (DI2_2). For example, official information indicated that cesarean kits were free of charge. However, upon arrival in a care structure, patients often found that these kits were incomplete and they had to pay for the missing elements (DI2_3). Such a divergence led to patient frustration and confrontations with HWs, which in turn affected the latter’s motivation and the quality of the service they provided (DI2_4, DI2_5, NI1_1, NI1_2). Some health facilities had implemented strategies to address the financial challenges posed by delayed reimbursement and salary payments, such as implementing partial payments (up to 50%) for medications which under the UHC scheme were supposed to be free of charge (BI4) and financial bonuses for HWs post-reimbursement in order to compensate for the time they were without a salary (NI1_3). However, this compensation was seen by some as inadequate, as it failed to meet their day-to-day needs (NI1_4). Furthermore, HWs felt that UHC lacked sufficient support in terms of human and material resources to effectively serve the Senegalese population (BI1). Overall, HWs questioned the effectiveness of UHC in the country, citing financial difficulties, drug stock-outs, and limited support as key challenges to achieving the goal of providing accessible healthcare for vulnerable populations, especially in rural areas where poverty is higher (NN3_3).

Impact of UHC programs on healthcare evolution and working conditions in Senegal

Discussion

HWs in rural areas face compounded adverse working conditions, including a lack of medical equipment and limited opportunities for training and professional development—factors identified by participants as key to retaining healthcare workers in these areas. [20, 22, 23, 33, 34]. In this context, where structural factors compromise the continuum and quality of care [35–37], HWs are constrained to make additional efforts to preserve patient trust in the health system. Although HWs who worked in urban areas also highlighted a lack of equipment, the main issue in these areas was the lack of specialized personnel for equipment maintenance. For these professionals, this was a major obstacle to ensuring quality health services. In turn, the consequence was a shorter job tenure, especially for doctors [4, 20]. All these results demonstrate that strategies for the improvement of HWs’ working conditions and job retention should consider the specific needs of HWs according to the position of their healthcare structure in the health system pyramid, the composition of the HW staff in the structure and the predominant activities performed there (diagnoses, treatment and/or prevention) [20, 22, 34]. Furthermore, our results highlight that HW retention in rural areas also depends on opportunities for training/knowledge acquisition that contribute to the improvement of human capital, and by consequence to the quality of care provided [38–40]. Financial issues such as low wages, scarce/inexistent opportunities for complementary income from a second job, and contract insecurity are also factors that must be taken into account in public policies promoting HW retention in rural areas [23, 26]. Furthermore, our results demonstrate that improving the working environment as a strategy for HW retention in rural areas should be combined with improving these professionals’ living conditions. Specifically, our results highlight that the distance from one’s family is an important obstacle to retention in rural areas of HWs coming from an urban setting. However, this is not the case for local HWs from rural areas who work in proximity to their family and their community. Working close to one’s loved ones strengthens engagement with the community, which in turn partially counterbalances poor working conditions. For example, our study shows that there is little mobility among CHWs and that their activity is mostly driven by non-financial motivations such as faith, dedication, and recognition. This reflects previous findings [26].

This study highlights that these poor working conditions for Senegalese HWs are exacerbated by the implementation of UHC—especially, though not exclusively in rural areas. While most healthcare workers did not fully understand the UHC programs, they clearly denounced their daily consequences. Frequent delays in reimbursement for UHC-related services, which healthcare facilities must pre-finance, were among the main issues negatively affecting the organization and functioning of these facilities [35, 41, 42]. Our results highlight that these UHC-related financial issues contribute to the poor HWs’ working conditions in three different ways: i) lack of motivation caused by the late payment of wages; ii) difficulties in providing quality services due to frequent drug and material stock-outs; and iii) patient dissatisfaction with expected UHC-related services, which led to a deterioration of the patient–HW relationship. With regard to the latter point, the patient–HW relationship plays a crucial role in the day-to-day activities of HWs [23, 43], especially in rural areas, where the UHC programs are supposed to help the most.

This study has limitations. First, it was conducted in the Fatick and Kedougou regions which host the two main demographic observatories in Senegal. These two regions alone may not be representative of the results in Senegal’s rural areas. Second, urban-based HWs all came from only one national hospital in Dakar. Health centers and health posts were excluded in this urban region, whereas they were included in rural areas. Thus, the results for urban areas cannot be generalized and the elements raised do not reflect the situation of all HWs in the Dakar region. Having said that, this structure is one of the biggest in the country, and offers a general overview of the organizational problems which urban healthcare structures in Senegal face. Indeed, given that this important structure faced financial difficulties, and a lack of both equipment and human resources, we can suppose that smaller structures are also confronted with these problems. Third, this qualitative investigation, with small samples in each region might not have captured the diversity of health workers’ profiles in Senegal in the context of UHC. Nevertheless, ANRS-Soignants was a mixed-method project with an exploratory design and this qualitative investigation helped to build the quantitative survey [44].

Our study provides in-depth information about the situation of healthcare structures in rural areas of Senegal in the context of UHC implementation and reopens the debate about the need for efficient public policies to retain HWs in rural areas. This includes CHWs, a population who, despite indicating their satisfaction with the work they perform, still endure the poor working conditions highlighted by other HWs in our study. More specifically, these policies should consider the lessons learned on the ground about how the engagement of CHWs with their community and their proximity to their family help the health system to function, despite their working conditions. Broader than CHWs, rural pipeline strategies, i.e., training health workers locally to deploy them locally [45], have shown promising results across sub-Saharan Africa but no studies were found in Senegal in a recent scoping review [46]. As stated by this review, implementing a rural pipeline strategy implies a strong State commitment with infrastructure building in remote areas. Our findings corroborate this review and add that increasing medical density and improving HW retention in rural areas require cross-sectoral cooperation (education, housing, transport, etc.) not only to form and deploy health workers locally, but also to facilitate the process of health workers’ families moving to rural areas [8, 23, 28–32]. Solving preexisting issues concerning HWs’ working conditions would appear to be a fundamental step for the continued implementation of UHC in Senegal.

Conclusion

Working conditions for Senegalese HWs appeared to be more difficult for those working in rural areas. The decision to stay in rural areas is complex and goes beyond financial considerations. Authorities need to take into account the impact of UHC programs, as it would appear that it accentuates preexisting poor working conditions of HWs, especially in rural areas, but urban HWs also reported difficulties with UHC. Paradoxically, this threatens the primary goal of UHC, which is equitable access to care. The success of UHC in Senegal will depend on an overall improvement in healthcare workers’ working conditions, on adequate training of healthcare workers and healthcare users regarding the program’s coverage, and on effective job retention. For the latter, rural pipeline strategies have shown promising results in neighboring countries.
