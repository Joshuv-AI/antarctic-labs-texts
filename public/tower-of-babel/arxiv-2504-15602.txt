Mean Curvature Flow for Isoparametric Submanifolds in Hyperbolic Spaces
Xiaobo Liu, Wanxu Yang

Abstract
Mean curvature flows of isoparametric submanifolds in Euclidean spaces and spheres have been studied by Liu and Terng in [ 12 ] and [ 13 ] . In particular, it was proved that such flows always have ancient solutions. This is also true for mean curvature flows of isoparametric hypersurfaces in hyperbolic spaces by a result of Reis and Tenenblat in [ 15 ] . In this paper, we study mean curvature flows of isoparametric submanifolds in hyperbolic spaces with arbitrary codimension. In particular, we will show that they always have ancient solutions and study their limiting behaviors.



1
Introduction

The
mean curvature flow
(abbreviated as MCF) of a Riemannian submanifold
M
in a semi-Riemannian manifold X is a map

f:M\times I\rightarrow X
satisfying

\displaystyle\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=H(\cdot,t),

(1.1)

where
I
is an interval containing
0
,
f(\cdot,0)
is the immersion of
M
in
X
, and
H(\cdot,t)
is the mean curvature vector field of
f(\cdot,t)
for all
t\in I
.
A solution to this equation is
ancient
if it exists over an interval
I=(-\infty,T)
for some
T>0
.
Ancient solutions play an important role in the study of singularities of MCF. A large class of ancient solutions
are given by MCF of isoparametric submanifolds.

A submanifold in a space form is
isoparametric
if its normal bundle is flat and principal curvatures along any parallel normal vector field are constant (cf.
[
16
]
and
[
19
]
).
In
[
12
]
and
[
13
]
, Terng and the first author of this paper studied MCF
of isoparametric submanifolds
M
in Euclidean spaces and spheres.
In particular, a recursive method to construct explicit solutions of MCF for such submanifolds were given in
[
12
]
.
It was also proved that MCF of
M
always converges to a focal submanifold in a finite positive time if
M
is not minimal.
In
[
13
]
, it was proved that MCF of all
M
have
ancient solutions
. This gives many explicit examples with complex topological types for ancient solutions of MCF in Euclidean spaces and spheres. These examples were used to study sharpness of conditions in Huisken and Sinestrari’s rigidity theorem about ancient solutions for MCF of hypersurfaces in spheres modeled on shrinking
spherical caps (cf.
[
8
]
). Rigidity conjectures modeled on MCF of general isoparametric hypersurfaces in spheres
were also proposed in
[
13
]
.
On the other hand, in
[
15
]
, Reis and Tenenblat gave explicit ancient solutions for MCF of isoparametric
hypersurfaces in hyperbolic spaces
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
with sectional curvature equal to
-1
.

A notion of isoparametric submanifolds in general Riemannian manifolds was introduced in
[
7
]
.
Equifocal submanifolds in compact symmetric spaces defined by Terng and Thorbergsson in
[
18
]

is equivalent to isoparametric submanifolds with flat sections in
[
7
]
.
Results analogous to those in
[
12
]
were obtained for MCF of equifocal submanifolds in compact symmetric spaces by Koike in
[
9
]
, and for MCF of regular leaves in generalized isoparametric foliations with compact leaves on compact Riemannian manifolds by Alexandrino and Radeschi in
[
2
]
. The compactness condition for leaves and ambient spaces in
[
2
]
was replaced by a weaker conditon for the compactness of leaf spaces by Alexandrino, Cavenaghi and Gonçalves in
[
1
]
.
Results analogous to those in
[
13
]
were obtained for MCF of regular leaves of polar foliations (they are in fact isoparametric) in simply connected symmetric spaces with non-negative curvature by Radeschi and the first author of this paper in
[
11
]
.
In
[
10
]
, Koike also studied MCF for curvature-adapted isoparametric submanifolds with flat sections in non-compact
symmetric spaces. Note that isoparametric submanifolds with flat sections in hyperbolic spaces must be hypersurfaces.
Above results do not cover MCF for isoparametric submanifolds in hyperbolic spaces with codimension bigger than one.

The purpose of this paper is to study MCF for isoparametric submanifolds in hyperbolic spaces with arbitrary codimension.
One of the main results of this paper is the following

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
M
be a complete isoparametric submanifold in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
, and
f(\cdot,t)
the MCF of
M
in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
. Then
f(\cdot,t)
is always an ancient solution. Moreover, let
(-\infty,T)
be the maximum existence interval for
f(\cdot,t)
with

T\geq 0
. Then we have the following:

(1)

If
T<\infty
, then
f(\cdot,t)
collapses to a focal submanifold of
M
as
t\rightarrow T
.

(2)

If
T=\infty
and
M
is not flat, then
f(\cdot,t)
converges to a totally geodesic submanifold of
H^{m}(-1)
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

(3)

If
T=\infty
and
M
is flat, then
f(\cdot,t)
converges to a point on the ideal boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

(4)

Assume
M
is not totally geodesic in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
(otherwise
f(\cdot,t)
is independent of
t
). As
t\rightarrow-\infty
,
f(\cdot,t)
converges to a smooth submanifold
\widetilde{M}
with flat normal bundle in the ideal boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
. Moreover,
\widetilde{M}
and
M
have the same dimension.

The precise meaning of smooth submanifold with flat normal bundle in the ideal boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
will be given in Definition
2.5
. In this paper,
we will also prove that minimal isoparametric submanifolds in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
are always totally geodesic (see Lemma
4.5
).

During the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we also obtain a recursive method for constructing explicit solutions for MCF of
isoparametric submanifolds in hyperbolic spaces (see Remark
4.7
). The relation between
MCF of such submanifolds in hyperbolic spaces and MCF of them considered as submanifolds in Lorentz spaces
plays a vital role in the proof of above theorem. However, unlike in Euclidean spaces and spheres considered in
[
13
]
, MCF of some isoparametic submanifolds in Lorentz spaces do not have ancient solutions.

This paper is organized as follows:
In Section
2
, we review basic definitions and properties for isoparametric submanifolds and ideal boundaries of
hyperbolic spaces. In Section
3
, we study MCF for isoparametric submanifolds in Lorentz spaces. The proof of Theorem
1.1
will be given in Section
4
.

2
Preliminary

In this section, we review basic concepts and properties for isoparametric submanifolds and ideal boundaries
of hyperbolic spaces, which will be needed later in this paper. We will also clarify the meaning of
smooth submanifolds with flat normal bundles in the ideal boundaries of hyperbolic spaces.

2.1
Isoparametric submanifolds in hyperbolic spaces.

For any non-negative integer
m
, let
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
be the standard Lorentz space, which is an
m+1
dimensional real vector space equipped with the following Lorentzian metric:

\displaystyle\left<x,y\right>=\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}y_{i}-x_{m+1}y_{m+1}

for
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{m+1}),y=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{m+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
.
We refer to the book
[
14
]
for general theory of Lorentzian geometries.
The hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
with sectional curvature
-1/r
can be realized as a submanifold
of
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
with the induced Riemannian metric in the following way

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m,1}\mid\left<x,x\right>=-r,\,x_{m+1}>0\},

where
r>0
is a constant.
It is well known (cf.
[
16
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
19
]
) that any isoparametric submanifold in
space forms
(i.e. Euclidean spaces, spheres, and Hyperbolic spaces) is an open part of a complete isoparametric submanifold. Without loss of generality, we require all isoparametric submanifolds in space forms are complete throughout this paper.

The concept of isoparametric submanifolds in space forms can be extended to Lorentz spaces. A submanifold
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
is said to be
Riemannian
(also called spacelike in some literature) if the induced metric on
M
is positive definite.

M
is called an
isoparametric submanifold
in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
if
M
is a Riemannian submanifold, the normal bundle of
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
is globally flat, and the principal curvatures along any parallel normal vector field are constant (cf.
[
20
]
).
The following lemma is essentially due to Wu (cf.
[
19
]
):

Lemma 2.1
.

For any submanifold
M\subset\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)\subset\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
,

M
is isoparametric in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
if and only if
M
is isoparametric in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
.

In fact, Proposition 1.10 in
[
19
]
gives one direction of this lemma. The proof of another direction is straightforward.

A submanifold
M
of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
is
full
if
M
is not included in any totally umbilical hypersurface of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
.
The following decomposition theorem was proved by Wu (see Corollary 2.6 in
[
19
]
):

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume
M\subset\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)\subset\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
. If
M
is a full isoparametric submanifold,
then there exist
r\geq 1
and a Lorentzian subspace
V
of
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
with dimension
l+1
such that

\displaystyle M=\mathbb{H}^{l}(-r)\times M^{\prime},

where
\mathbb{H}^{l}(-r)\subset V
is one sheet of the hyperboloid

\{x\in V\mid\left<x,x\right>=-r\}

and
M^{\prime}
is an isoparametric submanifold of the sphere
S^{m-l-1}(-1+r)\subset V^{\perp}
.

In this theorem
S^{k}(a)
denotes the
k
-dimensional sphere with radius
\sqrt{a}
centered at origin.

If an isoparametric submanifold
M\subset\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
is not full, then it must be an isoparametric
submanifold of a totally umbilical hypersurface (with induced metric) in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
(see Proposition 1.3 in
[
19
]
).
It is well known that any totally umbilical complete hypersurface of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
must have the form

L(V,u)=\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)\bigcap(V+u)
, where
V
is a linear hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
(see, for example,
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
and
[
19
]
). With the induced metric,

L(V,u)
is isometric to a Euclidean space, or a sphere, or a hyperbolic space if the restriction of
Lorentz metric
\left<\cdot,\,\cdot\right>
to
V
is degenerate, or positive definite, or a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form with index
1
respectively. Hence if an isoparametric submanifold
M\subset\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
is not full, it must be
an isoparametric submanifold in a Euclidean space, or a sphere, or a lower dimensional hyperbolic space.

2.2
Ideal boundaries of hyperbolic spaces.

Let
X
be a complete Riemannian manifold with non-positive curvature.
Two geodesic rays
c_{i}:[0,\infty)\longrightarrow M
for
i=1,2
are equivalent if the distance between
c_{1}(t)
and
c_{2}(t)
are bounded from above by a constant for all
t\geq 0
.
The
ideal boundary
of
X
, denoted by
\partial X
, is defined to be the set of all equivalence classes of geodesic rays in
X
.
There exists a natural topology on
\overline{X}=X\bigcup\partial X
whose restriction to
X
is the original topology
induced from the Riemannian metric on
X

(see, for example, Chapter II.8 section 8.5 in
[
5
]
).

To prove parts (3) and (4) of Theorem
1.1
, we need a more explicit description of the ideal boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
.
Let
\mathbb{B}^{m}
be the open unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with the standard Euclidean metric, i.e.

\mathbb{B}^{m}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid\|y\|^{2}<1\}.

(2.1)

For
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{m},x_{m+1})\in\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m,1}
, define

\psi(x):=\left(\frac{x_{1}}{1+x_{m+1}},\cdots,\frac{x_{m}}{1+x_{m+1}}\right).

(2.2)

This gives a conformal diffeomorphism
\psi:\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}^{m}
.
Moreover,
\psi
can be extended to a homeomorphism, also denoted by
\psi
, from
\overline{\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)}

to
\overline{\mathbb{B}^{m}}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid\|y\|^{2}\leq 1\}
.
In particular, it induces a homeomorphsim from the ideal boundary of
{\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)}

to

\partial\mathbb{B}^{m}:=S^{m-1}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid\|y\|^{2}=1\}.

Remark 2.3
.

Note that
\psi^{-1}
is the map given in Chapter I.6 section 6.7 of
[
5
]
.
Pulling back the metric on
{\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)}
using
\psi^{-1}
, we obtain a hyperbolic
metric on
{\mathbb{B}^{m}}
which has the form
4(1-\|y\|^{2})^{-2}g_{E}
for
y\in{\mathbb{B}^{m}}
, where
g_{E}
is
the standard Euclidean metric.

{\mathbb{B}^{m}}
with this hyperbolic metric is called the Poincaré ball model.
In this paper, we will always use the standard Euclidean metric on
{\mathbb{B}^{m}}
unless otherwise stated.

More generally, let

\epsilon=\{\epsilon_{1},\cdots,\epsilon_{m+1}\}
be an arbitrary
orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}

satisfying the condition

\left<\epsilon_{i},\epsilon_{i}\right>=1,\hskip 20.0pt\left<\epsilon_{m+1},\epsilon_{m+1}\right>=-1,\hskip 20.0pt\left<\epsilon_{m+1},(0,\cdots,0,1)\right><0

(2.3)

for
1\leq i\leq m
.
For
a_{1},\cdots,a_{m+1}\in\mathbb{R}
, define

\Phi_{\epsilon}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}a_{i}\epsilon_{i}\right):=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{m+1}).

Then
\Phi_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{m,1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m,1}

is an isometric transformation which preserves
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
for all
r>0
.
We can define

\Psi_{\epsilon,r}:\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}^{m}
by

\Psi_{\epsilon,r}\left(x\right):=\frac{1}{a_{m+1}+\sqrt{r}}(a_{1},\cdots,a_{m})

(2.4)

for
x=\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}a_{i}\epsilon_{i}\in\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
.
Then
\Psi_{\epsilon,r}
is a conformal diffeomorphism which can be extended to a homeomorphism,
also denoted by
\Psi_{\epsilon,r}
,
from
\overline{\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)}
to
\overline{\mathbb{B}^{m}}
.
In particular, it induces a homeomorphism from ideal boundary
\partial{\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)}
to
\partial{\mathbb{B}^{m}}
.
This gives an identification of
\partial{\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)}
with
\partial{\mathbb{B}^{m}}
which depends on
the choice of the orthonormal basis
\epsilon
.

Let
e=\{e_{1},\cdots,e_{m+1}\}
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
, i.e.

e_{i}=(0,\cdots,0,1,0,\cdots,0)
where the
i
-th coordinate is
1
and all other coordinates are
0
.
Then the map
\Psi_{e,r}\circ\Psi_{\epsilon,r}^{-1}
is a homeomorphism from
\overline{\mathbb{B}^{m}}
to itself, which is also a diffeomorphism when restricted to
{\mathbb{B}^{m}}
.
We have the following

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\Theta:S^{m-1}\longrightarrow S^{m-1}
be the restriction of
\Psi_{e,r}\circ\Psi_{\epsilon,r}^{-1}

to
\partial\mathbb{B}^{m}=S^{m-1}
. Then
\Theta
is a conformal transformation with respect to the standard
metric on the unit sphere
S^{m-1}
.

Proof
:
For each
i=1,\cdots,m+1
, write
\epsilon_{i}=(v_{i},c_{i})
where
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
consists of the first
m
coordinates of
\epsilon_{i}
and
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the last coordinate of
\epsilon_{i}
. Then for every
a=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{m})\in{\mathbb{B}^{m}}
,

\Psi_{e,r}\circ\Psi_{\epsilon,r}^{-1}(a)=\frac{(1+\|a\|^{2})v_{m+1}+2\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}v_{i}}{1-\|a\|^{2}+(1+\|a\|^{2})c_{m+1}+2\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}c_{i}}.

Therefore, if
\|a\|=1
, i.e.
a\in S^{m-1}=\partial{\mathbb{B}^{m}}
, we have

\Theta(a)=\frac{1}{h(a,c)}\left(v_{m+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}v_{i}\right),

(2.5)

where

h(a,c):=c_{m+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}c_{i}.

To show
\Theta
is a smooth map, we only need to show
h(a,c)\neq 0
for all
a\in S^{m-1}
.
We first observe that
c_{i}=-<\epsilon_{i},e_{m+1}>
for all
i
. Hence

e_{m+1}=c_{m+1}\epsilon_{m+1}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\epsilon_{i},

and

-c_{m+1}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}^{2}\,=\,<e_{m+1},e_{m+1}>=-1.

Together with condition (
2.3
), we obtain
c_{m+1}\geq 1
. So
by Cauchy inequality, we have

h(a,c)\geq c_{m+1}-\|a\|\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=c_{m+1}-\sqrt{c_{m+1}^{2}-1}>0

for all
a\in S^{m-1}
.
Hence
\Theta
is smooth. Since we already know
\Theta
is a homeomorphism by construction, this also shows that

\Theta
is a diffeomorphism from
S^{m-1}
to itself.

Now we prove that
\Theta
is a conformal map. In fact, for

w=(w_{1},\cdots,w_{m})\in T_{a}S^{m-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m},

we have

\Theta_{*}(w)=\frac{1}{h(a,c)^{2}}\left(s(c,w)v_{m+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}(a,c,w)v_{i}\right),

where

s(c,w):=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}w_{i},\hskip 20.0ptr_{i}(a,c,w):=h(a,c)w_{i}+s(c,w)a_{i}.

Since
\epsilon_{1},\cdots,\epsilon_{m+1}
are orthonormal in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
, we have

\|v_{m+1}\|^{2}=c_{m+1}^{2}-1,\hskip 10.0pt\|v_{i}\|^{2}=c_{i}^{2}+1,\hskip 10.0pt<v_{j},v_{k}>=c_{j}c_{k}

for all
i=1,\cdots,m
and
j,k=1,\cdots,m+1
with
j\neq k
.
Hence

\displaystyle h(a,c)^{4}\|\Theta_{*}(w)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(s(c,w)c_{m+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}(a,c,w)c_{i}\right)^{2}-s(c,w)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}(a,c,w)^{2}

It is straightforward to check that

s(c,w)c_{m+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}(a,c,w)c_{i}=0

and

-s(c,w)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}(a,c,w)^{2}=h(a,c)^{2}\|w\|^{2}

using the fact
\|a\|^{2}=1
and
\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}w_{i}=0
since
w\in T_{a}S^{m-1}
.
Therefore we have proved that

\|\Theta_{*}(w)\|^{2}=\frac{\|w\|^{2}}{\left(c_{m+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}c_{i}\right)^{2}}

(2.6)

for all
a\in S^{m-1}
and
w\in T_{a}S^{m-1}
.
This shows that
\Theta
is a conformal map and the lemma is thus proved.

\Box

Definition 2.5
.

Let
M
be a subset of
\partial\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
.
M
is called a smooth submanifold in the ideal boundary
of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
if
\Psi_{\epsilon,r}(M)
is a smooth submanifold of
S^{m-1}
for any
orthonormal basis
\epsilon
of
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
satisfying condition (
2.3
).
We say
M
has flat normal bundle in the ideal boundary if
the normal bundle of
\Psi_{\epsilon,r}(M)
in
S^{m-1}
is flat with respect to the standard metric on
S^{m-1}
.

Lemma
2.4
shows that the notion of ”smooth submanifold in the ideal boundary” is independent
of the choice of orthonormal basis
\epsilon
. Together with the lemma below, it also implies that
the notion for
”flatness of normal bundle of
M
” does not depend on the choice of
\epsilon
.
Hence both notions are well defined.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
g^{\prime}
and
g
be two conformally equivalent Riemannian metrics on a smooth manifold
N

such that
g^{\prime}=e^{2\rho}g
, where
\rho
is a smooth function on
N
.
Assume
M
is an
n
-dimensional submanifold of
N
.

(1)

Let
R^{\prime\perp}
and
R^{\perp}
be the curvature operators on the normal bundle
\nu M
of
M
in
N

with respect to
g^{\prime}
and
g
respectively. Then we have

R^{\prime\perp}=R^{\perp}.

In particular,
\nu M
is flat with respect to
g^{\prime}
if and only if
it is flat with respect to
g
.

(2)

Let
H^{\prime}(x)
and
H(x)
be the mean curvature vectors of
M
at point
x
with respect to
g^{\prime}
and
g
respectively.
Then for all
x\in M
,

H^{\prime}(x)=-ne^{-2\rho(x)}({\rm grad}(\rho)\mid_{x})^{\perp}+e^{-2\rho(x)}H(x),

where superscript
\perp
denotes the projection from
T_{x}N
to the normal space
\nu_{x}M
,

{\rm grad}(\rho)
is the gradient of
\rho
with respect to
g
.

Proof
:
First observe that since
g^{\prime}
and
g
are conformally equivalent, the normal bundles of
M
with respect to these two metrics are the same, which is denoted by
\nu M
. In particular, the normal projection
\perp
is well defined.
Let
\overline{D}
,
D
,
D^{\perp}
(or
\overline{D^{\prime}}
,
D^{\prime}
,
D^{\prime\perp}
) be the Levi-Civita connections on
TN
,
TM
,
\nu M
with respect to
g
(or
g^{\prime}
) respectively. For any local vector fields
X
and
Y
on
N
, it is well known that

\overline{D^{\prime}}_{X}Y=\overline{D}_{X}Y+S(X,Y),

(2.7)

where

S(X,Y):=X(\rho)\,Y+Y(\rho)\,X-g(X,Y)\,{\rm grad}(\rho)

(2.8)

(see, for example, Chapter 8 Exercises 5 in
[
6
]
).

Proof of part (1):
For any local tangent vector field
Y
on
M
and local normal vector field
\eta
on
M
, we have

\displaystyle S(Y,\eta)^{\perp}

\displaystyle=\big(Y(\rho)\,\eta+\eta(\rho)\,Y-g(Y,\eta)\,{\rm grad}(\rho)\big)^{\perp}\,=Y(\rho)\,\eta,

(2.9)

and

\displaystyle D^{\prime\perp}_{Y}\,\eta

\displaystyle=\big(\overline{D^{\prime}}_{Y}\,\eta\big)^{\perp}\,=\,\big(\overline{D}_{Y}\,\eta+S(Y,\eta)\big)^{\perp}\,=\,D^{\perp}_{Y}\,\eta+Y(\rho)\,\eta.

(2.10)

By equation (
2.10
), for any local tangent vector fields
X
,
Y
on
M
and local normal vector field
\eta

on
M
, we have

\displaystyle D^{\prime\perp}_{X}D^{\prime\perp}_{Y}\,\eta

\displaystyle=D^{\perp}_{X}\big(D^{\prime\perp}_{Y}\,\eta\big)+X(\rho)D^{\prime\perp}_{Y}\,\eta

\displaystyle=D^{\perp}_{X}\big(D^{\perp}_{Y}\,\eta+Y(\rho)\,\eta\big)+X(\rho)\big(D^{\perp}_{Y}\,\eta+Y(\rho)\,\eta\big)

\displaystyle=D^{\perp}_{X}D^{\perp}_{Y}\,\eta+XY(\rho)\,\eta+Y(\rho)\,D^{\perp}_{X}\,\eta+X(\rho)D^{\perp}_{Y}\,\eta+X(\rho)Y(\rho)\,\eta.

Hence

\displaystyle D^{\prime\perp}_{X}D^{\prime\perp}_{Y}\,\eta-D^{\prime\perp}_{Y}D^{\prime\perp}_{X}\,\eta

\displaystyle=D^{\perp}_{X}D^{\perp}_{Y}\,\eta-D^{\perp}_{Y}D^{\perp}_{X}\,\eta+[X,Y](\rho)\,\eta.

(2.11)

Using equations (
2.10
) and (
2.11
), we have

\displaystyle R^{\prime\perp}(X,Y)\eta=

\displaystyle\big(D^{\prime\perp}_{X}D^{\prime\perp}_{Y}\,\eta-D^{\prime\perp}_{Y}D^{\prime\perp}_{X}\,\eta\big)-D^{\prime\perp}_{[X,Y]}\,\eta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(D^{\perp}_{X}D^{\perp}_{Y}\,\eta-D^{\perp}_{Y}D^{\perp}_{X}\,\eta+[X,Y](\rho)\,\eta\big)-\big(D^{\perp}_{[X,Y]}\,\eta+[X,Y](\rho)\eta\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R^{\perp}(X,Y)\eta.

By definition,
\nu M
is flat if its curvature operator is
0
. This finishes the proof of part (1)
of this lemma.

Proof of part (2):
Let
A^{\prime}
and
A
be the shape operators of
M
in
N
with respect to metrics
g^{\prime}
and
g
respectively.
By equations (
2.7
) and (
2.8
), for any local tangent vector field
X
on
M
and local normal
vector field
\eta
on
M
, we have

A^{\prime}_{\eta}X=-(\overline{D^{\prime}}_{X}\eta)^{\top}=-\left(\overline{D}_{X}\eta+S(X,\eta)\right)^{\top}=A_{\eta}X-\eta(\rho)X,

(2.12)

where superscript
\top
means projection from
TN
to
TM
.

Let
k
be the codimension of
M
in
N
.
Choose a local orthonormal frame
\{e_{i}\mid i=1,\cdots,n\}
for
TM
and
a local orthonormal frame
\{\eta_{\alpha}\mid\alpha=1,\cdots,k\}
for
\nu M

with respect to metric
g
.
Then
\{e^{\prime}_{i}:=e^{-\rho}e_{i}\mid i=1,\cdots,n\}
is a local orthonormal frame of
TM

and
\{\eta^{\prime}_{\alpha}:=e^{-\rho}\eta_{\alpha}\mid\alpha=1,\cdots,k\}
is a local orthonormal frame for
\nu M

with respect to metric
g^{\prime}=e^{2\rho}g
.

By equation (
2.12
), for any
x\in M
, we have

\displaystyle H^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}g^{\prime}(A^{\prime}_{\eta^{\prime}_{\alpha}}e^{\prime}_{i},\,e^{\prime}_{i})\,\eta^{\prime}_{\alpha}=e^{-2\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}g(A^{\prime}_{\eta_{\alpha}}e_{i},\,e_{i})\,\eta_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=e^{-2\rho}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}g(A_{\eta_{\alpha}}e_{i}-\eta_{\alpha}(\rho)e_{i},\,e_{i})\,\eta_{\alpha}=e^{-2\rho}\left(H(x)-n({\rm grad}(\rho))^{\perp}\right).

This finishes the proof of the lemma.

\Box

Remark 2.7
.

Part (2) of Lemma
2.6
shows that the constancy of
\|H(x)\|
may not be preserved under conformal transformations.
This indicates that we may not have a definition of minimal or isoparametric submanifolds in the ideal boundary of
hyperbolic spaces which does not rely on
the choice of orthonormal basis
\epsilon
of
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
.

The following lemma will be useful in studying the limit of a curve in a hyperbolic space which converges to a point in the ideal boundary.

Lemma 2.8
.

Given a curve
\gamma(t)\in\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
.
Let
\epsilon=\{\epsilon_{1},\cdots,\epsilon_{m+1}\}

be an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
satisfying condition (
2.3
).
Write
\gamma(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}a_{i}(t)\epsilon_{i}
. Then
\gamma(t)
converges to a point
in the ideal boundary
\partial\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
as
t\rightarrow T
for some
T
if and only if

a_{m+1}(t)\rightarrow\infty
and
\frac{\gamma(t)}{a_{m+1}(t)}
converges to a point in

\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
as
t\rightarrow T
. In this case,

\lim_{t\rightarrow T}\,\,\Psi_{\epsilon,r}(\gamma(t))\,\,=\,\,\lim_{t\rightarrow T}\,\,\frac{1}{a_{m+1}(t)}(a_{1}(t),\cdots,a_{m}(t))\in S^{m-1}.

(2.13)

Proof
:
First observe that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_{i}(t)^{2}=a_{m+1}(t)^{2}-r
since
\gamma(t)\in\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
. Hence

\|\Psi_{\epsilon,r}(\gamma(t))\|^{2}=\frac{a_{m+1}(t)-\sqrt{r}}{a_{m+1}(t)+\sqrt{r}},

and
\|\Psi_{\epsilon,r}(\gamma(t))\|\rightarrow 1
if and only if

a_{m+1}(t)\rightarrow\infty
.

Since
\Psi_{\epsilon,r}:\overline{\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)}\longrightarrow\overline{\mathbb{B}^{m}}

is a homeomorphism,
\gamma(t)
converges to a point
on
\partial\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
if and only if
\Psi_{\epsilon,r}(\gamma(t))
converges to a point
on
S^{m-1}
. This implies
a_{m+1}(t)\rightarrow\infty
and
equation (
2.13
) holds since

\lim_{t\rightarrow T}\,\,\frac{a_{m+1}(t)}{a_{m+1}(t)+\sqrt{r}}=1
.
Moreover, existence for the limit on the right hand side of equation (
2.13
)
is equivalent to the existence of

\lim_{t\rightarrow T}\frac{\gamma(t)}{a_{m+1}(t)}=\sum_{i=1}^{m+1}\lim_{t\rightarrow T}\frac{a_{i}(t)}{a_{m+1}(t)}\epsilon_{i},

which is a point in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
if exists.
The lemma is thus proved.

\Box

3
MCF for isoparametric submanifolds in Lorentz spaces

Note that in Theorem
2.2
, the ambient Lorentz space
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
splits as a product
of
V
and
V^{\perp}
, but the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
does not split as a product of two Riemannian
manifolds. Moreover,
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
is a linear space while
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
is not linear.
Therefore it is convenient to study MCF in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
instead of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
.
For a submanifold
M\subset\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)\subset\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
, we call MCF of
M
in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
(respectively
in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
) the
hyperbolic MCF
(respectively
Lorentzian MCF
) of
M
.
In this section, we will study relations between hyperbolic MCF and Lorentzian MCF as well as properties of Lorentzian
MCF for isoparametric submanifolds.

We first recall a basic fact in semi-Riemannian geometry:
Assume
X
is a semi-Riemannian manifold and
M,N
are Riemannian submanifolds in
X

with
M\subset N
.
Then the mean curvature vector field of
M
as a submanifold in
N
is just the orthogonal projection
of the mean curvature vector field of
M
as a submanifold in
X
to the tangent spaces of
N
.
In particular, we have

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume
M
is an
n
-dimensional submanifold of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)\subset\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
. Let
H(x)
and
H^{L}(x)
be the mean curvature vectors of
M
in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
and
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
respectively at
x\in M
. Then

\displaystyle H^{L}(x)=H(x)+\frac{n}{r}x.

Proof
:
Let
T_{x}\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
and
\nu_{x}\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
be the tangent and normal spaces of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
at
x
respectively.
Since the orthogonal projection of
H^{L}(x)
to
T_{x}\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
is
H(x)
, we only need to show that the orthogonal
projection of
H^{L}(x)
to
\nu_{x}\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
is
\frac{n}{r}x
.

Note that
\nu_{x}\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
is spanned by

\eta(x):=\frac{x}{\sqrt{r}}
.
Let
\nabla^{L}
be the Levi-Civita connection on
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
.
Then
\nabla^{L}_{v}\eta=\frac{v}{\sqrt{r}}
for any tangent vector
v
.
Let
\{e_{1},\cdots,e_{n}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
T_{x}M
and
A^{L}
the shape operator
of
M
as a submanifold in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
. Then

\displaystyle\left<H^{L}(x),\eta(x)\right>

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left<A^{L}_{\eta(x)}e_{i},e_{i}\right>=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left<\nabla^{L}_{e_{i}}\eta,e_{i}\right>=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\left<e_{i},e_{i}\right>=-\frac{n}{\sqrt{r}}.

Since
\left<\eta(x),\eta(x)\right>=-1
, the orthogonal projection of
H^{L}(x)
to

\nu_{x}\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
is

\displaystyle-\left<H^{L}(x),\eta(x)\right>\eta(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{r}x.

The lemma is thus proved.
\Box

The relation between hyperbolic MCF and Lorentzian MCF is given by the following:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
f(\cdot,t)
and
F(\cdot,t)
be respectively the hyperbolic MCF and Lorentzian MCF of an
n
-dimensional submanifold

M
in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)\subset\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
. Then for any
x\in M
,

\displaystyle F(x,t)=\sqrt{1+\frac{2nt}{r}}f(x,\frac{r}{2n}\ln(1+\frac{2nt}{r}))

(3.1)

if both sides of this equation are well defined.

Proof
:
Let
s(t)=\frac{r}{2n}\ln(1+\frac{2nt}{r})
. The function on the right hand side of equation (
3.1
)
can be written as

\displaystyle G(x,t)=\sqrt{1+\frac{2nt}{r}}f(x,s(t)).

Let
H(\cdot,s)
be the mean curvature vector field of
f(\cdot,s)
in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
. Then

\frac{\partial}{\partial s}f(x,s)=H(x,s)
since
f(x,s)
satisfies the hyperbolic MCF equation.
By the chain rule, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\frac{2nt}{r}}}\left\{H(x,s(t))+\frac{n}{r}f(x,s(t))\right\},

which is exactly the mean curvature vector of
G(\cdot,t)
as a submanifold in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
at point
G(x,t)
by Lemma
3.1
. Moreover,
G(x,0)=f(x,0)=F(x,0)
. By the uniqueness of the solution to MCF equation,
we have
G(x,t)=F(x,t)
. This finishes the proof of the lemma.

\Box

An immediate consequence of this lemma is the following

Corollary 3.3
.

If
f(.,t)
exists over an interval
(-\infty,T)
for some
T\geq 0
, then
F(.,t)
exists for all

t\in(-\frac{r}{2n},\frac{r}{2n}(e^{\frac{2nT}{r}}-1))
.
On the other hand, if
F(.,t)
exists over an interval
(-\frac{r}{2n},T^{\prime})
for some
T^{\prime}\geq 0
, then

f(.,t)
exists for
t\in(-\infty,\frac{r}{2n}\ln(1+\frac{2nT^{\prime}}{r}))
and it is given by

\displaystyle f(x,t)=e^{-\frac{nt}{r}}F(x,\frac{r}{2n}(e^{\frac{2nt}{r}}-1)).

(3.2)

Remark 3.4
.

Since the image of
f(\cdot,t)
always lies in the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
,
for equations (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) to hold, the image of
F(\cdot,t)
must also lie in a
hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{m}(d)
for some
d<0
. In particular, it must lie in the time cone

\displaystyle\mathcal{TC}:=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{m,1}\mid\left<v,v\right><0\}.

(3.3)

Moreover, it is possible that
F(\cdot,t)
may exist for
t\leq-\frac{r}{2n}
. In this case,
there is no hyperbolic MCF corresponds to
F(\cdot,t)
for
t\leq-\frac{r}{2n}
.
For example,
let
P=\{(x_{1},x_{2},2)\mid x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2,1}
and
M=P\bigcap\mathbb{H}^{2}(-1)
.
Then
P
is a plane with induced Euclidean metric and
M
is a circle in
P
.
The Lorentzian MCF
F(\cdot,t)
of
M
exists for all
t\in(-\infty,T)
for some
T>0
, and it gives
a foliation of
P\setminus\{(0,0,2)\}
by circles centered at
(0,0,2)
.
But only those circles inside the time cone
\mathcal{TC}
correspond to
hyperbolic MCF
f(\cdot,t)
through equation (
3.1
). The circles outside the time cone
do not correspond to
f(\cdot,t)
for any
t
. Note that for this example,

f(\cdot,t)
also exists for all
t\in(-\infty,T^{\prime})
for some
T^{\prime}>0

and the image of
f(\cdot,t)
is also a circle for each
t

(possibly lies in different plane).

Since minimal submanifolds do not move under MCF, another immediate consequence of Lemma
3.2
is the following:

Corollary 3.5
.

If
M
is an
n
-dimensional minimal submanifold in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
with inclusion map
f_{0}
,
then MCF of
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
is given by

\displaystyle F(x,t)=\sqrt{1+\frac{2nt}{r}}f_{0}(x),\,\,\,{\rm for}\,\,\,t\in(-\frac{r}{2n},\,\infty),x\in M.

Remark 3.6
.

As observed in
[
13
]
, the Euclidean MCF of minimal submanifolds in the sphere always have ancient solutions.
But for minimal submanifolds in hyperbolic spaces, their Lorentzian MCF may not have ancient solutions, as demonstrated in the above corollary.

Lemma
3.2
allows us to study hyperbolic MCF via the study of Lorentzian MCF.
In particular, we can use this relation to obtain the following

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
M
be an isoparametric submanifold in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)\subset\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
,
F(\cdot,t)
and
f(\cdot,t)

the Lorentzian and hyperbolic MCF of
M
respectively.
Then
F(\cdot,t)
and
f(\cdot,t)
are isoparametric whenever they exist.

Proof
:
This proposition is the Lorentzian analog of Proposition 2.1 in
[
12
]
.
If
M
is an isoparametric submanifold in a hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
, it is also isoparametric
in Lorentz space
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
by Lemma
2.1
. As proved in
[
19
]
, such submanifolds have
the structure of curvature distributions and curvature normals similar to those of isoparametric submanifolds
in Euclidean spaces obtained in
[
16
]
. To show that
F(\cdot,t)
is an isoparametric submanifold parallel
to
M
, we can use
the same proof as that of Proposition 2.1 in
[
12
]
with possibly a small modification for the sign due to the fact that
\left<x,x\right><0
for
x\in\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
.
By Lemma
3.2
,
f(\cdot,t)
is also isoparametric.

\Box

Remark 3.8
.

The proof of Theorem 2.2 in
[
12
]
also works for Lorentzian MCF
F(\cdot,t)
of an
n
-dimensional isoparametric
submanifold
M
in a hyperbolic space. Hence for any
x\in M
, we have

\displaystyle\left<F(x,t),F(x,t)\right>=\left<x,x\right>-2nt

(3.4)

whenever
F(x,t)
exists. This implies that for
F(x,t)
staying in the time cone
\mathcal{TC}
,
t
can not be smaller than
\frac{\left<x,x\right>}{2n}
. If
t\leq\frac{\left<x,x\right>}{2n}
, even if
F(\cdot,t)
exists, it does not correspond to a hyperbolic MCF via equation (
3.1
).

4
Proof of Theorem
1.1

In this section, we give a proof for Theorem
1.1
based on Theorem
2.2
and results for MCF
of isoparametric submanifolds in Euclidean spaces and spheres obtained in
[
12
]
and
[
13
]
.
During the proof, we also give a recursive
construction for explicit solutions of MCF of isoparametric submanifolds in hyperbolic spaces.

We first consider the case when
M
is full in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
, i.e.
M
is not contained in any umbilical hypersurface
in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
M
be a full isoparametric submanifold in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)

and
f(\cdot,t)
the solution to hyperbolic MCF of
M
.
Then
f(\cdot,t)
is an ancient solution.
Moreover, if
M\neq\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
,
then there exists a finite
T>0
such that the maximal existence interval for
f(\cdot,t)
is
(-\infty,T)
, and
f(\cdot,t)
converges to a focal submanifold of
M
as
t\rightarrow T
.
Moreover, as
t\rightarrow-\infty
,
f(\cdot,t)
converges to a smooth submanifold
\widetilde{M}
with flat normal bundle in the ideal boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
and
\widetilde{M}
is diffeomorphic to
M
.

Proof
:
Since
M
is full and isoparametric, by Theorem
2.2
, there exists
r\geq 1
and a Lorentzian subspace
V
of
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
with dimension
l+1
, such that

\displaystyle M=\mathbb{H}^{l}(-r)\times M^{\prime},

where
\mathbb{H}^{l}(-r)
is one sheet of the hyperboloid

\{x\in V\mid\left<x,x\right>=-r\}\subset V
and

M^{\prime}
is an isoparametric submanifold in the sphere
S^{m-l-1}(r-1)\subset V^{\perp}
.

Let
F_{1}(\cdot,t),F_{2}(\cdot,t)
be the solutions to MCF of
\mathbb{H}^{l}(-r)
in
V
and MCF of
M^{\prime}
in
V^{\perp}
respectively. Since
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
splits as the product
V\times V^{\perp}
, the MCF of
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}

is given by

\displaystyle F((x,y),t)=(F_{1}(x,t),F_{2}(y,t))

(4.1)

for
x\in\mathbb{H}^{l}(-r)
and
y\in M^{\prime}
.

By Corollary
3.5
,
F_{1}(\cdot,t)
exists for all

t\in(-\frac{r}{2l},\,\infty)
. Since
r\geq 1
and
l\leq n
where
n
is the dimension of
M
,
we have
-\frac{r}{2l}\leq-\frac{1}{2n}
. Hence
F_{1}(\cdot,t)
exists for all
t\in(-\frac{1}{2n},\,\infty)
.

If
M^{\prime}
is just a point, then
M
is contained in a translation of
V
.
Since
M
is full, we must have
V=\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
and
M=\mathbb{H}^{m}(-r)
.
In this case
f(\cdot,t)
exists for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

If
M^{\prime}
is not a point, then by Theorem 1.1 in
[
12
]
and Theorem 1.1 in
[
13
]
,
F_{2}(\cdot,t)
exists for all
t\in(-\infty,T^{\prime})
for some finite
T^{\prime}>0
and collapses to a focal submanifold of
M^{\prime}
when
t
goes to
T^{\prime}
.
Thus,
F(.,t)
exists for
t\in(-\frac{1}{2n},T^{\prime})
, and it collapses to a focal submanifold when
t
goes to
T^{\prime}
. Let
T=\frac{1}{2n}\ln(1+2nT^{\prime})
. Then
by Corollary
3.3
, the MCF
f(\cdot,t)
of
M
in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
exists for
all
t\in(-\infty,T)
, and it collapses to a focal submanifold of
M
when
t
goes to
T
.

Now we consider the limit of
f(\cdot,t)
as
t\rightarrow-\infty
.
Note that
V
is a Lorentz space and
V^{\perp}
is a Euclidean space. We can
choose an orthonormal basis
\{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{l+1}\}
of
V
and
an orthonormal basis
\{\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{m-l}\}
of
V^{\perp}

such that

\left<\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i}\right>=\left<\beta_{j},\beta_{j}\right>=1,\hskip 10.0pt\left<\alpha_{l+1},\alpha_{l+1}\right>=-1,\hskip 10.0pt\left<\alpha_{l+1},(0,\cdots,0,1)\right><0

for
1\leq i\leq l
and
1\leq j\leq m-l
.
Let
\epsilon=\{\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{l},\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{m-l},\alpha_{l+1}\}
.
Then
\epsilon
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
satisfying condition
(
2.3
).
Let
\Psi_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}^{m}
be
the map
\Psi_{\epsilon,1}
defined by equation (
2.4
).

For every

x=\sum_{i=1}^{l+1}x_{i}\alpha_{i}\in V,\hskip 20.0pty=\sum_{j=1}^{m-l}y_{j}\beta_{j}\in V^{\perp},

let

\overline{x}:=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{l}),\hskip 20.0pt\overline{y}=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{m-l}).

Then

\|\overline{x}\|^{2}+\|\overline{y}\|^{2}=x_{l+1}^{2}-1
if
(x,y)\in\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
and

\Psi_{\epsilon}(x,y)=\frac{1}{1+x_{l+1}}(\overline{x},\,\,\overline{y}).

(4.2)

By Corollary
3.5
and equation (1.1) in
[
13
]
, MCF of
\mathbb{H}^{l}(-r)
in
V

and MCF of
M^{\prime}
in
V^{\perp}
can be written as

F_{1}(x,t)=a_{1}(t)x,\hskip 10.0ptF_{2}(y,t)=a_{2}(t)f_{2}(y,q(t))

for
x\in\mathbb{H}^{l}(-r)\subset V
and
y\in M^{\prime}\subset V^{\perp}
,
where

a_{1}(t):=\sqrt{1+\frac{2lt}{r}},\hskip 10.0pta_{2}(t):=\sqrt{1-\frac{2(n-l)}{r-1}t},\hskip 10.0ptq(t):=-\frac{r-1}{2(n-l)}\ln\left(1-\frac{2(n-l)}{r-1}t\right),

and
f_{2}(.,t)
is the MCF of
M^{\prime}
in the sphere
S^{m-l-1}(r-1)\subset V^{\perp}
.
By equations (
3.2
) and (
4.1
), MCF of
M
in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
is given by

\displaystyle f((x,y),t)=e^{-nt}(a_{1}(w(t))x,\,\,a_{2}(w(t))f_{2}(y,q(w(t))))\in V\times V^{\perp},

where

w(t):=\frac{1}{2n}(e^{2nt}-1).

Hence

\Psi_{\epsilon}(f((x,y),t))=b(x,t)\left(\overline{x},\,\,\frac{a_{2}(w(t))}{a_{1}(w(t))}\,\,\overline{f_{2}(y,q(w(t)))}\right),

where

b(x,t):=\frac{e^{-nt}a_{1}(w(t))}{1+e^{-nt}a_{1}(w(t))x_{l+1}}.

Since

\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}w(t)=-\frac{1}{2n},\hskip 10.0pt\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}a_{1}(w(t))=\sqrt{1-\frac{l}{nr}}>0,\hskip 10.0pt\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}a_{2}(w(t))=\sqrt{1+\frac{n-l}{n(r-1)}},

we have

\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}b(x,t)=\frac{1}{x_{l+1}},\hskip 20.0pt\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}\frac{a_{2}(w(t))}{a_{1}(w(t))}=\sqrt{\frac{r}{r-1}},

\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}q(w(t))=q*:=-\frac{r-1}{2(n-l)}\ln\left(1+\frac{n-l}{n(r-1)}\right)\in(-\infty,0).

Since
x\in\mathbb{H}^{l}(-r)
and

By Theorem 1.1 in

which is diffeomorphic to

.
Let

\displaystyle\Phi(x,z):=\frac{1}{x_{l+1}}(\overline{x},\overline{z}),

For any

\Phi_{*}(v,u)=\frac{1}{x_{l+1}}(\overline{v},\overline{u})-\frac{v_{l+1}}{x_{l+1}^{2}}(\overline{x},\overline{z}).

Equip

\displaystyle\|\Phi_{*}(v,u)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x_{l+1}^{2}}(\|\overline{v}\|^{2}+\|\overline{u}\|^{2})+\frac{v_{l+1}^{2}}{x_{l+1}^{4}}(\|\overline{x}\|^{2}+\|\overline{z}\|^{2})-2\frac{v_{l+1}}{x_{l+1}^{3}}(\left<\overline{v},\overline{x}\right>+\left<\overline{u},\overline{z}\right>).

.

Lemma 4.3

Assume

n
-dimensional submanifold of

(respectively

) be the mean curvature vector of
M
in
L(V,u)
(respectively in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
) at

\displaystyle H(x)=H_{1}(x)-n\alpha(\alpha x+\beta\xi),

where

\beta:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left<\xi,\,\xi\right>+a^{2}}},\hskip 10.0pt\,\,\,\,\,\,\alpha:=\beta a,

(4.6)

and
\xi
,
a
are defined before equation (
4.5
).

Proof
:
The proof of this lemma is similar to the proof of Lemma
3.1
. Since the orthogonal projection of

H(x)
to the tangent space
T_{x}L(V,u)
is
H_{1}(x)
, we only need to prove that the orthogonal
projection of
H(x)
to the normal space
\nu_{x}L(V,u)
in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
is

-n\alpha(\alpha x+\beta\xi)
.

It is straightforward to check that
\left<\xi,\,\xi\right>+a^{2}>0
if

is non-empty.
To prove this, we can use Theorem 4.9.1 in
[
14
]
if

and Theorem 4.8.10 in
[
14
]

(i.e. the reversed Cauchy-Schwarz inequality)
if

and
\left<\xi,\xi\right><0
. Hence
\alpha
and
\beta
are well defined.
Moreover it is also known that

\eta(x):=\alpha x+\beta\xi
defines a unit normal vector field of
L(V,u)
in
H^{m}(-1)

(see, for example, equation (3.1) in
[
3
]
and Example 3 in Chapter 2.1 of
[
21

Let

be the shape operator of

,

and

the Levi-Civita connections on

for any tangent vector

since
\xi
is constant.
Choose an orthonormal basis
\{e_{1},\cdots,e_{n}\}
of

. We have

\displaystyle-\left<H(x),\eta(x)\right>

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left<A_{\eta(x)}e_{i},e_{i}\right>=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left<\nabla_{e_{i}}\eta,e_{i}\right>=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left<\nabla^{L}_{e_{i}}\eta,e_{i}\right>=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left<\alpha e_{i},e_{i}\right>=n\alpha.

, the orthogonal projection of

to
\nu_{x}L(V,u)
is

\left<H(x),\eta(x)\right>\eta(x)=-n\alpha(\alpha x+\beta\xi).

Lemma 4.5
.

Minimal isoparametric submanifolds in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
must be totally geodesic.

Proof
:
Let
M
be an
n
-dimensional minimal isoparametric submanifold in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
.

If
M
is full, then by Theorem
2.2
,
there exists
r\geq 1
and a Lorentzian subspace
V
of
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
with dimension
l+1
, such that

\displaystyle M=\mathbb{H}^{l}(-r)\times M^{\prime},

where
\mathbb{H}^{l}(-r)
is one sheet of the hyperboloid

\{y\in V\mid\left<y,y\right>=-r\}\subset V
and

M^{\prime}
is an isoparametric submanifold in the sphere

.
By Lemma
3.1
, the mean curvature vector of
\mathbb{H}^{l}(-r)
in
V
at

\frac{l}{r}x
.
Let

be the mean curvature vector of

in
V^{\perp}
at
y\in M^{\prime}
.
The the mean curvature vector of
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}=V\times V^{\perp}
at
(x,y)
is given by

(\frac{l}{r}x,H^{\prime}(y))
.
By Lemma
3.1
, the mean curvature vector of

in
\mathbb{H}^{l}(-1)
is

\displaystyle H(x,y)

\displaystyle=(\frac{l}{r}x,H^{\prime}(y))-n(x,y)=((\frac{l}{r}-n)x,H^{\prime}(y)-ny).

Since

and

, we have
\frac{l}{r}-n\leq 0
with equality if and only

and

.
Hence if

, we must have

and

, which in turn implies that

is a point and

is a totally geodesic submanifold of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
.

We then prove the lemma by induction on the codimension

of

If

, then

is totally geodesic in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
since they are the same space.

Assume the lemma holds for minimal isoparametric submanifolds with codimension less than
k
.
We want to show any minimal isoparametric submanifold
M
in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
with codimension

We have proved that
M
is indeed totally geodesic if it is full. So we can assume that
M
is not full.
In this case
M
must be an isoparametric submanifold in some totally umbilical hypersurface

with
V+u
given by equation (
4.5
).

For
x\in M
, we denote

and

the mean curvature vectors of
M
in
L(V,u)
and
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)

respectively.
Then by Lemma
4.3
,

where

and

at

. By assumption,

since

in minimal in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
. Hence we must have

and
\alpha=0
(we can assume

is a point, which is automatically totally

\alpha=0

). Hence by Remark

,

.

Lemma 4.6
.

For any
y=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{m+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
, let
\overline{y}=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{m})
.
Define a map
\varphi:L(V,u)\longrightarrow S^{m-1}
by

\varphi(y):=\frac{\overline{y}+c\,\overline{\xi}}{y_{m+1}+c\,\xi_{m+1}},

where
c:=\frac{\beta}{\alpha+1}
and
(\overline{\xi},\xi_{m+1})=\xi
. Then
\varphi
is a conformal map.

Proof
:
We first check
\varphi
is well defined and
\varphi(y)\in S^{m-1}
if
y\in L(V,u)
. If fact,
y\in L(V,u)
implies that

\|\overline{y}\|^{2}=y_{m+1}^{2}-1,\hskip 10.0pt<\overline{y},\overline{\xi}>=a+y_{m+1}\xi_{m+1},\hskip 10.0pt\|\overline{\xi}\|^{2}=\xi_{m+1}^{2}+<\xi,\xi>.

Hence

\|\overline{y}+c\,\overline{\xi}\|^{2}=(y_{m+1}+c\xi_{m+1})^{2}-1+2ca+c^{2}<\xi,\xi>.

Using definition of
\alpha,\beta,c
, it is straightforward to check
-1+2ca+c^{2}<\xi,\xi>=0
.
So we have

\|\overline{y}+c\,\overline{\xi}\|^{2}=(y_{m+1}+c\xi_{m+1})^{2}.

(4.7)

This implies
y_{m+1}+c\xi_{m+1}\neq 0
since otherwise we would have
y=-c\xi
, which is not possible by Remark
4.4
. Hence
\varphi
is well defined. Moreover, equation (
4.7
) also implies
\|\varphi(y)\|=1

and
\varphi(y)\in S^{m-1}
.

For any
w\in T_{y}L(V,u)\subset\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
, we have

\varphi_{*}(w)=\frac{\overline{w}}{y_{m+1}+c\xi_{m+1}}-\frac{w_{m+1}(\overline{y}+c\overline{\xi})}{(y_{m+1}+c\xi_{m+1})^{2}}.

Since
<w,y>=<w,\xi>=0
, we have

<\overline{w},\overline{y}>=w_{m+1}y_{m+1}
and
<\overline{w},\overline{\xi}>=w_{m+1}\xi_{m+1}
.
Together with equation (
4.7
), we obtain

\|\varphi_{*}(w)\|^{2}=\frac{<w,w>}{(y_{m+1}+c\xi_{m+1})^{2}}.

Hence
\varphi
is a conformal map.

In this case, there exist a linear hyperplane

)

Let

and

Then

and

Define

L(V,u)
is isometric to a sphere.
By Theorem 1.1 in
[
12
]
,
T^{\prime}=\infty
if and only if
M
is minimal in
L(V,u)
,
which also implies that
f_{1}(\cdot,t)
is independent of
t
.
Note that
T^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{2n(\alpha^{2}-1)}
if
T^{\prime}=\infty
.
Since
\lim_{t\rightarrow T^{\prime\prime}}\,\,2nt(1-\alpha^{2})+1=0
,
F(\cdot,t)
converges to a point when
t\rightarrow T^{\prime\prime}
.
Consequently
f(\cdot,t)
also converges to a point (which is also a focal submanifold of
M
) when
t\rightarrow T
.

Part (2) of Theorem
1.1
:
If
T=\infty
, then
T^{\prime\prime}=T^{\prime}=\infty
and
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
.

If
\alpha=1
, then
\left<\xi,\xi\right>=0
. The restriction
of
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
to
V
is degenerate, and

L(V,u)
is isometric to a Euclidean space.
By Theorem 1.1 in
[
12
]
,
T^{\prime}=\infty
if and only if
M
is totally geodesic in
L(V,u)
.
In particular,
M
is flat. This case belongs to part (3) of Theorem
1.1
.

If
0\leq\alpha<1
, then
\left<\xi,\xi\right>>0
. The restriction
of
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
to
V
is non-degenerate with index 1, and

L(V,u)
is isometric to a hyperbolic space with dimension less than
m
.
In part (2) of Theorem
1.1
, we only need to consider the case where
M
is not flat.
In this case, by the induction hypothesis,
f_{1}(\cdot,t)
converges to a totally geodesic
submanifold
N\subset L(V,u)
as
t\rightarrow\infty
. In particular, this means for all
x\in M
,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}f_{1}(x,t)=h(x),

(4.14)

where
h:M\rightarrow L(V,u)
is the embedding of
N
in
L(V,u)
.

By equations (
3.2
) and (
4.9
), we have

\displaystyle f(x,t)

\displaystyle=v_{\alpha}(t)f_{1}(x,s_{\alpha}(w(t)))+\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha^{2}-1}(v_{\alpha}(t)-e^{-nt})\,\xi,

(4.15)

where

w(t):=\frac{1}{2n}(e^{2nt}-1),\hskip 10.0ptv_{\alpha}(t):=\sqrt{(1-e^{-2nt})(1-\alpha^{2})+e^{-2nt}}.

(4.16)

As
t\to\infty
, we have

v_{\alpha}(t)\to\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}},\,\,\,w(t)\to\infty,\,\,\,s_{\alpha}(w(t))\to\infty,\,\,\,f_{1}(x,s_{\alpha}(w(t)))\to h(x).

(4.17)

Consequently, for all
x\in M
, we have

\lim_{t\to\infty}f(x,t)=\tilde{h}(x),

(4.18)

where
\tilde{h}:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
is the map defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{h}(x)=\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\,h(x)-\frac{\alpha\beta}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}}\xi.

(4.19)

By construction,
f(x,t)\in\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
for all
x\in M
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Hence
\tilde{h}(x)\in\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
for all
x\in M
.
Let
\tilde{N}=\tilde{h}(M)\subset\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
. Then
f(\cdot,t)
pointwise converges to
\tilde{N}
.

We now show that
\tilde{N}
is a totally geodesic submanifold in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
.
In fact, since
N
is totally geodesic in
L(V,u)
, the mean curvature vector of
N
in
L(V,u)
is
0
.
Applying Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
4.3
to the inclusion

N\subset L(V,u)\subset\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
,
we can show that for all
x\in M
, the mean curvature vector of
N
in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
at point
h(x)
is equal to

\displaystyle-n\alpha(\alpha h(x)+\beta\xi)+nh(x)\,\,=\,\,\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\,n\tilde{h}(x).

Observe that
\tilde{N}
is just a translation of a multiplication of
N
by a scalar
\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}
.
So the mean curvature vector of
\tilde{N}
in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
at point
\tilde{h}(x)
is equal to
n\tilde{h}(x)
.
By Lemma
3.1
, the mean curvature vector of
\tilde{N}
in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
at point
\tilde{h}(x)

is equal to
0
. Hence
\tilde{N}
is minimal in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
.

Moreover, since
N
is totally geodesic in
L(V,u)
, by Proposition 1.3 in
[
19
]
and Lemma
2.1
,
N
is isoparametric in both
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
and
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
.
Since
\tilde{N}
is just a translation of a scalar multiplication of
N
,

\tilde{N}
is also isoparametric in both
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
and
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
.
Since
\tilde{N}
is also minimal in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
, by Lemma
4.5
,

\tilde{N}
is totally geodesic in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
.
This finishes the proof of part (2) of Theorem
1.1
.

Parts (3) and (4) of Theorem
1.1
concern limits in ideal boundary of hyperbolic spaces.
We first observe that the formula for
f(\cdot,t)
given in equation (
4.15
) holds for all
\alpha\geq 0
,
with the understanding that when
\alpha=1
, we should take limit for
\alpha\rightarrow 1
on the right hand side
of equation (
4.15
). In fact, we can rewrite this formula in the following way

\displaystyle f(x,t)

\displaystyle=v_{\alpha}(t)f_{1}(x,s_{\alpha}(w(t)))+\frac{\alpha\beta(e^{-nt}-e^{nt})}{e^{nt}v_{\alpha}(t)+1}\,\,\xi,

(4.20)

where
w(t)
and
v_{\alpha}(t)
are given by equation (
4.16
),
s_{\alpha}(t)
is given by equation (
4.8
).

Part (3) of Theorem
1.1
:
Assume
T=\infty
and
M
is flat. This only occurs when
0\leq\alpha\leq 1
.

If
\alpha=1
,
as explained in the proof of part (2),
we only need to consider the case where
M
is a totally geodesic submanifold in
L(V,u)
.
In this case
f_{1}(x,t)=x
for all
x\in M
and
t
.
By equation (
4.20
),

f(x,t)=e^{-nt}x+\frac{\beta}{2}(e^{-nt}-e^{nt})\xi.

For
x=(x_{1},\cdots x_{m+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
, we write

x=(\overline{x},x_{m+1})
where
\overline{x}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{m})
.
Let
\psi
be the map from
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
to
\mathbb{B}^{m}
defined by equation (
2.2
).
Then

\psi(f(x,t))=\frac{e^{-nt}\overline{x}+\frac{\beta}{2}(e^{-nt}-e^{nt})\overline{\xi}}{1+e^{-nt}x_{m+1}+\frac{\beta}{2}(e^{-nt}-e^{nt})\xi_{m+1}}.

Hence for all
x\in M
,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\psi(f(x,t))=\frac{\overline{\xi}}{\xi_{m+1}}.

Since
<\xi,\xi>=\|\overline{\xi}\|^{2}-\xi_{m+1}^{2}=0
,
\,\,\frac{\overline{\xi}}{\xi_{m+1}}\in S^{m-1}
.
This shows that
f(\cdot,t)
converges to a point in the ideal boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
.

If
0\leq\alpha<1
,
L(V,u)
is isometric to a hyperbolic space with dimension less than
m
.
If
M
is a flat isoparametric submanifold of
L(V,u)
, then by induction hypothesis,
f_{1}(\cdot,t)

converges to a point on the ideal boundary of
L(V,u)
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.
Since
V
is a Lorentzian subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
,
we can choose an orthonormal basis
\epsilon=\{\epsilon_{1},\cdots,\epsilon_{m+1}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}

satisfying condition (
2.3
) such that
\{\epsilon_{2},\cdots,\epsilon_{m+1}\}
is an orthonormal
basis of
V
.
By Lemma
2.8
, as
t\rightarrow\infty
,
<f_{1}(x,t),\,\epsilon_{m+1}>\rightarrow-\infty
and

\frac{f_{1}(x,t)}{-<f_{1}(x,t),\,\epsilon_{m+1}>}
converges to a point in
V+u

which is independent of
x
.
Since as
t\rightarrow\infty
,

v_{\alpha}(t)\rightarrow\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}},\hskip 20.0pts_{\alpha}(w(t))\rightarrow\infty,\hskip 20.0pt\frac{\alpha\beta(e^{-nt}-e^{nt})}{e^{nt}v_{\alpha}(t)+1}\rightarrow-\frac{\alpha\beta}{\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}},

by equation (
4.20
),

<f(x,t),\epsilon_{m+1}>\rightarrow-\infty
and

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\,\,\frac{f(x,t)}{-<f(x,t),\epsilon_{m+1}>}\,\,=\,\,\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\,\,\frac{f_{1}(x,t)}{-<f_{1}(x,t),\epsilon_{m+1}>}

converges to a point in
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
independent of
x
.
Hence, by Lemma
2.8
,
f(\cdot,t)
converges to a point on the ideal boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
.
This finishes the proof of part (3) of Theorem
1.1
.

Part (4) of Theorem
1.1
:
Now we consider the limit of MCF as
t\rightarrow-\infty
.
We assume
M
is not totally geodesic in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
.

If
\alpha=0
, then
v_{\alpha}(t)=1
,
s_{\alpha}(w(t))=t
, and
f(x,t)=f_{1}(x,t)
. By Remark
4.2
,

L(V,u)
is totally geodesic in
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
and
V+u=V
is a Lorentzian subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
.
We can choose
an orthonormal basis
\epsilon=\{\epsilon_{1},\cdots,\epsilon_{m+1}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{m,1}
satisfying
condition (
2.3
) such that

\epsilon_{1}=\xi
and
\{\epsilon_{2},\cdots,\epsilon_{m+1}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
V
.
Let
\Psi_{\epsilon,1}:\overline{\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)}\longrightarrow\overline{\mathbb{B}^{m}}
be the map defined by
equation (
2.4
). Then
\Psi_{\epsilon,1}
maps
L(V,u)
to the set

\{(0,a_{2},\cdots,a_{m})\mid\sum_{i=2}^{m}a_{i}^{2}<1\}
. In particular, it maps
the ideal boundary of
L(V,u)
to a totally geodesic subsphere in
S^{m-1}
.
By induction hypothesis,
f_{1}(\cdot,t)
converges to a smooth submanifold with flat normal bundle
in the ideal boundary of
L(V,u)
as
t\rightarrow-\infty
.
Hence
f(\cdot,t)
also converges to a smooth submanifold with flat normal bundle
in the ideal boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
. Moreover, the dimension of these limit submanifolds are the same
as the dimension of
M
.

Assume
\alpha>0
.
Define

\displaystyle T_{\alpha}:=\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}s_{\alpha}(w(t))=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2n(1-\alpha^{2})}\ln\alpha^{2},&{\rm if}\,\,\,\alpha\neq 1,\\
-\frac{1}{2n},&{\rm if}\,\,\,\alpha=1,\end{cases}

Then
T_{\alpha}<0
.
Let

z(x,t):=e^{nt}v_{\alpha}(t)f_{1}(x,s_{\alpha}(w(t)))+\frac{\alpha\beta(1-e^{2nt})}{e^{nt}v_{\alpha}(t)+1}\,\,\xi.

Then
f(x,t)=e^{-nt}z(x,t)
.

Note that, as
t\rightarrow-\infty
,
v_{\alpha}(t)\rightarrow\infty
and
e^{nt}v_{\alpha}(t)\rightarrow\alpha
, and

z(x,t)\rightarrow\alpha\left(f_{1}(x,T_{\alpha})+c\,\,\xi\right),

where
c:=\frac{\beta}{\alpha+1}
.
Since
f(x,t)\in\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
, the last coordinate of
z(x,t)
, denoted by
z_{m+1}(x,t)
, must be positive. Together with Remark
4.4
, we have

\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}z_{m+1}(x,t)=\alpha\left(f_{1}(x,T_{\alpha})_{m+1}+c\,\xi_{m+1}\right)\in(0,\infty),

where

f_{1}(x,T_{\alpha})_{m+1}
is the last coordinate of
f_{1}(x,T_{\alpha})
. Hence
the last coordinate of
f(x,t)
goes to
\infty
as
t\rightarrow-\infty
. By Lemma
2.8
,

\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}\psi(f(x,t))=\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}\frac{\overline{z(x,t)}}{z_{m+1}(x,t)}=\frac{\overline{f_{1}(x,T_{\alpha})}+c\overline{\xi}}{f_{1}(x,T_{\alpha})_{m+1}+c\xi_{m+1}}\in S^{m-1},

where
\overline{z(x,t)}
and
\overline{f_{1}(x,T_{\alpha})}
are vectors whose coordinates are
the first
m
coordinates of
z(x,t)
and
f_{1}(x,T_{\alpha})
respectively.
Hence
\psi(f(\cdot,t))
converges to
\widetilde{M}:=\{\varphi(f_{1}(x,T_{\alpha}))\mid x\in M\}

where
\varphi:L(V,u)\longrightarrow S^{m-1}
is a conformal diffeomorphism from

L(V,u)
to an open submanifold of
S^{m-1}
defined in Lemma
4.6
.
Note that
f_{1}(\cdot,T_{\alpha})
is an isoparametric submanifold in
L(V,u)
.
By Lemma
2.6
,
\widetilde{M}
is a smooth submanifold with flat normal bundle
in the ideal boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{m}(-1)
. Moreover dimension of
\widetilde{M}

is equal to dimension of
f_{1}(\cdot,T_{\alpha})
, which is also equal to dimension of
M
.
The proof of Theorem
1.1
is thus completed.

\Box

.

Since MCF flow of codimension
0

4.9
), Corollary
3.3
,

[
12

to obtain explicit solutions for MCF of all isoparametric submanifolds in hyperbolic spaces in a recursive manner.
