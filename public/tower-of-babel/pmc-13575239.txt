Causal deconfounding for robust link prediction in multimodal knowledge networks
Authors: Binghong Li, Chuxin Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLink prediction in multimodal knowledge networks jointly exploits topology, text, and images, but visual backgrounds may introduce spurious correlations that reduce robustness under distribution shift. We propose MKGC-CSR, a causal deconfounding framework that models visual context as a confounder and approximates backdoor adjustment through a visual concept dictionary and stratified causal attention. The dictionary discretizes the continuous visual context space into prototypes, while the attention module aggregates prototype-conditioned representations using empirical priors. Experiments on FB15k-237-IMG, WN18-IMG, DB15K, and MKG-W show that MKGC-CSR achieves competitive clean-data performance, including an MRR of 0.405 on FB15k-237-IMG. Its margins over the strongest recent baselines are modest (0.003–0.006 MRR) but statistically significant across five random seeds, with limited overhead (+ 2.3 ms inference latency and + 3M parameters). The main benefit lies in robustness: under Gaussian noise, occlusion, background replacement, illumination change, and style shift, MKGC-CSR exhibits consistently smaller performance degradation than clean-trained baselines, and remains more robust than noise-augmented baselines under Gaussian corruption. Sensitivity analysis further shows that the gains are mainly attributable to background-dominated prototypes, supporting the proposed visual-confounder assumption within its stated limits.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Knowledge graphs (KGs) represent factual knowledge as structured networks of relational triplets (h, r, t), where h and t denote head and tail entities, and r denotes their semantic relation. From a network science perspective, KGs can be viewed as large-scale heterogeneous directed networks with rich topological properties, including degree distributions, motifs, and community organization1,2. Traditional KGs such as Freebase and DBpedia rely primarily on symbolic representations and support numerous downstream applications, including semantic search, question answering, and recommendation systems3,4.

However, human cognition is inherently multimodal. To bridge this gap, researchers have developed multimodal knowledge graphs (MKGs) that associate network nodes with images and textual descriptions, thereby enriching network representations beyond pure symbols5,6. These multimodal knowledge networks exhibit complex topological characteristics, such as scale-free degree distributions, hierarchical community structures, and heterogeneous relation patterns, making them natural objects of study within the network science paradigm.

Despite their richer information content, multimodal knowledge networks remain inherently incomplete because of their massive scale and continuous evolution. Link prediction aims to leverage both network topology and multimodal node attributes to infer missing edges. For example, when predicting , visual style features may provide discriminative cues that topological patterns alone cannot capture. This problem is closely related to fundamental questions in network science concerning network inference, reconstruction, and the role of node attributes in shaping connectivity7.

However, incorporating the visual modality introduces a critical challenge: visual bias. Deep learning models are typically trained on statistical correlations8 and are therefore prone to capturing spurious associations in the data. A well-known example is the “wolf–snow” problem: if training images of wolves predominantly contain snowy backgrounds, a model may incorrectly treat snow as a discriminative feature for wolf identification. As a result, when wolves appear on grass, the model may fail substantially.

The root cause lies in the independent and identically distributed (IID) assumption underlying most existing methods9. These approaches implicitly assume that test data follow the same distribution as training data. In practice, however, visual conditions—including lighting, viewpoint, background clutter, and occlusion—may vary considerably, creating out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios. Under such conditions, models that rely on statistical co-occurrence often become fragile. This motivates the introduction of causal inference into multimodal knowledge network link prediction: causal relationships tend to remain invariant across environments, whereas statistical correlations may change with visual context8.

Recent studies in computer vision and natural language processing have shown that causal reasoning can improve robustness. Methods such as CF-VQA and deconfounded image captioning use causal graphs to reduce language priors and visual bias10,11. In scene graph generation, causal inference has also been used to alleviate long-tail relation bias12. Nevertheless, the systematic treatment of visual confounders in multimodal knowledge network link prediction remains largely unexplored.

To address this gap, we propose MKGC-CSR (Multimodal Knowledge Graph Completion with Causal Semantic Reasoning), a causal deconfounding framework grounded in Pearl’s Structural Causal Model (SCM)8. Our key insight is that visual context acts as a confounder: it simultaneously affects node representation learning and prediction outcomes through a backdoor path. By performing an approximate backdoor adjustment, the model can reduce the influence of this spurious path and learn predictions that are less sensitive to incidental visual contexts.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows. First, we construct an SCM for multimodal knowledge network link prediction, explicitly identifying the causal graph structure in which visual context serves as a confounder, and derive an (approximate) backdoor adjustment formulation suitable for multimodal network embeddings. Second, we propose the MKGC-CSR framework, which includes a clustering-based Visual Concept Dictionary to discretize the continuous visual context space into representative prototypes, and a Stratified Causal Attention mechanism to aggregate over visual concepts according to their empirical priors. Third, we conduct extensive experiments on four benchmarks (FB15k-237-IMG, WN18-IMG, DB15K, and MKG-W), demonstrating competitive performance on standard link prediction metrics with statistical significance testing over multiple random seeds, together with comprehensive ablation, robustness, prototype-design, and relation-type analyses, as well as an empirical verification that the learned visual prototypes correspond to background contexts. Fourth, we provide a computational efficiency analysis showing that the causal intervention module introduces only marginal overhead (+2.3 ms latency and +3M parameters).

Related work

Knowledge network embedding

Knowledge network embedding methods map nodes and edges into low-dimensional vector spaces to compute triplet plausibility scores. Translational models, represented by TransE, assume h + r ≈ t and are simple and effective, but they often struggle with complex relation patterns such as 1-to-N and N-to-1 mappings13. Extensions such as TransH and TransR introduce relation-specific projection spaces to improve the modeling of diverse relation types14,15. RotatE models relations as rotations in complex space, effectively capturing symmetric, antisymmetric, inversion, and composition patterns, and remains a strong unimodal baseline16.

Semantic matching models such as DistMult and ComplEx employ bilinear scoring functions. DistMult is limited to symmetric relations, whereas ComplEx handles asymmetry through complex-valued embeddings17,18. ConvE further uses two-dimensional convolutional neural networks to capture local interactions between node and relation embeddings19. Although these unimodal methods perform well on standard benchmarks such as FB15k-237, they cannot leverage external multimodal information, which limits their effectiveness for long-tail or structurally sparse nodes in the network20.

Multimodal knowledge network link prediction

Multimodal methods incorporate images and text to enhance node representations in knowledge networks. Early work such as IKRL extended TransE by introducing separate energy functions for structural and image representations5. More recently, attention-based fusion approaches have become prominent. MKGformer employs a Transformer architecture with cross-modal attention to dynamically fuse visual and textual features21. LAFA addresses the “modality conflict” issue by proposing a link-aware fusion mechanism that computes relation-specific importance weights for images22.

Recent advances have continued to improve multimodal link prediction. MoSE introduces modality-split ensemble learning, in which different modality combinations are processed by separate sub-models23. NativE tackles link prediction in the wild by handling missing modality scenarios through a relation-guided dual adaptive fusion module24. AdaMF-MAT explores adaptive multimodal fusion with adversarial training to enhance robustness against noisy modalities25. MMRNS proposes multimodal-aware negative sampling to provide more informative training signals26.

Despite these advances, existing methods predominantly follow an associative learning paradigm: they fit all features observed in the training data, including background noise and spurious correlations. As a result, visual bias can accumulate during training, making these models brittle under out-of-distribution (OOD) visual conditions. Our work addresses this limitation through a principled causal framework.

Causal inference in machine learning

Causal inference has been increasingly applied in computer vision and natural language processing to improve robustness and interpretability. In visual question answering, CF-VQA and deconfounded image captioning use causal graphs and counterfactual reasoning to reduce language priors and visual bias10,11. In scene graph generation, causal inference has been used to alleviate long-tail relation bias by adjusting prior effects and improving generalization12,27. In the knowledge network domain, some recent studies have also explored causal intervention for handling open-world noise28.

However, the systematic treatment of visual confounders in multimodal knowledge network link prediction remains limited. While LAFA mitigates modality conflict through link-aware weighting, it does not use formal causal reasoning to explicitly identify and remove confounders22. In contrast, our MKGC-CSR framework addresses this gap by providing a principled causal solution grounded in Pearl’s backdoor adjustment framework8.

Connections to structured reasoning, multimodal bias, and explanation reliability

Beyond the multimodal knowledge-graph literature, our work connects to several broader research threads. First, prototype and clustering-based representation learning has been used to organize latent semantic spaces in weakly supervised visual understanding29. Our Visual Concept Dictionary is conceptually related to such prototype-based representations, but is used here specifically to discretize a visual confounder space for causal adjustment rather than for direct prediction. Second, graph-based reasoning and recommendation studies have shown that relation-aware attention and knowledge-graph reasoning can improve structured semantic modeling30,31. MKGC-CSR differs from these works by focusing on link prediction in multimodal knowledge networks and by explicitly modeling visual context as a confounding variable. Third, recent interpretable multimodal systems and explanation methods emphasize that fused representations and post-hoc explanations may become unreliable when models exploit correlation shortcuts or when explanations are affected by feature correlation bias32–35. This line of work is closely related to our motivation: rather than only explaining a biased predictor after training, we aim to reduce one major source of bias during representation learning through approximate causal adjustment. Fourth, the rapid progress of large language models has motivated LLM-enhanced multimodal and tag-enriched fusion for cross-domain and sequential tasks36,37; while our current model does not depend on LLMs, integrating LLM-based semantic priors into the causal intervention module is a natural extension that we revisit in the Limitations section. Finally, the broader multi-view and multimodal learning literature has developed principled mechanisms for fusing heterogeneous information sources: interpretable deep unfolding architectures embed multi-view priors such as semantic consistency and complementarity directly into the network design38; uncertainty-driven reliable dynamic fusion weighs each view according to the estimated reliability of its features, avoiding the pitfalls of uniform averaging39; and dual self-supervision strategies exploit cluster assignments to refine fused graph representations40. These works underscore that the reliability of cross-modal fusion is itself a first-class modeling target; our framework shares this concern but approaches it from a causal perspective, attributing unreliable fusion to a confounding visual context rather than to view-level uncertainty.

Methods

Causal graph construction for multimodal knowledge networks

We model link prediction in multimodal knowledge networks using a directed acyclic graph (DAG)8,41, shown in Fig. 1. The graph contains five variables: H and T, denoting the learned representations of the head and tail nodes; R, denoting the semantic relation type; V, denoting the visual context associated with the head node (e.g., background, scene, lighting, or photographic style of its images); and Y, denoting the prediction outcome, i.e., the triplet plausibility score.

Structural causal model for link prediction in multimodal knowledge networks. Solid arrows denote desired causal effects (, , ) and the representation-formation edge (). The red dashed arrow is the spurious backdoor path induced by visual shortcuts. Conditioning on (adjusting for) V blocks the backdoor path .

To avoid ambiguity, we emphasize that H denotes the learned entity representation that is consumed by the scoring function, not the abstract, immutable identity of the entity. The abstract identity is exogenous and is not altered by the photographic environment; however, the representation H that a multimodal encoder actually produces is a function of the observed images, and therefore of their visual context V. This is precisely why we draw V → H: the visual environment shapes the encoded representation, not the underlying identity. Under this reading the edge is well defined and does not assert that backgrounds determine what an entity “is”.

In standard multimodal link prediction models, the prediction is based on the observational probability P(Y ∣ H, R, V). An examination of the data generation process reveals three causal paths. First, H → Y and R → Y represent the desired causal effects, since node identity and relation type should determine the target node. Second, V → H indicates that visual content contributes to the learned representation of the node, as explained above. Third, V → Y forms a spurious backdoor path, through which visual context—such as background, lighting, or photographic style—may directly influence the prediction outcome via statistical shortcuts that bypass node identity.

For example, if most training images of Horse contain grass backgrounds, the model may incorrectly associate “green background” with “grassland habitat”. When a horse appears on sand at test time, the model may then favor an incorrect prediction through the path V → Y, even though the underlying node identity remains unchanged.

Backdoor criterion We are interested in the causal effect of the node representation on the outcome, i.e., P(Y ∣ do(H), R). The only backdoor path from H to Y is . The adjustment set (i) blocks this path, since V is a non-collider on it, and (ii) contains no descendant of H, because V is a cause—not an effect—of H. Therefore satisfies Pearl’s backdoor criterion8, and the interventional distribution is identifiable by adjustment over V. We acknowledge that this identification rests on the modeling assumption that the relevant confounding is carried by the visual context and that no unobserved common cause of H and Y remains; we provide empirical support for the visual-confounder assumption in the section “Empirical verification of the visual confounder”, probe the consequences of violating it in the section “Sensitivity analysis of the visual-confounder assumption”, and discuss residual confounding in the Limitations.

Equation (1) can be interpreted as requiring the model to consider the node under different visual environments and to compute a weighted average of the corresponding predictions. In this way, the influence of the currently observed background is reduced, yielding predictions that are less sensitive to incidental visual context and more robust under out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions.

We stress that the implementation introduced in the next section is an approximation of Eq. (1) rather than its exact computation. Two approximations are involved: (i) the continuous, unbounded confounder space is replaced by a finite set of K prototypes obtained by clustering, so the sum over becomes a sum over prototypes; and (ii) the per-context predictive term P(Y∣ H,R,v) is realized by a learned feature transformation rather than re-running inference under each context. Consequently, throughout the paper we use phrases such as “backdoor adjustment”, “causal effect”, and “deconfounded representation” to refer to this principled approximation, and we quantify the gap between the empirical prior and a uniform prior in the ablation study.

Visual concept dictionary construction

The causal intervention module approximates the backdoor adjustment in Eq. (1). Since the visual context space is continuous and effectively unbounded, direct summation over all possible contexts is infeasible. We therefore adopt a dictionary-based approximation strategy.

Specifically, we collect visual features from all training nodes and cluster them into K groups using K-means. In deep visual feature space, common background patterns such as grass, sky, or indoor walls tend to occur frequently and with relatively low variance, whereas object-specific features are often more diverse. As a result, cluster centroids are more likely to capture shared contextual information than fine-grained object details.

Experimental setup

Datasets

We evaluate MKGC-CSR on four public multimodal knowledge network benchmarks20,26,47,48. FB15k-237-IMG is derived from Freebase and contains 14,541 nodes and 237 relation types, with inverse relations removed to increase reasoning difficulty. Each node is associated with 10 images. WN18-IMG is derived from WordNet and contains 40,943 nodes and 18 relation types, mainly involving hierarchical lexical relations such as hypernymy and hyponymy. To address the concern that two benchmarks are insufficient to demonstrate scalability and generality, we additionally evaluate on two additional and more diverse multimodal benchmarks: DB15K, derived from DBpedia with numerical, textual, and visual modalities48, and MKG-W, a Wikidata-based multimodal benchmark with naturally noisy and heterogeneous images26. Baselines

We compare MKGC-CSR with representative methods from two categories. The unimodal baselines include TransE13, RotatE16, and DistMult17. The multimodal baselines include IKRL5, MKGformer21, LAFA22, and MoSE23. To provide a broader comparison with recent literature, we additionally include three strong recent multimodal baselines: MMRNS26, AdaMF-MAT25, and NativE24.

Implementation details

Visual features are 768-dimensional vectors extracted by ViT-B/16, and textual features are 768-dimensional vectors extracted by BERT-base. The structural embedding dimension is set to d = 200, and the visual concept dictionary size is set to K = 64. We use the Adam optimizer49 with a learning rate of and a batch size of 512. Negative sampling uses 256 negative triplets per positive triplet. Training runs for 500 epochs with early stopping (patience = 50). All experiments are conducted on an NVIDIA A40 GPU. We report Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) and Hits@K (K = 1, 3, 10) under the filtered setting. Unless otherwise stated, every reported number is the mean over five runs with different random seeds, and we assess statistical significance of the improvement of MKGC-CSR over the strongest baseline using a paired two-sided t-test across seeds.

Reproducibility and statistical significance

Because several of the margins over recent baselines are modest, we explicitly quantify their reliability. Table 4 reports the mean and standard deviation of MRR over five random seeds on FB15k-237-IMG, together with 95% confidence intervals and the p-value of a paired two-sided t-test comparing MKGC-CSR against each baseline. The standard deviation of MKGC-CSR is small (about 0.002 MRR), and its improvements over LAFA, AdaMF-MAT, and NativE are statistically significant at the 0.01 level, supporting the conclusion that the gains are reproducible rather than artifacts of initialization.

First, we visualize the K=64 centroids with t-SNE (Fig. 3) and observe that high-frequency prototypes (large ) form compact clusters that, upon inspection of their nearest training images, correspond to recurring scenes such as vegetation, sky, indoor walls, and studio backgrounds. Second, two annotators independently labeled each centroid (using a montage of its 20 nearest images) as “context/background-dominated” or “object-dominated”. As summarized in Table 7, 46 of the 64 prototypes (71.9%) were judged background-dominated, with substantial inter-annotator agreement (Cohen’s κ = 0.78); critically, the background-dominated prototypes account for the large majority of the prior mass (), confirming that the empirical prior concentrates on contextual rather than object-specific patterns. Third, we test whether these high-prior background prototypes are associated with prediction errors of the non-deconfounded model. For the w/o causal intervention variant, the tail-prediction error rate is markedly higher for test triplets whose head image is assigned to a top-10 high-frequency (background) cluster (0.42) than for those in low-frequency clusters (0.31). MKGC-CSR substantially narrows this gap (0.34 vs 0.30), which is the behavior expected if the prototypes indeed encode confounding background context that backdoor adjustment removes.

Sensitivity analysis of the visual-confounder assumption

The causal identification in our framework rests on the modeling assumption that the visual context is the principal confounder and that the prototype dictionary adequately represents it. Because this assumption cannot be verified from observational data alone, we complement the annotation-based evidence above with a sensitivity analysis that asks two questions: (i) how does performance changes when the adjustment set deliberately deviates from the assumed confounder, and (ii) how sensitive is the method to misspecification of the prior P(v)?

Three observations follow. First, the background-only variant achieves nearly the same mean MRR as the full model (0.404 vs. 0.405 on clean data), showing that nearly all of the observed benefit is carried by the prototypes annotated as background context; because Table 8 does not report a formal equivalence test, we describe this result as near-identical mean performance rather than statistical indistinguishability. The object-dominated prototypes are largely inert. Second, when the adjustment set no longer captures background context—in the object-only and random-prototype variants—performance reverts to approximately the level of the w/o causal intervention baseline (0.372) rather than falling below it. This is a reassuring failure mode: if the visual-confounder assumption is violated in a new domain, the intervention degrades gracefully to a standard multimodal model instead of actively harming prediction. Third, the shuffled-prior variant retains only 0.001 MRR of the 0.010 improvement of the full model over the uniform-prior variant (0.396 vs. 0.395, compared with 0.405 for the full model), indicating that most of the benefit depends on the correct pairing between prototypes and their empirical frequencies, rather than merely on the presence of non-uniform weights.

Taken together, these analyses do not prove the causal assumption—no observational analysis can—but they bound the consequences of its violation: the gains are specifically attributable to background-context prototypes, and misspecification of either the adjustment set or the prior degrades the model toward, not below, its non-causal counterpart. Why causal reasoning matters for knowledge network link prediction

The results above show that causal intervention provides benefits beyond standard noise-handling strategies. The ablation study in Table 6 indicates that even when all multimodal information is retained, removing causal intervention leads to a clear performance drop from 0.405 to 0.372. This gap arises because standard fusion mechanisms cannot explicitly distinguish informative visual cues from spurious background correlations. By formalizing this distinction through an SCM and implementing backdoor adjustment, MKGC-CSR provides a principled mechanism for separating causal signal from visual confounding.

It is also useful to contrast our approach with heuristic alternatives. LAFA mitigates modality conflict by learning relation-specific image weights, but these weights are still estimated from observational statistics and may capture spurious correlations22. MoSE reduces the impact of noisy modalities by processing different modality combinations through separate sub-models, but it does not explicitly model or remove confounders23. In contrast, our method directly targets the bias-generating mechanism through causal intervention, which helps explain its consistent empirical advantage.

Limitations

Despite the promising results, the proposed framework has several limitations. First, the current model focuses only on visual confounders, whereas textual descriptions may also contain biases that should be jointly deconfounded. Second, our deconfounding is an approximation of exact backdoor adjustment: the continuous confounder space is discretized into K prototypes and the per-context term is realized by a learned transformation, so a gap between the approximation and the ideal interventional quantity necessarily remains. Third, identification relies on the assumption that visual context is the principal confounder and that no unobserved common cause of the entity representation and the outcome remains; although our annotation and association analyses support the visual-confounder assumption, and the sensitivity analysis shows that violations of the assumption degrade the model gracefully toward—rather than below—its non-deconfounded counterpart, neither analysis can rule out all residual confounding, and the causal graph should be regarded as a working model rather than a verified ground truth. In particular, the sensitivity analysis indicates that essentially all of the benefit is carried by background-dominated prototypes, so in domains where visual bias is not background-driven the intervention should be expected to yield little or no gain. Fourth, while we find that K ∈ [32,64] provides stable performance, the optimal dictionary size may vary across datasets with different levels of visual diversity, and developing adaptive strategies for automatically selecting K would improve generalizability. Fifth, the improvements over the strongest recent baselines, while statistically significant across seeds, are modest in absolute terms (0.003–0.006 MRR) and are accompanied by a non-negligible computational overhead relative to the structure-only baseline and a roughly 9% increase in inference latency over MKGformer; for applications in which only clean-data accuracy matters, this trade-off may not favor our method, and the qualitative case study is illustrative rather than a quantitative guarantee. Finally, our benchmarks, although now extended to four datasets, still consist largely of curated images; evaluation on real-world multimodal knowledge networks with naturally occurring distribution shifts would provide a more stringent test, and integrating causal deconfounding with large language model based methods is a promising direction for future work.

Conclusion

This paper addresses the problem of visual bias in link prediction for multimodal knowledge networks by proposing MKGC-CSR, a causal deconfounding framework grounded in Pearl’s Structural Causal Model. By constructing a visual concept dictionary and performing approximate backdoor adjustment through stratified causal attention, the proposed method aims to disentangle node-intrinsic semantics from spurious background correlations. Experiments on four benchmarks (FB15k-237-IMG, WN18-IMG, DB15K, and MKG-W) demonstrate competitive link prediction performance; however, we emphasize that the accuracy improvements over the strongest recent baselines are small in absolute terms (0.003–0.006 MRR), and although they are statistically significant across five random seeds, they are obtained at the cost of an additional +2.3 ms inference latency and +3M parameters relative to MKGformer. On clean-data accuracy alone, this trade-off between practical benefit and computational cost is therefore marginal, and we do not claim that MKGC-CSR should displace strong existing methods in settings where test-time visual conditions match those of training. The case for the proposed framework rests instead on its behavior under distribution shift: across Gaussian noise and the more targeted corruptions of occlusion, background replacement, illumination change, and style shift, MKGC-CSR is substantially more stable than clean-trained multimodal baselines. Under Gaussian noise, it also remains more stable than LAFA and NativE retrained with matched Gaussian-noise augmentation; for example, it incurs only a 3.2% relative MRR drop on FB15k-237-IMG, compared with 12.3% for clean-trained MKGformer and 9.3% for clean-trained LAFA. A semantic annotation of the visual prototypes, together with a sensitivity analysis over the adjustment set and the confounder prior, provides empirical support for the visual-confounder assumption while also delineating its limits: the gains are specifically attributable to background-context prototypes, and violations of the assumption degrade the model gracefully toward its non-deconfounded counterpart. Relation-type analysis further indicates that causal deconfounding is particularly effective for complex multi-mapping relations. In future work, we will explore the joint deconfounding of visual and textual modalities and investigate integration with large language models for richer semantic modeling of multimodal knowledge networks.
