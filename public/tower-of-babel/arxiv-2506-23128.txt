Are Large Language Models Capable of Deep Relational Reasoning? Insights from DeepSeek-R1 and Benchmark Comparisons Thanks: This work is supported by the Research Grants Council of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region, China under Grant UGC/FDS24/H25/24.
Chi Chiu So, Yueyue Sun 1 , Jun-Min Wang 2, Siu Pang Yung, Anthony Wai Keung Loh 3 , Chun Pong Chau 4

Abstract
How far are Large Language Models (LLMs) in performing deep relational reasoning? In this paper, we evaluate and compare the reasoning capabilities of three cutting-edge LLMs, namely, DeepSeek-R1, DeepSeek-V3 and GPT-4o, through a suite of carefully designed benchmark tasks in family tree and general graph reasoning. Our experiments reveal that DeepSeek-R1 consistently achieves the highest F1-scores across multiple tasks and problem sizes, demonstrating strong aptitude in logical deduction and relational inference. However, all evaluated models, including DeepSeek-R1, struggle significantly as problem complexity increases, largely due to token length limitations and incomplete output structures. A detailed analysis of DeepSeek-R1’s long Chain-of-Thought responses uncovers its unique planning and verification strategies, but also highlights instances of incoherent or incomplete reasoning, calling attention to the need for deeper scrutiny into LLMs’ internal inference dynamics. We further discuss key directions for future work, including the role of multimodal reasoning and the systematic examination of reasoning failures. Our findings provide both empirical insights and theoretical implications for advancing LLMs’ reasoning abilities, particularly in tasks that demand structured, multi-step logical inference. Our code repository will be publicly available at https://github.com/kelvinhkcs/Deep-Relational-Reasoning .

With the rise of LLMs, increasing attention has been directed toward understanding their reasoning abilities, widely considered a critical bottleneck in the pursuit of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)
[

,

]
. Prior studies have primarily evaluated reasoning performance through benchmarks involving arithmetic reasoning
[

and symbolic reasoning
[
10
,

]
etc. While many LLMs developed in recent years
[
12
,
13
]
have achieved impressive results on these benchmarks, they largely lack explicit deep reasoning mechanisms. Preliminary work has begun to explore the reasoning abilities of more recent LLMs with deep reasoning capabilities, such as DeepSeek-R1, in some reasoning domains, for example, arithmetic reasoning
[
14
]
. However, a comprehensive understanding of their general logical reasoning capacity remains underexplored.

Concurrently, there has been ongoing debate about whether the existing reasoning benchmarks are truly indicative of deep reasoning ability. Critics argue that many of these benchmarks require only shallow or pattern-based reasoning
[
15
]
, making them insufficient for validating the sophisticated reasoning capabilities of modern LLMs. One key area that warrants deeper investigation is relational reasoning, which is the ability to understand and reason about relationships between entities. This faculty is a cornerstone of intelligence, closely tied to logical thinking and problem-solving in unfamiliar contexts
[
16
,
17
]
. Recent research has also suggested that relational reasoning may remain a fundamental weakness of current LLMs
[
18
]
.

Motivated by these gaps, this paper investigates the relational reasoning abilities of LLMs with deep reasoning capabilities, particularly DeepSeek-R1, by comparing their performance against other widely-used LLMs. Our study aims to:

•

Evaluate the relational reasoning performance of state-of-the-art LLMs with deep reasoning capabilities, with a focus on DeepSeek-R1;

•

Explore the limitations of these models in complex relational problems, particularly in scenarios that remain underexamined in existing literature;

•

Derive actionable insights to inform future LLM development and research directions.

To conduct a thorough evaluation, we propose two classes of relational reasoning benchmarks: family tree and general graph reasoning, using a zero-shot prompting setup. Our family tree benchmark involves identifying four distinct family tree relations - sister, aunt, grandson, and paternal great aunt - within large and complex family structures, while our general graph reasoning benchmark include node connectivity and shortest-path problems.

Our experimental findings reveal several key insights:

1.

LLMs with deep reasoning capabilities, such as DeepSeek-R1, significantly outperform prior models without such capabilities in deep relational reasoning problems.

2.

The primary bottleneck in these models lies in the token limit inherent to current LLM architectures, which restricts their capacity to handle large relational structures.

3.

While DeepSeek-R1 demonstrates strong planning, verification, and step-by-step deduction abilities, some intermediate steps are unstructured, suggesting that, despite appearing coherent, the underlying reasoning rationale may not always be fully sound.

This paper offers the following key contributions:

1.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive evaluation of relational reasoning in state-of-the-art LLMs with deep reasoning capabilities, with a particular focus on DeepSeek-R1.

2.

We introduce and apply an experimental pipeline of two representative benchmarks, family tree and general graph reasoning, designed to systematically evaluate LLMs’ relational reasoning capacity in a zero-shot prompting manner.

3.

We demonstrate that DeepSeek-R1, known for its long Chain-of-Thought reasoning enabled by reinforcement learning, fundamentally outperforms other LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o and DeepSeek-V3) in relational reasoning problems.

II
Related Works

II-A

Large Language Models

Large Language Models

Large Language Models (LLMs) refer to generative, pre-trained, Transformer-based AI models. By leveraging massive corpora of data representing human knowledge, along with advanced pre-training and finetuning techniques, LLMs have demonstrated unprecedented, human-like reasoning capabilities and have achieved state-of-the-art performance in problems once considered infeasible, such as machine translation
[
19
]
, music composition
[
20
]
, and more. These models are typically trained using an autoregressive loss function to maximize the likelihood of predicting the next token given the preceding context
[
21
]
.

Multimodal LLMs

Unlike standard LLMs that process only textual input, multimodal LLMs are capable of understanding and generating responses from diverse input types, such as images, audio, and video
[
22
,
23
]
. A well-known example is Contrastive Language–Image Pre-training (CLIP)
[
24
]
. These models typically employ a unified embedding mechanism that enables the joint processing of multiple modalities by embedding and concatenating their token representations into a single sequence.

Logical Reasoning of LLMs

With the rapid evolution of LLMs, an increasing body of research has focused on their logical reasoning abilities, particularly in domains such as arithmetic reasoning
[
8
,
9
]
and symbolic reasoning
[
10
,
11
]
. While earlier LLMs have demonstrated strong performance on such benchmarks, recent studies reveal that models lacking explicit deep reasoning capabilities still struggle with problems requiring structured decision-making, such as the “blocksworld” problem, which involves arranging blocks in a specific order
[
15
]
. Moreover, despite the emergence of more powerful models with purported deep reasoning abilities, comprehensive evaluation of their performance on general logical reasoning problems remains limited and urgently needed.

II-B

Prompting Techniques

Few-shot Prompting

Few-shot prompting involves providing LLMs with a few guiding examples within the prompt to help the model better understand the task and generate more coherent and structured outputs
[
25
,
26
]
. This foundational strategy has inspired several prompt engineering variants, including Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting
[
26
]
, Chain-of-Verification (CoVe) prompting
[
27
]
, and Tree-of-Thought (ToT) prompting
[
28
]
. These approaches aim to introduce intermediate reasoning steps or verification structures that guide the model through more rigorous logical sequences.

Zero-shot Prompting

Zero-shot prompting refers to querying LLMs without providing any explicit problems examples
[
29
,
30
]
. This approach relies solely on the model’s pre-trained knowledge and reasoning capabilities, offering flexibility in unseen problems. However, it often comes with trade-offs in performance and robustness, especially for problems requiring structured, multi-step reasoning.

Multimodal Prompting

Multimodal prompting involves querying multimodal LLMs with inputs spanning multiple modalities - text, images, audio, or video
[
31
]
. This technique enables the model to engage in more holistic forms of reasoning, such as spatial, visual, and geometric reasoning. Recent studies suggest that multimodal prompting enhances reasoning robustness and generalization by mimicking the diverse ways in which humans process and interpret information
[
32
,
23
]
.

III-A

Family Tree Reasoning

The family tree reasoning benchmark consists of problems that require the deduction of complex family relationships based on a set of basic relations. Specifically, a family tree is constructed using four fundamental predicates:

•

IsMother
(x,y)
: True if
y
is the mother of
x
.

•

IsFather
(x,y)
: True if
y
is the father of
x
.

•

IsSon
(x,y)
: True if
y
is the son of
x
.

•

IsDaughter
(x,y)
: True if
y
is the daughter of
x
.

These basic relations are used to infer four higher-order target relations as described below:

1.

HasSister
(x)
:
Determines whether
x
has a sister.
Formally, this is evaluated as:

\exists\>y\not=x,\exists\>z
such that
\texttt{IsDaughter}(z,y)\wedge\texttt{IsDaughter}(z,x)
.

2.

IsGrandson
(x,y)
:
Determines whether
y
is the grandson of
x
.
Formally, this is evaluated as:

\exists\>z
such that
\texttt{IsSon}(z,y)\wedge(\texttt{IsSon}(x,z)\vee\texttt{IsDaughter}(x,z))
.

3.

IsAunt
(x,y)
:
Determines whether
y
is the aunt of
x
.
Formally, this is evaluated as:

\exists\>z
such that
\texttt{IsDaughter}(z,y)\wedge\texttt{IsGrandparent}(x,z)\wedge\neg\texttt{IsMother}(x,y)
,
where
\texttt{IsGrandparent}(x,z)
is evaluated by checking if
\exists\>w
such that

((\texttt{IsSon}(z,w)\wedge\texttt{IsFather}(x,w))\vee(\texttt{IsDaughter}(z,w)\wedge\texttt{IsMother}(x,w)))
.

4.

IsPaternalGreatAunt
(x,y)
:
Determines whether
y
is the paternal great aunt of
x
.
Formally, this is evaluated as:

\exists\>z,w
such that
\texttt{IsGrandfather}(x,z)\wedge\texttt{IsParent}(z,w)\wedge\texttt{IsDaughter}(w,y)
,
where
\texttt{IsGrandfather}(x,z)
holds if
\exists\;v
such that
\texttt{IsFather}(v,z)\wedge(\texttt{IsSon}(v,x)\vee\texttt{IsDaughter}(v,x))
.

Other family relations, for example,
IsUncle
(x,y)
and
IsGrandparent
(x,y)
), can be derived similarly. However, due to logical symmetry and comparable reasoning depth, we adopt the four relations above as representative problems for our family tree reasoning benchmark.

III-B

General Graph Reasoning

The general graph reasoning benchmark involves problems that require inferring high-level structural properties of graphs from a basic relation:

•

HasEdge
(x,y)
: True if there is a directed edge from node
x
to node
y
.

From this base relation, we define two key target problems:

1.

Connectivity
(x,y)
:
Determines whether a path exists from node
x
to node
y
.

2.

Shortest
(x,y)
:
Determines the number of edges on the shortest path from node
x
to node
y
.

While both problems can be solved using classical graph algorithms, for example, Breadth-First Search (BFS) and Dijkstra’s algorithms, our objective is not to test algorithmic knowledge. Instead, we prompt LLMs with natural language descriptions (see Section
IV-B
)) to assess their relational reasoning abilities, rather than their ability to mimic algorithmic behavior.

Other simpler problems in the general graph reasoning domain include:

•

4-Connectivity
(x,y)
: Determines if there is a path from node
x
to node
y
within four edges.

•

2-Outdegree
(x)
: Determines if node
x
has exactly two outgoing edges.

These problems are relatively straightforward, as they only require local neighborhood examination and do not challenge the model’s ability to conduct deep, multi-step reasoning across the entire graph.

Therefore, we focus exclusively on the problems of
Connectivity
(x,y)
and
Shortest
(x,y)
as they necessitate a more sophisticated, global understanding of the graph structure, mirroring the complexity found in our family tree reasoning benchmark problems. This allows us to conduct a more comprehensive and meaningful evaluation of the relational reasoning capabilities of LLMs.

IV
Experiments

This section outlines the LLMs evaluated in our study, followed by our data generation process, our experimental setup and the evaluation metrics adopted.

IV-A

Models

To comprehensively assess deep relational reasoning capabilities, we select DeepSeek-R1 as our primary model due to its demonstrated strengths in complex reasoning and its accessibility for large-scale testing. For meaningful comparative analysis, we also include DeepSeek-V3 and GPT-4o to represent models without deep reasoning capabilities.

IV-A
1
DeepSeek-R1 (671B parameters)

Released in early 2025, DeepSeek-R1 is a state-of-the-art LLM comprising 671 billion parameters. It is widely recognized for its remarkable reasoning performance and cost-efficiency, achieved through large-scale reinforcement learning. Notably, it is the first LLM to integrate several novel techniques, including Mixture of Experts (MoE) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), to simultaneously improve reasoning and computational efficiency. The model is specifically trained to construct long Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning paths, which are vital for solving problems requiring complex logical deduction across extended contexts
[
5
,
37
]
.

IV-A
2
DeepSeek-V3

DeepSeek-V3 is a smaller and more lightweight version of DeepSeek-R1. While it omits full implementations of MoE and long CoT frameworks, it retains core advancements in reinforcement learning and structured reasoning. It serves as a representative model without full deep reasoning capabilities, allowing for fair performance comparisons.

IV-A
3
GPT-4o

GPT-4o, a leading model in the GPT series, offers competitive reasoning capabilities and long-context understanding. However, it does not adopt the explicit long CoT methodology employed in DeepSeek-R1. It is included as a strong benchmark model without a dedicated deep reasoning architecture.

IV-C

Experimental Setup

We generate datasets of size 100 for each of the six benchmark problems across three different values of
n
: 10, 20, and 40. This yields 18 distinct evaluation sets. All generated data are with non-trivial target relations, i.e., the Boolean matrices of target relations are not zero matrices. Each prompt is input to DeepSeek-R1, DeepSeek-V3, and GPT-4o in a zero-shot approach, i.e., without any in-context examples. The models’ JSON responses are parsed, and the output matrices are compared to ground truth for evaluation.

IV-D

Evaluation Metrics

To quantitatively assess model performance, we use F1-score as the primary metric due to its effectiveness in measuring classification accuracy, especially under class imbalance.

•

Binary Classification Tasks
:
For problems such as
HasSister
(x)
,
IsGrandson
(x,y)
,
IsAunt
(x,y)
,
IsPaternalGreatAunt
(x,y)
and
Connectivity
(x,y)
, we compute the standard F1-score:

\text{Standard F1-score}=\frac{2\times\text{Precision}\times\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}.

•

Multiclass Classification Task
:
For
Shortest
(x,y)
, we adopt Macro-averaged F1-score to ensure balanced evaluation across all classes, regardless of their individual frequencies.

•

Special Evaluation Cases
:
To maintain evaluation consistency, we define the following conditions:

1.

If a model fails to return a valid JSON format, an F1-score of 0 is assigned.

2.

If the returned matrix has an incorrect shape (i.e., not
n\times n
), an F1-score of 0 is also assigned.

V
Result Analysis and Discussion

In this section, we analyze the experimental results, examine their implications in depth, and outline future directions. The average F1-scores of the LLMs across six family tree and general graph reasoning benchmark problems, evaluated over 100 runs, are summarized in Table
I
.

V-A

Family Tree Reasoning

Across all values of
n=10
,
20
and
40
, DeepSeek-R1 consistently achieves the highest average F1-scores, except at
n=40
where all three evaluated models fail completely on the problems
IsGrandson
(x,y)
,
IsAunt
(x,y)
and
IsPaternalGreatAunt
(x,y)
, each yielding an F1-score of 0.000. Also, for problems such as
HasSister
(x)
,
IsGrandson
(x,y)
and
IsAunt
(x,y)
, DeepSeek–R1 reaches impressive average F1-scores as high as 0.9 at both
n=10
and
20
, providing strong evidence of its advanced capability in deep relational reasoning.

However, performance of DeepSeek-R1 on
IsPaternalGreatAunt
(x,y)
remains relatively modest even at smaller scales (
n=10
and 20), with F1-scores only slightly above 0.3. This likely reflects the higher logical complexity involved in deducing the paternal great aunt relation compared to other family relations.

Among all problems,
HasSister
(x)
is the least challenging, which explains why all three models achieve their highest scores on this problem (all above 0.5). This further supports the notion that DeepSeek-R1’s performance edge becomes more pronounced as relational complexity increases.

The performances of GPT-4o and DeepSeek-V3 are generally comparable across all problems. While DeepSeek-V3 slightly outperforms GPT-4o in certain cases, the differences are not statistically significant. As both models lack specialized deep reasoning mechanisms, these findings indirectly affirm the superiority of DeepSeek-R1 in family tree reasoning problems.

Taken together, these results highlight DeepSeek-R1 as the most competent LLM for both types of relational reasoning, offering a marked performance advantage over models lacking deep reasoning capabilities.

V-C

In-depth Discussion

Intrinsic Token Limits

All LLMs inherently face token limits dictated by their architecture. This constraint appears to be a key factor behind DeepSeek-R1’s failure at
n=40
across most problems except
HasSister
(x)
. Closer inspection of failed runs reveals instances where the reasoning process is truncated, and the output matrix is either incomplete or malformed. In our evaluation protocol, any such output is assigned an F1-score of 0, which penalizes performance in evaluation.

The same limitation likely contributes to the performance breakdowns of GPT-4o and DeepSeek-V3 at higher
n
values. As prompt length and complexity grow with
n
, these models are unable to complete their reasoning process and reply within the allowed token budget.

Future Directions

Multimodal Reasoning

Our findings reaffirm that deep reasoning capabilities are critical for tackling relational reasoning tasks. However, recent studies have shown that LLMs may exhibit greater proficiency in multimodal reasoning in domains such as time-series analysis than in purely text-based reasoning tasks
[
1
]
. Multimodal reasoning involves processing and integrating information from non-textual sources such as images or videos, enabling spatial, geometric, and visual cognition. Given that humans naturally rely on spatial and visual reasoning for a wide range of everyday tasks
[
32
,
23
]
, an important future direction is to explore whether relational reasoning in LLMs can be further enhanced through multimodal prompts. This raises the question: could visual or diagrammatic representations of relational structures, for example, family trees or graphs, serve as a more effective input modality for enabling and strengthening deep relational reasoning?

Deeper Investigation into Long Chain-of-Thought Processes

As illustrated in our analysis of DeepSee–R1’s reasoning trace, LLMs equipped with deep reasoning capabilities can engage in remarkably thorough and robust inference prior to generating a final answer. However, as noted in the planning phase of the reasoning process, DeepSeek-R1 occasionally constructs its plan midway through reasoning rather than at the outset. This observation raises important concerns regarding the coherence and soundness of the internal reasoning rationale. While the output may appear logically consistent, the underlying reasoning process may, at times, lack proper structure or contain subtle flaws.

This opens up a rich line of inquiry into the internal reasoning dynamics of such models. A promising future direction involves conducting fine-grained analysis of reasoning traces that ultimately lead to incorrect conclusions. By scrutinizing these examples, we may uncover latent error patterns, such as premature assumptions, flawed inferences, or inconsistencies in intermediate steps, that can inform the design of novel training objectives, architectural modifications, or reasoning frameworks. Such efforts could significantly enhance the robustness and interpretability of deep reasoning in LLMs.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we systematically evaluated the deep relational reasoning capabilities of state-of-the-art LLMs through a series of benchmark problems in family tree and general graph reasoning. Among the three models evaluated, DeepSeek-R1, DeepSeek-V3 and GPT-4o, our experimental results consistently demonstrate the superior performance of DeepSeek-R1, especially at smaller problem scales (
n=10
and 20). Its ability to perform accurate and structured logical deductions across multiple layers of relational dependencies confirms its potential for deep reasoning tasks.

However, our analysis also reveals key limitations. All models, including DeepSeek-R1, exhibit performance degradation at larger problem sizes (
n=40
), primarily due to intrinsic token limits and incomplete output generations. A detailed investigation into DeepSeek-R1’s reasoning traces shows that, while its long Chain-of-Thought processes are a key strength, they are not always well-structured or logically sound throughout, raising important questions about the underlying reasoning mechanisms of such models.

Looking ahead, we identify two promising directions for future work. First, the potential of multimodal reasoning, incorporating visual and spatial inputs, may further enhance the relational reasoning capabilities of LLMs. Second, a deeper examination of long Chain-of-Thought responses, especially those that lead to incorrect outcomes, can offer insights into the cognitive patterns of LLMs and guide improvements in both training and inference strategies.

Overall, our study highlights both the promise and current limitations of LLMs in performing deep relational reasoning. It provides an empirical and analytical foundation for future research aimed at building more robust, interpretable, and human-aligned reasoning systems.
