Introduction

Renal involvement occurs in 4% to 23% of patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [1]. Among these, renal AA amyloidosis is a rare but serious complication, arising from chronic systemic inflammation and persistent elevation of serum amyloid A (SAA) levels. Although uncommon, it is associated with significant renal morbidity and poor prognosis [2].

The prevalence of AA amyloidosis in IBD is low, estimated at approximately 0.5% of patients, with Crohn’s disease more frequently implicated than ulcerative colitis. Risk factors include long-standing disease, chronic inflammation, extensive or fistulizing intestinal involvement, and the presence of extra-intestinal manifestations such as arthropathy or skin involvement [2].

The clinical presentation of renal AA amyloidosis is dominated by nephrotic-range proteinuria, observed in approximately 60–80% of cases, mainly due to predominant glomerular involvement. In addition to renal features, patients may develop systemic symptoms, including weight loss, hepatic involvement, and autonomic dysfunction [3].

Despite its clinical relevance, data regarding clinical presentation, management, and outcomes remain limited, particularly in underrepresented populations such as North Africa, where data are scarce.

This study aimed to describe the clinical, biological, and histological features, as well as renal outcomes, of IBD-associated AA amyloidosis in a multicenter cohort.

Results

Patient characteristics

Renal amyloidosis was identified in 17 patients, including 13 from the Nephrology Department of Charles Nicolle Hospital and 4 from the Nephrology Department of Sahloul Hospital, including 14 with CD and 3 with UC. There was a male predominance, with a male-to-female ratio of 4.66, and the median age at diagnosis was 37 years [IQR: 29.5–47, range: 19–68]. Comorbidities included diabetes without degenerative complications in one patient and hypertension in one patient.

IBD characteristics

The median interval between IBD diagnosis and detection of renal involvement was 3 years [IQR: 1–10, range: 0–25]. Renal amyloidosis was the initial presentation of IBD in two patients. Eight patients were experiencing an active IBD flare at the time of amyloidosis diagnosis.

Among CD patients, ten had ileocolonic involvement and four had isolated ileal disease. Two patients had a history of colectomy, performed 22 and 2 years prior to amyloidosis diagnosis.

Complications and extra-intestinal manifestations

Chronic suppurative complications were present in six patients, including perineal fistulas in three and anal margin abscesses in three. Extra-intestinal manifestations were observed in eight patients: spondyloarthritis in four, arthralgias in three, and uveitis in one.

Clinical presentation

Edema was noted in 12 patients, including generalized anasarca in three. Median systolic and diastolic blood pressures were 110 mmHg [IQR: 100–120, range: 90–150] and 60 mmHg [IQR: 60–80, range: 40–90], respectively. Extra-renal manifestations of AA amyloidosis were observed in several patients. Dysautonomia, presenting as orthostatic hypotension, was noted in 3 patients. Hepatic involvement, reflected by hepatomegaly, was identified in 1 patient, while weight loss was reported in 2 patients. Palpable ulnar nerves was observed in 2 patients, and macroglossia was noted in 1 patient.

Laboratory findings

Median serum albumin at diagnosis was 20.1 g/L [IQR: 9.4–23.4], total serum protein 49 g/L [IQR: 39.5–56.5], serum creatinine 98 µmol/L [IQR: 73–444], and eGFR 57.4 mL/min/1.73 m² [IQR: 13.7–111.6]. Renal involvement and biopsy

All patients exhibited significant proteinuria (> 3 g/24 h), NS was observed in 15 patients. Impaired renal function occurred in eight patients, including one patient with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) at presentation.

Diagnosis of amyloidosis was confirmed by minor salivary gland biopsy in 10 patients and renal biopsy in seven patients. Histology revealed amorphous, acellular, weakly basophilic deposits, sometimes striated on standard stains, Congo red positivity, and characteristic green-yellow birefringence under polarized light. Renal outcomes

Median eGFR at one-year follow-up was 16.4 mL/min/1.73 m² [IQR: 7.4–45; range: 3.5–151]. One patient was dialysis-dependent at baseline, and eight patients progressed to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) requiring dialysis over a median time of 19 months [IQR: 7–37; range: 2–108].

Discussion

AA amyloidosis represents a severe and potentially life-threatening complication of IBD, resulting from chronic systemic inflammation and sustained elevation of SAA. In this multicenter cohort, we report the clinical presentation, management, and renal outcomes of 17 patients with IBD-associated AA amyloidosis, highlighting the aggressive renal course of this condition.

In our study, renal involvement was characterized by heavy proteinuria in all patients and nephrotic syndrome in the majority, consistent with previous reports indicating that renal manifestations are the most frequent presentation of AA amyloidosis. Impaired renal function was also common at diagnosis, reflecting the often advanced stage of renal involvement at the time of detection. These findings are in line with prior studies reporting that most patients present with significant proteinuria and progressive renal dysfunction [4].

The median interval between IBD diagnosis and the onset of renal amyloidosis was relatively short in our cohort compared to earlier reports, where longer disease duration has traditionally been described [2]. This may suggest either more aggressive inflammatory disease or delayed recognition of renal involvement. Importantly, amyloidosis was the initial manifestation of IBD in some patients, emphasizing the need for clinicians to consider underlying inflammatory diseases in patients presenting with unexplained nephrotic syndrome.

Extra-renal manifestations were observed in a subset of patients, including dysautonomia, hepatomegaly, and constitutional symptoms [3]. Although less frequent than renal involvement, these features reflect the systemic nature of AA amyloidosis and should prompt a comprehensive clinical evaluation.

Diagnosis was confirmed by minor salivary gland biopsy and renal biopsy. Organ biopsy remains the gold standard, with less invasive methods preferred. Digestive endoscopic biopsies are particularly valuable in IBD: one study in 408 CD patients reported amyloid deposits in 83% of gastric, 100% of duodenal, and 67% of colonic/rectal biopsies [5].

Management strategies in our cohort were heterogeneous. Some patients continued their prior IBD therapy, while others received additional treatments such as colchicine, corticosteroids, or anti–TNF-α agents. This heterogeneity likely reflects changes in IBD management over the long inclusion period, as well as variability in disease severity and therapeutic availability. In addition, concerns regarding the risk–benefit balance of initiating or intensifying immunosuppressive therapy in patients with advanced renal impairment may have influenced therapeutic decisions.

Recent advances in the management of AA amyloidosis have focused on more effective control of systemic inflammation. In particular, biologic therapies such as anti–TNF-α agents and interleukin-6 (IL-6) inhibitors, including tocilizumab, have shown promising results in reducing SAA levels and stabilizing renal function [1,6,7]. However, data specifically addressing IBD-associated AA amyloidosis remain limited, and the reversibility of established renal damage is still uncertain. These emerging therapies highlight the importance of early and aggressive control of intestinal inflammation to prevent amyloid deposition.

In our study, despite treatment, renal outcomes were poor, with nearly half of the patients progressing to ESKD within a relatively short period. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting high rates of renal progression in AA amyloidosis associated with IBD [6,8]. The rapid decline in renal function observed in our cohort underscores the importance of early diagnosis and prompt control of systemic inflammation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size limits the generalizability of the findings and precludes robust statistical analysis. Second, the long inclusion period (1990–2022) introduces heterogeneity in therapeutic strategies, reflecting changes in clinical practice over time. Third, not all patients had biopsy-proven renal involvement, as diagnosis was based on minor salivary gland biopsy in a substantial proportion of cases, which may limit the certainty regarding renal amyloid deposition. Finally, treatment approaches used in this cohort may not fully reflect current standards of care, particularly with the increasing use of biologic therapies in IBD.

Conclusion

Renal AA amyloidosis is a rare but severe complication of IBD, associated with a high risk of rapid progression to ESKD. In our multicenter cohort, renal involvement was often diagnosed at an advanced stage, with poor renal outcomes despite treatment.

These findings highlight the critical importance of early detection through systematic monitoring of renal parameters and timely, effective control of chronic inflammation, particularly with the use of modern therapeutic strategies. Such approaches may help prevent amyloid deposition and improve renal prognosis in this high-risk population.

Further prospective studies are needed to better define optimal screening and management strategies for IBD-associated AA amyloidosis.
