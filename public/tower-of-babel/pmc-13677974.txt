Efficacy of immune-oncology combinations versus sunitinib in patients with bone metastases from renal cell carcinoma: A systematic review and meta-analysis of phase III trials with long-term follow-up
Authors: Clara Fish Araujo de Albuquerque, Leila Caroline Souza Reis, Eliza Del Fiol Manna, Ana Elisa Saragossa Zafalon, Angela Theresa Zuffo Yabrude, Camillo Porta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone metastases occur in approximately one-third of patients with metastatic clear-cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) and are associated with poor prognosis. Although immune-oncology (IO)-based combinations are the standard first-line treatment, the relative efficacy of IO plus tyrosine kinase inhibitor (IO+TKI) versus dual immunotherapy (IO+IO) in this subgroup remains uncertain.MethodsWe performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of phase III randomized trials comparing first-line IO-based combinations with sunitinib in treatment-naïve metastatic ccRCC. Eligible studies reported hazard ratios (HRs) for progression-free survival (PFS) and/or overall survival (OS) in patients with baseline bone metastases and had a median follow-up of at least 36 months. The primary analysis pooled IO+TKI trials, whereas the single eligible IO+IO study was analyzed separately. Fixed- and random-effects inverse-variance models were applied.ResultsThree phase III trials were included: CLEAR (lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab versus sunitinib), CheckMate 9ER (nivolumab plus cabozantinib versus sunitinib), and CheckMate 214 (nivolumab plus ipilimumab versus sunitinib). IO+TKI significantly improved PFS versus sunitinib (HR 0.46, 95% CI 0.35–0.61; p < 0.0001), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). In contrast, IO+IO showed no PFS benefit (HR 1.07, 95% CI 0.76–1.51). Pooling all trials introduced substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 85.9%), and subgroup analysis confirmed a significant interaction between treatment class and PFS (p = 0.0002). IO+TKI also improved OS (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.50–0.88; p = 0.0041), again without heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). IO+IO showed a non-significant trend toward improved OS (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.61–1.11), with no significant difference between treatment classes for OS (p = 0.314).ConclusionsIn patients with metastatic ccRCC and bone metastases, IO+TKI combinations were associated with significant improvements in PFS and OS compared with sunitinib, whereas IO+IO did not improve PFS. These findings suggest that treatment strategy may influence outcomes in this high-risk subgroup.Keywords: Metastatic renal cell carcinoma, Bone metastases, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, Tyrosine kinase inhibitors, First-line therapy, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) is the most common subtype of kidney cancer, and approximately one-third of patients present with metastatic disease, a condition historically associated with a poor prognosis [1]. Immuno-oncology (IO)-based combinations have replaced sunitinib as the first-line standard of care, demonstrating consistent survival superiority within a number of randomized controlled trials [2], [3], [4], [5]. Two strategies with distinct mechanisms of action have been established: the combination of two immunotherapeutic agents (IO+IO) and the combination of an immune checkpoint inhibitor together with an antiangiogenic tyrosine kinase inhibitor (IO+TKI), options which are currently recommended by all main international guidelines [6], [7], [8], [9]. The IO+TKI trials relevant to this analysis include CLEAR, whose three-arm design (lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab, lenvatinib plus everolimus, or sunitinib) means that only the lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab arm qualifies as an IO+TKI regimen [5]. Bone metastases affect approximately one-third of patients with metastatic renal cell carcinoma (RCC), are associated with substantial morbidity and increased mortality [10], often poorly responding to systemic therapy [11], [12]. In ccRCC, loss of the von Hippel-Lindau tumor suppressor gene (VHL) function amplifies vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) signaling, thus sustaining enhanced angiogenesis. Since bone lesions display prominent angiogenic signatures, there is clearly a strong biological rationale for the use of VEGF-targeted therapy in this setting [13]. However, despite these premises, the antitumor activity of single-agent antiangiogenic TKIs was relatively scarce [14], being only slightly improved by the combination with bisphosphonates [15]. As far as immune checkpoint inhibitors are concerned, their use, as a single agent, for the treatment of bone-predominant metastatic RCC yielded significantly lower activity and efficacy when compared with TKIs alone [16].

Furthermore, bone metastatic patients remain somewhat underrepresented in clinical trials, in part because bone lesions are not measurable by RECIST criteria in the absence of a soft-tissue component [13], [16]. As a consequence, it is still not completely known whether IO-based combinations are more effective as compared to TKI monotherapy and whether there are nominal differences between IO+IO and IO+TKI.

To address this gap, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of available phase III trials to assess the efficacy of IO-based combinations compared with sunitinib in patients with bone metastases from ccRCC. We also compared IO+TKI and IO+IO regimens to determine whether treatment strategy influences progression-free (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in this poor-prognosis patient population.

Materials and methods

Study design and registration

We conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [17]. We prospectively registered the study protocol in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD420261406915).

Search strategy

Two reviewers (L.C.S.R. and C.F.A.A.) independently searched MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). Searches combined terms for renal cell carcinoma, immune checkpoint inhibitors (including nivolumab, pembrolizumab, and ipilimumab), antiangiogenic tyrosine kinase inhibitors (including cabozantinib, lenvatinib, and sunitinib), and randomized controlled trial design filters. Conference proceedings from ASCO, ASCO-GU, and ESMO were additionally screened to capture long-term follow-up data not yet available as full publications. No language restrictions were applied, although only studies with full-text publications in English were included. The complete search strategies for all three databases are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Eligibility criteria

We included studies that met the following criteria: 1) phase III randomized controlled trials; 2) comparison of first-line IO-based combination therapy (IO+TKI or IO+IO) with sunitinib monotherapy in patients with clear-cell metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC); 3) treatment-naïve population; 4) reporting of PFS and OS data for the subgroup of patients with baseline bone metastases, reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs); and 5) a minimum median follow-up of 36 months in the publication providing the subgroup data. We excluded trials that reported only descriptive or non-stratified outcomes for patients with bone metastases or did not provide extractable HRs for quantitative analysis.

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers (L.C.S.R. and C.F.A.A.) independently screened titles and abstracts using the Rayyan platform. The full texts of potentially eligible reports were subsequently assessed against the predefined eligibility criteria for final inclusion. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and, when necessary, by a third reviewer (E.D.F.M.). The study selection process is summarized in Fig. 1.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram summarizing the study identification and selection process. CENTRAL, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials; PRISMA, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.

A total of 6842 records were identified across three databases (PubMed, n = 1845; EMBASE, n = 2907; CENTRAL, n = 2090). Following removal of 2935 duplicates, 3907 records were screened by title and abstract. Of 103 full-text reports subsequently assessed for eligibility, 39 were excluded, 36 due to absence of bone-metastasis subgroup data and 3 due to insufficient follow-up duration. Three phase III RCTs met all inclusion criteria and were selected for quantitative synthesis. The 64 reports retained in Fig. 1 correspond to these trials, each of which had generated multiple publications over time. Bone-metastasis subgroup data for quantitative synthesis were ultimately extracted from one report per trial, the longest available follow-up with bone-metastasis-specific outcomes for CLEAR (49.6 months) [18], CheckMate 9ER (67.6 months) [19], and CheckMate 214 (99.1 months) [20]., while the remaining reports provided background or supporting information.

Two reviewers independently extracted data from each eligible trial using a standardized extraction form. Extracted variables included the first author and publication year, trial name, experimental regimen and pharmacological class (IO+TKI or IO+IO), number of patients with baseline bone metastases in each treatment arm, median follow-up, HRs for PFS and OS with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) in the bone-metastasis subgroup, and median PFS and OS, when reported. When the main publication did not report subgroup-specific HRs, these were retrieved from the supplementary appendix. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Statistical analysis

Hazard ratios (HRs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were transformed to the natural logarithmic scale [ln(HR)]. The standard error (SE) of ln(HR) was derived from the 95% CI as SE = [ln(upper CI) − ln(lower CI)] / (2 × 1.959964), assuming an approximately normal distribution for ln(HR) [21].

We calculated pooled effect estimates using two complementary approaches: an inverse-variance fixed-effect model and a DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model [22]. The random-effects model accounts for between-study heterogeneity by incorporating the between-study variance (tau2) into the pooled estimate. We calculated the Z statistic by dividing the pooled ln(HR) by its standard error and obtained two-sided p values from the standard normal distribution.

Because the IO+TKI and IO+IO classes have distinct mechanisms of action, we prespecified separate analyses for each treatment class. The primary analysis pooled the two available IO+TKI trials, CLEAR [5] and CheckMate 9ER [4] (k = 2). We summarized the single available IO+IO trial, CheckMate 214 [2] (k = 1) descriptively because pooling was not possible. As a sensitivity analysis, we pooled all three trials regardless of treatment class (k = 3) to evaluate the impact of combining pharmacologically distinct regimens. We assessed differences between treatment classes using Cochran's Q test for subgroup differences, calculated as the difference between the overall heterogeneity and the sum of the within-subgroup heterogeneity, with one degree of freedom.

We assessed statistical heterogeneity using Cochran's Q statistic test and the I2 index, calculated as I2 = 100% × [(Q − df)/Q] [23], with the between-study variance (tau2) estimated with the DerSimonian–Laird method [22]. Although formal heterogeneity estimation is inherently imprecise with a small number of studies (only two studies contributed to the primary analysis, and three to the sensitivity analysis), the pattern of heterogeneity statistics is itself informative: I2 = 0% within the IO+TKI class versus I2 = 85.9% when all classes are combined provides independent corroboration of the between-class difference identified by the formal subgroup test. Heterogeneity estimates of I2 and tau2 should be interpreted cautiously, as they provide limited precision with a small number of studies.

A two-tailed p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. We performed all analyses independently in Python and R, and cross-validated using native spreadsheet formulas (LN, NORMSDIST, CHIDIST) in Microsoft Excel, with exact numerical agreement across all three implementations at every step of the analysis pipeline.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Three phase III RCTs met all eligibility criteria and were included in the quantitative synthesis: CLEAR (lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab; IO+TKI) [5], CheckMate 9ER (cabozantinib plus nivolumab; IO+TKI) [4], and CheckMate 214 (nivolumab plus ipilimumab; IO+IO) [2]. Bone-metastasis subgroup data for CLEAR were obtained from a dedicated post-hoc analysis (median follow-up, 49.6 months) [18], whereas subgroup data for CheckMate 9ER and CheckMate 214 were extracted from the longest available efficacy updates (67.6 and 99.1 months of median follow-up, respectively) [19], [20]. For CheckMate 214, the stratified HR was available only in the supplementary appendix of the 8-year follow-up update. Trial characteristics and subgroup outcomes are summarized in Table 1.

Progression-free survival in the bone-metastasis subgroup

PFS in the bone-metastasis subgroup consistently and significantly favored the IO+TKI class over sunitinib (Fig. 2). In Analysis 1 (primary), pooling of CLEAR and CheckMate 9ER yielded a fixed-effect HR of 0.46 (95% CI 0.35–0.61; Z = −5.39; p < 0.0001; Table 2), with an identical estimate under the random-effects model (I2 = 0%; Q = 0.271, df = 1; Table 3). In Analysis 2 (descriptive), the single available IO+IO trial, CheckMate 214, did not demonstrate a PFS benefit in this subgroup (HR 1.07, 95% CI 0.76–1.51; p = 0.70; Table 2), with the point estimate numerically favoring sunitinib (median PFS 8.3 vs 9.7 months).

In Analysis 3 (sensitivity), pooling all three trials irrespective of treatment class yielded a fixed-effect HR of 0.65 (95% CI 0.52–0.80; p = 0.0001; Table 2), whereas the random-effects estimate was not statistically significant (HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.34–1.11; p = 0.106; Table 2). A formal test for subgroup difference demonstrated a significant interaction between treatment classes and PFS benefit (Q-between = 13.869, df = 1; p = 0.0002).

Overall survival in the bone-metastasis subgroup

For OS, the IO+TKI class was associated with significantly improved survival compared with sunitinib in the bone-metastasis subgroup. In Analysis 1, pooling of CLEAR and CheckMate 9ER yielded a fixed-effect HR of 0.66 (95% CI 0.50–0.88; Z = −2.87; p = 0.0041; Table 4), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%; Q = 0.003, df = 1; Table 5). In Analysis 2 (descriptive), CheckMate 214 showed a numerical trend toward OS benefit that did not reach statistical significance (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.61–1.11; p = 0.194; Table 4; median OS 26.3 vs 20.0 months).

Exploratory assessment of publication bias

Only three trials were included in this meta-analysis. Consequently, we did not assess publication bias using Egger's or Begg's test because these methods are unreliable when fewer than ten studies are available. We provide a funnel plot (Supplementary Fig. 1) for completeness only. Given the small number of studies, the plot does not permit a meaningful assessment of publication bias or small-study effects. The apparent asymmetry in the PFS analysis is more likely explained by differences between the IO+IO and IO+TKI treatment strategies than by publication bias.

Discussion

ICI-based combination therapies represent the current standard first-line treatment for metastatic RCC. However, whether these strategies provide comparable benefit in patients with baseline bone metastases remains uncertain. In this context, our meta-analysis synthesizes randomized evidence on first-line immune-based therapy in treatment-naïve patients with metastatic ccRCC and baseline bone metastases. We found that IO+TKI combinations consistently improved PFS and OS compared with sunitinib, whereas the available long-term evidence for IO+IO did not demonstrate a comparable benefit.

Our findings are consistent with emerging evidence suggesting that the efficacy of immune-based combinations varies according to metastatic site. A multicenter retrospective study from the JK-FOOT Study Group including 579 patients with intermediate- or poor-risk metastatic RCC found comparable outcomes between IO+TKI and IO+IO in patients with lymph node, lung, or brain metastases, but a trend toward improved OS with IO+TKI in patients with bone metastases (p = 0.053) and a significant OS benefit in those with liver metastases (p = 0.011) [24]. Similarly, a multicenter retrospective study of 69 patients with bone-predominant metastatic RCC reported a longer OS with first-line TKI than ICI monotherapy (41.3 vs. 19.3 months; p = 0.036), with a trend toward improved PFS [16].

Our results are consistent with real-world evidence identifying bone metastases as a major adverse prognostic factor in advanced RCC [10], [11], [12]. Although immune-based combination therapies appear to mitigate the poor prognosis associated with bone involvement, our meta-analysis suggests that this benefit is primarily driven by IO+TKI rather than IO+IO regimens. These observations are further supported by real-world studies, which consistently indicate superior outcomes with VEGF-containing combinations in patients with bone metastases, reinforcing the role of angiogenesis inhibition in this clinical setting [25]. Together, these data support our findings that the clinical benefit observed in patients with bone metastases may be primarily driven by VEGF-containing regimens.

Evidence from randomized trials further reinforces this hypothesis. From a subgroup analysis of a network meta-analysis that included 4206 patients from five phase III trials, Yanagisawa et al. reported that patients with bone metastases derived greater benefit from IO+TKI combinations than from nivolumab plus ipilimumab, further supporting the hypothesis that VEGF-containing regimens may be particularly effective in this clinical setting [26].

The superior efficacy of IO+TKI combinations in bone metastasis is biologically plausible. Beyond their direct antitumor activity, VEGF-targeted TKIs modulate the tumor microenvironment by normalizing tumor vasculature, reducing hypoxia, enhancing T-cell infiltration, and decreasing the accumulation of immunosuppressive cell populations, including regulatory T cells and myeloid-derived suppressor cells [27]. These effects may be particularly relevant in bone metastases, where aberrant angiogenesis and immune suppression contribute to therapeutic resistance. Experimental studies have shown that bone exhibits a markedly hypoxic microenvironment, with prevailing oxygen tension frequently declining below 2% [28]; this hypoxic microenvironment induces the activation of hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) signaling, ultimately activating a cascade of events, including angiogenesis [29], thus potentially increasing bone metastases sensitivity to VEGF-targeted therapies.

Bone metastases harbor a distinct immunosuppressive microenvironment that may reduce the efficacy of ICIs [30]. The bone metastatic niche is indeed enriched with regulatory immune and myeloid cell populations, a neutrophil-rich bone marrow microenvironment that promotes immune evasion, tumor-induced bone remodeling, and potentially lower PD-L1 expression, all of which may contribute to resistance to immune checkpoint blockade [30]. Together, these biological features provide a mechanistic explanation for the greater clinical benefit observed with VEGF-containing combination regimens in this subgroup.

Limitations and strengths

Only three phase III trials reported extractable subgroup hazard ratios, and OS findings should be interpreted cautiously because subsequent therapies, crossover, and the limited number of patients with bone metastases may have reduced the ability to detect differences between treatment classes. In addition, analyses were based on published subgroup data rather than individual patient data, precluding a formal adjustment for important clinical factors such as IMDC risk, skeletal disease burden, bone-targeted therapies, radiotherapy, and concomitant visceral metastases. None of the included trials reported skeletal-related events (SREs) in the bone-metastasis subgroup, so it was not possible to assess whether IO-based combinations modify the skeletal course of the disease beyond their effect on PFS and OS, particularly given a recently reported SRE incidence of 26.8% in metastatic RCC [31]. Furthermore, the presence of other metastatic sites may have had an impact on each treatment's efficacy. Finally, comparisons between IO+TKI and IO+IO were indirect, and no comparison can be even proposed between the two different IO+TKI combinations, in this regard, it should be noted that the CLEAR trial's third arm (lenvatinib plus everolimus, a non-IO-based regimen) was not eligible for inclusion in this analysis, which is restricted to the lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab versus sunitinib comparison. Publication bias could not be formally assessed, and heterogeneity estimates were based on a small number of studies.

This study also has important strengths. It included only pivotal phase III randomized trials with the longest available follow-up and focused specifically on patients with baseline bone metastases, a clinically relevant but underrepresented subgroup in whom osseous lesions are frequently non-measurable by RECIST. By prespecifying analyses according to treatment mechanisms and demonstrating consistent findings across the IO+TKI trials, our study provides the most comprehensive synthesis currently available to inform first-line treatment selection in this challenging population.

Conclusion

In patients with metastatic ccRCC and baseline bone metastases, first-line IO+TKI combinations consistently improved PFS and OS, whereas long-term evidence for IO+IO showed no PFS benefit. These findings support IO+TKI as the preferred first-line strategy for this underrepresented subgroup and highlight the need for prospective trials with bone-specific endpoints.
