Early Presentation of Acute Digital Ischemia After Intra-Arterial Injection Drug Use
Authors: Nicolas Echeverria, Etka Kurucan, Karim Gubara, Mark Solarz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 37-year-old man with hypertension and asthma who injects fentanyl daily presented with rapidly progressive discoloration, swelling, and pain of the hand shortly after fentanyl injection into the radial artery and a reported crush injury. Despite preserved proximal arterial flow on computed tomography angiography and catheter-directed angiography, there was markedly sluggish perfusion in the digital arteries that did not respond to intra-arterial vasodilators. The patient was treated with topical nitroglycerin and supportive measures but subsequently developed dry gangrene of multiple digits requiring partial amputation. This case highlights an unusually early manifestation of microvascular ischemia despite patent major vessels, suggesting a multifactorial etiology involving vasospasm, chemical endothelial injury, distal embolic phenomena, and potential xylazine-associated toxicity. Early recognition and stepwise vascular assessment may support timely intervention while avoiding unnecessary escalation in similar presentations.Key words: Digital ischemia, Injection drug use

Injection drug use is associated with considerable physical morbidity, including soft tissue infection, vascular injury, compartment syndrome, limb ischemia, addiction, and overdose death.1, 2, 3 These complications are further exacerbated by the lack of sterile injection supply use, the practice of injecting drugs mixed with other drugs or substances, and the improper identification of injection sites, such as injecting into arteries rather than veins.

One of the most physically disabling complications seen in this population involves acute ischemia that often terminates with the loss of limb or digits. Intra-arterial drug injection of the upper extremity has been shown to carry an amputation incidence of nearly 30%.4 As such, understanding the etiologies that lead to such complications is warranted so providers can better identify and care for at-risk patients. Here, we describe a patient that developed acute limb ischemia and digital necrosis following the injection of drugs. This case is reported because of the unusually early presentation of digital ischemia with preserved proximal arterial flow, a pattern not commonly described in the existing literature.

Case Report

A 37-year-old patient with daily intravenous fentanyl injection, hypertension, and asthma presented with progressive blue-white discoloration, swelling, coolness, and pain of the right hand one day after a crush injury. The patient reported injecting fentanyl into the right radial artery at the volar wrist on the morning of the injury. His crush injury was that the hood of the car he was working on fell on the dorsum of his hand. On presentation, his heart rate was 111 beats/min, temperature 36.7 °C (98.1 °F), respiratory rate 18 breaths/min, oxygen saturation 99% on room air, and blood pressure 146/93 mm Hg. Admission laboratory studies were notable for anemia (hemoglobin 11.1 g/dL), elevated creatine kinase levels (1,360 U/L), and an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (43 mm/h). Urine toxicology screening was positive for fentanyl, opiates, oxycodone, and benzodiazepines. The right hand was globally edematous with cyanosis extending distal to the palmar crease (Figure 1, Figure 2). The digits were cool, and sensation distal to the crease was diminished to light touch. There was tenderness to palpation throughout. Compartments were soft and compressible. Motor examination was limited secondary to pain; however, the motor function of the anterior interosseous, posterior interosseous, radial, ulnar, and median nerves were grossly intact. Radial and ulnar pulses were palpable, and Doppler signals were strong in the superficial palmar arch.

Radiographs of the right hand demonstrated diffuse soft tissue swelling without fractures or retained radiopaque foreign bodies. To further evaluate potential vascular compromise, the vascular surgery team ordered a computed tomography angiogram that showed patent radial and ulnar arteries with intermittent focal stenosis and limited visualization of the digital arteries (Figure 3, Figure 4). The patient was admitted for observation by the general surgery trauma team and given acetaminophen 1000 mg every 6 hours for pain control and enoxaparin 30 mg every 12 hours for anticoagulation. Serial examinations were performed to evaluate the potential evolution of compartment syndrome.

There is surrounding soft tissue infiltration and skin induration overlying the radial artery.

Although consults for digital necrosis at our institution are seen primarily by the orthopedic hand service, the history and presentation of this patient did not fit the typical presentation as there was no necrosis present during initial evaluation. As a result, the vascular surgery team was involved. The patient was taken to the operating room, and a catheter-directed angiography of the right upper extremity was performed by vascular surgery which demonstrated dominant radial inflow and intact palmar arch perfusion with markedly slowed contrast flow in the digital arteries unresponsive to 250 μg of intra-arterial nitroglycerin. Topical nitroglycerin and supportive measures, including limb warming and elevation, were initiated.

Over the following three days, the patient’s swelling and cyanosis persisted (Figure 5, Figure 6) despite stable proximal pulses. Doppler ultrasounds were not repeated. Pain and limited range of motion continued. On hospital day 6, symptoms modestly improved with nitroglycerin therapy, but the patient elected to leave against medical advice and was discharged with topical treatment and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medication.

The patient returned to clinic 24 days after discharge with dry gangrene to the distal aspects of his index through small fingers, with no purulence or signs of infection (Figure 7, Figure 8). Flexion and extension of all digits was limited because of persistent swelling. Mild pain with active range of motion as well as decreased sensation to light touch at the distal aspect of the index finger through the small finger was reported. The patient was placed on gabapentin and advised to continue applying nitroglycerin paste to his digits, with follow-up in 2 weeks. At this follow-up, clear demarcation of necrotic tissue was evident in the index, long, and ring fingers, with a smaller eschar on the small finger (Figure 9, Figure 10). Given the clear demarcation and persistent pain, the need for partial amputation of the necrotic regions was discussed. After clear demarcation of nonviable tissue, the patient underwent revision amputation of the right index finger through the middle phalanx and the right long and ring fingers through the base of the middle phalanx for dry gangrene. Remaining tissues were viable and closed primarily without complication. At the 2-month postoperative appointment, the patient reported no pain or paresthesias. The incision sites were well healed (Figure 11, Figure 12). He was instructed to follow-up as needed.

Discussion

Although the clinical presentations following intra-arterial drug injections have been well-described, the underlying pathophysiologic mechanisms remain unclear. Various theories have been proposed to describe the resultant vascular damage and limb ischemia from intra-arterial injection of drugs.

The norepinephrine-mediated vasoconstriction theory postulates that vessels acutely vasospasm in response to the presence of specific foreign substances and/or vessel trauma, leading to downstream ischemia.3 At presentation, this patient’s cyanosis was believed to be due to vasospasm, which could have occurred from both the intra-arterial injection as well as from the crush injury. However, this mechanism fails to describe the long-term pathogenesis that led to eventual necrosis in this patient.

Another theory postulates that limb ischemia is due to thrombi at distal small vessels, which prevent adequate perfusion of downstream tissues.3,5 Because it is unknown whether this patient used a sterile or used syringe, this remains a possibility. It is also possible that the crush injury led to the dislodging of a thrombus from the radial artery to its distal digital branches. Septic emboli from infective endocarditis should be considered in patients with long-standing injection use.6

Drug composition may also influence distal ischemia because lipophilicity and other physicochemical properties affect tissue distribution and vascular injury.3 Highly lipophilic substances such as fentanyl have been associated with increased necrosis and gangrene formation, which may have contributed to the severity of ischemia in this case despite preserved proximal arterial flow.

Multiple other proposed etiologies, such as particulate emboli from crushed pills, thrombi formation from compounds often used as pill fillers, the recrystallization of injected drugs in distal small vessels, and others have also been described.3,5,7,8

This case illustrates that intra-arterial fentanyl injection can precipitate early microvascular compromise despite preserved proximal arterial flow. The pattern of rapidly evolving ischemia in the presence of patent major vessels supports the hypothesis that sluggish digital perfusion may result from combined mechanisms, including drug-induced vasospasm, particulate microembolization, and endothelial injury.

Further, it is important to consider the timing in which these mechanisms may occur. A delayed onset of ischemic symptoms may reflect evolving endothelial dysfunction or ongoing vasospastic activity, whereas more immediate changes may suggest embolic or mechanical obstruction.

In patients who inject drugs, preserved proximal arterial flow should not exclude distal ischemia, even when initial presentations mimic blunt trauma. Early vascular evaluation, careful physical examination, Doppler assessment, and repeated neurovascular examinations are essential, with vascular surgery consultation considered when cyanosis, coolness, or sensory changes are present. Supportive care, including analgesia, anticoagulation, vasodilator therapy, and management of withdrawal symptoms, may be reasonable in the acute phase. However, evidence guiding optimal therapy remains limited, and management should be individualized based on clinical progression rather than protocol-driven imaging. Escalation to advanced imaging or intervention is appropriate when proximal occlusion or embolic source is suspected, but routine angiography is not indicated in isolated digital ischemia with preserved proximal pulses. Once the ischemic trajectory is established and no revascularization target is identified, close outpatient follow-up for demarcation is generally appropriate.

At the time of this patient’s presentation, xylazine adulteration of illicit fentanyl was highly prevalent in Philadelphia. Surveillance data demonstrated xylazine detection in 99.1% of fentanyl-positive samples in Philadelphia.9 Xylazine exposure may exacerbate ischemic injury after intra-arterial injection.

A limitation of this report is the inability to confirm the precise mechanism of injury because of the lack of direct intravascular imaging at the digital artery level and incomplete follow-up after patient directed discharge.
