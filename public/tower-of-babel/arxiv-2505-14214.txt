Regularized least squares learning with heavy-tailed noise is minimax optimal
Mattes Mollenhauer, Nicole Mücke, Dimitri Meunier, Arthur Gretton

Abstract
This paper examines the performance of ridge regression in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces in the presence of noise that exhibits a finite number of higher moments. We establish excess risk bounds consisting of subgaussian and polynomial terms based on the well known integral operator framework. The dominant subgaussian component allows to achieve convergence rates that have previously only been derived under subexponential noise—a prevalent assumption in related work from the last two decades. These rates are optimal under standard eigenvalue decay conditions, demonstrating the asymptotic robustness of regularized least squares against heavy-tailed noise. Our derivations are based on a Fuk–Nagaev inequality for Hilbert-space valued random variables. Keywords. 2.1
Reproducing kernel Hilbert space

We consider the
reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(RKHS)

\mathcal{H}

consisting of functions from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{R}

induced by the
symmetric positive semidefinite kernel

k\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}.

with
canonical feature map

\displaystyle\phi(x):\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H},

\displaystyle x\mapsto k(\cdot,x),

i.e. we have the
reproducing property

f(x)=\langle f,\phi(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
f\in\mathcal{H}
.

Assumption 2.1

(Domain and kernel)
.

We impose the following standard assumptions throughout this paper
in order to avoid issues related to measurability
and integrability
[
33
, Section 4.3]
:

(i)

\mathcal{X}
is a second-countable locally compact Hausdorff space,
\mathcal{H}
is separable
(this is satisfied if
k
is continuous, given that
\mathcal{X}
is separable),

(ii)

k(\cdot,X)
is almost surely measurable in its first argument,

(iii)

k(X,X)\leq\kappa^{2}
almost surely for some finite constant
\kappa
.

Integral and covariance operators.

Under
2.1
, we may consider the
typical linear operators
associated with
\pi
and
k
.
We consider the
embedding operator

\displaystyle I_{\pi}\colon\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\pi),

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto[f]_{\pi}

identifying

f\in\mathcal{H}
with its equivalence class
[f]_{\pi}\in L^{2}(\pi)
.
The adjoint
I^{*}_{\pi}:L^{2}(\pi)\to\mathcal{H}

is given by

I^{*}_{\pi}f=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi(x)f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x)\in\mathcal{H},\quad f\in L^{2}(\pi).

We obtain the self-adjoint
integral operator

\mathcal{T}_{\pi}:=I_{\pi}I_{\pi}^{*}:L^{2}(\pi)\to L^{2}(\pi)
induced by

k
and
\pi
as

\mathcal{T}_{\pi}f=\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(\cdot,x)f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x),\quad f\in L^{2}(\pi).

The self-adjoint
kernel covariance operator

\mathcal{C}_{\pi}:=I_{\pi}^{*}I_{\pi}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}

is given by

\mathcal{C}_{\pi}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi(x)\otimes\phi(x)\,\mathrm{d}\pi(x).

2.1
(iii)
ensures that we have

\lVert I_{\pi}\rVert=\lVert I^{*}_{\pi}\rVert\leq\kappa

as well as

\lVert\mathcal{T}_{\pi}\rVert=\lVert\mathcal{C}_{\pi}\rVert\leq\kappa^{2}
.
Moreover, the operators
I_{\pi}
and
I^{*}_{\pi}
are Hilbert–Schmidt
and both
\mathcal{T}_{\pi}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\pi}
are therefore self-adjoint positive semidefinite and
trace class.
By the
polar decomposition
of
I_{\pi}
and
I_{\pi}^{*}
, there exist partial isometries
U:\mathcal{H}\to L^{2}(\pi)

and
\tilde{U}:L^{2}(\pi)\to\mathcal{H}
such that

I_{\pi}=U(I_{\pi}^{*}I_{\pi})^{1/2}=U\mathcal{C}_{\pi}^{1/2}\text{ and }I_{\pi}^{*}=\tilde{U}(I_{\pi}I_{\pi}^{*})^{1/2}=\tilde{U}\mathcal{T}_{\pi}^{1/2}.

(2.1)

In particular, we have

\lVert[f]_{\pi}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}=\lVert I_{\pi}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}=\lVert\mathcal{C}^{1/2}f\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}

for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
. We will
also frequently use the fact that
2.1
(iii)
implies
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}{|f(x)|}\leq\kappa\lVert f\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}
.

2.2
Ridge regression

We introduce the standard integral operator formalism for ridge regression
in RKHSs, see e.g.
[
3
]
.
We consider the standard
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
-orthogonal decomposition
of
Y
with respect to the closed subspace
L^{2}(\mathbb{P},\sigma(X))\subset L_{2}(\mathbb{P})
of
\sigma(X)
-measurable functions given by

Y=f_{\star}(X)+\varepsilon

(2.2)

with the
regression function

f_{\star}(X)=\mathbb{E}[Y|X]\in L_{2}(\mathbb{P})

and noise variable
\varepsilon\in L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon|X]=0
.
Based on the representation (
2.2
), we have

\mathcal{R}(f)=\lVert f-f_{\star}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\pi)}+\lVert\varepsilon\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}

for all
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
and hence
the excess risk satisfies

\mathcal{R}(f)-\mathcal{R}(f_{\star})=\lVert f-f_{\star}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\pi)}.

Regularized population solution.

We define the
regularized population solution

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}:

\displaystyle=\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\mathbb{E}[(Y-f(X))^{2}]+\alpha\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}=\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{H}}\lVert I_{\pi}f-f_{\star}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\pi)}+\alpha\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}},

with
\alpha>0
, which is alternatively expressed as

f_{\alpha}=(\mathcal{C}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}I_{\pi}^{*}f_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}

(2.3)

with the identity operator
\idop_{\mathcal{H}}
on
\mathcal{H}
.

Regularized empirical solution.

We consider sample pairs

(X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},Y_{n})\sim\mathcal{L}(X,Y)

independently obtained from the joint distribution of
X
and
Y
.
We define the empirical versions of the
operators above in terms of

\widehat{I}^{*}_{\pi}f:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(X_{i})f(X_{i})\in\mathcal{H},\quad f\in L^{2}(\pi)

as well as

\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(X_{i})\otimes\phi(X_{i}).

The
empirical solution
of the learning problem
in
\mathcal{H}
with regularization parameter
\alpha>0

is given by the empirical analogue of (
2.3
),
which we obtain in terms of

\widehat{f}_{\alpha}=(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}\widehat{\Upsilon}\in\mathcal{H},

(2.4)

where the empirical
right hand side
\widehat{\Upsilon}\in\mathcal{H}

of the inverse problem is given by

\widehat{\Upsilon}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(X_{i})Y_{i}=\widehat{I}_{\pi}^{*}f_{\star}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(X_{i})\varepsilon_{i}.

(2.5)

Here, we use the orthogonal decomposition

Y_{i}=f_{\star}(X_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}

in the second equivalence.
We note that
\widehat{\Upsilon}

directly serves as an empirically evaluable unbiased estimate of

I^{*}_{\pi}f_{\star}
,
as
\widehat{I}^{*}_{\pi}f_{\star}
itself cannot be empirically evaluated
because
f_{\star}
is unknown.
As usual, we interpret the above objects as random variables depending on the
product measure
\mathbb{P}^{\otimes n}

through their definition based on the observation pairs

.

Capacity-free excess risk bound

We now provide an excess risk bound

subgaussian behavior of the term

\eta(\delta,n)
defined
in (
3.4
) for different choices of

q
. For simplicity, we set
c_{1}=1
,

\sigma=2
and
Q=10
.

We illustrate the behavior of
n_{0}
depending on

1-\delta
for different choices of
q
in

Figure

(we note that the involved constant
c_{1}

stems from the Fuk–Nagaev inequality given in

Proposition

5.1
and generally depends
on
q
). We choose
c_{1}=1
for simplicity
to provide a basic intuition—note that
c_{1}
only affects

logarithmically.
We refer the reader to
[
59
]
for a detailed discussion
based on the bound for real-valued random variables.

Subgaussian confidence regime and convergence rates.

We now give an excess risk bound which is similar to the
setting with bounded or subexponential noise: it
exhibits a logarithmic dependence of the confidence
parameter
\delta
and a dependence of the sample size
up to
n^{-1/3}
depending on
the level of smoothness given by
\nu

[
4
,
6
]
.
In the asymptotic large sample context,
this bound is dominant and allows us to recover convergence
rates.

Corollary 3.2

(Subgaussian confidence regime)
.

Let (
MOM
) and (
SRC
) be satisfied.
Then there exist constants
\tilde{c}_{1},\tilde{c}_{2}>0
such
that with the regularization schedule

\alpha_{1}(n,\delta):=\tilde{c}_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log(6c_{1}/\delta)}{n}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\min\{\nu,1\}+1}}

we have

\displaystyle\lVert I_{\pi}\widehat{f}_{\alpha_{1}(n,\delta)}-f_{\star}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{c}_{1}R\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log(6c_{1}/\delta)}{n}}}\right)^{\frac{\min\{\nu,1\}}{2\min\{\nu,1\}+1}},

(3.5)

with confidence
1-\delta

for all
\delta\in D_{1}(n,q)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\alpha_{1}(n,\delta)\leq\kappa^{2}
.

The constants
\tilde{c}_{1}
and
\tilde{c}_{2}
in
Corollary

3.2

only depend on
R,\nu,\kappa,\sigma,c_{1},c_{2}
and can
be made explicit, but we omit their closed form
here for the sake of a more accessible presentation.
We refer the reader to the proof for more details.

Remark 3.3

(Convergence rates)
.

We directly obtain
convergence rates from the above consideration.
For
fixed confidence parameter

\delta\in(0,1)
,
we see that for all
n\geq n_{0}
, where

n_{0}

is the effective sample size given in (
3.4
),
we have

\delta\in D_{1}(n,q)
.
Furthermore,
there exists some
\tilde{n}_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\alpha_{1}(n,\delta)\leq\kappa^{2}
for all
n\geq\tilde{n}_{0}
.
Combining these two insights,
from
Corollary

3.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert I_{\pi}\widehat{f}_{\alpha_{1}(n,\delta)}-f_{\star}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{c}_{1}R\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log(6c_{1}/\delta)}{n}}}\right)^{\frac{\min\{\nu,1\}}{2\min\{\nu,1\}+1}}

with confidence
1-\delta

for all
n\geq\max\{n_{0},\tilde{n}_{0}\}
. We explicitly note
that the convergence rates as well as the regularization schedule

\alpha_{1}(n,\delta)

match exactly the known results for the
capacity-independent setting that have been derived under
the assumption of bounded or subexponential noise

[
4
,
6
]
.

Polynomial confidence regime.

By definition (
3.2
),
the polynomial confidence regime
\delta\in D_{2}(n,q)

is relevant in the nonasymptotic investigation whenever

n<n_{0}
.
For completeness,
we address this setting in
Appendix

B

and show that
Proposition

3.1
can yield
simplified risk bounds with suitable regularization schedules for

\alpha
based on
\delta
and
n
.
Depending on
\delta
, these bounds may
require a stronger regularization
\alpha_{2}(n,\delta)
than
the subgaussian confidence setting. In fact, the resulting bound
exhibits a polynomial worst-case dependence on
\delta
,
which is compensated by a better dependence on the sample size before
transitioning to the behavior from the subgaussian confidence
regime given by
Corollary

3.2
.

4
Capacity dependent bound and optimal rates

We now improve the results from the previous section and give
an excess risk bound that involves the
effective dimension
,
which has been established as a central tool
to quantify the algorithm-dependent capacity of the hypothesis space

\mathcal{H}
relative to the distribution

\pi
and regularization parameter
\alpha

in order to derive risk bounds in regularized kernel-based learning
under the assumptions of an eigenvalue decay of
\mathcal{C}_{\pi}

[
31
,
3
,
9
,
10
]
.

Definition 4.1

(Effective dimension)
.

For
\alpha>0
, we define

{\mathcal{N}}(\alpha):=\tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}({\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}}}\right)<\infty.

We now assume the standard polynomial eigenvalue decay of

\mathcal{C}_{\pi}

[
3
,
9
,
11
]
.

Assumption 4.2

(Eigenvalue decay)
.

We assume that the nonincreasingly ordered sequence of
nonzero eigenvalues
(\mu_{i})_{i\geq 1}

of
\mathcal{T}_{\pi}
satisfies the decay

\mu_{i}\leq Di^{-1/p},\quad i\in\mathbb{N}

(EVD)

for a constant
D>0
and some
p\in(0,1)
.

Under the additional assumption (
EVD
),
we can sharpen
Proposition

3.1
.

Proposition 4.3

(Capacity-dependent excess risk bound)
.

Let (
MOM
), (
SRC
) and (
EVD
) be satisfied.
Suppose that
\delta\in(0,1)
and

\log(2/\delta)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2\kappa^{2}}{n\alpha}+\frac{2\sqrt{\tilde{D}}\kappa}{\sqrt{n}\alpha^{(1+p)/2}}}}\right)\leq 1.

(4.1)

Then there exists a constant
c>0
not depending
on
\delta
and
n
such that with confidence
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\|I_{\pi}\widehat{f}_{\alpha}-f_{\star}\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle\leq c\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha^{\min\{\nu,1\}}+\frac{\log(8/\delta)}{\sqrt{\alpha}n}+\sqrt{\frac{{\mathcal{N}}(\alpha)\log(8/\delta)}{n}}+\sqrt{{\mathcal{N}}(\alpha)}\cdot\eta(\delta,n,\alpha)}}\right),

where we set

\eta(\delta,n,\alpha):=\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\delta n^{q-1}}}}\right)^{1/q}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{\alpha{\mathcal{N}}(\alpha)}}}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2q}},\sqrt{\frac{\log(8c_{1}/\delta)}{n}}}}\right\}.

The constant
c
is made explicit in the proof.
The key idea builds upon the original work
of
[
3
]
.
In particular, we incorporate the effective dimension
into the Fuk–Nagaev inequality as a proxy for the
q
-th absolute
moment appearing in the term
\eta(\delta,n,\alpha)
,
thereby generalizing the idea to use the effective dimension
as a variance proxy in the Bernstein inequality.

Corollary 4.4

(Convergence rates)
.

Let (
MOM
), (
SRC
) and
(
EVD
) be satisfied.
Then for every
\delta\in(0,1)
, there exists
some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such
that with the regularization schedule

\alpha(n,\delta):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log(8c_{1}/\delta)}{n}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\min\{\nu,1\}+p}},

we have

\displaystyle\lVert I_{\pi}\widehat{f}_{\alpha(n,\delta)}-f_{\star}\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle\leq c\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\log(8c_{1}/\delta)}{n}}}\right)^{\frac{\min\{\nu,1\}}{2\min\{\nu,1\}+p}}

(4.2)

with confidence
1-\delta
for all
n\geq n_{0}

with a constant
c>0

independent of
n
and
\delta
.

Remark 4.5

(Optimality of rates)
.

The rates provided by
Corollary

4.4

match the rates from the literature
derived for well-specified
kernel ridge regression under the assumption
of subexponential noise

[
3
,
34
]
.
In particular, these rates are known to be minimax optimal
over the class of distributions satisfying
(
SRC
), (
EVD
) and the
Bernstein condition (
1.3
).

Corollary

4.4
now
proves that one can significantly
relax the assumption of subexponential noise (
1.3
), as rate
optimality is already achieved under the condition (
MOM
).
Furthermore, the regularization schedule

\alpha(n,\delta)
is the same as in the light-tailed
setting—in particular, it does not depend on
q
.

5
Fuk–Nagaev inequality in Hilbert spaces

We discuss the central ingredient for the derivation of the previous results in more detail for convenience.
We refer the reader to
[
24
,
25
]
for the original
work in the setting of real-valued random variables and
[
59
]

for a discussion of the involved constants.
We present a sharpened version
of a result due to
[
27
, Theorem 3.5.1]
, which is formulated more
generally in normed spaces, but exhibits an excess term
that can be removed in the Hilbert space case.
We provide the proof in
Appendix

C
.

Proposition 5.1

(Fuk–Nagaev inequality; Hilbert space version)
.

Let
\xi,\xi_{1},\dots\xi_{n}
be independent and identically distributed random variables taking values
in a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
such that

\mathbb{E}[\xi]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\lVert\xi\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}}}\right]<\sigma^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\lVert\xi\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}^{q}}}\right]<Q,

for some constants
\sigma^{2}>0
,
Q>0
and
q\geq 3
.
Write
S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}\xi_{i}
.
Then there exist two constants
c_{1}>0

and
c_{2}>0
depending only on
q
such that for every
t>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\frac{1}{n}S_{n}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}>t}}\right]\leq c_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{Q}{t^{q}n^{q-1}}+\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-c_{2}\frac{t^{2}n}{\sigma^{2}}}}\right)}}\right).

(5.1)

Remark 5.2
.

For simplicity, we may assume that

1\leq c_{1}
when we apply
Proposition

5.1
.

Confidence regimes.

Directly rearranging (
5.1
) from a tail bound to a confidence
interval bound requires to solve a
transcendental equation which does not admit a simple closed form solution.
However, we can still derive an upper bound on the confidence intervals
that reflects the superposition of polynomial and sub-gaussian tail in
(
5.1
).
By introducing

\delta:=2\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{c_{1}Q}{t^{q}n^{q-1}},c_{1}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-c_{2}\frac{t^{2}n}{\sigma^{2}}}}\right)}}\right\},

(5.2)

we have
\mathbb{P}[n^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|S_{n}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq t]\geq 1-\delta
by (
5.1
).
Rearranging (
5.2
), we have

t\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2c_{1}Q}{\delta n^{q-1}}}}\right)^{1/q}\quad\text{and}\quad t\geq\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\log(2c_{1}/\delta)}{c_{2}n}},

(5.3)

immediately leading to the following confidence bound.

Corollary 5.3

(Confidence bound)
.

Under the assumptions of
Proposition

5.1
, for all
\delta\in(0,1)
, we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\frac{1}{n}S_{n}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2c_{1}Q}{\delta n^{q-1}}}}\right)^{1/q},\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\log(2c_{1}/\delta)}{c_{2}n}}}}\right\}

(5.4)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

For every fixed
\delta
and
n\to\infty
, this
shows the typical subgaussian behavior
and a convergence rate of
\frac{1}{n}S_{n}
of the order
n^{-1/2}

with high probability.
Interpreting the right hand side as a function
of
\delta
for a fixed sample size
n
however,
the above bound characterizes a
confidence regime change

at
\bar{\delta}(n)
, which we define as the solution to the equation

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2c_{1}Q}{\bar{\delta}(n)\,n^{q-1}}}}\right)^{1/q}=\sigma\sqrt{\frac{\log(2c_{1}/\bar{\delta}(n))}{c_{2}n}}.

(5.5)

In fact, in the
polynomial confidence regime

\delta<\bar{\delta}(n)
, the dependence of the upper bound given
in
Corollary

5.3
on
\delta
is clearly
worse than in the
subgaussian regime

\delta\geq\bar{\delta}(n)

which is characterized by a logarithmic dependence on
\delta
.
In contrast, the polynomial confidence regime allows for a better
sample dependence of
n^{-(q-1)/q}
.

Sharpness of the tail bound.

Both the subgaussian term and
the polynomial term in the right hand side
of the bound given by
Proposition

5.1

can generally not be improved without additional assumptions.
We repeat a similar argument as
the one given in
[
60
, Proposition 9]
,
which is given in the context of linear processes.
Let
\xi,\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
be independent real-valued random variables
drawn from a centered
t
-distribution with
q
degrees of freedom, i.e.
\mathbb{E}[\xi^{q-c}]<\infty
for all
0<c\leq q
and let

\sigma^{2}:=\mathbb{E}[\xi^{2}]=q/(q-2)
.
Then
[
25
, Theorem 1.9]

shows that we have

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{n}/n>t}}\right]=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{n}/\sigma>nt/\sigma}}\right]\sim 1-\phi(n^{1/2}t/\sigma)+n(1-F_{\sigma^{-1}\xi}(nt/\sigma))\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty

for
nt/\sigma\geq n^{1/2}
,
where
\Phi
is the standard normal cumulative distribution function
and
F_{\sigma^{-1}\xi}
is the cumulative distribution function of

\sigma^{-1}\xi
.
We now note that we have the basic property

F_{\sigma^{-1}\xi}(nt/\sigma)=F_{\xi}(nt)

and we can show that the distribution of
\xi
satisfies

1-F(nt)\sim C_{q}/(nt)^{q}

as
nt\to\infty
, where
C_{q}
is
a constant depending exclusively on
q
.
In total, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{n}/n>t}}\right]\sim 1-\Phi(n^{1/2}t/\sigma)+\frac{C_{q}}{t^{q}n^{q-1}}\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty

for
nt/\sigma\geq n^{1/2}
, showing
that
Proposition

5.1
is asymptotically optimal.

A.1
Proof of

A.1.1
Bounding the variance

We first collect some generic properties of

f_{\alpha}=(\mathcal{C}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}I_{\pi}^{*}f_{\star}

gives

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{I}_{\pi}^{*}(f_{\star}-\widehat{I}_{\pi}f_{\alpha})]=I_{\pi}^{*}(f_{\star}-I_{\pi}f_{\alpha})=\alpha f_{\alpha}.

(A.1)

Based on (
2.3
), (
2.4
) and
(
2.5
), we decompose the variance in
\mathcal{H}
as

\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{\alpha}-f_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\widehat{I}_{\pi}^{*}(f_{\star}-\widehat{I}_{\pi}f_{\alpha})-\alpha f_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(X_{i})\varepsilon_{i}}}\right\}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\widehat{I}_{\pi}^{*}(f_{\star}-\widehat{I}_{\pi}f_{\alpha})-I_{\pi}^{*}(f_{\star}-I_{\pi}f_{\alpha})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(X_{i})\varepsilon_{i}}}\right\}

by (

)

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}}}\right\},

where we introduce the
\mathcal{H}

\Delta_{1}=\widehat{I}_{\pi}^{*}(f_{\star}-\widehat{I}_{\pi}f_{\alpha})-\mathbb{E}[\widehat{I}_{\pi}^{*}(f_{\star}-\widehat{I}_{\pi}f_{\alpha})],\qquad\Delta_{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(X_{i})\varepsilon_{i}\;.

(A.2)

We now obtain an
L^{2}(\pi)
-norm bound on the variance
as

\displaystyle\lVert I_{\pi}(\widehat{f}_{\alpha}-f_{\alpha})\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle=\lVert\mathcal{C}_{\pi}^{1/2}(\widehat{f}_{\alpha}-f_{\alpha})\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}=\lVert\mathcal{C}_{\pi}^{1/2}(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}}}\right\}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\;{\mathcal{B}}(n,\delta,\alpha)^{1/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{\mathcal{H}})^{1/2}(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}}}\right\}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}}}\right\}}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}

(A.3)

with probability at least
1-\delta
,
where
{\mathcal{B}}(n,\delta,\alpha)
is defined in
(
C.4
) and
where we apply

C.10
in the first inequality,

C.7
in the second inequality, and additionally
the fact that we have

\lVert(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1/2}\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})}\leq\alpha^{-1/2}
in the last inequality.

We now provide individual
confidence bounds for
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
.

Bounding
\Delta_{1}
: Bennett inequality.

We introduce the independent and identically distributed random variables

\xi_{i}:=(f_{\star}(X_{i})-f_{\alpha}(X_{i}))\phi(X_{i})\in\mathcal{H}

and note that
\Delta_{1}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\xi_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\xi_{i}])

and proceed similarly as in the proof of
[
4

.

To apply Bennett’s inequality, we need to bound the norm of the
\xi_{i}
.
First, note that almost surely, we have by (
SRC
), for some
f\in L^{2}(\pi)
, such that
\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leq R

\displaystyle\lVert f_{\alpha}(X_{i})\phi(X_{i})\rVert_{{\mathcal{H}}}

\displaystyle=\lVert\langle f_{\alpha},\phi(X_{i})\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}}\phi(X_{i})\rVert_{{\mathcal{H}}}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{2}\|f_{\alpha}\|_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\kappa^{2}\|({\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{{\mathcal{H}}})^{-1}I_{\pi}^{*}f_{\star}\|_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\kappa^{2}\|I_{\pi}^{*}({\mathcal{T}}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{{\mathcal{H}}})^{-1}{\mathcal{T}}_{\pi}^{\nu}f\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

A short calculation shows that for

,

is increasing on the spectrum of

\sup_{t\in(0,\kappa^{2}]}|h(t)|=\sup_{t\in(0,\kappa^{2}]}t^{\nu-1/2}\frac{t}{t+\alpha}\leq\kappa^{2(\nu-1/2)},

\displaystyle\lVert\xi_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}

Furthermore, by
[

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\lVert\xi_{i}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}]

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{2}\lVert f_{\star}-I_{\pi}f_{\alpha}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle=\kappa^{2}\lVert(\idop_{{\mathcal{H}}}-{\mathcal{T}}_{\pi}({\mathcal{T}}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{{\mathcal{H}}})^{-1}){\mathcal{T}}_{\pi}^{\nu}f\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle\leq\kappa^{2}R^{2}\alpha^{2\min\{\nu,1\}}.

\displaystyle\lVert\Delta_{1}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(\kappa\lVert f_{\star}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\pi)}+R\cdot\kappa^{2\nu+1})\log(2/\delta)}{n}+\sqrt{\frac{2\kappa^{2}R^{2}\alpha^{2\min\{\nu,1\}}\log(2/\delta)}{n}}

(A.4)

where we use

Bounding
\Delta_{2}

We introduce the independent and identically distributed random variables

\zeta_{i}:=\phi(X_{i})\varepsilon_{i}

By

to

with probability at least

.

Collecting the terms.

We have

\displaystyle\lVert I_{\pi}(\widehat{f}_{\alpha}-f_{\alpha})\rVert_{L^{2}(\pi)}

Here,
we set

Hoeffding inequality in Hilbert spaces

The following bound is classical and follows as a special

Proposition C.1

Bennett inequality in Hilbert spaces

Proposition C.2

Proposition C.3

Compared to the result above,

5.1
removes the excess term

We now prove

5.1

based on the proof by
[

]

and emphasize that this approach

by incorporating arguments by

63

with adjusted constants.

The following statement sharpens
[

, Lemma 3.5.1]

in the Hilbert space setting.

Lemma C.4
.

Let
\xi_{1},\dots\xi_{n}
be independent random variables taking values
in a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
such that the conditions
(
C.1
) are satisfied and additionally

\lVert\xi_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}\leq L
holds almost surely for some

and
all

. Then for
all

, we have

\mathbb{E}[\cosh(h\lVert S_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}})]\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tilde{c}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}h^{2}B^{2}+\tilde{c}_{1}Ah^{q}e^{hL}}}\right),

where
\tilde{c}_{0},\tilde{c}_{1}>0

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\cosh(h\lVert S_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}})]

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[e^{h\lVert\xi_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}}-h\lVert\xi_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[e^{h\lVert\xi_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}}-1-h\lVert\xi_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}]}}\right)

where the first inequality is due to

[
27
, Theorem 3.3.4(a)]
,
the second follows from the fact that we have

x\leq\exp(x-1)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
the third inequality is shown in the proof of

[
27
, Lemma 3.5.1]
.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

5.1
.

We can now prove
Proposition

5.1
by
modifying the truncation argument and Chernoff bound given by

[
27
, Section 3.5.2]
in combination with

Lemma

C.4
, which sharpens
a more general bound that is used in the original proof
by
[
27
]
.
Let us assume that (
C.1
) holds.
For
L>0
,
we introduce the truncated random variables

\tilde{\xi}_{i}:=\xi_{i}\,\mathds{1}_{\,[\|\xi_{i}\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq L\,]},\qquad\tilde{S}_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{\xi}_{i}.

In contrast to
S_{n}
, the truncated sum
\tilde{S}_{n}
is not necessarily centered.
Here, our version of the proof differs from
the proof of
[
27
, Theorem 3.5.1]
.
The original
proof approximates the centered norm

\lVert S_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}-\mathbb{E}[\lVert S_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}]

with the truncated centered norm

\lVert\tilde{S}_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}-\mathbb{E}[\lVert\tilde{S}_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}]
, leading to the excess term

\mathbb{E}[\lVert S_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}]
in the event for which
the tail is bounded.
In contrast, we perform the centering directly in the norm:
we approximate
\lVert S_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}

with
\lVert\tilde{S}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{S}_{n}]\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}
.

We first note that the difference between
\lVert\tilde{S}_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}
and
its centered version

\lVert\tilde{S}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{S}_{n}]\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}

can be bounded conveniently:

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\lVert\tilde{S}_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}-\lVert\tilde{S}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{S}_{n}]\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mathbb{E}[\tilde{S}_{n}]\rVert_{\mathcal{H}}=\lVert\mathbb{E}[\tilde{S}_{n}]-\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\lVert\tilde{\xi}_{i}-\xi_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}_{\,[\|\xi_{i}\|_{\mathcal{X}}>L\,]}\,\lVert\xi_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}\,\ }}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}_{\,[\|\xi_{i}\|_{\mathcal{X}}>L\,]}\frac{\lVert\xi_{i}\rVert^{q-1}_{\mathcal{X}}}{\lVert\xi_{i}\rVert^{q-1}_{\mathcal{X}}}\,\lVert\xi_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}\,\ }}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A}{L^{q-1}}.

(C.2)

We now verify the conditions (
C.1
)
for the centered sum
\tilde{S}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{S}_{n}]
.
We obviously have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\lVert\tilde{\xi}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\xi}_{i}]\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{X}}]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\lVert\tilde{\xi}_{i}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{X}}]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\lVert\xi_{i}\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{X}}]\leq B^{2}.

Furthermore,
from Minkowski’s inequality followed by

(a+b)^{q}\leq 2^{q-1}(a^{q}+b^{q})
for
a,b\geq 0
and
Jensen’s inequality,
we analogously obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\lVert\tilde{\xi}_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\xi}_{i}]\rVert^{q}_{\mathcal{X}}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}[\lVert\tilde{\xi}_{i}\rVert^{q}_{\mathcal{X}}]^{1/q}+\mathbb{E}[\lVert\tilde{\xi}_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}]}}\right)^{q}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{q-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}[\lVert\tilde{\xi}_{i}\rVert^{q}_{\mathcal{X}}]+\mathbb{E}[\lVert\tilde{\xi}_{i}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}]^{q}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{q}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\lVert\tilde{\xi}_{i}\rVert^{q}_{\mathcal{X}}]\leq 2^{q}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\lVert\xi_{i}\rVert^{q}_{\mathcal{X}}]\leq 2^{q}A=:\tilde{A}.

We now provide the final tail bound
for the norm of
S_{n}
by approximating it
with the centered truncated sum
\tilde{S_{n}}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{S}_{n}]
.
For every
t>0
and
h>0
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\lVert S_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}\geq tB]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[S_{n}\neq\tilde{S}_{n}]+\mathbb{P}[\lVert\tilde{S}_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}\geq tB]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A}{L^{q}}+\mathbb{P}[\lVert\tilde{S}_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}\geq tB]

(Markov’s inequality)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A}{L^{q}}+\mathbb{P}[\lVert\tilde{S}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{S}_{n}]]\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}\geq tB-A/L^{q-1}]

(by (
C.3
))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A}{L^{q}}+\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-htB+\frac{hA}{L^{q-1}}}}\right)\,\mathbb{E}[\exp(h\lVert\tilde{S}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{S}_{n}]\rVert_{\mathcal{X}})]

(Chernoff bound)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A}{L^{q}}+\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-htB+\frac{hA}{L^{q-1}}}}\right)\,2\mathbb{E}[\cosh(h\lVert\tilde{S}_{n}-\mathbb{E}[\tilde{S}_{n}]\rVert_{\mathcal{X}})]

(
\cosh(x)=(e^{x}+e^{-x})/2
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{A}{L^{q}}+2\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-htB+\frac{hA}{L^{q-1}}+\tilde{c}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}h^{2}B^{2}+\tilde{c}_{1}\tilde{A}h^{q}e^{hL}}}\right)

\displaystyle(\text{\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{lem:exponential_moments}})

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\tilde{A}}{L^{q}}+\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-htB+\frac{h\tilde{A}}{L^{q-1}}+\tilde{c}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}h^{2}B^{2}+\tilde{c}_{1}\tilde{A}h^{q}e^{hL}}}\right)}}\right).

The last expression in the parentheses is minimized over
the choices of
L
and

as shown in detail by

[

, pp. 105]
, giving

\mathbb{P}\big[\,\lVert S_{n}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}\geq tB\big]\leq c_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{A}{B^{q}t^{q}}{}+\exp(-c_{2}t^{2})}}\right)

(C.3)

for some positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
only depending on

.
Note that we absorb the factor

from

into the generic constant

in the resulting bound.

Whenever the

satisfy

∎

We next give a Corollary, providing a simplified bound under a worst case assumption for
the effective dimension that can be applied without information about the eigenvalue decay.

Corollary C.7

(Empirical inverse, worst case scenario)
.

Let
2.1
hold.
Assume that
{\mathcal{N}}(\alpha)\leq c\alpha^{-1}
for some
c>0
. Then, with confidence
1-\delta
, we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}(\mathcal{C}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{\mathcal{H}})}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})}\leq 4,

provided that

C_{\kappa}\;\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{\alpha}\leq\sqrt{n},\qquad C_{\kappa}:=2(1+\sqrt{c})\cdot\max\{1,\kappa^{2}\}\;.

Proof.

Using
{\mathcal{N}}(\alpha)\leq c\alpha^{-1}
and plugging it
into (
C.4
), we obtain

\mathcal{B}(n,\delta,\alpha)\leq 1+\log^{2}(2/\delta)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2\kappa^{2}}{n\alpha}+\frac{2\sqrt{c}\kappa}{\sqrt{n}\alpha}}}\right)^{2}.

Note that

\frac{2\kappa^{2}}{n\alpha}+\frac{2\sqrt{c}\kappa}{\sqrt{n}\alpha}\leq\frac{2(1+\sqrt{c})\cdot\max\{1,\kappa^{2}\}}{\sqrt{n}\alpha}=\frac{C_{\kappa}}{\sqrt{n}\alpha}.

Therefore,

\mathcal{B}(n,\delta,\alpha)\leq 1+\log^{2}(2/\delta)\cdot\frac{C_{\kappa}^{2}}{n\alpha^{2}}.

To ensure
\mathcal{B}(n,\delta,\alpha)\leq 2
, it suffices that

\log^{2}(2/\delta)\cdot\frac{C_{\kappa}^{2}}{n\alpha^{2}}\leq 1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad C_{\kappa}\cdot\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{\alpha}\leq\sqrt{n}.

Under this condition, we obtain
\mathcal{B}(n,\delta,\alpha)\leq 2
, and hence

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}+\alpha I)^{-1}(\mathcal{C}_{\pi}+\alpha I)}}\right\|\leq 4,

as claimed.
∎

Remark C.8

(Worst case scenario)
.

Note that, by
2.1
,
we always have

\mathcal{N}(\alpha)\leq\tr(\mathcal{C}_{\pi})\alpha^{-1}=\mathbb{E}[\lVert\phi(X)\rVert^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}]\alpha^{-1}\leq\kappa^{2}\alpha^{-1}.

We therefore see that the constant
c

in the above assumption always
exists and satisfies

c\leq\kappa^{2}
.

Under the eigenvalue decay assumption (
EVD
),
the conditions of
Proposition

C.6

can be simplified.

Corollary C.9

(Empirical inverse under (
EVD
))
.

Let
2.1
and (
EVD
) hold.
Then, with confidence
1-\delta
, we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\widehat{\mathcal{C}}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{\mathcal{H}})^{-1}(\mathcal{C}_{\pi}+\alpha\idop_{\mathcal{H}})}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})}\leq 4,

provided that

\log(2/\delta)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2\kappa^{2}}{n\alpha}+\frac{2\sqrt{\tilde{D}}\kappa}{\sqrt{n}\alpha^{(1+p)/2}}}}\right)\leq 1,

where
\tilde{D}
is defined in
Lemma

A.1
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

A.1
, under (
EVD

\tilde{D}>0
such that
\mathcal{N}(\alpha)\leq\tilde{D}\alpha^{-p}
.
Inserting this bound into
(
C.4
) leads to

\mathcal{B}(n,\delta,\alpha)\leq 1+\log^{2}(2/\delta)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2\kappa^{2}}{n\alpha}+\frac{2\sqrt{\tilde{D}}\kappa}{\sqrt{n}\alpha^{(1+p)/2}}}}\right)^{2}.

∎
