Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitors and Hepatocellular Carcinoma Risk in Patients With Chronic Hepatitis B and Hypertension: A Nationwide Dose-Response Cohort Study
Authors: Jung-Min Yu, Wan-Ming Chen, Ming-Feng Chiang, Ming-Shun Wu, Szu-Yuan Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
INTRODUCTION:Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a leading cause of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) may reduce HCC risk by modulating hepatic fibrogenesis, but population-level evidence remains limited.METHODS:We conducted a nationwide cohort study using Taiwan's National Health Insurance Research Database (2010–2020), including adults with chronic HBV and hypertension. ACEI use was defined as ≥28 cumulative defined daily doses. We applied a new-user design, propensity score matching, time-varying Cox models, and competing risk analysis to assess incident HCC.RESULTS:After 1:1 propensity score matching, 118,715 patients were included in each group. ACEI users had a significantly lower HCC risk than nonusers (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 0.66; 95% confidence interval, 0.64–0.69). A strong dose-dependent effect was observed, with the highest cumulative defined daily dose quartile demonstrating the greatest risk reduction (aHR, 0.29; 95% confidence interval, 0.26–0.32). This protective association persisted regardless of concurrent nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) therapy. Furthermore, treatment persistence (>1 year) was associated with lower HCC risk compared with early discontinuation (aHR, 0.62 vs 0.71).DISCUSSION:ACEI use was associated with a robust, dose-dependent reduction in HCC risk among chronic HBV patients with hypertension. These findings highlight the translational potential of repurposing ACEIs for HCC chemoprevention, particularly for hypertensive HBV carriers currently ineligible for antiviral therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common type of primary liver cancer and ranks among the leading causes of cancer-related death globally (1). In 2020, over 900,000 new HCC cases were reported worldwide, with a particularly high burden in East Asia and sub-Saharan Africa (2,3). In Taiwan, the incidence of HCC remains among the highest globally, at approximately 30.6 cases per 100,000 population, largely attributable to the endemic prevalence of chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection (4,5). Chronic HBV accounts for up to 80% of HCC cases in the region, and the oncogenic risk persists even in the absence of cirrhosis (6,7). Although antiviral therapy with nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) effectively suppresses HBV replication and reduces HCC risk, it does not eliminate it (8–10). Moreover, before 2023, Taiwan's National Health Insurance (NHI) program limited reimbursement for NAs therapy to patients with elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels or advanced fibrosis (11,12), leaving many individuals with low-grade infection untreated but still at risk for HCC (13).

The renin-angiotensin system (RAS), traditionally recognized for its role in blood pressure regulation and cardiovascular disease, has been increasingly implicated in liver fibrogenesis and tumor progression (14,15). Experimental studies suggest that angiotensin II promotes hepatic stellate cell activation, inflammation, and extracellular matrix deposition—key processes in HBV-associated liver injury and hepatocarcinogenesis (14,16–18). Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), by blocking the conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II, may disrupt these pathways and exert antifibrotic and antitumor effects (19–23). Preclinical models have demonstrated that ACEIs reduce liver fibrosis and angiogenesis through downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and vascular endothelial growth factor (23–26). Nonetheless, clinical data on the chemopreventive role of ACEIs in HBV-related HCC remain inconclusive (27–30). Some observational studies report protective associations, whereas others fail to find significant benefit, likely because of differences in study design, follow-up duration, and patient selection (27–30).

To address these uncertainties, we conducted a nationwide, population-based cohort study to evaluate the association between ACEI use and the risk of HCC in adults with chronic HBV infection and hypertension. Using data from Taiwan's NHI Research Database (NHIRD) and linked cancer and death registries, we applied a new-user design, time-varying exposure modeling, and competing risk analysis to generate robust estimates. Our objective was to assess whether ACEI therapy, widely prescribed for cardiovascular and renal indications, may offer additional benefit in reducing HCC risk among individuals with chronic HBV infection who require antihypertensive treatment. In particular, we sought to determine whether this potential protective effect followed a dose-dependent pattern, thereby reinforcing the biological plausibility of ACEI as a chemopreventive agent.

METHODS

Study population

Our cohort study, spanning from 2010 to 2020, used the HBV sample file from Taiwan's NHIRD to investigate individuals with Chronic HBV and hypertension. This extensive database covers over 99% of Taiwan's population and contains encrypted patient data, encompassing information on diagnoses, procedures, and prescriptions (31–35). By integrating this Data set with both the Taiwan death registry and the Catastrophic Illness Patient Registry (which undergoes stringent evaluation for insurance reimbursement eligibility), we were able to determine patients' cancer or mortality status and causes of death. This integration facilitated a comprehensive analysis of our study cohort (31–35).

This study investigated the relationship between ACEI use and HCC risk among adults aged 18 years and older diagnosed with HBV infection and hypertension, using data from the NHIRD. Excluding patients with missing age data, ACEI use was defined as a cumulative intake of 28 or more defined daily doses (cDDDs) post-HBV diagnosis. The observation period began at ACEI initiation (index date) and extended until HCC diagnosis, death, or December 31, 2020. To reduce bias from prevalent user effects, a new-user design was implemented, excluding individuals with any ACEI prescription within 6 months before the index date. To mitigate immortal time bias, we applied both a new-user design and time-varying exposure modeling, with unexposed person-time assigned up to the first ACEI prescription. To ensure diagnostic accuracy, HCC cases were identified through linkage with the Taiwan Cancer Registry Database (TCRD). Patients were classified according to ACEI exposure, defined as receipt of at least 28 cDDDs, whereas time-varying exposure status was updated during follow-up for the primary analyses. Follow-up lasted 1 year post-ACEI initiation or cohort entry. The study aimed to clarify the association between ACEI use and HCC risk in HBV patients with concurrent hypertension.

In our investigation of HBV patients with hypertension, we implemented stringent exclusion criteria to ensure data integrity. These criteria excluded individuals who: (i) were diagnosed with HCC within 1 year following the index date; (ii) lacked complete demographic information including sex and age, or were younger than 18 years of age; (iii) had a follow-up period shorter than 1 year; or (iv) had a previous diagnosis of any cancer type before cohort entry.

In Taiwan, access to NAs therapy for chronic hepatitis B was strictly limited under the NHI program before 2023 (11,12). Reimbursement was granted only to patients with active viral replication, elevated aminotransferase levels, or advanced liver fibrosis. As a result, many individuals with less active disease remained untreated yet continued to face a residual risk of HCC. Identifying alternative chemopreventive strategies for this population—such as the potential protective role of ACEIs—therefore remains a clinically important and timely area of investigation.

Approval for our study protocols was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Tzu-Chi Medical Foundation (IRB Approval No. IRB109-015-B).

ACEI exposure

In this study, prescriptions for ACEIs were identified using the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system within the NHIRD Pharmaceutical subsidies, which provided access to pharmaceutical claim data (36). ACEI users were defined as patients prescribed at least 28 cDDDs of ACEIs. Conversely, patients who did not use any ACEI (0 cDDDs) during the entire follow-up period were categorized as ACEI nonusers. The association between cDDD and the hazard ratio of HCC risk was examined. Subsequently, patients were stratified into quartiles (Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4) based on cDDD to explore dose-response effects of ACEI utilization on HCC risk in individuals with HBV infection. All analyses were adjusted for covariates listed in Table 1 to mitigate potential biases.

Moreover, we evaluated the daily intensity of ACEI usage by computing the average dose of ACEI, calculated by dividing the defined daily dose (DDD) by the total number of days in the prescription. The intensity of ACEI utilization was then categorized into 2 groups based on the average daily dose: below 1 DDD and above 1 DDD. This standardized DDD approach facilitates pharmacoepidemiologic comparisons across different ACEI molecules, focusing on the intensity of RAS inhibition as a class effect rather than nominal drug identity (37).

Propensity score matching and covariates

In our analysis, we implemented control measures to address potential confounding factors by incorporating a diverse range of covariates. Study participants were categorized into 4 distinct age groups at the index date: 18–30, 31–40, 41–50, and over 50 years. The index date for ACEI users was defined as the initiation of ACEI therapy with a minimum cumulative intake of 28 cDDDs. For matched nonusers of ACEI, the index date corresponded to the assessment date of the same variables.

In our multivariate analysis, we ensured accuracy by eliminating duplicate comorbidities in CCI scores. Comorbidities were identified within 1 year of the index date using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes, from either primary inpatient diagnoses or at least 2 outpatient visits. Both ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM coding systems were used. We used a time-varying Cox proportional hazards model to assess the association between ACEI use and HCC risk, adjusting for potential confounders. ACEI prescriptions were tracked at three-month intervals, and this information was incorporated as a time-dependent variable in the analysis. ACEI exposure was treated as a time-varying covariate, updated at 3-month intervals. Follow-up time was segmented into exposed and unexposed periods based on the presence or absence of ACEI prescriptions during each interval.

For a robust comparison of HCC risk between ACEI users and nonusers, patient matching was performed based on propensity scores, considering various factors such as age, sex, income level, urbanization, CCI scores, comorbidities, antihypertensive medications, and specific drug usage (e.g., NAs therapy, statins, metformin, aspirin). Continuous variables were reported as means ± SDs or medians with interquartile ranges. Covariate balance after propensity score matching (PSM) was evaluated using absolute standardized mean differences (ASMD), with all covariates achieving ASMD <0.1, indicating adequate balance (38).

Primary endpoints

The primary end point was the occurrence of HCC, ascertained through linkage with the TCRD, which provides validated diagnoses of malignancy under the NHI Catastrophic Illness Certification system.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were collected as covariates, outlined in Table 1, with age grouped into decade intervals. Baseline characteristics between ACEI users and nonusers were compared using the χ2 test for categorical variables, the t-test for continuous variables, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for median comparisons. The cohort entry date was set as the baseline.

To assess the association between ACEI use and HCC risk, we calculated incidence rates (IRs) and incidence rate ratios (IRRs), and estimated adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using Cox regression models. These adjustments considered factors such as age, sex, income, urbanization, CCI scores, prevalent comorbidities, and medication usage as outlined in Table 1.In addition, time-varying Cox regression analysis was used to investigate the impact of ACEI class, different levels of cDDDs of ACEIs, and daily intensity (≥1 DDD or <1 DDD) on HCC risk in HBV patients. The Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard model was applied to account for the competing risk of non-HCC-related death, ensuring unbiased estimation of HCC incidence in the presence of competing events. Cumulative HCC incidence was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with comparisons made using the log-rank test.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS for Windows (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC). A 2-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics and PSM

Among 1,061,176 patients with chronic hepatitis B and hypertension identified from the NHIRD, 236,436 were ACEI users and 824,740 were nonusers. Before matching, ACEI users were older, more likely to be male, and had higher CCI scores, lower income levels, and a greater prevalence of comorbidities such as diabetes, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), and cardiovascular disease. They also received more concurrent medications, including statins, metformin, aspirin, and other antihypertensives (Table 1).

Following 1:1 PSM, 118,715 patients were included in each group. All covariates achieved adequate balance (ASMD <0.1), indicating successful confounding control. The mean follow-up durations were 7.84 and 7.15 years for ACEI users and nonusers, respectively.

Risk of HCC among ACEI users vs nonusers

After PSM, the incidence of HCC was significantly lower in ACEI users compared with nonusers (3.84% vs 5.53%, P < 0.0001). The aHR from the time-varying Cox model was 0.66 (95% CI, 0.64–0.69). When accounting for the competing risk of death using the Fine and Gray model, the subdistribution hazard ratio was 0.70 (95% CI, 0.68–0.73) (Table 2). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed a significantly lower cumulative incidence of HCC in ACEI users (log-rank P < 0.0001, Figure 1). Dose-response relationship between ACEI exposure and HCC risk

A clear dose-response gradient was observed across quartiles of cumulative ACEI exposure (cDDD). Compared with nonusers, the adjusted HRs for HCC were 0.88 (95% CI, 0.83–0.92) in Q1, 0.76 (0.71–0.80) in Q2, 0.57 (0.53–0.61) in Q3, and 0.29 (0.26–0.32) in Q4 (P for trend <0.0001) (Table 2). The lower bound of the 95% CI for Q4 remained below 0.4, supporting a consistent protective association at higher cumulative exposures. Kaplan-Meier curves stratified by cDDD quartiles demonstrated progressively reduced HCC incidence with increasing ACEI exposure (Figure 2). These findings were further supported by a dose-response curve in Supplemental Figure 1 (http://links.lww.com/CTG/B533).

Impact of ACEI daily dose intensity

Consistent with established ACEI prescribing patterns in Taiwan (39), the most frequently prescribed agents in our cohort were enalapril and lisinopril (Supplemental Table 3, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B533). When ACEI exposure was stratified by defined daily doses (DDD), patients receiving ≥1 DDD daily had lower risk of HCC (aHR, 0.64; 95% CI, 0.61–0.67) than those receiving <1 DDD daily (aHR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.67–0.74) and both groups had significantly lower risk than nonusers (P for trend <0.0001) (Figure 3). Notably, 68.82% of users maintained a daily dose intensity of ≥1 DDD.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the protective effect of ACEI persisted across strata defined by age (older than 50 vs 50 years and younger), presence or absence of NASH, and use of NAs therapy (Supplementary Table 2, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B533). Interaction terms were nonsignificant (P > 0.1), indicating consistent effects across subgroups.

In sensitivity analyses, results remained robust after varying the exposure threshold (≥14, ≥90 cDDDs), extending the HCC exclusion window to 2 years, and excluding patients with concurrent use of statins, metformin, aspirin, or ARBs (Supplemental Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B533).

Persistence and discontinuation of ACEI therapy

Among ACEI users, 62.8% maintained therapy beyond 1 year, whereas 20.5% discontinued within 6 months of initiation. Patients with persistent ACEI use exhibited lower HCC risk than those with early discontinuation (aHR, 0.62 vs 0.71), suggesting that treatment persistence may influence the magnitude of the protective effect (Supplemental Table 2, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B533).

HCC IRs and IRR comparisons

The IR of HCC was 48.96 per 10,000 person-years in ACEI users and 77.26 per 10,000 person-years in nonusers (IRR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.61–0.66; P < 0.0001) (Table 3). The IRR declined progressively across cDDD quartiles: Q1, 0.79; Q2, 0.68; Q3, 0.53; Q4, 0.37. Likewise, higher daily dosing intensity was associated with lower HCC incidence: DDD <1, IRR 0.75; DDD ≥1, IRR 0.58.

DISCUSSION

In this nationwide cohort study, we found that the use of ACEIs was associated with a significantly reduced risk of HCC among patients with chronic HBV infection and hypertension (Table 2). This association remained consistent across time-varying Cox models and competing risk analyses, underscoring the robustness of our findings. Notably, a clear dose-response relationship was observed (Tables 2 and 3, Figures 2 and 3), with higher cumulative ACEI exposure corresponding to progressively lower HCC incidence, supporting the biological plausibility of the observed association. Our study contributes novel evidence to a controversial area in the literature. Although previous observational studies have yielded mixed results regarding the protective role of ACEIs in HBV-related HCC (27–30), our findings are strengthened by the use of a large, nationally representative sample, a new-user design, time-varying exposure modeling, and rigorous adjustment for confounders through PSM. In contrast to earlier research with shorter follow-up periods (27–30), our extended observation window (up to 12 years) allowed for a more comprehensive assessment of long-term outcomes. In addition, we leveraged linkage with Taiwan's Cancer Registry and Death Registry to improve diagnostic accuracy and ascertain competing events, ensuring robust endpoint definition.

Importantly, we also found that treatment persistence played a critical role in modifying the protective effect of ACEIs. Among users, those who maintained therapy for more than 1 year experienced a more pronounced reduction in HCC risk than early discontinuers, reinforcing the clinical relevance of adherence in chemoprevention strategies (Supplemental Table 2, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B533). For example, patients who persisted with ACEI therapy beyond 1 year had an aHR of 0.62, compared with 0.71 among those who discontinued within 6 months. These findings support the hypothesis that sustained inhibition of the RAS may exert biologically meaningful antitumor effects in the liver (40–42).

Mechanistically, ACEIs may mitigate HBV-associated liver injury by attenuating hepatic inflammation, stellate cell activation, and fibrogenesis, all of which are mediated by angiotensin II signaling (14,43–45). Experimental models have demonstrated that RAS activation promotes oxidative stress, proinflammatory cytokine production, angiogenesis, and fibrotic remodeling—key processes in HBV-related hepatocarcinogenesis (46–49). Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that HBV replication itself may upregulate components of the RAS pathway, creating a feedback loop that accelerates disease progression. Inhibition of this pathway through ACEIs could disrupt this loop and prevent downstream oncogenic transformation (23,30,50,51).

The observed HCC incidence in our study was higher than the background rate in the general Taiwanese population (5). This is expected, as our cohort exclusively comprised patients with both chronic HBV and hypertension, representing a clinically high-risk population where cumulative cardiometabolic burden synergistically amplifies hepatocarcinogenesis (52). Our findings are especially relevant in the context of Taiwan's NHI policy before 2023, which restricted reimbursement of NAs therapy to patients with elevated ALT or advanced liver fibrosis (11,12). Consequently, the relatively low proportion of antiviral use in our cohort reflects these strict reimbursement criteria during the study period, rather than indicating a low-risk inactive carrier population. As a result, many asymptomatic HBV carriers—though at risk for HCC—did not receive antiviral treatment. For this clinically relevant population, ACEIs may warrant further evaluation as a potential adjunctive strategy for HCC risk reduction. Importantly, ACEIs are prescribed within a defined clinical context for blood pressure management. Therefore, our findings cannot be directly extrapolated to normotensive individuals with chronic HBV infection. This has important implications for global regions with similarly constrained access to antiviral therapy.

Notably, our analysis incorporated several advanced pharmacoepidemiologic techniques to strengthen internal validity and reduce major sources of bias, including a time-varying Cox model that updated ACEI exposure in 3-month intervals, thereby minimizing immortal time bias. We also applied a competing risk model (Fine and Gray method) to account for non-HCC-related mortality, allowing a more accurate estimation of HCC incidence. These modeling choices underscore the methodological rigor of our study. Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent protective effects across strata including age, NASH, and NAs therapy, without significant effect modification (P for interaction >0.10) (Supplemental Table 2, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B533). This uniform relative benefit indicates that ACEIs operate consistently across different clinical profiles, and should not be misinterpreted as these established risk factors lacking prognostic relevance for absolute HCC risk.

This study has several limitations. First, although we adjusted for a wide range of baseline covariates, we were unable to account for certain unmeasured confounders—such as alcohol consumption, smoking status, body mass index, and physical activity—because of limitations in the claims database. These lifestyle factors, as well as obesity, NASH, and cirrhosis, were partially captured through diagnosis-based proxies and may be underascertained. Furthermore, direct markers of HBV disease activity (e.g., HBV DNA, ALT, HBeAg) and fibrosis severity were unavailable, and NAs therapy receipt was heavily influenced by strict reimbursement policies. However, we adopted multiple strategies to mitigate potential confounding, including rigorous PSM, adjustment for proxy variables (e.g., alcohol-related liver disease, obesity, and comorbidities), and extensive sensitivity analyses, all of which yielded consistent results. Moreover, our E-value analysis suggested that relatively strong unmeasured confounding would be required to fully explain away the observed association; however, this sensitivity analysis does not eliminate the possibility of residual confounding (Supplemental Table 4, http://links.lww.com/CTG/B533) (53). Second, actual medication adherence could not be directly verified; our exposure definitions relied on prescription fill records. To address this, we incorporated time-varying exposure models and analyzed treatment persistence, which revealed dose-response and duration-response gradients consistent with biologic plausibility. Third, although a competing risk regression model was used to account for the high background mortality risk in patients with chronic HBV infection, the possibility of residual time-dependent confounding remains. Fourth, our study population was derived from a homogeneous Asian cohort with high HBV prevalence and genotype B/C predominance (54), limiting the generalizability to populations with different viral genotypes (e.g., genotype A or E) or healthcare systems with different NAs treatment thresholds. Although the RAS-mediated mechanism is biologically plausible across settings, our findings are most applicable to similar HBV-endemic populations, and further replication is needed before broader generalization. Finally, we lacked histopathologic data on liver fibrosis or necroinflammatory activity; however, we adjusted for cirrhosis-related comorbidities and restricted the cohort to individuals without prior HCC or decompensated liver disease to minimize heterogeneity in baseline HCC risk. Ultimately, our findings represent an observed association rather than proof of causality, and confirmatory evidence from randomized or quasi-experimental designs is required before clinical implementation.

In this nationwide cohort of patients with chronic HBV infection and hypertension, ACEI use was associated with a lower risk of HCC, with an apparent dose-response pattern and stronger associations among persistent users. Given the observational design and the possibility of residual confounding, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-supporting rather than causal and warrant confirmation in other populations and study designs.
