Stabilization of highly expansive clay using synergistic EAF slag and water treatment sludge: a mechanical performance study
Authors: Mohammadsaleh Abdipour, Seyed Hamid Lajevardi, Meghdad Payan, Seyed Taha Tabatabaei Aghda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study addresses the challenges of highly expansive clay soils by stabilizing them with two industrial by-products: electric arc furnace (EAF) slag and water treatment sludge (WTS), thereby addressing the limited research on their combined (synergistic) use in soil stabilization. The potential of these additives to enhance soil strength and stiffness while mitigating swelling was evaluated using clay samples treated with 0, 10, 20, and 30% EAF slag and 0, 5, 10, 20, and 30% WTS replacement, after curing for 7, 28, and 56 days. Unconfined compressive strength (UCS) and ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) tests were employed as primary indicators of mechanical performance, while free swelling tests were carried out to assess volume stability. In addition, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses were performed to provide micromechanical insights into the mechanisms underlying the observed improvements. Results indicate that the combined use of 30% EAF slag and 20% WTS replacement produced the most favorable outcomes in terms of strength, stiffness, and swelling reduction. UCS increased by more than fourfold compared with untreated soil, reaching approximately 2800 kPa, while UPV rose to nearly 1700 m/s. The swelling potential of the optimized mix decreased dramatically from 270% in the natural clay to only 8% after 56 days of curing. Microstructural analyses confirmed the development of cementitious gels, primarily calcium-silicate-hydrate (C–S–H) and calcium-aluminate-hydrate (C–A–H), which filled voids, bonded particles, and created a denser, more stable matrix resistant to moisture-induced expansion. These findings demonstrate that the combined use of EAF slag and WTS provides an effective approach to improving the mechanical and volumetric performance of expansive soils.Keywords: Expansive soil, Electric arc furnace (EAF) slag, Water treatment sludge (WTS), UCS, UPV, Swelling potentialSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Expansive soils exhibit considerable changes in volume when their moisture content fluctuates, posing a widespread challenge worldwide. The volumetric instability of these soils creates serious threats to infrastructure and buildings and can contribute to natural disasters3,75,87. Their unfavorable properties, including low strength, high compressibility, susceptibility to deformation under minor loads, and substantial volumetric changes, categorize them as problematic in geotechnical engineering90. To mitigate these challenges and meet the growing demand for construction-ready areas, expansive soils require proper treatment and stabilization5,51,71,86. In practice, numerous methods have been proposed to limit the volume change behavior of expansive soils. These include controlled compaction, reinforcement, soil replacement, pre-wetting, electrokinetic and chemical treatments, biological approaches, moisture regulation, and innovative foundation designs79,80.

Among these approaches, soil stabilization through the incorporation of additives is a widely applied approach, offering significant engineering and economic benefits16,42,62,92,93,99,100. This approach is particularly advantageous in cases involving unsuitable on-site soils, as it eliminates the costs associated with transporting suitable soil or disposing of unsuitable material in landfills81,40. There has been widespread research at the international level regarding the use of both traditional and non-traditional additives for treating expansive soils2,23,66,76. The use of cementitious, pozzolanic, and waste-based materials has increased in recent years to reduce cement usage59. Traditional stabilizers such as lime, cement, and gypsum are widely used to reduce swelling potential and improve soil strength. However, these conventional additives are often criticized for their environmental impact31,35,46,57. Moreover, studies indicate that some stabilizers, including lime and cement, may only temporarily reduce swelling potential, with their effectiveness diminishing over time41. Consequently, researchers have focused on identifying more durable and sustainable alternatives to address the limitations of traditional stabilizers, aiming to enhance both environmental compatibility and long-term performance26,38,5377,78.

One non-traditional approach to soil stabilization involves the use of waste materials such as slags, which are by-products of various stages in steel-making processes, including blast furnace, basic oxygen furnace, and electric arc furnace. Each type of slag has distinct properties and applications, such as serving as additives in cement, fillers, or stabilizers22,44,94–97. EAF slag, produced during crude steel manufacturing, is particularly notable for its high density, robust mechanical strength, and superior specific gravity compared to typical soils. Its versatility allows it to be refined into different aggregate sizes, making it a suitable substitute for natural aggregates. In construction, EAF slag is widely used for bases, subgrades, embankments, subbases, and bituminous mixtures in roads, highways, and railways. Additionally, it is a preferred material for producing hydraulic concrete48,73. In addition to its mechanical advantages, EAF slag contains a high proportion of calcium oxide (CaO), which plays a key role in soil stabilization. Upon hydration, CaO reacts with water to form calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂) in an exothermic reaction, increasing the alkalinity of the system. This highly alkaline environment promotes pozzolanic reactions with silica and alumina present in the soil, leading to the formation of cementitious compounds and a reduction in soil plasticity. These reactions enhance the overall strength and stability of the treated soil38,43,94–97. Recent studies have demonstrated that combining nanosilica with EAF slag significantly improves the mechanical properties of expansive soils, even within short curing periods68.

Similarly, water treatment sludge (WTS), a by-product of urban water purification, offers potential for sustainable reuse. Generated during processes such as coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, and filtration, WTS consists primarily of chemical oxides and organic compounds15,52. With increasing urban populations, WTS production has become a global challenge requiring innovative disposal and recycling solutions33,37. Traditional methods, such as landfilling and agricultural soil applications, are limited by high costs, regulatory restrictions, and environmental risks, particularly from heavy metals32,65. Landfill disposal is suboptimal, marked by stringent regulations and escalating costs. In agricultural soil application, WTS serves as a liming agent for acidic soils. Although it offers agricultural benefits, concerns arise regarding heavy metal content, posing risks to human, aquatic, and terrestrial ecosystems32,72,88. This scenario underscores the growing need to explore alternative and economically viable options for WTS reuse and recycling. Since WTS and clay have similar mineralogical compositions (primarily silica, aluminum, and ferric hydroxides and oxides), using WTS as a partial substitute for clay in manufacturing cement and other sintered ceramic materials has been strongly encouraged18,25. Due to the significant environmental consequences associated with clay extraction and its limited availability in various regions around the globe, there is increasing research focused on developing innovative and sustainable materials from alternative, locally abundant raw resources that comply with international standards17. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that calcium-rich systems can promote the formation of cementitious and geopolymeric products56,98,99,101. For instance, Suksiripattanapong et al.74, reported that calcium oxide (CaO) reacts with silica sources in sodium silicate (Na₂SiO₃) and WTS to form calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H), which coexists with sodium aluminosilicate hydrate (N–A–S–H) gels typical of geopolymer systems. In addition, WTS was selected due to its fine particle size and suitable chemical composition, enabling it to function as both a micro-filler and a reactive material. This dual role improves particle packing, reduces void spaces, and contributes to the development of a denser and more stable soil matrix56.

Despite extensive research on the stabilization of expansive soils, limited investigations have examined the behavior of these soils in terms of their strength and volumetric expansion when treated with sustainable alternatives like EAF slag and WTS. This study’s key innovation involves the simultaneous use of two industrial by-products, EAF slag and WTS, as complementary stabilizers, offering both physical reinforcement and chemical compatibility with expansive clays. Unlike previous studies that often assess a single additive in isolation, this research explores the synergistic effects of blending EAF slag and WTS in varying proportions to achieve optimal stabilization. Moreover, the study uniquely correlates macro-scale performance indicators (UCS, UPV, and swelling reduction) with detailed microstructural changes revealed through SEM, EDS, and XRD, providing a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms driving the observed improvements. Unlike previous studies that rely on single indicators, the present work simultaneously integrates strength (UCS), stiffness (UPV and secant modulus), energy absorption capacity (Eu), and volumetric stability to provide a more comprehensive understanding of soil behavior under stabilization. Furthermore, this research highlights, for the first time, the role of WTS not only as a supplementary pozzolanic material but also as a micro-filler that enhances particle packing and energy dissipation capacity within the soil matrix. This dual functionality contributes to improved toughness and durability, which are rarely addressed in expansive soil stabilization studies. Addressing this gap, the current research investigates the potential of EAF slag and WTS as eco-friendly stabilizers to enhance the compressive strength, stiffness, and swelling characteristics of expansive clays.

By leveraging the mechanical properties of EAF slag and the chemical compatibility of WTS with clay minerals, this study aims to develop a sustainable approach for improving expansive soils. The proposed stabilization method may offer potential economic advantages due to the use of industrial by-products that are often available at low cost. However, a comprehensive economic evaluation, including factors such as transportation, processing, and field application, is required to fully assess its cost-effectiveness, and this aspect is recommended for future research. To achieve the main objectives of this study, different proportions of EAF slag and WTS replacement are blended with expansive clay, and the samples are subjected to curing periods of 7, 28, and 56 days. Subsequently, a series of mechanical and microstructural tests is performed to evaluate their effectiveness as stabilizers, advancing both environmental sustainability and engineering performance in construction projects.

Mixing method and experimental program

The expansive clay utilized in this study was oven-dried at 110 °C for 24 h and passed through a No. 200 sieve to eliminate coarse particles and maintain uniformity. As the first stabilizing additive, EAF slag was incorporated into the clay at replacement levels of 0%, 10%, 20%, and 30% by dry weight. Standard Proctor compaction tests were performed on each clay–slag mixture to determine the maximum dry density (MDD) and optimum moisture content (OMC) in accordance with ASTM D698 (ASTM D698, 2021). Based on these results, the required amounts of clay, slag, and water were thoroughly mixed to achieve a uniform consistency. The mixtures were statically compacted into cylindrical steel molds. They were then cured at room temperature (approximately 25 °C) for 7, 28, and 56 days. After compaction, the specimens were carefully extruded from the molds, visually inspected for defects, and immediately all specimens were sealed in plastic bags to prevent moisture loss and stored in a chamber to prevent any moisture loss prior to curing. The curing conditions were maintained at a constant temperature of 25 ± 2 °C and a relative humidity of approximately 80% throughout the curing periods to ensure consistency and reproducibility of the results. The selected curing periods of 7, 28, and 56 days were adopted to evaluate the short-term, intermediate-term, and long-term behavior of the stabilized soil. These durations allow for monitoring the progression of hydration and pozzolanic reactions, which significantly influence strength development and swelling characteristics over time.

After the curing periods, mechanical tests were performed to assess the performance of each mix.

In the subsequent phase, WTS was introduced as a partial replacement for the EAF slag at varying replacement levels of 0%, 5%, 10%, 20%, and 30%. A similar procedure was followed to obtain the MDD and OMC values for the clay-slag-WTS mixtures using standard compaction tests. These mixtures were fabricated, compacted, and cured under the same conditions as previously described. All WTS-treated specimens were also sealed and cured under identical temperature and humidity-controlled conditions to ensure uniform curing across all mixtures. After 7, 28, and 56 days of curing, the samples were tested again to evaluate the combined effect of EAF slag and WTS on soil strength and stiffness. To further investigate the changes in soil behavior due to the additives, microstructural analyses were conducted on selected samples. The selection of the blending scheme and experimental matrix (Table 3) was based on both previous literature and the need to systematically evaluate the influence of stabilizer content on soil behavior. EAF slag contents of 0%, 10%, 20%, and 30% were selected as they fall within the commonly reported effective range for calcium-based stabilization, where sufficient CaO is available to promote pozzolanic reactions and strength development28. The WTS replacement levels (0%, 5%, 10%, 20%, and 30%) were chosen to capture both low and high substitution effects, allowing identification of the optimum content and potential performance reduction at excessive dosages. This range also aligns with previous studies on waste-based stabilizers, where similar replacement levels have been used to assess filler effects, matrix densification, and possible negative impacts of excess fines or organic matter19. Overall, the experimental program was designed to systematically investigate the individual and combined effects of EAF slag, WTS content, and curing time on the mechanical and volumetric properties of expansive soils. For each mix configuration, two replicate specimens were prepared and tested, and the average values were reported in the results. This ensured the reliability and repeatability of the experimental outcomes.

Testing procedure

Unconfined compressive strength (UCS) test

The primary objective of the UCS test was to assess the stress–strain behavior and compressive strength of the prepared specimens. The tests were performed using an automated loading frame that imposed a uniaxial vertical load at a constant displacement rate of 1 mm/min. Specimens were prepared with a height-to-diameter ratio of 2, following the guidelines outlined in ASTM D21669. UCS measurements were taken after curing durations of 7, 28, and 56 days. Two replicate specimens were tested for each condition, and the average value was recorded. Load data were captured at 0.2 mm displacement intervals to ensure a detailed and accurate representation of the stress–strain curve. This high-resolution data collection enabled a more accurate determination of the ultimate compressive strength and helped in analyzing the material’s mechanical response under unconfined compression.

Ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) test

Prior to conducting compression tests, non-destructive UPV tests were conducted on all cylindrical samples in accordance with ASTM C5977. This method was used to evaluate the stiffness and internal integrity of the stabilized soil samples. During the test, each specimen was placed between a pair of ultrasonic transducers one serving as the transmitter and the other as the receiver. Ultrasonic waves, typically at a frequency of 54 kHz, were emitted from the transmitting transducer, propagated through the specimen, and detected by the receiving transducer on the opposite side. Swelling test

Swelling behavior of the soil specimens was determined through one-dimensional oedometer tests following the ASTM D454611 standard. Two testing approaches were adopted: free swell under a minimal surcharge pressure of 1 kPa, and swelling under a higher surcharge of 12 kPa, simulating pressures found at shallow depths (approximately 0.5 to 1 m).

Test specimens, comprising untreated expansive clay and clay stabilized with 30% EAF slag and 20% WTS replacement (identified as the optimum mix based on strength and stiffness test results), were prepared at their respective OMC and MDD as determined from compaction tests. The soil mixtures were thoroughly blended to ensure uniformity, then sealed in plastic bags and stored in a controlled environment at 25 ± 2 °C with 80% relative humidity for 24 h to maintain consistent curing conditions. Subsequently, the samples were compacted into oedometer rings (50 mm internal diameter and 20 mm height) between two porous stones. Swelling measurements were conducted following curing periods of 7, 28, and 56 days by monitoring vertical deformation over time. This procedure allowed for a clear evaluation of the swelling potential of untreated and stabilized clay under varying loading conditions.

Microstructural analysis

To gain deeper insight into the behavior of both untreated and stabilized expansive clay, a comprehensive set of microstructural analyses was performed. These included X-ray Diffraction (XRD), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), and Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) performed on selected samples as outlined in Table 3.

XRD analysis was conducted to examine the mineralogical composition of untreated soil and samples treated with the optimal contents of stabilizing agents. SEM observations were conducted on crushed samples (less than 10 mm in size) using a TESCAN MIRA II instrument at magnifications of 3000× and 5000× to observe morphological changes in the soil matrix caused by the additives. Complementary EDS analysis was conducted on selected points within the SEM images using FESEM technology to qualitatively assess the elemental distribution and composition.

Results and discussion

Standard compaction tests

In this research, a series of standard Proctor compaction tests were conducted to evaluate the impact of EAF slag and WTS replacement on the OMC and MDD of highly expansive clay. The variations in MDD and OMC across different percentages of EAF slag and WTS replacement are represented graphically in Fig. 3a and b, respectively.

a MDD and b OMC values of highly expansive clay stabilized with various contents of EAF slag and WTS.

Figure 3a shows an increase in MDD from 1.37 g/cm³ to 1.4 g/cm³ with the increase in EAF slag content up to 30%. This increase in MDD is primarily attributed to the greater specific gravity of EAF slag relative to natural clay, which causes finer clay particles to fill the voids within the EAF slag, resulting in greater densification. Furthermore, the inclusion of up to 20% WTS replacement also led to an increase in MDD, likely due to the void-filling capacity of WTS between soil particles, as well as the stabilizing effects of minerals present in WTS, which improved the inter-particle bonding and enhanced the overall soil structure, contributing to better compaction20,39.

Figure 3b demonstrates that with the incorporation of EAF slag and WTS replacement, the OMC decreased. The reduction in OMC with the addition of EAF slag up to 30% and WTS replacement between 0 and 20% is ascribed to several factors. The lower Specific Surface Area (SSA) of these additives compared to clay particles reduces the soil’s water retention capacity, thereby lowering the water needed for optimal compaction. Additionally, replacing clay, which has a high water absorption capacity, with EAF slag and WTS reduces the overall moisture retention capacity of the soil. These additives also increase the compactibility of the soil by occupying interparticle voids, thereby reducing the water demand required to attain the target compaction. Moreover, the increased compaction and reduced pore volume in the soil-additive mixture further decrease the water needed to fill the voids, leading to a lower OMC. Therefore, the reduction in OMC is linked to the diminished water retention capacity and the improved compaction properties of the soil following the incorporation of these materials13.

Unconfined compression tests

Effect of EAF slag on UCS

Figure 4 illustrates the UCS of untreated soil and soil samples treated with varying EAF slag contents, cured for 7, 28, and 56 days. As the EAF slag content increases from 0% to 30%, a concomitant general increasing trend in compressive strength is observed across all curing durations, indicating the beneficial impact of EAF slag addition on soil stabilization. This increase in strength is attributed to the stabilizing effect of slag, which improves the structural stability of the soil.

Variation of UCS for samples containing various EAF slag contents at different curing times.

The initial reduction in UCS observed with 10% EAF slag content at 7 and 28 days, followed by an increase at 56 days, may be due to delayed chemical reactions and gradual structural adjustments within the soil-slag matrix. In the early curing stages (7 and 28 days), pozzolanic reactions between the CaO in the EAF slag and SiO₂ and Al₂O₃ in the soil may be incomplete. At this point, gel structures such as calcium-silicate-hydrate (C-S-H) and calcium-aluminate-hydrate (C-A-H), which are primary contributors to strength development, have not yet fully formed, resulting in lower initial strength due to insufficient stable bonds69. Additionally, the 10% EAF slag content may not have introduced enough CaO to effectively stimulate pozzolanic reactions during these early stages.

As curing time extends to 28 and 56 days, UCS increases across all slag contents. This trend suggests that, over time, continued chemical reactions between slag and soil components contribute to strength gains. By 56 days, the UCS reaches its peak, with values of 1561 kPa and 2208 kPa at 20% and 30% slag content, respectively, as pozzolanic and hydration reactions become more complete, forming stable gel products like C-S-H and C-A-H. These gels enhance interparticle bonding, substantially increasing soil strength. Thus, with longer curing times, even lower slag percentages can exhibit stabilizing effects68.

The highest UCS value is observed with 30% slag content at 56 days, highlighting that higher slag content combined with extended curing time can achieve optimal soil stabilization. For instance, the UCS for untreated soil (0% slag) was 747 kPa, highlighting the significant improvement observed with 30% slag after 56 days of curing (2208 kPa).

As highlighted in the UCS test results (Fig. 4), the inclusion of EAF slag up to 30% significantly improved the compressive strength of treated soils. This range of EAF slag content was therefore considered practical and effective for pavement applications. The obtained UCS values of 1561 kPa (20% EAF slag) and 2208 kPa (30% EAF slag) after 56 days of curing directly correspond to the strength requirements outlined in international guidelines. For instance, according to Transport Research Laboratory (TRL)82, a minimum UCS of 0.75–1.5 MPa is required for sub-base layers, while 1.5–3.0 MPa is prescribed for stabilized road-base layers. Similarly, the US Army83 manual specifies comparable UCS thresholds for stabilized base and sub-base courses, ensuring durability under heavy traffic. On the other hand, the Public Works Department (PWD)58 guideline recommends a UCS of not less than 2.9 MPa for cement-stabilized base courses, highlighting stricter requirements for base compared to sub-base layers.

Within this context, the 20% EAF slag content already fulfills the UCS requirement for rigid pavement subgrades (1379 kPa), while the 30% EAF slag content exceeds the flexible pavement subgrade/sub-base threshold (1724 kPa) as per TRL82. It should also be noticed that extending the EAF slag content beyond 30% would not be economically viable, as it would increase material costs disproportionately and essentially replace the soil matrix rather than stabilize it. Moreover, previous studies in the literature have also commonly adopted EAF slag replacement levels within the range of 20–30%, reinforcing the practical and technical rationale for limiting the maximum inclusion to this range49,68. Although the results indicate a continuous increase in strength up to 30%, this value represents the upper bound of the investigated range rather than a confirmed absolute optimum. Further increases in slag content may lead to diminishing returns, potential brittleness, and reduced workability due to excessive replacement of the soil matrix. Consequently, the adoption of 30% EAF slag content not only fulfills the required strength criteria for pavement layers but also aligns with sustainable engineering practices.

Effect of WTS replacement on UCS

Figure 5 presents the UCS results of soil specimens mixed with varying proportions of EAF slag (10, 20, and 30%) and different replacement percentages of WTS (0, 5, 10, 20, and 30%) at curing times of 7, 28, and 56 days. In the initial processing phase (7 days), the samples containing no WTS replacement (0%) exhibit the lowest UCS values across all EAF slag proportions, as shown in Fig. 5a. This suggests a potential deficiency in stabilization, indicating that the introduction of WTS may be necessary to enhance performance due to the absence of pozzolanic active ingredients. As the WTS replacement percentage increases to 20%, a concomitant improvement in compressive strength is observed, likely due to the reactivity of compounds within the WTS with both the EAF slag and the clay matrix, resulting in stronger inter-particle bonds. This interaction promotes the formation of stable gel phases, such as C–S–H and C–A–H. However, the relatively high loss on ignition (LOI) value of WTS, as shown in Table 2, indicates the presence of organic matter. This may negatively influence strength development at higher replacement levels by interfering with cementitious bonding and delaying pozzolanic reactions. This effect is reflected in the observed reduction in compressive strength at 30% WTS replacement. A high concentration of organic matter in the WTS may further hinder robust bonding mechanisms.

Variations of UCS for clay samples treated with various EAF slag and WTS replacement contents after a 7, b 28, and c 56 days of curing.

Figure 5b indicates that at 28 days, all samples exhibit improved UCS values compared to the samples tested at 7 days. The combination of 30% EAF slag and 20% WTS replacement yields the highest UCS of approximately 2145 kPa, representing optimal conditions for soil stabilization. At this stage, the synthesis of gel products occurs more effectively, contributing to enhanced compressive strength. The high EAF slag content, in conjunction with the 20% WTS replacement, fosters a stable structure with improved internal bonding. Nevertheless, further increasing the WTS proportion beyond 20% results in a decline in UCS values, likely due to compositional imbalances. This may lead to weak and incomplete bonds, reducing strength rather than enhancing it. Overall, these findings indicate that, similar to 7-day cured samples, at 28 days of curing, the optimum mixture comprising 30% EAF slag and 20% WTS replacement creates favorable conditions for strong inter-particle bonds within the soil structure.

Figure 5c reveals that at 56 days of curing, the samples achieve their maximum UCS of approximately 2695 kPa, signifying the completion of chemical reactions and full stabilization of the soil matrix. Consistent with previous observations, this peak strength is achieved with the optimal blend of 30% EAF slag and 20% WTS replacement, while increasing the WTS proportion to 30% results in a strength reduction. The peak UCS occurs at 56 days, following complete pozzolanic interactions leading to well-formed stable gel products throughout the soil matrix27. The ideal ratio of 30% EAF slag and 20% WTS replacement ensures optimal chemical reactions and the establishment of a robust structure. Conversely, elevating the WTS replacement content to 30% results in decreased compressive strength, likely attributable to excessive organic matter disrupting the balance between stabilizers.

Effects of EAF slag and WTS replacement on energy adsorption capacity (Eu)

Figure 6 depicts the variation of energy absorption capacity (Eu) in mixtures containing different slag contents (10%, 20%, and 30%) and various WTS replacement ratios (0%, 5%, 10%, 20%, and 30%) over curing durations of 7, 28, and 56 days. At 7 days, Eu generally increases with higher slag contents across all WTS replacement levels, with mixtures containing 30% slag consistently exhibiting superior performance compared to those with lower slag contents. For instance, at 0% WTS replacement, Eu increases from approximately 7 kJ/m³ with 10% slag to 13 kJ/m³ with 30% slag, highlighting the early-stage benefits of increased slag incorporation. This trend is consistent with previous studies, which reported improved mechanical properties and reduced porosity at similar slag dosages70,84. Furthermore, at a constant slag dosage, Eu improves with WTS replacement up to 20%, especially in mixtures containing 30% slag; however, a decline at 30% WTS replacement suggests that excessive WTS may introduce additional porosity and structural heterogeneity, adversely affecting energy absorption.

Eu values of clay stabilized with 10%, 20%, and 30% EAF slag, partially replaced with various contents of WTS, after 7, 28, and 56 days of curing.

After 28 days of curing, a significant improvement in Eu is shown across all mixtures, attributed to ongoing hydration and pozzolanic reactions. Mixtures with 30% slag maintain the highest Eu values, reaching approximately 25 kJ/m³ at 20% WTS replacement. While the trend of increasing Eu with WTS replacement up to 20% continues, the difference between 10% and 20% WTS replacement becomes less pronounced, indicating diminishing marginal benefits of WTS addition at this curing stage. A persistent reduction in Eu at 30% WTS replacement further confirms the adverse effects of excessive WTS content.

By 56 days, all mixtures exhibit further improvements in Eu, highlighting the essential contribution of extended curing to improving mechanical performance. The mixture with 30% slag and 20% WTS replacement achieves the maximum Eu, exceeding 31 kJ/m³, demonstrating a strong synergistic effect between slag and optimal WTS replacement content under prolonged curing conditions. Even mixtures with lower slag contents display notable enhancements compared to earlier curing ages, although the superior performance of higher slag content remains evident. These findings collectively suggest that increasing slag content and extending curing duration substantially improve energy absorption capacity, while a WTS replacement level of approximately 20% optimizes matrix densification and load distribution. Conversely, excessive WTS replacement content (30%) tends to deteriorate the matrix integrity, resulting in lower Eu values. Therefore, it can be concluded that the incorporation of 30% slag and 20% WTS replacement, combined with sufficient curing time, provides the most effective strategy for enhancing the toughness and impact resistance of stabilized soils. Overall, the observed trends in this study are in agreement with previous research findings49,89.

Effects of EAF slag and WTS replacement on secant modulus of deformation (E50)

Figure 7 illustrates the evolution of secant modulus (E50) for various combinations of slag (10%, 20%, 30%) and WTS replacement levels (0%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 30%) over curing periods of 7, 28, and 56 days. A persistent upward trend in E50 is observed over the curing period for all mixtures. For instance, the specimen containing 30% slag and 20% WTS replacement exhibits a marked increase in stiffness, with E50 rising from 810 MPa at 7 days to 1040 MPa at 28 days, and further to 1260 MPa at 56 days. The continued advancement of these reactions over time has also been confirmed by previous studies, which report significant gains in strength and modulus with extended curing durations12,54,86.

E50 values of clay stabilized with 10%, 20%, and 30% EAF slag, partially replaced with various contents of WTS, after 7, 28, and 56 days of curing.

The role of slag content is also clearly evident in the results. At 28 days, samples incorporating 10%, 20%, and 30% slag (with 0% WTS replacement) achieved E50 values of 444 MPa, 631 MPa, and 703 MPa, respectively. This trend confirms the positive correlation between slag dosage and stiffness enhancement. The underlying mechanism involves the activation of slag in alkaline environments, which promotes the production of additional C-S-H gels. These compounds enhance the interfacial transition zone and reduce overall porosity, thereby boosting mechanical performance. However, However, some studies have noted that slag-rich mixtures may exhibit lower early-age strength due to delayed reaction kinetics, followed by substantial gains at later stages12.

The effect of WTS replacement is more complex. At 28 days and a constant slag content of 20%, increasing WTS replacement from 0% to 20% leads to a substantial increase in E50 from 631 MPa to 1037 MPa, indicating improved matrix densification and particle interlock. Nonetheless, a further increase to 30% WTS replacement results in a noticeable drop in E50 to 748 MPa, suggesting that excessive WTS may introduce internal voids or disrupt the bonding network, thereby weakening the soil structure. The maximum recorded E50 value is 1267 MPa, corresponding to the mixture with 30% slag and 20% WTS replacement at 56 days of curing, highlighting a synergistic effect on long-term stiffness. Conversely, the lowest E50 value of 383 MPa is observed in the sample with 10% slag and 30% WTS replacement at 7 days, underscoring the importance of adequate slag content and sufficient curing for effective stabilization.

These findings suggest that an optimized combination of 30% slag and 20% WTS replacement, coupled with extended curing, provides the most favorable conditions for improving the stiffness of expansive soils. Conversely, insufficient slag content or excessive WTS adversely affects mechanical performance and should be carefully controlled in practical applications.

Ultrasonic tests

UPV test is a commonly applied non-destructive technique for assessing the stiffness and density of stabilized soils. This technique measures the velocity of primary waves traveling through a soil specimen, providing insight into its mechanical integrity. The UPV values are highly contingent on the elastic properties and density of the material, making it a reliable indicator of soil quality and stabilization efficiency. Figure 9 illustrates the UPV values of untreated clay and the soil treated with varying slag and WTS replacement levels after curing periods of 7, 28, and 56 days.

UPV results for highly expansive clay samples treated with different EAF slag and WTS replacement contents at a 7, b 28, and c 56 days of curing.

As observed in Fig. 9a, UPV values increase with higher slag content across all WTS replacement levels. For example, at 0% WTS replacement, UPV values rise from approximately 968 m/s with 10% slag to 1,116 m/s with 20% slag, reaching 1,270 m/s at 30% slag.

This enhancement stems from pozzolanic reactions between slag and soil, which generate cementitious products such as C-S-H and C-A-H gels. These gels improve particle bonding and reduce porosity, facilitating ultrasonic wave propagation30,45. The particularly high UPV values observed for mixtures with 30% slag and 20% WTS replacement are associated with the combined effects of enhanced cementation and improved particle packing due to fine WTS particles filling voids within the soil matrix. However, at higher WTS replacement levels (e.g., 30%), UPV decreases from 1,416 m/s to approximately 1,342 m/s, indicating that excessive WTS adversely affects the soil structure, likely increasing porosity and hindering wave transmission.

Extended curing allows further hydration and pozzolanic reactions, enhancing structural integrity30. Notably, specimens with 30% slag consistently show the highest UPV values across all WTS levels, confirming slag’s major role in soil stabilization. Additionally, improved particle bonding due to slag-WTS interaction enhances soil cohesion, resulting in increased stiffness and wave velocity. However, at lower slag contents (10% and 20%), WTS presence causes more pronounced UPV variability, suggesting that optimal slag-WTS combinations are necessary for maximum soil stiffness.

The highest UPV values occur in the sample with 30% slag and 20% WTS replacement, reaching approximately 1,593 m/s. The increase in UPV between 28 and 56 days is more significant for samples with lower slag content, indicating that extended curing is crucial for strength development when slag dosage is limited61. The peak UPV at 30% slag and 20% WTS suggests this combination achieves optimal stabilization performance. This improvement corresponds with long-term strength gains due to continued hydration and secondary pozzolanic reactions that produce denser, more rigid soil structures. Conversely, WTS replacement exceeding 20% results in UPV decline, likely caused by increased porosity, organic matter interference, and structural heterogeneity disrupting cementitious bonding.

Overall, the results confirm that slag effectively enhances soil stiffness, as reflected in higher UPV values. While WTS contributes positively at moderate replacement levels by improving matrix densification, excessive WTS content negatively impacts UPV due to its organic nature and porosity increase. Therefore, achieving an optimal balance between slag and WTS is critical for maximizing UPV and soil stiffness.

Swelling potential tests

Highly expansive clays, known for their significant volume changes upon moisture variation, pose a major challenge to the stability of foundations, pavements, and other structures. Excessive swelling can lead to surface heaving, cracking, and even structural failure, making it crucial to develop effective stabilization methods50. One promising solution involves the use of chemical stabilizers and reinforcing materials, such as EAF slag and WTS, which have been investigated in this study.

Figure 11 illustrates the swelling potential of both untreated clayey soil and samples treated with optimal percentages of EAF slag and WTS under free swelling and 12 kPa surcharge conditions. Under free swelling conditions, the untreated soil exhibited a swelling potential of 270%, posing a severe risk to structural integrity. It should be noted that the high free swelling value is attributed to both the intrinsic properties of the clay and the testing conditions. The specimens were prepared in a remolded and compacted state at optimum moisture content (OMC) and maximum dry density (MDD). Under the applied low surcharge pressure of 1 kPa, the soil was allowed to swell with minimal confinement, representing its maximum swelling potential. These controlled preparation conditions ensure the reproducibility of the results and explain the high swelling magnitude observed.

Swelling potential for untreated soil, soil treated with 30% EAF slag, and soil treated with 30% EAF slag + 20% WTS replacement after 7, 28, and 56 days of curing: (a) free swelling; (b) 12 kPa surcharge; (c) final swelling potential.

However, incorporating 30% EAF slag into the expansive clay led to a significant reduction in swelling potential, decreasing to 167% after 7 days, 99% after 28 days, and ultimately 21% after 56 days of curing. This significant reduction in swelling potential can be caused by a number of interrelated mechanisms. Firstly, the introduction of EAF slag alters the soil’s physical and chemical properties by promoting cementation reactions. These reactions lead to the formation of cementitious compounds, such as C-S-H and C-A-H, which strengthen the soil structure and reduce its ability to absorb and retain water. As a result, the treated soil experiences less expansion when exposed to moisture63.

Another key factor contributing to the decreased swelling potential is the replacement of expansive clay minerals with non-swelling particles from the EAF slag. Expansive clays, such as montmorillonite, have a high affinity for water due to their layered structure and large surface area. When exposed to moisture, these clay minerals absorb water molecules, causing significant swelling. However, by partially replacing these clay minerals with stable, non-expansive EAF slag particles, the overall water absorption capacity of the soil decreases, thereby limiting its expansion. Additionally, the incorporation of EAF slag leads to cation exchange reactions, which further stabilize the soil structure. Expansive clays typically contain high concentrations of sodium ions, which contribute to their high swelling potential by increasing the thickness of the diffuse double layer (DDL) surrounding clay particles. When EAF slag is added, calcium ions (Ca²⁺) from the slag replace sodium ions (Na⁺) through cation exchange, reducing the repulsive forces between clay particles. This leads to a more compact and stable soil matrix with lower swelling potential6,47. Over time, the prolonged curing process enhances these stabilization effects. As the hydration and pozzolanic reactions continue, more cementitious compounds are formed, further reducing porosity and improving soil strength64. The continued reduction in swelling potential from 7 to 56 days indicates that these chemical reactions are ongoing, leading to a progressively denser and more stable soil matrix. Furthermore, the mechanical interlocking of EAF slag particles with the clay matrix significantly contributes to minimizing expansion. The rough, angular particles of EAF slag provide additional resistance to volumetric changes by physically constraining the movement of clay particles. This reinforcement effect helps to mitigate swelling, particularly under external loading conditions such as the 12 kPa surcharge situation examined in this study.

The incorporation of 20% WTS into the soil-slag mixtures has led to a significant decline in swelling potential, reducing it to 106%, 50%, and ultimately 8% after 7, 28, and 56 days of curing, respectively. This substantial improvement can be ascribed to the combined influence of WTS and EAF slag, which work together to enhance soil stabilization.

The interaction between these materials promotes the formation of cementitious compounds that bind soil particles more effectively, reducing porosity and limiting water absorption. Additionally, WTS contributes to cation exchange processes, replacing expansive sodium ions with calcium and aluminum ions, which helps to compact the soil matrix and minimize swelling. Furthermore, the fine-grained nature of WTS allows it to fill microvoids within the soil structure, improving particle interlocking and overall density. As curing progresses, the continuous hydration and pozzolanic reactions further strengthen the treated soil, resulting in a more durable and stable composition with superior resistance to moisture-induced expansion.

The imposition of an overburden pressure of 12 kPa plays a crucial role in further mitigating the swelling behavior of expansive soil. This pressure accelerates the stabilization process, restricting excessive expansion within a shorter period. However, despite the presence of this surcharge load, untreated soil still exhibited a substantial swelling potential of 104%. This indicates that external pressure alone is insufficient to fully suppress the swelling tendency, which, if left unaddressed, could lead to serious structural complications, including foundation instability and pavement distress. To counteract these challenges, the incorporation of 30% EAF slag significantly improved soil stability by reducing the swelling potential from 99% to 24% after 28 days of curing. Additionally, the introduction of 20% WTS further enhanced the soil’s resistance to expansion, bringing the swelling potential down from 50% to a negligible and structurally acceptable 18%. These modifications suggest that the combination of EAF slag and WTS not only provides a dual mechanism for chemical and physical stabilization but also enhances the overall integrity of the soil matrix. Most importantly, after 56 days of curing, the swelling in the treated soil was completely controlled, demonstrating the long-term efficiency of this waste-based stabilization approach. The sustained reduction in swelling highlights the progressive nature of cementitious and pozzolanic reactions within the soil, leading to a denser and more durable structure over time. These findings reinforce the potential of utilizing readily available industrial by-products as a sustainable solution for addressing the challenges posed by expansive soils. By adopting such innovative stabilization methods, the construction industry can not only enhance soil performance but also contribute to environmental sustainability by repurposing waste materials in infrastructure projects.

Microstructural analysis

SEM

As depicted in Fig. 12a, the microstructure of expansive clay soil consists of a highly porous and loosely packed structure with two distinct types of pores: intra-assemblage and inter-assemblage pores. The intra-assemblage pores, which are the smallest, are located within the individual clay particles, whereas the inter-assemblage pores, the largest, are formed between clusters of clay particles1. The presence of these pores significantly influences the water absorption capacity and swelling potential of the soil21,67. The high porosity allows water molecules to penetrate deep into the structure, leading to a substantial increase in volume expansion when hydrated. Moreover, the loosely arranged flaky clay particles contribute to weak interparticle bonding, making the soil highly susceptible to deformation under external loading. Microcracks are also observed within the matrix, which can act as potential failure planes and reduce mechanical strength34. This microstructure is characteristic of expansive soils, where the presence of active clay minerals and a large specific surface area promotes water adsorption and swelling through the development of a thick diffuse double layer (DDL), resulting in significant volumetric instability.

SEM micrographs and corresponding porosity analysis of a, d untreated expansive clay, b, e clay treated with 30% EAF slag, and c, f clay treated with 30% EAF slag + 20% WTS replacement.

As illustrated in Fig. 12b, the microstructure of expansive clay stabilized with 30% slag exhibits a denser and more compact structure compared to the untreated sample. The previously observed high porosity and loosely packed arrangement have been significantly reduced due to the formation of cementitious bonds between soil particles. This stabilization effect is primarily attributed to the reaction between the slag and clay minerals, which results in pozzolanic and hydration reactions leading to the generation of secondary cementitious compounds such as C-S-H and C-A-H24. Additionally, the intra-assemblage pores, initially more pronounced in the untreated sample, appear to have diminished, suggesting an improvement in particle interlocking and densification. The presence of flaky and aggregated structures, commonly associated with expansive clay, has also been altered, with a noticeable reduction in inter-assemblage pores, further contributing to enhanced strength and reduced swelling potential. Moreover, the smoother and more compact appearance of the stabilized sample indicates the binding effect of slag, which improves the mechanical integrity of the soil. Adding slag also helps change the clay particles from a weak and scattered form into a flocculated structure, supporting the development of cement-like materials. The presence of a gel-like C-S-H substance within the voids further enhances bonding between soil particles, leading to increased cohesion and reduced porosity49,68. These structural transformations validate the effectiveness of slag as a stabilizing agent in minimizing swelling potential and enhancing the durability of expansive clay soils.

As illustrated in Fig. 12c, the microstructure of expansive clay stabilized with 30% slag and 20% WTS replacement reveals significant changes compared to both untreated and slag-only stabilized samples. The structure is denser and more compact, indicating the formation of cementitious compounds due to reactions between slag, WTS, and clay minerals. These reactions lead to the generation of C-S-H, C-A-H, and C-A-S-H gels, which enhance interparticle bonding and reduce porosity27. Compared to the slag-only stabilized sample, the incorporation of WTS introduces additional fine particles, which fill residual voids and further enhance microstructural integrity. Additionally, the presence of nanosilica and reactive alumina in WTS contributes to increased cementitious compound formation, leading to enhanced strength and durability. SEM observations confirm that the surfaces of clay particles are coated with C-S-H, C-A-H, and C-A-S-H gels, providing additional cohesion and stability. The increase in cementitious gel formation, particularly C-A-S-H phases, suggests improved mechanical performance of the soil. Studies indicate that these gels contribute to higher compressive strength and durability, ensuring a more stable and resilient soil matrix14,91. Furthermore, the role of cementitious products (C–S–H, C–A–H, and C–A–S–H) in improving interparticle bonding, reducing porosity, and limiting water ingress is directly linked to the observed improvements in strength, stiffness, and swelling behavior.

For the untreated clay sample, the calcium content was very low (3.03 wt%), while silica (40.98 wt%) and alumina (13.89 wt%) were dominant. The low Ca/Si ratio (0.07) indicates the absence of significant calcium-rich reaction products and confirms that the natural soil mainly consists of aluminosilicate clay minerals such as kaolinite, illite, or montmorillonite. In this condition, the soil particles are weakly bonded through electrostatic attractions and adsorbed water layers, resulting in poor engineering properties.

Following the addition of 30% EAF slag, the calcium content increased substantially to 24.25 wt%, accompanied by an increase in the Ca/Si ratio to 0.63. EAF slag is rich in free lime (CaO), calcium silicates, and other alkaline oxides. Upon contact with water, free lime undergoes hydration reactions, increasing the alkalinity of the system and promoting dissolution of silica and alumina from clay mineral surfaces. The dissolved silicate and aluminate species may subsequently react with calcium ions to form secondary cementitious products, including C–S–H, C–A–H, and calcium aluminosilicate hydrate phases.

These reaction products are believed to contribute to pore filling, particle bonding, and microstructural densification. The increase in the Ca/(Si + Al) ratio from 0.05 in untreated clay to 0.47 after slag treatment is consistent with the possible development of calcium-rich cementitious gels.

In the specimens containing WTS replacement, the calcium concentration further increased to 25.55 wt% and the Ca/Si ratio reached 0.68. Simultaneously, the Al/Si ratio slightly increased, indicating additional alumina participation in the reaction process. WTS may contain reactive silica, alumina, and fine particles that provide nucleation sites for hydration products. These fine particles may enhance dissolution kinetics and promote pozzolanic reactions. Moreover, the additional reactive surfaces provided by WTS may facilitate ion exchange reactions between calcium ions and clay mineral cations, contributing to particle flocculation and aggregation.

Additionally, carbonation reactions may occur during curing due to atmospheric carbon dioxide interaction with calcium hydroxide, potentially contributing to pore filling and matrix densification. Overall, the EDS and EDS-MAP analyses suggest that the stabilization mechanism may involve hydration, pozzolanic, ion-exchange, and carbonation reactions. The increase in calcium-related ratios, together with SEM observations and XRD results, is consistent with the possible development of C–S–H and C–A–S–H type reaction products, which may contribute to improved bonding, denser microstructure, and enhanced engineering performance of the stabilized clay samples containing EAF slag and WTS replacement.

However, the identification of specific cementitious phases is supported through combined interpretation with SEM and EDS analyses rather than XRD alone.

Environmental considerations

In stabilized systems, the formation of cementitious products such as C–S–H and C–A–H may contribute to the immobilization of heavy metals, thereby reducing their mobility and potential leaching. However, the long-term environmental behavior of such stabilized materials depends on factors including pH conditions, weathering, and exposure environment. Therefore, further investigation through standardized leaching tests (e.g., TCLP) is recommended to evaluate the environmental performance of the proposed mixtures prior to large-scale application. This aspect represents a limitation of the current study and should be addressed in future research.

In addition to environmental considerations, the use of EAF slag and WTS may offer potential sustainability benefits by reducing reliance on conventional stabilizers such as cement and lime, which are associated with high energy consumption and carbon emissions. The reuse of these industrial by-products may also contribute to waste minimization and resource efficiency. However, a comprehensive assessment comparing the proposed method with traditional stabilization techniques in terms of cost, energy demand, carbon footprint, and environmental impact was beyond the scope of this study and is recommended for future investigation.

Accordingly, although the present study demonstrates promising engineering performance, additional environmental validation is required before confirming the environmental sustainability or field-scale applicability of the stabilized soil system.

Implications for geotechnical applications and future research needs

This study examined the mechanical performance and microstructural evolution of expansive clay treated with EAF slag and WTS. The results revealed considerable enhancements in strength, stiffness, and swelling resistance. However, it should be noted that these outcomes are primarily based on controlled laboratory-scale tests. While the findings highlight the strong potential of the proposed stabilization method, further investigation is required before it can be confidently implemented in practical engineering applications.

It is also important to note that the optimum mixture identified (30% EAF slag and 20% WTS) represents a laboratory-based condition aimed at achieving maximum performance; however, such a relatively high replacement level may be limited in field applications due to practical and economic considerations. In particular, the present study does not include long-term durability assessments (e.g., wetting–drying or freeze–thaw cycles) or environmental validation through standardized leaching tests, which are critical for evaluating field performance and environmental safety.

Specifically, future studies should include a more comprehensive evaluation of critical engineering properties such as shear strength, California Bearing Ratio (CBR), permeability, compressibility, and long-term durability under field conditions. These parameters are essential for validating the laboratory results and ensuring reliable performance under realistic environmental and loading scenarios. Therefore, the current findings should be regarded as indicative of performance trends rather than conclusive evidence for field-scale application. The observed improvements, including substantial increases in UCS and stiffness along with a significant reduction in swelling potential, suggest that the combined use of EAF slag and WTS has strong potential for practical use in geotechnical engineering. Although durability tests were not conducted, the reduction in porosity and improved particle bonding observed in the microstructural analysis suggest enhanced resistance to environmental degradation mechanisms such as moisture variation and cyclic loading. In particular, these materials may be effectively utilized in applications such as road subgrades, embankments, and shallow foundation systems. Moreover, the reduction in porosity observed in the microstructural analysis indicates enhanced durability and potential suitability for applications requiring improved resistance to moisture ingress. Despite these promising outcomes, the proposed applications are inferred from laboratory observations and limited testing conditions. The behavior of stabilized soils in real-world environments is influenced by additional factors such as environmental exposure, cyclic loading, and long-term degradation, which were not addressed in this study. Furthermore, although the use of EAF slag and WTS offers a sustainable alternative to conventional stabilizers, a more detailed evaluation of environmental and economic aspects is necessary. Accordingly, future research should focus on long-term field performance, durability under cyclic environmental conditions, and comprehensive environmental impact assessments, including leaching behavior and life-cycle analysis. In addition, large-scale field trials and advanced numerical modeling are recommended to validate the laboratory findings and to optimize the mixture design for practical and sustainable implementation.

Concluding remarks

Highly expansive clays are among the most problematic soil types in geotechnical engineering due to their excessive volumetric changes, which often compromise the stability of structures built on them. This research investigates the use of EAF slag and WTS as sustainable stabilizers for expansive clay soils. Various mixtures of EAF slag and WTS were investigated to assess their effects on the mechanical behavior, swelling potential, and microstructural characteristics of the treated soils. UCS, UPV, and swelling tests were conducted, along with SEM, EDS, and XRD analyses to provide microstructural insights. The results obtained from the experimental program can be summarized as follows:

The addition of EAF slag and WTS increased the MDD due to their higher specific gravity and void-filling ability. Meanwhile, the OMC decreased as these additives reduced the soil’s water retention and improved compatibility.
