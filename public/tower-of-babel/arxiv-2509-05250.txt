A strong–weak duality for the 1d long-range Ising model
Dario Benedetti, Edoardo Lauria, Dalimil Mazáč, Philine van Vliet

Abstract
We investigate the one-dimensional Ising model with long-range interactions decaying as 1 / r 1 + s 1/r^{1+s} . In the critical regime, for 1 / 2 ≤ s ≤ 1 1/2\leq s\leq 1 , this system realizes a family of nontrivial one-dimensional conformal field theories (CFTs), whose data vary continuously with s s . For s > 1 s>1 the model has instead no phase transition at finite temperature, as in the short-range case. In the standard field-theoretic description, involving a generalized free field with quartic interactions, the critical model is weakly coupled near s = 1 / 2 s=1/2 but strongly coupled in the vicinity of the short-range crossover at s = 1 s=1 . We introduce a dual formulation that becomes weakly coupled as s → 1 s\to 1 . Precisely at s = 1 s=1 , the dual description becomes an exactly solvable conformal boundary condition of the two-dimensional free scalar. We present a detailed study of the dual model and demonstrate its effectiveness by computing perturbatively the CFT data near s = 1 s=1 , up to next-to-next-to-leading order in 1 − s 1-s , by two independent approaches: (i) standard renormalization of our dual field-theoretic description and (ii) the analytic conformal bootstrap. The two methods yield complete agreement.

Introduction and motivation

An important source of insights and results in quantum field theory (QFT) is provided by IR dualities, where two different UV models share the same IR behavior. If the latter is described by a conformal field theory (CFT), the UV models provide alternative ways of constructing the same CFT as an IR limit.

While closely related to the concept of universality, the term “IR duality” is commonly reserved for a more specific and surprising case of universality, where the two UV models are defined in terms of completely different degrees of freedom.

In other words, IR dualities mimic exact dualities, such as the one between the compact free boson and the free photon in 3d, or that between the sine-Gordon model and the massive Thirring model in 2d
[
1
]
. However, in contrast to these examples, they are only true up to IR-irrelevant operators
[
2
]
.
Famous examples of IR dualities are the Seiberg duality
[
3
]
, the particle/vortex duality
[
4
,
5
]
, and the “web of dualities” for Chern-Simons-matter theories
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
.

One particularly useful feature of IR dualities is that they are often of the strong–weak type: one side is weakly coupled when the other is strongly coupled, and vice versa.
A notable example, proposed in
[
9
,
10
]
, occurs in the context of the long-range Ising (LRI) model in
d\geq 2
dimensions.
On the
\mathbb{Z}^{d}

\beta\,\mathcal{H}_{{}_{\rm LRI}}=\frac{\mathcal{J}}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{(\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j})^{2}}{|i-j|^{d+s}}\,,\quad\mathcal{J}>0\,,

(1.1)

where

In the continuum limit, for

, the LRI model is described by a generalized free field (GFF)

For

The dual description proposed in

In this article, we consider the

17

17
]

For

Indeed, the one-dimensional SRI model exhibits a phase transition only at zero temperature, where it is not a CFT but a topological theory, with constant correlation functions. This theory is equivalent to that of a single qubit with two degenerate ground states
|\pm\rangle
. The spin field is a topological operator acting as
\sigma|\pm\rangle=\pm|\pm\rangle

Thus, while the generalized free field

\sigma

A different perspective is therefore needed in order to capture the full operator spectrum and scaling behavior near the crossover to short range in one dimension.

Such a perspective has been provided in our recent work
[
21
]
, where we constructed a candidate dual description, weakly coupled near
s=1
. Our proposal was inspired by the old works of Anderson and Yuval
[
22
]
and of Kosterlitz
[
23

as the domain walls, namely the sites

Using their Coulomb gas model, and a primordial version of Wilsonian RG, they derived a system of beta functions and established a phase diagram at
s=1

1-s

In
[
21
]

1-s

s=1
as a seed, we have developed a perturbative analytic conformal bootstrap that reproduces and extends the renormalization group (RG) results, thus providing an independent check of the field theoretic model and of the fact that the fixed-point theory is a CFT.

In this paper, we expand on the construction of
[
21
]
, presenting more details and results on the IR duality between the 1d LRI model and our generalized Kondo model.
The main results of our work can be summarized as follows:

•

At
s=1
, we refine the known duality between LRI and Kondo, originally established by Anderson and Yuval. In particular, we show that the correct treatment requires restricting the Kondo model to its
U(1)
-singlet sector, which allows us to identify the full spectrum of the LRI CFT at
s=1
.

•

For all
s\leq 1
, we propose a field theory that is weakly coupled near the crossover, exactly solvable at
s=1
, that reduces to the Kondo model at
s=1
, and that upon perturbative expansion reproduces the AYK model. We derive several predictions for CFT data at next-to-leading or next-to-next-to-leading order in perturbation theory, thus showing that such model provides not only a conceptual framework to recast the AYK model as a field theory, but also a computational tool to treat the near-crossover regime with systematic perturbative methods.

•

We recover the same results – along with further predictions – through analytic conformal bootstrap. This approach uses only the CFT data from
s=1
, together with the existence of protected operators
\sigma
,
\chi
for all
s\leq 1
, and the assumption that the CFT data admit an asymptotic expansion in nonnegative powers of
\sqrt{1-s}
. Therefore, the bootstrap provides an independent validation of our proposed model at
s<1
, and together they represent strong evidence for the conformal invariance of the IR fixed point.

2.2
The 1d long-range Ising CFT

The critical 1d long-range Ising model is expected to develop symmetry under Möbius transformations of the line, and is thus described by a 1d CFT
[
33
]
. Informally, a 1d CFT is a theory living on a line, whose correlators transform covariantly under the group
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
of real fractional linear transformations

with

\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
symmetry and invariant vacuum would necessarily be topological as the stress tensor vanishes. Examples of 1d CFTs include conformal boundary conditions of 2d CFTs, conformal line defects in general-d CFTs, as well as the boundary duals of QFT in AdS
2
, and fixed points of 1d long-range models. We refer the reader to
[
35
,
36

37
,
38
]
for some previous literature on general aspects of 1d CFTs.

Besides the
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
and global
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry, the 1d LRI also features parity symmetry, acting on the line as
x\mapsto-x
, which combines with
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
to form
\mathrm{PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Note that in the context of line defects, parity is usually called S-parity
[
39
,
40
]
. Furthermore, 1d LRI is a reflection-positive theory, and therefore its analytic continuation to Lorentzian signature is unitary. We will now describe the bootstrap definition of a unitary 1d CFT with parity symmetry, and then discuss several nonperturbative features of the 1d LRI CFT, namely its protected operators and OPE ratios.

Firstly, the interacting fixed point still possesses a

global and parity symmetry. Secondly, the long-range nature of the fixed point implies that certain scaling dimensions are protected. Only the Gaussian part of the model is long-range, while the
\varphi^{4}
interaction is fully local. It follows that (
2.1
) is valid also in the IR for all
0<s\leq 1
.

Furthermore, as a consequence of the Schwinger-Dyson equations, we have the following identification (up to contact terms):

\langle\int{\rm d}y\,C^{-1}(x-y)\varphi(y)\dots\rangle=\langle\lambda_{4}\varphi(x)^{3}\;\dots\rangle\,.

(2.14)

Since the inverse covariance is not a differential operator for
s<2
, and its action coincides with a shadow transform
[
42
]
,
\varphi
and
\varphi^{3}
are two distinct primaries that
form a shadow pair
[
33
]
. It follows that the dimension of
\varphi^{3}
is also protected

\Delta_{\varphi^{3}}=1-\Delta_{\varphi}=(1+s)/2\,.

We will refer to these protected operators more abstractly as
\sigma
and
\chi
, with the understanding that in the
\varphi^{4}
description we have the identifications
\sigma\sim\varphi
and
\chi\sim\varphi^{3}
. In particular, we have
\Delta_{\sigma}=(1-s)/2
and
\Delta_{\chi}=(1+s)/2
for all
s\in[1/2,1]
.

Finally, since the Schwinger-Dyson equation effectively allows us to build
\chi
as the shadow transform of
\sigma
, it implies nonperturbative relations between various OPE coefficients
[
33
,
43
,
14
]
. Specifically, for any four primaries
\phi_{i}
,
\phi_{j}
,
\phi_{k}
,
\phi_{\ell}
, we have

\frac{c_{\sigma ij}c_{\chi k\ell}}{c_{\chi ij}c_{\sigma k\ell}}=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{\sigma}+\Delta_{i}-\Delta_{j}+a_{ij}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{\sigma}-\Delta_{i}+\Delta_{j}+a_{ij}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1-\Delta_{\sigma}+\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{\ell}+a_{k\ell}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1-\Delta_{\sigma}-\Delta_{k}+\Delta_{\ell}+a_{k\ell}}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1-\Delta_{\sigma}+\Delta_{i}-\Delta_{j}+a_{ij}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1-\Delta_{\sigma}-\Delta_{i}+\Delta_{j}+a_{ij}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{\sigma}+\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{\ell}+a_{k\ell}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{\sigma}-\Delta_{k}+\Delta_{\ell}+a_{k\ell}}{2}\right)}\,,

(2.15)

where
a_{ij}=[1-(-1)^{J_{i}+J_{j}}]/2=J_{i}+J_{j}\mod 2
. A derivation appears in
[
43
]
and in our Appendix
G
.

In the particular case
\phi_{i}=\chi
,
\phi_{k}=\sigma
,
\phi_{j}=\phi_{\ell}=\mathcal{O}
, for a general primary
\mathcal{O}
, this relation becomes

\displaystyle(c_{\sigma\chi\mathcal{O}})^{2}=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1+J_{\mathcal{O}}-\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}}{2}\right)^{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{1+\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}+J_{\mathcal{O}}-2\Delta_{\sigma}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{2\Delta_{\sigma}+\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}+J_{\mathcal{O}}-1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}+J_{\mathcal{O}}}{2}\right)^{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{2\Delta_{\sigma}-\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}+J_{\mathcal{O}}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{2-2\Delta_{\sigma}-\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}+J_{\mathcal{O}}}{2}\right)}c_{\sigma\sigma\mathcal{O}}c_{\chi\chi\mathcal{O}}\,.

(2.16)

When
\mathcal{O}
is odd under parity, Bose symmetry (
2.9
) implies
c_{\sigma\sigma\mathcal{O}}=c_{\chi\chi\mathcal{O}}=0
. Equation (
2.16
) then implies that either
c_{\sigma\chi\mathcal{O}}=0
or
\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}
is a positive even integer, so that a pole of the first gamma function in the numerator has a chance to cancel the vanishing factor
c_{\sigma\sigma\mathcal{O}}c_{\chi\chi\mathcal{O}}
. These protected operators can be thought of as double traces built out of
\sigma
and
\chi
, together with an odd number of derivatives. As the mean field description near
s=1/2
shows, the case with one derivative is a descendant of
\varphi^{4}
, and the protected operators thus have dimensions
4,6,8,\ldots
. See also
[
44
]
for a recent discussion of these operators from a different point of view.

Since there are no local conserved currents in the long-range model, we expect all other operators to get nontrivial anomalous dimensions, and thus lift any degeneracies present in the GFF. We refer the reader to
[
43
,
25
,
26
,
14
,
27
]
, for detailed perturbative results in the
\varphi^{4}
formulation and up to three loops in
\epsilon
-expansion.

2.3
Anderson-Yuval-Kosterlitz model

In this section, we review the relation between the partition function of the 1d LRI model for
s\lesssim 1
,

\displaystyle Z_{\rm LRI}=\sum_{\{\sigma_{i}\}}e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}_{\rm LRI}}\,,

(2.17)

and a Coulomb gas of kinks with alternating charges. The main idea, which goes back to the work of Anderson and Yuval in
[
22
]
, is to view the locations of the domain walls, i.e. the sites
i
where the spins
\sigma_{i}
flip sign (see fig.
2
) as the weakly coupled degrees of freedom for 1d LRI. We will refer to a domain wall configuration from
1
to
-1
(
-1
to
1
) as a kink (anti-kink). For
s\lesssim 1
, such domain walls are indeed diluted at low temperature, where the model is in the ordered phase.

For
s=1
, upon rewriting LRI in terms of kinks and anti-kinks,
Z_{\rm LRI}
is seen to be equivalent to the following Coulomb gas partition function
[
22
]
(see also the related previous work
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
and the review in
[
48

7
7

7

In appendix
B

formulation of LRI.

Z_{\rm Coulomb}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}g^{2n}\int_{{\rm I}_{2n}(a)}\big(\prod_{i=1}^{2n}\frac{{\rm d}x_{i}}{a}\big)\,e^{2\mathcal{J}\sum_{i<j}(-1)^{i-j}\log(|x_{i}-x_{j}|/a)}\;,

(2.18)

where positive and negative charges are associated to kinks and anti-kinks, respectively, and in accordance with the domain wall interpretation they need to alternate along the line.
The domain of integration is:

{\rm I}_{m}(a)=\{L/2\geq x_{1}\geq x_{2}\geq\ldots\geq x_{m}\geq-L/2\mid x_{i}-x_{i+1}\geq a\}\;.

(2.19)

The fugacity of the gas of kinks is
g=\exp(-\mathcal{K}-c\,\mathcal{J})
, for some constant
c
and with
\mathcal{K}
being the short-range coupling that is added to the LRI in order to have two independently-tunable couplings.
The expansion in powers of the fugacity should be valid at low temperatures (i.e. large
\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{K}
, or small
g
), where domains are large and the domain walls are thus very diluted.

In writing (
2.18
), a continuum limit has been implemented, while keeping a UV cutoff
a
(inherited from the lattice cutoff) in the form of a hard core repulsion.
8
8

8

The UV cutoff can actually be removed for
\mathcal{J}<1/2
, as the singularity at coinciding points is then integrable.

The IR regularization instead has been implemented by choosing a finite
L
. Physically, this can be seen as defining the LRI model on the infinite line, but with a strong magnetic field in
\mathbb{R}\backslash[-L/2,L/2]
, forcing all the spins to point in the same direction in the complement of the interval
[-L/2,L/2]
, and thus excluding kinks in that region.
9
9

9

Alternatively, one can define the model with periodic boundary conditions; however, one should keep in mind that in this case we should also change the argument of the logarithm as in
\log(\frac{\sin(\pi|r_{i}-r_{j}|/L)}{\pi a/L})
.

For
s\lesssim 1
, as found by Kosterlitz in
[
23
]
, rewriting LRI in terms of kinks and anti-kinks leads to a modification of the Coulomb gas, where the logarithms of

are replaced by a power-law potential:

Z_{\rm AYK}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}g^{2n}\int_{{\rm I}_{2n}(a)}\big(\prod_{i=1}^{2n}\frac{{\rm d}x_{i}}{a}\big)\,e^{2\mathcal{J}\sum_{i<j}(-1)^{i-j}(1-s)^{-1}((|x_{i}-x_{j}|/a)^{1-s}-1)}\;.

(2.20)

This modified Coulomb gas model, which reduces
Z_{\rm Coulomb}
for
s\to 1^{-}
, is what we call the AYK model.

From the AYK model, by means of a primordial version of the Wilsonian RG, AYK obtained the RG flow equations for the renormalized parameters
\mathcal{J}(a),g(a)
with respect to the cutoff length
a
:

\begin{split}\frac{dg}{d\log(a^{-1})}&=(\mathcal{J}-1)g+\mathcal{O}(g^{3})\,,\\
\frac{d\mathcal{J}}{d\log(a^{-1})}&=\mathcal{J}(4g^{2}+s-1)+\mathcal{O}(g^{4})\,.\end{split}

(2.21)

The flow diagram associated to such beta functions is displayed in fig.

At
s=1
,the associated flow has a similar structure to the BKT transition
[
48

: a line of fixed points at
g=0
, parametrized by
\mathcal{J}

\mathcal{J}-1
; the line ending at
g=0
and
\mathcal{J}=1
in the IR corresponds to the phase transition between order (
g=0

At
s<1
, the line of fixed points disappears, leaving only an isolated fixed point, which is the continuation of the
g=0
and
\mathcal{J}=1
fixed point of
s=1
.
One of our purposes will be to reproduce these RG equations from a modern perspective, in the language of CFT, and at the same time provide a framework allowing to systematically improve them and produce other results.

2.4
Insights from the 1d short-range Ising model

Another important insight for developing a theory of the LRI model near
s=1
comes from scrutinizing the possible physical content of the theory at
s>1
. As reviewed above, for
d>1
and
s\geq s^{*}
, the LRI crosses to the SRI universality plus a decoupled GFF sector
[
10
,
9
]
. What changes when
d=1
? As we argue below, the physics is bound to be not so different from that of the classical 1d SRI model, indeed:

1.

For 1d LRI, there is no phase transition at finite temperature for
s>1
, just as for the 1d SRI. This was established rigorously in
[
19
]
;

2.

At
s=1
, from eq. (
2.1
), the LRI field has a vanishing scaling dimension, thus matching that of the 1d SRI at zero temperature, e.g.
[
49
]
. We return to this point below.

At the same time, the physics cannot be exactly that of the classical 1d SRI model.
The classical 1d SRI model in a magnetic field
h
is given by the Hamiltonian

\beta\,\mathcal{H}_{{}_{\rm SRI}}=\frac{\mathcal{K}}{2}\sum_{i}(\sigma_{i+1}-\sigma_{i})^{2}-h\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\,,\quad\mathcal{K}>0\,,

(2.22)

and it is easily solved, in particular by transfer matrix method. The latter underlines the standard classical-to-quantum mapping
[
50
,
51
,
52
]
, and in the continuum limit we can think of the classical 1d SRI model as a quantum SRI in transverse field in zero dimensions, i.e. the quantum mechanics of a single Ising spin, with Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{Q}=-\gamma\,\hat{\sigma}_{1}-h\,\hat{\sigma}_{3}\;,

(2.23)

where
\hat{\sigma}_{a}
are the standard Pauli matrices and

2\gamma=\log\coth(\mathcal{K})=\xi^{-1}\;,

(2.24)

with
\xi
the correlation length of the classical Ising chain at
h=0
.
We see that the correlation length diverges only for
\gamma\to 0
, i.e.
\mathcal{K}\to+\infty
, corresponding to the zero temperature limit of the classical model.
The critical SRI model is thus located at
\gamma=h=0
, that is, it is described by a trivial quantum Hamiltonian.

Correlation functions of the classical Ising chain with periodic boundary conditions are mapped to thermal expectation values of time-ordered operators in the quantum theory, where time-dependent operators are defined by the imaginary-time evolution under
\hat{H}_{Q}
.
The imaginary time and the inverse temperature of the quantum model are identified with the position
x
and the extension
L
of the classical model, respectively.
At the critical point, the two-point function of the classical Ising field then simply becomes

\langle\sigma(x)\sigma(0)\rangle=\frac{1}{Z_{Q}}\tr\left[e^{-L\hat{H}_{Q}}\hat{\sigma}_{3}(x)\hat{\sigma}_{3}(0)\right]\quad\xrightarrow{\gamma,h\to 0}\quad\frac{1}{2}\tr\left[\hat{\sigma}_{3}^{2}\right]=1\;.

(2.25)

Therefore, the Ising field becomes a field with vanishing scaling dimension, and operator representation given by the Pauli matrix
\hat{\sigma}_{3}
.
Notice that when approaching the critical theory along the
h
-axis at
\gamma=0
, we select (for
L\to\infty
) as ground state of
\hat{H}_{Q}
the eigenvector of
\hat{\sigma}_{3}
with eigenvalue
\pm 1
, depending on the sign of
h
, hence the spontaneous magnetization
\langle\sigma\rangle
is discontinuous at zero temperature.

Given the facts we just recalled about the SRI, we would expect that at
s\geq 1
the critical LRI, with classical Hamiltonian (
1.1
), would be described by the degrees of freedom of a
\mathbb{C}^{2}
Hilbert space, with trivial correlators.
However, as in
d>1
, this cannot be the end of the story, because it would imply that at the crossover some operators would disappear from the spectrum.
For example, by continuity of spectrum, at
s=1
we should find two
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-odd operators with opposite parity and of dimension exactly equal to one, corresponding to
\varphi^{3}
and
\partial\varphi
in the
\varphi^{4}
description (see Section
2.2
), but clearly the SRI alone cannot provide such operators.
10
10

10

Following
[
9
,
10
]
, we could try to account for the missing operators by adding to the 1d SRI a GFF
\chi
of scaling dimension
\Delta_{\chi}=(1+s)/2
, that could be identified with
\varphi^{3}
, and that should be decoupled from the spin degree of freedom at
s\geq 1
. However, we would still be missing the parity-odd operator corresponding to
\partial\varphi
, as it would be unclear how to introduce derivatives of
\hat{\sigma}_{3}
.
Moreover, it would be unclear how to couple the two sectors at
s<1
in order to flow to the LRI CFT: if for example, combining insights from the above reminder of 1d SRI and from
[
9
,
10
]
, we introduce the defect operator
\tr[\exp(h\,\int{\rm d}x\,\hat{\sigma}_{3}\,\chi(x))]
in correlators over the
\mathbb{C}^{2}+
GFF theory, we obtain a trivial result, as the trace vanishes.

The solution, to be presented in the following section, will come from matching the theory at
s=1
to the AYK model.

3
The weakly coupled model near crossover

As reviewed above, the dynamics of 1d LRI model at
s\lesssim 1
is described by a gas of kinks-antikinks with alternating charges. For
s=1
, the same Coulomb gas can also be obtained as a perturbative expansion of the Kondo model
[
22
]
. In its bosonized version, the latter is very similar to the boundary sine-Gordon model, except for the presence of Pauli matrices in its action, enforcing the alternating order of positive and negative charges in (
2.19
).
11
11

11

The relation between Kondo and boundary sine-Gordon models has been elucidated in
[
53
]
.
We refer also to
[
54
,
55
,
56
]
for reviews of the CFT approach to Kondo and boundary sine-Gordon models.

In this section, we introduce a model that, generalizing the bosonized Kondo model, provides a field theory whose perturbative expansion reproduces the AYK model also at
s<1
, and that has the correct operator content to match the
\varphi^{4}
description.
We elucidate various aspects of such model, arguing that even at
s=1
the observation by Anderson and Yuval needs a refinement, as the Kondo model leads to a larger spectrum than the LRI.

3.1
Compact generalized free fields with negative scaling dimension

As a preliminary step towards introducing the full dual model for 1d LRI near
s=1
, we first introduce a dual GFF, that will provide a basis around which to construct the perturbative treatment. From here on we set
s=1-\delta
, with
0\leq\delta\ll 1
.

Consider the 1d GFF
\phi
of negative dimension
\Delta_{\phi}=-\delta/2
, whose target space is a circle of circumference
2\pi/b_{0}
:

\displaystyle\phi\sim\phi+2\pi n/b_{0}\,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\,.

(3.1)

Note that the inverse radius has mass dimension
[b_{0}]=\delta/2
, and we thus write
b_{0}=\kappa^{\delta/2}
, for some mass scale
\kappa
, such that
b_{0}\to 1
for
\delta\to 0
.

As we review in Appendix
A
, for
-1<\Delta_{\phi}\leq 0
, the noncompact 1d GFF can be rigorously defined
[
57
]
as a probability measure on the space of distributions
\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined modulo an additive constant. In fact, the resulting random distribution is almost surely a continuous function (modulo an additive constant).
In other words, such GFF preserves shift symmetry. Hence, we can compactify the target space and define a compact version of such GFF on the space of continuous functions
\mathbb{R}\rightarrow S^{1}_{1/b_{0}}
.

We denote the expectation value in this compact GFF theory by
\langle\cdot\rangle_{0}
.
The covariance kernel
C(x)=\langle\phi(x)\phi(0)\rangle_{0}
is defined only up to an additive constant, as discussed in Appendix
A.2
.
This simply means that
\phi
is not a well-defined random variable. However, for practical calculations we still deal with its covariance in intermediate steps, hence it will be convenient to fix its form.
We will choose the normalization and the additive constant so that the covariance has a well-defined limit for
\delta\to 0
:
12
12

12

Notice that continuing to
\delta<0
this normalization would differ from that of the GFF in Section
2.1
.

C(x)=-\frac{2}{\delta}\,(|x|^{\delta}-\kappa^{-\delta})=-2\log(\kappa|x|)+O(\delta)\;.

(3.2)

Due to the identification in field space, all well-defined observables must be built out of
\partial^{n}_{x}\phi(x)
with
n\geq 1
, and vertex operators
e^{\mathrm{i}nb_{0}\phi(x)}
, with
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The correlators of
\partial^{n}_{x}\phi(x)
are obtained straightforwardly as derivatives of (products of)
C(x)
, and so they are clearly independent of the additive constant; those of the vertex operators will be discussed below.
All such operators have positive dimension, and the resulting theory is unitary, as proven rigorously in
[
58
]
.
13
13

13

More precisely, in
[
58
]
the authors proved reflection positivity of the fractional Brownian motion, that is obtained from the GFF
\phi
by fixing its value to be zero at the origin.

We point out that while this GFF might seem a bit exotic, its status is not much different from the standard massless scalar field in
d=1
. The latter corresponds to taking
\delta=1
, and thus has negative scaling dimension. Therefore, it also needs to be defined by modding out its zero mode, and thus also in this case the well-defined random variables are those that are independent of the zero mode. In particular, the variable
\phi(x)-\phi(0)
corresponds to the standard Wiener process (Brownian motion), and the growth of the covariance reproduces the well-known linear growth of the Brownian motion:
\langle(\phi(x)-\phi(0))^{2}\rangle_{0}\sim|x|
.
The generalization to
\delta\neq 1
is known as
fractional Brownian motion
, and it was introduced long ago in
[
59
]
.
Lastly, the compactification simply corresponds to the restriction of the random process to a circle.

Correlation function of vertex operators.

At
\delta=0
, the vertex operators
V_{n}(x)\equiv e^{\mathrm{i}nb_{0}\phi(x)}
are defined with normal ordering
14
14

14

The standard definition of normal ordering gives:

V_{n}(x)=\,:e^{\mathrm{i}nb_{0}\phi(x)}:\,\equiv\exp\bigg\{-\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}x_{1}{\rm d}x_{2}\;\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi(x_{1})}C(x_{1}-x_{2})\frac{\delta}{\delta\phi(x_{2})}\bigg\}e^{\mathrm{i}nb_{0}\phi(x)}=\exp\bigg\{\frac{n^{2}b_{0}^{2}}{2}C(0)\bigg\}e^{\mathrm{i}nb_{0}\phi(x)}\;,

where we typically regularize
C(0)
introducing a cutoff
a
and replacing
C(0)\to C(a)
.
We further multiply by
\kappa^{n^{2}b_{0}^{2}}
, so that by virtue of the neutrality condition
\sum_{i}n_{i}=0
the correlators are independent of
\kappa
.

and have the following correlators:

\left\langle V_{n_{1}}(x_{1})\cdots V_{n_{m}}(x_{m})\right\rangle_{0}=\delta_{0,\sum_{i}n_{i}}\prod_{i<j}|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2b_{0}^{2}n_{i}n_{j}}\;,

(3.3)

where we temporarily keep
b_{0}
although it equals one at
\delta=0
.
This is derived precisely as in 2d

60

, because as reviewed in Appendix
A.2
, at
\delta=0
the field
\phi(x)
is just the boundary value of a free boson in 2d.

If we take
n_{i}=\pm 1
, and introduce the simplified notation
V_{\pm}\equiv V_{\pm 1}
, the only non-vanishing correlators have an equal number of
V_{+}
and
V_{-}
, resulting in

\begin{split}\langle V_{+}(x_{1})\cdots V_{+}(x_{n})&V_{-}(x^{\prime}_{1})\cdots V_{-}(x^{\prime}_{n})\rangle_{0}=\frac{\prod_{j<k}|x_{j}-x_{k}|^{2b_{0}^{2}}|x^{\prime}_{j}-x^{\prime}_{k}|^{2b_{0}^{2}}}{\prod_{j,k}|x_{j}-x^{\prime}_{k}|^{2b_{0}^{2}}}\\

(3.4)

In the last expression, we recognize the structure of the Boltzman weight for the Coulomb gas in eq. (
2.18
), except for the ordering constraint.
Fitting with the Coulomb gas interpretation of such correlators, it is common to refer to the parameters
n_{i}
as “charges” and to the constraint on the total charge as “neutrality condition”.

At
\delta>0
, we can similarly obtain the correlators of vertex operators
by using a standard trick (e.g. proposition 23.6.1 of

).
For a GFF
\phi

, we have the formula

\langle e^{\mathrm{i}\phi[f]}\rangle_{0}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\langle\phi[f]\phi[f]\rangle_{0}}\;,

(3.5)

where
\phi[f]\equiv\int{\rm d}x\,\phi(x)f(x)
.
Therefore, choosing
f(x)=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}\delta(x-x_{i})
, where the test function constraint
\int{\rm d}x\,f(x)=0
(see Appendix
A.2
) demands that the neutrality condition
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=0
be satisfied, we have

\begin{split}\langle e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha_{1}\phi(x_{1})}\cdots e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha_{n}\phi(x_{n})}\rangle_{0}&=e^{-\frac{1}{2}C(0)\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}^{2}}\,\prod_{i<j}e^{-\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}C(x_{i}-x_{j})}\;.\end{split}

(3.6)

Since
C(x)
is defined only up to a constant, we could set

to zero. However, for the
\delta\to 0

C(x)
as in (

V_{n}(x)=\,\kappa^{n^{2}}\,:e^{\mathrm{i}nb_{0}\phi(x)}:\,=\kappa^{n^{2}}\,e^{\frac{1}{2}C(0)n^{2}b_{0}^{2}}e^{\mathrm{i}nb_{0}\phi(x)}\;.

(3.7)

Notice that while (
3.6
) is independent of
\kappa

\kappa

\delta>0

At this point it should be clear that, identifying
b_{0}^{2}a^{\delta}=\mathcal{J}
, the Boltzmann weight of a configuration of
2n

(3.8)

Therefore, in order to generate the terms appearing in the AYK partition function, we need to perturb the GFF by the vertex operators.
However, in order to force the charges to alternate, we will need to form a product of the vertex operators with an appropriate algebra of noncommuting operators.
Coincidentally, such algebra will be provided precisely by the Pauli matrices appearing in the 1d SRI.

3.2
Interacting model

We claim that the 1d LRI CFT can be identified with the IR fixed point of the following 1d continuum model:

Relation between

Given that

\mathcal{O}_{h}
with respect to

and the charge

In order to see that, we apply formula (

\begin{split}Z_{s}=2\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}g^{2n}\int_{{\rm I}_{2n}(3a)}\tr\langle&\mathcal{D}_{h}(L/2,x_{1}+a)\hat{\sigma}_{+}V_{+}(x_{1})\mathcal{D}_{h}(x_{1}-a,x_{2}+a)\hat{\sigma}_{-}V_{-}(x_{2})\cdots\\

At this stage, having decoupled the nonncommuting operators, we can perform the integration in the exponent, to get

(3.19)

to write

\begin{split}e^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}h}{\sqrt{2}}\hat{\sigma}_{3}\phi(x+a)}\,&\hat{\sigma}_{\pm}V_{\pm}(x)\,e^{+\frac{\mathrm{i}h}{\sqrt{2}}\hat{\sigma}_{3}\phi(x-a)}=\hat{\sigma}_{\pm}V_{\pm}(x)\,e^{\mp\sqrt{2}\mathrm{i}h\phi(x+a)}\,e^{+\frac{\mathrm{i}h}{\sqrt{2}}\hat{\sigma}_{3}(\phi(x-a)-\phi(x+a))}\;,\\

where in the last step we took into account the definition of normal ordered vertex operator (

\begin{split}Z_{s}&=2\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}g_{h}^{2n}\int_{{\rm I}_{2n}(a)}\tr\left\langle e^{\frac{\mathrm{i}h}{\sqrt{2}}\,\hat{\sigma}_{3}\,\phi(L/2)}\hat{\sigma}_{+}V_{1-\sqrt{2}h/b_{0}}(x_{1})\cdots\hat{\sigma}_{-}V_{-1+\sqrt{2}h/b_{0}}(x_{2n})e^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}h}{\sqrt{2}}\,\hat{\sigma}_{3}\,\phi(-L/2)}\right\rangle_{0}\\

(3.21)

where we have defined the multiplicatively renormalized coupling

g_{h}=g\,e^{h(b_{0}\sqrt{2}-h)C(0)}\;.

(3.22)

Therefore, up to a boundary contribution,

3.2.2
Covariant derivative and Schwinger-Dyson equation

The path ordering introduces some non-trivial dependence of correlators on the end-points of the partial defect operator
\mathcal{D}
appearing in the right-hand side of (
3.15
). Following
[
64
]
, it is convenient for this reason to define a defect covariant derivative
D_{x}
, by demanding that

\tr\left\langle\ldots D_{x}\mathcal{O}(x)\ldots\right\rangle_{0}=\frac{d}{dx}\tr\left\langle\ldots\mathcal{O}(x)\ldots\right\rangle_{0}\;,

(3.24)

which, because of (
3.15
), is equivalent to

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}(x^{\prime},x)D_{x}\mathcal{O}(x){\mathcal{D}}(x,x^{\prime\prime})\equiv\frac{d}{dx}\left({\mathcal{D}}(x^{\prime},x)\mathcal{O}(x){\mathcal{D}}(x,x^{\prime\prime})\right)\;.

(3.25)

For the model at hand we easily find

\displaystyle D_{x}\mathcal{O}(x)=\partial_{x}\mathcal{O}(x)-[g\mathcal{O}_{g}+h\mathcal{O}_{h},\mathcal{O}](x)\;,

(3.26)

where
[,]
is the matrix commutator.

The covariant derivative allows us to understand if some operator is a primary or a descendant at the conformal fixed point. Interestingly,
D_{x}O
can be a descendant of
O
even if
O
has no explicit dependence on the defect coordinate. An important example for us will be the case of
O=\hat{\sigma}_{3}
, for which we have

\displaystyle D_{x}\hat{\sigma}_{3}=2g(\hat{\sigma}_{+}V_{+}-\hat{\sigma}_{-}V_{-})\,.

(3.27)

This also motivates the following notation.
Since in the
\langle\ldots\rangle
notation the insertion of operators without explicit
x
-dependence is ambiguous, we will sometime write
\hat{\sigma}_{i}(x)
for the insertion of a Pauli matrix at point
x
. For example, for
L/2>x_{1}>x_{2}>-L/2
we have:

\langle\hat{\sigma}_{3}(x_{1})\hat{\sigma}_{3}(x_{2})\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}=\frac{1}{Z_{s}}\tr\left\langle\mathcal{D}(L/2,x_{1})\hat{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{D}(x_{1},x_{2})\hat{\sigma}_{3}\mathcal{D}(x_{2},-L/2)\right\rangle_{0}\;.

(3.28)

We can now derive Schwinger-Dyson equations for our model, assuming for simplicity periodic boundary conditions.
As usual, we perform an arbitrary infinitesimal variation
\phi(x)\to\phi(x)+\varepsilon(x)
in the functional integral, expand to linear order in
\varepsilon(x)
and demand that the correlators are invariant, because it is just a change of variable in the functional integral, where the measure is translation invariant.
Taking into account the GFF action (
A.15
), we obtain

\begin{split}0=&-\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}{\rm d}x\,\varepsilon(x)\left\langle C^{-1}\cdot\phi(x)\ldots\right\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}+\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}{\rm d}x\,\varepsilon(x)\,\mathrm{i}\,b_{0}\,g\langle\ldots(\hat{\sigma}_{+}V_{+}-\hat{\sigma}_{-}V_{-})(x)\ldots\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}\\
&+\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}{\rm d}x\,\frac{\mathrm{i}h}{\sqrt{2}}\langle\ldots\hat{\sigma}_{3}\,\partial_{x}\varepsilon(x)\ldots\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}\;,\end{split}

(3.29)

up to contact terms involving other inserted operators.
Integration by parts on the last term leads to

\begin{split}\left\langle C^{-1}\cdot\phi(x)\ldots\right\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}&=\mathrm{i}\,b_{0}\,g\langle\ldots(\hat{\sigma}_{+}V_{+}-\hat{\sigma}_{-}V_{-})(x)\ldots\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}-\frac{\mathrm{i}h}{\sqrt{2}}\langle\ldots D_{x}\hat{\sigma}_{3}(x)\ldots\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}\\
&=\mathrm{i}g(b_{0}-\sqrt{2}h)\langle\ldots(\hat{\sigma}_{+}V_{+}-\hat{\sigma}_{-}V_{-})(x)\ldots\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}\;,\end{split}

(3.30)

where in the last step we used the covariant derivative introduced above. Notice that we find again the combination
b_{0}-\sqrt{2}h
, confirming again that the role of
h
is to shift
b_{0}
by
-\sqrt{2}h
.

The Schwinger-Dyson equations (
3.30
), together with the fact that the inverse covariance
C^{-1}
acts on
\phi
as a shadow transform, imply that at the IR fixed point, if the latter is non-trivial, the spectrum of the model contains a protected operator of scaling dimension
1-\Delta_{\phi}
, odd under both
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and parity. Notably, this cannot be a primary, as in virtue of (
3.27
) it must be a descendant of
\hat{\sigma}_{3}
.
As we will see, for the
s=1
LRI the only IR fixed point is the trivial one, and the two primaries do not recombine. On the other hand, for the model at
s=1-\delta
, there is a non-trivial IR fixed point and the argument above implies the existence of a protected operator of dimension
1+\delta/2
, which must be a descendant of
\hat{\sigma}_{3}
.
Moreover, the latter must also have protected dimension
\Delta_{\sigma}=\delta/2
.

3.2.3
Symmetries

For
g=h=0
, the UV theory (
3.9
) has
\mathrm{O}(2)
global symmetry acting on the target space circle of
\phi
, and
\mathrm{PU}(2)
global symmetry acting on the
2\times 2
matrix degrees of freedom
A
by conjugation. By turning on
\mathcal{O}_{g}
and
\mathcal{O}_{h}
,
\mathrm{O}(2)\times\mathrm{PU}(2)
is broken to the diagonal
\mathrm{O}(2)
, generated by
\mathrm{U}(1)
rotations and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
reflection

\displaystyle\mathrm{U}(1):

\displaystyle\phi(x)\mapsto\phi(x)+\alpha/b_{0}\,,

\displaystyle A\mapsto e^{-\mathrm{i}\frac{\alpha}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{3}}Ae^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\alpha}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{3}},

(3.31)

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{2}:

\displaystyle\phi(x)\mapsto-\phi(x)\,,

\displaystyle A\mapsto\hat{\sigma}_{1}A\hat{\sigma}_{1}\,,

(3.32)

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
. The model (
3.9
) is natural because
\mathcal{O}_{g}
and
\mathcal{O}_{h}
are the only relevant or marginal operators invariant under this
\mathrm{O}(2)
.

The model also respects parity
x\mapsto-x
. In order for it to be compatible with the path ordering, and to preserve
\mathcal{O}_{g}
and
\mathcal{O}_{h}
, it must act as

\text{parity}:\quad\phi(x)\mapsto-\phi(-x)\,,\quad A\mapsto A^{T}\,.

(3.33)

In the next section, we will confirm the existence of an IR fixed point of (
3.9
) at
b=1+O(\delta)
,
g=O(\sqrt{\delta})
. The fixed-point theory thus exhibits
\mathrm{O}(2)\rtimes\text{parity}
symmetry.

From the point of view of the LRI model, the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry can be understood as the usual
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry for the original Ising spin in the absence of an external magnetic field. Indeed
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
maps kinks to antikinks, and vice versa, hence it must swap up-spins with down-spins.
Similarly, parity is in direct correspondence with parity on the Ising side.

On the other hand, the
U(1)
symmetry has no counterpart in the LRI model. Therefore, we should assume that the latter corresponds to a superselection sector of the model (
3.9
) with only operators that are neutral under
U(1)
. This is also known as the singlet sector, and we can restrict to it by gauging the model, as discussed in Appendix
C
.

U(1)
-invariance has the important consequence of excluding any vertex operators with charge
n_{i}
different from
\pm 1
.
Indeed, under the
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry, operators have the following charges

\begin{array}[]{c|c}\text{operator}&\mathrm{U(1)}\text{ charge}\\
\hline\cr\partial\phi&0\\
V_{n}&n\\
\hat{\sigma}_{3}&0\\
\hat{\sigma}_{\pm}&\mp 1\,.\end{array}

(3.34)

This means that the space of
\mathrm{U}(1)
-neutral local operators is generated by

A,\quad B\,\hat{\sigma}_{3},\quad C\,V_{+}\hat{\sigma}_{+},\quad D\,V_{-}\hat{\sigma}_{-}\,,

(3.35)

where
A,B,C,D
are words built out of the letters
\partial^{n}\phi
with
n>0
.

At first sight, such a restriction might seem inconsistent, because already at
g=h=0
, i.e. in the GFF, the OPE of
V_{+}
with itself contains
V_{-2}
.
However,
V_{+}
by itself is not in the spectrum of the singlet sector: neutrality under
U(1)
requires that
V_{+}
be always multiplied by
\hat{\sigma}_{+}
. And the product of
\hat{\sigma}_{+}V_{+}
with itself vanishes because
\hat{\sigma}_{+}^{2}=0
.

4
Renormalization group analysis

In this section, we discuss some perturbative computations for the model introduced in the previous section, which we rewrite here for convenience:

Z_{s}(b,g)=\Big\langle\!\tr\mathrm{P}\mathrm{exp}\Big\{\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}{\rm d}x\,\left[g\,\mathcal{O}_{g}(x)+h\,\mathcal{O}_{h}(x)\right]\Big\}\Big\rangle_{0}\;.

(4.1)

Expectation values
\langle\ldots\rangle_{0}
are computed with respect to the GFF theory (
A.15
), with covariance (
3.2
).
The operators
\mathcal{O}_{g}
,
\mathcal{O}_{h}
are defined in equation (
3.10
). We recall that the partition function depends on
g
and
b=b_{0}-\sqrt{2}h
, of mass dimension 0 and
\delta/2
, respectively.
At
\delta=0
, we are free to choose
b_{0}=1
, so that in this case the UV scaling dimension of both
\mathcal{O}_{g}
and
\mathcal{O}_{h}
equals 1, but we should keep in mind that for
\delta>0
, the coupling
b_{0}
has positive mass dimension and thus it has a (purely classical) running.

The UV divergences of the perturbative expansion are regularized by point-splitting, i.e. by introducing hard core repulsions as in (
2.19
), with cutoff length
a
. The UV divergences in the
a\to 0
limit are then removed by including the appropriate counterterms, so that correlation functions of renormalized operators are UV-finite when expressed in terms of the renormalized couplings. Beta functions and anomalous dimensions are then obtained from the Callan–Symanzik equation, which states that renormalized observables are cut-off independent.

We shall distinguish between the model with
s=1
, in which case a free boundary CFT is perturbed by marginal primary operators, from the model with
s<1
, in which case a GFF with a compact and dimensionful target space is perturbed by composite operators that are not scaling operators.
In the latter case we do not have at the moment a proof that the theory is renormalizable to all orders of the perturbative expansion, but we also found no evidence to the contrary in our computations.
Although we hope to come back to this question in the future, it should be said that the UV completeness of the model is not needed for our purposes, as we are only interested in its IR behavior.

4.1
Beta functions and fixed point at
s=1

We compute the beta functions for the model with
s=1
by employing conformal perturbation theory (see e.g.
[
71
]
for an extensive review). The basic idea is that it is sufficient to look at perturbative expansion of one point functions of the perturbing operators, inserted at infinity. For an operator
\mathcal{O}(x)
with dimension
\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}
in a given CFT, its insertion at infinity is defined as

\langle\mathcal{O}(\infty)\dots\rangle_{\rm CFT}\equiv\lim_{x\to\infty}x^{2\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}}\langle\mathcal{O}(x)\dots\rangle_{\rm CFT}\,,

(4.2)

and its finiteness is a simple consequence of the conformal properties of correlators.

In our case, we will be considering
\langle\mathcal{O}_{g}\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}
and
\langle\mathcal{O}_{h}\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}
, where the expectation value
\langle\ldots\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}
has been defined in (
3.15
), and it is here understood to be evaluated perturbatively in
g
and
h
. The fact that correlators appearing in such expansion are the GFF correlators, and hence they are all conformal at
\delta=0
, guarantees the existence of the limit.

In this setup, as long as the IR cutoff
L
is kept finite, UV divergences in the
a\to 0
limit can always be removed via coupling renormalization.
Indeed we do not need to renormalize the operator inserted at

Fixed points

From the computing the zeros of the beta functions above we find one line of RG fixed points at

, parametrized by

Along the line of fixed points we find one marginal operator (i.e.

The latter is the dimension of the vertex operator (with

As explained before, the vertex operators changing from relevant to irrelevant corresponds to the transition from the disordered to ordered phase.

However, the approach to such a fixed point is via a marginally irrelevant operator, because the operators
\mathcal{O}_{g}
and
\mathcal{O}_{h}
are marginal at the fixed point. Therefore, we expect to find logarithmic corrections to scaling in the IR. See Appendix
E

4.3
Beta functions and fixed point at
s<1

Next, we discuss beta functions for the model at
\delta>0
, i.e.
s<1
. While the coupling
g
that multiplies the generalized vertex operators remains dimensionless, the coupling
h
that multiplies
\chi
has now (mass) dimension equal to
\delta/2
, and so we expect the beta function of
h
to start linearly in
h
.
More drastically, while the correlators of
\mathcal{O}_{h}
remain conformal,
\mathcal{O}_{g}
is no longer a scaling operator at
\delta>0
and its correlators are exponential functions.
Nevertheless, the structure of perturbation theory remains similar to that of the
s=1
model. In particular, for the computation of beta functions, we will again restrict the interaction to a finite interval and renormalize one point functions of operators
\mathcal{O}_{g}
and
\mathcal{O}_{h}
inserted far away from it.

Generalizing (
4.2
), we define

\langle\mathcal{O}(\infty)\dots\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}\equiv\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\langle\mathcal{O}(x)\dots\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}}{\langle\mathcal{O}(x)\mathcal{O}(0)\rangle_{0}}\,.

(4.18)

Unlike for the conformal case (
4.2
), it is in general nontrivial to show that the above definition leads to a finite result. Fortunately, our UV theory is GFF and
upon applying Wick’s theorem, it is straightforward to see that the above limit is finite for any primary operator that is defined as a normal-ordered monomial in
\phi
(with derivatives).
The case of the vertex operators is less obvious, but if also all the other operators in the correlator are vertex operators, then the explicit formula (
3.8
), together with the fact that only an even number of alternating charges is allowed, leads to the same conclusion. The most intricate case is the one of mixed correlators, but again the finiteness of (
4.18
) can be proven with the help of Wick’s theorem, in the same way as it is employed in Appendix
D
.
Some examples of useful correlators with an operator inserted at infinity are provided in Appendix
D.1
.

As in the
\delta=0
case, we have, up to higher order perturbative corrections:

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\sigma}_{3}\chi(\infty)\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}=

\displaystyle\frac{h}{2}\tr(\hat{\sigma}_{3}^{2})\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}{\rm d}x\langle\chi(\infty)\chi(x)\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle+\frac{g^{2}}{2}\int_{-L/2+a}^{L/2}{\rm d}x_{1}\int_{-L/2}^{x_{1}-a}{\rm d}x_{2}\,\tr[\langle\hat{\sigma}_{3}\chi(\infty)\hat{\sigma}_{+}V_{+}(x_{1})\hat{\sigma}_{-}V_{-}(x_{2})\rangle_{0}+(12)]

\displaystyle+\frac{hg^{2}}{2}\int_{{\rm I}_{3}(a)}\tr\,[\langle\hat{\sigma}_{3}\chi(\infty)\hat{\sigma}_{+}V_{+}(x_{1})\hat{\sigma}_{-}V_{-}(x_{2})\hat{\sigma}_{3}\chi(x_{3})\rangle_{0,c}+\text{5 perm.}]

\displaystyle+\frac{h^{3}}{2}\int_{{\rm I}_{3}(a)}\tr\,\langle\hat{\sigma}_{3}\chi(\infty)\hat{\sigma}_{3}\chi(x_{1})\hat{\sigma}_{3}\chi(x_{2})\hat{\sigma}_{3}\chi(x_{3})\rangle_{0,c}+\dots\,,

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\sigma}_{+}V_{+}(\infty)\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}=

\displaystyle\frac{g}{2}\tr(\hat{\sigma}_{+}\hat{\sigma}_{-})\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}dx\,\langle V_{+}(\infty)V_{-}(x)\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle+\frac{gh}{2}\int_{-L/2+a}^{L/2}{\rm d}x_{1}\int_{-L/2}^{x_{1}-a}{\rm d}x_{2}\tr\,[\langle\hat{\sigma}_{+}V_{+}(\infty)\hat{\sigma}_{-}V_{-}(x_{1})\hat{\sigma}_{3}\chi(x_{2})\rangle_{0}+(12)]+\dots

\displaystyle+\frac{g^{3}}{2}\int_{{\rm I}_{3}(a)}\tr\,\langle\hat{\sigma}_{+}V_{+}(\infty)\hat{\sigma}_{-}V_{-}(x_{1})\hat{\sigma}_{+}V_{+}(x_{2})\hat{\sigma}_{+}V_{-}(x_{3})\rangle_{0,c}+\dots\,,

(4.19)

where the finite-

In practice, evaluating the divergent part of the integrals above for generic

\langle VVVV\rangle

\displaystyle\beta_{g}=-2gh(\sqrt{2}-h)+g^{3}+O(g^{3}h,g^{5})\,,\quad\beta_{h}=-\frac{\delta}{2}h-g^{2}(\sqrt{2}-2h)+O(g^{2}h^{2},g^{4})\,.

\displaystyle\beta_{g}=(b^{2}-1)g+g^{3}\,,\quad\beta_{b^{2}}=-\delta b^{2}+4b^{2}g^{2}\,,

We have thus shown how a perturbative computation in the model (

Fixed points

With the beta functions (
4.21

\displaystyle g_{*}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{4}}+O(\delta^{3/2})\,,\quad b^{2}_{*}=1-\frac{\delta}{4}+O(\delta^{2})\,.

(4.22)

We stress that although the fixed point of

is of order one, the result is consistent with perturbation theory, because using that
b^{2}_{*}=(b_{0}-\sqrt{2}h_{*})^{2}a^{\delta}
, we find that

h_{*}=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{2}}\Big(\frac{1}{8}+\frac{1}{2}\log(\kappa a)\Big)+O(\delta^{2})\,,

(4.23)

so that both
g_{*}
and
h_{*}
are perturbative in
\delta
.
The choice of sign for
g_{*}
is irrelevant, as the full set of observables is invariant under
g\to-g
. However, intermediate calculations do depend on the sign of
g_{*}
, hence in the following we will choose
g_{*}=-\sqrt{\delta/4}
.

4.4
Anomalous dimensions

4.4.1
Leading near-marginal operators at the fixed point

By standard RG arguments, linearizing the beta functions around the IR fixed point we find the linear combination of
\mathcal{O}_{h}
and
\mathcal{O}_{g}
that behave as scaling operators at the fixed point, along with their IR scaling dimensions
\Delta_{\pm}=1+\omega_{\pm}

\omega_{\pm}
are the eigenvalues of the stability matrix:

B_{ij}=\partial_{i}\beta_{j}|_{g_{*},b^{2}_{*}}=\begin{pmatrix}-1+b_{*}^{2}+3g_{*}^{2}&8b_{*}^{2}g_{*}\\
g_{*}&4g_{*}^{2}-\delta\end{pmatrix}\>,

(4.24)

where indices
i,j
run over the couplings
\{g,b^{2}\}
.
Diagonalizing this matrix, we find
\omega_{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{2\delta}+\delta/4+O(\delta^{3/2})
,
and thus the IR scaling dimensions

\displaystyle\Delta_{\pm}=1\pm\sqrt{2\delta}+\delta/4+O(\delta^{3/2})\,.

(4.25)

The associated scaling operators
\mathcal{O}_{\pm}
are the linear combinations

\mathcal{O}_{\pm}=a^{-\Delta_{\pm}}\sum_{i\in\{g,b^{2}\}}v_{\pm}^{i}\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{i}\,,

(4.26)

where we introduced the notation
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{i}\equiv\mathcal{O}_{i}a^{\Delta^{0}_{i}}
, with
\Delta^{0}_{i}
being the canonical (or engineering) dimension of
\mathcal{O}_{i}
,
and where
v_{\pm}^{i}
is the
i

B_{ij}
, corresponding to the eigenvalue
\omega_{\pm}
.
We stress that the scaling operators are written directly in terms of bare operators, because the beta functions of bare couplings are associated to the Callan-Symanzik equations for bare correlators.

We find
v_{\pm}=(\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{8}+O(\delta),-2)
, and thus:

\displaystyle a^{\Delta_{\pm}}\,\mathcal{O}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{h}\pm\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{g})+\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{8}\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{g}+O(\delta)\,,

(4.27)

where we used
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{b^{2}}=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\,\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{h}+O(\delta)

and
h
.
20
20

20

An integrated operator
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{i}

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{b^{2}}=(\frac{\partial b^{2}}{\partial(ha^{\delta/2})})^{-1}\,\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{h}

The signs in the definition of
\mathcal{O}_{\pm}

One might wonder how robust the

(

Lastly, we assume that the beta functions coefficients depend analytically on

\displaystyle\beta_{g}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}\sum_{m=0}a^{(1)}_{n,m}(\delta)g^{2n+1}(b^{2}-1)^{m}\,,\quad\beta_{b^{2}}=-\delta b^{2}+\sum_{n=1}\sum_{m=0}a^{(2)}_{n,m}(\delta)g^{2n}(b^{2}-1)^{m}\,,

(4.28)

with

\displaystyle a^{(1)}_{n,m}(\delta)=\sum_{k=0}a^{(1)}_{n,m,k}\delta^{k}\,,\quad a^{(2)}_{n,m}(\delta)=\sum_{k=0}a^{(2)}_{n,m,k}\delta^{k}\,,

(4.30)

4.4.2
Anomalous dimensions of
\hat{\sigma}_{3}
and
\chi

As we discussed in Section
2
, the spectrum of the 1d LRI CFT should contain two protected primary operators which are even under parity and odd under global
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.
In the near-crossover description, these two operators are
\hat{\sigma}_{3}
and
\chi
, with protected dimensions

\Delta_{\sigma}=\frac{\delta}{2}\>,\quad\Delta_{\chi}=1-\frac{\delta}{2}\>.

(4.31)

For
\hat{\sigma}_{3}
, we have argued in Section
3.2.2
that its dimension is protected as a consequence of the Schwinger-Dyson equations; for
\chi
, its dimension must be
1+\Delta_{\phi}
, and as usual in long-range models
\phi
has no anomalous dimension. We now want to verify that these conclusions are corroborated by actual computations with our model.

The anomalous dimension of an operator
\mathcal{O}
can be computed by treating
\mathcal{O}
as a perturbation with coupling
\lambda
, and computing the beta function
\beta_{\lambda}
for this new coupling.
From there, the scaling dimension is obtained from
\partial_{\lambda}\beta_{\lambda}
evaluated at the fixed point.
Considering the expectation value of
\sigma\equiv a^{-\Delta_{\sigma}}\hat{\sigma}_{3}
, where the factor of
a
ensures that the engineering and scaling dimension match, the first non-trivial contribution is at quadratic order in
g
and
h
, for which we obtain:

\displaystyle a^{\Delta_{\sigma}}\langle\sigma(\infty)\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda}{2}\int{\rm d}y\tr\langle\hat{\sigma}_{3}(\infty)\hat{\sigma}_{3}(y)\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda g^{2}}{2}\int{\rm d}y_{1}\,{\rm d}y_{2}\,{\rm d}y_{3}\,\tr\Big[\langle\hat{\sigma}_{3}(\infty)\hat{\sigma}_{3}(y_{1})\mathcal{O}_{g}(y_{2})\mathcal{O}_{g}(y_{3})\rangle_{0,c}+\text{ perms}\Big]

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda h^{2}}{2}\int dy_{1}\,dy_{2}\,dy_{3}\,\tr\Big[\langle\hat{\sigma}_{3}(\infty)\hat{\sigma}_{3}(y_{1})\mathcal{O}_{h}(y_{2})\mathcal{O}_{h}(y_{3})\rangle_{0,c}+\text{ perms}\Big]

\displaystyle=\lambda I^{\sigma}_{0}+\lambda g^{2}I^{\sigma}_{1}+\lambda h^{2}I^{\sigma}_{2}\>,

(4.32)

with

\displaystyle I^{\sigma}_{0}

\displaystyle=L\>,\quad I^{\sigma}_{1}=L\Big(2+\log 2+2\log\frac{a}{L}+\dots\Big)\>,\quad I^{\sigma}_{2}=-L(2+\log 2)+\dots\>,

(4.33)

where the dots represent higher-order corrections in
a
.
Removing the logarithmic divergences requires the introduction of the renormalized coupling
\lambda_{r}
via the relation

\lambda=\lambda_{r}-2\lambda_{r}g_{r}^{2}\log\frac{a}{L}+...\>,

(4.34)

from which we find the following beta function:

\beta_{\lambda_{\sigma}}=-\frac{d\lambda}{d\log a}=2\lambda g^{2}+\dots\>.

(4.35)

The corresponding anomalous dimension is

\gamma_{\sigma}=\partial_{\lambda}\beta_{\lambda}=2g^{2}\quad\to\quad\gamma_{\sigma}|_{g=g_{*}}=\frac{\delta}{2}\>.

(4.36)

That this dimension does not receive higher-order corrections in
\delta
is guaranteed by the Schwinger-Dyson equation (
3.30
), which fixes
D_{x}\hat{\sigma}_{3}
to be a descendant operator of protected scaling dimension
1+\delta/2
at the non-trivial IR fixed point.

Let us now turn to
\chi
, with tree-level dimension
1-\delta/2
. Repeating the same calculations we find that all terms are zero except the linear term, which evaluates to
\lambda L
. Hence,
\chi
does not get an anomalous dimension. It is not difficult to check that the same conclusion holds for higher orders in
\delta
.

All in all, we reproduce (
4.31
) and find that they are indeed protected and will not receive corrections at higher orders in
\delta
.

The crossing equations of light primaries up to

5.2.1
Other crossing equations

Let us analyze other four-point functions of

\displaystyle N=3:\quad\langle\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi\rangle

5.3

(5.25)

(5.26)

\displaystyle G^{1-m_{1},1-m_{2},1-m_{3},1-m_{4}}_{1-n}(z)G^{m_{1},m_{4},m_{3},m_{2}}_{n^{\prime}}(1-z)=O(z^{-2}\log|z|)

25

25

To evaluate (

(5.42)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m\in\{\sigma,\chi\}}\left[(c_{\sigma\chi m})^{2}\left.G^{\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\chi},\Delta_{\chi},\Delta_{\sigma}}_{\Delta_{m}}(1-z)\right]\right|_{\delta}\,.

To apply the analytic functionals and solve for
A^{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}_{n}
, we need to analyze the
z\rightarrow i\infty
limit of
\mathcal{G}^{s,H}_{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}(z)
. We have for all
z\in\mathbb{C}\backslash(-\infty,0]\cup[1,\infty)

\displaystyle\left|\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}_{n}\,G^{0,0,1,1}_{n}(z)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum\limits_{m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}\geq 2}^{\infty}c^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma m}c^{(0)}_{\chi\chi m}\,G^{0,0,1,1}_{n}(z)\right|

(5.43)

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}\geq 2}^{\infty}|c^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma m}||c^{(0)}_{\chi\chi m}|\,|G^{0,0,1,1}_{n}(z)|

\displaystyle=|z|^{-1}\sum\limits_{m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}\geq 2}^{\infty}|c^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma m}|\,\sqrt{|G^{0,0,0,0}_{n}(z)|}|c^{(0)}_{\chi\chi m}|\sqrt{|G^{1,1,1,1}_{n}(z)|}

\displaystyle\leq|z|^{-1}\sqrt{\sum\limits_{m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}\geq 2}^{\infty}(c^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma m})^{2}\,|G^{0,0,0,0}_{n}(z)|}\sqrt{\sum\limits_{m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}\geq 2}^{\infty}(c^{(0)}_{\chi\chi m})^{2}\,|G^{1,1,1,1}_{n}(z)|}\,.

The first equality follows by definition of
A^{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}_{n}
, and the last inequality is Cauchy-Schwartz. We have already estimated the argument of the first square root in (
5.35
). The argument of the second square root is essentially
\langle\chi\chi\chi\chi\rangle
. The standard radial-coordinate argument relating
z\rightarrow i\infty
to
z\rightarrow 1
implies it is bounded in the limit
z\rightarrow i\infty
. It follows

\left|\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}_{n}\,G^{0,0,1,1}_{n}(z)\right|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle z\rightarrow i\infty}}{{=}}O(|z^{-1}\log(z)|)\,.

(5.44)

The kernels defining the functionals
\omega^{0,0,1,1}_{n}
approach a constant at infinity. It follows that they do not necessarily commute with the sum over conformal blocks. To remedy the situation, we define the subtracted functionals

\widetilde{\omega}^{0,0,1,1}_{n}=\omega^{0,0,1,1}_{n}+(-1)^{n}\frac{((n-1)!)^{2}}{(2n-2)!}\omega^{0,0,1,1}_{1}

(5.45)

for
n\geq 2
. The kernel of
\widetilde{\omega}^{0,0,1,1}_{n}
is
O(z^{-1})
and therefore commutes with the sums over
n
in (
5.40
). We get

A^{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}_{n}=\widetilde{\omega}^{0,0,1,1}_{n}[\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}(z)-\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}(z)]\,.

(5.46)

The integrals defining the functional action can be done in a closed form. The condition that
A^{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}_{n}=0
for
n
odd is equivalent with the constraint

\sqrt{2}(c^{(1)}_{\chi\chi+}-c^{(1)}_{\chi\chi-})=8c^{(1)}_{\sigma\chi-}-\Delta^{(2)}_{+}-\Delta^{(2)}_{-}.

(5.47)

The final answer is

A^{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}_{n}=[1+(-1)^{n}]\frac{2(n-1)((n-2)!)^{2}}{n(2n-2)!}\,.

(5.48)

\mathcal{G}^{s,H}_{\sigma,\sigma,\chi,\chi}(z)
can now be evaluated in a closed form

\mathcal{G}^{s,H}_{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}(z)=\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}_{n}\,G^{0,0,1,1}_{n}(z)=\frac{[\log(1-z)]^{2}}{z^{2}}\,.

(5.49)

Let us now rewrite (
5.40
) as

\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\chi\chi\sigma}_{n}\,G^{0,1,1,0}_{n}(1-z)=\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}(z)+\mathcal{G}^{s,H}_{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}(z)-\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}(z)

(5.50)

The RHS admits the expansion on the LHS if and only if

c^{(2)}_{\sigma\chi+}+c^{(2)}_{\sigma\chi-}+\sqrt{2}(c^{(1)}_{\sigma\chi-})^{2}=0\,.

(5.51)

The formula for
A^{\sigma\chi\chi\sigma}_{n}
then reads

A^{\sigma\chi\chi\sigma}_{n}=[n(n-1)-2(-1)^{n}]\frac{((n-2)!)^{2}}{(2n-2)!}\,.

(5.52)

Note that this formula passes the nontrivial consistency check
A^{\sigma\chi\chi\sigma}_{n}\geq 0
for all
n\geq 2
.

At this point, we can obtain another constraint on the CFT data by recalling that the space of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-even primaries with
\Delta^{(0)}=2
is one-dimensional, spanned by
\rho=:\!\!\chi^{2}\!\!:/\sqrt{2}
. It follows that

A^{\sigma\sigma\sigma\sigma}_{2}=(c^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma\rho})^{2}\,,\qquad A^{\sigma\sigma\chi\chi}_{2}=c^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma\rho}c^{(0)}_{\chi\chi\rho}\,.

(5.53)

It follows directly from the definition of
\rho
that
c^{(0)}_{\chi\chi\rho}=\sqrt{2}
. Equation (
5.48
) then predicts

c^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma\rho}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,.

(5.54)

Hence
A^{\sigma\sigma\sigma\sigma}_{2}=1/2
. By comparing this prediction to (
5.37
), we obtain the constraint

8c^{(1)}_{\sigma\chi-}+\Delta^{(2)}_{+}+\Delta^{(2)}_{-}=1\,,

(5.55)

which in particular satisfies (
5.38
) and which allows us to simplify the formula for
A^{\sigma\sigma\sigma\sigma}_{n}

A^{\sigma\sigma\sigma\sigma}_{n}=\left[n(n-1)+2\right]\frac{((n-2)!)^{2}}{n^{2}(2n-2)!}\,.

(5.56)

5.3.4
Crossing of
\langle\sigma\chi\sigma\chi\rangle
at
O(\delta)

Our next step is to consider the crossing equation of
\langle\sigma\chi\sigma\chi\rangle
at
O(\delta)

\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\chi\sigma\chi}_{n}\,G^{0,1,0,1}_{n}(z)=\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\chi\sigma\chi}(1-z)-\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\chi\sigma\chi}(z)+\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\chi\sigma\chi}_{n}\,G^{0,1,0,1}_{n}(1-z)\,.

(5.57)

Here

A^{\sigma\chi\sigma\chi}_{n}=\sum\limits_{m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}=n}(-1)^{J_{m}}(c^{(1)}_{\sigma\chi m})^{2}\,,

(5.58)

and

\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\chi\sigma\chi}(z)=\sum\limits_{m\in\{+,-\}}\left[(c_{\sigma\chi m})^{2}\left.G^{\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\chi},\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\chi}}_{\Delta_{m}}(z)\right]\right|_{\delta}\,.

(5.59)

To study the swappability of functionals, we need to estimate

\mathcal{G}^{s,H}_{\sigma\chi\sigma\chi}(z)=\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\chi\sigma\chi}_{n}\,G^{0,1,0,1}_{n}(z)

(5.60)

as
z\rightarrow i\infty
. Note that we have
|A^{\sigma\chi\sigma\chi}_{n}|\leq A^{\sigma\chi\chi\sigma}_{n}
. It follows

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{G}^{s,H}_{\sigma\chi\sigma\chi}(z)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\chi\chi\sigma}_{n}\,|G^{0,1,0,1}_{n}(z)|

(5.61)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\chi\chi\sigma}_{n}\,|\tfrac{z}{z-1}|^{n-1}|{}_{2}F_{1}(n-1,n-1;2n;\tfrac{z}{z-1})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}[n(n-1)-2(-1)^{n}]\frac{((n-2)!)^{2}}{(2n-2)!}\,|\tfrac{z}{z-1}|^{n-1}{}_{2}F_{1}(n-1,n-1;2n;|\tfrac{z}{z-1}|)

\displaystyle=O(\log(z))\,,

To go to the second line, we used a standard transformation of
{}_{2}F_{1}
hypergeometric functions. To go to the third line, we used (
5.52
) and the positivity of coefficients of the
y
expansion in the
{}_{2}F_{1}
. To go to the last line, we explicitly evaluated the sum on the third line and took the limit
|y|\rightarrow 1
.

Since the kernels of the functionals
\omega^{0,1,0,1}_{n}
go like
z^{-2}
for
n\geq 2
, these can be safely swapped with the sum over conformal blocks. We find

A^{\sigma\chi\sigma\chi}_{n}=\omega^{0,1,0,1}_{n}[\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\chi\sigma\chi}(1-z)-\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\chi\sigma\chi}(z)]=[(-1)^{n-1}n(n-1)(\Delta^{(2)}_{+}+\Delta^{(2)}_{-})+1]\frac{2((n-2)!)^{2}}{(2n-2)!}\,.

(5.62)

Let us recall again that due to the factor
(-1)^{J_{m}}
, we must have
|A^{\sigma\chi\sigma\chi}_{n}|\leq A^{\sigma\chi\chi\sigma}_{n}
and note that
A^{\sigma\chi\chi\sigma}_{2}=0
. It follows
A^{\sigma\chi\sigma\chi}_{2}=0
, which gives the constraint

\Delta^{(2)}_{+}+\Delta^{(2)}_{-}=\frac{1}{2}\,.

(5.63)

Hence

A^{\sigma\chi\sigma\chi}_{n}=[(-1)^{n-1}n(n-1)+2]\frac{((n-2)!)^{2}}{(2n-2)!}=(-1)^{n-1}A^{\sigma\chi\chi\sigma}_{n}\,.

(5.64)

The last equality implies that, for all primary operators
\mathcal{P}_{m}
,
c^{(1)}_{\sigma\chi m}\neq 0
only if
(-1)^{J_{m}}=(-1)^{\Delta^{(0)}_{m}-1}
. Since there are no parity-odd primaries with
\Delta^{(0)}_{m}=2
, this fact explains why we found
A^{\sigma\chi\chi\sigma}_{2}=0
in the first place.

5.3.5
Summary of results so far

So far, we have implemented crossing of
\langle\sigma\sigma\mathcal{O}_{a}\mathcal{O}_{b}\rangle
up to
O(\sqrt{\delta})
,
\langle\sigma\sigma\chi\chi\rangle
and
\langle\sigma\chi\sigma\chi\rangle
up to
O(\delta)
, and
\langle\sigma\sigma\sigma\sigma\rangle
up to
O(\delta^{2})
. This has lead to the following constraints on the CFT data:

\displaystyle\Delta^{(1)}_{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{2}\,,\quad\Delta^{(2)}_{+}+\Delta^{(2)}_{-}=\frac{1}{2}\,,

(5.65)

\displaystyle c^{(0)}_{\sigma\chi\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,,\quad c^{(1)}_{\sigma\chi\pm}=\mp\frac{1}{16}\,,\quad c^{(2)}_{\sigma\chi+}+c^{(2)}_{\sigma\chi-}=-\frac{1}{128\sqrt{2}}\,,

\displaystyle c^{(1)}_{\sigma\sigma\pm}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,,\quad c^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma\pm}=-\frac{15}{16}\,,\quad c^{(3)}_{\sigma\sigma+}-c^{(3)}_{\sigma\sigma-}=\sqrt{2}\left(\frac{543}{256}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\right)\,,

\displaystyle c^{(1)}_{\chi\chi+}-c^{(1)}_{\chi\chi-}=0\,,

\displaystyle c^{(0)}_{+++}=c^{(0)}_{---}=\frac{3}{2}\,,\quad c^{(0)}_{++-}=c^{(0)}_{+--}=-\frac{1}{2}\,.

We will now consider crossing symmetry of the remaining correlators in (
5.17
).

5.3.6
Crossing of
\langle\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi\rangle
at
O(\delta^{3/2})

The crossing equation takes the form

\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi}_{n}\,G^{0,0,0,1}_{n}(z)=\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi}(z)-\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi}(z)+\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi}_{n}\,G^{0,1,0,0}_{n}(1-z)\,.

(5.66)

Here

A^{\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi}_{n}=\sum\limits_{m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}=n}c^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma m}c^{(1)}_{\sigma\chi m}\,,

(5.67)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m\in\{+,-\}}\left[c_{\sigma\sigma m}c_{\sigma\chi m}\left.G^{\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\chi}}_{\Delta_{m}}(z)\right]\right|_{\delta^{\frac{3}{2}}}

(5.68)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m\in\{+,-\}}\left[c_{\sigma\sigma m}c_{\sigma\chi m}\left.G^{\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\chi},\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\sigma}}_{\Delta_{m}}(1-z)\right]\right|_{\delta^{\frac{3}{2}}}\,.

To check for the swappability of functionals, we estimate

\displaystyle|\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi}_{n}\,G^{0,0,0,1}_{n}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}|A^{\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi}_{n}|\,|G^{0,0,0,1}_{n}(z)|

(5.69)

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}\sqrt{A^{\sigma\sigma\sigma\sigma}_{n}}\sqrt{A^{\sigma\chi\chi\sigma}_{n}}\,|\tfrac{z}{z-1}|^{n-1}|{}_{2}F_{1}(n-1,n;2n;\tfrac{z}{z-1})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{[(n-1)n+2]((n-2)!)^{2}}{n(2n-2)!}\,|\tfrac{z}{z-1}|^{n-1}{}_{2}F_{1}(n-1,n;2n;|\tfrac{z}{z-1}|)

\displaystyle=O(\log z)\,.

Since the kernels of the
\omega^{0,0,0,1}_{n}
functionals are
O(z^{-2})
for all
n\geq 2
, they can be swapped with the sum over conformal blocks.
We find

A^{\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi}_{n}=\omega^{0,0,0,1}_{n}[\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi}(z)-\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi}(z)]=0\,,

(5.70)

for all
n\geq 0
. Note that based on previous information, we could have predicted that
A^{\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi}_{n}=0
for
n
even. This is because
c^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma m}\neq 0
only if
(-1)^{J_{m}}=1
, and
c^{(1)}_{\sigma\chi m}\neq 0
only if
(-1)^{J_{m}}=(-1)^{\Delta^{(0)}_{m}-1}
. For
n
odd, (
5.70
) is a new fact.

We can now go back to the crossing equation (
5.66
), which becomes
\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi}(z)=\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma\sigma\sigma\chi}(z)
. This is in turn equivalent to the new constraint

\Delta^{(2)}_{-}+\sqrt{2}c^{(2)}_{\sigma\chi-}-\sqrt{2}c^{(3)}_{\sigma\sigma-}=\frac{303}{128}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\,.

(5.71)

5.3.7
Crossing of
\langle\sigma\chi\chi\chi\rangle
at
O(\sqrt{\delta})

The crossing equation takes the form

\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\chi\chi\chi}(z)+\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\chi\chi\chi}_{n}\,G^{0,1,1,1}_{n}(z)=\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\chi\chi\chi}(1-z)+\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\chi\chi\chi}_{n}\,G^{0,1,1,1}_{n}(1-z)\,,

(5.72)

where

\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\chi\chi\chi}(z)=\sum\limits_{m\in\{+,-\}}\left[c_{\sigma\chi m}c_{\chi\chi m}\left.G^{\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\chi},\Delta_{\chi},\Delta_{\chi}}_{\Delta_{m}}(z)\right]\right|_{\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}}\,,\quad A^{\sigma\chi\chi\chi}_{n}=\sum\limits_{m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}=n}c^{(1)}_{\sigma\chi m}c^{(0)}_{\chi\chi m}\,.

(5.73)

Based on previous findings, we can conclude that in fact
A^{\sigma\chi\chi\chi}_{n}=0
for all
n
. Indeed, since the
\chi\times\chi
OPE at
\delta=0
contains only the identity and double traces, we have
c^{(0)}_{\chi\chi m}\neq 0
only if
(-1)^{J_{m}}=(-1)^{\Delta^{(0)}_{m}}=1
. On the other hand, we have seen that
c^{(1)}_{\sigma\chi m}\neq 0
only if
(-1)^{J_{m}}=(-1)^{\Delta^{(0)}_{m}-1}
. Thus
c^{(1)}_{\sigma\chi m}c^{(0)}_{\chi\chi m}=0
for all primaries
\mathcal{P}_{m}
.

The crossing equation (
5.72
) does not impose any additional constraints on the CFT data.

5.3.8
Crossing of
\langle\sigma\sigma\mathcal{O}_{\pm}\mathcal{O}_{\pm}\rangle
at
O(\delta)

The crossing equations take the form

\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\sigma ab}_{n}\,G^{0,0,1,1}_{n}(z)=\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma\sigma ab}(z)-\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\sigma ab}(z)+\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma ab\sigma}_{n}\,G^{0,1,1,0}_{n}(1-z)\,,

(5.74)

with
a,b\in\{+,-\}
. Here

\displaystyle A^{\sigma\sigma ab}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}=n}c^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma m}c^{(0)}_{abm}\,,\quad A^{\sigma ab\sigma}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}=n}c^{(1)}_{\sigma am}c^{(1)}_{\sigma bm}\,,

(5.75)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\sigma ab}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m\in\{0,+,-\}}\left[c_{\sigma\sigma m}c_{abm}\left.G^{\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{a},\Delta_{b}}_{\Delta_{m}}(z)\right]\right|_{\delta}

(5.76)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma\sigma ab}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m\in\{\sigma,\chi\}}\left[c_{\sigma ma}c_{\sigma mb}\left.G^{\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{b},\Delta_{a},\Delta_{\sigma}}_{\Delta_{m}}(1-z)\right]\right|_{\delta}\,.

The situation with swappability of functionals is identical to the correlator
\langle\sigma\sigma\chi\chi\rangle
, discussed in Section
5.3.3
. In particular,
\omega^{0,0,1,1}_{n}
are not swappable, but the subtracted version
\widetilde{\omega}^{0,0,1,1}_{n}
, defined in (
5.45
), are swappable. We have

A^{\sigma\sigma ab}_{n}=\widetilde{\omega}^{0,0,1,1}_{n}[\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma\sigma ab}(z)-\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\sigma ab}(z)]\,.

(5.77)

We then perform the sums over conformal blocks to obtain

\mathcal{G}^{s,H}_{\sigma\sigma ab}(z)=\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\sigma ab}_{n}\,G^{0,0,1,1}_{n}(z)\,.

(5.78)

Then we extract
A^{\sigma ab\sigma}_{n}
from

\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma ab\sigma}_{n}\,G^{0,1,1,0}_{n}(z)=\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\sigma ab}(1-z)+\mathcal{G}^{s,H}_{\sigma\sigma ab}(1-z)-\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma\sigma ab}(1-z)\,.

(5.79)

It turns out that the existence of the expansion on the LHS imposes additional constraints on the CFT data. If these constraints are not satisfied, the RHS contains a spurious conformal block
G^{0,1,1,0}_{1}(z)
. The constraints are

2\Delta^{(2)}_{-}+\sqrt{2}(c^{(1)}_{+++}-c^{(1)}_{++-})=\frac{23}{8},\quad 2\Delta^{(2)}_{-}+\sqrt{2}(c^{(1)}_{---}-c^{(1)}_{+--})=-\frac{15}{8},\quad\sqrt{2}(c^{(1)}_{++-}-c^{(1)}_{+--})=\frac{1}{8}\,.

(5.80)

We can use these to solve for
c^{(1)}_{+++}
,
c^{(1)}_{++-}
, and
c^{(1)}_{+--}
in terms of
\Delta^{(2)}_{-}
and
c^{(1)}_{---}
. Once we impose the constraints, we obtain

\displaystyle A^{\sigma\sigma++}_{n}=A^{\sigma\sigma--}_{n}=\frac{(n-1)\left[n(n-1)+4+2(-1)^{n}\right]}{2n}\frac{((n-2)!)^{2}}{(2n-2)!}\,,

(5.81)

\displaystyle A^{\sigma\sigma+-}_{n}=A^{\sigma\sigma-+}_{n}=-\frac{(n-1)\left[n(n-1)-2(-1)^{n}\right]}{2n}\frac{((n-2)!)^{2}}{(2n-2)!}\,,

and

\displaystyle A^{\sigma++\sigma}_{n}=A^{\sigma--\sigma}_{n}=\left[n(n-1)+1-(-1)^{n}\right]\frac{((n-2)!)^{2}}{(2n-2)!}\,,

(5.82)

\displaystyle A^{\sigma+-\sigma}_{n}=A^{\sigma-+\sigma}_{n}=-[1+(-1)^{n}]\frac{((n-2)!)^{2}}{(2n-2)!}\,.

The above formulas for
A^{\sigma\sigma ab}_{n}
and
A^{\sigma ab\sigma}_{n}
pass several nontrivial consistency checks. Firstly, recall that the spaces of both
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-even and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-odd primaries with
\Delta^{(0)}=2
are one-dimensional, spanned by
\rho
,
\widetilde{\rho}
. As
\delta\rightarrow 0
, we have
\rho\rightarrow:\!\!\chi^{2}\!\!:/\sqrt{2}
and
\widetilde{\rho}\rightarrow:\!\!\sigma\chi^{2}\!\!:/\sqrt{2}
. It follows that

A^{\sigma\sigma ab}_{2}=c^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma\rho}c^{(0)}_{ab\rho}\,,\quad A^{\sigma ab\sigma}_{2}=c^{(1)}_{\sigma a\widetilde{\rho}}c^{(1)}_{\sigma b\widetilde{\rho}}\,.

(5.83)

Recall from (
5.54
) that
c^{(2)}_{\sigma\sigma\rho}=1/\sqrt{2}
. Equations (
5.81
) then predict

c^{(0)}_{++\rho}=c^{(0)}_{--\rho}=\sqrt{2}\,,\qquad c^{(0)}_{+-\rho}=0\,.

(5.84)

These results can be verified explicitly in the free theory at
\delta=0
. Indeed, they are equivalent to the following three-point functions between
\rho
and the operators
\mathcal{O}_{g}
,
\mathcal{O}_{h}

c_{gg\rho}=c_{hh\rho}=\sqrt{2}\,,\qquad c_{gh\rho}=0\,.

(5.85)

c_{gh\rho}=0
is immediate from the matrix structure,
c_{hh\rho}=\sqrt{2}
is a simple exercise with Wick contractions.
c_{gg\rho}=\sqrt{2}
requires more work but holds too.

For another consistency check, involving
\widetilde{\rho}
, note that (
5.83
) implies

A^{\sigma++\sigma}_{2}A^{\sigma--\sigma}_{2}=(A^{\sigma+-\sigma}_{2})^{2}\,.

(5.86)

Indeed, (
5.82
) predicts that both sides equal
1
.

5.3.9
Crossing of
\langle\sigma\mathcal{O}_{\pm}\sigma\mathcal{O}_{\pm}\rangle
at
O(\delta)

The crossing equations takes the form

\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma a\sigma b}_{n}\,G^{0,1,0,1}_{n}(z)=\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma a\sigma b}(z)-\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma a\sigma b}(z)+\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma a\sigma b}_{n}\,G^{0,1,0,1}_{n}(1-z)\,,

(5.87)

with
a,b\in\{+,-\}
. Here

A^{\sigma a\sigma b}_{n}=\sum\limits_{m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}=n}(-1)^{J_{m}}c^{(1)}_{\sigma am}c^{(1)}_{\sigma bm}\,,

(5.88)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma a\sigma b}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m\in\{\sigma,\chi\}}\left[c_{\sigma am}c_{\sigma bm}\left.G^{\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{a},\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{b}}_{\Delta_{m}}(z)\right]\right|_{\delta}

(5.89)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma a\sigma b}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m\in\{\sigma,\chi\}}\left[c_{\sigma am}c_{\sigma bm}\left.G^{\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{b},\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{a}}_{\Delta_{m}}(1-z)\right]\right|_{\delta}=\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma b\sigma a}(1-z)\,.

To study the swappability of functionals, note that

|A^{\sigma+\sigma+}_{n}|\leq A^{\sigma++\sigma}_{n}\,,\qquad|A^{\sigma-\sigma-}_{n}|\leq A^{\sigma--\sigma}_{n}=A^{\sigma++\sigma}_{n}\,,\qquad|A^{\sigma-+\sigma}_{n}|\leq\sqrt{A^{\sigma++\sigma}_{n}A^{\sigma--\sigma}_{n}}=A^{\sigma++\sigma}_{n}\,.

(5.90)

Using the same reasoning as in Section
5.3.4
, we conclude that the functionals
\omega^{0,1,0,1}_{n}
are swappable. Their application produces

\displaystyle A^{\sigma+\sigma+}_{n}=A^{\sigma-\sigma-}_{n}=\left[(-1)^{n}+1\right]\frac{n!(n-2)!}{2(2n-2)!}\,,

(5.91)

\displaystyle A^{\sigma+\sigma-}_{n}=A^{\sigma-\sigma+}_{n}=\left\{[(-1)^{n}-1]n(n-1)-4\right\}\frac{((n-2)!)^{2}}{2(2n-2)!}\,.

5.3.10
Crossing of
\langle\sigma\chi\mathcal{O}_{\pm}\mathcal{O}_{\pm}\rangle
at
O(\sqrt{\delta})

The crossing equations take the form

\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma\chi ab}_{n}\,G^{0,1,1,1}_{n}(z)=\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma\chi ab}(z)-\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\chi ab}(z)+\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma ba\chi}_{n}\,G^{0,1,1,1}_{n}(1-z)\,,

(5.92)

with
a,b\in\{+,-\}
. Here

\displaystyle A^{\sigma\chi ab}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}=n}(-1)^{J_{m}}c^{(1)}_{\sigma\chi m}c^{(0)}_{abm}\,,

(5.93)

\displaystyle A^{\sigma ab\chi}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}=n}(-1)^{J_{m}}c^{(1)}_{\sigma am}c^{(0)}_{b\chi m}=\sum\limits_{m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}=n}c^{(1)}_{\sigma am}c^{(0)}_{\chi bm}\,,

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\chi ab}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m\in\{+,-\}}\left[c_{\sigma\chi m}c_{abm}\left.G^{\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{\chi},\Delta_{a},\Delta_{b}}_{\Delta_{m}}(z)\right]\right|_{\sqrt{\delta}}

(5.94)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma\chi ab}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m\in\{\sigma,\chi\}}\left[c_{\chi am}c_{\sigma bm}\left.G^{\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{b},\Delta_{a},\Delta_{\chi}}_{\Delta_{m}}(1-z)\right]\right|_{\sqrt{\delta}}\,.

Following the same logic as in the previous subsections, we find that the functionals
\omega^{0,1,1,1}_{n}
are not swappable with the sum over conformal blocks on the LHS of (
5.92
). We can define subtracted functionals

\widetilde{\omega}^{0,1,1,1}_{n}=\omega^{0,1,1,1}_{n}+(-1)^{n}\frac{n((n-1)!)^{2}}{(2n-2)!}\omega^{0,1,1,1}_{1}\,,

(5.95)

which are swappable. We obtain

A^{\sigma\chi ab}_{n}=\widetilde{\omega}^{0,1,1,1}_{n}[\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma\chi ab}(z)-\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma\chi ab}(z)]\,.

(5.96)

Since the space of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-even primaries with
\Delta^{(0)}=2
is spanned by
\rho
, and since we know from (
5.52
) that
c^{(1)}_{\sigma\chi\rho}=0
, we conclude
A^{\sigma\chi++}_{2}=A^{\sigma\chi--}_{2}=A^{\sigma\chi+-}_{2}=0
. This condition imposes the following constraint on the CFT data

24\Delta^{(2)}_{-}+16\sqrt{2}c^{(1)}_{---}-8\sqrt{2}c^{(1)}_{\chi\chi-}+33=0\,.

(5.97)

The functionals (
5.96
) and the crossing equation (
5.92
) then lead to a unique answer for the coefficients

\displaystyle A^{\sigma\chi++}_{n}=-A^{\sigma\chi--}_{n}=[1-(-1)^{n}][n(n-1)+2]\frac{(n-1)!(n-2)!}{2(2n-2)!}\,,

(5.98)

\displaystyle A^{\sigma\chi+-}_{n}=-A^{\sigma\chi-+}_{n}=-[1+(-1)^{n}][n(n-1)-2]\frac{(n-1)!(n-2)!}{2(2n-2)!}\,,

and

\displaystyle A^{\sigma++\chi}_{n}=-A^{\sigma--\chi}_{n}=[(-1)^{n}(n-2)(n+1)-4]\frac{(n-1)!(n-2)!}{2(2n-2)!}\,,

(5.99)

\displaystyle A^{\sigma+-\chi}_{n}=-A^{\sigma-+\chi}_{n}=(-1)^{n+1}[n(n-1)+2]\frac{(n-1)!(n-2)!}{2(2n-2)!}\,.

5.3.11
Crossing of
\langle\sigma\mathcal{O}_{\pm}\chi\mathcal{O}_{\pm}\rangle
at
O(\sqrt{\delta})

The last crossing equation that we will consider takes the form

\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma a\chi b}_{n}\,G^{0,1,1,1}_{n}(z)=\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma a\chi b}(z)-\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma a\chi b}(z)+\sum\limits_{n=2}^{\infty}A^{\sigma b\chi a}_{n}\,G^{0,1,1,1}_{n}(1-z)\,,

(5.100)

with
a,b\in\{+,-\}
. Here

\displaystyle A^{\sigma a\chi b}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}=n}(-1)^{J_{m}}c^{(1)}_{\sigma am}c^{(0)}_{\chi bm}\,,

(5.101)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma a\chi b}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m\in\{\sigma,\chi\}}\left[c_{\sigma am}c_{\chi bm}\left.G^{\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{a},\Delta_{\chi},\Delta_{b}}_{\Delta_{m}}(z)\right]\right|_{\sqrt{\delta}}

(5.102)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma a\chi b}(z)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m\in\{\sigma,\chi\}}\left[c_{\sigma bm}c_{\chi am}\left.G^{\Delta_{\sigma},\Delta_{b},\Delta_{\chi},\Delta_{a}}_{\Delta_{m}}(1-z)\right]\right|_{\sqrt{\delta}}=\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma b\chi a}(1-z)\,.

Again, we can use the subtracted functionals
\widetilde{\omega}^{0,1,1,1}_{n}
of (
5.95
) to extract
A^{\sigma a\chi b}_{n}
by applying them to (
5.100
)

A^{\sigma a\chi b}_{n}=\widetilde{\omega}^{0,1,1,1}_{n}[\mathcal{G}^{t,L}_{\sigma a\chi b}(z)-\mathcal{G}^{s,L}_{\sigma a\chi b}(z)]\,.

(5.103)

The answer is

\displaystyle A^{\sigma+\chi+}_{n}=-A^{\sigma-\chi-}_{n}=[(n-2)(n+1)-4(-1)^{n}]\frac{(n-1)!(n-2)!}{2(2n-2)!}=(-1)^{n}A^{\sigma++\chi}_{n}\,,

(5.104)

\displaystyle A^{\sigma+\chi-}_{n}=-A^{\sigma-\chi+}_{n}=-[n(n-1)+2]\frac{(n-1)!(n-2)!}{2(2n-2)!}=(-1)^{n}A^{\sigma+-\chi}_{n}\,.

The relation
A^{\sigma a\chi b}_{n}=(-1)^{n}A^{\sigma ab\chi}_{n}
satisfied by these formulas is explained by observing that at
\delta=0
the
\chi\times\mathcal{O}_{\pm}
OPE contains only double trace operators
\chi\overset{\leftrightarrow\phantom{n}}{\partial^{n}}\mathcal{O}_{\pm}
with
\Delta=2+n
and parity
(-1)^{n}
.

5.4
Consistency checks

We can combine the previous results (
5.82
), (
5.91
) to obtain sums of
c^{(1)}_{\sigma am}c^{(1)}_{\sigma bm}
over
\mathcal{P}_{m}
with fixed
\Delta^{(0)}_{m}
and
(-1)^{J_{m}}
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle A^{\sigma ab\sigma}_{n,\text{even}}

\displaystyle:=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}=n\\
(-1)^{J_{m}}=+1\end{subarray}}c_{\sigma am}^{(1)}c_{\sigma bm}^{(1)}=\frac{A^{\sigma ab\sigma}_{n}+A^{\sigma a\sigma b}_{n}}{2}

(5.105)

\displaystyle A^{\sigma ab\sigma}_{n,\text{odd}}

\displaystyle:=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m:\Delta^{(0)}_{m}=n\\
(-1)^{J_{m}}=-1\end{subarray}}c_{\sigma am}^{(1)}c_{\sigma bm}^{(1)}=\frac{A^{\sigma ab\sigma}_{n}-A^{\sigma a\sigma b}_{n}}{2}\,.

The resulting expressions need to pass a number of consistency checks. The first consistency check arises from the observation that
A^{\sigma++\sigma}_{n,\text{even}}
,
A^{\sigma--\sigma}_{n,\text{even}}
,
A^{\sigma++\sigma}_{n,\text{odd}}
and
A^{\sigma--\sigma}_{n,\text{odd}}
are sums of squares. Therefore, they must be non-negative. We can easily check that this is indeed the case for the above formulas.

The second consistency check is to note that for any
n\geq 2
such that the space of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-odd and respectively parity even, odd is zero-dimensional, we must have respectively
A^{\sigma++\sigma}_{n,\text{even}}=A^{\sigma--\sigma}_{n,\text{even}}=A^{\sigma+-\sigma}_{n,\text{even}}=0
,
A^{\sigma++\sigma}_{n,\text{odd}}=A^{\sigma--\sigma}_{n,\text{odd}}=A^{\sigma+-\sigma}_{n,\text{odd}}=0
. We see from (
4.16
) that this is the case for odd parity with
n=2,4
and never the case for even parity. Again, this agrees with the above formulas.

The third consistency check are the Cauchy-Schwartz inequalities

(A^{\sigma+-\sigma}_{n,\text{even}})^{2}\leq A^{\sigma++\sigma}_{n,\text{even}}A^{\sigma--\sigma}_{n,\text{even}}\,,\qquad(A^{\sigma+-\sigma}_{n,\text{odd}})^{2}\leq A^{\sigma++\sigma}_{n,\text{odd}}A^{\sigma--\sigma}_{n,\text{odd}}\,.

(5.106)

Furthermore, whenever the relevant space of primaries is one-dimensional, the inequality must be saturated. From (
4.16
), we must have saturation for even parity with
n=2,3
and odd parity with
n=3,6
. Again, it is not hard to check that all of these properties are satisfied by the above formulas.

We can carry out another consistency check by noting that the space of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-odd, parity-even primaries with
\Delta^{(0)}=2
is one-dimensional. At
\delta=0
, it is spanned by
\widetilde{\rho}=\chi^{2}\hat{\sigma}_{3}/\sqrt{2}
. It follows from the field theory description at
\delta=0
that

c^{(0)}_{\chi+\widetilde{\rho}}=c^{(0)}_{\chi-\widetilde{\rho}}=1\,.

(5.107)

This is consistent with (
5.99
), which furthermore predicts

c^{(1)}_{\sigma+\widetilde{\rho}}=-1\,,\quad c^{(1)}_{\sigma-\widetilde{\rho}}=1\,.

(5.108)

We must therefore have

A^{\sigma++\sigma}_{2}=A^{\sigma--\sigma}_{2}=-A^{\sigma+-\sigma}_{2}=1\,,

(5.109)

which indeed agrees with (
5.82
).

One more check follows from considering the space of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-odd primaries with
\Delta^{(0)}=3
. For each parity, this space is one-dimensional (
4.16
). Let us denote the corresponding parity-even primary
\mu
, and parity-odd primary
\mu^{\prime}
. From (
5.82
) and (
5.91
), we find

c^{(1)}_{\sigma+\mu}=-c^{(1)}_{\sigma-\mu}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\,,\quad c^{(1)}_{\sigma+\mu^{\prime}}=c^{(1)}_{\sigma-\mu^{\prime}}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\,,

(5.110)

where the choice of sign on the RHS corresponds to the ambiguity in the definition of
\mu
,
\mu^{\prime}
. It follows that we must have

A^{\sigma++\chi}_{3}=A^{\sigma-+\chi}_{3}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}c^{(0)}_{\chi+\mu^{\prime}}\,,\quad A^{\sigma+-\chi}_{3}=A^{\sigma--\chi}_{3}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}c^{(0)}_{\chi-\mu^{\prime}}\,,

(5.111)

where the two signs on the RHS must be the same. This is consistent with (
5.99
), which furthermore predicts

c^{(0)}_{\chi+\mu^{\prime}}=-c^{(0)}_{\chi-\mu^{\prime}}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\,.

(5.112)

This result can be checked by a calculation in the free theory at
\delta=0
.

5.5
Solution for the CFT data

Let us combine the constraints we have derived to determine the CFT data. The constraints consist of (
5.65
) together with (
5.71
), (
5.80
), and (
5.97
). They constitute 9 linear equations for the 12 unknowns

\Delta^{(2)}_{\pm}\,,\;c^{(2)}_{\sigma\chi\pm}\,,\;c^{(3)}_{\sigma\sigma\pm}\,,\;c^{(1)}_{\chi\chi\pm},\,\;c^{(1)}_{+++}\,,\;c^{(1)}_{+--}\,,\;c^{(1)}_{+--}\,,\;c^{(1)}_{---}\,.

(5.113)

In order to find a unique solution, we will make use of the expected symmetry of the CFT data under the switch
\mathcal{O}_{+}\leftrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{-}
, accompanied by
\sqrt{\delta}\mapsto-\sqrt{\delta}
. Note that this symmetry does leave the lower-order results in (
5.65
) invariant. To implement the symmetry at higher orders, let us impose the following 3 equations

c^{(1)}_{\chi\chi+}=-c^{(1)}_{\chi\chi-}\,,\quad c^{(2)}_{\sigma\chi+}=c^{(2)}_{\sigma\chi-}\,,\quad c^{(3)}_{\sigma\sigma+}=-c^{(3)}_{\sigma\sigma-}\,.

(5.114)

We then find a unique solution

\displaystyle\Delta_{\pm}=1\pm\sqrt{2}\sqrt{\delta}+\frac{\delta}{4}+O(\delta^{\frac{3}{2}})\,,

(5.115)

\displaystyle c_{\sigma\chi\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mp\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{16}-\frac{\delta}{256\sqrt{2}}+O(\delta^{\frac{3}{2}})\,,

\displaystyle c_{\sigma\sigma\pm}=\pm\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{\sqrt{2}}-\frac{15}{16}\delta\pm\left(\frac{543}{256}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\right)\frac{\delta^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}+O(\delta^{2})\,,

\displaystyle c_{\chi\chi\pm}=O(\delta)\,,

\displaystyle c_{+++}=\frac{3}{2}+\frac{39}{16\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\delta}+O(\delta)\,,\qquad c_{++-}=-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{16\sqrt{2}}+O(\delta)\,,

\displaystyle c_{---}=\frac{3}{2}-\frac{39}{16\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\delta}+O(\delta)\,,\qquad c_{+--}=-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{16\sqrt{2}}+O(\delta)\,.

Reassuringly, the solution exhibits the symmetry also for
\Delta_{\pm}
and
c_{\pm\pm\pm}
, although we only imposed it for
c_{\chi\chi\pm}
,
c_{\sigma\chi\pm}
, and
c_{\sigma\sigma\pm}
. As promised, the solution agrees with predictions of the RG method of Section
4
.

5.6
Checking the OPE relation

As we discussed in Section
2.2
, the OPE coefficients involving
\sigma
and
\chi
in the critical 1d LRI must satisfy certain relations. Specifically, for any four primaries
\phi_{i}
,
\phi_{j}
,
\phi_{k}
,
\phi_{\ell}
, we have
[
43
]
(see also appendix
G
)

\frac{c_{\sigma ij}c_{\chi k\ell}}{c_{\chi ij}c_{\sigma k\ell}}=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{\sigma}+\Delta_{i}-\Delta_{j}+a_{ij}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{\sigma}-\Delta_{i}+\Delta_{j}+a_{ij}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1-\Delta_{\sigma}+\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{\ell}+a_{k\ell}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1-\Delta_{\sigma}-\Delta_{k}+\Delta_{\ell}+a_{k\ell}}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1-\Delta_{\sigma}+\Delta_{i}-\Delta_{j}+a_{ij}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1-\Delta_{\sigma}-\Delta_{i}+\Delta_{j}+a_{ij}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{\sigma}+\Delta_{k}-\Delta_{\ell}+a_{k\ell}}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{\Delta_{\sigma}-\Delta_{k}+\Delta_{\ell}+a_{k\ell}}{2}\right)}\,,

(5.116)

where
a_{ij}=[1-(-1)^{J_{i}+J_{j}}]/2=J_{i}+J_{j}\mod 2
.

Let us test whether these relations are satisfied by the CFT data (
5.115
) derived using the bootstrap. There are three choices for
(i,j,k,\ell)\in\{\sigma,\chi,\mathcal{O}_{+},\mathcal{O}_{-}\}^{4}
giving rise to independent constraints:

(i,j,k,\ell)=(\sigma,\mathcal{O}_{+},\sigma,\mathcal{O}_{-})\,,\quad(i,j,k,\ell)=(\sigma,\mathcal{O}_{+},\chi,\mathcal{O}_{+})\,,\quad(i,j,k,\ell)=(\sigma,\mathcal{O}_{-},\chi,\mathcal{O}_{-})\,.

(5.117)

Considering first the case
(i,j,k,\ell)=(\sigma,\mathcal{O}_{+},\sigma,\mathcal{O}_{-})
, the bootstrap solution (
5.115
) predicts

\frac{c_{\sigma\sigma+}c_{\sigma\chi-}}{c_{\sigma\chi+}c_{\sigma\sigma-}}=-1+\frac{7\sqrt{\delta}}{2\sqrt{2}}-\frac{49}{16}\delta+O(\delta^{\frac{3}{2}})\,.

(5.118)

At the same time, the RHS of (
5.116
) evaluates to

-1+\frac{7\sqrt{\delta}}{2\sqrt{2}}-\left[\frac{49}{16}+\frac{\Delta^{(3)}_{+}+\Delta^{(3)}_{-}}{\sqrt{2}}\right]\delta+O(\delta^{\frac{3}{2}})\,.

(5.119)

We see that (
5.116
) holds automatically up to
O(\delta)
since
\Delta^{(3)}_{+}+\Delta^{(3)}_{-}=0
by virtue of the
\sqrt{\delta}\mapsto-\sqrt{\delta}
symmetry. This agreement is a highly nontrivial consistency check of our results.

Moving on to the cases
(i,j,k,\ell)=(\sigma,\mathcal{O}_{\pm},\chi,\mathcal{O}_{\pm})
, we find that the corresponding relation is satisfied by the bootstrap solution (
5.115
) up to
O(\delta)
. When we impose the OPE relation up to
O(\delta^{2})
, we obtain the new result

c_{\chi\chi\pm}=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\delta\mp\frac{11\pi^{2}}{16\sqrt{2}}\delta^{\frac{3}{2}}+O(\delta^{2})\,.

(5.120)

6
Conclusions and future directions

In this paper, we elaborated on the weakly coupled description of the short-range crossover for the 1d long-range Ising model universality class, presented in
[
21
]
.

For all
s\in(1/2,1)
, the 1d LRI has an interacting IR fixed point. While a weakly coupled description near
s=1/2
is provided by a GFF with
\varphi^{4}
interaction, this model becomes strongly coupled near
s=1
and an alternative, weakly coupled field-theoretic description remained unknown for a long time.
We have filled this gap, introducing the model in equation (
3.9
), which generalizes the Kondo model by giving a non-vanishing canonical dimension
-\delta/2
, with
0\leq\delta<1
, to the scalar field
\phi
. We claim that the
U(1)
-singlet sector of its weakly interacting fixed point describes the 1d LRI CFT at
s=1-\delta
.

The idea behind the model is based on the physical description of the 1d LRI provided by Anderson and Yuval
[
45
,
46
,
47
,
22
]
for
s=1
, and extended by Kosterlitz
[
23
]
to
s<1
. They recognized that at low temperatures, the 1d LRI close to
s=1
could be written as a dilute gas of kinks and antikinks, with the (anti)kinks describing domain walls where the spins of the LRI flip. At
s=1
, this Coulomb gas admits various other descriptions, such as a perturbative expansion of the Kondo model, that in its bosonized version is similar to the boundary sine-Gordon model, but with an extra single spin-
1/2
degree of freedom implementing the constraint of alternating kinks and antikinks.
The latter description is the starting point for our model (
3.9
), that extends it to
s<1
.

There is a loose analogy between our construction and the corresponding higher-dimensional model of
[
9
,
10
]
.
In 1d, the zero-temperature SRI model has a single qubit degree of freedom, with the spin field represented by the Pauli matrix
\hat{\sigma}_{3}
, see Section
2.4
. We can thus view the
\mathcal{O}_{h}
interaction in our model (
3.9
) as the analog of the
\sigma\chi
interaction of
[
9
,
10
]
. Indeed our notation for the operator
\chi
was not accidental, as also in our case it represents the shadow of the spin field in the LRI CFT.
The main difference in 1d is the need to introduce operators that create kinks and antikinks, otherwise the model would be trivial, and in particular it would not lead to the LRI CFT at
s<1
, or to the logarithmic corrections at
s=1
.
As the (anti)kink-generating operators cannot be written in a local way in terms of
\chi
, we are led to introducing the ancestor field
\phi
, such that
\chi\sim\partial\phi
. In terms of
\phi
and of the
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triplet
\{\hat{\sigma}_{3},\hat{\sigma}_{+},\hat{\sigma}_{-}\}
, the (anti)kink-generating operators are the vertex operators
\hat{\sigma}_{\pm}e^{\pm\mathrm{i}b_{0}\phi}
, which we combined in the
\mathcal{O}_{g}
interaction in (
3.9
).

Our model (
3.9
) can appear to be nonstandard for at least three reasons:

(i)
the unperturbed theory is a compact GFF with negative scaling dimension;
(ii)
the perturbation
\mathcal{O}_{g}
is not a scaling operator of the unperturbed theory;
(iii)
the perturbations involve matrix degrees of freedom and a path-ordering.
However, as we have argued in the main text, and as corroborated by the overall self-consistency of our results, none of these aspects should cause headaches.

Concerning the first point, 1d GFF with negative scaling dimension is standard in the mathematical literature, corresponding to the fractional Brownian motion
[
59
]
. Indeed, as reviewed in Appendix
A
, this class of theories arises from a probability measure on the space of continuous functions, defined modulo a global additive constant. In other words, these are shift-symmetric Gaussian theories, their well-defined local observables have positive scaling dimension, and their target space can be consistently compactified.

Regarding the fact that
\mathcal{O}_{g}
is not a scaling operator of the unperturbed theory, we make the following remarks. Firstly, even in standard massive perturbation theory, operators such as
\varphi^{4}
are not scaling operators, they only become such in the UV limit, when the mass is neglected. Similarly, one could consider the UV limit of our model, in which case
b_{0}\to 0
. In this limit,
\mathcal{O}_{g}
reduces to a scaling operator of vanishing dimension.
Secondly, in that same limit, one could expand
\mathcal{O}_{g}
in an infinite series of relevant scaling operators. Since the
U(1)
symmetry protects their relative coefficients, they still only give rise to a single coupling. Unfortunately, we have not obtained a proof of renormalizability to all orders in this framework. Having said that, we found no evidence that counterterms of irrelevant operators should be added, up to second order of the perturbative expansion.
This also leads to our third remark: even if the model (
3.9
) turned out to not be UV complete (which we doubt), this would not affect its usefulness for the construction of the IR CFT. In fact, we are not proposing this model as a new fundamental theory, but rather as a tool to construct an IR CFT via standard RG computations. In the spirit of Wilsonian RG, the presence of irrelevant operators in the bare action has no effect on low-energy physics: the modern view is actually that all QFTs are only effective field theories.
Lastly, the RG computations of Section

As for the fact that the interactions involve matrix degrees of freedom, we notice that similar models appear naturally in condensed-matter systems, like the Kondo model itself, and more generally for systems with localized impurities, see for example

Appendix A
Generalized free field on the line

A
d
-dimensional generalized free field theory (GFF) is the quantum field theory of a single scalar
\varphi
, whose only nonvanishing connected
n
-point function is the two-point function
\langle\varphi(x)\varphi(y)\rangle\equiv C(x-y)
, assumed to be invariant under rotations and translations. All other (not connected)
n
-point functions are obtained as sums over Wick contractions.
In other words, a GFF is a centered Gaussian measure, and as such it can be defined without introducing any action functional, see e.g.
[
61
,
101
]
.

Assuming also reflection positivity, GFFs provide the simplest examples of QFTs satisfying the standard axioms
[
102
,
61
]
. If furthermore we take
C(x)\propto 1/|x|^{2\Delta_{\varphi}}
, with
\Delta_{\varphi}
being the scaling dimension of
\varphi
, then the GFF is also conformally invariant, it is known in the mathematical literature as
fractional Gaussian field

[
57
]
, and
for
\Delta_{\varphi}=(d-2)/2
it reduces to the standard massless free scalar.

We restrict now to a conformal GFF in
d=1
.
For
\Delta_{\varphi}>0
, it provides the simplest example of a 1d CFT as defined in Section
2.2.1
.
For
\Delta_{\varphi}<0
, its definition and conformal properties are slightly more subtle, but it is also a well studied random field, associated to the
fractional Brownian motion
with so-called Hurst parameter
H=-\Delta_{\varphi}
, see for example
[
57
,
58
,
103
]
.
The intermediate case
\Delta_{\varphi}=0
corresponds to the log-correlated Gaussian field
[
104
]
, which shares many technical aspects with the
\Delta_{\varphi}<0
case, and which in
d=1
coincides with the usual 2d free field theory restricted to a line, as we will recall below.

We review here in simplified terms the construction of the GFF probability measure and the associated action functional, referring for more details to the original literature from which we draw our presentation
[
61
,
57
,
103
,
105
]
.

The main idea behind the rigorous construction of a GFF is to use the Bochner-Minlos theorem.
Let
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
be the Schwartz space of test functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
whose derivatives of all orders exist and decay faster than any polynomial at infinity, and let
Z[f]
be a given complex-valued functional on such space.
Let us introduce also the space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
of tempered distributions, i.e. the space of continuous linear functionals on
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, so that if
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
and
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, then the canonical pairing
\varphi[f]=(\varphi,f)\in\mathbb{R}
exists, and we formally write

\varphi[f]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm d}x\,\varphi(x)f(x)\;.

(A.1)

One nice feature of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
is that the Fourier transform and its inverse act as linear endomorphisms on these spaces.

The Bochner-Minlos theorem states that there exists a unique probability measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
, such that
Z[f]
is the characteristic function (or generating functional) of
\mu
, i.e. its Fourier transform

Z[f]=\int_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})}d\mu(\varphi)\,e^{\mathrm{i}\varphi[f]}\;,

(A.2)

if and only if
Z[f]
is continuous,
Z[0]=1
, and
Z[f]
is positive definite, i.e.

\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}z_{j}\bar{z}_{k}Z[f_{j}-f_{k}]\geq 0\,,\qquad\forall f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}),\;\text{and }z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{C}\;.

(A.3)

A GFF is then defined by the assignment of a characteristic function of the form

Z_{{}_{\rm GFF}}[f]=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\langle\varphi[f]\varphi[f]\rangle}=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}x{\rm d}y\,C(x-y)f(x)f(y)}\;.

(A.4)

If the exponent can be cast in the form of an inner product in some space, then it is straightforward to show that
Z_{{}_{\rm GFF}}[f]
is positive (proposition 2.4 of
[
57
]
) and the Bochner-Minlos theorem applies.
In following this argument, it is important to treat separately the cases of positive and negative scaling dimension, as we will now explain.

This construction might seem beyond the scope of our paper: after all, except for the choice of covariance,
Z_{{}_{\rm GFF}}[f]
is the standard generating functional for Gaussian correlators, familiar to any field theorist.
And indeed at the end things are essentially as straightforward as they seem, if
0<\Delta_{\varphi}<1/2
. However, outside such range things become more involved, and following the steps of a rigorous construction leads to important insights.

Assume that
C(x)
is normalized in such a way that in momentum space it reads

\tilde{C}(p)=\frac{1}{|p|^{1-2\Delta_{\varphi}}}\;.

(A.5)

In other words, the covariance is precisely defined as the fractional Laplacian (e.g.
[
106
]
) of power
-\zeta=\Delta_{\varphi}-1/2
.
One would like to apply the Bochner-Minlos theorem with the characteristic functional being given as above, with the choice

\langle\varphi[f]\varphi[f]\rangle=||f||^{2}_{H^{\Delta_{\varphi}-1/2}(\mathbb{R})}\equiv\int\frac{{\rm d}p}{2\pi}\,\tilde{f}(-p)\,|p|^{2\Delta_{\varphi}-1}\,\tilde{f}(p)\;,

(A.6)

where
||f||_{H^{-\zeta}(\mathbb{R})}
is by definition the norm on the Sobolev space
H^{-\zeta}(\mathbb{R})
, i.e. the Hilbert space completion of the set of Schwartz functions having finite norm.
For
0<\Delta_{\varphi}<1/2
, i.e.
0<\zeta<1/2
, the norm (
A.6
) is finite for all Schwartz functions, hence the existence of the GFF as a probability measure is established.
27
27

27

The upper bound
\Delta_{\varphi}<1/2
comes from demanding that the norm is finite also in position space.

On the contrary, for
\Delta_{\varphi}<0
, i.e.
\zeta>1/2
, the singularity at
p=0
renders the norm divergent, unless
\tilde{f}^{(n)}(0)=0
for
n\leq\lfloor-\Delta_{\varphi}\rfloor
.
Therefore, in the latter case one introduces the subspace
\mathcal{S}_{r}(\mathbb{R})
of Schwartz space spanned by test functions that in momentum space vanish at the origin, together with all the derivatives of order less or equal to the nonnegative integer
r
(equivalently, such that in position space
\int{\rm d}x\,\mathcal{P}(x)f(x)=0
for all polynomials
\mathcal{P}(x)
of degree
r
).
The norm (
A.6
) is then finite in the restricted space of test functions
\mathcal{S}_{\lfloor-\Delta_{\varphi}\rfloor}(\mathbb{R})
, and we can establish the existence of the corresponding GFF measure on its dual space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\lfloor-\Delta_{\varphi}\rfloor}(\mathbb{R})
.
We refer to
[
57
]
for more details.
28
28

28

Notice that in
[
57
]
the parameter
s
corresponds to half our

, i.e. it coincides with the

introduced above (the latter being a notation used for example also in
[
25
,
26
]
).

In the remainder of this appendix we review some other aspects of the GFF, distinguishing the cases of positive and negative scaling dimensions, and introducing some of the notation used in the main body of the paper.

A.1
GFF with positive scaling dimension

As remarked above, the GFF can be introduced without the need of an action functional.
Nevertheless, it is convenient to write an explicit action, as for example this simplifies the derivation of Schwinger-Dyson equations. Moreover, this will make the nonlocal nature of the GFF explicit.

For positive scaling dimenssion, we write
\Delta_{\varphi}=(1-s)/2
, with
s<1
, as in Section
2
, which corresponds to taking
\zeta=s/2
.
In path integral language, we can view the 1d GFF as a functional integral over
\varphi
, and write for example
29
29

29

The normalization of the Gaussian is implicit in the functional measure
[d\varphi]
, i.e.
\int[d\varphi]e^{-S_{{}_{\rm GFF}}[\varphi]}=1
.

C(x)\equiv\langle\varphi(x)\varphi(0)\rangle\equiv\int[d\varphi]e^{-S_{{}_{\rm GFF}}[\varphi]}\varphi(x)\varphi(0)\;.

(A.7)

The action
S_{{}_{\rm GFF}}[\varphi]
is most naturally defined in momentum space. We normalize it as

S_{{}_{\rm GFF}}[\varphi]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{{\rm d}p}{2\pi}\,\tilde{\varphi}(-p)|p|^{s}\tilde{\varphi}(p)\;,

(A.8)

so that the covariance takes the precise form (
A.5
), as can be seen starting from (
A.2
) with
d\mu(\varphi)=[d\varphi]e^{-S_{{}_{\rm GFF}}[\varphi]}
, and using the translation invariance of
[d\varphi]
to obtain (
A.4
) with norm (
A.6
).

The appearance of
|p|^{s}
in the action makes evident its definition in terms of fractional Laplacian.
Indeed, among several equivalent definitions
[
106
]
, the easiest definition of the fractional Laplacian is in Fourier space, where
(-\partial_{x}^{2})^{s/2}\varphi(x)
is defined as the multiplication operator
|p|^{s}\tilde{\varphi}(p)
.
Going to position space one finds a representation as a hypersingular integral operator:

(-\partial^{2})^{s/2}\varphi(x)=\lim_{r\to 0}\,c_{s}\int_{|x-x^{\prime}|>r}{\rm d}x^{\prime}\,\frac{\varphi(x)-\varphi(x^{\prime})}{|x-x^{\prime}|^{1+s}}\;,

(A.9)

where

c_{s}=-\frac{2^{s}\,\Gamma(\frac{1+s}{2})}{\pi^{1/2}\Gamma(-s/2)}=\Gamma(s+1)\sin(\frac{\pi s}{2})/\pi\;,

(A.10)

This can be derived by first writing

(p^{2})^{s/2}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(-s/2)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}{\rm d}t\frac{e^{-tp^{2}}-1}{t^{1+s/2}}\,,

whose validity is trivially checked by rescaling
t\to t/p^{2}
and recognizing that the integral reduces to
|p|^{s}
times the Cauchy-Saalschütz representation of
\Gamma(-s/2)
for
0<s<2
.
The singular integral representation is then found by going back to position space and exchanging the order of integration
[
105
]
.

Multiplying (
A.9
) by
\frac{1}{2}\varphi(x)
and integrating over
x
, we arrive at the action written in position space:

\begin{split}S_{{}_{\rm GFF}}[\varphi]=&\frac{c_{s}}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm d}x_{1}{\rm d}x_{2}\frac{(\varphi(x_{1})-\varphi(x_{2}))^{2}}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{1+s}}\;,\end{split}

(A.11)

Written as in (
A.11
), the action is manifestly positive for
0<s<2
, and the integral is regular at
x_{1}\sim x_{2}
.
30
30

30

Sometimes the action is written as proportional to
\int{\rm d}x_{1}{\rm d}x_{2}\varphi(x_{1})\varphi(x_{2})/|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{1+s}
. This expression is clearly singular at
x_{1}\sim x_{2}
due to the kernel diverging with a power greater than one. It could be defined by analytic continuation from
s<0
, but that would make properties like positivity and shift invariance of the action less transparent.

The covariance in position space is of course the Fourier transform of
1/|p|^{s}
, which reads

C(x)=\frac{-c_{-s}}{|x|^{1-s}}\;,

(A.12)

and is a well-defined tempered distribution for
0<s<1
.
Notice that the restriction to positive
s
, i.e. the upper bound
\Delta_{\varphi}<1/2
, is also understood from the lattice point of view, as it is known that a positive

is needed for the existence of the thermodynamic limit
[

]
.
The restriction to
s<1
is instead needed for the positivity of

, without which the Schwartz space needs to be restricted, as explained above, and as further elaborated in the next subsection.

A.2
GFF with negative scaling dimension

We now consider the case
-1<\Delta_{\varphi}<0
, relevant to the construction of our model in Section
3
.
In this case, adopting a notation consistent with Section
3
, we rename the fundamental field as
\phi
, we denote the expectation value in this GFF theory by
\langle\cdot\rangle_{0}
, and we set
\Delta_{\phi}=-\delta/2
, i.e.
2\zeta=2-s=1+\delta
.

As explained above, in this range of scaling dimensions, the 1d GFF can be rigorously contructed as a probability measure on the space of distributions in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, the dual space of
\mathcal{S}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
, the latter being the space of Schwartz functions satisfying the constraint
\int{\rm d}x\,f(x)=0
.
Therefore, the covariance kernel is defined only up to an additive constant.
Indeed, the covariance kernel
C(x)
is defined as

\langle\phi[f_{1}]\phi[f_{2}]\rangle_{0}=\int{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y\,C(x-y)f_{1}(x)f_{2}(y)\;,

(A.13)

where
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
.
As a consequence, if a function
C(x)
satisfies (
A.13
), so does
C(x)+c
, for some constant
c
.
Notice, that by such definition, we also have

\langle\phi[f_{1}]\phi[f_{2}]\rangle_{0}=\int{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y\,\langle\phi(x)\phi(y)\rangle_{0}f_{1}(x)f_{2}(y)=-\frac{1}{2}\int{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}y\,\langle(\phi(x)-\phi(y))^{2}\rangle_{0}f_{1}(x)f_{2}(y)\;.

(A.14)

However, one should keep in mind that this equality only holds for
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(\mathbb{R})
. In particular, while
\langle\phi(x)\phi(y)\rangle_{0}
is defined only up to a constant,
\langle(\phi(x)-\phi(y))^{2}\rangle_{0}
is a well-defined quantity, the variance of the random variable
\phi(x)-\phi(y)
. The latter is indeed the stationary-increment form of the fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H=-\Delta_{\phi}
.
In field theoretic applications we are rather interested in local observables, hence the appropriate well-defined random variables are derivatives of
\phi
, and their products: since for example
\langle\partial\phi(x)\partial\phi(y)\rangle_{0}=\partial_{x}\partial_{y}\langle\phi(x)\phi(y)\rangle_{0}
, it is clear that no additive constant ambiguity survives.

The GFF theory has a similar action as before

S_{0}[\phi]=\frac{\mathcal{N}_{\delta}}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm d}x_{1}{\rm d}x_{2}\frac{(\phi(x_{1})-\phi(x_{2}))^{2}}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{2+\delta}}\;.

(A.15)

We can also (formally) express the action as
S_{0}[\phi]=\frac{1}{2}\phi\cdot C^{-1}\cdot\phi
, with obvious dot notation, and
C^{-1}\cdot\phi
to be interpreted as (proportional to) the fractional Laplacian (equation (
A.9
) with
s\to 2-s
).

For convenience, we will choose the normalization and the additive constant so that the covariance has our desired limit for
\delta\to 0
:

C(x)=-\frac{2}{\delta}\,(|x|^{\delta}-\kappa^{-\delta})=-2\log(\kappa|x|)+O(\delta)\;.

(A.16)

This normalization corresponds to choosing
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}=\frac{\delta}{2}c_{1+\delta}c_{-1-\delta}
in the action.

GFF as a boundary theory.

At
\delta=0
, the 1d GFF has a natural interpretation as a boundary theory.
Consider the half plane
\Sigma\simeq\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
, where we use coordinates
\mathbf{x}\equiv(x,y)
such that the boundary is located at
y=0
.
On
\Sigma
, we define the free theory of a bosonic bulk field
\Phi(\mathbf{x})
, with action normalized as

S_{0}[\Phi]=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Sigma}{\rm d}^{2}\mathbf{x}\,\partial_{\mu}\Phi\partial^{\mu}\Phi\;,

(A.17)

and with Neumann boundary conditions, i.e.

\displaystyle\partial_{y}\Phi(x,y)\rvert_{y=0}=0\,,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\,.

(A.18)

In other words, the restriction of the bulk field
\Phi(x,y)
to the boundary

\displaystyle\Phi(x,0)=\phi(x)\;,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\;,

(A.19)

is a dynamical field with propagator
31
31

31

If we denote
C(\mathbf{x})=-\log|\mathbf{x}|
the propagator on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, such that
-\frac{1}{2\pi}\partial_{\mu}\partial^{\mu}C(\mathbf{x})=\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{x})
, then the propagator on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
with Neumann boundary conditions at
y=0
is

C(\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2})+C(\mathbf{x}_{1}-\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{2})\,,

where we defined
\bar{\mathbf{x}}=(x,-y)
.
This gives an effective factor of two for the boundary-to-boundary propagator (
y_{1}=y_{2}=0
).

C(x)\equiv\langle\phi(x)\phi(0)\rangle_{0}=-2\log(\kappa|x|)\;.

(A.20)

The non-local action of eq. (
A.15
) is recovered from (
A.17
) with the help of a result by Caffarelli and Silvestre
[

, according to which a Dirichlet boundary condition is mapped to a Neumann boundary condition with the aid of a fractional Laplacian. In two dimensions, this means that if
\Phi(\mathbf{x})
satisfies

\partial^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\Phi(x,y)=0\,,

(A.21)

with Dirichlet boundary condition (

\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}\partial_{y}\Phi(x,y)=-(-\partial_{x}^{2})^{1/2}\phi(x)\equiv\frac{1}{\pi}\int{\rm d}x^{\prime}\,\frac{\phi(x)-\phi(x^{\prime})}{|x-x^{\prime}|^{2}}\;.

(A.22)

Therefore, integrating (
A.17
) by parts and using the equations of motion of
\Phi(\mathbf{x})
, that in the path integral formalism is equivalent to performing the Gaussian integral, we are left with the boundary term

S_{0}[\phi]=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm d}x_{1}{\rm d}x_{2}\frac{(\phi(x_{1})-\phi(x_{2}))^{2}}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{2}}\;,

(A.23)

that coincides with the
\delta\to 0

are equivalent to the field equations for
\phi(x)
.

By analytic continuation, this connection can be generalized to
\delta>0
, so that the GFF becomes equivalent to conformal line defect for a free theory in noninteger dimension
D=2-\delta
. Although unitarity of the combined bulk and defect system is not guaranteed in fractional dimension (see e.g.
[
109
]
), this defect description has the advantage that it is manifestly local.

Appendix B
From continuum 1d LRI to AYK model

We provide here a simple, but not rigorous, derivation of the AYK model, and its Coulomb gas limit, from the continuum
long-range Ising model of equation (
2.2
). First, we rewrite the latter as

\begin{split}S[\varphi]=&-\frac{c_{s}}{2s(1-s)}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y\,\left(|x-y|^{1-s}-a^{1-s}\right)\,\partial_{x}\varphi(x)\partial_{y}\varphi(y)+\frac{\lambda_{4}}{4}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm d}x\left(\varphi(x)^{2}-\rho^{2}\right)^{2}\\
&-\lambda_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm d}x\,\varphi(x)+\frac{\theta}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm d}x\,\big(\partial_{x}\varphi(x)\big)^{2}\;,\end{split}

(B.1)

where in the first term we have integrated by parts once in
x
and once in
y
, and we have subtracted a vanishing term (the product of two integrals of total derivatives) so that the limit
s\to 1
is manifestly going to produce a logarithm. In the potential we have instead introduced
\rho^{2}=-\lambda_{2}/\lambda_{4}
(assuming
\lambda_{2}<0
) and discarded a constant term, and we have introduced a constant source (
\lambda_{1}
) and a short-range term (
\propto\theta
) for full comparison to the original construction of Anderson and Yuval and of Kosterlitz.

We now consider the limit
\lambda_{4}\to+\infty
at finite
\rho
, which is justified by the behavior of the bare couplings near the fixed point, when using a momentum cutoff. In such limit, the potential enforces the condition
\varphi(x)=\pm\rho
, and a generic configuration is of the type drawn in figure
2
, which is a continuous version of the blocks of up or down Ising spins.
Therefore, the derivative of the field is nonzero (and divergent) only at the points where the sign changes (also known as kinks and anti-kinks).
That is, the field derivative configurations are restricted to take the form

\partial\varphi_{n}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }n=0,\\
\pm\rho\sum_{i=1}^{2n}(-1)^{i}\delta(x-x_{i}),&\text{if }n>0\text{ and }\frac{L}{2}>x_{1}>x_{2}>\ldots>x_{2n}>-\frac{L}{2},\end{cases}

(B.2)

where the even number of delta functions is due to having assumed that outside the interval
[-\frac{L}{2},\frac{L}{2}]
there are no kinks and the field has the same sign on both sides of it.

In order to tame singular expressions, we use a heat kernel regularization for the delta functions, namely, we replace

\delta(x)\to\delta_{a}(x)\equiv\frac{\sqrt{2}}{a}e^{-2\pi\frac{x^{2}}{a^{2}}}\;,

(B.3)

where the normalization is chosen such that
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm d}x\,\delta_{a}(x)^{2}=1/a
.
The latter will thus provide a regularization of the short-range term.

Figure 2
:
A typical low-temperature configuration of kinks and antikinks in the LRI model, with
n=2
and
a/L\sim 10^{-2}
.

Plugging the field configuration (
B.2
), with regularized delta functions, into the action (
B.1
), we obtain

\begin{split}S[\varphi_{n}]=&-\frac{c_{s}\,\rho^{2}}{2s(1-s)}\sum_{i\neq j}^{1\ldots 2n}(-1)^{i+j}\left(|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{1-s}-a^{1-s}\right)+\lambda_{1}\rho\sum_{i}(-1)^{i}(x_{i}-x_{i-1})\\
&+2n\rho^{2}\left(\frac{\theta}{2a}+\frac{c_{s}\,a^{1-s}f(1-s)}{2s(1-s)}\right)\;,\end{split}

(B.4)

where we have discarded terms of order
O(a|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{-s})
relative to those of order
O(|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{1-s})
, and we have introduced a function
f(z)=\kappa z+O(z^{2})
, which results from the integration over pairs of kinks at
x_{i}=x_{j}
.

The resulting action has the same form as the classical Hamiltonian of the AYK model
[
23
]
, see (
2.20
), and
in the limit
s\to 1
, it becomes

S[\varphi_{n}]=-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2\pi}\sum_{i\neq j}^{1\ldots 2n}(-1)^{i+j}\log(|x_{i}-x_{j}|/a)+\lambda_{1}\rho\sum_{i}(-1)^{i}(x_{i}-x_{i-1})+n\rho^{2}(\theta/a+\kappa/\pi)\;,

(B.5)

which was previously obtained by Anderson and Yuval
[
22
]
.

In the partition function, we accordingly replace the functional measure by a sum over configurations of the type (
B.2
), and we arrive at the partition function (
2.18
) of a Coulomb gas with charges of alternating sign, with the following identifications:

u=\exp(-\rho^{2}(\theta/a+\kappa/\pi)/2)\;,\quad K=\rho^{2}/2\pi\;,\quad H/a=2\lambda_{1}\rho\;.

(B.6)

Such relations are nonuniversal, so it is not surprising that in our derivation in the continuum they differ slightly from those obtained by Anderson and Yuval.
However, they are qualitatively similar, and in particular they imply that the LRI model is a line in the
\{u,K\}
plane, parametrized by
\rho^{2}
, because
\theta
is an irrelevant coupling.

Appendix C
Alternative formulations and gauging

C.1
Coherent state representation

The partition function in (
3.9
) is expressed as mix of path integral and operator pictures.
A more homogeneous representation is possible by noticing that the defect operator
\mathcal{D}
in (
3.13
) can be thought as an evolution operator with a time-dependent Hamiltonian, if
x
is interpreted as Euclidean time.
This leads to the so-called coherent state representation (see for example
[
65
,
52
,
63
,
64
]
), that is, a path integral representation in terms of a complex bosonic spinor
z(x)=\{z_{1}(x),z_{2}(x)\}
, subject to the constraint
\bar{z}(x)z(x)=1
.
Following the same construction as in the references above, we can write

\tr\mathcal{D}=\int[dzd\bar{z}]\delta(\bar{z}z-1)e^{-S_{\mathcal{D}}[z,\phi]}\;,

(C.1)

with

S_{\mathcal{D}}[z,\phi]=\int{\rm d}x\,\left(\bar{z}(x)\partial z(x)-g\big(\hat{S}_{+}(x)V_{+}(x)+\hat{S}_{-}(x)V_{-}(x)\big)-h\,\hat{S}_{3}(x)\,\chi(x)\right)\;,

(C.2)

where now the spin operators are represented as

\hat{S}_{\pm}(x)=\bar{z}(x)\hat{\sigma}_{\pm}z(x)\;,\qquad\hat{S}_{3}(x)=\bar{z}(x)\hat{\sigma}_{3}z(x)\;.

(C.3)

The coherent state representation makes it easier to exploit field equations, which now write

\frac{\delta(S_{0}+S_{\mathcal{D}})}{\delta\phi}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad C^{-1}\cdot\phi(x)=\mathrm{i}g\big(\hat{S}_{+}(x)V_{+}(x)-\hat{S}_{-}(x)V_{-}(x)\big)-\frac{\mathrm{i}h}{\sqrt{2}}\,\partial\hat{S}_{3}(x)\;.

(C.4)

In order to eliminate
\partial\hat{S}_{3}(x)
, and recover (
3.30
), we can then use a linear combination of the field equations for
z
and
\bar{z}
:

\bar{z}\hat{\sigma}_{3}\frac{\delta S_{\mathcal{D}}}{\delta\bar{z}}-\frac{\delta S_{\mathcal{D}}}{\delta z}\hat{\sigma}_{3}z=0\;,

(C.5)

with which we recover the equivalent of (
3.27
):

\partial_{x}\hat{S}_{3}=2g(\hat{S}_{+}V_{+}-\hat{S}_{-}V_{-})\;.

(C.6)

C.2
Gauging

In the coherent state representation, the
U(1)
symmetry acts as

\phi(x)\to\phi(x)+\alpha/b_{0}\;,\qquad z(x)\to e^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\alpha}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{3}}z(x)\;,

(C.7)

and it could thus be gauged by introducing a gauge field
A(x)
via the replacements
32
32

32

The first replacement requires that we first integrate by part twice in the nonlocal kinetic term:

\begin{split}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y\frac{(\phi(x)-\phi(y))^{2}}{|x-y|^{3-s}}&=-\frac{1}{(1-s)(2-s)}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y(\phi(x)-\phi(y))^{2}\partial_{x}\partial_{y}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{1-s}}\\
&=\frac{2}{(1-s)(2-s)}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y\frac{\partial\phi(x)\partial\phi(y)}{|x-y|^{1-s}}\;.\end{split}

\partial_{x}\phi(x)\to\partial_{x}\phi(x)+A(x)/b_{0}\;,\qquad\partial_{x}z(x)\to Dz(x)\equiv(\partial_{x}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\hat{\sigma}_{3}A(x))z(x)\;,

(C.8)

with the gauge transformation

A(x)\to A(x)-\partial_{x}\alpha(x)\;.

(C.9)

We thus obtain the total action

\begin{split}S_{\rm gauge}&[\phi,A,z,\bar{z}]=\frac{2\,\mathcal{N}_{s}}{(1-s)(2-s)}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y\frac{(\partial\phi(x)+A(x))(\partial\phi(y)+A(y))}{|x-y|^{1-s}}\\
&+\int{\rm d}x\,\left(\bar{z}(x)Dz(x)-g\big(\hat{S}_{+}(x)V_{+}(x)+\hat{S}_{-}(x)V_{-}(x)\big)-h\,\hat{S}_{3}(x)\,\left(\chi(x)+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\sqrt{2}}A(x)\right)\right)\;.\end{split}

(C.10)

Since in
d=1
gauge fields are non-dynamical, they could in principle be eliminated from the action. In fact
A(x)
only appears quadratically, so it could be easily be integrated out. This would lead to new terms in the effective action, which however are irrelevant under the RG flow.
Alternatively, we can fix the gauge
A(x)=0
. Either way, the only effect of gauging would be to restrict observables to be gauge singlets.
Moreover, we gain the equations of motion of
A(x)

\frac{\delta S_{\rm gauge}}{\delta A(x)}=0\quad\underset{A=0}{\Rightarrow}\quad(1-\sqrt{2}h)\,\hat{S}_{3}(x)\propto\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm d}y\frac{\partial\phi(y)}{|x-y|^{1-s}}\;,

(C.11)

that we recognize being the shadow relation between
\hat{S}_{3}(x)
and
\chi(x)

C.3
Nonlinear sigma model formulation

It is actually possible to recast the model (
3.9
) in yet another form, one that makes the gauging look more standard.
Introducing the complex field
\mathcal{U}(x)
, subject to the constraint
\bar{\mathcal{U}}(x)\mathcal{U}(x)=1
, we claim that (
3.9
) is equivalent to the partition function for the following
U(1)
nonlinear sigma model:

S_{\rm NLSM}[\phi]=-\frac{2\,c_{2-s}}{8\pi(1-s)(2-s)b_{0}^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y\frac{\bar{\mathcal{U}}(x)\partial_{x}\mathcal{U}(x)\,\bar{\mathcal{U}}(y)\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}(y)}{|x-y|^{1-s}}\mathds{1}-\log\Tr\mathcal{D}\;,

(C.12)

with

\mathcal{D}\equiv\mathcal{P}\exp\left(\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}{\rm d}x\,\big(g\left(\hat{\sigma}_{+}\mathcal{U}(x)+\hat{\sigma}_{-}\bar{\mathcal{U}}(x)\right)+\frac{h}{\sqrt{2}\,b_{0}}\,\hat{\sigma}_{3}\,\bar{\mathcal{U}}(x)\partial_{x}\mathcal{U}(x)\big)\right)\;.

(C.13)

We can indeed solve easily the constraint and identify
\mathcal{U}(x)=e^{\mathrm{i}b_{0}\phi(x)}
, and the correspondence of the interactions is obvious.
For the Gaussian part, we have used again a double integration by parts.

In this formulation it is clear that the fundamental field is
\mathcal{U}(x)
, and not
\phi(x)
, which is introduced to solve the constraint and parametrize the circle. Notice that
\mathcal{M}(x)\equiv\bar{\mathcal{U}}(x)\partial_{x}\mathcal{U}(x)
is the Maurer-Cartan form, so it is natural to express the kinetic term with it. In fact, if the theory was local, we would have a noninteracting action that is quadratic both in terms of
\mathcal{M}(x)
and of
\mathcal{U}(x)
, because
\mathcal{M}(x)^{2}=-\partial_{x}\bar{\mathcal{U}}(x)\partial_{x}\mathcal{U}(x)
, by virtue of the constraint.
In the long-range case instead it is quadratic only in
\mathcal{M}(x)
.

The
U(1)
symmetry is now

\mathcal{U}(x)\to\mathcal{U}(x)e^{2\mathrm{i}\alpha}\;,\qquad\hat{\sigma}_{i}\to e^{-\mathrm{i}\alpha\hat{\sigma}_{3}}\hat{\sigma}_{i}e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha\hat{\sigma}_{3}}\;,

(C.14)

and its gauging can be implemented by introducing a covariant derivative
D_{x}\mathcal{U}(x)=(\partial_{x}+2A(x))\mathcal{U}(x)
, plus the same treatment as above for the
z
sector.

Notice that the presence of the Pauli matrices sector, as well as the absence of rotation invariance in one dimension, allow the introduction of nontrivial interactions that would otherwise be incompatible with symmetries.

Notice also that the kinetic term in (
C.12
) differs from the long-range
O(2)
nonlinear sigma model studied in
[
93
]
in a crucial way. The action in
[
93
]
involves the product
\partial_{x}\bar{\mathcal{U}}(x)\,\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}(y)
, which differs from
-\bar{\mathcal{U}}(x)\partial_{x}\mathcal{U}(x)\,\bar{\mathcal{U}}(y)\partial_{y}\mathcal{U}(y)
for
x\neq y
. In particular, the latter is quadratic in
\phi
, while the former is not, thus explaining why the
O(2)
model of
[
93
]
has a nontrivial beta function, even without our
g
and
h
terms.

Appendix E
Logarithmic corrections to scaling near crossover

In this appendix, we study the logarithmic corrections to the scaling behavior of the critical 1d LRI Ising theory at the crossover. As discussed in the main text, at
s=1
the flow to the IR fixed-point theory at
g=h=0
is controlled by a marginally irrelevant operator, a linear combination of
\mathcal{O}_{g}
and
\mathcal{O}_{h}
. A standard argument
[
110
]
, also employed for the higher-dimensional LRI in appendix B of
[
9
]
, shows that in the presence of marginally irrelevant operators, the CFT predictions for the IR behavior of correlators at criticality receive logarithmic corrections.

Starting from the
s=1
theory, let us turn on the
\mathcal{O}_{h}
and
\mathcal{O}_{g}
perturbations.
The general solution of the the Callan–Symanzik equation for the two-point function of
\sigma(x)=\sigma_{3}
is

\displaystyle\langle\sigma(r)\sigma(0)\rangle=\frac{c(r)}{r^{2\Delta_{\sigma}}}\,,\quad r>0\,,

(E.1)

where
\Delta_{\sigma}
is the conformal dimension in the unperturbed theory.
Up to an overall function that is constant at leading order, we have that

\displaystyle c(r)\propto\exp\left\{-2\int_{1}^{r}{\rm d}\log r^{\prime}\;\gamma_{\sigma}(\bar{g}(r^{\prime},g_{0},h_{0}),\bar{h}(r^{\prime},g_{0},h_{0}))\right\}\,.

(E.2)

In the equation above,
\bar{g}
and
\bar{h}
are the running couplings, whereas
g_{0}
,
h_{0}
are the values of the couplings at
r=1
, i.e. the starting point of the RG flow. Finally,
\gamma_{\sigma}
is the anomalous dimension of
\sigma
, which in
4.4.2
was found to be

\displaystyle\gamma_{\sigma}=2g^{2}\,.

(E.3)

The running couplings are the solution to the differential equations

\displaystyle-\frac{dg}{d\log r}=\beta_{g}\,,\quad-\frac{dh}{d\log r}=\beta_{h}\,,

(E.4)

where
\beta_{g}
and
\beta_{h}
are the beta functions of the
s=1
theory in eq. (
4.12
), which here we truncate to the leading non-trivial order, i.e.
\beta_{g}=-2\sqrt{2}gh
and
\beta_{h}-\sqrt{2}g^{2}
.
In this approximation, the quantity
W=2h^{2}-g^{2}
is constant along the flow, i.e.
2h^{2}-g^{2}=2h_{0}^{2}-g_{0}^{2}
, and it parametrizes the deviation from the phase transition line, i.e. the separatrix between regions
I
and
II
in fig.
1
, panel
(a)
.

The flow equations are easily solved. In the region
I
(i.e.
W>0
,
h_{0}<0
,

), we find:

\displaystyle\bar{g}(r,g_{0},h_{0})=2\sqrt{W}\frac{\sqrt{A}e^{-2\sqrt{W}\log r}}{1-Ae^{-4\sqrt{W}\log r}}\,,

(E.5)

\displaystyle\bar{h}(r,g_{0},h_{0})=-\sqrt{W}\frac{\left(1+Ae^{-4\sqrt{W}\log r}\right)}{\sqrt{2}\left(1-Ae^{-4\sqrt{W}\log r}\right)}\,,

(E.6)

with
A=(\sqrt{2}h_{0}+\sqrt{W})/(\sqrt{2}h_{0}-\sqrt{W})
.

Tuning to the transition line,
W\to 0
, we obtain

\bar{g}^{2}(r,g_{0},h_{0})=\frac{4h_{0}^{2}}{\left(\sqrt{2}-4h_{0}\log r\right)^{2}}\,,

(E.7)

and thus

\begin{split}c(r)&\propto\exp\left\{-4\int_{1}^{r}{\rm d}\log r^{\prime}\,\bar{g}^{2}(r^{\prime},g_{0},h_{0})\right\}~\propto\exp\left\{-4\frac{h_{0}}{\sqrt{2}-4h_{0}\log r}\right\}~\\
&\simeq\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\log r}\right\}\simeq 1+\frac{1}{\log r}+\ldots\,,\end{split}

(E.8)

where in the last two steps we used the large
r
limit.
We see that the logarithmic corrections to scaling appear, but only as subleading behavior.
However, remembering that
\Delta_{\sigma}=0
and that at the critical temperature the 1d LRI model at
s=1
has a non-vanishing magnetization, the logarithmic correction gives a nontrivial result for the connected two-point function, as noticed early on in
[
111
]
.

It is interesting to also consider the two-point function of vertex operators, as it leads to a more standard outcome.
Consider the two-point function of
\mathcal{O}_{g}
, that at the fixed point
g=h=0
of the
s=1
theory is simply

\langle\mathcal{O}_{g}(r)\mathcal{O}_{g}(0)\rangle_{0}=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\,.

(E.9)

Turning on
g
and
h
, this will get corrections, starting at order
h
, and then with quadratic corrections
g^{2}
and
h^{2}
, and so on.
On the critical line (
W=0
),
g
and
h
are proportional to each other, hence to leading order we can retain just the linear contribution in
h
.
The latter leads to the correction

\langle\mathcal{O}_{g}(r)\mathcal{O}_{g}(0)\rangle=\frac{1}{r^{2(1-2\sqrt{2}h)}}+O(g^{2},h^{2})=\frac{1}{r^{2(1-\sqrt{2}h)^{2}}}+O(g^{2})\,,

(E.10)

where in the second step we used the exact result at
g=0
, see Section
3.2.1
.
This show that the anomalous dimension of
\mathcal{O}_{g}
is
-2\sqrt{2}h
, in agreement with what we obtain from the beta functions in the leading-order approximation.
In order to take into account the effect of
g>0
, we use again the CS equation, again tuned to the critical line
W=0
.
Using similar formulas as above, but with
\gamma_{\mathcal{O}_{g}}=-2\sqrt{2}h
, we find

\begin{split}c(r)&\propto\exp\left\{4\sqrt{2}\int_{1}^{r}{\rm d}\log r^{\prime}\,\bar{h}(r^{\prime},g_{0},h_{0})\right\}~\propto\exp\left\{-2\log(1-2\sqrt{2}h_{0}\log r)\right\}~\\
&\simeq\frac{1}{(1-2\sqrt{2}h_{0}\log r)^{2}}\sim\frac{1}{(\log r)^{2}}\,.\end{split}

(E.11)

Therefore, also the two-point function of
\mathcal{O}_{g}
displays logarithmic corrections to scaling, with exponent
-2
.

Appendix F
One- and two-point functions to
O(\delta)

In this appendix, we compute one- and two-point correlation functions with
\sigma,\chi
and
\mathcal{O}_{\pm}

2.2.1

F.1
One-point functions

The case of
\sigma
and
\chi

symmetry.
33
33

33

Away from criticality, we should find a symmetric phase where they still vanish, as well as a broken phase with
\langle\sigma\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}\neq 0

In the

\mathbb{C}^{2}

For
\mathcal{O}_{g}
, we find that
O(g^{2},h)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}_{g}(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=\frac{g}{2}\int{\rm d}y\,\tr\langle\mathrm{P}\mathcal{O}_{g}(x)\mathcal{O}_{g}(y)\rangle_{0}=\frac{2g}{a}+O(g^{3},hg)\,.

We can set the one-point function to zero by including a mixing term with the identity, i.e. we define the shifted operator

\langle[\mathcal{O}_{g}]\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}=O(g^{3},hg)

For
\mathcal{O}_{h}

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{O}_{h}(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{2}}{4}\int{\rm d}y_{1}\int{\rm d}y_{2}\,\tr\langle\mathrm{P}\mathcal{O}_{h}(x)\mathcal{O}_{g}(y_{1})\mathcal{O}_{g}(y_{2})\rangle_{0}+\frac{h}{2}\int{\rm d}y\,\tr\langle\mathrm{P}\mathcal{O}_{h}(x)\mathcal{O}_{h}(y)\rangle_{0}

\langle[\mathcal{O}_{h}]\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}=O(g^{4},hg^{2})

Lastly, we rewrite
\mathcal{O}_{\pm}
by replacing the bare
\mathcal{O}_{g,h}
in (
4.27

F.1
) and (

\displaystyle a^{\Delta_{\pm}}\,\mathcal{O}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}([\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{h}]\pm[\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{g}])+\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{8}[\tilde{\mathcal{O}}_{g}]+O(\delta)\,,

F.2
Two-point functions

We move on to the computation of the two-point functions. We have already computed the anomalous dimensions in Section
4.4
, and now focus on the finite part to extract the normalization of the operators, which is needed for the computation of OPE coefficients in Section
4.5
.

F.2.1
For
\sigma
and
\chi

Let us start with the two-point function of
\sigma
. Since
\tr\langle\sigma(x)\sigma(0)\rangle_{0}=1
, we have that:

\displaystyle\langle\sigma(x)\sigma(0)\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{g^{2}}{4}\int{\rm d}y_{1}\,{\rm d}y_{2}\tr\langle\mathrm{P}\sigma(x)\sigma(0)\mathcal{O}_{g}(y_{1})\mathcal{O}_{g}(y_{2})\rangle_{0,c}+\frac{h}{2}\int{\rm d}y\tr\langle\mathrm{P}\sigma(x)\sigma(0)\mathcal{O}_{h}(y)\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=1+4g^{2}\log\left(\frac{2a}{x}\right)+O(g^{4},hg^{2})\>.

(F.5)

(Above and below, we take
L/2>x>0>-L/2
.) We can remove the UV divergence as
a\to 0
by defining the renormalized operator

\displaystyle[\sigma]_{r}=Z_{\sigma}\sigma+\text{other possible mixings}\,,

(F.6)

with
Z_{\sigma}=1-2g^{2}\log(a/L)+O(g^{4},hg^{2})
. The renormalized two-point function then reads

\displaystyle\langle[\sigma(x)]_{r}[\sigma(0)]_{r}\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=1+4g^{2}\log\left(\frac{2L}{x}\right)+O(g^{4},hg^{2})\>.

(F.7)

In this renormalization scheme, the renormalized correlator does not depend on the UV cutoff, and thus one obtains the Callan–Symanzik equation for the bare correlator:

\displaystyle 0=a\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}a}\langle[\sigma(x)]_{r}[\sigma(0)]_{r}\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}=Z_{\sigma}^{2}\left(a\frac{\partial}{\partial a}-\beta_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial g_{i}}-2\gamma_{\sigma}\right)\langle\sigma(x)\sigma(0)\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}\,,

(F.8)

with the beta functions given in (
4.21
) and anomalous dimension
\gamma_{\sigma}\equiv-a\frac{d\log Z_{\sigma}}{da}=2g^{2}
.
We can use the Callan–Symanzik equation to resum the large logarithms in the
bare
correlator, and to find the explicit form of the latter at the IR fixed point

\displaystyle\langle\sigma(x)\sigma(0)\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}=\frac{a^{\delta}\mathcal{N}^{2}_{\sigma}}{(x^{2})^{\delta/2}}+O(\delta^{3/2})\,,\quad\mathcal{N}^{2}_{\sigma}=1+\delta\log 2\,,

(F.9)

where we have used the perturbative result (
F.2.1
) for the normalization factor.
Except for the latter, the result above turns out to be valid to all orders, thanks to the Schwinger-Dyson equation (
3.30
).
In fact, combining the latter with (
3.27
), we have

\partial_{x_{1}}\partial_{x_{2}}\langle\sigma(x_{1})\sigma(x_{2})\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}=-\frac{1}{g^{2}(b_{0}-\sqrt{2}h)^{2}}\langle C^{-1}\cdot\phi(x_{1})\,C^{-1}\cdot\phi(x_{2})\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}\propto\frac{1}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{2(1+d/2)}}\,,

(F.10)

where in the last step we used the fact that the dimension of
\phi
is not corrected, as usual in long-range models.

We now consider two-point functions involving
\chi
. First, note that there is a non-vanishing mixing with
\sigma
, as

\displaystyle\langle\sigma(x)\chi(0)\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{2}}{4}\int{\rm d}y_{1}\,{\rm d}y_{2}\tr\langle\mathrm{P}\sigma(x)\chi(0)\mathcal{O}_{g}(y_{1})\mathcal{O}_{g}(y_{2})\rangle_{0,c}+\frac{h}{2}\int{\rm d}y\tr\langle\mathrm{P}\sigma(x_{1})\chi(x_{2})\mathcal{O}_{h}(y)\rangle_{0}+O(g^{3},gh)\>.

\displaystyle=\frac{2h}{a}+\frac{2\sqrt{2}g^{2}\log 2}{a}+O(g^{3},gh)\,.

(F.11)

We can orthogonalize the two operators by defining a shifted
\chi
as

\displaystyle[\chi]=\chi+Z_{\chi\sigma}\sigma+\text{other possible mixings}\,,

(F.12)

with
Z_{\chi\sigma}=-\frac{2h}{a}-\frac{2\sqrt{2}g^{2}\log 2}{a}+O(g^{4},hg^{2})
.

Finally, for the two-point of
\chi
, corrections linear in
g
and in
h
are zero due to an odd number of Pauli matrices, while the
O(g^{2})
term

\displaystyle\frac{g^{2}}{4}\int{\rm d}y_{1}\,{\rm d}y_{2}\tr\langle\mathrm{P}\chi(x)\chi(0)\mathcal{O}_{g}(y_{1})\mathcal{O}_{g}(y_{2})\rangle_{0,c}\,,

(F.13)

vanishes, or in other words, the correlator
\int\tr\langle\mathrm{P}\chi(x)\chi(0)\mathcal{O}_{g}(y_{1})\mathcal{O}_{g}(y_{2})\rangle_{0}
is totally disconnected.
Hence, at the fixed point we have

\displaystyle\langle[\chi](x)[\chi](0)\rangle_{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\delta}{(x^{2})^{1-\delta/2}}+O(\delta^{3/2})\,,

(F.14)

which again is exact, up to normalization, because
\chi\propto\partial\phi
and the dimension of
\phi
is not corrected.

For
\mathcal{O}_{g}

Appendix G
The defect description of LRI

As demonstrated in
[
33
]
, the
p
-dimensional LRI CFT can be realized as a co-dimension
q=D-p
conformal defect for the free bulk massless scalar field
\Phi
, whihc propagates in the
D
-dimensional bulk. Reference
[
14
]
investigated the advantages of this construction to systematically constrain the dynamics of LRI using the defect conformal bootstrap approach
[
112
]
.

In this defect realization, the bulk is fictitious. The spectrum of the LRI CFT is built out of operators constrained to lie on the
p
-dimensional defect, in the zero transverse spin sector. This latter restriction follows from the fact that the conformal defect description enjoys a manifest
SO(p+1,1)\times SO(q)
symmetry, while in the original LRI there is no
SO(q)
symmetry.

G.1
The defect description from the
\varphi^{4}
formulation

As we discussed in Section
2
, the
\varphi^{4}
description of the
p
-dimensional LRI is given in terms of a generalized-free scalar field
\varphi
with quartic interaction – see equation (
1.2
). The non-local equation of motion implies that, at the IR fixed point,
\varphi
,
\varphi^{3}
form a shadow pair of operators with protected scaling dimensions

\Delta_{\varphi}=\frac{p-\epsilon}{4},\quad\Delta_{\varphi^{3}}=\frac{p+\epsilon}{4}\,,

(G.1)

where we have set
s=p/2+\epsilon
.

In the defect description, we interpret the field
\varphi(x)
as a defect mode of a free bulk massless scalar field
\Phi(y,x)
, which propagates in
D
fractional dimensions (
y
represent
q
transverse directions in the bulk, while
x
are coordinates along the
p
-dimensional defect). The action is
34
34

34

Compared to (
1.2
), here we have set
\lambda_{2}=0
in order to reach the IR fixed point.

S=\int{\rm d}^{q}y\,{\rm d}^{p}x\frac{1}{2}(\partial\Phi)^{2}+\int{\rm d}^{d}x\frac{\lambda_{4}}{4}\varphi(x)^{4}\,,

(G.2)

with the condition that
\Phi(0,x)=\varphi(x)
, so that integrating out the
q

In this description, we have that
\Delta_{\Phi}=D/2-1=\frac{p-\epsilon}{4}

The ‘defect modes’ of
\Phi
,
\varphi
and
\varphi^{3}

\Phi
(in the zero transverse spin sector) at the IR fixed point reads

\displaystyle\Phi(y,x)={b_{0}^{\Phi,+}}{}\psi_{0}^{+}(x)+{b_{0}^{\Phi,-}}{|y|^{p}}\psi_{0}^{-}(x)+\dots,

(G.3)

where the ellipsis denotes contributions from defect conformal descendants. The scaling dimensions of
\psi_{0}^{\pm}

(
\psi_{0}^{-}

It is interesting to note that the extension to the bulk necessarily comes with an ambiguity, corresponding to a change of boundary condition. For example, we are free to represent the same LRI as a defect in a different bulk theory, i.e. with the boundary condition
\Phi(0,x)=\varphi(x)^{3}

The defect description from the crossover for

As we discussed in Section
