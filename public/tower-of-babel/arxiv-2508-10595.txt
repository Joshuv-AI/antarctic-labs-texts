On Spectral Properties of Gradient-based Explanation Methods
Amir Mehrpanah, Erik Englesson, Hossein Azizpour

Abstract
Understanding the behavior of deep networks is crucial to increase our confidence in their results. Despite an extensive body of work for explaining their predictions, researchers have faced reliability issues, which can be attributed to insufficient formalism. In our research, we adopt novel probabilistic and spectral perspectives to formally analyze explanation methods. Our study reveals a pervasive spectral bias stemming from the use of gradient, and sheds light on some common design choices that have been discovered experimentally, in particular, the use of squared gradient and input perturbation. We further characterize how the choice of perturbation hyperparameters in explanation methods, such as SmoothGrad, can lead to inconsistent explanations and introduce two remedies based on our proposed formalism: (i) a mechanism to determine a standard perturbation scale, and (ii) an aggregation method which we call SpectralLens. Finally, we substantiate our theoretical results through quantitative evaluations.

3.2
Gradient Explainers with Perturbations Are Band-Pass Filters

In our probabilistic framework, various explanation methods can be decomposed into three components (1) an explainer, (2) a perturbation kernel and (3) a summary statistic. In this section, we analyze the spectral effects of the two core components, namely gradient and perturbation.
Importantly, we show that gradient acts as a high-pass filter, and perturbation acts as a low-pass filter.
Before that, we need a more formal definition for a high-pass and low-pass filter that suits our study. Assuming that
\widehat{m}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
denotes the FT of a perturbation kernel, we define low- and high-pass as follows.

Definition 1

A filter
\widehat{m}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
is low pass, if for a given
M>0
there exists a threshold
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{*}
such that for any
\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|>\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{*}\|
, we have
\|\widehat{m}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\|<M
, and it is high pass if such a condition holds for
\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|<\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{*}\|
.

For better readability, the proofs for the theorems and propositions are outlined in
Proof
, and we provide intuition as we progress.

Theorem 3.1

Any perturbation kernel
p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})
mitigates the attribution of high-frequency features; simply put,
any perturbation is a low-pass filter
.

The intuition is that any modification to a signal, would only remove information, and, as the intensity of perturbation increases, the removal goes from high-frequency components toward lower frequencies.

Theorem 3.2

The gradient operator amplifies the attribution of high-frequency features; simply put,
the gradient operator is a high-pass filter
.

From the stated theorems, one can see that a gradient-based explanation with perturbation creates a band-pass filter, whose shape is controlled by the perturbation hyperparameters (see
Figs.

2
and
3
).

Remark 2

This amplification-mitigation trade-off can be seen in several explanation methods, where perturbation kernels (low-pass filters) are utilized to mitigate the amplification effect (the high-pass filter) caused by the gradient. Therefore, the combined effect of the two filters usually forms
a band-pass filter
(see
Appendix

0.C
for a non-comprehensive list).

4.1
Finding an Optimal Perturbation Scale

Note that the squared gradients in spectral domain (
Eq.

3
) can be formulated as an inner product between the scaled PSD of the perturbation distribution and the PSD of the classifier
i.e
.

\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}\left[(\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}))^{2}\right]=4\pi^{2}\langle\operatorname{S}_{f},\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|^{2}\operatorname{S}_{\sqrt{p}}\rangle

Assuming unimodality, a nonzero contribution, and that the norm

\operatorname{S}_{f}

A higher cosine similarity guarantees recovering more information about the classifier.

Definition 2

The cosine similarity between the PSD of the classifier and that of the perturbation kernel given by

\text{Similarity}(\operatorname{S}_{f},\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|^{2}\operatorname{S}_{\sqrt{p}})=4\pi^{2}\frac{\langle\operatorname{S}_{f},\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|^{2}\operatorname{S}_{\sqrt{p}}\rangle}{\left\|\operatorname{S}_{f}\right\|\left\|\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|^{2}\operatorname{S}_{\sqrt{p}}\right\|}

(6)

This quantity can be used as a way to determine the optimal perturbation scale per pixel to maximize the information extracted with the squared gradients.
Note that, the word “optimal” refers to extracting the most information by the inner product,
whether deemed relevant by a human or not.

For one of the perturbation kernels we have presented earlier in

4.2
Aggregation of Information With Explanation Prior

We have shown that explanations are influenced by specific frequency bands, rather than representing the importance of individual pixels for prediction. Instead,
they indicate the contributions within a specific frequency range
. This observation motivates us to investigate a method for aggregation of information across different bands, which closely resembles an ensemble of IG and SG
[
57
,
55
]
which we call SpectralLens.

Definition 3

SpectralLens
is an ensemble explanation method, that aggregates information across different frequency bands as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p(\sigma)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}\left[(\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}))^{2}\right]\right]\propto\iint\operatorname{S}_{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|^{2}e^{-8\pi^{2}\sigma\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|^{2}}d\boldsymbol{\omega}p(\sigma)d\sigma

(9)

Where we refer to this method by SL
2
as it is using the squared gradients and we call
p(\sigma)
as an explanation prior, gauging the frequency bands that we choose to represent in an explanation.

Remark 7

SL
2
exhibits robustness against inconsistencies in SG
2
due to its aggregation of information from various bands. Therefore,
it is a preferable design choice not to employ a Dirac explanation prior with SG
2
, as it eliminates inconsistencies, depicted in
Fig.

1
. Nevertheless, as demonstrated in the
Section

5
, this approach may enhance the quantitative performance of explanations.

Intuition.

Considering
Eq.

4
, we realize that the combination of perturbation and gradient explainer creates overlapping band-pass carrier waves that modulate the amplitude of the spectral density of the classifier. Therefore, a new summary statistic is required that can reflect a wide range of frequency bands.
We explicitly identified the spectral properties of the explanations, recognizing that not all frequencies are equally desired. Explanations with very high frequencies primarily attribute predictions to pixels. Therefore, we utilize an
explanation prior

p(\sigma)
for a nuanced control of the spectral properties of explanations.
Although there are many possible alternatives for selecting an explanation prior, we used
p(\sigma)=\operatorname{Unif}(0,1)
for simplicity.

Definition 4

We define

2

\displaystyle\operatorname{ArgLens}(x_{i})=\arg\max_{\sigma}\left\{\operatorname{SG}^{2}(x_{i})\right\}

(10)

where

AL shows the frequency band at which the pixel’s attribution is maximized. Alternatively, it can be interpreted as the robustness of a pixel to noise.
We have visualized the AL for the Gaussian kernel in

The aggregation of explanations from different frequencies is visualized in

p(\sigma)=\delta(\sigma)

Interesting Properties of SpectralLens and ArgLens.

In the absence of a robust mathematical framework, the interpretation of explanations may be subjective. Thus, explanation methods must be grounded in solid theoretical principles. SL
2
addresses potential inconsistencies by aggregating attributions across frequency bands, ensuring fair representation of all pixels’ contributions.

As SG
2
results in bands-pass filters (see
Section

3.3
), its attributions reflect the changes in the decision boundary within a certain frequency band.
Furthermore, AL reveals the frequency band at which the attribution is maximized.
Hence, lower AL indicates that the attributions are likely governed by high-frequency components of the decision boundary, more sensitive to input noise, consequently less robust.
An ideal classifier with smooth decision boundaries,
i.e
.
robust features, leads to high AL everywhere, with a high intensity in SL
2
on relevant pixels and low intensity on irrelevant pixels.

Given the different purposes of pixels within an image, the frequency of activation may vary and can be visualized via AL. Since SL
2
and AL offer complementary information, they should be interpreted together. This approach offers a deeper understanding compared to a simplistic perspective, as it has been shown by Rahaman
et al
.

[
43
]
that high-frequency regions emerge later in training, are more challenging to fit, and are prone to change. On the contrary, low-frequency components of the decision boundary manifest earlier in training, are easier to fit, and exhibit greater stability with respect to noise.

It is important to clarify that “high-frequency” refers to features considered high-frequency by the model, and not necessarily frequency across spatial dimensions (
e.g
.
width, height).
Nonetheless, empirical findings demonstrate that high-frequency features in spatial domain are more likely to be identified as high frequency by the model (see

Limitations of Our Framework.

The choice of independent Gaussian perturbation is limited due to its inability to incorporate prior knowledge about the input space or data manifold characteristics, and other data modalities such as graphs and time series.
However, preferring simpler analytical forms within our framework limits the alternative choices.
Efficiency is another challenge compared to basic methods, as performing back-props at various noise scales is costly.
Moreover, our attribution of frequencies in visualizations, such as SL, may lack the desired generalizability across data points, as high-frequency regions in one image may correspond to low-frequency regions in another image (see
Appendix

0.I
).
Identifying generalizable attributions across images remains a subject for future investigation.

7
Conclusion

In this study, we deepen understanding of pixel attribution methodologies by rigorously analyzing their theoretical underpinnings, revealing a common spectral bias. Our analysis supports the experimental finding of recent works regarding the efficacy of using squared gradients. Moreover, we demonstrate how spectral properties can lead to inconsistent explanations and propose two design choices informed by our theoretical framework to address these inconsistencies: standardizing hyperparameters based on cosine similarity between the perturbation kernel and classifier, and aggregating explanations with a given prior. Despite the effectiveness of our approach, we acknowledge limitations and suggest avenues for further research. Lastly, we assess the suggested design choices using deletion and insertion scores.

Appendix 0.A
A Spectral Representation of Explanations

In this section, we derive spectral representation for explanations analogous to analysis of a linear time-invariant systems in spectral signal processing, and separate the analysis by the power of the gradients.
In
Section

3.3
, we have discussed the emergence of band-pass filters that cause the explanations to be inconsistent given different perturbation scales.

0.A.1
Spectral Representation of Prediction

To analyze explanations from the spectral viewpoint, we first need a version of
f
that acts on a signal
p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})
, which we denote by
F(p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}))
.
We assume linearity of response of
F
with respect to the input signal, which lets us denote the response of the signal by
\int F(\delta_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}(\boldsymbol{x}))p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
.
The linearity of response of a deep network is indeed a strong assumption, and it should be pointed out that the validity of this assumption is tightly coupled with the concentration of the perturbation distribution around the vicinity of the sample being explained.
As we are analysing the networks in small neighborhoods of a sample we assume that the Fourier transforms exist and our function has a linear response with respect to its signal.
Finally, assuming that the impulse response can be obtained by a forward pass
i.e
.

f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})=F(\delta_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}(\boldsymbol{x}))
, we can write the response of the system
F
to the signal
p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})
by

\displaystyle F(p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}))

\displaystyle=\int F(\delta_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}}(\boldsymbol{x}))p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}

(11)

\displaystyle=\int f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}

(12)

\displaystyle=\int\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\widehat{p}(\boldsymbol{\omega})d\boldsymbol{\omega}

(13)

where the last equality follows from the Plancherel theorem
[
46
]
.
The result of the application of a model to the signal (or perturbation distribution) can be written as a convolution
f*p
, therefore, one may use the “perturbation kernel” and “perturbation distribution” interchangeably.
This representation helps us to perform a spectral analysis of the explanations presented in the next section.

(15)

note that the actual pixel values cause a shift in Fourier domain, which can be ignored when assuming the origin is set at the sample being explained.
Let us denote the real even and imaginary odd components of a function
\widehat{f}
by
\widehat{f}_{E}
and,
\widehat{f}_{O}
respectively (note that as we are using real valued functions, other components are zero).
Then we can further simplify the last integral and gain interesting insights about the gradients and the information each perturbation kernel extracts

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}[\nabla f]

\displaystyle=\int i2\pi\boldsymbol{\omega}\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\widehat{p}(\boldsymbol{\omega})d\boldsymbol{\omega}

(16)

\displaystyle=

(17)

\displaystyle=

(18)

(19)

\displaystyle=

(20)

\displaystyle=

Squared Gradients in Spectral Domain

Let function
g(\boldsymbol{x})=\int\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\sqrt{p(\boldsymbol{x}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
,
expanding the autocorrelation function
\operatorname{R}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
for
g
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{R}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\tau})

\displaystyle=\int g(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\overline{g(\boldsymbol{x})}d\boldsymbol{x}

(22)

\displaystyle=\iint\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})\sqrt{p(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\tau}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})}d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}\overline{\int\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})\sqrt{p(\boldsymbol{x}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})}d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}}d\boldsymbol{x}

(23)

\displaystyle=\iint\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})\overline{\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})}\left(\int\sqrt{p(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\tau}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})}\overline{\sqrt{p(\boldsymbol{x}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})}}d\boldsymbol{x}\right)d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\iint\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})\overline{\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})}\operatorname{R}_{\sqrt{p}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\tau})d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}

(25)

where overbars show complex conjugate.
This can establish a connection between the autocorrelation of
g(\boldsymbol{x})
and that of the perturbation kernel
p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x})
.
Moreover, from the Wiener-Khinchin theorem
[
18
]
, we know that
\operatorname{S}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\widehat{\operatorname{R}}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
, i.e. the Fourier transform of autocorrelation function of
g
is equal to its spectral density.
By further expanding the expression, one can establish a connection between the spectral density of
g(\boldsymbol{x})
and
p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\operatorname{S}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\omega})

\displaystyle=\int\operatorname{R}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\psi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\tau})d\boldsymbol{\tau}

(26)

\displaystyle=\iiint\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})\overline{\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})}\operatorname{R}_{\sqrt{p}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\psi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\tau})d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}d\boldsymbol{\tau}

(27)

\displaystyle=\iint\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})\overline{\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})}\left(\int\operatorname{R}_{\sqrt{p}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\tau})\psi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\tau})d\boldsymbol{\tau}\right)d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}

(28)

\displaystyle=\iint\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})\overline{\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})}\left(\psi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})\operatorname{S}_{\sqrt{p}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\right)d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}

(29)

\displaystyle=\left(\int\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})\psi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}\right)\overline{\left(\int\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})\psi(\boldsymbol{\omega},\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2})d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2}\right)}\operatorname{S}_{\sqrt{p}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})

(30)

\displaystyle=\left|i2\pi\boldsymbol{\omega}\hat{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\right|^{2}\operatorname{S}_{\sqrt{p}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})

(31)

\displaystyle=4\pi^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|^{2}\operatorname{S}_{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\operatorname{S}_{\sqrt{p}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})

(32)

The major difference with the conventional spectral analysis of signals

56
]
, is the term multiplied by the spectra of

which is the result of the gradient operator. Finally, one can write the expected value of squared gradients in spectral domain as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}\left[(\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}))^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\int(\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}))^{2}\left(\sqrt{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}\right)^{2}d\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}

(33)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{R}_{g}(0)

(34)

\displaystyle=\int\operatorname{S}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\psi(-\boldsymbol{\omega},0)d\boldsymbol{\omega}

(35)

\displaystyle=\int\operatorname{S}_{g}(\boldsymbol{\omega})d\boldsymbol{\omega}

(36)

\displaystyle=\int 4\pi^{2}\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|^{2}\operatorname{S}_{f}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\operatorname{S}_{\sqrt{p}}(\boldsymbol{\omega})d\boldsymbol{\omega}

(37)

A counterintuitive result here is that any Dirac perturbation kernel would have the same form, which is the result of assuming that the origin is centered around the example being explained.

This section contains a more formal approach towards the theorems and propositions stated in this work.

Theorem 0.B.1

Any perturbation kernel
p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})
mitigates the attribution of high-frequency features; simply put,
any perturbation is a low-pass filter
.

Proof

Let
\widehat{p}(\boldsymbol{\omega})
denote the FT of the absolutely integrable perturbation kernel
p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})\in L^{1}
. It follows directly from the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma
[
52
]
, that for every
M\in(0,1)
3
3

3

The
\|\widehat{p}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\|\leq 1
inequality follows from the properties of the characteristic function of any probability distribution.
there exists a
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{*}
that
\forall\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|~>~\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{*}\|
we have

\|\widehat{p}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\|~<~M
.

Theorem 0.B.2

The gradient operator amplifies the attribution of high-frequency features; simply put,
the gradient operator is a high-pass filter
.

Proof

Given
M>0
, let
\boldsymbol{\omega}^{*}=\sqrt{M}\mathbf{1}
. Then for any
\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|<\|\boldsymbol{\omega}^{*}\|
we have
\|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|<M
, which shows that gradient acts as a high-pass filter in spectral domain.

Proposition 1

The SmoothGrad-Squared pixel attribution can contradict itself by changing the hyperparameters. Simply,

the Rashomon effect can occur in the SmoothGrad-Squared explanations
.

Proof

For simplicity, assume that the total contribution of all pixels to a prediction in the image
\boldsymbol{x}
is equal.
Formally, we have
\forall i;\int\widehat{f}_{i}(\omega)^{2}d\omega=1
, where
\widehat{f}_{i}(\omega)^{2}
represents the contribution of the pixel
x_{i}
to the prediction at frequency
\omega
.
Furthermore, assume that the features, although having a total equal contribution, would contribute at different frequencies.
For simplicity, we assume that
\widehat{f}_{i}(\omega)^{2}=\delta_{\omega_{i}}(\omega)
for
\omega\geq 0
.
Consequently, following
Eq.

4
, the contribution of pixel
x_{i}
at noise level
\sigma
is given by

\displaystyle{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}\left[(\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}))^{2}\right]\mathchoice{\vphantom{\big|}}{}{}{}\right|_{x_{i}}}

\displaystyle\propto\int\omega^{2}\delta_{\omega_{i}}(\omega)e^{-8\pi^{2}\sigma^{2}\omega^{2}}d\omega

(38)

\displaystyle=\omega_{i}^{2}e^{-8\pi^{2}\sigma^{2}\omega_{i}^{2}}

(39)

For brevity, denote
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}\left[(\nabla f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}))^{2}\right]\mathchoice{\vphantom{\big|}}{}{}{}\right|_{x_{i}}}=\operatorname{A}_{\sigma}(x_{i})

|\omega_{i}|<|\omega_{j}|

i
and

\operatorname{A}_{\sigma}(x_{i})<\operatorname{A}_{\sigma}(x_{j})

,

\operatorname{A}_{\sigma}(x_{i})>\operatorname{A}_{\sigma}(x_{j})
if
\sigma~>~|\bar{\omega}|

|\bar{\omega}|\propto\frac{\ln(|\omega_{j}|)-\ln(|\omega_{i}|)}{\omega_{j}^{2}-\omega_{i}^{2}}
.

This shows that a pixel that does not appear to be important with one hyperparameter may turn out to be important with another hyperparameter, therefore, leading to inconsistent explanations.

\nabla

\boldsymbol{x}\nabla

In

[

The PSD of this kernel is of the form

\widehat{p}(\boldsymbol{\omega})=\sum_{j}e^{2i\pi\boldsymbol{x}_{j}}+e^{2i\pi\boldsymbol{s}_{j}}

Closely related to the Dirac Comb distribution is when, instead of masking pixels, a Gaussian filter is applied to the image

Appendix 0.D
Finite Difference Explainers

To define an explainer, some of the explanation methods use the prediction of the model on a given sample and its perturbations
[
42
]
. We show that their explainer can be written in the form of a finite difference up to a constant with respect to the perturbation distribution. Assume
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
be the multiplicative perturbation mask sampled from some perturbation distribution with expected value of
\boldsymbol{M}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{PredictionExplainer}(f,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}[\boldsymbol{\alpha}f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})]

(42)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}[\boldsymbol{\alpha}f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})-\boldsymbol{\alpha}f(\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{\alpha}f(\boldsymbol{x})]

(43)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}[\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x})\left(\frac{f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})-f(\boldsymbol{x})}{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x}}\right)+\boldsymbol{\alpha}f(\boldsymbol{x})]

(44)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}[\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x})\Delta f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})+\boldsymbol{\alpha}f(\boldsymbol{x})]

(45)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}[\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x})\Delta f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})]+f(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]

(46)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}[\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x})\Delta f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})]+\boldsymbol{M}f(\boldsymbol{x})

(47)

.
Note that the subtraction does not change the visualizations due to the normalization.
Hence, after normalization, one can write
\operatorname{PredictionExplainer}(f,\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})}[\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x})\Delta f(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}})]
, which means that the explainer is set to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{x})\Delta
.
This demonstrates that these methods are also estimating the gradient, and the low-pass filter is typically attained by having a very low resolution for the perturbation mask.

Another way to understand the low-pass filter is comparing the derived finite difference by the definition of gradient. In the implementations of finite difference methods, we use expectation over perturbed samples, which is equivalent to assuming a constant denominator in the definition of finite difference. This means that small perturbations to the input are diminished (compared to gradient, where the denominator approaches to infinity for small perturbations).

Appendix 0.E
Notes on Reproducibility

It is essential to recognize that Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) should be integrated into the broader AI development pipeline. Consequently, the design of XAI methods must account for several logical considerations inherent to AI pipeline construction. A critical yet often overlooked aspect is the assumption that the label is unknown during the inference. This assumption should be consistently maintained when generating explanations. As a result, methods that rely on the label for explanation computation are likely to be less effective in practical applications. That being said, in all of our implementations, we have used the most probable class in the prediction as the class that needs to be explained. This, in turn, can make explanations less useful if the model has a low accuracy. Furthermore, we considered using log-softmax of the model output, as suggested by the recent work
[
60
]
.

0.E.1
Choosing a Proper Perturbation Cutoff

An underexplored subject regarding the practical details of generating explanations pertains to selecting a proper cutoff for perturbation scale. This is crucial for ensuring both reproducibility and a methodical approach to implementing explanation methods. Our experimental findings suggest that monitoring the trends in predictive entropy of a classifier across varying noise scales can serve as a heuristic. We have found that when working with Gaussian noise, we typically observe an anomalous trend in the predictive entropy of the classifier, as illustrated in
Fig.

6
.
It is worth noting that the values presented for perturbation levels in the main text are relative to this maximum cutoff
i.e
.

\sigma=1.0
, refers to one unit of maximum perturbation level.

We attribute this anomalous trend in predictive entropy to two primary factors. Firstly, the noise level scales beyond the range that is present in the training data, rendering it out-of-distribution. Secondly, the scale of logits is affected by the scale of the input. In probability space,
i.e
.
values after the softmax, the large scale of logits results in an overconfident prediction.

Figure 6
:

This figure shows the ResNet50’s predictive entropy at varying perturbation scales on two datasets. As can be seen in the plots, when the perturbation distribution is set to Gaussian, it increases gradually and reaches to a point where an anomalous trend occurs. The anomaly shows itself as a decrease in the predictive entropy in ImageNet and plateauing in Food101, despite our expectation that it should increase to its highest theoretical value. Since after the anomalous trend the visual quality of explanations degrade, we have selected the noise scale cutoff right before the anomaly starts, shown with red dashed lines.

Quantification of Explanation Inconsistency

As we have mentioned the inconsistency of explanations across hyperparameters in the paper, it is useful to quantify this term, so that we gain deeper insight and know potential underlying patterns.
It should be noted that the inconsistency in each explanation can be used as a heuristic for unreliability or uncertainty in explanations.

We define the inconsistencies in explanations quantitatively as follows,

\displaystyle\text{Inconsistency}(x)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{E}_{p(\sigma)}[\text{Cosine-similarity}(e_{0},e_{\sigma})]

(48)

\displaystyle=1-\int\text{Cosine-similarity}(e_{0},e_{\sigma})p(\sigma)d\sigma

(49)

\displaystyle\approx 1-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{N}\text{Cosine-similarity}(e_{0},e_{\sigma_{i}})

(50)

where
e_{\sigma}=\mathbb{E}_{p(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}};\sigma)}[(\nabla f)^{2}]
denotes the explanation at hyperparameter
\sigma
, and
p(\sigma)=\text{Unif}(0,1)
. We normalize, downsample and flatten explanations to compute the cosine similarity.
The quantified inconsistency reveals interesting patterns for two explanation methods, yet this quantity is not correlated with predictive entropy. The value of inconsistency is computed per image (each point in the scatter plot visualized in
Fig.

11
).
