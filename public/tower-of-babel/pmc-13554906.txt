When Cholesterol Can Kill at 20
Authors: Karl Chan, Louis Chun-Wai Choi, Kevin Ka-Ho Kam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

A 20-year-old man presenting with anterior ST-elevation myocardial infarction is unusual; the case reported by Gitte et al1 is extraordinary. Beyond the acute infarction, he was found to have left main and triple-vessel coronary artery disease, hemodynamically relevant supravalvular aortic stenosis (SVAS), and an underlying genetic diagnosis of homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HoFH). This case underscores the accelerated nature of atherosclerosis in HoFH and highlights key diagnostic and management challenges.

HoFH: A Diagnosis That Cannot Be Missed

Familial hypercholesterolemia is the most common inherited metabolic disorder predisposing to premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, but HoFH remains rare and frequently underrecognized. It is typically caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in the low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptor gene, leading to absent or severely impaired LDL receptor function. The resulting lifelong elevation in low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) drives aggressive atherosclerosis beginning in childhood, with clinical manifestations often emerging in the second or third decade if untreated.2,3

In this case, the diagnosis was clinically apparent even before genetic confirmation, with an LDL-C of 510 mg/dL, extensive premature atherosclerosis, and a strong family history of sudden cardiac death. These features should immediately prompt consideration of HoFH. The presence of xanthomas if any, especially before age 10 years can serve as a clinical hint for diagnosing HoFH. Genetic testing subsequently confirms the diagnosis and facilitates cascade screening. Identifying affected relatives by genetic screening is critical,3 as early initiation of therapy can substantially alter disease trajectory.

Supravalvular Aortic Stenosis: The Underappreciated Manifestation

Aortic involvement in HoFH is well described but remains underappreciated in practice. Cholesterol-driven atherosclerotic infiltration can affect the aortic valve leaflets, the aortic root, and the supravalvular region. SVAS in this context is less common than valvular aortic stenosis but carries a poor prognosis, reflecting the severity of the underlying atherosclerotic burden.4,5

This case highlights the importance of multimodality imaging. Doppler echocardiography revealed peak gradient of 43 mm Hg, but a mean of 12 mm Hg—values that in isolation may not trigger intervention. In contrast, computed tomography aortography revealed focal luminal narrowing with a planimetered area of 1.09 cm2, confirming anatomically significant obstruction. Transthoracic echocardiography confirmed a turbulent jet in the ascending aorta above a morphologically normal tricuspid aortic valve. Multimodality imaging was pivotal in guiding surgical decision-making, rather than recommending percutaneous coronary intervention.4,5

Elevated lipoprotein(a), as observed in this young patient, likely contributed additional pro-atherogenic and pro-calcific effects, accelerating both coronary and aortic pathology.3

Management: Integrating Surgical Care and Lifelong Strategy

Revascularization and structural intervention

The management of SVAS in patients with HoFH remains controversial. Current guidelines suggest operation in patient with SVAS with mean gradient equal to or >40 mm Hg but applies only to those with congenital heart disease or underlying elastopathy. For adult patients with SVAS, the 10-year survival is excellent with regular follow-up.6 Whether the patient in the case needs to be operated on for the SVAS with a relatively low gradient is up for debate. In this case, a single-stage surgical approach addressing both coronary and aortic pathology achieved a favorable early outcome. Small case series suggest that combined coronary artery bypass grafting and aortic repair in HoFH is feasible with acceptable perioperative risk.7,8

Lipid-Lowering Therapy: Beyond LDL Receptor Dependence

Therapeutic expectations in HoFH must be calibrated differently from heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia. Most conventional lipid-lowering therapies—including statins, ezetimibe, bempedoic acid, and PCSK9 inhibitors—depend on residual LDL receptor activity and are therefore less effective in patients with receptor-null variants.

Despite maximal oral therapy, this patient’s LDL-C remained markedly elevated at 249 mg/dL. Monoclonal PCSK9 inhibitors together with inclisiran, although mechanistically appealing, have shown variable efficacy in HoFH. In the ORION-5 (A Study of Inclisiran in Participants With Homozygous Familial Hypercholesterolemia) trial, significant reductions in circulating PCSK9 did not translate into meaningful LDL-C lowering overall, underscoring the limitations of receptor-dependent approaches in this population.9

Access to these therapies remains inequitable globally, and the financial constraints that prevented cascade screening in this family are likely to limit these options as well. This gap between therapeutic possibility and real-world availability represents one of the unresolved issues in the management of HoFH.

Conclusions

The case reported by Gitte et al1 is a stark reminder that the coronary arteries of a 20-year-old can look like those of a 70-year-old when the lipid burden has been present since birth. HoFH is a systemic vascular disease—coronary, aortic, and beyond—that demands early recognition, multimodality assessment, heart team planning, and an honest reckoning with the limitations of conventional lipid-lowering pharmacotherapy. Most importantly, it demands family cascade screening: the index case is never the only case. When a young patient presents with myocardial infarction and LDL-C above 400 mg/dL, the diagnosis must be on the differential—and confirmed genetically before the opportunity for early intervention in relatives is lost.
