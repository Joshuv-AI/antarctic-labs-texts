Prevalence and determinants of high-risk human papilloma virus among men who have sex with men in Benin: a cross-sectional study embedded in a demonstration project on pre-exposure prophylaxis against HIV
Authors: Souleymane Diabaté, Luc Behanzin, Fernand Guédou, Marius Olodo, Aude Ella Goma-Matsetse, Marlène Aza-Gnandji, Alban Dossouvo, Axel Akpaca, Elyote Chagas, Flore Armande Gangbo, Djimon Marcel Zannou, Michel Alary
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to assess the prevalence and factors associated with anal high-risk human papilloma virus (HR-HPV).DesignA cross-sectional study conducted from 24 August 2020 to 24 November 2020.SettingPrimary care, Cotonou, Benin.Participants204 HIV-negative men who have sex with men initiating oral pre-exposure prophylaxis.Primary outcome measureAnal HR-HPV genotypes using GeneXpert HPV assay. Fourteen HR-HPV were evaluated: HPV-16 and HPV-18/45 in 2 distinct channels and the 11 other genotypes as a pooled result (31, 33, 35, 39, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66 and 68). The potential independent variables analysed included anal gonorrhoea and chlamydia infections, and sociodemographic and sexual behaviour factors. To assess the determinants of HR-HPV, univariate and multivariate Poisson regression models were performed by using SAS V.9.4.ResultsMean age±SD was 25.9±4.8 years. 131/204 men claimed insertive sex procured more pleasure. Thirty-two participants, accounting for 15.7% of the study sample, had gonorrhoea and/or chlamydia. The prevalence of any HR-HPV genotype was 36.3% (95% CI 30.0% to 43.0%). In total, 7.8% of men had HPV-16 and 7.4% had HPV-18/45. The prevalence for the pooled genotypes (31, 33, 35, 39, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66 and 68) was 29.9%. Receptive anal sex during the last 6 months was strongly associated with prevalent HR-HPV infections. The adjusted proportion ratio (aPR) was 1.93 (95% CI 1.31 to 2.83). Gonorrhoea and chlamydia were also associated with the outcome of interest; p value for both infections was <0.05. The aPR comparing patients who perceived some risk of acquiring HIV to other men was 1.44 (95% CI 1.00 to 2.08).ConclusionsIn Benin, anal HR-HPV was common among HIV-negative men who have sex with men. Among this highly vulnerable population, there is a need for integrated preventive and management strategies targeting HPV and other sexually transmitted infections.Keywords: epidemiology, epidemiologic studies, molecular diagnostics, sexually transmitted disease, virology

Introduction

The transmission of human papilloma virus (HPV) among humans generally begins at the onset of sexual activity. In 10%–30% of the situations, the virus will persist in the epithelial cells for more than 3–24 months and cause genital and/or extragenital neoplasia.1 The genotypes 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 53, 56, 58, 59, 66 and 68 that are mostly observed in these neoplasias are called high-risk HPV (HR-HPV).1–3 In addition, low-risk genotypes 6 and 11 are of interest because they are responsible for 90% of genital warts worldwide.3 In 2018, 690 000 cancers attributable to HPV infection were estimated in the world. Sub-Saharan Africa had the highest age-standardised incidence rate at 19.3 cases per 100 000 person-years.4

HR-HPV is a necessary but not a sufficient cause in the process that leads to the persistence of the virus in the body and the development of neoplasia.5 Different factors involved in the acquisition of HR-HPV infections and/or the carcinogenesis of anal cancers render people such as men who have sex with men (MSM) more vulnerable. These factors include sexually transmitted infections (STIs) due to the HIV, Neisseria gonorrhoeae (NG) and Chlamydia trachomatis (CT).6 Receptive anal sex, and multiplicity and concurrency of sexual partnerships also contribute to this vulnerability.6 7 Almost all anal squamous cell carcinomas are related to HR-HPV.4

Some countries around the world are considering the extension of immunisation programmes against HPV to the most vulnerable populations including MSM. To achieve the greatest impact in terms of public health, this should be done through an integrative model of prevention and care that accounts for the joint distribution of different STIs, including HIV.4 In Benin, West Africa, for example, HIV prevalence is eight times higher among MSM than among the general adult population: 7% and 0.8%, respectively.8 9 Due to the relationship between HIV and HR-HPV,6 10 the vulnerability to HIV of MSM in Benin may hide a high prevalence of HR-HPV genotypes among them. Thus, most of the 44 anal cancers estimated in the country in 2020 might be related to MSM.11 Elsewhere, in sub-Saharan Africa, MSM are disproportionately affected by HPV infections.10 12 Unfortunately, so far, no study has investigated the distribution of HR-HPV among MSM in Benin. Accordingly, we took advantage of an HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis demonstration project conducted among MSM in Benin to assess the prevalence and factors associated with HR-HPV infections. The results will help the Beninese health authorities to better scrutinise the integration of HPV prevention within the STIs control programme that is already targeting HIV, NG and CT among MSM.

Methods

Study design, setting and population

This cross-sectional study was embedded in an oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) demonstration project conducted within the MSM community of Cotonou, the largest city of Benin.13 According to HIV prevalence and a mapping of the MSM community, conducted by Réseau Bénin Synergie Plus, a non-governmental organisation (NGO) exclusively composed of MSM, in 2017, the city was home to 3000 HIV-negative MSM.14 The study centre was the Dispensaire IST. This was the first public clinic dedicated to people at high risk of acquiring HIV and other STIs in Cotonou.

Potential participants in the study were HIV-negative people, aged ≥18 years and born male. Recognising oneself as an MSM and being a member of one of the two main MSM NGOs in Cotonou, namely Réseau Bénin Synergie Plus and Réseau Sida Bénin were also among the inclusion criteria. The two main exclusion criteria were having chronic hepatitis C virus (HCV; anti-HCV antibodies—IgG) and a creatinine clearance <60 mL/min (Cockcroft-Gault equation). For financial reasons, the study sample was set at 200 HIV-negative men. Potential participants were randomly selected using a two-stage sampling procedure: 7/13 districts were picked randomly with a probability proportional to size, and a fixed number of eligible men were selected consecutively from each of them using a random route sampling procedure.15 From Dispensaire IST, a direction was selected randomly and, based on the mapping carried out by BeSyP in 2017 and an updated list of networks’ members, all households with an MSM in the selected direction were visited by peer-educators. The sample size was large enough to estimate a prevalence of HR-HPV at 50% with a precision of 7%.

Enrolment and study procedures

Before enrolment, ten peer-educators from Réseau Bénin Synergie Plus and Réseau Sida Bénin led a mobilisation phase within the MSM community of the study area. To this end, they received standard training on the study objectives and procedures. During the mobilisation phase within the community, every MSM who reported himself as being HIV-negative was given a voucher with a serial number and the phone number of a supervisor. To account for non-eligibility, 266 men were given a voucher in the field. There was one supervisor for each NGO. Their role consisted of making an appointment at the Dispensaire IST for each HIV-negative man who agreed to take a voucher in the field. To reduce the waiting time at the clinic, there were no more than five appointments per day.

From 24 August 2020 to 24 November 2020, all men referred to the Dispensaire IST by the two supervisors were evaluated for their participation after being retested for HIV. Two laboratory tests were used for HIV retesting: One Step Multi-Infectious Disease TesT (Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg)/HCV/HIV/TP; InTec Products, Xinyang, China) followed by SD Bioline. Men with positive results were not enrolled. During a face-to-face interview led by a trained study team member, each of the recruited men answered a standard questionnaire on socioeconomic determinants and sexual behaviour. Subsequently, a study clinician examined the participants physically. During that examination, he collected an anal swab on dry cotton for HR-HPV screening. The dry cotton swab was stored at −28°C until the test was performed in a laboratory, near the study clinic. The qualitative screening was done with the GeneXpert HPV assay (Cepheid, Corporate Headquarters, Sunnyvale, California, USA).16 It consisted of a real-time PCR that was able to detect and report 14 HR-HPV in a single analysis: HPV-16 and HPV-18/45 in 2 distinct channels and the 11 other genotypes as a pooled result (31, 33, 35, 39, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66 and 68). The physician also collected an anal swab for NG and CT testing using GeneXpert CT/NG.

Statistical analysis

A data manager examined the data to ensure that all recruited men had completed the data collection process and that the information collected was valid. A double entry process with the EpiData software was adopted and the statistical analyses were performed with SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute). Men’s characteristics were presented as means±SD for continuous variables and as proportions for categorical ones. The prevalence of HR-HPV was calculated with a 95% CI. To assess the determinants of HR-HPV, the GENMOD procedure was used to perform univariate Poisson regression models. The main variables assessed were based on a review of the literature.17–19 They included STIs (anal NG/CT; since all participants were HIV-negative, HIV status was replaced by self-perceived risk of acquiring the infection), alcohol consumption, smoking, and multiplicity and concurrency of sexual partners. Variables with a p<0.20 were entered in a multivariate Poisson regression model. After a manual selection process, those with a p<0.05 or whose exclusion from the model led to a change of 10% or more in the prevalence ratio of the other variables were kept in the final model. The final model was adjusted for age, alcohol consumption and smoking.

Ethical considerations

Each participant provided written informed consent and received FCFA4000 (US$8) to account for travel cost and time devoted to the study procedures. At enrolment, all men infected with NG/CT were treated free of charge whereas those with HIV were immediately put on antiretroviral treatment (also free of charge). Men with HR-HPV genotypes were referred to the study clinic for follow-up according to local guidelines.

Patient and public involvement

Neither patients nor the public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Discussion

Among 204 HIV-negative MSM living in Benin and recruited in a demonstration project on oral PrEP, the prevalence of any anal HR-HPV (genotypes-16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66 and 68) was high, 36.3%. The vulnerability of MSM to HR-HPV has already been reported in various countries in sub-Saharan Africa.7 10 12 19 Overall, 2%–4% of those studied had at least two or three different high-risk genotypes. Multiple HR-HPV infections are frequently observed among MSM in sub-Saharan Africa. In two studies from Nigeria and Mali, two West African countries like Benin, being infected with at least two HR-HPV was reported among 32% and 45.7% of MSM, respectively.12 20 In the absence of a specific genotyping test in this study, the distribution of HR-HPV other than HPV-16/18/45 was not described. Elsewhere, in sub-Saharan Africa, HPV-16, 18, 31, 35, 45, 51, 58, 59 are among the high-risk genotypes commonly reported among MSM.7 12 19 20 The high prevalence of HPV genotypes, which are covered by the nonavalent vaccine, among MSM in this part of the world is consistent with the call to make HPV vaccine accessible to MSM whenever possible.21 However, according to the WHO, vaccination should be considered for vulnerable populations, such as MSM, only if it does not hinder vaccine coverage among the primary targets who are young girls aged 9–14 years.22 Results from different studies in sub-Saharan Africa showed that MSM tend to be young, with an average age around 25 years, as reported in this study.7 12 20 Hence, a single dose schedule could be appropriate for them, as the WHO is open to this immunisation strategy because growing evidence is showing comparable efficacy to two-dose or three-dose schedules.22

In this study, the prevalence of HR-HPV-16 (7.8%) and HPV-18/45 (7.4%) was minimal as compared with other studies among MSM in sub-Saharan Africa.7 10 12 This apparent discrepancy is likely due to the fact that the study was limited to HIV-negative men. Indeed, the risk of acquiring HPV and of its persistence is higher among people living with HIV than among those who are HIV-negative.7 12 19 23–25 Targeting only HIV-negative men about to initiate oral PrEP did not allow us to assess HIV status as a determinant of HPV infection. However, the likelihood of having any HR-HPV genotype was greater among men who considered that they had some risk of acquiring HIV. The aPR (95% CI) comparing these men to those who did not perceive themselves at risk of acquiring HIV was 1.44% (1.00% to 2.08%). In accordance with previous findings on the HPV dynamic,10 22 NG and CT infections were strongly associated with any HR-HPV. The microabrasions and mucosal inflammation caused by these infections may, indeed, increase the anal mucosa susceptibility to HR-HPV.6 The same biological mechanism could explain why HR-HPV infections are often associated with receptive anal sex as found in this study and in other contexts.7 10 26 In this study, the aPR (95% CI) for this association was 1.93 (1.31 to 2.83). Since the rectal mucosa is composed of a single-layer columnar epithelium, the microabrasions during anal sex could occur directly through frictions or indirectly through other STIs that are generally associated with anal sex among MSM.27 28 In the current study, MSM were generally well educated. In the long term, continuous awareness-raising messages about the prevention of HR-HPV and other STIs could be well understood and have a positive impact on risk taking among them.

Limitations

The study is subject to some limitations. Not being able to isolate each of the 14 HR-HPV genotypes has limited the scope of the results. Underestimation of HR-HPV prevalence may have occurred because the two NGOs involved in the study usually organise awareness sessions on safe sexual behaviour and STI prevention for their members. Such sessions could have increased participants’ knowledge about HPV infection and prevention as compared with other men. Some questions like those on the sexual role were quite sensitive and could have elicited dishonest answers from a handful of participants. To improve answers, face-to-face interviews were conducted by trained peer-investigators. The limited sample size has reduced statistical power in assessing the determinants of HR-HPV infections. Since HIV-positive men and MSM who were not affiliated with identity-networks were not included in this study, caution is needed regarding the generalisability of the results to all MSM living in Benin.

Conclusion

Anal HR-HPV infections were common among MSM about to initiate oral PrEP in Benin. Specific genotyping of HR-HPV and assessment of their relationship with anal precancerous and cancerous lesions among MSM, irrespective of HIV status, is required in this country. In the meantime, there is a need to develop HR-HPV control activities that could be integrated with the HIV control programme that is already targeting this highly vulnerable population in Benin.
