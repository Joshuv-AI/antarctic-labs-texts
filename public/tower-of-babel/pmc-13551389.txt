Emerging Metal–Organic Frameworks (MOFs): Synthesis, Bioactivities, Preservation, Active Packaging/Sensing System, and Future Perspectives for Seafood Applications
Authors: Abhishek Bisht, Gokulprasanth Murugan, Jun Tae Kim, Soottawat Benjakul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSeafood is highly susceptible to rapid microbial proliferation, lipid oxidation, and volatile spoilage compound accumulation, resulting in rapid quality deterioration and food waste. Metal‐organic frameworks (MOFs), a class of crystalline coordination materials with tunable porosity, high surface area, and diverse metal‐ligand architectures, have emerged as multifunctional platforms for advanced seafood preservation. This review presents a mechanistically grounded analysis of MOF‐based systems, emphasizing on how coordination environments and framework properties govern antimicrobial, antioxidant, adsorption, and sensing performance. A unified structure‐property‐function framework is proposed, classifying MOF functionalities into ion‐mediated, redox‐driven, adsorption‐dominant, and hybrid multifunctional modes relevant to seafood application. Particular attention is given to coordination‐driven processes and mode of action, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) regulation, metal ion interactions, selective host–guest adsorption, and controlled release under humid and biologically complex conditions. Key performance constraints that dictate practical performance are identified, including antimicrobial efficacy versus metal‐ion migration, ROS‐mediated preservation versus oxidative damage, adsorption strength versus reversibility, and sensing sensitivity versus operational robustness. These constraints are amplified in seafood systems due to high water activity, complex biochemical matrices, and rapid spoilage kinetics. Recent advances in MOF‐integrated packaging and intelligent sensing demonstrate the potential for simultaneous preservation and real‐time freshness monitoring. However, challenges related to hydrolytic instability, competitive adsorption, scalability, and regulatory compliance remain. Future research should focus on designing moisture‐stable, biocompatible MOFs with controlled functionality and scalable fabrication. This work provides a conceptual and design‐oriented foundation for translating MOFs into practical seafood preservation technologies.

Introduction

Seafood constitutes a critical component of the global food system; however, its high perishability severely limits shelf life and marketability. Rapid quality deterioration is primarily driven by microbial proliferation and lipid oxidation. In seafood, lipid oxidation is a complex process involving enzymatic pathways mediated by lipoxygenases and endogenous hemoproteins, non‐enzymatic autoxidation driven by transition‐metal‐catalyzed free‐radical chain reactions, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) induced oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs). These interconnected mechanisms collectively generate lipid hydroperoxides and secondary oxidation products responsible for off‐flavors, nutrient loss, discoloration, and reduced product safety (Ferreira et al. 2024). Concurrently, increasing global demand for protein, coupled with pressure on terrestrial livestock systems, has intensified reliance on marine resources. Despite its nutritional value, seafood processing generates substantial by‐products‐up to 75% of total biomass‐which remain underutilized, highlighting inefficiencies in current value chains (Benjakul et al. 2019). With the global seafood market projected to exceed $151 billion by 2030 (Cooney et al. 2023), the development of effective and sustainable preservation strategies has become increasingly urgent.

Conventional preservation techniques, including refrigeration, freezing, and synthetic additives, provide only partial mitigation of spoilage and often involve limitations such as oxidative instability, environmental burden, or deterioration of nutritional quality (Bisht et al. 2025). These limitations have driven the exploration of advanced functional materials, among which metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) have emerged as promising candidates. MOFs are crystalline coordination networks, which are composed of metal nodes and organic linkers, offering tunable porosity, high surface area, and chemically programmable functionality. These features enable the precise control over adsorption, catalytic activity, and molecular release, positioning MOFs as versatile platforms for active packaging, controlled delivery of bioactives, and sensing applications (Alizadeh Sani et al. 2024). Furthermore, diverse synthesis strategies, including hydrothermal, mechanochemical, and electrochemical routes as illustrated in (Figure 2a) allow tailoring of MOF structure and surface chemistry to specific application requirements (J. H. Wang et al. 2025).

In the context of seafood preservation, MOFs exhibit multifunctional behavior by integrating antimicrobial activity, oxidative stabilization, and real‐time sensing capabilities within a single material system. For example, MOFs can act as reservoirs for controlled release of antimicrobial metal ions, adsorb volatile spoilage compounds, and function as optical or electrochemical sensors for freshness monitoring (J. Wang et al. 2024). Recent developments in MOF‐based intelligent packaging, including cobalt‐based MOFs (Co‐MOFs) and fluorescence‐responsive systems, demonstrate enhanced mechanical stability, moisture resistance, and real‐time detection of spoilage indicators (Tang et al. 2023). However, despite these advances, practical implementation remains limited due to critical challenges, including hydrolytic instability under high‐moisture conditions, uncontrolled metal ion migration and the absence of comprehensive toxicological and regulatory evaluations (N. T. T. Nguyen et al. 2024; Payam et al. 2024; J. H. Wang et al. 2025).

Importantly, seafood systems present distinct physicochemical challenges compared to terrestrial meat. The presence of trimethylamine‐N‐oxide (TMAO), which is rapidly reduced to trimethylamine (TMA) during microbial spoilage, leads to the accumulation of characteristic off‐odor compounds. In parallel, the high PUFAs content is associated with rapid oxidative degradation, while elevated moisture and salinity promote microbial metabolism and volatile compound formation (Vardali et al. 2020). These conditions impose stringent requirements on preservation materials, particularly in terms of selective adsorption of volatile amines, resistance to hydrolysis, and stability under saline and aqueous environments. Consequently, the performance of MOFs in seafood applications cannot be extrapolated from other food systems and instead requires application‐specific design strategies that explicitly account for these conditions.

Unlike previous reviews that broadly discuss MOFs in food packaging, this review focuses specifically on seafood systems, where high water activity, salinity, TMA/TMAO transformation, PUFA oxidation, and rapid microbial spoilage impose unique material‐design requirements. The novelty of this review lies in integrating MOF coordination chemistry with seafood‐specific preservation constraints and proposing a structure‐property‐function framework for antimicrobial, antioxidant, adsorption, and sensing applications (Figure 1).

This review provides a critical and mechanistically grounded evaluation of MOFs for seafood preservation. Rather than cataloguing existing materials, it emphasizes the fundamental constraints that govern real‐world performance, including moisture‐induced instability, mass transfer limitations, scalability, and regulatory considerations. These challenges are analyzed to derive practical design principles for next‐generation MOF‐based materials aimed at improving seafood quality, extending shelf life, and reducing post‐harvest losses.

Synthesis and Characteristics of MOFs

The rational design of MOFs is fundamentally governed by coordination chemistry, where the interplay between metal‐ions, organic linkers, and synthesis conditions or processes (Figure 2a) dictate framework topology, porosity, and physicochemical stability (Payam et al. 2024). Commonly employed metal nodes, including Zn2 +, Co2 +, Fe3 +, Al3 +, Cr3 +, and Ni2 +, provide diverse coordination geometries and variable oxidation states, thus enabling the formation of stable metal‐ligand networks with tunable functionality (Park et al. 2024). In parallel, organic linkers such as carboxylates, imidazolates, phosphonates, and tetrazolates define pore architecture and connectivity by bridging metal centers through predictable coordination modes (J. Li et al. 2025). Together, these components enable the construction of highly ordered crystalline frameworks with controlled morphology and topology, ranging from zeolitic to diamond‐like structure (Figure 2b,c).

Beyond compositional selection, the stability of MOFs is fundamentally governed by the thermodynamics of metal‐ligand coordination. Bond strength depends on metal‐ion charge density, which integrates ionic radius and valence state, and directly influences resistance to hydrolysis and structural degradation. High‐valence, high‐charge‐density metal ions typically form stronger coordination bonds, resulting in more robust frameworks under identical ligand environments (Ahmad et al. 2024). This trend is consistent with Pearson's hard‐soft acid‐base (HSAB) theory, which predicts enhanced stability when hard Lewis acids (e.g., Fe3 +, Al3 +, Cr3 +) coordinate with hard base ligands such as carboxylates. For example, Materials Institute Lavoisier (MIL) series‐including MIL‐53, MIL‐100, and MIL‐101 demonstrate exceptional hydrolytic and thermal stability due to strong metal‐oxygen coordination bonds within trivalent metal clusters (Férey et al. 2004; Rosi et al. 2002; Serre et al. 2002).

In contrast, frameworks constructed from softer metal ions (e.g., Zn2+, Co2+,Ni2+, and Mn2+) and azolate ligands typically form zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs), characterized by tetrahedral coordination and robust metal‐nitrogen bonds. These systems often demonstrate superior chemical and thermal stability, compared to many carboxylate‐based MOFs due to their rigid framework geometry. However, this increased stability is frequently accompanied by reduced affinity for polar or hydrogen‐bonding molecules, highlighting a fundamental balance between structural robustness and adsorption selectivity (Ahmad et al. 2024). Thus, framework selection must be guided by application‐specific requirements rather than stability alone.

The physicochemical properties of MOFs are inherently structure‐dependent, making precise control over particle size, morphology, porosity, and surface chemistry essential for optimizing performance. In seafood preservation systems, these parameters directly influence dispersion within biopolymer matrices, interfacial interactions with proteins and lipids, and responsiveness to environmental factors such as moisture, pH, and ionic strength (Ahmadi et al. 2021; Vardali et al. 2020). For instance, particle size governs both loading capacity and release kinetics of encapsulated bioactives while also affecting colloidal stability and penetration into microbial biofilms. Smaller particles enhance antimicrobial efficacy but may increase migration risk, whereas larger particles improve stability at the expense of reduced surface reactivity (Siddiqui et al. 2023).

Morphology further modulates diffusion pathways and surface anisotropy, influencing the accessibility of active sites and interaction with microbial membranes. Through coordination modulation strategies‐such as the use of competitive ligands or growth inhibitors‐nucleation kinetics can be tuned to control crystal size and defect density, thereby tailoring functional performance (Ahmadi et al. 2021; Lu et al. 2024). Similarly, porosity and pore size distribution determine mass transfer behavior and encapsulation efficiency. While high surface area enhances loading capacity and sustained release of antioxidants or antimicrobial agents, excessively microporous structures may restrict diffusion under high‐moisture conditions typical of seafood systems. Consequently, hierarchical porosity is often preferred to balance adsorption capacity and transport kinetics (An et al. 2024; Lu et al. 2024).

Framework composition also plays a critical role in determining chemical stability and biodegradability. MOFs constructed from strong metal‐oxygen bonds (e.g., Zr‐O, Fe‐O) generally exhibit superior resistance to hydrolysis, whereas frameworks based on weaker coordination bonds are more susceptible to degradation in aqueous environments (An et al. 2024; Lu et al. 2024). This distinction becomes particularly important in seafood preservation, where high water activity and salinity accelerate framework destabilization and increase the risk of metal‐ion leaching. Therefore, the selection of metal‐ligand combinations must simultaneously consider stability, functionality, and safety.

From a synthesis perspective, MOF formation is governed by nucleation and crystal growth processes controlled by coordination equilibria. Conventional wet‐chemical methods, including hydrothermal and solvothermal synthesis, remain the most widely used due to their ability to produce highly crystalline frameworks with controlled morphology (Ahmad et al. 2024). However, these approaches often require high temperatures, long reaction times, and organic solvents, limiting scalability and environmental sustainability (Butt et al. 2023; Cereceda and Du 2026). In contrast, mechanochemical and microwave‐assisted methods offer faster synthesis, reduced solvent consumption, and improved scalability, although they may compromise crystallinity or structural uniformity. Reaction parameters including precursor concentration, temperature, pH, and metal‐to‐ligand ratiocritically influence the formation of secondary building units (SBUs), nucleation rate, and phase purity. Variations in these parameters can shift the balance between kinetically and thermodynamically favored structures, leading to differences in topology, defect density, and functional performance (Ahmad et al. 2024). Therefore, precise control over synthesis conditions is essential to achieve reproducible and application‐specific MOF architectures.

However, the intrinsic hydrophilicity and hydrolytic instability of many MOFs limit their performance in moisture‐rich seafood environments. To address this limitation, hydrophobic engineering strategies‐including pre‐synthetic design, post‐synthetic modification (PSM), and substrate immobilization have been developed to enhance water resistance and functional durability. These approaches are discussed in the following section.

Hydrophobic and Surface Modifications

Enhancing the hydrolytic stability of MOFs is a critical prerequisite for their deployment in seafood preservation systems, where high humidity, meltwater accumulation, and elevated salinity create highly aggressive environments. Under such conditions, water molecules readily penetrate MOF pores and disrupt metalligand coordination bonds, leading to framework degradation, pore collapse, and loss of functional performance (Tang et al. 2023). Consequently, hydrophobic engineering is not merely an auxiliary modification but a fundamental design is required to ensure structural integrity and long‐term functionality.

To address this limitation, three primary hydrophobization strategies have been developed: (i) pre‐synthetic molecular design, (ii) post‐synthetic surface modification, and (iii) immobilization on hydrophobic substrates. These approaches differ fundamentally in their mechanisms, structural implications, and practical applicability. Pre‐synthetic design introduces intrinsic hydrophobicity within the framework backbone, thereby enhancing resistance to hydrolysis but limiting post‐fabrication tunability. In contrast, PSM enables flexible and application‐specific surface functionalization, albeit with potential penalties in pore accessibility and long‐term stability. Immobilization strategies provide a scalable, system‐level solution by integrating MOFs into hydrophobic matrices, although they may introduce diffusion limitations. The selection of an appropriate strategy therefore requires careful balancing of stability, functionality, and process scalability (H. Zhang et al. 2025).

Pre‐Synthetic Hydrophobic Design

Pre‐synthetic hydrophobic design in MOFs involves the deliberate selection of ligands, functional groups, and metal nodes during framework assembly to reduce pore wettability and enhance the resistance to hydrolytic degradation. Because the organic linker defines the internal pore surface, its polarity, steric configuration, and electronic structure directly govern water‐framework interactions. Incorporation of hydrophobic moieties‐such as alkyl chain, aromatic groups, or fluorinated substituents‐reduces pore polarity, weakens hydrogen bonding with water molecules, and suppresses water adsorption. This, in turn, mitigates hydrolytic cleavage of metal‐ligand coordination bonds.

ZIF‐8 is a representative example of an intrinsically hydrophobic MOF. Constructed from Zn2+ nodes and 2‐methylimidazole linkers, the presence of methyl groups generates a relatively non‐polar pore environment that restricts water penetration while preserving high porosity. As a result, ZIF‐8 exhibits superior stability under humid conditions, compared with many carboxylate‐based MOFs. For instance, when ZIF‐8 was incorporated into a polyethyleneimine‐bacterial cellulose composite, a hydrophobic sensing layer that remained structurally stable across a wide relative humidity range (30%–93%) could be produced (L. Wang et al. 2021; Figure 3a). However, prolonged exposure to aqueous environments still induces gradual degradation, indicating that intrinsic hydrophobicity alone is insufficient to fully suppress hydrolysis. This highlights a key limitation: Surface hydrophobicity can delay, but not completely eliminate, water‐induced degradation.

(a) Hydrophobic ZIF‐8 structure blocks water molecules from entering porous films (J. L. Wang et al. 2021); (b) ligand rigidity enhances MOF stability (Lv et al. 2019); (c) charge‐separated ligands from highly stable MOF‐74‐Zn quasi isomers (Yang et al. 2019); (d) hydrophobic NMOF‐1 prepared via Zn2+ self‐assembly with bisphenol‐based carbonate oligomers (Roy et al. 2016).

Ligand rigidity represents another critical design parameter. Rigid ligands restrict rotational motion within the framework, thereby minimizing structural distortion that can facilitate water diffusion toward coordination sites. Zr‐based MOFs constructed with rigid ligands, such as hexamethylbiphenyl, maintained structural stability under extreme chemical conditions (2 M HCl to pH 11 NaOH), whereas frameworks assembled with more flexible ligands (e.g., biphenyl) exhibited the increased susceptibility to hydrolysis and structural collapse (Lv et al. 2019; Figure 3b). This comparison highlights the importance of steric constraints in limiting framework dynamics and enhancing water stability.

Electronic effects, particularly ligand charge distribution, also influence hydrophobicity and coordination stability. Yang et al. (2019) modified MOF‐74‐Zn using a high charge density ligand, (2,5‐dioxido‐1,4‐benzenedixaboxylate), generating related structures (CPM‐74 and CPM‐75) with enhanced stability. Among these, CPM‐74 retained structural integrity at temperatures up to 350°C and remained stable in aqueous environments over a broad pH range (3–10), even under boiling conditions (Yang et al. 2019; Figure 3c). While increased charge density strengthens metal‐ligand interactions, it may also increase framework polarity, suggesting a balance between coordination stability and hydrophobicity that must be balanced for specific applications.

An alternative strategy involves incorporating long hydrophobic chains or fluorinated groups into ligand structures to further reduce pore wettability. For example, Roy et al. (2016) synthesized a Zn‐based MOF incorporating a C18 alkyl‐functionalized ligand, resulting in a super‐hydrophobic material with a water contact angle (WCA) of approximately 160°–162° and stability across a wide pH range (1–9; Figure 3d). Although such modifications significantly enhance water repellency, they may partially block pore accessibility and reduce adsorption capacity, highlighting a key design constraint between hydrophobicity and functional performance.

In addition to ligand engineering, metal node selection plays a decisive role in hydrolytic stability. High‐valence metal ions such as Zr4+, AI3+, and Fe3+ form strong metal‐oxygen coordination bonds that resist hydrolytic cleavage. For instance, Liu et al. (2015) reported a series of mesoporous MOFs (PCN‐228, PCN‐229, and PCN‐230) constructed from 12‐connected Zr6 clusters and extended porphyrin ligands. Among these, PCN‐230 retained crystallinity after 24 h exposure to water across an extreme pH range (0–12), demonstrating exceptional stability (T. ‐F. Liu et al. 2015; Figure 4a). Compared with divalent metal‐based systems, these high‐valence frameworks exhibit significantly improved resistance to moisture‐induced degradation, making them more suitable for aqueous food environments.

(a) Replacing Zr8 clusters with 12 connected Zr6 clusters improves Zr‐O bond strength and stability (T. ‐F. Liu et al. 2015). (b) Different methods used in post‐synthetic MOF modification (Pawar and Patil 2025); (c) hydrophobic HKUST‐1 modified post‐synthesis with octyltrimethylsilane (M. Overall, pre‐synthetic hydrophobic design enables the construction of intrinsically stable MOFs with uniformly hydrophobic pore environments. This approach is particularly advantageous for applications requiring long‐term stability under high‐moisture or extreme pH conditions, such as seafood preservation. However, it is constrained by the complexity of ligand synthesis, limited scalability, and reduced flexibility for post‐synthetic functionalization. In comparison, PSM strategies offer greater tunability but often at the expense of structural robustness. Therefore, the selection between pre‐ and post‐synthetic approaches should be guided by the specific balance required between stability, functionality, and process scalability.

Post‐Synthetic Hydrophobic Modification

PSM represents a versatile and application‐oriented strategy to enhance the hydrolytic stability and interfacial properties of MOFs while preserving their intrinsic crystalline architecture. In contrast to pre‐synthetic design, which embeds hydrophobicity within the framework backbone, PSM introduces functional interfaces after framework formation. This enables modulation of surface energy, pore accessibility, and host‐guest interactions without disrupting the primary coordination network. Such adaptability is particularly advantageous in high‐moisture environments, such as seafood packaging systems, where both structural stability and functional responsiveness are required.

PSM‐based hydrophobization can be broadly classified into four principal approaches: (i) surface chemical anchoring, (ii) physical surface coating, (iii) hydrophobic guest loading, and (iv) stimuli‐responsive modulation (Pawar and Patil 2025; Figure 4b). Each strategy operates through distinct mechanisms and exhibits inherent differences in stability, tunability, and mass transport behavior.

Surface chemical anchoring is one of the most extensively employed approaches and involves the covalent grafting or self‐assembled attachment of hydrophobic moieties onto MOF surfaces. Common modifications include alkylation, fluorination, and silanization using reagents such as trimethylchlorosilane, octadecyltrimethoxysilane, and polydimethylsiloxane (Prorokova et al. 2020). These reagents react with surface hydroxyl groups or coordinatively unsaturated metal sites (CUSs), forming stable covalent bonds that reduce surface energy and establish hydrophobic barriers against water ingress (H. Zhang et al. 2025). For example, M. Kim et al. (2024) modified HKUST‐1 using octadecyltrimethoxysilane, in which the increased grafting density (40%), a WCA exceeding 90°, the reduced water vapor adsorption by ∼49%, and the preserved microporosity (approximately 99%) were achieved after prolonged exposure to humid air (Figure 4c). Similarly, post‐synthetic amine functionalization of MIL‐101(Cr) enabled systematic tuning of wettability, with WCA ranging from 17.9° to 146.5°, depending on the alkyl chain length (Y. Li et al. 2021; Figure 4d). Despite its precision, an excessive surface functionalization can partially block pore entrances and restrict mass transfer, highlighting a fundamental balance between hydrophobic protection and adsorption efficiency.

Physical surface coating provides an alternative strategy by forming an external hydrophobic barrier without altering the internal pore structure. In this approach, polymers, fluropolymers, ionic liquids, or vapor‐deposited films encapsulate MOF particles, limiting water penetration while maintaining gas permeability (DeCoste and Peterson 2013). For instance, (Nian et al. 2023) fabricated a fluorinated hydrophobic shell on HKUST‐1 via surface polymerization of fluorinated methacrylate monomers, achieving a WCA of 95° and significantly improving resistance to hydrolytic degradation. When incorporated into chitosan‐gelatin composite films, the coated MOF maintained structural integrity under humid conditions for up to 7 days, whereas unmodified counterparts exhibited pronounced degradation (Nian et al. 2023). Compared with chemical anchoring, coating strategies offer more uniform surface protection; however, excessive shell thickness can introduce diffusion resistance, thereby limiting mass transfer and functional performance.

Hydrophobic guest incorporation represents a dual‐functional approach in which hydrophobic molecules are introduced into MOF pores to simultaneously reduce water affinity and impart bioactivity. Encapsulated guests, such as essential oils, fatty acids, or phenolic compounds, can occupy hydrophilic adsorption sites while providing antimicrobial or antioxidant activities. For example, (Li et al. 2023) developed a light‐responsive UiO‐66 system functionalized with azobenzene and dispersed in a bulky ionic liquid, forming a sterically shielded interface that significantly exceeded the intrinsic pore dimensions. This configuration effectively reduced water accessibility while preserving internal adsorption capacity (M. ‐M. Li et al. 2023; Figure 5a). However, excessive guest loading reduces available pore volume and limits adsorption efficiency, necessitating careful optimization of loading levels to balance functionality and transport properties.

(a) Azobenzene‐modified UiO‐66 forms hydrophobic MOFs in large‐volume ionic liquids (S. Li et al. 2023), (b) light‐responsive hydrophobization changes water contact angle from ∼69° to ∼62° under visible and green light (Z. Zhang et al. 2022), (c) anthocyanins immobilized An‐BTC‐MOF (Xiao et al. 2026), and (d) HKUST‐1 loaded onto hydrophobic matrices to enhance hydrophobicity (An et al. 2024).

Stimuli‐responsive hydrophobization introduces dynamic control over surface wettability through reversible structural transformations. Photoresponsive ligands, such as azobenzene, undergo trans‐cis isomerization under light irradiation, altering surface polarity and enabling switchable hydrophobic behavior. For example, a Cu‐azobenzene MOF film exhibited reversible changes in WCA (∼69° to ∼62°) upon irradiation (530–400 nm), driven by ligand conformational transitions (Z. Zhang et al. 2022; Figure 5b). While this strategy enables adaptive functionality, the relatively modest change in wettability and reliance on external stimuli limit its practicality in static packaging environments, where passive and sustained performance is preferred.

Beyond surface‐level modification, covalent PSM and post‐synthetic ligand exchange (PSLE) enable deeper structural tuning of MOFs. Covalent PSM involves direct bonding between functional molecules and reactive linker groups, whereas multi‐step approaches generate intermediate species for subsequent functionalization (Pawar and Patil 2025). In contrast, PSLE replaces native linkers with alternative ligands via solvent‐assisted processes, producing mixed‐ligand frameworks with tailored properties (Tanabe and Cohen 2011). Although these strategies provide high tunability, their effectiveness is constrained by ligand size, diffusion kinetics, and framework thermodynamic stability. In structurally less robust systems, ligand exchange can induce partial framework degradation or generate defects, thereby compromising long‐term performance (Pawar and Patil 2025).

Overall, post‐synthetic hydrophobic modification offers superior versatility and precise interfacial control, compared with pre‐synthetic design, enabling application‐specific optimization without reconstructing the framework. However, this flexibility is inherently associated with limitations, including potential pore blockage, reduced mass transfer, and limited long‐term instability of surface modifications under continuous moisture exposure. From an application perspective, PSM provides a practical balance between performance and scalability, particularly for integrating MOFs into composite films and intelligent packaging systems. Nevertheless, achieving durable hydrophobicity while preserving intrinsic porosity and transport efficiency remains a central challenge for real‐world implementation in seafood preservation.

Immobilization on Hydrophobic Substrates

Immobilization of MOFs onto hydrophobic substrates has emerged as an effective interfacial strategy to enhance moisture stability while preserving functional activity in seafood preservation systems (Xiao et al. 2026). In this approach, MOF crystals are anchored or embedded within hydrophobic matrices‐such as biopolymer films, lipid‐modified surfaces, or low‐surface‐energy organic supports, thereby creating a protective microenvironment that limits direct exposure of the framework to water. This interfacial barrier suppresses capillary condensation and restricts water diffusion into pore channels, which are primary pathways for hydrolytic cleavage of metal‐ligand coordination bonds (H. Zhang et al. 2025).

Beyond passive protection, interfacial interactions between the MOF and substrate play a critical role in determining structural stability and functional performance. Hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and partial coordination between substrate functional groups and MOF surface sites enhance interfacial adhesion, reducing particle detachment and improving structural integrity under humid conditions. These interactions can also stabilize encapsulated functional molecules within MOF pores by restricting molecular mobility and enhancing retention through combined physical adsorption and intermolecular bonding (Q. Wu et al. 2025).

For example, Xiao et al. (2026) immobilized anthocyanins within a Zn‐BTC framework (ACN@Zn‐BTC), where hydrogen bonding and physical confinement reduced molecular migration and improved stability. When incorporated into composite films, the system exhibited enhanced mechanical strength (14.9 MPa), increased flexibility (28.1%), and improved UV resistance, alongside moderate hydrophobicity (WCA of 50.8°). Importantly, the immobilized anthocyanins retained strong pH responsiveness, enabling distinct colorimetric transitions during ammonia exposure and shrimp spoilage. The observed color variation showed a strong correlation with total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB‐N) levels (R 2 = 0.9557), demonstrating effective real‐time freshness monitoring (Xiao et al. 2026; Figure 5c).

This strategy also enables synergistic integration of the barrier properties of hydrophobic matrices with the high surface area and functional activity of MOFs, resulting in hybrid systems with improved durability in aqueous environments. For instance, An et al. (2024) synthesized a silver nanoparticle‐modified HKUST‐1 and immobilized it within a Cordyceps mycelium‐derived biopolymeric matrix to fabricate an antimicrobial composite film. Incorporation of Ag‐HKUST‐1 significantly altered interfacial wettability and moisture transport properties, increasing the WCA from 46.7° to 98.45°, and reducing water vapor permeability from 6.357 × 10− 1 1 to 5.949 × 10− 1 1gm− 1.Pa− 1.s− 1.

Additionally, the composite exhibited reduced moisture content (26.69% to 23.78%) and water solubility (64.33% to 61.68%), indicating enhanced resistance to water penetration and matrix dissolution (Figure 5d). These physicochemical improvements are particularly relevant for seafood packaging applications, where high humidity and water activity accelerate MOF degradation. By stabilizing the framework at the substrate interface while retaining antimicrobial and adsorption functionalities, this immobilization provides a viable pathway for integrating MOFs into practical preservation systems (Hong et al. 2024).

Compared with molecular‐level hydrophobic modification strategies, substrate immobilization offers a more application‐oriented solution by directly integrating MOFs into functional materials. This approach is especially advantageous for food packaging and sensing systems, where mechanical robustness, processability, and environmental compatibility are critical design criteria. However, embedding MOFs within dense matrices may reduce accessibility of active sites and introduce diffusion limitations; immobilization remains one of the most industrially feasible strategies due to its compatibility with established material processing techniques and scalability.

Characteristics of MOFs

Comprehensive characterization of MOFs is essential to establish definitive structure‐property relationships and to rationally optimize their coordination chemistry‐driven performance. Key framework attributes including crystallinity, phase purity, porosity, and specific surface area must be systematically evaluated to verify successful metal‐ligand assembly and ensure structural integrity under application‐relevant conditions (Payam et al. 2024).

X‐ray diffraction (XRD), encompassing both single‐crystal and powder techniques, remains the primary method for resolving coordination geometry, identifying SBUs, and determining framework topology and long‐range order. In addition, XRD enables rigorous verification of phase purity and detection of structural transformations following modification or environmental exposure (Subudhi et al. 2021). However, while XRD provides detailed crystallographic information, it is inherently limited in probing local disorder and nanoscale heterogeneity, necessitating complementary techniques.

Fourier‐transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) provide chemical and thermal information, which do not rely on diffraction and microscopy‐based techniques. FTIR verifies successful metal‐ligand coordination through characteristic shifts or disappearance of ligand vibrational bands, thereby confirming coordination bond formation, framework functionalization, and PSM (Payam et al. 2024). TGA evaluates framework thermal stability, solvent removal, guest loading, and decomposition behavior by monitoring mass loss as a function of temperature. The resulting decomposition profiles are particularly valuable for assessing framework robustness during food processing and storage, where elevated temperatures and moisture can compromise structural integrity (Subudhi et al. 2021). Together with XRD, these complementary techniques can indicate the chemical and thermal integrity of MOFs, while subsequent porosity analyses provide quantitative information on accessible surface area and molecular transport.

Morphology, crystal size and growth behavior are typically characterized using electron and scanning probe microscopic techniques, including scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and atomic force microscopy (AFM). These analytical methods provide critical insights into nucleation kinetics, crystal morphology, and microstructural heterogeneity, which directly influence mass transfer, surface reactivity, and dispersion within composite systems (Payam et al. 2024). AFM further enables nanoscale mapping of surface topography and mechanical properties, offering indirect evidence of framework rigidity and coordination bond strength. Compared with bulk crystallographic techniques, these imaging methods capture spatial variability that can significantly impact functional performance.

Porosity and accessible surface area are key determinants of adsorption capacity and molecular transport and are typically quantified using gas adsorption techniques. These measurements provide insights into pore characteristics that arise from the underlying coordination between structural design and functional behavior (Subudhi et al., 2021). However, conventional gas adsorption analyses may not fully represent transport behavior under high‐moisture or complex food matrices, highlighting the need for application‐relevant characterization conditions.

Increasing evidence indicates that structural defects, including vacancies, missing linkers, and coordination disorder, play a critical role in governing MOF reactivity. While defects can enhance catalytic activity and adsorption through the generation of unsaturated metal sites, they may simultaneously compromise structural stability, particularly under hydrolytic conditions. Therefore, defect engineering represents both an opportunity and a challenge, requiring precise control to balance functionality and durability.

Advanced spectroscopic and analytical techniques are essential for probing these structure‐function relationships at multiple length scales. Methods such as fluorescence microscopy, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), positron annihilation spectroscopy, and solid‐state spectroscopy provide detailed information on electronic structure, defect distribution, and local coordination environments (Payam et al. 2024). These approaches enable direct correlation between atomic‐scale features and macroscopic performance, particularly in applications involving sensing, catalysis, and controlled release.

Collectively, no single technique is sufficient to fully characterize MOFs. Instead, an integrated, multi‐modal characterization strategy is required to capture the interplay between crystallographic structure, surface chemistry, porosity, and defect states (Table 2). Such a comprehensive approach is essential for accurately predicting performance and guiding the rational design of MOFs for complex environments, including moisture‐rich seafood systems.

Bioactive Functions of MOFs Relevant to Seafood Preservation

MOFs exhibit a diverse bioactive function, including antimicrobial, antioxidant, and pro‐angiogenic activities, which have positioned them as promising platforms in biomedical therapeutic applications and food preservation applications. Their tunable porosity, high surface area, and chemically programmable coordination environments enable efficient loading, protection, and controlled release of bioactive compounds. These structural attributes can improve bioavailability, regulate release kinetics, and support targeted delivery while offering opportunities for biodegradability and safety optimization (Hu et al. 2024). In antimicrobial systems, MOFs can disrupt bacterial membranes, regulate metal‐ion release, and promote ROS‐mediated oxidative stress, making them effective against both foodborne and antibiotic‐resistant pathogens (D. Han et al. 2022). These properties are particularly relevant to seafood preservation, where microbial proliferation, oxidative degradation, and volatile spoilage metabolite formation occur simultaneously (R. Li et al. 2021).

Antimicrobial Activity

The antibacterial activity of MOFs arises from multiple mechanistically distinct pathways that can operate independently or synergistically within a single material system. These include (i) sustained release of bactericidal metal, (ii) catalytic generation of ROS, (iii) contact‐mediated membrane disruption, (iv) carrier‐assisted delivery of antimicrobial agents, and (v) photo or thermally activated processes (D. Han et al. 2022; Figure 6a).

Different mechanisms of MOF (a) antibacterial (D. Han et al. 2022); (b) major antibacterial released metal ions (D. Han et al. 2022); (c) antimicrobial mechanisms of MOFs, including membrane disruption, ROS generation, and oxidative damage to intracellular components; and (d) controlled release, photocatalysis chelation, and synergistic carrier effects, with metal ions, ligands, and functional components of MOF (R. Li et al. 2021).

From an application perspective, these mechanisms can be conceptually classified into ion‐dominant systems (Type I), ROS‐dominant systems (Type II), and hybrid multifunctional systems (Type III), Each exhibits distinct performance characteristics under seafood preservation conditions. Unlike conventional antimicrobial agents that rely on a single dominant mechanism, MOFs integrate these pathways through their coordination architecture, enabling multi‐target antibacterial activity that is particularly advantageous in complex food systems (R. Li et al. 2021; T. L. A. Nguyen and Bhattacharya 2022).

However, the relative contribution of each mechanism is strongly dependent on environmental conditions, and in seafood preservation systems characterized by high moisture content, dissolved oxygen, and low‐temperature storage, ion‐mediated toxicity and ROS generation represent the dominant antibacterial pathways, whereas photodynamic and thermally induced effects are typically secondary due to limited light penetration and controlled storage conditions (R. Li et al. 2021).

Metal ion‐mediated antibacterial activity remains the most extensively established mechanism, with ions such as Ag+, Cu2 +, Zn2 +, Co2 +, and Fe2 +/Fe3 + exerting bactericidal effects through electrostatic interactions with negatively charged bacterial membranes, leading to membrane destabilization, protein denaturation, and increased permeability (Tian et al. 2025; Figure 6b). Following membrane penetration, these ions interact with intracellular components, including DNA and metabolic enzymes, thereby disrupting replication, transcription, and essential biochemical pathways, while simultaneously promoting intracellular ROS formation that accelerates cellular damage (D. Han et al. 2022). Among these, Ag+ exhibits the highest intrinsic antibacterial potency due to its strong affinity for sulfhydryl groups in membrane proteins (Tian et al. 2025). However, its application in seafood systems is constrained by potential ion migration and toxicity concerns. In contrast, Zn‐based frameworks such as ZIF‐8 exhibit a comparatively moderate antibacterial effect through pH‐responsive Zn2+ release, while also serving as carriers for bioactive compounds. Owing to the relatively lower intrinsic toxicity of Zn, compared with Ag and Cu, ZIF‐8 has been widely investigated for food packaging applications. Nevertheless, its safety remains highly dependent on Zn2+ migration, material formulation, and exposure dose since the amount of ion released into the packed foods is directly associated with cytotoxicity. Quercetin@ZIF‐8 systems had the enhanced antibacterial activity against common spoilage bacteria due to the effective controlled release of the encapsulated bioactive compound (Bisht et al. 2026).

Copper‐based MOFs occupy an intermediate position, combining ion toxicity with catalytic ROS generation through redox cycling but similarly require careful regulation of Cu2 + release to ensure safety in food contact applications (Rauf et al. 2024). In addition, a gelatin/carrageenan‐based active‐intelligent film incorporating a Cu‐MOF and red cabbage anthocyanins has been developed. The film containing 3 wt% Cu‐MOF exhibited strong antibacterial activity against both gram‐positive and gram‐negative bacteria, along with excellent UV‐blocking, antioxidant performance, and pH‐responsive color changes for shrimp freshness monitoring (Khan et al. 2023).

ROS‐mediated antibacterial activity constitutes a second dominant pathway, particularly relevant in oxygen‐rich seafood environments. Reactive species, including hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide radicals (·O2 −), hydroxyl radicals (·OH), and singlet oxygen (1O2), induce oxidative damage to bacterial membranes, proteins, and nucleic acids, leading to irreversible cellular dysfunction (Rauf et al. 2024). Moreover, ROS can compromise bacterial viability by degrading the peptidoglycan layer of the cell wall, leading to structural instability and cell lysis (Figure 6c). MOFs enable tunable ROS generation through metal‐centered redox processes, ligand‐mediated photoactivity, and cooperative metal‐ligand interactions, providing a level of control, which is not achievable with conventional antimicrobial materials (Makabenta et al. 2021; Tian et al. 2025; Figure 6d). Nevertheless, in seafood systems, ROS generation introduces a critical balance between antibacterial efficacy and oxidative stability: while being effective in bacterial inactivation. However, excessive ROS can accelerate lipid oxidation of PUFAs, thereby compromising product quality. This dual role necessitates precise control over ROS generation kinetics and localization, representing a key design challenge unique to food preservation applications.

Hybrid MOF systems that integrate ion release, ROS generation, and carrier‐assisted delivery of antimicrobial or antioxidant compounds offer a promising strategy to address these competing effects by simultaneously targeting microbial growth and oxidative degradation. However, increasing functional complexity can introduce limitations, including pore blockage, reduced mass transfer, and difficulty in controlling multi‐component release profiles (H. Cheng et al. 2024; X. Han et al. 2023). In contrast, physical membrane disruption and thermally induced antibacterial mechanisms provide auxiliary effects that may enhance overall performance but are generally less dominant under typical seafood storage conditions due to limited particle‐cell contact and low processing temperatures (Tian et al. 2025).

Overall, the effectiveness of MOF‐based antibacterial systems in seafood preservation is governed not by maximal antimicrobial potency alone but also by the ability to achieve a controlled balance between efficacy, stability, and safety. Optimal designs should therefore prioritize moisture‐stable mechanisms, regulate ROS generation to minimize oxidative damage, and limit metal ion migration while maintaining sufficient mass transfer and accessibility. This application‐driven perspective highlights that the most suitable MOFs for seafood systems are those that integrate multifunctional antibacterial pathways within a framework capable of operating reliably under high‐moisture, oxygen‐rich, and low‐temperature conditions.

Antioxidant Properties

MOFs exhibit intrinsically tunable antioxidant behavior arising from the cooperative redox interactions between metal nodes (e.g., Fe, Co, Zn) and conjugated organic ligands, including catechol, polyphenols, and heterocyclic carboxylates‐based linkers (Benoit et al. 2016; Huang et al. 2018; J. Kim et al. 2016; Lin et al. 2020; Man et al. 2019; Sharma et al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2019). These coordination architectures regulate electron transfer processes and redox potential, thereby enabling targeted mitigation of oxidative spoilage in seafood systems, where lipid oxidation of PUFAs proceeds rapidly via ROS. From an application perspective, MOF‐based antioxidant mechanisms can be conceptually classified into three functional categories: (i) ligand‐dominant radical scavenging systems (Type I), (ii) metal‐centered redox cycling systems (Type II), and (iii) hybrid controlled‐release systems integrating both functionalities (Type III). This application provides a practical framework for correlating the coordination chemistry with antioxidant performance under complex food conditions.

Type I systems rely on ligand‐centered electron donation, where phenolic or polyphenolic ligands coordinate to metal centers through deprotonated hydroxyl groups to form metal‐phenolate complexes (Z. Chen et al. 2024). This coordination enhances π‐electron delocalization, lowers the redox potential of the ligand, and stabilizes phenoxyl radicals generated during ROS scavenging. Consequently, metal‐coordinated antioxidants exhibit improved radical‐scavenging efficiency and oxidative stability, compared with their free molecular counterparts. In contrast, Type II systems are governed by redox‐active metal centers capable of cycling between oxidation states system (e.g., Co2 +/Co3 +, Cu2 +/Cu+, Fe2 +/Fe3 +), thereby continuously regenerating active sites for ROS neutralization (Farasati Far 2024). These systems mimic natural antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), enabling sustained suppression of peroxide formation and secondary oxidation products responsible for off‐flavor development in seafood.

Type III hybrid systems combine these mechanisms with controlled release of antioxidant ligands or encapsulated bioactive compounds, providing both immediate and sustained protection against oxidative degradation. For example, curcumin–zinc MOFs exhibit enhanced stability, pH‐responsive degradation, and effective scavenging of DPPH, ABTS radicals, and H2O2 while also demonstrating anti‐inflammatory activity via TNF‐α suppression (L. ‐H. Chen et al. 2024). Similarly, frameworks incorporating gallic acid or tetrahydroxybenzoquinone motifs had the prolonged antioxidant activity through controlled presentation or release of active species (Park et al. 2024). Compared with conventional antioxidants, which are rapidly depleted, these hybrid systems offer extended functional lifetimes and improved compatibility with dynamic food environments.

However, in seafood systems, MOF‐based antioxidant activity is governed by a critical and often overlooked contradiction. Redox‐active metal centers that enable ROS scavenging can also catalyze ROS generation via Fenton or Fenton‐like reactions, particularly under high‐moisture and oxygen‐rich conditions. This dual behavior is especially pronounced for Fe‐ and Cu‐based frameworks, where uncontrolled redox cycling may accelerate lipid oxidation rather than suppress it. Therefore, the net antioxidant effect of MOFs is not solely determined by intrinsic radical‐scavenging capacity but by the balance between ROS quenching and ROS generation, which is strongly influenced by coordination environment, metal ion speciation, and local chemical conditions (Park et al. 2024; L. Wang et al. 2021).

In addition, antioxidant performance is highly dependent on structural parameters, including pore size, surface area, and the accessibility of redox‐active sites. Frameworks with high densities of accessible metal sites and electron‐rich ligands generally exhibit enhanced ROS neutralization efficiency (Hamarawf 2024). However, excessive pore confinement can limit diffusion of ROS and lipid oxidation intermediates, reducing effectiveness under high‐moisture conditions typical of seafood systems. Conversely, highly open structures may facilitate mass transfer but compromise structural stability and controlled release behavior, highlighting a balance between reactivity and durability (Park et al. 2024; L. Wang et al. 2021).

These consideration suggest that MOFs optimized for seafood preservation should favor moderate redox activity combined with controlled release rather than maximized catalytic activity, in order to minimize unintended pro‐oxidant effects. Zn‐based or ligand‐dominant systems (Type I) are generally more suitable for maintaining oxidative stability, whereas highly redox‐active Fe or Cu‐based systems (Type II) require careful engineering of coordination environment and release kinetics to prevent oxidative acceleration. Hybrid systems (Type III) offer the greatest potential by integrating antioxidant, antimicrobial, and delivery functions; however, they also introduce complexity in controlling multi‐component interactions and transport processes (R. Li et al. 2021; T. L. A. Nguyen and Bhattacharya 2022; Piryaei et al. 2026).

Overall, MOF‐based antioxidant systems provide a versatile platform for mitigating oxidative spoilage in seafood. Nevertheless, their effectiveness depends on the ability to precisely regulate redox processes within complex and moisture‐rich environments. Future research should therefore prioritize the development of frameworks with controlled redox kinetics, optimized pore architectures, and minimal metal ion migration, thereby achieving a balance between antioxidant efficacy, structural stability, and food safety compliance (Bunzen 2021; Maranescu and Visa 2022; Xue et al. 2021).

Application of MOFs in Seafood Preservation and Processing

The sensing capability of MOF‐based platforms depends not only on selective molecular recognition but also on the transduction mechanism, which converts analyte‐induced interactions occurring within the coordination framework into measurable optical or electronic signals. Upon adsorption of target molecules, perturbations in the coordination environment of metal nodes or organic linkers can alter the electronic structure of the framework, thereby generating detectable responses through several transduction pathways. These mechanisms can be broadly classified into four categories: (i) coordination‐induced electronic perturbation, which occurs when analyte coordination to open metal sites modifies ligand‐field splitting or d‐d transition energies, producing visible colorimetric changes, as demonstrated in ammonia‐responsive Co‐MOF systems; (ii) charge transfer modulation, involving ligand‐to‐metal charge transfer (LMCT), metal‐to‐ligand charge transfer (MLCT), or photoinduced electron transfer (PET), which alters fluorescence emission through quenching or enhancement of excited states; (iii) guest‐induced excited‐state energy‐transfer processes, including Forster resonance energy transfer (FRET) and related excited state interactions between framework‐confined fluorophores, which amplify fluorescence signals and improve detection sensitivity in indicator‐encapsulated systems; (iv) catalytic signal amplification, which exploits the intrinsic nanozyme activity of redox‐active MOFs, where analyte‐triggered redox cycling catalyzes the oxidation of chromogenic or fluorogenic substrates to produce amplified optical outputs. These transduction mechanisms are not mutually exclusive; the advanced dual‐mode sensing platforms frequently integrate the multiple pathways within a single architecture to generate complementary signals, thereby improving analytical sensitivity, selectivity, and detection reliability (Table 4).

The effectiveness of MOF‐based preservation systems is fundamentally governed by structure‐property relationships intrinsic to coordination frameworks. In particular, CUSs enhance adsorption affinity toward electron‐donating volatile compounds such as ammonia and TMA through strong Lewis acid‐base interactions (Zulfiqar et al. 2025). However, adsorption capacity alone does not determine performance; mass transfer kinetics play a decisive role in real‐time spoilage control. While microporous frameworks often exhibit high adsorption capacity, their restricted pore dimensions can limit diffusion rates, resulting in delayed response under dynamic storage conditions. In contrast, frameworks with hierarchical or mesoporous architectures facilitate faster molecular transport, enabling rapid capture of spoilage gases but sometimes at the expense of reduced selectivity (Q. Wang et al. 2020). This highlights a key balance between adsorption capacity and response kinetics.

In seafood systems, the relevance of adsorptive regulation is amplified by the rapid accumulation of volatile nitrogenous compounds during post‐mortem degradation. TMA and ammonia are generated through microbial reduction of TMAO and protein decomposition, respectively, and serve as key indicators of spoilage (X. Zhang et al. 2021). Beyond their role as markers, these compounds actively contribute to quality deterioration by disrupting cellular homeostasis, promoting oxidative stress, and accelerating tissue degradation (Gamal et al. 2025). Consequently, MOFs capable of selectively capturing these volatiles can directly delay sensory spoilage and indirectly suppress downstream oxidative processes.

In parallel with adsorption, MOFs contribute to shelf‐life extension through active antimicrobial mechanisms. Metal ions such as Ag+, Zn2 +, and Cu2 + released from MOF structures inhibit microbial proliferation via membrane disruption, enzyme inactivation, and induction of oxidative stress (N. T. T. Nguyen et al. 2024). For example, Sur/Ag@ZIF‐8 treatment significantly delayed spoilage progression in tuna during storage, as evidenced by slower increases in TVB‐N and microbial counts, compared to untreated controls. The treated samples remained within acceptable quality limits for approximately two additional days. Furthermore, the system suppressed lipid oxidation (lower thiobarbituric acid values), delayed pH rebound, and reduced myoglobin oxidation, thereby preserving both chemical and visual quality attributes (Y. Cheng et al. 2025). This example illustrates how MOFs can simultaneously target multiple degradation pathways, unlike conventional preservatives that typically address either microbial or oxidative spoilage independently.

Beyond direct preservation, MOF‐based sensing platforms provide an additional, indirect mechanism for shelf‐life extension by enabling real‐time quality monitoring and informed decision making. For instance, ZIF‐8‐encapsulated gold nanostars integrated into slippery liquid‐infused porous surfaces (AuNS@ZIF‐8‐SLIPS) demonstrated high sensitivity toward pH changes and biogenic amines during shrimp spoilage. The combination of MOF‐based gas adsorption and surface‐enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) signal amplification enabled accurate, non‐destructive freshness detection under both refrigerated and ambient conditions, supporting optimized storage management (H. Guo et al. 2023). The mechanistic basis of these sensing responses is elaborated in details in (Section 4.3), where colorimetric, fluorescent, electrochemical, and dual‐mode MOF sensing platforms are examined according to their underlying transduction pathways.

In addition to these functions, MOFs can serve as controlled‐release carriers for antioxidants and antimicrobial agents, enabling sustained inhibition of lipid oxidation and microbial growth over extended storage periods (Maranescu and Visa 2022). For example, copper‐based MOF nanozyme (MOF‐818) exhibits intrinsic enzyme‐like activities, including SOD and CAT‐mimicking behavior, allowing efficient scavenging of ROS and regulation of oxidative pathways. Through surface functionalization and cargo encapsulation, these systems provide integrated antioxidant delivery and oxidative stress mitigation, demonstrating the potential of MOFs as multifunctional preservation platforms (H. Zhang et al. 2026; Table 5).

Multifunctional MOF Integrated With Packaging

Multifunctional MOF‐integrated packaging systems represent an advanced strategy for extending seafood shelf life by embedding MOF directly within polymeric matrices to create active packaging materials with enhanced preservation performance. Unlike conventional packaging, which primarily provides passive barrier protection, MOF‐integrated films are designed to actively regulate the internal microenvironment of packaged seafood by combining moisture resistance, antimicrobial activity, and controlled release of functional agents within a single material platform (H. Zhang et al. 2025; Figure 7a).

MOF‐based biosensing strategies for seafood freshness and spoilage detection (a) Zn‐MOF‐based intelligent active hydrogel for real‐time monitoring and preservation of shrimp freshness via antioxidant and antibacterial functions (M. Cheng et al. 2025).

From a material design perspective, the effectiveness of MOF‐integrated packaging is governed by the interaction between the coordination framework and the host polymer matrix. Incorporation of MOFs into biopolymers such as poly(vinyl alcohol), gelatin, or cellulose derivatives enables the formation of composite films with improved mechanical strength, barrier properties, and environmental stability. In particular, hydrophobic surface modification of MOFs plays a critical role in packaging applications by enhancing compatibility with polymer matrices and preventing water‐induced framework degradation under high‐humidity storage conditions typical of seafood products (X. Li, Yu, et al. 2025; H. Zhang et al. 2025). This modification reduces moisture uptake within the film, stabilizes the embedded MOF structure, and maintains long‐term functional performance.

A key advantage of MOF‐integrated packaging lies in its ability to provide controlled release of active agents, including antimicrobial metal ions or encapsulated bioactive compounds. The porous structure of MOFs enables gradual diffusion of these agents into the surrounding environment, thereby sustaining antimicrobial activity over extended storage periods (Raveena and Kumari 2024). For example, incorporation of metal‐based MOFs into polymer films has been shown to suppress microbial growth and delay spoilage progression in seafood by maintaining a consistent release profile, in contrast to conventional additives that exhibit rapid depletion (N. T. T. Nguyen et al. 2024). This controlled‐release behavior is particularly important in seafood systems, where continuous microbial activity requires prolonged inhibition rather than short‐term intervention.

In addition to active preservation, MOF incorporation can significantly enhance barrier properties of packaging films. The presence of well‐dispersed MOF particles can reduce water vapor permeability and improve resistance to moisture‐induced degradation by creating tortuous diffusion pathways within the polymer matrix (Zhou et al. 2023). For instance, MOF‐loaded composite films have demonstrated increased WCA, reduced water solubility, and improved dimensional stability under humid conditions, all of which contribute to maintaining packaging integrity during seafood storage (H. Zhang et al. 2025). However, excessive MOF loading or poor dispersion can lead to particle aggregation, creating defects that compromise barrier performance and mechanical strength.

The performance of MOF‐integrated packaging is therefore governed by several critical performance constraints. Increasing MOF content can enhance antimicrobial activity and barrier properties but may reduce film flexibility and transparency. Similarly, strong interactions between MOFs and polymer matrices improve structural stability but can limit the accessibility of active sites and reduce the efficiency of controlled release. Achieving optimal performance requires balancing these competing factors through careful control of MOF loading, particle size, surface chemistry, and dispersion within the matrix (X. Li, Yu, et al. 2025; H. Zhang et al. 2025).

Despite these advantages, several challenges still remain for practical implementation. Long‐term stability of MOFs under continuous moisture exposure, potential migration of metal ions into food matrices, and scalability of composite film fabrication must be carefully addressed to ensure regulatory compliance and commercial viability.

Use of MOF as the Sensors in Food Applications

Colorimetric MOF‐Based Sensors

Colorimetric MOF‐based sensors represent a class of visually interpretable detection systems that enable rapid, instrument‐free monitoring of seafood freshness through analyte‐induced optical changes. Unlike conventional indicators that rely solely on pH‐sensitive dyes, MOF‐based colorimetric systems exploit coordination chemistry, redox activity, and host‐guest interactions within the framework to generate selective and amplified color responses toward spoilage‐related compounds (Ma et al. 2021; Tao et al. 2024; Xu, Liu, et al. 2024). These systems can be broadly classified into two categories: (i) framework‐responsive sensors, in which the MOF itself undergoes electronic or structural changes upon analyte interaction, and (ii) indicator‐encapsulated systems, where MOFs act as protective hosts that stabilize and modulate the response of embedded colorimetric dyes.

In framework‐responsive systems, direct interaction between analytes and coordinatively active metal centers induces measurable changes in the electronic structure of the MOF. For example, Li et al. (2023) developed an ammonia‐sensitive Co‐MOF incorporated into a poly(vinyl alcohol) film, which exhibited excellent stability during storage, maintaining minimal color variation after 30 days at 30°C and 57% relative humidity (ΔE < 1.3). Upon exposure to ammonia, the film displayed a distinct color transition from light blue to brown‐yellow (ΔE > 14), readily visible to the naked eye. This response arises from the coordination of ammonia molecules to Co2 + centers, which alters the ligand field environment and modulates d‐d electronic transitions within the metal nodes. When applied to shrimp storage, the observed color change correlated strongly with TVB‐N levels, enabling direct visual differentiation between fresh and spoiled states (S. Li et al. 2023). Compared with conventional dye‐based indicators, such framework‐driven systems offer improved selectivity due to the specificity of coordination interactions. Representative colorimetric MOF sensing strategies for volatile spoilage gases are illustrated in Figure 7b.

In contrast, indicator‐encapsulated systems enhance sensing stability and versatility by incorporating responsive dyes within MOF pores. The confinement of dye molecules within the framework prevents leaching, improves chemical stability, and enables multi‐analyte detection through sensor arrays. For instance, Ma et al. (2021) employed density functional theory (DFT) calculations to identify UiO‐66‐Br as an optimal host for constructing a colorimetric sensor array. Seven dyes‐methyl red, zinc tetraphenyl porphyrin, curcumin, chlorophenol red, bromocresol purple, bromocresol green, and bromophenol blue were encapsulated within the MOF and immobilized on an ice‐templated chitosan substrate. The resulting system exhibited low detection limits for ammonia (37.17 ppm), methylamine (25.90 ppm), and TMA (40.65 ppm) and was successfully applied to shrimp freshness monitoring. Integration with deep convolutional neural network models trained on over 31,000 labeled images enabled accurate classification of freshness states, where mixed color patterns indicated fresh samples (TVB‐N < 30 mg/100 g) and brown‐yellow dominance corresponded to spoilage (TVB‐N ≥ 30 mg/100 g; Ma et al. 2021). This highlights the potential of MOFs to function as both stabilizing matrices and signal amplifiers in advanced sensing systems.

Beyond volatile amines, MOF‐based colorimetric sensors have been extended to detect biochemical spoilage markers such as hypoxanthine, a key intermediate formed during ATP degradation in post‐mortem seafood. Unlike gaseous indicators, hypoxanthine provides a more direct measure of biochemical freshness. Z. Li et al. (2024) developed a bimetallic Fe‐Ni MOF nanozyme capable of detecting hypoxanthine across a linear range of 3–70 µM, with a detection limit of 1.39 µM and recovery rates between 90.04% and 107.37% in multiple seafood matrices, including sea bass, yellow croaker, shrimp, and scallops. The system produced a clear color transition from colorless to deep blue as hypoxanthine concentration increased, enabling quantitative assessment of spoilage progression. Similarly, H. Wu et al. (2023) reported 2D Fe‐MOF nanosheets that allowed visual discrimination between fresh, semi‐fresh, and spoiled shrimp over 0–24 h of storage based on concentration‐dependent color changes. Compared with volatile amine detection, these systems offer higher biochemical specificity but may require direct sample contact, limiting their applicability in non‐invasive packaging formats (H. Wu et al. 2023).

Despite their advantages, MOF‐based colorimetric sensors are facing several performance limitations. Framework‐responsive systems offer high selectivity but may suffer from limited tunability, whereas indicator‐encapsulated systems provide broader detection capability at the expense of increased system complexity (Jubayer et al. 2026). Additionally, environmental factors such as humidity, temperature fluctuations, and competing analytes can influence color stability and response accuracy. Therefore, effective sensor design requires balancing sensitivity, selectivity, and environmental robustness, particularly under the variable conditions encountered in seafood storage.

Fluorescent MOF‐Based Sensors

Fluorescent MOF‐based sensors represent a highly sensitive and quantitatively reliable platform for seafood freshness evaluation, offering significantly lower detection limits and improved analytical precision, compared with colorimetric systems. Unlike visually readable indicators, fluorescence‐based systems enable detection of subtle variations in analyte concentration through emission intensity changes, wavelength shifts, or energy‐transfer modulation (Pan et al. 2025). From a mechanistic standpoint, these sensors can be classified into three functional categories: (i) intrinsic framework‐responsive systems (Type I), (ii) dye‐loaded signal amplification systems (Type II), and (iii) nanozyme‐coupled catalytic systems (Type III). Each exhibits distinct performance characteristics in terms of sensitivity, selectivity, and operational complexity (H. Zhang et al. 2025).

In Type I systems, the MOF itself functions as the fluorescent probe, where analyte interaction induces modulation of emission properties via coordination, charge‐transfer, or fluorescence‐quenching mechanisms. These systems enable label‐free detection and benefit from structural simplicity and reduced risk of dye leaching. For example, Melendreras et al. (2025) developed Cu‐based nano‐MOFs (∼35 nm) immobilized on cellulose substrates for biogenic amine detection. Upon exposure to amine vapors, the system exhibited significant fluorescence quenching at 525 nm within 30 min, with detection limits as low as 2.25–4.33 ppm. The response originates from coordination between amines and Cu2+ centers, which perturbs the electronic structure of the framework and induces non‐radiative decay pathways. While these systems offer high selectivity and operational simplicity, their sensitivity is inherently limited by the intrinsic photophysical properties of the framework (Melendreras et al. 2025).

Type II systems enhance fluorescence performance through incorporation of external fluorophores within MOF pores, where confinement effects improve signal intensity, stability, and resistance to photobleaching. In these systems, MOFs serve as both protective hosts and microenvironmental regulators that modulate fluorophore‐analyte interactions. Xu, Wang, et al. (2024) developed a lanthanide MOF‐based sensor array incorporating fluorescein isothiocyanate and methyl cellulose, where covalent coupling suppressed dye leaching and enhanced signal stability. The system achieved detection limits of 3.94–4.80 ppm for ammonia, dimethylamine, and TMA. Amine‐induced pH elevation enhanced fluorescein emission while suppressing internal energy transfer within the MOF, resulting in a distinct fluorescence transition from red to green. Compared with intrinsic systems, dye‐loaded platforms provide higher tunability and signal amplification; however, they introduce additional complexity in synthesis and potential long‐term stability limitations under fluctuating environmental conditions (Xu, Wang, et al. 2024). Also, ratiometric fluorescence sensing using lanthanide MOF composite films for histamine detection is illustrated in Figure 7c.

Type III systems integrate enzyme‐mimetic catalytic activity with fluorescence detection, enabling highly sensitive and quantitative analysis through cascade reactions. These nanozyme‐based platforms typically rely on analyte‐triggered reactions that generate fluorescent products, significantly amplifying the detection signal. A representative example is the Fe‐Ni bimetallic MOF nanozyme reported by Z. Li et al. (2024) capable of detecting hypoxanthine via a cascade reaction involving xanthine oxidase and MOF‐mimicked peroxidase activity. The fluorescence signal generated through oxidation of 3,3′,5,5′‐tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) exhibited strong correlation with hypoxanthine concentration, enabling accurate quantification in real seafood matrices. While these systems offer superior sensitivity and reproducibility, they require additional reagents and controlled reaction conditions, limiting their applicability in simplified or on‐site detection scenarios.

Electrochemical MOF‐Based Sensors

Electrochemical MOF‐based sensors have emerged as one of the most promising analytical platforms for real‐time seafood freshness monitoring because they combine the high surface area, tunable pore architecture, and catalytic activity of MOFs with rapid electrochemical signal transduction (Gonçalves et al. 2021). Unlike conventional chromatographic techniques, electrochemical sensors provide rapid, portable, low‐cost, and highly sensitive detection of seafood spoilage biomarkers, making them attractive for on‐site quality assessment throughout the seafood supply chain. The sensing performance originates from the unique structural characteristics of MOFs, which concentrate target molecules near electroactive sites while facilitating catalytic oxidation or reduction reactions (Hosseinikebria et al. 2025). However, the intrinsically low electrical conductivity of many pristine MOFs remains a major limitation. Recent studies therefore integrate MOFs with conductive nanomaterials such as graphene, carbon nanotubes, MXenes, or noble metal nanoparticles to accelerate electron transport while preserving the molecular recognition capability of porous frameworks.

Electrochemical MOF sensors have been successfully applied for detecting histamine, hypoxanthine, xanthine, ammonia, TMA, and other biogenic amines generated during seafood spoilage. These biomarkers directly correlate with microbial metabolism and ATP degradation, allowing rapid assessment of freshness in fish, shrimp, and shellfish. Their compatibility with miniaturized electrodes further enables integration into portable analytical devices and intelligent packaging systems for continuous quality monitoring (Hosseinikebria et al. 2025). Despite remarkable analytical performance under laboratory conditions, several challenges remain before commercial implementation. Complex seafood matrices containing proteins, lipids, salts, and endogenous electroactive compounds may interfere with sensor selectivity and long‐term stability (Gonçalves et al. 2021). Future research should therefore prioritize antifouling electrode design, multiplex detection of multiple spoilage biomarkers, flexible wearable sensing platforms, wireless data transmission, and integration with artificial intelligence for shelf‐life estimation.

Dual‐Mode MOF‐Based Sensing System

Dual‐mode MOF‐based sensing systems have emerged as an advanced strategy to enhance detection accuracy and reliability in seafood freshness monitoring by integrating complementary signal outputs within a single platform. Unlike single‐mode sensors, which rely on either visual or instrumental readouts, dual‐mode systems combine colorimetric responses for rapid, intuitive interpretation with fluorescent signals for sensitive and quantitative analysis, thereby enabling multidimensional and real‐time assessment of spoilage progression (H. Zhang et al. 2025). This dual‐signal approach improves robustness against environmental variability, reduces false positives arising from matrix interference, and minimizes user subjectivity associated with visual evaluation alone.

Dual‐mode sensors can be functionally categorized into three classes: (i) reaction‐coupled systems (Type I), where a single analyte‐triggered reaction simultaneously generates colorimetric and fluorescent responses; (ii) signal‐decoupled systems (Type II), in which independent colorimetric and fluorescent pathways respond to the same analyte; and (iii) cascade‐amplified systems (Type III), where sequential catalytic or enzymatic reactions enhance both signal outputs. This classification provides a framework for understanding how signal integration influences sensitivity, selectivity, and system robustness.

Peroxidase‐mimetic MOFs are particularly well suited for Type I reaction‐coupled systems due to their intrinsic catalytic activity. Cheng et al. (2024) synthesized a cerium‐based MOF exhibiting peroxidase‐like behavior, capable of catalyzing the oxidation of TMB into a blue‐colored product. In the presence of hydrogen sulfide (H2S), a key volatile marker of seafood spoilage, this catalytic process is inhibited due to strong coordination between sulfide species and Ce centers, which disrupts the Ce4 +/Ce3 + redox cycle. As a result, the system undergoes simultaneous color fading and fluorescence enhancement, generating two correlated but distinct signal outputs. The sensor achieved detection limits of 0.262 µM (colorimetric) and 0.156 µM (fluorescent) and was successfully applied to monitor the freshness of salmon and shrimp, where fresh samples retained a blue color and spoiled samples became colorless with strong fluorescence signals (Q. Cheng et al. 2024). This coupled‐response mechanism enhances detection reliability by providing internal cross‐validation between optical modes. Representative dual‐mode sensing platforms integrating fluorescence and visual hydrogel responses for biogenic amine detection are illustrated in Figure 8a.

Synthesis and biogenic amine (BA) sensing mechanism of Ru@UiO‐OH MOFs and sensory hydrogel‐enabled visual assessment of seafood freshness (Jia et al. 2023) (a); Fe3O4@MIL‐100(Fe)@Pt nanozyme‐based colorimetric aptasensor for sensitive histamine detection (Xu, Liu, et al. 2024) (b).

Catalytic nanozyme‐based dual‐mode platforms have also been coupled with molecular recognition elements to improve analyte specificity. For example, Xu, Liu, et al. (2024) developed an Fe3O4@MIL‐100(Fe)@Pt nanozyme‐based colorimetric aptasensor for histamine detection. The catalytic Pt nanozyme amplified the oxidation reaction, while the histamine‐specific aptamer provided molecular selectivity, enabling sensitive and accurate detection of histamine in seafood matrices. This strategy illustrates how catalytic amplification and biomolecular recognition can be integrated into dual‐mode MOF sensing platforms to improve analytical performance (Figure 8b).

The selection of dual‐mode sensing strategies should be guided by application requirements. Reaction‐coupled systems (Type I) are particularly suitable for rapid and reliable detection due to their inherent signal correlation, while signal‐decoupled systems (Type II) provide flexibility in tuning sensitivity and selectivity across different analytes. Cascade‐amplified systems (Type III) offer the highest sensitivity but are best suited for laboratory or semi‐controlled environments where reaction conditions can be carefully regulated. Therefore, the optimal design of dual‐mode MOF sensors depends on balancing analytical performance with environmental robustness and practical deployability (C. Li et al. 2025).

Gas Adsorption and Regulation in Seafood Systems

Spoilage of seafood is dominated by the generation of small, volatile, basic, and sulfur‐containing gases such as ammonia (NH3), TMA, dimethylamine (DMA), and hydrogen sulfide (H2S), which arise from enzymatic deamination of amino acids and microbial breakdown of proteins and TMAO (Cooney et al. 2023). These volatile species not only serve as quantitative indicators of freshness loss but also directly contribute to sensory deterioration and potential safety risks. Consequently, their selective adsorption and regulation represent a critical strategy for extending shelf life and enabling intelligent packaging systems (Ferreira et al. 2024). In this context, MOF‐based platforms have demonstrated strong potential for both adsorption‐driven preservation and real‐time monitoring, as evidenced by heterometallic Co‐Mn‐BTC MOFs, integrated into quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) sensors, which exhibited sensitive and simultaneous detection of volatile amines with strong correlation to TVB‐N levels in salmon (G. Cheng et al. 2025).

Gas adsorption in MOFs can be classified into three functional modes: (i) physisorption‐dominant systems (Type I), governed by van der Waals interactions and pore confinement; (ii) chemisorption‐dominant systems (Type II), driven by strong coordination or acid–base interactions at open metal sites; and (iii) hybrid adsorption systems (Type III), where both mechanisms operate synergistically. This classification provides a useful framework for understanding how adsorption strength, selectivity, and reversibility can be tuned through coordination chemistry.

The high adsorption performance of MOFs arises from their well‐defined porous structures and the presence of CUSs, which act as strong binding centers for electron‐donating molecules. In particular, NH3, as a Lewis base, interacts preferentially with Lewis acidic metal centers or Brønsted acidic μ‐OH groups through coordination bonding and hydrogen bonding interactions (X. Han et al. 2023). Functionalization of MOF linkers further enhances adsorption by stabilizing guest molecules through electrostatic interactions and π‐electron coupling. Compared with conventional adsorbents such as activated carbon or zeolites, MOFs offer superior selectivity due to their tunable pore environments and specific host–guest interactions, enabling targeted capture of spoilage gases rather than non‐specific adsorption.

The adsorption of sulfur‐containing gases such as H2S is governed by similar but often stronger interactions due to the high affinity of sulfur species for transition metal centers. Mechanistic studies using MIL‐type frameworks provide detailed insight into these interactions. In MIL‐47(V) and MIL‐53(Cr), H2S initially adsorbs via hydrogen bonding with μ2‐O and μ2‐OH groups, followed by stabilization through interactions with carboxylate oxygen atoms and aromatic π‐systems within the framework. As pressure increases, cooperative interactions between H2S molecules lead to dimer formation within the pores. Notably, adsorption strength varies significantly across framework structures, following the order MIL‐53(Cr) NP > MIL‐47(V) > MIL‐53(Cr) LP, indicating that MIL‐47(V) provides an optimal balance between adsorption strength and reversibility (Barea et al. 2014). In contrast, highly porous frameworks such as MIL‐100(Cr) and MIL‐101(Cr) exhibit high uptake capacities but suffer from irreversible adsorption and partial structural degradation, limiting their practical reusability.

These observations highlight a critical design contradiction. Strong adsorption enhances capture efficiency but may compromise reversibility and structural stability, particularly for sulfur‐containing gases. This design constraint is especially relevant in seafood preservation, where continuous exposure to volatile compounds and high humidity requires materials can repeatedly adsorb and release gases without degradation. For example, Gamal et al. (2025) reported a biogenic Ca‐BDC MOF derived from fish bones and eggshells, where the fish‐bone‐derived material exhibited higher surface area (721.38 m2/g) and superior NH3 adsorption capacity (616.11 mg/g), compared with its eggshell counterpart, following Langmuir isotherm and pseudo‐second‐order kinetics. This demonstrates how precursor selection and structural tuning can directly influence adsorption performance.

In practical seafood systems, humidity represents a major constraint on adsorption efficiency. Water molecules compete with target gases for active sites, often reducing adsorption capacity and selectivity. This competitive adsorption effect necessitates the design of MOFs with hydrophobic pore environments or selectively functionalized surfaces that minimize water interference while maintaining affinity for volatile amines and sulfur compounds (Burtch et al. 2014). Furthermore, integration of MOFs into polymeric packaging matrices introduces additional challenges, including pore blockage, particle aggregation, and reduced mass transfer, which can significantly limit effective adsorption (Song et al. 2026). Addressing these challenges will require the rational design of hydrophobic pore environments, hierarchical channel architectures, and improved MOFpolymer interfacial compatibility.

These constraints suggest that optimal MOF design for seafood preservation should prioritize moderate binding strength with high reversibility, hierarchical pore architectures for efficient diffusion, and hydrophobic or selectively functionalized surfaces to mitigate moisture effects. Purely chemisorptive systems (Type II) may provide high adsorption capacity but are prone to irreversible binding, whereas physisorption‐dominant systems (Type I) offer better regenerability but lower selectivity. Hybrid systems (Type III) that balance these interactions are therefore potentially more suitable for real‐world applications after validation under realistic seafood storage conditions, where both efficiency and durability are required.

Challenges and Limitations in Applications of MOFs

Despite the remarkable structural tunability and multifunctionality of MOFs, several fundamental challenges continue to limit their large‐scale and real‐world implementation, particularly in sensitive applications such as food packaging, preservation, and biomedical systems (Maranescu and Visa 2022). One of the primary bottlenecks is scalability, as many high‐performance MOFs are synthesized via complex solvothermal routes that require prolonged reaction times, high temperatures, autoclaves, and large volumes of organic solvents. These batch‐based laboratory methods are inherently incompatible with industrial throughput, leading to poor reproducibility and limited yield (Xue et al. 2021). Although alternative strategies such as continuous‐flow synthesis, mechanochemical approaches, and microwave‐assisted methods have emerged as promising solutions, these techniques still require further optimization to ensure consistent crystal quality, controlled particle size, and cost efficiency at scale (Khalid Rahaman et al. 2024; J. Li, Lin, et al. 2025; Nath 2024).

In parallel with manufacturing constraints, chemical and thermal instability remains a critical limitation for MOF deployment under practical conditions. Many MOFs exhibit pronounced sensitivity to moisture, pH fluctuations, and elevated temperatures, which can induce hydrolysis of metal‐ligand coordination bonds, framework collapse, and loss of porosity (H. Zhang et al. 2025). This instability is particularly problematic in applications such as gas adsorption, catalysis, and food preservation, when exposed to humidity, organic acids, or thermal stress. The susceptibility of certain MOFs to degradation under real storage or processing conditions limits their long‐term performance and raises the concerns regarding structural integrity and safety (Jiang et al. 2025; Xue et al. 2021).

From an economic perspective, the high cost of raw materials and organic ligands significantly restricts the commercial viability of MOFs. Many top‐performing frameworks rely on expensive transition metals or synthetically complex aromatic ligands, resulting in production costs that far exceed those of conventional adsorbents, fillers, or antimicrobial agents (Comlek et al. 2023). Consequently, the development of cost‐effective synthesis routes and low‐cost frameworks is essential for enabling large‐scale adoption, particularly in price‐sensitive sectors such as food packaging (Venkatesan et al. 2025).

Apart from their performance and cost, the environmental and toxicological concerns represent one of the most critical barriers to MOF commercialization. A major safety risk arises from the potential leaching of MOF building blocks including metal ions and organic linkers into the surrounding medium, especially under moist or acidic conditions (H. Zhang et al. 2025). Several widely studied MOFs incorporate metals such as chromium (e.g., MIL‐series) or copper (e.g., HKUST‐1), which may exhibit toxicity at elevated concentrations. However, the direct incorporation of Co‐ or Ni‐based MOFs into food contact materials (FCMs) remains highly challenging from both toxicological and regulatory perspectives. In most cases, such frameworks are better regarded as proof‐of‐concept systems for sensing or catalytic applications or must be physically isolated from the food matrix through encapsulation or external sensing platforms. Likewise, many organic linkers are synthetic aromatic compounds whose metabolic fate, bioaccumulation potential, and long‐term health effects upon ingestion remain insufficiently understood. Consequently, a key research priority has been the design of biocompatible and “generally recognized as safe” (GRAS) MOFs, often referred to as bio‐MOFs. Significant progress has been achieved through the use of food‐grade metals such as Fe(III), Zr(IV), Ca(II), and Mg(II), combined with naturally derived ligands, including amino acids, peptides, nucleobases, and cyclodextrins (H. Zhang et al. 2025). In particular, zirconium‐based MOFs, such as the UiO‐66 family, are frequently drawing attention due to the exceptional stability of Zr‐O clusters, which minimizes metal ion release and confers superior resistance to hydrolytic degradation (H. Zhang et al. 2025). The nanoscale physical form of MOFs introduces further toxicological complexity. As many MOFs are employed as nanoparticles, their size, morphology, surface charge, and functionalization critically influence biological interactions, including absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME; Jiang et al. 2025). However, systematic investigations of MOF nanoparticles within the gastrointestinal tract, their potential for bioaccumulation, and their long‐term systemic effects still remain limited (Jiang et al. 2025). This knowledge gap poses a significant obstacle to regulatory approval, particularly for food‐contact applications.

Regulatory compliance represents another major challenge. For FCMs, safety evaluation must satisfy stringent requirements imposed by authorities such as the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA). These assessments focus primarily on migration behavior, toxicological profiles, and dietary exposure (H. Zhang et al. 2025). For example, HKUST‐1 has been shown to leach Cu2 + ions under humid or acidic conditions, potentially inducing oxidative stress or cytotoxicity. Similarly, several synthetic ligands used in MOFs lack the approval for direct food contact, and their degradation products are poorly characterized (H. Zhang et al. 2025). In vitro cytotoxicity studies, including MTT and lactate dehydrogenase assays, have demonstrated that MOFs exhibit highly framework‐dependent toxicity. ZIF‐8, for instance, shows notable cytotoxic effects in human epithelial cells at concentrations above 25–30 µg/mL, primarily due to Zn2 + ion release and ROS generation, whereas MIL‐100(Fe) displays significantly lower toxicity, with reported IC50 values approaching 700 mg/L in certain cell lines. In vivo studies further indicate that high doses of crystalline ZIF‐8 can cause systemic toxicity in animal models, while amorphous or polymer‐embedded systems exhibit improved biocompatibility (Singh et al. 2021; Wiśniewska et al. 2023).

Migration studies conducted using food simulants such as acetic acid and ethanol, in accordance with European regulations, have revealed the release of Zn2 + and Cu2 + ions from certain MOFs at levels that may approach or exceed specific migration limits. EFSA therefore requires comprehensive toxicological datasets‐including genotoxicity, sub‐chronic, and reproductive toxicity studies, when the exposure is significant. However, most current MOF studies focus primarily on short‐term cytotoxicity, leaving chronic exposure and long‐term migration effects largely unexplored. Prolonged release of metal ions, even at low concentrations, may accelerate lipid oxidation, alter food flavor profiles, or interfere with nutrient stability, emphasizing the need for systematic evaluation under realistic storage conditions (H. Zhang et al. 2025).

In the United States, the FDA regulates FCMs under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. The new substances or components intended for food contact must undergo pre‐market evaluation through mechanisms such as Food Contact Substance Notifications (FCNs) or gain GRAS status based on migration and toxicological evidence. Otherwise, they cannot be legally marketed for direct food contact (Grob 2019). In the European Union, materials intended to be in contact with food must comply with the general safety requirements of Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004 and specific authorization procedures coordinated through EFSA, which involve case‐by‐case assessment of migration and health risk data. Novel materials that do not yet fall under existing specific Union lists must also be assessed before the approval can be granted (Grob 2019). No current evidence indicates that any MOF‐based framework has been specifically authorized as a FCM under the FDA's FCN program or listed under EU food contact regulations, underscoring the ongoing regulatory uncertainty and the need for standardized safety assessment frameworks that consider migration behavior, nanoparticle effects, and long‐term exposure (Nerin et al. 2025).

To translate MOF‐based materials from laboratory studies to regulated food contact technologies, a clearly defined regulatory approval pathway must be established. To overcome the current regulatory barriers, a rational “design for regulation” strategy must be integrated into the early stages of MOF development. Instead of relying on traditional high‐performance frameworks containing potentially toxic metals or synthetic ligands, future food contact MOFs should prioritize components already recognized as safe within food systems (Sheik et al. 2024; Q. Wang et al. 2020). Frameworks constructed from food‐grade metal nodes such as Fe(III), Ca(II), Mg(II), or Zn(II), combined with biocompatible ligands including amino acids, organic acids, cyclodextrins, or naturally occurring polyphenols, offer a promising pathway toward regulatory acceptance. In addition, engineering strategies that minimize direct exposure, such as embedding MOF particles within polymer matrices, surface immobilization onto packaging substrates, or encapsulation with biodegradable coatings, can significantly reduce migration risks. Migration studies using standardized food simulants (e.g., ethanol, acetic acid, and oil‐based systems) should be coupled with comprehensive toxicological assessments including genotoxicity, chronic exposure, and gastrointestinal stability testing. Such datasets would support submissions through established regulatory pathways, including the US FDA, the FCN program, or EFSA's risk assessment framework for novel food‐contact materials. Furthermore, the development of standardized testing protocols specific to porous nanomaterials could greatly facilitate regulatory evaluation. Collaborative efforts between materials scientists, toxicologists, and regulatory agencies will therefore be essential to establish harmonized guidelines for MOF safety assessment. By aligning coordination chemistry design principles with regulatory requirements, it may become feasible to transition MOF‐based packaging systems from laboratory research to commercially approved food preservation technologies.

To further mitigate these risks, recent research has focused on design‐for‐safety strategies, including the immobilization of MOFs within polymer matrices to reduce direct exposure and ion migration, as well as the development of frameworks constructed entirely from edible ligands and food‐grade metals (Nerin et al. 2025). While these approaches show promise, regulatory acceptance still requires rigorous compliance with migration limits, proof of non‐genotoxicity, and confirmation of environmental safety through life‐cycle assessment (Venkatesan et al. 2025).

From a sustainability standpoint, large‐scale MOF deployment raises concerns regarding recyclability, disposal, and environmental persistence. Although some MOFs degrade into benign components, others may contribute to nanoparticle pollution if improperly managed. Consequently, increasing emphasis is being placed on biodegradable frameworks, recyclable composites, and green synthesis utilizing aqueous systems and renewable ligands. Nevertheless, the economic feasibility remains a decisive constraint (Singh et al. 2021; Venkatesan et al. 2025; Wiśniewska et al. 2023). Commercial MOFs are currently expensive, with prices often exceeding $7.38/g, severely limiting their applicability in bulk food systems. Techno‐economic analyses suggest that alternative synthesis routes, such as mechanochemical liquid‐assisted grinding and aqueous one‐pot methods, could reduce costs to below $10.8/kg for selected frameworks (Julien et al. 2017). Encouragingly, industrial‐scale production of MIL‐160(Al) under green conditions has demonstrated the costs as low as $29.5/kg at kiloton‐scale production, with projections below $10/kg when bio‐derived ligands are employed (Severino et al. 2021). Nevertheless, achieving cost parity with conventional food packaging materials remains a significant challenge.

Future Perspectives and Research Opportunities

The successful translation of MOF‐based smart packaging from laboratory concepts to industrial food systems requires targeted solutions to several challenges covering material performance, food compatibility, safety, scalability, and regulatory compliance. A primary scientific bottleneck lies in the intrinsic design constraint between porosity and hydrophobicity, particularly under high‐humidity conditions, typically found during seafood storage. Future research should focus on the potential design of MOFs by incorporating tailored hydrophobic functional groups, hierarchical pore architectures, and hybrid composite structures, enabling selective gas adsorption while maintaining moisture resistance. In parallel, integrating MOFs with smart regulatory or responsive polymeric matrices may enhance the mechanical integrity and long‐term stability of hydrophobic layers under fluctuating temperature and humidity, thereby ensuring sustained performance during cold‐chain logistics.

Next‐generation MOF systems must move beyond passive inhibition toward precisely controlled and stimulus‐responsive release mechanisms. The development of humidity, pH, or light‐triggered antimicrobial delivery systems, combined with improved interfacial compatibility between MOFs and food‐grade biopolymers, could significantly reduce premature loss of active agents while enhancing photocatalytic or contact‐based antimicrobial efficiency (Jiang et al. 2025; Venkatesan et al. 2025). Safety concerns related to nanoparticle migration and metal ion leaching necessitate rigorous toxicological evaluation under realistic food contact conditions. In addition, the potential migration of residual precursors, unreacted ligands, and degradation by‐products from MOF structures into food matrices must be carefully considered, as these may pose risks to consumer health. Strategies such as surface passivation or polymer encapsulation can help minimize such migration and residue levels. In this context, MOFs constructed from food‐grade metals (e.g., Ca, Fe, Zn) and naturally derived ligands (amino acids, organic acids, sugars) represent a promising pathway toward enhanced biosafety, biodegradability, and regulatory acceptance while maintaining functional performance.

To systematically address safety and compliance, a high‐priority research direction is the establishment of a “food‐grade MOF library” composed exclusively of GRAS components such as food‐grade metal ions such as Ca2+, Mg2+, Fe3+, and Zn2+ combined with GRAS ligands including amino acids, cyclodextrins, and naturally occurring organic acids (e.g., citric acid, lactic acid). Such a platform should be accompanied by standardized protocols for structural stability, metal and linker leaching, and degradation behavior, which are evaluated under simulated food processing, storage, and gastrointestinal conditions. Leaching levels below tolerable daily intake limits will be essential for long‐term consumer safety and regulatory approval, thereby providing a robust legal and scientific foundation for commercialization (Jiang et al. 2025).

Another critical barrier to industrial adoption is manufacturing scalability and cost. Future MOF synthesis must transition away from energy‐intensive solvothermal methods and move toward green, scalable production strategies, including water‐based synthesis, mechanochemical routes, and continuous‐flow processing (Barsoum et al. 2025). A decisive benchmark for success will be the ability to produce large quantities of structurally stable, high‐quality MOFs with high yields and production costs comparable to conventional adsorbents such as activated carbon or silica. Concurrently, advances in life‐cycle assessment and techno‐economic analysis are needed to validate the sustainability and economic viability of MOF‐based packaging solutions (Barsoum et al. 2025; Gagliardi and Yaghi 2023).

The true transformative potential of MOFs lies in their multifunctionality, and future research should prioritize the design of integrated smart packaging systems rather than single‐function materials. For example, MOF‐based composite films capable of simultaneous spoilage gas scavenging (e.g., ethylene, ammonia), humidity‐triggered antimicrobial release, and real‐time colorimetric freshness indication would offer holistic quality control within a single material platform. Emerging strategies such as molecular imprinting, dual‐responsive chromophores, and stable synthetic dyes can further enhance the sensitivity and specificity of colorimetric sensing toward multiple spoilage markers, enabling more accurate freshness assessment (Figure 9).

Bridging laboratory innovation to real‐world application requires rigorous prototyping under industrially relevant conditions. This includes testing MOF‐integrated films, sensor labels, and active packaging prototypes with real seafood products throughout storage, transportation, and cold‐chain distribution. Special attention should be given to microenvironmental variations within seafood packaging, including localized humidity accumulation, salt‐induced corrosion, and temperature fluctuations during cold chain interruptions. Designing MOF‐based systems that can adapt to these dynamic conditions through moisture buffering, salt‐resistant framework, and temperature‐responsive release mechanism will be essential for ensuring consistent performance under real‐world logistics scenarios. Such efforts demand close collaboration between materials chemists, food scientists, toxicologists, and industry stakeholders to co‐develop solutions that are not only scientifically advanced but also practical, safe, and economically feasible. Although intensive MOF research has been taken for more than 25 years, successful commercialization remains limited, underscoring the importance of applied research and translational frameworks, as highlighted in recent perspectives on moving MOFs from academia to market (Wright et al. 2025). While MOFs have been explored for antibiotic and antimicrobial adsorption, future work should expand toward the selective recovery of high‐value bioactives, including omega‐3 fatty acids, bioactive peptides, and antioxidants. The development of multifunctional MOF composites capable of exhibiting simultaneous antimicrobial activity and controlled bioactive release may open new paradigms in food preservation, functional foods, and nutritional enhancement.

Collectively, these research directions highlight the potential of MOF‐based smart packaging to evolve from passive preservation materials into intelligent multifunctional systems capable of sensing, protecting, and communicating food quality in real time.

Conclusion

MOFs offer promising opportunities for seafood preservation, although practical implementation remains dependent on safety, stability, scalability, and regulatory validation. Their unique properties are of benefit in active packaging, real‐time freshness monitoring, improving shelf life, and food safety. However, challenges like moisture stability, scalability, and regulatory approval must be overcome for wider adoption or further applications. Future research should focus on green synthesis, biocompatible MOFs, and hybrid composites with cost‐effective production methods, and life‐cycle assessments should be conducted via interdisciplinary collaboration between materials chemists, food scientists, toxicologists, and industry stakeholders. Their successful integration into food technologies may ultimately inaugurate a new paradigm in the application of advanced functional materials for enhancing global food quality, safety, security and human well‐being.
