A causal link between circulating immune cells and erectile dysfunction: Two-sample bidirectional Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Guanwei Wu, Jiawei Wang, Weiyu Zhu, Heqian Liu, Wei Ding, Yingqing Liu, Zhonglang Wang, Lingsong Tao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Emerging observational studies have highlighted a robust association between circulating immune cells and erectile dysfunction (ED); however, these associations may be confounded by underlying factors. To elucidate this relationship, we conducted a comprehensive two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to evaluate the potential causal links between circulating immune cell and ED. Utilizing large-scale genetic datasets from publicly available sources, we systematically investigated causal associations across 731 distinct immunophenotypes and ED risk, supported by rigorous sensitivity analyses to ensure robustness, evaluate heterogeneity, and detect horizontal pleiotropy. In forward MR analyses, 28 immune phenotypes were identified as having a causal association with ED. Notably, 3 specific phenotypes: CD25 + resting Treg cells, CD33dim HLA-DR + CD11b + antigen-presenting cells, and CD3- lymphocyte antigen-presenting cells, exhibited consistent significance across multiple MR methods, including inverse-variance weighted, MR-Egger, and weighted median analyses. Additionally, reverse MR analyses revealed that ED could causally influence 41 immune phenotypes, with 11 phenotypes showing consistent significance across the same analytical approaches. These findings underscore the complex bidirectional relationship between circulating immune cells and ED, providing critical insights into ED pathogenesis and potential targets for novel therapeutic interventions.

1. Introduction

Erectile dysfunction (ED) is a common condition among elderly men, characterized by the inability to achieve or maintain an erection sufficient for satisfactory sexual activity. This condition significantly affects the physical and mental well-being of both patients and their partners.[1] According to the Massachusetts Male Aging Study, the overall prevalence of mild to moderate ED among men aged 40 to 70 has reached 52%.[2] However, the etiology and underlying mechanisms of ED remain incompletely understood.[3] Although patients with ED can improve their symptoms with oral phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors (PDE5i), it is regrettable that approximately 35% of patients do not respond to these treatments, particularly those with diabetes-related ED.[4]

As research progresses, an increasing number of associations between the immune system and sexual dysfunction have been identified.[5–7] The immune system contributes to the development of ED through the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines induced by Toll-like receptors and immune cell infiltration, creating an inflammatory environment that leads to vascular injury and ED. Consequently, targeting the immune system may represent a promising strategy for improving ED by reducing vascular and systemic inflammation.[8] Compared to non-ED patients, individuals with ED exhibit significantly elevated levels of the systemic immune-inflammation index, which serves as an independent risk factor for ED.[9] Additionally, research has shown that PDE5i therapy not only decreases inflammatory cell infiltration and cytokine production in various tissues but also increases the levels of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, thereby enhancing immune competence.[10,11] However, given the complexity of ED pathophysiology and the heterogeneity of immune cell subtypes, the relationship between immune cell infiltration and the progression of ED remains unclear. Thus, there is a pressing need for more comprehensive and methodologically rigorous studies to elucidate these intricate associations.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is an innovative method in genetic epidemiology that uses genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to determine the causal effects of exposures on outcomes. This approach effectively minimizes the impact of confounding factors and addresses the limitations of traditional epidemiological research.[12–14] Based on an extensive literature review, we have not identified any MR studies investigating the causal effect between circulating immune cells and ED. Therefore, this study employs a bidirectional MR approach to evaluate the causal relationship between these factors.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

The bidirectional two-sample MR analysis process for immune cells and ED risk is depicted in Figure 1. In our study, single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) related to immune cells and ED were employed as IVs. The selected IVs adhere to 3 key assumptions: (1) the IVs are associated with the exposure factor; (2) the IVs are independent of any confounders that might affect the exposure–outcome relationship; (3) the IVs influence the outcome solely through the exposure factor and not via alternative pathways.[15]

Flowchart depicting the bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis.

The data used in this study were obtained from public databases, thus no additional ethical approval was required. This study is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Using Mendelian Randomization guidelines (STROBE-MR).

2.2. Data sources

The genome-wide association study (GWAS) immune cells dataset comprises 731 distinct phenotypes from a cohort of 3757 individuals of European descent from Sardinia. These phenotypes include 118 absolute cell counts (AC), 192 relative counts, 389 median fluorescence intensities of surface antigens, and 32 morphological parameters.[16] This dataset is publicly accessible through the GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/studies/) under accession numbers GCST0001391 to GCST0002121. The immune cell subpopulations are categorized into 7 groups: natural killer (NK) cells, myeloid cells, B cells, monocytes, Treg cells, cDC cells, and mature T cells.

The GWAS data for ED were obtained from the FinnGen database (https://www.finngen.fi/) using the latest R11 version. Case diagnoses were based on the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes. The study included 2548 cases and 196,451 controls (Table 1).

2.3. Selection of IVs

IVs were selected based on the following criteria: (1) SNPs associated with immune cells at a genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5 × 10-8) were selected as candidate IVs. Due to the limited number of SNPs meeting this criterion, a secondary threshold (P < 1 × 10-5) was applied to achieve more comprehensive SNP coverage. For ED IVs selection, we began with the stringent threshold of P < 5 × 10-8, but since no relevant SNPs were identified, we adjusted the threshold to P < 5 × 10-6. (2) Linkage disequilibrium checks were performed on the selected SNPs (r2 = 0.001, kb = 10,000), and palindromic SNPs were excluded. The efficacy of the remaining SNPs was assessed using the F-statistic (F = beta2/se2), with SNPs exhibiting an F-statistic < 10 deemed weak instruments and excluded.[17] Ultimately, 17,285 SNPs associated with immune phenotypes and 17 SNPs associated with ED were retained.

2.4. Statistical analysis

MR analysis was performed using the open-source statistical software R (version 4.4.1) with the “TwoSampleMR” and “MRPRESSO” packages.

The primary analysis employed the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method with random effects to assess the causal effect of immune cells on ED. MR-Egger regression and the weighted median (WM) method served as supplementary analyses.[18] Heterogeneity among instrumental variables (IVs) was evaluated using Cochran Q statistic, and if significant (P < .05), a random effects model was applied. Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using MR-Egger and MR-PRESSO, with P < .05 indicating its presence.[19] Associations between exposure and outcome were reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), with P < .05 considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. The causal link of immune cells on ED

Initially, we used immune cell SNPs from the 731 dataset as IVs to investigate their causal relationship with ED, primarily through IVW analysis. We identified 28 immune phenotypes associated with ED, with 14 serving as risk factors and 14 as protective factors, and detailed the results using heat maps (Fig. 2). Further analysis revealed that 3 immune phenotypes exhibited significant associations in 2 complementary methods (MR-Egger and WM) in addition to IVW. For instance, the OR for CD25 on resting Treg was 1.10, 95% CI = 1.03–1.19, P = 7.85 × 10-3 in IVW; MR-Egger: OR = 1.14, 95% CI = 1.03–1.27, P = 1.73 × 10-2; WM: OR = 1.13, 95% CI = 1.00–1.28, P = 4.43 × 10-2; the OR for CD33dim HLA-DR + CD11b + AC was 1.08, 95% CI = 1.01–1.15, P = 2.07 × 10-2 in IVW; MR-Egger: OR = 1.13, 95% CI = 1.01–1.24, P = 1.73 × 10-2; WM: OR = 1.11, 95% CI = 1.03–1.20, P = 8.88 × 10-3, these 2 immune phenotypes are risk factors for ED. Another immune phenotype, CD3-lymphocyte AC, was found to be a protective factor: IVW: OR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.79–0.96, P = 6.95 × 10-3; MR-Egger: OR = 0.80, 95% CI = 0.65–0.97, P = 3.70 × 10-2; WM: OR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.76–0.99, P = 3.78 × 10-2.

4. Discussion

In this study, we thoroughly investigated the causal relationships between 731 immune cell phenotypes and ED using publicly available GWAS databases. Our findings identified 28 immune cell phenotypes significantly associated with the incidence of ED. Furthermore, in the reverse MR analysis, we unexpectedly found that ED affects the expression levels of up to 41 immune cell phenotypes, an area rarely explored in previous MR studies.

We identified 7 types of immune cells variably involved in the pathogenesis of ED, with B cells, TBNK cells, and Treg cells being the most prominent. Chronic inflammation-mediated damage and dysfunction of penile vascular endothelial cells are a critical factor in the development of ED.[20] Research indicates that chronic low-grade inflammation (CLGI) conditions, such as diabetes and obesity, extensively activate B cells.[21,22] These B cells secrete various pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-6 (IL-6). In a state of chronic inflammation, these cytokines induce and exacerbate oxidative stress levels.[23] Additionally, activated B cells produce autoantibodies, such as anti-endothelial cell antibodies (AECA), which target the body’s endothelial cells, collectively worsening vascular endothelial damage and dysfunction.[24] However, the study found no B cell immune subtypes significant across all 3 tests.

NK cells are critical components of the innate immune system. In the TBNK immune cell cluster, CD3- lymphocytes, identified as NK cells by the absence of CD3 and the lack of T or B cell receptors,[25] exhibit a protective effect against ED. Although direct studies linking NK cells to ED are scarce, existing research demonstrates a significant relationship between NK cells and nitric oxide (NO) production.[26] IL-2 induces nitric oxide synthase (NOS) activity, promoting NO production and subsequently regulating NK cell activation.[27] NO is a key mediator of penile erection, increasing cGMP levels, activating protein kinase G, and reducing intracellular calcium, leading to vascular smooth muscle relaxation and erection. PDE5 reverses NO’s effects by breaking down cGMP, making PDE5i the primary treatment for ED.[28] Long-term administration of sildenafil, a widely used PDE5i for ED treatment, has been associated with alterations in CD3- NK cells in murine models.[29] Furthermore, NK cells have been shown to promote coronary artery remodeling, likely through the regulation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF),[30] which is pivotal for ameliorating vasculogenic ED.[31,32] Thus, we hypothesize that the protective effect of CD3- lymphocytes on ED may be mediated through the modulation of NO and VEGF production.

Additionally, we identified CD25 on resting Tregs in the Treg immune cell cluster and CD33dim HLA-DR + CD11b + cells in the myeloid cell cluster as risk factors for ED. CD25, also known as the IL-2 receptor alpha chain (IL-2Rα), is part of the high-affinity IL-2 receptor complex and is highly expressed on resting Tregs.[33,34] Studies have found defects in antigen-presenting cells (APCs) in nonobese diabetic (NOD) mice. APC-stimulated Tregs remain anergic and suppress the proliferation and activation of other CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, and NK cells by competitively binding IL-2. This suppressive effect may reduce immune surveillance, allowing CLGI to persist.[35,36] Similar mechanisms occur in SLE, where IL-2Rα levels may correlate with disease severity.[37] Both type 1 diabetes (T1DM) and SLE are closely associated with the development of ED.[38,39] In elderly populations and in patients with obesity and cardiovascular diseases, the number and functionality of Tregs may decline, impairing their ability to maintain immune tolerance and regulate immune responses. This decline could contribute to the higher prevalence of ED in these groups.[40] CD33dim myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are a subset of myeloid cells with significant immunosuppressive functions in various pathological conditions.[41] They are critical in tumor microenvironments, chronic inflammation, and autoimmune diseases.[42,43] HLA-DR + CD11b + cells are typically active antigen-presenting cells that can capture and process antigens, presenting antigen fragments to CD4 + T cells and thus initiating immune responses.[44] Research indicates that in the tumor microenvironment, CD33dim MDSCs and HLA-DR + CD11b + cells synergistically secrete inhibitory cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, suppressing antitumor immune responses and thereby promoting tumor growth and metastasis.[45] In atherosclerosis, these cells sustain chronic inflammation by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, exacerbating vascular inflammation and plaque formation, thereby worsening endothelial dysfunction.[46] Therefore, we propose that CD33dim HLA-DR + CD11b + cells exacerbate ED through immunosuppression and the mediation of chronic vascular endothelial damage.

Simultaneously, our observations indicate that ED is associated with changes in 41 immune phenotypes, 11 of which were confirmed through 3 independent tests. However, no significant immune phenotypes overlapped between both forward and reverse MR analyses. Of these 11 phenotypes, 9 were in a “protected state” (OR < 1), indicating low immune responsiveness, which aligns with previous research findings.[47,48] CLGI is a critical factor in the pathogenesis of ED and a potential intermediary in vascular endothelial dysfunction, particularly in metabolic diseases such as obesity, metabolic syndrome, and diabetes.[47] Persistent CLGI can lead to immune system exhaustion and decreased immune cell responsiveness. For example, CD4 + T cells may show low levels of activation markers, such as CD25 and HLA-DR[49]; Th1 and Th2 cell functions may be weakened[50]; and CD19 + B cell functionality may be reduced, leading to decreased antibody production and impaired humoral immunity.[51] This makes the body more susceptible to infections and other immune-related diseases. Therefore, we propose that in elderly populations prone to CLGI, ED may be a common manifestation of this condition. Furthermore, the severity of ED appears to be a potential predictor and measure of the severity of certain diseases, particularly cardiovascular diseases.[52]

To our knowledge, this is the first study to utilize MR methods to investigate the bidirectional causal relationship between immune cells and ED. By leveraging large-scale population data and employing various MR analytical methods, we minimized confounding factors, thereby enhancing the robustness and reliability of our findings. It is important to recognize that the pathogenesis of ED is complex and often involves multiple immune phenotypes. Therefore, comprehensive research into the interactions between innate and adaptive immune cells in ED patients is necessary. Recent literature has documented the application of immune cells in ED treatment, such as stem cells and peripheral blood mononuclear cells, demonstrating promising results.[10] These findings underscore the significant potential of immune cells in the therapeutic landscape of ED. However, our study has several limitations. Although our analysis included 731 immune cell phenotypes, some phenotypes could not be further examined due to limitations in the original data. The nature of the data also prevented us from performing stratified analyses, potentially affecting the generalizability and accuracy of the results. Additionally, due to an insufficient number of SNPs, we had to relax the threshold for selecting instrumental variables, which may influence the final outcomes. Finally, this study is limited to populations of European ancestry. While this reduces potential bias from population structure, it limits the generalizability of MR results to other populations. We hope that future GWAS databases will include larger sample sizes to further explore the relationship between immune cells and ED.

5. Conclusion

In summary, our study results revealed a complex causal interplay between circulating immune cells and ED, offering new insights for future research into ED pathogenesis and the development of novel therapeutic approaches. We anticipate that further molecular biology experiments will validate and refine these findings.
