Turbulence-Driven Corrugation of Collisionless Fast-Magnetosonic Shocks
Immanuel Christopher Jebaraj, Mikhail Malkov, Nicolas Wijsen, Jens Pomoell, Vladimir Krasnoselskikh, Nina Dresing, Rami Vainio

Abstract
Collisionless fast-magnetosonic shocks are often treated as smooth, planar boundaries, yet observations point to organized corrugation of the shock surface. A plausible driver is upstream turbulence. Broadband fluctuations arriving at the front can continually wrinkle it, changing the local shock geometry and, in turn, conditions for particle injection and radiation. We develop a linear-MHD formulation that treats the shock as a moving interface rather than a fixed boundary. In this approach the shock response can be summarized by an effective impedance determined by the Rankine–Hugoniot base state and the shock geometry, while the upstream turbulence enters only through its statistics. This provides a practical mapping from an assumed incident spectrum to the corrugation amplitude, its drift along the surface, and a coherence scale set by weak damping or leakage. The response is largest when the transmitted downstream fast mode propagates nearly parallel to the shock in the shock frame, which produces a Lorentzian-type enhancement controlled by the downstream normal group speed. We examine how compression, plasma β \beta , and obliquity affect these corrugation properties and discuss implications for fine structure in heliospheric and supernova-remnant shock emission.

II.2
Interfacial impedance
\mathcal{Z}(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})
and grazing-resonance structure

We now reduce the boundary system to an effective scalar relation between the shock displacement and the imposed upstream forcing,
\mathcal{Z}(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})\,\zeta(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})=\mathcal{S}(\omega,\mathbf{k}_{\perp})
, which defines the interfacial impedance
\mathcal{Z}
and source term
\mathcal{S}
.

We define

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})

\displaystyle=\ell_{\zeta}-\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\bm{m}_{\zeta},

(44)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})

\displaystyle=f_{\zeta}-\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{d}},

so that the transfer function is

\mathcal{T}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})=\frac{\zeta}{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})}.

(45)

Equivalently,
\mathcal{Z}
admits the bordered-determinant (Kreiss–Lopatinskii–Majda) representation
Kreiss 1970
;
Majda 1983
;
Métivier 2001
:

\mathcal{Z}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})=\frac{\det\!\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}&\bm{m}_{\zeta}\\
\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}&\ell_{\zeta}\end{bmatrix}}{\det\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}}\,,

(46)

with the explicit assembly given in Appendix
D
.

We focus on grazing points of the downstream fast branch where the normal group speed in the shock frame,
v_{g,n2}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})
, vanishes. This condition defines a narrow locus in
(k_{n2},\bm{k}_{\perp})
–space which we refer to as a resonance cone and illustrate schematically in Figure
2
. At these points the transmitted fast–mode packets propagate nearly parallel to the interface: their residence time near the shock becomes large and the interfacial impedance
\mathcal{Z}
develops a pronounced minimum. This is an impedance (scattering) resonance of the boundary problem, not a free surface eigenmode.
3
3

3

\mathcal{Z}
becomes small when the admissible fast branch has
v_{g,n2}\!\to\!0
in the shock frame; the packet’s residence time at the interface diverges and the interfacial Green’s function peaks. This is a genuine resonance of the transmission problem even though no capillary/Hall–MHD–type bound surface mode is present.

The elegance of this is due to two straightforward reasons. First, for generic obliquity and parameters the fast mode is the only admissible branch whose normal group speed
v_{g,n2}
crosses zero at fixed
(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})
under the radiation condition. Second,
\zeta
couples most strongly through compressive (pressure and magnetic-pressure) operators, for which the fast mode dominates the interfacial response. Slow and Alfvén branches may contribute, but typically do not produce isolated grazing resonances except under special alignments; their effect is retained in
\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}
outside the local fast-grazing analysis.

Further details of the bordered-determinant mapping and the linearization near a fast-mode grazing configuration are summarized in Appendix
C
.

The resonance structure of
\mathcal{Z}
follows from its dependence on the downstream normal roots through
\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}
. Denote the downstream fast-branch normal group speed by

v_{g,n2}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})=\hat{\bm{n}}\!\cdot\!\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\bm{k}}\quad\text{subject to}\quad D_{2}(\omega-\bm{k}\!\cdot\!\bm{U}_{2},\bm{k})=0.

(47)

A grazing configuration occurs when

v_{g,n2}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})=0.

(48)

Differentiation with respect to the fast-mode normal root
k_{n2}
along the dispersion surface shows that
\mathcal{Z}
varies linearly with
v_{g,n2}
near grazing. Using

\frac{\partial_{k_{n2}}D_{2}}{\partial_{\omega^{\prime}}D_{2}}=U_{n2}-v_{g,n2},\qquad\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\bm{k}}=\bm{U}_{2}-\frac{\partial_{\bm{k}}D_{2}}{\partial_{\omega^{\prime}}D_{2}},

(49)

one finds the coupling coefficient

C(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})\;\equiv\;\left.\frac{\partial_{k_{n2}}\mathcal{Z}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})}{\partial_{k_{n2}}v_{g,n2}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})}\right|_{k_{n2}=k_{n2}^{(g)}},

(50)

so that, to first order,

\mathcal{Z}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})=C(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})\,v_{g,n2}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})+\mathrm{i}\,\Gamma,

(51)

where
\Gamma
is a small net regularization (imaginary part) that can represent either damping or growth:
\Gamma>0
for net damping (dissipation, radiative leakage, effective spectral broadening) and
\Gamma<0
for net amplification. In particular,
\Gamma
may include weak shock–front instabilities (e.g., D’Iakov–Kontorovich corrugation
D’Iakov 1958a
;
D’Iakov 1958b
;
Kontorovich 1958
, and kinetic rippling effects
Winske and Quest 1988
) provided their linear rates are small over the grazing bandwidth and do not alter the admissible-mode set. When such mechanisms are strong or generate additional surface modes, they modify
\mathcal{Z}
itself (through
D_{2}
and the boundary operators) rather than entering as a simple
i\Gamma
. Practical, parameterized recipes to estimate
\Gamma
from along-surface advection, finite root width, and kinetic dissipation are collected in Appendix
F
while any assessment of instabilities is deferred. In the resonance cone of Figure
2
,
v_{g,n2}\simeq 0
and the small effective regularization
\Gamma
therefore sets both the width of the peak response and the lifetime of the coherent corrugation associated with the transmitted fast branch.

The surface response is therefore Lorentzian in

,

(52)

which is phenomenologically similar to the grazing resonance in the relativistic regime obtained by
Lemoine et al. 2016
.

Because
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{Z}
are separated, the mean-square drive decomposes naturally into Alfvénic and compressive contributions,

\displaystyle\left\langle|\mathcal{S}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})|^{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}_{A}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})\,E_{A}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})

(53)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{G}_{C}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})\,S_{C}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})

\displaystyle+2\,\Re\!\Big\{\mathcal{G}_{\!AC}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})\,\Phi_{AC}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})\Big\},

where
E_{A}
is the upstream Alfvénic spectral density projected onto the interface polarization,
S_{C}
is a compressive scalar spectrum associated with pressure and magnetic-pressure couplings, and
\mathcal{G}_{A,C}
are algebraic kernels (explicit quadratic forms of
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{d}}
acted on by
\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}
); their reduction can be found in Appendix
B
.
\Phi_{AC}
is the Alfvénic–compressive cross-spectrum which we set to
0
(no cross term) when these components are uncorrelated after azimuthal averaging, which is the assumption used in the qualitative scalings obtained here. The general spectrum of corrugations at fixed
(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})
is

\left\langle|\zeta(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})|^{2}\right\rangle=\frac{\left\langle|\mathcal{S}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})|^{2}\right\rangle}{|\mathcal{Z}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})|^{2}}.

(54)

Near a fast-mode grazing point, Eq. (
54
) is therefore a Lorentzian in the grazing variable
v_{g,n2}
. Assuming the drive varies slowly across the resonance width,
\left\langle|\mathcal{S}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})|^{2}\right\rangle\approx\left\langle|\mathcal{S}(\omega_{g},\bm{k}_{\perp})|^{2}\right\rangle
, we can pull it outside the narrow resonance integral. Evaluating the resonance contribution by integrating across the grazing set using the downstream fast normal root
k_{n2}
(at fixed
\bm{k}_{\perp}
) yields the standard Lorentzian integral

\displaystyle\int dk_{n2}\,\frac{1}{\big|C(\omega_{g},\bm{k}_{\perp})\big|^{2}\,v_{g,n2}^{2}+\Gamma^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{\big|C(\omega_{g},\bm{k}_{\perp})\big|\,\Gamma\,\big|\partial_{k_{n2}}v_{g,n2}\big|_{\omega_{g}}}.

Thus

\displaystyle\int dk_{n2}\,\left\langle|\zeta(\omega_{g},\bm{k}_{\perp})|^{2}\right\rangle

(55)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\pi}{|C(\bm{k}_{\perp})|\,\Gamma}\,\frac{1}{\bigl|\partial_{k_{n2}}v_{g,n2}\bigr|_{\omega_{g}}}

\displaystyle\times\Bigl[\mathcal{G}_{A}(\bm{k}_{\perp})\,E_{A}(\bm{k}_{\perp})+\mathcal{G}_{C}(\bm{k}_{\perp})\,S_{C}(\bm{k}_{\perp})\Bigr],

where all quantities are evaluated at the local grazing point
\omega_{g}(\bm{k}_{\perp})
.

In the limit
|U_{n2}|\ll c_{f2}
, the combination of the directional measure of the grazing set and the dwell-time weighting of near-grazing fast packets yields the residence scaling
\propto c_{f2}/|U_{n2}|
(Appendix

(up to geometry), one obtains

\displaystyle\int dk_{n2}\,\left\langle|\zeta|^{2}\right\rangle(\bm{k}_{\perp})

(56)

\displaystyle\propto\ \frac{(r-1)^{2}}{\rho_{1}U_{n1}\,\Gamma}\,\frac{c_{f2}}{|U_{n2}|}\,\Big[\cos^{2}\theta_{Bn}\,E_{A}(\bm{k}_{\perp})+\sin^{2}\theta_{Bn}\,S_{C}(\bm{k}_{\perp})\Big],

and with

the prefactor reduces to

, consistent with Eq. (

(Alfvénic) and

(compressive) weights reflect that Alfvénic drive couples through

while compressive drive couples through
B_{1\perp}
.

V
Conclusions

In this study, we present a novel interfacial formulation within linear MHD that offers an observation-ready map from upstream turbulence and geometry to surface corrugation without modifying the underlying conservation laws. Several conclusions can be made as follows:

1.

Fast-magnetosonic shocks act as directional filters. Broadband upstream power focuses into transmitted fast modes that approach near-grazing, producing mesoscale corrugations that drift along the front in the shock frame at approximately
v_{\rm corug}\simeq U_{2\perp}+c_{f2}\sin\theta_{g}
, where
c_{f2}\sin\theta_{g}
is the lateral fast-mode group speed in the downstream frame.

2.

The spectrum of corrugations follows the upstream spectrum of turbulence. With weak, roughly scale–independent damping, the corrugations inherit the driver dependence. Stronger damping and along–front advection (so that
\Gamma\propto k_{\perp}
) preferentially drain smaller scales and, over the range where the effective impedance varies slowly with
k_{\perp}
, steepen the spectrum by roughly one power in
k_{\perp}
(from
-5/3
toward
\simeq-8/3
), consistent with recent turbulence diagnostics in young SNRs
Petruk and Kuzyo 2025
.

3.

The amplitude and coherence of the corrugations are set by the interface response. At fixed scales, the amplitude grows with compression and is limited by the normal inflow and weak damping. Increasing
M_{f}
strengthens the response, while very large
M_{f}
can shorten coherence unless the selection shifts to larger scales.

4.

Obliquity and the mix of driver modes set the selection. Alfvénic fluctuations at small obliquity favor multiple co-moving bands with shorter along-front coherence. Compressive fluctuations toward perpendicular geometry favor fewer directions with coarser, longer-lived patches and larger anisotropy.

5.

Coherence grows with
c_{f2}/|U_{n2}|
and with a narrower selected
k_{\perp}
band, provided that the effective damping rate
\Gamma
remains modest and does not vary too strongly across that band. Because
\beta
changes both
c_{f2}
and the jump, the trends are not monotonic. Within this weak–damping picture we expect corrugations to be long and coherent in the low–
\beta
corona and patchier near 1 AU. Coherence is shortest at high–
M_{f}
bow shocks where
\Gamma
is expected to be stronger and more scale dependent.

6.

The slopes of the corrugations also perturb the local obliquity and modulate particle injection on top of whatever baseline efficiency is set by the available seed particles and Mach-number–dependent shock microphysics. In our model, keeping this baseline fixed, the injected fraction increases with geometric steepness
k_{\perp}|\zeta|
and predominantly changes the normalization of the accelerated population rather than its spectral slope.

Across heliospheric and astrophysical settings, a shock moving through a turbulent medium organizes broadband fluctuations into coherent corrugations that slide along the surface. This process is fundamentally different from instability-driven corrugations of fast-magnetosonic shocks and is compatible with well-known results showing that collisionless fast-magnetosonic shocks are linearly stable against MHD corrugational instability
D’Iakov 1958a
;
D’Iakov 1958b
;
Kontorovich 1958
under many realistic conditions
(
Zank and McKenzie 1987
;
Blokhin and Trakhinin 1999
, e.g.)
. In this view, turbulence-driven corrugation provides a minimal, self-consistent baseline description, while more specific kinetic or geometric effects can be added as required by a given system. In multi-spacecraft observations at interplanetary shocks and at the Earth bow shock, the outcome appears as changes in the local shock normal on scales larger than ion scales, limited by the correlation length of the upstream forcing and by damping at the shock
Neugebauer and Giacalone 2005
;
Kajdič et al. 2019
.

When the upstream forcing is coherent across a sector, the same filtering at bow shocks across the heliosphere can naturally contribute to the slow “breathing” with periods of minutes and excursions of a few hundred kilometres
(
Huterer et al. 1997
;
Madanian et al. 2025
;
Cheng et al. 2025
, e.g.)
. In such cases the global geometry remains nearly fixed while the local normal tilts by only a few degrees
Horbury et al. 2002
;
Meziane et al. 2014
. Those tilts are sufficient to change
M_{n}=M\cos\theta_{Bn}
and to move patches between more parallel and more perpendicular character, producing multi-crossings with nearly steady average normals and episodic changes in reflected-ion activity and wave power. In our framework, the amplitude and persistence follow from how long disturbances reside along the surface compared with how quickly they are drained, and from how coherent the driver is. The period and apparent drift then reflect the along-front scale selected by the interface response, so breathing becomes a natural and interpretable outcome of the shock’s interaction with turbulence, even in the absence of additional instabilities.

In the low corona, similar surface corrugation can help explain rapidly evolving type II radio sources and fine structure in dynamic spectra, including multiple bright bands that move along the front
Morosan et al. 2025
;
Magdalenić et al. 2020
. Recent studies of interplanetary shocks show that synchrotron features can separate into lanes with differing apparent drifts when relativistic electrons are preferentially confined to distinct corrugations
Jebaraj et al. 2024b
;
Wilson et al. 2025
, a behaviour that is naturally accommodated within our framework. In SNR shocks, an analogous organization is consistent with elongated stripes or smaller patches whose orientation follows the direction of the corrugations, while their spacing and persistence reflect the surface coherence and the damping
Eriksen et al. 2011
;
Raymond et al. 2020
. Corrugations that tilt the local obliquity
\theta_{Bn}
create pockets of enhanced injection and modulate synchrotron brightness along the front, providing observables that could, in principle, be used together with complementary constraints and detailed forward modelling to infer the jump parameters, obliquity, and the relative Alfvénic/compressive content of the upstream turbulence.

Beyond morphology, the interfacial framework clarifies how energy is redistributed at the boundary. Near-grazing transmission redirects part of the downstream fluctuation energy into along-front propagation, yielding an anisotropic flux tied to the selected directions of the corrugations and their coherence. In practice, this acts as an effective boundary condition for turbulence and particle transport across the shock, with the degree of along-front redirection set by geometry and compression. A full redistribution budget, including any nonlinear cascade downstream, lies beyond the present linear treatment.

At shocks such as young SNRs and strong heliospheric shocks energetic particles and reflected ions can feed energy back into the shock face. In our framework the shock is a resonant filter rather than a self-excited oscillator; amplification appears only if this feedback outweighs the net damping at the surface. When it does, the preference is for longer along-front wavelengths: larger corrugations give particles time to react within a cycle, while diffusion and other smoothing processes drain the smaller corrugations. Stronger compression enhances the coupling, and oblique magnetic geometry sets both the sign and the strength of the effect. If kinetic feedback becomes strong enough to change which waves are supported at the interface, the effect lies beyond the present linear-MHD closure and should be captured by updating the interfacial impedance rather than by a simple effective damping term.

Finally, a corrugated, drifting boundary makes the effective diffusion tensor anisotropic and time-dependent even when microscopic scattering is unchanged, thereby reshaping particle transport at the shock. Along the front, long coherent stretches increase residence, steady the source, and raise escape thresholds; short, broken stretches yield bursty injection and patchy acceleration. Across the front, slow wandering of the shock normal permits occasional cross-field (and non-diffusive) steps that a fixed plane would suppress. As a result, coarse-grained mean free paths, escape probabilities, and residence times depend on the coherence length, drift speed, and damping that set the corrugation pattern—providing concrete inputs for transport models near shocks.

With this linear baseline, one can quantify how rippling modifies particle injection and transport, revisit turbulence transport closures with anisotropic and time-dependent boundary forcing, and connect emissivity maps to parameters that describe both the shock and its environment. The clearest impacts are expected in effective diffusion near shocks, in escape and residence diagnostics used in acceleration models, and in the predicted variability of radio and X-ray emission.

(75)

Appendix C
Bordered Determinant, Grazing Linearization, and Response

The impedance admits the bordered–determinant (Kreiss–Lopatinskii) form

\mathcal{Z}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})=\frac{\det\!\begin{bmatrix}\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}&\bm{m}_{\zeta}\\
\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}&\ell_{\zeta}\end{bmatrix}}{\det\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}}\,,

(79)

with downstream roots constrained by
D_{2}(\omega-\bm{k}\!\cdot\!\bm{U}_{2},\bm{k})=0
. Differentiating numerator and denominator with respect to the fast–branch normal root
k_{n2}
at fixed
(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})
, and using

\displaystyle\frac{\partial_{k_{n2}}D_{2}}{\partial_{\omega^{\prime}}D_{2}}

\displaystyle=U_{n2}-v_{g,n2}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})=U_{n2}-\hat{\bm{n}}\!\cdot\!\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\bm{k}},

(80)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=\bm{U}_{2}-\frac{\partial_{\bm{k}}D_{2}}{\partial_{\omega^{\prime}}D_{2}},

relates variations along the fast normal root to variations of the normal group speed.

To make this connection explicit, introduce a coefficient
C(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})
that converts
\partial_{k_{n2}}
into
\partial_{v_{g,n2}}
by the chain rule implied by (
80
).

Let
k_{n2}=k_{n2}^{(g)}
denote the downstream fast root at a grazing point, i.e.

v_{g,n2}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})=0
on
D_{2}(\omega-\bm{k}\!\cdot\!\bm{U}_{2},\bm{k})=0
.
A Taylor expansion in the fast normal root gives

\mathcal{Z}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})\;=\;\big(\partial_{k_{n2}}\mathcal{Z}\big)_{g}\,(k_{n2}-k_{n2}^{(g)})\;+\;\mathrm{i}\,\Gamma\;+\;\cdots,

(81)

and similarly

v_{g,n2}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})\;=\;\big(\partial_{k_{n2}}v_{g,n2}\big)_{g}\,(k_{n2}-k_{n2}^{(g)})\;+\;\cdots.

(82)

Eliminating
(k_{n2}-k_{n2}^{(g)})
between (
81
) and (
82
) yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})\;=\;C(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})\,v_{g,n2}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})\;+\;\mathrm{i}\,\Gamma,

(83)

\displaystyle C\;\equiv\;\left.\frac{\partial_{k_{n2}}\mathcal{Z}}{\partial_{k_{n2}}v_{g,n2}}\right|_{g}.

With
A\equiv\partial_{\omega^{\prime}}D_{2}
and
B\equiv\partial_{k_{n2}}D_{2}
, the normal group speed is

v_{g,n2}=U_{n2}-B/A
.
Taking the total derivative along the dispersion surface and evaluating at grazing (
v_{g,n2}=0\Rightarrow B=U_{n2}A
)
gives the compact expression

\displaystyle\left.\partial_{k_{n2}}v_{g,n2}\right|_{g}=

(84)

\displaystyle-\left.\frac{\partial_{k_{n2}k_{n2}}D_{2}-2U_{n2}\,\partial_{k_{n2}\omega^{\prime}}D_{2}+U_{n2}^{2}\,\partial_{\omega^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}}D_{2}}{\partial_{\omega^{\prime}}D_{2}}\right|_{g}.

Compute
\partial_{k_{n2}}\mathcal{Z}
from the
k_{n2}
–dependence of the fast-mode column in
\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}

(as in (
87
)), and divide by (
84
):

C(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})=\left.\frac{\partial_{k_{n2}}\mathcal{Z}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})}{\partial_{k_{n2}}v_{g,n2}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})}\right|_{k_{n2}=k_{n2}^{(g)}}.

(85)

An equivalent and directly computable representation follows by differentiating

\mathcal{Z}=\ell_{\zeta}-\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\bm{m}_{\zeta}
at fixed
(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})
.
The
k_{n2}
–dependence of
\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}
enters through the downstream eigenmode columns; denote these by
\bm{c}_{j}
and let
F
be the transmitted fast–mode column. Since
\bm{m}_{\zeta}
and
\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{d}}
do not depend on
k_{n2}
, differentiating at fixed
(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})
gives

\frac{\partial\mathcal{Z}}{\partial k_{n2}}=\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}\,\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\,\big(\partial_{k_{n2}}\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}\big)\,\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\,\bm{m}_{\zeta}.

(86)

If only the transmitted fast–mode column
F
depends on
k_{n2}
at the evaluation point, then
\partial_{k_{n2}}\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}=(\partial_{k_{n2}}\bm{c}_{F})\,e_{F}^{\top}
, so

\frac{\partial\mathcal{Z}}{\partial k_{n2}}=\Big(\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}\,\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\,\partial_{k_{n2}}\bm{c}_{F}\Big)\Big(e_{F}^{\top}\,\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\,\bm{m}_{\zeta}\Big).

(87)

Therefore the grazing linearization coefficient is

C(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})=\left.\frac{\Big(\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{d}}^{\top}\,\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\,\partial_{k_{n2}}\bm{c}_{F}\Big)\Big(e_{F}^{\top}\,\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\,\bm{m}_{\zeta}\Big)}{\partial_{k_{n2}}v_{g,n2}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})}\right|_{k_{n2}=k_{n2}^{(g)}},

(88)

with
\partial_{k_{n2}}v_{g,n2}
evaluated on the downstream fast branch (e.g., by the analytic expression in Eq. (
84
) or by a centered difference taken along the fast normal root at fixed
(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})
).
At grazing, Eq. (
84
) may be inserted directly into the denominator of (
88
).

The derivatives
\partial_{k_{n2}}\bm{c}_{F}
are taken at fixed
(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})
along the downstream fast branch and can be formed analytically from the fast–mode polarization at the interface or numerically via a small centered difference in
k_{n2}
. The vectors
\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\bm{m}_{\zeta}
and
\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}^{-1}\partial_{k_{n2}}\bm{c}_{F}
follow from two linear solves with the same
\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}

\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}
and in its column derivative. If two transmitted modes approach grazing simultaneously,
\partial_{k_{n2}}\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}
must be restricted to the subspace they span and

replaced by the corresponding dual basis.

Therefore, to first order near grazing,

\mathcal{Z}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})=C(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})\,v_{g,n2}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})+\mathrm{i}\,\Gamma,

(89)

and the Lorentzian response

\displaystyle|\mathcal{T}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})|^{2}=\frac{1}{\,|C(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})|^{2}\,v_{g,n2}^{2}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})+\Gamma^{2}\,},\qquad

(90)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}},

follows. For dimensional consistency with

, write
\Gamma=|C|\,v_{\nu}
, where
v_{\nu}
is a physically motivated speed (e.g., along–front advection, leakage, or kinetic damping; Appendix
F
). Then
|\mathcal{T}|^{2}=\big(|C|^{2}[v_{g,n2}^{2}+v_{\nu}^{2}]\big)^{-1}
.

Appendix E
Normalization of Downstream Eigenmodes

For assembling
\mathsf{M}_{\mathrm{dd}}
it is convenient to set
(\delta U_{n2})^{(j)}=1
for each admissible mode
\psi_{2}^{(j)}
. A numerically robust alternative is to impose unit outgoing normal wave–action flux. On the dispersion surface
D_{2}(\omega^{\prime},\bm{k})=0
, the wave–action density and flux scale as

\mathcal{A}_{2}^{(j)}\propto\partial_{\omega^{\prime}}D_{2}\,\|\psi_{2}^{(j)}\|^{2},\qquad\bm{\mathcal{F}}_{2}^{(j)}=\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial\bm{k}}\,\mathcal{A}_{2}^{(j)}.

(111)

Imposing unit outgoing normal flux gives

\hat{\bm{n}}\!\cdot\!\bm{\mathcal{F}}_{2}^{(j)}=1\ \implies\ \big|v_{g,n2}^{(j)}\,\partial_{\omega^{\prime}}D_{2}\big|\,\|\psi_{2}^{(j)}\|^{2}=1,

(112)

with the overall sign absorbed into the mode phase. Consequently,

\|\psi_{2}^{(j)}\|\;\sim\;\big(|v_{g,n2}^{(j)}|\,|\partial_{\omega^{\prime}}D_{2}|\big)^{-1/2},

(113)

so the amplitude grows like
|v_{g,n2}|^{-1/2}
as
v_{g,n2}\!\to 0
. For numerical conditioning it is convenient to rescale each modal column by

\sigma_{j}=\sqrt{|v_{g,n2}^{(j)}|\,|\partial_{\omega^{\prime}}D_{2}|}\,,\qquad\tilde{\psi}_{2}^{(j)}=\sigma_{j}\,\psi_{2}^{(j)},

which leaves the Schur complement and bordered determinant invariant but keeps column magnitudes
\mathcal{O}(1)
near grazing. The grazing resonance therefore appears through
\mathcal{Z}
(the bordered determinant) rather than through an arbitrary normalization factor, as standard in wave–action theory
(
Bretherton and Garrett 1968
;
Whitham 1974
, e.g.)
.

Regarding the invarinace of the bordered determinant, when evaluating
\partial_{k_{n2}}\bm{c}_{F}
in the grazing linearization, the eigenmode normalization is held fixed with respect to
k_{n2}
. Equivalently, if a
k_{n2}
–dependent rescaling
\tilde{\bm{c}}_{F}=\sigma_{F}\,\bm{c}_{F}
is used for numerical conditioning, then
\partial_{k_{n2}}\tilde{\bm{c}}_{F}
is taken as
\sigma_{F}\,\partial_{k_{n2}}\bm{c}_{F}
with
\sigma_{F}
frozen at the evaluation point (no
\partial_{k_{n2}}\sigma_{F}
term). This preserves the column-rescaling invariance of the linearized coefficient
C
.

Appendix F
Effective Regularization
\Gamma

The small imaginary part in
\mathcal{Z}=C\,v_{g,n2}+\mathrm{i}\,\Gamma

,

\Gamma=|C(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})|\,v_{\nu}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp}).

(114)

We take

with a composite baseline damping rate

\nu=\nu_{\rm tan\!:\!adv}+\nu_{\rm bulk\penalty\ leak}+\nu_{\rm kin},

(115)

which collects sinks only; particle or reflected–ion feedback that can reduce or reverse the net damping is incorporated in the main text via impedance correction terms (e.g.,

Along–surface advection over an along–front coherence length

(116)

where
\theta_{g}

v_{g,n2}=0
and the last estimate is an order–of–magnitude fast–branch relation at grazing. Bulk leakage from sampling a finite normal–root width
\Delta k_{n2}
yields the rate

\nu_{\rm bulk\!:\!leak}\ \sim\ \bigg|\frac{\partial^{2}\omega}{\partial k_{n2}^{2}}\bigg|\,\Delta k_{n2}\,\frac{1}{\ell_{n}},

(117)

where
\ell_{n}
is an effective normal coherence length. Collisionless and non-ideal dissipation contribute

\displaystyle\nu_{\rm kin}

\displaystyle\sim\ \nu_{\rm fast}(\beta,\theta_{kB},k\rho_{s})\

(118)

\displaystyle+\ \nu_{\rm slow}(\beta,\theta_{kB},k\rho_{s})\

\displaystyle+\ \nu_{\rm Hall/FLR}(k),

with rates supplied by the chosen closure
(
Melrose 1986
;
Kulsrud 2005

.

At
1

\sim 3\times 10^{6}
km (range

Neugebauer and Giacalone 2005

\nu_{\rm tan\!:\!adv}\simeq\frac{v_{g,\perp,2}}{L_{\parallel}}\approx 1\times 10^{-5}\text{ to }1\times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}.

In nondimensional variables with speed scaled by
U_{n1}
and length by
L_{0}

\Gamma=|C|\,\nu_{\ast},\qquad\nu_{\ast}=\frac{L_{0}}{U_{n1}}\,\nu\ \approx\ \frac{v_{g,\perp,2}}{U_{n1}}\frac{L_{0}}{L_{\parallel}}.

For

Appendix G
Injected Turbulence and Polarization Content

The upstream driver is a statistically stationary, mean-zero field convected by
\bm{U}_{1}
with
\omega=\bm{k}\!\cdot\!\bm{U}_{1}
. One-dimensional perpendicular spectra over two decades are taken as

\displaystyle E_{A}(k_{\perp})=C_{A}\,k_{\perp}^{-5/3},\qquad

(120)

\displaystyle S_{C}(k_{\perp})=C_{C}\,k_{\perp}^{-5/3},\qquad k_{\perp,\min}\leq k_{\perp}\leq k_{\perp,\max},

normalized to achieve target rms amplitudes and the compressive fraction
\chi_{C}=S_{C}^{\rm tot}/(E_{A}^{\rm tot}+S_{C}^{\rm tot})
.

Power in
(k_{\perp},k_{\parallel})
is distributed with respect to the upstream field
\bm{B}_{1}
by a critically balanced envelope with lognormal scatter,

\mathcal{G}(k_{\parallel};k_{\perp})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\,\exp\!\left[-\frac{\big(\ln[k_{\parallel}/k_{\rm cb}(k_{\perp})]\big)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]\,\frac{1}{k_{\parallel}},

(121)

k_{\rm cb}(k_{\perp})=C_{\rm cb}\,k_{\perp}^{2/3},

To define spectra on
k_{\parallel}\in(-\infty,\infty)
we use the even extension

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\rm even}(k_{\parallel};k_{\perp})=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{G}(|k_{\parallel}|;k_{\perp}),

(122)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{\rm even}(k_{\parallel};k_{\perp})\,dk_{\parallel}=1.

so that
\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{G}(k_{\parallel};k_{\perp})\,dk_{\parallel}=1
. This yields the 3-D PSDs

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{A}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{E_{A}(k_{\perp})}{2\pi k_{\perp}}\,\mathcal{G}_{\rm even}(k_{\parallel};k_{\perp}),

(123)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{C}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{C}(k_{\perp})}{2\pi k_{\perp}}\,\mathcal{G}_{\rm even}(k_{\parallel};k_{\perp}).

which satisfy
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!d\phi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!dk_{\parallel}\,\mathcal{P}_{A,C}\,k_{\perp}=E_{A,C}(k_{\perp})
.

Alfvénic polarization is
\delta\bm{U}_{1}^{(A)}(\bm{k})=\delta U_{1}^{(A)}(\bm{k})\,\hat{\bm{e}}_{A}
with
\hat{\bm{e}}_{A}=(\bm{k}\times\bm{B}_{1})/|\bm{k}\times\bm{B}_{1}|
and

\delta\bm{B}_{1}^{(A)}=-\,\sigma\,\sqrt{\rho_{1}}\,\delta\bm{U}_{1}^{(A)},

(124)

consistent with the linear induction relation (with
\sigma=\pm 1
the Alfvén-branch sign). The set where
\bm{k}\times\bm{B}_{1}=\bm{0}
has zero measure in the axisymmetric construction. Compressive polarization follows Appendix
A
, with
\delta\bm{U}_{1}^{(C)}=\alpha\,\hat{\bm{k}}+\eta\,\hat{\bm{\xi}}
and
\eta/\alpha
given by Eq. (
74
); the linear relations (
28
)–(
31
) then determine
\delta\rho_{1}^{(C)}
,
\delta p_{1}^{(C)}
, and
\delta\bm{B}_{1}^{(C)}
used in the forcing vectors.

Since

Appendix H
Directional Measure and Residence Time at Grazing

For the downstream fast branch with
\omega=\omega^{\prime}_{2}+\bm{k}\!\cdot\!\bm{U}_{2}
and
\omega^{\prime}_{2}\simeq\pm c_{f2}\,k
, the group velocity is
\bm{v}_{g,2}\simeq\pm c_{f2}\,\hat{\bm{k}}+\bm{U}_{2}
. Let
\theta
be the angle between
\hat{\bm{k}}
and
\hat{\bm{n}}
and
\theta_{g}
the solution of

\displaystyle v_{g,n2}(\theta_{g})=\hat{\bm{n}}\!\cdot\!\bm{v}_{g,2}\;=\;U_{n2}\pm c_{f2}\cos\theta_{g}\;=\;0

(125)

\displaystyle\ \Rightarrow\ \cos\theta_{g}=\mp\,\frac{U_{n2}}{c_{f2}},\qquad\sin\theta_{g}=\sqrt{1-\frac{U_{n2}^{2}}{c_{f2}^{2}}}.

At fixed
k
and for an isotropic distribution of directions, the purely geometric directional measure of the grazing set follows from

\displaystyle\int\delta\!\big(v_{g,n2}(\theta)\big)\,d\Omega=

(126)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin(\theta)d\theta\,\delta\left(U_{n2}\pm c_{f2}\cos\theta\right)=\frac{2\pi}{c_{f2}}.

Thus, at fixed
k
, the geometric factor contributes
\propto c_{f2}^{-1}
, consistent with the discussion following Eq. (
57
).

Complementarily, the residence-time weighting for a packet of normal extent
\ell_{n}
convected by
U_{n2}
near the interface is

\tau_{\rm dwell}\sim\frac{\ell_{n}}{|U_{n2}|},\qquad\mathcal{R}\ \equiv\ \frac{v_{g,\perp,2}}{|U_{n2}|}\ \simeq\ \frac{c_{f2}\,\sin\theta_{g}}{|U_{n2}|}.

(127)

When statistics weight along–surface exposure,
\mathcal{R}
multiplies the angular factor; in the limit
|U_{n2}|\ll c_{f2}
one has
\sin\theta_{g}\to 1
and
\mathcal{R}\propto c_{f2}/|U_{n2}|
, consistent with Eq. (
57
).

The angular
\delta
–measure above should be understood as the narrow–resonance limit; in practice, the finite regularization
\Gamma=|C|\,v_{\nu}
(Appendix
F
) broadens this selection in
\theta
with local width
\Delta\theta\sim v_{\nu}/(c_{f2}\,\sin\theta_{g})
, as captured by the Lorentzian response in Eq. (
52
).

Appendix I
Reaction-Diffusion Model, Planar DSA Normalization, and
\mathcal{T}_{\rm cr}

We collect the reaction–diffusion closure that leads to Eq. (
65
) and its normalization by the planar diffusive–shock–acceleration (DSA) time. The energetic (cosmic–ray) pressure (or normal–stress) perturbation on the surface, denoted
\delta P_{\rm cr}(\bm{x}_{\perp},t)
, is modeled as a linear, along–surface reaction–diffusion field driven by corrugation–induced injection modulations:

\partial_{t}\delta P_{\rm cr}=-\,\frac{1}{t_{\rm acc}}\,\delta P_{\rm cr}\;+\;\mathcal{K}\,\nabla_{\!\perp}^{2}\delta P_{\rm cr}\;+\;\mathcal{A}_{\rm cr}\,\delta\Psi,

(128)

where
\mathcal{K}
is an effective tangential (along-front) diffusivity,
t_{\rm acc}
is a single acceleration/relaxation time, and
\mathcal{A}_{\rm cr}
sets the normalization from injected fraction to normal stress.

With the plane–wave convention
\exp\{\mathrm{i}(\bm{k}\!\cdot\!\bm{x}-\omega t)\}
, one has
\partial_{t}\mapsto-\mathrm{i}\omega
and
\nabla_{\!\perp}^{2}\mapsto-k_{\perp}^{2}
, so the Fourier transform of (
128
) gives

\big(-\mathrm{i}\omega+t_{\rm acc}^{-1}+\mathcal{K}k_{\perp}^{2}\big)\,\delta P_{\rm cr}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})=\mathcal{A}_{\rm cr}\,\delta\Psi(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp}).

(129)

The source
\delta\Psi
is set by the corrugation–induced obliquity modulation from Eqs. (
61
)–(
63
). Using

\delta\Psi_{\bm{k}_{\perp}}=\mathrm{i}\,\Psi^{\prime}(\theta_{Bn})\,\frac{\bm{B}_{1\perp}\!\cdot\!\bm{k}_{\perp}}{|\bm{B}_{1}|\,\sin\theta_{Bn}}\,\zeta_{\bm{k}_{\perp}},

(130)

we collect constants into

\Lambda_{\rm cr}(\bm{k}_{\perp})=\mathrm{i}\,\mathcal{A}_{\rm cr}\,\Psi^{\prime}(\theta_{Bn})\,\frac{\bm{B}_{1\perp}\!\cdot\!\bm{k}_{\perp}}{|\bm{B}_{1}|\,\sin\theta_{Bn}},

(131)

so that
\mathcal{A}_{\rm cr}\,\delta\Psi=\Lambda_{\rm cr}\,\zeta
. Solving (
129
) for
\delta P_{\rm cr}
and factoring
t_{\rm acc}
yields

\delta P_{\rm cr}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})=\frac{t_{\rm acc}}{\,1-\mathrm{i}\omega t_{\rm acc}+\mathcal{K}t_{\rm acc}k_{\perp}^{2}\,}\;\Lambda_{\rm cr}(\bm{k}_{\perp})\,\zeta(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp}),

(132)

which identifies the transfer function (Eq. (
65
)),

\mathcal{T}_{\rm cr}(\omega,\bm{k}_{\perp})=\frac{t_{\rm acc}}{\,1-\mathrm{i}\omega t_{\rm acc}+\mathcal{K}t_{\rm acc}k_{\perp}^{2}\,}.

(133)

The remaining ingredient is
t_{\rm acc}
. In the test–particle, planar DSA limit the acceleration time is given by
Drury 1983

t_{\rm acc}=\frac{3}{U_{n1}-U_{n2}}\left(\frac{\kappa_{1}}{U_{n1}}+\frac{\kappa_{2}}{U_{n2}}\right),

(134)

with
U_{n1}
and
U_{n2}
the upstream and downstream normal speeds in the shock frame and
\kappa_{1,2}
the corresponding diffusion coefficients. Using
U_{n2}=U_{n1}/r
and, for simplicity,
\kappa_{1}=\kappa_{2}=\kappa
,

\displaystyle t_{\rm acc}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{U_{n1}-U_{n1}/r}\left(\frac{\kappa}{U_{n1}}+\frac{\kappa}{U_{n1}/r}\right)

(135)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{U_{n1}(1-1/r)}\left(\frac{\kappa}{U_{n1}}+\frac{\kappa r}{U_{n1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{U_{n1}}\frac{r}{r-1}\,\frac{\kappa(r+1)}{U_{n1}}=\frac{\kappa}{U_{n1}^{2}}\,\frac{3r(r+1)}{r-1}

\displaystyle;\equiv\;\chi_{\rm acc}\,\frac{\kappa}{U_{n1}^{2}},

with
\chi_{\rm acc}=3r(r+1)/(r-1)
. If
\kappa_{1}\neq\kappa_{2}
, (
134
) should be retained; the subsequent algebra is unchanged.

To connect with the scalar interface equation, recall
\mathcal{Z}\,\zeta=\mathcal{S}
from Eq. (
44
). The energetic component contributes an additional compressive drive
S_{\rm cr}=\Lambda_{\rm cr}\,\mathcal{T}_{\rm cr}\,\zeta
on the right–hand side. Moving this term to the left renormalizes the impedance,

\big(\mathcal{Z}-\Lambda_{\rm cr}\,\mathcal{T}_{\rm cr}\big)\,\zeta=\mathcal{S},

(136)

and, near grazing where
\mathcal{Z}=C\,v_{g,n2}+\mathrm{i}\Gamma
[Eq. (
51
)], this reproduces Eq. (
67
). Evaluating
\mathcal{T}_{\rm cr}
on the drift of corrugations
\omega\simeq k_{\perp}v_{\rm corug}
then gives the along–front hot–spot scaling quoted in Eq. (
68
).
