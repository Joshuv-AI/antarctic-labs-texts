The Value of the High-frequency Ultrasound System in Lacrimal Gland Prolapse Treatment
Authors: Guang Zhang, Yuzhi Qiu, Tianyi Gu, Yuna Luo, Mengle Yan, Yongqian Wang, Hongli Chai, Jianjian Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of high-frequency ultrasound in the preoperative diagnosis of lacrimal gland prolapse to aid surgical planning, minimize complications, and enhance patient satisfaction.Methods:Five patients underwent preoperative assessment using high-frequency ultrasound. The diagnostic accuracy of the ultrasound examination was subsequently assessed in relation to intraoperative findings concerning lacrimal gland prolapse. Additionally, physical examination was used to assess the extent of swelling in the outer upper eyelid.Results:Five female patients (10 eyes), aged 25–40 (average age 33.2) years, underwent upper eyelid blepharoplasty at the Plastic Surgery Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, between October 2023 and March 2024. Preoperative high-frequency ultrasound identified lacrimal gland prolapse in 8 eyes, whereas intraoperative findings confirmed prolapse in 6 eyes. The ultrasound had an 80% (8 of 10) accuracy rate, with 2 eyes misdiagnosed. Lacrimal gland prolapse was identified in 6 eyes, whereas 4 eyes showed no prolapse based on physical examination. Preoperative diagnosis relying solely on physical examination led to 2 missed and 2 incorrect prolapse diagnoses, achieving a 60% accuracy rate (6 of 10).Conclusions:The high-frequency ultrasound system has high accuracy in the preoperative diagnosis of lacrimal gland prolapse, and it has good application value in the correct diagnosis and treatment of lacrimal gland prolapse.

Takeaways

Question: What is a method for diagnosing lacrimal gland prolapse more accurately before operation?

Findings: The high-frequency ultrasound system has high accuracy in the preoperative diagnosis of lacrimal gland prolapse.

Meaning: This study demonstrated that the high-frequency ultrasound system exhibits a high degree of accuracy in the preoperative diagnosis of lacrimal gland prolapse.

INTRODUCTION

Lacrimal gland prolapse means that the lacrimal gland is separated from the lacrimal gland fossa, which presents as a swollen appearance on the lateral side of the upper eyelid. Upper eyelid swelling is one of the characteristic manifestations in Asians.1 It is generally believed that upper eyelid swelling in Asians is mainly due to a large amount of retroseptal fat.2,3 Although lacrimal gland prolapse is also one of the causes of upper eyelid swelling, and its incidence can reach 10%–15% in upper eyelid surgery, it is often ignored in Asian upper eyelid surgery.3,4 To improve the swollen appearance of the upper eyelid, surgeons often open the orbital septum and remove part of the fat. However, for some patients, local swelling can still be observed even after fat removal due to the failure to reset the prolapsed lacrimal gland. If the prolapsed lacrimal gland is mistakenly removed as orbital septal fat, adverse events such as dry eye, corneal injury, and lacrimal fistula may occur.5,6 About 2.65% of patients will experience dry eye syndrome after surgery, whereas corneal injury and lacrimal fistula very rarely occur, mostly caused by improper operation.7,8 Therefore, the correct preoperative diagnosis of lacrimal gland prolapse is very important, which is beneficial for reducing the surgical complications and achieving satisfactory postoperative results. Once lacrimal gland prolapse is discovered, surgical techniques such as dacryoadenopexy, modified dacryoadenopexy, and dacryoplasty have been described to reposition the prolapsed lacrimal gland.

The main approaches for diagnosing lacrimal gland prolapse include physical examination and imaging assessment. The accuracy of physical examination can be compromised by the presence of local skin and adipose tissue, whereas the diagnostic costs associated with computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging are substantial. The high-frequency ultrasound system used in this study offers several advantages, including cost-effectiveness, diagnostic convenience, and ease of clinical implementation. Because of these advantages, we began using the high-frequency ultrasound system for the preoperative diagnosis of lacrimal gland prolapse.

In this study, we used the traditional method of physical examination to assess lacrimal gland prolapse before surgical intervention. Concurrently, patients underwent upper eyelid ultrasound examinations. Intraoperative observations provided a definitive assessment of the lacrimal gland prolapse. We compared the preoperative findings from both physical and ultrasound examinations with the intraoperative observations to evaluate the accuracy of the 2 preoperative assessment methods. The purpose of this study was to confirm the reliability of preoperative ultrasound detection of lacrimal gland prolapse by comparing it with physical examination.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Patients

All patients visited our outpatient clinic to improve the appearance of their upper eyelids. These patients had no history of upper eyelid surgery or lacrimal gland-related diseases. The doctor confirmed that all patients had surgical indications, and they consented to undergo upper eyelid blepharoplasty. Patients were informed of the potential risks associated with the exploration of lacrimal gland prolapse, and if lacrimal gland prolapse was confirmed during the operation, the lacrimal gland prolapse reset operation was planned. Informed consent forms were duly signed by all patients.

Preoperative Examination

A plastic surgeon scored the degree of swelling in the upper eyelids of 5 patients (10 eyes) according to physical examination (very depressed to very swollen: 1–5 points; 1 point means very depressed, 2 points means relatively depressed, 3 points means average, 4 points means relatively swollen, and 5 points means very swollen). A score of 4 or higher indicated significant swelling in the upper eyelid, warranting consideration of potential lacrimal gland prolapse.

All patients agreed and received preoperative bilateral upper eyelid high-frequency ultrasound examination (equipment: GE LOGIQ E9; high-frequency probe frequency: 15 MHz). Patients were examined with their eyes closed in a supine position. First, the doctor placed the high-frequency probe horizontally for cross-sectional examination, slowly moving it from the outer superior orbital margin toward the eyelid margin. When a distinct hypoechoic region, characterized by uniform echogenicity, is observed between the upper eyelid fat and the eyeball, and color Doppler flow imaging reveals abundant blood flow signals within the structure, the possibility of lacrimal gland prolapse should be considered (Fig. 1A, B). Following the transverse examination, the probe should be positioned vertically for a longitudinal assessment, gradually moving from the outer canthus to the inner canthus. If the glandular echo area identified during the cross-sectional examination remains visible and exhibits an outward and upward extension beneath the orbital bone, with the interruption of the glandular echo upon reaching the orbital margin, an ultrasonic diagnosis of lacrimal gland prolapse can be established. During the longitudinal examination, the distance from the most distal point of the glandular echo to the orbital margin is measured. The severity of the lacrimal gland prolapse is then determined based on this distance, classified as mild (<4 mm), moderate (4–7 mm), or severe (≥7 mm)4 (Fig. 1C).

Operative Manipulation

All patients underwent double eyelid blepharoplasty. During the procedure, the lateral orbital septum of the upper eyelid was meticulously incised, and hemostasis was achieved using bipolar electrocoagulation. Following the incision of the orbital septum, careful blunt dissection was performed, with particular attention given to distinguishing between the orbital septum fat and the lacrimal gland. The orbital adipose tissue appears as yellow, clustered, and soft in texture. In contrast, the lacrimal gland exhibits a pink-white coloration, is lobulated, and possesses a firm texture. Upon identification of the lacrimal gland during exploration, it is imperative to proceed with its dissection toward the superior outer margin of the orbital bone. Intraoperative photography and detailed records were taken (Fig. 2A). If the diagnosis of lacrimal gland prolapse is confirmed, a 5-0 monofilament nylon suture is used to anchor the capsule of the lacrimal gland to the lacrimal fossa, and the prolapsed lacrimal gland is suspended and repositioned. During reposition, it is crucial to avoid the lacrimal vessels and lacrimal ducts, and to perform full-thickness suturing and suspension (Fig. 2B).

RESULTS

Five patients (10 eyes) were finally included in this study. All patients were female, ranging in age from 25 to 40 years, with a mean age of 33.2 years. Intraoperative exploration revealed that 2 patients exhibited bilateral lacrimal gland prolapse, 2 patients had unilateral lacrimal gland prolapse, and 1 patient did not present with lacrimal gland prolapse. The intraoperative conditions, preoperative ultrasound examination results, and the score of swollen upper eyelids of 5 patients (10 eyes) are summarized in Table 1. Intraoperative assessment revealed lacrimal gland prolapse in 6 eyes, whereas 4 eyes exhibited no prolapse. Preoperative ultrasound diagnosed lacrimal gland prolapse in 8 eyes, whereas 2 eyes were determined to exhibit no prolapse. Compared with intraoperative findings, the preoperative ultrasound diagnosis was incorrect in 2 eyes, where prolapse was erroneously identified, resulting in an accuracy rate of 80% (8 out of 10). Additionally, based on the degree of lateral swelling of the upper eyelid, 6 eyes were diagnosed with lacrimal gland prolapse, and 4 eyes were diagnosed without prolapse. This method, however, missed prolapse in 2 eyes and incorrectly diagnosed prolapse in 2 eyes, yielding an accuracy rate of 60% (6 out of 10). DISCUSSION

The lacrimal gland is an organ that secretes tears, which is divided into the upper orbital lobe and the lower eyelid lobe by the aponeurosis of the levator palpebrae superioris muscle.9,10 The orbital lobe, which is about twice the size of the eyelid lobe, is the main structure of the lacrimal gland.11 Lacrimal gland prolapse generally refers to the prolapse of the orbital lobe, which becomes separated from the lacrimal gland fossa located at the anterolateral side of the orbital apex, and can lead to local swelling at the lateral side of the upper eyelid.12

The upper eyelids of Asian people often show a swollen appearance, which may be perceived as conveying a sense of listlessness.13,14 To enhance the aesthetic appeal of the eyes, “volume reduction” of the upper eyelid has become a common practice among surgeons performing upper eyelid surgery.15 Removing an appropriate amount of orbital septal fat, and even the posterior orbicularis oculi fat and orbicularis oculi muscle, can significantly improve the aesthetic appearance of swollen upper eyelids. However, for patients with lacrimal gland prolapse, if lacrimal gland reset is not performed, local swelling on the lateral side of the upper eyelid may still be observed after volume reduction surgery. If adequate attention is not given to the prolapse of the lacrimal gland during the operation, and the prolapsed lacrimal gland is mistakenly removed as fat, serious complications such as dry eye, corneal injury, and lacrimal gland fistula may occur.16 Lacrimal gland prolapse is common in upper eyelid surgery, and its incidence is reported to be 10%–15%.17,18 Massry19 showed that the incidence of lacrimal gland prolapse can be as high as 60% in older patients. The accurate preoperative diagnosis of lacrimal gland prolapse is of considerable importance, as it enables the surgeon to enhance the surgical plan and ensure thorough preoperative preparation. Additionally, it contributes to a reduction in the incidence of surgical complications, ultimately leading to satisfactory postoperative outcomes for patients.

A swollen upper eyelid is a common clinical manifestation of lacrimal gland prolapse.20 However, the diagnosis, based solely on the degree of swelling, is influenced by numerous factors, including the accumulation of orbital septal fat, redundancy of the upper eyelid skin, and lateral eyebrow ptosis. Kashkouli et al4 showed that the positive predictive value of a swollen upper eyelid for lacrimal gland prolapse was only 30.6%. Our study also confirmed that the accuracy of preoperative diagnosis based solely on the appearance of the upper eyelid is not high (60%), and it is easy to misdiagnose. In our study, 1 patient with lateral swelling of the upper eyelid scored 3 points (average), but bilateral lacrimal gland prolapse was confirmed during the operation.

To improve the accuracy of preoperative diagnosis, some scholars have proposed the supine position test.4,21 Although it has been reported that this method may enhance the accuracy of physical examinations, it remains susceptible to local soft tissue variations and is unable to accurately assess the extent of lacrimal gland prolapse. Some scholars have proposed the use of computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging for preoperative diagnosis of lacrimal gland prolapse,22 but the diagnosis costs are high, and the procedures are inconvenient. The high-frequency ultrasound system used in this study has low diagnostic cost and is a noninvasive examination method commonly used in clinical work. The high-frequency ultrasound system is less affected by the surrounding soft tissue and has higher accuracy. No lacrimal gland prolapse was missed in this study. Only 1 patient was diagnosed with bilateral lacrimal gland prolapse by the ultrasound system, but no lacrimal gland prolapse was found on either side during intraoperative exploration, resulting in a diagnostic accuracy of 80%. During the ultrasound examination, we used cross-sectional and longitudinal double-section scanning techniques to diagnose lacrimal gland prolapse and minimize the risk of missed diagnoses. Furthermore, the extent of lacrimal gland prolapse can be assessed through longitudinal examination. An additional significant function of preoperative ultrasound examination is its ability to preliminarily screen for other lacrimal gland pathologies, thereby contributing to the optimization of the clinical treatment scheme.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size is relatively small, necessitating further multicenter studies with larger cohorts to validate the efficacy of high-frequency ultrasound systems in the preoperative diagnosis of lacrimal gland prolapse. Second, incorporating additional diagnostic methods such as the supine position test, palpation of the upper eyelid, and other physical examinations into the accuracy evaluation framework for lacrimal gland prolapse assessment would facilitate a more objective appraisal of the clinical utility of high-frequency ultrasound.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrated that the high-frequency ultrasound system is a useful tool in the preoperative diagnosis of lacrimal gland prolapse. Consequently, it holds significant value in the diagnosis and management of lacrimal gland prolapse and merits further clinical promotion.
