On the Existence of the KMS Spectral Gap in Gaussian Quantum Markov Semigroups
Zheng Li

Abstract
In [ 15 ] , it was shown that the GNS spectral gap of a Gaussian quantum Markovian generator is strictly positive if and only if there exists a maximal number of linearly independent noise operators, under the assumption that the generated semigroup admits a unique faithful normal invariant state. In this paper, we provide a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of the KMS spectral gap, which also depends only on the noise operators of the generator. We further show that the existence of the GNS spectral gap implies the existence of the KMS spectral gap.



Keywords:
open quantum systems; Gaussian quantum Markov semigroups;

GKSL generators; KMS inner product; spectral gap.

1
Introduction

Quantum Markov semigroups (QMSs) are fundamental mathematical models for describing the time evolution of open quantum systems, where a system interacts with an external environment in a memoryless (Markovian) way. Mathematically, a QMS is a one-parameter semigroup of completely positive, identity-preserving maps acting on a von Neumann algebra, interpreted as the algebra of observables
[
19
,
23
]
. This framework provides a natural setting for the study of quantum dissipative dynamics.

Among these, Gaussian quantum Markov semigroups are those whose predual semigroups, consisting of completely positive trace-preserving operators (that is, quantum channels), map Gaussian states to Gaussian states
[
9
,
20
,
27
]
. They can be viewed as a quantum analogue of classical Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroups, since a classical Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup arises as the restriction of a Gaussian QMS without a Hamiltonian part to the algebra generated by the position operator
[
15
, Section 3]
. Recent research
[
1
,
14
]
shows that, despite several fundamental differences between the classical and quantum settings, namely, the enlargement to a noncommutative algebra, the presence of a Hamiltonian term, and the introduction of a symplectic structure, Gaussian QMSs share many similarities with classical Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroups. In particular, the dynamics of a Gaussian QMS is described by its drift and diffusion matrices, as in the classical case, so that its analysis reduces to linear algebraic problems and systems of differential equations involving these matrices. Since the dynamics of the semigroup is closely related to the spectrum of its generator, the spectral properties of the generator are likewise strongly linked to the drift and diffusion matrices. Consequently, in the Gaussian setting, many problems can be reduced to finite-dimensional ones, which makes Gaussian QMSs mathematically transparent and suitable for efficient analysis.

From a physical perspective, Gaussian states and Gaussian dynamics occupy a fundamental place in quantum theory, as they form one of the most accessible and experimentally relevant classes of quantum systems. They admit a complete description in terms of first and second moments which makes them particularly well suited for experimental implementation. Gaussian states have attracted sustained attention since the 1960s (see
[
21
]
and references therein), and they have since become indispensable in quantum optics, where they naturally describe coherent, squeezed, and thermal states of bosonic fields. More recently, Gaussian frameworks have also emerged as fundamental tools in quantum information theory, providing tractable models for continuous-variable quantum communication, computation, and noise characterization. We refer to several monographs for comprehensive treatments of this area
[
22
,
31
,
28
]
.

In this paper, we consider a Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with a faithful normal invariant state
\rho
. The semigroup
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
acts on
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
, the algebra of all bounded linear operators on the
d
-mode boson Fock space
\mathsf{h}
, where
d\geq 1
is an integer. Its generator, denoted by
\mathcal{L}
, is of Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL) form and is given as follows
[
29
,
27
]
.

\mathcal{L}(x)=\mathrm{i}[H,x]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\left(L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}x-2L^{*}_{\ell}xL_{\ell}+xL_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}\right),\quad\forall x\in\dom\mathcal{L}\subset\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h}),

(1.1)

where
1\leq m\leq 2d
and

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\left(\Omega_{jk}a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{k}+\frac{\kappa_{jk}}{2}a^{\dagger}_{j}a^{\dagger}_{k}+\frac{\overline{\kappa_{jk}}}{2}a_{j}a_{k}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\zeta_{j}}{2}a^{\dagger}_{j}+\frac{\overline{\zeta_{j}}}{2}a_{j}\right),

(1.2)

\displaystyle L_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\overline{v_{\ell k}}a_{k}+u_{\ell k}a^{\dagger}_{k}\right),

(1.3)

where
a_{j}
and
a_{j}^{\dagger}
denote the annihilation and creation operators on the
j
-th mode, respectively. The self-adjoint operator
H
is referred to as the
Hamiltonian
of the system, and the operators
L_{\ell}
are called
Kraus operators
(also known as
noise operators
or
jump operators
in different literature). In this paper, we use the notation
[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]
for the commutator and
\{\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\}
for the anticommutator.

For notational simplicity, we introduce the following parameter matrices. Let

\Omega:=\left[\Omega_{jk}\right]_{1\leq j,k\leq d}=\Omega^{*},\quad\kappa:=\left[\kappa_{jk}\right]_{1\leq j,k\leq d}=\kappa^{T}

(1.4)

be
d\times d
complex matrices. Here,
\cdot\,^{T}
denotes the transpose and
\cdot\,^{*}
the conjugate transpose of a complex matrix. Hence,
\Omega
is Hermitian and
\kappa
is symmetric. Moreover, let

V:=\left[v_{\ell k}\right]_{1\leq\ell\leq m,1\leq k\leq d},\quad U:=\left[u_{\ell k}\right]_{1\leq\ell\leq m,1\leq k\leq d}

(1.5)

be
m\times d
complex matrices, where
m
is the number of Kraus operators. Finally, let
\zeta:=(\zeta_{j})_{1\leq j\leq d}
be a
d\times 1
complex vector. Note that the square brackets
[\,\cdot\,]
are also used to denote matrices, and this notation should not be confused with the commutator
[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]
.

Since the invariant state
\rho
is not tracial, there exist several possible constructions of an induced semigroup
(T_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, which is a strongly continuous contraction semigroup on the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators on the boson Fock space
\mathsf{h}
. Specifically, for
s\in\{0,1/2\}
, one may define

T_{t}^{(s)}(\rho^{s/2}X\rho^{(1-s)/2}):=\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{T}_{t}(X)\rho^{(1-s)/2},\quad\forall X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h}).

(1.6)

When
s=0
, the above construction corresponds to the GNS embedding
[
17
]
, while the case
s=1/2
corresponds to the KMS embedding
[
11
]
.

The choice of embedding significantly influences the properties of the induced semigroup. For instance, many induced semigroups are symmetric with respect to the KMS embedding but not under the GNS embedding. These differences give rise to distinct mathematical challenges, necessitating different analytical techniques to investigate their respective structures (see, e.g.,
[
8
,
33
,
30
]
and references therein).

Here we recall that for a maximally dissipative operator
L
, its
spectral gap
is defined as follows:

\gap L:=\inf\left\{-\real\left\langle x,Lx\right\rangle:x\in\dom L,~\norm{x}=1,~x\in(\ker L)^{\perp}\right\}.

(1.7)

We say that
L
has a spectral gap if
\gap L>0
.

Roughly speaking, the existence of a spectral gap for the induced generator
L^{(s)}
is equivalent to the exponential stability of the induced semigroup when restricted to the subspace orthogonal to the invariant vectors. Therefore, the presence of a spectral gap is closely related to the ergodic properties of the QMS, such as entropy decay and logarithmic Sobolev inequalities; see
[
7
,
6
,
4
,
5
]
.

Earlier works, for instance
[
10
]
, studied the spectral gap of certain symmetric Gaussian quantum Markovian generators on the one-mode boson Fock space. In
[
15
]
, the focus shifts to the
d
-mode setting and to QMSs that are not necessarily symmetric. There, the authors proved that, in the GNS embedding, Gaussian quantum Markovian generators possess a spectral gap if and only if there exists a maximal number of linearly independent noise operators. They also provided an example of a generator that exhibits a spectral gap in the KMS embedding but not in the GNS embedding. More recent works have further shown that the KMS spectral gap always exists for Gaussian QMSs when the boson Fock space
\mathsf{h}
is one-mode
[
18
,
14
]
.

However, as shown in this paper, a Gaussian QMS in the multi-mode case may fail to admit a KMS spectral gap; see Example
4.5
. Accordingly, we study a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of the KMS spectral gap. The main result, Theorem
4.1
, states that a KMS spectral gap exists if and only if
\ker U_{n}\cap\ker V_{n}=\{0\}
for all
1\leq n\leq l
, where
l
is the number of distinct inverse temperatures among the
d
modes, and
U_{n}
and
V_{n}
are the submatrices obtained by collecting the columns of
U
and
V
corresponding to modes with the same inverse temperature, as specified in (
4.7
). This theorem shows that, in contrast to the GNS spectral gap, the existence of the KMS spectral gap depends crucially on the inverse temperatures of the individual modes. A similarity is that the existence of both GNS and KMS spectral gaps depends only on the parameters of the Kraus operators, after the standardization described in Appendix
B
. Moreover, Corollary
4.3
shows that, for a given Gaussian quantum Markovian generator, the existence of a GNS spectral gap implies the existence of a KMS spectral gap.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the preliminaries of Gaussian states and Gaussian QMSs. In Section
3
, we first present a new method to identify certain matrices whose first eigenvalue corresponds to the spectral gap of a Gaussian quantum Markovian generator, and then determine the matrices whose first eigenvalue gives the GNS and KMS spectral gap of the given generator. In Section
4
, we provide a necessary and sufficient condition for the existence of the KMS spectral gap. Finally, in Section
5
, we apply the obtained results to demonstrate the existence of the KMS spectral gap in a specific boson chain model.

Throughout this paper, we need to work with several real-linear operators. We therefore emphasize that, for real-linear operators, we use capital letters to denote the operators themselves, while their matrix identifications (see Appendix
A
) are denoted in boldface.

2
Gaussian Quantum Markov Semigroups

In this section, we briefly review the basic framework and some known properties of Gaussian states and Gaussian QMSs.

We consider the
d
-mode boson Fock space (also called symmetric Fock space)
\mathsf{h}:=\Gamma(\mathbb{C}^{d})
. It is known that
\Gamma(\mathbb{C}^{d})
is isometrically isomorphic to
\Gamma(\mathbb{C})\otimes\cdots\otimes\Gamma(\mathbb{C})
, where
\Gamma(\mathbb{C})
denotes the one-mode boson Fock space. Each
\Gamma(\mathbb{C})
corresponds to a mode and is isomorphic to
l^{2}(\mathbb{N})
. For clarity of notation, we denote the canonical orthonormal basis of
\Gamma(\mathbb{C}^{d})
by

\left\{e(n_{1},\cdots,n_{d}):=e_{n_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{n_{d}}:n_{1},\cdots,n_{d}\in\mathbb{N}\right\},

where
(e_{n_{j}})_{n_{j}\in\mathbb{N}}
is the canonical orthonormal basis of
\Gamma(\mathbb{C})
for each
1\leq j\leq d
.

Each mode has its own creation, annihilation, and number operators, denoted by
a_{j}^{\dagger}
,
a_{j}
, and
N_{j}
, respectively. They are defined on
\mathsf{h}
as follows:

\displaystyle a_{j}\,e(n_{1},\cdots,n_{d})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n_{j}}\,e(n_{1},\cdots,n_{j-1},n_{j}-1,n_{j+1},\cdots,n_{d}),

\displaystyle a_{j}^{\dagger}\,e(n_{1},\cdots,n_{d})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n_{j}+1}\,e(n_{1},\cdots,n_{j-1},n_{j}+1,n_{j+1},\cdots,n_{d}).

\displaystyle N_{j}e(n_{1},\cdots,n_{d})

\displaystyle=n_{j}\,e(n_{1},\cdots,n_{d}).

Two other important operators on the
j
-th mode are the momentum and position operators, denoted by
p_{j}
and
q_{j}
, respectively, and defined by

p_{j}:=\mathrm{i}\,\frac{a^{\dagger}_{j}-a_{j}}{\sqrt{2}},\quad q_{j}:=\frac{a^{\dagger}_{j}+a_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}.

Linear combinations of creation and annihilation operators are defined as

a(z):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\overline{z_{j}}a_{j},\quad a^{\dagger}(z):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}z_{j}a_{j}^{\dagger},

for
z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Similarly, we define the linear combination of momentum operators as

p(z):=\mathrm{i}\,\frac{a^{\dagger}(z)-a(z)}{\sqrt{2}}=\mathrm{i}\,\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{d}(z_{j}a_{j}^{\dagger}-\overline{z_{j}}a_{j})}{\sqrt{2}}.

(2.1)

Another class of operators crucial to our study is the Weyl operators (also known as displacement operators in different literature). For
z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, the Weyl operator associated with
z
is defined as the exponential of the anti-self-adjoint operator
-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2}\,p(z)
, that is,

W(z):=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\sqrt{2}\,p(z)}.

It is well-known that Weyl operators satisfy the relation

W(z)W(w)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\imaginary\langle z,w\rangle}W(z+w),\quad\forall z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

(2.2)

We consider the von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
, consisting of all bounded linear operators on the boson Fock space
\mathsf{h}
. The space of trace-class operators on
\mathsf{h}
is denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{1}(\mathsf{h})
, and the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators by
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
. The inner product on
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
is denoted by
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{2}
. It is well known that any normal state on
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
can be uniquely represented by a trace-class operator; accordingly, we do not distinguish between them and use the same symbol to denote both.

The
characteristic function
of a normal state
\rho
on
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
is defined by

z\mapsto\Tr(\rho W(z)),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

It is known that any normal state
\rho
is uniquely determined by its characteristic function
[
24
, Proposition 2.4]
. Moreover, the characteristic function establishes a noncommutative Fourier transform for the bosonic system
[
22
, Theorem 5.3.3]
.

A normal state
\rho
is said to be
Gaussian
if there exist
\omega\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
and a positive real-linear operator
S
such that its characteristic function is given by

\Tr(\rho W(z))=\exp{ 2 \mi\Im\langle\omega, z \rangle- \frac{1}{2} \Re\langle z, S z \rangle},\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

(2.3)

A necessary and sufficient condition for the real-linear operator
S
to be the covariance operator of a Gaussian state is

\mathbf{S}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{J}\geq 0,

where
\mathbf{S}
is the matrix identification of
S
(see Appendix
A
), and

\mathbf{J}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&\mathds{1}_{d}\\
-\mathds{1}_{d}&0\end{bmatrix}

(2.4)

is the matrix identification of the real-linear operator

J:z\mapsto-\mathrm{i}z.

(2.5)

Note that
J^{-1}=J^{\sharp}=-J
and
\cdot\,^{\sharp}
denotes the adjoint of a real-linear operator with respect to
\real\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
.

By Williamson’s theorem
[
32
]
, such a matrix
\mathbf{S}
can be symplectically diagonalized as

\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{G}^{*}\begin{bmatrix}D_{\coth}&0\\
0&D_{\coth}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{G},

(2.6)

where
\mathbf{G}
is a symplectic matrix and

D_{\coth}:=\diag\left\{\coth\frac{\beta_{1}}{2},\coth\frac{\beta_{2}}{2},\cdots,\coth\frac{\beta_{d}}{2}\right\},

(2.7)

with
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\cdots,\beta_{d}>0
. The parameters
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\cdots,\beta_{d}
are referred to as
inverse temperatures
.

Remark 2.1
.

Similar to (
2.7
), we also define the
d\times d
diagonal matrices
D_{\sinh}
,
D_{\cosh}
, and
D_{\csch}
in the same way. These diagonal matrices can naturally be viewed as real-linear operators on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and their matrix identifications will be denoted by the boldface symbols
\mathbf{D}_{\sinh}
,
\mathbf{D}_{\cosh}
, and
\mathbf{D}_{\csch}
, respectively. In particular, (
2.6
) can now be written as
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{D}_{\coth}\mathbf{G}
.

According to
[
25
, Section 4]
, the Gaussian state
\rho
with mean vector
\omega
and covariance operator
S
can be written as

\rho=W(\omega)B(\mathbf{M})\bigotimes_{j=1}^{d}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}})\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}N_{j}}B(\mathbf{M})^{-1}W(\omega)^{-1},

(2.8)

where
\mathbf{M}:=\mathbf{G}^{-1}
is a symplectic matrix, and
B(\mathbf{M})
is a unitary operator on the boson Fock space
\mathsf{h}
satisfying

B(\mathbf{M})W(z)B(\mathbf{M})^{*}=W(Mz),\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

Here,
M
denotes the symplectic transformation whose matrix identification is
\mathbf{M}
. The existence of such a unitary operator
B(\mathbf{M})
follows from the Stone-von Neumann theorem, and it is unique up to a scalar multiple of modulus unity.

We refer to
B(\mathbf{M})
as the
Bogoliubov transformation
associated with the symplectic matrix
\mathbf{M}
, or equivalently with the symplectic transformation
M
. Recall that a Bogoliubov transformation for a Bosonic system is a linear transformation of annihilation and creation operators that preserves the canonical commutation relations; equivalently, it is a symplectic transformation of the one-particle space inducing an automorphism of the CCR algebra.

More precisely, since
M
is a symplectic transformation, there exist complex matrices
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
satisfying (
A.4
) and (
A.5
). We then define new annihilation and creation operators
b(z)
and
b^{\dagger}(z)
, respectively, by

\displaystyle b(z)

\displaystyle:=B(\mathbf{M})\,a(z)\,B(\mathbf{M})^{*}=a(M_{1}z)-a^{\dagger}(M_{2}\overline{z}),

\displaystyle b^{\dagger}(z)

\displaystyle:=B(\mathbf{M})\,a^{\dagger}(z)\,B(\mathbf{M})^{*}=a^{\dagger}(M_{1}z)-a(M_{2}\overline{z}).

Then, for all
z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\big[b(z),b^{\dagger}(w)\big]=\langle z,w\rangle=\big[a(z),a^{\dagger}(w)\big],

and hence the canonical commutation relations are preserved.

A QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
is said to be
Gaussian
if its predual semigroup
(\mathcal{T}_{*t})_{t\geq 0}
maps Gaussian states to Gaussian states. We recall that the predual semigroup of
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, denoted by
(\mathcal{T}_{*t})_{t\geq 0}
, is defined by the following relation:

\Tr\left(\mathcal{T}_{*t}(\psi)X\right)=\Tr\left(\psi\mathcal{T}_{t}(X)\right),\quad\forall\psi\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\mathsf{h}),\quad\forall X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h}).

Now we let
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Gaussian QMS on
\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h})
. Its generator
\mathcal{L}
is given by the generalized GKSL form in (
1.1
), with the Hamiltonian and Kraus operators specified in (
1.2
) and (
1.3
), respectively. The generator of the predual semigroup
(\mathcal{T}_{*t})_{t\geq 0}
, denoted by
\mathcal{L}_{*}
, is given by

\mathcal{L}_{*}(\psi)=-\mathrm{i}[H,\psi]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\left(L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}\psi-2L_{\ell}\psi L_{\ell}^{*}+\psi L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}\right),\quad\psi\in\dom\mathcal{L}_{*}\subset\mathcal{B}_{1}(\mathsf{h}),

where the Hamiltonian
H
and the Kraus operators
L_{\ell}
are the same as those in (
1.2
) and (
1.3
).

The evolution of a Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on Weyl operators
W(z)
is described by the following result, which can be found in
[
2
, Theorem 2.4]
. We note that a Gaussian QMS is completely characterized by its explicit action on Weyl operators.

Theorem 2.2
.

We have

\mathcal{T}_{t}(W(z))=\exp{ - \frac{1}{2} \int_0^t \Re\left\langle\me^{s Z} z, C \me^{s Z} z \right\rangle\md s + \mi\int_0^t \Re\left\langle\zeta, \me^{sZ} z \right\rangle\md s}W(\mathrm{e}^{tZ}z),

where
Z,C
are real-linear operators on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
given by

\displaystyle Zz

\displaystyle=\left((U^{T}\overline{U}-V^{T}\overline{V})/2+\mathrm{i}\Omega\right)z+\left((U^{T}V-V^{T}U)/2+\mathrm{i}\kappa\right)\overline{z},

(2.9)

\displaystyle Cz

\displaystyle=(U^{T}\overline{U}+V^{T}\overline{V})z+(U^{T}V+V^{T}U)\overline{z}.

(2.10)

We recall that
\Omega,\kappa,U,V,\zeta
are complex matrices consisting of the parameters introduced in (
1.4
) and (
1.5
). We denote by
\mathbf{Z}
and
\mathbf{C}
the matrix identifications of the real-linear operators
Z
and
C
, respectively. Borrowing terminology from classical Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroups,
Z
is called the
drift operator
and
C
the
diffusion operator
, while
\mathbf{Z}
and
\mathbf{C}
are referred to as the
drift
and
diffusion matrices
, respectively.

For a Gaussian state
\sigma
with mean vector
\omega_{0}
and covariance operator
S_{0}
, by definition of a Gaussian state, its evolution under the predual semigroup
(\mathcal{T}_{*t})_{t\geq 0}
remains Gaussian. Let
\omega_{t}
denote the mean vector of the normal state
\mathcal{T}_{*t}(\sigma)
, and let
S_{t}
denote its covariance operator. By Theorem
2.2
and by using the method of characteristic functions (
2.3
), it follows immediately that

\displaystyle\omega_{t}

\displaystyle=J\mathrm{e}^{tZ^{\sharp}}J^{\sharp}\omega_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}J\mathrm{e}^{sZ^{\sharp}}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}s,

(2.11)

\displaystyle S_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{tZ^{\sharp}}S_{0}\mathrm{e}^{tZ}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{sZ^{\sharp}}C\mathrm{e}^{sZ}\,\mathrm{d}s,

(2.12)

where
J
is the real-linear operator defined in (

).

Moreover, the drift matrix
\mathbf{Z}
is said to be
stable
if all its eigenvalues have strictly negative real parts. It is easily verified that
\mathbf{Z}
is stable if and only if
Z
is stable. The following result is a direct consequence of (

) and (

).

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose the drift operator
Z
of the Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is stable. Then there exists a unique Gaussian state
\rho
with mean vector
\omega
and covariance operator
S
that is invariant under
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, given by

\omega=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}(Z^{\sharp})^{-1}\zeta,\quad S=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{sZ^{\sharp}}C\mathrm{e}^{sZ}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(2.13)

In particular,
S
satisfies the Lyapunov equation

Z^{\sharp}S+SZ=-C.

(2.14)

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
, and let
p(z)
be the associated momentum operator as in (
2.1
). The next result shows that the generator of the Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
maps a momentum operator to another momentum operator under a real-linear transformation of the argument determined by the drift operator

describes the action at the level of generators:
\mathcal{L}
generates the QMS

, while

generates the Weyl unitary group

Proposition 2.4
.

The action of
\mathcal{L}
on the momentum operator

is given by

\mathcal{L}(p(z))=p(Zz)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\real\left\langle\zeta,z\right\rangle.

(2.15)

Proof.

By (

), (

), and (

), we can proceed with direct computations and obtain

\displaystyle-\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{i}[H,p(z)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}(\Omega_{jk}z_{k}+\kappa_{jk}\overline{z_{k}})a_{j}^{\dagger}+\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}(\Omega_{jk}\overline{z_{j}}+\overline{\kappa_{jk}}z_{j})a_{k}+\real\langle\zeta,z\rangle

\displaystyle[p(z),L_{\ell}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\overline{v_{\ell k}}z_{k}+u_{\ell k}\overline{z_{k}}\right).

(2.17)

Then, by (
2.16
) and (
2.17
) we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(p(z))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}[H,p(z)]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\left(L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}p(z)-2L^{*}_{\ell}p(z)L_{\ell}+p(z)L_{\ell}^{*}L_{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}[H,p(z)]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\left([p(z),L_{\ell}^{*}]L_{\ell}-L^{*}_{\ell}[p(z),L_{\ell}]\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}(\Omega_{jk}z_{k}+\kappa_{jk}\overline{z_{k}})a_{j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}(\Omega_{jk}\overline{z_{j}}+\overline{\kappa_{jk}}z_{j})a_{k}

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2\sqrt{2}}\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\left((\overline{v_{\ell k}}v_{\ell j}-\overline{u_{\ell k}}u_{\ell j})z_{k}a_{j}^{\dagger}+(u_{\ell k}\overline{u_{\ell j}}-v_{\ell k}\overline{v_{\ell j}})\overline{z_{k}}a_{j}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+(u_{\ell k}v_{\ell j}-v_{\ell k}u_{\ell j})\overline{z_{k}}a^{\dagger}_{j}+(\overline{v_{\ell k}}\overline{u_{\ell j}}-\overline{u_{\ell k}}\overline{v_{\ell j}})z_{k}a_{j}\right)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\real\langle\zeta,z\rangle

\displaystyle=p(\mathrm{i}\Omega z)+p(\mathrm{i}\kappa\overline{z})-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Re{\overline{\zeta} z}

\displaystyle\quad+p\left((U^{T}\overline{U}-V^{T}\overline{V})z/2\right)+p\left((U^{T}V-V^{T}U)\overline{z}/2\right)

\displaystyle=p(Zz)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Re{\overline{\zeta} z},

which completes the proof.
∎

In the following, we make the main assumption throughout the paper.

Assumption
.

The Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
possesses a unique faithful normal invariant state
\rho
.

This main assumption states that the Gaussian QMS admits a normal invariant state and is irreducible (for details on the irreducibility of QMSs, we refer to
[
13
]
). Several equivalent characterizations of this assumption can be found in
[
18
, proof of Proposition 15]
. We emphasize that, under this assumption, the drift matrix
\mathbf{Z}
is necessarily stable, and the invariant state
\rho
is necessarily Gaussian.

Consequently,
\rho
can be written in the form of (
2.8
) with respect to its mean vector
\omega
and covariance operator
S
. In fact, one can always construct a new Gaussian QMS whose invariant state is the diagonalized Gaussian state

\rho_{0}:=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{d}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}})\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}N_{j}}.

(2.18)

This construction is referred to as the
standardization
of a Gaussian QMS, and we postpone the details to Appendix
B
. As will be shown, this procedure amounts only to a change of parametrization of the CCR algebra, and most properties of the semigroup are preserved. In particular, the main focus of this paper, namely the spectral gap of the induced operator, remains unchanged.

Working with the diagonalized Gaussian state simplifies many computations. For the invariant normal state
\rho_{0}
defined in (
2.18
), the mean vector is
0
and the covariance operator is
D_{\coth}
, as defined in (
2.7
). Therefore, throughout the remainder of the paper, we work exclusively with this diagonalized Gaussian state and with the corresponding Gaussian QMS whose invariant state is given in this form.

3
Spectral Gaps as Eigenvalues of Certain Matrices

The following proposition is a crucial tool in our analysis. It shows that if a complex-linear operator transforms momentum operators into a combination of momentum operators according to certain real-linear operators, then the eigenvalues of the combined real-linear operators are also eigenvalues of the complex-linear operator. In particular, these eigenvalues exhaust all eigenvalues of the complex-linear operator when it is regarded as acting on the space of first-order polynomials in annihilation and creation operators.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a complex-linear operator algebraically defined on momentum operators
p(z)
. Suppose there exist real-linear operators
A
and
B
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that

\mathcal{K}(p(z))=p(Az)+\mathrm{i}\,p(Bz).

(3.1)

Then the eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathbf{A}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{B}
are also eigenvalues of
\mathcal{K}
.

More precisely, if
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{2d}
is a generalized eigenvector of
\mathbf{A}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{B}
of order
n
associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, where
1\leq n\leq 2d
, then

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\mathbf{w}_{j}\cdot p(e_{j})+\mathbf{w}_{j+d}\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{j})\right)

(3.2)

is a generalized eigenvector of
\mathcal{K}
of the same order
n
, associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda
.

Proof.

If there exists
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{2d}
that is a generalized eigenvector of
\mathbf{A}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{B}
associated with
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
of order
n
, where
1\leq n\leq 2d
, then
(\mathbf{A}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{B}-\lambda)^{j}\mathbf{w}
is a generalized eigenvector of
\mathbf{A}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{B}
of order
n-j
for all
0\leq j\leq n-1
. We therefore define
\mathbf{x}:=(\mathbf{A}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{B}-\lambda)^{n-1}\mathbf{w}
, so that
\mathbf{x}
is actually an eigenvector associated with
\lambda
. In this case, we have

(\mathbf{A}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{B})\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}.

(3.3)

Observe that

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\mathbf{x}_{j}\begin{bmatrix}e_{j}\\
0\end{bmatrix}+\mathbf{x}_{j+d}\begin{bmatrix}0\\
e_{j}\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\mathbf{x}_{j}\begin{bmatrix}\real A(e_{j})\\
\imaginary A(e_{j})\end{bmatrix}+\mathbf{x}_{j+d}\begin{bmatrix}\real A(\mathrm{i}e_{j})\\
\imaginary A(\mathrm{i}e_{j})\end{bmatrix}\right),

and an analogous identity holds for
\mathbf{B}
. Therefore, from (
3.3
) we conclude that, for
1\leq k\leq d
,

\displaystyle\lambda\mathbf{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot(\real A(e_{j}))_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{x}_{j+d}\cdot(\real A(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))_{k}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot\mathrm{i}(\real B(e_{j}))_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{x}_{j+d}\cdot\mathrm{i}(\real B(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))_{k},

(3.4)

and for
d+1\leq k\leq 2d
,

\displaystyle\lambda\mathbf{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot(\imaginary A(e_{j}))_{k-d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{x}_{j+d}\cdot(\imaginary A(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))_{k-d}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot\mathrm{i}(\imaginary B(e_{j}))_{k-d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{x}_{j+d}\cdot\mathrm{i}(\imaginary B(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))_{k-d}.

(3.5)

We compute the action of
\mathcal{K}
on
\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot p(e_{j})+\mathbf{x}_{j+d}\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))
. By applying (
3.1
) together with (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{K}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot p(e_{j})+\mathbf{x}_{j+d}\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{j})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{x}_{j}\left(p(A(e_{j}))+\mathrm{i}p(B(e_{j}))\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{x}_{j+d}\left(p(A(e_{j}))+\mathrm{i}p(\mathrm{i}B(e_{j}))\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\left(\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot\real(A(e_{j}))_{k}\cdot p(e_{k})+\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot\imaginary(A(e_{j}))_{k}\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{k})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\left(\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot\mathrm{i}(\real B(e_{j}))_{k}\cdot p(e_{k})+\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot\mathrm{i}(\imaginary B(e_{j}))_{k}\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{k})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\left(\mathbf{x}_{j+d}\cdot\real(A(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))_{k}\cdot p(e_{k})+\mathbf{x}_{j+d}\cdot\imaginary(A(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))_{k}\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{k})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\left(\mathbf{x}_{j+d}\cdot\mathrm{i}(\real B(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))_{k}\cdot p(e_{k})+\mathbf{x}_{j+d}\cdot\mathrm{i}(\imaginary B(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))_{k}\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{k})\right)

(3.6)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\lambda\mathbf{x}_{k}\cdot p(e_{k})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\lambda\mathbf{x}_{k+d}\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{k})

\displaystyle=\lambda\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\cdot p(e_{k})+\mathbf{x}_{k+d}\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{k})\right)\right),

(3.7)

which shows that
\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot p(e_{j})+\mathbf{x}_{j+d}\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))
is indeed an eigenvector of
\mathcal{K}
associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda
.

Next, we define
\mathbf{y}:=(\mathbf{A}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{B}-\lambda)^{n-2}\mathbf{w}
, so we also have

(\mathbf{A}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{B}-\lambda)\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}.

(3.8)

Similar to (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), from (
3.8
) we obtain, for
1\leq k\leq d
,

\displaystyle\lambda\mathbf{y}_{k}+\mathbf{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{y}_{j}\cdot(\real A(e_{j}))_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{y}_{j+d}\cdot(\real A(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))_{k}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{y}_{j}\cdot\mathrm{i}(\real B(e_{j}))_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{y}_{j+d}\cdot\mathrm{i}(\real B(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))_{k},

(3.9)

and for
d+1\leq k\leq 2d
,

\displaystyle\lambda\mathbf{y}_{k}+\mathbf{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{y}_{j}\cdot(\imaginary A(e_{j}))_{k-d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{y}_{j+d}\cdot(\imaginary A(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))_{k-d}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{y}_{j}\cdot\mathrm{i}(\imaginary B(e_{j}))_{k-d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{y}_{j+d}\cdot\mathrm{i}(\imaginary B(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))_{k-d}.

(3.10)

Using (
3.9
) and (
3.10
), and mimicking the long computations in (
3.6
), we have

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{K}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\mathbf{y}_{j}\cdot p(e_{j})+\mathbf{y}_{j+d}\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\lambda\mathbf{y}_{j}+\mathbf{x}_{j})\cdot p(e_{j})+\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\lambda\mathbf{y}_{j+d}+\mathbf{x}_{j+d})\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\mathbf{x}_{j}\cdot p(e_{j})+\mathbf{x}_{j+d}\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{j})\right)+\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\mathbf{y}_{j}\cdot p(e_{j})+\mathbf{y}_{j+d}\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{j})\right),

which, together with (
3.7
), implies that

(\mathcal{K}-\lambda)^{2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\mathbf{y}_{j}\cdot p(e_{j})+\mathbf{y}_{j+d}\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))\right)=0.

Thus,
\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\mathbf{y}_{j}\cdot p(e_{j})+\mathbf{y}_{j+d}\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))
is a generalized eigenvector of
\mathcal{K}
of order
2
associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda
. By repeating this type of computation
n-2
more times, we can show that (
3.2
) is a generalized eigenvector of
\mathcal{K}
of order
n
associated with
\lambda
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Note that in the above result, we have found
2d
generalized eigenvectors of
\mathbf{A}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{B}
that span the space
\mathbb{C}^{2d}
. Moreover, the dimension of the linear space of first-order polynomials in annihilation and creation operators is exactly
2d
. Therefore, we can conclude that these eigenvalues exhaust all eigenvalues of the complex-linear operator
\mathcal{K}
when restricted to the space of first-order polynomials in annihilation and creation operators.

Remark 3.3
.

In Proposition
3.1
, suppose
B=0
and
\mathbf{A}
admits a real eigenvector; that is,
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}=\lambda\mathbf{w}
for some
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. Let
w\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
be the complex vector corresponding to
\mathbf{w}
under the identification. Then, the eigenvector of
\mathcal{K}
associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda
is simply the momentum operator
p(w)
.

The following corollary, which holds analytically in the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
, is immediate. Indeed, via the GNS or KMS embedding, that is, (
1.6
), polynomials in annihilation and creation operators are mapped to Hilbert-Schmidt operators, so the arguments apply not only at the algebraic level but also in the analytic setting.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
K
be an operator on
\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathsf{h})
and let
s\in\{0,1/2\}
. Suppose there exist real-linear operators
A
and
B
such that

K(\rho^{s/2}p(z)\rho^{(1-s)/2})=\rho^{s/2}\left(p(Az)+\mathrm{i}p(Bz)\right)\rho^{(1-s)/2}.

Then, the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{A}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{B}
are also eigenvalues of
K
.

More precisely, if
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{C}^{2d}
is a generalized eigenvector of
\mathbf{A}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{B}
of order
n
associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, where
1\leq n\leq 2d
, then

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\rho^{s/2}\left(\mathbf{w}_{j}\cdot p(e_{j})+\mathbf{w}_{j+d}\cdot p(\mathrm{i}e_{j})\right)\rho^{(1-s)/2}

is a generalized eigenvector of
K
of the same order
n
associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda
.

As shown in
[
14
]
, the spectrum of the induced generator
L^{(s)}
is strongly related to the eigenvalues of the drift matrix
\mathbf{Z}
. First, every eigenvalue of
\mathbf{Z}
is also an eigenvalue of
L^{(s)}
, and the corresponding eigenvectors are embedded first-order polynomials in annihilation and creation operators. This also follows directly from our Corollary
3.4
and Proposition
2.4
. Second, the remaining eigenvalues of
L^{(s)}
, whose eigenvectors are embedded higher-order polynomials in annihilation and creation operators, can be generated from those associated with the first-order embedded polynomials.

We do not discuss further details of the full spectrum of the induced generator
L^{(s)}
; instead, we simply cite the result we need from Theorem 4.13 in
[
14
]
. This result is relatively straightforward, because, roughly speaking, by the definition of the spectral gap (
1.7
) and the fact that polynomials in the annihilation and creation operators form a common core for both
L^{(s)}
and its adjoint
L^{(s)*}
, the spectral gap of
L^{(s)}
equals the extreme value in the numerical range of the self-adjoint operator
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
when restricted to the subspace orthogonal to the square root of the invariant Gaussian state. Note that this square root is a zero-th order embedded polynomial.

Theorem 3.5
.

The induced generator
L^{(s)}
has a spectral gap if and only if the restriction of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
to the embedded first-order polynomials in annihilation and creation operators does not have
0
as an eigenvalue.

According to Corollary
3.4
, if the action of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
on the embedded momentum operators has a convenient form, the existence of a spectral gap reduces to checking whether zero is the first eigenvalue of a certain matrix. The resulting matrix, whose first eigenvalue gives the spectral gap of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
, will be referred to as the
spectral gap matrix
. In this way, the problem of spectral gaps is reduced to a finite-dimensional one.

3.1
KMS Embedding

We are now in a position to determine the spectral gap matrix of the KMS induced generator
L^{(1/2)}
. This matrix is easy to obtain due to the “symmetry” of the KMS embedding. Replacing
K
in Corollary
3.4
with
L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)}
and performing the computation, we find that its action on the embedded momentum operators
\rho^{1/4}p(z)\rho^{1/4}
indeed takes a simple form.

Proposition 3.6
.

The KMS spectral gap matrix is given by

\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{D}_{\csch}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{*}\mathbf{D}_{\csch},

(3.11)

where
\mathbf{Z}
is the drift matrix, that is, the matrix identification of the drift operator
Z
as in (
2.9
), and
\mathbf{D}_{\csch}
is the diagonal matrix defined in Remark
2.1
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.4
for the generator of the Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, and by Proposition
C.1
for the generator of its dual QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
(see Appendix
C
for details), we have

\displaystyle(L^{(1/2)*}+L^{(1/2)})(\rho^{1/4}p(z)\rho^{1/4})

\displaystyle=\rho^{1/4}\left((\mathcal{L}^{\prime}+\mathcal{L})(p(z))\right)\rho^{1/4}

\displaystyle=\rho^{1/4}p(Z^{\prime}z)\rho^{1/4}+\rho^{1/4}p(Zz)\rho^{1/4}

\displaystyle=\rho^{1/4}p((Z+Z^{\prime})z)\rho^{1/4}.

\displaystyle=\rho^{1/4}p\left((Z+D_{\csch}^{-1}Z^{\sharp}D_{\csch})z\right)\rho^{1/4},

where
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
denotes the generator of the dual Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
and
Z^{\prime}
denotes its drift operator. By Corollary
3.4
and Theorem
3.5
, the matrix identification of
Z+D_{\csch}^{-1}Z^{\sharp}D_{\csch}
, which coincides with the expression in (
3.11
), is exactly the KMS spectral gap matrix we are seeking.
∎

3.2
GNS Embedding

In the GNS embedding case, the computations become less straightforward because the symmetry of the embedding is lost. As we will see, the action of the adjoint of the GNS-induced generator,
L^{(0)*}
, on the embedded momentum operator
p(z)\rho^{1/2}
is more complicated.

Proposition 3.7
.

The GNS spectral gap matrix is given by

\mathbf{Z}+\left(\mathbf{D}_{\cosh}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{D}_{\sinh}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{D}_{\csch}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{*}\mathbf{D}_{\csch}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\cosh}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{D}_{\sinh}\right),

(3.12)

where
\mathbf{J}
is defined as in (
2.4
).

Proof.

First note that

\displaystyle\rho^{-1/2}p(z)\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}/2}z_{j}a_{j}^{\dagger}-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}/2}\overline{z_{j}}a_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}/2}z_{j}(p(e_{j})-\mathrm{i}p(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}/2}\overline{z_{j}}(p(e_{j})+\mathrm{i}p(\mathrm{i}e_{j}))\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\cosh(\beta_j / 2)\real z_{j}+\mathrm{i}\sinh(\beta_j / 2)\imaginary z_{j}\right)p(e_{j})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(-\mathrm{i}\sinh(\beta_j / 2)\real z_{j}+\cosh( \beta_j / 2 )\imaginary z_{j}\right)p(\mathrm{i}e_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\cosh(\beta_j / 2)\cdot p(z_{j}e_{j})-\mathrm{i}\sinh(\beta_j /2 )\cdot p(\mathrm{i}z_{j}e_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=p\left(D_{\cosh}z\right)-\mathrm{i}p\left(\mathrm{i}D_{\sinh}z\right).

(3.13)

Using (
3.13
) we are able to compute that the action of the adjoint generator
L^{(0)*}
on the vector
p(z)\rho^{1/2}
, as:

\displaystyle L^{(0)*}(p(z)\rho^{1/2})

\displaystyle=\rho^{1/2}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\rho^{-1/2}p(z)\rho^{1/2})

\displaystyle=\rho^{1/2}\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(p(D_{\cosh}z)-\mathrm{i}p(\mathrm{i}D_{\sinh}z))

\displaystyle=\rho^{1/2}p(Z^{\prime}D_{\cosh}z)-\mathrm{i}\rho^{1/2}p(Z^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}D_{\sinh}z))

\displaystyle=p(D_{\cosh}Z^{\prime}D_{\cosh}z)\rho^{1/2}+\mathrm{i}p(\mathrm{i}D_{\sinh}Z^{\prime}D_{\cosh}z)\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad-\mathrm{i}p(D_{\cosh}Z^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}D_{\sinh}z))\rho^{1/2}+p(\mathrm{i}D_{\sinh}Z^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}D_{\sinh}z))\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle=p\left((D_{\cosh}Z^{\prime}D_{\cosh}+(\mathrm{i}D_{\sinh})Z^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}D_{\sinh}))z\right)\rho^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{i}p\left(((\mathrm{i}D_{\sinh})Z^{\prime}D_{\cosh}-D_{\cosh}Z^{\prime}(\mathrm{i}D_{\sinh}))z\right)\rho^{1/2},

(3.14)

where
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
is the generator of the dual QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
.

Note that the matrix identification of the real-linear operator
z\mapsto\mathrm{i}z
is
-\mathbf{J}
, and that
\mathbf{J}
commutes with
\mathbf{D}_{\sinh}
and
\mathbf{D}_{\cosh}
.

Then, by (
3.14
) and the equality

L^{(0)}(p(z)\rho^{1/2})=\mathcal{L}(p(z))\rho^{1/2}=p(Zz)\rho^{1/2},

Corollary
3.4
, Theorem
3.5
, and Proposition
C.1
together imply that

\displaystyle\quad\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{D}_{\cosh}\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}\mathbf{D}_{\cosh}+\mathbf{J}\mathbf{D}_{\sinh}\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{D}_{\sinh}+\mathrm{i}\left(-\mathbf{J}\mathbf{D}_{\sinh}\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}\mathbf{D}_{\cosh}+\mathbf{D}_{\cosh}\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{D}_{\sinh}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Z}+\left(\mathbf{D}_{\cosh}-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{D}_{\sinh}\right)\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}\mathbf{D}_{\cosh}+\left(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{D}_{\sinh}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{D}_{\cosh}\right)\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{D}_{\sinh}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Z}+\left(\mathbf{D}_{\cosh}-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{D}_{\sinh}\right)\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\cosh}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{D}_{\sinh}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Z}+\left(\mathbf{D}_{\cosh}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{D}_{\sinh}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{D}_{\csch}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{*}\mathbf{D}_{\csch}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\cosh}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{D}_{\sinh}\right)

is exactly the GNS spectral gap matrix as in (
3.12
), completing the proof.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

Note that
\mathbf{D}_{\csch}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\cosh}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{D}_{\sinh}\right)=\mathbf{D}_{\coth}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{J}
, so this result coincides with the matrix found by Fagnola et al. in Proposition 14 of
[
15
]
.

Comparing the KMS spectral gap matrix (
3.11
) with the GNS spectral gap (
3.12
), we observe that the GNS spectral gap matrix is more deformed in the part of the adjoint of the drift matrix. However, it remains unclear whether the KMS spectral gap is always greater than or equal to the GNS spectral gap. This conjecture was proposed in
[
15
]
and is known to hold in the one-mode case
[
14
]
.

4
Existence of the KMS Spectral Gap

It is known that, in either the GNS embedding or the KMS embedding, both
L^{(s)}
and
L^{(s)*}
are maximally dissipative operators sharing a common core. Consequently, all eigenvalues of
L^{(s)*}+L^{(s)}
are negative, which implies that both the GNS spectral gap matrix (
3.12
) and the KMS spectral gap matrix (
3.11
) have only negative eigenvalues. However, these spectral gap matrices are not necessarily Hermitian.

In
[
15
]
, it was shown that the GNS spectral gap matrix (
3.12
) has
0
as an eigenvalue if and only if there exists a maximal number of linearly independent Kraus operators. This condition is also equivalent to the kernel of the negative matrix

\mathbf{Z}^{*}(\mathbf{D}_{\coth}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{J})+(\mathbf{D}_{\coth}+\mathrm{i}\mathbf{J})\mathbf{Z}

(4.1)

being trivial, i.e.,
\{0\}
. Note that the matrix (
4.1
) shares the invertibility property with the GNS spectral gap matrix (
3.12
).

In the KMS embedding case, to investigate the existence of the spectral gap, one can examine the invertibility of the negative matrix

\mathbf{Z}^{*}\mathbf{D}_{\csch}+\mathbf{D}_{\csch}\mathbf{Z}.

(4.2)

As with (
4.1
), the negativity of (
4.2
) is not immediately evident. Since its negativity is equivalent to that of the real-linear operator
Z^{\sharp}D_{\csch}+D_{\csch}Z
, we will, in the following, first express
Z^{\sharp}D_{\csch}+D_{\csch}Z
in terms of
U
and
V
to clarify how the negativity arises.

Theorem 4.1
.

We have

\displaystyle(Z^{\sharp}D_{\csch}+D_{\csch}Z)z

\displaystyle=\left[-\frac{2(U^{T}\overline{U})_{jk}}{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{k}/2}}-\frac{2(V^{T}\overline{V})_{jk}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}/2}}\right]_{j,k}z

\displaystyle\quad+\left[-\frac{2(U^{T}V)_{jk}}{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}/2}}-\frac{2(V^{T}U)_{jk}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{k}/2}}\right]_{j,k}\overline{z},

(4.3)

where
1\leq j\leq d
and
1\leq k\leq d
. Therefore,
Z^{\sharp}D_{\csch}+D_{\csch}Z
is negative and we can write it as
-\int_{0}^{\infty}A_{t}^{\sharp}A_{t}~\mathrm{d}t
, in which

A_{t}z:=\left[\exp{\me^{-\beta_k / 2} t}\overline{u_{jk}}\right]_{j,k}z+\left[\exp{\me^{\beta_k / 2} t}v_{jk}\right]_{j,k}\overline{z},

(4.4)

where
1\leq j\leq m
and
1\leq k\leq d
.

Proof.

By the Lyapunov equation (
2.14
) we have

Z^{\sharp}D_{\coth}+D_{\coth}Z=-C.

(4.5)

Note that the complex-linear and conjugate-linear parts on both sides of (
4.5
) coincide, respectively. Therefore, recalling the definitions of
Z
and
C
in (
2.9
) and (
2.10
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\{(U^{T}\overline{U}-V^{T}\overline{V})/2,D_{\coth}\right\}+\mathrm{i}\left[D_{\coth},\Omega\right]=-(U^{T}\overline{U}+V^{T}\overline{V}),

\displaystyle\left[(V^{T}U-U^{T}V)/2,D_{\coth}\right]+\mathrm{i}\left\{\kappa,D_{\coth}\right\}=-(U^{T}V+V^{T}U),

which implies

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\Omega_{jk}

\displaystyle=\frac{-(\coth(\beta_{j}/2)+\coth(\beta_{k}/2))/2-1}{\coth(\beta_{j}/2)-\coth(\beta_{k}/2)}(U^{T}\overline{U})_{jk}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(\coth(\beta_{j}/2)+\coth(\beta_{k}/2))/2-1}{\coth(\beta_{j}/2)-\coth(\beta_{k}/2)}(V^{T}\overline{V})_{jk}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}-1-\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}}}{2(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}}\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}})}(U^{T}\overline{U})_{jk}+\frac{2\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}}-1-\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}}}{2(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}}\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}})}(V^{T}\overline{V})_{jk},

(4.6)

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\kappa_{jk}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\coth(\beta_{k}/2)-\coth(\beta_{j}/2))/2-1}{\coth(\beta_{k}/2)+\coth(\beta_{j}/2)}(U^{T}V)_{jk}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(\coth(\beta_{j}/2)-\coth(\beta_{k}/2))/2-1}{\coth(\beta_{k}/2)+\coth(\beta_{j}/2)}(V^{T}U)_{jk}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-1}{2(\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-1)}(U^{T}V)_{jk}+\frac{2\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}-\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-1}{2(\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-1)}(V^{T}U)_{jk},

for all
1\leq j\leq d
and
1\leq k\leq d
. Note that in (
4.6
) we require
j\neq k
. In fact, the Lyapunov equation (
2.14
) provides no information about the diagonal entries of the Hermitian matrix
\Omega
. Fortunately, these diagonal entries do not affect the real-linear operator
Z^{\sharp}D_{\csch}+D_{\csch}Z
.

Next, using the above results, we can compute

\displaystyle\quad\left(\left\{(U^{T}\overline{U}-V^{T}\overline{V})/2,D_{\csch}\right\}+\mathrm{i}\left[D_{\csch},\Omega\right]\right)_{jk}

\displaystyle=\frac{\csch(\beta_k /2)+\csch(\beta_j / 2)}{2}\left((U^{T}\overline{U})_{jk}-(V^{T}\overline{V})_{jk}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{i}\Omega_{jk}\left(\csch(\beta_j / 2)-\csch(\beta_k / 2)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}/2}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}-1}+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}/2}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-1}+\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}/2}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-1}-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}/2}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}-1}\right)\frac{2\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}-\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}}\right)(U^{T}\overline{U})_{jk}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}/2}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}-1}-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}/2}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-1}+\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}/2}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-1}-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}/2}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}-1}\right)\frac{2-\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{k}}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}}\right)(V^{T}\overline{V})_{jk}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2(U^{T}\overline{U})_{jk}}{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{k}/2}}-\frac{2(V^{T}\overline{V})_{jk}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}/2}},

and

\displaystyle\quad\left(\left[(V^{T}U-U^{T}V)/2,D_{\csch}\right]+\mathrm{i}\left\{\kappa,D_{\csch}\right\}\right)_{jk}

\displaystyle=\frac{\csch(\beta_j /2)-\csch(\beta_k / 2)}{2}\left((U^{T}V)_{jk}-(V^{T}U)_{jk}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{i}\kappa_{jk}\left(\csch(\beta_j / 2)+\csch(\beta_k / 2)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}/2}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-1}-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}/2}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}-1}+\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}/2}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-1}+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}/2}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}-1}\right)\frac{2\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}-1}\right)(U^{T}V)_{jk}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}/2}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}-1}-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}/2}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-1}+\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}/2}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-1}+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}/2}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}-1}\right)\frac{2\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}-\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-1}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}}\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-1}\right)(V^{T}U)_{jk}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2(U^{T}V)_{jk}}{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{k}/2}}-\frac{2(V^{T}U)_{jk}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}/2}+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{k}/2}},

which are the
(j,k)
-th entries of the complex-linear and conjugate-linear parts of the real-linear operator
Z^{\sharp}D_{\csch}+D_{\csch}Z
, completing the proof of (
4.3
).

Now, by (
4.4
) we have

A_{t}^{\sharp}z=\left[\exp{\me^{-\beta_j / 2} t}u_{kj}\right]_{j,k}z+\left[\exp{\me^{\beta_j / 2} t}v_{kj}\right]_{j,k}\overline{z},

where
1\leq j\leq d
and
1\leq k\leq m
, so
Z^{\sharp}D_{\csch}+D_{\csch}Z=-\int_{0}^{\infty}A_{t}^{\sharp}A_{t}~\mathrm{d}t
is immediate.
∎

We now introduce a notation useful for analyzing the existence of the KMS spectral gap; namely, we separate the columns of the parameter matrices
U
and
V
according to their associated inverse temperatures.

Note that the inverse temperatures
\beta_{j}
of the
j
-th mode,
1\leq j\leq d
, are obtained from the symplectic diagonalization (
2.6
) of the covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}
of the invariant Gaussian state
\rho
of the semigroup. In general, different modes may share the same inverse temperature.

Let
I_{1},I_{2},\cdots,I_{l}
,
1\leq l\leq d
, be a partition of the index set
\{1,2,\dots,d\}
such that all indices in each subset
I_{n}
,
1\leq n\leq l
, correspond to modes with the same inverse temperature. For each
n
, define the submatrices
U_{n}
and
V_{n}
by

U_{n}:=\begin{bmatrix}Ue_{i}\end{bmatrix}_{i\in I_{n}},\quad V_{n}:=\begin{bmatrix}Ve_{i}\end{bmatrix}_{i\in I_{n}},

(4.7)

where
e_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq d
, denotes the
i
-th vector of the canonical orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Note that
U_{n}
and
V_{n}
have size
m\times|I_{n}|
, where
m
is the number of Kraus operators. In other words,
U_{n}
and
V_{n}
are obtained by grouping together the columns of
U
and
V
whose indices correspond to modes with the same inverse temperature.

Theorem 4.2
.

There exists a KMS spectral gap for the Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
if and only if

\ker U_{n}\cap\ker V_{n}=\{0\},\quad\forall 1\leq n\leq l,

(4.8)

where
U_{n}
and
V_{n}
are defined as in (
4.7
), and
l
denotes the number of distinct inverse temperatures among the
d
modes.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.5
and Proposition
3.6
, there exists a KMS spectral gap if and only if

\ker(\mathbf{Z}+\mathbf{D}_{\csch}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{*}\mathbf{D}_{\csch})=\{0\},

which is equivalent to

\ker(Z^{\sharp}D_{\csch}+D_{\csch}Z)=\{0\}.

(4.9)

Since
Z^{\sharp}D_{\csch}+D_{\csch}Z
is negative, (
4.9
) is further equivalent to the statement that
w=0
is the only vector in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
satisfying

\real\left\langle w,(Z^{\sharp}D_{\csch}+D_{\csch}Z)w\right\rangle=0.

(4.10)

By Theorem
4.1
, (
4.10
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\real\left\langle w,-(Z^{\sharp}D_{\csch}+D_{\csch}Z)w\right\rangle=\real\left\langle w,\int_{0}^{\infty}A_{t}^{\sharp}A_{t}~\mathrm{d}t~w\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\real\left\langle w,A_{t}^{\sharp}A_{t}w\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}\real\left\langle A_{t}w,A_{t}w\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}\absolutevalue{A_t w}^{2}\mathrm{d}t,

(4.11)

where
A_{t}
is defined in (
4.4
). Hence, there exists a KMS spectral gap if and only if
w=0
is the only vector in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
for which (
4.11
) holds.

Moreover, since the map
t\mapsto A_{t}w
is continuous, (
4.11
) holds if and only if

A_{t}w=0,\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(4.12)

Recalling (
4.3
), (
4.12
) is equivalent to

\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left(\exp{\me^{-\beta_k / 2} t}\overline{u_{jk}}w_{k}+\exp{ \me^{\beta_k / 2} t}v_{jk}\overline{w_{k}}\right)=0,\quad\forall t\geq 0,\quad\forall 1\leq j\leq m.

(4.13)

Note that exponential functions with different exponents are linearly independent. It is now clear that if (
4.8
) holds, then
w=0
is the only vector in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
satisfying (
4.13
). Conversely, suppose that there exists
1\leq r\leq l
such that
\ker U_{r}\cap\ker V_{r}\neq\{0\}
, or equivalently,
\ker\overline{U_{r}}\cap\ker\overline{V_{r}}\neq\{0\}
. Then there exists a non-zero vector
w_{0}
with dimension
\absolutevalue{ I_r }
such that
\overline{U_{r}}w_{0}=0
and
V_{r}\overline{w_{0}}=0
. Define a vector
w\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
by setting its components indexed by
I_{r}
equal to those of
w_{0}
, and all remaining components equal to
0
. This vector
w
is non-zero and satisfies (
4.13
). Therefore, the proof is complete.

∎

Corollary 4.3
.

If there exists a GNS spectral gap, then there exists a KMS spectral gap.

Proof.

The GNS spectral gap exists if and only if there are
2d
linearly independent Kraus operators. From (
1.3
), it is clear that this is equivalent to requiring the
2d\times 2d
matrix
[U\,\,\,\overline{V}]
to be full rank, that is, to have
2d
linearly independent columns. Consequently, both
U
and
V
have
d
linearly independent columns, which implies that

\ker U_{n}=\ker V_{n}=\{0\},\quad\forall 1\leq n\leq l.

Thus, (
4.8
) holds immediately, and we conclude that the KMS spectral gap exists.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

From the proof of Theorem
4.2
, we see the crucial role played by the inverse temperatures in the existence of the KMS spectral gap. For instance, in a
d
-mode system, if all modes have distinct inverse temperatures, then each submatrix
U_{n}
and
V_{n}
, as defined in (
4.7
), is a column vector with a necessarily trivial kernel. Hence, (
4.8
) holds, and a KMS spectral gap exists. This also explains why a Gaussian QMS on the one-mode boson Fock space always has a KMS spectral gap.

In the following, we construct an example of a Gaussian QMS with a unique faithful normal invariant state for which the KMS spectral gap does not exist. The key point is that different modes share the same inverse temperature.

Example 4.5
.

We consider the case
d=2
. We let
U=[u,u]
and
V=[v,v]
with some
v>u>0
. So, there is only one Kraus operator
va_{1}+ua_{1}^{\dagger}+va_{2}+ua_{2}^{\dagger}
, and

U^{T}V=V^{T}U=vu\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix},\quad U^{T}\overline{U}=u^{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix},\quad V^{T}\overline{V}=v^{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}.

We also let

\kappa=\mathrm{i}uv\cdot\frac{v^{2}-u^{2}}{v^{2}+u^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix},\quad\Omega=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
b&c\end{bmatrix},

for some
a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}
with
a\neq c
.
Due to simplicity of notation we shall define
E
as the square matrix with all entries are
1
. In this way, we have constructed a drift operator that is

Zz=\left(\frac{u^{2}-v^{2}}{2}E+\mathrm{i}\Omega\right)z+\left(-uv\cdot\frac{v^{2}-u^{2}}{v^{2}+u^{2}}E\right)\overline{z},

whose matrix identification is given by

\mathbf{Z}=\begin{bmatrix}\left(\frac{u^{2}-v^{2}}{2}-uv\cdot\frac{v^{2}-u^{2}}{v^{2}+u^{2}}\right)E&-\Omega\\
\Omega&\left(\frac{u^{2}-v^{2}}{2}+uv\cdot\frac{v^{2}-u^{2}}{v^{2}+u^{2}}\right)E\end{bmatrix}.

(4.14)

Therefore, we have

\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{Z}^{*}+\mathbf{Z})=\begin{bmatrix}\left(\frac{u^{2}-v^{2}}{2}-uv\cdot\frac{v^{2}-u^{2}}{v^{2}+u^{2}}\right)E&0\\
0&\left(\frac{u^{2}-v^{2}}{2}+uv\cdot\frac{v^{2}-u^{2}}{v^{2}+u^{2}}\right)E\end{bmatrix}.

(4.15)

Since
v>u>0
, all eigenvalues of the matrix (
4.15
) are less than or equal to
0
. In particular, the eigenvectors of (
4.15
) corresponding to the eigenvalue
0
are
(1,-1,0,0)
and
(0,0,1,-1)
. By the well-known Bendixson inequality, the real parts of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Z}
, given in (
4.14
), are also less than or equal to
0
. Moreover, if
w\in\mathbb{C}^{2d}
is an eigenvector of
\mathbf{Z}
associated with an eigenvalue whose real part is
0
, then
w
must lie in the linear span of
(1,-1,0,0)
and
(0,0,1,-1)
, since the Rayleigh–Ritz theorem states that an extremal value of the numerical range of a Hermitian matrix is attained at an eigenvector. However, such an eigenvector cannot exist because
a\neq c
. Consequently, all eigenvalues of the drift matrix
\mathbf{Z}
have strictly negative real parts, that is,
\mathbf{Z}
is stable.

Then, by Proposition
2.3
, there exists a unique Gaussian state that is invariant under the semigroup. A direct computation shows that the real-linear operator

Sz=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{v^{2}+u^{2}}{v^{2}-u^{2}}&0\\
0&\frac{v^{2}+u^{2}}{v^{2}-u^{2}}\end{bmatrix}z

solves the corresponding Lyapunov equation (
2.14
) and hence is the covariance operator of the invariant state. This implies that the two modes have the same inverse temperature,

\beta_{1}=\beta_{2}=\log\left(v^{2}/u^{2}\right),

(4.16)

and the invariant state is diagonal. Consequently, the matrix
D_{\csch}
appearing in the KMS spectral gap matrix (
3.11
) is a scalar multiple of the identity matrix. Hence, by (
4.16
) we have

Z^{\sharp}D_{\csch}+D_{\csch}Z=\frac{2uv}{v^{2}-u^{2}}(Z^{\sharp}+Z),

and, by (
4.15
), we immediately find that
\ker(Z^{\sharp}D_{\csch}+D_{\csch}Z)\neq\{0\}
. Therefore, the KMS spectral gap does not exist in this case.

5
Application to a Boson Chain Model

In this section, we consider a boson chain model with
d=3
. Boson chain models with a larger number of modes are also of interest; however, as the number of modes increases, numerical methods become more convenient, while analytical results are more difficult to obtain.

The Hamiltonian
H
is given by

H=\omega\sum_{j=1}^{2}\left(a_{j}a^{\dagger}_{j+1}+a^{\dagger}_{j}a_{j+1}\right),

(5.1)

where
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
is non-zero, and the Kraus operators
L_{\ell}
are

L_{1}=\gamma_{1}^{-}a_{1},\quad L_{2}=\gamma_{1}^{+}a_{1}^{\dagger},\quad L_{3}=\gamma_{3}^{-}a_{3},\quad L_{4}=\gamma_{3}^{+}a_{3}^{\dagger},

(5.2)

where

\gamma_{j}^{-}=\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-1}},\quad\gamma_{j}^{+}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{\beta_{j}}-1}},\quad j\in\{1,3\}.

Apparently, the number of independent Kraus operators is insufficient; therefore, the boson chain model does not possess a GNS spectral gap. We also assume that
\beta_{1}\neq\beta_{3}
. Indeed, when
\beta_{1}=\beta_{3}
, the invariant state is already diagonalized and all modes share the same inverse temperature. Consequently, as in Example
4.5
, no KMS spectral gap arises. For this reason, we exclude the case
\beta_{1}=\beta_{3}
to avoid repetition. Moreover, although we use
\beta_{1}
and
\beta_{3}
at this stage, they are not the final inverse temperatures of the modes, as will be shown in (
5.11
).

By (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) we have

U=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
\gamma_{1}^{+}&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&\gamma_{3}^{+}\end{bmatrix},\quad V=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1}^{-}&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&\gamma_{3}^{-}\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad\Omega=\begin{bmatrix}0&\omega&0\\
\omega&0&\omega\\
0&\omega&0\end{bmatrix}.

Apparently, in this model we have
\kappa=0
and
\zeta=0
. By direct computation, it also follows that
U^{T}V=V^{T}U=0
, and

\displaystyle U^{T}\overline{U}-V^{T}\overline{V}

\displaystyle=\diag\{-1,0,-1\},

\displaystyle U^{T}\overline{U}+V^{T}\overline{V}

\displaystyle=\diag\{\coth(\beta_{1}/2),0,\coth(\beta_{3}/2)\}.

By (
2.9
) and (
2.10
), we can compute the drift operator
Z
and the diffusion operator
C
, from which it follows that

\mathbf{Z}=\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\diag\{1,0,1\}&-\Omega\\
\Omega&-\frac{1}{2}\diag\{1,0,1\}\end{bmatrix}

and

\mathbf{C}=\begin{bmatrix}\diag\{\coth(\beta_{1}/2),0,\coth(\beta_{3}/2)\}&0\\
0&\diag\{\coth(\beta_{1}/2),0,\coth(\beta_{3}/2)\}\end{bmatrix}.

Using the same method as in Example
4.5
, we can show that
\mathbf{Z}
is stable. Moreover, using the trick introduced as in
[
12
, Appendix A]
, we shall write
\mathbf{C}
as

\mathbf{C}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\coth\frac{\beta_{1}}{2}+\coth\frac{\beta_{3}}{2}\right)\mathbf{C}_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\coth\frac{\beta_{1}}{2}-\coth\frac{\beta_{3}}{2}\right)\mathbf{C}_{\delta},

(5.3)

where
\mathbf{C}_{1}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\delta}
are defined as

\mathbf{C}_{1}:=\begin{bmatrix}\diag\{1,0,1\}&0\\
0&\diag\{1,0,1\}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{C}_{\delta}:=\begin{bmatrix}\diag\{1,0,-1\}&0\\
0&\diag\{1,0,-1\}\end{bmatrix}.

For simplicity of notation, we shall define

\lambda:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\coth\frac{\beta_{1}}{2}+\coth\frac{\beta_{3}}{2}\right),\quad\mu:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\coth\frac{\beta_{1}}{2}-\coth\frac{\beta_{3}}{2}\right),

(5.4)

and then (
5.3
) can be rewritten as
\mathbf{C}=\lambda\mathbf{C}_{1}+\mu\mathbf{C}_{\delta}
.

Now we aim to solve the Lyapunov equation
\mathbf{Z}^{*}\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{S}\mathbf{Z}=-\mathbf{C}
for
\mathbf{S}
. Clearly, if we can solve
\mathbf{Z}^{*}\mathbf{S}_{1}+\mathbf{S}_{1}\mathbf{Z}=-\mathbf{C}_{1}
for
\mathbf{S}_{1}
and
\mathbf{Z}^{*}\mathbf{S}_{\delta}+\mathbf{S}_{\delta}\mathbf{Z}=-\mathbf{C}_{\delta}
for
\mathbf{S}_{\delta}
, then by (
5.3
), we obtain

\mathbf{S}=\lambda\mathbf{S}_{1}+\mu\mathbf{S}_{\delta}.

(5.5)

Since the drift matrix
\mathbf{Z}
is stable, it follows that the above Lyapunov equations admit unique solutions. Moreover, it can be easily verified that these solutions are

\mathbf{S}_{1}=\mathds{1}_{6},

and

\mathbf{S}_{\delta}=\frac{2}{4\omega^{2}+1}\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{2}\diag\{-1,0,1\}&\begin{bmatrix}0&-\omega&0\\
\omega&0&-\omega\\
0&\omega&0\end{bmatrix}\\
\begin{bmatrix}0&\omega&0\\
-\omega&0&\omega\\
0&-\omega&0\end{bmatrix}&-\frac{1}{2}\diag\{-1,0,1\}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus, we obtain the covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}
of the boson chain model as in (
5.5
).

Remark 5.1
.

Note that, for a general covariance matrix
\mathbf{S}
, its symplectic eigenvalues can be obtained from the eigenvalues of the matrix
\mathrm{i}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{S}
. By (
2.6
), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{S}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{G}^{*}\mathbf{D}_{\coth}\mathbf{G}=\mathrm{i}\mathbf{G}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{D}_{\coth}\mathbf{G}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{i}\begin{bmatrix}0&D_{\coth}\\
-D_{\coth}&0\end{bmatrix}\cdot\mathbf{G}.

(5.6)

Since
\mathbf{G}
is a symplectic matrix, (
5.6
) shows that
\mathrm{i}\mathbf{J}\mathbf{S}
is similar to a matrix whose eigenvalues are clearly given by

\left(\pm\coth(\beta_{1}/2),\pm\coth(\beta_{2}/2),\cdots,\pm\coth(\beta_{d}/2)\right).

Then, with the help of Remark
5.1
, we turn to compute

\mathbf{J}\mathbf{S}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{2\mu}{4\omega^{2}+1}\begin{bmatrix}0&\omega&0\\
-\omega&0&\omega\\
0&-\omega&0\end{bmatrix}&\diag\left\{\lambda+\frac{\mu}{4\omega^{2}+1},\lambda,\lambda-\frac{\mu}{4\omega^{2}+1}\right\}\\

Appendix A
Matrix Identification of Real-linear Operators

Let
S
be a real-linear operator on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Then there exist two complex-linear operators (or equivalently, two complex matrices)
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that

Sz=S_{1}z+S_{2}\overline{z},\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

To gain a clearer understanding of these operators, we can use the natural isomorphism between the real vector space
(\mathbb{C}^{d},\real\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle)
and
(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle)
, obtained by separating each complex vector into its real and imaginary components. In practice, it is often convenient to represent
S
and
z
by a real matrix
\mathbf{S}
acting on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and a vector
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, defined by

\mathbf{S}:=\begin{bmatrix}\real S_{1}+\real S_{2}&\imaginary S_{2}-\imaginary S_{1}\\
\imaginary S_{1}+\imaginary S_{2}&\real S_{1}-\real S_{2}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{z}:=\begin{bmatrix}\real z\\
\imaginary z\end{bmatrix}.

(A.1)

The real matrix
\mathbf{S}
, written in boldface, is referred to as the
matrix identification
of the real-linear operator
S
throughout this paper. Often, we denote the complexification of
\mathbf{S}
by the same symbol
S
.

Conversely, any real matrix
\mathbf{S}
acting on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
naturally induces a real-linear operator
S
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Namely, if

\mathbf{S}=\begin{bmatrix}S_{11}&S_{12}\\
S_{21}&S_{22}\end{bmatrix},

then the real-linear operator
S
can be defined as

Sz=\left(\frac{S_{11}+S_{22}}{2}+\mathrm{i}\,\frac{S_{21}-S_{12}}{2}\right)z+\left(\frac{S_{11}-S_{22}}{2}+\mathrm{i}\,\frac{S_{12}+S_{21}}{2}\right)\overline{z}.

(A.2)

The adjoint of the real-linear operator
S
with respect to the real inner product
\real\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
is denoted by
S^{\sharp}
. A straightforward computation shows that

S^{\sharp}z=S_{1}^{*}z+S_{2}^{T}\overline{z},\quad\forall z\in\mathbb{C}^{d},

(A.3)

and under the matrix identification,
S^{\sharp}
corresponds simply to
\mathbf{S}^{*}
.

The following identities are frequently useful, although we do not explicitly cite them in computations:

Lemma A.1
.

Let
S
be a real-linear operator on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
y,z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Let
\mathbf{S},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}
be their corresponding matrix identifications, respectively. We have

\mathbf{S}\mathbf{z}=\begin{bmatrix}\real Sz\\
\imaginary Sz\end{bmatrix},\quad\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}\rangle=\real\langle y,z\rangle.

Consequently, powers and the exponential of a real-linear operator
S
can be represented in the same way:

\mathbf{S}^{n}\mathbf{z}=\begin{bmatrix}\real S^{n}z\\
\imaginary S^{n}z\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{S}}\mathbf{z}=\begin{bmatrix}\real\mathrm{e}^{S}z\\
\imaginary\mathrm{e}^{S}z\end{bmatrix}.

Sometimes we need to work with a particular class of real-linear operators, namely symplectic transformations. Let
M
be a real-linear operator. We say that
M
is a
symplectic transformation
if

\imaginary\langle z,w\rangle=\imaginary\langle Mz,Mw\rangle,\quad\forall z,w\in\mathbb{C}^{d}.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be its matrix identification. Then
\mathbf{M}
is a
2d\times 2d
symplectic matrix. It is well-known that
\mathbf{M}^{*}
and
\mathbf{M}^{-1}
are also symplectic. By (
A.2
), the corresponding real-linear operators are
M^{\sharp}
and
M^{-1}
, respectively.

If we write the symplectic transformation as
Mz=M_{1}z+M_{2}\overline{z}
, then the complex matrices
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
necessarily satisfy

\displaystyle M_{1}^{*}M_{1}-M_{2}^{T}\overline{M_{2}}=\mathds{1}_{d},\quad M_{2}^{T}\overline{M_{1}}=M_{1}^{*}M_{2},

(A.4)

\displaystyle M_{1}M_{1}^{*}-M_{2}M_{2}^{*}=\mathds{1}_{d},\quad M_{1}M_{2}^{T}=M_{2}M_{1}^{T}.

(A.5)

Moreover, the inverse of the real-linear operator
M
takes the form

M^{-1}z=M_{1}^{*}z-M_{2}^{T}\overline{z}.

(A.6)

Appendix B
Standardization and Invariance of Spectral Gaps

When we have a Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with a unique faithful normal invariant state

\rho=W(\omega)B(\mathbf{M})\bigotimes_{j=1}^{d}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}})\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{j}N_{j}}B(\mathbf{M})^{-1}W(\omega)^{-1},

(B.1)

we can always construct a new Gaussian QMS
(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
whose invariant state is the diagonalized state

Remark B.2
.

It is worth noting that different choices of
U
and
V
in the generator (
1.1
) may produce the same drift operator
Z
and diffusion operator
C
. Let
\widehat{U}
and
\widehat{V}
be another pair of parameter matrices. These pairs yield the same drift and diffusion operators if and only if there exists an
m\times m
unitary matrix
Q
such that

\widehat{U}=QU,\quad\widehat{V}=\overline{Q}V.

In this case the corresponding Kraus operators
L_{\ell}
differ, yet the noise part of the generator, namely
\mathcal{L}-\mathrm{i}[H,\,\cdot\,]
, remains unchanged, as one can verify by direct computation. Therefore,
\widetilde{U}
and
\widetilde{V}
in (
B.6
) are unique in this sense.

In the sequel, we use
\widetilde{L}^{(s)}
to denote the generator induced by the standardized QMS
(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, and we show that the standardization process (
B.3
) has no effect on the spectral gap, either in the GNS embedding or in the KMS embedding.

Proposition B.3
.

For
s\in\{0,1/2\}
, we have

\gap L^{(s)}=\gap\widetilde{L}^{(s)}.

Proof.

By direct computation,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}p(z)\mathcal{U}^{*}

\displaystyle=W(\omega)B(\mathbf{M})p(z)B(\mathbf{M})^{*}W(\omega)^{*}

\displaystyle=W(\omega)p(Mz)W(\omega)^{*}

\displaystyle=p(Mz)+\sqrt{2}\imaginary\langle\omega,Mz\rangle,

and, similarly,

\mathcal{U}^{*}p(z)\mathcal{U}=p(M^{-1}z)-\sqrt{2}\imaginary\langle\omega,z\rangle.

Since any polynomial in annihilation and creation operators can be written as a finite sum of finite products of momentum operators, it follows that both
\cdot\mapsto\mathcal{U}\cdot\mathcal{U}^{*}
and
\cdot\mapsto\mathcal{U}^{*}\cdot\mathcal{U}
send polynomials in these operators to polynomials. Therefore, the map
\cdot\mapsto\mathcal{U}\cdot\mathcal{U}^{*}
is a bijection on the space of polynomials in the annihilation and creation operators.

Now, let
X
be a polynomial in annihilation and creation operators. Then
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}X\mathcal{U}^{*})
is algebraically well-defined, and from (
B.3
) we directly obtain the identity
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(X)=\mathcal{U}^{*}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}X\mathcal{U}^{*})\mathcal{U}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\widetilde{L}^{(s)}(\rho_{0}^{s/2}X\rho_{0}^{(1-s)/2})

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}^{s/2}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(X)\rho_{0}^{(1-s)/2}=\rho^{s/2}_{0}\mathcal{U}^{*}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}X\mathcal{U}^{*})\mathcal{U}\rho^{(1-s)/2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}^{*}\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{U}\mathcal{U}^{*}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}X\mathcal{U}^{*})\mathcal{U}\mathcal{U}^{*}\rho^{(1-s)/2}\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}^{*}\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U}X\mathcal{U}^{*})\rho^{(1-s)/2}\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}^{*}L^{(s)}(\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{U}X\mathcal{U}^{*}\rho^{(1-s)/2})\mathcal{U}.

(B.10)

Moreover, according to Lemma 4.3 in
[
14
]
, the polynomials in annihilation and creation operators embedded in the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators with respect to the invariant Gaussian state of the QMS, form a core for the induced generator. This core will be used to approximate the spectral gap, as shown below. Together with (
B.10
), we find

\displaystyle\gap\widetilde{L}^{(s)}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{-\real\left\langle Y,\widetilde{L}^{(s)}(Y)\right\rangle_{2}:Y\in\dom L^{(s)},~\norm{Y}_{2}=1,~Y\perp\rho_{0}^{1/2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{-\real\left\langle\rho^{s/2}_{0}X\rho_{0}^{(1-s)/2},\widetilde{L}^{(s)}(\rho^{s/2}_{0}X\rho_{0}^{(1-s)/2})\right\rangle_{2}:\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.\norm{\rho^{s/2}_0 X \rho_0^{(1-s)/2}}_{2}=1,~\rho^{s/2}_{0}X\rho_{0}^{(1-s)/2}\perp\rho_{0}^{1/2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{-\real\left\langle\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{U}X\mathcal{U}^{*}\rho^{(1-s)/2},L^{(s)}(\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{U}X\mathcal{U}^{*}\rho^{(1-s)/2})\right\rangle_{2}:\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.\norm{\rho^{s/2} \cU X \cU^* \rho^{(1-s)/2}}_{2}=1,~\rho^{s/2}\mathcal{U}X\mathcal{U}^{*}\rho^{(1-s)/2}\perp\rho^{1/2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\gap L^{(s)}.

In the last step, we again used the fact that
\cdot\mapsto\mathcal{U}\cdot\mathcal{U}^{*}
is a bijection on the space of polynomials in annihilation and creation operators.
∎

Appendix C
Drift Matrix of the Dual Gaussian Quantum Markov Semigroups

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Gaussian QMS with a unique faithful normal invariant state
\rho
. We recall that the dual QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
, which describes the evolution in the dual quantum system, is defined through the following relation (see, for example,
[
3
, Theorem 2.1]
and
[
16
, Theorem 1]
):

\Tr\left(\rho^{1/2}\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime}(X)\rho^{1/2}Y\right)=\Tr\left(\rho^{1/2}X\rho^{1/2}\mathcal{T}_{t}(Y)\right),\quad\forall X,Y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h}).

(C.1)

We emphasize that the symbol
′
has nothing to do with derivatives.

Furthermore, the dual QMS is closely related to the adjoint semigroup (with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product) of the induced semigroup
(T_{t}^{(s)})_{t\geq 0}
. Specifically, the relation

T_{t}^{(s)*}\left(\rho^{(1-s)/2}X\rho^{1/2}\right)=\rho^{(1-s)/2}\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime}(X)\rho^{s/2},\quad\forall X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathsf{h}),

(C.2)

can be verified directly and should be compared with (
1.6
). (
C.2
) provides the key to analyzing the adjoint generator
L^{(s)}
and investigating the behavior of

.

It is known that the dual QMS of a Gaussian QMS is also Gaussian. Roughly speaking, if one applies (
C.1
) to compute the explicit form of the generator of the dual QMS, denoted by
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
, one finds that it remains a GKSL generator as in (
1.1
). Its Hamiltonian part is still a second-order polynomial in the annihilation and creation operators, and its Kraus operators remain first-order polynomials in these operators. Although the dual Gaussian QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
shares the same invariant state as
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, the parameters in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
differ from those in
\mathcal{L}
. In principle, all parameters in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
can be computed directly from (
C.1
). However, such detailed information is not needed here. Our main interest lies in the drift matrix of the dual QMS, denoted by
\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}
, since it encodes most of the relevant information about the evolution generated by the dual Gaussian QMS.

Proposition C.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Gaussian QMS with a unique faithful normal invariant state
\rho
, which is diagonal. Then the drift matrix
\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}

\mathbf{D}_{\csch}^{-1}\mathbf{Z}^{*}\mathbf{D}_{\csch}.

Observe that the QMS
(\mathcal{T}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and its dual
(\mathcal{T}_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
share the same invariant Gaussian state. Therefore, by (
