SORDINO: silent, sensitive, specific and artifact-resistant fMRI in awake, behaving mice
Authors: Martin J. MacKinnon, Sheng Song, Tzu-Hao Harry Chao, Li-Ming Hsu, Scott T. Albert, Yuncong Ma, Tatiana A. Shnitko, Tzu-Wen Winnie Wang, Randy J. Nonneman, Corey D. Freeman, Samuel Booth, Siddhi S. Ozarkar, Paul B. Manis, Uzay E. Emir, Eric R. Muir, Mark D. Shen, Benjamin D. Philpot, Adam W. Hantman, Sung-Ho Lee, Wei-Tang Chang, Yen-Yu Ian Shih
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Blood-oxygenation-level-dependent (BOLD) functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) has advanced brain mapping across species. This widely used technique, however, faces challenges such as acoustic noise, electromagnetic interference, motion artifacts, magnetic-field inhomogeneity and limitations in sensitivity and specificity. Here we introduce Steady-state On-the-Ramp Detection of INduction-decay with Oversampling (SORDINO), an improved zero echo time (ZTE)-based fMRI technique that maintains ultra-low slew rate and acquires data exclusively during gradient direction changes. At 9.4 T, SORDINO outperforms conventional gradient-recalled-echo-based echo-planar imaging and ZTE in metrics crucial for fMRI while being silent, sensitive, specific and resistant to motion and susceptibility artifacts. We showcase SORDINO’s superior compatibility with multimodal experiments and reveal T1-based contrast mechanisms distinct from BOLD that are expected to generalize across ZTE-based fMRI techniques. We further demonstrate SORDINO brain-wide activity and functional connectivity mapping in awake, behaving mice, overcoming stress-related, motion-related and sensitivity-related limitations that remain common barriers in current animal fMRI studies.

Gradient-recalled echo (GRE)-based echo-planar imaging (EPI) has been the gold standard in fMRI because of its ability to rapidly acquire effective transverse relaxation time (T2*)-weighted BOLD functional contrast throughout the brain1,2. However, EPI remains susceptible to acoustic noise, electromagnetic interference, motion and magnetic field inhomogeneity, while offering limited sensitivity and poor spatial specificity owing to its bias toward venous vasculature. These limitations are particularly problematic for rodent fMRI3–5 because acoustic noise often forces the use of anesthesia6–8, compromising brain function and limiting translational relevance to human fMRI. In addition, awake imaging requires extensive habituation to mitigate stress and motion9–16, and susceptibility artifacts are amplified at the high magnetic field strengths (>7 T) commonly used in rodent studies5–7.

‘Zero’-acquisition-delay MRI methods, including ZTE and multi-band sweep imaging with Fourier transformation (MB-SWIFT), primarily known for structural MRI applications17–19, acquire data within a few microseconds after radiofrequency (RF) pulsing and offer potential solutions to challenges in fMRI. These methods acquire center-out free-induction-decay (FID) signals under steady gradients and are inherently less susceptible to acoustic noise, electromagnetic interference and common EPI-related artifacts1 ,18. In the preclinical setting, the utility of ZTE variants for functional imaging was first demonstrated by MB-SWIFT in 2017, showing inflow-dependent fMRI responses following deep brain stimulation20. Building on this work, hard-pulse-based radial ZTE was first applied to fMRI with iron oxide nanoparticles in 2020 (ref. 21), followed by the demonstration of intrinsic ZTE-fMRI responses without contrast agents through minimized gradient ramp and settling times and the modeling of multiple potential contrast origins in 2021 (ref. 22) (see detailed implementation in ref. 23). In 2023, we proposed acquiring data during gradient ramping for ZTE-fMRI24. These studies established the feasibility of hard-pulse ZTE for fMRI without the frequency-modulated excitation used in MB-SWIFT, while preserving the core advantages of zero-acquisition-delay imaging.

Although ZTE-based techniques have been adapted for fMRI applications20–29, their sensitivity relative to GRE-EPI has not been systematically demonstrated at commonly used magnetic field strengths, limiting broader adoption because reduced sensitivity requires additional experimental repetitions or larger sample sizes. Furthermore, as these methods are insensitive to BOLD contrast, the mechanisms underlying their functional signal changes remain incompletely understood. Importantly, there remains limited demonstration of compelling applications, particularly in awake and behaving mouse models, whereby these methods would offer distinct advantages over conventional approaches.

In this study, we improve ZTE-fMRI efficiency and address challenges, including acoustic noise, electromagnetic interference, ghosting, motion, susceptibility artifacts, sensitivity and specificity, by introducing SORDINO, named after the mute of a musical instrument. Although existing ZTE techniques acquire data only on steady gradients, SORDINO instead acquires data only during gradient ramps. By maintaining a nearly constant total gradient amplitude and continuously rotating the gradient direction, SORDINO achieves an ultra-low slew rate, improving acquisition efficiency while reducing acoustic noise and gradient-related artifacts. We benchmarked SORDINO against GRE-EPI and ZTE on a 9.4 T preclinical MRI system. We demonstrate SORDINO’s improved compatibility with simultaneous electrophysiology, electrochemistry and calcium imaging at cellular resolution, and model and characterize its contrast mechanisms that are generalizable to ZTE-based fMRI. We further demonstrate applications including mapping naturalistic circuit activity in behaving, head-fixed mice and simultaneously imaging two mouse brains during social interactions. Our findings suggest that SORDINO offers robust sensitivity and holds potential to transform functional brain mapping. We release the SORDINO sequence and accompanying database, providing an accessible resource for new research opportunities and further technical advances.

Results

SORDINO sequence

To date, no MRI technology has achieved imaging by clamping both total gradient amplitude (GTotalAmp) and gradient angular change (α) throughout the entire duration of a scan (Fig. 1a). Our development of SORDINO began with the simple goal of efficiently distributing the gradient changes needed for 3D encoding without rapid gradient switching or resets. Building on this idea, we identified the simplest strategy to achieve 3D encoding with the lowest possible α. After a one-time initialization, GTotalAmp is maintained nearly constant, while α is controlled to yield minimal incremental changes between successive encoding points, resulting in a continuous and smoothly varying gradient trajectory that approaches dGTotalAmp / dt ≈ 0 and dα / dt ≈ 0. This condition is maintained throughout all encoding points and imaging volumes. An analogy for SORDINO’s gradient control to existing radial sampling methods is akin to the smooth, continuous motion of a modern clock’s second hand versus the discrete, ticking motion of a traditional clock. As the gradient direction continuously changes, we apply a phase-incrementing, spatially nonselective RF pulse to induce the FID signal while reducing spurious echoes (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b). The FID naturally realizes ‘frequency encoding’ as a curved, center-out spoke in k-space. Acquiring data during ramping gradient incurs no measurable penalty (Extended Data Fig. 1c,d). Figure 1b illustrates spoke evolution, whereas Fig. 1c and Supplementary Video 1 show that successive spokes and imaging volumes remain continuously connected, eliminating gradient resets. This straightforward approach results in an ultra-low slew rate for fMRI (mean of 0.21 T m−1 s−1), which is four orders of magnitude lower than GRE-EPI (1,263.62 T m−1 s−1) and ~75-fold lower than ZTE (15.66 T m−1 s−1) at identical spatiotemporal resolution (see Methods). Furthermore, by eliminating traditional gradient ramping and settling times (Fig. 1h) while incorporating an oversampling anti-aliasing strategy (Extended Data Fig. 1e,f), SORDINO can achieve a lower bandwidth and maximize acquisition efficiency, resulting in improved signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and fewer samples lost during transmit–receive switching dead time (Extended Data Fig. 1g–i) compared to high-bandwidth ZTE-fMRI methods.

SORDINO contrast modeling

First, we conducted modeling experiments to explore parameters capable of producing longitudinal relaxation time (T1) contrast changes at 9.4 T. These models are expected to be generalizable to ZTE-based methods, capturing inflow-enhanced vascular signal changes and alterations in tissue oxygen (tO2) levels. Conventionally, the inflow effect observed in MRI is primarily attributed to the apparent T1 shortening resulting from incoming spins that experience few or no RF pulses30, which generates a stronger signal than that from stationary tissue. When using SORDINO with head-only RF excitation, frequent nonselective RF pulsing rapidly leads the magnetization of stationary tissue to a steady state (Fig. 1d, green dashed trace); however, inflowing arterial blood experiences fewer RF pulses, resulting in a higher signal than static tissue (Fig. 1d, red trace and blue vertical dashed line). Collectively, the voxel signal stabilizes at a combined ‘steady state’ when the blood transit time and cerebral blood volume (CBV) remain stable (Fig. 1d–f, blue dashed lines), providing the baseline condition for SORDINO-fMRI.

We first modeled the effect of local activation-induced increases in CBV, as it is both the most tractable to simulate and among the earliest physiological responses in the neurovascular coupling process, leading to regional magnetization differences. Given that the arterial blood signal is higher than the tissue (Fig. 1d), an increase in vascular-space-occupancy (VASO)-like contrast31,32 due to arterial vasodilation33, translates into positive SORDINO signal changes (Fig. 1e). Extended Data Fig. 2a,b shows raw SORDINO images of a rat brain in live and postmortem conditions, respectively, with robust inflow contrast present only in the live brain under head-only RF excitation. Extended Data Fig. 2c shows raw SORDINO images acquired at different spatial resolutions.

Next, we evaluated the influence of alterations in tO2 levels. Guided by invasive tO2 ground-truth measurements34,35 and prior studies relating molecular oxygen concentration to the T1 relaxation rate (R1)36, we estimated that changes in tO2 contribute to SORDINO contrast at a similar order of magnitude as CBV (Fig. 1f). Given the localized nature of tO2 and CBV metrics32,33,37–40, SORDINO may provide a compelling alternative to GRE-EPI BOLD-fMRI with improved spatial localization (vide infra).

Regional vasomotor activity may also influence SORDINO signals through cerebral blood flow (CBF)-based contributions (Extended Data Fig. 3). Arterial blood water with higher magnetization (Fig. 1d) is continuously delivered to the tissue, which exchanges during transit through the microvasculature into the surrounding tissue, leading to flow-dependent enhancement of the tissue compartment. Such exchange underlies arterial spin labeling (ASL) MRI41,42, although the more modest magnetization difference between inflowing blood and tissue in SORDINO compared to conventional ASL may limit the extent of this effect. Consequently, tissue magnetization at any given time reflects cumulative contributions from inflowing spins over preceding time points, shaped by continuous exchange between vascular and extravascular compartments. Although our modeling focuses on localized activation owing to its relevance for functional brain mapping, a global increase in blood velocity would shorten transit time and expose blood to even fewer RF pulses, thereby enhancing the SORDINO signal and reflecting global CBF changes. This contrasts with the transient and localized increases in blood velocity during focal activation, in which the number of RF pulses experienced by blood remains relatively unchanged (see Methods for details).

Regardless of the individual contributions from tO2, CBV or CBF, the SORDINO signal reflects vasomotor responses to neuronal activity and thus aligns with the principles of neurovascular coupling2. We expect the contrast mechanisms explored here to also apply to the backbone ZTE method. Notably, such functional signal varies linearly with T1 changes in both the rodent brain at 9.4 T and the human brain at 3 T, supporting accurate mapping of brain activity across the physiological T1 range (Fig. 1g). Using a whole-body coil instead of a head-only coil for RF excitation shifted the dominant mechanism toward tO2, such that CBV increases produced slight negative signal changes owing to the longer T1 of blood relative to brain tissue (Extended Data Fig. 4).

SORDINO efficiency and sensitivity gains from ZTE

For general brain fMRI applications, high receiver bandwidths designed to capture ultrashort T2* components at the expense of sensitivity are unnecessary (Extended Data Fig. 1h). Instead, achieving high sensitivity without sacrificing imaging speed and angular sampling resolution is paramount. As illustrated in Fig. 1h, ZTE acquires data under a steady gradient, requiring additional time for gradient ramping, settling and spoiling, resulting in a short readout duration within each spoke repetition time (TR) and reduced overall efficiency. In contrast to our previous approach to minimizing gradient ramping time in ZTE-fMRI23, SORDINO extends the gradient ramp and acquires k-space data continuously throughout gradient ramping. Combined with RF spoiling (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b), SORDINO achieves higher acquisition efficiency. This design enables lower receiver bandwidths to enhance temporal SNR (tSNR) (Fig. 1h, middle plot and Fig. 1i), or higher receiver bandwidths to acquire more spokes per unit time and thereby improve k-space coverage (Fig. 1h, bottom plot and Fig. 1j). Using a water phantom (Supplementary Fig. 1), Fig. 1i demonstrates SORDINO’s tSNR gain by progressively reducing receiver bandwidth, benchmarked against ZTE, with up an eightfold increase in tSNR under identical spoke TR and matrix size (see also Extended Data Fig. 1h,i). Figure 1j illustrates the trade-off between receiver bandwidth and spoke number in ‘fully loaded’ SORDINO conditions (>95% efficiency, which conventional ZTE cannot achieve). Here, the spoke TR is minimized to match the readout duration for each selected bandwidth, maximizing the number of spokes acquired within a fixed imaging time. Higher bandwidths yield higher spatial resolution, whereas lower bandwidths yield higher tSNR. Collectively, receiver bandwidth should be selected such that the readout fully occupies the minimum achievable spoke TR, maximizing acquisition efficiency and tSNR.

SORDINO acquisition parameters and sampling efficiency for fMRI

To examine SORDINO parameter optimization for fMRI, we modeled SORDINO parameter selection and validated it in vivo. Extended Data Fig. 5a–c shows spoke signal evolution across various combinations of TR and flip angle (FA). Although a shorter spoke TR constrains T1 recovery, it allows more spokes to be acquired within a fixed volume TR (Extended Data Fig. 5d). We thus considered an efficiency factor, calculated as the square root of spoke TR43, to account for the number of spokes acquired within a fixed volume TR. Extended Data Fig. 5e,f shows modeling of fMRI efficiency, and Extended Data Fig. 5g–j summarizes the contributions of T1, CBV, CBF and their combined changes across different TR–FA combinations. FAs ranging from 2° to 5° provided optimal efficiency with short spoke TRs. To validate the model, we conducted forepaw stimulation experiments in rats, assessing contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) across five different TR–FA combinations (Extended Data Fig. 5k,l).

SORDINO versus GRE-EPI

Next, we benchmarked SORDINO against the gold-standard GRE-EPI across various metrics, including acoustic noise, electromagnetic interference, ghosting, distortion, susceptibility artifacts, motion artifacts, sensitivity and specificity. To ensure a fair comparison, we configured the SORDINO and GRE-EPI parameters to achieve identical nominal spatial and temporal resolution (see Methods).

Negligible acoustic noise.

SORDINO demonstrated negligible acoustic noise (Fig. 2a, Extended Data Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Across the recorded frequency range (0.1–100 kHz), the sound pressure level (SPL) profile for SORDINO closely matched the scanner’s idle state, whereas the SPL was higher for both GRE-EPI and ZTE. This acoustic noise reduction, combined with our awake-imaging platform, holds the potential to reduce subject stress and habituation time (Fig. 2b,c). Head-fixed awake mice undergoing longitudinal SORDINO exhibited elevated cortisol levels on the first day of imaging, with cortisol levels decreasing by day 3 and showing no significant deviation from the baseline by day 5. By contrast, awake mice undergoing GRE-EPI scans showed elevated cortisol levels throughout (Fig. 2b). This potential reduction in stress was also evident in body weight measures (Fig. 2c).

Ultra-low electromagnetic interference.

We performed electrophysiological and electrochemical recordings (fast-scan cyclic voltammetry (FSCV))34,35 during active MRI scanning. These experiments were performed in vitro, allowing repeatable ground-truth measurements using a spike signal simulator to generate defined signal transients, or a dummy cell to test system stability without confounders such as physiological noise (Fig. 2d–f). Owing to the smooth gradient transitions, SORDINO induced minimal artifacts in spike recording, allowing spikes to be identified using a conventional online sorting algorithm (Fig. 2e). Additionally, there was no noticeable difference in FSCV readout between SORDINO OFF and ON conditions (Fig. 2f). By contrast, GRE-EPI and ZTE produced substantial artifacts that compromised real-time electrophysiological and electrochemical recordings during fMRI. This drastically reduced electromagnetic interference also enables simultaneous calcium imaging at cellular resolution using a head-mounted miniscope during fMRI (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Video 2). Analysis of calcium signal dynamics during SORDINO OFF and ON conditions showed no significant differences in signal quality (Fig. 2g,h).

Minimized ghosting, distortion, susceptibility and motion artifacts.

Using an established analysis approach, we demonstrated the absence of ghosting in SORDINO (Fig. 3a). We also assessed spatial distortion by comparing data against Cartesian-sampled turbo spin echo (TurboRARE) images, showing negligible distortion in SORDINO relative to EPI (Fig. 3b). To compare susceptibility artifacts, raw images showed that brain regions most prone to air–tissue interface dephasing and signal dropout in GRE-EPI were preserved in SORDINO (Fig. 3c). Regional homogeneity (ReHo) analysis of resting-state data further demonstrated measurable ReHo in the amygdala, a region challenging to assess by GRE-EPI (Fig. 3d). To evaluate motion sensitivity, we remotely induced passive limb movements in deeply anesthetized mice by pulling a nylon monofilament string (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Video 3), which showed markedly reduced unwanted motion correlation in SORDINO compared with GRE-EPI (Fig. 3f). Finally, motion parameters extracted from awake mouse fMRI showed significantly lower motion and framewise displacement with SORDINO than with GRE-EPI (Fig. 3g).

Robust sensitivity.

Using an established forepaw electrical stimulation task in rats, we assessed the CNR. SORDINO consistently demonstrated robust sensitivity to functional activation within the forepaw region of the primary somatosensory cortex (S1), with CNR comparable to, if not exceeding, that of GRE-EPI (Fig. 3h). In another cohort, forepaw-evoked responses acquired under different conditions and acquisition parameters also demonstrated comparable CNR between SORDINO and GRE-EPI (Supplementary Fig. 3). Time-series analysis revealed higher tSNR with SORDINO, supporting its robust CNR through reduced baseline signal fluctuations (Fig. 3i). Seed-based analysis of resting-state data was performed to assess functional connectivity (FC) variation and spatial reliability of the somatosensory homotopic pattern. FC metrics were evaluated as a function of randomly sampled time-series lengths (100 random selections per length), illustrating the dependence of FC performance on scan duration for both sequences (Fig. 3j). Collectively, these data suggest that SORDINO maintains sensitivity comparable to GRE-EPI BOLD-fMRI at 9.4 T while offering additional benefits.

Specific contrast origin.

Our initial modeling suggests that SORDINO is sensitive to CBV (Fig. 1e), tO2-induced T1 changes (Fig. 1f) and CBF (Extended Data Fig. 3). We conducted five experiments to empirically validate inflow and tO2 contrasts and confirm that SORDINO does not carry conventional BOLD contrast. In Fig. 4a, we present data from a flowing phantom inside an RF coil. Given that inflowing spins retain higher magnetization by experiencing fewer RF excitations before signal readout (Fig. 1d and Extended Data Fig. 2a), they generate higher signals than the stationary fluid. This forms the basis for detecting CBF and CBV changes, as increases in either introduce more inflowing spins, enhancing voxel signals through ASL-like effects41,42 and higher vascular space occupancy31,32. In Fig. 4b, we estimate the inflow contribution to SORDINO by comparing forepaw-evoked responses in rats under two RF excitation settings: head-only versus whole-body. Whole-body excitation yielded approximately 40% weaker evoked responses than head-only excitation, consistent with modeling predictions (Fig. 1e,f and Extended Data Fig. 4). Under whole-body excitation, inflow-related blood signal enhancement is suppressed because blood magnetization approaches a steady state below that of gray matter (Extended Data Fig. 4a), causing CBV increases to produce slightly negative signal changes (Extended Data Fig. 4b) and leaving tO2 as the dominant positive signal contributor.

In Fig. 4c, we imaged freshly collected arterial and venous blood samples using SORDINO, GRE-EPI and TurboRARE sequences. The latter two sequences exhibited the typical ‘BOLD contrast’, with higher signals in oxygenated arterial blood, whereas SORDINO showed the opposite, with higher signals in deoxygenated venous blood. This contrast inversion probably arises from SORDINO’s insensitivity to the paramagnetic deoxyhemoglobin (HbR) T2* effects and its efficiency in capturing HbR’s T1 shortening44. These findings indicate that positive responses in SORDINO (Fig. 3h) do not arise from intravascular hemoglobin oxygenation, suggesting a potential solution to overcome the low spatial specificity and draining vein dominance seen in BOLD-fMRI39.

In Fig. 4d, we conducted a gas challenge experiment with head-only RF excitation to dissociate tO2 from CBV. Here, we strategically established a hypercapnic–hyperoxic baseline (10% CO2 and 90% O2), followed by a hypoxic challenge (10% CO2 and 10% O2). These physiological changes were validated by simultaneous measurements of SORDINO signals (head-only RF excitation), oxyhemoglobin (HbO) and total hemoglobin (HbT) concentration changes (indicative of CBV) using an established fiber photometry technique45. The single-voxel SORDINO signals measured at the implanted optical fiber tip closely tracked decreased oxygenation rather than increased HbT, suggesting a non-BOLD (Fig. 4c) yet oxygen-dependent contrast (Fig. 4d).

To further evaluate tO2 effects in vivo, in Fig. 4e, we performed another gas challenge experiment, inducing positive (21% O2 baseline followed by 100% O2 hyperoxia) and negative (10% CO2 and 90% O2 hyperoxia followed by 10% CO2 and 10% O2 hypoxia) tO2 changes while concurrently acquiring SORDINO (head-only RF excitation) and FSCV-derived tO2 ground truth34,35, a setup enabled by SORDINO’s minimal gradient-induced artifacts (Fig. 2f). The single-voxel SORDINO signals measured at the implanted FSCV electrode tip tightly tracked with tO2 levels under both conditions, demonstrating robust sensitivity to tO2 changes. Given that oxygen is predominantly released at capillaries and arterioles37,38, in close proximity to the source of neuronal activity39, and that free molecular oxygen typically diffuses less than 30 μm from vascular sources40, SORDINO may provide improved spatial specificity over GRE-EPI BOLD-fMRI.

SORDINO for resting-state fMRI in awake mice

To demonstrate the feasibility of SORDINO for mapping FC in awake mice, we designed a dual-functional headplate coil using dielectric PCB-board material with insulated coil traces. The coil functions as the RF transceiver while supporting mouse head fixation. Figure 5a shows an awake mouse fitted with the coil, without restraining its body or limbs. Given that the animals were awake and able to move, they were not strictly ‘at rest’ throughout the acquisition, and the data may therefore contain locomotor and related physiological components. Under this setting, subjects completed a 5 day habituation protocol (Fig. 2b,c). Following nuisance signal removal, seed-based analysis using the left S1 and retrosplenial cortex (RSC) seeds revealed the expected FC patterns6,8,9 (Fig. 5b–f). Reliability and specificity analyses (Fig. 5g–i) confirmed robust FC measurements, and independent component analysis (ICA) identified classical ‘triple networks’ of the mouse brain46, comprising the default mode network (DMN), lateral cortical network and salience network (Fig. 5j)47. Furthermore, we transformed the SORDINO data into the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas space48. This transformation facilitates direct comparisons between SORDINO-derived FC and the structural connectivity ground truth (Fig. 5k). The low-stress, distortion-free functional connectome data hold promise for future comparative studies and provide a valuable resource for the neuroimaging community.

SORDINO for mapping skilled motor actions in mice

Motion artifacts and stressful acoustic noise often confine conventional rodent fMRI studies to passive sensory stimulation experiments. In principle, SORDINO’s motion-insensitive (Fig. 3e–g), acoustically silent (Fig. 2a–c) and high-sensitivity (Figs. 1i and 3h,i) features provide a unique opportunity to map complex active behaviors. To test this capability, we designed a pneumatically driven food pellet delivery system (Fig. 6a) and trained mice to reach and grasp a food pellet once every 20 s under head-fixed conditions. This task requires delicate coordination of activity across the brain and triggers a motor sequence initiated by the animal (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Video 4). Figure 6c–e shows brain-wide activity changes and time courses from various regions of interest (ROIs). Activity consistently initiated in the contralateral motor cortex, with concurrent deactivation in the RSC, a key hub of the DMN known to deactivate during task performance49,50. Subsequently, robust activation occurred in the bilateral sensorimotor cortices, dorsolateral striatum, thalamus and cerebellum, followed by a return to baseline. Notably, the activation patterns identified with SORDINO closely align with brain regions known to have a causal role in the control of voluntary limb movements in mice51–53. This represents the first whole-brain map of a dexterous motor behavior in rodents and demonstrates that SORDINO can map active behaviors that are difficult to study with conventional fMRI sequences.

SORDINO for cross-brain coupling during mouse social behavior

Leveraging SORDINO’s resistance to magnetic field inhomogeneity and motion, we developed a platform for simultaneous imaging of two mouse brains during social behavior. This platform includes a 3D-printed cradle that mounts two awake mice face-to-face, separated by a divider plate that is removable from outside the MRI scanner room (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Video 5). Each mouse wears a headplate coil, whereby RF signals are individually transmitted and received, enabling dual-brain ‘hyperscanning’ of two socially interacting mice (Fig. 7b). Upon removal of the divider plate, the subjects immediately display signs of social behavior, most notably through increased whisking (Supplementary Video 5). SORDINO data showed transient activations in the RSC, prelimbic and cingulate cortices of the DMN and the anterior insula of the salience network; and deactivations in primary motor cortex (M1) and S1 forepaw and S1 whisker regions of the lateral cortical network (Fig. 7c). With the divider in place, no significant inter-brain correlation was detected; however, upon its removal, mirrored inter-brain connectivity emerged in the RSC, cingulate, prelimbic and anterior insula, suggesting synchronization of activity between the two brains (Fig. 7d). ROI analysis revealed significant inter-brain, cross-region connectivity among M1, DMN and salience network nodes after divider removal, with no such connectivity while the divider remained in place (Fig. 7e). Divider removal also induced a significant increase in mean cross-brain spatial correlation within the DMN and salience network (Fig. 7f). Voxel-wise analysis demonstrated consistent inter-brain synchronization within the DMN and salience network, aligning with the partner’s RSC, cingulate, prelimbic, anterior insula and M1, but not S1 (Fig. 7g). This pattern suggests that inter-brain synchronization arises from perceiving the partner’s actions and broader brain states, rather than direct sensory coupling. Altogether, these findings establish a platform for measuring and manipulating inter-brain communication.

SORDINO for mouse MEMRI and glymphatic dynamics

In addition to fMRI, we conducted two proof-of-concept studies to demonstrate SORDINO’s ability to capture T1 changes using paramagnetic manganese ion (Mn2+) and gadolinium (Gd)-based contrast agents. Modeling predicted a 1.8-fold gray matter signal increase with Mn2+ enhancement (Extended Data Fig. 7a). By comparing SORDINO images from the same mouse before and 7 days after subcutaneous osmotic mini-pump infusion of MnCl2, we demonstrated robust enhancement in key regions, including the pituitary gland, hippocampus, cerebellum and olfactory bulb, consistent with findings in classical MEMRI studies54 (Extended Data Fig. 7b and Supplementary Video 6).

Next, we demonstrated SORDINO’s ability to image brain-wide cerebrospinal fluid influx using Gd administered by cisterna magna55. Upon Gd injection, SORDINO signals increased first in the cisterna magna, followed by the fourth ventricle, third ventricle, lateral ventricle and cortex (Fig. 8 and Supplementary Video 7). Notably, ventricular contrast reversed from darker than gray matter to brighter (Fig. 8a). Compared to a Cartesian-sampled FLASH sequence at minimal echo time (TE) (Fig. 8b), SORDINO avoided the susceptibility-related signal loss near the injection site, enabling brain-wide signal normalization to the cisterna magna following Gd injection (Fig. 8c), thereby controlling variability from injection differences.

Discussion

We introduce SORDINO. Drawing from musical terminology, SORDINO signifies the muting and damping of vibrations in a musical instrument, reflecting the principles underlying its sequence design. We evaluated SORDINO alongside GRE-EPI, dissected its functional contrast and demonstrated its utility for rodent brain imaging under awake and behaving conditions. These studies suggest SORDINO is silent, sensitive, specific and resilient to artifacts commonly seen in GRE-EPI, enabling new opportunities for functional brain mapping during active behavior.

SORDINO is silent and minimizes electromagnetic interference by avoiding large, transient gradient changes and resets. Within an fMRI experiment, SORDINO maintains continuous gradient motion with minimal angular changes, distributing gradient transitions evenly over time to achieve an ultra-low slew rate (Fig. 1). This design effectively eliminates acoustic noise and reduces an animal’s stress (Fig. 2a–c), while also minimizing electromagnetic interference (Fig. 2d–f). Currently, most simultaneous electrophysiology–fMRI studies rely heavily on postprocessing and/or regression of MRI-induced noise. Our findings highlight the potential of SORDINO to facilitate simultaneous, real-time electrophysiological–electrochemical recordings during fMRI, paving the way for future closed-loop studies. Additionally, the feasibility of performing cellular-resolution calcium imaging during SORDINO-fMRI (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Video 2) holds promise for bringing novel insights into cellular mechanisms underlying large-scale brain networks.

SORDINO is resistant to ghosting, distortion, susceptibility and motion artifacts. Building on a 3D radial sampling strategy, SORDINO densely samples around the k-space center, making motion-related errors spatially incoherent across thousands of spokes. Imaging sequences such as PROPELLER56 or other center-out encoding schemes1 ,18,25 have shown reduced ghosting and motion artifacts. As shown in Fig. 3, SORDINO inherits these artifact-resisting properties. As all data are sampled using frequency encoding within a very short readout, phase errors are minimized, and the encoding bandwidth remains uniform along all spokes. This makes SORDINO insensitive to geometric distortion, enabling studies of brain regions susceptible to field inhomogeneity, such as the amygdala in the rodent brain (Fig. 3c,d). The lack of distortion also reduces compounded errors typically encountered during rigid-body registration-based motion correction.

The most efficient sequence for mapping functional activations should minimize contrast preparation time while maximizing data acquisition. SORDINO represents a great example of this concept by acquiring data throughout the entire TR (Extended Data Fig. 1), and exclusively samples data on-the-ramp to afford low bandwidth acquisition within an extremely short TR (for example, 0.6 ms for all awake mouse studies here). Such a short TR is difficult to achieve with conventional ZTE or MB-SWIFT1 ,18,28 (Fig. 1h) unless gradients are rapidly ramped, which reintroduces acoustic noise, high slew rates and gradient-related artifacts. Our data demonstrated robust CNR compared to GRE-EPI (Fig. 3), supporting the effectiveness of this acquisition strategy while minimizing gradient-related confounds. Furthermore, unlike other short-acquisition-delay sequences that rely on high bandwidth, SORDINO’s flexible design permits two distinct strategies (Fig. 1h): one may opt for a low-bandwidth approach to improve sensitivity (Fig. 1h middle plot and Fig. 1i) and reduce missing samples at the center of k-space (Extended Data Fig. 1g), or maintain a high bandwidth similar to conventional ZTE and instead allocate the additional available time to acquiring more spokes (Fig. 1h bottom plot and Fig. 1j). The latter strategy significantly enhances angular sampling in k-space, leading to improved image quality (Extended Data Fig. 8 and Supplementary Fig. 4).

The effective resolution of an MRI acquisition is determined by its point spread function, which is determined by the k-space sampling pattern and reconstruction57. In this regard, SORDINO is not fundamentally different from other MRI acquisition schemes. Here, the nominal resolution is defined by the maximum sampled k-space extent (kmax; Fig. 1b), corresponding to the conventional field of view (FOV) or matrix definition of voxel size, whereas the effective resolution is determined by the point spread function. In this study, effective resolution was assessed by measuring the full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) using a phantom feature smaller than the voxel dimensions (Fig. 1j and Supplementary Fig. 1). We also quantified the modulation transfer function (MTF) (Supplementary Fig. 4), illustrating trade-offs between spatial contrast preservation, acquisition bandwidth and angular undersampling acceleration. It should be noted that in SORDINO, the achievable kmax is determined by the product of gradient strength and the readout duration of each spoke. Given that SORDINO samples continuously throughout the TR, kmax approximates gradient strength × spoke TR, whereas the temporal resolution (volume TR) is spoke TR × number of spokes. As illustrated in Extended Data Fig. 5d, the same kmax can be reached even when the number of spokes is reduced by half. In practice, the most noticeable trade-offs associated with reducing the number of spokes are not spatial resolution per se but rather CNR and undersampling artifacts (Extended Data Fig. 8). Asymmetric k-space sampling strategies (for example, partial Fourier) are generally not necessary in SORDINO because temporal acceleration can be achieved through sparse angular sampling while still maintaining symmetric and uniform k-space coverage (Extended Data Fig. 5d). This represents an advantage of the SORDINO strategy: even under accelerated acquisitions, k-space coverage remains well distributed, and sampling during gradient ramps specifically mitigates the large, transient gradient steps typically required in sparsely sampled ZTE-fMRI. The curved spoke trajectory used in SORDINO also allows each spoke to traverse a broader region of k-space compared with straight radial spokes, with the extreme case conceptually approaching spiral sequences often used in magnetic resonance fingerprinting58, thereby improving sampling efficiency and coverage uniformity during accelerated acquisitions.

In the case of localized activation, SORDINO demonstrates tO2 or combined tO2 and inflow-related signal changes depending on the RF delivery strategy, setting it apart from conventional BOLD contrast1,2. The insensitivity to HbR’s paramagnetic effects (Fig. 4c), together with the high spatial specificity of tO2 (refs. 25,37,40) and arterial CBV changes33,39, suggests that SORDINO can offer more precise localization of neuronal activity by minimizing the influence of draining veins, a common issue in BOLD-fMRI2,39. Although head-only RF excitation further enhances sensitivity through arterial inflow effects (Fig. 4b), this extra sensitivity gain depends on blood transit times59 because the inflowing blood signal inherently reflects the number of RF pulses received. Notably, SORDINO uses 3D nonselective RF excitation, resulting in an inflowing blood signal that carries a spin history shaped progressively as it enters the coil’s B1 field. This produces a more stable arterial inflow contrast than conventional 2D acquisitions, in which slice-selective excitation makes the signal more susceptible to transient through-plane arterial and venous inflow and associated physiological fluctuations (Extended Data Fig. 9). Our observations support a mixed-mechanism interpretation of SORDINO functional contrast, arising from a combination of tO2 sensitivity and inflow-related arterial CBV–CBF effects, whose relative weighting may vary depending on RF excitation strategy, vascular architecture and physiological state. Dissecting inflow-related CBV and CBF contributions is scientifically important for defining the origins of SORDINO contrast; however, because CBV and CBF are typically tightly coupled, their relative contributions would need to be disentangled through manipulation beyond normal physiological conditions60, and it may be difficult to identify a fixed fractional contribution that generalizes across brain regions or experimental contexts.

It should be noted that although SORDINO offers several advantages, its application to high-resolution laminar fMRI remains challenging, primarily because of the use of nonselective RF pulses and a 3D encoding trajectory, which would require long imaging times if resolution were substantially increased. Higher SORDINO spatiotemporal resolution may be achievable by limiting the spatial extent of RF excitation and reducing the spatial encoding burden (Extended Data Fig. 9). Additionally, although angular undersampling can accelerate acquisition, high undersampling introduces artifacts (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Extended Data Fig. 8) that may be mitigated by advanced reconstruction strategies. In this work, we focus on demonstrating the feasibility of SORDINO and providing a fair comparison against GRE-EPI, while leaving additional acceleration and regularized reconstruction approaches for future studies. Translating SORDINO-fMRI or ZTE-fMRI to human MRI systems61, in which multi-channel coils provide additional acceleration opportunities, could be important for further demonstrating its capabilities and spatial specificity.

Looking forward, we believe that future avenues to further boost SORDINO-fMRI and ZTE-fMRI sensitivity should focus on enhancing blood and tissue contrast (Fig. 1d). Potential strategies include (1) elevating blood signal relative to brain tissue21; (2) reducing the tissue steady-state magnetization at higher magnetic fields, where tissue T1 is lengthened (Extended Data Fig. 10) and blood transit time is preserved; (3) inverting the inflowing blood magnetization, as in ASL41,42, thereby generating reversed functional contrast; and (4) increasing the tissue steady-state magnetization to exceed that of blood, for instance, by Mn2+-induced tissue T1 shortening (with blood Mn2+ cleared) while simultaneously nulling blood signal using whole-body RF excitation, creating a VASO-like condition31,32. All of these strategies are probably worth exploring, as they may further elucidate the contrast mechanisms underlying SORDINO and improve ZTE-based functional neuroimaging. Specifically, the second strategy is particularly attractive to the high-field MRI community because higher magnetic fields enhance T1-based contrast and intrinsic SNR through increased spin polarization and reduced thermal noise62, while the blood transit time and molecular oxygen relaxivity remain relatively unchanged63. At the same time, many of the associated technical limitations of high-field fMRI, including susceptibility artifacts, distortion, eddy current effects and shortened T2*, are largely avoided by SORDINO. The third strategy may be important for human translation at 3 T, as longer arterial transit times cause inflowing blood to experience more RF excitations before reaching the tissue, thereby reducing intrinsic inflow enhancement in the absence of blood signal manipulation.

SORDINO and the awake-imaging platform presented herein have the potential to transform fMRI studies in awake mice, offering opportunities to map brain function during resting-state, naturalistic behaviors, social interactions and glymphatic dynamics, previously constrained by conventional MRI. In a series of four studies, we demonstrated that SORDINO enabled FC mapping in awake mice with significantly reduced habituation time from the month-long process typically required9–11 to just 5 days. The shortened habituation period aligns with what is observed in head-fixed optical imaging studies, in which the animals are not exposed to noisy environments and have the ability to move64. SORDINO further enabled whole-brain mapping of voluntary skilled motor behavior, overcoming traditional challenges of fMRI in moving subjects. As SORDINO contrast depends on tO2, CBF and CBV, physiological and behavioral covariates should be considered or concurrently monitored when interpreting event-related responses. Additionally, simultaneous imaging of two mouse brains during social behavior reveals cross-brain coupling in large-scale brain networks. This platform opens opportunities for future closed-loop and social neuroscience studies. Using Gd contrast, SORDINO provided clear, artifact-free imaging of cerebrospinal fluid influx, distinct from traditional methods that suffer from susceptibility artifacts and Gd-related transverse relaxation time (T2) shortening. Its silent acquisition may further enable glymphatic imaging during awake, sleep, and naturalistic conditions without the confounds of anesthesia.

In this study, we describe the theoretical framework and implementation of SORDINO, identify robust in vivo parameters, benchmark the method against modeled biophysical properties and multiple ground-truth validations and highlight several applications enabled by SORDINO. We trace the contrast origins of SORDINO to tO2, CBV and CBF, providing a better understanding of ZTE-based fMRI contrast. This study introduces a silent, sensitive and specific fMRI method with reduced artifact interference, laying the groundwork for future translation to human MRI systems. In addition to fMRI, the underlying acquisition strategy may also benefit dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI65 and molecular MRI66.

MRI hardware, sequence and modeling

All MRI experiments were performed on a Bruker 9.4 T, 30 cm horizontal bore system (Bruker Corporation), with a BFG 240/120 gradient insert (Resonance Research) and an AVNEO console, configured with a maximum gradient amplitude of 922 mT m−1.

SORDINO sequence.

The SORDINO sequence demonstrated here was initially programmed on Bruker ParaVision 6 and established on ParaVision 360, building upon the default Bruker ZTE sequence. Software and hardware configuration remained unchanged between the two versions, ensuring consistent sequence performance throughout this work. The general framework of the single-spoke acquisition in SORDINO is shown in Fig. 1a. Following a one-time initialization of the encoding gradient, a short, hard RF pulse is applied. This RF pulse, lasting only a few μs, enables a short-acquisition delay.

Clamping the encoding gradient slew rate.

The primary aim of SORDINO was to develop a sequence with minimal gradient switching to minimize acoustic noise and electromagnetic interference. In MRI, fast switching currents induce Lorentz forces in the gradient coils, causing vibrations in the surrounding structures and resulting in the loud knocking sound characteristic of EPI. The rapidly varying magnetic fields induced by gradient switching generate concomitant currents in metallic components such as wires and electrode leads, making electrical recordings challenging without proper artifact regression or interleaving the MRI acquisition. Conventional ZTE realizes low gradient switching by ramping the encoding gradient before RF application while keeping the gradient switched on throughout the acquisition, with only incremental adjustments in amplitude between spokes. By default, the ZTE gradient amplitude on our system (Bruker Corporation) is rapidly adjusted over the configured ramp time, which was 0.21 ms, followed by another 0.21 ms of settling time (see Fig. 1h). This limits the efficiency of ZTE in sampling data along each spoke and constrains the minimum achievable spoke TR, unless they are minimized as we proposed previously23. Although minimizing the ramping and settling time can free up more readout time and improve overall SNR in ZTE, aggressive gradient ramps over very short durations increase gradient slew rate, eddy current induction and mechanical vibration, and inevitably make the sequence more EPI-like. SORDINO takes an opposing strategy and achieves a significantly lower slew rate than ZTE by maintaining a near-constant gradient amplitude and enforcing smooth, minimal changes in gradient direction throughout the acquisition. This was accomplished by having one initial gradient ramp to GTotalAmp and extending the ramp time to its maximum allowable duration (Fig. 1a). In this case, the ramping is no longer inefficient dead time within the spoke TR, but rather overlaps with data acquisition (Fig. 1h). As a result, because the ramping becomes less transient, additional settling time is no longer required. Notably, uninterrupted ramping was not feasible on our system owing to potential memory constraints with the sequencer. Instead, a 10 μs plateau was introduced between spokes before the gradient resumed ramping. The low slew rate strategy of SORDINO offers an additional potential benefit for fMRI. Enabling continuous ramping throughout acquisition eliminates delays associated with switching the gradient ramping on and off, allowing for a lower receiver bandwidth. A reduced bandwidth proportionally decreases noise variance in the sampled data and increases SNR by reducing the thermal noise component of fMRI data70. Furthermore, given that gradient strength decreases proportionally with receiver bandwidth, a lower bandwidth enables sampling closer to the center of k-space (Extended Data Fig. 1g) because fixed delays in the sequence—such as RF pulse duration, ADC initialization and coil ringdown—are independent of bandwidth. Importantly, in addition to this low-bandwidth regime, SORDINO also supports acquisition at higher receiver bandwidths, which enables broader k-space coverage per unit time and can further improve SNR through more efficient sampling (Fig. 1h).

RF spoiling and pulse length.

Given that our goal was to maximize gradient ramping and acquisition duration, we did not use gradient spoiling in SORDINO. Instead, we used RF spoiling wherein the phase of the RF pulse was incremented by 117° upon each repetition of the excitation pulse, which mixes transverse coherence pathways, reducing the formation of spurious echoes and the temporal variability of the signal71. In this work, spurious echoes refer to unintended echo formation regardless of the mechanism. Throughout all experiments, we selected a rectangular RF pulse with a 4 μs length as a compromise between minimizing the acquisition delay, ensuring RF power stability and maintaining sufficient B1 homogeneity (see ‘SORDINO-fMRI B1 homogeneity’ for details).

Spoke oversampling.

On our system, the first sample in all SORDINO acquisitions was corrupted because of a digital filter group delay. With spoke oversampling, each sample represents a smaller time step, meaning that the number of delayed samples corresponds to a shorter duration. This enables the acquisition of samples closer to the center of k-space, mitigating the impact of group delay on image quality. Given that the reconstructed FOV is inversely proportional to sampling distance (Fig. 1b), oversampling provides the flexibility to reconstruct images that mitigate aliasing from unwanted signals outside the nominally prescribed FOV. This is particularly important for SORDINO, which is sensitive to signals from short-T2 materials, such as those from coil insulation materials (Extended Data Fig. 1h). All experiments described in this paper used an oversampling factor of eight as a compromise between data size and mitigating the effect of digital filter group delay.

Comparison of gradient slew rates in SORDINO, ZTE and GRE-EPI acquisitions.

The slew rates of EPI, conventional ZTE and SORDINO were compared based on the acquisition parameters used in rats, as described in ‘Comparison of SORDINO-fMRI and GRE-EPI-fMRI sensitivity’, considering gradient increments in a single direction. The relationship between the change in k-space position, gradient strength and ramp time is expressed by Δkrˆ=γGΔt, where Δkrˆm−1 is the change in k-space position, representing movement along a spatial frequency range; γ (MHz T−1) is the gyromagnetic ratio; G (T m−1) is the gradient amplitude; and ∆t is the gradient ramp time. Rearranging this equation to determine the gradient amplitude required to traverse a specified distance in k-space and normalizing the result to units per second yields the slew rate: SR=ΔkrˆγΔt2. Under selected rat SORDINO imaging parameters, with a TR of 1.82 ms, a temporal resolution of 2 s and an isotropic spatial resolution of 0.6 mm3, the k-space extent is 1,666.67 m−1. As SORDINO encoding progresses from the center of k-space to the periphery, the signal is encoded over 833.34 m−1 per projection, with a ramp time of 1.81 ms. Although the total gradient slew rate and gradient amplitude are tightly controlled across the acquisition of a volume, they are not identical for any given encoding direction. The mean incrementation between each projection is 0.00038 T m−1, corresponding to a slew rate of 0.21 T m−1 s−1. The maximum incrementation between projections is 0.00063 T m−1, corresponding to a slew rate of 0.35 T m−1 s−1.

The conventional ZTE sequence on a Bruker system differs from SORDINO in that the encoding gradients are incremented between successive spokes. With a calibrated ramp time of 0.21 ms, the mean gradient incrementation of an equivalent ZTE sequence is 0.0033 T m−1, yielding slew rates of 15.66 T m−1 s−1, two orders of magnitude higher than SORDINO.

In our rat fMRI experiments using GRE-EPI, a 2D acquisition with an in-plane spatial resolution of 0.6 mm also has a k-space extent of 1,666.67 m−1. The acquisition used a matrix size of 42 and a 50 μs gradient blip between successive frequency encoding steps, resulting in a phase encoding gradient amplitude of 0.018 T m−1 and a slew rate of 355.84 T m−1 s−1. With a 250 kHz receiver bandwidth, sampling 44 points along the frequency encoding direction takes 0.176 ms. This corresponds to a gradient amplitude of 0.22 T m−1 and a slew rate of 1263.62 T m−1 s−1, approximately three to four orders of magnitude higher than SORDINO.

Bloch equation modeling of SORDINO signal.

To understand the characteristics and potential sources of the SORDINO functional contrast, we carried out Bloch equation modeling. The Bloch equations describe how the SORDINO signal evolves over time in response to elements of the pulse sequence and relaxation72. We analyzed the Bloch equations in the rotating frame and did not consider the effect of B1 or gradients on precession. Furthermore, our simulations focused solely on isochromats at the isocenter, disregarding off-resonance precession. With these assumptions, the evolution of the net magnetization vector, M, as a function of time is: M(t)=AM+B, where

• A is a 3 × 3 matrix representing relaxation:e−tT2000e−tT2000e−tT1,

• B is a 3 × 1 vector representing recovery of the longitudinal component of the net magnetization vector towards equilibrium:001−e−tT1.

RF excitation was modeled as a rotation about the y axis: Ry(∅)=cos⁡(∅)0sin⁡(∅)010−sin⁡(∅)0cos⁡(∅)

where:

• ∅ is the FA and Ry is the rotation matrix about the y axis.

Given that the acquisition delay of SORDINO—on the order of microseconds—is negligible compared to the T2* of the rodent brain, the SORDINO signal was taken as the transverse component of M immediately following excitation. Consequently, precession was only considered over the TR. In some cases, as RF spoiling was implemented (Extended Data Fig. 1b), which is expected to significantly reduce the formation of spurious echoes, perfect spoiling was assumed, and the transverse component of M was set to 0 at the end of every TR interval. Putting these components together, the M after n repetitions can be expressed as: Mn+1=ATRRy(∅)Mn+BTR.

Modeling of SORDINO functional contrast.

Bloch equation modeling was carried out to estimate the source of the SORDINO functional contrast, considering changes in inflow-enhanced CBV and tO2 levels (Fig. 1e,f). Given that RF spoiling was implemented (Extended Data Fig. 1b)71, which is expected to significantly reduce the formation of spurious echoes, perfect spoiling was assumed. The voxel signal was estimated by modeling the extravascular and intravascular compartments separately. Assuming the voxel-level T1 of 1,900 ms, based on our own measurements (data not shown), a blood T1 of 2,429 ms at 9.4 T (ref. 73) and a vascular volume fraction of 5%, we estimated that the T1 value of extravascular tissue is 1,878 ms. With head-only excitation, the inflow effect leads to apparent T1 shortening of the intravascular signal, resulting in a stronger signal than the stationary extravascular tissue at steady state, as flowing spins are subjected to RF pulses only when within the sensitivity region of the transmitter. If the arterial transit time represents the duration that flowing blood remains within the sensitivity region of the transmitter before reaching the brain, the blood signal will be in a dynamic state influenced by the arterial transit time, TR and FA of the sequence. Here, we assumed an arterial transit time of 280 ms (ref. 74), a TR of 1 ms and an FA of 3°. We estimated that the voxel signal at baseline comprises 95% extravascular tissue signal at a steady state and 5% intravascular signal in a dynamic state. To model the effect of inflow-enhanced CBV, we assumed a local blood flow acceleration of 1.5 mm s−1 over an activation radius of 1 mm from a baseline flow of 10 mm s−1 (ref. 75). This results in regionally accelerated blood being subjected to 13 fewer RF pulses, along with a 20% increase in CBV. We estimated the effect of alterations in tO2 levels, guided by invasive measurements34 and molecular oxygen spin-lattice relaxivity studies36. Our model restricted the influence of tO2 to the extravascular compartment, as physiological changes in blood oxygenation are not expected to significantly influence T1 (ref. 44). Given that the change in R1 is linearly dependent on concentration and assuming a 30 μM activation-induced increase in pO2 and an R1 of 0.3 mM−1 s−1 (ref. 36), we estimated that the T1 of the extravascular tissue would be reduced by 37 ms. Together, these effects are expected to result in an increase in the SORDINO signal. To model the effect of whole-body excitation, the hardware setup most commonly used in the clinical environment, we neglected the effect of inflow on blood signal in SORDINO contrast. Under this condition, we expect that an increase in CBV would result in a small decrease in the SORDINO signal owing to the shorter T1 of blood compared to tissue. Modeling suggests that the combined effect of CBV and tO2 on the SORDINO signal would result in an increase in signal with magnitude approximately 50% of that observed under head-only excitation (Extended Data Fig. 4). Additional modeling of CBF contrast is described in Extended Data Fig. 3.

TR and FA selections in SORDINO.

To understand how the physical properties of the brain and imaging parameters influence the temporal evolution of the SORDINO signal, we modeled the SORDINO signal from the first excitation to the steady state, assuming perfect spoiling (Extended Data Fig. 5a–c). This was done across five TRs (0.5, 0.7, 1, 2 and 3 ms), five FAs (1°, 2°, 4°, 6° and 8°) and five baseline T1 values (500, 1,000, 1,500, 2,000 and 3,000 ms), while keeping non-varying parameters constant (TR = 2 ms, FA = 2° and T1 = 3,000 ms). Our findings indicated that the steady-state SORDINO signal is sensitive to both TR and FA. Next, we sought to understand how SORDINO contrast varies with these imaging parameters (Extended Data Fig. 5e,f). Although a longer TR increases steady-state signal magnitude, a shorter TR allows for more spokes to be acquired in a given time. This reduces noise variance and is expected to minimize angular undersampling artifacts in SORDINO images. To account for this effect, we modeled contrast efficiency, defined as the raw contrast at steady state divided by TR(ref. 43) at five TRs (0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9 and 1 ms) and five FAs (1°, 2°, 3°, 4°, 5° and 6°). We assumed a 2% reduction in T1 from a physiological baseline of 1,900 ms, with fixed parameters of TR = 0.7 ms and FA = 4°. Together, these models revealed that the sensitivity of SORDINO to T1 changes is dependent on both TR and FA. To identify an optimal parameter range for fMRI, we modeled the SORDINO efficiency for tO2, CBV and CBF changes under the assumption of perfect spoiling, across ten equally spaced TRs between 0.6 ms and 2.4 ms and six FAs between 1° and 6° (Extended Data Fig. 5g–j). The models assumed a 2% decrease in T1 from a physiological baseline of 1,900 ms owing to tO2, based on our own measurements (data not shown), a 20% increase in CBV from a baseline of 5% and a 40% increase in CBF from a baseline of 1 ml g−1 min−1.

SORDINO efficiency with MEMRI.

To guide imaging parameter selection for proof-of-concept MEMRI experiments, we modeled SORDINO efficiency as described in ‘TR and FA selections in SORDINO’ at 9.4 T (Extended Data Fig. 7a). Based on literature findings, we assumed a 50% reduction in T1 (ref. 54) from a baseline of 1,900 ms. SORDINO efficiency was calculated across eight equally spaced TRs and FAs ranging from 0.7 ms to 1.4 ms and 1° to 8°, respectively.

SORDINO-fMRI efficiency as a function of field strength.

To estimate the performance of SORDINO-fMRI at various magnetic field strengths, we calculated efficiency as described in ‘TR and FA selections in SORDINO’ (Extended Data Fig. 10). In all cases, we assumed a 30 μm3 activation-induced increase in tO2 and an R1 of 0.3 mM−1 s−1 (ref. 36) but different baseline T1 values. Guided by the literature, we assumed T1 values of 1,035, 1,272, 1,600, 1,900 and 2,030 ms at field strengths of 1.5 (ref. 76), 3 (ref. 77), 4.7 (ref. 78), 9.4 and 17.6 T (ref. 79), in the rodent gray matter, respectively.

SORDINO-fMRI B1 homogeneity.

To estimate the homogeneity of the SORDINO RF pulse, we used the small tip angle approximation80, in which the excitation profile is modeled as a trajectory through k-space. We estimated the homogeneity region of the transmitter over a distance of ±6 cm from the magnet and transmitter isocenter, corresponding to the reported transmitter 12 cm homogeneity region. Using parameters from the SORDINO sequence in this experiment (see ‘Comparison of SORDINO-fMRI and GRE-EPI-fMRI sensitivity’) with an RF pulse duration of 4 μs and an encoding gradient amplitude of 22.23 mT m−1, we calculated that the FA decreased from 4° at the isocenter to approximately 3.87° at the edge of the transmitter homogeneity region. For comparison, we estimated that the surface transceiver excited signal only with a range of ±1.6 mm. Therefore, the use of a volume transmit coil with SORDINO results in a substantially broader excitation volume, leading to a reduction in blood magnetization. This finding supports our hypothesis that the use of a volume transmit coil will result in a reduced CNR in rat electrical forepaw stimulation experiments, as the diminished inflow effect should weaken the functional signal change relative to the surface transceiver setup.

Phantom experiments and SORDINO sequence characteristics

To generate hypotheses about SORDINO’s ability to detect functional activations and its potential underlying contrast mechanisms, we conducted experiments to assess the temporal properties of the SORDINO signal and evaluate its suitability for functional imaging.

Assessment of gradient ramp time on SORDINO signal.

In preliminary experiments, we assessed whether the strategy of increasing gradient ramp time (that is, reducing slew rate) impacts SORDINO image quality (Extended Data Fig. 1c). Using a homemade 1 cm ID surface transceiver, we acquired SORDINO images of deionized water and CuSO4 phantoms at a 3 s temporal resolution with the following parameters: TR = 1 ms, FA = 3°, receiver bandwidth = 99 kHz and spokes = 3,042. Data were acquired at nine equally spaced ramp times between 0.1 ms and 0.9 ms, as well as an additional acquisition at 0.99 ms. Six repeated measurements of the phantoms were acquired at each ramp time. Image quality was assessed by computing tSNR and CNR metrics.

Assessment of receiver bandwidth on SORDINO tSNR.

After determining that extending the gradient ramping time does not adversely impact data quality, we assessed whether the low receiver bandwidth of SORDINO increases tSNR as expected. We compared SORDINO acquisitions at various receiver bandwidths in deionized water phantoms (Extended Data Fig. 1d) and a perfusion-fixed mouse brain (Extended Data Fig. 1i). SORDINO images of deionized water were acquired using a homemade 1 cm ID surface transceiver at a 3 s temporal resolution with the following parameters: TR = 1 ms, FA = 3°, pulse length = 4 μs, spokes = 3,042, FOV = 40 × 40 × 40 mm3, matrix size = 60 × 60 × 60 and 80 repetitions at steady state at two receiver bandwidths: 32 kHz and 100 kHz. Six repeated measurements of the phantoms were acquired at each receiver bandwidth. The differences between each pair of measurements were tested for normality using a Shapiro–Wilk test, and the difference between the two measurements was assessed with a paired sample t-test. Next, we acquired six repeated measurements of a perfusion-fixed mouse brain at receiver bandwidths of 33, 50, 75 and 100 kHz. The common imaging parameters were: TR = 2.5 ms, FA = 10°, spokes = 1,966, FOV = 40 × 40 × 40 mm3, matrix size = 60 × 60 × 60 and 300 repetitions at steady state. Furthermore, we compared SORDINO acquisitions at various receiver bandwidths in a resolution phantom using imaging parameters approximating our fMRI settings and benchmarked them against a ZTE acquisition obtained with identical parameters, except that ZTE was limited to its lowest achievable receiver bandwidth (250 kHz) for the selected TR (Fig. 1i). Ten repeated measurements were acquired for SORDINO at receiver bandwidths of 57, 74, 89, 104, 125, 156, 179, 208, 234 and 250 kHz. The common imaging parameters were: TR = 0.625 ms, FA = 3°, number of spokes = 3,200, FOV = 40 × 40 × 40 mm3, matrix size = 64 × 64 × 64 and 450 repetitions acquired at steady state. Finally, we examined the effects of receiver bandwidth and spoke number on SORDINO tSNR and spatial resolution under conditions of optimal acquisition efficiency. At a fixed matrix size, we varied the receiver bandwidth while minimizing spoke TR and maintained comparable volume TRs (22.44 s) across conditions by acquiring more spokes at higher bandwidths with proportionally shorter spoke TR. The common imaging parameters for this comparison were: FOV = 40 × 40 × 40 mm3, matrix size = 200 × 200 × 200 and 150 repetitions acquired at steady state. Receiver bandwidths, TRs, FAs and numbers of spokes used in each condition are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Assessment of RF spoiling on FID signal using single-spoke acquisition.

RF spoiling minimizes the unintended magnetization coherence formation, resulting in the generation of echoes, referred to as spurious echoes in this work. We qualitatively assessed the effect of RF spoiling on echo formation by implementing a single-spoke acquisition. This model comprises periodic repetitions of identical RF pulses and gradients, leading to the formation of stimulated echoes at predictable intervals. We chose this model because it reliably produces strong and predictable stimulated echoes, allowing us to assess the maximum effect of RF spoiling. The single-spoke acquisition was implemented by maintaining constant encoding gradient amplitudes. RF spoiling was implemented by incrementing the phase of the RF pulse by 117° upon each subsequent excitation. The acquisition parameters were as follows: TR = 0.517 ms, FA = 4°, 60 samples per projection, 120,760 repetitions, receiver bandwidth = 100 kHz, x andy gradient amplitudes = 1.92 × 10−4 T m−1 and z gradient amplitude = 0.013 T m−1. The FID amplitude was defined as the absolute value of the first sample of each projection that was acquired, following the dead time gap.

Analysis of water phantom data.

Data from the three phantom experiments described above were reconstructed using an in-house script with the Berkeley Advanced Reconstruction Toolbox (BART)81. Python (v.3.7) and the NumPy69, Statsmodels82, SciPy83 and Pingouin84 toolboxes were used for the analysis of phantom data. ROIs were manually drawn using ITK-SNAP85. The effect of ramp time on tSNR and CNR (Extended Data Fig. 1c) was assessed using a repeated-measures ANOVA. Mauchly’s test confirmed that the variances of differences between all groups were equal. Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test was performed for post hoc analysis. tSNR was calculated at the voxel level by dividing the mean signal intensity in each phantom across time by the standard deviation of the signal. CNR was calculated by subtracting the mean signal intensity of the water phantom across time from the corresponding CuSO4 signal intensity and dividing by the standard deviation of the signal across time. The effect of receiver bandwidth on deionized water phantom tSNR was assessed using a two-sided paired t-test (Extended Data Fig. 1d). The differences between each pair of measurements were tested for normality using a Shapiro–Wilk test. A linear mixed-effects model was used to assess the effect of receiver bandwidth on tSNR (Extended Data Fig. 1i). Receiver bandwidth was modeled as a fixed effect, and trial number was modeled as a nested random effect to account for repeated measurements. Tukey’s HSD was performed for post hoc analysis.

Assessment of acoustic noise.

Acoustic noise measurements were taken for the scanner idle state, SORDINO (volume TR = 2,000 ms, spoke TR = 0.625 ms, spokes = 3,200, receiver bandwidth = 62.5 kHz, FA = 3°, matrix size = 64 × 64 × 64, FOV = 25.6 × 25.6 × 25.6 mm3, voxel size = 400 μm isotropic), ZTE (volume TR = 2,000 ms, spoke TR = 0.625 ms, spokes = 3,200, receiver bandwidth = 267.9 kHz, FA = 3°, matrix size = 64 × 64 × 64, FOV = 25.6 × 25.6 × 25.6 mm3, voxel size = 400 μm isotropic) and single-shot GRE-EPI (volume TR = 2,000 ms, TE = 14 ms, bandwidth = 250 kHz, FA = 70°, matrix size = 64 × 64 × 22, FOV = 25.6 × 25.6 × 8.8 mm3, voxel size = 400 μm isotropic) sequences using a ¼-inch microphone (ACO Pacific, 7016) with a flat (±1 dB) response curve up to 100 kHz. The microphone was placed −3 m away from the scanner isocenter. Placement closer to the system affected the microphone response, probably because the metallic elements in the microphone were displaced by the scanner’s B0 magnetic field, changing the sensitivity. The microphone was amplified by 20 dB (ACO Pacific, 4012 with a PS9200 power supply). The microphone sensitivity was measured with a 1 kHz calibrator (ACO Pacific) at both 94 and 104 dB SPL. The sensitivity was checked immediately before and after the measurements were made. The noise floor measurement was made in a custom-built sound booth, with the microphone inserted into the calibrator coupler, but with the calibrator off. The microphone signal was digitized with a Tucker Davis Technologies RP2.1 at 195,312.5 Hz (5.12 μs per point) in 15 s epochs. Measurements for each protocol were repeated two to three times. The digitized signal was analyzed by clipping the first 1.5 s of the recording to remove transients and then converting the recorded microphone voltage to sound pressure (in pascals). The pressure spectrum was computed using Welch’s method (from scipy.signal.welch, scipy v.1.17.1, in Python v.3.13.7) using a Hanning window and 2,048 point segments, and then was converted to dB SPL. For reference, an exemplar mouse hearing threshold curve was extracted from Fig. 3 (control dataset) of a previous publication67, chosen because it is derived from behavioral thresholds over nearly the entire mouse hearing range. Human thresholds were extracted from Fig. 1 of another publication68.

We also repeated the acoustic measurements using an MR-compatible low-frequency microphone (Paladou ASIN B07RHZ2T4C) and obtained spectra both at the scanner isocenter and at the same location where we placed the ACO Pacific microphone for the extended frequency measurements (Extended Data Fig. 6). The microphone was taped to a custom-built MRI-compatible animal cradle and placed at the magnet isocenter. The recorded acoustic noise was calibrated using a frequency response curve with known volume monotones at 80 dB across frequencies of 0.2, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 1.75, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 16, 18 and 23 kHz (Extended Data Fig. 6e). Short-time Fourier transform was applied to convert the raw data into a time–frequency representation. For statistical analysis, data were converted to ΔdB, which was calculated by subtracting the scanner idle state SPL from the SORDINO, ZTE and GRE-EPI recordings (Supplementary Fig. 2). The effect of sequence on SPL was tested using a one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s HSD test for post hoc comparisons.

Assessment of electromagnetic interference using electrophysiology.

Electrophysiological spiking activity signals were generated using a Blackrock Digital Neural Signal simulator (Blackrock Microsystems). The simulator was connected to a homemade MRI-compatible headstage that allows for flexible switching between two modalities: electrophysiological and electrochemical recordings35. The wire junction between the headstage and simulator was attached to a water phantom placed inside the MRI bore using a 3D-printed cradle. The electrophysiological signals were recorded using a 16-channel Blackrock Cerebus System (Blackrock Microsystems). Spiking activity data were recorded at a sampling rate of 30 kHz, with bandpass filtering between 0.25 and 5 kHz, during 1 min periods, with each sequence initiated 30 s after the start of data acquisition. Electrophysiology data were preprocessed using the NeuroExplorer 5 data analysis software package (Nex Technologies). In brief, 1 min voltage versus time courses were plotted to identify the effects of each sequence application. Simulated spikes were sorted and averaged during baseline recording (30 s before sequence initiation) to verify the signal of interest under high magnetic field conditions. Similarly, spikes were sorted and averaged during control and SORDINO conditions.

Assessment of electromagnetic interference using FSCV dummy cell.

A ‘dummy cell’ was fabricated to characterize FSCV signals recorded within the MRI bore while three sequences were applied. The cell consisted of a 30 kΩ resistor and a 0.33 nF capacitor connected in series on a circuit board86, mimicking solution resistance and the double-layer capacitance of a carbon fiber electrode, respectively. During the experiment, the cell was attached to a water phantom, connected to the MRI-compatible FSCV headstage and placed inside the bore. Dummy-cell-based FSCV signals were collected in 30 s recordings per sequence, including periods before and during the sequence application.

Assessment of ghosting artifacts in water phantom.

Ghosting was measured following the guidelines of the American College of Radiology8 ,88 (Fig. 3a). A total of 19 volumes of a deionized water phantom were acquired using a 35 mm volume coil (Bruker Corporation). The imaging parameters for SORDINO were: TR = 1 ms, FA = 3°, receiver bandwidth = 300 kHz, spokes = 2,000, matrix size = 100 ×100 × 100 and FOV = 60 × 60 × 60 mm3. The imaging parameters for GRE-EPI were: TR = 2 s, TE = 14 ms, FA = 70°, receiver bandwidth = 300 kHz, matrix size = 100 × 100 × 100, FOV = 60 × 60 × 60 mm3, slices = 43 and slice thickness = 0.6 mm. Percent signal ghosting was quantified as: G=|(D+V)−(L+R)|2×S, where S is the signal intensity of the phantom, and D, V, L and R are the background noise signal intensities at the dorsal, ventral, left and right sides of the phantom, respectively. The differences between each pair of measurements were tested for normality using a Shapiro–Wilk test, and the difference in ghosting between the two SORDINO and GRE-EPI was assessed using a paired-sample t-test.

Assessment of distortion in phantom.

We quantified the sensitivity of SORDINO and EPI sequences to geometric distortion by comparing them to a Cartesian-sampled spin echo sequence (Fig. 3b), which serves as a reliable ground truth. A spin echo (TurboRARE) sequence is a reliable reference owing to its well-documented insensitivity to static magnetic field inhomogeneities. A spin echo EPI sequence was compared to SORDINO, as GRE-EPI acquisitions exhibited additional distortion. Images of a resolution phantom were acquired using a 72 mm volume coil (Bruker Corporation). SORDINO parameters were: TR = 0.625 ms, FA = 3°, spokes = 31,756, receiver bandwidth = 125 kHz, matrix size = 100 × 100 × 100 and FOV = 40 × 40 × 40 mm3. TurboRARE parameters were: TR = 3600 ms, TE = 31.5 ms, echo spacing = 10.5 ms, RARE factor = 8, matrix size = 100 × 100, FOV = 40 × 40 mm2, slices = 32, slice thickness = 0.4 mm and 32 averages. Spin echo EPI parameters were: TR = 2,000 ms, TE = 14 ms, receiver bandwidth = 259 kHz, matrix size = 100 × 100, FOV = 40 × 40 mm2, slices = 32 and slice thickness = 0.4 mm. For each structure in the resolution phantom, circular features were detected using the Hough transform-based algorithm in T2-weighted TurboRARE, EPI and SORDINO images. Images were manually inspected, and outliers were removed to ensure accurate localization. To assess spatial consistency across imaging modalities, the largest center object in each modality was designated as the reference center. The Euclidean distances from this central object to all other objects within each modality were computed. The T2-weighted TurboRARE sequence served as the standard reference, and the differences in distances between T2 and the other modalities (SORDINO and EPI) were calculated. Specifically, for each corresponding object, the difference between its distance from the center in SORDINO or EPI and its distance in T2 was determined. These distance deviations quantified spatial distortions between imaging modalities. The differences in spatial localization (that is, distortion) between the SORDINO and EPI sequences were assessed using a two-tailed two-sample t-test.

Inflow phantom.

The sensitivity of SORDINO to inflow was assessed using a flowing phantom comprising PE-50 tubing connected at one end to a syringe and filled with deionized water (Fig. 4a) at room temperature. The tubing was placed inside a 35 mm volume transceiver, and SORDINO images were acquired using the default mouse fMRI parameters described below. The experiment followed a block design comprising 450 volumes, with two 1 min inflow blocks starting at the 90th and 300th volumes. During analysis, signals were extracted from 383 baseline volumes and 52 inflow volumes, focusing on the central view of the 3D image at the magnet isocenter. Individual time series were converted to percent signal change by subtracting the baseline signal intensity from the time course, followed by division by the mean baseline signal intensity and multiplication by 100. All data were then normalized to the mean baseline signal intensity across all trials. The effect of inflow on the SORDINO signal was assessed using a two-tailed unpaired t-test.

Freshly collected blood phantoms.

We evaluated the sensitivity of SORDINO, GRE-EPI and RARE sequences to the BOLD contrast by imaging freshly collected arterial and venous blood samples from a single rat, leveraging the natural difference in oxygenation between venous (deoxygenated) and arterial (oxygenated) blood. Blood samples were obtained by catheterizing the femoral artery and vein with PE-50 tubing, pretreated with heparin (1,000 units ml−1, intravenous) to prevent coagulation. Blood was drawn using a 1 ml syringe and immediately placed inside a 3D-printed holder designed to fit within a 35 mm volume transceiver. Low-resolution SORDINO data were acquired with TR = 0.625 ms, FA = 3°, receiver bandwidth = 62.5 kHz, spokes = 3,200, matrix size = 64 × 64 × 64 and FOV = 25.6 × 25.6 × 25.6 mm3. High-resolution SORDINO data were acquired with TR = 1.2 ms, FA = 3°, receiver bandwidth = 73.5 kHz, spokes = 80,892, matrix size = 160 × 160 × 160 and FOV = 25.6 × 25.6 × 25.6 mm3. GRE-EPI was acquired with TR = 2,000 ms, TE = 14 ms, FA = 70°, receiver bandwidth = 250 kHz, matrix size = 64 × 64, FOV = 40 × 40 mm2 and a single 1 mm-thick slice. RARE images were acquired with TR = 2,500 ms, TE = 33 ms, receiver bandwidth = 54.65 kHz, matrix size = 256 × 256, FOV = 40 × 40 mm2 with a single 1 mm-thick slice. In analysis, the signal intensities were normalized to the arterial blood signal intensity. A two-tailed unpaired t-test was used to compare the effect of blood source on signal intensity.

Assessment of receiver bandwidth and angular undersampling factor on SORDINO spatial resolution and noise.

We investigated how characteristics of the SORDINO sequence impact the contrast preservation across spatial frequencies and noise of reconstructed images (Supplementary Fig. 4). A defining property of SORDINO is its efficient k-space sampling by maintaining a constant total gradient angular change, enabling an extremely low acquisition bandwidth, a strategy that increases SNR by decreasing noise variance. However, as a trade-off, a longer readout may introduce spatial blurring owing to signal attenuation from T2* decay during acquisition. Another factor that may affect the spatial resolution of SORDINO images is angular undersampling of k-space. Like all MRI sequences, SORDINO requires a balance between sufficient temporal and spatial resolution for rodent functional brain mapping. To achieve this balance, SORDINO data are angularly undersampled in k-space. However, undersampling may also introduce noise and artifacts into the reconstructed image owing to violation of Nyquist’s sampling criterion. To assess how these factors impact SORDINO spatial resolution and noise, we scanned a custom-made acrylic Derenzo phantom across a wide range of receiver bandwidths and angular undersampling factors. The phantom featured six different hole sizes (4.7, 3.9, 3.1, 2.3, 1.5 and 1.1 mm), arranged in triangular patterns around its center and filled with deionized water. The outer diameter of the phantom was 50 mm (Supplementary Fig. 4). To evaluate the impact of imaging parameters on contrast preservation across spatial frequencies, we computed the MTF. The MTF quantifies the system’s ability to preserve contrast as a function of spatial frequency, providing a frequency-domain measure of effective resolvability rather than intrinsic spatial resolution89. At higher spatial frequencies, the MTF decreases towards zero, indicating poorer definition of small structures. Conversely, higher MTF values at lower spatial frequencies indicate that the sequence effectively preserves larger structures. To quantify how imaging parameters impact noise, we measured the CNR for each hole in the phantom. SORDINO images were acquired using a 72 mm quadrature transceiver with the following imaging parameters: TR = 2 ms, FA = 3°, receiver bandwidth = 100 kHz, spokes = 283,648, matrix size = 300 × 300 × 300, FOV = 60 × 60 × 60 mm3 and repetitions = 4. To evaluate the effect of receiver bandwidth on the MTF and CNR, SORDINO images were acquired at four receiver bandwidths: 78, 100, 150 and 200 kHz. To assess the effect of the angular undersampling factor on the MTF and CNR, images were acquired with undersampling factors of 1, 4, 7 and 10. Python (v.3.7) and the NumPy69, Statsmodels82 and Pingouin84 toolboxes were used for analysis of resolution phantom data. Line profiles were drawn through the phantom in ITK-SNAP85. The MTF was calculated by taking the Fourier transform of line profiles through the center of the phantom holes. The maximum resolvable frequency was determined using Nyquist’s theorem, calculated as f=12Δx, where ∆x is the spatial resolution. The MTF was normalized to 1 at DC, and the resolution was quantified using a threshold at 25% MTF, representing the spatial frequency at which 25% of contrast is preserved. The CNR was calculated as the difference between the mean signal within each phantom hole and the mean signal of a noise ROI, divided by the standard deviation of the noise ROI signal. The effect of imaging parameters on MTF and CNR was assessed with a linear mixed-effects model, with either receiver bandwidth or undersampling factor held as a fixed effect and each hole held as a random effect to account for the four repeated measurements.

To investigate the effects of receiver bandwidth on SORDINO spatial resolution, we scanned a custom-made acrylic Derenzo phantom across receiver bandwidths of 59, 66, 78, 89, 104, 125 and 170 kHz. SORDINO images were acquired with matrix size and FOV fixed at 200 × 200 × 200 and 40 × 40 × 40 mm3, respectively. For each bandwidth, the minimum achievable TR was used, and the FA was set to the corresponding Ernst angle. The number of spokes was adjusted to maintain a constant volume acquisition time of 22.4 s (Fig. 1j and Supplementary Fig. 1). The phantom contained six sets of holes with diameters of 0.5, 0.7, 1.25, 1.55, 1.85 and 2.25 mm, arranged in triangular patterns around the center, along with an outer rim of 0.15 mm thickness, and was filled with deionized water. To quantify effective spatial resolution at each bandwidth, cross-sectional signal intensity profiles were extracted along the outer rim at 1° angular intervals. These profiles were averaged every 60° to improve SNR, yielding six segments for FWHM estimation. As the rim thickness (0.15 mm) is smaller than the reconstructed voxel size (0.2 mm), the measured profiles are dominated by sequence-dependent blurring rather than the physical geometry of the phantom, enabling empirical estimation of effective spatial resolution. To further evaluate the effect of angular undersampling across different nominal SORDINO resolutions, the same phantom was scanned at isotropic resolutions of 0.2, 0.4 and 1 mm (TR = 1.166, 0.625 and 0.625 ms; FA = 1.9°, 1.4° and 1.4°; FOV = 40 × 40 × 40 mm3) under full sampling as well as 2×, 4× and 8× angular undersampling (Extended Data Fig. 8). Each condition was repeated 50 times and averaged to minimize SNR-related confounds in resolution assessment.

Rat experiments

Anesthetized rat MRI preparation.

We followed established protocols for rat MRI procedures4,8,49,50,90–93. Rats were anesthetized with 4% isoflurane in atmospheric air and orally intubated with a 13-gauge catheter secured with surgical tape and sutures around the cheek. While under 2% isoflurane, PE-50 tubing lines were inserted into the lateral caudal vein using a 24-gauge catheter (Terumo) for anesthesia delivery. Rats were then secured in a custom-built cradle for imaging and were maintained on a sedation protocol comprising 0.5% isoflurane with a constant infusion of dexmedetomidine (0.05 mg kg−1 h−1) and pancuronium bromide (0.5 mg kg−1 h−1). Inhaled isoflurane was gradually reduced from 2% to 0.5% over the course of 30 min. Rats were allowed 50 min to establish stable physiological parameters before acquisitions began. During experiments, rats were mechanically ventilated (MRI-1 Ventilator; CWE), end-tidal CO2 (ETCO2) was measured by a capnometer (Surgivet v9004; Smith Medical), oxygen saturation (SpO2) was measured by a pulse oximetry sensor (V1703; Smith Medical) on the foot and temperature was monitored by a rectal probe and maintained by a circulating warm water bath (Thermo Scientific). During experiments, ventilator flow rate, tidal volume, inspired oxygen concentration and water bath temperature were adjusted to maintain rats in a healthy and stable physiological condition (ETCO2 = 2.7–3.3%, SpO2 > 92%, rectal temperature = 37 °C).

SORDINO contrast in live and postmortem rat brain.

To assess the SORDINO contrast in vivo, inspired by seminal experiments investigating the BOLD contrast94, we acquired SORDINO images of the rat brain before and immediately after killing the subject with pentobarbital (120 mg kg−1 intraperitoneally), followed by shutting off the ventilator inside the magnet. SORDINO data were acquired at 0.25 × 0.25 × 0.25 mm3 isotropic spatial resolution with the following parameters: TR = 0.62 ms, FA = 3°, spokes = 65,140, receiver bandwidth = 131.58 kHz, matrix size = 144 × 144 × 144 and FOV = 36 × 36 × 36 mm3.

Forepaw electrical stimulation in rats.

Unilateral forepaw stimulation was delivered to the right forepaw of anesthetized rats95–99. Two 27-gauge needle electrodes were inserted between digits II and III, and III and IV, and then secured with surgical tape. Stimulation current accuracy was verified using a multimeter before electrode insertion. The current was delivered by a stimulus isolator (A385RC, World Precision Instruments), triggered in synchronization with the waveform generator by a transistor–transistor logic signal at the start of the fMRI acquisition. The stimulation parameters were set as follows for all experiments: current = 2 mA, pulse width = 0.5 ms and frequency = 9 Hz. A block-design paradigm was used, comprising 10 s of dummy scans to allow magnetization to reach a steady state, followed by an initial 60 s rest period and five repetitions of a 30 s stimulation period alternating with 90 s rest periods.

Validating TR and FA selection in SORDINO.

Our Bloch equation modeling identified a range of parameters with the strongest sensitivity for fMRI. We carried out electrical stimulation of the rat forepaw using SORDINO parameter choices to validate our model at a 2 s temporal resolution and an isotropic spatial resolution of 0.6 mm in six rats. All images were reconstructed to an isotropic matrix size of 70 × 70 × 70 and FOV of 42 × 42 × 42 mm3 and acquired using a homemade 1 cm ID surface transceiver. The parameter pairs examined were: (1) TR = 0.60 ms, FA = 3°, bandwidth per pixel = 905.8 Hz, spokes = 3,330; (2) TR = 1.82 ms, FA = 4°, bandwidth per pixel = 284 Hz, spokes = 1,098; (3) TR = 2.44 ms, FA = 5°, bandwidth per pixel = 208 Hz, spokes = 888; (4) TR = 2.44 ms, FA = 2°, bandwidth per pixel = 208 Hz, spokes = 888; and (5) TR = 0.60 ms, FA = 5°, bandwidth per pixel = 905.8 Hz, spokes = 3,330. Rat physiological conditions were recorded before each of the SORDINO and GRE-EPI acquisitions. Physiological parameters are expressed as mean ± s.e.m.: SpO2 = 93 ± 1%, ETCO2 = 3.00 ± 0.1% and rectal temperature = 37.1 ± 0.45 °C.

Comparison of SORDINO-fMRI and GRE-EPI-fMRI sensitivity.

We compared the sensitivity of SORDINO with an optimized GRE-EPI protocol8 in fMRI experiments using a rat forepaw electrical stimulation model at identical temporal (2 s) and spatial (0.6 mm isotropic) resolutions in 16 rats using a homemade 1 cm ID surface transceiver for acquisition. SORDINO was acquired with TR = 1.82 ms, FA = 4°, bandwidth per pixel = 284 Hz, spokes = 1,098, reconstructed matrix size = 70 × 70 × 70. The FOV was reconstructed to 42 × 42 × 42 mm3 to minimize the most severe aliasing artifacts from signal outside the rat brain, which acted to reduce tSNR. GRE-EPI was acquired with a 2D encoding trajectory: TR = 2 s, TE = 14 ms, FA = 70°, bandwidth per pixel = 5,208.33 Hz, matrix size = 48 × 48, FOV = 28.8 × 28.8 mm2, slices = 32 and slice thickness = 0.6 mm. The rats’ physiological conditions were recorded before each of the SORDINO and GRE-EPI acquisitions. Physiological parameters are expressed as mean ± s.e.m.: SpO2 = 95 ± 1%, ETCO2 = 2.9 ± 0.1% and rectal temperature = 37.7 ± 0.40 °C.

Data preprocessing.

All SORDINO and GRE-EPI datasets underwent minimal preprocessing using the Analysis of Functional NeuroImages software suite (AFNI)100,101 to ensure that the evoked responses were compared with minimal confounding influences. The datasets were motion-corrected using the 3dvolreg command. GRE-EPI data underwent slice-timing correction using the 3dTshift command. Next, mean images were calculated from each acquisition and skull-stripped. One mean image from each subject was used to generate a study-specific template using the AntsMultivariateTempalteConstruction2 function from the Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) package102. Mean images were then aligned to the template using a series of transformations: first with rigid-body alignment, followed by affine transformation and finally with non-linear SyN transformations using the ANTsRegistration function.

Analysis of SORDINO and GRE-EPI forepaw electrical stimulation data.

Python (v.3.7) and the NumPy toolboxes were used for analyzing minimally preprocessed and skull-stripped SORDINO and GRE-EPI data. Individual subject-level fMRI activity was analyzed using a generalized linear model, which was solved algebraically. To minimize biases from canonical hemodynamic response functions, the hemodynamic response function was empirically estimated as the average across all subjects for both SORDINO and GRE-EPI. Generalized linear model analysis incorporated regressors for a constant, first-order and second-order polynomials, and a stimulation paradigm represented by four box-car functions with variable delays extending up to three volumes post-stimulation onset. The beta value corresponding to the maximum response for each voxel was selected for further analysis. Active voxels were defined using a threshold of q < 0.001 following false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Time courses from active voxels were subsequently extracted from the S1 forepaw region after removing first-order and second-order polynomial trends. The same analysis was repeated for SORDINO and GRE-EPI data, replacing the box-car function with the estimated hemodynamic response functions. Sensitivity was assessed by comparing z-scores, calculated as the difference between the time-course signal and the mean signal intensity of the initial rest period, divided by the standard deviation of the same rest period. The CNR was defined as the average peak z-score during the stimulation period at the individual voxel level. Percent signal change was calculated similarly by subtracting the mean signal intensity of the initial rest period from the time course, dividing by the mean rest-period intensity and multiplying by 100. In the TR and FA selection validation experiments, the effect of parameter combinations on CNR was assessed using a linear mixed-effects model, with parameter combination as a fixed effect and subject as a random effect to account for five repeated measurements per subject. Similarly, in the sensitivity comparison experiments, a linear mixed-effects model was used to assess the effect of sequence on CNR, with sequence as a fixed effect and subject as a random effect. In the head-only versus whole-body excitation experiments, the five repeated CNR measurements of each subject were averaged. To test whether the paired differences in percent signal change between the two coil configurations followed a normal distribution, a Shapiro–Wilk test was conducted. A paired t-test was then conducted to compare the sensitivity between the two coil configurations.

Comparison of SORDINO and GRE-EPI resting-state FC.

A subset of nine rats from the forepaw stimulation experiments underwent 15 min resting-state scans with identical imaging parameters. All analyses were conducted on minimally processed SORDINO and GRE-EPI data. ReHo analysis88,103 was performed, with mean ReHo values computed for the sensorimotor cortex and amygdala. To compare the temporal variability of FC and the spatial similarity of FC maps between SORDINO and GRE-EPI, the bilateral M1 was selected as the seed region for evaluating tSNR and FC. For tSNR calculation, the mean time series of all voxels within the bilateral M1 seed region was extracted. tSNR was computed as the ratio of the mean signal intensity to the standard deviation across time points within each temporal window. Sliding windows of varying lengths (ranging from ten to 440 time points in increments of ten) were applied. For each window length, 100 randomly selected time segments were analyzed and tSNR was computed for each windowed segment. For FC analysis, the mean time series of all voxels within the M1 seed was extracted, and the Pearson correlation coefficient was computed between the seed time series and the whole brain on a voxel-by-voxel basis. Sliding windows of varying lengths (ranging from ten to 440 time points in increments of ten) were applied. For each window length, 100 randomly selected time segments were analyzed, with FC values z-transformed, and their variability was estimated as the standard deviation across windows. To evaluate spatial similarity of FC, the right M1 was used as the seed region. The mean time series of the right M1 was taken as the seed, and voxel-wise Pearson correlation was performed between the seed and all other voxels within the brain. As in the analysis of the temporal variability of FC, temporal windows of varying lengths were applied, and for each window length, 100 randomly selected time segments were analyzed. Spatial similarity was assessed by computing the Pearson correlation between each windowed FC map and the full-time FC map. A two-tailed paired t-test was used to assess the effect of sequence on ReHo in the amygdala.

SORDINO contrast mechanism in vivo: comparison of head-only and whole-body excitation.

After confirming that SORDINO detects functional activations in rats with similar sensitivity to GRE-EPI, we designed experiments to test our hypothesis that the SORDINO contrast arises from a combination of inflow-weighted CBV and tO2. As a first step in validating the SORDINO contrast mechanism in vivo, we aimed to determine the fractional contributions of CBV and tO2 by comparing the evoked response magnitudes under two conditions: head-only and whole-body excitation. Whole-body excitation significantly reduces inflow-related vascular signal enhancement, causing all blood to reach a similar steady state as stationary tissue. In this condition, an increase in CBV is expected to cause a modest decrease in the SORDINO signal. On the other hand, with head-only excitation, blood is excited only while flowing within the sensitivity region of the transmitter. Based on published arterial transit times74, blood will remain in a dynamic state, leading to an enhanced vascular signal compared to whole-body excitation. Here, an increase in CBV is expected to enhance the SORDINO signal. For both the head-only and whole-body excitation conditions, the same homemade 1 cm ID surface coil functioned as a transceiver and receiver, respectively. For whole-body excitation, a 72 mm volume transmitter (Bruker Corporation) was activated while the surface coil was decoupled. The coil configuration was switched without repositioning the animal or the coils. By comparing the evoked response amplitude between the two conditions, we estimated the fractional contribution of CBV and tO2 to the SORDINO contrast. Acquisitions were performed in a randomized order in six rats using the rat SORDINO imaging parameters described above.

SORDINO contrast mechanism in vivo: FSCV.

For in vivo FSCV experiments, polyimide/fused silica carbon fiber microelectrodes were fabricated following established methods34,104,105. In brief, a 7 μm-diameter carbon fiber (Thornel T-650) was threaded through a silica/polyimide capillary (1,068,150,381; Polymicro Technologies) and sealed with clear epoxy. Approximately 100 μm of carbon fiber protruded from one end of the capillary to serve as the active surface area. On the opposite end, carbon fibers were adhered to a silver connection wire using consecutive layers of silver epoxy, silver paint and clear epoxy. The electrodes were chronically implanted into the ventral striatum (coordinates: 1.3 mm anterior–posterior, 2.0 mm medial–lateral, 6.8–7.8 mm dorsal–ventral relative to bregma) 4 days before the experimental data acquisition to ensure post-surgical recovery. In addition to the carbon fiber electrodes, an MRI-compatible guide cannula (BASi Research Products) was implanted in the opposite hemisphere. The guide cannula was used for placing a freshly chloridized Ag/AgCl reference electrode on the day of FSCV recording. For FSCV experiments in MRI, data were acquired using High-Definition Cyclic Voltammetry and Analysis software, respectively (UNC Electronics Facility, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill). Custom instrumentation from the UNC Electronics Facility was used, including an MRI-compatible headstage connected to the chronically implanted electrodes with a triaxial cable. An oxygen-sensitive voltage waveform106 was scanned at the electrode surface at 400 V s−1 and a rate of 10 Hz (ref. 107). The waveform first scans from 0 V to +0.8 V to oxidize dopamine, and then down to −1.4 V to reduce molecular oxygen and finally returns to 0 V (Fig. 4e). Current versus time traces recorded at the reduction currents of the analytes of interest were extracted and converted to concentration using fiber length-normalized in vitro calibration factors acquired previously (−0.19 nA μM−1 for oxygen34) at fused silica capillary fiber microelectrodes.

SORDINO contrast mechanism in vivo: spectral fiber photometry.

An established spectral fiber photometry system was used45,50,108–110. A 561 nm CW laser (OBIS 561 LS-50, Coherent) was directed into a fluorescence cube (DFM1, Thorlabs). Neutral density filters (NEK01, Thorlabs) placed before the fluorescence cube adjusted the laser power. The fluorescence cube incorporated a dichroic mirror (ZT488/561rpc, Chroma Technology Corporation) to reflect and direct the laser beam through an achromatic fiber port (PAFA-X-4-A, Thorlabs) into a 105/125 μm core/cladding multi-mode optical fiber patch cable. The distal end of this cable was connected to an implanted optical fiber probe, enabling both excitation laser delivery and emission fluorescence collection. Emission fluorescence traveled back along the patch cable to the fluorescence cube, passed through the dichroic mirror and an emission filter (ZET488/561 m, Chroma Technology Corporation) and was directed by an aspheric fiber port (PAF-SMA-11-A, Thorlabs) into an AR-coated 200/230 μm core/cladding multi-mode patch cable (M200L02S-A, Thorlabs). This cable delivered the fluorescence to a spectrometer (QE Pro-FL, Ocean Optics) for spectral data acquisition, controlled by OceanView software (Ocean Optics). To minimize background light, the scan room was darkened before each session, and laser power was adjusted to balance spectral amplitudes (maximum of <100 μW). A background spectrum was recorded and subtracted during acquisition. Spectral fiber photometry data were collected at 10 Hz. Using an established workflow in MATLAB (v.R2024b, MathWorks)45,108, the TdTomato emission spectrum was processed to derive HbT, HbO and HbR based on known molar extinction coefficients, photon path lengths from Monte Carlo simulations and spectral data. The generalized method of moments was used to calculate molar concentration changes for HbO and HbR, with HbT determined as their sum. HbO and HbT changes were z-normalized for group-level analyses.

SORDINO contrast mechanism in vivo: concurrent SORDINO-FSCV and SORDINO-photometry.

We aimed to measure SORDINO signals during gas challenge experiments alongside ground-truth photometry measurement of hemodynamic parameters and CBV, as well as FSCV measurement of tO2. The experiments were designed to dissociate changes in tO2 from CBV. Four rats underwent simultaneous SORDINO acquisitions and spectral fiber photometry, while three rats underwent simultaneous SORDINO and FSCV recordings. All gas challenge trials followed a block design, consisting of 300 s of baseline gas inhalation, followed by 300 s of a gas challenge, repeated three times. SORDINO imaging parameters were TR = 1.79 ms, FA = 4°, spokes = 1,098, bandwidth per pixel = 309 Hz, reconstructed matrix size = 70 × 70 × 70 and reconstructed FOV = 42 × 42 × 42 mm3. Rats were prepared for MRI as outlined in the ‘Anesthetized rat MRI preparation’ section, with differences described in the relevant sections.

In spectral fiber photometry, changes in HbT (a.k.a. CBV), HbO and HbR were computed using our previously published spectral fiber photometry approach45. TdTomato was expressed under the CAG promoter in the rat S1 (coordinates: 1 mm anterior–posterior, 4 mm medial–lateral, 0.9 mm dorsal–ventral) by AAV (59462-AAV9, Addgene) microinjection, followed by a 3 week incubation for viral expression. Concurrent SORDINO-fMRI and spectral fiber photometry recordings during a gas challenge were performed (Fig. 4d). The experiment investigated the hemodynamic response to different inhaled gas conditions. Initially, rats were ventilated with a hypercapnic-hyperoxic mixture (10% CO2 and 90% O2), which was expected to cause vasodilation compared to when breathing atmospheric air. During the gas challenge, the inhaled mixture was switched to a hypercapnic-hypoxic mixture (10% CO2 and 10% O2), which we expected to reduce tO2 and further increase CBV owing to hypoxia-induced vasodilation111.

In FSCV, a silica-based carbon fiber electrode was chronically implanted into the ventral striatum. The voltammetric electrodes were connected to an MRI-compatible FSCV headstage with a triaxial cable34, and the animal was positioned inside the MRI scanner. An N-shaped waveform was applied to detect changes in tO2 levels. This oxygen-sensing waveform consisted of an 11 ms scan holding at 0 V, ramping up to +0.8 V, descending to −1.4 V and returning to 0 V at a scan rate of 400 V s−1 versus Ag/AgCl (refs. 104,106,107). The waveform was applied at 10 Hz, with data sampled at 100 kHz. Two different gas challenge experiments were carried out with simultaneous SORDINO and FSCV. Each experiment was designed to elicit an opposite change in tO2: one an increase and another a decrease. In the first experiment, rats initially inhaled baseline compressed room air (21% O2), which was then switched to a hyperoxic mixture (100% O2) to induce an increase in tO2. In the second experiment, rats first inhaled a hypercapnic-hyperoxic baseline (10% CO2 and 90% O2) before switching to a hypercapnic-hypoxic mixture (10% CO2 and 10% O2) to induce a decrease in tO2.

SORDINO data were reconstructed using an in-house tool incorporating the mri-nufft112 Python module and finufft113,114 as the backend. All data were analyzed in MATLAB (v.R2024a). Time courses from a voxel at the tip of the fiber-photometry fibers were converted to z-scores by subtracting the mean signal intensity of the baseline period from the time course and dividing by the standard deviation of the same baseline period. Simultaneously collected FSCV and SORDINO data were normalized to the mean of nine trials from three subjects. All time courses were aligned to a time corresponding to the beginning of the gas challenge paradigm.

Mouse experiments

Awake mouse fMRI preparation: implantable headplate coil.

We developed a head-fixed setup for awake mouse imaging using a sterile, implantable PCB headplate designed for both head stabilization and as a transceiver for imaging. The headplate, measuring 1.2 × 2.8 × 1.5 cm, featured a 0.9 cm circular opening at the center for recording probe implantation. The PCB incorporated copper circuit traces (short axis: 15 mm; long axis: 15 cm; 1 mm trace width with a thickness of 70 μM), pre-printed on both sides and insulated by a 10 μM soldermask layer. This headplate coil functioned as an RF transceiver or receiver, depending on the resonance circuit board connected. As a head-fixation system, the headplate locked into two securing plates attached to the animal cradle and interfaced with a custom resonance circuit board with a two-pin socket connection. Given that the animals were unanesthetized during imaging, physiological parameters such as SpO2, ETCO2, heart rate and respiration rate were not recorded, as they were maintained through natural homeostatic regulation. Mice exhibited minimal signs of stress, indicating effective habituation to the MRI environment (Supplementary Video 4).

Awake mouse fMRI preparation: headplate implantation procedures.

The implantation was performed during the same surgical session as the virus injections and GRIN lens implantation. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (1–1.5%) in medical air. The head was stabilized in a stereotaxic frame, and if no existing incision from a prior procedure was present, local anesthesia (0.1 ml, 0.5% bupivacaine subcutaneous) or lidocaine spray was applied before carefully removing a piece of scalp (<1 cm × 1 cm) to expose the skull. The headplate was secured trace-side down using a C&B Metabond Quick Adhesive Cement (Parkell). The skull was cleaned with Bactine followed by application of dental etching gel for 5–10 min and then rinsed again with Bactine. The headplate was then positioned, and adhesive cement was applied to seal the incision and secure the headplate to the skull. Post-surgical care comprised saline (1.5 ml, subcutaneous) for hydration and access to hydrogel inside the home cage. Meloxicam (0.1 mg kg−1, oral) was provided for 2–3 days post surgery as needed. Lincomycin hydrochloride (30 mg kg−1, intramuscular) was administered to prevent infection. Mice were allowed at least 1 week to recover before further experimental procedures.

Awake mouse fMRI setup and SORDINO parameters.

The headplate coil was mounted directly atop the mouse skull as described above. The mouse was positioned on a custom-built cradle, and the headplate coil was connected to a circuit board. Once secured to the holder, the mouse was allowed to walk freely on a custom-built, MR-compatible treadmill, a strategy used in head-fixed two-photon imaging studies to reduce stress-related confounds115,116. A high-resolution SORDINO image was acquired as a reference with the following parameters: TR = 5 ms, FA = 7°, spokes = 80,876, receiver bandwidth = 75 kHz, matrix size = 160 × 160 × 160 and FOV = 25.6 × 25.6 × 25.6. Functional imaging with SORDINO was performed using the following parameters: spoke TR = 0.625 ms, FA = 3°, spokes = 3,200, receiver bandwidth = 62.5 kHz, matrix size = 64 × 64 × 64 and FOV = 25.6 × 25.6 × 25.6 mm3 for single-animal fMRI and 25.6 × 25.6 × 64 mm3 for dual-animal hyperscanning. We selected a spoke TR of 0.625 ms, which is close to the shortest stable TR achievable with the current AVNEO hardware and allows the acquisition of 3,200 spokes within exactly 2 s. A receiver bandwidth of 62.5 kHz was then chosen, providing the optimal tSNR with high acquisition efficiency. This acquisition is referred to as the default SORDINO mouse fMRI parameters throughout the study.

Serum cortisol level.

To evaluate stress in awake mice undergoing SORDINO and GRE-EPI, serum cortisol levels were measured during habituation (Fig. 2b). Mice were divided into three cohorts: 32 mice undergoing habituation with SORDINO, six mice with GRE-EPI117 and an age-matched baseline control group (49 mice) corresponding to day 1 of habituation. Serum samples were collected from the submandibular gland of mice on days 1, 3 and 5 using Microtainer Tubes with Serum Separator Additive and Microgard Closure (BD, 365967). Blood was allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 2,000g for 10 min to separate the serum, which was then stored at −80 °C until analysis. Cortisol levels were quantified using the Cortisol Parameter Assay Kit (KGE008B, R&D Systems, Bio-Techne) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and cortisol concentrations were calculated using a standard curve. A one-way ANOVA tested the effect of sequence on serum cortisol levels, followed by Tukey’s HSD for post hoc comparisons.

Body weight.

The weights of a subset of 15 and six C57BL/6J mice undergoing habituation with SORDINO and GRE-EPI, respectively, were measured on days 1, 3 and 5 of habituation (Fig. 2c). A repeated-measures ANOVA was used to test the effect of habituation day on body weight. Tukey’s HSD was used for post hoc comparisons.

Miniscope calcium imaging.

We performed calcium imaging in an awake mouse with an in-house-modified MRI-compatible miniscope system (base model: nVista system, Inscopix) (Fig. 2g,h). A ProView Integrated GRIN lens (Inscopix) with a 0.6 mm diameter was surgically implanted into the medial prefrontal cortex (2.0 mm anterior–posterior, 0.35 mm medial–lateral, −2.1 mm dorsal–ventral), positioned approximately 0.1 mm above the virus injection site, following AAV-CaMKIIα-jGCaMP8m injection. The virus was injected at a volume of 1 μl at a flow rate of 0.1 μl min−1, with an additional 10 min diffusion period before slow retraction of the microsyringe needle. Animal preparation was as described for awake mouse fMRI, and imaging was acquired using the default mouse SORDINO imaging parameters. Data were recorded over a 10 min period, with equal durations of scanner idle state and SORDINO acquisition. Raw calcium miniscope data were preprocessed using Inscopix Data Processing Software, which included motion correction, spatial filtering, ΔF/F normalization and principal component analysis (PCA)/ICA for neuronal component identification.

Assessment of motion artifacts.

The sensitivity of SORDINO and GRE-EPI to motion was evaluated in two experiments. In the first experiment, the impact of motion on SORDINO and GRE-EPI data was compared in an anesthetized mouse using controlled motion. The setup for MRI was as described in ‘Awake mouse fMRI setup and SORDINO parameters’ above, except that the mouse was anesthetized with pentobarbital (50 mg kg−1, intraperitoneal). A nylon monofilament string was tied around the right forepaw, allowing controlled movement from the console workstation during scanning. The experiment followed a block design, alternating between 10 s of rest and 10 s of controlled forepaw movement at a frequency of 1 Hz, repeated nine times. In the second experiment, 30 awake mice underwent both SORDINO and GRE-EPI scans for motion assessment. Images from each time series were aligned to the first volume, which was used as the reference, using AFNI’s 3dvolreg command. This command generated six motion parameters: three for rotation (roll, pitch and yaw) and three for displacement (inferior–superior, left–right and anterior–posterior). Python (v.3.7) with NumPy69, MATLAB (v.R2024a) and GraphPad Prism 10 were used for data analysis.

To compare the effect of SORDINO and GRE-EPI on motion parameters, motion for each subject was quantified as the mean absolute value of rotation and displacement across the time series. Paired t-tests were performed to compare the effect of imaging sequence on each of the six motion parameters. In the controlled motion experiment, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated between the brain signal time courses and the movement blocks for both SORDINO and GRE-EPI data on a voxel-by-voxel basis. The correlation coefficient values underwent Fisher’s z-transformation, and results were visualized on a histogram.

Awake mouse resting-state fMRI experiment.

A total of 25 well-habituated male C57BL/6J mice, weighing 28–32 g, were used in this study. Functional (30 min per session, 2 s volume TR) and anatomical high-resolution SORDINO volumes were acquired using the default mouse SORDINO imaging parameters and reconstructed using an in-house tool that incorporates the mri-nufft112 Python module with finufft113,114 as the backend. Brain masks for skull stripping were manually drawn for each subject using ITK-SNAP. Spatial normalization was performed to generate a population average brain template. To ensure alignment to a common framework, the Allen Mouse Brain Common Coordinate Framework image, including its annotated ROIs, was realigned to RAS+ orientation and centered at the anterior commissure. Subsequently, the population average brain template was registered to the anterior commissure-centered Allen Mouse Brain Template. Functional SORDINO reconstruction involved spoke timing correction applied to the FID signal, followed by a non-uniform Fourier transform. Motion correction was performed using six-degree-of-freedom (6-DOF) rigid-body registration to estimate head motion parameters for each volume. The functional images were coregistered to the population average brain template. Signal processing steps included nuisance regression of 28 parameters: a third-order polynomial (four parameters), six rigid motion parameters (six parameters), one TR time shift and its squared terms (12 parameters), along with their squared terms. Temporal filtering was applied using a bandpass filter with a frequency range of 0.01–0.15 Hz. Spatial smoothing was performed using a Gaussian kernel with an FWHM of 0.6 mm, corresponding to 1.5 times the voxel size (0.4 mm isotropic). Motion censoring was evaluated at framewise displacement thresholds of 0.04, 0.06 and 0.08 mm, corresponding to 11.9%, 5.9% and 3.3% data removal, respectively. FC estimates were highly consistent across thresholds. A direct comparison of the averaged connectivity matrices obtained with framewise displacement thresholds of0.04 mm and 0.08 mm revealed only small cell-wise differences in Pearson’s r values, ranging from −0.01 to 0.015 (Supplementary Fig. 5). Given the robustness of the results and the substantially lower data loss, a framewise displacement threshold of 0.08 mm was used for final analyses.

Assessment of awake mouse resting-state fMRI quality.

To ensure data quality, we performed multiple quality control assessments, including framewise displacement, D-variate temporal standard deviation (DVARS), tSNR and FC specificity measures ,8,118 (Fig. 5b–e). For framewise displacement and DVARS, framewise displacement was calculated from the estimated motion parameters using a 5 mm radius for the mouse brain and was used to identify frames with excessive movement for motion censoring. DVARS, a well-established metric, was calculated to measure the rate of change in the BOLD signal intensity between consecutive time points, serving as an additional check on signal stability. The tSNR was computed as the ratio of mean signal intensity to the standard deviation of the noise over time for each voxel. This metric evaluates the signal quality across the dataset; higher tSNR values are indicative of better data quality. tSNR was initially high but decreased before stabilizing, probably because of increasing physiological noise, minor head motion and slow BOLD signal drift over time119. As part of the quality control process, FC specificity was assessed using a non-spatial Gaussian/gamma mixture modeling approach120 to determine the threshold for connectivity metrics. Voxel-wise connectivity maps derived from seed-based analyses of the S1L and S1R were used to create a sample distribution. The data were Fisher z-transformed and standardized before the mixture modeling. A probability map was generated across a range of z-values, where voxels with low connectivity to the seed region were modeled by the Gaussian component (blue), and voxels with high connectivity to the seed region were captured by the gamma component (red). The intersection point between these distributions was identified and used as the threshold for defining functionally specific connectivity.

Mapping intrinsic mouse brain FC networks.

To identify intrinsic brain FC networks from awake SORDINO-fMRI data, group-level ICA was performed using the MELODIC tool from the FSL software package121. ICA analysis was conducted with the number of components fixed at 20. To threshold the ICA spatiotemporal maps, a Gaussian/gamma mixture model was applied to the histogram of intensity values using the expectation–maximization algorithm implemented in MELODIC. The resulting z-score maps of the ICA components were thresholded at a probability level of >0.3, facilitating the identification of significant independent components representing intrinsic FC networks. Among the identified ICA components, the connectivity structure representing the triple networks, including the DMN, lateral cortical network and salience network, was selected as a representative map owing to its comparable spatial patterns. This selection highlights the intrinsic brain FC networks identified in the dataset.

Pairwise ROI FC matrix and clustering.

The Allen Institute’s software development kit was used to provide the mouse brain reference template space and anatomical connectivity data through their Mouse Connectivity Database. ROIs and atlas images were generated using structural set ID 687527945 with the AllenSDK. The atlas image was aligned to the population average template and downsampled to a voxel size of 400 μm isotropic. To ensure robustness, only ROIs larger than four voxels were retained, resulting in a total of 148 ROIs for analysis. FC between the 148 ROIs was estimated using pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients. The resulting correlation matrix was Fisher z-transformed to improve normality. A permutation-based one-sample t-test with 5,000 permutations was conducted to statistically evaluate the FC matrix. Significance was assessed at P < 0.05 (two-sided), using the distribution of minimum and maximum t-values derived from the permuted datasets. To identify functional modules, the group-level FC matrix derived from the permutation t-test was thresholded at <0.1 to remove low-connectivity edges. The thresholded matrix was then converted into a weighted graph. The Louvain community detection algorithm was applied 1,000 times to this graph, and the partition that maximized modularity was selected as the final modular structure. To further refine and organize the detected modules, hierarchical clustering was performed, using Ward’s hierarchical clustering method. A dendrogram was constructed, and thresholds were iteratively applied. The number of modules identified by the Louvain community detection algorithm was used as the minimal partition, while higher thresholds were applied to generate hierarchical organization and assign cluster labels. The same analysis pipeline used for FC was applied to the anatomical connectivity matrix to identify anatomical modules, ensuring consistency between clustering approaches for functional and anatomical data. To integrate these results, a combined classifier was generated by concatenating functional and anatomical cluster labels assigned to each ROI. This multimodal classifier provided a comprehensive representation of the connectivity features for each ROI. To ensure uniform scaling of features, the combined classifier data were standardized. PCA was then used to reduce the dimensionality of the dataset while retaining 99% of its variance, facilitating the clustering process without significant information loss. The DBSCAN clustering algorithm was used to classify the PCA-transformed data. To determine the optimal ϵ (epsilon) value, which defines the maximum allowable distance between points within a cluster, a k-distance graph was generated by estimating the distance to the kth nearest neighbor for each point. The KneeLocator algorithm was applied to identify the ‘elbow point’ on the graph, which corresponds to the optimal ϵ value. Using the optimal ϵ and setting a minimum cluster size of k = 3 ROIs, DBSCAN was applied to assign cluster labels to each ROI, representing their membership in specific functional and anatomical clusters. The resulting clusters were systematically sorted and organized for subsequent interpretation and analysis.

Mapping the reach-and-grasp task using SORDINO-fMRI in mice.

Male VGAT-ChR2-EYFP mice (C57BL/6J background, n = 4), weighing 23–25 g, were used in this study. To leverage SORDINO’s insensitivity to motion-induced artifacts and negligible acoustic noise, we trained mice to execute a forelimb reaching task simultaneously with whole-brain mapping with SORDINO. Following recovery from headplate implantation, four mice underwent a multi-stage food restriction protocol to facilitate training in a reach-to-grasp task. Initially, body weight was reduced by 10%, after which mice were gradually acclimated to head restraint. This acclimation involved placing the head-fixed mice in their home cage for 15 minutes per day over three consecutive days. After the initial phase of acclimation, mice were introduced to a darkened training environment, with a rotary motor-powered table. Food pellets, weighing 10 mg, were positioned at the edge of the table within licking distance. If mice did not spontaneously consume the pellets, their weight was gradually reduced to 80–85% of its baseline, with a maximum restriction of 70%, stopping once voluntary pellet consumption was observed. At this stage, pellets were presented directly in front of the mouth using blunt forceps. This process was repeated until the mouse reached for and grabbed the pellet from the forceps. Once the association was established, pellets were again placed on the rotary table but positioned beyond licking distance to encourage reaching behavior. Training progressed as mice gradually learned to grab pellets from the table. Mice were then trained to associate an auditory cue of 5 kHz frequency and 500 ms duration with food delivery. Pellets were withheld and then presented by rotating the training table in synchrony with the auditory cue. Training continued until mice consistently reached for the pellet in response to the tone and achieved reaction times of less than approximately 250 ms. At the next stage of training, mice were transferred to a custom-built cradle designed to replicate the reach-to-grasp behavior within the MRI environment. The custom-built cradle comprised (1) a conveyor belt with slots to hold the pellets; (2) a pellet dispenser for controlled food delivery; (3) an MR-compatible speaker positioned behind the mouse for auditory cue presentation; (4) a light source made from a flexible plastic tube to transmit light from a laser; and (5) a vacuum and air-line system integrated with the pellet dispenser for automated dispensing. All components of the system were controlled using an Arduino program. The cradle was attached to a motor using a pulley system, which was secured to a wooden base. During each trial, the conveyor belt rotated, dropping a pellet into the dispenser. An auditory cue was delivered, and a small air line propelled the food pellet. Once reliable performance was achieved, a back cover was added to the cradle to prevent mice from arching their back and neck towards the headplate. Training continued until mice reached sufficient accuracy, typically achieving 65–90% success rates. In the final phase of training, mice were gradually acclimated to the MRI scanner environment. Initially, the cradle was placed outside the scanner. Mice were trained, as previously described, in this location for three consecutive days. Then, mice were placed inside the magnet bore and training continued. Scanning sessions commenced once mice achieved a success rate of more than 50%. This final stage of training typically required an additional 3–7 days. Each fMRI session, using the default mouse SORDINO imaging parameters, comprised 75–150 reaching trials, with each trial lasting 20 s. At the start of each trial, the pellet was dispensed with the auditory cue. The reaching movements were recorded at 30 Hz using an MR-compatible camera. To maintain precise alignment throughout the recording, the light source was briefly flashed off for 100 ms before being turned back on. The flash served as a timestamp to correct for potential drift of the Arduino clock. The Arduino microcontroller was programmed to send a transistor–transistor logic pulse to the MRI scanner every 2 s, triggering the acquisition of a 1.987 s volume and ensuring that each volume was precisely synchronized with the Arduino trigger. The pellet dispensing and auditory cue presentation occurred at the start of the corresponding volume, ensuring that reaching movements consistently aligned with the beginning of each volume, minimizing variability in movement timing relative to volume acquisition. In the analysis, the time series were averaged and extracted from four regions of interest: RSC, M1, S1 and thalamus. A linear mixed-effects model was used to assess the signal changes at time points 0, 2 and 4 s after pellet dispensing, with the mean baseline signal change averaged over 2, 4 and 6 s before pellet dispensing. To account for repeated measurements of the same subject, subject was included as a random effect. A threshold was set at an FDR-corrected P < 0.05.

Mapping inter-brain synchronization in socially interacting mice.

Male C57BL/6J mice (n = 22), weighing 25–28 g, were used in this study. The mice were grouped into 11 pairs and underwent hyperscanning with SORDINO. Mice were prepared as described above, except that a custom-designed 3D-printed cradle was used to position two awake mice face-to-face, separated by a remotely retractable divider. Each mouse was equipped with a head-mounted surface RF transceiver coil, enabling simultaneous dual-brain imaging during social interaction. Mice were habituated to the setup over five consecutive days before scanning. During hyperscanning, mice were head-fixed face-to-face with a preinstalled divider before being placed in the scanner. Mice underwent a 40 min continuous recording using the default mouse SORDINO imaging parameters, comprising 20 min before and after divider removal. For all analyses, ROIs were defined using the AllenSDK (structural set ID 687527945) following the same approach described in ‘Pairwise ROI FC matrix and clustering’. To investigate evoked responses to social encounters, we performed peri-event analysis on the SORDINO-fMRI data. Voxel-wise SORDINO-fMRI time courses were normalized to percentage signal changes, averaged across subjects and thresholded at 0.6% to visualize activation and deactivation time-locked to divider removal. Based on these results, we conducted peri-event analysis using SORDINO signals extracted from selected ROIs, which were normalized to the pre-divider removal baseline using Fisher’s z-transformation. Mirrored inter-brain connectivity was assessed by computing voxel-wise Pearson correlation coefficients between spatially corresponding voxels in the two interacting brains. A one-sample t-test was performed to generate a parametric map, with significance assessed at P < 0.01. Inter-brain cross-region connectivity was evaluated by computing Pearson correlation coefficients among the ROIs from the two interacting subjects. A one-sample t-test was then conducted for each inter-brain ROI pair, with significance assessed at P < 0.001 (FDR-corrected for multiple comparisons). Spatial correlation within the DMN and salience network was computed for each scan volume (500 volumes before and after divider removal). Distributions of spatial correlations were analyzed across scan volumes and subjects. To examine inter-brain connectivity to specific brain regions in the interacting partner, the SORDINO signal time course from a selected ROI in one brain was extracted and used to compute a voxel-wise Pearson correlation map in the partner’s brain. The resulting correlation maps for each subject were standardized using Fisher’s z-transformation, followed by a paired t-test (post-divider removal vs pre-divider removal) to generate a parametric map, with significance assessed at P < 0.001.

Mn-enhanced SORDINO.

Male C57BL/6J mice (n = 3), weighing 25–26 g, were used in this study. To ensure MRI compatibility, the metallic flow moderator in osmotic mini-pumps (ALZET 1007D; DURECT) was replaced with a PEEK alternative. The pumps, with a release rate of 0.5 μl h−1 for 7 days, were implanted subcutaneously. Each pump contained 100 μl of 3.45 μM μl−1 MnCl2·4H2O (Sigma-Aldrich) in 100 mmol bicine buffer (Sigma-Aldrich). Following 7 days of continuous Mn infusion, reaching a total dose of 175 mg kg−1, the mice underwent MRI. SORDINO images (Extended Data Fig. 7b) from the same mice, taken before and 7 days after subcutaneous osmotic mini-pump infusion of MnCl2 at a total dose of 175 mg kg−1, were compared54. Mice exhibited no signs of illness, distress or other abnormal behaviors throughout the course of the experiment.

Mapping glymphatic dynamics in mice.

Male (n = 4) and female (n = 1) C57BL/6J mice, weighing 27–30 g, were used in this study. Animals were anesthetized with isoflurane (4% induction, 1–2% maintenance) and maintained at physiological body temperature using a rectal temperature probe and heat lamp throughout the procedure. Hair over the lower skull and neck was removed using Nair, and the surgical site was sterilized with alcohol pads. A midline incision was made along the neck to expose the atlanto-occipital membrane by carefully dissecting through two layers of muscle. A borosilicate glass pipette, pulled using a micropipette puller and blunted for safe use, was inserted perpendicularly through the atlanto-occipital membrane into the cisterna magna, with proper placement confirmed by a reduction in resistance. The pipette was secured in place with Vetbond and superglue to prevent movement during imaging. An intraperitoneal catheter was inserted to administer a mixture of ketamine (100 mg kg−1) and xylazine (15 mg kg−1) anesthesia during the scanning session. The mouse was positioned on a custom sled and carefully transferred into the 9.4 T MRI scanner. Two capillary tubes containing gadoteridol (Prohance, 0.067 mM) and saline were placed alongside the animal to serve as reference phantoms for the imaging session. Dynamic contrast-enhanced SORDINO-fMRI was performed to evaluate glymphatic dynamics by imaging the influx of Gd contrast agent into the brain parenchyma. SORDINO images were acquired using the following parameters: scan time per volume = 1.5 min, TR = 1.1 ms, FA = 6°, receiver bandwidth = 78.1 kHz, spokes = 80,892, matrix size = 160 × 160 × 160, FOV = 25.6 × 25.6 × 25.6 mm3. A baseline equilibrium magnetization (M0) scan was acquired for 10.5 min (seven volumes), followed by dynamic imaging, initiated immediately after administering 10 μl gadoteridol (Prohance, 0.067 mM) with a 2 μl saline chase by intracisterna magna injection using an MRI-compatible syringe pump at a rate of 0.5 μl min−1. Anesthesia was transitioned from isoflurane to ketamine–xylazine by bolus injections administered through the intraperitoneal catheter, guided by physiological monitoring of respiratory rate and oxygen saturation. The total imaging session lasted 100.5 min. The acquired DCE-SORDINO data were reconstructed using an in-house non-uniform Fourier transform-based pipeline with spoke timing correction applied to the k-space data. Motion correction was applied to reduce head motion artifacts. Reconstructed images were coregistered to a well-oriented subject dataset using SyN diffeomorphic non-linear registration implemented in the ANTs software for cross-subject alignment. Signal intensity changes were extracted from predefined ROIs, including the cerebral cortex, lateral ventricles, third ventricle, fourth ventricle and cisterna magna, using binary masks aligned with the registered data. Dynamic contrast signals were normalized using a two-point normalization approach, leveraging the Gd and saline reference intensities obtained from the capillary tube phantoms.

2D-SORDINO experiments.

To achieve higher temporal and spatial resolution with SORDINO, trajectories were generated only in the x and y directions, while the z-encoding gradient was set to zero. Pseudo-slab selection was achieved using a custom PCB-embedded transmit–receive coil with an elliptical geometry and a minor axis of ~2 mm. This coil produced a narrow B1 excitation profile that restricted excitation to a thin slab in the absence of z-encoding. For 2D-SORDINO visual stimulation experiments, a mouse (n = 1) was anesthetized with a bolus of dexmedetomidine (0.025 mg kg−1, isotonic saline). After 15 min, anesthesia was maintained with continuous intraperitoneal infusion of dexmedetomidine (0.05 mg kg−1 h−1) and 0.3% inhaled isoflurane. The animal was secured in a Bruker mouse cradle with ear bars and a bite bar, and the coil was pressed against the skull over the visual cortex. Visual stimulation was delivered using a 473 nm blue laser (BL473–200FC, Shanghai Laser and Optics Century) coupled to an optical fiber (200 μm core, 0.22 NA, 7 m length; Thorlabs). The fiber tip was fixed to the coil and directed toward a sheet of cardstock positioned ~3 cm in front of the mouse, allowing reflected light to fill the visual field. Stimulation consisted of 1 Hz light pulses (30 ms pulse width) delivered in 16 s blocks separated by 44 s rest periods.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.0, GraphPad Software) and Python (v3.7) with NumPy, SciPy, Statsmodels and Pingouin packages. Statistical tests used for individual experiments are described in the corresponding Methods sections and figure legends. Unless otherwise specified, all statistical tests were two-sided. Exact sample sizes, statistical tests, test statistics, degrees of freedom and P values are reported in the corresponding figure legends.

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were selected based on previous studies and laboratory experience with rodent MRI experiments. The number of animals (n) for each experiment is reported in the corresponding figure legends and Methods sections. No data were excluded from the analyses.

Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups where applicable. Blinding during data acquisition was not possible owing to the technical requirements of the imaging setup. Wherever feasible, data analysis was performed using automated processing pipelines without reference to group labels.

Biological replicates corresponded to independent animals unless otherwise stated. For experiments involving repeated measurements from the same animal, appropriate repeated-measures statistical models were used. In Fig. 4c, individual voxel measurements were used as technical replicates for signal characterization and do not represent independent biological replicates.

Repeated-measures data were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA or linear mixed-effects models as appropriate, with subject included as a random effect where applicable. Multiple-comparison correction procedures, including Tukey’s post hoc test and FDR correction, were applied where indicated and are specified in the corresponding figure legends. Data are presented as mean ± s.e.m. unless otherwise stated.

Effect of angular undersampling across different nominal SORDINO resolutions.

(a–l) Images of the spatial-resolution phantom acquired with SORDINO at three nominal voxel sizes (top: 0.2 mm isotropic; middle: 0.4 mm isotropic; bottom: 1 mm isotropic). For each nominal resolution, images were acquired with full sampling and with 2×, 4×, and 8× angular undersampling. The maximum sampled k-space extent (kmax) was kept constant across undersampling levels for each spatial resolution. (m) Cross-sectional signal profiles (green line) extracted across seven 0.5-mm-ID tubes from the same phantom location for each condition. With 50 averages to minimize SNR-related confounds in resolution assessment, profiles within each voxel-size group show minimal difference across undersampling levels. Collectively, reducing the number of spokes proportionally increases temporal resolution, with trade-offs primarily in increased noise and streaking artifacts rather than spatial resolution. This is mainly because SNR scales with the square root of the number of spokes; thus, reducing the number of spokes manifests primarily as reduced sensitivity. Notably, undersampling of 3D inside-out trajectories progressively affects higher spatial frequencies; however, because the maximum sampled k-space extent (kmax) remains unchanged and sampling density remains sufficient to capture the relevant spatial frequencies even at 8× undersampling, the nominal spatial resolution is largely preserved in practice.

2D SORDINO using RF coil-based slice selection.

(a) Conventional headplate coil used for standard 3D SORDINO encoding, in which spatial localization is achieved through full 3D k-space encoding. This approach provides whole-brain coverage but requires a large number of spokes per volume to reach sufficient k-space coverage. (b) ‘Profile’ headplate coil designed to introduce slice selectivity. When used with conventional 3D SORDINO encoding, the coil constrains RF excitation along the slice axis. Profile headplate coil combined with 2D SORDINO encoding (that is, without Gz slice encoding gradient). As spatial selectivity along the slice direction is achieved through RF coil geometry, SORDINO encoding is necessary only within the kxy plane. The resulting 2D SORDINO image is consistent with spatially averaged signals along the z-axis from the 3D SORDINO data. (c) Representative photographs of the conventional headplate coil (upper panel) and the ‘profile’ headplate coil (lower panel). (d) Proof-of-concept visual fMRI response map and time-course from activated voxels in an anesthetized mouse using the 2D SORDINO configuration (TR = 0.62 ms, FA = 2.7 degree, 200 μm nominal in-plane spatial resolution, 0.25 s temporal resolution, responses to 1 Hz, 30 ms pulsewidth visual stimulation under anesthesia with 0.3% isoflurane and i.p. infusion of dexmedetomidine at 0.05 mg/kg/h, n = 1). By sacrificing whole-brain coverage and focusing on a single slice, this approach leverages the same principle used in high spatiotemporal resolution single-slice EPI as seen in Gil et al.127 and Cabral et al.128 Although preliminary, these results illustrate the potential of extending SORDINO toward higher spatiotemporal resolution when full volumetric coverage is not required. The evoked response pattern may also imply pushpull dynamics described by Gil et al.129, reflecting the interplay between fast excitatory input and delayed inhibitory circuitry, which can produce an early peak followed by transient suppression and a rebound. In addition, a stronger through-plane inflow effect is observed as expected, suggesting that the 2D experiments may be more susceptible to macroscopic inflow contamination (see images in a and b). To facilitate positioning, the RF coil was not implanted to the skull, resulting in weaker penetration to deeper brain regions. Future work will focus on improving RF coil design and shielding to enhance slice selectivity and robustness for meaningful applications. Alternatively, Slice selectivity may also be introduced using preparatory modules (for example, DANTE-based excitation or a nonselective pulse followed by a selective pulse to isolate a slab of interest). Restricting excitation130 in this manner and reading out signals in 2D k-space would substantially reduce k-space.

SORDINO efficiency to changes in tissue oxygen as a function of Field Strength.

Assuming baseline T1 values of 1035, 1272, 1600, 1900 and 2030 ms at field strengths of 1.5, 3, 4.7, 9.4, 17.6 T, respectively, and a 30 μM increase in tO2 with a spin-lattice relaxivity (r1) of 0.3 mM−1s−1, SORDINO efficiency is expected to increase with field strength. Assuming baseline T1 values of 1035, 1272, 1600, 1900 and 2030 ms at field strengths of 1.5, 3, 4.7, 9.4, 17.6 T, respectively, and a30 μM increase in tO2 with a spin-lattice relaxivity (r1) of 0.3 mM−1s−1, SORDINO efficiency is expected to increase with field strength. This is because the relationship between T1 and molecular oxygen is non-linear, as described by: 1T1new=1T1baseline=r1.[tO2]. Since the additive increase in relaxation rate causes a proportionally larger drop in T1 when T1 is high, the efficiency of SORDINO improves at higher field strengths.
