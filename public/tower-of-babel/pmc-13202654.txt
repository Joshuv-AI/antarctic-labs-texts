A copper uptake ABC transport system is essential for Mycobacterium tuberculosis virulence
Authors: Zhihong Xu, Huang Tang, Meiyi Yan, Haifeng Li, Aiping Zhou, Yana Chen, Chong-Huai Yan, Petros C. Karakousis, Jinjing Ni, Danni Wang, Jing Tao, Wenjuan Wu, Qing Xie, Jie Lu, Lingjun Zhan, Yi-Cheng Sun, Wei Zhao, Yu-Feng Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) is a facultative intracellular pathogen that thrives primarily inside the phagosome of macrophages. A key Mtb survival strategy is to exploit and manipulate metal cation trafficking inside macrophages. Copper plays dual roles in host–pathogen interactions, i.e. as a bactericidal toxin for the host, as well as micronutrients for the bacteria. In contrast to the well-studied copper resistance pathways, the copper uptake machinery of Mtb remains poorly characterized. We here show that Rv1273c and Rv1272c form a heterodimeric copper influx ABC transporter in Mtb, and their expression is induced by copper and phagocytosis. Intracellular copper imported by Rv1273c-Rv1272c promotes mycobacterial sedimentation and alters bacterial envelope structure. Moreover, copper broadly affects the mycobacterial transcriptome and metabolome, as well as glycolipid and phospholipid contents of the mycobacterial envelope, thereby contributing to mycobacterial survival in macrophages. Finally, the murine model results show that the deletion of Rv1273c-Rv1272c causes lower bacterial burdens in the lungs and spleens, suggesting that Rv1273c-Rv1272c is essential for Mtb virulence. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that mycobacteria utilize the copper uptake ABC transport system to facilitate pathogenesis.

Introduction

As a facultative intracellular pathogen, Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) thrives primarily inside macrophages [1,2] where it encounters multiple antimicrobial defences, including the production of reactive nitrogen and oxygen intermediates, phagosomal acidification and nutrient limitation [3,4]. It has been recognized that copper is an integral component of innate immune responses [5]. Several lines of evidence indicate that copper redistribution and mobilization in mammalian tissues and individual cells contribute to key immune responses to bacterial infections [6-9]. However, copper plays dual roles in host–pathogen interactions, acting both as a bactericidal toxin for the host as well as an important micronutrient for bacteria, for Mtb, it is a critical substance that the bacterium itself can exploit and manipulate [8].

Copper homeostasis is essential for mycobacteria [10]. The reversible oxidation states of copper make it a critical cofactor for enzymes involved in electron transfer reactions in the presence of oxygen. In Mtb, one of the most prominent copper-binding enzymes is the aa3-type cytochrome c oxidase, which is a key component of the oxidative phosphorylation pathway for ATP production [11] and whose function is essential for mycobacterial survival in the mouse model [12]. Another copper-associated enzyme is copper-containing superoxide dismutase [13], which contributes to the resistance of Mtb against oxidative burst products generated by activated macrophages [14]. Furthermore, excessive copper is also lethal to bacteria. In bacterial cells, the accumulation of copper leads to the aggregation of lipoylated proteins, which inhibits the TCA cycle and reduces the activity of electron transport chain enzymes. This disrupts energy metabolism and promotes the accumulation of intracellular peroxides, ultimately leading to a cuproptosis-like cell death [15,16]. Given the bacterial toxicity induced by copper accumulation, Mtb has evolved various strategies to persist in mammalian tissues by maintaining an extremely low intracellular free copper content [10,17]. Several copper resistance pathways have been identified independently, including in Mtb [18-21]. Given the importance of copper in mycobacterial physiology, bacteria have evolved regulation and transport mechanisms such as copper uptake and efflux pumps, metallothioneins and copper chaperones[20–22]. Among these, copper uptake is required to ensure sufficient copper as a cofactor for essential enzymes in Mtb. However, the copper uptake machinery in Mtb remains poorly understood.

The ATP-Binding Cassette (ABC) transporters are a transport system superfamily that is one of the largest and possibly one of the oldest families from prokaryotes to eukaryotes [23]. Bacterial transporters are essential for nutrient uptake and are a key factor in host–pathogen interactions. Furthermore, an array of these transporters play a critical role in the survival and proliferation of pathogens within the host [24]. In this study, we identify a copper influx ABC transporter, Rv1273c-Rv1272c, in Mtb. Our findings show that mycobacteria use this transporter to take up copper as a strategy for growth and to evade host innate immunity.

Results

Rv1273c and Rv1272c form an ABC transporter and are required for bacterial growth in vitro

Bioinformatics analyses have revealed that Mtb contains 38 genes encoding ABC transporters, occupying about 2.5% of the genome. Among these, there are ABC transporters encoded by Rv1273c and Rv1272c that have been annotated as multidrug transporters [25,26]. To characterize the transport directionality of this ABC transporter in Mtb, we used a universal efflux pump substrate, ethidium bromide (EtBr), to examine the directionality of transport [27]. We overexpressed Rv1273c and Rv1272c individually or simultaneously in Mycolicibacterium smegmatis and then monitored the dynamics of EtBr in the cytoplasm by real-time fluorometry. Overexpression of either gene increased the accumulation of intracellular EtBr compared to the empty vector control, and co-expression of Rv1273c and Rv1272c further increased accumulation (Figure 1(A)). Ms5008 and Ms5009 from M. smegmatis are close homologues of Rv1273c and Rv1272c, respectively, sharing 69% and 75% sequence identities with their Mtb counterparts [28] (Figure S1). A typical ABC transporter contains two cytoplasmic nucleotide-binding domains, which include a highly conserved Walker A motif (GxxGxGKT/S) essential for ATP binding, and which hydrolyze ATP to drive the transport process [25]. To examine whether Rv1273c and Rv1272c form an ABC transporter, we mutated the conserved amino acids in the Walker A motifs of Rv1273c and Rv1272c and corresponding amino acids in Ms5008 and Ms5009 (Figure S1). The EtBr accumulation assay showed that co-expression of the Rv1273c/G374A (Ms5008/K374A) and Rv1272c/G435A (Ms5009/K430A) decreased EtBr accumulation relative to the wild type control (Figure 1(A and B)).

Then, we deleted ms5008-ms5009 simultaneously in M. smegmatis strain MC2155 by homologous recombination [29,30]. The deletion mutant was named Δ5008-5009. First, we monitored the axenic growth of Δ5008-5009 in LBG broth. Surprisingly, when cultured in nutrient-rich media, Δ5008-5009 not only exhibited no growth defect but also displayed a faster growth rate during logarithmic phase and reached a higher plateau in stationary phase compared to the isogenic wild type as shown in (Figure 1(C and D)). Complementation with either rv1273c-rv1272c or ms5008-ms5009 restored the growth rate of the mutant to that of the wild type, but this growth phenotype was abrogated when the mutant was complemented with either mutated rv1273c-rv1272c or ms5008-ms5009 (Figure 1(C and D)). These findings suggest that in nutrient-rich environments, this ABC transporter influences the growth of Mtb by participating in the transport of another critical metabolic substrates.

Rv1273c and Rv1272c are involved in copper uptake

Given the above findings, we speculate that this ABC transporter might transport unknown component(s) from the medium, thereby inhibiting mycobacterial growth. Since LBG broth composition is highly complicated, we next used the chemically defined Middlebrook 7H9 broth to monitor bacterial growth. Similar to the results observed in LBG broth, Δ5008-5009 had a more rapid growth rate compared with its parental strain, and complementation of the mutant with ms5008-ms5009 showed the same growth rate as the wild-type strain in Middlebrook 7H9 broth (Figure S2(A)). To identify the substrate of Rv1273c-Rv1272c, we employed nutrient depletion assays to monitor the growth of each strain in customized 7H9 broth, each lacking one specific component. Results show that the significant growth rate difference between the Δ5008-5009 and the wild-type strain in complete Middlebrook 7H9 broth was abolished only when cultured in Middlebrook 7H9 broth depleted of copper or zinc (Figure 2(A)). In contrast, this growth disparity persisted following the removal of any other components (Figure S2(B)). We next determined the cytoplasmic concentrations of copper, zinc, calcium, magnesium and iron of each strain during logarithmic growth using Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS) [31,32]. When the wild type and mutant strains were cultured in complete Middlebrook 7H9 broth, the copper concentration in the mutant strain was found to be significantly lower than that in the wild-type strain (Figure 2(B)). No significant differences were observed in the intrabacterial concentrations of any of the other metal ions between the two strains (Figure 2(B)). To determine whether copper may interfere with the transport of other metal ions, we also determined the concentrations of these metal ions in copper-depleted broth. Under these conditions, the wild-type and mutant strains contained comparable zinc, calcium, magnesium and iron concentrations (Figure 2(C)). Therefore, we concluded that the ABC transporter encoded by rv1273c and rv1272c is involved in copper transport in Mtb.

Copper is a redox-active metal, which undergoes reversible oxidation states between reduced Cu+ and oxidized Cu2+ and serves as a critical cofactor for enzymes used for electron transfer reactions [21,33]. However, it will also become highly cytotoxic when its concentration exceeds the cellular capacity to sequester the ion [34]. Therefore, we speculate that Rv1273c-Rv1272c (Ms5008-Ms5009) would increase the susceptibility of mycobacteria to excess copper in vitro. We monitored the in vitro growth of the wild type and Δ5008-5009 under different concentrations of copper. The growth of the wild type, but not that of the mutant, was inhibited in customized 7H9 broth containing copper 5 μM (Figure 2 (D and E)). When the copper concentration was increased to 10 μM or 20 μM, the growth rates of the wild-type strain were significantly lower than those of mutant strain, although both concentrations of copper dramatically inhibited mycobacterial growth in general (Figure 2 (D and E)). To further solidify the evidence that Rv1273c-Rv1272c functions as a copper importer in Mtb, we performed the intracellular copper concentration detection assays using the Rv1273c-Rv1272c knockout strain, complemented strain and wild-type H37Rv. The results demonstrated that the intracellular copper concentration in the Δrv1273c-rv1272c knockout strain was significantly reduced compared with the wild-type H37Rv, while the intracellular copper level in the complemented strain was fully restored to that of the wild-type with copper supplemented (Figure 2(F)). These findings indicate that Rv1273c-Rv1272c serves as a key transporter in the copper metabolism of mycobacteria.

Furthermore, as copper concentration increased, its inhibitory effect on Mtb growth became more pronounced, and the growth rate difference between the Δ5008-5009 and the wild type widened (Figure 2(D and E)). We hypothesize that this is due to the Ms5008-Ms5009 transporter continually importing copper into the cell under high external concentrations. Moreover, we found that the expression of the ms5008 and ms5009 genes was significantly higher in copper-supplemented media than in copper-depleted media during both the logarithmic and stationary growth phases (Figure 2(G)). This indicates that the copper transport process is substrate concentration-dependent, whereby environmental copper ions upregulate ms5008 and ms5009 expression to enhance copper uptake.

Copper transported by Rv1272c-Rv1273c alters mycobacterial metabolism

To further investigate the impact of Rv1273c-Rv1272c mediated copper transport on cellular metabolism, we performed RNA-seq to determine the transcriptional profiles of the wild type and Δ5008-5009 M. smegmatis cultured in broth containing or lacking copper. Complete RNA-seq data are included in Table S3. Significantly dysregulated genes were filtered by comparing the wild type vs. the mutant in the copper-depleted condition and the wild type vs. the mutant in the copper-replete condition, respectively, with fold change ≥ 1.5. In the copper-depleted condition, 351 genes were differentially expressed. In the copper-replete condition, 577 genes were differentially expressed (Figure 3(A)). To screen for potential copper-dependent genes, we then filtered for those that were differentially expressed only in copper-replete broth but not in copper-depleted medium, yielding a total of 400 genes. To gain insight into the physiological roles of these genes, we performed KEGG enrichment analysis to obtain their functional annotation. Among the 400 genes, 118 were annotated and enriched in 73 pathways (Figure S3(A)). In the oxidative phosphorylation pathway, 11 type I NADH dehydrogenase-coding genes were upregulated, while a bd-type cytochrome oxidase-coding gene was downregulated in the wild type. We also observed that glycerolipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism pathways were enriched in RNA-seq data, suggesting that phospholipid synthesis might be affected by intracellular copper in mycobacteria (Figure 3(B and C)). We analyzed the transcriptome data for genes involved in glycopeptidolipids (GPL) and dimycolate (TDM) synthesis pathways. Our RNA-seq data demonstrated that in copper supplemented medium, four genes involved in the TDM synthesis pathway (ms2078, ms3580, ms6393 and ms6583) were significantly upregulated in Δ5008-5009, while such transcriptional differences were abrogated in copper-depleted medium (Figure S3(B)). These findings were validated by qRT-PCR, which confirmed that the expression of ms2078, ms3580, ms6393 and ms6583 was strongly increased in Δ5008-5009 with copper supplemented, and no significant upregulation was detected in this mutant compared with the wild-type under copper-depleted conditions (Figure S3(C)). One gene (ms0393) in the GPL synthesis pathway was significantly upregulated. These results indicate that intracellular copper concentrations might modulate lipid biosynthesis in mycobacteria, this finding is consistent with previous studies [25,35]. We next performed LC-MS to compare the phospholipid content in the cell envelopes of the wild type and mutant. The abundance of diverse phospholipids in the cell envelope of the wild type was distinct from that of the mutant in the presence of copper. In the wild type, 30 phospholipids decreased and 2 increased in abundance. Conversely, in the mutant, the quantities of 20 phospholipids decreased and 11 increased (Figure 3(C)). These findings demonstrate that intracellular copper levels affect the cell envelope phospholipid content in mycobacteria. These results suggest that copper exposure can induce its transporter and alter mycobacterial redox and lipid metabolism. Rv1273c-Rv1272c contributes to bacterial sedimentation

Having observed that copper transport by Rv1273c-Rv1272c alters the cell envelope composition of mycobacteria, we next focused on the impact of the altered envelope composition on the bacterial cell surface properties. We found that neither the wild-type nor mutant strain showed sedimentation in the absence of copper in the culture broth, which implies that deletion of Rv1273c-Rv1272c alone is not sufficient for sedimentation. Conversely, supplementation of copper in the broth promoted mycobacterial sedimentation in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure S4(A)). Of note, the wild type clearly exhibited sedimentation but the mutant did not in the presence of 5 μM copper. Consistent with this phenotype, the sedimentation index (ODsupernatant/ODsediment) of the wild type dramatically decreased significantly in the presence of 5 μM copper (Figure 4(A)). Furthermore, the sedimentation status of Δ5008-5009 was restored to that of the wild type by complementation (Figure 4(A) and S4(A)). This sedimentation phenotype indicates that by importing copper into the cell, Rv1273c-Rv1272c alters the bacterial cell envelope composition and surface properties, and consequently leading to sedimentation. Rv1273c-Rv1272c contributes to mycobacterial phagocytosis and intramacrophage survival

Copper accumulates in the phagosomes of macrophages to inhibit intracellular bacterial growth [33], suggesting that copper is accessible to mycobacteria during infection. We next tested whether copper uptake affects mycobacterial intracellular survival in murine RAW264.7 macrophages in DMEM medium with or without copper supplementation. Phagocytosis of the wild type was significantly reduced relative to the mutant when each strain was pre-cultured with 5 μM copper and complementation restored the phagocytosis rate of the mutant to that of the wild type (Figure 4(B)). These data demonstrate that copper uptake by mycobacteria inhibits their recognition and phagocytosis by macrophages. As the first line of cellular defence against invading pathogens, macrophages express a broad range of immune receptors that mediate phagocytosis. Among them, PRRs, such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and scavenger receptors (SRs), are known to recognize highly conserved PAMPs found on the surface of bacteria [36]. To investigate the PRRs responsible for the differential phagocytosis of the two strains under copper-limited conditions, we determined the uptake of the wild type and Δ5008-5009 by peritoneal macrophages isolated from TLR2−/–, TLR4−/– or SR−/– mice. When the mycobacteria were pre-cultured in copper-depleted broth, phagocytosis of the wild type by macrophages decreased when TLR4 or SR was deleted, while that of the mutant strain decreased in all three sets (TLR2−/–, TLR4−/– and SR−/–) of macrophages (Figure 4(C)). Interestingly, when mycobacteria were pre-cultured with 7H9 broth containing standard concentrations of copper, the phagocytosis rate of the wild-type strain significantly increased in TLR2−/– macrophages but decreased in TLR4−/– macrophages (Figure 4(C)). In contrast, the phagocytosis rate of Δ5008-5009 decreased dramatically in TLR4−/– and SR−/– macrophages, but remained unchanged in TLR2−/– macrophages (Figure 4(C)). These results indicate that TLR4 and SR contribute to mycobacterial recognition by macrophages in a copper uptake-independent manner, and TLR2 mediates the phagocytosis of Mtb by macrophages in a copper-dependent manner.

We also found that in the copper-depleted condition, the deletion mutant showed decreased survival compared to the wild type at 24 h (37% vs 42%) post-infection, however the wild-type survival phenotype was not restored by complementation of the mutant. In the copper-replete condition, the intracellular survival of the mutant was dramatically decreased compared to the wild type at 24 h (20% vs 40%) post-infection, and complementation of the mutant partially restored wild-type survival (Figure 4(D)). We also quantified the expression of the genes ms5008 and ms5009 by the wild type by qPCR at 24 h post-infection, and found that phagocytosis induced their transcription, which was further increased by copper supplementation of the DMEM media (Figure S4(C)). Taken together, these data indicate that copper uptake contributes to mycobacterial survival inside macrophages. We hypothesized that mycobacteria may utilize intraphagosomal copper to promote their intracellular survival. To test this hypothesis, we interrupted ATP7A, a copper importer at the phagosome membrane in RAW264.7 macrophages, using CRISPR/Cas9 [6]. We then infected these two cells with each strain. The intracellular survival of the deletion mutant in these cells decreased dramatically compared to that of the wild type strain in ATP7A+/+ RAW264.7 macrophages. However, both strains showed similar intracellular survival curves at all time points in ATP7A−/– RAW264.7 macrophages (Figure 4(E)). These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that Rv1273c-Rv1272c contributes to mycobacterial intracellular survival by taking up copper in the phagosome.

Rv1273c-Rv1272c contributes to mycobacterial virulence in the mouse model

To further investigate the role of rv1273c-rv1272c in mycobacterial pathogenesis, we deleted rv1273c-rv1272c in the Mtb strain H37Rv (yielding the mutant Δrv1273c-rv1272c), and complemented this mutant with the rv1273c-rv1272c operon under control of its native promoter (yielding the strain Δrv1273c-rv1272c::rv1273c-rv1272c, henceforth referred to as the “complementation).” The wild-type, Δrv1273c-rv1272c and complementation strains were used to challenge C57BL/6 mice (Figure 5(A)). A clinical scoring system including appetite, mobility and appearance was used to compare quantitatively the virulence of each strain [37,38]. Moreover, spleen sizes normalized to body weight were recorded after sacrifice. Twenty-eight days post-infection, the clinical score of mice infected with Δrv1273c-rv1272c was significantly higher than that of mice infected with the wild type, suggesting attenuated virulence of the mutant, and complementation with rv1273c-rv1272c partially restored wild-type virulence (Figure 5(B)). The lungs, spleens and livers were harvested from each group of mice at 28 days post-infection, homogenized and plated on L-J plates to determine organ bacillary burden. The log10 CFU/mL values of Δrv1273c-rv1272c were lower than those in the wild type, especially in the lungs (Figure 5(C)) and spleens (Figure 5(D)), indicating reduced replication of Δrv1273c-rv1272c in these organs. Complementation showed higher bacillary burdens than Δrv1273c-rv1272c in lung, spleen and liver tissues, indicating that complementation with rv1273c-rv1272c partially restored the replication deficiency of Δrv1273c-rv1272c in mice (Figure 5(C-E)). Histological analysis of the infected lungs in each group revealed that Δrv1273c-rv1272c and complementation induced less pathology than did the wild type (Figure 5(F)). In addition to fewer granulomatous lesions, lungs infected with Δrv1273c-rv1272c exhibited the least degree of inflammation, as measured by lung inflammation score, and complementation had slightly milder inflammation than wild type (Figure 5(F)). Relative to the histological lesions induced by the wild type, those induced by Δrv1273c-rv1272c and complementation were less extensive in the lungs and other tissues, consistent with attenuated virulence of the mutant strain and partial restoration of virulence in the complemented strain (Figure 5(F)). Discussion

Rv1273c and Rv1272c were predicted to form a multidrug transporter in Mtb [25], but their transport directionality and native substrates have remained unclear [28,35,39,40]. In a previous study, the Rv1273c/72c complex was characterized as an isoniazid efflux pump, and its structures resolved by Cryo-EM revealed distinct asymmetric features [28]. Other studies reported that this transporter mediates long-chain fatty acid or lecithin import to support Mtb cell wall assembly and linoleic acid biosynthesis [35,40]. These conflicting reports highlight the need for direct experimental evidence of substrate specificity. In this study, overexpression of Rv1273c and Rv1272c or their homologues Ms5008 and Ms5009 in M. smegmatis enhanced intracellular EtBr accumulation. Nutrient depletion and ICP-MS assays further demonstrated that Rv1273c-Rv1272c specifically mediates copper import. Growth assays confirmed that wild-type bacteria are more susceptible to copper toxicity than Δ5008-5009, consistent with impaired copper uptake in the mutant strain. Therefore, we propose that Rv1273c-Rv1272c forms a heterodimeric ABC transporter involved in influx or efflux in Mtb, which may depend on the availability of substrates. This conformational flexibility is conserved among ABC transporters, as exemplified by eukaryotic ABCA3/4 proteins that switch between lipid import and export [41]. We suggest that the transport function of Rv1273c-Rv1272c may be tightly dependent on the physiological state of Mtb. Under antibiotic stress, the high extracellular concentration of isoniazid drives Rv1273c-Rv1272c to act as an efflux pump to reduce intracellular drug accumulation; and for nutritional demand, the transporter mediates the import of long-chain fatty acids and lecithin to support Mtb cell wall synthesis, or imports copper to maintain the activity of copper-dependent enzymes and ion homeostasis essential for Mtb (our study). Regarding the substrate diversity associated with Rv1273c-Rv1272c, MmpL4 and MmpL5 are multifunctional transporters in Mtb that transport lipids, metal ions, and drugs. These proteins belong to the RND efflux pump superfamily and are secondary active transporters that utilize proton motive force to pump substrates such as lipids or antibiotics out of the cell, while also exporting siderophores, such as mycobactins [42,43]. The identification of such transporters provides further insights into the study of transport substrates for Rv1273c-Rv1272c.

Copper homeostasis is crucial for mycobacterial growth in vitro, intracellular survival and virulence. Mtb has evolved a copper homeostasis regulation network comprising multiple layers, including copper efflux pumps (CtpV and MctB) [21,44,45], intracellular copper chelation (MymT) [18], enzymatic conversion (MmcO) [46,47], and active acquisition (via carrier molecules) [10], all of which are precisely regulated by factors such as CsoR and RicR [48,49]. While copper efflux is known to promote Mtb virulence [17,21], our work reveals that copper influx also contributes to pathogenesis. We propose a biphasic model: during early growth phase, ms5008-ms5009 is transcriptionally induced by low copper to drive copper import, while CtpV/MctB are suppressed to preserve intracellular copper, as confirmed by previous studies demonstrating that CtpV is upregulated only under high copper stress [21,44,50]. Upon entry into stationary phase, elevated intracellular copper triggers CtpV/MctB upregulation to mediate efflux and avoid toxicity. This coordinated regulation ensures optimal copper levels for mycobacterial physiology, and is supported by our observation that Ms5008-Ms5009 expression is dependent on copper concentration. In M. smegmatis, the copper-responsive repressor GfcR (Ms2173) activates Ms5008-Ms5009 expression, and its Mtb ortholog Rv0494 may mediate similar regulation [51]. Considering that intrabacterial copper concentrations in Mtb are 100 times lower than those in M. smegmatis [21], we propose that these two species might use distinct mechanisms to maintain copper homeostasis.

Copper is involved in biological processes in Mtb, as it is commonly incorporated into metalloenzymes responsible for catalyzing electron transfer reactions [18,52]. Conversely, copper also serves as a bactericidal toxin during the host's antimicrobial defence [21]. Accordingly, the cellular copper concentration dictates the effect of copper on Mtb. Owing to differences in detection methods and units, the absolute physiological copper concentration of Mtb is difficult to determine directly, and it varies with the copper concentration in the culture environment. Intracellular copper levels increase correspondingly as extracellular copper concentration rises [21,53,54]. In our measurements, under standard 7H9 medium conditions, the intracellular copper concentration was approximately 200 ng/mg protein in M. smegmatis and 30 ng/mg protein in M. tuberculosis. There is a striking difference in physiological copper levels between these two mycobacterial species, and this discrepancy has been reported in previous studies [21,53]. From our growth curve assays, we observed that 5 μM copper significantly inhibited the growth of M. smegmatis, indicating that this concentration is toxic to bacterial cells. Accordingly, deletion of Ms5008-Ms5009 conferred M. smegmatis enhanced tolerance to toxic levels of copper under 5 μM copper treatment. A similar phenotype has been documented previously: knockout of ms4702, the homologue of M. tuberculosis rv0102 (encoding a membrane protein), resulted in a fourfold decrease in intracellular copper levels and enhanced tolerance to elevated extracellular copper concentrations [53,55]. Furthermore, the growth difference caused by copper toxicity became more pronounced with increasing environmental copper concentration, implying that higher copper levels exert stronger toxic effects. We propose that these results indicate that M. smegmatis requires only a low copper level to meet its physiological needs. Once cellular copper demand is satisfied, any excess copper becomes toxic and impairs the growth of M. smegmatis.

In the nutrient depletion assay, we observed that the significant difference in growth rate between Δ5008-5009 and WT strains observed in complete 7H9 broth was eliminated not only in copper-depleted broth but also in zinc-depleted broth. However, this phenomenon does not necessarily indicate that Rv1273c-Rv1272c transports Zn2⁺, but rather is a reflection of the evolutionarily conserved copper–zinc metabolic interaction in mycobacteria. For example, when the enzyme superoxide dismutase has access to both zinc and copper, the Zn2⁺ plays a crucial structural role and has also been shown to finely tune the redox properties of the Cu2⁺ [13,52]. Zinc can also replace pro-oxidant copper species, preventing the generation of free radicals [56]. Thereby, zinc depletion can indirectly alter the mycobacterial cellular response to copper, and offset the growth phenotype caused by the loss of copper transport function in Δ5008-5009. For other metal ions (e.g. Ca2⁺, Mg2⁺, and Fe2⁺), the growth difference between Δ5008-5009 and WT persisted after depletion, further excluding the involvement of Rv1273c-Rv1272c in their transport.

Our data further link copper uptake to bacterial sedimentation and cell envelope remodelling. Copper imported by Rv1273c-Rv1272c alters mycobacterial phospholipid and glycolipid profiles, including TDM synthesis, which directly modulates cell surface properties and sedimentation. These envelope changes reduced mycobacterial phagocytosis by RAW264.7 macrophages in a TLR2-dependent manner. Phagocytosis of wild-type and Δrv1273c-rv1272c was comparable in TLR2⁻/⁻ macrophages, indicating that TLR2 recognizes copper-modified cell wall components to mediate uptake [57]. This mechanism may allow Mtb to evade innate immune detection by remodelling its envelope in response to phagosomal copper. We also delineated the role of host copper trafficking during mycobacterial infection. Macrophages upregulate CTR1 and ATP7A to mobilize copper into phagosomes, where excess copper triggers Fenton chemistry and antimicrobial activity [58,59]. In ATP7A⁻/⁻ macrophages, the survival difference between wild-type and Δrv1273c-rv1272c mycobacteria was abolished, confirming that Rv1273c-Rv1272c promotes intracellular survival by utilizing phagosomal copper. Copper import globally reshapes the mycobacterial transcriptome and metabolome, enhancing resistance to macrophage killing and supporting long-term intracellular persistence.

In summary, we have identified a novel mycobacterial copper influx transporter, Rv1273c-Rv1272c, which can import copper from the environment to alter mycobacterial gene expression and cell envelope composition as a virulence strategy to inhibit macrophage phagocytosis and promote its survival in macrophages and host tissues.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain and culture conditions

M. smegmatis strain mc2 155 was used as the parental strain. All strains were grown in LBG (LB medium supplemented with 0.5% glycerol) or Middlebrook 7H9 broth supplemented with 0.5% glycerol, 0.05% Tween-80, and cultured at 37°C with shaking at 250 rpm. When required, kanamycin was used at a concentration of 50 μg/mL.

Construction of deletion mutants and complementation strains

For the double knockout of ms5008-ms5009 in M. smegmatis strain MC2155, the recombinant plasmid pMZ-Ms5008-Ms5009 was constructed and electroporated into M. smegmatis as described previously [29,30]. Allelic exchange mutants were verified by PCR analysis using the primers on each side of Ms5008-Ms5009 and the hygromycin gene. Plasmid pMN234 was then electroporated into M. smegmatis to remove the hygromycin gene. The final allelic exchange mutants (Δ5008-5009) were verified by PCR and sequencing using primers flanking Ms5008-Ms5009.

A Rv1273c-Rv1272c-deficient mutant of Mtb strain H37Rv was constructed by the CRISPR-assisted NHEJ method using paired sgRNA to generate larger deletions, as described previously [60]. Briefly, paired sgRNA sequences targeting Rv1273c and Rv1272c, respectively, were commercially synthesized (GenScript) and then cloned into sgRNA-expressing plasmid pYC1876 digested with BamHI and NheI. The resulting plasmid was transferred by electroporation into Mtb H37Rv harbouring pNHEJ-recX-sacB. Recovered cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3000 g for 10 min and spread onto 7H10 agar plates supplemented with kanamycin (25 ng/mL), zeocin (50 ng/mL), and ATc (100 ng/mL). After 28 days of incubation, individual transformants were picked and validated by PCR and sequencing analysis. One Mtb clone (strain Δrv1273c-rv1272c) with a 3036 bp-deletion in rv1273c-rv1272c was selected. A complemented strain (strain Δrv1273c-rv1272c::rv1273c-rv1272c) was obtained by introducing a copy of rv1273c-rv1272c into the Mtb genome at the attL5 attachment site under the control of 253 bp of the Rv1273c 5′ UTR, which contains the putative promoter sequence [61]. For this, we constructed an integrated plasmid pYC2160, containing a zeocin resistance cassette, the rv1273c-rv1272c sequence, the integrase gene and the phage attP sites for site-specific integration into the mycobacterial chromosome.

For the construction of recombinant plasmids, the target gene fragments, rv1273c-rv1272c (from genomic DNA of Mtb H37Rv) and ms5008-ms5009 (from genomic DNA of M. smegmatis strain MC2155), both containing restriction sites, were amplified by PCR. The primers are as follows: P7372 F and P7273 R for rv1273c-rv1272c, P5008-5009 F and P5008-5009 R for ms5008-ms5009. The digested fragments were then ligated into the pMV261 vector and transformed into DH5α competent cells. Following sequencing confirmation, the resulting recombinant plasmids were designated pMV261-Rv1273c-Rv1272c and pMV261-Ms5008-Ms5009. For the construction of point mutation recombinant plasmids, using pMV261-Rv1273c-Rv1272c and pMV261-Ms5008-Ms5009 as templates, the entire plasmid was amplified. The primers are as follows: 1273 G374A F, 1273 G374A R, 1272 G435A F and 1272 G435A R for rv1273c-rv1272c point mutation, 5008 K374A F, 5008 K374A R, 5009 K430A F and 5009 K430A R for ms5008-ms5009 point mutation. The PCR products were digested with the restriction endonuclease DpnI to remove the methylated plasmid template, incubated at 37°C for 1 h. The digested products were transformed into DH5α competent cells. Single colonies grown on selective plates were picked, and the size of the amplified fragments was verified using validation primers to confirm consistency with the expected size. Finally, sequencing was performed to confirm the successful introduction of the point mutation and to check for any other mutations, deletions, or insertions. All the primers are listed in Table S1.

Mycobacterial growth determination

Growth rates of the wild type M. smegmatis and Δ5008-5009 strains were grown in 5 mL of LBG or customized copper-depleted broth by reformulating 13 chemicals in Middlebrook 7H9 broth for 48 h to reach stationary phase before being diluted into 50 mL of customized 7H9 broth to achieve a starting bacterial density of 106/mL. The cultures were incubated at 37°C on a shaker at 250 rpm for 5 days. Aliquots were taken at the indicated times. OD600 was measured, and 100-μL aliquots of the culture were plated onto LBG-agar plates for enumeration of colony-forming units (CFU). The wild type Mtb, rv1273c-rv1272c deletion mutant Δrv1273c-rv1272c, and the complemented strain Δrv1273c-rv1272c::rv1273c-rv1272c were grown in Middlebrook 7H9 broth at 37°C to an OD600 of 0.6-0.8 and then diluted to an OD600 of 0.025 in 10 mL Middlebrook 7H9 broth supplemented with the indicated concentrations of CuSO4 or without copper supplementation (Control). Bacterial growth was monitored by turbidity measurement (OD600).

Macrophage infection

For in vitro infection studies, the murine macrophage cell line RAW 264.7 (ATCC, #TIB-71) was cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were then infected with bacteria at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. After phagocytosis, infected cells were incubated in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 150 μg/mL gentamicin for 4 h to kill extracellular bacteria. Following this incubation, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Gibco Invitrogen) to remove any remaining bacteria and antibiotic. For the phagocytosis assay, cells were lysed immediately with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS. For the bacterial survival assay, cells were maintained in medium containing 25 μg/mL gentamicin for 24 h before being lysed with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS. Lysates from both time points were diluted, plated on Middlebrook 7H10 agar supplemented with 10% OADC, and incubated at 37°C until colonies were visible. Colony-forming units (CFUs) were then enumerated.

RNA isolation and RNA sequencing

Wild-type M. smegmatis and the Δ5008-5009 strain were cultured to late log phase with or without 5 μM CuSO₄. Total RNA was extracted using the RiboPure™-Bacteria Kit following the manufacturer's protocol. One microgram of total RNA was used for library preparation using the NEBNext Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit and loaded onto an Illumina HiSeq 2500 instrument for sequencing. Cutadapt was used to process the technical sequences in fastq format. Clean data were aligned to the reference genome using Hisat2 software (v2.0.1). Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 Bioconductor package, and Padj of genes was set < 0.05 to detect differentially expressed genes.

Quantitative RT–PCR (qPCR)

Isolation of RNA and cDNA preparation were performed as described previously [62], and qPCR analysis was subsequently carried out following the manufacturer's instructions. Amplification specificity was assessed using a melting curve analysis. Gene expression levels were normalized to the levels of 16S rRNA gene transcripts. The degrees of expression change were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Average relative expression levels and standard deviations were determined from two independent experiments.

Bacterial sedimentation assay

The wild type and Δ5008-5009 were cultured in customized Middlebrook 7H9 broth with 0, 5, 10 or 20 μM copper to late logarithmic phase and harvested for determination of sedimentation. A 1-mL aliquot was taken and left standing for 30 min at room temperature. OD600 was measured of the supernatant and sediment. Sedimentation index was calculated as ODsupernatant/ODsediment.

Cell phospholipid determination by LC-MS

Phospholipid profiling was performed using LC-MS following an adapted protocol based on previously described methodology [63]. Bacterial cells (∼200 mg wet weight) were harvested by centrifugation and lysed in 1 mL of ice-cold methanol/water/chloroform (4:1:1, v/v/v) via ultrasonication. After centrifugation (15,000 rpm, 10 min, 4°C), 0.8 mL of the supernatant was collected for analysis. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Acquity BEH C18 column (Waters, Milford, USA) at 45°C using a binary gradient consisting of 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B). The gradient programme was set as follows: 5% to 20% B over 2 min, followed by a linear increase to 100% B. Mass spectrometry was performed on a Waters VION IMS Q-TOF instrument operating in positive ionization mode. Key source parameters included a source temperature of 120°C, a desolvation temperature of 500°C, and a desolvation gas flow of 900 L/h. Data were acquired in centroid mode across a mass range of m/z 50–1000, with a scan time of 0.1 s and an interscan delay of 0.02 s, yielding a total run time of 13 min. A pooled quality control sample, prepared by combining equal volumes from each experimental sample, was analyzed periodically to monitor system performance.

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of atp7A in RAW264.7 macrophages

Guide RNA (gRNA) targeting Atp7A was selected using ZiFit (available at http://zifit. partners.org/ZiFiT/ChoiceMenu.aspx). The gRNA was cloned into lentiCRISPR (AddGene 52961) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Lentivirus was produced in HEK293 T cells by transfecting lentiCRISPR – Atp7A-gRNA with helper plasmids pMD2.G (AddGene 12259) and psPAX2 (AddGene 12260) following standard protocols. After 72 h, harvested viruses were filtered and then incubated with RAW264.7 cells and 8 μg/mL polybrene. Transduced RAW264.7 cells were allowed to rest for 4 h in fresh medium before selection with puromycin 3 μg/mL; clones propagated from a single cell were picked after 2 days of antibiotic selection. Complete knockout of Atp7A in selected clonal lines was verified by both immunoblotting and DNA sequencing.

Bacterial phagocytosis and survival assays

The wild type and Δ5008-5009 were grown to stationary phase (load group) and incubated with macrophages at a macrophage/bacteria ratio of 1:10. Stationary phase bacteria were plated onto LBG-agar plates for CFU counting to provide bacterial load values. Phagocytosis was allowed to proceed for 4 h at 37°C and the extracellular bacteria were removed by two washes with PBS. One set of samples (uptake group) was lysed in 0.025% SDS and plated onto LBG-agar plates for CFU counting to provide bacterial uptake values. The remaining set (kill group) was further incubated for 24, 48 or 72 h, as indicated, at 37°C in DMEM medium containing gentamycin (25 μg/mL) and CuSO4 (20 μM) when needed to allow bacterial killing to occur. Bacterial cells were lysed and plated onto LBG-agar for CFU counting. The bacterial phagocytosis rate was determined by dividing the number of colonies in the uptake group by those in the load group. The bacterial survival rate was determined by dividing the number of colonies in the killing group by those in the uptake group.

Ethics statement and biosafety guarantee

All animal procedures strictly adhered to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Institute of Laboratory Animal Science (ILAS) at the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (CAMS) (Approval No. ZLJ21001). Mice were bred and housed under specific-pathogen-free conditions at the CAMS animal facility (Certificate No. SYXK (Jing) 2014-0029). All experiments involving M. tuberculosis infection, both in vitro and in vivo, were reviewed and approved by the Biosafety Committee of the Animal Biosafety Level 3 (ABSL-3) laboratory at ILAS, CAMS.

Mouse infection experiments

Mice were intravenously infected with 2 × 107 CFU/mouse of one of the following strains: Mtb H37Rv, mutant strain Δrv1273c-rv1272c or complementation strain Δrv1273c-rv1272c::rv1273c-rv1272c on day 0. On day 28, the mice were euthanized. The clinical signs (such as appetite, mobility and appearance) were observed and recorded during the infection period, the organs including lung, spleen and liver were collected for gross pathological and histopathological changes analysis, spleen size was measured and compared between groups (normalized to body weight). Each of the above organs were sectioned into two parts, one for pathological analysis, and one for CFU analysis, which was homogenized in 1 mL normal saline and 100 μL in 1 mL was plated on L-J medium and incubated for 28 days at 37°C for evaluation the whole organ CFUs. Tissues used for CFU analysis were the left upper lobe of the lung, half of the spleen near the head, and the right ventral dorsal lobe of the liver, and the rest of lung, spleen and liver were used for histopathological analysis.

Histopathological analysis

Lung, spleen and liver tissues were isolated and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and then embedded in paraffin for haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Histopathological changes were assessed by image analysis software NDP.VIEW 2 (Hamamatsu Photonics). The lesions in lungs, spleens and livers were quantified as percentage of tissues occupied by lesions (the sum of lesion areas / total tissue area) in the H&E stained digital slides. As for lung lesion, the percentages of TB granulomatous lesion and inflammation of the alveolar wall were calculated independently, and granulomatous lesions were circled and measured individually. The percentage of lesions was obtained by calculating the sum of lesion areas occupied the visual field area. The percentage of inflammatory lesions was similarly calculated. In addition, the percentage of the total area of splenic white pulp destruction and the percentage of the total area of liver granulomatous lesions were calculated under the full view of tissue slices. Results of three or more independent experiments are shown as mean ± SEM.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 6, with data presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05. Functional enrichment analysis of these DEGs was performed based on the KEGG database using the R package clusterProfiler (version 3.6.0). To capture all available annotations, the significance cutoff for pathway inclusion was set to p < 1. Results were visualized using bar plots or dot plots. Group comparisons were evaluated by Student's t-test or two-way ANOVA, as appropriate, with statistical significance denoted as follows: *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001.
