Association between Maxillary and Mandibular Length and Crowding in Classes 1, 2, and 3 Skeletal Malocclusion: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Marshal Lucas, Annapurna Kannan, Vignesh Kailasam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between dental crowding and skeletal base length is essential to recognize and consider during the stage of treatment planning.AimTo determine the correlation between maxillary and mandibular length and the quantum of crowding in various malocclusions.MethodsSeven electronic databases—PubMed, OVID, LILACS, Web of Science, Scopus, Cochrane Library, and SciELO—were searched until April 2024 with no restriction on the year of publication. The risk of bias (RoB) assessment was performed using Downs and Black's quality assessment tool.ResultsA total of five articles that fulfilled the eligibility criteria were included, and two articles were used for meta-analysis. Four studies showed a low RoB, and one showed a moderate RoB. Meta-analysis revealed that in class 2 skeletal malocclusion, there was a 4 mm reduction in mandibular length in patients with mandibular crowding >3 mm (95% CI: −5.69 to −2.32; I2 = 0%) in comparison with patients with mandibular crowding <3 mm.ConclusionAccording to the existing evidence, the quantum of crowding was moderate to severe in class 2 skeletal malocclusion with reduced mandibular base length and minimal in class 3 skeletal malocclusion with greater mandibular base length.Clinical significanceThe study highlights a significant correlation between maxillary and mandibular lengths and dental crowding across classes 1, 2, and 3 skeletal malocclusions. Understanding these associations can improve diagnostic accuracy and treatment planning in orthodontics. The findings emphasize the need for tailored approaches based on skeletal discrepancies to effectively manage dental crowding.How to cite this articleLucas M, Kannan A, Kailasam V. Introduction

Dental crowding is defined as the discrepancy that arises between tooth size and the space available for proper alignment.1 Factors such as tooth morphology, arch dimensions, teeth size, premature loss of deciduous teeth, incisor and molar inclination, direction of mandibular growth, and increased perioral musculature are contributory factors toward dental crowding.2–7 Crowded teeth irritate the gingiva, accentuating the difficulty of brushing and flossing effectively, resulting in dental caries.8 It can also cause excessive pressure on the occlusion, leading to temporomandibular disorders.9 Crowded teeth are more likely to chip or crack, which could result in bruxism and radiating pain.9 However, in patients with no history of previous anomalies, there seems to exist an intricate relationship between the length of the maxilla and mandible and crowding.10–12 Few articles have suggested that the quantum of dental crowding was inversely associated with the length of the maxilla and mandible.11,12 Individuals having smaller maxillary and mandibular base lengths experienced greater crowding.12 The quantum of crowding in class 2 skeletal malocclusion was high, while it was low in class 3 skeletal malocclusion. Further, it has been suggested that no sexual dimorphism was present in the degree of crowding observed.13 While in class 1 malocclusion, some authors have reported that a positive correlation existed between maxillary base length and crowding of the maxillary and mandibular arch,12 while others have suggested that there was no association between the maxillary and mandibular base lengths and severity of dental crowding.13

With more literature regarding the etiology behind crowding emerging, understanding these factors will aid in better treatment planning. As the available literature exhibited diverse outcomes across the spectrum, this necessitated a thorough review to systematically assess and synthesize findings to establish a more conclusive understanding.

Methods

Protocol and Registration

The systematic review was prepared as per the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. This systematic review was registered with PROSPERO under the ID number CRD42023491804.14

Research Question

The review question was, “Is there a relationship between crowding and maxillary and mandibular base length in patients seeking orthodontic treatment?”

Exclusion Criteria

Fractured restorations or broken crowns, any tooth anomaly of number, size, form, and position, premature or delayed exfoliation or extraction of deciduous teeth, which may cause secondary crowding, periodontal problems, and any syndromes related to craniofacial structures.

Search Strategy

A systematic search was carried out across seven electronic databases: Medline (via PubMed), Cochrane Library, Scopus, Web of Science, OVID, LILACS, and SciELO. The search approach used Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and the Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” with the combination of the following key terms: (maxilla) OR (mandible) AND (base length) OR (jaw length) AND (crowding) OR (teeth irregularity). The search was performed with no publication year limitations until January 5, 2025. No language restrictions were applied to the search strategy. A manual search was also performed of the included articles' reference lists (Table 1).

Data Collection Process and Analysis

Studies reporting an association between maxillary and mandibular base length and crowding in classes 1, 2, and 3 skeletal malocclusion were identified. Two investigators (ML and AK) selected studies in two phases, commencing with an independent screening of articles based on the research question and eligibility criteria. Titles and abstracts were screened in the initial screening process. In case of incomplete information in the abstract or title, a full-text review of the article was done. Furthermore, the reference lists of the included publications were manually searched to ensure that no relevant articles were excluded. The pool of articles was finally assessed for eligibility for qualitative and quantitative reviews. The two reviewers settled discrepancies through discussion. Any disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (VK). Two reviewers (ML and AK) conducted data analysis independently using a standard, predefined table. The data to be extracted were the author's name, study design, number of participants, age, type of malocclusion, methods of assessment, and outcome (Tables 2 and 3).

Two reviewers independently assessed the RoB for all included studies, and any disagreements were resolved by discussion with the third reviewer.

Data Synthesis

The meta-analysis was performed for studies that were similar in their methodology of conduction, assessment, and reporting. The data were analyzed using the RevMan software (version 5.4). The continuous data of the maxillary base length [Condylion–Point A (Co-A)], mandibular base length [Condylion–Gnathion (Co-Gn)], maxillary crowding, and mandibular crowding were presented as mean and standard deviation. Heterogeneity was evaluated with the I2 statistics ranging from 0 to 100%. I2 values <25% represented low heterogeneity. I2 values ranging from 25 to 75% were regarded as average heterogeneity. I >75% was defined as high heterogeneity.16

Results

Literature Results

The search of the seven databases revealed 4,540 studies. After the exclusion of 879 duplicates, 3,661 records were screened based on title, and 3,597 irrelevant records were eliminated. A total of 64 articles were subjected to abstract-level screening and 57 articles were eliminated after reading the abstract. Seven full-text articles were assessed for eligibility using the framed inclusion and exclusion criteria as guidelines. One article was excluded as the patients were not categorized according to the skeletal base17 and in another, no continuous data for classes 1, 2, and 3 skeletal malocclusions were presented.18 Five studies were included for qualitative assessment. Two studies that assessed class 2 malocclusion were included for meta-analysis. The outcomes of the search strategy were illustrated using the PRISMA flowchart in Figure 1.

Study Characteristics and Risk of Bias

The included studies consisted of retrospective studies published between 2010 and 2020. All five studies included participants from both sexes. Three studies measured crowding using a vernier caliper on dental casts1,13,19 while one study7 used a Boley gauge for the same. Four studies1,7,13,19 assessed the base length of the maxilla from Co-A and mandible from Co-Gn on pretreatment lateral cephalograms. In one study, the amount of crowding was analyzed on the scanned images of the maxillary and mandibular arch using software, and the measurements of the maxilla and mandible base length were analyzed three-dimensionally using CBCT.12 Classes 17,12,13 and 2 1,13,19 skeletal malocclusion were analyzed in three studies respectively, while class 3 skeletal malocclusion13 was analyzed in one study.

The modified Downs and Black assessment tool was used for assessing the RoB in retrospective studies.15 The total number of good-quality studies was four1,7,12,13 while one study was considered to be of moderate quality.19

Outcomes from Included Studies

Qualitative Synthesis of the Result

Maxillary and mandibular length: Khoja et al.13 and Singh and Shivaprakash19 concluded that in class 1 malocclusion, there was no correlation present between the length of the maxilla and mandibular crowding. Janson et al.1 suggested that in class 1 skeletal malocclusion patients, a correlation was present between smaller maxillary lengths and severe mandibular crowding. A strong correlation in class 1 skeletal malocclusion was noted by Montasser and Taha7 between the length of the maxilla and mandibular crowding.7 The same author also noted that smaller mandibular lengths were observed in patients with moderate to severe crowding.7 Crossley et al.12 proposed that male patients had an increased maxillary length. However, Khoja et al.13 found no gender predilection. Janson et al.1 and Singh and Shivaprakash19 suggested that in class 2 skeletal malocclusion patients with smaller mandibular length, severe mandibular crowding was found.

Maxillary and mandibular crowding: According to Janson et al.,1 in class 2 skeletal malocclusion patients, a significant correlation was present between the severity of maxillary and mandibular crowding. Singh and Shivaprakash19 found greater maxillary crowding in class 2 division 1 patients. A direct correlation between maxillary and mandibular crowding was found by Montasser and Taha7 in class 1 skeletal malocclusion.7 No significant difference between maxillary and mandibular arch crowding was found by Khoja et al.13 in classes 1, 2, and 3 skeletal malocclusion patients.

Mandibular crowding: Significant variations in mandibular arch crowding across different malocclusions were found in the study by Khoja et al.,13 with the highest crowding observed in class 1 skeletal malocclusion and the lowest in class 3 skeletal malocclusion.

Quantitative Synthesis of Result

Comparison between maxillary length and mandibular crowding lesser and >3 mm in class 2 skeletal malocclusion: For both group I (mandibular crowding >3 mm) and group II (mandibular crowding <3 mm), the length of the maxilla showed no significant differences, with a range of −1.97 mm (95% CI: −6.59 to 2.66). Discussion

The orthodontist considers factors such as tooth size, arch dimensions, and base length while planning treatment for a patient. Dental crowding is a phenomenon observed by various individuals throughout history. Several factors, including skeletal disparities, eruption patterns, tooth size, and maxillary and mandibular length, might contribute to dental crowding. Crowding is also caused by irregular tooth sizes, limited dental arches, and lack of space for teeth to erupt.3–7 Arch dimensions, including width and length, play a critical role in determining the susceptibility to crowding. Narrow dental arches are more prone to crowding due to limited space for tooth eruption.10

This systematic review evaluated the relationship between maxillary and mandibular length and the quantum of crowding in various malocclusions using data from 380 subjects (152 males and 128 females, classified according to skeletal base (class 1—160, class 2—180, and class 3—40) based on molar relationship and A–Nasion–B (ANB) angle, originating from five retrospective studies.1,7,12,13,19 The age-group of the patients ranged from 11 to 44.5 years for both male and female patients. All patients below 11 years of age were excluded, as all permanent teeth are required to determine the arch perimeter and amount of crowding. This is the first systematic review to determine whether base length and dental crowding are associated.

The research question was framed to answer whether there is a relationship between the length of the jaw bases and the quantum of crowding. It would be logical to expect greater crowding in a reduced jaw base length. However, the method of acquisition for the parameters assessed, that is, crowding and mandibular and maxillary base lengths, differed among the included studies.

We included all standard cephalometric and CBCT measurements for the base lengths and standard cast-based measurements for crowding assessment. Examples of the various methods included vernier calipers on dental casts,1,13,19 Boley gauge,7 and software analysis of scanned dental models.12 For base lengths, the method of acquisition for maxillary base length was Co-A1,7,13,19 and mandibular base length was Co-Gn.1,7,13,19 Crossley et al.12 employed three-dimensional (3D) imaging (CBCT) for base length analysis. Similar landmark-based measurements were subjected to quantitative analysis. In our systematic review, we anticipated that heterogeneity in the included studies would manifest in the various methods of measurement of the base lengths and the crowding assessment. While the heterogeneity in these segments was minimized by combining similar measurements in the meta-analysis, the inherent heterogeneity, which would include variations in the native populations and gender distributions across the included studies, was assessed by I2 values. A subgroup analysis to identify which study contributed most significantly to the heterogeneity was not feasible due to the inclusion of only two studies in the meta-analyses.

In class 2 malocclusion, Janson et al.1 and Singh and Shivaprakash19 showed that individuals with moderate to severe crowding (>3 mm) had reduced maxillary and mandibular base length compared with individuals with slight dental crowding (<3 mm). Khoja et al.13 study reported that in class 2 skeletal malocclusion, the maxillary base length was largest and the mandibular base length was reduced. Overall, these studies showed that crowding was more commonly encountered in class 2 skeletal malocclusion, with reduced mandibular length by 4 mm in group I, with I value of 0% suggestive of low heterogeneity. Overall, the qualitative assessment for studies involving class 2 skeletal malocclusion was moderate to good. A complex relationship between skeletal base lengths and dental crowding in class 1 orthodontic cases is suggested by Montasser and Taha.7 Though there were moderate to high correlations between maxillary and mandibular crowding, the authors suggested that skeletal measures might not have any effect on dental crowding.

With class 1 skeletal malocclusion, Crossley et al.12 study reported that the size of the maxillary and mandibular base length were positively correlated, and mandibular base length size was not related when compared to maxillary or mandibular crowding. According to Montasser and Taha,7 the amount of dental crowding was independent of the skeletal measurements. No significant difference between maxillary and mandibular arch crowding was found by Khoja et al.13 in classes 1, 2, and 3 skeletal malocclusion patients. The qualitative assessment was good for all three studies. For the quantitative analysis, the data by Crossley et al.12 was excluded as it was the only study with 3D measurements. The data given by Khoja et al.13 was not included as the number of male and female participants were not available to correlate the data. This left Montasser and Taha7 to be the only study to have assessed class 1 skeletal malocclusion, and therefore meta-analysis for class 1 skeletal malocclusion could not be performed.

Khoja et al.13 was the only study that assessed class 3 skeletal malocclusion and showed that class 3 skeletal malocclusion individuals had significantly larger mandibular base size, thereby less crowding in the mandibular arch. The qualitative assessment for the study was good. The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was used to assess the certainty of evidence on the relationship between maxillary and mandibular base lengths and dental crowding. For all evaluated relationships, the RoB, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias was rated as “not serious,” indicating robust, consistent, and directly relevant evidence with adequate precision. Nevertheless, the overall certainty of evidence was low (Fig. 5).

GRADE analysis

Compared to the maxillary posterior and overall size factors, there was a positive and stronger association between maxillary tooth size arch length discrepancy (TSALD) and the maxillary anterior size factor. There was no significant association shown between the mandibular anterior size factor and maxillary TSALD. The maxillary anterior size factor and mandibular TSALD showed a weak association.12 The study conducted by Montasser and Taha7 demonstrated that there is no direct correlation between the sex or age of the individuals and the lengths of the mandible and maxilla. Male apical base measurements were statistically significant and consistently larger than analogous female measurements.12,13 The total area, total outer limits, and maximum basal arch width of the maxillary and mandibular basal bones all exhibited statistically significant sex differences.12 The study by Yuvashree et al.18 concluded that children had more crowding when compared to adults.

This systematic review provides an intricate relationship between skeletal factors, arch dimensions, and dental crowding is illuminated by the discussed studies, providing valuable insights for orthodontic treatment planning. Most of the included articles only used a two-dimensional form of assessment.1,7,13,19 Results from a 3D examination would have been more accurate. Comparison was challenging due to the disparities in the cephalometric analyses and the skeletal and dentoalveolar characteristics evaluated between the studies. Future research on the relationship between maxillary and mandibular length and dental crowding, leveraging advanced imaging techniques like CBCT and 3D morphometric analysis, is required to further validate the results of this systematic review. Additionally, studies may investigate the genetic factors that influence jaw dimension variations and their impact on dental alignment.

Conclusion

In class 1 skeletal malocclusion, the quantum of crowding was not associated with the maxillary and mandibular lengths. In class 2 skeletal malocclusion, moderate to severe crowding was associated with reduced mandibular length. In class 3 skeletal malocclusion, minimal crowding and greater mandibular base length were observed. The RoB ranged from moderate to good.

Clinical Significance

The study provides valuable insights into the relationship between jaw lengths and dental crowding in different skeletal malocclusions (classes 1, 2, and 3). By systematically reviewing and analyzing existing data, it highlights how variations in maxillary and mandibular lengths contribute to dental crowding. This knowledge enhances diagnostic precision, allowing orthodontists to tailor treatment plans based on individual skeletal patterns. The findings emphasize the importance of a comprehensive assessment of skeletal structures in managing dental crowding. Ultimately, the study supports the development of more effective and individualized orthodontic interventions.
