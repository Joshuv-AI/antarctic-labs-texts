Machine learning based retrospective cohort study to predict mortality risk in Chinese patients undergoing extracorporeal membrane oxygenation
Authors: Li Tong, Hui Wang, Ming xiang Yin, Jinhua Shen, Ye Yang, Man-man Tang, Suxia Zhang, Yafen Guo, Xueqing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) has emerged as a pivotal temporary extracorporeal life support in critically ill patients, it is associated with a high risk of mortality. Establishing a risk prediction model for ECMO-related mortality is crucial for early identification and timely intervention to improve survival. This study aimed to develop and validate a machine learning (ML) model to predict the risk of ECMO-related mortality among patients admitted to intensive care units (ICUs). Retrospective cohort study. In the ICUs of the three hospitals in Changsha and Changde, two cities of Hunan Province, China. Patients aged ≥ 18 years old undergoing ECMO who were admitted to the ICUs, and the duration of ECMO was ≥ 24 h. The study was conducted among 204 patients undergoing ECMO from January 01, 2020, to December 31, 2023. The patients were randomly divided into a training set (n = 142) and a validation set (n = 62) at a ratio of 7:3. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and multivariate logistic regression were used to screen for factors associated with mortality. Three ML classification models and the Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) interpretation were used to identify the optimal model and assess its diagnostic performance. The final model identified the following factors associated with ECMO-related mortality: body mass index (BMI), APACHE II, history of smoking, white blood cell count (WBC), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), red blood cell distribution width (RDW), and platelet (Plt). The Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB) model was the optimal model, with an average area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.906 (0.893–0.926) in the training set and 0.888 (0.75–1.00) in the validation set. This study presents an internally validated, explainable machine learning model for predicting ECMO mortality, developed using a specific Chinese cohort. Introduction

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is a lifesaving technology commonly used in critical care medicine to provide temporary extracorporeal life support to patients with severe cardiac or pulmonary dysfunction refractory to conventional therapy1. It works by draining venous blood into an extracorporeal circuit, where oxygen and carbon dioxide exchange through an artificial lung, and then returning the re-oxygenated blood to the body2. Over the past two decades, ECMO has been increasingly used to treat a variety of health conditions, including acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), pneumonia, cardiogenic shock, and congenital disabilities, as well as aiding in the recovery of heart and lung transplant patients3. ECMO has evolved into a pivotal life-supporting intervention in treating critically ill patients, with increased clinical applications and improved survival rates4.

Despite the ongoing progress of ECMO as a potential lifesaving support, challenges exist in achieving sufficient pulmonary gas exchange under limited extracorporeal ventilation, leading to a high risk of mortality5. It is estimated that the proportion of patients who died while on ECMO was 35% in the neonatal group, 45% in the pediatric group, and 50% in the adult group3. A meta-analysis found that 37% of patients who received ECMO for COVID-19 in 2020 did not survive to hospital discharge6. Critically ill patients in intensive care units (ICUs) require intensive care services and the expertise of a highly qualified multidisciplinary team, which is challenged by workforce shortages, limited medical resources, and high healthcare costs7,8. Therefore, establishing a predictive model to detect patients undergoing ECMO who are at high risk of mortality is of paramount importance to guide timely interventions to reduce mortality.

Predicting outcomes for ECMO patients is critical for guiding clinical decision-making, resource allocation, and patient-family communication. While several scoring systems, such as the Respiratory ECMO Survival Prediction (RESP) score and the Survival After Venoarterial ECMO (SAVE) score, have been developed to predict mortality in ECMO patients, they have several known limitations. First, many established scores use traditional logistic regression, which may not adequately capture the complex, non-linear interactions within ECMO patient data. Second, existing models were often derived from Western populations and may lack robust external validation across different patient populations, such as the Chinese cohort.

In this study, we aim to bridge these gaps by developing a modern, explainable machine learning model derived from a contemporary Chinese ECMO cohort. Machine learning (ML) is an emerging field in medicine, encompassing a powerful array of algorithms adept at representing, adapting, learning, predicting, and analyzing data9. Our approach leverages ML to capture more nuanced relationships and utilizes SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations), which provides an intuitive explanation of risk predictions by generating individual probabilities of clinical events through integrating determinants10. The tool also meets the need for a combination of biological and clinical models, contributing to the development of personalized medicine.

Methods

Study design and settings

To address the rarity of ECMO cases, we performed a retrospective cohort study utilizing patient data from three hospitals with specialized ECMO centers in Hunan Province: Xiangya Changde Hospital (Changde City), the Second Xiangya Hospital (Changsha City), and the Third Xiangya Hospital (Changsha City). These hospitals were selected due to their high volume of ECMO cases, which was necessary to achieve an adequate sample size. The selected hospitals are all affiliated with Xiangya Hospital of Central South University, one of the best hospitals in southern China known for medical treatment, education, and research. As members of a shared medical alliance, they benefit from pooled resources, expertise, and standardized treatment protocols. These standards are maintained through regular critical care training provided by the central Xiangya Hospital, thereby mitigating potential variations in ECMO practice across the study sites. Our study was conducted in accordance with the TRIPOD + AI reporting guidelines11.

Participants

All patients undergoing ECMO who were admitted to the ICUs of the three hospitals from January 01, 2020, to December 31, 2023, were included in this study. The inclusion criteria of the participants were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18; (2) duration of ECMO ≥ 24 h. The 24-hour ECMO duration criterion was selected to exclude cases of early mortality and immediate technical failures, thereby focusing on patients receiving a sustained course of ECMO. We excluded patients with missing values for the two critical variables: ECMO-model (V-V/V-A) and mortality. Of the 368 patients who received ECMO during the study, 257 met the pre-defined inclusion criteria. Exclusion of patients with missing data for either ECMO (n = 15) or mortality (n = 38) yielded a final analytic sample of 204 patients. Details of patient enrollment are shown in Fig. 1.

Study indicators

All data were obtained from the patients’ electronic medical records at first admission to the ICU. The data sources for all variables used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Two independent investigators (XX and YY) performed data extraction using a standardized data collection form. To assess inter-rater reliability, a random subset of 20% of patient charts was double-extracted. Cohen’s kappa statistics were calculated for categorical variables, and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated for continuous variables. The results demonstrated a high degree of inter-rater agreement (kappa > 0.8; ICC > 0.9). All discrepancies between the two investigators were identified and resolved through a consensus-based discussion, with a senior clinician (ZZ) providing adjudication when necessary.

The primary outcome was ECMO-related mortality. The independent variables included 35 factors under the following categories: (1) General data, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), diagnosis, past medical history, history of smoking, and history of alcohol use; (2) Clinic data, including ECMO-model, ECMO duration (hours), the length of hospital stay (hours), the length of ICU stay (hours), ventilation duration (hours), anticoagulants use, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation score (APACH II), temperature (T,°C), respiration rate (RR, cycles per minute), heart rate (HR, beats per minute), systolic blood pressure (SBP, mmHg), diastolic blood pressure (DBP, mmHg), and blood oxygen saturation (SpO2,%); (3) Laboratory examination data, including lymphocyte (109/L), neutrophil (109/L), white blood cell count (WBC,109/L), red blood cell count (RBC,1012/L), C-reactive protein (CRP, mg/L), procalcitonin (PCT, ug/L), hemoglobin (Hb, g/L), platelet (109/L), red blood cell distribution width (RDW,%), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT, second), international normalized ratio (INR), thrombin time (TT, second), prothrombin time (PT, second), fibrinogen (Fbg, g/L), and D-Dimer (D-II, mg/L). The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality of patients undergoing ECMO, who were admitted to the ICUs of the three hospitals from January 01, 2020, to December 31, 2023.

Statistical analysis

All Statistical analyses were performed using R version 3.6.3 and Python version 3.7. For covariates with incomplete data, we employed random forest imputation using the missForest algorithm, assuming data are Missing at Random (MAR) and following the methodological framework for prediction model studies described by Zamanipoor Najafabadi et al.12. This method can maximize statistical power and minimize bias from case exclusion. A sensitivity analysis was performed to compare the imputed dataset with the original raw data (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3), confirming that the imputation did not significantly alter the data’s overall structure under the MAR assumption.

Continuous data were described using means±standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed variables and medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for non-normally distributed variables. The student’s t-test was used to compare group differences in normally distributed data, and the Mann–Whitney U-test was used to compare group differences in non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (%), and the chi-square test was used to compare group differences. A two-sided P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The prediction model was built as follows. First, the random number method was used to randomly divide the patients into a training set (n = 142) and a validation set (n = 62) according to the ratio of 7:3. Second, we used the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis to select the optimal set of predictive features based on P < 0.05. LASSO is a statistical tool that helps build a simpler, more efficient prediction model13. LASSO performs variable selection by penalizing the coefficients of less influential predictors, and, in some cases, completely removing them from the model13. We used this technique to address the high number of candidate predictors and prevent overfitting. A known limitation, however, is that when predictors are highly correlated, LASSO tends to arbitrarily select only one of them and discard the others, even if they possess similar predictive power.

Third, from a pool of machine learning approaches, we selected three models—Logistic regression, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB)—to build prediction models. Logistic regression was chosen as a simple, interpretable baseline model, which is particularly valuable in a clinical context. GNB was selected for its computational efficiency and effectiveness on smaller datasets, as well as its simple, probabilistic modeling approach. XGBoost was used as a powerful ensemble method to capture potential complex, non-linear relationships within the dataset. The performance of these three models was evaluated on both the training and validation sets, based on which the optimal model was selected. The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to assess the diagnostic performance of the predictive models14. Fourth, the optimal model was verified and tested by cross-verifying the training set with 10 folds and evaluating the testing set.

Fifth, Python (shap 0.39.0) was used to generate SHAP importance and contribution plots by calculating each feature’s contribution to the model’s predictions. SHAP is a tool that explains how a machine learning model makes predictions for individual patients15,16. Instead of treating the model as a “black box,” SHAP provides transparency by assigning an importance value to each factor15,16. It shows which factors increase or decrease the predicted risk, helping clinicians understand the model’s reasoning rather than relying on a single, uninterpreted score15,16.

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the ethics committee of the First People’s Hospital of Changde (No. YX-2023-113-02). The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the study’s retrospective design. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Sample characteristics

The study population comprised 204 subjects in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Table 1 presents the sample characteristics at baseline and the comparison between the training and validation sets. The patients had a median age of 53.0 (37.0–60.0), with two-thirds being males (66.7%). Their mean body mass index (BMI) was 25.22 ± 3.88, and their mean acute physiology and chronic health evaluation II (APACHE II) score was 31.68 ± 8.39. Nearly one-third had a history of alcohol use (33.3%), and 41.7% had a history of smoking. There were no significant differences in all sample characteristics between the two groups (p > 0.05). Among the 204 patients undergoing ECMO, 101 died, accounting for 49.51% of the total patients.

Screening of factors associated with mortality

In the training cohort (n = 142, 71 events), we addressed the low Events Per Variable (EPV) ratio of 2.03 by using LASSO regression for variable selection. After optimizing the penalty parameter via 10-fold cross-validation, LASSO identified nine predictors (EPV = 7.89) from a total of 35 independent variables (Table 2). The nine predictors were BMI, APACHII, percutaneous oxygen saturation (SPO2), history of smoking, white blood cell count (WBC), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), red cell distribution width (RDW), international normalized ratio (INR), and platelet (Plt) (Fig. 2a and b). These were then refined using a multivariable logistic regression with backward elimination (P < 0.05), yielding a final model with 7 predictors (BMI, APACHII, history of smoking, WBC, APTT, RDW, and Plt) and a robust EPV of 10.14. For validation, we used a separate cohort (n = 62, 30 events, EPV = 4.29). A lower EPV is acceptable in this context, as the validation set was used only to assess the model’s performance with fixed coefficients, not to train it further.

Comparison of three predictive models

Logistic, XGBoost, and GNB were trained and repeated 10 times, and the models were evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC) values. The results showed that the AUCs of the XGBoost, GNB, and Logistic models on the training set were 1.0, 0.92, and 0.88, respectively (Fig. 3a). The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value of the three models are shown in Table 3. The AUC of the GNB model was 0.91 (0.79–1.00.79.00), the highest in the validation set (Fig. 3b). The decision curve analysis (DCA) showed that the GNB model had the best clinical utility (Fig. 3c). The Calibration curves for the GNB model demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities in the validation cohort (Fig. 3c). This was further supported by a Brier score of 0.136 (SD: 0.040), where lower values indicate better calibration (Fig. 3d). Therefore, the GNB model was identified as optimal.

Final model evaluation

GNB analysis and 10-fold cross-validation were performed on the training set and validation set. The results showed that the average AUC was 0.906(0.893–0.926) in the training set (Fig. 4a) and 0.888 (0.75–1.00) in the validation set (Fig. 4b). The AUCs of the training and validation sets finally stabilized at about 0.88. The learning curves for the training and validation sets showed a good fit and high stability (Fig. 4c). The model demonstrated acceptable calibration in the validation set (Fig. 4d). We conducted a sensitivity analysis by introducing a center interaction term into the final GNB model. The interaction terms were found to be non-significant (P > 0.05 for all interactions). These results indicated that the GNB model could be used to establish the final model.

As shown in Fig. 5a and b, the contributions of the features to the model were evaluated using average SHAP values, which were displayed in descending order. Additionally, the SHAP dependence plot was used to help understand how a single feature affected the model’s output. BMI, smoking, and APACHE II scores were identified as the top three most important indicators. In addition, we provided two typical examples to illustrate the model’s interpretability: a surviving patient with a low SHAP score (0.08) (Fig. 5c) and a deceased patient with a higher SHAP score (0.75) (Fig. 5d).

Development of a calculator based on the GNB model

We developed a calculator of the ECMO-pred with seven factors (BMI, APACHII, history of smoking, WBC, APTT, RDW, Plt) for ECMO-related mortality risk in critically ill patients, clearly labeling it as a research prototype for illustrative purposes only (Supplementary File S2). Users can predict ECMO-related mortality risk in critically ill patients by entering values for seven features into the text boxes. After calculating the outputs from the GNB model ECMO-pred, the mortality risk probability will be displayed for ICU patients undergoing ECMO.

Discussions

Since the 1970 s, ECMO has been recognized as a therapeutic and supportive treatment for failing lungs and/or heart when standard supportive therapy, such as mechanical ventilation, is insufficient17. Therefore, the application of ECMO in critically ill patients previously considered unsalvageable instills hope of saving lives through advanced techniques and improved management18. However, ECMO is also associated with a high risk of mortality, which may be affected by patient selection, timing of ECMO cannulation, and strategic planning and preparation19. Although many risk factors for mortality in ECMO patients have been identified, no predictive model has yet been established. In this retrospective cohort study, we developed a risk-prediction model for ECMO-related mortality using multiple ML methods among ICU patients.

Our finding that the GNB model is the best for ECMO patient mortality in our cohort fills a specific gap in the literature. While established scores such as RESP and SAVE have been used, our model offers potential advantages in this patient population. Both the RESP and SAVE scores were primarily derived from large, multinational registries that predominantly included Western patient data (e.g., the ELSO registry). The generalizability of these specific predictive variables and their assigned weights to Chinese populations, which may differ in underlying comorbidities, treatment patterns, or genetic predispositions, is under ongoing clinical investigation. Our model, trained and validated exclusively on data from three hospitals within a unified Chinese network, likely captures specific regional or clinical practice patterns pertinent to this localized setting. Moreover, existing scores do not offer the patient-specific explainability provided by our SHAP analysis, which is critical for clinician trust and for identifying modifiable risk factors. For example, our SHAP results provide transparent insights into which specific patient variables contributed most to a positive or negative outcome, enhancing clinical understanding beyond a single-point score. By leveraging modern machine learning techniques and prioritizing interpretability, our model provides a complementary tool for clinical decision-making that addresses shortcomings in current prediction methods.

Our results showed that among the patients undergoing ECMO in the ICU, 49.51% died during the study period. We used three ML models to predict ECMO-related mortality. After analyzing AUC, DCA, and calibration curves, we found that GNB performed better than other ML models. However, it has always been a challenge to explain ML predictive models more comprehensively and present the predictive results to clinicians in a visual manner. Therefore, we applied the SHAP method to GNB to achieve the best predictive performance and interpretability. The final model identified the following factors associated with ECMO-related mortality: BMI, APACHE II score, smoking history, WBC count, APTT, RDW, and platelet count.

Our study identified multiple biological blood-related indicators associated with mortality in ECMO, which is consistent with previous studies. A study showed that higher APTT and APACHE II scores were associated with a higher risk of hemorrhagic complications in patients on ECMO20. Coagulopathy can cause thrombus and thromboembolism in both the patient and the circuit itself, leading to increased morbidity and mortality in ECMO patients21. A meta-analysis demonstrated that patients with mixed or intracerebral bleeding have a longer mean APTT than those without bleeding22. The initiation of extracorporeal support triggers complex coagulation and inflammatory processes, thereby increasing the need for systemic anticoagulation23. However, therapeutic anticoagulation bears an additional risk of severe bleeding. A meta-analysis identified ECMO as a significant risk factor for poor outcomes and mortality22. Therefore, anticoagulation monitoring is necessary to maintain the balance between anticoagulatory and procoagulant factors22.

RDW is a standard, readily available metric that quantifies the variation in red blood cell (RBC) size and volume in a blood sample. In the context of critically ill patients on ECMO, an elevated RDW is associated with worse adverse outcomes mainly through several interconnected pathways, including systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and impaired oxygen delivery24. It also reflects the patient’s overall physiological reserve and the severity of their underlying pathology. WBC reflects the inflammatory response and potential for infection, both of which are common and critical issues in ECMO patients25. A higher WBC count indicates a stronger and potentially less controlled inflammatory state, which can lead to further organ damage and ultimately death25. Low platelet counts significantly increase the risk of bleeding complications, including potentially fatal intracranial hemorrhages26. Bleeding is a frequent and severe complication during ECMO and is directly linked to higher mortality26.

In addition, we found some clinical factors associated with increased mortality of ECMO, with BMI, smoking, and APACHE II as the top three critical indicators. ECMO is an effective strategy for the salvage treatment of critically ill patients with refractory cardiac or respiratory failure. Prevention and treatment of bleeding, infection, and organ dysfunction should be emphasized during ECMO use21. Clinicians are increasingly confronted with potential ECMO indications in obese patients. Severely obese patients may challenge the risk-benefit balance due to anticipated difficulties with cannulation and achieving sufficient blood flow27. A study showed that patients with BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2 more frequently suffered from cardiovascular, renal, and device-related complications compared to patients with BMI < 35 kg/m227. Smoking history has been identified as a risk factor for mortality among patients undergoing EMCO in various studies, indicating that special attention should be paid to patients with a history of smoking28. The APACHE II is a scoring system for disease severity that has been well established to be associated with mortality risk, further justifying its inclusion in our current model among patients undergoing EMCO29.

While SHAP analysis provides critical insights into the features driving our model’s predictions, it is essential to distinguish between model interpretability and clinical actionability. For instance, while high BMI was identified as a significant risk factor, this finding primarily explains a risk profile rather than suggesting an immediate, modifiable intervention. The utility of our SHAP analysis lies in its ability to transparently identify the relative importance of factors like WBC, APTT, and Plt. For a specific patient, this provides clinicians with a deeper understanding of the model’s prediction, which can complement their clinical expertise and inform patient management strategies. Crucially, our SHAP results describe correlations captured by the model and should not be interpreted as causal relationships. Interventions based on a single SHAP plot require careful consideration and further clinical investigation.

This study has several limitations. First, the study’s sample size of 204 cases is recognized as small in the context of general research. However, this sample is of significant size for a study on ECMO patients, given the rarity of the procedure and its confinement to specialized medical centers. While this practical constraint affects the external validity of our findings, this sample represents a substantial group of ECMO patients from a specific regional context. Future studies, potentially through larger collaborative registries, would be needed to overcome this limitation and provide more generalizable insights. Second, the convenience sample drawn from high-volume ECMO centers may introduce selection bias. This trade-off was made to ensure a sufficient sample size for this rare procedure. Consequently, our findings may not be fully representative of patients treated in less-specialized hospital settings. Future investigations utilizing a random sampling approach across a broader range of healthcare environments are necessary to confirm and extend our results. Third, our study included only patients who received ECMO support for ≥ 24 h, which may introduce selection bias by excluding patients who experienced early mortality or technical failure. This biases the study cohort towards a more stable, healthier patient population. Due to limitations in our data collection, we couldn’t perform further analysis to compare the baseline characteristics between included patients and those excluded. Future studies should enhance data collection protocols to allow for such comparisons, which would help to better quantify the extent of selection bias.

Fourth, the exclusion of patients with missing values for the critical variables of the ECMO model and mortality may introduce missing data bias, particularly as the missingness may not be random. Due to data constraints, we were unable to formally compare the characteristics of included and excluded patients to quantify this bias. Future studies should prioritize complete data collection and, when necessary, employ more advanced analytical techniques to address missing data. Fifth, the validation cohort’s modest sample size (n = 62) resulted in an EPV ratio of 4.29, which is below the traditional threshold of 10. However, the EPV in the training set (10.14) was adequate for model development. Since the validation set was used solely to assess the model’s performance with fixed coefficients, not for parameter development, this lower EPV is methodologically acceptable. This smaller sample size for validation may lead to less precise estimates of predictive performance, resulting in wider confidence intervals. Future studies using larger, multicenter cohorts are warranted to validate our findings and confirm the model’s generalizability externally. Sixth, during the model development phase, the XGBoost algorithm was found to exhibit a significant overfitting problem, achieving a perfect AUC of 1.0 on the training data. This issue persisted despite our application of standard regularization methods. While the XGBoost model was ultimately excluded from our final analysis, its behavior underscores the importance of a rigorous validation process, especially when using complex, high-capacity models on a dataset of this size. The final selection of the more stable and GNB model was a deliberate decision to mitigate this risk.

Seventh, the lack of external validation is another significant limitation. The performance of our model on the validation set does not guarantee similar performance across different populations, settings, or time frames. The model is a research prototype that requires a robust, multicenter external validation using independent patient data to assess its generalizability and predictive performance across diverse clinical settings. An impact analysis would be needed after successful external validation to determine if the model’s use improves clinical outcomes. Eighth, we were unable to incorporate granular smoking history, such as pack-years or specific exposure categories, due to data limitations. Relying solely on a binary smoking variable may not fully capture the dose-dependent risk associated with smoking. Future research efforts should prioritize the collection of detailed, longitudinal smoking data to better characterize this important risk factor. Ninth, another limitation concerns our use of SHAP to interpret model predictions. While a powerful tool, SHAP assumes feature independence, and its attributions can be misleading when features are correlated. This potential instability in feature attribution underscores the need for cautious interpretation of our SHAP plots, which reflect the model’s learned correlations rather than definitive causal drivers.

Conclusion

This study presents an internally validated, explainable machine learning model for predicting ECMO mortality, developed using a specific Chinese cohort. The model demonstrated strong performance within our study population and identified key factors associated with ECMO-related mortality, including BMI, APACHE II, smoking history, WBC, APTT, RDW, and platelet count. While the model performed robustly within our dataset, its generalizability and clinical utility remain to be established through external validation.
