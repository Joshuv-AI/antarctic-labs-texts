DEtect : Determining Ease and Textual Clarity of German Text Simplifications
Maria Korobeynikova Alessia Battisti Lukas Fischer Yingqiang Gao, Department of Computational Linguistics, University of Zurich, Switzerland {maria.korobeynikova, yingqiang.gao}@uzh.ch

Abstract
Current evaluation of German automatic text simplification (ATS) relies on general-purpose metrics such as SARI, BLEU, and BERTScore, which insufficiently capture simplification quality in terms of simplicity, meaning preservation, and fluency. While specialized metrics like LENS have been developed for English, corresponding efforts for German have lagged behind due to the absence of human-annotated corpora. To close this gap, we introduce DEtect , the first German-specific metric that holistically evaluates ATS quality across all three dimensions of simplicity, meaning preservation, and fluency, and is trained entirely on synthetic large language model (LLM) responses. Our approach adapts the LENS framework to German and extends it with (i) a pipeline for generating synthetic quality scores via LLMs, enabling dataset creation without human annotation, and (ii) an LLM-based refinement step for aligning grading criteria with simplification requirements. To the best of our knowledge, we also construct the largest German human evaluation dataset for text simplification to validate our metric directly. Experimental results show that DEtect achieves substantially higher correlations with human judgments than widely used ATS metrics, with particularly strong gains in meaning preservation and fluency. Beyond ATS, our findings highlight both the potential and the limitations of LLMs for automatic evaluation and provide transferable guidelines for general language accessibility tasks. Automatic text simplification (ATS) aims to improve language accessibility for diverse target group users, including language learners, persons with cognitive disabilities, and persons with low literacy levels
(
Gao et al. 2025
)
. While German ATS research has advanced with the emergence of multilingual LLMs and new parallel datasets
(
Ebling et al. 2022
;
Stodden et al. 2023
)
, automatic evaluation relies on metrics like BLEU
(
Papineni et al. 2002
)
, SARI
(
Xu et al. 2016
)
, and BERTScore
(
Zhang et al. 2020
)
, which focus on N-gram overlap or embedding similarity rather than directly measuring the core criteria of simplification quality:
simplicity
,
meaning preservation
, and
fluency
, leading to weak correlations with human assessments
(
Alva-Manchego et al. 2021
)
. Recent English-specific metrics like LENS
(
Maddela et al. 2023
)
have addressed this challenge by training models on human-rated quality scores based on core criteria, achieving stronger alignment with human judgments than traditional metrics. However, no equivalent metric exists for German
(
Stodden 2024a
)
.

To address this research gap, we introduce
DEtect
, the first German-specific ATS evaluation metric designed to directly assess text simplification quality across the three core dimensions mentioned above. Unlike LENS, which relies on human annotations, we leverage LLMs as synthetic evaluators to generate training data for this learnable metric. We construct
SimpEvalDE
, a benchmark dataset combining existing German corpora with outputs from six ATS models, annotated with quality scores from three open-source LLMs. Additionally, we explore LLM-based refinement of grading criteria to improve evaluation consistency. Our work addresses three key research questions (RQs) in the ATS context:

RQ1

Can quality-based evaluation metrics be adapted for German without human-annotated data?

RQ2

Can LLMs provide reliable supervision for training learnable evaluation metrics?

RQ3

Can LLMs systematically refine evaluation rubrics to improve judgment consistency?

With
DEtect
, we aim at proposing general guidelines for language accessibility tasks when leveraging LLMs in a Human-in-the-Loop setting, thereby contributing to a deeper understanding of how synthetic evaluation pipelines can complement human judgment and foster the development of more reliable, cross-linguistic evaluation frameworks.

2
DEtect
: Learnable Evaluation Metric for German Text Simplification

The methodology of
DEtect
is designed to closely follow the LENS framework
Maddela et al. 2023
, but it replaces human-annotated data with LLM-generated synthetic data and refines the grading rubric through an iterative LLM-human feedback loop. It constructs a synthetic supervision pipeline in four main steps (see Figure
2
):

•

STEP 1
: We compile a parallel corpus of complex sentences and reference simplifications, denoted
SimpEvalDE
.

•

STEP 2
: For each complex sentence in
SimpEvalDE
, six alternative simplifications are generated.

•

STEP 3
: Each generated simplification is scored along three dimensions:
simplicity
,
meaning preservation
, and
fluency
, by prompting three open-source LLMs as judges. These scores are then used to train
DEtect
. Specifically:

–

Prompts are initialized and iteratively refined with expert feedback in a multi-round process;

–

The test set of
SimpEvalDE
is manually scored by human annotators according to the same criteria, following a simplified version of the
Rank & Rate
protocol introduced in LENS;

–

The training set is used to fine-tune
DEtect
, implemented as a RoBERTa-based regression model
(
Liu et al. 2019
)
, to predict LLM-derived quality scores from the similarity between a simplification, its complex source, and references.

•

STEP 4
: The trained
DEtect
model is evaluated on the test set by measuring its correlation with both human and LLM quality scores, as well as with established ATS metrics such as SARI
(
Xu et al. 2016
)
, BLEU
(
Papineni et al. 2002
)
, and BERTScore
(
Zhang et al. 2020
)
.

2.1
SimpEvalDE

The
SimpEvalDE
dataset is designed to mirror the structure of
SimpEval
past
(100 training sentences) and
SimpEval
2022
(60 test sentences) introduced by LENS, where the target simplification level was not explicitly defined. Our manual analysis, however, shows that the simplifications in these datasets achieve an average Flesch Reading Ease (FRE) score of 67
(
Flesch 1948
)
, which roughly corresponds to CEFR level B1
(
Council of Europe 2020
;
Natova 2021
)
.

Building on this observation, we turn to existing German resources for ATS, which provide material at CEFR levels B1 and A2. Two variants are available: (i) LHA-APA
(
Spring et al. 2021
)
, consisting of 1,828 automatically aligned Complex-Simple pairs at CEFR B1/A2, and (ii) the smaller
DEplain-apa

(
Stodden et al. 2023
)
, which is manually aligned but lacks complex sentences. Given the importance of gold-standard references for training our metric, we attempt to merge
DEplain-apa
with exact matches from LHA-APA. This procedure produces only 19 complete matches, and further attempts to expand alignments for the B1/A2 levels reveal many incorrect pairs.

Manual inspection confirms that the APA corpus, originally constructed for document-level simplification, poses challenges for sentence-level alignment. Even correctly matched sentences often fail to correspond fully, with simplifications either omitting key information from the complex sentence or introducing unrelated content (see Appendix
A.1
for examples). To address this, we design a systematic filtering approach: adjusted BERTScore is used to identify high-similarity matches across the LHA-APA corpus, followed by a manual review of candidate pairs. While this strategy successfully eliminates low-quality pairs, i.e., 219 out of 532 randomly selected low-similarity cases prove to be entirely incorrect, it ultimately yields only 26 additional gold-standard pairs.

To reach a size of 160 rows, we therefore relax the multi-reference requirement by manually including single-reference matches (38 Complex-Simple pairs at CEFR B1 and 19 at CEFR A2) and incorporate test set pairs from
DEplain-apa
, where both sentences fully preserve the same meaning.

The final dataset is then stratified across training and test sets based on match type and simplification strategy, following the LENS categories of
delete
,
split
, and
paraphrase
operations; the distributions can be found in Appendix
A.4
. We use an empirical algorithm to identify the simplification strategy, shown as follows:

2.2
Generating Text Simplifications

To generate ATS outputs for evaluation, we follow the two-category approach of LENS by selecting six high-performing LLMs: general-purpose instruction-tuned models and task-specific fine-tuned models.

For the instruction-tuned models, we include both German-specific systems (LeoLM;
Björn Plüster and Christoph Schuhmann 2023
and DiscoLlama;
DiscoResearch Team 2024
) and multilingual variants (Qwen2-7B;
Qwen Team 2024
and Llama3-8B;
Llama Team 2024
). All instruction-tuned models are prompted using a German translation of the LENS prompt, augmented with few-shot examples balanced across three simplification strategies. This design follows prior findings that few-shot prompting outperforms zero-shot settings
(
Ryan et al. 2023
)
.

For the task-specific models, we use mBART-
DEplain
and mT5-
DEplain

(
Stodden et al. 2023
)
, both fine-tuned on the
DEplain-apa
dataset underlying
SimpEvalDE
. This setup avoids the need for additional training.

All models are run with their default parameters, except for LeoLM, where adjustments to
temperature
and
top_p
were required to mitigate nonsensical generations observed during preliminary trials. Full configuration details and prompts are provided in Appendix
B
.

2.3
Quality Score Labeling with LLMs

Given the resource constraints that made full human evaluation infeasible, and motivated by the rationale to test the capacity of LLMs for this task, we rely on the LLM-as-a-Judge
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
approach to produce quality score annotations for Complex-Simple pairs. Specifically, we adapt the LENS grading rubric into an LLM-compatible prompt through a Human-in-the-Loop
(
Chang et al. 2024
)
process with a high-capacity model. This prompt is then applied to three smaller distilled models (Distil-Llama-8B, Distil-Qwen-7B, and Zephyr-7B), whose outputs are averaged to yield the final
LLM-Judge
score.

The original LENS rubric employs five discrete rating levels (0, 25, 50, 75, 100) to jointly assess simplicity, meaning preservation, and fluency. However, this design poses several challenges for LLM-based evaluation: (1) vague definitions of core criteria such as “
simplicity
” and the associated simplification levels; (2) an implicit assumption of parallel progression across all dimensions, despite evidence that simplicity and meaning preservation often trade off against each other
(
Cripwell et al. 2024
)
; and (3) hidden assumptions that require explicit clarification when translated into prompts for LLMs. These limitations hinder LLMs from consistently applying the rubric and exacerbate ambiguity in edge cases.

To overcome them, we propose evaluating each criterion separately and then aggregating the results with a weighted formula for the total score, with S =
simplicity
, MP =
meaning preservation
, and F =
fluency
:

\begin{aligned} &\text{Total Score}\\
&=\begin{cases}\min(\text{MP},\text{S},\text{F}),&\text{if }\min(\text{MP},\text{S},\text{F})<25;\\
0.4\text{MP}+0.4\text{S}+0.2\text{F},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\end{aligned}

Following prior findings that
simplicity
and
meaning preservation
are more failure-prone than fluency
(
Cripwell et al. 2024
)
, we assign double weight to the former relative to the latter. In addition, we ground our prompt optimization in concrete German guidelines for Easy Language (German:
Leichte Sprache
,
Bredel and Maaß 2016
), incorporating explicit assumptions, for example, allowing models to assign any score between 0 and 100 rather than restricting them to five discrete levels. Prompt refinement is carried out with the assistance of GPT-4o
(
OpenAI Team 2024
)
as an iterative process:

•

Initial Prompt Creation
: GPT-4o generates
Prompt-Zero
based on the original rubric and its identified limitations.

•

Iterative Refinement
: Four diagnostic examples (meaning omission, added complexity, fluent but incorrect meaning, and unchanged input/output) are used to reveal weaknesses of the current prompt, with human feedback guiding revisions and discussions for the optimized prompt.

•

Final Prompt Generation
: LLM scores and human feedback are combined to produce
Prompt-Final
.

•

Few-shot Enhancement
: Six additional examples graded by GPT-4o are incorporated to distill knowledge into smaller models.

All prompts, examples used, GPT-4o and human feedback, and the final few-shot examples can be found in Appendix
C
. Our inspection of the refined prompt and the few-shot examples confirms that our approach successfully addresses most of the identified issues, providing clearer definitions of the evaluation criteria and improving the handling of edge cases.

To select the three models for the final prompt and compute the
LLM-Judge
score, we analyzed a set of DeepSeek distilled models
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
, including Distil-Qwen-1.5B, Distil-Qwen-7B, Distil-Qwen-14B, Distil-Llama-8B, and Zephyr-7B
(
Tunstall et al. 2024
)
. The analysis focused on correlations between the scores of smaller models (
\leq
8B) and the larger 14B model in order to assess whether the smaller models could serve as reliable proxies and whether they could produce sufficiently diverse outputs. Details of this analysis are provided in Appendix
D
.

Our results show that Distil-Qwen-7B achieves correlations between
r=0.51
and
r=0.78
with Distil-Qwen-14B across the individual evaluation criteria. Although Zephyr-7B exhibits lower diversity in its scores, it still reaches correlations between
r=0.41
and
r=0.58
with Distil-Qwen-14B. Across all models, the highest correlations are consistently observed for
meaning preservation
and the lowest for
fluency
.

Based on these findings, we selected Distil-Llama-8B, Distil-Qwen-7B, and Zephyr-7B to calculate the final
LLM-Judge
scores, aggregated as the arithmetic mean of their individual output quality scores.

2.4
DEtect
Training and Validation

DEtect
builds on the LENS architecture, which concatenates embeddings of the complex sentence, simplified output, and references, along with their dot products and differences. These representations are then fed into a neural network. We introduce three key modifications. First, the model is adapted to predict three separate scores, i.e.,
simplicity
,
meaning preservation
, and
fluency
, instead of the single composite score used in LENS. Second, we incorporate German-specific embeddings WECHSEL
(
Minixhofer et al. 2022
)
in place of the multilingual RoBERTa-XML
(
Liu et al. 2019
)
encoder used by LENS. Third, to address the considerably smaller training dataset, we tune hyperparameters such as the learning rate, dropout, and hidden layer size to reduce overfitting.

We explore several
DEtect
variants with different architectural and training configurations, with each variant building on insights from the previous run, all trained on five epochs and evaluated on the validation set using Pearson correlation and Spearman rank. The final model is selected based on the best validation performance across these metrics. To support multi-criteria prediction, the loss function is defined as the arithmetic mean of the three output scores. We report the configurations of different
DEtect
variants in Table
15
in Appendix
E
.

2.5
Human and Automatic Evaluation

We evaluate the predictions of
DEtect
on the
SimpEvalDE
test set against two reference measures: (1) averaged LLM quality scores (
LLM-Judge
), which capture direct model accuracy using the same criteria on which the model was trained, and (2) human quality scores (
Human-Judge
), which provide an independent benchmark based on human judgment.

Human scores were collected using a customized Google Sheets tool implementing a simplified
Rate & Rank
procedure
(
Maddela et al. 2023
)
. Evaluators first classified each simplification by strategy (split, paraphrase, delete), then rated simplicity, meaning preservation, and fluency according to the same criteria used in the LLM-grading prompt. Finally, they verified score consistency via a separate ranking sheet (see Appendix
F
for details). Three native German-speaking experts independently graded all 360 test pairs.

Finally, we benchmark
DEtect
against standard ATS evaluation metrics (i.e., BLEU
(
Papineni et al. 2002
)
, SARI
(
Xu et al. 2016
)
, BERTScore Precision
(
Zhang et al. 2020
)
) by comparing their correlations with both
LLM-Judge
and
Human-Judge
.

4
Discussion

DEtect
exhibits strong alignment trained on LLM-generated quality scores, demonstrating effective adaptation even under the constraints of smaller datasets and fewer references compared to the original LENS framework:

RQ1: Metric Performance and Adaptability.

Performance varies across evaluation criteria, with
meaning preservation
achieving the highest correlation (
r=0.68
), followed by
fluency
(
r=0.35
) and
simplicity
(
r=0.32
). The metric performs particularly well on split-based simplifications, while paraphrase-based cases remain more challenging, likely reflecting architectural constraints from reduced hidden layer sizes and the reliance on single-reference training. Overall,
DEtect
consistently outperforms standard ATS metrics across all simplification strategies. We observe that its predictions tend to group into high- and low-quality clusters, which is advantageous for identifying strong versus weak outputs, though it is less suited to fine-grained ranking among similarly scored candidates. Finally, because outputs are rescaled for comparability, they no longer map directly onto the discrete levels of the original rubric, and the current evaluation is limited to the news domain. These aspects highlight opportunities for future extensions toward more interpretable scaling and broader domain coverage.

RQ2: LLM-Based Annotation Reliability.

LLM-generated quality scores provided reasonably reliable supervision signals, showing correlations with human judgments ranging from
r=0.77
for
meaning preservation
to
r=0.28
for
simplicity
. Error analysis, however, highlights several limitations: (1) instability across repeated evaluations of identical inputs; (2) occasional German-specific misinterpretations, likely due to the underlying models being trained primarily on English and Chinese data
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
; and (3) incomplete adherence to grading instructions, particularly regarding the penalization of hallucinated content. Moreover, LLMs exhibited artificially high intra-criterion correlations (
r>0.8
), in contrast to human evaluators (
r=0.25
between
simplicity
and
meaning preservation
), suggesting limited sensitivity to the independence of criteria, even when providing seemingly coherent rationales. The reliance on distilled models may have amplified these issues, indicating that larger and more capable models could offer more stable and instruction-following behavior, thereby improving the reliability of synthetic data.

RQ3: Rubric Refinement Impact.

The LLM-refined grading rubric substantially improved evaluation consistency, raising inter-rater agreement from
\alpha=0.32
(as originally reported in LENS) to
\alpha=0.75
. This improvement indicates that LLMs can play a role in enhancing rubric clarity for both human and machine evaluators. Nonetheless, the analysis of LLM prompt feedback highlights remaining ambiguities, particularly in defining what qualifies as “important information” in deletion-based simplifications and in determining how to assess one criterion when others fail completely, pointing to areas where even refined rubrics require further clarification.

5
Related Work

ATS aims to transform complex text into simpler variants while preserving meaning and grammaticality
(
Alva-Manchego et al. 2021
)
. Originally rule-based, ATS progressed through statistical machine translation
(
Alva-Manchego et al. 2020
)
and neural sequence-to-sequence models
(
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997
)
, with transformer architectures becoming dominant for both sentence- and document-level simplification
(
Nisioi et al. 2017
;
Zhang and Lapata 2017
;
Gonzales et al. 2021
)
. Recent advances in LLMs enable zero- and few-shot simplification, which often outperforms task-specific models on English benchmarks
(
Kew et al. 2023
;
Maddela et al. 2023
)
. In German, ATS work ranges from rule-based systems implementing
Leichte Sprache
guidelines
(
Suter et al. 2016
)
to neural approaches, with mBART-based adaptations prevailing as the state-of-the-art
(
Gonzales et al. 2021
;
Anschütz et al. 2023
;
Carrer et al. 2024
;
Stodden 2024b
)
. Emerging multilingual LLMs such as BLOOM
(
Scao et al. 2022
)
show promise for German in zero- and few-shot regimes
(
Stodden 2024b
)
.

Sentence-level ATS requires parallel corpora of complex-simple pairs obtained through manual or automatic alignment
(
Nikolov and Hahnloser 2019
)
. Such resources often involve many-to-many mappings that complicate supervision
(
Ebling et al. 2022
;
Stodden et al. 2023
)
. A central stream of work relies on the Austrian Press Agency (APA) news with CEFR B1/A2 targets, including APA-RST, APA-LHA, and
DEplain
variants
(
Säuberli et al. 2020
;
Spring et al. 2021
;
Stodden et al. 2023
;
Hewett 2023
)
. Other corpora include the Simple German Corpus targeting
Leichte Sprache

(
Toborek et al. 2023
)
, as well as document-level collections such as
20 Minuten
and
Klexikon

(
Gonzales et al. 2021
;
Aumiller and Gertz 2022
)
. Consolidation efforts like De-Lite
(
Jablotschkin et al. 2024
)
aim to unify and deduplicate sources while normalizing complexity labels. Web-derived datasets provide larger coverage but are heterogeneous, whereas professionally simplified texts are of higher quality but less accessible
(
Battisti et al. 2020
;
Ebling et al. 2022
)
.

Evaluation of ATS typically combines human and automated assessment. Human evaluation focuses on
simplicity
,
meaning preservation
, and
fluency

(
Alva-Manchego et al. 2020
)
, though inter-rater reliability remains fragile, particularly for simplicity under absolute scales
(
Stodden 2021
;
Alva-Manchego et al. 2021
)
. Furthermore, the definition of “simplicity” remains highly audience-dependent, shaped by goals in accessibility, domain adaptation, or language learning
(
Stajner 2021
;
Garimella et al. 2022
)
. Automatic metrics are widely used despite their shortcomings. BLEU
(
Papineni et al. 2002
)
and SARI
(
Xu et al. 2016
)
rely on N-gram overlap, while BERTScore
(
Zhang et al. 2020
)
leverages contextual embeddings but may favor conservative or ungrammatical outputs. Readability measures such as Flesch Reading Ease (FRE;
Flesch 1948
) and Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL;
Kincaid et al. 1975
) are often reported but assess
simplicity
only. Structure-aware or reference-free approaches such as SAMSA
(
Sulem et al. 2018
)
, QuestEval
(
Scialom et al. 2021
)
, and MeaningBERT
(
Beauchemin et al. 2023
)
target structural edits or semantic preservation. Recent learnable metrics trained on human judgments, such as LENS
(
Maddela et al. 2023
)
that models continuous quality scoring based on scores grading
simplicity
,
meaning preservation
, and
fluency
directly. For German, EASSE-DE
(
Stodden 2024a
)
adapts tokenization and metric choices, though LENS-style learnable metrics was not applied. Parallel research has also modeled German sentence complexity directly from human labels
(
Naderi et al. 2019
;
Mohtaj et al. 2022b
;
Mohtaj et al. 2022a
)
.

Recent work further explores LLMs for evaluation and grading. Instruction-tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) align models with instructions and preferences
(
Wei et al. 2022a
;
Ouyang et al. 2022
;
Touvron et al. 2023
)
, while distillation transfers these capabilities to smaller, more cost-efficient models
(
Li et al. 2024
)
. Prompt engineering, including example-based prompts
(
Zhou et al. 2023
)
, chain-of-thought prompting
(
Wei et al. 2022b
)
, and automatic prompt or rubric induction
(
Honovich et al. 2023
)
, can substantially improve zero- and few-shot evaluation
(
Gupta et al. 2024
)
. Within the paradigm of LLM-as-a-Judge
(
Zheng et al. 2023
)
, structured prompting methods such as G-Eval
(
Liu et al. 2023
)
increase agreement with expert judgments and scale effectively to complex, multi-criteria assessments
(
Gu et al. 2024
)
.

6
Conclusion

We introduced
DEtect
, the first German-specific learnable metric for evaluating text simplification across
simplicity
,
meaning preservation
, and
fluency
. Developed by adapting the English LENS framework with synthetic LLM generated quality scores,
DEtect
fills the research gap in German text simplification evaluation and consistently outperforms BLEU, SARI, and BERTScore, with particularly strong results in
meaning preservation
. The refined grading rubric derived from LLM feedback also improved inter-rater agreement and reduced the manual effort required for supervision, showing that rubric refinement can enhance consistency for both human and automated evaluation. While some challenges remain, such as confounding factors, LLM instability, linguistic ambiguities specific to German, and residual rubric uncertainty, our study demonstrates the feasibility of creating learnable evaluation metrics through human guided synthetic supervision and provides a scalable foundation for future research on text simplification assessment for language accessibility.

Limitations

Our work has several limitations.
DEtect
relies on synthetic supervision from LLM-generated annotations, which can be unstable across runs, conflate criteria, and occasionally misinterpret German-specific constructions. The evaluation is restricted to the news domain, limiting generalizability to other genres, such as educational or medical texts. In addition, the model tends to cluster outputs into broad quality groups rather than providing fine-grained rankings, and the score rescaling procedure reduces interpretability relative to the original rubric. Our future work would therefore explore more robust annotator models, broader domain coverage, and rubric refinements that better balance interpretability with predictive power.

Appendix C
LLM Grading Rubric Development

C.1
Instruction to Generate
Prompt-Zero

I need you to rewrite the following grading rubric so that it can be used as a prompt for a large language model to evaluate the quality of a simplified sentence(s) compared to its original version and fixes the issues outlined below.

A complex sentence can be simplified by replacing complex words with simpler synonyms (i.e. paraphrasing), deleting unimportant information (i.e. condensing) and/or splitting a long complex sentence into several simpler sentences. The final simplified sentence(s) must be grammatically correct, flow smoothly and retain the main ideas of its original counterpart without changing its meaning.

Current Rubric
:

Rate the simplified sentence(s) based on how well it simplifies the original sentence using the following rubric:

•

100 - Only when the sentence is fully simplified, entirely fluent, and preserves the core meaning of the original sentence.

•

75 - The sentence is somewhat simpler, mostly fluent, and the meaning is close to the original sentence.

•

50 - The sentence is simpler, somewhat fluent, and the meaning is similar to the original sentence.

•

25 - The sentence is equivalently simple, still has some fluency, but the meaning is lost.

•

0 - The sentence is completely unreadable.

Issues with the Current Rubric
:

1.

Lack of clear definitions - The rubric does not explicitly define what makes a sentence simple, fluent, or meaningfully preserved.

2.

Vague language: Words like “somewhat” and “similar” lack precise meaning and could lead to differing interpretations by different evaluators. A good example of a grading criteria level could be: “75 - Mostly simple but may retain minor complexity, such as one or two long words or slightly complex phrasing.”

3.

No separation of criteria - The rubric does not evaluate simplicity, meaning preservation, and fluency independently.

4.

Limited scoring flexibility - The model should be able to assign any score between 0 and 100, not just fixed levels.

5.

Simplicity should not be about how well it is simplified compared to the original but how simple it is in general (i.e. if the original is already simple, it does not need to be simplified).

6.

Meaning preservation does not account for situations where additional information is introduced not present in the original.

7.

It is not clearly stated that the simplification can consist of two sentences if the splitting of a complex sentence simplifies reading.

Definitions of Key Criteria
:

1.

Simplicity: Measures how easy to understand the simplified sentence is, irrespective of the complexity of the original. A simple sentence in German should:

•

Use simple and common words (replace rare or long nouns with a synonym or split compound words using a hyphen, e.g. Atomabkommen -> Atom-Abkommen). Avoid difficult terms and explain if necessary.

•

Use simple sentence structures with minimal subordinate clauses, coordinated clauses, and genitive constructions.

•

Avoid including information in parentheses (e.g. Angela Merkel (die ehemalige Bundeskanzlerin)) or in nested commas (e.g. Angela Merkel, die ehemalige Bundeskanzlerin, …).

•

Use active as opposed to passive voice.

•

Use past perfect instead of past simple.

•

Avoid metaphors, initials/abbreviations, special characters, percentages, or big numbers.

•

Replace numbers written as words (vier) with digits (4).

•

Avoid foreign terms unless they are very well known.

•

Use positive rather than negative sentences and direct speech with words like
“du”
or
“Sie”
where appropriate.

2.

Meaning Preservation: Measures whether the simplified output adequately expresses the meaning of the original sentence, perhaps omitting the least important information.

3.

Fluency: Measures grammatical correctness and fluency of the output.

How to Rewrite the Rubric
:

1.

Each criterion (Simplicity, Meaning Preservation, Fluency) should be scored separately from 0 to 100.

2.

Before providing the grading levels for each criterion, the criterion should be defined in detail, especially simplicity, and then grading levels provided.

3.

The grading rubric should have clearly defined levels (100, 75, 50, 25, 0), with concise and objective descriptions at each level that specify what makes a sentence highly simplified, moderately simplified, barely simplified, etc. Ensure that two different evaluators would not interpret the criteria differently.

4.

The model should be instructed to assign a continuous score between 0-100.

The output format should be structured as follows:

Feedback: Explain strengths and weaknesses for each criterion, referencing original and simplified.

Score: Simplicity: {X}, Meaning Preservation: {Y}, Fluency: {Z}.

Now, generate the revised grading instruction prompt for a large language model following these guidelines and addressing the issues.

C.2
Prompt-Zero

Task Overview
:

Evaluate the quality of a simplified sentence or set of sentences by comparing it to its original version. The evaluation should focus on three main criteria: Simplicity, Meaning Preservation, and Fluency. Assign a separate score for each criterion on a scale from 0 to 100, and provide feedback highlighting strengths and weaknesses. The simplified version can consist of one or more sentences if splitting enhances readability.

Criteria Definitions and Grading Levels
:

1.

Simplicity

•

Definition: Simplicity measures the ease of understanding the simplified sentence, irrespective of the original sentence’s complexity. Key factors influencing simplicity include vocabulary, sentence structure, and clarity.

•

Guidelines for Simplicity:

–

Use straightforward and common words, replacing rare or compound words with simpler synonyms.

–

Maintain simple sentence structures with few subordinate clauses and minimal use of coordination and genitive forms.

–

Present information without parentheses or nested commas.

–

Favor active voice and past perfect tense over passive voice and simple past tense.

–

Avoid metaphors, initials, special characters, percentages, big numbers, and complex terms.

–

Replace numbers with digits and use positive sentences with direct speech (using
“du”
or
“Sie”
appropriately).

•

Grading Levels:

–

100: Exceptionally simple language, very easy to understand, adheres strictly to simplicity guidelines.

–

75: Mostly simple with minor complexities, such as infrequent longer words or slightly complex structures.

–

50: Moderate simplicity, noticeable complexity in vocabulary or structure, room for improvement.

–

25: Complexity levels similar to the original, little effort towards simplification.

–

0: Highly complex and difficult to understand, fails to simplify the original sentence.

2.

Meaning Preservation

•

Definition: Meaning preservation evaluates whether the simplified version accurately conveys the core meaning of the original sentence, omitting only the least important details without introducing additional, unrelated information.

•

Grading Levels:

–

100: Fully maintains the original meaning, with no significant omissions or extraneous additions.

–

75: Mostly preserves the core message, with some minor omissions or slight additions.

–

50: Meaning is generally preserved but with notable omissions or the introduction of some unrelated information.

–

25: Basic meaning is lost, with significant omissions or many added details not present in the original.

–

0: Fails to convey the original meaning, with major discrepancies or irrelevant information.

3.

Fluency

•

Definition: Fluency assesses grammatical correctness and the natural flow of the simplified sentence. A fluent sentence reads smoothly, without awkward phrasing or grammatical errors.

•

Grading Levels:

–

100: Completely grammatically correct, flows naturally and reads smoothly.

–

75: Mostly fluent with minor grammatical errors or slight awkwardness.

–

50: Moderately fluent, with noticeable errors impacting reading flow.

–

25: Fluency is compromised, with frequent grammatical mistakes or clumsy phrasing.

–

0: Completely lacks fluency, with critical grammatical issues making it unreadable.

Evaluation Output Format
:

•

Feedback: Describe the strengths and weaknesses of the simplified sentence in comparison to the original. Use specific examples from the text to support the assessment within each criterion.

•

Score:

–

Simplicity: {X}

–

Meaning Preservation: {Y}

–

Fluency: {Z}

Example Output
:

•

Feedback:

–

Simplicity: The
{simplified_sentence}
sentence uses approachable vocabulary, but includes one complex compound word.

–

Meaning Preservation: The main idea is preserved in
{simplified_sentence}
, but some minor details were omitted.

–

Fluency:
{simplified_sentence}
reads smoothly except for one awkward phrase in the middle.

•

Score:

–

Simplicity: 85

–

Meaning Preservation: 70

–

Fluency: 80

This comprehensive rubric and evaluation format ensures clear and objective assessment of sentence simplification, allowing for varied interpretations without losing consistency across different evaluators.

Example 1

Complex:

Wenn es gelinge, die Todeszahl durch Eindämmungsmaßnahmen auf 100.000 zu begrenzen, dann haben wir alle zusammen einen guten Job gemacht, sagte Trump am Sonntagabend im Weißen Haus.
(If we manage to limit the number of deaths to 100,000 through containment measures, then we have all done a good job together, Trump said on Sunday evening in the White House.)

C.4
Prompt-Final

Task Overview
:

Evaluate the quality of a simplified German sentence or set of sentences by comparing it to its original version. The evaluation should focus on three main criteria: Simplicity, Meaning Preservation, and Fluency. Assign a separate score for each criterion on a scale from 0 to 100. The simplified version can consist of one or more sentences if splitting enhances readability.

Ways to Simplify
:

A complex sentence can be simplified through:

•

Paraphrasing: Replacing complex words with simpler synonyms.

•

Condensing: Deleting unimportant information.

•

Splitting: Dividing a long, complex sentence into several simpler sentences.

Evaluation Criteria and Definitions
:

1.

Simplicity

•

Definition: Simplicity measures how easy the simplified sentence is to understand, irrespective of the original sentence’s complexity. This includes vocabulary, sentence structure, and clarity. Simplicity should not be assessed by comparing directly to the original but by how simple the sentence is in general. If the original is already very simple, an unchanged sentence might still score high.

•

Guidelines for Simplicity:

–

Use simple words and common words (replace rare or long nouns with a synonym or split compound words using a hyphen, e.g. Atomabkommen -> Atom-Abkommen). Avoid difficult terms and explain if necessary.

–

Use simple sentence structures with minimal subordinate clauses, coordinated clauses and genitive constructions.

–

Avoid including information in parentheses (e.g. Angela Merkel (die ehemalige Bundeskanzlerin)) or in nested commas (e.g. Angela Merkel, die ehemalige Bundeskanzlerin,…).

–

Use active as opposed to passive voice.

–

Use past perfect instead of past simple

–

Avoid metaphors, initials/abbreviations, special characters, percentages or big numbers

–

Replace numbers (vier) with digits (4)

–

Avoid foreign terms unless they are very well-known.

–

Use positive rather than negative sentences and direct speech with words like
“du”
or
“Sie”
where appropriate.

•

Grading Levels:

–

100: Exceptionally simple, very easy to grasp; adheres strictly to simplicity guidelines.

–

75: Mostly simple with minor complexities, such as infrequent longer words.

–

50: Moderate simplicity; noticeable complexity in vocabulary or structure.

–

25: Similar complexity level to the original; minimal simplification.

–

0: Overly complex, difficult to understand.

2.

Meaning Preservation

•

Definition: Meaning preservation evaluates whether the simplified version genuinely conveys the core meaning of the original sentence without introducing unrelated information along with omitting less important details.

•

Grading Levels:

–

100: Fully retains the original meaning without omissions or unrelated additions.

–

75: Mostly preserves the message, with minor omissions.

–

50: Generally preserves meaning but with notable omissions or some unrelated information.

–

25: Loses basic meaning with significant omissions or many irrelevant details.

–

0: Fails to convey the original meaning accurately.

3.

Fluency

•

Definition: Fluency assesses the grammatical correctness and natural flow of the simplified sentence.

•

Grading Levels:

–

100: Fully grammatically correct and naturally flowing.

–

75: Mostly fluent with minor grammatical errors.

–

50: Moderately fluent with noticeable errors affecting flow.

–

25: Many grammatical mistakes, affecting readability.

–

0: Lacks fluency significantly; unreadable.

:

•

Feedback: Describe the strengths and weaknesses of the simplified sentence in comparison to the original. Include specific examples to support the assessment. Score cannot be less than 0 or more than 100.

•
