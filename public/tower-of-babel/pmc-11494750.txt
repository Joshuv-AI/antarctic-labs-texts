Relationship of the Prime Diet Quality Score (PDQS) and Healthy Eating Index (HEI-2015) with depression and anxiety: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Amirhossein Ataei Kachouei, Farzam Kamrani, Fahimeh Haghighatdoost, Noushin Mohammadifard, Farid Najafi, Hossein Farshidi, Masoud Lotfizadeh, Tooba Kazemi, Simin Karimi, Hamidreza Roohafza, Mohammadreza Sabri, Nizal Sarrafzadegan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies have suggested a significant association between diet quality and mental health. However, a limited number of studies have utilized the Prime Diet Quality Score (PDQS) to examine this association. Additionally, no study has yet compared the PDQS and Healthy Eating Index-2015 (HEI-2015) in terms of their association with depression and anxiety.AimThis cross-sectional study aimed to investigate the association between diet quality, measured by the PDQS and HEI-2015, and the odds of depression and anxiety in adults.MethodsThis study utilized data from the LIPOKAP study, which was conducted between February 2018 and July 2019 in five cities in Iran. We included 1994 adults aged 18 and above who were selected through a multistage cluster sampling method. Participants completed a validated semiquantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) to evaluate their dietary intake. The FFQ was used to calculate the PDQS and HEI-2015. Depression and anxiety levels were determined using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS).ResultsThe participants had a mean age of 39.79 ± 13.87 years, with females accounting for 1,041 (52.2%) of the total population. PDQS showed a significant inverse association with the odds of depression (OR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.28–0.71) and anxiety (OR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.25–0.62) in the fully adjusted model. Similarly, the highest quartile of HEI-2015 showed a significantly lower odds of depression (OR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.40–0.90) and anxiety (OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.42–0.92) compared to the lowest quartile.ConclusionBoth PDQS and HEI-2015 were associated with a reduced risk of depression and anxiety. However, the PDQS demonstrated a stronger inverse association with these risks compared to the HEI-2015. This suggests that the PDQS could be a more beneficial dietary pattern for preventing depression and anxiety compared to HEI-2015. Further large-scale studies are required to confirm these findings. Introduction

Major depressive disorder and generalized anxiety disorder are the leading causes of disability and premature mortality around the world and contribute significantly to the global burden of disease [1, 2]. According to the latest Global Burden of Disease Study, over 264 million and 284 million individuals have depressive and anxiety disorders, respectively [3]. Low-income societies tend to have higher rates of depression [4]. In Iran specifically, studies have reported a prevalence of approximately 43%, which is higher than the 15% to 25% range reported in general public references [5]. These disorders arise from a complex interaction of psychological, biological, social, financial, and lifestyle factors [6]. Evaluating these risk factors can contribute to the development of more comprehensive treatment and prevention strategies.

Recently, significant attention has been given to the role of diet quality as a lifestyle risk factor for depression and anxiety. Previous observational studies have shown varying levels of protection against depression through adherence to a healthy diet, particularly a traditional Mediterranean diet, or by avoiding a pro-inflammatory diet [7]. However, there are limited studies examining the relationship between emerging diets, such as the Prime Diet Quality Score (PDQS) and depression or anxiety. Additionally, few studies have compared different diet quality indices to identify the most beneficial one for a given society [8].

The PDQS is a straightforward food-based diet quality index that was specifically designed as a simple tool to evaluate the association between diet quality with the risk of cardiovascular diseases [9]. The Healthy Eating Index (HEI) also assesses diet quality by determining whether a specific set of foods aligns with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans (DGA) [10]. Despite the similarities between the PDQS and HEI-2015, they differ in certain aspects. The PDQS is more specific for certain food groups, while the HEI-2015 takes a more holistic approach. For example, the PDQS classifies poultry, fish, and eggs as healthy foods while considering red meat unhealthy, whereas the HEI-2015 includes red meat in the total protein foods category. Similarly, the HEI-2015 score does not distinguish low-fat dairy products from other dairy products, whereas the PDQS considers low-fat dairy products to be healthy.

Scarce epidemiological data exist regarding the relationship between the PDQS and mental health disorders. One study by Cano-Ibáñez et al. [11] found that a greater PDQS was associated with lower levels of depression in a cross-sectional analysis. Additionally, Haghighatdoost et al. [12] reported a significant inverse association between PDQS and depression and anxiety symptoms among Iranian adults. However, they failed to compare PDQS with any other diet quality index. Although several studies have explored the relationship between the HEI and mental health, evidence regarding HEI-2015 is limited and inconclusive. For example, while some earlier studies have reported a significant inverse association between the HEI-2015 and depression symptoms [13, 14], Boutté et al. found no significant association between the HEI-2015 and depression or anxiety among pregnant women [15]. Additionally, HEI is widely recommended by clinician as a healthy eating pattern while some dietary patterns, such as Mediterranean diet, cost a lot and are not affordable by all in particular in low-income countries. Therefore, we aimed to explore less investigated dietary patterns such as HEI or PDQS in a multi-center study of Iranian adults.

Methods

Study population

This cross-sectional study utilized baseline data from the Knowledge and Practice of Dyslipidemia Prevention, Management, and Control study (LIPOKAP), conducted between February 2018 and July 2019 in five Iranian cities, namely, Isfahan, Birjand, Bandar Abbas, Kermanshah, and Shahrekord [16]. The primary aim of the LIPOKAP trial was to enhance participants’ knowledge and practice regarding dyslipidemia through community-based educational strategies [16].

Participants were selected through a stratified multistage random cluster sampling method. In brief, the study included individuals without chronic kidney disease, liver disease, cancer, immune system disorders, dyslipidemia-related diseases, or systemic diseases and excluded those who had under- or overestimated their energy intake (< 800 or > 4200 kcal/day). Ultimately, after exclusion of 24 participants due to missing data, a total of 1994 adults aged 18 years and older were enrolled in the current analysis. The sample size was determined based on the suggested formula for cross-sectional studies with prevalence (N = [(Z 1-α/2)2 * P * (1-P)] / d2), we calculated adequate sample size. Considering a prevalence of 15% for depression in Iranian elderly, type I error (α) = 5% and d = 0.03 (one-fifth of our P), a sample size of 544 was estimated to be adequate. Therefore, our study sample size (1994 individuals) would be large enough to provide adequate power [17, 18]. A questionnaire completed by trained interviewers was used to gather information on sociodemographic characteristics, including age, sex, marital status, smoking status, sleep patterns, socioeconomic status (SES), and education level. Details on the evaluation of SES have been provided elsewhere [19]. The International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) was used to assess physical activity [20]. Furthermore, sleep duration was assessed by a simple question about the number of hours that a person is used to sleep in 24 h. All individuals provided a written informed consent for their participation. The protocol of this study was approved by the ethics committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUI.PHANUT.REC.1403.079).

Dietary assessment

To assess dietary intake, a validated semiquantitative food frequency questionnaire (SFFQ) was used [21]. The questionnaire included 110 items, along with standard portion sizes. Participants were asked to report their average consumption frequency and portion sizes for each item over the past year. They selected the most appropriate response from nine possible categories, ranging from never/seldom to more than 6 times per day. The average intake of each food item (g/day) was calculated for all participants based on the weight of each portion size and the consumption frequency. Subsequently, Nutritionist IV software (version 7.0; N-Squared Computing, Salem, OR, USA), which was adjusted for Iranian foods, was used to determine daily energy and nutrient intake [22].

PDQS calculation

The PDQS is a diet quality score that classifies foods as healthy or unhealthy. The healthy food groups include vegetables (dark leafy greens, cruciferous vegetables, carrots, and other vegetables), fruits (whole citrus fruits and other whole fruits), legumes, nuts, poultry, fish, eggs, whole grains, liquid vegetable oils, and low-fat dairy. The unhealthy food groups are red meat, potatoes, processed meat, whole milk dairy, refined grains and baked goods, sugar-sweetened beverages, fried foods obtained outside of home, and desserts and ice creams. Points are assigned based on the consumption frequency of each food component. For example, consuming 0–1 servings per week of a healthy food group scored 0 points, 2–3 servings per week scored 1 point, and ≥ 4 servings per week scored 2 points. Conversely, points are deducted for unhealthy food groups. The PDQS ranges from 0 to 42 points, with higher scores indicating a higher perceived diet quality [23]. Then, the scores were categorized into quartiles (Q), with Q1 (13–21), Q2 (22–24), Q3 (25–27), and Q4 (28–37) as the cutoff points.

HEI-2015 calculation

The HEI-2015 ranges from 0 to 100 and indicates adherence to the dietary guidelines recommended by the DGA. This score is based on 13 food components, which include 9 adequacy components and 4 moderation components. The adequacy components are further divided into 6 components (total fruits, whole fruits, total vegetables, greens, beans, total protein foods, seafood, and plant proteins) that are scored on a scale of 0 to 5 points and 3 components (whole grains, dairy, and fatty acids) that are scored on a scale of 0 to 10 points. The moderation components are scored on a scale of 0 to 10 points and include sodium, refined grains, added sugars, and saturated fats. The final HEI-2015 score is calculated by summing the points of each component. As sodium could not be estimated through the FFQ, it was excluded from the calculation of the HEI-2015 score, leaving its range from 0 to 90. Notably, the calculation is based on the energy density per 1000 kcal and not on the absolute amount of components [10]. The HEI-2015 score is categorized into quartiles (Q) based on the cutoff points, which are Q1 (27–59), Q2 (60–65), Q3 (66–71), and Q4 (72–90).

Depression and anxiety assessment

A validated Iranian version of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [24] was utilized to assess depression and anxiety. Participants were asked about their emotions over the last week. The assessment consisted of two sections, each comprising seven items with a 4-point rating scale, and was conducted under staff supervision. The final scores varied from 0 (representing the lowest degree of anxiety and depression) to 21 (representing the highest degree of anxiety or depression). The scores were then utilized to classify participants based on depression and anxiety severity. A score of 7 or less in each section was considered normal, indicating the absence of depression or minimal anxiety. Conversely, a score of 8 or higher indicated the presence of depression or anxiety.

Statistical analysis

The demographic characteristics, dietary intake, and mental health status of the participants were summarized using descriptive statistics across the quartiles of the PDQS and HEI-2015. Differences in variables between groups were evaluated using the chi-square test for categorical variables and ANOVA for continuous variables. ANCOVA, adjusted for age, sex, and energy, was used to measure dietary intake in the PDQS and HEI-2015 quartiles. The mean depression and anxiety scores were compared by ANOVA in the crude model and ANCOVA in the adjusted model across the PDQS and HEI-2015 quartiles. Logistic regression models were employed to explore the associations between PDQS and HEI-2015 scores and the odds of depression and anxiety. The first model was adjusted for age, sex, energy intake, and city of residence to account for confounding effects. Model 2 was adjusted for education, physical activity (< vs. > median of physical activity level), sleep, smoking status, and socioeconomic status (SES). Folate, cobalamin, iron, pyridoxine, zinc, and magnesium were further adjusted in Model 3. All confounders were regarded as covariates in the statistical analysis and were chosen through their relevance according to literature or significant difference in their distribution across the categories of dietary indices in our study population. The results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The normal distribution of data was evaluated using Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Q-Q plots (Supplementary Figure 1 and 2). Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp. Released 2019. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0). A p-value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance, and all tests were two-tailed.

Individuals with higher HEI-2015 and PDQS scores exhibited significantly higher educational levels (Q4 vs. Q1: P < 0.001 for both) but had shorter sleep durations (Q4 vs. Q1: P < 0.001 for both). Moreover, in higher quartiles of HEI-2015, individuals were more likely to be female (Q4 vs. Q1: P = 0.002), physically active (Q4 vs. Q1: P = 0.004, P < 0.001, respectively), and less likely to be smokers (Q4 vs. Q1: P = 0.004). Participants in the higher quartiles of the PDQS exhibited significantly lower rates of both depression and anxiety compared to those in the first quartile. Conversely, participants in the highest quartile of the HEI-2015 demonstrated significantly lower rates of depression, but no significant difference in anxiety rates. The mean age was significantly different across PDQS quartiles (Q4 vs. Q1: P = 0.006), while no significant differences were observed in other demographic characteristics among participants across the PDQS and HEI-2015 quartiles.

Table 2 provides an overview of dietary intake among groups categorized by PDQS and HEI-2015 quartiles. Individuals in the highest quartile of the PDQS exhibited significantly greater energy intake (Q4 vs. Q1: P < 0.001). However, no significant difference in energy intake was observed among individuals across the HEI-2015 quartiles (Q4 vs. Q1: P = 0.773). Individuals in the highest quartile of both PDQS and HEI-2015 exhibited significantly higher intake of protein, fiber, whole grains, fruit, nuts, legumes, dairy, meat, vegetables, and several micronutrients, including folate, cobalamin, iron, pyridoxine, zinc, and magnesium (Q4 vs. Q1: P for all ≤ 0.001). Conversely, they had a lower intake of carbohydrates, refined grains, and unhealthy vegetable oils (Q4 vs. Q1: P for all < 0.001). The intake of healthy vegetable oils significantly decreased across both PDQS and HEI-2015 quartiles. (Q4 vs. Q1: P < 0.001). In the highest quartile of the PDQS, there was a significant decrease in fat intake (Q4 vs. Q1: P < 0.001). Conversely, in the highest quartile of the HEI-2015, there was a significant increase in fat intake (Q4 vs. Q1: P = 0.001). Additionally, fish and sea food intake were significantly greater in the highest quartiles of PDQS and HEI-2015 (P < 0.001 and P = 0.008, respectively). Reverse causation is plausible in observational studies, particularly those with a cross-sectional design, we assessed the associations when the top 20% of patients with depressive and anxiety symptoms were excluded (Supplementary Table 1). The overall results for PDQS did not change and after adjustment for potential confounders and participants in the top quartile of PDQS compared with those in the first had 30% and 60% lower risk for depression and anxiety, respectively. In contrast, while the highest quartile of HEI-2015 was associated with lower odds of depression and anxiety compared to the lowest quartile, this association was not statistically significant.

The stratified analysis exhibited a significant inverse association between PDQS and anxiety and depression in both sexes and both adjusted and crude model. Additionally, the results showed substantially stronger negative association between PDQS and the risk of depression and anxiety among men compared to women. In the fully adjusted model, men had a 69% reduced risk of depression and a 73% reduced risk of anxiety, while women had a 38% reduced risk of depression and a 59% reduced risk of anxiety, based on comparisons between the highest and lowest quartiles (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). In contrast, only women in the highest quartile, as compared to those in the lowest quartile in the fully adjusted model, showed a significant reduction in the risk of depression and anxiety for HEI-2015.

Discussion

In the present cross-sectional study, higher PDQS was significantly associated with lower odds of both depression and anxiety, specifically in men. This association was strong and remained significant after multiple adjustments for confounding factors and mediators. Furthermore, a significant inverse association between HEI-2015 scores and depression was observed in all the adjusted and crude models; however, after exclusion of top 20% cases of depression and anxiety, this association became nonsignificant. Additionally, based on the stratified analysis, the association between HEI-2015 and both depression and anxiety were only significant among women.

Development of mental disorders, such as depression and anxiety, is influenced by several factors, including genetic, hormonal and immunological factors, all of which can be modulated by diet [25]. Traditionally, studies on the association between diet and depression or anxiety have focused primarily on specific nutrients, such as fatty acids [26] and B vitamins [27], which play a role in neurotransmitter synthesis. However, the effects of diet on human health arise from the cumulative and synergistic impact of nutrients sourced from different food groups rather than from individual nutrients. As a result, current studies have focused on the potential association between dietary patterns and mental health [11].

The associations between diet quality and depression or anxiety have been studied extensively by utilizing various diet quality indices [28]. To our knowledge, only two studies have used the PDQS to explore this association [11]. Cano-Ibáñez et al. [11] investigated the association between PDQS and depressive symptoms in a Mediterranean population with metabolic syndrome. In line with our findings, their results suggested that a higher PDQS was associated with lower odds of depression and lower depression symptomatology in Spanish overweight/obese individuals with metabolic syndrome. Additionally, Haghighatdoost et al. [16] have assessed the relationship between PDQS and depression and anxiety, and in line with our findings, reported a significant inverse association between PDQS and depression and anxiety symptoms among Iranian adults. Nonetheless, no study has compared this dietary pattern with other dietary indices regarding their mental health outcomes.

Moreover, several studies have explored the association between the HEI-2015 and depression or anxiety [14, 15, 29]. In line with our findings, a large number of studies have suggested an inverse association between HEI-2015 scores and the odds of depression and anxiety [14, 29]. However, Boutté et al. [15] found no significant association between the HEI-2015 and depression or anxiety among pregnant women.

Our findings suggest a stronger connection between adherence to PDQS and a reduced risk of depression and anxiety in both sexes compared to HEI-2015. The observed relationship, along with inconsistencies between previous studies on the association between HEI and depression or anxiety might be attributed to the construction of HEI-2015, which exhibits fundamental differences compared to the construction of PDQS. While both dietary patterns are considered healthy, the PDQS is more specific in its classification of certain food groups, including meats and dairy. It categorizes poultry and fish as healthy foods while considering red meat unhealthy [9]. In contrast, the HEI-2015 includes red meat in the total protein foods category and does not assign a separate score to it [10]. Additionally, the PDQS emphasizes limiting ultra-processed foods (UPFs), whereas the HEI-2015 includes some processed foods in the total protein foods category. Although the HEI-2015 promotes the consumption of anti-inflammatory food groups, it does not restrict the intake of certain pro-inflammatory products, such as red meat and several UPFs, potentially weakening its association with inflammation [30, 31]. Therefore, as inflammation is a well-documented risk factor for depression and anxiety, the association between HEI-2015 and these mental health conditions may be weaker than that of PDQS. Supporting this, a systematic review of observational studies by Hart et al. reported that diet scores derived from national dietary guidelines, like the HEI, are less consistently associated with biomarkers of inflammation with 15 of 29 studies reporting no association [32]. Additionally, consumption of both red meat and UPFs has been reported to be associated with higher odds of mental health disorders [33, 34]. Consequently, the inability of HEI-2015 to differentiate and restrict certain food groups may limit its effectiveness in reducing inflammation and, consequently, health outcomes like mental health conditions.

Our stratified analysis suggests that the associations between diet and mental health may be sex-specific. While a significant inverse association between PDQS and depression and anxiety was observed in both men and women, only women showed a significant reduction in the risk of depression and anxiety across the quartiles of HEI-2015. The sex-specific differences could be attributed to several factors. First, males and females often experience different risk factors for mental health conditions, including hormonal modulation and stress response, which could influence how dietary patterns like the HEI-2015 impact mental health [35, 36]. Additionally, females are more likely to receive social and medical support when addressing depression or anxiety [37]. This enhanced support may amplify the positive effects of a healthy diet, such as the HEI-2015, on mental health in women compared to men.

Several biological mechanisms have been proposed to explain the link between diet quality and mental health, including the modulation of pathways involved in oxidative stress, inflammation, epigenetics, mitochondrial dysfunction, the gut microbiota, tryptophan-kynurenine metabolism, the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, neurogenesis, brain-derived neurotrophic factor, epigenetics, and obesity [38, 39]. A meta-analysis revealed that diet quality could potentially affect systemic inflammation and reduce neuronal damage caused by oxidative stress, which in turn can affect depression and mental health [7]. In line with this, our previous publication showed a beneficial association between a lower dietary inflammatory index and mental disorder risk in Iranians [40]. Additionally, evidence has suggested that diet quality may influence mood and behavioral disorders by affecting the gastrointestinal microbiome [41]. Although the exact underlying mechanisms are still unclear, it has been suggested that the gut microbiome, one of the first bodily systems to interact with consumed food, may play a role in modulating many other mechanisms implicated in depression pathophysiology, including inflammation, neurogenesis, and tryptophan metabolism [38]. Moreover, some studies have reported a reciprocal relationship between depression and diet quality [42–44]. It has been suggested that depressive symptoms may lead to poor dietary choices through emotional eating behavior, where food is used as a way of regulating negative emotions [44]. Therefore, further investigations are required to clearly understand the mechanisms that connect habitual diet and mental health.

This study has several strengths. First, to date, this study is the first to compare the HEI-2015 and PDQS regarding their relationships with depression and anxiety. and is the first to utilize PDQS in a Middle Eastern country. Moreover, our study included a large multi-center and hence representative sample of Iranian adults.

However, several limitations of the study should be noted. Given the cross-sectional design of this study, establishing a causal relationship between participants’ poor diet quality and depression or anxiety is impossible. Another limitation of our study is the lack of information on some potential confounders such as body mass index (BMI) and participants medication which may influence the direction and strength of our findings. However, we adjusted for energy intake as a proxy for BMI. While this approach provided a feasible alternative, it may not fully capture the variability associated with BMI. Furthermore, we did not perform energy-adjusted analysis using residuals. Instead, the energy intake was accounted as a covariate in our regression models, along with other relevant variables. While we recognize that using residuals for energy-adjusted analysis could have served as an additional sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of our results, we chose our approach due to the complexity of our dataset and the extensive range of covariates already incorporated in our models. We believe this approach adequately accounts for any potential effects of energy intake. It is also worth noting that previous studies have indicated comparable outcomes between the two methods, suggesting they are equally effective in achieving the desired results [45]. Additionally, although all questionnaires were completed by interviewers, it is possible that respondents answered questions in a manner that made them appear psychologically healthy. Furthermore, since the FFQ is a tool based on memory, measurement errors cannot be entirely avoided, as in other nutritional epidemiologic studies. Moreover, owing to the variations in depression and anxiety scoring criteria, it is possible that this variation causes inconsistency in findings. To support this, Faravelli et al. [46] suggested that different concepts of depression form the basis for various scales, and caution must be exercised when comparing results obtained from different depression assessment scales. Furthermore, Hansson et al. [47] found only moderate agreement between the two commonly used scales, the HADS and Patient Health Questionnaire-9, with the most appropriate cutoff. This suggests that the scales do not fully identify the same patients. It is important to note that although both scales identified a similar prevalence, this study was conducted with a small sample size (n = 737), and the results may vary in larger populations. Additionally, although the HADS has shown valid results in general populations, it was initially validated among cancer patients [24, 48, 49]. Therefore, the interpretation of this questionnaire’s results should be cautiously.

In conclusion, a higher PDQS and HEI-2015 score were associated with lower odds of depression and anxiety. However, this association was stronger for PDQS, especially among men. Based on our findings, the PDQS might be a more appropriate score for improving the mental health status of Iranian population than the HEI-2015. However, further studies are required to confirm our results.
