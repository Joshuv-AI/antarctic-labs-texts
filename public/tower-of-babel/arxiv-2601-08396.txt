Kantorovich Distance via Spanning Trees: Properties and Algorithms Thanks: The authors gratefully acknowledge financial support from the Agence Nationale de la Recherche (MaSDOL grant ANR-19-CE23-0017).
Jérémie Bigot and Luis Fredes

Abstract
. We study optimal transport between probability measures supported on the same finite metric space, where the ground cost is a distance induced by a weighted connected graph. Building on recent work showing that the resulting Kantorovich distance can be expressed as a minimization problem over the set of spanning trees of this underlying graph, we investigate the implications of this reformulation on the construction of an optimal transport plan and a dual potential based on the solution of such an optimization problem. In this setting, we derive an explicit formula for the Kantorovich potential in terms of the imbalanced cumulative mass (a generalization of the cumulative distribution in ℝ {\mathbb{R}} ) along an optimal spanning tree solving such a minimization problem, under a weak non-degeneracy condition on the pair of measures that guarantees the uniqueness of a dual potential. Our second contribution establishes the existence of an optimal transport plan that can be computed efficiently by a dynamic programming procedure once an optimal spanning tree is known. Finally, we propose a stochastic algorithm based on simulated annealing on the space of spanning trees to compute such an optimal spanning tree. Numerical experiments illustrate the theoretical results and demonstrate the practical relevance of the proposed approach for optimal transport on finite metric spaces.



Keywords:
Optimal transport; Kantorovich distance; finite metric spaces; spanning trees; Arens–Eells norm; Kantorovich potentials; stochastic optimization; simulated annealing.

AMS classifications:
Primary 60C05, 62G05; secondary 62G20.

1.
Introduction

This paper focuses on optimal transport (OT) between probability measures supported on the same finite metric space, where the ground distance is induced by a weighted connected graph
G
.
Recent work
[
16
]
has shown that, using the fact that this OT problem admits a closed-form solution when
G
is a tree
[
7
,
15
,
14
,
19
,
4
]
, the computation of the Kantorovich distance (i.e., the 1-Wasserstein distance induced by this ground metric) on an arbitrary graph
G
can be reformulated as a minimization problem over the set of all spanning trees of
G
.
The present work is motivated by the research question introduced in
[
16
]
regarding the potential computational applications of this result.
To this end, we first extend the analysis in
[
16
]
by studying the properties of the optimal spanning trees of
G
which solve such a minimization problem, and thus OT on the probability simplex associated with the finite metric space.
We then propose a stochastic algorithm based on simulated annealing over the space of spanning trees to estimate both the Kantorovich distance (K-distance), an associated optimal transport plan and a dual potential.

1.1.
Kantorovich distance on a finite metric space

Before presenting the main contributions of this work, we first provide some background on the K-distance on a finite metric space. Let
\mathcal{X}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}\}
denote a set of
N
points (that are pairwise distincts) endowed with a distance
d_{G}
that is induced by an edge-weighted connected graph
G=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E}_{G},w)
whose vertices (or nodes) are the elements of
\mathcal{X}
, and
\mathcal{E}_{G}
denotes the set of edges where
w:\mathcal{E}_{G}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
is a given weight function that is symmetric and positive that is
w(x,y)>0
for all
(x,y)\in\mathcal{E}_{G}
, with
x\neq y
, and
w(x,x)=0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
This means that
\mathcal{X}
is a finite metric space endowed with the distance
d_{G}(x,y)
that is equal to the length of the shortest path, according to
w
, connecting
x
and
y
. We recall that a path between two vertices
x
and
y
is a sequence of at least two vertices
x_{0}=x,x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}=y
such that
\{x_{i-1},x_{i}\}\in\mathcal{E}_{G}
for all
i=1,\ldots,m
, and its length is
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w(x_{i-1},x_{i})
. Throughout the paper, we assume that the graph
G
is connected (implying the existence of a path between any pair of vertices) and loops have distance zero, meaning that
w(x,x)=0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. We also consider
\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{G}
the set of oriented edges of
G
, meaning that for every edge
\{x,y\}\in\mathcal{E}_{G}
there are two edges
(x,y)
and
(y,x)
in
\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{G}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
the set of probability measures supported on
\mathcal{X}
. For
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, we write
\mu=(\mu(x):x\in\mathcal{X})
and we interpret it as a vector in the
N
-dimensional simplex, meaning that
\mu(x)\geq 0
and
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\mu(x)=1
. For a subset
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{X}
of vertices, we also write
\mu(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}\mu(x)
. Given a pair
(\mu,\nu)
of such probability measures, the space
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
of admissible transport plans (also called transportation polytope) is defined as the set of joint probability measures
\gamma
supported on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
having marginals equal to
\mu
and
\nu
, that is satisfying the constraints

\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\gamma(x,y)=\mu(x),\quad\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\gamma(x,y)=\nu(y).

Definition 1.1
.

For the distance
d_{G}
induced by the graph
G
, the Kantorovich distance between two probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is the value of the Linear Programming (LP) problem

(1.1)

K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)=\min_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}d_{G}(x,y)\gamma(x,y).

The LP program (
1.1
) is known as the optimal transport (OT) problem between
\mu
and
\nu
in the sense of Kantorovich. We refer to
[
18
,
21
]
for a mathematical introduction to OT, and to
[
17
]
for a detailed presentation of its computational aspects. In the OT literature, the distance
K_{d_{G}}
is also referred to as the Wasserstein distance with respect to the ground cost
d_{G}
. A transport plan (also called coupling)
\gamma_{G}
is called optimal if it is a minimizer of the LP program (
1.1
). A minimizer of this problem is not necessarily unique.

We also recall the well-known property of Kantorovich duality in the OT literature.

Proposition 1.1
.

The K-distance (
1.1
) also satisfies

(1.2)

K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)=\max_{u\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{G})}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}u(x)(\mu(x)-\nu(x)),

where
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{G})
denotes the set of functions
u
on
\mathcal{X}
satisfying
|u(x)-u(y)|\leq d_{G}(x,y)
for all
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
.

A function
u_{G}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{G})
is called an optimal dual (or Kantorovich) potential if it is a maximizer of the optimisation problem (
1.2
). It is well known in the OT literature that simple counterexamples exist where the uniqueness of Kantorovich potentials fails to hold for standard ground costs. Nevertheless, conditions ensuring uniqueness (up to an additive constant) of dual potentials for OT on finite spaces have long been established using techniques from linear programming
[
13
,
12
]
. The uniqueness of Kantorovich potentials for arbitrary probability measures (beyond the discrete setting) has also been addressed in the recent work
[
20
]
to which we refer for a detailed overview of existing works on this topic. Following these works, the uniqueness of Kantorovich potential typically arises whenever the measures (not necessarily supported on the same space) are non-degenerate in the following sense.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be two discrete sets of points. A pair of probability measures
(\mu,\nu)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
is said to be non-degenerate if for any non-empty proper subsets
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{Y}
, it holds that
\mu(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})\neq\nu(\mathcal{Y}^{\prime})
.

1.2.
Main contributions

In this paper, we build on the known connection between the K-distance and the Arens-Eells (AE) norm
[
24
]
, together with a recent result by
[
16
]
showing that solving the linear program (LP) (
1.1
) is equivalent to the following minimization problem over the set
\mathrm{ST}(G)
of rooted spanning trees of the graph
G
:

(1.3)

K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)=\min_{T\in\mathrm{ST}(G)}K_{d_{T}}(\mu,\nu),

where
K_{d_{T}}(\mu,\nu)
denotes the K-distance between
\mu
and
\nu
, which admits a closed-form expression when the ground cost is given by the distance
d_{T}
induced by a rooted spanning tree
T\in\mathrm{ST}(G)
with edge weights defined by the function
w
.
Extending the analysis developed in
[
16
]
, our main result (see Theorem
3.3
) establishes a connection between an optimal spanning tree
T_{\ast}
minimizing (
1.3
) and an optimal Kantorovich potential, under the following weak non-degeneracy condition on the measures
\mu
and
\nu
.

Definition 1.3
.

A pair of probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is said to be weakly non-degenerate if, for every non-empty proper subset
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{X}
, it holds that
\mu(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})\neq\nu(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})
.

1.2.1.
An explicit expression of the Kantorovich potential

In particular, we show that when the pair of measures is weakly non-degenerate, the Kantorovich potential
u_{G}
is unique up to an additive constant.
Definition
1.3
of weak non-degeneracy exponentially reduces the number of conditions to be verified compared with Definition
1.2
of non-degeneracy, when
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}
.
Moreover, under the weak non-degeneracy of the pair
(\mu,\nu)
, the Kantorovich potential
u_{G}
admits an explicit form that depends on the optimal spanning tree
T_{\ast}
rooted at
r\in\mathcal{X}
. More precisely, we show that (under weak non-degeneracy) for every
y\in\mathcal{X}
,

(1.4)

u_{G}(y)=\sum_{y\lesssim x\neq r}d_{T_{\ast}}(x,x^{+})\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x))\text{ with the convention that }u_{G}(r)=0,

where
y\lesssim x
denotes the partial order induced by the rooted tree
T_{\ast}
(with edges oriented towards the root),
x^{+}
is the unique parent of
x\neq r
(see Section
2.4
, for more precise definitions),
\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\cdot)
is the usual sign function, and the quantity
\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x)
is the imbalanced cumulative function defined as

(1.5)

\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x)=\sum_{y\lesssim x}\mu(y)-\nu(y),\quad\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathcal{X}.

The function
\Xi_{T_{\ast}}
is the difference between cumulative functions (with respect to the partial order induced by
\lesssim
on
T_{\ast}
)

F_{\mu}(x)=\sum_{y\lesssim x}\mu(y)\text{ and }F_{\nu}(x)=\sum_{y\lesssim x}\nu(y)

of the measures
\mu
and
\nu
. This generalizes the standard cumulative functions of probability measures supported on the real line.

1.2.2.
The analogy with monotone optimal transport on the real line

The expression (
1.4
) of the Kantorovich potential
u_{G}
also reveals a structural analogy between OT with respect to a graph-induced ground metric and monotone transport on the real line. To illustrate this connection, consider the special case where
\mathcal{X}
is a set of reals
X_{1}<X_{2}<\ldots<X_{N}
and the ground metric is
d_{G}(x,y)=|x-y|
. This corresponds to the setting where
G
is a line tree. As
G
is already a tree, the optimal spanning tree
T_{\ast}
of problem (
1.3
) is the line tree
G
itself. We will see in Section
2.4
that the computation of the
K
-distance is invariant under rerooting so we can impose without loss of generality the root
r=X_{N}
, then

X_{1}\to X_{2}\to\cdots\to X_{N}\quad\text{with}\quad X_{i+1}=X_{i}^{+}.

In this one-dimensional setting and for this ground cost, it is well known (see e.g.
[
18
]
[Chapter 2]) that the
K
-distance between one-dimensional measures
\mu
and
\nu
supported on
{\mathbb{R}}
is

K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)=\int_{0}^{1}|F_{\mu}(x)-F_{\nu}(x)|dx\quad\text{ with }F_{\mu}(x)=\mu((-\infty;x])\text{ and }F_{\nu}(x)=\nu((-\infty;x]).

Therefore,

K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}(X_{i+1}-X_{i})|F_{\mu}(X_{i})-F_{\nu}(X_{i})|=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}}d_{G}(x,x^{+})\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x))\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x),

given that in the case of the line tree, the imbalance function
\Xi_{T_{\ast}}
defined in (
1.5
) satisfies

\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(X_{i})=F_{\mu}(X_{i})-F_{\nu}(X_{i})
. Since, for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x)=\sum_{y\lesssim r}\left(\mu(y)-\nu(y)\right)\mathbf{1}_{\{y\lesssim x\}}

and
d_{G}(x,x^{+})=d_{T_{\ast}}(x,x^{+})
, one has that

\displaystyle K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y\lesssim r}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}}d_{T_{\ast}}(x,x^{+})\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x))\mathbf{1}_{\{y\lesssim x\}}\left(\mu(y)-\nu(y)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{y\lesssim r}u_{G}(y)\left(\mu(y)-\nu(y)\right),

with
u_{G}(y)=\sum_{y\lesssim x\neq r}d_{T_{\ast}}(x,x^{+})\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x))
which is the Kantorovich potential in the one-dimensional case satisfying
u_{G}(X_{N})=0
. This highlights the fact that, given the knowledge of an optimal spanning tree
T_{\ast}\in\mathrm{ST}(G)
minimizing (
1.3
), the expression (
1.4
) of a Kantorovich potential generalizes the structure of one-dimensional monotone transport.

1.2.3.
The construction of an optimal coupling on a weighted tree by dynamic programming

In addition, a second contribution is to derive a new algorithm to compute an optimal transport plan
\gamma_{G}
in the case where
G=T
is a tree. Our approach differs from the standard use of the network simplex algorithm implemented in the Python Optimal Transport (POT) library
[
9
,
8
]
based on the cost matrix between the elements of
\mathcal{X}
. Such an OT plan can be easily computed through an algorithmic procedure based on dynamic programming and the knowledge of the imbalance cumulative function
\Xi_{T}(\cdot)
.

1.2.4.
Computation of an optimal spanning tree of (
1.3
) by simulated annealing

Finally, we propose a stochastic algorithm to compute an optimal spanning tree
T_{\ast}
. The algorithm relies on a random walk, based on simulated annealing, over the set
\mathrm{ST}(G)
of spanning trees in order to optimize the functional
T\mapsto K_{d_{T}}(\mu,\nu)
. This immediately yields an estimation of the
K
-distance. Then, once
T_{\ast}
has been found by simulated annealing, we can compute an OT plan thanks to our dynamic programming based on the knowledge of
\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(\cdot)
. Finally, the computation of a Kantorovich potential follows from the explicit expression (
1.4
).

2.
A connection between the Kantorovich distance and the Arens-Eels norms

In this section, we first recall usefull properties of optimal transport plans. Then, we recall the notion of Kantorovich duality. Finally, we detail the existing links between the K-distance on a finite metric space and the notion of Arens-Eels norms using notation that are mainly borrowed from
[
16
]
.

2.1.
Properties of optimal transport plans

For a given transport plan
\gamma
, its support is defined as

\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\gamma):=\left\{(x,y)\mid\gamma(x,y)>0\;:\;(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\right\}.

Throughout the paper, we also refer to
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\gamma)
as the directed graph with vertices
\mathcal{X}
such that the oriented edge
(x,y)
is in the support of
\gamma
if and only if
\gamma(x,y)>0
. It is important to remark that the edge
(x,y)
belonging to the support of
\gamma
lives in
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
, meaning that
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\gamma)
is a subgraph of the complete graph
K_{\mathcal{X}}
together with an orientation of its edges. Denoting
\mathcal{S}_{m}
the permutation group of size
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let us now recall the definition of
d_{G}
-cyclical monotonicity.

Definition 2.1
.

A subset
\Gamma\subset\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
is said to be
d_{G}
-cyclically monotone if for any
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, every permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{m}
and every finite family of points
(x_{0},y_{0}),(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{m-1},y_{m-1})
in
\Gamma
the following inequality holds

\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}d_{G}(x_{i},y_{i})\leq\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}d_{G}(x_{i},y_{\sigma(i)}).

Then, it is well known (see e.g. Theorem 5.9 in
[
22
]
) that the support
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\gamma_{G})
of an optimal transport plan
\gamma_{G}
is necessarily
d_{G}
-cyclically monotone. Therefore, the support of an optimal plan is necessarily a forest that is a union of trees. Indeed, checking that there is no oriented cycle comes easily from the
d
-cyclically monotone property. If there were an oriented cycle
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
in
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\gamma_{G})
of size
m+1
with
x_{m}=x_{0}
that is not a loop (i.e. such that
d_{G}(x_{i},x_{i+1})>0
for a least one
i
), then by the
d_{G}
-cyclical monotonicity of
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\gamma_{G})
and the permutation
\sigma(i)=i-1
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,m-1\}
and
\sigma(0)=m-1
, we would obtain the inequalities

0<\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}d_{G}(x_{i},x_{i+1})\leq\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}d_{G}(x_{i},x_{i})=0,

which is a contradiction.
In this paper, we consider trees as connected graphs without cycles but with loops being allowed, since keeping mass at a node has a zero cost and this will turn out to be relevant for the construction of an OT plan when
G=T
is a tree as highlighted in Proposition
3.3
and Section
3.3
.

This is even more intricate, as no cycle of length bigger or equal than two, when forgetting the orientation, can be present in a vertex of the transportation polytope. More formally, we have the following result.

Proposition 2.1

(Theorem 4 in
[
13
]
)
.

Let
\gamma
denote any vertex of the transportation polytope
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. Then, when forgetting the orientations, its support
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\gamma)
is a forest of
K_{\mathcal{X}}
(that is a union of trees) with at most
2N-1
edges (including loops). As a consequence, there exists at most
N-1
proper edges in
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\gamma)
.

Remark 2.1
.

In Proposition
3.3
, we shall prove that any optimal transport plan can be transformed into a canonical one that maximizes the mass remaining at each node, namely with diagonal entries satisfying
\gamma(x,x)=\min\{\mu(x),\nu(x)\}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. This characterization makes possible to identify precisely the loops present in the plan and will be used in the construction of transport plans when the spanning tree structure is known.

In the rest of this sub-section, we further investigate the implications of Proposition
2.1
on the structure of the support of an OT plan. Recalling that the OT problem is a linear programming problem, there always exists an optimal plan that is a vertex of the polytope
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. Then, for a vertex
\gamma
in
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
, let us now denote by
\mathcal{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{X}_{r}
the components of the forest
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\gamma)
that form a partition of the set of vertices
\mathcal{X}
such that
\gamma(x,y)=0
for all
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}_{i}\times\mathcal{X}_{j}
whenever
i\neq j
. One has that

\mu(\mathcal{X}_{i})=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}\mu(x)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\gamma(x,y)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}_{j}}\gamma(x,y)\right)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}\gamma(x,y).

Similarly, one has that
\nu(\mathcal{X}_{i})=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}\gamma(x,y)
, and therefore we obtain that

(2.1)

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{i}}(\mu(x)-\nu(x))=0\quad\mbox{for all}\quad 1\leq i\leq r.

The property (
2.1
) that the measures
\mu
and
\nu
have equal mass on the components of the support of the optimal transport plan
\gamma_{G}
is in direct relation with Definition
1.3
on the weak non-degeneracy of a pair of probability measures which yields the following result on the support of any vertex
\gamma
of
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose that the pair of probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is weakly non-degenerate. Then, every vertex
\gamma
of the transportation polytope
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
has a support that is a spanning tree of
K_{\mathcal{X}}
(up to loops), when forgetting the orientation, that is it has exactly
N-1
proper edges.

Proof.

Let
\gamma
be a vertex of the transportation polytope, and suppose that the pair of probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is weakly non-degenerate. From Proposition
2.1
one has that
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\gamma)
, when forgetting the orientation, is a forest. Then, let us suppose it is not connected. By property (
2.1
), there exists a proper subset
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{X}
satisfying
\mu(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})=\nu(\mathcal{X}^{\prime})
which contradicts the weak non-degeneracy of
(\mu,\nu)
. Therefore the support of
\gamma
is necessarily a tree (up to loops).
∎

2.2.
Kantorovich duality

We recall below some well-known properties of Kantorovich potentials in the OT literature.

Remark 2.2
.

It holds that
u
belongs to
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{G})
if, and only if (see e.g.
[
16
]
[Proposition 5]),

(2.2)

|u(x)-u(y)|\leq d_{G}(x,y)\quad\mbox{for all}\quad\{x,y\}\in\mathcal{E}_{G}

Hence, in the optimisation problem (
1.2
), the constraint that
u\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{G})
can be replaced by the condition (
2.2
).

Now, by the so-called complementary slackness between the primal Kantorovich problem (
1.1
) and its dual formulation (
1.2
), there exists a key relationship between any optimal transport plan and any optimal dual potential that is recalled below, see e.g.
[
17
]
[Section 3.3 and Section 6].

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\gamma_{G}
be any optimal transport plan and
u_{G}
be any optimal dual potential. If
\gamma_{G}(x,y)>0
then necessarily
u_{G}(x)-u_{G}(y)=d_{G}(x,y)
, and if
u_{G}(x)-u_{G}(y)<d_{G}(x,y)
then necessarily
\gamma_{G}(x,y)=0
. Conversely, if
\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
and
u\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{G})
are complementary with respect to
d_{G}
in the sense that

(2.3)

\mbox{ for all $(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}$,}\quad\gamma(x,y)>0\quad\mbox{implies that}\quad u(x)-u(y)=d_{G}(x,y),

then
\gamma
is an optimal transport plan and
u
is an optimal dual potential.

2.3.
Definition of the Arens-Eels norm

Let us define the vector space
M_{0}(\mathcal{X})
as the set of all 0-mass functions
\xi
on
\mathcal{X}
, that is, verifying
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\xi(x)=0
. Given
\mu
and
\nu
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, an example of a key function belonging to
M_{0}(\mathcal{X})
is
\xi=\mu-\nu
in what follows. Introducing the notation
\delta_{x}
for the function on
\mathcal{X}
such that
\delta_{x}(y)=0
if
y\neq x
and
1
otherwise, it follows that the difference of delta functions spans the whole space of 0-mass functions. This means that every function
\xi
in
M_{0}(\mathcal{X})
can be written as

(2.4)

\xi=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}a(x,y)(\delta_{x}-\delta_{y}),

for some set of coefficients
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}
. Obviously, there exists many ways to find a matrix
a
of real coefficients verifying the representation (
2.4
) of
\xi
, and this representation can also be expressed as

\xi(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}a(x,y)-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}a(y,x).

Following
[
24
]
, and as also explained in
[
16
]
, the vector space
M_{0}(\mathcal{X})
can be endowed with the following norm, and the resulting normed vector space is called the Arens-Eells space.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
d
be a distance on
\mathcal{X}
. For a function
\xi\in M_{0}(\mathcal{X})
, its Arens-Eells norm is defined by

(2.5)

\|\xi\|_{\mathrm{AE}(d)}=\inf\left\{\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}|a(x,y)|d(x,y)\right\},

where the above infimum is taken over all the representation of
\xi
by coefficients
a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}
in (
2.4
).

As explained in
[
16
]
[Section 2.1], using the dual formulation (
1.2
) of OT combined with a dual expression of Arens-Eells (AE) norms, the fundamental connection between the K-distance and the AE norm is that

(2.6)

K_{d}(\mu,\nu)=\|\mu-\nu\|_{\mathrm{AE}(d)},

for any probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
and distance
d
on
\mathcal{X}
.

2.4.
The special case of a distance induced by a tree

Given Definition
2.2
, computing the AE-norm is generally a delicate optimisation problem. Nevertheless, when the distance on
\mathcal{X}
is induced by a tree, the AE-norm has a closed-form expression.

To be more precise, we first recall some basic properties and notation for trees. Let
T=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E}_{T},w)
be a weighted tree, that is, a connected graph without cycles with elements of
\mathcal{X}
as vertices. Throughout the paper, it is assumed that the edges of
T
are weighted by the symmetric function
w:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
defined previously.
Then, as before, for such a weighted tree, the distance
d_{T}(x,y)
between two points
(x,y)
is the
w
-weighted length of the (unique) path connecting them. Notice that in particular if
\{x,y\}\in\mathcal{E}_{T}
, then
d_{T}(x,y)=w(x,y)
.

For our purposes it will be useful to assign a canonical orientation to the trees we will work with. To this end, we will consider a distinguished vertex
r
that we refer as the root and the (canonical) orientation is defined in terms of the root as follows: each edge
\{x,y\}
will be oriented going towards the root
r
, with orientation to be denoted as
y\to x
if
x
is the point that is the closest to the root in the sense that
d_{T}(x,r)<d_{T}(y,r)
. The property that will be useful for us about this orientation is that every vertex, but the root, has exactly one outgoing edge and the graph is connected if and only if the directed graph is a rooted tree. Given a vertex
x
in a rooted tree, the set
\mathrm{child}(x)
of children of
x
is defined as the set of all vertices
y
such that
y\to x
, while the unique parent of
x\neq r
is denoted by
x^{+}
. A rooted tree thus induces a partial order between vertices that we denote as
y\lesssim x
, including
x
as a descendant of itself, which reads as
y
is a descendent of
x
or
x
is an ancestor of
y
. For a given vertex
x
in a rooted tree
T
, we denote by
T_{x}
the subtree of
T
, rooted at
x
, that contains all the vertices
y
such that
y\lesssim x
. Finally, for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
, we denote by
P_{x,y}\subset\mathcal{E}_{T}
the unique path going from
x
to
y
in the tree
T

Then, the following important property holds for the AE-norm when the distance
d
is induced by a tree.

Proposition 2.4

(Theorem 4 and Theorem 7 in
[
16
]
)
.

Let
T=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E}_{T},w,r)
be a weighted tree rooted at
r
and suppose that
d_{T}
is the distance on the tree
T
induced by the weight function
w
. Then, for any
\xi\in M_{0}(\mathcal{X})
,

\|\xi\|_{\mathrm{AE}(T)}:=\|\xi\|_{\mathrm{AE}(d_{T})}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}}d_{T}(x,x^{+})|\Xi_{T}(x)|

where for
x\in\mathcal{X}
the quantity
\Xi_{T}(x)
is the cumulative function defined as

(2.7)

\Xi_{T}(x)=\sum_{y\lesssim x}\xi(y).

Moreover, one has that

(2.8)

\|\xi\|_{\mathrm{AE}(d_{T})}=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}u_{T}(y)\xi(y),

where

(2.9)

\displaystyle u_{T}(y)=\sum_{y\lesssim x\neq r}d_{T}(x,x^{+})\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T}(x))\quad\mbox{ for all }y\in\mathcal{X},

with
\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\cdot)\in\{-1,+1\}
the usual sign function having value at zero either
\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(0)=+1
or
\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(0)=-1
.

Hence, when the distance
d=d_{T}
on
\mathcal{X}
is induced by a rooted tree, the AE-norm has a simple closed-form expression which only depends on the tree structure and not on the optimal plan chosen, and as a byproduct for the K-distance
K_{d_{T}}(\mu,\nu)
by taking
\xi=\mu-\nu
as shown by Equality (
2.6
). In this setting, such a closed-form expression for the K-distance is also known in the OT literature. It has been initially derived in
[
7
]
, and we refer to
[
15
]
for a detailed course on the K-distance when the metric is induced by trees. For applications in machine learning on the use of ptimal transport on trees, we also refer to
[
14
,
19
,
4
]
.

For a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
and a rooted tree
T
, we define the notation

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mu(T_{x})=\sum_{y\lesssim x}\mu(y).

Then, combining Equality (
2.6
) with Proposition
2.4
, we obtain that the K-distance, associated to a rooted tree, has the following expression

(2.11)

K_{d_{T}}(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}}d_{T}(x,x^{+})\left|\mu(T_{x})-\nu(T_{x})\right|.

As a consequence of this formula we obtain that the K-distance is invariant when replacing
\mu(T_{x})
by
\mu(\mathcal{X}\setminus T_{x}):=1-\mu(T_{x})
(this is the sum of
\mu
over the vertices not belonging to
T_{x}
)
at any
x
in the interior of the sum since
\left|\mu(T_{x})-\nu(T_{x})\right|=\left|\mu(\mathcal{X}\setminus T_{x})-\nu(\mathcal{X}\setminus T_{x})\right|
and this implies that
K_{d_{T}}(\mu,\nu)
it is invariant with respect to the choice of the root.

Finally, for the function
u_{T}
defined by Equation (
2.8
), it is not difficult to check that it belongs to the set
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{T})
. Indeed, let
\{y_{1},y_{2}\}\in\mathcal{E}_{T}
with
y_{1}\lesssim y_{2}
that is
y_{2}=y_{1}^{+}
. Then, using the decomposition

\displaystyle u_{T}(y_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{T}(y_{1},y_{1}^{+})\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T}(y_{1}))+\sum_{y_{2}\lesssim x\neq r}d_{T}(x,x^{+})\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T}(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d_{T}(y_{1},y_{1}^{+})\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T}(y_{1}))+u_{T}(y_{2}),

one has that
u_{T}(y_{1})-u_{T}(y_{2})=\pm d_{T}(y_{1},y_{1}^{+})
and thus, since
y_{2}=y_{1}^{+}
,

(2.12)

|u_{T}(y_{1})-u_{T}(y_{2})|=d_{T}(y_{1},y_{2})=w(y_{1},y_{2})\quad\mbox{ for all }\quad\{y_{1},y_{2}\}\in\mathcal{E}_{T}.

Consequently, using Remark
2.2
, we have finally proved the following proposition.

Proposition 2.5
.

The function
u_{T}
defined by Equation (
2.9
) belongs to
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{T})
, and it satisfies

u_{T}(y)-u_{T}(y^{+})=d_{T}(y,y^{+})\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T}(y)),\;\mbox{ for all }y\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}.

Now, using Proposition
2.4
with
\xi=\mu-\nu
, it follows that

(2.13)

K_{d_{T}}(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}u_{T}(y)(\mu(y)-\nu(y)).

Therefore,
u_{T}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{T})
is a maximiser of the dual Kantorovich problem (
1.2
), meaning that
u_{T}
is an optimal dual potential for the OT problem with
d_{T}
as the ground cost. Notice that it is imposed that
u_{T}(r)=0
. This comes from the fact that
u_{T}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{T})
has an additive constant as a degree of freedom, meaning that every shift of
u_{T}
by a constant value, also gives function belonging to
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{T})
and thus another optimal dual potential.

Remark 2.3
.

It is easy to see that changing the root of the tree shifts the optimal potential (
3.9
) by a constant.

Remark 2.4
.

As discussed in
[
16
]
, there may exists multiple solution
u_{T}
of the form (
2.9
) that satisfy Equality (
2.8
) due to the indeterminacy of the sign function for the vertices
x
at which
\Xi_{T}(x)
vanishes. However, when the measures
\mu
and
\nu
are weakly non-degenerate
and taking
\xi=\mu-\nu
, it follows that
\Xi_{T}(x)\neq 0
for any
x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}
. Indeed, suppose by contradiction that
\Xi_{T}(x)=0
for
x\neq r
, then

\displaystyle 0=\Xi_{T}(x)=\sum_{y\lesssim x}(\mu(y)-\nu(y))=\mu(T_{x})-\nu(T_{x}),

which contradicts the weak non-degeneracy of
\mu
and
\nu
. Hence, under this non-degeneracy condition, Theorem
3.3
and Proposition
2.4
imply there exists a unique Kantorovich potential
u_{T}
satisfying
u_{T}(r)=0
whose expression is given by (
2.9
).

2.5.
Optimization over spanning trees

We now provide further details on the minimization problem (
1.3
) over spanning trees to compute the K-distance.
In this section, we assume that
\mathcal{X}
is a finite metric space with a distance
d_{G}
that is induced by an edge-weighted graph
G=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E}_{G},w)
assumed to be connected and without loops. We denote by
\mathrm{ST}(G)
the set of rooted spanning trees of
G
that is the set of subgraphs of
G
that are rooted trees containing all the vertices of
G
and whose edges belong to
\mathcal{E}_{G}
. A tree in
\mathrm{ST}(G)
rooted at
r
is written as
T=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E}_{T},w,r)
, and a key remark is that, for any
\{x,y\}\in\mathcal{E}_{T}\subset\mathcal{E}_{G}
,

(2.14)

d_{T}(x,y)=w(x,y).

Then, we have the following fundamental property.

Proposition 2.6
.

[
16
]
[Theorem 10] Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two probability measures in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
.
For the ground cost
d_{G}
, the K-distance between two probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
is the value of the following optimization problem:

(2.15)

\displaystyle K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{T\in\mathrm{ST}(G)}K_{d_{T}}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{T\in\mathrm{ST}(G)}\|\mu-\nu\|_{\mathrm{AE}(T)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{T\in\mathrm{ST}(G)}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}}d_{T}(x,x^{+})\left|\mu(T_{x})-\nu(T_{x})\right|,

where the above minimum is taken over the set of weighted rooted spanning trees of
G
, with
d_{T}
denoting the metric induced by a given tree
T=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E}_{T},w,r)\in\mathrm{ST}(G)
.

Remark 2.5
.

The right statement of
[
16
]
[Theorem 10] is to take the minimum over the set of spanning forest of the graph, but we can reduce the optimization problem to spanning trees by adding transfers with zero mass between components of the forest. From the Caley’s formula one obtains that the number of rooted spanning trees of the complete graph on
N
vertices is
N^{N-1}
, while the number of spanning forests of the same graph is
(N+1)^{N-1}
. This gives a (small) reduction of the problem since
(N+1)^{N-1}\sim eN^{N-1}
as
N\to\infty
.

Remark 2.6
.

In the equalities of Proposition
2.6
the root of the tree is only needed in order to obtain the closed form formula in the last line (
2.15
), but since it is invariant under rerooting, one can use any root.

Remark 2.7
.

It is important to disambiguate an optimal spanning tree
T_{\ast}
of
G
minimizing (
1.3
) from the support the optimal plan
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
that is a vertex of
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
to which it is associated when considering optimal transport with ground cost
d_{T_{\ast}}
, which is an forest of
K_{\mathcal{X}}
. There may exist
(x,y)\notin\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{G}
with
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)>0
, meaning that a positive quantity of mass is send from
x
to
y
using
P_{x,y}
in
T_{\ast}
, as illustrated in Figure
1
.

We claim that if the optimal plan
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
associated to
K_{d_{T_{\ast}}}(\mu,\nu)
is a spanning tree, then
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
uses every edge of
T_{\ast}
(in some direction) to send mass. To prove the claim notice that if an edge
\{x,y\}\in\mathcal{E}_{T}
is not used in any direction, the flow of mass is restricted to
T_{x}
and
\mathcal{X}\setminus T_{x}
, meaning that the support of
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
is a forest and not a tree.

Figure 1
.
The red dots represent a set of
N=6
vertices, and the blue segments are the edges in
T_{\ast}
. The green and dashed arrow is an oriented edge
(x,y)
in the support of
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
(that is such that
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)>0
) which does not belong to
\mathcal{E}_{T_{\ast}}
. The mass sent from
x
to
y
follows the blue path.

3.
Theoretical results : properties of the optimal spanning trees, plan and potentials

In this section, we study several properties of the optimal spanning trees that minimize (
2.15
), and we study their connections to the solutions of the primal (
1.1
) and dual (
1.2
) formulations of Kantorovich OT when taking the metric
d_{G}
as the ground cost.

3.1.
From spanning trees to optimal couplings

Let us first remark that a closed-form of an optimal coupling
\gamma_{G}
between two arbitrary probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is generally not available. One has thus to rely on computational approaches to solve the linear program (
1.1
) that yield a numerical approximation of an OT plan. Interestingly, thanks to the closed-form expressions (
2.11
) and (
2.13
) of the K-distance when the ground cost is a metric induced by a tree, it is possible to find an explicit expression of
\gamma_{G}
and the related optimal dual potential
u_{G}
under restrictive assumptions on
\mu
and
\nu
when the ground cost is the metric induced by the weighted graph
G=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E}_{G},w)
.

The result below is partly inspired from
[
16
]
[Theorem 6]. Before stating it, we need the following definitions.
For
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
we define its positive part
a^{+}
and negative part
a^{-}
as

a^{+}=\max(a,0)\quad\text{and}\quad a^{-}=\max(-a,0).

It is easy to see that that at most one of them is different from
0
,
a=a^{+}-a^{-}
and
|a|=a^{+}+a^{-}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two probability measures in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
. Let
T_{\ast}\in\mathrm{ST}(G)
be a minimizer of the optimization problem (
2.15
) rooted at
r
. Suppose that
T_{\ast}
satisfies the following condition

(3.1)

\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x)\cdot\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(y)<0,\;\mbox{ for all }y\in\mathrm{child}(x),\;\mbox{ and }\;x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\},

where
\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x)=\sum_{y\lesssim x}\xi(y)
for
\xi=\mu-\nu
, and
\cdot
denotes the usual multiplication between two scalars.
Then, the mapping
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by

\displaystyle\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,x^{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{+}(x),

\displaystyle\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x^{+},x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{-}(x),

\displaystyle\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu(x)-\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{+}(x)-\sum_{y\in\mathrm{child}(x)}\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{-}(y),

\displaystyle\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\mbox{ otherwise},

is an admissible transport plan between
\mu
and
\nu
, that is
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
.
Moreover, define the function

(3.2)

u_{T_{\ast}}(y)=\sum_{y\lesssim x\neq r}d_{T_{\ast}}(x,x^{+})\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x)),\quad\mbox{ for all }y\in\mathcal{X},

and suppose that it satisfies the condition

(3.3)

u_{T_{\ast}}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{G}).

Then, one has that
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
is an optimal coupling for the ground cost
d_{G}
and

u_{T_{\ast}}
is an optimal dual potential, that is

K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}d_{G}(x,y)\gamma_{\ast}(x,y)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}u_{T_{\ast}}(y)(\mu(y)-\nu(y)).

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Let us first prove that
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
is feasible, that is
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. Using the definition of
\gamma_{\ast}
, one has that

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in X}\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,x)+\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,x^{+})+\sum_{u\in\mathrm{child}(x)}\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,u)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,x)+\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{+}(x)+\sum_{u\in\mathrm{child}(x)}\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{-}(u)

\displaystyle=\mu(x).

Then, using the fact that

\xi(y)=\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(y)-\sum_{u\in\mathrm{child}(y)}\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(u),

it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(y,y)+\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(y^{+},y)+\sum_{u\in\mathrm{child}(y)}\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(u,y)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(y,y)+\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{-}(y)+\sum_{u\in\mathrm{child}(x)}\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{+}(u)

\displaystyle=\mu(y)-\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{+}(y)-\sum_{u\in\mathrm{child}(x)}\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{-}(u)+\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{-}(y)+\sum_{u\in\mathrm{child}(x)}\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{+}(u)

\displaystyle=\mu(y)-\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(y)+\sum_{u\in\mathrm{child}(x)}\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(u)

\displaystyle=\mu(y)-\xi(y)

\displaystyle=\nu(y).

Obviously, by construction, one has that
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)\geq 0
for all
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
with
x\neq y
. Therefore, thanks to the above computations, it only remains to prove that
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
to conclude that
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
. To this end, let
x\in\mathcal{X}
and suppose that
\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x))>0
. By condition (
3.1
), one has thus that
\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(y))<0
for all
y\in\mathrm{child}(x)
. Now, using the equality

(3.4)

\xi(x)=\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x)-\sum_{y\in\mathrm{child}(x)}\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(y)

combined with the facts that
\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x)=\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{+}(x)
and
\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(y)=-\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{-}(y)
for all
y\in\mathrm{child}(x)
,
we obtain that

\mu(x)-\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{+}(x)=\nu(x)+\sum_{y\in\mathrm{child}(x)}\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{-}(y).

This implies that

\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,x)=\mu(x)-\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{+}(x)-\sum_{y\in\mathrm{child}(x)}\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{-}(y)=\nu(x)\geq 0.

Now, supposing that
\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x))<0
, implies, by condition (
3.1
), that
\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(y))>0
for all
y\in\mathrm{child}(x)
. Therefore, by definition of
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,x)
, this yields

\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,x)=\mu(x)-\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{+}(x)-\sum_{y\in\mathrm{child}(x)}\Xi_{T_{\ast}}^{-}(y)=\mu(x)\geq 0.

We have thus proved that
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
implying that
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
.

Let us now prove that
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
and
u_{T_{\ast}}
are complementary in the sense of (
2.3
). By construction of
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
, its support is restricted to the pairs
\{x,x\}
,
\{x,x^{+}\}
and
\{x^{+},x\}
of edges in
\mathcal{E}_{T_{\ast}}
. We first remark that
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,x)>0
necessarily implies that
u(x)-u(x)=0=d_{G}(x,x)
. Now, for
x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}
, one has that
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,x^{+})>0
implies that
\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x))>0
, and thus by Proposition
2.5
one has that
u_{T_{\ast}}(x)-u_{T_{\ast}}(x^{+})=d_{T}(x,x^{+})=w(x,x^{+})=d_{G}(x,x^{+})
. Similarly, one has that
\gamma(x^{+},x)>0
implies that
\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x))<0
, and thus by Proposition
2.5
one has that
u_{T_{\ast}}(x)-u_{T_{\ast}}(x^{+})=-d_{T_{\ast}}(x,x^{+})=-w(x,x^{+})=-d_{G}(x,x^{+})
, meaning that
u_{T_{\ast}}(x^{+})-u_{T_{\ast}}(x)=d_{G}(x^{+},x)
by symmetry of a distance. Hence, we have proved that
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
and
u_{T_{\ast}}
are complementary.

Now, since
u_{T_{\ast}}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{G})
(by assumption) it follows from Proposition
2.3
that
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
is an optimal coupling for the ground cost
d_{G}
and
u_{T_{\ast}}
is an optimal dual potential which completes the proof of Proposition
3.1
.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

The first part of Proposition
3.1
on a closed form expression for an optimal transport plan is due to
[
16
]
[Theorem 6]. Under the condition (
3.1
), this expression of
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
as an optimal transport plan associated to the
K
-distance with ground cost
d_{T}
is valid for any tree
T
.

The novelty of Proposition
3.1
is to prove that, under the Lipschitz condition (
3.3
), the function
u_{T_{\ast}}
is a Kantorovich potential for the
K
-distance associated to the ground cost
d_{G}
. More precisely, if
T_{\ast}\in\mathrm{ST}(G)
is a minimiser of the optimisation problem (
2.15
), it follows from Equality (
2.13
) that

K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)=K_{d_{T_{\ast}}}(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}u_{T_{\ast}}(y)(\mu(y)-\nu(y)).

Then, if the following two conditions hold:
T_{\ast}
satisfies (
3.1
) and
u_{T_{\ast}}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{G})
, then Proposition
3.1
proves how one obtains an optimal transport plan (in a closed form) for OT with ground cost
d_{G}
by solving the optimisation problem (
2.15
) on the set
\mathrm{ST}(G)
of spanning trees of

. We next discuss these two conditions in more detail. Before doing so, we justify in the next section that condition (
3.1
) is exactly the setup where an optimal transport plan is equal to the solution of a minimum cost flow problem called the Beckmann formulation of OT.

3.1.1.
The connection with the Beckmann formulation of OT on a graph

It is well known in the OT literature that the transportation problem using the ground cost
d_{G}
induced by a graph
G
is intimately related with the minimum cost flow problem, see e.g.
[
17
]
[Chapter 6], and the so-called Beckmann formulation
[
2
]
of OT that reads as:

(3.5)

K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)=\min_{f\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{G}}}\left\{\sum_{(x,y)\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{G}}d_{G}(x,y)f(x,y)\;:\;\text{div}(f)=\mu-\nu\right\},

where the divergence operator
\text{div}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{G}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{X}}
of the flow
f
is defined on the set
\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{G}
of oriented edges of
G
as:

\text{div}(f)(x)=\sum_{y:(x,y)\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{G}}f(x,y)-\sum_{y:(y,x)\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{G}}f(y,x),\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{X}.

As explained in
[
17
]
[Chapter 6], the minimization problem (
3.5
) is derived from the dual formulation of the OT problem (
1.2
) when restricted to the Lipschitz constraints (
2.2
) on the edges of
G
combined with duality arguments.

When
G=T
is a rooted tree with orientation towards the root, the set of oriented edges of
T
is
\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{T}=\{(x,x^{+}),\;x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}\}\cup\{(x^{+},x),\;x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}\}
. In this framework, it is not difficult to see that there exists an explicit solution to the minimization problem (
3.5
)

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two probability measures in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
. Let
G=T
be a rooted tree and consider OT with ground cost
d_{T}
. A solution
f_{T}
of the Beckmann problem (
3.5
) (i.e. an optimal flow) is given by

\displaystyle f_{T}(x,x^{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Xi_{T}^{+}(x),

\displaystyle f_{T}(x^{+},x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Xi_{T}^{-}(x),

for every
x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}
.

Proof.

The flow
f_{T}
is by definition positive and by definition of
\Xi_{T}
and since
\Xi_{T}(x)=\Xi^{+}_{T}(x)-\Xi^{-}_{T}(x)
, one has that, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}
,

\displaystyle\text{div}(f_{T})(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{T}(x,x^{+})+\sum_{y\in\mathrm{child}(x)}f_{T}(y^{+},y)-f_{T}(x^{+},x)-\sum_{y\in\mathrm{child}(x)}f_{T}(y,y^{+})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Xi^{+}_{T}(x)-\Xi^{-}_{T}(x)-\sum_{y\in\mathrm{child}(x)}\Xi^{+}_{T}(y)-\Xi^{-}_{T}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Xi_{T}(x)-\sum_{y\in\mathrm{child}(x)}\Xi_{T}(y)=\xi(x)=\mu(x)-\nu(x).

Moreover, using the fact that
\Xi_{T}(r)=0
, one also has that

\displaystyle\text{div}(f_{T})(r)=\sum_{y\in\mathrm{child}(r)}\left(f_{T}(y^{+},y)-f_{T}(y,y^{+})\right)=\Xi_{T}(r)-\sum_{y\in\mathrm{child}(r)}\Xi_{T}(y)=\mu(r)-\nu(r).

Therefore, the divergence of
f_{T}
satisfies the constraint
\text{div}(f_{T})=\mu-\nu
. Finally, since the distance
d_{T}
is symmetric and given that
|\Xi_{T}(x)|=\Xi^{+}_{T}(x)+\Xi^{-}_{T}(x)
, one has that

\displaystyle\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{E}_{T}}d_{T}(x,y)f_{T}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}}d_{T}(x,x^{+})f_{T}(x,x^{+})+\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}}d_{T}(x^{+},x)f_{T}(x^{+},x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}}d_{T}(x,x^{+})(\Xi^{+}_{T}(x)+\Xi^{-}_{T}(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}}d_{T}(x,x^{+})|\Xi_{T}(x)|=K_{d_{T}}(\mu,\nu),

which proves the assertion.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

By similar arguments, when
G=T
is a rooted tree, a solution of the minimization problem (
2.5
) involved in the Definition
2.2
of the Arens-Eells norm of
\xi=\mu-\nu
is given by

a(x,x^{+})=\Xi_{T}(x)\text{ and }a(x,y)=0\text{ when }y\neq x^{+},

for all
x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r\}
.

Comparing the expression of the optimal flow
f_{T}
in Proposition
3.2
for the Beckmann problem with the one of the optimal coupling
\gamma_{T}
given in Proposition
3.1
, we see that they coincide outside of the diagonal of
\gamma_{T}
. Therefore, under the restrictive Condition (
3.1
), a solution of the Beckmann problem when
G=T
immediately provides the values of the off-diagonal entries of an optimal transport plan. But this is restrictive since transport plans naturally translate into flows but they are generally not equal. More formally, observe that a transport plan is indexed by
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
,
which can be interpreted as the set of oriented edges of the complete graph

K_{\mathcal{X}}
, whereas a flow is indexed by the set
\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{G}
of oriented
edges of the graph
G
. From a given feasible plan
\gamma
, one can create a flow
f_{\gamma}
, in the case of a tree
T
, by making the sum over all the mass sent over an oriented edge in this direction by
\gamma
, i.e. for every oriented edge
\vec{e}=(u,v)
define:

(3.6)

f_{\gamma}(u,v)=\sum_{(x,y):(u,v)\in P_{x,y}}\gamma(x,y).

3.2.
Relationships between OT problems with ground cost either
d_{G}
or
d_{T^{*}}

Recall that we denote by
T_{\ast}
an optimal spanning tree in
\mathrm{ST}(G)
satisfying

K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)=K_{d_{T_{\ast}}}(\mu,\nu),

which implies the following equalities

(3.7)

K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}d_{G}(x,y)\gamma_{G}(x,y)=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}d_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)=K_{d_{T_{\ast}}}(\mu,\nu),

and

(3.8)

K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}u_{G}(x)(\mu(x)-\nu(x))=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}u_{T_{\ast}}(x)(\mu(x)-\nu(x))=K_{d_{T_{\ast}}}(\mu,\nu),

where
\gamma_{G}
(resp.
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
) and
u_{G}
(resp.
u_{T_{\ast}}
) denote an optimal transport plan and an optimal dual potential for OT with ground cost
d_{G}
(resp.
d_{T\ast}
).

We now investigate the relationships between the optimal couplings
\gamma_{G}
and
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
, as well as between the corresponding potentials
u_{G}
and
u_{T_{\ast}}
. To this end, we first need to introduce the notion of a geodesic graph.

Definition 3.1
.

The geodesic graph of
G
is the spanning graph containing all the edges belonging to a path with minimum distance, called geodesic path, between two vertices of
G
.

The geodesic graph of a given graph
G
together with the minimum cost paths for every pair of vertices can be obtained from the Floyd-Warshall algorithm
[
10
,
23
]
which takes
O(N^{3})
where
N
is the number of vertices of the graph
[
5
]
.

For some specific cases of measures
\mu
and
\nu
, Proposition
3.1
gives a way to find an optimal coupling for the K-distance with ground cost
d_{G}
from the knowledge of the optimal spanning tree
T_{\ast}
solving (
2.15
). However, given two arbitrary measures
\mu
and
\nu
, it is generally not clear whether
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
in (
3.7
) is an optimal plan for OT with ground cost
d_{G}
. The following result gives a positive answer to this question.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two probability measures in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
. Consider
T_{\ast}
a spanning tree realizing the minimum in the optimisation problem (
2.15
). Then, one has that
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
is also an optimal plan associated to OT with ground cost
d_{G}
meaning that

K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}d_{G}(x,y)\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y).

Moreover, for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
with
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)>0
, one has that
d_{G}(x,y)=d_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)
, meaning that the edges with positive transport in
T_{\ast}
belong to the geodesic graph of
G
.

Proof.

By definition of the
K
-distance, the fact
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
is an admissible plan and Equatily (
3.7
), it follows that

\displaystyle 0\leq\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)d_{G}(x,y)-K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)d_{G}(x,y)-K_{d_{T_{\ast}}}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)d_{G}(x,y)-\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)d_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)\left(d_{G}(x,y)-d_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)\right).

Since
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)\geq 0
and
d_{G}(x,y)\leq d_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)
(as
T_{\ast}
is a spanning tree of
G
), the above inequality implies that
\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)\left(d_{G}(x,y)-d_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)\right)=0
. Therefore, one obtains that
K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)d_{G}(x,y)
. Moreover, for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
with
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)>0
, one necessarily has that
d_{G}(x,y)-d_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)=0
which proves the last statement of Theorem
3.1
.
∎

The following result complements Theorem
3.1
for the dual formulation of the
K
-distance giving a relationship between Kantorovich potentials for optimal transport with ground cost either
d_{G}
or
d_{T_{\ast}}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two probability measures in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
. Consider
T_{\ast}
a spanning tree realizing the minimum in the optimisation problem (
2.15
). Then, one has that any Kantorovich potential
u_{G}
belongs to
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{T_{\ast}})
and realizes
K_{d_{T_{\ast}}}(\mu,\nu)
that is

K_{d_{T_{\ast}}}(\mu,\nu)=\max_{u\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{T_{\ast}})}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}u(x)(\mu(x)-\nu(x))=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}u_{G}(x)(\mu(x)-\nu(x)).

Moreover, for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
with
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)>0
, one has that

|u_{G}(x)-u_{G}(y)|=d_{G}(x,y)=d_{T_{\ast}}(x,y).

Proof.

Since any Kantorovich potential
u_{G}
belongs to
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{G})
one has that

|u_{G}(x)-u_{G}(y)|\leq d_{G}(x,y)\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}.

But since the metrics
d_{G}
and
d_{T_{\ast}}
are defined from the same weight function
w
, one has that
d_{G}(x,y)\leq d_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)
for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
implying that

|u_{G}(x)-u_{G}(y)|\leq d_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X}.

meaning that
u_{G}
belongs to
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{T_{\ast}})
.
Now, it follows that
u_{G}
is also an Kantorovich potential of
K_{T_{\ast}}(\mu,\nu)
since, from the dual formulation of the
K
-distance
K_{d_{T_{\ast}}}
and Equality (
3.8
), one has that

\max_{u\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(d_{T_{\ast}})}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}u(x)(\mu(x)-\nu(x))=K_{d_{T_{\ast}}}(\mu,\nu)=K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}u_{G}(x)(\mu(x)-\nu(x)).

Finally, to prove the last statement, we know from Theorem
3.1
that
d_{G}(x,y)=d_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)
whenever
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)>0
. Moreover, since
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
is an optimal plan it follows from Proposition
2.3
that for every
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)>0
one has

|u_{G}(x)-u_{G}(y)|=d_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)

implying that
|u_{G}(x)-u_{G}(y)|=d_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)=d_{G}(x,y)
whenever
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}(x,y)>0
.
∎

A positive result concerning the lifting of the Kantorovich potential
u_{T_{\ast}}
with ground cost
d_{T_{\ast}}
to a Kantorovich potential for the OT problem with ground cost
d_{G}
is provided by the following corollary. This result is an immediate consequence of Theorem
3.2
, since any Kantorovich potential for the OT problem with ground cost
d_{G}
is also a Kantorovich potential for OT with ground cost
d_{T_{\ast}}
.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
T_{\ast}
be an optimal spanning tree of
G
achieving
K_{d_{T_{\ast}}}(\mu,\nu)=K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)
. If the optimal potential
u_{T_{\ast}}
which realizes
K_{d_{T_{\ast}}}(\mu,\nu)
is unique (up to an additive constant), then necessarily the optimal potential
u_{G}
of
K_{d_{G}}(\mu,\nu)
is unique with
u_{G}=u_{T_{\ast}}
and its explicit expression is given by

(3.9)

u_{G}(y)=u_{T_{\ast}}(y)=\sum_{y\lesssim x\neq r}d_{T_{\ast}}(x,x^{+})\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi_{T_{\ast}}(x)),\quad\mbox{ for all }y\in\mathcal{X}.

Finally, if the pair
(\mu,\nu)
is weakly non-degenerate, the following result is the main contribution of this paper that establishes a connection between an optimal spanning tree minimizing (
1.3
) and the Kantorovich potential
u_{G}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose that the pair of probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is weakly non-degenerate. Then, there exists a unique Kantorovich potential
u_{G}
(up to an additive constant), whose explicit explicit expression is given by (
3.9
).

Proof.

Suppose that the pair of probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is weakly non-degenerate and that
G=T
is a tree. Let
\gamma_{T}
be any optimal transport plan that is a vertex of the transportation polytope, and let
u
be any dual potential. By Proposition
2.2
, one has that
\mathop{\rm supp}\nolimits(\gamma_{T})
is a tree (up to loops) with exactly
N-1
proper edges. Therefore, from Proposition
2.3
, one has that

(3.10)

\displaystyle u(x)-u(y)=d_{T}(x,y)

\displaystyle\text{ for all }x\neq y\text{ such that }\gamma_{T}(x,y)>0,

which are
N-1
linearly independent equations for
N

unknown variables
(u(x))_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
, meaning that the Kantorovich potential

obtained from the knowledge of the support of
\gamma_{T}
is unique up to an additive constant. We remark that the linear independence of the

.

Now, from Proposition
2.5

and the conclusion for general

∎

3.3.1.
Maximizing the mass transported on the diagonal

The maximization of the mass on the diagonal terms is inspired by the
Beckmann problem (
3.5
). Indeed, in this formulation, the behavior
at a node
x\in\mathcal{X}
is entirely determined by the sign of

\mu(x)-\nu(x)
through the divergence constraint imposed on admissible
flows. Intuitively, since mass can be retained at a node
x
at zero cost,
it is natural to first remove the maximal possible amount of mass before
sending the remaining mass throughout the graph. Hence, a key result is the following proposition showing that one may reduce the complexity of constructing an optimal coupling by maximizing its diagonal.

Proposition 3.3
.

Consider the OT problem with ground cost
d_{T}
between two measures
\mu
and
\nu
. Then, there always exists an
optimal transport plan
\gamma_{T}
satisfying

\gamma_{T}(x,x)=\min\{\mu(x),\nu(x)\}\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{X}.

Proof.

Let
\gamma_{T}
be an optimal transport plan for the OT problem between two measures
\mu
and
\nu
with ground cost
d_{T}
. Since
\gamma_{T}
is an admissible transport plan, one necessarily has that
\gamma_{T}(x,x)\leq\min\{\mu(x),\nu(x)\}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, else the marginal constraints cannot be satisfied. Suppose that there exists
x\in\mathcal{X}
such that
\gamma_{T}(x,x)<\min\{\mu(x),\nu(x)\}
. Since

\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\gamma_{T}(x,y)=\mu(x)

and

\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\gamma_{T}(y,x)=\nu(x),

there necessarily exists
y_{1}\neq x
and
y_{2}\neq x
such that
\gamma_{T}(x,y_{1})>0
and
\gamma_{T}(y_{2},x)>0
. Now, let

m=\min\left(\gamma_{T}(x,y_{1}),\gamma_{T}(y_{2},x),\delta_{x}\right)\quad\text{with}\quad\delta_{x}=\min\{\mu(x),\nu(x)\}-\gamma_{T}(x,x),

and construct the plan
\tilde{\gamma}_{T}
having all its entries equal to those of
\gamma_{T}
except for the following ones:

(3.11)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tilde{\gamma}_{T}(x,y_{1})&=\gamma_{T}(x,y_{1})-m,\\
\tilde{\gamma}_{T}(y_{2},x)&=\gamma_{T}(y_{2},x)-m,\\
\tilde{\gamma}_{T}(x,x)&=\gamma_{T}(x,x)+m,\\
\tilde{\gamma}_{T}(y_{2},y_{1})&=\gamma_{T}(y_{2},y_{1})+m.\end{aligned}\right.

By the definition of
m
, all the entries of the plan
\tilde{\gamma}_{T}
are positive, and it can be easily checked that it satisfies the marginal constraints
\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\gamma}_{T}(u,y)=\mu(u)

and

\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\tilde{\gamma}_{T}(y,u)=\nu(u),

for all
u\in\mathcal{X}
. Hence,
\tilde{\gamma}_{T}
is an admissible transport plan. Then, the difference between the cost of the plans
\gamma_{T}
and
\tilde{\gamma}_{T}
has to be negative since
\gamma_{T}
is an optimal transport plan and satisfies

\displaystyle 0\geq\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}d_{T}(x,y)\gamma_{T}(x,y)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y\in\mathcal{X}}d_{T}(x,y)\tilde{\gamma}_{T}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\left(d_{T}(x,y_{1})+d_{T}(y_{2},x)-d_{T}(x,x)-d_{T}(y_{2},y_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m\left(d_{T}(x,y_{1})+d_{T}(y_{2},x)-d_{T}(y_{2},y_{1})\right).

Combining the above inequality with the triangle inequality we thus obtain that

(3.12)

\displaystyle d_{T}(x,y_{1})+d_{T}(y_{2},x)=d_{T}(y_{2},y_{1}),

meaning that the node
x
belongs to
P_{y_{1},y_{2}}
, the unique path connecting
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
in
T
.
Consequently, the transport costs of
\tilde{\gamma}_{T}
and
\gamma_{T}
are equal, and we thus deduce that
\tilde{\gamma}_{T}
is also an optimal transport plan with

\min\{\mu(x),\nu(x)\}\geq\tilde{\gamma}_{T}(x,x)>\gamma_{T}(x,x)

since
m\geq\delta_{x}>0
. Hence, until there exists
x\in\mathcal{X}
with
\tilde{\gamma}_{T}(x,x)<\min\{\mu(x),\nu(x)\}
, we may repeat this construction by modifying two rows and two columns of
\tilde{\gamma}_{T}

for all

, which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

From the proof of Proposition
3.3
, it follows that every optimal transport plan
\gamma_{T}
admits a canonical representative
\tilde{\gamma}_{T}
that maximizes the mass retained at each vertex. This naturally induces an equivalence relation on the set of OT plans, where two plans are equivalent if they share the same canonical representative. Proposition
3.3
also plays a key role in the implementation of Algorithm

described in Section

, as it justifies the construction of an optimal transport plan that maximizes the mass on the diagonal.

Remark 3.4
.

It is easy to see that any solution

is only a sender of mass or a receiver of mass. In other words there is a line or a column on

4.
Algorithms and its properties

In this section we present two algorithms. First, we present a dynamic programming way to find an optimal transport plan of a given tree together with a proof of its correctness and properties of the solution. Secondly, we present a simulated annealing type stochastic algorithm to find a spanning tree
T_{\ast}
of
G
minimizing (

).

These algorithms should be thought as first running the second algorithm to find the structure of the spanning tree
T_{\ast}
of

is found it can be used as an input of the first algorithm to find an optimal plan
\gamma_{T_{\ast}}
for OT with ground cost

as justified by Theorem
3.1
.

4.1.
Algorithm 1: dynamic programming to find the optimal plan from a given tree
T
.

We propose below an algorithm based on the principles of dynamical programming to construct an optimal coupling
\gamma_{T}
(when the ground cost is
d_{T}
) that works for any pair of measures
\mu
and
\nu
.

The dynamical programming procedure to construct
\gamma_{T}
consists in reducing the total mass of the measures at each step of the algorithm, and iteratively fill the transport plan on a sequence of reduced problems. We start by filling the diagonal entries of the OT plan as
\gamma_{T}(x,x)=\min\{\mu(x),\nu(x)\}
for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
. We interpret the imbalance mass
\xi(x)=\mu(x)-\nu(x)
as the offer when it is positive and demand otherwise. It
\xi(x)=0
then the node
x
is said to be balanced. At each step, our algorithm takes a leaf of the current tree, say
x
, and it finds a specific node
y
with the opposite demand/offer behavior (i.e.
\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\xi(x))\neq\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\xi(y))
). We send from the node offering to the node demanding the maximum amount of mass possible, in such a way that one remains consistent with the total cumulative offer/demand
\Xi_{T}(u)
on the nodes
u
belonging to the unique path between
x
and
y
. Once this is accomplished, we can update
\mu
and
\nu
by decreasing their total mass, which is equivalent to updating the imbalance function
\xi
and the cumulative function
\Xi_{T}
. In this way, the total imbalance mass
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\xi(x)|
decreases at each iteration of the algorithm which guarantees its convergence.

A formal description of this procedure is detailed in Algorithm
1
, where it is understood that
\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\cdot)
is the usual sign function with
\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(0)=0
. This algorithm proceeds by recursively peeling leaves and propagating the excess mass from offering nodes to demanding nodes, while updating the imbalance function
\xi
and the cumulative function
\Xi_{T}
.
In the literature, existing algorithms for optimal transport using tree metrics follow a similar bottom-up dynamic programming approach to produce optimal couplings
[
19
,
15
]
.
The closest procedure to Algorithm
1
is the one described in
[
15
, Section 4.2]
, which (informally) operates in two distinct phases:
in the first phase, the excess mass of all subtrees
T_{x}
with
\mu(T_{x})-\nu(T_{x})>0
is pushed toward the root, while,
in the second phase, this mass is sent toward the leaves in the subtrees
T_{x}
with a deficit of mass
\mu(T_{x})-\nu(T_{x})<0
.
While the algorithm in
[
15
, Section 4.2]
also yields an optimal transport plan, both its description and the proof of its convergence are more involved than those of Algorithm
1
.
To the best of our knowledge, this construction of a transport plan has not been explicitly considered before.
We therefore analyze below several properties of the matrix
\gamma_{T}
produced by Algorithm
1
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Algorithm
1
terminates after a finite number of steps. Moreover, one has that:

(i)

during the iterations of the algorithm, every entry
\gamma_{T}(x,y)
is assigned a positive value at most once. Equivalently, at a given iteration, if the algorithm transfers a mass of size
m>0
between a leaf
x
and a node

(x,y)
cannot be used at a subsequent iteration to update the transport plan.

(ii)

if
\gamma_{T}(x,y)>0
with

then necessarily
\gamma_{T}(y,x)=0
.

(iii)

Let
P_{x,y}
denotes the set of edges on the unique path between two nodes
x
and
y
. If
\gamma_{T}(x,y)>0
, it follows that

(iii+)

\Xi_{T}(v)=\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\xi(x))
for any
\{v,v^{+}\}
belonging to
P_{x,y}

(iii-)

\Xi_{T}(v)=-\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\xi(x))
for any
\{v^{+},v\}
belonging to
P_{x,y}

Proof.

To simplify the notation, we write
\Xi=\Xi_{T}
.
Let us first prove that the Algorithm
1
terminates after a finite number of steps. To this end, it suffices to show that, at each iteration of the algorithm, there always exists a node
y
satisfying conditions (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) that can be associated to
x
with
m>0
. Indeed, let assume that, at a given iteration of the algorithm, one peaks a leaf with
\xi(x)>0
(the other case being symmetric). Then, the algorithm moves up in the tree until finding a node

satisfying:

(a)

\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\Xi(v))>0
for all nodes

on the path

from

(b)

\Xi(u)=\Xi(u_{-})+\Delta(u)

denotes the child of

and
\Delta(u)=\sum_{v\in\mathrm{child}(u)\setminus\{u_{-}\}}\Xi(v)
is such that
\Delta(u)<0
. Therefore, there necessarily exists a node

with
\Xi(v)<0
. Hence, thanks to the definition of the cumulative function
\Xi
, we may prove in a recursive manner that there necessarily exists a node
y
in the subtree

satisfying (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) with
\xi(y)\neq 0
and
\Xi(v)<0
on any edge

belonging to
P_{u,y}
.

Then, by definition, the quantity
m
is either equal to

\min\{\xi(x),-\xi(y)\},\;\min_{(v,v^{+})\in P_{x,u}}\{\Xi(v)\}\quad\text{or}\quad\min_{(v^{+},v)\in P_{u,y}}\{-\Xi(v)\}.

Consequently, the update

and
\xi(y)\leftarrow\xi(y)+m
implies that either the imbalance function
\xi
or the cumulative function
\Xi
has one more entry equal to zero after each iteration. Therefore, during the iterations, all the entries of
\xi(x)_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
will progressively become equal to zero, implying that the algorithm terminates after a finite number of steps, which proves the first statement of Proposition
4.1
.

Let us now prove the statements (i) and (ii). The value
\gamma_{T}(x,x)
is fixed to
\min\{\mu(x),\nu(x)\}
at initialization for all

, and it is clearly not modified during the iterations of the algorithm. Then, when a leaf
x
of the current tree

with
\xi(x)\neq 0

is chosen, a nearest node

with opposite sign of

is found, and a quantity

is transferred from the node offering to the node demanding. Assume that
\xi(x)>0
and
\xi(y)<0

and

. The function

\xi(x)\leftarrow\xi(x)-m,\qquad\xi(y)\leftarrow\xi(y)+m.

If

becomes balanced and it is removed from the tree

becomes balanced and, if it is a leaf, it is removed. In either of these two cases, at least one of the vertices

or

becomes balanced. Since, at the following iterations, the algorithm never updates

where either vertex

(x,y)
or

cannot be used at a later iteration. In the case, where
\xi(x)-m\neq 0
and

from

to

or

belonging to this path. Consequently, at subsequent iterations, the algorithm can never associate

Finally, statement (iii) is an immediate consequence of Properties (

We shall now prove that the matrix
\gamma_{T}
given by Algorithm

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\gamma_{T}
be the matrix produced by Algorithm
1
.
Then
\gamma_{T}
is an admissible transport plan that is
\gamma_{T}\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)

Proof.

The algorithm starts with

\gamma_{T}(x,x)=\min\{\mu(x),\nu(x)\},\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{X},\text{ and }\gamma_{T}(x,y)=0\text{ for }x\neq y,

and one has that
\xi=\mu-\nu
at its initialization.
At each step of the algorithm, the updating of
\xi
records the residual excess or deficit of mass at each vertex: a positive value
\xi(x)>0
represents a remaining offer at
x
, and a negative value
\xi(x)<0
a remaining demand.
For a matrix
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}
, we introduce the notation

\mathrm{row}_{A}(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}A(x,y),\qquad\mathrm{col}_{A}(y)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}A(x,y).

In what follows, we shall prove by induction, that at each step of the algorithm, the following equalities always hold:

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{row}_{\gamma_{T}}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu(x)-\max\{\xi(x),0\}\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{X},

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathrm{col}_{\gamma_{T}}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu(y)-\max\{-\xi(y),0\}\text{ for all }y\in\mathcal{X},

where it is understood that the value of
\xi
is evolving according to Algorithm
1
. Indeed, at initialization of the algorithm, one has that, for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

\mathrm{row}_{\gamma_{T}}(x)=\gamma_{T}(x,x)=\min\{\mu(x),\nu(x)\}=\mu(x)-\max\{\mu(x)-\nu(x),0\}=\mu(x)-\max\{\xi(x),0\},

and similarly, for every

,

\mathrm{col}_{\gamma_{T}}(y)=\gamma_{T}(y,y)=\nu(y)-\max\{-\xi(y),0\}.

Thus, Equalities (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) hold at initialization. Let us now consider one step of the algorithm, and suppose that it chooses a leaf
x
and a node
y
with opposite signs

\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\xi(x))\neq\mathop{\rm Sgn}\nolimits(\xi(y))
, and it pushes

m\leq\min(|\xi(x)|,|\xi(y)|)

units of mass from a node offering to a node demanding. We distinguish the following two cases:

(1)

Consider first the case
\xi(x)>0
(offer at
x
) and
\xi(y)<0
(demand at
y
).
The algorithm updates the matrix
\gamma_{T}
and the excess/deficit function

as follows:

\gamma_{T}(x,y)\leftarrow\gamma_{T}(x,y)+m,\qquad\xi(x)\leftarrow\xi(x)-m,\qquad\xi(y)\leftarrow\xi(y)+m.

Hence, assuming that Equalities (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) hold at the previous iteration, one has that the quantities
\mathrm{row}_{\gamma_{T}}(x)
and
\mathrm{col}_{\gamma_{T}}(y)

since

by definition of

.
Similarly,

\mathrm{col}_{\gamma_{T}}(y)\leftarrow\mathrm{col}_{\gamma_{T}}(y)+m=\big(\nu(y)+\xi(y)\big)+m=\nu(y)-(-\xi(y)-m)=\nu(y)-\max\{-(\xi(y)+m),0\},

by definition of

.
All other rows and columns of

remain unchanged, and therefore Equalities (

(2)

The second case
\xi(x)<0

\xi(x)
and
\xi(y)

At each iteration of the algorithm, a non-negative amount

Therefore the matrix
\gamma_{T}
produced by Algorithm

has positive entries and its marginals (when summing either over rows or columuns) are

The following proposition proves the optimality of

equals
|\mu(T_{u})-\nu(T_{u})|
, meaning that the total mass crossing the edge
e
goes in the direction of the sign of
\mu(T_{u})-\nu(T_{u})
.

To prove this property, we let
\Xi(u)=\sum_{x\lesssim u}\xi(x)=\mu(T_{u})-\nu(T_{u})
be the imbalance mass of subtree
T_{u}
, and suppose that
\mu(T_{u})-\nu(T_{u})>0
at the initialization of the algorithm. During the iterations of the algorithm, a leaf
x\in T_{u}
with
\xi(x)>0
will eventually be picked and associated to a node
y
with
\xi(y)<0
. Then, for a positive
m>0
, the algorithm will update the transport plan and the function
\xi
as follows:
\gamma_{T}(y,x)\leftarrow m
,
\xi(x)\leftarrow\xi(x)-m
and
\xi(y)\leftarrow\xi(y)+m
. Therefore, if
y\in T_{u}
then the value of the cumulative function
\Xi(u)
will not change. To the contrary, if
y\in T_{u}^{c}
then
\Xi(u)
will decrease by an amount of
m
, meaning that a quantity
m
of mass is pushed from
T_{u}
to
T_{u}^{c}
. As shown in the proof of Proposition
4.1
, the value of the cumulative function
\Xi(u)
decreases along the iterations until reaching zero, implying that the total of mass transferred from
T_{u}
to
T_{u}^{c}
through the edge
e=(u,u^{+})
is

\mu(T_{u})-\nu(T_{u})=\sum_{x\in T_{u}}\sum_{y\in T_{u}^{c}}\gamma_{T}(x,y),

and that

(4.10)

\sum_{x\in T_{u}^{c}}\sum_{y\in T_{u}}\gamma_{T}(x,y)=0.

Indeed, by construction of the algorithm, sending mass from a leaf
x\in T_{u}^{c}
with
\xi(x)>0
to a demanding node
y\in T_{u}
with
\xi(y)<0
by passing through the edge
e=(u,u^{+})
necessarily requires that
\Xi(u)<0
which is not possible as the sign of
\Xi(u)
always remains unchanged (and thus positive here) during the iterations of the algorithm by definition (
4.3
) of the quantity of mass
m
that can be transferred between two nodes. Hence, transferring mass from
T_{u}^{c}
to
T_{u}
is not allowed implying that
\gamma_{T}(x,y)=0
for any pair
(x,y)
with
x\in T_{u}^{c}
and
y\in T_{u}
which proves that equality (
4.10
) holds. This completes the proof.
∎

Equations (
4.6
) and (
4.7
) may be interpreted as follows. Recall that, for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}

P_{x,y}
the unique path going from

in the tree

4.3
implies that if
\gamma_{T}(x,y)>0
for some

\gamma_{T}(u,v)=0
for every

P_{u,v}
in the tree
T
uses an edge in

in the opposite direction. We illustrate this property in Figure

4.2.
Algorithm 2: simulated annealing to find an optimal spanning tree of
G

We now describe a stochastic algorithm based on simulated annealing (SA) to find a solution to the optimisation problem (
1.3
) over the set
\mathrm{ST}(G)
of spanning trees of a given edge-weighted graph
G=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E}_{G},w)
. The algorithm starts with an initial spanning tree of
G
that is denoted as
T_{0}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E}_{0},w)
and rooted at an arbitrary vertex
r_{0}
. Then, our stochastic algorithm at iteration
m\geq 1
consists in randomly generating a new candidate spanning tree
\hat{T}_{m}
rooted at a new root
\hat{r}_{m}
. Based on the principles of simulated annealing, we first compute the following Hamiltonian function

H(T_{m-1},\hat{T}_{m})=K_{d_{T_{m-1}}}(\mu,\nu)-K_{d_{\hat{T}_{m}}}(\mu,\nu).

We define the probability

p_{m}=\min(1,\exp(\beta_{m}H(T_{m-1},\hat{T}_{m}))),

where
\beta_{m}>0
is a temperature parameter that is calibrated during the iterations using an adaptive cooling procedure, to be described below. We accept or reject the candidate spanning tree from a sample
U_{m}
of the uniform distribution on
[0,1]
and
\hat{T}_{m}
as follows:

-

if
U_{m}\leq p_{m}
then
T_{m}=\hat{T}_{m}
and
r_{m}=\hat{r}_{m}
(accept),

-

if
U_{m}>p_{m}
then
T_{m}=T_{m-1}
and
r_{m}=r_{m-1}
(reject).

Notice that
H(T_{m-1},\hat{T}_{m})
is positive if the candidate tree
\hat{T}_{m}
has smaller AE-norm than
T_{m-1}
, so we improve the value of the functional of the optimisation problem (
1.3
) by replacing
T_{m-1}
with
\hat{T}_{m}
. When this happens, the parameter
p_{m}=1
and the candidate spanning tree is thus always accepted.

4.2.1.
Choice of the random transitions and fast evaluation of the Hamiltonian

The crucial steps in the above algorithm are the random choice of a candidate spanning tree
\hat{T}_{m}
and the fast evaluation of the Hamiltonian function. These two steps are described below.

Random choice of
\hat{T}_{m}
. From iteration
m-1
, one has a weighted spanning tree
T_{m-1}=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E}_{m-1},w)
rooted at
r_{m-1}
that is oriented from the leaves to the root due to the partial ordering that has been chosen in Section
2.4
.
Hence, every vertex but the root has exactly one outgoing edge.
Starting from the spanning tree
T_{m-1}
rooted at
r_{m-1}
, we randomly choose a vertex
\hat{r}_{m}\neq r_{m-1}
by a uniform sampling in the neighbors of
r_{m-1}
in the graph
G=(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{E}_{G},w)
. Then, we define
\hat{T}_{m}=(\mathcal{X},\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{m},w)
as the tree rooted at
\hat{r}_{m}
, formed by the new set of edges

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{m}=\{\mathcal{E}_{m-1}\cup{\overline{e}_{m}}\}\setminus{\{\underline{e}_{m}\}},

for

\overline{e}_{m}=\{r_{m-1},\hat{r}_{m}\},\mbox{ and }\underline{e}_{m}=\{\hat{r}_{m},\hat{r}_{m}^{+}\};

where the edge
(\hat{r}_{m},\hat{r}_{m}^{+})
, a directed version of
\underline{e}_{m}
, was the unique outgoing edge of
\hat{r}_{m}
in
T_{m-1}
. It is easy to verify that

\hat{T}_{m}:=(\mathcal{X},\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{m},w)

is still a spanning tree, since it is still connected and every vertex but the root has exactly one outgoing edge.

Fast computation of the Hamiltonian.
In the graph
G
, we denote by
\mathcal{C}_{m}
the cycle obtained by the union of the path from
\hat{r}_{m}
to
r_{m-1}
in the tree
T_{m-1}
and the new edge
\overline{e}_{m}=\{r_{m-1},\hat{r}_{m}\}
. Then, a key remark is that
\Xi_{T_{m-1}}(x)=\Xi_{\hat{T}_{m}}(x)
for all edges
\{x,x^{+}\}
not belonging to this cycle. Therefore, noticing that

\Xi_{\hat{T}_{m}}(x)=\Xi_{T_{m-1}}(x)-\Xi_{T_{m-1}}(\hat{r}_{m})\text{ for }\{x,x^{+}\}\in\mathcal{C}_{m}\setminus\{\overline{e}_{m},\underline{e}_{m}\},

and that

\Xi_{\hat{T}_{m}}(r_{m-1})=-\Xi_{T_{m-1}}(\hat{r}_{m}),

it follows that computing the Hamiltonian simply amounts to a summation over edges only belonging to the cycle
\mathcal{C}_{m}
as shown by the expression below:

\displaystyle H(T_{m-1},\hat{T}_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{r_{m-1}\}}w(x,x^{+})|\Xi_{T_{m-1}}(x)|-\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{\hat{r}_{m}\}}w(x,x^{+})|\Xi_{\hat{T}_{m}}(x)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\{x,x^{+}\}\in\mathcal{C}_{m}\setminus{\overline{e}_{m},\underline{e}_{m}}}w(x,x^{+})|\Xi_{T_{m-1}}(x)|-\sum_{\{x,x^{+}\}\in\mathcal{C}_{m}\setminus{\overline{e}_{m},\underline{e}_{m}}}w(x,x^{+})|\Xi_{\hat{T}_{m}}(x)|

\displaystyle+w(\hat{r}_{m},\hat{r}_{m}^{+})|\Xi_{T_{m-1}}(\hat{r}_{m})|-w(r_{m-1},\hat{r}_{m})|\Xi_{\hat{T}_{m}}(r_{m-1})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\{x,x^{+}\}\in\mathcal{C}_{m}\setminus{\overline{e}_{m},\underline{e}_{m}}}w(x,x^{+})\left(|\Xi_{T_{m-1}}(x)|-|\Xi_{T_{m-1}}(x)-\Xi_{T_{m-1}}(\hat{r}_{m})|\right)

\displaystyle+\left(w(\hat{r}_{m},\hat{r}_{m}^{+})-w(r_{m-1},\hat{r}_{m})\right)|\Xi_{T_{m-1}}(\hat{r}_{m})|,

which is an expression that is fast to compute for the graph considered in these numerical experiments.

4.2.2.
An adaptive cooling schele

Finally, we use an adaptive strategy inspired by the general adaptive MCMC
framework of
[
1
]
, in which the temperature parameter

\beta_{m}
is progressively adjusted according to the observed acceptance rate
of the proposed transitions from
T_{m-1}
to
\hat{T}_{m}
. The underlying idea is to control the frequency with which proposed transitions are accepted.
When proposed transitions are accepted too frequently, the temperature is increased
in order to allow for a broader exploration of the space of trees.
Conversely, when the acceptance rate becomes too small, the temperature is
decreased in order to concentrate the search around the current tree. More precisely, let
a_{m}
denote the empirical acceptance rate at iteration
m
,
computed over a sliding window of the last
M
iterations (with
M=100
in our
experiments), and let
a^{\ast}\in(0,1)
be a prescribed target acceptance rate.
Starting from an initial temperature
\beta_{0}>0
, the temperature is updated
according to the multiplicative rule

\beta_{m+1}=\beta_{m}\bigl(1+\eta(a_{m}-a^{\ast})\bigr),

where
\eta>0
is a parameter chosen sufficiently small to ensure a smooth
evolution of the temperature across the iterations. When
a_{m}>a^{\ast}
, the temperature
\beta_{m}
increases, which results in a
higher probability of accepting moves that increase the Hamiltonian
H(T_{m-1},\hat{T}_{m})
.
On the contrary, when
a_{m}<a^{\ast}
, the temperature decreases, thereby
reducing the acceptance of such transitions, which favors exploration
around the current tree. The adaptation parameter
\eta
is chosen sufficiently small to ensure a smooth
evolution of the temperature across iterations. In all numerical experiments, we use
\beta_{0}=0.1
,
a^{\ast}=0.01
, and

\eta=0.01
.

6.
Conclusion and perspectives

We studied optimal transport between probability measures supported on the same finite metric space endowed with a graph-induced distance, leveraging a reformulation of the problem as a minimization over spanning trees. Under a weak non-degeneracy condition, we proved the uniqueness of the Kantorovich potential and derived an explicit expression based on an imbalanced cumulative function along an optimal spanning tree, revealing a close analogy with monotone transport on the real line. From a computational viewpoint, we proposed a stochastic algorithm based on simulated annealing to estimate the Kantorovich distance, together with a dynamic programming procedure to compute an optimal transport plan and a dual potential. Overall, our approach provides a computational alternative to classical network simplex and minimum-cost flow methods for optimal transport on graphs.

An interesting direction for future research concerns the theoretical analysis of the convergence of the simulated annealing algorithm for minimizing the OT functional over the space of spanning trees. Another interesting perspective is the application of the proposed framework to large-scale problems in computational optimal transport.
