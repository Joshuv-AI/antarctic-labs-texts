Osteoporosis Knowledge of Older Women with Fragility Fractures: A Cross-Sectional Study Based on Latent Profile Analysis
Authors: Mengqi Shao, Qingqing Su, Mingxuan Liu, Chen Qiu, Mi Song, Jianan Li, Nan Tang, Youting Wang, Mingxing Lei, Jie Song, Yuan Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate osteoporosis knowledge (OK) profiles among older women with fragility fractures using latent profile analysis, identify subgroup characteristics, and explore associated factors.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 9212 older women with fragility fractures covering 31 provinces in China from September to November 2023. The survey utilized a self-designed general information questionnaire and the Chinese version of the Osteoporosis Knowledge Assessment Tool. Univariate analysis and logistic regression were employed to analyze associated factors. Latent Profile Analysis was applied to classify the older women with fragility fractures based on their OK levels, and multinomial logistic regression was used to identify factors influencing their OK levels.ResultsOlder women with fragility fractures were classified into five distinct latent profiles according to their OK levels: Low OK group (9.9%), Medium-Low OK group (11.1%), High OK group (50.9%), Medium OK group (18.8%), and High awareness and low management group (9.3%). Key factors influencing the classification of OK levels included marital status, BMI, education level, filial care, postoperative self-care ability, receipt of anti-osteoporosis therapy, use of calcium or vitamin D supplements, perceived necessity of fall prevention, receipt of health education, content of health education, and methods of health education delivery.ConclusionThe OK levels of older women with fragility fractures can be stratified into five distinct profiles. Understanding the heterogeneity of OK levels and its associated factors among this population offers valuable insights for healthcare professionals to formulate targeted interventions. This study makes a significant contribution to the advancement of secondary prevention strategies for osteoporosis.

Introduction

Osteoporosis, often referred to as a “silent epidemic,” is a critical global public health issue. The World Health Organization (WHO) describes it as a skeletal condition marked by reduced bone mass and microstructural deterioration, which results in weakened bones and an increased risk of fracture, particularly at the spine, hip, and wrist.1 These fractures, which can occur with minimal force, have the potential to cause substantial morbidity, diminish quality of life, and even result in early death.2 In Europe, the risk of hip fracture in individuals over 50 is comparable to the risk of stroke, with age-standardized incidence rates continuing to rise in many regions despite stabilization trends observed in Western countries.3 Hip fractures alone are associated with serious morbidity, reduced quality of life, disability, and increased mortality, with one-year mortality rates reaching 20–30% in older adults.4 The occurrence of fragility fractures is strikingly high, with about one-third of women and one-fifth of men suffering from them after the age of 50.5 As the population continues to age, the challenges posed by osteoporosis and related fractures are escalating, placing a significant strain on healthcare systems and resulting in considerable economic costs.6,7 Osteoporosis predominantly affects postmenopausal women. The hormonal shifts during menopause lead to a faster rate of bone loss, which makes postmenopausal women more susceptible to the condition.8

Osteoporosis is often overlooked, with insufficient screening, diagnosis, and treatment. It typically remains hidden until multiple fractures take place.9 Data reveal that in the United States, fewer than one in four patients advised to undergo osteoporosis screening actually complete the required evaluations.10 Despite the availability of effective treatments and preventive measures, many older women remain uninformed about their osteoporosis risk and the importance of adopting strategies to mitigate these risks. This knowledge gap can lead to significant delays in diagnosis and treatment, thereby increasing the likelihood of further fractures. Therefore, enhancing osteoporosis knowledge (OK) among older women who have experienced fragility fractures is essential not only for preventing subsequent fractures but also for enabling informed health decisions.11

China, with its large and aging population, is not immune to this challenge. A systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that the incidence of osteoporosis-related fractures among older Chinese individuals is alarmingly high, reaching 18.9%. This figure has seen a significant rise, climbing from 13.2% (2000–2010) to 22.7% (2012–2022). Notably, the prevalence of fragility fractures is greater in women compared to men (18.5% versus 14.3%) and tends to increase with advancing age.12 The impact of osteoporosis and associated fragility fractures is substantial and keeps expanding, underscoring the critical need for targeted interventions. However, to devise effective strategies, it is crucial to assess the current understanding of osteoporosis among older Chinese women who have experienced fragility fractures and identify the factors influencing this knowledge.

Although previous research has delved into osteoporosis awareness across diverse groups, a thorough grasp of the distinct subgroups of OK levels among older Chinese women with fragility fractures remains elusive. Our research team previously conducted a nationwide cross-sectional study specifically targeting older patients with upper limb fragility fractures, identifying key sociodemographic and clinical factors associated with adequate OK levels.13 Additionally, a qualitative study explored the deep-seated cultural and psychological barriers within Chinese families regarding secondary fracture prevention, revealing a disconnect between “knowing and doing.”14 However, the potential heterogeneity of knowledge structures within the broader population of older women with fragility fractures has not yet been investigated. Understanding this heterogeneity is critical because knowledge alone does not guarantee behavioral change. Identifying these heterogeneous subgroups would enable the design of interventions focused on behavior change rather than merely information provision. This distinction is essential for moving beyond generic education toward targeted strategies that address specific knowledge-action gaps, ultimately improving secondary fracture prevention outcomes. Most existing studies have primarily focused on categorizing osteoporosis knowledge through total or average scores, emphasizing the overall level of awareness and its related factors while often neglecting variations among different individuals. Latent profile analysis (LPA), a powerful person-centered statistical approach, is frequently employed to determine the number of subgroups within a specific sample.15 This approach surpasses traditional clustering techniques by employing more objective and stringent fit indices to determine the number of categories. Furthermore, LPA allows for the inclusion of demographic data and other covariates to construct a mixture regression model, which is well suited for examining intricate variable interactions.16

This study seeks to conduct a cross-sectional survey to investigate and categorize the levels of osteoporosis knowledge among older women with fragility fractures in China. Utilizing LPA, our goal is to segment this population based on their osteoporosis knowledge levels and to uncover the determinants that shape these levels. By identifying distinct knowledge profiles, this research aims to provide actionable insights for tailoring interventions to specific subgroup needs.

Understanding the heterogeneity of OK levels and their associated factors among older women with fragility fractures in China can provide valuable insights for healthcare professionals to develop targeted interventions. Consequently, this research plays a vital role in advancing secondary prevention strategies for osteoporosis, with the ultimate goal of improving the quality of life for older women who have sustained fragility fractures.

Methods

Study Setting and Participants

Considering geographical distribution and regional economy, 594 medical institutions across 31 Chinese provinces were chosen for data collection via convenience sampling. From September to December 2023, data were gathered on 9212 older women hospitalized due to fragility fractures. Inclusion criteria encompassed individuals aged ≥60 years, with a medical diagnosis of fragility fracture followed by surgical treatment, with intact cognitive function, and capable of independently completing the questionnaire. Exclusion criteria comprised high-energy fractures, pathological or periprosthetic fractures, illiteracy hindering communication and questionnaire completion, severe physical or mental illness, as well as cognitive impairment.

Data Collection and Ethical Considerations

An electronic questionnaire was meticulously crafted through a series of expert consultations. Data collection was carried out on the day before the participants’ discharge from the hospital. A rigorous three-tiered quality control protocol was put in place, overseen by the project’s general leader, the provincial research leader, and the hospital research leader. This approach was designed to guarantee that the gathered data were thorough, uniform, accurate, and reliable. To this end, a work deployment meeting, a mid-term review meeting, and a summary meeting were held prior to the survey. Our nurse researchers, who had received standardized training, approached potential participants and provided them with a detailed explanation of the study’s objectives and procedures. After obtaining written informed consent, eligible participants were invited to complete a series of questionnaires in a private and quiet setting. Participants were instructed to complete the questionnaires on-site and return them to the researchers for a thorough review. This review process was conducted to identify missing responses and rectify logical inconsistencies within the data.

Measures

The data collection instrument is a structured, self-reported questionnaire divided into two sections.

Part 1: Participants’ demographic characteristics and history of osteoporosis were examined using a general information questionnaire. Variables potentially associated with osteoporosis knowledge were identified from clinical practice and literature, and organized into 3 categories: (1) sociodemographic characteristics, including age group, marital status, Body Mass Index (BMI), education level, number of children, average annual household income, current smoking status, current drinking status, and filial care; (2) clinical characteristics, including comorbidities (e.g, hypertension, diabetes, eye disease, rheumatic disease, asthma, anemia, hyperlipidemia, etc), surgical history, number of operations, family history, fracture history, fracture site, admissions for falls, postoperative self-care ability, diagnosis of severe osteoporosis, anti-osteoporosis therapy, postoperative complications (e.g, postoperative pneumonia, postoperative urinary tract infection, post incision infection, etc), use of walking aids, use of calcium or vitamin D supplements, use of anti-osteoporosis medication (AOM), and necessity of fall prevention; (3) health education status in-hospital of participants [contents (e.g, fracture, osteoporosis, medicine, diet, exercise, complication, rehabilitation, psychology, and fall prevention) and form (e.g, oral explanation, video, phone, manual, and WeChat)].

Part 2: The Osteoporosis Knowledge Assessment Tool (OKAT) was utilized to evaluate the participants’ understanding of osteoporosis. This questionnaire, initially developed by Winzenberg et al in 2003 for Australian women, was utilized.17 The OKAT is widely acknowledged for its validity and reliability, establishing itself as a strong and accurate tool for evaluating knowledge of osteoporosis. Additionally, the OKAT has been successfully translated into Chinese.18 It demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha reliability of 0.81 and a Pearson’s test-retest reliability coefficient of r = 0.92. It consists of 20 items with four dimensions: knowledge regarding osteoporosis (Q1, 2, 8, 9, 11), awareness associated with osteoporosis (Q3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 12, 18), understanding of preventive strategies for osteoporosis (Q10, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17), and treatment availability (Q19, 20).19 In this study, each multiple-choice question offered three options: true, false, and “I don’t know.” Questions left unanswered or marked as “I don’t know” were considered incorrect. A binary scoring method was used, awarding 1 point for correct answers and 0 points for incorrect ones. The OKAT showed strong internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s α value of 0.822.

Statistical Analysis

This study conducted an LPA to classify the latent profiles of osteoporosis knowledge of older women with fragility fractures. This method elucidates the relationships between external continuous variables via latent categorical variables and ensures local independence among exogenous variables. In the LPA model, the subscores corresponding to the four dimensions of the OKAT were used as continuous indicator variables. LPA was performed on the four dimensions of the OKAT using Mplus 8.3 software. The optimal number of classes was determined using several criteria, including the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC), adjusted BIC (aBIC), the P-value of the Lo–Mendell–Rubin test (P-LMR), the P-value of the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (P-BLRT), entropy, and the proportion of patients in each group relative to the total number of patients. Lower values of AIC,20 BIC,21 and aBIC22 indicate a better model fit. A P-LMR23 or P-BLRT value less than 0.05 suggests that the model with k classes was preferable to the model with k-1 classes. The recommended entropy value, which reflects model classification accuracy, was greater than 0.8,24 indicating that more than 80% of the samples were accurately grouped.

Using R software (version 4.3.3), we performed multinomial logistic regression analysis with LPA categories as the dependent variable and sociodemographic characteristics of older women with fragility fractures and OK-related information that showed statistical significance in univariable analysis as independent variables. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. For measures that followed a normal distribution, we reported the mean and standard deviation (SD). For those that did not follow a normal distribution, we used the median and interquartile range. Categorical data were presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%).

Results

Comorbidities were reported by 6899 individuals (74.89%), with hypertension (4538, 49.26%) and diabetes (1860, 20.19%) being the most prevalent conditions observed. Previous fractures were documented in a total of 1661 patients (18.03%), while falls accounted for hospitalization in the majority of cases (6346 patients, 68.89%). Sociodemographic Characteristics

Among individual-level factors, middle and high school education significantly increased the odds of Category 3 (OR = 1.33) and Category 4 (OR = 1.36). Higher education was significantly associated with Category 3 only (OR = 2.18). Notably, widowed marital status was significantly associated with Category 5 (OR = 2.00), with no significant associations observed for Categories 2–4. BMI ≥24.0 kg/m2 was also a significant predictor of Category 5 (OR = 1.60). Among social support sources, filial care was significantly associated with Category 3 (OR = 1.63) and Category 4 (OR = 2.03).

Clinical Characteristics

Postoperative self-care ability showed a significant association with Category 5. Compared with patients who were completely independent, those requiring some assistance (OR = 1.84), requiring a lot of help (OR = 1.84), and being completely dependent (OR = 2.76) all showed significantly higher odds of Category 5. Anti-osteoporosis therapy was significantly associated with Category 3 (OR = 1.37) and Category 4 (OR = 1.39). Use of calcium or vitamin D supplements was strongly and consistently associated with all higher-knowledge categories (Category 2: OR = 1.92; Category 3: OR = 2.18; Category 4: OR = 1.81; Category 5: OR = 1.72; all P < 0.01). Perceived necessity of fall prevention showed the strongest effects across all clinical variables, substantially increasing the odds of all higher-knowledge categories (Category 2: OR = 4.72; Category 3: OR = 3.73; Category 4: OR = 3.15; Category 5: OR = 10.23).

Health Education Status in–Hospital

The content and delivery methods of health education exhibited divergent and clinically meaningful patterns. Receiving health education showed significant inverse associations with Category 3 (OR = 0.14) and Category 5 (OR = 0.15). Among targeted content areas, osteoporosis education demonstrated the broadest significant associations (Category 3: OR = 1.75; Category 4: OR = 1.41; Category 5: OR = 1.72). Fracture education was significantly associated with Category 3 (OR = 1.68) and Category 4 (OR = 1.50). Fall prevention education showed a significant positive association for Category 3 only (OR = 1.87). Rehabilitation education was significantly associated with Category 3 (OR = 1.54) and Category 5 (OR = 1.77). Regarding delivery methods, manual-based education was significantly associated with Category 3 (OR = 1.74) and Category 4 (OR = 1.36). WeChat-based education exhibited a significant effect only in Category 3 (OR = 1.39).

Discussion

The OK Level Among Older Women with Fragility Fractures Needs to Be Further Improved

This study found that older women with fragility fractures had a median score for osteoporosis knowledge [11 (7, 13)]. While measurement scales varied across studies—previously reported scores include (10.71 ± 4.21) among Saudi women25 and 50.9–51.3 out of 100 among Jordanian women26—all indicate suboptimal knowledge levels. These consistently modest scores suggest a significant osteoporosis knowledge gap among older women, particularly those with fragility fractures. This gap may be attributed to physical and cognitive challenges associated with aging, as well as potential disparities in healthcare and educational resources. These findings suggest that healthcare providers should prioritize osteoporosis education for this population, potentially through routine healthcare appointments, targeted educational materials, and technology-based outreach.

Five Distinct OK Profiles Identified Through LPA

Latent profile analysis revealed five OK subgroups: Low OK (9.9%), Medium-Low OK (11.1%), High OK (50.9%), Medium OK (18.8%), and High awareness and low management (9.3%). The predominance of the High OK group is encouraging and suggests educational initiatives have reached many individuals. However, the Low OK and High awareness and low management groups (totaling 19.2%) represent particularly vulnerable subpopulations lacking essential prevention and management knowledge. The Medium-Low and Medium OK groups (totaling 29.9%) demonstrate intermediate understanding but may lack comprehensive knowledge or practical self-management skills. This heterogeneity underscores the need for tailored educational approaches that address specific knowledge deficits rather than one-size-fits-all interventions.

The identification of the “High awareness and low management” profile (Category 5, 9.3%) represents a novel and clinically significant finding. Unlike the Low OK group, these women have adequate disease awareness. They can identify osteoporosis as a fracture risk factor and recognize the role of menopause. However, they exhibit critical deficits in actionable knowledge. This pattern aligns with the knowledge–behavior gap framework in health psychology, where information acquisition does not automatically lead to behavioral change.27 Social Cognitive Theory posits that behavioral execution requires self-efficacy, which may be undermined by the postoperative dependency and widowed status that predicted Category 5 membership.28 Without confidence in one’s ability to perform preventive behaviors, knowledge remains inert. Our prior qualitative work identified Confucian family dynamics as a barrier: adult children may assume decision-making responsibility for older parents, inadvertently disempowering them from active self-management despite their disease awareness.14 The significant association of filial care with Categories 3–5 indicates that supportive family involvement promotes knowledge acquisition across higher-knowledge profiles. However, the specific prediction of Category 5 membership by widowed status and postoperative dependency suggests that, among well-informed individuals, the absence of spousal support and greater functional limitations may independently compromise behavioral execution, contributing to the knowledge–action gap. From a clinical outcomes perspective, this subgroup is at a disproportionately high risk of refracture. They have already suffered one fragility fracture and are aware of their condition, yet they fail to engage in preventive behaviors. The 9.3% prevalence of this profile in our sample, when applied to China’s aging population, translates to a substantial number of women who are knowledgeably primed but behaviorally unprotected, representing a critical target for secondary prevention programs. Intervention implications for this subgroup must fundamentally differ from conventional education. Instead of providing information, strategies should incorporate behavioral activation techniques.

Factors Associated with Five OK Profiles

Multinomial logistic regression identified several factors associated with different levels of osteoporosis knowledge and awareness. To move beyond fragmented, variable-by-variable reporting, we synthesize these findings within an integrated framework encompassing three interconnected domains—sociodemographic determinants, clinical and functional determinants, and educational and behavioral determinants—and explicitly identify key modifiable drivers within each.

Sociodemographic Determinants

Among individual-level factors, middle and high school education emerged as a significant predictor, significantly increasing the odds of belonging to Categories 3 and 4, which highlights the role of health literacy. College education and beyond showed a stronger but more selective effect, specifically for Category 3. The study revealed that individuals with higher education and higher socio–economic status achieved significantly higher scores in both men and women (P < 0·05).29 Notably, widowed marital status was associated with Category 5, possibly reflecting increased autonomy in health management or greater exposure to health information in social networks following the loss of a spouse. BMI ≥24.0 kg/m2 was specifically associated with Category 5. This finding suggests that overweight/obesity, rather than healthy weight per se, may be linked to a specific knowledge pattern characterized by high awareness but low management understanding. This is consistent with the notion that both obesity and low weight can adversely affect bone health.30 Therefore, promoting a healthy weight as part of osteoporosis management is essential. Clinicians should offer guidance on nutrition and physical activity to help patients maintain a healthy BMI for improved health outcomes. Among social support sources, filial care was specifically increasing the odds of Categories 3 and 4. This implies that interventions targeting these factors could potentially improve osteoporosis knowledge and management. Clinicians can encourage patients to involve family members in their care and provide resources for social support networks. A key modifiable driver at this level is the implementation of family-centered strategies that leverage filial care networks to improve osteoporosis knowledge among socially isolated subgroups such as widowed patients.

Clinical and Functional Determinants

Functional status played a significant role. Postoperative self-care ability increased the odds of Category 5. Specifically, compared with patients who were completely independent, those requiring some assistance, a lot of help, or complete dependence all showed significantly higher odds of belonging to Category 5. This could be due to increased interaction with healthcare providers and the need for more education to manage their condition independently. The association between self-care ability and OK levels suggests that postoperative care should include education on self-management. This can help patients regain independence and confidence in managing their condition. Active treatment engagement was consistently favorable. Receipt of anti-osteoporosis therapy was linked to higher OK levels, specifically increasing the odds of Category 3 and Category 4. Calcium and/or vitamin D supplementation was strongly and consistently associated with all higher-knowledge categories. This underscores the importance of not only nutritional support but also pharmacological intervention in the comprehensive management of osteoporosis.

Educational and Behavioral Determinants

The specificity of educational content and delivery methods constituted the behavioral domain. Health education showed significant inverse associations with Categories 3 and 5, possibly suggesting that broad, non-specific health education may be insufficient—or even counterproductive—for women with higher knowledge levels or those exhibiting awareness-management discrepancies. In contrast, targeted educational content exhibited divergent associations. Osteoporosis education demonstrated the broadest significant associations across Categories 3, 4, and 5. Fracture education showed significant positive associations with Categories 3 and 4, whereas rehabilitation education was positively associated with Categories 3 and 5. Notably, although the perceived necessity of fall prevention exhibited the strongest effects across all clinical and behavioral variables—substantially increasing the odds of all higher-knowledge categories—fall prevention education itself showed a significant positive association only for Category 3. This disconnect highlights a critical gap: patients across multiple knowledge profiles recognize the importance of fall prevention, yet targeted education in this domain appears to reach only the highest-knowledge group. Regarding delivery methods, manual-based education showed significant associations with Categories 3 and 4, whereas WeChat-based education exhibited a significant effect only for Category 3. These findings suggest that digital and remote modalities may require enhanced content quality or patient engagement strategies to extend their reach beyond the highest-knowledge group. In conclusion, our findings underscore the critical importance of targeted education on fractures, osteoporosis, rehabilitation, and fall prevention management for improving knowledge among older women with fragility fractures. The results support the development of tailored educational programs that utilize effective delivery methods, while avoiding generic, non-specific health education that may not be effective for this population.

Limitations

Our study is subject to several limitations. First, the use of convenience sampling may introduce selection bias. Second, the cross-sectional study design precludes making causal inference: the observed associations between knowledge profiles and factors such as treatment engagement or health education exposure cannot establish temporal directionality, and data collection at discharge captures a transient post-education window that may not reflect long-term knowledge states. Third, self-reported data are susceptible to social desirability bias, recall bias, and electronic questionnaires may be limited by participants’ digital literacy. Fourth, we did not perform multilevel analysis to account for clustering effects; this may inflate the Type I error rate and should be addressed in future research using hierarchical models.

Conclusions

Using latent profile analysis (LPA), this nationwide cross-sectional study explored the subgroup characteristics of OK levels among older women with fragility fractures and associated factors. Five potential profiles were identified: Low OK group (9.9%), Medium-Low OK group (11.1%), High OK group (50.9%), Medium OK group (18.8%), and High awareness and low management group (9.3%). Key factors influencing the classification of OK levels included marital status, BMI, education level, filial care, postoperative self-care ability, receipt of anti-osteoporosis therapy, use of calcium or vitamin D supplements, perceived necessity of fall prevention, receipt of health education, content of health education, and methods of health education delivery. By recognizing the distinct profiles of OK levels and the associated factors, healthcare providers and policymakers can develop more effective, targeted interventions to improve osteoporosis education and management. This, in turn, can lead to better self-care practices, reduced fracture risks, and ultimately, improved quality of life for this vulnerable population.
