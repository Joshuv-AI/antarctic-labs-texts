When Does Valve Durability Matter?
Authors: Sung-Han Yoon, Perry Wengrofsky, Ryan Kaple
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: balloon expandable valve, BVF, TAVI

In this issue of JACC: Asia, Ikuta et al1 report 10-year outcomes from the OCEAN-TAVI (Optimized transCathEter vAlvlar INtervention) registry of 316 patients (mean age 83.8 years, Society of Thoracic Surgeons [STS] score 7.5%), with a 10-year outcome rate including survival of 13.8% as well as cumulative incidence of structural valve deterioration of 3.0% and bioprosthetic valve failure (BVF) of 10.8%, derived using the Fine-Gray method to account for the competing risk of death. Beyond the durability analysis, the authors also performed a multivariable analysis of all-cause mortality and identified elevated STS score, frailty, reduced renal function, and low serum albumin as independent predictors. Taken together, these 2 analyses converge on a single clarifying message: whether valve durability matters to a given patient's outcome is not a fixed property of the device, it depends on who is being treated. In this elderly, comorbidity-laden cohort, long-term outcomes were governed far more by who the patient was than by what happened to the valve.

The Mortality Model Tells the Real Story

The independent predictors of mortality identified in the present study, including STS score, frailty, renal dysfunction, and hypoalbuminemia, are notable precisely because none of them are valve-related. They are, instead, a composite portrait of physiological reserve and multimorbidity. This is an important and somewhat underappreciated finding. In a field that has devoted enormous energy to comparing valve platforms, leaflet designs, and durability endpoints, this study is a useful reminder that for a patient actually sitting in front of the heart team who is 83.8 years of age, with STS 7.5%, and often frail and hypoalbuminemic, the dominant determinant of survival is not valve-related outcomes such as paravalvular leakage, but the burden of comorbidity and physiological frailty that the patient brings to the procedure. The valve, in this population, was essentially never the limiting factor.

Why the Low BVF Rate Is a Consequence of Limited Exposure Time, Not of Valve Performance

This context is essential to interpreting the durability data correctly, and it is where this editorial urges some caution. The 10-year BVF rate of 10.8%, which numerically low and by conventional benchmarks “acceptable,” should not be read in isolation as a reassuring statement about valve performance. It is, in large part, a mechanical consequence of the mortality findings just described. It is widely known that the bioprosthetic aortic valve failure occurs 8 to 10 years after implantation. With a 10-year survival of only 13.2%, driven predominantly by the nonvalve-related factors identified in the multivariable model, the majority of implanted valves in this cohort were never given the time for the cumulative “valve-years” of mechanical and hemodynamic loading to manifest deterioration in the first place. Structural valve deterioration and BVF are inherently exposure-dependent phenomena; a valve cannot fail if the patient does not survive long enough to load it.

The authors' use of the Fine-Gray method is the correct way to estimate the true cumulative incidence of BVF within this cohort, and it appropriately avoids the overestimation bias that a naive Kaplan-Meier approach would have introduced by treating competing deaths as noninformative censoring.2 But, competing-risk adjustment corrects the statistical estimate; it does not, and cannot, correct for the fact that the underlying population was, on average, not going to live long enough to fully test the valve's durability. The 10.8% figure is therefore an accurate description of what happened, not a projection of what would happen in a younger population that survived longer. The mortality analysis in this same paper is precisely what explains why so few patients survived long enough for that question to be answered.

Consistency With, and Limits of, the Comparative Literature

This interpretation is broadly consistent with data from lower-risk, longer-surviving trial populations. The NOTION (Nortic Aortic Valve Intervention) trial (mean age 79.2 years, STS <4% in ∼80%) reported a similar 10-year BVF of 9.7% using comparable competing-risk methodology,3 although it used a first-generation self-expanding platform, limiting direct comparison. More instructively, the PARTNER 3 (Placement of Aortic Transcatheter Valves) low-risk trial reported a lower competing-risk-adjusted BVF of 6.9% at 7 years,4 and the Evolut Low Risk trial that enrolled a similarly young, low-risk population with much longer expected survival has already reported a significantly higher reintervention rate with transcatheter aortic valve replacement than surgery by 7 years (9.8% vs 6.0%; P = 0.02), driven predominantly by aortic regurgitation.5 These signals, emerging at shorter follow-up in populations expected to live considerably longer, are consistent with the exposure-time argument previously mentioned: as competing mortality recedes, valve durability rather than patient frailty becomes more clinically relevant, and the reassuring low BVF rates seen in elderly, high-competing-mortality cohorts should not be expected to persist unchanged.

Implications for Practice

For the elderly, high-comorbidity population actually represented in this registry, these findings are reassuring in the sense that matters most clinically: valve durability is very unlikely to be the limiting factor in a patient's remaining lifespan, and the mortality model helps identify which patients (frail, hypoalbuminemic, renally impaired) are least likely to live long enough for durability to become clinically relevant information of direct value for heart team risk stratification and shared decision-making. What this study does not, and cannot, establish is durability in the fundamentally different population now increasingly receiving transcatheter aortic valve replacement: younger, lower-risk patients with a realistic expectation of 15 to 20 years of survival, for whom the valve, not the patient's comorbidity burden, will ultimately determine the durability question. The 2 populations should not be conflated, and this study's acceptably low BVF rate should be cited as evidence about the former, not the latter.
