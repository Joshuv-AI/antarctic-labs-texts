Soft skills between professional ideals and organizational constraints: healthcare professionals’ perspectives
Authors: Lior Naamati-Schneider
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealthcare professionals operate in complex and rapidly changing environments, where quality of care depends not only on clinical expertise but also on soft skills such as empathy, communication, teamwork, emotional regulation, and adaptive decision-making. Yet soft skills are often treated as professional ideals, and less is known about how healthcare professionals understand and enact them under routine organizational constraints. This qualitative study examined how professionals define, interpret, and experience soft skills in everyday work within a complex and rapidly changing healthcare context. By moving beyond normative accounts of soft skills, the study seeks to clarify how these competencies are experienced as context-dependent practices embedded within organizational realities.MethodsBased on 37 in-depth semi-structured online interviews conducted between 2023 and 2024 with healthcare professionals working in large public hospitals and health maintenance organizations, including physicians, nurses, and healthcare providers. Data were analyzed inductively using thematic analysis.ResultsFive interrelated themes were identified: (1) Soft Skills as a Shared Professional Ideal (2) Time and Workload Pressure (3) Emotional Strain and the Gradual Erosion of Soft Skills (4) Soft Skills as Developable Capacities Within Supportive Organizational Environments; and (5) Navigating Change and Digital Transformation in a VUCA Healthcare Environment. The findings reveal a persistent gap between the normative framing of soft skills and their conditional enactment in practice, shaped by workload intensity, emotional strain, and systemic pressures.ConclusionOverall, soft skills emerged as essential yet fragile professional resources whose enactment is contingent on time, emotional capacity, and organizational support. The study contributes theoretically by conceptualizing soft skills not as stable individual attributes, but as situated professional practices mediated by organizational conditions and systemic change. The findings suggest that strengthening soft skills requires closer alignment between educational preparation and workplace realities through longitudinal development, structured practice opportunities, and organizational cultures that recognize and protect relational work as a core component of healthcare practice.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Soft skills, Healthcare professionals, Organizational constraints, VUCA, Organizational resilience, Digital transformation

Introduction

Over recent decades, healthcare systems worldwide have faced growing complexity driven by demographic, technological, economic, and political forces [1–2]. Population aging, increased life expectancy, and the rising prevalence of chronic conditions have intensified demand for healthcare services, placing organizations and professionals in environments characterized by continuous change and instability that require ongoing adaptation in everyday practice.

Healthcare professionals working under these conditions are exposed to heavy workloads, sustained pressure, and emotionally demanding situations involving uncertainty, ethical tension, and competing priorities. Responding to such challenges requires not only clinical expertise but also interpersonal, emotional, and cognitive capabilities that support professional functioning in complex organizational settings [3–4]. Research on healthcare organizations has increasingly emphasized the importance of resilience, adaptability, and the capacity to function under conditions of ongoing change [5–7]. Alongside structural and managerial responses, growing attention has been directed toward the human capacities through which professionals collaborate, cope with pressure, and respond to emotional and ethical demands in everyday work.

Within this context, soft skills, including communication, empathy, teamwork, emotional regulation, and adaptive decision making, are widely acknowledged as essential components of effective healthcare practice [8–10]. Although frequently invoked in professional discourse, policy documents, and educational frameworks, soft skills are often discussed at a normative or conceptual level, with limited empirical attention to how they are understood and enacted in everyday organizational conditions. Previous research has also pointed to gaps in competency-based education and to the challenges of sustaining these skills under conditions of workload, time pressure, and emotional strain [11–14].

Despite their recognized importance, less is known about how healthcare professionals themselves understand soft skills in the context of routine organizational constraints, and how these competencies are negotiated in everyday practice within complex and unstable work environments. Against this background, the present qualitative study examines how healthcare professionals define, interpret, and experience soft skills in their everyday work. The study focuses on professionals’ perspectives on these competencies within the context of organizational conditions, workload pressures, multidisciplinary collaboration, and emotional and ethical challenges. Conceptually, the study approaches soft skills as situated professional practices embedded within organizational and systemic conditions, rather than as stable individual traits.

This study, therefore, seeks to examine how healthcare professionals understand and experience soft skills in their everyday work within complex organizational environments. The study was guided by the following research question: How do healthcare professionals define and enact soft skills in everyday practice under routine organizational constraints? The objective of the study is to explore how soft skills are understood, negotiated, and experienced within daily clinical work, and how their enactment is shaped by organizational conditions. By doing so, it seeks to clarify the gap between normative expectations regarding soft skills and their conditional enactment in contemporary healthcare environments.

Literature review

The VUCA framework as a conceptual description of contemporary work environments

The concept of VUCA was originally developed in a military context to describe conditions of volatility, uncertainty, complexity, and ambiguity that emerged after the Cold War [15], and later gained broader prominence in organizational and policy research [16]. Over time, the framework has been widely adopted as a lens for understanding contemporary work environments characterized by rapid change, systemic disruption, and limited predictability [17–21]. Within this framework, volatility refers to the speed and intensity of change, uncertainty to limited predictability of outcomes, complexity to the interaction of multiple interdependent factors, and ambiguity to situations lacking clear precedents or interpretive frameworks [22]. These conditions increasingly characterize healthcare systems, where professionals must make decisions and deliver care amid evolving information, technological change, and shifting organizational demands.

Recent global developments further illustrate the widespread nature of such conditions, as economic instability, geopolitical tensions, and major events such as the COVID-19 pandemic have disrupted healthcare systems, labor markets, and organizational routines across sectors [23–25]. These conditions have intensified pressures for organizational adaptation, including the expansion of remote work and digitally mediated care models [26]. While institutional responses have often lagged behind the pace of change [27], VUCA environments have also been associated with innovation and resilience-building processes within organizations [24, 28]. In the present study, the VUCA concept is used as a contextual and conceptual background for understanding contemporary healthcare environments, positioning soft skills within work settings characterized by ongoing volatility, uncertainty, complexity, and ambiguity. It is not used as a predefined analytic framework, and did not guide the interview design, coding process, or theme development, but served only as an interpretive lens for situating the findings within broader organizational conditions.

Organizational conditions and resilience in healthcare settings

In healthcare systems operating under conditions of ongoing change and uncertainty, organizational resilience has emerged as a central concept for understanding how institutions and professionals cope with disruption [7]. Organizational resilience refers to the capacity to adapt to environmental pressures, maintain functional stability, and respond constructively to challenges over time [6]. In healthcare contexts, resilience shapes the everyday work environment in which professionals operate rather than representing only an abstract organizational outcome [29].

Previous research conceptualizes resilience as a multidimensional process involving cognitive, behavioral, and contextual components that support organizational sustainability and effectiveness [29, 30]. Factors such as internal coordination, professional networks, and inter-organizational collaboration contribute to adaptive capacity [31–32], while the quality of relationships between organizations and employees influences responses to uncertainty and change [33]. The Employee-Organization Relationship framework highlights the role of organizational support, communication, and recognition in shaping employees’ ability to cope with demanding conditions, and supportive organizational climates have been shown to strengthen professionals’ capacity to manage uncertainty and sustain engagement during periods of disruption [34–36]. Contemporary perspectives therefore describe resilience as a dynamic and ongoing process that supports organizational agility rather than as a response to isolated crises [37–38]. Empirical studies in healthcare settings further demonstrate the relevance of these conditions for professional experience. For example, Gröschke et al. [39] found that hospital environments characterized by higher organizational resilience during the COVID-19 pandemic were associated with more adaptive behaviors, improved team functioning, and better coping among healthcare staff. These findings suggest a reciprocal relationship in which organizational conditions shape professional functioning, while professional practices also influence the organizational environment. This perspective informs the present study by framing soft skills as context-sensitive resources shaped by institutional conditions.

Soft skills as situated professional practices

Soft skills are widely recognized as essential components of professional functioning in healthcare, including communication, teamwork, emotional regulation, empathy, and adaptive decision-making [40–43]. These competencies are considered critical for effective collaboration, patient-centered care, and responsible professional practice, and are frequently emphasized in educational frameworks, policy documents, and professional guidelines [9, 44]. Despite their widespread use, soft skills do not rest on a single unified definition. The literature on twenty-first century skills highlights a lack of consensus regarding how these competencies should be defined and measured, and various frameworks, including life skills, twenty-first century skills, and classifications proposed by the National Research Council and the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), distinguish between cognitive, intrapersonal, and interpersonal domains [45, 46].

Several conceptual models have been proposed to organize soft skills, including life skills [47], twenty-first century skills [48], and the key competencies identified by the OECD [49]. In the present study, soft skills are approached through the framework developed by the Mediating Soft Skills at Higher Education Institutions (ModEs) European Project, which conceptualizes these competencies across personal, social, and methodological dimensions [50]. This erspective allows soft skills to be examined as multidimensional professional capacities embedded within organizational and systemic contexts rather than as isolated individual traits.

The essential role of soft skills in healthcare settings

Within healthcare settings, competencies such as effective communication, teamwork, time management, and leadership are central to the social and organizational functioning of medical teams, as demonstrated by Bos-van den Hoek et al. [51]. While soft skills are recognized as important across occupational fields [52], their relevance is especially pronounced in clinical and healthcare contexts, where they are regarded as fundamental rather than supplementary competencies. In these settings, soft skills support teamwork, professional collaboration, and effective functioning under conditions of pressure and uncertainty [53].

Beyond their organizational value, soft skills play a critical role in clinical encounters and are associated with improved doctor-patient relationships, greater empathic engagement, and higher levels of patient satisfaction and trust [54]. In nursing practice, where professionals maintain continuous contact with patients, communication, emotional regulation, and teamwork directly influence the ability to provide safe and patient-centered care. Empirical evidence links these competencies to improved clinical decision-making, treatment effectiveness, reduced patient anxiety, and greater adherence to recommended care [55, 56]. Communication skills are therefore widely regarded as essential for everyday healthcare practice, shaping interactions with patients, families, and professional teams [57].

Soft skills are also closely related to critical thinking and problem-solving in complex clinical situations. Healthcare professionals frequently face uncertain and multifaceted conditions that require not only technical knowledge but also empathy, flexibility, active listening, and effective communication [58]. These competencies support emotional intelligence and collaborative decision-making, contributing to patient-centered care, constructive teamwork, and the ability to manage demanding clinical environments. Emotional regulation, in particular, enables professionals to respond sensitively to patients while maintaining professional judgment, and has been associated with improved care quality and patient outcomes [56, 59].

Although soft skills are widely emphasized in healthcare education, policy, and professional guidelines, the literature provides limited empirical insight into how these competencies are understood and enacted in everyday professional practice under routine organizational conditions. This gap highlights the need for research that examines healthcare professionals’ own perspectives on soft skills and explores how these competencies are sustained, negotiated, or constrained within specific organizational environments.

This study

Taken together, the literature highlights the importance of soft skills in healthcare, as well as the role of organizational conditions, resilience, and ongoing change in shaping professional functioning. However, there is limited empirical understanding of how these competencies are understood and enacted in everyday practice under routine organizational constraints. This qualitative study, therefore, examines how healthcare professionals perceive and experience soft skills in their everyday work, focusing on the relationship between their recognized importance and their reported ability to enact them in practice. The study explores how soft skills are understood, described, and positioned within daily professional routines and interactions in healthcare settings. The study was guided by the following research question: How do healthcare professionals define and enact soft skills in everyday practice under routine organizational conditions?

By foregrounding professionals’ perspectives, the study provides an empirically grounded understanding of soft skills as lived professional practices, allowing patterns and tensions between awareness and enactment to emerge from the data.

Method

This study employed a qualitative exploratory design using inductive thematic analysis to examine how healthcare professionals understand and experience soft skills within everyday organizational contexts. The exploratory approach was selected to enable in-depth examination of participants’ perspectives in complex healthcare settings where empirical understanding remains limited.

Participants

Data were collected between 2023 and 2024 through in-depth interviews with 37 healthcare professionals working in large public hospitals and health maintenance organizations (HMOs). The sample included physicians and nurses working across hospital and community settings, including nurses employed in outpatient clinics, primary care services, and maternal and child health clinics, representing a range of professional roles involved in both direct patient care and organizational decision-making. The healthcare settings were characterized by complex organizational structures, high service demand, and intensive clinical activity, contexts in which soft skills are routinely required in everyday practice. Including participants from varied professional roles and organizational settings allowed exploration of shared experiences of soft skills across different healthcare contexts, rather than focusing on formal comparison between professional groups. Participants were recruited through snowball sampling, a strategy chosen to facilitate access to healthcare professionals across diverse roles and organizational settings, where direct recruitment may be limited by workload constraints and institutional gatekeeping. Snowball sampling was considered appropriate for this study because participants were required to have direct experience in demanding clinical and organizational environments, making formal recruitment through institutions difficult.

To reduce the risk of network-related selection bias, recruitment was initiated through multiple entry points and continued across different professional roles and settings, including hospital departments, community clinics, and maternal and child health services. Attention was given to including variation in profession, workplace, and level of experience in order to capture a broad range of perspectives on soft skills in everyday practice.

Recruitment began with email invitations and continued iteratively until thematic saturation was achieved, defined as the point at which no new substantive insights emerged [60, 61]. Saturation was assessed throughout the analytic process by comparing newly collected interviews with previously coded data. As data collection progressed, recurring patterns became stable, and later interviews mainly elaborated existing themes rather than generating new ones.

Because the study aimed to capture perspectives from professionals working in different healthcare roles and settings, recruitment continued until sufficient variation in participants’ backgrounds and work environments was achieved. This resulted in a relatively large qualitative sample, which was considered necessary in order to ensure that the range of experiences relevant to the study aims was adequately represented.

Data collection and ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with established ethical standards for qualitative research involving human participants. Ethical approval was obtained from the ethics committee of the author’s academic institution prior to participant recruitment (number 299). Healthcare professionals who met the inclusion criteria were invited to participate via email. Prior to participation, all participants received written information outlining the aims of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality safeguards, and their right to withdraw at any point without penalty. Written informed consent was obtained before data collection commenced.

All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim with participants’ consent. Transcription was carried out by the research team, and transcripts were reviewed for accuracy before analysis. All transcripts were de-identified at the time of transcription, and any names or identifying details referring to individuals or institutions were removed. To ensure confidentiality, identifying details were removed from transcripts and all data were anonymized prior to analysis. In the presentation of the findings, pseudonyms were used to protect participants’ identities. Audio files and transcripts were stored on a password-protected computer accessible only to the author, and all data were handled in accordance with institutional guidelines for research involving human participants. Throughout the research process, care was taken to ensure that neither individuals nor institutions could be identified in any publication or presentation of the findings, in line with ethical guidelines for qualitative research [62].

Interview process and thematic focus

Between 2023 and 2024, trained student researchers, under the author’s supervision, conducted in-depth semi-structured interviews with healthcare professionals. Prior to data collection, the interviewers received training that included familiarization with the study aims, the interview guide, and principles of qualitative interviewing. A common semi-structured interview guide was used across all interviews to ensure consistency in the topics covered. The guide was reviewed and discussed with the interviewers before data collection began, and interviews were conducted under the author’s supervision, with ongoing consultation throughout the data collection process. Each interview lasted approximately 45 min. A semi-structured interview guide provided a common framework while allowing participants to elaborate freely on their experiences.

The interviews focused on how participants understand and describe soft skills in their everyday professional practice, and how they relate these skills to interactions with patients, colleagues, and the healthcare system more broadly. Topics included perceptions of empathy, communication, and emotional regulation, as well as reflections on everyday work situations in which these skills were enacted, constrained, or challenged. Participants were encouraged to draw on concrete examples from their daily work, enabling an in-depth exploration of lived experiences and meanings attached to soft skills, and supporting inductive identification of recurring patterns and tensions in the data.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis. Interviews were conducted by trained student researchers under the supervision of the author, while data analysis was conducted by the author. All interview transcripts were read repeatedly to ensure familiarity with the data. Initial codes were generated inductively, grounded in participants’ language and narratives, focusing on how soft skills were described and enacted in everyday practice. Coding remained open and iterative, allowing patterns to emerge from the data rather than being imposed in advance. The analysis was not structured according to predefined theoretical categories. Conceptual frameworks discussed in the literature, including the VUCA framework, were used as sensitizing concepts during the interpretive stage of analysis, allowing the findings to be understood in relation to contemporary organizational conditions rather than guiding the coding process itself. Emerging codes and theme boundaries were discussed in analytic consultations with academic colleagues familiar with qualitative research, and revisions were made where alternative interpretations were identified. These consultations included independent review of a subset of transcripts and preliminary coding by an academic colleague, followed by structured discussions comparing interpretations. Divergent readings were examined by returning to the original data, and theme definitions were refined to better reflect participants’ accounts. In several cases, this process led to the reorganization of theme boundaries and clarification of distinctions between overlapping categories. Themes were refined through constant comparison across interviews to ensure analytic coherence and grounding in participants’ accounts.

To enhance methodological rigour, analytic decisions were documented throughout the coding process, and attention was given to identifying recurring patterns as well as deviant or contradictory cases. The inclusion of professionals from diverse roles and settings further strengthened the credibility and transferability of the findings. This iterative and consultative analytic process supported the trustworthiness of the findings.

In the presentation of the findings, pseudonyms were used to protect participants’ identities. Professional roles were indicated in quotations to provide contextual information about participants’ perspectives, and not for the purpose of formal comparison between professional groups as the analysis focused on shared patterns across roles and settings. The analysis focused on identifying shared patterns across participants rather than examining systematic differences between professional roles or organizational settings, as the aim of the study was to explore common experiences of soft skills in everyday healthcare practice.

Reflexivity

The author’s professional familiarity with healthcare organizational contexts informed sensitivity to participants’ accounts, while requiring ongoing critical reflection regarding potential assumptions during interpretation. Reflexive notes were maintained throughout coding and theme development to document analytic decisions and support transparency in the analytic process, including reflection on how prior professional knowledge might shape theme interpretation.

Findings

The findings are organized around five interrelated themes, each further elaborated through a set of sub-themes that reflect recurring patterns and points of variation in participants’ accounts. Together, these themes capture how healthcare professionals understand, value, and experience soft skills in everyday practice under organizational, emotional, and systemic constraints. While interrelated, each theme captures a distinct dimension: professional ideals (Theme 1), situational constraints (Theme 2), cumulative emotional processes (Theme 3), developmental trajectories (Theme 4), and systemic transformation (Theme 5). The five themes are titled: (1) Soft Skills as a Shared Professional Ideal; (2) Time and Workload Pressures; (3) Emotional Strain and the Gradual Erosion of Soft Skills; (4) Soft Skills as Developable Capacities Within Supportive Organizational Environments; and (5) Navigating Change and Digital Transformation in a VUCA Healthcare Environment. Themes, sub-themes, and representative quotations derived from the data

Theme 1: soft skills as a shared professional ideal

Participants described soft skills such as empathy, listening, and respectful communication as integral to their professional identity and ethical self-understanding. They were presented as foundational elements of what it means to practice healthcare responsibly and humanely.

This emphasis was articulated most strongly in relation to empathy and emotional attunement to patients. As Eli, a physician, explained: It’s all about empathy - the ability to momentarily step into the patient’s shoes and understand what they are going through, even though I am not the one living that experience.

Similarly, Didi, a nurse, described empathy as a defining feature of everyday professional conduct, extending beyond technical care:

It has to be very empathetic, truly empathetic. To allow the patient the time to talk about what they are going through in their everyday life, and to feel that they receive not only medical care, but also emotional support through the way they are treated.

Across accounts, such descriptions positioned these soft skills as inseparable from the moral foundations of caregiving, shaping how professionals believe care ought to be delivered.

Soft skills as self-evident professional values

Participants consistently spoke about soft skills as qualities that should be inherent to the professional role in healthcare. They described communication, empathy, and attentiveness as essential components of what it means to be a “good professional,” framing these skills as integral to the definition of caregiving and teamwork rather than as optional or supplementary abilities. In this sense, soft skills were articulated as a professional ideal and as part of the moral and professional core of healthcare work. Apart from soft skills related to the patient-caregiver aspect, this was particularly evident in accounts of teamwork and professional collaboration. Hamdi, a physician, emphasized communication and consultation as integral to responsible practice:

Being a professional means having the capacity to communicate and consult with other physicians and colleagues. It involves knowing how to speak with hematologists, endocrinologists, orthopedists… In this sense, teamwork is not an added skill, but a core part of professional practice.

Similarly, nurses emphasized respectful interpersonal conduct as a foundation for communication and mutual attentiveness within teams. As Shani, a hospital nurse, explained: If you want people to listen to each other, you need to treat one another properly, and that creates good teamwork.

Didi further pointed to misunderstandings as a central factor that undermines collaboration in practice:

Things take longer and become more complicated simply because of misunderstandings within the team. That’s why teamwork, in my opinion, is extremely important.

Importantly, participants’ accounts reflected how soft skills are expected to be present as part of the role, particularly in relation to patient care and collaboration within teams. They emphasized the necessity of these skills for effective teamwork and daily functioning, yet spoke primarily at the level of professional norms and expectations rather than as a description of their consistent enactment in everyday practice.

Moral and normative framing of soft skills

The language participants used to describe soft skills was often normative and value-laden. Soft skills were predominantly associated with being humane, ethical, and respectful, and were framed as a moral obligation toward patients rather than as a formal requirement imposed by the organization. This framing positioned soft skills within a broader ethical narrative of care, emphasizing responsibility, dignity, and attentiveness to the patient as a person rather than solely as a clinical case.

This ethical orientation appeared most prominently in discussions of empathy, emotional sensitivity, patience, and compassion. Shir, a community nurse reflected on the emotional demands of patient care: You need a great deal of emotion and a lot of patience to relate to each child, especially those who are resistant. At the beginning, I didn’t have these skills, and gradually I developed them.

Notably, this moral framing emerged most strongly in relation to empathy, emotional sensitivity, and compassion in patient care. In contrast, participants referred far less frequently to soft skills in connection with decision-making processes, teamwork, adaptability, or coping with organizational change. Overall, participants framed soft skills primarily as morally grounded expectations tied to patient care, while references to their role in other domains of practice appeared less prominent in the accounts. Across accounts, soft skills were described as part of a shared moral and professional ideal, articulated most clearly in patient-facing interactions.

Theme 2: Time and workload pressures

Across professional roles, participants repeatedly described heavy workload, accelerated pace, and time pressure as central features of their everyday work, shaping how and when soft skills can be expressed. While participants emphasized the importance of soft skills within professional values, they also articulated a persistent tension between this ideal and the practical realities of daily clinical work, in which time scarcity and performance demands often take precedence. Gili, a physician, reflected on this tension as follows: I think this is extremely important… I feel that the time patients have for these kinds of interactions is very, very limited. Personally, I tried to explain things and talk with patients…but I didn’t feel there was enough space for it. There is a lot of pressure in the healthcare system, and I felt this was lacking.

Accelerated pace and shortened encounters

Participants described a work environment characterized by constant pressure to maintain pace and meet productivity expectations, often at the expense of slowing down for meaningful interpersonal engagement. The need to “keep up” with schedules and throughput was frequently positioned as an obstacle to sustained empathic interaction.

Eran, a physician, described this tension as follows: I try to remind myself that despite the workload and the pressure to meet certain productivity targets, each patient is ultimately an individual. Sometimes you need to slow down, at least occasionally, for specific people.

Other participants illustrated how time pressure makes it difficult to contain patients’ narratives once they unfold. Abed, a physician, reflected on the challenge of setting boundaries within time-limited encounters:

She sits for forty minutes and keeps talking, and someone tells you to say that the time is up. It is hard to cut someone off. I like to listen, and it is difficult for me to interrupt, difficult to tell someone, ‘That’s it, time is over. Go home and we’ll talk tomorrow.’ And then they say, ‘But I came to you because of something else,’ and after forty minutes of talking about one issue, suddenly there is something urgent. And then it all starts again.

Time was also framed as a scarce and contested resource within the healthcare system. Adi, a physician, pointed to the structural dimension of this constraint: The question is whether they are willing to allow it. There are people who see this as coming at the expense of other things, because it comes at the expense of time, and time is money. There is overload in the system, understaffing, and a lack of manpower.

Together, these accounts describe accelerated pace and structural time constraints as shaping everyday clinical encounters and limiting the time available for sustained interpersonal engagement.

Prioritization of technical and measurable tasks

Several participants explicitly contrasted soft skills with tasks that are formally measured, monitored, and prioritized by the system. Technical procedures, protocols, and life-threatening risks were described as receiving institutional emphasis, while soft skills were perceived as less visible and therefore more easily sidelined. Einaya, a physician, articulated this tension explicitly:

It always seems right to come with soft skills and to use them, but the problem is that there is no time. And why is that? If the system placed the same emphasis on soft skills as it does on infections, which are, undeniably, life-threatening. We are taught about infections, about ventilators, about practical things. So why not also about soft skills? They are no less important.

Her statement highlights how organizational priorities implicitly signal what “counts” as essential work, pushing soft skills into a secondary position despite their perceived importance.

Soft skills as conditional or eroded under pressure

Participants also described soft skills as vulnerable to erosion over time, particularly under conditions of sustained workload and emotional strain. Even when framed as deeply rooted in professional identity, soft skills were represented as capacities whose enactment is conditional and may diminish in the absence of sufficient time, space, or organizational support.

Adi, a physician, articulated this tension between possessing soft skills and being able to use them consistently in practice:

I think my soft skills are good, but that does not necessarily mean that I use them in every situation. I can say that I have them and that I know how to use them, but because of the nature and workload of the job, many times communication with patients becomes difficult, and it is not always easy to provide those soft skills, even when you have them.

This account illustrates how soft skills are not experienced as stable personal attributes that are automatically enacted, but as fragile professional resources that are sensitive to workload intensity and everyday organizational pressures.

Overall, Theme 2 describes how immediate structural constraints, particularly time pressure and productivity demands, shape the situational enactment of soft skills in everyday clinical encounters.

Theme 3: Emotional strain and the gradual erosion of soft skills

Participants described emotional strain as a cumulative and gradual process that develops over time under conditions of sustained workload, pressure, and repeated exposure to demanding clinical situations. Rather than framing diminished emotional engagement as a lack of professionalism or commitment, participants portrayed it as an outcome of prolonged effort within constrained organizational environments. In this sense, soft skills were not described as disappearing, but as becoming increasingly difficult to sustain, requiring emotional resources that are gradually depleted.

Across accounts, emotional strain emerged as a process of erosion rather than withdrawal. Participants emphasized that soft skills demand time, attention, and emotional investment, and that under continuous pressure these capacities may wear down, even among professionals who strongly identify with caring values.

Emotional exhaustion and erosion of soft skills over time

Several participants described emotional fatigue as a cumulative experience that affects the ability to consistently enact soft skills in practice. This erosion was linked to long-term exposure to workload, emotional demands, and repetitive clinical encounters, rather than to a lack of awareness or valuation of soft skills.

As Renana, a community nurse, explained: Time does its thing, and there is burnout. And the longer you work in the same profession, over time, soft skills also get worn down.

This account positions soft skills as capacities that require ongoing emotional investment, and that may diminish over time not because they are unimportant, but because they depend on resources that are gradually strained.

Declining emotional investment under prolonged pressure

Participants also referred to a reduction in emotional investment, particularly among professionals with longer tenure. This shift was not described as indifference toward patients, but as a pragmatic adjustment to prolonged exposure and pressure. Emotional engagement was portrayed as something that becomes harder to mobilize consistently over time.

Adi, a physician, described this process as follows: I recognize soft skills, but I also recognize that among older professionals, they have quite lost the motivation to use them. Maybe when they were younger they had more ambition. They have already seen everything, and they say they already know all the patients and what will happen to them. And now we give this less attention and less personal engagement, we just do what we know how to do.

This reflection suggests a movement toward more task-oriented practice, understood as a way to cope with ongoing demands rather than as a rejection of caring values.

At the same time, participants also raised concerns regarding the selection and training of healthcare professionals, emphasizing that emotional capacity is not always assessed or cultivated systematically. As Shir, a community nurse, noted:

In both medicine and nursing, you have to be humane. Many times I see doctors or nurses and I honestly do not understand how they were even allowed to enter this profession. They are like some kind of robotic monster who does not know how to relate to human beings. But unfortunately, what determines everything are the grades, entrance grades. They can get a hundred, but as human beings they are monsters.

This critique draws attention to structural and educational processes that prioritize academic achievement over emotional and interpersonal capacities, indicating that uneven expression of soft skills may be shaped already during professional selection and training.

Beyond age: experience, exposure, and uneven erosion

Importantly, emotional erosion was not attributed solely to age or seniority. Participants emphasized variability across individuals and rejected a linear association between experience and diminished empathy. Both highly empathic and emotionally distant professionals were described across different career stages.

Eran, a physician, articulated this complexity: I have seen it with my own eyes, I have seen both. I have seen older professionals with more empathy and older ones who are less good, and also among younger doctors. I did not notice any pattern that really changes.

Similarly, Avi, a paramedic, underscored both the importance and vulnerability of emotional sensitivity:

It is important that it exists. Unfortunately, I feel that in some people it gets worn down. It is important to have the heart. The heart, the sensitivity, the sensitivity toward the person standing in front of you.

These accounts describe emotional strain in relation to workload, repeated exposure, and ongoing pressure. Taken together, Theme 3 portrays emotional strain as a gradual process that influences professionals’ reported ability to sustain soft skills over time.

Theme 4: Soft skills as developable capacities within supportive organizational environments

Across participants’ accounts, soft skills were widely perceived as essential to healthcare practice and professional identity. At the same time, participants emphasized that while a basic personal disposition or value orientation may serve as an important starting point, soft skills are not fixed traits. Rather, they were described as capacities that can be strengthened, shaped, and refined over time, particularly within supportive organizational environments.

Participants emphasized that while soft skills may be rooted in personal disposition, their continued development depends on reinforcement, learning opportunities, and supportive organizational contexts. highlighting the importance of intentional support and ongoing development. As Renana, a community nurse, explained:

I think that in our profession, nursing, soft skills are part of the profession’s DNA… but over time, wear and tear sets in. Sometimes you need something to help reawaken them or shine a different spotlight on them, and that’s why it never hurts to actively strengthen soft skills.

A shared starting point, not a finished product

Participants commonly distinguished between an initial personal foundation and the ongoing development of soft skills through professional practice. Having a basic human orientation toward empathy or respect was seen as necessary, yet insufficient on its own. Most participants emphasized that these skills require cultivation, reinforcement, and learning over time.

Hamdi, a physician, articulated this dual perspective:

I think there is a very strong component of innate abilities… but I also have reservations. I am really convinced that these skills can be acquired. Even if this is not something that is part of your DNA, it is definitely possible to work on them.

This framing positions soft skills not as static traits one either possesses or lacks, but as capacities that evolve through experience, exposure, and professional context.

Formal training as a necessary but insufficient foundation

Several participants referred to formal training during their professional education as an important initial exposure to soft skills, particularly through courses focused on patient–caregiver relationships and simulation-based learning. These experiences were described as meaningful and formative, especially when they involved role-playing, reflection, and discussion of emotionally complex situations. As Adi, a physician, recalled:

Yes, I feel that I learned this during my studies… there was a course over several years, in small groups of students, and part of it involved role-playing difficult everyday situations… delivering bad news, admitting a mistake… and then we discussed the communication elements.

At the same time, participants emphasized that such training often took place many years earlier and was not consistently reinforced in later professional life. As a result, its impact was seen as limited unless supported by ongoing opportunities for development within the organization.

Organizational responsibility, time constraints, and competing demands

Participants pointed to the organizational environment as a decisive factor in whether soft skills are cultivated or gradually eroded. Supportive environments were described as those that allow space for learning, dialogue, and reflection, while environments dominated by time pressure, efficiency demands, and workforce shortages were perceived as constraining. In this sense, organizational context was described not as neutral, but as actively shaping everyday professional conduct. Hamdi, a physician, emphasized the link between workplace climate and patient relations:

In general, you need a very good, very pleasant work environment, because a pleasant work environment also creates a positive attitude toward the patient.

At the same time, participants described a clear gap between widespread recognition of the importance of soft skills and the limited extent to which organizations formally invest in their development. Adi, a physician, articulated this tension as follows:

I don’t feel that in the organization where I work there is any real training in these areas. There wasn’t any workshop or course. You might get it through personal example from specific people in the organization, but not at the organizational level.

Several participants explicitly noted that even when awareness exists, soft skills often compete with other priorities for time and attention. As one physician reflected:

I think there is no one in medicine today who isn’t aware that this is important… the question is whether they are willing to give it space. For some people, it comes at the expense of other things, because it takes time, and time is money. There is overload in the system and a shortage of staff.

Professional role models and informal mentoring as bridges between ideal and practice

Beyond formal training and organizational policies, participants repeatedly emphasized the importance of personal role models and informal mentoring in developing soft skills. Observing senior colleagues, receiving real-time feedback, and witnessing how experienced professionals navigate difficult interactions were described as powerful learning mechanisms through which abstract ideals are translated into everyday practice. Eran, a physician, described a moment of learning through example:

One time someone commented when I approached a patient and said, ‘Hello, please, I came to take a blood test.’ A doctor who was a bit older than me hinted from behind the curtain and said, ‘How are you? How are you feeling?’ That’s something I took with me, and afterwards I tried to pause a bit more with each patient and really listen.

Such accounts describe mentoring and role modeling as important mechanisms through which professionals learn and reinforce soft skills within everyday clinical work. Through these interactions, soft skills were not only discussed or valued but also actively embodied, reinforced, and made attainable in practice.

Overall, Theme 4 describes soft skills as capacities that can be strengthened through learning and role modelling, with participants referring to organizational conditions as influencing their ongoing development and maintenance.

Theme 5: Navigating change and digital transformation in a changing healthcare environment

Participants described the contemporary healthcare work environment as undergoing profound and ongoing transformation, marked by rapid technological change, digitalization, and shifts in professional roles and patient relationships. These processes were consistently framed as accelerated, far-reaching, and sometimes disruptive, reflecting characteristics commonly associated with volatile and rapidly changing healthcare environments, often described in the literature as VUCA. Change was not presented as incremental or episodic, but as a broader and ongoing transformation in how healthcare is practiced, including shifts in professional norms and clinician-patient relationships. Several participants described recent years as marking a fundamental turning point in this process. As Ludmila, a community doctor, explained:

I think that what has been happening recently is simply a revolution, a real revolution. It’s a 180-degree turn, because medicine, especially when I started, was much more paternalistic than it is today.

Participants described recent changes as requiring ongoing adaptation in professional roles, communication patterns, and modes of care delivery.

Digitalizatin and remote car as expressions of ongoing change

Participants highlighted digital technologies and remote care as prominent manifestations of recent organizational change. These developments were often described as expanding possibilities for communication, monitoring, and patient engagement, while simultaneously requiring professionals to adjust established routines and professional practices.

As Abed, a physician, explained:

These things are new. It’s not like conventional medicine where the patient had to be physically in the room with the doctor. Today you can know a lot about the patient and stay in contact with them without them even leaving home, and that’s a good thing.

Participants also referred to the COVID-19 period as a critical turning point that accelerated the normalization of remote technologies, particularly in professional learning and guidance. Renana, a community nurse, described how this shift reshaped everyday professional routines:

I think that since the COVID era, we suddenly discovered that many things can be done remotely, especially in training and guidance. Professional lectures are now delivered as webinars and via Zoom. You don’t have to physically go out to receive the same training.

These accounts illustrate how digital tools have become embedded in everyday professional life, reshaping not only care delivery but also learning and professional development.

Technological change and evolving demands on soft skills

While technological developments were often described as improving efficiency and accessibility, participants also linked these changes to evolving expectations regarding soft skills. Digital systems were portrayed as reshaping not only workflows, but also the emotional and relational dimensions of care. Miki, a community nurse, described this dual process:

Of course, we’ve gone through many changes. Just take the computer, for example. They add new software and systems that shorten work processes, which really helps. But at the same time, the relationship with patients has changed. We know that now we need to put even more emphasis on the patient, because the patient is at the center of the whole story.

Rather than framing digitalization as a technical challenge, participants described it as a context that increases the need for communication, emotional sensitivity, and patient-centered interaction.

Adaptation, organizational support, and soft skills under VUCA conditions

Participants’ accounts linked the accelerated pace of technological and organizational change to ongoing demands for adaptation in everyday professional practice. Change was described not as episodic, but as a continuing feature of the healthcare work environment, requiring adjustments in work routines, communication patterns, and professional roles. Within this context, soft skills were repeatedly mentioned in relation to coping with uncertainty, change, and evolving expectations.

Several participants referred to digitalization and technological developments as reshaping both clinical processes and interpersonal interactions. As Debby, a community nurse, noted:

There is a range of soft skills. Of course, now much more digital literacy is required… and the relationship with the patient has also changed, so we really need to pay more attention to how you speak and what you say, to be more aware of the way communication takes place.

This description reflects how technological change was experienced as increasing the need for attentiveness to communication and relational aspects of care, alongside technical competence.

Participants also described organizational support as relevant to their ability to adapt to changing conditions. While formal training was often perceived as focused primarily on clinical or technical domains, some participants referred to organizational initiatives that addressed emotional coping, communication, or interpersonal challenges. As Miki, a community nurse, explained:

What exists in the system is mainly professional training on clinical topics… but from time to time we are also given lectures on developing soft skills, such as how to cope with burnout or violence. It provides tools and confidence for dealing with these situations.

In addition, participants associated ongoing change with the need to remain professionally updated, particularly in relation to new technologies and sources of information. This was described as affecting professional interactions with patients and expectations regarding knowledge and communication. As Abed, a physician, stated:

Technology, especially artificial intelligence… there are always new things, and the physician needs to stay updated. Patients also access information and want to ask questions, so you need to keep your finger on the pulse.

Taken together, these accounts depict adaptation as a routine and ongoing aspect of healthcare work within rapidly changing and uncertain healthcare environments, sometimes described in the literature as VUCA conditions.

Soft skills were referenced in connection with managing technological change, sustaining communication, and responding to evolving professional demands, while participants also pointed to organizational conditions as influencing their capacity to cope with continuous change. Overall, Theme 5 describes ongoing digital and organizational transformation as reshaping professional interactions, with participants referring to soft skills in relation to adaptation, communication, and patient-centred care.

Discussion

This study set out to explore how healthcare professionals understand and experience soft skills in their everyday work within complex and changing healthcare environments. The findings indicate that soft skills are widely recognized as essential components of ethical and responsible care, particularly in relation to empathy, attentive listening, respectful communication, and collaborative teamwork. Across roles and settings, participants described these skills as part of the moral and professional core of healthcare practice. They were closely tied to normative expectations regarding how care ought to be delivered. This framing aligns with literature positioning soft skills as integral to ethical and humane care, particularly in patient-facing roles [51, 54]. While these findings are consistent with previous research, the present study adds empirical insight into how these expectations are negotiated in everyday organizational conditions, highlighting the gap between normative definitions of soft skills and their enactment in routine practice. This suggests that the meaning and enactment of soft skills are shaped not only by professional values but also by the organizational contexts in which these values must be realized.

Participants’ narratives suggest that soft skills function primarily as moral and professional values rather than as explicitly articulated practices. They were repeatedly described in terms of how care ought to be delivered. This language reflects a deeply embedded ethical orientation toward patients and colleagues. This mirrors prior research emphasizing that in healthcare settings, soft skills such as empathy and communication are often treated as foundational elements of caregiving, closely linked to dignity, trust, and patient-centered care [55–56]. This emotional and moral emphasis is further supported by recent research highlighting the foundational role of compassion and empathy in healthcare education and professional formation [63].

At the same time, the findings suggest a conceptual narrowing in how soft skills are understood in everyday professional discourse. Participants most frequently associated soft skills with emotional and relational dimensions of patient care. They referred far less explicitly to their relevance in organizational coordination, decision-making, or adaptation to systemic change. Similar patterns have been noted in the literature, where soft skills are often conceptualized primarily as interpersonal qualities rather than as broader professional capacities that support collaboration and complex system functioning [53, 57]. Such narrowing may reduce the visibility of soft skills as organizational resources that contribute to system performance beyond individual encounters [51]. This contrasts with multidimensional conceptualizations that define soft skills as encompassing personal, social, and methodological competencies rather than emotional capacities alone [11, 50]. A recent narrative synthesis similarly emphasizes communication, emotional intelligence, professionalism, teamwork, critical thinking, and reflective practice as integrated competencies requiring broader conceptual framing [64].

The present study extends this literature by providing empirical evidence of how this conceptual narrowing operates in everyday professional discourse, and by showing how it limits the recognition of soft skills as organizational and systemic resources rather than only interpersonal qualities. Conceptually, the findings suggest that the moral framing of soft skills, while affirming their ethical importance, may simultaneously obscure their systemic and organizational dimensions. This helps explain why participants rarely described soft skills as structured competencies requiring sustained institutional investment, despite evidence that such skills are shaped by educational, organizational, and systemic conditions [12, 65]. In this sense, the study contributes to existing research by demonstrating how the everyday interpretation of soft skills influences their visibility, development, and organizational support. These findings also resonate with broader educational trends, where healthcare management students report stronger acquisition of methodological skills compared to personal and social competencies, underscoring the need to better balance training across domains [11].

Beyond conceptual framing, the findings reveal a persistent gap between the acknowledged importance of soft skills and professionals’ reported ability to consistently enact them. Heavy workload, time pressure, and productivity demands emerged as central constraints shaping clinical encounters. Under such conditions, soft skills were experienced not as automatically enacted attributes. Rather, they were described as context-dependent resources whose expression depends on available time, emotional capacity, and organizational support. Quantitative evidence has documented discrepancies between educational emphasis on soft skills and their realization in professional settings [10], and prior research has shown that sustained workload pressures constrain the consistent enactment of empathy, communication, and teamwork [66, 67].

The present findings extend prior research by showing that this gap is not only the result of workload or emotional strain, but is experienced by professionals as structurally embedded in everyday organizational conditions that shape when and how soft skills can be enacted. Rather than attributing inconsistency to a lack of commitment or awareness, participants described it as a consequence of ongoing organizational constraints that delimit what can realistically be enacted in daily work. In this sense, the study contributes to existing literature by reframing soft skills as context-sensitive professional resources that are activated or constrained within specific organizational environments. The findings also provide empirical insight into everyday organizational dynamics that are often discussed conceptually in the literature but less frequently examined through professionals’ lived experiences, particularly regarding how institutional constraints shape the enactment of relational and communication skills in routine practice.

This interpretation aligns with research on organizational resilience, which emphasizes that competencies are sustained or eroded within particular contextual conditions rather than functioning independently of them [5]. Evidence from interprofessional healthcare settings similarly indicates that communication and teamwork skills are contingent upon structural and organizational factors rather than individual disposition alone [68]. By foregrounding professionals’ lived experiences, the current study deepens understanding of how these contingencies are negotiated in routine practice.

Importantly, participants also conceptualized soft skills as developable capacities rather than fixed personal traits. While many described an initial personal orientation toward empathy and respectful communication, they emphasized the roles of experience, observation, and organizational context in shaping these skills over time. This view is consistent with research demonstrating that complex professional competencies evolve through ongoing learning processes influenced by educational and organizational conditions [69].

Formal training, particularly simulation-based learning and patient–caregiver communication courses, was described as meaningful. However, it was viewed as insufficient when not reinforced in everyday practice. Empirical studies support the effectiveness of structured empathy education programs in enhancing communication and emotional competence [70], and simulation and role-play have been shown to strengthen relational competencies in medical education [64]. However, the present findings suggest that initial gains require continued organizational reinforcement to remain sustainable. Evidence further indicates that competency-based learning environments are positively associated with the development of personal, social, and methodological soft skills, while highlighting the importance of sustained support beyond initial training [10–12].

Beyond formal curricula, participants emphasized informal mentoring and role modeling as central mechanisms through which professional values are translated into practice. Observing senior colleagues and receiving real-time feedback were described as powerful forms of situated learning. Research supports the importance of role modeling and reflective practice in cultivating ethical awareness and interpersonal competencies beyond formal instruction [64], and longitudinal mentoring relationships embedded within supportive ecosystems have been shown to sustain complex skill acquisition over time [71]. These findings indicate that informal professional interactions play a critical role in maintaining soft skills within demanding organizational environments.

Finally, the findings situate soft skills within a broader healthcare context characterized by continuous technological, organizational, and professional transformation, often described in the literature as VUCA conditions. Participants described digitalization, remote care, and evolving professional norms as ongoing features of their work, requiring adjustments in communication practices and relational engagement. Prior research has emphasized that digital transformation intensifies the need for communication, adaptability, and emotional regulation as interactions become increasingly technologically mediated [8–9, 56]. Within such environments, soft skills were closely linked to professionals’ capacity to manage uncertainty, remain updated, and sustain patient-centered care amid shifting expectations.

Although many of the themes identified in this study have been reported in previous research, the present analysis contributes by showing how these elements are experienced and interpreted by healthcare professionals within everyday organizational contexts. The findings highlight that soft skills are not only interpersonal qualities but are closely shaped by working conditions, organizational expectations, and ongoing systemic pressures. This perspective adds empirical depth to existing literature by demonstrating how the enactment of soft skills depends on the interaction between personal commitment and contextual constraints in contemporary healthcare environments.

Theoretical contributions

This study contributes to the existing literature by providing empirical and contextual insight into soft skills in healthcare in three key ways. First, it demonstrates how soft skills are discursively narrowed in everyday professional accounts, being framed predominantly in moral and interpersonal terms, while their organizational and systemic dimensions remain less explicitly articulated. This finding adds empirical support to existing multidimensional models by showing how conceptual reduction operates in practice.

Second, the study highlights the context-sensitive nature of soft skills, showing that they function as professional resources shaped by everyday organizational conditions rather than as stable individual traits. Drawing on professionals’ lived experiences, the findings suggest that soft skills may be activated, constrained, or weakened within particular organizational environments.

Third, the study examines soft skills within contemporary healthcare environments often described in the literature as VUCA, demonstrating how systemic conditions shape the enactment of soft skills in everyday practice. The findings suggest that soft skills may function as mechanisms linking broader organizational and systemic conditions with everyday professional action. Taken together, the study supports a more contextualized understanding of soft skills, showing how moral commitment, emotional capacity, organizational structures, and systemic conditions interact in shaping the enactment of soft skills in contemporary healthcare settings.

Study limitations

This qualitative study is based on interviews with healthcare professionals working in large public hospitals and health maintenance organizations within a specific organizational and national context. The findings are therefore not intended to be statistically generalizable, but rather to provide in-depth insight into professionals’ perceptions and experiences of soft skills in complex healthcare environments. Because healthcare systems differ in their organizational structures, regulatory frameworks, and resource allocation models, the ways in which soft skills are enacted and constrained may vary across national and institutional settings.

The study relies on self-reported accounts, which reflect participants’ interpretations and meanings rather than direct observation of practice. The absence of observational data limits the ability to examine how soft skills are enacted in real-time clinical interactions and how discrepancies may arise between reported values and actual practice. In addition, the analysis focuses on professionals’ perspectives and does not include patient views or organizational documents, which could further enrich understanding of how soft skills are enacted and supported in practice.

Conclusions

This study provides an in-depth examination of how soft skills are understood, valued, and enacted by healthcare professionals working within complex and continuously changing healthcare environments. The findings indicate that soft skills are perceived not as peripheral competencies but as central to ethical, professional, and patient-centered care. Rather than simply confirming their importance, the analysis shows that the meaning and enactment of soft skills are shaped by everyday organizational conditions in which professionals must balance relational expectations with structural constraints. Across roles and settings, participants described empathy, communication, attentiveness, and relational sensitivity as integral to their professional identity, while also emphasizing the difficulty of sustaining these practices under routine workplace pressures.

These findings highlight the structural nature of the gap between the recognized importance of soft skills and professionals’ ability to consistently enact them in everyday practice. Rather than reflecting a lack of awareness or motivation, this gap appears to be embedded in organizational and systemic conditions that constrain relational work. Workload intensity, time pressure, efficiency-driven priorities, and sustained emotional demands limit opportunities for interpersonal engagement, indicating that the enactment of soft skills depends on contextual support rather than individual intention alone. This interpretation challenges views that frame soft skills as stable personal attributes and instead conceptualizes them as context-dependent professional resources whose sustainability is shaped by organizational and systemic conditions.

From a theoretical perspective, the study supports a contextualized understanding of soft skills as situated professional practices embedded within organizational and systemic environments. Rather than functioning solely as individual competencies, soft skills emerge as relational and context-dependent resources shaped by working conditions in VUCA healthcare environments. In this sense, the findings suggest that the sustainability of soft skills depends not only on individual commitment but also on the organizational and systemic contexts in which professional practice takes place, linking everyday clinical experience with broader processes of organizational resilience [5].

Implications

The findings carry important implications for healthcare education. While formal training and competency-based learning were described as meaningful foundations for soft skills development, they were widely perceived as insufficient when not reinforced in everyday professional practice. This points to the need for curricula that better integrate personal, social, and methodological skills and that align educational preparation with the realities of healthcare work [8–12]. Technology-supported simulation approaches may provide structured opportunities for practicing communication and relational skills; however, their long-term effectiveness depends on continuity with workplace conditions and organizational reinforcement [72–73].

At the organizational level, the findings highlight the decisive role of work environments in shaping whether soft skills are sustained or eroded. Organizations that foster supportive cultures, protect time for relational work, and recognize the emotional dimensions of care may be better able to support employee wellbeing and adaptive capacity. Conversely, environments that prioritize efficiency and measurable outputs without acknowledging relational work may risk undermining the very competencies required to sustain quality care and teamwork [5, 74, 75].

At the policy level, the findings suggest that soft skills can be understood not only as educational goals but also as system-level resources. Workforce policies, accreditation standards, performance metrics, and digital transformation strategies need to account for the relational and emotional dimensions of care. Without structural alignment between policy priorities and everyday clinical realities, efforts to promote patient-centered care and organizational resilience may remain aspirational rather than operational.

In the context of ongoing digital transformation and systemic change, supporting the development and sustainability of soft skills is not an optional enhancement but a strategic imperative. As healthcare systems continue to navigate uncertainty and technological acceleration, the capacity to sustain communication, empathy, adaptability, and ethical responsiveness may play a central role in maintaining trust, quality, and resilience. Future research and policy initiatives should therefore move beyond individual-level training and address the structural conditions that enable soft skills to be enacted consistently and sustainably within contemporary healthcare environments.
