Investigation of licorice extract as a green additive on microstructure, hardness, and wear behavior of electrodeposited nickel coatings
Authors: Seyed Mohammad Jesmani, Mahdi Aberi, Hafez Banasaz, Sadegh Mirzamohammadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this research, the effect of the utilization of licorice extract as an environmentally friendly additive in the electrodeposition process of nickel coatings on copper substrates was studied. Various concentrations of licorice extract, including 0 wt%, 0.033 wt%,0.066 wt%, and 0.132 wt% were added to a modified Watts bath, and the microstructure, hardness, and wear behavior of the Ni-electrodeposited coating were investigated. The results showed that the addition of licorice extract improved the surface morphology and refined the grain structure of the coating. In addition, a significant improvement in hardness occurred from 136 HV0.3 to 283 HV0.3 with the optimal amount of the additive, which was determined to be 0.066 wt%, owing to better nucleation, structure refinement, and lack of cracks. The wear tests showed enhanced wear resistance and reduced coefficient of friction up to 0.066 wt%. Beyond this, 0.132 wt% of the additive induced internal stress cracks, limiting the balance between hardness and structural integrity. In general, licorice extract can be considered an effective green additive for industrial applications in the Ni electrodeposition process.Keywords: Electrodeposition, Licorice extract, Hardness, Wear, MicrostructureSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The application of nickel coatings on metal substrates such as copper is important for the enhancement of corrosion resistance, wear resistance, and appropriate appearance in the electronics, automotive, and decorative industries. Electrodeposited nickel coating leads to the creation of a robust protective layer, which extends the lifetime of components in harsh environments such as fuel systems and electronic connectors. The electrical conductivity of Ni results in its consistent performance in circuit boards, whereas decorative applications benefit from its bright appearance1–4.

To enhance the performance of these coatings, additives can be added to the composition of the coatings. As a result, the functional and aesthetic properties of the Ni coating can be improved. Organic additives such as saccharin, polyethylene glycol (PEG), and sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) have been added to nickel coatings for these purposes. The outcome of adding these additives includes grain refinement of the coating structure, reduction of internal stresses, enhanced adhesion, increased hardness, and better wear properties. There are several researches dealing with the addition of additives to the Ni coating. For instance, Demir et al.5 added saccharin to the Ni–Cr coating and found that saccharin-modified coatings did not contain any microcracks unlike the saccharin-free ones, exhibited increased nano-hardness of the modified coating, and showed decreased wear rate in the saccharin-modified samples. Jesmani et al.6,7 used 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium bromide as the additive to modify the Watts bath composition and reported that in the presence of this additive, by changing the pH of the modified Watts bath, the Mo content changes in the Ni-Mo/Ni coating structure and converts the structure to an almost amorphous one, and reduces the crystallite size. They also found that modifying the modified Watts bath by this additive influences the role of current density so that a 1–6 A dm− 2 increase in the current density decreased the Mo content in the Ni–Mo/Ni coatings. Kim et al.8 added saccharin to Fe–36 wt% Ni (Invar alloy) coating and showed that the concentration of saccharin addition had a major effect on the appearance and growth of the crystals of the coating. Nayana et al.9 added sodium lauryl sulphate (SLS) to Ni–Co3O4 composite coatings and reported that SLS modified chemical composition, surface morphology, and microstructure of the Ni–Co3O4 composite coating, which affected the performance of this coating. Wang et al.10 studied the effect of polyethylene glycol (PEG) on the electrodeposition of nickel and found that PEG plays a pivotal role in controlling characteristics of the Ni deposits.

However, all the above-mentioned additives can result in health and environmental dangers. For example, Ma et al.11 and Pang et al.12 found that saccharin is a potentially ecotoxic contaminant, which can pollute drinking waters, surface, and ground. Bondi et al.13 reported that SLS could potentially cause oral, dermal, and inhalation exposure. Asio et al.14 studied SLS and found empirical evidence of this compound contributing to environmental toxicity at various concentrations. Rogers et al.15 reported shallow groundwater contamination by PEG. Therefore, the use of green additives can overcome the issues mentioned. One of the green additives, which has not been used yet, is licorice extract. The use of this green additive has been reported in copper and zinc electrowinning16,17, but no report is available on the utilization of licorice extract in the field of electrodeposition.

This study is an attempt to find the effect of using licorice extract as an additive in the electrodeposition of nickel on pure copper substrates. In this research, the influence of various contents of licorice extract on the morphology, hardness, and wear properties of Ni coatings has been investigated.

Experimental procedure

Materials

The materials used for the electrodeposition of nickel coatings on copper substrates can be found in Table 1. It is worth noting that both nickel sulfate hexahydrate (NiSO₄·6 H₂O) and sodium citrate Na₃C₆H₅O₇ were 0.2 M. The bath is a modified Watts bath with 0.2 M NiSO₄·6 H₂O and 0.2 M Na₃C₆H₅O7 as primary components. Although this bath is still a conventional Watts bath, it is modified as it uses citrate instead of boric acid and chlorides for better stability and increased uniformity of the coating.

Contents of the electrodeposition bath.

The Glycyrrhiza glabra root extract was prepared according to the literature18. The Glycyrrhiza glabra root was washed several times with deionized water. Then, 50 g of the root was finely cut, stirred with 150 mL of deionized water at 27 °C for 5 min, and filtered to obtain the extract. The additive concentrations in the 50 mL plating bath were: 0 ml (0 wt%), 0.25 ml (0.033 wt%), 0.5 ml (0.066 wt%), and 1 ml (0.132 wt%), relative to the bath volume.

Preparation of substrates

The copper substrates, made of pure Cu (99.99%), were first polished using sandpapers of various mesh sizes (80, 120, 200, 400, and 600) to achieve a smooth surface, followed by cleaning in acetone using an ultrasonic bath for 10 min to eliminate any possible organic contaminants. Then, the substrates were immersed in a solution composed of distilled water and nitric acid (65%, w/w) with a ratio of 1:1 for 30 s to activate and enhance the adhesion of the Ni-electrodeposited coating.

Electrodeposition process

The electrodeposition of nickel on the as-prepared Cu substrates was performed in a modified Watts bath using the technical parameters shown in Table 2. It is worth noting that the stirring process was performed using a magnetic stirrer to achieve a uniform composition.

The electrodeposition process was performed with different amounts of licorice extract. i.e., 0 wt%, 0.033 wt%,0.066 wt%, and 0.132 wt% to investigate its effect on the coating properties.

In addition, ammonia solution and sulfuric acid were utilized to adjust the pH of the electroplating bath.

Characterization of the coatings

The coatings were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM-TESCAN-Vega 3) to observe the surface morphology and microstructure of the coatings. After the wear tests, the surfaces were characterized using the same SEM apparatus.

Microhardness was measured using a Vickers indenter (Koopa Universal Hardness Tester, model MCV-H1; diamond pyramid indenter, 136° angle) with a 300 g load (HV 0.3), applied at 50 μm/s rate, with 10 s dwell and 15 s unload time. Five indentations per sample were performed with a minimum spacing of 100 μm. The values reported are the average of at least five indentations.

The wear resistance of the coatings was examined using a pin-on-disc (FARAGIR SANAT MEHR BIN) apparatus according to the ASTM G99-05 standard. The WC-Co pin used had a diameter of 6 mm with a Vickers hardness of about 1500 HV and the coated disc had a diameter of 50 mm. The conditions applied to perform the wear tests were as follows: room temperature, relative humidity of 15–18%, and sliding distance of 100 m. In addition, the load applied was 20 N with a speed of 0.5 m/s. It is noteworthy that the wear rate was calculated as follows: volume loss (mm³) = (mass loss, mg / density, 8.9 g/cm³) divided by (load × distance, N·m), yielding mm³/Nm.

Results and discussion

Morphology of coatings

Figure 1 shows the SEM images of the Ni-electrodeposited coatings. Without any additives, the SEM image (Fig. 1a) shows the nickel coating having a nodular, cauliflower-like surface that is rough. It can be observed that there are areas called nodules that have different sizes between 0.5 and 2 μm with irregular three-dimensional growth patterns, which are also some star-shaped or dendritic. This type of structure can be formed by pure nickel electrodeposits, which come from the lack of additives and cause indiscriminate grain growth, which is coarse and uneven. This is in agreement with the observations reported by Boukhouiete et al.19.

SEM images of a Ni-coated substrate with no licorice extract, b Ni-coated substrate with 0.033 wt% of licorice extract, c Ni-coated substrate with 0.066 wt% of licorice extract, d Ni-coated substrate with 0.132 wt% of licorice extract.

After adding 0.033 wt% of the additive (Fig. 1b), some refinement in the structure is noted. The nodules are diminished to 0.3 μm to 1 μm, and the cauliflower structures are less accentuated. In addition, the surface looks more even. As such, the surface is still rough, but the nodules have become smoother and more rounded. This implies that the surface coverage of the additive starts to increase, which would moderate the grain growth while improving the control on the deposition rate.

With the addition of 0.066 wt% of the additive (Fig. 1c), the surface shows further refinement. At this stage, the nodules are 0.2–0.8 μm, which indicates the surface texture is smoother and homogeneous. The large, irregular structures that were present in the sample without any additives have been largely replaced by fine and rounded grains. This suggests that the additive has had a greater impact, which is likely to have increased the nucleation rates while restraining undue coarsening of grains, resulting in a denser microstructure.

Among the samples analyzed, the one containing 0.132 wt% of the additive shows the best result in terms of uniformity and smoothness of the surface. The nodules having very fine diameters between 0.1 and 0.5 μm appear featureless at this level of zoom. The lack of any dendritic or cauliflower-like structures indicates a compact and refined microstructure, which is hypothesized to be a result of the significant inhibition of grain growth by the additive coupled with an increase in nucleation at higher additive levels.

Nonetheless, the SEM images of lower magnification reveal that the sample with 0.132 wt% of the additive contained multiple structural cracks with widths appearing to be on the order of a few micrometers. However, sample with 0.066 wt% of the additive did not show any cracks in its structure. In fact, it can be stated that a high additive concentration in sample with 0.132 wt% of the additive led to elevated nucleation and effective inhibition of grain growth. However, the occurrence of cracks suggests a rise in internal stress, most probably from over-inhibition of grain growth coupled with possible hydrogen gas evolution during deposition20. In this case, a relatively brittle structure is obtained, where the coating is unable to sustain the stress due to contraction or hydrogen entrapment, leading to crack formation. The presence of cracks has been reported in previous studies with different coatings and substrates6,21.

Hardness and wear behavior

Figure 2 depicts the hardness variation of the samples. As can be seen, the uncoated pure copper substrate has a starting hardness around 72 HV0.3 which shows the softness of the material. When nickel was electrodeposited on the copper without any additives, the hardness increased to approximately 136 HV0.3, and exhibited a rough, nodular surface morphology with cauliflower-like structures, dominated by nodules 0.5 to 2 μm in diameter. The increase in hardness is moderate and beneficial, which is attributed to nickel deposition as a harder metal than copper, albeit soft copper results in uncontrollable coarse microstructure evolution. Grain growth is uncontrolled due to insufficient heterogeneous nucleation sites from additive adsorption, leading to larger grains and lower hardness1.

Hardness and wear rate of the samples studied in this research, from the pure copper substrate to the samples coated with various contents of the additive.

The application of 0.033 wt% of licorice extract refines the microstructure so that the nodules are now between 0.3 and 1 μm, while the hardness increases to about 185 HV0.3. The increase in hardness indicates that the additive is either expanding nodule dispersion or altering surface adsorption, leading to improved grain homogeneity. Enhanced hardness also corroborates the Hall-Petch phenomenon, whereby strengthening is achieved due to increased boundary density from smaller grain size22,23. With 0.066 wt% of the additive, the nodule size reduces further to 0.2–0.8 μm and hardness to roughly 283 HV0.3, suggesting an even more homogenous, compact microstructure. This suggests a balanced electrodeposition where the additive controls current density and slows down boundary migration, resulting in a reduced pore structure. The increase in uniformity and hardness indicates the additive has a favorable influence on nucleation kinetics, which is consistent with the refining effect of the additive during nickel deposition.

The impact of the additive is evident in the increase of hardness from 136 HV0.3 with an additive content of 0 wt% to 307 HV0.3 with an additive content of 0.132 wt% due to microstructural refinement through mechanical property enhancement from controlled nucleation and growth processes. Nonetheless, the observed cracking at 0.132 wt% of the additive suggests that the optimum additive concentration is likely between 0.066 wt% and 0.132 wt%, which maximizes the hardness without structural failure. The trade-off illustrates that while increased hardness is associated with finer grains, moderate brittleness is counterproductive for structural refinement, indicating that further adjustments are needed for practical use. To summarize, the use of licorice extract as a green additive alters the microstructure and hardness of nickel coatings more effectively at a concentration of 0.066 wt%, balancing hardness with structural integrity.

Figure 2 displays the wear rates of the samples synthesized in this study. The uncoated pure copper substrate has the greatest wear rate of approximately 0.004 mm³/mm. Additionally, a relatively high COF is present, as can be seen in Fig. 3, indicating that the wear resistance is poor, and it is easily abraded and worn down due to its soft nature. As nickel was electrodeposited with no additives, the wear rate greatly improved to about 0.00085 mm³/mm, along with new rough nodular surface features with nodule diameters of 0.5 to 2 μm. This improvement confirmed the beneficial impact of the nickel coating, which boosted the hardness to about 136 HV0.3 and improved wear resistance. The COF is roughly 0.7, showing moderate frictional behavior that matches the coarse microstructure, which may enhance surface roughness and friction24. Applying 0.033 wt% of licorice extract improved the microstructure, decreasing nodule size to 0.3–1 μm, which led to a decrease in the wear rate to approximately 0.0004 mm³/mm and COF of 0.4. These changes are attributed to the increase in the hardness to 185 HV0.3, suggesting better wear resistance, which is explained by Archard’s Eqs25,26, which states that the higher the hardness of the material, the lower the wear rate. The reduced value of COF indicates that the additive may be starting to work by smoothing the surface, lessening adhesive interactions. Furthermore, this relates to the principle whereby grain refinement improves tribological properties (Hutchings, 1992). With 0.066 wt% of the additive, the COF and wear rate maintained low values of approximately 0.00038 mm³/mm with a COF value of about 0.27. The nodule size decreases to 0.2–0.8 μm, and hardness increases to 283 HV0.3. The superior wear resistance stems from higher hardness and greater grain boundary density that hinder dislocation during the sliding wear process and movement of wear debris. A decrease in COF suggests further smoothing beyond that attributed to the effect of the additive upon nucleation and growth during surface smoothing. With 0.132 wt% of the additive, the grain structure was the finest at 0.1–0.5 μm while wear rate reached the lowest value of approximately 0.00035 mm³/mm and COF of about 0.25. However, these values are very close to those of the sample containing 0.066 wt% of the additive, which can be ascribed to the presence of cracks in the coating, as was observed in Fig. 4. In fact, the positive effect of the structure refinement was counterbalanced by the presence of cracks in this sample.

The coating with 0.132 wt% of the additive demonstrates the highest hardness (307 HV₀.₃) outstanding wear rate (1.2 × 10⁻⁵ mm³/Nm) and COF (0.28) with the lowest wear. However, these improvements are negated with delamination due to cracks under extended loading, which can be seen in the SEM image of the wear surface (Fig. 5e), as spallation and cracks formed on the surface. This greatly accelerates wear through crack tip fatigue which is estimated to increase effective wear volume. The coating with 0.066 wt% of the additive is the reverse of this. It has the peak hardness of 283 HV₀.₃ and is crack free (Fig. 5c), which allows stable tribological performance (- wear rate: 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ mm³/Nm; COF: 0.32).

Wear surfaces of the samples studied in this research: a uncoated Cu substrate, b Ni-coated substrate, c Ni-coated substrate with 0.033 wt% of licorice extract, d Ni-coated substrate with 0.066 wt% of licorice extract, e Ni-coated substrate with 0.132 wt% of licorice extract.

The wear surfaces of the samples can be found in Fig. 5. The uncoated copper substrate (Fig. 5a), possessing a hardness around 72 HV0.3 and a wear rate of 0.004 mm³/mm, likely undergoes major adhesive and abrasive wear. Because of its softness and ductility, copper readily underwent transfer to the counterface and ploughing by asperities, undergoing substantial loss. With no coating to provide protection, deep grooves and surface damage occurred, which were consistent with adhesive wear. A nickel coating without the additive showed a hardness value of 136 HV0.3, a wear rate of 0.00085 mm³/mm, and a coefficient of friction (COF) equal to 0.74 shows that the coating has a microstructure that is rough and nodular with grain sizes of 0.5 to 2 μm. The rough wear surface texture likely indicates that abrasive wear occurred, but ploughing and cutting were dominant, with a lower severity compared with that of the uncoated substrate. The moderate hardness supports some level of wear resistance, but due to the irregular grain structure, resistive forces create micro-cutting through contact and friction. High values of COF suggest some form of adhesive interaction where bond formation and shear occur between surface asperities, leading to the wear debris.

The coating containing 0.033 wt% of the additive likely experienced a combination of abrasive and mild adhesive wear, resulting from a microstructure with grains of 0.3–1 μm and a hardness of 185 HV0.3, a wear rate of 0.0004 mm³/mm, and a COF value of 0.36. Compared to the sample with no additive, the smoother surface achieves a reduction in ploughing minus friction, but the still coarser grains may permit some micro-cutting from the counterface. Reduced adhesive junctions indicated by the lower COF value suggest that frictional contacts caused by static contacts are lowered due to the leveling effect of the additive on the interface, but the wear occurred by localized plastic flow and shallow material loss.

The coating containing 0.066 wt% of the additive, possessing a more refined microstructure of 0.2–0.8 μm, 283 HV0.3 hardness, a wear rate of 0.00038 mm³/mm, and a coefficient of friction (COF) value of 0.3, seems to undergo primarily abrasive wear with little to no adhesive wear involvement. The compact, uniform microstructure increases the ability to sustain loads, decreasing the removal of material through ploughing or cutting. In fact, fine wear products are created and removed, and the coating hardness and surface smoothness restrict adhesive wear.

The SEM micrograph in Fig. 5e shows that the coating containing 0.132 wt% of the additive, with the finest grain structure (0.1–0.5 μm) and hardness of 307 HV0.3, exhibits the best wear resistance and surface refinement, but significant cracking. It indicates delamination wear as the primary wear mechanism, aggravated by brittle fracture. Ultra-fine grains increase hardness while reducing material loss during sliding. Excessive refinement, coupled with hydrogen evolution, causes internal stress that results in crack growth and spalling.

It can be said about the mechanism of the licorice extract action that at lower concentrations (0.033–0.066 wt%), the polyphenolics compounds of licorice extract adsorb on growing Ni nuclei, promoting 2D nucleation and inhibiting mobility27, leading to grain refinement. This results in a reduction of crystallite size from 0 wt% to 0.066 wt% and increase in hardness without stress buildup. At higher amounts (0.132 wt%), excessive adsorption leads to H2 evolution and the incorporation of organic residues. This results in compressive stresses and organic residue-induced stresses that exceed the tensile limit of the coating, micro-cracking through hydrogen embrittlement, and lattice mismatch28.

Conclusion

Licorice extract was used as an effective green additive in the electrodeposition process of nickel on copper substrates. Various amounts of this additive were studied to determine the effect of this additive on the microstructure, hardness, and wear behavior of the Ni coating. The most important conclusions of the present study are as follows:

The addition of licorice extract refined the grain structure of nickel coatings, reducing nodule size and producing a more uniform and compact surface. As the additive content increased, the grains became finer. However, 0.132 wt% of the licorice extract led to the formation of cracks in the structure.

The Vickers hardness increased noticeably when licorice extract was added to the coating, i.e., it increased from 136 HV0.3 (without additive) to 283 HV0.3 at the optimal concentration (0.066 wt% and 307 HV0.3 (0.132 wt%, because of grain refinement and improved nucleation kinetics.

The increase in the concentration of the additive led to an enhancement of wear resistance and a reduction in the coefficient of friction (COF). Again, the addition of 0.066 wt% of the additive led to the optimal combination of wear resistance and surface integrity, whereas with 0.132 wt% of the additive, the presence of cracks degraded the coating performance despite its lower wear rate and COF.
