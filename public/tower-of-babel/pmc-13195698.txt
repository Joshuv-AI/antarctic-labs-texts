Metabolic Dysfunction–Associated Steatotic Liver Disease Is Associated With Impaired Health-Related Quality of Life in People With HIV
Authors: Felice Cinque, Michael Nudo, Sahar Saeed, Jihoon Lim, Dana Kablawi, Gabrielle Mariano, Amanda Lupu, Wesal Elgretli, Erica EM Moodie, Colleen Price, Ken Monteith, Curtis Cooper, Sharon Walmsley, Nadine Kronfli, Joseph Cox, Cecilia Costiniuk, Alexandra De Pokomandy, Jean-Pierre Routy, Marina B Klein, Bertrand Lebouche, Giada Sebastiani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsMetabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is common in people with HIV (PWH), yet its impact on health-related quality of life (HRQoL) remains poorly characterized. We evaluated the association between MASLD and both generic and liver-specific HRQoL.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis within a national multicenter cohort of PWH. MASLD was defined as hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter >248 dB/m) plus at least metabolic comorbidity and significant liver fibrosis as liver stiffness measurement ≥8 kPa on FibroScan. Generic HRQoL was assessed using the Short Form-36 and liver-specific HRQoL using the Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire (CLDQ). Adjusted estimated mean differences were estimated using multivariable linear regression.ResultsAmong 519 participants, the prevalence of MASLD and significant liver fibrosis was 41.4% and 7.2%, respectively. MASLD was independently associated with lower generic HRQoL, including reduction of physical functioning (−4.6; 95% confidence interval [CI] −9.12 to −0.01) and general health (−4.5; 95% CI −8.58 to −0.48) on the Short Form-36. Liver-specific HRQoL was more affected; MASLD was associated with a lower overall CLDQ score (−0.29; 95% CI −0.49 to −0.09), driven by worse fatigue, systemic symptoms, emotional symptoms, and worry. Among PWH with MASLD, increasing metabolic comorbidity burden was associated with a dose–response decline in physical HRQoL, whereas liver-specific HRQoL did not differ across comorbidity strata. Significant fibrosis was associated with lower CLDQ activity (−0.62; 95% CI −1.20 to −0.05) and worry (−0.60; 95% CI −1.15 to −0.05).ConclusionIn PWH, MASLD is associated with impaired HRQoL, particularly fatigue, systemic symptoms, and emotional well-being. Greater metabolic burden and liver fibrosis further worsen patient-reported outcomes.Keywords: Patient-Reported Outcomes, Transient Elastography, Liver Fibrosis, Fatigue, Systemic Symptoms

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a leading cause of chronic liver disease globally and is increasingly recognized as a multisystem condition that extends well beyond hepatic steatosis.1 Closely linked to obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and type 2 diabetes, MASLD reflects a state of systemic metabolic dysfunction that affects multiple organ systems and contributes to cardiovascular disease, functional impairment, and overall disease burden.2 Although fibrosis remains the principal determinant of liver-related morbidity and mortality, growing evidence suggests that the clinical impact of MASLD begins earlier in the disease course and is not fully captured by histologic severity or liver-specific outcomes alone. Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) has therefore gained prominence as a critical patient-centered outcome in MASLD.3 In the general population, MASLD has been associated with impaired physical functioning, fatigue, reduced vitality, and emotional distress, even in individuals without advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis.4 These impairments likely reflect the combined effects of systemic inflammation, metabolic comorbidities, symptom burden, and disease-related anxiety. Importantly, liver-specific patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures capture dimensions of disease impact that are not reflected by biochemical markers or noninvasive fibrosis assessment, highlighting the limitations of traditional liver-centered end points and underscoring the importance of the patient perspective in evaluating the true burden of MASLD.

People with HIV (PWH) represent a population in whom the multisystem consequences of MASLD may be particularly pronounced. Advances in antiretroviral therapy (ART) have transformed HIV into a chronic, manageable condition, shifting morbidity and mortality toward age-related and non–AIDS-related comorbidities.5 Metabolic disorders are more prevalent in PWH than in the general population, driven by a complex interplay between traditional risk factors, HIV-related immune and inflammatory perturbations, and long-term ART exposure.6 In this context, MASLD is now one of the most frequent comorbidities in PWH,7,8 conferring a substantial risk of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis and advanced liver fibrosis in 24% and 12%, respectively, in meta-analyses of contemporary cohorts.9 PWH often experience reduced baseline HRQoL related to chronic disease burden, comorbidities, psychosocial stressors, and stigma.10,11 MASLD may therefore represent an underrecognized contributor to impaired well-being in this population. Moreover, MASLD in PWH rarely occurs in isolation; metabolic comorbidities commonly cluster and may independently or synergistically impair physical functioning and vitality, while the presence of significant liver fibrosis may impose an additional psychological and functional burden, even in the absence of overt cirrhosis.12 Despite the significant prevalence and severity of MASLD in PWH, data on its impact on HRQoL remain limited.12,13 As a result, the patient-perceived burden of MASLD in PWH and the relative contributions of metabolic comorbidity burden and fibrosis severity remain poorly defined.

The primary objectives of this study were to (1) evaluate the association between MASLD and both generic and liver-specific HRQoL in a well-characterized, contemporary cohort of PWH without viral hepatitis, significant alcohol intake, or advanced liver disease; and (2) examine whether increasing metabolic comorbidity burden and the presence of significant liver fibrosis were associated with incremental impairments in HRQoL.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Population

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis within the Canadian multicenter prospective LIVEr disease in HIV (LIVEHIV) cohort, an ongoing observational study designed to characterize the burden and determinants of liver disease in PWH.14 Participants were consecutively recruited from the following 3 tertiary care centers in Canada: the McGill University Health Centre (Montreal; n = 286), the Ottawa Hospital (Ottawa; n = 183), and the Toronto General Hospital (Toronto; n = 50). Between January 2021 and January 2025, we included consecutive patients aged ≥18 years with confirmed HIV infection ART for ≥6 months available transient elastography (TE) with both liver stiffness measurement (LSM) and controlled attenuation parameter (CAP). During the study period, participants were consecutively enrolled into the LIVEHIV cohort according to the study protocol, which included protocol-mandated annual liver assessment using TE across all participating centers. The present study represents a cross-sectional analysis of baseline data from all enrolled LIVEHIV participants. Relevant clinical and biochemical data collected within 1 month of TE were required for inclusion. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) positive hepatitis B surface antigen or active hepatitis C virus coinfection (positive hepatitis C virus RNA); (2) other known liver diseases (autoimmune, cholestatic, genetic, or active drug-induced liver disease); (3) excess alcohol intake, defined as >20 g/d for women or >30 g/d for men; (4) history of hepatocellular carcinoma or liver transplantation; (5) contraindication to TE (pregnancy, pacemaker); (6) failure or unreliable TE; (7) presence of advanced chronic liver disease, defined as LSM ≥10 KPa, at study entry. Patients with advanced chronic liver disease at baseline were excluded because the LIVEHIV cohort was specifically designed to evaluate the incident development of advanced chronic liver disease during follow-up.

Ethics

All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The LIVEHIV cohort study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of the Research Institute of the McGill University Health Centre (study code 14-182-BMD), with additional approvals obtained at participating sites as required.

Clinical and Laboratory Assessment

Collected data included demographic characteristics, HIV history (time since diagnosis, ART class, and exposure), comorbidities, body mass index (BMI), liver biochemistry, lipid profile, hematological indices, and immuno-virological parameters. The serum fibrosis biomarker fibrosis-4 was calculated for each participant. Time since HIV diagnosis was defined as the interval between the first positive HIV test and the TE visit. ART classes included nonnucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors, protease inhibitors, integrase strand transfer inhibitors, and nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors. An undetectable viral load was defined as <50 copies/mL. Alcohol use was assessed using the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test–Consumption questionnaire.15 Overweight was defined as a BMI of 25–29.9 kg/m2 and obesity as a BMI ≥30 kg/m2. Prediabetes was defined as fasting serum glucose ≥5.6 mmol/L or glycated hemoglobin ≥5.7%. Type 2 diabetes mellitus was defined as a glycated hemoglobin ≥6.5%, current use of antihyperglycemic treatment, or a prior clinical diagnosis. Dyslipidemia was defined as triglycerides >1.7 mmol/L, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol ≤1.0 mmol/L in men or ≤1.3 mmol/L in women, or lipid-lowering therapy. MASLD was defined as the presence of hepatic steatosis (CAP >248 dB/m) in absence of excess alcohol intake, plus ≥1 cardiometabolic risk factor1:

Type 2 diabetes or prediabetes;

3.Blood pressure ≥130/85 mmHg or antihypertensive treatment;

Lifestyle behaviors, including physical activity habits and snacking behavior, were assessed by trained research staff during study visits.

Transient Elastography With CAP

TE examinations were performed on a 4-hour fasting patient by 1 of 2 experienced operators per center (each with >500 prior examinations).16 The standard M probe was used in all patients. The XL probe was applied if M probe failed or BMI >30 kg/m2, as previously described.17 LSM results were considered reliable when >10 validated measures were obtained with an interquartile range <30% of the median value.

Assessment of Health-Related Quality of Life

HRQoL was assessed using the following 2 validated PRO instruments: the Short Form-36 (SF-36)18 and the Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire (CLDQ).19 Questionnaires were administered during study visits by trained research staff. The SF-36 is a widely validated generic measure of HRQoL, commonly used to evaluate the impact of chronic conditions, including HIV20 and metabolic liver disease,21 on physical and mental health. The SF-36 comprises 8 domains—physical functioning, role limitations due to physical health, bodily pain, general health, vitality (energy/fatigue), social functioning, role limitations due to emotional problems, and emotional well-being. Domain scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better perceived health status and were calculated using standard scoring procedures. The 8 domains were additionally aggregated into the Physical Component Summary, reflecting physical functioning, physical role limitations, pain, and general health, and the Mental Component Summary, capturing emotional well-being, emotional role limitations, social functioning, and vitality. The EuroQol five-dimension questionnaire (EQ-5D) utility scores were estimated from SF-36 domain scores using the algorithm developed by Ara and Brazier, which predicts EQ-5D health states based on SF-36 physical and mental health dimensions.22 The resulting EQ-5D utility scores range from values below 0 (health states worse than death) to 1 (perfect health), with higher scores indicating better HRQoL. The CLDQ is a liver disease–specific instrument consisting of 29 items across 6 domains (fatigue, activity, emotional function, abdominal symptoms, systemic symptoms, and worry). Each item is scored on a 7-point Likert scale, with higher scores reflecting better liver-related HRQoL. Domain-specific scores and an overall CLDQ score were calculated.

Statistical Analysis

The primary analyses evaluated the association between MASLD and HRQoL outcomes. Multivariable linear regression models were used to estimate adjusted least square (estimated marginal) means and to compute mean differences in SF-36 and CLDQ scores associated with MASLD status. Models were adjusted a priori for clinically relevant covariates, including sex, age, undetectable viral load, smoking status, alcohol use, regular exercise, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, snacking, and significant fibrosis status. Among participants with MASLD, secondary analyses examined the association between increasing metabolic comorbidity burden and HRQoL outcomes, as well as the impact of significant liver fibrosis. All tests were 2-sided with a significance level of α = 0.05. Analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Cohort Characteristics

The study included 519 PWH, with a median age of 48 years; 74% were male and 89% had an undetectable HIV viral load (Table 1). MASLD was present in 232 participants (41.4%). Compared with participants without MASLD, those with MASLD had higher alanine aminotransferase and LSM, with a greater prevalence of significant fibrosis (11% vs 3.9%). Lifestyle factors, including smoking, alcohol intake, physical activity, health-care utilization, and specialist visits, were similar between groups. Participants with MASLD reported significantly worse HRQoL compared with those without MASLD. On generic measures, MASLD was associated with lower SF-36 Physical Component Summary scores (mean [standard deviation (SD)]: 51 (11) vs 53 (10), reflecting poorer physical functioning, greater bodily pain, worse general health, and reduced vitality. EQ-5D utility values were likewise lower in participants with MASLD (mean [SD]: 0.81 [0.18] vs 0.86 [0.17]). In contrast, SF-36 Mental Component Summary scores and other mental or social functioning domains did not differ significantly between groups. Consistently, liver-specific quality of life was significantly impaired in participants with MASLD, who had lower overall CLDQ scores (mean [SD]: 5.59 ± 1.18 vs 5.93 ± 0.99), with significantly lower scores across all domains, including abdominal symptoms, fatigue, systemic symptoms, activity, emotional symptoms, and worry.

Generic Health-Related Quality of Life—SF-36

After multivariable adjustment, MASLD was associated with significantly worse generic HRQoL in selected SF-36 domains (Table 2). Compared with participants without MASLD, those with MASLD had significantly lower scores in physical functioning (adjusted mean difference −4.81; 95% confidence interval [CI] −9.38 to −0.24; P = .040) and general health perceptions (−4.68; 95% CI −8.77 to −0.58; P = .026). Other SF-36 domains—including role limitations due to physical or emotional problems, bodily pain, vitality, mental health, and social functioning—as well as the Physical and Mental Component Summary scores, were numerically lower in participants with MASLD; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance after adjustment. EQ-5D utility was also lower in participants with MASLD, with a borderline adjusted difference (−0.03; 95% CI −0.07 to 0.00; P = .052).

Liver-Specific Health-Related Quality of Life—CLDQ

Liver-specific HRQoL assessed by the CLDQ was significantly lower in participants with MASLD (Table 3). MASLD was associated with a lower overall CLDQ score (mean difference −0.28; 95% CI −0.48 to −0.08; P = .007). Domain-specific analyses demonstrated significantly lower scores for fatigue (−0.34; 95% CI −0.62 to −0.06; P = .018), systemic symptoms (−0.28; 95% CI −0.49 to −0.06; P = .013), emotional symptoms (−0.32; 95% CI −0.58 to −0.06; P = .018), and worry (−0.22; 95% CI −0.44 to −0.00; P = .048). Scores for abdominal symptoms and activity were numerically lower among participants with MASLD but did not differ significantly between groups.

Impact of Metabolic Comorbidity Burden and Significant Fibrosis on Quality of Life Among Participants With MASLD

Among participants with MASLD, individual metabolic comorbidities showed limited additional impact on HRQoL after multivariable adjustment (Table 4). On the SF-36, overweight status was associated with significantly worse bodily pain (mean difference −11.1; 95% CI −21.4 to −0.85), as well as a lower Physical Component Summary score (−5.54; 95% CI −10.1 to −0.98). In contrast, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, and low HDL cholesterol were not consistently associated with impairments across individual SF-36 domains or with either the Physical or Mental Component Summary scores. Significant liver fibrosis was not consistently associated with poorer generic HRQoL across SF-36 domains. On the CLDQ, most comparisons according to individual metabolic comorbidities revealed small and nonsignificant differences across domains. In contrast, significant fibrosis was associated with lower scores in specific liver-related HRQoL domains, including activity (−0.62; 95% CI −1.20 to −0.05) and worry (−0.60; 95% CI −1.15 to −0.05). Interestingly, increasing comorbidity burden was associated with progressively lower physical and social SF-36 scores, with individuals with ≥4 comorbidities showing substantially reduced physical functioning (74.2 vs 85.3), limitations due to physical health (70.2 vs 84.5), bodily pain (73.1 vs 80.4), and social functioning (74.8 vs 84.3), as well as Physical Component Summary (47.96 vs 53.06), compared with those with 0–1 comorbidity (Figure 1A). In adjusted analyses, physical functioning was significantly lower among participants with 2–3 comorbidities compared with those with 0–1 comorbidities (mean difference −10.7; 95% CI −20.6 to −0.88). SF-36 Physical Component Summary scores were also reduced in participants with ≥4 comorbidities (−5.10; 95% CI −9.77 to −0.43), whereas no other SF-36 domains differed significantly (Figure 1B, Supplementary Table 1). EQ-5D utility also declined with increasing comorbidity burden, from 0.85 in those with 0–1 comorbidity to 0.78 in those with ≥4, consistent with a dose–response relationship, although these differences were not significant after adjustment. In contrast to the SF-36 findings, increasing metabolic comorbidity burden was associated with only modest and inconsistent differences in liver disease-specific quality of life as assessed by the CLDQ (Figure 2A). Compared with individuals with 0–1 comorbidities, those with ≥4 comorbidities showed lower adjusted marginal mean scores for fatigue (4.52 vs 4.77), systemic symptoms (5.19 vs 5.71), and activity (5.67 vs 5.95), while differences in abdominal symptoms, emotional symptoms, and worry were minimal. In adjusted analyses, only the systemic symptoms domain demonstrated a significant decline among participants with ≥4 comorbidities compared with the reference group (mean difference −0.53; 95% CI −1.05 to −0.00), whereas differences in overall CLDQ score and other domain-specific scores were not significant (Figure 2B, Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

In this study of over 500 PWH, MASLD was associated with clinically meaningful impairments in HRQoL, even among a relatively young population with well-controlled HIV, preserved immune function, and no advanced chronic liver disease. Notably, to our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively characterize both generic and liver-specific HRQoL in PWH with MASLD. By integrating generic (SF-36/EQ-5D) and liver-specific (CLDQ) instruments, we provide a comprehensive assessment of MASLD-related patient burden in PWH. While generic measures primarily captured impairments in physical functioning and general health, liver-specific measures identified broader deficits, particularly in fatigue, systemic symptoms, emotional symptoms, and worry. Increasing metabolic comorbidity burden and significant fibrosis further contributed to worse PROs. Together, these findings highlight that MASLD contributes to clinically meaningful burden in PWH that extends beyond traditional liver-related end points.

Our findings highlight the clinical relevance of MASLD in PWH and support a patient-centered approach aligned with the “fourth 90” agenda, which emphasizes quality of life, social well-being, and long-term outcomes beyond viral suppression.10,23 Importantly, the observed impairments were detectable even in early stages of disease, suggesting that MASLD contributes to reduced well-being before the development of advanced liver complications.

A key contribution of this study lies in the use of the CLDQ to capture aspects of disease burden not fully reflected by generic HRQoL instruments in the setting of HIV. Liver-specific measures identified more consistent and pronounced impairments than generic tools, particularly in domains such as fatigue, systemic symptoms, and worry.19 These findings likely reflect the impact of metabolic and inflammatory processes, as well as heightened awareness and concern about liver disease progression, even in the absence of advanced fibrosis.24, 25, 26 They support the integration of liver-specific HRQoL instruments in both clinical practice and future studies of MASLD in PWH.

Another novel contribution is the cumulative, dose-dependent impact of metabolic multimorbidity on HRQoL in PWH with MASLD. In the general population, individual metabolic comorbidities have been associated with poorer HRQoL in MASLD, predominantly affecting physical functioning.4,26, 27, 28 In our cohort, overall metabolic burden—rather than individual conditions—was associated with worse physical HRQoL, supporting a cumulative effect of metabolic dysfunction on PROs in PWH. Accordingly, the observed impairments are unlikely to be attributable to MASLD alone, as obesity and related metabolic comorbidities likely play a major role. Because obesity is embedded in the definition of MASLD, the independent contribution of hepatic steatosis vs adiposity cannot be disentangled, and the observed associations should be interpreted as reflecting the combined effect of metabolic dysfunction, including obesity and related comorbidities. In contrast, liver-specific HRQoL appeared less sensitive to cumulative metabolic burden, suggesting that CLDQ domains preferentially capture liver-related symptomatology rather than the broader effects of systemic metabolic disease.

Across generic instruments, MASLD was most consistently associated with poorer physical health, whereas mental health, vitality, and social functioning were largely preserved. These findings suggest that MASLD primarily affects somatic health rather than psychological well-being in its early stages. These findings are consistent with prior reports in both the general population and PWH showing no differences in mental health summary scores according to MASLD or fibrosis severity.25,26 Importantly, HRQoL in PWH is strongly influenced by psychosocial factors, including stigma, chronic disease burden, and mental distress. As all participants in our study were PWH, comparisons were performed within a population sharing a similar psychosocial context, reducing confounding by HIV-related factors. Nevertheless, residual psychosocial confounding cannot be excluded, and MASLD should be interpreted as associated with, rather than solely responsible for, impaired HRQoL in this population.

In the general population, greater MASLD severity, including metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis29 and advanced fibrosis,30 has been consistently linked to poorer HRQoL. Similarly, among PWH, significant fibrosis has been linked to worse quality of life.12,25 In our study, we observed no consistent association with global HRQoL measures, but significant fibrosis was linked to impairments in specific liver-related domains, including activity and worry. This pattern suggests that early fibrosis may influence functional limitations and disease-related concern before affecting overall health perception, highlighting the importance of domain-level HRQoL assessment.

Strengths of this study include the use of multiple validated HRQoL instruments, detailed metabolic and liver phenotyping, and a well-characterized multicenter HIV cohort with high rates of viral suppression. Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The predominance of men may limit generalizability to women with HIV. We lacked detailed socioeconomic measures, including education, income, housing stability, and social support, which may influence HRQoL and contribute to residual confounding. In addition, although participants were predominantly treated with contemporary ART regimens, we cannot fully exclude that prior exposure to older nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors associated with mitochondrial toxicity may have contributed to hepatic steatosis in some individuals, potentially leading to misclassification of steatotic liver disease etiology. Finally, awareness of liver disease during study participation may have influenced PROs, particularly on liver-specific measures. Although the CLDQ has not been specifically validated in PWH, its domains are directly relevant to chronic liver disease across etiologies.

Conclusion

MASLD in PWH should be recognized not only as a risk factor for fibrosis and liver-related events but also as a condition associated with meaningful impairment in quality of life, particularly in physical and liver-related domains. Liver-specific HRQoL instruments provide complementary information to traditional clinical assessments and may play a central role in patient-centered MASLD care. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether improvements in metabolic health and liver disease translate into meaningful gains in HRQoL.
