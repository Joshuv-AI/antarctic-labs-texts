Training People to Reward Robots
Endong Sun, Yuqing Zhu, Matthew Howard

Abstract
Learning from demonstration (LfD) is a technique that allows expert teachers to teach task-oriented skills to robotic systems. However, the most effective way of guiding novice teachers to approach expert-level demonstrations quantitatively for specific teaching tasks remains an open question. To this end, this paper investigates the use of machine teaching (MT) to guide novice teachers to improve their teaching skills based on reinforcement learning from demonstration (RLfD) . The paper reports an experiment in which novices receive MT -derived guidance to train their ability to teach a given motor skill with only 8 8 demonstrations and generalise this to previously unseen ones. Results indicate that the MT -guidance not only enhances robot learning performance by 89 % 89\% on the training skill but also causes a 70 % 70\% improvement in robot learning performance on skills not seen by subjects during training. These findings highlight the effectiveness of MT -guidance in upskilling human teaching behaviours, ultimately improving demonstration quality in RLfD .



I
Introduction

Learning from demonstration (LfD)
has emerged as a highly effective method to teach robots new skills by leveraging human expertise, bypassing the complexities of traditional programming, and significantly enhancing robotic deployment efficiency and reliability. However, the efficacy and efficiency of
LfD
heavily depend on the quality and quantity of the demonstrations, which varies substantially with the teacher’s expertise
[
1
]
. This variation presents a significant challenge, as not all demonstrators are experts, and even experts cannot provide sufficient optimal demonstrations for every target skill
[
2
]
.

To address these limitations, several recent studies have investigated how concepts in
machine teaching (MT)

, a field of research concerned with devising a minimum amount of optimal data for machine learning models, can be combined with
LfD
to train novice teachers to provide demonstrations akin to those of experts.
For instance,
[
3
]
demonstrated that using
augmented reality (AR)
, along with information entropy inspired by
MT
, can reduce the number of demonstrations while maintaining teaching efficacy.
[
4
]
proposed an
MT
framework combined with robot
LfD
in which trainee teachers showed a 79% improvement in the accuracy of the taught robot motor skills. These studies show the potential of
MT
to transform novices into skilled teachers who can provide high-quality demonstrations with the least demonstration quantity.

However, prior
LfD
studies primarily focus on
supervised learning problems
, limiting their applicability. In general,
reinforcement learning (RL)
is better suited than supervised learning for tasks that extend over long horizons or those that involve adaptation to changes in dynamics
[
5
]
. To this end, this paper addresses the issue of training non-expert users to teach robots through
reinforcement learning from demonstration (RLfD)

by employing a training process derived from
MT
. It introduces a formulation of
MT
suitable for guiding users to provide the least amount of high-quality rewards in the context of robot
RLfD
, and a framework to deliver this guidance through scaffolding training. The effectiveness of the framework is evaluated through experiments in which novices are first trained to teach simple optimal control tasks where the true reward function is known. Then their overall improvement in teaching skill is measured on a new skill for which no teacher training has been given and no known rewards. Experimental results show that novices trained in this way produce demonstrations with
64\%
higher quality on skills that do not appear in training, leading to
70\%
improvement in learning outcomes for the robot. These findings suggest that
MT
could be used to upskill workers efficiently in jobs requiring interaction with robots that learn through
RL
, addressing the challenges of job displacement and the evolving needs of automation-driven industries
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
.

II
Problem Definition

II-A

RLfD

This paper addresses the issue of training novices to teach robot learners target skills through
RLfD
. Specifically, it is assumed that target skills are optimal with respect to some reward function and can be represented in the form

\displaystyle\bm{u}=\bm{\pi}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm arg~max}\limits_{\bm{u}}Q^{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{x},\bm{u})

(1)

where

Q^{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{x},\bm{u})=\bm{\omega}^{\top}\bm{\psi}(\bm{x},\bm{u})

(2)

is the action-value function. Here,
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{{P}}}
is the system state,
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{{Q}}}
is the action,
\bm{\psi}(\bm{x},\bm{u})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}
is a vector of basis functions and
\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}}
is a parameter vector corresponding to the value function for the target skill.

The robot can be taught the target skill by providing a reward
j_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
for the action
\bm{u}_{n}
taken by the robot when it encounters a state
\bm{x}_{n}
,
i.e.,

\bm{\mathcal{D}}=(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{u}_{n})_{n=1}^{{N}}
. The robot learns by estimating the parameters
\bm{\omega}
of the action-value function from
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
through an
RL
algorithm

\bm{\omega}=\mathcal{A}(\bm{\mathcal{D}}).

(3)

The efficacy and efficiency of this process are significantly influenced by the quality and quantity of
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
. Traditionally, reward functions are designed based on expert knowledge, however, the real-world deployment of such learners for use by non-experts, demands that means are found to
train
those users to produce high-quality teaching rewards.

II-B

Machine Teaching

Previous studies in
supervised
LfD

have shown that an effective way to train novice teachers is to find demonstrations that are close to optimal for an example task, and guide them toward using these in their teaching practice
[
4
]
. This paper adapts this approach to the
RLfD
setting, as illustrated in Fig.
1
.

In this framework,
MT
offers a quantitative way to find the minimal amount of optimal data
\bar{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}
for a given example skill. It can be expressed as a bi-level optimisation
[
9
]

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm arg~min}\limits_{\bm{\mathcal{D}}\in\bm{\mathfrak{D}}}\mathcal{\rho}(\bm{\omega},\bar{\bm{\omega}})

(4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\bm{\omega}=\mathcal{A}(\bm{\mathcal{D}})\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\mathcal{\varepsilon}(\bm{\mathcal{D}})\leq\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{max}

(5)

where
\mathcal{\rho}(\bm{\omega},\bar{\bm{\omega}})
is the
teaching risk
and
\mathcal{\varepsilon}(\bm{\mathcal{D}})
is the
teaching effort
. The teacher’s problem is to minimise the teaching risk, subject to a constraint on the effort, to derive a training set
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
from the space of possible data sets
\bm{\mathfrak{D}}
.
In the context of
RLfD
, the
learner’s
problem (
5
) is to find the optimal state-action value function for a given skill, based on the demonstration
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
under a certain effort budget
\mathcal{\varepsilon}_{max}
(e.g. least demonstrations).

Notably, an implicit assumption is that the model can only be trained via
demonstrations
rather than directly giving the target parameters
\bar{\bm{\omega}}
to the learner. This is because in many cases,

(i)

the true reward function
\bar{\jmath}
is
hard to define
or only known
implicitly
and

(ii)

the
MT
problem is computationally intractable

, particularly for complex skills where the value function lacks a closed-form solution.
The approach proposed here improves user teaching strategies when solutions to (
4
)-(
5
) are available. When such solutions do not exist, it still enables effective
RLfD
by leveraging users’ enhanced skills and their ability to generalise across
RL
tasks.

To this end, this paper tests the following hypotheses.

h_{1}

When teaching through
RLfD
, teaching quality is measurably improved when teachers are given guidance derived from the principles of
MT
.

h_{2}

Teachers’ improved teaching ability in one skill can be transferred to another.

Machine Teaching Training Framework

This section outlines the foundation of the
MT

III-A

MT
Formulation

The core of the proposed framework is to derive guidance from
MT
as a training signal for novice human teachers. For this, the following modelling assumptions and design choices are made.

III-A
1
Task Representation

To balance the demands of simplicity, robustness and scalability, this paper assumes target skills can be represented in the form

\displaystyle\bm{\pi}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm arg~max}\limits_{\bm{u}}{\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}j(\bm{x}_{k+1},\bm{u}_{k+1})}

(6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle j(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{u}_{k})=-\bm{\phi}_{k}^{\top}\bm{Q}\bm{\phi}_{k}-\bm{u}_{k}^{\top}\bm{R}\bm{u}_{k}

(7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{and}\quad

\displaystyle\bm{\phi}_{k+1}=\bm{A}\bm{\phi}_{k}+\bm{B}\bm{u}_{k}

(8)

with solution

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{k}=\bf{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}\bm{\phi}(\bm{x}_{k}).

(9)

Here,
\bm{x}_{k}
and
\bm{u}_{k}
represent the state and action at the
k
th time step, respectively,
\bm{\phi}(\bm{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{S}}
is a mapping from the state to a suitable feature space (with
\bm{\phi}_{k}=\bm{\phi}(\bm{x}_{k})
),
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
are matrices determining state transitions,
\gamma\in(0,1]
is a discount factor and
\bf{\bar{\bm{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathcal{{Q}}\mathcal{S})\times 1}
is a vector containing the policy parameters. Note that, the feature space may have arbitrary complexity and may include robot-specific features (such as kinematic mappings, see §
IV
).

Within this representation, skills differ based on reward function parameters (
\bm{Q}

and
\bm{R}
). In

III-A
2
Modelling the Learner

Numerous
RL
algorithms may be used by a robot learner to derive task-oriented policies. Here, the focus is on
RLfD
frameworks that

(i)

are sufficiently fast to enable an interactive experience for the user, and

(ii)

do not require the user to have any expert knowledge to specify the transition dynamics

. To this end, the
RL
algorithm used in the systems studied in this paper is
least squares policy iteration (LSPI)
which is widely used in robotic control systems
[
10
,
11
]
.

LSPI
works by iterating between

(i)

policy evaluation, and

(ii)

policy improvement

from an arbitrary initialisation. In its discrete-time, infinite horizon formulation, the state-action value function is defined as the expected cumulative discounted reward

\displaystyle Q^{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{x},\bm{u})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}j(\bm{x}_{k+1},\bm{u}_{k+1}).

(10)

In the
policy evaluation
step,
LSPI
approximates this using a function of the form

Q^{\bm{\pi}}(\bm{x},\bm{u})=\bm{\omega}^{\top}\bm{\psi}(\bm{x},\bm{u}).

(11)

For this, it makes a closed-form estimate based on minimising the temporal difference error

\displaystyle\bm{\omega}=(\bm{\Psi}^{\top}(\bm{\Psi}-\gamma\bm{\Psi}^{\prime}))^{-1}\bm{\Psi}^{\top}\bm{j}

(12)

\bm{\Psi}=\left(\bm{\psi}(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{u}_{i})\right)_{i=1}^{{N}}
,

\bm{\Psi}^{\prime}=\left(\bm{\psi}(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{i},\bm{\pi}(\bm{x}^{\prime}_{i}))\right)_{i=1}^{{N}}
,
both in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{T}\times{N}}
denotes the state the system transitions to when applying
\bm{u}_{n}
in
\bm{x}_{n}
. Here, and throughout the remainder of the paper, the shorthand notation
\bm{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{{N}}
is used to denote the rewards gathered into a column vector,
i.e.,

\bm{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{{N}})^{\top}
.

In the
policy improvement
step, the policy is estimated by substituting (
11
) into (
1
) and solving

\frac{\partial Q^{\bm{\pi}}}{\partial\bm{u}}=0.

(13)

Note that, this may be solved in closed form for an appropriate choice of
\bm{\psi}(\bm{x},\bm{u})
.

The iteration between policy evaluation and policy improvement continues until convergence is met (measured as a minimum change in the parameter
\bm{\omega}
).

III-A
3
Modelling the Teacher

The aim of the teacher is to produce a data set
\bm{\mathcal{D}}
that enables the learner to estimate the desired value function and learn the target skill. Therefore, the risk function for
MT
is

\mathcal{\rho}(\bm{\omega},\bar{\bm{\omega}})=\|\mathbf{\bm{\omega}-\bar{\bm{\omega}}}\|_{2}

(14)

i.e.,
the
\mathnormal{\ell_{2}}
-norm of the difference between
\bm{\omega}
and
\bar{\bm{\omega}}
.
By providing reward demonstrations reflective of the target reward function
\bar{\jmath}(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{u}_{k})
, it is expected that
\bm{\omega}
will approach
\bar{\bm{\omega}}
, thereby leading to better learning outcomes in terms of the policy parameters
\bf{\tilde{\bm{\theta}}}
.

Moreover, in line with the formulation of
MT
outlined in §
II
, the teacher must teach the target skill with a fixed budget of teaching effort. In the following, the teaching budget (
II-B
) is set as

\mathcal{\varepsilon}(\bm{\mathcal{D}})={N}_{TD}

(15)

where
{N}_{TD}
is the
teaching dimension
for the problem, defined as the minimum number of training items required to teach the target model to the learner. For the learner defined in §
III-A2
, the teaching dimension is
\mathcal{T}
,
i.e.,
the dimension of the features
\bm{\psi}

[
12
]
.

III-B

MT
Problem

Combining (
12
), (
14
) and (
15
), the optimal data for the problems considered in this paper, therefore, is

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm arg~min}\limits_{\bm{\mathcal{D}}\in\bm{\mathfrak{D}}}\|\mathbf{\bm{\omega}-\bar{\bm{\omega}}}\|_{2}

(16)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.\quad}\bm{\omega}=(\bm{\Psi}^{\top}(\bm{\Psi}-\gamma\bm{\Psi}^{\prime}))^{-1}\bm{\Psi}^{\top}\bm{j}

(17)

\displaystyle\mathrm{and}\quad\mathcal{\varepsilon}(\bm{\mathcal{D}})=\mathcal{T}.

(18)

Denoting the ideal reward demonstrations as
\bar{\bm{\jmath}}
, the parameter learnt by
LSPI
from ideal teaching is

\bar{\bm{\omega}}=(\bm{\Psi}^{\top}(\bm{\Psi}-\gamma\bm{\Psi}^{\prime}))^{-1}\bm{\Psi}^{\top}\bar{\bm{\jmath}}.

(19)

Noting that the state-action pairs
(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{u}_{n})_{n=1}^{{N}}
are given, and noting the sub-multiplicative property of matrix norms
[
13
]

[
4

\|\bm{\omega}-\bar{\bm{\omega}}\|_{2}\propto\|\bm{j}-\bar{\bm{\jmath}}\|_{2}.

(20)

Therefore, in the setting considered in this paper, training users to provide rewards that are close to the ideal rewards evaluated on the sample state action pairs is expected to lead to high-quality estimates of the value function, and in turn, improved outcomes in terms of the control policy.

Research in human computer interfaces has shown that visualising feedback improves learning by reducing cognitive load and facilitating quick adjustments
[

\tilde{\jmath}

\bar{\jmath}
as derived from
MT

III-C
2
Scaffolding Training

The approach of progressively increasing task complexity, often referred to as
scaffolding training
or
curriculum learning
, is particularly effective in educating novices
[
17
,
18
]
. Here, the scaffolding training process ensures a structured progression of how reward demonstrations impact behaviour. As illustrated in Fig.
3
and the video
2
2

2

The video is submitted as supplementary material.
, it consists of the following phases.

P3

Understanding reward magnitude consistency
: Participants learn that for same-magnitude actions directed at the target, states equidistant from the target should receive equal rewards.

P4

Identifying the action-direction-reward relationship
: Keeping state and action magnitude constant while varying action directions, participants discover how reward assignment should vary. (Counterintuitively, in the target-reaching task considered here, the reward does not depend on action directions.)

P5

Identifying the state-reward relationship
: Participants learn how increasing the state (distance from the target) while keeping actions fixed affects reward values.

P6

Identifying the action-magnitude-reward relationship
: Participants learn how increasing the action magnitude while keeping the state as maximum affects reward values. Note that, the final training demo in P
5
is identical to the first step in P
6
, ensuring a smooth transition before introducing action magnitude variability (P
6
). After P
6
, participants understand that reward is the sum of independent contributions from state and action magnitudes. (Counterintuitively, the 8th demonstration in P
6
reaches the target with the largest action but receives the lowest (
-100
) reward. This is because the reward discourages large actions, ensuring a smoother trajectory and preventing potential damage to the robot)

P7

Developing robust reward assignment
: To enhance their ability to assign rewards without predefined rules, participants practice inferring rewards for randomly selected state-action pairs, strengthening their implicit understanding before encountering novel tasks.

Note that, the generic nature of this curriculum makes it easily adaptable to training reward assignments for a wide variety of
RLfD

V
CONCLUSIONS

This paper proposes a scaffolding,
MT
-based framework to train novices to teach robots skills through
RLfD
. The findings demonstrate that guidance significantly improves demonstration quality in the skills used for training the teacher and, critically, causes enhanced teaching quality in new, unseen tasks for which training has not been received. Improvements to robots’ learning outcomes are particularly pronounced in long-horizon tasks. This may have far-ranging impact since it demonstrates that novice teachers can be upskilled very quickly without engaging in high-level, long-term education programmes. In future work, the framework will be extended to the teaching of more complex tasks, for instance, by using local optimal control methods
[
19
]
.
