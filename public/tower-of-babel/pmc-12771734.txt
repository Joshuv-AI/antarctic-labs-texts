A new predictor of disease activity in Takayasu’s arteritis patients: pericarotid fat density
Authors: Yu Lan, Ziwen Xie, Dongbo Li, Yuxue Dang, Li Sun, Huachen Ma, Yang Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore the incremental value of carotid pericarotid fat density (PFD) in the assessment of disease activity in Takayasu’s arteritis (TA).MethodsSixty-one consecutive patients with TA from January 2010 to December 2021 were included in this study. Demographic and clinical characteristics, acute-phase reactants (APRs) and CTA indicator parameters were recorded. The effectiveness of CTA parameters including PFD in determining disease activity was assessed, with Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) used as a reference. A disease activity evaluation model was constructed to compare the benefits of the new model including PFD with ESR and Kerr scores by evaluating the consistency index (C-index), the net reclassification index (NRI) and the integrated discriminant index (IDI).ResultsIn total, 29 (47.54%) cases showed active disease. The C-index of ESR to indicate disease activity was 0.778 (95% CI: 0.635, 0.874). The new activity assessment model combining ESR, maximal wall thickness and PFD showed significant improvement in C-index over the ESR method (0.902 vs. 0.778, P < 0.01; NRI 0.948, P < 0.01; and IDI 0.204, P < 0.01). In addition, the new model demonstrated a significant superiority over the Kerr score in terms of the C-index (0.902 vs. 0.771, P < 0.05), NRI (1.142, 95% CI: 0.730, 1.556, P < 0.01) and IDI (0.300, 95% CI: 0.187, 0.423, P < 0.01).ConclusionsIn this cohort, CTA-derived perivascular fat density (PFD) showed association with disease activity in Takayasu’s arteritis patients and may provide complementary information to conventional assessment methods, suggesting its potential role in refining risk stratification.Keywords: Takayasu’s arteritis, Disease activity, Multidetector CT angiography, Biomarkers

Introduction

The pathological changes observed in Takayasu’s arteritis (TA) primarily arise from the infiltration of CD4 + T cells and macrophages into the arterial wall, along with neovascularization, granuloma formation, and vessel wall swelling [1]. Persistent inflammation and subsequent remodeling of the arterial wall can gradually result in stenosis, occlusion, or dilation of the affected vessels, ultimately leading to ischemia and infarction of the affected organs. Early initiation of appropriate treatment can help mitigate the inflammatory response, slow disease progression, and reduce the risk of cardiovascular complications. Therefore, assessing inflammatory activity plays a crucial role in the clinical management of TA patients [2, 3].

The current widely used clinical assessment method for disease activity in TA was developed by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) in 1994 [4]. It relies on clinical features, laboratory finds, and angiographic results to calculate the Kerr score [4]. However, the clinical features incorporated in the Kerr score exhibit a high degree of variability, with more than 50% of changes in acute phase reactant not consistently correlating with disease activity [5]. Another scoring system, known as the ITAS 2010, was proposed by Indian scholars and includes 44 items, with 33 characteristics related to the cardiovascular system. The system introduced the first quantitative scoring approach for assessing disease activity [6]. However, the ITAS scoring system lacks the evaluation of image parameters.

Vascular imaging plays a critical role in the diagnosis and assessment of disease status in arteritis. Commonly used imaging modalities in clinical practice include angiography, MRI, ultrasound, and PET/CT. In the early stages of inflammation, TA may only exhibit thickening of the vessel wall, resulting in negative angiographic findings [3]. MRI has been utilized for the diagnosis and assessment of disease activity in TA, however, it can be time-consuming, has limitations in depicting calcifications, and may yield false positives in diagnosing occlusive lesions [7]. Ultrasound plays a significant role in evaluating lesion in the carotid and axillary arteries, and the thickness of the intima media can serve as an effective indicator for assessing TA disease activity. However, ultrasound requires skilled operators for immediate examination and diagnosis [8]. PET/CT demonstrates high sensitivity in assessing inflammatory activity in TA, but its specificity can vary significantly. Moreover, PET/CT has limitations in displaying small vessels due to its lower spatial resolution [9]. Although CTA carries the risk of ionizing radiation, contrast agent allergy, and renal toxicity, its exceptional spatial and temporal resolution offers significant advantages for patients undergoing CTA examinations [10]. It not only enables the visualization of changes in the arterial lumen but also allows for the assessment of structures within and around the arterial wall [10]. In the “Chinese multidisciplinary recommendations on the chronic diseases management throughout the entire course of Takayasu arteritis”, CTA is recommended for the systematic evaluation of large and medium arteritis, and its ability to assess vascular structure surpasses that of MRA and PET/CT [11]. Previous studies have indicated that wall thickening, wall strengthening, and wall calcification observed in CTA images are specific manifestations of artery wall involvement in TA patients. These features may coexist with lumen stenosis, occlusion or aneurysm formation [12–14]. Additionally, Researches have demonstrated that the measurement of peri-carotid fat density in CTAs can provide insights into the inflammatory state surrounding the vessel wall [15–17]. Zhang et al. recently discovered an association between carotid pericarotid fat density (PFD), assessed through CTA, and cerebral small vascular disease and intraplaque hemorrhage in the carotid artery. They speculated that these associations were influenced by the transmission of inflammatory mediators [15, 16].

Consequently, we hypothesize that the density of adipose tissue surrounding the carotid artery could potentially reflect active carotid wall inflammation in patients with arteritis, thereby enhancing the diagnostic effectiveness of disease activity assessment. In this study, we aimed to explore various parameters in CTA that can be employed to assess active TA disease. Furthermore, we sought to developed a simple, quantifiable, and validated model based on CTA findings for the assessment of disease activity. Additionally, we compared the diagnostic efficacy of the Kerr score a current scoring system for identifying active disease, with a novel model that integrates different CTA parameters.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

We selected consecutive patients with arteritis who presented to our hospital between January 2010 and December 2021 and met the 1990 American College of Rheumatology’s TA criteria for the diagnosis of arteritis [18]. Inclusion criteria were as follow: Inclusion criteria were as follows: patients exhibiting the manifestation of type I arteritis (Involving the aortic arch and its branches above) and who had undergone a combined head and neck CTA examination. Exclusion criteria: (1) acute or chronic inflammatory diseases; (2) canc er; (3) Poor image quality or partial image missing.

This retrospective study was approved by ethics committee of Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University (No.2023PS658K), and informed consent was waived due to its retrospective nature.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

We recorded demographic data and clinical characteristics of the enrolled TA patients, including age, sex, disease duration, symptoms at the visit (systemic/vascular/ systemic and vascular), and biochemical tests such as Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) and C-reaction protein (CPR). The normal value for ESR is l-20 mm/h, and the normal range for CRP is 0–8 mg/L. Systemic symptoms included fever, musculoskeletal pain (when no other cause was identified), and appearance of vascular symptoms and/or signs, indicating vascular ischemia or inflammation, such as claudication, diminished or absent pulse, bruit, vascular pain (carotodynia), or asymmetrical blood pressure in the upper or lower limbs (or both).

Due to the chronic nature of the disease and the changing inflammatory status of the patients, many of them underwent multiple evaluations. All patients included in this study underwent combined head and neck CTA.

The Physician Global Assessment (PGA) was used as the gold standard for assessing TA disease activity [6]. The PGA criteria encompassed clinical symptoms, laboratory tests, imaging findings, and recent treatment outcomes [19]. The total score ranged from 0 to 10, where a score of 0 indicates clinical remission, and an increasing score indicates active disease [20]. Two rheumatologists independently evaluated each patient, and in case of discordance, consensus was reached through discussion. Additionally, Kerr scores were recorded for all patients.

CTA image acquisition protocol

Carotid artery CTA examinations were conducted using Ingenuity CT (Philips Healthcare), and iCT (Philips Healthcare). Carotid CTA scanning parameters were set as follows: tube voltage of 120 kV, pitch of 0.984, reconstructed slice thickness of 1.25 mm, reconstructed slice interval of 0.625 mm, and rotation time of 0.5 s. We acquired and analyzed the carotid CTA images, specifically focusing on the involved artery, which could be the left common carotid artery (LCCA) or the right common carotid artery (RCCA).

The common carotid artery was selected as the index vessel for several reasons. First, in accordance with the inclusion criteria, all enrolled patients had Type I TA, which involves the aortic arch and its branches, making the carotid artery a representative and commonly affected site. Second, the carotid artery, due to its superficial and relatively straight course, allows for highly reproducible and standardized measurements of both wall thickness and pericarotid fat density on CTA images. This is more challenging in other frequently involved vessels, such as the subclavian arteries, due to their anatomic curvature and proximity to bony structures. Finally, a growing body of research has established the carotid artery as a valid window for assessing systemic vascular inflammation, as changes in its perivascular adipose tissue reflect the overall inflammatory burden.

Analysis of arterial wall parameters

The recorded parameters included arterial wall thickness, intensity of arterial wall enhancement, and the presence of lumen abnormalities. The assessment of CTA imaging findings prioritized the side with more severe involvement of the common carotid artery. The ‘more severely involved’ side was objectively defined as the common carotid artery (LCCA or RCCA) with the greater maximal wall thickness as measured on the arterial phase axial image.

The thickest part of the arterial vessel wall was determined through a subjective and collaborative process involving both observers. Each observer independently performed measurements. The arterial vessel wall thickness was measured on the arterial phase axial image, and the maximum value from the two measurements was recorded. The CT value of the vessel wall was measured on delay phase axial image. A circular region of interest (ROI) was placed in the same location as the maximum wall thickness measurement on the corresponding image slice. The size of the ROI was manually defined by the observer, based on the thickness of the vessel wall, to encompass the entire thickened vessel wall. It was preferred to select a larger measured area of interest as long as it did not exceed the thickness of the vessel wall (see Fig. 1A for reference). Lumen abnormalities were assessed and included the degree of lumen stenosis, such as the presence of > 70% stenosis, as well as the presence of arterial occlusion and aneurysms.

Quantification of pericarotid adipose tissue density

The density of the pericarotid adipose tissue surrounding the carotid artery was quantified using a dedicated software called Perivascular Fat Analysis Tool, developed by Shukun Technology, in Beijing, China. Pericarotid adipose tissue was defined as the adipose tissue located within a radial distance from the outer vessel wall equal to the average diameter of the target vessel (refer to Fig. 1B for visualization). We employed a previously established measure of peri-coronary fat, which encompassed all the voxels within the range of − 190 HU and − 30 HU located in the target area. Semi-automatic measurements were utilized to track the stenosis and obtain the pericarotid fat density at the thickest part of the vessel wall on arterial phase axial images [21]. The measurement process for pericarotid fat density is depicted in Fig. 2. The arterial phase was chosen for PFD measurement to ensure temporal consistency with the assessment of wall thickness and luminal abnormalities, which are optimally evaluated in this phase. Furthermore, the perivascular fat analysis software algorithm is designed to exclude the contrast-enhanced vessel lumen itself, mitigating direct partial volume effects.

Two cardiovascular radiologists D.B.L (with 10 years of cardiovascular imaging experience) and Y.X.D. (with 8 years of cardiovascular imaging experience), independently analyzed the data without knowledge of the patients’ clinical history or disease activity status. Any discrepancies between the two radiologists’ measurements were resolved by consensus. The average of the two measurements from the two observers was used for subsequent data analysis.

Statistical analysis

The data that followed a normal distribution was presented as mean ± standard deviation. The independent sample t test was used to compare the inactive group and the active group. Non-normally distributed data were described as median (range of quartiles [IQR]), and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for comparison. Count data were presented as frequency and percentage, and chi-square test was used for group comparisons. The ability to evaluate the original ESR model and the CTA modified model expressed as the C-index, was determined using receiver operating curve (ROC) analysis. The classification improvement of net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were recorded to assess the classification and reclassification performance of different diagnostic models, based on the Pencina et al. method [22].

All P-values were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. The statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 26.0; IBM, Inc., Chicago, IL) and R (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) software packages. Graphs were generated using Graph Pad Prism 8.0 (Graph Pad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA).

Inconsistencies between the different disease activity assessment methods

Based on PGA, 29 out of 61 (47.54%) TA patients were classified as having active disease, while the remaining 32 (52.46%) patients had inactive disease. Notably, the values of APRs, specially ESR (25.00 [13.00–56.00] vs. 6.00 [4.50–12.00] mm/h, P < 0.01) and CRP (17.50 [5.40–30.50] vs. 4.45 [2.00–7.70] mg/l, P < 0.01) were significantly higher in the active group compared to the inactive group (Table 1).

Among the patients classified as having active disease based on PGA, all 29 cases (100.00%) had a Kerr score equal to or > 2. On the other hand, among those with inactive disease, 29 out of 32 patients (90.63%) had a Kerr score of 2 or more.

The characteristics of CTA in patients with active and inactive TA

There were no significant differences observed between the two groups in terms of the involved artery, degree of lumen stenosis, presence of arterial occlusion, and presence of aneurysms (P > 0.05) (Table 2).

An optimized evaluation model for TA disease activity

To systematically evaluate the incremental value of CTA parameters beyond ESR, we constructed a series of nested models based on clinical relevance and prior literature. Model 1 combined ESR and CRP with PFD to test if fat density added value to standard biomarkers. Model 2 combined ESR and CRP with maximal wall thickness to test the contribution of a key structural CTA parameter. Model 3 replaced CRP with the CT attenuation of the wall, testing a different imaging biomarker of inflammation. The superior performance of Model 4, which combined ESR, maximal wall thickness, and PFD, indicates that PFD provides unique information related to perivascular inflammation that is not fully captured by wall thickness alone or by combining ESR with CRP. The direct comparison of C-index, NRI, and IDI between these models (Tables 3 and 4) underscores the specific contribution of PFD to the final optimal model.

The C-index of ESR to identify active TA was determined to be 0.778 (95% CI: 0.635, 0.874), which served as a reference for further analysis. It was observed that no single parameter, including CRP and CTA parameters, could individually enhance the C-index of ESR by at least 0.01 (Table 3). Subsequently, an optimized disease activity assessment model was developed by combining the aforementioned parameters, based on ESR (Table 4). The best model was achieved by integrating three markers: ESR, maximal wall thickness and PFD. We conducted a multivariate analysis of disease activity in order to develop a nomogram that can effectively identify active TA (refer to Fig. 4). The calibration of the nomogram was excellent, as indicated by a non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow test (P = 0.72), suggesting no significant deviation between predicted and observed probabilities of disease activity. The C-index of this model improved significantly compared to ESR alone (0.902 vs. 0.778, P < 0.01), and the sensitivity of the model also showed a significantly improvement (0.931 vs. 0.759) (Table 3). The improvement was confirmed by positive reclassification (NRI, 0.948, 95% CI: 0.506, 1.391, P < 0.01) and an increased sensitivity (IDI, 0.204, 95% CI: 0.092, 0.317, P < 0.01) (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, a new diagnostic model was developed by analyzing various parameters in the combined head and neck CTA images of patients with Type I Takayasu’s arteritis. The aim was to enhance the diagnostic efficiency of the traditional model in evaluating disease activity. Evaluating disease activity in TA patients is crucial for guiding clinical treatment, yet there is a lack of specific indicators and evaluation systems [23]. The Kerr score is a commonly used in clinical practice to assess disease activity, however, several studies have suggested that it underestimates the proportion of active TA patients [24, 25]. Histopathological examination revealed active vascular inflammatory changes in 44% of patients classified as having no active disease according to the Kerr score [4].

The pathological alterations in TA primarily involve inflammatory changes and fibrosis of the vascular wall, resulting in lumen stenosis, occlusion or dilation. Therefore, determining disease activity should rely on biochemical indicators and imaging parameters that can reflect inflammation and vascular structure. This study also confirmed the incremental diagnostic value of the parameters such as arterial wall thickness and pericarotid adipose tissue density obtained from CTA images.

Previous studies have demonstrated that PFD can be used to assess arterial wall inflammation [15–17]. Under normal physiological conditions, perivascular adipose tissue (PVAT) secretes protective adipokines and bioactive molecules such as adiponectin and NO to maintain vascular homeostasis. however, in pathological conditions, PVAT-derived adipokines, chemokines, and cytokines target various cell types including endothelial (ECs), macrophages, T cells, and vascular smooth muscle cells, leading to endothelial dysfunction, immune cell infiltration, and smooth muscle cell migration and proliferation [26].The interaction between the vascular wall and surrounding adipose tissue is a bidirectional process [27]. The inflammatory bioactive factors released by the vascular wall can infiltrate into PVAT, inhibit the differentiation of adipocyte precursors, reducing intracellular lipid content, and causing a transformation from a lipid to a water phase. The change in PVAT phenotype and density is reflected in an increase in attenuation value on CT images, forming the basis of PVAT imaging [28]. In this study, the sensitivity of PFD in distinguishing TA activity was 93.1%, which can be attributed to its ability to detect peripheral adipose tissue inflammation caused by vessel wall inflammation, as demonstrated in previous studies on coronary arteries and aorta in patients with arteritis [29]. Elevated HU values in perivascular fat reflect inflammatory phenotypic changes, including adipocyte dedifferentiation, reduced lipid content, and increased water content due to cytokine-mediated inflammation. This is consistent with the concept of “adipose tissue remodeling” in the context of vascular inflammation, as supported by recent literature [26].

Previous studies have indicated that vascular wall thickening with enhancement and low attenuation loops on delay-phase images is correlated with TA disease activity [30]. In CTA imaging, wall thickening without enhancement does not appear to be associated with active disease. The utility of vascular wall enhancement in identifying active disease on CTA remains controversial. Clinical assessment and APR have shown that the presence of low attenuation rings is associated with 100% specificity for disease activity but lower sensitivity (34–57%) [13, 31]. Following enhancement, the sensitivity and specificity were 95% and 100%, respectively [13]. Vascular wall thickening alone did not demonstrate an association with increased APR [13].

To enhance the accuracy of assessing TA disease activity, we optimized a simplified, quantifiable and validated CTA-based model. Currently, there are no new biomarkers available to replace ESR and CRP for disease activity assessment [32]. However, various factors, including age, gender, smoking status, blood pressure, disease status and inflammatory status of the patient, can influence CRP and ESR levels [33, 34]. Previous studies have confirmed that CRP is less sensitive than ESR in assessing disease activity, with a sensitivity of 65% compared to 76% [35, 36]. Therefore, we used ESR as reference indicator. In the present study, the C-index for ESR to identify TA disease activity was 0.778 (95% CI: 0.635, 0.874). In addition, although CTA parameters and biomarkers exhibited significant differences between the active and inactive groups, none of them individually improved the C-index of ESR in evaluating disease activity. These results suggest that the value of any single biomarker or imaging indicator in identifying disease activity is limited, highlighting the need to consider multiple parameters comprehensively. We further evaluated the efficiency of several models to improve the diagnostic efficacy of ESR by combining multiple CTA parameters and biological markers. The optimal model includes the sum of PDF, maximum wall thickness and ESR, with a C-index value of 0.902 (95% CI: 0.798, 0.963) with a 17.2%improvement in sensitivity. Importantly, the new model also demonstrated significant advantages over Kerr scores, mitigating the influence of a single indicators affected by other factors such as infection or tumor.

Having established the diagnostic value of our CTA-based model, it is instructive to contextualize these findings by comparing them with established imaging techniques for assessing vasculitis activity, such as Positron Emission Tomography-Computed Tomography (PET-CT) and Contrast-Enhanced Magnetic Resonance Imaging (CE-MRI). PET-CT is highly sensitive for detecting metabolic activity in inflamed vessel walls and is often considered a reference standard, but its utility is limited by high cost, radiation exposure, and limited spatial resolution for small vessels [9, 25]. CE-MRI excels at depicting mural edema and enhancement without ionizing radiation, yet it can be time-consuming and less accessible [7]. Our model, derived from routinely acquired CTA, offers a distinct advantage by leveraging existing imaging data to provide a quantitative measure of activity. While it does not directly assess metabolism like PET-CT or edema like CE-MRI, the PFD component captures a downstream effect of vascular inflammation on the perivascular environment. Therefore, our approach may serve as a practical and complementary tool, particularly in settings where PET-CT or CE-MRI are not readily available, or for patients in whom serial follow-up with reduced cumulative radiation is desirable.

Our study focused on the carotid artery for quantifying PFD. While TA often involves the aortic arch and subclavian arteries, the choice of the carotid artery is well-justified. It is a frequently affected vessel in the most common form of the disease (Type I) and serves as an accessible and reliable site for standardized imaging biomarker quantification. The reproducibility of measurements in the carotid artery is superior to that in more tortuous vessels like the subclavian artery. Furthermore, the concept of the “vascular unit” posits that inflammation in one segment of the arterial tree is often reflective of a systemic process. Therefore, the inflammatory status of the carotid artery, as captured by PFD, is likely indicative of the overall disease activity in TA patients with aortic arch involvement.

An important consideration for the clinical application of PFD is its specificity. Elevated PFD is a marker of perivascular inflammation and is not specific to Takayasu’s arteritis. Other conditions, particularly atherosclerosis, can also lead to increased PFD in the carotid region [15, 17]. In clinical practice, distinguishing TA from atherosclerosis relies on a combination of factors. TA typically affects younger, predominantly female patients and demonstrates long, smooth, concentric wall thickening often affecting the entire vascular circumference. In contrast, atherosclerotic plaques are more common in older patients with cardiovascular risk factors and are often eccentric and focal. Therefore, the interpretation of an elevated PFD must be integrated with the patient’s clinical profile, demographic characteristics, and the morphological pattern of vascular involvement on CTA to accurately attribute the inflammation to TA activity.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design and restricted predictor variables may limit the generalizability of our findings. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts should incorporate a broader range of predictors to validate the observed association between PFD and disease activity. Secondly, PFD was measured on arterial-phase images. While the software excludes the vessel lumen, we cannot entirely rule out potential influences from beam-hardening artifacts or partial volume effects from the adjacent enhanced wall on the immediate perivascular fat measurements. Future studies could compare PFD measurements between non-contrast and arterial phases to further validate this approach. Finally, we must acknowledge a potential limitation in our reference standard. The Physician Global Assessment (PGA), while a comprehensive and clinically accepted composite tool, inherently incorporates imaging findings. This could introduce a degree of incorporation bias, potentially inflating the apparent diagnostic performance of our CTA-based model. An ideal, purely non-imaging based gold standard for disease activity in TA remains elusive. Future studies using independent clinical endpoints or alternative imaging standards (e.g., PET-CT) for validation would be valuable to confirm our findings.

Conclusions

In this cohort, CTA-derived PFD showed association with disease activity in TA patients and may provide complementary information to conventional assessment methods, suggesting its potential role in refining risk stratification. Potential clinical applications could include serving as a supplementary tool in cases where clinical suspicion of active disease is high despite normal ESR/CRP levels, or for objectively monitoring treatment response over time using a quantifiable imaging biomarker. Future prospective studies should validate these potential use-cases.
