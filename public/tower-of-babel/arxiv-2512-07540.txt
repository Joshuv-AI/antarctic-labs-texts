Minimum Bayes Risk Decoding for Error Span Detection in Reference-Free Automatic Machine Translation Evaluation
Boxuan Lyu, Haiyue Song, Hidetaka Kamigaito, Chenchen Ding, Hideki Tanaka, Masao Utiyama, Kotaro Funakoshi, Manabu Okumura

Abstract
Error Span Detection (ESD) extends automatic machine translation (MT) evaluation by localizing translation errors and labeling their severity. Current generative ESD methods typically use Maximum a Posteriori (MAP) decoding, assuming that the model-estimated probabilities are perfectly correlated with similarity to the human annotation, but we often observe a higher likelihood assigned to an incorrect annotation than to the human one. In contrast, we apply Minimum Bayes Risk (MBR) decoding to generative ESD. We use a sentence- or span-level similarity function for MBR decoding, which selects candidate hypotheses based on their approximate similarity to the human annotation. Experimental results on the WMT24 Metrics Shared Task show that MBR decoding significantly improves span-level performance and generally matches or outperforms MAP at the system and sentence levels. To reduce the computational cost of MBR decoding, we further distill its decisions into a model decoded via greedy search, removing the inference-time latency bottleneck. † † This work was done during the first author’s internship at National Institute of Information and Communications Technology, Kyoto, Japan. 1 1 1 Our models and code are available at: https://github.com/vlaks425/MBR-ESD

Introduction

Automatic metrics for machine translation (MT) provide a fast proxy for costly human evaluation.
From early n-gram metrics such as BLEU (
Papineni et al. 2002
) to learned metrics such as COMET (
Rei et al. 2022
), correlations with human judgments have improved at the system and sentence levels.
However, most metrics still output a single scalar score per sentence (
Freitag et al. 2024
), hiding diagnostic information such as error location and severity, which are crucial for model analysis and post-editing.

Error Span Detection (ESD) extends MT evaluation by asking metrics not only to score a translation but also to identify error spans and their severities.
ESD has been part of the WMT Quality Estimation shared task since 2023 (
Blain et al. 2023
).
Current ESD methods fall into two paradigms: token-classification approaches, such as xCOMET (
Guerreiro et al. 2024
), which treat ESD as per-token labeling, and generative approaches, such as GEMBA-MQM (
Kocmi and Federmann 2023
), which prompt large language models (LLMs) to output MQM-style (
Freitag et al. 2021a
) error spans in zero- or few-shot settings.

Current state-of-the-art LLM-based generative ESD methods (
Freitag et al. 2024
) typically rely on Maximum a Posteriori (MAP) decoding.
The underlying assumption of MAP decoding is that the model-estimated probabilities are perfectly correlated with similarity to the human annotation.
However, this assumption does not always hold.
As illustrated in Table
1
,
we observe instances where the model log-likelihood is higher for an incorrect annotation produced by MAP decoding than for the correct human annotation, signifying a model error.
This discrepancy, consistent with findings in other text generation tasks (
Freitag et al. 2022a
;
Liu et al. 2022
), leads us to question the optimality of MAP as a decoding objective for ESD.

Minimum Bayes Risk (MBR) decoding is a reranking method widely studied in MT (
Kumar and Byrne 2004
;
Tromble et al. 2008
) and speech recognition (
Stolcke et al. 1997
;
Goel and Byrne 2000
).
In this work, we apply MBR decoding to the ESD model with the objective of maximizing utility against the human annotation, independent of the model-estimated probabilities.
Since the human annotation is unavailable at test time, we approximate it with a set of model-generated “support hypotheses” and select the hypothesis with the highest expected utility against this set.

We define the utility function as a similarity function over hypotheses pairs and consider both sentence-level and span-level variants.
The proposed sentence-level utility function measures similarity based on the difference between the severity-weighted error span counts of the two hypotheses.
For the span-level utility function, we examine the official
\textsc{F1}(\cdot,\cdot)
metric (referred to as the “meta-metric” in prior work) from the WMT QE Shared Task (
Blain et al. 2023
).
However, we identify a defect in
\textsc{F1}(\cdot,\cdot)
related to its handling of empty (i.e., error-free) annotations, which prevents it from performing soft evaluation.
We therefore propose
\textsc{SoftF1}(\cdot,\cdot)
, a novel utility function that enables soft evaluation by addressing this defect.

Experimental results on the WMT24 Metrics Shared Task show that our MBR decoding, when instantiated with the proposed
\textsc{SoftF1}(\cdot,\cdot)
utility function and applied with sufficiently large candidate sets, significantly improves span-level performance and generally matches or outperforms the MAP decoding baseline at the system and sentence levels; partial results are shown in Figure
1
.
To avoid the high computational cost of MBR decoding, we further distill its decisions into a model decoded via greedy search, removing the inference-time latency bottleneck.

2
Related Work

To our knowledge, our work is the first to apply reranking techniques to MT metrics.

2.1
MT Automatic Metrics

Early MT metrics such as BLEU (
Papineni et al. 2002
), METEOR (
Banerjee and Lavie 2005
), and chrF (
Popović 2015
) rely on surface-level matching and output a single scalar score per sentence.
Neural metrics, including BertScore (
Zhang et al. 2020
), BLEURT (
Sellam et al. 2020
), COMET (
Rei et al. 2020
), and MetricX (
Juraska et al. 2024
), show higher correlations with human judgments (
Freitag et al. 2022b
;
Freitag et al. 2023
;
Freitag et al. 2024
) but usually still produce sentence-level scores only, limiting their usefulness for applications such as post-editing.

As human evaluation has shifted from scalar scores (e.g., Direct Assessment) to multidimensional annotations such as MQM (
Freitag et al. 2021a
), some metrics now target finer-grained evaluation, including ESD.
xCOMET (
Guerreiro et al. 2024
) predicts error spans via token classification, while GEMBA-MQM (
Kocmi and Federmann 2023
) and AutoMQM (
Fernandes et al. 2023
) use generative models.
Generative metrics have attracted attention for their flexibility—often requiring only prompt changes—and for their competitive performance (
Lu et al. 2025
;
Huang et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2023
;
Baswani et al. 2023
;
Feng et al. 2025
;
Freitag et al. 2024
).

2.2
Evaluation Metrics for ESD

The alignment between model-predicted ESD annotations and the human annotation can be evaluated at the system, sentence, and span levels.

Soft Pairwise Accuracy (SPA) (
Thompson et al. 2024
) and Pairwise Accuracy with Tie Calibration (
{\text{Acc}}_{\text{eq}}^{*}
) (
Deutsch et al. 2023
) measure ranking consistency.
Specifically, SPA operates at the system level by evaluating pairwise rankings of different MT systems based on aggregated scores, whereas
{\text{Acc}}_{\text{eq}}^{*}
functions at the sentence level, assessing the relative ordering of individual translation sentence pairs.

At the span level, to our knowledge, the only existing metric for ESD is the official metric of the WMT QE Shared Task (
Blain et al. 2023
),
\textsc{F1}(\cdot,\cdot)
, which computes character-level precision and recall between a hypothesis and a reference annotation.
This metric can also be used as an MBR utility function when the reference is a support hypothesis.

However, we identify a critical defect in
\textsc{F1}(\cdot,\cdot)
, namely its handling of empty annotations.
As detailed in Appendix A.1, when the reference annotation is empty,
\textsc{F1}(\cdot,\cdot)
returns its minimum value for any non-empty hypothesis, regardless of how minor the error might be.
This is problematic for two reasons.
First, as an evaluation metric, it fails to provide a soft penalty when the human annotation is error-free.
Second, as an MBR utility function, it assigns minimum utility to any non-empty candidate whenever the support hypothesis is empty.
Given the diversity of support hypotheses (
Kamigaito et al. 2025
) and the growing prevalence of perfect translations (
Freitag et al. 2024
), cases with empty human annotations or empty support hypotheses are likely to be frequent.
We therefore regard this behavior as a significant limitation, which motivates our soft alternative,
\textsc{SoftF1}(\cdot,\cdot)
, introduced in Section
5.2.3
.

2.3
MBR Decoding

MBR decoding has a long history in speech recognition (
Stolcke et al. 1997
;
Goel and Byrne 2000
), word alignment (
Kumar and Byrne 2002
), and statistical MT (
Kumar and Byrne 2004
;
Tromble et al. 2008
).
Recently, several studies have revisited MBR decoding for text generation, demonstrating promising results (
Wu et al. 2025
;
Heineman et al. 2024
;
Lyu et al. 2025
).
These studies suggest that MBR decoding can help overcome some of the limitations inherent in MAP.
Furthermore, the properties (
Müller and Sennrich 2021
;
Kamigaito et al. 2025
) and efficiency (
Cheng and Vlachos 2023
;
Vamvas and Sennrich 2024
;
Deguchi et al. 2024
) of MBR decoding remain active areas of research.

3
MAP and MBR Decoding

In this section, we describe MAP and MBR decoding in the context of general conditional generative models.

Given an input
x
, a conditional generative model
M(\cdot|x)
defines a probability distribution over the hypothesis space.
Decoding for this model can be viewed as consisting of two phases:
hypothesis generation
and
decision
.
In the hypothesis generation phase, a procedure such as sampling or beam search is used to produce
N
candidate hypotheses
\mathcal{C}=\{h_{0},\ldots,h_{N-1}\}
from the model
M(\cdot\mid x)
.
In the decision phase, a decision rule assigns a score
{score}_{h}
to each
h\in\mathcal{C}
and returns
\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\limits_{h\in\mathcal{C}}score_{h}
as the final output.

3.1
MAP Decoding

Formally, let
\mathcal{H}
denote the entire hypothesis space. MAP decoding selects the hypothesis with the highest model-estimated probability from
\mathcal{H}
:

\displaystyle h^{\mathit{MAP}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\limits_{h\in\mathcal{H}}score^{\mathit{MAP}}_{h},

\displaystyle score^{\mathit{MAP}}_{h}

\displaystyle=M(h\mid x).

Finding the global maximizer over
\mathcal{H}
is typically intractable due to the enormous size of the space.
In practice, MAP decoding is approximated by restricting the search to a finite set of candidate hypotheses
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{H}
:

\displaystyle h^{\mathrm{MAP}}\approx\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\limits_{h\in\mathcal{C}}M(h\mid x).

3.2
MBR Decoding

Unlike MAP, MBR explicitly optimizes task utility rather than model-estimated probability.
Let
\mathcal{Y}
denote the space of the (unknown) ground-truth output
y
for input
x
and
u(\cdot,\cdot)
be a utility function,
MBR decoding selects the hypothesis that
minimizes the expected risk
—or equivalently,
maximizes the expected utility
—with respect to
\mathcal{Y}
:

\displaystyle h^{\mathit{MBR}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\limits_{h_{c}\in\mathcal{C}}score^{\mathit{MBR}}_{h},

\displaystyle score^{\mathit{MBR}}_{h}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left[u(h,y)\mid x\right]}.

(1)

Since
y
is unknown in practice, MBR decoding approximates this expectation using a set of “support hypotheses”
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
drawn from
M(\cdot|x)
:

\displaystyle score^{\mathit{MBR}}_{h}

\displaystyle\approx{\mathbb{E}_{h_{s}\in\mathcal{S}}\left[u(h,h_{s})\mid x\right]},

(2)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{h_{s}\in\mathcal{S}}u(h,h_{s}){M}(h_{s}\mid x).

A common practice is to use the same set for candidate and support hypotheses (
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{S}
) and to assume a uniform distribution over support hypotheses, yielding a simple average utility:

\displaystyle score^{\mathit{MBR}}_{h}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{h_{s}\in\mathcal{S}}u(h_{c},h_{s}).

(3)

4
Error Span Detection with MAP Decoding

4.1
Generative ESD Task

We focus on the ESD task in the reference-free setting: given an input
x=(s,t)
consisting of a source sentence
s
and its translation
t
, the model outputs an error annotation (a set of error spans with associated severities).

Let
E
denote a hypothesis containing a finite number of major and minor error spans, and let
\mathcal{H}_{\text{ESD}}
represent the space of all admissible hypotheses.
We denote the distribution of the (unknown) ground-truth human annotation as
\mathcal{Y}_{\text{ESD}}
.
The generative ESD model,
M_{\text{ESD}}(E|x)
, estimates the probability of a hypothesis
E\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{ESD}}
.

For notation, we use
E_{c}
to denote a candidate hypothesis,
E_{s}
for a support hypothesis, and
E_{y}
for the human annotation.
Similarly,
\mathcal{C}_{\text{ESD}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\text{ESD}}
represent the sets of candidate and support hypotheses, respectively.

4.2
MAP Decoding for ESD

Under MAP decoding, the decision score for a hypothesis
E\in\mathcal{C}_{\text{ESD}}
is defined as its model-estimated probability:

\displaystyle score^{\mathit{MAP}}_{E}=M_{\text{ESD}}(E\mid x).

As discussed in §
1
, the MAP objective implicitly assumes that model-estimated probabilities are perfectly correlated with similarity to the human annotation.
However, as illustrated by the failure case in Table
1
, this assumption frequently does not hold for ESD.
This discrepancy motivates the adoption of MBR decoding as a robust alternative.

Proposed Method

5.1
MBR Decoding for ESD

We propose applying MBR decoding to generative ESD models to address the limitations of MAP decoding.
Figure

provides an overview of our method.

Analogous to Eq.
1
, the MBR score for a hypothesis
E
is defined as:

\displaystyle score^{\mathit{MBR}}_{E}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}_{E_{y}\in\mathcal{Y}_{\text{ESD}}}\left[u(E,E_{y})\mid x\right]}.

(4)

As in Eq.
3
, we approximate this expectation using a finite sample
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{ESD}}\subset\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ESD}}
:

\displaystyle score^{\mathit{MBR}}_{E}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_{\text{ESD}}|}\sum_{E_{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{\text{ESD}}}u(E,E_{s}).

(5)

We specify the utility functions
u(\cdot,\cdot)
below.

5.2
Utility Functions of MBR Decoding in ESD

In this section, we describe three instantiations of MBR decoding for generative ESD models.
The utility functions fall into two categories: sentence and span level.

Notably, although SPA and
{\text{Acc}}_{\text{eq}}^{*}
(mentioned in §
2.2
) are standard metrics for ESD, they cannot serve as MBR utility functions.
This is because they measure ranking consistency over a dataset rather than computing a similarity score for a single pair of error annotations.

5.2.1
MBR-
ScoreSim

We introduce
MBR-
ScoreSim
, which employs our proposed
\textsc{ScoreSim}(\cdot,\cdot)
as the utility function.

\textsc{ScoreSim}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a sentence-level utility function that measures the similarity between two hypotheses based on severity-weighted error span counts.

Let
N^{\text{maj}}(\cdot)
and
N^{\text{min}}(\cdot)
denote the counts of major and minor error spans in
E
, respectively.
Following common practice (
Freitag et al. 2021a
;
Juraska et al. 2024
), we first convert the error spans into a scalar sentence-level score using the standard MQM scoring function:

\textsc{Score}(E)=\max(w_{\text{maj}}N^{\text{maj}}(E)+w_{\text{min}}N^{\text{min}}(E),\alpha).

(6)

Here,
w_{\text{maj}}
and
w_{\text{min}}
are negative severity weights (
w<0
).
These negative values reflect the subtractive nature of the MQM protocol (
Freitag et al. 2021a
), which penalizes the accumulation of error spans rather than rewarding quality.
Finally,
\alpha<0
defines a lower bound on the total penalty.

We then define the similarity between
E_{c}
and
E_{s}
as one minus the normalized absolute difference between their scores, using
|\alpha|
for normalization to yield a value in
[0,1]
:

\textsc{ScoreSim}(E_{c},E_{s})=1-\frac{|\,\textsc{Score}(E_{c})-\textsc{Score}(E_{s})\,|}{|\alpha|}.

5.2.2
MBR-
F1

For span-level utility, we examine
MBR-
F1
, which employs the standard
\textsc{F1}(\cdot,\cdot)
metric from the WMT QE Shared Task.
This function calculates the harmonic mean of character-level precision and recall between two annotations (see Appendix A.1 for details).

While
\textsc{F1}(\cdot,\cdot)
is widely used, it exhibits a defect when handling empty annotations.
Specifically, if the support hypothesis
E_{s}
is empty (i.e., contains no errors),
\textsc{F1}(\cdot,E_{s})
assigns the minimum utility (
0
) to any non-empty candidate
E_{c}
, regardless of how minor the error in
E_{c}
might be (see Appendix A.1 for the derivation).
This “all-or-nothing” behavior fails to capture the nuance that a hypothesis with a single minor error is arguably better—and more similar to an error-free translation—than one with multiple major errors.

5.2.3
MBR-
SoftF1

We introduce
MBR-
SoftF1
, an instance of MBR decoding using our proposed
\textsc{SoftF1}(\cdot,\cdot)
as the utility function.

\textsc{SoftF1}(\cdot,\cdot)
provides a continuous, “soft” evaluation of mismatch, ensuring robustness even when comparing against empty support hypotheses.

Formally, let
L
be the number of characters in translation
t
.
An error annotation
E
consists of two index sets
E^{maj},E^{min}\subseteq\{1,\dots,L\}
, representing the character positions of major and minor errors.
We represent this annotation not as discrete sets, but as a dense severity vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
.
For each character position
i\in\{1,\dots,L\}
, the vector element
\mathbf{v}^{(i)}
represents the aggregated severity:

\mathbf{v}^{(i)}=\beta\cdot\mathbb{I}[i\in E^{maj}]+\gamma\cdot\mathbb{I}[i\in E^{min}]
,
where
\beta
and
\gamma
represent the penalties for major and minor errors, respectively.
The total error severity of an annotation is the
\ell_{1}
norm of its vector,
\|\mathbf{v}\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}|\mathbf{v}^{(i)}|
.

We measure the discrepancy between a candidate hypothesis
E_{c}
(with vector
\mathbf{v}_{c}
) and a support hypothesis
E_{s}
(with vector
\mathbf{v}_{s}
) using the
\ell_{1}
distance,
\|\mathbf{v}_{c}-\mathbf{v}_{s}\|_{1}
.
Based on these vector representations, we define Soft Precision (
SoftP
) and Soft Recall (
SoftR
) as:

\displaystyle\textsc{SoftP}(E_{c},E_{s})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\|\mathbf{v}_{c}-\mathbf{v}_{s}\|_{1}}{L+\|\mathbf{v}_{c}\|_{1}},

\displaystyle\textsc{SoftR}(E_{c},E_{s})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\|\mathbf{v}_{c}-\mathbf{v}_{s}\|_{1}}{L+\|\mathbf{v}_{s}\|_{1}}.

Here,
L
is included in the denominator for normalization.
Finally,
SoftF1
(E_{c},E_{s})
is computed as the harmonic mean of
SoftP
(E_{c},E_{s})
and
SoftR
(E_{c},E_{s})
.

Unlike the standard
\textsc{F1}(\cdot,\cdot)
,
\textsc{SoftF1}(\cdot,\cdot)
returns a continuous value reflecting the degree of mismatch.
Crucially, even if the support hypothesis is empty (
\mathbf{v}_{s}=\mathbf{0}
), a candidate with a small error vector
\mathbf{v}_{c}
will yield a high utility score closer to
1
, rather than collapsing to
0
(see proof in Appendix A.2).
This property enables MBR decoding to meaningfully rank candidates even when the consensus among support hypotheses is that the translation is error-free.
Furthermore,
\textsc{SoftF1}(\cdot,E_{y})
can also serve as an effective evaluation metric for ESD.

At

Conclusion

While MBR-
ScoreSim
and MBR-
F1
are constrained by optimization trade-offs or defects in their utility functions, MBR-
SoftF1
largely mitigates these issues when

is sufficiently large.
Overall, these results support the conclusion that MBR decoding, instantiated with MBR-
SoftF1
and a sufficiently large candidate set, significantly improves span-level performance and generally matches or outperforms MAP at system and sentence levels.
Finally, we note a limitation regarding span-level conclusions, which depend on
\textsc{SoftF1}(\cdot,\cdot)
being a reliable span-level metric.
Since MBR-
SoftF1
was directly optimized by
\textsc{SoftF1}(\cdot,\cdot)
as the utility function, the evaluation metric intrinsically favors this method, a phenomenon known as

Lacking a neutral metric, we leave further investigation of this bias to future work.

8
Experiment on MBR Distillation

Despite the significant performance gains demonstrated in §
6
,
MBR decoding comes at a high computational cost. Specifically, it relies on generating and scoring a large set of hypotheses (i.e., a large
N
), which incurs substantial latency and compute overhead during inference.
To alleviate this bottleneck, we investigate
MBR distillation
, a technique designed to transfer the superior performance of MBR decoding into the model weights, thereby enabling comparable results using efficient greedy search.

Following prior work on MBR distillation (
Finkelstein and Freitag 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
;
Yang et al. 2024
), we employ Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) (
Rafailov et al. 2023
) to train the model to approximate MBR outputs.
DPO is a pairwise ranking optimization method typically used to align models with human preferences by maximizing the margin between a
preferred
and a
rejected
output.
We adapt DPO for MBR distillation by constructing a pairwise dataset where the candidate hypothesis with the highest utility is treated as the preferred output, and the one with the lowest utility serves as the rejected output.

8.1
Experimental Setup

Datasets and Model

The training dataset was derived from
(s,t)
pairs in the WMT20–22 Metrics Shared Task datasets (
Mathur et al. 2020
;
Freitag et al. 2021b
;
Freitag et al. 2022b
).
We used the WMT24 Metrics Shared Task as the test set.
We used
Llama-3.3-70B-Inst
as the model.
To generate the training dataset, we employed the same MBR setup as in §
6
:
N=256
with MBR-
SoftF1
.
We refer to the model distilled via DPO as
Distill-Greedy
, which employs standard greedy decoding at inference time.
See Appendix C for details.

Evaluation

The evaluation metrics for ESD are identical to those used in §
6
.

9
Conclusions and Future Work

In this work, we introduced MBR decoding for generative ESD models, marking the first application of reranking techniques to automatic MT metrics.
Extensive experimental results show that our MBR decoding, when instantiated with MBR-
SoftF1
and applied with sufficiently large candidate sets, significantly improves span-level performance and generally matches or outperforms MAP at system and sentence levels.
Furthermore, we established that MBR distillation allows a model decoded via greedy search to match the performance of MBR, effectively resolving the inference latency bottleneck.

We believe the following are promising directions for future work:
(1) Achieving further performance gains by integrating multiple utility functions.
(2) Applying MBR decoding to self-improvement training in the ESD model.

Appendix A
\textsc{F1}(\cdot,\cdot)
vs.
\textsc{SoftF1}(\cdot,\cdot)

A.1
\textsc{F1}(\cdot,\cdot)

For each character position
i\in[1,L]
, we define binary indicators for the presence of major or minor errors:

m^{i}=\mathbb{I}[i\in E^{maj}],\quad n^{i}=\mathbb{I}[i\in E^{min}]
,
and these indicators for the hypothesis and reference as
(m_{c}^{i},n_{c}^{i})
and
(m_{s}^{i},n_{s}^{i})
, respectively.
The character-level true-positive credit,
c^{i}\in\{0,\gamma,\beta\}
, is calculated based on the agreement between severities.
Assuming
\beta
is the weight for major errors and
\gamma
for minor errors (typically
\beta>\gamma
):

c^{i}=\max(\beta m_{c}^{i}m_{s}^{i},\beta n_{c}^{i}n_{s}^{i},\gamma m_{c}^{i}n_{s}^{i},\gamma n_{c}^{i}m_{s}^{i}).

We denote the total severity score of an annotation
E
as
S(E)
:

S(E)=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\max(m^{i},n^{i})
,
then precision
P
and recall
R
are defined as:

\displaystyle\textsc{P}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }S(E_{c})=S(E_{s})=0,\\
\frac{\sum_{i}c^{i}}{S(E_{c})},&\text{if }S(E_{c})>0,\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\textsc{R}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }S(E_{c})=S(E_{s})=0,\\
\frac{\sum_{i}c^{i}}{S(E_{s})},&\text{if }S(E_{s})>0,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Finally,

F1
(E_{c},E_{s})
is the harmonic mean of
P
and
R
,
with the boundary condition that
F1
(E_{c},E_{s})=1
if
P+R=0
.

A Defect in
\textsc{F1}(\cdot,\cdot)

If
E_{s}=\emptyset
, then
m_{s}^{i}=n_{s}^{i}=0
, and hence
c^{i}=\max(\beta m_{c}^{i}0,\beta n_{c}^{i}0,\gamma m_{c}^{i}0,\gamma n_{c}^{i}0)=0
, for all
i
, so
\sum_{i}c^{i}=0
.
Thus, when
E_{c}=\emptyset
, it perfectly matches
E_{s}
, and
\textsc{F1}(\cdot,\cdot)
correctly returns
1
.
However, if
E_{c}\neq\emptyset
, even if it marks only a single character as a minor error (i.e. very close to empty), the score collapses to
0
.

A.2
\textsc{SoftF1}(\cdot,\cdot)
: Non-empty vs. Empty Spans

If
E_{c}\neq\emptyset
and
E_{s}=\emptyset
, then:

\displaystyle\textsc{SoftP}(E_{c},E_{s})=1-\frac{\|\mathbf{v}_{c}\|_{1}}{L+\|\mathbf{v}_{c}\|_{1}}=\frac{1+L}{L+\|\mathbf{v}_{c}\|_{1}}>0,~~~\textsc{SoftR}(E_{c},E_{s})=1-\frac{\|\mathbf{v}_{c}\|_{1}}{L}>0.

which implies
\textsc{SoftF1}(E_{c},E_{s}=\emptyset)>0
.
This behavior differs from
\textsc{F1}(E_{c},E_{s}=\emptyset)
and highlights a desirable property of
\textsc{SoftF1}(E_{c},E_{s})
: when only one of
E_{c}
and
E_{s}
is empty, it does not simply return
0
or
1
, but a decimal value between
0
and
1
that reflects the degree of the match.

Appendix B
Details of Hypothesis Generation and Decision

We used guided generation (
Willard and Louf 2023
) to ensure that each hypothesis adheres to the specified JSON format.

Furthermore, due to the presence of empty translations (
L=0
) in the WMT Metrics
Shared Task dataset that do not conform to the ESD task definition, we added
1
to the denominators of
SoftP
(E_{c},E_{s})
and
SoftR
(E_{c},E_{s})
in practice to prevent division by zero.

Appendix C
Training Details of MBR Distillation

We used the Lion optimizer (
Chen et al. 2023
) with a learning rate of
10^{-7}
, a warmup step of
50
, a weight decay of
0.1
, a batch size of
256
, and train for a maximum of
5
epochs.
We set the regularization term weight of DPO to
0.5
.
We trained the model in BF16 precision on a machine equipped with 8 NVIDIA H200 GPUs.
We used DeepSpeed (
Rajbhandari et al. 2020
) Zero3 for multi-GPU training.
Our training codebase is based on the TRL library (
von Werra et al. 2020
).

The dataset comprises a total of
110,699
samples. We randomly split the data, reserving
10\%
for the validation set and using the remainder for the training set.
Upon completion of training, the checkpoint with the lowest loss on the validation set was selected as the final model.
