A unified method for generating closed-form point estimators for exponential families: An example with the beta distribution applied to proportions of land used for farming
Roberto Vila 1 and Helton Saulo 1,2 1 Department of Statistics, University of Brasilia, Brasilia, Brazil 2 Department of Economics, Federal University of Pelotas, Pelotas, Brazil

Abstract
We show that, after a simple power–transform reparameterization of the (vector) exponential family, the solutions to the likelihood equations coincide with moment-type estimating equations. This equivalence enables a unified route to closed-form point estimators for multi-parameter models that typically lack explicit maximum likelihood (ML) solutions. Within this framework we (i) recover, as special cases, several recent closed-form estimators from the literature; (ii) derive new families of estimators indexed by monotone transformations g g ; and (iii) establish strong consistency and asymptotic normality under mild regularity, including a dominated differentiation condition. As a detailed illustration, we derive closed-form estimators for parameters that index the beta distribution. A Monte Carlo simulation study is carried out to evaluate and compare the performance of proposed and existing estimators. Finally, we illustrate the approach with a novel municipal data set regarding proportions of land used for farming in Roraima (Brazil), which, to the best of our knowledge, has not been analyzed in the literature before, demonstrating the method’s practical usefulness.

1
Introduction

Closed-form point estimators are attractive wherever computational speed, robustness to initialization, or guaranteed convergence are at stake. By contrast, maximum likelihood (ML) for multi-parameter families often requires iterative optimization and may be sensitive to starting values, flat likelihood regions, or boundary estimates. This is particularly salient within exponential families, where, despite their elegant structure, ML estimators rarely admit explicit formulas once we move beyond the one-parameter case.

A classical way to bypass iterative ML is to use moment-type estimators. For exponential families,
Davidson and Daniel, 1974
showed that moment-type estimators can be linked to likelihood equations in canonical parametrizations. More recently, a number of interesting constructions have delivered closed-form estimators by manipulating likelihood equations directly; see, for instance,
Ye and Chen, 2017
for the gamma model, and subsequent developments for the Nakagami, Lindley-type, and related families
(
Cheng and Beaulieu, 2002
;
Ramos et al., 2016
;
Zhao et al., 2021
;
Kim et al., 2022
, e.g.,)
. For the beta distribution,
Tamae et al., 2020
proposed a score-adjusted approach that yields explicit estimators, whereas
Chen and Xiao, 2024
derived another pair of closed-form estimators.

In this paper, we derive a unified method to generate closed-form estimators for vector exponential families by exploiting a simple reparameterization of the sample through a power transformation
Y=[g^{-1}(X)]^{1/p}
, where
g
is a monotone twice-differentiable function. For the induced model, we show that the score components can be written as empirical minus population functionals, leading to estimating equations with the same fixed points as the likelihood equations. Then, we can

(i)

construct closed-form estimators by picking convenient
g
’s that linearize the score components in the unknown parameters;

(ii)

recover known closed-form estimators as special cases (for instance,
Chen and Xiao, 2024
and
Tamae et al., 2020
for the beta model);

(iii)

generate new families of estimators indexed by
g
(hence by
(r,s)
in our beta illustration);

(iv)

establish large-sample results. Under a dominated differentiation condition, we prove strong consistency and a joint central limit theorem for the resulting estimators.

We take the beta distribution as a detailed example. Using a general score identity with specific choices of
g
, we directly retrieve the closed-form estimators of
Chen and Xiao, 2024
and
Tamae et al., 2020
. We then introduce a new two-parameter transformation
g(x)=(1-x^{1/s})^{1/r}
that yields explicit estimators for the parameters that index the beta distribution. Because
(r,s)
regulate the algebraic simplification of the score, different pairs lead to different closed-form estimators. We therefore propose a simple profile selection: compute the closed-form
(\widehat{\alpha}_{r,s},\widehat{\beta}_{r,s})
over a small grid of
(r,s)
and keep the pair that maximizes the beta log-likelihood function.

The rest of this paper proceeds as follows. In Section
2
, we present some preliminary definitions which will be used throughout the paper. In Section
3
, we show that moment-type estimators of the population parameters can be derived from the likelihood equations for distributions within the exponential family. In Section
4
, based on the results of Section
3
, we propose closed-form estimators for the parameters indexing the beta distribution. In this section, we also show that our approach has as special cases the closed-form estimators of
Chen and Xiao, 2024
and
Tamae et al., 2020
. In Section
5
, we present a Monte Carlo simulation study to evaluate the finite-sample performance of the proposed estimators for the beta model. In Section
6
, we develop an empirical application to municipal farming shares in Roraima (Brazil). Finally, in Section
7
, we provide some concluding remarks, and the Appendix shows that our method recovers, as special cases, the closed-form estimators for weighted exponential families proposed in
Vila et al., 2024a
;
Vila et al., 2024b
.

2
Preliminary definitions

A random variable
X
belongs to the (vector) exponential family if it has a probability distribution function (PDF) of the form

\displaystyle f_{X}(x;\bm{\theta})={1\over Z(\bm{\theta})}\,h(x)\exp\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}(\bm{\theta})T_{j}(x)\right\},

(1)

where
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k})^{\top}
is a parameter vector,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
Z(\bm{\theta})
is called the partition function and ensures
f_{X}(x;\bm{\theta})

sums to one over the domain of

x
.
Here,
T_{j}(x)
,
h(x)
,
\eta_{j}(\bm{\theta})
, and
Z(\bm{\theta})
are known functions. The function
h(x)
must be non-negative and
0<Z(\bm{\theta})<\infty
.
The exponential family is said to be in canonical form if
\eta_{j}({\bm{\theta}})=\theta_{j}
,
\forall j=1,\ldots,k
.

It is well-known that most ML estimators for distributions in the exponential family (
1
) do not admit simple closed-form expressions. The next section aims to extend the exponential family to a broader class of distributions (not necessarily within the exponential family) to derive new closed-form expressions for the estimators of population parameters. Additionally, we demonstrate that the estimators obtained from the ML equations coincide with those derived using a moment-type method, which is a modification of the classical method of moments.

By using the power transformation
Y=[g^{-1}(X)]^{1/p}
,
p>0
, inspired by the work of
Ye and Chen, 2017
and
Cheng and Beaulieu, 2002
, where
g:D\subset(0,\infty)\to\text{supp}(X)\subset\mathbb{R}
is a twice-differentiable monotonic function with inverse
g^{-1}
,
\text{supp}(X)
is the support of the random variable
X
, which has PDF given in (
1
), it is simple to see that the PDF of
Y
is written as

\displaystyle f_{Y}(y;\bm{\theta},p)=p\,{1\over Z(\bm{\theta})}\,h(g(y^{p}))y^{p-1}\exp\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}(\bm{\theta})T_{j}(g(y^{p}))\right\}|g^{\prime}(y^{p})|.

(2)

In the above,
g^{\prime}(x)
denotes the derivative of
g(x)
with respect to
x
.

3
Moment-type estimators derived from likelihood equations

Let
\{Y_{i}:i=1,\ldots,n\}
be a univariate random sample of size
n
from
Y
having PDF (
2
).
The (random) log-likelihood function for
(\bm{\theta},p)
is given by

\displaystyle l(\bm{\theta},p)

\displaystyle=n\log(p)-n\log(Z(\bm{\theta}))+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(h(g(Y_{i}^{p})))

\displaystyle+(p-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(Y_{i})+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}(\bm{\theta})\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{j}(g(Y_{i}^{p}))+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(|g^{\prime}(Y_{i}^{p})|).

The (random) score vector are composed of the following partial derivatives

\displaystyle{\partial l(\bm{\theta},p)\over\partial\theta_{j}}=-{n\over Z(\bm{\theta})}\,{\partial Z(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\theta_{j}}+{\partial\eta_{j}(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\theta_{j}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{j}(g(Y_{i}^{p})),\quad j=1,\ldots,k,

\displaystyle{\partial l(\bm{\theta},p)\over\partial p}={n\over p}+{1\over p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{h^{\prime}(g(Y_{i}^{p}))g^{\prime}(Y_{i}^{p})\over h(g(Y_{i}^{p}))}\,Y_{i}^{p}\log(Y_{i}^{p})+{1\over p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(Y_{i}^{p})

\displaystyle\hskip 48.36958pt+{1\over p}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}(\bm{\theta})\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{j}^{\prime}(g(Y_{i}^{p}))g^{\prime}(Y_{i}^{p})Y_{i}^{p}\log(Y_{i}^{p})+{1\over p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(Y_{i}^{p})}{g^{\prime}(Y_{i}^{p})}\,Y_{i}^{p}\log(Y_{i}^{p}).

Since
g(Y_{i}^{p})=X_{i}
and
Y_{i}^{p}=g^{-1}(X_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, the above equations can be written as

\displaystyle{\partial l(\bm{\theta},p)\over\partial\theta_{j}}=-{n\over Z(\bm{\theta})}\,{\partial Z(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\theta_{j}}+{\partial\eta_{j}(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\theta_{j}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{j}(X_{i}),\quad j=1,\ldots,k,

\displaystyle{\partial l(\bm{\theta},p)\over\partial p}={n\over p}+{1\over p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{h^{\prime}(X_{i})g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(X_{i}))\over h(X_{i})}\,g^{-1}(X_{i})\log(g^{-1}(X_{i}))+{1\over p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(g^{-1}(X_{i}))

\displaystyle\hskip 48.36958pt+{1\over p}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}(\bm{\theta})\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{j}^{\prime}(X_{i})g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(X_{i}))g^{-1}(X_{i})\log(g^{-1}(X_{i}))

\displaystyle\hskip 48.36958pt+{1\over p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(g^{-1}(X_{i}))}{g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(X_{i}))}\,g^{-1}(X_{i})\log(g^{-1}(X_{i})).

Hence,

\displaystyle{\partial l(\bm{\theta},p)\over\partial\theta_{j}}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle{1\over Z(\bm{\theta})}\,{\partial Z(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\theta_{j}}={\partial\eta_{j}(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\theta_{j}}\,\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{j}(X_{i})\right],\quad j=1,\ldots,k,

(3)

\displaystyle{\partial l(\bm{\theta},p)\over\partial p}=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle{1\over n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}(\bm{\theta})\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{j}^{\prime}(X_{i})g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(X_{i}))g^{-1}(X_{i})\log(g^{-1}(X_{i}))

\displaystyle=-1-{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(g^{-1}(X_{i}))}{g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(X_{i}))}\,g^{-1}(X_{i})\log(g^{-1}(X_{i}))

\displaystyle-{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{h^{\prime}(X_{i})g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(X_{i}))\over h(X_{i})}\,g^{-1}(X_{i})\log(g^{-1}(X_{i}))-{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(g^{-1}(X_{i})).

(4)

Remark 3.1
.

When Equation (
3
) provide only

(with

)
linear equations involving the parameters
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}
, the remaining

Remark 3.2
.

Let

have density

given in (
1

A simple calculation shows that

\displaystyle{\partial\eta_{j}(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\theta_{j}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[T_{j}(X)\right]

\displaystyle={1\over Z(\bm{\theta})}\int{\partial\eta_{j}(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\theta_{j}}\,T_{j}(x)h(x)\exp\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}(\bm{\theta})T_{j}(x)\right\}{\rm d}y

\displaystyle={1\over Z(\bm{\theta})}\,{\partial Z(\bm{\theta})\over\partial\theta_{j}},\quad j=1,\ldots,k,

where in the last equality the dominated convergence Theorem was used.
Therefore, Equation (
3
) is written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{j}(X)]={1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{j}(X_{i}),\quad j=1,\ldots,k.

(5)

On the other hand, suppose that for some
p\in[a,b]
, the partial derivative
\partial f_{Y}(y;\bm{\theta},p)/\partial p

exists on

, and that there exist an integrable function

\displaystyle\left|{f_{Y}(y;\bm{\theta},p)\over\partial p}\right|\leqslant H(y).

Then, by applying the dominated convergence Theorem, we may interchange the order of integration and partial differentiation for the improper integral; that is,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\partial l(\bm{\theta},p)\over\partial p}\right]=n\mathbb{E}\left[{\partial\log(f_{Y}(Y;\bm{\theta},p))\over\partial p}\right]=n\left[\int{\partial f_{Y}(y;\bm{\theta},p)\over\partial p}{\rm d}y\right]=n\left[{\partial\over\partial p}\int{f_{Y}(y;\bm{\theta},p)}{\rm d}y\right]=0.

Thus, using the identity above and taking the expectation of both sides of (

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}(\bm{\theta})\mathbb{E}\left[T_{j}^{\prime}(X)g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(X))g^{-1}(X)\log(g^{-1}(X))\right]=-1-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(g^{-1}(X))}{g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(X))}\,g^{-1}(X)\log(g^{-1}(X))\right]\\[5.69046pt]
-\mathbb{E}\left[{h^{\prime}(X)g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(X))\over h(X)}\,g^{-1}(X)\log(g^{-1}(X))\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\log(g^{-1}(X))\right].

(7)

Equations (
5
) and (
7
) demonstrate that, for distributions within the exponential family, moment-type estimators of the population parameters can be derived from the likelihood equations.

\displaystyle=\dfrac{{1+\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{3,1}(X)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{4,1}(X)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{5,1}(X)\right]\over\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{2,1}(X)\right]}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{2,2}(X)\right]-1-\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{3,2}(X)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{4,2}(X)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{5,2}(X)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{1,2}(X)\right]-{\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{1,1}(X)\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{2,2}(X)\right]\over\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{2,1}(X)\right]}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{fund-eq}}}{{=}}\alpha

and

\displaystyle\xi_{2}(\mathbb{E}(\bm{X}))

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{1,1}(X)\right]+1+\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{3,1}(X)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{4,1}(X)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{5,1}(X)\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[\vartheta_{2,1}(X)\right]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{fund-eq-1}}}{{=}}\beta.

Hence, the proof is complete.
∎

Letting

be the identity function, that is,
g_{1}(x)=x
, and using the definitions in (
4
), along with the identities

\displaystyle g_{1}^{-1}(x)=x,\quad g_{1}^{\prime}(g_{1}^{-1}(x))=1,\quad\frac{g_{1}^{\prime\prime}(g_{1}^{-1}(x))}{g_{1}^{\prime}(g_{1}^{-1}(x))}=0,

Equation (
4
) becomes

\displaystyle\alpha\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(X_{i})\right]-\beta\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}\over 1-X_{i}}\,\log(X_{i})\right]=-1-{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}\over 1-X_{i}}\,\log(X_{i}).

(24)

Letting
g_{2}:D=(0,1)\to\text{supp}(X)=(0,1)
be defined by
g_{2}(x)=1-x
, based on the definitions given in (
4
), together with the identities

\displaystyle g_{2}^{-1}(x)=1-x,\quad g_{2}^{\prime}(g_{2}^{-1}(x))=-1,\quad\frac{g_{2}^{\prime\prime}(g_{2}^{-1}(x))}{g_{2}^{\prime}(g_{2}^{-1}(x))}=0,

Equation (
4
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle-\alpha\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{1-X_{i}\over X_{i}}\,\log(1-X_{i})\right]+\beta\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1-X_{i})\right]=-1-{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{1-X_{i}\over X_{i}}\,\log(1-X_{i}).

(25)

Solving the system composed of Equations (
24
) and (
25
) yields estimators for
\alpha
and
\beta
as

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}=\dfrac{\left[1+{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\log({X_{i}\over 1-X_{i}})\right]\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1-X_{i})\right]+\left[1+{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-X_{i})\log({1-X_{i}\over X_{i}})\right]\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\log({X_{i}\over 1-X_{i}})\right]}{\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\log({X_{i}\over 1-X_{i}})\right]\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-X_{i})\log({1-X_{i}\over X_{i}})\right]-\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(X_{i})\right]\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1-X_{i})\right]}

and

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}=\dfrac{\left[1+{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-X_{i})\log({1-X_{i}\over X_{i}})\right]\mskip-3.0mu\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(X_{i})\right]\mskip-3.0mu+\mskip-3.0mu\left[1+{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\log({X_{i}\over 1-X_{i}})\right]\mskip-3.0mu\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-X_{i})\log({1-X_{i}\over X_{i}})\right]}{\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\log({X_{i}\over 1-X_{i}})\right]\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-X_{i})\log({1-X_{i}\over X_{i}})\right]-\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(X_{i})\right]\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1-X_{i})\right]},

respectively, which coincide with those proposed by

.

4.3
Obtaining the estimators proposed by
Tamae et al., 2020

By defining
g_{1}:D=(0,\infty)\to\text{supp}(X)=(0,1)
as
g_{1}(x)=x/(x+1)
, and by using the definitions provided in (
4
),
along with the identities

\displaystyle g_{1}^{-1}(x)={x\over 1-x},\quad g_{1}^{\prime}(g_{1}^{-1}(x))=(x-1)^{2},\quad\frac{g_{1}^{\prime\prime}(g_{1}^{-1}(x))}{g_{1}^{\prime}(g_{1}^{-1}(x))}=2(x-1),

we can express Equation (
4
) as

\displaystyle\alpha\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-X_{i})\log\left({X_{i}\over 1-X_{i}}\right)\right]-\beta\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\log\left({X_{i}\over 1-X_{i}}\right)\right]=-1.

(26)

Choosing
g_{2}:D=(0,1)\to\text{supp}(X)=(0,1)
where
g_{2}(x)=-\log(x)/[1-\log(x)]
, and applying the definitions from (
4
), together with the identities

\displaystyle g_{2}^{-1}(x)=\exp\left({x\over x-1}\right),\quad g_{2}^{\prime}(g_{2}^{-1}(x))=-(x-1)^{2}\exp\left({x\over 1-x}\right),\quad\frac{g_{2}^{\prime\prime}(g_{2}^{-1}(x))}{g_{2}^{\prime}(g_{2}^{-1}(x))}=-(2x-1)\exp\left({x\over 1-x}\right),

Equation (
4
) takes the following form:

\displaystyle\alpha\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(1-X_{i})\right]-\beta\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right]=0.

(27)

From Equations (
26
) and (
27
), the estimators for
\alpha
and
\beta
are obtained as:

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}=\dfrac{{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\log({X_{i}\over 1-X_{i}})-\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right]\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log({X_{i}\over 1-X_{i}})\right]}

and

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}=\dfrac{1-{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}{{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\log({X_{i}\over 1-X_{i}})-\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right]\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log({X_{i}\over 1-X_{i}})\right]},

respectively, which are identical to those proposed by
Tamae et al., 2020
.

4.4
Proposing new closed-form estimators for
\alpha
and
\beta

Defining
g:D=(0,1)\to\text{supp}(X)=(0,1)
as
g(x)=(1-x^{1/s})^{1/r}
where
r,s>0
, and following the definitions in (
4
), together with the identities

\displaystyle g^{-1}(x)=(1-x^{r})^{s},\quad g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(x))=-{(1-x^{r})^{1-s}x^{1-r}\over rs},\quad\frac{g^{\prime\prime}(g^{-1}(x))}{g^{\prime}(g^{-1}(x))}={(1-x^{r})^{-s}x^{-r}[r-1+(1-rs)x^{r}]\over rs},

Equation (
4
) becomes

\displaystyle-\alpha\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{1-X_{i}^{r}\over X_{i}^{r}}\,\log(1-X_{i}^{r})\right]+\beta\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{1-X_{i}^{r}\over(1-X_{i})X_{i}^{r-1}}\,\log(1-X_{i}^{r})\right]

\displaystyle=-r-{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{[r-(1-X_{i}^{r})-rsX_{i}^{r}](1-X_{i}^{r})^{s}\over X_{i}^{2s-r}}\,\log(1-X_{i}^{r})

\displaystyle-{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{1-X_{i}^{r}\over X_{i}^{r}}\,\log(1-X_{i}^{r})+{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{1-X_{i}^{r}\over(1-X_{i})X_{i}^{r-1}}\,\log(1-X_{i}^{r})-rs\left[{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1-X_{i}^{r})\right].

(28)

By solving the system consisting of Equations (
24
) and (
28
), we obtain the following estimators for
\alpha
and

\beta
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}={(\widehat{\beta}-1)B-1\over A}

(29)

and

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}={-r-E-C+D-rsF-{(B+1)C\over A}\over D-{BC\over A}},

(30)

respectively,
where the following definitions have been adopted:

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\equiv{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(X_{i}),

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\equiv{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{X_{i}\over 1-X_{i}}\,\log(X_{i}),

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\equiv{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{1-X_{i}^{r}\over X_{i}^{r}}\,\log(1-X_{i}^{r}),

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\equiv{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{1-X_{i}^{r}\over(1-X_{i})X_{i}^{r-1}}\,\log(1-X_{i}^{r}),

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle\equiv{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{[r-(1-X_{i}^{r})-rsX_{i}^{r}](1-X_{i}^{r})^{s}\over X_{i}^{2s-r}}\,\log(1-X_{i}^{r}),

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle\equiv{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(1-X_{i}^{r}).

Remark 4.2
.

The estimators above are functions of the parameters
r>0
and
s>0
; hence, multiple estimators for
\alpha
and
\beta
can be derived depending on the specific values of
r
and
s
. In this paper, we propose the selection of
r>0
and
s>0
through a profile approach. For each grid point
(r,s)
we compute the closed–form
(\widehat{\alpha}_{r,s},\widehat{\beta}_{r,s})
from (
29
)-(
30
) and evaluate the corresponding beta log-likelihood at these values. The selected pair

(\widehat{r},\widehat{s})\in\arg\max_{r\in\mathcal{R},\;s\in\mathcal{S}}\;\ell\mskip-3.0mu\left(\widehat{\alpha}_{r,s},\widehat{\beta}_{r,s}\,\middle|\,x\right)

(31)

defines the proposed estimator
(\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{\beta})=(\widehat{\alpha}_{\widehat{r},\widehat{s}},\widehat{\beta}_{\widehat{r},\widehat{s}})
.
For example,

\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{S}=\{0.1,0.2,\ldots,2.5\}
. Profiling the likelihood over
(r,s)
chooses, in a data-driven manner, the member whose implied likelihood is maximal on the grid, thereby mimicking ML accuracy while avoiding a full two-dimensional numerical optimization. The procedure is tuning-free, initialization-free, and numerically stable.

Remark 4.3
.

For the particular case
r=s=1
, we observe that
E=0
and
D=F
, leading to the simplified expressions for the estimators of

\alpha
and
\beta
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}={(\widehat{\beta}-1)B-1\over A}

and

\displaystyle\widehat{\beta}={-1-C-{(B+1)C\over A}\over D-{BC\over A}},

respectively,
which coincide with the estimators (of

\alpha
and
\beta
) presented in Subsection
4.2
.

5
Monte Carlo simulation study

This section presents a Monte Carlo study of the finite-sample performance of our closed-form, profile-likelihood-based estimator (Eq. (
31
)) for the
\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
model. We compare it with ML and two recent closed-form competitors
(
Chen and Xiao, 2024
;
Tamae et al., 2020
)
across several
(\alpha,\beta)
settings and sample sizes
n
. We also document how the proposed procedure selects
(r,s)
on a prespecified grid.

5.1
Finite-sample performance

We conducted a Monte Carlo study to assess the finite-sample performance of four estimators of the Beta
(\alpha,\beta)
shape parameters: ML, Chen-Xiao
(
Chen and Xiao, 2024
, Sect. 4.2)
, Tamae et al.
(
Tamae et al., 2020
, Sect. 4.4)
, and the proposed profile-likelihood-based built on the closed-form
r,s
-family of Sect.
4.4
, defined in Equation (
31
).

We considered the following data-generating scenarios:

n\in\{10,20,50,100\},\qquad\alpha\in\{0.5,1,2\},\qquad\beta\in\{0.5,1,2\}.

We generated
R
i.i.d. samples
X^{(r)}=(X^{(r)}_{1},\ldots,X^{(r)}_{n})
with
X^{(r)}_{i}\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
and computed all four estimators
(\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{\beta})
on each replication. We set
R=1000
.

Let
\theta\in\{\alpha,\beta\}
and
\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}
be the estimate on replication
r
. We report, for each estimator and scenario,

\displaystyle\text{MARE}(\widehat{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\Bigl|\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}/\theta-1\Bigr|,

\displaystyle\text{RMSE}(\widehat{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bigl(\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}-\theta\bigr)^{2}},

\displaystyle\text{SE}(\widehat{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\Bigl(\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}-\overline{\widehat{\theta}}\Bigr)^{2}},\qquad\overline{\widehat{\theta}}=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\widehat{\theta}^{(r)}.

Hence, the mean absolute relative error (MARE) is a relative bias (in absolute value), the root mean squared error (RMSE) combines variance and squared bias, and SE is the Monte Carlo standard deviation.

Tables
1
–
9
report the Monte Carlo summaries for all nine data-generating scenarios and
n\in\{10,20,50,100\}
. Across the board, all estimators tend to improve as the sample size increases. For example, under the symmetric case
(\alpha,\beta)=(1,1)
, the MARE decreases from about
0.51
at
n=10
to about
0.11
at
n=100
for both
\widehat{\alpha}
and
\widehat{\beta}
irrespective of the estimator, mirroring the reductions seen in RMSE and Monte Carlo SE. Similar patterns obtain in the remaining scenarios, with more pronounced gains between
n=10
and
n=50
.

In terms of accuracy, the proposed profile estimator is essentially indistinguishable from ML. Its RMSE and SE track ML to three decimals in many cases (e.g.,
(\alpha,\beta)=(2,2)
at
n=100
), which is consistent with its construction: profiling over
(r,s)
selects the closed-form pair whose induced likelihood is (nearly) maximal on the grid. Between the two closed-form competitors, Chen-Xiao is generally closer to ML/Proposed than Tamae et al. in both RMSE and SE. The score-adjusted estimator of Tamae et al. tends to show slightly higher dispersion in small samples. For instance, when
(\alpha,\beta)\in\{(0.5,2),(2,0.5)\}
at
n=10
, although the differences shrink quickly as
n
increases.

In general, across all scenarios and sample sizes considered, the proposed profile-likelihood estimator achieves ML-level accuracy while maintaining a closed-form implementation. Chen-Xiao is a strong no-tuning alternative with performance close to ML/Proposed, whereas the score-adjusted estimator of Tamae et al. is modestly less efficient in the most extreme small-sample regimes. As
n
grows, performance differences among the four methods become negligible, as expected.

5.3
Remarks about the simulation results

Across all Monte Carlo scenarios considered (varying
n
,
\alpha
, and
\beta
), the proposed selector achieved accuracy essentially indistinguishable from numerical ML: its RMSE and Monte Carlo SE track ML, and its MARE is consistently on par with, or better than, the fixed closed-form competitors (Chen-Xiao and Tamae et al.), with the largest gains in the more small-sample settings. A central advantage is that all computations rest on closed-form formulas, avoiding delicate iterative optimization, or initial values, while remaining fast and numerically stable. More broadly, our construction yields a family of closed-form estimators indexed by the choice of the transformation
g(\cdot)
. In the beta case, with

g(x)=(1-x^{1/s})^{1/r}
, the proposed closed-form, profile-likelihood-based estimator performs particularly well and, crucially, estimates
(r,s)
from the data rather than fixing them a priori.

7
Concluding remarks

We showed that a simple power-transform reparameterization makes the moment-type estimating equations coincide with the likelihood equations for vector exponential families, yielding a unified route to closed-form estimation. We established strong consistency and asymptotic normality for the moment-type estimators. Taking the beta distribution as case study, we introduced closed-form, profile-likelihood-based estimators (Sect.
4.4
). A Monte Carlo simulation study indicated ML-level results for the proposed estimators, with typical gains over existing closed-form competitors in small samples (Sect.
5
). A new application to municipal farming shares in Roraima, Brazil, attained ML-level AIC/BIC (Sect.
6
). Although our exposition focused on the beta model, the derivations are given for generic vector exponential families (Eq. (
1
)) and extended to weighted variants (Appendix). As part of future research, it will be of interest to go beyond the beta case, applying the same approach to additional vector exponential families (gamma-type, Nakagami, logit-normal). Finally, the estimation for parameters associated with multivariate distribution are natural topics for future research.

Appendix A
Closed-form estimators for weighted exponential family

We here consider the case where the random variable
X

follows the weighted exponential distribution proposed by
Vila et al., 2024b
, and its corresponding PDF is given by:

\displaystyle f_{X}(x;\bm{\theta})={(\mu\sigma)^{\mu+1}\over(\sigma+\delta_{ab})\Gamma(\mu+1)}\,[1+\delta_{ab}S(x)]\,{|S^{\prime}(x)|\over S(x)}\,\exp\left\{-\mu\sigma S(x)+\mu\log(S(x))\right\},\quad x>0,

(32)

where
\bm{\theta}=(\mu,\sigma)^{\top}
,
\mu,\sigma>0
,
S:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is a
real strictly monotone twice differentiable function and
\delta_{ab}
is the Kronecker delta function, that is,
\delta_{ab}
is 1 if
a=b
, and 0 otherwise.
In the above,
S^{\prime}(x)
denotes the derivative of
S(x)
with respect to
x
.

Remark A.1
.

A wide range of distributions results from the choice of generator

S(x)
in (
32
), notably including Nakagami
Laurenson, 1994
, Rayleigh
Rayleigh, 1880
, Gamma
Stacy, 1962
, Weibull
Johnson et al., 1994
, Generalized gamma
Stacy, 1962
, Chi-squared
Johnson et al., 1994
, Scaled inverse chi-squared
Bernardo and Smith, 1993
, Weighted Lindley
(
Kim and Jang, 2021
)
, Weighted inverse Lindley, Weighted Nakagami, and Weighted inverse Nakagami. Further examples are provided in Table 1 of
Vila et al., 2024a
and
Vila et al., 2024b
.

It is clear that the distribution of
X
in (
