BRAR-anchored multimodal dataset of panoramic radiographs for periodontal bone resorption grading
Authors: Yiru Xia, Zhi Li, Zhikai Lin, Shuai Wang, Yaqi Wang, Yufeng Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate assessment of alveolar bone resorption from panoramic radiographs is crucial for diagnosing and managing periodontal disease. However, the development of robust, automated diagnostic tools is hindered by a lack of large-scale, expertly annotated multimodal datasets. To address this, we present a comprehensive dataset from 1,104 patients, integrating demographic information, full-mouth panoramic radiographs, and clinician-annotated clinical indicators at both patient and tooth levels. A key feature is the Bone Resorption Age Ratio (BRAR), which standardizes bone resorption assessment. Annotations were validated by experienced periodontists to ensure high data quality. This dataset, with its rich multimodal structure and expert validation, provides a foundational resource to accelerate AI-driven diagnostic model development and large-scale clinical research in periodontics, advancing periodontal disease diagnosis, prognosis, and epidemiology.

Background & Summary

Periodontal disease, a prevalent chronic condition and a leading cause of tooth loss worldwide1,2, is pathologically defined by progressive alveolar bone resorption3. Accurate assessment of bone loss is therefore critical for diagnosis, treatment planning, and tooth prognosis. However, conventional evaluation methods rely heavily on clinicians’ visual interpretation of radiographs, a process that is inherently subjective, time-consuming, and prone to inter-observer variability. While artificial intelligence (AI) promises to automate and standardize bone loss assessment, its development is critically hampered by the scarcity of large-scale, expertly annotated datasets for training and validation4,5.

Among available imaging modalities, panoramic radiography (orthopantomography, OPG) stands out as an ideal tool for population-level screening and longitudinal monitoring. It is widely used in routine clinical practice due to its accessibility, relatively low cost, and minimal radiation exposure, making it particularly suited for large-scale epidemiological research. However, despite its extensive clinical adoption, there is a striking lack of publicly available OPG datasets that integrate radiographic images with essential demographic and clinical metadata. This shortage contrasts sharply with other imaging fields—such as chest X-rays or cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT)—where well-established, large-scale repositories have fueled substantial advances in automated analysis and clinical applications. In periodontology, the absence of comparable resources significantly limits progress in AI-driven diagnostics, objective disease stratification, and epidemiological investigations.

To address this critical need, we present a comprehensive, multimodal dataset comprising 1,104 expertly annotated panoramic radiographs and associated clinical metadata from patients treated at the periodontology department of our institution. This dataset introduces the Bone Resorption Age Ratio (BRAR), a standardized, age-adjusted metric that enables objective quantification of alveolar bone resorption severity. BRAR allows reproducible stratification into three clinically meaningful severity levels, bridging the gap between traditional radiographic interpretation and quantitative disease grading. As a key component of this dataset, BRAR provides a powerful tool for understanding bone loss in the context of patient demographics and clinical history.

Methods

Patient selection and ethical approval

Considering the potential hazards of obtaining radiological images, this study did not design a prospective experiment to recruit volunteers for unnecessary radiological imaging examinations. Instead, we used existing patient data. A total of 1104 patients who underwent routine dental examination including panoramic radiography at Shanghai Stomatological Hospital, between 1st January, 2025 and 31st March, 2025, were retrospectively enrolled in this study. Inclusion criteria were: (1) availability of complete demographic information; (2) high-quality panoramic radiographs; and (3) no systemic conditions affecting bone metabolism; (4) ages ranging from 18 to 75 years.

This study received formal approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Shanghai Stomatological Hospital (Approval No: SHDC22024247), and all data underwent anonymization prior to analysis. All participants (or their legal guardians, where applicable) provided written informed consent for participation and for public data sharing under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 (CC BY 4.0) license, which permits redistribution and reuse without field-of-use restrictions. The approved scope specifically encompasses the collection of imaging data, the reconstruction of patients’ three-dimensional oral models, and the sharing of anonymized imaging data. All personally identifiable information, excluding patient gender and age, has been meticulously removed or regenerated to ensure full alignment with U.S. HIPAA regulations. Informed consent was appropriately secured from every patient included within this dataset.

Data collection

Demographic data were extracted from medical records. Panoramic images were acquired using Panoramic radiography machine (Oc 200D, DEXIS, Envista, USA) with standardized exposure parameters. Positioning followed manufacturer guidance: the midsagittal plane aligned perpendicular to the floor, the Frankfort plane parallel to the floor, and anterior teeth centered in the focal trough using the bite block and light guides; patients were instructed to keep the tongue against the palate to reduce palatoglossal air space. Retakes were permitted when clear positioning errors were present (e.g., incisor foreshortening/elongation indicating off-focal placement, severe motion blur, excessive palatoglossal air space, or overlapping premolars beyond one cusp width). Images failing to meet minimal diagnostic criteria after a single retake were excluded from BRAR. Radiographic evaluations were independently performed by three junior Periodontal specialists who had received professional training in dental radiographic interpretation, and conducted a consistency test. Two senior specialists in periodontology conducted the final adjudication of bone loss grading. The output resolution and imaging parameters were maintained consistently across all subjects.

Data labeling and annotation

Tooth-level characteristics such as missing teeth, implants, residual roots, and functional tooth pairs were recorded. Alveolar bone resorption was independently graded by three trained periodontists using BRAR—Bone Resorption Age Ratio—a standardized, age-normalized index defined as BRAR = (BL/RL)/Age, where BL denotes radiographic bone loss (the vertical distance from the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ) to the alveolar crest for the tooth with the greatest loss on the panoramic radiograph); RL denotes the corresponding tooth root length (CEJ to root apex), and Age is the chronological age in years at the time of imaging. To align with the 2018 classification7,8 convention of anchoring severity on the most affected site, the patient-level BL% was defined as the maximum tooth-level BL% across the dentition. We did not compute whole-mouth averages of BL% by design, because averaging across all teeth can underestimate severity in patients with localized advanced destruction.

CEJ was defined radiographically as the most apical point of the enamel-cement interface on each surface, avoiding restorative margins when present. The alveolar crest landmark was the most coronal interproximal bone peak between adjacent teeth, excluding lamina dura projections. In cases of reduced cortication, overlapping anatomy, or restorations, annotators followed a written protocol with visual exemplars; if the CEJ or crest could not be localized within ±1.5 mm confidence, the site was flagged for consensus review and, if necessary, excluded from quantitative analysis.

BRAR thus provides a unitless, age-adjusted severity index for cross-patient comparisons of bone remodeling burden. Based on BRAR, bone resorption is categorized into three grades: Grade 1 corresponds to BRAR < 0.25, Grade 2 corresponds to BRAR between 0.25 and 1.0, and Grade 3 corresponds to BRAR > 1.0. Disagreements among the three raters were resolved by majority voting, and final annotations were reviewed by two senior periodontal specialists to ensure accuracy.

Annotator training and consensus

Two board-certified periodontists underwent calibration using pilot cases and reference exemplars before formal labeling. All cases were independently annotated; disagreements in grade were resolved by consensus discussion, and unresolved cases were adjudicated by a senior periodontist. Inter-rater agreement is reported in ‘Technical Validation’ on a held-out subset (n = 50).

Data privacy and anonymization

All personally identifiable information has been removed or replaced with anonymized identifiers. The patient ID, gender, and age are the only personal details retained for research purposes. Dates have been randomized to protect patient privacy, and all data are compliant with HIPAA standards for data protection.

Data storage and access

The dataset has been meticulously organized and submitted to Figshare for public access. The files are structured in a hierarchical manner for ease of use and organized as follows:

- Radiographs: High-resolution panoramic radiograph images in JPG format, anonymized with identifiers.

- Annotations: CSV files containing patient metadata (e.g., age, gender), bone resorption details, Bone Resorption Age Ratio (BRAR), and tooth-level indicators (e.g., missing teeth, implants, residual roots, opposing pairs of functional teeth).

- Codebook: Provides definitions, variable names, and coding schemes for the dataset.

- Assessment Protocol: A detailed guide explaining the image interpretation process and BRAR calculation method.

Annotation consistency

To ensure reliability of radiographic assessments, inter-rater agreement between the two attending physicians was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa statistic, yielding a value of 0.88, indicating strong consistency. Discrepant cases (n = 132) were jointly reviewed to reach consensus, and all final classifications were verified by two senior specialists with over 20 years of clinical and radiographic experience. Image quality criteria

Images were rated as acceptable, suboptimal and unreadable using pre-specified criteria. Acceptable: (i) no gross motion blur (enamel-dentin junction and lamina dura are visibly distinct at standard zoom), (ii) no severe overlapping that obscures CEJ and crest at both mesial and distal sites for the majority of posterior teeth, (iii) focal trough alignment sufficient for incisors and premolars (no marked foreshortening/elongation), and (iv) palatoglossal air space not obscuring maxillary anterior crests. Suboptimal: minor blur or localized overlap limiting measurements on a minority of sites but preserving tooth-level grading on most teeth. Unreadable: if CEJ or crest could not be localized within ±1.5 mm on most posterior interproximal sites, or severe artifacts (double contours/ghosting) precluded grading. Only images rated as acceptable or suboptimal were included in the dataset. Data completeness and consistency were verified via automated scripts and manual checks.

Computational benchmark

To validate the computational utility and the integrity of our expert-derived labels, we established a computational benchmark by developing a deep learning model for the automated classification of alveolar bone resorption. The task was framed as a three-class problem, categorizing panoramic radiographs into severity grades (Grade 1, 2, and 3) based on the Bone Resorption Age Ratio (BRAR). To avoid underestimation of severity in the presence of localized advanced destruction, we used the worst-tooth BL/RL as the patient-level anchor, consistent with the 2018 periodontitis classification that bases radiographic severity on the most affected site7,8. Users wishing to explore alternative summaries (e.g., mean or median BL% across present measurable teeth) should be aware these can down-weight focal severe lesions and shift apparent severity downward. An EfficientNet-B0 architecture was trained on the dataset, achieving an overall accuracy of 63.3% on an unseen test set. This performance, substantially exceeding the random-guess baseline, confirms that our dataset contains consistent and machine-learnable radiographic features that directly correspond to the quantitative disease grades.

A detailed analysis of the model’s performance, illustrated in Figure 5, reveals key characteristics of our dataset and its value as a realistic clinical benchmark. The confusion matrix (Fig. 5A) and per-class accuracy plot (Fig. 5B) show that the model excelled at identifying Grade 2 (moderate) cases, achieving a high recall of 83.5%. However, its primary challenge was distinguishing the minority classes, with lower recall for Grade 1 (44.8%) and Grade 3 (34.8%). The class distribution plot (Fig. 5D) visually confirms the model’s tendency to over-predict the majority class (Grade 2), a classic behavior when faced with the natural class imbalance present in clinical data. This is further supported by the prediction probability distributions (Fig. 5C), which show high model confidence primarily for Grade 2 predictions. The most frequent error was misclassifying Grade 3 as Grade 2, highlighting the subtle radiographic transition between these stages and positioning our dataset as an ideal resource for developing more sophisticated AI models.

The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis (Fig. 5E) provides a deeper, more nuanced insight into the dataset’s feature separability. Intriguingly, the model achieved the highest Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.83 for Grade 1. This indicates that despite its low recall, the radiographic features of mild bone loss are highly distinct and were consistently labeled by experts, allowing for strong discrimination. In contrast, the more modest AUCs for Grade 2 (0.67) and Grade 3 (0.68) confirm the significant feature overlap between these advanced stages, reinforcing the diagnostic complexity captured within our data. The performance radar chart (Fig. 5F) offers a holistic summary of these metrics.

In summary, this computational benchmark successfully validates the quality and utility of our dataset. The baseline model’s performance not only proves that our novel, expert-annotated BRAR grades are grounded in discernible visual evidence but also establishes this resource as a robust and challenging benchmark. It is poised to drive future research in developing AI tools capable of navigating the nuanced, continuous spectrum of periodontal bone loss.

Limitations and comparative context

We contextualized BRAR against prior radiographic datasets for periodontal assessment, noting that BRAR contributes expert-validated labels at the tooth level with multimodal metadata10,11. For instance, earlier datasets often lacked fine-grained annotations (e.g., some only provided tooth-level segmentation without expert validation12), whereas BRAR’s comprehensive approach parallels emerging resources that integrate expert-derived landmarks or multi-format clinical data13. This resource complements prior work by offering a combination of detailed, tooth-specific annotations with diverse metadata (e.g., imaging types and health conditions), contrasting with datasets that focused primarily on image-only or single-modal representations (e.g., Rasnayaka, Sumudu et al.13 limited to panoramic radiographs with basic tooth segmentation; Hao, Jing et al.14 included multiple imaging modalities but only about 80% provided annotated imagery, which may not capture expert-verified labels consistently). Limitations include (i) intrinsic constraints of panoramic imaging (geometric distortion, superimposition, focal trough sensitivity)15, as seen in studies where panoramic deformations impacted model accuracy and required specialized processing to address; (ii) possible residual confounding by systemic or behavioral factors not captured in our metadata (e.g., smoking, glycemic control/HbA1c, and medications such as bisphosphonates)16,17. Smoking status, HbA1c, and medication history were not recorded in the source records and are therefore unavailable in BRAR; downstream analyses should interpret grade distributions with this limitation in mind, similar to other datasets where proxies (e.g., registry data) were used but direct clinical factors remained unmeasured16. And (iii) the relatively short acquisition window (three-month) and single-center sampling, which may introduce demographic or referral bias, as such biases were noted in studies relying on localized cohorts (e.g., Persson, Peter et al.16 involved a single-center cohort, and Tao, L-R et al.18 used an external dataset to partially mitigate but still faced challenges in representing diverse populations).

Usage Notes

This dataset facilitates research and applications in periodontal disease epidemiology, AI-assisted radiographic image analysis, and the study of age-related bone changes, including disease progression modeling. Users are encouraged to preprocess radiographs for standardization and consider patient age when interpreting resorption levels. Figure 3 and Fig. 4 may guide researchers in feature selection and stratification strategies. Researchers should be aware of limitations including its single-center origin, lack of longitudinal follow-up, and the inherent constraints of 2D panoramic imaging (such as distortion and superimposition). Additionally, the BRAR index, developed specifically for this study, may require external validation in other populations or with different imaging modalities. While BRAR provides tooth-level BL% enabling diverse summarization strategies, we caution that whole-mouth averages may dilute focal severe disease. For tasks requiring patient-level stratification, we recommend using the worst-tooth BL% anchor for severity, consistent with the 2018 periodontitis classification. We encourage users to consider sensitivity analyses and model calibration strategies that are robust to these factors. BRAR grades exhibit natural class imbalance. We recommend (i) patient-level stratified splitting to avoid leakage, (ii) loss reweighting (e.g., class-balanced or focal loss), (iii) balanced sampling at the tooth level, (iv) reporting macro-averaged metrics (macro-F1/macro-AUROC) in addition to micro-averaged ones, and (v) calibration assessment (e.g., expected calibration error) and per-class threshold tuning. For small labeled subsets, consider cost-sensitive learning or semi-supervised strategies with conservative pseudo-labeling.
