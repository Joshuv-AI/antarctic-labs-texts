Phenotype‐specific immune profiles and outcomes in childhood autoimmune neutropenia: A 20‐year cohort study
Authors: Ioanna Saougou, Chrysoula Kosmeri, Nikolaos Chaliasos, Irene Mavroudi, Charalampos Pontikoglou, Alexandros Makis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryChildhood autoimmune neutropenia (AIN) encompasses heterogeneous entities; phenotype‐specific immunological profiles and their relationship to infection outcomes remain incompletely defined. To characterise clinical, immunological and long‐term outcomes across distinct phenotypes of childhood AIN. We conducted a retrospective cohort study of 112 children with antibody‐confirmed AIN at a tertiary centre over 20 years. Patients were classified into three phenotypes: primary autoimmune neutropenia (pAIN), long‐lasting neutropenia (LL‐Np) and late‐onset neutropenia (LO‐Np). Outcomes included lymphocyte subsets, immunoglobulin levels, infection‐related hospitalisation rates and time to resolution. The cohort comprised 90 pAIN (80.4%), 10 LL‐Np (8.9%) and 12 LO‐Np (10.7%) patients. LO‐Np was characterised by higher neutrophil counts, marked leucopenia, multi‐lineage lymphopenia (CD3+, CD4+, CD19+), frequent antinuclear antibodies (ANA) positivity (50%) and associated autoimmune disease (25%). No infection‐related hospitalisations occurred in LO‐Np (0.00/100 person‐years [PY]; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.00–2.30) versus 1.38/100 PY in pAIN and 5.58/100 PY in LL‐Np. Immunoglobulin levels were normal across all patients. Median time to resolution was 19, 67 and 59 months in pAIN, LL‐Np and LO‐Np respectively (both p < 0.001 vs. pAIN). Late‐onset AIN represents a distinct phenotype with systemic immune dysregulation and multi‐lineage lymphopenia, yet paradoxically absence of infection‐related hospitalisations, reflecting preserved humoral immunity. Phenotype‐based stratification may guide clinical monitoring and therapeutic decisions.

INTRODUCTION

Autoimmune neutropenia (AIN) is an important cause of acquired childhood neutropenia, driven by autoantibodies against neutrophil‐specific antigens causing accelerated peripheral destruction. 1 , 2 It is classified into primary (pAIN) and secondary (sAIN) forms, which differ in epidemiology, associated conditions and clinical course. 2 In pAIN, autoantibodies most often target human neutrophil antigens (HNA), particularly HNA‐1 (FcγRIIIb/CD16b). 3 , 4 Bone marrow granulopoiesis is preserved, consistent with peripheral destruction rather than a production defect. 5

Primary AIN, also known as AIN of infancy, is the most common form of chronic neutropenia in children <3 years and typically follows a benign, self‐limited course. 1 , 2 Its incidence is ~1 per 100 000 children annually. 2 , 6 Genetic susceptibility has been linked to human leucocyte antigens (HLA) class II haplotypes 7 and polymorphisms in regulatory T‐cell–related genes (IL2, IL10, FOXP3). 8

According to European Hematology Association (EHA)/EuNet‐INNOCHRON, neutropenia is classified as mild (absolute neutropenia count, ANC, 1.0–1.5 × 109/L), moderate (0.5–1.0), severe (<0.5) or agranulocytosis (<0.2), the latter carrying high infection risk. 2 Despite often severe counts, most pAIN cases have mild infections, reflecting preserved marrow and immune function. 1 , 2 , 9 Diagnosis relies on demonstration of antineutrophil antibodies by granulocyte immunofluorescence test (GIFT) and/or granulocyte agglutination test (GAT), though antibody detection is technically challenging and repeat testing is often required. 2

In contrast, sAIN in children occurs in the context of an underlying disorder and is generally associated with a higher risk of severe infections. 1 , 2 It may coexist with additional immune cytopenias within the Evans syndrome spectrum 10 and has also been recognised in the context of allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation. 11

Two additional phenotypes—long‐lasting neutropenia (LL‐Np) and late‐onset neutropenia (LO‐Np)—do not fully conform to classic pAIN and differ in clinical and immunohaematological features; LO‐Np is more strongly associated with autoimmune disease and shows lower spontaneous resolution rates. 12 In such cases, comprehensive evaluation—including bone marrow examination, genetic testing and extended immunological assessment—is recommended. 2 , 13

This study aimed to comprehensively describe the clinical characteristics, immunohaematological profile and long‐term outcomes of a cohort of paediatric patients with AIN managed at a tertiary university hospital in Northwestern Greece over 20 years.

METHODS

Study population

This single‐centre retrospective cohort included 112 AIN patients managed at the University Hospital of Ioannina (2005–2025). Patients lacking diagnostic data or with non‐autoimmune neutropenia were excluded. Ethical approval was obtained (IRB 11‐03‐2026/211).

Diagnostic criteria and phenotype classification

AIN was defined as ANC below age‐adjusted thresholds on ≥3 occasions (<1.0 × 109/L ≤ 1 year; <1.5 × 109/L > 1 year) persisting >3 months, with secondary causes excluded and positive GIFT and/or GAT. At least one positive test in the appropriate clinical context was required; borderline results were repeated after ≥3 months. All 112 patients had confirmed positivity. Secondary causes excluded included drug‐induced and infectious neutropenia (Epstein Barr Virus [EBV]; Cytomegalovirus [CMV]; parvovirus B19; hepatitis), autoimmune disease (ANA, anti‐double stranded DNA [anti‐dsDNA] antibodies, complement), lymphoproliferative disorders (immunophenotyping) and congenital neutropenia.

Three mutually exclusive phenotypes were defined as per Fioredda et al. 12 (i) pAIN: onset <3 years, positive GIFT and/or GAT, no autoimmune markers at diagnosis, resolving within 36 months. (ii) LL‐Np: onset <3 years (fulfilling pAIN criteria at presentation), with persistence >3 years from diagnosis; (iii) LO‐Np: onset ≥5 years. Patients aged 3–5 years were classified using additional criteria (ANA or autoimmune disease, lymphocyte subset abnormalities as per Shearer 14 and family history of autoimmunity). Resolution was defined as sustained ANC >1.5 × 109/L on ≥3 measurements over 3 months.

Molecular genetic analysis (Sanger sequencing of ELANE) was performed in selected cases in accordance with EHA/EuNet‐INNOCHRON guideline recommendations. 2

Data collection

We recorded retrospectively: demographic data (age at diagnosis, sex), personal and family history of autoimmune diseases, reason for testing (incidental vs. fever/infection‐driven), ANC at diagnosis and during follow‐up, full blood count, immunoglobulin (Ig) levels (IgG, IgM, IgA), ANA status, blood flow cytometry (CD3+, CD4+, CD8+, CD19+, CD16+/56+ natural killer [NK cells]), bone marrow biopsy results, molecular genetic studies, granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF) use, hospitalisation events with infection type and resolution status with time to resolution.

Family history of autoimmunity was defined as a diagnosed autoimmune condition in a first‐ or second‐degree biological relative (autoimmune thyroid disease, systemic lupus erythematosus [SLE], rheumatoid arthritis, type 1 diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease, Sjögren syndrome, autoimmune hepatitis or psoriasis). Family history was obtained during routine clinical evaluation using structured intake forms and subsequently extracted from medical records.

Lymphocyte subsets and immunoglobulin reference intervals

Lymphocyte subset abnormalities were defined using age‐adjusted reference intervals from Shearer et al. 14 Lower limits of normal (5th percentile) were CD3+ <2.0 × 109/L (1–6 years) and <1.4 × 109/L (6–12 years); CD4+ <1.0 × 109/L and <0.65 × 109/L; CD8+ <0.5 × 109/L and <0.37 × 109/L; CD19+ <0.39 × 109/L and <0.27 × 109/L; NK cells <0.10 × 109/L and <0.09 × 109/L respectively. These widely used intervals were validated against institutional laboratory standards during the study period.

Immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgA, IgM) were assessed against age‐adjusted reference intervals from Jolliff et al., 15 with patients classified as low (<5th percentile), normal or high (>97.5th percentile) for age at AIN diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are reported as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and categorical variables as n (%). Non‐normality was assessed with Shapiro–Wilk and D'Agostino–Pearson tests; group comparisons used Kruskal–Wallis and Mann–Whitney U‐tests. Fisher's exact test was applied for binary variables with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Time‐to‐resolution was analysed by Kaplan–Meier analysis. Infection‐related hospitalisation rates were expressed per 100 person‐years with exact Poisson 95% confidence intervals.

Because the LL‐Np definition requires persistence beyond 36 months, an unrestricted log‐rank comparison of pAIN versus LL‐Np resolution times is inherently biased by inclusion criteria. We therefore performed a prespecified landmark sensitivity analysis including only patients still neutropenic at 36 months, comparing post‐landmark resolution using a log‐rank test. In contrast, the pAIN versus LO‐Np comparison is not affected, as LO‐Np is age‐based rather than duration‐based, and is reported unadjusted.

RESULTS

Cohort composition and demographics

The cohort included 90 pAIN (80.4%), 10 LL‐Np (8.9%) and 12 LO‐Np (10.7%) patients. Median follow‐up was 13.9 years (0.6–17.2) for pAIN, 16.3 (6–17.1) for LL‐Np and 15.5 (8.2–16.2) for LO‐Np. Patient characteristics are summarised in Table 1. Demographic differences were noted. Male sex predominated in pAIN (61.1%) versus LL‐Np (50.0%) and LO‐Np (41.7%), without statistical significance. Age at diagnosis differed markedly: 1.12 years (IQR 0.77–1.56) for pAIN, 1.75 years (IQR 1.33–2.21; trend vs. pAIN, Bonferroni‐corrected p = 0.051) for LL‐Np and 6.42 years (IQR 5.60–8.92; p < 0.001 vs. both) for LO‐Np. Neutropenia was incidentally detected in 64.4%, 70.0% and 41.7% of patients respectively.

Family history of autoimmunity

Family history of autoimmunity showed the steepest phenotype gradient, present in 10/90 pAIN patients (11.1%), 8/10 LL‐Np patients (80.0%; p < 0.001 vs. pAIN) and 7/12 LO‐Np patients (58.3%; p = 0.002 vs. pAIN) (Table 1).

Haematological profile at diagnosis

ANC at diagnosis differed significantly across groups (Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.001). The LO‐Np group demonstrated substantially higher ANC values (median 0.66 × 109/L, IQR 0.61–0.73) compared with pAIN (median 0.21 × 109/L, IQR 0.11–0.33) and LL‐Np (median 0.19 × 109/L, IQR 0.14–0.24). Applying the EHA/EuNet‐INNOCHRON severity classification, 2 agranulocytosis (ANC < 0.2 × 109/L), the most extreme form associated with life‐threatening infection risk, was present in 45.6% of pAIN and 60.0% of LL‐Np patients, but in none of the LO‐Np patients. Moderate neutropenia (ANC 0.5–1.0 × 109/L) predominated in LO‐Np (83.3%) (Table 1).

Total white blood cell count (WBC) differed significantly between groups (p < 0.001), with LO‐Np showing leucopenia (median 3.85 × 109/L, IQR 3.60–5.34) compared with pAIN (6.92 × 109/L, IQR 6.26–7.44) and LL‐Np (6.55 × 109/L, IQR 6.17–6.62). Haemoglobin was higher in LO‐Np (median 132 g/L, IQR 128–138) than in pAIN (120 g/L, IQR 109–129; p = 0.005 after correction) and LL‐Np (113 g/L, IQR 108–118). Platelet counts did not differ significantly.

Immunological profile

Age‐adjusted assessment of immunoglobulin levels against Jolliff et al. reference intervals15 demonstrated that IgG, IgM and IgA were within the normal range for age in all 112 patients (100%); no patient had hypogammaglobulinaemia. Median values: IgG 565 (IQR 546–594) mg/dL in pAIN, 600 (564–648) in LL‐Np and 890 (771–1010) in LO‐Np; IgM 50 (47–55), 56 (50–64) and 102 (84–120) mg/dL; IgA 42 (33–57), 60 (42–76) and 135 (106–169) mg/dL (Table 1). Significant overall differences were identified for all immunoglobulin classes (Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.001), with post hoc Mann–Whitney U‐testing demonstrating LO‐Np versus pAIN and LL‐Np differences (Bonferroni‐corrected p < 0.001 for all classes); pAIN versus LL‐Np did not differ significantly (p = 0.14).

ANA positivity increased progressively across phenotype groups: 2.2% (2/90) in pAIN, 20.0% (2/10) in LL‐Np and 50.0% (6/12) in LO‐Np (p < 0.001 after correction for pAIN vs. LO‐Np). Associated autoimmune diseases were present exclusively in the LO‐Np group (3/12, 25.0%; p = 0.004 vs. pAIN), SLE (n = 2) and Graves' disease (n = 1).

Lymphocyte subset abnormalities (age‐adjusted)

Lymphocyte subset analysis applied age‐adjusted lower reference limits (5th percentile) as per Shearer et al. 14 CD3+ T‐cell counts were reduced in 4/12 (33.3%) of LO‐Np patients; CD4+ helper T cells were reduced in 4/12 (33.3%). CD19+ B‐cell lymphopenia was the most prevalent finding, affecting 6/12 (50.0%) of LO‐Np patients. CD8+ lymphopenia was present in 1/12 (8.3%) and did not reach statistical significance. No LO‐Np patient had NK‐cell counts below the age‐adjusted lower reference limit (0/12, 0.0%). All lymphocyte subset abnormalities were exclusive to the LO‐Np group (p < 0.001 for CD3+, CD4+ and CD19+ vs. pAIN and LL‐Np; Table 2).

Diagnostic work‐up

Bone marrow biopsy was performed more frequently in LO‐Np (8/12, 66.7%) than in pAIN (18/90, 20.0%; p = 0.005 after correction) and LL‐Np (3/10, 30.0%). All biopsies showed reactive myeloid maturation arrest (left‐shifted granulopoiesis with preserved cellularity and intact erythroid and megakaryocytic lineages). No aplasia, dysplasia or large granular lymphocyte (LGL) expansion was observed; LGL lymphocytosis was excluded in all eight LO‐Np cases by morphology and immunophenotyping. Formal T‐cell receptor clonality testing was not performed, so subclinical T‐cell clonal disorders cannot be definitively excluded.

Molecular testing (Sanger sequencing of ELANE) was performed in 9/12 LO‐Np (75.0%), 5/90 pAIN (5.6%; p < 0.001) and 2/10 LL‐Np (20.0%) patients; all were negative for pathogenic variants, supporting an autoimmune rather than congenital aetiology. Testing was not performed in three LO‐Np cases due to limited early availability and low clinical suspicion based on phenotype and bone marrow findings.

Infection‐related outcomes

Infection‐related hospitalisations occurred in 14/90 pAIN patients (15.6%) and 4/10 LL‐Np patients (40.0%), with none in LO‐Np (0/12). Incidence rates were 5.58 per 100 person‐years (95% CI 2.41–10.99) for LL‐Np and 1.38 (95% CI 0.75–2.31) for pAIN, indicating a fourfold higher rate in LL‐Np. LO‐Np had 0.00 per 100 person‐years (95% CI 0.00–2.30) over 160.3 person‐years of follow‐up.

Outpatient infection events (e.g. visits requiring oral antibiotics) were not systematically recorded across the 20‐year study period and could not be reliably retrieved; the present analysis, therefore, reports the rate of hospitalisations due to infections only.

Infection types in the pAIN group included urinary tract infections (n = 5), pneumonia (n = 2), influenza (n = 2), acute otitis media (n = 2), cervical lymphadenitis/abscess (n = 1), skin infection (n = 1) and Salmonella enteritis (n = 1). In the LL‐Np group, infections included pneumonia (n = 3), acute otitis media (n = 4) and mastoiditis (n = 1).

G‐CSF was required in 20.0% of LL‐Np patients, 1.1% of pAIN patients and 0.0% of LO‐Np patients (Table 3). In all cases, G‐CSF was administered exclusively in response to severe infectious episodes requiring hospitalisation in patients with concurrent agranulocytosis (ANC < 0.2 × 109/L); no patient received G‐CSF prophylactically for elective dental or surgical procedures.

Resolution of neutropenia and Kaplan–Meier analysis

Overall, 88/112 patients (78.6%) achieved resolution: 81.1% (73/90) in pAIN, 80.0% (8/10) in LL‐Np and 58.3% (7/12) in LO‐Np, without significant differences after Bonferroni correction (Table 3).

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed distinct kinetics (Figure 1): pAIN resolved fastest (median 19.0 months, IQR 16.0–26.0), LL‐Np was markedly delayed (67.0 months, IQR 58.0–71.2; p < 0.001 vs. pAIN) and LO‐Np was intermediate (59.0 months, IQR 46.0–NR; p < 0.001 vs. pAIN), with 41.7% remaining persistent. LL‐Np versus LO‐Np showed a non‐significant trend (Bonferroni‐corrected p = 0.052). The unrestricted log‐rank comparison of pAIN versus LL‐Np (p < 0.001) is inherently biased by the LL‐Np definition (no resolution before 36 months). In a prespecified landmark analysis of patients still neutropenic at 36 months (pAIN n = 22; LL‐Np n = 10), median additional time to resolution was ~6 vs. ~31 months, respectively (log‐rank p < 0.001), confirming slower post‐36‐month resolution in LL‐Np. The pAIN versus LO‐Np comparison is unaffected.

DISCUSSION

This 20‐year single‐centre study characterises three clinically and immunologically distinct phenotypes of childhood AIN, demonstrating significant differences in haematological severity, immunological profiles, infection burden and resolution kinetics that have direct implications for clinical management.

Our findings regarding pAIN are consistent with the established literature. The median age at diagnosis of 1.12 years and the high prevalence of agranulocytosis (45.6%) are consistent with previous reports 1 , 9 , 16 , 17 , 18 and underscore the paradoxical mildness of infections that characterise this phenotype. The benign clinical course despite severe neutropenia is consistent with preserved marrow reserve and intact immune function.

The LL‐Np group was characterised by markedly delayed resolution (median 67.0 months) and a strikingly high rate of familial autoimmune aggregation (80.0% vs. 11.1% in pAIN; p < 0.001). Fioredda et al. 12 reported a resolution rate of 58%; our series achieved 80.0%, likely reflecting longer follow‐up (median >15 years). Immune dysregulation gene variants have been described in long‐lasting neutropenia, 12 , 19 which may account for the familial clustering, prolonged course and higher G‐CSF requirement (20% in LL‐Np vs. 1.1% in pAIN). Although ELANE sequencing was negative in our cohort, broader multigene panels were not systematically applied and may reveal relevant immune dysregulation variants in future studies.

The most distinctive findings concern LO‐Np. Patients presenting after age 5 showed multi‐lineage lymphopenia—reduced CD3+ and CD4+ T cells (33.3% each) and CD19+ B cells (50.0%)—absent in pAIN and LL‐Np, with preserved NK cells indicating sparing of innate cytotoxic immunity. This extends prior reports by demonstrating combined T‐ and B‐cell involvement exclusively in LO‐Np. 12 , 20 High ANA positivity (50.0% vs. 2.2% in pAIN; p < 0.001) and exclusive systemic autoimmune associations (25.0%) support a model of systemic immune dysregulation rather than isolated neutrophil autoimmunity. 9 , 21 , 22 This profile overlaps with findings by Zama et al., 23 linking combined lymphopenia to inborn errors of immunity (IEI), suggesting LO‐Np may represent an early manifestation of IEI or evolving systemic autoimmunity rather than a distinct entity. Despite this, no infection‐related hospitalisations occurred over 160.3 person‐years, likely reflecting higher ANC at diagnosis and preserved humoral immunity, as demonstrated by normal immunoglobulin levels. Outpatient infection morbidity (mucosal, respiratory or cutaneous infections requiring oral antibiotics) was not systematically captured in our registry and the absence reported here therefore refers specifically to infections requiring inpatient admission. Formal common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) exclusion was incomplete as functional antibody responses were not assessed, 24 , 25 , 26 consistent with intact humoral function despite reduced B cells. 27

Kaplan–Meier analysis confirmed rapid pAIN resolution (median 19 months), consistent with prior reports, 6 , 28 , 29 contrasted with markedly delayed LL‐Np resolution (67.0 months) and intermediate LO‐Np kinetics (59.0 months). The LO‐Np resolution rate of 58.3% exceeds registry series, 12 , 19 likely reflecting the extended follow‐up period in our cohort (median > 15 years).

Age at diagnosis is the most informative prognostic determinant. Children presenting before age 3 with isolated neutropenia and antineutrophil antibodies can be managed conservatively, with resolution expected in the majority within 5 years. Patients after age 5 warrant comprehensive immunological evaluation including lymphocyte subset, autoimmune serology and immunoglobulins. Given the overlap with IEI‐associated cytopenias 27 and the fact that normal immunoglobulin levels do not exclude functional humoral defects, LO‐Np patients should undergo functional antibody testing (e.g. vaccine responses) and, where feasible, multigene next‐generation sequencing (NGS) panels for IEI. Multi‐lineage lymphopenia, ANA positivity or autoimmune features should prompt long‐term surveillance for systemic autoimmune diseases including SLE. 24 , 25 , 26 , 30 A key insight is that infection risk in LO‐Np is not determined by lymphocyte counts or ANC alone; preserved humoral immunity appears critical, and G‐CSF should be guided by infection history per EHA/EuNet‐INNOCHRON guidelines. 2

Sanger sequencing of ELANE, the first‐line test to exclude congenital neutropenia, was negative in all tested patients. Current guidance recommends multigene NGS panels for suspected IEI when ELANE is negative; these were not widely available during the study. Future studies should include panels covering CTLA4, LRBA, STAT3, PIK3CD, PIK3R1, RAG1/2, ADA2 and related genes.

This study has limitations. Its retrospective, single‐centre design introduces potential selection bias, and phenotype classification as per Fioredda et al. 12 entails partial outcome dependency (left‐truncation bias for LL‐Np Kaplan–Meier analysis). Infection outcomes were restricted to hospitalisations, with outpatient morbidity not captured; thus, the absence of infections in LO‐Np applies only to admissions.

Functional antibody responses were not assessed, precluding exclusion of CVID and other humoral defects, and multigene NGS panels for IEI were not performed; hence, underlying IEI cannot be excluded in LO‐Np. Formal T‐cell receptor clonality testing was also not undertaken, so subclinical T‐cell clonal disorders cannot be definitively ruled out. Lymphocyte subset and immunoglobulin measurements lacked standardised longitudinal time points and IEI multigene panels were not applied. Furthermore, the small size of the LL‐Np (n = 10) and LO‐Np (n = 12) groups limits statistical power, and the single‐centre origin limits generalisability.

Strengths include the large cohort (n = 112) with 100% GIFT/GAT diagnostic confirmation, comprehensive immunological phenotyping with age‐adjusted reference intervals and long follow‐up (median > 15 years), enabling reliable characterisation of resolution patterns.

In conclusion, autoimmune neutropenia in children is heterogeneous with distinct clinical and immunological phenotypes. pAIN is benign and self‐limited with excellent prognosis. LL‐Np shows familial autoimmune clustering, higher infection burden and delayed but eventual resolution. LO‐Np emerges as a qualitatively distinct entity characterised by extensive lymphopenia, ANA positivity and systemic autoimmune disease associations, yet without severe infections requiring hospitalisation in this cohort. This pattern is consistent with preserved quantitative humoral immunity, but does not exclude an underlying IEI, which remains an important consideration requiring extended immunological and genetic evaluation. Recognition of these phenotypes enables risk‐stratified prognostic counselling and tailored immunological monitoring.
