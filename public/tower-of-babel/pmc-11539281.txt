Intracranial pleomorphic liposarcoma misclassified as a pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma by a DNA methylation classifier: illustrative case
Authors: Kohichi Go, Yasuhide Makino, Yasuhide Takeuchi, Sachiko Minamiguchi, Shigeki Takada, Noritaka Sano, Masahiro Tanji, Yohei Mineharu, Masato Hojo, Hironori Haga, Yoshiki Arakawa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDRecently, it has been shown that DNA methylation arrays and German Cancer Research Center (Deutsches Krebsforschungszentrum) methylation classifiers are useful aids in brain tumor diagnosis for cases in which histopathological diagnosis is difficult. Liposarcoma (LPS) is a tumor of primitive cellular origin that can differentiate into fat. Previous reports have shown that it accounts for approximately 9.8%–18% of soft tissue sarcomas, second in incidence only to malignant fibrous histiocytoma.1 LPS is primarily an adult tumor, with a peak incidence between 40 and 60 years of age and a slightly higher incidence in males.2 When LPS occurs in children, it tends to occur more frequently in the teenage years.3 Although quite infrequent, metastatic soft tissue tumors and secondary soft tissue tumors caused by radiotherapy also occur.

The DNA methylation-based profiling classifier from the German Cancer Research Center (Deutsches Krebsforschungszentrum [DKFZ]) is currently being used by many institutions as a valuable tool for determining tumor classification and subsequent treatment strategies in difficult cases.4, 5 Initially introduced as a tool for successfully classifying central nervous system tumors, this classification system has also been extended to sarcomas.6, 7 However, data from routine clinical experience with this sarcoma classification method are lacking. In particular, there is a lack of knowledge regarding sarcomas of intracranial origin.

Here, we describe the case of a patient with an intracranial pleomorphic LPS that was misclassified as a pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma (PXA) by a DNA methylation classifier.

Illustrative Case

Clinical Summary

An 18-year-old woman presented with a 1-week history of diplopia. She had a history of natural killer (NK)/T-cell lymphoma, which had been treated with an allogeneic bone marrow transplant and total body irradiation (TBI; 12 Gy, 6 fractions) at the age of 11 years. She was referred to our department after computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examinations of the head showed a tumor in the left temporal lobe. On CT, it was determined to be a neoplastic lesion with severe edematous changes in the left temporal lobe. No internal hemorrhage or high-density calcified areas were observed (Fig. 1A). MRI showed a tumor measuring 3.9 × 4.9 × 4.3 cm in size, with hypointensity on T1-weighted (T1W) imaging and hyperintensity on T2-weighted (T2W) imaging from the posterior left temporal lobe to the parietal lobe. The peritumor area showed severe edematous changes and a midline shift of up to 1.3 cm (Fig. 1B and C). Gadolinium-enhanced MRI examination revealed uniform contrast enhancement of the tumor (Fig. 1D). 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) examination showed no increased uptake in the intracranial lesion but revealed uniform hyperaccumulation of FDG (maximum standardized uptake value 5.5) in a 2.5-cm-diameter soft tissue nodule located in the subcutaneous fat at the level of the left femoral head (Fig. 1E and F). Cerebral angiography showed a tumor stain from the anterior branch of the left middle meningeal artery extending toward the lateral side of the tumor base. Because the patient reported worsening diplopia and headaches, resection was planned.

The surgery was completed without incident, and the contrast-enhanced lesion in the left temporal region was successfully removed, as indicated by the results of postoperative follow-up MRI. The postoperative course was favorable, with the resolution of diplopia and headache. The patient was discharged from the hospital 2 weeks after surgery, and no further intracranial recurrence was observed.

Histological Findings

Histopathological examination of the tumor removed via craniotomy showed spindle-shaped tumor cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm and similarly round or oval nuclei, growing in irregular bundles with blood vessels and a small number with nuclear fission (Fig. 2A and B). Immunohistochemical staining showed that the tumor cells were negative for glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), and isocitrate dehydrogenase 1. Ki-67 staining was present in approximately 2% of the tumor cells (Fig. 3A). A definitive diagnosis was difficult to make based on the histopathological findings of the intracranial lesion alone, and a central pathology laboratory was consulted, which also indicated that it was difficult to assign a specific disease name to the lesion, and it was diagnosed as a malignant neuroectodermal neoplasm. DNA methylation array profiling was performed to establish a diagnosis, and it was classified as a PXA by the DNA methylation-based profiling classifier of the DKFZ (Fig. 4).

DNA methylation array profiling classifies this tumor as a PXA.

A left femoral tumor, removed by orthopedic surgery at the same time, was composed of spindle cells with oval nuclei and highly pleomorphic cells. There were few mitoses, no coagulation necrosis, and positive immunostaining for cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4) and murine double minute 2 (MDM2), leading to the diagnosis of radiation-induced LPS (Fig. 2C and D). Based on these results, immunohistochemical staining of the brain lesion was performed again. The results showed that the tumor cells in the intracranial lesion were positive for S100, CDK4, MDM2, fascin, follicular dendritic cells (FDCs), and calponin; H3K27me3 was not expressed in most of them. These findings were similar to those of the left femoral dedifferentiated LPS, which was composed of spindle-shaped cells with oval nuclei and highly pleomorphic cells, had few nuclear divisions, and was positive for CDK4 and MDM2 (Fig. 3B–D).

Based on the patient’s history of NK/T-cell lymphoma, history of TBI and malignant spindle cell intracranial and femoral tumors, and the pathological diagnosis of radiation-induced LPS (CDK4 and MDM2 positive) in the femoral tumor, the intracranial lesion was diagnosed as a primary pleomorphic LPS, and the femoral tumor was diagnosed as a metastatic tumor of the primary tumor.

Discussion

Observations

Here, we reported the case of patient with a pleomorphic LPS that was classified as a PXA by DNA methylation array profiling.

Sarcomas are a heterogeneous group of rare malignant tumors, with more than 100 subtypes described in the current World Health Organization (WHO) classification.8 Half of all sarcomas lack characteristic molecular and histomorphological features, often leading to misclassification and disagreement among pathologists.9–11

Primary intracranial LPSs are exceedingly rare, with only seven documented cases in the medical literature. These cases are summarized in Table 1.12–18 LPSs are tumors of primitive cellular origin that can differentiate into fat. Previous reports have shown that they account for approximately 9.8%–18% of soft tissue sarcomas, second in incidence only to malignant fibrous histiocytoma.1 Soft tissue sarcomas are also relatively rare compared to other malignancies, as reported in Japan; statistics from the National Soft Tissue Tumor Registry for 2018 indicate that 1980 soft tissue sarcoma patients (approximately 3 per 100,000 population) were diagnosed and treated in Japan each year. Based on molecular and clinicopathological differences, there are five subtypes of LPS recognized by the WHO in the latest update of the LPS classification published in 2020. They are as follows: type 1, hyperdifferentiated LPS; type 2, dedifferentiated LPS; type 3, myxoid LPS; type 4, pleomorphic LPS; and type 5, myxoid pleomorphic LPS, also called “atypical lipomatous tumor.” The present case was a pleomorphic LPS.2 The peak incidence of LPS is between 40 and 60 years of age, with a slightly higher incidence in males.19 Pediatric cases of LPS are rare but tend to occur in the teenage years.3 There are also rare cases in which radiotherapy can cause metastatic soft tissue tumors and secondary soft tissue tumors. Plaza et al. reported that 118 (1.6%) of 7237 soft tissue tumors were metastatic soft tissue tumors.20

Espat et al. evaluated 3829 patients with soft tissue tumors and found brain metastases in 0.5% (19 of 3829) of the total patient group.21 Among the total number of patients, only five had brain metastases resulting from LPS. Of the 19 patients who developed brain metastases, 18 had lung metastases as the immediate site of disease, with brain metastases subsequently identified. This indicates that the brain is an exceedingly uncommon initial site of metastasis in LPS. Furthermore, extracranial LPS has a relatively slow progression, with an average latency period of 10–20 years before the onset of brain metastasis. The most common primary site is the lower extremities.22, 23 In the present case, the discovery of the intracranial lesions occurred concurrently with the absence of metastatic lesions in the lungs and the presence of a single, very small lesion in the lower extremities as the only other site of involvement. If the intracranial lesion was a metastatic lesion, the lesion in the lower extremities had probably existed for a considerable period. It is unlikely that it would have been identified upon closer examination after the onset of symptoms of the intracranial lesion, as observed in the present case.

The DNA methylation-based profiling classifier of the DKFZ resulted in the tumor being classified as a PXA due to the presence of a deletion on chromosome 9, including the CDKN2A/B region, which is relatively specific for PXA in intracranial lesions. The calibrated score was 0.95, which was highly reliable. Based on the results of histopathological examination of the tumor in the left femoral region, immunohistochemical staining was performed again, and the tumor cells were positive for S100, CDK4, MDM2, fascin, FDC, and calponin, and the intracranial lesion was classified as a primary pleomorphic LPS. The lesion in the left femoral region was finally determined to be a dedifferentiated metastatic LPS. DNA methylation array profiling showed a deletion in chromosome 9, which includes the CDKN2A/B region, and this diagnosis was made because PXA is relatively specific for intracranial lesions. Groisberg et al. reported that patients with leiomyosarcoma, dedifferentiated LPS, and well-differentiated LPS had mutations in CDK4 (23.5%), MDM2 (21.6%), and CDKN2A/B (13.7%), respectively.24

Although DNA methylation array profiling is very useful, it is still a research tool and faces multiple challenges before it can be applied in routine clinical practice. For instance, DNA methylation array profiling can clearly separate and diagnose pleomorphic sarcomas from non-mesenchymal neoplasms such as mesothelioma and melanoma.25, 26 In contrast, several tumor types, including myxofibrosarcoma and many other pleomorphic LPSs, are still largely indistinguishable by DNA methylation patterns, since 25% of cases in the DKFZ cohort and 39% of cases in the external validation cohort were unclassifiable in a study by Koelsche et al.4 Furthermore, a study by Lyskjaer et al. reported that a subtype that was less frequently classified correctly using this classifier was pleomorphic LPS (sensitivity 29%).6 It has also been said that there are problems related to minimal sample purity for stable classification accuracy, high costs, and other limitations.4–6

Thus, sarcomas could be diagnosed by methylation profiles that differ from their respective reference sets. Therefore, in clinical practice, the results of the classifier are considered a second, unbiased diagnostic finding. If the classifier is consistent with a diagnosis suspected from other tests, the diagnosis is more likely to be accepted. However, if the results are consistent with the classifier but they contradict pathological and other findings, a thorough re-evaluation and additional molecular diagnostics are necessary. Over the past several years, DNA methylation array profiling has played a role in the characterization of sarcomas with inconclusive morphological and molecular features, and it has helped to discover new subgroups.27–30

In the future, the incorporation of new sarcoma subtypes not currently represented in the classifier, as well as the expansion of the dataset, will most likely increase the accuracy of the classifier. More interestingly, the combination of methylation analysis with the evolving multitopic characterization of sarcomas will synergistically address the diagnostic ambiguity of sarcomas by improving individual tumor profiling.31, 32 It is therefore with great interest that further clinical experience with and validation studies of the sarcoma classifier are awaited.

Lessons

We described a case in which DNA methylation array profiling was performed as a diagnostic aid for a brain tumor that was difficult to diagnose through histopathology alone, and the classifier identified a pleomorphic LPS as a PXA. In such cases, DNA methylation array profiling is often an aid to diagnosis. However, it should not be forgotten that there are cases, such as metastatic tumors, for which the DKFZ methylation classifier does not match DNA methylation array profiling. Therefore, even if the DNA methylation array matches the diagnosis, a thorough review of the clinical picture and pathological findings is still necessary.
