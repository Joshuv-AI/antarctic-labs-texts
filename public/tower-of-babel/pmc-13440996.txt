From parent–teacher collaboration to young children's numeracy skills: An examination of the underlying mechanisms
Authors: Melissa Pearl Caldwell, Bertha H. C. Kum, Pui Shan Yip, Tik Sze Carrey Siu, Joyce Lok Yin Kwan, Sum Kwing Cheung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGrowing evidence shows that parent–teacher collaboration benefits parents and their children. Yet, there is limited understanding of whether and how parent–teacher collaboration contributes to children's early numeracy skills.AimsThis study examined whether parent–teacher collaboration is linked to children's early numeracy skills via parents' stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction, perceived importance of their role in early math learning, perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math and the frequency of home numeracy activities.SampleOne hundred eighty‐two kindergarteners in Hong Kong and their parents participated in the study.MethodsThe children were individually tested, whereas their parents completed a questionnaire.ResultsStructural equation modelling revealed that higher levels of parent–teacher collaboration were associated with better early numeracy skills in children via: (1) reduced parents' stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction and then increased home numeracy activities; (2) increased parents' perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math and then increased home numeracy activities. The higher the level of parent–teacher collaboration, the more parents perceived themselves as playing an important role in early math learning. However, parents' perceived importance of their role was not a significant correlate of the frequency of home numeracy activities.ConclusionsThis study sheds light on the importance of promoting a positive relationship between parents and teachers. It also demonstrates the necessity to promote parents' positive emotions towards their parent–child interactions and enhance parents' skills in supporting children's early math learning.

INTRODUCTION

Family and school are two primary environments where children learn and develop. They contribute to children's developmental journey in different but complementary ways (Rimm‐Kaufman & Pianta, 2005). In recent years, there has been growing evidence of the benefits of parent–teacher collaboration for parents and their children (Ivanova et al., 2020; Lang et al., 2020; Lekli & Kaloti, 2015). Although early numeracy skills are crucial for later math learning (Jordan et al., 2009; LeFevre, Fast, et al., 2010) and deserve research attention, up until now, we know little about the relation between parent–teacher collaboration and young children's numeracy skills and its underlying mechanisms.

In the literature, parent–teacher collaboration has been found to alleviate parent–child conflict, reinforce parents' support for their children's learning and enhance parents' abilities to teach their children (Ivanova et al., 2020; Lang et al., 2020; Lekli & Kaloti, 2015). Meanwhile, there is evidence that parental stress, especially stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction, may create barriers to parental involvement in their children's learning at home (Baker, 2013; Can & Ginsburg‐Block, 2016). Parental beliefs about the importance of their involvement in children's education and beliefs about their ability to support children's academic development have also been shown as essential determinants of the home learning environment (Cheung et al., 2021; Cheung, Ho, et al., 2024; Surrain & Luk, 2023). In light of the above, the present study investigated whether parent–teacher collaboration is associated with children's early numeracy skills through parents' stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction, perceived importance of their role in early math learning, perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math and the frequency of home numeracy activities. The study can expand knowledge in the home math literature by providing insights into the mechanisms linking parent–teacher collaboration to children's early numeracy skills.

Benefits of parent–teacher collaboration

Various theoretical perspectives can provide insights into the potential benefits of parent–teacher collaboration for parents and children. Bronfenbrenner's (1979) ecological systems theory, for example, posits that children's development is influenced by interrelated environmental systems. While the microsystem (which constitutes the immediate settings and interactions with the surrounding people) has direct impacts on children's development, the mesosystem (which refers to the reciprocal connections and interactions between microsystems, such as the parent–teacher relationship) can also contribute to development both directly and indirectly through microsystem processes (Bronfenbrenner, 1979). A strong parent–teacher partnership may therefore benefit children's academic performance by helping parents gain deeper insights into their children's learning needs and enabling them to respond effectively to their children. Meanwhile, Hobfoll's (2011) conservation of resources theory presumes that individuals experience stress when their available resources are not sufficient to meet the challenges they encounter. Based on this notion, parent–teacher collaboration can function as a valuable source of resource gain for parents, as they can acquire knowledge about how to support their children to learn and receive emotional support from teachers. These gains can help parents build their resource caravans, thereby reducing their parenting stress, including stress associated with interacting with their children. Role theory (e.g., Biddle, 1986), on the other hand, postulates that individuals construct their roles based on others' expectations. Accordingly, parent–teacher collaboration can help parents better understand their role in children's learning. Last but not least, Bandura's (1997) theory asserts that mastery experiences, vicarious experience and social persuasion are key sources influencing individuals' self‐efficacy in performing a task. Through parent–teacher collaboration, teachers can empower parents with confidence and effective strategies for helping their children learn. When parents apply these strategies at home, they gain mastery experiences, which in turn can increase their self‐efficacy in helping their children learn.

Currently, several studies have provided empirical support for the positive impact of parent–teacher collaboration on children's academic success, the home learning environment and the parent–child relationship. Specifically, Cook et al. (2018) found that first graders whose parents and teachers reported strong cooperation had better academic skills (e.g., reading and mathematics). Meanwhile, Lin et al. (2019) showed that parents' perception of parent–teacher communication was positively associated with the frequency of specific learning activities, such as letter and number recognition, at home. Lang et al. (2020), on the other hand, revealed that parents who reported positive collaboration with their children's teachers showed fewer parent–child conflicts.

Despite the aforementioned benefits of parent–teacher collaboration for both parents and their children, little is still known about whether and how parent–teacher collaboration contributes to children's early numeracy skills. Further investigations in this area are therefore required.

Parental antecedents of the home learning environment

According to the model of the parental involvement process proposed by Hoover‐Dempsey et al. (2010), parents' personal motivators (such as their perceptions of the role of parents in children's learning and their efficacy in helping their children succeed in school), as well as their life contexts (such as their knowledge, skills, time and energy), can influence the extent to which they become involved in children's education (e.g., creating a stimulating home learning environment). Wigfield and Eccles's (1992) expectancy‐value theory, on the other hand, posits that an individual's motivation to engage in a particular behaviour is determined by expectancy for success (i.e., one's belief in their ability to perform a task successfully) and subjective task value (i.e., the perceived intrinsic value of that task). Therefore, whether parents are motivated to support their children's learning depends on the extent to which they feel competent to provide this support and regard their role as important (Monteiro et al., 2015).

Based on the above theoretical frameworks, one parental factor that is likely to influence the home learning environment parents create for their children is parenting stress, that is, the emotional strain linked to parenting roles (Abidin, 1990). This is because parenting stress may limit parents' available emotional energy for engaging their children in learning activities. It may also undermine parents' perceptions of both the value of and their ability to get involved in children's learning. As one type of parenting stress, stress from parent–child dysfunctional interactions, in particular, has been shown to significantly link with the home learning environment (Abidin, 1995). For instance, in a study of low‐income preschoolers aged three to five years and their families, Can and Ginsburg‐Block (2016) studied the relationship between parenting stress and home‐based literacy interactions. Stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction was found to negatively influence parental general home‐based involvement activities (e.g., engaging in academic activities together, maintaining a consistent morning and bedtime routine) and literacy practices (e.g., parent–child interactive reading and parental modelling of literacy behaviours). In the studies of Baker and Iruka (2013) and Nievar and Luster (2006), parents with high parenting stress also found it difficult to provide learning materials like books and become disengaged from interactive parent–child activities. From the above, it can be seen that parenting stress may contribute to a less‐supportive home learning environment, potentially hindering young children's early learning and development. Notably, research on the impact of parental stress on home learning activities and academic development is still an emerging area of study. Although several recent studies have examined how parental stress influences literacy‐related home learning activities (Baker, 2013; Can & Ginsburg‐Block, 2016), there is still very limited attention given to the impact of parenting stress on home numeracy activities and children's numeracy skills.

Furthermore, consistent with the model proposed by Hoover‐Dempsey et al. (2010) and expectancy‐value theory (Wigfield & Eccles, 1992), several studies have demonstrated that parents' beliefs about the importance of their involvement in children's education, as well as their beliefs about their own competence in supporting children's learning, can affect their teaching practice at home. For instance, Katz et al. (2011) found that parents of primary school children who view their involvement as important and perceive themselves as competent were more actively engaged in helping their children with homework. Similarly, Green et al. (2007) indicated that parents with higher self‐efficacy regarding their role in their children's education were more likely to be involved in home learning activities. More recently, Cheung, Ho, et al. (2024) revealed that parents of preschool children who believed in their ability to support their children's math learning were more likely to engage in home math activities. While existing research highlights the critical role of parents' perceived importance and competence in supporting their children's learning at home, there remains a minimal understanding of whether parent–teacher collaboration contributes to children's numeracy skills through these parental beliefs and the numeracy learning activities parents initiate at home. It is thus essential to conduct more research on this topic.

Home numeracy activities and children's early numeracy skills

Home numeracy activities are a fundamental component of the home learning environment. Examples of these activities include formally teaching and practicing numeracy skills, talking about numbers in day‐to‐day life and playing games that involve numbers (Cheung et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2017). According to skill development models (Pomerantz et al., 2012), when parents frequently engage their children in learning activities at home, children gain more opportunities to acquire and practice various academic skills, which in turn can enhance their academic performance. It can therefore be assumed that frequent participation in home numeracy activities can promote children's numeracy performance.

Nonetheless, the relation between home numeracy activities and children's numeracy skills has shown mixed results in the literature. For example, in the studies of Mutaf Yıldız et al. (2018) and Vasilyeva et al. (2018), positive associations were found between the frequency of formal numeracy activities (e.g., writing numbers, counting objects, reading number books) at home and preschool children's math abilities (e.g., enumeration skills, arithmetic skills and symbolic number knowledge). Susperreguy et al. (2020) also showed that the more frequently parents engage their preschool children in operational numeracy activities (e.g., manipulation of digits), the greater their children's early numeracy skills (e.g., arithmetic skills). However, in the studies of Deng et al. (2015) and Missall et al. (2015), the frequency of home numeracy activities did not play a significant role in children's math abilities. Given these mixed findings, it is important to consider whether key variables have been overlooked when examining the relation between home numeracy activities and young children's numeracy skills.

Current study

The current study aimed to explore the mechanisms linking parent–teacher collaboration to young children's early numeracy skills. In particular, we are interested in examining whether parent–teacher collaboration is related to young children's early numeracy skills through parents' stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction, their perceived importance of their role in early math learning, perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math and the frequency of home numeracy activities.

This study recruited children and their parents in Hong Kong as participants. In Hong Kong, children typically attend three years of kindergarten starting at age three, prior to entering primary school (Education Bureau, 2025). The teacher‐to‐child ratio in each kindergarten class is at most 1:11, and children usually stay with the same teachers and classmates throughout the school year (Education Bureau, 2025). The primary goal of the kindergarten education curriculum is to promote children's holistic and balanced development (Curriculum Development Council, 2017). Children generally learn through play and integrated learning activities designed by their teachers (Curriculum Development Council, 2017).

This study adds to the existing literature in two ways. First, while parent–teacher collaboration has been shown to contribute to children's academic performance positively, little research has examined the relationship between parent–teacher collaboration and young children's early numeracy skills. Second, although previous studies have emphasized the importance of certain parental factors for the home learning environment parents create for their children, the mechanisms underlying how parent–teacher collaboration contributes to children's early academic outcomes are still underexplored. The present study offers a comprehensive examination of various potential pathways from parent–child collaboration to children's early numeracy skills. The pathways under investigation cover different aspects of parenting, including parental well‐being (parents' stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction), parental role perception (parents' perceived importance of their role in early math learning), parental competence (parents' perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math), as well as parental practice (home numeracy activities).

Based on Hobfoll's (2011) conservation of resources theory, role theory (e.g., Biddle, 1986) and Bandura's (1997) theory, parent–teacher collaboration can help parents reduce parenting stress by providing valuable informational and social resources for interacting with their children, understand their important role in children's learning and gain mastery experiences in supporting their children's learning. Therefore, we hypothesized that parent–teacher collaboration would be negatively associated with parents' stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction and positively associated with parents' perceived importance of their role in early math learning and perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math. In addition, according to the model proposed by Hoover‐Dempsey et al. (2010) and expectancy‐value theory (Wigfield & Eccles, 1992), parenting stress can hinder parental involvement in children's learning by undermining parents' available emotional energy and their perceived value of, and ability to engage in, such involvement. In contrast, parents' expectancy of successfully supporting their children's learning and the subjective value they associate with this task may enhance their motivation to do so. Therefore, we hypothesized that parents' stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction would have a negative link with home numeracy activities, whereas parents' perceived importance of their role in early math learning and perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math would positively relate to the frequency of home numeracy activities. Finally, drawing on skill development models proposed by Pomerantz et al. (2012), as well as empirical evidence regarding the relation between home numeracy activities and children's numeracy outcomes (e.g., Deng et al., 2015; Mutaf Yıldız et al., 2018; Susperreguy et al., 2020), we hypothesized that greater engagement in home numeracy activities would be positively associated with children's numeracy skills. Although the present study employed a cross‐sectional design, which hinders firm causal inference (Hamaker et al., 2020), we rule out alternative equivalent model based on our theoretical assumptions underlying the relationships of the variables as suggested by MacCallum et al. (1993) and Thoemmes (2015). Specifically, the proposed directional pathways are grounded in established theories and prior empirical evidence suggesting that relational factors, such as parent–teacher collaboration, may contribute to parental stress and beliefs. We included children's gender, non‐verbal intelligence and family income as covariates in the model based on prior literature. Children's gender has been linked to home numeracy activities and early math outcomes, partly due to stereotypes that may shape parental practices (Flore & Wicherts, 2015; Gunderson et al., 2012; LeFevre, Fast, et al., 2010). Non‐verbal intelligence has also been identified as a domain‐general predictor of early numeracy skills (Bernabini et al., 2020; Lee et al., 2012; Purpura & Logan, 2015). Lastly, family income is positively associated with children's math skills through access to educational resources and enriched learning environments (Chiu & Zeng, 2008; Duncan & Magnuson, 2011; Reardon & Portilla, 2016).

METHODS

Participants and procedure

The data of this study came from a larger study about early numeracy development. Parent–child dyads were included in the sample only if both parties completed the tasks required for the study.

Participants of this study included 182 second‐year kindergarteners (46.2% girls) and their parents (85.7% mothers). A priori power analysis using the R package ‘semPower’ (Moshagen & Bader, 2024) suggested that a minimum sample size of 127 was required to detect model misspecification based on RMSEA given a model with 31 degree of freedom and statistical power of 0.8. The age of the children ranged from 3.75 to 5.50 years (M = 4.35 years, SD = .35 years). The median monthly family income reported by the parents was HK$30,001–50,000 (approximately US$3846.3–6410.3), which is comparable to the median monthly income of three‐person households (i.e., HK$36,000; approximately US$4615.4) and that of four‐person households (i.e., HK$49,900; approximately US$6397.4) in Hong Kong in 2024 (Census and Statistics Department, 2025a).

Ethical approval for our study was granted by the Human Research Ethics Committee, which is affiliated with the first author. Prior to data collection, written informed consent was obtained from both the participating kindergartens and parents. Children were tested individually on their numeracy skills and non‐verbal intelligence in a quiet area at their kindergartens by trained research assistants and undergraduate students. Meanwhile, parents were asked to report their levels of collaboration with their children's teachers, stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction, perceived importance of their role in early math learning, perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math, the frequency of home numeracy activities and various demographic information through a paper‐and‐pencil questionnaire. Parents completed the questionnaire at home and returned it in a sealed envelope via their respective kindergarten.

Measures

Parent–teacher collaboration

The 14‐item Collaboration subscale of the Parent–Caregiver Relationship Scale (Elicker et al., 1997) was used to examine the level of collaboration between parents and teachers and was translated into Chinese. No changes were made to the original content except for one point: as the original scale was developed to evaluate the relationship between parents and caregivers in child care centres and family child care homes, the word ‘caregiver’ in all items was replaced with ‘teacher’ to better reflect the school context. Parents rated on a five‐point scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree) to indicate their agreement with the statements presented (e.g., I trust the teacher will tell me important things about my child). Reversed items were recoded, and the mean score was then calculated for subsequent analysis. A higher score represents a higher level of parent–teacher collaboration. The Cronbach's alpha for the measure was .87 in this study.

Parents' stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction

The Parent–child Dysfunctional Interaction subscale from the Parenting Stress Inventory – Short Form (Abidin, 1995) was adopted and translated into Chinese without any modification to its original content. There was a total of 12 items. A sample item was ‘Sometimes I feel my child does not like me and does not want to be close to me’. Parents were given a five‐point scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree) to indicate their level of agreement to the statements provided. The mean score was obtained by averaging the item scores. A higher mean score indicates a higher level of parenting stress. In this study, the Cronbach's alpha for the measure was .87.

Parents' perceived importance of their role in early math learning

The four‐item scale by Cheung et al. (2018) was adapted. A six‐point scale (1 = strongly disagree; 6 = strongly agree) was provided for parents to indicate their level of agreement with the given statements (e.g., Parents play an important role in young children's math development). The mean score across items was computed. A higher mean score reflected a higher level of perceived importance of parents' role in children's early math learning. The scale had a Cronbach's alpha of .91 in this study.

Parents' perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math

The five‐item scale by Cheung, Dulay, et al. (2024), adapted from the Brief Parental Self Efficacy Scale (Woolgar et al., 2025), was adopted. Parents rated on a five‐point scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree) to indicate their degree of agreement with the statements in the scale (e.g., I can make an important difference to my child's math learning). The mean score across items was computed for later analysis. A higher mean score showed a higher level of perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math. In this study, the scale had a Cronbach's alpha of .88.

Home numeracy activities

The seven‐item scale by Cheung et al. (2018) was employed. Parents were given a six‐point scale (1 = never; 6 = several times a day) to indicate the frequency of their children's participation in various numeracy activities (e.g., reading children's books related to numbers) at home over the past month. Scores of the items were averaged to obtain the mean score, with a higher score indicating a higher frequency. The Cronbach's alpha of the scale was .83 in this study.

Children's early numeracy skills

Three tasks, including Object Counting (5 items), Numerical Identification (18 items) and Next Number (15 items), were used to evaluate children's early numeracy skills. The first two tasks were adapted from Cheung et al. (2018), whereas the last one was adapted from LeFevre, Polyzoi, et al. (2010). In the Object Counting task, pictures with different numbers of animals (ranging from 5 to 17) were shown to children, and they were asked to give the correct number of animals in each picture. In the Number Identification task, children were presented with a list of Arabic numerals (including five numerals below 20, five between 21 and 50, five between 51 and 100 and three above 100) and were asked to read them out. In the Next Number task, a number (ranging from 2 to 98) was shown to children in each item, and they were asked to provide the number that comes next. For all three tasks, each correct answer was awarded one point, and the total score was used for subsequent analysis. The minimum possible score was 0 for all three tasks, while the maximum possible scores were 5 for the Counting Objects task, 18 for the Number Identification task and 15 for the Next Number task. In this study, the Spearman‐Brown reliability coefficients of the three tasks were .89, .96 and .95, respectively.

Children's non‐verbal intelligence

Sets A and B of Raven's Coloured Progressive Matrices (Raven et al., 1998) were applied. There were 24 items in total. In each item, children were presented with a picture with a missing part and were asked to identify one that fits in from six choices. Each correct answer scored one point. The total score was calculated, with a minimum possible score of 0 and a maximum possible score of 24. A higher total score indicates a higher level of non‐verbal intelligence. In this study, the Spearman‐Brown reliability coefficient of the task was .67.

Demographics

Parents were instructed to report their children's age. They were also asked to indicate their monthly family income by selecting from the following options: HK$5000 or below (coded as ‘1’), HK$5001–10,000 (coded as ‘2’), HK$10,001–20,000 (coded as ‘3’), HK$20,001–30,000 (coded as ‘4’), HK$30,001–40,000 (coded as ‘5’), HK$40,001–50,000 (coded as ‘6’), HK$50,001–60,000 (coded as ‘7’), HK$60,001–70,000 (coded as ‘8’), HK$70,001–80,000 (coded as ‘9’), HK$80,001–90,000 (coded as ‘10’) and HK$90,001 or above (coded as ‘11’).

As demonstrated in Table 1, parent–teacher collaboration, parents' perceived importance of their role in early math learning, parents' perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math, and home numeracy activities were positively correlated with each other, with rs ranging from .17 to .42 (p < .05). Parents' stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction was negatively correlated with parent–teacher collaboration (r = −.31, p < .001) and home numeracy activities (r = −.21, p < .01), but did not show any significant correlations with parents' perceived importance of their role in early math learning and parents' perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math (ps > .05).

Meanwhile, children's gender was positively correlated with parents' perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math (r = .16, p < .05), home numeracy activities (r = .16, p < .05) and children's performance on the Next Number task (r = .16, p < .05). In contrast, children's age did not show any significant correlations with the parental variables and children's performance on the three early numeracy tasks. Hence, only children's gender, children's non‐verbal intelligence and family income were included in the structural equation model as covariates.

Structural equation modelling

To examine the underlying mechanism between parent–teacher collaboration and children's early numeracy development, structural equation modelling was conducted to test our hypothesized model. Path analyses were performed using the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012) in R (R Core Team, 2021). Based on the results of our preliminary analysis and prior research (e.g., Duncan & Magnuson, 2011; LeFevre, Fast, et al., 2010; Purpura & Logan, 2015), in our structural equation model, children's gender, children's non‐verbal intelligence and family income were treated as covariates of children's early numeracy skills. We also correlated the error terms of parents' perceived importance of their role in early math learning and parents' perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math, children's gender and the error term of home numeracy activities, and children's gender and the error term of parents' perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math. Results showed that our model yielded an adequate goodness‐of‐fit: χ2(31) = 41.819, p = .093; CFI = .965; TLI = .941; RMSEA = .044 (CI: [.000, .075], p = .592); SRMR = .047; AIC = 6036.144; BIC = 6183.528. As evidenced by the 95% bias‐corrected bootstrap confidence intervals (BCCIs), parent–teacher collaboration was indirectly associated with children's early numeracy skills through two pathways: (1) through reduced parents' stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction first and then increased home numeracy activities (β = .02, 95% BCCI: [.005, .093]); and (2) through increased parents' perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math first and then increased home numeracy activities (β = .02, 95% BCCI: [.008, .114]).

DISCUSSION

The current study investigated the mechanisms underlying the relationship between parent–teacher collaboration and young children's numeracy skills. Our findings revealed that there was a pathway from parent–teacher collaboration to children's early numeracy skills via parents' stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction and then home numeracy activities. Parent–teacher collaboration also contributed to children's early numeracy skills first through parents' perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math and then home numeracy activities.

Consistent with our hypothesis, greater collaboration between parents and teachers was linked with better early numeracy skills in children through reduced parental stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction first and then increased home numeracy activities. These findings, on the one hand, align with past research studies showing that parent–teacher collaboration can alleviate parent–child conflict (Lang et al., 2020), as well as those demonstrating that parenting stress reduces parents' likelihood to create a stimulating home learning environment (e.g., Can & Ginsburg‐Block, 2016; Nievar & Luster, 2006). They, on the other hand, echo the wealth of literature regarding the important role played by home numeracy activities in young children's foundational math skills (e.g., Cheung et al., 2023; Ouyang et al., 2021; Susperreguy et al., 2020). Our results can be further explained using Hobfoll's (2011) conservation of resources theory, Hoover‐Dempsey et al.'s (2010) model of the parental involvement process and Pomerantz et al.'s (2012) skill development models. Specifically, when parents communicate closely with teachers about their children's behaviours, they can gain skills for interacting with their children as well as emotional support from teachers. These resources can reduce parental stress associated with parent–child interactions and, in turn, leave parents with greater emotional energy for initiating home learning activities, including those related to numeracy. With more opportunities to acquire and practice various numeracy concepts and skills at home, the children are likely to develop better early numeracy skills.

In line with our hypothesis, we identified a positive pathway from parent–teacher collaboration to young children's numeracy skills through parents' perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math and home numeracy activities. These findings are consistent with previous research suggesting that the relationship between parents and teachers can influence parents' teaching approaches and behaviours at home (e.g., Christenson, 2004; Lin et al., 2019). They also align with the study by Cheung, Ho, et al. (2024), which found a positive correlation between parents' perceived competence in assisting their children with math and their home‐based involvement in kindergarten children's math learning. To account for our findings, Bandura's (1997) theory, Wigfield and Eccles's (1992) expectancy‐value theory, and Pomerantz et al.'s (2012) skill development models can be applied. Parent–teacher collaboration can empower parents with skills for interacting with their children, thereby fostering mastery experiences in supporting their children's learning. As a result, parents feel more confident in supporting their children's math learning and are more likely to engage their children in home numeracy activities, which subsequently benefits children's early numeracy skills.

Interestingly, although parent–teacher collaboration was found to be positively associated with parents' perceived importance of their role in early math learning, there was no significant association between parents' perceived importance of their role in early math learning and the frequency of home numeracy activities. These results are contradictory to the expectancy‐value theory (Wigfield & Eccles, 1992), which indicates that individuals' subjective value assigned to a particular behaviour is crucial for their motivation to engage in the behaviour. One plausible explanation of our findings is that close collaboration between parents and teachers may help parents recognize the vital role they play in providing support to children for early math learning, but parents may not necessarily be able to translate their beliefs into practice and actively engage their children in numeracy activities at home. Indeed, contextual factors may contribute to this discrepancy. In Hong Kong, long working hours are common, with the median weekly time at 44 h (Census and Statistics Department, 2025b), and annual averages are relatively high compared to global levels (Union Bank of Switzerland, 2016). When combined with high living costs, job insecurity and limited income support, these demands frequently compel parents to place financial stability above family time (Kim, 2021; Kim, 2022; Lau & Wong, 2018). From a cultural perspective, sacrificing time with children in order to work longer hours is often perceived as a way of fulfilling parental responsibilities by ensuring long‐term benefits (Chen et al., 2022; Le et al., 2020; Redding et al., 1994), such as paying for their children's education, extracurricular activities and private tutoring. As a result, these constraints limit the extent to which they can enact their beliefs about the importance of supporting children's math learning. Indeed, as evidenced in our final model, compared to parents' perceived importance of their role in early math learning, parents' perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math matters more for their engagement in their children's numeracy learning at home.

Practical implications

Our results have several practical implications. First, it is essential to support parents and teachers in maintaining close relationships with each other. This may not only provide parents with emotional support to help them get through challenges encountered during interactions with their children but also reinforce their role and confidence in supporting children to learn math at home.

Second, it is crucial to provide parents with practical strategies for how to communicate and interact with their children effectively. When parents gain more positive and rewarding experiences during parent–child interactions, they may be more motivated to engage their children in home learning activities and scaffold their children's learning.

Third, it is important to enhance parents' confidence in supporting children's math learning by equipping them with knowledge and skills to engage their children in numeracy activities at home. Specifically, structured workshops and online resources could be provided to parents to help them understand examples of numeracy activities suitable for children of different ages. Practical tips on how to introduce numeracy concepts to children in everyday life in an engaging manner can also be offered.

Limitations and future directions

There are several limitations about this study that should be acknowledged. First, the present study assessed parent–teacher collaboration only from parents' perspectives, without incorporating teachers' perspectives. As parents and teachers may have different views of their collaboration, future research may fill this gap by combining both perspectives to better understand how the relationship between parents and teachers contributes to parental engagement in home numeracy activities and young children's numeracy skills.

Second, our sample was limited to kindergarten children and their parents. As the levels of parent–teacher collaboration, parents' perceived importance of their role in children's learning, and parents' perceived competence in helping children learn may change as children transition to primary school, further studies are needed to explore whether the mechanisms linking parent–teacher collaboration to children's numeracy skills observed in the present study may change across developmental stages.

Lastly, this study employed a cross‐sectional design, and causal inferences could not be made. The relations between parent–teacher collaboration and certain parental variables may also be bidirectional. For instance, although we argue in the present study that high levels of parent–teacher collaboration are associated with reduced parental stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction, high parental stress may also hinder parent–teacher collaboration, as there is evidence that teachers' depressive symptoms are associated with lower mathematics achievement in children through poorer family–teacher relationships (Jeon et al., 2021). To further support the robustness of our proposed model, we conducted additional analyses to compare alternative pathways of parent–teacher collaboration and parental variables. Although in the reverse direction, the relations between parent–teacher collaboration and parental variables remained significant in the alternative models. However, none of the results supported parent–teacher collaboration as a predictor of home numeracy activities and children's early math learning. Future studies can adopt longitudinal designs to explore whether parent–teacher collaboration predicts children's math development over time and whether there are any reciprocal relations between parent–teacher collaboration, various parental variables and children's math achievement.

CONCLUSIONS

Grounded in multiple theoretical perspectives (e.g., Bandura, 1997; Biddle, 1986; Hobfoll, 2011; Hoover‐Dempsey et al., 2010; Pomerantz et al., 2012; Wigfield & Eccles, 1992), the present study extends our understanding of the role of parent–teacher collaboration in early development by exploring how parent–teacher collaboration contributes to children's early numeracy skills. Our findings indicate that greater collaboration between parents and teachers was associated with better early numeracy skills in children through two pathways: (1) reduced parents' stress from parent–child dysfunctional interaction and then increased home numeracy activities; (2) increased perceived behavioural control about helping children learn math and then increased home numeracy activities. In conclusion, this study not only provides a comprehensive framework of the mechanisms linking parent–teacher collaboration to children's early numeracy skills but also offers some directions for what parent education activities can cover.
