Improving sampling efficacy on high-dimensional distributions with thin high-density regions using Conservative Hamiltonian Monte Carlo
Authors: Geoffrey McGregor, Andy T S Wan, Michael Harris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHamiltonian Monte Carlo is a prominent Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithm, which employs symplectic integrators to sample from high-dimensional target distributions in many applications, such as statistical mechanics, Bayesian statistics, and generative models. However, such distributions tend to have thin high-density regions, posing a significant challenge for symplectic integrators to maintain the small energy errors needed for a high acceptance probability. Instead, we propose a variant called Conservative Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, using R-reversible energy-preserving integrators to retain a high acceptance probability. We show our algorithm can achieve approximate stationarity with an error determined by the Jacobian approximation of the energy-preserving proposal map and its approximation error due to inexact solutions of implicit schemes. Numerical evidence shows improved convergence and robustness over integration parameters on target distributions with thin high-density regions and in high dimensions. Moreover, a version of our algorithm can also be applied to target distributions without gradient information.Keywords: Markov Chain Monte Carlo, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, energy-preserving integrator, approximate stationarity

Recent progress in Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithms have enabled scientists and engineers to quantify the uncertainties in model parameters and to make reliable predictions by sampling probability distributions from observed data. This work introduces a variant to the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) algorithm by utilizing symmetrized energy-preserving numerical methods. The proposed algorithm can lead to higher acceptance probability and sampling efficiency for distributions with thin high-density regions, common for high-dimensional distributions. While further studies are needed to establish a convergence theory and improve efficiency of solving energy-preserving schemes, this work contributes to the on-going efforts to improve sampling efficacy of HMC for uncertainty quantification and reliable forecasting in high-dimensional applications.

Introduction

For more than half a century, Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms have been utilized in numerous applications across science and engineering, from its early days in statistical mechanics (1, 2) to Bayesian statistics (3, 4), and more recently in generative models (5, 6). A gradient-based MCMC algorithm known as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) (7–10) has seen recent wide adoption for many applications in Bayesian statistics. Specifically, given a target distribution π(θ), HMC extends the sample space by interpreting θ as generalized coordinate variables q∈Rd and introducing momentum variables p∈Rd giving rise to a joint distribution π(q,p)∝exp(−H(q,p)), where H(q,p)=K(p)+U(q) is the associated Hamiltonian function with the kinetic energy K(p)=12pTM−1p and the potential energy U(q)=−logπ(q). From a current sample (qi,pi), a new proposal (q*,p*) is obtained by numerically solving the associated Hamiltonian system q˙=M−1p,p˙=−∇U(q) over a prescribed time interval t∈[0,T] using a symplectic integrator (11) of step size τ, where typically a Leapfrog or Strömer–Verlet integrator is used. Under appropriate conditions, HMC would satisfy the stationarity condition (10), ensuring the generated samples converging to the target distribution.

One advantage of employing such a proposal map is far distant samples can be obtained via Hamiltonian dynamics, thus improving sampling efficiency over traditional random-walk MCMC algorithms. As symplectic integrators preserve volume (ie detJΨSYM=1 where ΨSYM denotes a symplectic proposal map), the Metropolis adjustment step can avoid the costly computation of the Jacobian of the proposal map JΨSYM (10), leading to HMC having an acceptance probability of αSYM=min(1,exp(−ΔH)), where ΔH is the energy difference between the proposed and current samples. For the Leapfrog integrator, it is known that ΔH=O(τ2) via backward error analysis (11, 12), leading to a high acceptance probability if τ is sufficiently small. Moreover, progress has been made to tune the integration parameters τ,T and mass matrix M, such as No-U-Turn sampler (13), tuning step sizes (12), and generalizing to M(q) to include curvature information in Riemannian Manifold HMC (7, 14). Other HMC variants have also been introduced for high-dimensional problems, including Split HMC (15) and stochastic gradient HMC (16).

However, despite the successes of HMC, there remains aspects of the algorithm which can still be improved. In particular, symplectic integrators do not in general preserve the Hamiltonian exactly. As the acceptance probability α depends on the error in the Hamiltonian, this can lead to more rejected proposals as the dimension d increases. Indeed, as discussed in Ref. (12) with suitable regularity assumptions on π, the step size τ used in the Leapfrog integrator of HMC must scale as O(d−14) in order to maintain a constant acceptance probability as d increases, and similarly for No-U-Turn sampler due to concentration effect of the canonical Gaussian (17). One intuitive explanation behind this performance decrease is that high-dimensional distributions can concentrate on thin high-density regions (18). Thus without adjusting the step size, increasing d leads to a decrease in sampling efficacy for HMC, as its proposals are likely to be rejected with Leapfrog integrator being unable to remain near the constant energy surface of thin high-density regions. Instead, we propose using energy-preserving integrators to alleviate this difficulty in sampling from high-dimensional distributions with concentrated high-density regions. Building upon ideas from Compressible Generalized HMC (19), which uses nonsymplectic integrators with exact Jacobian of the proposal map, our approach focuses on energy-preserving schemes with approximate Jacobian.

Conservative Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

In order to obtain samples which stay on the same Hamiltonian or energy level set (up to machine precision) after numerical integration, we propose to use energy-preserving integrators, instead of symplectic integrators.a From the field of geometric numerical integration (11), there are a number of well-known energy-preserving integrators,b such as the Itoh–Abe discrete gradient scheme (20), average vector field (AVF) discrete gradient scheme (21), or discrete multiplier method (DMM) (22–24). Any symmetrized version of these approaches could be used within our proposed algorithm, called Conservative Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (CHMC).

The CHMC algorithm is similar to that of HMC but with two distinct differences. First is the usage of an energy-preserving proposal map ΨEP, where a new proposal is obtained by integrating the associated Hamiltonian system using an energy-preserving integrator, starting at (q0,p0) with a uniform step size τ and a fixed integration length T.c In contrast, HMC typically utilizes symplectic integrators, or other integrators which do not preserve energy (19). Second is the appearance of the Jacobian approximationd of the proposal map, detJΨEP, due to the nonvolume preserving transformation of such energy-preserving integrators. While exact stationarity can be achieved for CHMC using the full Jacobian, as discussed in Supplementary Material A, we instead propose using an approximate Jacobian to strike a balance between computational efficiency and approximate stationarity. Implementation details

For a general target distribution π, numerical schemes which preserve H are typically implicit, where a nonlinear system needs to be solved at each step τ using iterative methods. These include fixed point iteration, quasi-Newton, and Newton methods (26), with respective linear, superlinear, and quadratic convergence, and respective computational costs of O(kd),O(kd2), and O(kd3), where k is the number of iterations utilized within each MCMC iteration.

Despite obtaining only inexact solutions due to solving implicit schemes using iterative methods or round-off errors for explicit schemes, the next corollary shows the error from stationarity can still be bounded by the determinant error ϵ and proposal map approximation error E due to inexact solutions, as proved in Supplementary Material C.

For sufficiently small step size, each iteration reduces the residual of the current energy error ΔH until it reaches below a desired energy tolerance δ. Thus, for CHMC to be efficient in practice, a balance needs to be struck between the energy tolerance δ and the number of iterations to solve the implicit energy-preserving scheme.

As discussed in Corollary 1 for p=2, employing a second-order energy-preserving R-reversible scheme in Algorithm 1 with detJΨEP=1 leads to samples satisfying approximate stationarity with an error of O(τ2). For instance, the symmetrized Itoh–Abe discrete gradient or DMM scheme (Supplementary Material E.1), or the AVF scheme (Supplementary Material E.2), are second-order energy-preserving R-reversible schemes, as detailed in Supplementary Material F–H. Specifically, the AVF scheme requires gradient information of H, followed typically by a quadrature approximation of an integral associated with the scheme. In contrast, the symmetrized scheme of the Itoh–Abe discrete gradient or DMM does not require gradient information of H, but with potential regularization needed for small divisors.

Moreover, the error from stationarity can be further reduced by choosing detJΨEP to be a higher order approximation of detJΨEP, such as using an improved approximation of the determinant involving traces, as detailed in Supplementary Material I. However, higher order approximations of the determinant generally add computational costs, which may outweigh the benefits of the improved error from stationarity. In our numerical experiments, we have employed CHMC with detJΨEP=1, to minimize the computational cost of the Jacobian. It is also worthwhile to point out that choosing detJΨEP=1 and an energy-preserving R-reversible integrator not requiring gradient information of H yields a H-gradient-free version of Algorithm 1, such as using the symmetrized Itoh–Abe discrete gradient scheme or symmetrized DMM scheme. The gradient-free CHMC may be useful in applications where first derivative of the target distribution is not readily accessible.

Results

We compare the sampling efficacy of CHMC versus HMC on two target distributions, p-generalized χ distribution and p-generalized Gaussian distribution (27). Unless stated otherwise, we utilized HMC with the Leapfrog integrator (HMC-LF), and CHMC with the symmetrized Itoh–Abe discrete gradient or DMM scheme and detJΨEP=1. Two Newton iterations are used with O(d) cost for each Newton iteration (Supplementary Material J), as the associated Jacobian matrices are diagonal. Furthermore, we include comparisons with CHMC-FullJ (ie detJΨEP=detJΨEP), which satisfies exact stationarity as shown in Supplementary Material A. For a baseline comparison, a uniform step size τ and a fixed integration length T are used for both algorithms, with adaptive step size and variable integration length left for future work.

p-generalized χ distribution

We first demonstrate the sampling efficacy of CHMC over HMC on distributions with concentrated high-density regions. Specifically, the p-generalized χ-distribution with d degrees of freedom (27) has the density function πd,p(ξ)=I(0,∞)(ξ)p1−dpΓ(dp)−1ξd−1exp(−ξpp).

As shown in Lemma 1 of Supplementary Material D, the majority of the density of πd,p lies in an annulus in ℓp norm with its width decreasing exponentially when p>2 and d≫1. Specifically, increasing p while fixing d, or vice-versa, increasing d while fixing p>2, leads to an exponential decrease in the width of the interval Id,p(ϵ) centered at ξ*=(d−1)1p which contains the majority of the density. Thus, despite being a 1D distribution, the exponentially thinning of the density πd,p makes this an ideal test case for comparing HMC-LF versus CHMC.

The first set of results are shown in Fig. 1, where the sampling efficacy of HMC-LF and CHMC are compared as the degrees of freedom d is varied. Figure 1a illustrates the thinning of the probability density πd,p(ξ) as d is increased. Figure 1b shows the histograms of combined samples generated by HMC-LF in blue, CHMC in green, and CHMC-FullJ in yellow, with Fig. 1c showing the associated violin plots across all ten chains. These results highlight HMC-LF’s slower rate of convergence due to the thin density region, as well as nonconvergence due to instability of the Leapfrog integrator. In contrast, CHMC continues to sample the target distribution effectively using the energy-preserving integrator. Additionally, as detailed in Supplementary Material K, Fig. 1d highlights the improved running time of CHMC over HMC-LF on fixed error measured in the Wasserstein-1 (28) and Kolmogorov–Smirnov (29, 30) distances. High-dimensional I.I.D. p-generalized Gaussian

We consider the family of independent identically distributed (I.I.D.) p-generalized Gaussian in Rd with the joint density π(x)∼exp(−p−1‖x‖pp). Here, 1<p<∞ and ‖x‖p denotes the ℓp norm of a random vector x∼Rd. Recalling from Ref. (27, Theorem 6), the random variable ξ:=‖x‖p is equivalent to the p-generalized χ distribution. The main result of Lemma 1 in Supplementary Material D shows that the p-generalized Gaussian distribution has the majority of its density living on a thin-strip in d-dimensional space. In particular, as we increase the dimension d, for p>2, the width of this strip exponentially decreases, and therefore we expect HMC-LF’s performance to decrease due to thinning of the high-density region.

Figure 3 consists of six convergence plots of increasing dimension d, each showing reduction in the errors as the running time increases, measured in the Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Wasserstein-1 distances, as well as covariance. Since each component of x from the joint p-generalized Gaussian distribution is I.I.D., we computed the maximum of the two distances across each of their individual d marginal distributions to save computational costs. Moreover, the error in covariance is also simplified and computed by taking the l∞ norm along the diagonal of the sample covariance matrix. Figure 3 shows improved performance of CHMC over HMC-LF as d increases. Furthermore, the use of CHMC over CHMC-FullJ is justified by the improved running time on fixed accuracy, with a greater difference in their running time to be expected for general high-dimensional target distributions. These results further highlight sampling performance in high-dimensional distributions with thin high-density regions can be improved by employing energy-preserving integrators. Approximate stationarity and dimensional scaling of acceptance probability

To study approximate stationarity of CHMC in high dimensions, we first compare the acceptance probability of HMC-LF versus CHMC-FullJ. Specifically, the improvement on acceptance probability of CHMC-FullJ’s αEP=min(1,exp(−δ)detJΨEP) over HMC-LF’s αSYM=min(1,exp(−ΔH)) hinges on favorable dimensional scaling of the Jacobian for conservative integrators over the negative exponential of the energy error for symplectic integrators.

Figure 4a and b each include three sets of histograms, comparing exp(−ΔH) of HMC-LF versus detJΨEP of CHMC-FullJ, with increasing dimensions (d=2,560,10,240,40,960) and decreasing step sizes (τ=0.1,0.05,0.025) across the columns. Figure 4c shows split violin plots comparing the acceptance probability of HMC-LF and CHMC-FullJ across the same dimensions and step sizes as above. As Fig. 4a and b illustrates, HMF-LF has larger variances on exp(−ΔH) than CHMC-FullJ’s detJΨEP, across all d and τ. As a result, the violin plots show CHMC-FullJ has higher acceptance probability over HMC-LF, especially for large step sizes τ. This indicates the growth of the Jacobian of the symmetrized Itoh–Abe scheme or symmetrized DMM scheme is slower than the growth of the energy error of the Leapfrog scheme, as d increases. Moreover, since δ≈0 for energy-preserving integrators, αEP will concentrate near 1 for sufficiently small τ, which supports the choice of CHMC with detJΨEP=1 as a reasonable approximation in practice.

Comparison of HMC-LF, CHMC, and CHMC-FullJ. a) Histogram of exp(−ΔH). b) Histogram of detJΨEP. c) Violin plot of α. d) Histogram of samples, ξ.

Finally, we look at the impacts of approximate stationarity in high dimensions by assessing the effects of the transformation ξ=‖x‖p on samples between the two equivalent distributions, the p-generalized Gaussian and p-generalized χ distributions (27). Specifically, we compare the transformed samples ξ obtained from directly sampling the p-generalized Gaussian by HMC-LF, CHMC, and CHMC-FullJ. Despite CHMC’s improvements over HMC-LF across various metrics discussed in previous examples, we do observe CHMC having a slight bias toward larger ξ values as d increases, corresponding to step size τ=0.1 in the first panel of Fig. 4d. One explanation for this bias is due to approximate stationarity of CHMC with the approximate Jacobian detJΨEP=1, as outlined in Theorem 1. Specifically, as highlighted in the first panel of Fig. 4b, the exact Jacobian detJΨEP becomes less concentrated at 1 as d increases, leading to extraneous samples being accepted by CHMC. In contrast, this bias is not present for CHMC-FullJ as it satisfies exact stationarity. On the other hand, since detJΨEP=1+O(τ2) as shown in Supplementary Material L.2, reducing τ leads to detJΨEP being more concentrated at 1, as seen in Fig. 4b. Thus by reducing τ, the samples of CHMC and CHMC-FullJ become more similar, mitigating the observed bias without the need to compute the full Jacobian.

Discussion

We have introduced a variant of HMC, called CHMC, where an R-reversible energy-preserving integrator can be used to increase the acceptance probability and improve sampling efficacy of distributions with thin high-density regions. To avoid computing the full Jacobian expression in the acceptance probability, an approximate Jacobian was introduced leading to the notion of approximate stationarity, where the associated error is determined by the choice of energy-preserving integrator, Jacobian approximation and step size. Our numerical studies showed various improvements of CHMC over HMC on the p-generalized χ and p-generalized Gaussian distributions across various parameters values and in high dimensions.

With the promising results of CHMC presented so far, there are various directions which warrant further investigation.

Iterative solvers and inexact R-reversibility

Due to the implicit nature of R-reversible energy-preserving integrators discussed so far, more efficient nonlinear solvers can be investigated to improve computational costs. For example, quasi-Newton, such as Broyden’s method (26), can be applied to solve the proposed energy-preserving schemes with O(kd2) cost per MCMC iteration while retaining superlinear convergence. Moreover, Anderson acceleration (31, 32) can improve the convergence rate of fixed point iteration while maintaining a lower cost of O(kd) per MCMC iteration. To avoid explicit computation of Jacobian matrices, Newton–Krylov methods (33) with automatic differentiation (34) can also be applied, as it has been utilized for solving implicit midpoint schemes in HMC (35). Furthermore, detailed comparisons on the overall computational cost of CHMC versus HMC-LF should take into account of the larger stability regions of implicit energy-preserving schemes over the explicit Leapfrog scheme.

In practice, R-reversibility is only satisfied approximately due to round-off errors with explicit schemes, and inexact solutions obtained from iterative solvers. For example, such inexactness in R-reversibility is already present with the implicit solvers employed in HMC (35) and Riemannian Manifold HMC (14, 36). As proposed by Ref. (37), one approach is to restore R-reversibility for the inexact solution, with added computational cost after each proposal. On the other hand, Corollary 2 shows the effects of inexact R-reversibility can be controlled, as the stationarity error with inexact solutions can be bounded by the errors of the determinant approximation and the proposal map approximation, both of which can be controlled by either the step size, or the chosen tolerance for the iterative solver. Also, this error bound is consistent with observations from a detailed numerical study (38) on how error tolerances of the implicit schemes can affect the error in R-reversibility and sample quality for Riemannian Manifold HMC. In particular, it was observed on a wide range of Bayesian estimation problems that effective samples can still be obtained over a wide range of large error tolerances used in the iterative solvers. Thus, Corollary 2 justifies the effects of inexact R-Reversibility can be mitigated by controlling the errors in the determinant approximation and proposal map approximation.

Improvements for large scale problems

It is of practical interest to assess CHMC’s effectiveness on large-scale applications, such as statistical physics, Bayesian statistics and generative models. For example, other energy-preserving integrators can be explored to improve robustness and efficiency, in conjunction with using adaptive step size and variable integration length from No-U-Turn sampler. To mitigate potential bias introduced by the approximation of detJΨEP=1, Hutchinson trace estimation (39, 40) and matrix-vector products can aid in computing the trace of matrix powers more efficiently, which appears in the higher order terms of the Taylor approximation of detJΨEP in Lemma 6 of Supplementary Material I. Finally, the gradient-free aspect of the symmetrized Itoh–Abe or DMM scheme provides a promising alternative for applications with target distributions lacking gradient information.

Extensions to other HMC variants

On the prospect of extending CHMC to other HMC variants, we first note that CHMC can readily be extended to Riemannian Manifold HMC (14) by using the proposed energy-preserving integrators. Specifically, the nonseparable Hamiltonian H(q,p)=12pTM−1(q)p+12logdetM(q)+U(q) from Ref. (14) can be preserved using discrete gradient schemes and the identities, ∇qM−1(q)=−M−1(q)∇qM(q)M−1(q) and ∇qlogdetM(q)=Tr(M−1 (q)∇qM(q)). On the other hand, incorporating CHMC into Split HMC (15) hinges on designing energy-preserving schemes that are compatible with additive splitting methods. Furthermore, combining stochastic gradient HMC (15) with CHMC would enable efficient sampling with large sample sizes and streaming data. Such extension would require detailed analysis of the perturbed dynamics of the stochastic differential equation induced by using minibatch gradient and the proposed energy-preserving integrators, as well as devising appropriate momentum damping corrections.

Outlook on analysis of CHMC convergence and bias

Finally, we suggest how approximate stationarity of CHMC could be utilized within the growing literature on the convergence theory and bias estimation on HMC (41–45). Specifically, let π0 denote an initial distribution and Pτ,mnπ0 denote the nth composition of the transition operator of CHMC applied to π0, using an m-order Taylor approximation of the exact Jacobian. In particular, Pτ,0 denotes the transition operator of CHMC with detJΨEP=1 (ie partially unadjusted HMC) and Pτ,∞ denotes the transition operator of CHMC with the exact Jacobian, ie CHMC-FullJ. As CHMC-FullJ satisfies stationarity (implied by Theorem 1), we speculate that it is likely to converge to some stationary distribution πτ,∞ under appropriate hypothesis on π, such as strongly log-concavity typically assumed in the convergence analysis of HMC (42, 43, 46). While several gaps remain to be filled, the above analysis helps situate the current theory of CHMC within the broader literature of HMC theory and paves the way for future investigation.
