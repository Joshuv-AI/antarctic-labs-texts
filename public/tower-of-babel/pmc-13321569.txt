Retroperitoneal Castleman disease masquerading as a pancreatic neoplasm: a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Sanket Solanki, Sri Aurobindo Prasad Das, Seema Rao, Samiran Nundy, Naimish N. Mehta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCastleman disease (CD) is a rare lymphoproliferative disorder of unclear etiology, often presenting as an asymptomatic, incidentally detected mass. The predominant pathological variants include hyaline vascular, plasma cell, and mixed types. Clinically, CD is classified into unicentric (UCD) and multicentric (MCD) forms.Case presentationHere we report the case of a 34-year-old Indian female with vague left-sided abdominal pain and bilateral lower limb pain, in whom contrast-enhanced CT revealed an incidental well-defined 5.7 × 3.8 × 3.8 cm mass in the gastrohepatic space, initially suggestive of a pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor or gastrointestinal stromal tumor. However, endoscopic ultrasound-guided biopsy indicated a lymphoproliferative neoplasm. Given the discordant imaging and biopsy findings, surgical resection of the mass was undertaken, and the final histopathology confirmed unicentric Castleman disease. No adjuvant therapy was required, and the patient remains disease-free at 2 years of follow-up.ConclusionThis case underscores the favorable prognosis of UCD—complete surgical resection is associated with near 100% long-term survival—and the importance of differentiating such lesions from other retroperitoneal tumors, which are usually malignant. A thorough preoperative evaluation, including an excisional lymph node biopsy or intraoperative frozen section, is recommended to establish the diagnosis and guide management.

Background

Castleman disease (CD), also known as angiofollicular lymph node hyperplasia, encompasses a heterogeneous group of rare lymphoproliferative disorders with characteristic lymph node histopathology. First described by Castleman in the 1950s [1], CD is not a true malignancy but rather a benign (non-clonal) proliferation of lymphoid tissue driven by dysregulated immune signaling. Clinically, CD has two major forms: unicentric CD (UCD), in which disease is localized to a single lymph node or region, and multicentric CD (MCD), characterized by generalized lymphadenopathy, systemic inflammation, and potential multi-organ dysfunction. Histopathologically, CD is subclassified into three main variants—hyaline vascular (HV), plasma cell (PC), and mixed—based on lymph node architecture. UCD cases are most often of the hyaline-vascular subtype and generally occur in younger immunocompetent individuals, whereas MCD more commonly exhibits the plasma cell variant and tends to affect older or immunocompromised patients.

Due to its nonspecific radiological appearance and rarity, CD is frequently misdiagnosed as a neoplasm. This is especially true for retroperitoneal or abdominal lesions, which may simulate pancreatic, gastrointestinal stromal, or lymphoid malignancies. Accurate diagnosis is crucial, as complete surgical resection is curative for UCD, while MCD requires systemic therapy. We report a case of retroperitoneal UCD mimicking a pancreatic tumor, which was diagnosed only after surgical excision.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old Indian woman with no known comorbidities presented with complaints of vague left upper abdominal pain and intermittent bilateral lower limb discomfort for the past one month. She had no history of fever, weight loss, or gastrointestinal symptoms. Clinical examination revealed mild pallor. No masses were palpable, and there was no peripheral lymphadenopathy. Initial laboratory investigations showed a hemoglobin of 8.8 g/dL (microcytic anemia), white blood cell count of 7600/µL and platelet count of 320,000/µL. Inflammatory markers were modestly elevated (erythrocyte sedimentation rate 40 mm/hr, C-reactive protein 18 mg/L). Liver and kidney function tests were within normal ranges. Tumor markers, including CEA, CA 125, CA 19-9, and CA 15-3, were within normal limits. Serologies for HIV, hepatitis B and C, and autoimmune markers were negative. Abdominal ultrasound revealed a 6.6 × 4.4 × 3.4 cm well-defined, hypoechoic, homogenous mass in the upper abdomen, located in the lesser sac region. Subsequently, a contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) was done which confirmed a lobulated, well-defined 5.7 × 3.8 × 3.8 cm hypervascular mass in the gastrohepatic space, superior to the pancreas and posterior to the left liver lobe (see Fig. 1) with strong arterial enhancement and partial portal phase washout. A feeding arterial branch from the celiac axis and a draining vein into the portal circulation were observed. The mass closely abutted the pancreatic tail and splenic artery, obscuring the fat plane, with no signs of direct invasion. No other enlarged lymph nodes were noted elsewhere. To further evaluate the mass, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) was performed, and a fine-needle biopsy (FNB) revealed reactive lymphoid tissue with follicular structures, but no evidence of carcinoma, sarcoma, or lymphoma. There were polyclonal lymphocytes without atypia. Although no definitive diagnosis was made from the biopsy, the findings suggested a benign lymphoproliferative process. An fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) scan showed mildly increased FDG uptake in the mass (SUVmax ~ 5.8), consistent with a metabolically active tumor but not as high as typically seen in aggressive malignancies. No other hypermetabolic lymph nodes or lesions were identified in the body. Given the diagnostic uncertainty, a multidisciplinary tumor board was constituted and the consensus was to proceed with surgical exploration and resection of the lesion. She underwent exploratory laparotomy and intraoperatively, a 7 × 5 × 3 cm encapsulated mass was found in the lesser sac. The mass was separate from the pancreas but densely adherent to the pancreatic capsule and splenic artery. Several enlarged feeding vessels were seen on the surface of the tumor, confirming its hypervascular nature. The mass was dissected free after the feeding vessels were ligated. The tumor was completely excised en bloc without necessitating any pancreatic resection (see Fig. 2). Grossly, the specimen was a lobulated, firm lymph node mass with a tan-white cut surface and a fibrous capsule. The estimated blood loss was 50 mL and no major intraoperative complications were encountered. Histopathology revealed small lymphoid follicles with atretic germinal centers and hyalinized penetrating vessels, surrounded by concentric lymphocyte rings—the classic “onion-skin” appearance of the hyaline-vascular variant of Castleman disease (see Fig. 3). [2] Immunohistochemistry confirmed a reactive polyclonal pattern (CD20+B cells, CD3+T cells), with expanded follicular dendritic cells (CD21+) and scattered S100 + Langerhans cells. No pancreatic tissue or malignancy was identified. A diagnosis of unicentric Castleman disease, hyaline-vascular type, was made. Postoperatively, the patient recovered uneventfully and was discharged on fourth postoperative day. At a follow-up of 2 years, the patient is doing well and is under periodic surveillance as per guidelines for UCD. Discussion

Castleman disease is a rare, benign, slow-growing lymphoid disorder. It is characterized by non-clonal (reactive) lymphoproliferation. Chronic inflammation is believed to play a role in its pathogenesis; in MCD especially, excessive IL-6 drives lymphoid hyperplasia, and HHV-8 infection is frequently implicated in HIV-positive patients. [3] It comprises two clinical types: unicentric (UCD, localized) and multicentric (MCD, systemic) [4]. Keller et al. subclassified CD histologically into hyaline vascular, plasma cell, and mixed variants [5]. UCD constitutes about 90% of cases, typically presenting in the 3rd–4th decade (mean age ~ 35 years), often as hyaline-vascular subtype. UCD is usually asymptomatic; symptoms, when present, arise from compression of adjacent structures by the mass, such as the abdominal discomfort and referred lower limb pain in our patient. In contrast, MCD is less common but more severe, presenting with generalized symptoms: fever, night sweats, weight loss, anemia, hypoalbuminemia, widespread lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, anasarca, and effusions. Laboratory abnormalities often include elevated CRP and polyclonal hypergammaglobulinemia. Paraneoplastic pemphigus (PNP), reported in about 20% of CD cases (usually multicentric/plasma cell variant), indicates a poorer prognosis [6]. CD can also predispose to malignancies like Kaposi’s sarcoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and POEMS syndrome, necessitating long-term surveillance given the small risk of late malignant transformation or associations.

Imaging studies are useful but not definitive for diagnosis. On ultrasound, UCD typically appears as a well-defined, homogeneously hypoechoic mass, sometimes with a central echogenic focus if fibrosis or calcification is present. CT features include a solitary, well-circumscribed lymph node mass with intense post-contrast enhancement (reflecting vascularity), occasionally with fine, punctate calcifications. In our case, CT revealed a hypervascular mass with a feeding artery—classic for CD [7]. MRI findings are nonspecific: lesions are usually isointense or hypointense on T1-weighted images, hyperintense on T2, and enhance avidly post-gadolinium. These radiologic patterns, though suggestive, overlap with other hypervascular tumors (for example, neuroendocrine tumors, paragangliomas, hemangiomas), and are not diagnostic [8]. Laboratory tests such as HIV, HHV-8 serologies, and IL-6 levels may support clinical suspicion (especially in MCD), but are insufficient for diagnosis and tissue sampling is often necessary. FNAC often fails to preserve architecture, showing only reactive lymphocytes. Core-needle biopsy may suggest the diagnosis if sufficient material is obtained, but excisional biopsy remains the gold standard. In our patient, the core biopsy showed a benign lymphoproliferative process but was inconclusive, prompting surgical excision.

Management of unicentric and multicentric Castleman disease (CD) differs substantially. UCD is typically curable with surgical excision of the affected lymph node or region. Figure 4 shows a simplified approach to a patient with UCD. Numerous case series report long-term disease control in 95–100% of cases following complete resection. A systematic review by Talat et al. of 404 cases found a 93% success rate with very low recurrence. In our patient, complete (R0) resection obviated the need for additional therapy, aligning with current consensus that surgery remains the gold standard for UCD. For large hypervascular lesions, preoperative embolization of feeding arteries has been used to minimize intraoperative blood loss. [10] In situations where a Castleman lesion is suspected but a malignant tumor cannot be definitively excluded, an initial limited resection or biopsy with frozen section can confirm the diagnosis, followed by completion of the excision if CD is confirmed. This strategy avoids unnecessary radical surgery (such as a Whipple procedure in the case of a peripancreatic mass) for what is a benign condition. [11] In the present case, the use of intraoperative frozen section (had the diagnosis been uncertain) could have prevented a pancreatic resection. For unresectable UCD, radiotherapy achieves tumor control in ~ 80% of cases. Moderate-dose radiation (30–45 Gy) can shrink nodal masses or halt progression, sometimes yielding durable remission. In select cases, partial debulking followed by radiotherapy has been effective in controlling unresectable UCD.

In contrast to UCD, management of MCD generally requires systemic therapy and carries a worse prognosis, with a median survival of 2–3 years if untreated. In 2017, an international expert group published the first consensus diagnostic criteria for idiopathic MCD (iMCD) [12], requiring: (1) multicentric lymphadenopathy with characteristic histopathology, (2) at least two clinical or lab abnormalities (for example, elevated IL-6/CRP, cytopenias, hypoalbuminemia, renal dysfunction), and (3) exclusion of alternative causes (for example, infections, autoimmune diseases, malignancy). This framework has enhanced recognition and treatment of iMCD (see Fig. 5). Furthermore, novel treatments targeting IL-6 have dramatically improved outcomes in iMCD. [13] Siltuximab, a monoclonal antibody against IL-6, became the first FDA-approved therapy for iMCD (HHV-8 negative) in 2014. It was shown in a phase II trial to induce durable remission in about one-third of patients, with additional patients achieving partial responses. [14] Tocilizumab, an IL-6 receptor blocker, has also shown efficacy in iMCD and is used especially where siltuximab is not available or not approved. [15] In fact, tocilizumab was used in Japan for iMCD for years before siltuximab’s introduction. Both of these IL-6 directed therapies can dramatically improve iMCD outcomes by quelling the cytokine storm that drives the disease. For patients with MCD associated with HHV-8 (often HIV-positive), rituximab (anti-CD20) is a mainstay of therapy. [16] Rituximab depletes B cells (which harbor HHV-8), thereby reducing viral IL-6 production and improving symptoms. Studies have shown high efficacy of rituximab in HHV-8–MCD, with one series reporting a 5-year survival > 90% when treated appropriately. [17] Rituximab can be given alone or in combination with antivirals and chemotherapy if needed. In HIV-positive MCD patients, treating the HIV and using rituximab dramatically alters the course of the disease, and patients often do much better than in the pre-rituximab era. Notably, even in idiopathic (HHV-8–negative) MCD, rituximab has activity: it has been used off-label in non-severe iMCD cases, especially when IL-6 blockers are unavailable or contraindicated. The consensus guidelines list rituximab as an alternative first-line for iMCD in patients without high systemic inflammation. However, recent real-world data suggest that IL-6–directed therapy yields better and more durable responses in iMCD than rituximab-based regimens.Fig. 5Proposed treatment algorithm for a patient with MCD

In a study from the ACCELERATE registry, siltuximab or tocilizumab therapy was associated with significantly higher response rates and longer remission as compared to rituximab (with or without steroids). [18] Thus, except in HHV-8–MCD or special situations, IL-6 blockade is preferred in iMCD. In patients who fail IL-6 blockers, traditional chemotherapeutic regimens (such as CHOP or CVAD combinations) have been used, especially in severe iMCD flares or organ failure situations. Chemotherapy can induce remission or stabilize disease in a subset of patients (response rates ~ 50% have been reported), but toxicity is a concern and it does not address the underlying driver in idiopathic cases. Other agents reported in iMCD include immunomodulators like thalidomide and cyclosporine, mTOR inhibitors (sirolimus), and even anti-IL-1 therapy (Anakinra) in refractory cases. For the especially aggressive subset known as TAFRO syndrome (iMCD-TAFRO, characterized by thrombocytopenia, anasarca, fever, reticulin fibrosis of marrow, organomegaly), a more intensive approach is often required because TAFRO can be rapidly fatal. [19] These patients may need combined therapy (siltuximab plus high-dose corticosteroids, and if no improvement, escalation to chemotherapy or immunosuppressants like cyclosporine). Splenectomy has been performed in some MCD cases with splenomegaly as part of cytoreduction, but its benefit is not clearly established. Multicentric Castleman disease, being a systemic condition, is not usually managed with radiation, except possibly for palliative control of focal problematic sites. In rare instances of asymptomatic MCD, a watchful waiting strategy may be adopted, with close monitoring, since some cases can remain stable without immediate intervention. Our patient’s course highlights several key management principles. First, obtaining tissue diagnosis before definitive surgery is invaluable. In this case, the EUS-guided biopsy, though not diagnostic of Castleman disease, helped rule out malignancy and steered us away from treating the mass as a presumptive pancreatic tumor. If the biopsy had suggested lymphoma, treatment would have shifted to chemotherapy; conversely, if it had confirmed Castleman disease, medical therapy might have been considered. Given the uncertainty, limited surgical resection served both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Second, multidisciplinary care is essential, especially in rare diseases like CD where individual clinician experience is limited.

Conclusion

In summary, unicentric Castleman disease can mimic more common abdominal tumors. This case and review underscore the need for tissue diagnosis to differentiate CD from malignancy and demonstrate the curative potential of surgical resection for UCD. With early recognition and correct management, UCD patients enjoy near-100% long-term survival. CD should be considered in the differential for isolated retroperitoneal masses, as accurate identification can prevent overtreatment or enable timely systemic therapy in MCD. Continued research and updated guidelines are refining the diagnosis and management of Castleman disease, and coordinated interdisciplinary care was key in resolving this diagnostic challenge.
