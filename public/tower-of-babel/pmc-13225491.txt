Associations of TyG-related indices with NAFLD-related outcomes: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Jinyang Li, Wantao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and related composite indices have been increasingly studied as accessible metabolic markers for fatty liver disease. However, evidence remains difficult to interpret because different TyG-related indices have been evaluated across heterogeneous populations and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)-related outcomes. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to clarify the pooled associations that could be formally synthesized and to summarize the broader descriptive evidence.Methods:We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, VIP Database, and China Biology Medicine Database from inception to December 2025. Observational studies evaluating the TyG index or related composite indices, including TyG-body mass index, TyG-waist circumference, and TyG-waist-to-height ratio, in relation to NAFLD-related outcomes were eligible. Random-effects meta-analyses were performed in R. Only studies with sufficient comparability in exposure operationalization, outcome definition, and effect-size reporting were quantitatively pooled; the remaining studies were retained in the descriptive synthesis.Results:Nineteen studies involving 212,618 participants were included in the systematic review, including 16 cross-sectional studies and 3 cohort studies. Of these, 8 studies contributed to at least 1 quantitative synthesis. In continuous-scale analyses, higher TyG levels were associated with increased odds of NAFLD/steatosis (5 studies; pooled odds ratio [OR], 2.72; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.08–3.55; I2 = 85.7%). In highest-versus-lowest analyses, the association remained significant (4 studies; pooled OR, 4.36; 95% CI, 3.39–5.61; I2 = 92.8%). TyG-body mass index (2 studies; pooled OR, 1.04; 95% CI, 1.03–1.05; I2 = 0.0%) and TyG-waist circumference (2 studies; pooled OR, 1.01; 95% CI, 1.01–1.01; I2 = 15.1%) were also positively associated with NAFLD/steatosis. TyG-waist-to-height ratio showed a positive association in supportive synthesis, but evidence for liver fibrosis and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis remained limited and was mainly descriptive.Conclusion:TyG-related indices were consistently associated with NAFLD/steatosis across the available literature. The basic TyG index had the broadest evidence base, whereas composite indices suggested possible additional value in selected settings. These markers may be useful for metabolic fatty liver risk stratification, but further studies with standardized outcome definitions and clearer exposure parameterization are needed.

1. Introduction

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) has become one of the most common chronic liver diseases worldwide and is closely linked to obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and other manifestations of metabolic dysfunction.[1] Its clinical importance extends beyond simple steatosis because a subset of patients may progress to steatohepatitis, fibrosis, cirrhosis, and liver-related complications.[1] Early identification of individuals at increased risk is therefore of practical value, particularly in settings where imaging- or biopsy-based assessment is not readily available.

Because insulin resistance plays a central role in the development of NAFLD, simple metabolic surrogate markers have attracted increasing interest. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has been widely explored as an accessible marker of insulin resistance-related metabolic risk, and several recent studies have suggested that TyG-related composite indices may further improve the identification of fatty liver disease by incorporating anthropometric information.[2–4] In population-based or screening-oriented settings, composite indices such as TyG-body mass index (BMI), TyG-waist circumference index (TyG-WC), and TyG-waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) have shown stronger discriminatory performance than the basic TyG index in some cohorts.[2–4]

However, the current evidence remains difficult to interpret as a whole. Existing studies have evaluated different TyG-related indices across heterogeneous populations, including general adults, nonobese individuals, adolescents, chronic kidney disease populations, and biopsy-characterized clinical cohorts.[5–7] The reported outcomes have also varied considerably, ranging from NAFLD/steatosis to liver fibrosis, nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), and at-risk NASH.[5,8–10] In addition, some studies have suggested that the performance of TyG-related indices may differ according to disease stage or clinical context, particularly when moving from simple steatosis toward fibrosis- or inflammation-related phenotypes.[8,11,12] These differences make it difficult to judge the relative evidential strength of the basic TyG index and its composite derivatives from individual studies alone.

Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies evaluating TyG-related indices in relation to NAFLD-related outcomes. Our aims were to clarify the pooled associations that could be formally synthesized, to summarize the broader descriptive evidence that could not be quantitatively pooled, and to compare the overall evidence profiles of TyG and its related composite indices across NAFLD/steatosis, liver fibrosis, and NASH-related domains.

2. Methods

2.1. Protocol and reporting standard

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 statement. The review protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD420251153116). Because this study was a systematic review and meta-analysis based exclusively on previously published aggregate data, it did not involve any new recruitment of human participants, collection of individual-level patient data, or animal experiments. Therefore, approval by an ethics committee or institutional review board was not required, and informed consent was not applicable.

2.2. Literature search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted in 3 English-language databases (PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library) and 4 Chinese-language databases (China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, VIP Database, and China Biology Medicine Database). The search covered the period from database inception to December 31, 2025.

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms and free-text keywords related to the TyG index and its derivative indices, including TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR, together with terms related to NAFLD, metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), steatosis, liver fibrosis, and NASH. Reference lists of relevant articles were also manually screened to identify additional eligible studies. The full database-specific search strategies are provided in the Supplementary Search Appendix.

2.3. Eligibility criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they met the following criteria: observational study design, including cross-sectional or cohort studies; evaluation of the TyG index or related composite indices, including TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, or TyG-WHtR, as the exposure of interest; reporting of NAFLD-related outcomes, including NAFLD/steatosis, liver fibrosis, or NASH; and provision of sufficient effect-size data for qualitative synthesis and, where applicable, quantitative pooling.

Studies were excluded if they were reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, conference abstracts without extractable data, animal studies, or studies conducted in populations not relevant to the review question. Studies were also excluded if the exposure of interest was not a TyG-related index, if the outcome definition was incompatible with the review scope, or if no usable association estimates or sufficient raw data were available.

2.4. Study selection

After removal of duplicates, all records were screened independently by 2 reviewers in 2 stages: title/abstract screening followed by full-text assessment. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. Studies meeting the predefined eligibility criteria were included in the systematic review. 2.5. Data extraction and evidence structuring

Data were extracted independently by 2 reviewers using a standardized form. Extracted items included study characteristics, country, study design, population characteristics, sample size, exposure definition, outcome definition, diagnostic method, covariate adjustment, and effect-size estimates with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Because the included literature was methodologically heterogeneous, evidence synthesis was structured at 2 levels. First, all eligible studies were retained in the systematic review and contributed to the descriptive synthesis, evidence map, and synthesis framework. Second, only studies with sufficient comparability in exposure operationalization, outcome definition, and effect-size reporting were entered into quantitative pooling.

For synthesis purposes, reported outcomes were grouped into 3 prespecified domains: NAFLD/steatosis, liver fibrosis, and NASH. Original outcome definitions as reported in the source articles were preserved during data extraction and were used to determine whether a study was sufficiently comparable for formal meta-analysis.

When a study reported multiple estimates for the same exposure-outcome combination, only one estimate was selected for the primary pooled analysis within each analysis stratum. Priority was given to the most fully adjusted model that was methodologically appropriate and did not introduce obvious overadjustment related to intrinsic components of the composite index. Studies not meeting these criteria remained in the descriptive synthesis.

To further assess robustness, we applied 2 pooling rules. A lenient eligibility rule was used for the primary quantitative synthesis to retain clinically interpretable and methodologically acceptable studies. A stricter eligibility rule was then applied as a supplementary robustness analysis. Because most exposure-outcome pathways became too sparse under the strict rule, only the continuous-scale TyG-NAFLD/steatosis analysis remained eligible for additional strict-rule pooling.

2.6. Quality assessment

Methodological quality was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies and the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) checklist for cross-sectional studies. Quality assessments were performed independently by 2 reviewers, with disagreements resolved by discussion. Study-level quality profiles are presented in the Supplementary Figures.

2.7. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing), primarily using the metafor, readxl, and dplyr packages. Random-effects models were used for all quantitative syntheses because clinical and methodological heterogeneity was anticipated across studies.

Odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% CIs were used as the common effect measure for pooled analyses. Continuous-scale and highest-versus-lowest analyses were synthesized separately and were not combined within the same meta-analysis. Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic. Leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed for pooled outcomes to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the overall estimates.

Formal assessment of publication bias was not performed because the number of studies in each pooled analysis was too small to support meaningful funnel plot interpretation or statistical testing. Studies that could not be pooled quantitatively were retained in the descriptive synthesis and incorporated into the evidence map and synthesis framework.

Additional methodological details and supplementary results are provided in the Supplementary Methods and results file.

3. Results

3.1. Study selection and study characteristics

A total of 19 studies involving 212,618 participants were included in the systematic review (Fig. 1). Of these, 16 were cross-sectional studies and 3 were cohort studies. The included studies were conducted across Asia, North America, Europe, and South America, with China and the United States contributing the largest number of studies.

The study populations were heterogeneous and included both general-population and clinically enriched cohorts, such as nonobese adults, older adults, individuals with metabolic syndrome or obesity, adolescents, and biopsy-based NAFLD populations. The evaluated exposures comprised the TyG index and related composite indices, including the TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR.

NAFLD/steatosis was the most frequently reported outcome, whereas liver fibrosis and NASH were reported less often and were mainly synthesized descriptively. Outcome ascertainment varied across studies and included ultrasonography, vibration-controlled transient elastography/FibroScan (Echosens)-based measures, and liver biopsy, with a few studies using surrogate scores for steatosis- or fibrosis-related phenotypes. 3.2. Quality assessment

Methodological quality was assessed using the NOS for cohort studies and the AHRQ checklist for cross-sectional studies. Study-level quality profiles are presented in Figure S5 and Figure S6.

Overall, the cohort studies were of moderate-to-good methodological quality on the NOS, with generally acceptable performance in participant selection and outcome assessment. However, as expected for observational studies, comparability across studies was influenced by differences in adjustment strategies and covariate selection.

Most cross-sectional studies showed acceptable methodological quality on the AHRQ checklist, although several items were variably reported across studies. In particular, limitations were more commonly observed in the reporting of handling of missing data, assessment and control of potential confounding, and response-related methodological details. These methodological patterns were taken into account when interpreting the pooled associations, especially in analyses with substantial between-study heterogeneity.

3.4. Association between the TyG index and NAFLD/steatosis

3.4.1. Continuous-scale analyses

In continuous-scale analyses, higher TyG levels were significantly associated with an increased likelihood of NAFLD/steatosis (Fig. 3).[13–17] Based on 5 studies involving 123,717 participants, the pooled OR was 2.72 (95% CI 2.08–3.55), although between-study heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 85.7%). The direction of effect was generally consistent across studies despite variation in effect magnitude, suggesting that the heterogeneity mainly reflected differences in study populations, exposure scaling, diagnostic approaches, and adjustment strategies rather than reversal of the overall association.

Association between the TyG index and NAFLD/steatosis in continuous-scale analyses. Forest plot of the pooled association between TyG and NAFLD/steatosis in continuous-scale analyses under the lenient eligibility rule. CI = confidence interval, NAFLD = nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, OR = odds ratio, TyG = triglyceride-glucose.

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that the direction of the pooled association remained stable after sequential exclusion of individual studies (Fig. S3). Under the strict eligibility rule, the continuous-scale TyG analysis remained poolable and showed a directionally similar association based on 4 studies involving 118,933 participants (pooled OR 2.69, 95% CI 1.99–3.63; I2 = 89.9%) (Fig. S7 and Fig. S8).[13,14,16,17] Taken together, these findings support the robustness of the positive association between TyG and NAFLD/steatosis, while also indicating that the magnitude of association should be interpreted with caution in light of the marked between-study variability.

3.4.2. Highest-versus-lowest analyses

A similar pattern was observed in highest-versus-lowest analyses (Fig. 4).[13,17–19] Based on 4 studies involving 110,000 participants, individuals in the highest TyG category had a significantly higher likelihood of NAFLD/steatosis than those in the lowest category (pooled OR 4.36, 95% CI 3.39–5.61), although heterogeneity remained considerable (I2 = 92.8%).

Sensitivity analysis further supported the stability of this finding (Fig. S4). Sequential omission of individual studies did not materially alter the direction of the pooled effect, suggesting that the observed association was not driven by any single study alone. Overall, the categorical and continuous analyses were concordant in showing that higher TyG was associated with a greater burden of NAFLD/steatosis.

3.5. Association between the TyG BMI and NAFLD/steatosis

In continuous-scale analyses, TyG-BMI was also positively associated with NAFLD/steatosis (Fig. 5).[14,16] Based on 2 studies involving 13,763 participants, the pooled OR was 1.04 (95% CI 1.03–1.05), with no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%). The numerical effect size was not directly comparable with those of other indices because of differences in exposure parameterization; however, the association remained directionally positive and statistically stable.

Because TyG-BMI incorporates BMI as an intrinsic component, interpretation of this finding should take into account differences in study populations, adiposity profiles, and multivariable adjustment strategies. Nonetheless, the absence of observed heterogeneity and the consistency of direction across the included studies support a stable positive association between TyG-BMI and NAFLD/steatosis.

3.6. Association between the TyG-WC and NAFLD/steatosis

TyG-WC was likewise associated with NAFLD/steatosis in continuous-scale analyses (Fig. 6).[14,20] Based on 2 studies involving 1960 participants, the pooled OR was 1.01 (95% CI 1.01–1.01), with low heterogeneity (I2 = 15.1%). Although the magnitude of the pooled estimate should not be directly compared with that of other indices because of differences in exposure scaling, the direction of association was consistent.

Despite the relatively coherent pooled signal, interpretation of the TyG-WC findings still requires caution. The number of eligible studies was limited, and study-level differences in participant characteristics, NAFLD ascertainment, and covariate adjustment may still have influenced the pooled estimate. Thus, TyG-WC appears to be positively associated with NAFLD/steatosis, but the supporting evidence base remains modest.

3.7. Supplementary and descriptive findings

Additional evidence beyond the main pooled analyses is summarized in the evidence map (Fig. S1) and the synthesis framework (Fig. 2). These visual summaries show that TyG was the most extensively studied exposure, whereas evidence for TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR was comparatively more limited and unevenly distributed across outcome domains.

For TyG-WHtR, only a small number of studies were sufficiently comparable for quantitative synthesis. In the highest-versus-lowest analysis, based on 2 studies involving 4830 participants, TyG-WHtR showed a positive association with NAFLD/steatosis (pooled OR 9.62, 95% CI 4.09–22.66), but the limited number of eligible studies and substantial between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 89.5%) precluded firm inference (Fig. S2).[18,19] Accordingly, this result was treated as supportive rather than definitive evidence.

Evidence for liver fibrosis and NASH was relatively sparse overall and did not support broad quantitative synthesis under the prespecified framework. These outcomes were therefore incorporated primarily into the descriptive synthesis and evidence map. This approach allowed retention of clinically relevant but methodologically heterogeneous studies without overextending the scope of formal pooling.

As an additional robustness analysis, the continuous-scale association between TyG and NAFLD/steatosis was reexamined under the strict eligibility rule (Fig. S7). Although the number of eligible studies decreased, the direction of association remained consistent with the main analysis. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis under the strict rule likewise showed no reversal of the overall signal (Fig. S8), supporting the stability of the primary finding while underscoring the reduced evidence base under stricter pooling criteria.

4. Discussion

In this revised systematic review and meta-analysis, higher TyG-related indices were consistently associated with NAFLD/steatosis, although the amount and stability of evidence differed across indices. Among the pooled analyses, the basic TyG index provided the broadest quantitative evidence base, whereas TyG-BMI and TyG-WC were also positively associated with NAFLD/steatosis but were supported by fewer directly comparable studies. Accordingly, our findings support a positive overall association between TyG-related metabolic markers and NAFLD/steatosis, while suggesting that the evidential maturity of individual indices is not uniform.

A key clinical implication of the present analysis is that TyG-related indices should be viewed as accessible tools for risk stratification rather than as substitutes for imaging, elastography, or histology. The basic TyG index appears to be the most broadly applicable marker because it was evaluated most often and remained directionally stable across analytic approaches. Composite indices that incorporate anthropometric information may provide incremental value in selected settings, particularly where adiposity is closely linked to hepatic metabolic risk. A similar pattern was reported in a metabolically high-risk population, in which TyG-related composite indices, particularly TyG-WHtR, showed stronger discriminatory relevance for steatosis and fibrosis than TyG alone.[18] However, the current evidence does not support declaring any single index universally optimal, especially for fibrosis- or NASH-related outcomes, where the evidence base remains more limited.

The non-pooled studies retained in the descriptive synthesis help place the pooled findings into context. Several studies suggest that TyG-related indices may extend beyond simple steatosis detection toward stratification of disease severity. This interpretation is also supported by Guo et al,[15] who reported that TyG was associated not only with NAFLD but also with steatosis severity and the presence of fibrosis, while its discrimination appeared stronger for steatosis than for fibrosis. In biopsy-based NAFLD cohorts, TyG-BMI was associated with histologic NASH activity, at-risk NASH, and fibrosis progression.[5] In metabolically high-risk biopsy cohorts, the basic TyG index was also associated with definite NASH and significant fibrosis.[9] In addition, composite TyG-related indices, particularly TyG-WHtR, showed possible value for identifying at-risk NASH in a population-based setting.[10] These data support extension of TyG-related markers toward more advanced disease phenotypes, but the current evidence remains too sparse and heterogeneous for broad formal pooling.

Other descriptive studies support the practical screening relevance of composite TyG-related markers in broader populations. In a nationally representative United States cohort, TyG-WC was strongly associated with MAFLD and with greater steatosis and fibrosis severity.[2] Another NHANES-based study reported that TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, and TyG-BMI had stronger discriminatory performance than TyG alone for NAFLD/MAFLD detection and fibrosis-related stratification.[3] A large Chinese screening cohort also showed positive dose-response relationships between TyG-related indices and MAFLD, with composite indices outperforming TyG alone.[4] These studies do not carry the same interpretive weight as pooled evidence, but they support the view that anthropometric augmentation may improve the practical screening value of TyG-based markers.

The descriptive literature also suggests that the performance of TyG-related indices varies across populations and disease settings. In nonobese adults, Li et al[14] further suggested that adding WC to TyG may enhance screening performance, supporting the view that TyG-related composite indices can capture metabolically unfavorable phenotypes even outside overt obesity. In children and adolescents, modified TyG indices performed better than the basic TyG index for identifying NAFLD.[6] In patients with chronic kidney disease, TyG-related metabolic information also remained clinically informative for ultrasound-defined NAFLD.[7] By contrast, in bariatric-surgery candidates, the basic TyG index showed limited value for identifying significant steatosis or NASH, although it may still help exclude advanced fibrosis.[11] Similarly, biopsy-based comparative work suggested that TyG may detect the presence of steatosis but is less reliable for grading disease severity and may be influenced by fibrosis and necroinflammation.[12] Taken together, these studies indicate that TyG-related indices are not interchangeable across all clinical scenarios.

An important strength of the present revision is the clearer separation between overall evidence and pooled evidence. Although 19 studies were included in the systematic review, only 8 contributed to at least 1 quantitative synthesis. This was not due to arbitrary exclusion, but to prespecified requirements for comparability in exposure operationalization, outcome definition, and effect-size reporting. The remaining studies were retained as descriptive evidence and were incorporated into the synthesis framework and evidence map rather than being discarded. This approach addresses the analytical clarity concerns raised during peer review and provides a more transparent account of why the quantitative evidence base was necessarily narrower than the full review dataset.

Heterogeneity was substantial in several pooled analyses and should be interpreted carefully. In our view, this heterogeneity mainly reflects structural and clinical differences across studies rather than simple statistical instability. The included studies varied markedly in population composition, including general adults, nonobese individuals, adolescents, elderly populations, obesity-enriched cohorts, and biopsy-based clinical samples. Outcome assessment also differed across ultrasonography, FibroScan-based measures, liver biopsy, and surrogate scoring systems. These approaches do not capture exactly the same disease construct. In addition, exposure parameterization varied across studies, with some analyses using continuous-scale models and others highest-versus-lowest comparisons. Taken together, these differences provide a coherent explanation for why substantial between-study heterogeneity persisted despite an overall stable direction of association.

The TyG-BMI analysis merits particular attention because it was specifically questioned by the reviewer. TyG-BMI is mathematically derived from both TyG and BMI. If a multivariable model further adjusts for BMI itself, WC, or closely related adiposity covariates, part of the biological signal carried by the composite exposure may be statistically absorbed by the adjustment set. This is not merely a matter of including many confounders; rather, it reflects adjustment for variables that overlap structurally or biologically with the index itself. Under such conditions, the apparent independent association of TyG-BMI may be attenuated and may become less stable across studies. This provides a plausible explanation for why the TyG-BMI results were more sensitive to study-level modeling differences, precisely the issue highlighted in peer review.

Within this context, the influence of Xiuli Wang 2025 should not be framed as a post hoc exclusion undertaken to obtain a preferred result. Rather, high heterogeneity was identified first, and leave-one-out analysis was then used, as planned, to explore potential contributors. Xiuli Wang 2025 focused on a nonobese population, in which the biological meaning of a BMI-containing index may differ from that in general or obesity-enriched populations. In such a setting, the same increment in TyG-BMI may not reflect the same adiposity-related metabolic gradient. If BMI-related or central adiposity covariates are also included in the adjustment set, the incremental information carried by TyG-BMI may be further reduced. From this perspective, the change in heterogeneity after exclusion of Xiuli Wang 2025 is analytically interpretable and supports model sensitivity and effect modification rather than arbitrary data trimming. This framing is also directly aligned with the reviewer’s request that the exclusion be presented as an investigation of heterogeneity rather than a data-driven attempt to strengthen the conclusion.

The fibrosis-focused literature further supports cautious interpretation beyond simple steatosis. In a Korean longitudinal NAFLD cohort, TyG-related markers were not uniformly positively associated with incident advanced fibrosis.[8] This may reflect stage-specific metabolic remodeling in more advanced disease, where lipid handling and insulin resistance patterns become more complex. Such evidence helps explain why fibrosis-related findings were less stable and why we deliberately avoided overextending formal pooling in that domain. At present, TyG-related indices appear most mature as markers of fatty liver risk, whereas their extension to fibrosis-or NASH-focused stratification remains promising but less settled.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, most included studies were cross-sectional, which limits causal inference. Second, although 19 studies were included in the systematic review, only 8 contributed to at least 1 quantitative synthesis. We addressed this explicitly by separating pooled evidence from descriptive evidence, but several exposure-outcome pathways remained too sparse for formal meta-analysis. Third, evidence for liver fibrosis and NASH was limited and should be regarded as supportive rather than definitive. Fourth, heterogeneity remained substantial in several pooled analyses even after sensitivity analyses. Finally, subgroup-like signals derived from limited descriptive evidence, including findings in adolescents, nonobese populations, or fibrosis-focused settings, should be considered hypothesis-generating and require external validation, as specifically recommended during peer review.

Overall, the present evidence supports a consistent positive association between TyG-related indices and NAFLD/steatosis, while also showing that the evidential depth differs across indices and outcome domains. The basic TyG index currently has the broadest evidence base, whereas composite indices may provide additional information in selected settings. However, further studies with more standardized designs and clearer exposure-outcome definitions are still needed to strengthen cross-study comparability.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, TyG-related indices were consistently associated with NAFLD/steatosis across the available literature. The basic TyG index had the broadest evidence base, whereas composite indices suggested possible additional value in selected settings. At present, these markers are best regarded as accessible tools for metabolic fatty liver risk stratification rather than as substitutes for established diagnostic methods. Future studies should use more standardized outcome definitions, clearer exposure parameterization, and prospective validation across diverse clinical populations to improve comparability and reduce heterogeneity.
