Home health care, e-health, and telemedicine policies in selected middle and high-income countries: a rapid policy review
Authors: Jesus Felix Valenzuela, Mia Desiree Verdeprado, Abigail Tan, Kristine Ann Carandang, Michael Dorosan, Christian Alis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Home health care (HHC) is the provision and delivery of health care services where patients live. In this work, with the Philippines as an exemplar for lower-middle income countries in a similar situation (young-skewing demographics, fragmented health care system, and nascent HHC sector), we review policies regarding HHC services, telehealth, and e-Health in the Philippines and seven other jurisdictions (USA, UK, Canada, Australia, EU, Malaysia, and Thailand), identify specific “best-practice” policies covering aspects such as service delivery, personnel training, financing and governance, and analyze their feasibility of adoption in the Philippines. Barriers to access to quality HHC by patients in the analyzed jurisdictions remain, precluding naive efforts to copy these policies verbatim to the Philippine setting. Finally, we sketch out a timeline for the implementation of HHC efforts in the Philippines, which, due to the local political landscape, needs to be strongly legislation-based.

Introduction

Home health care (HHC) is an umbrella term for a wide variety of home-based initiatives and solutions to help individuals suffering from acute to chronic disorders (e.g., hypertension, cardiopulmonary disorders, and physical injuries). Due to the progressive aging of the worldwide human population, its importance has grown over the years: the global HHC market is estimated at US$ 167.28 billion1 in 2020 and is projected to grow by 8.65% per year over the next decade, as populations around the world grow and age. Demand is primarily driven by North American countries at present, with aging populations elsewhere (e.g., Western Europe, Japan, South Korea, China, and India) expected to eventually contribute a higher global share. Because HHC is highly valued across different countries and jurisdictions, the policy frameworks and regulations that govern it have taken on corresponding importance in these contexts.

The global share of the aging population living in lower- and middle-income countries is also increasing and is expected to reach 80% by 2030, which means that having proper policies in place to regulate HHC will also become important in these countries. However, in 2013, 75% of LMICs had insufficient data to inform the planning of health and social care for this segment of the population2. This data insufficiency is accompanied by policy and regulatory gaps in LMICs such as the Philippines, the subject country of the present study, which operate under significantly heavier constraints (whether societal, fiscal, or political) on the formulation and implementation of health care policies.

The Philippines is a Southeast Asian LMIC3,4. Compared to its geographical neighbors in the LMIC and upper middle-income country categories, HHC in the country faces a double challenge. The country’s health care system is fragmented, consisting of a mix of publicly funded and private for-profit health care providers, and no effective mechanisms to regulate the latter5. Furthermore, and of more immediate relevance to the present study, the country’s HHC sector is substantially underdeveloped, with a low degree of awareness of it in legislative and regulatory circles.

Due to the infancy of the HHC sector, as well as telemedicine and e-health in the Philippines, the regulatory framework for such services in the country is limited beyond the relevant provisions concerning electronic health records in the Universal Health Care Act of 2019 (Republic Act 11223)6 and its Implementing Rules and Regulations (IRR), jointly issued by the Department of Health (DOH) and the Philippine Health Insurance Corporation (PhilHealth). However, both the law and its IRRs do not explicitly cover the HHC sector. This has the consequence that PhilHealth, which was established to provide health insurance coverage to residents, does not provide coverage for the few existing local HHC providers; patients who wish to avail of such services bear costs out of pocket. The impact thus falls disproportionately on those of lower income groups, who have little to no access to private health insurance which covers HHC services. At the same time, a regulatory framework for the local HHC sector will require provisions for provider payouts from PhilHealth. Although there are ongoing legislative proposals to address this (such as House Bill No. 7422, the Philippine E-Health and Telemedicine Development Bill of 2020)7, these do not outline detailed rules and regulations, leaving the task to relevant government agencies.

Thus, the state of HHC in the country is an opportunity for local policymakers to formulate and implement appropriate policy frameworks without the inertia of preexisting regulations. The need for such frameworks has also grown due to the COVID-19 pandemic, accompanied by a growth in the local HHC, telemedicine, and e-Health sector8 on the one hand, and exposed several gaps in both public health care infrastructure and national health care policies9, on the other.

The formulation of policy frameworks suitable for the Philippine HHC context requires the identification of key characteristics of similar HHC, telemedicine, and e-Health policies in other countries as well as the current status of local policies pertaining to these three areas. With the Philippines as an exemplar to healthcare jurisdictions in a similar situation, this study thus aims to identify key features and practice patterns of HHC, telemedicine, and e-Health policies in selected overseas jurisdictions (countries from the Asia Pacific, Europe, and the Americas) and compare them to existing rules and policies in the Philippines. By doing this, we can compare features from countries with more developed HHC policies, and those whose structures are closer to the Philippines’ state of HHC.

The primary objectives of the present study are twofold. First, by conducting a review of existing policy frameworks governing HHC and related fields in a selection of countries and jurisdictions that have them and comparing them with the state of the Philippine regulatory landscape on HHC, this study aims to establish itself as a foundation for filling in gaps in the local HHC regulatory framework and thus a useful resource for Philippine policymakers. Subsidiary to this, we wish to examine whether there had been notable impacts brought about by the pandemic on the local policy landscape for HHC and related concepts, such as telemedicine and e-Health.

Second, since the conditions prevalent in the Philippines are broadly similar to those in other LMICs and fragmentation in health care systems is prevalent in countries regardless of income status10–14, the policies identified as appropriate may also find applicability in them.

First is the theme “Guidelines on Data Management”. This key theme recognized by the coding framework revolves around data management standards and guidelines, as well as data security measures. This overarching theme can be further broken down into the following subthemes.

Specific frameworks and guidelines in accessing data

In 2019, the Philippine government developed and implemented the E-Government Master Plan 202215 to provide a safe network for government information systems and standardize IT systems in the public sector. Among the projects under the Medium-Term Information and Communication Technology Harmonization Initiative was the development of the Philippine Health Information Exchange Lite and the Standards Conformance and Interoperability Validation16. The National Health Data Repository (NHDR) Framework, which contains several services and applications for centralized or decentralized data submission, integration, and access requests, was issued by DOH and PhilHealth in 202217.

Common data standards

To ensure mandatory adoption of health data standards and interoperability between health care sectors, policies such as AO No. 2013-0025: National Implementation of Health Data Standards for e-Health Standardization and Information Interoperability18 and Joint AO No. 2021-0001: Guidelines on the Implementation of the Standards Conformance and Interoperability Validation were developed19. Accurate and timely sharing of health care data among various providers and systems is ensured by adherence to these criteria. The creation of a public health information system is required by RA No. 11332: Mandatory Reporting of Notifiable Diseases and Health Events of Public Health Concern20 in order to ensure the reporting of data. The NHDR, which aims to build an integrated approach to data management, processing, sharing, and governance for evidence-based sectoral policy and planning for Universal Health Coverage, requires all health care-related businesses to submit their health data to it. Data storage, compute services, and client services are included in the NHDR framework17.

Data security measures

Policies have been put in place to ensure that health care data are submitted, reported, stored, and processed in a reliable and organized manner. This is done with the aim of improving access, availability, and sharing of medical information between different geographic areas and health care sectors. The development and implementation of health data standards for e-Health standardization and interoperability is critical for the country to have a responsive health care system. The policies also include data security guidelines, such as maintaining privacy and limiting sharing. The NHDR framework recommends the following architectural components: data storage, compute services, and client services. The framework preserves the commitment to protect the privacy, confidentiality, and security of health information and records of citizens, as stipulated by applicable laws, regulations, guidelines, court rulings, and ethical standards of healthcare providers. The NHDR framework strikes a balance between the free flow of information and the rights of individuals to privacy, to develop a trustworthy e-Health environment that prioritizes data protection as a key component to achieve the best results17. When collecting and sharing personal information, it is necessary to obtain consent and respect the individual’s right to health privacy, which is protected by RA No. 10173, also known as the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (DPA)21, and AO No. 2020-0030: Data Privacy Guidelines on the Processing of Health Information22.

Service requirements

The New Implementing Guidelines on Health Technology Assessment (HTA)23 require a thorough examination of all health technologies, including e-Health systems. HTA is a priority-setting tool in the pursuit of universal health care, assessing the clinical, economic, organizational, social, and ethical aspects of a health intervention or technology. Guidelines were also provided for e-Health systems such as electronic Konsulta (eKonsulta) and other health information systems. The Integrated Chronic Noncommunicable Disease Registry System establishes standards for system architecture, components, data flow, reporting, security, and confidentiality, which must be adhered to locally24. Other policies in the review for this subtheme include medical transport and referral system standards and guidelines imposed by the Minimum Health System Capacity Standards for COVID-19 Preparedness and Response Strategies25. The policies are intended to ensure fair and transparent decision-making, efficient disease-related data reporting, and coordinated health care systems.

The second theme obtained from the thematic analysis, “Types of Services”, concerns the services covered by the reviewed policy and research literature, with the subthemes categorized as follows:

e-Health services

The purpose of e-Health services is to improve the health outcomes of citizens8. These services are designed not only to help economically disadvantaged individuals, but also to provide rapid, efficient, and equally valuable virtual health services to the general population, including those who can afford private health care26. The e-Health services evaluated in the articles under review include the promotion of healthy habits through e-Learning and the provision of evidence-based knowledge, information, and advice on the management of e-Health services.

Telemedicine services

Telemedicine has been widely adopted in the Philippines during the COVID-19 pandemic as a means to continue to provide health services while maintaining social distance and reducing the risk of virus transmission in both patients and health providers27. The technology has been utilized for various purposes, including remote consultations, eye exams, occupational therapy, and neurosurgical outpatient care, among others28–30. Patients and healthcare providers have shown a positive attitude towards telemedicine services, particularly for non-urgent conditions31, and telerehabilitation programs have been successful for people with disabilities32. Telemedicine has also been effective in increasing the accessibility and availability of pathology services in remote or underserved regions33.

HHC services

According to the Expanded Senior Citizens Act (RA No. 9994), senior citizens are eligible for discounts on health care services provided by registered professional health care providers through private hospitals or HHC companies. Although there are regulations in place to license various health facilities, there are no policies to regulate new types of health facilities and services, such as geriatric care, convalescent care, mobile health services, telemedicine, and telehealth services. The OHG per Lifestage and OHG for Managers guidelines explain the functions of HHC facilities and services, such as directing patients to rehabilitation, palliative, or hospice care34.

For content analysis, we used the Policy Analysis Triangle (PAT) framework. We extracted the characteristic words for each component of the PAT (context, content, actor, and process), the latter divided into which policies or interventions worked and which did not. For each theme, we obtained the top words according to their occurrence in the literature database.

Actor

All parties involved in both the formulation and implementation of policies are also taken into account in the analysis, as the “actor” component of PAT. This includes mainly national government agencies and public sector entities, but also includes the private sector, academic institutions, and health care facilities, who play crucial roles in policy implementation. The main government agencies involved are the DOH and PhilHealth. DOH is “the principal health agency in the Philippines, responsible for ensuring access to basic public health services to all Filipinos through the provision of quality health care and regulation of providers of health goods and services”35. PhilHealth, on the other hand, was organized to provide health insurance coverage and to ensure that health care services are affordable, acceptable, available, and accessible to all Filipino citizens36. The UHC Act creates the Health Technology Assessment Council (HTAC), an impartial advisory organization whose primary mission is to advise the DOH and PhilHealth on government funding of health technologies and interventions. The HTAC is responsible for evaluating the clinical and economic impact of these technologies on the Philippine health system to improve overall health outcomes and ensure that health coverage is accessible, equitable, and sustainable for all residents. During its evaluations, the Council takes into account the ethical, legal, social, and health care system aspects37.

Context

The target audience and demography, as well as the specific focus of the research or policy, are determined by the “context” component of PAT. Ten of the 45 policies examined focus on promoting health equity, with a particular emphasis on underserved or distant areas. The World Health Organization defines health equity as the absence of avoidable disparities between people and groups38. Access to health care is considered a basic human right, and this is a serious problem in the Philippines, particularly in rural and neglected areas. A specific policy to address this issue is the Guidelines for Identifying Geographically Isolated and Disadvantaged Areas and Strengthening Their Health Systems39. COVID-19 policies are the second most common group of policies examined (n = 5). The rest of the policies address health literacy, ethics, and specific ailments such as diabetes, cancer, hypertension, tuberculosis, and mental health issues. The primary target demographics of these policies are diabetic patients and seniors, while other people with special health conditions are also addressed.

In the context of Philippine HHC, e-Health and telemedicine, two subthemes emerge: (1) the need for national policies for e-Health, telemedicine, and HHC, and (2) the enhancement of the delivery of services to residents. The policies described earlier were established due to the need for standardization and interoperability of the systems used for e-Health and telemedicine and to protect the rights of patients with respect to data privacy and receiving quality health care. Studies also showed that service delivery when done using e-Health technologies, or through telemedicine or home health, improved, as evidenced by (1) improved health outcomes, (2) increased access, (3) cost reduction, and (4) increased efficiency, among others, for certain target groups and health services as previously discussed.

Content

The “content” component of the analysis triangle refers to the essential elements of a policy. The two primary themes that emerged from the analysis of these policies were (1) national standards for HHC, e-Health, and telemedicine and (2) guidelines for the implementation of HHC, e-Health, and telemedicine services. In our review of Philippine policies, we found that they usually contain general definitions and guidelines, but lack detailed information on technology. Laws, AO, and circulars outline frameworks and stakeholder responsibilities, but do not provide specific information about activities, plans, and program logic related to HHC, e-Health, and telemedicine. The qualitative research articles included in our review focused on the views of healthcare providers or intended users of these technologies, rather than discussing the technologies themselves. Most of the research work we reviewed presented specific e-Health services or telehealth innovations designed for particular target groups, such as telerehabilitation for speech-language pathology and telepathology for patients in remote or underserved areas of the Philippines32,33.

The reviewed policies are intended to provide national guidance for the local implementation of health care services for local patients. There is a focus on senior citizens’ health requirements, as well as PhilHealth’s role as the country’s principal purchaser of health care. Diagnostic services, primary care services, mental health, nutrition, dental health, vaccination services, foster care, geriatric care, custodial care, and palliative care are among the specialized services covered. These interventions are already supported by policies, but may need to be reviewed and updated to ensure health equity, particularly for underserved populations.

Process

The “process” component of PAT involves the analysis of successful e-Health and telemedicine programs in the Philippines. The main subthemes of this component of the analysis are (1) improvement in access and service delivery, and (2) positive impact on patients and workers. Notable programs include the E-sakto program and an mHealth-based patient and nurse education program, which have effectively improved health care delivery and patient outcomes40,41. Telemedicine has been particularly effective in improving access to care and improving health outcomes in underserved areas42. Patients and health care workers have also experienced positive impacts from e-Health and telemedicine interventions, particularly during the COVID-19 pandemic31.

We also analyzed aspects of policies that did not work as planned when implemented. Barriers to the implementation of e-Health and telemedicine in the Philippines include limited infrastructure and connectivity, limited training and support for healthcare professionals, challenges in access due to various factors, and limited assessment and regulation43,44, which were also the main subthemes resulting from the content and thematic analysis done. The lack of reliable Internet connectivity in rural areas creates disparities, while inadequate training for health care professionals hinders the effectiveness of telesonography and telemedicine. The absence of policies or guidelines to assess the impact or monitor the progress of e-Health and telemedicine programs is also a concern45,46.

Key features of HHC, telemedicine and e-health policies in selected model countries

A total of 274 works from selected jurisdictions (Australia, Canada, the European Union, Malaysia, Thailand, the United Kingdom, and the United States) met the inclusion criteria. Reviews and comparative analyses (including cross-country comparisons) made up the largest single category of the included works (n = 112). Other types of research make up a slightly higher amount (n = 132), but are a catch-all category consisting of various types of studies (e.g., descriptive and case studies, reports, cohort and qualitative studies), editorials, and other types of technical literature. Books and book chapters are far behind with 12, while legal documents and gray literature each have only 9. In some ways, this arose naturally out of the research protocol, where the search for international literature made sole use of Google Scholar, which privileges technical and research works. However, the substantial number of reviews, comparative analyses, and cross-country studies that met the inclusion criteria offer insight into aspects of HHC, home care, or their equivalents in non-Philippine countries. Summary of themes, subthemes and categories identified from the surveyed global literature

The first theme identified pertains to “Home Health”. Some notable existing practices related to HHC in other countries include standardizing orders for home health nursing with remote monitoring, the implementation of a Digital Care Plan, and optimization of home care scheduling, among others47–49. These practices not only would improve the transition from hospital to home care, but will also streamline some of the processes involved in HHC.

Practice patterns positively affect patients and healthcare workers. Among these are good working environments50 and the implementation of specific policies that benefit employees in the HHC sector51. The 2015 Amendment of the Fair Labor Standards Act in the United States is meant to benefit companionship service providers such as those who work formally or informally in the HHC sector. Despite the fact that Medicaid provisions are limited by eligibility requirements for care recipients, several state-level Medicaid policies have direct effects on family caregivers by providing case management, homemaker/home health aide/personal care services, adult day care, and respite care52. The standardization of orders, assessment tools, and service procedures for HHC appeared to improve the completeness and consistency of orders for reference by HHC workers. Implementing these changes also increased professional satisfaction and empowerment among members of the HHC team53.

Barriers to effective HHC practices were also mentioned in the global literature. These include discrimination or a negative attitude towards patients54, inequitable access to HHC55, and lack of funding, support, and infrastructure56,57. In addition, there is a lack of empirical research, clinical guidelines, and laws covering the protection of HHC workers58,59.

The second theme identified with the coding framework pertains to “Participants in the HHC Services”. Many articles in the global literature mentioned the stakeholders involved in the provision of HHC services60–63. Home care and HHC professionals help patients receive personalized medical and nonmedical care in the comfort of their own homes. Professionals in home care services and nurses provide excellent medical treatment and monitor the health of patients. Community-based home care agencies help their communities by providing accessible care. Critical care specialists treat severe medical conditions. Long-term care providers help people who are chronically ill or disabled. Palliative care specialists provide comfort to terminally ill patients. Informal caregivers, such as family and friends, provide loved ones with free in-home care. Finally, companions help people with daily duties and offer emotional support. This wide range of HHC providers ensures complete and personalized home care.

Other parties involved, in addition to actual care providers, are technology and infrastructure providers, policymakers, and financial sources.

All in all, we identify seven sub-themes, each of which can be mapped onto one of the themes in the PAT. We consider each PAT theme in turn and look at the sub-themes identified from the content analysis linked to it.

Context

Some of the included works had been associated with specific health issues or populations: “COVID-19”, “seniors”, and “health equity”, consistent with the increased focus on the health concerns of the elderly associated with the COVID-19 pandemic, which disproportionately affected elderly populations. Other tags like “dementia”, “mental health”, and “palliative care” are also strongly associated with the elderly, indicating the strong focus of HHC in non-Philippine countries on elderly populations and those with end-stage medical conditions. The most common words occurring (e.g., “services”, “elderly”, “patients”, “providers”, and “healthcare”) relate to the provision of care services to elderly patients and are surrounded by other terms (e.g., “community”, “telehealth”, “rural”, “policy”, “regulations”) which set the context of care services: community care, telehealth, and e-Health. This is supported by the main subthemes based on the results of the combined thematic and content analysis, which are: (1) HHC, e-Health and telemedicine services for the elderly and other patients, and (2) health care regulations and policies for areas and health workers.

Discussions of several HHC types are found scattered among included works. For example, consumer-driven HHC programs are mentioned in passing in a study of workplace violence among HHC workers in the United States64. HHC services mandated by the Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic and Treatment (EPSDT) program of Medicaid (also in the United States) are mentioned in the context of proposals to establish federal oversight over providers, and of patient complaints over access to care under Medicaid, which, while jointly funded by the states and the federal government, is state-administered65. Some identified types of HHC services are in the context of vulnerable populations, such as children in the United States65, seniors in Malaysia66 or gender identity minorities in Australia, Canada, the United States, and the United Kingdom54. Others are policy-based: the importance of a national, framework-based regime for the regulation and financing of home care services also emerged, with four European jurisdictions (Germany, Sweden, Scotland, and the Netherlands) discussed as examples67. Finally, proposals have been made to make permanent emergency measures impacting home care taken by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic68,69.

Content

The most common words for the “content” component of the analysis are “ethical”, “providers”, “implications”, “social”, “services” and “technology,” which indicate a focus on ethical and social implications of HHC and telehealth policies. The main subthemes based on the results of the combined thematic and content analysis are: (1) the social implications of HHC, e-Health and telemedicine services, (2) government support, and (3) criteria for effectiveness, equity and the provision of ethical services.

Several recommended policies are therefore discussed in the included literature. Policy recommendations range in scope from the national level, such as national government policies and policy proposals for the financing and governance of HHC services65,69–75, standards and minimum requirements for the training of HHC personnel54,76–80, down to the level of the service provider: consumer-directed home care in the United States81, personalization of home care services in the UK82, and the role of informal home care providers83.

Process

The most common words in the “Process” component (“what worked”) stress the importance of receiving home care services (“individuals”, “patients”) and access to such services (“access”, “support”, “providers”, “services”). The main subthemes based on the results of the combined thematic and content analysis are: (1) improved access and service delivery for individuals and patients, (2) government support, and (3) management and training policies for health workers.

Several policies or interventions discussed in the included works can be considered as “best practices”, which may be worthy of consideration for local implementation, though still subject to suitability for local conditions. At least two policies deal with the collaboration among HHC personnel providing the services. Pagaiya et al.84 conducted a study to determine the optimal combination of skills for a multidisciplinary team of HHC providers. Jones et al.85 made proposals for collaboration between HHC nurses and pharmacists to improve medication reconciliation and outcomes for patients in the United States with heart failure. Foster et al.86 proposed integrating telehealth into HHC for children with complex medical conditions, in particular videoconferencing and remote home monitoring of clinical parameters such as respiratory physiology and blood sugar levels. This promises to link immediate providers of HHC services (nurses) with specialists based in medical institutions such as hospitals. The results of a modeling study by ref. 87 suggest that home telemonitoring (HTM) and nurse telephone support (NTS) are viable solutions to support patients with chronic heart failure in the Netherlands.

Comparison of Philippine and global features

In this section, we compare the Philippines with other countries in terms of the various components of PAT. Context-wise, HHC, telehealth, and e-Health in the surveyed global (that is, the non-Philippine) literature exhibit a strong association with the provision of care for the elderly, for those with end-stage medical conditions, and other vulnerable populations, with a subsidiary emphasis on the importance of a national, framework-based regulation and financing regime for such services. Content-wise, analysis revealed a focus on ethical and social implications of HHC, telehealth and e-Health policies, and recommendations on, e.g., financing and governance of HHC services, minimum standards or requirements for the training of HHC personnel, and the role of informal providers. Process-wise, the main themes that emerged include improved access and service delivery for individuals and patients, government support and management, and training policies for health workers. Notable studies are those that touch on collaboration between people in multiple disciplines (such as HHC nurses and pharmacists) and the optimal combination of skills for such teams.

In the following, we will go into detail on the comparisons made for each component of PAT, as well as the suggested changes to the current state of e-Health, telemedicine, and HHC in the Philippines.

Actor

Philippine articles reviewed mentioned neurosurgeons, neurologists, sonologists, and ophthalmologists as providers of e-Health and telemedicine consultation, while other countries included OB-GYNs. Occupational therapists, speech pathologists, and nurses are common allied health professions in Philippine and international publications. Psychometricians are cited in Philippine papers, but physical therapists and midwives are listed in foreign literature. Community pharmacists are included in the ancillary providers of e-Health, telemedicine, and HHC in other countries, but not in the Philippines. Both Philippine and foreign articles highlight long-term care specialists. The Philippines has community rehabilitation personnel, while others have palliative and geriatric care providers. Finally, medical social service workers, unpaid or informal caregivers, family caregivers, independent or agency-based caregivers, and HHC workers provide HHC in various countries. The Philippine literature does not discuss these. The absence of mention of these HHC providers in the articles from the Philippines implies a lack of research and policies related to HHC in the country and a lack of awareness of their role in the health care system.

Compared to other countries, the Philippine articles provide less coverage of health care providers offering e-Health, telemedicine, and HHC services, particularly in the areas of ancillary care, long-term care, and other health care providers. However, the articles from the Philippines identify specialized consultation providers such as neurosurgeons, neurologists, sonologists, and ophthalmologists that are absent from the other reviewed articles.

The proposed change in the current workforce of e-Health, telemedicine, and HHC in the Philippines is to include informal caregivers and family caregivers as HHC human resources. By incorporating them into the programs, they can receive the formal training necessary to acquire the competencies required for their position. Furthermore, other health care providers, such as palliative care professionals, geriatric care providers, midwives, and pharmacists, should be included in training and support to improve the quality of HHC services in the Philippines. This change in the current workforce can result in a more comprehensive and efficient health care system that can meet the nation’s increasing demand for e-Health, telemedicine, and HHC services. In addition to the aforementioned measures, it is imperative to establish educational initiatives that address the literacy needs of all health care providers. By doing so, they will be better equipped to use current and new technologies in e-Health and telemedicine effectively, thus improving their ability to provide superior care to their patients.

Context

The Philippines appears to be on par with other countries in terms of who benefits from HHC, e-Health, and telemedicine. Most of the Philippine policies evaluated, on the other hand, had limited or overly broad scopes. National policies mentioning HHC, for example, apply only to seniors, adolescents with special health care needs or serious and/or life-threatening illness, adults living with cancer, and people with disabilities, while telemedicine was initially recommended only for seniors and other vulnerable groups, and e-Health to reduce health disparities and improve health outcomes. As a result, policies must be broadened to address (1) the needs of citizens suffering from infectious diseases, noncommunicable diseases, neurologic diseases, and mental health conditions, (2) the services needed to maintain or improve family health, and (3) the needs of disaster-affected communities or communities located in geographically isolated and disadvantaged areas. This could improve health service delivery and health outcomes and eventually address health inequities.

The reviewed material also emphasized the importance of developing regulatory frameworks for HHC, e-Health, and telemedicine to properly and efficiently implement existing policies, as the Philippines does not appear to have a unified framework for these services other than compliance with national health data standards. Policymakers may be inspired by consumer-driven HHC initiatives, early and periodic screening, diagnostic, and treatment programs, and regulatory and financing schemes in other countries.

Content

All countries recognize the value of ICT, telemedicine, and e-Health in increasing access to health care services, particularly for people who would otherwise have difficulty accessing them due to geographic, economic, or sociocultural barriers. The Philippines, along with neighboring countries in Southeast Asia and Australia, places a high value on the training of healthcare workers. Australian providers, in particular, employ risk and needs assessment methods to develop strategic actions and interventions to improve health outcomes.

The Philippines, like other countries, has policy documents that govern e-Health and primary care. However, we discovered a lack of recent tangible measures for their implementation; e-Health or telemedicine-based treatments are often driven by local medical institutions or academia, and so are fragmented in nature. Thus, concrete, coordinated, and persistent efforts must be made to implement these policies.

A corresponding policy or strategy document should also be developed specifically for HHC. Compared to other countries, the Philippines currently lacks a particular strategy or framework for HHC, either as a standalone service or as part of primary care. Although this may be due to the relative youth of the Philippine population, as HHC is especially associated with older people in higher-income countries, aging is unavoidable, and Philippine authorities should consider having such a framework in place for when the current young population reaches old age. HHC in the context of primary care may be more appropriate for local circumstances and is recognized as such in the National Health Objectives for the Philippines 2017–2022.

PhilHealth provides limited public health coverage (required by the Universal Health Care Act) to all citizens through a capitation (lump-sum per patient) based on the provider’s performance (based on PhilHealth’s percentage utilization of the provider’s services and per-condition case rates published by the latter). This differs from “fee-for-service” and “value-based purchasing,” which are utilized in the US. In Ontario, Canada, and the UK, qualified patients receive a lump sum to cover health care expenditures. Each payment model has pros and cons, and government agencies can evaluate modifications to PhilHealth’s funding and payment structure.

HHC, e-Health, and telemedicine are critical components of global health service delivery. The methods and focus of each country in implementing these standards differ. e-Health and telemedicine programs are used to increase access and service delivery in the Philippines. These initiatives have been found to help patients and physicians. Training the health workforce is a significant priority in Southeast Asian countries, including Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia, and Vietnam. These countries provide a diverse range of health promotion activities for individuals, communities, and populations. Additionally, their e-Health policies and guidelines are comprehensive, including strategies, toolkits, and feedback or monitoring regulations.

Through HHC, e-Health, and telemedicine, Australia has improved access to services for many communities and disadvantaged populations. Staff training and assistance are governed by regulations and guidelines, and clinicians employ methods such as risk assessment and needs assessment to improve health outcomes. European countries recognize the value of ICTs in the implementation of HHC programs. ICT can help improve service delivery, cut health care costs, and improve the quality of life of their patients and their families. Informal caregivers can also benefit from telehealth and telemedicine. Most of the countries we surveyed provide partial or full financial support for home care services. Finally, in the United States and Canada, the emphasis is on training and support for health workers, as well as increasing access to services through telehealth. Acceptance of e-Health and telemedicine programs is also dependent on the patient, the health workforce, and organizational participation.

Although each country approaches practices in HHC, e-Health, and telemedicine differently, some principles are universal, such as (1) improving accessibility, (2) providing training and support for health care personnel, and (3) advancing health outcomes through stakeholder participation and efficient execution.

Several challenges have been encountered in the development and implementation of HHC, e-Health, and telemedicine programs in various countries. The absence of infrastructure and support for the implementation of these programs is one of the key similarities among all of the countries. In the Philippines, the lack of ICT infrastructure and connection, as well as the lack of Internet access, have been key impediments to the implementation of e-Health and telemedicine programs. Training and support for health personnel are also needed, as well as better access due to geographical location and lack of infrastructure. The lack of policies and procedures to analyze the impact and monitor the effectiveness of these interventions is also a concern. The lack of ICT infrastructure and the higher rates of illiteracy in rural areas have hampered efforts in e-Health technology in Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia, and Vietnam. A lack of information among health personnel and patients, as well as a poor image of government assistance and NGOs, are also significant impediments to program implementation. In Australia, a lack of funding has resulted in a lack of ICT infrastructure and support and training for providers, which results in a lack of end-user adoption. Government assistance is also needed to overcome problems in partnership with state-oriented government operations. In European countries, there is a lack of universal regulation for HHC, e-Health, and telemedicine initiatives. There is also a recognized need for comprehensive and consistent laws, regulations, or particular recommendations. There is a lack of common regulations or standards for telehealth in the United States and Canada, as well as a need for HHC financing and regulatory policies. There is also minimal support for health workers, prompting a need for laws to protect their rights, as well as to provide training and support for them.

In LMICs, the efficacy of mHealth interventions (the use of mobile phones and other wireless technologies) is becoming more evident, particularly in terms of improving access to care in low-resource settings. There is also increasing evidence that these interventions have positive health outcomes, such as increased physical activity and healthy diets. On the contrary, governments in HICs provide substantial support for HHC services through financing policies and support programs. In addition, it has been shown that standardizing policies and guidelines increases the efficiency of program implementation. In addition to being a crucial component in improving services and health outcomes in HICs, training of health workers is also essential. This includes training in cultural competency and specific skills for HHC or the use of e-Health and telemedicine equipment.

In terms of observed practices for HHC, e-Health and telemedicine, LMICs and HICs share the need for effective health interventions and enhanced health outcomes. Differences occur in the types of support and resources available, such as government funding, standardization, and training for health workers.

In LMICs, the lack of impact and economic evaluation studies on mHealth, as well as feasibility studies to investigate the scalability of interventions, is one of the major limitations. The ineffective implementation of mHealth interventions is also impeded by a lack of infrastructure, understanding, and acceptance of technology, as well as funding. On the other hand, in HICs, telehealth is not regulated, necessitating regulatory frameworks and policies to ensure the quality of service of e-Health and telemedicine programs. Furthermore, the lack of government support and funding results in the absence of the ICT infrastructure required for e-Health and telemedicine programs, as well as the lack of sustainability and equitable access for HHC programs.

LMICs, such as the Philippines, face the typical challenges of a country that is just beginning to adopt HHC, e-Health, and telehealth. Primarily, these can be categorized as infrastructure, technology, and policy and financial assistance. HICs are distinguished by the rapid adoption of technologies, which requires policies that keep up with innovation to safeguard adopting stakeholders. Despite their differences, HICs and LMICs share the value of HHC, e-Health, and telehealth as a link between hard-to-reach populations and affordable health care.

Based on the practice patterns and limitations explored, it is suggested that the Philippines employ a multi-stakeholder strategy to advance the agenda for HHC, e-Health, and telehealth. The DOH, PhilHealth, and the Department of Information and Communications Technology are responsible for making health care technologies accessible through an all-encompassing ICT infrastructure and a qualified and well-trained health care workforce. To provide safeguards for the adoption and use of ICT infrastructure and solutions, standardization of policies or guidelines, training of health workers, and robust economic evaluation must be in place.

Discussion

In its Global Strategy on Digital Health, 2020–202588, the World Health Organization recognized particular challenges facing low- and middle-income countries in the implementation of digital health technologies, and suggested exploring the potential of international collaborations and shared services in overcoming these challenges. To this are added three further strategic objectives, calling on WHO member states to work toward implementing digital strategies, strengthening digital health governance, and developing human-centered health systems. From our results, it is apparent that progress in the Philippines towards the implementation of the four is insufficient, with efforts for digital health governance and human-centered health systems (the latter of which depends on the other three for its foundations) lagging the most of all, primarily due to the absence of unified regulatory frameworks and the scattered and piecemeal nature of potentially relevant regulations. Of the four, the Philippine authorities have made the most substantial steps in the implementation of the digital strategy, but much work (including unifying the scattered pieces of relevant legislation and regulations) remains to be done. Health authorities, along with those of the other member countries of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), had recently resolved to intensify efforts and initiatives to share knowledge and with those of the United States for digital health89,90; however, concrete details remain to be seen at the time of writing. The local situation for HHC is similar in terms of (insufficient) progress, with a unified framework being absent in official policy documents, and HHC not forming a significant portion of the local health policy discourse, which is still oriented substantially towards primary health.

With the current situation, what would be useful for Philippine health policymakers is a series of short, medium, and long-term proposals for HHC. In assembling this, we keep in mind the following features, both official and unofficial, of the local political system:A presidential form of government where the ultimate authorities for health policymaking (the Secretary and Undersecretaries of the DOH) serve at the pleasure of the President (the head of the executive branch);The Presidency being an elective position, with presidents serving for a single, non-repeatable, six-year term;Insufficient continuity in policy between successive administrations due to health policymakers ultimately serving at the pleasure of Presidents and going out of government with them at the end of their terms, and due to incoming administrations typically subjecting persons and policies associated with the previous one to stringent auditing and investigations “for transparency purposes”; andThe term length for Congress (the bicameral legislative branch) being three years (synchronized with legislative elections held during the middle of a sitting President’s term), which impairs continuity between successive terms; any pending legislative item during a Congressional term expires with it and has to be re-introduced ab initio during the next legislative term.

Thus, due to the substantial risks to policy continuity accompanying transitions in presidential administration, we take a heavily legislative-oriented approach to the proposed measures below. We take short-term as achievable within a single legislative term of three years, medium-term as within one six-year presidential term, and anything requiring a longer time-frame to be long-term.

In the short term, one of the most urgent priorities is for local policymakers, in conjunction with other stakeholders such as representatives of formal and informal health care providers, to formulate mutually-agreed-upon definitions of HHC, telemedicine, telehealth, and e-Health. Such a definition would serve as a common ground for the various stakeholders that are engaged in both the delivery of such services and policymaking related to it. The present review is intended to be one of the works that could contribute to this. Furthermore, as mentioned previously, health care policymakers, specifically in the local DOH and PhilHealth, should define and facilitate the incorporation of the roles played by informal health care providers, such as family members, into their policy frameworks; this will benefit not just HHC, but the local health care sector at large. In addition, they can convene a multi-sectoral, multi-stakeholder core group, which will be tasked to assist in drafting policy documents which can be issued without needing to be coursed through the legislative process; this group can also assist in collating the scattered pieces of policy and legislation, and drafting a codifying law (a “Magna Carta for HHC”) to put local HHC, including funding, governance and oversight mechanisms, on a solid legislative basis, and propose it to lawmakers. Since in the Philippines, much of the actual details for financing, governance, and oversight associated with any given law is left to the appropriate government agency to implement via IRR, the IRR should also be developed in tandem with the proposed codifying law at this stage; however, ideally, the funding and governance mechanisms should be directly incorporated into the codifying law. Finally, they would need to advocate for HHC to the President and leading members of the legislature, so that the legislative processes involved in the medium term (see below) could proceed smoothly. Of the proposed short-term proposals, this last item is both of the highest importance due to this entire series of HHC proposals being heavily predicated on legislative action. Politically, advancing HHC legislative action is challenging at the present, as HHC is currently not a major topic of local health policy discourse.

In the medium term, assuming that the actors involved in the short-term measures above (the health care policymakers and any associated multi-sectoral core group) have done the ground work, the burden of action shifts from them to the President as head of the executive branch, and Congress as the legislative branch. One of the Philippine President’s powers is to certify pending legislation as urgent, which allows them to be passed through the three Constitutionally-required readings on the same day instead of three separate days otherwise; it will be up to the President to certify the proposed HHC codification bill as urgent, and their legislative allies to guide the bill through the (fast-tracked) legislative process before being signed by the President into law. Due to the features of the local political system, this should be accomplished well before the end of the current President’s term, to minimize the chance that it will stall, or worse, be abandoned altogether, due to a potentially hostile President taking over after the next general election. The short-term actors do still have roles to play at this stage, in keeping abreast of developments during the legislative process, and making adjustments to the proposed law and IRR as required. With the codifying law being enacted at this stage, initial steps should also be immediately taken to set up the financing, oversight, and governance mechanisms (e.g., a new government agency with oversight over HHC, which can be attached to the DOH, and empowered to periodically review all aspects of HHC policy and implementation) required by it.

All of the proposed measures above are highly feasible (formulating common terminology and definitions, issuance of policy documents by health care agencies) to moderately feasible (collating scattered pertinent regulations and crafting a codificatory legislative proposal) to feasible with heavy but ultimately resolvable difficulties (guiding the codificatory proposal through Congress and enacting it into law) within a sitting President’s six-year term. The long-term proposals, which will focus on providing policy continuity across presidential terms/administrations, will require much more effort and are less feasible at the current time, due to government agency heads being appointive (and thus not being assured security of tenure past the current President’s term); modifying this will need changes on the level of Constitutional amendments, which is highly contentious within current Philippine political discourse; past amendment attempts have either been for, or perceived to be for, lifting term limits for elected officials and legislators, and thus carries a stigma of corruption, perceived or otherwise. More feasible would be the continued operation and empowerment of the governance and oversight agency mandated by the codificatory law envisioned in the previous stages, as well as deepening cooperation and knowledge-and-technology-sharing with regional (ASEAN+) and international partners.

Methods

Conceptual framework

In this study, we focus on HHC and three other concepts which are commonly associated with it in policy discourse: telemedicine, telehealth, and e-Health. HHC, as defined previously, is the provision of health services to a patient in a home setting, as opposed to a hospital or health care center. The “home” in this setting may refer to the patient’s actual home, or, as common in jurisdictions with heavily elderly-skewed population profiles, long-term care facilities such as nursing homes or “homes for the aged”. The service may be delivered in person, or remotely, and it is commonly in connection with the latter mode of delivery that telehealth, telemedicine and e-Health are mentioned.

However, in contrast to HHC, the meanings of telemedicine, telehealth and e-Health had long been contested. Ambiguity remains in their definitions, and past works exist which use the three interchangeably91. Nevertheless, for the purposes of this work, we broadly follow Mangion and Piller’s definitions91: telehealth is the remote delivery of health care services using information and communications technologies (ICT), and telemedicine is the remote delivery of medical, diagnostic and treatment-related services using ICT. The two are not identical due to health care services extending beyond simply medical, diagnostic or treatment services to include physical rehabilitation, psychological counseling, and social support; thus, telemedicine can be thought of as a subset of telehealth92. e-Health, on the other hand, refers to how digital technologies for improving health (which are not limited to health care services alone, as in telehealth and telemedicine) are delivered through ICT. For example, the use of electronic health record systems to store, manage and access patients’ health care data fall under the ambit of e-Health, but neither telehealth or telemedicine (as it is not a health care service)91.

Study design

For this study, we chose to integrate two review approaches: rapid realist review (RRR) and six-stage scoping review93,94. This was done due to the existence of an external time constraint (two months) at the time of the study, in order to ensure quality in spite of this time constraint, since the pre-estimated time frame for the completion of the study (five weeks) fitted within the two-month time limit. Our protocol consists of parts of the RRR and scoping review in tandem: the literature screening stages were taken from the RRR protocol, while the data processing and analysis stages were taken from that of the scoping review.

In the preparation and screening stages, taken from the RRR, we formulated a purpose statement and included published and (for the Philippine side of the study) gray literature, while also accepting recommended literature from topic experts. This was due to a general dearth of published literature and discussions on Philippine health care policies that may be relevant to the study, but can be found on websites of official government agencies or policy study groups hosted by local academic institutions. Thus, we consulted with a local healthcare research specialist to add additional literature in the data collection stage.

From the methodology of the scoping review, we adopted qualitative content analysis with iterative data processing and thematic analysis94. For the analysis, we used the PAT framework95 to define the themes for the deductive thematic analysis approach.

The following subsections detail the stages of the composite methodology we have chosen to use for this review, from the strategy we used in the literature search, up to the data analysis.

Search strategy

We used two databases, Google Scholar and library e-resources, to discover relevant materials. This review included only English-written articles that were (1) published from 2012 to 2022 (for journals and books); (2) located on the first two pages of search results; and (3) conducted in the Philippines or selected countries and jurisdictions (USA, UK, Canada, Australia, EU, Malaysia, and Thailand). The chosen time period covers work before, during, and immediately after the COVID-19 pandemic. Gray literature such as websites, unpublished reports from government departments, media pieces, expert comments, and policy documents was also considered.

The search strategy Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study Design (PICOS)96 guided the selection of search terms, with a focus on population, intervention, and study design. The population search criteria were “USA” or “United States of America”, “UK” or “United Kingdom”, “Canada”, “Australia”, “EU” or “European Union”, “Malaysia”, “Thailand”, and “Philippines”. These countries and jurisdictions were selected, due to them either (1) having mature policy frameworks governing HHC, or (2) countries located within Southeast Asia with similar socio-economic profiles as the Philippines. Policymakers in the latter are known to emulate policies on those countries and jurisdictions falling in either category; for example, Philippine data privacy regulations are closely modeled on those of the European Union, such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and associated rules.

The keywords for intervention were “home health care”, “home care service”, “home health nursing”, “home care agencies”, “telemedicine”, “e-health”, “Mobile Health”, “Health, Mobile”, “mHealth”, “Telehealth”, and “e-Health”. The study design keywords were “Policies”, “Regulations”, “Laws”, “Ordinances”, “Memorandums”, “Rules”, “Department Orders”, “Administrative Orders”, “Guidelines”, “Systematic Review”, “Narrative Review”, “Scoping Review”, and “Rapid Review.” Each search used a combination of three terms: one from the population, one from the intervention, and one from the research design.

There were a total of five reviewers in the team that performed the screening procedure, all of whom are authors of this paper. Motivated by the objective to compare the international policy regime vis-a-vis the local one, we grouped the candidate literature items into two: the Philippines-related items and the non-Philippines-related (global) items. Two primary reviewers each were assigned to screen the items in each group, while the fifth verified the data inputs of the primary reviewers and served as a tie-breaker in case of rater disagreement during the selection stages.

Following the removal of duplicates, the reviewers evaluated the abstracts based on whether or not they pertain to one or more of the inclusion criteria keywords specified previously (that is, whether they are about, e.g., “home health care”, “telemedicine” or another inclusion criteria keyword). The fifth reviewer served as a tiebreaker when the two primary reviewers rated a title or abstract differently. Only abstract-screened items (i.e., those that passed the second screening stage) were chosen for full-text review and data extraction.

Data extraction

To extract pertinent data from the articles selected for full-text review, we developed separate literature databases for Philippine and global literature. The extracted data included author, year published, country, study design/type of literature, policy title, health outcomes addressed, cost-effectiveness value, context, process (what worked and what did not), content, and actor. These data elements correspond to the primary RRR questions93 of what worked and what did not work, for whom, and in what context, as well as the components of the policy triangle framework95.

Data analysis

The literature databases were extracted as Excel files and imported into NVivo97 for thematic and content analyses. In this work, thematic analysis refers to automatic extraction of theme, sub-theme, and category codes by the NVivo software, while content analysis refers to word frequency-based analysis, also performed by NVivo.

For the thematic analysis, using all of the data from the literature databases, NVivo automatically created theme codes for each literature item, which were then reviewed, revised, and reorganized by the Qualitative Consultant. The Consultant also created short descriptive labels for each theme code through a reading of the literature database texts NVivo classified under that code.

For content analysis, we used a deductive approach, where topics have been defined in advance (in this case, the elements of the PAT framework95). Each column, representing the elements of the PAT, was selected and analyzed using the NVivo word frequency option. Depending on the amount of data in the selected column, the top 50–300 5-letter words were selected, accounting for their synonyms, with the exception of stop words (consisting of filler words or common terms) that were manually selected and removed by the Consultant to improve the quality of the results.

Data from the literature database were filtered based on the respective regions of the countries to facilitate comparisons of practice patterns and limitations. The countries were then categorized as either lower-middle-income or high-income and subjected to thematic and content analyses in NVivo, similar to the process described previously, with a concentration on the “Process” columns for each region.
