Knowledge, attitude and practice of pharmacy students compared to other medical fields students regarding self-medication with OTC medicines in kosovo: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Selvete Shuleta-Qehaja, Nita Kelmendi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSelf-medication with over-the-counter (OTC) medicines is a common global practice, yet it carries risks related to incorrect usage, delayed diagnosis, and adverse reactions. This cross-sectional study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding OTC self-medication among pharmacy students and those in other medical fields in Kosovo.MethodsA convenience sample of 800 students was targeted, and 625 completed questionnaires were returned (78.1% response rate). Participants were enrolled in Pharmacy, Nursing, Dentistry, and Physiotherapy at Bachelor’s and Master’s levels. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27, employing descriptive statistics and chi-square tests to determine significant associations (p ≤ 0.05).ResultsOf the 625 respondents, 72.2% indicated current use of OTC drugs, while 56.2% reported having self-medicated in the last six months without consulting a healthcare professional. Headache (93.1%), flu/colds (81.8%), and vitamins (88.6%) were the most frequently cited reasons for OTC usage. Pharmacy students exhibited significantly higher knowledge of OTC medications (p = 0.001), and Master’s-level students were slightly more knowledgeable than their Bachelor’s-level counterparts (p = 0.03). Although 73.8% recognized that all medicines can cause adverse events, around 40–50% believed self-medication is safe without professional guidance. Females and pharmacy students more consistently checked expiry dates and reported a slightly lower tendency to consider self-medication completely safe.ConclusionSelf-medication with OTC medicines is prevalent among these student populations, particularly for managing mild symptoms. Pharmacy and graduate-level students demonstrated a stronger awareness of potential risks, highlighting the role of targeted education in promoting safer self-medication practices. Strengthening curricula to emphasize responsible OTC use and encouraging students to seek professional consultation when necessary could help mitigate associated risks.

Introduction

Self-medication is the use of medicine based on the patient’s self-diagnosing symptoms without a medical prescription. According to EU legislation, non-prescription medicinal products are also called OTCs (over-the-counter medication), which can be purchased without a medical prescription [1].

As stated by the World Health Organization (WHO), self-medication involves using medicines to address self-diagnosed disorders or symptoms or periodically or continuously using prescribed drugs for chronic or recurring conditions even beyond the recommended duration. Additionally, the WHO defines products intended for self-medication as those not requiring a prescription, which are produced, distributed, and sold with the expectation that consumers will choose and use them on their initiative and responsibility whenever they deem appropriate. The term “over-the-counter (OTC) medicines’ is used to describe this category of products [2].

Kosovo is located in Southeastern Europe, and the World Bank classifies it as an uppermiddle-income country [3]. At present, there is no public health insurance, and no publicly funded outpatient medicines are available. Because a reimbursement system has yet to be established, patients must pay out of pocket the entire cost of outpatient medications. [4] This financial burden may discourage people from visiting a doctor and instead lead them to purchase medicines directly from pharmacies without a prescription.

According to Hughes et al. (2001), self-medication has advantages because it increases patient independence in managing minor ailments and helps the health system reduce costs related to prescription drugs. On the other hand, it also has disadvantages, such as errors in diagnosis, overdose, and prolongation of drug treatment, as well as drug interactions and polypharmacy [5].

The use of OTC medicines as part of self-medication is constantly increasing worldwide, and it is frequently practised among university and college students. Different studies have analyzed the prevalence of self-medication amongst different groups of students. The results were from 64.8 to 98.2% [6–19]. The results of the systematic review and meta-analysis article by Behzadifar et al. (2020) showed that the prevalence of self-medication among students worldwide was 70.1% [20].

The study assesses the prevalence of self-medication with OTC medicines in Kosovo and students’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices in pharmacy and other medical fields. The College Ethics Committee approved the study protocol (Protocol No. AD-5731/24).

Materials and methods

The college is accredited in several medical programs, and at the time of the research, there were 1,770 enrolled students. Most students were enrolled in Pharmacy, Dentistry, Nursing, and Physiotherapy. The Pharmacy and Physiotherapy program is divided into Bachelor’s (3 years) and Master’s levels (two years), Dentistry (a five-year program), and Nursing (a Bachelor’s degree). Our study analyzed the 4th and 5th year Dentistry students with the Master’s group of Pharmacy and Physiotherapy. The number of female students enrolled at the college is twice that of male students. Based on the number of students in each program, a convenient sampling technique was used to collect data from the study population. The selection of the 800 students was not based on probabilistic or stratified sampling but rather on convenience sampling with the intent to include proportional representation from each program (Pharmacy, Nursing, Dentistry, and Physiotherapy) and level of study (Bachelor, MSc). The convenience sampling approach was used due to practical constraints, including limited access to more diverse student population and logistical feasibility within the institution involved.

Of the total number of 1770 students registered in medical fields, around 1,400 are registered in the fields that were analyzed, such as around 400 in pharmacy, around 350 in nursing, over 500 in dentistry and over 100 in physiotherapy. The aim was to cover approximately 50% of the enrolled students with proportional allocation by distributing questionnaires based on the approximate enrollment figures from each academic program. While the sampling was convenient, efforts were made to approach a representative share per group.

The researchers distributed the questionnaires during class breaks in paper form, and the students were verbally informed about the purpose of the study and the confidentiality of their responses. To promote participation, verbal follow-up reminders were made during subsequent class visits within the same week for students who had not yet participated. The questionnaires were anonymous. The students also gave verbal consent to participate in the survey. The process of obtaining verbal consent was explained to and approved by the Ethics Committee of the College (Protocol No. AD-5731/24). After receiving the questionnaires, students were asked to return them to a designated person in the administration, who collected them, placed them in separate envelopes, and then delivered them to the researchers. No identifying information was collected, ensuring full participant anonymity. Of the 800 questionnaires distributed, 632 were completed and returned. Out of 632, seven questionnaires were excluded because more than 50% of the questions were left blank, which was defined as the minimum threshold for valid participation. As a result, the final number of valid responses was 625, yielding the overall response rate of 78.1%.

The questionnaire was customized using pre-existing questionnaires from the published literature in the previous studies [6, 14, 21]. It was composed of four main sections: (1) Demographics (gender, age, study program, and academic level), (2) Knowledge regarding OTC medicines (e.g., familiarity with the term “OTC,” understanding of potential side effects, and awareness of proper usage), (3) Attitudes toward self-medication practices (e.g., safety perceptions, time-saving justification, and reasons for self-treatment), and (4) Practice behaviors (e.g., frequency and reasons for OTC use, types of conditions treated, places from where they purchase OTC medicines, and checking expiration dates).

The questionnaire consisted of both close-ended and multiple-choice questions, including “Yes/No/I don’t know” items, Likert-scale type option (e.g., “Always,” “Sometimes,” “Never”), and checklists for multiple responses (e.g., types of conditions for which OTC medicines were used). The questionnaire was prepared in Albanian language. A pre-evaluation test of the translated version of the questionnaire was carried out by randomly choosing ten students from each group. They evaluated the translated questionnaire in Albanian, which was slightly modified and amended to suit students. The study did not include the students who took the pre-evaluation test. To manage potential bias from convenience sampling, questionnaires were proportionally distributed based on program size to ensure representation across study fields and levels. Additionally, students who participated in the pilot test were excluded from the main data collection phase to avoid priming bias.

Using Cronbach’s alpha, a test of the questionnaire’s reliability revealed internal consistency reliability of α = 0.74.

A descriptive, cross-sectional study was adopted for the current study. Data was collected from December 2024 to February 2025 and were analyzed using SPSS Statistics, version 27. A descriptive analysis (percentages and proportions) was performed, and a chi-square test was used for testing associations. The value of p ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The data in Table 2 show that the most frequently cited factor for OTC usage was the perception that symptoms were mild, manageable independently (94.4%), or recognizable from experience (80.2%). The availability of OTC products (63.4% of respondents), particularly at pharmacies, emerged as a major facilitator. Over half the respondents (52.2%) pointed to time constraints as a reason to avoid professional medical advice. 30.1% mentioned that OTC medicines are less expensive than a formal medical consultation, and only a small number of students (19%) cited geographical barriers as a reason for self-medication.

Most respondents (97.6%) purchase OTC medications at a pharmacy, while online purchasing and herbal shops are relatively low (11.7%, respectively 18.2%). However, 34.2% of students obtain OTCs from family or friends, which could raise concerns about the appropriateness of product storage and dosage instructions. Only 14.2% of students reported having experienced side effects from using OTC medications. 82.9% of participants agree that self-medication can lead to incorrect usage, whereas 6.7% disagree, and 10.4% are uncertain. This indicates a significant majority expressing concern regarding the potential misuse of OTC medicines.

77.6% of students declared they “always” check the expiration date before purchasing OTC drugs, signaling a positive safety practice.

As shown in Table 3, most respondents used OTC medicines for headaches (93.1%) and flu/colds (81.8%). A high percentage of students (88.6%) reported using vitamins. Conditions such as fever (68%), sore throat (73.6%), and cough (64.6%) were also frequently self-managed. However, lower usage was reported for more specific issues such as skin problems (23.5%), sleeplessness (24%), and infections (11.7%).

Regarding respondents’ opinions on whether all medicines can have side effects, the majority (69.3–80.1%) believe that all medicines can cause side effects, and there are no significant differences between genders, fields, or levels of academic study.

Furthermore, most students do not consider it safe to reuse OTC medicines without medical guidance; however, the percentage of those who view them as “safe” varies significantly by study program, with pharmacy students being more inclined to believe that this practice is not safe (p = 0.001).

The majority (79.1–87%) of participants agree that self-medication can lead to incorrect use of medications. However, Master’s level students are even more convinced of this (p = 0.001, respectively). Also, the study results show that physiotherapy, dentistry, and pharmacy students appear more affirmative than nursing students (p = 0.008) in their opinion that self-medication can lead to the wrong use of medicines. About 49.3–64% of the surveyed students mentioned time-saving as a reason to avoid visiting a doctor when minor symptoms appear, and there was a significant association between the study program and time-saving as a factor of self-medication (p = 0.05) (Table 5).

Based on the data from Table 6, about half of all groups report using OTC medications in the last 6 months without consulting a healthcare professional, with no significant differences between genders, level of studies or fields of study. The vast majority of respondents (around 97%) purchase OTC medicines at a pharmacy, regardless of gender, study program, or level of studies, indicating that the pharmacy remains the main point of supply for OTC medicines. Online purchasing is generally low overall, but there is a statistically significant association by gender, field of study, and academic level (higher among males, dentistry students, and master ‘s-level students). Pharmacy students were less likely to take OTC from family members or friends than other health programs (p = 0.03). Male students reported obtaining OTC from family or friends more frequently (p = 0.006) and buying OTC products online or in herbal shops (p = 0.001).

Regarding the experience of side effects, males reported experiencing side effects more than females (p = 0.008). There is a significant difference (p = 0.03) in checking the expiration date, where 78.9% of female respondents stated that they check the OTC medication’s expiration date at purchase, compared to 75% of male respondents. Master’s-level students also appear to check the expiration date more often (85.7% vs. 74.8% Bachelor’s-level students), and this difference is significant (p = 0.01).

Discussion

The findings of this study indicate that self-medication with OTC medicines is a prevalent practice among the surveyed students, with 72.2% reporting the use of OTC medicines and about half indicating they had done so in the last six months without consulting a healthcare professional. This rate falls within the middle range of prevalence reported in the global literature on self-medication among university students. For instance, our results are higher than those from Turkey (45%) [22], 49.3% in Dammam City, Saudi Arabia [23], 50.2% in Kerman, Iran [24] and the Chengalpattu District in India (66.2%) [10], but lower than in study from coastal South India 78.6% [19], from Croatia (88%) [25], Hong Kong (94%) [7], Kuwait (97.8%) [14], and Hail in Saudi Arabia (98.2%) [8], over 80% in Serbia [17], 78.5% in Jordan [26]. 92% in South India [18], 79.7% in Gondar, Northwest Ethiopia [27], 79.04% in Albania [42].

In our study, the usage of OTC medicines for minor ailments was notably high for headaches (93.1%) and flu/colds (81.8%), as well as for fever (68%), sore throat (73.6%), and cough (64.6%). These rates exceed those reported by Abay and Amelo (2010) [28], where students reported lower frequencies for fever and headache (24.8%) and for cough and common cold (23.9%) [28]. Likewise, in the study by Badiger et al. (2012) [18], the most commonly reported conditions for OTC use were common cold (69%), fever (63%), and headache (60%) [18)]. Bekele et al. (2020) found an even higher usage for fever (80.2%) compared to our findings. Still, they reported a lower rate of headache medicines (24.4%) [27]. Meanwhile, Beyene et al. (2017) observed a 69.3% usage rate for fever and headache among pharmacy students [29]. More recently, Brian et al. (2025) reported that self-medication for headaches (78.97%), as well as for colds and coughs (79.91%), was common among pharmacy students [30]. In a research by Hashemzaei, et al. in 2021 in Iran among pharmacy and medical students was found there were cold and headache ailments usage was 93.2%, and 60.7%, respectively [31].

A substantial proportion of our students (94.4%) reported self-medicating for mild symptoms—a frequency that far exceeds the findings of several previous studies, including 46.9% [32], 55.4% [33], 82% [18], 54% [34], 30.5% [28], 47.19% [35], 48.3% [8], 80.84% [30], and 58% [11]. In our cohort, 80.2% of participants reported having previously self-treated with the same medicines—a prevalence exceeding those published in earlier studies (30.9% [32], 14.2% [23], 48.8% [34], 50.1% [6], 29 [11], and 35.4% [28] and closely matched only by the 79.2% [36].

52.2% of students in the present study reported choosing OTC self-medication primarily because it saved time. This figure is markedly higher than the rates of 17.9%, 11%, 5.6%, and 12.2% reported by Mumtaz et al. (2011) [32], Badiger (2012) [18], Albusalih et al. (2017) [23], and Zafar et al. (2008) [6], respectively. However, it is closer to the 54.91% reported by Brian et al. (2025) [30] and 45.89% by Alduraibi & Altowayan, 2022) [37]. The majority of students (63.4%) cited “easy access” to OTC medications as a principal reason for self-medication, which is considerably higher than the 15.6% reported by Zafar et al., 2008 [6].

Our findings regarding factors for self-medication among students were more prevalent than in many other published research studies. The possible explanation might be due to easy access to OTC medicines from pharmacies and the possible impact of electronic and broadcast media advertisements. However, subsequent studies should investigate the factors contributing to this behavior pattern among students.

Pharmacies emerged as the primary source of OTC medicines in our study (97% of respondents), mirroring the 97% reported by Albayrak, 2024 [38], but lower than the results of 69.3% found by Araia et al., 2019 [39].

In our survey, 63.4% of students reported having easy access to OTC medicines—nearly double the 35.20% of pharmacy students in the research article by Seam et al., 2018 [40]. Conversely, online purchasing is relatively low in our sample. However, there were significant differences by gender, field of study, and academic level—patterns that reflect broader global increases in internet usage for health-related purchases.

Our study’s Pharmacy students displayed higher knowledge of OTC medicines than students from other medical fields (p = 0.001). The results are similar to those reported in Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and other regions, where pharmacy curricula emphasize pharmacology, rational drug use, and patient safety by Saeed et al., 2014 [12], Al-Hussaini, Mustafa & Ali, 2014 [14]. Similarly, master’s-level students in the present study were slightly more knowledgeable than their bachelor-level counterparts (p = 0.03), similar to published research by Hassan & Koabar (2025) [9] suggesting that more advanced training heightens awareness of proper medication use and potential risks [9].

Although 73.8% recognized that all medicines can cause adverse events, about 40–50% believed self-medication is safe without professional guidance. This finding is in line with other published studies showing a disconnect between students’ understanding of medication risks and their actual self-medication behaviours [41]. Notably, a significant proportion cited time-saving and previous experience with the same medication as primary motivators. Such reasons have been observed globally, underlining the perceived convenience of self-medication when facing mild symptoms [6].

Gender-related differences in side-effect experiences were also noted, as males reported to have experienced more side effects than females. Differences in health-seeking behaviour, risk perception, and OTC medication types could explain these patterns. However, further qualitative investigation may be needed [5]. Additionally, female and master ‘s-level students checked medication expiration dates more consistently, suggesting that female students may pay more attention when buying OTC medicines, and possibly greater health literacy of master’ s-level students could influence safer self-medication practices.

Overall, these results reinforce global concerns regarding self-medication, particularly the risk of incorrect drug use, adverse drug interactions, and delayed professional diagnosis [35].

It should be emphasized that the findings of this study have important implications for both healthcare policy and educational planning in Kosovo. The high prevalence of OTC self-medication among medical field students, combined with knowledge gaps and risky attitudes, underscores the need for a national strategy promoting responsible self-care practices. Policymakers should consider integrating pharmaceutical care awareness campaigns, strengthening pharmacy regulation, and improving access to affordable professional consultations.

The study also highlights a clear need for curricular improvements, especially in non-pharmacy programs. This suggests that curricula in all healthcare fields should include mandatory modules on pharmacovigilance, OTC risk education, and patient counseling skills. Ensuring consistent education across disciplines can help align professional practice with safe medicine use standards, ultimately contributing to better public health outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

The study included students from four healthscience programs (Pharmacy, Nursing, Dentistry, Physiotherapy) and both Bachelor’s and Master’s levels, allowing meaningful between-group comparisons. The study relied on student self-reports, which may be subject to bias. Although the response rate was relatively high, a convenience sampling method was used, which could limit the generalizability of the results. Another limitation of this study is the lack of a priori sample size calculation. Although the sample included students across different programs and levels of study, it was based on convenience sampling, constrained by feasibility and access. This may affect the generalizability of the findings and the statistical power of certain inferential analyses.

Moreover, since the research was conducted at one college with specific medical programs, it could also limit the generalizability of the results to other non-medical program students. Another limitation of this study is the potential for recall bias, as some questions required students to recall behaviors over the previous six months, and this may have led to under-reporting or over-reporting of certain practices.

Conclusion

This study highlights that self-medication with OTC drugs is a prevalent practice among students, driven primarily by ease of access, perceived mildness of symptoms, and time-saving considerations. Although a majority of students possess a basic understanding of OTC medicines and acknowledge potential side effects, essential differences are evident across study programs, academic levels, and, to a lesser extent, gender. Pharmacy and master ‘s-level students generally exhibit more substantial knowledge and cautious attitudes, suggesting that targeted education can improve responsible self-medication in other groups. Strengthening current curricula to emphasize responsible OTC use and increasing students’ awareness of safe medication practices among other non-pharmacy students may help mitigate risks associated with improper OTC medicine use. Regulatory authorities should enhance supervision of OTC medicine access and promote public education campaigns on self-medication risks.
