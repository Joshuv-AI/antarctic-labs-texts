Beyond Fat Loss: Addressing the Sarcopenia Challenge of Incretin-Based Therapies
Authors: Ji Yoon Kim, Nam Hoon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Incretin-based therapies have transformed the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity by producing a weight-loss magnitude that was previously achievable only through intensive caloric restriction or bariatric surgery. This therapeutic shift has moved the clinical focus from the quantity of weight loss to the quality of weight loss. Lean mass reduction is frequently observed during weight loss induced by glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide/GLP-1RAs, or emerging multi-agonists. However, loss of lean body mass does not necessarily indicate equivalent loss of contractile skeletal muscle or sarcopenia. Incretin-based therapy is generally associated with preferential fat mass reduction, relative preservation of lean mass, and potential improvement in muscle quality through reduced myosteatosis. Thus, the clinically relevant question is whether incretin-associated body-composition changes lead to deterioration in muscle strength or physical performance, an increased risk of falls or fractures, or impaired quality of life. Available evidence does not show obvious functional harm; however, long-term data remain limited, especially in people with reduced muscle reserve. A muscle-conscious approach that integrates body-composition assessment, functional monitoring, resistance training, nutritional optimization, and emerging pharmacologic muscle-preserving strategies is needed to achieve high-quality weight loss.

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

Highlights

• Incretin-based therapies reduce fat mass more than lean mass during weight loss.

• Lean mass loss is often ~25–40% of weight loss but is not equivalent to sarcopenia.

• Muscle quality may improve through reduced myosteatosis despite lean mass loss.

• Functional harm has not been consistently shown, but long-term data are limited.

• Resistance training, nutrition, and emerging drugs may help preserve muscle health

INTRODUCTION

The rapid expansion of incretin-based therapies has changed the therapeutic landscape of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and obesity. Glucose-lowering therapies are no longer judged only by their glycemic efficacy. Contemporary glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/GLP-1RA tirzepatide, and emerging multi-agonists can produce substantial reductions in body weight while improving glycemic control and cardiometabolic risk [1-8]. In many patients with T2DM and obesity, these therapies now function as metabolic diseasemodifying agents rather than as conventional antihyperglycemic drugs.

This success has created a new clinical challenge. If pharmacologic treatment achieves 10% to 25% body weight reduction, the absolute amount of weight lost is no longer the only meaningful outcome. The composition and functional consequence of the weight loss may become central. Loss of fat mass, particularly visceral and ectopic fat, is metabolically desirable. In contrast, disproportionate loss of skeletal muscle mass, deterioration in muscle strength, or decline in physical performance could offset part of the clinical benefit, particularly in older adults and in patients with T2DM who already have impaired muscle quality [9,10].

Therefore, the starting question of this review is fundamental: is pharmacological weight loss always beneficial in people with obesity or T2DM? The answer depends on whether weight loss improves metabolic health while preserving functional reserve. Decreasing body weight with less visceral fat, better glycemic control, lower inflammatory burden, and preserved mobility represents high-quality weight loss. On the contrary, decreasing body weight with weakness, falls, reduced walking speed, and frailty does not. This distinction is reflected in the emerging concern that incretin-based therapies may aggravate sarcopenia or sarcopenic obesity. However, this concern requires careful interpretation, because lean mass reduction alone is insufficient to define clinically meaningful muscle loss or sarcopenia. Sarcopenia is not defined by body composition alone. Contemporary frameworks emphasize muscle strength and physical performance [11]; the Sarcopenia Definition and Outcomes Consortium (SDOC) specifically highlighted grip strength and usual gait speed as clinically relevant predictors of adverse health outcomes [12].

This review first clarifies how muscle change should be evaluated and interpreted by integrating body-composition measures with muscle strength, muscle quality, and physical performance. Then we summarize body-composition changes during incretin-based therapy, discuss why lean mass loss is frequently associated with substantial weight reduction, examine whether these changes translate into clinical harm, and review strategies to preserve muscle health during treatment. The purpose of this review is to define an approach for weight reduction during incretin-based therapy that is metabolically effective, functionally safe, and muscle-conscious.

DEFINING AND EVALUATING MUSCLE CHANGE

Evaluation of muscle changes during incretin-based therapy should begin by clarifying the body-composition terminology used to describe weight-loss composition (Fig. 1). At the simplest two-compartment level, body weight is divided into fat mass and fat-free mass [13]. Fat-free mass can be further divided into bone mineral content and lean soft tissue, and lean soft tissue includes skeletal muscle, organs, connective tissue, extracellular and intracellular water, and other non-fat, nonbone components. Thus, skeletal muscle mass represents only one component of the lean soft tissue, and appendicular lean mass is commonly used as a clinical proxy for limb skeletal muscle. This hierarchy is important because a decrease in lean mass should not be interpreted automatically as equivalent to loss of contractile skeletal muscle or sarcopenia [14].

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is the most widely used body-composition method in incretin-based therapy clinical trials. In DXA terminology, the body is usually partitioned into fat mass, bone mineral content, and lean soft tissue mass [15]. Lean mass in DXA-based reports generally refers to lean soft tissue, whereas fat-free mass includes all non-fat components, which comprise lean soft tissue and bone mineral content. Because DXA-derived lean-tissue mass includes not only skeletal muscle but also organ mass, connective tissue, body water, glycogen-associated water, and other non-fat components, a reduction in lean mass does not provide a one-toone estimate of skeletal muscle mass loss [16]. During active weight loss, DXA-derived lean mass reduction may overestimate clinically relevant loss of contractile skeletal muscle because changes in hydration, glycogen stores, extracellular fluid, and organ size can contribute to the measured decrease. Conversely, DXA may underestimate adverse muscle remodeling because it cannot directly assess muscle fat infiltration, muscle density, or muscle function. Therefore, lean mass reduction following incretin-based therapy should be interpreted cautiously, with the recognition that skeletal muscle mass generally accounts for only approximately 45% to 60% of DXA-derived lean mass, given that this proportion may vary according to age, sex, adiposity, hydration status, and body-composition method [17].

Nevertheless, DXA remains difficult to replace in clinical trial settings and, to some extent, in clinical practice. It is a relatively accessible, low-radiation modality capable of providing regional estimates such as appendicular lean mass [18]. Thus, the practical issue is not whether DXA should be abandoned or replaced, but how DXA-derived lean mass should be precisely interpreted.

Other techniques provide complementary information. Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) is simple and inexpensive; however, its assumptions are highly sensitive to hydration and may be less reliable during active weight loss [19]. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can quantify regional muscle area or volume and characterize muscle fat infiltration [20], but they are more costly and less feasible for routine clinical monitoring. Therefore, these methods are particularly valuable for evaluation of muscle quality.

For clinical interpretation, muscle change should be assessed across three linked domains: muscle mass, muscle strength, and muscle quality. Muscle mass represents the quantity of skeletal muscle or appendicular lean tissue. Muscle strength represents force-generating capacity and is commonly assessed using handgrip strength or chair-stand tests [21]. Muscle quality refers to the functional and structural characteristics of the muscle, including strength per unit muscle mass, intramuscular fat infiltration, muscle density, and MRI- or CT-derived adverse muscle composition [22]. These domains can move in different directions during weight loss; therefore, interpreting the changes in muscle mass alone, without concomitant assessment of muscle strength or quality, may be incomplete from the perspective of muscle health.

This distinction is central to sarcopenia diagnosis. The European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP2) recommends low muscle strength as the primary parameter of probable sarcopenia, low muscle quantity or quality to confirm diagnosis, and low physical performance to identify severe sarcopenia [21]. The Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 consensus requires low muscle mass together with low muscle strength and/or low physical performance, while also defining possible sarcopenia for community settings [23]. More recently, the AWGS 2025 consensus simplified the diagnosis of sarcopenia to concurrent low muscle mass and low muscle strength, regarded physical performance as an outcome measure rather than a diagnostic criterion, and introduced specific diagnostic cutoffs for adults aged 50 to 64 years [24]. The SDOC places particular emphasis on low grip strength and slow gait speed because of their associations with adverse outcomes and does not include DXA-derived lean mass as a core component [12]. The European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN)/European Association for the Study of Obesity (EASO) consensus for sarcopenic obesity uses a two-step diagnostic approach that combines reduced muscle function with excess adiposity and low skeletal muscle mass [25]. These consensus criteria differ in diagnostic sequence, measurement methods, and cutoff values (Table 1).

Application of these criteria to incretin-based trials requires caution. Most pivotal GLP-1RA and dual-agonist trials enrolled middle-aged adults, often with a mean age below 65 years, and were not designed to diagnose sarcopenia. They generally measured total lean mass or patient-reported function rather than the full combination of appendicular muscle mass, objective strength, and physical performance required by consensus definitions. Fixed geriatric cutoffs may also have different sensitivity and specificity in younger adults with obesity or T2DM, in whom high absolute muscle mass can coexist with poor muscle quality or low muscle mass relative to body size. Accordingly, the term sarcopenia should be reserved for participants who meet a prespecified definition incorporating muscle mass, strength, and/or physical performance; an isolated reduction in DXA-derived lean mass should instead be described as lean mass reduction. Among the studies reviewed, SEMALEAN was one of the few to report sarcopenic obesity according to ESPEN/EASO criteria, whereas most pivotal incretin trials did not formally assess or adjudicate sarcopenia [25,26].

BODY-COMPOSITION CHANGES DURING INCRETIN-BASED THERAPY: WHAT IS KNOWN

Representative clinical studies examining body-composition and muscle-related changes during incretin-based therapy are summarized in Table 2 [26-40]. These studies collectively show that reductions in lean mass are observed among GLP-1RAs, dual GLP-1/GIP receptor agonism, and emerging multi-agonist therapies, although the magnitude of reduction and interpretation of this change vary according to the drug potency, treatment duration, population, and measurement method. A systematic review and network meta-analysis of 22 randomized controlled trials involving GLP-1RAs and GLP-1/GIP receptor co-agonists reported significant reductions in total body weight, fat mass, and lean mass [41]. The mean reduction in total body weight, fat mass, and lean mass was 3.55, 2.95, and 0.86 kg, respectively. Lean mass loss accounted for approximately one-quarter of total weight loss, while relative lean mass, expressed as the proportion of lean mass within the total body weight, was largely preserved. This finding supports interpreting incretin-associated lean mass loss as one component of weight reduction rather than as isolated skeletal muscle wasting, although proportionality alone does not establish that the loss is physiologically appropriate or functionally neutral.

However, with newer and more potent incretin-based therapies, the proportional contribution of lean mass loss to total weight loss appears to be somewhat greater in several analyses, particularly for high-dose semaglutide and triple agonists. Fig. 2 descriptively summarizes DXA-based findings from representative studies; however, differences among studies in population characteristics, treatment duration, co-interventions, analytical methods, and denominator definitions preclude direct comparisons among agents. A recent meta-analysis comparing incretin-based therapy with lifestyle interventions reported that lean mass loss constituted 26.8% of weight loss with liraglutide, 35.2% with semaglutide, and 25.4% with tirzepatide, whereas lifestyle interventions showed a broadly comparable proportion of 26.2% and lifestyle plus resistance training showed a lower proportion of 17.5% [42].

A key semaglutide study addressed this point. In Semaglutide Treatment Effect in People with Obesity (STEP) 1 trial, once-weekly semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced body weight by 14.9% at 68 weeks, compared with 2.4% with placebo in adults with obesity [27]. In the DXA subpopulation (trial product estimand), semaglutide reduced total fat mass by 10.40 kg and regional visceral fat mass by 0.47 kg, while total lean body mass decreased by 6.92 kg. Although the proportion of lean body mass relative to total body mass increased by 3.61 percentage points, the absolute decline in lean body mass was substantial. Lean mass loss accounted for approximately 40% of DXA-derived tissue loss in this trial.

Body-composition evidence from T2DM populations is particularly limited but clinically important, because T2DM is frequently accompanied by impaired muscle quality, insulin resistance, and reduced functional reserve. In the Semaglutide Unabated Sustainability in Treatment of Type 2 Diabetes (SUSTAIN) 8 DXA substudy of 178 participants, semaglutide 1.0 mg reduced total fat mass by 3.4 kg and total lean mass by 2.3 kg over 52 weeks [28]. In a post hoc calculation based on DXAderived measures, lean mass loss represented approximately 40% of the measured total lean plus fat mass loss. This does not necessarily imply harmful muscle wasting, because the proportion of lean mass increased by 1.2 percentage points. Nonetheless, it reinforces that absolute lean mass decline may be clinically apparent even when relative body composition improves.

Tirzepatide provides additional insight into body-composition changes during larger-magnitude pharmacologic weight loss. In SURMOUNT-1, tirzepatide produced dose-dependent mean body weight reductions of 15.0%, 19.5%, and 20.9% with 5, 10, and 15 mg, respectively, over 72 weeks in adults with obesity or overweight without diabetes [29]. In the DXA substudy, tirzepatide (pooled doses) induced weight loss that was fat-dominant: approximately 74% was fat mass and approximately 26% was lean mass. The fat mass was reduced by 15.9 kg (33.9%) and lean mass by 5.6 kg (10.9%) from baseline [30]. The relative contribution of lean mass to total weight loss was not substantially different from that observed with placebo, suggesting that the larger absolute reduction in lean mass seen with tirzepatide reflected the greater magnitude of overall weight loss rather than disproportionate lean-tissue loss.

Retatrutide, a GIP, GLP-1, and glucagon receptor agonist, provided early evidence for next-generation multi-agonist therapy in T2DM [43]. In a phase 2 body-composition substudy, total fat mass decreased substantially over 36 weeks, particularly with the higher doses [31]. In the 8 mg and 12 mg retatrutide groups, total fat mass decreased by approximately 10.9 kg (26.1%) and 10.5 kg (23.2%), respectively, while lean mass decreased by about 6.5 and 6.4 kg, respectively. Contemporary interpretation of these data indicated that lean mass accounted for approximately 38% of the total weight loss. This value is above the conventional one-quarter estimate and close to the upper range reported for semaglutide, suggesting that highly potent incretin or multi-agonist therapies may generate larger reductions in the absolute as well as proportional lean mass.

Recent muscle-preservation trials also provided useful DXA data from their incretin control arms. In the BELIEVE study, semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced fat mass by approximately 9.6 kg and lean mass by 3.9 kg, and in the EMBRAZE study, tirzepatide plus placebo reduced fat mass by approximately 8.0 kg and lean mass by 3.5 kg [32,33]. These control-arm data suggested lean mass fractions of approximately 30%, broadly consistent with a fat-dominant pattern of weight loss.

Taken together, these studies support a few conclusions. First, incretin-based therapies consistently reduce body weight, fat mass, and visceral adiposity, while absolute lean mass also decreases as a relatively smaller but measurable component of overall weight loss. Second, the relative contribution of lean mass to total weight loss is variable and may be more substantial with some of the newer incretin-based therapies. Third, the observed changes occur over approximately 24 to 72 weeks, far more rapidly than the gradual muscle loss associated with physiological aging. Early DXA changes may partly reflect alterations in water, glycogen, extracellular fluid, and organ mass; nevertheless, clinically relevant loss of actual skeletal muscle cannot be excluded. Finally, proportionality to total weight or tissue loss alone does not establish that lean mass reduction is appropriate, harmless, or functionally neutral. Interpretation should therefore consider the rate and absolute magnitude of loss, baseline muscle reserve, objective strength and performance, and the limitations of cross-trial comparisons.

MUSCLE QUALITY DURING INCRETINBASED THERAPY

Muscle quality may be particularly relevant in people with T2DM, because T2DM is characterized by multiple pathophysiologic features that may impair muscle quality, including insulin resistance, mitochondrial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and increased intramuscular adipose tissue [44]. Thus, patients with T2DM may have impaired muscle function despite preserved or even increased absolute muscle mass [45,46]. In this setting, reduction in myosteatosis or muscle fat infiltration may represent a favorable change in muscle quality even if the muscle volume decreases [36,40].

The available MRI-based incretin studies support the distinction between changes in muscle quantity and muscle quality. In the SURPASS-3 MRI analysis of people with T2DM and high fatty liver index, tirzepatide reduced thigh muscle volume by 0.64 L and muscle-volume Z score by 0.22 standard deviation, but also reduced thigh muscle fat infiltration by 0.36 percentage points [36]. The investigators interpreted overall muscle-volume reduction as proportional to the expected changes in body composition and the degree of weight loss achieved, whereas the reduction in muscle fat infiltration suggested potentially favorable remodeling of muscle composition.

Similar observations have been reported with liraglutide. In a randomized clinical trial of overweight or obese adults without diabetes, MRI-based assessment showed that liraglutide reduced thigh muscle fat compared with placebo. Over a median follow-up of 36 weeks, the mean percent change in thigh muscle fat was −2.87% with liraglutide and 0.05% with placebo. The proportion of participants with adverse muscle composition also decreased from 11.0% to 8.2% with liraglutide [40,47].

Collectively, these findings suggest that incretin-based therapy may improve muscle quality, particularly through reductions in myosteatosis, despite concomitant reductions in muscle quantity or lean mass [36,40]. However, few studies have simultaneously assessed body composition, muscle quality, and functional outcomes. Therefore, current evidence is insufficient to determine whether the improvement in muscle quality offsets the reduction in muscle quantity, or to define the relative contribution of each domain to physical function. Further studies integrating quantitative muscle assessment, imagingbased muscle quality, and objective functional outcomes are required.

PHYSIOLOGICAL BASIS OF LEAN MASS LOSS DURING WEIGHT REDUCTION

Lean mass loss during incretin-based therapy is understood as a dynamic, time-dependent process rather than a static consequence of treatment. During the early titration phase, usually over weeks to months, GLP-1 receptor activation suppresses appetite through central pathways and slows gastric emptying, and both effects reduce energy intake [48,49]. This negative energy balance does more than lower fat mass: it also depletes non-fat components such as glycogen-bound water, extracellular fluid, and gastrointestinal contents, alongside modest reductions in fat mass and lean soft tissue [14]. Simultaneously, lower energy and protein intake, together with reduced mechanical loading owing to loss of body mass, shift muscle protein turnover toward a net negative balance, driving the decline in DXA-derived lean mass. In the acute, large-deficit phase this reflects blunted postprandial muscle protein synthesis (MPS); but as caloric restriction continues, accelerated proteolysis becomes the predominant driver, while MPS itself often recovers [50-52].

As caloric restriction continues and weight loss enters the sustained treatment phase, the biological drivers of body-composition changes shift. Lipolysis and the mobilization of endogenous triglyceride stores become dominant, producing preferential loss of visceral and ectopic adipose depots [9]. The reduction in harmful adiposity may attenuate adipose tissue macrophage infiltration and chronic low-grade inflammation [53]. Improved insulin sensitivity may support skeletal muscle metabolism through more favorable substrate delivery and trafficking and by reducing the lipotoxic effects associated with myosteatosis [9,44,51]. Muscle quality may therefore improve even when the absolute muscle quantity declines.

The transition from the active phase of weight loss to the maintenance phase is defined by physiological adaptation. Energy intake often recovers partially, driven by counter-regulatory appetite signals and behavioral adaptation; the central appetite-suppressing effect may remain comparatively durable even as the drug’s effect on gastric emptying diminishes because of tachyphylaxis [48,49]. In parallel, adaptive thermogenesis, encompassing a lower resting metabolic rate, reduced triiodothyronine levels, and related homeostatic responses, slows the rate of further weight loss [54]. As a new energy equilibrium is achieved, total body weight and fat mass tend to plateau, and muscle mass may also stabilize, particularly when protein intake and resistance training are adequate [10]. At this stage, the clinical focus shifts from the initial decline in lean mass to whether the patient retains sufficient muscle reserve to support functionality at the new, lower body weight.

Actual skeletal muscle loss could also have metabolic and long-term implications beyond immediate strength. Skeletal muscle contributes to resting energy expenditure, is a major site of insulin-mediated glucose disposal, and supports spontaneous and structured physical activity [14,51]. Because highmetabolic-rate organs contribute disproportionately to resting energy expenditure, modest muscle loss is unlikely to explain adaptive thermogenesis by itself; nevertheless, excessive skeletal muscle loss could lower energy requirements and reduce activity capacity, thereby complicating long-term weight maintenance [14,54]. Weight regain after withdrawal of incretin therapy is well documented [55], but the composition of regained weight and whether prior lean mass loss independently predicts fat-predominant regain have not been adequately characterized. This biologically plausible concern remains an important research gap.

These temporal dynamics closely resemble those of established, non-incretin weight-loss modalities. Very-low-calorie diets and intensive lifestyle modification clearly reduce skeletal muscle mass [56]; however, fat is lost preferentially, and lean tissue accounts on average for roughly one-quarter of total weight loss, although this fraction varies according to baseline adiposity and the rate of loss [57]. Similarly, bariatric surgery produces profound, time-dependent reductions in lean mass, fat-free mass, and absolute skeletal muscle mass, with the most drastic changes occurring during the first postoperative year [58].

These comparative data suggest that lean mass decline during incretin-based therapy is usually part of the physiological response to the substantial negative energy balance and smaller body size. Therefore, it should not be interpreted automatically as a unique, drug-specific effect. Nonetheless, the same degree of lean mass loss may have different clinical implications across patients [59].

FUNCTIONAL CONSEQUENCES OF LEAN MASS LOSS DURING INCRETIN-BASED THERAPY

Taken together, the available evidence suggests that incretinbased therapy creates a bidirectional change in terms of muscle health. On the one hand, the treatment is accompanied by absolute reduction in muscle mass or lean mass. On the other hand, muscle mass is relatively preserved compared with fat mass, and muscle quality may improve through reductions in myosteatosis and ectopic fat. These are, however, intermediate phenotypic changes. The more important clinical question is whether these changes translate into outcomes that may affect patients’ health, including muscle strength, physical function, frailty, falls, fractures, disability, and quality of life.

Direct clinical evidence linking incretin therapy-induced lean mass loss to adverse outcomes remains limited. Most pivotal trials and body-composition substudies were not designed to detect sarcopenia, frailty progression, or disability. Moreover, patient-reported physical function, objective muscle strength, objective physical performance, and exercise capacity represent distinct constructs and should not be interpreted interchangeably. Representative studies are therefore categorized by outcome domain in Table 3 [26,27,29,32,33,55,60-66].

In STEP 1, semaglutide 2.4 mg improved patient-reported physical function, assessed using the generic Short Form-36 version 2 (SF-36v2) Physical Functioning domain, which evaluates limitations in daily physical activities; and the obesityspecific Impact of Weight on Quality of Life-Lite Clinical Trials Version (IWQOL-Lite-CT) Physical Function composite, which captures weight-related limitations such as bending, standing, breathlessness, physical inactivity, and walking capacity in overweight or obese adults [27]. Also in the STEPheart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) trial, semaglutide treatment significantly improved health status, reduced physical limitations, and increased the 6-minute walk distance in patients with obesity-related heart failure with preserved ejection fraction [60]. Similarly, in SURMOUNT-2, tirzepatide showed improvements in weight-related quality of life and physical functioning, with larger gains in the higher dose [61]. A recent translational study also reported that GLP-1 medicines did not cause disproportionate loss of muscle mass or function in obese mice and humans [67].

Objective muscle-function data are more limited, but available findings remain generally reassuring. In the SEMALEAN prospective observational study, semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced body weight and fat mass over 12 months, while lean mass declined early and then stabilized [26]. Importantly, handgrip strength increased by approximately 4.5 kg, and the prevalence of sarcopenic obesity decreased from 49% to 33%.

Evidence from other methods of substantial weight loss also supports the concept that loss of lean mass does not necessarily imply functional deterioration. In a meta-analysis of bariatric metabolic surgery including three randomized trials and seven prospective cohort studies, bariatric surgery reduced lean mass by 7.4 kg and fat mass by 22.3 kg; however, handgrip strength did not change significantly (mean difference, −0.46 kg; 95% confidence interval, −1.76 to 0.84 kg) [68]. Similarly, in the Type of Energy Manipulation for Promoting Optimum Metabolic Health and Body Composition in Obesity (TEMPO) Diet randomized clinical trial, severe energy restriction produced greater weight loss, greater fat loss, and greater absolute loss of whole-body lean mass and thigh muscle area compared with moderate energy restriction [69]. However, these leantissue losses were proportional to total weight loss, while handgrip strength did not differ between groups.

Overall, available studies have not demonstrated consistent deterioration in functional outcomes and frequently report improvement in patient-reported physical function or exercise capacity. However, these findings do not establish that lean mass reduction is functionally harmless. Improvements in patient-reported function or walking capacity may largely reflect overall weight loss and reduced mechanical loading, driven mainly by preferential fat mass reduction. Few studies have di-rectly assessed muscle strength or performance using measures central to contemporary sarcopenia definitions, such as handgrip strength, chair-stand test, and usual gait speed, and direct long-term data remain limited.

Fracture and bone mineral density outcomes represent a related but distinct clinical outcome area. Weight loss can reduce bone mineral density by lowering mechanical loading and altering bone turnover [70,71], which is particularly relevant when weight loss is rapid or accompanied by reductions in lean mass. Nevertheless, available data did not show consistent evidence. In the TEMPO Diet trial, severe energy restriction caused greater loss in total hip bone mineral density compared to moderate energy restriction [69]. A systematic review on skeletal health in T2DM found no consistent evidence that incretin-based therapy increases fracture risk, although the data were heterogeneous and considered insufficient to draw firm conclusions [72]. In a retrospective study of 255 semaglutide or tirzepatide users with increased fracture risk, use of these agents was associated with a decline in total hip bone mineral density, similar in magnitude to matched controls overall. However, among patients without diabetes, hip bone loss was significantly greater with GLP-1RA use than with controls [73]. A randomized trial of liraglutide, exercise, or their combination after diet-induced weight loss further confirmed that exercise is important for skeletal protection. Combination therapy preserved hip, spine, and forearm bone mineral density, whereas liraglutide alone reduced hip and spine bone mineral density compared with exercise alone despite similar weight loss [74].

Overall, it remains difficult to definitively link reductions in muscle mass or lean mass induced by incretin-based therapy to clinically meaningful adverse outcomes. In general, available studies suggest that physical functioning may improve in patients with obesity receiving these agents, whereas effects on bone health appear neutral or potentially harmful. Data on long-term functional outcomes with newer, more potent incretin-based therapies remain insufficient. An interesting perspective is provided by recent trials of pharmacologic musclepreserving strategies. In both BELIEVE and EMBRAZE, bimagrumab and apitegromab, respectively, attenuated lean mass loss significantly during incretin-based weight reduction [32,33]. However, these effects did not translate into consistent improvements in functional measures such as handgrip strength or chair sit-to-stand testing. Therefore, this finding is conceptually important. While lean mass loss does not directly indicate loss of muscle function, preservation of lean mass does not necessarily guarantee preservation or improvement of muscle function. Whether this reflects the relatively short duration of these studies, an insufficient magnitude of lean mass difference to influence functional outcomes, or a true partial dissociation between lean mass and muscle function remains uncertain. Future studies should therefore evaluate whether lean mass preservation leads to meaningful improvements in muscle strength, physical performance, or long-term musculoskeletal consequences.

MUSCLE-PRESERVING STRATEGIES

Resistance training, nutrition, and dose-escalation

Data from previous studies suggest that incretin-based therapy is accompanied by variable reductions in lean mass or muscle mass, which do not necessarily translate into impaired functional outcomes. However, it is also evident that newer and more potent incretin-based therapies can induce absolute reductions in lean mass that are non-negligible and cannot be regarded as uniformly favorable from a muscle-health perspective. Accordingly, several muscle-preserving strategies have been proposed to improve the quality of weight loss during incretin-based therapy.

Nonpharmacologic muscle-preserving strategies remain the foundation of care because they directly address the mechanisms through which lean mass declines during weight loss. Caloric restriction and pharmacologic appetite suppression reduce energy availability, may lower protein intake, and decrease mechanical loading as the body weight falls [52]. Resistance training counteracts these effects: it increases mechanical loading, stimulates MPS, improves neuromuscular recruitment, and can increase muscle strength even when total body weight is decreasing [75]. A systematic review and meta-analysis in older adults with obesity showed that combining resistance training with caloric restriction attenuates the loss of lean mass induced by dieting alone while preserving the reduction in fat mass and body weight [76]. Therefore, incorporating resistance training as a key component of incretin-based therapy should be prioritized, provided it is clinically feasible, rather than being simply treated as optional lifestyle advice.

A practically applied program should include progressive resistance exercise of the major muscle groups two to three times per week, combined with aerobic activity and balance training when appropriate [77]. The goal of treatment should be multidimensional, extending beyond preservation of lean mass, thus encompassing maintenance or enhancement of exercise capacity, muscular strength and endurance, and cardiorespiratory fitness [78]. The trial by Lundgren et al. [79] provides a useful clinical model: after diet-induced weight loss, the combination of exercise and liraglutide improved weight-loss maintenance and reduced fat mass more effectively than either strategy alone, while supporting better functional and metabolic adaptation [66].

Nutritional management may help prevent protein and energy undernutrition that can arise during appetite suppression [80,81]. Patients receiving GLP-1RAs or dual agonists frequently report early satiety, nausea, altered food preference, or reduced meal size [82,83]. These changes may improve caloric balance but can also reduce protein intake. For many adults undergoing active weight loss, a protein intake of approximately 1.0 to 1.2 g/kg/day, calculated using adjusted or ideal body weight, is a reasonable practical target [80,82], with higher targets being considered in selected older adults if kidney function and clinical status permit. Protein intake should be distributed across meals, and leucine-rich protein sources may be useful if the total intake is low [80,82]. In patients with chronic kidney disease, protein targets should be individualized according to kidney function, albuminuria, nutritional status, and with nephrology guidance [84].

Dose escalation can be another potentially modifiable component of muscle-conscious incretin-based therapy. The optimal dose is not necessarily the highest dose for every patient. In older patients with T2DM, low baseline strength, or slow gait, slower titration, closer follow-up, or maintenance at a tolerable submaximal dose may be considered as a reasonable expert-opinion-based approach. However, direct evidence that slower titration preserves muscle mass or function is largely lacking. The retatrutide DXA substudy showed smaller absolute and proportional reductions in lean mass in lower-dose groups than in higher-dose groups; lean mass accounted for approximately 16% and 30% of weight loss in the 0.5- and 4-mg groups, respectively, compared with approximately 37% and 38% in the 8- and 12-mg groups [31]. These dose-related findings are hypothesis-generating and do not establish that submaximal dosing preserves muscle function or maintains equivalent long-term efficacy.

Pharmacologic muscle-preserving strategies

Pharmacologic muscle-preserving strategies are advancing rapidly from concept to early clinical testing [85]. Inhibition of myostatin and activin signaling represents the leading therapeutic approach, particularly through pathways involving growth differentiation factor 8 (GDF8; or myostatin), activin A, and activin type II receptors. This strategy is biologically plausible because myostatin and activin A are negative regulators of skeletal muscle growth; therefore, blockade of these pathways can shift the response to weight loss toward greater fat loss and better lean mass preservation [86]. Preclinical work has shown that combined blockade of GDF8 and activin A can prevent or reverse GLP-1–induced muscle loss in obese mice and non-human primates while enhancing fat loss and improving metabolic measures [87]. These findings support the concept that lean mass loss during incretin-induced weight reduction is a modifiable component of the weight-loss response and not a fixed biological effect.

Bimagrumab is the most clinically developed example of this approach. It is a monoclonal antibody targeting activin type II receptors, with effects on both skeletal muscle and adipose tissue [88]. In a 48-week phase 2 trial in adults with T2DM and obesity, bimagrumab reduced total body fat mass, visceral adipose tissue, and intrahepatic fat while increasing lean mass and improving glycated hemoglobin [89]. This study finding is important because it shows that fat mass reduction and lean mass preservation, or even lean mass gain, can be dissociated pharmacologically in a population directly relevant to T2DM.

The more recent BELIEVE phase 2 trial extended this concept by testing bimagrumab, semaglutide, and their combination in adults with obesity [32]. At week 72, semaglutide 2.4 mg reduced body weight by 15.7% and fat mass by 27.8%, whereas bimagrumab 30 mg/kg reduced body weight by 10.8% and fat mass by 28.5%. The high-dose combination produced greater body weight reduction of 22.1% and fat mass reduction of 45.7%, while predominantly preserving lean mass compared with semaglutide alone. These data suggest that combining appetite-centered incretin therapy with tissue-directed activin receptor blockade may improve the quality of weight loss. However, bimagrumab treatment was associated with higher treatment discontinuation due to increased adverse events in bimagrumab-containing groups than those in semaglutideonly or placebo groups, and long-term functional outcomes remain insufficiently defined.

Apitegromab represents a more selective anti-myostatin strategy. It is an investigational monoclonal antibody that selectively inhibits activation of myostatin. In the randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 EMBRAZE trial, 102 adults who were overweight or obese received tirzepatide plus apitegromab or tirzepatide plus placebo for 24 weeks [33]. Total body weight loss was similar between groups, but apitegromab preserved 1.9 kg of lean mass compared with placebo. Lean mass loss was 1.6 kg with apitegromab plus tirzepatide versus 3.5 kg with tirzepatide plus placebo, corresponding to 54.9% relative retention of lean mass. In addition, lean mass accounted for 14.6% of total weight loss with apitegromab versus 30.2% with placebo. The rate of adverse events was generally similar between groups. Although this was a small proofof-concept trial and relied on DXA-derived lean mass rather than long-term clinical outcomes, it provided direct human evidence that selective myostatin inhibition can preserve lean mass during tirzepatide-induced weight loss.

Additional approaches are being explored. The phase 2 COURAGE program evaluated semaglutide combined with trevogrumab, an anti-GDF8/anti-myostatin antibody, with or without garetosmab, an anti-activin A antibody. Conference-reported complete 26-week data suggested that 33% of weight loss with semaglutide monotherapy was attributable to lean mass loss. Addition of trevogrumab reduced the lean mass fraction to approximately 17% to 18%, while the triplet combination reduced it further to approximately 7% while increasing the proportion of fat mass loss (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06299098) [90]. These results are hypothesis-generating because they have not yet been fully validated in peer-reviewed outcome studies. Moreover, tolerability concerns were raised with the triplet regimen. Nevertheless, they reinforce the emerging therapeutic concept that the composition of incretin-induced weight loss can potentially be modified pharmacologically.

Although these clinical findings are promising in terms of lean mass preservation, pharmacologic muscle-preserving therapy should not yet be considered standard care. Current studies have not shown consistent improvements in muscle strength or functional outcomes, and long-term durability remains unknown. In addition, careful safety evaluation is required because myostatin and activin pathways are involved in multiple tissues and physiological processes. Therefore, further studies are needed before these agents can be integrated into routine care.

CONCLUSIONS

As incretin-based therapy makes substantial weight loss achievable, the clinical focus has shifted from the quantity of weight lost to the quality of weight lost. Incretin-based therapy commonly reduces DXA-derived lean mass, while fat mass is usually reduced to a greater extent. These changes may reflect a combination of physiological adaptation to negative energy balance and smaller body size, changes in hydration and glycogen, and actual tissue loss. Proportionality to total weight loss alone does not establish that the reduction is benign or clinically unimportant.

Available imaging data suggest that incretin-based therapy may reduce muscle fat infiltration and improve adverse muscle composition despite reductions in muscle volume or lean mass [36,40]. Current trials have not demonstrated consistent deterioration in muscle strength, physical function, or exercise capacity, but the available functional evidence is limited, heterogeneous, and predominantly indirect. Thus, current evidence does not establish that incretin-based therapy causes sarcopenia, but neither does it exclude clinically important skeletal muscle loss in susceptible individuals. Considering the broad metabolic, renal, hepatic, and cardiovascular benefits of these agents [91-94], concern about lean mass should be contextualized rather than dismissed. Most evidence comes from middle-aged adults with obesity and relatively preserved muscle reserve. As use expands to older adults, non-obese people, patients with long-standing T2DM, and individuals with low baseline muscle reserve, the generalizability of current findings remains uncertain. Pharmacologic muscle-preserving strategies seem promising, but their functional benefit, long-term safety, delivery route, cost, and appropriate target population remain unresolved.

Future research should clearly define when the rate or magnitude of lean mass loss becomes clinically meaningful, identify vulnerable phenotypes, determine whether lean mass loss independently affects resting energy expenditure, glucose disposal, physical activity, and weight maintenance, and establish whether muscle-preserving interventions improve strength, performance, and long-term patient-centered outcomes.
