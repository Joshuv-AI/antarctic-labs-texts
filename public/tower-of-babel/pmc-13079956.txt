Unlocking the Potential of Health Technology Assessment (HTA) in Indonesian Midwifery: Challenges and Opportunities
Authors: Qorinah Estiningtyas Sakilah Adnani, Intan Gumilang Pratiwi, Ari Indra Susanti, Bibek Giri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHealth Technology Assessment (HTA) supports evidence‐based decision‐making, resource allocation, and healthcare quality. Although Indonesia has established national HTA policies, their application in midwifery remains limited. Given ongoing efforts to reduce maternal mortality and advance health transformation, integrating HTA into midwifery is essential for safe, effective, and cost‐efficient care.AimsThis paper critically examines HTA's potential in Indonesian midwifery, identifying implementation gaps, challenges, and opportunities to improve maternal and child health outcomes.MethodsThis study employed a narrative review and policy analysis, examining national health policies, HTA guidelines, institutional frameworks, and relevant scientific literature on HTA and midwifery in Indonesia. The analysis focused on identifying structural barriers, implementation challenges, and strategic opportunities for integrating HTA into community‐based midwifery services.ResultsThe analysis revealed that Indonesia possesses a foundational HTA policy framework supported by national health transformation strategies. However, a significant implementation gap exists between national HTA policies and frontline midwifery practice. HTA activities primarily focus on high‐cost hospital technologies, while routine midwifery tools and digital health applications are often adopted without systematic evaluation. Key challenges include limited data availability, decentralized procurement systems, insufficient local HTA capacity, funding constraints, and inadequate knowledge translation mechanisms. Opportunities include strengthened stakeholder collaboration, digital health integration, capacity building for midwives, and development of standardized evaluation mechanisms tailored to midwifery technologies.ConclusionIntegrating HTA into Indonesian midwifery can enhance evidence‐based practice, optimize resource allocation, and improve maternal and child healthcare quality. Addressing policy‐practice gaps through capacity development, institutional coordination, and investment in data systems is essential. Strategic HTA implementation can support Indonesia's health transformation agenda and contribute to improved maternal and newborn health outcomes.

Introduction

The term “Health Technology Assessment” (HTA) refers to a rigorous and systematic approach to evaluating the impact of health technology on patient outcomes and healthcare systems [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines HTA as ‘the systematic evaluation of properties, effects and impacts of health technologies and interventions [2]. It covers both the direct, intended consequences of technologies and interventions and their indirect, unintended consequences. It entails detailed assessments of therapies' clinical effectiveness and their economic, social, and ethical implications [3]. The goal of HTA is to provide information to decision‐makers so they can support a high‐quality, affordable, and equitable healthcare system. The approach informs policy and healthcare decision‐making, especially when allocating limited funds to health interventions and technologies [4].

The ever‐changing landscape environment of healthcare technologies has opened new avenues for mother and child health outcomes. These improvements have resulted in the development of various health technologies and interventions to improve the quality of care. However, the efficacy, safety, and cost‐effectiveness of these technologies must be thoroughly evaluated to ensure their optimal and evidence‐based incorporation into midwifery practice. This need is underscored by Indonesia's ongoing efforts to reduce its maternal mortality ratio (MMR), which, despite significant progress, was reported at 189 deaths per 100,000 live births (2019) and remains a key national priority [5].

In 2022, the Ministry of Health launched the Six Pillars of Health Transformation, a strategic framework aligned with the national vision for a healthy and productive society. The explicit inclusion of “Transformation of Health Technology” as one pillar creates a direct policy mandate for systematic technology evaluation [6]. Supporting this, the Ministry has issued official HTA Guidelines to standardize evaluations and ensure resource allocation is guided by evidence of value and impact [7]. Furthermore, the commitment to integrating health data based on the life cycle across all facilities highlights the growing infrastructure necessary to inform such assessments [8]. Given Indonesia's large and diverse population, prioritizing access to high‐quality maternal and child health services is imperative. HTA is therefore crucial as the formal mechanism to guide decisions about adopting, funding, and scaling health technologies within midwifery practices, ensuring they contribute effectively to national health goals.

When applied to midwifery practices, the importance of HTA becomes clear. Midwives routinely engage with a wide array of technologies from screening tools and monitoring equipment to digital platforms for recording and reporting Maternal and Child Health (MCH) data. Systematically evaluating these technologies through the HTA framework allows midwives and policymakers to make informed decisions about their benefits, risks, and value. This is critical for improving service quality and safety. Moreover, in a rapidly evolving technological landscape, understanding the current strengths and limitations of HTA application in midwifery is essential for harnessing breakthroughs effectively. As we explore HTA in the Indonesian context, we recognize the midwife's pivotal role in safeguarding the health of future generations. HTA can empower midwives with a structured, critical‐thinking framework for clinical decision‐making, enhancing the entire continuum of care‐from accurate data recording based on a woman's life cycle to the implementation of evidence‐based interventions.

Thus, HTA can empower midwives, improve maternity and childcare services, and ultimately lead to healthier and thriving communities. Therefore, this paper aims to provide a critical perspective on the integration of HTA within the specific context of Indonesian midwifery. By analyzing existing national HTA frameworks and policies, such as the Ministry of Health's HTA guidelines and the role of the Health Technology Assessment Committee (HTAC), in relation to the realities of midwifery practice, we identify unique institutional barriers and opportunities. This analysis intends to inform locally relevant policy reforms, resource allocation, and capacity‐building strategies to improve maternal and newborn health outcomes.

The Strategic Value of Health Technology Evaluation (HTA) for Advancing Indonesian Midwifery

Health Technology Assessment (HTA) in midwifery ensures that pregnant mothers and newborns receive evidence‐based, safe, and cost‐effective healthcare services. HTA assists midwives and policymakers in making informed decisions by systematically analyzing health technologies and interventions used in midwifery, ultimately leading to improved maternal and child health outcomes. The importance of HTA in midwifery is multidimensional, involving various factors that contribute to overall healthcare quality and efficiency. The incorporation of HTA into midwifery practices encourages evidence‐based decision‐making grounded in factual data.

HTA should be conducted as evidence‐based health technology assessment (EB‐HTA) to address problems in clinical practice using an evidence‐based medicine (EBM) approach (Figure 1).

Problem Formulation based on PICO format.

Maternal and child health problems in clinical practice can be addressed using an EBM approach: (1) Formulate the problem into specific and answerable questions containing the four elements of the PICO format, consisting of: patient/population or MCH problem, intervention/indicator or index, comparison, and outcome/expected results; (2) Use the keywords in the clinical questions to search for valid scientific evidence on the internet; (3) Critically appraise the scientific evidence with regarding three aspects: validity, importance, and applicability (VIA); (4) If the results are good, then the evidence can be applied or recommended to improve the quality of maternal and child health.

Midwives can confidently use established efficacy and safety treatments by rigorously analyzing the clinical effectiveness of health technologies. Additionally, a technological tool that utilizes machine learning through an application or website with extensive big data from KIA (Kesehatan Ibu dan Anak, Indonesian term for Maternal and Child Health‐MCH) results can be used to predict diseases experienced by mothers and children. For example, machine learning can predict which pregnant women are at risk of delivering low‐birth‐weight babies (LBW) by analyzing the mother's health history from adolescence and her pregnancy history, enabling midwives to provide targeted care to prevent LBW.

This guarantees that expecting moms and newborns receive care consistent with the most recent scientific findings and best practices, lowering the likelihood of adverse events and problems. HTA assists policymakers in analyzing the cost‐effectiveness of health technologies used in midwifery, allowing them to allocate resources more efficiently. Healthcare systems can optimize their budgets and target funding to areas significantly impacting mother and child health by identifying initiatives that provide the best value for money. This is especially important in resource‐constrained countries where optimizing healthcare expenses is critical.

HTA has thus garnered policymakers' attention at various healthcare system levels. As a result, institutionalizing HTA has become necessary for achieving universal health coverage (UHC) goals [9]. UHC aims to provide adequate healthcare services to all community members, regardless of financial constraints, and the development and implementation of HTA can play a critical role in achieving this objective [10]. Many countries have already begun implementing HTA as a significant effort towards achieving their UHC goals [11]. Policymakers can leverage HTA to inform their decisions regarding health system priorities and identify interventions that benefit the health system most [12].

Improved Safety and Quality of Care: During the critical periods of pregnancy and childbirth, midwives are entrusted with the well‐being of pregnant women and infants. Integrating HTA into midwifery guarantees that the technologies and interventions are safe and meet quality standards. This lowers the likelihood of adverse events and medical errors, resulting in a safer healthcare environment for mothers and newborns.

HTA helps to promote incorporating emerging health technologies and interventions into midwifery practices. Midwives should embrace developments that have the potential to alter maternity and child healthcare delivery by examining the impact of novel technology such as telemedicine applications, digital health platforms, or point‐of‐care diagnostic gadgets.

Indonesian Addressing HTA Policy Landscape and the Midwifery Implementation Gap

The importance of HTA in guiding healthcare policies and resource allocation is recognized in Indonesia, aligning with the global understanding of HTA as a crucial tool for governance under fiscal constraints [13]. The country has established a foundational policy framework, beginning with the official HTA Guideline issued by the Ministry of Health in 2017 [7]. Institutionally, the Health Technology Assessment Committee (HTAC), operating under the National Agency of Drug and Food Control (NA‐DFC), serves as the primary advisory body for technology appraisal [14]. This framework is strategically reinforced by the Six Pillars of Health Transformation (2022) [15], which explicitly prioritizes the transformation of the health technology sector, creating a high‐level mandate for systematic evaluation [2].

Despite this policy infrastructure, a critical analysis reveals a pronounced implementation gap concerning midwifery. The operational focus of the national HTA system, particularly the HTAC, has historically been on evaluating high‐cost pharmaceuticals and complex medical devices for hospital‐based care and reimbursement decisions. This focus is evident in the global trend where HTA initiatives concentrate on pharmaceuticals and devices, often overlooking non‐pharmacological interventions [16].

Consequently, the core technologies of community‐based midwifery practice remain largely outside the scope of formal national HTA. Essential, routine tools such as handheld Doppler devices, specific partograph designs, misoprostol kits for postpartum hemorrhage, and mobile health (mHealth) applications for KIA (Kesehatan Ibu dan Anak) data management are typically adopted based on procurement patterns or donor programs without undergoing systematic, evidence‐based assessment for the Indonesian context [17]. This creates a two‐tiered system: rigorous assessment for tertiary‐care technologies, but ad‐hoc adoption for the foundational tools used by Indonesia's vast frontline midwifery workforce [18].

This gap is reflected in the professional identity of midwives. Midwives often see themselves as hands‐on practitioners who protect women from unnecessary technological intervention, advocating for normal, physiological birth [19]. The absence of clear, evidence‐based assessments of midwifery technologies may inadvertently reinforce a dichotomy between “natural” care and “technology,” rather than fostering the use of HTA to identify technologies that truly support safe, physiologic birth and prevent over‐medicalization.

The challenge of translating HTA policy into midwifery practice is exacerbated by Indonesia's decentralized health system. Procurement decisions for midwifery equipment are largely made at the district (kabupaten/kota) level. While this supports local responsiveness, it presents a fundamental structural hurdle: district authorities lack the specialized capacity and data to conduct or apply localized HTAs. They cannot effectively implement the national HTA guideline.

Findings are not always translated into implementation, and reports risk being untimely or purely advisory [20]. In Indonesia, this is not just a procedural failure but a systemic one. The mismatch between a centrally designed policy framework and a decentralized procurement reality means that even if a national HTA on a midwifery technology were conducted, there is no clear pathway for its findings to inform thousands of independent district‐levels purchasing decisions. This structural gap undermines the potential of HTA to guide the evidence‐based in‐service training and resource allocation needed to reduce maternal mortality [21]. Therefore, advancing HTA in Indonesian midwifery requires moving beyond establishing policies to solving the critical difficulty of contextual implementation. It necessitates bridging the gap between the national HTAC and district health offices, potentially through mechanisms like national priority technology lists for midwifery with pre‐conducted HTAs, simplified assessment tools for local use, and integrating HTA evidence into midwifery update training curriculam [22]. Only by addressing this implementation gap can Indonesia harness the full potential of its HTA framework to improve maternal and child health outcomes.

Challenges in Implementing HTA

In Indonesia, there is a growing acknowledgment of the importance of HTA in midwifery, driven by increased awareness among healthcare professionals, legislators, and researchers, fostering a conducive environment for HTA development and integration. Joint research endeavors between academic institutions, healthcare organizations, and governmental agencies have led to specific HTA studies in midwifery, facilitating evidence generation and Fact results. HTA's potential in optimizing resource allocation in midwifery practices by evaluating the cost‐effectiveness of health technologies is recognized, aiming to achieve optimal health outcomes for mothers and infants [16]. However, challenges persist, notably the scarcity of comprehensive and reliable data on health technology efficacy and safety, hindering thorough analyses and effective decision‐making. The lack of a consolidated data repository makes it difficult to conduct thorough analyses and may result in poor decision‐making. Limited financial and technological resources pose additional barriers, impacting the ability to conduct comprehensive HTA studies, potentially leading to regional disparities in care quality and access to effective therapies. Limited funding and access to modern technology may limit the ability to conduct thorough evaluations of health technologies used in midwifery. In addition, Funding for data collection, analysis, and infrastructure development is required when investing in HTA research. Furthermore, skilled HTA experts conducting complete assessments may need more supply, particularly in resource‐constrained contexts.

Addressing these challenges through increased funding, capacity‐building, and collaboration efforts is crucial for promoting HTA in Indonesian midwifery, ultimately contributing to improved maternal and child health outcomes nationwide [7].

Efforts and Recommendations

In Indonesia, there is a growing recognition of the necessity of HTA in midwifery practices. This increased awareness among healthcare professionals, legislators, and researchers creates a favorable climate for HTA development and integration. Enhancing HTA within Indonesian midwifery services holds immense promise for bolstering data driven decision‐making and elevating maternal and child health outcomes. Realizing this potential necessitates a concerted effort to foster collaboration among a spectrum of stakeholders invested in maternal and child healthcare. By orchestrating partnerships among midwives, healthcare administrators, policymakers, researchers, and professional organizations, a comprehensive framework for HTA implementation can be forged. These collaborations could span joint research endeavors, the exchange of best practices, and the establishment of working groups dedicated to formulating standardized HTA guidelines tailored to midwifery practices.

Moreover, the integration of health technology stands as a transformative avenue for advancing HTA methodologies within midwifery [23]. Leveraging digital health solutions, telemedicine platforms, and health data analytics promises to streamline evaluation processes, enabling real‐time decision‐making through enhanced data collection, analysis, and dissemination. Embracing these technological innovations can markedly enhance the efficiency and efficacy of HTA research in the midwifery domain. Crucially, robust research endeavors and diligent data‐gathering initiatives are paramount for advancing HTA in midwifery. Encouraging research institutions, healthcare organizations, and academic entities to conduct rigorous studies on the impact of health technologies in midwifery is essential for building a comprehensive evidence base. Such endeavors not only underpin comprehensive HTA evaluations but also inform evidence‐based actions crucial for guiding clinical practice and policy development.

Furthermore, the successful integration of HTA findings into midwifery decision‐making processes necessitates structured knowledge translation efforts. Ensuring that HTA results are effectively communicated to key stakeholders in a comprehensible and practical manner is imperative for their meaningful integration into clinical practice and policy formulation.

Investing in capacity development and training initiatives for midwives and healthcare professionals represents a cornerstone for bolstering the acceptance and impact of HTA. Equipping these stakeholders with the requisite skills and knowledge in HTA principles, methodologies, and data interpretation fosters a culture of fact‐based data and continuous quality improvement within midwifery services. Advocacy initiatives also play a pivotal role in garnering policy support for HTA integration within midwifery. Engaging legislators, healthcare professionals, and relevant authorities to underscore the importance of HTA in enhancing maternal and child health outcomes can catalyze policy reforms prioritizing evidence‐based practices [24]. Collaborative endeavors with professional associations and non‐governmental organizations dedicated to maternal and child health advocacy can amplify these efforts. Finally, to bolster HTA in midwifery, the Indonesian government should prioritize healthcare research funding, with a specific focus on HTA initiatives. By allocating sufficient resources, robust HTA frameworks can be established, fostering analytic decision‐making and sustainable improvements in maternal and child healthcare.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while HTA presents a powerful paradigm for strengthening healthcare globally, its integration into Indonesian midwifery requires a deliberate and context‐specific pathway. Our analysis reveals that Indonesia possesses a foundational HTA policy framework but has yet to systematically apply it to the technologies fundamental to midwifery care. The primary hurdles are therefore not a lack of policy, but rather policy prioritization, alignment with midwifery's scope of practice, and targeted resource allocation.

Key challenges including budgetary constraints, fragmented data availability, and the absence of standardized evaluation protocols for midwifery technologies hinder the full realization of HTA's potential. Nevertheless, this nascent stage presents significant opportunities for growth. Advancing HTA in Indonesian midwifery necessitates a multi‐faceted approach: fostering robust collaboration among policymakers, healthcare providers, and researchers; harnessing digital health innovations for better data and evaluation; and intensifying focused research to build a localized evidence base.

Undoubtedly, the integration of HTA into midwifery offers the promise of more informed decision‐making, optimized resource allocation, and enhanced safety and quality of care directly contributing to improved health outcomes for mothers and newborns nationwide. To realize this promise, urgent, concerted action is warranted. This must include conducting comprehensive HTA studies on routine midwifery technologies and implementing sustained capacity‐building initiatives to equip midwives with the skills to apply HTA principles. Through these efforts, Indonesia can foster a resilient, evidence‐based midwifery sector that safeguards maternal and child health and advances the goals of its health transformation agenda.
