Scoping Review of Dietary Quality Indices: Heterogeneity of Definitions and Health Associations among Adults
Authors: Christine El-Khoury, Franziska Jannasch, Matthias B Schulze
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe application of dietary quality indices (DQIs) is limited by inconsistent methods and suboptimal reporting, but the effect of index composition heterogeneity on the strength of disease associations is not clear. A scoping review was carried out to identify evidence on the most common disease-related DQIs and assess the heterogeneity of their application and its possible implications. We systematically identified umbrella reviews, systematic reviews, and primary studies that investigated the association of DQIs with all-cause mortality, risk of or mortality from cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes, and cancer among adults. Compositions of the prioritized DQIs were explored in primary studies and the degree of deviance from the original DQI versions was quantified. A meta-regression was conducted to investigate the association between the degree of score modification and disease risk estimates. From 175 eligible primary studies, 51 DQIs were identified and retrieved from 20 systematic reviews included in 2 umbrella reviews. The most common indices were the Mediterranean diet (MedDiet scores), Alternative Healthy Eating Index (AHEI), Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH), Healthy Eating Index (HEI), and Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII). Higher diet quality reflected by these indices was associated with beneficial health outcomes in 17 meta-analyses. Heterogeneity of MedDiet scores was not considered, because it has been addressed in previous reviews. Substantial heterogeneity was identified in the remaining DQI applications. Among studies that reported composition details, AHEI-2010 and DII were modified in almost all studies, and DASH and HEI-2015 in half of the studies. The underlying reasons were mainly related to population- and study-specific characteristics. DQI modifications did not appear to substantially influence the direction or strength of associations with mortality and disease risk. However, heterogeneity of index composition and its suboptimal reporting limit the reproducibility and comparability of results from studies on DQIs and health outcomes, and standard applications are preferred.Keywords: dietary quality indices, all-cause mortality, cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes, cancer

INTRODUCTION

Dietary pattern analysis considers combinations of various foods and nutrients, allowing the investigation of their joint effects on health outcomes.1 This more holistic approach, compared with investigating single foods or nutrients, facilitates the generation of public health recommendations.2 Several methods are available to define dietary patterns, which can be roughly categorized as being either a posteriori/exploratory or a priori/hypothesis-driven.3 The latter, also known as index-based patterns, consist of calculating a score to reflect the level of adherence to a predefined reference dietary pattern.4 These scores, frequently referred to as dietary quality indices (DQIs), are multicomponent indices that can be based, for example, on dietary guidelines and recommendations; on particular dietary composition, focusing on nutrients and/or food groups associated with health outcomes; or can reflect population-specific or regional usual diets.5 A multitude of DQIs have been developed and assessed against a variety of disease outcomes.6–10 Although dietary pattern analysis has contributed to the growing evidence for dietary recommendations, several limitations have been discussed, such as the heterogeneity of scoring systems for defined diet patterns, as well as the substantial variation in their application and reporting.11,12 Moreover, heterogeneity in the application of indices reflecting the same diet has been highlighted, such as the Mediterranean diet (MedDiet).13–15 This complicates the comparison of results from different studies assessing the same diet, and subsequently hinders synthesizing the evidence and translating it into public health guidance.12,16 Inconsistency has been highlighted before; however, there is limited knowledge on whether this heterogeneity affects the associations with disease risk. Therefore, this scoping review aims to investigate whether using heterogeneous versions of the same DQI across different studies might have led to heterogeneity in disease risk association. We focus here on the most commonly used DQIs reported in umbrella reviews to be associated with health effects among adults.

METHODS

Review Method

A scoping review approach was followed to systematically identify available evidence on chronic disease-related DQIs and assess key methodological aspects of their application in dietary-pattern research.17,18 The reporting of this review is in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension for Scoping Reviews.19 An a priori–defined protocol was developed and was published in Open Science Framework registries (accessible via https://osf.io/c4qtn).

Search Strategy and Selection Process

In this scoping review, we aimed to cover different levels of the hierarchy of evidence, starting with umbrella reviews, followed by systematic reviews/meta-analyses, and extending to original studies at the foundational level. Umbrella reviews were used to identify systematic reviews and meta-analyses that provided summary estimates of associations of DQIs with health outcomes. The systematic reviews were also the basis for the identification of primary studies to evaluate the impact of DQI scoring systems. To serve this purpose, a systematic approach was developed to identify the relevant information sources. First, a search strategy was developed and applied in May 2023 in PubMed to identify relevant umbrella reviews, as follows: (“Umbrella review”[tiab]) AND ((“Diet”[Mesh] OR “dietary quality indices” OR Diet* OR “dietary pattern”) AND ((“Cardiovascular Diseases”[Mesh] OR cardiovascular) OR (“Diabetes Mellitus, Type 2”[Mesh] OR diabetes OR “Type 2 diabetes”) OR (“Neoplasms”[Mesh] OR Cancer) OR “mortality”)). A reference list hand search was also carried out to identify further umbrella reviews. Then, systematic reviews/meta-analyses included in the selected umbrella reviews were assessed for eligibility. Subsequently, primary studies (observational and intervention studies) from the included systematic reviews were considered for further data extraction.

The eligibility criteria were defined as follows. First, umbrella reviews were included if they focused on more than 1 DQI in relation to more than 1 of the endpoints of interest, which are: risk of and/or death due to overall cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), overall cancer, type 2 diabetes (T2D), and all-cause mortality among adults. Second, the systematic reviews, identified from the eligible umbrella reviews, were included if they assessed any DQI with any of the outcomes of interest in adults aged ≥18 years, with the exception of pregnant women. Third, the primary studies included in those systematic reviews were evaluated against the same eligibility criteria for inclusion. In particular, primary studies that assessed 2 or more subtypes of CVD combined were included, whereas those that assessed 1 subtype only or different subtypes separately were not further considered. Studies published in English were included, and no limitations on dates of publication were imposed. One reviewer (C.E.-K.) screened the studies against these criteria to select the studies that were then included in the review after abstract and full-text screening, respectively. Any doubts were resolved after discussion with a second reviewer (F.J.).

Data Extraction Process

Required data extracted from umbrella reviews, systematic reviews/meta-analyses, and primary studies were recorded in data extraction forms specifically created to serve the aims of this scoping review. For umbrella reviews, collected information included first author, year of publication, DQIs, and endpoints of focus. In addition, for systematic reviews/meta-analyses, the number of included studies and their design and the effect size with 95% CIs and I2 values were also extracted. For primary studies, the standardized form included information on the first author, year of publication, study name and design, country, follow-up duration, size of the study population, sex, age range of participants, baseline health status, dietary assessment method, investigated DQI(s), assessed health outcome(s), statistical parameters, and adjustment variables.

To be able to prioritize the most frequently used DQIs, we extracted the following detailed information from each primary study: reference(s) to sources for the DQI construction, the DQI label used, as well as the corresponding assessed health outcomes. Subsequently, prioritizing of indices was done on the basis of the frequency of application across the studies and their inclusion in meta-analyses on association with chronic disease outcomes. The composition of the different MedDiet scores was not evaluated in this scoping review, because they already were comprehensively covered in an umbrella review by Galbete et al.15 For the remaining prioritized DQIs, a supplementary data extraction form was introduced. It included a comprehensive overview of the DQI construct reported in each publication. For instance, reported data on components and weights, considered food items in the respective components, and scoring criteria were extracted.

Systematic Investigation of DQI Composition Heterogeneity

To investigate heterogeneity, we compared the study-specific DQI composition with the originally reported construct. This required additional search for the background source that introduced the DQI composition. When several updates were available for the same index, we identified the most recent version used in the included primary studies and considered its reference publication the standard for comparison. The following modification aspects were identified and subsequently examined: inclusion and exclusion of components, reported reason for exclusion, modification of the component at the food-group level (eg, low-fat dairy vs dairy), included food items, scoring cutoffs, reason for modification, and range of total possible score points.

Assessment of DQI Modifications

To determine whether using modified versions of the DQIs could affect the diet-disease association estimates, we developed a scoring system to reflect the degree of modification. Such “modification scores” were applied subsequently for each index in each primary study, following this systematic approach. The scoring was based on the comparison between the study-specific DQI version and the original DQI version, as introduced in the previous step. The scoring system includes 2 different scores. Modification score 1 (MS1) was constructed by assigning 0-4 points to each component modification, depending on the degree of modification. Zero points were assigned to nonmodified components and increasing points were allocated according to the degree of deviance from the original index version. A “minor” modification (1 point) referred to a difference of food items included in the component group (eg, excluding peas from the legumes group). A “moderate” modification (2 points) was defined as an alteration of component scoring (eg, adopting a lower cutoff of intake). A “significant” modification (3 points) corresponded to a reformulation of the component or food group (eg, low-fat dairy vs total dairy), and a “major” modification (4 points) was defined as the omission of the component. If individual components had more than 1 type of modification, points were assigned for each modification separately and then summed for the component (eg, dairy instead of low-fat dairy and modified scoring cutoff: 3 + 2 = 5 points).

For the modification score 2 (MS2), when a modification of a component was detected, the component was assigned 1 point, irrespective of the degree of modification and of whether multiple modifications were applied to the component. Components without any modification received 0 points. The individual component points were then summed to obtain MS1 and MS2 as follows: ∑c=1nscorec, where n is the total number of index components c, and the total modification score is calculated as the sum of scores of each component c across all n components constituting the index. Dietary quality indices with higher scores on both the MS1 and MS2 were considered to be further deviating from the original index construct, compared with indices with lower MS1 and MS2 scores. Modification scores were calculated for DQI versions if primary studies reported index composition in sufficient details to allow comparison of the index to the original version.

To assess whether MS1 or MS2 might be effect modifiers, we conducted univariate meta-regression on prospective studies. We only considered DQI-endpoint subgroups that had more than 10 observations.20 Extracted effect size estimates (ie, odds ratios [ORs], hazard ratios [HRs]) comparing highest vs lowest diet quality were interpreted as risk ratios (RRs).21 A random-effects model was fitted to the data with the restricted maximum likelihood method and Knapp-Hartung adjustments.22–24 Each study was weighted after calculating the SE of the log-transformed RR. For DQI-endpoint subgroups with fewer than 10 observations, we plotted the derived 2 modification scores and risk estimates corresponding to each DQI, and visual inspection of the scatterplots was used to assess the presence of an association pattern. If the original publication did not report results from a pooled main analysis but rather only for specific subgroups, results from these subgroups were included as separate observations. The meta-regression was conducted using the metafor R package,25 and all plots were created in R (version 4.3.0).

RESULTS

Study and DQI Selection

The initial search resulted in 2 umbrella reviews that investigated the association between several DQIs and the health outcomes of interest.26,27 These umbrella reviews included 51 systematic reviews, of which 20 were eligible for further consideration.21,28–46 These systematic reviews included 277 DQI-related primary studies, of which 175 met the predefined eligibility criteria and were subsequently included in this scoping review (Figure 1).36,46–219 These studies were conducted across 6 continents, reporting data from 45 countries, with 57% of evidence generated from Europe and 30% from North America (Table S1). Dietary quality indices investigated in primary studies were grouped into 51 DQIs, following a systematic approach illustrated in Figure S1. A full list of identified scores with the corresponding citing publications is given in Table S2.

PRISMA Flow Diagram for the Study Selection Process

The evaluation of the frequency with which each of the 51 retrieved DQIs was included in primary studies investigating association with chronic disease outcomes resulted in the following most commonly used scores: MedDiet scores, Alternative Healthy Eating Index (AHEI), Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH), Healthy Eating Index (HEI), and Dietary Inflammatory Index (DII). These scores were applied in 139 of the primary studies (79.4%), corresponding to 72% of the total number of identified DQI-endpoint assessments (n = 222 of 308). The relative frequency of assessment of each of these scores was 32.5% for the MedDiet scores, 12.7% for AHEI, 12% for DASH, 9.7% for HEI, and 5.2% for DII (Table S3).

Association of DQIs With Health Outcomes

Higher diet quality, assessed by the prioritized DQIs, was generally associated with favorable health outcomes. From a total of 63 pooled risk estimates reported from the included meta-analyses, 60 indicated a significantly lower risk of mortality or chronic diseases with higher DQI adherence (Table 1).21,28–37,39,40,42,43,45,46 The effect size varied across different DQIs and endpoints. For instance, inverse associations were detected between the adherence to the MedDiet and chronic disease risk and mortality, with risk reduction ranging from 5% for all-cause mortality (per 1-point increase, HR = 0.95; 95% CI, 0.93-0.96) to 41% for CVD mortality (for highest vs lowest category, RR = 0.59; (95% CI, 0.38-0.93). For the AHEI, lower risk between 10% for cancer mortality (for highest compared with the lowest category, RR = 0.9; 95% CI, 0.85-0.95) and 26% for CVD mortality (for highest compared with the lowest category, RR = 0.74; 95% CI, 0.71-0.78) were observed. Higher adherence to the DASH diet was associated with a 3% lower risk of cancer and CVD mortality (for each 5-point increase, HR = 0.97; 95% CI, 0.95-0.98), whereas the risk estimate for lower T2D incidence was more pronounced (27%; for highest vs lowest category, RR = 0.73; 95% CI, 0.65-0.83). Beneficial effects were also observed with a higher adherence to the HEI, varying from 12% lower risk for T2D incidence (for highest vs lowest diet quality, RR = 0.88; 95% CI, 0.82-0.94) to 22% for all-cause mortality (for highest vs lowest category, RR = 0.78; 95% CI, 0.76-0.79). Moreover, a higher inflammatory potential of the diet, reflected by higher DII scores, was associated with a higher risk of chronic diseases, ranging from 4% (per 1 SD increase, HR = 1.04; 95% CI, 1.03-1.05) for all-cause mortality to 41% for CVD incidence (for highest vs lowest DII, RR = 1.41; 95% CI, 1.12-1.78).

DQI Composition Heterogeneity

Alternative Healthy Eating Index

Primary studies reporting on the AHEI referred to 2 different versions of this DQI: the initial version (AHEI)56 and the updated AHEI-2010.78 We only included studies investigating the latter in further analysis. The AHEI-2010 components as originally published78 served as the standard against which all AHEI-2010 versions used in the included studies were compared. Composition details were available from 1858,61–66,68,69,71–73,75,78–80,146,149 of 26 studies (Table S2). This index was modified in all applications except in the study by Chiuve et al,78 who developed the tool (Table S4 and Supplementary Data File).

The components vegetables, fruit, nuts and legumes, red or processed meat, long-chain n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid, polyunsaturated fatty acid, sodium, and alcohol were included in all detailed index versions (Figure S2). However, the component trans fat was omitted in approximately 43% of detailed applications, due to the lack of data. All the components were modified in at least 1 individual study, and the most modified components were alcohol, trans fats, and whole grains (modified in 85.7%, 61.9%, and 61.9%, respectively, of the detailed applications). The modification justifications were mentioned in 8 studies, and they were mainly undertaken due to lack of data and to reflect population-specific consumption patterns (Table S5).

Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension

Three main groups of DQIs intended to reflect the DASH diet were identified; DASH as structured by Fung et al (DASH-Fung),220 DASH as outlined by Mellen et al,221 and other indices reflecting the DASH diet using different index compositions. DASH-Fung was considered for the comparative analysis because it was cited as reference for index composition in 28 of 36 reports assessing a DASH diet pattern (Table S2). This index was modified in 1358,61,65,66,76,77,97,99,101–103,146,149 of the 2649,54,58,61,62,64–66,68,71,75–77,80,81,96–103,126,146,149 studies that reported its detailed composition (Table S4 and Supplementary Data File). All components were consistently included except sodium, which was excluded from 1 study (Figure S2). Sodium was the only nonmodified component in the studies in which it was included; the remaining components all underwent modification in at least 1 study, with the fruit component being the most frequently modified (23% of the detailed applications). The reasons for these modifications were documented in 7 studies and were attributed to lack of data; food frequency questionnaire specifications; reflecting healthful aspects of components; and to population specific consumption (Table S5).

Healthy Eating Index

Primary studies reporting on the HEI referred to 4 versions of the HEI: HEI,222 HEI-2005,223 HEI-2010,224 and HEI-2015,225 with the latter selected as the reference for the assessment of modifications (Table S2). This index was modified in 289,149 of the 4 studies68,89,149,150 that reported its detailed composition (Table S4 and Supplementary Data File). The modifications were related to the whole-grains component (exclusion, modified scoring) and were due to the lack of specific data (Table S5 and Figure S2).

Dietary Inflammatory Index

Fourteen of 16 reports46,112–115,117–120,122–126 provided details on how the inflammatory potential of the diet was assessed using the DII (Table S2), investigating its association with health outcomes (Table S4 and Supplementary Data File). The DII, as originally reported, has 45 components.226 Fifteen of these were included in all the identified DII versions: carbohydrate, protein, fiber, cholesterol, saturated fat, monounsaturated fatty acid, polyunsaturated fatty acid, niacin, thiamin, riboflavin, Fe, Mg, vitamin A, vitamin E, and folic acid. Moreover, 10 components were included in more than 10 reports: total fat, alcohol, long-chain n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, n-6 polyunsaturated fatty acids, vitamin C, β-carotene, vitamin B12, vitamin B6, Zn, and vitamin D. Five components—eugenol, saffron, turmeric, thyme/oregano, and rosemary—were not included in any of the identified studies. Omitting components was mainly due to the lack of data and population-specific consumption patterns (Table S5). Furthermore, the component green/black tea was ambiguous in 2 reports in which the consumption of tea in general was considered instead of only these 2 types.

Degree of Modification and Disease Risk Estimates

We next evaluated if the degree of modifications of the DQIs might be linked to the heterogeneity of disease associations reported in primary studies. The modification scores were calculated for 65 of 78 DQI versions, having provided enough details on the index composition. The MS1, which puts emphasis on the level of modification of the DQI components, ranged across the primary studies from 0 to 8 for DASH-Fung, from 0 to 27 for AHEI-2010, from 0 to 4 for the HEI-2015, and from 36 to 96 for DII. As for the MS2, which emphasizes the modification regardless of its level, scores ranged from 0 to 3 for DASH-Fung (maximum possible score of 8), from 0 to 11 for AHEI-2010 (maximum possible score of 11), from 0 to 1 for the HEI-2015 (maximum possible score of 13), and from 9 to 24 for DII (maximum possible score of 46: 45 modified components × 1 point + 1 point for modified overall score calculation method, ie, intake standardization to the study population instead of the global database).

For the relation of index modification with the reported association with health outcomes, we did not find strong evidence that modifying the DQIs substantially affects the strength or direction of the overall investigated associations in the univariate meta-regression, in DQI-endpoint subgroups with more than 10 observations for MS1 (Figure 2) and MS2 (Figure S3). Statistically significant associations were only detected in the meta-regression of DASH modification scores with CVD mortality (Figure 2E and Supplementary Figure 3E). A 1-point increase in MS1 was associated with a 1.3% decrease in the relative risk of CVD mortality (β = −.013; RR = 0.987), whereas each 1-point increase in MS2 was associated with a 3.4% decrease in this relative risk (β = −.034; RR = 0.966). For the DQI-endpoint subgroups with fewer than 10 observations, the data points were dispersed across the scatterplots, indicating heterogeneity with no clear pattern of association (Figures S4-S7).

DISCUSSION

In this scoping review, we identified 51 DQIs used in studies of the association of diet quality with the risk of or mortality due to CVD, T2D, and cancer, as well as all-cause mortality among adults. From this large number, 5 DQIs were most commonly used in the identified studies and were included in meta-analyses investigating their association with health outcomes. Better diet quality, reflected by these indices, was associated with lower risk of chronic disease and mortality in 17 meta-analyses reports. We detected substantial variations in the use of the same DQI in different studies for most DQIs, with modifications related to the included components and the food groups or items they reflect, as well as the scoring approach. However, we could not identify a strong association between the degree of modifications made and the reported risk estimates for all assessed chronic disease endpoints.

Findings highlight the variability in DQI reporting and application, which has been discussed previously in the literature. For instance, a recent systematic review of methods12 used to assess and report dietary patterns found that one-third of all the identified DQIs were modified index versions. Additionally, relevant information on index composition was not reported in several applications, including rationale for cutoff points and food profiles.12 In particular, Abdelhamid et al14 highlighted variability in food and nutrient intakes according to the adherence to the Mediterranean diet in different studies, as well as inconsistent definitions of the food groups across studies referencing the same score. Moreover, a recent scoping review that explored plant-based DQIs showed that around half of the indices applications were modified, and approximately 10% of the studies did not report the underlying reasons for modifying index food groups.227

The heterogeneity of DQI definitions was not shown to affect the disease associations in our scoping review. However, it is important to acknowledge that we did not directly compare different scores calculations within studies; rather, we carried out an indirect comparison of the impact of DQI heterogeneity on disease associations. Previous reviews assessed the heterogeneity of indices and corresponding disease risk in different approaches. Galbete et al15 found 34 definitions of the Mediterranean diet, performed subgroup meta-analyses to compare the associations with different indices and found little difference in risk associations. More recently, Stubbendorff et al228 carried out a systematic evaluation of 7 scores representing the EAT-Lancet diet. The authors developed 2 scores for the qualitative assessment of those indices: the diet index construction score, which covered aspects suggested by Burggraf et al,9 such as index dimensions, components and their types, scaling procedure, cutoffs, and valuation function; and the index consistency score that reflected the ability of the indices to group individuals according to the reference EAT-Lancet diet across 3 cohorts. Findings highlighted variation in indices construction and consistency, and showed differences in dietary intake, as well as in their association with stroke, death, and greenhouse gas emissions; 2 of the 7 indices were most consistent.

Although modifications were identified for all 4 assessed DQIs in the present review, they may not have drastically altered the core features of the assessed diets. Adapting food groups or scoring cutoffs to the study population or the available dietary assessment specifications thus may not mask the healthful aspect of the dietary pattern. For example, even though DII scores were calculated from fewer components in all primary studies than the full list of 45 components proposed for this DQI,226 scores should still reflect the inflammatory potential of the diet to some degree. Moreover, higher adherence to a healthy diet—even when assessed slightly differently across studies—would still have beneficial health implications. This can be also justified because, when assessing dietary patterns, we are interested in the overall combination of different foods and nutrients complexly interacting and leading to detectable health effects.1 Thus, the combined effect of different foods might be large enough not to be affected by slight alterations of individual food items. This could partially explain why the effect of higher adherence to the different versions of the same diet or DQI, nevertheless not deviating from its main features, was still evident despite increasing modification scores. However, this would need to be directly assessed by comparing different DQI versions within the same study population. For instance, previous research compared 2 MedDiet indices within the European Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition—Potsdam (EPIC-Potsdam), namely the traditional MedDiet score and the MedPyr, based on the MedDiet pyramid, and found that both indices were associated with a 16% to 20% reduced risk of T2D, respectively.229

The modifications identified in our review were partly undertaken due to the lack of specific dietary data. This was particularly noticeable for the DII, which includes a wide range of parameters, with many not or infrequently available in the different studies. Generally, the level of detail covered by the dietary assessment tool used in a study affects the ability to include individual DQI components and affects DQI total scores.230 The level of coverage of different food composition databases also leads to differences in items available for inclusion in the indices used across studies, which challenges their comparison.231 Another part of the detected modifications was due to population-specific consumption patterns. For example, Wang et al73 replaced the whole-grains component with cereal fiber because whole grain intake in their Chinese population was “extremely low and lacked variation,”73 whereas Neelakantan et al65 used a modified index in which they defined optimal alcohol intake as less than 1 serving per day for both men and women instead of 0.5-1.5 servings for women and 0.5-2 for men, because alcohol consumption might be “prudent in East Asians compared with other ethnic groups as a result of genetic differences in alcohol metabolism.”65 It is well known that diet is influenced by many socioeconomical, cultural, and geographic factors.6,232 For instance, Hashemian et al.149 attributed a score of zero to all participants for the alcohol component in AHEI-2010 because alcohol intake is not common in the Golestan Cohort study in Iran. Furthermore, many modifications were undertaken without providing the underlying justifications, which highlights the gap in reporting practices.

Given these persistent challenges, the need for standardization of DQIs and their components, as well as the harmonization of their application and reporting, has been previously highlighted.11,12,231 For instance, the Dietary Patterns Methods Project16 aimed to address the issue of inconsistent methodology and followed a standardized, uniform approach in the application of 4 DQIs across 3 cohorts. The standardization was on the level of index construct and harmonized mortality outcomes and model adjustment sets. Authors found mostly consistent results in the associations of the HEI-2010, AHEI-2010, DASH, and alternate MedDiet with all-cause, cancer, and CVD mortality across the included cohorts. This emphasizes the relevance of consistent DQI application for the subsequent generation of evidence-based guidelines.

To ensure comparability and reproducibility across different studies, the assessment of diet quality according to a specific DQI should preferably be as close as possible to the original construct. However, in case of the DII, this seems largely unfeasible, and an alternative standard is needed. Our results highlight that 15 of the original 45 DII components were included in all assessed index versions. Omitting several components of the original DII did not appear to influence disease risk estimates, as seen in the meta-regression findings. Therefore, we propose to use these 15 components as core components. Using this DII alternative in new studies enhances their comparability with older reports. However, more systematic evaluations of score modifications would still be needed (eg, comparing different DIIs within the same study) to support the suggestion that core components are, indeed, sufficient.

As for the other DQIs assessed in our review, although there might be difficulty in applying predefined scores in different settings, this should not necessarily lead to modifying the index. Because the original scores of these indices have defined specific cutoffs of intake, they could still be used, even though the intake might be much lower in a given population. In fact, diet quality as measured by a given DQI might be different in different populations. Although this might not be the ideal way to reflect diet quality across all populations or subgroups of a population, applying the DQI-specific standard construct would still be relevant from a reproducibility and comparability perspective. An alternative would be to develop or use a population-specific DQI that is constructed in way to reflect the specificities of its setting, including the cultural and socioeconomic contexts. Still, indices with absolute cutoffs would allow more comparability than those that use a relative scoring system, where the scores depend on the intake distribution of the population. Here, applying the scoring across populations with different absolute intakes would lead to associations that are more difficult to compare across studies.

Other factors should also be considered for the standard application of DQIs. Population characteristics and consumption patterns, cooking methods, available dietary data, factors associated with the assessed endpoints, and the index construction criteria should lead the decision toward the choice of a DQI.9,233 It is of major relevance, as well, to consider detailed and harmonized reporting for more transparency and clarity regarding the use of the indices. Mentioning which food items were included in the index components, and documenting the reasons for any deviance from the original versions or any specific decision made with regards to the index composition, would pave the way for better comparability and reproducibility.12,13,233 Several supporting references are available now to optimize reporting in this context. These include STROBE-nut,234 the HEI application checklist,235 and more recently, a recommended reporting checklist highlighting process steps, starting from the choice of a dietary assessment tool until the decision on using data-driven or literature-based adherence levels or continuous data.230

Strengths and Limitations

This scoping review has several strengths. We carried out a systematic search for umbrella reviews of meta-analyses leading to several primary studies on the association between DQIs and a wide range of chronic disease endpoints. We identified a large number of DQIs used in studies from various countries and populations. We then performed an in-depth analysis of the composition of the most commonly used indices. Modifications were summarized in 2 modification scores to quantify the level of deviance from the original index versions. We also assessed possible associations between index modification and disease risk estimates, which is a novel approach within the framework of advancing standardized dietary quality assessment. In fact, existing reviews either explored the different DQI definitions of the same diet with15,228 or without13,14,236,237 calculating corresponding disease risk, or described the application of different DQIs reflecting different diets.12,227 However, our approach focused on the 4 most commonly assessed diets, considered 1 DQI definition for each, assessed the variability of its application across different studies, and indirectly investigated whether this heterogeneity could affect disease risk estimates.

Nevertheless, some limitations should be mentioned. First, although our search for DQIs through umbrella reviews was comprehensive and allowed the identification of a broad range of indices, the primary studies included in our review were limited to those covered by the systematic reviews included in those umbrella reviews. In particular, more recently developed DQIs were not identified in our review. For example, the Food-Based Global Diet Quality Score238 and diet scores reflecting the EAT-Lancet diet239–246 may well be relevant alternative DQIs. However, it is likely that those indices would not have been prioritized in our analysis, because they are relatively more recent and, thus, less frequently used compared with the indices we considered. The identified umbrella reviews were published recently26,27 and, therefore, should be a timely resource for identifying health-related DQIs. Noteworthy is also that the most recent update of the HEI (HEI-2020) was also not included in this review, but its construction is similar to that of the HEI-2015 and only was renamed to indicate that it reflects the recent 2020-2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans.247 Nevertheless, we still could not verify the heterogeneity of the missed indices, or its possible implications. Similarly, our approach for DQI heterogeneity assessment focused on the most recent update for indices with several updates, thereby excluding older versions form the analysis. Moreover, evidence identified from the included studies was mainly generated from high-income countries, highlighting a significant gap in data from lower-income settings. Although we prioritized DQIs on the basis of their reported risk associations, these DQIs may not be the best indicators of diet quality and its health impact in populations from a socioeconomic or cultural background not well represented in the studies included in our review. Second, although the chronic disease outcomes we preselected were comprehensive, we left out other diet-related outcomes that could also be relevant to identify DQIs, such as neurodegenerative diseases, hypertension, dyslipidemia, subtypes of CVD, site-specific cancers, and undernutrition. Third, several primary studies did not provide sufficiently complete information on the DQI composition and so were not considered for the evaluation of modifications, which also limited the number of studies included in the analysis.

Regarding the modification scores, a few limitations should be mentioned. First, we allocated similar points to different components even though some components might be more relevant contributors to the assessed dietary pattern. However, in the original indices (except the HEI and DII), all components are weighted similarly. Second, although omitting a component due to lack of data might be of secondary importance than an intentional modification when data are available, this was not reflected in our scoring approach. However, this does not change the fact that the index, indeed, was modified compared with the original construct, and the modification scores would still reflect this variation. Third, in MS1, modification points for the same components were added, which can lead to a higher score than that of an omitted component. Nevertheless, this was addressed by MS2, in which the component received 1 point regardless of the different types of modifications undertaken. Finally, we are not aware of a gold standard to validate or calibrate our approach, and alternative approaches to evaluate score modifications might lead to different conclusions. Additionally, by prioritizing DQIs based on their frequency of application and their inclusion in meta-analyses investigating their association with health outcomes, we cannot rule out that modifications have affected health associations of other DQIs. Moreover, the reporting of the index composition was not always clear, so some assumptions had to be made based on the reported data. For example, it was often not clearly stated whether fruit juices were included in the “fruits” component or beverages such as “sugar-sweetened beverages,” or if the “total fruits” component included juices and dried fruits. The suboptimal reporting also limited our modification assessment, because the index was evaluated solely based on the reported information. This does not exclude the possibility of having undertaken other modifications, for example, on the level of items included in the component group due to nutritional database restrictions but not having been reported.

It is also worth mentioning that other factors besides the scoring and the index components affect the associations with disease risk and mortality, including the quality of dietary data, which depends on the dietary assessment method, the quality of food composition databases, and the choice of model covariates. For example, food frequency questionnaires do not allow correct quantification of intake, resulting in misclassification of study participants when quantitative DQI cutoffs are applied. Observational studies are prone to confounding bias, and the choice of confounder adjustment, which varied between studies, is likely to affect associations. Covariates selected for confounder adjustment also included DQI components in some studies. For example, for the DII,226 which includes total energy intake as a component, adjusting for energy would mean adjusting for 1 component. Similarly, alcohol is a component of several indices, including DII,226 AHEI-2010,78 and MedDiet scores.15 Such factors were not considered in our investigation but could, indeed, influence the risk estimates.

CONCLUSION

In summary, a systematic search for umbrella reviews and evaluation of meta-analyses and primary studies suggest that the MedDiet, DASH, HEI, AHEI, and DII are DQIs that have been most commonly used in studies on the association of diet quality with mortality and chronic disease risk. Although these DQIs all support a preventive potential of healthy diets, substantial heterogeneity in the application of these DQIs exists. We did not find strong evidence that such modifications affected the associations with mortality or risk of the selected diseases of interest in this scoping review. Our comprehensive summary emphasizes the importance of optimal reporting and standardization of DQI application for enhanced reproducibility and comparability across different studies. It additionally highlights the relevance of careful attention to the methods used for diet quality assessment. Future research is needed to directly assess the impact of index modification, ideally within the same study population. Further insights would reveal whether indices standardization is fundamental or whether their flexibility contributes to adaptability without compromising their reliability in disease risk estimation.
