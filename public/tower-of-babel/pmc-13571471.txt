Postoperative refractive changes in second-generation light adjustable lenses possibly associated with tanning bed exposure: a bilateral case report with laboratory analyses
Authors: Jenna Vroman, Brian Groat, Liliana Werner, Ruth Sahler, Max Liu, Zoha Mian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe Light Adjustable Lens with ActivShield™ technology (LAL 2.0; RxSight, Inc.) is an intraocular lens (IOL) that enables postoperative refractive adjustments using targeted ultraviolet (UV) light. The ActivShield was designed to enhance protection against ambient UV exposure to reduce the risk of unintentional polymerization and subsequent unintentional refractive changes. Here we report a case of bilateral LAL 2.0 implantation complicated by progressive visual decline and refractive instability despite initial reported adherence to UV protective glasses.CaseA 70-year-old woman with a history of LASIK and visually significant cataracts underwent uncomplicated bilateral LAL 2.0 implantation with intended monovision. Despite reported compliance with postoperative UV protective glasses, she developed worsening vision and varying refractions. Examination revealed central lens changes consistent with hyperpolymerization. The patient later disclosed frequent tanning bed use possibly without appropriate eye protection. Both lenses were explanted and replaced with Softport (Bausch + Lomb) IOLs.ResultsLaboratory analysis of the explanted lenses showed central thickening without opacification. UV–visible spectrophotometry demonstrated ∼85% transmittance in the visible range, with a curve consistent with a lens with blue-light and UV filters. Wavefront analysis revealed irregular power distribution suggestive of unintentional macromer polymerization. Following lens exchange, best-corrected visual acuity improved to 20/20 bilaterally.ConclusionsActivShield technology does not appear to fully prevent unintended polymerization, especially under extreme UV exposure such as tanning beds. Strict adherence to UV protective eyewear and patient education remain essential in preventing irreversible changes to the lens’ structure prior to the final lock-in treatment.Keywords: ActivShield, Light adjustable lens, Manifest refraction, Second generation, Ultraviolet radiation

Introduction

The Light Adjustable Lens (LAL; RxSight Inc.) is an intraocular lens (IOL) that allows for adjustments of both spherical and cylindrical refractive errors after implantation. The LAL consists of a silicone matrix with embedded ultraviolet (UV) absorbers, a photoinitiator, and photo-reactive macromers.1 The macromers undergo polymerization when exposed to UV light allowing for postoperative manipulation of refraction and correction of residual astigmatism.1 Postoperative changes to the LAL are made with a Light Delivery Device (LDD) that utilizes a 365 nm UV light beam.1 Up to three light treatments to adjust refraction can be made usually starting three to four weeks after surgery to reduce likelihood of postoperative inflammatory changes impacting visual acuity (VA) and lens refraction.1,2 These adjustments are subsequently followed by two additional light treatments that “lock-in” the macromers of the lens which solidifies this personalized refraction.

After implantation and until 24 hours after final lock-in treatment, the lenses are vulnerable to unintentional macromer polymerization secondary to ambient UV light exposure. This exposure can lead to irreversible structural changes to the lens which impairs final VA and refraction. Therefore, patients are instructed to wear RxSight-specified UV protective eyewear during all waking hours to prevent irreversible alterations to the LAL's refraction secondary to ambient UV light.3 Currently, there are two generations of LALs. The second-generation LAL (LAL 2.0), approved in 2021, has an enhanced UV absorber in the anterior portion of the lens optic, known as the ActivShield™. This new feature is stated to provide additional protection from environmental UV light, reducing the risk of unintentional changes to lens refraction between light treatments.3

Here we present a case of bilaterally explanted LALs with ActivShield found to have significant changes in refractive power prior to manipulation with LDD. Extensive laboratory analyses after explantation suggest the ActivShield in the LAL 2.0 does not prevent uncontrolled polymerization of macromers in the case of noncompliance with UV protection glasses.

Case

A 70-year-old female with a past medical history significant for prior LASIK OU with intended monovision presented for cataract evaluation. At presentation, she had decreased visual acuity particularly at night. Her uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA) was OD 20/150 and OS 20/60. Her best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was OD 20/40 with MRx (manifest refraction) of −2.25 + 1.50 x 180 and OS 20/40 with MRx of −4.00 + 1.25 x 175. On exam, she was found to have grade 3 nuclear sclerotic cataracts OU. Surgical options were discussed with the patient at which time LAL OU with intended monovision was determined to be the best course of action.

The patient underwent an uncomplicated implantation of +18.0 LAL 2.0 OD and +17.5 LAL+ 2.0 OS on two separate days. At her first follow-up appointment, the patient's UCVA was OD 20/25 (near J8) and OS 20/30 (near J4). The patient was given postoperative instructions, including wearing UV protective sunglasses during all waking hours until final lock-in treatment was complete, and told to follow up with her optometrist.

She returned one month later for her first LDD treatment. At this time, the patient reported strict adherence to her UV protective glasses. Evaluation revealed BCVA OD 20/25 with MRx of −0.75 + 0.50 x 120 and OS 20/30 with MRx of −1.50 + 0.50 x 90. There was a significant discrepancy in the refractions between the surgeon and comanaging optometrist. Due to the varying refractions, as well as the presence of mild posterior capsule opacification and capsule wrinkles, a YAG capsulotomy OU was performed, and the initial LDD treatment was postponed. She returned the following week with persistent fluctuation in refraction leading to further postponement of LDD treatment. She was instructed to begin lubricating drops to address all possible causes of her persistent blurry vision and variable refractions. Two weeks later, the refraction was still unstable and BCVA was OU 20/40 (near J10). The patient was subsequently referred to glaucoma, retina, and neuro-ophthalmology specialists for further evaluation to determine the etiology of her substantial refractive changes, as she still reported adherence to her UV protective glasses. Despite medical advice to return to her cataract surgeon, she did not return for follow-up for about seven months.

Once she returned to her cataract surgeon, her UCVA was count fingers OU and BCVA was OD 20/70 with MRx of −3.50 + 1.00 x 180 and OS 20/200 with MRx of-7.50 + 1.00 x 180. Hyperpolymerization of her lenses was noted centrally on slit lamp exam which was attributed to causing her blurry vision and varying MRx. At this time, the patient reported wearing her protective eyewear “most of the time” in outside activities. She also reported undergoing multiple tanning bed sessions with inconsistent use of UV protective eyewear during these sessions. Lens exchange was discussed and performed about one month later with Softport (Bausch & Lomb) 3-piece silicone lenses, which were sulcus fixated. Follow-up six weeks after the exchange revealed BCVA OU 20/20 with manifest refraction of OD -1.75 + 0.75 x 003 and OS -2.25 sphere.

Laboratory analyses

The LALs explanted from both eyes of the patient were submitted to the Intermountain Ocular Research Center for laboratory analyses. Both lenses underwent gross (macroscopic) evaluation, as well as a thorough evaluation under a light microscope (Olympus Optical Co., Ltd.) (Fig. 1). Examination revealed that both lenses had a distinct yellow optic color, but no opacification. Both also showed the presence of a round optic elevation of approximately 3.0-3.5 mm in diameter, which was slightly decentered. At the highest elevation point, the thickness of the optic was approximately 2.0 mm. Side view evaluation showed irregularity of the anterior and posterior surfaces of the lenses, with a notably convex anterior optic surface, and a plano to slightly concave posterior optic surface (Fig. 2).

(A-D). Anterior and side view macroscopic evaluation of explanted light adjustable lenses. Lens from right eye depicted in top row. Lens from left eye featured in bottom row.

The explanted lenses were immersed in distilled water for 24 hours and then underwent light transmission testing at room temperature using a Lambda 35 UV/Vis spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, Inc.) coupled with a Lab Sphere RSA-PE-20 integrating sphere. Mean light transmittance in the visible spectrum (700 to 400 nm; %T) was obtained from each explanted lens, as well as from a control, never implanted second-generation LAL. %T of the LALs explanted from OD and OS was 85.6% and 85.8%, respectively; it was 84.9% for the control LAL. Although both of the transmission curves of the explanted lenses did not overlap with the curve of the control LAL, all of them showed similar changes in the blue spectrum (approximately at 450 to 500 nm) similar to lenses containing a blue-light filter, in addition to the UV filter (blocking light at 400 nm and lower wavelengths) (Fig. 3). In Fig. 3, the light transmission curve of a control AcrySof Natural lens (Alcon), which contains a blue-light filter is shown for reference. Between 400 nm to approximately 450 nm, the shape of the curves of the 3 LALs was similar but significantly differed from that of the AcrySof Natural lens.

Light transmission curves obtained from both explanted LALs described in this study, a control, never implanted LAL of the same model, as well as an AcrySof Natural (Alcon) IOL for reference.

Wavefront aberrations of the explanted lenses were analyzed with the Nimo TR0815 instrument (Lambda X, Belgium), which displayed different aberrations and large power variations in both lenses. Discussion

Since LALs can be adjusted postoperatively, they are particularly beneficial in cases where preoperative IOL refractive calculations are less reliable, such as with prior refractive surgery (LASIK, photorefractive keratectomy, and radial keratotomy).4 Despite improvements in the formulas used to calculate the lens power in this patient population prior to surgery, fewer than 70% of eyes typically achieve a result within ±0.50 D of the intended target when a standard or toric lens is implanted.5,6 However, recent studies have shown enhanced refractive outcomes in eyes with prior refractive surgery after LAL implantation and LDD treatments. For example, Folden et al. reported 100% of eyes (n = 20) were within ±0.50 D of target and 95% of eyes had 20/20 or better UDVA.2 Jones et al. described 93% of eyes (n = 71) correcting to 20/20 or better and 86% of eyes (n = 65) were within ±0.50 D of refractive target.7 The improved VA and refractive outcomes reported in these studies depend on strict adherence to UV protective glasses until 24 hours after the final lock-in to prevent unintentional macromer polymerization as seen in this case.

The LDD emits UV light at a wavelength of 365 nm to change the refraction of the LAL. Despite the ActivShield present in the anterior portion of the lens optic, the refraction of LAL 2.0 can be manipulated by a wavelength of less than 400 nm because the concentration of UV light during LDD treatment surpasses the protective capacity of the ActivShield. During LDD treatment, the initial UV exposure reversibly alters the structure of and disables the ActivShield for as long as its shape remains compromised.8 Once the ActivShield has been momentarily disarmed, the macromers are vulnerable to the UV light and can be manipulated to change the refraction of the lens. The varying LDD treatment times (ranging from 8 seconds to 120 seconds)3 are what allows for ± 2D of postoperative refractive changes.

In a tanning bed, the UV light exposure is delivered in a short, highly concentrated burst comparable to LDD treatments. UVA (320–400 nm) exposure is estimated to be 10–15 times greater than typical outdoor levels.9,10 The overall UV intensity within a tanning bed is approximated to be a UV index of 12-13.9 In comparison, this is substantially higher than the midday summer sun at mid-latitudes, where the UV index typically peaks around 8.5.9 The precise amount of ocular UV radiation received within a tanning bed is difficult to quantify due to numerous variables, including facial anatomy, use of protective eyewear, tanning bed type, and duration of exposure. In the case reported here, it is difficult to precisely determine the extent to which the tanning bed sessions contributed to the observed changes in the LALs. A recently published case describes irregular astigmatism induced by presumed premature photopolymerization of a LAL with ActivShield exposed to ambient light over a 2-year period without postoperative light treatments.11 Therefore, we hypothesize that in the present case, combined UV exposure from both the tanning bed and ambient light contributed to the unintended structural changes of the LALs. The number and duration of tanning bed sessions reported by the patient in this present case could not be accurately obtained. It is noteworthy that for beginners, it is usually recommended to start slow, with two to three sessions during the first week lasting between three to 10 min, depending on skin type.12 In any event, the errant refractions observed in the present case before any LDD treatment were consistent with patterns previously reported in other cases of LAL explantation associated with noncompliance with UV-protective eyewear before final lock-in treatment.13,14

Though an extreme case, the analyses underscore the consequences of inadequate protection against UV light after LAL 2.0 implantation before the final lock-in treatment despite the presence of ActivShield. Additionally, this case emphasizes the importance of comprehensive patient counseling on minimizing UV light exposure postoperatively from both ambient and artificial sources, including, but not limited to, tanning beds, UV lamps used for gel manicures, and dermatologic phototherapy treatments, given their potential impact on refractive outcomes.

Further investigation is needed to determine whether a UV exposure threshold exists beyond which the ActivShield becomes permanently altered and unable to function as intended. Identifying and quantifying such a threshold could help mitigate the risk of unintended and irreversible postoperative refractive changes due to UV light exposure. Ultimately, this case highlights the ongoing importance of patient adherence to UV protective eyewear following LAL 2.0 implantation, particularly in patients exposed to abnormally high concentrations of UV light.
