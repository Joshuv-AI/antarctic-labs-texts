Consumptive coagulopathy and fatal outcome in hepatic hemangioma: a rare adult case of Kasabach–Merritt syndrome

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Kasabach–Merritt phenomenon (KMP) is a life-threatening consumptive coagulopathy that occurs in association with vascular lesions. Introduction

Hepatic hemangiomas are the most common benign liver tumors; most remain asymptomatic and require no treatment[1,2]. When exceeding 10 cm, they are classified as “giant” and may rarely trigger a consumptive coagulopathy analogous to Kasabach–Merritt phenomenon (KMP) – characterized by thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia[3,4]. Importantly, the term “Kasabach–Merritt Syndrome” (KMS) strictly refers to the coagulopathy associated with kaposiform hemangioendothelioma (KHE) or tufted angioma (TA). In adults with giant hepatic hemangiomas, the analogous syndrome is more precisely designated “KMP,” a distinction maintained throughout this report[3,5].

Adult presentations of KMP remain exceedingly sparse and pose a formidable diagnostic and therapeutic challenge[6,7]. The present case is distinguished by the rapidity of deterioration, the absolute refractoriness of thrombocytopenia to transfusion, and a fatal intracranial hemorrhage occurring during the preoperative stabilization period – prior to any definitive intervention.

Case report

Patient information and clinical background

A 42-year-old woman with no significant comorbidities, no chronic medications, and no personal or family history of hematological or liver disease presented with a 48-hour history of progressive right upper quadrant pain. She denied fever, jaundice, or weight loss. Surgical history included an umbilical hernia repair and two cesarean sections. She was a non-smoker and did not drink alcohol.

Physical examination

The patient was hemodynamically stable at presentation (BP 115/70 mmHg, HR 88 bpm, SpO₂ 98%, temperature 37.2 °C). Abdominal examination revealed a large, firm, tender, non-pulsatile** mass in the right hypochondrium. No stigmata of chronic liver disease, jaundice, or lymphadenopathy were present.

The differential diagnosis included KMP secondary to a giant hepatic hemangioma, DIC from an occult source, and thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP). The absence of fever, normal inflammatory markers, the absence of renal impairment, and the presence of a large vascular hepatic lesion strongly favored KMP. ADAMTS13 testing was unavailable in the acute setting.

Imaging

Triple-phase CT of the abdomen (Figs 1 and 2) demonstrated an 11 cm hepatic lesion in segments VII and VIII. The arterial phase showed peripheral nodular hyperenhancement; delayed phases demonstrated progressive centripetal fill-in, consistent with the classical pattern of giant cavernous hemangioma. Internal heterogeneity suggested intralesional thrombosis or hemorrhage. A small amount of pelvic free fluid was noted, with no other visceral abnormalities. Clinical timeline

On Day 0, the patient was admitted to the ICU following the diagnosis of a giant hepatic hemangioma with critical consumptive coagulopathy. She received six units of PRBC, eight units of FFP, and six pools of platelet concentrates; a transient platelet rise to approximately 18 000/mm3 was followed by a rapid decline to 4000/mm3 within hours. On Day 1, a multidisciplinary consultation with hematology, hepatobiliary surgery, and interventional radiology was obtained; surgical resection was planned but deferred given the extreme coagulopathy. Tranexamic acid was withheld due to the risk of thrombosis; corticosteroids were not administered. TAE was evaluated but deemed impractical given the patient's hemodynamic fragility. On Day 2, the patient suffered a sudden loss of consciousness; an emergency brain CT (Fig. 3) confirmed a massive spontaneous right parieto-occipital intracerebral hemorrhage. The patient died on Day 3. No autopsy was performed. Figure 3.Axial slice demonstrating a large, spontaneous right parieto-occipital lobar intracerebral hemorrhage. Arrow indicates the giant mass.

Discussion

KMP in adults with giant hepatic hemangiomas is rare but potentially lethal[1,9]. Unlike KHE or TA – where compact glomeruloid vascular architecture efficiently traps platelets – adult cavernous hemangiomas produce KMP through large-volume, sluggish flow within dilated sinusoids, explaining why clinically significant coagulopathy typically requires tumors exceeding 10 cm[3,5,6]. The mechanism involves platelet sequestration, local fibrin deposition, clotting factor consumption, and secondary fibrinolysis[2,5]. In this case, the full laboratory constellation – critical thrombocytopenia, hypofibrinogenemia, elevated D-dimer, prolonged coagulation times, and peripheral schistocytes – confirmed the diagnosis. The absence of histological confirmation, given the patient’s death and the lack of an autopsy, is an acknowledged limitation; the diagnosis of cavernous hemangioma rests on the classical triple-phase CT pattern.

The hallmark of this case was the absolute failure of transfusion to sustain platelet count correction. The transient platelet increment to 18 000/mm3 followed by a rapid return to 4000/mm3 typifies the consumptive sink phenomenon, in which the tumor continuously negates any transfused platelet increment[2,10]. This distinguishes KMP from immune-mediated or marrow-suppressive thrombocytopenia. Systemic hypocoagulability resulting from this process predisposes to remote spontaneous hemorrhage; intracranial hemorrhage represents its most lethal manifestation[9,10], as was fatally demonstrated in this patient.

Medical therapies (corticosteroids, vincristine) have limited evidence in adult hepatic KMP[11]. Tranexamic acid may theoretically interrupt secondary fibrinolysis but carries a risk of intra-tumoral thrombosis[5,11]. Transarterial embolization (TAE) is the most evidence-supported bridging strategy, reducing lesion vascularity to allow safer surgical resection[5]. Huang et al[1] documented the only directly comparable surviving adult case, managed successfully with TAE followed by resection. In the present case, TAE was considered but rejected due to extreme instability. The core dilemma in adult hepatic KMP is irresolvable through conventional transfusion: operating under extreme thrombocytopenia carries a prohibitive hemorrhagic risk, yet deferral allows the consumptive source to persist, increasing the risk of fatal extra-hepatic hemorrhage[11,12]. This case supports the principle that when thrombocytopenia is absolute and refractory, the window for any intervention closes with alarming speed.

Adult hepatic KMP is documented in fewer than 50 published cases. Huang et al and Sarkar et al reported survivable cases through TAE-assisted resection and conservative support, respectively[1,2]. Berrada et al and Valla et al documented fatal hemorrhagic outcomes despite aggressive management, consistent with the present case[4,10]. Key limitations include the absence of an autopsy, the unavailability of ADAMTS13 testing (precluding formal TTP exclusion), and the inability to formally exclude other causes of intracranial hemorrhage given the acute setting.

Conclusion

Adult KMP associated with a giant hepatic hemangioma is a rare and rapidly fatal surgical emergency.
