Giant Cardiac Glomangioma With Extensive Intravascular Extension

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac glomangioma is an exceptionally rare subtype of glomus body tumors, with most found in the dermis and soft tissues of the extremities. History of Presentation

A 42-year-old woman presented with progressive dyspnea on minimal exertion over several weeks. She denied chest pain, palpitations, syncope, or constitutional symptoms.

Past Medical History

The patient had no significant medical history and no known history of malignancy or vascular tumors.

Physical Examination

Physical examination was unremarkable. No peripheral edema, murmurs, or signs of systemic glomus tumors were identified.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included intracardiac myxoma, thrombus, sarcoma, metastatic tumor, intravenous leiomyomatosis with cardiac extension, and primary cardiovascular tumors.

Investigations

Chest radiography demonstrated cardiomegaly with mild bilateral pleural effusions. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a large right atrial mass extending toward the right ventricular outflow tract (Video 1). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) confirmed an intracardiac lesion impinging on the inferior vena cava (IVC) (Video 2) and identified a heterogeneous uterine lesion measuring 3.8 × 4.5 cm. Pelvic ultrasound suggested a uterine fibroid.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) demonstrated a large heterogeneous soft tissue mass occupying the right-sided cardiac chambers and extending through the IVC into the left common iliac vein (Video 3). The lesion showed complex signal characteristics and contrast enhancement patterns consistent with a neoplastic process, while also allowing detailed assessment of tumor extent, tissue composition, and its relationship to adjacent cardiac and vascular structures.

Management

Following multidisciplinary team discussion, urgent surgical intervention was undertaken. The procedure was performed via median sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass established using bicaval and aortic cannulation and systemic cooling to 32 °C. After aortic cross-clamping and cardioplegic arrest, the right atrium was opened, revealing a reddish blue, gelatinous yet firm mass occupying the right atrium and extending into the right ventricle, pulmonary artery, and IVC.

The tumor was excised en bloc (Figure 1, Video 4) with careful dissection to preserve surrounding cardiac structures. It measured approximately 42 cm in total length, extending proximally across the tricuspid valve into the right atrium and distally through the IVC into the left common iliac vein. No tumor invasion of the pulmonary artery was identified. The patient was successfully weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass without complications, extubated within 4 hours postoperatively, and discharged in stable condition on postoperative day 6.

Pathology

Histopathologic examination revealed a well-circumscribed lesion composed of dilated, thick-walled blood vessels surrounded by uniform glomus cells arranged in nests. The cells exhibited eosinophilic cytoplasm without nuclear atypia or mitotic activity, consistent with benign glomangioma (Figure 2).

Outcome and Follow-Up

Follow-up imaging at 6 months demonstrated complete tumor resection with no evidence of recurrence. The patient remained asymptomatic.

Discussion

Glomangiomas are benign vascular tumors arising from the glomus body, a thermoregulatory structure. These tumors are usually dermal or subcutaneous, particularly in subungual or acral locations.1 Visceral or cardiac glomangiomas are very rare. They originate from modified smooth muscle cells and are histologically categorized into 3 subtypes: solid glomus tumors (75%), glomangiomas (20%), and glomangiomyomas (5%).2

Cardiac glomangiomas are exceedingly rare, with only a few cases reported.2, 3, 4 Clinical presentation depends on size and location and can include dyspnea, palpitations,3 arrhythmias, heart failure, electrocardiogram abnormalities,3,4 or even stroke.4 Diagnostic work-up includes blood tests, echocardiography, CT, CMR, positron emission tomography, and angiography. Among these, CMR is the modality of choice owing to its superior tissue characterization and ability to delineate extracardiac involvement, which is crucial for surgical planning.

Surgical excision remains the cornerstone of treatment, serving both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Complete resection with clear margins is advised because of the risk of recurrence. In our case, cardioplegic arrest facilitated complete removal and inspection of the IVC. However, in some cases, complete excision may be technically unfeasible owing to involvement of critical cardiac structures.4 Massey et al5 have recommended the use of electroanatomical mapping to enhance the safety of right atrial tumor resection. However, this modality was not available at our institution because of resource limitations typical of a developing-world setting.

Glomangiomas may be multifocal, and differentiation between low- and high-grade variants can be challenging. In the present case, no additional lesions were identified, and the tumor demonstrated 2 discrete attachment sites along the right ventricular free wall. The mass extended distally into the right ventricular outflow tract and proximally across the tricuspid valve into the right atrium and IVC, reaching the left common iliac vein. This pattern of retrograde intravascular extension against venous flow is highly unusual.

Although preoperative biopsy is sometimes considered, we avoided it because of the risk of embolism and tumor seeding. Given the size and extent of the mass, urgent surgical removal was deemed necessary and life saving.

Conclusions

Cardiac glomangiomas, though rare and benign, can pose significant clinical challenges because of their size and extension. Complete surgical excision guided by preoperative imaging is essential for diagnosis, treatment, and reduction of recurrence risk. This case also underscores the importance of including glomangioma in the differential diagnosis of cardiac masses.
