Miniature Soft Robot With Magnetically Reprogrammable Surgical Functions
Authors: Chelsea Shan Xian Ng, Yu Xuan Yeoh, Nicholas Yong Wei Foo, Keerthana Radhakrishnan, Guo Zhan Lum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMagnetic miniature robots are untethered actuators, which can prospectively make existing minimally invasive surgery considerably safer and painless, and enable unprecedented treatments because they are much smaller and dexterous than existing surgical robots. However, such actuators are restricted to possessing at most two on‐board functionalities, having limited five degrees‐of‐freedom (DOF) locomotion, or are only operational under specialized environments where actuation from strong external magnets must be at very close proximity (<4 cm away). Here we present smart magnetic composites with deliberately engineered symmetry and heterogeneous material properties. Leveraging these composites, we construct a millimeter‐scale soft robot whose magnetization profile can be reprogrammed to dispense drugs, cut through biological tissues, grip and store biological samples, and heat remotely. By possessing full six‐DOF motions, including the sixth‐DOF rotation about its net magnetic moment, our soft robot can also roll and two‐anchor crawl across challenging unstructured environments, which are impassable by its five‐DOF counterparts. Because the applied magnetic fields are relatively uniform and weak (10–30 mT, 0.21–0.31 T/m for actuation; 60–65 mT for reprogramming), they can theoretically penetrate human tissues safely and enable actuation beyond 5 cm.

Introduction

Miniature robots are untethered actuators at the millimeter length scale or smaller [1, 2, 3]. Because these actuators are much smaller and more dexterous than existing macro‐scale surgical robots, they have significant potential to make minimally invasive surgery considerably safer and painless [4, 5, 6]. Furthermore, as miniature robots have greater access to highly confined and enclosed spaces in the human body than their macro‐scale counterparts [7, 8, 9], they can prospectively enable unprecedented minimally invasive treatments in the future too [10, 11, 12]. Of the miniature robots, the magnetically actuated ones are especially promising since they are known to be the most functional and dexterous [13, 14, 15]. Such attributes have been observed because unlike other classes of miniature robots that are actuated by heat [16], electricity [17], light [18], chemicals [19] and pressure [20], the actuating fields of these magnetic miniature robots can be specified not only in magnitude but also in their directions and spatial gradients [21, 22, 23]. Since magnetic fields can potentially penetrate through biological tissues harmlessly, such an actuation method is also compatible for the targeted medical applications of miniature robots [24, 25, 26].

There exist two classes of magnetic miniature robots. The first class possesses slime‐ or liquid‐based bodies, and these miniature robots can be actuated by external magnetic fields to actively deform their bodies and locomote [27, 28, 29]. However, the non‐solid bodies of these actuators do not have the necessary structural integrity to output sufficient forces for surgery and they may leave residues of their body in the environment [30, 31, 32]. Such attributes are generally undesirable for minimally invasive treatments [25]. While there exist slime‐ or liquid‐based robots that can stiffen their body via phase change [31] or under actuation by strong magnetic fields [32], such mechanisms have several critical drawbacks which render them impractical for surgery. Specifically, these actuators require very strong, non‐uniform magnetic fields (20–150 mT, 8–10 T/m) to stiffen up [32] or to reach a desired geometry before they are frozen [31]. Therefore, they can only operate in specialized environments where actuation from strong external magnets must be at close proximity (<4 cm away) [31, 32]. Again, such constraints are undesirable for surgery since miniature robots may have to access in‐depth regions within the human body for such treatments (>5 cm under the skin) [33]. For the slime‐based robots to induce their phase‐changing mechanisms, they must be heated beyond 60°C [31] and such temperatures may also cause cell death within the patients’ body [34, 35].

The second class of magnetic miniature robots have solid elastomeric bodies which do not leave residues of their body in the environment, and they can be actuated wirelessly from a distance (<30 cm) [36]. While such features are fundamentally compatible for medical treatments, it is challenging to expand the functionality of these elastomeric actuators [37, 38, 39]. These miniature robots generally experience uniform actuating fields across their body because generating spatially varying magnetic fields at millimeter or smaller scales is very challenging [40, 41, 42]. This uniformity imposes a critical constraint on the maximum degree of actuation achievable, thereby limiting their functional capabilities [43, 44, 45]. Another major limitation is that most magnetic miniature robots with elastomeric bodies have a fixed magnetization profile, which restricts the range of magnetic responses that they can produce [46, 47, 48]. Although some miniature robots can reprogram their magnetization profile via external re‐magnetizing fields [3, 49] or lasers [50, 51, 52], their magnetization and stiffness profiles are not designed to complement their reprogramming strategy, limiting their functional versatility. Additionally, laser‐based reprogramming is unsuitable for in situ treatments because laser light cannot penetrate human tissue [50, 51, 52]. Due to all the aforementioned constraints, existing magnetic miniature robots with elastomeric body are unable to realize more than two types of on‐board functionalities [7, 14, 40]. Moreover, most of these magnetic miniature robots can only achieve a maximum of five degrees‐of‐freedom (DOF) motions because they cannot control their sixth‐DOF rotation about the net magnetic moment [38, 45, 46]. Hence, while these actuators can translate along three axes, they can only rotate about two axes. [26, 41, 42] Without full six‐DOF motions, it is very challenging for magnetic miniature robots to negotiate across the unstructured environments in the human body [10, 21, 22]. Although there exist magnetic miniature robots with six‐DOF, these rare actuators are constrained with fixed magnetization profiles [10, 21, 22]. Thus, they can only grasp [21, 22] or dispense drugs [10]. It remains a great challenge to create magnetic miniature robots with elastomeric bodies that have more than two on‐board functions and full six‐DOF motions. If such actuators can be created, they would have great potential to transform future surgery.

Here we present smart magnetic composites which feature deliberately engineered, system‐level architectures that integrate multiple magnetic constituents with distinct coercivities, stiffnesses, and actuation bandwidths. The heterogeneous material architecture enables a broad range of magnetic responses. By incorporating constituents with differentiated coercivities, selected regions of the smart composites can be reversibly magnetized and demagnetized by magnetic fields (B⃗) of 60–65 mT. Other regions with higher magnetic coercivities will not be affected by such magnetizing and demagnetizing processes. The diversity of their magnetic constituents enables us to reprogram the smart composites’ magnetization profile on demand and generate multiple addressable magnetic states. Unlike existing small‐scale magnetic actuators [1, 40, 41], we exploit symmetry as a key material design principle. We program the magnetization and stiffness profiles to be symmetric across multiple cross‐sectional planes. This symmetry allows deterministic control of the magnetic moments in reprogrammable regions, enabling selective plane‐specific, magneto‐mechanical responses. These material design principles allow the smart composites’ magnetization and stiffness profiles to complement their reprogramming strategies and enhance their mechanical versatility. Selective constituents of the smart composites can also incorporate ferrimagnetic materials, such as Fe3O4, to generate heat remotely under high‐frequency alternating B⃗ (55–500 kHz) [53, 54]. This capability further extends the functional diversity of our smart magnetic composites, while decoupling thermal actuation from mechanical responses. Combining symmetry and heterogeneous material properties, we establish a generalizable strategy to reprogram smart magnetic composites with multifunctional behavior.

Using our material design principles and reprogramming strategy, we construct a millimeter‐scale soft robot from smart magnetic composites that can perform five surgical functions. It can dispense drugs, cut through biological tissues (gelatin or chicken liver), grip, store biological samples and heat remotely. By possessing full six‐DOF motions, our soft robot can also negotiate across challenging unstructured environments, which are impassable by its five‐DOF counterparts [3, 47, 49]. Consequently, our actuator can realize far more dexterous locomotion and greater functionalities than existing magnetic miniature robots [37, 39, 42]; current five‐ and six‐DOF miniature robots are limited to at most two and one mechanical functionalities, respectively [10, 52, 55]. Unlike those with slime‐ or liquid‐based bodies [28, 30, 31], our soft robot's elastomeric body also has the necessary structural integrity to potentially output sufficient forces for surgery and it will not leave body residuals in the environment (Section S1A,B). These features make the soft robot fundamentally compatible for biomedical applications [56].

To evaluate the safety aspects of our soft robot, we have also subjected it to material biocompatibility tests similar to those demonstrated by Jiang et al. [57] and Yang et al. [10]. In these tests, we evaluated if anonymous human dermal fibroblast cells would remain viable when they were exposed to our soft robot. On average, 99.08% and 99.53% of the anonymous human dermal fibroblast cells remained viable following exposure to our soft robot and under control conditions, respectively (Section S1B). Since the viability rate was higher than 98% and they were similar to the viability rate of a control group, our soft robot is therefore biocompatible (Section S1B) [10]. Our magnetic constituents are inherently secure, as the cured elastomeric matrix effectively immobilizes the embedded magnetic particles, preventing particle leakage [1, 10, 58]. Our experiments confirmed these outcomes consistently. Nevertheless, it is theoretically feasible to further prevent particle leakage by coating the external surfaces of our soft robot with biocompatible thin films [10, 59]. Such films will provide an additional barrier to secure the embedded particles and we wish to explore such investigations in the future. These thin films can potentially also enhance the biocompatibility of the soft robot and make it less adhesive (Section S1B). For proof‐of‐concept purposes, here we have minimized the surface energy of the soft robot by conducting most of the experiments in oil‐based environments.

Our soft robot has a main body, two soft tentacles, a reprogrammable module, and a remote heating component (Figure 1a; Section S1A). Within the main body, there are three soft inner beams that are arranged in a rotary symmetrical configuration (Figure 1b). These inner beams can be selectively activated to dispense drugs, cut, grip and store objects via undergoing active deformation (Figure 1e–g). By applying our material design principles, we arrange the soft robot's tentacles in a symmetrical configuration too. The magnetization profiles within the soft robot's main body and tentacles are pre‐programmed while the magnetic moment of the reprogrammable module can be magnetized or demagnetized upon command (Figure 1b). Our remote heating component contains embedded Fe3O4 particles and exhibits negligible magnetization magnitude. However, it can generate induction heating when an alternating B⃗ of high frequency (amplitude: 9.34 mT, frequency: 75.4 kHz) is applied (Section S1A). Therefore, the remote heating component is placed at the top of our soft robot to better facilitate potential hyperthermia treatments (Figure 1a). To elaborate on the magnetic and stiffness properties of the actuator, we have assigned a material coordinate frame to the soft robot where all the axes in this frame are denoted with the subscript: {M} (Figure 1ci‐ii). The actuator's soft tentacles are magnetized along the Y {M}‐axis and their magnetization profile is symmetrical about the Z {M}‐axis (Figure 1cii). They are also designed to be compliant about the X {M}‐axis but stiff about other axes (Figure 1cii). The free ends of the inner beams are magnetized along their corresponding length (described as mini magnets in Figure 1bi), and these beams are designed to be compliant about the Z {M}‐axis but stiff about other axes (Figure 1cii). Depending on the magnetic moment of the reprogrammable module, the proposed soft robot can operate under several modes: its locomotion or specific function modes. When the reprogrammable module is demagnetized, the soft robot will assume its locomotion mode such that it can actively control the deformation of the soft tentacles to adopt different gaits (Figure 1ciii–iv; Video S1). The soft robot can also be quickly reprogrammed to a specific function mode by magnetizing its reprogrammable module with a uniform B⃗ of 60 mT (<1 s) (Figure 2aii; Video S1 and Section S1C). To revert to the locomotion mode, the reprogrammable module can be demagnetized quickly (<1 s) via an alternating B⃗ of 45 Hz, which has a linearly decaying amplitude (Figure 2aiv,vii,x; Video S1 and Section S1C). The presented reprogramming B⃗ can theoretically penetrate through the human body harmlessly (Section S7A). Hence, the proposed reprogramming strategy is viable to be implemented on our actuators in enclosed spaces, and such features are compatible for biomedical applications (Section S7A).

During the reprogramming processes, our soft robot will remain stationary and not produce uncontrollable motions (Video S1). This is a critical feature because the actuator must remain controllable by surgeons during treatments. At present, the experiments in Video S1 are conducted in static environments with no fluid flows. In the future, we aim to incorporate anchoring mechanisms [60] into our soft robot to enable more reliable execution of its reprogramming strategy in environments with dynamic fluid flow and vortexes, such as the gastrointestinal tract. The magnetization profile within the main body and tentacles of our soft robot will not be re‐magnetized during the reprogramming processes because these components have magnetic coercivities of 93.3–614 mT (Section S1A), which are higher than those of our reprogramming |B⃗| (60–65 mT). Once magnetized, the reprogrammable module will have a functional magnetization magnitude of 5.18 kA/m but its magnetization magnitude will be reduced by 16.8‐fold and become negligible upon demagnetization (Figure S3b and Section S1A).

In its locomotion mode, the soft robot's tentacles will bend about their compliant X {M}‐axis to deform into either an upright or inverted ‘U’‐shape when B⃗ is applied along the Z Loco,{L}‐axis (denoted as B⃗Z in Figure 1ciii‐iv; Section S2A). Once the soft robot deforms, its physics can be described by a local coordinate frame, where all the axes in this local frame are denoted with the subscript: Loco,{L} (Figures 1ciii and 3ai). The axes of this frame coincide with those of the material coordinate frame. The tentacles will deform because the dipoles in their magnetization profile will tend to align with the applied B⃗Z (Figure 1ciii‐iv), generating a distributed torque about their compliant axis [21, 22]. The interaction between B⃗Z and the magnetic moments of the inner beams will also generate torques at their free ends (Figure 1ciii‐iv). Two of these inner beams will experience torque about their X Loco,{L}‐ and Y Loco,{L}‐axes while one of them will only experience torque about the X Loco,{L}‐axis (Figure 1ciii). However, since these beams are designed to be stiff about these axes, they will produce negligible deformations under such actuation. In general, the soft robot's symmetric configuration ensures that only the tentacles are actuated in the locomotion mode. The deformed tentacles will endow the soft robot with a net magnetic moment, which is parallel to the Z Loco,{L}‐axis (Figure 1ciii‐iv and Section S2). The inner beams do not contribute to the actuator's net magnetic moment as they are arranged in a rotary symmetrical configuration (Figure 1b). Likewise, as the reprogrammable module is demagnetized in the locomotion mode, it does not contribute to the actuator's net magnetic moment. Because the actuator's net magnetic moment aligns perfectly with the applied B⃗, it will not rotate as its tentacles deform into an upright or inverted ‘U’‐shape, which is its minimum potential energy configuration (Section S2B). Since the soft robot will always assume a minimum potential energy configuration upon actuation, similar deformation outcomes will occur even when the magnetic fields are not applied perfectly along the Z Loco,{L}‐axis (Section S2B). For such scenarios, the soft robot will deform and rotate concurrently until the tentacles reach an upright or inverted ‘U’‐shape and its net magnetic moment is aligned with the applied B⃗ (Section S2B).

When the soft robot is reprogrammed to its function modes, its tentacles and inner beams can deform upon actuation (Section S5). Similar to the locomotion mode, the physics of the soft robot can be described by a spatial, local coordinate frame after it deforms in the function modes. The axes in this local coordinate frame are denoted with the subscript, Func,{L} (Figure 1d), and they coincide with those of the material coordinate frame. This local frame can be used across all the function modes. By summing up the magnetic moments of these deformed components with those of the reprogrammable module, the soft robot will eventually possess a net magnetic moment that aligns with the applied B⃗ (Figure 1d). Both the actuator's net magnetic moment and B⃗ will always be between the Z Func,{L}‐axis and the X Func,{L}‐Y Func,{L} plane when they align (Figure S9bii,cii). Once aligned, the soft robot will not rotate when its compliant components deform, and this enables us to analyze the deformation of the actuator and its locomotion separately (Section S2).

In general, the symmetrical configuration of the soft robot ensures that, in each respective function mode, only the tentacles and a pair of inner beams can be actuated. To explain how the soft robot deforms, we first decompose the applied B⃗ into two components (Figure 1d; Figure S9): the components (i) along the Z Func,{L}‐axis (B⃗Z) and (ii) along the magnetic moment of the reprogrammable module (B⃗Func). The interaction between B⃗Z and the tentacles’ magnetization profile will generate distributed torques about the compliant X Func,{L}‐axis of the tentacles and these torques will deform the tentacles into either an upright or inverted ‘U’‐shape (Figure 1ciii–iv; Section S2A). A pair of inner beams will also bend about the Z Func,{L}‐axis. This is because the magnetic moments located at their free ends will always tend to align with the applied B⃗Func, and this will in turn induce magnetic torques about the compliant axis of the beams to deform them (Section S2A). Due to the magnetization and stiffness profiles of the inner beams, the actuation of B⃗Z will have negligible effects on these beams. Similarly, the magnetization and stiffness profiles of the tentacles are designed in such a way that they will not produce significant deformations due to B⃗Func. To further explain the deformations of the beams under different function modes, we define an angle φ to describe the direction of the magnetic moment of the reprogrammable module (Figure 1bii). When this component is magnetized along the axes where φ is equal to 90°, 330° or 210°, our soft robot is reprogrammed to its drug‐dispensing, cutting or gripping/storage modes, respectively (Figure 1b). Using the cutting mode as an example, the soft robot can be actuated by a B⃗Func of 15 mT to deform a pair of inner beams which will in turn push out a retractable cutting tool (Figure 1fii and 2aiii; Video S1 and Section S1C). Similarly, our soft robot can also be reprogrammed to activate its drug‐dispensing, gripping and storage functions (Figures 1e,g, 2av–vi,viii–ix; Video S1 and Section S1C). Like its locomotion mode, the soft robot will eventually assume a minimum potential energy configuration even when its net magnetic moment is initially not perfectly aligned with the applied B⃗ under the function modes (Section S2B). In such cases, the soft robot produces similar deformation outcomes but also rotates concurrently until its net magnetic moment aligns with B⃗ (Section S3B). To further evaluate the deformation of the beams in the actuator's main body visually, we also created a replica of our soft robot in which its main body was placed above the remote heating component (Figures S14–S16). In theory, when this replica is actuated, its motions and deformation will be similar to those of the soft robot in Figure 1e–g. In Figures S14–S16, we showed that this replica could either open the drug‐dispenser's doors, protrude the cutting tool or extend the grippers, depending on the function mode to which it is reprogrammed. Such experimental outcomes agreed with our theoretical predictions (Figure 1e–g; Section S2), confirming that the soft robot could be actuated as anticipated. Except for Figure 2; Video S1, we document all our actuating magnetic fields in Figures S30–S47. This is because it is very challenging to draw such 3D temporal vectors and their spatial gradients on experimental images like those in Figure 3g. By removing these actuating fields, we can also illustrate our actuator's motions in the figures of the main text and videos clearer.

To evaluate if the soft robot can continuously be reprogrammed from one mode to another reliably, we subjected it to 100 cycles of switching tests (Figure 2b). Each cycle involved magnetizing and then demagnetizing the soft robot's reprogrammable module. After every 20 cycles of such switching tests, the soft tentacles and inner beams of the robot were actuated by corresponding B⃗Z and B⃗Func of 9 and 18 mT to evaluate their deformations. Under the drug‐dispensing, cutting and gripper/storage modes, the shortest distance between the doors of the drug chamber (a Disp, Figure 2bi), extension of the cutting tool (a Cut, Figure 2bii) and shortest distance between the grippers (a Grip, Figure 2biii) were measured, respectively (Figure S13). For the locomotion mode, we measured the amplitude of the soft robot's deformed tentacles (a Loco, Figure 2biv; S24e). In Figure 2b, n represented the number of cycles, and the results showed that the soft robot produced consistent deformations throughout 100 cycles of switching tests. This implied that our soft robot could indeed reliably switch between its function and locomotion modes with no observable deterioration in terms of its mechanical performance. The switching tests also suggested that the magnetic remanences of our soft robot's tentacles and inner beams had negligible reduction after continuous reprogramming, as similar deformations were observed throughout these tests. Consequently, the magnetic properties of our soft robot remained unchanged after these switching tests. To evaluate the durability and fatigue aspects of our soft robot, we also deformed its soft tentacles and inner beams continuously for 200 times using corresponding B⃗Z and B⃗Func of 15 mT (Figure S24a–d; Section S7C). After performing these actuations, the deformation magnitudes of the soft tentacles and inner beams were observed to remain consistent. This suggested that our soft robot had negligible wear and it showed no signs of fatigue after 200 cycles of actuation (Figure S24a–d).

When the soft robot is reprogrammed to the locomotion mode, its net magnetic moment will increase when the tentacles deform more, and there is a positive correlation between the tentacles’ curvature and the magnitude of the applied B⃗ (Sections S2A, S3A). As the soft robot's net magnetic moment will always tend to align with B⃗, this actuator will be able to generate two axes of rotation via controlling the direction of B⃗ (e.g., magnitude: 15 mT in Figure 3a; Video S2 and Sections S2B and S4A). However, to rotate about the soft robot's net magnetic moment (i.e., the sixth‐DOF axis) [21, 22, 23], appropriate magnetic spatial gradients must be applied (Figure 3b; Video S2 and Sections S2B and S4A). Our soft robot could achieve angular velocities of 1.56–16.5 rad/s about its three independent axes (Figure 3a,b; Figure S16a and Video S2 and Section S4A). By applying other magnetic spatial gradients (0.21–0.31 T/m), the soft robot could also translate along three independent axes with velocities of 0.126–0.911 mm/s (Figure 3c; Video S2 and Section S4A). The experiments in Figure 3a–c showed that our actuator had six‐DOF motions since it could produce three independent axes of rotations and translations [21, 22, 23]. To levitate the soft robot against gravity in an oil‐based environment, a buoyant component was added to this actuator to increase its buoyancy (Figure 3c; Video S2 and Section S4A). The buoyant component was required due to the limitations of our electromagnetic coil system (Section S1D), and not by the working principles of our soft robot (Section S4A).

Our soft robot can roll and two‐anchor crawl in its locomotion mode. Rolling is one of the fastest modes of terrestrial locomotion [1, 8, 22]. Because our soft robot had six‐DOF, it could choose to roll along its length or width and steer its rolling direction via controlling its sixth‐DOF angular displacement (Figure 3d,e; Video S3 and Section S4B). The soft robot could achieve speeds of 1.29–6.75 mm/s with this gait (a rotating B⃗ of 20 mT and 0.1–1 Hz, Figure 3k,l; Section S4B). Rolling along the width of the actuator was slower than rolling along its length but its smaller rolling radius could allow the soft robot to squeeze through narrower openings (Figure 3f,k–l,). Our soft robot is therefore very robust; it could roll along its length to quickly maneuver across obstacle‐free terrains, or it could roll along its width if it wished to overcome barriers with narrow openings (Figure 3f; Video S3 and SI Section S4B). A large deviation in speed was observed when our soft robot rolls at 1 Hz as it started to slip. The no‐slip boundary condition might have been violated at that frequency because the soft robot was unable to induce sufficient friction from the substrate (Figure 3k). In Figure 3l, the soft robot's rolling velocity did not have a positive correlation with the actuating field's frequency (f Roll) beyond 0.75 Hz when it rolled in an oil environment because it had reached its step out frequency. Since air is less viscous than oil, our soft robot did not reach its step out frequency across the entire range of f Roll when rolling in air. As a result, our soft robot's rolling velocity had a positive correlation across the entire range of f Roll in air (Figure 3l). Our soft robot could also two‐anchor crawl by alternating the free ends of its tentacles as anchors (Figure 3g; Figure S20 and Video S4 and Section S4C). This gait could be activated by rotating B⃗ about the soft robot's X Loco,{L}‐axis with varying magnitudes between 8 and 22 mT (Figure S20; Section S4C). Based on this gait, the soft robot could achieve speeds of 0.4–1.06 mm/s (Figure 3m; Section S4C). Because our soft robot had six‐DOF and its stride length could be precisely controlled via the two‐anchor crawling locomotion, it could steer its crawling direction, climb up a slope and move across obstacles with strict shape constraints (Figure 3g–j; Video S4 and Section S4C). By having such high dexterity, our soft robot will have great potential to negotiate across the highly unstructured environments within the human body.

After our soft robot reaches its destination via the locomotion mode, it can be reprogrammed to a desired surgical function mode. While our soft robot is unable to execute the two‐anchor crawling locomotion in its function modes, it can still realize rolling or spin‐walking gaits with six‐DOF (Sections S2B and S5C). Rolling is faster than spin‐walking, but the latter gait can allow the soft robot to maintain an upright orientation during locomotion. As it is advantageous to analyze the locomotion of a six‐DOF miniature robot when its net magnetic moment is aligned to an axis in the local coordinate frame, we have introduced additional spatial coordinate frames for each function mode. These coordinate frames are denoted by the subscripts, Disp,{S}, Cut,{S} and Grip,{S}, which correspond to the drug‐dispensing, cutting and gripping/storage modes of our soft robot, respectively. The detailed mapping between the local and spatial coordinate frames of our soft robot is explained in SI Sections S2B and S3B and shown in Figure S12.

When our soft robot was reprogrammed to its drug‐dispensing mode, it could roll to a targeted location and dispense a simulated solid drug (Figure 4a; Video S5 and Section S5A). If the soft robot is required to deliver liquid medicine, such drugs can potentially be stored in a biodegradable capsule so that the actuator can deliver this solid cargo to the targeted location [9]. From the medical perspective, it is highly advantageous to deliver drugs with miniature robots because these actuators can potentially increase the delivery efficiency by 78.6‐fold compared to traditional delivery modes that transport the drugs via the human's circulatory system [10, 44, 61]. The maximum capacity of drugs that our soft robot could carry is theoretically sufficient for targeted drug delivery treatments as the amount of drugs that it could carry was twofold of what was required for practical targeted chemotherapy applications (Section S5A) [44].

By reprogramming the soft robot to its cutting mode, it can potentially perform incisions to remove tumors or unwanted tissues. A retractable cutting tool concealed in the soft robot's main body could protrude when a B⃗ of 18–22 mT was applied (Figure 4bi). To characterize the soft robot's cutting performance in a repeatable manner, the chicken liver tissues were shaped into a cylinder via a biopsy punch (Figure 4b; Video S6). By rotating B⃗ about the X Cut,{S}‐axis, the soft robot would produce a corresponding rotation until its cutting tool established contact with the liver tissues (Figure 4bii; Video S6). Upon contact, the cutting tool would begin to stretch the liver tissue, eventually cutting through a portion of the liver (Figure 4biii–iv; Video S6). The extent of tissue stretching (l Str) and the cutting depth (l Cut) at which the liver tissues ruptured were measured (Figure 4d). In general, these experimental results indicated that our soft robot could generate sufficient pressure for its cutting tool to make incisions on biological tissues. We also investigated the feasibility of using primarily magnetic forces for our actuator to penetrate through gelatin, which was derived from biological tissues (applied magnetic spatial gradients: 1.5 T/m in Figure 4c; Video S6 and Section S5B). In this experiment, we first protruded the soft robot's cutting tool by applying a B⃗Func of 29.2 mT (Figure 4ci‐ii; Video S6 and Section S5B). Subsequently, magnetic spatial gradients of 1.5 T/m were applied to translate the soft robot until it established contact and eventually penetrate through the gelatin (Figure 4ciii‐iv; Video S6 and Section S5B). From the actuation perspective, although it is also possible to cut biological tissues via such magnetic forces, it might be easier for our soft robot to perform incisions with B⃗ of 18–22 mT than with magnetic spatial gradients of 1.5 T/m. In the future, we aim to investigate if the soft robot can cut more effectively by actuating it with both B⃗ and magnetic spatial gradients concurrently. A notable advantage of our cutting mechanism is that the retractable cutting tool can be safely concealed within the soft robot's body.

Our soft robot can also be reprogrammed to enable gripping functions; a pair of grippers would extend out of the soft robot's main body when a B⃗Func of 25 mT was applied (Figure 5a‐b). We could control the grippers accurately because we had characterized their extensions with respect to different |B⃗Func| (Sections S3B, and S5C). As a demonstration of our soft robot's gripping function (Video S7), we commanded it to pick up a piece of chicken liver (Figure 5a) and a Polyvinyl Chloride (PVC) object separately (Figure S22a). Subsequently, these cargos were transported to their targeted locations via the spin‐walking locomotion (Figures 5a; Figure S22a and Video S7). Using its grippers, our soft robot could also lift a 3.11 mg PVC object (B⃗Func of 10–28 mT, Figure S21c). Since our actuator had a normally‐closed gripping mechanism, increasing |B⃗Func| would generally cause the mechanism to open further, thereby loosening its grip. The gripping mechanism can potentially assist an incision procedure by adjusting a partially carved‐out tissue to a more favorable orientation before resuming the cutting process. This can be an important step during incision since it can help surgeons to avoid removing healthy tissues from the patients. Furthermore, the gripping function could also allow our soft robot to pick up and subsequently encase the excised tissue (Video S7), which was simulated with chicken liver tissues (Figure 5bi–iii) or a PVC object (Figure S22bi–iii). After the excised tissue was stored within the actuator's main body, the soft robot could be reprogrammed to its locomotion mode again (Figure 5biii–iv; Figure S22biii‐iv and Video S7). This ensured that the gripping function would not be accidentally activated to release the encased tissue, and that the soft robot could safely store and transport this sample out for biopsy. Since our soft robot could grasp, store and transport the chicken liver (10–30 mT, Figure 5b), this indicated that it could manipulate biological tissues effectively. The volume of samples that our soft robot could carry was theoretically also sufficient for biopsy purposes; for example, our PVC object's volume in Figure S22b were 1.78‐fold of those required for deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) extraction and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification (Section S5C) [16]. We have used the PVC object as a reference because the volume and weight of this structured object can be more accurately determined compared to those of the unstructured chicken liver tissues. To evaluate the soft robot's grasping stability, we commanded it to grasp a piece of chicken liver while subjected to an airflow of 7.81–8.05 m/s for 1 min. Throughout the experiment, the soft robot was able to grip on the liver securely (Figure S22c). For this experiment, the soft robot's tentacles were adhered to the substrate because our main objective was to evaluate if the actuator can maintain its grip against flow. A similar experiment was conducted on the soft robot to assess if it could transport objects stably. In this experiment, the soft robot was able to roll against an airflow of 6.90–7.10 m/s when actuated by a rotating B⃗ of 20 mT and 0.5 Hz, while stably encasing the chicken liver in its storage compartment (Figure S22d).

Our actuator's remote heating function can be executed across all its locomotion or function modes (Section S5D). To enable this heating function, alternating B⃗ of high frequency was applied to the soft robot (amplitude: 9.34 mT, frequency: 75.4 kHz). Because the frequency of the actuating signals was too high for the soft robot to respond and produce any motion, we could effectively decouple the actuator's heating function from its locomotion via controlling the frequency of the applied B⃗. As demonstrated in Figure 5c and Video S8, our soft robot could remain stationary while its temperature was raised from 26.3°C to 40°C within 35 s to trigger a color change in a thermochromic dye at 40°C (Section S5D). It is important to be able to raise the local temperature of a surface to 40°C as this is a necessary requirement to execute a wide range of hyperthermia treatments [34, 35]. As our soft robot is only heated to 40°C, such temperatures will allow numerous drugs used for chemotherapy to remain chemically stable and pharmacologically effective [62, 63]. In general, it is also possible to engineer other types of chemotherapy drugs such that they remain viable at 40°C [63]. To characterize the heat transfer properties of our soft robot, we created a replica and coated it with a similar thermochromic dye (Figure S25a). An alternating B⃗ of 9.34 mT and 75.4 kHz was applied to activate its heating function. The initial temperature of the replica was 24°C and the experiments were conducted in air. After 5: 04 ± 0: 15 min, the thermochromic dye on the replica's remote heating component turned pink, indicating that this component had reached 40°C (Figure S25b). When the heating function continued for 30 min, the dye on the main body and reprogrammable module of the soft robot remained blue, indicating that these regions did not reach 40°C throughout the experiment (Figure S25c,d). This observation suggested that heating was largely confined to the remote heating component. These experiments were repeated 3 times with consistent outcomes. In aquatic environments, where convective heat transfer is higher, even less heat would be expected to spread from the remote heating component to the rest of the soft robot. We also evaluated the soft robot's capability to provide targeted, localized heating on a substrate coated with thermochromic ink by observing the heat distribution within the substrate (Section S5D). In these experiments, the remote heating component of our soft robot was brought into contact with the substrate and an alternating B⃗ of 9.34 mT and 75.4 kHz was applied for 15 min. Experiments were conducted in air, with an initial temperature of 24°C for both the environment and the soft robot. During heating, we tracked the area on the substrate that reached 27°C or higher at 2.5‐min intervals. While this circular area gradually expanded, its radius, r, always remained within 5.78 mm after 15 min (Figure 5e). These experiments were repeated for five trials, yielding consistent results. In a control experiment, we coated a thermochromic dye on the soft robot's remote heating component. The dye turned from blue to pink after 5: 04 ± 0: 15 min, indicating that the remote heating component had reached 40°C. The control experiments were repeated with three trials with consistent outcomes. Overall, these experiments demonstrated that our soft robot was able to provide localized heating to the substrate. Even after the remote heating component reached 40°C, only a radius of 5.78 mm around the robot exceeded 27°C after 15 min, indicating precise thermal confinement. This localized heating capability is promising for applications such as hyperthermia treatments [34, 35].

The potential of deploying our soft robot for surgery was demonstrated via in vitro biological phantoms (Figure 6; Videos S9 and S10 and Section S6). In its locomotion mode, the six‐DOF soft robot could roll across unstructured environments (Figure 6ai‐ii; Video S9 and Section S6A). Once the soft robot reached its destination, it could be reprogrammed to dispense simulated drugs at another targeted location (Figure 6aiii; Video S9 and Section S6A). Subsequently, the soft robot could be reprogrammed to its locomotion mode again so that it could two‐anchor crawl toward a synthetic tumor (Figure 6aiv; Video S9 and Section S6A). By reprogramming to its cutting mode, the soft robot could then perform an incision to remove this tumor (Figure 6av; Video S9 and Section S6A). To transport the excised tumor out for biopsy, the soft robot could be reprogrammed to its gripping mode to pick up and encase the biological sample within its main body (Figure 6avi‐vii; Video S9 and Section S6A). Finally, our soft robot could assume its locomotion mode and execute the two‐anchor crawling gait to exit the phantom by backtracking to the entrance (Figure 6aviii; Video S9 and Section S6A). Likewise, the soft robot could also roll to a desired location and perform hyperthermia treatments (simulated by the color change of a thermochromic dye at 40°C in Figure 6b; Video S10 and Section S6B). These demonstrations showed that our six‐DOF soft robot could navigate well in unstructured terrains, and it possessed unprecedented functionalities for magnetic miniature robots with elastomeric bodies; specifically, such existing actuators have at most two on‐board functions [7, 22, 55] and most of them possess limited five‐DOF motions [16, 37, 42].

Discussion

A notable advantage of our soft robot is that it has great potential to be wirelessly controlled while it operates within the depths of the human body (>5 cm under the skin). This is because our soft robot generally requires only 10–30 mT and 0.21–0.31 T/m to realize its locomotion and mechanical functions, while 60–65 mT is needed for reprogramming (Figures S30–S47 and Sections S4–S6). Although creating an electromagnetic coil system with such actuation capabilities is still challenging, it is theoretically possible. As an example, the actuation system of Sikorski et al. can generate magnetic fields of 20–80 mT and 0.6–1.0 T/m with a workspace of 78.3 cm by mounting their electromagnets on the end‐effector of a robotic arm [36, 64]. These electromagnets can also be placed at least five centimeters away from the actuators [36, 64]. As a result, our soft robot is theoretically not constrained to lab‐based environments where actuation from external magnets must be at very close proximity like those with slime‐ or liquid‐based bodies (< cm away) [31, 32]. For this first study, we used a lab‐based electromagnetic coil system for proof‐of‐concept purposes (Section S1D). This coil system uses the OctoMag configuration, which is known to be able to generate uniform fields [11]. It can generate a 90% homogenous field across a workspace of 1.6 cm × 1.6 cm × 1.6 cm (Section S1D) [21, 22]. We also employed a pair of electromagnetic coils arranged in a Helmholtz configuration to generate B⃗ of 65 mT from a distance of 5.5 cm (Figure S7). This experiment demonstrated that our soft robot could indeed be actuated and reprogrammed by external electromagnets positioned 5 cm away. In the future, we aim to use similar strategies like those presented by Sikorski et al. [36, 64] and use multiple robotic arms to carry our electromagnets so that we can create an actuation system that is more compatible for biomedical applications. Such electromagnetic actuation systems can generate 65 mT from a distance of 10 cm [36, 64]. Our calculations also indicate that the magnetic fields used to actuate and reprogram the soft robot are theoretically safe for patients (Section S7A). In the context of magnetic miniature robots, 30 mT represents a relatively large range of magnetic field strength because most of them operate within a field strength range of 1–30 mT [1, 11, 38]. In our work, we use field strengths of 10–30 mT to drive the soft robot's locomotion and surgical functions, while higher field strengths of 60 –65 mT are applied to reprogram its operating modes. This demonstrates a clear distinction between actuation and reprogramming purposes. Likewise, the soft tentacles and inner beams will not be re‐magnetized until 93.3 mT, providing a substantial safety margin over the 65 mT used for reprogramming.

In this study, we observe our soft robot's motions via a standard camera. However, we can potentially use imaging techniques such as ultrasound, X‐radiation (X‐rays), computed tomography (CT) scans and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to observe its position and orientation when it is operating within the enclosed human body [1, 4, 5, 7, 14, 24, 25]. Of these imaging techniques, ultrasound is especially promising because this imaging technique can complement well with magnetic actuation, showing minimal interference between them [1, 14]. As a proof‐of‐concept demonstration, we showed that our soft robot could be imaged via ultrasound during actuation. In these experiments, an ultrasound probe (128‐element linear probe, 0.3 mm pitch) was positioned above a water reservoir containing our soft robot. The probe generated sound waves with a wavelength of 0.197 mm, and used transducers with a 100 MHz sampling rate to detect reflected waves. The central frequency of the probe was 7.5 MHz. This experimental setup enabled cross‐sectional imaging of our soft robot as it rolled (Video S11). Five trials were conducted, during which the soft robot was actuated by a rotating B⃗ of 20 mT at 0.05 Hz. These experimental results demonstrated the feasibility of concurrently using magnetic actuation and ultrasound imaging on our soft robot. We note, however, that these experiments were preliminary, and the ultrasound setup could be optimized to generate images of much higher resolutions, as reported in previous studies [1]. If the soft robot is operating in deep tissues, X‐rays and CT scans can be ideal alternatives too as such imaging techniques can easily penetrate through such tissues [4, 5, 7]. Future research is warranted to integrate such imaging techniques with magnetic actuation, while minimizing potential interference between them [1, 14]. Using such imaging techniques, we also aim to automate our soft robot to further enhance its speed and positioning accuracy, which may be currently limited by our human operator's manual control. To accomplish such future in vivo automations, we can implement closed‐loop control with prospective algorithms like model predictive control and dynamic programming algorithms [65, 66]. Path‐planning algorithms, such as the A* algorithm, can potentially be implemented on our soft robot too [67]. Using such algorithms, the actuator may be able to avoid obstacles as it navigates through the human body to administer treatments in the future [67]. We aim to explore these automation strategies during future in vivo applications of the soft robot. In the future, we also aim to create a master‐slave system for surgeons to control the proposed actuators because such systems have proven to be highly effective for the operation of macro‐scale surgical robots [68].

For our initial study, the soft robot operates in low‐viscosity environments such as air and oil for most of its experiments. Under similar actuation conditions, its locomotion speed is theoretically expected to decrease as the viscosity of the surrounding medium increases [69]. This is due to the inverse relationship between the actuator's speed and the viscosity of the environment [69]. While higher viscosity does reduce the soft robot's speed, the actuator can theoretically still remain mobile in such viscous environments [70]. One potential approach to compensate for the reduced speed is to increase the magnitude of the actuating B⃗ (e.g., from the current 10–30mT to 20–40 mT). We aim to investigate this possibility more thoroughly in the future. Although our soft robot has not been deployed for medical treatments in the human body yet, it has been characterized in environments that are as realistic and challenging as possible. For example, our soft robot has been deployed into a synthetic phantom that is based on the geometry of a human stomach (Figure 6) and it can roll against an airflow of 6.90–7.10 m/s (Figure S22). We also demonstrated that our soft robot could roll against flowing water. In these experiments, we placed our soft robot in a water reservoir, where the flow speed was varied from 0.368 to 0.580 cm/s in 0.12 cm/s increments (Figure S26). Under these conditions, our soft robot was actuated by a rotating B⃗ of 20 mT and 0.5 Hz to roll stably against the flow. For each flow speed, five trials of experiments were conducted, and our soft robot achieved rolling speeds of 6.64–7.40 mm/s (Figure S26a). These results indicated that our soft robot could be actuated to achieve stable locomotion in a controllable manner even under dynamic flow conditions. Furthermore, we evaluated our soft robot's locomotion and functionalities in a urine phantom (synthetic urine). Although synthetic urine lacks the full biological complexity such as proteins of human urine, it is considered as a biomimetic surrogate which has mechanical and chemical properties similar to those of human urine [71, 72]. Using a Bohlin Gemini HR nano rheometer, the viscosity of five samples was measured to be 93, 420 ± 0.0691 µPa·s. When placed in this medium, our soft robot could be reprogrammed to its locomotion mode and roll at 6.57 ± 0.541 mm/s under a rotating B⃗ of 20 mT and 0.5 Hz (Figure S27a; Video S11), with five trials conducted. Likewise, our soft robot was able to enable its two‐anchor crawling gait in the synthetic urine, achieving speeds of 0.335 ± 0.0262 mm/s in locomotion mode across five trials (Figure S27b; Video S11). This gait was activated by rotating B⃗ about the soft robot's X Loco,{L}‐axis with varying magnitudes between 20 and 8 mT. When the soft robot was reprogrammed to its corresponding function mode, it was able to execute its drug‐dispensing, gripping and cutting functionalities when subjected to B⃗Func of 28, 31 and 24 mT, respectively (Video S11). For each function evaluation, five trials were conducted. These experimental results suggested that our soft robot could operate stably in synthetic urine. In the future, we aim to evaluate the performance of our soft robot in biological media such as gastric juice, blood and bile. In general, we believe that our current experimental results represent a significant step toward the eventual deployment of our soft robot for its targeted medical applications.

Since evaluating the sterilizability of our soft robot is critical, we subjected it to steam sterilization process in an autoclave (Tomy SX‐700). In this procedure, the robot was exposed to saturated steam at 121°C and 110 kPa for 15 min, consistent with standard clinical practices [73, 74]. After sterilization, the soft robot retained its ability to perform both rolling (6.22 ± 0.125 mm/s) and two‐anchor crawling (0.319 ± 0.0188 mm/s) gaits in synthetic urine (Figure S28a‐b). It also successfully executed drug‐dispensing, gripping and cutting functionalities (Figure S28c–e). All evaluations were conducted over five trials. In future work, we plan to investigate the robot's compatibility with other sterilization methods, such as gamma irradiation or gas‐based techniques [75].

Although our soft robot can also roll with six‐DOF in its function modes, it will be better to execute this gait when the actuator is in its locomotion mode. This is because it is guaranteed that the surgical functions of the soft robot will not be accidentally activated when the actuator rolls in its locomotion mode, as confirmed in all of our experiments. Nonetheless, we have programmed our soft robot such that a minimum |B⃗| is required to activate their surgical functionality in a respective function mode. Our experimental results indicate that we require at least an actuating B⃗Func of 4 mT, 5 mT and 7 mT to activate the drug‐dispenser, cutting tool and gripper/storage, respectively (Figure S13 and Section S3B). Due to this feature of our soft robot, we did not observe accidental triggering of our soft robot's function during locomotion across all of our experiments. In the future, we aim to also tune the stiffness profile of our soft robot such that all its surgical functions can only be activated when |B⃗Func| exceeds 10 mT.

In summary, we present a general reprogramming strategy for smart magnetic composites to realize multifunctional behavior. By deliberately engineering symmetry and heterogeneous material properties, these composites demonstrate how intrinsic material design can be leveraged to control magnetic and mechanical responses. Building on these smart composites, we construct a miniature soft robot that is highly dexterous, and can be magnetically reprogrammed to dispense drugs, cut, grip and store samples, and heat remotely. Our actuator has great potential to revolutionize surgery, and it also represents a significant advancement for smart magnetic composites, soft actuators and small‐scale robotics.

Outlook

For easy characterization purposes, we have created a larger prototype of our soft robot in this first study (length: 4.4 mm, Section S7B). Nonetheless, this prototype can potentially be deployed for treatments in the gastrointestinal tract since such medical procedures can be administered by centimeter‐scale robots [24]. A potential method for delivering the soft robot into the gastrointestinal tract is to first encase it in a cube of ice, which the patient can then ingest [76]. Once the ice melts in the stomach, the soft robot can potentially navigate through the gastrointestinal tract to administer treatments. After completing its task, the actuator could either exit the body naturally via the digestive system or reverse its trajectory and be retrieved through the mouth [77, 78]. It is theoretically possible to miniaturize our soft robot until its length is 2.5 mm, while still allowing it to execute all the functions and locomotion based on the same actuating B⃗ that is used on the present version (Section S7B). This can be achieved by reducing the stiffness of our soft robot's compliant parts and the surface area of its cutting tool. We aim to investigate such possibilities in the future (Section S7B). Using a miniaturized version, our soft robot with a length of 2.5 mm can potentially access the central nervous system of approximately 50% of the human population [4], and it will have great potential to enable unprecedented minimally invasive surgery such as those for the brain. In contrast to existing surgical robots that have difficulties reaching a patient's inner brain [4], our soft robot can potentially non‐invasively access this organ via the following procedure: first, inject this actuator into the spine, and subsequently navigate it through the central nervous system to reach the interior and exterior part of the brain via the cerebral aqueduct [4]. After completing its treatments at the brain, the soft robot can be retrieved by the surgeons via reversing its trajectory and exiting through the patient's spine (L3‐L4) [4]. If such novel procedures can be realized, surgeons can potentially exploit our actuator to develop a wide range of unprecedented brain surgeries. A critical challenge for enabling such procedures is the development of a system that effectively integrates magnetic actuation with medical imaging techniques. Addressing this challenge will be a key focus of our future work. In theory, our soft robot can potentially also enable other types of radical minimally invasive surgery for the heart, ear and uterus, as discussed by Nelson et al. [4].

Materials and Methods

As shown in Figure 1a, our proposed soft robot can be decomposed into its remote heating component, main body, reprogrammable module, and tentacles. Its tentacles can be further broken down into the sixth‐degree‐of‐freedom (DOF) enhancement module, spacer, and soft parts of the tentacles (Figure S1a).

A combination of soft magnetic constituents, including passive and active components, enables our soft robot to possess a diverse range of magnetic responses. Specifically, the sixth‐DOF enhancement module and the magnetized components in the main body are created by a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) polymer matrix (Dow Corning) embedded with NdFeB microparticles (average size: 5 µm), while the tentacles’ soft components are created by embedding the Ecoflex 00–10 polymer matrix with NdFeB microparticles (average size: 5 µm). To fabricate the proposed soft robot, all of its components were first molded separately via techniques similar to those reported in Xu et al. [21, 22]. In general, all the magnetic constituents within the soft robot are created by uniformly distributing the magnetic particles (fillers) within the elastomer matrix to ensure homogeneity. During these molding processes, the components were cured at 80°C in an oven. Except for the remote heating component and the soft components of the tentacles that required 12 h and 1 h to cure, respectively, the remaining components were cured within 0.5 h. Once the magnetic constituents are cured, their elastomer matrix will lock the magnetic particles (fillers) in place [79]. After they were cured, those that have embedded NdFeB microparticles were magnetized individually with 1.1 T (Figure S1). Subsequently, all the components of our soft robot were assembled manually and bonded adhesively together. During the assembly process, a cutting tool was adhesively attached to the robot (Figure S1b).

Additional experiments were conducted to evaluate the batch consistency of our reprogrammable module. Five samples of the reprogrammable module were fabricated and magnetized by a uniform |B⃗| of 60 mT. Their magnetization magnitudes (per volume) were characterized by customized actuation tests that are explained in Section S1A. In general, these tests were able to evaluate the samples’ magnetization magnitudes by observing how their generated magnetic torques would deform an elastic beam. To produce sufficient magnetic torque for these tests, the samples are isotopically scaled up by two factors compared to the one used in our soft robot. In theory, the magnetization magnitudes (per volume) do not differ when these samples are larger in size [4]. Based on our experimental results, the magnetization magnitude of these samples had a mean of 5.18 kA/m and a standard deviation of 0.487 kA/m (Figure S3bii–vi). Since the obtained standard deviation is within 10%, this suggests that we can indeed consistently fabricate our reprogrammable module. We did not conduct similar experiments on magnetic materials that embed NdFeB and Fe3O4 particles because these materials are well‐established and can be reliably fabricated [4, 15, 80]. The discussion in this section is relatively brief and more details pertaining to the soft robot's material properties and fabrication details can be found in Section S1A.
