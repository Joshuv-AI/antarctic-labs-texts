Controlled Release of the IL-1R Antagonist, Anakinra, from Microgels with an Extended Intra-articular Residence Time
Authors: Ruben J. Trujillo, Sara Lok, Lawrence J. Bonassar, David Putnam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo establish intra-articularly injected microgel compositions that can control the rate of release of anakinra, a protein-based IL-1R antagonist, at therapeutically relevant concentrations for an extended period of time.MethodsUptake and release of the IL-1Ra from microgels was tuned by simple post-synthesis modifications via the addition of calcium chloride after protein loading, or conjugation of small molecules to existing microgels to study the effects of negative (unmodified), positive (dimethylethylenediamine, agmatine), polar (ethanolamine), and non-polar (phenethylamine) side chains on protein delivery.ResultsMicrogels modified with phenethylamine enhanced the loading capacity of the IL-1Ra to 242%. Both calcium-treated and small molecule-modified microgels had a decrease in initial burst release from the microgel matrix, and both extended the release of IL-1Ra from 6 h for unmodified microgels to over 60 days within the active therapeutic index of the drug. Microgels persist in the joint space for at least 8 weeks, and follow a bi-exponential decay curve with a ß half-life of 93.2 days.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate the application of simple post-synthesis modifications for improved drug delivery via polyanionic microgels, and support the potential use of poly(acrylic acid) microgels as intra-articular drug delivery vehicles for osteoarthritis treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11095-026-04196-z.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a debilitating joint disease and leading cause of disability among adults, affecting more than 33 million people in the United States [1, 2]. As osteoarthritis progresses, patients experience joint swelling, pain, and an overall decreased quality of life [3]. Currently, OA treatments include physical therapy, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroids, viscosupplements, and total joint replacements [4–6]. Pro-inflammatory and pro-catabolic cytokines such as IL-1ß and TNF-α are upregulated in patients with OA and are key mediators in matrix degeneration, cell apoptosis, and activation of inflammatory pathways such as nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and mitogen activated protein (MAP) kinase pathways [7]. Inhibition of the NF-κB and MAP kinase pathways with protein kinase inhibitors leads to reduction in structural changes within the joint, a decrease in inflammation, and downregulation of catabolic molecules such as matrix metalloproteinases [8–11].

More recently, there is a push towards biologic therapies such as mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), platelet-rich plasma (PRP), and interleukin-1 receptor antagonists (IL-1Ra), known as orthobiologics, for the intra-articular treatment of OA. Orthobiologics accelerate the regeneration of soft tissue, promote anti-inflammtory processes within the joint, and inhibit inflammatory pathways that lead to joint catabolism [12]. Both MSCs and PRP treatments have therapeutic promise, but their biologic and ethical complexities make clinical trial interpretation and implementation difficult [13–15]. Therapeutics that block upstream inflammation triggers, like IL-1Ra (Anakinra), are promising immediate biologic therapies for OA. Anakinra is a well-characterized protein (unlike PRPs and MSCs), and is recombinantly manufactured at commercial scale, making the potential future use of this protein for OA treatment more likely. Anakinra (hereby denoted as IL-1Ra) acts by binding to IL-1 receptor type I (IL-1RI) and competitively inhibits binding of IL-1α and IL-1ß, preventing downstream activation of inflammatory processes involving the NF-κB and MAP kinase pathways. Additionally, IL-1Ra possesses high receptor binding affinity and specificity, has low toxicity, and a large maximum tolerated concentration (150 mg/mL) for OA treatment [7, 16].

IL-1Ra currently has a limited capability to treat OA due to a short half-life, typically on the order of hours to days [6, 17, 18]. Specifically for OA, where select joints are affected compared to rheumatoid arthritis, localized delivery becomes a treatment of choice over systemic delivery to increase bioavailability and reduce systemic exposure of potent therapeutics [19–21]. In fact, intra-articular injections of IL-1Ra have been evaluated for OA treatment in several clinical trials [16, 18, 22–25]. In these studies, single intra-articular injections of IL-1Ra failed to demonstrate consistent improvements in patient outcomes over an extended period of time. However, numerous in vitro and in vivo studies show that sustained IL-1Ra treatment of articular cartilage cells and explants stimulated with IL-1 is effective at inhibiting the activity of IL-1, minimizing downstream effects related to glycosaminoglycan loss, nitric oxide production, collagen loss, and lubricin production [17, 26–30]. These confounding results of IL-1Ra efficacy between clinical trials and experimental studies strongly suggest that achieving a controlled and sustained release of IL-1Ra within its therapeutic index over weeks or months could lead to improved long-term effects of individuals with OA [31]. To effectively inhibit IL-1α and IL-1ß activity, a minimum effective concentration of 10-100X IL-1Ra is needed, making sustained delivery of a therapeutically relevant dose of IL-1Ra crucial to minimize downstream effects of inflammation [28, 32]. Many studies report the efficacy of different injectable delivery systems to improve the half-life of IL-1Ra, achieve a sustained delivery of IL-1Ra, and preserve the activity of IL-1Ra [32, 33]. Generally, half-life improvements of IL-1Ra are derived from PEGylation or fusion of IL-1Ra to a protein/nanoparticle. However, these modifications negatively impact IL-1Ra activity, reaching as low as 10% of the original activity [34]. Sustained delivery of IL-1Ra via microparticles and nanoparticles show promising results for the inhibition of IL-1α and IL-1ß activity both in vitro and in vivo. Particles composed of poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid), gelatin, poly(N-isopropylacrylamide), dextran, chitosan, and calcium phosphate have all been investigated for the controlled delivery of IL-1Ra [32]. Generally with microparticle and nanoparticle systems, the encapsulation of the protein can lead to degradation and loss of bioactivity due to solvents used for encapsulation, denaturation of the protein within the particles due to the chemical environment, and protein aggregation within the particles [35–37]. Overall, there is a need for an injectable system that achieves sustained delivery of IL-1Ra, improves the overall half-life of the molecule in vivo, and minimizes the impact on bioactivity to create an improved therapy for OA treatment.

Previously, our lab reported the uptake and delivery of bioactive model proteins via biocompatible polyanionic microgels [38]. However, although these microgels effectively encapsulated small, negatively charged proteins such as ß-lactoglobulin (18.4 kDa, pI = 5.1), complete protein release was observed within 6 h. Similarly, IL-1Ra is a 17.3 kDa anti-inflammatory protein with an isoelectric point (pI) of 5.46, making it negative at physiologic pH. As such, we expect a similar release profile to ß-lactoglobulin using the same polyanionic microgels, since protein release is largely governed by crosslinking density, pore size, charge, pH, temperature, and ionic strength [39, 40]. Therefore, we considered the application of simple modifications of an existing hydrogel structure and its functional groups to evaluate the effects of hydrogel composition on IL-Ra release kinetics. Our goal was to improve upon our microgel design and demonstrate the controlled delivery of a therapeutically relevant dose of IL-1Ra for an extended period of time via polyanionic microgels. The work herein describes simple post-synthesis modifications of polyanionic microgels via two methods to sustain the delivery of IL-1Ra: 1.) Addition of CaCl2 after protein uptake to mitigate the burst release of IL-1Ra due to intraparticle and interparticle ionic crosslinks; and 2.) conjugation of pAA’s carboxyl groups with small molecules to study the effects of negative, positive, polar, and non-polar side chains on the uptake and release of IL-1Ra. Furthermore, we evaluate the intra-articular residence time of these microgels in vivo to determine if there is a sufficient half-life for long-term therapeutic delivery.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Anhydrous acrylic acid, tetraethylene glycol (TEG), Pluronic L35®, 4,4’-azobis(4-cyanopentanoic acid) (A-CPA), 4-cyanopentanoic acid dithiobenzoate (CPA-DB), L-lysine, and potassium bromide (KBr) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Dimethyl sulfoxide and methanol were purchased from Fisher Chemical. Neutral aluminum oxide was purchased from J.T. Baker. Phenethylamine, N,N-dimetylethylenediamine, and ethanolamine were purchased from Krackeler Scientific, Inc. Biotechnology-grade glycine was purchased from Amresco®. 4-methylmorpholine (NMM) was purchased from BeanTown Chemical. 4-(4,6-dimethoxy-1,3,5-triazine-2-yl)−4-methylmorpholinium chloride (DMTMM) was purchased from Tokyo Chemical Industry. 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)−1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) and sodium pyruvate were purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific. Recombinant human IL-1ß was purchased from SinoBiological. Phenol-free Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium, fetal bovine serum, and penicillin/streptomycin were purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific. Agmatine sulfate was purchased from AstaTech. Phosphate buffered saline (PBS) was purchased from Corning. Cyanine7 amine (Cy7) and rhodamine6G were purchased from Lumiprobe. IL-1Ra was received by prescription as Kineret® (Anakinra). All chemicals were used as received.

Microgel Synthesis

Microgels with either low crosslinking density (Low XLD) or high crosslinking density (High XLD) were synthesized using a two-phase microemulsion as previously reported [38, 41]. Briefly, poly(acrylic acid) (120 mg/mL DMSO, 1.11 M COOH, 12.1–13.6 kDa, Ð = 1.05–1.28; Supplemental Figure S1), synthesized following previously reported methods [41–43] was dissolved in DMSO as the dispersed phase. DMTMM (184.33 mg/mL DMSO, 0.67 M DMTMM) and varying amounts of NMM (Low XLD: 7.33 µL/mL DMSO, 0.067 M NMM; High XLD: 73.3 µL/mL DMSO, 6.7 M NMM) were added to the solution and stirred at 450 rpm until dissolved. After dissolution, TEG (95.87 µL/mL DMSO, 1.11 M OH) was added as the crosslinker and the mixture was stirred at 450 rpm for two minutes. For fluorescently-tagged microgels, 0.05 mol% Cy7 amine or rhodamine6G were added to the reaction mixture relative to moles of COOH. The dispersed phase was pipetted into a scintillation vial with Pluronic L35® (15 g Pluronic L35®/1.5 mL DMSO) as the continuous phase and vortexed to create a homogenous pre-emulsion. The pre-emulsion was added to a 100 mL beaker with Pluronic L35® (25 g Pluronic L35®/15 g pre-emulsion Pluronic L35®) and this mixture was homogenized at 750 rpm for 10 min at room temperature using a Silverson L5M-A Homogenizer with a 1-inch slotted head. The beaker was then placed on a stir plate and stirred at 250 rpm for 4 h at room temperature.

After the reaction was complete, microgels were pelleted by centrifugation at 9500 rpm at 25⁰C for five minutes. The supernatant was decanted using a serological pipette and microgels were resuspended by vortexing in 25 mL of DI water. DI water (25 mL) was added to the microgels, followed by sonication (10 min) using a VWR® Ultrasonic Cleaner. Microgels were left suspended in 50 mL of DI water for an additional 20 min unperturbed and at room temperature after the first wash to allow any solvent, Pluronic L35®, and unreacted reagents to diffuse into the water phase. Microgels were subsequently pelleted at 5000 rpm at 25⁰C for 5 min. The supernatant was decanted and the microgels were resuspended in a solution with excess (> 10 mmol in 40 mL DI water) of one of the following molecules: glycine, lysine, phenethylamine, N,N-dimethylethylenediamine, ethanolamine, or agmatine sulfate. As there are likely activated carboxylic acid groups remaining from the microgel reaction, incubation with the aforementioned small molecules that contain primary amines allows for amides to form, ultimately removing the activated carboxylic acids and functionalizing the microgels. Therefore, these reactions with small molecules do not alter the crosslinking density of the microgels, but rather decorate the microgels with unique functional groups that can alter their interactions with proteins. solutions were incubated overnight at 4⁰C to remove any remaining activated carboxyl groups and replace them with the small molecules. Microgels were washed the following day by 3 cycles of re-suspension in 50 mL of deionized water, centrifugation at 5000 rpm at 25⁰C for 5 min, and isolated by removal of the supernatant. Lastly, microgels were suspended in DI water (5 mL), flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and lyophilized to dryness.

Microgel Size Analysis

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Tescan Mira3 FESEM) was used to characterize the size of unmodified lyophilized microgels. Conductive double-sided carbon tape was placed on SEM pin stub mounts. Microgel formulations for SEM were prepared via the droplet evaporation technique with microgels suspended in deionized water (0.05–0.1 mg/mL). SEM stubs containing microgels were dried in a desiccator, then sputter-coated with gold and palladium. Microgels were imaged at a working distance of 9 mm at 5 kV. Microgel size was determined using Fiji to measure particle diameters by hand.

Hydrated microgel diameters were acquired by using rhodamine-labeled microgels and confocal microscopy. Rhodamine6G functionalized lyophilized microgels were suspended in 1X PBS, 10X PBS, and 0.1 M HEPES, incubated for 24 h, and the hydrated diameters were measured using a multi-photon confocal microscope (LSM880 Confocal multiphoton inverted, Zeiss). Images were analyzed using Fiji by applying a threshold to the images, using the particle analysis plugin, and further correcting for misidentified microparticles.

FTIR of Small Molecule Modified Microgel Batches

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR, Bruker Vertex 80v Vacuum System) was used to characterize and confirm the qualitative addition of small molecules in microgel formulations as previously described [41]. Potassium bromide (KBr) was dried overnight in an oven to remove moisture. FTIR pellets (13 mm diameter) were made with 300 mg KBr and 5 mg sample and compressed to 10 tons with a die kit. Liquid samples were analyzed using ATR on the same system under vacuum with a solvent trap. Samples were analyzed from 400 cm−1 – 4000 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 64 total scans. Absorbance data were baseline corrected and normalized to the largest peak.

IL-1Ra Loading Into Microgels

Assessment of IL-1Ra uptake was conducted following a similarly published protocol [38]. Dry microgel formulations (4 mg, n = 3 per formulation) were first suspended in 1 mL of 0.1 M HEPES and neutralized using 1 M NaOH in an Eppendorf tube. Subsequently, 1 mL of 10 mg/mL IL-1Ra was added to each tube and allowed to incubate for 24 h at 37⁰C under stirring at 8 rpm. After 24 h, microgels were centrifuged at 13,200 rpm for 5 min and the supernatant was collected. Microgels were washed once by resuspending and centrifuging with 2 mL of PBS to remove any remaining free protein. The release (t = 0) was started at the initiation of the first wash when the microgels were resuspended once again in 2 mL of PBS. All supernatants were stored at 4⁰C. Total IL-1Ra uptake was quantified by analyzing the supernatants using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific) with IL-1Ra as the protein for the standard curve. IL-1Ra Loading with Calcium-treated Microgels

To reduce the initial burst release of IL-Ra from unmodified microgels, solutions of either 15 mM CaCl2, 30 mM CaCl2, or 60 mM CaCl2 were added to microgel suspensions with encapsulated IL-1Ra. The same IL-1Ra protein loading protocol was performed as described in Sect. 2.4.1. After 24 h, microgels were centrifuged at 13,200 rpm for 5 min and the supernatant was collected. Microgels were washed once by resuspending and centrifuging with 2 mL of PBS to remove any remaining free protein. Microgel suspensions with encapsulated IL-1Ra were then treated with either 15 mM CaCl2, 30 mM CaCl2, or 60 mM CaCl2 for 5 min at 4⁰C to minimize any release. Microgels were centrifuged, the supernatant was removed, and the microgels were washed once more in 2 mL of PBS to remove any free protein and CaCl2. The release (t = 0) started at the initiation of the first wash step. Total IL-1Ra uptake was quantified by analyzing the supernatants using a BCA Protein Assay Kit with IL-1Ra as the protein for the standard curve. Microgel encapsulation efficiency and loading efficiency were calculated using Eq. 1 and Eq. 2, respectively.

In Vitro Release of IL-1Ra with Small Molecule-Modified Microgels

As described in the previous sections, the release (t = 0) was started at the initiation of the first wash step. Microgels were centrifuged at 13,200 rpm for 2 min and 1 mL of the supernatant was collected, with initial time points of t = 30 m, 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h, 6 h, 8 h, 12 h and 24 h. Subsequent time points were taken for up to 65 days. The buffer was replaced with 1 mL of fresh PBS at each time point. All supernatants were stored at 4⁰C until analysis. The total release of IL-1Ra was determined by running BCA assays or Micro BCA assays on the supernatant when appropriate. From this data, cumulative IL-1Ra release (%) and IL-1Ra concentration delivered (mg/mL) were calculated for each microgel formulation.

In Vitro IL-1ß Challenge Study

Cartilage plugs were challenged with IL-1ß and treated with IL-1Ra-loaded microgels using modified methods [44, 45]. Femoral condyles from the stifle joint of neonatal bovids (Copper City Meats, Rome, NY) were harvested under sterile conditions and used to make cartilage plugs (3 mm wide by 2 mm thick, n = 7 per group, condyle matched across groups). Cartilage plugs were then cultured in a 24-well plate with transwell membranes in media containing phenol-free, low glucose, Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium with 1% FBS, 2 v/v% sodium pyruvate (2 mM), 1 v/v% penicillin/streptomycin, 1 v/v% L-glutamine, 2.5 v/v% HEPES, 1 v/v% amino acid mix (5 mg/mL ascorbic acid, 3 mg/mL α-ketoglutaric acid, and 1 mL L-glutamine), and with or without 10 ng/mL of recombinant human IL-1ß at 37°C and 5% CO2. Cartilage plugs were split into the following treatment groups: negative control (-IL-1ß, + IL-1Ra), positive control (+ IL-1ß, -IL-1Ra), single IL-1Ra treatment (1 µg/mL) (+ IL-1ß, + IL-1Ra), constant IL-1Ra treatment (1 µg/mL) (+ IL-1ß, + IL-1Ra), empty microgels without IL-1ß (-IL-1ß, -IL-1Ra), empty microgels with IL-1ß (+ IL-1ß, -IL-1Ra), and loaded 30 mM CaCl2 microgels (+ IL-1ß, -IL-1Ra). The dosing for both the single IL-1Ra treatment and constant IL-1Ra treatment were chosen to achieve 100X excess relative to the dose of IL-1ß. Additionally, 30 mM CaCl2 treated microgels were chosen based on their previous in vitro release data, where they achieved a consistent release of 1 µg/mL or greater for the 65 days of release. For microgel loading, 2 mg of microgels were suspended in 0.5 mL sterile 0.1 M HEPES, neutralized to pH = 7 using 1 M NaOH, combined with 0.5 mL of 10 mg/mL IL-1Ra, and allowed to incubate for 24 h at 37⁰C under stirring at 8 rpm. After 24 h, microgels were centrifuged at 13,200 rpm for 5 min and the supernatant was collected. Microgels were immediately resuspended in 30 mM CaCl2, incubated for 10 min, centrifuged, the supernatant was removed, and microgels were resuspended in 0.5 mL media. IL-1ß media (0.5 mL) was added directly to the well plates while all IL-1Ra treatments (0.5 mL) were added into the transwell. The media was changed every 2 days for 8 days and media aliquots were collected for biochemical analysis. On days 2 and 4, two condyle plugs from each group were taken and immediately flash frozen in liquid nitrogen for transcriptomics analysis. On day 8, three condyle plugs from each group were weighed (wet weight of the whole plug) and bisected, with one half processed for histology and the other half weighed (wet weight of the half plug) and lyophilized for biochemical analysis. Lyophilized cartilage explants were digested in 1.25 mg/mL papain solution overnight and sulfated glycosaminoglycan (sGAG) content of digested explants and media aliquots was measured using a modified DMMB assay [46, 47]. Histological samples were stained with Safranin-O/Fast green to qualitatively measure sGAG content in cartilage explants. Histological images were taken using a Keyence BZ-X810 and were used to quantify GAG depletion through custom methods. Images were processed in Fiji where a minimum threshold was applied, and the GAG area loss was measured from the first 500 µm relative to the articular surface due to lack in Safranin-O intensity. The ratio of white pixels to black pixels from the binarized selected areas gave the percent of GAG loss for that area.

In Vivo Residence Time

All animal procedures were approved and reviewed by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Cornell University (IACUC protocol number: 2023–0216). Twelve-week-old male Sprague–Dawley rats (Charles River Laboratories) were housed in pairs following a standard 12-h light/dark cycle and fed a low fluorescence, alfalfa-free diet to minimize any autofluorescent background signal. Rats were induced under general anesthesia using isoflurane, and the hair was clipped from the hindlimbs to the abdomen prior to the intra-articular injections and following imaging time points. Rats were assigned to one of two groups: free Cy7 in PBS (20 µg/mL) or Cy7-tagged XLDLow microgels (10 mg/mL). Cadaveric bi-lateral injections of varying doses of Cy7-tagged microgels showed no self-quenching of Cy7 up to 10 mg/mL (Supplemental Figure S5). The knees were aseptically prepared, followed by randomized bi-lateral para-patellar intra-articular injections of sterile PBS or one of the injection groups. All injection volumes were 25 µL and performed using a 27G needle. Anesthetized rats were imaged using the in vivo imaging system (IVIS, PerkinElmer Health Sciences, USA) at the following time points: 0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h, followed by weekly intervals from 1 to 8 weeks. Images were taken with an auto exposure time using the 710/760 excitation/emission pair. Images were analyzed using a commercial software (LivingImage 4.7.2; Perkin-Elmer, USA) where fluorescence intensity was measured in units of total radiant efficiency (TRE). The TRE of the contralateral control limb for each rat with a PBS injection was subtracted from the Cy7 or Cy7-tagged microgels limb to remove background signal, and finally normalized to the TRE at t = 0 for each individual rat. A small percentage of free dye (12.1% relative to conjugated dye) was found on Cy7-tagged microgels after the termination of the study. To account for the free dye, the same 12.1% was taken from the average TRE of free dye injected rats at all time points. This signal was then subtracted from the TRE signal at each time point for rats injected with Cy7-tagged microgels before normalizing. Statistics

A one-way ANOVA was used to compare IL-1Ra uptake, encapsulation efficiency, and loading capacity across all microgel formulations. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons between formulations were performed using Tukey’s HSD method to control the Type I error rate. For the in vitro IL-1ß challenge study, the percent area loss at the surface of cartilage samples was analyzed using a linear mixed effects model with a fixed of treatment group and a random effect of condyle. Model assumptions of normality and homogeneous variance were assessed using residual plots. The fixed effect of treatment group was tested using an F-test and pairwise comparisons between groups were performed using Tukey’s HSD method to control the family-wise Type I error rate. Custom contrasts were used to make comparisons between groups that received microgels (yes vs no), IL1Ra (yes vs no), and IL1Beta (yes vs no). Only the groups with n = 3 were statistically compared, while the group with n = 1 was included only for graphical representation. A two-way ANOVA was used to compare the in vivo residence time of free Cy7 to Cy7-tagged microgels with Šídák’s multiple comparisons test. Statistical significance between groups was considered at p ≤ 0.05. All statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism or RStudio.

Results

Polyanionic microgels composed of poly(acrylic acid) and crosslinked with tetraethylene glycol were used for this study (Fig. 1A) based on their previously reported capacity to encapsulate a wide variety of model protein therapeutics [38]. From our previous work, IL-1Ra (17.3 kDa, pI = 5.46) is biologically similar to ß-lactoglobulin (18.4 kDa, pI = 5.1) with regards to molecular weight and isoelectric point. Therefore, we expected IL-1Ra to yield similar results relating to burst release of the protein within a short period of time. Because of the expected burst release of IL-1Ra, the microgels were further modified using two separate methods. First, microgels were treated with calcium chloride (CaCl2) after protein encapsulation to form ionic crosslinks. We hypothesized the calcium would form intra-particle or inter-particle crosslinks, thereby inhibiting the initial burst release of IL-1Ra from the microgel matrix (Fig. 1B). Second, microgels were conjugated with amino acid inspired small molecules containing a primary amine immediately after the microgel synthesis via an amide bond to covalently attach either polar, non-polar, zwitterionic, or positive functional groups to the microgels without directly impacting microgel morphology or synthesis conditions (Fig. 1C). As these microgels interact with proteins via ionic interactions, modifying the polyanionic structure with various functional groups could impact protein uptake and release. Initially, both low crosslinking (Low XLD) and high crosslinking (High XLD) density microgels were investigated for the controlled release of IL-1Ra. Interestingly, High XLD microgels did not successfully encapsulate IL-1Ra, with Low XLD microgels taking up 5.2 mg of IL-1Ra (Fig. 2A). Specifically for Low XLD microgels, encapsulation efficiency and loading capacity were 46.3% and 130%, respectively (Fig. 2B and Fig. 2C). Total release of encapsulated IL-1Ra was observed within 6 h from Low XLD microgels (Fig. 2D). The uptake of IL-1Ra into Low XLD microgels in 1X PBS was also investigated, but demonstrated a diminished total uptake that was 70% less compared to uptake in 0.1 M HEPES (Supplemental Figure S4). Successful conjugation of microgels with small molecules was verified using FTIR (Fig. 3). The FTIR figures show the spectra of unmodified microgels, the spectra of the small molecule, and the spectra of the microgels modified with the small molecule. Most noticeable for all FTIR spectra of small molecule modified microgels are the presence of the amide I peak (1640 cm−1), representative of the C = O stretch, and the amide II peak (1570–1515 cm−1), representative of the NH bend associated with secondary amides. Additionally, the spectra display multiple bands in the 3330–3060 cm−1 region, corresponding to NH stretching. Ethanolamine microgels (Fig. 3A), displayed a presence of an OH stretch from 3650–3584 cm−1, a C-O stretch for the primary alcohol from 1260–1000 cm−1, and an OH bend from 1420–1330 cm−1. Phenethylamine microgels (Fig. 3B) showed the presence of an out of plane CH bend from 710–675 cm−1 and an in plane CH bend around 1050 cm−1 associated with the aromatic phenyl carbons. The CH stretch from the aromatic group was also present at 3050 cm−1, and a peak present at 1400 cm−1 relating to the C–C stretch from the phenyl carbons. Lysine functionalized microgels (Fig. 3C) exhibited a broad, strong peak from 3100–2600 cm−1 and a peak from 2220–2000 cm−1 corresponding to a NH3+ stretching and asymmetric bending, respectively. These peaks suggest the presence of lysine as a zwitterion, in addition to a peak at 1400 cm−1 relating to the carboxylate ion group. Dimethylethylenediamine microgels (Fig. 3D) showed a greater incorporation of methylene groups as demonstrated by the asymmetrical and symmetrical stretching found near 2926 cm−1 and 2853 cm−1, respectively. Additionally, a methylene rocking peak was found near 720 cm−1 and a series of bands found between 1350–1150 cm−1, characteristic of long chain amides. Discussion

The objective of this study was to investigate post-synthesis modifications of poly(acrylic acid) microgels on the upake and release of IL-1Ra, in addition to evaluating the in vivo residence time of poly(acrylic acid) microgels. IL-1Ra has an isoelectric point of 5.46, making it negatively charged at physiologic pH. Previous work by our lab showed that a similarly sized and charged protein using unmodified microgels, although successfully taken up by the microgels, released all encapsulated protein in less than 6 h [38]. In this work we show that the release of IL-1Ra also occurred within 6 h from unmodified Low XLD microgels, and zero uptake was demonstrated in High XLD microgels, further solidifying that the polyanionic microgel matrix is not suitable for extended release of this type of protein. Since hydrated diameters of High XLD microgels are similar to those of Low XLD microgels (Supplemental Figure S3B and S3C), we hypothesize this lack of uptake is caused by fewer carboxylic acid groups being present due to the higher crosslinking, suggesting that this protein uptake is ionically driven. As such, we aimed to improve the release profile of IL-1Ra by modifying microgels either treating with CaCl2 after loading with IL-1Ra, or by using small molecules with primary amines to conjugate to carboxylic acid groups after microgel synthesis.

Modifying microgels with small molecules post-synthesis was inspired by the high-throughput, combinatorial design approach most commonly used for the formation of lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), allowing for the tunability of LNPs for delivery and accumulation [50]. This method of modifying microgels with small molecules was unique in the sense that it allowed us to evaluate to impact of different functional groups on the microgels for protein release, without altering the reaction conditions or the crosslinking density of the microgels. First, FTIR of small molecule-modified microgel formulations showed successful conjugation to the poly(acrylic acid) backbone through the presence of amide I and amide II peaks, in addition to key overlapping peaks representative of the unique small molecule functional groups. It is important to highlight that differences in small molecule conjugation regarding percent conjugation could be a contributing factor for the uptake and release of IL-1Ra. However, differences in percent conjugation of small molecules do not detract from the overall trends that are observed in this study with small molecule modified microgels. The loading capacity of IL-1Ra in small molecule formulations demonstrated that the R-group, or key functional group associated with the small molecule, impacted total IL-1Ra loading. When looking at the loading capacity of the positively charged small molecules, dimethylenediamine and agmatine decreased the loading capacity relative to unmodified microgels to 20% and 83%, respectively. Although the positively charged small molecules did not increase the loading capacity, it is apparent that the tertiary amine of dimethylethylenediamine is less effective at sequestering IL-1Ra compared to the charged guanidine found on agmatine, highlighting the importance of the charged molecule’s chemical composition. Additionally, the loading capacities of ethanolamine and lysine-modified hydrogels were lower relative to unmodified microgels, showing that polar and zwitterionic functional groups do not enhance IL-1Ra absorption into the microgel matrix. Interestingly, microgels modified with phenethylamine enhanced loading capacity of IL-1Ra to 243%. We hypothesize that this improved non-polar, hydrophobic interaction of phenethylamine with IL-1Ra is due to the significant amino acid composition of IL-1Ra is primarily hydrophobic (~ 44.6%, Supplemental Figure S2). Functionalization of the microgels using small molecules could have also impacted the overall mesh size of each formulation, impacting both uptake and release results of IL-1Ra. Initial burst release of IL-1Ra from the microgel matrix was hypothesized to be related to differences in the hydrated microgel pore size as microgels are transferred from 0.1 M HEPES to 1X PBS. However, hydrated diameters of unmodified microgels in 0.1 M HEPES and 1X PBS do not differ, suggesting a separate mechanism of burst release from the microgel matrix (Supplemental Figure S3A). It is important to note that all modified microgel formulations exhibited a similar initial burst release from the microgel matrix, but the extent of this burst release was mitigated for these modified gels and demonstrated a plateau behavior with respect to cumulative release. However, when we analyzed the concentration of IL-1Ra released at each time point, we observed that each modified microgel formulation achieved a release of IL-1Ra greater than 14.6 ng/mL for at least 65 days, achieving the theoretically determined minimum effective concentration necessary for in vivo efficacy in patients with OA. Specifically for phenethylamine-modified microgels, these uptake and release results align with work by Leonard et al. where hydrophobically modified alginate hydrogels demonstrated improved encapsulation of bovine serum albumin compared to unmodified alginate [51]. The high encapsulation efficiencies and retention of bovine serum albumin were attributed to the hydrophobic portions of the protein interacting with the hydrophobic domains within the alginate hydrogel. Similar studies have investigated the delivery of model proteins from drug delivery systems composed of poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), chitosan, hyaluronic acid, gelatin crosslinked with genipin, and ﻿poly(2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate)-pyridine [26, 27, 52–55]. Although these studies demonstrated a relatively high encapsulation efficiency, the loading capacities were no greater than 10%, significantly lower than the lowest loading capacity reported in this study for dimethylethylenediamine microgels (20%) [56].

The second approach to improve the release profile of IL-1Ra from polyanionic microgels consisted of creating ionic crosslinks using CaCl2 after the loading of IL-1Ra. Deriving inspiration from ionic hydrogels, calcium has commonly been used for the formation of calcium-alginate hydrogels by ionic crosslinking between the divalent calcium ion and the mannuronic/guluronic acid units from alginate [57, 58]. As unmodified poly(acrylic acid) microgels possess a large number of unoccupied carboxylic acid groups, these groups are available for further ionic crosslinking. As the loading capacity of IL-1Ra for unmodified microgels was 144% but complete release was achieved in 6 days, we sought to minimize the burst release of IL-1Ra from the microgel matrix by creating ionic crosslinks with CaCl2 after the protein loading. When evaluating the cumulative release of IL-1Ra from microgel formulations treated with varying concentrations of CaCl2, it is evident that calcium treatment mitigates the preliminary burst release and is dependent on the concentration of calcium. Increasing calcium concentrations causes an increase in crosslinking density, ultimately decreasing the mesh size of the hydrogel matrix. Work by Kopač et al. showed that hydrogels composed of either alginate or TEMPO-oxidized cellulose made from cellulose nanofibers (TOCNF) and crosslinked with calcium had a decreased diffusivity of theophylline due to a decrease in the mesh size [59, 60]. Zhou et al. investigated the effect of different cationic molecules to create alginate microspheres followed by the coating of the alginate microsphere using poly-D,L-lactide-poly(ethylene glycol) (PELA) [61]. Alginate was crosslinked using either polylysine, calcium, or chitosan in the presence of human serum albumin as the model protein, and results showed rate of degradation and the in vitro release profile were impacted due to these modifications, with release for at least 36 days. We hypothesize that the ionic crosslinks using calcium mitigated the burst release of IL-1Ra by decreasing the size of the pores, as well as the calcium interacting with the negative carboxylic acid groups. Additionally, it was observed that calcium crosslinking caused inter-particle crosslinks, and it is possible that IL-1Ra can diffuse from one microgel into another microgel, without diffusing into the release medium. These data collectively show that post-synthesis modifications of poly(anionic) microgels enhance the sustained release of IL-1Ra from less than 6 h to greater than 60 days in vitro.

The bioactivity of IL-1Ra released from calcium-treated microgels was successfully evaluated using an IL-1ß stimulated cartilage explant model. Biochemical analysis of whole cartilage explants did not show any significant differences across treatment groups. However, this lack of difference in the present study is consistent with previous work, showing that changes in biochemical composition and mechanical properties of IL-1ß-treated explants is confined to a thin region of tissue near the edge of samples [62]. Additionally, cartilage explant degradation using IL-1ß or matrix enzymes follows a dose dependent response, impacting the measurable sGAG loss from whole explants using biochemical analysis while histological sections can highlight matrix loss due to changes in staining [63]. As such, histology of cartilage explants were quantified to measure the relative amound of sGAG loss from the articular surface. Histology samples from this study were visually comparable to other studies that observed sGAG loss from the surface of articular cartilage explants [64, 65]. A trend was present that explants treated with IL-1Ra decreased sGAG loss from the articular surface when evaluating histological samples when compared to explants without IL-1Ra treatment. This trend along with similar sGAG loss values across IL-1Ra treatment groups suggests IL-1Ra released from microgels treated with 30 mM CaCl2 remained bioactive, as well as delivered a minimum effective concentration of IL-1Ra to minimize sGAG degradation, consistent with the previous in vitro delivery results. Similar studies that measured the release of IL-1Ra from poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) microparticles demonstrated controlled release of IL-1Ra, but released IL-1Ra bioactivity was 20 times less potent than soluble IL-1Ra [30, 32]. Sustained release of IL-1Ra was similarly achieved in hyaluronic acid-chitosan microspheres, where chondrocyte apoptosis, nitrite concentrations, and prostaglandin concentrations were reduced by released IL-1Ra [26]. However, GAG concentrations were also reduced in IL-1Ra treatment groups, suggesting released IL-1Ra did not completely prevent GAG loss. A more recent alternative approach studied the delivery of IL-1Ra using a cationic peptide carrier composed of arginine with conjugated IL-1Ra [29]. Over the course of a 16 day cartilage culture stimulated with 2 ng/mL IL-1α, GAG loss was inhibited, cellular metabolism was improved, cumulative nitrite release was mitigated, and chondrocytes exhibited improved viability.

Gene expression differences of key inflammatory molecules were evaluated to determine if treatment of free IL-1Ra or IL-1Ra-loaded microgels downregulated inflammatory molecules that are associated with the progression of osteoarthritis (OA). Transcriptomics was performed on paired condyle explants at days 2 and 4 of IL-1ß culture for all groups (Fig. 8). Due to their increased prevalence in OA, the genes selected were part of the NF-κB and MAP kinase pathways, IL-6, and major cartilage matrix degradative enzymes belonging to the matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs (ADAMTS) families [7, 66]. IL-1Ra-loaded microgels significantly downregulated the expression of IL-6, ADAMTS4, ADAMTS5, and MMP-1 and −3 when compared to the positive control (+ IL-1ß, -IL-1Ra) in cartilage explants from days 2 and 4 of culture. These data suggest that the IL-1Ra released from the microgels remained bioactive to facilitate the downregulation of inflammatory cytokines and catabolic enzymes. The suppression of inflammatory gene expression further confirms the results from the histological explants trending towards greater GAG retention, as discussed above. Interestingly for day 2 and day 4, differential gene expression for NF-κB genes, although significant, was minimally decreased compared to IL-6 and the catabolic enzymes. Additionally, no differences were shown for MAPK1 gene expression for either days, suggesting that this pathway did not play a significant role in the inflammation and catabolism for this cartilage explant model. Minimal differences were observed across groups from days 2 and 4 of the IL-1ß challenge study, and this is likely due to the proximity of these time points. Early time points were chosen to minimize differences across groups due to apoptosis and ensure the preservation of RNA for transcriptomic analysis. IL-1Ra-loaded microgels downregulated inflammatory gene expression similar to both the single and continuous dose of IL-1Ra groups for both time points, approaching similar gene expression levels of negative controls cartilage explant groups. Numerous in vitro and clinical studies have explored various inhibitors that impact IL-6, TNF-α, MMPs, and ADAMTS to prevent the progression of joint degeneration [19, 66–72]. Osteoarthritis (OA) is a multi-factorial disease and includes numerous inflammatory pathways that lead to degradation of the cartilage matrix. As such, an ideal therapeutic would successfully downregulate the activity of multiple inflammatory molecules. Specifically in this study, we show that treatment of IL-1ß challenged cartilage explants with IL-1Ra from loaded microgels significantly downregulates the gene expression of NF-κB, IL-6, ADAMTS4, ADAMTS5, and MMP-1, −3, and −13, highlighting the efficacy of IL-1Ra as a relevant therapeutic molecule for continued OA treatment. Overall, these results suggest that IL-1Ra released from microgels remains bioactive, and future experiments will expand upon these results with modified-microgel formulations.

Cy7-tagged microgels exhibited a prolonged intra-articular in vivo residence time compared to free Cy7. Within the joint space and with the development of OA, the clearance time of molecules is significantly increased, limiting the long-term efficacy of intra-articular therapies [6, 73]. With clearance time in mind, various microparticle formulations and polymers have been studied for OA treatment, and specifically with microparticles, supporting that the larger size of these microparticles increases the residence time in vivo relative to other drug delivery systems such as liposomes and nanoparticles [74–78]. HA conjugated with poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) formed nanoparticles above the lower critical solution temperature and when injected into the knee of 6-week-old male C57BL/6 mice that underwent a destabilization of the medial meniscus was found localized in the knee joint and synovium for at least 21 days [79]. Another study that evaluated the residence time of hyaluronic acid microparticles crosslinked with bis-carbonylimidazolide showed 10% of the initial dose after 34 days in healthy female C57BL/6 J mice [80]. Microparticles of similar size to the microgels used in the present study were found to have varying residence times in the joint, ranging from a half-life of 6 days to the presence of microparticles in the synovium after 12 weeks, indicating the importance of material composition [76, 81]. More recent work by Colville et al. studied the intra-articular half-life of recombinant lubricin in healthy Sprague–Dawley rats [82]. Fluorescently-tagged lubricin was found to persist in the joint space for up to 84 days with a ß half-life of 45 days. In this study, free Cy7 dye, although consistent with results published by Colville et al. (ß half-life = 17.4 days), exhibits a greater retention than expected for small molecules and other studies showing rapid clearance of free dye [83]. We believe this discrepancy in residence time is explained by the presence of a primary amine group in the Cy7, having a greater interaction with the negative articular cartilage than similar sulfo-Cy7 derivatives. Differences in the imaging technique and quantification of residence time could also lead to larger discrepancies compared to other studies showing a shorter residence time for free dye, as the imaging modality used in this study allows for the detection of small concentrations of dye. Microgels in this study tagged with Cy7 exhibited a prolonged residence time in vivo, with an impressive ß half-life of 93.2 days.

In vivo optical imaging systems allow for the non-invasive fluorescent imaging of animals while having high specificity, temporal resolution, and ease of use to measure fluorescent signal intensities and locations [84]. However, in vivo optical imaging systems lack the tissue penetration depth that is found in magnetic resonance imaging, computed tomography, and positron emission tomography, limiting its use for deeper tissue imaging. This lack of tissue penetration could explain the slight increase in fluorescent intensity seen at days 3 and 7 if the microgels were non-homogenously distributed within the joint space and, in this scenario, largely present towards the patellar tendon during imaging. In addition to the lack of tissue penetration, tissue autofluorescence directly impacts overall fluorophore signal at lower wavelengths (< 700 nm). Other techniques have been explored for measuring in vivo joint retention time using a 14C-labeled crosslinker [85]. However, to measure the 14C signal, animals needed to be euthanized with their joints further processed, not allowing for the measurement of signal within each animal over time. Overall, the in vivo imaging system used in this study allowed for the temporal measurements of fluorescent signal within individual animals, and utilized a Cy7 dye with fluorescent signal at wavelengths greater than tissue autofluoresence to maximize signal measurements.

Our previous work demonstrated that poly(acrylic acid) microgels, although non-viscous, effectively lubricate articular cartilage, potentially due to unique interactions with the articular cartilage [41]. These interactions that lead to improved lubrication of articular cartilage could also enhance retention in the joint. Histological sections of the joint capsule for both the free Cy7 and Cy7-tagged microgels demonstrate comparable morphologies. Articular cartilage of both the condyles and the tibial plateau do not exhibit signs of surface wear, suggesting the Cy7-tagged microgels were not destructive to the joint space. A study evaluating poly(ethyleneglycol) microspheres intra-articularly injected into sheep found the presence of these spheres in histological sections at both 1 and 4 weeks [86]. In the present study, there are not apparent signs of microgel accumulation or entrapment within the synovial capsule, but this might be due to the timepoint of the histological sections being at 8 weeks versus an earlier timepoint. Overall, these histological sections highlight that poly(acrylic acid) microgels are biocompatible and are viable intra-articular vehicles for osteoarthritis treatment.

The goal of this manuscript was to evaluate the controlled delivery of a relevant osteoarthritis therapeutic via simple post-synthesis modifications of polyanionic microgels in addition to determining the residence time of these microgels in vivo. However, there are a few limitations we would like to address. Although there was IL-1Ra release based on the microBCA assays for this period, we did not continue the study to record data points past these 60 days to see if microgels would achieve 100% cumulative release. The concentration of IL-1Ra released for all modified microgels remained steady over the 65 day study (Figs. 4 and 5), and we hypothesize that this trend would continue, although we cannot definitively assume 100% cumulative release. Further work is needed to elucidate the release mechanisms of IL-1Ra from modified microgels (hydrophobic interactions, ionic interactions, etc.) and inform future controlled release studies, but this does not detract from the impact of the current work highlighting the effectiveness of post-synthesis modifications. For the in vitro IL-1ß challenge study, we recognize the number of samples is small. We had limited quantities of IL-1Ra to treat cartilage samples with, as it must be prescribed by a physician to the institution, and could not perform the study with more samples per group due to this limiting factor. To minimize noise from signal, all sample groups were biologically paired by condyle to remove any effects due to sample to sample variance. Finally, we recognize the absence of an OA animal model in this study to investigate the therapeutic efficacy of IL-1Ra loaded microgels. However, we collectively showed release of IL-1Ra within the therapeutic window, treatment of IL-1ß stimulated cartilage using IL-1Ra loaded microgels, and in vivo residence time of microgels in a healthy rat model, where future work will study the efficacy of OA treatment in a relevant animal model. Ultimately, we believe that this study highlights promising trends of poly(acrylic acid) microgels as therapeutic delivery vehicles of bioactive molecules for OA treatment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have shown that simple post-synthesis modifications of poly(acrylic acid) microgels either treating loaded microgels with calcium chloride or modifying microgels with small molecules impacts the uptake and release of IL-1Ra. These modifications allow for the release of IL-1Ra for greater than 60 days, improving upon the initial burst release from unmodified microgels that exhibited 100% cumulative release in less than 6 h. More broadly, this post-loading treatment methodology using calcium can be applied to these gels with different therapeutic proteins, in addition to applying this to other polyanionic drug delivery systems, potentially improving release profiles. More importantly, this post-loading treatment can be applied to a wide variety of drug delivery systems, independent of the drug cargo as this is modifying the hydrogel matrix. With small molecule modified microgels, this creates a simple and efficient way to evaluate the effects of key functional groups on protein interactions to tune the release of relevant therapeutics. In vitro release of IL-1Ra from microgels suggests IL-1Ra remains bioactive and presents a trend of minimizing sGAG loss, inflammation, and degradation from cartilage explants stimulated with IL-1ß. Finally, poly(acrylic acid) microgels exhibited an extended residence time in vivo after intra-articular injections, suggesting that these microgels can be used as controlled drug delivery vehicles for the delivery of therapeutics for OA treatment.
