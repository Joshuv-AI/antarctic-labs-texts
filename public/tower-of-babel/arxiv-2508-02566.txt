Dynamic Feature Selection based on Rule-based Learning for Explainable Classification with Uncertainty Quantification
Javier Fumanal-Idocin, Raquel Fernandez-Peralta, Javier Andreu-Perez

Abstract
Dynamic feature selection (DFS) offers a compelling alternative to traditional, static feature selection by adapting the selected features to each individual sample. Unlike classical methods that apply a uniform feature set, DFS customizes feature selection per sample, providing insight into the decision-making process for each case. DFS is especially significant in settings where decision transparency is key, i.e., clinical decisions; however, existing methods use opaque models, which hinder their applicability in real-life scenarios. This paper introduces a novel approach leveraging a rule-based system as a base classifier for the DFS process, which enhances decision interpretability compared to neural estimators. We also show how this method provides a quantitative measure of uncertainty for each feature query and can make the feature selection process computationally lighter by constraining the feature search space. We also discuss when greedy selection of conditional mutual information is equivalent to selecting features that minimize the difference with respect to the global model predictions. Finally, we demonstrate the competitive performance of our rule-based DFS approach against established and state-of-the-art greedy and RL methods, which are mostly considered opaque, compared to our explainable rule-based system.



Introduction

Many real-world decision-making scenarios involve prohibitively high-dimensional information spaces, where acquiring all possible data is unfeasible due to cost or time constraints
(
Erion et al. 2022
)
. Consider, for instance, a doctor diagnosing a patient. The doctor must select only the right clinical trials to conduct, as they are expensive in terms of both money and time, which are usually scarce resources. Only a small subset will be truly relevant in accurately diagnosing the patient’s specific condition, and this subset will be different for each patient. The most intuitive and effective approach in such situations is to adapt the information acquisition process to the subject’s condition. This methodology, where features are acquired dynamically based on their evolving relevance, is commonly referred to as Dynamic Feature Selection (DFS)
(
Melville et al. 2004
)
.

The existing machine learning literature on DFS primarily explores two main approaches: reinforcement learning (RL)
(
Liu et al. 2021
;
Janisch, Pevnỳ, and Lisỳ 2019
)
and greedy optimization
(
Chen et al. 2015
)
. RL-based approaches offer the potential to discover optimal policies for feature acquisition, but they come with the inherent challenges of RL training, including significant computational demands and potential convergence issues
(
Erion et al. 2022
)
. Greedy optimization methods, on the other hand, maximize a measure that relates the known inputs with the response variable
(
Chattopadhyay et al. 2023
)
. Usually, this measure is the conditional mutual information (CMI) between input variables and the response variable, which has some desirable qualities in terms of uncertainty and prediction power
(
Covert et al. 2023
;
Gadgil, Covert, and Lee 2023
)
.

Significant research within CMI-based DFS methods focuses on the task of accurately estimating CMI itself, which often requires strong prior assumptions about the data distribution. It is possible to use a generative model to learn the feature distribution, which then makes the CMI estimation straightforward
(
Ma et al. 2018
)
, though these can be computationally expensive to train and lead to slow inference. More recent research has also proposed gradient-based optimization with a neural regressor for CMI estimation, which does not need generative models, using a value network with a loss function that recovers CMI at optimality
(
Gadgil, Covert, and Lee 2023
)
.
Other strategies bypass direct CMI computation, focusing instead on identifying the feature with maximum CMI without explicitly calculating its value
(
Covert et al. 2023
)
. A good property of these CMI-based estimation models is that maximizing CMI during the selection process naturally leads to uncertainty minimization
(
Chen et al. 2018
)
.

However, these methods have been tied to neural-based classifiers, which is not good for explainability. For example, suppose we want to analyze the impact of each feature in the final classification. In that case, we still need to use a post hoc method, such as LIME or SHAP, even when an intuitive policy has controlled the feature selection process.
Recent works have focused on using interpretable-by-design models, which makes decisions easier to understand by a human user
(
Rudin 2019
;
Molnar 2020
)
. Families of such methods include rule-based and tree-based models, where the output is the direct consequent of one or various logical predicates
(
Marques-Silva 2023
)
, linear and logistic regressions, whose weights clearly reveal the relationship between feature and prediction
(
Letzgus et al. 2022
)
, and nearest neighbour models, like prototype models, where the output is explained by their similarity to other samples where ground truth was available
(
Nauta, Van Bree, and Seifert 2021
;
Fernández et al. 2022
)
. Besides, the notion of uncertainty in previous works is constrained to the relations between variables, but they do not consider how well the model approximates those variables.

Our focus in this paper is to create a robust DFS algorithm that is intuitive and explainable to the user and has sound uncertainty management. This allows the user to have a transparent feature acquisition process, where the information gained in each feature query is clear, and the final decision is also interpretable.

To do that, we focus on the following issues regarding DFS:

•

We propose to use a global model that is trained on the whole set of features as an oracle model. This will allow us to model uncertainty without needing more data than other approaches.

•

We discuss the advantages of using interpretable-by-design rule-based classifiers for interpretability and uncertainty estimation. For that, we also study both fuzzy and classical logic-based rules.

•

We adapt the aleatoric uncertainty estimation error of the existing CMI-based DFS approaches to rule-based models, and we propose a new way of estimating the epistemic uncertainty, which has not been considered by those methods.

Background

Dynamic Feature Selection: Problem Formulation

Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{M})
be an input feature vector and
y\in\{1,\dots,C\}
a corresponding target label in a supervised learning paradigm. The complete set of
N
input samples is denoted as
X=\{\mathbf{x}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{N}\}
. A partially observed sample, where only features indexed by
S
have been observed, is denoted as
\mathbf{x}_{S}=\{x_{i}:i\in S\}
. The set of unobserved features is denoted as
\bar{S}=\{1,\dots,M\}\setminus S
.

The objective of DFS is to design a feature selection policy that aims to correctly classify each sample by selecting the most informative features. This policy adapts the selection process based on the specific characteristics of each instance, improving both classification accuracy and interpretability.

Conditional Mutual Information for Dynamic Feature Selection

Conditional Mutual Information (CMI) provides a principled measure for evaluating feature relevance in dynamic feature selection. Given three random variables
X
,
Y
, and
Z
, the CMI quantifies the information shared between
X
and
Y
when
Z
is known. We present two equivalent formulations:

I(X;Y|Z)=H(X|Z)-H(X|Y,Z),

(1)

where
H(\cdot|\cdot)
denotes the conditional entropy. This formulation measures the reduction in uncertainty about
X
when
Y
is observed, beyond what is already known from
Z
. The CMI can also be expressed as the Kullback-Leibler divergence between joint and marginal distributions:

I(X;Y|Z)=D_{\text{KL}}\left(p(X,Y|Z)\parallel p(X|Z)p(Y|Z)\right).

(2)

For dynamic feature selection, we instantiate these variables as:

\displaystyle I(y;x_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{S})

\displaystyle=D_{\text{KL}}\left(p(x_{i},y|\mathbf{x}_{S})\parallel p(x_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{S})p(y|\mathbf{x}_{S})\right)

(3)

\displaystyle=H(y|\mathbf{x}_{S})-H(y|\mathbf{x}_{S},x_{i}),

(4)

where
\mathbf{x}_{S}
represents the currently observed features and
x_{i}
is a candidate feature.

Rule-Based Reasoning for Classification

In rule-based reasoning systems, classifiers are constructed as ensembles of logical rules. Each rule
r
consists of two components:

•

The antecedent: a set of conditions on the input features
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{M})
, denoted as
r_{A}
.

•

The consequent: a predicted class or value that is activated when the antecedent conditions are satisfied.

The truth degree of a rule
r
for a given input
\mathbf{x}
is computed as the logical conjunction (AND operation) of its antecedent conditions:

r(\mathbf{x})=\bigwedge_{t\in r_{A}}t(\mathbf{x}),

(5)

where each condition
t(\mathbf{x})
evaluates to either true (1) or false (0). This framework can also be generalized using non-classical logic: in fuzzy logic, conditions evaluate to real values in the
[0,1]
range, and the AND operation generalizes to triangular norms (most commonly the standard multiplication).

A typical classification rule might take the form:

If

x_{1}\in[a,b]

AND

x_{2}\leq\theta

then predict
class
c
,

where
[a,b]
defines an interval for feature
x_{1}
and
\theta
is a threshold for feature
x_{2}
.
A fuzzy rule, however, would look like this:

If

x_{1}\textit{ is }\text{Medium}

AND

x_{2}\textit{ is }\text{Low}

then predict
class
c
,

where “Medium” and “Low” would be fuzzy membership functions that correspond to the user’s interpretation of the input space.

Independent of the type of logical rule used, for sufficient rule systems, the outcome is determined by the rule with the highest firing degree, which we denote as
r^{*}
:

r^{*}=\argmax_{r\in R}r(\mathbf{x}).

(6)

Rule-based systems can also be organized hierarchically, with different levels of rule specificity. For example, rules can form decision trees where each path from root to leaf represents a rule. These structures partition the state so that only one rule can fire for each sample at the same time; however, they can also make the model harder for the user to understand if they are too complicated.

Dynamic Feature Selection with respect to a Rule-Based Global Model

DFS is usually solved by having a value estimator for each feature and a model that predicts the target variable using any subset of input features. Here, we are going to suppose that we already have a sufficient rule-based model,
f_{\theta}
, trained on the whole set of features, and whose performance is optimal compared to any model trained on any subset of features.
We shall refer to the prediction of the global model as
p(\hat{y}|\mathbf{x})
, and to the prediction done by any sub-model
f_{\theta,S}
on the
S
feature index set as
p(\hat{y}|\mathbf{x}_{S})
. Our DFS policy will focus on approximating the global model’s predictions for each sample, instead of directly solving the classification problem.

Our value function,
v_{q}
, computes the expected quality increase with respect to the new variable, measured using a function
q(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{S})
that quantifies the improvement of the prediction quality of
f_{\theta,S\cup\{i\}}
with respect to
f_{\theta}
:

v_{q}(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{S})=\mathbb{E}_{p(x_{i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S})}\left[q(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{S})\right].

(7)

The quality function is based on the idea of which feature will help the sub-model recover the same decision that the full model would make. Other approaches define this function by computing the prediction difference between the two models. However, these approaches consider that if
p(\hat{y}|\mathbf{x})\simeq p(\hat{y}|\mathbf{x}_{S})
, then those probabilities should have a similar calibration and confidence, which is not necessarily true
(
Postels et al. 2021
;
Chen, Choi, and Darwiche 2015
)
. This is why in this work we also take into account the epistemic uncertainty. Indeed, our quality function involves two core concepts
(
Hüllermeier and Waegeman 2021
)
:

•

The aleatoric uncertainty with respect to the final model,
u(\mathbf{x}_{S})
, which is usually measured by the difference between
p(\hat{y}|\mathbf{x}_{S})
and
p(\hat{y}|\mathbf{x})
.

•

The epistemic uncertainty,
e(\mathbf{x}_{S})
, which estimates how confident the model is in its decision. This is important because we want the sub-model to have robust decisions, as confident as possible in comparison with the global model.

Thus, our policy will consist of greedily selecting the next feature that will make the sub-model approximate the decision of the full model, given the observed data. For this, we are interested in adding in each step the feature that minimizes the overall uncertainty with respect to the global model:

q(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{S})=u(\mathbf{x}_{S}\cup\{x_{i}\})+\lambda e(\mathbf{x}_{S}\cup\{x_{i}\}).

(8)

Where lambda is a multiplier that regulates the importance of epistemic uncertainty, as there are multiple solutions with the same, and we are interested in the one with better predictive value (which should be prioritized).

Next, we discuss how we compute each component of Eq. (
8
).

Uncertainty concerning the prediction

For computing the prediction difference of the sub-model defined by
S
with respect to the full model, we can consider the Kullback–Leibler divergence:

u(\mathbf{x}_{S})=D_{\text{KL}}(p(\hat{y}|\mathbf{x})\|p(\hat{y}|\mathbf{x}_{S})).

(9)

We can parametrise this function easily to include the additional feature queried,
x_{i}
:

u(\mathbf{x}_{S}\cup\{x_{i}\})=D_{\text{KL}}(p(\hat{y}|\mathbf{x})\|p(\hat{y}|\mathbf{x}_{S}\cup\{x_{i}\})).

(10)

Under the assumption that the global and partial models produce proportional predictions, we can show that this expression is minimized by the same
x_{i}

as maximizing the CMI, since the following proportionality holds:

I(x_{i};\hat{y}|\mathbf{x}_{S})\propto\mathbb{E}_{p(x_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{S})}\left[u(\mathbf{x}_{S})-u(\mathbf{x}_{S}\cup\{x_{i}\})\right].

(11)

We prove this fact in Appendix A. This shows that, although minimizing the aleatoric uncertainty with respect to the global model is closely related to the CMI and KL-based approaches in the literature (e.g.,
(
Covert et al. 2023
;
Gadgil, Covert, and Lee 2023
;
Ma et al. 2018
)
), the two approaches are strictly equivalent only under the assumption that the subset of features considered already recovers the global model.

Uncertainty concerning the model’s confidence

Epistemic uncertainty allows us to distinguish between cases where the probability output came from a rule observation with high truth degree (and thus, low epistemic uncertainty) and cases where the sample was not very well covered by any rule (which would show high epistemic uncertainty).

A classical way to do this in fuzzy association rule classifiers is to compute the negation of the truth degree of the firing rule
(
Shiraishi, Ishibuchi, and Nakata 2025
)
:

e_{r}(\mathbf{x}_{S})=1-r^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{S}).

(12)

This expression, however, can be somewhat misleading, as sometimes the confidence metrics of a rule can be better calibrated for samples whose firing strength is closer to the expected average firing strength than samples where the firing strength is
1
(a further discussion on this can be found in Appendix B). Even more, this metric is quite different from the standard notion of epistemic uncertainty, which is related to the statistical robustness of the parameters of the model
(
Hüllermeier and Waegeman 2021
)
. To avoid using ensemble methods to quantify uncertainty, as they are expensive to compute for fuzzy systems, we leverage the fact that what determines the output for a sample is solely the region in which the sample is contained and its corresponding confident estimates. The most natural way to check how sure the model is of the final prediction is to measure how it would change if the firing rule were different, or if it were another area that fired. So, we propose to use the following expression:

e_{R}(\mathbf{\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{r\in R}r(\mathbf{x})D_{\text{KL}}(p_{r}(\hat{y})||p_{r^{*}}(\hat{y})),

(13)

where
p_{r}(\hat{y})
is the vector of rule confidence for the rule
r
for each class. This expression only uses the rules already present in the classifier, so we do not need to compute additional rules or classifiers. Since in DFS we are not working with all the features, the evaluation of
r(\mathbf{x})
must be substituted by
r(\mathbf{x}_{S})
, which is equivalent to setting the conditions where features are not observed as 1, which makes them irrelevant when computing the truth degree of the rule.

For classical logic systems, ensemble and Bayesian methods are used to compute this uncertainty. Bayesian methods are impractical because Bayesian additive regression trees require adding up multiple paths, so they are not as explainable as conventional decision trees
(
Chipman, George, and McCulloch 2010
)
. However, we can use different auxiliary CART models to compute their predictions to obtain a similar expression as in Eq. (
13
). Considering the set of these auxiliary classifiers as
I
, and
p^{i}_{r^{*}}
as the prediction of the winning rule of the
i
-th model:

e_{R}(\mathbf{\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{|I|}\sum_{i\in I}D_{\text{KL}}(p^{i}_{r^{*}}(\hat{y})||p_{r^{*}}(\hat{y})).

(14)

When adapting this from
\mathbf{x}
to
\mathbf{x}_{S}
, we can proceed as in the previous equation. However, classical logic-based systems are usually used in tree structures. This approach would cause problems because it can make two different rules fire, which is problematic for tree structures. Our solution for this is to substitute the missing features with the average value of that feature in the training set. This is more efficient than having to traverse diverse paths in the tree, and it works well in practice, as we show in our experimentation section.

Leveraging Rule-Based Reasoning as Global Model

Besides the inherent explainability of rule-based reasoning, we can also leverage it to make the problem computationally lighter and to ensure that uncertainty in every step of the process is properly estimated. We show this in the following.

Reducing Search Space

The output of a rule-based classifier is determined by:

\hat{y}=c,\quad\text{where }r^{*}=\argmax_{r\in R}r(\mathbf{x})

where
c
denotes the consequent of rule
r^{*}
. A key property emerges from the conjunctive nature of rule antecedents; the truth value of any rule
r
is bounded by its weakest condition:

r(\mathbf{x})\leq\min_{t\in r_{A}}t(\mathbf{x})

where
r_{A}
. This bound enables efficient search space reduction through two mechanisms:

1.

Early termination: if any condition
t(\mathbf{x})
evaluates to 0 (completely false), we can immediately discard rule
r
without evaluating remaining conditions. We can also set that value to an arbitrary threshold
\theta>0
, which can be especially useful when working with fuzzy logic conditions.

2.

Feature selection: the active feature space can be reduced to:

S_{\text{{active}}}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in R\\
r(\mathbf{x}_{S})>\theta\end{subarray}}\bigcup_{t\in r_{A}}\text{feature}(t)

where
\text{feature}(t)
denotes index of the feature involved in condition
t
.

This optimization significantly reduces computational complexity while preserving the exactness of the rule evaluation process.

Uncertainty estimation for subrules

In rule-based systems, we can also quantify aleatoric uncertainty through rule confidence, defined for a rule
r_{A}\Rightarrow c
as:

\text{Confidence}(r_{A}\Rightarrow c)=\frac{\text{Support}(r_{A}\cap c)}{\text{Support}(r_{A})}=p(c|r_{A}).

Even when we have incomplete observations that affect the rule, we can evaluate the expression using only the observed conditions,
r_{A_{S}}
as explained before.

Obtaining a similar estimation with neural classifiers is more complicated because neural networks are not calibrated in standard training procedures, which makes the predictions significantly overconfident
(
Guo et al. 2017
)
, even when temperature in training is controlled to address this
(
Van Amersfoort et al. 2021
)
.

Estimating the value function

There are two possibilities to compute the value function: one is to use a generative model that learns
p(x_{i}|\mathbf{x}_{S})

(
Ma et al. 2018
)
; however, this is computationally expensive and tends to overfit to training data. The other solution is to learn a neural estimator that approximates the quality function in Eq. (
8
) for any
S_{active}
using feature masks, which is a similar methodology to the one proposed for CMI approximation in
(
Covert et al. 2023
;
Gadgil, Covert, and Lee 2023
)
.

For our case, one option is to use the estimator to directly
learn to approximate Eq. (
7
), taking
\mathbf{x}_{S}
and
p(\hat{y}|\mathbf{x}_{S})
as input, which results in the following training objective:

\min_{\phi}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{S}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{i}}l(h_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{S}),q(x_{i},\mathbf{x}_{S})),

(15)

where
l(a,b)=(a-b)^{2}
,
h
is the neural estimator and
\phi
its parameters.

However, in order to monitor epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty at the same time, we can train a network with two heads that jointly estimates both uncertainties. So, the training objective becomes:

\begin{split}\min_{\phi}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{S}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{i}}l(h_{u,i}(\mathbf{\mathbf{x}_{S}}),u(\mathbf{x}_{S}\cup\{x_{i}\}))+\\
+l(h_{e,i}(\mathbf{x_{S}}),e_{R}(\mathbf{x}_{S}\cup\{x_{i}\})).\end{split}

(16)

Related work

Feature selection is a classical problem in the static setting, where each feature is typically assigned a global importance score
(
Jović, Brkić, and Bogunović 2015
)
. Existing approaches include heuristic methods
(
Oreski and Oreski 2014
)
, gradient-based continuous relaxations
(
Balın, Abid, and Zou 2019
;
Lee, Imrie, and van der Schaar 2022
)
, and computationally demanding metaheuristics like genetic optimization
(
Oh, Lee, and Moon 2004
)
. A notable variation of this problem incorporates feature acquisition costs, transforming it into a knapsack-style optimization task that can be addressed via linear programming
(
Erion et al. 2022
)
. However, this approach cannot be extended to DFS because the assumption of sub-modularity does not hold
(
Chen et al. 2015
)
.

For DFS, Reinforcement learning (RL) has been proposed to solve this problem
(
Chen, Gao, and Wu 2024
;
Li and Oliva 2024
;
Yin et al. 2020
)
, as RL methods have been extremely popular for policy learning in many control settings
(
Wang et al. 2022
)
. However, existing RL-based methods often suffer from training instability in DFS, which leads to subpar performance compared to other DFS and static approaches
(
Covert et al. 2023
)
. Another popular method is to use feature scoring methods, which select features individually
(
Ma et al. 2018
;
Covert et al. 2023
)
or group-wise
(
Takahashi, Takeuchi, and Kashima 2025
)
based on a predefined metric and greedy policy. Our method falls into this category.

DFS is also connected to explainability, as many applications where it is relevant also require transparent reasoning.
From the family of explainable AI classifiers, rule-based ones are considered one of the most reliable and trustworthy
(
Lim, Dey, and Avrahami 2009
)
. The main issue with rule-based reasoners is that when they consist of very complex rules, or they have many of them, then the rule base itself becomes as complex as a black box model
(
Sweller et al. 2011
;
Fumanal-Idocin, Fernandez-Peralta, and Andreu-Perez 2025
)
. The main issue of rule-based classification is that optimizing a rule set that is compact enough to be useful and that also performs well is not a solved problem. Existing rule-based classification algorithms solve this by exploiting sub-modularity
(
Lakkaraju, Bach, and Leskovec 2016
)
, fuzzy logic, and genetic optimization to obtain a compact system
(
Fumanal Idocin and Andreu-Perez 2024
)
and continuous relaxations with gradient descent
(
Wang et al. 2021
;
Fumanal-Idocin, Fernandez-Peralta, and Andreu-Perez 2025
)
.

Results

Comparing fuzzy rule bases and CART

(Figure
1
)
We tried the fuzzy rule-based classifier (FRBC) method on three datasets only, as the computational cost of fitting the genetic algorithm to fit the fuzzy rules is prohibitive for larger datasets. Performance-wise, the FRBC is less accurate, though it is also the most compact one in terms of complexity, i.e., it has the lowest number of rules and average condition length (Table
2
). This is especially visible in the wine dataset, where the FRBC required all features to significantly improve the accuracy, while CART had a much smoother progression. The evolution of the prediction uncertainty was also smoother in CART. Epistemic uncertainty, however, is not so easily reduced as prediction uncertainty. For example, in the Heart dataset, epistemic uncertainty remains high until
50\%
of the features have been collected. However, a strong correlation between the two is observed in all cases.

In the DFS setting, we found that the results were good for a low number of features, but the evolution of the accuracy is somewhat flat compared to other methods, which meant that the DFS method works well (compared to the global model) when the number of features is low, but requires a lot of features to recover the full model. The most notable example of this is the Wine dataset, where the last feature made the system gain almost
20\%
of accuracy. We can also see that up until that last feature, the evolution of the uncertainty reduction was almost flat as well.

Comparison with other methods

(Table
3
)
To compare with other methods, we tested the accuracy obtained for the first
10
features. Our experimental results demonstrate that the proposed rule-based DFS approach achieves competitive performance compared to both static and dynamic feature selection methods. As shown in Table 3, the CART-DFS variant of our method achieves an average accuracy of 69.43%, and outperforms several neural-based DFS methods such as CWCF (
64.96\%
) and INVASE (
66.29\%
). Notably, our method performs particularly well on datasets like Wine and Cirrhosis, achieving

Conclusions and Future Lines

This work introduces a rule-based classifier as a global model for Dynamic Feature Selection (DFS), where the feature acquisition process is designed to recover the global model’s decision properties. Specifically, we focus on prediction similarity and epistemic uncertainty of the predictions, which builds a robust estimation of the global decision for each subset of features. We used fuzzy rule-based and CART classifiers, as they provide a transparent decision to the final user, which can be very relevant in some contexts where DFS is especially promising, like clinical settings.

In our experiments, we have studied five real-world datasets, both clinical and non-clinical, which demonstrate that rule-based DFS achieves accuracy comparable to state-of-the-art neural methods while offering superior explainability in terms of decision transparency and uncertainty management. We also found that reinforcement learning-based DFS methods offered less performance than static and greedy-based DFS algorithms.

Our future research shall focus on some of the limitations of this work. First, the rule-based classifier was trained a priori in this study, but recent research introduces a framework in which it could be jointly trained with the DFS selector using gradient descent
(
Fumanal-Idocin, Fernandez-Peralta, and Andreu-Perez 2025
)
, which can also help performance. Additionally, we are going to extend the framework to non-uniform feature costs and investigate ways to exploit the DFS setting to improve the performance-explainability trade-off.

Appendix A

First of all, we prove the following equality regarding the difference between the aleatoric uncertainty of considering an additional feature to the already considered set
S
.

Lemma 1
.

\Delta u_{i}=-\sum_{\hat{y}}p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\log\left(\frac{p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S},x_{i})}{p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S})}\right),

where
\Delta u_{i}=u(\mathbf{x}_{S}\cup\{x_{i}\})-u(\mathbf{x}_{S})
.

Proof.

\displaystyle u(\mathbf{x}_{S}\cup\{x_{i}\})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\hat{y}}p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\log\left(\frac{p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x})}{p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S},x_{i})}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\hat{y}}p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\log\left(\frac{p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x})}{p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S},x_{i})}\right)

\displaystyle\pm\sum_{\hat{y}}p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\log(p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u(\mathbf{x}_{S})+\sum_{\hat{y}}p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\log\left(\frac{p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S})}{p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S},x_{i})}\right).

∎

Now, suppose we assume that the partial models produce predictions that are proportional to those of the global model. In that case, we can show that the CMI is also proportional to the expected reduction in aleatoric uncertainty.

Proposition 1
.

If
p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\propto p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S},x_{i})
then

-\mathbb{E}_{p(x_{i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S})}[\Delta u_{i}]\propto I(x_{i};\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S}).

Proof.

According to Lemma
1
we have

\Delta u_{i}=-\sum_{\hat{y}}p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\log\left(\frac{p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S},x_{i})}{p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S})}\right).

If
p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\propto p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S},x_{i})
then

\Delta u_{i}\propto-p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S},x_{i})\log\left(\frac{p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S},x_{i})}{p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S})}\right)

Now, since
p(x_{i},\hat{y}\mid x_{S})=p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S},x_{i})p(x_{i}\mid x_{S})
we have that

\Delta u_{i}\propto-\frac{p(x_{i},\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S})}{p(x_{i}\mid x_{S})}\log\left(\frac{p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S},x_{i})}{p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S})}\right).

Since,

I(x_{i};\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S})=\sum_{x_{i},\hat{y}}p(x_{i},\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S})\log\left(\frac{p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S},x_{i})}{p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S})}\right),

we have obtained the desired result.
∎

Thus, in this case, the next feature obtained by minimizing the expected aleatoric uncertainty coincides with the one selected by those methods that maximize the CMI. This shows that our methodology is only equivalent to those methods in the case of already having a feature subset that recovers the global model.

Corollary 1
.

If
p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x})\propto p(\hat{y}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S},x_{i})
then

\argmin_{i\in\bar{S}}\mathbb{E}_{p(x_{i}\mid\mathbf{x}_{S})}[u(x_{S}\cup\{x_{i}\})]=\argmax_{i\in\bar{S}}I(x_{i};\hat{y}|x_{S}).

Appendix B: Rule Confidence Calibration

The calibration error of fuzzy rule confidence estimates varies as a function of membership degree
\mu_{r}(\mathbf{x})
, which may not occur at maximal membership (
\mu_{r}(\mathbf{x})=1
) but rather at intermediate membership values.

For rule
r:r_{A}\Rightarrow c
, the fuzzy support is defined as:

\text{Support}(r_{A})=\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}}\mu_{r}(\mathbf{x})}{|\mathcal{D}|},

(17)

and the rule confidence is:

\text{Confidence}(r)=\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}}\mu_{r}(\mathbf{x})\mathbb{I}(y(\mathbf{x})=c)}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}}\mu_{r}(\mathbf{x})}.

(18)

To analyse calibration, we partition the data by membership degree intervals. For interval
I_{k}=[\mu_{k-1},\mu_{k})
, let
\mathcal{D}_{k,r}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}:\mu_{r}(\mathbf{x})\in I_{k}\}
. The empirical confidence at membership level
k
is:

\text{Confidence}_{k}(r)=\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}_{k,r}}\mu_{r}(\mathbf{x})\mathbb{I}(y(\mathbf{x})=c)}{|\mathcal{D}_{k,r}|}.

(19)

However, the predicted confidence for samples in
\mathcal{D}_{k,r}
when using the global rule confidence is still
\text{Confidence}(r)
. Moreover, we can see that the global confidence is a weighted average of local confidences:

\text{Confidence}(r)=\sum_{k}\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}_{k,r}}\mu_{r}(\mathbf{x})\mathbb{I}(y(\mathbf{x})=c)}{\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{D}}\mu_{r}(\mathbf{x})}.

(20)

This means
\text{Confidence}(r)
will be closest to
\text{Confidence}_{k}(r)
for membership levels
k
that have the highest weight in the fraction. For example, if the membership distribution is skewed toward lower values, the confidence will be best calibrated for those regions with the highest membership-weighted representation, not necessarily at
\mu_{r}(\mathbf{x})=1
.
