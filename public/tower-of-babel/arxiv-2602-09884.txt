Grouped Stirling complexes
Alessia Revelli and Steven Scheirer

Abstract
. Given a graph G G , a configuration space of G G can be thought of as the set of all possible configurations of “robots” which can move throughout G G , subject to some constraints. We introduce a type of configuration space which we call Grouped Stirling complexes , denoted by S r → ​ ( G ) S_{\vec{r}}(G) , in which we place robots in groups subject to two constraints. First, there must be at least one robot on each vertex of G G , and second, any two robots from the same group must be “separated by at least one full open edge” of G G . The space S r → ​ ( G ) S_{\vec{r}}(G) has a closed cell structure, which means it can be built out of cells of various dimensions. Our main results show S r → ​ ( G ) S_{\vec{r}}(G) is path-connected, provided there are at least three groups, and determine the number of cells of S r → ​ ( G ) S_{\vec{r}}(G) in certain cases.

2.
Definitions and Basic Properties of
S_{\vec{r}}(G)

We start with the definition of a grouped Stirling complex. We view a graph
G
as a topological space, so that the elements of
G
are individual points on the graph. We use
V(G)
and
E(G)
to denote the set of vertices and closed edges of
G

|V(G)|
by
n
and denote
|E(G)|
by
m.
By a “closed edge”
e,
we mean the set of all points on the edge
e,
including the endpoints. By the “interior” of
e,
we mean all points on
e
, excluding the endpoints.

Definition 2.1
.

A
color vector
is a vector of the form
\vec{r}=(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\dots,\ell_{r})
with
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}.
We refer to the integers
1,2,\dots,r
as
colors
, and for each color
i

\ell_{i}
is the number of
i
-colored robots. Given a graph
G
and a color vector
\vec{r}
, the
grouped Stirling complex

S_{\vec{r}}(G)
consists of all
r
-tuples of the form

A=(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{r})

such that:

(a)

A_{i}\subset G
and
|A_{i}|=\ell_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,r
. We refer to
A_{i}
as the
locations of the
i
-colored robots,
and
A
as a
configuration
in
S_{\vec{r}}(G).

(b)

For each vertex
v\in V(G)
, there is some
i
such that
v\in A_{i}
. In other words, for each vertex
v,
there is some color
i
such that there is an
i
-colored robot on

(c)

If
A_{i}
contains a point on the interior of some

, then
A_{i}
cannot contain either endpoint of
e
, or any other points on the interior of any edge which shares an endpoint with
e.
In other words, any two
i
-colored robots must be separated by at least a full open edge of
G
.

Remark
.

If the color vector
\vec{r}
is of the form
\vec{r}=(1,1,\dots,1)
(i.e. there is only one robot of each color), then the grouped Stirling complex

As a first observation, we determine necessary and sufficient conditions for the space
S_{\vec{r}}(G)
to be non-empty.

Proposition 2.3
.

If
G
is any graph and
\vec{r}=(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{r})
is any color vector, then
S_{\vec{r}}(G)
is non-empty if and only if:

(1)

\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{r}\geq n
, and

(2)

\ell_{i}\leq n
for each
i=1,\dots,r
.

Proof.

If
\vec{r}
satisfies conditions (1) and (2), we can define a configuration
A=(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{r})\in S_{\vec{r}}(G)
by first placing each vertex of
G
in some
A_{i}
while keeping
|A_{i}|\leq\ell_{i}
(this is possible due to condition (1)). Then, for each
i
, if
|A_{i}|<\ell_{i},
condition (2) allows us to arbitrarily place
\ell_{i}-|A_{i}|
additional vertices in
A_{i}
. The resulting
r
-tuple
A
is a configuration in
S_{\vec{r}}(G).

Conversely, if
A=(A_{1},\dots,A_{r})
is a configuration in
S_{\vec{r}}(G),
then since each
v\in V(G)
must be in some
A_{i},
we must have
\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{r}\geq n
, and property (c) of
2.1
forces
\ell_{i}\leq n
for each
i=1,\dots,r
.
∎

We make two additional observations. First, if
\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{r}=n
, then in any configuration, there must be exactly one robot at each vertex, so that no robots can move. In this case,
S_{\vec{r}}(G)
is a discrete space.

Second, if
\ell_{i}=n
for some

, then the
i
-colored robots must be located at each vertex of

is automatically satisfied by color

is homeomorphic to a product of unordered discrete spaces of the form

\mathrm{UD}^{\ell_{1}}(G)\times\cdots\times\mathrm{UD}^{\ell_{r}}(G).

So, we will only be interested in the case in which
\ell_{1}+\cdots+\ell_{r}>n
and
\ell_{i}<n
for each

4.
Cell Counts of
S_{\vec{r}}(G)

In this section, we determine the number of cells of
S_{\vec{r}}(G)
for two classes of color vectors when
G
is a simple graph (i.e. a graph with no loops or multiple edges). We first consider the case in which
\vec{r}=(2,1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. That is, we have a total of
n
colors, with two 1-colored robots and one
k
-colored robot for
k\geq 2,
so that we have a total of
n+1
robots.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
G
be a simple graph with
n\geq 2
vertices and
m
edges, and let
\vec{r}=(2,1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. In
S_{\vec{r}}(G)
we have

\#(\text{i-cells)}=\begin{cases}\frac{n!(n^{2}+n-2)}{4}&\text{if }i=0\\
\frac{m(n-1)!(n^{2}+n-4)}{2}&\text{if }i=1\\
0&\text{if }i\geq 2\\
\end{cases}.

Proof.

In any 0-cell
c
of
S_{\vec{r}}(G)
, there must be two distinct vertices,
x\neq y
, occupied by the two 1-colored robots; there are
\binom{n}{2}
choices for these two vertices. Since there are a total of
n+1
robots and
n
vertices, there must be exactly one available vertex
v
, with
|c(v)|=2.
So, for each of the choices of the vertices for the two 1-colored robots, we have the following two cases:

First, suppose either
v=x
or
v=y
. For each of these two possibilities, there must be some non-available vertex
u
(either
y
or
x
) with
1\in c(u),
so the remaining
n-1
robots can be permuted among the
n-1
vertices in
V(G)\setminus\{u\}
. So, there are
2(n-1)!
possibilities in this case.

Second, if
v\neq x
and
v\neq y,
there must be two other colors
i\neq j
with
i,j\in c(v).
There are
\binom{n-1}{2}
choices for these two colors, then
n-2
choices for the vertex
v
, followed by
(n-3)!
ways to permute the remaining robots among the remaining vertices in
V(G)\setminus\{x,y,v\}
. So, there are
\binom{n-1}{2}\cdot(n-2)\cdot(n-3)!=\binom{n-1}{2}\cdot(n-2)!
possibilities in this case.

Thus, in total, we have

\#(\text{0-cells)}=\binom{n}{2}\left(2(n-1)!+\binom{n-1}{2}(n-2)!\right)=\frac{n!(n^{2}+n-2)}{4}.

For any
1
-cell
c
, there must be one edge
e
with
|c(e)|=1,
and
|c(v)|=1
for each
v\in V(G).
There are
m
choices for the edge. If
c(e)=\{1\},
there are
n-2
choices for the location of the other 1-colored robot (since it can’t fall on either endpoint of
e
), and
(n-1)!
permutations of the remaining
n-1
robots among the remaining
n-1
vertices. If
c(e)=\{i\},
with
i\neq 1,
there are
n-1
choices for
i
,
\binom{n}{2}
choices for the locations of the two
1
-colored robots, and
(n-2)!
permutations of the remaining
n-2
robots.

Thus, in total, we have

\#(\text{1-cells})=m\left((n-2)(n-1)!+(n-1)\binom{n}{2}(n-2)!\right)=\frac{m(n-1)!(n^{2}+n-4)}{2}.

The fact that there are no
i
-cells for
i\geq 2
follows immediately from
2.9
.
∎

Note that under the hypotheses of
4.1
,
S_{\vec{r}}(G)
has only 0-cells and 1-cells, so that
S_{\vec{r}}(G)
is itself a graph. For example,

The main feature of

is in some sense similar to the two circles joined at a point on the right side of

In general, under the hypotheses of

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
G
be a connected simple graph with
n\geq 2
vertices and
m
edges, and let
\vec{r}=(2,1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. The space
S_{\vec{r}}(G)
is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of
L
circles, where

L=\frac{m(n-1)!(n^{2}+n-4)}{2}-\frac{n!(n^{2}+n-2)}{4}+1.

Proof.

For
n=2,
the two 1-colored robots must occupy the two vertices of
G
, so that the one 2-colored robot can move freely throughout
G
. Therefore
S_{\vec{r}}(G)
is homeomorphic to
G
and thus
S_{\vec{r}}(G)
is connected. For
n\geq 3,

S_{\vec{r}}(G)
is connected by
3.10
. The result then follows immediately from the counts of the 0- and 1-cells given in
4.1
.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
G
be a simple graph with
n
vertices and
m
edges, and let
\vec{r}=(n-1,n-1,\dots,n-1)\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
for some integer
r\geq 2
. In
S_{\vec{r}}(G)
we have

\#(\text{i-cells)}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\binom{r}{i}\left(m^{i}n^{r-i}-\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(G)}\deg(v)^{i}\right)&\text{if }0\leq i<r\\
m^{r}+m-\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V(G)}\deg(v)^{r}&\text{if }i=r\\
0&\text{if }i>r\\
\end{cases}.

Proof.

As noted above, to count the number of
i
-cells of
S_{\vec{r}}(G),
it suffices to count the number of valid
i
-edge-
r
-tuples by subtracting the number of invalid
i
-edge-
r
-tuples from (
4
).

If
\tilde{c}
is an invalid
i
-edge-
r
-tuple, then any vertex in
\tilde{c}
must be a
k
-missed vertex for
every
vertex-only color
k
. This means that each vertex in
\tilde{c}
is equal to some fixed vertex
v.
Furthermore, every edge in
\tilde{c}
must have
v
as an endpoint, since if
k
is an edge-color and
e
is the
k
-colored edge, and
v
is not an endpoint of
e
, there would be a
k
-colored robot on
v
(and hence
\tilde{c}
would be valid).

Therefore, every invalid
i
-edge-
r
-tuple is of the form
\tilde{c}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{r}),
where there is some fixed vertex
v
where
x_{k}=v
when
x_{k}
is a vertex, and
x_{k}
has
v
as an endpoint if
x_{k}
is an edge. Thus for each
v\in V(G),
we get an invalid
i
-edge-
r
-tuple
\tilde{c}
by choosing
i
of the
r
components for the edges, then for each of these components, choosing one of the
\deg(v)
edges which have
v
as an endpoint. The remaining components of
\tilde{c}
must be equal to
v.
So, for each fixed vertex
v,
we get
\binom{r}{i}\deg(v)^{i}
invalid
i
-edge-
r
-tuples in which
v
appears.

Furthermore, for
i<r,
any
i
-edge-
r
-tuple must contain at least one vertex, so that in this case, distinct vertices give distinct invalid
i
-edge-
r
-tuples, so, the total number of invalid
i
-edge-
r
-tuples is

(5)

\sum_{v\in V(G)}\binom{r}{i}\deg(v)^{i}.

Subtracting (
5
) from (
4
) gives the result for
i<r.

For
i=r
, any invalid
r
-edge-
r
-tuple
\tilde{c}
must be of the form
\tilde{c}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{r}),
where each
x_{k}
is an edge, with a common endpoint
v.
So for each vertex
v,
we must count the number of
r
-tuples of edges where each edge has
v
as an endpoint. As in the previous case, there are
\deg(v)
choices for each of the
r
edges, so there are a total of
\deg(v)^{r}
invalid
r
-edge-
r
-tuples in which all edges have
v
as an endpoint. Consider the sum

(6)

\sum_{v\in V(G)}\deg(v)^{r}.

Since an invalid
r
-edge-
r
-tuple consists exclusively of edges, for any edge
e
with endpoints
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, the invalid
r
-edge-
r
-tuple
\tilde{c}=(e,e,\dots,e)
is double-counted in (
6
): once in the term corresponding to
v_{1}
and once in the term corresponding to
v_{2}.
If
\tilde{c}
consists of at least two different edges, then the edges in
\tilde{c}
only have one common endpoint, so
\tilde{c}
is only counted once. So, the only invalid
r
-edge-
r
-tuples which are double-counted are of the form
(e,e,\dots,e).
Since there are
m
edges in
G
, there are
m

r
-edge-
r
-tuples which are double-counted.

Therefore, the total number of invalid
r
-edge-
r
-tuples is

(7)

\left(\sum_{v\in V(G)}\deg(v)^{r}\right)-m.

Subtracting (
7
) from (
4
) (with
i=r
) gives the result.

The fact that there are no
i
-cells for
i>r
follows from the fact that for each color
k
, there is at most one
k
-colored edge in any given cell
c
, so there are at most
r
edges in total in
c
.
