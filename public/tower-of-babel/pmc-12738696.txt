Anticholinergic burden and its associated factors among adult outpatients in a resource-limited setting: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Eyob Alemayehu Gebreyohannes, Biniam Siyum Shibe, Wagaye Atalay Taye, Eyayaw Ashete Belachew, Kenneth Lee, Segenet Bizuneh Mengistu, Roy Louis Soiza, Phyo Kyaw Myint, Ousman Abubeker Abdela
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExisting evidence links anticholinergic burden (ACB), the cumulative effect of medicines with anticholinergic activity, with serious adverse effects. There is scarcity of evidence on the extent of use of such medicines in Ethiopian outpatient settings. To assess the prevalence and determinants of high ACB (score ≥ 3) among adult patients, a cross-sectional study was conducted among adult patients (≥ 18 years) attending ambulatory clinic of University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (UoGCSH) between April and September 2022. ACB was calculated based on the medications patients were currently taking, identified from medical records and patient interviews. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of participants. Logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors associated with high ACB (ACB score ≥ 3). 409 patients, median age 50 years (IQR 37–62, range 18–85), participated in this study. About a third (32%) of participants were exposed to at least one medication with anticholinergic activity. Approximately one-tenth (9.5%) of them were exposed to high ACB. Logistic regression analysis indicated that the increase in the number of medications was associated with a 1.57-fold (AOR 1.57; 95% CI 1.22–2.02) increase in the odds of high ACB. Medicines with anticholinergic activity are commonly prescribed to outpatients at UoGSCH, despite relatively younger demographics. Introduction

Medicines with anticholinergic activity work by blocking the action of acetylcholine transmission in muscarinic receptors1,2. Blockage of cholinergic transmission can be either their primary therapeutic action or a secondary effect, unintended anticholinergic activity, which may lead to adverse effects3. It has been well documented that clinicians prescribe medicines with anticholinergic activity based on their therapeutic benefits, but may overlook their risk of adverse effects4. The cumulative effect of medicines with anticholinergic activity is known as anticholinergic burden (ACB) and has been shown to be associated with adverse drug events, primarily central nervous system-related events including confusion, delirium, cognitive impairment, and falls5,6. For example, cholinergic blockade in the central nervous system has been linked to adverse effects such as confusion, behavioral disturbances, reduced executive and motor functions, altered emotions and increased risk of falls, delirium, chronic cognitive impairment and mortality7.

Older patients are more vulnerable from adverse effects of ACB than younger patients due to a decrement in metabolic activity, deferred elimination, downgrading of cholinergic transmissions, and markup permeability of blood–brain barrier1. Nonetheless, some studies also reported adverse effects related to ACB in children and younger adults8,9. For example, ACB and associated xerostomia in younger adults were linked with higher incidences of dental problems such as tooth decay10.

Many of the studies addressing ACB have been conducted in the inpatient setting, on older patients and/or patients with existing cognitive or neuropsychiatric impairment, neglecting the outpatient setting7,11–16. Outpatient settings often experience fragmented care, where services are delivered by multiple providers. This lack of effective coordination across different aspects of care increases the risk of inappropriate prescribing17. Furthermore, studies from a low and middle-income setting are lacking. Understanding ACB in the outpatient setting is crucial. Unlike medications administered for acute disease management in hospitalized patients, those prescribed for outpatients are typically intended for long-term use especially in patients with chronic conditions. Consequently, the prolonged intake of these medications heightens the risk of enduring ACB over time. Additionally, considering that only 3% of the Ethiopian population is estimated to be aged 65 and above18, focusing exclusively on the older patients would overlook a substantial segment of the population. To the best of authors’ knowledge, no published study is available to date which highlights the extent of ACB in the outpatient settings in Ethiopia. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of ACB among outpatients in a resource-limited setting. A secondary aim was to investigate the determinants of ACB in this setting.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study was carried out as part of a larger research project. In the current report, we present findings from a cross-sectional study conducted between April and September 2022 at the ambulatory clinic (outpatient setting) of University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (UoGCSH). The clinic operates primarily under the expertise of medical doctors, encompassing various specialties, alongside dedicated nursing staff, available 5 days a week. It primarily serves patients with chronic conditions.

Eligibility criteria

Adult patients (≥ 18 years of age) attending follow-up appointments at the ambulatory clinic of UoGCSH were invited to participate in the study ahead of data collection. Those who declined to participate and those with incomplete medical records were excluded.

Sample size determination and sampling techniques

A minimum sample size of 331 was determined by using a single population proportion formula to estimate the prevalence of anticholinergic medication use among adult outpatients, with a 31.3% prevalence of anticholinergic medication use19, 5% margin of error and 95% level of confidence20. A convenience sampling technique was used to recruit participants.

Study variables and data collection

Relevant data were obtained from medical records and self-administered questionnaires. In instances where participants either preferred assistance or faced difficulty completing the questionnaires independently, interviews were conducted with them instead. The outcome variable was ACB, which was defined as the cumulative exposure to anticholinergic effects resulting from taking one or more medicines possessing anticholinergic properties. It was determined by using the Anticholinergic Cognitive Burden Score (ACBS) as described by Boustani and colleagues. We used the ACBS because it is one of the most widely validated tools globally allowing for easier comparison of our findings with the existing literatures. Accordingly, each medication is assigned a potency rating of 1, 2, or 3, where 1 indicates possible anticholinergic activity without consistent clinical evidence, 2 indicates moderate evidence of cognitive effects, and 3 indicates strong clinical evidence of adverse cognitive outcomes. The overall burden is then calculated as the cumulative sum of these scores21. A total score ≥ 3 was classified as high ACB, while a score < 3 was classified as low ACB, based on the common utilization of this cut-off point in other studies, ensuring consistency and comparability2,21,22. Both prescription and over-the-counter medications were taken into account in our ACB calculations. We included medications used by each patient at the time of data collection, drawing from review of their medical records and comprehensive patient interviews. Medications prescribed at the time of data collection, but not yet taken by patients, were excluded from the calculations.

The independent variables were socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants including age, sex, residence, marital status, educational status, and socioeconomic class. We used participants’ self-reported jobs for categorizing socioeconomic status based on an occupation-based classification scheme of the Registrar General’s occupation-based classification scheme across five categories23,24. Social Class I consists of professionals, Class II includes managerial and technical occupations, Class III includes skilled workers, Class IV consists of partly skilled workers, and Class V comprises unskilled manual workers. In instances involving retired participants, their most recent employment was utilized for categorization purposes.

Other independent variables included clinical and medicine-related information such as medications each participant was taking, comorbid conditions, Charlson comorbidity index score (CCI, a method of categorizing comorbidities of patients by taking into accounts both the number and severity of 19 pre-defined comorbid conditions. Each condition was assigned a weight from 1 to 6, with total scores ranging from 0 to 3725), the number of medicines (which was also used to define polypharmacy, the use of ≥ 5 concurrent medicines26), and prior hospital admission in the past 30 days.

Data quality management

Following a virtual (by EAG, PKM, RLS, and OAA) and in-person (by OAA) training encompassing instrument contents, data collection techniques, data management, documentation procedures, and ethical considerations, a postgraduate clinical pharmacy student collected the data. Prior to commencing data collection and following consent, random 5% of the overall sample underwent a pre-test, with the subsequent exclusion of their data from the final analysis. Throughout the data collection phase, daily checks were performed by the local principal investigator (OAA) to ensure completeness, accuracy, and consistency of the collected data. Ensuring the accuracy of each patient’s medication record involved meticulous cross-referencing of data from medical records with input from patients, caregivers, attending nurses, and conducting physical checks of medications in patients who brought their medications with them. The local principal investigator (OAA) supervised the data collection process.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics including median, interquartile range (IQR), frequencies and percentages were used to summarize sociodemographic and clinical data. To assess associations between independent variables and ACB as a dichotomized outcome (high vs. low), Chi-squared test, Mann–Whitney U-test; Fisher’s exact test were used as appropriate. The Mann–Whitney U-test was used for continuous variables (e.g., age, Charlson Comorbidity Index, number of medications per patient), while the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was applied for categorical variables (e.g., age category, sex, residency, marital status, education, occupation, smoking status, comorbidity, polypharmacy, and prior admission within 30 days). Binary logistic regression was used to identify determinants of high ACB. Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was employed to evaluate multicollinearity, with a VIF greater than 5 indicating its presence27. The multiple regression model underwent variable selection via backward elimination using the stepAIC function from the MASS package (version 7.3.60). We used the Akaike information criterion (AIC) for selecting the final model, C-statistic and Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness of fit to assess model fitness (Appendix 1), and a significance threshold of p < 0.05 to determine statistical significance. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.2).

Ethical consideration

The Institutional Ethical Review Board of the University of Gondar approved the research (VP/RTT/05/359/2021) on the 1st of December 2021, as did the sponsor of the project, University of Aberdeen School of Medicine, Medical Sciences and Nutrition Ethics Review Board (SERB) (CERB/2021/7/2108) on the 2nd of November 2021. UOGCSH Clinical Directorate granted permission to collect data. Respondents gave both written and verbal consent after being briefed on the study’s objectives. To safeguard confidentiality, respondent identifiers were not recorded on the questionnaire. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations of the Journal.

Results

Population characteristics

A total of 409 patients participated in this study. The median (IQR) age of the respondents was 50.0 (37, 62) years. More than half of participants were female (56.7%) and had one or more chronic conditions (57.2%). Two-third (66.3%) of the participants were married, with a comparable portion being urban dwellers (64.6%). Most (55.8) participants were Class I to IV workers, and almost half of the participants (48.7%) had no formal education. Nearly 5% of the participants reported being former or current smokers (Table 1).

Exposure to anticholinergics

Overall, 32% (n = 131) of the study participants used at least one medicine with anticholinergic activity. A total of 280 patients took no medicine with anticholinergic activity, whereas 101 patients took 1 medicine with anticholinergic activity (69 medicines had a score of 1, 5 a score of 2, and 27 a score of 3). Similarly, 18 patients took 2 medicines with anticholinergic activity (18 medicines had a core of 1, 1 a score of 2, and 1 a score of 3). In addition, 10 patients took 3 medicines with anticholinergic activity (10 medicines had a score of 1, 1 a score of 2, and 1 a score of 3). From the 131 patients exposed to at least one medicine with anti-cholinergic activity, 39 had high ACB. Eight medicines contributed to the ACB according to ACBS, with furosemide (n = 69), atenolol (n = 21), and warfarin (n = 21) emerging as the most frequent, followed by metoprolol (n = 16), carbamazepine (n = 6), amitriptyline (n = 3), digoxin (n = 3), and nifedipine (n = 1). Except for amitriptyline and carbamazepine, which had ACB scores of 3 and 2, respectively, the remaining six medications all scored 1 (Fig. 1).

Factors associated with high ACB

The results of our multiple logistic regression analysis indicate that socio-demographic variables such as age and sex showed no significant association with high ACB. Conversely, the increase in the number of medications per patient was associated with a 1.57-fold (AOR 1.57; 95% CI 1.22–2.02) increase in the odds of high ACB (Table 3). Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness of fit test revealed no evidence of lack of fit (X-squared = 5.2435, df = 8, p-value = 0.7313).

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the prevalence of ACB and its determinants among outpatients at a teaching hospital in northwest Ethiopia. The study provides important information on ACB from a low-income and outpatient setting. In this study, a third of participants were on at least one medicine with anticholinergic activity, while the prevalence of high ACB was 9.5%.

It is crucial to highlight that, in contrast to most previous studies conducted elsewhere7,12,14, the current study encompassed adult patients spanning all age ranges, a notably younger population with a median age of 50 years. Our findings align with a prior investigation conducted in an outpatient setting in Slovenia, which investigated ACB across all age groups. In this study, with a mean age of 47 years, 29.8% and 7.6% of the participants were exposed to medications with anticholinergic activity and high ACB, respectively19. Another study among outpatients conducted in Germany revealed that 37.8% of participants were exposed to medications possessing anticholinergic activity, with 9.6% exhibiting high ACB6. It is important to note that age was not associated with high ACB in this study, which could be attributed to the relatively younger demographics. In contrast, the study from Germany revealed a correlation between age and a heightened occurrence of high ACB among adult outpatients6. In contrast, a study from Thailand reported that greater than half of the participants (53.39%) compared to 32% in the current study were prescribed at least one medicine with anticholinergic property28. The Thai study included only older adults (≥ 60 years), among whom polypharmacy was common (53.86% vs. 9.3% in the current study), thereby increasing the likelihood of being prescribed medicines with anticholinergic properties.

In this study, the majority of participants who were administered medications with anticholinergic activity were mainly prescribed drugs such as furosemide, atenolol, warfarin, and metoprolol, all of which with an ACB score of 1. While only three participants were prescribed amitriptyline, a medication that has high ACB, 9.5% of participants were exposed to high ACB because of cumulative effects. Not surprisingly, the number of medications per patient was found to be associated with high ACB. Previous studies also reported significant positive association between the number of medications and ACB, including high ACB12,13,16. This may be explained by the increased likelihood of being prescribed one or more drugs with anticholinergic properties as the total number of medications rises. Therefore, it is crucial to emphasize to clinicians the cumulative nature of ACB, as it can impose a significant burden on patients who are on multiple medications.

Future theory-informed implementation studies investigating interventions aimed at reducing ACB are imperative, especially given the findings of a prior systematic review which concluded that interventions among older adults failed to demonstrate any significant difference in reducing the overall ACB score29. Striking a delicate balance between reducing ACB and avoiding adverse consequences requires good communication and relationship among patients, carers and healthcare providers and clear role boundaries among healthcare providers30. In Ethiopia, though clinical pharmacy services are not well established, implementation research based on adequate stakeholder engagement, including patients, pharmacists, and prescribers has the potential to reduce ACB and its associated impact on patient outcomes through effective deprescribing strategies.

Limitations

Given the lack of a universally accepted tool for measuring ACB, we relied on a commonly used an expert-based scale21. Additionally, because of the observational and cross-sectional nature of our study, we were unable to account for all potential confounders. For example, we did not account for treatment duration in assessing ACB. Since it is a single center study, results may not be generalizable to the whole of Ethiopia or other resource-limited settings. Moreover, newly prescribed but not-yet-initiated medications at the time of data collection were excluded during ACB calculation which may underestimate the overall burden. However, the study was set in a large university teaching hospital clinic where patients with a wide range of presentations see one of many different clinicians, limiting the influence of unusually high or low prescribing due to preferences of individual clinicians or patient type (e.g. patients with bladder instability seeing a urologist would be more likely to be prescribed anticholinergics). We were unable to ascertain determinants of ACB status beyond the number of medications described, possibly due to the study being underpowered for this purpose. Despite these limitations, this study makes a valuable addition to the current body of literature concerning ACB among adult outpatients across all age groups, providing comprehensive insights within a low-income setting. The study also recruited an adequate sample to address the main aim of the study.

Conclusion and recommendation

Despite a relatively younger demographics, medicines with anticholinergic activity are commonly prescribed among outpatients at UoGSCH. Close to one-third of participants were on at least one medicine with anticholinergic activity, while the prevalence of high ACB was 9.5%. High ACB was shown to be cumulative, and clinicians must remain vigilant regarding the potential for high ACB in patients prescribed multiple medications with anticholinergic activity, despite each individual medication carrying a low risk of ACB. Future research should aim to validate and apply more comprehensive, contemporary tools, such as the CRIDECO Anticholinergic Load Scale (CALS)31, for estimating ACB, particularly in resource-limited settings.
