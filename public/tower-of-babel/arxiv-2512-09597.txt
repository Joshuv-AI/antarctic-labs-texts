Self-Trapping of Microorganisms Steering Toward their Own Trail
Aymeric Lutier, Frédéric van Wijland, Jean-Baptiste Fournier

Abstract
Active matter systems comprise self-propelled particles that move on a substrate while leaving chemical trails that influence other particles through chemotaxis (e.g., slime-depositing bacteria). Orientational chemotaxis manifests as a torque that steers the particle toward the chemical gradient. As each particle is coupled to its own trail, the dynamics exhibits an instability: when the particle gently diffuses, it abruptly transitions to trajectories with a radius of curvature comparable to its own size, becoming apparently trapped. We argue that, contrary to intuition, this trajectory instability occurs for any chemotactic coupling strength. Depending on the coupling regime, this arises either through a potential-barrier first-passage problem or from a rare event analysis.



Active matter encompasses systems of particles that extract energy from their surroundings and convert it into motion or internal state changes. These systems cover a broad diversity of types—from cytoskeletal proteins and cellular structures
23
;
13
, to bacteria, synthetic colloids, and animal collectives such as flocks, schools, and herds
20
;
3
. A prime example of active particles is that of bodies immersed in a solvent, which are driven into motion by gradients in solute concentration. This mechanism occurs in chemical systems as diffusiophoresis (or diffusio-osmosis)
7
;
2
and in biological systems as chemotaxis
21
.

In this work we shall focus on orientational chemotaxis, but since the underlying modelling is common to many problems, we begin by casting our work within this broad context. Diffusiophoresis refers to the fluid flow past a surface that is induced by tangential pressure gradients arising from spatial variations in molecular interactions between the solute and the surface under a concentration gradient
11
. It was shown in Ref.
2
that in itself it can drive colloidal transport. Both the translational and the angular velocity of the particle are generically coupled to the chemical gradient
2
. Catalytically active colloids are capable of generating their own solute gradients, thereby undergoing self-diffusiophoresis
10
;
9
. Collective interactions mediated by the solute concentration field can lead to the formation of dynamic clusters, collapses, and wave patterns
28
;
22
;
17
.

Chemotaxis refers to the ability of a microorganism (
e.g
., a bacterium) to sense chemical gradients through its internal machinery, resulting in motion either along or against the gradient. Bacteria can detect chemical gradients using a time-delay mechanism
6
or employ molecular appendages, such as pili, to spatially detect the gradient
16
and pull themselves along surfaces
19
. Keller and Segel showed that chemotactic interactions between bacteria generically drive an instability yielding the aggregation and collapse of bacterial colonies
14
;
15
;
5
.
Bacteria can also react to their own secreted chemicals,
i.e.
, self-chemotaxis, due to the coupling between the translational velocity and the chemical gradient
12
;
26
;
27
. This leads to slowed-down effective diffusion
12
;
25
or self-trapping (with slow diffusion) in the case of strong couplings
29
. Depending on the microorganism, the secreted chemical may diffuse faster than the microorganism (
e.g.
, in the case of
Dictyostelium
, neutrophils), at a comparable rate (e.g.,
E. coli

4
), or with negligibly small diffusion coefficient (
e.g.
,
P. aeruginosa

31
). In the latter case, the bacterium deposits a fixed trail of slime, which influences the movement of other cells or its own trajectory when it crosses its path again.

Recently, Kranz
et al.
reported a case of orientational self-chemotaxis in
P. aeruginosa
, arising from the coupling between the bacterium’s angular velocity and the surrounding chemical gradient
16
;
8
. When the trajectory of the bacterium bends, the chemical concentration increases toward the center of curvature, generating a torque that steers the particle toward regions of higher concentration, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. At sufficiently strong coupling, it is argued in Ref.
16
that this leads to an instability in which the particle rotates with a microscopic curvature radius and becomes effectively trapped. These authors analyzed the effective diffusion below the instability threshold using a theory linear in the angular velocity and validated the self-chemotaxis mechanism experimentally
8
.
In this work, we build upon
16
by taking into account all nonlinearities in the angular velocity, and we show that the instability disappears; self-trapping occurs at arbitrary coupling.

Before addressing the full nonlinear problem, let us estimate the value
\mu^{\prime}=\mu^{\prime}_{0}
at which the angular diffusion is significantly affected by the torque, and the typical value
\mu^{\prime}=\mu^{\prime}_{c}
around which the self-trapping discussed above might occur. We consider the angular variation produced by the torque term
\mu^{\prime}\bm{u}_{\perp}\cdot\bm{\nabla}c
over the time
\tau=R/v_{0}
that the particle takes to move a distance comparable to the width of its trail (or equivalently, to its own size). It is given by

\delta\theta=\mu^{\prime}\bm{u}_{\perp}\cdot\bm{\nabla}c\times\tau\simeq\mu^{\prime}R^{-1}\times\frac{h\tau}{\pi R^{2}}\times\tau=\frac{\mu^{\prime}h\tau^{2}}{\pi R^{3}}

(4)

(see Fig.

When this deterministic
\delta\theta
is of the same order as the stochastic one due to noise, namely
\sqrt{2D_{r}\tau}
, we obtain
\mu^{\prime}_{0}
. Similarly, when
\delta\theta
is of order unity (in practice we take
2
), we obtain
\mu^{\prime}_{c}
:

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}_{0}=\frac{\sqrt{2D_{r}}\pi R^{3}}{h\tau^{3/2}},\qquad\mu^{\prime}_{c}=\frac{2\pi R^{3}}{h\tau^{2}}.

(5)

Note that this value corresponds to the threshold
\mu^{\prime}_{c}

16

To simplify the theoretical analysis, we normalize lengths by
R
, times by the bare persistence time
\tau_{p}=1/(2D_{r})
and we scale the concentration

with
h/(2D_{r}R^{2})
. The velocity is thus scaled with

and the coupling
\mu^{\prime}
by
4D_{r}^{2}R^{3}/h
. This rescaling amounts to setting

,

,
h=1
,
2D_{r}=1
and
\tau=\epsilon
(together with
D=k=0)
which we henceforth adopt. The equations of motion become

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bm{r}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-1}\bm{u},

(6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\theta

\displaystyle=\mu^{\prime}\,\bm{u}_{\perp}\cdot\bm{\nabla}c+\eta,

(7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\Theta\!\left(1-\left(\bm{x}-\bm{r}\right)^{2}\right),

(8)

with
\langle\eta(t)\eta(t^{\prime})\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})
. With these normalizations, we obtain
\mu^{\prime}_{c}{\varepsilon}^{2}=2\pi
and
\mu^{\prime}_{0}{\varepsilon}^{2}=\pi\sqrt{\varepsilon}
.

The dynamics of the particle is actually very complex and non-Markovian, since it can cross its past trail and interact with it many times. Similarly to Ref.
16
, our goal is not to analyze this complex diffusion, but rather to investigate the particle’s dynamics in the regime where it does not cross its past trail. The main questions we address and answer in this work are i) Can the particle experience self-trapping by interacting with its immediate trail?, ii) How is the diffusion affected before a potential trapping?

We first show how to arrive at a generalized Langevin equation for the angle. The torque term in the dynamical equation for
\theta(t)
can be computed with the concentration profile

from

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}c(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{t}(\bm{r}(t)-\bm{r}(t^{\prime}))\,\delta\!\left(1-(\bm{r}(t)-\bm{r}(t^{\prime}))^{2}\right)\,dt^{\prime},

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{t^{\prime}_{i}}\frac{\bm{r}(t)-\bm{r}(t^{\prime}_{i})}{\left|\left(\bm{r}(t)-\bm{r}(t^{\prime}_{i})\right)\cdot\frac{d\bm{r}(t^{\prime})}{dt^{\prime}}|_{t^{\prime}_{i}}\right|}

(9)

where the

\bm{r}(t)
. These times can be computed from

\theta

\displaystyle\bm{r}(t)-\bm{r}(t^{\prime})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(t^{\prime}-t)^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\,\theta^{(n)}\bm{u}_{\perp}=\frac{t-t^{\prime}}{\varepsilon}\bm{u}

\displaystyle-\frac{\dot{\theta}^{2}}{6}\frac{(t-t^{\prime})^{3}}{\varepsilon}\bm{u}+\frac{1}{24}\frac{(t-t^{\prime})^{4}}{\varepsilon}\left(\dot{\theta}^{3}\bm{u}_{\perp}+3\dot{\theta}\ddot{\theta}\bm{u}\right)+\ldots,

where the ellipsis refer to

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}\!=\!\frac{\mu^{\prime}\!\varepsilon^{2}}{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\,\frac{(-\varepsilon)^{n-1}}{(n+1)!}\theta^{(n)}\!+\!\frac{\dot{\theta}^{3}}{16}\varepsilon^{2}\!-\frac{7}{60}\dot{\theta}^{2}\ddot{\theta}\varepsilon^{3}\!+\!O(\varepsilon^{4})\right]\!+\eta.

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}^{\prime}=\mu^{\prime}\varepsilon^{2},

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\hat{\mu}^{\prime}}{\hat{\mu}_{c}^{\prime}}\right)\dot{\theta}=\frac{\hat{\mu}^{\prime}\varepsilon}{\pi}\bigg[-\frac{\ddot{\theta}}{6}+\left(\frac{\dot{\theta}^{3}}{16}+\frac{\theta^{(3)}}{24}\right)\varepsilon

\displaystyle\Gamma(\varphi^{\star})\varepsilon=1,

\displaystyle P_{\text{traj}}\sim\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{\varphi^{\star}}\!\frac{d\varphi}{\omega}\,\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\right)\sim T^{-1}.

In summary, we have explored the chemotactic bacterium model proposed in Ref.
16
and the related trapping instability of the bacterium’s trajectory. We found that there is no threshold in the chemical coupling allowing to avoid this instability, owing to nonlinear mechanisms. It is interesting to note that in the weak coupling
\mu^{\prime}\to 0
limit, the angular diffusion coefficient matches that of
16
up to
\mu^{\prime}\simeq 0.2
, thus confirming that their model is physically relevant for their
P. aeruginosa
system in that regime
8
. It would be interesting to find out whether, in the trapping regime, the particle undergoes translational diffusion or whether it remains stuck at a given location in space. Does the rotational diffusion affect the scenario discussed in Refs.
29
;
12
? In our work, we have neglected translational noise (
D_{t}=0
), but it is for us an open question whether restoring a nonzero
D_{t}
could help the particle escape the instability, with or without a threshold in
D_{t}
(a similar question arises for
D
that controls the slime diffusion). In the same vein, we have limited our analysis to self-trapping occurring at the tip of the trajectory, although both diffusion and trapping should be affected by the whole imprinted trail left by the bacterium in the past, which raises another set of questions. Finally, in assembling many of the bacteria, it would be very interesting to find out how individual trails affect collective behavior
18
.
