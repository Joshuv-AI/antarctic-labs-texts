Global and regional burden of colorectal cancer potentially related to Helicobacter pylori exposure: a risk attribution modelling study
Authors: Yunhao Li, Can Cui, Dianqin Sun, Kimberly Ho, Cong Qian, Yanyan Guo, Wai Keung Leung, Shailja C Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmerging epidemiological and mechanistic studies suggest that Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) exposure may be associated with colorectal cancer (CRC); however, the findings remain controversial. We aim to quantify the CRC burden potentially related to H. pylori exposure, with the overarching objective to inform prioritisation and future evaluation of H. pylori control as a candidate adjunct to existing CRC prevention efforts in settings with high prevalence and limited resources.MethodsWe estimated global and regional H. pylori exposure and its epidemiological association with CRC using data from systematic reviews and meta-analyses, stratified by region, diagnostic method and eradication status, among others. We derived CRC estimates from the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN). To evaluate the related CRC burden, we calculated the estimated related proportion (ERP) with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) and absolute and rate-based burdens using Monte Carlo simulations. We further examined correlations between country-level ERPs, H. pylori prevalence and CRC incidence. Lasty, we used a pseudo-cohort approach to explore birth cohort-related patterns based on GLOBOCAN 2022.ResultsExposure to H. pylori was associated with a 1.59-fold increased risk of CRC (95% CI 1.36 to 1.87), consistent across most subgroups. In limited observational studies, H. pylori eradication was associated with a reduced risk of CRC, but only after 10 years of follow-up. Globally, under the pooled risk-attribution model based on estimates from 43 studies, an estimated 22.0% (95% UI 14.7% to 29.4%) of CRC cases were potentially related to H. pylori exposure; corresponding estimates were lower when restricted to population-based and cohort studies (n=14). Higher H. pylori-related burdens were observed in settings with lower CRC incidence and increased with rising H. pylori prevalence. Exploratory pseudo-cohort patterns suggested higher ERPs in later birth cohorts, while absolute case counts remained relatively stable.ConclusionH. pylori exposure may meaningfully contribute to global CRC burden, with the extent of preventable burden dependent on the causal nature and other modifying variables related to H. pylori exposure (eg, timing, duration). This underscores the importance of well-designed randomised clinical trials and other prospective investigations evaluating the impact of H. pylori and its eradication on CRC risk, as well as potential modifying factors.Keywords: Helicobacter pylori, Colorectal neoplasms, Incidence, Risk Factors, Population Surveillance

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, representing a significant global health burden. In 2022, CRC affected approximately 1.9 million individuals and accounted for 903 859 deaths.1 The aetiology of CRC is multifactorial and includes both non-modifiable (eg, genetics, age) and modifiable risk factors, such as those related to lifestyle and diet.2–4 According to the Global Burden of Disease study, in 2021, the population-attributable fractions (PAFs) of behavioural risks (eg, high alcohol use, obesity, low physical activity, smoking) and dietary risks (eg, diet high in red meat, processed meat) for CRC were estimated at 48.2% and 38.7%, respectively.5

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) is an established gastric carcinogen,6 with a reported PAF of 74.77–89.0%8 for gastric cancer. However, the causal relationship between H. pylori and CRC remains highly controversial. Accumulating evidence suggests that H. pylori exposure may be linked to colorectal carcinogenesis and aligns with several Bradford Hill considerations.9–11 Epidemiological consistency is demonstrated by multiple epidemiological studies from diverse global populations, which consistently report a 1.5–2-fold increased risk of CRC among infected individuals.12–15 The modest magnitude of the effect estimate does not exclude causality but increases susceptibility to residual confounding. A dose–response pattern is suggested in some studies, including higher CRC risk among carriers of virulence-factor–positive H. pylori strains (eg, cytotoxin-associated gene A (CagA), vacuolating cytotoxin A (VacA)) or those with premalignant lesions.13,16,17 From a biological plausibility and experimental perspective, Ralser et al demonstrated that H. pylori infection directly promotes CRC development by modulating intestinal and colonic immune responses in mice, resulting in significant alterations in the gut and colonic microbiota and epithelial homeostasis.18 Eradicating H. pylori infection prevented these tumour-promoting effects in mice, independent of antibiotic exposure alone. Third, treatment effect is suggested by a recent nationwide cohort study among 812 736 US Veterans,19 where H. pylori eradication was associated with a reduced incidence of CRC and mortality compared with persistent infection.

To date, evidence for a clear exposure–response remains incomplete, and definitive evidence from randomised clinical trials (RCTs) with CRC as the primary endpoint is still lacking. Although limited in number, observational studies consistently suggest that the potential benefit of H. pylori eradication on CRC risk might require over 10 years of follow-up,19–21 which may be due to the long latency period of colorectal carcinogenesis. This latency hypothesis aligns with RCTs of H. pylori eradication and gastric cancer risk, where the protective benefits of H. pylori eradication become apparent after prolonged follow-up.22,23

Our study aims to evaluate the putative burden of CRC that may be related to H. pylori, a modifiable and preventable risk factor. We applied a modified PAF risk modelling framework to estimate the global and regional burden of CRC potentially related to H. pylori exposure, fully acknowledging that causality is not definitively established. The overarching goal is to provide evidence to inform public health and research prioritisation, and to guide infection-targeted cancer prevention strategies at both global and regional levels that complement and extend beyond H. pylori eradication for gastric cancer prevention.

Methods

H. pylori prevalence and CRC burden data

We collected and generated the baseline data for the model from the following sources. We obtained global, regional and national H. pylori prevalence data from our previously published systematic reviews and meta-analyses.24 We further stratified H. pylori prevalence estimates by diagnostic methods, sex and WHO regions (Africa, the Americas, South-East Asia, Europe, Eastern Mediterranean and Western Pacific). Seropositivity may persist after spontaneous clearance or eradication and therefore can overestimate current or active infection in settings with rapidly declining prevalence or widespread eradication programmes. Accordingly, our prevalence inputs are interpreted as markers of H. pylori exposure, and we present diagnostic-modality-stratified estimates (serology vs non-serology) to provide complementary bounds for exposure-based versus active-infection-based interpretations. We obtained the latest data on global, regional and country-specific CRC estimates, including age-standardised incidence rates (ASIRs) and newly diagnosed CRC cases from Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) 2022.1

H. pylori exposure and CRC association

To assess the risk of CRC related to H. pylori exposure, we conducted an updated, comprehensive meta-analysis building on prior systematic reviews,25–29 with extension of the search to 31 July 2025. The detailed literature search strategy, inclusion and exclusion criteria, data extraction procedures and risk-of-bias assessment are presented in online supplemental table 1.

We conducted subgroup analyses to explore potential effect modifiers of the association between H. pylori exposure and CRC risk, given the heterogeneity across populations and study characteristics, and recognising that such factors have been rarely examined in previous studies of H. pylori and CRC risk. Subgroups were defined by: (1) study population setting (population-based or hospital-based); (2) age group stratification, with <60 vs ≥60 years as the predetermined cut-off since this was most commonly used in the included studies; (3) sex; (4) birth cohort (divided into participants born in 1930s and before, 1940s and 1950s, 1960s and 1970s, categorised based on available literature); (5) WHO regions; (6) diagnostic modality for H. pylori detection (non-serological modalities including urea breath test, stool antigen test, histology, rapid urease test and culture; serology; and mixed modalities when more than one diagnostic modality was used without stratified effect estimates by test type). We grouped ‘mixed’ with non-serology methods because these approaches typically include tests indicative of current or active infection; (7) risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS).30 The original NOS was applied to cohort and case-control studies, while a modified version of the NOS was used for cross-sectional studies31; (8) study design (case-control study, cross-sectional study and cohort study); (9) tumour anatomical location (colon vs rectum); (10) presence of a nationwide CRC screening programme (yes vs no); (11) H. pylori eradication status (eradicated vs non-eradicated and subsequent follow-up time post eradication), where available; (12) presence of H. pylori virulence factors (primarily CagA, VacA and GroEL), where available. Effect measures were harmonised prior to pooling. Because the majority of included studies reported odds ratios (ORs), we used OR as the primary metric; when studies reported relative risks (RRs) or hazard ratios (HRs), we treated these as approximations of OR and pooled them on the log scale with ORs when necessary. We further assessed robustness by converting the pooled OR to RR using baseline risks (ASIR-based) for burden estimation.

Estimation of H. pylori exposure-related CRC burden

We used three measures to estimate the H. pylori exposure-associated CRC burden: (1) estimated related proportion (ERP); (2) corresponding CRC incidence rate; (3) estimated absolute case numbers. To calculate ERP, we used the standard Levin formula,32 modified from the PAF framework, as recommended in the GATHER (Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting) statement.33,34 We favoured using the term ERP rather than PAF, as ERP enables cautious, descriptive burden estimation rather than intervention-effect prediction, while simultaneously retaining the PAF-based computational framework.

We estimated ERPs using the Monte Carlo simulation with 10 000 iterations to balance computational efficiency and numerical stability. For each iteration, ERPs were calculated based on randomly sampled prevalence and OR values, and the 2.5th, 50th and 97.5th percentiles were used to determine point estimates and 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs).8,36,37 When available, country-specific ORs were used, provided that at least two eligible studies (including one population-based study with a sample size ≥500) were identified. In countries lacking sufficient data, regional ORs were used. If regional estimates were unavailable, global pooled ORs were applied.

We estimated the burden at the global and regional levels, and stratified by sex and diagnostic modality. In addition to estimates based on all eligible studies, we performed sensitivity analyses restricted to high-quality population-based studies and cohort studies, which typically provide larger sample sizes, longer follow-up and more rigorous exposure and outcome ascertainment than case-control and cross-sectional studies. We conducted an exploratory pseudo-cohort analysis using repeated cross-sectional age-specific CRC incidence rates from GLOBOCAN 2022. Age groups were cross-sectionally aligned to approximate birth cohorts, and external estimates of H. pylori prevalence24 and pooled RRs were applied to generate hypothesis-generating patterns of H. pylori–related CRC burden. Detailed methods and cohort definitions are provided in the online supplemental table 2.

Statistical analysis

We used random-effects models, employing the DerSimonian and Laird method with inverse-variance weighting, to perform a meta-analysis of H. pylori exposure and CRC risk. The Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman adjustment was applied to improve variance estimation accuracy.38 Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, with I² ≥50% indicating significant heterogeneity. To assess the robustness of the pooled estimates to unmeasured confounding, E-values were calculated for the pooled effect and its lower 95% confidence bound.39

To explore sources of heterogeneity, random-effects meta-regression was performed using mixed-effects models implemented in the “metafor” R package. Candidate moderators included the midpoint year of the study period, diagnostic method, study design, screening programme status and study population setting. Model selection was guided by the Akaike information criterion (AIC).40 Additionally, we conducted an ecological correlation between country-level ASIRs and ERPs using Spearman’s rank correlation, and modelled the exposure–response relationship between H. pylori prevalence and ERP using a generalised additive model (GAM) with penalised splines (k=5, restricted maximum likelihood).

We applied funnel plots and Egger’s regression test to assess potential publication bias. To evaluate the robustness of the pooled effect estimates and identify influential studies, we performed a leave-one-out analysis, in which each study was sequentially omitted and the pooled OR and its 95% CI were recalculated. Except for the estimate based on population-based and cohort studies only, we conducted additional sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of global estimation by (1) increasing the Monte Carlo simulations to 20 000 to assess the robustness of results against potential simulation variability; (2) limiting the risk estimates to studies that reported multivariable-adjusted risk to minimise residual confounding; (3) we repeated the global, regional and sex-specified estimation after converting pooled ORs to RRs using stratum-specific baseline risks according to the formula41 RR=OR/[(1-P0)+P0xOR], where P0 denotes the baseline risk of CRC, approximated by the ASIR per 100 000 population as reported in GLOBOCAN 2022; (4) adopting the global summary estimate with the pooled OR derived exclusively from countries without established national CRC screening programmes.

Results

Association between H. pylori exposure and CRC risk

Based on 43 included studies involving 48 694 403 individuals globally, H. pylori exposure was associated with a 1.59-fold higher risk of CRC (95% CI 1.36 to 1.87), with substantial heterogeneity (I²: 92.6%). The E-value for the pooled estimated risk was 2.56 (95% lower bound: 2.06). Details of the included studies are shown in online supplemental table 3. Significant differences by study setting (P for subgroup=0.009) and study risk of bias (P for subgroup=0.013) were noted. There was some regional variation in risk estimates, but most CIs overlapped or crossed 1 (p=0.428). No statistically significant subgroup differences were observed across sex, age group, diagnostic method or presence of a national CRC screening programme (all P for subgroup >0.05; table 1). Detailed subgroup analysis results are shown in table 1 and online supplemental figures 1–12.

Only four studies,19–21,42 including one study that set CRC as a secondary endpoint,42 reported data on the impact of H. pylori eradication on CRC risk. Two cohort studies19,20 conducted subgroup analyses over a follow-up period of more than 10 years and demonstrated that H. pylori eradication was associated with reduced risk of CRC (RR: 0.90 (95% CI 0.84 to 0.96)). This benefit was not demonstrated in shorter-term follow-up (≤5 years: RR=1.01 (0.78–1.30); 5–10 years: RR=0.92 (0.81–1.05)). The best-fitting model (lowest AIC=64.2) retained four covariates from the prespecified set of potential study-level factors listed in the methods section above: study midpoint year, study population setting, study risk of bias and H. pylori diagnostic methods. These covariates were identified as the most informative predictors. The selected model reduced between-study variance (τ²) to 0.2 and accounted for 6.8% of the observed heterogeneity. Results of the random-effects meta-regression are presented in online supplemental table 4.

Global and regional CRC burden related to H. pylori exposure

Overall, under the pooled risk-attribution model based on all 43 included studies, H. pylori exposure was potentially related to 22.0% (95% UI 14.7% to 29.4%) of global CRC burden (table 2). The corresponding ERP restricted solely to high-quality population-based and cohort studies (n=14 studies; median follow-up 14 years, IQR: 8.7 years) was lower (11.9%, 95% UI 0.7% to 22.8%); the wider UI is an expected statistical consequence of the modelling approach, as the Monte Carlo framework jointly propagates uncertainty from both prevalence and RR estimates. ERP varied according to the WHO region. The Western Pacific Region had the highest related burden (ERP: 25.1%, 95% UI 16.4% to 34.1%). Females had a higher ERP compared with males (24.8%, 95% UI 10.8% to 38.7% vs 19.9%, 95% UI 8.1% to 31.4%). When stratified by diagnostic method, individuals with confirmed active H. pylori infection based on non-serology or mixed methods had higher ERP versus individuals diagnosed based on serology only, but with overlapping UI (ERP 25.4%, 95% UI 14.9% to 35.8% vs ERP of 19.9%, 95% UI 8.9% to 30.5%). The latest (based on GLOBOCAN 2022 data) estimations of the global CRC burden related to H. pylori exposure across subgroups are shown in table 3.

Birth cohort trends in CRC burden related to H. pylori exposure

Based on the limited pseudo-cohort data derived from GLOBOCAN 2022, H. pylori prevalence showed a declining gradient across successive pseudo-cohorts, from the earliest (<1947) to the most recent (1963–1977) group (figure 4A). Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

Studies assessing the association between H. pylori exposure and CRC risk showed no apparent asymmetry on the funnel plot, indicating low concern for publication bias (online supplemental figure 13). Egger’s regression test yielded a non-significant result (p=0.230), with a bias coefficient of 0.3. The leave-one-out analysis indicated that no single study had a disproportionate impact on the overall pooled estimate. Sequential omission of individual studies yielded pooled ORs ranging from 1.52 to 1.73, with all estimates remaining statistically significant (p<0.001) and in the same direction as the primary analysis. Altering the number of iterations to 20 000 in the Monte Carlo simulation yielded an ERP of 22.7% (95% UI 11.6% to 33.6%), with comparable global estimates for H. pylori–related CRC ASIR (4.2 per 100 000 (95% UI 2.1 to 6.2)). Applying a multivariable-adjusted pooled OR of 1.53 (95% CI 1.30 to 1.87) produced a slightly lower ERP of 18.3% (95% UI 11.4% to 25.2%), corresponding to a global H. pylori-related ASIR of 3.4 per 100 000 (95% UI 2.1 to 4.6). Similarly, re-estimating ERPs after converting pooled ORs to RRs using stratum-specific baseline risks showed minimal changes (<1 percentage point difference) across global, sex and regional strata. Using the OR derived from countries without national CRC screening programmes (OR: 1.82; 95% CI 1.40 to 2.41) resulted in an ERP of 26.5% (95% UI 15.6% to 37.8%) and an ASIR of 4.9 per 100 000 (95% UI 2.9 to 6.9). These estimates, however, are primarily based on hospital-based studies with limited sample sizes and should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

In this risk-attribution modelling study, we estimated that H. pylori exposure may have contributed to approximately 22.0% of global CRC cases, which is lower than attribution from other common modifiable factors. This proportion decreased to 11.9% when risk estimates were derived from population-based and cohort studies. We noted regional variations, including a higher burden in the Western Pacific. Across countries, potential H. pylori-related CRC burden was higher in settings with lower overall CRC incidence and increased non-linearly with rising H. pylori prevalence. We also observed country-level heterogeneity and an inverse correlation between overall CRC ASIR and ERP. Across pseudo-cohorts, the corresponding CRC burden related to H. pylori exposure was higher in later-born groups. Taken together, our findings provide an integrated view of the magnitude, regional heterogeneity and birth cohort dynamics of CRC burden potentially related to H. pylori exposure.

In a nationwide cohort study in the US through 15-year follow-up, H. pylori eradication was associated with an absolute risk reduction of 0.23–0.35%, which is on par with the 0.15–0.30% absolute risk reduction in fatal CRC afforded by colonoscopy (colonoscopy yields 0.84–1.22% absolute reduction in incident CRC).19 A recent pragmatic RCT in Changhua County, Taiwan (Province of China), demonstrated that co-screening for gastric cancer and CRC with H. pylori stool antigen and faecal immunochemical testing (FIT) versus FIT alone also suggested potential added reduction in CRC incidence. However, follow-up was short, and treatment uptake varied, limiting causal inference.43 Several factors can bias the observable benefit after eradication. A recent cluster-RCT42 conducted in Linqu County, China, implemented community-based H. pylori screen-and-treat primarily to prevent gastric cancer, enrolling adults aged 25–54 years and using gastric neoplasia endpoints as the primary outcomes. CRC was not the primary endpoint, and the estimated effect on CRC incidence was not significant (HR: 1.02, 95% CI 0.78 to 1.34). This finding should be interpreted cautiously, as trials designed and powered for gastric cancer prevention may be underpowered to detect CRC effects, which may require longer follow-up given the prolonged adenoma–carcinoma sequence, as well as require concurrent colonoscopy to detect asymptomatic early-stage CRC; this particular trial also enrolled a relatively young population. Differences in the timing of intervention and prevention windows between gastric cancer and CRC should therefore be considered.44,45 Moreover, it is plausible that in many real-world settings, patients undergoing H. pylori testing or eradication might have upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and colonoscopy performed at the same encounter, enabling same-day detection and removal of colorectal polyps.46 Such procedural overlap may contribute to a positive association between H. pylori exposure and CRC.

The Western Pacific region demonstrated the highest estimated H. pylori-related CRC burden. This finding may reflect multiple factors, including high background prevalence of H. pylori infection (such as China, Mongolia and Vietnam),24,47 limited population-wide eradication programmes,48 as well as rising CRC incidence due to rapid urbanisation,49 dietary shifts50 and ageing demographics. By contrast, despite similarly high H. pylori prevalence levels in the African, South-East Asian and Eastern Mediterranean regions, the related CRC burden was comparatively lower. Indeed, there is a well-documented epidemiological observation known as the ‘African enigma’51 and the ‘Indian enigma’,52 which describes the paradoxical phenomenon where populations exhibit high H. pylori prevalence but low rates of H. pylori-related gastric cancer. Understanding the complex interactions among host genetics, H. pylori genetics and environmental factors that may plausibly contribute to the observed trend is beyond the scope of this work, but should motivate future research. Notably, we did not observe differences in the H. pylori–CRC association by national CRC screening programme status, suggesting that screening intensity alone might not account for the regional profile. The relative contribution of H. pylori in these regions may be diluted by competing behavioural risks (eg, diet, sedentary lifestyle, obesity, alcohol), which are more prominent risk factors for CRC in Western countries.2,53

The rise in CRC incidence is partly driven by ‘contemporary’ risk factors, such as dietary changes (eg, high-fat, low-fibre diets), increasing rates of unhealthy living habits and enhanced screening efforts that improve detection, which may dilute the relative impact of H. pylori. Indeed, across successive pseudo-birth cohorts, H. pylori prevalence declined markedly, yet the ERP for CRC increased, rising from 9.5% to 15.0%. This discordance might reflect a latency effect, whereby the carcinogenic consequences of early-life infection manifest decades later, combined with reduced competing mortality and improved longevity that allow individuals to reach ages at which CRC typically develops.54 This latency effect is also consistently observed for gastric cancer.

In our subgroup analyses, ERP point estimates were slightly higher when H. pylori exposure was defined using non-serology or mixed modalities compared with serology-only definitions, but UIs overlapped. Non-serology tests capture current or active infection, which may indicate a longer duration of infection, whereas serology also includes individuals with prior but not current infection (eg, eradicated). Non-concordance between seroprevalence and active infection might be amplified in settings undergoing rapid epidemiological transition (eg, countries with rapid socio-economic development), in countries with organised endoscopic screening for gastric cancer (eg, Japan, Korea) and/or with population-level ‘screen-and-treat’ strategies, as recommended by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC)/WHO, with concurrent high H. pylori eradication treatment uptake. Accordingly, serology-based prevalence may overestimate the prevalence of current infection. Diagnostic modality may also be a proxy for healthcare setting and study context, and contribute to residual confounding or selection bias that influence observed effect sizes. It is also relevant to acknowledge that studies of the PAF of gastric cancer related to H. pylori include both serological and non-serological diagnostic modalities, given that prior exposure is still associated with increased cancer risk. All points considered, we did not observe statistically significant differences in our estimates based on detailed analysis according to diagnostic modality (non-serology or mixed vs serology; invasive vs non-invasive).55,56

This study has several strengths. First, it is comprehensive synthesis to date quantifying the global and regional burden of CRC potentially related to H. pylori exposure, incorporating data from studies globally and the latest GLOBOCAN data. The inclusion of diverse study designs, populations and detection methods enhances the generalisability of our findings. Second, we applied robust statistical methods, such as Monte Carlo simulation, to jointly account for uncertainty in both exposure prevalence and RR estimates, thereby generating more reliable estimates of attributable fractions; it is acknowledged, though, that this also has the consequence of inflating uncertainty when study numbers are limited, as in some of our sensitivity analyses. A recent policy-focused analysis by Mulder et al suggested that approximately 18% of CRC burden might be related to H. pylori exposure when considering outcomes beyond gastric cancer alone.57 While broadly consistent in magnitude, that estimate was derived from a single previously published pooled association and did not incorporate stratification by region, sex or diagnostic modality, and did not consider study quality as we did here to further assess the robustness of the estimates and reasons for heterogeneity. Third, we conducted multiple subgroup and sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our estimates.

Nevertheless, the limitations need to be acknowledged. First, the ERP estimates are structurally sensitive to the assumed prevalence and pooled RR, which may lead to overestimation or underestimation of the H. pylori-related burden; however, this limitation is not unique to H. pylori and applies to other CRC risk factors as well. Data gaps exist for specific areas in the relationship between H. pylori and CRC risk. For instance, in the African and Southeast Asia regions, we used average values derived from broader WHO regions or global estimates, which may introduce bias and reduce the specificity of country-level estimates.

Second, our comprehensive subgroup analyses and meta-regressions were able to explain only a modest proportion of the observed heterogeneity (6.8%).58–60 The potential sources of residual heterogeneity might originate from exposure assessment variability, CRC ascertainment, study population risk and follow-up duration across the individual studies, which we cannot fully address in this meta-analysis. In addition, overall heterogeneity and pooled risk estimates were largely influenced by the Region of the Americas, which contributed the majority of participants despite a limited number of studies, and by the predominant use of serological assays for H. pylori diagnosis. Moreover, the evidence base underlying the pooled risk estimates is dominated by case-control and cross-sectional studies (n=38), with comparatively fewer prospective cohorts (n=5). Hospital-based designs may be more susceptible to selection bias and differential healthcare-seeking and testing patterns than population-based samples, which could partly contribute to the stronger associations observed in some settings and to residual confounding. Accordingly, we also conducted sensitivity analyses restricted to high-quality population-based and cohort studies (n=14) and observed a lower ERP, which provides a conservative lower bound. It should be emphasised, though, that interpretation of the UI is limited for this sensitivity analysis, since the wide interval is a primary statistical consequence of the modelling approach propagating uncertainty based on the lesser number of studies versus the primary analysis. Our birth-cohort-related findings, derived from cross-sectional age-specific data in GLOBOCAN 2022, should therefore be interpreted as pseudo-cohort patterns rather than evidence from longitudinal follow-up. This pragmatic approach was adopted because harmonised, age-specific CRC incidence series using comparable methods with coverage across multiple historical years are not consistently available at the global scale. Accordingly, the 2022 estimates provide a standardised snapshot of age patterns rather than the full life-course burden across birth cohorts. These results may be affected by survivor bias, period-specific screening intensity and other unmeasured confounders and should therefore be viewed distinctly as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Third, evidence directly evaluating H. pylori eradication and subsequent CRC risk remains sparse and largely observational. Existing eradication studies are often limited by short follow-up durations and potential confounding factors, such as variations in endoscopy indications, antibiotic exposure, concomitant colonoscopy with upper endoscopy and disparities in healthcare access, all of which may influence the apparent association between H. pylori exposure and CRC. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, and further prospective and interventional studies are needed.

Regarding the public health implications, population-level H. pylori control may help prevent gastric cancer and, if the link with CRC is causal, could reduce CRC burden in high-prevalence areas. However, success depends on local epidemiology, healthcare capacity and integration with existing CRC screening. As with primary gastric cancer prevention, large-scale H. pylori eradication raises concerns about antibiotic stewardship and resistance, treatment side effects and population versus individual CRC risk modulation, which also considers the latency effect of H. pylori eradication on cancer. Therefore, H. pylori control should be implemented based on local context, prioritising high-prevalence, resource-limited settings. Prospective studies and trials with predefined CRC outcomes are needed to confirm benefits and guide integration into current cancer prevention programmes.

Conclusion

H. pylori exposure may be related to an estimated 22.0% of global CRC cases under a causal assumption, with regional, sex and birth cohort variability. These findings may inform primary CRC prevention strategies, particularly in regions with high H. pylori prevalence and limited resources. However, high-quality, prospective studies with long-term follow-up are warranted to clarify whether H. pylori eradication causally reduces CRC risk.
