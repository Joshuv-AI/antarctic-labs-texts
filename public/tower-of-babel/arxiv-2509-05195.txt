Constraint effective action and critical correlation functions at fixed magnetization
Félix Rose, Adam Rançon, Ivan Balog

Abstract
We present an extension of the functional renormalization group (FRG) framework developed to compute critical probability distributions of the order parameter to momentum-dependent observables. Focusing on the constraint effective action at fixed magnetization for the Ising universality class, we derive its exact flow equations and solve them at the second-order of the derivative expansion (DE2). We solve these flow equations numerically for two- and three-dimensional systems, extract universal rate functions and momentum-dependent correlation functions, and benchmark them against Monte Carlo simulations. In three dimensions, we recover the rate function and accurately reproduce the first few Fourier modes of the constrained correlation function, and demonstrate the convergence of the method. In two dimensions, the lowest order approximations such as local potential approximation (LPA) fail, and it is required to consider at least the DE2 to describe the critical point. Our results are in qualitative agreement with the numerics. We confirm the robustness of the FRG approach for calculating both zero- and finite-momentum critical observables at fixed magnetization.



I
Introduction

Continuous phase transitions exhibit universal behavior, characterized by scaling laws of correlation functions and critical exponents, independent of microscopic details. A striking manifestation of this universality arises in the probability distribution function (PDF) of macroscopic observables, such as the order parameter. Near criticality, when the correlation length diverges, the central limit theorem, valid when variables are weakly correlated, fails due to strong, system-wide correlations. Instead, the PDF of the order parameter adopts a universal, non-Gaussian form described by a scaling function called the rate function
Bouchaud and Georges 1990
. The study of such PDFs has been the subject of intense theoretical and numerical
Bruce et al. 1979
;
Binder 1981a
;
Binder 1981b
;
Rudnick et al. 1985
;
Eisenriegler and Tomaschitz 1987
;
Kuti and Shen 1988
;
Nicolaides and Bruce 1988
;
Brankov and Danchev 1989
;
Bruce and Wilding 1992
;
Tsypin 1994
;
Hilfer and Wilding 1995
;
Esser et al. 1995
;
Chen et al. 1996
;
Bruce 1997
;
Rudnick et al. 1998
;
Tsypin and Blöte 2000
;
Portelli and Holdsworth 2002
;
Hilfer et al. 2003
;
Hilfer et al. 2005
;
Malakis et al. 2014
;
Xu et al. 2020
;
Balog et al. 2022
;
Rançon et al. 2025
;
Sahu et al. 2025
;
Balog et al. 2025
;
Sahu 2025a
;
Sahu 2025b
, as well as experimental
Bramwell et al. 1998
;
Joubaud et al. 2008
;
Takeuchi and Sano 2010
;
Allemand et al. 2025
works.

For the paradigmatic case of the Ising model at its critical temperature
T_{c}
, the PDF of the magnetization density
\hat{s}=L^{-d}\sum_{i}\hat{s}_{i}
(with
\hat{s}_{i}
local spins,
L
system size, and
d
spatial dimension) scales exponentially with system volume,
P_{L}(\hat{s}=s)\sim e^{-L^{d}I(s)}
. The scaling hypothesis predicts that the rate function
I(s)
is universal, governed by the anomalous dimension
\eta
, and a function of the rescaled variable
\tilde{s}=L^{(d-2+\eta)/2}s
. Moreover, away from criticality, universality persists more generally, defining an entire family of universal scaling functions parameterized by the ratio
L/\xi_{\infty}
, where
\xi_{\infty}
is the bulk correlation length.

The connection between the rate function, scaling, and universality naturally points towards the renormalization group (RG), the fundamental theoretical framework describing critical phenomena. Historically, this link has been acknowledged since the early stages of RG theory
Jona-Lasinio 1975
;
Gallavotti and Knops 1974
;
Gallavotti and Martin-Löf 1975
, yet it was only recently explicitly elucidated within the context of the functional renormalization group (FRG)
Balog et al. 2022
. The FRG formalism, built upon Wilsonian RG applied at the level of the effective action (the Legendre transform of the free energy), provides a systematic and nonperturbative method to address critical phenomena
Dupuis et al. 2021
.

In this context, the rate function can be computed via the
constraint effective action

Fukuda and Kyriakopoulos 1975
;
O’Raifeartaigh et al. 1986
;
Hasenfratz et al. 1989
;
Göckeler and Leutwyler 1991
;
Göckeler and Leutwyler 1991
;
Mukaida and Shimada 1996
;
Balog et al. 2022
, an analog of the standard effective action specifically designed to extract the rate function. This construction highlights the conceptual and practical distinctions between the rate function and the RG fixed point potential, emphasizing that, unlike the fixed point potential, the rate function is a universal function that explicitly depends on the system size and remains independent of the RG scheme. Within this framework, Refs.
Balog et al. 2022
;
Rançon et al. 2025
computed the rate function for the three-dimensional Ising and O(
N
) model using the simplest FRG approximation, the local potential approximation (LPA), obtaining good agreement with Monte Carlo (MC) simulations.

However, several aspects remain unresolved, which we address in this paper. First, the LPA is a relatively crude approximation that neglects field renormalization, predicting a vanishing anomalous dimension
\eta
. We thus aim to test the convergence of the FRG by pushing the calculation to the second-order derivative expansion (DE2). This approach enables us to determine error bars for the rate function. It also makes it possible to compute correlation functions subject to the global constraint of a fixed order parameter, observables that are accessible in Monte Carlo (MC) simulations.

Finally, we explore the applicability of our formalism to the two-dimensional Ising model. In this case, many properties are known rigorously.
However, the moment generating functional of the order-parameter PDF is directly related to the partition function in a homogeneous magnetic field, presenting a theoretical challenge that remains open. Currently, rigorous results are limited to the tail of the PDF
Camia et al. 2016
. In two dimensions, the large anomalous dimension (
\eta=1/4
) renders the LPA inadequate and the DE2 is needed.
We compare our theoretical predictions with MC simulations in two and three dimensions, achieving good agreement and underscoring the robustness and precision of the FRG.

Section

V
, while our conclusions are given in
Section

VI
.

II
Constraint effective action

We seek to compute observables of a theory while imposing a constraint on its order parameter. The formalism to do so relies on the constraint effective action
\check{\Gamma}
. Akin to what the effective action is in absence of constraint,
\check{\Gamma}
encodes information about all the correlations in the theory. Here, we define
\check{\Gamma}
and provide some of its properties.

For simplicity, we consider a field theory for a scalar field
\hat{\phi}(\textbf{x})
in
d
dimensions and we constrain the total magnetization
\hat{\phi}_{0}\equiv L^{-d}\int_{\textbf{x}}\hat{\phi}(\textbf{x})
to be given by a fixed value
s
. Here
\int_{\textbf{x}}\equiv\int d^{d}x
and
L^{d}
is the volume the
d
-dimensional system of finite size
L
with periodic boundary conditions. The field is sampled with a microscopic action (or Hamiltonian)
\mathcal{H}[\hat{\phi}]
. We note averages with respect to the Boltzmann weight, without the constraint, as
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle\propto\int\mathcal{D}[\hat{\phi}]\mathcal{O}e^{-\mathcal{H}(\hat{\phi})}
. We assume that the theory is regularized at a microscopic scale
a
, e.g. the lattice spacing of the underlying microscopic model.

The PDF
P_{L}(s)
of the order parameter can be expressed as a constrained average,

P_{L}(s)=e^{-L^{d}I(s)}\propto\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\phi}\,\delta(\hat{\phi}_{0}-s)\,e^{-\mathcal{H}[\hat{\phi}]}.

(1)

Equation

1
defines the rate function
I(s)
, a quantity know in quantum field theory as the constraint effective potential
Fukuda and Kyriakopoulos 1975
;
O’Raifeartaigh et al. 1986
;
Göckeler and Leutwyler 1991
.

Our goal is to define a generalization of the constraint effective potential, the constraint effective action, just as the effective action is of the effective potential. In field theory, the effective action
\Gamma[\phi]
, or Gibbs free energy, is defined as the Legendre transform of the free energy
\log\mathcal{Z}[h]=\log\langle e^{h.\hat{\phi}}\rangle
, with
h.\hat{\phi}=\int_{\textbf{x}}h(\textbf{x})\hat{\phi}(\textbf{x})
, i.e.
\Gamma[\phi]=-\ln\mathcal{Z}[h]+h.\phi
with
h(\textbf{x})=\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta\phi(\textbf{x})}
. Naively, one could hope to define
I(s)
as the Legendre transform of the logarithm of
\check{\mathcal{Z}}[h]=\langle e^{h.\hat{\phi}}\delta(\hat{\phi}_{0}-s)\rangle
. However, since in constant field
h(\textbf{x})=h
,
\check{\mathcal{Z}}[h]\propto e^{hL^{d}s}P_{L}(s)
, its logarithm is linear in
h
and the Legendre transform is ill-defined.

To remediate this problem, we follow
Balog et al. 2022
and mollify the delta constraint as a Gaussian with parameter
M
,

\mathcal{Z}_{M}[h]=\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\phi}\,e^{-\mathcal{H}_{M}[\hat{\phi}]+h.\hat{\phi}},

(2)

where
{\cal H}_{M}[\hat{\phi}]={\cal H}[\hat{\phi}]+\frac{M^{2}}{2}[\int_{\textbf{x}}(\hat{\phi}(\textbf{x})-s)]^{2}
. Here, the
M
-dependent part plays the role of a soft constraint on the zero-momentum mode of the field. In the limit
M\to\infty
, this constraint becomes, up to a constant, a Dirac-delta, and we get
\lim_{M\to\infty}\mathcal{Z}_{M}[h]\propto\check{\mathcal{Z}}[h]
. Similarly, we note the limit
M\to\infty
of any quantity
O_{M}
by
\check{O}
. In the opposite limit, we recover the standard generating function
\lim_{M\to 0}\mathcal{Z}_{M}[h]={\mathcal{Z}}[h]
.

The soft constraint makes it possible to define a modified Legendre transform, using
\phi(\textbf{x})=\frac{\delta\ln{\mathcal{Z}}_{M}}{\delta h(\textbf{x})}
, as

\Gamma_{M}[\phi]=-\ln\mathcal{Z}_{M}[h]+h.\phi-\frac{M^{2}}{2}\left[\int_{\textbf{x}}(\phi(\textbf{x})-s)\right]^{2},

(3)

with
h(\textbf{x})
given by (and thus depending implicitly on
\phi(\textbf{x})
and
s
)

\frac{\delta\Gamma_{M}}{\delta\phi(\textbf{x})}=h(\textbf{x})-M^{2}\int_{\textbf{y}}(\phi(\textbf{y})-s).

(4)

This in turn implies

\begin{split}e^{-\Gamma_{M}[\phi]}=\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\phi}\,e^{-{\cal H}(\hat{\phi})+\frac{\delta\Gamma_{M}}{\delta\phi}.(\hat{\phi}-\phi)}e^{-\frac{M^{2}}{2}\left[\int_{\textbf{x}}(\hat{\phi}(\textbf{x})-\phi(\textbf{x}))\right]^{2}}.\end{split}

(5)

Note that due to the definition of the modified Legendre transform,
\Gamma_{M}[\phi]
does not depend on
s
, even though
\mathcal{Z}_{M}[h]
does so implicitly.

Let us now give some physical interpretation to
\Gamma_{M}
. First, for
M=0
, one recovers the standard effective action
\lim_{M\to 0}\Gamma_{M}[\phi]=\Gamma[\phi]
. In the opposite limit, we obtained the constraint effective action
\check{\Gamma}[\phi]\equiv\lim_{M\to\infty}\Gamma_{M}[\phi]
.
When evaluated in a constant field
\phi(\textbf{x})=\bar{\phi}
,

\frac{\delta\Gamma_{M}}{\delta\phi(\textbf{x})}\bigg|_{\phi(\textbf{x})=\bar{\phi}}=h-M^{2}L^{d}(\bar{\phi}-s)

(6)

is a constant, such that
\langle\hat{\phi}(\textbf{x})\rangle=\bar{\phi}
by translation invariance. Therefore,

e^{-\Gamma_{M}[\phi(\textbf{x})=\bar{\phi}]}=\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\phi}\,e^{-{\cal H}(\hat{\phi})-\frac{M^{2}}{2}[\int_{\textbf{x}}(\hat{\phi}(\textbf{x})-\bar{\phi})]^{2}+\int_{\textbf{x}}(\hat{\phi}(\textbf{x})-\bar{\phi})\frac{\delta\Gamma_{M}}{\delta\phi(\textbf{x})}\big|_{\bar{\phi}}},

(7)

and thus

\begin{split}\lim_{M\to\infty}e^{-\Gamma_{M}[\phi(\textbf{x})=\bar{\phi}]}&\propto\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\phi}\,\delta\left(\bar{\phi}-\hat{\phi}_{0}\right)e^{-{\cal H}(\hat{\phi})}\\
&\propto P_{L}(s=\bar{\phi}),\end{split}

(8)

that is,
I(s=\bar{\phi})=L^{-d}\check{\Gamma}[\phi=\bar{\phi}]
just as, in the absence of constraint, the effective potential is given by
U(\bar{\phi})=L^{-d}\Gamma[\phi=\bar{\phi}]
.
As
\bar{\phi}
and
s
play the same role in
Eq.

8
, we will use
s
instead of
\bar{\phi}
in the remainder of the manuscript.
We further note that
\check{\Gamma}[\phi=s]
is not the Legendre transform of
\ln\check{\mathcal{Z}}[h]
in constant field, since the
M\to\infty
limit and the modified Legendre transform do not commute. Indeed
\check{\Gamma}[\phi]
is well defined for all
\phi
because we subtracted an infinite term in
Eq.

3
.

The interpretation of the constraint effective action for a non-constant field is less obvious. However, its functional derivatives, evaluated in a constant field
\phi(\textbf{x})=s
have a clear meaning. We introduce the
n
-point connected correlation functions

\begin{split}G_{M}^{(n)}(\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1,\ldots,n};s)&=\frac{\delta^{n}\ln\mathcal{Z}_{M}[h]}{\delta h(\textbf{x}_{1})\ldots\delta h(\textbf{x}_{n})}\bigg|_{h(\textbf{x})=\bar{h}},\end{split}

(9)

such that
\check{G}^{(n)}(\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1,\ldots,n};s)
is the connected
n
-point correlation function at fixed magnetization. For instance, for
n=2
, we have

\begin{split}\check{G}(\textbf{x}_{1},\textbf{x}_{2};s)\equiv\check{G}^{(2)}(\textbf{x}_{1},\textbf{x}_{2};s)&=\frac{\langle\hat{\phi}(\textbf{x}_{1})\hat{\phi}(\textbf{x}_{2})\delta(s-\hat{\phi}_{0})\rangle}{\langle\delta(s-\hat{\phi}_{0})\rangle}-\frac{\langle\hat{\phi}(\textbf{x}_{1})\delta(s-\hat{\phi}_{0})\rangle}{\langle\delta(s-\hat{\phi}_{0})\rangle}\frac{\langle\hat{\phi}(\textbf{x}_{2})\delta(s-\hat{\phi}_{0})\rangle}{\langle\delta(s-\hat{\phi}_{0})\rangle}.\end{split}

(10)

It is well known that for the second functional derivative of the
standard
effective action is the inverse of the two-point correlation function. For the constrained effective action, the result almost holds, except at vanishing momentum. Indeed, standard manipulations involving Legendre transforms, adapted to the modified definition used here, show that

\int_{\textbf{y}}\left(\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma_{M}[\phi]}{\delta\phi(\textbf{x})\delta\phi(\textbf{y})}+M^{2}\right)\frac{\delta^{2}\ln\mathcal{Z}_{M}}{\delta h(\textbf{y})\delta h(\textbf{z})}=\delta(\textbf{x}-\textbf{z}).

(11)

Evaluating this result in a constant field
\phi(\textbf{x})=s
, and calling the
n
-point vertex function in constant field

\begin{split}\Gamma^{(n)}_{M}(\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1,\ldots,n};s)&=\frac{\delta^{n}\Gamma_{M}[\phi]}{\delta\phi(\textbf{x}_{1})\ldots\delta\phi(\textbf{x}_{n})}\bigg|_{\phi(\textbf{x})=s},\end{split}

(12)

we obtain

\int_{\textbf{y}}\left(\Gamma^{(2)}_{M}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y};s)+M^{2}\right)G_{M}(\textbf{y},\textbf{z};s)=\delta(\textbf{x}-\textbf{z}),

(13)

or, using translation invariance and going to momentum space,

G_{M}(\mathbf{p};s)\equiv G^{(2)}_{M}(\mathbf{p},-\mathbf{p};s)=\left(\Gamma^{(2)}_{M}(\mathbf{p};s)+M^{2}\delta_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{0}}\right)^{-1}.

(14)

In a finite size system the momenta are discrete,
\mathbf{p}=2\pi\mathbf{n}/L
,
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and
\delta_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{0}}
stands for the Kronecker delta.
In particular, since
\lim_{M\to\infty}\Gamma^{(2)}_{M}=\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}
is well defined, we get that

\check{G}(\mathbf{p};s)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{0}$},\\
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}(\mathbf{p};s)^{-1}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(15)

The fact that
\check{G}(\mathbf{p}=0;s)=0
is a sum-rule implied by the constraint, since from
Eq.

10

\begin{split}\check{G}(\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{0};s)&\propto L^{-d}\int_{\textbf{x}}\check{G}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y};s)\\
&=\frac{\langle\hat{\phi}_{0}\hat{\phi}(\textbf{y})\delta(s-\hat{\phi}_{0})\rangle}{\langle\delta(s-\hat{\phi}_{0})\rangle}-s^{2}\\
&=0,\end{split}

(16)

where we used
\langle\hat{\phi}(\textbf{y})\delta(s-\hat{\phi}_{0})\rangle=s\langle\delta(s-\hat{\phi}_{0})\rangle
by translation invariance. Importantly, in our framework the sum-rule comes from the
M^{2}\to\infty
term in the right-hand-side of
Eq.

14
and not from the behavior of
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}(\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{0};s)
. In fact, using the method outlined in
Blaizot et al. 2006
one readily shows that
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}(\mathbf{0}=0;s)=I^{\prime\prime}(s)
(where the primes denote derivatives with respect to the argument). As we will see in
Section

III.2
, the behavior of
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}(\mathbf{p};s)
at
\mathbf{p}\neq\mathbf{0}
and that of
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}(\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{0};s)
are in a certain sense unrelated.

The relationship between arbitrary
n
-point correlation functions and vertex functions follows from textbook methods
Dupuis 2023
, while the sum-rule also generalizes to
n
-point correlation functions when any one momentum vanishes. For instance,

\check{G}^{(3)}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y},\textbf{z};s)=-\int_{\textbf{x}^{\prime},\textbf{y}^{\prime},\textbf{z}^{\prime}}\check{\Gamma}^{(3)}(\textbf{x}^{\prime},\textbf{y}^{\prime},\textbf{z}^{\prime};s)\check{G}(\textbf{x}^{\prime},\textbf{x};s)\check{G}(\textbf{y}^{\prime},\textbf{y};s)\check{G}(\textbf{z}^{\prime},\textbf{z};s),

(17)

and
\int_{\textbf{x}}\check{G}^{(3)}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y},\textbf{z};s)=\int_{\textbf{y}}\check{G}^{(3)}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y},\textbf{z};s)=\int_{\textbf{z}}\check{G}^{(3)}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y},\textbf{z};s)=0
.

Of course, computing
\check{\Gamma}
is as hard as computing any generating function and cannot be done exactly in general. In the following, we leverage the methodology of FRG to compute approximate flows of the constraint effective action.

III
Scale-dependent constraint effective action

III.1
Definition and exact flow equation

To implement the machinery of the FRG, still following
Balog et al. 2022
, we construct a one-parameter family of models denoted by
{\cal Z}_{M,k}[h]
by modifying the original Hamiltonian
{\cal H}_{M}
into
{\cal H}_{M}+\Delta{\cal H}_{k}
.
Here,
\Delta{\cal H}_{k}
is a term aimed at effectively freezing the low wavenumber fluctuations
\hat{\phi}(|\mathbf{q}|<k)
while leaving the high wavenumber modes
\hat{\phi}(|\mathbf{q}|>k)
unchanged. It is chosen to be quadratic,
\Delta{\cal H}_{k}=1/2\,\hat{\phi}.R_{k}.\hat{\phi}
,
where
R_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
satisfies the following conditions: (i) when
k\sim a^{-1}
,
R_{k\sim a^{-1}}(|\mathbf{q}|)
is very large for all
|\mathbf{q}|
, implying that all fluctuations are frozen; and (ii)
R_{k=0}(|\mathbf{q}|)\equiv 0
, so that all fluctuations are integrated over, and
{\cal Z}_{M,k=0}[h]={\cal Z}_{M}[h]
. Varying the scale
k
between
a^{-1}
and 0 induces the RG flow of
{\cal Z}_{M,k}[h]
, where fluctuations of wavenumbers
|\mathbf{q}|>k
are progressively integrated over.

Defining the scale-dependent constraint effective action as

\Gamma_{M,k}[\phi]=-\ln\mathcal{Z}_{M,k}[h]+h.\phi-\frac{1}{2}\phi.R_{k}.\phi-\frac{M^{2}}{2}\left[\int_{\textbf{x}}(\phi(\textbf{x})-s)\right]^{2},

(18)

the equivalent of
Eq.

5
is

e^{-\Gamma_{M,k}[\phi]}=\int\mathcal{D}\hat{\phi}\,e^{-{\cal H}[\hat{\phi}]-\frac{1}{2}(\hat{\phi}-\phi).R_{M,k}.(\hat{\phi}-\phi)+\frac{\delta\Gamma_{M,k}}{\delta\phi}.(\hat{\phi}-\phi)},

(19)

where
R_{M,k}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})=R_{k}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})+M^{2}
, or in momentum space
R_{M,k}(\mathbf{q})=R_{k}(\mathbf{q})+M^{2}\delta_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{0}}
. Up to the
M^{2}
term in
R_{M,k}
,
Eq.

19
is formally identical to the usual scale-dependent effective action
\Gamma_{k}
introduced in FRG
Dupuis et al. 2021
, and in particular
\Gamma_{k}[\phi]=\Gamma_{M=0,k}[\phi]
. The exact FRG equation satisfied by
\Gamma_{M,k}[\phi]
is the usual Wetterich equation in the presence of the regulator
R_{M,k}
:

\partial_{k}\Gamma_{M,k}[\phi]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\textbf{x},\textbf{y}}\partial_{k}R_{M,k}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})\left(\Gamma_{M,k}^{(2)}+R_{M,k}\right)^{-1}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y}),

(20)

where
\Gamma_{M,k}^{(2)}=\Gamma_{M,k}^{(2)}[\textbf{x},\textbf{y};\phi]=\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma_{M,k}}{\delta\phi(\textbf{x})\delta\phi(\textbf{y})}
. The flow equation of
\check{\Gamma}_{k}=\lim_{M\to\infty}\Gamma_{M,k}
is given by the limit
M\to\infty
of the right-hand-side of
Eq.

20
, i.e. the derivative
\partial_{k}
and the limit
M\to\infty
commute.
This equation has been solved for the three dimensional Ising universality class in the simplest approximation, the local potential approximation (LPA) in
Balog et al. 2022
and in
Rançon et al. 2025
for the O(
N
) model, with good agreement for the shape of the rate function between FRG and Monte Carlo simulations.

Defining as above the
n
-point (scale-dependent) vertex function in a constant field

\begin{split}\Gamma^{(n)}_{M,k}(\{\textbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1,\ldots,n};s)&=\frac{\delta^{n}\Gamma_{M,k}[\phi]}{\delta\phi(\textbf{x}_{1})\ldots\delta\phi(\textbf{x}_{n})}\bigg|_{\phi(\textbf{x})=s},\end{split}

(21)

as well as the scale-dependent effective potential
U_{M,k}(s)=L^{-d}\Gamma_{M,k}[\phi]\big|_{\phi(\textbf{x})=s}
, and using translational invariance when the field is constant, we obtain a hierarchy of flow equations

\displaystyle\partial_{k}U_{M,k}(s)={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\partial_{k}R_{M,k}(\mathbf{q})G_{M,k}(\mathbf{q};s),

(22)

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\Gamma^{(2)}_{M,k}(\mathbf{p};s)={}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\partial_{k}R_{M,k}(q)G_{M,k}^{2}(\mathbf{q};s)\Big(\Gamma^{(4)}_{M,k}(\mathbf{p},-\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{q};s)

\displaystyle-2\Gamma^{(3)}_{M,k}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p};s)G_{M,k}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p};s)\Gamma^{(3)}_{M,k}(-\mathbf{p},-\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q};s)\Big),

(23)

where
G_{M,k}(\mathbf{q};s)=\left(\Gamma_{M,k}^{(2)}(\mathbf{q};s)+R_{M,k}(\mathbf{q})\right)^{-1}
.

The corresponding equations for the scale-dependent constraint action are obtained by taking the limit
M\to\infty
. As discussed in
Section

II
, the vertex functions are well behaved in this limit (the inclusion of the regulator does not change this),
\lim_{M\to\infty}U_{M,k}(s)=I_{k}(s)
and
\lim_{M\to\infty}\Gamma^{(n)}_{M,k}=\check{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{k}
, while

\lim_{M\to\infty}G_{M,k}(\mathbf{q};s)=\check{G}_{k}(\mathbf{q};s)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}$},\\
\left(\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}(\mathbf{q};s)+R_{k}(\mathbf{q})\right)^{-1}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(24)

Further noting that
\partial_{k}R_{M,k}=\partial_{k}R_{k}
, the flow equations
Eqs.

22
and
23
become

\displaystyle\partial_{k}I_{k}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\neq 0}\partial_{k}R_{k}(\mathbf{q})\check{G}_{k}(\mathbf{q};s),

(25)

\displaystyle\partial_{k}\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{p};s)={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\neq\mathbf{0}}\partial_{k}\check{G}_{k}(\mathbf{q};s)\check{\Gamma}^{(4)}_{k}(\mathbf{p},-\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{q};s)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\neq\mathbf{0},-\mathbf{p}}\partial_{k}\left(\check{G}_{k}(\mathbf{q};s)\check{G}_{k}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{p};s)\right)\check{\Gamma}^{(3)}_{k}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{q}-\mathbf{p};s)\check{\Gamma}^{(3)}_{k}(-\mathbf{p},-\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q};s).

(26)

Note that these two equations are consistent with the fact that
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{0};s)=I_{k}^{\prime\prime}(s)
. Indeed, for
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{0}
, the second reads

\begin{split}\partial_{k}\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{0};s)&=\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\neq\mathbf{0}}\partial_{k}\check{G}_{k}(\mathbf{q};s)\check{\Gamma}^{(4)}_{k}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0},\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{q};s)-\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\neq\mathbf{0}}\partial_{k}\left(\check{G}^{2}_{k}(\mathbf{q};s)\right)\left(\check{\Gamma}^{(3)}_{k}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{q};s)\right)^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\neq 0}\partial_{k}\check{G}_{k}(\mathbf{q};s)\partial_{s}^{2}\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{q};s)-\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\neq\mathbf{0}}\partial_{k}\left(\check{G}^{2}_{k}(\mathbf{q};s)\right)\left(\partial_{s}\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{q};s)\right)^{2}\\
&=\partial_{s}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\neq\mathbf{0}}\partial_{k}R_{k}(\mathbf{q})\check{G}_{k}(\mathbf{q};s)\right)\\
&=\partial_{k}I_{k}^{\prime\prime}(s),\end{split}

(27)

where we have used the exact relationship between vertices
Blaizot et al. 2006

\begin{split}\check{\Gamma}^{(3)}_{k}(\mathbf{p},-\mathbf{p},\mathbf{0})=\partial_{s}\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{p}),\\
\check{\Gamma}^{(4)}_{k}(\mathbf{p},-\mathbf{p},\mathbf{0},\mathbf{0})=\partial_{s}^{2}\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{p}).\end{split}

(28)

III.2
Finite-size effect

At finite size, since the momenta
\mathbf{p}=2\pi\mathbf{n}/L
,
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
are quantized, the behavior of the vertex
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{p};s)
at small momenta has to be investigated with special care.
Physically, the reason why one expects
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{0};s)
to behave differently from
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{p};s)
for
\mathbf{p}\neq\mathbf{0}
arises from the construction of the constraint effective action, with an infinite mass affecting only the zero-momentum mode. This is already evidenced in the properties of the constraint correlation function, which vanishes at zero momenta by the sum rule while for
\mathbf{p}\neq\mathbf{0}
,
\check{G}(\mathbf{p};s)^{-1}=\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}(\mathbf{p};s)
[see
Eq.

14
].

This is reflected at the level of the flow equations. Comparison of
Sections

III.1
and
27
reveals a peculiar difference between the flow equation of
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{0};s)
and
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{p};s)
for
\mathbf{p}\neq\mathbf{0}
. Indeed, for
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(0;s)
only the
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}
term is absent from the sum in the bubble diagram (the second term of both
Sections

III.1
and
27
), while for
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{p};s)
both the
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{q}=-\mathbf{p}
terms are absent. As a consequence, this discrepancy results in a behavior of the vertex at
\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{0}
, see
Fig.

1
. Indeed, while one can formally describe the
\mathbf{p}
dependence of
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{p};s)
at small
\mathbf{p}
as

\begin{split}\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{p};s)&=\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{0};s)+(1-\delta_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{0}})\check{\Delta}_{2,k}(s)+\check{Z}_{k}(s)\mathbf{p}^{2}+O(|\mathbf{p}|^{4}),\end{split}

(29)

a function
\check{\Delta}_{2,k}(s)\neq 0
becomes necessary.
1
1

1

This behavior has also been observed in a zero-dimensional quantum system, see
(
Rançon 2014
, Appendix B)
. In that context, the finite temperature implies that Matsubara frequencies are discrete, and some vertex functions have a non-trivial frequency dependence that prevents a naive interpolation to zero frequency.

In the
L\to\infty
limit,
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{p};s)
becomes a function of a continuous variable with
\check{\Delta}_{2,k}(s)\to 0
and then
Eq.

29
reduces to the usual small momenta expansion of the inverse propagator.
In the thermodynamic limit, sums become integrals,

\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}f(\mathbf{q})\to\int_{\bm{\mathsf{q}}}f(\bm{\mathsf{q}})\equiv\int\frac{d^{d}\bm{\mathsf{q}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}f(\bm{\mathsf{q}}),

(30)

which are insensitive to the absence of isolated points (such as
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{q}=-\mathbf{p}
). Accordingly,
\partial_{k}\check{\Delta}_{2,k}=O(L^{-d})
vanishes so
\check{\Delta}_{2,k}(s)=0
while
\check{Z}_{k}(s)=\partial_{{\bm{\mathsf{p}}}^{2}}\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\bm{\mathsf{p}};s)\big|_{\bm{\mathsf{p}}=0}
and
Section

III.1
reduces to the FRG equation of the Ising model in the thermodynamic limit
Benitez et al. 2012
.

The corresponding behavior for higher-order vertices
\check{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{k}
, which necessitates the introduction of a corresponding
\check{\Delta}_{n,k}(s)
, is discussed in
Appendix

A
.

III.3
Derivative expansion

The most commonly used approximation scheme for FRG is the derivative expansion, where one assumes that the effective action can be expanded in the gradient of the field.
Naively, the corresponding ansatz to second order (DE2) for the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
universality class would read

\check{\Gamma}_{k}[\phi]=\int_{\textbf{x}}\left(\check{Z}_{k}(\phi(\textbf{x}))\frac{(\nabla\phi(\textbf{x}))^{2}}{2}+I_{k}(\phi(\textbf{x}))+\mathcal{O}(\nabla^{4})\right).

(31)

If, in addition, one imposes
\check{Z}_{k}(\phi(\textbf{x}))=1
, one recovers the local potential approximation used in
Balog et al. 2022
and
Rançon et al. 2025
.
The inverse propagator in a constant field corresponding to the ansatz (
31
) is

\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{p};s)=I^{\prime\prime}_{k}(s)+\check{Z}_{k}(s)\mathbf{p}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{p}^{4}).

(32)

While perfectly justified in the thermodynamic limit, this ansatz misses the zero-momentum behavior at finite size discussed above (
Eq.

29
).
There is no ansatz for
\check{\Gamma}
based on the derivative expansion that would reproduce such behavior.
To remedy the problem, we assume that the two-point vertex takes the form similar to
Eq.

29
,

\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{p};s)=I^{\prime\prime}_{k}(s)+(1-\delta_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{0}})\check{\Delta}_{2,k}(s)+\check{Z}_{k}(s)\mathbf{p}^{2},

(33)

with the definitions

\displaystyle\check{Z}_{k}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{p}_{2};s)-\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{p}_{1};s)}{\mathbf{p}_{2}^{2}-\mathbf{p}_{1}^{2}},

(34)

\displaystyle\check{\Delta}_{2,k}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\mathbf{p}_{1};s)-I^{\prime\prime}_{k}(s)-\mathbf{p}_{1}^{2}\check{Z}_{k}(s),

(35)

and
\mathbf{p}_{n}=2n\pi/L(1,0,\ldots,0)
. Note that in the thermodynamic limit the definition of the function
\check{Z}_{k}
in Eq. (
34
), reduces to a derivative
\check{Z}_{k}(s)=\partial_{{\bm{\mathsf{p}}}^{2}}\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}(\bm{\mathsf{p}};s)\big|_{\bm{\mathsf{p}}=\mathbf{0}}
, similar to a usual definition of the field renormalization function in the derivative expansion.

To derive the flow equation for
\check{Z}_{k}
, one needs the three- and four-point vertices
\check{\Gamma}_{k}^{(3)}
and
\check{\Gamma}_{k}^{(4)}
. As discussed in
Appendix

A
, these vertices generally involve distinct “finite-momentum shifts”
\check{\Delta}_{3,k}
and
\check{\Delta}_{4,k}
, defined analogously to
\check{\Delta}_{2,k}
but being independent functions. As the flow of
\check{\Delta}_{n,k}
depends on
\check{\Delta}_{n,k+1}
and
\check{\Delta}_{n,k+2}
, the derivative expansion does not close the hierarchy of equations. One therefore needs an additional approximation. One possibility would be to just neglect these terms and equate them to zero.
Instead, here we assume that we can approximate
\check{\Delta}_{3,k}(s)\simeq\check{\Delta}_{2,k}^{\prime}(s)
and
\check{\Delta}_{4,k}(s)\simeq\check{\Delta}_{2,k}^{\prime\prime}(s)
in analogy with
Eq.

28
.
We leave for future work the detailed study of the various
\check{\Delta}_{n,k}(s)
functions and the range of validity of this additional approximation.
Thus, we assume the following form for the three-point and four-point vertex functions that contribute to the flow of
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}_{k}
(with
\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}\neq\mathbf{0}
) ,

\begin{split}\check{\Gamma}^{(3)}_{k}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q};s)&=I_{k}^{(3)}(s)+\check{\Delta}_{2,k}^{\prime}(s)+\check{Z}_{k}^{\prime}(s)(\mathbf{p}^{2}+\mathbf{q}^{2}+\mathbf{p}.\mathbf{q}),\\
\check{\Gamma}^{(4)}_{k}(\mathbf{p},-\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{q};s)&=I_{k}^{(4)}(s)+\check{\Delta}_{2,k}^{\prime\prime}(s)+\check{Z}_{k}^{\prime\prime}(s)(\mathbf{p}^{2}+\mathbf{q}^{2}).\end{split}

(36)

The terms involving
I_{k}
and
\check{Z}_{k}
correspond to the standard vertex functions found in the literature in the thermodynamic limit, as obtained from
Eq.

31
. At second order in momenta, the terms written here are the ones allowed by
\pi/2
rotations, parity, and exchange of momenta. While they are invariant under continuous rotations, additional terms are allowed at higher orders (e.g.
p_{x}^{4}+p_{y}^{4}+\ldots
) due to the breaking of that symmetry induced by the boundaries.
For the constraint effective action, the DE2 approximation amounts to solving the coupled flows of
I_{k}
,
\check{Z}_{k}
and
\check{\Delta}_{2,k}
, contrary to the standard derivative expansion where only two functions are needed.

IV
Critical constraint effective action

IV.1
Scaling form

We now study the properties of the constraint effective action at the critical point between the ferromagnetic and paramagnetic phases.
The corresponding observables are expected to be universal, however the universal scaling function depends on how the limit
T\to T_{c}
and
L\to\infty
are taken
Balog et al. 2022
. For simplicity, we choose here to work directly at
T=T_{c}
and consider sizes
L
very large compared to the inverse UV cut-off (or lattice spacing).

At
T=T_{c}
, the rate function obeys the finite-size scaling law

I(s)=L^{-d}\tilde{I}(sL^{(d-2+\eta)/2}),

(37)

where
\eta
is the anomalous dimension (
\eta=1/4
in two dimensions and
\eta\simeq 0.036
in three dimensions), while

\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}
obeys the finite-size scaling law

\begin{split}\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}(\mathbf{p};s)&=L^{-2+\eta}\tilde{\Gamma}(\mathbf{p}L;sL^{(d-2+\eta)/2}).\end{split}

(38)

Considering first the mean-field scaling
\Gamma^{(2)}(\mathbf{p})-\Gamma^{(2)}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{p}^{2}
and second the zero-mode discrepancy, it is convenient to work with the function (
n\geq 2
)

A(\mathbf{p}_{n};s)=\frac{\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}(\mathbf{p}_{n};s)-\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}(\mathbf{p}_{1};s)}{\mathbf{p}_{n}^{2}-\mathbf{p}^{2}_{1}},

(39)

where
\mathbf{p}_{n}=2\pi n/L(1,0,\ldots,0)
. With this definition,
\check{Z}(s)=A(\mathbf{p}_{2};s)
, see
Section

III.3
.
Note that at criticality

A(\mathbf{p}_{n};s)=L^{\eta}\tilde{A}(\mathbf{p}_{n}L;sL^{(d-2+\eta)/2}).

(40)

To compare observables of different models belonging to the same universality class, e.g. that of Ising lattice model to those from a continuum scalar field theory, one must account for two independent nonuniversal amplitudes: one fixing the order-parameter scale and one fixing the correlation-length amplitude.
This is the essence of two-scale-factor universality
Bervillier 1976
. Furthermore, by a similar argument to that of Privman and Fisher
Privman and Fisher 1984
, one shows that
L^{d}I
is a universal function of the correctly rescaled magnetization, without any additional non-universal amplitude.
2
2

2

Privman and Fisher have argued that the singular part of the free energy density
f
scales as
f(t,h)=L^{-d}\tilde{f}(L/\xi_{0}t^{\nu},L^{(d+2-\eta)/2}h/h_{0})
, with
t=T/T_{c}-1
and
h
the magnetic field. The scaling function
\tilde{f}
is universal when both non-universal amplitudes
\xi_{0}
and
h_{0}
are fixed (for instance, one can choose
\xi_{0}
such that
\xi_{0}t^{-\nu}
equals the thermodynamic limit correlation length). The magnetization in turn obeys the scaling form
m=L^{-\frac{d-2+\eta}{2}}/h_{0}\tilde{f}^{(0,1)}(L/\xi_{0}t^{-\nu},L^{\frac{d+2-\eta}{2}}h/h_{0})
with
\tilde{f}^{(0,1)}(x,y)=\partial_{y}\tilde{f}(x,y)
, and the Gibbs free energy density (related to the effective action in constant field) reads
g(t,m)=L^{-d}\tilde{g}(L/\xi_{0}t^{\nu},L^{(d-2+\eta)/2}mh_{0})
with
\tilde{g}
the Legendre transform of
\tilde{f}
. It implies that the Gibbs free energy, and as a consequence the rate function, also obeys the two-scale universality. This corrects a wrong statement in
Balog et al. 2022
which asserts that there is an additional amplitude associated with the scale of the rate function.

Here, as we focus on the case
T=T_{c}
, the correlation length in the thermodynamic limit is infinite and we do not need to fix the corresponding amplitude. See however
Balog et al. 2022
;
Sahu et al. 2025
;
Rançon et al. 2025
for discussions of this aspect in the context of the rate function.
To fix the scale of the magnetization
s_{0}
, we choose it to be the position of the minimum of the rate function, i.e. such that
I^{\prime}(s_{0})=0
. Note that
s_{0}=b\,L^{-(d-2+\eta)/2}
, where
b
is the corresponding non-universal amplitude. Then all other quantities are measured in terms of
L
and
s_{0}
, using the following rules

\begin{split}I(s)\to&L^{d}(I(s/s_{0})-I(0)),\\
A(\mathbf{p}_{n};s)\to&L^{d-2}s^{2}_{0}A(\mathbf{p}_{n}L;s/s_{0}),\\
\check{Z}(s)\to&L^{d-2}s^{2}_{0}\check{Z}(s/s_{0}).\end{split}

(41)

We subtract a constant from the rate function, corresponding to the normalization of the PDF.
Note that
\check{Z}(s)
scales as
L^{\eta}
, as expected for the field renormalization. All the results presented below have been rescaled following this procedure. Then the functions plotted are fully universal (i.e. completely independent of the microscopic details of the models).

We have performed MC simulations of the two- and three-dimensional Ising model at

s

to improve the statistics, corresponding effectively to about

configurations for each size

. The histogram is normalized at the end to determine the PDF.

for various system sizes. The results are converged and show scaling. Therefore, the oscillations in

are not due to finite-size corrections. All MC data have been filtered for clarity using a cubic Savitzky–Golay filter over a hundred points, with shaded bands indicating the data’s maximum and minimum fluctuations.

We now turn to the analysis of the momentum dependence of the constraint correlation functions, using the function
A(\mathbf{p}_{n};s)
.
Fig.

6
shows its first three modes extracted from Monte Carlo simulations, plotted alongside the
\check{Z}
function at LPA and DE2.
The lowest Monte Carlo modes lie almost entirely within the DE2 result, up to error bars and closely follow its amplitude and form, up to the statistical noise. Note that the LPA result already gives the typical amplitude of
A(\mathbf{p}_{n};s)
around
s=0
, even though it cannot describe its magnetization dependence.
While strictly speaking
\check{Z}(s)
corresponds to
A(\mathbf{p}_{2};s)
only, the fact that
A(\mathbf{p}_{3};s)
and
A(\mathbf{p}_{4};s)
are also well approximated by
\check{Z}(s)

\check{\Gamma}(\mathbf{p};s)
has a quadratic dependence in
\mathbf{p}
at small enough
\mathbf{p}
. (Recall that the dimensionfull
\check{\Gamma}^{(2)}
scales as
L^{-2+\eta}
, as expected.)

Together with our results for the rate function in three dimensions, this confirms that the DE2 expansion faithfully captures all significant features of the Monte Carlo data, and that the derivative expansion is a good approximation for the constraint effective action in three dimensions.

In two dimensions,
Fig.

7
, the Monte Carlo results show a rather surprising behavior in the second-momentum component
A(\mathbf{p}_{2},s)
, which departs markedly from the behavior of all higher-momentum modes. The latter are more similar to the three-dimensional case, with a slow rise and fall. We find that the
\check{Z}
function obtained from FRG at order DE2 is in relatively good agreement with the
A(\mathbf{p}_{n};s)
for
n>2
. We have checked that oscillations observed in
A(\mathbf{p}_{2};s)
are genuine, and not due to finite size corrections. Indeed, the inset of
Fig.

7
shows that these oscillations obey scaling, as observed by changing the system size. It is still unclear what the cause is for these oscillations, but we hypothesize that they might be due to droplet effects induced by the fixed magnetization, which introduces an additional length in the system. Indeed, a similar, and much more pronounced, effect can be found in the one-dimensional Ising model in its scaling limit (
T\to 0
and
L\to\infty
), where a fixed magnetization imposes the existence of domain walls, and thus an additional length-scale associated to the domains that induces oscillations
pre
.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we have developed a robust framework to compute the constraint effective action at criticality using the FRG. Our approach extends beyond the LPA by incorporating a second-order derivative expansion, significantly enhancing the accuracy of the results, particularly evident in two-dimensional systems where the anomalous dimension plays a crucial role. Our results demonstrate excellent agreement with Monte Carlo simulations, validating the reliability and predictive power of the FRG method for computing universal scaling functions, including the rate function and momentum-dependent correlations at critical points.

We have also shown that the momentum dependence of the constraint vertices is rather non-trivial and differs from the usual behavior in the thermodynamic limit. Taking the example of the two-point constraint vertex, we have shown that it involves a new function
\check{\Delta}_{2}(s)
which vanishes in the thermodynamic limit. This is also the case for higher vertices, with additional functions that need to be studied in more detail.
Finally, it would be particularly insightful to apply the Blaizot–Mendez-Galain–Wschebor (BMW) approximation
Blaizot et al. 2006
;
Benitez et al. 2012
within this framework to explore the full momentum dependence of the constraint correlation functions because it could clarify the subtle field and momentum behaviors observed, particularly in lower dimensions.

Appendix A
Momentum structure of the vertices

As discussed in
Section

III.2
, at finite size, the two-point vertex in constant field is expected to take the form

\check{\Gamma}_{k}^{(2)}(\mathbf{p};s)=I^{\prime\prime}_{k}(s)+(1-\delta_{\mathbf{p},\mathbf{0}})\check{\Delta}_{2,k}(s)+\check{\Sigma}_{2,k}(\mathbf{p};s),

(46)

where
\check{\Sigma}_{2,k}(\mathbf{p})
includes the finite-momentum variation of the vertex, with
\check{\Sigma}_{2,k}(\mathbf{0})=0
, while
\check{\Delta}_{2,k}(s)
corresponds to a “finite-momentum shift” compared to the zero-momentum part
I^{\prime\prime}_{k}(s)
. As explained in
Section

III.3
, a derivative expansion approximation of
\check{\Sigma}_{2,k}(\mathbf{p})
is then
\check{\Sigma}_{2,k}(\mathbf{p})\simeq\check{Z}_{k}(s)\mathbf{p}^{2}+\ldots
.

In the present Appendix, we discuss the momentum structure of the
n
-point functions at finite size, which differs from that in the thermodynamic limit. For this, it is convenient first to rewrite
Eq.

46
as
3
3

3

In this Appendix only,
\{\mathbf{p}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
correspond to
n
arbitrary momenta indexed by the letter
i
, rather than to specific momenta
2\pi/L(i,0,\dots,0)
along some direction, as in the other Sections.

\check{\Gamma}_{k}^{(2)}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2};s)=\delta_{\mathbf{p}_{1}+\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{0}}\left(I^{\prime\prime}_{k}(s)+K_{2}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2})\check{\Delta}_{2,k}(s)+\check{\Sigma}_{2,k}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2};s)\right).

(47)

Here we have written the two-point vertex function as depending on two momenta, with the conservation of momentum included in the right-hand side (i.e.
\check{\Gamma}_{k}^{(2)}(\mathbf{p};s)=\check{\Gamma}_{k}^{(2)}(\mathbf{p},-\mathbf{p};s)
), and we have introduced the symbol
K_{i}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n})
, which is equal to
1
if exactly
i
of the
n
momenta in its argument are not zero, and vanish otherwise. For instance,
\delta_{\mathbf{p}_{1}+\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{0}}K_{2}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2})=\delta_{\mathbf{p}_{1}+\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{0}}(1-\delta_{\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{0}})
.

With this notation, the three-point vertex function can thus be written, in analogy with
Eq.

47
, as

\check{\Gamma}_{k}^{(3)}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{p}_{3};s)=\delta_{\mathbf{p}_{1}+\mathbf{p}_{2}+\mathbf{p}_{3},\mathbf{0}}\left(I^{(3)}_{k}(s)+K_{3}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{p}_{3})\check{\Delta}_{3,k}(s)+K_{2}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{p}_{3})\partial_{s}\check{\Delta}_{2,k}(s)+\check{\Sigma}_{3,k}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{p}_{3};s)\right).

(48)

Here
\check{\Sigma}_{3,k}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{p}_{3};s)
is constrained when one momenta vanish, e.g.
\check{\Sigma}^{(3)}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{0};s)=\partial_{s}\check{\Sigma}_{k}^{(2)}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2};s)
, which in addition to the term proportional to
K_{2}
insures that the exact relationship
\check{\Gamma}^{(3)}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{0};s)=\partial_{s}\check{\Gamma}_{k}^{(2)}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2};s)
is obeyed. (All vertices are invariant under exchange of momenta, and the corresponding relationships when a momentum is put to zero are assumed.)
Here again,
\check{\Sigma}_{3,k}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{p}_{3};s)
is assumed to vanish when all its momenta are zero, and to have a derivative expansion, with
\check{\Delta}_{3,k}
playing the role of a shift.

This can be generalized to an arbitrary
n
-point vertex as

\check{\Gamma}_{k}^{(n)}(\{\mathbf{p}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n};s)=\delta_{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{p}_{i},\mathbf{0}}\left(I^{(n)}_{k}(s)+\sum_{l=2}^{n}K_{l}\left(\{\mathbf{p}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)\partial_{s}^{n-l}\check{\Delta}_{l,k}(s)+\check{\Sigma}_{n,k}\left(\{\mathbf{p}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n};s\right)\right),

(49)

with once again
\check{\Sigma}_{n,k}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n-1},\mathbf{0};s)=\partial_{s}\check{\Sigma}_{n-1,k}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n-1};s)
.
Note that the symbol
K_{1}
never appears, since
\delta_{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{p}_{i},\mathbf{0}}K_{1}\left(\{\mathbf{p}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)
always vanish.

Importantly, in a derivative expansion, the various
\check{\Sigma}_{n,k}
are not independent from each other, as happens in the thermodynamic limit. For instance, to order two in momenta, we have
\check{\Sigma}_{2,k}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2};s)=\check{Z}_{k}(s)(\mathbf{p}_{1}^{2}+\mathbf{p}_{2}^{2}+\mathbf{p}_{1}.\mathbf{p}_{2})
, and consistency between vertices implies

\begin{split}\check{\Sigma}_{3,k}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{p}_{3};s)&=\check{Z}_{k}^{\prime}(s)(\mathbf{p}_{1}^{2}+\mathbf{p}_{2}^{2}+\mathbf{p}_{3}^{2}+\mathbf{p}_{1}.\mathbf{p}_{2}+\mathbf{p}_{2}.\mathbf{p}_{3}+\mathbf{p}_{1}.\mathbf{p}_{3}),\\
\check{\Sigma}_{4,k}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\mathbf{p}_{2},\mathbf{p}_{3},\mathbf{p}_{4};s)&=\check{Z}_{k}^{\prime\prime}(s)(\mathbf{p}_{1}^{2}+\mathbf{p}_{2}^{2}+\mathbf{p}_{3}^{2}+\mathbf{p}_{4}^{2}+\mathbf{p}_{1}.\mathbf{p}_{2}+\mathbf{p}_{2}.\mathbf{p}_{3}+\mathbf{p}_{3}.\mathbf{p}_{4}+\mathbf{p}_{1}.\mathbf{p}_{3}+\mathbf{p}_{1}.\mathbf{p}_{4}+\mathbf{p}_{2}.\mathbf{p}_{4}).\\
\end{split}

(50)

However, no such relationships exist between the
\check{\Delta}_{n,k}
, which are a priori independent functions.

In fact, it is possible to show that due to the constraint, the derivative expansion does not allow for closing the flow equations in terms of a finite number of functions (
I_{k}(s)
,
\check{Z}_{k}(s)
,
\check{\Delta}_{2,k}(s)
, etc.). The simplest way to show this is to consider the flow at order DE2 of some arbitrary vertex function when all momenta are finite (otherwise, it is related directly to a lower-order vertex). It reads

\partial_{k}\check{\Gamma}^{(n)}_{k}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n};s)=\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{\mathbf{q}\neq\mathbf{0}}\partial_{k}\check{G}_{k}(\mathbf{q};s)\check{\Gamma}^{(n+2)}_{k}(\mathbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{n},\mathbf{q},-\mathbf{q};s)+\ldots,

(51)

where we only keep the simplest diagram to make our point.
Since all momenta on the right-hand side are finite, only
\check{\Delta}_{n+2,k}(s)
and
\check{Z}^{(n)}
enter in the vertex
\check{\Gamma}^{(n+2)}_{k}
.

A similar argument shows that in every diagram, all vertices are evaluated at finite momenta, and a vertex
\Gamma^{(m)}_{k}
will contribute to the flow through
\check{\Delta}_{m,k}
and
\check{Z}_{k}^{(m)}
. Thus, the flow of
\check{\Delta}_{n,k}
depends on
\check{\Delta}_{n+1,k}
and
\check{\Delta}_{n+2,k}
, and the hierarchy does not close. One should in principle compute the flow of all
\check{\Delta}_{n,k}
, even within a finite order of the derivative expansion.

Appendix B
Implementation of the FRG approach

In this Appendix, we discuss some technical and numerical aspects of the FRG.
The FRG approach involves the introduction of an infrared regulator function
R_{k}(\mathbf{q})
, see
Eq.

20
. We use in the present work the Exponential regulator

R_{k}(\mathbf{q})=\alpha Z_{k}k^{2}e^{-\mathbf{q}^{2}/k^{2}}

(52)

which has been successfully used to study DE to high orders
Balog et al. 2019
.
Here
Z_{k}
is a scale of field renormalization needed to recover the critical physics (see below) and
\alpha
is a numerical prefactor. While the results should not, in principle, depend on
R_{k}(\mathbf{q})
, any approximation induces a weak regulator dependence. We choose the value of
\alpha
such that the results depend the least on the regulator, a procedure known as the
principle of minimal sensitivity (PMS)
Canet et al. 2003
;
Kos et al. 2016
. The PMS is necessary to obtain a convergence of the results with the order of the DE
Balog et al. 2019
. In practice, we choose the values of
\alpha
found in the literature for the PMS of the critical exponent
\nu
.

In order to explore physics near criticality, it is necessary to introduce dimensionless variables through rescaling by appropriate powers of
k
. Indeed,
k
explicitly breaks the scale invariance at criticality. We thus define the dimensionless quantities (noted with tildas)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \tilde{\mathbf{p}}&=\mathbf{p}/k,&\tilde{s}&=Z_{k}k^{2-d}s,&\tilde{I}_{k}(\tilde{s})&=k^{-d}I_{k}(s),&\tilde{\check{\Delta}}_{2,k}(\tilde{s})&=Z_{k}^{-1}k^{-2}\check{\Delta}_{2,k}(s),&\tilde{\check{Z}}_{k}(\tilde{s})&=Z_{k}^{-1}\check{Z}_{k}(s).&\end{aligned}

(53)

The number
Z_{k}
is necessary to take into account the anomalous dimension
\eta
of the field. It is defined by the renormalization condition
\tilde{\check{Z}}_{k}(0)=1
and at criticality
\partial_{t}Z_{k}\propto k^{-\eta}
. In dimensionless variables, the flow reaches a fixed point as
k
goes to zero when the initial condition is tuned to criticality. In practice, we tune the initial condition close to criticality, and first run the flow down to some mesoscopic scale
k^{*}
, taken to be small with respect to the ultraviolet scales (e.g. inverse lattice spacing) and at which the potentials are very close to their fixed point values. We introduce the RG time
t=\ln(k/k^{*})
. Length scales are measured in units of
{k^{*}}^{-1}
.

Numerically, we integrate the flow equations using the explicit Euler method with a RG time step of
10^{-4}
. For the field dependence of the potential, we change to the variable
\tilde{\rho}=\tilde{s}^{2}/2
and use an evenly spaced grid of two hundred points between
\rho=0
and
\tilde{\rho}_{\text{max}}
. We used the so-called strict implementation of DE2, where all terms of order four or more in momental are dropped from the intergrals defining the flow equations (e.g.,
|\mathbf{p}|^{4}
,
|\mathbf{q}|^{4}
,
\mathbf{q}^{2}\mathbf{p}^{2}
). Results with those parameters are stable up to at least five digits.

We now discuss implementation details specific to the problem of the determination of the rate function and working with a system of a finite size
L^{d}
.
To investigate critical physics we take
L\gg{k^{*}}^{-1}
.
The finite size of the system implies that the momenta are discrete: in dimensionless variables
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}=2\pi\mathbf{n}/kL
with
\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. In the flow equations (
25
) and (
III.1
),
\partial_{t}R_{k}(\mathbf{q})
acts as an ultraviolet regulator and only modes with
|\mathbf{q}|\lesssim ck
(with
c
some constant) contribute to the sums, which include a number
O((kL)^{d})
of terms. At the beginning of the flow,
k\gg L^{-1}
makes directly computing the sums not tractable numerically. Rather, we remark that the difference between two consecutive modes
\Delta q=2\pi/kL
is small and approximate sums with integrals, e.g.

\frac{1}{(kL)^{d}}\sum_{\tilde{\mathbf{q}}\neq 0}f(\tilde{\mathbf{q}})=\int\frac{d^{d}\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{q}}}}{(2\pi)^{d}}f(\tilde{\bm{\mathsf{q}}})-\frac{1}{(kL)^{d}}f(0)+o\bigg(\frac{1}{(kL)^{d}}\bigg).

(54)

Since the initial conditions
I_{k=k^{*}}(\rho)
,
\check{Z}_{k=k^{*}}(\rho)
and
\check{\Delta}_{2,k=k^{*}}(\rho)=0
are the same as the potentials
U_{k=k^{*}}(\rho)
and
Z_{k=k^{*}}(\rho)
in the thermodynamic limit, at the beginning of the flow (
kL\gg 1
) the flow equations are identical, up to
O(1/(kL)^{d})
terms.

As
k
approaches zero the approximation
54
is eventually not justified anymore and we revert back to computing the sums directly. We note
k_{\text{sum}}
the value of
k
at which we make the switch; we typically pick
k_{\text{sum}}L\sim 40
corresponding to an error in approximating sums with integrals smaller than
10^{-12}
.

Lastly, in order to determine the value of the rate function at finite magnetization of order
L^{-(d-2+\eta)/2}
, we switch back to a dimensionful field grid at the end of the flow. Indeed, the dimensionless grid
0\leq\tilde{\rho}\leq\tilde{\rho}_{\text{max}}=k^{d-2+\eta}\tilde{\rho}_{\text{max}}
shrinks around
0
as
k
is lowered. The change is made when
k
is such that the largest dimensionless
\tilde{s}_{\text{max}}=\sqrt{2\tilde{\rho}_{\text{max}}}
corresponds to the desired dimensionful
s
, typically two times the position of the minimum.
