Investigating the effects of employing recycled carpet fibers and micro-silica on the mechanical properties of concrete under elevated temperatures
Authors: Mahdi Shariati, Mahdiyeh Saedi, Faezeh Nejati, Ali Toghroli, Ali Shariati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The present research explores the behavioral characteristics of concrete produced with recycled carpet fibers and micro-silica additives, examined under both room temperature (25 °C) and high-temperature conditions (200 °C and 600 °C). Carpet fibers were incorporated into the mixtures at volume ratios of 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1%, and experimental investigations were carried out to determine the compressive strength, tensile resistance, and elastic modulus of the re-sulting composites. Unlike previous studies that examined fibers or micro-silica separately and primarily under ambient conditions, this work is original in combining recycled carpet fibers with micro-silica and assessing their synergistic effects under elevated temperatures (200 °C and 600 °C) through laboratory-scale testing of cubes, cylinders, and beams. The study highlights the combined use of recycled carpet fibers and micro-silica to enhance mechanical strength while preserving performance under thermal exposure, an aspect overlooked in earlier research. This dual focus enhances understanding of both mechanical behavior and sustainability, offering practical guidelines for structural applications. The results show that incorporating 0.5% carpet fibers with 10% micro-silica achieved the highest performance, improving CS by 21% compared with the reference mix. Higher fiber contents (0.75% and 1%) reduced performance due to poor fiber dispersion and bonding. At elevated temperatures, both compressive and tensile strength decreased, with the tensile strength exhibiting a more pronounced reduction due to fiber degradation. Furthermore, using 0.5% recycled carpet fibers promotes sustainability by diverting non-biodegradable waste from landfills and reducing the need for virgin materials, thereby lowering the environmental footprint of concrete production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-30671-5.

Introduction

Concrete remains the most extensively utilized material in the construction industry and is frequently described as the dominant building material of the modern era1. Despite its advantages such as durability, recyclability, environmental compatibility, and excellent CS it remains inherently weak in tension and prone to cracking, which limits its structural performance2. To address these shortcomings, researchers have developed Fiber-Reinforced Concrete (FRC), a composite material consisting of cement, sand, aggregates, water, and fibers, often enhanced with chemical or mineral admixtures. While a wide variety of fibers have been studied, most existing works emphasize either synthetic or natural fibers in isolation, often focusing on short-term improvements in strength under ambient conditions. However, there is limited critical evaluation of how these fibers perform under harsh thermal exposure or when combined with supplementary cementitious materials such as micro-silica. Carpet fibers, though promising, remain underexplored in this dual context, especially considering the environmental urgency of recycling Poly Propylene (PP)-based wastes3,4. In addition to its use as a primary construction material, concrete is increasingly used as a waste disposal medium. Although recycling waste in concrete is widely advocated, most previous studies have focused on the environmental benefits alone, with less attention to the trade-offs in long-term durability or performance under variable temperature conditions. In particular, while carpet fibers offer cost and sustainability advantages, their mechanical contribution has not been systematically benchmarked against other fiber types under both ambient and elevated temperatures. This gap highlights the need for a combined mechanical–environmental assessment, which this study aims to address5,6. The rapid expansion of PP carpet production has intensified the challenge of waste disposal, which is predominantly managed through landfilling or incineration. Both approaches pose serious environmental risks incineration contributes to air pollution, while landfilling consumes valuable land resources. ST-PP carpets are composite materials comprising face fibers, latex adhesives, and other components, making their recycling particularly challenging7. The integration of recycled carpet fibers into concrete mixtures provides an eco-friendly and sustainable solution, enhancing tensile performance, minimizing crack formation, and resulting in a more ductile fracture behavior rather than the brittle failure pattern commonly observed in conventional concrete. Moreover, the incorporation of micro-silica, a secondary product derived from electric arc furnace operations, has been demonstrated to improve the mechanical characteristics of concrete while simultaneously reducing cement usage. This material replacement not only contributes to higher structural performance but also lessens the ecological footprint of concrete manufacturing, considering that cement production is a significant source of greenhouse gas emissions8. The incorporation of nanomaterials, particularly Nano-Silica (NS), has been widely explored to enhance the performance of Portland Cement (PC)9. NS promotes the hydration of cementitious binders, accelerates the dissolution of C3S/C2S, and increases the formation and aggregation of Calcium–Silicate–Hydrate (C–S–H), thereby refining the pore structure and improving the density of the Interfacial Transition Zones (ITZs)10,11. Acting both as a pozzolanic material and as a micro-filler, NS reduces porosity, refines microstructure, and inhibits the growth of calcium hydroxide crystals, resulting in greater matrix stability12. Mechanical improvements are also well documented: low dosages of NS increase CS by up to 20%, although excessive content may reduce strength by more than 13%13. In addition to enhancing strength, NS contributes significantly to the durability of concrete by lowering water permeability, increasing resistance to chloride ion intrusion, and enhancing performance under freeze–thaw cycles14,15. Despite these benefits, the relatively high cost of NS and the lack of consensus on optimal dosage remain limitations16. Another widely researched strategy for enhancing concrete is the use of fibers to produce FRC. Fibers increase tensile strength, flexural toughness, and resistance to cracking and impact17. However, the cost of natural or polymer fibers is high, leading to a growing interest in waste and Recycled Fibers (RFs) as sustainable alternatives18. Among these, recycled carpet fibers (RCFs) have received attention due to the massive stockpiles of carpet waste approximately 1.6 million tons per year in Europe19, 400,000 tons in the UK20, and 3.4 million tons in the United States21 much of which is landfilled or incinerated, releasing harmful emissions20. Incorporating Recycled Carbon Fibers (RCFs) into concrete not only offers a sustainable disposal method but also improves mechanical performance22,23. Studies have reported improvements in flexural behavior, durability (particularly impermeability), and tensile toughness, with RCFs effectively bridging cracks and reducing shrinkage24. However, the influence of RCFs on CS remains inconsistent, with some findings noting reductions25,26. Overall, these studies highlight RCFs as a promising but underdeveloped solution for producing environmentally friendly FRC. Recent research has begun exploring the combined use of NS and fibers to exploit their complementary effects, with NS providing microstructural refinement and fibers contributing to macro-scale reinforcement27,28. Even though both materials improve PC performance on their own, many questions remain unanswered. For example, how much RCF volumetric content is optimal for mechanical performance. Additionally, NS tends to agglomerate at higher dosages, making it more challenging to use effectively. Lastly, it is not yet clear how NS and RCFs synergise to affect mechanical strength, microstructure, and durability, especially frost resistance29. It is also necessary to systematically evaluate the embodied energy, carbon footprint, and cost factors related to the sustainability of NS and RCF combinations30. To address the existing research gaps, the present investigation aims to: (1) employ nano-silica (NS) and recycled carpet fibers (RCFs) through an environmentally sustainable approach to enhance the mechanical strength and durability of concrete; (2) determine the optimum material proportions for large-scale applications; (3) conduct microstructural evaluations to analyze strength characteristics and resistance to freeze–thaw cycles; (4) develop a predictive model for estimating material lifespan under cyclic freezing and thawing conditions; and (5) examine both the environmental and economic implications of the proposed system31,32. As a result, recycling carpet waste has emerged as an effective strategy for mitigating environmental impacts while contributing to resource efficiency and sustainability. Previous studies on RCFs in FRC have reported significant improvements in toughness, shrinkage reduction, and crack control, with large-scale construction trials further demonstrating their practical feasibility33. Nevertheless, the heterogeneous composition of carpets, which typically contain multiple polymer layers and fillers, makes recycling processes complex and costly, thereby limiting their widespread adoption34. Furthermore, while encouraging results highlight the potential of RCFs as sustainable reinforcements, critical questions remain regarding their compatibility with supplementary cementitious materials such as micro-silica, as well as their performance under thermal and mechanical stresses. Addressing these gaps, the present study investigates the synergistic use of RCFs and micro-silica in concrete to improve mechanical properties while promoting environmental sustainability. Previous investigations have revealed that the inclusion of recycled carpet fibers can significantly improve the mechanical behavior of concrete composites. According to the findings of Hosseini and Yatim2 combining carpet waste fibers with palm oil fuel ash led to notable enhancements in tensile, flexural, and impact strength characteristics, whereas Mohammadhosseini et al.35 eported complementary results for other fiber types. Found that PP fibers, though slightly reducing CS, maintained acceptable mechanical performance. Pakravan et al.36 further confirmed that RCFs increase load and strain capacity despite their low modulus of elasticity. More recently, Nochaiya, Aisheh, and Tayeh37,38 investigated the combined use of micro-silica and fibers, reporting improvements in durability, abrasion resistance, and high-temperature stability, highlighting the potential of such combinations as sustainable reinforcements.

Gap, objective, and novelty of the study

While previous studies have explored the use of various fibers and additives, such as ST-PP and glass fibers, to enhance concrete’s mechanical properties, limited research exists on the simultaneous use of recycled carpet fibers and microsilica powder in concrete, particularly under elevated temperatures39. Moreover, most studies have focused on ambient temperature conditions, overlooking the thermal degradation of FRC, which is critical for structures exposed to high temperatures. This research seeks to bridge the existing knowledge gap by examining the influence of various volume ratios of recycled carpet fibers on the compressive and tensile strength of concrete under both ambient (25 °C) and elevated temperature conditions (200 °C and 600 °C). While previous investigations have typically focused on the use of waste fibers or micro-silica in isolation mainly under normal temperature settings there is still a scarcity of studies exploring their combined utilization at high temperatures40. The present study aims to explore the synergistic interaction between recycled carpet fibers and micro-silica, assessing their contribution to enhancing the mechanical behavior and environmental sustainability of concrete. The novelty of this research lies in its integrated approach, which combines a readily available waste material (carpet fibers) with an industrial by-product (micro-silica), to evaluate their joint performance under elevated thermal exposure. This dual perspective not only advances the scientific understanding of FRCs but also provides practical value for sustainable construction41. It is essential to note that this study was conducted using laboratory-scale specimens (cubes, cylinders, and beams) to assess the mechanical properties of concrete under controlled conditions. While bridge-scale or full structural analysis is beyond the present scope, the results offer preliminary understanding that may inform future larger-scale applications. Compared with previous works that mainly examined waste fibers or micro-silica separately under ambient conditions, the contribution of this study lies in demonstrating their synergistic effect under elevated temperatures.

Methodology

Materials

The main goal of this research is to examine how RCFs and micro-silica powder influence the mechanical behavior of concrete when subjected to both ambient and elevated temperature conditions. The materials utilized in this investigation comprised Type II Portland cement, natural sand, gravel, micro-silica, and recycled carpet fibers. The Type II cement, obtained from the Bojnurd Cement Factory, was chosen due to its moderate sulfate resistance and low heat of hydration, making it well-suited for structural applications exposed to temperature fluctuations42. A summary of its chemical and physical characteristics is presented in Tables 1 and 2. The fine aggregate (sand), procured from the Marzanabad factory, had a specific gravity of 1700 kg/m3 and a moisture content of 0.2%, with an angular particle shape that favored optimal bonding and packing within the concrete matrix. Its angular texture was advantageous for interlocking with the cement paste. The coarse aggregate (gravel), obtained from the Amol factory, had a specific weight of 1800 kg/m3 and a moisture content of 2%. Both aggregates were subjected to sieve analysis to confirm compliance with ASTM gradation standards. Their particle size distributions were within the acceptable ASTM limits, ensuring workability, compactness, and mechanical performance in the concrete mix43. The micro-silica powder utilized in this study was procured from the Azna Factory located in Lorestan Province. It possessed a specific gravity of 250 g/cm2 and consisted of approximately 96.4% silicon dioxide (SiO2). Owing to its high pozzolanic reactivity, the material was incorporated as a partial cement substitute at a replacement ratio of 10% by weight, aiming to increase concrete density, minimize porosity, and enhance long-term durability44. The RCFs were extracted from a 30-year-old machine-made PP carpet. The fibers were manually separated from the carpet pile, producing hydrophilic fibers with a density of 910 kg/m3, a length of 30 mm, and a diameter of 2 mm (Table 3). This fiber size was selected based on prior research indicating that medium-length synthetic fibers (20–40 mm) provide a balance between crack-bridging efficiency and ease of dispersion in the concrete mix. PP was specifically chosen due to its thermal stability, availability as a significant waste stream, and documented performance in improving toughness and reducing shrinkage in FRC. Mix design

Several trial mixes were prepared to determine the optimal composition that balances workability, strength, and durability. The final selected mix design provided an ideal compromise between flowability and mechanical performance, as shown in Table 4. The mix consisted of 900 kg of sand, 900 kg of gravel, 400 kg of cement, and 200 kg of water, corresponding to a water-to-cement (w/c) ratio of 0.533. Additionally, 1.2 kg of superplasticizer (polycarboxylate-based) was incorporated to improve the workability and cohesion of the concrete mix, preventing segregation and ensuring uniform fiber dispersion51. This optimized mix design was consistently used for all specimens tested under both ambient and elevated temperature conditions43,47.

The experimental program involved preparing five concrete mix types that incorporated recycled carpet fibers and micro-silica. Carpet fibers were added at three volume fractions (0.5%, 0.75%, and 1% by volume), while micro-silica powder was used as a 10% partial replacement for cement by weight. All mixtures were tested under three temperature conditions—ambient (25 °C), moderate (200 °C), and high (600 °C)—to evaluate their mechanical and thermal performance. Each specimen type was cast and cured under identical conditions to ensure comparability across tests. Experiments and testing methods

The mechanical behavior of the concrete samples was evaluated through a series of standardized tests conducted in accordance with ASTM and BS specifications. The testing program included compressive strength (CS), indirect tensile strength (Brazilian test), and flexural strength (FS) assessments, performed under ambient (25 °C) and elevated temperature conditions (200 °C and 600 °C) to examine thermal resistance and structural integrity. Before mechanical testing, granulometric (sieve) analyses of both sand and gravel were conducted to verify that their particle-size distributions met ASTM C33 specifications, ensuring proper aggregate grading and uniformity56. The gradation curves for sand and gravel are shown in Figure S.F.1 and Figure S.F.2, respectively. These experiments aimed to determine how the combined addition of recycled carpet fibers and 10% micro-silica powder influences key mechanical parameters—compressive, tensile, and flexural strength—under varying thermal conditions. This comprehensive testing approach highlights the mechanical enhancement and thermal durability of sustainable concrete composites incorporating ST-PP fiber reinforcement derived from waste materials.

FS test (modulus of rupture)

The FS evaluation was performed to assess the bending resistance of concrete specimens, a key property for structural components such as slabs and beams that are subjected to tensile stresses on their outer surfaces. Results and discussion

The mechanical performance of concrete incorporating recycled carpet fibers (RCFs) and micro-silica powder under varying temperature conditions is presented and discussed in this Sect68. All specimens were subjected to a series of standardized tests to determine their modulus of elasticity, compressive strength (CS), and tensile strength at room temperature (25 °C), as well as at elevated temperatures of 200 °C and 600 °C. The objective of these experiments was to evaluate the effects of incorporating RCFs at volume fractions of 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1%, in combination with 10% micro-silica as a partial cement replacement, on both the fresh and hardened properties of concrete. For structural applications—such as bridge decks, pavements, and other infrastructure components exposed to elevated thermal environments, understanding the behavior of concrete under heat is of critical importance. The experimental results are presented in the following subsections through detailed tables and figures, facilitating comparative analysis and interpretation of how variations in RCF and micro-silica content influence strength development, ductility, and thermal degradation. These findings provide a comprehensive understanding of the mechanical response and sustainability potential of recycled fiber–reinforced concretes under thermal exposure.

CS results

The CS test serves as a key method for assessing the axial load-bearing capacity of concrete, an essential property for structural elements including bridges and other load-carrying components. The initial testing phase was performed after seven days of water curing under ambient laboratory conditions (25 °C). As illustrated in Fig. 6, the specimens containing 10% micro-silica powder (Mi) exhibited a 4% increase in CS compared to the control mix (Ref) without micro-silica or fibers. The inclusion of RCFs further enhanced performance. The mix containing 0.5% RCFs and micro-silica (C-0.5) demonstrated the most significant improvement, achieving a CS of 36.5 MPa, representing a 21% increase relative to the micro-silica-only sample (31 MPa). However, as the fiber content increased, the gains in CS diminished. The C-0.75 and C-1 samples showed reductions to 32 MPa and 33 MPa, respectively, suggesting that higher fiber content may impair uniform dispersion and fiber–matrix bonding.

This likely leads to the formation of micro-voids and stress concentration zones, which in turn reduce the load-bearing capacity. These results demonstrate that the optimum fiber dosage for enhancing compressive performance is 0.5%, where fibers are well distributed and effectively bridge microcracks, improving ductility without compromising cohesion. To ensure data reliability, each compressive strength value represents the mean of three specimens, and the corresponding standard deviation is shown as error bars in Figs. 6 and 7. These error bars illustrate the variability among replicates and support the reproducibility of the experimental results.

Beyond this threshold, fiber agglomeration and weak interfacial adhesion negate the benefits of reinforcement, resulting in reduced strength. Overall, the 7-day results confirm that the combined use of micro-silica and recycled carpet fibers produces a synergistic improvement in compressive strength and fracture resistance, leading to a more gradual and ductile failure mode compared with plain concrete6,75. The increase in CS observed at 0.5% fiber incorporation is mainly due to the micro-bridging mechanism of the fibers, which hinders crack growth and slows the merging of microcracks. The presence of micro-silica further enhances the material’s microstructure by refining pore distribution and forming a denser ITZ between the cement paste and aggregates, thereby improving load transfer efficiency. However, when the fiber content was raised to 0.75–1%, fiber agglomeration likely produced localized voids and weak bonding zones, which offset the advantages typically associated with fiber reinforcement.

When analyzing the 28-day CS results at ambient (25 °C), 200 °C, and 600 °C (Fig. 7), it was observed that the micro-silica-modified sample (Mi) exhibited a 19% higher strength than the control mix without additives. The addition of 0.5% recycled carpet fibers (C-0.5) further enhanced performance, showing a 9% increase in CS compared to the micro-silica sample alone. However, higher fiber dosages (0.75% and 1%) produced no significant improvement, indicating that excessive fiber content does not proportionally enhance compressive behavior. At 200 °C, the micro-silica specimens retained approximately 18% more strength than the control concrete.

In contrast, CS decreased by 1%, 18%, and 29% for the 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1% fiber-reinforced samples, respectively. This strength reduction can be attributed to the loss of fracture toughness in carpet fibers, which, while not melting at 200 °C, lose elasticity and bonding capacity, thereby reducing their crack-bridging effectiveness. At 600 °C, the decline became more pronounced: CS dropped by 28%, 38%, and 45% for the 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1% fiber contents, respectively. This substantial degradation is primarily caused by the thermal decomposition, burning, and charring of carpet fibers, which generate voids and microcracks within the cement matrix, leading to loss of internal cohesion and increased porosity76,77.

As illustrated in Fig. 8, the fibers remained intact but structurally weakened at 200 °C, while Fig. 9 shows that at 600 °C, the fibers were pulverized and charred, leaving microvoids throughout the matrix. This transformation significantly reduces strength, as the loss of fiber integrity compromises load transfer and crack control capacity. Overall, the results indicate that the synergistic interaction between micro-silica and recycled carpet fibers is most effective at ambient temperature, remains moderately beneficial up to 200 °C, but deteriorates sharply beyond this threshold due to fiber decomposition and increased matrix porosity.

At elevated temperatures, the melting and degradation of polymeric carpet fibers result in loss of bonding and the formation of microvoids, which weaken the concrete matrix. Micro-silica, however, partially compensates for this loss through the formation of secondary C–S–H, which improve thermal stability up to approximately 200 °C. Above this, the C–S–H begins to dehydrate, which explains the steep decline in strength at 600 °C.

Tensile strength test results

The tensile strength test, conducted using the Brazilian (split-cylinder) method, revealed the behavior of concrete under tensile stress, an aspect in which conventional concrete typically performs poorly. The 7-day tensile strength results at ambient temperature (25 °C) are presented in Fig. 10. The incorporation of RCFs improved the concrete’s resistance to tensile cracking. The sample containing 0.5% fibers exhibited a 7% increase in tensile strength, while the mixes with 0.75% and 1% fiber content showed 2% and 11% increases, respectively, compared to the micro-silica reference mix. These enhancements suggest that the fibers serve as practical crack-bridging elements, capable of restraining crack propagation and delaying fracture initiation during tensile loading. The improvement is particularly notable at a 0.5% fiber content, where the fibers are evenly dispersed and well-bonded within the matrix, resulting in a balanced combination of strength enhancement and workability. However, at higher fiber dosages, fiber clustering and weak interfacial bonding may limit the efficiency of stress transfer, resulting in variability in the results. Each tensile strength value represents the mean of three tests, and the corresponding error bars (Figs. 10 and 11) denote ± 1 standard deviation, demonstrating the consistency and reliability of the data.

Overall, the findings confirm that incorporating a moderate proportion of carpet fibers (0.5–1%) enhances the tensile capacity of micro-silica–modified concrete, primarily by improving ductility and post-cracking behavior.

The 28-day tensile strength results at ambient (25 °C), 200 °C, and 600 °C (Fig. 11) further illustrate the influence of micro-silica and RCFs on concrete performance. At ambient temperature, specimens containing micro-silica exhibited an average 29% increase in tensile strength compared to the control mix, confirming the beneficial effect of densifying the cement matrix and improving fiber–matrix adhesion. As the temperature increased, however, a progressive decline in tensile strength was observed. At 200 °C, the tensile strength decreased by 18%, 31%, and 26% for the mixes containing 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1% fibers, respectively. This reduction is attributed to the softening of fibers and partial loss of bonding strength, which weakens their ability to arrest cracks. At 600 °C, the deterioration was more severe, with tensile strength losses of 46%, 59%, and 55%, respectively. This marked reduction is due to fiber degradation, surface charring, and the formation of microvoids, all of which increase porosity and disrupt stress transfer between fibers and the cement paste. The fibers primarily carry tensile stresses through crack-bridging mechanisms. As the temperature increases, thermal softening and oxidation reduce the fiber’s tensile capacity, and debonding at the fiber–matrix interface becomes dominant, leading to sharp losses in tensile strength.

Results of density and modulus of elasticity

The density measurements of concrete specimens tested at ambient (25 °C), 200 °C, and 600 °C are illustrated in Fig. 12. The results revealed that mixtures incorporating RCFs exhibited slightly reduced densities compared with the reference concrete. This decrease can be mainly linked to the lower specific gravity of the carpet fibers, which partially substituted natural aggregates within the mixture. Additionally, the presence of fibers led to the formation of small voids and interfacial discontinuities, further contributing to the overall density reduction. The modulus of elasticity, representing a key measure of stiffness and deformation resistance in concrete, was observed to be strongly affected by both CS and density. Generally, concretes with higher density and CS displayed higher elastic modulus values, indicating greater stiffness and load-bearing capacity. Nevertheless, the specimens incorporating carpet fibers exhibited a slight decrease in the modulus of elasticity compared with the micro-silica reference mixture, especially under elevated temperature conditions. This reduction is primarily attributed to the softening of fibers and the weakening of interfacial adhesion between the fibers and the cementitious matrix when exposed to heat. Despite this, at ambient temperature, the mix containing 0.5% fibers and 10% micro-silica retained a favorable balance between stiffness, ductility, and density, confirming it as the optimal composition for achieving structural stability without excessive rigidity.

The relationship between tensile strength and CS

The relationship between tensile strength and CS was analyzed by converting the cubic CS values into standard cylindrical CS values and comparing the results at ambient temperatures (25 °C), 200 °C, and 600 °C. At ambient temperature (Table 7), the cylindrical CS ranged from 30.8 MPa to 41.46 MPa, while the tensile strength varied between 2.9 MPa and 4.1 MPa. The tensile-to- CS ratio was approximately 9%, confirming concrete’s inherent behavior of being stronger in compression than in tension. At 200 °C (Table 8), the CS ranged from 28.74 MPa to 42.62 MPa, while the tensile strength declined to 2.6 MPa–3.8 MPa, reducing the ratio slightly to about 8%. This decrease reflects the softening of fibers and a reduction in bond efficiency, resulting in less effective tensile reinforcement at elevated temperatures. At 600 °C (Table 9), both strengths dropped sharply: CS decreased to 15.31–30.2 MPa, and tensile strength fell to 0.9–2.2 MPa, yielding a ratio of approximately 6%. The severe reduction results from fiber charring and degradation, which form microvoids and weaken the concrete matrix, disproportionately reducing tensile performance compared to CS. These findings confirm that temperature elevation significantly affects tensile properties, while CS remains relatively more stable. The results reinforce that RCFs enhance ductility and crack resistance under normal conditions but lose effectiveness when thermal degradation occurs beyond 200 °C. The observed 21% improvement in CS, combined with the recycling of approximately 1820 kg of carpet fibers per typical building project, provides both mechanical and environmental validation of the ductile and sustainable performance claims. The results show that while both strengths decrease with rising temperatures, tensile strength experiences a more pronounced reduction due to fiber degradation, particularly at 600 °C.

Environmental evaluation of carpet fibers

Based on the experimental findings, the optimal dosage of recycled carpet fiber (RCFs) for enhancing the mechanical properties of concrete without compromising its structural integrity was determined to be 0.5% by volume. At this concentration, the characteristic strength of the concrete remained within the acceptable range for structural applications, confirming its suitability for practical construction use.

In addition to improving performance, the incorporation of 0.5% carpet fibers offers significant environmental and material benefits by diverting waste from landfills and reducing the demand for virgin materials. To quantify this potential, a hypothetical six-story building with a total floor area of 1000 m2 and a concrete requirement of approximately 400 m3 was analyzed. Using a 0.5% fiber dosage would allow the integration of about 1820 kg of waste carpet fiber into the structure. This represents a substantial reduction in landfill waste and supports the reuse of non-biodegradable polypropylene (PP) fibers that would otherwise contribute to environmental pollution.

The environmental advantages extend beyond waste diversion. By incorporating RCFs into concrete, the construction industry contributes to a circular material economy, reducing resource consumption and the carbon footprint associated with both carpet disposal and cement production. This approach aligns with global sustainability goals promoting low-impact, resource-efficient construction practices. From a mechanical perspective, the inclusion of 0.5% carpet fibers and 10% micro-silica improved the tensile strength, crack resistance, and ductility of the concrete, while maintaining satisfactory compressive strength (CS), as demonstrated in Tables 6, 7 and 8.

Therefore, incorporating 0.5% recycled carpet fibers into concrete mixtures not only improves the material’s mechanical performance but also minimizes landfill disposal, thereby promoting a more environmentally sustainable construction practice. This aligns with the growing global emphasis on sustainable building practices, where materials that would otherwise be discarded are repurposed for structural use, thereby reducing environmental impact and conserving natural resources. Analyzing the results of the study

This research investigated the mechanical behavior of concrete mixtures containing RCFs and 10% MS when subjected to ambient temperature (25 °C) as well as elevated thermal conditions of 200 °C and 600 °C. The investigation focused on CS, tensile strength, modulus of elasticity, and density to determine how varying RCF contents (0.5%, 0.75%, and 1%) influence both the strength and thermal resistance of the composite. At ambient temperature, the CS of the reference mix (Ref) without micro-silica or fibers was 30 MPa. Incorporating 10% MS increased this value to 31 MPa, while the addition of 0.5% RCF together with MS (C-0.5) produced the highest improvement, reaching 36.5 MPa—a 21% increase compared with the micro-silica-only sample. However, further increases in fiber content (0.75% and 1%) reduced strength to 32 MPa and 33 MPa, respectively, suggesting that excessive fibers cause poor dispersion and weak bonding within the matrix.

After 28 days, the MS-modified mix showed a 19% higher CS than the control and adding 0.5% RCF provided an additional 9% improvement. At elevated temperatures, strength decreased significantly: at 200 °C, CS declined by 1%, 18%, and 29% for RCF contents of 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1%, respectively; and at 600 °C, reductions of 28%, 38%, and 45% were recorded. The loss of strength at elevated temperatures can be attributed to the thermal degradation and charring of polymeric fibers, which generate microvoids and weaken the matrix. Meanwhile, micro-silica partially mitigated this deterioration through the formation of secondary calcium–silicate–hydrate (C–S–H), improving thermal stability up to about 200 °C before dehydration and microcracking caused further weakening at 600 °C.

The tensile strength followed a similar trend. Under ambient conditions, tensile strength increased by 7%, 2%, and 11% for mixes with 0.5%, 0.75%, and 1% RCFs, respectively, compared to the MS-only mix. This improvement confirms that RCFs function as effective crack-bridging elements, enhancing ductility and delaying fracture initiation. However, as temperature rose, tensile strength declined markedly: at 200 °C, decreases of 18%, 31%, and 26% were recorded, while at 600 °C, reductions of 46%, 59%, and 55% occurred for the same fiber contents. These reductions are due to fiber softening, oxidation, and decomposition, which diminish bonding strength and increase porosity, leading to a loss of tensile integrity.

The inclusion of RCFs slightly reduced concrete density because of the fibers’ lower specific gravity compared to natural aggregates. Although the reduction was minor, it indicates partial replacement of aggregates by lighter fibers. The modulus of elasticity, representing stiffness, showed that micro-silica increased the modulus by 6–7% across all temperatures, while 0.5% RCF improved stiffness by 4% at 25 °C and 1% at 200 °C. At 600 °C, however, stiffness decreased by 16% for 0.5% fibers and up to 31% for 1% fibers, reflecting structural degradation due to microcracking and breakdown of hydration products.

The relationship between tensile and compressive strength remained consistent with established behavior. At 25 °C, tensile strength was about 9% of CS; at 200 °C, it decreased to 8%, and at 600 °C, to 6%. This progressive decline highlights that tensile properties are more sensitive to thermal degradation than compressive ones since fiber charring and void formation primarily weaken tensile load transfer.

From an environmental standpoint, using 0.5% RCFs offers an optimal balance between mechanical performance and sustainability. In a typical six-story building with a 1000 m2 footprint requiring 400 m3 of concrete, approximately 1820 kg of waste carpet fibers could be repurposed, diverting them from landfills and reducing demand for virgin polypropylene (PP). This approach supports circular construction practices by minimizing waste, conserving raw materials, and lowering the environmental footprint of both carpet disposal and cement production.

The results align with previous studies: Wang et al. reported that 1% RCF increased CS by 12.9–17.5% at 28 days, Zareei et al.78 observed a 5% rise in flexural strength (FS) using 1% PP fibers, and Mohammadhosseini et al. found that increasing carpet waste fibers from 0.25 to 1.25% improved tensile but slightly reduced compressive strength. These findings collectively emphasize the importance of optimizing fiber dosage to balance ductility and strength.

Overall, the combined use of RCFs and micro-silica enhances the mechanical, thermal, and environmental performance of concrete, making it suitable for pavements, slabs, shotcrete, and tunnel linings. Further validation is recommended for critical load-bearing structures, particularly under long-term thermal and cyclic loading conditions.

Comparison with previous studies and broader structural implications

The addition of FRCs to concrete represents a significant step toward developing more environmentally friendly materials79,80. Plastic packaging waste, a major contributor to municipal solid waste, is non-biodegradable and poses serious environmental concerns when disposed of in landfills81,82. To mitigate this issue, numerous studies have focused on transforming plastic waste into fibers for reinforcing concrete structures83,84. Research findings indicate that the inclusion of 1–1.5% waste plastic fibers can lead to an improvement of up to 70% in the FS of concrete; however, excessive fiber content may result in strength reduction84. Given the rising global demand for concrete and the increasing emphasis on sustainable recycling, the incorporation of waste-derived RFs into Cement-Based Composites (CBCs) has attracted growing scientific interest85. Specifically, the application of Waste Recycled Steel Fibers (WRSF) has been shown to enhance flexural, tensile, and post-cracking performance, while simultaneously improving residual strength and crack resistance86,87. Likewise, the use of waste tire crumb rubber and recycled aggregates supports the long-term sustainability of concrete materials88,89. Research on WRSF-reinforced concrete further indicates improved impact resistance, reduced shrinkage, and enhanced durability compared to plain concrete15,90. While some studies have reported that incorporating WRCFs slightly decreases CS, others have observed improvements in tensile and flexural performance, especially at moderate fiber dosages91,92. Overall, the use of RFs whether steel, plastic, or carpet offers an effective and sustainable approach to improving the mechanical properties and environmental performance of concrete93,94. The results of this study are in line with those of other studies that investigated the effect of NS and RCFs on the performance of concrete. Due to the impermeable fibers’ capacity to hinder ion transport, prior research found that the permeability of FRC decreased as the RCF concentration increased up to 0.75 vol %. On the other hand, mechanical strength and durability were negatively impacted when the RCF concentration exceeded 1 vol % and numerous ITZs developed within the matrix95. Similarly, the mechanical performance was found to be excellent at 0.25 vol % RCF, supporting the results of this study, which indicate that the optimal fiber dosage lies between 0.5% and 0.75%, beyond which strength declines due to poor dispersion. Furthermore, prior work has shown that adding 2 wt %–3 wt % NS significantly enhances CS. In contrast, higher dosages above 3 wt % can lead to agglomeration and a reduction in strength43, which is consistent with the balanced micro-silica content (10%) used in this research. Additionally, Tian Su et al.96 developed a nano–silica–modified Recycled Carpet Fiber-Reinforced Concrete (RCFRC) to enhance frost resistance and sustainability, demonstrating that incorporating 2 wt % NS and 0.12 vol % RCF yielded optimal mechanical strength and freeze–thaw durability. Their study highlighted the material’s potential to extend service life, lower carbon emissions, and reduce production costs, positioning RCFRC as a sustainable and high-performance construction material. In contrast, the present study focused on thermal resistance rather than frost durability. Still, it reached similar conclusions regarding the synergistic effect of combining RCFs and silica-based additives in improving mechanical performance and sustainability. Furthermore, past studies showed that RCFRC maintained 80%–85% structural integrity even after 50 freeze–thaw cycles, with minimal surface cracks or spalling97. This behavior supports the hypothesis that RCF–silica composites can enhance resilience under various environmental stressors whether thermal or cyclic making them promising for structural applications in cold or high-temperature regions, such as bridge decks, pavements, and fire-exposed elements. Compared with prior works that studied waste fibers or micro-silica individually, the results of this study show a distinct contribution. Previous studies have often reported strength improvements at ambient conditions; however, limited research has addressed the combined effect of recycled carpet fibers and micro-silica under thermal exposure. The present findings demonstrate that the optimal 0.5% carpet fiber content, when used with micro-silica, not only enhances compressive and tensile strength at room temperature but also provides relatively sustained performance at elevated temperatures (200 °C and 600 °C). This combined behavior extends current knowledge and highlights the practical value of using industrial and domestic waste materials together for durable and sustainable concrete applications. By demonstrating the effective use of 0.5% recycled carpet fibers combined with 10% micro-silica powder, this study makes a significant contribution both technically and environmentally, improving tensile and CS while promoting sustainable construction practices through the recycling of non-biodegradable carpet waste and reducing the demand for virgin materials.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that combining 0.5% recycled carpet fibers (RCFs) with 10% micro-silica (MS) significantly enhances the mechanical performance and sustainability of concrete. The optimal fiber dosage of 0.5% improved compressive strength (CS) by approximately 21% and tensile strength by 7% under ambient conditions (25 °C). In contrast, higher fiber contents (0.75–1%) led to reduced strength, primarily due to fiber agglomeration and weak interfacial bonding between the fibers and cement matrix.

Although both compressive and tensile strengths declined at 200 °C and 600 °C, the RCF–micro-silica concrete retained a greater proportion of its load-bearing capacity compared with conventional concrete, confirming its potential for thermally stressed applications. Mechanistically, micro-silica refines pore structure, densifies the interfacial transition zone (ITZ), and enhances fiber–matrix adhesion, while the carpet fibers act as micro-bridges that delay crack initiation and propagation. This interaction promotes a more ductile fracture response, particularly at moderate temperatures, thereby improving toughness and energy absorption.

From an environmental perspective, incorporating 1820 kg of recycled carpet waste into the concrete used for a six-story building (approximately 400 m3) represents a substantial reduction in landfill disposal and consumption of virgin polypropylene (PP). This approach directly links mechanical optimization with measurable sustainability impact, contributing to resource efficiency and a reduction in lifecycle carbon emissions. The dual enhancement of mechanical resilience and environmental sustainability positions RCF–micro-silica concrete as a viable and eco-efficient alternative for next-generation infrastructure.

The findings also have direct implications for bridge and structural engineering, where materials are frequently exposed to variable temperatures, high loads, and environmental stress. The 0.5% RCF–micro-silica mix demonstrated improved compressive and tensile performance at both ambient and moderate temperatures, indicating suitability for bridge decks, girders, and approach slabs, where crack control, fatigue life, and thermal stability are critical to long-term durability. Moreover, the enhanced ductility and reduced microcracking observed in this mix can extend service life and improve fatigue resistance of reinforced concrete bridge components. From a sustainability perspective, using recycled carpet fibers contributes to greener bridge construction, reducing lifecycle carbon emissions and material waste without compromising structural reliability.

Practical implications

The findings of this study have practical implications for field-scale applications where enhanced thermal and mechanical performance is required. The improved compressive and tensile strengths, along with the retained structural integrity at elevated temperatures, suggest that the developed mix designs could be effectively utilized in pavement substructures, tunnel linings, and fire-resistant concrete elements. In particular, the superior performance of the C-0.5 mix under high-temperature exposure indicates its suitability for infrastructure subjected to thermal gradients or fire hazards, such as underground structures, industrial flooring, or structural components in high-risk zones. These results highlight the potential for transitioning the proposed formulations from laboratory evaluation to practical, large-scale construction applications, contributing to the development of safer and more durable civil infrastructure.
