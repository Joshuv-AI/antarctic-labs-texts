The impact of alexithymia, anxiety, social pressure, and academic burnout on depression in Chinese university students: an analysis based on SEM
Authors: Mingyang Sun, Ming Piao, Zhaona Jia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWith societal development and increasing talent demands, college students face escalating pressures primarily stemming from academic burdens, uncertainties about the future, and complex social environments. These pressures have profound effects on mental health, particularly negatively impacting emotions and behavior. In this context, depressive symptoms are becoming increasingly prevalent among college students. Given the potential links between emotional disorders, anxiety, social pressure, and academic burnout, this study aims to explore how these factors collectively influence depression in college students. MethodsThis study utilized a questionnaire approach, resulting in 599 valid responses. The collected data were analyzed using Smart-PLS for SEM to investigate the relationships among the variables.ResultsThe SEM analysis indicated that the comprehensive model explained 83.2% of the variance in depression. Alexithymia significantly positively predicted anxiety and social pressure. Academic burnout significantly positively influenced social pressure, anxiety, and depression. Anxiety played a partial mediating role in the impact of alexithymia and academic burnout on depression. Social pressure and alexithymia did not have a significant positive impact on depression. In addition, there is a significant gender difference in the impact of academic burnout on depression, with the path coefficient for males being significantly higher than that for females.ConclusionThis study validated the complex relationships among alexithymia, anxiety, social pressure, academic burnout, and depression. As an emotional expression disorder, alexithymia not only directly impacts anxiety levels in college students but also indirectly affects depression through anxiety. Furthermore, the positive influence of academic burnout on anxiety and depression indicates that excessive academic burdens are significant sources of mental health issues for college students. This research offers new insights into understanding college students’ mental health issues, aiming to provide more comprehensive intervention strategies for their psychological well-being.

Introduction

Depression (DEP) is a prevalent mental disorder marked by a lasting low mood or diminished interest in activities, significantly affecting individuals’ daily lives [1]. College students are at a critical transitional stage from adolescence to adulthood, facing multiple challenges such as academic pressure, career planning, social adaptation, and changes in living environments. During this stage, students often experience psychological stress as they navigate the demands of academic workloads, societal expectations, and self-development. A 2022 mental health assessment of nearly 80,000 Chinese college students revealed a risk detection rate for DEP of approximately 21.48% [2], Moreover, the incidence of DEP among the 18–24 age group in China reached 35.32%, indicating a trend of increasing youth DEP [3]. DEP in college students manifest in various forms, including low mood, reduced interest in daily activities, low energy levels, and trouble concentrating, diminished self-worth, and disruptions in appetite or sleep. These symptoms can adversely affect students’ academic performance, interpersonal relationships, and future development [4–6]. The occurrence of DEP among college students is closely related to various protective and risk factors. Protective factors, such as perceived control, social support, and mindfulness, can help individuals reduce the negative emotional impact on their mental health [7–10]. In contrast, risk factors, such as social pressure (SOPR), alexithymia (ALE), and academic burnout (ACBU), may lead to more negative effects on students’ psychological and emotional well-being, thus increasing the risk of DEP [3, 11, 12]. Existing research has identified common triggers for DEP among college students, including insufficient social support, ACBU, SOPR, and anxiety (ANX) [13]. If these issues are not addressed promptly and effectively, DEP may worsen, impacting long-term mental health and quality of life [14–16]. Therefore, understanding the multiple causes of DEP in college students and taking timely measures can provide more effective psychological support and interventions, thereby reducing the incidence of DEP.

Alexithymia and depression

ALE is a psychological trait characterized by an individual’s difficulty in recognizing, understanding, and expressing their own or others’ emotions, reflecting a deficiency in emotional processing. DEP is a common emotional disorder [17]. College students are at a critical developmental stage for emotional and psychological growth, making them particularly susceptible to mental health issues such as ALE and DEP [18]. Given the unique psychological characteristics during this period, exploring the relationship between ALE and DEP among college students is of significant importance. Existing research indicates a positive influence between ALE and DEP [19]. Aleisa, et al. [20] found that 48% of patients with DEP exhibited ALE, while 40% might have it, and only 12% showed no signs of ALE. Tang, et al. [21] also demonstrated that ALE significantly increases the risk of DEP in adolescents. Furthermore, ALE is correlated with DEP across different genders and cultural backgrounds among college students [22]. ALE may increase the risk of DEP through various mechanisms. Firstly, individuals with ALE struggle to identify and express their emotions, making it difficult for them to cope effectively with emotional stimuli or stressors. This inability leads to emotional suppression, which can accumulate and gradually manifest as persistent ANX and unease, potentially culminating in DEP [23]. Secondly, ALE individuals often lack effective emotional regulation strategies, making them more vulnerable to feelings of helplessness and despair when faced with life’s challenges. From a physiological perspective, this is associated with dysfunction in brain regions involved in emotional processing, such as the prefrontal cortex and amygdala, which may further contribute to the emergence of DEP [24]. Additionally, factors like ANX and SOPR may serve as mediators that exacerbate the relationship between ALE and DEP [25, 26]. Because individuals with ALE often find it challenging to express their emotions, they may struggle to gain effective social support and emotional understanding, which heightens feelings of loneliness and low self-worth. The accumulation of these negative emotions further increases the risk of DEP [27].

Academic burnout and depression

ACBU is a psychological state triggered by prolonged academic stress and burden, primarily characterized by emotional exhaustion, decreased motivation for learning, and negative attitudes toward academic pursuits [28]. Among college students, ACBU is a widespread issue that not only diminishes interest and motivation for learning but also fosters feelings of helplessness and frustration, which can easily evolve into DEP [1]. Therefore, understanding the relationship between ACBU and mental health issues is crucial. Existing research indicates a close correlation between ACBU and DEP, suggesting that higher levels of burnout are associated with more severe DEP [14]. Moreover, ACBU is not only an independent phenomenon but may also interact with other psychological issues, such as ANX, further exacerbating mental health problems. For instance, Wang, et al. [29] validated the multidimensional structure of burnout in a study on ACBU and DEP among college students, emphasizing that burnout is a critical factor affecting DEP. The significant correlation between ACBU and DEP has been confirmed in several studies [30–32]. Fernández-Castillo [33] investigated ANX and ACBU among college students during exams, finding a specific high correlation between ANX levels and burnout, and underscoring the need to address ANX issues to reduce burnout levels. ACBU can influence the development of DEP through multiple mechanisms. Firstly, prolonged academic stress and ineffective coping strategies can lead students to feel helpless and frustrated, resulting in emotional exhaustion [34]. Secondly, ACBU can cause students to lose interest and motivation for learning, leading to the accumulation of negative emotions that may eventually manifest as DEP [35]. From a psychological perspective, ACBU reduces individuals’ positive emotions and psychological energy, resulting in diminished interest in academic and life activities, alongside an increase in negative emotional experiences such as feelings of helplessness and emotional exhaustion-all of which may raise the risk of DEP [14]. Additionally, the relationship between ACBU and DEP may be influenced by other factors, such as coping strategies and the learning environment.

The potential mediating role of social pressure

SOPR refers to the stress and ANX individuals experience in social situations, particularly prevalent among college students due to the social demands and interpersonal challenges of university life [36]. SOPR can lead individuals to withdraw from and avoid social activities, exacerbating feelings of loneliness, while also undermining their self-evaluation. As SOPR accumulates, individuals may enter a vicious cycle of declining self-worth, which may increase the risk of DEP [37]. Numerous studies indicate a close association between SOPR and the onset and exacerbation of mental health issues, particularly DEP [37, 38].

SOPR may complicate depressive issues in individuals with ALE. Those with ALE may feel particularly uncomfortable in social environments, struggling to establish deep emotional connections with others, which can result in heightened SOPR and an increased risk of DEP [39]. College students with ALE often face challenges in social interactions due to their difficulties in understanding or expressing their emotions and responding to others’ feelings, leading to increased social isolation and interpersonal conflicts, thereby intensifying their SOPR [40]. Additionally, the accumulation of SOPR may further lead to the emergence of mental health problems, especially the onset of DEP. Jansen, et al. [41] examined the psychological resilience of college students under stress, finding that those in prolonged stress conditions were more susceptible to mental health impacts, including DEP. Moreover, individuals with ALE face greater challenges and pressures in social interactions due to their difficulties in emotional recognition and expression, which can result in higher levels of isolation and helplessness, further exacerbating DEP [40].

Furthermore, SOPR plays a mediating role in the impact of ACBU on DEP. ACBU is often accompanied by emotional exhaustion, declining academic performance, and a loss of interest in studies, conditions that can lead students to experience heightened SOPR and an increased risk of DEP. Specifically, when students feel overwhelmed by academic burdens, they may withdraw from social activities, resulting in isolation and a lack of social engagement, which further intensifies their feelings of burnout [42]. In this context, SOPR not only exacerbates students’ ANX but may also trigger doubts about their self-worth. As SOPR escalates, ACBU may transition into more severe DEP [43]. For instance, Liang, et al. [44] found that students experiencing ACBU were more prone to social isolation, which in turn contributed to increased DEP. Students with ACBU not only develop a disdain for their studies but also encounter numerous issues arising from this, such as SOPR. For individuals who are chronically subjected to high levels of SOPR, the combination of sustained ACBU and negative emotions can lead to a greater likelihood of experiencing DEP [45].

The potential mediating role of anxiety

ANX is a common psychological issue prevalent among college students [1]. Research by Türk, et al. [46] indicates that ANX levels are notably high in young populations. ANX manifests as uncertainty about the future, excessive tension, and worry, often accompanied by feelings of fear and unease, potentially leading to a variety of emotional, cognitive, physical, and behavioral signs [47]. When prolonged ANX goes unaddressed, individuals may gradually lose their ability and confidence to cope with stress, eventually succumbing to DEP. Studies have shown a close correlation between ANX and DEP, making ANX a significant predictor of DEP [48].

ANX plays a mediating role in the relationship between ALE and DEP. Specifically, ALE indirectly leads to DEP by increasing ANX levels, indicating that ALE first triggers ANX, which may subsequently provoke or intensify DEP [49, 50]. For example, Liu, et al. [48] found that over half of individuals with severe ALE exhibited ANX, with the majority experiencing emotional suppression due to their ANX, which in turn exacerbated their DEP. Research suggests that individuals with ALE often struggle to identify and articulate their feelings in social contexts, making them more prone to ANX. This ANX frequently accompanies worries, fears, and negative thoughts, leading individuals to develop pessimistic expectations about the future, thereby increasing the risk of DEP [21]. Studies have indicated that the anxious state of individuals with ALE not only worsens their existing DEP but may also precipitate new episodes of DEP [51].

Moreover, ACBU can lead to feelings of frustration and helplessness, triggering ANX, which further heightens the risk of DEP. ALE diminishes an individual’s ability to express and process emotions, increasing ANX among students and subsequently leading to DEP [51]. For instance, Liu, et al. [14] highlighted that ACBU leads to decreased academic performance, increased absenteeism, and emotional exhaustion, all of which can generate ANX in a competitive environment and contribute to mental health issues, thus increasing the risk of DEP over time. Related research shows that ANX activates brain regions involved in emotional processing, further exacerbating the impacts of ALE and ACBU on DEP [45]. Consequently, ANX the influence of ACBU on DEP. The frustration and helplessness stemming from ACBU often lead to increased ANX, which intensifies feelings of emotional exhaustion and deepens DEP [38].

In summary, this study systematically explores the mediating roles of SOPR and ANX in the relationships among ALE, ACBU, and DEP, providing new insights into the existing literature. While some research has investigated ANX as a mediating factor between academic stress and mental health outcomes [52] or examined how social support mitigates the negative impact of ACBU on mental health [53]. However, the mechanisms by which ALE and ACBU directly influence DEP, as well as the factors through which they exert indirect effects, remain unclear in the existing literature. Therefore, this study, based on social cognitive theory, integrates variables such as ALE, ACBU, SOPR, anxiety, and DEP. Social cognitive theory emphasizes the interaction between the individual, environment, and behavior, suggesting that an individual’s psychological and behavioral outcomes are shaped by the combined effects of social environmental factors (such as SOPR) and cognitive factors (such as ALE and ACBU). Using this framework, the study systematically analyzes the relationships between these variables, exploring the direct impact of ALE and ACBU on DEP, while revealing the mediating roles of SOPR and anxiety in the relationship between ALE, ACBU, and DEP. This integrative perspective not only extends the application of social cognitive theory within the field of mental health but also enriches research on the direct and indirect effects of ALE and ACBU on DEP. Additionally, it provides more targeted and effective strategies for the design of interventions addressing mental health issues. Consequently, we have constructed the research hypothesis model shown in Fig. 1 and validated the following hypotheses: (1) ALE has a significant positive effect on DEP; (2) ACBU has a significant positive effect on DEP; (3) SOPR mediates the effects of ALE and ACBU on DEP; (4) ANX mediates the effects of ALE and ACBU on DEP. Methods

Sample source and data collection

This study was conducted from June to August 2024, aimed at exploring emotional and psychological issues during the college phase. We employed a random sampling method for distributing and collecting questionnaires. Initially, the research team coordinated with teachers from various classes to clarify the purpose and significance of the questionnaire, ensuring that teachers could accurately convey this information. Subsequently, teachers introduced the background and importance of the questionnaire to students during class to enhance participation willingness. Teachers chose students at random to take part in the survey, utilizing methods such as drawing lots or random number generators to ensure the randomness and representativeness of the sample. Once the students were selected, teachers shared the questionnaire link through class group chats and reminded students of the voluntary nature of participation. Participants were informed that they could choose to participate at any time, and the confidentiality of their responses would be strictly maintained. All participants voluntarily engaged after understanding the study’s objectives, and measures were taken to ensure the confidentiality of personal information. Throughout the data collection phase, invalid responses were excluded, such as those that were incorrectly answered or filled out multiple times, resulting in 599 valid questionnaires. In the sample, there were 278 males (46.4%) and 321 females (53.6%); the majority were aged 18–20 years (83.8%); second-year students comprised 74.5% (446 individuals), while third-year students accounted for 21.2% (127 individuals); 71.8% were not only children; most parents had a secondary school education (74.13%); and the monthly family income was primarily between 5000 and 10,000 RMB (55.26%). Measurement tools

This study employed two parts in the questionnaire: demographic information and constructs relevant to the research. Each construct was measured using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The Toronto Alexithymia Scale-20 (TAS-20) by Leising, et al. [54] was used to assess ALE among college students (e.g., “I often confuse my feelings”). The scale is divided into three dimensions: difficulty in emotion recognition (7 items), difficulty in emotion expression (5 items), and externalized thinking (8 items), with a total of 20 items. Among these, 5 items are reverse-scored. The final score is obtained by summing the individual scores, with higher scores indicating a greater severity of ALE. The Cronbach’s α of 0.963, indicating good reliability. The Brief Symptom Inventory-18 (BSI-18) by Geng, et al. [55] measured ANX and DEP. The scale is divided into three aspects: anxiety, DEP, and somatization, with a total of 18 items. It assesses the psychological distress experienced by the participants over the past 7 days (e.g., “I often feel sad or depressed”). The Cronbach’s α of 0.954. The scale demonstrates good reliability. The Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) adapted by Zhang, et al. [56] assessed ACBU. The scale consists of three dimensions: emotional exhaustion (8 items), academic indifference (6 items), and reduced personal accomplishment (6 items), with a total of 20 items(e.g., “I feel that studying drains a lot of my emotional resources”). Higher scores indicate more severe ACBU among students. With a Cronbach’s α of 0.954. The scale demonstrates good reliability. A scale on SOPR based on Cheng, et al. [38] and Wang, et al. [57], The scale contains 6 items (e.g., “I feel anxious when speaking in front of a group”), with the 4th item being a reverse-scored question. Higher scores indicate greater SOPR. With a Cronbach’s α of 0.925. The scale demonstrates good reliability.

The scales were initially translated by two independent researchers: a content expert fluent in both Chinese and English and a linguist proficient in Chinese. A back-translation was performed, and discrepancies were resolved to form a unified version, which was pilot-tested with 20 Chinese college students. Inconsistencies with the original English version were corrected to finalize the Chinese version. All the scales used in this study have been validated in relevant Chinese research and demonstrate good reliability and validity in general college student samples.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SMART-PLS 4.0, with PLS-SEM focusing on predictive capabilities, crucial for understanding relationships between different psychological states in the college student population [58, 59]. This method identified significant predictors of students’ psychological states, aiding in designing effective interventions. Thus, PLS-SEM was utilized to analyze the relationships among ALE, ANX, SOPR, ACBU, and DEP.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics were performed using SPSS on the data collected from 599 college students, revealing 10 students with significant DEP and 151 with mild to moderate symptoms. Gender differences showed a higher prevalence of DEP in males (P < 0.05), and students with less educated parents exhibited higher ANX (P < 0.05). No statistically significant differences in DEP were found regarding age, grade, family income, or only-child status (P > 0.05).

SEM analysis

In this study, the PLS-SEM method was employed to test both the measurement and structural models. PLS-SEM has several advantages: it is a prediction-oriented data analysis tool [60], performing well with small samples or non-normal distributions [60], and accommodating complex models [61]. Given the study’s objective to analyze the relationships among ALE, ANX, SOPR, ACBU, and DEP, a total of 599 valid questionnaires were collected, with the research model consisting of 5 constructs and 45 items, making it appropriate for this study due to its complexity.

Common method bias

Common method bias (CMB) was assessed using two methods. Harman’s single factor test indicated no single factor accounted for most variance [62]. The first factor accounted for approximately 21.3% of the variance, which did not exceed the 50% threshold. Second, a marker variable method was employed, adding a theoretically unrelated marker variable to the research model to test for CMB [63]. The maximum estimated shared variance with other factors was 0.0268 (2.68%), indicating a very low proportion. Thus, based on the results of these two methods, it was inferred that significant common method bias did not exist.

Measurement model

Following Leguina [60], the measurement model was evaluated for the reliability and validity of the scales. Reliability indicators included item factor loadings and the composite reliability of the scales. According to standards, item factor loadings should exceed 0.708 [60]. However, if the average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) exceeded the threshold, items could be retained even if their factor loadings were between 0.4 and 0.7 [60]. Thus, variables with loadings below 0.708 were excluded, retaining only the qualified variables (see Table 2). Internal consistency reliability was assessed using composite reliability, with all values required to be greater than 0.708 [60]. Table 2 shows that both the item loadings and composite reliability of the scales demonstrated high reliability. Subsequently, validity was assessed through convergent and discriminant validity. Convergent validity was evaluated using AVE, which must be greater than 0.5 [60]. Discriminant validity was assessed using both the heterotrait-monotrait ratio (HTMT) and the traditional Fornell-Larcker criterion [60]. All HTMT values (see Table 3) were below the critical threshold of 0.85 [60], and the correlation coefficients between constructs were lower than the square root of the AVE (see Table 4), thus satisfying the Fornell-Larcker criterion [60]. Therefore, both HTMT and Fornell-Larcker methods indicated that all constructs exhibited high discriminant validity.

Mediation analysis

To evaluate the mediating roles of ANX and SOPR between ALE, ACBU, and DEP, this study employed bootstrapping [65], utilizing 5000 subsamples to calculate indirect effects, direct effects, and their confidence intervals, thereby assessing the significance and type of the mediating effects. According to bootstrapping standards, the significance of indirect effects is determined by calculating confidence intervals. If the confidence interval (e.g., 95% confidence interval) does not include zero, the indirect effect is considered significant. The results indicate that in the path ALE → ANX → DEP, the indirect effect is 0.265, with a 95% confidence interval of [0.178, 0.356], and the direct effect is 0.226, indicating that ANX plays a partial mediating role in this pathway. Similarly, in the path ACBU → ANX → DEP, the indirect effect is 0.449, with a 95% confidence interval of [0.356, 0.552], and the direct effect is 0.399, suggesting that ANX also has a partial mediating role in this pathway [66]. In contrast, for the paths ALE → SOPR → DEP and ACBU → SOPR → DEP, both the indirect and direct effects were not significant, indicating no mediation effect in these pathways. Discussion

This study aims to explore how ALE, ANX, SOPR, and ACBU individually and collectively influence DEP among university students. Utilizing SEM, the research reveals the complex relationships among these variables. Additionally, the study examines the mediating roles of SOPR and ANX in the effects of ALE and ACBU on DEP. It was hypothesized that both ALE and ACBU have direct significant impacts on DEP, with SOPR serving as a mediator between ALE and DEP, as well as between ACBU and DEP. A multigroup analysis was conducted to examine gender differences. The results indicated that the effects of ALE, SOPR, and ANX in DEP did not differ significantly between genders. However, the gender difference in the path “ACBU → DEP” was significant (difference = 0.079, t = 2.541, p = 0.011), with the path coefficient for males being higher than for females. This suggests that the impact of ACBU on DEP is stronger in males, highlighting the need for further attention to the mental health of males in high academic pressure situations. The detailed discussion of the research findings related to the initial research questions and hypotheses is as follows.

ACBU significantly positively impacts DEP. This result aligns with the research conducted by Hwang and Kim [70], which investigated the negative effects of ACBU among nursing students, revealing that burnout explained 33% of the variance in ANX and DEP among students with clinical experience, while the effect was even more pronounced (44%) among those without clinical experience. University students often face prolonged academic pressure and frustration, leading to increased feelings of sadness and ANX. Many students lack effective coping mechanisms or psychological support when dealing with ACBU, resulting in their emotional and psychological needs being overlooked. As a result, they struggle to alleviate their burnout through emotional expression or by seeking social support. If ACBU remains unaddressed over time, the psychological pressure may intensify, ultimately increasing the risk of DEP [34, 70, 71]. The significant influence of ACBU on DEP among university students likely stems from long-term academic stress and frustration, which undermine students’ psychological resilience and heighten the risk of negative emotions. Furthermore, many students lack effective coping strategies, such as time management and emotional regulation skills, making them more susceptible to ANX and DEP, especially those with limited practical experience [14]. Therefore, universities should provide more psychological support and coping strategies, such as counseling, support groups, and stress management training, to help students manage academic pressure and enhance their psychological resilience. Faculty and counselors should also pay attention to students’ psychological states and offer timely assistance [72]. Future research should further validate the effectiveness of these strategies and explore how to personalize support for different student populations.

ALE did not show a significant positive effect on DEP. This finding is inconsistent with the study by Preece, et al. [51], which indicated a significant correlation between ALE and DEP. Their research analyzed the relationship between ALE (measured by PAQ scores) and DEP, ANX, and Stress (measured by DASS-21 scores), finding that individuals with high levels of psychiatric symptoms (i.e., DEP, ANX, and Stress) tend to exhibit more pronounced difficulties across various dimensions of ALE. Potential reasons for the discrepancies in findings may include individual-level factors, where individuals’ perceptions of their emotional expression abilities may differ from the actual severity of their ALE. Some individuals may not perceive their difficulties in emotional expression as significant problems, thereby affecting the potential association between ALE and DEP [73]. On a societal level, most Chinese university students spend significant time on campus and interact frequently with peers, fostering strong social networks. This social environment may mitigate the emotional stress caused by ALE to some extent, thus reducing the correlation between ALE and DEP [74].

SOPR did not mediate the relationship between ALE and DEP. The mediating role of SOPR between ALE and DEP was not effectively validated. This conclusion contradicts the findings of Adzrago, et al. [75], which posited that SOPR acts as a bridge connecting ALE and DEP, highlighting that the prevalence of DEP is significantly higher among individuals experiencing SOPR compared to those who do not. However, this finding is consistent with Goodman, et al. [76], who suggested that ALE is a key influencing factor for SOPR, with individuals suffering from ANX exhibiting maladaptive social and regulatory behaviors that may induce specific stress without mediating effects, thereby not directly leading to DEP. This discrepancy may arise from several factors. First, university students may have already developed coping and emotional management strategies due to the high academic pressure experienced during secondary education. Therefore, even if they exhibit ALE, they may not directly experience DEP through SOPR [77]. In other words, the bridging role of SOPR between ALE and DEP may be weakened. Second, during the questionnaire completion, students may not have fully disclosed their ALE and related emotional states due to social desirability or privacy concerns, potentially underestimating the actual impact of SOPR on the relationship between ALE and DEP. These factors may collectively account for the lack of significant mediating effects of SOPR between ALE and DEP.

SOPR also did not mediate the relationship between ACBU and DEP. This finding is inconsistent with Jiang, et al. [78], whose research involving over a thousand adolescents validated the predictive role of SOPR on DEP in students experiencing ACBU, emphasizing the importance of fostering social skills and peer relationships to reduce depressive emotions through social support. The differences in findings may arise from the fact that the influence of ACBU on DEP primarily manifests through individuals’ internal emotional responses rather than external social contexts. ACBU often entails long-term emotional exhaustion and feelings of helplessness, and these direct internal negative experiences can directly translate into depressive emotions [79], without necessarily requiring mediation through SOPR. On the other hand, the impact of SOPR on university students may more prominently affect changes in ANX or self-esteem rather than directly triggering DEP. Additionally, students may be more inclined to perceive academic pressure as a personal challenge rather than a social issue, thereby weakening the mediating role of SOPR between ACBU and DEP.

ANX plays a mediating role in the relationship between ALE and DEP. This is supported by the study by Sun, et al. [18], which found that students with ALE perceive less social support, leading to accumulated stress and ANX, as they lack sufficient coping skills, making them more susceptible to DEP. Individuals with ALE often struggle to effectively recognize and express their emotions, making them prone to ANX; prolonged ANX can lead to emotional exhaustion and depressive feelings [80]. In this process, ANX serves as a bridge, exacerbating the emotional distress brought about by ALE and triggering DEP. ANX plays a significant mediating role between ALE and DEP by intensifying individuals’ emotional burdens and social difficulties, significantly increasing the risk of DEP.

ANX also mediates the relationship between ACBU and DEP. This aligns with the research conducted by Alshehri, et al. [81], which found through a cross-sectional study involving undergraduates from three universities that students experiencing ACBU face more negative self-perceptions, exacerbating their ANX and increasing their risk of DEP. Although ANX and DEP are distinct psychological states, they often intertwine; the presence of ANX may significantly elevate the risk of developing DEP [82]. ANX serves as a mediating factor in the transition from ACBU to DEP, acting as a crucial link between the two [81]. On one hand, once university students experience ACBU, it not only affects their learning state but also leads to persistent negative thoughts and worries. These cognitive processes may alter individuals’ thought patterns, causing them to adopt a pessimistic outlook and see no positive solutions, subsequently heightening their ANX. When students experience prolonged ANX, they are more likely to feel overwhelmed by burdens and pressures, which may further evolve into DEP [83]. On the other hand, ANX may also lead individuals to reduce social activities, lacking social support and interpersonal interactions, thereby increasing feelings of loneliness and isolation, further elevating the risk of DEP.

There is a significant gender difference in the impact of ACBU on DEP, with the path coefficient for males being significantly higher than for females. This finding is consistent with the study by Liu, et al. [84], which suggests that the negative impact of ACBU on students’ mental health may vary depending on gender and environmental factors. Male students may experience a stronger influence of ACBU on DEP due to cultural restrictions on emotional expression and a lower tendency to seek social support. The results of this study indicate that males are more likely to internalize ACBU as negative emotions, thereby increasing the risk of DEP [84–86]. In contrast, females may mitigate the negative effects of ACBU on DEP through higher emotional regulation abilities or a greater tendency to seek social support. College students, particularly those lacking effective coping mechanisms, are more prone to negative emotions and psychological issues due to prolonged academic pressure and frustration.

Impact

This study employs Smart-PLS for SEM to explore key factors influencing DEP among university students. The model explains 83.2% of the variance in DEP, demonstrating its effectiveness in understanding the influencing factors related to university students’ mental health. Therefore, the findings hold significant theoretical and practical implications.

Theoretical impact

This study provides an in-depth analysis of the relationships between ALE, ANX, SOPR, ACBU, and DEP using SEM, thereby enriching the theoretical understanding of mental health in university students.

Firstly, the study identifies ALE and ACBU as key variables, aiming to expand the application of existing mental health theories within the university population. The findings indicate that ALE enhances our comprehension of the relationship between emotional expression and mental health, influencing individual emotional management, social interaction, and psychological adaptation. This understanding paves the way for more effective therapeutic interventions. ACBU reflects emotional exhaustion stemming from prolonged academic pressure and reveals deep connections to negative psychological states such as ANX and DEP. By elucidating the multifaceted mechanisms through which ALE and ACBU impact university students’ mental health, this study enriches the theoretical framework regarding the effects of academic pressure on mental health and provides new insights into individual psychological adaptability in high-pressure academic environments.

Secondly, the research systematically clarifies the mechanisms by which ALE and ACBU indirectly influence DEP through ANX. It explores the mediating roles of SOPR and ANX in the relationships between ALE, ACBU, and DEP. The results indicate that ANX serves as a mediator between both ALE and DEP, as well as ACBU and DEP, while SOPR does not exhibit the expected mediating effect. This highlights ANX’s central role among these psychological factors. This theoretical innovation offers new perspectives for understanding the complex causes of DEP and provides theoretical support for future targeted psychological interventions.

Finally, the study reveals differences in the relationship between ALE and DEP compared to previous research. The finding that ALE does not have a significant direct effect on DEP challenges the view that ALE is a direct predictor of DEP. This suggests that ALE may more likely influence DEP indirectly through other psychological states, such as ANX, rather than acting directly. Additionally, the lack of significant impact of SOPR on DEP, which contradicts some existing studies, indicates that the role of SOPR in the formation of DEP may be complex and requires further exploration in different contexts. These conclusions enrich the theoretical research on the causes of DEP and provide new insights for clinical interventions.

Practical impact

The findings of this study provide practical guidance for mental health interventions and management among university students, particularly regarding ALE, ACBU and SOPR.

Firstly, since ALE significantly influences DEP, interventions for students with ALE are essential. This can be achieved through emotion recognition training, psychological counseling, and emotional intelligence development. These interventions can help students improve their ability to recognize and express emotions, thereby reducing the accumulation of negative emotions caused by emotional regulation difficulties. Secondly, given that ANX also significantly affects DEP. Building social support networks and conducting group counseling activities can help students better cope with stress. In particular, teaching student relaxation techniques, mindfulness meditation, and cognitive-behavioral therapy to alleviate ANX can effectively reduce the impact of ANX on DEP and lower the risk of DEP. To help anxious students better cope with stress, thereby mitigating their risk of ANX and DEP. Additionally, the significant impact of ACBU on DEP necessitates preventive measures and schools should pay attention to students’ academic progress and adjust the pace of instruction appropriately to prevent ACBU caused by excessive academic workload. This can be achieved by optimizing course design, reducing exam pressure, and providing academic guidance to help students adjust their learning pace and enhance their interest in learning. Moreover, research has found a gender difference in the impact of ACBU on DEP, with the effect being stronger for males than for females. These findings highlight the importance of targeted psychological interventions for male students. For example, schools can provide targeted mental health support, such as emotional regulation training, social support networks, and stress management courses, to help students strengthen their psychological resilience and coping abilities. Furthermore, teachers and counselors should pay closer attention to the mental health of male students, actively identifying potential issues and providing timely support. These measures are crucial in mitigating the impact of ACBU on students’ mental health. Future research should further explore intervention strategies for different gender groups and assess their long-term effectiveness.

From the students’ perspective, enhancing emotional regulation and social skills can boost their confidence and improve interpersonal relationships. Challenges encountered in academic and daily life often correlate closely with their emotional management abilities. By guiding students to actively participate in mental health activities and training, they can learn effective methods for identifying and coping with negative emotions, such as Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy, weekly reflection, and event description techniques. These approaches can help students develop healthier emotional regulation strategies and improve their overall mental well-being. Enhancing their psychological resilience. For students with ALE. Building strong peer relationships and support networks is crucial. Students should actively step out of their comfort zones and engage in collective activities such as volunteer service and interest-based clubs. These activities help enhance their sense of belonging and security, fostering stronger social connections and emotional well-being. This positive psychological environment helps students engage more effectively in their studies and reduces the risk of ANX and DEP.

Moreover, to minimize the prevalence of mental health issues among university students, institutions should establish a multi-level mental health intervention system that encompasses emotional regulation, social support, and academic management. This system should not only address individual mental health issues but also consider the complex relationships among psychological variables to provide more comprehensive support. Schools can organize mental health seminars and promote mental health awareness, advocating that psychological issues should not be a source of shame. This can help increase students’ understanding and acceptance of mental health problems. Additionally, schools can implement personalized mental health assessments and develop tailored intervention plans to support students in improving their mental well-being across various areas, including emotions, social interactions, and academics. This holistic approach can contribute to the overall enhancement of students’ mental health. Such a holistic intervention strategy will better address current mental health challenges and lay a solid foundation for students’ future psychological well-being and academic success.

Limitations and future directions

This study aims to explore the relationships among ALE, ANX, SOPR, ACBU, and DEP among Chinese university students. However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, regarding sample selection, the participants were primarily drawn from specific universities, introducing geographical limitations that may affect the representativeness of the sample. As a result, the findings may not fully reflect the psychological health status of all university students in China, potentially impacting the broader applicability of the results. Secondly, the research design employed a cross-sectional method, with data gathered at one specific time. This limits the ability to observe changes in variables over time and restricts the analysis of developmental trends. Additionally, the reliance on self-report measures introduces another limitation. While self-report scales are commonly used to gather mental health data, they may be influenced by social desirability bias and individual subjective distortions. Future research should address these limitations by utilizing a more diverse sample and incorporating varied research designs. Additionally, exploring a broader range of variables could enhance the reliability and applicability of the findings.

Conclusion

Based on Social Cognitive Theory, this study examines ALE as a limiting factor in emotional expression and investigates its indirect effects on DEP through ANX. ACBU, reflecting emotional exhaustion due to prolonged academic pressure, is included in the framework to explore its pathways to DEP. SOPR is assessed from the perspective of interpersonal interactions to evaluate its potential impact on the mental health of university students. To validate the relationships among these variables, the study employed Smart-PLS for SEM analysis, revealing key factors influencing DEP in university students. The model explains 83.2% of the variance in DEP, indicating strong explanatory power and effectiveness in understanding the influencing factors. This model provides a more comprehensive perspective for research on university student mental health and further refines the related theoretical framework. The findings offer new tools and insights for academic research and serve as a reference for future studies on mental health across diverse populations, holding significant theoretical and practical implications.
