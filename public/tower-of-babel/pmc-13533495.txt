Squamous Cell Carcinoma Developing from an Epidermal Inclusion Cyst on the Buttock
Authors: Chantaramon Thanapaisal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Epidermal inclusion cysts of the skin rarely undergo malignant transformation into squamous cell carcinoma. We present a case of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma in a 68-year-old male patient who exhibited clinical features resembling an infected epidermal inclusion cyst on the buttock. Presenting symptoms included rapid growth of the mass, tenderness, and purulent drainage. Although symptoms commonly indicate infection, underlying malignancy must remain a differential consideration. Definitive diagnosis requires histopathologic confirmation. The patient underwent wide excision of the mass and left inguinal node dissection, with the resultant defect covered by a posterior thigh flap. Wide local excision with appropriate margins remains the recommended treatment to ensure complete tumor clearance and minimize the risk of recurrence and pathological analysis essential for definitive diagnosis.

Epidermal inclusion cysts are the most common benign subcutaneous tumors, accounting for 85%–90% of all excised cysts.1,2 They arise from the implantation of epidermal elements into the dermis and are lined by a thin layer of epithelium. Major histogenesis mechanism involves such implantation, often following trauma.3

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is the second most common type of skin cancer. Risk factors for SCC include exposure to ultraviolet radiation.4 Although rare, epidermal inclusion cysts can undergo malignant transformation to SCC. The incidence of this malignant change ranges from 0.011% to 0.045%.2

We report a rare case of cutaneous SCC in a 68-year-old man, presenting as an infected cyst arising from a long-standing epidermal inclusion cyst on the buttock.

CASE REPORT

A 68-year-old man presented to his primary care physician with an asymptomatic mass that had been present on the buttock for 6 years. Initially, a 2 × 3 cm subcutaneous cyst was noted. Within the past year, the cyst had progressively enlarged. Although painless with no ulceration, the increase in size caused discomfort.

The primary care hospital sent the patient for an initial magnetic resonance imaging, which revealed a well-defined, lobulated subcutaneous cystic lesion measuring 1 × 12 × 5 cm at posterolateral aspect of left buttock, suggestive of an epidermal inclusion cyst. Neurovascular bundles were intact, adjacent bony structures appeared normal, and the muscles and fascia around the buttock showed normal signal intensity without atrophic change. An infected epidermal inclusion cyst was diagnosed, and incision and drainage were then performed.

Postoperatively, a nonhealing ulcer developed on the lesion, which failed to respond to antibiotic therapy. Over the next 6 months, the ulcerative mass continued to enlarge and was erythematous and purulent. Concern for malignant transformation prompted an incisional biopsy, which confirmed cutaneous SCC. The patient was then referred to our department for further management.

In our department, an ulcerative tumor on the left buttock was observed, measuring approximately 15 cm in overall diameter, including the surrounding indurated and hyperpigmented skin (Fig. 1). Palpable left inguinal lymphadenopathy was noted.

Preoperative findings. A 68-year-old man with an ulcerative tumor on the left buttock measuring 15 cm in overall diameter, including the surrounding indurated and hyperpigmented skin.

A repeat MRI demonstrated a loculated subcutaneous mass measuring 6.1 × 3.2 × 6.3 cm with associated skin opening. Additionally, a separate 1.5-cm inclusion cyst was identified in the left posterior upper thigh. Left inguinal lymphadenopathy was present, with the largest node measuring 1.7 cm.

The patient underwent wide excision of the left buttock mass with a 1-cm margin deep into fascia and left inguinal node dissection. A cyst in the left posterior upper thigh was also excised. The skin defect, measuring 15 × 10 cm, was successfully covered with a posterior thigh flap (Fig. 2).

Microscopic examination revealed a well-differentiated SCC measuring 12 × 6.7 × 2 cm with a free resected margin. DISCUSSION

Epidermal inclusion cysts are the most common benign cutaneous tumors, typically presenting as a nontender soft subcutaneous mass. Malignant transformation is rare.

McDonald et al analyzed 637 cases of epidermal cysts and identified malignant transformation in 7 cases, with only 1 presenting as SCC. However, more recent studies report that a variety of malignant neoplasms can arise in an epidermal inclusion cyst, including basal cell carcinoma, malignant melanoma, and Paget disease.1,5 SCC is the most frequently observed.1 Although the development of SCC in a preexisting epidermal cyst is rare, chronic irritation of the skin caused by inflammation or infection can trigger malignant transformation.5–7

In 2018, Frank et al2 reviewed well-documented cases of SCC arising within epidermal inclusion cysts. The most affected regions were head and neck, followed by lower limbs, trunk, perineum, and buttocks.1,6,8 However, Japanese reports have identified the buttocks as the most frequently affected region.7

Cutaneous SCC should be suspected when cystic lesions present as a firm subcutaneous mass fixed to underlying tissue; when long-standing tumors show rapid growth, especially with ulceration; when atypical findings appear on MRI; and when antibiotic-resistant infection is observed. An inflamed, hardened mass can simulate and mask underlying carcinoma.

In our case, the long-standing cyst on the buttock showed clinical features suggestive of an infected cyst, including erythema, tenderness, and purulent drainage. Therefore, histopathological analysis is essential for diagnosis. The staging of this case was T3N0M0 with free margin.

According to National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines, sentinel lymph node biopsy should be considered in cases with multiple high-risk features. However, this was not an option at our institution due to limited resources. Management should be based on a risk-benefit discussion with the patient. Previous studies have reported 38% of lymph node metastasis in advanced SCC (T3, T4) of the trunk and extremities, with the risk significantly increasing with previous ulcer at the site of SCC.9 It is necessary to consider the risks and benefits of inguinal lymph node dissection, given that lower extremity lymphedema has been reported in 24% of cases in a meta-analysis.10 Given the patient’s overall risk of disease progression, reliable long-term follow-up was improbable. Therefore, the potential harm from lymphedema had to be weighed against the benefits of lymph node dissection.

In this case, the standard margin of 1 cm was made, with deep margin taken down to fascia to ensure a high level of confidence in complete tumor removal. Immediate flap reconstruction was chosen to cover the large defect, restore shape and function, and provide durable coverage with adequate soft-tissue cushioning and aesthetic contour.

The clinical challenge in this case lies in the lesion’s atypical evolution. The mass consisted of a 6-year-old benign-appearing cyst that had only grown larger over the past year, displaying clinical features such as erythema, tenderness, and purulent discharge, mimicking infection and obscuring diagnosis.

Our case highlights the importance of clinicians maintaining a heightened suspicion of SCC developing in a long-standing epidermal inclusion cyst with a rapidly changing cystic mass that remains unresolved despite appropriate treatment.

CONCLUSIONS

Although malignant transformation of epidermal inclusion cysts is rare, clinicians should be aware of cases exhibiting rapid growth, ulceration, or frequent recurrence. We report a case of cutaneous SCC arising from a long-standing epidermal inclusion cyst on the buttock that exhibited recent enlargement, tenderness, and erythema, which, although commonly observed in infected cysts, can obscure underlying malignancy. Therefore, histopathologic analysis remains essential for definitive diagnosis. Wide excision with an adequate margin is recommended to ensure complete tumor removal and reduce the risk of recurrence.
