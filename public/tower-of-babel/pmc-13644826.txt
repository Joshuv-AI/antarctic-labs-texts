ToxMamba: a novel method for toxic peptide prediction based on the fusion of pretrained embeddings and multi-scale state space models
Authors: Jingya Fang, Wenqi Shen, Yanru Li, Yu Jin, Haodong Li, Yuanyuan Chen, Zhihui Xu, Jinhu Huang, Cong Pian, Zutan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeptide therapeutics offer great promise in drug discovery, yet the accurate identification of peptide toxicity remains essential for both safety assessment and biodefense. We developed ToxMamba, a toxic peptide prediction framework that integrates Implicit Structure Model (ISM)-derived pretrained embeddings, state space models, multi-scale feature learning, and ensemble inference. Built upon the Mamba architecture, ToxMamba enables efficient sequence modeling and captures contextual patterns across multiple receptive fields from parallel branches. On the independent test set, ToxMamba consistently surpasses existing methods, improving the F1 score and Matthews Correlation Coefficient by 3.9–18.3 and 7.5–46.9 percentage points, respectively. Ablation analyses further showed that Mamba-based models generally achieved stronger overall performance than Transformer-, Convolutional Neural Network-Long Short-Term Memory-Attention-, and Kolmogorov–Arnold Network-based variants. Feature visualization showed that ISM embeddings yielded clearer separation between toxic and non-toxic peptides in the latent space. Intermediate-layer feature analysis highlighted residue-level regions associated with toxicity-related sequence motifs, supporting the biological interpretability of ToxMamba. Overall, this study provides an efficient and interpretable computational framework for high-throughput toxic peptide screening.

Introduction

Peptides usually consist of 6–50 amino acid residues and have shown considerable potential in antitumor, antimicrobial, and other therapeutic applications owing to their high target specificity [1], good biocompatibility, and low immunogenicity [2]. Compared with conventional small-molecule compounds and macromolecular protein drugs, peptide therapeutics generally exhibit stronger tissue affinity and relatively controllable pharmacokinetic properties [3]. Despite their considerable drug development potential, peptide drugs still face challenges such as toxicity, immunogenicity, and in vivo stability, among which toxicity is one of the major factors restricting clinical translation [4]. Peptide toxins are peptide molecules that may exert harmful effects on organisms [5, 6]. Toxic peptides can compromise normal host-cell function, exert cytotoxic or hemolytic effects [7], and, in some instances, lead to adverse reactions such as intestinal barrier disruption and immunopathic tissue damage [8]. Therefore, efficient and reliable toxicity prediction methods are important for ensuring the safety of peptide drugs. Traditional peptide toxicity assessment mainly relies on in vitro cytotoxicity assays, including the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide assay, lactate dehydrogenase assay, and adenosine triphosphate assay, as well as hemolysis assays and immunotoxicity tests, which evaluate the damage caused by compounds to cells and blood cells [7, 9]. In addition, animal-based acute toxicity tests are also classical toxicity assessment methods [10, 11]. However, these experimental methods are generally time-consuming, costly, and subject to ethical constraints, making them difficult to meet the needs of large-scale screening. Thus, developing efficient and economical computational methods for peptide toxicity prediction has practical significance.

Peptide toxicity prediction methodologies can be broadly classified into two categories: similarity-based approaches and machine learning-based approaches. Similarity-based approaches infer the potential toxicity of unknown peptides by computing their similarity to known toxic peptides [12, 13]. These methods are straightforward to implement and have relatively low computational costs, thus possessing certain application value in the early-stage screening of drug discovery [14]. However, their performance is highly contingent upon the coverage of known toxic peptides and requires manually defined sequence similarity thresholds, which restricts their generalization ability in complex sequence spaces and large-scale screening scenarios. Machine learning-based approaches have achieved more substantial advancements. Early approaches primarily relied on manually designed sequence features, such as amino acid composition, dipeptide frequency, and physicochemical properties, and combined them with models such as support vector machine and random forest (RF) for toxicity prediction [15, 16]. For example, Khabbaz et al. constructed a prediction model based on an updated antimicrobial peptide dataset by integrating physicochemical properties and linguistic features of peptides, offering a representative example of feature engineering-driven toxicity prediction [17]. Nevertheless, such methods rely heavily on manual feature engineering and struggle to fully capture the deep semantic information in peptide sequences.

Recent advances in deep learning have shifted the field toward data-driven representation learning. ToxDL used a convolutional neural network (CNN) with sequence-derived structural information to evaluate peptide toxicity [18]. In 2021, Wei et al. introduced ATSE, which further incorporated graph neural networks and attention mechanisms to integrate structural and evolutionary information [19]. In 2022, Wei et al. further introduced ToxIBTL, which combined information bottleneck theory with transfer learning to address short-peptide toxicity prediction under limited data settings [20]. ToxGIN, published in 2024, later employed a graph isomorphism network to jointly model amino acid sequences and 3D structural information, improving short-peptide toxicity prediction [21]. Inspired by large pretrained models in natural language processing, protein language models have also been introduced into this task. For instance, VISH-Pred, developed by Mall et al. improved peptide representation by using ensemble fine-tuned Evolutionary Scale Modeling (ESM) models [22]. Other recent models have explored multimodal or multi-architecture designs, reflecting the growing importance of integrating complementary representations for peptide toxicity prediction. ToxiPep, published in 2025, combined bidirectional gated recurrent units with Transformer to capture sequence dependencies, and used multi-scale CNNs to extract fine-grained structural features from simplified molecular-input line-entry system representations [23]. The ToxinPred 3.0 model developed by Rathore et al. in 2024 integrated motif-based machine learning with deep learning strategies [24]. ToxMSRC, published in 2025, combined continuous bag-of-words, synthetic minority oversampling technique, multi-scale CNN, and bidirectional long short-term memory to alleviate class imbalance and overfitting [25]. More recently, the PTP-SMGCA model developed by Li et al. in 2026 introduced bimodal fusion of peptide sequences and molecular graphs with bidirectional cross-attention, achieving improved predictive performance [26]. Beyond peptide toxicity prediction, recent biological sequence modeling studies have also emphasized the value of feature embedding and fusion [27, 28]. Together, these studies highlight the importance of integrating complementary sequence, structural, and motif-level information. However, efficient long-range dependency modeling, adaptive multi-scale feature integration, and robust generalization remain open challenges.

In this work, we developed ToxMamba, an interpretable toxic peptide prediction framework that integrates Implicit Structure Model (ISM)-derived pretrained embeddings with a Mamba-based feature extraction module, a selective state space model (SSM) architecture. By combining structurally informative residue-level representations with adaptive multi-scale contextual modeling, ToxMamba is designed to capture toxicity-related sequence patterns at different contextual scales while preserving efficient long-range dependency modeling. The main contributions of this study are:

(1) To improve predictive performance, the SSM framework was first applied to the prediction of toxic peptides, and an end-to-end prediction model based on ISM representation was achieved.

(2) A multi-scale feature extraction module captures sequence contextual information across different window scales via parallel branches.

(3) A five-fold model ensemble inference mechanism enhances model robustness and stability.

(4) The combination of ablation experiments and interpretability analysis demonstrates the contribution of each module to model performance and identifies key residue sites associated with toxicity.

Materials and methods

Dataset construction

To ensure comparability of results, this study adopted the same dataset configuration as PTP-SMGCA [26] as the benchmark. Positive samples were constructed from multiple publicly available datasets curated by Li et al., including toxic peptides from ATSE [19] and ToxinPred 3.0 [24], as well as manually reviewed toxic peptides from the latest versions of DRAMP 4.0 [29] and dbAMP 3.0 [30]. These datasets, widely used in existing studies, provide toxic peptide samples with high label consistency.

Negative samples were derived from manually reviewed entries in the UniProt [31] and Swiss-Prot [32] databases. Specifically, non-toxic peptide sequences were obtained by excluding toxin-related annotations: the keywords “NOT toxin NOT toxic AND reviewed: yes” were used for UniProt, and “NOT KW-0800 AND NOT KW-0020” for Swiss-Prot [26]. This strategy is consistent with previous benchmark studies and helps reduce potential label noise. Subsequently, Cluster Database at High Identity with Tolerance (CD-HIT) [33] was applied to all sequences for redundancy removal with a similarity threshold of 0.9, minimizing data redundancy and potential information leakage.

To further evaluate model generalization capability, Li et al. additionally constructed an independent test set comprising 479 toxic peptides from the Database of Antimicrobial Activity and Structure of Peptides (DBAASP) [34] with experimentally determined hemolytic activity, where toxicity labeling was assigned based on commonly used hemolytic thresholds. Unlike the internal dataset, the toxicity labels in DBAASP originate from experimental measurements rather than multi-source integration, thus offering a more realistic assessment of model generalizability on external, experimentally derived data. This independent test set was kept completely separate throughout the entire model development process, taking no part in model training, parameter tuning, or threshold determination. Its negative samples were generated following the same filtering strategy and subsequently subjected to redundancy removal, ensuring no sequence overlap with the training set. This rigorous separation guarantees the integrity and objectivity of evaluation, preventing data leakage and overfitting bias. All negative samples retained for the independent test set after redundancy removal were strictly reserved for final evaluation, preserving their statistical independence from both training and development splits. Model architecture

ToxMamba consists of four main modules (Fig. 1): (i) ISM-based pretrained representation module, which maps the raw amino acid sequences into dense vector representations; (ii) multi-scale feature extraction module, which further extracts high-order features at different scales via parallel convolutional and Mamba encoders; (iii) representation fusion module, which performs adaptive weighted fusion of the multi-scale features; and (iv) toxicity prediction module, which outputs the class probabilities of toxic and non-toxic categories based on the fused features. This module is implemented as a multilayer perceptron (MLP) comprising two fully connected layers and an output layer.

Overall architecture of ToxMamba; the model comprises four main components: sequence embedding, multi-scale feature extraction, feature fusion, and prediction modules; raw sequences are processed by the ISM model to produce sequence embeddings; the multi-scale feature extraction module applies three parallel branches with distinct window sizes to capture embeddings at varying scales; these multi-scale embeddings are then integrated into a unified feature matrix by the feature fusion module; finally, the prediction head generates the output through a flattening layer followed by fully connected layers; a five-fold ensemble inference strategy is employed to enhance robustness and reduce prediction variance.

Multi-scale feature fusion module

Sequence patterns related to toxicity may appear in different local contexts, ranging from short residue motifs to broader sequence fragments, rather than treating the three receptive fields as having equal information content. The fused feature matrix is first flattened into a 1D feature vector, which is then fed into a MLP consisting of two hidden fully connected layers, ReLU activation functions, and Dropout. The model outputs binary classification probabilities: the toxicity probability P(toxic|S) and the non-toxicity probability P(non-toxic|S) for the input peptide sequence S. To enhance model robustness and generalization capability, a five-fold cross-validation strategy is adopted during the training phase.

Five-fold ensemble inference strategy

To improve the stability and generalization capability of the model, ToxMamba incorporates a five-fold cross-validation ensemble inference mechanism. Specifically, the complete training dataset is randomly partitioned into five equal-sized subsets, ensuring that each subset maintains the same class distribution as the original data. A five-round training-validation cycle is then performed: in each round, one subset is held out as the validation set, while the remaining four subsets are combined to form the training set.

In each round, a complete ToxMamba model is trained with all parameters optimized independently. After the five rounds of training, five model instances with identical architecture but independent parameters are obtained. During inference, each model independently makes a prediction for the same input sample, and the final result is obtained via a multi-model ensemble inference strategy, thereby reducing single-model bias and enhancing the robustness of the prediction outcome. During inference, the five trained models output toxicity probabilities for each sample. The final probability is obtained by averaging these probabilities, and samples with a final probability ≥0.5 are classified as toxic.

ToxMamba training

The model is trained using the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10−4 and a weight decay of 0.01. Early stopping is applied with a patience of 10 epochs, terminating training when validation performance does not improve for 10 consecutive epochs to mitigate overfitting. All experiments are conducted on a system equipped with NVIDIA A100 GPUs, using Python 3.9 and PyTorch 2.8.0.

Evaluation metrics

To comprehensively evaluate the predictive performance of ToxMamba on the binary peptide toxicity classification task, we adopt several quantitative metrics. TP, TN, FP, and FN denote true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively. The metrics are defined as follows:

(1) Accuracy (ACC): the overall proportion of correctly classified samples.

(2) Recall: the ability to identify positive (toxic) samples.

(3) Precision: the reliability of positive predictions.

(4) Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC): a balanced measure accounting for all four elements of the confusion matrix, ranging from −1 to 1 (closer to 1 indicates better performance).

(5) F1 score: the harmonic mean of precision and recall, balancing both metrics.

In addition, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPR) are employed to assess overall discriminative ability.

Model and parameter selection

To evaluate the impact of different embedding methods and feature extraction backbones on model performance, we compared 12 model variants: embedding methods (ISM, ESM, one-hot) and backbone architectures [Mamba, Kolmogorov–Arnold Network (KAN), Transformer, Convolutional Neural Network-Long Short-Term Memory with Attention (CNNLSTM_ATT)]. The results are shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table S1. Among all variants, ISM_Mamba achieved the best performance, with recall of 83.8%, precision of 88.8%, MCC of 73.4%, AUC of 93.8%, F1 score of 86.2%, and AUPR of 94.1%.

Considering that ISM and ESM may provide complementary information, we compared different representation strategies, including ISM + ESM concatenated representations, in the Results section and Supplementary Figs S1 and S2. Based on comparisons performed on the internal test set, ISM, which achieved the best performance, was selected as the input representation for the subsequent sequence modeling module in the final model. We further evaluated the design of the multi-scale branches. First, alternative kernel-size combinations, including (1, 3, 5), (3, 5, 7), and (5, 7, 9), were compared. Based on the internal test-set results, the (3, 5, 7) configuration was selected as the default setting. A post hoc analysis on the independent test set showed the same overall trend (Supplementary Figs S9 and S10). Second, we compared single-scale kernels (k = 3, 5, 7, and 9) with the multi-scale configuration (k = 3, 5, 7). As shown in Supplementary Figs S11 and S12, the multi-scale setting outperformed the single-scale alternatives in major metrics, including F1 score, MCC, AUC, and AUPR. These results suggest that the performance gain comes from the complementary integration of local patterns across multiple receptive fields, rather than from simply using a larger kernel.

Analysis of embedding and feature extraction strategies

We further systematically compared the performance of different embedding and feature extractor combinations. As shown in Fig. 3A–C, the ISM encoding generally outperformed ESM and one-hot encoding on most metrics, with a more concentrated performance distribution, indicating both higher predictive performance and better stability. Performance distributions for other metrics across encoding methods are provided in Supplementary Fig. S13.

Comparative analysis of different embedding and feature extraction strategies on peptide toxicity datasets; (A–C) boxplots showing the distributions of ACC, MCC, and F1 for different sequence representation methods (ISM, ESM, and one-hot); the results indicate that ISM encoding outperforms ESM and one-hot encoding across all metrics; (D) Fowlkes–Mallows Index (FMI) scores of intermediate features for the three encoding methods, showing that the ISM embedding achieves better performance under the Mamba architecture; (E–G) features from different embedding methods (ISM, ESM, and one-hot) visualized using t-SNE; brown spots represent non-toxic peptides, and yellow spots represent toxic peptides; (H–J) boxplots showing the distributions of ACC, MCC, and F1 for different extraction backbones, including Mamba, Transformer, KAN, and CNNLSTM_ATT.

t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) visualization of intermediate layer features (Fig. 3E–G) further supports the above conclusions. t-SNE is a non-linear dimensionality reduction method that projects high-dimensional feature representations into two dimensions while preserving local neighborhood structures. The compactness and spatial separation of clusters intuitively reflect the quality of feature representations learned by each model. In the feature space learned by ISM_Mamba, toxic and non-toxic peptides exhibited good separation, with high intra-class compactness and clear inter-class boundaries. In contrast, the feature spaces of ESM_Mamba and Onehot_Mamba showed substantial overlap and poorer separation. In addition, the visualization based on Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection shows consistent separation trends with t-SNE on both the independent test set and the internal test set (Supplementary Figs S14 and S15), further supporting the above findings. Collectively, these results indicate that ISM representations enable the learning of more discriminative feature spaces, thereby enhancing the model's ability to distinguish positive from negative samples.

The Fowlkes–Mallows Index is a sample-pair-based metric that evaluates the agreement between clustering results and ground-truth labels. Experimental results (Fig. 3D) quantitatively supported this observation, showing that ISM_Mamba achieved higher agreement between feature-space clustering and ground-truth labels than the control embeddings. Additional clustering quality metrics on the independent test set and the internal test set are provided in Supplementary Figs S16 and S17.

Finally, the model-family comparison in Fig. 3H–J shows that Mamba-based models generally outperformed Transformer-, KAN-, and CNNLSTM_ATT-based models across ACC, MCC, and F1. Together, these results indicate that ISM representations improve feature discriminability, while Mamba-based sequence modeling further enhances predictive performance.

Comparison of loss functions

We evaluated the impact of different loss functions on the performance of the model, including PolyLoss [43], Focal Loss [38], Cross-Entropy (CE), and L1 loss [44]. Focal Loss delivered the best overall performance (Fig. 4), achieving the highest AUC (93.77%), F1 score (86.25%), and AUPR (94.14%), while also ranking first in ACC, Precision, and MCC (Supplementary Fig. S18). It also showed the largest average improvement across the combined metrics (Fig. 4D). These results suggest that Focal Loss provides a more balanced optimization objective for peptide toxicity prediction.

Comparative analysis of different loss functions on peptide toxicity datasets; (A–C) comparison of AUC, F1 score, and AUPR across loss functions; Focal Loss achieves the highest AUC of 93.7%, the highest F1 score of 86.2%, and the best AUPR of 94.1%; (D) relative performance heatmap of the model under different loss functions across evaluation metrics (“+” indicates above the average level, “–” indicates below the average level); the results show that Focal Loss is superior in overall performance; (E) performance ranking of each loss function across multiple evaluation metrics (ACC, recall, precision, MCC, AUC, F1, and AUPR), where rank = 1 indicates the best.

Compared with standard CE, Focal Loss employs a dynamic reweighting mechanism that reduces the weight of easily classified samples while increasing the contribution of hard samples, thereby directing the training process to focus more on difficult-to-distinguish samples. These results confirm that the down-weighting mechanism of Focal Loss for easily classified samples is particularly effective in the task of toxic peptide identification with complex class boundaries, significantly enhancing the discriminative ability of ToxMamba between toxic and non-toxic peptides. The Focal Loss hyperparameter γ was set to 2.0, which achieved the best overall performance in a grid search over (0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0) on the internal test set with α fixed at 0.25. All metrics remain stable across the tested γ range, demonstrating that the model is robust to the choice of γ. Post hoc sensitivity analysis on the independent test set shows a similar trend (Supplementary Figs S3 and S4).

Model interpretability analysis

Identifying residue regions associated with model predictions is useful for improving the interpretability of computational toxicity screening methods. In this study, we extracted activation signals from the intermediate layers of ToxMamba and used the L2 norm of the activation vector at each position as the residue-level activation score, which reflects the activation intensity of the model at that residue position (Fig. 5). Two toxic peptides randomly selected from the internal test set were analysed (Fig. 5A and B). The results showed that the fragments “KSVRR” and “RLLR” ranked highest in activation scores, consistent with the observation that arginine-rich functional peptides often possess potential toxicity [45]. The alternating arrangement of positively charged Arg and hydrophobic Leu residues in the RLLR motif may facilitate the formation of amphipathic structures, thereby enhancing membrane interactions and promoting membrane perturbation. Previous studies have indicated that this alternating pattern in RLLR helix-containing transmembrane peptides is closely associated with membrane disruption and cytotoxicity [46].

Interpretability analysis; activation vectors from intermediate layers were extracted, and the residue-level activation score of each residue position was calculated based on the L2 norm; the top-ranking residues were highlighted as the high-activation positions of the model for prediction; (A and B) activation scores of individual residues for two toxic peptides randomly selected from the internal test set; residues ranked in the top 10 are shown in red, and the others in blue; (C–E) toxicity prediction score, hemolysis assay results, and residue-level activation scores for the natural peptide sequence exp_4s predicted as toxic by the model; (F–H) toxicity prediction score, hemolysis assay results, and residue-level activation scores for the generated peptide sequence exp_4g predicted as toxic by the model; for hemolysis assays, data are presented as mean ± SD from three technical replicates.

To further illustrate the application potential of ToxMamba, we selected two candidate sequences predicted as toxic by the model from natural data and AI-generated peptide drug data: exp_4s (natural sequence, prediction score 0.99, Fig. 5C–E) and exp_4g (generated sequence, prediction score 0.74, Fig. 5F–H). In hemolysis validation experiments, exp_4s exhibited greater than 90% hemolysis at 128 μg/mL in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 5D). Residue importance analysis revealed that lysine (K), leucine (L), and tryptophan (W) in exp_4s received high scores: positively charged lysine enhances membrane affinity through electrostatic attraction [47]; the hydrophobic side chain of tryptophan facilitates anchoring into the hydrophobic core of the membrane, with its aromatic ring stabilizing interactions [48]; and the hydrophobic side chain of leucine promotes membrane insertion and lysis [49]. As generative models are increasingly applied to the design of functional peptides, the candidate sequence space expands rapidly, imposing higher demands on early-stage toxicity screening. We therefore further analysed exp_4g, a sequence previously generated in our laboratory. Hemolysis assays showed that exp_4g caused ~24% hemolysis at 256 μg/mL (Fig. 5G), indicating a certain hemolytic risk. Key residue analysis indicated that K, R, and G in exp_4g received high scores. Previous studies have reported that K and R residues in spider toxins are critical for insecticidal activity [50]; moreover, glycine substitutions in analogs of heat-stable enterotoxin from Escherichia coli significantly affect biological activity [51].

In summary, the high-activation residues highlighted by ToxMamba show broad consistency with known toxicity-related sequence features, supporting the potential of the model for toxicity prediction and auxiliary mechanistic interpretation.

Discussion

This study proposes ToxMamba, a deep learning framework for peptide toxicity prediction. ToxMamba showed a better balance across multiple metrics, indicating more stable overall classification performance. The performance advantage of ToxMamba may be attributed to the complementary integration of ISM-derived residue representations, multi-scale PSConv-based local motif extraction, and Mamba-based contextual modeling, enabling the model to capture both latent structural information and toxicity-related sequence patterns. Compared with multimodal methods using molecular graphs or structural descriptors, ToxMamba provides a lightweight sequence-level framework without requiring explicit graph or structural inputs. Additional length-stratified analysis showed that ToxMamba maintained robust AUC across different peptide length intervals. Feature space analysis and key residue identification further support the biological interpretability of ToxMamba and its potential application in early safety screening of functional peptides, including antimicrobial, antitumor, and cell-penetrating peptides. To facilitate the practical application of ToxMamba, parameter selection guidelines tailored to different application scenarios are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Despite the promising results, this study still has certain limitations. First, the current model relies primarily on primary sequence information and has not yet integrated higher-level biological information such as 3D structures, conformational dynamics, and membrane interactions from the real world. Although residue-level activation analysis provides model-level interpretability, these scores reflect intermediate-layer activation intensity rather than causal residue importance; site-directed mutagenesis will be needed to determine whether the highlighted residues contribute to toxicity. Second, annotation bias and label noise in public datasets may affect model generalization. Negative samples defined by the absence of toxin-related annotations cannot be regarded as experimentally confirmed non-toxic peptides, and the DBAASP-derived independent test set mainly reflects hemolysis-associated toxicity. Therefore, in the future, additional rigorously validated experimental data are needed. Future work should extend ToxMamba from binary toxicity classification to multi-endpoint toxicity prediction, covering phenotypes such as hemolysis, cytotoxicity, neurotoxicity, immunotoxicity, and dose-dependent toxicity. This will require more comprehensively annotated benchmark datasets with experimentally validated toxicity endpoints, standardized assay conditions, and quantitative potency labels. Integrating these experimental phenotypes with multimodal information, such as 3D structures and molecular graphs, may further improve the modeling of complex peptide toxicity mechanisms. In addition, more sophisticated fusion strategies, such as attention-based gating mechanisms, cross-modal Transformers, and learnable dimensionality-reduction modules applied before feature fusion, represent promising directions for future research.
