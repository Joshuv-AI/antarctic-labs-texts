Robust epileptic seizure prediction: A 3D-SERESNet framework for patient-specific and multi-patient generalization
Authors: Lu Meng, Lijun Zhou, Wentao Zhang, Keli Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryEpilepsy is a prevalent neurological disorder. Effective seizure prediction methods can warn of impending seizures, enabling patients to take preemptive measures. In this study, we propose an efficient epilepsy prediction model, 3D-SERESNet (squeeze-and-excitation residual network). We use short-time fourier transform (STFT) to convert EEG segments into time-frequency representations. We construct 3D representations from these time-frequency graphs, which serve as input to a subsequent 3D residual network designed to learn spatio-spectral features hierarchically. To address data imbalance, we employ the focal loss function. We evaluated the model’s performance on the CHB-MIT dataset under both patient-specific and patient-independent settings. The model achieved an average sensitivity of 90.77% with a false positive rate (FPR) of 0.090/h in patient-specific experiments, and 84.41% sensitivity with 0.232/h FPR in patient-independent experiments.Subject areas: health sciences, neuroscience, computational bioinformatics

Introduction

Epilepsy is a common neurological condition which presents across all age groups, affecting around 65 million individuals globally.1,2 Studies indicate that only about two-thirds of patients are able to control their condition with the help of antiepileptic medications and medical oversight.3 As a result, effective epilepsy prediction algorithms have the potential to introduce innovative ways to treat the disease, significantly improving the lives of those who suffer from it.4 Electroencephalography (EEG) captures the minute electrical signals produced by neuronal activity in the brain and is the most commonly used tool in clinical settings for monitoring, diagnosing, and treating epilepsy-related neurological disorders.5 There are two kinds of EEG signals, scalp EEG signals and intracranial EEG signals, named according to the position of the electrodes.6 Scalp EEG signals are extensively utilized and more readily accessible compared to intracranial EEG, making them a preferred choice in various clinical and research contexts due to their convenience and availability.7 The EEG of epilepsy patients can be categorized into interictal, preictal, ictal, and postictal periods. Therefore, epilepsy seizure prediction involves accurately distinguishing between the interictal and preictal phases.8 Despite relentless efforts, predicting seizures remains a significant challenge due to the low variability of preictal EEG signals.9 Consequently, effective seizure prediction algorithms are of paramount importance. Epilepsy seizure prediction relies on EEG signals, with a workflow involving data acquisition, preprocessing, feature extraction, and classification.6 Short-time fourier transform (STFT), due to its low computational complexity, is commonly used for EEG feature extraction, despite requiring a trade-off in time-frequency resolution.10 Traditional classifiers, such as support vector machines (SVMs) and multi-layer perceptron, achieve approximately 89% sensitivity on the CHB-MIT dataset,11,12 but are limited by manual feature engineering and handling complex EEG patterns. Deep learning, with its automatic feature learning and robust pattern recognition, offers new opportunities to improve prediction accuracy.

Against the backdrop of deep learning’s extensive application in healthcare, cross-disease research methodologies have provided critical references for epileptic seizure prediction. Rahman et al.13 proposed a method that integrates extended independent component analysis for EEG artifact removal, multi-class common spatial pattern for feature extraction, and bidirectional long short-term memory networks for emotion state classification, achieving an accuracy of 94.61% on the DEAP dataset. Rasheed et al.14 proposed an innovative approach integrating convolutional neural networks (CNNs) with hybrid attention mechanisms for classifying gliomas, meningiomas, pituitary tumors, and non-tumor cases, achieving a classification accuracy of 98.33% in benchmark tests. Mazhar et al.15 developed an expert diagnostic system based on fuzzy inference rules that automates early-stage cardiac risk assessment (low/high/risky) through fuzzification processing, knowledge base comparison, and defuzzification output procedures. Tufail et al.16 systematically analyzed the synergistic effects of batch normalization and dropout in three distinct network architectures (no dropout, single dropout before softmax, and convolutional layer between dropout and batch normalization) for early classification of Alzheimer’s disease using positron emission tomography and magnetic resonance imaging modalities. Findings demonstrate that minimal or zero dropout utilization yields superior performance compared to higher dropout values. Bilal et al.17 proposed a hybrid machine learning model named ETCXGB, which integrates the predicted probability outputs of ExtraTreeClassifier with the original feature space to construct an enhanced feature matrix. This matrix is then fed into an XGBoost model for final diagnosis, achieving early and accurate cardiac disease detection with a 3.91% improvement in accuracy compared to conventional methods. Rasheed et al.18 proposed a framework that integrates the powerful feature extraction capability of VGG19 with the collective strengths of ensemble classifiers (logistic regression, random forest, and K-nearest neighbors), achieving a brain tumor classification accuracy of 98.27% on the Br35H dataset through 5-fold stratified cross-validation.

CNNs,19,20,21 and long short-term memory networks (LSTMs)22 are common deep learning algorithms, with CNNs being the most prevalent architecture for epilepsy seizure prediction.23 For instance, Khan et al.8 derived time-frequency characteristics using continuous wavelet transform and employed a 7-layer CNN, achieving 87.8% sensitivity on the CHB-MIT dataset for classifying interictal and preictal states. Similarly, Zhang et al.24 combined wavelet decomposition with common spatial patterns and utilized a 4-layer CNN, reaching 92.2% sensitivity. Ozcan et al.25 derived spatiotemporal features from EEG signals and employed a 3D CNN model as the classifier, achieving a sensitivity of 85.7% with the CHB-MIT dataset. Romney et al.26 employed a deep neural network model for classification, achieving 86.7% sensitivity and 89.5% specificity with the CHB-MIT dataset. Toraman et al.27 used STFT to obtain time-frequency features from EEG signals and applied three classifiers—VGG19, ResNet, and DenseNet—achieving their best performance, which included 92.32% sensitivity, 89.76% specificity, and 91.05% accuracy with the CHB-MIT dataset. Usman et al.28 employed STFT to extract features from EEG and implemented a classifier model that integrates CNN and SVM, attaining a sensitivity of 92.7% with the CHB-MIT dataset. Furthermore, by synthesizing handcrafted features with those from a three-layer CNN and employing a hybrid SVM-CNN-LSTM classifier, they achieved a sensitivity of 96.28% on the CHB-MIT dataset and 94.2% on the Kaggle dataset.6 Ryu et al.29 employed discrete wavelet transform to derive features from EEG signals and combined dense convolutional networks with LSTM as classifiers. Their method achieved an accuracy of 93.28%, with sensitivity and specificity values of 92.92% and 93.65%, respectively, upon evaluation with the CHB-MIT dataset. Liang et al.23 introduced a semi-supervised domain adaptation (DA) seizure prediction model, adapting using limited labeled target data and additional unlabeled target data, achieving a sensitivity of 88.8% and an FPR (false prediction rate) of 0.182/h. Ahmad et al.30 proposed an epileptic seizure prediction method based on multi-domain EEG feature fusion and supervised contrastive learning. By integrating multi-domain features through deep canonical correlation analysis and leveraging a contrastive learning mechanism to mitigate inter-individual variability, the method achieved outstanding prediction performance on two public EEG datasets, CHB-MIT and Kaggle. An advanced multi-view deep feature learning framework was proposed for epileptic seizure detection, which integrates traditional time-frequency domain features with 1D CNN-based deep features through a multi-view forest classifier, significantly improving the accuracy of EEG feature recognition.31

Although existing methods have shown good performance in experiments, the results are largely patient specific, with studies focusing on designing one classifier per subject due to the significant variability in EEG data across patients.32 However, such patient-specific approaches often suffer from overfitting to individual data patterns, limiting their generalizability to unseen subjects or diverse clinical scenarios. To comprehensively evaluate the performance of our model, we not only conducted experiments in a patient-specific context but also explored the model’s performance in a patient-independent setting, which aims to design a classifier capable of recognizing seizures across multiple subjects.32,33 This addresses a critical gap in prior work, where models trained on single-subject data fail to generalize due to inter-patient variability and small sample sizes. Moreover, most existing studies utilize 2D CNNs to process time-frequency representations of EEG signals. However, a significant limitation of this approach is its neglect of the crucial spatial correlations inherent in multi-channel EEG data. Epileptic seizures typically result from the coordinated activity of multiple brain regions. To address this, our method constructs 3D representations from the multi-channel time-frequency maps and employs 3D convolution for feature extraction. Critically, by integrating residual connections, our network mitigates the vanishing gradient problem and facilitates the training of this deeper architecture, allowing effective learning of complex spatiotemporal dependencies. This innovative approach enables our model to learn features simultaneously across the temporal, spectral, and spatial dimensions, thereby capturing richer and more discriminative EEG patterns that are beyond the reach of conventional 2D methods. Concurrently, our network incorporates an attention mechanism to automatically enhance the feature channels most relevant to seizure prediction while suppressing irrelevant or noisy information, significantly improving the model’s representational power. A common challenge with medical datasets is the issue of data imbalance, where the number of samples from patients in a normal state greatly exceeds those from patients during illness. This imbalance consistently presents a significant obstacle for analysis and model training,34 often exacerbating overfitting to the majority class and reducing sensitivity to rare seizure events. In contrast to most existing approaches that address data imbalance by creating training batches with equal numbers of positive and negative samples, our method provides a more efficient solution at the algorithmic level through the adoption of Focal loss.35 It dynamically scales cross-entropy to focus learning on hard-to-classify examples, thereby improving generalizability. We directly train on the original, unsampled complete dataset, preserving all information in the data distribution and enabling the model to learn more authentic overall data characteristics. Ultimately, we propose a prediction model—3D-SERESNet (squeeze-and-excitation residual network). The key contributions of our work can be summarized as follows.

Results

Dataset

The dataset used in this study is CHB-MIT.37 In accordance with the definitions provided by Truong et al.38 and Ma et al.,39 we define the interictal period as data recorded 4 h before or 4 h after a seizure and we define the 30 min preceding a seizure as the preictal period. Therefore, if the interval between two consecutive seizures is less than 30 min, they are merged and treated as a single seizure event. The onset time of this merged event is defined as the start of the first seizure, and its conclusion is defined as the end of the second seizure. We designate the 5 min after a seizure as the postictal period. Figure 1 illustrates the segmentation of EEG signals into different phases. The objective of seizure prediction is to provide an early warning before the onset of a seizure, focusing on identifying preictal EEG activity. Therefore, only data from the preictal and interictal periods are used. Moreover, for patients with excessively frequent seizures, the clinical significance of seizure prediction is relatively limited. Thus, in accordance with the patient selection criteria adopted in previous studies,23,38,40 we only included subjects with fewer than ten seizures per day. Table 1 presents the 13 subjects who meet our selection criteria. Additionally, due to variations in electrode counts among patients and between different recordings from the same patient, we selected 18 bipolar leads common to all patients for analysis: FP2-F4, C4-P4, T8-P8, FP1-F3, FP1-F7, P7-O1, F4-C4, T7-P7, P8-O2, P3-O1, F8-T8, FZ-CZ, FP2-F8, CZ-PZ, F3-C3, C3-P3, P4-O2, and F7-T7.

In epileptic seizure prediction tasks, classification results based on “segments” (e.g., preictal/interictal labels for 10-s EEG segments) only reflect local EEG characteristics and thus hold limited clinical value. To achieve more clinically meaningful warnings, we employ an event-based decision mechanism that aggregates these continuous segment-level predictions to determine whether to trigger an alarm.

Prior to introducing evaluation metrics, it is necessary to define the seizure occurrence period (SOP) and the seizure prediction horizon (SPH). As illustrated in Figure 2, the SPH refers to the time interval from the issuance of an alarm to the beginning of the SOP, while the SOP represents the time window during which a seizure is expected to occur, typically equal to the preictal duration. A correct prediction must satisfy the condition that the actual seizure onset occurs after the end of the SPH and within the SOP. Conversely, if the system triggers an alarm but no seizure occurs during the SOP, it is considered a false alarm. In this study, consistent with most research, the SPH is set to 5 min and the SOP is set to 30 min. We employ sensitivity (Sens) and the FPR as evaluation metrics. Sensitivity measures the proportion of correctly predicted seizures among all actual seizures, while the FPR represents the number of false alarms per hour. Additionally, we used the area under the curve (AUC) as the primary metric to evaluate segment-based classification performance.

Definitions of SOP and SPH in event-based prediction

The SPH represents the intervention period following an alarm. The SOP defines the time window during which a seizure is anticipated to begin after the SPH.

To reduce false alerts caused by system misjudgments, a k-of-n43 post-processing strategy is introduced: an alarm is triggered only if at least k consecutive positive predictions are observed within a sequence of n predictions. In this experiment, k and n are set to eight and ten, respectively. However, consecutively triggered warnings within short time intervals can lead to elevated FPR. Therefore, drawing on event-based prediction literature,23,44 this study defines a 30-min “refractory period”—during which any subsequent warning signals are ignored for 30 min following an initial warning trigger.

The data partitioning strategy employed in this study is crucial for ensuring the final accuracy and reliability of the model. In both patient-specific and patient-independent evaluation settings, a consistent approach is adopted. For a subject with N epileptic seizures, the interictal segments are divided into N parts. In each iteration, the preictal segments of one seizure and a corresponding interictal segment are used as the test set, while the data from the remaining N-1 seizures form the training set. To prevent overfitting, the last 20% of samples in the training set, based on temporal order, are allocated as the validation set, with the remainder used for model training (see Figure 3 for details). We select the best-performing model on the validation set (using AUC as the primary metric) for final testing.

Data partitioning strategy for patients with N seizures

One seizure event (preictal phase and matched interictal segment) is held out for testing, while remaining seizures form the training set. The last 20% of temporal segments in the training set are allocated for validation to prevent overfitting.

In the patient-specific evaluation, this partitioning framework is implemented using a strict leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) procedure. The process is repeated N times, each time leaving out a different seizure as the test set, and the final performance metrics are averaged over the N results.

In the patient-independent evaluation, a total of 7 rounds of experiments are conducted to ensure that every seizure of every patient has the opportunity to be included in the test set, as patient Chb01 has the maximum number of seizures (totaling seven). In each round, the LOOCV principle is applied across all patients. Specifically, for every patient, one seizure event is held out for testing. The data from the remaining seizures of all patients are then merged to form a unified training set and a unified validation set. Once all seizure events of a given subject have been tested once, that subject’s test set will no longer be tested in subsequent rounds, though its data will continue to participate in training and validation. The model is ultimately evaluated based on the test results from all seizure events of each patient.

The model is trained with a batch size of 32 and an epoch count of 100. Adam with weight decay is used as the optimizer, with a learning rate of 0.003 and a weight decay coefficient of 0.00003. The learning rate scheduler employed is a cosine annealing rate scheduler, with an initial period T0 set to 10, Tmult set to 2, and a minimum learning rate of 0.0003. The model computations are GPU accelerated on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090, running on a Windows system with an Intel core i9-14900K processor and 64 GB RAM. Since our testing procedure utilizes all available subject data, we deliberately avoid hyperparameter tuning on the validation set to prevent potential data leakage. The hyperparameter configuration is primarily determined empirically.

Model performance

To achieve robust seizure prediction by jointly learning spatio-spectral features from EEG signals, we propose the 3D-SERESNet framework (Figure 4). The process begins with the preprocessing of raw EEG data, including 60 Hz notch filtering (Figure 5) and segmentation into non-overlapping 10-second windows (Figure 6). Subsequently, STFT is applied to convert each window into a time-frequency representation. Crucially, to capture the inherent spatial correlations across multiple EEG channels, we construct a 3D input representation by stacking the time-frequency maps from all channels (Figure 7). This 3D representation serves as the input to our architecture. The core of the 3D-SERESNet consists of a sequence of specially designed SE-ResNet modules. The detailed architecture of a single SE-ResNet module, which integrates 3D convolutional residual blocks with SE attention mechanism, is depicted in Figure 8. This design enables the model to hierarchically extract discriminative features while adaptively recalibrating channel importance, thereby enhancing relevant spatio-spectral patterns indicative of preictal states and suppressing noise. The processed features are then passed through fully connected layers for final classification.

The pipeline begins with the preprocessing of raw EEG data and proceeds to the construction of 3D feature representations. These representations are processed through a deep network that integrates 3D convolution, residual blocks, and channel attention mechanisms for joint spatio-spectral feature learning. The system ultimately generates event-based predictions by identifying preictal states indicative of impending seizures.

The experimental results demonstrate that the prediction performance on the balanced dataset surpasses that on the imbalanced dataset, which aligns with expectations. As shown in Table 1, the CHB-MIT dataset inherently exhibits significant data imbalance. Although we introduce the focal loss function to mitigate class imbalance, its primary role lies in alleviating the negative impact of data distribution bias on model training rather than fundamentally altering the imbalanced data distribution. Subsequent ablation studies further validate this observation: compared to the model without focal loss, the incorporation of this loss function demonstrates improvements across all performance metrics, confirming its effectiveness in mitigating training bias induced by data imbalance.

In the patient-specific experiments, subjects Chb01 and Chb20 demonstrate excellent or even near-perfect prediction performance under both balanced and imbalanced data conditions, indicating that their seizure event patterns exhibit high intra-class consistency and good feature discriminability. In contrast, although Chb14 achieves 100% sensitivity, it is accompanied by a high FPR, reflecting the model’s failure to effectively grasp the discriminative patterns of this subject’s seizure features. Particularly noteworthy is the performance of Chb19, which shows the lowest AUC values and larger p-values under both experimental conditions. Combined with the data in Table 1, this subject not only has the lowest preictal ratio but also only three total seizure events. This highly imbalanced and limited sample size severely restricts the model’s ability to effectively learn discriminative features, ultimately resulting in insufficient generalization performance.

In the patient-independent experiments, the inter-subject AUC variability shows significant reduction compared to patient-specific experiments, indicating that the generalized model effectively captures common seizure patterns across patients. However, this paradigm reveals two notable limitations: first, a marked decrease in sensitivity, and second, a widespread increase in FPR. Although most results maintain p values below the 0.05 significance threshold (consistent with our predefined statistical criterion where p ≤ 0.05 indicates prediction performance significantly superior to random chance), these p values demonstrate substantial increase compared to patient-specific experiments, reflecting the model’s limited adaptability to individual-specific patterns. These findings suggest that while the patient-independent model performs well in capturing universal seizure patterns, its practical application still faces the challenge of balancing sensitivity against FPR. These findings highlight the need to enhance the modeling of individual differences while preserving generalization capability, which represents an important direction for our future research.

Effectiveness of the SE-ResNet module

To validate the effectiveness of the proposed method, this study conducts a systematic comparative analysis with two classical deep learning models: the CNN-based benchmark model,38 which represents pioneering work as the first to apply deep learning to epilepsy prediction and consists of three convolutional blocks and two fully connected layers; and ResNet,46 a widely adopted classical architecture that effectively mitigates the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks through residual connections.

Results show that although the 2D network significantly reduces the number of parameters and requires only 0.22 ms training time per sample, it demonstrates substantial degradation across all three key metrics, particularly with FPR rising to 0.512/h. This primarily stems from 2D convolution’s limitation in processing channel-time-frequency maps independently, failing to capture epilepsy-specific cross-channel synchronous discharge patterns. In contrast, 3D convolution extracts features simultaneously across temporal, spectral, and spatial dimensions, enabling more effective identification of pre-seizure patterns originating from brain network synchronization, thereby significantly enhancing discriminative capability.

Replacing focal loss with cross-entropy loss reduces model sensitivity to 73.54% with substantially increased missed detections. This highlights focal loss’s crucial role in addressing class imbalance. By adjusting focus parameters, the model prioritizes hard-to-classify preictal samples over being dominated by numerous interictal samples during training. This mechanism effectively counteracts the model’s bias toward majority classes, proving essential for sensitivity improvement.

Removing the SE attention module similarly causes performance degradation across all three evaluation metrics. This confirms our introduced SE module, through adaptive feature channel weighting, it effectively enhances key features containing epileptiform discharges while suppressing noisy channel interference, thereby improving discrimination accuracy.

Although the complete model (3D-SERESNet) requires more parameters, it achieves significant prediction improvement while maintaining fast computational speed (0.19 ms per inference). To further evaluate its potential for edge deployment, we conduct tests in a CPU environment, which recorded a training time of 13.99 ms and an inference time of 3.31 ms per sample. These results demonstrate the model’s strong potential for practical deployment in clinical environments with limited computational resources.

Discussion

In EEG-based seizure prediction research, generating clinically meaningful alarm signals is crucial for clinical translation. Although existing studies10,39 have achieved outstanding classification accuracy in binary classification of preictal and interictal periods, such segment-level evaluation fails to reflect the practical requirements of real-world clinical prediction scenarios. To address this, our study adopts a rigorous event-based evaluation framework to systematically investigate the seizure prediction task. We propose a 3D-SERESNet model that not only captures the time-frequency characteristics of EEG signals but also incorporates the spatial relationships between multiple EEG channels. By introducing the SE attention mechanism, which adaptively adjusts channel weights, the model enhances relevant features while suppressing less informative ones, further improving performance. Under a strict data partitioning and training strategy, evaluation results demonstrate that the model achieves superior performance in event-based prediction tasks, showing strong potential for clinical application.

The class imbalance issue has a significant impact on the performance of classification models. Most existing approaches address this problem at the data level by constructing balanced datasets. In contrast, our study tackles it from an algorithmic perspective, making full use of all available data and introducing the focal loss function, which effectively mitigates the negative effects of class imbalance to a certain extent and ensures good comparability. However, since the CHB-MIT dataset itself exhibits severe imbalance (e.g., the preictal phase lasts only 1.5 h, while the interictal phase spans up to 24.9 h), model performance still faces certain challenges. We also conducted comparative experiments under balanced data conditions and achieved more meaningful, and in some cases even superior, performance under rigorous evaluation settings.

In terms of training strategy, some studies47,48 adopted the hold-out method, randomly splitting the data into training and test sets. This global shuffling approach may allow the model to indirectly access information from the test set during training, thereby inflating the reported performance metrics. In contrast, our study employs LOOCV, strictly adhering to temporal order and event boundaries for data partitioning. By preserving temporal continuity during the testing phase, our evaluation protocol is not only more rigorous but also better aligned with the practical challenges of epileptic seizure prediction.

Messaoud et al.41 developed a complex handcrafted feature pipeline that initially extracted nearly a thousand dimensions, later refined to 735 features through selection. Notably, this study only partitioned the data into training and test sets, and performed grid search for hyperparameter optimization directly on the test set—an approach that may lead to data leakage. Similarly, studies30,41,47,48,49,50,52,53,54 employed only a train/test split, with one study30 also conducting hyperparameter search on the test set. Such optimization based on test information is likely to overestimate the model’s generalization capability. In contrast, our work completes model tuning on an independent validation set, with final evaluation carried out on an unseen test set, ensuring the reliability of reported results. Under imbalanced data and LOOCV settings, our approach achieved comparable, and in some cases even superior, performance relative to certain studies using the hold-out strategy.54

Yang et al.52 proposed a dual self-attention residual network that enhances performance by separately extracting spectral and channel attention features followed by fusion. However, this architecture lacks effective information interaction between the two modules. In contrast, our method constructs a 3D EEG representation enabling simultaneous analysis of spectral and channel characteristics, thereby more effectively capturing seizure-related patterns in a multidimensional space. Under balanced data conditions, our approach demonstrates superior performance even with more rigorous data partitioning.

Ozcan et al.25 employed a 3DCNN to capture spatiotemporal correlations in epileptic signals, though their model lacks discriminative capability for critical channels and achieved an FPR of 0.18 with random data splitting. Our study introduces a channel attention mechanism that adaptively focuses on EEG channels more relevant to seizure onset, thereby improving the utilization of discriminative information. Our approach maintains more stable performance under stricter evaluation protocols.

To address this issue, DA methods have been proposed and have made considerable progress. It should be noted, however, that although the method proposed by Wu et al.32 reported a sensitivity of 94.8%, their experimental design carries a risk of data leakage. By shuffling the target patient’s data and employing 5-fold cross-validation, the model may have had access to test data that should have remained unseen. Although they did not maintain data balance, they applied overlapping segmentation to the preictal phase to mitigate the class imbalance issue.

Furthermore, existing DA methods commonly rely on complex mechanisms such as adversarial training and knowledge distillation. While these techniques help reduce inter-domain distribution discrepancies, they also introduce increased model uncertainty and higher training difficulty.

In contrast to the DA paradigm, which focuses on optimizing personalized predictors for individual target patients, we propose a unified seizure prediction model. Centrally trained on a diverse multi-patient dataset, our model aims to construct a universal predictor that operates without relying on case-specific adaptation procedures. Theoretically, DA methods should hold a performance advantage due to their explicit modeling of individual differences. However, when comparing our patient-independent model against existing DA approaches, results demonstrate that our method achieves comparable—and in some cases superior—predictive performance relative to state-of-the-art DA techniques. This finding suggests that through large-scale training on heterogeneous data, the model can spontaneously learn cross-patient invariant characteristics of preictal phases, thereby forming a feature representation that is robust to inter-subject variability and exhibits enhanced generalization capability. Beyond its competitive performance, our approach also offers notable practical advantages: it significantly reduces both the complexity and computational cost of model deployment.

Certainly, this study also has certain limitations. For some difficult patients whose data distributions differ substantially from the training set, the performance of our model may still fall short compared to DA models specifically optimized for them. Additionally, predicting for new patients currently requires retraining the model on all patient data. Future work will explore leveraging this general model as a foundational pre-trained backbone and combining it with lightweight personalized fine-tuning strategies. This approach aims to further enhance prediction accuracy for specific individuals while preserving the model’s generalization capability.

Limitations of the study

Although we implemented LOOCV in our experimental design to preclude data leakage during the training phase, this study still has significant limitations regarding clinical causality. The current model relies on the static prior hyperparameter α in the loss function, which is derived from the training data. However, epilepsy is fundamentally a dynamic condition where seizure thresholds vary with circadian rhythms, medication levels, and disease progression. Our current framework cannot adapt to these temporal variations. Particularly for new patients whose seizure patterns are typically unknown, this absence of causal mechanisms may result in unpredictable performance degradation when the model encounters unseen data, thereby introducing potential risks for clinical deployment. Besides, to prevent data leakage, we did not perform hyperparameter tuning on the validation set. Consequently, the current network architecture and parameter configuration retain substantial room for improvement. In our future work, we will focus on implementing automated hyperparameter search strategies such as Bayesian optimization while ensuring strict data isolation protocols, and incorporating neural architecture search techniques to adaptively optimize critical structural parameters. These improvements are designed to enable the model to dynamically adjust parameters based on real-time monitoring data.

The 3D-SERESNet model developed in this study holds clear potential for clinical application. It can be packaged as a lightweight software module and directly integrated into EEG monitoring devices in hospital intensive care units or epilepsy monitoring units. The system can process EEG signals in real time, and once a pre-seizure state is detected, it automatically sends an alert to healthcare workers’ workstations, providing a valuable time window for clinical interventions (such as medication preparation and safety measures). However, the clinical translation of the model still faces several challenges. These include false positive alarms, missed detections, prediction delays, and patient data privacy issues. In particular, the use of the CHB-MIT dataset, while publicly available and widely adopted in epilepsy prediction research, presents inherent limitations for real-world generalization. Its relatively small sample size and limited patient diversity may constrain the model’s ability to adapt to the broader variability seen in clinical practice—where individual differences are more pronounced and data distributions are more complex. A model trained on a smaller, more homogeneous dataset may experience performance degradation when deployed in realistic clinical environments with greater demographic and pathophysiological heterogeneity. These challenges represent both the current limitations of this research and directions for future work. In terms of model generalizability, we will systematically evaluate the model’s performance on datasets from diverse sources, including EEG data collected from multiple medical centers using different acquisition devices, to verify its applicability across institutions and equipment. At the same time, we will explore multimodal data fusion approaches, attempting to combine EEG signals with other physiological signals, thereby enhancing the model’s robustness and prediction accuracy through complementary information. Regarding clinical translation and application, we will focus on advancing the practical deployment of the model: improving computational efficiency through model light-weighting techniques and developing inference engines that support real-time EEG data processing; employing data encryption and privacy protection technologies to ensure patient information security; and designing intuitive visualization interfaces to reduce alert fatigue among healthcare workers. The ultimate goal is to integrate the model into embedded devices or mobile platforms, building a low-power, highly real-time epileptic seizure warning system.

Method details

We propose an efficient epilepsy prediction model named 3D-SERESNet. As illustrated in Figure 4, the model takes EEG data as input and accomplishes seizure prediction through three core stages: data preprocessing, feature extraction and optimization, and classification.

The specific workflow of the network is as follows: First, STFT is applied to the raw EEG data for time-frequency analysis. The resulting time-frequency maps from multiple EEG channels are then stacked to form 3D representations. A 3D convolutional operation is applied to these generated 3D representations to simultaneously capture spatial, temporal, and frequency characteristics of the EEG signals. Subsequently, three feature-fusion residual blocks incorporating SE attention mechanisms are employed to deeply refine the initially extracted 3D features. The Focal loss function is specifically adopted to address class imbalance between preictal and interictal periods. Ultimately, seizure events are predicted based on the classification results.

Data preparation and feature extraction

The CHB-MIT dataset contains 60 Hz powerline interference, a type of noise primarily originating from power systems in regions that use 60 Hz alternating current. This interference, superimposed on EEG signals, can obscure intrinsic physiological characteristics (such as preictal abnormal electrical activity) and subsequently compromise the accuracy of feature extraction and model prediction performance. To address this issue, we apply a 60 Hz notch filter, which selectively attenuates signals at the 60 Hz frequency. This approach effectively removes powerline noise while preserving the original physiological information in the EEG signals to the greatest extent, thereby establishing a reliable data foundation for subsequent analysis. Figure 5A displays the power spectral density of the raw EEG signal, showing a prominent spike at 60 Hz indicating the presence of powerline interference. After applying the notch filter, as shown in Figure 5B, this spectral peak is effectively eliminated, demonstrating successful suppression of the interference.

Owing to the widespread clinical use of EEG devices such as Nihon Kohden and Cadwell, which are set by default to display 10 seconds of data per page, we segment the filtered EEG signals into non-overlapping 10-second intervals for both preictal and interictal periods (Figure 6). This segmentation strategy offers three key advantages: first, it aligns the research workflow with clinical standards and enhances the applicability of the subsequent clinical translation of the model; second, the 10-second time window can fully capture the critical changes in preictal EEG signals, preventing feature omission caused by an overly short window or data redundancy from an excessively long one; third, it achieves a balance between computational efficiency and the robustness of model training, thereby improving the accuracy and practicality of epilepsy prediction.

Subsequently, we perform Z-Score normalization individually for each channel of every EEG image segment. This ensures that signals from all channels participate in model training on a consistent scale. The method effectively prevents any data leakage, maintains strict isolation between the training and test sets, and preserves the spatiotemporal specificity of EEG signals. We employ STFT to derive time-frequency domain features, transforming all EEG segments into time-frequency maps. To utilize the spatial characteristics across multiple channels, we generate 3D representations from the multi-channel time-frequency maps. We choose to represent EEG signals with 3D representations because, while 2D images can capture only the time-frequency characteristics of EEG signals, 3D representations also incorporate spatial information from multiple EEG channels. This approach offers a more detailed and holistic representation of the features present in EEG data. SE-resnet module

Figure 8 illustrates the structure of the SE-ResNet module. Our model includes three such modules. As shown in Figure 8A, the residual module mitigates the vanishing gradient issue in deep neural networks by incorporating residual connections, which enhance the flow of information. First, the input feature map is transformed via a 3D convolution layer with a 1x1x1 kernel to modulate the channel dimensions. This adjustment is achieved by linearly combining the channels at each position. Next, batch normalization is applied, followed by activation with the LeakyReLU function. Then, spatial features are derived through a 3D convolution layer employing a 1x3x3 kernel, succeeded by batch normalization and LeakyReLU activation. Subsequently, a 3D convolution layer with a 1x1x1 kernel is used to restore the number of channels, accompanied by batch normalization. The input feature map is then added to the output from this convolution process, forming a residual connection, and the resulting feature map is activated using the LeakyReLU function. Ultimately, a Dropout layer is utilized to mitigate overfitting and produce the final output feature map. This structure effectively alleviates the vanishing gradient problem, thereby enhancing information transfer and feature learning.

As shown in Figure 8B, the SE Attention module enhances useful features and suppresses irrelevant ones by weighting each channel, thereby improving the network's feature extraction capability. The input feature map is first processed through global average pooling (AdaptiveAvgPool3D) to obtain global information for each channel. The pooled output, which is reshaped to the dimensions (batch_size, channel), is first processed through a fully connected layer with a ReLU activation function for dimensionality reduction. Subsequently, it is fed into another fully connected layer with a Sigmoid activation function, which generates the weights for each channel. Next, the weights are employed to weight the channels of each input feature to obtain a weighted feature map. The SE Attention module enhances our model's feature extraction capability through channel recalibration, leading to superior model performance. By combining the residual module and SE Attention modules, the model's accuracy and robustness are improved, resulting in outstanding performance in the epilepsy prediction task.

Focal loss function

The Focal loss function, initially proposed by Lin et al.,35 was designed to deal with class imbalance issues in object detection tasks. The Focal loss function is defined by the following equation:

For binary classification problems, the formula specifies that when the sample label is 1, pt=p,αt=α. When the sample label is 0, pt=1-p,αt=1-α. Here, pis the model's predicted probability for the positive class, and α is the balancing factor used to adjust the weight of the minority class in imbalanced datasets. The parameter γ, known as the focusing parameter, serves to amplify the influence of hard-to-classify samples while diminishing the impact of those that are easier to classify, thereby addressing challenges associated with sample difficulty. The function dynamically scales the cross-entropy loss, enabling the model to focus more on learning hard-to-distinguish samples and enhancing its modeling capability for imbalanced data.

We adopt this loss function to manage the class imbalance in our EEG-based seizure prediction task. Lin et al.35 noted in their paper that the best results were achieved with α=0.25 and γ=2. However, their dataset had a positive-to-negative sample ratio of 1:3. As shown in Table 1, our dataset has varying ratios of preictal to interictal samples. Therefore, to adapt to our experimental dataset, we set α to the preictal ratio within the training data: for patient-specific models, it is derived from each subject's own training set, while for patient-independent models, it is based on the aggregated training set from multiple subjects. This approach prevents data leakage and preserves causality, as no test data is involved in the calculation. For the parameter γ, we set it to 2.

Web platform development

To facilitate the translation and application of our research, we have developed an online platform for epileptic seizure prediction that implements the 3D-SERESNet model. The platform is publicly available at www.neuai4science.cn:5001/eeg_ictal_prediction. Built on a browser/server architecture with a Flask backend and HTML/CSS/JavaScript frontend, it provides a comprehensive end-to-end prediction pipeline.

The workflow is as follows: users upload standard EDF files through the web interface, triggering an automated preprocessing pipeline that segments the data into 10-second windows, performs STFT analysis, and stacks multi-channel time-frequency maps to generate 3D inputs. These preprocessed data are then fed into the 3D-SERESNet model for seizure prediction. The system automatically generates visualization results and pushes them to the frontend interface, achieving real-time updates of processing status. The resulting graphs display time sequences on the horizontal axis, identify “Preictal” and “Interictal” state transitions on the vertical axis, and mark all detected alarm positions.

In terms of performance optimization, the platform demonstrates relatively high computational efficiency: for a 5-minute EDF file, the complete testing process from data preprocessing to output generation takes only 0.36 seconds. In real-world user scenarios, the end-to-end response time from frontend data submission to obtaining prediction results is 22.42 seconds. The primary latency in the current end-to-end response time stems from data transmission between the frontend and backend. Future optimizations will focus on reducing user-perceived waiting time through techniques such as optimizing network communication protocols and enabling data compression for transmission.
