Exploring potential underestimates in fatal overdose mortality across major metropolitan areas in Mexico between 2005 and 2019
Authors: Raul Bejarano-Romero, Jaime Arredondo Sánchez-Lira, Claudia Rafful, Eileen V. Pitpitan, Luis E. Esparza-Méndez, Michelle Poimboeuf, Chelsea L. Shover, David Goodman-Meza
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Mexico, low numbers of overdose deaths reported by government agencies, coupled with evidence of increased substance use in certain regions, suggest underreporting of overdose mortality. We explore the potential miscoding and underreporting of overdose deaths.MethodsWe assembled a mortality dataset using a combination of publicly available data and systematic freedom of information requests to Mexican state jurisdictions. Using data from post-mortem toxicology analyses, we identified proxy International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 codes for causes of death that were significantly more common (using p < 0.05 as a threshold) in decedents with positive toxicology for opioids, methamphetamine, cocaine, or benzodiazepines compared to decedents with negative toxicology for these substances. We then estimated annual crude overdose mortality rates, for individuals aged 20 to 39 with both standard overdose-specific codes (i.e., X40-44, X60-64, X85, and Y60-64), and derived proxy ICD-10 codes.ResultsWe identified acute myocardial infarction (I21), acute pulmonary edema (J81), and acute respiratory failure (J96) as proxy ICD-10 codes for potential overdose deaths. Using these codes, the estimated crude overdose mortality rate per 100,000 aged 20 to 39 in Mexico increased from 4.14 in 2005 to 6.92 in 2019, compared to estimates of 0.31 to 0.37 using standard overdose codes for the same period.ConclusionsOur findings suggest a potential underestimate of overdose deaths due to possible misclassification and underscore the critical need for improved forensic capabilities to more accurately identify overdose deaths.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25249-9.

Background

Mexico’s reported illicit drug overdose mortality rates are significantly lower than those of the United States (US) and Canada. Official mortality data reported 82 overdose deaths in 2010, which increased to 235 in 2019. Based on these counts, the overdose death rate in 2019 was 0.18 per 100,000 in Mexico, compared to 21.6 per 100,000 in the US and 15.1 per 100,000 in Canada [1–3]. These differences may be related, in part, to different patterns of substance use and opioid prescribing [4–6]. However, recent community-based studies conducted in Mexico’s northern border region suggest significant increases in non-fatal opioid overdoses [7, 8], and wastewater analyses have confirmed the presence of fentanyl in the drug supply in some Mexican cities [9]. In addition, a pilot project in Baja California, a state on the US-Mexico border, tested 1,775 consecutive deaths from 2022 to 2024, and found that 39% were positive for fentanyl. Of these deaths, 71% were coded as acute respiratory failure, consistent with opioid overdoses [10].

National overdose statistics are based on death certificates. Death certificates in Mexico are completed either by physicians who have cared for the deceased prior to death or by medical examiners at Forensic Medical Services (Servicio Médico Forense, SEMEFO). Medical examiners do not perform or report toxicological examinations unless specifically mandated by judicial authorities [11, 12]. This omission is further compounded by the unavailability of necessary reagents and equipment in many forensic services [13]. Even in the United States, where resources abound in comparison to Mexico, there has been concern about systematic underreporting of overdose deaths [14].

In the present study, we aimed to investigate the potential underreporting of overdose deaths in Mexico by identifying non-specific International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes that may have been used in place of specific codes. Our main objective was to use these proxy ICD codes to estimate drug overdose death rates at both the national and metropolitan levels. This approach involved a comprehensive review of official public records and datasets obtained through freedom of information requests, providing a more nuanced understanding of regional and demographic variation in the overdose burden across Mexico.

Methods

Data sources

For this retrospective observational study, we obtained data through public datasets or via requests for freedom of information through the Mexican National Institute of Transparency for Access to Information and Personal Data Protection (Instituto Nacional de Transparencia, Acceso a la Información y Protección de Datos Personales, INAI). As no human subjects were included in this study, the Institutional Review Boards at the University of California, Los Angeles (CA, US), and San Diego State University (CA, US) deemed the study as not subject to ethical review.

Official mexican mortality data

We obtained individual-level mortality data between 2005 and 2019 from the General Directorate of Health Information (Dirección General de Información en Salud) under the Mexican Ministry of Health (Secretaría de Salud) [15]. The dataset included comprehensive details for each registered death, location (i.e., municipality and state), age at death, sex, date, occupation, education, marital status, and the cause of death as classified by the ICD, 10th Edition (ICD-10).

Medical examiner data

We submitted freedom of information requests to all 32 Mexican states (Supplemental Table 1). We requested data from autopsies where post-mortem toxicology analyses for substances were performed, including year, age, gender, probable cause of death, municipality, and identified substances in toxicological examinations. Complete responses were received from seven states, providing a dataset representative of four regions defined by INEGI: North (Baja California, Sonora, and Coahuila), Central-West (Zacatecas), Central (Mexico City and Oaxaca), and Southeast (Yucatán) [16]. The remaining states either omitted the cause of death, indicated that toxicological testing data was not available, provided aggregate data only, or declined to provide data. Only Mexico City and Baja California provided information where toxicological analysis results were negative.

Population data

To derive estimates for the denominator for calculating crude mortality rates, we obtained municipal-level population estimates, segmented by 5-year age groups from the Mexican National Institute of Statistics and Geography (Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Geografía, INEGI) for the years 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020 [17].

International mortality data

We obtained public mortality data from Argentina, Chile, Colombia, Brazil, and the US to offer a comparative perspective (sources are available in Supplemental Table 2). We selected these Latin American countries as their population size and economy, measured both by gross domestic product (GDP) and GDP per capita, are comparable to that of Mexico [18].

Mortality codes

Derived proxy codes

We aggregated individual decedent data from the Medical Examiner Dataset including age, year of death, municipality, potential cause of death, and substance(s) found. We created a new variable indicating whether each decedent had a positive toxicological test for opioids, methamphetamine, cocaine, or benzodiazepines. Cases with either negative toxicology results, or only positive results for other substances (e.g., cannabis, alcohol, antidepressants, inhalants, cyanide, and misoprostol) were coded as having a negative test result. Using keywords, we classified the causes of death into common categories (Table 1). Uncommon causes of death regardless of the state (counts less than 15) were combined by organ systems (e.g., renal, cardiac, or central nervous system). We then compared the frequencies of registered causes of death between those classified as having a positive or negative test for opioids (e.g., morphine, heroin, and fentanyl), methamphetamine, cocaine, and benzodiazepines. We excluded trauma-related deaths (e.g., accidents, firearms, hemorrhage) because their underlying cause is not attributable to drug overdose. For example, fatalities from accidents or gunshot wounds result directly from external injury, while deaths from hemorrhage reflect acute physiological trauma; in both cases, the presence of drugs in toxicology does not indicate that overdose was the proximate cause of death. We included causes of death that were more frequent (e.g., acute myocardial infarction) in decedents who had positive toxicological tests for these substances compared to decedents with a negative toxicological test or a positive test for other substances (e.g., alcohol, cannabis, inhalants). Statistically significant differences were determined by a p-value of less than 0.05 using Fisher’s exact test. Finally, we mapped identified causes of death to specific ICD-10 codes.

Standard overdose-specific codes

We identified official overdose-related deaths based on ICD-10 codes as the underlying cause of death: X40-X44 (i.e., unintentional poisoning by drugs, medications, and biological substances), X60-X64 (i.e., intentional self-poisoning by drugs, medications, and biological substances), X85 (i.e., homicide by drugs, medications, and biological substances), and Y60-Y64 (i.e., undetermined poisoning by drugs, medications, and biological substances) [19].

Data analyses

We analyzed data at the national and metropolitan or municipal metropolis level as defined by the National Commission on Population (Consejo Nacional de Población, CONAPO) and INEGI [20]. Metropolitan areas in Mexico are defined as joint municipalities with a high grade of physical or functional integration between the municipalities and have a population of more than 200,000 people. Municipal metropolises are municipalities that do not form part of a metropolis but have a population of more than 300,000 people. For example, the Metropolitan Area of Mexico City is composed of 76 municipalities over three states, whereas Mexicali, Juárez, and Hermosillo are municipal metropolises. We chose this unit of analysis as many individual municipalities in a metropolitan area may not have their own SEMEFO and rely on a central forensic service within the metropolitan area. For example, as of 2022, there were 1,004 forensic service laboratories across Mexico, serving over 2,400 municipalities [21]. From here forward, a municipal metropolis is considered within the term metropolitan area.

In this analysis, we included all metropolitan areas with more than 300,000 inhabitants (n = 49). In 2019, 58.3% of the country’s population lived in these metropolitan areas. We stratified these metropolitan areas into four regions according to INEGI: Central (Mexico City, Guerrero, Hidalgo, State of Mexico, Morelos, Puebla, Tlaxcala, Oaxaca); Central-Western (Aguascalientes, Colima, Guanajuato, Jalisco, Michoacán, Nayarit, Querétaro, San Luis Potosí, Zacatecas); North (Baja California, Baja California Sur, Chihuahua, Coahuila, Durango, Nuevo León, Sinaloa, Sonora, Tamaulipas); and Southeast (Campeche, Chiapas, Quintana Roo, Tabasco, Veracruz, Yucatán).

We estimated the annual mortality rates per 100,000 individuals within the 20 to 39-year-old demographic, utilizing our derived proxy ICD codes and the standard overdose-specific codes. We chose the 20- to 39-year-old age group due to a lower expected rate of medical diseases leading to deaths [22], and highest rates of drug treatment admissions compared to other age groups as shown by official SISVEA (Sistema de Vigilancia Epidemiológica de las Adicciones) data [23]. We included data up to 2019, to avoid introducing the confounding factor of changed causes of death at the height of the COVID-19 pandemic (e.g. respiratory failure in younger people as a result of COVID-19 infection). To estimate crude yearly estimated overdose mortality rates per 100,000 people, we divided the number of overdose-related deaths identified through derived proxy and standard overdose codes by the population estimates for the specified age group in each metropolitan area and at the national level. We compared national standard overdose-specific and proxy crude rates per 100,000 people with similar calculated rates for the US, Argentina, Chile, Colombia, and Brazil to contextualize our derived proxy codes. To depict the distribution of overdose death rates in Mexico, we created a four-panel map figure showing crude rates for 2005 and 2019 using both standard overdose and derived proxy codes. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.2, and maps were created in QGIS-LTR 3.40 Bratislava.

Results

Medical examiner data and proxy code identification

The Medical Examiner Data contained 33,807 cases, with 43% (n = 14,546) in the 20 to 39 age group. Case data ranged from 2013 to 2023. A total of 13.9% of cases tested positive for opioids, methamphetamine, cocaine, or benzodiazepines (n = 2,017), while the remaining 86.1% (n = 12,529) had a negative toxicological result or a positive result for other substances (e.g., cannabis, alcohol, antidepressants, inhalants, cyanide, and misoprostol). Most cases originated from Mexico City (n = 12,782), followed by Baja California (n = 1,181), Zacatecas (n = 359), Coahuila (n = 105), Oaxaca (n = 49), Yucatán (n = 40), and Sonora (n = 30). Table 1 presents the distribution of positive and negative toxicology results for opioids, methamphetamine, cocaine, or benzodiazepines by probable cause of death. We found a statistically significant association between toxicology results (positive vs. negative) and acute myocardial infarction (I21), acute pulmonary edema (J81) and acute respiratory failure (J96) (sepsis, hemorrhage, trauma, other cardiac, and malnutrition were excluded due to low probability of being overdose-related). Supplemental Table 3 shows the distribution disaggregated by substance. Most cases were positive for methamphetamine (n = 966), followed by cocaine (n = 610), opioids (n = 516), and benzodiazepines (n = 308).

Overdose rates based on derived proxy ICD codes

Official Mexican Mortality Data contained 11,844,794 cases, of which 92.5% (n = 10,958,756) were excluded based on the age criteria, and 3.1% (n = 368,797) were excluded for not pertaining to an included metropolitan area (final n = 517,241). Derived proxy codes included 16,325 cases from 2005 to 2019 (0.14% of the original dataset). In 2005, based on the derived proxy ICD codes, the national proxy overdose rate was 4.14 per 100,000, and 6.92 per 100,000 in 2019. In contrast, the official overdose rate (based on standard overdose-specific codes) was 0.31 per 100,000, and 0.37 per 100,000 in 2019. According to our proxy estimates, there were 28,962 additional overdose deaths in Mexico between 2005 and 2019.

In 2005, the rates of overdose based on ICD-10 standard overdose-specific codes were 0.14 per 100,000 in Argentina, 0.36 per 100,000 in Chile, 0.26 per 100,000 in Colombia, 0.24 per 100,000 in Brazil, and 14.67 per 100,000 in the US. By 2019, these rates changed to 0.08 per 100,000 in Argentina, 0.37 per 100,000 in Chile, 0.82 per 100,000 in Colombia, 0.93 per 100,000 in Brazil, and 34.26 per 100,000 in the US. Based on the derived proxy ICD codes, estimates in 2005 were recorded at 1.18 per 100,000 in Argentina, 2.25 per 100,000 in Chile, 3.77 per 100,000 in Colombia, 4.30 per 100,000 in Brazil, and 1.55 per 100,000 in the US; in 2019 estimates were 1.15 per 100,000 in Argentina, 2.44 per 100,000 in Chile, 2.61 per 100,000 in Colombia, 4.29 per 100,000 in Brazil, and 1.22 per 100,000 in the US. Discussion

Using both public and previously unpublished data obtained through freedom of information requests, we identified a set of ICD codes that were statistically more common in decedents with positive toxicology for illicit substances commonly implicated in deaths internationally (e.g., opioids, methamphetamine, cocaine, or benzodiazepines). We estimated crude overdose mortality rates with our derived proxy codes. We sought to contextualize these rates through comparison to the US and other Latin American countries. These data suggest an otherwise hidden drug-related death epidemic in Mexico, although overdose rates in the country are still far below the observed rates for the US. In the international comparison, we show that deaths measured with standard overdose and proxy codes were rarely reported in Latin America for the analyzed age group. Yet, the rates of deaths identified using proxy codes were severalfold higher in Mexico than the comparator Latin American countries and the US.

Of note, the slope of the curve of our estimated overdose crude rate for Mexico mirrors the slope of the curve for the overdose rate in the US after the introduction of illicitly manufactured fentanyl, suggesting that similar dynamics may be unfolding in Mexico. The increase in overdoses in Mexico related to illicitly manufactured fentanyl in the drug supply has been corroborated by community-based organizations and researchers since 2018 [24–26]. In contrast, estimated overdose mortality rates for other Latin American countries remained relatively flat. These stable patterns may reflect the expected trend for the underlying conditions associated with our identified proxy codes in the absence of substantial underreporting and misclassification, underscoring the likelihood that excess increases observed in Mexico are attributable to drug-related causes rather than changes in the true incidence of these medical conditions. Interestingly, Brazil had the proxy rates closest to Mexico, where cocaine, benzodiazepine, and opioid use is prevalent [27].

The codes we identified in our analysis as overdose proxy codes are consistent with histopathological outcomes that would be expected with overdose deaths. Our study finding suggests that anatomical findings were likely registered as the cause of death in lieu of drug overdose as the underlying cause. Many of the anatomical causes that were identified are on the pathophysiological pathway from drug overdose or toxicity to death. For example, opioid-related overdoses will lead to acute respiratory failure and may be complicated by pulmonary edema [28, 29]. Stimulant drug overdoses can precipitate myocardial infarction by either reducing myocardial oxygen supply or increasing demand [30].

The reliance on other codes, such as those found in our analysis as derived proxy ICD codes, instead of the standard overdose-specific ICD-10 codes may be attributed to several factors. Legal procedures in Mexico limit medical examiners from performing or reporting toxicological examinations unless mandated by judicial authorities, such as in the case of a suspected homicide. Often, there is a shortage of the necessary reagents and equipment for quantitative toxicological analyses, which restricts medical examiners’ ability to conclusively determine overdose as the cause of death [13]. Medical examiners may sometimes file death certificates before performing a toxicological analysis due to time constraints. Insufficient training among personnel responsible for filling out death certificates may also contribute to the frequent use of broader, less specific ICD-10 codes [31]. The constraints imposed by limited time, resources, and training at forensic facilities are exacerbated by a significant increase in homicides since 2008, further straining already scarce resources and compelling medical professionals to prioritize rapid case processing over detailed toxicological assessments [32]. As of 2021, there were over 52,000 unidentified bodies in the country, the majority of which were buried in mass graves [33].

Regional disparities in overdose mortality rates across Mexico not only align with the expected drug use hotspots but also highlight areas previously unrecognized for high overdose deaths [34, 35]. In 2019, metropolitan areas along the US-Mexico border, including Mexicali, Reynosa, Matamoros, Nuevo Laredo, and Hermosillo, recorded some of the highest overdose rates based on our analysis. This pattern aligns with the known prevalence of opioid use in this region, where higher overdose rates are expected [26]. Emerging hotspots in areas not previously associated with high overdose deaths, such as Mexico’s central region (Cuernavaca, Toluca, and Puebla-Tlaxcala), central-west (Puerto Vallarta and Colima-Villa de Álvarez), southeastern region (Córdoba, Poza Rica, Xalapa), suggest a different landscape than previously known. These areas are less impacted by opioid use but may be experiencing rising overdose deaths due to the widespread availability of methamphetamine [36]. Unlike opioids, methamphetamine has a broader geographic distribution across the country, potentially explaining the unexpected high number of overdose deaths found in our analysis in this and other regions of the country [37].

Limitations

There are several limitations to our findings. The identified proxy codes may only provide an approximation of the overdose burden in Mexico, as these deaths cannot be definitively confirmed as overdoses. Our approach for estimating mortality rates using proxy codes assumed that all deaths coded under proxy conditions represented potential overdoses, although only a minority tested positive in available toxicology data. Omitting this misclassification proportion in the calculation may have resulted in substantial overestimation of true overdose mortality. The age group selected (20–39 years old) is limited, and we doubt the proxy codes could be used reliably in older age groups as the true mortality from these medical causes of death increase with age. We limited our analyses before 2020 due to the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, as respiratory insufficiency and pulmonary edema could be used to code COVID-related deaths, increasing the number of identified cases but decreasing the likelihood that these would be related to overdose. The data obtained from freedom of information requests were limited to a small number of states, and although representative of every region, our estimates may not be generalizable to the entire nation. The variability in screening practices and access to resources across state-based forensic laboratories likely contributes to underreporting or misclassification of certain substances, thereby affecting the accuracy and comparability of our overdose mortality estimates.

Conclusion

We provide an estimate of crude overdose mortality rates in Mexico and metropolitan areas from 2005 to 2019 while underscoring the urgent need for comprehensive forensic investigations into drug-related deaths. Recently, Mexican coroners were trained in Canada and the US with the intent of improving the quality and accuracy of forensic practices in Mexico [31]. Interventions such as this should continue, and their efficacy should be measured. Different levels of government must equip state-based medical examiner facilities with the necessary resources to conduct qualitative screening and quantitative confirmatory toxicological analyses. Coordinated efforts among all parties involved in investigating causes of death and establishing minimum standards for detecting specific substances in forensic toxicology services are essential for accurately categorizing overdose deaths and ensuring the proper completion of death certificates. Systematic reporting of toxicological analysis results should be conducted, and these findings ought to be made publicly available. Finally, only widely used substances have their own ICD codes (e.g., cocaine, amphetamine, opioids), leaving out all new psychoactive substances (e.g., mephedrone, NBOMe), including those like xylazine that have recently been detected in Mexico [38]. Recent efforts by the Mexican government to include a specific code for fentanyl may prove insufficient [39], as this substance is primarily used in the northern region of the country, and not all services have the necessary materials to test for it.
