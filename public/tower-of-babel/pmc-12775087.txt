Comparison of novel and traditional anthropometric indices: which is the best indicator of frailty in older adults?
Authors: Aijun Song, Siying Yu, Taotao Huang, Jian Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty is a critical geriatric syndrome associated with adverse health outcomes and exhibits a complex relationship with obesity. Traditional anthropometrics, such as body mass index (BMI), inadequately assess obesity-related risks in the aging population. Emerging indices better capture visceral adiposity, potentially enhancing frailty prediction. This study evaluates these indices’ associations with frailty in older adults.MethodsThis study analyzed existing data collected between 2002 and 2007 as part of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). BMI, body roundness index (BRI), a body shape index (ABSI), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), and waist/height0.5 (WHT·5R) were calculated using waist circumference, weight, and height. We used a modified frailty index (FI) with 36 standardized parameters to quantify the severity of frailty by the cumulative proportion of deficits.ResultsIn the adjusted model, increases in all indices except ABSI were statistically significantly associated with increases in FI (BRI: β(95%CI) = 0.004(0.003, 0.005); BMI: β(95%CI) = 0.001(0.0001, 0.001); ABSI: β(95%CI) = 2.034(1.627, 2.440); WHtR: β(95%CI) = 0.093(0.067, 0.119); WHT·5R: β(95%CI) = 0.007(0.005,0.009)). Bootstrap analysis showed that BRI had a greater impact on FI than BMI (difference = 0.0019), while WHtR and WHT·5R had a stronger impact on FI than BRI (difference = −0.0746 and −0.0027, respectively). The effect of WHtR on FI was significantly higher than that of WHT·5R (difference = 0.0719).ConclusionThis study highlighted that WHtR was more strongly associated with FI than BMI, BRI, and WHT·5R, and highlighted the priority of waist-centered obesity measures in frailty risk assessment and clinical intervention in the elderly.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03293-0.

Introduction

Frailty, a complex geriatric syndrome marked by reduced physiological reserve and increased susceptibility to stress, has grown into a significant public health issue in older adults [1]. Frailty occurs when individuals are unable to withstand multiple stressors, or stressors like healthy individuals, resulting in imbalances in the regulation of multiple physiological processes. Frailty is linked to a higher prevalence of negative health outcomes, encompassing, but not restricted to, mortality, disability, and depression [2, 3]. With the global population of older adults projected to double by 2050, identifying modifiable risk factors and early predictors of frailty has become a public health imperative [4]. Obesity affects over 40% of older adults in high-income countries and presents a paradoxical relationship with frailty [5]. Increasing evidence demonstrates that obesity elevates frailty risk [5, 6], challenging the traditional characterization of frailty as a “wasting disorder”. Adipose tissue-driven chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and sarcopenic obesity may accelerate frailty pathogenesis [7]. Anthropometric indices, such as body mass index (BMI), body roundness index (BRI), a body shape index (ABSI), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), and waist/height0.5 (WHT·5R), have gained attention as potential predictors of age-related health deterioration. The most widely used method to measure obesity is BMI, but it is an imperfect measure of obesity. BMI serves as a general proxy for excessive adiposity, but it fails to account for critical distinctions between adipose and lean tissue composition or the proportional distribution of central versus peripheral fat depots [8, 9]. Findings from a cohort analysis indicated that abdominal obesity shows a heightened correlation with frailty occurrence [10]. Emerging metrics like WHtR and BRI, which integrate waist circumference and height, theoretically offer superior precision in capturing visceral fat accumulation, a key mediator of age-related health risks [11–13]. Although BMI is generally considered suboptimal for older adults due to its inability to differentiate between fat mass and lean tissue, the implementation of advanced body composition assessment techniques remains logistically challenging in most clinical settings [14]. Investigating associations between frailty and easily assessable anthropometric indices is critically important. These metrics are pragmatic and routinely measured in clinical practice. Few studies have focused on new anthropometric measures in relation to frailty. Zhang et al. showed for the first time that BRI is more closely associated with frailty than BMI in adults aged 40 and older [15]. However, this study did not specifically examine older adults—a high-risk group for frailty—or comprehensively integrate anthropometric measures to elucidate their associations with geriatric frailty.

Our study aimed to confirm the associations between these emerging anthropometric indices and frailty in older adults, as well as to elucidate which metric demonstrates the strongest association for frailty risk in this population. This study will enhance the understanding of the clinical relevance of anthropometric indices in geriatric frailty assessment and give evidence-based recommendations for optimizing early intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Study sample

The study was based on data collected between 2002 and 2007 as part of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). NHANES employs complex sampling methodology to obtain nationally representative health statistics from non-institutionalized U.S. residents. The survey systematically collects multidimensional data encompassing demographic characteristics, nutritional intake patterns, socioeconomic indicators, and comprehensive health metrics through standardized interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory testing. We excluded participants with missing information on the frailty index (FI) (N = 20,474), waist circumference (WC) (N = 57), height and weight (N = 12), covariates (N = 502), and an age of less than 60 years (N = 1,612), leaving 5,213 participants for the analysis (Fig. 1).

BRI, BMI, ABSI, WHtR, and WHT⋅5R

Three key anthropometric parameters—WC, weight, and height—were systematically recorded at specially equipped mobile assessment centers. BRI was calculated as 364.2 − 365.5 × √ (1 − [WC in centimeters / 2π]2 / [0.5 × height in centimeters]2) [11]. BRI was separated into four groups according to quartiles since there were no set reference values. Weight (kg) and height (m) were used to determine BMI. Using standard BMI categorization thresholds, the cohort was stratified into four distinct categories: 18.5–24.9 kg/m², 25.0–29.9 kg/m², 30.0–34.9 kg/m², and ≥ 35.0 kg/m² [16]. ABSI was derived from the standardized formula: 1,000 × WC (m) × [weight (kg)] ^ (2/3) × [height (m)] ^ (-5/6) [17]. Four groups were also divided according to quartiles. WHtR, calculated as WC divided by height. Central obesity was operationally defined as having a WHtR reaching or exceeding the 0.5 threshold [18]. Nevill et al. [19] developed an enhanced anthropometric index, WHT·5R, which demonstrated height-independence when normalizing waist circumference measurements across individuals of varying stature.

Outcome: frailty

In our NHANES analysis, we adapted a frailty index methodology from prior investigations, incorporating 36 standardized variables that remained consistent across all study cohorts [20, 21]. This modified approach excluded 10 of the original 46 parameters, specifically those associated with dietary consumption or nutritional biomarkers. The frailty quantification was computed through proportion calculation, where the count of observed deficits was divided by the total measured parameters. The resultant index values spanned a continuum from 0 (no frailty) to 1 (maximum frailty), with ascending values denoting increased frailty severity (Table S1).

Covariables

Age, gender (male and female), race (hispanic, non-hispanic white, non-hispanic black, and other), marital status (yes and no), education (less than 9th grade, 9th-11th grade, high school graduate, some college, and ≥ college graduate), family income-to-poverty ratio (PIR) (PIR < 1 and PIR ≥ 1), smoking status (yes and no), alcohol consumption (yes and no), working status (yes and no), and physical activity level (moderate and vigorous). According to previous studies, these variables potentially influence the association of the independent variables and frailty.

Statistical analyses

The NHANES initiative employs a stratified multistage probability sampling methodology to ensure nationally representative demographic coverage. This survey framework features a four-tiered selection process progressing successively through counties, geographic clusters, residential units, and ultimately individual participants. The WTMEC2YR/3 composite weighting variable was systematically applied to maintain analytical validity. Linear relationships between exposure and outcome variables were observed by scatter plots. Variance inflation factor (< 2.0) was used to test for collinearity between variables based on previous literature. Bivariate analyses were assessed via ANOVA was employed for assessing continuous covariates across quartiles of BRI. Categorical variables were assessed across BRI quartiles using χ² tests. Separate linear regression analyses were conducted to examine FI associations with each anthropometric parameter (BRI, BMI, ABSI, WHtR, WHT⋅5R). Unadjusted statistical frameworks specifically evaluated these Anthropometric measures without covariate control. Adjusted models included the covariates of gender, race, marital status, education, age, smoking status, alcohol consumption, working status, family income-to-poverty ratios, and physical activity level. Bootstrap analysis assessed the variance and 95% confidence interval (CI) of the differences in coefficients between different body composition indices (BRI, BMI, ABSI, WHtR, WHT⋅5R). A statistically significant difference between coefficients was established when the confidence interval (CI) excluded zero (i.e., p < 0.05). The independent variables were categorized into gender-specific quartiles, with all statistical coefficients computed by contrasting each subsequent quartile group (Q2 through Q4) against the baseline reference category (Q1).

Survey weighting methodology was implemented for all analytical procedures, including both statistical computations and demographic evaluations. The study employed R software (version 4.3.1) for data processing, with a predetermined significance threshold of p < 0.05 used for hypothesis testing.

Discussion

This study integrated traditional and novel obesity indicators to assess their associations with frailty. Our results suggest that measures representing abdominal obesity (BRI, WHtR, and WHT⋅5R) may be more strongly associated with frailty than BMI. Our findings were similar to those of Zhang et al., in that BRI was more strongly associated with FI than with BMI [15]. However, on this basis, we further explored other indicators, and WHtR showed more significant power. Consistent with previous studies, WHtR [22–25] and BRI [26, 27] serve as phenotypic markers of both general adiposity and abdominal obesity, demonstrating clinical utility in identifying patients with shorter stature who exhibit abdominal adiposity for predicting adverse health outcomes. Previous comparative studies have found that WHtR and BRI demonstrate comparable predictive performance in assessing cardiovascular disease risk [28], insulin resistance [29], and metabolic syndrome [30] among adult populations. We found that among older adults, WHtR exerts a greater influence on FI compared to BRI. This discrepancy may be attributed to age-stratified variations in the effects of BRI and WHtR on FI across different demographic cohorts.

The WHtR provides a direct measure of abdominal obesity using a simple calculation of the waist-to-height ratio. In contrast, the BRI integrates both waist circumference and height into a mathematically optimized model, enhancing its sensitivity to whole-body composition and fat distribution patterns. As an indicator of central obesity, WHtR is linked to chronic inflammation, endocrine imbalance, and insulin resistance [31], which are closely associated with frailty. However, BRI not only measures waist circumference and height, but also takes into account the distribution of fat in different parts of the body, as well as some potential protective factors that may be hidden. This might be one of the reasons [32].

The results of Shafran et al. showed that ABSI was more strongly associated with Frailty than BMI in older adults [33]. However, there was no discernible correlation between ABSI and FI in our study. This difference might stem from the different definitions of weakness. Shafran et al. used Fried’s biological phenotype to define weakness (e.g., grip strength, gait speed), whereas the FI incorporates multidimensional clinical and functional deficits. Alternatively, the dependence of ABSI on height factors might have compromised its ability to identify fracture risks within the study population [34, 35]. Obesity and FI were found to be related, which is in line with previous research. Being overweight or obese was found to be significantly associated with FI in a sample of middle-aged and older adults [5, 13, 36]. The relationship between obesity and frailty is multifaceted. Abnormal fat distribution, particularly visceral fat, is associated with metabolic syndrome [37], cardiovascular diseases [38], and diabetes [38, 39], and may increase the risk of frailty. Furthermore, psychological factors also play a significant role, as obesity is often accompanied by depression [40] and social isolation [41], which may further affect the quality of life and lead to frailty. Furthermore, obesity may lead to a decline in muscle contractile function [42] and exercise tolerance, limiting daily functional capacity. The accumulation of these factors increases health deficits, which in turn increase the risk of frailty. Several limitations in this study should be mentioned. To preserve the differences in specific numerical values and distributional characteristics as much as possible, while avoiding masking subtle variations near critical thresholds that could lead to reduced statistical power, we employed a continuous FI rather than a binary dependent variable. Secondly, temporal sequencing and causal inference cannot be established due to the cross-sectional methodology of this study. Thirdly, the samples provided are exclusively from the US population. Consequently, the findings necessitate prudent extrapolation when applying to other population cohorts. Furthermore, all anthropometric indices are indirect measures of body composition, and residual confounding may persist despite adjustment for numerous covariates. Finally, the community-based nature of NHANES may introduce survivorship bias, potentially underestimating the true association.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study suggests that compared to BMI, newer anthropometric indices focused on abdominal adiposity demonstrate stronger associations with frailty. Among these, WHtR showed the most robust association with the FI in this cohort of U.S. older adults. These findings highlight the potential utility of simple waist-based measurements, like WHtR, in the risk assessment of frailty. However, further longitudinal research is necessary to confirm these associations and explore their causal mechanisms before firm clinical recommendations can be made.
