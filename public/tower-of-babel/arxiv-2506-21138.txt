How Good Are Synthetic Requirements ? Evaluating LLM-Generated Datasets for AI4RE
Abdelkarim El-Hajjami, Camille Salinesi

Abstract
The shortage of publicly available, labeled requirements datasets represents a major barrier to advancing Artificial Intelligence for Requirements Engineering (AI4RE) research. While Large Language Models offer promising capabilities for synthetic data generation, systematic approaches for controlling and optimizing the quality of generated requirements remain underexplored. This paper presents Synthline v1, an enhanced Product Line approach for generating synthetic requirements data that extends our earlier v0 version with advanced generation strategies and curation techniques. We investigate four research questions examining how prompting strategies, automated prompt optimization, and post-generation curation affect synthetic data quality across four requirements classification tasks: defects detection, functional vs. non-functional classification, quality vs. non-quality classification, and security vs. non-security classification. Our experimental evaluation demonstrates that multi-sample prompting significantly improves both utility and diversity compared to single-sample generation, with F1-score improvements ranging from 6-44 percentage points. The application of PACE (Prompt Actor-Critic Editing) for automated prompt optimization shows task-dependent results, substantially improving functional requirements classification (+32.5 percentage points) while degrading performance on other tasks. Counterintuitively, similarity-based curation consistently improves diversity metrics but often hurts classification performance, revealing that not all redundancy is detrimental to Machine Learning effectiveness. Most significantly, our results establish that synthetic requirements can match or surpass human-authored requirements for specific classification tasks, with synthetic data achieving superior performance for security (+7.8 percentage points) and defects classification (+15.4 percentage points). These findings provide actionable insights for the AI4RE community and demonstrate a viable path toward addressing dataset scarcity through systematic synthetic data generation.

Introduction

Artificial Intelligence for Requirements Engineering (AI4RE) has emerged as a rapidly evolving research domain, leveraging machine learning (ML) and natural language processing (NLP) techniques to automate and enhance RE tasks
[
5
]
. The effectiveness of these AI-based methods is fundamentally dependent on the availability of high-quality, task-specific datasets that serve as the foundation for training and evaluation

Although generative AI and Large Language Models (LLMs) with in-context learning have reduced reliance on extensive training datasets, achieving optimal performance still requires sufficient high-quality data for fine-tuning
[
11
]
. Additionally, evaluating these new approaches requires adequate benchmarks specific to RE
[

.
However, a fundamental challenge persists within the RE community, as the shortage of publicly available, labeled requirements datasets continues to represent a major barrier to advancing automated RE research
[
6
,
10
,
12
,

]
.
Consequently, addressing this data scarcity has become essential for advancing AI4RE research and enabling effective automated solutions in RE.

Over the past decade, surveys of AI4RE have reported roughly forty open dataset entries
[
14
,
15
]
. However, a close inspection of the supplementary material attached to
[
14
]
—cross-checked with the corpus descriptions summarized in
[

—shows that the number of truly distinct, openly accessible, and ML-ready datasets is far smaller. Many of the cited resources correspond to different versions or repackagings of the same underlying data. The most notable case is the PROMISE family, which includes the original PROMISE corpus, its extended version PROMISE_exp, and the Tera-PROMISE redistribution—all derived from the same base dataset. Once such duplications are consolidated, and after excluding entries linked to dead URLs, restricted-access sites, or raw unannotated sources, the number of genuinely distinct, labeled, and openly reusable datasets falls below twenty. This small core—centering on PROMISE, iTrust, PURE, NASA CM-1 and MODIS, and a small number of security or NFR corpora—accounts for the vast majority of dataset use in AI4RE research. These datasets share several limitations: most are small in scale, typically containing only a few hundred requirements; they commonly have class imbalance, offer limited domain and linguistic diversity as nearly all originate from information system projects and are written exclusively in English, and provide annotations that can be inconsistent—as even trained annotators frequently produce divergent labels when classifying requirements
[
13
]
—which makes reliable benchmarking difficult. The proprietary nature of most industrial requirements adds further barriers, as organizations are generally unwilling to release specifications they consider strategic assets. These limitations point to an urgent need for the AI4RE community to take a data-centric direction, where building high-quality datasets becomes the key to real progress. Such datasets should be large enough, with balanced class distributions, and truly diverse — covering different domains, languages, specification formats, levels, requirement types, and stakeholder perspectives, while staying faithful to the semantics of real requirements. They must also provide consistent, high-quality annotations to enable reliable benchmarking, while minimizing unintended biases that could distort model behavior. Prioritizing data quality over marginal improvements in modeling techniques is essential to enable reproducible, trustworthy, and practically useful AI4RE systems.

Synthetic data generation using Large Language Models (LLMs) emerges as a viable solution to mitigate this data scarcity in AI4RE. Recent advances in LLMs enable the automated creation of large volumes of requirements-like text that can be tailored to specific tasks or domains through prompt engineering. This approach promises not only to alleviate the dependence on proprietary industrial datasets but also to improve coverage of underrepresented domains, requirement types, and linguistic properties.
To the best of our knowledge, our prior work was the first to systematically apply LLM-based synthetic data generation in the context of RE
[
1
]
. We introduced the first version of Synthline (v0), a Product Line (PL) approach for controlling synthetic requirements data generation. Synthline v0 applied systematic variability management to configure key properties of generated data via a Feature Model (FM). Our empirical evaluation demonstrated that models trained exclusively on Synthline-generated data consistently outperformed models trained solely on human-authored data in both precision and recall. Specifically, synthetic data enabled improvements of up to +37% in precision and +30% in recall compared to the real-data-only baseline. The best results were obtained through hybrid training strategies that combined real and synthetic data, achieving precision gains of up to +85% and a doubling of recall relative to models trained exclusively on real data. However, this initial study also revealed important challenges: the synthetic data exhibited lower diversity than real data, and combining synthetic sources indiscriminately sometimes degraded classifier performance. These findings highlighted the need for more advanced generation control techniques and systematic post-generation curation to improve the quality and utility of synthetic data.

This paper presents the second iteration within our Design Science Research (DSR) methodology
[
2
]
, extending Synthline with enhanced generation and curation strategies aimed at optimizing synthetic data quality.
Building upon our initial findings, this iteration focuses on optimizing the generation process itself. We investigate how different prompting strategies including Prompt Actor-Critic Editing (PACE)
[

and post-generation curation influence the quality—defined by diversity and utility—of synthetic requirements data. Specifically, we address the following research questions:

•

RQ1:

•

RQ2:
How does the application of PACE-based prompt optimization influence the quality of the resulting synthetic data?

•

RQ3:
How does a similarity-based curation affect the quality of the resulting synthetic data?

•

RQ4:
To what extent can synthetic requirements replace or surpass human-authored requirements for classification tasks?

3
The Synthline v1 Design

This research follows the DSR methodology
[
2
]
, with iterative design and evaluation of artifacts. This paper presents the second iteration of Synthline, building on insights from the initial version
[
1
]
. We retain the PL-based control approach, extending it with new generation strategies and a curation approach.

Synthline employs a systematic configuration-based approach where synthetic requirements are generated by LLMs through prompts whose parameters can be entirely controlled by the user. These configuration parameters are formally specified using an FM in which each variable configuration parameter (such as domain, language, or stakeholder type) is defined as a feature, with all features organized hierarchically and linked by dependencies and constraints that ensure semantically correct parameter value combinations. Synthline v1 offers 18 parameters for controlling synthetic requirements generation; these parameters are formalized in a tree-structured model consisting of 4 main feature groups organized across 3 hierarchical levels with cross-cutting dependencies and alternative group constraints, enabling the systematic generation of multiple parameter combinations through a PL engineering approach.

3.1
Synthline v1 Architecture

Synthline v1 extends the original PL-based architecture while preserving its use of an FM as the foundation for controlled and systematic data generation. As illustrated in Figure
1
, the enhanced architecture keeps the end-to-end workflow intact—from the Web UI Configurator to the generation of synthetic data—while introducing a conditional optimization branch that enables the integration of the PACE method detailed in
3.3
.

Figure 1:
High-level architecture of the enhanced Synthline (v1).

The workflow begins with the Web UI Configurator processing user-selected features and generating an FM-based configuration that flows to the Promptline component.
Promptline interprets the FM-based configuration to generate a series of atomic configurations. When users select multiple values for certain features, Synthline systematically creates atomic configurations—individual combinations where each feature has exactly one specific value. This decomposition ensures complete coverage of all possible combinations defined by the user’s multi-selections, with each atomic configuration serving as a distinct, fully-specified context for prompt generation. For each atomic configuration, Promptline constructs a corresponding prompt using standardized templates that support both single-sample and multi-sample generation strategies, based on the
samples_per_prompt
feature, which defines whether each prompt requests one requirement or several (see
3.2
).

If selected by the user, Promptline also orchestrates an automated prompt optimization process by invoking the PACE module. When the default prompting strategy is used, prompts flow directly from Promptline to the Generator. However, when PACE optimization is enabled, Promptline coordinates with the PACE component to optimize the prompts before they proceed to generation. The primary role of Promptline is thus to systematically prepare and manage the prompts that will drive the LLM.

The PACE component operates through bidirectional communication with the LLM Service, performing iterative optimization using the Actor-Critic framework detailed in
3.3
. This process refines the initial prompts generated by Promptline, handling multiple atomic configurations in parallel to maintain systematic coverage of the feature space, while operating asynchronously to enable efficient processing during potentially lengthy optimization cycles.

Once the prompts are finalized—either directly from Promptline or after PACE optimization—the Generator module orchestrates the main data generation phase. It manages large-scale interaction with the LLM Service, using the prepared prompts and operational generation parameters from the user’s configuration to produce the synthetic requirements.

Interaction with various LLMs is handled by the LLM Service component, which acts as a unified client that abstracts the specific communication protocols and API details of different LLM providers.

Finally, the Output Handler is responsible for persisting the generated synthetic data. It takes the structured samples from the Generator and saves them to the file system in the user-specified format.

Compared to Synthline v0, v1 introduces key advancements: (1) the PACE module for automated prompt optimization; (2) multi-sample generation capability, which required enhanced prompt templates and parsing mechanisms to reliably handle structured LLM outputs while maintaining compatibility with single-sample generation; and (3) an enhanced web-based interface that provides real-time feedback and progress tracking during generation and optimization.

The modular architecture ensures that each enhancement can be independently validated and configured, supporting the experimental evaluation posed by our research questions while preserving the controlled variability that distinguishes Synthline from ad-hoc generation methods.

3.3
PACE

To explore advanced prompt engineering techniques for enhancing synthetic data quality, we integrated PACE
[
3
]
into Synthline. PACE is an iterative approach designed to automatically optimize prompts for specific LLM tasks.

As illustrated in Figure
2
, the core PACE loop involves using an LLM in two key roles. An
LLM Actor
executes the current prompt to generate synthetic requirements, while an
LLM Critic
evaluates these generated requirements against the prompt’s objectives and provides textual critiques suggesting improvements. These critiques are then used to automatically update and refine the prompt for the next iteration (Update). This cycle repeats for a predefined number of iterations, aiming to find a prompt that maximizes a specific scoring function based on the quality of the generated outputs.

Figure 2:
The PACE Approach.

In our implementation within Synthline, we employed PACE specifically to optimize prompts for generating diverse sets of requirements. Therefore, we configured the scoring function within PACE to maximize the average cosine distance between the embeddings of the generated requirements within a batch, encouraging semantic diversity.

4.3
Synthetic Data Curation

To investigate RQ3, we applied a curation pipeline to the generated datasets (synthetic_1, synthetic_20, synthetic_20_pace) to create curated_synthetic_* variants. This pipeline involves three steps:

1.

Deduplication:
Removal of exact duplicate requirements.

2.

Similarity Filtering:
Removal of the top 20% most semantically similar samples within the dataset. Similarity is calculated using cosine similarity on Sentence-BERT (all-mpnet-base-v2) embeddings. The average similarity of each sample to all others determines the ranking for removal. We chose to remove the top 20% most semantically similar samples, an admittedly somewhat arbitrary threshold selected to provide a moderate level of curation that would be sufficient to observe measurable effects on diversity metrics while preserving the majority of generated data. This threshold represents a pragmatic choice for our experimental investigation rather than an empirically optimized value. The 20% removal rate ensures sufficient data retention for reliable classifier training while creating a meaningful contrast with the uncurated datasets for comparison purposes.

3.

Class Balancing:
Undersampling the majority class(es) within each dataset to match the number of samples in the minority class, ensuring perfect class balance.

This curation process enhances diversity by reducing redundancy and balancing each dataset to avoid bias toward majority classes.

4.4
Evaluation Setup

We evaluated the generated datasets along two dimensions: diversity and utility.

Diversity Evaluation

We calculated diversity metrics for all dataset variants (real, synthetic, curated) for each classification task. The metrics include:

•

INGF (Inter-sample N-gram Frequency):
This metric assesses lexical and phrase-level diversity by measuring how frequently the same n-grams (contiguous sequences of n words, here typically trigrams) appear across different samples in the dataset. Formally, we compute the average frequency of unique n-grams across all text samples. A high INGF score means the dataset contains repeated phrases across multiple samples, suggesting lower diversity at the expression level. Conversely, a lower INGF score reflects greater lexical variety and less phrase repetition between samples.

•

APS (Average Pairwise Similarity):
This metric captures semantic diversity by computing the average cosine similarity between Sentence-BERT embeddings of text pairs. For each dataset, we compute embeddings for all samples, then calculate cosine similarities between all possible pairs of samples. The APS score is the mean of these pairwise similarities. A higher APS indicates that samples tend to convey semantically similar content, while a lower APS suggests richer semantic diversity — the samples are more different in meaning.

Utility Evaluation

To evaluate the utility of the synthetic data, we adopted the standard train-on-synthetic, test-on-real paradigm. This approach measures how well models trained purely on synthetic data generalize to unseen, real-world examples, providing a direct assessment of the synthetic data’s practical value for downstream tasks.
Specifically, each synthetic dataset variant (e.g., synthetic_1, curated_synthetic_20_pace), classifiers were trained exclusively using that variant. These models, along with a baseline model trained solely on 70% of the available real data, were then consistently evaluated on a held-out test set comprising the remaining 30% of the real data specific to each classification task. This protocol allows for direct comparison of the utility provided by different synthetic data generation strategies relative to each other and the real-data baseline.
We used BERT-base-uncased
[
20
]
as the downstream model for all experiments.
To obtain statistically reliable performance estimates and mitigate the effects of stochasticity inherent in model training, we conducted 5 independent runs for each dataset configuration, varying the random seed for each run.
For each configuration, hyperparameter optimization was performed initially using Optuna
[
21
]
(32 trials) on a 90%/10% stratified train/validation split derived from the first run’s data. This process identified the optimal learning rate, batch size, weight decay, and number of training epochs by maximizing the weighted F1-score on the validation set. These best hyperparameters were then held constant across all 5 runs for that configuration, ensuring consistency while assessing performance variability.
Model performance was quantified using weighted Precision, Recall, and F1-score. The final reported values represent the mean and standard deviation calculated across the 5 independent runs for each configuration. Weighted metrics were selected to provide an unbiased performance measure by accounting for any potential class imbalance within the real test sets.

A more effective approach might involve utility-aware curation that preserves informative examples, regardless of similarity, or techniques targeting high-value examples near decision boundaries.

6
Validity Threats

In our study, we identified several threats to validity across different dimensions.

Internal validity
threats primarily come from our experimental design choices. The selection of GPT-4.1 nano as the sole LLM backend introduces potential model-specific biases, as different LLMs may show varying capabilities in synthetic data generation. Our PACE parameter configuration followed recommendations from the original paper but was not empirically optimized for synthetic requirements generation specifically, potentially limiting the effectiveness of prompt optimization. Additionally, using cosine distance as the PACE scoring function may not align well with classification utility - prompts could generate semantically diverse but low-quality requirements that still receive high optimization scores.

External validity
is limited by several factors. Our evaluation focused exclusively on English-language requirements from software development contexts, limiting generalizability to other languages or domain-specific requirements practices. The four classification tasks, while covering important RE areas, represent only a subset of possible requirements analysis tasks. The real datasets used for evaluation are relatively small and may not reflect the complexity and variability of industrial requirements. Furthermore, our evaluation using BERT-base-uncased as the downstream classifier may not generalize to other ML architectures or more recent language models.

Construct validity
concerns arise from our choice of diversity metrics. The INGF and APS measures, while established, may not capture all aspects of meaningful linguistic and semantic diversity relevant to requirements. Our focus on classification utility, while appropriate for our research questions, represents one specific aspect of synthetic data value in RE.

Conclusion validity
faced threats from the relatively small number of experimental runs (5 per configuration) and the inherent stochasticity in both LLM generation and neural network training. Additionally, our similarity-based curation approach used fixed thresholds (top 20% removal) that were not empirically validated as optimal for each task.

7
Conclusion

This paper presented Synthline v1, an enhanced PL approach for generating synthetic requirements data using LLMs. Through systematic evaluation across four requirements classification tasks, we investigated how different generation strategies and curation techniques affect synthetic data quality.
Our key findings show that
generating multiple requirements per prompt substantially improves both utility and diversity
compared to single-sample generation, with F1-score improvements of 6-44 percentage points across tasks. This approach appears to help LLMs generate more coherent and varied requirement sets.

PACE-based prompt optimization produced mixed results
- it consistently improved phrase-level diversity but had task-dependent effects on classification performance. While PACE dramatically helped functional requirements classification (+32.5 percentage points F1-score), it hurt performance on security and defects tasks. This suggests that diversity-focused optimization doesn’t universally improve classification utility.
Surprisingly,
similarity-based curation improved diversity but often hurt classification performance
. This reveals that removing similar examples may actually remove valuable training information, challenging the assumption that higher diversity automatically means better utility for ML.
Most importantly,
synthetic requirements can match or exceed human-authored requirements for certain classification tasks
. Synthetic data achieved better performance for security (+7.8 percentage points F1-score) and defects classification (+15.4 percentage points), offering a scalable solution to dataset scarcity in RE research.

Future work
should explore better alignment between prompt optimization objectives and downstream task performance, investigate curation techniques that preserve informative examples, and extend evaluation to additional RE tasks beyond classification. Cross-linguistic studies and industrial case studies would strengthen the generalizability of these findings.
