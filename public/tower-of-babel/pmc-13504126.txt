Prevalence and determinants of obesity among women aged 15-49 years in the Ashanti region, Ghana: Analysis of the 2022 Ghana demographic and health survey
Authors: Godfred Darko, Norbert Dampaak Satun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity among women of reproductive age is an increasing public health concern and contributes to the burden of non-communicable diseases globally. Rapid urbanisation and lifestyle transitions may be contributing to rising obesity levels among women. However, limited region-specific evidence exists in the Ashanti Region. This study assessed the prevalence and determinants of obesity among women aged 15-49 years in the Ashanti Region using data from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS).MethodsThis cross-sectional study analysed secondary data from the 2022 GDHS. A total of 578 women aged 15-49 years residing in the Ashanti Region were included after excluding pregnant women and respondents with missing anthropometric data. Survey-weighted descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and multivariable logistic regression were used to account for the complex sampling design. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.ResultsThe prevalence of obesity among women aged 15-49 years in the Ashanti Region was 22.0%. In the bivariate analysis, obesity was significantly associated with age, household wealth status, place of residence, employment status, and parity. After adjusting for potential confounders, women from poor households had significantly lower odds of obesity compared with those from rich households (AOR = 0.27, 95% CI: 0.10-0.74). Women residing in urban areas had higher odds of obesity compared with rural residents (AOR = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.13-3.55). Additionally, women with no children had significantly lower odds of obesity compared with women with three or more children (AOR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.13-0.74).ConclusionApproximately one in five women of reproductive age in the Ashanti Region was obese. Obesity was independently associated with household wealth, urban residence, and parity. These findings highlight the need for context-specific interventions addressing urban lifestyle changes, socioeconomic inequalities, and reproductive-related factors to reduce obesity risk among women in the region.

Introduction

Obesity is a major public health problem and a leading contributor to the global burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs). 1 It is associated with cardiovascular disease, hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, several cancers, musculoskeletal disorders, and premature mortality. 2 In 2022, the World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that more than 1 billion people worldwide were living with obesity. 3 Among women of reproductive age, obesity is also associated with infertility, gestational diabetes, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, caesarean delivery, and adverse birth outcomes.4,5 The rising prevalence of obesity therefore has important implications for both population health and maternal health outcomes. 6

Over the past three decades, obesity has increased substantially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). 7 This rise has been linked to rapid urbanisation, declining levels of physical activity, increased consumption of processed and energy-dense foods, and changes in daily lifestyles. 8 Although obesity was previously considered a major challenge in high-income countries, many LMICs are now facing a growing obesity burden while still dealing with infectious diseases and undernutrition. 9 This coexistence of obesity and undernutrition reflects the ongoing nutrition transition occurring in many developing settings. 10 Recent evidence suggests that economic growth, changing food environments, and shifts in dietary behaviours have contributed to increasing obesity rates in LMICs. 11 As a result, obesity has become an emerging public health concern, particularly because of its contribution to the rising burden of non-communicable diseases. 12

In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), obesity is increasingly occurring alongside persistent undernutrition, creating a double burden of malnutrition. 13 Recent evidence shows rising overweight and obesity prevalence among women across several SSA countries. 14 Urban growth, dietary changes, economic transition, and sedentary lifestyles have been linked to this pattern 15 Previous studies in SSA have consistently reported higher obesity prevalence among women living in urban areas and among individuals from wealthier households. 16 Cultural perceptions that associate larger body size with beauty, fertility, and social status may also contribute to obesity risk among women in some African settings. 17

Ghana has undergone substantial demographic, economic, and lifestyle changes over recent decades. 18 These changes have contributed to increasing obesity prevalence among women. 19 Evidence from previous Ghana Demographic and Health Surveys (GDHS) shows a steady increase in overweight and obesity among women aged 15-49 years. 20 The 2014 GDHS reported that nearly 43% of women were overweight or obese, 21 while obesity alone affected nearly 15% of women nationally. 22 Increasing urbanisation, reduced physical activity, dietary changes, and socioeconomic inequalities have been identified as important factors associated with obesity in Ghana. 23 The growing burden of obesity is concerning because of its contribution to hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and other NCDs that place increasing pressure on the health system. 24

The Ashanti Region is one of Ghana’s most populous and rapidly urbanising regions and serves as an important commercial and economic hub. 25 It includes highly urbanised areas such as Kumasi, as well as peri-urban and rural communities with different socioeconomic conditions and lifestyle patterns. 26 These differences in living environments, work-related activities, transportation practices, and access to food may be associated with obesity risk among women in the region. 27

Despite the increasing burden of obesity in Ghana, limited evidence exists on the prevalence and factors associated with obesity among women of reproductive age at the regional level. Previous studies have largely focused on national estimates or selected urban populations, which may not fully capture the variations in socioeconomic conditions, lifestyles, and environmental factors within specific regions. Recent evidence on obesity patterns in the Ashanti Region remains limited. Understanding the factors associated with obesity in this setting is important for identifying vulnerable groups and developing interventions that respond to local needs.

Our study assessed the prevalence and determinants of obesity among women aged 15-49 years in the Ashanti Region using data from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS). The findings may help inform targeted interventions and policies aimed at reducing obesity and related non-communicable diseases among women in Ghana.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a cross-sectional analytical design using secondary data from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS).

Study population and setting

The Ashanti Region, one of Ghana’s sixteen administrative regions is located in the southern part of the country. 28 It is the most populous region and comprises a mix of highly urbanised areas such as the Kumasi Metropolis, as well as peri-urban and rural districts. 29 The region is socioeconomically diverse, with variations in education, income, occupation, and access to health services. 30

The study population comprised women aged 15-49 years residing in the Ashanti Region who participated in the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS). 31 This age group represents women of reproductive age as defined by the DHS. 32 The analysis was restricted to women in this age range to obtain region-specific estimates of obesity and its associated factors.

Study sample and eligibility criteria

The study utilised secondary data from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS). A total of 1,131 women aged 15-49 years residing in the Ashanti Region were identified from the dataset. Respondents were excluded if they were pregnant at the time of the survey (n = 68), as pregnancy-related physiological changes can influence Body Mass Index (BMI) and may not reflect usual obesity status. 33 In addition, women with missing or invalid anthropometric measurements required for BMI calculation and other incomplete records (n = 485) were excluded, as obesity status could not be reliably determined. 34 After applying these exclusion criteria using a complete-case approach, the final analytic sample consisted of 578 women (Figure 1).Figure 1.Flow diagram of study sample selection from the 2022 GDHS.

Flow diagram of study sample selection from the 2022 GDHS.

Data source and sampling technique

Data were obtained from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS), a nationally representative household survey conducted by the Ghana Statistical Service (GSS) in collaboration with ICF, which provided technical assistance through The DHS Program. 35 The GDHS employed a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design. In the first stage, enumeration areas were selected using probability proportional to size (PPS). In the second stage, households were systematically selected within each cluster. This design ensured representativeness at national and regional levels and enabled reliable subgroup analyses.

Study variables

The outcome variable was obesity, assessed using Body Mass Index (BMI) obtained from the 2022 individual recode dataset. In the GDHS dataset, BMI is stored in variable v445, which represents BMI multiplied by 100. The v445 variable was divided by 100 to obtain the actual BMI values in kg/m2. BMI (v445/100) was used as provided in the GDHS dataset. In accordance with classification criteria, obesity was defined as BMI ≥30 kg/m2. 36 For the analysis, respondents were coded as obese (1) and non-obese (0).

Selection of independent variables was guided by established evidence on determinants of obesity in low- and middle-income settings and by the availability of harmonised indicators in the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS), ensuring both conceptual relevance and analytical feasibility.

Age was categorised into four groups (15-24, 25-34, 35-44, and 45-49 years), reflecting the reproductive age range defined in the GDHS. 37 Educational attainment was grouped as no formal education, primary, secondary, and tertiary. Marital status was classified as never married, married or cohabiting, and previously married (divorced, separated, or widowed). Household wealth was categorized based on tertiles of the DHS wealth index factor score (v191): poor (lowest tertile), middle (middle tertile), and rich (highest tertile).

Place of residence was defined as urban or rural. Employment status referred to current working status at the time of the survey and was categorised as employed or unemployed. Parity was defined as the total number of children ever born and grouped as no children, one to two children, and three or more children. 38 Media exposure was defined as exposure to at least one mass medium (radio, television, or print media) and classified as exposed or not exposed. 39 Religion was grouped as Christianity, Islam, or other affiliations.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 27. The Complex Samples module was used to account for the multistage sampling design of the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS), incorporating sampling weights, clustering, and stratification in all analyses. The complex sample plan file incorporated primary sampling units (clusters), stratification variables, and sampling weights as specified in the DHS recode manual. Sampling weights (v005/1,000,000) were applied in line with DHS analytical guidelines to ensure nationally and regionally representative estimates.

A subpopulation (domain) analysis approach was used within the Complex Samples procedure to correctly estimate standard errors for the study population without altering the original survey design. All estimates, standard errors, and confidence intervals were therefore survey-adjusted.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the socio-demographic characteristics of respondents and the prevalence of obesity. Categorical variables were presented as weighted percentages with corresponding unweighted frequencies (n).

Bivariate analysis was conducted using survey-adjusted chi-square tests to assess associations between obesity and independent variables. Consequently, all variables were included in the multivariable analysis based on theoretical relevance and prior evidence, with no exclusion of variables during model building.

Multivariable binary logistic regression was used to identify independent determinants of obesity, and results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). A complete-case (listwise deletion) approach was used. Respondents with missing values in the outcome variable (BMI) or key covariates were excluded from the analysis. The final analytic sample consisted of 578 respondents after exclusions. Given the use of secondary data, multiple imputation was not performed.

The logistic regression model was specified as:

log (p/(1−p))=β0+β1X1+β2X2+⋯+βnXn , where p represents the probability of obesity, β0 is the intercept, β1 to βn are regression coefficients, and X1 to Xn represent the independent variables included in the model.

Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were reported. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with values ranging from 1.03 to 1.91, indicating no evidence of multicollinearity (Supplementary File 1; Table S1).

Ethical considerations

Permission to access and use the data was granted by the DHS Program. This study involved a secondary analysis of publicly available, anonymised data from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS). During the original GDHS data collection, ethical approval was obtained from the relevant ethical review committees, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before interviews and anthropometric measurements were conducted. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed about the purpose of the survey, confidentiality of their information, and their right to decline participation.

For the present analysis, no additional contact with participants occurred, and no personally identifiable information was available to the authors. The dataset was accessed for research purposes on 6 May 2026 through the DHS Program. The released dataset contains no names, addresses, or direct identifiers, and geographic information is restricted to regional-level identifiers to protect participant confidentiality. All analyses were conducted using anonymised data.

Discussion

Prevalence of obesity

This study found an obesity prevalence of 22.0% among women aged 15-49 years in the Ashanti Region, which indicates that approximately one in five women is obese. This reflects a sustained epidemiological and nutritional transition in Ghana among women of reproductive age. 22 The prevalence observed in this study is slightly higher than earlier national estimates from the 2014 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey, which reported obesity at about 15% among women aged 15-49 years. 40 More recent national analyses using the 2022 GDHS have reported obesity levels ranging from 18% to 20%, depending on age group and analytic approach.41,42 The slightly higher estimate observed in this study may reflect the demographic and socioeconomic characteristics of the Ashanti Region, which is highly urbanised and economically active. These characteristics have been consistently associated with a higher risk of obesity.43,44

Comparable studies have also reported that obesity has increased steadily over time, rising from below 10% in earlier surveys to nearly one-fifth in recent years.45,46 This pattern aligns with broader evidence from low- and middle-income countries where rapid urbanisation and dietary transition have shifted disease profiles from undernutrition to overnutrition. 47 At the global level, the findings are consistent with the increasing burden of obesity in LMICs, where shifts toward sedentary lifestyles and energy-dense diets have accelerated weight gain among women. 48 The current estimate therefore reinforces the view that obesity has become a structural public health challenge rather than an isolated behavioural issue.

Socioeconomic status and obesity

Household wealth was the strongest socioeconomic factor associated with obesity in this study. However, wealth should be interpreted as a marker of broader socioeconomic and environmental conditions rather than a direct explanation for obesity. It may capture differences in food access, dietary patterns, occupational activities, transportation use, and living environments that shape daily lifestyles. 49 Women from wealthier households may have greater access to processed and energy-dense foods, while also being more likely to engage in sedentary occupations or use motorised transport.50,51

The higher odds of obesity among women from rich households compared with those from poor and middle-income households are consistent with previous evidence from Ghana and sub-Saharan Africa.14,40,52 National analyses using Ghana Demographic and Health Survey data have also reported higher overweight and obesity prevalence among women with greater household wealth, particularly in urban areas. 53 This differs from patterns observed in many high-income countries, where obesity is often more common among lower socioeconomic groups.7,54 In the Ghanaian context, greater household resources may coexist with greater exposure to energy-dense foods, sedentary occupations, motorised transport, and other conditions associated with weight gain.

The association between urban residence and obesity observed in this study also points to an important dimension of health inequality. Women living in urban areas may experience greater exposure to commercial food environments, prepared foods, sugar-sweetened beverages, and sedentary forms of work and transport than women in rural settings.55–57 At the same time, opportunities for safe and convenient physical activity may differ across neighbourhoods according to the availability of walkable spaces, recreational facilities, and transport infrastructure. 58 These differences may be relevant in the Ashanti Region, where rapid urbanisation has occurred alongside substantial socioeconomic and lifestyle changes. 26

Although these factors were not directly measured in the present study, they provide context for interpreting the observed associations. The findings do not establish that wealth or urban residence causes obesity. Rather, household wealth and place of residence may capture differences in social, economic, food, and physical environments that were not fully measured in the GDHS. This distinction is important for policy because interventions that focus only on individual dietary choices or physical activity may not adequately address the conditions underlying observed inequalities.

Additionally, our findings also reflect wider nutritional changes occurring in Ghana and other low- and middle-income countries, where traditional dietary patterns increasingly coexist with commercially available foods that are often higher in energy, fats, and sugars.59,60 It also aligns with Ghana’s National Policy for the Prevention and Control of Chronic Non-Communicable Diseases and National Nutrition Policy, which emphasise health promotion, improved nutrition, physical activity, and multisectoral action.61,62

Age and obesity

Obesity prevalence increased with age in the descriptive analysis, and this pattern was partly maintained after adjustment for potential confounders. Women aged 15-24 years had significantly lower odds of obesity than those aged 45-49 years. Although women aged 25-34 and 35-44 years also had lower odds of obesity, these associations were not statistically significant after adjustment. This pattern indicates that obesity was more prevalent among older women, although the observed differences may reflect variations in reproductive history, lifestyle, and socioeconomic circumstances. 37

Similar findings have been reported in Ghana and sub-Saharan African, where younger women consistently have lower obesity prevalence than older women.21,63 The lack of significant differences among the intermediate age groups indicates that age alone may not fully explain obesity risk, as its effects are closely intertwined with socioeconomic circumstances and reproductive factors.64,65

Urban residence, parity, and employment

Urban residence, employment status, and parity were significantly associated with obesity in the bivariate analysis. However, after adjustment for potential confounders, urban residence and the comparison between women with no children and those with three or more children remained statistically significant, whereas employment status was no longer independently associated with obesity. This indicates that some of the crude associations may be explained by differences in other demographic and socioeconomic characteristics. 66

Women residing in urban areas had higher odds of obesity than those living in rural areas. This finding is consistent with evidence from sub-Saharan Africa, where urban populations often have higher obesity prevalence in the context of dietary transition, reduced physical activity, and greater reliance on motorised transport. 67 The observed association should not, however, be interpreted as evidence that urban residence itself causes obesity. Urban residence may instead reflect differences in food availability, transportation, employment patterns, neighbourhood design, and opportunities for physical activity.68,69 The persistence of the association after adjustment suggests that these or other contextual factors not captured in the GDHS may remain relevant. 70 This finding highlights the importance of obesity prevention strategies that extend beyond individual behaviour change to address the broader urban environment. Coordinated action across health services, local government, urban planning, transport, and food systems could help improve access to healthier foods, support active transport, and increase opportunities for safe physical activity.71,72 Such approaches may be important for women in rapidly urbanising LMICs, where socioeconomic and environmental conditions vary substantially across communities. They should be designed to be equitable, context-specific, and sustainable within available local resources.

Women with no children had significantly lower odds of obesity compared with women with three or more children. Several pathways may plausibly explain this association. Repeated pregnancies may be accompanied by gestational weight gain and incomplete postpartum weight loss. 73 Over successive pregnancies, accumulated postpartum weight may contribute to differences in body weight. 74 Childcare responsibilities may also influence physical activity, dietary routines, sleep, and opportunities to engage in weight-management behaviours. 75 In addition, parity is closely related to age, household structure, and socioeconomic circumstances, which may partly overlap with other factors associated with obesity. 74 Women with one to two children also had lower odds of obesity than those with three or more children, although this association was not statistically significant.

These possible pathways should be interpreted cautiously. The cross-sectional design does not establish whether differences in body weight preceded or followed pregnancies, and the GDHS data used in this analysis did not provide direct measures of gestational weight gain, postpartum weight retention, dietary intake, physical activity, or other mechanisms through which parity might be related to obesity. The finding therefore represents an observed association rather than evidence that higher parity causes obesity. Nevertheless, the association suggests that maternal and reproductive health services may provide useful opportunities to support healthy weight management before pregnancy, during pregnancy, and after delivery.

Employment status was significantly associated with obesity in the crude analysis but not after adjustment. This suggests that the initial association may have reflected differences in household wealth, occupation type, income, or working conditions.76,77 These characteristics were not fully captured in the present analysis and may help explain why employment status was no longer independently associated with obesity after adjustment. Employment status alone may provide limited information about the relationship between work and obesity, especially where occupations differ substantially in physical demands, working hours, income, and access to food and transportation.

Education, marital status, religion, and media exposure

No significant associations were observed between obesity and education, marital status, religion, or media exposure in the adjusted model. This finding aligns with recent study by Lartey et al., 78 which reported that the association between educational attainment and obesity is becoming less consistent as nutritional transitions progress and obesity increasingly affects multiple social groups.

The lack of association with media exposure may reflect measurement limitations, as exposure does not capture the nature, intensity, and behavioural influence of health messaging. Similarly, marital status and religion may influence obesity indirectly through household structure, cultural norms, and dietary practices rather than acting as direct determinants.

Taken together, the results indicate that obesity among women in the Ashanti Region appears to be strongly associated with socioeconomic inequality rather than isolated demographic characteristics. The dominance of wealth as an independent predictor highlights the role of consumption patterns, lifestyle transitions, and access to obesogenic environments in shaping current obesity trends. 79

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study utilised data from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS), which employed a nationally and regionally representative sampling design. This enhances the generalisability of the findings to women aged 15-49 years in the Ashanti Region. The use of standardised DHS procedures, including trained fieldworkers, calibrated anthropometric measurements, and objectively measured height and weight data, improved the reliability and comparability of obesity classification across respondents while minimising reporting bias. In addition, the application of complex survey weighting, clustering, and stratification strengthened the validity of the estimates by accounting for the multistage sampling design. These analytical approaches are important in epidemiological research because they reduce sampling bias, improve population-level inference, and ensure that estimates appropriately reflect the underlying population structure. The use of multivariable logistic regression further allowed adjustment for potential confounding factors, providing more reliable estimates of the independent factors associated with obesity.

This study should be interpreted while considering some important limitations. The cross-sectional nature of the GDHS data limits the ability to establish temporal relationships or causal effects. Although several factors were associated with obesity, these findings should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than causal relationships. For example, the relationship between obesity and factors such as employment status and parity may involve complex pathways that cannot be fully assessed using cross-sectional data.

Obesity was assessed using BMI, which is widely used for population-level surveillance because of its simplicity and comparability across studies. However, BMI does not distinguish between fat mass and lean body mass and does not provide information on fat distribution, particularly central obesity, which is more strongly associated with cardiometabolic risk. Some misclassification of obesity status may therefore have occurred. Future studies could strengthen obesity assessment in Ghana by incorporating additional measures such as waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, or other validated indicators of body composition.

The GDHS provides extensive sociodemographic and health information; however, some important behavioural and environmental determinants of obesity were not available. Variables such as dietary intake, physical activity, sedentary behaviour, food environments, and built environment characteristics were not directly measured. Additionally, contextual factors including cultural perceptions of body size, menopausal status, reproductive history beyond parity, and access to preventive healthcare services were not assessed. These unmeasured factors may have contributed to residual confounding and limited the ability to fully explain the pathways influencing obesity risk among women in the Ashanti Region.

Although the GDHS employs rigorous sampling procedures and provides nationally representative data, the present analysis was not based on an independently calculated sample size because it relied on an existing survey dataset. The final analytical sample was determined by the availability of eligible women in the Ashanti Region with complete BMI and relevant covariate information. Consequently, some subgroup analyses may have had limited statistical power, particularly among categories with smaller numbers of respondents. In addition, exclusion of women with missing BMI measurements may have introduced selection bias if excluded participants differed systematically from those included, although survey weighting procedures were applied to account for the complex sampling design.

Finally, this study focused specifically on women aged 15-49 years in the Ashanti Region. Although the 2022 GDHS uses a nationally representative sampling design, the present analysis was restricted to respondents from Ashanti Region and therefore provides region-specific estimates. The findings may be relevant to other urbanising or socioeconomically comparable populations in Ghana, but they should not be assumed to represent women in other regions. This is due to differences in socioeconomic conditions, urbanisation, food environments, and lifestyle patterns, which may influence obesity prevalence and its associated factors.

Implications for policy and practice

The findings have important implications for obesity prevention and health system planning in Ghana. The observed association between household wealth and obesity indicates that prevention strategies should consider the wider social and economic context in which women make health-related decisions. Obesity prevention should be incorporated into existing non-communicable disease (NCD) programmes rather than addressed as an isolated health issue. National and local policies should support interventions that promote healthier diets, active lifestyles, and equitable access to preventive services.

Primary healthcare facilities provide a practical setting for early identification and management of obesity. Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) compounds, health centres, and outpatient clinics can provide routine BMI assessment, lifestyle counselling, and follow-up support. For effective implementation, the health system will require trained frontline healthcare workers, functional weighing scales and stadiometers, standardised counselling approaches, and clear referral pathways for women requiring additional support. Within maternal and reproductive health services, obesity screening and counselling could be incorporated into routine antenatal, postnatal, and family planning contacts rather than delivered as a separate service. Weight and height routinely measured during antenatal and postnatal visits could be used to identify women with obesity, followed by brief counselling on healthy dietary practices, physical activity, appropriate gestational weight gain, and postpartum weight management. Women requiring further assessment or specialised support could be referred through existing pathways to NCD clinics, nutrition services, or other appropriate providers. This approach could strengthen continuity of care while making use of existing health-system structures and reducing the need for additional clinic visits.

Implementation will also require clear coordination among responsible stakeholders. The Ghana Health Service and Ministry of Health can provide leadership for clinical integration, workforce training, and programme monitoring. Local government authorities can support community-based activities, while sectors responsible for agriculture, education, transport, and urban planning can contribute to creating environments that support healthier choices. For example, agricultural policies could improve the availability and affordability of nutritious foods; education programmes could strengthen nutrition literacy and physical activity promotion; transport policies could support walking and other forms of active transport; and urban planning could incorporate safe pedestrian infrastructure, recreational spaces, and healthier community food environments. Coordinating these actions with health-sector interventions would provide a broader response to the socioeconomic and environmental conditions associated with obesity.

Monitoring and evaluation should be included from the beginning of programme implementation. Relevant indicators may include the proportion of women screened for obesity, the availability of counselling services, referral completion, follow-up attendance, and changes in overweight and obesity trends over time. For maternal and reproductive health services, monitoring could additionally assess the proportion of eligible women receiving BMI assessment and documented counselling during antenatal and postnatal care, as well as referral and follow-up completion. These measures can be incorporated into existing health information systems to assess programme coverage and identify areas requiring improvement.

Obesity prevention also requires population-level approaches that extend beyond healthcare facilities. Actions across food systems and community environments are needed to support healthier choices. Strategies may include improving access to affordable nutritious foods, strengthening nutrition education, encouraging physical activity, and developing community spaces that support walking, recreation, and active living. Efforts to promote healthier food environments should involve collaboration between health authorities, food producers, local communities, and relevant government agencies. Such interventions should also consider differences between urban and rural communities and socioeconomic groups to ensure that obesity prevention does not widen existing health inequalities.

The observed association between parity and obesity also highlights an opportunity to incorporate weight-related prevention into reproductive health services. Antenatal and postnatal care provide opportunities to discuss healthy nutrition, appropriate weight gain during pregnancy, breastfeeding practices, and postpartum lifestyle behaviours. Counselling can be provided during pregnancy and continued after delivery to support healthy weight management across the reproductive life course, particularly among women with multiple previous pregnancies. However, these opportunities should be viewed as preventive and supportive rather than as evidence that parity causes obesity, given the cross-sectional nature of the present study.

Experiences from other low- and middle-income countries suggest that obesity prevention is more effective when health services, community interventions, and supportive policies are implemented together.80,81 Future implementation research in Ghana should assess the feasibility, acceptability, cost implications, and sustainability of context-specific obesity prevention approaches. Such evidence will support the development of interventions that are practical, scalable, and responsive to local needs.

Conclusion

This study identified obesity as an important public health concern among women of reproductive age in the Ashanti Region, with approximately one in five women classified as obese. Obesity was independently associated with household wealth, urban residence, and parity. These findings indicate that obesity patterns among women are closely related to socioeconomic, residential, and reproductive characteristics within the study population.

The findings highlight the need for obesity prevention strategies that consider the broader context in which women live. Interventions should not focus solely on individual health education but should also strengthen opportunities for healthier choices through improved access to healthy foods, promotion of physical activity, and integration of obesity screening and counselling into routine healthcare services. Maternal and reproductive health platforms may provide additional opportunities to support healthy weight management among women across different stages of life.

Addressing obesity requires coordinated efforts across multiple sectors, including health, agriculture, education, urban planning, transport, and local government. Future research should explore longitudinal relationships between socioeconomic conditions, urban environments, reproductive factors, and obesity risk. Further studies should also assess the feasibility, effectiveness, and sustainability of context-specific obesity prevention interventions in Ghana.

These findings contribute important evidence on the distribution of obesity among women in Ghana and highlight the need for comprehensive, equitable, and sustainable approaches to obesity prevention in Ghana and other low- and middle-income countries experiencing similar demographic and lifestyle transitions.
