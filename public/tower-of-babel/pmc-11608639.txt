Insurance-funded Breast Reduction in the United States: Ethical Management of Subaxillary Fat Pads
Authors: Ross I. S. Zbar, Lisa D. Taylor, John W. Canady
Published: 2024

Surgeons now understand the axillary tail of Spence not as an extension of breast tissue, but as a distinct fat pad in the upper-outer chest, which can enlarge in individuals with higher body mass.1 This subaxillary fat pad, located near the axilla, is often overlooked in breast reduction surgery discussions but can contribute to patient dissatisfaction if not addressed.

Drivers of dissatisfaction after breast reduction surgery typically include poor scarring, persistent ptosis, or wound breakdown.2,3 However, some patients also express concern over the continued prominence of the subaxillary region.4 Although articles on breast reduction techniques rarely discuss the subaxillary fat pad, many surgeons address this area with liposuction to enhance postoperative satisfaction and maintain a competitive edge.5 The proliferation of social media has led to a misconception that addressing the subaxillary fat pad is automatically included in breast reduction procedures. Managing this fat pad is a separate procedure, requiring its own Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code and informed consent. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays CPT codes from the American Medical Association for procedures, which address the subaxillary fat pads, http://links.lww.com/PRSGO/D684.) Including resected subaxillary fat as part of a breast reduction specimen is misleading. Combining these procedures without proper preauthorization from third-party payors or failure to separately bill the patient for a noncovered procedure exposes the surgeon, anesthesiologist, and facility to liability.

Misrepresenting services can result in negative consequences from state licensing boards and certifying organizations. Cosmetic procedures that enhance appearance without restoring function are not covered by healthcare insurance, whether it is commercial, state (Medicaid), or federal (Medicare, TRICARE). Performing subaxillary fat pad removal during an insurance-financed breast reduction without separate billing constitutes improper bundling of medically necessary and cosmetic procedures if only billing the third-party payor. This breach of agreement with the insurer causes financial harm and leads to claims of performing cosmetic surgery under the guise of reconstructive reimbursement. This holds true regardless of whether the surgeon is in-network or out-of-network with the healthcare plan. Extracting funds under false pretenses can result in administrative, civil, or even criminal proceedings at the state or federal level. Submitting fraudulent claims, which inaccurately represent the services provided, can lead to serious legal consequences.

Financing a surgical revision performed solely for aesthetic improvement by billing insurance is a false claim. In such instances, surgeons may opt to either charge the patient directly or waive their portion of the revision fees. However, anesthesia and facility fees must still be addressed appropriately. Certain plastic surgical procedures—breast reconstruction, cleft work, and rhinoplasty—have significant aesthetic components but are primarily considered medically necessary. These procedures have specific CPT codes recognized by healthcare plans for revisional work. (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays CPT codes from the American Medical Association for procedures that involve revision commonly performed by surgeons, http://links.lww.com/PRSGO/D685.) However, no specific CPT codes or modifiers exist for revision procedures that do not have such recognition. A patient dissatisfied with the postoperative outcome of a breast reduction at the subaxillary roll presents a downstream issue.

Ethical billing practices in plastic surgery, particularly when aesthetic outcomes are a metric, require thorough preoperative discussions, appropriate disclosures, and proper billing to avoid potential legal and professional pitfalls.
