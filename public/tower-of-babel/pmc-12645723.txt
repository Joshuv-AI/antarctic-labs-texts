The effect of the COVID-19 pandemic on racial disparities in suicide and homicide by firearm rates in Missouri
Authors: Samuel Kim, Rithvik Talluri, Audrey Yu, Megan Evans, Apurva Bhatt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMissouri homicide rates rose during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2023), spiking in 2022. Data obtained from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention database showed from 2018 to 2023, Black/African American American youth experienced the most firearm-related homicides before and during the pandemic. This study also found demographic differences between youth firearm suicide and homicide rates in Missouri.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06340-x.Keywords: Pandemic, Firearm-related homicide, Firearm-related suicide, Racial disparity

Background

In 2022, Goldstick and colleagues reported that firearm-related injuries were the leading cause of death among youth in 2020 [1]. Firearm safety has historically garnered national attention, with the prior U.S. Surgeon General Vivek Murthy declaring it a priority in June 2024 [2]. Indeed, public policy has a direct impact on the outcomes for firearm-related injury. In Missouri, public policy has been shown to directly impact firearm-related mortality rates. In the recent work by Bhatt and colleagues, the weakening Missouri’s permit-to-purchase and concealed carry firearm laws contributed to increased rates of firearm suicide in Missouri youth [3].

There is rising public interest in addressing firearm-related mortality, and there must be recognition that this crisis has been shown to disproportionately affect minorities and low-income communities. For instance, African American men face a nearly seven times higher mortality rate from firearm homicide based on age-adjusted mortality rates compared to White men [4]. Mortality rates continue to rise for both groups in the US with differences between groups possibly widening over time [4].

Recently, Stevens and colleagues demonstrated a statistically significant increase in the post-pandemic era for total pediatric firearm-related trauma cases in Colorado [5]. Lopez and colleagues described the focal exacerbation of racial health disparities during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic [6]. Marginalized communities suffered more from dramatic changes in economic and social normatives due to decreased financial flexibilities, which inflamed existing healthcare inequity and access issues [6]. These stressors may contribute to the 37% increase in firearm-related emergency department visits in 2020 compared to 2019 [7]. The effects of COVID-19 and subsequent policies have had a durable impact on marginalized groups.

This study aimed to evaluate trends in firearm-related mortality among youth in Missouri during and after the COVID-19 pandemic in Missouri, with attention to race and cause of death (suicide and homicide by firearms).

Methods

De-identified public population data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) WONDER database on firearm-related deaths in Missouri from 2018 to 2023 was evaluated, focusing on individuals ages 10–24 years old. Mortality rates for suicide by firearm were included by International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes X72 (by handgun) and X73 (by rifle, shotgun, or larger firearm), and mortality rates for homicide by firearm were identified using ICD-10 codes X93 (by handgun) and X94 (by rifle, shotgun, or larger firearm). Race/ethnicity was categorized into six groups (determined by the CDC): American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Black/African American/African American, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, White, and more than one race. Rates are described in deaths per 100,000 individuals between 10 and 24 years old. Mortality rates and counts are suppressed in the WONDER database if low counts are observed (defined by the CDC as less than 9 deaths). The pre-COVID-19 era was defined as 2018–2019, and the COVID-19 era was defined as 2020–2023 for analysis.

Analyses were performed using the XLMiner Analysis ToolPak add-in for Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA). General trends in firearm-related mortality were graphically visualized using the Excel charting functions to generate line graphs and summary figures. Linear regression was conducted with year as the independent variable and race-specific mortality rates and absolute death counts as the dependent variable. Regression analysis was performed for race groups across the study period, in the pre-COVID-19 era, and the post-COVID-19 era if possible. Pearson correlation coefficients were also calculated using XLMiner functions to assess relationships between rates across race groups. Differences between groups were evaluated using two-tailed t-tests. Significant results were defined as a p < 0.05.

Results

Trends over time

Between 2018 and 2023, firearm-related mortality from both suicide and homicide increased in Missouri (Fig. 1). Firearm homicide rates peaked in 2020 and 2022 with mortality rates of 17.5/100,000 and 18.8/100,000, respectively. Firearm suicide rates peaked in 2021 with mortality rates of 10.8/100,000. Pre-COVID-19 and COVID-19 eras did not have a significant difference on mortality rates due to firearms for both suicide (p = 0.16) and homicide (p = 0.24) on two-tail t-tests.

Trends by race

Only Black/African American and White individuals were included in the racial disparity analysis due to suppressed or incomplete data in other race groups due to low counts and crude rates. Crude mortality rates due to homicide from gun violence for White and Black/African American individuals have increased during the study period across both the pre-COVID-19 and COVID-19 eras as seen in Fig. 2. Black/African American individuals have a higher overall crude mortality rate with a crest of 104.2/100,000 in 2022 across time. Differences for homicide mortality rates between White and Black/African American youth have been noted on student’s t-test (p < 0.0001) across both periods. Still, across time, Black/African American mortality rates and White mortality rates generally followed the same trends (R = 0.556). Black/African American individuals made up over 75% of total death counts due to homicides by firearm throughout the study period (Fig. 2). In the pre-COVID-19 era, there was a marginal correlation between Black/African American youth and homicide by firearm mortality rates on two-tailed student’s t-test (p = 0.057). In the COVID-19 era, there was a distinct correlation between Black/African American youth and homicide by firearm mortality rates on two-tailed student’s t-test (p < 0.001).

Patterns in homicide by firearm mortality rates and counts by Race

Both White and Black/African Americanindividuals saw similar increased rates of suicide by firearm over the study period as seen in Fig. 3. Black/African American counts and crude mortality rates for the pre-COVID-19 era could not be analyzed due to data suppression by the CDC. As such, all comparisons between the two rates could only occur with COVID-19 era data. There was a strong correlation with increasing suicide crude mortality rates for both White (R2 = 0.738) and Black/African American (R2 = 0.791) and increasing year as seen in Fig. 3. Strong correlation was seen between Black/African American race and absolute death counts (R2 = 0.787) but not in White race and absolute death counts (R2 = 0.326) across time. In the COVID-19 era, however, a Pearson correlation analysis revealed a strong, inverse association between the two rates (r = −0.75) indicating that crude mortality rates with higher-than-average mortality in the Black/African American group tended to have lower-than-average mortality in the White group. In the COVID-19 era, there was a difference between White and Black/African American youth and suicide by firearm mortality rates on two-tailed student’s t-test (p < 0.01). Suicide mortality rates did not differ significantly for White youth between the pre-COVID-19 and COVID-19 eras (p = 0.608).

Discussion

This study found that in Missouri, firearm-related mortality rates (e.g. homicide, suicide) worsened during the COVID-19 pandemic with encouraging decreasing mortality rates in the late COVID-19 era for both suicides and homicides. Even so, notable racial disparities between Black/African American and White Missourians were described from 2020 to 2023. Black/African American youth experienced higher rates of firearm homicides in the COVID-19 era than White youth. Additionally, Black/African American youth suicides rose dramatically in the COVID-19 era. Even so, White youth also saw rising rates of suicide over time, although, no significant differences were noted between the pre-COVID-19 and COVID-19 suicide by firearm mortality rates in White youth. These findings echo previous research from Rees and colleagues, emphasizing the persistence of racial disparities in firearm-related mortality [8] and the novel reactivity of race-based disparities in Missouri to changing social pressures. Indeed, recent studies have expanded the established disparities in firearm injury including disparities in both mortality and non-fatal injury rates [9]. A large analysis of the CDC Wonder database by Klein-Cloud and colleagues re-established this trend for pediatric patients [10]. Moreover, Klein-Cloud et al. found that rates decreased in 2023 despite escalatory trends between 2020 and 2022 during the COVID-19 pandemic which is reflected in our investigation [10]. Still, the investigation found that firearms continue to be the leading cause of death in children with Black/African American and American Indian and Alaskan Native (AIAN) children disproportionately affected in the US [10]. Our investigation in Missouri found that Black/African American and White children were most heavily impacted by firearm injury. Previous investigations of Missouri-specific mortality rates establish this trend [11]. Although not investigated in our study, the study by Rivara and colleagues demonstrates national patterns for the association of urban environments and Black/African American patients for increased risk of firearm-related death in Missouri as well [11].

It has been well-established that suicide and homicide rates differ between Black/African American and White patients with White youth at a higher risk for suicide and Black/African American youth at a higher risk for homicide [1, 4, 8]. Reportedly, these patterns are heterogenously dispersed among states largely due to differences in policies [12]. Missouri has been shown to have higher homicidal rates than other states [12]. Our investigation found that Missouri firearm-related mortality rates followed similar patterns for pediatric patients. This investigation is the first to demonstrate a persistent association between suicide by firearm and homicide by firearm lethality between White and Black/African American patients respectively through the COVID-19 pandemic. Missouri is not excluded from other national trends in firearm-related injury as well [13]. The study by Bhatt and colleagues demonstrated the effects of Missouri policy on firearm-related mortality rates [3]. Similar effects have been seen in the laxation of other Missouri firearm policies on mortality rates in the state [14, 15]. Missouri faces unique challenges in managing firearm-related deaths due to the variations seen between rural and urban mortality rates [11]. Our investigation supports the enduring effects of structural racism in urban Missouri cities [16]. In fact, our investigation found that the social and environmental changes of the COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated pre-existing disparities in firearm-related homicide rates for Black/African American youth. As such, policies determining the ability to purchase, carry, and maintain firearms in Missouri must be thoroughly examined and implemented in acknowledgement of the sectionality of Missouri residents by race and rurality. In fact, compared to other states, relaxing firearm regulations and significant modifications to social normatives in Missouri may have an exceedingly exacerbated effect on urban Black/African American youth; this effect should be heavily considered in both state-level, municipal-level, and hospital-level policymaking.

Altogether, Missouri policies should shift towards protective outcomes for patients at high risk for firearm-related injury and mortality. These results suggest the need to consider multi-level interventions, including the spectrum of policy changes towards community-based strategies tailored to state-specific and racial and/or ethnic context. Moreover, hospital-based interventions should have a higher emphasis to improve access to proper healthcare for marginalized communities and provide long lasting firearm-related education and prevention. Even so, the most urgent provision is to increase the national and state-level access to healthcare services [17, 18]. In fact, decreased utilization of mental health care resources has been shown to be correlated with increased risk for suicide [17]. Rural communities are considerably disadvantaged in this area [19], and Missouri policies should coordinate incentives to increase hospitals and resulting hospital-based interventions to protect individuals from firearm violence in rural towns. Indeed, our investigation found that White youth is at risk for suicide by firearm disproportionately with previous studies showing that youth in rural areas are at an increased risk of suicide [20]. White youth in rural areas with poor access to mental health services in Missouri are at risk of suicide by firearm. Efforts should be made to increase mental health and primary care services in rural Missouri territories.

As the national recovery from the 2020 pandemic continues, addressing the exacerbation of the ongoing firearm violence will become critical and leveraging existing evidence and programs will provide insurmountable support for diverse and heterogenous people groups at risk for from suicide and homicides from gun violence. Esposti and colleagues argue for a multi-level approach alongside individualistic intervention methods1 [21]. Pediatric and adolescent providers can play a crucial role in reducing firearm injuries via targeted counseling on firearm safety, especially in higher-risk communities. Moreover, community-health programs play an especially vital role in protecting children from firearm injury risk through the education of parents and the dissemination of locks [22]. As the U.S. mobilizes their efforts to prevent death by firearms, understanding these disparities is essential to developing effective interventions. As Dr. Murthy once said in an interview with the Journal of the American Medical Association, “Health is something that historically has brought us together.”[2] Our findings contribute to further understanding racial and/or ethnic disparities in firearm-related deaths in Missouri.
