The Role of b-value Diffusion-weighted Imaging in Differentiating High-grade and Low-grade Brain Tumors: A Comprehensive Study with Standard b-value DWI
Authors: Arezoo Kazemzadeh, Pariya Naseri, Somayeh Shirazinejad, Iraj Abedi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Accurate differentiation between high- and low-grade brain tumors is pivotal for guiding therapy and predicting prognosis. While diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) mapping offer non-invasive insights into tumor microstructures, conventional b-value (~1000 s/mm²) protocols often blur these distinctions, limiting diagnostic specificity. This study systematically investigated the impact of multiple b-values on DWI and ADC values to optimize tumor grade differentiation.Materials and Methods:Forty patients with histologically confirmed brain tumors (18 glioblastoma multiforme, 11 astrocytoma’s, 5 oligodendrogliomas, and 6 ependymomas) underwent MRI between April 2023 and March 2024 at our institution. DWI sequences were acquired at b-values of 0, 500, 1000, 1500, and 2000 s/mm². Three radiologists independently scored the images for quality, lesion conspicuities, anatomical delineations, artifacts, and noise using a 4-point Likert scale. The ADC values were correlated with histopathological grade. Inter-reader reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Cohen’s kappa.Results:High b-values (1500–2000 s/mm²) substantially enhanced lesion conspicuity, especially in low-grade tumors, while preserving the anatomical details. The ADC values were inversely correlated with the tumor grade across all b-values (P < 0.05). The inter-reader agreement was excellent (ICC = 0.82). Compared with conventional b = 1000 s/mm² imaging, higher b-value DWI revealed subtle microstructural differences undetectable at standard diffusion weightings, improving the differentiation between tumor grades.Conclusion:High-b-value DWI markedly improves noninvasive brain tumor grading by enhancing ADC contrast and lesion visibility. Integrating high-b-value protocols into routine MRI can facilitate early and accurate diagnosis, support personalized treatment planning, and potentially transform clinical neuro-oncological practice.

INTRODUCTION

Brain tumors remain a leading cause of neurological morbidity and mortality worldwide, with high-grade gliomas, such as glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), carrying a particularly poor prognosis.[12] Early and accurate differentiation between high- and low-grade tumors is critical for guiding therapeutic decisions, predicting outcomes, and stratifying patients for personalized treatments.[3] However, conventional imaging methods frequently fail to provide sufficient contrast between tumor grades, leading to diagnostic uncertainty and potential treatment delays in patients.[4]

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has become the gold standard for noninvasive brain tumor assessment, offering high-resolution anatomical information. Among advanced MRI techniques, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) uniquely captures the microscopic motion of water molecules within tissues, with the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) serving as a quantitative biomarker for tumor cellularity and grade.[567] Despite its promise, the diagnostic performance of DWI is highly dependent on the choice of b-value, which determines the sensitivity of the sequence to diffusion. Standard clinical protocols typically employ b-values of approximately 1000 s/mm²; however, this setting can lead to signal overlap between high- and low-grade tumors, limiting specificity.[8]

High-b-value DWI has been proposed to improve lesion conspicuity and enhance differentiation between tumor grades by increasing diffusion weighting. Nevertheless, higher b-values introduce challenges, such as a reduced signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and greater susceptibility to artifacts.[91011] Synthetic high-b-value techniques have emerged as a strategy to overcome these limitations without prolonging the scan time; however, systematic evaluations comparing image quality, ADC metrics, and tumor differentiation across multiple b-values remain scarce.[121314]

In this comprehensive study, we systematically assessed DWI and ADC maps acquired at multiple b-values, including standard and high b-value protocols, in patients with histologically confirmed brain tumors. Our aim was to identify the b-value range that optimizes image quality, enhances diffusion contrast, and maximizes the ability to differentiate between high- and low-grade tumors. By clarifying the role of b-value selection, this study provides evidence to guide clinical DWI protocol optimization, ultimately improving diagnostic confidence and supporting more precise and personalized neuro-oncology care.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients

We prospectively enrolled 40 consecutive patients with brain tumors who underwent clinically indicated MRI between April 2023 and March 2024 at our institution. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they had histopathological confirmation of a primary brain tumor according to the 2021 WHO classification, had not received prior surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy before MRI, and had complete MRI data, including diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) sequences at all selected b-values. Patients with recurrent or metastatic lesions, poor-quality images that precluded diagnostic evaluation, or contraindications to MRI, such as pacemakers or metallic implants, were excluded. All tumor samples were reviewed by an experienced neuropathologist blinded to the imaging data. Among the study cohort, 18 patients had glioblastoma multiforme, 11 had astrocytomas, 5 had oligodendrogliomas, and 6 had ependymomas.

MR image acquisition

All MR images were acquired using a 1.5-T MR scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen Germany). Different scan protocols like T1-weighted, T2-weighted and DWI, with different sequence orders, were performed. Diffusion-weighted gradients of 0, 500, 1000, 1500, and 2000 s/mm2 were used for b-values. ADC values were calculated using a monoexponentially model S (b) = S0× e −b⋅ADC, which is the standard approach for clinical brain tumor imaging. We selected b-values of 0, 500, 1000, 1500, and 2000 s/mm² to represent a range from conventional to higher diffusion weighting. The value of 1000 s/mm² was included as the clinical standard, while lower (500 s/mm²) and higher values (1500 and 2000 s/mm²) were chosen to explore potential improvements in lesion conspicuity and diagnostic accuracy within the technical limitations of our scanner.

Qualitative image analysis

Three radiologists with four (readers 1 and 2) and two (reader 3) years of experience independently performed qualitative image analysis. Before the study, all radiologists participated in a calibration session using five representative cases (not included in the final dataset) to standardize the scoring criteria and reduce interobserver variability. Brain MRI scans were interpreted by radiologists blinded to the clinical and pathological information. All three radiologists independently scored the images, and inter-rater reliability was assessed using ICC and pairwise Cohen’s kappa. Additionally, the readers were blinded to the results. The DWI images at different b-values were anonymized and presented to radiologists in a randomized order, independent of the acquisition order. All images were reviewed on PACS workstations. A 4-point Likert scale served as the basis for qualitative analysis. The general image quality (4 = excellent, 3 = good, 2 = fair, and 1 = poor), lesion detection and delineation, visualization of anatomical structures, absence of distortion, severity of artifacts, noise, and DWI signal intensity were evaluated.

For ADC analysis, regions of interest (ROIs) were manually drawn on the solid-enhancing portion of the tumor on the b = 1000 s/mm² image, carefully excluding necrotic, cystic, or hemorrhagic areas. Three ROIs (30–50 mm² each) were placed on contiguous slices, and the mean ADC value was calculated for every patient. ROI placement was performed independently by each reader, and discrepancies greater than 10% were resolved by the consensus.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 22.0.32; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were compared using independent-sample t-tests, and non-normally distributed variables were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Differences in DWI quality ratings across b-values were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Based on a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Cohen’s kappa for each reader pair were used to assess the inter-reader reliability for the 4-point Likert scale. Both ICC and Cohen’s kappa values were reported along with their 95% CIs to provide a measure of precision; ICC values >0.75 were interpreted as indicative of good to excellent agreement, and kappa values were interpreted according to standard benchmarks (0.61–0.80 = substantial; 0.81–1.00 = nearly perfect). Reader 1’s ratings are displayed as an example; the inter-rater reliability analysis included the scores of all three readers.

To assess the association between ADC values and histopathological tumor grade (gold standard), Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was calculated. The 95% CI was also reported using Fisher’s z-transformation. Tumor grades were coded numerically (1 = low-grade, 2 = high-grade), and ADC values from all b-values were included in the analyses. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

According to Table 3, which represents different aspects of the image quality of low-grade tumors, the overall image quality score of DWI with b-values of 0, 500, and 1000 s/mm2 was higher than that with b-values of 1500 and 2000. In contrast, lesion detection was better at higher b-values. Anatomical structure visualization was not significantly different among the various b-value images, except for a b-value of 1000 s/mm2. Distortion was observed in images with higher b-values. Moreover, there were no significant differences between the different DWI images. However, various b-value images showed a continuous increase in rank with increasing b-values.

The image quality results for high-grade tumors are listed in Table 4. Overall, the trends of the results were mostly in agreement with the low-grade tumor results, with minor discrepancies in some of the b-values. The overall image quality score improved for high-grade tumors; however, the highest b-value achieved a lower score. However, for anatomical structure visualization, a higher b-value resulted in a lower Likert score than that for low-grade tumors. Indeed, compared with low-grade tumor data, high-grade tumors had lower noise for b-values of 1000 s/mm2.

Inter-reader agreement

The inter-reader reliability for the 4-point Likert scale ratings of DWI image quality was high. Pairwise Cohen’s kappa values were 0.76 (reader 1 vs 2), 0.79 (reader 1 vs 3), and 0.77 (reader 2 vs 3), indicating substantial agreement [Table 5]. The overall intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) across all three readers was 0.82 (95% CI: 0.76–0.87), reflecting excellent reliability. These results confirmed the consistency of qualitative assessments among the radiologists.

To further evaluate the association between ADC values and histopathological tumor grade, Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed [Table 6]. A significant inverse correlation was observed between ADC values and tumor grade across all b-values, indicating that lower ADC values were associated with high-grade tumors. These results highlight the potential of ADC as a quantitative biomarker for brain-tumor grading.

DISCUSSION

Despite an increase in the number of ongoing investigations related to DWI with a wide range of b-values, there has been a corresponding increase in interest and knowledge surrounding this area. Subsequently, this study evaluated various b-values in DWI and ADC images to find a high b-value range compared to the standard b-values (b = 1,000 s/mm2), which provided the best image quality for diagnosing low- and high-grade tumors. We illustrated that the impact of DWI0, cDWI500, and cDWI000 on the image quality of low-grade tumors was more significant than that of DWI1000 and DWI1500 images. Notably, the results for high-grade tumors showed almost the same pattern as those for low-grade tumors, with minor discrepancies. It is also worth mentioning that higher b-values had a greater influence on lesion detection in low-grade tumors, while they presented lower Likert scores in high-grade tumors than in low-grade tumors. The results demonstrated that the visualization of anatomical structures in low-grade tumors was significantly different, with a b-value of 1000 s/mm2. However, high-grade tumors exhibited lower noise at this b-value. Overall, in comparison with images generated with the commonly used b-value of 1000 s/mm2, we observed that the trends between the two groups (low- and high-grade tumors) became more evident. Although this study was conducted on a 1.5T MRI system, the general trends observed across different b-values are expected to be reproducible on 3T systems, which may provide a higher SNR and slightly different contrast characteristics. Previous research on the impact of various b-values on DWI and ADC images has demonstrated that recent advancements in MR technology have enabled and upgraded DWI with higher b-values, reaching up to 10,000, which has the potential to provide greater contrast and more detailed information about tissues and lesions.[15] Although a b-value of 1000 is widely used and highly effective in clinical practice, further advancements in tissue characterization and lesion detection require the use of a higher b-value in future research and applications.[16] Several studies that focused on using a high b value confirmed that the best cutoff point was the mean ADC value for both high- and low-grade tumors obtained at a high b value.[17]

Chen et al.[18] reported an inverse correlation between tumor cellularity and ADC values. Doskaliyev et al.[19] also demonstrated an inverse correlation between tumor cellularity and ADC values, which was more pronounced at a high b-value than at the standard b-value (b = 1000 s/mm2). Alvarez-Linera et al.[20] have found a lower amount of ACD for high-grade tumors compared with this amount for low-grade tumors at standard and high b-values. Generally, the study findings suggested that the higher tumor cellularity in high-grade tumors, which is associated with lower ADC values, likely contributes to the observed differences in tumor differentiation between high- and low-grade tumors, as reflected in DWI.[1] Minor differences between our results and those of previous studies may be attributed to variations in the MRI field strength, tumor heterogeneity, and specific b-values employed. High b-value DWI has proven to be a valuable tool for grading brain tumors, especially gliomas.[21] Additionally, diffusion-weighted imaging with high b-values generates more prominent hyperintense signals in high-grade gliomas and less intense signals in low-grade gliomas than conventional b-value DWI, allowing for a clear distinction between tumor grades.[2] Notably, the combination of higher magnetic field strengths and advanced gradient coils in MRI scanners allows for the use of higher b-values and increased diffusion sensitivity, providing greater contrast and accuracy in deafferenting between low- and high-grade gliomas.[22] Furthermore, a low b-value primarily influences the diffusion signal intensity of fast-diffusing water molecules, whereas a high b-value primarily affects the signal intensity of slow-diffusing water molecules, which can provide valuable information about the structural and functional characteristics of tissues and lesions.[2324] Gliomas can be effectively graded using a high b-value compared with the ADC value based on the standard b-value.[25] In 2017, Cihangiroglu et al.[26] reported that a cutoff point at a high b-value was associated with acceptable sensitivity, specificity and accuracy. Hu et al.[22] showed that high b-value impresser in the low- and high-grade gliomas discrimination played an important role which gained with confirmable and high sensitivity compared with standard b-value. Mansour Al-Agha et al.[1] investigated different b-values on grading gliomas. To accurately assess glioma grade, it is essential to evaluate DWI with high b-values in conjunction with other routine MRI sequences to gain a comprehensive understanding of the tumor and its surrounding tissues. Overall, this study compared the b-value differences in both low- and high-grade gliomas and investigated their impact on brain tumor diagnosis when the b-values can influence the sensitivity and accuracy of MR images. Diffusion-weighted imaging and apparent diffusion coefficient images are two significant patterns that affect the various b-values. The results show that a higher b-value plays a significant role in increasing rank with increasing b-values in both low- and high-grade tumors; however, there is little difference between high-grade and low-grade gliomas standard b-values since high-grade gliomas have lower noise for b-value 1000 s/mm2. However, a limitation of our study is that we did not perform receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis to identify an optimal b-value cutoff for differentiating between low- and high-grade gliomas. While such an approach could provide valuable information regarding diagnostic accuracy, we recognize that the sample size and heterogeneity of tumor types may limit the statistical power of this study. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as preliminary, and future studies with larger cohorts are required to confirm these findings. Future studies with larger patient cohorts are warranted to establish robust cutoff values with adequate sensitivities and specificities. In addition, the use of higher b-values in diffusion-weighted imaging can increase the presence of distortion artifacts, mainly due to echo-planar imaging (EPI) susceptibility effects. This may reduce image quality and should be considered when interpreting the results and applying them in clinical practice. Although noise was assessed qualitatively using a Likert scale, future studies should incorporate objective quantitative measures, such as the background standard deviation, to complement subjective evaluations.

CONCLUSION

High-b-value diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) provides enhanced contrast and sensitivity, enabling more robust and accurate differentiation of tumor grades compared with conventional b-value protocols. This approach facilitates early, noninvasive tumor characterization, supports precise treatment planning, and improves clinical decision-making. The routine incorporation of high-b-value DWI into clinical practice promises more accurate diagnoses and better patient outcomes by offering a comprehensive view of the tumor microstructure.
