Enhancing speech recall and reducing cognitive load through rhythmic amplitude modulated vibrotactile stimulation in noise
Authors: Jacqueline Urakami, Toshikazu Kanaoka, Akito Moriwaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study introduces and evaluates Rhythmic Amplitude Modulated Vibrational (RAM-Vib) stimulation for improving speech recall and reducing cognitive load in noisy environments. Building on multisensory integration and neural entrainment theories, three experiments tested and refined RAM-Vib for Japanese speech in noise. Initial experiments focused on speech perception and attention using either amplitude-envelope (Exp 1) or speech-rhythm alone (Exp 2). While these approaches reduced cognitive load, they did not improve recall, often due to perceived feedback quality or synchronicity issues. However, Experiment 3, combining both rhythmic patterns with dynamic amplitude modulation (RAM-Vib), yielded significant improvements. This integrated RAM-Vib not only enhanced speech recall but also reduced objective cognitive load (measured through Skin Conductance Level), indicating a more efficient memory resource allocation. These findings demonstrate that RAM-Vib using rhythmic patterns and dynamic amplitude changes synchronized with the speech amplitude guides attention and facilitates multisensory processing. This could be a promising, non-intrusive tool to improve speech recall and reduce cognitive load in noisy or difficult listening situations.Keywords: Speech recall, Multisensory, Vibrotactile stimuli, Cognitive loadSubject terms: Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Speech recall is the ability to remember and reproduce spoken words and is as such a vital part of daily conversations. Recalling speech even when there is background noise or other disruptions is a common occurrence, and problems in speech recall can negatively affect quality of life by limiting participation in social, vocational, and educational activities. Many approaches to find solutions for the “speech-perception-in-background-noise” problem focus on the development of machine learning algorithms to filter out background noise1–4. However, there are also numerous studies following a user-centered approach by enhancing the speech signal through multisensory-feedback such as visual cues of lib movements5, visual captions6, or translating the speech signal into haptic-vibrational signals7–10. The study presented here falls into the later category. By incorporating findings from multisensory integration and speech entrainment11, the process of aligning neural activity to the temporal fluctuations of speech, we developed a method of translating the speech signal into rhythmic amplitude modulated vibrational signals (RAM-Vib) to enhance speech recall and reduce cognitive load. Our approach goes beyond existing methods that have mainly focused on directly translating the speech signal into vibrations by considering special characteristics of the target language (in our case Japanese), as well as considering perceptual constrains of vibrations transmitted through a vibration accelerator.

This paper presents a series of three experiments investigating how vibrotactile signals could enhance speech recall in noisy environments by supporting perceptual (Experiment 1), attention (Experiment 2), and memory processes (Experiment 3), without increasing cognitive load or impairing memory performance. Our study specifically focuses on speech recall, the ability to remember and reproduce spoken words in challenging listening conditions. Unlike the commonly used term speech comprehension, which refers solely to understanding spoken language, speech recall includes both comprehension and the subsequent retrieval of information. We experimentally tested our approach by delivering vibrations to participants’ fingertips while they listened to naturally spoken Japanese sentences in a noisy environment. This paper makes two key contributions. First, we introduce a non-intrusive technique to enhance speech recall in noise through RAM-Vib. Second, our experimental study shows that RAM-Vib, aligned with speech rhythm, can not only reduce cognitive load but also improves recall when certain conditions are met. Our work is based on the neural entrainment theory applied in a multisensory context, which emphasizes the importance of synchronizing brain rhythms with rhythmic external stimuli, such as speech12,13,14. Unlike existing neural entrainment methods such as transcranial magnetic stimulation or transcranial alternating current stimulation15,16 that require equipment placed at specific head locations, our approach utilizes a user-friendly, non-intrusive, and non-stigmatizing approach. We propose that enhancing the entrainment of speech signals through synchronized dynamic rhythmic stimuli is a promising strategy for enhancing and selecting relevant information, thereby reducing cognitive load and improving information processing. This opens up new avenues for research on the use of RAM-Vib for various applications, including language learning, assistive technologies for individuals with auditory processing challenges, and improving communication in noisy settings.

Related work

Multisensory integration

Multisensory integration refers to the ability to combine and synthesize information from multiple sensory modalities. In everyday life, both behavior and perception are shaped by the integration of inputs from various senses, for instance, the way we experience food is influenced not only by its taste but also by its appearance, smell, and even sound. Recent research suggests that the integration of information from different sensory modalities occurs at early stages of sensory processing17, indicating that multisensory integration is a fundamental aspect of cognition. Consequently, perception results in a unified representation that merges diverse sensory inputs. This process is influenced by both bottom-up, stimulus-driven mechanisms and top-down, attention-based modulation. Top-down selection is guided by the observer’s goals, intentions, and the relevance of specific items, while bottom-up mechanisms rely on stimulus salience and the spatial and temporal alignment of sensory inputs across different modalities18–20. Talsma et al.21 proposed a framework for multisensory integration and attention. According to this framework, the brain attempts the spatiotemporal alignment of salient stimuli with less salient inputs of other senses. Sensory inputs from different modalities that are aligned in both time and space are prioritized for further processing over those that are not. Multisensory integration appears to follow three basic guiding principles22,23. First, cross-modal cues that are in close spatial (1) and temporal (2) proximity are more likely to be integrated than stimuli that are spatially or temporally distant. Second, multisensory integration adheres to the principle of inverse effectiveness (3) the benefit or enhancement from combining information across senses is greatest when each individual sensory cue is weak or difficult to detect on its own. When signals from each sense are weak, their combination produces a stronger effect than when the signals are already strong24. This may also explain why multisensory integration is particularly advantageous under conditions of high perceptual load. For example Marucci et al.25 compared unisensory and multisensory stimuli in a target detection task and found that performance not only improved in the multisensory condition, but workload also decreased relative to the unisensory condition. Haptic feedback, in particular, proved especially effective in reducing workload. Yuan et al.26 demonstrated that dynamic temporal visual information correlated with the auditory speech signal enhanced speech perception in noise. However, Benz et al.27 who used a similar approach by modulating the size of a circle according to three speech features (envelope, formants, word onset), found no effect of these visual cues on word recognition. Similarly, Varano et al. 28 reported that the level of naturalness in the visual signal is an important factor for speech-in-noise benefit, showing that natural facial cues, such as cartoons or real faces, improved comprehension, whereas an animated disk had no effect.

Multisensory integration and memory

While speech intelligibility, the ability to recognize spoken words, is a useful measure of how difficult it is for someone to perceive speech29, it may be even more relevant in practice to assess how much of the information is actually retained and later recalled. Studies indicate that when poor acoustic conditions or other challenges increase the mental effort required for listening, recall performance declines even if speech intelligibility remains at an acceptable level30–32. Furthermore, previous studies have suggested that recall can be a more sensitive measure than speech intelligibility for assessing the impact of noise on performance30,33. It is important to note, however, that there is a strong link between speech intelligibility and speech recall. A listener must first recognize and understand spoken words before being able to accurately recall the message. Nevertheless, we wish to emphasize the practical relevance of recall, as memorization requires time and substantial cognitive resources to process and store information, making the management of cognitive load crucial in speech-in-noise situations. One factor that may influence speech recall is the brain’s ability to integrate information from multiple senses. To account for such multisensory interactions at earlier stages of information processing, Baddeley34 proposed the episodic buffer in his working memory model. The episodic buffer is a capacity limited storage system within the working memory model, that integrates information from different sensory modalities and long-term memory into a coherent, conscious representation of an event. Research findings suggest that the integration of information from different modalities into a single object representation might be advantageous for recall19. For example, Thompson and Paivio35 found recall improved for audiovisual presentations compared to visual or audio only presentations. Similarly, recall rates for picture/spoken word and written/spoken word combinations were higher compared to double visual presentation of pictures and written words 36. It has been argued that multimodal stimuli result in stronger, interconnected memory traces and engages more cognitive processes because there is additional encoding in multisensory areas 37. Neurobiological evidence further shows extensive multisensory interaction in the neocortex 17.

However, even though there is some evidence, not all studies have obtained such benefit, e.g. Cohen et al. 38 showed that auditory memory was systematically inferior to visual memory suggesting an asymmetry between auditory and visual processing. Canits et al. 39 found no benefit of congruent motor actions on later memory for pictures even though congruency did affect immediate motor responses during the study task. Pecher and Zeelenberg 40 examined memory performance for items studied as unimodal or bimodal stimuli but results showed little evidence for a multisensory benefit of memory. Multisensory integrative processes seem to result in stronger memories more likely when information from different modalities is congruent, activating the same object in long-term memory 19,37. Furthermore, attention might play an essential role in regulating how sensory input is combined with representations stored in long-term memory. Multisensory stimuli can capture attention more effectively than unisensory stimuli, known as multisensory enhancement 41.

Overall, these findings suggest that the benefits of multisensory integration for memory depend on factors such as congruency and, importantly, attention. Attention not only shapes how sensory information is combined but also influences how effectively it is encoded and recalled. This connection between attentional processes and sensory input is especially relevant in dynamic contexts like speech perception, where rhythmic patterns play a key role. The next section examines how neural entrainment to rhythmic stimuli, such as speech, supports attentional selection and cognitive processing.

Neural entrainment and attention

Rhythmicity is a fundamental feature of living organisms, playing a crucial role in many daily activities. Rhythm refers to the pattern of time intervals in a sequence of stimuli and can be observed in activities such as walking, speaking, or singing42. Neural entrainment, the automatic synchronization of oscillatory patterns, enables coordination between organisms and their environment and as such is a foundational mechanism of brain functions13. In the context of speech processing, rhythm plays a vital role as speech itself is a rhythmic input. Speech entrainment is evoked by characteristics of the linguistic structure, or by slow amplitude modulations in the temporal envelop of the speech signal43–45. Speech-brain entrainment is critical for speech comprehension especially in impaired listening conditions (e.g. noise)13. Disrupting speech through gaps of silence or bursts of noise impairs speech perception, while inserting amplitude modulated noise that restores the original speech envelop has been shown to improve perception46. Furthermore, it has been shown that speech perception can be improved by transcranial stimulation with speech-enveloped-shaped currents16,47. Houtgast and Steeneken48 demonstrated that accurate perception of amplitude modulations in the speech signal is important for speech intelligibility. Giraud and Poeppel49 described that rhythmic structure in the temporal envelop is given by regular modulations of the speech signal, such as the speech rate. The rhythm of a language depends on several characteristics and can differ across languages. Japanese is called a mora-timed language contrary to English which is a stressed-timed language. A mora is a unit of sound that measures the length of words or utterances. Mora is relatively regularly timed and is a good predictor of word duration50. Previous studies have shown that Japanese speakers use mora for auditory perception and speech segmentation51. Contrary to other languages, prosody, grammatical structure, or articulatory constraints have only a small effect on mora duration52. Thus, the mora rate per second is relatively stable at approximately 8Hz, which is somewhat faster than the speech rate for English. We will use this rate as the base unit to define the speech rhythm in our study.

Attention plays an important role in speech perception enabling the listener to isolate the speaker’s voice from background noise or selectively listening to one speaker while ignoring others in multi-talker situations53,14. According to recent studies, attentional processes in speech perception are guided through neural entrainment11,54. By promoting or suppressing the detection of external stimuli, entrainment decreases attentional load and increases cognitive processing quality55,56. Thus, entrainment filters noise, increasing the attention that can be directed to the processing of relevant stimuli57. When listening to a speaker in a noisy environment, entrainment should guide attention to the speech signal and suppress surrounding noise. Thus, more attentional resources can be allocated to the processing of the relevant speech signal reducing cognitive load and improving memorization.

Vibrotactile stimulation for speech entrainment

Time-domain characteristics of vibrations such as frequency, amplitude, and duration make it predestined to transmit biological or artificial wave signals9. A few studies have investigated the use of vibration stimuli to enhance speech perception in noisy environments. Guilleminot and Reichenbach8 provided vibrotactile stimuli at the syllable rate, demonstrating that such stimulation can modulate and enhance the speech signal. In their study, participants held a voice-coil actuator between the thumb, index, and middle fingers. Cieśla et al.7 used two piezoelectric plates to simultaneously stimulate two fingers showing that when the auditory signal was degraded, speech-in-noise perception improved with the addition of vibrotactile stimulation. Vibration signals were generated by extracting the f0 contour from the original sound file resulting in vibrations ranging from 50 to 500 Hz. Oh et al.10 used a flat button-type DC vibration motor attached to participants’ wrists to study the multisensory integration of auditory, visual, and tactile modalities for speech recognition in noise. They found the greatest improvements when all three modalities were present.

These studies indicate that vibrotactile stimulation might improve speech perception under noise but fall short on providing evidence if memorization is affected. Furthermore, studies differ in both the type of vibration stimuli used and the methods of administration making it difficult to derive general guidelines for the design of such vibrotactile stimulation.

Research goal and hypotheses

The primary aim of this study was to investigate whether, and which type of, vibrotactile stimulation can reduce cognitive load and enhance speech recall in noisy environments. To address this, we conducted a series of three experiments, each examining the effect of vibrotactile stimulation on a different cognitive process related to speech: perception, attention, and memory.

In Experiment 1, we explored the impact of vibrotactile stimuli derived from the amplitude envelope of the speech signal. Using “babble talk” to distort the speech envelope, we assessed whether vibrotactile stimulation that was temporally aligned with the original speech signal could compensate for this distortion and enhance speech perception.

Experiment 2 shifted the focus to attention. Participants were exposed to speech-in-background-noise with fluctuating loudness and intermittent distracting beeps. Here, we employed rhythmic vibrotactile stimuli synchronized with the speech rhythm, mirroring listeners’ natural processes for auditory perception and speech segmentation, to determine its effect on maintaining attentional focus in challenging listening conditions.

In Experiment 3, we examined memory by requiring participants to recall longer texts presented at a lower signal-to-noise ratio, thus ensuring speech intelligibility while increasing cognitive load. In this experiment, we integrated both approaches, amplitude envelope and speech rhythm, by generating a rhythmic amplitude-modulated vibrotactile feedback (RAM-Vib). Across all experiments, we hypothesized that vibrotactile stimulation would reduce cognitive load (H1) and improve speech recall (H2).

Experiment 1

Method

Participants

13 participants with an age range from 20 to 60 (11 men) took part in the study. The ethical guidelines of Kyocera Corporation, and HQL as well as the ethical guidelines according to the declaration of Helsinki for research involving Human Subjects were maintained. Participation was voluntary and informed consent was obtained from all participants. All participants were of normal hearing.

Experimental design

In a within-subjects factorial design, speech recall in noise was assessed under three conditions: audio-only, audio-visual, and audio-vibrotactile. The order of both the target sentences and the experimental conditions was counterbalanced across participants. Although the primary focus was to examine the effect of audio-vibrotactile feedback, a visual condition similar to that used by Yuan et al.26 was included to verify the effectiveness of our experimental manipulation. Previous research has demonstrated the effectiveness of audio-visual cross-modal integration, thus serving as a reference for multisensory benefit (e.g.28,35). In contrast, audio-tactile integration is less well understood and comparatively underexplored. By incorporating the visual modality, we could compare the effectiveness of envelop extraction and its application across sensory modalities.

Experimental material

Audio stimuli: A Japanese translation of the Harvard sentences58 was used in the study. The sentences were first translated from English into Japanese and then back translated into English to ensure accuracy. Three independent raters evaluated the translated sentences for naturalness, and only those receiving high ratings from all three raters were selected for the experiment. Selected sentences were organized into three sets, each containing six sentences. Care was taken to ensure that sentences across sets were similar in length and number of mora and had comparable naturalness ratings. Sentences were recorded by a male and a female speaker differing from the speakers used for the background babble talk.

Background noise: Harvard sentences that were not used in the main study were recorded by four native Japanese speakers (male and female) and combined into a single audio to create babble talk used as background noise set at 63 dBA. Previous studies have shown that the competing speech rhythm of babble talk can hinder the envelope tracking of the target voice59.

Vibrotactile stimulation: Vibration intensity was synchronized with the amplitude of the audio stimuli. The Hilbert transformation was used to obtain the amplitude envelop from the audio waveform. To smooth the signal, a moving average was calculated and applied to the envelope. The resulting vibration signal was delivered through an 8 mm diameter coin vibration motor.

Visual stimulation: The brightness of three red LED bulbs was synchronized with the audio’s amplitude, using the same envelope extraction process described above. The brightness of all three LEDs changed simultaneously in response to the audio signal.

Cognitive load: Cognitive load was assessed with the Japanese version of the NASA Task Load Index, Cronbach’s alpha = 0.7160.

Stimuli evaluation: Four questions assessed the perceptibility of visual and vibrotactile stimuli. Four items (The signal was easy to see (feel) / The signal was well synchronized with the audio / The meaning of the signal was easy to understand / The signal was comfortable) had to be answered with 7-point Likert scales ranging from 1- strongly disagree to 7- strongly agree.

Experimental set-up and procedure

Participants were seated in front of a personal computer and used a keyboard to type the sentences they heard. The sentences were delivered via open-ear, bone-conduction headphones. Although bone-conduction headphones transmit sound vibrations through the bones of the skull, these vibrations are typically only noticeable to listeners at high volumes or with low-frequency sounds. In our experimental setting, with natural speech presented at conversational volume, perceivable vibrations were not present. Background noise was played through a loudspeaker. Since the LED cannot be directly controlled by the PC, we used an Arduino board to synchronize both the visual (LED) and vibrotactile feedback stimuli with the audio. To adjust for any time delay introduced by the Arduino board, the onset of visual/vibrotactile stimuli and audio was calibrated by the experimenter and pre-tested. In the audio + vibrotactile condition participants placed the index finger of their left hand on the vibration device. In the audio + visual condition, participants were instructed to direct their gaze toward the LED lights, which were positioned to the left side of the participant.

Participants first received general instructions about the experiment and provided written informed consent. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) was adjusted to a level at which participants could correctly understand approximately 50% of the audio. Afterwards, participants could experience each of the experimental conditions (audio only, audio + vibrotactile, audio + visual). They then completed three experimental blocks, corresponding to the three conditions: audio only, audio + visual, and audio + vibrotactile, which were counterbalanced across participants. Participants first listened to each spoken sentence and then typed any words or phrases they could recall. After each experimental block, participants completed the NASA TLX and the stimuli evaluation questionnaire. The procedure is described in Fig. 2.

Procedure of Experiment 1.

Data evaluation

Speech recall rate was measured as number of correctly recalled words divided through total number of words per sentence. All data were tested for normal distribution using the Kolmogorov Smirnov test and for homogeneity of variance using the Levene test. No violation of assumptions was found. The audio only condition served as the baseline, against which the audio + vibrotactile and audio + visual conditions were compared using planned comparisons to test hypothesis H1 and H2.

Results

Stimuli evaluation

Visibility, synchronicity, understandability, and comfortability of signals were evaluated on 7-point Likert scales. The visual stimuli received high ratings for all four categories, whereas the vibrotactile stimuli received lower ratings for comfortability and synchronicity (see Table 1). An evaluation score was generated as an average of the four rating categories and a comparison between the visual and vibrotactile stimuli revealed that the visual stimuli received a significantly higher evaluation than the vibrotactile stimuli, t (12) = 3.36, p = 0.006.

Speech recall

Speech recall was measured as number of correctly recalled words divided through total number of words per sentence. Speech recall rate was lowest in the audio only (M = 0.46; SD = 0.13) and audio + vibrotactile condition (M = 0.52, SD = 0.15) being close to the original 50% SNR setting, and highest in the audio + visual condition (M = 0.61, SD = 0.15) showing an improvement of about 10%. Planned contrasts revealed that speech recall was significantly higher in the audio + visual than audio only condition (t (12) = -2.38, p = 0.035), but no differences were found between audio + vibrotactile and audio only condition (t (12) = -0.93, p = 0.37). Furthermore, stimuli evaluation and speech recall correlated negatively, r = -0.47, p = 0.02 showing that perceived signal quality had an impact on speech recall.

Cognitive load

Perceived cognitive load was highest in the audio only condition (M = 5.72, SD = 0.85) and lowest in the audio + visual condition (M = 4.66, SD = 0.98), with the audio + vibrotactile condition falling in between (M = 4.73, SD = 1.04). Planned contrasts revealed that cognitive load was significantly lower in the audio + visual than audio only condition (t (12) = 3.93, p = 0.0002), and in the audio + vibrotactile compared to audio only condition (t (12) = 3.54, p = 0.004), see Fig. 3. Furthermore, perceived cognitive load and speech recall correlated negatively (r = -0.40, p = 0.01, indicating that an increase in cognitive load resulted in a lower speech recall rate.

Discussion

Both visual and vibrotactile stimuli reduced cognitive load, however, only visual stimulation yielded a measurable improvement in speech recall. The positive effect of synchronizing LED lights with amplitude changes in the speech envelope is particularly noteworthy, given that other studies using abstract graphical presentations, such as modulated circles, did not yield similar results27. One possible explanation is that detecting changes in LED brightness is primarily a bottom-up sensory process, whereas interpreting dynamic geometric shapes involves more complex, higher-order cognitive processing. This interpretation is consistent with the findings of Varano et al.28 who demonstrated that natural facial cues, which humans are highly adept at processing, improve speech comprehension, whereas animated disks do not produce the same benefit.

The absence of an effect for vibrotactile stimuli may be attributed to perceived stimuli quality, which was rated lower for the vibrotactile condition, particularly in terms of temporal synchronicity. Since synchrony is a critical factor in multisensory integration21,23, the inadequate synchronicity of the vibrotactile stimuli may account for its limited impact on recall. It is likely that the time delay introduced by the Arduino board could not be fully eliminated through calibration. Therefore, we employed a different method in Experiment 2 to ensure precise synchronization between the vibration and audio signals. Furthermore, the negative correlation between cognitive load and speech recall highlights the need to manage cognitive resources when processing and storing information in speech-in-noise conditions, consistent with Hygge et al.30.

Experiment 2

In Experiment 2, the focus shifted to the role of attention by presenting speech in background noise with fluctuating loudness and intermittent distracting beeps. We investigated whether vibrotactile stimuli synchronized to the speech rhythm decreases cognitive load (H1) and improves speech recall (H2). Since our target language was Japanese, mora rate per second was used as basis for the speech rhythm. A mora is a unit of sound that measures the length of words of utterances. Mora is relatively regularly timed at a rate of 8 mora per second (8 Hz) and is a good predictor of word duration50. In addition to the subjective measure of cognitive load, participants’ EDA (electrodermal activity) was also recorded. Previous studies have shown that an increase in SCL (skin conductance level) is a reliable indicator of cognitive load61,62.

Pretesting rhythmic vibrotactile stimuli

To develop rhythmic vibrotactile stimuli for speech signals, factors such as vibration perceptibility, rhythm detection, and comfort were considered. We used the same compact coin vibration motor as in Experiment 1. Initially, we evaluated the perceived strength of six vibration frequencies (50 Hz, 100 Hz, 150 Hz, 200 Hz, 250 Hz, 300 Hz), finding 150 Hz and 200 Hz to be the most perceptible. For rhythmic vibrations of 200 Hz, durations of 100ms were most preferred for both perceptibility and comfort. Testing the vibration rhythm with speech input showed that a tempo of 1:3 (one vibration for every third mora) was optimal for rhythm perceptibility, comfort, and perceived synchronization.

Method

Participants

21 participants with an age range from 24 to 65 (M = 42, SD = 14; 3 women, 18 men) took part in the study. The ethical guidelines of Kyocera Corporation, and HQL as well as the ethical guidelines according to the declaration of Helsinki for research involving Human Subjects were maintained. Participation was voluntary and informed consent was obtained from all participants. All participants were of normal hearing.

Experimental design and procedure

The effect of rhythmic vibrotactile stimuli on speech recall and cognitive load was examined in a within-subject design. Participants, seated in front of a computer, experienced two conditions: audio only and audio + vibrotactile stimulation. The same experimental set-up as in Experiment 1 was used. However, to achieve more precise synchronization between the vibration and audio stimuli, we used a Python program that simultaneously played both files on separate output devices, ensuring stable and high levels of synchrony. The order of both the sentence sets and the experimental conditions was counterbalanced across participants. The same procedure as in Experiment 1 was used with the only difference that participants completed only two test blocks (audio only and audio + vibrotactile) in randomized order. Since light flickering at 8 Hz could cause negative effects such as eye strain and visual fatigue, we chose not to include visual stimuli in this experiment.

Materials and measurements

Experimental stimuli

Audio stimuli: Japanese translations of Harvard sentences that were not used in Experiment 1 were used for this study. Only sentences spoken by a native Japanese female speaker were used this time to reduce variance in sentence quality. Two sets of target sentences, each containing eleven sentences, were created. All audio stimuli were normalized for loudness and presented at a rate of 8 mora per second (the average speech rate of Japanese which is somewhat higher than the speech rate for English). It was ensured that sentences in each set contained the same number of mora.

Background noise: Random bar noise, including sounds of people speaking and laughing, was used as background noise. The average noise level was 63 dBA with a minimum of 60 and a maximum of 65 dBA. Distracting sounds such as alerting beep tones, were added to the background noise to increase the level of distraction.

Vibrotactile stimuli: Parameter settings for the rhythmic vibrotactile stimuli were determined in a pretest as described in Section Pretesting rhythmic vibrotactile stimuli. The rhythmic vibrotactile stimulation consisted of vibration bursts of 200 Hz sinusoidal waves with a duration of 100 ms. The tempo of the vibration bursts was set at a 1:3 vibration : mora ratio (one vibration burst every 3rd mora). The same vibration device as in Experiment 1 was used.

Questionnaires

Cognitive Load: Cognitive load was assessed with the Japanese version of the NASA Task Load Index, Cronbach’s alpha = 0.7160. Furthermore, SCL (Skin Conductance Level) derived from the EDA (Electrodermal activity) measures were used to estimate cognitive load as well.

Attention difficulties: Self-assessment of attentional difficulties (perceived distraction) was conducted by rephrasing the items used by Moore and Urakami63, Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.89. Participants had to access four items on 7-point Likert scales ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree): (1) I had difficulties concentrating; (2) I had difficulties concentrating on the target sentences; (3) I was easily distracted; (4) The background noise was distracting.

Perceived calm: The perceived level of comfort (perceived calm) was measured by adapting four items from the Godspeed questionnaire64; Cronbach’s Alpha = 0.71: (1) I could relax; (2) I felt calm; and the two reversed items (3) I felt anxious; (4) I felt agitated. All items were assessed by 7-point Likert scales ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

Vibrotactile stimuli evaluation: Subjective evaluation of the effectiveness of the rhythmic vibration stimuli were assessed by the question “How did the vibration alter your experience listening to sentences?”. Participants had to choose one out of five answering levels “The effect made listening: significantly worse / slightly worse / neither better nor worse / slightly better / significantly better”.

Electrodermal activity

EDA was measured with sensors from Biosignalsplux, a multi-sensor platform for bio signal acquisitions, using gelled electrodes placed on the pointer and middle finger of the right hand. The sampling rate was set to 100 Hz. The EDA signal comprises two components: a tonic skin conductance level (SCL) and a phasic skin conductance response (SCR). Previous studies have shown that SCL is a reliable indicator of cognitive load, with higher values corresponding to increased cognitive demand61,62,65. Accordingly, our analysis focused on the SCL component.

Speech recall was evaluated by computing the percentage of correctly recognized mora, and percentage of correctly recognized words of target sentences. A two-way repeated measures ANOVA with the factor vibrotactile stimuli and sentence set revealed no significant effect for vibration or sentence set for percentage of correctly recalled mora (Fvibration (1, 18) < 1; Fsentences (1, 18) < 1), or percentage of correctly recalled words (Fvibration (1, 18) < 1; Fsentences (1, 18) < 1). Cognitive load correlated positively with perceived distraction (r = 0.58, p < 0.01), indicating a relationship between experiencing attentional difficulties and cognitive load.

Participants varied widely in their evaluation of the vibrotactile stimuli ranging from “The (vibration) effect made listening slightly worse (n = 5) till slightly/significantly better (n = 4). Interestingly, cognitive load correlated negatively with evaluation of vibration (r = -0.53, p = 0.013) indicating that lower cognitive load resulted in a more positive evaluation of the vibration stimuli. Additionally, perceived calm correlated positive with evaluation of vibration (r = 0.56, p < 0.01) suggesting that participants who felt comfortable with the vibration evaluated it more positively.

Electrodermal activity (EDA)

EDA data was processed, and skin conductance level (SCL) in µS was computed. The data of one participant was not used because of a measurement error. No differences in SCL between audio + vibrotactile (M = 5.01, SD = 3.10) and audio only (M = 4.95, SD = 3.38) conditions were found, t (19) = -0.5, p = 0.30.

Discussion

The experiment yielded mixed results. While we observed a positive impact on reducing cognitive load (verifying H1) agreeing with findings of previous studies53,66, no significant improvement in speech recall (rejecting H2) was observed, contrary to findings by Riecke et al.13, Zoefel et al.16. Several factors may account for the absence of a measurable effect on speech recall. First of all, individual preferences and comfort levels with the vibrotactile stimuli might have played a significant role. Despite adjusting the vibration strength to personal preferences, some participants reported discomfort or found the vibrotactile stimuli unpleasant. Moreover, participants differed in their perceived matching of speech and vibrotactile signals. While some felt that the signals were well synchronized, others perceived them as mismatched and distracting. The ability of participants to integrate both signals likely influenced the effectiveness of the vibrotactile stimulation, as indicated by correlations showing more positive evaluations of the vibration stimuli when perceived calmness was high. Additionally, a subjective lower cognitive load was associated with a more positive assessment of the vibrational signal, suggesting that successful integration improved the management of cognitive resources.

Despite these challenges, the observed decrease in subjective cognitive load suggests that vibrational stimulation may help direct attention to speech signals and reduce distractions from background noise as indicated by neural entrainment theory11. The vibrotactile rhythmic stimuli synchronized with the speech rhythm might have supported participants to focus on relevant speech units (mora) making it easier to ignore the background noise resulting in a decrease in perceived cognitive load, a phenomenon known as multisensory enhancement41. However, this effect was not reflected in objective measures. SCL showed no differences between conditions. While the NASA TLX captures a multifaceted view of workload including frustration and perceived effort, SCL primarily reflects arousal and general effort61. The demanding signal-to-noise ratio targeting a 50% recognition rate may have heightened participant frustration and perceived effort, influencing their subjective ratings.

Experiment 3

Experiment 2 demonstrated that achieving precise synchronicity between vibrotactile and the speech signals remains a significant challenge. Although temporal alignment to the mora rate was beneficial in reducing cognitive load, it was not sufficient on its own. Consequently, Experiment 3 employed a vibrotactile stimuli design that combined both features tested in the previous experiments, rhythmic patterns and dynamic amplitude changes. Furthermore, the focus of the experiment shifted from speech perception to memorization by increasing the signal-to-noise ratio to ensure intelligibility, while simultaneously increasing the information load, thus making it easier to detect the impact of cognitive load on recall. Because memorization requires time and cognitive resources to process and store information, managing cognitive load is especially important in speech-in-noise conditions30,31. To address this, we used an auditory memory task involving larger speech segments, thereby increasing the amount of content to be recalled. Once again, the experiment tested whether vibrotactile stimulation could reduce cognitive load (H1) and improve recall (H2).

Developing and pretesting vibrotactile rhythm

In Experiment 1, vibrotactile stimulation was derived from the amplitude envelope of the speech signal. Experiment 2 employed rhythmic vibrotactile stimulation based on an 8 Hz mora per second rate, consisting of 100 ms vibration bursts. In Experiment 3, features of both approaches were combined and pretested, resulting in rhythmic vibrotactile stimuli with dynamic amplification (see Fig. 1 for reference). The base rhythm used the same 8 Hz mora per second rate as in Experiment 2. To enhance the effect of dynamic amplitude modulation and the perceived synchrony between speech and vibration, we tested different speech frequency bands as the basis for the modulation of the vibration signal (entire spectrum, fundamental frequency 80–260 Hz, and the most sensitive hearing range of 2000–5000 Hz). The 2000–5000 Hz range where human hearing is most sensitive67 produced the strongest perceived synchrony and was thus used to modulate the amplitude change in the vibrotactile signal. Finally, to make intensity variations more perceptible, vibration bursts were shortened to 70–85 ms, producing briefer, more salient tactile sensations (Fig. 5).

Method

Participants

22 participants (5 females, 17 males) with an age range between 26–57 years old (M = 35.59; SD = 10.10) took part in this study. The ethical guidelines of Kyocera Corporation, and HQL as well as the ethical guidelines according to the declaration of Helsinki for research involving Human Subjects were maintained. Participation was voluntary and informed consent was obtained from all participants. All participants were of normal hearing.

Experimental design

In a within-experimental design investigating the effect of multisensory stimuli (audio only / vibration + audio / light + audio) on speech recall and cognitive load, we aimed to primarily assess the impact of vibrotactile stimulation. To ensure that observed effects were not simply due to the presence of an additional sensory cue, we included a light condition as a control. In this condition, light flickers were generated by modulating the brightness of a computer monitor in accordance with the temporal pattern of the vibrotactile stimuli. Having experienced synchronization issues with LED-based visual stimuli using an Arduino board in the first experiment, we opted to manipulate monitor brightness instead. This approach provided two significant advantages: first, both vibration and brightness changes could be precisely controlled using the same rhythmic amplitude-modulated stimuli pattern, allowing for more consistent temporal alignment; second, a custom Python program was used to simultaneously synchronize audio, vibration, and light outputs, further enhancing synchrony. Additionally, subtle changes in monitor brightness can be fine-tuned more accurately than LED flickers, enabling a closer match to the vibrational amplitude variations and improving overall experimental control. All participants were of normal hearing (confirmed with a hearing test: https://panasonic.jp/hochouki/download/dagehg/hearing2.html).

Materials and measurements

Audio stimuli: We used test items from the Wechsler Memory Scale (WMS)68 for auditory memory as examples to create the memory test. The WMS measures the ability to retain and recall orally presented information such as stories. Three test sets containing 6 items (1 practice item and 5 test items) each were developed and pretested (see Table 3). Each test set contained an item covering 6 different topics such as “work meeting”, “café”, “a sport event”, “lost item”, “an accident”, “going out”. By keeping item content similar across test sets we ensured that each test set had a similar difficulty level and included information that was comparable across test sets. Difficulty level of items was pretested. Each item contained 15 core information that had to be recalled, resulting in a total of 75 information units to be recalled per test set (5 Items × 15 core information). The following is a sample item of the test set sport event with core information indicated in bold: Three years ago / in summer, a soccer tournament was held at the “Dribble Center” in Fukushima. Ms. Miyu / Maeda participated as the goalkeeper for the girls’ team. The match ended in a 2–2 / draw, and she blocked five shots. After the game, Coach / Okada thanked Miyu for her courageous play, and she wore a satisfied expression (translated for Japanese original). The Google text-to-speech generator with the Japanese male voice ja-JP-Wavenet-C was used to generate the spoken test items. We used natural spoken audio in the previous experiments but opted for a computer generated audio for this experiment to have higher consistency and control eliminating changes in tone or emphasis that could affect memorization and recall.

Vibrotactile stimuli: A combination of the rhythmic vibrotactile stimuli of experiment 2 and the amplitude adjusted stimuli from experiment 1 was used. The result was a vibrotactile stimulation synchronized to the speech rhythm with the amplitude of each vibration burst adjusted based on the speech envelop as described in Section Developing and pretesting vibrotactile rhythm. The rhythmic amplitude modulated vibrotactile stimulation consisted of vibration bursts of 200 Hz sinusoidal waves with a duration of 70–85 ms, presented at a rate of 8 Hz. The vibrotactile stimuli was generated with a python program using the audio stimuli as input.

Light flickers: The same rhythmic amplitude modulated stimuli pattern as for the vibrotactile stimuli was used.

Background noise: Dialogs between a male and a female created by ATR (Advanced Technology Research Institute: https://www.atr.jp/index_e.html) were used. The dialogs mimicked typical office conversations such as making appointments, reservations, or discussing meeting details. An SNR of + 5 dB was selected to maintain clear intelligibility of the target audio and prevent masking by the background voice. Pilot testing at SNRs of 0 dB and + 2 dB indicated that participants were unable to recall the audio content, thereby justifying the use of + 5 dB in the main experiment.

Questionnaires

Cognitive load: Cognitive load was assessed with the Japanese version of the NASA Task Load Index (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.7160.

Attention difficulties: The same attention test as in Experiment 2 was used.

Stimuli evaluation: Participants rated the comfortness (7-point Likert scale ranging from uncomfortable to comfortable) and calmness (7-point Likert scale ranging from calm to arousing) of the vibrotactile and light stimuli and indicated whether the stimulation improved or worsened the listening performance (5-point scale ranging from “1-significantly reduced comprehensibility of the oral test” till “5-significantly improved comprehensibility of the test” with a neutral value “3-did not have any effect on comprehensibly” in between).

Electrodermal activity (EDA)

Electrodermal activity (EDA) was measured using the same settings as in Experiment 2.

Experimental setup

Participants were seated on a desk in front of a 27-inch computer screen at a distance of 75 cm. The screen had a light grey background with a black fixation cross at the middle of the screen. The EDA sensors were placed on the pointer and middle finger of the right hand. A small pat with a coin vibration motor with a diameter of 8 mm was placed on the left-hand side of the participant. In the vibration condition participants placed the pointer of the left hand over the vibration motor. Participants were wearing Shure SE215 ear buds.

Procedure

Participants were briefed on the study, gave informed consent, and completed questionnaires on personal information, followed by a hearing test. They practiced the recall task first without, then with background noise. EDA sensors were attached to the right-hand fingers. After a 5-min relaxation phase, the main test began: each block started with a practice item, followed by five test items. Participants fixated on a cross presented at a computer screen in front of them while listening, then recalled the content orally within 40 s after a signal tone. After each block, participants completed the NASA TLX, the attention questionnaire, and (if applicable) the stimuli evaluation questionnaire. The procedure was repeated for three blocks, with 2-min relaxation phases before the second and third blocks (see Fig. 6).

Experimental procedure for experiment 3.

Results

The data were tested for normality and homogeneity of variance. No violation of assumptions was detected. The audio-only condition served as the baseline, and planned comparisons were conducted between the audio + vibrotactile condition and the audio-only condition, as well as between the audio + light condition and the audio-only condition.

Speech recall

Planned Contrasts revealed that more items were recalled correctly in the audio + vibrotactile (M = 34.86, SD = 7.38) than audio only condition (M = 32.23, SD = 7.48), t (21) = -3.01, p = 0.0007, whereas no difference was found between audio + light (M = 33.95, SD = 7.69) and audio only condition, t (21) = 1.46, p = 0.15 (see Fig. 7).

Speech recall in audio only, audio + vibration, and audio + light conditions.

Questionnaires

Cognitive Load Questionnaire (NASA TLX): Planned Contrasts did not show significant differences between audio + vibrotactile (M = 5.18, SD = 0.74) and audio only condition (M = 5.30, SD = 0.96), t (21) = 1.09, p = 0.29, as well as between audio + light (M = 5.45, SD = 0.65) and audio only condition, t (21) = 0.78, p = 0.45.

Attention questionnaire : Planned contrasts revealed no differences between audio + vibrotactile (M = 4.05, SD = 1.28) and audio only condition (M = 4.29, SD = 1.05), , t (21) = 0.90, p = 0.38, as well as between audio + light (M = 4.36, SD = 1.30) and audio only condition, t (21) = 0.28, p = 0.78.

Stimuli evaluation: Three participants did not provide a rating for one of the stimulation types and were excluded from the analyses. The vibrotactile stimulation (M = 4.32, SD = 3.00) was rated as being more comfortable than the light stimulation (M = 2.58, SD = 2.37), t (18) = -3.32, p = 0.004. No differences were found in regard to perceived calmness: vibrotactile (M = 4.32, SD = 0.78), light (M = 4.63, SD = 1.13), t (18) < 1. Furthermore, even though participants did not perceive the vibrotactile stimuli as improving the listening performance (M = 3.10, SD = 0.78), it was overall rated more positively than the light stimuli (M = 2.42, SD = 0.59), t (18) = -2.97, p = 0.004.

Electrodermal activity (EDA)

Planned Contrasts revealed a lower SCL (in µS) in audio + vibrotactile (M = 4.60, SD = 2.23) compared to audio only condition (M = 5.27, SD = 2.44), t(21) = 3.80, p = 0.001, whereas SCL was comparable between audio + light (M = 5.10, SD = 2.19) and audio only condition, t(21) = -1.03, p = 0.31.

Correlational relationship between measured variables

Subjective reported attention problems correlated positively with subjective cognitive load (NASA TLX), r = 0.43, p = 0.0004. The more attention problems occurred the higher the cognitive load was estimated. Furthermore, cognitive load (NASA TLX) and speech recall correlated negatively, ρ = -0.27, p = 0.03 indicating that a higher cognitive load was associated with a lower recall performance.

Discussion

The results of the experiment revealed an improvement in speech recall in the audio + vibrotactile condition compared to audio only, but no effect for audio + light were shown. While participants recalled more items with vibrotactile stimuli, subjective reports showed no significant differences in perceived cognitive load or attention difficulties. Although a two-point improvement on a test with a maximum score of 45 may not seem substantial at first glance, it is important to consider this change within the context of our within-subjects design. In practical terms, this means that, on average, participants were able to recall two additional facts in one condition compared to the other. While the absolute number may be modest, small improvements in memory performance can be meaningful, especially in research settings such as ours, where each fact represents specific, targeted information.

Electrodermal activity (EDA) analysis indicated a significantly lower SCL in the audio + vibrotactile condition compared to audio only, suggesting a lower experienced cognitive load. Further analysis showed that higher scores on reported attention difficulties positively correlated with higher ratings of subjective cognitive load, and higher ratings of subjective cognitive load correlated with lower scores in speech recall. Thus, subjective and physiological measurements indicate that the effective use of memory resources in the audio + vibrotactile condition might have resulted in an improved memory performance. The results imply that the vibrotactile stimuli did make the cognitive processing more efficient (leading to lower SCL and better memory), but this efficiency gain was not explicitly registered by participants as a subjective reduction in workload.

General discussion

The aim of this study was to evaluate vibrotactile stimuli synchronized with specific features of the speech signal, and to determine whether this stimulation could reduce cognitive load while enhancing speech recall. A previous study by Yuan et al.26 demonstrated that dynamic visual feedback derived from the amplitude envelope of the speech signal improved speech perception. Following a similar approach, the first experiment employed vibrotactile stimuli based on the amplitude envelope. Although participants reported a perceived reduction in cognitive load, no improvement in speech recall was observed. Low ratings for the synchrony between the vibrotactile and audio signals suggested that multisensory integration was unsuccessful. While synchrony, in this case, alignment with changes in the amplitude envelope, is a prerequisite for multisensory integration21 the distinct characteristics of individual sensory channels, as well as the ways in which signals are processed within them, must also be considered for effective integration to occur.

The second experiment shifted the focus to attentional processes, adopting a different approach, based on research on how speech signals are processed. Previous studies have shown that speech entrainment (the alignment of neural activity with the temporal fluctuations of speech) is a critical component of speech comprehension43,44. Speech has an inherent rhythmic structure, determined by the number of syllables (or mora, in Japanese) per second51,50. Entrainment ensures that neural processes synchronize with this rhythm, enabling efficient allocation of attention and processing resources11,54. Building on this, the second experiment employed vibrotactile stimuli in the form of rhythmic vibration bursts synchronized with the speech rhythm. As in the first experiment, participants reported a reduction in cognitive load, however, speech recall did not significantly improve. This outcome stands in contrast to the findings of Guilleminot and Reichenbach8, who observed that rhythmic vibrotactile stimuli can enhance speech perception. At the same time, our results regarding cognitive load align with those of Marucci et al.25, who found that vibrotactile stimuli can effectively reduce cognitive load in high-demand conditions. Such load management is particularly important in speech-in-noise situations, where attentional resources are stretched30,33,69. In our case, the vibrotactile stimuli appeared to guide attentional processes, yet it did not lead to successful multisensory integration of the vibrotactile and auditory signals.

Synchronizing vibrotactile stimuli with either the amplitude envelope or the speech rhythm alone, as in the first and second experiments, proved insufficient. The third experiment, which focused specifically on memory processes, employed a combined approach. Previous research suggests that effective multisensory integration depends on factors such as spatial and temporal proximity, as well as the principle of inverse effectiveness21,23,24. To ensure both spatial and temporal proximity while also supporting rhythmic speech comprehension, the stimulation consisted of rhythmic vibration bursts aligned with the speech rhythm, with the amplitude of each burst modulated according to the amplitude envelope of the speech signal. Furthermore, because multisensory integration may be particularly advantageous under conditions of high perceptual load25,26, we created a high cognitive load condition by instructing participants to memorize orally presented stories, similar to the procedure used in the Wechsler Memory Test, in order to increase inverse effectiveness. Results of experiment three revealed that this combined approach proved most effective. The rhythmic amplitude-modulated vibrotactile stimuli not only reduced cognitive load but also enhanced memory performance.

Our study examined observable outcomes, but the underlying cognitive processes remain speculative. In the third experiment, participants had to process and remember a continuous flow of information, adding a crucial temporal element. Multisensory stimuli seemed especially helpful under these time-pressured conditions, where efficient allocation of cognitive resources was critical. When feedback is synchronized with the rhythm of speech, it can guide attention to important speech units and improve processing. This effect may be even stronger when competing stimuli introduce conflicting rhythms, as selectively enhancing the rhythm of the target can support neural entrainment and focus. Matching rhythms across different sensory inputs may also strengthen multisensory integration, as the brain tends to treat temporally aligned stimuli as related, thereby fostering unified memory representations. Future research should investigate these cognitive mechanisms by incorporating temporal features into multisensory stimuli, and applied studies should explore how to harness the brain’s tendency to link and integrate stimuli that consistently occur together.

Limitations

This study specifically focused on the Japanese language, using mora as the underlying speech unit. Further research is needed to determine whether these findings can be generalized to stress-timed languages such as English. We previously conducted a study demonstrating that amplitude-modulated rhythmic stimuli can help non-native speakers distinguish between stressed and unstressed syllables in English phrases70. However, we did not test vibrotactile stimulation under conditions involving speech-in-noise.

The discrepancies between subjective and objective cognitive load measures observed in Experiments 2 and 3 highlight the complex and multifaceted nature of cognitive load. Subjective measures capture conscious experience and perceived effort, which can be influenced by factors such as comfort, expectations, and perceived control. In contrast, objective physiological measures reflect the unconscious allocation of cognitive resources, which may not always align with an individual’s self-reported experience.

The lack of significant findings related to speech recall in the first and second experiments may, in part, be due to participants’ discomfort or dislike of vibrotactile stimulation. Some individuals appear to have an aversion to such stimili in general. To better accommodate individual preferences and enhance user experience, future research should explore alternative feedback modalities. It is also important to note that the three experiments investigated distinct aspects of speech recall, namely speech perception, attention, and memory. As a result, the effects of the different vibrotactile stimuli across the experiments are not directly comparable.

Conclusion

Overall, the findings of our study indicate that vibrotactile stimulation can be beneficial in tasks that place high demands on attentional resources and involve memory processes. Specifically, rhythmic amplitude-modulated vibrotactile stimuli, when synchronized with the rhythm of speech, may help regulate the temporal allocation of attention in accordance with the timing of speech-relevant units (mora), thereby supporting speech comprehension. By matching the rhythmic structure of the vibrotactile stimulation to the beat of the speech signal and dynamically modulating vibration intensity to mirror the amplitude profile of the auditory stream, the proposed stimulation more closely resembles the temporal and energetic patterns of natural speech. This approach is believed to improve multisensory integration by increasing the similarity and synchronicity between auditory and vibrotactile signals. Thus, the proposed vibrotactile stimulation has two essential features:

Rhythm – vibrations delivered in a recurring, patterned sequence similar to a beat.

Dynamic amplitude modulation – variation of vibration intensity over time in accordance with the amplitude profile of the speech signal.

Together, these features may enhance temporal attention and promote more effective multisensory processing in speech-related tasks.
