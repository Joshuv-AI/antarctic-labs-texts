Clinical Outcomes of Hinged Craniotomy as an Alternative Surgery Technique Compared to Conventional Decompressive Craniectomy for Traumatic Brain Injury: A Systematic Review
Authors: Luh Ari Devanita S, I Gede Raka Adhyatma, I Putu Ananta Wijaya Sabudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hinge craniotomy (HC) is a relatively underused surgical technique for managing increased intracranial pressure in patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI). As an alternative to conventional decompressive craniectomy (DC), HC preserves the native bone flap and may obviate the need for subsequent cranioplasty. This systematic review summarizes and compares the clinical outcomes of HC and DC in adult patients with TBI. Electronic databases were systematically searched for studies evaluating these 2 techniques. Outcomes, including functional recovery, mortality, complications, and reoperation rates, were analyzed and albatross plots were used to synthesize the data. Across studies, HC demonstrated outcomes broadly comparable to those of DC, with some studies suggesting trends toward lower early mortality and fewer poor functional outcomes, although these findings were not statistically significant. Overall, both procedures showed similar safety and efficacy profiles, whereas HC offered the potential advantage of avoiding secondary cranioplasty. Larger, well-designed prospective studies are required to confirm these findings and to better define the appropriate patient selection criteria.Trial RegistrationPROSPERO Identifier: CRD42025638233

INTRODUCTION

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is one of the most common traumas in emergency rooms. Around 1.2 million people die from severe TBI each year, according to the World Health Organization.2) By 2025, TBI is projected to become the leading cause of death and long-term disability globally.26) Beyond clinical results, TBI’s high death and morbidity rates cost society. Many survivors experience persistent neurocognitive and psychological deficits, which in turn impair their capacity to work and participate in social life.2) Despite its major impact, the development of an evidence-based framework to optimise management strategies continues to be a critical priority in global public health.

Following TBI, intracranial pressure (ICP) may rise as a consequence of mass effect caused by hematomas, cerebral contusions, diffuse swelling, or hydrocephalus.21) Elevated ICP compromises cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP), thereby predisposing the brain to ischemia.11) Numerous studies have demonstrated that intracranial hypertension after TBI is strongly linked to increased mortality.34) Management of TBI involves a stepwise approach, in which neurosurgical decompression plays a pivotal role in cases of severe intracranial hypertension.

Decompressive craniectomy (DC) relieves high ICP by removing a large section of the cerebral vault with dural opening.17) While this technique has demonstrated therapeutic benefit, several studies have highlighted ongoing concerns about the increased disability rates among survivors. Complications arising from bone removal have been well documented, including contralateral lesion expansion, infections, hydrocephalus, and the trephination syndrome.926) Recent randomised controlled trials have further clarified these issues. The DECRA trial5) Bifrontal DC for mild ICP did not preserve neuronal cells, however the RESCUEicp trial12) found that last-tier DC for severe, refractory ICP decreased death but increased disability.51213)

More recently, hinge craniotomy (HC) has been introduced as a less frequently applied surgical option for controlling elevated ICP in patients with TBI, aimed at achieving cerebral decompression.114) decompression is followed by posterior “floating” or “hinged” bone flap relocation. This approach expands to accommodate cerebral oedema without cranioplasty by keeping the bone flap in place. HC may have benefits over DC, including: (1) effective brain decompression while preserving the structural integrity of the skull, (2) better cosmetic outcomes by maintaining the cranial contour, (3) reduced risk of infection because the bone flap is preserved and re-secured, (4) maintenance of ICP equilibrium that may lower the incidence of trephination syndrome, and (5) decreased long-term expenses as the repositioned bone flap reduces the need for secondary cranial repair procedures, consequently lowering surgical, hospitalization, and postoperative care costs.7) Nevertheless, limitations have been reported, particularly the possibility of insufficient ICP reduction, which in some cases necessitates conversion to DC due to uncontrolled intracranial hypertension—often described as “HC failure.”

The comparison between HC and DC hinges not merely on survival after surgery, but equally on the quality of functional outcomes and the degree of morbidity patients experience.1418) Therefore, we conducted this study which aimed to provide an overview of the clinical outcomes comparison between HC and DC for TBI patients.

Study design and search strategy

PRISMA guides this systematic review and meta-analysis. Goal: Compare HC and DC outcomes in TBI patients. SSRN, PubMed, ScienceDirect, and the Cochrane Library were searched for all items from database inception to January 2025. TBI, HC, hinged skull flap, osteoplastic craniotomy, and DC were searched. Also used was reference list screening of qualifying papers and past reviews, as well as backward and forward citation monitoring to guarantee completeness.

Data extraction and statistical analysis

Rayyan.ai analyzed titles and abstracts to exclude non-eligible research. Both LADS and IGRA reviewed the selection procedure separately. A third reviewer (IPAWS) helped address disagreements until consensus was reached. research characteristics (author, year, sample size, research design) and patient demographics (age, sex, cause of injury) were extracted from each study using a standardized data extraction form, admission GCS, radiological findings, intervention details, and outcome measures (primary and secondary).

Risk-of-bias assessment

The included studies were evaluated for methodological quality using the Risk of Bias in Non-Randomised Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) instrument for observational studies19) and the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2) for randomized.20) All studies showed low, moderate, severe, or critical bias risk and “some concerns” or “no information.” LADS and IGRA separately evaluated risk-of-bias, and differences were resolved by consensus or majority decision.

Data synthesis and statistical methods

Interventions, research sites, designs, and outcome measures were too heterogeneous for a meta-analysis. When more than 5 studies contributed to a result, STATA albatross plots were used to graphically summarize the data. These graphs help analyze p-values in relation to research sample size and indicate connection strength.8) Each point represents a study, with its p-value displayed versus sample size and contour lines representing anticipated risk ratios. However, albatross plots provide only approximate visualization based on p-values and sample sizes and do not yield precise pooled effect estimates or confidence intervals comparable to meta-analysis.

RESULTS

One hundred twenty-six duplicates were found in the 10,574 entries found in the database search, leaving 10,446 for screening. Title and abstract screening eliminated 10,354 entries, leaving 92 for full-text examination. Seven more studies were found via citation searching. Poor functional outcomes

Seven studies reporting association between HC and poor functional outcomes (GOS 1–3 or severe disability) compared with standard DC were analyzed using albatross plot (FIGURE 4). Across 3 studies at hospital discharge, effect estimates were heterogeneous but largely non-significant in the smaller cohorts. Enomoto et al.6) and Mezue et al.15) reported trivial and non-significant associations, but in opposing directions (p=0.813, 0.837). Vilcinis et al.’s large cohort research25) (n=643) found a significant negative correlation (p<0.0001), indicating that HC decreased early bad outcomes compared to DC.

Albatross plot for poor functional outcomes.

RR: risk ratio.

At 3 months, Vankipuram et al.23) reported a non-significant trend toward reduced risk with HC (p=0.556). By 6 months, all observed associations favored HC but none reached statistical significance. Finally, at 12 months, Mishra et al.16) likewise showed a non-significant negative association (p=0.682). Overall, while the large discharge study suggests a potentially protective effect of HC early on, later follow-up data are underpowered and yield imprecise, non-significant estimates.

Mortality outcome

Seven studies reporting association between HC and mortality rates compared with standard DC was analyzed using albatross plot (FIGURE 5). At hospital discharge, an albatross plot demonstrated a majority negative association between HC and mortality rates, indicating HC serves as a protective factor. Significancy achieved in 2 studies, including Vilcinis et al.,25) as the largest cohort (p=0.0001) and smaller study by Enomoto et al.6) (p=0.021).

Albatross plot for mortality rate.

RR: risk ratio.

By 3 months, effect estimates hovered close to the null line, suggesting no detectable difference between HC and standard DC techniques. At 6 months, results were mixed, with Enomoto et al.6) again showed a small but significant negative association (p=0.03), whereas Vankipuram et al.23) showed non-significant positive association (p=0.60). Finally, at 12 months, Mishra et al.16) observed a non-significant trend toward negative association with HC (p=0.197). Overall, while discharge data, driven by a large cohort, indicate higher lower mortality risk with HC, later follow-up studies are underpowered and yield imprecise, inconsistent estimates.

Complications outcome

Across 4 studies reporting on any postoperative complication, the albatross plot points all lie to the negative association, indicating a trend toward fewer complications with HC versus standard decompression, but none reach statistical significance (FIGURE 6). When examining infection specifically, Mezue et al.15) likewise report a non-significant negative association (p=0.587), with the point near the null contour. Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) data from 3 cohorts are inconsistent. Mezue et al.15) and Vankipuram et al.23) suggest positive association in risk reductions, whereas Tsermoulas et al.22) trends toward a slight increase risk of ICH. However, all 3 ICH points remain close to the null line indicating non-significant effect.

Across the 3 studies reporting the need for re-operation, the albatross plot points lie on both sides but none reach statistical significance (FIGURE 7). DISCUSSION

In this systematic review, we present a comprehensive evaluation of HC versus standard DC in patients with TBI. While the number of included studies remains limited, the results suggest that HC may offer advantages in terms of both functional outcomes and mortality risk, particularly in the early postoperative period.

Functional outcomes

Our findings indicate a trend toward improved functional outcomes with HC, particularly evident in the large-scale cohort by Vilcinis et al.,25) which showed a statistically significant decrease in poor functional outcomes at the time of discharge (p<0.0001). Although most other studies reported non-significant results, they consistently showed trends favoring HC. These findings support previous scoping reviews suggesting that HC provides sufficient decompression while preserving CPP and minimizing secondary injury risks compared to DC.1418) Furthermore, the maintenance of bone flap integrity may contribute to better cerebrospinal fluid dynamics and reduce the risk of trephination syndrome.7)

Mortality outcomes

The analysis also reveals that HC may be associated with lower early mortality rates. Significant findings from Vilcinis et al.25) and Enomoto et al.6) support HC as a potentially protective surgical approach in the acute phase of TBI management. These results are in contrast with the DECRA trial, which reported no functional benefit and potential harm from early bifrontal DC in moderate intracranial hypertension.5) Similarly, the RESCUEicp trial showed that although DC can lower mortality in cases of refractory intracranial hypertension, it is associated with higher rates of severe disability.12) HC, therefore, may offer a middle ground—providing decompression while avoiding the full morbidity associated with DC.

Postoperative complications

Preservation of the bone flap may lower the risk of infection, hematoma growth, and hydrocephalus, which are prevalent after DC bone excision and delayed cranioplasty.791726) HC also avoids the need for a second-stage procedure, thus reducing the risk of cranioplasty-related complications.714)

Reoperation rates

A potential drawback of HC noted in 2 studies (Enomoto et al.6) and Gamboa-Oñate et al.7)) is the slightly higher trend toward reoperation, even though the observed differences did not attain statistical significance. This observation may reflect the limitation of HC in cases of extreme cerebral edema, where decompression is insufficient, and conversion to DC becomes necessary. These findings highlight the importance of intraoperative assessment and careful patient selection to minimize the risk of HC “failure.”18)

Selection bias and indication heterogeneity

An important limitation of this comparison is the inherent selection bias arising from different clinical indications and severity profiles between HC and DC. In most included studies, DC was preferentially applied in patients with more severe cerebral edema or refractory intracranial hypertension, whereas HC was often selected for patients with anticipated moderate swelling or intraoperative brain expansion. This confounding by indication may partially explain the observed trends favoring HC and limits causal interpretation, particularly in observational cohorts.

Clinical implications

Overall, this review found that HC and DC provide comparable levels of safety and effectiveness in treating TBI. A number of studies indicated that HC may reduce early mortality and improve functional recovery, but these observations were not consistently statistically significant, highlighting the need for cautious conclusions.

HC’s main therapeutic benefit lies in achieving cerebral decompression while preserving the native bone flap, thereby reducing the need for secondary cranioplasty and its associated complications. Across the included studies, HC was most commonly applied in patients with acute subdural hematoma, cerebral contusions, or intraoperative brain swelling in whom moderate and potentially reversible cerebral edema was anticipated, whereas standard DC was generally reserved for patients with more severe or refractory intracranial hypertension and extensive hemispheric swelling. Such advantages render HC an appealing option in resource-constrained environments, particularly as it reduces the likelihood of additional surgery. However, based on the current evidence, HC cannot be recommended as a standard strategy and should instead be considered an alternative approach in carefully selected patients.

Strengths and limitations

A thorough search technique, PRISMA methodology, independent dual risk-of-bias evaluations, and albatross plots to visually synthesize varied findings are strengths of this research8) Nonetheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged, including the limited number of randomized controlled trials, inconsistency in outcome definitions and follow-up periods, and the inherent susceptibility of observational studies to bias. In addition, restricting inclusion to English-language publications may have introduced publication bias. Two randomized trials by Vankipuram et al.2324) were considered independent based on study design and reporting; however, because the later publication does not explicitly state whether participants from the pilot study were excluded, partial population overlap cannot be completely ruled out. Lastly, the use of albatross plots, while useful for visual synthesis, does not allow integrated estimation of effect sizes or formal quantitative pooling, limiting the precision of comparative inferences.

CONCLUSION

This systematic review suggests that HC and DC may yield broadly comparable outcomes in selected adult patients with TBI. However, the available evidence is limited by small sample sizes, retrospective designs, and substantial heterogeneity, and does not permit definitive conclusions regarding superiority of either technique.

The key practical advantage of HC is the avoidance of a second cranioplasty procedure, which may reduce overall surgical burden and long-term complications. Future large-scale randomized controlled studies are required to establish HC’s involvement and discover which patient groups may benefit most.
