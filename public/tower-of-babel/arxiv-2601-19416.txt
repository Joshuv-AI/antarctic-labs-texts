Jacobi-Piñeiro Multiple Orthogonal Polynomials on the simplex
Lidia Fernández, Ana Foulquié-Moreno and Juan Antonio Villegas

Abstract
. It is known that Rodrigues formulas provide a very powerful tool to compute orthogonal polynomials with respect to classical weights. We provide an example of bivariate multiple polynomials on the simplex defined via a Rodrigues formula. This approach offers a natural generalization of Jacobi–Piñeiro polynomials to the multivariate setting. Moreover, we apply these polynomials to the study of the bivariate Hermite–Padé problem on the triangle.

33C45, 42C05

1.
Introduction

Within the framework of Orthogonal Polynomials theory
[
15
, Chapter 1]
, the properties of the so-called classical weight functions are well known. These weights are functions
\omega(x)
that satisfy the Pearson equation

\displaystyle(\phi(x)\omega(x))^{\prime}+\varphi(x)\omega(x)=0,

(1)

where
\phi(x)
and
\varphi(x)
are polynomials of degrees two and one respectively. Among other characteristic properties, orthogonal polynomials with respect to classical weights always admit a representation in terms of a Rodrigues formula. Indeed, if
\omega(x)
is a classical weight satisfying (
1
), then the associated orthogonal polynomials sequence
\{P_{n}\}
can be expressed (up to a multiplicative constant) as

P_{n}(x)=\dfrac{1}{\omega(x)}\dfrac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}(\omega(x)\phi(x)^{n}).

(2)

In general, Rodrigues formulas provide a powerful and efficient method for computing orthogonal polynomials associated with classical weights.

In recent years, one of the most active research topics in Orthogonal Polynomials and Special Functions theory has been multiple orthogonality
[
27
,
38
]
. In this extension of the standard theory, polynomials – known as Multiple Orthogonal Polynomials – satisfy orthogonality conditions with respect to more than one measure (see Section
2
). Within this framework,
r
classical weight functions
\omega_{j}
are considered in
[
6
,
9
]
. Moreover, explicit expressions for several families of type II multiple orthogonal polynomials with respect to classical measures are obtained by applying several Rodrigues operators. Analogous results for classical discrete measures are presented in
[
7
]
.

The concept of the Rodrigues formula has also been generalized to the multivariate case using different approaches
[
2
,
4
,
22
,
26
,
28
,
35
]
.

One of the most studied and well-known extensions of Jacobi polynomials to the bivariate setting are the families of orthogonal polynomials associated with the weight function

W^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x,y)=x^{\alpha}y^{\beta}(1-x-y)^{\gamma},\qquad\text{with }\alpha,\beta,\gamma>-1,

(3)

supported on the triangular domain

T=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x\geq 0,\;y\geq 0,\;x+y\leq 1\},

(4)

see
[
4
, Section XXXIII]
or
[
22
, Section 2.4]
. In the bivariate framework, uniqueness up to a multiplicative constant no longer holds, in contrast to the univariate case. As a consequence, several bases of
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{2}(W^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)})
exist, where
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{2}
denotes the set of polynomials of degree
n
, orthogonal to all polynomials of degree less than or equal to
n-1
with respect to the weight (
3
). For
0\leq k\leq n
, one such basis is given by the Rodrigues-type formula (see
[
22
, Proposition 2.4.3]
,
[
35
, Chapter V, Section 4]
or
[
4
, Section XXXIII, Eq. (5)]
).

U^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}_{k,n}(x,y)=\dfrac{1}{W^{(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)}(x,y)}\dfrac{\partial^{n}}{\partial x^{\,n-k}\partial y^{\,k}}\left(x^{\alpha+n-k}y^{\beta+k}(1-x-y)^{\gamma+n}\right).

(5)

Observe that this expression constitutes a bivariate extension of the Rodrigues formula for Jacobi polynomials
[
36
, Section 4.3]

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{n}n!}(1-x)^{-\alpha}(1+x)^{-\beta}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}\left[(1-x)^{n+\alpha}(1+x)^{n+\beta}\right].

Although univariate multiple orthogonality and multivariate standard orthogonality have been, and still are, extensively researched, the study of bivariate multiple orthogonality is only now taking its first steps. In
[
23
]
, a general definition of type I and type II bivariate multiple orthogonal polynomials was introduced on the two-dimensional lattice, together with several extended results and examples. More recently,
[
29
]
addressed the case of mixed-type bivariate multiple orthogonal polynomials on the step-line. However, to date, no extensions of Rodrigues formulas have been provided for constructing any kind of multiple orthogonal polynomials in the multivariate setting.

Another point to consider is the relevance of Orthogonal Polynomials (and their extensions) in some other topics
[
31
]
. One of the most representative applications concerning (multiple) orthogonal polynomials is the problem of rational approximation of functions
[
37
]
, which has been even studied in the multivariate case; see, for example,
[
10
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
16
]
, where several possible extensions of Padé approximation are proposed. In
[
34
]
, a new approach to Hermite–Padé approximation is introduced, which inspired the methodology used in Section
4
.

The structure of the manuscript is as follows: in Section
2
we provide a preliminary introduction to multiple orthogonal polynomials and their application to Hermite–Padé rational approximation in the univariate setting. Next, in Section
3
, we define the bivariate Jacobi–Piñeiro polynomials, which satisfy orthogonality conditions with respect to different Jacobi measures on the triangle, along with other desirable properties. This section is divided into three cases: the basic case with only two measures and two variables, the case with
r\geq 2
measures and two variables, and finally we address the case with
d\geq 2
variables. Once these polynomials and their main properties are established, Section
4
introduces a bivariate analogue of the Hermite–Padé approximation problem and its solution, which relies on the bivariate Jacobi–Piñeiro polynomials. Next, in Section
5
, we choose a particular pair of Jacobi measures on the triangle and present a numerical implementation of the approximants constructed in the previous section. Finally, Section
6
summarizes the main conclusions of this work and discusses several open questions and possible directions for future research.

2.
Preliminaries: Multiple Orthogonal Polynomials

As previously mentioned, Multiple Orthogonal Polynomials (MOP) extend the standard theory of Orthogonal Polynomials by satisfying orthogonality conditions with respect to several measures. In this section, we present the main definitions of multiple orthogonality in the univariate setting, together with the relevant notation and illustrative examples, with special emphasis on Jacobi–Piñeiro polynomials.

From now on, let us consider a number
r\geq 2
of positive measures supported on
\mathbb{R}
, denoted by
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r}
. Throughout this section, we assume that these measures are absolutely continuous,
i.e.
, there exist positive weight functions
\omega_{j}(x)
such that
\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x)=\omega_{j}(x)\mathrm{d}x
, for
j=1,\dots,r
. This collection of measures is commonly referred to as a
system of measures
, and it will be fixed for the remainder of the section.

There are two types of multiple orthogonality: type I and type II. In type II multiple orthogonality, a single polynomial is considered, and its orthogonality conditions are distributed among the measures. In contrast, type I multiple orthogonality involves
r
distinct polynomials, possibly of different degrees, which jointly satisfy a system of orthogonality conditions. The way in which the conditions are split in type II, as well as the degree constraints in type I, is determined by a multi-index
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r}
, whose
\ell_{1}
norm or modulus is defined as
|\vec{n}|=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}
.

More precisely, we define type I and type II MOP as follows:

Definition 2.1
.

Given
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r}
, the type I Multiple Orthogonal Polynomials
A_{\vec{n},1}(x),\dots,A_{\vec{n},r}(x)
satisfy
\deg(A_{\vec{n},j})\leq n_{j}-1
for
j=1,\dots,r
, and

\sum_{j=1}^{r}\int_{\mathbb{R}}A_{\vec{n},j}(x)x^{l}\omega_{j}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\begin{cases}0\qquad\text{ if }0\leq l<|\vec{n}|-1,\\
1\qquad\text{ if }l=|\vec{n}|-1.\end{cases}

(6)

Definition 2.2
.

Given
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r}
, the type II Multiple Orthogonal Polynomial
P_{\vec{n}}(x)
is the unique monic polynomial of degree
|\vec{n}|
satisfying

\int_{\mathbb{R}}P_{\vec{n}}(x)x^{l}\omega_{j}(x)\mathrm{d}x=0\qquad\text{for }0\leq l<n_{j},\quad j=1,\dots,r.

(7)

In this work, we focus exclusively on type II MOP. For a more detailed introduction and a comprehensive discussion of their properties as extensions of standard orthogonality, we refer the reader to
[
23
, Section 2]
,
[
27
, Section 23.1]
, or
[
38
, Section 1]
.

The most well-known examples of MOP are those associated with classical weights
[
6
]
: Hermite, Laguerre, and Jacobi. This framework leads, for instance, to the multiple Hermite polynomials
[
12
]
, which find applications in random matrices
[
11
]
and non-intersecting Brownian motions
[
20
]
; multiple Laguerre polynomials
[
12
]
; and the special case of multiple Jacobi polynomials, which we describe in more detail below.

The Jacobi weight for
\alpha,\beta>-1
is given by
(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}
, defined on the interval
[-1,1]
. The main ways of constructing multiple Jacobi polynomials are:

•

Using Jacobi weights defined on
r
disjoint intervals, which gives rise to the Jacobi–Angelesco polynomials
[
21
]
,
[
27
, Section 23.3.1]
.

•

Assigning
r
different values
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}
to the parameter
\alpha
and mapping the interval
[-1,1]
to
[0,1]
. This procedure yields the Jacobi–Piñeiro polynomials
[
9
,
33
]
.

Throughout this document, we focus on the latter family. As mentioned, Jacobi polynomials are defined as orthogonal polynomials with respect to the weight
(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}
,
x\in[-1,1]
, but, up to a linear transformation, we might consider the weight
\omega^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)=x^{\alpha}(1-x)^{\beta}
with
x\in[0,1]
. Then, employing
r
weight functions of this type but with different values of
\alpha
, we define

\omega_{j}(x)=x^{\alpha_{j}}(1-x)^{\beta},\qquad x\in[0,1],\quad j=1,\dots,r,

(8)

where
\alpha_{j},\beta>-1
. To guarantee the existence of MOP for every multi-index
\vec{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, it is necessary to impose that
\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}\notin\mathbb{Z}
whenever
i\neq j

[
5
]
. In this setting, for a multi-index
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})
, the type II Jacobi–Piñeiro polynomial
P^{(\vec{\alpha},\beta)}_{\vec{n}}(x)
is monic and satisfies the orthogonality conditions (
7
)
[
38
, Section 3.7]
.

These polynomials have been widely studied ever since the emergence of multiple orthogonality. Several representations in terms of hypergeometric functions can be found in
[
6
,
9
]
, as well as applications in rational approximation, random walks, number theory, urn models, among others
[
13
,
14
,
24
,
25
,
30
,
31
]
. For our purposes, the most convenient representation is given by the Rodrigues formula
[
6
]
:

P^{(\vec{\alpha},\beta)}_{\vec{n}}(x)=\left(\dfrac{1}{\omega_{r}(x)}\dfrac{d^{\,n_{r}}}{dx^{n_{r}}}\omega_{r}(x)\right)\cdots\left(\dfrac{1}{\omega_{1}(x)}\dfrac{d^{\,n_{1}}}{dx^{n_{1}}}\omega_{1}(x)\right)\phi^{|\vec{n}|}(x),

(9)

where
\phi(x)=x(x-1)
. Note that (
9
) is the composition of the
r
Rodrigues operators

D_{j}[f]=\dfrac{1}{\omega_{j}(x)}\dfrac{d^{n_{j}}}{dx^{n_{j}}}(\omega_{j}(x)f(x)),\qquad j=1,\dots,r,

(10)

applied to a polynomial. In fact, (
2
), up to a linear transformation, is a particular case of (
9
) with
r=1
.

Concerning the possible applications of multiple orthogonal polynomials, and in particular Jacobi–Piñeiro polynomials, it is essential to mention the Hermite–Padé rational approximation problem. This problem extends the classical Padé approximation problem, with the main objective of finding rational approximations to functions given by a Stieltjes transform

f_{j}(z)=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x)}{z-x},

(11)

with a common denominator
[
32
, Chapter 4]
. More precisely, for a multi-index
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})
, the type II Hermite–Padé approximation (see
[
37
, Sections 2.1 and 2.2]
for type I Hermite–Padé approximation) consists of finding a polynomial
P_{\vec{n}}
of degree
|\vec{n}|
and
r
polynomials
Q_{\vec{n},1},\dots,Q_{\vec{n},r}
such that

f_{j}(z)P_{\vec{n}}(z)=Q_{\vec{n},j}(z)+\mathcal{O}(z^{-n_{j}-1}),\qquad z\to\infty,\quad j=1,\dots,r.

(12)

The solution to this problem is provided by the type II multiple orthogonal polynomial
P_{\vec{n}}
for the system of measures
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r}
, and

Q_{\vec{n},j}(z)=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\dfrac{P_{\vec{n}}(z)-P_{\vec{n}}(x)}{z-x}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x).

In the Jacobi–Piñeiro case,
i.e.
, when
\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x)=x^{\alpha_{j}}(1-x)^{\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x
on
[0,1]
, this problem produces rational approximations to certain values of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(z)
. In particular, it has been proved that
\zeta(3)
is irrational
[
3
]
, that infinitely many
\zeta(2k+1)
are irrational
[
8
]
, and that at least one among
\zeta(5),\zeta(7),\zeta(9),\zeta(11)
is irrational
[
39
]
.

Having established these preliminaries on MOP and their applications in the univariate setting, we now state the objectives of this paper. The main purpose is to address some of the gaps mentioned throughout Sections
1
and
2
: we provide an example of bivariate multiple polynomials defined via a Rodrigues formula, analogous to (
5
) and (
9
). Using these polynomials, and motivated by their application to Hermite–Padé rational approximation, we define and study the Hermite–Padé problem in two variables. Specifically, we construct approximants for two functions, analogous to (
11
), using two distinct Jacobi measures on the triangle, see (
13
). These approximants share a common denominator, which is precisely the aforementioned bivariate polynomial. In this way, we introduce a natural analogue of Jacobi–Piñeiro multiple orthogonal polynomials and their application to Hermite–Padé approximation in the bivariate setting.

3.
Jacobi–Piñeiro polynomials on the Triangle

As described in Section
2
, the Type II Jacobi–Piñeiro polynomials
P^{(\vec{\alpha},\beta)}_{\vec{n}}(x)
are multiple orthogonal with respect to the weight functions
\omega_{j}(x)
given in (
8
), and can be expressed via a Rodrigues formula (
9
).

In this section, we will consider a bivariate extension of these polynomials in a triangular domain

T=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x\geq 0,y\geq 0,x+y\leq 1\},

(
4
)

often also referred to as the
simplex
, and provide a representation of these polynomials based on a Rodrigues-type formula.

3.1.
The two-measure case

For now on, in order to simplify the discussions, we will consider the case
r=2
, so we will work with two weights. The general case
r\geq 2
is analogous and will be discussed in Subsection
3.2
.

Define the following weights on
T
and their respective measures:

\begin{array}[]{cc}W_{1}(x,y)=x^{\alpha_{1}}y^{\beta_{1}}(1-x-y)^{\gamma},&\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}(x,y)=W_{1}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\\
W_{2}(x,y)=x^{\alpha_{2}}y^{\beta_{2}}(1-x-y)^{\gamma},&\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}(x,y)=W_{2}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\end{array}

(13)

where
\alpha_{j},\beta_{j},\gamma>-1,\quad j=1,2
and
(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})\neq(\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})
. Next, we will introduce a differential Rodrigues operator, extending (
10
), that will be useful for the following definitions and properties.

Definition 3.1
.

For a fixed
j\in\{1,2\}
, consider the weight
W_{j}(x,y)=x^{\alpha_{j}}y^{\beta_{j}}(1-x-y)^{\gamma}
and two non-negative integers
0\leq k_{j}\leq n_{j}
. Given a function
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we define the operator

D_{j}[f(x,y)]:=\dfrac{1}{W_{j}(x,y)}\dfrac{\partial^{n_{j}}}{\partial x^{n_{j}-k_{j}}y^{k_{j}}}\left(W_{j}(x,y)x^{n_{j}-k_{j}}y^{k_{j}}f(x,y)\right).

(14)

Using some extensions of the Rodrigues formula for Jacobi–Piñeiro polynomials (
9
), we define the following bivariate function:

Definition 3.2
.

Consider two pairs of non-negative integers
0\leq k_{1}\leq n_{1}
and
0\leq k_{2}\leq n_{2}
and denote
\vec{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
,
\vec{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})
. Define

\begin{split}P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(x,y):=&D_{2}\circ D_{1}[(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+n_{2}}]\\
=&\frac{1}{W_{2}(x,y)}\frac{\partial^{n_{2}}}{\partial x^{n_{2}-k_{2}}\partial y^{k_{2}}}\Big(x^{n_{2}-k_{2}+\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}}y^{k_{2}+\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}}\times\\
&\;\;\;\times\frac{\partial^{n_{1}}}{\partial x^{n_{1}-k_{1}}\partial y^{k_{1}}}\left(x^{n_{1}-k_{1}+\alpha_{1}}y^{k_{1}+\beta_{1}}(1-x-y)^{\gamma+n_{1}+n_{2}}\right)\Big).\end{split}

(15)

Next, we will show that this function is a polynomial of degree
n_{1}+n_{2}
in the variables
x
and
y
, and that it satisfies certain orthogonality conditions with respect to the weights
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
on the triangle
T
. First, let us introduce the following result:

Lemma 3.3
.

Given
l,m,n\geq 0
with
n\geq l+m
, consider
x^{l}y^{m}(1-x-y)^{n-l-m}
, a polynomial of degree
n
. For a fixed
j
, let
D_{j}
be the operator introduced in (
14
). If
n>n_{j}
, then
D_{j}[x^{l}y^{m}(1-x-y)^{n-l-m}]
is a linear combination of polynomials of degree
n
, therefore a polynomial of degree
n
.

Proof.

Splitting the derivatives respect to
x
and
y
:

D_{j}[x^{l}y^{m}(1-x-y)^{n-l-m}]

=x^{-\alpha_{j}}y^{-\beta_{j}}(1-x-y)^{-\gamma}\dfrac{\partial^{n_{j}}}{\partial x^{n_{j}-k_{j}}y^{k_{j}}}\left(x^{\alpha_{j}+n_{j}-k_{j}+l}y^{\beta_{j}+k_{j}+m}(1-x-y)^{\gamma+n-l-m}\right)

=x^{-\alpha_{j}}y^{-\beta_{j}}(1-x-y)^{-\gamma}\dfrac{\partial^{n_{j}-k_{j}}}{\partial x^{n_{j}-k_{j}}}\left(x^{\alpha_{j}+n_{j}-k_{j}+l}\dfrac{\partial^{k_{j}}}{\partial y^{k_{j}}}(y^{\beta_{j}+k_{j}+m}(1-x-y)^{\gamma+n-l-m})\right).

Focus on the first derivative and apply Leibniz’s rule:

\dfrac{\partial^{k_{j}}}{\partial y^{k_{j}}}(y^{\beta_{j}+k_{j}+m}(1-x-y)^{\gamma+n-l-m})=\sum_{i=0}^{k_{j}}\binom{k_{j}}{i}\dfrac{\partial^{k_{j}-i}}{\partial y^{k_{j}-i}}y^{\beta_{j}+k_{j}+m}\,\dfrac{\partial^{i}}{\partial y^{i}}(1-x-y)^{\gamma+n-l-m}

=\sum_{i=0}^{k_{j}}c(i)\,y^{\beta_{j}+m+i}(1-x-y)^{\gamma+n-l-m-i}

where

c(i):=(-1)^{i}\binom{k_{j}}{i}(\beta_{j}+m+i+1)_{k_{j}-i}(\gamma+n-l-m-i+1)_{i},\qquad 0\leq i\leq k_{j}.

(16)

Multiplying by
x^{\alpha_{j}+n_{j}-k_{j}+l}
and computing the derivative with respect to x we obtain:

\dfrac{\partial^{n_{j}-k_{j}}}{\partial x^{n_{j}-k_{j}}}\left(x^{\alpha_{j}+n_{j}-k_{j}+l}\dfrac{\partial^{k_{j}}}{\partial y^{k_{j}}}(y^{\beta_{j}+k_{j}+m}(1-x-y)^{\gamma+n-l-m})\right)

=\sum_{i=0}^{k_{j}}c(i)y^{\beta_{j}+m+i}\dfrac{\partial^{n_{j}-k_{j}}}{\partial x^{n_{j}-k_{j}}}x^{\alpha_{j}+n_{j}-k_{j}+l}(1-x-y)^{\gamma+n-l-m-i}.

Again, by Leibniz rule,

\dfrac{\partial^{n_{j}-k_{j}}}{\partial x^{n_{j}-k_{j}}}x^{\alpha_{j}+n_{j}-k_{j}+l}(1-x-y)^{\gamma+n-l-m-i}=\sum_{p=0}^{n_{j}-k_{j}}(-1)^{p}\binom{n_{j}-k_{j}}{p}(\alpha_{j}+l+p+1)_{n_{j}-k_{j}-p}\times\\
\times(\gamma+n-l-m-i-p+1)_{p}\,x^{\alpha_{j}+l+p}(1-x-y)^{\gamma+n-l-m-m-i-p}.

If we denote

c(p,i):=c(i)(-1)^{p}\binom{n_{j}-k_{j}}{p}(\alpha_{j}+l+p+1)_{n_{j}-k_{j}-p}(\gamma+n-l-m-i-p+1)_{p},\quad 0\leq p\leq n_{j}-k_{j},\ 0\leq i\leq k_{j},

(17)

then, we get

D_{j}[x^{l}y^{m}(1-x-y)^{n-l-m}]

=x^{-\alpha_{j}}y^{-\beta_{j}}(1-x-y)^{-\gamma}\sum_{p=0}^{n_{j}-k_{j}}\sum_{i=0}^{k_{j}}c(p,i)x^{\alpha_{j}+l+p}y^{\beta_{j}+m+i}(1-x-y)^{\gamma+n-l-m-i-p}

and consequently,

D_{j}[x^{l}y^{m}(1-x-y)^{n-l-m}]=\sum_{p=0}^{n_{j}-k_{j}}\sum_{i=0}^{k_{j}}c(p,i)x^{l+p}y^{m+i}(1-x-y)^{n-l-m-i-p},

(18)

which is a linear combination of polynomials of degree
n
,
i.e.
, a polynomial of degree
n
.
∎

A direct consequence of the previous Lemma is that
P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(x,y)
is a polynomial of total degree
n_{1}+n_{2}
. In the following proposition, we nest the operators
D_{2}
and
D_{1}
to get the explicit expression of the polynomial.

Proposition 3.4
.

Consider two pairs of non-negative integers
0\leq k_{1}\leq n_{1}
and
0\leq k_{2}\leq n_{2}
. Then, the function
P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(x,y)
is a polynomial of total degree
n_{1}+n_{2}
in the variables
x
and
y
, and its explicit expression is given by:

P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(x,y)=\sum_{l=0}^{n_{1}+n_{2}-k_{1}-k_{2}}\ \sum_{m=0}^{k_{1}+k_{2}}c(l,m)x^{l}y^{m}(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+n_{2}-l-m}.

(19)

where the coefficients
c(l,m)
are given by:

c(l,m)=\sum_{\tilde{p}=0}^{l}\sum_{\tilde{i}=0}^{m}A(\tilde{p},\tilde{i})B(\tilde{p},\tilde{i},l-\tilde{p},m-\tilde{i}),

(20)

with

A(p,i)=\dfrac{(-1)^{2p+2i}}{p!\,i!}(k_{1}-n_{1})_{p}(-k_{1})_{i}(\alpha_{1}+p+1)_{n_{1}-k_{1}-p}(\beta_{1}+i+1)_{k_{1}-i}\times\\
\times(\gamma+n_{1}+n_{2}-i+1)_{i}(\gamma+n_{1}+n_{2}-i-p+1)_{p}

and

B(p,i,h,s):=\dfrac{(-1)^{2h+2s}}{h!s!}(k_{2}-n_{2})_{h}(-k_{2})_{s}(\alpha_{2}+p+h+1)_{n_{2}-k_{2}-h}\times\\
\times(\beta_{2}+i+s+1)_{k_{2}-s}(\gamma+n_{1}+n_{2}-p-i-s+1)_{s}(\gamma+n_{1}+n_{2}-p-i-h-s+1)_{h},

for
0\leq p\leq n_{1}-k_{1}
,
0\leq i\leq k_{1}
,
0\leq h\leq n_{2}-k_{2}
,
0\leq s\leq k_{2}
.

Proof.

Since
P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(x,y)=D_{2}\circ D_{1}[(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+n_{2}}]
, from Lemma
3.3
with
l=m=0
,
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
, we obtain that
P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(x,y)
is a polynomial of degree
n_{1}+n_{2}
. Moreover, we use (
18
) to calculate an explicit expression. Thus, we have

D_{1}[(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+n_{2}}]=\sum_{p=0}^{n_{1}-k_{1}}\sum_{i=0}^{k_{1}}A(p,i)x^{p}y^{i}(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+n_{2}-i-p},

where, using
\binom{a}{b}=(-1)^{b}\frac{(-a)_{b}}{b!}
in (
17
), we denote

A(p,i)=\dfrac{(-1)^{2p+2i}}{p!\,i!}(k_{1}-n_{1})_{p}(-k_{1})_{i}(\alpha_{1}+p+1)_{n_{1}-k_{1}-p}(\beta_{1}+i+1)_{k_{1}-i}\times\\
\times(\gamma+n_{1}+n_{2}-i+1)_{i}(\gamma+n_{1}+n_{2}-i-p+1)_{p}

for
0\leq p\leq n_{1}-k_{1}
,
0\leq i\leq k_{1}
. Since
D_{2}
is a linear operator, then we have

P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(x,y)=\sum_{p=0}^{n_{1}-k_{1}}\sum_{i=0}^{k_{1}}A(p,i)D_{2}[x^{p}y^{i}(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+n_{2}-i-p}],

so that, again, we can use Lemma
3.3
to get

D_{2}[x^{p}y^{i}(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+n_{2}-i-p}]=\sum_{h=0}^{n_{2}-k_{2}}\sum_{s=0}^{k_{2}}B(p,i,h,s)x^{p+h}y^{i+s}(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+n_{2}-i-p-h-s}

where

B(p,i,h,s):=\dfrac{(-1)^{2h+2s}}{h!s!}(k_{2}-n_{2})_{h}(-k_{2})_{s}(\alpha_{2}+p+h+1)_{n_{2}-k_{2}-h}\times\\
\times(\beta_{2}+i+s+1)_{k_{2}-s}(\gamma+n_{1}+n_{2}-p-i-s+1)_{s}(\gamma+n_{1}+n_{2}-p-i-h-s+1)_{h},

for
0\leq p\leq n_{1}-k_{1}
,
0\leq i\leq k_{1}
,
0\leq h\leq n_{2}-k_{2}
,
0\leq s\leq k_{2}
. This way, the explicit expression of the polynomial would be

P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(x,y)=\sum_{p=0}^{n_{1}-k_{1}}\sum_{i=0}^{k_{1}}\sum_{h=0}^{n_{2}-k_{2}}\sum_{s=0}^{k_{2}}A(p,i)B(p,i,h,s)x^{p+h}y^{i+s}(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+n_{2}-i-p-h-s}

Finally, for
0\leq l\leq n_{1}+n_{2}-k_{1}-k_{2}
and
0\leq m\leq k_{1}+k_{2}
, the coefficient
c(l,m)
in (
20
), the one multiplying the term
x^{l}y^{m}(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+n_{2}-l-m}
is obtained by summing all the terms where
p+h=l
and
i+s=m
, and taking into account that
A(p,i)=0
, for
p>n_{1}-k_{1}
or
i>k_{1}
, and
B(p,i,h,s)=0
, for
h>n_{2}-k_{2}
or
s>k_{2}
.
∎

Next, we will show the symmetry of the construction given in (
15
), that is, the polynomial does not change by swapping the measures
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
. First, let us show the key property of the operators
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Given a function
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, the operators
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
commute, i.e.:

D_{1}\circ D_{2}[f(x,y)]=D_{2}\circ D_{1}[f(x,y)].

(21)

Proof.

According to (
14
), we have that

D_{1}\circ D_{2}[f(x,y)]=x^{-\alpha_{1}}y^{-\beta_{1}}(1-x-y)^{-\gamma}\frac{\partial^{n_{1}}}{\partial x^{n_{1}-k_{1}}\partial y^{k_{1}}}\Big(x^{n_{1}-k_{1}+\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}}y^{k_{1}+\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}}\times\\
\times\frac{\partial^{n_{2}}}{\partial x^{n_{2}-k_{2}}\partial y^{k_{2}}}\left(x^{n_{2}-k_{2}+\alpha_{2}}y^{k_{2}+\beta_{2}}(1-x-y)^{\gamma}f(x,y)\right)\Big)

Now, let us take the formal Laurent series expansion of the function
(1-x-y)^{\gamma}f(x,y)
as

(1-x-y)^{\gamma}f(x,y)=\sum_{\nu,\sigma=-\infty}^{+\infty}c_{\nu,\sigma}x^{\nu}y^{\sigma}.

(22)

This way, we have

D_{1}\circ D_{2}[f(x,y)]=(1-x-y)^{-\gamma}\sum_{\nu,\sigma=-\infty}^{+\infty}c_{\nu,\sigma}x^{-\alpha_{1}}y^{-\beta_{1}}\frac{\partial^{n_{1}}}{\partial x^{n_{1}-k_{1}}\partial y^{k_{1}}}\Big(x^{n_{1}-k_{1}+\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}}y^{k_{1}+\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}}\times\\
\times\frac{\partial^{n_{2}}}{\partial x^{n_{2}-k_{2}}\partial y^{k_{2}}}\left(x^{n_{2}-k_{2}+\alpha_{2}+\nu}y^{k_{2}+\beta_{2}+\sigma}\right)\Big)

Let us focus on the term

x^{-\alpha_{1}}y^{-\beta_{1}}\frac{\partial^{n_{1}}}{\partial x^{n_{1}-k_{1}}\partial y^{k_{1}}}\Big(x^{n_{1}-k_{1}+\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}}y^{k_{1}+\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}}\frac{\partial^{n_{2}}}{\partial x^{n_{2}-k_{2}}\partial y^{k_{2}}}\left(x^{n_{2}-k_{2}+\alpha_{2}+\nu}y^{k_{2}+\beta_{2}+\sigma}\right)\Big).

Computing the derivative inside the parentheses, we get

x^{-\alpha_{1}}y^{-\beta_{1}}(\alpha_{2}+\nu+1)_{n_{2}-k_{2}}(\beta_{2}+\sigma+1)_{k_{2}}\frac{\partial^{n_{1}}}{\partial x^{n_{1}-k_{1}}\partial y^{k_{1}}}\Big(x^{n_{1}-k_{1}+\alpha_{1}+\nu}y^{k_{1}+\beta_{1}+\sigma}\Big),

and, computing the remaining derivative

(\alpha_{2}+\nu+1)_{n_{2}-k_{2}}(\beta_{2}+\sigma+1)_{k_{2}}(\alpha_{1}+\sigma+1)_{n_{1}-k_{1}}(\beta_{1}+\sigma+1)_{k_{1}}\,x^{\nu}y^{\sigma}.

Therefore, we finally get

D_{1}\circ D_{2}[f(x,y)]

=(1-x-y)^{-\gamma}\sum_{\nu,\sigma=-\infty}^{+\infty}c_{\nu,\sigma}(\alpha_{2}+\nu+1)_{n_{2}-k_{2}}(\beta_{2}+\sigma+1)_{k_{2}}(\alpha_{1}+\sigma+1)_{n_{1}-k_{1}}(\beta_{1}+\sigma+1)_{k_{1}}\,x^{\nu}y^{\sigma},

and the result follows.
∎

As a consequence, since
P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{((\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}),(\beta_{1},\beta_{2}),\gamma)}(x,y)=D_{2}\circ D_{1}[(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+n_{2}}]
, the bivariate Jacobi–Piñeiro polynomials (
15
) satisfy the following property:

Corollary 3.6
.

Consider two pairs of non-negative integers
0\leq k_{1}\leq n_{1}
and
0\leq k_{2}\leq n_{2}
. Then, the following symmetry condition holds

P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{((\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}),(\beta_{1},\beta_{2}),\gamma)}(x,y)=P_{(n_{2},k_{2}),(n_{1},k_{1})}^{((\alpha_{2},\alpha_{1}),(\beta_{2},\beta_{1}),\gamma)}(x,y).

This means that, given two measures and two pairs of non-negative integers, the polynomial is independent of the choice of the indices 1 and 2. Once we know that this construction is a polynomial of degree
n_{1}+n_{2}
, analogue to (
9
) but in the bivariate setting, and satisfies a desirable symmetry property, it is time to prove its multiple orthogonality conditions.

Proposition 3.7
.

Consider two pairs of non-negative integers
0\leq k_{1}\leq n_{1}
and
0\leq k_{2}\leq n_{2}
. Then, the polynomial
P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(x,y)
satisfies the following orthogonality conditions:

\int_{T}P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(x,y)x^{n-k}y^{k}W_{j}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y=0\ \ \text{ if }\ 0\leq n-k\leq n_{j}-k_{j},\,\text{ or }\ 0\leq k\leq k_{j},\ \ j=1,2.

(23)

Proof.

First of all, we will prove the orthogonality conditions for
j=2
. Consider the monomial
x^{n-k}y^{k}
for any pair
0\leq k\leq n
. Denoting
\bar{P}(x,y)=x^{n_{1}-k_{1}+\alpha_{1}}y^{k_{1}+\beta_{1}}(1-x-y)^{\gamma+n_{1}+n_{2}}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{T}P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(x,y)\,x^{n-k}y^{k}W_{2}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

=\displaystyle\int_{T}\frac{\partial^{n_{2}}}{\partial x^{n_{2}-k_{2}}\partial y^{k_{2}}}\Big(x^{n_{2}-k_{2}+\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}}y^{k_{2}+\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}}\frac{\partial^{n_{1}}}{\partial x^{n_{1}-k_{1}}\partial y^{k_{1}}}\bar{P}(x,y)\Big)\,x^{n-k}y^{k}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

By applying integration by parts
n_{2}
times and using Lemma
3.3
to note that the terms evaluated on the boundary of
T
vanish, we obtain

(-1)^{n_{2}}\int_{T}x^{n_{2}-k_{2}+\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{1}}y^{k_{2}+\beta_{2}-\beta_{1}}\frac{\partial^{n_{1}}}{\partial x^{n_{1}-k_{1}}\partial y^{k_{1}}}\bar{P}(x,y)\frac{\partial^{n_{2}}}{\partial x^{n_{2}-k_{2}}\partial y^{k_{2}}}x^{n-k}y^{k}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

Now, if
n-k<n_{2}-k_{2}
or
k<k_{2}
, then
\frac{\partial^{n_{2}}}{\partial x^{n_{2}-k_{2}}\partial y^{k_{2}}}x^{n-k}y^{k}=0
and the first orthogonality condition is satisfied.

For
j=1
, recall the symmetry condition (
21
), so that

P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(x,y)=D_{2}\circ D_{1}[(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+n_{2}}]=D_{1}\circ D_{2}[(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+n_{2}}]

and it is possible to apply the previous argument.
∎

3.2.
The general multi‑measure case

Once the simplest case, but still multipleorthogonal, has been defined and analyzed, it is natural to proceed to a more general case, considering an arbitrary number of measures. In this second part of Section
3
, we introduce bivariate Jacobi–Piñeiro multiple orthogonal polynomials satisfying orthogonality conditions with respect to an arbitrary number of measures on the triangle
T
. Then, consider
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{r},\gamma>-1
with
(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})\neq(\alpha_{j},\beta_{j})
if
i\neq j
and define
r\geq 2
weights supported on
T
and their respective measures, which will be denoted following the same notation as in (
13
).

W_{j}(x,y)=x^{\alpha_{j}}y^{\beta_{j}}(1-x-y)^{\gamma},\qquad\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x,y)=W_{j}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y,\qquad j=1,\dots,r.

Now, for every measure, choose a pair of non-negative integers
0\leq k_{j}\leq n_{j}
and its associated Rodrigues operator defined in (
14
). With these elements, we can define the polynomial that generalize (
15
) in the case of more than two measures.

Definition 3.8
.

Consider
r
pairs of non-negative integers
0\leq k_{j}\leq n_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,r
, and denote
\vec{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
,
\vec{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{r})
. Define the function

\begin{split}P^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}_{\{(n_{j},k_{j})\}}(x,y):&=D_{r}\circ\cdots\circ D_{1}[(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}}]\\
&=\dfrac{1}{W_{r}(x,y)}\frac{\partial^{n_{r}}}{\partial x^{n_{r}-k_{r}}\partial y^{k_{r}}}\Big(x^{n_{r}-k_{r}+\alpha_{r}-\alpha_{r-1}}y^{k_{r}+\beta_{r}-\beta_{r-1}}\cdots\\
&\ \ \ \cdots\;\frac{\partial^{n_{1}}}{\partial x^{n_{1}-k_{1}}\partial y^{k_{1}}}(x^{n_{1}-k_{1}+\alpha_{1}}y^{k_{1}+\beta_{1}}(1-x-y)^{\gamma+n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}})\Big).\\
\end{split}

(24)

This function is a polynomial, as stated in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.9
.

Consider
r
pairs of non-negative integers
0\leq k_{j}\leq n_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,r
. Then, the function
P^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}_{\{(n_{j},k_{j})\}}(x,y)
defined in (
24
) is a polynomial of total degree
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}
.

Proof.

Use Lemma
3.3
taking
l=m=0
,
n=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}
. Since
P^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}_{\{(n_{j},k_{j})\}}(x,y)=D_{r}\circ\cdots\circ D_{1}[(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}}]
, then it is a polynomial of degree
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}
.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

As a consequence of Proposition
3.5
, this polynomial satisfies a symmetry condition analogous to that of Corollary
3.6
.

To end this section, we will show that these polynomials are multiple orthogonal polynomials in the sense of Proposition
3.7
.

Proposition 3.11
.

Consider
r
pairs of non-negative integers
0\leq k_{j}\leq n_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,r
. Then the polynomial
P^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}_{\{(n_{j},k_{j})\}}(x,y)
satisfies the following orthogonality conditions:

\int_{T}P^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}_{\{(n_{j},k_{j})\}}(x,y)\,x^{n-k}y^{k}\,W_{j}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y=0\ \quad\text{ if }\,0\leq n-k<n_{j}-k_{j}\,\text{ or }\,0\leq k\leq k_{j},

for
j=1,\dots,r
.

Proof.

Consider
j\in\{1,\dots,r\}
, using the commutation of the operators
D_{1},\dots,D_{r}
(Proposition
3.5
), we can express the polynomial
P^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}_{\{(n_{j},k_{j})\}}(x,y)
as

P^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}_{\{(n_{j},k_{j})\}}(x,y)=(D_{1}\circ\,\cdots\,\circ\,D_{j}\circ\,\cdots\,\circ\,D_{r})[(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}}]

=(D_{j}\circ D_{1}\circ\,\cdots\,\circ\,D_{j-1}\circ\,D_{j+1}\circ\,\cdots\,\circ D_{r})[(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}}]

In this way, the integral we have to compute is

\int_{T}P^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}_{\{(n_{j},k_{j})\}}(x,y)x^{n-k}y^{k}W_{j}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

=\int_{T}(D_{j}\circ D_{1}\circ\cdots\circ D_{j-1}\circ D_{j+1}\circ\cdots\circ D_{r})[(1-x-y)^{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}}]x^{n-k}y^{k}W_{j}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y

=\int_{T}\dfrac{1}{W_{j}(x,y)}\frac{\partial^{n_{j}}}{\partial x^{n_{j}-k_{j}}\partial y^{k_{j}}}\Big(x^{n_{j}-k_{j}+\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{r}}y^{k_{j}+\beta_{j}-\beta_{r}}\cdots\\
\cdots\,\frac{\partial^{n_{1}}}{\partial x^{n_{1}-k_{1}}\partial y^{k_{1}}}(x^{n_{1}-k_{1}+\alpha_{1}}y^{k_{1}+\beta_{1}}(1-x-y)^{\gamma+n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}})\Big)x^{n-k}y^{k}W_{j}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

By applying integration by parts
n_{j}
times and using Lemma
3.3
to note that the terms evaluated on the boundary of
T
vanish, we obtain

(-1)^{n_{j}}\int_{T}\Big(x^{n_{j}-k_{j}+\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{r}}y^{k_{j}+\beta_{j}-\beta_{r}}\cdots\frac{\partial^{n_{1}}}{\partial x^{n_{1}-k_{1}}\partial y^{k_{1}}}(x^{n_{1}-k_{1}+\alpha_{1}}y^{k_{1}+\beta_{1}}(1-x-y)^{\gamma+n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}})\Big)\times\\
\times\frac{\partial^{n_{j}}}{\partial x^{n_{j}-k_{j}}\partial y^{k_{j}}}(x^{n-k}y^{k})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

Finally, observe that
\frac{\partial^{n_{j}}}{\partial x^{n_{j}-k_{j}}\partial y^{k_{j}}}(x^{n-k}y^{k})
vanishes whenever
n-k<n_{j}-k_{j}
or
k<k_{j}
, and this argument is valid for
j=1,\dots,r
.
∎

3.3.
The multivariate case

It is possible to extend these polynomials and their properties to the multivariate case considering an arbitrary number
d\geq 2
of variables. We might consider the case where we use an arbitrary number
r\geq 2
of measures, but we will consider the case
r=2
in order to simplify the notation. However, that case is analogous to the one described in this subsection, but using a higher number of parameters.

Let us consider the
d
-dimensional
simplex

T^{d}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x_{1}\geq 0,\dots,x_{d}\geq 0,x_{1}+\cdots+x_{d}\leq 1\}.

(25)

and the multivariate weights

\displaystyle W_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\,\cdots\,x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}(1-|\mathbf{x}|)^{\gamma},

\displaystyle W_{2}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\,\cdots\,x_{d}^{\beta_{d}}(1-|\mathbf{x}|)^{\gamma},

where
\vec{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})\neq\vec{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})
,
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d},\gamma>-1
,
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in T^{d}
and
|\mathbf{x}|=x_{1}+\cdots+x_{d}
is the
\ell^{1}
norm or modulus of
\mathbf{x}
. Now, consider 2 multi-integers
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})
,
\vec{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}
, and define the following operators, extending the ones in (
14
):

\begin{array}[]{c}D_{1}[f(\mathbf{x})]:=\dfrac{1}{W_{1}(\mathbf{x})}\dfrac{\partial^{|\vec{n}|}}{\partial x_{1}^{n_{1}}\,\cdots\,\partial x_{d}^{n_{d}}}\left(x_{1}^{n_{1}+\alpha_{1}}\,\cdots\,x_{d}^{n_{d}+\alpha_{d}}(1-|\mathbf{x}|)^{\gamma}f(\mathbf{x})\right)\\
D_{2}[f(\mathbf{x})]:=\dfrac{1}{W_{2}(\mathbf{x})}\dfrac{\partial^{|\vec{m}|}}{\partial x_{1}^{m_{1}}\,\cdots\,\partial x_{d}^{m_{d}}}\left(x_{1}^{m_{1}+\beta_{1}}\,\cdots\,x_{d}^{m_{d}+\beta_{d}}(1-|\mathbf{x}|)^{\gamma}f(\mathbf{x})\right).\end{array}

(26)

Then, the polynomial

P^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}_{\vec{n},\vec{m}}(\mathbf{x}):=D_{2}\circ D_{1}[(1-|\mathbf{x}|)^{|\vec{n}|+|\vec{m}|}]

is a polynomial of degree
|\vec{n}|+|\vec{m}|
and satisfy the following orthogonality conditions

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\int_{T^{d}}P^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}_{\vec{n},\vec{m}}(\mathbf{x})x_{1}^{l_{1}}\,\cdots\,x_{d}^{l_{d}}W_{1}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,\cdots\,\mathrm{d}x_{d}=0,\qquad\text{if }l_{i}\leq n_{i}\text{ for any }i\in\{1,\dots,d\},\\
\displaystyle\int_{T^{d}}P^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}_{\vec{n},\vec{m}}(\mathbf{x})x_{1}^{l_{1}}\,\cdots\,x_{d}^{l_{d}}W_{2}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,\cdots\,\mathrm{d}x_{d}=0,\qquad\text{if }l_{i}\leq m_{i}\text{ for any }i\in\{1,\dots,d\}.\end{array}

(27)

The proofs of these results are analogous to the proofs of Proposition
3.9
, using an analogous version of Lemma
3.3
, and Proposition
3.7
, but in the multivariate setting.

4.
Hermite–Padé approximation

In this section, we will show that the bivariate Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials (
24
) can be used to construct analogous of Hermite–Padé approximants of bivariate functions defined as Stieltjes transforms
[
1
]
. Along this section, we will consider
r=2
, although the calculations would be the same ones in the general case. Define the following function:

E_{j}(z,w):=\int_{T}\frac{W_{j}(x,y)}{(z-x)(w-y)}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,\quad j=1,2,

(28)

where
W_{j}(x,y)
is defined in (
13
). This will be the target function to approximate, playing the same role as
f_{j}(z)
(
11
) in the univariate setting. This function can be expanded as a double Laurent series at infinity:

E_{j}(z,w)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{l,m}^{(j)}}{z^{l+1}w^{m+1}},\quad\text{ where }\,c_{l,m}^{(j)}:=\int_{T}x^{m}y^{n}W_{j}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y,\quad j=1,2.

(29)

Taking into account that
\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{a-b}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{b^{k}}{a^{k+1}}
if
|b|<|a|
, this function must be defined outside
T
.

On the other hand, the coefficients
c_{l,m}^{(j)}
can be computed explicitly:

c_{l,m}^{(j)}=\frac{\Gamma(\gamma+1)\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+l+1)\Gamma(\beta_{j}+m+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\beta_{j}+\gamma+l+m+3)},\quad j=1,2.

(30)

Following the methodology in
[
34
]
, we will use the following notation for a formal series. If

F(z,w)=\sum_{l=-\infty}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{F_{l,m}}{z^{l+1}w^{m+1}},

then, we will use the notation
\{F\}_{l,m}=F_{l,m}
to refer to the coefficient of
z^{-(l+1)}w^{-(m+1)}

and

\displaystyle\{F\}=\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\frac{F_{l,m}}{z^{l+1}w^{m+1}}

is the so-called fractional part of the series.

Given two pairs of nonnegative integers
0\leq k_{1}\leq n_{1}
,
0\leq k_{2}\leq n_{2}
, according to
[
34
, Problem 1]
, we will consider that the first step of the Hermite–Padé approximation problem for the functions
E_{1}(z,w)
and
E_{2}(z,w)
consists of finding a polynomial
A_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}(z,w)
of total degree at most
n_{1}+n_{2}
such that

\{A_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}E_{j}\}_{l,m}=0,\quad\text{ for }(l,m)\in\mathbb{L}_{(n_{j},k_{j})},\quad j=1,2,

(31)

where

\mathbb{L}_{(n_{j},k_{j})}=\{(l,m)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{2}:l<n-k\text{ or }m<k\}.

If the polynomial
A_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}(z,w)
has degree
n_{1}+n_{2}
, it has
\frac{1}{2}(n_{1}+n_{2}+1)(n_{1}+n_{2}+2)
unknown coefficients, so that:

A_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}(z,w)=\sum_{p=0}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p}a_{p-i,i}z^{p-i}w^{i}.

(32)

If we calculate the product
A_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}(z,w)E_{j}(z,w)
, we obtain

A_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}E_{j}(z,w)=\left(\sum_{p=0}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p}a_{p-i,i}z^{p-i}w^{i}\right)\left(\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{c_{l,m}^{(j)}}{z^{l+1}w^{m+1}}\right).

Then, for a fixed pair
(l,m)\in\mathbb{L}_{(n_{j},k_{j})}
, condition (
31
) leads to the following linear equation for coefficients
a_{p-i,i}
:

\sum_{p=0}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p}a_{p-i,i}c_{l+p-i,m+i}^{(j)}=0.

Now recall from (
29
) the integral expression of coefficients
c_{l,m}^{(j)}
. Therefore, the previous equation can be expressed as

\sum_{p=0}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p}a_{p-i,i}\int_{T}x^{l+p-i}y^{m+i}W_{j}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y=0.

Interchanging the order of summation and integration, we obtain

\int_{T}\left(\sum_{p=0}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p}a_{p-i,i}x^{p-i}y^{i}\right)x^{l}y^{m}W_{j}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y=0,

and, from (
32
),

\int_{T}A_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}(x,y)x^{l}y^{m}W_{j}(x,y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y=0,\quad(l,m)\in\mathbb{L}_{(n_{j},k_{j})},\quad j=1,2.

(33)

Consequently, the Hermite–Padé approximation problem (
31
) is equivalent to finding a polynomial
A_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}(x,y)
of total degree at most
n_{1}+n_{2}
satisfying the orthogonality conditions (
33
). Now, observe that these conditions are exactly the same orthogonality conditions in (
23
), satisfied by the polynomial
P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(x,y)
. We have proven the following result.

Proposition 4.1
.

The polynomial
P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(x,y)
defined in (
15
) is a solution of the Hermite–Padé approximation problem (
31
) for the functions
E_{1}(z,w)
and
E_{2}(z,w)
.

For now on, in order to simplify the notation, we will write
A_{n}(z,w)
(where
n=n_{1}+n_{2}
is the total degree of the polynomial) instead of
A_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}(z,w)
, as the parameters
n_{1},k_{1},n_{2}
and
k_{2}
are supposed to be fixed along this discussion. The main difference between the work in
[
34
]
and this work is that, once the problem (
31
) is solved, we need some other element in order to get an approximation of
E_{j}(z,w)
. Observe the univariate case described in Section
2
, equation (
12
). In this moment, we know an analogue of
P_{\vec{n}}(x)
, which can be the bivariate Jacobi–Piñeiro polynomial
P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(x,y)
. However, we need an analogue to
Q_{\vec{n},j}(x)
, which in the univariate case is a polynomial. In this bivariate case, we will study the corresponding construction, but we will see that it is no longer a polynomial, but still computable.

Let us focus on the integral representation of
E_{j}(z,w)
, see (
28
). If we multiply and divide by
A_{n}(x,y)
, we obtain

\displaystyle E_{j}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{n}(z,w)}\int_{T}\frac{A_{n}(z,w)\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x,y)}{(z-x)(w-y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{A_{n}(z,w)}\int_{T}\frac{A_{n}(z,w)-A_{n}(x,y)}{(z-x)(w-y)}d\mu_{j}(x,y)+\frac{1}{A_{n}(z,w)}\int_{T}\frac{A_{n}(x,y)}{(z-x)(w-y)}d\mu_{j}(x,y).

This means

A_{n}(z,w)E_{j}(z,w)=\int_{T}\frac{A_{n}(z,w)-A_{n}(x,y)}{(z-x)(w-y)}d\mu_{j}(x,y)+\int_{T}\frac{A_{n}(x,y)}{(z-x)(w-y)}d\mu_{j}(x,y).

Let us focus on the second integral:

\int_{T}\frac{A_{n}(x,y)}{(z-x)(w-y)}\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x,y)=\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{z^{l+1}w^{m+1}}\int_{T}x^{l}y^{m}A_{n}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x,y),

whenever
z,w
are big enough. Therefore, since we have the orthogonality conditions (
33
), this integral has the following shape, which will be denoted as
\mathcal{O}(z^{-n_{j}+k_{j}-1}w^{-k_{j}-1})

\sum_{l=n_{j}-k_{j}}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=k_{j}}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{z^{l+1}w^{m+1}}\int_{T}x^{l}y^{m}A_{n}(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x,y)=\mathcal{O}(z^{-n_{j}+k_{j}-1}w^{-k_{j}-1}).

As a consequence, we have the following equality, which extends (
12
):

A_{n}(z,w)E_{j}(z,w)=\int_{T}\frac{A_{n}(z,w)-A_{n}(x,y)}{(z-x)(w-y)}\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x,y)+\mathcal{O}(z^{-n_{j}+k_{j}-1}w^{-k_{j}-1}),\quad j=1,2.

(34)

This expression tells us that it is possible to approximate the functions
E_{1}(z,w)
and
E_{2}(z,w)
with a common denominator
A_{n}(z,w)
. For now on, we will consider a fixed value of
j\in\{1,2\}
, and the super index
(j)
in the following constructions will refer to the measure
\mu_{j}
and the approximant for the function
E_{j}(z,w)
.

Next, we will study the integral

\Phi^{(j)}(z,w):=\int_{T}\frac{A_{n}(z,w)-A_{n}(x,y)}{(z-x)(w-y)}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x,y),

(35)

which will be the numerator of the approximation and the analogue of
Q_{\vec{n},j}(x)
in the univariate case. First, observe that, by adding and subtracting
A_{n}(x,w)
, we get

\Phi^{(j)}(z,w)=\int_{T}\frac{\frac{A_{n}(z,w)-A_{n}(x,w)}{z-x}}{w-y}\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x,y)+\int_{T}\frac{\frac{A_{n}(x,w)-A_{n}(x,y)}{w-y}}{z-x}\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x,y).

(36)

On one hand, we will work on the first integral

\psi_{1}^{(j)}(z,w):=\int_{T}\frac{\frac{A_{n}(z,w)-A_{n}(x,w)}{z-x}}{w-y}\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{\int_{0}^{1-y}\frac{A_{n}(z,w)-A_{n}(x,w)}{z-x}x^{\alpha_{j}}y^{\beta_{j}}(1-x-y)^{\gamma}\mathrm{d}x}{w-y}\mathrm{d}y.

(37)

Using the notation
a_{p-i,i}
for the coefficients of
A_{n}(z,w)
introduced in (
32
), observe that

\frac{A_{n}(z,w)-A_{n}(x,w)}{z-x}=\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p}a_{p-i,i}\frac{z^{p-i}w^{i}-x^{p-i}w^{i}}{z-x}=\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\sum_{h=0}^{p-i-1}a_{p-i,i}w^{i}z^{p-i-1-h}x^{h},

then, the integral in the numerator of (
37
) becomes

\int_{0}^{1-y}\frac{A_{n}(z,w)-A_{n}(x,w)}{z-x}x^{\alpha_{j}}y^{\beta_{j}}(1-x-y)^{\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}x

=\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\sum_{h=0}^{p-i-1}a_{p-i,i}w^{i}z^{p-i-1-h}y^{\beta_{j}}\int_{0}^{1-y}x^{\alpha_{j}+h}(1-x-y)^{\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}x

=\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\sum_{h=0}^{p-i-1}a_{p-i,i}w^{i}z^{p-i-1-h}y^{\beta_{j}}(1-y)^{\alpha_{j}+h+\gamma+1}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+1)\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\gamma+2)}

=\left(\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\sum_{h=0}^{p-i-1}a_{p-i,i}w^{i}z^{p-i-1-h}(1-y)^{h}\right)\left(\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+1)\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\gamma+2)}y^{\beta_{j}}(1-y)^{\alpha_{j}+\gamma+1}\right)

=P^{(1,0)}(z,w,y)\omega_{j}^{(1,0)}(y),

where we denote

\begin{array}[]{rl}P^{(1,0)}(z,w,y)&:=\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\sum_{h=0}^{p-i-1}a_{p-i,i}w^{i}z^{p-i-1-h}(1-y)^{h},\\[12.0pt]
\omega_{j}^{(1,0)}(y)&:=\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+1)\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\gamma+2)}y^{\beta_{j}}(1-y)^{\alpha_{j}+\gamma+1},\\[10.0pt]
\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(1,0)}(y)&:=\omega_{j}^{(1,0)}(y)\mathrm{d}y.\end{array}

(38)

With this computation, we get

\psi_{1}^{(j)}(z,w)=\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{P^{(1,0)}(z,w,y)}{w-y}\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(1,0)}(y)

=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{P^{(1,0)}(z,w,y)-P^{(1,0)}(z,w,w)}{w-y}\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(1,0)}(y)+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{P^{(1,0)}(z,w,w)}{w-y}\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{1,0}(y).

Denoting

\psi_{1,1}^{(j)}(z,w)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{P^{(1,0)}(z,w,y)-P^{(1,0)}(z,w,w)}{w-y}\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(1,0)}(y),

\psi_{1,2}^{(j)}(z,w)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{P^{(1,0)}(z,w,w)}{w-y}\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{1,0}(y),

so that
\psi_{1}^{(j)}(z,w)=\psi_{1,1}^{(j)}(z,w)+\psi_{1,2}^{(j)}(z,w)
, we will study these integrals separately.

Let us now take into account

P^{(1,0)}(z,w,y)-P^{(1,0)}(z,w,w)

=\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\sum_{h=0}^{p-i-1}a_{p-i,i}w^{i}z^{p-i-1-h}(1-y)^{h}-\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\sum_{h=0}^{p-i-1}a_{p-i,i}w^{i}z^{p-i-1-h}(1-w)^{h}

=\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\sum_{h=0}^{p-i-1}a_{p-i,i}w^{i}z^{p-i-1-h}\left((1-y)^{h}-(1-w)^{h}\right),

so that

\psi_{1,1}^{(j)}(z,w)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{P^{(1,0)}(z,w,y)-P^{(1,0)}(z,w,w)}{w-y}\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(1,0)}(y)

=\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\sum_{h=0}^{p-i-1}a_{p-i,i}w^{i}z^{p-i-1-h}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{(1-y)^{h}-(1-w)^{h}}{w-y}\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(1,0)}(y).

Now, using the following equality

\frac{(1-y)^{h}-(1-w)^{h}}{w-y}=\frac{(1-y)^{h}-(1-w)^{h}}{(1-y)-(1-w)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\text{ if }h=0\\
\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}(1-y)^{h-1-k}(1-w)^{k}&\text{ if }h\geq 1,\end{array}\right.

it holds that

\psi_{1,1}^{(j)}(z,w)=\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\sum_{h=1}^{p-i-1}a_{p-i,i}w^{i}z^{p-i-1-h}\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}(1-w)^{k}\int_{0}^{1}(1-y)^{h-1-k}\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(1,0)}(y)\\

=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+1)\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\gamma+2)}\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\sum_{h=1}^{p-i-1}a_{p-i,i}w^{i}z^{p-i-1-h}\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}(1-w)^{k}\int_{0}^{1}(1-y)^{h-1-k}y^{\beta_{j}}(1-y)^{\alpha_{j}+\gamma+1}\mathrm{d}y

=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+1)\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\gamma+2)}\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\sum_{h=1}^{p-i-1}a_{p-i,i}w^{i}z^{p-i-1-h}\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}(1-w)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(\beta_{j}+1)\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\gamma+h-k+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\beta_{j}+\gamma+h-k+2)}.

Then, the explicit expression for
\psi_{1,1}^{(j)}(z,w)
is

\psi_{1,1}^{(j)}(z,w)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+1)\Gamma(\beta_{j}+1)\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\gamma+2)}\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\sum_{h=1}^{p-i-1}a_{p-i,i}w^{i}z^{p-i-1-h}\times\\
\times\sum_{k=0}^{h-1}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\gamma+h-k+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\beta_{j}+\gamma+h-k+2)}(1-w)^{k}

(39)

Next, we will study the integral
\psi_{1,2}^{(j)}(z,w)
. We can also identify

\begin{split}\psi_{1,2}^{(j)}(z,w)&=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{P^{(1,0)}(z,w,w)}{w-y}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(1,0)}(y)\\
&=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+1)\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\gamma+2)}P^{(1,0)}(z,w,w)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{w-y}y^{\beta_{j}}(1-y)^{\alpha_{j}+\gamma+1}\,\mathrm{d}y\\
&=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+1)\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\gamma+2)}P^{(1,0)}(z,w,w)\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\int_{0}^{1}y^{k}y^{\beta_{j}}(1-y)^{\alpha_{j}+\gamma+1}\,\mathrm{d}y}{w^{k+1}}\\
&=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+1)\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\gamma+2)}P^{(1,0)}(z,w,w)\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\frac{\Gamma(\beta_{j}+k+1)\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\gamma+2)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\beta_{j}+\gamma+k+3)}}{w^{k+1}}\\
&=\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+1)\Gamma(\gamma+1)P^{(1,0)}(z,w,w)\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\beta_{j}+k+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\beta_{j}+\gamma+k+3)w^{k+1}},\end{split}

for
w
big enough. Now recall the hypergeometric function

{{}_{2}}F_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}a,\,b\\
c\end{matrix}\,;\,z\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(a)_{k}(b)_{k}}{(c)_{k}}\dfrac{z^{k}}{k!}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\Gamma(a+k)\Gamma(b+k)\Gamma(c)z^{k}}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)\Gamma(c+k)k!}.

Then, the final expression for
\psi_{1,2}^{(j)}(z,w)
is

\psi_{1,2}^{(j)}(z,w)=\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+1)\Gamma(\beta_{j}+1)\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+\beta_{j}+\gamma+3)w}P^{(1,0)}(z,w,w)\,{{}_{2}}F_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}\beta_{j}+1,1\\
\alpha_{j}+\beta_{j}+\gamma+3\end{matrix}\,;\,\dfrac{1}{w}\right)

(40)

So now we have an explicit formula for both
\psi_{1,1}^{(j)}(z,w)
and
\psi_{1,2}^{(j)}(z,w)
. Observe that
\psi_{1,1}^{(j)}(z,w)
is a polynomial of total degree
n_{1}+n_{2}-2
and
\psi_{1,2}^{(j)}(z,w)
is a polynomial times an hypergeometric function. That is the reason why we previously said that
\Phi^{(j)}(z,w)
, the bivariate analogue of
Q_{\vec{n},j}(z)
, would no longer be a polynomial.

Up to this moment, looking at the expression (
36
), we have computed explicitly the first integral of
\Phi^{(j)}(z,w)
. Now, we will work on the second one:

\psi_{2}^{(j)}(z,w):=\int_{T}\frac{\frac{A_{n}(x,w)-A_{n}(x,y)}{w-y}}{z-x}\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{\int_{0}^{1-x}\frac{A_{n}(x,w)-A_{n}(x,y)}{w-y}x^{\alpha_{j}}y^{\beta_{j}}(1-x-y)^{\gamma}\,\mathrm{d}y}{z-x}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Employing an analogous argument, we obtain that the integral in the numerator is

\int_{0}^{1-x}\frac{A_{n}(x,w)-A_{n}(x,y)}{w-y}x^{\alpha_{j}}y^{\beta_{j}}(1-x-y)^{\gamma}\mathrm{d}y

=\left(\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{h=0}^{i-1}a_{p-i,i}x^{p-i}w^{i-1-h}(1-x)^{h}\right)\left(\frac{\Gamma(\beta_{j}+1)\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{\Gamma(\beta_{j}+\gamma+2)}x^{\alpha_{j}}(1-x)^{\beta_{j}+\gamma+1}\right)

=P^{(0,1)}(x,w)\,\omega_{j}^{(0,1)}(x),

where we denote

\begin{array}[]{rl}P^{(0,1)}(x,w)&:=\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{h=0}^{i-1}a_{p-i,i}x^{p-i}w^{i-1-h}(1-x)^{h},\\[12.0pt]
\omega_{j}^{(0,1)}(x)&:=\dfrac{\Gamma(\beta_{j}+1)\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{\Gamma(\beta_{j}+\gamma+2)}x^{\alpha_{j}}(1-x)^{\beta_{j}+\gamma+1},\\[10.0pt]
\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(0,1)}(x)&:=\omega_{j}^{(0,1)}(x)\mathrm{d}x.\end{array}

(41)

Thus, the integral
\psi_{2}^{(j)}(z,w)
can be computed as

\psi_{2}^{(j)}(z,w)=\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{P^{(0,1)}(x,w)}{z-x}\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(0,1)}(x)

=-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{P^{(0,1)}(x,w)-P^{(0,1)}(z,w)}{x-z}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(0,1)}(x)+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{P^{(0,1)}(z,w)}{z-x}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(0,1)}(x).

Let us denote

\psi_{2,1}^{(j)}(z,w)=-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{P^{(0,1)}(x,w)-P^{(0,1)}(z,w)}{x-z}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(0,1)}(x),

\psi_{2,2}^{(j)}(z,w)=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{P^{(0,1)}(z,w)}{z-x}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}^{(0,1)}(x),

so that
\psi_{2}^{(j)}(z,w)=\psi_{2,1}^{(j)}(z,w)+\psi_{2,2}^{(j)}(z,w)
. Since
P^{(0,1)}(x,w)
is a polynomial of total degree
n_{1}+n_{2}-1
, we will use the following decomposition of
P^{(0,1)}(z,w)
:

P^{(0,1)}(x,w)=\sum_{p=0}^{n_{1}+n_{2}-1}\sum_{i=0}^{p}b_{p-i,i}x^{p-i}w^{i}.

With these notations and using analogous arguments, we find that
\psi_{2,1}^{(j)}(z,w)
and
\psi_{2,2}^{(j)}(z,w)
have the following expressions:

\psi_{2,1}^{(j)}(z,w)=-\Gamma(\beta_{j}+1)\Gamma(\gamma+1)\sum_{p=1}^{n_{1}+n_{2}-1}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\sum_{h=0}^{p-i-1}\frac{\Gamma(h+\alpha_{j}+1)}{\Gamma(h+\alpha_{j}+\beta_{j}+\gamma+3)}b_{p-i,i}w^{i}z^{p-i-1-h},

(42)

\psi_{2,2}^{(j)}(z,w)=\dfrac{\Gamma(\alpha_{j}+1)\Gamma(\beta_{j}+1)\Gamma(\gamma+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1}+\gamma+3)z}P^{(0,1)}(z,w)\,{{}_{2}}F_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}\alpha_{j}+1,1\\
\alpha_{j}+\beta_{j}+\gamma+3\end{matrix}\,;\,\dfrac{1}{z}\right).

(43)

Remark 4.2
.

Another way to compute
\psi_{1,2}^{(j)}(z,w)
and
\psi_{2,2}^{(j)}(z,w)
is to replace the use of the hypergeometric function

, which is in fact an infinite series, with univariate Padé rational approximations of the functions

Summarizing, the integral
\Phi^{(j)}(z,w)
defined in (

\Phi^{(j)}(z,w)=\psi_{1,1}^{(j)}(z,w)+\psi_{1,2}^{(j)}(z,w)+\psi_{2,1}^{(j)}(z,w)+\psi_{2,2}^{(j)}(z,w),

(44)

where the expressions for

,
\psi_{1,2}^{(j)}(z,w)
,
\psi_{2,1}^{(j)}(z,w)
and

), (

), (

) and (

), respectively.

With these equalities, we have that

A_{n}(z,w)E_{j}(z,w)=\Phi^{(j)}(z,w)+\mathcal{O}(z^{-n_{j}+k_{j}-1}w^{-k_{j}-1}),

(45)

and, from Proposition

, the polynomial

To end this section, observe that we have constructed two approximations to twodifferent functions

6.
Conclusions

Finally, to close this paper, we will list some of the main conclusions of this work. We have defined a family of bivariate polynomials satisfying orthogonality conditions with respect to more than one measure. These polynomials are defined by a concatenation of commutative Rodrigues operators, resulting in a Rodrigues formula that:

•

Extends the univariate Rodrigues formula for multiple orthogonal polynomials (
9
) by increasing the number of variables, and

•

Extends the bivariare Rodrigues formula for orthogonal polynomials on the triangle (
5
) by increasing the number of measures.

Regarding the relation between multiple orthogonal polynomials and Hermite–Padé approximation, since multiple orthogonal polynomials solve the problem of approximating functions
f_{j}
employing rational approximants with a common denominator
\frac{Q_{\vec{n},j}}{P_{\vec{n}}}
, we wanted to extend this problem and its solution to the bivariate case. This way, we approximated the analogue of
f_{j}(z)
, which are the functions
E_{j}(z,w)
defined in (
28
), with approximants
\frac{\Phi^{(j)}(z,w)}{P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(z,w)}
, where
P_{(n_{1},k_{1}),(n_{2},k_{2})}^{(\vec{\alpha},\vec{\beta},\gamma)}(z,w)
(resp.
\Phi^{(j)}(z,w)
) plays the role of the multiple orthogonal polynomial
P_{\vec{n}}(z)
(resp.
Q_{\vec{n},j}(z,w)
). However, while in the univariate case, the integral
Q_{\vec{n},j}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x)}{(z-x)}
yields a polynomial, so that the approximant is a rational function, in the bivariate case the integral
\Phi^{(j)}(z,w)=\int_{T}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}(x,y)}{((z-x)(w-y))}
is not a polynomial, but still computable in terms of hypergeometric functions. Another crucial difference lies in the uniqueness: in the univariate case, if the polynomial
P_{\vec{n}}
exists, then it is unique up to a constant. Conversely, there may exist several possible solutions
A_{n}(x,y)
to the problem (
31
) in the bivariate case, with the polynomial (
15
) being one of these possible solutions.
