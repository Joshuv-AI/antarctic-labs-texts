Abstract
The effectiveness of denoising-driven regularization for image reconstruction has been widely recognized. Two prominent algorithms in this area are Plug-and-Play ( PnP ) and Regularization-by-Denoising ( RED ). We consider two specific algorithms PnP-FISTA and RED-APG , where regularization is performed by replacing the proximal operator in the FISTA algorithm with a powerful denoiser. The iterate convergence of FISTA is known to be challenging with no universal guarantees. Yet, we show that for linear inverse problems and a class of linear denoisers, global linear convergence of the iterates of PnP-FISTA and RED-APG can be established through simple spectral analysis.

The effectiveness of denoiser-driven regularization has motivated researchers to investigate its convergence properties

,

]
. We refer the reader to the recent article
[

]
for an excellent survey on denoiser-driven regularization.The convergence theory in this area is primarily motivated by convex optimization and monotone operator theory
[
2
,

]
. Indeed, a common theme in many of these papers is to guarantee convergence by designing an appropriate regularizer
[

,

1.1
PnP and RED

We consider two popular regularization methods, Plug-and-Play (
PnP
) and Regularization-by-Denoising (
RED
). In particular, we revisit the
PnP-FISTA
and
RED-APG
(
RED
–Accelerated Proximal Gradient) algorithms
[
10
,
30
]
that are modeled on the classical
FISTA
algorithm (Fast Iterative-Shrinkage Thresholding Algorithm). This is an accelerated first-order method for solving convex optimization problems of the form

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ f(\boldsymbol{x})+g(\boldsymbol{x}),

(1)

where
f
is smooth and
g
is nonsmooth but proximable
[
4
]
. In image reconstruction,
f
is a model-based loss function, and
g
is an image regularizer. Starting with an initialization
\boldsymbol{y}_{1}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
and
t_{1}=1
,
FISTA
generates a sequence of images
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}_{k\geqslant 1}
using the updates:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{prox}_{\gamma g}\big(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}-\gamma\nabla\!f(\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\big),

(2a)

\displaystyle t_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\sqrt{1+4t_{k}^{2}}\right),

(2b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\frac{t_{k}-1}{t_{k+1}}\big(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}\big),

(2c)

where
\mathrm{prox}_{\gamma g}
is the proximal operator of
\gamma g
and
\gamma>0
is the step size. For sufficiently small
\gamma
and appropriate technical assumptions on
f
and
g
, it was shown by Beck and Teboulle that the objective
f(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})+g(\boldsymbol{x}_{k})
converges sublinearly to the minimum of
eq.

1
.

In
PnP-FISTA
, the proximal operator (which effectively performs image denoising) is substituted by a powerful denoiser, i.e., the update in
eq.

2a
is performed as

\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\mathbf{W}\big(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}-\gamma\nabla\!f(\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\big),

(3)

where
\mathbf{W}
is a denoising operator. In this note, we will work with a particular class of linear denoisers. This includes kernel denoisers such as
NLM

[
6
,
34
]
,
LARK
, and
GLIDE

[
23
]
. This is the simplest
PnP
model that works well in practice
[
10
,
11
,
34
]
and yet is analytically tractable.
We wish to emphasize that this class of denoisers is not strictly “linear” like the simple Gaussian or the box filter. Rather, the action of the denoiser is given by
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{x}_{g})\,\boldsymbol{x}
which acts linearly on the input image
\boldsymbol{x}
and depends non-linearly on a guide image
\boldsymbol{x}_{g}
; the latter serves as a surrogate for the ground truth image. In fact, it is precisely this data-driven nature, referred to as “pseudo-linearity”
[
22
]
, that makes them more effective than classical linear denoisers. In iterative image reconstruction, the initial guide image serves as a rough estimate that evolves over the first few iterations as per the updates in
eq.

3
. To achieve high-quality reconstructions
[
10
,
34
]
, a common practice is to fix the guide image after a certain number of iterations. Thus, as far as the question of convergence is concerned, we can assume the denoiser to be a purely linear operator
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{x}
. While the choice of guide determines the reconstruction quality, it has no direct bearing on the convergence problem.

In
PnP-FISTA
, we treat the loss
f
as the smooth component of the objective function. On the other hand, in
RED-APG
, the accelerated variant of
RED
, we work with the first-order approximation of the regularizer
g

[
30
, Sec. V]
. More precisely, starting with
t_{0}=1
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the updates in
RED-APG
are given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{prox}_{(\lambda L)^{-1}\,f}(\boldsymbol{v}_{k-1}),

(4a)

\displaystyle t_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\sqrt{1+4t_{k-1}^{2}}\right),

(4b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\frac{t_{k-1}-1}{t_{k}}\,(\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}),

(4c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{k}

\displaystyle=\theta\,\mathbf{W}(\boldsymbol{y}_{k})+(1-\theta)\,\boldsymbol{y}_{k},

(4d)

where
\lambda>0,\,L\geqslant 1
are the internal parameters and
\theta=1/L
.

1.2
Relation to prior work

Although
PnP
was originally conceived from an algorithmic point-of-view, the iterations in
PnP-FISTA
can nonetheless be seen as solving an optimization problem of the form
eq.

1
. As a result, we can establish objective convergence of
PnP-FISTA
and
RED-APG
using existing results
[
3
,
4
]
. In this work, we analyze their iterate convergence. This was motivated by a recent work
[
1
]
, where we were able to establish global linear convergence of
PnP-ISTA
, which is modeled on the
ISTA
algorithm for minimizing
eq.

1
. It is known that
FISTA
is faster than its non-accelerated counterpart
ISTA
in terms of objective convergence
[
4
]
; however, there is no direct relation in terms of iterate convergence (see
Figure

5
). In fact, while we can guarantee iterate convergence of
ISTA
using the concept of Mann iterations
[
3
]
, establishing iterate convergence of
FISTA
is challenging with no universal convergence guarantees. In this direction, Chambolle and Dossal
[
7
]
have shown that a different choice of momentum parameters can guarantee iterate convergence. Lorenz and Pock obtained similar results
[
21
]
, although neither work established the convergence rate. On the other hand, asymptotic linear convergence of
FISTA
was established by Tao et al.
[
35
]
and Johnstone and Moulin
[
19
]
for sparse minimization such as LASSO. Similar to our work, spectral analysis of the underlying operators comes up in
[
35
]
; however, their overall analysis is much more intricate due to the nonlinearity of the denoising operator in LASSO. Notably, the results mentioned above do not apply to
PnP-FISTA
and
RED-APG
. In fact, to the best of our knowledge, convergence analysis of these algorithms has not been reported in the literature.

The present contribution lies in establishing global linear convergence of the
PnP-FISTA
and
RED-APG
iterates in the context of linear inverse problems and data-driven linear denoisers. In this regard, we wish to comment on prior convergence results for linear denoisers
[
10
,
12
,
20
,
34
]
. In
[
10
,
34
]
, it was demonstrated that
PnP
with kernel denoisers can be formulated as a convex optimization problem of the form
eq.

1
; objective convergence of
PnP-FISTA
and
PnP-ADMM
was subsequently established using standard optimization theory. Iterate convergence of
PnP-ISTA
and
PnP-ADMM
(but not
PnP-FISTA
) follows from standard results
[
2
]
. However, since the smooth part
f
in
eq.

1
is typically not strongly convex, it is non-trivial to establish linear convergence. Yet, it was shown in
[
12
,
20
]
that linear convergence can be established for
PnP-ISTA
. However, the analysis in these papers is tailored to the inpainting problem and relies on assumptions that are difficult to interpret and validate. More recently, we developed a convergence theory for
PnP-ISTA
and
PnP-ADMM
in
[
1
]
based on practically verifiable assumptions. A key difference with
[
1
]
is that we work with the spectral radius instead of controlling a specific norm.

Background

We consider the problem of recovering an unknown image
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
from linear measurements

\boldsymbol{b}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\xi}+\boldsymbol{e}
,
where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the forward operator,
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the observed image, and
\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is white Gaussian noise. In particular, we will use the standard loss function

f(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}^{2}.

(5)

As mentioned earlier, we will abstractly work with a linear denoiser
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
that transforms a noisy image
\boldsymbol{x}
into
\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{x}
, where
\boldsymbol{x}
is a vectorized representation of the image and
n
is the number of pixels. We will initially perform the analysis for a symmetric
\mathbf{W}
. Later, in
Section

3.3
, we will extend the results to nonsymmetric denoisers. We do not require the precise definition of
\mathbf{W}
but rather consider the following property.

Assumption 1
.

\sigma(\mathbf{W})\subset[0,1]
and

\ker(\mathbf{A})\,\cap\,\mathrm{fix}(\mathbf{W})=\{\mathbf{0}\}.

We will subsequently show that the above assumption holds for a specific class of denoisers called kernel denoisers
[
22
]
, which includes
NLM

[
6
]
and its symmetric variants
[
23
,
34
]
. Broadly speaking, a kernel denoiser is obtained by considering a kernel function and using it to measure the affinity between pairs of pixels. This gives us a kernel operator
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, where
\mathbf{K}_{ij}
encodes the affinity between pixels
i
and
j
. As mentioned earlier,
\mathbf{K}
is computed from a surrogate of the ground truth, called the guide image. More specifically, the kernel in
NLM
has the form

\mathbf{K}_{ij}=\varphi\big(\zeta(i)-\zeta(j)\big)\,h(i-j),

(6)

where
\varphi
is a multivariate Gaussian,
\zeta(i)
is a patch around pixel
i
extracted from the guide image, and
h
is a symmetric box or hat function supported on a window around the origin. The denoiser is obtained by normalizing
\mathbf{K}
:

\mathbf{W}_{\texttt{NLM}}=\mathbf{D}^{-1}\,\mathbf{K},\quad\mathbf{D}:=\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{K}\,\mathbf{1}).

(7)

It is evident that
eq.

7
need not be symmetric. We can construct a symmetric variant of
eq.

7
using the protocol for
DSG-NLM

[
34
]
:

\mathbf{W}_{\texttt{SYM}}=\frac{1}{\|\hat{\mathbf{1}}\|_{\infty}}\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}+\diag\left(\mathbf{1}-\frac{1}{\|\hat{\mathbf{1}}\|_{\infty}}\,\hat{\mathbf{1}}\right),\quad\hat{\mathbf{1}}:=\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{1},

(8)

where we use the subscript
SYM
to distinguish it from
eq.

7
. We refer the reader to
[
22
]
for more details on such denoisers.

Kernel denoisers come with a rich set of mathematical properties. We abstract out the specific properties required for our analysis.

Proposition 2
.

Suppose
\mathbf{K}
is symmetric positive semidefinite, irreducible, and nonnegative with
\mathbf{K}_{ii}>0
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. Then
eq.

7
and
eq.

8
are well-defined, stochastic, and irreducible, and their eigenvalues are in
[0,1]
.

In particular,
eq.

6
satisfies the assumptions in
Proposition

2
. The details can be found in
[
34
, Sec. IV]
, which also has the proof of the assertions in
Proposition

2
.

We next verify
Assumption

1
for denoisers
eq.

7
and
eq.

8
, when
\mathbf{A}
corresponds to inpainting, deblurring, or superresolution. We assume that at least one pixel is sampled for inpainting, and the lowpass blur is nonnegative (and nonzero) for deblurring and superresolution; these technical conditions are typically met in practice
[
5
]
.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose
\mathbf{W}
is
eq.

7
or
eq.

8
, and the forward operator
\mathbf{A}
corresponds to inpainting, deblurring, or superresolution. Then
Assumption

Proof.

From
Proposition

2
, we have
\rho(\mathbf{W}_{\texttt{NLM}})=1
and
\mathbf{1}\in\mathrm{fix}(\mathbf{W}_{\texttt{NLM}})
. Moreover, since
\mathbf{W}_{\texttt{NLM}}
is irreducible, we can conclude from the Perron-Frobenius theorem
[

that
\mathrm{fix}(\mathbf{W}_{\texttt{NLM}})=\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{1})
. Thus, we are done if we can show that
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{1}\neq\mathbf{0}
. Indeed, it is clear that
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{1}\neq\mathbf{0}
for inpainting and deblurring. Moreover, since
\mathbf{A}
is a lowpass blur followed by downsampling in superresolution, we cannot have
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0}
. A similar argument also goes for
\mathbf{W}_{\texttt{SYM}}

3.1
Preliminaries

We say that a sequence
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
converges linearly to
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if there exists a norm
\|\cdot\|
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
K\geqslant 1,\,C>0
, and
\beta\in[0,1)
such that

\|\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\|\leqslant C\beta^{k}
for all
k\geqslant K
. This asymptotic form of convergence is called “local” linear convergence in
[
19
,
35
]
. However, since we use the term “global convergence” to mean that the sequence converges for any initialization, we will not use “local” and refer to this as linear convergence. The notion of linear convergence does not depend on the choice of norm.

We make the following elementary observation regarding the convergence of a time-varying linear dynamical system.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose we are given
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a sequence of linear operators
\{\mathbf{R}_{k}\}_{k\geqslant 1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. For all
k\geqslant 1
, define the affine map
\mathtt{T}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\mathtt{T}_{k}(\boldsymbol{z})=\mathbf{R}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{s}
. Suppose there exists
\mathbf{R}_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that

1.

\rho(\mathbf{R}_{\infty})<1
,

2.

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbf{R}_{k}=\mathbf{R}_{\infty}
, and

3.

\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\,\mathrm{fix}(\mathtt{T}_{k})\neq\O
.

Then there exists an unique
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that, for any
\boldsymbol{z}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the sequence
\{\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\}_{k\geqslant 0}
generated via
\boldsymbol{z}_{k+1}=\mathtt{T}_{k}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})

converges linearly to
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
.

Proof.

Fix
\beta<1
such that
\rho(\mathbf{R}_{\infty})<\beta
. By
[
17
, Lemma 5.6.10]
, there exists a nonsingular matrix
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that, if we define the norm
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\beta}:=\|\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol{x}\|_{1}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then

\rho(\mathbf{R}_{\infty})\leqslant\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert\mathbf{R}_{\infty}\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert_{\beta}<\beta,

where
\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert\cdot\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert_{\beta}
is the operator norm induced by
\|\cdot\|_{\beta}
.

Now, since
\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert\mathbf{R}_{k}-\mathbf{R}_{\infty}\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert_{\beta}\to 0
, we can find
K\geqslant 1
such that
\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert\mathbf{R}_{k}\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert_{\beta}\leqslant\beta
for all
k\geqslant K
.

Let
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\in\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{fix}(\mathtt{T}_{k})
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}=\mathtt{T}_{k}(\boldsymbol{z}^{*})
for all
k\geqslant 1
. We can write

\boldsymbol{z}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{z}^{*}=\mathtt{T}_{k}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k})-\mathtt{T}_{k}(\boldsymbol{z}^{*})=\mathbf{R}_{k}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}-\boldsymbol{z}^{*}).

Hence, for all
k\geqslant K
, we have

\|\boldsymbol{z}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\|_{\beta}\leqslant\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert\mathbf{R}_{k}\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert\hskip-1.19995pt\rvert_{\beta}\,\|\boldsymbol{z}_{k}-\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\|_{\beta}\leqslant\beta\|\boldsymbol{z}_{k}-\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\|_{\beta}.

Iterating this, we get

\forall\,k\geqslant K:\qquad\|\boldsymbol{z}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\|_{\beta}\leqslant\|\boldsymbol{z}_{K}-\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\|_{\beta}\cdot\beta^{k+1-K}=C\beta^{k+1},

where
C
does not depend on
k
. Thus, we have shown that
\{\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\}
converges linearly to
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
for any initialization
\boldsymbol{z}_{0}
.

Finally, note that
\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{fix}(\mathtt{T}_{k})
must be a singleton. This is because
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}=\mathbf{R}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}^{*}+\boldsymbol{s}

for all
k\geqslant 1
. Consequently, letting
k\to\infty
, we get

(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\mathbf{R}_{\infty})\boldsymbol{z}^{*}=\boldsymbol{s}

(9)

As
\rho(\mathbf{R}_{\infty})<1
,
\mathbf{I}_{d}-\mathbf{R}_{\infty}
is nonsingular, and hence
eq.

9
has at most one solution.

We record a result that is required later in the analysis.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be such that
\sigma(\mathbf{P})\subset[0,1)
. If
\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n}
is defined to be

\mathbf{R}=\begin{pmatrix}2\mathbf{P}&-\mathbf{P}\\
\mathbf{I}_{n}&\phantom{-}\mathbf{0}_{n}\end{pmatrix},

then
\rho(\mathbf{R})<1
.

Proof.

Let

be a nonzero eigenvalue of
\mathbf{R}

\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}_{2})\neq\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle 2\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{z}_{1}-\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{z}_{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda\boldsymbol{z}_{1},

(10a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda\boldsymbol{z}_{2}.

(10b)

We claim that
\lambda\neq 1/2
and
\boldsymbol{z}_{1}=\mathbf{0}
. Indeed, if
\lambda=1/2

\boldsymbol{z}_{1}=\boldsymbol{z}_{2}=\mathbf{0}

\boldsymbol{z}_{1}=\mathbf{0}
would imply (as
\lambda\neq 0

\boldsymbol{z}=\mathbf{0}
.

Substituting
eq.

10b

10a
, we have

\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{z}_{1}=\mu\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\qquad\mu:=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2\lambda-1}.

(11)

Since
\boldsymbol{z}_{1}\neq\mathbf{0}
, it follows from
eq.

11
that
\mu\in\sigma(\mathbf{P})
. Moreover, we have

\lambda^{2}-2\mu\,\lambda+\mu=0.

As
\mu\in[0,1)
by assumption, we have
\lambda=\mu\pm i\sqrt{\mu-\mu^{2}}
. In either case,
\lvert\lambda\rvert=\sqrt{\smash[b]{\mu}}<1
.

We next prove a result about the spectrum of a particular operator involving the forward model

Lemma 6

For

\mathbf{P}_{\gamma}=\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\gamma\mathbf{A}^{\top}\!\mathbf{A})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbf{P}_{\mu,\theta}=(\mathbf{I}_{n}+\mu\mathbf{A}^{\top}\!\mathbf{A})^{-1}\,(\theta\,\mathbf{W}+(1-\theta)\mathbf{I}).

Then
\sigma(\mathbf{P}_{\gamma})\subset[0,1)
for all

and
\sigma(\mathbf{P}_{\mu,\theta})\subset[0,1)
for all
\theta\in[0,1],\,\mu>0
.

We need the following observation to prove

6
.

Proposition 7
.

Let
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
\sigma(\mathbf{M})\subset[0,1]

if and only if
\mathbf{M}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}

Proof of

\sigma(\mathbf{P}_{\gamma})\subset[0,\infty)

is positive semidefinite. Using

3.2
Linear convergence

We now discuss the main result of the paper. The idea is to express
Algorithm

1
and
Algorithm

2
as a dynamical system in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. The transition operator is time-varying but is shown to converge to an operator with a spectral radius strictly less than
1
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
eq.

5
be the loss in
Algorithm

1
and
Algorithm

2
,
\lim_{k\to\infty}\,\alpha_{k}=1
, and
that
\mathbf{W}
and
\mathbf{A}
satisfy
Assumption

1
. Then the iterates of
Algorithm

1
exhibit global linear convergence for any
\gamma\in(0,1/\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\!\mathbf{A}))
. On the other hand, the iterates of
Algorithm

2
exhibit global linear convergence for any
L\geqslant 1
.

Proof.

The gradient of
eq.

5
is
\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\!(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{b})
. Therefore, we can write step
4
of
Algorithm

1
as

\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\mathbf{P}_{\gamma}\,\boldsymbol{y}_{k}+\boldsymbol{q}
, where
\mathbf{P}_{\gamma}
is defined in
eq.

12
and
\boldsymbol{q}:=\gamma\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\!\boldsymbol{b}
. Furthermore, from step
5
, we have

\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=(1+\alpha_{k})\,\mathbf{P}_{\gamma}\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}-\alpha_{k-1}\mathbf{P}\boldsymbol{x}_{k-2}+\boldsymbol{q}.

(13)

For
k\geqslant 1
, define the state space variables

\boldsymbol{z}_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\\
\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}.

(14)

Since
\ker(\mathbf{A})\,\cap\,\mathrm{fix}(\mathbf{W})=\{\mathbf{0}\}
and
\gamma\in(0,1/\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\!\mathbf{A}))
, we know from
Lemma

6
that
\rho(\mathbf{P}_{\gamma})<1
. In particular, this means
\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{P}_{\gamma}
is nonsingular, and we define

\boldsymbol{x}^{*}:=(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\mathbf{P}_{\gamma})^{-1}\boldsymbol{q},\qquad\quad\boldsymbol{z}^{*}:=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\\
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}.

(15)

We now use
Lemma

4
to show that
\{\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\}_{k\geqslant 1}
converges linearly to
\boldsymbol{z}^{*}
. From
eq.

13
, we can write
\boldsymbol{z}_{k}=\mathtt{T}_{k}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k-1})
, where

\mathbf{R}_{k}:=\begin{pmatrix}(1+\alpha_{k})\mathbf{P}&-\alpha_{k}\mathbf{P}\\
\mathbf{I}_{n}&\phantom{-}\mathbf{0}_{n}\end{pmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{s}:=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{q}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathtt{T}_{k}(\boldsymbol{z}):=\mathbf{R}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{s}.

The setup is now similar to
Lemma

4
. We define

\mathbf{R}_{\infty}:=\begin{pmatrix}2\mathbf{P}_{\gamma}&-\mathbf{P}_{\gamma}\\
\mathbf{I}_{n}&\phantom{-}\mathbf{0}_{n}\end{pmatrix},

and verify the assumptions in
Lemma

4
. Since
\alpha_{k}\to 1
, it is evident that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbf{R}_{k}=\mathbf{R}_{\infty}
. On the other hand, it follows from
Lemma

6
that
\sigma(\mathbf{P}_{\gamma})\subset[0,1)
. Hence, from
Proposition

5
, we have
\rho(\mathbf{R}_{\infty})<1
. Finally, we can check from
eq.

15
that

\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\in\mathrm{fix}(\mathtt{T}_{k})
for all
k\geqslant 1
. Therefore, we can conclude from
Lemma

4
that there exist
\beta\in[0,1)
, constant
C>0
, norm
\|\cdot\|
on
\mathbf{R}^{2n}
, and
K\geqslant 1
such that

\forall\,k\geqslant K:\quad\|\boldsymbol{z}_{k}-\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\|\leqslant C\,\beta^{k}.

(16)

Since
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\|\leqslant c\,\|\boldsymbol{z}_{k}-\boldsymbol{z}^{*}\|
for some constant
c>0
(see definition
eq.

14
), it is immediate from
eq.

16
that
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}_{k\geqslant 1}
converges linearly to
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}
.

The analysis for
Algorithm

2
is similar. For the loss in
eq.

5
, we have

\mathrm{prox}_{(\lambda L)^{-1}\,f}(\boldsymbol{y})=\mathbf{H}_{\mu}\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{r},

where
\mu:=(\lambda L)^{-1},\,\mathbf{H}_{\mu}:=(\mathbf{I}_{n}+\mu\mathbf{A}^{\top}\!\mathbf{A})^{-1}
, and
\boldsymbol{r}:=\mu\,\mathbf{H}_{\mu}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\!\boldsymbol{b}
. Thus, we can write step
4
of
Algorithm

2
as
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\mathbf{H}_{\mu}\boldsymbol{v}_{k-1}+\boldsymbol{r}
. Combining this with steps
5
and
6
, we have

\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=(1+\alpha_{k-1})\,\mathbf{P}_{\mu,\theta}\,\boldsymbol{x}_{k-1}-\alpha_{k-1}\mathbf{P}_{\mu,\theta}\,\boldsymbol{x}_{k-2}+\boldsymbol{r},

\mathbf{P}_{\mu,\theta}:=\mathbf{H}_{\mu}\mathbf{W}_{\theta}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbf{W}_{\theta}:=\theta\mathbf{W}+(1-\theta)\mathbf{I}_{n}.

Beyond this point, the analysis proceeds as before. Namely, we move from the iterates
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k}\}
to the state space sequence
\{\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\}

The convergence analysis for

is given by
2
2

2

Note that
\mathbf{W}_{\texttt{NLM}}
is self-adjoint with respect to

17

[

,

\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle_{\mathbf{D}}:=\boldsymbol{x}^{\top}\mathbf{D}\boldsymbol{y}.

(17)

17

is positive definite by construction.

Accordingly, step

in

(and

) must be changed to

where
\nabla_{\mathbf{D}}f
is the gradient of
f
with respect to the inner-product
eq.

17
. In fact, we have
\nabla_{\mathbf{D}}f(\boldsymbol{y})=\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\!(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{b})
. Hence, we can write
eq.

18

\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\mathbf{W}_{\texttt{NLM}}\big(\boldsymbol{y}_{k}-\gamma\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\!(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}-\boldsymbol{b})\big)=\mathbf{P}_{\gamma}\boldsymbol{y}_{k}+\boldsymbol{q},

\mathbf{P}_{\gamma}:=\mathbf{W}_{\texttt{NLM}}(\mathbf{I}_{n}-\gamma\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\!\mathbf{A})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\boldsymbol{q}:=\gamma\mathbf{W}_{\texttt{NLM}}\mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\!\boldsymbol{b}.

(19)

The other updates in
PnP-FISTA
stay unchanged. Following
[
10
]
, we will refer to this modified algorithm as
scaled-PnP-FISTA
. Similar to
Corollary

9
, we have the following result
3
3

3

Conclusion

We presented a concise analysis of the global linear convergence of
FISTA
in the context of regularizing linear inverse problems with symmetric denoisers. Additionally, we showed how the results can be extended to nonsymmetric denoisers by operating in an appropriate Euclidean space and using a scaled variant of the original algorithm. We restricted the scope to linear denoisers to keep the analysis tractable. However, data-driven linear denoisers are interesting in their own right in that they come with fast algorithms
[
24
]
and give good reconstructions for different applications. It would be interesting to see if the present analysis could shed some insights on the convergence theory of trained nonlinear denoisers that give state-of-the-art reconstructions.
