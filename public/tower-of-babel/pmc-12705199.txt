Adjunctive phenobarbital for severe alcohol withdrawal
Authors: Sarah Whynot, Braden Bouchard, Danielle Buell, Wiplove Lamba
Published: 2025

Benzodiazepine-resistant alcohol withdrawal is uncommon but associated with substantial morbidity

Most patients with alcohol withdrawal symptoms improve with benzodiazepines; however, certain individuals with severe withdrawal and functional γ-aminobutyric acid deficiency experience a blunted response. Benzodiazepine-resistant withdrawal is characterized by persistent or worsening symptoms (e.g., agitation, tachycardia, seizures) despite use of high-dose benzodiazepines (e.g., > 40 mg/h diazepam), which can lead to intubation and long hospital stays. Around 15% of patients with severe alcohol withdrawal have benzodiazepine resistance.1

Phenobarbital is an effective adjunctive medication for alcohol withdrawal

Adjunctive phenobarbital is recommended for patients with severe alcohol withdrawal requiring hospitalization, including those demonstrating benzodiazepine resistance.2 Phenobarbital acts synergistically with benzodiazepines and is associated with reduced intensive care unit (ICU) admissions, intubation rates, and hospital length of stay.3–5

Clinicians can consider a single loading dose or smaller repeated doses

Common approaches include a 1-time intravenous (IV) dose of 7.5 to 10 mg/kg (ideal body weight) or smaller doses of, for example, 130 mg IV every 30 minutes until the patient is lightly sedated.3 Subsequently, symptom-triggered treatment can be continued with phenobarbital or benzodiazepines. Cautious dosing is recommended in patients with hepatic dysfunction (given impaired clearance), advanced age, or concurrent sedative use. Phenobarbital is a cytochrome P450 3A4 inducer with multiple drug interactions (e.g., chemotherapy, warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants, ticagrelor, antiretroviral therapy). Contraindications include porphyria and severe liver impairment.

Severe alcohol withdrawal should be managed in monitored settings

Although use of phenobarbital alone does not necessitate ICU admission, adverse effects (e.g., sedation, respiratory depression, hypotension) are dose dependent. Patients with severe withdrawal and hemodynamic instability receiving high-dose benzodiazepines and adjunctive phenobarbital should be observed in high-acuity areas (e.g., emergency department, step-down unit, ICU) until symptoms improve.

Phenobarbital’s long half-life facilitates smooth automatic tapering

Once symptom control is achieved in hospital, no further doses or prescriptions are required.
