First-line ET plus palbociclib versus standard mono-chemotherapy in high-risk HR-positive/HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer and indication for chemotherapy: primary results from the randomized phase IV PADMA study☆
Authors: M. Schmidt, M. Thill, J. Rey, B. Rautenberg, V. Bjelic-Radisic, T. Decker, J. Rom, M. Kögel, K. Lübbe, A. Nacke, S. Seiler, M. Just, V. Müller, R. Buss-Steidle, J. Terhaag, C. Mundhenke, C. Denkert, J. Holtschmidt, S. Loibl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPADMA is a prospective, randomized, open-label, multicenter phase IV study comparing palbociclib and endocrine therapy (ET) with mono-chemotherapy (CT) +/− maintenance ET (at the discretion of the treating physician) as first-line therapy in patients with hormone receptor–positive/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2–negative (HR-positive/HER2-negative) metastatic breast cancer (mBC).Patients and methodsPatients were randomly assigned 1:1 to receive first-line treatment with palbociclib + ET or investigator-selected CT (capecitabine, epirubicin, paclitaxel, or vinorelbine) +/− maintenance ET. The primary endpoint was time-to-treatment failure (TTF). Secondary endpoints were progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), safety, and treatment adherence.ResultsOne hundred twenty patients, of whom 106 (88.3%) were postmenopausal, were randomly assigned and started treatment (palbociclib + ET, n = 61; CT, n = 59). Most patients in the CT group received capecitabine (n = 40, 67.8%) or paclitaxel (n = 17, 28.8%). In 13 patients (22%), CT was followed by maintenance ET. At a median follow-up of 36.8 months, the median TTF was 17.2 months with palbociclib + ET versus 6.1 months with CT [hazard ratio 0.46, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.31-0.69, P < 0.001]. The TTF benefit in subgroups, including AKT signaling alterations or g/tBRCA1/2 mutations, was consistent with that in the overall population. Median PFS was 18.7 months with palbociclib + ET versus 7.8 months with CT (hazard ratio 0.45, 95% CI 0.29-0.70, P < 0.001). OS did not differ significantly between the study arms (P = 0.494). Hematologic toxicity was significantly higher in the palbociclib + ET arm than in the CT-based arm (96.8% versus 70.7%; P < 0.001), although nonhematological toxicity was comparable in both arms.ConclusionPADMA is the first randomized multicenter study to show that palbociclib + ET as first-line treatment compared with CT also leads to improvements in TTF and PFS in predominantly postmenopausal patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC.

Introduction

Survival of patients with metastatic breast cancer (mBC) has improved over the past two decades, particularly for patients with hormone receptor–positive (HR-positive), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2–negative (HER2-negative) mBC.1 Guidelines recommend using endocrine-based therapy (ET) as first-line treatment for most patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC, except for those with rapidly progressive, life-threatening disease, also referred to as visceral crisis, a term that remains inconsistently defined across trials.2,3

Over the past decade, combining cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 inhibitors (CDK4/6i) with ET has demonstrated significant treatment efficacy as first-line therapy in mBC, improving progression-free survival (PFS), and, in some cases, overall survival (OS).4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Based on these results, therapy with ET + CDK4/6i is now a widely accepted first-line treatment for HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC.2,3,10 Although real-world evidence has shown that the relevance of chemotherapy (CT) in the first-line treatment of HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC is decreasing, patients with a supposedly unfavorable prognosis often still receive CT as initial treatment.11,12 At the time of the PADMA trial conception, no clinical trials were actively investigating CDK4/6i + ET versus CT in patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC. Meanwhile, results from other clinical trials were subsequently published, yet none investigated ET combined with a CDK4/6i as first-line treatment compared with standard-of-care mono-CT as postulated in international guidelines at the time of the trial.13, 14, 15 Although several randomized trials have demonstrated superior outcomes with ET + CDK4/6 inhibition compared with CT, data in predominantly postmenopausal European patient populations, exclusively in the first-line setting, and in real-world-informed clinical decision settings, remain limited.

Here, we present the results of the PADMA study (NCT03355157; GBG 93), the first prospective, randomized, multicenter trial to compare ET with palbociclib to mono-CT +/− maintenance ET as per the treating physician’s choice as first-line therapy for patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC.

Patients and methods

Study design

The PADMA study is a prospective, randomized, open-label, multicenter, controlled, trial (NCT03355157) that compared treatment with palbociclib and ET versus mono-CT +/− maintenance ET of the treating physician's choice as first-line treatment for patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC. Although formally registered as a phase IV trial due to the approved status of palbociclib, the randomized comparative design and sample size are more consistent with a phase II study. The study design is presented in Supplementary Figure S1 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107707). The trial complied with Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. Study protocol and amendments were approved by the competent authorities and an independent ethics committee. An independent data monitoring committee oversaw the study.

Patients

Female or male patients aged ≥18 years with histologically confirmed invasive HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC who, at investigator’s discretion, had an indication for mono-CT for the treatment of first-line mBC, were eligible. More than 6 months’ life expectancy and adequate organ function immediately before randomization were required. The indication for CT was determined by the treating physician based on clinical criteria including visceral involvement, symptomatic disease, rapid disease progression, and high tumor burden. No formal guideline-based algorithm was mandated. This approach reflects real-world clinical decision-making, where the choice between ET and CT is often individualized. Patients with uncontrolled visceral crisis were excluded from the study. Patients with oligometastatic disease were eligible for inclusion. Patients with asymptomatic bone metastases as the only site of distant disease, or uncontrolled/untreated brain metastases, were ineligible, as were those with prior systemic therapy for mBC, or requiring combination CT, ET alone, or bevacizumab.

Randomization and masking

Randomization was carried out centrally via permuted block randomization, stratified by (i) hormone-resistant (relapse at or within 12 months after completing adjuvant ET) versus hormone-sensitive (relapse >12 months after the end of ET or de novo metastatic HR-positive/HER2-negative BC) and (ii) symptomatic versus asymptomatic disease. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Procedures

Patients were randomly assigned 1:1 to receive oral palbociclib (125 mg/daily; 3-week-on, 1-week-off) + ET (letrozole 2.5 mg, anastrozole 1 mg, or exemestane 25 mg/daily, or fulvestrant 500 mg intramuscularly every 14 days for the first three injections, then every 28 days, all combined with gonadotropin-releasing hormone analog in pre-/perimenopausal or male patients) or investigator's choice of guideline-supported first-line CT for mBC. Permitted CT agents included capecitabine, paclitaxel, epirubicin, and vinorelbine, reflecting standard clinical practice at the time of study design.

Measurable and nonmeasurable lesions were documented, and clinical disease assessment was carried out according to national guidelines, which are broadly consistent with RECIST criteria, although formal RECIST-based central review was not mandated. Disease assessments were carried out at regular intervals according to national guidelines, typically every 8-12 weeks, depending on clinical context.16 Patients with progressive disease, unacceptable toxicity, intolerability of the study drug, or lack of compliance were removed from study therapy, and any subsequent treatment was at the investigator’s discretion. Patients were followed up regularly for progression. The follow-up period ended 8 months after the last randomization (14 December 2023).

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was time-to-treatment failure (TTF), defined as time from randomization to discontinuation of treatment due to disease progression, treatment toxicity, patient’s preference, or death. Secondary endpoints included PFS, defined as the time from randomization to first progression as assessed by the investigator or death, whichever occurs first; OS, defined as the time from randomization to death due to any reason; time-to-first subsequent treatment (TFST), defined as the time from randomization to start of first subsequent anticancer treatment or death, time-to-first subsequent CT (TFSCT), defined as the time from randomization to start of first subsequent CT or death, and time-to-second subsequent treatment regimen (TSST), defined as the time from randomization to start of second subsequent treatment regimen or death. A timeline of the key efficacy endpoints is presented in Supplementary Figure S2 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107707). Time to response (TTR), defined as the time from randomization in first-line mBC treatment to response as assessed by the investigator (response, no response, not evaluable), was one of the exploratory endpoints of the study. A second exploratory endpoint was the detection of gene mutations in tumor and blood samples for the prevalence of pathogenic variants in preselected genes using next-generation sequencing (NGS). NGS was carried out using the Illumina NextSeq platform with a custom Archer/IDT panel (Archer, Inc./IDT, Boulder, CO). The panel was carried out using standard short-range sequencing, which detects single nucleotide variants and small rearrangements, but not large rearrangements. In order to translate the results into a clinical therapy recommendation, the Molecular Health Guide was used.17

Safety by toxicity grades were defined by the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4.0 and assessed based on any grade (grade 1-4) adverse events (AE) and serious adverse events (SAE, including AEs of grade 5). Compliance was assessed based on treatment reductions, delays, interruptions, and permanent discontinuations of study medication with reasons.

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation was based on the primary endpoint TTF. Overall, 110 events were required to achieve 80% power to detect a hazard ratio of 0.667 (corresponding to a risk reduction by 33%) in favor of palbociclib and ET, using a two-sided log rank test with a significance level of α = 0.2. This required randomly assigning 150 patients. Initially, sample size calculation was based on a randomized phase IV trial with a phase III significance level of α = 0.05 (n = 360 patients). Due to slow accrual, the trial design was adapted to a phase II design to compensate for a smaller sample size than initially planned.

The primary and secondary efficacy analyses were carried out based on the modified intent-to-treat (mITT) set, i.e. randomly assigned patients who received at least one dose of study medication. Patients receiving maintenance ET following CT were not censored at the time of ET initiation; TTF was defined irrespective of subsequent maintenance treatment.

The Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate 6- and 12-month probabilities (and for the endpoint OS, the 2- and 3-year probabilities) and the median time of the time-to-event data (TTF, PFS, TFST, TFSCT, TSST, and OS), together with the corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to analyze time-to-event endpoints, reporting hazard ratio with 95% CI and adjusting for predefined covariates. If the proportional hazard assumption was violated, the hazard ratio may not be an adequate measure of effect. Instead, the restricted mean survival time (RMST) per treatment arm was reported and compared using a Wald-type test based on an approximate chi-square statistic. The RMST estimates the mean survival time up to a pre-specified time point (see statistical methods in the supplemental material).18 Median follow-up time was estimated with the inverse Kaplan–Meier method, and the completeness of the follow-up was assessed according to the approach reported by Clark et al.19

Safety and compliance analyses were carried out in the safety analysis set which corresponds to the mITT analysis set, except for one patient who started palbociclib instead of CT and was analyzed as treated for safety/compliance. Differences in compliance and safety data were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test.

Data were considered ‘clean’ after completion of predefined data validation procedures, including consistency checks and resolution of data queries. Analyses were carried out with SAS® (Statistical Analysis Software, Cary, NC) version 9.4 with SAS Enterprise Guide version 8.3 on Microsoft Windows 10 Enterprise.

Results

Between April 2018 and December 2023, 140 patients were screened across 28 sites in Germany, and 130 patients were enrolled (65 patients in both arms; Figure 1). Although a total of 150 patients were initially planned for randomization, the study was stopped early due to slow accrual. A power analysis before stopping the trial did not reveal a significant loss of power (>78%), also due to the longer recruitment period. The median duration of treatment with palbociclib + ET was 51 weeks [interquartile range (IQR), 15.0-121.0 weeks]. Median duration of CT in the CT arm was 19.5 weeks (IQR, 11.0-30.0 weeks). For 13 patients (22.4%), CT was followed by maintenance ET with a median duration of 32 weeks (IQR, 10.0-46.0 weeks). The most common CT administered was capecitabine (n = 40, 69.0%) followed by paclitaxel (n = 17, 29.3%) and only one patient received vinorelbine (1.7%; Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107707). Median duration of CT was 24.5 weeks (IQR, 10.5-37.5) for patients treated with capecitabine and 17.0 weeks for patients treated with paclitaxel (IQR, 12.0-23.0). Most patients received letrozole as ET [n = 44, 71.0% in the palbociclib + ET arm and seven (53.8%) patients as ET maintenance in the CT arm; Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107707].

Primary endpoint: TTF

After a median follow-up of 36.8 (IQR, 15.0-47.7) months, 45 (73.8%) patients in the palbociclib + ET arm and 55 (93.2%) in the CT arm experienced a TTF event. The leading cause of treatment failure was disease progression. In the palbociclib + ET group, 32 (52.5%) patients showed progression, compared with 45 (76.3%) patients in the CT group. An overview of the causes of treatment failure is given in Supplementary Table S3 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107707).

The median TTF was significantly longer for the palbociclib + ET arm with 17.2 months (95% CI 7.2-25.9) compared with 6.1 months (95% CI 4.3-8.8) for the CT arm (hazard ratio 0.46, 95% CI 0.31-0.69, P < 0.001; Figure 2). Secondary endpoints: PFS, OS, TFST, TFSCT, and TSST

As shown in Figure 4A, the median PFS was significantly longer for palbociclib + ET at 18.7 months (95% CI 10.6-29.1) compared with 7.8 months (95% CI 6.0-10.0) for CT (hazard ratio 0.45, 95% CI 0.29-0.70, P < 0.001). Subgroup analysis confirmed improved PFS across major subgroups (Supplementary Figure S3 available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107707).Figure 4Kaplan–Meier curve for (A) PFS and (B) OS per treatment arm.CI, confidence interval; CT, chemotherapy; ET, endocrine therapy; OS, overall survival; PFS, progression-free survival.

During follow-up, 69 (57.5%) patients received at least one anticancer treatment after the end of treatment (Supplementary Table S4, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107707). Regarding the 30 patients in the palbociclib + ET arm and the 39 patients in the CT arm who received subsequent therapy (i.e. any subsequent therapy), CT was given to 21 (70.0%) patients in the palbociclib + ET arm and to 13 (33.3%) in the CT arm. ET as subsequent therapy was given to 18 (60.0%) patients in the palbociclib + ET arm and 34 (87.2%) of those in the CT arm (Supplementary Table S5 available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107707). In the CT arm, 31 (79.5%) patients received subsequent CDK4/6 inhibitor-based therapy after disease progression, which may have influenced OS outcomes. Details on post-study treatments, including CDK4/6 inhibitor use, are provided in Supplementary Tables S4 and S5 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107707).

There was no statistically significant difference in OS. There was a numerical improvement in median OS with 46.1 months (95% CI 27.2-NA) in the palbociclib + ET arm versus 36.8 months (95% CI 25.2-NA) in the CT arm (Figure 4B). The OS data were relatively immature at database cutoff date, with 25 (41.0%) and 24 (40.7%) deaths in the palbociclib and CT arms, respectively. As the proportional hazard (PH) assumption was violated for OS at this early analysis, the RMST for OS was estimated instead up to τ = 46 months: 33.6 months (95% CI 29.3-37.9) for the palbociclib + ET arm and 31.5 months (95% CI 27.1-35.9) for the CT arm (P = 0.494).

As shown in Supplementary Figure S4 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107707), the median TFST was significantly longer for palbociclib + ET with 19.9 months (95% CI 11.0-30.7) compared with 8.0 months (95% CI 6.8-13.1) for CT (hazard ratio 0.50, 95% CI 0.32-0.77, P = 0.002). Median TSST was 13.2 months longer with palbociclib + ET compared with CT; however, due to a violation of the PH assumptions at this early analysis, RMST estimates were used for TSST, which did not show significant differences (RMST until τ =35 months: 24.9 versus 21.5 months, respectively; P = 0.133) (Supplementary Figure S5, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107707). Further details of TFST, TFSCT, and TSST events are outlined in Supplementary Tables 6-8 (available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107707), respectively.

The exploratory endpoint TTR did not differ between arms [median TTR, month (95% CI) palbociclib + ET 4.5 (3.5-8.9) versus CT 7.3 (3.9-NA); hazard ratio 1.29 (0.76-2.20)].

Gene mutations analyzed

Tumor and blood samples from 103 (85.8%) patients were analyzed. As shown in Table 1, the most common pathogenic variants were found in the AKT pathway (28.0% in the palbociclib + ET arm versus 32.1% in the CT arm), as well as TP53 (10.0% in the palbociclib + ET arm versus 11.3% in the CT arm). g/tBRCA1/2 and/or g/tPALB2 mutations were found in 8% in the palbociclib + ET arm and 13.2% in the CT arm. There was no significant difference in the efficacy of palbociclib + ET in relation to AKT pathway (Pinteraction 0.503) or g/tBRCA1/2 and/or g/tPALB2 mutations (Pinteraction 0.148) (Figure 3; Supplementary Figure S6A,B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107707).

Almost all patients in the trial experienced an AE of any grade (n = 119, 99.2%). Hematological toxicity was significantly higher in the palbociclib + ET arm compared with the CT arm [n = 60 (96.8%) versus n = 41 (70.7%) P < 0.001]. The proportion of nonhematological toxicity was comparable in both arms [n = 61 (98.4%) versus n = 58 (100%); P = 1.000]. Clinically relevant nonhematological toxicities included palmar–plantar erythrodysesthesia in the capecitabine-treated patients and peripheral neuropathy in the paclitaxel-treated patients, both of which contributed to treatment modifications and discontinuations.

High-grade hematological AEs were significantly more frequent in the palbociclib + ET compared with the CT-based arm [n = 35 (56.5%) versus n = 6 (10.3%), respectively; P < 0.001]. There was no significant difference in high-grade nonhematological AEs, which occurred in 34 (54.8%) patients in the palbociclib + ET arm versus 25 patients (43.1%) in the CT arm (P = 0.208). Peripheral neuropathy reported in the palbociclib arm was predominantly low-grade and may reflect prior treatments or comorbidities rather than treatment-related toxicity.

SAEs occurred in 18 (29.0%) patients and 16 (27.6%) patients in the palbociclib + ET and CT arm (P = 1.000), respectively. The majority of which were infections and nervous system disorders. During therapy, six patients (all of whom were in the palbociclib + ET arm) experienced a fatal outcome, one of which was considered treatment related (septic shock). The other fatal events (all considered not treatment related) were pneumatosis intestinalis, hepatic failure, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, hematemesis, and pneumonia.

Compliance

Overall, 26 (41.9%) patients required dose reductions of palbociclib, mainly due to treatment-related hematological AEs (n = 24, 38.7%). Seventeen (29.3%) patients required dose reductions of CT, mainly due to treatment-related nonhematological AEs (n = 13, 22.4%). Dose reductions in the CT arm (29.3%) were predominantly driven by nonhematological toxicities, including gastrointestinal AE, palmar–plantar erythrodysesthesia, and neuropathy.

Dose delays of study treatment occurred more often in patients receiving palbociclib + ET compared with patients in the CT arm [n = 42 (67.7%) versus n = 26 (44.8%), respectively; P = 0.016]. The median delay was 7 days (IQR, 7-10 days) in the palbociclib + ET arm compared with 10 days (IQR, 3-19 days) in the CT arm. These delays were mainly due to organizational reasons (n = 26, 41.9%) or treatment-related hematological toxicity (n = 25, 40.3%). Interruption of study treatment was not significantly different between the treatment arms [n = 34 (54.8%) versus n = 32 (55.2%), respectively; P = 1.000].

As indicated by the TTF rate, the discontinuation rate was higher in the CT arm than in the palbociclib + ET arm. An overview of the reasons for treatment discontinuation is shown in Supplementary Table 3.

Discussion

The PADMA trial in patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC demonstrated a superior TTF for first-line palbociclib with ET compared with mono-CT +/- subsequent maintenance ET (median TTF 17.2 versus 6.1 months, respectively; hazard ratio 0.46, 95% CI 0.31-0.69, P = 0.0002). A significant and clinically meaningful improvement was observed in most subgroups and was also evident for PFS. TTF is an important clinical endpoint in oncologic studies, offering a more patient-centric measure than PFS alone, especially when comparing different drug modalities. Unlike PFS, TTF also considers treatment discontinuation due to toxicity and patient preference.20

The leading cause of treatment failure was disease progression, which was more frequent in the CT arm, hence why TTF correlated highly with PFS. Patients receiving palbociclib + ET displayed a significantly lower risk for progression compared with patients receiving CT-based therapy (median PFS 18.7 versus 7.8 months, respectively; hazard ratio 0.45, 95% CI 0.29-0.70, P = 0.0002). However, OS was not improved. More than two-thirds of patients in the CT arm received subsequent ET combined with a CDK4/6 inhibitor, which may attenuate potential differences in OS between treatment strategies. In addition, no new safety signals were observed.

CDK4/6i with ET has been gradually implemented in international guidelines in the post-first-line setting since 2016, and they are nowadays clearly recommended as first-line therapy for HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC.2 Yet, with rare exceptions, CDK4/6i treatment was always compared with ET-based treatments and not to CT-based approaches in the first-line setting. Before the PADMA trial, the absence of prospective data resulted in many patients receiving first-line CT.11 As second-line treatment, however, the results varied: the youngPEARL study13 showed that second-line treatment with palbociclib + aromatase inhibitor (AI) compared with capecitabine mono-CT in premenopausal women with HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC lead to enhanced PFS outcomes (median PFS 20.1 versus 14.4 months, respectively; hazard ratio 0.66, 95% CI 0.44-0.99), whereas the PEARL trial14 using the same treatment showed no benefit in postmenopausal women (median PFS 7.5 versus 10.0 months, respectively; hazard ratio 1.13, 95% CI 0.85-1.50).

Long after the initiation of the PADMA trial, the randomized, open-label, phase II trial RIGHT Choice reported results comparing ribociclib with AI to poly-CT in high-risk patients as first-line treatment in HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC.15 First-line ribociclib + ET showed a significant PFS benefit, similar response rates, and better tolerability over poly-CT (median PFS 21.8 versus 12.7 months, respectively; hazard ratio 0.61, 95% CI 0.43- 0.87).

Our results confirm the findings of the youngPEARL and RIGHT Choice trials by showing an improved PFS via treatment with CDK4/6i + ET over CT.13,15 The present study adds to the existing body of evidence by evaluating this treatment strategy in a clinically relevant population in which upfront CT was actively considered. In contrast to the PADMA study, youngPEARL and RIGHT Choice enrolled predominantly pre-/perimenopausal Asian patients. The youngPEARL study included mBC patients with multiple previous palliative therapy lines, and the RIGHT Choice trial used a different CDK4/6i (ribociclib) and combination CT as comparator in a group of patients with clinically aggressive BC. Most patients enrolled in the RIGHT Choice trial had symptomatic disease (81.5%), youngPEARL excluded patients with symptomatic severe visceral metastases, whereas 45% of the patients in the PADMA study were symptomatic. In addition, and while most patients in the PADMA trial were postmenopausal women (88.3%), this trial still reported a TTF and a PFS advantage with palbociclib + ET, unlike the PEARL study. One explanation for the apparent difference in results between PADMA and PEARL could be that in PEARL, the majority of patients were treated with second- or third-line therapy, whereas PADMA was a first-line study. To our knowledge, the PADMA study is the first to demonstrate the superior efficacy of palbociclib + ET compared with CT in patients with postmenopausal mBC.

Recently, Schettini et al.21 compared CDK4/6i + ET with CT followed by maintenance ET as initial treatment in HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC in the randomized KENDO study. Capecitabine was the most commonly used cytostatic agent. The study was terminated early due to slow accrual and showed no significant differences between treatment groups. The efficacy of the CT group could not be improved by maintenance ET after CT. In our study, CT was followed by maintenance ET in a subset of patients; however, a formal comparative analysis to assess its impact on outcomes was not reasonable due to a high selection bias. Overall, two randomized trials13,15 have previously shown improved PFS for CDK4/6i + ET compared with CT in HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC, whereas two other randomized trials14,21 have not been able to confirm this. The PADMA trial provides further evidence to the recommendation that CDK4/6i + ET should be preferred over CT as first-line treatment for HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC with an indication for CT. Importantly, PADMA is the first randomized study to show improved PFS with CDK4/6i + ET versus CT in first-line even in postmenopausal HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC.

Additional findings from the recent phase II ABIGAIL trial demonstrated that abemaciclib + ET resulted in a superior 12-week objective response rate compared with paclitaxel (58.8% versus 40.2%, respectively; P = 0.0193) as first-line treatment in previously untreated HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC patients with aggressive disease.22 Although long-term efficacy outcomes including PFS are not yet available, these results are in line with our findings.

The higher hematologic toxicity observed with palbociclib + ET compared with CT in PADMA is not unexpected and is consistent with results from other studies.13,14 However, non-hematologic toxicity was not increased, and the significantly improved TTF with palbociclib + ET, taking into account the prolonged median duration of therapy and the possibility of dose reductions, underscores that this regimen is more effective and better tolerated in HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC with an indication for CT.

Meanwhile, targeted therapies against mutations in BRCA1/2, PIK3CA, AKT, PTEN, and ESR1 have significantly improved treatment options for patients with HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC.23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28 Importantly, our results from 103 (85.8%) patients treated in the PADMA trial showed no significant impact of BRCA1/2 or AKT signaling pathway mutations on treatment efficacy. However, the analysis of BRCA1/2 and PALB2 mutation carriers is limited by small sample size and should be considered exploratory.

Our study is limited by the low numbers of patients recruited, which has subsequently led to the trial’s premature termination. Moreover, the study was open-label, which could introduce potential bias, and there was no independent assessment of PFS. In addition, the investigator-assessed indication for CT may introduce variability across centers and represents a limitation of the study. Despite this, PADMA’s strengths lie in the fact that the inclusion criteria were not restricted for menopausal status or symptomatic metastases, allowing the vast majority of HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC with CT indication to be included in first-line treatment. Mono-CT was used in the trial, which is recommended in most guidelines and typically used in routine clinical practice. In addition, the primary endpoint TTF includes various factors (e.g. progression, toxicity, patient choice, or death) that lead to treatment discontinuation, thus providing a broader perspective on treatment efficacy and tolerability and reflecting a more patient-centric efficacy assessment. Moreover, genetic analyses of the most frequently encountered mutations, including BRCA1/2 and the AKT pathway, could be carried out on archived FFPE at the time of the primary analysis, with no significant differences in the efficacy of palbociclib + ET observed.

In conclusion, the PADMA study is the first prospective, randomized study to compare first-line treatment of HR-positive/HER2-negative mBC with CDK4/6i + ET to single-agent CT and shows that the former is associated with clinically meaningful and statistically significant improvements in TTF compared with CT, also supported by PFS outcomes.
