Intra-pol proviral open region of HTLV-1 controls the transcription from both long terminal repeats
Authors: Miyu Sonoda, Azusa Tanaka, Xueda Chen, Wenyi Zhang, Helen Kiik, Saumya Ramanayake, Kisato Nosaka, Norihiro Takenouchi, Masanori Nakagawa, Akihiro Fujimoto, Charles R M Bangham, Masao Matsuoka, Jun-ichirou Yasunaga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman T-cell leukemia virus type 1 (HTLV-1) infects mainly CD4+ T lymphocytes and causes both malignant and inflammatory diseases: the aggressive malignancy known as Adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATL) and several chronic inflammatory syndromes. HTLV-1 infection is established by integration of proviral DNA (~9000 bp) into the host genome. In HTLV-1, two viral genes, tax and HBZ, play critical roles in viral transcription and promotion of T-cell proliferation, respectively. The present study was undertaken to test the hypothesis that the higher-order structure of proviral chromatin regulates its transcription on both the plus strand and the minus strand.ResultsATAC-seq analysis identified an open chromatin region in the pol gene of proviral DNA, which we name IPOR, in many ATL cases. Using reporter assays, it was found that the sequence of IPOR suppresses the transcription of the plus strand and activates that of the minus strand. Binding motifs of Eomes and TEAD proteins were predicted in this region, and we confirmed recruitment of the transcription factors to their respective motifs by ChIP-qPCR. A mutant of IPOR which cannot bind the transcription factors weakened the transcriptional activating effects compared with the wild type, suggesting that the IPOR and those transcription factors suppress the 5' long terminal repeat (LTR) but activate the 3'LTR. In addition, a mutant HTLV-1 molecular clone, which possesses the IPOR mutant, produced a higher titer of virus than the wild type. RNA-seq analysis of HTLV-1-infected cell lines, in which Tax expression can be traced after induction, revealed that EOMES expression decreases during the tax transcriptional burst and resumes following termination of the burst. These findings suggested that the expression dynamics of Eomes affect the transient expression of Tax.ConclusionsThe IPOR sequence appears to regulate the transcription from both LTRs, suppressing the 5'LTR but activating the 3'LTR. Recruitment of Eomes to the IPOR is likely to influence the expression of Tax and HBZ. Since both viral genes are involved in diverse mechanisms for viral replication, cellular proliferation, and immune regulation, the IPOR may play a role in fine-tuning the modes of viral persistence in vivo.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12977-025-00671-4.Keywords: HTLV-1, Tax, IPOR, EOMES, HBZ

Introduction

Human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 (HTLV-1) causes both adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATL) and inflammatory diseases including HTLV-1 associated myelopathy/tropical spastic paraparesis (HAM/TSP) [1]. Another human retrovirus, human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1), is the causative agent of AIDS in humans. Although both retroviruses target CD4+ T cells, replication and viral gene expression differ between the two retroviruses. HIV-1 replicates vigorously in vivo, which leads to massive apoptosis of CD4+ T cells and profound immunodeficiency. To enable massive replication of HIV-1, CpG sites, which are targets for DNA hypermethylation, are very few in the provirus of HIV-1 [2]. By contrast, the steady-state replication level of HTLV-1 is very low in vivo and the virus maintains persistent infection during the long chronic phase of infection chiefly by proliferation of infected cells [1, 3]. New HTLV-1-infected T cell clones are established mainly in the early stage of infection [3]. Thereafter, HTLV-1-infected clones are selected in vivo, and clonal proliferation is mainly responsible for maintaining the number of infected cells [4]. At a given instant, most HTLV-1-infected cells silence expression of the immunodominant viral proteins to evade the host immune surveillance. Unlike HIV-1, the HTLV-1 provirus contains a large number of CpG sites [2], which are frequently methylated in vivo.

In HTLV-1, the mechanisms and pattern of viral gene transcription differ between the sense and anti-sense directions. Transcription of the sense strand depends strongly on the viral transcriptional activator protein Tax. Tax is transiently expressed, causing robust transcription of the sense strand of the HTLV-1 provirus [5]. Since viral replication requires transcription of the structural gene cluster encoded on the plus strand, transcription of tax is essential for HTLV-1 infection. By contrast, transcription of the anti-sense strand from the 3' long terminal repeat (LTR) is relatively constant. HBZ expression is regulated by the transcription factor Sp1 [6, 7]. JunD, a Jun family protein constituting AP-1, cooperates with Sp1 to increase the transcriptional activity of HBZ [8]. In some ATL cases the 5'LTR is highly methylated, causing repression of tax transcription [9]. However, it has been shown that the 3'LTR and the coding region of HBZ do not show DNA methylation. Recently, it was found that a CTCF-binding region exists in the pX region and forms the boundary of DNA methylation [10]. It is thought that the insulator activity by CTCF borders the epigenomic changes, resulting in a difference in transcriptional regulation between the plus and minus strand, leading to stable expression of HBZ.

The transcription factor Eomesodermin (Eomes) belongs to the family of T-box transcription factors and shares a sequence-specific T-box DNA-binding domain with T-bet (T-box expressed in T cells). Eomes was originally identified in Xenopus [11] and has since been found in many vertebrates, playing important roles in mesoderm formation and early embryogenesis [12]. In the immune system, Eomes and T-bet enhance IFN-γ expression in CD8+ T cells [13]. Eomes is also known to promote CD8+ T-cell exhaustion [14, 15].

Both Eomes and T-bet are involved in the differentiation of murine CD4+ T cells. Eomes can induce both Th1 cell differentiation and the expression of IFN-γ and perforin in murine CD4+ T cells [13, 16]. In addition, Eomes, compensates for the deficiency of T-bet in CD4+ effector T cells and promotes multifunctionality in human CD4+ T cells [17].

In this study, we discovered an open region in HTLV-1 provirus which regulates viral transcription in cooperation with Eomes, suggesting an important role in persistence of the virus in vivo.

Results

ATAC-seq identifies an open chromatin region in HTLV-1 provirus

We recently reported the genome-wide landscape of chromatin accessibility in ATL cells and non-malignant HTLV-1-infected cells obtained from ATL patients and HAM/TSP patients by analyzing ATAC-seq [18, 19]. Using the ATAC-seq data of those clinical samples and HTLV-1-infected cell line, HPB-ATL-2, we searched for evidence of open chromatin regions in the HTLV-1 provirus. As shown in Fig. 1, open regions were identified in both LTRs and in the pX region, which is known to function as an enhancer [20]. In addition to these regions, we observed a further open chromatin region in the center of the provirus in many samples at positions 4099–4923 of the HTLV-1 proviral DNA sequence (isolate B1033-2009; GenBank accession number AB513134). Although the peaks of this open chromatin region were absent in two ATL samples (ATL 14 and 15), further analysis of the provirus structure revealed that those two ATL cases harbored a defective provirus, which lacked this region. Importantly, this open region was also observed in non-malignant HTLV-1-infected cells from HAM/TSP patients. These findings suggest that the chromatin structure on this locus remains open regardless of the disease status.

Since the sequence identified by ATAC-seq was located at the region encoding integrase in the pol gene, we named it the intra-pol proviral open region (IPOR). We hypothesized that IPOR acts as a cis-regulatory element in viral transcription, in addition to encoding part of pol. To understand the effects of IPOR on viral expression, we first searched for possible transcription factor binding sites in the region using The MEME Suite, Motif-based sequence analysis tools [21]. Interestingly, there are five elements containing similar sequences (Fig. 2A) in IPOR. The consensus sequence of the most frequently detected motif is shown in Fig. 2B. This motif was well conserved in the HTLV-1 proviral sequences examined, although there were slight variations in some isolates (Supplementary Fig. 1). We next looked for the transcription factors predicted to bind to that consensus sequence. Several proteins, including TEA domain family member 1, 3 (TEAD-1, TEAD-3), and Eomes, were identified as potential binders (Fig. 2C). These findings suggested that the five repetitive motifs regulate viral gene transcription by recruitment of the transcription factors identified.

Suppressive effects of IPOR on transcription from 5′LTR

To verify the possibility that IPOR has regulatory functions in viral transcription, we focused on the five repetitive motifs above which span nucleotides 4057–4626 in the proviral sequence. To evaluate the functions of this 573 bp sequence, we carried out a reporter assay using Jurkat cells. The HTLV-1 provirus is transcribed in both the sense and antisense strands, with the 5′LTR and 3′LTR functioning as promoters for each respective strand. To analyze the role of the IPOR sequence we focused on sense-strand transcription. We created a reporter plasmid composed of the following sequence: 5′LTR (promoter)-NanoLuc gene (reporter of the transcription from the promoter)-IPOR(573) (proviral internal region) (Fig. 3A). As a control for the IPOR reporter vector, another plasmid was created by inserting an unrelated 573 bp sequence from the provirus into the same location. The results of the reporter assays showed that the IPOR sequence suppressed the transcription from the 5′LTR (Fig. 3B, left). As Tax protein is a potent transcriptional transactivator and is thought to be critical for viral replication, we next evaluated the suppressive effect of IPOR in the presence of Tax. As expected, Tax strongly enhanced the transcription from the 5′LTR; however, IPOR still inhibited the reporter activity even in this setting, indicating that IPOR contains an element that strongly suppresses plus-strand transcription of both viral genomic RNA and other the regulatory and accessory genes. We conclude that IPOR has a negative effect on the viral replication.

Activation of anti-sense transcription from the 3′LTR by IPOR

The 3′LTR contains the promoter of the anti-sense-encoded viral gene, HBZ [7]. Whereas the sense transcription from the 5′LTR is typically suppressed in each HTLV-1-infected cell at a given time in vivo [9, 22], HBZ mRNA is frequently detected in primary ATL and HTLV-1-infected cells [23, 24]. We have previously reported that Tax is transiently expressed in ATL cells upon stimulation by genotoxic stresses, and HBZ transcripts were decreased in response to that temporary change [5]. These data highlight contrasting attributes of sense and antisense transcriptions from each respective LTR. Therefore, we analyzed the effect of IPOR on the transcription from the promoter of HBZ in the 3′LTR. It was known that the 300 bp DNA region (comprising 175 bp from the U5 region and 125 bp from the R region) in the 3′LTR acts as the promoter for anti-sense transcription [7]. Following the strategy outlined above to construct the 5′LTR-NanoLuc-IPOR(573) plasmid, a reporter vector was generated to test the effect of IPOR on the activity of the 3′LTR (Fig. 3C). In that plasmid, the complementary sequence of the IPOR was inserted as follows: 3′LTR-300 (promoter)-NanoLuc (reporter of the anti-sense transcription)-inverted IPOR(573) sequence. Interestingly, insertion of the inverted IPOR sequence enhanced the transcription from 3′LTR-300, which is the promoter for the HBZ gene. Thus, our findings suggest that IPOR possesses contrary effects on sense and antisense transcription from the 5′ and 3′LTR, respectively.

Transcription factors associated with the activities of IPOR

The five repetitive sequences within the IPOR were predicted to bind several transcription factors, raising the possibility that these transcription factors are implicated in the control of viral transcription via IPOR. We focused on TEAD-1, TEAD-3 and Eomes, which have high predicted scores, and carried out ChIP assay to evaluate their recruitment to the IPOR. As for the Eomes binding motifs, they seemed to exhibit relatively high variability, we also evaluated genomic regions serving as positive and negative controls for Eomes recruitment by ChIP assay [25], and compared the results. As shown in Supplemental Fig. 2A, accumulation of Eomes was confirmed at the IPOR as amplification level at the IPOR was higher than negative control. ChIP-qPCR confirmed bindings of TEAD-1, TEAD-3 and Eomes to the IPOR in HPB-ATL-2 cells, in which IPOR had been detected by ATAC-seq (Fig. 4A). Based on these results, we hypothesized that the repetitive motifs within IPOR and binding of TEAD-1, TEAD-3, or Eomes (or a combination of the three factors) to these motifs are important for the regulation of viral transcription. To evaluate the significance of the effects of IPOR, we generated a mutant which cannot be bound by either TEADs or Eomes. Since the TEAD-1/3 and Eomes binding sequences overlap (Fig. 2B and C, and 4B), the mutant sequence was designed to replace multiple nucleotides in regions important for binding to each protein while avoiding any effect on the amino acid sequence of integrase (Fig. 4B). It was experimentally confirmed that recruitment of each transcription factor to the mutant IPOR was reduced compared with wild type (Supplementary Fig. 2B). This wild-type IPOR fragment (IPOR(573)-WT) was replaced with the mutant IPOR fragment (IPOR(573)-Mut) in the reporter vectors to test the effects on sense- and antisense-transcription (Fig. 4C and E). As shown in Fig. 4D, the mutations present in IPOR-Mut partially but significantly impeded the suppressive effects on sense-strand transcription regardless of whether Tax is co-expressed or not. By contrast, the IPOR-Mut repressed the activating effects of IPOR-WT on the anti-sense transcription (Fig. 4F).

Repressive effects of IPOR on virion production and transcription from the integrated provirus

Since replication of HTLV-1 virions depends on the transcription of the plus strand, we hypothesized that the IPOR has a repressive effect on virion production. To verify our hypothesis, a mutant molecular clone in which IPOR was replaced with the IPOR-Mut, and its ability to generate virions was compared with that of WT (Fig. 5A). Although multiple silent mutations were introduced into the IPOR within the integrase coding region of mutant HTLV-1, no functional difference in integrase activity between the wild type and the mutant was expected. This was experimentally confirmed (Supplementary Fig. 2C). Viral production was quantified by the p19 ELISA method, and our results demonstrated that the mutant IPOR resulted in higher virus production level than WT, as expected (Fig. 5B). Next, we analyzed the role of the IPOR in viral transcription from integrated proviruses. A reporter cell line, JET cells, which express tdTomato under the control of five tandemly repeated Tax-responsive element [26, 27], was infected with either WT or Mut HTLV-1 virions (Fig. 5A), and tax expression in WT- or Mut-HTLV-1-infected JET cells was evaluated. Real-time qPCR results showed that tax expression in JET cells infected with IPOR-Mut HTLV-1 was significantly higher than in those infected with WT (Fig. 5C), indicating that the activity of the 5′LTR of IPOR-Mut is higher than that of WT even in the context of integrated provirus. Based on these results, we concluded that HTLV-1 has an inhibitory mechanism of viral replication, using the proviral IPOR sequence.

Tax burst and Eomes

Tax is not constantly expressed in vivo. Instead, Tax is transiently expressed in ATL cells and HTLV-1-infected cells [5]. This transient expression is thought to be due to the high immunogenicity of Tax protein, which is the dominant target of HTLV-1-specific CTLs in vivo [4]. However, Tax is essential for replication of HTLV-1. Therefore, HTLV-1 minimizes Tax expression. It is known that Tax is induced by activation of the p38-MAPK signaling which is triggered by cellular stresses such as hypoxia [28]. In contrast, inhibitory mechanisms of Tax expression remain unknown. Inhibition of plus-strand transcription by the IPOR might be a mechanism that limits Tax expression to transient bursts. A recent study showed that Tax-expressing cells can be traced by using a fluorescent protein named Timer [29], which changes its color after transient expression [30]. The fluorescent Timer protein is blue at the beginning of expression, but changes to red over time. Using the transcriptome data from this study, we analyzed the expression dynamics of several host genes after Tax expression (Fig. 6A). It is well known that Tax activates the NF-κB pathway. Indeed, the expression dynamics of NF-κB transcription factors (NFKB1, NFKB2, RELA, and RELB) and their target genes (BCL2 and IRF4) were consistent with transient expression of Tax (Fig. 6A). LCK was reported to be repressed by Tax [31], and its expression was transiently suppressed by Tax induction. Importantly, EOMES expression decreased temporally in parallel with the rapid increase in Tax and was significantly induced at the late stage (Fig. 6A). This result suggests that Eomes functions to suppress Tax expression at the late stage of Tax burst. We found that EOMES overexpression enhanced the suppressive effect of IPOR on transcription from the 5′LTR (Supplementary Fig. 3A), whereas EOMES knockdown reduced this effect (Supplementary Fig. 3B and 3 C). These findings are consistent with our hypothesis. TBX21, which is a member of the same T-box family, showed a similar trajectory. Transcription of the inhibitory receptors, PDCD1 and TIGIT, was also transiently down-regulated: since these receptors are known to be the targets of HBZ [32], their expression dynamics might be affected by HBZ expression. Regarding TEAD1 and TEAD3, their expression levels were very low at each time point and undetectable in several samples (Supplementary Fig. 4). Furthermore, the values varied considerably between replicates, making it difficult to identify a clear trend.

Finally, we investigated EOMES expression during normal-T-cell activation. Yukawa et al. have analyzed the gene expression dynamics in normal T cells after T-cell activation [33]. Human naive CD4+ T cells isolated from the blood of healthy donors were activated with anti-CD3/CD28 beads. We found that EOMES expression decreased with T-cell activation, in contrast to the expression of the NF-κB-related genes (Fig. 6B). The results also suggested that EOMES expression is lower in fresh ATL cells, which are thought to be in the resting state, than in normal naive T cells (Fig. 6C).

Discussion

Various retroviruses are known to possess DNA elements that regulate viral replication in their proviruses. The presence of an enhancer element in the gag gene of avian retroviruses has been reported [34]. Similarly, enhancer activity was identified in part of the pol gene of HIV-1 [35]. This region also enhances transcription from the 5′LTR [36]. Mutations of intragenic AP-1 sites influence infectivity of recombinant HIV-1 in monocyte-derived macrophages [37], indicating that this region is critical for HIV-1 infection. In contrast to these reports, this study shows that a similar region of the HTLV-1 provirus suppresses transcription from the 5′LTR, resulting in reduced viral replication.

Both HTLV-1 and HIV-1 mainly target CD4+ T cells in vivo. However, their viral replication and transcription are contrasting. Vigorous replication is a striking characteristic of HIV-1, which induces massive cell death of CD4+ T cells. By contrast, de novo infection is largely suppressed in the case of HTLV-1. Instead, this virus persists during the chronic infection in vivo mainly by proliferation of infected CD4+ T cells. The plus strand of the HTLV-1 provirus encodes Tax, a trans-activator of transcription of viral genes, which is essential for de novo infection. Since viral proteins (the gag, env and tax genes) encoded in the plus strand of the HTLV-1 provirus show higher immunogenicity, minimized transcription of these viral genes allows infected cells to survive under the host immunosurveillance. On the other hand, the immunogenicity of HBZ, which is encoded by the minus strand of the provirus, is very low compared to viral proteins encoded on the plus strand [38, 39]. Therefore, HBZ expression is much more frequent in each infected cell.

How dose HTLV-1 minimize transcription of the plus strand? Transcription of the plus strand depends on Tax. It has been reported that Tax is transiently expressed in a proportion of ATL cells, but is absent in most ATL cells [5], indicating that Tax-dependent transcription of the plus-strand viral genes is silenced for most of the time. However, it remained unknown what mechanisms suppress Tax-mediated viral gene transcription. We show here that HTLV-1 has a strategy to inhibit transcription of the plus-strand viral genes, suggesting that it facilitates the survival of infected cells under host immunosurveillance.

Interestingly, HTLV-1 utilizes EOMES for this silencer. Expression of the EOMES gene in T cells is suppressed after T-cell activation by anti-CD3 and anti-CD28 antibodies [33], in contrast to the upregulated NF-κB-related genes (RELA, RELB) and BCL2. We infer that suppressed EOMES expression releases silenced plus-strand transcription of viral genes after T-cell activation. From another perspective, Tax may also regulate the expression of EOMES. Given that Tax is well known to strongly activate NF-κB, the opposing expression patterns of EOMES and NF-κB-related genes shown in Fig. 6 suggest that Tax may indirectly modulate EOMES expression to regulate its own expression.

EOMES expression is profoundly suppressed in ATL cells compared with control T cells. Although Tax expression is disabled in approximately half of ATL cases, its expression appears to be critical in the remaining cases. It is speculated that decreased EOMES expression is a mechanism to facilitate Tax expression in response to the stimulation triggered by cellular stresses [5]. Regarding TEAD proteins, their expression patterns and functions in T cells have been largely unexplored. Therefore, this study focused on the role of Eomes. However, the function of TEADs in HTLV-1-infected cells remains an issue to be addressed in future studies.

In addition to the suppressive effect on the plus-strand transcription, the IPOR activates the minus-strand transcription from the 3′LTR. Mutations in the Eomes/TEADs binding sites weakened the effect, suggesting that these transcription factors are positive regulators of HBZ expression. A previous study reported that the expressions of Tax and HBZ vary reciprocally at the single-cell level [5]; however, the molecular mechanism underlying this phenomenon remains unclear. The IPOR sequence identified in this study may function to switch the activity between the 5′LTR and 3′LTR. Because the 3′LTR construct used in luciferase assays (3′LTR-300) contains a reversed U5-R region, it may influence the interpretation of the reporter data [7]. This possibility should be examined in greater detail in future research. Tax promotes de novo infection through both viral replication and cell proliferation, while HBZ promotes the proliferation of infected cells [40]. Therefore, IPOR may play a part in switching the mode of viral propagation. Although monoclonal proliferation of leukemic cells is the underlying cause of ATL, polyclonal infected cells are present in HAM/TSP, and per-cell Tax expression in HAM/TSP is higher than ATL [41, 42]. It is possible that IPOR and the associated transcription factors influence the pathogenesis of HTLV-1-associated diseases by regulating the balance between de novo infection and clonal expansion. Further detailed analysis is needed to elucidate the significance of IPOR activity and the transcription factors recruited in different pathological states.

Recently, another group has reported that the central open chromatin region of HTLV-1 provirus has a repressive effect on transcription from the 5′LTR [43]. They used a different approach from ours in searching for binding motifs and identified RUNX1 as the transcription factor involved. It is noteworthy that the RUNX1 binding site showed little suppressive effect to the 3′LTR promoter, whereas the IPOR sequence mentioned in our paper rather promotes transcription from the 3′LTR. This open chromatin region contains various transcription factor binding sites, each of which is suspected to have diverse functions, and it is possible that the binding of multiple factors may result in complex effects. In addition, it has been reported that HTLV-1 provirus actually has a CTCF-binding region and its insulator activity functions as a border for epigenetic changes [10]. This binding site located in the pX region causes differences in transcriptional control between the plus and minus strand. Further investigation is needed to determine how transcriptional control of IPOR is affected by the CTCF-binding region.

The present study shows that the IPOR regulates the expression of the HTLV-1 provirus; it suppresses the transcription of the plus strand and enhances that of the minus strand. It is conceivable that the observed decrease in Eomes expression in HTLV-1-infected cells following the Tax burst suppresses this effect. After the transient expression of Tax, during the maintenance of infection, the repressive effect on transcription of the plus strand may be beneficial in that it allows HTLV-1 to escape host immunity and remain latent. On the other hand, the IPOR is likely to activate the HBZ promoter, resulting in growth promotion of infected cells. This regulatory mechanism, which exploits host-cell molecules, reveals part of HTLV-1’s ingenious survival strategy.

Methods

Cell culture

HEK293T cells (RRID: CVCL_0063) and HEK293 cells (RRID: CVCL_0045) were maintained in DMEM (Nacalai Tesque) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Jurkat (RRID: CVCL_0065), JET cells [26], and HPB-ATL-2 cells (RRID: CVCL_VN78) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Nacalai Tesque) containing 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin.

Generation of reporter constructs

The pNL[Nluc/CMV] vector was purchased from Promega. The CMV promoter was substituted with the HTLV-1 5′LTR promoter via KpnI and XhoI restriction sites. The control sequence and IPOR were PCR-amplified from pX1MT-M [44], a plasmid containing the full-length HTLV-1 proviral genome. The region spanning nucleotides 1069 to 1641 of the HTLV-1 genome (GenBank: AB513134) was selected as the control sequence, and we confirmed the binding motifs present in IPOR were not detected by MEME (RRID: SCR_001783) [45]. The mutant variant of IPOR was synthesized by GenScript Corp. Each construct was assembled into the pNL backbone using the GeneArt Gibson Assembly HiFi Cloning Kit (Invitrogen). The control sequence or inverted IPORs, along with the Nluc reporter, were inserted into the pGL4-3′LTR300 vector [7] using the same assembly method. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Nano luciferase assay

The control vector for the luciferase assay, pGL4.10[luc2], was purchased from Promega. The Tax expression vector was constructed as previously described [7]. The Eomes expression vector pRP-CAG-EOMES was purchased from VectorBuilder. Jurkat cells were transfected with the indicated plasmids and pGL4.10[luc2] as a control, with or without Tax, using the TransIT-Jurkat Transfection Reagent (Mirus). Following a 24-hour incubation, cells were harvested and analyzed using the Nano-Glo Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Luciferase activity was quantified with a TriStar LB941 microplate reader (Berthold Technologies, Germany). Results are shown as the relative luciferase activity. All experiments were conducted in technical triplicates.

Knockdown of EOMES

Small hairpin RNA (shRNA)-expression lentiviral vectors, pLV-U6[shRNA-EOMES], were constructed (VectorBuilder). Target sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 1. After 48-hour incubation, luciferase activity was measured as described above.

ATAC-seq analysis

ATAC-seq data were retrieved from the DNA Data Bank of Japan (DDBJ) under accession number PRJDB18166 [18, 19]. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from patients with ATL and HAM were thawed and washed with PBS containing 0.1% BSA. To exclude non-viable cells, the LIVE/DEAD Fixable Dead Cell Stain Kit (Invitrogen) was employed. For surface marker staining, cells were incubated for 30 min at 4 °C with APC anti-human CD4 antibody (clone: RPA-T4; BioLegend, RRID: AB_2562051) and anti-SynCAM (TSLC1/CADM1) mAb-FITC (MBL, RRID: AB_3472018), followed by washing with PBS. HTLV-1-infected cells (CADM1+ CD4+) were sorted using a FACS Aria (Beckman Coulter), achieving a purity of 98–99%. Flow cytometry data were analyzed with FlowJo software (Treestar). Immediately post-sorting, 10,000–50,000 HTLV-1-infected cells were centrifuged and subjected to ATAC-seq. Total RNA was extracted from the remaining cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) and processed for RNA-seq. Library preparation and high-throughput sequencing were conducted at Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, Korea). Read trimming and quality filtering were performed using fastp (RRID: SCR_016962, v0.20.0). Reads were aligned to the HTLV-1 reference genome (GenBank: AB513134) using Bowtie2 (RRID: SCR_016368, v2.4.1) with the “--no-mixed” and “--no-discordant” options. SAMtools (RRID: SCR_002105, v1.10) was utilized to convert SAM files to compressed BAM format, sort them by chromosome coordinates, and isolate properly paired reads. Epigenomic data were visualized using IGV software (RRID: SCR_011793, v2.19.4) [46].

Search for DNA-binding motifs

DNA-binding motifs within IPOR sequence were identified using MEME (RRID: SCR_001783) [45] part of The MEME Suite [21]. The most enriched motif was compared against databases of known transcription-factor-binding motifs, and candidate matches were ranked by p-value using Tomtom (RRID: SCR_024809) [47] from The MEME Suite.

ChIP qPCR

ChIP assays were performed using the SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Magnetic Beads; Cell Signaling Technology) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cross-linked chromatin from 4 × 10⁶ cells was immunoprecipitated overnight with normal Rabbit IgG and antibodies targeting Eomes (RRID: AB_778267), TEAD-3 (RRID: AB_3472019), and TEAD-1 (RRID: AB_2884896). Immunocomplexes were conjugated with ChIP-Grade Protein G Magnetic Beads (Cell Signaling Technology) for 2 h at 4 °C. Following four washes, DNA was eluted from the beads by gentle vortexing (1,200 rpm) for 30 min at 65 °C. Purified DNA was quantified by ChIP-qPCR relative to a 2% input control. HPB-ATL-2 and HEK293T cells were used for ChIP-qPCR. The protein expression of Eomes, TEAD-1, and TEAD-3 in these cell lines was confirmed by Western blotting (Supplementary Fig. 5). Positive and negative control regions for Eomes binding have been described previously [25]. Primer sequences used for ChIP-qPCR are provided in Supplementary Table 1. All ChIP-qPCR assays were performed in triplicate as technical replicates, and multiple independent experiments were conducted to ensure reproducibility. The figures present representative results.

ELISA

HEK293T cells (3 × 10⁵ cells/well, n = 3 wells) were transfected with either HTLV-1-WT or Mut molecular clones using the TransIT-LT1 Transfection Reagent (Mirus). After 48 h, p19 levels in the virus-containing supernatants were quantified using the RETRO-TEK HTLV p19 Antigen ELISA Kit (ZeptoMetrix). All experiments were conducted in technical triplicates.

Quantitative analysis of tax expression in JET cells

Total RNA was extracted from JET cells infected with WT or Mut HTLV-1 using the ReliaPrep RNA Cell Miniprep System (Promega). cDNA was synthesized using SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen). Expression values of tax and 18 S rRNA (as an internal control) were measured using THUNDERBIRD Probe qPCR Mix (TOYOBO) on an Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primer and probe sequences for tax are listed in Supplementary Table 1. 18 S rRNA was quantified using 18 S RNA (Hs99999901_s1) TaqMan Gene Expression Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). PCR amplification was performed under the following confition: 94℃ for 20 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ for 3 s and 60℃ for 30 s. Proviral load (PVL) was quantified by real-time PCR as described previously [48]. Genomic DNA of JET cells infected with WT or Mut HTLV-1 was extracted using DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN) and subjected to quantification of PVL.

RNA-seq analysis

Gene expression trajectories were inferred from RNA-sequencing data available in NCBI’s Gene Expression Omnibus under accession number GSE197110 [30]. To separate HTLV-1-infected T cells into different phases of the tax transcriptional burst, the infected T cells were transduced with a Tax-responsive Timer Protein reporter construct. The time-dependent change in the wavelength of fluorescent emission from the Timer Protein enabled the separation of T cells into four successive phases of Tax expression: silent; early burst (blue); mid-burst (blue-red double positive); and late burst (red). Cells in each respective phase were isolated by flow-sorting and processed for RNA-seq analysis. Full details of the construct and the protocol are given in [30].

Gene expression during T-cell activation was assessed using data from the public repository GSE116697 [33]. EOMES expression in healthy donors and ATL patients was quantified from GSE137143 [49] and PRJDB18259 [18, 19], respectively. For the latter, total RNA was extracted from HTLV-1-infected cells derived from ATL patients, as previously described. Library preparation and high-throughput sequencing were conducted by Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, Korea). Low-quality reads were trimmed and filtered using Trimmomatic (RRID: SCR_011848, v0.39). Read alignment to the human reference genome GRCh38 (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/all/GCF/000/001/405/GCF_000001405.26_GRCh38/) was performed using STAR (RRID: SCR_004463, v2.7.11b). RSEM (RRID: SCR_000262, v1.3.3) was employed to convert SAM files to BAM format, sort the alignments, and extract properly paired reads. Missing values in the dataset were imputed via regression analysis.

Comparison of integrase function between wild-type and mutant HTLV-1

Retroviral DNA circles are generated as by-products during integration into the host genome; therefore, the quantities of circularized viral DNA and integrated proviral DNA reflect the integrase function of retroviruses at the initial stage of infection [50]. We measured integrated and circularized viral DNA by modifying a previously established method for quantitative analysis of HIV [51]. Briefly, HEK293T cells were transfected with a WT- or Mut- HTLV-1 molecular clone. After 48 h, total DNA was extracted, and integrated HTLV-1 proviral DNA, 1 L DNA, 2 L DNA, and plasmid DNA were quantified by real-time qPCR. Relative values were calculated by normalizing to the amount of transfected plasmid. Primer sequences used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in R (v4.5.0) [52] and RStudio (v2024.12.1 + 563) [53].
