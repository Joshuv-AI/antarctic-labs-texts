Characteristics and management of patients attending primary asthma clinics in primary health care in the Kingdom of Bahrain: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Fatema Ahmed Al Jerdabi, Mahmood Alawainati, Abeer AbdulRahman AlSaweer, Fadhel Abbas Arafat, Mohamed Alhalwaji, Eman Sayed Mahmood Sharaf, Lamees Al Aradi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Asthma remains poorly controlled worldwide despite clinical guidelines, continuing to cause high morbidity and mortality. This study examines the clinical characteristics, management practices, and factors affecting asthma control in patients attending asthma clinics within primary healthcare centers in the Kingdom of Bahrain, providing insights to enhance chronic disease management in frontline care.Materials and Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted across 27 primary healthcare centers in Bahrain from June 2022 to June 2023. Participants aged 12 years and older diagnosed with bronchial asthma were included. The Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) tool was used to evaluate asthma control. Data including sociodemographic information and asthma management characteristics were collected. Descriptive and inferential analyses were performed.Results:Among 198 participants (mean age 48.4), most were Bahraini (82.3%), female (65.7%), and nonsmokers (94.4%) with an average BMI of 30.58 kg/m². Gastroesophageal reflux (23.2%) and diabetes (20.2%) were the most prevalent comorbidities. Based on the GINA classification, 49.5% had well-controlled asthma. The treatment adherence rate was 75.8%. Uncontrolled asthma was linked to nighttime symptoms (P = 0.001), diabetes mellitus (P = 0.028), higher SABA use per month (P = 0.011) and year (P < 0.001), low asthma control test scores (P < 0.001), and treatment steps 3-5 (P < 0.001). By contrast, better asthma control was associated with medication adherence (P = 0.025) and influenza (P = 0.014) and pneumococcal (P = 0.019) vaccination.Conclusion:Asthma control in Bahrain’s primary healthcare centers is suboptimal, especially among patients with nighttime symptoms, diabetes, frequent SABA use, and low control test scores. Enhancing patient education, optimizing treatment strategies, and addressing comorbidities are critical for improving asthma outcomes.

Introduction

Asthma is a chronic, noncommunicable inflammatory disease marked by recurring episodes of shortness of breath (dyspnea), wheezing, coughing, and chest tightness.[1] It ranks among the most frequently encountered conditions in primary care. Globally, asthma affects an estimated 300 million people, with prevalence rates varying by region and country.[1] According to the CDC’s 2024 report, based on data from the 2022 National Health Interview Survey, approximately 26.8 million individuals in the United States have asthma—accounting for 8.2% of the population.[2] This includes 4.5 million children (6.2%) and 22.3 million adults (8.7%).[2] In Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) Countries, the prevalence of asthma ranges from 4.7% to 32.0%.[3] Among children aged 6–7 years in Bahrain, asthma was noted in 11.3% of them, which is higher compared with the mean prevalence of asthma in other GCC countries (7.6%).[45]

Asthma is considered a significant cause of morbidity and mortality. In 2019, it led to nearly 500,000 deaths and ranked 16th among the leading causes of years lived with disability worldwide, resulting in over 15 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) per year.[6] Asthma has detrimental effects on patients, caregivers, and the community by contributing to absenteeism from work and educational facilities, impairing quality of life, and leading to higher rates of hospitalization.[7] It is projected that by 2040, the direct costs of uncontrolled asthma will exceed $300.6 billion, while the total direct and indirect costs will surpass $963.5 billion in the United States.[8] In GCC countries, the average annual medical costs for an asthmatic patient exceed thousands of dollars. Therefore, prompt and accurate diagnosis and management of asthma are critical to minimizing its costs, morbidity, and mortality.[910]

Several guidelines have been developed to ensure adequate management of asthma worldwide, with the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) being the most widely used.[1111213] According to GINA guidelines, the main goals of asthma management include controlling patients’ symptoms, reducing the risks associated with uncontrolled asthma, and improving the quality of life for affected patients.[1] Hence, asthma management includes nonpharmacological interventions such as reducing triggers, addressing risk factors, educating patients, and providing asthma self-management plans, along with pharmacological treatments, with ICS-containing treatments being the cornerstone of asthma management.[1]

In 2019, GINA updated its guidelines, advising against the use of short-acting beta-agonists (SABAs) as a standalone treatment.[13] Instead, it recommended inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) for all asthmatic patients aged 6 years and older[1] as the overuse of SABAs (using three or more canisters per year) is linked to increased risks of asthma exacerbation, hospitalizations, and mortality, while regular ICS use has been shown to reduce symptoms and complications.[1141516171819] In a Kenyan cohort study, 71.9% of patients were prescribed SABAs beyond recommended levels, with 15.3% using SABAs alone.[18] In Europe, the SABINA studies found significant SABA overuse across several countries, with notable rates in the United Kingdom (38%) and Spain (30%). SABA overuse is prevalent among all asthmatic patients.[19]

Despite existing guidelines, up to 60% of asthma patients continue to experience inadequate control of their condition.[20] Studies across Europe, Latin America, and Asia-Pacific indicate poor asthma control, with uncontrolled asthma rates ranging from 56.5% in Europe to over 90% in many Asian and Latin American countries.[212223] The asthma control rate was also assessed in the Middle East and North Africa. In the overall population, 29.4% were controlled, 29.1% had partially controlled asthma, and 41.5% had uncontrolled asthma. The rates of controlled asthma were lower in Jordan (14.8%) and Iraq (17.5%), while higher rates were observed in Kuwait (42.6%), Qatar (41.1%), and Tunisia (40.2%).[24] In addition, only 42.3% of general practitioner document asthma control parameters in patients’ medical records.[25] Key factors contributing to poor control include noncompliance with treatment, ineffective inhaler techniques, and concerns about the safety and effectiveness of inhaled corticosteroids (ICS).[262728] In Oman, a study found that 57.8% of patients had poorly managed asthma, primarily due to inadequate inhaler technique (18.3%) and poor medication adherence (40.8%).[28]

Although many patients with asthma are managed by primary care providers, published data on the characteristics and outcomes of asthma patients in this setting in GCC countries are scarce.[29] This research aimed to assess the clinical characteristics, management practices and determinants of asthma control in patients attending asthma clinics in primary healthcare centers in Bahrain.

The significance of this research lies in its ability to identify key factors influencing asthma control in primary care settings, which is crucial for designing targeted interventions that enhance patient outcomes and alleviate the strain on health care systems.

Materials and Method

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted on all patients who attended asthma clinics in the 27 primary healthcare centers in Bahrain from June 2022 to June 2023. Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board via a letter dated 22 June 2023. Asthma patients aged 12 years and above who attended the asthma clinics during the study period were included, while patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease were excluded.

Definitions

Bronchial asthma was defined as a physician-diagnosed condition characterized by recurrent episodes of wheezing, cough, chest tightness, and dyspnea. The diagnosis was confirmed by demonstrating reversibility using a peak expiratory flow meter (PEF), along with a positive response to inhaled bronchodilators and/or inhaled corticosteroids (ICS), or oral prednisolone.

Asthma control was assessed using the GINA tool and the Asthma Control Test (ACT). The GINA tool evaluated daytime symptoms, nighttime symptoms, use of relievers, and limitations of activities over the past 4 weeks. Based on the responses, asthma was classified into three categories: uncontrolled, partly controlled, and controlled.[1] The ACT tool assesses frequency of shortness of breath, general asthma symptoms, and use of rescue medications, the effect of asthma on daily functioning, and overall self-assessment of asthma control. Each item was scored using a Likert scale ranging from 1 (worst) to 5 (best). ACT scores of ≥20 indicated controlled asthma, scores of 16-19 indicated partly controlled asthma, and scores of <16 indicated uncontrolled asthma.[1] SABA overuse was defined as using one or more SABA canisters (200 activations) per month or three or more canisters per year.[1]

Data collection process

Data were retrieved from electronic medical records and logged into a Microsoft Excel sheet by three family consultants. The data collection sheet consisted of three parts: sociodemographic and baseline characteristics, asthma characteristics, and asthma management data. The data were then transferred and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS). A pilot study was conducted on 30 samples to identify potential challenges in the data collection process, and the data collection sheet was modified accordingly.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26. Descriptive analysis was performed using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations for continuous variables. Inferential analysis was then conducted to determine statistical significance using Chi-square tests, Fisher exact test, and T tests. In addition, logistic regression analysis was performed, generating odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. For all analyses, a P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline and sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 198 participants were recruited for this study, with an average age of 48.4 years. Most patients were Bahraini (n = 163, 82.3%), female (n = 130, 65.7%), and had never smoked (n = 187, 94.4%). The mean BMI of the patients was 30.58 kg/m². Regarding comorbidities, gastroesophageal reflux disease (n = 46, 23.2%), diabetes mellitus (n = 40, 20.2%), and essential hypertension (n = 23, 11.6%) were the most prevalent comorbid conditions [Table 1].

Asthma severity and level of asthma control

Approximately one in every four patients reported more than two daytime symptoms per week and limitations in activities (n = 52, 26.3%), while more than one-third reported nighttime symptoms (n = 71, 35.9%). The level of asthma control was assessed using the ACT and the GINA control classification. Out of 198 asthma patients, the ACT was documented for 175 (n = 88.4%), showing that more than half had well-controlled asthma (n = 102, 58.3%), while one quarter had poorly controlled asthma (n = 41, 23.4%). Most patients reported no asthma exacerbations in the last year (n = 154, 77.8%) [Table 2].

Patients with diabetes had a higher prevalence of partly controlled/uncontrolled asthma compared to their counterparts (P = 0.028). Adherence to medications (P = 0.025) and vaccination against influenza (P = 0.014) and pneumococcal (P = 0.019) vaccines were linked to higher asthma control rates. The analysis also revealed a significant association between asthma control status and treatment steps (P < 0.001), as patients on steps 3, 4, and 5 treatments were more likely to have suboptimal asthma control. Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics and factors influencing asthma control among patients in Bahrain. The results showed that most participants were middle-aged women, with common comorbidities including diabetes, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and obesity, consistent with findings from the Ghanname I study.[27]

Regarding the asthma control rates, this study found that about half of the patients had well-managed asthma which is higher than the reported rates in Qatar and Kuwait (41-43%).[24] This could be attributed to the high adherence rates noted among the participants in this study compared with other studies.[2526] By contrast, higher rates of uncontrolled asthma were reported by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (60%), Oman (57.8%), and a cohort from Kenya (78.1%).[182028]

In line with Larsson et al.[29]’s report, this study found that most participants had mild to moderate asthma (32.9% and 52.0%, respectively). In addition, a significant majority of asthma patients in this study were on ICS/LABA (77.2%) and only 2% were on SABA only, reflecting effective prescribing practices compared with other studies which showed poorer practices.[1830] The prevalence of SABA overuse in this study was 17.2%, higher than Italy’s rates, comparable with Germany’s data, and lower than Sweden, the United Kingdom, and Kenya’s rates.[1819] Instead of using prescription refill data, SABA usage was evaluated based on physicians’ records of how many SABA Canisters patients actually used each month and year which may account for the lower prevalence compared with other studies.

Previous studies assessed determinants of asthma control including patient demographics, assessment tools, medication accessibility, patient education, and exposure to risk factors.[28313233] In this study, nighttime symptoms, SABA usage, and ACT scores were strong predictors of uncontrolled asthma, highlighting the importance of monitoring these indicators in primary clinical settings. Consistent with many studies, this study found that SABA overuse was linked to a higher risk of uncontrolled asthma.[1415] The literature showed that ICS use is associated with symptom reduction and fewer complications.[1617] The high prevalence of ICS prescription in this study could also explain the low rate of exacerbation and overall asthma control.

Consistent with numerous previous studies, our findings revealed significant associations between asthma control and factors such as treatment step, adherence to therapy, vaccination status, and the presence of diabetes.[343536] Notably, however, body weight, smoking, and inhaler technique did not seem to affect asthma control in our study—an observation that differs from results reported in another research.[343738]

This study has several strengths. It included all patients attending asthma clinics in Bahrain’s primary care centers and evaluated various features and outcomes, supported by inferential analyses. However, there are also some limitations. Data collection relied on physician assessments and management documentation in patient’s electronic medical records, which may have been affected by incomplete documentation. Furthermore, although the study included all patients attending asthma clinics in primary care centers, the sample size remains relatively small. The diagnosis of asthma was physician-based, which could introduce a risk of bias.

Conclusion

In summary, asthma control in Bahrain’s primary healthcare centers remains suboptimal, particularly among patients with nighttime symptoms, frequent SABA use, diabetes, and low control test scores. Despite good medication adherence, many patients continue to experience symptoms and activity limitations. Improving outcomes and quality of life may require targeted symptoms control, effective management of comorbid conditions, reduced reliance on SABA, vaccination promotion, and personalized care.
