Formation of tertiary lymphoid structures drives Parkin antitumor immunity
Authors: Michela Perego, Andrew V. Kossenkov, Jagadish C. Ghosh, Chiara Camisaschi, Nicholas J. Tursi, David B. Weiner, Dario C. Altieri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitochondria activate immunity, but the role in cancer is unknown. Here, we report that transgenic expression of Parkin, a regulator of mitochondrial fitness, induces inflammation and interferon (IFN) gene signatures, suppresses prostate cancer formation and generates CD8+ and CD20+ intraprostatic immune aggregates. These had hallmarks of mature tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS), expressing markers of B cell maturation (CXCL13, CCL21), germinal center formation (BCL6, GL7), mature dendritic cells (MHC-II/CD208) and high endothelial venules (LYVE-1). Parkin TLS showed high Ig gene expression, recruitment of CXCR5+ follicular T helper cells and expansion of CD69+/KLRG1+ effector T cells. Conditioned medium from Parkin-positive cells expanded memory B and plasma cells, increased IgG1 production, and sustained B and T cell migration. Finally, conditional expression of Parkin induced TLS formation, upregulated Ig chains and inhibited prostate cancer growth, in vivo, whereas Parkin reconstitution in IFNAR1, CD8 or CD20 knockout mice had no effect. Therefore, mitochondrial immunity orchestrates antitumor responses, and TLS formation contributes to tumor suppression.

INTRODUCTION

In addition to bioenergetics, mitochondria are important signaling hubs for inflammatory signaling (1) and innate and adaptive immunity (2). This pathway protects against pathogens, mostly viruses (3), but a role in other diseases, such as cancer, has remained elusive.

One molecule at the interface between mitochondria and tumor immunity is Parkin, a regulator of organelle quality control via mitophagy (4). Lost in human cancer by multiple mechanisms (5), including gene methylation (6), reintroduction of Parkin in transformed cells inhibits intrinsic tumor traits of cell motility and invasion (7), mitosis (8) and metabolism (5, 9), suppressing tumor growth in genetic (10) and syngeneic (11) models, in vivo. However, this may not be the only mechanism for inhibition of tumor growth (12, 13), as Parkin expression induces transcriptional activation of inflammation and interferon (IFN) responses, intratumoral accumulation of CD8+ effector T cells (6), and improved tumor antigen presentation (14). How such reprogramming of antitumor immunity is orchestrated in the microenvironment is unknown, and the role of Parkin in this process is controversial, recently linked to paradoxical tumor-promoting immunosuppression (15).

A potential mechanism of mitochondria/Parkin antitumor immunity may include the formation of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) (16, 17). These are inflammation- and IFN-driven (18, 19) intratumoral aggregates of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells that surround a germinal center of proliferating B cells, follicular dendritic cells and stromal components, including high-endothelial venules (20). Such ‘mature’ TLS orchestrate humoral and cellular antitumor immunity (21), promoting B cell expansion, Ig production and infiltration of T cells with effector and T helper 1 (Th1) properties (16, 17). In the clinic, TLS formation correlates with improved response to therapeutic immune checkpoint inhibitors (22) and extended patient survival (23, 24).

To elucidate how a mitochondria/Parkin axis regulates antitumor immunity and resolve recent controversies (15), to our knowledge, we have now generated the first transgenic mouse model with tissue-specific expression of Parkin in the prostate.

RESULTS

Transgenic expression of Parkin suppresses prostate cancer formation, in vivo

We generated transgenic mice with tissue-specific expression of Parkin in the prostatic epithelium under the control of the Probasin (Pb) promoter (fig. S1A). Established colonies of male Parkin transgenic mice (PbPRKN) on a C57BL/6 J background were born viable, fertile and showed no histologic changes in the prostatic gland, compared to wild type (WT) prostate (fig. S1B). In addition, no obvious tissue or systemic abnormalities were observed in PbPRKN transgenic mice throughout an 18-month observation period.

By RNA-Seq, prostate tissues of PbPRKN mice exhibited extensive changes in gene expression, compared to WT mice (fig. S1C). Reinforcing a role in tumor suppression (12, 13), this involved upregulation of p53 and Rb pathways, transcriptional networks of immune regulation (NFAT, GATA1, LEF1, SYK, KLF4, BCL6) as well as inflammatory mediators (SASH1, NFκB) (Fig. 1A). Consistent with Parkin stimulation of IFN gene expression in antitumor immunity (6), prostate tissues of PbPRKN mice showed increased expression of IFNs, their cognate receptors (IFNAR) and IFN-regulated factor(s) (IRF1) (Fig. 1A). Conversely, PbPRKN transgenic mice showed decreased expression of multiple growth factor signaling pathways driving prostatic tumorigenesis (IGF1, AKT, ERK, EGR2, FGFR1) (Fig. 1A).

Next, we crossed PbPRKN transgenic mice with Transgenic Adenocarcinoma of the Mouse Prostate (TRAMP) mice (25). In this model, prostate-specific expression of the SV40 large T antigen oncogene under the Pb promoter drives stepwise prostate cancer formation, with progression from severe atypical hyperplasia (16–20 weeks) to localized prostate cancer (24–30 weeks) to full blown prostate cancer with metastatic dissemination (>40 weeks) (26).

In our cohort, prostate tissues of TRAMP mice harvested at 40 weeks of age exhibited a highly proliferative transcriptome by RNA-Seq (fig. S1D) and strong reactivity for the SV40 oncogene, by immunohistochemistry (IHC) (fig. S1E). Conversely, endogenous Parkin was undetectable in prostate tissues of TRAMP mice by 40 weeks of age (fig. S1E), consistent with the loss of Parkin during prostatic tumorigenesis, in vivo (5).

Under these conditions, double transgenic TRAMP-PbPRKN mice were born viable, fertile and showed no obvious organ or systemic phenotype. By 40 weeks of age, all TRAMP mice examined (n = 12) exhibited advanced prostate cancer, with large increases in prostate weight (Fig. 1B), poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma as well as neuroendocrine histology, and metastatic dissemination to the liver, by IHC (Fig. 1C). In contrast, none of the TRAMP-PbPRKN mice (n = 12) showed changes in prostate weight by the same age (Fig. 1B). Similarly, no changes in prostate weight were observed in PbPRKN mice at 40 weeks of age (n = 5) (Fig. 1B).

Prostate tissues of TRAMP-PbPRKN mice (40 weeks of age) contained CD8+ and CD20+ immune aggregates, by IHC, whereas TRAMP mice of comparable age did not contain intra-prostatic immune aggregates (Fig. 1D). Accordingly, prostate tissues of TRAMP-PbPRKN mice contained 2.6 ± 0.5 CD8+ aggregates with 28 ± 10.5 CD8+ cells/mm2 (n = 11) vs. 0.28 ± 0.14 aggregates and 7.5 ± 4.2 CD8+ cells/mm2 (n = 7) in TRAMP mice (Fig. 1E). Similarly, transgenic expression of Parkin generated 9.1 ± 1 CD20+ aggregates with 30.2 ± 3.3 CD20+ cells/mm2 (n = 11) vs. 1 ± 0.5 CD20+ aggregates and 0.4 ± 0.1 CD20+ cells/mm2 in TRAMP mice (n = 5) (Fig. 1F). Parkin CD20+ and CD8+ immune aggregates were detected at 36 weeks of age and persisted throughout 42 weeks (Fig. 1G).

Immune profiling of Parkin TLS

Next, we asked if immune aggregates formed in Parkin transgenic mice represented tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) that mediate antitumor immunity (21). By multiplex tissue immunofluorescence labeling, immune aggregates in TRAMP-PbPRKN prostates reacted with T (CD8) and B (CD20) cell markers, expressed germinal center-associated GL7 and BCL6 and contained CD4+ T cells (Fig. 2A and fig. S2). In addition these immune aggregates expressed markers of follicular and mature dendritic cells (CD21/MHC-II/CD208) as well as high endothelial venules (LYVE-1) (Fig. 2A and fig. S2), all hallmarks of mature TLS (20). TRAMP-PbPRKN immune aggregates also stained intensely positive for CXCL13, and reacted, albeit less diffusely, with CCL21, by IHC (Fig. 2B), chemokines implicated in T and B cell recruitment, lymphotoxin signaling, and germinal center formation in mature TLS (19, 27, 28). In contrast, prostate tissues of TRAMP mice expressed negligible levels of CXCL13 and CCL21 (Fig. 2B).

Based on these results, we next carried out spatial transcriptomic analysis of Parkin immune aggregates using the GeoMx platform. This approach allows for guided selection of multiple regions of interest (ROIs) based on expression of DNA, CD8 and Pan-CK fluorescence markers, as well as overlay of CD8+ staining by IHC. Compared to ROIs with high PanCK expression, i.e. ROI #9 (Fig. 2C), 5 ROIs in this analysis exhibited a unique morphology, i.e. ROI #3, characterized by DNA-dense, Pan-CK−, CD8+ regions referred to as “Brisk” (Fig. 2C). Comparing the gene expression profile of Brisk ROIs and 13 non-Brisk ROIs segmented on the CD8+ signal, 830 genes were identified as differentially expressed in Brisk ROIs by at least 2-fold (FDR <5%) (fig. S3A).

By gene expression analysis, the transcriptional signature of Brisk ROIs included key mediators of B cell migration to TLS (CXCL13, see also Fig. 2B), antigen presentation (H2-Ob, H2-Dmb2, H2-Q9, H2-Aa, H2-Eb1, Zeb2) and complement signaling (C3, C4b), which enable TLS antitumor activity (29) (Fig. 2D, fig. S3A). The expression of Ig chains for IgM (Ighm, ↑129-fold), IgA (Igha, ↑6-fold), IgG (Ighg, ↑5.2-fold; Igkg, ↑222-fold) and B cell receptor (BCR, Iglc, ↑5.4-fold) was also highly represented in Brisk ROIs, compared to non-Brisk regions (fig. S3B). Finally, key regulators of inflammation (TNF, TLR4), IFN signaling (IFNG) and cytokine/chemokine activity (IL1B, IL2, IL6, IL15, CXCL8) were prominently enriched in the Brisk gene expression dataset (Fig. 2E). These also included High Mobility Group Box 1 (HBGB1), a cytokine-like Damage-Associated Molecular Pattern (DAMP) that is released extracellularly and is required for Parkin antitumor immunity (30) (Fig. 2E). In line with these results, Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) identified multiple gene networks differentially upregulated in Brisk ROIs compared to non-Brisk regions, including cytokine and BCR signaling, Th1 and Th2 pathways, and inflammation (fig. S3C), associated with mature TLS functions, in vivo (16, 17).

B cell characterization of Parkin TLS

We next used the gene expression profile of selected ROIs to de-convolute the immune cell repertoire in Parkin TLS by CIBERSORT. Compared to non-Brisk regions, Brisk ROIs exhibited significant (p = 7x10−6) enrichment of B cells (Fig. 3A), in particular memory B cells and plasma cells, whereas a trend for reduction in naïve B cells did not reach statistical significance (fig. S3D). Consistent with the results above, the top genes (LM22 matrix) upregulated in Brisk regions included key regulators of B cell differentiation and function (Mzb1, CD37, CD22, Fcgr2b, Ms4a1, Blk, Igkc, Ighm), BCR signaling (CD79a, CD79b, Bank1, CD72), antigen presentation and plasmacytoid dendritic cell development (H2-Ob, Sbip) (fig. S4A).

To experimentally test these predictions, we next profiled prostate tissues of TRAMP-PbPRKN mice by multiparametric flow cytometry. In these experiments, transgenic expression of Parkin decreased the percentage of naïve B cells, while expanding the fraction of memory B cells and plasma cells (Fig. 3B). In line with the expression of CXCL13 in Parkin TLS (fig. S2B), prostate-associated B cells in TRAMP-PbPRKN mice contained high levels of its cognate receptor, CXCR5 (Fig. 3C, left) (31), providing a key receptor-ligand pair for enhanced B cell migration and maturation, germinal center formation, and B-T cell interactions in TLS formation, in vivo (31, 32). In addition, transgenic expression of Parkin increased the population of CD4+/CXCR5high/PD-1high follicular T helper cells (Fig. 3D, left, fig. S4B), another key cellular component of mature TLS (27, 33). Finally, prostate tissues of TRAMP-PbPRKN transgenic mice were enriched in KLRG1+/CD69+ resident effector T cells (Fig. 3E, top, fig. S4C), previously identified as a component of the Parkin-expressing tumor microenvironment (6).

Next, we asked whether these changes were specific to the prostate microenvironment of Parkin transgenic mice. Consistent with this, splenocytes isolated from TRAMP, PbPRKN or TRAMP-PbPRKN transgenic mice did not exhibit modulation of naïve B cells, memory B cells or plasmablasts (Fig. 3B). The subsets of CXCR5+ B cells (Fig. 3C, right), CD4+/CXCR5+/PD-1+ follicular T helper cells (Fig. 3D, right, fig. S4B) or CD19−/CD3+/CD8+ effector T cells (Fig. 3E, bottom, fig. S4C) were also unchanged in the splenocytes of the various mouse groups.

To test the specificity of Parkin-induced B cell reprogramming, we next co-cultured B cells isolated from spleens of WT C57BL/6 J mice with conditioned medium (CM) of MPten1 murine prostate cancer cells expressing Parkin under the control of a tetracycline-regulated promoter (Tet-ON system) induced by doxycycline (Doxy) (6). Previous studies have shown that conditional expression of Parkin in prostate cancer cells induces the release of an immune-inflammatory secretome (6), containing HMGB1 (30), which potently stimulates CD8+ T cell activation (6).

Here, co-culture of B cells with CM from Doxy-induced, Parkin-positive MPten1 cells expanded the population of memory B cells, transitional B cells and plasma cells, while the fraction of naïve B cells was reduced (Fig. 3F). This was associated with increased expression of B cell activation and differentiation marker, CD80 and membrane-associated IgG, whereas the level of the early activation marker, CD86 (34) was reduced after a 3-d co-culture (Fig. 3F). Non-induced control CM or CM from Parkin-negative MPten1 cells induced by vehicle (DMSO) had no effect (Fig. 3F). Addition of IL4 plus IL5 to the co-culture conditions did not further modulate B cell differentiation and expression of activation markers induced by Parkin-positive CM (fig. S4D). Consistent with the release of active chemokines in the Parkin secretome (6), co-incubation with Parkin-positive CM potently stimulated B and T cell migration across Transwell membranes, whereas CM from DMSO-induced Parkin-negative MPten1 cells had no effect (Fig. 3G). Finally, non-induced CM, or CM from Parkin-negative or Parkin-positive MPten-1 cells did not significantly affect B cell proliferation, by cell-trace quantification and flow cytometry (fig. S4E).

Requirements of Parkin TLS for antitumor immunity

To test the hypothesis that Parkin-induced TLS formation suppresses primary and metastatic prostate cancer, in vivo, we next profiled prostate tissues from TRAMP-PbPRKN mice by RNA-Seq. Gene expression analysis of this dataset revealed that transgenic expression of Parkin did not alter the prominent proliferative gene signature of TRAMP mice, characterized by multiple cell division, mitotic checkpoints and chromosome segregation gene networks (Fig. 4A). Instead, TRAMP-PbPRKN mice exhibited activation of inflammatory, cytokine and IFN signaling pathways (Fig. 4A). This involved upregulation of type I and II IFN (IFNAR, IFNB, IFNA, IFNG), multiple transcriptional networks of B cell differentiation (TFEB, TOX, ETS1, CSF2, EGR1), NFKB, and pleiotropic cytokines implicated in T and B cell activation (IL6, IL2, IL15, IL33, STAT3) as well as TLS formation (CXCR4) (Fig. 4B).

To test the potential requirement of Parkin IFN gene expression in TLS formation and tumor suppression (18, 19), we next established a syngeneic tumor model, where Tet-ON Parkin MPten1 cells are engrafted onto the flanks of WT C57BL/6 J mice. Consistent with recent results (6), Doxy-regulated induction of Parkin was sufficient to inhibit MPten1 tumor growth and cause tumor regression (Fig. 4C). In contrast, MPten1 cells induced with vehicle (DMSO) exhibited exponential tumor growth (Fig. 4C). Despite the shorter time frame of Parkin expression in syngeneic tumors compared to PbPRKN transgenic mice, Doxy-regulated Parkin induction was associated with the formation of CD20+ immune aggregates, whereas DMSO-treated animals were negative, by IHC (Fig. 4D).

To expand these findings, we next examined the proteome of MPten1 tumors harvested from animals receiving DMSO (n = 3) or Doxy (n = 3) by mass spectrometry. Of a total of 8,902 proteins identified, 2466 were significantly differentially modulated in Doxy-treated tumors compared to DMSO (minimum absolute fold change of 2, adjusted p value <0.05). In this analysis, conditional induction of Parkin profoundly remodeled the tumor proteome (fig. S4F) with decreased expression of oncogenes (TFAP2A, BAG4, BCL9) and upregulation of protein networks of exocytosis, (STXBP6, SYN1, STX1B, AP3B2), extracellular matrix remodeling (CELA1, LAMB1, PPL, LVRN), lipid metabolism (THRSP, ACSS3) and ROS production (DUOX1, VAT1L) (fig. S4G). In addition, gene ontology analysis of the Doxy-induced tumor proteome revealed modulation of cellular networks implicated in complement activation, immune processes, and host response (fig. S4H). Against this backdrop, MPten1 tumors conditionally expressing Parkin exhibited strongly increased expression of IgG, compared to DMSO-treated animals (Fig. 4E, inset) and high levels of multiple Ig chains (Fig. 4E), suggestive of oligoclonal B cell expansion, somatic hypermutation and Ig class switching (35).

Using the same syngeneic tumor model, we next engrafted Tet-ON Parkin MPten1 cells onto the flanks of IFNAR1 knockout (KO) mice. Doxy-induced Parkin expression had no effect on MPten1 tumor grown in IFNAR1 KO mice, compared to animals given DMSO (Fig. 4F). Similar results were obtained by reconstituting Tet-ON Parkin MPten1 cells in CD20 KO (Fig. 4G) or CD8 KO (Fig. 4H) mice, resulting in no difference in tumor growth in Doxy- or DMSO-treated animals (Fig. 4, G and H).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we used multidisciplinary approaches of digital spatial transcriptomics, immune profiling and syngeneic and transgenic mouse models of advanced prostate cancer to uncover a comprehensive pathway of antitumor immunity mediated by Parkin (4). Accordingly, prostate-specific transgenic expression of Parkin elicited potent inflammatory and IFN responses, selectively in the microenvironment (6), driving the formation of immune aggregates that expressed key hallmarks of mature TLS (16, 17). This was associated with recruitment and expansion of memory B cells and plasma cells, increased production of multiple Ig chains, accumulation of effector T cell subsets as well as conventional DC, and formation of specialized high endothelial venules. Interference with this pathway by genetic deletion of IFNAR1, CD8 or CD20 independently abolished Parkin antitumor immunity, in vivo.

As a gene altered in familial Parkinson’s disease (36), and characterized as a regulator of mitochondrial quality control (37), Parkin has attracted attention for its roles outside the CNS, in particular as a nearly universal tumor suppressor in genetically unrelated malignancies (12). The results here reinforce the model that Parkin may function as a unique ‘dual mode’ tumor suppressor, inhibiting intrinsic tumor traits in the transformed cell population (5, 7, 9), while also reprogramming the microenvironment (6, 30) for cellular and humoral antitumor immunity.

In this context, a role of TLS (20) in orchestrating endogenous tumor suppression, in this case, by Parkin, has not been previously proposed. Inflammation and IFN signaling are important drivers of TLS formation in cancer (17), and transgenic expression of Parkin potently upregulated cytokine, chemokine and IFN gene expression signatures, in vivo. Mechanistically, this may reflect the ability of Parkin to activate cGAS/STING signaling in response to released mitochondrial DNA (6), and to mediate the regulated release of the cytokine-like DAMP, HMGB1 in large extracellular vesicles (30). Taken together, these data fit well with a role of mitochondria as signaling hubs of inflammation and immunity (38, 39), where sensors of organelle ‘fitness’, such as Parkin, orchestrate DAMP signaling (40), inflammasome assembly (41) and IFN gene expression (3). Although these pathways protect against invading pathogens and viruses (42), the results here suggest that similar mechanisms of mitochondrial ‘viral mimicry’ (43) may also drive antitumor immunity.

IFN signaling is thought to drive TLS formation via production of CXCL13, as well as other pivotal chemokines CXCL9, CXCL10 and CCL19 (44, 45) that recruit B- and T-cells, induce vascular remodeling and enable DC accumulation (46) in lymphotoxin-β receptor (LTβR)-dependent mechanisms (47). Consistent with this model, Parkin transgenic mice prominently expressed CXCL13 and CXCR5, accompanied by expansion of B and T cell subsets, recruitment of MHC-II+/CD208+ mature DC, and formation of LYVE-1+ high endothelial venules (20, 48). In this context, DC density has been linked to efficient T cell infiltration in TLS, predominantly with effector memory and cytotoxic phenotypes (49), as well as enhanced ‘stem-like’ T cell differentiation properties (50) (see below).

More work is required to elucidate how B cell expansion and increased Ig production in Parkin TLS may contribute to antitumor immunity (51), as these responses have been associated with tumor promotion (52) or tumor suppression (53, 54). What is known is that Parkin TLS recruited and expanded specialized CD8+ T cell subsets that play a critical role in antitumor immunity. By immune profiling, these included resident effector T cells (CD69+/KLRG1+), highly cytotoxic subsets (KLRG1+/GrzB+) and self-renewing, ‘stem-like’ populations (TCF1+/PD-1+) (6) that limit T cell exhaustion (55) and improve the response to immunotherapy (56) in the clinic.

While addressing recent controversies about an immunological role of Parkin in cancer (15), a limitation of this study is the single transgenic approach using TRAMP mice (57). Although widely used, this model may not fully recapitulate the spectrum of prostatic tumorigenesis in humans, particularly for its propensity to generate neuroendocrine disease variants (58). Notwithstanding, the results may open actionable therapeutic opportunities (59), in line with the importance of mature TLS for improved patient responses to treatment (35), including immune checkpoint inhibitors (22). In this context, approved demethylation therapy has been shown to reverse epigenetic silencing of Parkin in select malignancies, including prostate cancer, restoring antitumor immunity in preclinical models (6). On this basis, it may be possible to reactivate Parkin-TLS formation, in vivo (21), uniquely reprogram antitumor immunity in the microenvironment and restore patient sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors (22), including in refractory cancers (60).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell lines

The murine prostate cancer MPten1 cell line (the generous gift of Dr. Ruscetti, UMass Chan Medical School, Worcester, MA) was cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 using DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were maintained for fewer than 40 passages from the time of receipt. Authentication was performed by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling with the AmpFlSTR Identifiler PCR Amplification Kit (Life Technologies) at The Wistar Institute Genomics Facility. To ensure culture integrity, mycoplasma testing was carried out by PCR using Bioo Scientific Mycoplasma Primer Sets (#375501) and QIAGEN Hot Start polymerase, both at study initiation and every two months thereafter. The generation and characterization of MPten1 cells with doxycycline (Doxy)-inducible Parkin expression was reported in recent studies (6). Briefly, cultures were co-transduced with lentiviral particles from the Dual Tet vector system (Vector Builder). Cells at ∼50% confluence were exposed to Parkin-expressing virus (pLV[Exp]-Puro-TRE > mPrkn[NM_016694.4], Vector ID: VB220330-1318vfp) together with the tTA construct (pLV[Exp]-CMV > tTS/rtTA/Hygro, Vector ID: VB010000-9369xhm) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10 in medium containing tetracycline-free FBS (Gibco) and 5 μg/ml polybrene. Twenty-four hours later, cells were washed and maintained in medium with Tet-free FBS supplemented with 150 μg/ml hygromycin (Millipore Sigma) and 2 μg/ml puromycin (Millipore Sigma). Induction of Parkin was evaluated by Western blotting following treatment with a dose range of Doxy (1–1000 ng/ml) for 72 hours (6). Based on these tests, 100 ng/ml Doxy was selected for subsequent experiments. After 10 passages under dual antibiotic selection, MPten1 cells carrying the PRKN Tet-ON system were stably maintained in culture.

Mouse strains

All animal studies followed the guidelines outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health (NIH). The animal protocol #201129 governing the experiments described in this manuscript was approved by The Wistar Institute’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Sample sizes were established through power analysis, and all animals enrolled in the experiments were considered in the final analysis. Transgenic Adenocarcinoma of the Mouse Prostate (TRAMP) (C57BL/6 J-Tg(TRAMP)8247Ng/J), CD8 knockout (KO) (B6.129S2-Cd8atm1Mak/J), CD20 KO (B6.129S2-Ighmtm1Cgn/J) IFNAR1 KO (B6.Cg-Ifngr1tm1Agt Ifnar1tm1.2Ees/J) and wild type (WT) C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. TRAMP transgenic mice express the SV40 Large T Antigen oncogene in the prostatic epithelium under the control of the Probasin (Pb) promoter starting at puberty (25) and were backcrossed on a C57BL/6 J background.

Transgenic mouse generation

We used a protocol established in our laboratory (61) to generate mice with constitutive prostate-specific expression of Parkin under the control of the Pb promoter. For these experiments, a cDNA encoding mouse Parkin (Origene, CAT#: MR227497) was cloned into the pcDNA6/myc-His A plasmid (Invitrogen) using EcoRV and XbaI restriction sites. A rat Pb promoter fragment (−716/+28), identified through the Genomatix database and amplified from rat genomic DNA (Millipore Sigma, CAT#: 69238), was inserted upstream of the Parkin cDNA into the pcDNA6/myc-His construct via EcoRI and EcoRV sites. For transgenic mouse generation, virgin female mice were hormonally induced to super ovulate by intraperitoneal injection of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG; 5 IU/0.1 ml) followed by hCG (5 IU/0.1 ml) ∼48 h later, after which they were paired with stud males. On the following day, females were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and oviducts were collected under aseptic conditions. Fertilized embryos were flushed from the oviducts and used for pronuclear DNA microinjection. A Parkin plasmid DNA fragment (5 ng/μl in 1 mM Tris, 0.1 mM EDTA, pH 7.2) was introduced into the pronucleus of each embryo using a glass capillary injection needle and a holding pipette. Injected embryos were subsequently transferred into the oviduct ampulla of day 0.5 postcoital pseudo pregnant females. Recipient females were anesthetized with either a ketamine-acepromazine-xylazine cocktail (ketamine, 80–100 mg/kg; xylazine, 8–12 mg/kg; acepromazine, 1–3 mg/kg, i.p.) or a ketamine-xylazine combination (ketamine, 80–100 mg/kg; xylazine, 8–12 mg/kg, i.p.). Embryo transfer was carried out using a 500 μm inner diameter glass pipette inserted through the oviductal wall. Offspring were genotyped to confirm construct integration, and positive animals were established as founders for colony development. The Pb-Parkin (PbPRKN) mouse line was maintained through breeding and confirmed by genotyping. To generate double TRAMP-PbPRKN transgenic mice, female TRAMP mice were crossed with male PbPRKN mice, and the offspring was routinely genotyped to confirm status. Cohorts of double transgenic male mice were sacrificed at 34–42 weeks of age and processed for GeoMx analysis, immunohistochemistry (IHC), multiplex immunofluorescence and multiparametric flow cytometry. Spleens harvested from 8-to-10 weeks old C57BL/6 J mice were used as a source of isolated B and T cells for co-culture experiments.

Mouse genotyping

Seven-to-ten-day old mice were genotyped by tail clipping and DNA extraction using Phire Tissue direct PCR Master mix kit (Thermo Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The primers used for PCR amplification are listed in table S1. Reactions were performed using conditions recommended for TRAMP transgene expression by the manufacturer.

Mouse xenograft tumors

Murine prostate cancer MPten1 cells engineered to conditionally express Parkin in response to Doxy treatment (Tet-ON system) were injected (5 × 105 cells in 150 μl of a 1:1 PBS:Matrigel solution) s.c. in cohorts of C57BL/6 J mice. Once tumors reached a volume of ∼150–200 mm3, half of the mice in each group received Doxy (500 ng/ml, Millipore) added to the drinking water. Tumor growth was quantified at increasing time intervals with a caliper. At the end of the study, tumors were harvested and processed for further experiments.

Tumor proteomics profiling

MPten1 tumors harvested from animals treated with vehicle (DMSO, n = 3) or Doxy (n = 3) were cryogenically pulverized with a liquid nitrogen-cooled mini-mortar and a pestle. The frozen powder was suspended in lysis buffer (200 μl/20 μg of material) and further disrupted by probe sonication. The samples were clarified, quantified by BCA assay and sample quality was evaluated by full length SDS-PAGE analysis. For each sample, 20 μg of protein was loaded onto 0.5 cm preparative SDS gels and each sample/lane was reduced with tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), alkylated with iodoacetamide and digested in-gel with trypsin. Each tryptic digest was then analyzed using a 40-min LC/MS-MS run on a Thermo Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer in Data-Independent Acquisition Mode. MS data were searched against a spectral library generated from the UniProt mouse database with curated isoforms (UP000000590, downloaded 2025-04-25) and a contaminant database using DIA-NN v2.2.0. Variable modifications searched include N-term M excision, N-terminal acetylation, and M oxidation. Static modifications searched include C carbamidomethylation. The “match between runs” and cross-run normalization features were enabled. The non-normalized abundance data was utilized in this analysis. Precursor, peptide and protein false discovery rates were set at 1%. To calculate the fold change, protein Norm Abundance values were processed by Log2 transformation of the abundance values and replacing missing values (protein not detected) with minimal values. For comparison between two sample groups, Log2 ratio, fold change and moderated t-test P value were calculated using the imputed Log2 Norm Abundance values and the p-values were adjusted to account for multiple testing using Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction (p.adj). In this analysis, proteins with significant change had a minimum absolute fold change of 2 and P adj < 0.05.

GeoMx digital spatial profiling

Whole prostate tissue samples harvested from 40-week-old TRAMP-PbPRKN male transgenic mice were embedded in paraffin and 5 μm-thick sections were placed on a high adhesive slide. The slide was positioned entirely within the designated Scan Area and baked for 2 hours at 60°C. Tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through two washes in 100% ethanol (5 min each), a 5-min wash in 95% ethanol, and a 1-min wash in PBS. For antigen retrieval, the slide was briefly dipped in DEPC-treated H2O2 for 10 sec, then incubated in preheated (99°C) 1x Tris-EDTA buffer for 20 min, followed by Proteinase K treatment (1 μg/ml) for 15 min at 37°C and washes in NBF, stop buffer, and PBS.

For hybridization, the chamber was cleaned with RNase AWAY, Buffer R, RNA probes were mixed at 22°C and 200 μl aliquots of hybridization solution were applied under a HybriSlip coverslip for 16 h at 37°C. Following hybridization, washes were performed with 100% formamide/4x SSC at 37°C and 2x SSC to remove the coverslip, followed by washes in Stringent Wash solution (2x25 min) and 2x SSC (2x2 min). The slide was then incubated with 200 μl solution of morphology markers for 1 hour at 22°C, post-fixed for 20 min, and washed sequentially in NBF, stop buffer, 10% NBF, and 1x PBS. CD8 staining was performed with a rabbit anti-mouse CD8 primary antibody (Cell Signaling) at 1:100 dilution for 16 hours followed by washes in stringent buffer and goat anti-rabbit A647 antibody (1:500) for 1.5 h at 22°C. Concurrent with CD8 staining, the slide was incubated with an antibody to PanCK (1:40, Bruker), washed 3 times with stringent buffer, and further incubated with morphology marker solution (Syto13 1:10 from stock, Bruker) prepared in Buffer W adjusted to 220 μl per slide. The hybridization was repeated with 200 μl solution under a HybriSlip coverslip for 16 hours at 37°C. Positive control probes were prepared at 1:100 dilution, and all steps were performed to prevent tissue drying and preserve probe integrity.

Image acquisition was performed with scan parameters set to FITC/525 nm (Syto 13, 100 ms), Cy3/568 nm (PanCK, 100 ms), and Cy5/666 nm (CD8, 50 ms). A total of 35 regions of interest (ROIs) with 89 wells were analyzed, with ROIs segmented as PanCK-positive, CD8+, CD8−, or collected as whole ROIs without segmentation. For library preparation, NGS libraries were generated following the GeoMx DSP NGS Readout User Manual (MAN-10153-03). PCR reactions consisted of 2 μl 5x PCR Master Mix, 4 μl Primer Mix, and 4 μl DSP aspirate from the rehydrated collection plate. Libraries were amplified for 18 cycles to incorporate GeoMx Seq Code primers (i5 and i7 indices) under the following cycling conditions: UDG incubation for 30 min at 37°C, UDG deactivation for 10 min at 50°C, initial denaturation for 3 min at 95°C, followed by 18 cycles of 95°C for 15 sec, 65°C for 60 sec, and 68°C for 30 sec, with a final extension for 5 min at 68°C and hold at 4°C. Amplified products were pooled, bead-purified with AMPure XP (Beckman Coulter), and quality-checked using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher) and Agilent TapeStation (HS D5000). Sequencing was carried out on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform with a P3 100-cycle kit, producing approximately 1.2 billion total reads. Libraries were sequenced in paired-end mode (27 bp for Read 1 and Read 2; 8 bp for Index 1 and Index 2) and loaded at a final concentration of 675 pM.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissue samples were collected and fixed for 16 hours at 22°C in 10% neutral buffered formalin (ThermoFisher), transferred to 70% ethanol and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 5 μm thickness were cut, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained for individual markers. For deparaffinization, slides were sequentially treated with xylene (6 min), xylene substitute (6 min), 100% ethanol (2 x 2 min), 95% ethanol (2 x 2 min), and distilled H2O2 (1 min). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Leica, Surgipath Hematoxylin 560) was performed on a Daekawe automated stainer following the manufacturer’s instructions.

For CD8 detection, antigen retrieval was carried out using DAKO EDTA buffer in a pressure cooker for 20 min at 95°C, followed by 15 min permeabilization at 22°C with 0.1% Triton-X 100 (Millipore) in PBS and 2.5% horse serum (Vector) saturation for 45 min at 22°C. Aliquots of rabbit anti-mouse CD8α (D4W2Z) XP mAb (Cell Signaling) were incubated for 16 hours at 4°C at 1:200 dilution. DAB development was performed for 90 sec. For CD20 detection, a rabbit anti-mouse antibody (Cell Signaling) was used, and DAB development was carried out for 30 sec. For Parkin detection, antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer using a steamer for 20 min, and sections were incubated for 16 hours at 4°C with a rabbit anti-PARK2/Parkin polyclonal antibody (Proteintech) at 1:400 dilution, followed by a 2 min DAB detection reaction. The secondary antibody used was HRP-conjugated horse anti-rabbit IgG polymer reagent (VectorLabs) at 1:200 dilution, incubated for 30 min at 22°C. For SV40 detection, after saturation, avidin solution (Vector) was incubated for 15 min at 22°C, followed by one wash and incubation with biotin solution for 15 min at 22°C (Vector). A primary anti-mouse SV40 antibody (SantaCruz) was incubated at 1:50 dilution for 30 min at 22°C, washed and a biotinylated secondary antibody (Vector) was incubated for 15 min at 22°C, followed by 40 sec DAB development. For CXCL13 detection, a primary rabbit anti-mouse (Bioss) antibody was incubated at 1:200 dilution for 16 hours at 4°C followed by incubation with a biotinylated secondary anti-rabbit antibody (1:200) for 30 min at 22°C and ABC for HRP detection for 30 min at 22°C. All tissue sections were counterstained with Harris Hematoxylin solution (Leica), for nuclei detection as per manufacturer’s instruction. Slides were imaged using either a Nikon 80i microscope or a NanoZoomer S60 digital scanner (Hamamatsu), and quantitative analyses were performed with QuPath software.

Flow cytometry

Spleens and prostate tumors from the various mouse groups were mechanically dissociated into single-cell suspensions, filtered through a 70 μm mesh, and treated with ACK lysis buffer to remove red blood cells. LIVE/DEAD staining was performed with Aqua or Zombie UV dyes (1:300). Cells were stained for surface markers in Brilliant Stain Buffer (50 μl/sample) for 15 min at 4°C, washed in MACS buffer (1% FBS, 5 mM EDTA in PBS), and, when required, stained for intracellular markers using the eBioscience Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set for 45 min at 4°C. In some experiments, spleens from naïve WT C57BL/6 J mice were mechanically dissociated, red blood cells removed with ACK buffer and B cells were purified using a mouse negative B cell selection kit (Miltenyi). Isolated B cells were seeded in 96-well U-bottom plates (1 x 105/well) with 100 μl of RPMI plus 10% FBS and supplemented with 100 μl of conditioned culture medium (CM) from Parkin-expressing cells with or without IL4 + IL-5 (100 ng/ml). Unconditioned medium (with or without cytokines) was used as control. After 72 hours, the culture medium was removed, and B cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

To obtain Parkin-regulated CM, MPten1 TetON Parkin cells were seeded at 5 x 105 cells/ml and cultured for 4 days in medium with tetracycline-free FBS (Parkin-) or FBS containing Doxy for activation of the TetON system (Parkin+). The CM was collected, centrifuged to remove cell debris and added to the B cell culture at 37°C before flow cytometry analysis. In some experiments, CD8+ T cells were isolated from unfractionated WT splenocytes by negative selection using a mouse CD8α+ T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec), following the manufacturer’s protocol for cell magnetic separation. Compensation controls were prepared with UltraComp eBeads and ArC Compensation Beads. Samples were acquired on a FACSymphony Cell Analyzer (BD) and analyzed using FlowJo 10.7. As gating strategy, single CD45+ cells were analyzed for IgD+/CD27+ cells in the CD19+/CD3− population (memory B cells), IgD−/CD27+/CD138+ subset (plasma cells) and IgD+/CD27− subset (naïve B cells). The primary antibodies used in this study are listed in table S2.

Multiplex tissue immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded prostate tissues from TRAMP-PbPRKN transgenic mice (34 weeks) were cut into 5 μm thick sections. Slides were baked for 45 min at 60°C, deparaffinized in xylene (6 min) and xylene substitute (6 min), rehydrated through graded ethanol (100%, 2 x 2 min; 95%, 2 x 2 min), and rinsed in deionized H2O2 (2x2 min). Antigen retrieval was performed in 1x EDTA buffer (DAKO, ref. #S2367, diluted in MilliQ H2O2) by heating in a pressure cooker for 20 min at 95°C. Sections were then permeabilized for 30 min at 22°C in 1x PBS with 5% goat serum and 0.3% Triton X-100, washed three times in PBS (5 min each), and blocked for 1 hour at 22°C with 10% goat serum. Primary antibodies (table S2) were diluted in 1x PBS with 5% goat serum and incubated for 16 hours at 4°C in a humid chamber. After 3 washes in PBS (5 min each), tissue sections were incubated with Hoechst 33258 (2 μg/ml in 1x PBS with 5% goat serum) for 5 min at 22°C, washed three times (5 min each), and mounted with ProLong Antifade (Thermo Fisher). For secondary staining, samples were subject to an additional saturation step in 10% goat serum for 30 min at 22°C before addition of secondary reagent of appropriate specificity, Alexa Fluor 647-anti-rabbit (1:300), Alexa Fluor 594-anti-rat (1:100) or Alexa Fluor 488-anti-mouse (1:20) IgG for 90 min at 22°C.

For image acquisition, high resolution, confocal images of fixed tissue preparations were captured using a Leica STELLARIS 8 3X TauSTED laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica Micro-systems, Inc., Deerfield, IL) equipped with a white-light laser (440–790 nm), five hybrid detectors (HyD type S, X, and R) operating in photon counting mode, and a conventional point scanner. Images were acquired in a frame sequence with 16-line accumulations using a 63X/1.40 oil objective, 2X zoom and a pinhole of 1 AU, resulting in a pixel size of 92.26 nm. Laser powers were set to 2%. For analysis, all images were first deconvolved using **Huygens Professional version 25.04** (Scientific Volume Imaging, The Netherlands, (http://svi.nl) with the standard Deconvolution Express profile. Individual image files were opened in Huygens, acquisition parameters were verified, and images were optimized using the Deconvolution Express – Standard profile.

The resulting files were saved as 16-bit OME-TIFs, each as a single six-channel greyscale image with no compression and intensity scaled using the linked scale profile. In the second phase, images were enhanced using **NIS-Elements AR version 6.10.01, Build 2027** (Nikon Instruments Inc., 2025). OME-TIF files were opened in NIS-Elements, and the following colors were assigned to each channel: Ch1 – DAPI (white), Ch2 – A488 (green), Ch3 – A555 (yellow), Ch4 – A594 (cyan), Ch5 – A647 (red), and Ch6 – A700 (magenta). Brightness and contrast were adjusted for each channel, and the processed images were saved as Nikon “.nd” files. Merged and single-channel images were then exported as RGB “.tif” files. ImageJ was then used to despeckle and smoothen the images.

Cell migration

Spleens from WT C57BL/6 J mice were processed as described above, and purified CD20+ B or CD8+ T cells were isolated. B cells (5 x 104 per well) were seeded in the upper chamber of 24-well Transwell inserts with 4 μm pores (Thermo Fisher) in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. CD8+ T cells (5 x 104 per well) were plated in 24-well Transwell inserts with 8 μm pores. Aliquots of CM (300 μl) harvested from MPten1 TetON-Parkin cells (5 x 105 cells/ml) treated with vehicle (DMSO) or Doxy for 4 days at 37°C were applied to the lower chamber of Transwell plates as a source of chemoattractant(s). Cell migration across Transwell membranes was assessed by harvesting and counting viable cells that reached the lower chamber after 4 hours (CD20+ B cells) or 6 hours (CD8+ T cells) using trypan blue exclusion. Results were expressed as the percentage of migrated cells relative to the total number seeded.

RNA-Seq

Total RNA was isolated from snap-frozen prostate tissue samples isolated from TRAMP, PbPRKN and TRAMP-PbPRKN transgenic mice by first homogenizing 25 mg of tissue per ml of Tri-Reagent (Sigma, St. Louis, MO) using the Qiagen TissueRuptor and corresponding sterile probes (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Homogenized lysate was processed using Direct-zol RNA Miniprep kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA) as per manufacturer’s instructions. On-column DNaseI treatment was performed. Total RNA quality was assessed using the 4200 TapeStation and the RNA 5000 assay (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) while concentration was determined using the Qubit Fluorometer 2.0 (Thermofisher, Waltham, MA). Libraries for differential gene expression studies were prepared using the Quant Seq 3’ mRNA-Seq V2 Library Prep Kit FWD (Lexogen, Wien, Austria) per manufacturer’s instructions starting with an input of 500 ng of RNA and 13 cycles of final PCR amplification.

Overall library size was determined using the 4200 TapeStation (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) and libraries were quantitated using the Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA). Libraries were pooled and Next Generation Sequencing with a single-end 76 bp run length was done on the Nextseq 2000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA). A minimum of 20 million reads per sample was acquired for each sample. RNA-Seq data were aligned using the STAR algorithm (62) against mm10 mouse genome version and RSEM v1.2.12 software (63) was used to estimate read counts and RPKM values using gene information from Ensemble transcriptome version GRCm38.89. Raw counts were used to estimate significance of differential expression between two experimental groups using EdgeR (64) with default parameters. Significant genes that passed FDR < 5% threshold were tested for enrichment using QIAGEN’s Ingenuity Pathway Analysis software (IPA, QIAGEN Redwood City, analysis.ingenuity.com) using “Canonical pathways” and “Upstream Regulators”.

Statistical analysis

For GeoMx data analysis, raw data from 95 ROIs selected during GeoMx data were processed using standard Nanostring GeoMx DSP pipeline v2.5 (https://nanostring.com) to produce estimated gene expression. Data for 19,963 mouse genes were normalized using upper quartile/Q3 method according to the pipeline default parameters and log2-scaled. Quantitative proportions of immune cells were estimated using absolute relative mode by CIBERSORT (65) with corresponding human homolog genes as identifiers. The LM22 cell model matrix was used for estimation analysis. Genes that showed at least 2-fold enrichment in a cell type vs. average across all cell types were considered for visualization as cell-type specific. The R version of limma software (66) was used to estimate significance of differential gene expression or cell abundance between groups of ROIs. Results that passed FDR <5% threshold were considered significant unless stated otherwise. Gene set enrichment analysis was done using QIAGEN’s Ingenuity Pathway Analysis software (IPA, QIAGEN Redwood City, analysis.ingenuity.com) using “Canonical pathways” and “Upstream Regulators” options. Pathways that passed FDR <5% and predicted to be activated based on positive Z-score activation state were reported. Upstream regulators results were presented in the form of volcano plots demonstrating significance of target enrichment vs. predicted activation of the regulators. Data are expressed as mean ± SE of results from a minimum of three independent experiments. Unpaired, two-sided Student’s t test was used for two-group comparative analyses. In some cases, correction for multiple testing by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was obtained. For multiple-group comparisons, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. All statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad software package (Prism 11) for Windows. A P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
