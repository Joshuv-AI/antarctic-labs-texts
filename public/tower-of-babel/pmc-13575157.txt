Liquid-phase synthesis of a series of superionic sulfide electrolytes for all-solid-state lithium-sulfur battery
Authors: Minkang Wang, Han Su, Juner Kuang, Yu Zhong, Xiuli Wang, Jiangping Tu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInorganic solid electrolytes offer transformative opportunities for next-generation lithium batteries by enabling new chemistries and cell architectures. Among them, sulfide electrolytes are particularly attractive due to their high ionic conductivity and favorable mechanical processability. However, scalable synthesis of sulfide electrolytes with high ionic conductivity remains a significant bottleneck. Here, we report a liquid-phase synthesis platform based on a Li2S-SiS2-P2S5 ternary system, capable of producing electrolytes with high ionic conductivity at kilogram scale. Beyond fast ionic transport kinetics, the electrolytes prepared via this platform function effectively as catholytes for high-loading sulfur electrodes, promoting uniform ion transport and enhanced electrochemical kinetics. Building on this approach, a Li2S-SiS2-P2S5-LiI quaternary system is developed to produce lithium-metal compatible anolytes. As a result, we realize a long-cycling all-solid-state lithium-sulfur battery in which the catholyte, interlayer, and anolyte are all synthesized via liquid-phase methods. This work proposes a scalable route for producing sulfide electrolytes, potentially contributing to the industrial development of all-solid-state batteries.Subject terms: Batteries, Energy, Batteries

Introduction

Inorganic solid electrolytes (ISEs) enable transformative chemistries and architectures for next-generation lithium battery technologies1–3. Replacing flammable organic electrolytes with ISEs in the construction of all-solid-state Li metal batteries (ASSLMBs) offers the potential for enhanced safety and improved energy density4–6. Among ISEs, sulfide solid electrolytes (SSEs) have attracted intense research interest due to their high ionic conductivity and favorable mechanical processability7–10. However, the industrial production of SSEs remains quite distant from realization, hindered by the lack of scalable techniques for synthesizing SSE materials with desirable electrochemical performances11,12.

SSE synthesis is conventionally classified into solid-state and liquid-phase routes, differentiated by precursor mixing. While solid-state synthesis dominates laboratory-scale fabrication, it suffers from high energy consumption and complex equipment requirements7,13–16. Liquid-phase synthesis presents a promising alternative, offering reduced energy input and scalable processing17,18. This approach subdivides into dissolution-precipitation and suspension techniques. Dissolution-precipitation involves fully dissolving precursors in protonic solvents such as ethanol19, hydrazine20, or N-methylformamide (NMF)21, which can induce detrimental side reactions with sulfides, thereby impairing ionic conductivity. Conversely, suspension methods utilize aprotic solvents like acetonitrile (ACN)22, tetrahydrofuran (THF)23, or 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME)24, which better preserve sulfide integrity and conductivity. Despite these advantages, sulfides synthesized via suspension methods typically exhibit ionic conductivities below the threshold required for practical application17,22. Overcoming this limitation mandates the development of liquid-phase synthetic strategies capable of delivering SSEs with enhanced ionic transport characteristics. Moreover, ASSLMBs impose distinct electrochemical and mechanical requirements on individual cell components, including the Li metal negative electrode, interlayer, and positive electrode17,25. Consequently, a liquid-phase synthesis platform that can produce compositionally tailored SSEs optimized for each functional interface is essential.

Here, we report a Li2S–SiS2–P2S5 ternary system enabling the liquid-phase synthesis of a series of xLi4SiS4–(1–x)Li3PS4 (LSPSx) electrolytes that overcome the limitations of traditional Li2S–P2S5 binaries (Fig. 1a, b). The incorporation of SiS2 modifies the sulfide network structure and phase evolution of the Li2S–P2S5 system, leading to simultaneous improvements in ionic conductivity and structural stability. With increasing SiS2 content, the LSPSx electrolytes evolve from an orthorhombic to a tetragonal phase, accompanied by a substantial increase in ionic conductivity. For 0 <x ≤ 0.25, LSPSx crystallizes in an orthorhombic phase with ionic conductivity reaching 1.94 mS cm–1 at x = 0.25. Between 0.33 ≤ x ≤ 0.5, the structure transitions to tetragonal, with LSPS0.45 achieving an ionic conductivity of 5.42 mS cm–1. LSPS0.45 also exhibits robust compatibility with cost-effective, high-capacity sulfur-positive electrodes. However, the LSPSx series shows limited stability against lithium metal. To resolve this, we further developed a Li2S–SiS2–P2S5–LiI quaternary system, synthesizing Li7.25Si0.25P1.75S8I (LSPSI) electrolytes with enhanced lithium metal interfacial stability (Fig. 1c). Implementing LSPSI as the anolyte and LSPS0.45 as the interlayer and catholyte (Fig. 1d), the resulting all-solid-state Li metal | |sulfur battery (ASSLMSB) demonstrates stable cycling over 300 cycles. This study advances scalable SSE synthesis strategies for practical all-solid-state battery applications.

Results and discussion

Superionic Li4SiS4–Li3PS4 series SSEs

Research on liquid-phase synthesis of SSEs, particularly via the suspension method, has predominantly centered on the Li2S–P2S5 binary system. Despite extensive optimization efforts, including solvent system refinement and tuning the Li2S/P2S5 molar ratio, performance improvements appear to have plateaued. A benchmark electrolyte in this system, Li7P3S1122,26, achieves a room-temperature (RT, 25 ± 1 °C) ionic conductivity of only ~1 mS cm−1, which substantially limits its practical application (Fig. S1). To transcend this bottleneck, we developed a ternary Li2S–SiS2–P2S5 system built upon the Li2S–P2S5 framework. Employing the suspension method with ACN as the solvent, a series of LSPSx precursors was synthesized. The photograph of synthesized kilogram-scale electrolyte powders is provided in Fig. S2.

The incorporation of SiS2 significantly alters the synthetic chemistry from precursor to final electrolyte. Without SiS2, the Li3PS4 precursor synthesized via suspension methods can only form the highly conductive β-Li3PS4 phase under low-temperature sintering conditions. Previous studies have shown that low-temperature sintering preserves abundant surface defects critical for stabilizing the β phase, whereas direct high-temperature sintering induces transformation to the γ-Li3PS4 phase, characterized by markedly lower ionic conductivity23,27. Introduction of SiS2, however, enables the formation of thermodynamically stable and highly conductive phases upon high-temperature sintering. As evidenced by X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Fig. S3), compositions within 0 <x ≤ 0.25 show stabilization of the β-Li3PS4 structure by SiS2 incorporation (Fig. 2a), yielding orthorhombic phases consistent with thio-LISICON I type electrolytes after high-temperature treatment. For compositions within 0.33≤ x ≤ 0.5, SiS2 promotes conversion into tetragonal phases analogous to thio-LISICON II type structures (Fig. 2b) following sintering28–30.

The ionic conductivity and Li-ion conduction activation energy (Ea) of the synthesized LSPSx electrolyte series were systematically evaluated (Fig. 2c), with corresponding Nyquist and Arrhenius plots provided in Fig. S4. Within the orthorhombic phase region (0 <x ≤ 0.25), ionic conductivity increases monotonically with rising SiS2 content, concomitant with a reduction in Ea. Notably, at x = 0.25, orthorhombic LSPSx attains a peak ionic conductivity of 1.94 mS cm−1, which is significantly higher than that of β-Li3PS4 (x = 0). Upon transitioning from orthorhombic to tetragonal symmetry (0.33 ≤ x ≤ 0.5), LSPSx electrolytes exhibit a stepwise increase in ionic conductivity accompanied by a corresponding decrease in Ea. For instance, LSPS0.33 demonstrates an ionic conductivity of 3.39 mS cm−1, nearly double that of orthorhombic LSPS0.25, while the maximum conductivity within this range reaches 5.42 mS cm−1 at x = 0.45. These results suggest that liquid-phase synthesis within the Li2S–SiS2–P2S5 ternary system can yield LSPSx electrolytes with competitive ionic conductivities, in some cases several times higher than that of Li7P3S11.

To elucidate the atomistic origins of enhanced Li-ion transport, ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations were performed on three representative compositions: β-Li3PS4 (x = 0), Li3.25Si0.25P0.75S4 (0 <x ≤ 0.25), and Li10SiP2S12 (0.33 ≤ x ≤ 0.5) (Fig. 2d–f). Lithium mean square displacement (MSD) was tracked over 40 ps simulations (Fig. 2g). Representative structures during AIMD simulations are provided in Supplementary Data 1–3. In β-Li3PS4, Li diffusion is highly anisotropic, predominantly along the crystallographic b-axis, consistent with prior theoretical reports31. Introduction of Si within the orthorhombic phase notably enhances Li diffusivity and alters transport anisotropy: Li diffusion along the a- and c-axes is significantly improved, resulting in near-isotropic lithium mobility. This behavior is attributed to the increased configurational entropy from the additional Li ions introduced via Si doping, which lowers energy barriers for diffusion along previously sluggish directions, as corroborated by earlier studies27. Upon transitioning to the tetragonal phase, Li diffusion kinetics are further enhanced, with Li10SiP2S12 exhibiting the highest MSD values. Its conduction mechanism parallels that of Li10GeP2S12 (LGPS)-type electrolytes, characterized by dominant diffusion along the c-axis and comparatively weaker transport along a- and b-axes32,33.

In summary, SiS2 incorporation modulates lithium diffusion via two key mechanisms: for 0 <x ≤ 0.25, LSPSx stabilizes β-Li3PS4 orthorhombic structure and benefits from increased Li-ion concentration and configurational entropy, shifting Li transport from anisotropic to near-isotropic and thereby enhancing kinetics; for 0.33 ≤ x ≤ 0.5, a structural phase transition to tetragonal symmetry occurs, adopting LGPS-like diffusion pathways that enable fast Li-ion conduction in LSPSx electrolytes.

Liquid-phase reaction mechanism of Li2S–SiS2–P2S5 ternary system

The precursors of Li4SiS4–Li3PS4 series SSEs were synthesized via liquid-phase reaction within the Li2S–SiS2–P2S5 ternary system using ACN as the solvent. Previous studies have elucidated the reaction mechanism of the Li2S–P2S5 binary system22,34. In ACN solvents, partially dissolved Li2S functions as a “network modifier”, with the lone-pair electrons on its sulfur atom attacking the bridging sulfur bonds in the network of P2S5 via nucleophilic substitution. The cleavage of these bridging bonds generates an intermediate product, LixPySz, which dissolves in ACN due to its moderate polarity. When the molar ratio of Li2S is sufficiently high, the bridging sulfur bonds in LixPySz will be further cleaved, resulting in the formation of Li3PS4. Due to its low solubility in ACN, Li3PS4 precipitates out as Li3PS4·ACN. Similarly, nucleophilic substitution reactions occur between Li2S and SiS2. However, in the Li2S–SiS2–P2S5, the reaction thermodynamics between Li2S and SiS2 differ significantly from that between Li2S and P2S5. XRD analysis of the Li4SiS4–Li3PS4 precursor, obtained after the liquid-phase reaction of the Li2S–SiS2–P2S5 system, reveals only the characteristic peaks of Li3PS4·ACN crystals and a small amount of residual Li2S (Fig. S5). No detectable crystalline signals related to Si species are observed, suggesting that the product formed from the reaction between SiS2 and Li2S is likely amorphous. This deduction was further confirmed by advanced cryo-transmission electron microscopy (TEM) characterization of the Li4SiS4–Li3PS4 precursors. Figure 3a shows the particle morphology of the Li4SiS4–Li3PS4 precursor, with energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) mapping revealing localized Si- and P-enriched regions at the edges of the precursor particles (Fig. 3b–e). Selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns from the Si-enriched region (Region 1 in Fig. 3c) only exhibit the diffraction ring of Li2S (111) (Fig. 3f), while those from the P-enriched region (Region 2 in Fig. 3c) predominantly show characteristic diffraction rings of Li3PS4·ACN (Fig. 3g), further confirming that the Si species in the precursors are amorphous. Time-dependent Raman spectroscopy was also employed to investigate the chemical characteristics of the precipitate phase at various liquid-phase reaction stages (Fig. S6). After 2 h of reaction, Raman signals corresponding to Li3PS4·ACN, Li2S, and SiS2 were detected in the precipitate. As the reaction progressed, the SiS2 signal gradually diminished, and upon completion of the reaction, only the Raman signals of Li3PS4·ACN and Li2S remained, with no signals associated with Si species. Based on these observations, abductive reasoning suggests that the reaction between Li2S and SiS2 does not produce a crystalline phase. Instead, the product is amorphous and soluble in ACN. In addition, the presence of unreacted Li2S in the Li4SiS4–Li3PS4 precursors implies that the bridging sulfur bonds in SiS2 are not fully cleaved by Li2S.

First-principles calculations were employed to probe the underlying thermodynamic differences between Li2S/SiS2 and Li2S/P2S5 reactions (Fig. 3h–j). In the Li2S/P2S5 system, the intermediate dimer Li4P2S7 features positively charged bridging sulfurs, facilitating continued nucleophilic attack by Li2S and subsequent cleavage of these bonds to form Li3PS4 monomers (Fig. 3h). Conversely, in the Li2S–SiS2 system, the analogous intermediate Li4Si2S6 dimer exhibits negatively charged bridging sulfurs, which repel the lone-pair electrons on sulfur atoms in Li2S, inhibiting further nucleophilic substitution. Consequently, this intermediate resists further reaction to generate the SiS44− cluster, explaining the observed amorphous nature and incomplete reaction of the Si-containing species (Fig. 3i).

Performance of LSPS0.45 catholyte in sulfur-positive electrode

The LSPS0.45 electrolyte exhibiting the highest ionic conductivity among LSPSx can function well in sulfur composite positive electrodes. Solid-state sulfur-positive electrodes and all-solid-state Li | |S batteries (ASSLSBs) were fabricated and systematically evaluated. The S/LSPS0.45-positive electrode, comprising sulfur, conductive carbon, and LSPS0.45 catholyte, was prepared via ball milling, incorporating a high sulfur loading of 45 wt%. For performance comparison, S/LPSC-positive electrodes were similarly prepared using benchmark Li5.5PS4.5Cl1.5 (LPSC) with an ionic conductivity of 6.93 mS cm−1 (Fig. S7) as the catholyte. As shown in Fig. S8, both the S/LSPS0.45 and S/LPSC composite particles exhibit spherical morphologies, with elemental mapping confirming uniform distribution and intimate interfacial contact among components. ASSLSBs employing S/LSPS0.45 and S/LPSC composite positive electrodes were evaluated over a voltage window of 1.5−3.0 V vs. Li/Li⁺, using a Li–In alloy negative electrode and LPSC as the interlayer. All the assembled ASSLSBs were tested at 30 ± 1 °C and under a stack pressure of ~50 MPa.

Figure 4a shows the initial galvanostatic charge-discharge profiles of the S/LSPS0.45 and S/LPSC-positive electrodes at 0.1 C (1 C = 1675 mA g−1). The S/LSPS0.45-positive electrode delivers a higher initial discharge capacity of 1423.5 mAh g−1, compared to 1320.6 mAh g−1 for the S/LPSC-positive electrode. Differential capacity (dQ/dV) curves reveal differences in their electrochemical behavior. The S/LSPS0.45-positive electrode exhibits well-defined sulfur redox peaks at 2.01 V (Peak I) and 1.91 V (Peak II) during discharge, corresponding to two-step sulfur reduction reactions35–37, and at 2.33 V (Peak III) during charge, corresponding to the direct oxidation of Li2S to S8. In contrast, Peaks I and II in the S/LPSC-positive electrode become indistinct and decrease in intensity, which indicates the sluggish conversion of the two-step sulfur reduction reactions. Meanwhile, the Peak III for the S/LPSC-positive electrode shifts to a higher potential, indicating increased polarization and slower reaction kinetics (Fig. 4b). The S/LSPS0.45-positive electrode shows enhanced rate capability, maintaining discharge capacities of 1419.3, 1383.5, 1265.7, 1064.9, and 853.6 mAh g−1 at 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, and 1 C, respectively, and retaining 1272.1 mAh g−1 when returned to 0.3 C (Fig. 4c, d). By contrast, S/LPSC exhibits rapid capacity decay at higher current rates. Cycling stability further highlights the advantage of the S/LSPS0.45-positive electrode. The S/LSPS0.45-positive electrode delivers a capacity of 958.6 mAh g−1 after 1300 cycles at 0.5 C, with Coulombic efficiency (CE) consistently above 99% (Fig. 4e). The S/LPSC-positive electrode, in comparison, suffers pronounced capacity fading from 842.5 to 399.4 mAh g−1 over 500 cycles. At a higher sulfur loading (~ 5 mg cm−2) and an N/P ratio of 3, S/LSPS0.45 demonstrates a cycle life exceeding 2000 cycles while sustaining high-areal capacities. The areal capacity increases from 4.54 to 4.97 mAh cm−2 during the first 600 cycles, likely due to gradually improved sulfur reactivity35, and remains above 4.02 mAh cm−2 even after 2000 cycles (Fig. 4f). Notably, the performance of ASSLSBs with S/LSPS0.45 is competitive compared to recent reports, highlighting its potential for practical applications. The enhanced electrochemical performance of the S/LSPS0.45-positive electrode is primarily attributed to the formation of an efficient and robust Li-ion conducting network. As shown in Fig. S9, the solid-state-synthesized LPSC particles consist of relatively large grains, whereas the liquid-phase-synthesized electrolyte is composed of aggregated nanograins (Figs. S10 and 11). Particle size distribution analysis after ultrasonic dispersion reveals that the LSPS0.45 electrolyte exhibits a much narrower size distribution and a significantly smaller average particle size compared with LPSC. Specifically, the D50 and D90 values of LSPS0.45 are 0.786 μm and 1.764 μm, respectively, whereas those of LPSC reach 24.74 μm and 94.12 μm (Fig. S12). Benefiting from the smaller particle size of LSPS0.45, the S/LSPS0.45 composite positive electrode demonstrates a smoother cold-pressed positive electrode surface with fewer pores and cracks compared to the S/LPSC-positive electrode (Fig. S13). In addition, the mechanical integrity of the S/LSPS0.45-positive electrode is better preserved during cycling due to the homogeneous distribution of the fine electrolyte particles. After 100 cycles, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images show that the surface of the S/LSPS0.45-positive electrode remains smooth and uniform with minimal cracks and voids, while the corresponding EDS elemental mapping confirms a homogeneous compositional distribution (Figs. 4g and S14). Cross-sectional SEM further shows that the cycled S/LSPS0.45-positive electrode maintains intimate contact with the electrolyte layer, ensuring continuous and efficient lithium-ion transport pathways (Fig. 4g). In contrast, due to the localized stress accumulation38, the cycled S/LPSC-positive electrode develops more cracks and voids, causing the breakdown of the ionic conduction network and significantly reducing the utilization of sulfur species (Fig. 4h).

Beyond these mechanical factors, the intrinsic electrochemical behavior of LSPS0.45 and LPSC also plays an important role in determining the cycling stability of the positive electrode. Within the operating voltage window of the sulfur electrode, sulfide electrolytes with relatively narrow electrochemical stability windows can participate in redox reactions39,40. To investigate the differences in electrolyte redox behavior, C/LSPS0.45 and C/LPSC composite electrodes were prepared and evaluated within the working voltage range of the sulfur electrode (Fig. S15). The results show that the C/LSPS0.45 electrode exhibits better cycling stability than the LPSC/C. Based on the XPS analysis (Figs. S16–18), we speculate that the free S2− species present in the LPSC lattice cannot reversibly participate in the redox process, leading to gradual structural degradation during repeated electrochemical reduction. In this regard, the improved redox reversibility of LSPS0.45 within the operating voltage range of the sulfur electrode may represent another key factor contributing to its enhanced cycling stability. In addition, it should be noted that the stable redox of LSPS0.45 can contribute a limited but stable background capacity for the battery (Fig. S19).

To further verify the positive role of LSPS0.45 in constructing an efficient and stable lithium-ion conduction network, cyclic voltammetry (CV) at various scan rates (Fig. S20a, b) was first employed to analyze lithium diffusion behavior in the sulfur electrodes. Design of electrolyte for Li metal negative electrode

Despite exhibiting high ionic conductivity and good compatibility with sulfur-positive electrodes, LSPS electrolytes (exemplified by LSPS0.45) suffer from inherent instability when in contact with lithium metal. This instability is primarily attributed to the considerable Si4+ content within the LSPS electrolyte. During the reduction process at the electrolyte/negative electrode interface, substantial Si/Li-Si alloys with considerable electronic conductivity are formed. This results in a mixed-conducting interphase that fails to block electron transfer from the lithium metal to the electrolyte, thereby promoting continued electrolyte reduction.

To address this limitation, Li7.25Si0.25P1.75S8I (LSPSI) electrolytes were developed through liquid-phase synthesis. The corresponding XRD pattern is presented in Fig. S21. The ionic conductivity value of LSPSI electrolytes reaches a satisfactory level of 1.14 mS cm−1, which is almost two times higher than that of Si-free Li7P2S8I electrolytes prepared by liquid-phase synthesis41 (Figs. S22 and 23). Resting electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) tests were applied to compare the Li metal compatibility between LSPS0.45 and LSPSI (Fig. 5a, b). As shown in Fig. S24, the resistance of the Li symmetric cell using LSPS0.45 increased to ~4000 Ω over the course of 5 days of rest. In comparison, the resistance of the Li symmetric cell using LSPSI remained stable at a level of ~200 Ω under the same conditions, indicating the enhanced Li metal compatibility of LSPSI electrolytes. AIMD simulations of the Li/LSPS (Li10SiP2S12 is selected as a representative component for LSPSx, and Li27Si2P4S24I7 is constructed to represent LSPSI) and Li/LSPSI interface further corroborate these experimental findings. The structures before and after AIMD simulations are provided in Supplementary Data 4–7. As depicted in Figs. S25 and 26, the ongoing reaction between Li and LSPS results in the violent degradation of the electrolyte structure and consumption of metallic Li. From the time-dependent radial distribution function (RDF) profiles of Si–S and P–S pairs (Fig. 5c), severe decomposition of framework clusters can be observed. Besides, Si/Li-Si species, which are believed to be the culprit of mixed conducting interphase, are substantially generated at the Li/LSPS interface, as revealed by the time-dependent RDF profile of the LiM (Li atoms from Li metal)-Si pair. In sharp contrast, the Li/LSPSI interface demonstrated an enhanced stability throughout the AIMD simulation (Figs. S27 and 28). The decomposition of SiS44− and PS43− is greatly relieved through LiI incorporation. Moreover, almost no generation of Si/Li–Si species is detected at the Li/LSPSI interface (Fig. 5d). By tracing the evolution of LiM-S, LiM-P, and LiM-I pairs in Li/LSPSI (Fig. S29), it is discovered that highly insulating LiI forms earlier than Li2S and reduced P, which then plays an indispensable role in suppressing SSE reduction.

Then, using LSPSI as the anolyte, Li symmetric cells using a LSPSI/LSPS/LSPSI sandwiched electrolyte structure were assembled. Li symmetric cells using the benchmark LPSC electrolytes were utilized for performance comparison. Step-increased galvanostatic discharging/charging tests were first performed on a Li symmetric cell using LSPSI/LSPS/LSPSI and LPSC. As shown in Fig. 5e, f, the LSPSI/LSPS/LSPSI-sandwiched electrolyte displays a critical current density (CCD) value of 1.7 mA cm−2, exceeding the CCD value of LPSC (1.1 mA cm−2). Long-time galvanostatic cycling tests were also conducted on Li symmetric cells (Fig. 5g, h). At a current density of 0.1 mA cm−2 and a cut-off capacity of 0.1 mAh cm−2, the Li symmetric cell using LPSC encounters a short-circuit after ~600 h cycling, while the Li symmetric cell using a sandwiched structure functions stably for over 10,000 h. To study the mechanical evolution of the Li/SSE interface, the symmetric cells using sandwiched electrolyte and LPSC were disassembled after 100 cycles of charging/discharging at 0.1 mA cm−2/0.1 mAh cm−2. The cycled Li/SSE interface was then subjected to SEM observation. As shown in Fig. 5i, j, the cycled Li/LPSC interface presents severe mechanical degradation of electrolyte structure, as well as Li intrusion. In comparison, the cycled Li/sandwiched electrolyte interface remains intact after cycling. These results demonstrate that the sandwiched electrolyte structure is sufficiently qualified as an interlayer in ASSLMBs. Further, ASSLSBs pairing with a Li metal negative electrode and S/LSPS0.45-positive electrode were constructed by using LSPSI as the anolyte and LSPS0.45 as the interlayer, and operated under a stack pressure of ~10 MPa. Followed by 0.05 C (1 C = 1675 mA g−1) activation, the Li|LSPSI | LSPS0.45 | S/LSPS0.45 cell displays a high initial discharge capacity of 1260.2 mAh g−1 with 90.4% capacity retention (with regard to the 1st cycle at 0.2 C) over 300 cycles at 0.2 C (Fig. 5k, l). In contrast, the Li|LPSC | S/LPSC cell underwent short-circuit after 35 cycles (Fig. S30).

In summary, this work presents a scalable liquid-phase synthesis platform for SSEs based on a ternary Li2S–SiS2–P2S5 system, enabling the production of a series of LSPSx electrolytes with high ionic conductivities. The optimized composition, LSPS0.45, not only achieves a high room-temperature ionic conductivity of 5.42 mS cm−1, but also demonstrates robust compatibility with high-areal-capacity sulfur-positive electrodes, enabling all-solid-state sulfur electrodes with improved rate performance and long cycle life. To overcome the intrinsic instability of Si-containing SSEs against lithium metal, a LiI-doped quaternary system was developed, yielding the LSPSI electrolyte, which exhibits enhanced interfacial stability while maintaining satisfactory ionic conductivity. By integrating LSPSI as the anolyte and LSPS0.45 as the interlayer/catholyte, a stable-cycling ASSLMSB was constructed. This study proposes a scalable approach for producing sulfide electrolytes, providing critical insights into SSE design and advancing the development of practical ASSLMSBs.

Methods

Electrolyte synthesis

For the β-Li3PS4 and Li7P3S11 electrolytes, stoichiometric amounts of Li2S (99.98%, Sigma-Aldrich) and P2S5 (99%, Macklin) were dispersed in acetonitrile (Dry, Sigma-Aldrich) to form a suspension, which was stirred at 800 rpm for 30 h. The solvent was then removed by vacuum drying at 120 °C, yielding a precursor powder. This powder was subsequently sintered at 220 °C for 2 h to produce the final electrolytes. For LSPSx (x > 0) electrolyte, Li2S, P2S5, and SiS2 (99.99%, Macklin) were mixed in the appropriate stoichiometric ratio in acetonitrile and stirred under the same conditions (800 rpm, 30 h). After vacuum drying at 120 °C, the resulting precursor was sintered at 550 °C for 10 h to obtain the final electrolyte. The Li7.25Si0.25P1.75S8I (LSPSI) electrolyte was prepared using stoichiometric amounts of Li2S, P2S5, SiS2, and LiI (99.95%, anhydrous, Alfa Aesar), which were processed under the same mixing and drying conditions. The precursor powder was then sintered at 450 °C for 5 h to yield the LSPSI electrolyte. As for the Li7P2S8I electrolyte, reagents (Li2S, P2S5, LiI) were processed using the same conditions as mentioned above. The precursor powder was then sintered at 280 °C for 2 h to yield the LPSI electrolyte. All the samples were heated to the target temperature at a rate of 5 °C min–1, held for the specified duration, and then naturally cooled to room temperature in the furnace. The LPSC electrolytes were purchased from Ganfeng Lithium Co., Ltd. All synthesis procedures were carried out in an argon-filled glovebox with O2 and H2O levels maintained below 1 ppm.

Sulfur-positive electrode preparation

The S/LSPS0.45 and S/LPSC composite positive electrodes were prepared using a two-step process. First, sulfur powders (Sigma-Aldrich, 98%) and carbon nanotubes (CNTs, Macklin) were mixed at a weight ratio of 3:1 by grinding, followed by annealing at 155 °C for 10 h to form the S/CNT composites under an inert atmosphere. Then, the as-prepared S/CNT composites were ball-milled with the corresponding electrolyte and CNTs in a weight ratio of 6:3.5:0.5 (S/CNT composite: electrolyte: additional CNT) at 500 rpm for 10 h to obtain the final composite electrode powders. Ball milling was performed on a RETSCH PM100 planetary ball mill using a 50 mL stainless-steel jar and stainless-steel balls (8 mm in diameter, 30 balls, total mass ~63.5 g), corresponding to a ball-to-powder mass ratio of 40:1. The milling program consisted of 10 min milling intervals separated by 5 min rest periods. The additional CNTs introduced during ball-milling serve to further reinforce the electronic percolation network throughout the electrode, ensuring efficient electron transport given the insulating nature of sulfur and its discharge products. Based on this formulation, the final electrode composite comprises 45 wt% sulfur, 35 wt% solid electrolyte, and 20 wt% total CNTs. To evaluate the redox behavior of solid electrolytes within the working voltage of the sulfur-positive electrode, C/LPSC and C/LSPS0.45 electrodes were prepared by mechanically mixing carbon (20 wt%) and solid electrolytes (80 wt%) in the same ball-milling procedure.

Cell assembly

All the cells used in this study were assembled in a Swagelok-type cell setup. The cell mold comprises two conductive die stainless-steel bars and a poly(ether ether-ketone) (PEEK) cylinder with an internal diameter of 10 mm. All the cells are assembled in an argon-filled glovebox with O2 and H2O levels maintained below 1 ppm. The temperature inside the glovebox is ~30–32 °C. The detailed information for different cell assemblies is listed below. (1) Blocking cells for conductivity measurements. Firstly, the testing solid electrolyte powders were cold-pressed inside the PEEK mold into a pellet (approximately 1.2 mm in thickness) under 360 MPa for 3 min. Then, two carbon-coated Al (C@Al) foils with a diameter of 10 mm were attached to both sides of the electrolyte pellet, forming a C@Al|solid electrolyte|C@Al structure. The C@Al|solid electrolyte|C@Al structure is further cold-pressed under 360 MPa for 3 min. (2) Li symmetric cells. In the case of Li|LSPS0.45 | Li cells, 120−140 mg of LSPS0.45 was cold‑pressed into a dense pellet (approximately 1.1 mm in thickness) under 360 MPa for 3 min, after which lithium metal foil (China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd.) with a diameter of 10 mm and a thickness of 100 μm was affixed to both sides to complete the symmetric structure. The process for Li|LPSC|Li symmetric cells followed the same procedure, replacing LSPS with LPSC (with a pellet thickness of ~1.05 mm). For Li|LSPSI/LSPS0.45/LSPSI|Li cells, a sandwiched structure was first constructed as follows. First, 20–40 mg of LSPSI powder was compressed under 360 MPa for 1 min to form a base layer, followed by deposition of 80−100 mg LSPS0.45 and a second cold-press under 360 MPa for another 1 min to yield an LSPSI/ LSPS0.45 bilayer. Finally, a further 20–40 mg of LSPSI was added on top and pressed again under 360 MPa for 3 min to complete the LSPSI/ LSPS0.45/LSPSI sandwiched structure. The total thickness of the sandwiched structure is ≈0.90 mm. Lithium metal foils were then attached to both faces of the sandwich. (3) All-solid-state lithium sulfur batteries. To construct configurations of LiIn|LPSC | S/LSPS0.45 and LiIn|LPSC | S/LPSC cells, 100−120 mg LPSC (thickness ≈0.94 mm) was first cold-pressed inside the PEEK mold into a pellet under 360 MPa for 3 min. Then, composite sulfur electrode powders were spread on one side of the LPSC electrolyte and pressed under 380 MPa for 5 min. The thickness of sulfur composites is ≈35 μm for loading of 2 mg cm–2 and is ≈ 85–90 μm for loading of 5 mg cm–2. Finally, a LiIn alloy foil (the molar ratio of Li:In = 0.5:1) with a diameter of 10 mm was attached to the negative electrode side. The LiIn alloy foil (~ 420 μm) was prepared by stacking Li foils (China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd.) and In foils (99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich) with a molar ratio of 1:2 under a stack pressure of 350 MPa. The Li|LSPSI(LPSC)/LSPS0.45 | S/LSPS0.45 cells were assembled following identical procedures, substituting the Li–In alloy by Li metal foil (China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd.) with a diameter of 10 mm and a thickness of 100 μm. The Li metal foil is used without further surface cleaning. In this cell configuration, the electrolyte thickness is ≈0.8–0.9 mm, and the positive electrode thickness is ≈35 μm.

Electrochemical test

Conductivity measurements of the blocking cells were performed on a PARSTAT MC multichannel electrochemical workstation within the frequency range of 1 Hz to 1 MHz under an amplitude of 20 mV. Time-resting EIS tests of Li symmetric cells were also conducted on a PARSTAT MC multichannel electrochemical workstation within the frequency range of 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz under an amplitude of 20 mV. Prior to each EIS measurement, the cell was kept at open-circuit potential for at least 30 min to reach a quasi-stationary state. CCD test and long-time Li plating/stripping measurement for Li symmetric cells were conducted on the Neware CT-4008 battery test system. Galvanostatic charge-discharge measurements of the assembled Li | |S (LiIn | |S) cells were performed on a Neware CT-4008 battery test system in a voltage range of 1.5–3.0 V (Li | |S) or 0.9–2.4 V (LiIn | |S). The cells were initially activated at a rate of 0.05 C (1 C = 1675 mA gsulfur−1) and then tested at a set current density. GITT tests were conducted with a 600 s discharge/charge process at 0.05 C (1 C = 1675 mA gsulfur−1), following an 1800 s relaxation process. The cell voltage was recorded at one data point per second. CV curves in the potential window of 1.5–3.0 V vs. Li/Li+ were conducted on a CHI 660E at a scan rate from 0.1 to 2 mV s–1. All the electrochemical tests other than conductivity measurements were conducted at a temperature of 30 ± 1 °C in ambient atmosphere. The LiIn | |S cells are tested under a stacking pressure of 50 MPa, while the Li | |S cells and the Li symmetric cells are tested under a stacking pressure of 10 MPa. All specific and areal capacities reported in this work are based on the mass of sulfur in the positive electrode. The gravimetric specific capacity (Cg, mAh g−1) was calculated as Cg = (I × t) / ms, where I is the applied current (mA), t is the discharge or charge time (h), and ms is the mass of sulfur (g). The areal capacity (Careal, mAh cm−2) was obtained from Careal = (I × t) / A, where A is the electrode geometric area (cm2).

Characterization

All the sample preparation processes were performed in an argon-filled glovebox with O2 and H2O levels maintained below 1 ppm. Powder XRD patterns were acquired using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54178 Å) across a 2θ angular range of 10–80° with a step size of 0.02° and a scan rate of 5° min–1. The powdered samples were placed in a zero-background sample holder and sealed with a Kapton film to minimize background scatter. Raman spectra were collected using a Renishaw inVia Reflex Raman spectrometer, with excitation provided by a 532 nm laser source. The laser power was maintained at 5 mW to avoid sample damage, and the spectra were recorded over a wavenumber range of 200–800 cm–1 with an acquisition time of 30 s and two accumulations. Raman samples were sealed with a Kapton film to avoid air exposure. Cryogenic TEM observations and the corresponding EDS of the LSPS precursors were conducted on Talos F200X G2 (ThermoFisher SCIENTIFIC) at a beam voltage of 200 kV with a cryo-stage at Westlake University. A small quantity of electrolyte particles was collected with a capillary tube and subsequently transferred onto a copper grid. The entire preparation procedure was carried out without the involvement of any solvents. The cryo-TEM samples were transferred from the glovebox to the microscope using a vacuum transfer holder to avoid air exposure during sample handling. XPS was conducted on a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha+ system. Powder samples and pellets were mounted onto the sample holder inside an Ar-filled glovebox and transferred to the spectrometer under an inert atmosphere to avoid air exposure. Binding energies were referenced to the adventitious C 1 s peak at 284.8 eV, and the spectra were acquired using monochromatic Al Kα X-ray radiation (1486.6 eV). The SEM characterizations were conducted using HITACHI SU8010 at a beam voltage of 5 kV. Sample preparation was performed inside an Ar-filled glovebox, followed by sealing the samples in aluminum–plastic laminate bags for transfer. During the transfer from the aluminum–plastic laminate bags to the SEM chamber, the samples were briefly exposed to air for approximately 10 s.

Calculation details

The ESP evaluation of Li4SiS4 and Li4P2S6 molecules was calculated by using the Gaussian 16 package. The molecular geometries were optimized by density functional theory at the B3LYP/6-311 G level. The charge analysis on S atoms was realized using the Multiwfn package.

AIMD calculations were carried out in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (version 5.4.4), employing the projector augmented-wave (PAW) method to describe electron–ion interactions, together with the generalized gradient approximation of Perdew, Burke, and Ernzerhof (PBE) for the exchange–correlation functional42,43. All calculations used a plane-wave cutoff of 280 eV and sampled the Brillouin zone at the Γ point only. As for Li-transport dynamics in representative electrolytes, the simulations were conducted in the NVT ensemble with a time step of 2 fs over a period of 40 ps at 600 K after being equilibrated for 4 ps. For the Li/LSPS and Li/LSPSI interfaces, the structural evolution was tracked in the NVT ensemble at 300 K, again using a 2 fs time step over a 40 ps trajectory. Radial distribution functions (RDFs) for the relevant atomic pairs were subsequently extracted with the vasppy package.
