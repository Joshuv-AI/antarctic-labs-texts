Addressing low-value care (LVC) in Asia: a narrative review of Choosing Wisely and other initiatives across Asia
Authors: Jia Xuan Yeo, Yasuharu Tokuda, Shu Sasaki, Jay Pan, Hanin Kamaruzaman, Priya Ranganathan, Liyong Lu, Aliza Mohamad Yusof, Azarinah Izaham, C S Pramesh, Hairil Rizal Abdullah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLow-value care (LVC) research originates predominantly from Western healthcare systems. This narrative review offers the first synthesis of LVC patterns, determinants and reduction initiatives across six diverse Asian countries (Japan, Singapore, South Korea, China, Malaysia and India), comparing them with Western models to inform context-specific de-implementation strategies and identify key priorities for further research.MethodsThis is a narrative review of 132 English and non-English literature (2000–2025) from academic databases, grey literature, and policy documents across the six countries that was analysed using a novel framework examining LVC landscapes (healthcare challenges and measurement), determinants (structural, organisational, clinician, patient) and reduction initiatives.ResultsAsian systems mirror Western pressures from demographic shifts, escalating costs and a growing burden of non-communicable diseases, underscoring the urgency of reducing LVC. Despite this, systematic measurement of LVC in Asia remains limited. Common LVC determinants include fee-for-service incentives, medicolegal concerns and a ‘more is better’ cultural perception, though further research, particularly on cross-country comparisons and from the patient’s perspective, is needed. LVC reduction initiatives are still emerging in Asia, with existing approaches shaped by local cultural norms and resource constraints, suggesting that Western strategies provide valuable insights but require adaptation for successful local de-implementation.ConclusionsThis multicountry review establishes a foundation for further collaborative LVC research in Asia via a holistic framework linking country-specific contexts to tailor de-implementation strategies. Key priorities for LVC research in Asia include systematic LVC quantification, development of contextualised evidence-based recommendations and implementation of contextualised multicomponent interventions, supported by further research and regional collaboration.Keywords: Clinical practice guidelines, Health policy, Implementation science

Introduction

Low-value care (LVC), where costs of medical services outweigh their expected benefits,1 has been estimated to constitute 30% of healthcare use in high-income settings.2 With global healthcare spending outpacing economic growth,3 curbing avoidable expenditure from LVC,4 which diverts resources away from essential care and subjects patients to avoidable harm,5 has become an urgent priority for both patient safety5 and health system sustainability.6

LVC research has grown in recent years, encompassing diverse measurement methodologies7,9 yielding widely varying estimates of its economic burden in Western countries.10 11 Multiple drivers have also been identified, including cultural beliefs that ‘more is better’, limited health literacy, clinicians’ medico-legal concerns and systemic incentives like fee-for-service (FFS) payment.12,14 However, the relative importance of these factors differs across sociocultural and health system contexts,11 suggesting that strategies effective in one setting may not be directly transferable to another.

Global initiatives to address LVC have emerged, notably the Choosing Wisely campaign. Initially focused on developing specialty-specific lists of commonly overused practices,5 15 it has since been adopted in >20 countries and expanded to include de-implementation strategies.16 However, evidence on de-implementation outcomes and real-world effectiveness remains limited.

Moreover, most published data on LVC originate from Western or high-income countries,4 with significant gaps in Asia, a region with significant socioeconomic diversity and rapidly evolving healthcare systems.17 18 Addressing these gaps requires a comprehensive synthesis of LVC-related evidence from Asia to inform context-appropriate strategies for LVC reduction.

This narrative review, conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines,19 represents the first attempt to examine the current evidence on LVC in Asia, identify its key drivers and existing reduction initiatives to highlight context-specific considerations to inform strategies and priority areas for future research.

Six countries—China, India, Japan, Singapore, South Korea and Malaysia—were selected to capture diverse health systems17 18 and economic contexts.20 Authors were connected through the Choosing Wisely International network and were actively involved in LVC research or initiatives, providing a purposive sample with country-specific expertise.

Academic and grey literature (2000–2025) in English, Japanese, Korean and Mandarin were analysed across major regional databases and policy sources. Search terms reflected various established LVC terminologies while avoiding overly-broad concepts. Inclusion criteria encompassed all study designs and policy documents explicitly addressing LVC, while non-Asia-specific cost-effectiveness analyses without reference to LVC and duplicate materials were excluded. In total, 102 sources were included.

Country-level narrative reviews applied a shared analytic framework (table 2), and cross-country synthesis was iteratively validated by all authors to ensure accuracy and mitigate bias.

Across the six countries, reducing LVC is a shared policy priority, though motivations differ by economic context. Almost all countries face similar challenges from ageing populations, rising healthcare expenditure and increasing burden of non-communicable disease, mirroring global patterns.21 22 However, these pressures shape the imperative for LVC reduction differently. In high-income countries, reducing LVC is framed as a forward-looking strategy to strengthen healthcare sustainability. In contrast, for upper-middle-income economies with comparatively fewer resources, rising healthcare expenditure and utilisation that do not yield proportional health gains or even generate public-health risks make reducing LVC a priority to improve efficiency and redirect resources towards higher-value care. India, as a lower-middle-income country, faces acute resource constraints that make avoiding LVC essential, yet these same constraints restrict the capacity for de-implementation efforts.

Systematic LVC measurement is limited in Asia. Japan is the only country with broad LVC quantification across utilisation and expenditure and South Korea has an isolated patient survey, while data from the other countries are limited to specific tests, medications, diseases or specialties. Barriers cited include technical and institutional challenges and the lack of contextualised guidelines for local LVC measurement, though further research is required to fully understand contributing factors.

Across the six Asian countries, there is an even representation of healthcare provision models, spanning predominantly privately funded systems in high-income settings (Japan and South Korea), where regulation is used to curb overpricing; largely publicly funded systems in high- and upper-middle-income contexts (Singapore and China); and mixed public-private systems in middle-income countries (Malaysia and India). Despite these differences, private sector involvement in outpatient care is substantial across all systems, with FFS payment predominating in privately financed systems or sectors. Universal health coverage is broadly supported by social health insurance and tax financing across all countries; however, full coverage has been achieved in all except India and China, where gaps in coverage persist but are narrowing. Nevertheless, out of pocket spending remains substantial in settings where UHC is incomplete or insurance coverage is limited,

Asian and Western settings share common LVC drivers at structural, organisational, clinician, and patient levels, including FFS payment models, medicolegal concerns, high workload, knowledge gaps, and a ‘more is better’ mindset.23,25 However, patterns of LVC determinants in Asia remain poorly understood, including cross-country differences and the role of healthcare context. While Western literature reports inconsistent associations for factors such as practice location and patient or physician characteristics,23 25 these have been examined in only a few Asian studies, and the lack of systematic investigation limits cross-country comparability and understanding of their relative influence.

Two notable Asia-specific determinants emerged from this review. First, the absence of contextualised frameworks, including explicitly de-implementation-focused guidelines and guidance tailored to the resource context of middle-income countries, hampers providers’ ability to deliver value-based care or engage in shared decision making to reduce LVC. Second, hierarchical medical culture was also cited, consistent with evidence that Asian contexts exhibit higher power distance than Western settings.26 Hierarchy has been reported to prevent junior clinicians from speaking up,27 representing a barrier that may be more pronounced in Asia and limiting junior-doctor-led approaches to de-implementing LVC.

Lastly, systematic research on patients’ perspectives on LVC determinants in Asia remains limited, yet such insights are likely crucial given potential differences in patient perceptions compared with Western countries. While shared decision making underpins Choosing Wisely initiatives, doctor-patient interactions in Asia may be more physician-directed than the bilateral discussion commonly observed in the West.28 This highlights the urgent need to study patient perspectives in Asia to contextualise shared decision making and strengthen LVC de-implementation.

Half of the sampled Asian countries have established national Choosing Wisely campaigns with published recommendations. However, these initiatives remain nascent compared with Western counterparts, with notable variability in the number of specialties engaged and the extent of systematic de-implementation. In countries without national programmes, de-implementation has been driven by specialty-led or institution-led initiatives.

Although the absence of contextualised guidelines is a common barrier to identifying areas of LVC, emerging local research provides a promising alternative approach. In de-implementation, Asian countries can also draw on Western experiences, where active, multimodal interventions were substantially more effective than passive guideline dissemination,29,31 while adapting strategies to regional cultures and resource constraints. Incorporating local patient perspectives is essential for co-designing de-implementation approaches tailored to specific health systems.

While Choosing Wisely emphasises physician-led approaches over payor- or government-led models, macro-level interventions including payment reforms and value-based policies have been identified as influential levers to reduce LVC in Asia. Systemic reforms and inter-agency coordination are also consistently highlighted as priorities across the six countries. These observations suggest the potential of unique Asian models that integrate clinician-led initiatives like Choosing Wisely with government policy, aligning efforts to improve patient outcomes while avoiding the perception of a cost-cutting programme, in contrast to the independent, physician-led approach typical in the West.

Discussion

This is the first comprehensive analysis of both English and non-English literature on LVC patterns across diverse Asian healthcare systems. Most published studies focus on a single analytic component and offer limited insight into how contextual factors shape LVC determinants and subsequent de-implementation initiatives. In contrast, this review proposes a three-component conceptual framework that links local healthcare landscapes and LVC determinants to explain existing de-implementation strategies and associated barriers.

Western frameworks, such as the Choosing Wisely De-Implementation Framework (CWDIF),32 are designed for systematic reduction of specific tests or treatments where recommendations and CW campaigns are already established. However, in Asia, CW campaigns are nascent and recommendations remain scarce. This proposed framework is therefore better suited to map the broader healthcare landscape, including identifying system-level interventions and elucidating unique contextual factors in settings where knowledge on LVC remains limited. Nonetheless, CWDIF remains applicable in more mature Asian settings or specific clinical areas where contextualised recommendations exist and the focus is on de-implementation of a particular test or treatment. Together, the two frameworks can complement: this study’s framework provides a heuristic for understanding nascent initiatives and systemic barriers, while CWDIF can guide targeted action once the local evidence base is sufficiently developed.

As non-English literature was crucial for understanding LVC in Asia, this review drew on local expertise to identify relevant initiatives, interpret findings, and ensure contextual accuracy. The involvement of only one to three contributing authors per country, recruited through a shared professional network, may introduce selection bias, particularly in how LVC is framed within national healthcare challenges and priorities. Nonetheless, this network represents the only known regional community actively engaged in LVC and de-implementation research, and cross-verification against published literature helps mitigate this limitation.

The discussion is also limited by heterogeneity in the scope and scale of available literature, as most studies are small or descriptive and lack systematic analyses of LVC determinants. To address this, the search inclusion period was expanded from 2015–2025 to 2000–2025 to capture more articles and provide a fuller understanding. This reflects the current state of evidence rather than a methodological gap. As LVC research in Asia grows, follow-up systematic reviews stratified by study design and methodology are warranted.

Additionally, the six-country sample does not fully reflect the diversity of health systems across Asia. Nevertheless, the proposed analytic components are likely adaptable to other settings and can be refined as new evidence emerges. Future research should investigate patterns of LVC determinants in Asia, including cross-country differences and the influence of healthcare context, and identify behavioural, organisational and policy levers effective for de-implementation in these settings.

Conclusions

This review provides the first integrated synthesis of English and non-English literature on LVC across multiple Asian health systems and introduces a conceptual framework to interpret LVC patterns in settings where evidence remains limited. By linking healthcare system characteristics, LVC determinants and emerging de-implementation efforts, the framework offers a pragmatic structure for identifying unique context-specific barriers and informing the development of locally appropriate strategies.

The findings highlight the need to strengthen locally generated evidence and to account for cultural and resource constraints when adapting de-implementation approaches in Asia. They also underscore a pressing need for systematic evaluations and comparative studies to deepen understanding of LVC in Asia and support national LVC measurement, guide the development of contextualised, evidence-based guidelines and inform multicomponent interventions tailored to local contexts. In addition, the findings point to a distinct opportunity for clinician-policymaker collaboration to improve patient outcomes without positioning LVC reduction as a cost-cutting exercise.

Beyond its analytic contribution, this work demonstrates the value of regional collaboration in consolidating fragmented evidence and strengthening capacity for LVC and de-implementation research. It provides a foundational reference for advancing coordinated, contextually informed efforts to reduce LVC and improve healthcare value across the region.
