Understanding Generalization of Federated Learning: the Trade-off between Model Stability and Optimization
Dun Zeng ∗, Zheshun Wu ∗, Shiyu Liu, Yu Pan, Xiaoying Tang, Zenglin Xu

Abstract
Federated Learning (FL) is a distributed learning approach that trains machine learning models across multiple devices while keeping their local data private. However, FL often faces challenges due to data heterogeneity, leading to inconsistent local optima among clients. These inconsistencies can cause unfavorable convergence behavior and generalization performance degradation. Existing studies mainly describe this issue through convergence analysis , focusing on how well a model fits training data, or through algorithmic stability , which examines the generalization gap. However, neither approach precisely captures the generalization performance of FL algorithms, especially for neural networks. This paper introduces an innovative generalization dynamics analysis framework, named as Libra, for algorithm-dependent excess risk minimization, highlighting the trade-offs between model stability and optimization. Through this framework, we show how the generalization of FL algorithms is affected by the interplay of algorithmic stability and optimization. This framework applies to standard federated optimization and its advanced variants, such as server momentum. Our findings suggest that larger local steps or momentum accelerate convergence but enlarge stability, while yielding a better minimum excess risk. These insights can guide the design of future algorithms to achieve stronger generalization.

Introduction

Federated Learning (FL) has become a new solution for training AI models on distributed and private data
(
McMahan et al. 2017
;
Kairouz et al. 2021
)
. Traditional centralized learning approaches require data to be gathered in one place, posing risks to privacy and security
(
Voigt & Von dem Bussche 2017
;
Li et al. 2019a
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
. In response, FL allows models to be trained directly on local devices, keeping raw data decentralized. This paradigm has enabled many applications in fields like healthcare
(
Antunes et al. 2022
)
, finance
(
Long et al. 2020
)
, and IoT
(
Nguyen et al. 2021
)
, where protecting sensitive data is crucial. However, FL often faces significant performance challenges due to heterogeneous data in real-world scenarios. Many experimental and theoretical results of FL have observed that the heterogeneity issues degenerate the FL performance on both training behavior and testing accuracy
(
Huang et al. 2023
)
. Therefore, numerous studies in FL have been proposed to alleviate the negative impacts of heterogeneity.

However, most existing studies of FL only emphasize convergence to empirical optimal solutions based on training datasets
(
Reddi et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2022b
;
Li et al. 2019c
)
and usually ignore their generalization properties. In response, recent generalization studies of FL
(
Sun et al. 2024c
;
Sun et al. 2024b
)
raise this important issue of generalization in the FL community.
Although their generalization analysis is established on the most popular technique called
Uniform Stability

(
Hardt et al. 2016
)
, they usually do not work well under general non-convex regimes
(
Chen et al. 2018
;
Charles & Papailiopoulos 2018
;
Zhou et al. 2018
;
Li et al. 2019b
)
. Unfortunately, modern neural networks, the most advanced techniques in the era of artificial intelligence, are also non-convex. Therefore, understanding the generalization of FL under non-convex regimes is essential for future applications.
Analyzing the
generalization dynamics
is a feasible solution to understanding the generalization of non-convex FL
(
Teng et al. 2021
)
. This generalization dynamics measures the generalization gap during the neural network training process. However, the previous uniform stability analyses are less practical. They ignored the gradient norm dynamics in neural network training which is non-zero. On the other hand, the convergence analysis of non-convex FL only focuses on the gradient norm, however, it omits the generalization dynamics. Therefore, the existing approach cannot precisely describe the generalization properties.

Related Work

Generalization theory.

Generalization in learning theory has been a focus for decades. Uniform convergence, which uses VC dimension or Rademacher complexity to bound generalization error, is a common approach
(
Shalev-Shwartz et al. 2010
;
Yin et al. 2019
)
. However, this method often provides less meaningful bounds for neural networks
(
Zhang et al. 2021
)
because it only considers the model class, not the training algorithms used to generate the models.
PAC-Bayes learning is another valuable approach for analyzing generalization errors in neural networks
(
Dziugaite & Roy 2017
)
. It has been used to analyze bounds for stochastic optimization algorithms
(
Pensia et al. 2018
;
Neu et al. 2021
)
. Similar to the PAC-Bayes framework,
algorithmic stability
is increasingly used in recent analyses of randomized learning algorithms
(
Hardt et al. 2016
;
Lei & Ying 2020
)
. This concept measures how sensitive the loss function is to changes in the training data. Stability analysis has been extended to various areas, including randomized learning algorithms
(
Elisseeff et al. 2005
)
, transfer learning
(
Kuzborskij & Lampert 2018
)
, and privacy-preserving learning
(
Dwork & Feldman 2018
)
. It can derive tighter generalization bounds in expectation.

Generalization theory in FL.

Many works have studied the properties of generalization in FL. Most works derived PAC-Bayes or information-theoretic bounds for the general FL paradigm using the Donsker-Varadhan variational representation
(
Wu et al. 2023a
;
Wu et al. 2023b
;
Barnes et al. 2022
)
. Based on the theories, corresponding works
(
Reisizadeh et al. 2020
)
mainly developed robust FL methods to enhance generalization performance against heterogeneous clients. Moreover,
Qu et al. 2022
proposes to improve the flatness of loss landscape by adopting the sharpness aware minimization (SAM) optimizer on FL. Follow-up works
(
Caldarola et al. 2022
;
Sun et al. 2023
;
Shi et al. 2023
)
propose some variants based on SAM that can improve FL performance. However, these works only focus on the final generalization performance of the FL model, while ignoring the training dynamics during the federated learning procedure. Hence, there are lack of understanding of key factors affecting the generalization errors at the algorithm level. To the best of our knowledge, only two works provide explicit analysis for algorithmic stability in FL.
Sun et al. 2024c
provides a straightforward extension of work
(
Hardt et al. 2016
)
for FedAvg
(
McMahan et al. 2017
)
, FedProx
(
Li et al. 2020
)
, and SCAFFOLD
(
Karimireddy et al. 2020
)
, highlighting the impacts of data heterogeneity on generalization.
Sun et al. 2024b
mainly prove the stability of the proposed algorithm FedInit and show its generalization error bound is also dominated by heterogeneity issues. And, FedInit simply plus optimal convergence rate and stability for denoting excess risk bound. However, the optimal convergence rate and stability cannot obtained simultaneously as they rely on different hyperparameters of the training algorithm. Hence, it omitted the interplay between convergence and stability, inaccurately guiding neural network training.

Problem Formulation

We consider a standard FL setting with
N
clients connected to one aggregator. The basic problem in FL is to learn a prediction function parameterized by
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
to minimize the following global population risk:

F(\boldsymbol{x}):=\frac{1}{N}{\sum}_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{P}_{i}}[\ell(\boldsymbol{x};z)],

(1)

where
\mathcal{P}_{i}
is the underlying data distribution on client
i
and may differ across different clients,
\ell(\boldsymbol{x};z):\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is some nonnegative loss function,
z
denotes the sample from the sample space
\mathcal{Z}
.

Since
\mathcal{P}_{i}
is unknown typically, the global population risk
F(\boldsymbol{x})
cannot be computed. Instead, we can access to a training dataset
\mathcal{D}=\cup_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{D}_{i}
, where
\mathcal{D}_{i}=\{z_{j}^{(i)}\}_{j=1}^{n_{i}}
is the training dataset on client
i
with size
n_{i}
. Then, we can estimate
F(\boldsymbol{x})
using global empirical risk:

f(\boldsymbol{x}):=\frac{1}{N}{\sum}_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x}),

(2)

where
f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{n_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\ell(\boldsymbol{x};z_{j}^{(i)})
is the local empirical risk on client
i
,
z_{j}^{(i)}\overset{\text{ i.i.d. }}{\sim}\mathcal{P}_{i}
represents the sample from local data distribution
\mathcal{P}_{i}
.

In practice, a family of algorithms to solve (
2
) has been proposed
(
Hsu et al. 2019
;
Reddi et al. 2020
;
Xu et al. 2021
;
Zeng et al. 2023b
;
Wang et al. 2020
)
, which take the form of alternating optimization between clients and server:

Client:

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{t,k+1}=\textsc{ClientOpt}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{t,k},\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{t,k},\eta_{l});

Server:

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1}=\textsc{ServerOpt}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t},\boldsymbol{d}^{t},\eta_{g}),

where
ClientOpt
and
ServerOpt
are gradient-based optimizers with learning rates
\eta_{l}
and
\eta_{g}
respectively. Essentially,
ClientOpt
aims to minimize the loss on each client’s local data with stochastic gradient
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{t,k}
, while
ServerOpt
updates the global model based on the aggregated clients’ model updates
\boldsymbol{d}^{t}
. For the simplicity of the following analysis, we assume that both
ClientOpt
and
ServerOpt
are in the form of SGD. See Algorithm
1
for more details.

Excess risk.

Understanding the mechanism of this basic FL algorithm is vital for designing efficient and generalizable algorithms
(
Sun et al. 2024b
;
Teng et al. 2021
)
. To this end, we are interested in the
excess risk

\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{x}):=F(\boldsymbol{x})-F(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})
, where
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}:=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}F(\boldsymbol{x})
denotes the population risk minimizer. Noting that the excess risk measures how the learned parameter
\boldsymbol{x}
performs on the population risk, it can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=F(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{x})+f(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle+\underbrace{f(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})-f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})}_{\leq 0\text{ under ERM }}+\underbrace{f(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})-F(\boldsymbol{x}^{*})}_{\approx 0~\text{by concentration inequality}},

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
denotes the empirical risk minimizer (ERM). Empirically, the last two terms of the above equation are algorithm-independent
(
Teng et al. 2021
)
. To provide deeper insights on neural networks training, we prefer to study the
empirical excess risk
related to learned parameter
\boldsymbol{x}
and optimization algorithms:

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})=\underbrace{F(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{x})}_{\mathcal{E}_{G}:\text{ generalization gap}}+\underbrace{f(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})}_{\mathcal{E}_{O}:\text{ optimization error }},

(3)

where the first term
\mathcal{E}_{G}
denotes the estimation error due to the approximation of the unknown data distribution
\mathcal{P}
. The second term
\mathcal{E}_{O}
means the optimization error based on training data
\mathcal{D}
.

Libra: Trade-off Stability and Convergence

This section elaborates on the details of the Libra framework, which measures the excess risk of models during any given learning procedures covered by Algorithm
1
.

4.1
Establishing Excess Risk Dynamics

Algorithmic stability.
is one of the most popular theoretical frameworks for analyzing generalization gap
(
Hardt et al. 2016
;
Lei & Ying 2020
)
. Our analysis relies on the
on-average model stability

(
Lei & Ying 2020
)
, which gives tighter bounds than
uniform stability

(
Hardt et al. 2016
)
.
In detail, model stability means any perturbation of samples on datasets cannot lead to a big change in the model trained by the algorithm in expectation. Formally, the upper bound of generalization gap
\mathcal{E}_{G}(\boldsymbol{x})
is related to model stability:

Theorem 4.1

(Modified generalization gap from Theorem 2 in
Lei & Ying 2020
)
.

Let
\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
differ in at most one data sample (perturbed sample). For the two models
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
trained by a learning algorithm on these two sets, if function
f
is
L
-smooth, the generalization gap is upper bounded:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{G}(\boldsymbol{x})\leq\frac{L+\gamma}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}}[\|\boldsymbol{x}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\|^{2}]+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})\|^{2},

(4)

where
\gamma>0
is a free parameter for tuning depending on the property of problems.

Remark 4.1
.

Compared with the original theory, we removed the Holder continuity assumption and replaced its empirical loss term with the gradient norm, as shown in Appendix A. Under the Holder-continuity condition, the model stability dominates the generalization gap
\mathcal{E}_{G}
since
\mathcal{E}_{O}
is considered minor. Hence, the original theory proves that if an algorithm is stable, its excess risk is small. In contrast, the theorem above emphasizes that the generalization gap is also subjected to the gradient norm. As the gradient norm
\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})\|^{2}
typically is non-zero in neural network training, omitting the effects of gradient norms may lead to suboptimal results in algorithmic stability analysis.

In this work, we introduce a new quantity termed as
excess risk dynamics
for generalization analysis:

Corollary 4.1

(Excess risk dynamics)
.

Under conditions of Theorem
4.1
, an training algorithm creates a model trajectory
\{\boldsymbol{x}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
on dataset
\mathcal{D}
, and a virtual trajectory
\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
on datasets
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. Substituting (
4
) into (
3
) and using
\mathcal{E}_{O}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\leq\frac{1}{2\mu}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}
(
\mu
-PL condition), the excess risk dynamics is bounded by the sum of model stability and gradient norm as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\leq\frac{L+\gamma}{2}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\|^{2}}_{\text{Model stability}}+(\frac{1}{2\gamma}+\frac{1}{2\mu})\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}}_{\text{Gradient norm}}.

The gradient norm of the non-convex training process is related to its convergence status. To see this, we assume the risk
f
is
\mu
-PL condition, which is a practical assumption in non-convex functions
(
Jain et al. 2017
)
. As the empirical optimal
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
typically considered unknown in the non-convex analysis, it allows us to use the gradient norm
\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})\|^{2}
to bound the optimization error
\mathcal{E}_{O}(\boldsymbol{x})
.

Remark 4.2

(Conflicts between stability and optimization)
.

The model stability and gradient norm conflict in excess risk bound. Concretely, the stability expands while the gradient norm decreases over model iteration as illustrated in Figure
1
. From the generalization perspective, we expected the model stability bounds to grow slowly (e.g., model stability of vanilla SGD typically grows at a speed of
\mathcal{O}(T^{c})

(
Hardt et al. 2016
)
). That is, a smaller difference between the true sequence and the virtual sequence indicates better model stability and a lower generalization gap.
In contrast, the gradient norm should decrease fast in non-convex convergence analysis, indicating that the model is fitting training datasets well. And, the convergence speed roughly matches
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{T})

(
Reddi et al. 2020
)
.

We aim to bound the
minimum excess risk dynamics
of a model iteration trajectory, which is not fully characterized:

Definition 4.1

(Minimum excess risk dynamics)
.

Given an iterative learning algorithm with proper hyperparameters, it yields a model trajectory
\{\boldsymbol{x}\}_{t=0}^{T}
. The model achieves the minimum excess risk overall time at a specific time point. We define the excess risk at the specific time as the minimum excess risk dynamics termed as
\mathcal{E}_{\text{min}}=\min_{t\in[T]}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})
.

The minimum excess risk dynamics indicate the best generalization performance in a model training procedure. Understanding the minimum excess risk bound is to identify the algorithm-dependent factors that affect the models’ generalization. It is also crucial to design FL algorithms that are both efficient and generalizable, which is the primary motivation for this work.

Model Stability and Convergence Analysis

In this section, we provide the model stability and convergence analysis of Algorithm
1
with detailed proof provided in Appendix B. Our analysis additionally relies on common assumptions on local stochastic gradients
(
Reddi et al. 2020
;
Sun et al. 2024b
;
Zeng et al. 2023b
)
:

Assumption 4.1
.

The stochastic gradient
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}
(Line 6 in Algorithm

) is unbiased, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{g}_{i}\right]=\nabla f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})
. And, the variance of
\boldsymbol{g}_{i}
is
\sigma_{l}
-bounded (local) variance as
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{g}_{i}-\nabla f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\sigma_{l}^{2}
, for all
i\in[N],z\in\mathcal{D}_{i}
. Here, the expectation is taken over the randomness of SGD.

Assumption 4.2
.

The global variance is bounded as
\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})\right\|^{2}\leq\sigma_{g}^{2}
for all
i
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

Now, we provide the FL global model stability as follows:

Theorem 4.2

(Federated global model stability)
.

Under conditions of Theorem
4.1
and Assumptions
4.1

4.2

K
, batch size and
\eta_{l}=\Theta(\frac{1}{KL})
for local mini-batch SGD, there is a global learning rate
\eta_{g}\leq\sqrt{\frac{c}{t}},c>0
such that the global model stability is expansive in expectation at the speed:

\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{T}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}\|^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(K\sigma_{n}^{2}\cdot T^{c\psi}\right),

where
\sigma_{n}^{2}=\sigma_{l}^{2}+\sigma_{g}^{2}/n
and
\psi=(1+4\eta_{l}L)^{K}\in(1,2)
.

Sketch of proof

The vanilla algorithmic stability theory analyzes the perturbation of data samples in the training datasets. In FL, the training datasets are decentralized and distributed across clients. Noting that Algorithm
1
consists of local multi-step SGD and global SGD, we consider the global model’s stability inherits the local models’ stability. Technically, we provide the local stability analysis in Lemma
B.5
. Then, we derive the global model stability by taking the local model stability inherited by the global model during local updates aggregation steps in FL.

Compared with existing algorithmic stability.

This theorem proves that the federated global model stability expands over time
T
.
Compared with vanilla algorithmic stability of SGD
(
Hardt et al. 2016
;
Lei & Ying 2020
;
Lei et al. 2023
)
, it matches the rate of
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
w.r.t the size of dataset. Beyond the rate of dataset size, the model stability is enlarged by local gradient variance
\sigma_{l}^{2}
, and global variance
\sigma_{g}^{2}
. It means that data heterogeneity across clients worsens the stability of FL algorithms, which matches previous works
(
Sun et al. 2024c
)
. In contrast, our theorem first shows that the model stability is linearly scaled by local update steps

. This emphasizes that if the FL system utilizes large

for communication efficiency
(
McMahan et al. 2017
)
, it potentially induces a large generalization gap.

1
for notation consistency. Our analysis roughly follows previous non-convex federated optimization works
(

;
Reddi et al. 2020

Jhunjhunwala et al. 2022
)
with slight modifications for adapting our mild assumptions and notations in model stability.

4.3
Excess Risk Dynamics Minimization

This section provides a trade-off analysis between the gradient norm and model stability dynamics, outputted by the Libra framework.
Then, we discuss deep insights into excess risk minimization, especially focusing on the algorithm-dependent factors, such as the global learning rate
\eta_{g}
and local update step
K
. Please refer to Appendix
B
for the technical details of Libra.

We emphasize that
model stability and convergence analysis examine the same training process from different perspectives, and the algorithm-dependent hyperparameters should be jointly considered to achieve a practical outcome.

Hence, we minimize the upper bound of excess risk by tuning the global stepsize
\eta_{g}
for Corrolary
4.1
.
As a result, we derives an
explicit
upper bound of minimum excess risk for Algorithm
1
with resolved trade-off:

Theorem 4.4
.

Aligning with the conditions of Theorem
4.2

4.3
, for an model trajectory
\{\boldsymbol{x}^{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
generated on the server by Algorithm
1
. There is a constant global learning rate
\eta_{g}\leq\sqrt{\frac{c}{T}},c>0
such that
\mathcal{E}_{\text{min}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\text{min}}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{K}^{2}D}{KT}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma_{K}^{2}}{T}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}D^{2}}{Kc}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)^{\frac{1-c\psi}{3}}+\frac{D}{K\sqrt{Tc}}\right).

Sketch of Libra

We derive that the gradien dynamics
\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}
basically follows that
\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}\leq r_{0}/(t\eta_{g})+r_{1}\eta_{g}
. Analogously, we also know that
\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\|^{2}\leq r_{2}\eta_{g}^{2}
.
Libra is required to analyze algorithm-dependent terms
r_{0},r_{1},r_{2}
according to the given learning methods. According to Corollary
4.1
, we roughly have an algorithm-dependent excess risk dynamics
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\leq r_{0}/(t\eta_{g})+r_{1}\eta_{g}+r_{2}\eta_{g}^{2}
.
Noting that
\mathcal{E}_{\text{min}}\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})
, minimizing the summarized excess risk dynamics by Lemma

obtains the final results.

The theorem proves the upper bound of minimum excess risk that Algorithm
1
possibly achieves with proper hyperparameters. In intuition, the above theorem also shows that the test loss (generalization performance) always decays slower than train loss due to the model stability terms. And, we argue that the algorithm-dependent hyperparameters should be set for minimizing excess risk dynamics instead of optimization error, especially in modern neural network training.
Considering the trade-off relation, we intend to discuss three statuses of fitting: under-fitting, benign-fitting, and over-fitting as illustrated in Figure
1
, and provide deep insights into understanding model status in the training process.

Global learning rate matters to FL generalization.

The selection of
\eta_{g}
coordinates the model stability and gradient norm dynamics in the excess risk.
If we expected the excess risk to decrease stably during the
T^{*}
training rounds, the
\eta_{g}
should be smaller than a constant
\sqrt{c/T^{*}}
. Under the ideal conditions, the training process could achieve the minimum excess risk at an expected time
T^{*}
as proved in Theorem
4.4
.
And, we say it is benign-fitting if the minimum excess risk is obtained at time
T^{*}
. For all rounds before
T^{*}
, we say they are under-fitting as the gradient norm dynamics dominate the excess risk dynamics. Moreover, if the algorithm continues running after time
T^{*}
, the model stability dynamics dominate the excess risk dynamics. Then, we call the afterward period overfitting.

Large global learning rate makes FL overfit early.

Given a training procedure with all hyperparameters set except
\eta_{g}
, the benign-fitting time
T^{*}
is highly related to
\eta_{g}
. At an early stage of a training process, the gradient norm typically decreases faster than the expansion of model stability. Therefore, relatively large
\eta_{g}
is useful for generalization improvement during early training. However, if we set a large
\eta_{g}\leq\sqrt{c/\tilde{T}}
where
\tilde{T}<T^{*}
, then the training process is supposed to start over-fitting around early time
\tilde{T}
. This is because the condition about
\eta_{g}
in Theorem
4.4
is no longer satisfied for the round after
\tilde{T}
. From the trade-off perspective, a large global learning rate makes the model stability expansive very fast as shown in Lemma
B.6
. Therefore, the model stability dominates the excess risk bound earlier with larger
\eta_{g}
. Hence, the excess risk stops decreasing and the model starts over-fitting.

Global learning rate decay makes FL generalization better.

Common beliefs in how learning rate decay works come from the optimization analysis of (S)GD
(
You et al. 2019
;
Kalimeris et al. 2019
;
Ge et al. 2019
)
: 1) an initially large learning rate accelerates training or helps the network escape spurious local minima; 2) decaying the learning rate helps the network converge to a local minimum and avoid oscillation. According to our theory, we provide another explanation:
learning rate decay helps the excess risk dynamics decrease stably over a sufficiently long period of the training process
. In detail, a small learning rate makes the Theorem
4.4
hold after even numerous rounds. Supposing an ideal learning rate schedule rule, it can continually balance the relation between gradient norm and model stability, keeping the model in a benign-fitting state. Moreover, we surprisingly find our theories match recent practice in
Local SGD

(
Gu et al. 2023
)
, which shows a small learning rate, long enough training time, and proper local SGD steps can improve generalization.

Trade-off on Hyperparameters
K
.

Local update steps
K
are considered a hyperparameter of FL (Local SGD). It is a vital trade-off factor in our theory. For example, the convergence rate suggests clients conduct more steps of local updates for fast convergence. In contrast, large local steps also worsen federated global stability as shown in Theorem
4.2
. Thus, Theorem
4.2
and
4.3
show another naive trade-off between model stability and convergence rate on local update step
K
. Setting a learning rate conditioned on the
K
can resolve the trade-off, i.e., setting large
K
induces tighter excess risk as shown in Theorem
4.4
. However, in an FL system with great system heterogeneity, the local update steps can be unstable due to heterogeneous computation power across clients. In practice, we may carefully tune the
K
for generalization improvement. Libra can be a feasible framework for future work to estimate the best local steps for clients for generalization enhancement.

6
Limitation & Conclusion

This work focuses on assessing Libra within the FL context, though its principles could extend to other optimization methods. We do not propose specific strategies for improving FL algorithms, as the emphasis is on analyzing Libra’s theoretical properties. Our theories are built on a simplified FL setting with full client participation and identical local training configurations. Future research could explore the impact of system heterogeneity on FL generalization and ways to optimize system efficiency in realistic scenarios.

Overall, this paper proposes a novel framework Libra to analyze the minimum excess risk dynamics in general federated optimization methods. We prove that jointly optimizing the model stability and optimization trade-off can better describe the excess risk dynamics. Experiments evaluate the efficacy of theories and guide future excess risk analysis and FL practices. Importantly, we believe that Libra can greatly enhance the understanding of generalization in neural network training for future works.

Generalization by On-average Model Stability

Model stability
(
Lei & Ying 2020
)
means any perturbation of samples across all clients cannot lead to a big change in the model trained by the algorithm in expectation.
It measures the upper bound of algorithm results (final model) differences trained on similar datasets. And, these similar datasets for evaluating stability are called
Neighbor datasets

(
Hardt et al. 2016
;
Sun et al. 2024c
)
:

Definition A.1

(Neighbor datasets)
.

Given a global dataset
\mathcal{D}=\cup_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{D}_{i}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\}
, where
\mathcal{D}_{i}
is the local dataset of the
i
-th client with
|\mathcal{D}_{i}|=n_{i},\forall i\in[N]
. Another global dataset

be drawn independently from
\mathcal{Z}
such that
z_{j},\tilde{z}_{j}\sim\mathcal{P}_{i}
if
z_{j}\in\mathcal{D}_{i}
.
For any
j=1,\dots,n
, we define
\mathcal{D}^{(j)}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{j-1},\tilde{z}_{j},z_{j+1},\dots,z_{n}\}
is neighboring to
\mathcal{D}
by replacing the
j
-th element with
\tilde{z}_{j}
.

Then, given a learning algorithm
\mathcal{A}(\cdot)
and a training dataset
\mathcal{D}
, we denote
\boldsymbol{x}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})
as the model generated by the method
\mathcal{A}
with given data. Then, the upper bound of the model’s generalization gap is established in the following theorem:

Theorem A.1

(Modified generalization via model stability)
.

Let
\mathcal{D},\tilde{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{D}^{(j)}
be constructed as Definition
A.1
.
Let
\gamma>0
, if for any

is nonnegative and
L

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}}[F(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}))-f(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}))]\leq\frac{1}{2\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}}\|\nabla f(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}))\|^{2}+\frac{L+\gamma}{2}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\tilde{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{A}}[\|\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}^{(j)})-\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})\|^{2}].

(5)

Notation remark
: we use

to denote the empirical loss of one data sample

.

Recalling Definition
A.1
,
\mathcal{D}^{(j)}
is the neighbor datasets of
\mathcal{D}
by replacing the

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(F(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}))\big)\Big]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}}\Big[f(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}))\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{z}_{j}}\big[f(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}),\tilde{z}_{j})\big]\Big]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}}\Big[f(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}))\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{z}_{j}}\big[f(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}),\tilde{z}_{j})\big]\Big]-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D});z_{j})\Big]\quad\quad\quad\triangleright\text{Definition of empirical loss}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mathcal{D}}}\big[f(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}),\tilde{z}_{j})\big]\Big]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\tilde{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{A}}\Big[f(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}^{(j)});\tilde{z}_{j}))\Big]

B.3
Step 2: Convergence Analysis

Here, we prove the convergence rate of the global descent rule:

\displaystyle\text{{Client: }}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}^{t}=\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{t,K}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{t,0}=\eta_{l}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{t,k};

\displaystyle\text{{Server: }}\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1}=\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\eta_{g}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}^{t}=\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\eta_{g}\boldsymbol{d}^{t}.

Specifically, we analyze the update rule on the server-side gradient descent

\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1}=\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\eta_{g}\boldsymbol{d}^{t}.

Without loss of generality, we rewrite the global descent rule as:

\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1}=\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\eta\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}=\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\eta\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\boldsymbol{d}_{i}^{t}}{K}=\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\frac{\eta}{K}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{t,k-1},

and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}=\boldsymbol{d}^{t}/\eta_{l}K

Lemma B.7

(Bounded divergence of regularized global updates)

L

4.1

4.2

4.3
)

Using the smoothness of
f
and taking expectations conditioned on
x
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\eta\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t})]\leq f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\eta\mathbb{E}[\left\langle\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t}),\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}\right\rangle]+\frac{L}{2}\eta^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}\|^{2}\right]

(16)

\displaystyle\leq f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\eta\mathbb{E}[\left\langle\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t}),\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}\right\rangle]+\frac{L}{2}\eta^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\eta\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}+\eta\mathbb{E}[\left\langle\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t}),\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}\right\rangle]+\frac{L}{2}\frac{\eta^{2}}{\eta_{l}^{2}K^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\boldsymbol{d}^{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\frac{\eta}{2}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}+\underbrace{\frac{\eta}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}\|^{2}\right]}_{\text{Bounded by}~\eqref{eq:local_divergence}}+\underbrace{\frac{L}{2}\frac{\eta^{2}}{\eta_{l}^{2}K^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\boldsymbol{d}^{t}\|^{2}\right]}_{\text{Bounded by}~\eqref{eq:local_drift}},

where the last inequality follows since
\langle a,b\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|a\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|b\|^{2},\forall a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Substituting corresponding terms of the above equation with (
15
) and (
6
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\mathbb{E}\left[f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1})\right])}{\eta}

\displaystyle+\frac{15}{16}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}+60L^{2}\left(9\sigma_{l}^{2}+K\sigma_{g}^{2}\right)K\eta_{l}^{2}

\displaystyle+L\frac{\eta}{\eta_{l}^{2}K^{2}}\left(5K\eta_{l}^{2}(\sigma_{l}^{2}+6K\sigma_{g}^{2})+30K^{2}\eta_{l}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\right\|^{2}\right).

Reorganizing the above equation w.r.t,
\eta_{g},\eta_{l},K
and denoting
\tilde{\eta}=\eta_{l}\eta_{g}
, we have

\displaystyle\rho\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\mathbb{E}\left[f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1})\right])}{K\tilde{\eta}}+\kappa_{1}\tilde{\eta}+\kappa_{2}K\eta_{l}^{2},

(17)

where

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1}{16}\cdot(1-480LK\tilde{\eta})>0,\quad\kappa_{1}=5L(\sigma_{l}^{2}+6K\sigma_{g}^{2}),\quad\kappa_{2}=60L^{2}\left(9\sigma_{l}^{2}+K\sigma_{g}^{2}\right).

Taking full expectation of (
17
) and summarizing it from time
0
to
T-1
, we have

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}\leq\frac{2D}{\rho TK\tilde{\eta}}+\bar{\kappa}_{1}\tilde{\eta}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\kappa_{2}}{\rho},

(18)

where
\bar{\kappa}_{1}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\kappa_{1}}{\rho}
and
f(\boldsymbol{x}^{0})-f(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}})\leq D
denotes initialization bias.

Noting that
\rho>0
as a constant for sufficiently large
T
, setting
\eta_{l}\leq\min\{\frac{1}{8KL}
,
\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}KL}\}
, and
\tilde{\eta}\leq\frac{1}{480KL}
,
there exists an constant
\tilde{\eta}
such that the convergence rate of Algorithm
1
w.r.t the global objective
f
given by:

\min_{t\in[T]}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{D\sigma_{K}^{2}}{TK}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma_{K}^{2}}{T}\right),

where
\sigma_{K}^{2}=(\sigma_{l}^{2}+K\sigma_{g}^{2})
is variance of local and global gradients.

B.4
Step 3: Excess Risk Analysis via Joint Minimization

Following Corollary
4.1
, we investigate the upper bound of
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\leq\phi_{1}\cdot\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\|^{2}+\phi_{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2},

where
\phi_{1}=\frac{L+\gamma}{2}
and
\phi_{2}=\frac{\gamma+\mu}{2\gamma\mu}
for notation simplicity.

We propose studying the dynamics of excess risk’s upper bound for neural networks’ training. In detail, taking a summarization of the above equation from time
t=0
to
T
, we know

\displaystyle\min_{t\in[T]}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})

\displaystyle\leq\phi_{1}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\|^{2}}_{\text{stability dynamics}}+\phi_{2}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}}_{\text{gradient dynamics}}.

(19)

Now, we assemble stability and gradient dynamics. Then, we jointly minimize the excess risk dynamics upper bound.

We investigate the general form of stability-bound below

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\psi(\eta_{g}^{t})^{2}\right)\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\|^{2}+\psi_{\sigma}(\tilde{\eta}^{t})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\psi_{\sigma}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\left(\prod_{k=\tau+1}^{t}\exp\{\psi(\eta_{g}^{k})^{2}\}\right)(\tilde{\eta}^{\tau})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\psi_{\sigma}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\left(\exp\{c\psi\log(\frac{t}{\tau})\}\right)(\tilde{\eta}^{\tau})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\psi_{\sigma}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}(\frac{t}{\tau})^{c\psi}(\frac{\tilde{\eta}^{\tau}}{\tilde{\eta}^{t}})^{2}\cdot(\tilde{\eta}^{t})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\psi_{\sigma}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}(\frac{t}{\tau})^{1+c\psi}(\tilde{\eta}^{t})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\psi_{\sigma}}{c\psi}t^{1+c\psi}(\tilde{\eta}^{t})^{2},

where
\tilde{\eta}^{t}\leq\eta_{g}^{t}\leq\sqrt{\frac{c}{t}}
for some
c>0
. Then, we know that global stability is expansive by Theorem
4.2
. Then, substituting
t+1
with
T
of the above equation, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{T}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}\|^{2}\leq\kappa_{\text{stab}}\cdot(\tilde{\eta}^{t})^{2},

(20)

where
\kappa_{\text{stab}}=\frac{\psi_{\sigma}}{c\psi}T^{1+c\psi}
.

Joint minimization.

Substituting (
19
) with stability dynamics in (
20
) and gradient dynamics in (
18
), we have

\min_{t\in[T]}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\leq\underbrace{\frac{\bar{D}}{T\tilde{\eta}}+\bar{\kappa}_{1}\cdot\tilde{\eta}+\kappa_{\text{stab}}\cdot(\tilde{\eta})^{2}}_{\text{stability and convergence trade-off}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\phi_{1}\kappa_{2}}{\rho}}_{\text{client drift error}},

where

\bar{D}=\frac{2\phi_{1}D}{\rho K},\quad\bar{\kappa}_{1}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\phi_{1}\kappa_{1}}{\rho},\quad\bar{\kappa}_{\text{stab}}=\frac{\phi_{2}\psi_{\sigma}}{c\psi}T^{1+c\psi}.

Letting
\tilde{\eta}\leq\sqrt{\frac{c}{T}}
and Lemma
B.1
yielding
d=\sqrt{\frac{T}{c}},r_{0}=\bar{D},b=\bar{\kappa}_{1}
, and
e=\bar{\kappa}_{\text{stab}}
, we obtain

\min_{t\in[T]}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\leq\left(\frac{\bar{\kappa}_{1}\bar{D}}{T}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+2\kappa_{\text{stab}}^{\frac{1}{3}}\left(\frac{\bar{D}}{T}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}+\frac{\bar{D}}{\sqrt{Tc}}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\kappa_{2}}{\rho}.

(21)

Substituting the rate of all terms, we have the final rate of excess risk

\min_{t\in[T]}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{K}^{2}D}{KT}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sigma_{K}^{2}}{T}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}D^{2}}{Kc}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)^{\frac{1-c\psi}{3}}+\frac{D}{K\sqrt{Tc}}\right),

(22)

which concludes the proof.

C.1
Model Stability Analysis

We recall the updated rule of the global model as

\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1}=\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\eta_{g}\boldsymbol{m}^{t},\quad\boldsymbol{m}^{t}=\beta\boldsymbol{m}^{t-1}+\nu\boldsymbol{d}^{t},

where
\boldsymbol{d}^{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}^{t}
.
Then, we know

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\eta_{g}\boldsymbol{m}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}+\eta_{g}\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t}\|^{2}

(23)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|(\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})-\eta_{g}(\boldsymbol{m}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}-\eta_{g}\beta(\boldsymbol{m}^{t-1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t-1})-\eta_{g}\nu(\boldsymbol{d}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t})\|^{2}\quad\quad\quad\triangleright\text{$\boldsymbol{m}^{t-1}=(\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}-\boldsymbol{x}^{t})/\eta_{g}$}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}-\beta(-(\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})+(\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t-1}))-\eta_{g}\nu(\boldsymbol{d}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|(1+\beta)(\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})-\beta(\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t-1})-\eta_{g}\nu(\boldsymbol{d}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|(1+\beta)(\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})-\beta(\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t-1})-\eta_{g}\nu(\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\boldsymbol{x}^{t,K}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t,K})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|(1+\beta-\eta_{g}\nu)(\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t})-\beta(\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t-1})-\eta_{g}\nu(-\boldsymbol{x}^{t,K}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t,K})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\beta-\eta_{g}\nu)^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t-1}\|^{2}+\eta_{g}^{2}\nu^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t,K}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t,K}\|^{2}.

Substituting the last term of the above equation with (
12
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\beta-\eta_{g}\nu)^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\eta_{g}^{2}\nu^{2}\left((1+4\eta_{l}L)^{K}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\|^{2}+16K(\sigma_{l}^{2}+\frac{3b\sigma_{g}^{2}}{n})\eta_{l}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left((1+\beta-\eta_{g}\nu)^{2}+\eta_{g}^{2}\nu^{2}\psi\right)\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t-1}\|^{2}+\nu^{2}\psi_{\sigma}(\tilde{\eta}^{t})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left((1+\beta)^{2}+\eta_{g}^{2}\nu^{2}\psi\right)\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t-1}\|^{2}+\nu^{2}\psi_{\sigma}(\tilde{\eta}^{t})^{2},

where the last inequality denotes
\psi=(1+4\eta_{l}L)^{K}
,
\psi_{\sigma}=16K(\sigma_{l}^{2}+\frac{3b\sigma_{g}^{2}}{n})
and
\tilde{\eta}^{t}=\eta_{l}\eta_{g}^{t}
and modifies
\eta_{g}
to
\eta_{g}^{t}
.

Let

\alpha^{t}=(1+\beta)^{2}+(\eta_{g}^{t})^{2}\nu^{2}\psi,\quad\gamma^{t}=\nu^{2}\psi_{\sigma}(\tilde{\eta}^{t})^{2}

to obtain

\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}\|^{2}\leq\alpha^{t}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t-1}\|^{2}+\gamma^{t}

Then, unrolling the recursion from
t
down to
0
with
\mathbb{E}\left\|\boldsymbol{x}^{0}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{0}\right\|^{2}=0
, we have the stability of FOSM as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\gamma^{\tau}\prod_{k=\tau+1}^{t}(\alpha^{k}+\beta^{2})

\displaystyle=\nu^{2}\psi_{\sigma}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}(\tilde{\eta}^{\tau})^{2}\prod_{k=\tau+1}^{t}((1+\beta)^{2}+(\eta_{g}^{t})^{2}\nu^{2}\psi+\beta^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\nu^{2}\psi_{\sigma}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}(\tilde{\eta}^{\tau})^{2}\prod_{k=\tau+1}^{t}(1+2\beta(\beta+1)+(\eta_{g}^{t})^{2}\nu^{2}\psi)

\displaystyle\leq\nu^{2}\psi_{\sigma}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}(\tilde{\eta}^{\tau})^{2}\prod_{k=\tau+1}^{t}2\beta(\beta+1)\cdot\prod_{k=\tau+1}^{t}(1+(\eta_{g}^{t})^{2}\nu^{2}\psi)

\displaystyle\leq\nu^{2}\psi_{\sigma}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}(\tilde{\eta}^{\tau})^{2}\cdot(2\beta(\beta+1))^{t-\tau}\cdot\prod_{k=\tau+1}^{t}\exp\{(\eta_{g}^{t})^{2}\nu^{2}\psi\},

where the last two inequalities uses
\prod_{t=0}^{T}(a+b)\leq\prod_{t=0}^{T}a\cdot\prod_{t=0}^{T}
if
a>0,b>0,(a-1)(b-1)\geq 1
and
1+a\leq\exp\{a\},\forall a>0
.

Analogous to previous analysis, let let
\tilde{\eta}^{t}\leq\eta_{g}^{t}\leq\sqrt{\frac{c}{t}}
for some
c>0
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\nu^{2}\psi_{\sigma}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}(2\beta(\beta+1))^{t-\tau}\cdot\exp\{\nu^{2}c\psi\sum_{k=\tau+1}^{t}\frac{1}{k}\}\cdot\frac{(\tilde{\eta}^{\tau})^{2}}{(\tilde{\eta}^{t})^{2}}\cdot(\tilde{\eta}^{t})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\nu^{2}\psi_{\sigma}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}(2\beta(\beta+1))^{t-\tau}\cdot\exp\{\nu^{2}c\psi\log(\frac{t}{\tau})\}\cdot\frac{t}{\tau}\cdot(\tilde{\eta}^{t})^{2}

\displaystyle=\nu^{2}\psi_{\sigma}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}(2\beta(\beta+1))^{t-\tau}\cdot\exp\{\nu^{2}c\psi\log(\frac{t}{\tau})\}\cdot\frac{t}{\tau}\cdot(\tilde{\eta}^{t})^{2}

\displaystyle=\nu^{2}\psi_{\sigma}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}(2\beta(\beta+1))^{t-\tau}\cdot(\frac{t}{\tau})^{1+\nu^{2}c\psi}\cdot(\tilde{\eta}^{t})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\nu^{2}\psi_{\sigma}t^{1+\nu^{2}c\psi}\frac{(2\beta(\beta+1))^{t+1}-1}{2\beta(\beta+1)-1}\cdot\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}(\frac{1}{\tau})^{1+\nu^{2}c\psi}\cdot(\tilde{\eta}^{t})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\psi_{\sigma}}{c\psi}\frac{(2\beta(\beta+1))^{t+1}-1}{2\beta(\beta+1)-1}t^{1+\nu^{2}c\psi}(\tilde{\eta}^{t})^{2}.

In short, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{T}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{T}\|^{2}\leq\Psi_{\text{stab}}\cdot(\tilde{\eta}^{t})^{2},

(24)

where we let
\Psi_{\text{stab}}=\frac{\psi_{\sigma}}{c\psi}\psi_{\beta}T^{1+\nu^{2}c\psi}
and
\psi_{\beta}=\frac{(2\beta(\beta+1))^{T}-1}{2\beta(\beta+1)-1}
denotes the scale effects from momentum parameter
\beta
. Moreover, we note that
0<\beta<1
, which induces that
(1+\beta)^{T}
dominates the
\psi_{\beta}
.

C.2
Convergence Analysis

We recall the updated rule of the global model as

\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1}=\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\eta_{g}\boldsymbol{m}^{t},\quad\boldsymbol{m}^{t}=\beta\boldsymbol{m}^{t-1}+\nu\boldsymbol{d}^{t},

where
\boldsymbol{d}^{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{d}_{i}^{t}
.
For simplicity, we consider the regularized momentum as:

\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1}=\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\eta\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t}=\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\eta\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}_{i}^{t}}{K},

where

\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}_{i}^{t}=\beta\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}_{i}^{t-1}+\nu\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}_{i}^{t},\quad\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}_{i}^{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\boldsymbol{g}_{i}^{t,k-1},

denotes the local momentum of the
i
-th client and
\eta=K\eta_{l}\eta_{g}
. We note that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t}=\frac{1}{\eta_{l}K}\boldsymbol{m}^{t}
. Moreover, we unroll the recursion of
\boldsymbol{m}^{t}
for
t=0,\dots,T
with
\boldsymbol{m}^{0}=\nu\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{0}
to obtain

\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t}=\nu\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\beta^{t-\tau}\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{\tau}.

Analogous to (
16
), we have similar descent lemma with momentum
\boldsymbol{m}^{t}
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1})\right]

\displaystyle\leq f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\frac{\eta}{2}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t}\|^{2}\right]}_{T_{1}}+\frac{L}{2}\eta^{2}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t}\|^{2}\right]}_{T_{2}}.

(25)

Bounding
T_{1}
.
Using the definition of momentum, we know

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\beta\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t-1}-\nu\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}\right\|^{2}\quad\quad\quad\triangleright\text{Let $\nu=1-\beta$}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\|\beta(\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}))+\beta(\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t-1})+(1-\beta)(\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{2}W\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1})\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t-1}\|^{2}+(1-\beta)^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{2}WL^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t-1}\|^{2}+(1-\beta)^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t-1})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t-1}\|^{2}+\beta^{2}L^{2}W\eta^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t-1}\|^{2}+\nu^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{t}\|^{2}

In the above equations, we suppose a constant
W
that always holds the third equation for all
t
. Then, we can shrink the global learning to absorb the scaling constant
W
, i.e.,
\eta^{2}\leq W\eta^{2}
in the last equation.

Then, unrolling the recursion from time
t
down to
0
with edge conditions
\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{-1})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{-1}\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{-1}\|^{2}=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{2}L^{2}\eta^{2}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\beta^{2(t-\tau)}\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\tau}\|^{2}+\nu^{2}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\beta^{2(t-\tau)}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{\tau})-\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{\tau}\|^{2}}_{\text{Bounded by \eqref{eq:local_divergence}}}

(26)

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{2}L^{2}\eta^{2}\underbrace{\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\beta^{2(t-\tau)}\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\tau}\|^{2}}_{\text{Bounded by \eqref{eq:temp_bounded_cumulated_momentum}}}

\displaystyle+\nu^{2}\beta^{2}\frac{1-\beta^{2t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\left(\frac{15}{16}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}+60L^{2}\left(9\sigma_{l}^{2}+K\sigma_{g}^{2}\right)K\eta_{l}^{2}\right).

Bounding
T_{2}
.

Given the definition of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t}=\nu\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\beta^{t-\tau}\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{\tau}
, we know

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\|\nu\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\beta^{t-\tau}\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{\tau}\|^{2}\leq\nu^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\beta^{t-\tau}\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{\tau}\|^{2}\leq\nu^{2}(t+1)\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\beta^{2(t-\tau)}\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}^{\tau}\|^{2}

(27)

\displaystyle\leq\nu^{2}(t+1)\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\beta^{2(t-\tau)}\frac{1}{\eta_{l}^{2}K^{2}}\left(5K\eta_{l}^{2}(\sigma_{l}^{2}+6K\sigma_{g}^{2})+30K^{2}\eta_{l}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\right\|^{2}\right)\quad\quad\quad\triangleright\text{Lemma~\ref{lemma:local_drift}}

\displaystyle\leq\nu^{2}(t+1)\frac{1-\beta^{2(t+1)}}{1-\beta^{2}}\frac{1}{\eta_{l}^{2}K^{2}}\left(5K\eta_{l}^{2}(\sigma_{l}^{2}+6K\sigma_{g}^{2})+30K^{2}\eta_{l}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\right\|^{2}\right).

Based on the above results, we bound the cumulated momentum in
T_{1}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\beta^{2(t-\tau)}\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\tau}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\nu^{2}}{1-\beta^{2}}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\beta^{2(t-\tau)}(\tau+1)(1-\beta^{2(\tau+1)})\frac{1}{\eta_{l}^{2}K^{2}}\left(5K\eta_{l}^{2}(\sigma_{l}^{2}+6K\sigma_{g}^{2})+30K^{2}\eta_{l}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\right\|^{2}\right)

(28)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\nu^{2}}{1-\beta^{2}}\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\beta^{2(t-\tau)}(\tau+1)\frac{1}{\eta_{l}^{2}K^{2}}\left(5K\eta_{l}^{2}(\sigma_{l}^{2}+6K\sigma_{g}^{2})+30K^{2}\eta_{l}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\nu^{2}}{1-\beta^{2}}(t+1)\frac{1-\beta^{2(t+1)}}{1-\beta^{2}}\frac{1}{\eta_{l}^{2}K^{2}}\left(5K\eta_{l}^{2}(\sigma_{l}^{2}+6K\sigma_{g}^{2})+30K^{2}\eta_{l}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\right\|^{2}\right).

Putting together.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})-\mathbb{E}\left[f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1}))\right]}{\eta}

\displaystyle\quad+L^{2}\eta^{2}\frac{\nu^{2}\beta^{2}}{1-\beta^{2}}(t+1)\frac{1-\beta^{2(t+1)}}{1-\beta^{2}}\frac{1}{\eta_{l}^{2}K^{2}}\left(5K\eta_{l}^{2}(\sigma_{l}^{2}+6K\sigma_{g}^{2})+30K^{2}\eta_{l}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\right\|^{2}\right)

(29)

\displaystyle\quad+\nu^{2}\beta^{2}\frac{1-\beta^{2t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\left(\frac{15}{16}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}+60L^{2}\left(9\sigma_{l}^{2}+K\sigma_{g}^{2}\right)K\eta_{l}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+L\eta\nu^{2}(t+1)\frac{1-\beta^{2(t+1)}}{1-\beta^{2}}\frac{1}{\eta_{l}^{2}K^{2}}\left(5K\eta_{l}^{2}(\sigma_{l}^{2}+6K\sigma_{g}^{2})+30K^{2}\eta_{l}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\right\|^{2}\right).

Aligning with the previous analysis, we denote

,

\eta_{l}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{T}KL}

for a constant

C.3
Excess Risk Analysis

Now, we provide the excess risk upper bound of FOSM methods. Analogous to our previous analysis, we have

\displaystyle\min_{t\in[T]}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\leq\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})

\displaystyle\leq\phi_{1}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{x}^{t}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t}\|^{2}}_{\text{stability dynamics}}+\phi_{2}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\|\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\|^{2}}_{\text{gradient dynamics}}.

(32)

Then, substituting corresponding terms with (
31
) and (
24
), we have

\displaystyle\min_{t\in[T]}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\leq\underbrace{\frac{\tilde{D}}{\tilde{\eta}T}+\bar{\Psi}_{1}\tilde{\eta}+\bar{\Psi}_{\text{stab}}\tilde{\eta}^{2}}_{\text{stability and convergence trade-off}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\Psi_{2}}{\hat{\rho}}}_{\text{client drift error}},

where

\tilde{D}=\frac{2\phi_{1}D}{\hat{\rho}K},\quad\bar{\Psi}_{1}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\phi_{1}\Psi_{1}}{\hat{\rho}},\quad\bar{\Psi}_{\text{stab}}=\phi_{2}\frac{\psi_{\sigma}}{c\psi}\psi_{\beta}T^{1+\nu^{2}c\psi}.

Using
\eta_{g}\leq\sqrt{\frac{c}{T}}
and Lemma
B.1
with
d=\sqrt{\frac{T}{c}},r_{0}=\tilde{D},b=\bar{\Psi}_{1}
, and
e=\Psi_{\text{stab}}
, we obtain

\min_{t\in[T]}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})\leq 2\left(\frac{\bar{\Psi}_{1}\tilde{D}}{T}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+2\bar{\Psi}_{\text{stab}}^{\frac{1}{3}}\left(\frac{\tilde{D}}{T}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}+\frac{\tilde{D}}{\sqrt{Tc}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\Psi_{2}}{\hat{\rho}}}_{\text{client drift error}}.

(33)

Noting the rate of each term satisfies:

\displaystyle\bar{\Psi}_{1}=\mathcal{O}\left((1-\beta^{T})\cdot\sigma_{K}^{2}\right),\Psi_{\text{stab}}=\mathcal{O}\left((1+\beta)^{T}K\sigma_{n}^{2}T^{1+\nu^{2}c\psi})\right),

we obtain the bound of the minimum excess risk:

\displaystyle\min_{t\in[T]}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x}^{t})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{(1-\beta^{T})\cdot\frac{\sigma_{K}^{2}D}{KT}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left((1-\beta^{T})\cdot\frac{\sigma_{K}^{2}}{T}\right)

(34)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\left(\left((1+\beta)^{T}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{n}^{2}D^{2}}{Kc}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{T}\right)^{\frac{1-\nu^{2}c\psi}{3}}+\frac{D}{K\sqrt{Tc}}\right),

which concludes the proof.
