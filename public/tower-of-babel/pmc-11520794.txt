Kiss1 receptor knockout exacerbates airway hyperresponsiveness and remodeling in a mouse model of allergic asthma
Authors: Nilesh Sudhakar Ambhore, Premanand Balraj, Ashish Kumar, Mohammad Irshad Reza, Yogaraj S Ramakrishnan, Jacob Tesch, Sahil Lohana, Venkatachalem Sathish
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn asthma, sex-steroids signaling is recognized as a critical regulator of disease pathophysiology. However, the paradoxical role of sex-steroids, especially estrogen, suggests that an upstream mechanism or even independent of estrogen plays an important role in regulating asthma pathophysiology. In this context, in our previous studies, we explored kisspeptin (Kp) and its receptor Kiss1R’s signaling in regulating human airway smooth muscle cell remodeling in vitro and airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) in vivo in a mouse (wild-type, WT) model of asthma. In this study, we evaluated the effect of endogenous Kp in regulating AHR and remodeling using Kiss1R knockout (Kiss1R−/−) mice.MethodsC57BL/6J WT (Kiss1R+/+) and Kiss1R−/− mice, both male and female, were intranasally challenged with mixed-allergen (MA) and/or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). We used flexiVent analysis to assess airway resistance (Rrs), elastance (Ers), and compliance (Crs). Following this, broncho-alveolar lavage (BAL) was performed for differential leukocyte count (DLC) and cytokine analysis. Histology staining was performed using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for morphological analysis and Masson’s Trichrome (MT) for collagen deposition. Additionally, lung sections were processed for immunofluorescence (IF) of Ki-67, α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), and tenascin-c.ResultsInterestingly, the loss of Kiss1R exacerbated lung function and airway contractility in mice challenged with MA, with more profound effects in Kiss1R−/− female mice. MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− mice showed a significant increase in immune cell infiltration and proinflammatory cytokine levels. Importantly, the loss of Kiss1R aggravated Th2/Th17 biased cytokines in MA-challenged mice. Furthermore, histology of lung sections from Kiss1R−/− mice showed increased collagen deposition on airway walls and mucin production in airway cells compared to Kiss1R+/+ mice. In addition, immunofluorescence analysis showed loss of Kiss1R significantly aggravated airway remodeling and subsequently AHR.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate the importance of inherent Kiss1R signaling in regulating airway inflammation, AHR, and remodeling in the pathophysiology of asthma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-024-03017-4.Keywords: Asthma, Airway inflammation, FlexiVent, Lung function, Airway smooth muscle, α-smooth muscle actin

Background

Asthma is a common chronic inflammatory airway disease with various clinical phenotypes and intricate genetic and environmental influences [1, 2]. Currently, more than 339 million people are affected with asthma, and the prevalence of asthma is expected to increase by 50% every decade [3]. With the recent advancements, we now have a better understanding of the disease pathophysiology and various associated factors contributing to the severity and prevalence of asthma [4]. Despite the advanced clinical treatments available to manage airway inflammation and acute airway narrowing, the incidence of asthma, including severe asthma, continues to increase [5, 6]. Clinically, asthma is characterized by episodes of airway narrowing and airflow obstruction which leads to higher mortality if uncontrolled [7–9]. The major clinical manifestations of severe asthma include airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR), inflammation, goblet cell hyperplasia, and fibrotic airway remodeling [10, 11]. Here, exploring the mechanisms that influence the structural changes in airways, thereby airway remodeling and AHR, is crucial given that currently available therapies including corticosteroids are ineffective in targeting these remodeling aspects of asthma progression [12, 13].

A recognized aspect of asthma is the age and sex-related differences in epidemiology and clinical manifestations. The prevalence of asthma in prepubertal boys is higher than in girls, but this trend reverses following puberty, and women experience an increase in asthma prevalence that then diminishes with menopause [14, 15]. These data provide evidence for the modulatory role of sex steroids, particularly estrogen, in regulating asthma pathophysiology [2, 9, 16]. However, it remains unclear whether estrogen is protective or detrimental in asthma, with some studies showing estrogens enhance inflammation [17, 18], while others associate estrogens with an asthma-mitigating role [19, 20]. This paradoxical role of estrogen represents possible mechanisms upstream to or independent of estrogen, or conversely a loss of other relevant protective pathways during disease progression. Kisspeptin (Kp) regulates gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) and gonadal steroids, while estrogen, via estrogen receptor α, negatively regulates Kp neurons in the hypothalamic arcuate (ARC) nuclei [21]. Furthermore, it was observed that the number of Kp neurons increased in females after menopause, but decreased after estrogen administration [22, 23]. Accordingly, we explored Kp and its receptor Kiss1R which are negatively regulated by estrogen in managing asthma pathophysiology [10].

Kp is a product of the KiSS1 gene, which is located on chromosome 1q32.1 in humans and 1E4 in mice [24]. When the KiSS1 gene undergoes transcription and translation, it produces a 145-amino acid precursor. This precursor is then proteolytically modified to create a C-terminally amidated 54-amino acid peptide, as well as several shorter fragments such as kisspeptin-10, kisspeptin-13, and kisspeptin-14 [25, 26]. All of these shorter peptides contain an active essential 10-amino acid element and act as endogenous ligands for KISS1R activation [10]. Previous reports have shown that Kp via Kiss1R activation inhibits cancer cell metastasis and plays a key role in regulating puberty [27]. Recent studies from our group have shown for the first time the expression of Kp and KISS1R in the airways of human lungs, especially in the airway smooth muscle (ASM) cells. Additionally, we have observed downregulated Kp and KISS1R expression in airways during the asthmatic condition, suggesting a loss of Kp/KISS1R signaling during the disease’s progression. Furthermore, our research has shown that activating KISS1R using Kp-10 blunts PGDF-induced proliferation in human ASM cells via inhibition of the ERK and P38/MAPK signaling pathways [26]. Recently, we also demonstrated the anti-migratory effect of KISS1R in human ASM cells via a cAMP/PKA-dependent mechanism [28]. These in vitro findings were supported by an in vivo mouse model of asthma, where we found that activating Kiss1R with a specific agonist, Kp-10 in WT C57BL/6J mice alleviated AHR and airway remodeling [10]. Additionally, studies from other groups showed that diet-induced obesity may disrupt Kp/Kiss1R signaling which leads to the accumulation of adipose tissue in airways, and results in airway remodeling [29]. Except for these studies, very limited reports are available on the role of Kp/Kiss1R in regulating airway function and structure in mouse models of asthma.

In this present study, we utilized a new Kiss1R knockout (Kiss1R−/−) mouse line generated with advanced Cre/lox genome recombinant technology to ascertain if endogenously secreted Kp regulates Kiss1R signaling and influences asthma pathophysiology. Earlier reports show that impaired Kiss1R signaling is associated with the spontaneous development of obesity and metabolic disorders, particularly in female mice [29, 30]. However, the impact of intrinsic Kiss1R signaling on lung functions and airway remodeling during asthma progression is not yet fully understood. For the first time, we sought to explore the intrinsic mechanism of Kp/Kiss1R signaling in normal versus asthmatic conditions using Kiss1R−/− male and female mice, and Kiss1R+/+ mice as controls in a mixed allergen (MA)-induced murine model of asthma. Our results showed that Kiss1R−/− mice exhibited exacerbated AHR and remodeling when compared to Kiss1R+/+ mice upon MA challenge. Interestingly, in comparison between Kiss1R−/− male and female mice, female mice showed more profound changes in the airway structure and functions compared to male mice.

Methods

Animals

All animal study protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of North Dakota State University and conducted following guidelines derived from the “National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” [31]. The Kiss1R knockout (Kiss1R−/−) C57BL/6 mice were generated by Jackson Laboratory, USA by using the KOMP ES cell line Kiss1Rtm1(KOMP)Vlcg, RRID: MMRRC_057618-UCD. The cell line was obtained from the Mutant Mouse Resource and Research Center (MMRRC) at the University of California at Davis, an NIH-funded strain repository and was donated to the MMRRC by David Valenzuela, George Yancopoulos, Regeneron Pharmaceuticals, Inc. Briefly, in these mice, the parental Kiss1Rrtm1(KOMP)Vlcg allele containing a neomycin selection cassette was removed via Cre-mediated excision and replaced with a mutant lacZ receptor sequence. The introduction and excision of the KOMP insert disrupts the 1st exon and excises the 2nd -5th exon of Kiss1R. Afterward, in-house breeding was performed using heterozygous mice to obtain the pups. At 3 weeks of age, mice were genotyped by using genomic DNA PCR following the previously described method [32]. The mice were identified for their genotype according to the observed bands in the gel imaging (Fig. 1B) and separated into Kiss1R+/+ (WT; littermate control), Kiss1R+/− (heterozygous), and Kiss1R−/− (homozygous). All experimental mice were housed on a 12:12 light: dark cycle with maintained temperature between 18 and 24 °C, and provided with food and water ad libitum. Mice from either sex were used in this study with a minimum of 6–8 mice in each group.

Chemicals, reagents, and allergens

Ovalbumin from chicken egg white was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The allergens comprising Alternaria alternata, Aspergillus fumigatus, and Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus (house dust mite) were purchased from Greer Laboratories (Lenoir, NC, USA). Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS; Cat# 14190-144) and protease and phosphatase inhibitor (PPI) cocktail (Cat# 1861284) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Primary Ab for α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA, Cat# A2547) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Ki-67 (Cat# 9129 S) and tenascin-c (Cat# 33352 S) were procured from Cell Signaling Technologies. Fluorescence-tagged secondary antibodies of AlexaFluor-488 (Cat# A21202) and AlexaFluor-647 (Cat# A31573) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Fluoro-Gel II containing DAPI-AF408 counterstain (Cat# 17985-50) was from Electron Microscopy Sciences. Other chemicals and reagents were procured from Sigma-Aldrich unless otherwise indicated.

Mixed allergen (MA)-induced asthma mouse models

Mice from both genotypes (Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/−) were randomly assigned to PBS and MA groups based on age and body weight. The MA groups were administered with a range of clinically relevant allergens to induce allergic airway inflammation and hyperresponsiveness [33]. MA regimes involved alternate-day intranasal administration of a mixture of equal amounts (10 µg) of ovalbumin, and extracts from Alternaria alternata, Aspergillus fumigatus, and Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus (OAAH) in 25 µL of DPBS, on alternate days (starting from day 1) for 4 weeks (3 days/week), as previously described [20, 34]. Mice allotted in the vehicle group received 25 µL of DPBS alone for a similar time duration. An overview of the experimental plan is depicted in the schematic in Fig. 1A.

Lung function measurements

All the male and female mice from both vehicle and MA-challenge groups were subjected to measure Methacholine (MCh)-induced AHR using flexiVent analysis. Twenty-four hours after the last dose of the MA challenge, we recorded lung parameters such as total respiratory system resistance (Rrs), compliance (Crs), and elastance (Ers) as per the previously described method [6, 10, 20, 29]. Animals were anesthetized using ketamine (100 mg/kg i.p.) and xylazine (10 mg/kg i.p.; Midwest Veterinary Supply, Lakeville, MN, USA), and Rrs, Crs, and Ers were recorded at baseline (0 mg/mL MCh) and increasing doses of nebulized MCh (6.25, 12.5, 25.0, and 50.0 mg/mL, respectively) for 5 min per dose. Max Rrs, max Crs, and, max Ers values were used for comparison between different treatment groups across the two study populations. Throughout the flexiVent analysis, mice were maintained at 37 °C using a heated light source placed above at 45°, with continuous observation of the heart rate.

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and lung tissue collection

After the lung function measurements, 1.0 mL of DPBS (supplemented with a PPI cocktail) was injected into the lungs via tracheal cannula to obtain the BALF. With about 60% recovery rate we collected ~ 600 µL of fluid in the tubes. The collected BALF was stored at -80 °C until analysis. Following this, the lungs were perfused with 10 mL of DPBS via the right ventricle to eliminate circulating blood before collecting each lobe. The left lobe of each mouse lung was stored in Carnoy’s solution (100% ethanol, chloroform, and glacial acetic acid in a ratio of 6:3:1 with ferric chloride, (Sigma-Aldrich) for a few hours, followed by the replacement of the solution with 70% ethanol and preparation for histology studies [35–37].

Total and differential leukocyte count in BALF

The collected BALF from the respective groups of mice was processed for total and differential leukocyte count (DLC) using previously published methods [36, 38]. Briefly, BALF samples were centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was retained for cytokine analysis and the cell pellet was mixed with 100 µl of ACK Lysing Buffer (Cat# A1049201, Thermofisher Scientific) to lyse red blood cells. Following another centrifugation, the cell pellet was resuspended in 200 µL of PBS followed by performing a total BAL cell count using Countess-II FL cell counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The remaining sample was processed using a cytospin on glass slides and the resultant air-dried smear was stained using the Differential Quick Staining kit (Cat# 26096-50, Modified Giemsa, EMS) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The processed slides were then observed by an inverted phase contrast microscope (Accu-Scope EXI-410) at X40 magnification for DLC. We have provided the actual cell count number for the total BAL cell count. We counted a minimum of 100 cells per sample for differential leukocyte counts and then converted them to an absolute value based on the total number of cells with respective samples.

Cytokine enzyme-linked immunoassay (ELISA) multiplex assay in BALF

The collected supernatant from BALF was aliquoted into 0.5–0.6 mL microcentrifuge tubes and processed for Mouse Cytokine 32-Plex Discovery Assay as per the manufacturer’s protocol (Eve Technologies Pvt. Ltd, Calgary, Canada).

Precision cut lung section (PCLS) study

A separate set of Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− animals (both male and female) were challenged with vehicle and MA. After the treatment period, mice were euthanized with an excess dose of sodium pentobarbital. Mice were placed in a supine position and the chest was opened following tracheal cannulation. The lungs were inflated with warm low melting 2% agarose at 37 °C in HBSS, followed by 0.2mL of air to flush the agarose. The lungs were sliced into 200 μm sections in 4 °C cold HBSS without calcium using VF-310Z vibratome (Pecisionary Instruments, Winchester, MA, USA). The sliced PCLS were transferred to DMEM media without serum and containing 2% antibiotic-antimycotic (AbAm). The sections were transferred to fresh media with gentle shaking at least three times to remove the residual agarose and incubated in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. After 24 h of incubation, PCLS sections were mounted on an imaging slide, and baseline images were captured by an inverted phase contrast microscope (Accu-Scope EXI-410). Afterward, sections were stimulated with 100 µM of MCh, and changes in the airway lumen area were recorded for 12 min for each lung section from individual Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− mice. Airway length and width were analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA, https://imagej.net/software/fiji/) to calculate airway contraction area and changes were expressed as the percentage change compared to the initial baseline area.

Histopathology analysis

The collected left lung from the respective groups were embedded in paraffin wax blocks and sectioned to 5-µm slices. The sectioned slides were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (Sigma-Aldrich) for assessing pulmonary pathological changes, whereas periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) (EMS, Hatfield, PA, USA) staining was used to examine goblet cell hyperplasia. Masson’s trichrome (Chondrex, Redmond, WA, USA) stain was used to analyze collagen deposition around the airways [10]. The whole lung sections on slides were scanned using Epredia Pannoramic MIDI II 20 × (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary). The scanned sections were then viewed on CaseCenter 2.9 software with a magnification level of up to 40X in order to capture the airway images from the respective groups. The captured airways of H&E sections were examined for inflammatory cell recruitment by determining the optical density (O.D.) around the airways using ImageJ software. We standardized the OD measurement using a standard step tablet with an R2 value of 0.9991. To avoid false-positive and false-negative results, we measured the OD in the lung parenchymal region without inflammatory cell recruitment and used it for background normalization. The PAS-stained sections were analyzed for mucin expression by counting the number of PAS-positive epithelial cells in the airways with approximately similar diameters (~ 50 μm). The results are expressed as the percentage of cells per airway that were PAS-positive [39]. Collagen was stained with Masson’s trichrome and the positively-stained area around the airways was measured using ImageJ. The collagen area was then normalized for airway size (collagen area µm2/airway perimeter µm), as previously described [40].

Immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded lungs from the respective groups were used to create 6 μm thick lung sections, followed by standard immunofluorescence techniques [41]. Sections were baked (56 °C for 2 h) and deparaffinized using xylene and ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed by steaming the sections in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 45 min, cooling down at room temperature for 30 min, and further rehydrated in Millipore water. Sections were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS (1XPBS), blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Following blocking, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with α-SMA, Ki-67, and tenascin-c antibodies at the dilution of 1:100 for respective antibodies. The next day, sections were washed three times with PBS and incubated with either Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 647 tagged secondary antibodies (1:200 dilution) with Fluoro-Gel II containing DAPI-AF408 counterstaining for nuclei. High-resolution images were captured on a Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope with an Airyscan detector. The arithmetic means fluorescence intensity for respective proteins was measured by Zeiss software.

Soluble collagen assay

The total soluble collagen level in the lung tissue lysates was measured by using the Sircol collagen assay (Biocolor Life Science Assay, Carrickfergus, UK) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and previously described process [10, 42]. Briefly, the lung tissue samples were lysed to collect homogenate and mixed with Sircol Dye reagent. The developed color was measured by colorimetric absorbance at 556 nm using a Synergy HTX Plate Reader (Biotek, Winooski, VT, USA).

Statistical analysis

All the experimental groups consisted of a minimum of six to eight mice. Statistical analysis was performed using an unpaired t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons using GraphPad Prism version 9.1.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± SEM with statistical significance tested at a minimum of p < 0.05 level.

Results

Kiss1R and mutant gene expression in the lung

The experimental plan of the study is presented in the schematic in Fig. 1A. The confirmation of genotyping of Kiss1R+/+, Kiss1R+/−, and Kiss1R−/− mice is shown in the representative gel image in Fig. 1B. The Kiss1R−/− mice were generated by Cre-mediated excision of the parental Kiss1rtm1(KOMP)Vlcg allele leaving behind the inserted lacZ reporter mutant sequence, as shown in Fig. 1C; left panel. According to changes in the gene sequence, we designed primers (as shown in Fig. 1C; right panel) to determine the Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− mice. To further confirm the Kiss1R knockout, we determined the mRNA expression levels of Kiss1R (Fig. 1D) and inserted mutant gene sequences (Fig. 1E) in the mice lung samples from both Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− groups.

Effect of Kiss1R on total respiratory system resistance (Rrs)

In this study, we assessed the lung functions using the forced oscillation technique by flexiVent FX1 module with an in-line nebulizer (SCIREQ, Montreal, Canada). Male and female mice from both Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− genotypes showed a dose-dependent linear increase in Rrs (representing airway constriction) in response to nebulized methacholine concentrations (MCh; 0–50 mg/mL) in both PBS and MA-challenged group (Fig. 2A, B). For comparison between the different groups, we used max Rrs at the concentration of 50 mg/mL of MCh (Fig. 2C). MA-challenged male and female mice from both Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− groups showed a significant increase in max Rrs (p < 0.001) compared to their respective PBS-challenged group. Further, we observed a significant increase in max Rrs in MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− female mice (p < 0.01) compared to Kiss1R−/− male mice (Fig. 2C). Mice challenged with PBS alone did not show any significant changes in baseline max Rrs between different study populations. In comparison between MA-challenged mice, we observed a significant increase in max Rrs in Kiss1R−/− male (p < 0.01) and Kiss1R−/− female (p < 0.001) mice compared to the respective Kiss1R+/+ MA-challenged group.

Effect of Kiss1R on total respiratory system compliance (Crs)

Crs, which is inversely proportional to resistance and represents the ease of lung function was measured in PBS and MA-challenged, male and female mice from both Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− groups. Mice challenged with PBS and MA showed a decrease in the Crs with increasing doses of MCh concentration (Fig. 3A, B). Max Crs at the concentration of 50 mg/mL of MCh was used for comparison between the different groups (Fig. 3C). At the baseline, PBS-challenged Kiss1R−/− male mice showed a significant decrease (p < 0.001) in max Crs compared to PBS-challenged Kiss1R+/+ male mice, whereas no significant changes were observed between Kiss1R+/+ male and female mice. MA-challenged mice from both Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− groups showed a significant decrease in max Crs in males (p < 0.01 for Kiss1R+/+; p < 0.05 for Kiss1R−/−) and females (p < 0.001 for Kiss1R+/+; p < 0.01 for Kiss1R−/− ) mice compared to their respective PBS-challenged group. Amongst the comparison between MA-challenged mice, we observed a significant decrease in max Crs in Kiss1R−/− male (p < 0.01) mice compared to the Kiss1R+/+ MA-challenged male mice. MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− female mice tend to show a decrease in max Crs in comparison to Kiss1R+/+ MA-challenged female mice, albeit not statistically significant.

Effect of Kiss1R on airway elastance (Ers)

We measured the Ers to determine the elastic stiffness of the lungs, which also represents the inhalation capacity of the lungs and energy conservation in the alveoli to relax to a normal state [32]. Ers was measured in PBS and MA-challenged, male and female mice from both Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− groups. Mice challenged with PBS and MA are shown to increase in the Ers with increasing doses of MCh concentration (Figure S1A, B). Max Ers at the concentration of 50 mg/mL of MCh was used for comparison between the different groups (Figure S1C). In comparison between male and female mice, we observed a significant increase in max Ers in MA-challenged female mice (p < 0.01 for Kiss1R+/+; p < 0.05 for Kiss1R−/−) compared to respective MA-challenged male mice. Moreover, MA-challenged mice from both Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− groups showed a significant increase in max Ers in males (p < 0.05 for Kiss1R+/+; p < 0.001 for Kiss1R−/−) and females (p < 0.001 for both Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/−) mice compared to their respective PBS-challenged group. In a comparison of genotypes, MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− male (p < 0.01) and Kiss1R−/− female (p < 0.05) mice showed a significant increase in max Ers compared to the respective Kiss1R+/+ MA-challenged group.

Effect of Kiss1R on airway contractility

To evaluate the effect of Kiss1R on airway contractility, Precision-Cut Lung Slices (PCLS) were separately prepared from Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− mice (both male and female) exposed to PBS and/or MA (Fig. 4A, B). In comparison between male and female mice, we did not observe any significant changes in airway contractility in PBS-challenged mice. Within the MA-challenged group, female mice showed a significant increase in airway contractility compared to male mice (p < 0.01 for Kiss1R+/+ females; p < 0.001 for Kiss1R−/− female, Fig. 4D). Further in comparison between genotypes, PBS-challenged mice from Kiss1R−/− male and female groups showed a significant increase in airway contractility (p < 0.05 for males; p < 0.001 for females, Fig. 4C) in response to 100 µM of MCh in a time-dependent manner over 12 min, compared to respective PBS-challenged Kiss1R+/+ groups. Further, PBS-challenged Kiss1R−/− female mice showed a significant increase in airway contractility (p < 0.01, Fig. 4C) compared to Kiss1R−/− male mice. MA-challenged mice from Kiss1R−/− female groups showed a significant increase in airway contractility (p < 0.05, Fig. 4D) compared to MA-challenged Kiss1R+/+ female mice. Furthermore, MA-challenged Kiss1R+/+ female mice also showed an increase in airway contractility (p < 0.01) compared to Kiss1R+/+ male mice. While, in comparison between MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− mice, we observed a significant increase in airway contractility (p < 0.001) in Kiss1R−/− female mice compared to Kiss1R−/− male mice.

Effect of Kiss1R on airway structure and morphology

To assess whether loss of Kiss1R alters the airway inflammation and structure, we stained the lung sections of Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− mice challenged with PBS or MA with H&E (Fig. 5A) stain. MA-challenge significantly induced airway inflammation with immune cell infiltration in Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− mice (p < 0.001 for both males and females, Fig. 5B) compared to respective PBS-challenged groups. In comparison between male and female mice, we observed a significantly increased airway immune cell infiltration with MA-challenge in both Kiss1R+/+ female (p < 0.01) and Kiss1R−/− female (p < 0.001) mice compared to respective MA-challenged male mice. Here, MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− female mice showed a significant increase (p < 0.001) in airway immune cell infiltration compared to MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− male mice. We further measured the total BAL cell count and found a significant increase in total BAL cell count after the MA challenge in both Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− mice (p < 0.001, Fig. 5C). The effect of MA was more profound in the Kiss1R−/− mice, particularly MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− male mice showed significantly high total BAL cell count (p < 0.01) compared to MA-challenged Kiss1R+/+ male mice (Fig. 5C). Additionally, we determined whether the loss of Kiss1R had any effect on specific immune cell types by differential counting the immune cells in BAL samples. Consistent with the above finding, the MA challenge significantly increased the number of eosinophils and neutrophils in BAL samples from Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− mice (p < 0.001 for both Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− mice, Fig. 5D) compared to respective PBS-challenged groups. In a comparison of male and female mice, MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− female mice showed a significant increase in neutrophil (p < 0.01) and eosinophil (p < 0.05) count compared to MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− male mice. Mucin production from the airway epithelial cells or goblet cell hyperplasia is considered an important hallmark feature of allergic asthma. We performed PAS staining (Fig. 5E) in the lung section of Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− mice challenged with PBS or MA to determine the effect of Kiss1R on mucus-producing goblet cells in the airways. Effect of Kiss1R on the cytokine milieu of BALF

An altered inflammatory cytokine profile due to allergen exposure in the lungs plays a crucial role in the progression of asthma. It is widely recognized that the interaction of inflammatory cytokines with resident cells can lead to a cascade of events that contribute to chronic inflammation of airways and result in AHR and remodeling [43, 44]. To investigate the role of Kiss1R in regulating airway inflammation, and thereby AHR and remodeling, we analyzed cytokine levels in the BALF samples from the lungs of PBS or MA-challenged Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− mice. We have detected and measured eight different cytokines in the BALF. Among the cytokines, the baseline differences were only noted for interleukin-7 (IL-7, Fig. 7G), where PBS-challenged Kiss1R−/− mice showed a significant decrease in IL-7 levels (p < 0.05 for male; p < 0.001 for female) compared to respective PBS-challenged Kiss1R+/+ mice. In comparison with PBS-challenged mice, MA-challenged Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− mice exhibited differential inflammatory cytokine levels in both male and female animals, as demonstrated by increased levels of Eotaxin (p < 0.001 for Kiss1R−/− male and female mice; Fig. 7A), keratinocyte-derived chemokine (KC, p < 0.01 for Kiss1R+/+ male, p < 0.001 for Kiss1R−/− male and female mice; Fig. 7C), Leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF, p < 0.05 for Kiss1R+/+ male, p < 0.001 for Kiss1R−/− male, p < 0.05 for Kiss1R−/− female; Fig. 7D), IL-6 (p < 0.05 for Kiss1R−/− male, p < 0.01 for Kiss1R−/− female; Fig. 7E), IL-4 (p < 0.001 for Kiss1R−/− male, p < 0.01 for Kiss1R−/− female; Fig. 7F), and Interferon-gamma inducible Protein 10 (IP-10, p < 0.001 for Kiss1R−/− female; Fig. 7H). Conversely, MA-challenged Kiss1R+/+ female mice showed a significant decrease in IL-7 level (p < 0.001, Fig. 7G) compared to PBS-challenged Kiss1R+/+ female mice. Here, we observed that MA-challenge tends to increase G-CSF levels in both Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− mice, albeit not significant (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, in comparison between MA-challenged Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− mice (both male and female), we observed a significant increase in Eotaxin (p < 0.05 for Kiss1R−/− male, p < 0.001 for Kiss1R−/− female; Fig. 7A), and IP-10 (p < 0.001 for Kiss1R−/− female; Fig. 7H) levels compared to MA-challenged Kiss1R+/+ mice. Notably, in MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− female mice, we observed a significant decrease in LIF (p < 0.05; Fig. 7D), while an increase in IP-10 (p < 0.01; Fig. 7H) levels compared to MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− male mice. These data demonstrate that an MA challenge significantly altered BALF cytokine levels in Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− mice. Here, we observed significant changes in BALF cytokine levels in Kiss1R−/− mice, with significant changes observed in Kiss1R−/− female mice.

Discussion

Airway remodeling, which involves structural changes in the airway wall, is often considered to be a pathological factor in the development of airway obstruction in people with asthma [8, 12, 48]. In the past, it was believed that airway remodeling was a result of airway inflammation. However, recent advancements suggest that airway remodeling can occur alongside chronic inflammation [49]. This is evident for some asthmatic patients who experience irreversible or partially irreversible airway obstruction despite using traditional anti-inflammatory drugs and bronchodilators [49]. Airway remodeling and inflammation are clinically associated with severe asthma, making a substantial contribution to the severity of asthma. Both processes are interrelated and work hand in hand to increase asthmatic symptoms. Besides the above, chronic inflammation leads to structural changes within the airway wall, further, prolonging and intensifying an already present inflammatory response. This cycle can result in persistent asthma symptoms with diminished lung function [50, 51].

During the process of airway remodeling, irreversible changes happen in the airway wall resulting in an excessive narrowing of airways in response to inhaled constrictor agonists and resulting in AHR, a cardinal feature of asthma [8, 12, 52]. Currently, inhaled corticosteroids are the mainstay treatment to reduce inflammation and underlying AHR [53]. However, recent studies have reported limited success in reversing structural changes caused by airway remodeling [54]. Over time a portion of asthmatics will develop partial or full corticosteroid resistance; those experiencing significant airway remodeling may experience persisting symptoms and airflow limitations despite taking high medication doses. Addressing corticosteroid resistance requires an alternative therapeutic approach targeting airway remodeling pathways to prevent or reverse airway obstruction.

Previously, we found downregulated Kp/KISS1R expression in asthmatic human airways, specifically in ASM cells [26]. Our in vitro studies revealed that KISS1R activation with the specific agonist Kp-10 inhibits PDGF-induced proliferation of human ASM cells by regulating the p38/MAPK signaling pathways [26]. Furthermore, we also demonstrated that KISS1R activation displayed anti-migratory properties through cAMP/PKA-dependent mechanisms in human ASM cells [28]. With the integration of these in vitro studies, we performed in vivo studies using the pharmacological activator of Kiss1R in MA-challenged wild-type mice and showed downregulated AHR and remodeling with activation of Kiss1R [10]. These findings demonstrate a potential therapeutic application of Kp/Kiss1R signaling in regulating airway remodeling. However, the intrinsic role of Kiss1R signaling in the context of asthma pathophysiology has not been characterized yet. To understand these intrinsic mechanisms and to negate the possible cross-reactivity of the pharmacological agonists’ effects, we developed and employed Kiss1R−/− male and female mice in the context of allergic asthma.

Analysis of lung mechanics using flexiVent showed higher Rrs and Ers, and lower Crs in MA-challenged male/female Kiss1R−/− mice compared to MA-challenged male/female Kiss1R+/+ mice. This suggests compromised lung function due to impaired intrinsic Kiss1R signaling. Interestingly, female Kiss1R−/− mice showed more reduced lung function compared to male Kiss1R−/− mice. This indicates a possible negative feedback mechanism of estrogen in regulating Kp/Kiss1R signaling which aligned with previous clinical evidence [55]. It is well-reported that these lung mechanics can be directly influenced due to altered ASM cell functionality and associated airway contractility. During airway contractility, numerous luminal changes occur which alter the airway diameter or shape and eventually affect the airflow dynamics of the airway [56]. Our results from the PCLS study indicated that MA-challenged female Kiss1R+/+/Kiss1R−/− mice exhibited higher airway contractility compared to MA-challenged male Kiss1R+/+/Kiss1R−/− mice. The additional contractile effect in female Kiss1R−/− mice compared to female Kiss1R+/+ mice suggests that Kiss1R signaling may play a part in airway contractility. Several studies have shown that β2-adrenergic receptor activation in ASM cells increases cAMP levels which in turn activates protein kinase A (PKA). Activated PKA reduces intracellular calcium levels and myosin light chain kinase (MLCK) activity, leading to smooth muscle relaxation and bronchodilation [57]. Similarly, in our recent studies, we reported Kp/KISS1R signaling increases intracellular cAMP and controls ASM cell migration via PKA-dependent signaling [28]. Chronic exposure to MA-challenge in mice causes histological modifications in the airway, such as immune cell infiltration, hyperplasia or hypertrophy of smooth muscles, and increased mucus production resulting from goblet cell hyperplasia [10, 20]. We observed these cardinal features were more prominent in MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− mice, which could explain the more severely impaired lung function observed in our lung mechanic’s study. In addition, excess collagen deposition or secretion in MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− mice aligns with the previous finding of Kp’s antifibrotic activity in the bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis mouse model [58]. The technical limitation identified during the histology analysis in our current study pertains to the absence of pressure-perfused insufflation of the lungs with fixative. However, this limitation did not compromise the integrity of the lung airway structures, as we were still able to observe intact airways.

Studies have reported that diverse groups of signaling molecules like cytokines contribute to the shaping of the airway microenvironment through complex signaling pathways. Any alterations of these cytokines during airway pathological conditions affect the structural integrity of airway cells and imbalance the lung function [59]. Most of the cytokine’s expression levels were not altered in the PBS-challenged mice, but were significantly upregulated or downregulated in MA-challenged mice. It has been reported that stimulation of human ASM cells with IL-1β or tumor necrosis factor (TNFα) elicits maximal eotaxin release within 24 h [60]. In addition to our previous studies [10], several studies have indicated that treatment with Kp-10 attenuates the expression levels of both IL-1β and TNFα [61, 62]. These findings suggest a potential association between impaired Kiss1R function and the heightened eotaxin levels observed in MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− mice. Furthermore, the observed elevation of eosinophil levels in MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− mice aligns with the established role of eotaxin in eosinophil recruitment to the airways during asthmatic conditions [63]. In asthmatics, IL-6 and IL-4, which are key Th2 cytokines, are elevated in the serum and BAL fluid at baseline and following allergen challenge [64–66]. Interestingly, we observed a significant increase in expression levels of these cytokines (IL-6 and IL-4) exclusively in MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− mice but not in the PBS-challenged mice. The significant increase in IL-6 in MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− mice is more likely derived from the eosinophils in these mice as eosinophils secrete IL-6 to contribute to the allergic response [65]. While MA-challenged Kiss1R+/+ mice showed elevated levels of eosinophils, the secretory levels of IL-6 in MA-challenged mice are not significant indicating the need for further detailed study. Further, increased levels of granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF) due to Th17 cells were reported in the sputum of patients with asthma and it is also associated with severe neutrophilic asthma development [67, 68]. Increased neutrophils in BAL fluid from MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− mice correlate with the findings that MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− mice tend to increase Th17 cell-mediated G-CSF levels. However, it remains unclear why there are no changes in G-CSF expression levels in MA-challenged Kiss1R+/+ mice indicating potential differences in the regulatory mechanism of G-CSF production between Kiss1R+/+ and Kiss1R−/− mice. At baseline, Kiss1R−/− mice were found to have decreased levels of IL-7 compared to Kiss1R+/+ mice. This change in expression may influence T lymphocyte maturation and differentiation, altering the inflammatory cytokine profile. Other cytokines like interferon (IFN)-γ-induced protein 10 (IP-10), a marker of allergic inflammatory responses in atopic asthma, leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), and keratinocyte-derived cytokine (KC), termed as CXCL-1, were differentially regulated in the MA-challenged Kiss1R+/+/Kiss1R−/− mice. These inflammation-induced changes in expression were observed to be amplified within Kiss1R−/− mice. Here, a notable Th2/Th17-biased effect was observed with loss of Kiss1R signaling during allergen exposure.

The altered or imbalanced inflammatory milieu in MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− mice perpetuated the remodeling process, resulting in a vicious cycle involving inflammation and tissue restructuring. The significant role of Th2 and Th17 cytokines in perpetuating, aggravating, or moderating the remodeling process [34, 69, 70] can be observed in the lung histological and functionality data, where MA-challenged Kiss1R−/− mice exhibited severe structural changes due to excessive airway remodeling. It was evident from the altered expression of airway remodeling-associated proteins such as α-SMA, Ki67, and tenascin-C. These findings suggest the involvement of Kiss1R signaling in modulating inflammation-induced changes in lung structure and functionality. The loss of Kiss1R signaling most likely resulted in increased inflammatory cell infiltration and excess activation of immune responses. The lack of activation of additional compensatory mechanisms to modulate immune activation underscores the clinical significance of Kp/Kiss1R in the lung.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the significance of inherent Kp/Kiss1R signaling in regulating AHR and remodeling in asthma. The loss of Kiss1R in the mice worsened the MA-induced changes in lung functions and structure, resulting in exacerbated airway remodeling and AHR. Additionally, we observed a limited effect on airway inflammatory cytokines, majorly suggesting Th2/Th17 skewed inflammatory cytokines in MA-exposed Kiss1R−/− mice. Importantly, we found that the intrinsic role of Kiss1R in regulating airway contractility is possibly due to changes in remodeling-associated proteins. Together, these findings underline the inherent protective role of Kp/Kiss1R signaling in regulating airway remodeling, inflammation, and AHR and may identify it as a potential therapeutic target against allergic asthma.
