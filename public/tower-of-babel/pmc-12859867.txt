Latent classes of self-management and associated factors in patients with COPD
Authors: Jie Yu, Jianmei Fu, Shujuan Zhao, Shuting Zhang, Qing Li, Dan Zhang, Yulan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective self-management is crucial for Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) patients, yet significant heterogeneity exists in their self-management behaviors. Understanding this variation is key to providing personalized care. This study aimed to identify distinct latent classes of self-management behaviors among COPD patients and explore the factors associated with class membership.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted with 315 COPD inpatients from a tertiary hospital in Hebei, China. Data were collected using the COPD Self-Management Scale, the modified Medical Research Council Dyspnea Scale (mMRC), the Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS), the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), and a sociodemographic/clinical questionnaire. Latent Class Analysis (LCA) was used to identify unobserved subgroups based on self-management patterns. Factors influencing class membership were identified using multivariate logistic regression analysis.ResultsLCA revealed three distinct latent classes of self-management: “Comprehensive Weakness” (45.4%), characterized by poor performance across all domains; “Symptom-Disconnected” (35.9%), showing adequate symptom management but poor daily life/emotion management; and ‘Balanced Management” (18.7%), demonstrating strong performance in all areas. Multivariate analysis indicated that higher education level, longer disease duration, and higher social support were significant predictors of belonging to the “Balanced Management” class compared to the “Comprehensive Weakness” class. Anxiety was a significant negative factor distinguishing the classes.ConclusionsThis study identifies three distinct profiles of self-management behavior in COPD patients, highlighting the need for tailored interventions rather than a one-size-fits-all approach. Factors such as education, disease duration, and social support are key determinants of better self-management. Clinicians should prioritize assessing and addressing anxiety and leverage social support to help patients transition towards more balanced and effective self-management practices.Trial registrationNot applicable. This study is an observational, cross-sectional survey and does not involve a health care intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-04074-1.Keywords: Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Latent class analysis, Influencing factors, Self-management

Introduction

Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), as a common chronic respiratory disorder characterized by persistent airflow limitation, has become the third leading cause of death worldwide. With a prolonged and progressive course, the clinical progression of this disease is marked by acute exacerbations. Frequent acute exacerbations not only accelerate the decline of patients' lung function but also subject them to persistent physical symptoms such as dyspnea, often accompanied by psychological issues like anxiety and depression, leading to severe deterioration in quality of life [1–3]. Moreover, they serve as a critical risk factor for increased readmission rates and mortality [4], imposing a substantial disease burden on both society and families [5–9]. In the long-term management of COPD, relying solely on acute-phase treatment during hospitalization is far from sufficient to achieve optimal disease control. Therefore, self-management has been established as a core strategy for chronic disease management. It requires patients to actively engage in a series of behaviors through collaboration with healthcare teams, including symptom monitoring, regular medication use, rehabilitation exercises, lifestyle adjustments, and coping with acute disease exacerbations [10, 11]. Strong evidence demonstrates that effective self-management can significantly improve the health status of COPD patients, including alleviating dyspnea, enhancing exercise tolerance, reducing the frequency of acute exacerbations and hospitalizations, and ultimately improving their quality of life [12]. However, despite the widely recognized benefits of self-management, clinical practice and research have revealed significant individual variations in patients' self-management behaviors and abilities [13–15]. Traditional variable-centered research approaches typically treat self-management as a homogeneous entity, examining its influencing factors by analyzing correlations between single variables (e.g., total self-management scores) and other factors (e.g., social support, self-efficacy) [16, 17]. While this approach can uncover general relationships between variables, it overlooks potential heterogeneity within patient populations—fundamental differences that may exist among distinct patient types in terms of behavioral patterns, cognitive levels, and skill sets. Simply categorizing individuals with significant differences into a single group for analysis may obscure key subgroup characteristics, thereby diminishing the targeting and effectiveness of interventions. As a person-centered advanced statistical method, Latent Class Analysis (LCA) can identify latent, unobservable subgroups or categories based on individuals' response patterns across multiple manifest indicators (such as various dimensions of self-management) [18]. Compared to traditional cluster analysis, LCA is model-driven, employs objective statistical criteria to determine the optimal number of classes, and provides posterior probabilities for each individual's class membership, yielding more robust and precise results [19]. This method has been successfully applied to the self-management research of patients with chronic diseases such as diabetes and hypertension, revealing distinct patient subgroups with varying characteristics [20–22]. However, in the field of COPD research, studies employing LCA to thoroughly explore the latent class structure of self-management behaviors remain in their infancy and warrant further investigation.

Based on the above background, this study intends to introduce the cutting-edge method of latent class analysis to systematically identify potential categories of self-management among Chinese COPD patients and thoroughly explore the key factors influencing different category affiliations. The implementation of this study will not only theoretically deepen the understanding of the complexity and heterogeneity of COPD patients' self-management but also provide empirical evidence for constructing precise and stratified nursing intervention systems in practice. This will enable precise empowerment for different patient groups with varying needs, ultimately improving the overall management outcomes and quality of life for COPD patients.

Methods

Research methods

Study design: Cross-sectional study. Inclusion criteria: ① Age ≥ 40 years; ② Patients diagnosed with COPD according to GOLD guidelines; ③Hospitalized patients in stable condition; ④ Capable of self-expression and able to complete questionnaires. Exclusion criteria: ① Comorbid severe cognitive impairment or mental illness; ② Comorbid severe acute diseases (e.g., acute myocardial infarction, stroke) or other end-stage diseases; ③ Recent (within 3 months) exposure to major stressful events (e.g., bereavement,major family changes).Sample size estimation: The sample size for latent class analysis was determined based on methodological recommendations. Nylund-Gibson et al. [23] suggest that the minimum sample size for latent class analysis should not be less than 300, with a recommended range of 300–600 participants to ensure model estimation stability and reliable category identification [24].

Participant recruitment process

This study employed convenience sampling of hospitalized COPD patients in the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine at a tertiary hospital in Hebei Province from June to December 2024. The research team initially assessed 389 patients meeting inclusion criteria. After medical record verification and preliminary communication, 33 patients were excluded based on exclusion criteria, specifically: severe cognitive impairment or psychiatric disorders, other terminal illnesses (e.g., advanced cancer, n = 18), recent major stressful events within 3 months (n = 15), or refusal to participate (n = 28). Ultimately, after excluding patients who could not complete the survey due to changes in their condition (n = 3), 325 patients signed informed consent forms. Following the survey, 315 valid questionnaires were retrieved, yielding a valid response rate of 96.92%.The screening flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Survey instrument

General information questionnaire

Self-designed, including age, gender, BMI, smoking history, education level, marital status, and economic status.

Disease-related characteristics questionnaire

The following information will be collected by the investigator through review of the patient's electronic medical record system: number of acute exacerbations in the past year, number of comorbidities, and GOLD staging determined based on pulmonary function test results (FEV1% predicted) [25]. Concurrently, the investigator will assess the severity of dyspnea at the time of questionnaire completion using the mMRC scale, based on the patient's self-reported symptoms.

Symptom assessment tool

Self-management ability: The COPD Self-Management Scale (COPD-SMS) developed by Zhang Caihong et al. [26] was used, which was constructed based on Bandura's social cognitive theory, self-determination theory, and self-efficacy theory. It consists of five dimensions (totaling 51 items): symptom management dimension (8 items); daily life management dimension (14 items); emotional management dimension (12 items); information management dimension (8 items); and self-efficacy dimension (9 items). The scale adopts the Likert 5-point scoring method, with responses categorized as “Always” (5 points), “Often” (4 points), “Sometimes” (3 points), “Rarely” (2 points), and “Never” (1 point). The total score ranges from 51 to 255 points, with higher scores indicating stronger self-management abilities. The scale's Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.92 [27].

Measurement tools for influencing factors

Dyspnea

Graded using the mMRC scale [28], where grade 0 indicates breathlessness only during strenuous exercise; grade 1 denotes shortness of breath when walking up a slight incline or brisk walking on level ground; grade 2 refers to patients walking slower than peers on level ground due to breathlessness or needing to stop for rest; grade 3 indicates patients needing to stop for breath after walking about 100 m or after a few minutes on level ground; grade 4 describes patients being too breathless to leave home or experiencing dyspnea during dressing/undressing.

Cough and sputum production

Assessed using the CAT scale [29], which includes items such as chest tightness, sputum production, sleep, energy, mood, cough, and mobility. The total score ranges from 0 to 40 points, with a score ≥ 2 points/item recorded as “present.” Higher scores indicate more severe disease conditions.

Fatigue

The Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) was used for assessment. This scale was developed by American scholar Krupp et al. [30], and was translated into Chinese by domestic scholar Wu Chunwei [31]. It is a unidimensional scale consisting of 9 items, each rated using a Likert 7-point scoring method. The total score of the scale is 63 points, with higher scores indicating more severe fatigue. A total mean score of the 9 items ≥ 4 points is considered indicative of post-stroke fatigue. The scale demonstrates good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach's α coefficient of 0.929.

Anxiety and depression

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) was used for participants to self-assess their emotional status over the past month [32]. The odd-numbered items constitute the anxiety subscale (items 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, and 13), while the even-numbered items form the depression subscale (items 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14). Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms (0 = absence of symptoms, 3 = severe symptoms). A score of ≥ 8 on either subscale was classified as “presence” of the condition.

Sleep disorders

Assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [33], which consists of 7 component questions. Each component is scored from 0 to 3 points, with a total score > 7 indicating “presence” of sleep disturbance. The scale score ranges from 0 to 21 points, where higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality.

Pain

The BPI is a brief self-administered questionnaire primarily assessing pain severity and the degree to which pain interferes with life [34]. The pain severity score is evaluated through four items: “worst pain, least pain, average pain, and current pain” (pain at the time of completing the questionnaire) experienced by the patient in the past 24 h. Each item is scored on a scale from 0 = "no pain" to 10 = "pain as bad as you can imagine,” with equal weighting in the final score, which ranges from 0 to 40. The pain interference score is calculated based on seven sub-items assessing how pain interferes with “enjoyment of life, general activity, walking ability, mood, sleep, and normal work.”

Social support

The Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS) [35] was used to measure the level of social support among study subjects. The SSRS consists of 10 items, including three dimensions: objective support (3 items), subjective support (4 items), and support utilization (3 items). Higher scores indicate a higher level of corresponding social support [35].

Data collection

Uniformly trained researchers conducted the questionnaire survey using standardized instructions after obtaining patients' consent. For patients unable to complete the forms independently, researchers read each item aloud and recorded responses on their behalf to ensure data quality. All data were collected anonymously and entered with dual verification.

Data collection is conducted face-to-face using paper questionnaires by researchers who have undergone standardized training. This occurs either during the patient's hospitalization when their condition is stable or during outpatient follow-up visits. After obtaining the patient's informed consent, the researcher explains the purpose of the survey and how to complete it using standardized instructions.

To ensure data quality, we implemented the following measures: (1) Investigator Training: All investigators received standardized training to familiarize themselves with the instructions, scoring criteria, and potential responses for each questionnaire. (2) Standardized Assistance: For patients unable to complete the questionnaire independently due to visual impairment, limited literacy, or physical weakness, investigators read each question and option aloud in a neutral, non-leading tone and recorded responses based on the patient's verbal selections. The entire process ensures patients fully comprehend each question's meaning. (3) Immediate Verification: Upon completing the questionnaire, investigators promptly check for omissions on-site and confirm key information (e.g., pain scores, emotional state) reflects the patient's true intentions. (4) Data Entry: All data undergoes dual independent entry and cross-verification to ensure accuracy.

Statistical methods

Data management

Double data entry was performed, with statistical analyses conducted using SPSS 26.0 and Mplus 8.3. Descriptive analysis: Patient demographic characteristics were described using mean ± standard deviation and frequency (percentage). Latent class analysis: Step 1: Determine the number of classes. Models with 1 to 5 latent classes were sequentially fitted. Step 2: Model comparison and selection. The optimal number of classes was determined based on the following fit indices:Log-Likelihood (LL), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), adjusted BIC (aBIC)—smaller values indicate better fit; Entropy: > 0.8 indicates high classification accuracy. Likelihood Ratio Test (LMR) and Bootstrap Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT): P < 0.05 indicates that the model with k classes is significantly superior to the model with k-1 classes. Step 3: Class Naming and Interpretation. Based on the conditional probability distribution characteristics of each class across various self-management indicators, the latent classes were named and described. Univariate Analysis: Chi-square tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to compare differences in general characteristics among different latent classes. Multivariate Analysis: Using latent class membership as the dependent variable (with the class having the highest probability as the reference group), variables with statistical significance in the univariate analysis were included as independent variables in a multivariate logistic regression analysis to explore independent factors influencing class membership. The significance level was set at α = 0.05.

Result

A total of 315 COPD patients were included in the final analysis.

Common method bias test

To avoid common method bias resulting from the exclusive use of questionnaires for data collection, which may introduce subjective responses from participants, this study employed Harman's single-factor test to examine the COPD-SMS scale. The results revealed 18 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, among which the first factor accounted for 23.68% of the variance—below the critical threshold of 40%. This indicates that common method bias was not substantial in this study.

Latent class analysis of self-management in COPD patients

A latent class analysis was conducted on the scores of each dimension of the COPD-SMS, using the scores as manifest variables, and models with 1 to 5 latent classes were fitted. As the number of latent classes increased, the AIC, BIC, and aBIC indices showed a decreasing trend in the 1- to 4-class models. However, the aBIC value of the 5-class model was higher than that of the 4-class model, and the BLRT and LMR tests of the 5-class model did not show statistically significant differences. Additionally, the 4-class model included classifications with class probabilities below 5%, indicating that the goodness-of-fit of these models was not superior to that of the 4-class model. Taking into account the indicator results, the practical significance of the classification, and the interpretability of the classification probabilities, the 3-class model (3C) was ultimately determined. Discussion

Characteristics of potential categories in COPD patients’ self-management

This study identified three distinct self-management patterns with significant heterogeneity among 315 COPD patients through latent class analysis (LCA). Each category demonstrated marked differences in demographic characteristics, disease features, and psychological status, which aligns with the core findings of previous subgroup studies on chronic disease self-management [36]. This further validates the inherently heterogeneous nature of self-management behaviors in COPD patients.

The comprehensively deficient type (56.19%) represents the most prevalent category, characterized by the lowest conditional probabilities across all self-management dimensions (symptoms, daily life, emotions, information, and self-efficacy) ranging from 0.046 to 0.372, with self-efficacy (0.046) being nearly absent in particular. The formation of this subgroup may be associated with multiple overlapping factors. At the disease awareness level, such patients often harbor the misconception that their condition is irreversible and thus requires no management, leading to insufficient proactive symptom monitoring, medication adherence, and regular rehabilitation exercises [37]. From a psychological perspective, extremely low self-efficacy undermines patients' confidence in the ability of self-management to improve prognosis, creating a vicious cycle of “attempt-failure-abandonment” [38, 39]. Additionally, this subgroup has a higher proportion of females (49.7%) and elderly individuals (23.2% aged 75–90 years), whose physiological decline (e.g., reduced muscle strength and memory impairment) may further limit their capacity to perform complex self-management behaviors (e.g., medication dosage calculation and information acquisition) [40, 41]. It is noteworthy that this group of patients accounts for more than half of the total, suggesting that current COPD self-management interventions may lack generalizability. There is an urgent need to develop foundational intervention programs targeting core weaknesses across all dimensions.

Significant differences were observed across categories in demographic characteristics, disease features, and psychological states (see Table 2). This aligns with core findings from chronic disease self-management subgroup studies [36], further validating the inherent heterogeneity of self-management behaviors among COPD patients.

The “Comprehensive Weakness” category (56.19%) was the most prevalent in this study, characterized by the lowest conditional probabilities across all self-management dimensions (symptoms, daily life, emotions, information, and self-efficacy) ranging from 0.046 to 0.372, with self-efficacy (0.046) being nearly absent. The formation of this subgroup likely involves multiple overlapping factors. Regarding disease cognition, these patients may hold the misconception that “the disease is irreversible, so management is unnecessary” [37]. Psychologically, extremely low self-efficacy undermines patients' confidence that self-management can improve outcomes, potentially creating a vicious cycle of “attempt-failure-abandonment” [38, 39]. Furthermore, this subgroup in our study exhibited a higher proportion of females (49.7%) and older age (23.2% aged 75–90 years). Their physiological decline (e.g., reduced muscle strength, memory impairment) may further limit their ability to perform complex self-management behaviors (e.g., medication dose calculation, information retrieval) [40, 41]. Notably, these patients constituted over half of the total sample, suggesting that current COPD self-management interventions may lack universal applicability. There is an urgent need to design foundational intervention programs targeting core weaknesses across multiple dimensions.

The symptom-disengaged subtype (9.21%) exhibits a paradoxical profile of strong psychological-daily management but extremely weak symptom management: its conditional probabilities for daily life management (0.999), emotional regulation (0.965), information management (1.000), and self-efficacy (1.000) approach maximum values, while symptom management (0.003) remains critically deficient. This distinctive pattern may stem from patients' cognitive bias regarding the value of symptom management [42]: although these patients proactively acquire information through measures like reviewing disease guidelines and employ mindfulness-based stress reduction to regulate emotions—thereby maintaining quality of life—they may perceive symptoms such as dyspnea and coughing as inherent disease attributes rather than modifiable indicators, reflecting inadequate disease understanding [43]. Moreover, this patient group had a high proportion of males (75.9%) and individuals with a smoking history exceeding 10 years (69.0%). The long-term smoking exposure may have led to symptom tolerance, causing them to overlook the warning significance of symptom changes [44]. Although these patients demonstrate strong self-management awareness, deficiencies in symptom management may become hidden triggers for their increased risk of acute exacerbations, necessitating focused correction of cognitive biases [45].

The “Symptom Disengagement” subtype (9.21%) exhibits a distinct management contradiction pattern: it demonstrates near-perfect conditional probabilities across daily life management (0.999), emotional regulation (0.965), information management (1.000), and self-efficacy (1.000) dimensions, yet shows extreme deficiency in symptom management for direct discomfort relief (0.003). In this study, this subgroup exhibited high proportions of males (75.9%) and long-term smokers (69.0% with > 10 years of smoking history). Their CAT score (16.52 ± 5.902) was the highest among the three groups, indicating potentially the heaviest symptom burden. This dichotomy of “strong psychological-daily life coping, weak symptom management” may stem from patients' cognitive biases regarding symptom management value [42]: although they actively seek information and employ mindfulness techniques to regulate emotions and maintain quality of life, they may perceive symptoms like dyspnea and cough as inherent, unchangeable features of the disease rather than modifiable indicators. This reflects inadequate disease cognition [43]. Additionally, a long history of smoking may lead to heightened tolerance for respiratory symptoms, causing patients to overlook warning signals of symptom changes [44]. Although such patients possess strong self-management awareness and skills, the absence of symptom management—a critical component—may become a latent trigger for acute exacerbation risk, necessitating targeted correction of their cognitive biases [45].

The Balanced Management type (34.60%) represents an ideal model with prominent symptom management core and multi-dimensional coordination: the conditional probability of symptom management reaches 0.999, while emotional and information management also maintain high levels (0.959, 0.999). Only daily life management (0.565) and self-efficacy (0.457) are slightly lower. The advantages of this patient subgroup may stem from a virtuous cycle of disease cognition, behavioral execution, and social support [46]. On one hand, these patients demonstrate higher proficiency in symptom monitoring and acute exacerbation management, enabling effective symptom control to reduce discomfort and thereby enhance willingness to perform daily self-management [47–49]. On the other hand, this group exhibits a higher proportion of married individuals (82.6%) and households with monthly incomes exceeding ¥10,000 (12.8%), which may reinforce self-management sustainability through spousal support (e.g., medication reminders) and economic resources (e.g., purchasing home spirometry devices) [50]. The existence of this subgroup validates the feasibility of improving COPD prognosis through scientific management, providing a replicable behavioral template for other patient categories.

On one hand, these patients demonstrate high proficiency in symptom monitoring and acute exacerbation management, enabling them to alleviate discomfort through effective symptom control, thereby enhancing their willingness to perform daily self-management behaviors [47–49]. On the other hand, stable marital status (e.g., medication reminders from spouses) and better economic conditions (e.g., affordability of home spirometers) provide crucial social support and resource guarantees for sustainable self-management [50]. The existence of this subgroup validates the feasibility of improving COPD prognosis through scientific management and offers a behavioral model for other patient categories to emulate.

In summary, this study represents the first application of the LCA method to systematically identify three distinct latent categories of self-management among hospitalized COPD patients in China, revealing their unique demographic and clinical characteristics. This approach addresses the limitations of previous variable-centered research that treated patients as a homogeneous group, providing new, person-centered evidence for understanding the complexity and heterogeneity of COPD self-management. This classification framework serves as a prerequisite for implementing precision interventions in subsequent studies.

Analysis of influencing factors for potential categories of self-management in COPD patients

Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that fatigue level (FSS), anxiety and depression symptoms (HADS), monthly household income, and marital status were independent factors influencing patients' self-management category classification. Fatigue is one of the most common and distressing symptoms in COPD patients, with severe fatigue significantly impairing both the willingness and ability to perform self-management behaviors. The results of this study demonstrate that higher fatigue levels increase the likelihood of patients belonging to either the symptom-disconnected type or the comprehensively weak type, suggesting that fatigue management should be an essential component of comprehensive COPD interventions. Anxiety and depression not only affect patients' quality of life but also interfere with cognitive function and behavioral decision-making, leading to diminished self-management capacity [51]. In this study, higher HADS scores indicated greater difficulty for patients in establishing effective self-management models, further confirming the central role of psychological status in chronic disease management [52]. Economic and marital status reflect patients' socioeconomic support levels. Low income may restrict patients' access to medical resources, medications, and rehabilitation equipment, while the absence of marital relationships may diminish emotional and instrumental support, collectively leading to reduced self-management capacity. Notably, divorced patients showed significantly lower proportions in the balanced management type, suggesting that marital stability has a protective effect on the formation of effective self-management behaviors.

This study identified fatigue (FSS), anxiety and depression (HADS), monthly household income, and marital status as key factors influencing the category attribution of self-management in COPD patients through multivariate logistic regression. The underlying mechanisms require in-depth analysis based on the biopsychosocial model.

Fatigue (FSS): a cross-category common “behavioral regulation factor”

Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) showed significant effects on both non-global deficiency subtypes (C2 and C3): In C2 (symptom-disconnection type) vs C1 (global deficiency type), each 1-point increase in FSS was associated with a 32.9% higher probability of being classified as C2 (OR = 1.329, 95%CI: 1.104–1.600); In C3 (balanced-management type) vs C1, each 1-point increase in FSS was associated with a 15.1% higher probability of being classified as C3 (OR = 1.151, 95%CI: 1.033–1.282). This finding aligns with previous research concluding that fatigue is a double-edged sword in COPD patients' self-management behaviors: On one hand, moderate to severe fatigue may prompt patients to actively adjust their daily activity rhythms and alleviate fatigue-related irritability through emotional management, resulting in higher scores in daily living and emotional dimensions (characteristics of C2). On the other hand, some patients may perceive fatigue as a signal of poor symptom control, thereby proactively intensifying symptom monitoring and optimizing medication regimens, forming a virtuous cycle of fatigue perception-symptom management-fatigue relief (characteristics of C3) [53, 54]. In contrast, patients with generalized weakness may experience behavioral energy depletion due to fatigue [55], lacking both the capacity to adjust daily activities and the motivation to cope with symptoms, ultimately falling into a vicious cycle of worsening fatigue and abandoned management [56].

Anxiety and depression (HADS): symptom-dissociated “psychological drivers”

The anxiety and depression score (HADS) showed significant influence only on C2 (symptom-disconnected type) versus C1 (globally-deficient type) (OR = 1.521, 95% CI: 1.197 ~ 1.933), indicating that patients with more severe anxiety and depression symptoms were more likely to be classified as symptom-disconnected type rather than globally-deficient type. From the perspective of psychological mechanisms, anxiety and depression in COPD patients may influence self-management priorities through attentional bias: On one hand, patients with anxiety and depression tend to focus more on stabilizing their emotional state. They actively seek psychological support and learn emotion regulation techniques to alleviate negative emotions, resulting in higher scores in the emotional management dimension [57]. On the other hand, negative emotions may obscure the perception and prioritization of physical symptoms—patients might attribute dyspnea to anxiety attacks rather than the disease itself, or view coughing and sputum as insignificant accompanying symptoms, leading to significant deficiencies in symptom management behaviors [58]. This finding suggests that interventions for symptom-disconnected patients require a dual approach combining psychological and symptom management [59], avoiding the potential risk of focusing solely on emotional improvement while neglecting symptom control.

Household monthly income: a “resource protection factor” for symptom dissociation type

Household monthly income serves as a significant protective factor for C2 (symptom-disengaged type) versus C1 (comprehensively deficient type): Compared with the < 3000 RMB group, patients in the 3000–6000 RMB group (OR = 0.005, 95%CI: 0.001–0.050) and 6000–10000 RMB group (OR = 0.005, 95%CI: 0.001–0.045) showed significantly lower probabilities of being classified as C2. This outcome can be explained by the resource accessibility theory: higher-income patients have greater access to professional medical resources (such as regular follow-ups and personalized symptom management guidance), family support resources (such as hiring caregivers for symptom monitoring), and health information resources (such as subscribing to specialized disease journals), thereby avoiding the disconnect phenomenon of prioritizing psychological-daily life over symptom management [60–62]. In contrast, lower-income patients may be forced to focus their self-management efforts on maintaining basic daily living activities while neglecting symptom management due to the inability to afford pulmonary function monitoring devices (e.g., peak flow meters) or rehabilitation training costs (e.g., pulmonary rehabilitation programs). Moreover, high-income patients may have higher education levels (although no significant difference in education was observed in this study), leading to better awareness of the association between symptom management and acute exacerbations, thereby further reducing the risk of symptom disconnect [63].

Marital status (Divorced): the “Social Support Bottleneck” of balanced management type

Among C3 (balanced management type) vs C1 (comprehensively deficient type), divorced patients showed significantly lower probability of being classified as C3 (OR = 0.074, 95%CI: 0.006–0.961), suggesting that lack of marital support may hinder patients from achieving balanced management across all dimensions. The role of marital support in COPD self-management is primarily reflected in three aspects: First, behavioral reminders—spouses can assist patients in recording medication schedules and symptom changes (e.g., nighttime cough frequency) to prevent omissions in critical management steps. Second, emotional motivation—spousal encouragement enhances patients' self-efficacy, strengthening their willingness to adhere to rehabilitation exercises and regular medical visits. Third, resource coordination—spouses can accompany patients to appointments and help access medical information, reducing the difficulty of information management. Deficiencies in these areas (e.g., medication adherence, symptom management during acute exacerbations without assistance) may lead to imbalanced management levels [64–66]. This finding further validates the classic theory that “social support is a core pillar of chronic disease self-management”.

For the comprehensive weakness type: constructing a foundational capacity empowerment intervention program

Cognitive Restructuring Phase: Correct the misconception that “disease management is unnecessary if the condition is irreversible” by improving disease awareness. Emphasize the connection between symptom monitoring and acute exacerbation prevention, using case studies to enhance persuasiveness. Skill Training Phase: Adopt a simplified operation + repeated practice model to train core skills. Distribute an illustrated Self-Management Manual and conduct one-on-one simulation assessments to ensure skill mastery. Efficacy Reinforcement Phase: Introduce a micro-goal motivation method, such as completing three abdominal breathing exercises daily for a week, with point rewards for each goal achieved. Simultaneously, establish peer support groups and invite well-managed patients to share experiences, thereby boosting self-efficacy.

For the symptom-disconnection type: implement symptom management compensation + cognitive correction intervention

Visually demonstrate the logical chain of uncontrolled cough and sputum → aggravated airway inflammation → increased risk of acute exacerbation through a symptom-prognosis correlation map. This is combined with the patient’s own CAT score (in this study, the CAT score of C2 reached 16.52 ± 5.902) to help them recognize the urgency of symptom management. Provide a portable symptom diary and a home peak flow meter, guiding patients to record daily and upload data via the WeChat Mini Program. Healthcare professionals provide weekly feedback on symptom trends and promptly alert patients to abnormal signals (e.g., a 10% decline in FEV1 from baseline). While preserving their strength in emotional management, combine mindfulness-based stress reduction with symptom coping strategies. For example, design mindfulness breathing exercises during dyspnea episodes to alleviate anxiety while enhancing proactive symptom management capabilities.

For the balanced management type: leverage the peer education + exemplary leadership role

Peer educator training

Select well-balanced, communicative patients with strong self-management skills to undergo peer mentoring training, enabling them to become core members of community-based COPD self-management groups. 2. Experience Dissemination Mechanism: Share their symptom-emotion-daily life co-management experiences through patient storytelling sessions, short video sharing, and other formats. For example, monitoring peak flow after breakfast while listening to 10 min of soothing music to alleviate fatigue, providing replicable templates for other patient categories. 3. Prognosis Reinforcement Intervention: Regularly provide them with advanced management content to prevent skill regression due to stagnant self-management practices.

Addressing key influencing factors: establishing a multi-dimensional support and safeguard system

Fatigue and anxiety-depression intervention

In addition to routine pulmonary function tests, implement FSS + HADS screening. For patients with abnormal scores (FSS ≥ 4, HADS ≥ 8), provide cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) combined with breathing relaxation exercises in collaboration with the psychology department, while optimizing medication regimens. Low-Income Patient Support: Partner with healthcare insurance authorities to include COPD self-management kits in medical insurance coverage, offer free pulmonary rehabilitation courses at community health service centers, and provide one-on-one home visitation services for low-income patients. Divorced/Living-Alone Patient Social Support: Establish a community-family-healthcare linkage mechanism, match divorced/living-alone patients with health volunteers for regular assistance with medical visits and medication procurement, and create peer support groups for living-alone patients to facilitate daily communication and mutual reminders.

Study limitations and future perspectives

Although this study is the first to systematically identify potential categories and influencing factors of self-management in COPD patients, it still has the following limitations: Limitations in study design: The cross-sectional design can only describe the distribution of categories and the association of influencing factors, without inferring causality; moreover, the latent class analysis is based on patient self-reported scale data, which may be subject to social desirability bias. Limitations in sample representativeness: The sample was exclusively sourced from hospitals in a single region, failing to include patients from community or rural areas, with the age distribution predominantly concentrated between 60–74 years (accounting for 51.7%−65.5%). The underrepresentation of younger COPD patients (< 60 years) may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, while the sample size (315 cases) meets the basic requirements for LCA (300–600 cases), it remains insufficient for validating a 5-class model, potentially overlooking niche subgroups.

Given the aforementioned limitations, future research could advance in the following directions: Conduct prospective cohort studies lasting 1–2 years to track the stability of patients' self-management categories, analyze the associations between different categories and prognostic indicators such as acute exacerbation rates, hospitalization rates, and quality of life (e.g., SGRQ scores), thereby clarifying the clinical significance of each category; Adopt multicenter, stratified sampling methods to include COPD patients from communities, rural areas, and different age groups to enhance the generalizability of the results.

Conclusion

This study conducted a systematic analysis of self-management behaviors in 315 COPD patients using latent class analysis (LCA). The results revealed that COPD patients' self-management could be distinctly categorized into three latent classes: comprehensively deficient type (56.19%, exhibiting poor self-management across all dimensions with extremely low self-efficacy), symptom-disconnected type (9.21%, demonstrating strong daily living, emotional and information management but extremely weak symptom management), and balanced management type (34.60%, featuring prominent core symptom management with coordinated multidimensional balance). Significant differences were observed among these classes in terms of demographic characteristics, disease features, and psychological status. Fatigue severity (FSS), anxiety-depression levels (HADS), monthly household income, and marital status (divorced) emerged as key factors influencing patients' self-management classification: patients with more severe fatigue and pronounced anxiety-depression were more likely to be classified as symptom-disconnected or balanced management types rather than comprehensively deficient type; higher monthly household income decreased the probability of being classified as symptom-disconnected type; divorced patients showed significantly lower probability of being classified as balanced management type compared to non-divorced patients. The study highlights significant heterogeneity in COPD patients' self-management behaviors, indicating the necessity to abandon one-size-fits-all intervention models in clinical practice. Instead, classification-stratified and targeted precision management strategies should be implemented based on patients' self-management categories and key influencing factors, coupled with establishing a multidimensional support system, to effectively enhance COPD patients' self-management capacity and ultimately improve their prognosis and quality of life.
