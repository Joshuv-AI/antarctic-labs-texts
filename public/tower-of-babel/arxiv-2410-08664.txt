Modelling of warm dense hydrogen via explicit real time electron dynamics: Electron transport properties
Pontus Svensson, Patrick Hollebon, Daniel Plummer, Sam M. Vinko, Gianluca Gregori

Abstract
We extract electron transport properties from atomistic simulations of a two-component plasma, by mapping the long-wavelength behaviour to a two-fluid model. The mapping procedure is performed via Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling over multiple spectra simultaneously. The free-electron dynamic structure factor and its properties have been investigated in the hydrodynamic formulation to justify its application to the long-wavelength behaviour of warm dense matter. We have applied this method to warm dense hydrogen modelled with wave packet molecular dynamics, and showed that the inferred electron transport properties are in agreement with a variety of reference calculations, except for the electron viscosity, where a substantive decrease is observed when compared to classical models.

II
Hydrodynamic model

The hydrodynamic model for the electron-electron dynamic structure factor,
S_{ee}(\bm{k},\omega)
, must capture plasma oscillations which are primarily damped by the interaction with ions. Therefore, a two-fluid model is examined which has collisional momentum and energy transfer between electrons and ions, in combination with Poisson’s equation for the large scale electric fields. Schmidt
et al.

[
29
]
considered a quantum hydrodynamic formulation for electrons in a single fluid treatment, however, the correct limiting behaviour of the plasmon feature was not obtained due to a lack of electron-ion collisions. We therefore consider a two-fluid formulation, but limit ourselves to a classical hydrodynamic formulation, which is further motivated in section
III
. Specifically, the fluid equations of interest are for electrons (
\mu=e
,
\bar{\mu}=i
) and ions (
\mu=i
,
\bar{\mu}=e
) respectively,

\frac{D\rho_{\mu}}{Dt}+\rho_{\mu}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v}_{\mu}=0,

(1a)

\rho_{\mu}\frac{D\bm{v}_{\mu}}{Dt}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{P}_{\mu}=-\frac{q_{\mu}}{m_{\mu}}\rho_{\mu}\bm{\nabla}\phi-\bm{R}_{\mu\bar{\mu}}\left(\rho_{\mu},\bm{v}_{\mu}-\bm{v}_{\bar{\mu}}\right),

(1b)

\displaystyle\rho_{\mu}\frac{D\epsilon_{\mu}}{Dt}+\mathbf{P}_{\mu}\colon\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{\mu}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{q}_{\mu}=

\displaystyle-\frac{q_{\mu}}{m_{\mu}}\rho_{\mu}\bm{v}_{\mu}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\phi

(1c)

\displaystyle-g\left(T_{\mu}-T_{\bar{\mu}}\right),

\frac{D}{Dt}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\bm{v}_{\mu}\cdot\bm{\nabla},

(2)

The constitutive relations for the pressure tensor are chosen to be of Navier–Stokes type

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\left[p_{\mu}-\zeta_{\mu}\left(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v}_{\mu}\right)\right]\mathbf{I}

(3)

\displaystyle-\eta_{\mu}\Big[\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{\mu}+\left(\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{\mu}\right)^{\intercal}-\frac{2}{3}\left(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v}_{\mu}\right)\mathbf{I}\Big],

where

is the (thermodynamic) pressure and

as shear and bulk viscosity respectively. The heat flux is described by Fourier’s law
[

,

\bm{q}_{\mu}=-\kappa_{\mu}\bm{\nabla}T_{\mu},

(4)

Momentum transfer via collisions is modelled by

\bm{R}_{\mu\bar{\mu}}\left(\rho_{\mu},\Delta\bm{v}\right)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\tau\;\rho_{\mu}(\tau)r_{\mu\bar{\mu}}(\tau)\,\Delta\bm{v}(t-\tau),

[

,

, commonly used in combination with Fourier heat flow, e.g. Refs.

,
36

, and is a special case of a more general heat transport model
[

Lastly, the system of equations (

\varepsilon_{0}\bm{\nabla}^{2}\phi=-\left(\frac{q_{i}}{m_{i}}\rho_{i}+\frac{q_{e}}{m_{e}}\rho_{e}\right).

(6)

where

Using thermodynamic relations, the energy conservation equation (

\displaystyle\rho_{\mu}C_{V,\mu}\frac{DT_{\mu}}{Dt}

\displaystyle=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{q}_{\mu}+\left(\zeta_{\mu}-\frac{2\eta_{\mu}}{3}\right)\left(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v}_{\mu}\right)^{2}

(7)

\displaystyle+\eta_{\mu}\left[\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{\mu}+\left(\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{\mu}\right)^{\intercal}\right]\colon\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}_{\mu}

\displaystyle-\rho_{\mu}C_{V,\mu}\frac{\gamma_{\mu}-1}{\alpha_{T,\mu}}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v}_{\mu}

\displaystyle-\frac{q_{\mu}}{m_{\mu}}\rho_{\mu}\bm{v}_{\mu}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\phi-g\left(T_{\mu}-T_{\bar{\mu}}\right),

\bm{\nabla}p_{\mu}=\frac{c_{s,\mu}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mu}}\left(\bm{\nabla}\rho_{\mu}+\rho_{\mu}\alpha_{T,\mu}\bm{\nabla}T_{\mu}\right),

II.1
Linearised hydrodynamic equations

To investigate correlation spectrum of small amplitude density-density fluctuations, the fluid system is linearised around a static and homogeneous equilibrium. Due to the large mass ratio
m_{i}/m_{e}
, motions develop on two distinct time scales, the comparatively
slow
ion and screening dynamics, and the
fast
plasma oscillations. Specifically, we consider the perturbations,

\begin{aligned} \rho_{e}&=\rho_{0,e}+\delta\bar{\rho}_{e}+\delta\rho_{e},\\
\bm{v}_{e}&=\delta\bar{\bm{v}}_{e}+\delta\bm{v}_{e},\\
T_{e}&=T_{0,e}+\delta\bar{T}_{e}+\delta T_{e},\end{aligned}\quad\begin{aligned} \rho_{i}&=\rho_{0,i}+\delta\bar{\rho}_{i,0},\\
\bm{v}_{i}&=\delta\bar{\bm{v}}_{i},\\
T_{i}&=T_{0,i}+\delta\bar{T}_{i},\end{aligned}

(9)

where
0
index represents the equilibrium properties, the ”bar” represents perturbations on slow time scales and the remaining perturbations are fast. Ions only exhibit slow perturbations. Charge neutrality requires
q_{i}\rho_{0,i}/m_{i}=-q_{e}\rho_{0,e}/m_{e}
and temperature equilibrium is assumed
T_{0,i}=T_{0,e}
. The separation into slow and fast dynamics directly corresponds to the screening cloud and free electron terms in the Chihara decomposition
[
41
,
42
]
, which is often used to interpret electron structure factors. For now, we are mainly concerned with free electron dynamics (fast perturbations), but the slow dynamics may also be solved to describe the electron screening of ions and ion-acoustic waves.

To isolate the fast dynamics, the electron equations are time averaged over time scales that are long with respect both the inverse (electron) plasma frequency
\omega_{p}^{-1}
and the time scale of thermal electron motion. Over these longer time scales fast perturbations are assumed to average out and subtracting the result from the original equations isolates the fast perturbations to linear order,

\frac{\partial\delta\rho_{e}}{\partial t}+\rho_{0,e}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\delta\bm{v}_{e}=0,

(10a)

\displaystyle\rho_{0,e}\frac{\partial\delta\bm{v}_{e}}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle-\frac{c_{s,e}^{2}}{\gamma_{e}}\left(\bm{\nabla}\delta\rho_{e}+\rho_{0,e}\alpha_{T,e}\bm{\nabla}\delta T_{e,0}\right)

(10b)

\displaystyle+\eta_{e}\bm{\nabla}^{2}\delta\bm{v}_{e}+(\zeta_{e}+\eta_{e}/3)\bm{\nabla}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\delta\bm{v}_{e}

\displaystyle-\frac{q_{e}}{m_{e}}\rho_{0,e}\bm{\nabla}\delta\phi-\bm{R}_{ei}\left(\rho_{0,e},\delta\bm{v}_{e}\right),

\displaystyle\rho_{0,e}C_{V,e}\frac{\partial\delta T_{e}}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle-\rho_{0,e}C_{V,e}\frac{\gamma_{e}-1}{\alpha_{T,e}}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\delta\bm{v}_{e}

(10c)

\displaystyle+\kappa_{e}\bm{\nabla}^{2}\delta T_{e}-g\delta T_{e},

\bm{\nabla}^{2}\delta\phi=-\frac{q_{e}}{m_{e}\varepsilon_{0}}\delta\rho_{e},

(10d)

and the transport coefficients are constant to linear order. In a higher order treatment – an equivalent of
Zakharov’s equations

[
43
]
– the slow and fast dynamics couple together, however, such treatment is beyond the scope of the current manuscript.

The density (
10a
) and temperature (
10c
) equations only couple to the divergence of the perturbed velocity field
\bm{\nabla}\cdot\delta\bm{v}_{e}
. Therefore, two velocity degrees of freedom – the shear modes – decouple from the density fluctuations
[
30
]
and only three modes will appear in the density dynamics, which will be shown to correspond to one entropy and two plasmon modes.

II.2
Hydrodynamic fluctuations and dynamic structure factors

The spectrum of density-density correlations is examined following the well-established methodology of Refs.
[
44
,
40
,
30
,
32
,
29
,
39
,
45
]
, where the dynamics of the perturbations are analysed through a spatial Fourier transform in combination with a temporal Laplace transform. Using the notation,

\delta\tilde{\bm{x}}^{\mu}_{\bm{k}}(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!dt\,e^{-st}\int d^{3}\bm{r}\,e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\delta\bm{x}_{\mu}(\bm{r},t),

(11)

for a vector quantity
\delta\bm{x}_{\mu}
, the linearised system (
10d
) may be written as,

\left[s\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{D}_{e}(\bm{k})\right]\,\delta\tilde{\bm{y}}^{e}_{\bm{k}}(s)=\delta\bm{y}^{e}_{\bm{k}}(0),

(12)

for combined perturbation variables
\delta\tilde{\bm{y}}^{\mu}_{\bm{k}}(s)
and initial conditions
\delta\bm{y}^{\mu}_{\bm{k}}(0)
, where

\delta\tilde{\bm{y}}^{\mu}_{\bm{k}}(s)=\Big[\delta\tilde{\rho}^{\mu}_{\bm{k}}(s),\,i\rho_{0,\mu}\bm{k}\cdot\delta\tilde{\bm{v}}^{\mu}_{\bm{k}}(s),\,\rho_{0,\mu}\delta\tilde{T}^{\mu}_{\bm{k}}(s)\Big]^{\intercal},\quad\delta\bm{y}^{\mu}_{\bm{k}}(0)=\Big[\delta\rho^{\mu}_{\bm{k}}(0),\,i\rho_{0,\mu}\bm{k}\cdot\delta\bm{v}^{\mu}_{\bm{k}}(0)+i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{R}_{0,\bm{k}}/s,\,\rho_{0,\mu}\delta T^{\mu}_{\bm{k}}(0)\Big]^{\intercal}.

(13)

The variables without a tilde are evaluated in the time domain and represent the initial condition.
\bm{R}_{0,\bm{k}}
describes the contribution to the momentum transfer from
t<0
. The linearised dynamics is completely determined by the hydrodynamic matrix,

\mathbf{D}_{e}(\bm{k})=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\
-\left(\omega_{p}^{2}+\gamma_{e}^{-1}c_{s,e}^{2}\bm{k}^{2}\right)&\nu_{ei}+\nu_{L,e}\bm{k}^{2}&-\alpha_{T,e}\gamma_{e}^{-1}c_{s,e}^{2}\bm{k}^{2}\\
0&\alpha_{T,e}^{-1}\left(\gamma_{e}-1\right)&\gamma_{e}\left(\mathfrak{g}_{e}+\chi_{e}\bm{k}^{2}\right)\end{bmatrix},

(14)

and its eigenvalues describe the modes present in the system. The hydrodynamic matrix is defined in terms of the plasma frequency
\omega_{p}^{2}=\rho_{0,e}q_{e}^{2}/(m_{e}^{2}\varepsilon_{0})
, the longitudinal (kinematic) viscosity
\nu_{L,e}=\left(4\eta_{e}/3+\zeta_{e}\right)/\rho_{0,e}
, thermal diffusivity
\chi_{e}=\kappa_{e}/(\rho_{0,e}\gamma_{e}C_{V,e})
and the normalised heat transfer
\mathfrak{g}_{e}=g_{e}/(\rho_{0,e}\gamma_{e}C_{V,e})
.

The (classical) electron dynamic structure factor, for a system of
N_{e}
electrons, is

S^{0}_{ee}(\bm{k},\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!dt\,\frac{e^{i\omega t}}{N_{e}m_{e}^{2}}\left\langle\delta\rho^{e}_{\bm{k}}(t)\delta\rho^{e*}_{\bm{k}}(0)\right\rangle,

(15)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle
is the thermal average. Using the transformed variables it may be evaluated as
[
30
,
32
]
,

\frac{S^{0}_{ee}(\bm{k},\omega)}{S^{0}_{ee}(\bm{k})}=\frac{1}{\pi}\real\left[\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\langle\delta\tilde{\rho}^{e}_{\bm{k}}(\varepsilon-i\omega)\delta\rho^{e*}_{\bm{k}}(0)\rangle}{\langle\delta\rho^{e}_{\bm{k}}(0)\delta\rho^{e*}_{\bm{k}}(0)\rangle}\right],

(16)

normalised by its integral
S^{0}_{ee}(\bm{k})
, the static structure factor. Under the common equilibrium assumption that the initial perturbations in density, velocity and temperature are statistically independent
[
40
,
30
,
29
,
39
,
45
]
, cross-correlations vanishes when equation (
12
) is inverted and thermally averaged with the initial density perturbations,

\langle\delta\tilde{\rho}^{e}_{\bm{k}}(s)\delta\rho^{e*}_{\bm{k}}(0)\rangle=\left(s\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{D}(\bm{k})\right)^{-1}_{11}\;\langle\delta\rho^{e}_{\bm{k}}(0)\delta\rho^{e*}_{\bm{k}}(0)\rangle.

(17)

The final result is an explicit form for the dynamic structure factor,

\frac{\pi S^{0}_{ee}(\bm{k},\omega)}{S^{0}_{ee}(\bm{k})}=\real\left[\frac{s^{2}+A(\bm{k})s+B(\bm{k})}{s^{3}+A(\bm{k})s^{2}+C(\bm{k})s+D(\bm{k})}\right]_{\mathrlap{s=-i\omega}},

(18)

where

\displaystyle A(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{v}_{e}(\bm{k},\omega)+\gamma_{e}\mathfrak{h}_{e}(\bm{k}),

(19a)

\displaystyle B(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma_{e}-1}{\gamma_{e}}c_{s,e}^{2}\bm{k}^{2}+\gamma_{e}\mathfrak{h}_{e}(\bm{k})\;\mathfrak{v}_{e}(\bm{k},\omega),

(19b)

\displaystyle C(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\omega_{p}^{2}+c_{s,e}^{2}\bm{k}^{2}+\gamma_{e}\mathfrak{h}_{e}(\bm{k})\;\mathfrak{v}_{e}(\bm{k},\omega),

(19c)

\displaystyle D(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\left(\gamma_{e}\omega_{p}^{2}+c_{s,e}^{2}\bm{k}^{2}\right)\mathfrak{h}_{e}(\bm{k}),

(19d)

with
\mathfrak{v}_{\mu}(\bm{k},\omega)=\left[\nu_{\mu\bar{\mu}}(\omega)+\nu_{L,\mu}\bm{k}^{2}\right]
and
\mathfrak{h}_{\mu}(\bm{k})=\left[\mathfrak{g}_{\mu}+\chi_{\mu}\bm{k}^{2}\right]
. Note that, the structure factor is independent of the thermal expansion coefficient
\alpha_{T,e}
which therefore can’t be inferred from the scattering spectrum or the dynamic structure factor. The schematic dependence of
S_{ee}^{0}(\bm{k},\omega)
on the remaining coefficients
c_{s,e},\;\mathfrak{h}_{e}
and
\mathfrak{v}_{e}
is shown in figure

. The position of the plasmon feature is mainly set by

and
\mathfrak{v}_{e}
sets its width. The entropy feature at
\omega=0
is primarily determined by
\mathfrak{h}_{e}
, however, all coefficients modify multiple parts of the spectrum to some degree.

c_{s,e}
, (b)

). Arrows indicate the change as each variable is increased respectively.

The form in equation (

) is not written in terms of three Lorentzians, common for hydrodynamic forms, however, it still exhibits similar behaviour. The hydrodynamic form is obtained by expansion in small

, however, such treatment does not directly extend to non-zero

II.3
Limiting behaviours of the hydrodynamic formulation

In the limit where dissipative effects are small both via inter-species collisions as well as viscous and heat conductive effects, i.e.
\mathfrak{h}_{e}\ll\omega_{p}
and
\mathfrak{v}_{e}\ll\omega_{p}
, the dispersion
|s\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{D}_{e}(\bm{k})|=0
can be evaluated analytically,

\displaystyle s_{0}

\displaystyle=-\left(\frac{\gamma_{e}\omega_{p}^{2}+c_{s,e}^{2}\bm{k}^{2}}{\omega_{Q}^{2}}\right)\times\mathfrak{h}_{e},

(20a)

\displaystyle s_{1,2}

\displaystyle=\pm i\omega_{Q}-(\gamma_{e}-1)\frac{c_{s,e}^{2}\bm{k}^{2}}{2\omega_{Q}^{2}}\mathfrak{h}_{e}-\frac{\mathfrak{v}_{e}(\bm{k},\mp\omega_{Q})}{2},

(20b)

where
s=-\gamma-i\omega
describes the resonance frequency
\omega
and damping rate
\gamma
, and

\omega_{Q}^{2}=\omega_{p}^{2}+c_{s,e}^{2}\bm{k}^{2}.

(21)

Equation (
20
) show an entropy mode at
\omega=0
which is damped by heat transfer, and two plasma modes at
\omega\approx\pm\omega_{Q}
. The plasma modes are primarily damped by viscous drag and momentum transfer
\mathfrak{v}_{e}
, and not energy conduction or transfer
\mathfrak{h}_{e}
because the plasma mode is fast
\omega_{p}\gg c_{s,e}\bm{k}
, as previously illustrated in figure
1
. This differs from standard hydrodynamics where the heat conduction dampens the acoustic mode
[
30
]
, something also noted by Ref.
[
44
]
.

By comparing the generalised Bohm-Gross dispersion
[
47
]
and equation (
21
), one identifies the ideal behaviour as

c_{s,e}^{2}\approx\langle\bm{v}_{e}^{2}\rangle=\begin{cases}3k_{\text{B}}T_{0,e}/m_{e}\quad&k_{B}T\gg E_{F}\\
\frac{3}{5}v_{F}^{2}\quad&k_{B}T\ll E_{F}\end{cases},

(22)

where
\langle\bm{v}_{e}^{2}\rangle
is the averaged square electron velocity,
v_{F}
is the Fermi-speed,
E_{F}
the Fermi-energy and
k_{\text{B}}
is the Boltzmann constant. In the absence of inter-species collisions, the damping rates are proportional to
\bm{k}^{2}
and likewise the width of the resonance features in
S_{ee}^{0}(\bm{k},\omega)
, which is the behaviour predicted by Schmidt
et al.

[
29
]
. Therefore, inter-species collisions are needed for the appropriate long-wavelength behaviour for two-component systems which typically have a finite width plasmon feature
[
48
,
49
,
50
]
.

The effect of momentum transfer between the electrons and ions is the most clear when
\bm{k}\rightarrow 0
and
g=0
. In this limit, the dynamic structure factor (
18
) yields,

\frac{\pi S^{0}_{ee}(\bm{k},\omega)}{S^{0}_{ee}(\bm{k})}=\frac{\real\{\nu_{ei}\}\,\omega_{p}^{2}}{\left(\omega^{2}-\omega_{p}^{2}-\Im\{\nu_{ei}\}\omega\right)^{2}\!+\!\left(\real\{\nu_{ei}\}\omega\right)^{2}}

(23)

where
\real\{\nu_{ei}\}
and
\Im\{\nu_{ei}\}
are the real and imaginary parts of the collision frequency respectively. This limit agrees with the Drude-like limit of Mermin’s relaxation time approximation
[
50
]
.

Lastly, one-component systems have been extensively studied through
dynamic local field corrections

G_{ee}(\bm{k},\omega)

[
51
,
52
,
53
]
, which are related to the viscosity of the electron fluid
[
54
,
55
]
. In the long-wavelength limit
G_{ee}
approaches zero, specifically
G_{ee}(\bm{k},\omega)\propto\bm{k}^{2}

[
50
]
, and therefore, for the one-component case, the hydrodynamic limit of
S_{ee}^{0}
is the Random Phase Approximation (RPA)
[
50
]
, which is reflected in the hydrodynamic model when
\mathfrak{v}_{e}\rightarrow 0
and
\mathfrak{h}_{e}\rightarrow 0
.

II.4
Electron electrical conductivity

A commonly discussed property for the electrons not explicitly shown in the hydrodynamic formulation is the electrical conductivity, which relates to the system response under an external potential
\delta\phi_{\text{ext}}
. Introducing an external potential through
\phi\rightarrow\phi+\delta\phi_{\text{ext}}
, which is assumed to be small, a driving term

\tilde{\bm{d}}=\Big[0,\;\frac{q_{e}\rho_{e,0}}{m_{e}}\bm{k}^{2}\delta\tilde{\phi}^{\text{ext}}_{\bm{k}},\;0\Big]^{\intercal}

(24)

is added to the right-hand side of equation (
12
). On thermal averaging, initial perturbations vanish and the dielectric function
\varepsilon(\bm{k},\omega)
can be obtained from the density response
[
56
]
of the hydrodynamic model, and yields:

\frac{1}{\varepsilon(\bm{k},\omega)}=1+\frac{q_{e}^{2}\rho_{0,e}}{\varepsilon_{0}m_{e}^{2}}\left(s\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{D}_{e}(\bm{k})\right)^{-1}_{12}\Big|_{s=-i\omega}.

(25)

The dielectric response relates to the off-diagonal component of the hydrodynamic matrix, as the density respond (row 1) to a source in the momentum equation (column 2). The resulting macroscopic conductivity model
[
57
]
,

\displaystyle\sigma(\omega)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon_{0}\lim_{\bm{k}\rightarrow 0}\omega\imaginary\left\{\varepsilon(\bm{k},\omega)\right\}

(26)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}\real\{\nu_{ei}\}\omega_{p}^{2}}{\real\{\nu_{ei}\}^{2}+\left(\omega-\Im\{\nu_{ei}\}\right)^{2}}

has a Drude like form with DC value
\sigma_{\text{DC}}=\varepsilon_{0}\omega_{p}^{2}/\real\{\nu_{ei}(\omega=0)\}
. Electron-electron collisions do not appear explicitly as they adhere to the conservation relations in equation (
1c
), however, they affect the conductivity by altering the distribution function
[
58
,
59
]
, which in a hydrodynamic formulation modifies the effective momentum transfer between electrons and ions
\nu_{ei}

[
60
]
.

Note that equation (
25
) constitutes a separate derivation of the dynamic structure factor using the (classical) fluctuation-dissipation theorem
[
61
,
62
,
56
]
, which is consistent with the hydrodynamic form (
18
) given the static structure factor

S^{0}_{ee}(\bm{k})=\frac{k_{\text{B}}T}{m_{e}\omega_{p}^{2}}\frac{\bm{k}^{2}}{1+\lambda_{e}^{2}\bm{k}^{2}}

(27)

and
\lambda_{e}^{-2}=\gamma_{e}\omega_{p}^{2}/c_{s,e}^{2}
. This is in agreement with the static structure in the RPA approximation
[
62
]
for a general screening length
\lambda_{e}
and recovers Debye and Thomas-Fermi screening
[
63
]
in their corresponding limits, see appendix
A.1
.

III
Test system and model requirements

The transport coefficients in the hydrodynamic model remain unspecified and will be extracted via comparison with molecular dynamics, where individual particle trajectories are resolved and no concrete concept of transport coefficients is needed. As we are interested in the electron dynamics, the WPMD method
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
is considered, specifically we employ the model presented in Ref.
[
64
]
. For an initial test system we take a fully ionised hydrogen plasma with density
r_{s}=\left(4\pi m_{e}^{-1}\rho_{e}/3\right)^{-\frac{1}{3}}/a_{\text{B}}=2.0
(
a_{\text{B}}
is the Bohr radius) and temperature
k_{\text{B}}T\approx 21.5\,\text{eV}
. Further details of the MD model and the properties of the test system are provided in paper I.

The hydrodynamic description is valid as
\bm{k}\rightarrow 0
and before attempting to apply it to the MD data an upper limit for
\bm{k}
must be determined. A purely kinetic effect missing in the fluid model is Landau damping, which will act to broaden the resonance. Figure

compares the classical Landau damping
[

]
with the expected broadening due to electron-ion collisions in the first Born approximation
[

within the Mermin-relaxation time approximation
[

. Requiring the collisional broadening to be an order of magnitude larger than Landau damping, restricts the domain of validity to
\bm{k}\lesssim 0.19\,a_{\text{B}}^{-1}
.

The required system size to model in the MD is set by the number of needed spectra within the regime of validity of the hydrodynamic model. Requiring access to at least three distinct spectra with
\bm{k}\leq 0.19\,a_{\text{B}}^{-1}
for the inference procedure and assuming MD simulations performed in a cubic box with periodic boundary conditions, at least
N_{\text{ion}}\approx 5600
ions are needed. In combination with the necessity to resolve electron dynamics, a method like WPMD is therefore preferable compared to other
ab initio
approaches. Here a system of
N_{\text{ion}}=5628
has been simulated.

A commonly discussed generalisation to the hydrodynamic formulation is quantum hydrodynamics (QHD), where an additional Bohm-like contribution is added to the momentum
[
65
,
68
,
69
,
70
]
and potentially higher order equations
[
65
,
29
]
. Classical hydrodynamics can still capture some quantum effects by modifying the equation of state and transport coefficients, but it can’t capture the non-local Bohm contribution. We have opted for a more classical formulation for two main reasons. Quantum hydrodynamics adds (in quadrature) a quantum recoil
\omega_{R}=\hbar\bm{k}^{2}/(2m_{e})
to the plasmon dispersion
[
65
,
68
]
. Comparing the shift in the dispersion due to quantum recoil and the pressure contribution for the test case,

\frac{\omega_{R}^{2}}{c_{s,e}^{2}\bm{k}^{2}}\approx 0.1\times\left(a_{\text{B}}\bm{k}\right)^{2},

(28)

it is clear that for our
\bm{k}
-vectors this contribution is negligible. In the above expression, the high-temperature approximation for
c_{s,e}^{2}
has been assumed. Secondly, care must be taken for the Bohm contribution to have the desired effect in many-body systems
[
71
]
, partially amended by the introduction of an additional pre-factor for the Bohm potential
[
72
]
which might be time- and length-scale dependent
[
69
]
. Furthermore, the Bohm pressure tensor is necessarily anisotropic in the presence of perturbations, preventing a straightforward isotropic second-order closure
[
65
]
, i.e. closing on the level of temperature as in section
II
. Not properly accounted for this results in an erroneous recoil term
[
73
]
. Moreover, closing the equations on the level of the momentum equation would miss the entropy modes at
\omega=0

[
30
,
74
]
.

IV
Fitting model, degeneracy and error estimation

It is common practice to fit hydrodynamic expressions to the simulated ion dynamic structure factor to extract transport properties, e.g. Refs.
[
75
,
76
,
77
,
28
]
, closely related to how it would be done using experimental data
[
78
,
79
]
. Multiple
\bm{k}
-vectors (angles) are commonly needed to extract the transport properties. What we propose here is similar, however, here we are interested in the electron dynamics.

For the ionic properties, a two-stage fitting procedure is commonly employed, where a hydrodynamic model is fitted for each
\bm{k}
and the parameters in the fit are plotted as functions of
\bm{k}
to extract transport properties
[
75
,
76
,
77
,
28
]
. A similar procedure using equation (
18
) could also be implemented in our case, however, we are limited to fewer
\bm{k}
-vectors than in the ion dynamics investigations. It is therefore desirable to have a combined fit where uncertainties are systematically propagated.

For this, we turn to Bayesian inference
[
24
]
, which is now often applied to scattering data for warm dense matter systems
[
80
,
81
,
82
,
83
]
. The fitting model is based on
Bayes’ theorem
,

P\left(\bm{x}|\bm{y}\right)=\frac{P\left(\bm{y}|\bm{x}\right)P\left(\bm{x}\right)}{P\left(\bm{y}\right)},

(29)

for a set of transport coefficients and error parameters
\bm{x}=[c_{s,e},\chi_{e},\nu_{ei},\nu_{L,e},b_{\bm{k}_{1}},s_{\bm{k}_{1}},\dots,b_{\bm{k}_{n}},s_{\bm{k}_{n}}]^{\intercal}
and a set of observed (normalised) structure factors

\displaystyle\bm{y}

\displaystyle=[S(\bm{k}_{1},\bm{\omega}_{\bm{k}_{1}})/S(\bm{k}_{1}),\dots,S(\bm{k}_{n},\bm{\omega}_{\bm{k}_{n}})/S(\bm{k})]^{\intercal}

(30)

\displaystyle\equiv[y_{1}(\bm{\omega}_{\bm{k}_{1}}),\dots,y_{n}(\bm{\omega}_{\bm{k}_{n}})]^{\intercal},

where
S(\bm{k},\omega)
was obtained via a FFT of correlation data from MD. The effect of the finite time window in the MD was investigated in terms of window functions
[
84
]
and had a negligible effect.
P\left(\bm{x}|\bm{y}\right)
is the resulting distribution of transport properties (and error parameters) given the data. The likelihood function
P\left(\bm{y}|\bm{x}\right)
is a model choice. We consider the computations for different
\bm{k}
to be independent measurements and each frequency point is modelled with a gaussian error around the hydrodynamic model
y_{i}^{\text{HD}}
(equation (
18
)),

\displaystyle\ln P\left(\bm{y}|\bm{x}\right)=-\hskip-2.84544pt\sum_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle\sum_{\omega\in\bm{\omega}_{\bm{k}_{i}}}\Bigg\{\ln\left[2\pi\sigma_{i}^{2}(\omega)\right]

(31)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{y_{i}(\omega)-y_{i}^{\text{HD}}(\omega;\bm{x})}{\sigma_{i}(\omega)}\right]^{2}\Bigg\}.

The error is modelled as a floored relative error,

\sigma_{i}(\omega)=\min\left[s_{i},b_{i}y_{i}^{\text{HD}}(\omega)\right],

(32)

where
s_{i}
and
b_{i}
are allowed to be different for each
\bm{k}
-vector and are inferred from the data.

The prior distribution
P\left(\bm{x}\right)
for transport properties is assumed to be constant for positive values.
Jefferys’s prior

[
24
]
was adopted for the scale parameters
s_{i}
and
b_{i}
, based on the Fisher information matrix of a multivariate gaussian
[
85
]
. The likelihood for the observation
P\left(\bm{y}\right)
will not be needed for parameter estimation
[
24
]
.

The resulting distribution,
P(\bm{x}|\bm{y})
, will be sampled numerically in the following section, using a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm
[
86
,
87
]
. For this, the
emcee
implementation
[
88
]
of the affine-invariant ensemble sampler by Goodman and Weare
[
89
]
was employed.

The energy relaxation rate
\mathfrak{g}_{e}
is a slow process compared to the damping of plasma oscillations, typically
\mathfrak{g}_{e}/\nu_{ei}\approx m_{e}/m_{i}

[
90
]
, and therefore the free electron spectrum is not particularly sensitive to it and
\mathfrak{g}_{e}
is omitted from the fitting. To further constrain the fitting procedure the adiabatic index is assumed to be
\gamma_{e}=3
– common practice for fluid models of fast plasma oscillations
[
91
]
– and a parameterisation for the dynamic collision frequency is introduced:

\nu_{ei}(\omega)=\real\{\nu_{ei}(\omega_{p})\}\times\frac{\nu_{ei}^{\text{BMA}}(\omega)}{\real\{\nu_{ei}^{\text{BMA}}(\omega_{p})\}},

(33)

where
\real\{\nu_{ei}(\omega_{p})\}
will be sampled and
\nu_{ei}^{\text{BMA}}(\omega)
is the collision frequency in the Born-Mermin approximation evaluated as described in Ref.
[
67
]
using the ion structure from paper I.

An alternative route for computing transport properties is from the Green-Kubo relations
[
30
,
32
,
10
,
11
]
where the transport properties are related to integrals of auto-correlation functions of corresponding currents. However, they are derived from solutions of equations similar to (
12
). For ionic systems good agreement has been found between the Green-Kubo and hydrodynamic approach
[
92
]
and the methods are largely interchangeable. A benefit of the hydrodynamic method is the validation of the model via inspection of the fits and its direct applicability to experimental data.

VI
Conclusion

In an effort to describe the long-wavelength behaviour of atomistic two component simulations of warm dense matter systems, a phenomenological two fluid model is considered. In particular, the hydrodynamic model includes self-consistent electrostatic fields and collisional effects, and models ion screening on the ion time scales and plasma oscillations on the electron time scales.

The dynamic structure factor for free-electron oscillations is derived both by considering the dynamics of density fluctuations and the response of the systems due to external potentials, via linearisation of the hydrodynamic model. In taking the appropriate limits, it is shown explicitly that the model incorporates one entropy and two plasmon modes, and agrees with the Born-Mermin approximation in the long-wavelength limit. It is therefore an appropriate description of the electron dynamics with ion collisions on long length scales.

The dynamic structure factor is parameterised using transport parameters, and therefore, by mapping the structure factor to the results of atomistic simulations, transport properties can be extracted. The benefit here is the direct and clear interpretation of the transport coefficients in the original hydrodynamic formulation. We perform the mapping process via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling, over multiple spectra simultaneously, which allows for consistent error propagation.

The methodology is general and can be applied to any simulation of two-component plasmas in which the electron dynamics is probed. In this work, we have applied it to the results from wave packet molecular dynamics (WPMD) of warm dense hydrogen. Fractionally small error margins in the transport properties are obtained, and the inferred values are in agreement with a number of reference calculations. Discrepancies are within expectations as no reference calculation is a direct match to the WPMD system, either due to being limited to small coupling or having neglected the presence of ions. The exception is the electron viscosity, which, when compared to classical models, is suggested to be at least an order of magnitude smaller. This discrepancy is most likely due to quantum effects in the electron-electron interaction not present in the classical references.

A.2
Stanton and Murillo (2016)

The Stanton and Murillo transport (SMT) model is a classical kinetic transport model based on collisions in an effective Boltzmann description using Yukawa interactions
[

[

99

[

11

The SMT model has been used for the computation of a variety of transport properties. For the viscosity and thermal conductivity a single-component formulation
[
96
]

[
97
]
.
