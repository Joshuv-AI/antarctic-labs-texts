Validation of the climate change worry for adolescents and climate concern impacts on daily life scales
Authors: Gina Martin, Cerina Dubois, Violeta Faulkner, Maria Ojala, Matt Treble, Tasha Roswell, Jason Gilliland, Kiffer G. Card, Maya Gislason, Ekroop Grewal, Alina Cosma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionValidated survey instruments are needed to better understand how concerns about climate change affect adolescents’ cognitive-emotional experiences and everyday functioning.Using a Canadian adolescent sample, this study aimed to validate two survey scales: a Climate Change Worry Scale for Adolescents (CWS-A) and a Climate Concern Impact on Daily Life Scale (CCIDLS).MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted with 804 Canadian adolescents (aged 13–18). A confirmatory factor analysis approach was used to affirm that the items measured the factors specified. Item response theory was used to further examine the properties of the scales at item level. Multigroup confirmatory factor analyses were conducted to examine invariance by gender (boys versus girls) and age (age 13–15 versus 16–18). Convergent validity was examined against self-perceived impacts of climate change on mental health and climate change concern.ResultsFor the CWS-A, a two-factor measure of micro- and macro-worry had excellent model fit and was invariant for age and gender. For the CCIDLS a one-factor model had adequate fit and showed invariance for age and gender. The CCIDLS and CWS-A were significantly higher among those who perceived their mental health as being impacted by climate change and those concerned about climate change.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the importance of distinguishing between more affective responses, such as climate worry, and impairment in daily functioning. The CWS-A and CCIDLS demonstrate evidence of validity of these measures for Canadian adolescents; future studies should examine the validity of these survey instruments in other countries and languages.

Introduction

Climate change is a critical issue that burdens the health and wellbeing of adolescent populations [1,2]. UNICEF’s Children’s Climate Risk Index estimates that nearly half of all children globally are at extremely high risk of the impacts of climate change [3]. Its effects on adolescents are wide-reaching, including increased risk of injury, disease, and disruptions to daily life [2]. Additionally, adolescents may feel powerless to address systemic issues like climate change, reducing their sense of agency and security [4]. Recent research has explored the effects of climate change on adolescents’ mental and emotional health and wellbeing, including the potentially complex and difficult feelings they have about it [1,5].

The concepts of worry and anxiety are the most frequently studied emotional responses to climate change among adolescent populations [1,6]; such concepts that deal with negative emotional responses to climate change, fall under the umbrella term of "climate change distress", which is defined as “various kinds of distress that are significantly shaped by the climate crisis” [7]. Surveys have been used better understand adolescents’ distressful climate change emotions (the emotional responses of people to the threat of climate change) [6]; yet there is a lack of validated measurement instruments for this age group, and most studies focus on older adolescents (aged 15+) [6,8].

Measuring such psychological constructs, which cannot be directly observed as they deal with internal processes, falls under psychometrics. A recent review of the psychometric properties of measures of climate change distress found that the most significant concern for existing measures was poor content validity [9], which is the extent to which the scale items measure the underlying trait of interest [10]. Given that measuring climate change distress is an emerging area, and there is a lack of consensus on the definitions of it and related constructs, such as climate change worry, anxiety, etc. [9,11], it is not surprising that measurement implementation has outpaced conceptual clarification, resulting in instruments that may insufficiently capture these constructs for adolescent populations.

Although there is a lack of validated measures for use with adolescents, some scales have been developed or adapted, particularly to examine climate change worry. Climate change worry involves negative or apprehensive thoughts about present or potential changes in the climate system and their implications, which can be accompanied by anxiety-like affect [[12], [13], [14]]. Climate change worry can be considered through the lens of appraisal theory, which consider emotions as processes as opposed to states [15]. Through this lens climate change worry is considered an internalized process that is a reaction to the appraisal of the threats posed from climate change [11]. A study explored the psychometric properties of a Climate Change Worry Scale among Italian adolescents and found evidence of good validity [16]. However, this instrument was originally developed for use with adults and then applied to younger people [17]. An example of a climate change worry measure that was developed for use specifically with adolescents is a 5-item measure established by Ojala (referred to here as Ojala’s Climate Worry Scale). This scale addresses both the magnitude and the nature of the worry (i.e., worry for self, worry for future generations, etc.). It has been implemented as a single factor scale for Swedish adolescents [18,19] and later utilized as a two-factor measure: macro- (focus on external entities rather than oneself) and micro- (focus on self or an in-group) worry [20]; however, current evidence around the validity of this scale in contexts outside of Sweden is lacking.

Beyond worry, other measures have been used to examine how concerns about climate change impacts functioning in daily life. As outlined by Chan et al., this represents a more symptoms-based response (i.e., functional impairment due to feelings about climate change), rather than an affect-based response (i.e., difficult feelings about climate change) [21]. A survey of 10,000 young people (aged 16–25) in ten countries included a single item examining if feelings about climate change impacted aspects of daily life (e.g., concentrating, work, school, sleeping, having fun), though its validity was not reported [22].

Climate change worry constitutes an internalized response based on feelings of worry, while functional impairments reflect when such cognitions and emotions become disruptive to important areas of daily functioning [23]. In this sense, the impacts of difficult thoughts and feelings about climate change can be thought of as stemming from the severity of negative emotions about climate change as well as individual factors, such as personality, coping approaches, and context. In their scoping review and development of a conceptual framework of eco-anxiety, Boivin et al. (2025) grouped impairment in daily functioning under a behavioral dimension; this dimension also includes other aspects, like family planning. Examining disturbances to daily life may be of interest as it may be linked to experiencing mental health problems [24].

Overall, more development and validation work are needed to support the measurement of adolescents' emotional responses to climate change. Such work will support a more nuanced understanding of how climate change is experienced at this stage of life. Further, conceptual clarity (e.g., distinguishing between measures of internal processes, such as feelings of worry, and functional impairments) is a consideration of such instruments to avoid jingle-jangle fallacies, which is an issue in research of climate change distress [25]. As such, the objective of this study is to examine the psychometric properties of two survey scales, (1) an adaptation of Ojala’s Climate Worry Scale [19] and (2) a Climate Concern Impact on Daily Life Scale (CCIDLS). Through this process we explore the scales’ reliability (by examining internal consistency of items), structural validity (through classical test theory and item response theory), measurement invariance for age and gender (through multi-group confirmatory factor analysis), and convergent validity (through correlation with related factors) [10,26]. We also touch on content validity when discussing the development of the survey, which utilized an interdisciplinary team, youth co-researchers, focus groups, and cognitive interviews [26].

Methods

From October-November 2023, a sample of Canadian adolescents (ages 13–18) who are part of a Qualtrics Research Panel (n = 804) completed a cross-sectional web-based survey. Research panels consist of individuals who preregister to participate in research. The Qualtrics panel has been used successfully in prior adolescent survey instrument validation studies [27]. A quota sampling approach was applied based on region, gender, age, and urbanicity (see Supplementary Material). A target sample of 800 was chosen to exceed the recommended minimum of 450 for confirmatory factor analysis with two factors [28] and 200 per subgroup for measurement invariance testing [29]. Survey invitations were sent to 13,884 panelists and closed once quotas were met. Qualtrics conducted data quality checks to remove poor-quality responses (e.g., duplicates, straight lining).

The full survey was developed by an interdisciplinary team (health science, psychology, counselling, geography) including three high school-aged youth co-researchers. Instrument design was informed by literature reviews [6] and seven online focus groups with adolescents (ages 15–18), who reviewed and discussed existing climate emotion measures that had been used with adolescents. Measures receiving mainly positive impressions were chosen and then modified according to feedback from focus group participants. Cognitive interviews with adolescents (ages 13–18) were then conducted on the selected items (climate worry (n = 6) and functional impacts (n = 7)) to ensure clarity and support face validity. Cognitive interviews are a qualitative research approach in which participants verbalize their thought processes while answering survey questions in order to check interpretation, clarity, and comprehension [30]. The survey was administered in English.

Parent/guardians linked to panel members <18 years approved that the potential participant could be contacted to ask to take part in the study, as per Qualtrics panel requirements. Potential participants were then presented with a Letter of Information outlining the study’s purpose, duration, content, and assurances of anonymity, confidentiality, and voluntary participation. Consent was obtained from the participants before they received survey access. Participants were compensated based on preselected preferences when joining the panel. The study was approved by the Athabasca University Research Ethics Board (REB #25,013).

Prior to being asked the suite of questions about climate change, participants were presented with a definition of climate change (see Supplementary Material) and asked if they thought that climate change is mainly due to human causes ("yes", "no", "I don’t know", "I don’t believe that climate change is happening"). Participants who did not believe that climate change is happening (n = 17) skipped the climate change emotions questions as they would seem either not applicable or might be interpreted differently.

Scale measures

The Climate Worry Scale - Adolescents (CWS-A)

The CWS-A was adapted from five items originally developed by Ojala [19]. In focus groups this measure was more positively received than a single item worry measure that asked about worry about climate change generally. The response scale was modified from a 6-point scale (1 = not at all to 6 = very much) to a 4-point scale (1 = not at all worried to 4 = very worried) to reduce complexity of interpreting subtle differences in the response scale. Additionally, the item “people in economically deprived countries” was revised to “people living in different countries than me” to reflect the awareness of broader global impact of climate change since the original scale’s development, and to reduce the complexity of needing to consider both economic and environmental circumstances of countries. The term “oneself” was changed to “yourself” as more commonly used language for Canadian adolescents. These adaptations were made prior to cognitive interviews. No further changes were deemed necessary following the interviews.

The Climate Concern Impacts on Daily Life Scale (CCIDLS)

The CCIDLS is a 5-item scale developed to extend the conceptualization and operationalization of a single item reported in Hickman et al. [22], which asks: “My feelings about climate change negatively affect my daily life (in at least one of the following: eating, concentrating, work, school, sleeping, spending time in nature, playing, having fun, relationships),” with response options “yes,” “no,” or “prefer not to say.” This was adapted into a 5-item scale to assess the extent to which thoughts and/or feelings about climate change impact daily life in five areas: concentrating on school or work, sleep, enjoying time in nature, having fun, and relationships with friends, family, and loved ones, as suggested in focus groups. A 3-point scale (0 = “no“ 1 = “yes, sometimes”, 2 = “yes, a lot”) was used to allow more nuance than a dichotomous response. “Prefer not to say” was excluded, as participants could skip this question if they wished. From focus group feedback “playing” was removed due to conceptual overlap with “having fun”; “eating” was excluded to avoid confusion with environmentally motivated dietary changes. Of note, several items (sleeping and concentrating on school or work) are conceptually similar to those in existing climate or eco-anxiety scales developed for use with adult populations [31,32].

Statistical procedure

Confirmatory factor analysis

As appropriate for scales designed to assess defined constructs, we employed Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) to verify that the items aligned with specified factor structures [33]. Only participants with complete data were included (n = 780 for CWS-A; n = 772 for CCIDLS). We used Weighted Least Square Mean and Variance (WLSMV) estimators and a polychoric correlation matrix, because of the ordinal nature of the data. Our analysis focused on confirming a two-factor structure (micro- and macro-worry, allowing covariance between the two factors) for the CWS-A, and a single-factor structure for the CCIDLS.

Model fit was assessed using a non-significant Chi-square test, with the understanding that this test is sensitive to sample size. Therefore, we also relied on additional fit indices: Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) (< 0.06 = good; 0.06–0.08 = acceptable; > 0.08 = poor) [34] Comparative Fit Index (CFI) (> 0.95 = good) [35], and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR) (< 0.08 = good) [36]. Scaled values were reported, as appropriate for ordinal data [37]. CFA analyses were conducted using the lavaan package in R.

Item response theory

Item Response Theory (IRT) was used to examine the properties of individual items within the scales. While CFA and IRT are complementary, IRT provides both overall model fit and item-level insights [38]. We estimated the IRT model using a full information marginal maximum likelihood method and applied a graded response model (GRM), as appropriate for ordinal responses [38]. Model fit was determined with the M2 index, which is appropriate for ordinal IRT models. To evaluate item fit, we used item-level RMSEA values, with values below 0.06 indicating adequate fit. We also reported slope parameters (a-parameters), which reflect how well items differentiate individuals across the latent trait. Slope magnitude cutoffs were: 0 = none; 0.01–0.24 = very low; 0.25–0.64 = low; 0.65–1.34 = moderate; 1.35–1.69 = high; >1.70 = very high [39]. IRT was conducted using the mirt package in R.

Measurement invariance

Multigroup Confirmatory Factor Analyses (MCFA) were conducted to assess measurement invariance across age and gender, ensuring the scales functioned equivalently across groups [37,[40], [41], [42]]. For gender, due to the small number of gender-diverse participants, only boys and girls were included in these analyses.

We used theta parameterization and diagonally weighted least squares (WLSMV) estimators, following Wu and Estabrook (2016) [43]. Invariance testing involved fitting increasingly restrictive nested models: configural invariance (same factor structure across groups), threshold invariance (equal item thresholds), metric invariance (equal factor loadings, indicating items relate similarly to the latent construct), and scalar invariance (equal intercepts, allowing for meaningful comparison of latent means) [43].

Model fit at each level was evaluated using changes in fit indices. Following others, invariance was supported if at least two of the following were met: ΔCFI ≤ −0.010, ΔSRMR ≤ 0.030, or ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015, along with a non-significant Chi-square difference test using the Satorra method [44]. MCFA analyses were conducted in R using the lavaan package and the measEq.syntax function from the semTools package.

Convergent validity

Convergent validity assesses whether a constructs positively correlate with theoretically related constructs [45]. We examined convergent validity by testing correlations (Pearson’s r) between micro- and macro-worry from the CWS-A and the CCIDLS.

Convergent validity was also assessed using measures of self-perceived mental health impact of climate change and concern for climate change. These variables were selected based on the expectation that they would correlate with both the CWS-A and CCIDLS. We hypothesized that scores on both CWS-A dimensions and the CCIDLS would be higher among participants who reported mental health impacts from climate change and concern about climate change (“yes, a little/sometimes” or “yes, a lot”) compared to those who reported no impact or concern.

Scale scores were calculated as the means of item responses and converted to z-scores to enable comparison across the CWS-A and CCIDLS variables. One-way ANOVA was used to test differences across groups, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant, and effect size measured using Eta squared (η2).

Discussion

In this study, we examined the psychometric properties of two scales measuring aspects of climate distress. Both measures demonstrated good face, construct, and convergent validity, supporting their use with Canadian adolescents. This is useful given the limited availability of validated tools for this population [6].

The CWS-A findings aligned with another study which identified micro- and macro-worry as distinct but related constructs among Swedish adolescents [20]. Our results support this structure in a Canadian context and indicate the measure’s suitability for comparing boys and girls and younger versus older adolescents. Additionally, the Swedish study found that micro-worry showed a stronger association with negative affect and life satisfaction than macro-worry [20], and in our study micro-worry was more strongly associated with impacts on daily life than macro-worry, suggesting that climate change worry is multi-faceted and relates to well-being in different ways. Further, this scale can be considered high in face validity as the items in this scale can be considered explicit in that they ask about worry directly. Explicit measures are considered high in terms of face validity [46].

The five-item CCIDLS showed acceptable fit; however, post hoc modification indices indicated some conceptual overlap between “enjoying time in nature” and “having fun.” This may reflect that enjoying nature is a specific form of fun. Although “enjoying time in nature” had high discriminative ability, its slope was lower than the other items. Still, the item may be valuable, as nature is often seen as a coping strategy for climate-related emotions [47], but, if climate distress reduces enjoyment of nature, this could undermine its restorative effects. Future research should explore this further. Additionally, this item was more normally distributed than the others, so it may help in reducing the skew in the scale by reflecting experiences of those who are more moderately impacted in their daily lives.

Positive skewness on most items in the CCIDLS indicate that low levels of impacts on daily life were found in the majority of the sample (i.e., evidence of floor effects); this is the same for previous measures of climate and eco-anxiety measures with adults [[48], [49], [50]] and measures of climate change interfering with sleep and work among adolescents in the Canadian province of Quebec [51]. It has been suggested that the low prevalence of scores on these phenomena in the general population is unsurprising, particularly given the clinically relevant nature of functional impairments [52]. However, it is important to consider that floor effects reduce variability and may weaken associations with other variables [52]. As this has been noted in other studies, it has been suggested that scales are needed that differentiate between cognitive-emotional measures and impairment [52]. Our study supports such assertions as we found that climate change worry followed a more normal distribution than impacts on daily function, indicating that such cognitive-emotional responses occur to a greater degree in the general population and with greater variability than impairment in daily life which occurs with lesser frequency. Of note, there was a stronger association of CCIDLS with self-perceived climate change impacts on mental health compared to worry, indicating that assessing the two constructs separately yields distinct information with impacts on daily life being more indicative of concerns that may impact mental health.

Some recent studies have explored the use of measures of climate change worry and anxiety in clinical populations (such as emergency rooms and outpatient psychiatric clinics) [[53], [54], [55]]. They have found that such scales can be applied in clinical populations. Assaf et al. (2025) advocate for including such measures during pediatric mental health assessments as climate anxiety was high among adolescents seeking care for a mental health complaint in emergency rooms, arguing that this may be an additional stressor rather than a primary complaint (however, their study did not distinguish between functional impairment and cognitive–emotional domains). Before widely adopting such practices with these measures, we suggest future studies are warranted exploring the properties of the CWS-A and CCIDLS in clinical populations.

This study has several strengths. It is among the few to validate instruments assessing climate worry and functional impacts among younger adolescents (<15 years). The scales were developed with adolescents’ input through focus groups, cognitive interviews, and youth co-researchers. The quota sample enabled invariance testing across age and gender with suitable statistical power. Additionally, combining classical test theory with IRT allowed scale- and item-level evaluation.

Some limitations should be noted. The cross-sectional design did not allow for test–retest reliability assessment. The small number of gender-diverse participants limited invariance testing to cis-gender boys and girls. Additionally, we only tested for invariance across gender and age, while other factors, such as race/ethnicity, were not examined. Future studies should be designed to examine other forms of invariance. Selection bias may be present, as participants opted in to the survey with the knowledge that it was climate change focused. Additionally, self-reported measures are subject to social desirability and recall biases. Futher, the timing of data collection followed Canada’s 2023 wildfire season, which was the most destructive on record at the time [56]; this may have influenced respondents’ perceptions and reporting. The use of a non-probabilistic quota sampling design limits the representativeness of the sample; as a result, point estimates should not be interpreted as estimates of the population parameters of Canadian adolescents. Additionally, we did not meet the desired quota for the Northern Territories, so the experiences of those living in the north are underrepresented. Canada covers a large land mass that experiences different forms of climate change impacts (e.g., wildfire, flooding) and it is topic that is addressed in socio-political contexts and education with the form varying across jurisdictions, so underrepresentation of regions may impact on study findings. Additionally, the survey was conducted only in English. Validation of the scales in other languages and cultural contexts is recommended; this would allow for cross-cultural validity to be explored. Moreover, as there is no gold standard measure of climate change worry or climate concerns having impacts on daily life, we could not test for criterion validity. Future studies in clinical settings could compare professional observations with scale measures to assess this. Finally, similar to studies of the adult-developed climate change anxiety scales [52], the CCIDLS seemed to exhibit floor effects with general population samples; therefore, users of this measure should consider distribution of the variable in their statistical approaches and interpretations.

Conclusions

The CWS-A and CCIDLS show evidence of reliability and validity and can be used in research examining adolescents’ cognitive-emotional and functional responses to climate change. These tools can also inform the development and evaluation of programs and policies aimed at supporting young people as they navigate their climate change-related thoughts, worries, and daily-life impacts. Our findings highlight the importance of distinguishing between affective responses, such as climate worry, and impairment in daily functioning, as each provides unique insights. Further research is needed to understand how climate change worry and impacts on daily life relate to coping strategies, mental health outcomes, and broader environmental engagement. Deepening our understanding of how adolescents experience climate change is essential for shaping timely, evidence-informed interventions that support and empower youth in the context of accelerating climate change-related risks.
