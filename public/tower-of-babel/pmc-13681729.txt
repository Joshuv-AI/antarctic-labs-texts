Skull infarction is a rare complication of SCD, and the extra-axial collections produced can closely mimic infection. Epidural and subgaleal hematomas from calvarial infarction have been described, but spontaneous subdural collections are exceptionally rare, and their sterile exudative properties are poorly characterized. We report the case of a pediatric patient with SCD in whom calvarial infarction produced subdural and subperiosteal orbital collections mimicking subdural empyema and orbital abscess. We describe the imaging, intraoperative findings, and multidisciplinary features that distinguished this sterile process from infection. This case highlights calvarial infarction as a noninfectious cause of intracranial collections in SCD, identifies the radiographic and operative findings that differentiate it from empyema, and discusses management considerations including precautions surrounding middle meningeal artery (MMA) embolization in this population.

Illustrative Case

A 6-year-old boy with nonverbal autism and homozygous SCD (homozygous hemoglobin S [HbSS]) presented to the emergency department with a 4-day history of progressive right periorbital swelling, intermittent fevers, left leg and wrist pain, and headache. His medical history was notable for prior acute chest syndrome and multiple vaso-occlusive episodes requiring hospitalization. His outpatient medications included hydroxyurea 400 mg (21 mg/kg) daily, to which he had been poorly adherent. Transcranial Doppler screening 7 months earlier was normal. Hemoglobin electrophoresis confirmed HbSS with an hemoglobin S (HbS) fraction of 77.4%.

On arrival he was febrile at 38.9°C, tachycardic at 141 beats per minute, and tachypneic. Examination revealed marked right periorbital edema with overlying erythema and warmth, mild proptosis, and lesser left periorbital edema without erythema. Extraocular movements were intact and the pupils were equal and reactive. Neurological examination was constrained by his nonverbal baseline, although he was alert, tracked visually, and moved extremities without focal deficit.

Laboratory studies at presentation demonstrated a leukocytosis of 16.0 K/µL with 69.1% neutrophils (absolute neutrophil count 11.10 K/µL) and 0.7% immature granulocytes, hemoglobin 6.1 g/dL, hematocrit 19.5%, and reticulocytes 4.79%. C-reactive protein was markedly elevated at 21.6 mg/dL. Importantly, the platelet count was normal at 338 K/µL, and there was no personal or family history of bleeding diathesis. Coagulation studies were unremarkable. A respiratory pathogen panel was negative, urinalysis unremarkable, and blood cultures showed no growth. He was empirically started on intravenous ceftriaxone, vancomycin, and metronidazole for presumed orbital cellulitis prior to any tissue sampling.

Contrast-enhanced CT of the orbits and face demonstrated pre- and postseptal orbital cellulitis with a 1.2-cm subperiosteal collection along the right lateral and superior orbital walls exerting mass effect on the extraocular muscles and globe with resultant proptosis. An 8 × 8 × 14–mm preseptal extension, a 5-mm right frontal subdural collection interpreted as subdural empyema, and bilateral zygomatic collections were favored to represent subperiosteal abscesses, with near-total opacification of the right frontal sinus. Notably, bone window reconstructions demonstrated no cortical breach, lucency, or periosteal reaction, and the calvarial infarcts that would be demonstrated on MRI were not appreciable (Fig. 1). This discordance was entirely consistent with a multifocal suppurative process.

MRI of the brain and orbits reframed the diagnosis. The calvarium demonstrated diffuse heterogeneous marrow signal with multifocal regions of nonenhancement and restricted diffusion, the radiographic signature of acute bone infarction rather than osteomyelitis (Fig. 2B–D).1,2 Arising in continuity with these nonenhancing osseous segments were rim-enhancing collections in the epidural space, orbits, and scalp soft tissues, predominantly subperiosteal in location, many exhibiting scattered susceptibility artifact and intrinsic T1 hyperintensity without significant restricted diffusion (Fig. 2E), a constellation favoring hemorrhagic exudate over purulence. The largest epidural collection measured 7 mm along the right parietal convexity, with 4-mm right frontal and left parietal collections, each subjacent to nonenhancing marrow.

Along the right convexity there was nonspecific T2 and FLAIR hyperintense subdural fluid tracking beneath diffuse, smooth right hemispheric dural enhancement (Fig. 2A). This collection showed neither the internal diffusion restriction nor the irregular, nodular dural enhancement characteristic of empyema. At the time of the initial MRI, there was no intracranial mass effect and no midline shift. In the orbits, multiloculated subperiosteal fluid along the right lateral and superior orbital walls produced moderate proptosis with mass effect on the superior and lateral recti, without extraocular muscle involvement and with preserved intraconal fat planes. Smaller left lateral subperiosteal and left malar collections were also present (Fig. 2F). The prospective radiological impression was multifocal calvarial infarction with epidural and subperiosteal hematomas, with superimposed osteomyelitis not excluded.

Given the diagnostic ambiguity and the hemispheric extent of the subdural collection, the patient was taken to the operating room for emergency right frontotemporoparietal craniotomy with subgaleal drain placement and orbital exploration. The epidural space contained thick, white proteinaceous material underlying visibly necrotic and discolored bone flap and surrounding calvarium. Diffuse hemorrhagic oozing was noted at each stage. The MMA was deliberately preserved rather than coagulated in anticipation of its potential role as a collateral conduit should moyamoya vasculopathy develop.

Intraoperative ultrasound demonstrated only a thin subdural rim, prompting two small linear durotomies. The dura was tense, and each opening yielded high-pressure extrusion of dark, hemosiderin-stained brown clear fluid resembling the effluent of a chronic subdural hematoma. There was no subdural membrane, no purulence, no fibrinous exudate, and no subarachnoid perivascular purulence, the hallmark of subdural empyema. The underlying cortex appeared healthy, without ischemia, hyperemia, or edema. Both durotomies were closed primarily, after ensuring there was no vascular injury or continued source of active hemorrhage. Good hemostasis was achieved. The cranial flap was replaced, and a subgaleal drain was left in place.

The ophthalmology team then performed a right anterior orbitotomy with drainage of the superior and inferior orbital subperiosteal collections and the upper eyelid collection, encountering blood and clotted material with only equivocal purulent-appearing debris. Abnormal tissue was biopsied. The otolaryngology team performed nasal endoscopy with right frontal sinus outflow dilation and found no inflammation or purulence within the nasal cavity or frontal sinus. All specimens were submitted for Gram stain and aerobic, anaerobic, and fungal cultures, all of which returned negative. The estimated blood loss was 50 mL, and the patient received 1 unit of packed red blood cells intraoperatively and remained hemodynamically stable.

The immediate postoperative ultrafast MRI demonstrated only trace residual subdural fluid without mass effect or midline shift. However, on postoperative day (POD) 1, contrast-enhanced MRI showed complete nonenhancement of the craniotomy bone flap due to infarct, a stable 5-mm epidural hematoma beneath the flap, and increased right subdural fluid of CSF signal intensity, together producing 4 mm of leftward midline shift. By POD 3, the right convexity subdural effusion had enlarged to 8 mm in thickness with local mass effect and 6–7 mm of leftward midline shift. MR venography excluded dural venous sinus thrombosis. The CSF signal characteristic and progressive accumulation indicated a subacute reactive effusion from inflamed dura rather than a postoperative hematoma. With durotomies closed primarily, no vascular injury, and a patent subgaleal drain, an external source was excluded and no surgical target existed.

Throughout this interval, he remained neurologically at baseline with no clinical signs of raised intracranial pressure. He was extubated on POD 3. Standard antiedema measures were maintained with strict intake and output monitoring, levetiracetam prophylaxis, and avoidance of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. Hyperosmolar therapy was not required. After multidisciplinary deliberation, reoperation was judged to carry an unfavorable risk given the friable, infarcted calvarium and anticipated propensity for rebleeding. Dexamethasone (intravenous 16 mg) was initiated, with the hematology team confirming no SCD-specific contraindication, producing progressive reduction in effusion size and mass effect on serial imaging (Fig. 3). The patient was discharged neurologically intact, and his SCD management was continued with hydroxyurea (21 mg/kg) daily with adherence support initiated.

Nine months later, the patient re-presented with recurrent right periorbital swelling and low-grade fever. Repeat imaging demonstrated new subperiosteal hemorrhagic fluid collections overlying previously infarcted calvarium, without evidence of infection (Fig. 4). The recurrent, self-limited nature of these episodes further corroborated the diagnosis of spontaneous hemorrhage arising from chronically infarcted bone. Conservative management was again successfully implemented, and he recovered without operative intervention.

Discussion

Pathophysiology of Calvarial Infarction in SCD

SCD is characterized by polymerization of abnormal HbS under deoxygenated conditions, producing rigid, sickle-shaped erythrocytes that occlude the microvasculature and precipitate tissue ischemia.3,4 Bony infarction is a well-established consequence of this ­vaso-occlusive process in the appendicular skeleton, yet calvarial involvement remains uncommon. The skull’s diploic space, supplied by a relatively sparse medullary vascular network, is still susceptible to the same microvascular occlusion that afflicts long bones. When infarction does occur, the resulting marrow necrosis triggers a robust inflammatory cascade: periosteal elevation, exudative fluid accumulation, and granulation tissue formation, which can closely simulate an infectious process.

The hemorrhagic component of these collections likely reflects disruption of friable, ischemic periosteal and dural vasculature. In our patient, the hemosiderin staining observed at surgery and the susceptibility artifacts on MRI both pointed to chronically evolving hemorrhage rather than acute suppurative disease. This pathogenic mechanism of sterile hemorrhagic exudation from infarcted bone is distinct from the purulent collections seen in osteomyelitis or hematogenous seeding, although there may be clinical and radiographic overlap.

Observations

Differentiating Sterile Exudates From Infection

The diagnostic challenge in this case was considerable. The patient presented with clinical hallmarks of infection, including fever, periorbital swelling, and C-reactive protein greater than 21 mg/dL with leukocytosis in the context of functional asplenia, which places SCD patients at heightened risk for encapsulated bacterial infections and intracranial suppurative complications.5 Initial CT findings of rim-enhancing extra-axial collections were interpreted as concerning for empyema, and empiric antimicrobial therapy was therefore appropriate. However, several MRI features in this case provided critical diagnostic nuance.

First, the diffuse heterogeneous calvarial marrow signal with multifocal restricted diffusion was more consistent with bone infarction than osteomyelitis, a distinction described by Tuna et al. as the “T2-blackout effect” on DWI (Fig. 5).2 Second, the subperiosteal and epidural collections lacked central diffusion restriction, which carries high specificity for abscess. Third, susceptibility artifacts and intrinsic T1 hyperintensity within the epidural collections pointed toward hemorrhagic contents. And lastly, the smooth, linear dural enhancement pattern contrasted with the irregular, thick enhancement more characteristic of empyema.

However, it is ultimately the intraoperative findings and culture data that are definitive. The absence of frank purulence, the hemosiderin-stained character of the subdural effluent, the absence of subarachnoid perivascular purulence, the necrotic bone, and the uniformly negative cultures from five compartments collectively established sterile hemorrhagic exudation secondary to calvarial infarction. It is worth noting that antibiotics were given roughly 24 hours before operative sampling, and partially treated infection cannot be excluded on culture data alone. However, there are several features that argue against this: the hemosiderin-stained rather than purulent fluid, the absent subdural membrane and perivascular purulence, the negative sinonasal endoscopy results that eliminated the presumed source, the concordant imaging signature of infarction, and the self-limited recurrence 9 months later without antimicrobial therapy. The pretreatment sequence nonetheless limits certainty here, and will recur in most such patients, for whom empiric coverage cannot be withheld.

Management Considerations and the Role of MMA Embolization

When postoperative imaging revealed an expanding chronic subdural collection with 6–7 mm of midline shift, the team faced a decision between surgical reintervention and conservative management. The friability of the infarcted calvarium, the absence of infection requiring source control, the anticipated propensity for recurrent hemorrhage in the SCD patient population, and the absence of any surgical target all favored a trial of corticosteroids. The dura had been closed primarily, there was no vascular injury, the subgaleal drain remained patent, and the fluid was radiographically a reactive effusion from inflamed dura rather than an evacuable hematoma. Repeated drainage would have palliated a symptom without addressing its cause.

Medical management with dexamethasone was selected for its ability to transcriptionally suppress inflammatory mediator expression and vascular endothelial growth factor activity within the inflamed dura, attenuating the pathological angiogenesis and capillary permeability sustaining the chronic subdural accumulation. The sterile, ­inflammation-driven origin of this collection made the patient particularly amenable to corticosteroid therapy without risk of masking an occult infectious process. Serial imaging confirmed a durable response.

MMA embolization has emerged as an increasingly utilized treatment for chronic subdural hematomas, with growing evidence supporting its efficacy in reducing recurrence rates. Its application was actively considered here; however, in the SCD population this approach warrants circumspection. Patients with SCD carry an elevated risk of moyamoya vasculopathy, a progressive stenotic arteriopathy of the major intracranial vessels that leads to compensatory collateral formation.6,7 The MMA and its transdural branches may serve as critical supply pathways in these patients. Embolization of the MMA in the setting of SCD could limit a potential future source of collateral vascular supply, a risk that must be weighed carefully against the potential benefit of reduced subdural recurrence.

The recurrence of subperiosteal hemorrhage at 9 months further illustrates the chronic, relapsing nature of calvarial complications in SCD. Conservative management was again successful, reinforcing the principle that these sterile collections, once properly diagnosed, may not require aggressive surgical intervention. An individualized, multidisciplinary approach remains essential for optimal outcomes.

Prior Descriptions of Calvarial Infarction in SCD

There are more than 30 cases in the existing literature detailing epidural and subgaleal hematomas as a complication of calvarial infarction in SCD.8 Mallouh et al. described a case similar to ours of skull infarction, epidural hematoma, retro-orbital hemorrhage, and fever closely mimicking orbital cellulitis, but without subdural involvement, MRI characterization, or longitudinal follow-up.9 Spontaneous subdural hematoma in this context is exceedingly rare, with only two prior reports. Chipongo et al. reported acute subdural hematoma in an adolescent with SCD, but without orbital involvement and skull infarction as the inciting event.3 Nelson and Scarfone detailed a similar case that was linked to calvarial infarction in SCD; however there was no fever, infectious mimic, or surgical exploration.10 Most recently, Fong et al. reported on a 6-year-old boy with SCD and orbital vaso-occlusive crisis mimicking orbital abscess, in whom surgical drainage revealed dark red-brown fluid without purulence and pathology consistent with an organizing hematoma—independent, contemporaneous corroboration of the sterile exudate phenomenon, although confined to the orbital compartment.11 To our knowledge, the present case is the first to describe the triad of calvarial infarction, spontaneous subdural exudative collection, and periorbital hemorrhage mimicking both subdural empyema and orbital abscess. We also document the chronic relapsing outcome of these collections over a 9- month follow-up and the use of corticosteroids to manage the subdural reaccumulation without surgery.

Lessons

Calvarial infarction in SCD can generate sterile subdural and epidural fluid collections that are radiographically indistinguishable from empyema or abscess on initial imaging. The combination of fever, periorbital swelling, and rim-enhancing intracranial collections in a functionally asplenic patient creates a compelling clinical picture for infection; however, the underlying etiology may be entirely noninfectious.

Specific MRI features provide diagnostic clues. Multifocal calvarial restricted diffusion suggests infarction rather than osteomyelitis, the absence of central restricted diffusion within subperiosteal collections argues against true abscess, and susceptibility artifacts and T1 hyperintensity indicate hemorrhagic rather than purulent contents. These radiographic nuances, interpreted in clinical context, can guide surgical planning and inform expectations at the time of operation.

Intraoperative findings and culture results remain the definitive path toward a diagnosis. When surgical exploration reveals ­hemosiderin-stained fluid, necrotic bone, and absence of subarachnoid perivascular purulence, and when cultures are uniformly negative, clinicians can pivot from broad antimicrobial therapy to targeted management of the underlying hematological condition while keeping in mind that antibiotics given before sampling limit the certainty of any negative culture.

Conservative management, including corticosteroids, may be effective for chronic subdural collections associated with bone infarction, particularly when surgical reintervention carries elevated risk. MMA embolization, while an attractive option for recurrent chronic subdural hematomas, requires careful consideration of the current and future cerebrovascular landscape in SCD, where moyamoya vasculopathy may make the collateral dependent territories vulnerable to ischemia.

Ultimately, this case reminds us that the SCD phenotype extends beyond the hematological system. Neurosurgeons must maintain a broad differential when evaluating SCD patients with cranial pathology, recognizing that the inflammatory and hemorrhagic sequelae of bone infarction can imitate infectious disease with profoundly different implications for treatment.
