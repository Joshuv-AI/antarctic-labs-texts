Educational needs of the EAES young surgeons
Authors: Marta Botti, Audrey C H M Jongen, Clarisa Bîrlog, Emanuele Botteri, Piers R Boshier, Nader Francis, Salvador Morales-Conde, Ana Senent-Boza
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSurgical residency programs are facing ongoing challenges since the pandemic. The Young Member (YM) Task Force of the European Association for Endoscopic Surgery (EAES) has been created to support surgical residents and surgeons under 40, promoting activities that could be useful for young surgeons and to improve networking between members of different countries. This project aimed to explore educational needs among European surgical residents to inform the EAES curriculum for young surgeons.MethodsWithin the EAES surgical society, the YM developed a survey to capture residents’ views on their professional needs. Different aspects were considered, from demographic information to the major topics of interest on a professional side. Semi-automatic answers and personal suggestions have been gathered and considered for the analysis.ResultsData were captured from 386 respondents across 57 different countries, major responders were represented by the group of 31–35 years old (40%). Considering professional growth, young surgeons rely on scientific research (56%), but they struggle finding adequate support of facilities. In the education and training section, fellowships and hands-on-courses (27%) were the most proficient activities. Mentorships and webinars were important to develop leadership, as well as social events to build up a professional network.ConclusionThis project has identified young surgeons’ educational and professional needs that can inform the EAES future projects and activities.Keywords: EAES, Survey, Young surgeon, Education, Leadership, Networking

The training of residents and young surgeons takes anywhere between 5 and 10 years to be completed and many differences can be seen throughout European countries [1] regarding the academic program for the Degree in Medicine and General Surgery Residency. It is clear that theoretical knowledge and practical skills required vary strongly and are difficult to compare [2], as for example personal logbooks on progression and number of interventions are not mandatory in all the European countries. Furthermore, research opportunities are sparse or even completely lacking in some regions, resulting in a heterogeneous spectrum of possibilities for professional growth of a young surgeon [3].

The recent COVID-19 pandemic that challenged health systems around the world, caused a decrease in care for elective surgical pathology, with a consequent increase in surgical waiting lists. This reduced exposure to operative procedures had a negative impact on surgical training [4–7]. Moreover, the exponential development and adoption of robotic surgery in recent years, require that specialists and senior surgeons complete their own training in this surgical approach, to the detriment of resident training [8].

As a recognition of the above challenges, the Young Members (YM) Task Force has been recently created by the European Association for Endoscopic Surgery (EAES). It consists of a group of young surgeons from different european countries that aims to promote integration of young surgeons in one of the most important european surgical societies, as well as cultivating activities to support their training, improve accessibility among young surgeons in various fields and develop a supportive network for the beginning of new careers. In order to obtain a better understanding of young surgeons’ needs with regards to education, research, and professional growth, this survey was conducted.

Methods

A questionnaire was developed by the Young Member Task Force (YMTF) of the EAES and assessed by the senior members, in order to ensure that all major topics were covered and that the questions were not ambiguous or misleading.

The survey was distributed among the EAES members by e-mail, addressing young surgeons. A ‘young surgeon’ was defined within the EAES as a surgical doctor under 40 years of age, or who had ended their residency in General Surgery within the last 5 years. In order to reach a greater number of surgeons, every recipient also had the possibility to forward the survey to other residents and young surgeons, including non-EAES members.

The survey used a 18-item questionnaire with a semi-automatic self-completion, addressing educational and professional needs as well as peer support. Respondents had to select their answers or to insert a few words, collecting demographic information, such as age, country of origin, type of hospital, surgical specialty, and years of experience. For other questions, respondents were asked to prioritize points of interest they felt were most needed for professional growth in terms of training, research, leadership, and peer support. A free text box to provide suggestions or proposals was added at the end of the survey.

Data were gathered and processed with the use of a semi-automatic analysis and tables were created. All variables were categorical and they were presented as frequencies and percentages. Considering the answers of this questionnaire, a quantitative analysis was done for certain aspects, proposing a qualitative overview on topic and personal suggestions gathered through the survey. Statistical analysis was conducted using the SPSS version 24.0 (IBM SPSS, Inc, Armonk, NY). Univariate analysis was performed using χ2 analysis or Fisher exact test, as appropriate.

Results

A total of 386 surgeons and surgical residents from 57 different countries completed the questionnaire (Fig. 1). The majority of them took their residency in Italy (28,76%), followed by Spain (7,51%) and Romania (6,48%). The median duration of the general surgery residency is 5 (43%) or 6 (36%) years, with a minor presence of different programs, ranging from 3 to 8 years.

Residency countries of the responders

Young specialist surgeons represented 73% of the total number of respondents, while the remaining 26% was formed by residents. Considering a distribution per age range (Fig. 2), 39.6% were in the group of 31–35 years old, 30.3% in the group of 36–40 years old and 17.1% in the group of 26–30 years old. The remaining small groups were distributed among the other age groups. Most respondents were working in university hospitals (50.3%), followed by general public non-academic hospitals (29.3%), both in public and private (10.1%) and private (8.3%) hospitals. Beside surgical residents, the exploration of surgical subspecialties found a quite uniform distribution in the most representative areas (upper-GI and bariatric, HPB, colorectal, abdominal wall surgery), leaving a smaller percentage of endocrine surgery and urology.

Age of responders to the survey

Certain clear subjects were deemed most important by young surgeons to support their professional growth. The majority of responders (70.7%) were convinced that conducting scientific research was important for their career progression, but the degree of support they received varied strongly according to their responses (49.7% received high support, 23.3% described medium support, and 26.4% low or absent support). Nevertheless, 37.3% stated that they felt infrastructures were deficient and support to undertake research was lacking. This percentage reached 53.1 and 46.9% among respondents working in private and general hospitals, respectively, being inferior (27.5%) among those working in university hospitals (p = 0.04).

The survey then deepened the analysis at three different domains. With regards to education and training (Fig. 3), a fellowship program (27,72%) and hands-on courses for basic (24,09%) and advanced laparoscopic training (31,61%) were deemed the most valuable additions to regular programs. When it comes to leadership (Fig. 4), young surgeons rated mentorship programs (39,12%), online lectures (22,54%), and webinars (23,83%) as the most important activities to support their professional development.

The third aspect analyzed in the survey was peer support (Fig. 5). Network building (54,66%) followed by social events during international congresses (35,75%) were considered as most needed among respondents.

Finally, the last question assessed the availability or use of surgical facilities for training such as wet or dry lab, and laparoscopic or virtual training boxes. The results from the survey revealed that more than 60% lacked access to these facilities or had never used them. The laparoscopic training box seemed to be the only instrument accessible to most surgeons.

From the analysis of the free texts and suggestions and proposals for further improvements, it became clear that young surgeons expressed the desire to increase the access of training, with the ultimate goal of creating a pan-European training curriculum. Others stated that there is an intrinsic cultural failure in surgery to support individual needs such as less than full time training and/ or trainees who require long term leave to raise young children while in the beginning of their surgical careers.

Discussion

One of the missions of the EAES is to improve professional development and collaboration of its members, with particular interest in young members. In order to foster innovation and inclusivity, the YMTF was established with a specific mission: to engage young colleagues in key decision-making processes and cultivate an expansive international network of skilled surgeons. One of the primary objectives of this study was to identify and address the challenges faced by young surgeons across Europe, aimed at enhancing education and research opportunities. This study has identified the educational and professional needs of young surgeons. The educational needs of the young members that were identified in this study can be summarized in three domains. First, easy access to fellowship programs and hands-on laparoscopic training courses could improve education and training of residents. Secondly, good mentorship, supported by lectures and webinars, is fundamental for the development of a leadership attitude. Finally, network creation between colleagues, even with participation in social events, is important for peer support and collaboration.

These domains fit very well with the agenda of the EAES as it has been actively committed to bridging these disparities in recent years. Efforts have been made to engage Eastern European countries and provide grants for qualified surgeons facing financial constraints. Furthermore, the concept of equity extends beyond economic considerations; notably gender disparity, particularly in Eastern Europe or low-income countries [9]. A noteworthy proposal emerging from the survey is the development of a pan-European training curriculum, aimed at standardizing professional education across the continent.

During the past years, EAES has demonstrated its dedication to nurturing the careers of young surgeons through various initiatives. These include online sessions on academic writing, research mentorship programs, but also research grants in order to provide economical support to colleagues who demonstrate good potential but lack financial support from their home country or institution. While these opportunities have been widely appreciated, there is still room for further support for the younger generation.

The results of the survey reveal that the most sought-after form of support is the implementation of mentorship programs to support the holistic development of young surgeons, rather than just focusing on technical skills, online sessions about academic writing and grant applications following closely behind. To address these pressing needs, we have identified three core domains in which EAES can structure its actions [10]: training and education, leadership, and peer support.

When it comes to leadership development, respondents identified the creation of a mentorship program as the most important activity, closely followed by online lectures regarding leadership. Within this process, a leader could become a mentor, helping new generations of surgeons finding their own place in surgical societies and daily practice. In response to this survey, the EAES has already commenced a mentorship program to personal needs of young surgeons and to prepare future leaders of the association. This program started with a series of online workshops from experts and leaders to aspire young surgeons by offering insights into career development that can bridge generational gaps and dismantle prejudices [11].

Within the domain of education and training, young surgeons express a strong interest in hands-on courses for laparoscopic training and fellowship programs. Acquiring practical surgical skills is often a challenging task for emerging surgeons, in which simulation training can play a very important role [12, 13] during the first years of training, but also for advanced surgeons or during fellowships [14].

Finally, one of the essential factors that underpin optimal leadership is peer support. This is the reason why building a solid network of colleagues is one of the most important aspects in the third domain. Social events during congresses offer invaluable opportunities to strengthen friendships, exchange ideas, and collaborate for future projects.

This study has a number of limitations. First, although young surgeons and residents from 56 different countries replied to our questionnaire, a clear overrepresentation was seen from Italy, Spain, and Romania. This bias can easily be explained by the uneven distribution of EAES members across Europe and the rest of the world. Throughout Europe, residents and young surgeons have different educational pathways and also their clinical practice is heterogeneous among countries [15, 16]. Disparities exist in the competencies acquired during residency, as well as involvement in research and financial support for attending medical congresses. Additionally, although our survey captured the opinion of members from over 50 different countries, it reduced our ability to calculate a response rate which would have informed our assessment over selection bias. It is appropriate to imagine that survey non-respondents are less likely to significantly engage in the EAES initiatives. Nevertheless, the outcome of this survey serves as a foundation for future EAES professional and training curricula to support surgical residents and to provide valuable insights to enhance professional growth of young surgeons within the society. Knowledge of weak points and disparities is crucial to overcome problems and difficulties, but further work is required to attain young surgeons from all over Europe the same opportunity of personal and professional growth.

Conclusion

This study provides insight to the current needs of the young members of the EAES and highlights the ongoing educational and professional gaps to inform future strategies of the Association. However, further research and observational studies over the European scenario are needed, especially in the educational field. A detailed knowledge about the differences in surgical residency and operative curricula could improve the professional growth and enhance international exchange of health-care professionists. Besides mentorship programs and online sessions about academic writing, other important aspects should be implemented: hands-on laparoscopic courses, fellowship programs and occasions to ease the creation of a network of colleagues. Incorporation of a formal mentorship program to the current curricula requirements for the EAES surgical trainees is likely to aid expansion of new leaders for the EAES and surgical community.
