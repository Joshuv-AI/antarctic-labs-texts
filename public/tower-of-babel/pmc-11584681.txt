Enhanced characterization and utilization of cowpea protein isolate: rheological and textural properties of heat-induced gels for plant-based egg omelet formulation
Authors: Hyun Woo Choi, Jungwoo Hahn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the potential of cowpea isolate (CPI) as an effective plant-based egg substitute by comparing its composition and physicochemical properties with those of commercial isolates, including mung bean, soy, and pea proteins. Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis identified a distinctive vicilin-like globulin in CPI that is critical for the unique functional properties of CPI. CPI has a smaller particle size, resulting in superior gelation capacity and water retention, which are essential for egg substitute applications. Thermal analysis revealed the high stability of CPI, which is advantageous for food processing. Notably, the unique heat-induced gelation behavior of CPI, characterized by a smooth transition from solid- to liquid-like states, provided a strength not observed in its commercial counterparts. Microstructural analysis showed that the CPI gel closely resembled the egg gel, underscoring the potential of CPI to mimic the texture of eggs, particularly in omelets.Keywords: Plant-based egg omelet, Cowpea protein, Protein isolate, Protein gel

Introduction

The global shift toward sustainable and ethical food consumption has led to a burgeoning demand for plant-based protein substitutes, particularly egg alternatives. This demand is driven by the necessity to find more environmentally sustainable and health-conscious protein sources, as traditional animal-based protein production is increasingly scrutinized for its environmental impact and health implications. Egg substitute ingredients are anticipated to reach a market value of approximately US$ 2.13 billion by 2033 and are vital for diversifying our protein sources (Lu et al., 2023). Vegan egg products, which have traditionally been applied in baked goods, have expanded into a broader range of culinary uses (Lin et al., 2017). However, the challenge lies in the intricate interactions between protein materials and their functionalities, as highlighted by recent studies exploring mung bean and soy proteins (Lu et al., 2022).

Soybeans, peas, and mung beans are preferred owing to their favorable gelling properties (Grizio & Specht, 2018). However, these proteins also present significant challenges. For instance, soybeans cause allergenic reactions in many people, limiting their widespread use in food products. While generally hypoallergenic, pea proteins often exhibit suboptimal gelation, resulting in weaker gel structures that may not provide the desired texture in certain applications. Mung bean proteins, though capable of forming gels, often suffer from textural limitations, such as a grainy or less cohesive consistency, which can impact the mouthfeel and overall acceptance of the final product (Wang et al., 2022). These disadvantages underscore the need to explore novel plant-based protein sources to overcome these limitations and meet the growing demand for high-quality, functional plant-based ingredients.

Amidst this setting, our study focused on cowpea protein, a source yet to be fully explored. Cowpeas exhibit a unique protein structure and gelation kinetics, making them potential candidates for plant-based egg alternatives (Shrestha et al., 2023). Previous research on the gelation properties of other plant protein isolates, such as those from soy, pea, and mung bean, shows that gel formation and stability differ significantly depending on the protein source, extraction method, and processing conditions (Shan et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2021). For example, soy protein isolate has strong gelation capabilities due to its high content of β-conglycinin and glycinin, which contribute to gel network formation (Wu et al., 2022). Pea and mung bean protein isolates also exhibit gelation properties, although they often require specific pH conditions or the addition of salts to achieve desirable gel characteristics (Jeong & Cho, 2024; Zhu et al., 2021). In comparison, cowpea protein isolate (CPI) gels have not been extensively studied, leaving a gap in understanding how CPI behaves under various conditions. The extraction process of cowpea protein is particularly crucial, as commercial protein powders often have similar nutritional profiles but exhibit different functional properties depending on the extraction methods. These differences arise from variations in the types of proteins extracted, extent of protein denaturation, and functional properties of food colloids, such as emulsification, foaming, and coagulation. The influence of these extraction processes on the functional characteristics of CPI remains largely unexplored. The gelation dynamics of proteins, including those of cowpeas, involve complex molecular conformational changes, intermolecular associations, and aggregation phenomena (Ben-Harb et al., 2018; McCann et al., 2018). Understanding these processes is crucial for developing adequate plant-based egg substitutes. Despite the introduction of various plant-based egg substitute products, our understanding of the coagulation mechanisms of the proteins used in these formulations is incomplete. Therefore, systematic investigations into the functional characteristics of diverse plant proteins compared to those of conventional animal-derived proteins are needed (O'Kane et al., 2004).

This study aimed to explore the potential of CPI as a viable alternative to egg proteins by focusing on its rheological, textural, sensory, and nutritional attributes. Specifically, we conducted a comparative analysis of CPIs with commercial alternatives such as soy, pea, and mung bean proteins. We analyzed their compositional and physicochemical properties, which facilitated the development of plant-based omelets. Thus, we sought to identify the potential of cowpea protein as a viable alternative in the plant-based egg substitute market.

Materials and methods

Materials

Cowpeas, commercial mung bean protein isolate (CMPI), and whole egg liquid were provided by Intake Co. (Seoul, Korea). Commercial soy protein isolate (CSPI) and pea protein isolate (CPPI) were purchased from Jung Woo Korea Co., Ltd. (Seoul, Korea) and Roquette (Lestrem, France), respectively. Canola oil was purchased from CJ Co. (Seoul, South Korea). All other chemicals were of analytical grade.

Protein extraction

Cowpeas were peeled and processed into a paste that was subsequently combined with water at a ratio of 1:5 (w/v). The pH of the mixture was adjusted to 8.5 using 1 N NaOH and stirred at 900 rpm for 1 h. The solubilized protein was recovered through centrifugation (9,000 rpm, 25 min, 4 °C), and then, the pH was adjusted to 5 using 1 N HCl. Subsequently, precipitated proteins were collected by centrifugation (9,000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C) and pH-normalized to 6 via neutralization. The resulting protein isolates were lyophilized. The freeze-dried protein isolates were pulverized and passed through a 100-mesh sieve to achieve particle size homogenization. They were then stored in an airtight container at 20 °C for subsequent analyses.

The protein content of laboratory-extracted CPI was 84.95 %. Commercially obtained proteins exhibited protein contents of 89.10, 86.82, and 86.46 % for CSPI, CPPI, and CMPI, respectively.

Characterization of protein isolates

Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis

Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) of protein isolates was conducted using Mini-PROTEAN® TGX™ Precast Gels (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Protein isolates were prepared at a concentration of 1 mg/mL by dissolving them in distilled water. Subsequently, a 2× Laemmli sample buffer (10 μL) was mixed with the protein supernatant (10 μL). The mixture was heated to 100 °C for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 6,000 rpm for 1 min. The running buffer was composed of 10× Tris/Glycine/SDS buffer diluted to 1× using distilled water. Next, a volume of 10 μL of the prepared sample supernatant and 3 μL of a protein molecular marker were loaded onto the precast gel. Electrophoresis was performed at 200 V for 30 min. After electrophoresis, the gel was stained using sun-gel staining solution (LPS Solution, Daejeon, Korea). The staining process involved continuous stirring for 24 h. Subsequently, the stained gel was rinsed with 200 mL of deionized water, followed by a destaining process involving constant agitation for 48 h with intermittent solution replacement.

Secondary structure

The secondary protein structure of the protein isolates was investigated using an FT-IR spectrometer (Vertex-80 V, Bruker Corp., Billerica, MA, USA). FT-IR spectra were recorded over the range of 4000–600 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1. Overall, 32 scans were performed. The secondary protein structure was determined using the PeakFit software (version 4.12, Bruker Co., Billerica, MA, USA) with the Gaussian peak fitting algorithm.

Particle size analysis

The protein isolates were dispersed in distilled water at a concentration of 10 % (w/v) and allowed to hydrate overnight. Subsequently, the diluted samples were analyzed using a particle size analyzer (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern Instruments Ltd., Malvern, Worcestershire, UK). For analysis, the sample solution was dispersed in distilled water at 2,000 rpm until an obscuration rate of 12 % was achieved. The refractive index was set to 1.52 for accurate measurements.

Thermal properties

The thermal properties of the protein isolates were investigated using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC 4000, PerkinElmer Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The protein isolate was prepared as a 15 % (w/w) suspension using distilled water as the solvent, followed by stirring at a constant rate of 500 rpm for 24 h at room temperature. Subsequently, aliquots of the prepared suspension were pipetted into stainless steel pans and sealed. The resultant pan was scanned over a temperature range of 10–135 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C/min, whereas the reference pan was empty.

Zeta potential

The zeta potential of the protein isolates was determined at 25 °C employing a Nano ZS90 Zetasizer (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Malvern, Worcestershire, UK). Prior to measurement, the protein solution was diluted by a factor of ten with distilled water. The zeta potential was assessed by placing the diluted solution in a cuvette.

Rheological properties

The rheological properties of the protein isolates were determined by modifying established methodologies (Shrestha et al., 2023). Proteins at varying concentrations (10, 15, and 20 % w/v) were dispersed within distilled water and subjected to stirring for 3 h at 800 rpm and 25 °C, employing an overhead stirrer (PL-SS20DW, Pltech, Seoul, Korea). The rheological characteristics of the protein isolates were subsequently assessed using a dynamic rheometer (DHR1, TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA) equipped with a parallel plate-plate geometry system (20 mm in diameter). The distance between the Peltier plate and geometry was set to 1 mm. To prevent moisture loss during testing, a solvent trap module was employed and silicone oil was applied.

Temperature sweep test

Protein dispersions were conditioned on Peltier plates at 25 °C for 2 min. Then, a temperature sweep test was conducted at a frequency of 1 Hz and a strain of 1 % (within the linear viscoelastic range) in accordance with a predetermined temperature profile: heating from 25 to 95 °C at a heating rate of 2 °C/min, holding at 95 °C for 10 min, and cooling from 95 to 25 °C at a cooling rate of 2 °C/min. The concentration-dependent effect of the heat-induced protein gel network was analyzed based on the phase angle (tan δ values at the end of the cooling phase) and GR (Greinforcement = G' at the end of cooling – G' at the end of heating).

Frequency sweep test

A frequency sweep test was carried out at a strain of 1 % and a temperature of 25 °C across an angular frequency range of 0.1–100 rad/s. The test was conducted after a temperature sweep to track alterations in the viscoelastic properties attributed to heat-induced gelation.

Characterization of heat-induced protein gels

Protein isolates were prepared at varying concentrations (10, 15, and 20 % w/v) according to the procedure outlined in the section on rheological properties (see Section 2.4). Subsequently, the protein dispersion and the whole egg liquid were introduced into separate glass vials and exposed to a 30-min heating period within a water bath maintained at 90 °C. Following the heating process, cooling was facilitated by immersing the vial in ice water for 12 h, after which the samples were stored at 4 °C. The properties of the heat-induced protein and egg gels were comprehensively analyzed, including appearance, field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), molecular force, gel strength, and WHC. Except for the SEM and molecular force analyses, all analyses were performed across a range of concentrations (10, 15, and 20 % w/v). For FE-SEM and molecular force analysis, the assessment was performed specifically on protein gel samples at a concentration of 20 %.

Appearance

Alterations in the visual characteristics of the protein and egg gels were captured using a fixed camera (450D, Canon, Tokyo, Japan) positioned within a black box.

Fe-SEM

The microstructures of the protein and egg gels were examined using FE-SEM (SIGMA, Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Following platinum coating, the specimens were affixed to aluminum stubs and observed at a magnification of 500× and an accelerating voltage of 5 kV.

Molecular forces

Molecular interaction was assessed using three dissociation reagents: SDS (1 % w/v), urea (2 M), and β-mercaptoethanol (β-me; 0.2 M). Each reagent served a distinct role in hydrophobic interactions (SDS), hydrogen bond (urea), and disulfide bond (β-me) cleavage. Prepared gel samples (1 g) were dispersed in each dissociation reagent (4 mL) and vortexed for 30 s. The resultant mixture was subjected to heating in a water bath at 80 °C for 1 h, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 ×g for 20 min. The mixture was then cooled to room temperature. Protein solubility in the supernatant was quantified using the Bradford method, which allows calculation of protein gel solubility as the proportion of soluble protein within the supernatant to the overall protein content.

Gel strength

Gel strength assessment of both protein and egg gels was conducted using a texture analyzer (TA.XT plus, Stable Micro Systems, Godalming, Surrey, UK). The gel was shaped into cylindrical forms (diameter 4 cm, height 3 cm) and acclimatized to a temperature of 25 °C. The testing procedure employed a P/5 probe (5 mm diameter) at a strain of 40 % and a crosshead speed of 2 mm/s. The peak force attained during gel penetration was used as a measure of gel strength (Zisu et al., 2010).

Water holding capacity

Characterization of plant-based egg omelets

Plant-based liquid eggs were created by amalgamation of all samples for 30 min at 1,000 rpm using an overhead stirrer (PL-SS20DW, Pltech, Seoul, Korea). The preparation of both plant-based and conventional egg omelets ensued by carefully pouring 100 g of the plant-based liquid eggs or whole egg liquid, respectively, alongside 5 g of sunflower oil, into a pre-heated frying pan maintained at 120 °C for a span of 2 min. Each side of the omelet was heated for 2 min and then allowed to cool to room temperature for 30 min before analysis. The physicochemical attributes of both plant-based and conventional egg omelets were analyzed, including appearance, textural properties, and specific volume (Lu et al., 2022). Electronic tongue analysis

Electronic tongue analysis was conducted using an E-tongue (ASTREEII system, Alpha MOS Company, France). Minced egg or plant-based egg omelet (45 g) was mixed with distilled water (200 mL) and extracted at 85 °C for 10 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 3,000 ×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was filtered through filter paper for use in the experiment. The control group (egg omelet) for the plant-based egg omelet was considered the standard (0) in the current analysis. The electronic tongue system comprises five different taste sensors. Based on their taste detection characteristics, the sensors are named AHS (for sour substances), NMS (for umami substances), ANS (for bitter substances), PKS (for sweet substances), and CTS (for salty substances).

Statistical analysis

Protein gels and plant-based egg omelets were prepared in at least three separate experiments. All assessments related to characterization were conducted on at least three occasions. After data collection, the experimental information was analyzed employing analysis of variance (ANOVA). The presented data are denoted as mean values accompanied by standard deviations. Significant disparities between the mean values of individual traits were substantiated using Tukey's test at a significance level of p < 0.05. The SPSS software (version 25.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used to facilitate statistical computations and analyses.

Results and discussion

Research methodology

This study aimed to evaluate the potential of CPI as an effective plant-based egg substitute by comparing its composition and physicochemical properties with those of commercial protein isolates (CSPI, CPPI, and CMPI). Since commercial protein isolates can exhibit different functional properties depending on the extraction process, we sought to carefully present the extraction conditions for CPI and analyze its functional properties accordingly. We hypothesized that CPI, with its unique globulin protein composition, exhibits functional properties that closely mimic those of traditional egg proteins, particularly during heat-induced gelation. To test this hypothesis, CPIs were extracted from cowpeas using a method designed based on pH control to minimize changes in their intrinsic properties. The extracted CPI was compared with commercially available soy, pea, and mung bean protein isolates using a comprehensive characterization sequence. These included SDS-PAGE for protein profiling, particle size analysis for textural insights, and DSC for determining thermal stability. Functional properties such as the WHC and OHC, EAI, ESI, and foaming properties (FC and FS) were evaluated, and zeta potential analysis was performed to understand the surface charge behavior. Rheological analysis using temperature and frequency sweep tests was performed to explore the gelation dynamics of the protein gels, and FE-SEM provided microstructural insights.

In the omelet study, we aimed to assess the structural, textural, and sensory properties of omelets made from CPI compared with those of traditional egg omelets. Plant-based omelets were prepared using CPI and the commercial isolates. The physicochemical properties of these omelets were evaluated using specific volume measurements, TPA for hardness and springiness, and visual assessment of structural integrity. These tests were designed to determine how closely CPI omelets replicate the properties of traditional egg omelets, thereby validating their potential as egg substitutes. These results are discussed in detail in the following sections.

Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and secondary structure

The SDS-PAGE analysis identified the predominant protein components within CPI and other legume protein isolates (Fig. 1A). Notably, CPI and CMPI exhibited a significant band at 48 kDa, indicative of vicilin-like proteins. This similarity suggests a potential overlap in functional properties, particularly gelation and texture, which are crucial for egg substitutes. This observation is consistent with previous studies that identified mung bean proteins as being predominantly vicilin-like within the total globulin content (Lee & Hong, 2023). Notably, three β-conglycinin subunits, specifically α’ (∼72 kDa), α (∼68 kDa), and β (∼50 kDa), were identified, along with a noticeable band at 35 kDa that corresponds to the glycinin subunit. The presence of β-conglycinin and glycinin subunits in CSPI and CPPI indicates varied protein composition, which may influence CSPI and CPPI gelation behavior differently compared to that of CPI and CMPI (Shrestha et al., 2023). Vicilin-like proteins in CPI and CMPI confer unique gelation properties that are advantageous for mimicking the texture and consistency of egg whites in omelet formulations.

The significant band at 48 kDa observed in CPI and CMPI during SDS-PAGE analysis was indicative of vicilin-like proteins. Vicilin-like proteins are known for their ability to form strong gels, which is a crucial property for egg substitutes. The presence of these proteins suggests that CPI and CMPI have gelation properties similar to those of egg proteins, particularly for the formation of heat-induced gels that mimic the texture of cooked egg whites. Further studies on the exact role of these proteins in gelation could provide deeper insights into the optimization of CPI for plant-based egg applications.

The secondary structure of proteins can be estimated using FT-IR spectroscopy, enabling the evaluation of protein structures during and after heat treatment (Law et al., 2008). The components of the secondary structure of protein isolates are shown in Table S2. Generally, the secondary structure of protein isolates is predominantly composed of β-sheets, whereas β-turns were relatively less abundant. Proteins with a high β-sheet content maintain a more thermally stable structure and exhibit limited digestibility due to restricted access by proteolytic enzymes (Carbonaro et al., 2012). This phenomenon is attributed to the formation of β-sheet aggregates during heat treatment, which limits the access of proteolytic enzymes (Carbonaro et al., 2012). The second most abundant structure, random coils, can affect hydrogen bonding during heating, leading to increased protein disorder (Shevkani et al., 2019). The secondary structure of proteins can be reorganized by acids and bases during the extraction process of protein isolates, suggesting that extraction conditions and methods change the secondary structure (Tang & Ma, 2009).

Particle size distribution, water holding capacity, and oil holding capacity

When analyzing the particle size distribution, WHC, and OHC values of CPI and other commercial plant-based protein isolates, we observed a significant interaction between these characteristics, which influenced the functionality of egg substitutes (Table 1, Fig. 1B, C). Particle size distribution analysis revealed a marked difference between the laboratory-extracted CPI and commercially extracted proteins. CPI demonstrated a smaller particle size (35.00 μm) than the commercial isolates CSPI, CPPI, and CMPI, with D4,3 values of 190.33, 61.17, and 46.63 μm, respectively, which fell within previously documented ranges (Yada, 2017). This difference in particle size is not only a physical attribute, but closely tied to functional properties such as gelation capacity and moisture retention, which are crucial in mimicking the texture and consistency of egg whites in omelet formulations.

Analysis of the WHC and OHC of protein isolates provided insights into their functional properties for food applications. Specifically, the WHC is crucial for retaining food moisture, whereas OHC enhances flavor. WHC and OHC analyses further confirmed the results of the particle size distributions. As shown in Fig. 1C, WHC values ranged between 3.37 and 7.40 g/g, with commercially extracted proteins showing higher capacities. This finding correlates with previous research indicating that specific extraction processes can reduce nitrogen solubility, consequently affecting the WHC (Shrestha et al., 2023). Proteins with larger particle sizes typically exhibit a higher WHC, as noted in commercially extracted samples, which can be advantageous for retaining moisture in food products such as plant-based omelets (Lee et al., 2021). Interestingly, CPI exhibited a small particle size of 35.00 μm, suggesting enhanced functionalities such as improved gelation capacity and water retention—critical attributes for egg substitute formulations (Wang et al., 2020). The small particle size of CPI might confer superior binding and water holding capabilities, closely mimicking the desirable qualities of egg whites in omelets. The larger particle sizes of the commercial isolates, likely due to the extraction process and different sources, may influence the texture and WHC of the final product (Osen et al., 2014). Additionally, all samples showed relatively high OHC values between 1.98 and 3.44 g/g, further emphasizing the role of protein structure in food formulations. In particular, CMPI had the highest OHC, which could have resulted from exposure to hydrophobic amino acids owing to structural unfolding during extraction. These results suggest that extraction conditions may improve flavor retention by modifying protein conformation to increase interactions with oil molecules.

Thermal properties

The Tp value (peak temperature) indicates the degree of disruption of the tertiary and quaternary structures of a protein. It reflects the temperature at which significant conformational changes occur and serves as a benchmark for thermal stability (Mession et al., 2015). In contrast, the ΔH value represents the enthalpy change associated with a thermal transition, indicating the strength of protein interactions and the compactness of the protein structure (Shrestha et al., 2023). While investigating the thermal properties (Table 1, Fig. 1D), we observed that CPI exhibited clear thermal transition peaks, characterized by Tp values from 92 to 93 °C and ΔH values between 5 and 6 J/g. These findings indicate that CPI has distinct thermal stability and robust protein interactions. In contrast, as shown in Table 1, commercially obtained isolates exhibited diminished or absent thermal transitions, likely due to the extensive denaturation caused by the harsh extraction process.

To ensure a controlled and consistent thermal process, we conducted heat treatment at 90 °C in a water bath. This temperature was carefully selected to simulate common culinary conditions, in which proteins are often subjected to similar temperatures; although it was slightly lower than the Tp values observed in the DSC analysis, we observed significant gelation at 90 °C, indicating that even at slightly reduced temperatures, the thermal energy was sufficient to induce substantial conformational changes in the protein structure. This observation underscores that heat transfer dynamics in a water bath differ significantly from those in a DSC environment. In a water bath, the protein samples experience more uniform heating, leading to different gelation kinetics compared to those of the localized heating and cooling cycles typical of DSC analysis.

Additionally, the pronounced presence of vicilin-like globulins in CPI, as revealed by the SDS-PAGE analysis, likely contributes to its thermal stability, which is crucial for applications involving heat-induced gelation, such as plant-based egg omelets, where controlled protein unfolding and refolding are necessary to achieve the desired texture and mouthfeel. The thermal behavior of CPI suggests that it may more effectively mimic the cooking properties of egg whites, which undergo specific thermal transformations to develop the characteristic texture required in omelets.

Emulsifying and foaming properties, and zeta potential

CPI displayed lower EAI and ESI values than CSPI (Table 1), which exhibited the highest values. This variation was likely due to the unique protein composition of CPI, potentially resulting in less stable emulsions at the oil-water interface (Tang, 2017; Zhao et al., 2018). Regarding foaming properties, the CSPI demonstrated a significantly high FC of 150 %, which is beneficial for egg substitutes for specific culinary applications, such as meringues or mousses. In contrast, CPI showed a lower FC value of 56.67 %, but a comparatively higher FS of 71.11 %. The elevated FS of CPI indicates its potential utility in applications in which foam stability is paramount. Thus, while CPI may have a lower emulsifying potential than commercial isolates, its foaming properties, particularly its stability, are promising. Enhancing the foaming characteristics of CPI through protein composition adjustments or addition of suitable additives could broaden its applicability in culinary uses that traditionally depend on the unique properties of egg whites (Zhao et al., 2018).

Significant variations were observed in the zeta potential of the various protein isolates, indicating their diverse physicochemical and functional characteristics, further complementing the abovementioned findings. The zeta potential, which is influenced by elements such as non-polar hydrophobic residues and ionic groups, is crucial for determining protein properties (Tang & Sun, 2011). Our findings reveal that the isolates differed in surface charge, with CMPI (−35.23 mV), CSPI (−34.40 mV), and CPI (−29.57 mV) exhibiting more negative values compared to CPPI (−12.70 mV; Table 1). Interestingly, the zeta potential of CPPI, which was the closest to zero, correlated with its lower EAI, ESI, FC, and FS values. However, the more negatively charged isolates did not exhibit a consistent trend in these properties, suggesting that a complex interplay affects protein behavior.

The combined insights gained based on the emulsifying and foaming properties, along with zeta potential analyses, highlight the need for further research into how variations in the zeta potential, especially around the isoelectric point of CPI, affect CPI functional characteristics. For example, investigating how pH or ionic strength shifts impact the zeta potential and protein-protein interactions could offer valuable insights into optimizing the emulsifying and foaming properties of CPI. Moreover, exploring the potential to enhance these properties by combining CPI with other ingredients, such as gums or stabilizers, may further improve CPI application in products requiring stable foams or emulsions, such as plant-based egg alternatives.

Rheological properties

Temperature sweep characteristic of the protein gel

Heating promoted the aggregation of isolated solutions to form a gel structure. Temperature sweep analysis was performed to observe the heat-induced gelation behavior of the protein isolates to simulate the thermal coagulation mechanism during pan-frying of eggs (Fig. 2). Based on our preliminary experiments, an oscillatory strain of 1 % was adopted within the linear viscoelastic range. Temperature sweep tests revealed distinct gelation characteristics among the isolates. Most protein dispersions transitioned to a gel-like structure during heating, except for CSPI 10 %. Unlike other isolates, CPI exhibited a significant increase in the storage modulus (G') with increasing temperature, indicating a strong heat-set gelation system, probably due to the formation of more cross-links (Chen et al., 2021). Additionally, as the concentration increased, higher G' values were obtained when the temperature was lowered, suggesting that CPI, which exhibits an irreversible thermal gelation profile, has the potential to provide a texture similar to that of cooked egg whites, depending on the control concentration. CSPI and CMPI showed decreasing G' and G" values during heating owing to processes such as polypeptide rearrangement and hydrogen bond breaking, which is consistent with prior findings (Peyrano et al., 2019). CPPI displayed slightly increased parameters with increasing temperature, reflecting their unique gelation systems. In conclusion, CPI exhibits a greater range of protein-protein interactions in response to temperature changes than other proteins and has the potential to form robust gels (Liu et al., 2017).

Variations in storage (G′) and loss (G′′) modulus in response to simulated temperature profiles of protein isolates as a function of concentration. (A) CPI; (B) CSPI; (C) CPPI; (D) CMPI.

The tan δ value determines if a protein behaves more like a liquid or a solid (Table 2). As protein interactions increased, tan δ values decreased, indicating a shift toward a solid-like behavior. Moreover, the tan δ values remained below 0.3 at concentrations of 20 %, indicating the formation of a solid-like gel. CPI consistently showed strong gelation, whereas CSPI started in a liquid-like state at 10 %, and its firmness increased with increasing concentration. Changes in protein components such as vicilin-like and legumin-like globulins influence these behaviors. Previous research confirmed that legumes with more vicilin-like globulins have stiffer gels (Mendoza et al., 2001). The GR value represents the increment in the storage modulus (G') at the conclusion of the cooling phase (25 °C) compared to the termination of the heating phase (95 °C; Table 2). As the protein concentration increased, the GR value displayed an upward trend, with CPI achieving the highest value among all proteins. This aligns with earlier studies indicating that fractions rich in vicilin-like components tend to yield superior gelation structures, as discussed previously (Kimura et al., 2008).

Frequency sweep characteristic of the protein gel

A frequency sweep test was performed to assess the response of the physical gel to different frequencies after heating and cooling (Fig. 3). All protein isolates showed a tendency for their overall viscoelastic properties to increase with increasing concentration, showing solid-like behavior, with G' consistently surpassing G“ across frequencies at a concentration of 20%. In addition, CPI showed the highest G' value at 20%, indicating the most robust elastic properties. However, at a concentration of 10%, only CPI maintained a higher G' than G", suggesting effective gelation even at low concentrations. In contrast, other protein isolates showed less cohesive sol network structures at low concentrations, suggesting that concentration optimization is required to achieve the desired gel strength (Peyrano et al., 2019). The rheological properties of CPI, particularly its robust gelation ability at suitable concentrations, underscore its potential as a viable ingredient for plant-based egg substitutes.

Heat-induced protein gel characterization

Macro/microstructure

Macroscopic and microscopic structures of the protein gels were analyzed to evaluate their potential as egg substitutes (Fig. 4(1)–(2)). Notably, the CPI gels exhibited a solid consistency across all concentrations, resembling the structure of traditional egg gels. In contrast, other isolates demonstrated gelling properties only at higher concentrations, with CPPI forming a weak gel even at 20%. SEM images revealed that gel morphology is influenced by protein composition and structure (Clark et al., 2001). The egg gel displayed a consistent structure, reflective of its oil content, whereas CPI showed a complex gel morphology with a dense, homogeneous structure and minimal porosity, akin to cooked egg whites. In comparison, other protein gels exhibited larger and more porous structures (Chen & Campanella, 2022; Liang et al., 2021).

Visual appearance (1) and SEM images (2) of plant protein and egg gels, along with the visual appearance (3) of omelets made from plant proteins or eggs. (A) Egg; (B) CPI; (C) CSPI; (D) CPPI; (E) CMPI.

Moreover, the correlation between the microstructural characteristics observed in the SEM analysis and the rheological properties identified in the frequency sweep tests provided further insights into the functional potential of CPI as a plant-based egg substitute. The dense and homogeneous microstructure of the CPI gels, which closely mirrors that of cooked egg whites, aligns with the robust elastic properties indicated by the high G' values across various frequencies. This structural integrity at the microscopic level likely contributed to effective gelation, even at low concentrations, as evidenced by the superior viscoelastic behavior of CPI compared to that of the other isolates. Furthermore, the minimal porosity within the CPI gels facilitates a more robust and stable network formation, which is crucial for maintaining structural integrity under dynamic conditions, such as those simulated during frequency sweep tests.

Molecular force

Protein and egg gels, derived from the different protein isolates, underwent evaluation concerning their solubility in three specific solvents (Fig. 5A). SDS, urea, and β-ME solvents are utilized to selectively disrupt hydrophobic, non-covalent, and disulfide bond interactions, respectively, thereby revealing the underlying binding properties that are essential for the structural integrity and functionality of gels (Shrestha et al., 2023). Our findings indicated that all protein gels, including CPI, predominantly exhibited hydrophobic and hydrogen bond interactions, with minimal involvement of disulfide bonds. This pattern is consistent with those reported in previous studies on vicilin-like globulins from legumes, which typically exhibit a scarcity of sulfur-containing amino acids that could lead to fewer disulfide bonds, potentially affecting the heating stability and texture of gels (Zhu et al., 2022).

Molecular interactions in plant protein and egg gels (A) along with the textural profile (B) and specific volume (C) of omelets made from plant proteins or eggs.

Hhydrophobic interactions are critical for stabilizing the structure of protein gels, particularly under thermal stress, by helping proteins associate with more compact structures. The dominance of hydrophobic interactions in CPI gels suggests that they may be particularly resistant to changes in environmental conditions such as temperature and pH. In addition, the prevalence of hydrogen bonding indicates a network of protein interactions that contribute to the gel's viscoelastic properties. These bonds are crucial for the ability of the gel to absorb and retain water, thereby influencing the texture and mouthfeel of plant-based egg substitutes. The robust network formed by hydrogen bonds could explain the superior WHC and textural similarity to egg whites observed in CPI gels. Finally, disulfide bonds showed minimal involvement in CPI and other protein gels. Although disulfide bonds provide structural strength and elasticity to many protein structures, their limited presence indicates that the structural integrity and elasticity of the gel are primarily maintained through other interactions. Furthermore, a similar trend observed in egg gels suggests that the gelling properties of eggs do not rely solely on protein-based interactions.

Gel strength and water holding capacity

The gel strength and WHC of the protein and egg gels were examined (Table 3). Protein gels generally exhibit weaker strength than that of egg gels, primarily due to differences in the molecular composition of plant and animal proteins (Chen & Campanella, 2022). Animal proteins, such as those in eggs, are rich in disulfide bonds and hydrophobic interactions, which contribute to forming strong, cohesive gels. On the contrary, plant proteins, such as those derived from soybeans, peas, and mung beans, rely more on hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions, which are less stable, leading to weaker gel structures. Additionally, plant proteins tend to form larger, less uniform aggregates, which weakens the gel network.

Among the isolates, CPI was the only one that consistently retained its gel structure across all concentrations. However, at a concentration of 20 %, there was no significant difference in the gel strength and WHC between CPI and CSPI, indicating that both proteins have similar functional properties at high concentrations (Shrestha et al., 2023). When the gel structure assumes a solid particle network configuration, the mobility of water droplets is restricted, resulting in a higher WHC, which is consistent with the visual appearance of the gel (Fig. 4(1)). However, it is essential to note that the gel strength of CPI, even at a concentration of 20 %, remained significantly lower than that of egg gels. This highlights a fundamental limitation in the ability of CPI to fully replicate the gelation properties of egg proteins. While the gelation properties and WHC of CPI were similar to those of CSPI, they still comply with some of the requirements of a successful egg substitute, particularly in applications such as plant-based egg alternatives, in which a balance of texture and water retention is crucial.

Physiochemical and sensory properties of plant-based egg omelets

Finally, we evaluated the physicochemical properties of plant-based egg omelets using protein isolates and compared them with those of traditional egg omelets (Fig. 4(3)). The visual assessment revealed that omelets prepared with CPI exhibited a complete and cohesive structure that closely resembled that of traditional egg omelets. In contrast, CMPI showed an omelet shape but a weak gel structure, and the remaining isolates did not successfully mimic the omelet shape. Therefore, further experiments were performed using only omelets made from CPI and CMPI. CPI showed increased hardness but reduced springiness compared to egg omelets (Fig. 5B). This suggests that, although CPI cannot perfectly replicate an egg omelet, it can effectively reproduce its robustness. In addition, the slightly lower springiness of CPI indicates the need for further formulation adjustments to fully match the textural nuances of traditional eggs. CMPI, which formed only a weak gel, exhibited significantly lower hardness and springiness than CPI. These textural discrepancies reflect structural differences in gel properties.

Specific volume measurements provided insights into the structural properties of the omelets (Fig. 5C). Egg omelets had the highest specific volume, followed by CPI and CMPI omelets. This specific volume tendency can be attributed to the differences in porosity and network structure between the egg and CPI gels (Fig. 4(2)). Specific volume measurements indicated that the egg omelet, with a wide interface between its emulsion droplets, had the highest specific volume, and the CPI omelet, which formed a protein gel network with small porosity, had a specific volume relatively close to that of the egg omelet (Ashwini et al., 2009). The ability of CPI to form strong gels with a texture and consistency similar to those of egg whites suggests that it can be effectively used in products for which these properties are crucial. The superior WHC and gel strength observed in CPI are particularly advantageous for enhancing the moisture retention and structural cohesiveness of traditional egg-based dishes.

Therefore, the results indicate that CPI has considerable potential as a plant-based egg substitute, particularly in replicating the structural and textural properties of traditional egg omelets. As the consumer demand for plant-based alternatives continues to grow, CPI could be a key ingredient in the formulation of new plant-based products that meet nutritional and sensory expectations.

The taste characteristics of omelets made from plant proteins and eggs were evaluated in terms of sourness, bitterness, sweetness, umami, and saltiness (Fig. S1). Among the five tastes, sourness and bitterness were higher in CPI, sweetness and saltiness were higher in the egg, and umami was high in the CPI and egg. The increased bitterness in CPI may be attributed to tannins derived from plant-based ingredients (Kobayashi et al., 2010). Additionally, based on the results from the five sensors, the taste characteristics of omelets made with eggs and CMPI were similar, except for umami. However, omelets made with CPI showed significant differences from those made with eggs in terms of sweetness, saltiness, and bitterness. This suggests that the taste characteristics of CMPI are similar to those of eggs, indicating that plant-based ingredients can partially replicate the taste of animal-based ingredients.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed an extraction method for CPI and compared its functional properties with those of commercial protein isolates derived from soybeans, peas, and mung beans. We found that CPI is an effective plant-based egg alternative due to its unique composition and functional characteristics. Vicilin-like globulins in CPI enables the formation of robust gel networks that are essential in replicating the texture and structural integrity of eggs in various culinary applications. CPI exhibits superior gelation capacity, enhanced water retention, and greater thermal stability than its commercial counterparts. Moreover, the smooth heat-induced gelation behavior and microstructure of CPI closely resemble those of egg gels, suggesting that it could effectively mimic the sensory properties of eggs in various applications. While CPI did not fully replicate every aspect of traditional egg omelets, it significantly outperformed commercial protein isolates in terms of textural properties and overall appearance, underscoring its potential as a promising ingredient for developing plant-based egg substitutes. This research contributes to the growing field of alternative proteins by providing a new, viable option to meet consumer demands for sustainable, functional, and versatile plant-based products. Future studies could further optimize CPI properties and explore its application in various food products.
