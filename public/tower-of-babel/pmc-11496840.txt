Intelligent cell images segmentation system: based on SDN and moving transformer
Authors: Jia Wu, Yao Pan, Qing Ye, Jing Zhou, Fangfang Gou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Diagnosing diseases heavily relies on cell pathology images, but the extensive data in each manual identification of relevant cells labor-intensive, especially in regions with a scarcity of qualified healthcare professionals. This study aims to develop an intelligent system to enhance the diagnostic accuracy of cytopathology images by addressing image noise and segmentation issues, thereby improving the efficiency of medical professionals in disease diagnosis. We introduced an innovative system combining a self-supervised algorithm, SDN, for image denoising with data enhancement and image segmentation using the UPerMVit model. The UPerMVit model’s novel attention mechanisms and modular architecture provide higher accuracy and lower computational complexity than traditional methods. The proposed system effectively reduces image noise and accurately segments annotated images, highlighting cellular structures relevant to medical staff. This enhances diagnostic accuracy and aids in the accurate identification of pathological cells. Our intelligent system offers a reliable tool for medical professionals, improving diagnostic efficiency and accuracy in cytopathologic image analysis. It provides significant technical support in regions lacking adequate medical expertise.

Introduction

Over 10 million people die from cancer worldwide each year, with developing countries accounting for more than half of this number, exceeding 80%. The lack of cancer diagnosis in developing countries is a significant reason for this issue. Furthermore, there is a shortage of skilled medical professionals, and the degree of automation in diagnosis is low. According to the World Health Organization’s 2022 report, many countries worldwide are experiencing significant shortages of healthcare workers and medical resources. Developing countries, in particular, are frequently affected by shortages of medical resources. Additionally, the equipment required for cancer diagnosis is costly and resource-intensive1, posing a challenge for hospitals in underdeveloped areas or developing countries to sustain the expenses of such testing.

Traditionally, the diagnosis of various cancers is based on expert medical examination of cell pathology images. However, generating cell pathology images for each patient is expensive, and pixel-level annotation of pathology images is prohibitively costly. For instance, the TCGA Kumar dataset only contains 30 high-definition cell pathology images at 40x magnification, yet more than 20,000 cell nuclei were annotated.

Recently, with the rapid advancement of deep learning and computer vision technologies, substantial strides have been achieved in the domains of object detection2and image segmentation3. Complex cellular pathology images pose various impediments for conventional segmentation tasks, including problems in determining segmentation boundaries and feature extraction. Currently, deep learning and computer vision-aided image processing methods, more specifically semantic segmentation techniques, have become commonly utilized for diagnosing cytopathology images in clinical settings4. This technology enables accurate image processing, automatic cell classification and segmentation, and can provide specialized medical practitioners with high-precision image components. However, the current technology has not made significant progress in dealing with a vast amount of high-precision cytopathology images. Difficulties in diagnosing various cancers still exist in developing countries due to the scarcity of medical resources and the dearth of experienced medical practitioners5.

To address these critical issues in the field of digital pathology images, this study clearly focuses on the following research questions: How can advanced deep learning techniques and computer vision algorithms be effectively utilized to improve the classification and segmentation of cells in digital pathology images? What are the specific challenges posed by noise and data insufficiency in this context, and how can they be mitigated? This study employs advanced deep learning techniques and computer vision algorithms, focusing on semantic segmentation, to precisely classify and segment cells in digital pathology images. What sets this study apart is its groundbreaking use of state-of-the-art semantic segmentation techniques in the cellular pathology image analysis arena. By employing the proposed computer vision technique, this study achieves self-supervised image denoising and high-precision, low-complexity cell segmentation, providing crucial image components for clinical analysis. This novel approach enhances the proficiency of skilled medical professionals and presents a promising response to the predicament of cancer diagnosis owing to scarce medical resources and a shortage of experienced physicians.

In recent years, the use of Transformers in artificial intelligence has advanced significantly, outperforming traditional CNN-based designs in many areas and garnering significant attention. Transformer-based designs have also become increasingly prevalent in image segmentation6,7. However, it is important to note that for any design based on a Vision Transformer, the required space and computational complexity increase quadratically with the number of tokens. The high pixel density of cell pathology images8results in a quadratic increase in computation complexity and memory requirements, making it challenging to use Vision Transformers for cell pathology image segmentation. Furthermore, the presence of various types of noise in practical applications can impact the effectiveness of image segmentation. Median filtering9and Gaussian filtering10are often insufficient for removing these noises. New neural network-based denoising algorithms can solve these problems, while more complex methods like the BM3D algorithm11are not suitable for real-time processing. However, existing denoising networks are mostly fully supervised12or semi-supervised13,14denoising networks, which require training on a dataset consisting of a copious amount of clean and noisy paired images15. This is challenging in medical applications, where acquiring a large collection of cell pathology images is difficult. Additionally, capturing numerous autonomous, cacophonous images within a singular setting is arduous. Additionally, machine learning methods that incorporate simulated noise for training purposes often demonstrate inferior performance when presented with real-world data.

We propose UPerMVit, a Transformer-based approach that employs the Moving Transformer layer in place of the conventional Transformer layer. This modification substantially decreases the computational complexity and space need for handling high-density pathology images, while still achieving exceptional segmentation outcomes. Consequently, the computational expenses are lowered, and it particularly supports easing diagnostic stress in regions with limited medical resources. To tackle the issue of limited cellular images, we utilize Albumentations to enhance the data by applying various techniques such as scaling, left-right rotation, and flipping the cellular images16. This enhances the variety of training data. To address the challenge of insufficient segmentation caused by noise present in cellular pathology images, we introduce a self-supervised deep learning denoising method known as SDN (Self-supervised Denoising of Noisy Images). By sampling a single noisy image twice and constructing a loss function to eliminate the noise from the source image, we were able to successfully address this problem. SDN generates denoised images that are sampled twice and employs regularization to overcome the challenge of denoising in the absence of sufficient cytopathology images.

Overall, our research successfully tackles the challenges presented by the computational complexity, data insufficiency, and noise issues in the segmentation of high-density pathology images. This is achieved through the implementation of a Moving Transformer layer, Albumentations data enhancement, and SDN denoising methods. Our study makes the following key contributions:

Related work

Quantitative analysis in digital pathology is a crucial tool for cancer diagnosis and quantitative analysis. Numerous experts and scholars have made significant contributions to the field of quantitative pathology analysis and digital pathology data17–20, greatly advancing the global development and application of digital pathology. However, there remain significant research gaps in effectively addressing noise removal and precise segmentation in high-density pathological images, especially in resource-limited environments.

In recent years, digital pathology has witnessed the advent of whole-slide imaging (WSI) technology21, which converts pathological tissue slides into high-resolution digital images known as whole-slide images. These images contain vast amounts of information, making them well-suited for implementing deep learning techniques in the pathology domain. Despite these advancements, the segmentation of digital slides during the diagnostic and analytical processes in pathology remains a critical challenge. The complex arrangement of contextual information in pathological images, densely distributed tissues, adhesion, and overlap complicate the accurate segmentation of pathological images. Moreover, noise generated during image acquisition and external factors can interfere with the presentation of internal details, impacting image quality. Removing image noise is another challenge that requires further exploration.

Osteosarcoma, as the most common malignant bone tumor, exhibits high malignancy and a high misdiagnosis rate. Pathological images play a crucial role in its accurate diagnosis. However, in underdeveloped regions, the shortage of pathological manpower is widespread, leading to lower accuracy and efficiency in osteosarcoma diagnosis. Multiple studies22,23 have shown that these issues can be partially addressed through data augmentation, denoising, and improved image segmentation models, yet there is still a need for innovative solutions that are efficient and feasible in resource-constrained settings.

Many researchers have extensively studied the issue of image noise. However, traditional denoising techniques such as wavelet transform24, Gaussian filtering, and median filtering show limited denoising effectiveness. More effective methods like BM3D require excessive computational resources, making them unsuitable for real-time applications. Recently, deep learning-based image denoising algorithms have become popular and have been used as a method to address this issue25. Compared to traditional image denoising techniques, deep learning-based denoising techniques have stronger learning capabilities, can adapt to complex noise distributions, and save computational time. Although neural network-based denoising algorithms such as DnCNN-B26and CBDNet27 have achieved outstanding results, they are supervised learning algorithms that require large datasets, which are difficult to obtain in medical applications.

Therefore, there is a growing interest in self-supervised learning research in this domain. Algorithms like N2N28, N2V29, and VDN30have shown potential, but challenges remain in achieving effective denoising without the need for large paired datasets. Additionally, training with artificially added simulated noise often performs poorly on real data, as they lack generalization capability31. Our proposed SDN algorithm optimizes the traditional N2N algorithm by using a sampler for two independent samplings on the pathological image, ensuring denoising effectiveness without requiring original and noisy image pairs or artificially added noise. However, further research is needed to balance denoising effectiveness and computational efficiency32.

Image data augmentation techniques are a key technology in deep learning, improving model robustness and generalization capabilities33. Common data augmentation methods include image rotation, translation, cropping, flipping, brightness enhancement, and saturation enhancement. These techniques are widely adopted in deep learning model training and have been shown to significantly improve model performance34. Some studies have demonstrated that using image translation and rotation augmentation techniques can significantly enhance the effectiveness of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) in handwritten digit recognition tasks35,36. Additionally, better data augmentation methods such as image stretching and affine transformations have been proposed, further improving segmentation model efficiency. Zhang et al. introduced a new data augmentation technique called Mix-up, which generates new training samples by randomly linearly combining the inputs and labels of different samples, effectively reducing overfitting and improving generalization performance37. Other data augmentation techniques have also been suggested and proven to enhance the performance of deep learning models. For instance, using image flipping data augmentation has been shown to improve CNN training and generalization performance. Furthermore, applying automated augmentation techniques can further enhance the performance of deep reinforcement learning38. There remain prominent issues in further researching how to effectively leverage these advanced augmentation strategies in pathological image segmentation.

In recent years, deep learning-based methods have made significant progress in pathological image segmentation. This is due to the limited feature expression capabilities of traditional methods, which hinder their analysis performance in pathological image segmentation. In contrast, deep learning algorithms based on artificial neural networks have stronger feature extraction capabilities on large pathological datasets. Currently, research on deep learning-based pathological image instance segmentation39–41 mainly focuses on improving network structures to acquire more meaningful contextual information, introducing auxiliary output layers in the network, and organically combining classical modeling theories with deep feature representation learning mechanisms.

First, a CNN-based method for pathological image segmentation is introduced. This method constructs a CNN network model that achieves target localization and segmentation by obtaining the maximum probability pixel map through layer-by-layer training and prediction. This method has been widely applied in areas such as renal cell carcinoma, colorectal cancer, and osteosarcoma42. Global researchers have optimized image detection and segmentation based on CNN. However, due to high complexity and noise interference, existing methods still face issues in balancing segmentation accuracy and computational complexity. This highlights the problem of performance instability when processing high-complexity or noisy images.

Meanwhile encoder-decoder models have been widely applied in computer vision and have introduced several representative models with good performance, such as U-Net43. The main idea of U-Net is to capture detailed features through the compression path and achieve precise localization through the expansion path, effectively addressing the segmentation challenge of complex neural structures. U-Net++44addresses the challenge of unknown network depth by effectively combining skip connections of different depths in U-Net. The PSPNet network45 further reduces contextual information loss between different subregions by creating a hierarchical universal prior framework, effectively completing unified perception and analysis tasks. UPerNet uses the pyramid pooling module from PSPNet in the last layer of the backbone network to achieve collaborative reasoning and extract rich visual information from the image.

In recent years, the VIT method has achieved satisfactory results in computer vision and medical image segmentation. This method combines Transformer with convolutions46,47, especially in small sample data studies, which can assist doctors in preliminary segmentation, thereby reducing the workload48,49. However, we have found that when performing cell pathology image segmentation, the required spatial and computational complexity remains high, making it difficult to balance accuracy and complexity. Performance is often unstable when dealing with high-complexity or noisy images50. We believe that current methods are not suitable for addressing the automatic diagnosis problem in regions with limited medical resources.

System model design

The continuous advancement of medical and computational disciplines has led to an increasingly significant role of computer technology in addressing complex medical conditions. Nevertheless, the unequal allocation of medical resources remains an issue of major concern, particularly in the area of automated cancer diagnosis in developing nations.

The practice of cancer diagnosis has traditionally relied heavily on the manual scrutiny of cytopathologic images by qualified medical professionals. The vast quantity of cytopathology images generated by every patient, coupled with the substantial amount of information embedded into each image, renders the manual recognition and diagnosis of these images by physicians highly demanding.

Therefore, we aim to use computer vision techniques to screen important information for doctors, reducing their workload and enhancing diagnostic efficiency. This has significant implications for the automated diagnosis of cancer in developing countries. The SDN denoising algorithm is used initially to remove common noise in cellular pathological images, such as uneven staining, non-uniform illumination, and polarization noise. This improves the image segmentation effect and visual quality. In the development of the SDN-Moving Transformer system, several significant challenges were encountered, particularly in the integration of self-supervised learning and segmentation algorithms. Firstly, the key to self-supervised learning lies in effectively extracting and utilizing the features from unlabeled data, a process that becomes particularly challenging in pathological images due to noise and complex cellular structures. Secondly, designing a model architecture that effectively combines self-supervised learning with segmentation tasks poses a major challenge, necessitating the model to adequately capture cellular features while maintaining strong segmentation performance. Additionally, the computational complexity of the model is a crucial consideration; reducing time and space overhead, while ensuring accuracy, is key to making the system applicable in real clinical scenarios. Therefore, addressing these challenges is essential for ensuring the effectiveness and applicability of the SDN-Moving Transformer system. Diverse data augmentation methodologies are then employed to generate a large volume of training data from the acquired clean image collection. Finally, the UPerMVit image segmentation network is used to process these datasets.

Image denoising module

Medical imaging is an important technological tool for assisting doctors in diagnosis and treatment. However, certain modalities of medical imaging, such as electronic computed tomography and single-photon emission computed tomography, expose patients to significant amounts of radiation, which can affect patient safety. Low-dose imaging technology can reduce radiation dose to 0.61–1.50 mSv51, which is well below the safe standard for radiological detection set by the American Association of Medical Physicists. The standard requires a single examination dose below 50 mSv and a cumulative dose below 100 mSv for multiple short-term accumulations. However, the use of low-dose imaging technology can affect image quality and often results in significant noise during image acquisition. This can be caused by inconsistent staining depth, background spatter interference, and uneven staining. These factors not only reduce image quality but also affect the efficacy of image segmentation.

Obtaining a large number of cancer cell pathological images can be challenging, especially when trying to obtain images that contain both noise and clean areas. To address this issue, we propose a self-supervised denoising algorithm called SDN. Our experiments have shown that this algorithm significantly reduces the impact of noise on image segmentation and improves the clarity of denoised images.

Existing methods such as Noise2void29, Noise2self52, and Noise2noise28 have proposed various theories. The self-supervised denoising algorithm SDN proposed in this paper is based on these theoretical works. Among them, Noise2Noise is an approach to denoising images that does not require clean data. This method has effectively resolved the challenge of training with numerous pairs of noisy and pristine images that are difficult to obtain. However, this method still has limitations. It requires collecting multiple images with independent noise in the same scene, which is difficult to implement in our problem background. Therefore, we aim to optimize this algorithm to make it more versatile. Our improved version can train the model and complete denoising with just one medical image containing noise.

Based on Fig. 3, we can observe that the subsampler S initially partitions an image into regions, where the case of k = 2 is considered. In selecting the subsampling parameter k, we tested values of k = 2, 3, 4 and choose k = 2 to balance image detail preservation and noise sensitivity. Fig. 3The operating principle of Subsampler.

The operating principle of Subsampler.

The image is partitioned into non-overlapping regions, for instance, an 8 × 8 image is divided into sixteen 2 × 2 sub-images. This division is crucial for maintaining the granularity of the denoising process. In each small sub-image, two adjacent pixels are randomly selected. These pixels are marked distinctively—this marking dictates the sampling method described subsequently. The marked sub-images are then used to generate two separate subsampled images. One is derived from the sub-images marked with the first designation (e.g., yellow), while the other from sub-images marked with the second designation (e.g., purple). This step ensures that the critical details within each small region are preserved and contributes to the overall robustness against noise proliferation.

In the context of our study, we address the scarcity of large, well-annotated datasets of cancer cell pathology images, particularly those labeled by authoritative physicians, in developing countries. To mitigate this limitation, we implement a robust data augmentation module designed to enhance dataset size and variation. This approach compensates for the lack of high-quality annotations and addresses the difficulties in the exchange and digitization of medical imaging data in these regions53. The preprocessing and data augmentation stages aim to enhance the quantity and diversity of the training set, thereby reducing the risk of overfitting caused by insufficient data and ensuring the model’s generalizability.

The operations involved in this study include image rotation, translation, cropping, flipping, brightness enhancement, and saturation enhancement. Each image in the dataset undergoes a sequence of these augmentation techniques, significantly increasing the effective size and diversity of the data. Data augmentation plays a crucial role in generating annotated images, especially when large, high-quality annotated datasets are scarce. By applying various techniques such as image rotation, translation, cropping, flipping, brightness enhancement, and saturation enhancement, the data augmentation module substantially increases the quantity and diversity of the training set. This method effectively reduces the risk of overfitting due to insufficient data and enhances the model’s generalization capability. By expanding the dataset’s size and diversity, these techniques improve the accuracy of cellular structure segmentation, making the model more robust when faced with diverse and challenging input data.

The integration of these six methodologies enables us to partially mitigate the limitations imposed by the lack of well-annotated, large-scale cancer cell datasets. These modifications not only enhance the segmentation capability of our model but also improve its robustness when faced with diverse and challenging data inputs.

Cell pathology image segmentation model

We employed the UPerMVit model for cell pathology image segmentation, which integrates the UperNet45with our Moving Transformer networks. The model architecture is inspired by UperNet45, Feature Pyramid Network (FPN)54, and incorporates the ParC structure, enabling our model to surpass UperNet in terms of performance while demanding lower computational and space requirements. This is particularly beneficial for medical-assisted treatments in developing countries, where reduced computational costs can lead to better segmentation results for high-definition pathology images. The comprehensive configuration of our model is illustrated in Fig. 1, specifically in the image segmentation section. Below, we delve into the structures and functions of each component of the model.

The In the segmentation of cell pathology images model’s modular architecture facilitates the segmentation process by breaking down the image segmentation task into several independent and interchangeable modules. Each module focuses on specific tasks such as feature extraction, context modeling, and decision layer integration. This design enables the model to handle different types of medical images more effectively and allows adjustments and optimizations to be made based on specific application requirements. Additionally, UPerMVit incorporates multi-scale feature fusion and self-attention mechanisms to capture rich contextual information, thereby enhancing segmentation accuracy and robustness.

Compared to other transformer-based models used in medical image analysis, UPerMVit demonstrates superior performance in dealing with complex pathological images. Traditional models often rely on fixed feature extraction methods, making it challenging to adapt to the diverse characteristics of medical images. In contrast, UPerMVit’s modular design permits dynamic adjustment of processing strategies based on the characteristics of the input data. This enables UPerMVit to achieve higher segmentation precision and better adaptability in tasks such as cell segmentation and lesion detection, especially when handling challenging image data.

We propose an innovative attention mechanism termed Moving Attention, which diverges from the conventional Self Attention mechanism. This novel mechanism ensures a more flexible and efficient allocation of attention. In Moving Attention, each pixel’s attention can move to k neighboring pixels, whereas, in Self Attention, attention spans all pixels.

The UPerMVit model integrates a novel attention mechanism known as Moving Attention, which enhances image segmentation accuracy and reduces computational complexity. Unlike traditional Self Attention that considers all pixels, Moving Attention allows each pixel to focus on k neighboring pixels, significantly reducing the attention scope. This localized attention mechanism is more efficient, as it limits the computational overhead by narrowing the focus to nearby pixels, thereby conserving resources and improving processing speed. The model also employs a series of 3 × 3 overlapping convolutions for downsampling, which introduces bias induction and enhances segmentation performance. This approach, inspired by effective CNN architectures, leads to improved efficiency over traditional methods. The combination of these techniques allows UPerMVit to deliver superior segmentation results with lower computational and space requirements, making it particularly suitable for medical applications in resource-constrained environments.

The cross-entropy loss function55 is commonly used to address classification issues when the number of samples per class is roughly equal. However, in imbalanced classification problems, its use may cause the model to favor predicting the more frequent classes and ignore the less frequent ones. In cell pathology image segmentation, the cells only occupy a small portion of the image. Therefore, we use the Lovász-SoftMax Loss function as it is better suited for imbalanced classification problems.

Lovász-SoftMax Loss56 is a loss function designed for imbalanced classification problems, with the advantage of helping the model better handle the imbalance between classes. Unlike traditional cross-entropy loss functions, Lovász-SoftMax Loss can measure the difference between predicted results and true labels by comparing their relative position instead of directly comparing probability distributions. Specifically, Lovász-SoftMax Loss is a continuously differentiable convex function that imposes smaller penalties on correctly classified samples and larger penalties on misclassified samples, thus improving the accuracy of classifying a small number of samples.

Therefore, in cell pathology image segmentation, the Lovász-SoftMax Loss can better handle class imbalance issues and outperform traditional cross-entropy by better distinguishing between positive and negative samples, thereby improving the model’s classification performance. Additionally, Lovász-SoftMax Loss not only considers the sample’s classification label but also considers the relative position of the predicted value, making it more accurate in capturing cell boundary information, thus improving cell segmentation accuracy. The Lovász-SoftMax Loss possesses properties that enhance robustness in managing noisy or irregular segmentation outcomes. It reduces sensitivity to noise by ordering and computing prediction scores, resulting in a minimal effect on noise or outliers. Consequently, it better deals with the challenge of a sizeable volume of intricate cytopathology images that exhibit high noise levels. Finally, compared to cross-entropy loss functions, Lovász-SoftMax Loss has smoother gradients, regularization with this loss function facilitates more stable model training, reduces overfitting, and enhances the model’s generalization performance. This is especially beneficial for tasks such as cellular image segmentation, which involve small sample sizes and datasets containing noise and uncertainty. Experiments and results

Experimental environment and dataset introduction

Dataset: The dataset used in this paper is from the Hunan University of Medicine General Hospital, consisting of 1000 pathological images of cancer cells. We cropped 10 regions from each original image at a magnification of 40 times. After filtering out invalid data, we obtained 2146 usable data as the dataset for this experiment. Our training-test set was divided into 1700 − 464. After chromatic normalization, the size of the images is 2048*2048.

Noise dataset: We found that there are many cell pathology images with uneven staining and uneven lighting in our dataset. Therefore, we selected 300 images with poor coloring effect and visual effect in the dataset as the dataset for noise reduction experiments. To prove the robustness of our segmentation model, we further conducted data augmentation on the noise reduction experimental dataset. We processed the selected images with poor coloring effects, added random chromatic and luminance noise and Gaussian noise.

Evaluation Metrics: To verify the performance of the SDN-Moving Transformer system, we used several evaluation metrics, focusing on key indicators that reflect its effectiveness in segmenting cell pathology images. We employed six evaluation metrics: Accuracy, Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC), Intersection over Union (IoU), Precision, Recall, and F1-score. Accuracy measures the percentage of correctly predicted pixels out of the total pixels, offering a basic correctness overview. The Dice Similarity Coefficient and IoU assess the overlap between the predicted and actual segmentations, which is crucial in handling unbalanced category segmentation where background pixels may dominate. DSC is particularly informative in settings with skewed class distributions. Precision and Recall are critical in ensuring the model correctly identifies lesions and minimizes false negatives. The F1-score balances precision and recall, providing a comprehensive performance measure. For noise evaluation, both subjective and objective methods were utilized. Objectively, we used Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) to quantify image quality improvements over other self-supervised models, while subjectively, evaluators compared images visually before and after denoising.

Experimental details: For our experiments, we trained our model on a computer equipped with an NVIDIA 4090 GPU. The operating system used was Windows 11, and we employed PyTorch 2.2.1 with Python version 3.9 as our deep learning framework. Firstly, we preprocessed the data, including image denoising and data augmentation. The self-supervised image denoising algorithm SDN has been described in detail in the previous section. The data augmentation we performed included image rotation, translation, cropping, flipping, brightness enhancement, and saturation enhancement. We set 500 epochs per training session. For the backbone of our network, i.e., Moving Transformer, we set the loss function to Lovász-SoftMax Loss, the value of k for MOV to 2. In order to ameliorate the efficacy of the model, we also conducted pre-training on the PanNuKe dataset. In the denoising experiment, we set 80 epochs per training session, the initial value of gamma as 2, and the initial value of learning rate (LR) as 0.0005. The parameter settings in our experiments were chosen based on empirical evidence and best practices in the field of deep learning and image processing. The initial learning rate (LR) of 0.0005 for the denoising experiment was selected to ensure a stable and gradual convergence during training, preventing the model from making large, unstable updates that could hinder the learning process. The value of gamma set to 2 was chosen to balance the influence of different loss components in the SDN algorithm, optimizing the denoising performance. For the Moving Transformer, the loss function was set to Lovász-SoftMax Loss due to its effectiveness in handling class imbalance and improving segmentation accuracy. The value of k for MOV was set to 2 to capture sufficient temporal information without overcomplicating the model. The batch size of 16, an initial learning rate of 0.001, momentum of 0.9, and decay rate of 0.0001 were chosen based on standard practices that offer a good trade-off between convergence speed and model stability. The use of Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) as the optimizer is a common choice for its robustness and efficiency in training deep neural networks. The time node K set to 3 was determined to capture adequate temporal dynamics for the Moving Transformer. Finally, Conditional Random Fields (CRF) were used for post-processing to refine the segmentation results, as they are effective in reducing noise and improving the overall accuracy of the model outputs.

In medical pathology image segmentation, due to the relatively low proportion of positive samples (such as cancerous regions), traditional ROC may not accurately reflect model performance. Specifically, the False Positive Rate (FPR) in ROC is not sensitive to changes in imbalanced datasets, potentially leading to an overestimation of model performance. Consequently, this study opts to use the Precision-Recall (PR) point for evaluation. The PR point focuses on the performance concerning positive samples, providing a more intuitive representation of the model’s precision and recall across different thresholds. This advantage makes PR points particularly suitable for imbalanced datasets, offering a more accurate assessment of the model’s ability to identify positive samples.

Conclusion

This study proposes an intelligent system for denoising and segmenting cell pathology images, comprising three key modules: denoising, data augmentation, and segmentation. We employ a Self-supervised Denoising of Noisy Images (SDN) algorithm to effectively reduce noise in the input images. Subsequently, strategic data augmentation is performed, followed by the application of the UPerMVit model to segment the denoised images. Our empirical results demonstrate that our method not only achieves superior segmentation performance compared to traditional methods but also excels in handling cell images with various interference classifications. This significantly aids medical experts in identifying cancerous conditions and enhances diagnostic efficiency. Our method’s robustness in processing diverse and complex cytopathological images positions it as a pioneering model for cancer diagnosis, offering substantial advantages in both typical and complex medical imaging scenarios. This advancement improves the efficiency of medical professionals and opens new possibilities for rapid and accurate cancer diagnosis.

Our system offers several practical advantages. Firstly, it significantly reduces the dependency on annotated data by leveraging self-supervised learning and robust data augmentation techniques, which is crucial in the resource-constrained field of medical image analysis. Secondly, the optimized segmentation model UPerMVit demonstrates exceptional performance even under challenging conditions, ensuring high-resolution accuracy and reliable segmentation outcomes. These attributes collectively contribute to a faster and more accurate diagnostic process, ultimately benefiting patient care.

Despite these advantages, the system has certain limitations. In highly complex multicellular images, the model occasionally fails to accurately segment all cell regions, indicating the need for further improvements in segmentation precision and robustness under such challenging conditions. Additionally, although significant strides have been made in reducing computational complexity, there is still a need to enhance processing speed and resource utilization efficiency, particularly for high-resolution images. Our future research directions are improving segmentation accuracy by adopting more advanced deep learning techniques and optimization algorithms. Implementing advanced semi-supervised algorithms may also address challenges related to difficult data labeling. Furthermore, extensive collaboration with clinical pathology experts will be pursued to gather real-world feedback, refining the model’s performance in medical image analysis to ensure its practical applicability and effectiveness in diverse clinical settings.
