Kindness has a future in health care
Authors: Victor M. Montori, Dominique Allwood, Leonard L. Berry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Kindness can seem out of place in contemporary health care systems often shaped by efficiency, industrial logic, and digital surveillance. Yet kindness – understood not as sentiment but as a deliberate practice of kinship expressed in action – is essential to care, the core mission of healthcare. We argue that careful care (individualised, evidence-based, responsive) must be inseparable from kind care (care offered as if to one’s kin, respectful of each person’s time, energy and attention). The convergence of hyper-clinical fragmentation and hyper-technological optimisation risks producing technically competent but emotionally barren systems that reduce patients to data and clinicians to compliance managers. This erosion of relational practice contributes to moral injury, inequity, and loss of trust. Kindness requires structural conditions – time, continuity, supportive leadership – and must be cultivated intentionally at every health system level. A forward-facing model of careful and kind care would treat unhurried, co-creative, relational conversations as essential work. In an era drifting toward artificial care, kindness is not nostalgic; it is a strategic and radical re-imagining of health care’s future.

Kindness can feel strangely out of place on the front lines of health care, with patients seen by clinicians in a setting often so hurried and transactional that even the word ʻcare’ seems anomalous. Too often in daily practice, delivering care embedded in kindness can seem incompatible with the serious business of providing technically complex, highly consequential health services to patients correctly and efficiently – services that must be itemised, coded, and optimised. But in the quest for efficient, high-tech speed, the deliberative, high-touch care that anxious, often frightened patients require and deserve can be viewed as waste. Kindness then withers.

In health care, kindness is best understood not as a feeling, but as a practice. It is kinship expressed in action: a deliberate response toward someone whose life touches ours as clinicians, a responsible engagement with our shared vulnerability. Kindness is more than empathy, compassion, emotional perception and resonance. It is behaviour that aims to help others flourish.1 It is voluntary, forthcoming not from a job description but from our inner being.

To foster healing, even when cure and remission are not possible, takes two intertwined forms of care: Careful care is individualised, evidence-based, and responsive to each person’s clinical situation; kind care is what professionals would offer to their ‘kin’. It respects the time, energy, and attention of patients and health professionals. It spares them the cruelty of professional or institutional indifference.2

As health systems necessarily focus on efficiency and value – on achieving financial balance, making a margin above their costs to grow, innovate, compete for staff, and keep the doors open – an industrialised mindset can start to predominate. But treating a patient is not an industrialised endeavour. Failing to recognise that fact risks moral injury to health professionals and to their patients as care becomes less personalised and more undifferentiated.3 Service delivery metrics, adapted often smartly from management best practices, may get met – but are they the right or only metrics to use? A mountain of evidence now clearly documents the hazards of operating a health care organisation primarily as a business-focused entity.4

The kindness deficit in health care, when it occurs, is usually not accidental. Other forces have likely crowded it out. A hyper-clinical view has fragmented patients into their symptoms, dysfunctions, physiologic data, probabilities, and risk scores, with scientific detachment celebrated as clarity and professional identity built around distance, mastery, certainty, and control. In contrast, subjective perspectives may be experienced as disruptions or interference; ambiguity may be viewed as inadequate investigation, and relational judgement as indulgence. In this context, kindness occupies a low tier in the value hierarchy.5 Industrialised medicine may work well when people present with a condition that has a known cause and treatment, needing care that interrupts life only briefly. However, it is rarely adequate when people have unclear, confused, ongoing forms of illness and distress, conditions that permeate daily living, requiring treatment that accounts for each person’s peculiarities, practicalities, and priorities.

A parallel set of forces, from the technology sector, has heightened the challenge. It’s one that distils lives to data streams, dashboards, automation, and remote behavioural manipulation. It commoditises human experience in the form of data and analytics. This can have the effect of potentially disempowering clinicians from fully harnessing their core values, their humanness, while, paradoxically, claiming that the data empowers the people from whom it is gleaned.6 If these forces are allowed to function predominantly as surveillance,7 this can reduce patients to behavioural profiles and convert clinicians into data curators and overseers of digital compliance. In a system that becomes governed by algorithms, treating human variation as a data point relative to norms, risks casting interpersonal dynamics as largely irrelevant.

The confluence of the hyper-clinical and the hyper-technological, despite their undeniable merits, is impacting health care, leaving kindness undervalued and under practiced as a future of artificial care emerges. In that future, patients risk becoming data clouds, clinicians risk being reduced to algorithmic functions; clinical encounters increasingly seem unnecessary or inefficient; and care is replaced by technical, touchless, falsely objective systems that process people in assembly-line fashion. That future looks technically competent while being emotionally barren, fast but indifferent, measurable but hollow. Yet an alternative future is possible – one that places kindness at the centre of health care.

Kindness involves stepping into relationships with openness to its consequences – uncertainty, risk, creativity. It intertwines clinicians’ well-rounded perspectives with patients’ interests in ways that no protocol or algorithm can fully capture. It is a form of courage that manifests as a responsive presence. It requires a clinician who listens, encourages with gentle honesty, steadies; who cares for and about each patient; who seeks to understand with curiosity not only what is going on but also what could be done about it8; who sees the patient as kin whose life deserves careful attention and response, even – or especially – when extra effort is essential to responding well.9 This may manifest in many ways such as shifting, from focusing solely on a ‘What’s the matter with you’ encounter to one that also includes ‘what matters to you.’10

At the system level, health care is kind when it enables clinicians and their health systems to attend to the burdens of illness and treatment and when it endeavours to mitigate those burdens as much as possible.9, 11 People in the midst of illness often feel unsettled, mistrustful, unanchored. Kindness helps build the trust that makes the work of recognition, connection, and cooperation possible.12 Without it, care collapses into processing and compliance.

Kindness requires certain external conditions: time to listen, continuity that lets relationships take shape, stability that permits attunement.9, 13 These conditions also protect clinicians from the moral injury that arises when the ambiguity and uncertainty inherent in people’s situations must be met with oversimplified answers, rushed decisions, or protocols that leave too little room for judgement. Older adults, people with chronic or mental health conditions, and anyone who struggles to fit the system’s templates often require care especially tailored to meet their needs and activate their full participation.14 But the system’s templates do not fail people evenly. Race, gender, class, disability, migration status, and social exclusion shape who is believed, who is blamed, who is hurried, who gets to participate in care, who is made to feel out of place. When patients encounter speed, fragmentation, suspicion, and rigid transitions choreographed to optimise efficiency, those who question, challenge gaps in care or struggle to navigate the system may be labelled as ʻcomplex,’ a polite fiction that often means ʻinconvenient.’ These labels, and others including ʻnonadherent’ and ʻdifficult’, can obscure a deeper structural unkindness: the everyday ways in which racism, economic inequality, sexism, ableism, and exclusion enter the clinical encounter. These patients become unwelcome presences in a machine that was not designed to serve them well. It is an unspoken, but persistent and cruel, form of discrimination. Kindness, therefore, must mean refusing to let structural injustice be translated into personal blame or indifference, making room for the person’s reality in the work of care, and organising health care so that those most burdened by illness and inequality are not asked to also bear the burden of fitting the system, enduring its cruelty, and exhausting their capacity in self-advocacy.

Leadership suffers a similar distortion. Health care leaders are pulled into a universe of dashboards, inspections, and governance demands that separate them from the care task.2, 15 Their energy is consumed by systems management rather than human engagement. In a context where resources feel increasingly scarce, leaders’ gaze is often myopic, prioritising short term productivity gains, such as increasing throughput, at the expense of careful and kind practices. Yet the two are not mutually exclusive. Feeling known by a caregiver through continuity can reduce duplicative testing and preventable acute care use. But this requires a different strategic allocation of resources and leaders willing to prioritise such factors, where gains may not show up immediately in dashboards and accounts of institutions.

Early-career clinicians may be left without guidance while trying to carry the emotional and relational burdens of care.16 Structurally unsupported, kindness becomes accidental – a byproduct of individual temperament rather than a shared endeavour. Without active cultivation, it disappears at scale.9, 17 Leadership starts with kindness: listening deeply, being respectful, communicating hard news truthfully but gently, caring for others’ well-being and investing time, effort, and money in enhancing it. Health care marked by hierarchy, paternalism, and exclusion cannot be our model. Kindness in leaders is not weakness; it is strength.

The task is to invent a form of care that, for now, certainly exists but is increasingly endangered and at risk of extinction: care based on solidarity, cooperation, and the deliberate practice of kindness from the back office to the front lines.

Such a future will not be brought about by hyper-clinical and hyper-technological forces that are blind to human complexity while purporting to capture it. Instead, a new vision for careful and kind care for all begins where people live complicated, frightened, hopeful lives.3 It takes shape in the unhurried conversations where clinicians and patients think together, imagine together, co-craft responses that fit the reality of the moment.11, 18 These productive conversations are not remnants of an older medicine; they are the building blocks of a newer one that has never fully existed.

A forward-facing health care system – one oriented toward careful and kind care19 – would treat these unhurried conversations not as inefficiencies but as essential. It would value relational intelligence alongside technical skill.17 It would cultivate leaders who nurture cultures of cooperation and flourishing, who see kindness not as softness but as strategic courage, and the work of care as one of constant regeneration, where kindness begets kindness.15

Kindness, understood in this way, is not a revival but an invention. It challenges the seemingly inevitable future of industrialised, artificial health care. It opens a path toward an alternative future. In that future, care gains meaning less from measurement or control than from human connection and the ability to make a difference in one another’s lives. Kindness is radical. And in an age hurtling toward artificial care, kindness offers health care not as its past, but as its possibility.
