Function of follicular helper 17 T cells in driving B cells for anti-DNA autoantibody production in patients with SLE
Authors: Tipanan Khunsri, Pachara Tianpothong, Piyawan Kochayoo, Pongsakorn Thawornpan, Thanitta Suangtamai, Chaniya Leepiyasakulchai, Pintip Ngamjanyaporn, Prapaporn Pisitkun, Patchanee Chootong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFollicular helper T (TFH) cells play a crucial role in the dysregulated antibody responses in SLE. Circulating TFH17 (cTFH17) subsets are elevated in active lupus, and their frequency is associated with increased disease activity and autoantibody levels, indicating their potential role in disease development by promoting B-cell and autoantibody production. However, how activated cTFH17 cells drive B cell dysregulation for autoantibody production in SLE remains incompletely understood.ObjectiveTo investigate the phenotypic function of interleukin (IL)-17A-producing cTFH cells in subjects with SLE, explore their association with clinical parameters and inflammatory markers and demonstrate potential contributions to B cell-mediated autoantibody productionMethodsForty subjects with SLE were enrolled to characterise and phenotype cTFH or circulating peripheral helper T cell subsets, based on molecule surface expression and intracellular cytokine production by flow cytometry. The correlations of cTFH17 or cTFH17.1 cells with disease activity, anti-double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) autoantibodies and inflammatory cytokines were analysed. The interactions of cTFH17 cells with autoreactive B cell responses were studied in co-culture assays. The proliferation and inducible T cell costimulator (ICOS) upregulation were detected in cTFH17 cells, while autoantibody-secreting cell differentiation and autoantibody production were detected in B cells.ResultsThe frequencies of cTFH17 and cTFH17.1 cells were significantly elevated in subjects with SLE compared with healthy controls, particularly in those with active disease. These cells exhibited increased activation, as indicated by the expression of ICOS and CD40L. The frequency of activated cTFH17 or cTFH17.1 cells correlated positively with SLE disease activity index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K) scores but not with anti-dsDNA antibody levels. Profiling of inflammatory cytokines revealed the elevated levels of IL-6, IL-8 and IL-17A in individuals with SLE. However, the increased levels of these cytokines did not correlate with the frequency of cTFH17 cells nor SLEDAI-2K scores. In cTFH17-B cell co-cultures, proliferation and activation of cTFH17 cells were detected. There, B cells differentiated into antibody-secreting cells, which secreted anti-DNA autoantibodies.ConclusioncTFH17 cells were significantly expanded and activated in subjects with active SLE, showing a positive correlation with clinical activity. Functionally, cTFH17-B cell interactions enhanced plasma cell generation and autoantibody production. These findings suggest that cTFH17 cells play a key role in SLE pathogenesis.

Introduction

SLE is one of the most common antibody-mediated autoimmune diseases. In it, multiple organs are targeted and damaged by the abnormal functioning of the immune system. This results in a wide array of symptoms and signs in individual patients, including skin rashes, splenomegaly and glomerulonephritis.13 Thus, a personalised approach to the treatment and care of each patient is required. The hallmark of SLE is the aberrant production of autoantibodies. Consequently, therapeutic strategies have been developed primarily targeting B cells that drive this process.4 5 Regarding mechanisms of T cell-dependent B cell responses that lead to autoantibody production, helper CD4+ T cells are critical for driving B cell proliferation and differentiation.6 7 Therefore, a detailed analysis of the roles and subsets of helper T cells involved and an in-depth investigation of the molecular mechanisms underlying SLE pathogenesis are important for tailoring personalised treatment strategies.

Follicular helper T (TFH) cells represent a distinct lineage of CD4+ T cells that are indispensable for the formation and function of germinal centres (GCs).8 They play a central role in mediating B cell help during affinity maturation, immunoglobulin class-switch recombination and differentiating B cells into both high-affinity antibody-secreting cells (ASCs) and long-lived memory B cells.9 10 Aberrations in TFH cell differentiation or function are implicated in the pathogenesis of antibody-mediated autoimmune diseases, contributing to the breakdown of B cell tolerance along with the production of pathogenic autoantibodies and systemic inflammation.1113 The human TFH cells are identified by their co-expression of CXCR5, PD-1 and transcription factor BCL6. In blood circulation, the circulating TFH (cTFH) were defined into four subsets, cTFH1 (CXCR3+CCR6−), cTFH2 (CXCR3−CCR6−), cTFH17 (CXCR3−CCR6+) and cTFH17.1 (CXCR3+CCR6+), based on chemokine receptor expression, which are each characterised by distinct cytokine profiles and functional properties that may differentially influence disease pathogenesis.14 15 In addition to TFH cells, a related population of PD-1+CXCR5−CD4+T cells, known as peripheral helper T (TPH) cells, has also been described.16 The TPH cells lack CXCR5 expression and do not migrate into GCs, but instead, they act in inflamed peripheral tissues.17 The circulating TPH (cTPH) cells have four subsets (cTPH1, cTPH2, cTPH17 and cTPH17.1). The cTPH2 and cTPH17 levels were significantly increased in patients with active SLE, and their numbers correlated with disease activity and the presence of autoantibodies.18

In patients with SLE, distinct alterations have been observed among cTFH subsets, underscoring their differential contributions to disease pathogenesis.19 20 The phenotypic analysis of surface markers among the four subsets (cTFH1, cTFH2, cTFH17 and cTFH17.1) showed elevations of cTFH17 and cTFH17.1 cells in active lupus, and their frequencies were strongly correlated with disease activity and autoantibody titre.2123 Activation of inducible T cell costimulator (ICOS)+cTFH17 cells was significantly related to the expansion of both activated naive (aNAV) and double-negative 2 (DN2) B cells.23 24 These activated cTFH17 cells have been reported to produce interleukin (IL)-21 in combination with IL-17A or interferon-gamma (IFN-γ).25 These data indicated that the aberrant enrichment of cTFH17 and cTFH17.1 cells might drive extrafollicular expansion of DN2 B cells, leading to autoantibody secretion. The combination of IL-17A and IFN-γ production could synergistically promote B cell differentiation into plasmablasts and support proinflammatory environments conducive to autoimmunity.2628 However, knowledge of the detailed mechanisms involved in driving cTFH17 activation, which is linked to inflammation by IL-17A secretion and the autoreactive B cells that produce autoantibodies in patients with SLE, is still limited.

To address this limited understanding of how IL-17-producing cTFH or TPH cells contribute to inflammation and to B cell generation of autoantibodies in patients with SLE, we investigated the functional phenotypes of cTFH17 and cTFH17.1 cells as well as cTPH17 and cTPH17.1 in subjects with SLE using surface marker and cytokine intracellular staining. The activation of these cells was correlated with clinical parameters of the patients, as well as their levels of anti-dsDNA autoantibodies and proinflammatory cytokines. To demonstrate the contribution of cTFH17 cell activation to SLE pathogenesis via their interaction with B cells, cTFH17-B cell co-cultures were developed to assess the function and signals that promote B cell for auto-ASC differentiation and anti-DNA secretion.

Methods

Subjects and study design

Forty adult patients with SLE (aged above 18 years) were recruited from the Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Bangkok, Thailand. All met the 2012 SLE International Collaborating Clinics or American College of Rheumatology classification criteria for diagnosis of SLE. Disease activity was evaluated with the SLE disease activity index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K). The SLEDAI-2K scores define disease activity as follows: 0 was inactive; 1–2 was serologically active clinically quiescent; 3–5 was mildly active; 6–11 was moderately active, and >12 was severely active. For the purpose of this study, subjects were categorised as inactive with a SLEDAI-2K score of 0 (n = 20) and active with a SLEDAI-2K score of ≥1 (n=20). Patients received treatment based on the severity of their disease and the extent of organ involvement. Those with concurrent infections, malignancies and/or other autoimmune diseases were excluded from the study. Adults who lived in Bangkok, Thailand, and did not have SLE were recruited as healthy controls (HCs). All subjects provided written informed consent. Clinical characteristics and medication details were summarised in online supplemental Table S1.

Cell staining and flow cytometry

Fresh peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were prepared and stained with monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) according to manufacturer recommendations (described in online supplemental Table S2). The characteristics of cTFH or cTPH cells and their subsets were identified based on surface and intracellular expression of CD3, CD4, CXCR5, PD-1, CXCR3, CCR6, ICOS, CD40L, IFN-γ and IL-17A. Plasma cells were identified using mAbs against CD19, CD27 and CD38. To phenotype cTFH subsets, PBMCs were cultured at 37°C with 5% carbon dioxide for 6 hours in the presence of 10 µg/mL Brefeldin A (Sigma, USA). Following incubation, cells were stained with Zombie Green viability dye (BioLegend, USA). Surface markers and intracellular cytokines (IFN-γ and IL-17A) were stained according to the manufacturer’s protocol prior to flow cytometric acquisition. Data were acquired on a FACSymphony A1 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, California, USA) and analysed using FlowJo software V.10.10.0 (BD Biosciences).

The cTFH (CXCR5+PD-1+) cells were identified within total CD4+ T cells. To further delineate functional subsets, Boolean gating was performed within the cTFH population using single gates for IL-17A, IFN-γ, CXCR3 and CCR6 expression. Boolean combinations were applied to define cTFH1 (IFN-γ+CXCR3+CCR6−), cTFH2 (IL-17A-IFN-γ-CXCR3−CCR6−), cTFH17 (IL-17A+IFN-γ−CXCR3−CCR6+) and cTFH17.1 (IL-17A+IFN-γ+CXCR3+CCR6+) subsets. All results are presented as percentages of the gated parent population.

Human inflammatory cytometric bead assay

The Cytokine Bead Array (Human Inflammatory Cytokines Kit; BD Biosciences, USA), a multiplex analysis method that uses specific antibody-conjugated beads with known size and fluorescence to measure a set of cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17A and tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α)), was used with plasma of patients with SLE (n=40). In brief, 50 μL of capture beads specific to cytokines, 50 μL of plasma and 50 μL of phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated detection antibodies were mixed and incubated for 3 hours at room temperature. Then, beads were washed and resuspended with wash buffer according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The fluorescence intensity from beads represented the type of cytokine, while that from the PE-conjugated detection antibody was proportional to the quantity of cytokine. Samples were measured by flow cytometry with a FACS Canto II, and data were analysed by CBA Analysis Software, or ‘CBAAS’ (BD Biosciences).

cTFH17-B cell co-cultures

To generate cTFH17-like cells in an in vitro study, naive CD4+T cells (CD3+ CD4+ CD45RA+) were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) using a FACSMelody Cell Sorter (BD Biosciences). The sorted cells were cultured in 96-well plates under stimulation with anti-CD28/CD49d (10 ng/mL), IL-21 (20 ng/mL), IL-6 (20 ng/mL) and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β, 10 ng/mL) at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 5 days. Following culture, cells were harvested and analysed for the differentiation of the cTFH17-like cell phenotype.

To investigate the function of cTFH17-B cell interactions, autologous naive B cells and cTFH17-like cells were co-cultured in medium containing anti-CD28 and anti-CD49d (10 ng/mL), IL-21 (20 ng/mL) and B-cell activating factor (20 ng/mL). The calf thymic DNA (ctDNA) and R848, previously reported to trigger B cell activation for autoantibody secretion in in vitro studies, were used as a surrogate antigen for human double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) and the TLR7/8 activation ligand, respectively.29 30 Cells were stimulated under four conditions: stimulus 1 (S1), 10 µg/mL ctDNA; stimulus 2 (S2), 1 µg/mL R848 and 10 µg/mL ctDNA; stimulus 3 (S3), cells with no stimuli (negative control); and stimulus 4 (S4), 1 µg/mL phytohaemagglutinin (PHA) stimulation (positive control). After 5-day co-cultures, the cells were collected to assess ICOS expression on cTFH17 cells. To evaluate B cell responses, the frequency of plasma cells was determined using mAbs against CD19, CD27 and CD38 (online supplemental Table S2); levels of total immunoglobulin (Ig)G and anti-DNA autoantibodies were detected in culture supernatants.

Proliferation assay

To evaluate the proliferation of cTFH17-like cells after co-culture with autologous B cells, a carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) dilution assay was performed.31 PBMCs (5×106) were labelled with 0.5 µM CFSE (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA), and naive CD4+ T cells were subsequently sorted by flow cytometry. The sorted cells were cultured for 5 days in the presence of anti-CD28/CD49d (10 ng/mL), IL-21 (20 ng/mL), IL-6 (20 ng/mL) and TGF-β (10 ng/mL) to induce the differentiation of cTFH17-like cells. Then, autologous B cells were added for co-culture under the following stimulation conditions: 10 µg/mL ctDNA (S1) or ctDNA plus 1 μg/mL R848 (S2). Cells cultured without ctDNA served as negative controls (S3), while cells stimulated with 1% (v/v) PHA (Thermo Fisher Scientific) served as positive controls (S4). After 5 days of co-culture, cells were harvested and analysed for CFSE dilution by flow cytometry. Cell division was quantified using a Proliferation Index (PI), which was calculated using FlowJo software (TreeStar, Oregon, USA). PI represented the average number of divisions that responding cells had undergone, defined as the total number of cell divisions divided by the number of cells that had undergone at least one division.

Measurement of total IgG and anti-DNA autoantibody levels

Levels of total IgG and anti-DNA autoantibodies in co-culture supernatants were determined by ELISA as previously reported.32 Briefly, 1 µg/mL antihuman IgG (Mabtech, Nacka, Stockholm, Sweden) or 10 μg/mL of ctDNA was coated on 96-well plates and blocked with 10% phosphate-buffered saline with 10% foetal bovine serum. Undiluted supernatants were added to wells, followed by goat antihuman IgG conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (KPL, Milford, Massachusetts, USA). Plates were developed using tetramethylbenzidine as the enzyme substrate, and the optical density was read at 450 nm. Supernatants from non-stimulated aNAV B cells were assayed as background.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using GraphPad Prism software V.9 (GraphPad Software, San Jose, California, USA). Group comparisons were performed using two-tailed, unpaired Mann-Whitney U tests, and results are presented as median with IQR. Correlations between the frequencies of circulating TFH/TPH subsets (cTFH17, cTFH17.1, cTPH17 and cTPH17.1) and cytokine levels were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients (r). Group differences with p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Frequency and activation of cTFH17 and cTPH17 subsets

An increase of cTFH1 and cTFH17 cells, based on surface phenotype, was observed in subjects with SLE.23 In this study, the frequency of IFN-γ and/or IL-17A cytokine-producing cTFH or cTPH cells was determined using flow cytometry. Gating strategies are illustrated in figure 1A, where the cTFH and cTPH populations were defined as CXCR5+PD−1+ and CXCR5−PD-1+, respectively, within CD4+T cells. Each compartment was further classified into cTFH1 or cTPH1 (CXCR3+CCR6−IFN-γ+), cTFH17 or cTPH17 (CXCR3−CCR6+IL-17A+) and cTFH17.1 or cTPH17.1 (CXCR3+CCR6+IL-17+IFN-γ+) subsets (figure 1A). The frequency and activation of each cTFH/cTPH subset in patients with SLE and HCs were summarised in table 1. A significant increase in the frequency of cTFH1 cells (p<0.001), cTFH17 cells (p<0.0001) and cTFH17.1 cells (p<0.0001) was detected in subjects with SLE, compared with HCs. In contrast, only cTPH17 (p<0.01) and cTPH17.1 cells (p<0.0001) were elevated in subjects with SLE (figure 1B).

Subjects with SLE exhibited significantly increased frequencies of ICOS+cTFH17 cells (p<0.05), CD40L+cTFH17 (p<0.001), ICOS+cTFH17.1 cells (p<0.05) and CD40L+cTFH17.1 cells (p<0.01), compared with HCs. Frequency of cTFH17 and cTFH17.1 cells and relation to disease activity

Since increased frequencies of activated ICOS+cTFH17 and ICOS+cTPH17 cells were detected in subjects with SLE, we further analysed whether the increased frequencies of cTFH17 and cTFH17.1 cells correlated with levels of disease activity or anti-dsDNA autoantibodies. The subjects with SLE were grouped as active or inactive based on disease activity scores, and these cell frequencies were similarly determined in subjects with or without anti-dsDNA autoantibodies. The cTFH17 (active: median 14.74 (13.12–15.13) vs inactive: median 11.55 (10.52–12.09), p<0.0001) and cTFH17.1 cells (active: median 16.52 (14.48–17.17) vs inactive: median 14.84 (13.58–15.65), p<0.01) were significantly more frequent in subjects with SLE with active compared with those with inactive disease (figure 2A). In contrast, there were no significant differences in activated cTPH17 (active: median 16.85 (15.72–17.55) vs inactive: median 16.83 (14.99–17.80), ns) nor cTPH17.1 cells (active: median 16.19 (15.57–16.88) vs inactive: median 16.00 (15.31–16.79), ns) between the two disease activity groups (figure 2A). Therefore, correlation analyses of SLEDAI-2K scores with cTFH17 and cTFH17.1 cells were determined for all subjects with SLE and also for the active disease subgroup. The frequency of cTFH17 cells was positively correlated with SLEDAI-2K score, both with all subjects with SLE (r=0.420, p<0.01) and in the subgroup with active disease (r=0.489, p<0.05). A significant correlation was found between cTFH17.1 cell frequency and SLEDAI-2K score in all subjects with SLE (r=0.387, p<0.05), but not in the active disease subgroup (figure 2B).

Since T helper 17 (TH17) cells contribute to B cell activation and to the generation of humoral immunity,30 we assessed the association of the increased frequency of cTFH17 or cTPH17 cells and the presence of anti-dsDNA autoantibodies in the 40 subjects with SLE. No significant differences were observed in the frequencies of cTFH17 (median 13.77 (11.51–14.88) vs median 12.27 (11.37–14.70), ns), cTFH17.1 (median 16.35 (13.86–16.86) vs median 15.30 (14.18–16.48), ns), cTPH17 (median 16.88 (16.04–17.74) vs median 16.82 (14.83–15.67), ns), nor cTPH17.1 cells (median 16.07 (15.68–16.52) vs median 16.68 (15.54–17.77), ns) between subjects with SLE positive or negative for anti-dsDNA autoantibodies (figure 2C). Additionally, the cell frequencies of none of these four cell subsets were positively correlated with levels of anti-dsDNA autoantibodies in the 40 subjects with SLE (online supplemental Figure S1).

Elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines were not related to an increased frequency of cTFH17 cells.

The IL-17 cytokine is involved in inflammation and the pathogenesis of SLE.30 The present study determined cytokine profiles (IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17A and TNF-α) in the plasma of 40 subjects with SLE. Levels of IL-6, IL-8, IL-1 and IL-17A were higher than those of HCs, while IL-1, IL-12p70 and TNF-α levels were not different (online supplemental Figure S2). We further investigated whether these elevated cytokine levels were associated with the disease status (active or inactive) of subjects (figure 3A). The results showed no difference in levels of IL-6, IL-8, IL-10 or IL-17A between the active and inactive disease groups (figure 3A and B). Furthermore, levels of these cytokines were not correlated with SLEDAI-2K scores (figure 3C). To relate the frequencies of cTFH17 or cTPH17 cells to levels of these inflammatory cytokines in the study subjects, a correlation analysis was performed. No significant positive correlation was found between the frequency of cTFH17, cTFH17.1, cTPH17 and cTPH17.1 cells and the levels of any of the tested cytokines (figure 3D).

cTFH17 cells proliferated and became activated upon stimulation with DNA antigen

To investigate the contribution of cTFH17-B cell interaction in autoantibody secretion, in vitro cTFH17-B cell co-cultures were established. Naive CD4+T cells were sorted from SLE or HC subjects and cultured under cytokine conditions (IL-21 and TGF-β) to generate cTFH17-like cells enriched for CCR6+ subsets. Then, autologous B cells were added for co-culture. After 5 days, the cells were detected for proliferation and activation of differentiated cTFH cells, along with ASC by flow cytometry (figure 4A). The cTFH-like cells from SLE and HC subjects predominantly exhibited cTFH17-like (median; SLE=45.37 (43.21–46.51) and HC=41.25 (39.57–43.75)) and cTFH17.1-like phenotypes (median; SLE=32.84 (30.37–36.3) and HC=39.77 (37.25–41.12)), whereas a lower frequency of cTFH1-like (median; SLE=3.27 (3.09–3.85), HC=5.05 (4.96–5.12)) and cTFH2-like cells (median; SLE=18.46 (17.66–18.05), HC=14.28 (11.15–18.99)) was generated in the cultures (figure 4B and online supplemental Figure S3A). Next, we assessed the interactions between cTFH-like and naive B cells from SLE or HC subjects in co-cultures. Representative CFSE histograms of SLE- or HC-derived cTFH17-like cells are shown in figure 4C (left), illustrating proliferative shifts across stimulation conditions (S1-S4). The lupus cTFH17-like cells exhibited significantly greater proliferation on ctDNA stimulation compared with unstimulated conditions (SLE; S1: PI=2.57 (2.37–3.00) vs S3: PI=1.95 (1.63–2.00); p<0.01) (figure 4C). Discussion

TFH cells play a central and pathogenic role in SLE.33 The number of cTFH17 cells is increased during active disease, and their frequency positively correlates with both disease activity and the presence of anti-dsDNA autoantibodies.23 Given the heterogeneity of cTFH phenotypes and functions, our study aimed to determine the role of cTFH17 cells in promoting autoreactive B cells for antibody secretion by detecting these cells based on both surface markers and intracellular cytokine expression. The result revealed that both lupus cTFH17 and cTFH17.1 cells were increased and overexpressed T cell costimulatory ICOS molecules. These activated cells were directly related to SLEDAI-2K scores of subjects with SLE, indicating that cTFH17 cells are useful as a biomarker to reflect the flare status of subjects with SLE. Notably, co-cultures of cTFH17 and naive B cells, stimulated with TLR-7/8 ligand and ctDNA, result in proliferation and activation of cTFH17-like cells. After receiving cTFH17 cell signals, B cells differentiated into auto-ASCs and increased production of anti-DNA autoantibodies. Altogether, our data supported that lupus cTFH17 cells cooperated with B cells in promoting B cell differentiation and their production of autoantibodies.

Monitoring and predicting disease activity in patients with SLE remains among the most challenging aspects of disease management.34 35 To date, it remains unclear whether immunological biomarkers such as anti-dsDNA autoantibodies, complement levels and cytokines can effectively monitor disease status and/or predict flares. Here, we found that increased frequencies of cTFH17 and cTFH17.1 cells were positively related to SLEDAI-2K scores. Between subjects with active and inactive disease, these two subsets were more frequent in active SLE, while cTPH17 and cTPH17.1 cells were not different. The analysis of inflammatory cytokines in subjects with SLE showed elevations of IL-6, IL-8 and IL-17A. However, these levels were not significantly different between subjects with active and inactive disease, nor were these cytokines associated with SLEDAI-2K scores. These findings contrast with those of a previous study that showed levels of IL-6, IL-8 and IL-18 to be significantly increased in active SLE and correlated with SLEDAI-2K scores.36 Variations in cytokine profiles related to disease activity in SLE may be attributed to the disease’s clinical heterogeneity and the influence of diverse treatments.37 38 Additionally, the timing of cytokine detection is a factor, as single measurements do not fully reflect the individual patient’s immune status over time. These factors present challenges in the use of cytokines as biomarkers for monitoring SLE flares. Here, we provide evidence that an increased frequency of proinflammatory cytokine-producing cells, such as cTFH17 and cTFH17.1 cells, might better reflect the inflammatory status or disease activity in SLE than the soluble mediators. Thus, the detection of cTFH17 cells might be useful as an immunological biomarker for monitoring or predicting disease activity in SLE.

To date, the role of TFH17 cells in contributing to SLE pathogenesis remains unclear. However, several studies have demonstrated a key role of T helper 17 (TH17) cells in the generation of inflammation at tissue sites in SLE.3941 Here, proinflammatory cytokines were profiled in subjects with SLE who were phenotypes of cTFH cells. Levels of IL-6, IL-8 and IL-17A were significantly higher in patients with SLE than in HCs. We further examined whether the increased frequencies of cTFH17 and cTFH17.1 cells were associated with elevated inflammatory cytokine levels. However, no positive association was observed between cytokine levels (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, IL-12p70, IL-17A or TNF-α) and the frequency of cTFH17 or cTFH17.1 cells. These results contrast with studies of TH17 cells, which showed positive correlations between their frequencies and levels of cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6 and IL-17) linked to SLE disease activity.42 43 To our knowledge, the increased frequency and activation of cTFH17 cells in active subjects with SLE may not be directly promoted by a pro-inflammatory cytokine environment, such as high levels of IL-6, IL-8 and IL-17A. Instead, signals that trigger the activation of transcription factors such as RORγT and c-Maf, as well as the production of IL-21, may be necessary to promote cTFH17 cell differentiation.25 44 A deep investigation of the factors and signals that trigger the polarisation and function of cTFH17 cells in patients with SLE could be beneficial for optimising strategies and targeting therapy.

To date, the mechanisms that drive lupus cTFH cell activation and promote autoreactive B cell-mediated autoantibody production remain unclear. In the present study, we found that cTFH17–B cell interactions, together with stimulation by ctDNA and TLR7/8 ligands, induced the proliferation and activation of cTFH17 cells. These findings are consistent with the observed increase in ICOS+cTFH17 cell frequency and the positive correlation between cTFH17 cells and disease activity in patients with SLE. Based on our data, we hypothesised that the cTFH17 cells play a role by overexpressing ICOS co-stimulatory molecules upon receiving BCR and TLR7/8 signals from B cells, acting as antigen-presenting cells. To determine whether the activated cTFH17 cells help B cell function in autoantibody secretion, we co-cultured cTFH-like cells with naive B cells and measured the frequency of resulting ASCs and the level of secreted IgG. BCR signalling constitutes a prerequisite for functional interactions between cTFH17 cells and B cells. The synergistic integration of BCR and TLR7/8 signals facilitates B cell differentiation into autoantibody-secreting cells (auto-ASCs) and subsequent release of autoantibodies. Notably, cTFH17-like and B cells from both SLE and HC responded to stimulation and exhibited functional activity upon interaction. These findings highlight the cooperative function of these cells in the immune system, where aberrant regulatory function in SLE leads to pathogenesis. Altogether, our data demonstrated that interactions between cTFH17 and autoreactive B cells resulted in the proliferation and activation of cTFH17 cells. These activated cTFH17 cells drove the process of B cell differentiation into autoreactive plasma cells and their secretion of autoantibodies, contributing to the pathogenesis of lupus. In-depth investigation of the mechanisms and signals that drive cTFH17-B cell interactions would likely lead to improved strategies for B cell-targeted therapies.

A limitation of this study was the small sample size of patients with active SLE. A larger cohort study, including patients with varying degrees of disease activity, is needed to provide more specific and dynamic measures of cTFH17 cells and their correlation with the elevated levels of IL-6, IL-8 and IL-17A cytokines, which would hold great promise as a biomarker for monitoring disease flares. Due to the very low frequency of cTFH17 cells, it is challenging to study their function in vitro study using co-culture systems. Here, we sorted naive CD4+ T cells from individual SLE or HC subjects and differentiated them into cTFH-like cells before adding autologous naive B cells. Although not all cTFH-like cells differentiated into cTFH17-like and cTFH17.1-like cells, with differentiation ranging from 80–89% in the cultures, the interaction between cTFH-like and B cells in co-cultures resulted in significant proliferation and activation of cTFH17-like cells, whereas cTFH1, cTFH2 and cTFH17.1 cells did not show activation. Thus, our data provide proof of concept for a cooperative role between cTFH17 cells and B cells in subjects with SLE.

In conclusion, this study highlighted the critical role of cTFH17 cells in SLE pathogenesis. The activation of cTFH17 cells, characterised by upregulated ICOS expression, was closely associated with disease activity and drove both the differentiation of autoreactive B cells and their production of autoantibodies through TLR7/8 ligand and DNA antigen stimulation. These findings provided new insights into the cooperative functions between B cells and cTFH17 cells in autoantibody generation, thereby contributing to a better understanding of SLE pathogenesis.
