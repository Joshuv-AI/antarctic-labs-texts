Primary gastric melanoma in an Asian patient: a rare case report of early detection and therapeutic uncertainty
Authors: Binh V. Pham, Nam H. Ha, Van T. Ta, Hoa T.T. Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Primary gastric melanoma (PGM) is an exceedingly rare malignancy, especially in Asian populations, that presents major diagnostic and therapeutic dilemmas.Presentation of the case:We report the case of a 53-year-old Vietnamese man who presented with only vague epigastric discomfort. During a routine esophagogastroduodenoscopy, a small pigmented lesion was incidentally discovered in the stomach. An initial biopsy confirmed the presence of malignant melanoma, supported by immunohistochemical positivity for SOX10. A subsequent comprehensive workup – including dermatological and ophthalmological evaluations and comprehensive systemic imaging – found no other primary melanocytic sites, thereby fulfilling three of Blecker’s criteria. A total laparoscopic gastrectomy with D2 lymphadenectomy was performed. Postoperative histopathology showed no residual tumor in the gastric specimen or in any of the lymph nodes examined.Clinical discussion:The absence of established treatment standards for PGM necessitated a complex multidisciplinary discussion. Key questions centered on balancing oncologic radicality (total gastrectomy, D2 lymphadenectomy) with patient quality of life, especially given the likelihood of complete excision at the time of the initial biopsy. The finding of no residual disease post-resection complicated the indication for adjuvant systemic therapy. The diagnosis was supported by SOX10 staining and the fulfillment of Blecker’s criteria (pending completion of the 6-month surveillance period). The upfront radical approach, though potentially excessive, preempted the need for a second operation to confirm pathological clearance.Conclusion:This report contributes to the limited literature on PGM, highlighting the importance of early detection, multidisciplinary discussion, and long-term follow-up in such rare presentations.

Introduction

Primary gastric melanoma (PGM) is an exceedingly rare clinical entity, with fewer than 50 cases reported in the global literature to date[1–5]. Given that melanomas typically arise from cutaneous, ocular, or mucosal surfaces with melanocytic cells, the stomach is an exceptionally rare primary site. The etiology of PGM remains poorly understood, and its diagnosis requires meticulous exclusion of more common metastatic disease[2,6]. Clinical guidelines for diagnosis and management are not well established, particularly in the Asian context where data are sparse. Here, we report a Vietnamese patient with a presumptive PGM discovered during routine endoscopy, and we review diagnostic challenges and therapeutic decisions. Case presentation

A 53-year-old Vietnamese male, previously healthy, presented with mild epigastric discomfort. Routine endoscopy revealed a 0.5 cm flat, pigmented lesion at the gastric fundus with no ulceration or bleeding (Fig. 1). Biopsy showed malignant melanoma, confirmed by H&E staining and SOX10 immunohistochemistry, with tumor tissue infiltrating the gastric mucosa (Fig. 2). PRAME was not performed. Dermatological and ophthalmologic evaluations ruled out other primary lesions. PET/CT revealed no evidence of systemic metastasis. Figure 1.Endoscopic images of the patient’s stomach (arrow). Discussion

The diagnosis of PGM remains a diagnosis of exclusion, guided by Blecker’s criteria: (1) absence of cutaneous melanoma, (2) no ocular involvement, (3) histological confirmation of melanoma within the stomach, and (4) at least 6 months of recurrence-free follow-up[4]. In our case, the first three criteria were met, and surveillance is ongoing to confirm the fourth, which includes the absence of prior or concurrent cutaneous, ocular, or mucosal melanoma, and at least 6 months of recurrence-free follow-up. This case fulfills the first three criteria and is pending the fourth.

Immunohistochemical confirmation relied on SOX10. PRAME, a newer melanoma marker, was not performed but may aid in future diagnosis and surveillance[8,9].

The rarity of PGM translates into a lack of standardized treatment guidelines. Consequently, therapeutic decisions often rely on extrapolation from gastric adenocarcinoma or mucosal melanoma protocols[1,3,5,6,10–14]. In this case, the decision to perform total gastrectomy with D2 lymphadenectomy (covering stations 1, 2, 3a, 3b, 4sa, 4sb, 4d, 7, 8a, 9, 11p, 11d, and 12 as per the Japanese guidelines) reflected a desire for oncologic radicality, given the malignant nature of the lesion and the absence of robust precedents. However, such an aggressive approach may exceed necessity, particularly for a 0.5 cm localized lesion. Total gastrectomy carries significant morbidity and long-term impact on the patient’s quality of life, including nutritional deficits and altered gastrointestinal physiology.

Conversely, opting for a more conservative approach – such as wedge resection or partial gastrectomy – poses a different set of challenges. Surgical oncologists may worry about inadequate resection margins or failure to harvest relevant nodal basins, especially in the absence of precise tumor mapping[15]. The possibility of leaving behind microscopic disease or omitting sentinel nodal stations generates considerable anxiety in the absence of established pathways for intraoperative assessment in such rare tumors.

Notably, in our case, postoperative pathology revealed no residual melanoma in the gastric specimen or lymph nodes. While this may suggest complete excision by the initial endoscopic biopsy, it also opens a difficult counterfactual: had limited surgery been performed, the absence of residual tumor might have raised concerns about sampling error or lesion misidentification. This could prompt reoperation – specifically, a completion gastrectomy – not for therapeutic reasons, but to secure pathological confirmation. Thus, the upfront decision for total gastrectomy, although arguably excessive, might have preempted a second, potentially avoidable, operation.

Surgical management remains the mainstay of treatment for PGM despite its rarity. A review of nearly 40 reported cases in the literature shows that surgical resection – most often gastrectomy with lymphadenectomy – was performed in the majority of patients and remains the only potentially curative intervention[1–6,10–14]. The prognosis appears closely linked to stage at presentation and completeness of resection, with localized disease offering the best chance for disease-free survival.

Importantly, surgical resection also serves a diagnostic role. Differentiating primary from metastatic melanoma can be particularly challenging, as melanoma most commonly metastasizes to the gastrointestinal tract – especially the small bowel and stomach – from a cutaneous origin. Features favoring a primary origin include a solitary lesion, the lack of prior or concurrent skin or ocular lesions, and fulfillment of Blecker’s criteria. However, without surgically obtained tissue for thorough pathological and immunohistochemical examination, definitive classification remains uncertain.

This case also illustrates the complexities of adjuvant treatment. With no residual disease, the indication for systemic therapy – either chemotherapy or immunotherapy – is unclear. There is currently no consensus on whether adjuvant treatment improves outcomes in cases of completely resected PGM without nodal involvement. Given the risks and side effects associated with these modalities, we elected active surveillance with close multidisciplinary follow-up. Should recurrence occur, immune checkpoint inhibition remains a viable option, particularly in the current therapeutic landscape of advanced melanoma.

Adjuvant therapy in PGM is controversial. Mucosal melanomas are generally considered biologically distinct from cutaneous subtypes, with lower mutational burdens and poorer responses to traditional chemotherapies. Immunotherapy has emerged as a cornerstone of treatment in advanced melanoma, particularly with checkpoint inhibitors. Agents such as ipilimumab (a CTLA-4 inhibitor), nivolumab, and pembrolizumab (PD-1 inhibitors) have shown significant improvements in survival in patients with metastatic and unresectable melanoma[16]. For instance, the CheckMate-067 trial demonstrated that combination immunotherapy (nivolumab plus ipilimumab) resulted in a 5-year overall survival rate of 52%, compared to 26% for ipilimumab monotherapy[17].

Despite these advances, data specific to mucosal or gastric melanomas remain sparse, and extrapolation from cutaneous melanoma should be done cautiously. Moreover, adjuvant immunotherapy in resected stage II/III disease is still under evaluation. Several studies have shown recurrence-free survival benefits with pembrolizumab and nivolumab, but most primarily included patients with cutaneous melanoma. The KEYNOTE-054 trial, for example, reported a 3-year recurrence-free survival of 63.7% with pembrolizumab in patients with resected stage III melanoma[18].

Given these factors and the patient’s favorable early-stage profile, we elected active surveillance. This decision prioritized quality of life while ensuring readiness to initiate immunotherapy should a recurrence occur. This approach, although conservative, aligns with real-world considerations in low-resource settings and underscores the need for pragmatic oncology tailored to the context.

In summary, this case underscores the dilemmas posed by extremely rare malignancies such as PGM: the tension between overtreatment and undertreatment, the limits of existing oncologic algorithms, and the need to balance oncologic thoroughness with preservation of patients’ quality of life. It also highlights the importance of careful documentation, serial imaging, and multidisciplinary discussion in navigating such therapeutic gray zones.

Conclusion

This case represents one of the few documented instances of early-stage PGM in an Asian patient. It highlights the importance of thorough diagnostic workup, multidisciplinary decision-making, and individualized surgical planning. Long-term surveillance remains crucial due to the potential for late recurrence or metastasis.
