First Case Report of Agnathia–Otocephaly Complex Reported in Niger
Authors: Hamidou Soumana Diaouga, Yves Zakari Kossi Odjo Dogbe, Rahamatou Madeleine Garba, Madi Nayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAgnathia–otocephaly complex (AOC) is an extremely rare congenital malformation resulting from a disorder of the first branchial arch. In the past, this condition was considered lethal. However, milder variants of the isolated type of AOC have been reported as non‐lethal. The condition is characterized by hypoplasia or the absence of the mandible (micrognathia/agnathia), variable displacement of the ears (melotia) with or without auricular fusion (synotia), and a small oral aperture or absence of mouth (microstomia/astomia), with or without tongue hypoplasia or absence (aglossia). AOC can occur as a stand‐alone condition or in conjunction with other congenital abnormalities. Antenatal ultrasound is essential for early diagnosis, allowing appropriate counseling and management decisions in severe cases of AOC. However, in developing countries, where pregnancies are not well monitored, this malformation is often not detected until after delivery. We hereby report the first case of AOC in Niger, discovered after delivery in a premature newborn. The objective was to share our department's experience and emphasize the necessity for prenatal diagnosis through effective pregnancy monitoring in our regions.

Introduction

Agnathia–otocephaly complex (AOC) is an extremely rare congenital malformation characterized by hypoplasia or absence of the mandible (micrognathia/agnathia), variable displacement of the ears (melotia), with or without auricular fusion (synotia), and a small oral aperture or absence of mouth (microstomia/astomia), with or without tongue hypoplasia or absence (aglossia) [1]. Since Kerckring's 1717 description of the first case of AOC, only a small number of cases have been reported in the literature. AOC can occur as a stand‐alone condition or in conjunction with other congenital abnormalities. Isolated forms of AOC are characterized by microstomia, agnathia, and synotia. AOC is frequently associated with midline defects, including holoprosencephaly, proboscis, hypotelorism, and cyclopia. Further possible malformations include cephalocele, cardiac defects, situs inversus, adrenal hypoplasia, and neural tube defects [2]. The precise etiology of AOC remains unclear, although it has been associated with several genetic alterations and proposed risk factors. Genetic mutations involving PRRX1, OTX2, GLI3, and SMAD3 genes, as well as unbalanced chromosomal translocations, have been reported. In addition, proposed environmental risk factors, such as exposure to salicylates, theophylline, radiation, and alcohol during pregnancy, have been suggested [1, 3]. From a clinical perspective, the severity of AOC signs can range from mandibular hypoplasia to situs inversus and holoprosencephaly [4]. Globally, AOC is a lethal malformation, with only a few cases of survival having been reported [5]. The survival of these rare cases of fetuses poses a significant challenge to the proposal to terminate the pregnancy. Currently, there are no prognostic classifications for AOC during the prenatal period. Prenatal diagnosis of AOC is now possible through obstetric ultrasound and recent advances in prenatal imaging. However, in countries with limited resources, where pregnancies are not closely monitored or not monitored at all, this malformation is often not detected until after delivery.

We present the first documented case of AOC in Niger, discovered in a premature newborn after delivery. The objective was to share the experience of our department and emphasize the necessity for prenatal diagnosis through effective pregnancy monitoring in our regions.

Case History/Examination

A 20‐year‐old, 31‐week pregnant Black African woman presented to our emergency department with preterm labor. She had no significant past medical or surgical history. The pregnancy was complicated by polyhydramnios. Twelve minutes later, she underwent spontaneous vaginal delivery of a male newborn, weighing 1000 g. The Apgar score was 2 and 2 at the first and fifth minute, respectively. The patient was from a non‐consanguineous marriage. There was no history of maternal infection, gestational diabetes, or exposure to alcohol, tobacco, drugs, medications, or toxins during pregnancy. There was no family history of oromaxillary, facial, or other congenital anomalies in siblings. However, the patient did not receive any prenatal care or biological assessments prior to delivery. At birth, the infant was found to have the following congenital anomalies: low‐set ears (synotia), an absent mandible (agnathia), an absent mouth and lips (astomia), and an elongated neck. The infant's limbs and thorax were normal (Figure 1). To rule out tracheoesophageal anomalies, a nasogastric tube was inserted through the nose. Auscultation confirmed that the tube was correctly positioned in the stomach during the procedure. No other abnormalities were noted. However, the newborn died 10 min after delivery.

Differential Diagnosis

This clinical presentation raises the following differential diagnoses: Treacher Collins syndrome, which is characterized by midface hypoplasia, micrognathia, and abnormalities of the external ear and lower eyelid. Other possible differential diagnoses include Nager syndrome, which is embryologically related and characterized by craniofacial, limb, and musculoskeletal anomalies, as well as maxillomandibular synostosis with mouth agenesia.

Investigations and Treatment

Unfortunately, the baby died 10 min after birth. The parents were informed of the option to perform an autopsy, genetic testing, and neonatal imaging, but they declined on social and religious grounds. Based on the clinical findings, we were able to confirm the final diagnosis of AOC.

Conclusion and Results

We reported the first documented case of AOC in our country. This condition poses a substantial challenge for parents and healthcare providers due to its rarity and complexity. Early antenatal scans play a crucial role in identifying this congenital anomaly. However, in our medically underserved regions, prenatal diagnosis is challenging, and AOC is often diagnosed after delivery, as demonstrated in this case report. A prognostic classification showing the different stages of severity of AOC with their postnatal survival rates is necessary in order to facilitate the process of practitioners advising parents on whether to terminate or continue the pregnancy in cases of AOC.

Discussion

This report documents the first case of agnathia–otocephaly complex in Niger, observed in a premature newborn. The infant was born following an unmonitored pregnancy. The diagnosis was made after delivery. There was no evidence of consanguinity between the parents nor any known medical or surgical history. AOC is a rare congenital anomaly with an incidence of about 1/70,000 births [4].

The underlying causes of this condition are not yet fully understood. AOC is an anomaly of the ventral portion of the first branchial arch, due to the migration failure of neural crest cells (NCC) from the hindbrain in the early embryonic stage, or the premature proliferation of the embryonic disk [6, 7]. Since Kerckring's first description in 1717, less than 80 cases have been reported in the literature [3, 6].

With regard to the proposed risk factors of the condition, in addition to genetic factors (such as mutations in the PRRX1, OTX2, GLI3, and SMAD3 genes or unbalanced translocations), other environmental factors have been identified. These include salicylates, theophylline, radiation, and alcohol exposure during pregnancy, which have been identified as teratogenic factors [1, 3]. In this case report, there is no known personal or family history of disease nor any history of medication or exposure to teratogenic factors during pregnancy.

Clinically, there is considerable variability. Indeed, a range of clinical presentations have been documented in the literature [1, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Isolated forms of AOC are characterized by microstomia, agnathia, and synotia, without any other associated abnormalities [3]. In addition, the majority of patients with this malformation also have other congenital abnormalities, including holoprosencephaly, proboscis, hypotelorism, or cyclopia. Further possible malformations include cephalocele, cardiac defects, situs inversus, adrenal hypoplasia, and neural tube defects [2]. In this case report, it is noted that an autopsy and neonatal imaging for associated malformations were not performed. However, a physical examination revealed no additional congenital anomalies.

Recent literature has highlighted the heterogeneity of agnathia–otocephaly complex (AOC) and suggested the existence of distinct phenotypic subsets. According to a recent, comprehensive review [8], a subgroup of AOC without associated holoprosencephaly, craniosynostosis, or situs inversus has been described, representing a more isolated craniofacial form of the condition. Our case appears to belong to this subset, characterized by agnathia, synotia, and astomia without clinically detectable associated malformations.

Several similar cases have been reported in the literature, including those by Brown and Marsh (1990), Erlich et al. (2000), Thauvin‐Robinet et al. (2002), Hwang et al. (2007), Dasouki et al. (2013), Donnelly et al. (2012), Corona‐Rivera et al. (2013), Patat et al. (2013), and Alexander et al. (2020) [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Overall, these cases share a relatively homogeneous craniofacial phenotype with the absence of major systemic malformations, yet they are consistently associated with poor neonatal outcomes. Our findings support the notion that, even in the absence of severe associated anomalies, isolated forms of AOC remain associated with a very poor prognosis, mainly due to critical airway compromise at birth.

Due to limited access to detailed clinical and genetic data in previously reported cases, this comparison is based primarily on key phenotypic features described in the literature. This highlights the need for systematic reporting, including genetic investigations and postmortem examinations, to better delineate the phenotypic spectrum and improve prognostic stratification.

With regard to diagnosis, prenatal ultrasound is an indispensable tool. It enables the early diagnosis of malformations, as early as the first trimester of pregnancy, through the detection of hypoplastic or absent mandibles and low‐presentation ears [3, 7, 18]. Other diagnostic methods, such as CT and MRI, have also been employed to provide further details of the malformation [19]. This advanced prenatal imaging allows us to distinguish between different forms of AOC [2, 5, 18]. This is important because some forms are often viable, while others are severe and incompatible with life, indicating termination of pregnancy. In viable cases of AOCs, treatment may involve aggressive interventions such as gastrostomy, tracheostomy, followed by three‐stage mandibular distraction. The existence of rare surviving AOC cases is the main difficulty in proposing termination of pregnancy for AOC in the absence of reliable prognostic criteria during the prenatal period. In Niger, for instance, termination of pregnancy is only authorized in cases of non‐viable fetal malformations. In this case report, the patient was seen for the first time during delivery. It should be noted that the patient had not had any prenatal consultations and that the emergency ultrasound did not allow for an antenatal diagnosis of AOC.

Regarding the prognosis, the majority of cases of AOC are lethal, and living patients show intrauterine growth retardation, prematurity, and impaired ventilation [3]. After birth, endotracheal intubation is difficult due to severe airway malformations, and to date, fewer than approximately 11 isolated AOC patients have survived beyond infancy [3, 5]. A multidisciplinary team, family information, premature birth planning, early gastrostomy and tracheostomy, and a long‐term treatment plan are essential to ensure proper patient management [3, 20].
