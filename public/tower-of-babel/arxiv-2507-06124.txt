Abstract
. In the context of ideally exact categories, we introduce the notions of internal coherent action and internal ideal action that generalize different aspects of unital actions of rings and algebras. We prove that every ideal action is coherent, and that the converse statement holds in some relevant ideally exact contexts. Furthermore, a connection with G. Janelidze’s notion of semidirect product in ideally exact categories is analyzed.

03B52; 06D35; 08C05; 16B50; 18C05; 18E13

1.
Introduction

The concept of action—or of external operation—is a pervasive algebraic notion, as it enables algebraic structures to interact beyond their intrinsic environment of definition.

In categorical terms, such actions can be defined as functors.
For instance, an action of a group
G
on a set
X
can be described as a functor

\alpha\colon G(*)\rightarrow\mathbf{Set},

where
G(*)
is the group
G
viewed as a one-object groupoid, and the unique object
*
is sent to
X
by
\alpha
.

However, it is sometimes necessary to consider
internal actions
, where the acting and the acted objects belong to the same category. These kinds of actions are useful tools for studying the algebraic structures themselves, as it happens systematically in cohomological algebra. In the case of groups, a functor
\alpha\colon G(*)\rightarrow\mathbf{Grp}
corresponds to a group homomorphism
G\rightarrow\Aut(X)
, where
X=\alpha(*)
and
\Aut(X)
denotes the group of automorphisms of
X
.

On the one hand, this approach offers certain advantages, as it enables the expression of the notion of action within the internal language of the category of groups. On the other hand, it also presents several drawbacks. Firstly, many algebraic categories, otherwise well-behaved, do not admit an internal construction that plays the role of
\Aut(-)
. Secondly, even in cases where such a construction exists, it is typically not functorial.

The right take on this subject was developed by D. Bourn and G. Janelidze in
[
13
]
, and later systematized by F. Borceux, G. Janelidze and G. M. Kelly in
[
7
]
. In these two papers, the authors make a strong point on what an internal action should be from a
category-theoretic perspective
. The first paper centers on the algebraic (monadic) description of split epimorphisms, i.e., of the category of
points
(see
Section

2
). In a Barr-exact pointed protomodular category
\mathbb{C}
with pushouts of split monomorphisms, the split epimorphisms with a fixed codomain
B
are given by algebras on the kernel of the epimorphism, for a specified monad
B\flat(-)\colon\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
. When the category is not pointed, the general theory introduced in
[
13
]
still applies, albeit with a caveat: it only allows us to express split epimorphisms over
B
in terms of split epimorphisms over another object
E
, whenever there is a morphism
E\rightarrow B
.

However, the “working mathematician” knows that fair notions of (external) actions have been defined in many non-pointed algebraic contexts, as for example for unital algebras over a field
\mathbb{F}
or for unital rings
[
37
]
, and they are often obtained by imposing additional axioms to a corresponding notion of action in a pointed context (see, for instance,
[
43
]
, where external actions are described in the context of groups with operations).

The present paper aims to partially bridge this gap, by proposing the new notions of
coherent action
and of
ideal action
. From an algebraic perspective, the former generalizes unital actions of rings and algebras, where a multiplicative unit is required to
act
like a unit, while the latter stems from the classical situation where a unitary algebra acts on an ideal.

The manuscript is structured as follows. After this introduction, Section 2 presents some necessary background.
Ideally exact categories
are recalled in Section 3, while in Section 4 we propose the notions of coherent and ideal actions and morphisms. In
Theorem

4.10
, we show that all ideal actions are coherent; the problem of whether the converse of the theorem holds in general remains open. We call
BAT
the ideally exact contexts with a
good theory of actions
, i.e., where the converse of
Theorem

4.10
holds for objects and morphisms. A connection with semidirect products in the ideally exact context is analyzed in Section 5, and equivalent conditions for an ideally exact context to be BAT are given (
Corollary

5.3
).
The final section presents case studies of BAT contexts, namely: unitary non-associative
\mathbb{F}
-algebras or rings, MV-algebras, product algebras, and
\mathbf{Set}^{\op}
, the dual of the category of sets.

Notation.
We often identify algebraic varieties with their corresponding categories; therefore, we shall adopt the same notation for both.

3.
Ideally exact categories

Ideally exact categories have been introduced by G. Janelidze in
[
30
]
as a non-pointed counterpart of semi-abelian categories. In fact, ideally exact categories are closely related to a categorical setting introduced by S. Lapenta, L. Spada and the second named author in
[
34
]
in order to define a
categorical notion of ideal
relative to a given adjunction
U\vdash F
(a
basic setting for relative
U
-ideal
, see
[
30
, Definition 3.3]
). In the present article, we stick to G. Janelidze’s definition, since, among many of its features, it clarifies how the notion of relative
U
-ideal can be defined intrinsically.

Definition 3.1
.

[
30
, Definition 3.2 and Theorem 3.1]

A category
\mathbb{U}
is
ideally exact
if it is Barr exact, Bourn protomodular, has finite coproducts, and the unique morphism
\iota\colon 0\rightarrow 1
in
\mathbb{U}
is a regular epimorphism.

In addition to all semi-abelian categories, examples of ideally exact categories include the categories
\mathbf{Ring}
and
\mathbf{CRing}
of unital and commutative unital rings respectively, every category of unital algebras over a field
\mathbb{F}
(see
Section

6.1
), the categories of
MV-algebras
,
product algebras
(see
[
34
]
and
Section

6.14
), and any cotopos (see
Section

6.40
).

Since the pullback functor
\mathbb{U}\rightarrow(\mathbb{U}\downarrow 0)
along the regular epimorphism
0\rightarrow 1
is monadic, ideal exactness can be related to adjoint pairs.

Theorem 3.2
.

[
30
, Theorem 3.1]

Let
\mathbb{U}
be a category with pullbacks. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\mathbb{U}
is ideally exact;

(2)

\mathbb{U}
is Barr-exact, has finite coproducts and there exists a monadic functor
\mathbb{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}
, where
\mathbb{V}
is a semi-abelian category;

(3)

There exists a monadic functor
\mathbb{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}
, where
\mathbb{V}
is a semi-abelian category, such that the underlying functor of the corresponding monad preserves regular epimorphisms and kernel pairs. ∎

A

6.14.
Varieties of hoops, MV-algebras and product algebras

The algebraic structure now known as a
hoop
was first introduced by B. Bosbach in
[
8
,
9
]
, where it appeared under the name
complementary semigroups
(
komplementäre Halbgruppen
). The term hoop itself was later coined in an unpublished manuscript by J. R. Büchi and T. M. Owens
[
14
]
, and has since become standard in the literature on substructural logics and residuated structures. We refer the reader to
[
5
]
for more details about the structures of hoops.

In this section, we investigate the notion of coherent actions in some varieties of hoops. We aim to prove that the variety of
Wajsberg hoops
and that of
product hoops
define two BAT ideally exact contexts.

Definition 6.15
.

A
hoop
is an algebra
H=(H,\cdot,\rightarrow,1)
of type
(2,2,0)
such that

(H1)

(H,\cdot,1)
is a commutative monoid;

(H2)

x\rightarrow x=1;

(H3)

x\cdot(x\rightarrow y)=y\cdot(y\rightarrow x)
;

(H4)

(x\cdot y)\rightarrow z=x\rightarrow(y\rightarrow z)
,

for every
x,y,z\in H
.

Given two hoops
H,K
, a
hoop homomorphism
is a map
f\colon H\rightarrow K
that preserves both the binary operations
\cdot
and
\rightarrow
, and the constant
1
.

Remark 6.16
.

Every hoop
H
is endowed with a partial order
\leq
, which is defined by the following equivalent conditions: for every
x,y\in H

(1)

x\leq y
;

(2)

x\rightarrow y=1
;

(3)

there exists
z
in
H
such that
x=z\cdot y
.

Given a homomorphism of hoop
f\colon H\rightarrow K
, its kernel
F=\ker f
is a
filter
of
H
, i.e., a subset
F\subseteq H
such that
(F,\cdot,1)
is a submonoid of
(H,\cdot,1)
which is upward closed with respect to the natural order
\leq
of
H
. Conversely, every filter
F
of
H
may be seen as the kernel of the canonical projection
\pi\colon H\rightarrow H/F
.

Remark 6.17
.

[
34
]

The variety
\mathbf{Hoops}
of hoops is a semi-abelian category.

As mentioned above, we focus on the study of coherent actions in two relevant subvarieties of the variety
\mathbf{Hoops}
: the variety of
Wajsberg hoops
and that of
product hoops
.

Definition 6.18
.

A
bounded hoop
is a hoop
H
with a constant
0
such that

(B)

0\rightarrow x=1
,

for any
x\in H
.

Definition 6.19
.

A
Wajsberg hoop
is a hoop
H
such that

(W)

(x\rightarrow y)\rightarrow y=(y\rightarrow x)\rightarrow x
,

for every
x,y\in H
.

It was proved in
[
19
]
that bounded Wajsberg hoops are term equivalent to the class of
MV-algebras

[
16
,
17
]
, which constitutes the equivalent algebraic semantics of
Łukasiewicz logic

[
36
]
.

Definition 6.20
.

[
16
]

An
MV-algebra
is an algebra
A=(A,\oplus,\neg,0)
of type
(2,1,0)
such that

(MV1)

(A,\oplus,0)
is a commutative monoid;

(MV2)

\neg\neg x=x
;

(MV3)

x\oplus\neg 0=\neg 0
;

(MV4)

\neg(\neg x\oplus y)\oplus y=\neg(\neg y\oplus x)\oplus x
,

for every
x,y\in A
.

Example 6.21
.

The set
A=[0,1]
endowed with the operations

,
\neg x=1-x
and constant

is an MV-algebra. In fuzzy logic
[
18
,
26
,
28

, as it forms the standard real-valued semantics of Łukasiewicz logic.

Remark 6.22
.

The class of MV-algebras forms an algebraic variety which we denote by

. Furthermore, the initial object of

is the two-element Boolean algebra

, with

, while the terminal object is the trivial MV-algebra

.

Every MV-algebra

is endowed with a partial order

, called the

, which is characterized by the following equivalent conditions: for any

;

(2)

x\rightarrow y=1;

(3)

there exists

such that

Such a order determines a lattice structure, where the

and the

are given by

x\vee y=(x\rightarrow y)\rightarrow y\quad\text{ and }\quad x\wedge y=x\odot(x\rightarrow y).

Given a homomorphism of MV-algebras

, its kernel

is an

of

such that

which is downward closed with respect to the natural order

of

and

. We start by recalling the following definitions.

Definition 6.30
.

[
3
]

A
basic hoop
is a hoop

such that

((x\rightarrow y)\rightarrow z)\rightarrow((y\rightarrow x)\rightarrow z)\rightarrow z)=1,

for any
x,y,z\in H
.

Definition 6.31
.

[
2
]

A
product hoop
is a basic hoop
H
satisfying the identity

(y\rightarrow z)\vee((y\rightarrow(x\cdot y))\rightarrow x)=1,

for any
x,y,z\in H

We denote by
\mathbf{PHoops}
the variety of product hoops. Similarly to the case of Wajsberg hoops, it was proved in
[
2
]
that bounded product hoops are term equivalent to the class of
product algebras
, which were introduced in
[
29
]
and constitutes the equivalent algebraic semantics of
product logic

[
2
,
20
]
.

Definition 6.32
.

[
28
]

A
BL-algebra
is an algebra
A=(A,\vee,\wedge,\cdot,\rightarrow,0,1)
such that

(i)

(A,\vee,\wedge,\cdot,\rightarrow,0,1)
is a bounded residuated lattice
[
50
]
;

(ii)

x\wedge y=x\cdot(x\rightarrow y)
;

(iii)

(x\rightarrow y)\vee(y\rightarrow x)=1
,

for any
x,y\in A
.

Definition 6.33
.

[
29
]

A
product algebra
is a BL-algebra
A
satisfying the identity

\neg x\vee((x\rightarrow x\cdot y)\rightarrow y)=1,

for any
x,y\in A
, where
\neg x\coloneqq x\rightarrow 0.

We denote by
\mathbf{PrAlg}
the variety of product algebras. Since
\mathbf{PrAlg}
is protomodular, it is an ideally exact category and it has as initial object the two-element Boolean algebra
L_{2}
.

Example 6.34
.

An example of product algebra is given by the set
A=[0,1]
endowed with the operations
x\cdot_{\prod}y=xy
and

x\rightarrow_{\prod}y=\begin{cases}1,\ \ &\text{if}\ x\leq y,\\
\frac{y}{x},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In fuzzy logic, this is called the
standard product algebra
, as it forms the standard real-valued semantics of
product logic
.

Remark 6.35
.

Given a product algebra
A=(A,\vee,\wedge,\cdot,\rightarrow,0,1)
, one may check that
(A,\cdot,\rightarrow,1)
is a bounded product hoop. Thus, it is defined a forgetful functor
U\colon\mathbf{PrAlg}\rightarrow\mathbf{PHoops}
. In
[
27
]
the authors provide a description of the left adjoint of such
U
. We now recall the construction provided that freely adds the constant
0
to a product hoop.

Let
H
be a product hoop. It can be shown that every element
x\in H
can be decomposed in a boolean and a cancellative component given by the following terms:

b(x)=(x\rightarrow x^{2})\rightarrow x,\quad c(x)=x\rightarrow x^{2}.

Moreover, the set
G(H)=\{b(x)\;|\;x\in H\}
is a
generalized Boolean algebra

[
23
]
,
C(H)=\{c(x)\;|\;x\in H\}
is a
cancellative hoop

[
5
]
and the MV-closure
B(H)=M(G(H))
of
G(H)
is a Boolean algebra.

Now, let
H^{\bullet}\coloneqq\{x^{\bullet}\;|\;x\in H\}
and let
\sim
be the equivalence relation on
H\cup H^{\bullet}
defined by

x\sim x^{\prime}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad b(x)=b(x^{\prime})\ \text{and}\ \neg b(x)\vee_{H}c(x)=\neg b(x)\vee_{H}c(x),

where
\vee_{H}\colon B(H)\times C(H)\rightarrow C(H)
is defined by

b\vee_{H}c=\begin{cases}b\vee c,\ &\text{if}\ b\in G(H),\\
\neg b\rightarrow c,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

One may check that the set
H\cup H^{\bullet}/\sim
endowed with the operations

\displaystyle xy^{\bullet}=((b\rightarrow b^{\prime})\wedge cc^{\prime})^{\bullet},

\displaystyle x^{\bullet}y^{\bullet}=((b\vee b^{\prime})\wedge cc^{\prime})^{\bullet},

\displaystyle x^{\bullet}\rightarrow y^{\bullet}=(b^{\prime}\rightarrow b)\wedge(b\vee(c\rightarrow c^{\prime})),

\displaystyle x^{\bullet}\rightarrow y=(b\vee b^{\prime})\wedge(b\vee(c\rightarrow c^{\prime}))

\displaystyle x\rightarrow y^{\bullet}=(b\wedge b^{\prime}\wedge(bc\rightarrow c^{\prime}))^{\bullet},

where
b=b(x)
,
c=c(x)
,
b^{\prime}=b(x^{\prime})
and
c^{\prime}=c(x^{\prime})
, is a product algebra. Moreover, the free functor
K\colon\mathbf{PHoops}\rightarrow\mathbf{PrAlg}
, which sends a product hoop
H
to the product algebra
K(H)=H\cup H^{\bullet}/\sim
, is the left adjoint of the forgetful functor
U\colon\mathbf{PrAlg}\rightarrow\mathbf{PHoops}
.

Finally, the unit
\eta\colon 1_{\mathbf{PHoops}}\Rightarrow HK
of the adjunction
K\dashv U
is cartesian. Indeed, for any product hoop
H
, the morphism
\eta_{H}\colon H\rightarrow UK(H)\colon x\mapsto x
is the kernel of

\displaystyle U(p_{H})\colon UK(H)

\displaystyle\rightarrow U(L_{2})

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{cases}1,\ &\text{if}\ x\in H,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Thus, by
Remark

3.3
, we have that

(6.3)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbf{PrAlg}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathbf{PHoops}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dashv}

is, up to an equivalence, the adjunction associated with the unique morphism

in
\mathbf{PrAlg}
.

holds for the ideally exact context (

([*_{X},\id_{X}]\circ f)(a)=k(a)=*_{X}.

As a consequence
a\in\ker(k)
, i.e., there exists
x\in 1+B
such that
p(x)=a
. If
x\in B
, then

f(a)=f(p(x))=((1+*_{X})\circ(1+!_{B}))(x)=*_{X}\in X,

which is a contradiction since
f(a)=1\notin X
. Thus,
a=p(1)
.

To conclude the proof, we take
A^{\prime}=A\setminus\{*_{A}\}
,
p^{\prime}=p_{\downharpoonright_{B}}
and
s^{\prime}=s_{\downharpoonright_{A^{\prime}}}
. One may easily check that
U(p^{\prime})=p
,
U(s^{\prime})=s
and the map

\sigma\colon(A,*_{A})\rightarrow 1+A^{\prime}

defined by

\sigma(a)=\begin{cases}a,\quad&\text{if }a\neq*_{A}\\
1,\quad&\text{if }a=*_{A}\end{cases}

is an isomorphism in
\mathbf{Set}_{*}
. Thus, the split monomorphism (
6.5
) is ideal.
∎

Let
