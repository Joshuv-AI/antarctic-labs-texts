On testing for independence between generalized error models of several time series
Kilani Ghoudi 1, Bouchra R. Nasri 2, Bruno N. Rémillard 1, 3

Abstract
We define generalized innovations associated with generalized error models having arbitrary distributions, that is, distributions that can be mixtures of continuous and discrete distributions. These models include stochastic volatility models and regime-switching models with possibly zero-inflated regimes. The main novelty of this article is to be able to test for conditional independence between several time series with arbitrary distributions, extending previous results of Duchesne, Ghoudi & Rémillard (2012), obtained only for stochastic volatility models. We define families of empirical processes constructed from lagged generalized errors, and we show that their joint asymptotic distributions are Gaussian and independent of the estimated parameters of the individual time series. Möbius transformations of the empirical processes are used to obtain tractable covariances. Several tests statistics are then proposed, based on Cramér–von Mises type statistics and dependence measures, as well as graphical methods to visualize the dependence. In addition, numerical experiments are performed to assess the power of the proposed tests. Finally, to show the usefulness of our methodologies, examples of applications for financial data and crime data are given to cover both discrete and continuous cases. All developed methodologies are implemented in the CRAN package IndGenErrors ( Ghoudi et al.,, 2025 ) .

Introduction

Testing conditional independence between time series is crucial for understanding and modeling their relationships. For example, if the time series
X_{1t}
and
X_{2t}
are conditionally independent with respect to the past, denoted by

P\left(X_{1t}\leq x_{1},X_{2t}\leq x_{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right)=P\left(X_{1t}\leq x_{1}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right)P\left(X_{2t}\leq x_{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right)=G_{1t}(x_{1})G_{2t}(x_{2}),

(1)

then, this is equivalent to saying that
X_{2t}
can be ignored for predicting
X_{1t}
, or is not a leading indicator
(
Haugh,, 1976
)
, meaning that

P(X_{1t}\leq x_{1}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1},X_{2t}=x_{2})=P(X_{1t}\leq x_{1}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}).

(2)

In fact, if
G_{2t}
is discontinuous at
x_{2}
, then (
1
) implies that
P(X_{1t}\leq x_{1},X_{2t}=x_{2}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=G_{1t}(x_{1})P(X_{2t}=x2)
, showing that (
2
) holds, and if the density of
G_{2t}
is continuous and positive at
x_{2}
, then (
1
) also implies that (
2
) holds true. One can even remove the continuity assumption by using properties of conditional expectations.
According to
Haugh, (1976)
, finding out if one time series can act or not as a leading indicator for the other is one of the main motivations for checking the independence between time series. It is also the first step in trying to model multivariate time series, since having independence between “innovations” of time series simplifies the underlying model.
In addition, if the conditional distribution functions
G_{1t}
and
G_{2t}
are continuous, then (
1
) is equivalent to having independence between the innovations
U_{1t}=G_{1t}(X_{1t})
and
U_{2t}=G_{2t}(X_{2t})
. Note that these innovations are independent and uniformly distributed in
(0,1)
, as noted in
Bai, (2003)
. The independence test between innovations can serve as a diagnostic tool to discover if there is remaining dependence, and if so, add a covariate to reduce dependence, as exemplified in Section
6.1
.

Several tests of dependence between innovations have been proposed in the literature that depend mainly on the properties of the data and the characteristics of the proposed models. Basically, there are three methods for testing independence: using cross-correlations or other dependence measures, using empirical processes, and using tests on coefficients of multivariate models.

For example,
Haugh, (1976)
proposed a test that is based on cross-correlations at different lags between residuals of two ARMA time series models. A similar approach was later proposed by
Hong, (1996)
using kernel methods to be able to consider all possible lags; see also
Duchesne and Roy, (2003)
for a robust approach based on Hong’s idea. However, it is known that
cross-correlation-based methods can detect dependence for Gaussian models but they can fail to detect dependence for non-Gaussian models; see, e.g., the results for the so-called Tent Map in Table 1 of
Duchesne et al., (2012)
.

To overcome the problem of estimating dependence using cross-correlations,
Kim and Lee, (2005)
proposed the use of statistics based on empirical processes constructed from residuals of time series models, by comparing the joint empirical distribution function with the product of the empirical margins at different lags. Their work was carried out in the context of infinite autoregressive bivariate models. One can also think of copula-based methods as a natural way to characterize dependence. This approach was proposed in
Genest and Rémillard, (2004)
for
independent and identically (i.i.d.) observations and was later adapted to time series in
Genest et al., 2007a ()
and
Duchesne et al., (2012)
. In the latter case, stochastic volatility models were fitted to data, taking into account exogenous variables, and empirical processes of lagged residuals were used for testing independence between lagged innovations. However, stochastic volatility models do not cover the whole range of attractive time series models. For example, one could be interested in fitting regime-switching models
(
Rémillard,, 2013
)
.

Finally, a third way of testing independence between multivariate time series is to fit a multivariate model such as the Dynamic Conditional Correlation GARCH model
(
Engle,, 2002
)
, or Vector autoregressive models (VAR), and test the null hypothesis that some coefficients are zero. In general, these coefficients are chosen so that the estimation of individual time series gives the same results as the joint estimation when the series are conditionally independent. For other models, such as VAR, the null hypothesis of independence between innovations is often equivalent to choosing diagonal matrices of parameters. For example, if
\mathbf{X}_{t}={\boldsymbol{\mu}}+A\mathbf{X}_{t-1}+{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{t}
, with
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}N_{d}(0,\Omega)
, the null hypothesis of independence would be that
\Omega
is diagonal. Again, note that it does not mean that the series are independent; only that their innovations
\varepsilon_{1t},\ldots,\varepsilon_{dt}
are independent, or equivalently that the series are conditionally independent given the past.

The previous articles all dealt with the continuous case, where the distribution of each time series is assumed to be continuous. However, in many interesting cases, time series can be discrete or a mixture of discrete and continuous distributions, as in the case of zero-inflated models. To model this kind of dependence, there are a few models, such as the INGARCH model introduced in
Ferland et al., (2006)
; see also
Davis et al., (2016)
and references therein. For example, based on a bivariate Poisson model introduced by
Lakshminarayana et al., (1999)
,
Cui and Zhu, (2018)
studied independence between two count data series, where the joint distribution is given by

\displaystyle P(X_{1}=x_{1},X_{2}=x_{2})=f_{\lambda_{1}}(x_{1})f_{\lambda_{2}}(x_{2})\left\{1+\delta\left(e^{-x_{1}}-e^{-cx_{1}}\right)\left(e^{-x_{2}}-e^{-cx_{2}}\right)\right\}
,
where
f_{\lambda}
is the density of a Poisson distribution with parameter
\lambda>0
,
\displaystyle|\delta|\leq\frac{1}{\left(1-e^{-cx_{1}}\right)\left(1-e^{-cx_{2}}\right)}
, with
c=1-e^{-1}
. In this case, the independence is equivalent to
\delta=0
.

However, in general, it is difficult to produce multivariate time series models, apart from these particular models, especially if one of the series does not have a continuous distribution. Recently, in the continuous case,
Nasri and Rémillard, (2019)
introduced copula-based models for multivariate time series, where (continuous) univariate time series were combined by using copulas between “innovations” or “generalized errors”. Recall that for any
d
-dimensional random vector
X
with joint distribution function
H
and margins
F_{1},\ldots,F_{d}
, there exists a joint distribution function
C
with uniform margins, so that for all
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
,

H(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=P(X_{1}\leq x_{1},\ldots,X_{d}\leq x_{d})=C\left\{F_{1}(x_{1}),\ldots,F_{d}(x_{d})\right\}.

(3)

Here,
C
is called a copula, and if the margins
F_{1},\ldots,F_{d}
are continuous, then
C
is the joint distribution of the random vector
\mathbf{U}=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
, where

U_{j}=F_{j}(X_{j})
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. However, if one of the margins is not continuous, an infinite number of copulas satisfy (
3
). Fortunately, whatever the margins, if
\mathbf{U}
has distribution
C
, denoted
\mathbf{U}\sim C
, for a copula
C
satisfying (
3
), then
\left(F_{1}^{-1}(U_{1}),\ldots,F_{d}^{-1}(U_{d})\right)\sim H
. Thus, a copula
C
and margins
F_{1},\ldots,F_{d}
can be combined to recover any joint distribution function. This idea can be adapted for building multivariate time series models with arbitrary margins. This methodology is not new for non-continuous distributions. In fact, it was used to simulate dependent Markov chains in
(
Rémillard,, 2013
, Section 8.2)
, as well as for simulating multivariate data with arbitrary distributions in
Genest et al., (2019)
. For inferential purposes, this technique was also applied recently in
Debaly and Truquet, (2023)
for modeling dependence between time series, with either continuous or discrete margins, while here we can use time-varying copulas and arbitrary distributions as well.

In what follows, using recent results on the construction of the multilinear copula
(
Genest et al.,, 2017
)
, we present a way for constructing “innovations” for multivariate time series with arbitrary distributions, and we propose tests of independence between lagged series of these innovations. We adopt a two-stage approach in the sense that individual time series are modeled and time-varying copulas are used to introduce additional dependence between series, on top of the dependence due to exogenous variables. This particular construction of multivariate copula-based models is described in Section
2
. Next, in Section
3
, the main theoretical results on empirical processes for testing independence are presented. In particular, we show that the methodology proposed in
Duchesne et al., (2012)
, obtained in the special case of stochastic volatility models, can be applied to the new models. We also extend the results of
Kheifets and Velasco, (2017)
for univariate discrete data to our multivariate setting. In Section
4
, the main test statistics are presented, and numerical experiments are performed in Section
5
to assess the finite sample performance (level and power) of the proposed statistics for different models. Finally, examples of applications are given in Section
6
.

2
Multivariate time series models

The main idea for modeling univariate times series is similar to the one used to define generalized error models
(
Du,, 2016
)
in the continuous case. In fact, for a time series
X_{t}
, a generalized error model assumes the existence of a series of random mappings
H_{t}
so that
\varepsilon_{t}=H_{t}(X_{t})
are i.i.d. with common continuous margin
F
, and the
\varepsilon_{t}
can be called “innovations”. Here,
H_{t}
is a
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable function, where
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
contains information about the past values
X_{t-1},\ldots
, and potentially information about exogenous variables. Note that generalized error models contain stochastic volatility models as special cases, where
H_{t}(y)=\frac{y-\mu_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}
,
\mu_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}^{2}
are, respectively, the conditional mean and the conditional variance given
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
. Other interesting examples include
the setting proposed by
Bai, (2003)
, where
G_{t}(y)=P(X_{t}\leq y|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
is a measurable mapping not depending on
X_{t}
, so
\epsilon_{t}=G_{t}(X_{t})
is well defined and has a uniform distribution.
In fact, for
u\in(0,1)
,
P\{G_{t}(X_{t})\leq u|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\}=P\left\{X_{t}\leq G_{t}^{-1}(u)|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right\}=G_{t}\circ G_{t}^{-1}(u)=u
if
G_{t}
is continuous.
In this case,
F=D
, where
D
is the uniform distribution function. These models are particularly well suited for regime-switching models where there is no natural innovations, and include the previous models by taking
G_{t}=F\circ H_{t}
.

Remark 1

For specifications tests related to such models, see, e.g.,
Bai, (2003)
and
Kheifets, (2015)
for continuous cases, and
Kheifets and Velasco, (2013)
;
Kheifets and Velasco, (2017)
for discrete cases.

In what follows, being interested in arbitrary distributions, e.g., a Poisson distribution, we need another approach, since the
G_{t}(X_{t})
are not necessarily independent or uniformly distributed. To solve this problem, we shall use a randomization technique proposed by
Brockwell, (2007)
. More precisely, let
X_{t}
be a given time series and let
\mathcal{V}_{t}
be a sequence of i.i.d. uniform variables independent of
X_{t}
. Then, define the sequence of i.i.d. uniform variables
U_{t}
by setting

U_{t}=\mathcal{H}_{t}(X_{t},\mathcal{V}_{t})=G_{t}(X_{t}-)+\mathcal{V}_{t}\Delta G_{t}(X_{t}),

(4)

where
G_{t}(y)=P(X_{t}\leq y|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
,
G_{t}(y-)=P(X_{t}<y|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
, and
\Delta G_{t}(y)=G_{t}(y)-G_{t}(y-)=P(X_{t}=y|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
.
It is well known
(
Ferguson,, 1967
;
Brockwell,, 2007
;
Nešlehová,, 2007
)
that the
U_{t}
s are uniformly distributed conditionally to the past, meaning that
P(U_{t}\leq u|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=u
. To prove that they are independent, note that using the properties of conditional expectations,

\displaystyle P(U_{1}\leq u_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\leq u_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left[E\left[\left.\prod_{j=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{1}\{U_{j}\leq u_{j}\}\right|\mathcal{F}_{n-1})\right]\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\boldsymbol{1}\{U_{j}\leq u_{j}\}P(U_{n}\leq u_{n}|\mathcal{F}_{n-1})\right]=u_{n}P(U_{1}\leq u_{1},\ldots,U_{n-1}\leq u_{n-1}).

Repeating the same argument
n-1
times, we end up with

P(U_{1}\leq u_{1},\ldots,U_{n}\leq u_{n})=\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}
, completing the proof.
Note that if
G_{t}
is continuous, then
U_{t}=G_{t}(X_{t})
, that is, there is no randomization effect. Hence, our proposed model is an extension of Bai’s setting. To simplify the exposition, somewhat imprecisely, we will call the
U_{t}
s generalized errors as well.

Example 1

For example, let
X_{t}
is a Markov chain on
\{0,1\}
with transition matrix
Q=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}p&1-p\\
1-p&p\end{array}\right)
,
p\in(0,1)
, and initial value
X_{0}=1
. In this case,
G_{t}(0)=P(X_{t}=0|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=p\boldsymbol{1}(X_{t-1}=0)+(1-p)\boldsymbol{1}(X_{t-1}=1)
,
\Delta G_{t}(0)=G_{t}(0)=G_{t}(1-)
,
G_{t}(0-)=0
, and
\Delta G_{t}(1)=P(X_{t}=1|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=(1-p)\boldsymbol{1}(X_{t-1}=0)+p\boldsymbol{1}(X_{t-1}=1)
. As a result, using (
4
), one gets

\displaystyle U_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{t}(X_{t},\mathcal{V}_{t})=p\mathcal{V}_{t}\boldsymbol{1}(X_{t-1}=0,X_{t}=0)+(p+(1-p)\mathcal{V}_{t})\boldsymbol{1}(X_{t-1}=0,X_{t}=1)

\displaystyle\qquad+(1-p)\mathcal{V}_{t}\boldsymbol{1}(X_{t-1}=1,X_{t}=0)+(1-p+p\mathcal{V}_{t})\boldsymbol{1}(X_{t-1}=1,X_{t}=1).

It follows that for any
u\in[0,1]
,
P(U_{t}\leq u|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=u
, proving that the “generalized innovations”
U_{t}
are i.i.d. and uniformly distributed.

Finally, to define the dependence between time series, let
\tilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}=(\tilde{U}_{1t},\ldots,\tilde{U}_{dt})
be a sequence of random vectors with uniform
(0,1)
margins, so that
P(\tilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}\leq\mathbf{u}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=C_{t}(\mathbf{u})
, where
\mathcal{F}_{t}
is a filtration which might contain exogenous random variables, and with respect to which
\tilde{\mathbf{U}}_{t}
is measurable. Examples of time-varying copulas
C_{t}
include parametric copula families with time-varying parameters
(
Nasri et al.,, 2019
)
and regime-switching copulas
(
Fink et al.,, 2017
;
Nasri et al.,, 2020
)
. Then, for conditional cdf
G_{jt}
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, set
\tilde{X}_{jt}=G_{jt}^{-1}(\tilde{U}_{jt})
,
t\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. It then follows that we have the so-called conditional Sklar’s equation
(
Patton,, 2004
)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle P\left(\tilde{X}_{1t}\leq y_{1},\ldots,\tilde{X}_{dt}\leq y_{d}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right)=C_{t}\left\{G_{1t}(y_{1}),\ldots,G_{dt}(y_{d})\right\}
.
In particular, if
\mathbf{U}_{t}=(U_{1t},\ldots,U_{dt})
, where
U_{jt}=\mathcal{H}_{jt}(X_{jt},\mathcal{V}_{jt})
, and
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{t}=(\mathcal{V}_{1t},\ldots,\mathcal{V}_{dt})\sim\Pi
, with
\Pi
the independence copula, then
P(\mathbf{U}_{t}\leq\mathbf{u}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=C_{t}^{\maltese}(\mathbf{u})
, where

C_{t}^{\maltese}
is called the multilinear copula
(
Genest et al.,, 2017
;
Nasri and Rémillard,, 2024
)
, and
C_{t}^{\maltese}
also satisfies the conditional Sklar’s equation,

P(X_{1t}\leq y_{1},\ldots,X_{dt}\leq y_{d}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=C_{t}^{\maltese}\left\{G_{1t}(y_{1}),\ldots,G_{dt}(y_{d})\right\}.

(5)

Recall that if all margins
G_{jt}
are continuous, then
C_{t}
is unique and
C_{t}=C_{t}^{\maltese}
.
Observe that the time series
X_{1t},\ldots,X_{dt}
are conditionally independent iff
C_{t}^{\maltese}=\Pi
for all
t\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. This is our null hypothesis.

Remark 2

Under this null hypothesis, by using properties of conditional expectations, it follows that for any
{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and conditionally to the past,

\mathbf{U}_{t+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}=(U_{1,t+\ell_{1}},\ldots,U_{d,t+\ell_{d}})\sim\Pi
; here, for
t+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}
, the past is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1+\min\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}\}}
.
To show that this is true, assume without loss of generality that
0=\ell_{1}\leq\ell_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\ell_{d}
. Then, we have to show that

\displaystyle P(U_{1,t}=U_{1,t+\ell_{1}}\leq u_{1},\ldots,U_{d,t+\ell_{d}}\leq u_{d}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}
.
If
\ell_{d}=0
, there is nothing to prove. So suppose that
k=\max\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}\}>0
and set
m=\min\{1\leq j\leq d:\ell_{j}=k\}
.
Using the null hypothesis that for any
s\geq 1
,
P(U_{1,s}\leq u_{1},\ldots,U_{d,s}\leq u_{d}|\mathcal{F}_{s-1})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}
, we have

\displaystyle P(U_{1,t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U_{1,t+\ell_{1}}\leq u_{1},\ldots,U_{d,t+\ell_{d}}\leq u_{d}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left[\boldsymbol{1}\{U_{1,t}\leq u_{1}\}\cdots\boldsymbol{1}\{U_{m-1,t+\ell_{m-1}}\leq u_{m-1}\}P\left(U_{m,t+\ell_{m}}\leq u_{m},\ldots,U_{d,t+\ell_{d}}\leq u_{d}|\mathcal{F}_{t+k-1}\right)|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E\left[\boldsymbol{1}\{U_{1,t}\leq u_{1}\}\cdots\boldsymbol{1}\{U_{m-1,t+\ell_{m-1}}\leq u_{m-1}\}P\left(U_{m,t+k}\leq u_{m},\ldots,U_{d,t+k}\leq u_{d}|\mathcal{F}_{t+k-1}\right)|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\prod_{j=m}^{d}u_{j}\right\}P\left(U_{1,t}\leq u_{1},\ldots,U_{m-1,t+\ell_{m-1}}\leq u_{m-1}|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right).

To complete the proof, we just have to repeat the previous arguments whenever
\max\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m-1}\}>0
.

Testing the conditional between lagged innovations might be interesting in applications to detect if two series are conditionally dependent for some lag. For example, the effect of a decrease of oil price can affect the price of gas at the pump after some days. Therefore, from now on, based on Remark
2
, we consider the following null hypothesis of independence

H_{0}:C_{t}^{\maltese}\sim\Pi,\quad\text{ for any }t\geq 0,

while the alternative hypothesis is

H_{1}:C_{t}^{\maltese}\sim\Pi,\quad\text{ for some }t\geq 0.

In a forthcoming paper, we will present tests of specifications for the general multilinear conditional copulas
C_{t}^{\maltese}
, but here, we restrict our attention to the specific case of a constant copula, i.e.,
C_{t}^{\maltese}=C
, for all
t>0
and for some copula
C
.

3
Empirical processes for testing independence

In what follows, we consider parametric univariate models of the form
H_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},jt}
with continuous unknown margin
F_{j}
, or the extended generalized error models of the form
\mathcal{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},jt}
, with continuous margin
F_{j}=D
. For simplicity, set
\mathcal{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},jt}(y_{j},v_{j})=H_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},jt}(y_{j})
and define the generalized errors
\varepsilon_{jt}=\mathcal{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0},jt}(X_{jt},\mathcal{V}_{jt})
. Since exogenous variables may be common to several series, all parameters are grouped in a meta-parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}
, and it is assumed that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}
is a consistent estimator of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}
such that
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{n}=n^{\frac{1}{2}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0})
converges in law to
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
, denoted
{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_{n}\rightsquigarrow{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}
.
For simplicity, set
G_{jt}=G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0},jt}
.
Next, define the pseudo-observations
e_{n,jt}=\mathcal{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n},jt}(X_{jt},\mathcal{V}_{jt})
. Note that even if one uses randomized values, we will show that the limiting distributions of our proposed statistics are parameter free and independent of the random vectors
\mathcal{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{V}_{n}
. We will also examine the effect of randomization on the finite sample behavior of the proposed tests.

Following
Duchesne et al., (2012)
, to test independence between the generalized errors of time series, we will study
the empirical processes based on lagged pseudo-observations.
To deal with more than two time series,

Duchesne et al., (2012)
introduced multivariate lags
{\boldsymbol{\ell}}=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as the

d
-vector of time lags.
For example, in the classical case
d=2
, the
vector
{\boldsymbol{\ell}}=(0,\ell)
allows us to consider the usual
l
-dependence between the two
random variables
\varepsilon_{1,t}
and
\varepsilon_{2,t+\ell}
.
For any
{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we are interested in the limiting distribution of the lagged empirical processes
\mathbb{K}_{n,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}=n^{\frac{1}{2}}(K_{n,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}-\Pi\circ\mathbf{F})
, where the empirical joint distribution function of the lagged pseudo-observations
K_{n,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}
is defined by

K_{n,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}(\mathbf{y})=n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{1}\{\mathbf{e}_{n,t+{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\leq\mathbf{y}\}=n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{1}\left\{e_{n,j,t+\ell_{j}}\leq y_{j}\right\},\;\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where the generalized errors
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{t}
and the pseudo-observations
\mathbf{e}_{n,t}
are defined in a circular way, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{t+n}={\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{t}
and
\mathbf{e}_{n,t+n}=\mathbf{e}_{n,t}
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
. Further set
\mathbb{F}_{n,j}=n^{\frac{1}{2}}(F_{n,j}-F_{j})
, with
\displaystyle F_{n,j}(y_{j})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{1}\{e_{n,j,t}\leq y_{j}\}
.
To express the asymptotic limit of empirical processes, we also need to define lagged versions of the empirical process of generalized errors, viz.

\displaystyle\alpha_{n,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}(\mathbf{y})=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{1}\{\varepsilon_{j,t+\ell_{j}}\leq y_{j}\}-\prod_{j=1}^{d}F_{j}(y_{j})\right\},\quad\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
and

\displaystyle\beta_{n,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}(\mathbf{u})=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{d}\boldsymbol{1}\{U_{tj}\leq u_{j}\}-\prod_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}\right]
,
\mathbf{u}\in[0,1]^{d}
,
where

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{n,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}(\mathbf{u})=E\left\{\beta_{n,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}(\mathbf{u})|\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}\right\}=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{d}J_{G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0},j,t}}(X_{j,t+l_{j}},u_{j})-\prod_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}\right\}
and

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}(\mathbf{u})=E\left\{\beta_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}(\mathbf{u})|\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}\right\}=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\prod_{j\in A}\left\{J_{G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0},j,t}}(X_{j,t+l_{j}},u_{j})-u_{j}\right\}
.

Kheifets and Velasco, (2017)
used this transform to construct tests of goodness-of-fit and test of serial dependence for univariate discrete time series
X_{jt}
. It is easy to check that not only in the discrete case, but also in the general case, one has

E\left\{J_{G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0},j,t}}(X_{jt},u_{j})|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right\}=u_{j}
, so for fixed

and

are martingale differences. In
Kheifets and Velasco, (2013)

t\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

k

\beta_{n,k}^{(j)}(u_{j})=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left[\boldsymbol{1}\{G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0},j,t}(X_{jt}-)+\mathcal{V}_{jt}^{(k)}\Delta G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0},j,t}(X_{jt})\leq u_{j}\}-u_{j}\right],

is an approximation of
\displaystyle\beta_{n}^{(j)}(u_{j})=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\{J_{G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0},j,t}}(X_{jt},u_{j})-u_{j}\}
. In fact, they showed numerically that taking
M=25
was often enough. The same procedure can also be applied here to the processes
\beta_{n,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}
,
\beta_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}
,
\mathbb{K}_{n,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}
,
\mathbb{K}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}
, and
\mathbb{K}_{n}^{(j)}

. Next, define

\displaystyle\tilde{}\mathbb{K}_{n,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}(\mathbf{u})=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{d}J_{G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n},j,t}}(X_{j,t+\ell_{j}},u_{j})-\prod_{j=1}^{d}u_{j}\right\}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{}\mathbb{K}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}(\mathbf{u})=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\prod_{j\in A}\left\{J_{G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n},j,t}}(X_{j,t+l_{j}},u_{j})-u_{j}\right\}

\displaystyle\tilde{}\mathbb{K}_{n}^{(j)}(u_{j})=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left\{J_{G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n},j,t}}(X_{jt},u_{j})-u_{j}\right\}
.
We now study the convergence of the process
\tilde{\beta}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}
and
\tilde{}\mathbb{K}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}

For any

Next, rename all other assumptions (A0’), …, (A6’) when applied to

If Assumptions (A0’)–(A7’) hold, then, under the null hypothesis of independence, for any finite set

(\tilde{\beta}_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}},\tilde{\beta}_{A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}},\tilde{}\mathbb{K}_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}},\tilde{}\mathbb{K}_{A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}})

In particular, for any

Remark 5

First, note that the convergence of

in

4
Test statistics

Theorems
1
and
2
extend the results of
Duchesne et al., (2012)
to the context of (extended) generalized error models and show that the limiting behaviour of the empirical processes used to develop independence tests are identical to those obtained for stochastic volatility models. Therefore, all test statistics used in
Duchesne et al., (2012)
are applicable in our context and have the same limiting behaviour as the ones in
Duchesne et al., (2012)
. All these tests are implemented in the CRAN package IndGenErrors
(
Ghoudi et al.,, 2025
)
. Here, we will just review those used in our simulation analysis.

4.1
Cramér–von Mises test statistics

For a given set

A\in\mathcal{A}

and multivariate lag index

{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the Cramér–von Mises test statistic is defined by

\displaystyle S_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left\{\mathbb{C}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}(\mathbf{u})\right\}^{2}d\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\prod_{j\in A}\left\{\frac{2n+1}{6n}+\frac{R_{j,t+\ell_{j}}\left\{R_{j,t+\ell_{j}}-1\right\}}{2n(n+1)}\frac{R_{j,s+\ell_{j}}\left\{R_{j,s+\ell_{j}}-1\right\}}{2n(n+1)}-\frac{\max\left(R_{j,t+\ell_{j}},R_{j,s+\ell_{j}}\right)}{n+1}\right\}.

Their limiting distributions follow directly from Theorem
2
and are summarized in the next proposition.

Proposition 1

Under the conditions given in Theorem
1
,
and under the null hypothesis of independence, for any finite set
L\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
the sequences

\{S_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}
,
A\in\mathcal{A}
,
{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in L\}

converge in distribution to random variables

\{S_{A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}},A\in\mathcal{A}
,
{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in L\}

which are defined as
\displaystyle S_{A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left\{\beta_{A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}(\mathbf{u})\right\}^{2}d\mathbf{u}
.
The random variables

S_{A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}

and

S_{B,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}

are independent if

A\neq B

or if
A=B

and

{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\prime}{\not\equiv}_{A}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}
.
Moreover,

S_{A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}=S_{A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\prime}}

if

{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\prime}\equiv_{A}{\boldsymbol{\ell}}
.

As pointed out in
Duchesne et al., (2012)
, for
any
A\in\mathcal{A}
of cardinality
|A|
,
S_{A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}

has the same distribution as the random variable

\displaystyle\xi_{|A|}=\sum_{(i_{1}\geq 1,\ldots,i_{|A|}\geq 1)}\frac{1}{\pi^{2|A|}(i_{1}\cdots i_{|A|})^{2}}\ Z_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{|A|}}^{2}
,
where
Z_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{|A|}}
are independent
N(0,1)
random
variables.

4.2
Generalized cross-correlations

As defined in
Duchesne et al., (2012)
, the generalized cross-correlation
\hat{r}_{A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}
is defined by

\hat{r}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}=\frac{\hat{\gamma}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}}{\prod_{j\in A}s_{nj}},

(15)

where
\hat{\gamma}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\prod_{j\in A}\left(e_{n,j,t+\ell_{j}}-\bar{e}_{j}\right)
,

s^{2}_{nj}=n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(e_{n,jt}-\bar{e}_{j})^{2}
and
\bar{e}_{j}=n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}e_{n,jt}
.
Using Theorem
2
and arguments similar to
Duchesne et al., (2012)
, we obtain that under the null hypothesis, for any finite set
L\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the sequences
\left\{n^{\frac{1}{2}}\hat{r}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}},A\in\mathcal{A},{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in L\right\}
converge in distribution to standard Normal random variables

\left\{r_{A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}},A\in\mathcal{A},{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in L\right\}
with

\displaystyle\mbox{{cov}}\left(r_{A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}},r_{B,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\prime}}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\mbox{if }A=B\mbox{ and }{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\prime}\equiv_{A}{\boldsymbol{\ell}},\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

4.3
Other dependence measures

Recently,
Nasri and Rémillard, (2024)
studied the asymptotic behavior of copula-based dependence measures for multivariate data with arbitrary distributions, including their Möbius transforms.
These include, for example, multivariate extensions of Spearman’s rho, Savage’s coefficient, and van der Waerden’s coefficient.
From now on, let
\mathbf{K}=(K_{1},\ldots,K_{d})
be a vector of margins with mean
\mu_{j}
and finite variance
\sigma_{j}^{2}
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, and define

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{K_{j}}(u)=\int_{0}^{u}K_{j}^{-1}(v)dv
.
Next, for any cdf
G
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{j,G}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}K_{j}^{-1}\left\{G(x-)+s\Delta_{G}(x)\right\}ds
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.
Then
\mathcal{K}_{j,G}(x)=K_{j}^{-1}\{G(x)\}
, if
G
is continuous at
x
, and

\mathcal{K}_{j,G}(x)=\dfrac{\mathcal{L}_{K_{j}}\{G(x)\}-\mathcal{L}_{K_{j}}\{G(x-)\}}{\Delta_{G}(x)}
,
if
G
is not continuous at
x
. The copula-based dependence measures are defined in the following way:

For any
A\in\mathcal{A}
, set

\gamma_{\mathbf{K},n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\left(\widehat{C}_{n}^{\maltese}\right)=n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\prod_{j\in A}\left\{\mathcal{K}_{j,F_{n,j}}(e_{n,j,t+\ell_{j}})-\mu_{j}\right\}.

(16)

For example, for Spearman’s rho,
K_{j}\equiv D
, so
\mathcal{L}_{j}(u)={u^{2}}/{2}
; for van der Waerden’s coefficient,
K_{j}\equiv\Phi
, so
\mathcal{L}_{j}=-\phi\circ\Phi^{-1}
,
\mu_{j}=0
; for Savage’s coefficient, we take
K_{j}(x)\equiv e^{x}
,
x\leq 0
, so
\mathcal{L}_{j}(u)=u\log{u}-u
,
\mu_{j}=-1
, with the convention that
0\ln{0}=0
. The following result is an immediate consequence of Theorem
2
and Theorem 1 in
Nasri and Rémillard, (2024)
.

Corollary 3

Under the null hypothesis of independence, for any finite set
L\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
n^{\frac{1}{2}}r_{\mathbf{K},n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}=n^{\frac{1}{2}}\dfrac{\gamma_{\mathbf{K},n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\left(\widehat{C}_{n}^{\maltese}\right)}{\prod_{j\in A}s_{K_{j},F_{nj}}}
,
A\in\mathcal{A}
, converge jointly in distribution to standard Normal random variables

\left\{r_{\mathbf{K},A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}},A\in\mathcal{A},{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in L\right\}
satisfying

\displaystyle\mbox{{cov}}\left(r_{\mathbf{K},A.{\boldsymbol{\ell}}},r_{\mathbf{K},B,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\prime}}\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\mbox{if }A=B\mbox{ and }{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\prime}\equiv_{A}{\boldsymbol{\ell}},\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.
, where

s_{K_{j},F_{nj}}^{2}=n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left[\dfrac{\mathcal{L}_{j}\{F_{n,j}(e_{n,j,t})\}-\mathcal{L}_{j}\{F_{n,j}(e_{n,j,t}-)\}}{\Delta_{F_{n,j}}(e_{n,j,t})}-\mu_{j}\right]^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle pr}}{{\longrightarrow}}\varsigma_{K_{j},F_{j}}^{2},\qquad j\in\{1,\ldots,d\},

and where, for any cdf
G
,
\displaystyle\varsigma_{K_{j},G}^{2}=\int\left\{\mathcal{K}_{j,G}\{G(x)\}-\mu_{j}\right\}^{2}dG(x)
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

The latter variances generally depend on the margins when there are discontinuities; see, e.g., Appendix
C
.

4.4
Combining test statistics

To combine test statistics for different lags and different subsets, we also follow the approach proposed
in
Duchesne et al., (2012)
. Clearly, tests for a fixed set or fixed lag will detect a particular type of dependence but will not be consistent for all alternatives. Therefore, combining test statistics with different subsets and different lags could improve the power of the test against a wider range of alternatives.
In the context of tests of independence, following
Littell and Folks, (1973)
,

Duchesne et al., (2012)
showed that combing
P
-values produced powerful test procedures.
To this end, for each subset
A\in\mathcal{A}
, a finite set of multivariate lags
D_{A}

can be created so that for any
{\boldsymbol{\ell}},{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\prime}\in D_{A}
one has
{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\,{\equiv}_{A}\,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\prime}
if and only if
{\boldsymbol{\ell}}={\boldsymbol{\ell}}^{\prime}
.
The proposed statistics are

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\;\pi^{2(|A|-2)}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in D_{A}}\{{\rm S}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}-{\rm B}(n,|A|)\},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{n}=-2\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\;\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in D_{A}}\log\left(p_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\right),

(17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\;\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in D_{A}}{\hat{r}}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}^{2},\qquad\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{K},n}=n\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\;\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in D_{A}}{\hat{r}}_{\mathbf{K},n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}^{2},

(18)

where
{\rm B}(n,|A|)
is a bias correction term given by

{\rm B}(n,d)=\left(\frac{n-1}{6n}\right)^{d}-\frac{1}{6^{d}}+(n-1)\left(\frac{-1}{6n}\right)^{d}
,
and
p_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}=P(\xi_{|A|}>{\rm S}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}})
is the estimated
P
-value of the statistic
{\rm S}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}
.
Under the null hypothesis of independence, the test statistic
\mathcal{F}_{n}
converges in law to a chi-square distribution with

\displaystyle 2\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}|D_{A}|
degrees of freedom, while the Wald’s statistics
\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{K},n}

converge in law to a chi-square distribution with
\displaystyle\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}|D_{A}|
degrees of freedom. Finally,
the test statistic
\mathcal{W}_{n}
converges in law to
\displaystyle\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\pi^{2(|A|-2)}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in D_{A}}{\rm S}_{A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}
,
under the null hypothesis of independence.
Duchesne et al., (2012)
argued that the Edgeworth’s expansion, using the first six cumulants, leads to a satisfactory approximation of the
P
-value of
\mathcal{W}_{n}
.

Finally, one could define the versions of the previous statistics when limited to pairs. More precisely, set

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{n,2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A},|A|=2}\;\pi^{2(|A|-2)}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in D_{A}}\{{\rm S}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}-{\rm B}(n,|A|)\},\qquad\mathcal{F}_{n,2}=-2\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A},|A|=2}\;\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in D_{A}}\log\left(p_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}\right),

(19)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{n,2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A},|A|=2}\;\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in D_{A}}{\hat{r}}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}^{2},\qquad\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{K},n,2}=n\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A},|A|=2}\;\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in D_{A}}{\hat{r}}_{\mathbf{K},n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}^{2},

(20)

Under the null hypothesis of independence, the test statistic
\mathcal{F}_{n,2}
converges in law to a chi-square distribution with

\displaystyle 2\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A},|A|=2}|D_{A}|
degrees of freedom, while the Wald’s statistics
\mathcal{H}_{n,2}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{K},n,2}

converge in law to a chi-square distribution with
\displaystyle\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A},|A|=2}|D_{A}|
degrees of freedom. Finally,
the test statistic
\mathcal{W}_{n,2}
converges in law to
\displaystyle\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A},|A|=2}\pi^{2(|A|-2)}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\ell}}\in D_{A}}{\rm S}_{A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}
.

4.5
Implementation steps

Assume we observe a
d
-dimensional times series
\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}
. Then, use the following steps:

1.

Choose parametric models
G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},jt}
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,
t\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The adequacy of these models can be tested using the tools cited in Remark
1
, namely
Bai, (2003)
;
Kheifets, (2015)
for continuous cases, and
Kheifets and Velasco, (2013)
;
Kheifets and Velasco, (2017)
for discrete cases.

2.

For each
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, estimate
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}
, using for examples the methodologies in
Francq and Zakoïan, (2010)
,
Moysiadis and Fokianos, (2014)
,
Fokianos et al., (2020)
, and
Debaly and Truquet, (2021)
.

3.

Generate iid random vectors
\mathbf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{V}_{n}
, where
\mathbf{V}_{t}=(\mathcal{V}_{t1},\ldots,\mathcal{V}_{td})
are independent and uniformly distributed, and compute the pseudo-observations
e_{n,jt}=\mathcal{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n},jt}(X_{jt},\mathcal{V}_{jt})
, where

\mathcal{H}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n},jt}(X_{jt},\mathcal{V}_{jt})=G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n},jt}(X_{jt}-)+\mathcal{V}_{jt}\left\{G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n},jt}(X_{jt})-G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n},jt}(X_{jt}-)\right\}.

Furthermore, extend the pseudo-observations
e_{n,jt}
in a circular way, i.e.,
e_{n,j,t+n}=e_{n,jt}
,
t\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

4.

Compute the statistics
S_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}
(according to Formula (
4.1
), where the ranks are defined in (
8
)),

\hat{r}_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}
, defined by (
15
), and
r_{\mathbf{K},n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}
, defined in Corollary
3
.

5.

Compute the test statistics
\mathcal{W}_{n},\mathcal{F}_{n},\mathcal{H}_{n},\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{K},n}
according to formulas (
17
)–(
18
). One can also compute
these statistics restricted to pairs, defined respectively by (
19
)–(
20
).

6.

The
P
-values of
\mathcal{W}_{n}
can be computed using the Edgeworth’s expansion of the first 6 cumulants, while the
P
-values of
S_{n,A,{\boldsymbol{\ell}}}
can be tabulated according to
n
. In addition, the limiting distributions of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
,
\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{K},n}
are chi-square distributions with
\displaystyle 2\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}|D_{A}|
degrees of freedom for
\mathcal{F}_{n}
and
\displaystyle\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}|D_{A}|
degrees of freedom for
\mathcal{H}_{n}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbf{K},n}
.

Remark 6

Note that Steps 4–6 are implemented in the CRAN package
IndGenErrors

(
Ghoudi et al.,, 2025
)
, so one only needs to compute the pseudo-observations
e_{n,jt}
using Steps 1–3.

5
Numerical experiments

This section presents two sets of numerical experiments. The first set of experiments deals with testing independence between generalized innovations of two time series while in the second set,
we consider the context of three time series.

5.1
Test of independence between the generalized innovations of two series

For testing for independence between the generalized errors of two time series

X_{1,t}
and
X_{2,t}
,
t=1,\ldots,n
, two data generating processes (DGP
1
and DGP
2
) were simulated.
The processes

\{X_{1,t}\}

and

\{X_{2,t}\}

have been chosen so that the degrees of dependence vary between the lagged generalized errors and that the univariate processes are generalized error models.
The two DGPs are described next, after the introduction of some definitions.

Definition 1

A process
\{X_{t}\}
is said to follow an AR(p)Z-Gaussian HMM with
J
regimes and covariates
\mathbf{Z}_{t}
(with first column
\equiv 1
), if the regime at time
t
,
\tau_{t}\in\{1,\ldots,J\}
, is a finite Markov chain with transition probability matrix
Q
, such that

\displaystyle Q_{j,k}=P(\tau_{t}=k|\mathcal{F}_{t-1},\tau_{t-1}=j)
,
j,k\in\{1,\ldots,J\}
, and if the conditional distribution of
X_{t}
given
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
and
\tau_{t}=j
is Gaussian with mean

\displaystyle\mu_{t,j}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_{i,j}X_{t-i}+{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}
and variance
\sigma_{j}^{2}
, or equivalently, the distribution of
(X_{t}-\mu_{t,j})/\sigma_{j}
given
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
and
\tau_{t}=j
is normal with mean
0
and variance
1
. Here
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
is the information (sigma-algebra) generated by
\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{t-1},X_{1},\ldots,X_{t-1},\mathbf{Z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{Z}_{t}\}
.
Note that, more generally, an AR(p)Z-G HMM is obtained if the distribution of
(X_{t}-\mu_{t,j})/\sigma_{j}
given
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
and
\tau_{t}=j
is
G
.

Next, a process
\{X_{t}\}
is said to follow an AR(p)Z-Poisson HMM with
J
regimes and covariate
\mathbf{Z}_{t}
(with first column
\equiv 1
), if the conditional distribution of
X_{t}
given
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
and
\tau_{t}=j
is a Poisson distribution with mean
\displaystyle\mu_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_{i,j}X_{t-i}+{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}_{t}
, with the restriction that all parameters and covariates are non negative.

Remark 7

Note that the theoretical values of Kendall’s tau, Spearman’s rho and van der Waerden coefficient are all
0
for the independence copula and the Tent Map copula
(
Rémillard,, 2013
)
. However, the theoretical Savage’s coefficient, defined by
\rho_{E}=-1+E\left\{\log(U)\log(V)\right\}
,
(U,V)\sim C
, is
0
for the Independence copula, but its value is

1-\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}+\frac{1}{2}\{\log{2}\}^{2}\approx 0.41776
for the Tent Map copula. If, instead, one takes the classical Savage’s coefficient, then

\rho_{E}=-1+E\left\{\log(1-U)\log(1-V)\right\}=1-\frac{\pi^{2}}{8}\approx-0.2337
for the Tent Map copula.

The results for DGP
1
are displayed in Table
2
for
\ell=0
. The results for
\ell=2
are similar so they are not displayed. These results show, in particular, that the empirical levels of the test statistics are quite close to the nominal levels, even for a sample size as low as
n=100
. For all dependence types, the test statistic
\mathcal{W}_{n}
has the best or close to the best empirical powers, with
\mathcal{W}_{n}
showing slightly better power than
\mathcal{F}_{n}
in all our simulations. Correlation type statistics
\mathcal{H}_{n}
,
\mathcal{H}_{S,n}
,
\mathcal{H}_{G,n}
and
\mathcal{H}_{E,n}
tend to have a good power in detecting correlated alternatives, but fail to detect alternatives for which their theoretical value is zero. In particular, among these correlation statistics only
\mathcal{H}_{E,n}
has some power in detecting the Tent map alternative. Note it is the only one with non-zero theoretical value under this alternative. As pointed in
Duchesne et al., (2012)
, when the dependence structure of the innovations exhibits correlation, the test statistic
\mathcal{H}_{n}
tends to be more powerful than
\mathcal{W}_{n}
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, confirming the importance of cross-correlations in empirical work. The statistics
\mathcal{H}_{S,n}
and
\mathcal{H}_{G,n}
have powers quite similar to those of
\mathcal{H}_{n}
, while
\mathcal{H}_{E,n}
is extremely powerful in detecting Clayton type dependence and much less powerful in detecting Frank type dependence. One also see that
\mathcal{H}_{G,n}
is slightly more powerful for detecting Gaussian type dependence. This confirms the finding of
Nasri and Rémillard, (2024)
, who showed that among
\mathcal{H}_{n}
,
\mathcal{H}_{S,n}
,
\mathcal{H}_{G,n}
and
\mathcal{H}_{E,n}
, the locally most powerful statistics is
\mathcal{H}_{S,n}
for Frank’s copula,
\mathcal{H}_{G,n}
for the Gaussian copula and
\mathcal{H}_{E,n}
for the Clayton copula.

We also note that the power levels for the test statistics
\mathcal{W}_{n}
,
\mathcal{F}_{n}
and
\mathcal{H}_{n}
are quite similar to those reported by
Duchesne et al., (2012)
for residuals of stochastic volatility models, i.e., the finite sample behavior of our test procedures are quite similar for both continuous errors or generalized error models. Note that, in general, the test based on
\mathcal{W}_{n}
is slightly better than the one based on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, which might be explained by a better approximation of the finite sample distribution. Moreover, the computation of the statistics
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is time consuming and requires many numerical integrations to evaluate the
P
-values appearing in its definition. Though, theoretically,

\mathcal{F}_{n}
represents the optimal way to combine our tests statistics, in practice
\mathcal{W}_{n}
is to be recommended for the simplicity of its computation and for superiority of its performance. The results also confirm that the power increases within a given copula family as the dependence parameter
\tau
increases.

\mathcal{V}_{t}

M

Test of independence between the generalized innovations of three time series

For testing the independence between three time series

\{X_{1,t}\}
,
\{X_{2,t}\}
and
\{X_{3,t}\}
,
t\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we generated data according to the following data generating process DGP3.

DGP
3
: Generate
(u_{t},v_{t},w_{t})
following a copula
C
and define

X_{1,t}=G_{t}^{-1}(u_{t})
where
G_{t}
is the cdf of a Poisson random variable with mean
\lambda_{t}=1+0.1X_{1,t-1}
,

X_{2,t}=0.5X_{2,t-1}+\varepsilon_{t}
, where
\varepsilon_{t}=\Phi^{-1}(v_{t})
, with
\Phi
the cdf of the standard normal distribution, and

X_{3,t}=0.5X_{3,t-1}+\eta_{t}
, where
\eta_{t}=\Phi^{-1}(w_{t})
.
The copula
C
is either the Independence copula, a Frank copula with
Kendall’s tau
\in\{0.1283,1/3\}
, a Gaussian copula with
Kendall’s tau
\in\{0.1283,1/3\}
, a Clayton copula with Kendall’s tau
\in\{0.1283,1/3\}
or
Romano’s copula
(
Romano and Siegel,, 1986
)
associated with
(U,V,W)
,

\displaystyle W=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\eta&\mbox{ if }(U-\frac{1}{2})(V-\frac{1}{2})(\eta-\frac{1}{2})\geq 0\\
1-\eta&\mbox{ if }(U-\frac{1}{2})(V-\frac{1}{2})(\eta-\frac{1}{2})<0\end{array}\right.
,
where
(U,V,\eta)
are independent uniform
(0,1)
random variables. Note that
(U,V,W)
are pairwise independent but not jointly independent.

Overall, one sees that when working with continuous generalized errors, the behavior of our tests statistics are almost identical to those reported in
Duchesne et al., (2012)
for continuous innovations. When discrete or non-continuous generalized errors are considered, then applying our test procedures with a single randomization has the advantage of producing distribution-free test statistics at the expense of a small loss of power. Averaging over

\xi_{t,A,B}(\mathbf{u},{\boldsymbol{\eta}})=\prod_{j\in B}\left\{J_{G_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},jt}}(u_{j})-J_{G_{jt}}(u_{j})-d_{G_{jt},G_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},jt}}(u_{j})\right\}\times\left\{\prod_{j\in A\setminus B}d_{G_{jt},G_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},jt}}(u_{j})\right\}\prod_{k\notin A}J_{G_{kt}}(X_{kt},u_{k})

and

\displaystyle\tilde{\delta}_{n,2,A,2}(\mathbf{u},{\boldsymbol{\eta}})=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\prod_{j\in A}d_{G_{jt},G_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}},jt}}(u_{j})\prod_{j\notin A}J_{G_{jt}}(u_{j})
.
Mimicking the same arguments as in the proof of
\tilde{\delta}_{n,1,1}^{j}
, one easily shows that
\tilde{\delta}_{n,2,A,1}
is a sequence of martingale differences which converges to zero in probability.
To complete the proof, note that
\displaystyle\left|\tilde{\delta}_{n,2,A,2}(\mathbf{u},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n})\right|\leq n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\prod_{j\in A}\left|d_{G_{jt},G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n},jt}}(u_{j})\right|\leq\Upsilon_{n}
, where

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{n}=n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}|d_{G_{j_{1},t},G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n},j_{1},t}}(u_{j_{1}})||d_{G_{j_{2},t},G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n},j_{2}},t}(u_{j_{2}})|
, where
j_{1}<j_{2}\in A
.
Using Proposition
2
in Appendix
A.2
,
one sees that
\Upsilon_{n}\leq\frac{9}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\|G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n},jt}-G_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0},jt}\|^{2}=o_{P}(1)
,
by Assumptions (A5’) and
(A0)
. Finally, the convergence of
\tilde{}\mathbb{K}_{n,A}
is similar to the convergence of
\tilde{\beta}_{n,A}
.

Appendix C
Examples of dependence measures when the margins are not continuous

Suppose
(U_{1},U_{2})\sim C
and
(V_{1},V_{2})\sim\Pi
are independent. Set
U_{i}^{\star}=F_{i}(X_{i}-)+V_{i}\Delta F_{i}(X_{i})
, where
X_{i}=F_{i}^{-1}(U_{i})
,
i\in\{1,2\}
. Then,

\displaystyle\rho_{K_{1},K_{2},F_{1},F_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm cor}\left\{K_{1}^{-1}\left(U_{1}^{\star}\right),K_{2}^{-1}\left(U_{2}^{\star}\right)\right\}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\left\{K_{1}^{-1}(u_{1})-\mu_{1}\right\}\left\{K_{2}^{-1}(u_{2})-\mu_{2}\right\}dC^{\maltese}(u_{1},u_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\left\{\mathcal{K}_{1,G_{1}}\circ F_{1}^{-1}(u_{1})-\mu_{1}\right\}\left\{\mathcal{K}_{2,F_{2}}\circ F_{2}^{-1}(u_{2})-\mu_{2}\right\}dC(u_{1},u_{2}).

If
F_{2}
is continuous and
F_{1}
is Bernoulli with parameter
1-p
, i.e.,
F_{1}(0)=p
, then one gets

\displaystyle\rho_{K_{1},K_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho_{K_{1},K_{2},F_{1},F_{2}}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}\left[\frac{1}{p}\int_{0}^{p}\left\{K_{1}^{-1}(s)-\mu_{1}\right\}ds\right]E\left[\boldsymbol{1}\{U_{1}\leq p\}\left\{K_{2}^{-1}(U_{2})-\mu_{2}\right\}\right],

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}\left[\frac{1}{1-p}\int_{p}^{1}\left\{K_{1}^{-1}(s)-\mu_{1}\right\}ds\right]E\left[\boldsymbol{1}\{U_{1}>p\}\left\{K_{2}^{-1}(U_{2})-\mu_{2}\right\}\right].

Suppose
C
is the Tent Map copula and set
H_{j}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\left\{K_{j}^{-1}(u)-\mu_{j}\right\}du
,
j\in\{1,2\}
. Then,

\rho_{K_{1},K_{2}}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}\frac{H_{1}(p)H_{2}(2p)}{2p(1-p)}
if
0<p\leq\frac{1}{2}
, and

\rho_{K_{1},K_{2}}=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}\frac{H_{1}(p)H_{2}(2(1-p))}{2p(1-p)}
, if
\frac{1}{2}\leq p<1
.
Since
H_{j}(1)=0
by hypothesis, it follows that
\rho_{K_{1},K_{2}}=0
when
p=1/2
.
In particular, for Spearman’s rho,
H(u)=-\frac{u(1-u)}{2}
, so if
p\leq\frac{1}{2}
,
\rho_{S}=6p\left(\frac{1}{2}-p\right)
and if
p\geq\frac{1}{2}
,
\rho_{S}=-6(1-p)\left(p-\frac{1}{2}\right)
. For Savage’s coefficient,
H(u)=u\log{u}
, so
\rho_{E}=\frac{p\log{p}\log{(2p)}}{1-p}
if
0<p\leq\frac{1}{2}
, and if
\frac{1}{2}\leq p<1
,

\rho_{E}=-\log{p}\log{(2(1-p))}
.

Appendix D
Examples of checking assumptions for time series

D.1
ARMA(p,q)

For simplicity, consider an
ARMA(1,1)
process
Y
, defined by
Y_{t}-\mu-\phi(Y_{t-1}-\mu)=\varepsilon_{t}-\theta\varepsilon_{t-1}
, with
\max(|\phi|,|\theta|)<1
.
Then, for
t\geq 1
,
\varepsilon_{t}=Y_{t}-\mu+(\theta-\phi)\sum_{k=1}^{t-1}\theta^{k-1}(Y_{t-k}-\mu)+\theta^{t}Z_{0}
, where
Z_{0}=(\theta-\phi)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\theta^{k-1}(Y_{-k}-\mu)
.
As a result, if
\tilde{Y}
is the time series defined by the previous equation with
\tilde{Z}_{0}=0
, then

\left|\tilde{Y}_{t}-Y_{t}\right|\leq|\theta|^{t}|Z_{0}
, which tends to
0
exponentially fast. Next, if the cdf
G
of
\varepsilon_{t}
has a bounded and continuous density, then

\left|P\left(Y_{t}\leq y|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right)-P\left(\tilde{Y}_{t}\leq y|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right)\right|\leq|\theta|^{t}|Z_{0}|
, and

G_{t}(y)=P\left(Y_{t}\leq y|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right)=G\left\{y-\mu+(\theta-\phi)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\theta^{k-1}\left(Y_{t-k}-\mu\right)\right\}.

As a result,

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}G_{t}\left\{G_{t}^{-1}(u)\right\}=-g\circ G^{-1}(u)\left(\frac{1-\phi}{1-\theta}\right)
,
\displaystyle\partial_{\phi}G_{t}\left\{G_{t}^{-1}(u)\right\}=-g\circ G^{-1}(u)\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\theta^{k-1}\left(Y_{t-k}-\mu\right)\right\}
, and
\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}G_{t}\left\{G_{t}^{-1}(u)\right\}=g\circ G^{-1}(u)\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\theta^{k-2}\left(\theta+(k-1)(\theta-\phi)\right)\left(Y_{t-k}-\mu\right)\right\}
. Hence,
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{t}(u)
is continuous on
[0,1]
, stationary and ergodic, and
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}(u)=-g\circ G^{-1}(u)\left(\frac{1-\phi}{1-\theta}\right)\mathbf{e}_{1}
, where
\mathbf{e}_{1}=(1,0,0)^{\top}
. For an ARMA(p,q) model with
\phi(z)=1-\sum_{k=1}^{p}\phi_{k}z^{k}
and
\theta(z)=1-\sum_{k=1}^{q}\theta_{k}z^{k}
, where the zeros of
\phi(z)
and
\theta(z)
are outside the unit ball, then

{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}(u)=-g\circ G^{-1}(u)\frac{\phi(1)}{\theta(1)}(1,0,\ldots,0)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p+q+1}
.

D.2
AR(p)-Poisson

Another interesting example is an
AR(1)
-Poisson process, where the conditional distribution of
X_{t}
given the past is Poisson, with mean
\lambda_{t-1}=\mu+\phi X_{t-1}
,
\mu>0
, and
0\leq\beta<1
. Note that if
F_{\lambda}
is the cdf of a Poisson distribution with mean
\lambda
, with density
f_{\lambda}
, then
F_{\lambda}(y)
is strictly decreasing in
\lambda
, because
\partial_{\lambda}F_{\lambda}(y)=-f_{\lambda}(y)
. As a result,
F_{\lambda}^{-1}(u)
is non-decreasing in
\lambda
, and if
U_{t}
is an i.i.d. sequence of uniform variates on
(0,1)
, then, for
t\geq 1
,
Y_{t}=F_{\lambda_{t-1}}(U_{t})\geq\tilde{Y}_{t}=F_{\tilde{\lambda}_{t-1}}(U_{t})
, where
\tilde{Y}_{0}=0
,
\tilde{\lambda}_{t-1}=\mu+\phi\tilde{Y}_{t-1}
, and
\tilde{\lambda}_{0}=\mu\leq\lambda_{0}
,
t\geq 1
. Next,
E\left(Y_{t}-\tilde{Y}_{t}\right)=\phi^{t}E(Y_{0})
. As a result, by Borel-Cantelli’s lemma, if
0<\phi<\alpha 1
, then

P\left(Y_{t}-\tilde{Y}_{t}\geq\alpha^{t}\right)\leq\left(\frac{\phi}{\alpha}\right)^{t}E(Y_{0})
, so
P\left(Y_{t}-\tilde{Y}_{t}\geq\alpha^{t}\text{ i.o.}\right)=0
, showing that with probability
1
,
0\leq Y_{t}-\tilde{Y}_{t}\leq\alpha^{t}
eventually. Similar computations can be performed for an AR(p)-Poisson, showing that stating points are not important and that the convergence of

Y_{t}-\tilde{Y}_{t}
to
0
is exponentially fast.

D.3
Gaussian HMM

According to
Nasri et al.,
2020a ()
or
Nasri et al., (2024)
, for a Gaussian Hidden Markov model with
r
regimes, the conditional distribution of the process
Y_{t}
verifies
\displaystyle G_{t}(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\Phi\left(\frac{y-\mu_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}\right)Q_{\tau_{t-1},j}
, where
\Phi
(resp.
\phi
) is the cdf (resp. pdf) of a standard Gaussian variable, where
\tau_{t}
is the process of regimes with values in
\{1,\ldots,r\}
and transition matrix
Q
. It is assumed that
\tau_{t}
is stationary and ergodic, with stationary distribution
\pi
. Hence,
G_{t}
is stationary and
G_{t}
converges to
\displaystyle G_{\infty}(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\pi_{j}\Phi\left(\frac{y-\mu_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}\right)
.
It follows from the calculations in Remark
3
that
G_{t}^{-1}
is also stationary. Next, for simplicity, take
r=2
, so the parameters are
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},Q_{11},Q_{21}
.
As a result,
\partial_{\mu_{j}}G_{t}(y)=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}}\phi\left(\frac{y-\mu_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}\right)Q_{\tau_{t-1},j}
,
\partial_{\sigma_{j}}G_{t}(y)=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}^{2}}\phi\left(\frac{y-\mu_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}\right)Q_{\tau_{t-1},j}
,
\partial_{Q_{11}}G_{t}(y)=\boldsymbol{1}\{\tau_{t-1}=1\}\left\{\Phi\left(\frac{y-\mu_{1}}{\sigma_{1}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{y-\mu_{2}}{\sigma_{2}}\right)\right\}
, and

\partial_{Q_{21}}G_{t}(y)=\boldsymbol{1}\{\tau_{t-1}=2\}\left\{\Phi\left(\frac{y-\mu_{1}}{\sigma_{1}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{y-\mu_{2}}{\sigma_{2}}\right)\right\}
. It then follows that
\dot{G}_{t}
is stationary. It follows that
\dot{G}_{t}(y)
is Lipschitz in
Y
, having a bounded continuous density. Since
G_{t}^{-1}(u)\to G_{\infty}^{-1}(u)
, it follows that
\left\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{t}(u)-\dot{G}_{t}\circ G_{\infty}^{-1}(u)\right\|\leq c\left|G_{t}^{-1}(u)-G_{\infty}^{-1}(u)\right|
. By ergodicity, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{t}(u)-\dot{G}_{t}\circ G_{\infty}^{-1}(u)\right\|\leq\frac{c}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left|G_{t}^{-1}(u)-G_{\infty}^{-1}(u)\right|\to 0
.
