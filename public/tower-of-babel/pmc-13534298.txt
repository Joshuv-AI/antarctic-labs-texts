Emerging Indices Outperform Traditional Fibrosis Scores in Young Metabolic Dysfunction‐Associated Steatotic Liver Disease Across Divergent Screening Goals
Authors: Ahlim Lee, Chang In Han, Keungmo Yang, Hyun Yang, Pil Soo Sung, Si Hyun Bae, Jaejun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAlthough numerous noninvasive indices have been proposed to assess significant fibrosis (SF), none have been validated specifically in young adults with metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). This study evaluated their performance in younger patients across two cohorts with distinct screening goals.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed two MASLD cohorts: a primary care (PC) cohort (n = 972) and a tertiary care (TC) cohort (n = 342). SF was defined as liver stiffness ≥ 7.0 kPa on vibration‐controlled transient elastography. The diagnostic performance of emerging indices—fibrosis‐8 index, steatosis‐associated fibrosis estimator, metabolic‐associated fibrosis score‐5—was compared with traditional scores—fibrosis‐4 index, aspartate aminotransferase‐to‐platelet ratio index, and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease fibrosis score.ResultsAcross both settings, emerging indices consistently outperformed traditional scores for SF discrimination. The areas under the receiver operating characteristic curves were higher for the emerging indices than for the traditional indices in both cohorts. Decision curve analyses demonstrated that emerging indices provided greater net clinical benefit and larger reductions in unnecessary procedures, with thresholds differing by setting such that lower thresholds favored referral in the PC cohort, whereas higher thresholds were more appropriate for biopsy decisions in the TC cohort. Conventional cutoffs widely used in practice demonstrated limited sensitivity in this population, suggesting that refinement may be required.ConclusionsTraditional noninvasive fibrosis indices showed limited performance in young adults with MASLD, whereas emerging indices appeared more suitable for achieving the screening goals of referral in PC and biopsy decision‐making in TC.Keywords: fibrosis, metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease, noninvasive index, young adults

Introduction

The prevalence of metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) in Korea is increasing, reported at 30% to 40% in recent publications [1, 2], consistent with the global trend [3]. Notably, MASLD is also prevalent and rising among young adults, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 16% [4], and is predicted to reach 44% by 2035 [1].

MASLD represents a wide spectrum of hepatic pathology, from isolated steatosis to steatohepatitis, progressive fibrosis, and cirrhosis [5, 6]. Recent evidence indicates that liver‐related mortality is driven predominantly by the presence of significant fibrosis (SF) rather than by metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH) per se [7, 8]. For this reason, identifying patients with MASLD at risk of liver fibrosis is the primary objective. Although liver biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing MASH and accurately staging liver fibrosis, its invasive nature, potential complications, and limited patient acceptability make it impractical for routine use in the large and growing MASLD population.

The development and use of noninvasive fibrosis tests (NITs), such as the fibrosis‐4 index (FIB‐4), aspartate aminotransferase‐to‐platelet ratio index (APRI), and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) fibrosis score (NFS), have significantly reduced the need for liver biopsy [9, 10, 11]. Their utility has been demonstrated in various guidelines, such as the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) [12], the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) [13], and the Korean Association for the Study of the Liver (KASL) [14], and they are used as tools to identify high‐risk patients and guide biopsy decisions. Meanwhile, recent studies have shown that emerging NIT scores, such as the fibrosis‐8 index (FIB‐8), metabolic‐associated fibrosis score‐5 (MAF‐5), and steatosis‐associated fibrosis estimator (SAFE), are superior to preexisting NITs [15, 16, 17]. Because of the low prevalence of SF in MASLD patients, these tests, with a high negative predictive value, can be used to guide referrals to the hepatology department and to provide an efficient means to improve outcomes [18]. Nonetheless, existing validation studies of these indices have largely focused on middle‐aged and older populations, thereby constraining generalizability to younger patients with MASLD [19, 20]. Consequently, further evaluation in younger populations is warranted.

In this study, we assessed the accuracy and efficacy of the NITs for predicting liver fibrosis in young adults with MASLD. We further compared emerging and preexisting NITs in their ability to guide clinical decision‐making, including whether to refer patients from primary care (PC) centers to tertiary care (TC) centers or to perform a liver biopsy at TC centers using diagnostic accuracy metrics and decision curve analysis (DCA). Lastly, we sought to assess the suitability of existing NIT thresholds for young adults and to propose alternative thresholds.

Methods

Patients

We conducted a retrospective study using data selected consecutively from patients who visited Armed Forces Goyang Hospital (AFGH) between July 2022 and February 2024 and Eunpyeong St. Mary's Hospital (ESMH) between April 2019 and July 2025. We enrolled 1314 patients aged 18 to 35 years who were diagnosed with MASLD based on abdominal ultrasonography, including 972 from the AFGH and 342 from ESMH. Given that AFGH primarily serves active‐duty military personnel undergoing routine health examinations, it was designated a community‐based PC cohort, whereas ESMH, a university‐affiliated institution, was designated a TC. Clinical information, medical history, and laboratory analyses, including complete blood count, routine biochemical tests, and vibration‐controlled transient elastography (VCTE), were obtained. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients diagnosed with MASLD based on abdominal ultrasonography, (2) patients aged 18–35 years, and (3) patients who underwent VCTE for liver stiffness measurement (LSM) during the study period. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) insufficient clinical data and (2) transaminase level above twice the upper normal range limit due to concern for overestimation of VCTE [21].

This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Korean Armed Forces Medical Command (AFMC 2024‐03‐005) and the Catholic University of Korea (PC25RISI0187). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the requirement for informed consent was waived because of its retrospective nature.

Definition of MASLD

MASLD was defined as hepatic steatosis on abdominal ultrasonography, in conjunction with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor [22]. These risk factors included the following: (1) body mass index (BMI) ≥ 23 kg/m2 or waist circumference (WC) ≥ 90 cm for males and ≥ 85 cm for females [23]; (2) fasting serum glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL or hemoglobin A1c ≥ 5.7% or a prior diagnosis of type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM) or ongoing antidiabetic therapy; (3) blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or current use of antihypertensive agents; (4) hypertriglyceridemia (≥ 150 mg/dL) or treatment with lipid‐lowering medications; and (5) reduced high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (≤ 40 mg/dL for males and ≤ 50 mg/dL for females) or use of lipid‐lowering medications.

Assessment of Liver Fibrosis

The stage of liver fibrosis was assessed through LSM using VCTE (FibroScan; Echosens). All participants were instructed to fast for at least 8 h before the examination. LSM was obtained from the right hepatic lobe via an intercostal approach, with patients in the supine position and the right arm in maximal abduction [24]. The M probe was applied in individuals with a BMI < 30 kg/m2, whereas the XL probe was utilized in those with a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 [25]. For each participant, 10 valid measurements were obtained, and the median was used to represent hepatic elasticity. Only examinations yielding 10 successful acquisitions were included in the analysis. Liver stiffness values were reported in kilopascals (kPa).

The cut‐off values for fibrosis were determined based on previous studies and widely accepted standards. SF (F2) and advanced fibrosis (AF) (F3) were defined as LSM values ≥ 7.0 and ≥ 8.7 kPa, respectively [26]. Sensitivity analyses were performed with alternative thresholds: ≥ 8.0 and ≥ 9.0 kPa for SF and AF, respectively [27]. The time period between assessment of the LSM and collection of clinical laboratory data for liver fibrosis markers was within 1 month.

Noninvasive Fibrosis Indices

Several NITs have been validated in patients with NAFLD from simple and readily available clinical tests. Six NITs were included for the assessment of predictive performance, namely, FIB‐4 [28], APRI [29], NFS [30], FIB‐8 [17], MAF‐5 [16], and SAFE [15] score. Among them, FIB‐4, APRI, and NFS, which were developed earlier, were classified as traditional indices, whereas FIB‐8, MAF‐5, and the SAFE score, which were developed more recently, were classified as emerging indices. In this study, all six indices were assessed in the PC cohort, whereas MAF‐5 could not be evaluated in the TC cohort due to missing WC data. The formulas for all scoring systems are summarized in Table S1.

Histologically Confirmed MASLD Cohort

For sensitivity analysis using histologic fibrosis staging, we additionally identified MASLD patients at ESMH who underwent percutaneous liver biopsy during the study period (n = 61). Fibrosis stage was histologically assessed using the NASH Clinical Research Network scoring system [31].

Different Screening Goals Across Care Settings

Different screening objectives for SF were assumed across healthcare settings. PC institutions were considered to perform fibrosis screening primarily to identify patients requiring referral to TC institutions, whereas TC institutions aimed to identify candidates for liver biopsy. Accordingly, DCA was applied to compare the clinical utility of NITs according to each screening objective. Under this framework, clinical utility was quantified using the concept of net benefit, which integrates the benefits and harms of a diagnostic strategy by weighting the harm of an intervention according to a given threshold probability. The threshold probability represents the probability at which a clinician would opt for intervention after balancing the expected benefit against potential harm. Net benefit was estimated across a range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities and illustrated using DCA and net reduction‐in‐intervention plots, in accordance with the methodology proposed by Vickers and Elkin [32].

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were compared between groups using Student's t‐test and are presented as means with standard deviations. Categorical variables were evaluated using either the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, depending on the sample size. Linear regression models were applied to assess associations among steatosis indices, and Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to quantify the strength of these relationships. Correlations between NITs and LSM were classified as strong when the correlation coefficient (r) exceeded 0.6 and as moderate when r ranged from 0.4 to 0.6. Diagnostic performance was visualized using receiver operating characteristic curves. Comparisons of the area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) curves were performed using DeLong's test. AUROC values of 0.7–0.8 were interpreted as indicating fair accuracy, 0.8–0.9 as good accuracy, and > 0.9 as excellent accuracy. To determine optimal cutoff values and strengthen internal validity, Youden's J statistic was applied alongside bootstrap resampling and repeated fivefold cross‐validation. Bootstrap resampling was performed using 2000 samples with replacement, each equal in size to the original cohort, with the Youden‐optimal cutoff independently identified in each resample; the distribution of 2000 cutoffs provided 95% CIs. For repeated fivefold cross‐validation (100 repeats), data were partitioned into five folds; the Youden‐optimal cutoff was derived from the training fold (4/5 of data) and applied to the held‐out test fold (1/5 of data), with sensitivity and specificity averaged across all 500 fold‐evaluations (5 folds × 100 repeats) to provide an out‐of‐sample performance estimate with reduced overfitting bias. A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.0.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; http://cran.r‐project.org, accessed September 3, 2025).

The mean age was younger, and the male proportion was higher in the PC cohort than in the TC cohort, reflecting the specificity of the Korean army group. Comparison between the PC and TC cohorts revealed that the TC cohort patients were older (28.9 vs. 23.7 years, p < 0.001) and had higher BMI (29.2 vs. 26.6 kg/m2, p < 0.001). The TC cohort patients had a higher prevalence of DM (42.7 vs. 4.1%, p < 0.001) than the PC cohort. Laboratory findings indicative of inflammation and liver function, such as white blood cell (WBC) count, C‐reactive protein, total bilirubin, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), gamma‐glutamyl transferase, albumin levels, and prothrombin time, were higher in the TC cohort patients than in the PC cohort. In terms of the controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) score and LSM, the TC group had higher scores than the PC group (CAP: 292.7 vs. 261.1 dB/m, p < 0.001; LSM: 6.4 vs. 4.9 kPa, p < 0.001, respectively).

Further analyses of baseline characteristics were performed according to the presence or absence of SF, as defined by a VCTE cutoff of 7.0 kPa. In both cohorts, the group with SF had a higher diagnosis rate of DM, higher BMI, WBC count, AST, ALT, the CAP score, and a lower high‐density lipoprotein level than the group without SF (Tables S2 and S3). In the PC cohort, the group with SF was older (25.3 vs. 23.6 years, p = 0.038), had longer WC (104.1 vs. 90.1 cm, p < 0.001), and higher diagnosis rate of hypertension and dyslipidemia than the group without SF (Table S2).

Comparison of Noninvasive Indices Depending on the Presence of SF

For each of the six NITs, differences in score distributions were examined by SF presence. Figure S1 illustrates the median score of each NIT depending on the presence of SF, through the violin plot in the PC cohort. There are significant differences in median scores between the two groups with or without liver fibrosis in emerging NITs, including the FIB‐8 index, SAFE score, and MAF‐5. In contrast, the traditional indices FIB‐4, APRI, and NFS did not show significant differences between the two groups. Comparison of Performance Among Noninvasive Fibrosis Indices

All NITs were assessed for their predictive performance in identifying liver fibrosis. Figure 2 shows the AUROC of each index for SF (≥ F2) and AF (≥ F3). Panels (A, C) and (B, D) represent the AUROC values for the PC and TC cohorts, respectively. For SF in the PC cohort (Figure 2A), MAF‐5 displayed the highest AUROC value (0.812), followed by SAFE (0.766), FIB‐8 (0.735), APRI (0.659), NFS (0.608), and FIB‐4 (0.528). For determining SF in the TC cohort, the AUROC values are 0.719 for SAFE, followed by FIB‐8 (0.691), APRI (0.635), NFS (0.621), and FIB‐4 (0.572) (Figure 2B). For AF in the PC cohort, SAFE represents the highest AUROC value (0.860), followed by MAF‐5 (0.844), FIB‐8 (0.834), APRI (0.689), NFS (0.604), and FIB‐4 (0.576) (Figure 2C). In the TC cohort for AF, the AUROC values are 0.709 for SAFE, followed by FIB‐8 (0.704), APRI (0.642), NFS (0.634), and FIB‐4 (0.585) (Figure 2D). The analyses for the pooled cohort were depicted in Figure S3. Overall results show that the predictivity for liver fibrosis is higher in emerging NITs than in traditional NITs. Similarly, MAF‐5, SAFE, and FIB‐8 showed significantly higher AUC values than FIB‐4 for predicting AF. In the TC cohort, SAFE (p = 0.007), FIB‐8 (p = 0.004), and APRI (p = 0.012) had significantly higher AUCs than FIB‐4 for predicting SF. Correlation Between LSM by VCTE and Noninvasive Fibrosis Indices

Next, the linear correlations between NITs and LSM obtained by VCTE were assessed (Figure S5). In the PC cohort, MAF‐5 displayed the strongest correlation coefficient (r = 0.116), followed by SAFE (r = 0.050), FIB‐8 (r = 0.038), and APRI (r = 0.017), all of which were statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Figure S5A). In the TC cohort, FIB‐8 showed the strongest correlation coefficient (r = 0.035), followed by SAFE (r = 0.026), NFS (r = 0.015), and FIB‐4 (r = 0.009), all of which reached statistical significance (p < 0.05) (Figure S5B). Although the absolute magnitudes of these correlations were modest, emerging NITs showed numerically higher correlations with LSM than traditional indices; however, these differences should be interpreted with caution given the limited effect sizes.

Subgroup Analysis

Subgroup analyses of the diagnostic performance of NITs for predicting SF were performed (Figure S6). The AUROCs for FIB‐4, APRI, FIB‐8, NFS, SAFE, and MAF‐5 were compared across subgroups stratified by BMI (≥ 30 vs. < 30 kg/m2) and age (≥ 25 vs. < 25 years). In the PC cohort, the emerging indices generally demonstrated higher AUCs than the traditional indices across all subgroups, except in participants with BMI < 30 kg/m2, where APRI (0.671) had a higher AUC than the FIB‐8 index (0.629) (Figure S6A). In the TC cohort, the emerging indices also achieved higher AUCs than the traditional indices, except in the subgroup with BMI < 30 kg/m2, where APRI (0.721) had a higher AUC than the FIB‐8 index (0.685) (Figure S6B).

Sex‐stratified analyses were performed in the full and TC cohorts, whereas formal assessment in the PC cohort was limited by the very small number of female SF events (n = 1) (Table S4). The relative performance of NITs was generally consistent across sexes, with no significant sex‐by‐NIT interaction observed for FIB‐8 or SAFE.

Performance Among Noninvasive Fibrosis Indices in the Histologically Confirmed Cohort

NIT performance was further assessed in a biopsy‐proven MASLD cohort (Figure S7; baseline characteristics in Table S5). Within this histologically confirmed subgroup, emerging indices showed numerically higher AUROCs than traditional indices—FIB‐8 (0.772), SAFE (0.757), APRI (0.704), FIB‐4 (0.677), and NFS (0.653)—although these differences did not reach statistical significance, likely reflecting the limited number of fibrosis events (n = 12) (Table S6). No statistically significant AUROC differences were observed between the biopsy‐proven and the full cohort for any NIT (Table S7). Overall, the direction of the findings was generally consistent with the main analysis, although the rank order varied across cohorts.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis was conducted using an alternative cutoff for SF (LSM ≥ 8.0 kPa) (Figure S8). In the PC cohort, MAF‐5 displayed the highest AUROC value (0.816), followed by SAFE (0.801), FIB‐8 (0.760), APRI (0.666), NFS (0.655), and FIB‐4 (0.591) (Figure S8A). In the TC cohort, the AUROCs are 0.713 for SAFE, followed by FIB‐8 (0.687), APRI (0.654), NFS (0.645), and FIB‐4 (0.577) (Figure S8B). Overall, predictive performance for SF was higher in emerging NITs than in traditional NITs, regardless of the VCTE‐defined SF cutoff.

DCA and Net Benefit

DCA was performed to compare the standardized net benefit of NITs across varying risk thresholds (Figure 3). Standardized net benefit curves are presented for the PC cohort (Figure 3A) and the TC cohort (Figure 3B). Table 3 summarizes the changes in net benefit and net reduction values at each threshold. In the PC cohort, the highest net benefit and net reduction were observed in MAF‐5, followed by SAFE, FIB‐8, APRI, NFS, and FIB‐4. Emerging indices consistently yielded higher net benefits across a broader range of thresholds than traditional fibrosis indices, with pronounced differences at threshold probabilities of 0.05–0.15. Similar patterns of net benefit and net reduction were observed in the TC cohort. The SAFE and FIB‐8 scores demonstrated higher net benefits and net reductions with a wider range of thresholds than APRI, FIB‐4, and NFS, especially between 0.20 and 0.35. Notably, the TC cohort demonstrated higher net benefit and net reduction values at higher threshold probabilities than the PC cohort, suggesting that tertiary settings may operate at higher decision thresholds for fibrosis screening.

Thresholds for Noninvasive Indices in Determining SF in Young Adults

Table 4 summarizes the diagnostic performance of established and bootstrap‐derived Youden‐optimal cutoffs, including sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, overall accuracy, and likelihood ratios. In the PC cohort, all established NITs except MAF‐5 demonstrated insufficient sensitivity, indicating that the currently established rule‐out thresholds were suboptimal for diagnostic purposes. Consequently, Youden's J statistic was applied to determine optimal cutoff values. Internal validation via bootstrap resampling and repeated fivefold cross‐validation confirmed the reproducibility of the proposed cutoffs for most NITs. Application of the Youden‐optimal cutoffs substantially improved diagnostic accuracy for SF prediction compared with established thresholds, with sensitivity increasing for FIB‐4 (0.0%–63.0%), APRI (0.0%–68.9%), FIB‐8 (15.6%–83.2%), NFS (0.0%–65.9%), and SAFE (8.9%–83.0%).

Estimated Impact on Referrals and Biopsies Per 1000 Patients

To further translate cutoff‐based findings into expected clinical consequences, we estimated the number of referrals or biopsies, detected SF cases, missed SF cases, and unnecessary procedures per 1000 patients across care setting scenarios (Table S8).

In the PC referral scenario, established cutoffs for traditional indices detected few or no SF cases, missing nearly all expected cases. In contrast, bootstrap‐derived Youden‐derived cutoffs for emerging indices substantially improved SF detection; FIB‐8 detected 37.0 of the 46.3 expected SF cases per 1000 patients, albeit at the cost of 374.0 unnecessary referrals. SAFE demonstrated a similar detection profile with fewer unnecessary referrals, whereas MAF‐5 offered a more balanced trade‐off by reducing unnecessary referrals while maintaining moderate SF detection.

In the TC biopsy scenario, where SF prevalence was higher, the trade‐off shifted toward minimizing unnecessary biopsies. Nevertheless, established cutoffs missed a substantial proportion of SF cases, particularly for traditional indices (NFS, FIB‐4, and APRI: 187.1–204.7 missed cases per 1000 patients). SAFE reduced unnecessary biopsies to 120.8 per 1000 patients—an 84.4% reduction compared with a biopsy‐all strategy—but still missed 112.6 SF cases. FIB‐8 identified more SF cases than SAFE but required more unnecessary biopsies. These findings support the need for care setting‐specific thresholds that balance SF detection against unnecessary procedures according to the intended clinical action.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated and compared the predictive performance of six NITs—APRI, NFS, FIB‐4, FIB‐8, MAF‐5, and SAFE—for identifying SF among young MASLD patients aged 35 or younger. The emerging NITs, such as MAF‐5, SAFE, and FIB‐8, demonstrated higher AUROC values than traditional NITs, regardless of cohort or fibrosis set point. Our study validated the net benefit and net reduction values of these indices using DCA. This analysis found that emerging indices consistently yielded higher net benefits across a broader range of thresholds than traditional indices. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the diagnostic performance of these indices specifically in individuals aged 18–35 years.

The prevalence of MASLD among young adults is increasing [4], and identifying SF is crucial to preventing liver‐related mortality [7, 8]. Previous studies have demonstrated that NITs, such as FIB‐4, APRI, and NFS, can serve as practical alternatives to liver biopsy for assessing liver fibrosis [9, 10, 11, 28, 29, 30]. Accordingly, these indices have been incorporated into prognostic and risk‐stratification models across diverse liver diseases, leading major clinical guidelines to recommend the use of markers to identify patients at risk of advanced disease [33, 34, 35]. However, the diagnostic performance of these indices has been variable across studies, with particularly reduced accuracy reported in younger populations [19, 20]. More recently, several studies have suggested that emerging NITs, including FIB‐8, MAF‐5, and SAFE, show favorable performance in predicting liver fibrosis [15, 16, 17], findings consistent with those of the present study. In our analysis, AUROCs for each NIT in predicting SF and AF showed that emerging indices consistently outperformed traditional indices across individual cohorts and in the pooled cohort. In contrast, the AUROCs of traditional NITs remained below 0.7 across cohorts and liver stiffness cutoffs, suggesting limited clinical acceptability in this specific age group [36].

The clinical implications of applying NITs across different screening settings were further evaluated using DCA. In this analysis, emerging indices were associated with higher net benefits and greater net reductions across a broader range of threshold probabilities than traditional fibrosis indices, suggesting superior clinical utility in these screening scenarios. Notably, the threshold probability range differed between cohorts: 0.05–0.15 in the PC cohort and from 0.20 to 0.35 in the TC cohort. This divergence likely reflects the distinct screening objectives of each setting, namely, referral to TC in PC institutions and decision‐making regarding liver biopsy in TC institutions. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate differences in threshold probability according to these screening goals in young MASLD patients. While traditional indices have limited utility for predicting liver fibrosis in young adults with MASLD, emerging indices showed greater clinical utility than traditional indices within the evaluated screening scenarios.

Our study also demonstrated that the established cutoffs of commonly used NITs were suboptimal for discriminating SF in young adults with MASLD, largely due to low sensitivity across both cohorts. Clinical impact analysis further clarified this limitation, showing that conventional thresholds translated into a substantial number of missed SF cases. In the PC referral scenario, established cutoffs for traditional indices detected few or no SF cases, missing nearly all expected cases. Even in the TC biopsy scenario, where SF prevalence was higher, established cutoffs continued to miss a substantial proportion of SF cases, particularly for traditional indices. Although conservative thresholds may reduce unnecessary referrals or biopsies, this apparent benefit should be interpreted with caution, as it may come at the expense of missed SF. When cutoffs were refined using Youden's J statistic, diagnostic performance improved, revealing a substantial discrepancy between established thresholds and cohort‐specific optimal values. This discordance was observed not only in traditional indices but also in emerging NITs and was consistently present in both PC and TC settings. These findings suggest that currently recommended thresholds may not adequately reflect the fibrosis risk profile of young MASLD patients, highlighting the need for either tailored cutoff values or the development of NITs specifically derived for this population.

This study has several notable strengths. First, it specifically focused on young adults with MASLD, a population that has been underrepresented in previous fibrosis validation studies, allowing for a more accurate assessment of NIT performance in this distinct clinical group. Second, by comparing two cohorts from primary and tertiary care settings, this study evaluated the robustness and generalizability of emerging NITs across different screening contexts, demonstrating their consistent superiority over traditional indices. Third, the inclusion of a histologically confirmed MASLD cohort enabled validation of these findings against a pathological reference standard, reinforcing the consistency of results observed in liver stiffness‐based analyses.

Nevertheless, our study also has several limitations. First, each cohort was derived from a single center and comprised relatively homogeneous demographic characteristics, which may limit the generalizability of the study results. In addition, the male‐predominant composition, particularly in the PC cohort, constrained sex‐stratified analyses. Although the overall NIT ranking was largely preserved across sexes and FIB‐8 and SAFE demonstrated no sex‐by‐NIT interaction, these results are limited by imbalanced sex composition and data unavailability, and further validation in adequately powered, sex‐balanced cohorts is warranted. Furthermore, hepatic fibrosis was assessed using LSM by VCTE rather than liver biopsy, which remains the diagnostic gold standard. Although LSM has demonstrated strong predictive capability for hepatic fibrosis [11], incorporating histological assessment could further strengthen the robustness of the results. However, further analysis of histologically confirmed MASLD patients demonstrated consistency with LSM‐diagnosed hepatic fibrosis, supporting the reliability of this study. Finally, the MAF‐5 score was not assessed in the TC cohort because WC data were unavailable. Further research is needed on MAF‐5 among young adults with MASLD in the TC cohort.

In conclusion, our study showed that emerging indices outperform traditional NITs in young MASLD patients across divergent screening goals. Additionally, results from this study suggest that refined cutoff values or the development of NITs specifically derived for young populations may be necessary to optimize fibrosis screening in this age group. Further validation studies using large, histologically confirmed MASLD cohorts are warranted to confirm these findings and refine age‐specific screening strategies.
