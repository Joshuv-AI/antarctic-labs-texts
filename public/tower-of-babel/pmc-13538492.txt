Racialized economic concentration predicts storm-related injuries and deaths in the United States, 2005–2022
Authors: Jason T H Cheung, Seleeke Flingai, Kate Burrows
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtreme storms across the United States pose substantial and unequally distributed health burdens. We linked 111,252 county-event observations from the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration Storm Events Database (2005–2022) to the Index of Concentration at the Extremes (ICE), a measure of racialized economic concentration reflecting the relative concentration of high-income non-Hispanic White households compared with low-income Black households within U.S. counties, to examine whether ICE predicts storm-related injuries and deaths. Using multilevel zero-inflated negative binomial models with county random intercepts and population offsets, each one-quintile increase in ICE (greater concentration of high-income, non-Hispanic White households relative to low-income Black households) was associated with 52% lower rates of direct injuries (IRR = 0.48, 95% CI: [0.44, 0.53]) and 36% lower rates of direct deaths (IRR = 0.64, 95% CI: [0.59, 0.69]). Associations for indirect outcomes were less precise but directionally consistent (indirect injuries IRR = 0.70, 95% CI: [0.54, 0.91]; indirect deaths IRR = 0.66, 95% CI: [0.48, 0.89]). Spline models suggested elevated risk concentrated in the most disadvantaged counties, with risk flattening in more advantaged areas. County-level maps show that structural disadvantage and storm exposure frequency do not systematically co-locate, suggesting that realized harm is driven by social and infrastructural context rather than exposure alone. These findings identify racialized economic concentration as a structural determinant of disaster harm and support targeting resilience investments toward the most structurally disadvantaged and economically marginalized counties.Subject terms: Socioeconomic scenarios, Climate-change mitigation, Climate-change policy, Geography

Introduction

Storm-related hazards, including hurricanes, typhoons, and other extreme storm systems, have intensified in many parts of the world, driven by warming sea surface temperatures and increasingly unstable atmospheric conditions1. Although these storms are shaped by global climate forces, the destruction they cause unfolds locally and unevenly2–4. Their impacts are filtered through the existing social, political, and economic structures of the communities they strike. Storms do not choose their targets based on race or income, but the devastation they leave behind consistently reveals and reinforces longstanding inequities.

In disaster studies, an important distinction is made between natural hazards and disasters. Hazards are physical events such as hurricanes, floods, tornadoes, or earthquakes. Disasters, by contrast, arise when these hazards interact with socially produced vulnerability, institutional arrangements, and policy choices that shape exposure, preparedness, response, and recovery5–7. Accordingly, this study treats storms as natural hazards, while interpreting storm-related injuries and deaths as socially patterned outcomes whose distribution is in part conditioned by structural inequality.

In the United States, these unequal burdens are particularly evident in the geography of storm-related injuries and fatalities. Black, Latino, and low-income communities are disproportionately located in areas that are vulnerable to flooding, storm surge, and infrastructure failure8. These same communities may lack the resources needed to prepare for, survive, and recover from extreme weather events. They face barriers such as limited access to early warnings, reduced access to shelter, and inadequate insurance coverage9–11. Moreover, institutional delays in emergency response and unequal distribution of disaster aid compound their risk12. These disparities are rooted in a history of policy decisions that have systematically marginalized particular groups. One of the clearest examples of such structural exclusion is redlining.

Redlining emerged in the 1930s when the federal government’s Home Owners’ Loan Corporation created residential security maps to assess mortgage lending risk. Neighborhoods with large populations of Black, immigrant, and working class residents were designated as hazardous and marked in red13. These areas were systematically denied access to credit, public investment, and essential services. The practice not only intensified racial inequality but also laid the groundwork for decades of infrastructural decay and disinvestment in urban communities. Although redlining was eventually outlawed, its legacy continues to shape the spatial distribution of risk. Many of the neighborhoods that were once redlined remain home to economically disadvantaged populations and still suffer from under resourced schools, aging infrastructure, poor environmental conditions, and inadequate healthcare services14. A growing body of research has linked historical redlining to contemporary outcomes such as reduced greenness15, increased heat-related emergency department visits16, elevated exposure to air pollution17, and delayed viral suppression of infectious diseases such as HIV18.

While redlining maps offer a historical lens for understanding the geography of disadvantage, they are, by definition, static and cannot fully capture the ongoing and evolving dynamics of racialized economic concentration. For this reason, this paper turns to the Index of Concentration at the Extremes (ICE), a metric that quantifies the degree of economic and racial concentration within a given geography19. First introduced by sociologist Douglas Massey in 2001, ICE has gained widespread use in studies of neighborhood-level determinants of health20–22. A systematic review documented the wide use of ICE in public health etiologic studies to investigate concentrated disadvantage as an upstream factor of health outcomes23, including adverse birth/pregnancy outcomes19,24–33, cancer34–37, premature mortality26,38,39, infectious disease40–42, hypertension43, and body mass index44, as well as youth trauma45 and educational readiness46.

ICE measures the extent to which households within a geographic unit (e.g., county, zip code, etc.) are concentrated at opposite ends of the socioeconomic spectrum, allowing researchers to identify areas where privilege and deprivation are most starkly concentrated. ICE is responsive to current data and can be used to track disparities over time. This makes it particularly valuable for identifying communities at heightened risk and for tracing the ongoing impacts of structural injustice. Importantly, ICE captures racialized economic concentration rather than spatial segregation per se. In this study, ICE is constructed by jointly classifying households according to both race and income, contrasting high-income non-Hispanic White households with low-income Black households. This formulation reflects the historical and ongoing role of racialized economic stratification in the United States, where Black communities have been disproportionately affected by discriminatory housing policies, residential segregation, disinvestment, and unequal access to economic and institutional resources. Prior public health research has widely used this ICE specification to operationalize structural racialized economic inequality within U.S. contexts23. While many vulnerability metrics incorporate both racial composition and socioeconomic indicators, ICE differs in that it measures the relative concentration of structural advantage and disadvantage within the same geographic unit, rather than simply the presence or proportion of vulnerable populations. By explicitly contrasting privileged and deprived groups, ICE captures the degree of structural polarization within counties. However, ICE does not measure the spatial arrangement of populations within counties. It does not distinguish whether advantaged and disadvantaged groups are spatially separated into distinct neighborhoods or intermixed across space. Instead, ICE captures structural polarization within a geographic unit, reflecting population composition shaped by historical and ongoing structural inequalities. Such structural polarization may influence differential exposure to hazards, access to protective resources, infrastructure investment, and post-disaster recovery capacity. A score of −1 indicates that all individuals belong to the most disadvantaged group, whereas a score of 1 reflects complete concentration in the most advantaged group (see the “Methods” section for calculation details).

Composite vulnerability indices, such as the Social Vulnerability Index, aggregate numerous socioeconomic indicators into a single summary score intended to represent overall vulnerability. While widely used in disaster planning and research, these indices have also been subject to methodological and theoretical critiques. Studies have shown that vulnerability rankings can be sensitive to methodological choices during index construction, including the selection of indicators, weighting schemes, and spatial scale47. Empirical validation studies further suggest that vulnerability indices may not consistently predict observed disaster impacts across contexts48. Additional critiques note that composite indices often combine many correlated variables capturing multiple dimensions of vulnerability, which can obscure causal mechanisms and complicate interpretation49. Recent reviews also highlight conceptual inconsistencies in how vulnerability indices are applied across hazard studies, sometimes conflating vulnerability, exposure, and resilience within a single metric50. These debates underscore the importance of clearly specifying what dimension of vulnerability a given metric captures. In contrast to many composite indices, ICE focuses explicitly on the concentration of advantage and disadvantage along a racialized economic dimension, providing a theoretically grounded measure of structural polarization.

The advantage of ICE over other commonly used indicators of inequity such as the Gini coefficient (which captures overall income inequality) and the Index of Dissimilarity (which measures racial segregation) has been well documented elsewhere19. In short, ICE focuses on the concentration of advantaged and disadvantaged populations within geographic units. For example, two counties composed entirely of low-income or high-income residents may yield identical Gini scores due to uniform income distribution, despite being socially distinct. Similarly, the Index of Dissimilarity cannot distinguish between areas that are uniformly White and those that are uniformly Black, since it does not capture internal demographic composition. In contrast, ICE offers a directional and intuitive metric that reveals the concentration of privilege and deprivation, making it a powerful tool for understanding spatial inequality.

While previous research, particularly in the context of hurricanes and other major storm events, has examined the health and economic consequences of extreme weather, storm-related injuries and fatalities remain underexamined as systematically reported outcomes. Much of the existing literature relies on cause-specific hospitalizations or all-cause mortality51,52, which capture broader health impacts but may not be directly attributable to storm exposure. In contrast, storm-related injuries and deaths represent outcomes that are most directly linked to storm events and provide highly specific indicators of storm-attributable harm. Although these endpoints do not capture the full spectrum of storm-related health impacts, they provide the most direct and attribution-specific indicators of acute storm vulnerability available at the national scale. Storms may also contribute to delayed mortality, exacerbation of chronic disease, disruption of healthcare access, and mental health impacts that are not fully reflected in officially classified storm-related injuries and deaths. This study addresses these limitations by focusing on storm-attributable outcomes while explicitly examining their structural patterning across U.S. counties.

This study examines reported storm-related injuries and fatalities across multiple storm hazard types in the United States while explicitly linking these outcomes to racialized economic concentration. First, we leverage event-level data on storm-related injuries and deaths from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) Storm Events Database, enabling direct attribution of health harms to specific storm events across time and geography53. Second, we examine a broad range of storm types, including hurricanes, tropical storms, storm surges and tides, coastal floods, flash floods, high winds, and tornadoes, across multiple years and counties, allowing for a more generalizable understanding of storm exposure and risk. Third, we apply ICE to quantify racialized economic concentration and assess its role as a structural determinant of vulnerability. Finally, we distinguish between direct and indirect storm-related injuries and fatalities to explore the multiple pathways through which racialized economic concentration contributes to storm-related injuries and fatalities.

This study investigates whether counties with higher ICE values (greater concentration of high-income non-Hispanic White households relative to low-income Black households) experience lower rates of storm-related injuries and deaths. By linking ICE with NOAA’s Storm Events Database, we test the hypothesis that lower ICE values are associated with increased vulnerability to storm-related injuries and deaths, independent of population size. Importantly, we frame this investigation within a socioecological model of public health, which emphasizes the interaction between individual, interpersonal, community, institutional, and policy-level factors in shaping health outcomes. Racialized economic concentration (as operationalized through ICE) does not operate in isolation. It interacts with systems of community, housing, transportation, healthcare, and emergency preparedness to create cumulative and spatially patterned risks. Neighborhood segregation, disinvestment, discriminatory housing policies, and unequal access to basic services jointly determine which populations are most vulnerable to harm and which have the capacity to recover54. This systems-level perspective moves beyond explanations that focus solely on individual behavior or hazard exposure, highlighting the deeply rooted and socially patterned nature of climate vulnerability. Importantly, while contemporary hazard assessment and emergency management increasingly incorporate social vulnerability indicators into preparedness and resource allocation frameworks55–58, less is known about whether racialized economic concentration itself predicts systematically reported storm-attributable injuries and deaths across counties. By linking event-level storm records with measures of racialized economic concentration, this study examines whether structural inequality is associated with the most direct indicators of storm-related harm.

Results

Distinct regional patterns in racialized economic concentration are apparent. Counties in the Deep South and parts of the Midwest are disproportionately represented in the lowest ICE quintile, indicating greater structural disadvantage, while counties along the coasts and the northern Midwest are more likely to fall into the highest quintiles. Storm exposure was widespread, and its spatial distribution did not systematically align with ICE quintiles, suggesting that more disadvantaged counties were not necessarily more likely to experience storm events.

These findings indicate that structural disadvantage is most strongly associated with elevated storm-related injury and mortality risk among the most disadvantaged counties.

Indirect injuries and deaths

ICE was also inversely associated with indirect injuries and indirect deaths, although estimates were less precise than for direct outcomes. Each one quintile increase in ICE was associated with lower rates of indirect injuries (IRR = 0.70, 95% CI: [0.54, 0.91]) and indirect deaths (IRR = 0.66, 95% CI: [0.48, 0.89]). Spline models did not materially improve model fit for indirect outcomes, consistent with limited information content and sparse counts for these endpoints.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses adjusting for county level percent non-Hispanic Black population, median household income, and percent aged 65 years or older attenuated ICE estimates (Supplementary Table 1). This pattern is expected given conceptual overlap, as ICE is constructed from the joint distribution of race and income extremes. Accordingly, these models are interpreted as robustness and interpretation checks rather than primary specifications aimed at isolating an independent ICE effect. Associations remained directionally consistent across outcomes.

Categorical analyses modeling ICE quintile as an exposure variable yielded results consistent with the primary and spline-based analyses (Supplementary Table 2; Supplementary Fig. 2). Compared with counties in the most disadvantaged quintile, counties in progressively more advantaged quintiles experienced substantially lower rates of direct storm-related injuries and deaths. For example, counties in the most advantaged quintile had 96 percent lower rates of direct injuries (IRR = 0.04, 95% CI: [0.03, 0.06]) and 86 percent lower rates of direct deaths (IRR = 0.14, 95% CI: [0.10, 0.20]) relative to the most disadvantaged counties.

Discussion

This study provides national evidence that racialized economic concentration, as measured by ICE, is a significant predictor of storm-related injuries and fatalities in the United States. Descriptive patterns and multivariable models consistently showed that counties marked by high structural disadvantage faced a disproportionately high burden of direct storm-related harm. These associations held even after adjusting for population and were strongest in the most structurally marginalized counties, underscoring the intersection of climate vulnerability and entrenched social inequities.

Beyond documenting disparities, this study contributes to the hazards and disaster literature in several ways. First, it links event-level storm records from the NOAA Storm Events Database with county-level structural inequality across nearly two decades, allowing injuries and deaths to be directly attributed to specific storm events rather than inferred from broader mortality indicators. Second, it identifies racialized economic concentration as a structural predictor of storm-attributable harm across multiple storm hazard types at the national scale. Third, the results suggest that structural polarization captured by ICE may provide complementary insight alongside existing social vulnerability metrics already used in hazard assessment and emergency management55–58, highlighting how the concentration of privilege and deprivation within places may shape the translation of hazard exposure into injury and mortality.

Our findings complement and extend prior research on environmental justice, hazard vulnerability, and disaster risk by showing that even when storm frequency does not differ systematically across counties, structurally disadvantaged populations are more likely to suffer physical harm when storms occur12. This suggests that the realized impact of storms is shaped not only by meteorological severity but also by the social and spatial distribution of resilience factors, including housing quality, infrastructure robustness, access to healthcare, and emergency response systems59.

While direct outcomes showed the clearest structural gradients, associations for indirect injuries and deaths should be interpreted cautiously despite inverse estimates, given the rarity of these outcomes and the likelihood of reporting heterogeneity across counties. Several factors may explain this. First, indirect outcomes are inherently more difficult to measure and are inconsistently reported across counties. Misclassification or underreporting, particularly in rural or under-resourced jurisdictions, could introduce substantial noise into estimates. Second, indirect deaths may unfold over longer timeframes (e.g., days or weeks after a disaster), making attribution more challenging. This is illustrated by prior work such as the widely cited study on Hurricane Maria in Puerto Rico, which documented significant excess mortality beyond the official death toll60. These dynamics likely contribute to wider uncertainty and potential heterogeneity in indirect outcome estimates.

Sensitivity analyses adjusting for county-level demographic and socioeconomic characteristics attenuated ICE estimates, reflecting shared structural determinants between ICE and its component characteristics. This attenuation is conceptually expected because ICE is defined based on the joint distribution of race and income, and adjustment for these characteristics partially accounts for structural mechanisms through which ICE operates. Importantly, ICE captures structural concentration of privilege and deprivation rather than racial composition or income alone, supporting its interpretation as a structural measure of racialized economic inequality.

Several limitations should be noted. First, this study is observational and identifies associations between racialized economic concentration and storm-related injuries and fatalities rather than establishing causal relationships. Although the analyses adjusted for population and multiple structural characteristics in sensitivity analyses, unmeasured confounding and residual sources of bias may still influence the observed associations.

Second, all analyses were conducted at the county level, which may obscure important within-county variation in both exposure and structural conditions. Counties are heterogeneous geographic units, and structural inequality often operates at finer spatial scales, such as neighborhoods or census tracts. Relatedly, ICE is an aspatial metric that captures the concentration of advantaged and disadvantaged populations within counties but does not directly measure the spatial arrangement of populations within those counties. As such, ICE does not quantify residential segregation in the spatial sense captured by indices such as the dissimilarity or isolation index. Rather, ICE reflects structural polarization shaped by historical and ongoing processes of racial and economic stratification at the county level. Future research using finer spatial scales and spatially explicit segregation measures may help clarify how within-county spatial segregation contributes to storm-related vulnerability.

Third, we did not adjust for storm severity in our models, despite its recognized role in shaping storm-related injuries and fatalities. This decision was based on both empirical and conceptual considerations. Empirically, storm intensity indicators such as maximum wind speed, rainfall totals, and surge height are inconsistently recorded in the NOAA Storm Events Database. These data are frequently missing or poorly standardized across time, event types, and locations, limiting their suitability for national, county-level analysis over an extended period. Including such variables would likely introduce substantial measurement error and reduce model precision.

Conceptually, meteorological storm intensity is unlikely to function as a confounder in our analysis. Although storm intensity is clearly associated with storm-related health outcomes, it is unlikely to be systematically associated with our exposure of interest, racialized economic concentration as measured by ICE. Therefore, storm intensity does not meet the criteria for confounding. In contrast, the severity of storm impacts (i.e., the extent of damage and disruption experienced on the ground) may indeed be shaped by structural inequality. Communities with high ICE disadvantage often face aging infrastructure, inadequate housing, and limited emergency services, all of which can intensify harm from a given storm. In this sense, realized storm impacts may act as mediators, lying on the causal pathway between structural inequality and health outcomes. Adjusting for realized storm impacts could result in overadjustment, potentially attenuating the estimated associations. Future studies with more complete and granular storm intensity data may explore whether storm severity modifies the relationship between ICE and health outcomes.

Fourth, while we examined a broad range of storm types, including hurricanes, tropical storms, coastal floods, and tornadoes, we did not differentiate among them in our analyses. This decision was based in part on limited sample sizes for several event types, which reduced the statistical power to conduct separate models for each. Some storm types occurred infrequently or resulted in few reported injuries and deaths, making disaggregated analysis less reliable. Nonetheless, different storm types may involve distinct mechanisms of harm and affect communities in different ways. Future research may benefit from exploring whether the relationship between racialized economic concentration and storm-related health outcomes varies across specific types of extreme weather events.

Fifth, indirect deaths and injuries may be undercounted in official records, as noted above, particularly in counties with limited surveillance infrastructure or political will to classify such deaths accurately. Finally, although ICE captures racialized economic concentration, it represents just one dimension of structural inequality. It does not account for other mechanisms such as political disenfranchisement, immigration status, or access to social services that may also inform vulnerability. While our approach foregrounds structural drivers, future studies may further benefit from incorporating additional indicators to capture the full complexity of disaster-related inequity.

Implications for intervention and policy

The findings of this study underscore the urgent need to center equity in hazard preparedness, disaster response, and recovery planning. Traditional public health frameworks that focus on individual preparedness, behavior change, or information dissemination are limited in the face of structural inequity. The disparities we observe in storm-related injuries and deaths are not merely the result of individual choices but are shaped by long-term disinvestment and exclusion. Addressing these disparities requires structural solutions.

First, investments could prioritize counties with low ICE scores, especially those in the most negative range, where risk is highest, and capacity is lowest. This includes upgrading infrastructure, improving storm drainage and housing safety, expanding evacuation resources, and increasing access to early warning systems61. Resources could be allocated based on vulnerability, not just population size or storm history, to ensure that communities at greatest risk receive the support they need.

Second, emergency response planning could integrate measures of structural inequality alongside meteorological indicators when targeting assistance. In recent years, federal, state, and local agencies have increasingly incorporated social vulnerability metrics into hazard mitigation, preparedness, and resource allocation. For example, many state hazard mitigation plans now explicitly incorporate social vulnerability indicators to guide mitigation strategies and equity-oriented risk assessment55. Similarly, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention developed the Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) to help emergency planners identify communities that may require additional support before, during, and after disasters56. These indicators have also been applied operationally in public health emergencies, including the allocation of COVID-19 vaccines to socially vulnerable communities57. In addition, FEMA’s National Risk Index integrates social vulnerability measures with hazard exposure to inform disaster risk assessment and has been used to support federal disaster preparedness and resource allocation decisions58. These developments represent important progress in recognizing the social determinants of disaster risk. At the same time, our findings suggest that racialized economic concentration, as measured by ICE, may capture a distinct structural dimension of vulnerability that complements existing composite indices. Incorporating ICE alongside established vulnerability tools may improve the identification of communities where hazard exposure is most likely to translate into injury and death.

Third, public health systems could build long-term partnerships with local organizations in structurally marginalized communities. These partnerships can foster trust, improve communication, and facilitate the delivery of culturally appropriate disaster preparedness materials62. Community-based strategies that reflect the lived experience of residents are more likely to succeed than top-down approaches that neglect social context. Finally, policies could aim not only to reduce harm during disasters but also to address the root causes of vulnerability. This includes redressing the legacy of discriminatory housing policies, increasing funding for infrastructure in historically excluded neighborhoods, and enacting equitable climate adaptation policies63. Structural problems require structural responses. ICE is not simply a research tool; it is a policy signal that can guide investments toward resilience, justice, and sustainability. Together, these recommendations reflect a shift from reactive disaster management to proactive and justice-informed public health policy. The goal is not just to survive storms, but to transform the social and environmental conditions that determine who bears their burden.

This study shows that storm-related injuries and deaths in the United States are not random consequences of extreme weather but the outcome of deeply embedded structural inequalities. Counties marked by lower ICE values experience significantly greater injuries and deaths during storms. These findings support a growing body of literature that frames hazard-related vulnerability and disaster risk as products of social and spatial injustice rather than isolated individual risk. Structural conditions such as segregation, disinvestment, and discriminatory policy decisions shape who is exposed, who is protected, and who is left behind. The unequal geography of harm observed here is not incidental. It is the predictable result of systems that have long organized power, resources, and safety along racial and economic lines.

Ultimately, this study takes seriously the idea that climate vulnerability is, in part, socially produced and, therefore, socially reversible. By tracing the enduring link between racialized economic concentration and storm vulnerability, this research offers both a diagnosis of injustice and a call to action.

Methods

Socioecological framework: structural drivers of hazard vulnerability and disaster risk

This study applies the socioecological model to examine how vulnerability to storm-related exposures emerges and persists at the intersection of place, policy, and structural inequality. The socioecological model provides structural depth by mapping how risks are shaped by interactions among individuals, communities, institutions, and policies64. This framework enables us to see climate-related vulnerability not simply as the product of individual behaviors or proximity to danger, but as a reflection of nested and cumulative disadvantages that interact across scales. To address climate-related health disparities, it will be necessary to address not only individual but also structural risk and resilience. The socioecological model has been widely adopted in public health to illustrate how individual health is shaped by nested systems of influence65. These include relationships and environments at the interpersonal, community, institutional, and policy levels. As seen in Fig. 4, these levels interact to systematically constrain some groups’ capacity to prepare for, respond to, and recover from climate events.

Community-level factors may include housing quality, infrastructure, transportation systems, and social cohesion. Policy-level factors may include land-use planning, zoning regulations, disaster preparedness, and resource allocation. Racialized economic concentration, as measured by the Index of Concentration at the Extremes (ICE), may contribute to vulnerability across these levels through differential access to resources, resilience capacity, and structural disadvantage.

At the individual level, health status, age, and physical ability can constrain a person’s ability to respond to a storm. Chronic illnesses, disabilities, or mobility limitations can prevent evacuation or increase susceptibility to injury66. At the interpersonal level, social ties (e.g., family, friends, caregivers) can provide critical lifelines, facilitating early warnings, transportation, or shelter67. At the community level, infrastructure plays a central role. Residents in under-resourced neighborhoods may rely on aging power grids, degraded stormwater systems, or buildings that do not meet modern safety codes68. Community trust and cohesion also matter: neighborhoods with strong social bonds are better positioned to coordinate and recover69, although it remains inconclusive about the relationship between marginalized communities and the strength of social bonds in the context of acute disasters. At the institutional level, the availability and accessibility of healthcare facilities, emergency responders, and public health services shape outcomes during and after disasters70. Communities with a higher number of patients seeking local medical care were disproportionately clustered in areas characterized by greater socioeconomic disadvantage, more vulnerable household structures, and more severe flooding71. Finally, at the policy level, decisions about land use, zoning, floodplain mapping, and disaster relief allocation reflect and reinforce social stratification72,73. Policies that prioritize property value or economic development often ignore the needs of historically excluded communities74. In this way, risk becomes spatialized and institutionalized, reflecting not only the presence of hazards but also who is deemed worthy of protection.

The power of the socioecological model lies in its ability to connect these layers, showing how upstream drivers (e.g., racial concentration and disinvestment) manifest downstream consequences like injury and death. It compels us to ask not just who is vulnerable, but why they are vulnerable, and what systems maintain that vulnerability. This perspective challenges the notion that disasters are simply random acts of nature. Instead, it emphasizes that while hazards may be natural, disasters emerge through social arrangements that structure vulnerability, protection, and recovery. Moreover, the socioecological model challenges assumptions that all individuals or communities are equally at risk within the same residential geographic unit. Even within a single county, significant variation exists in who is exposed, who can respond, and who can recover. These disparities are not random; they are the predictable outcome of structural inequalities rooted in racial and economic stratification. For example, two households in the same storm path may face vastly different outcomes depending on whether they reside in reinforced housing, have access to transportation, or are supported by robust institutional safety nets.

ICE is an especially apt measure within this framework. By capturing both economic status and racial composition in a single metric, ICE reflects the concentrated effects of privilege and exclusion. A county with a highly negative ICE score is not just economically disadvantaged; it reflects racialized economic concentration and structural polarization shaped by historical and ongoing stratification. Such spatial patterns are the legacy of deliberate policies, and they shape both exposure to harm and access to resources. In our analysis, we use ICE to operationalize the structural dimension of the socioecological model, mapping how patterns of racialized economic concentration correlate with storm-related injuries and deaths.

Storm events data

To evaluate storm-related health impacts, we used publicly available data from the NOAA Storm Events Database spanning from 2005 to 202253. This dataset includes records of weather-related events across all U.S. counties, with information on event type, timing, location, and associated injuries and fatalities. We restricted our analysis to event types most closely linked to storms and their secondary hazards. Specifically, we included Hurricanes (Typhoons), Tropical Storms, Storm Surge/Tide, Coastal Floods, Flash Floods, High Winds, and Tornados. These event types were chosen because they collectively capture the compound hazard profile of storms, encompassing both primary meteorological forces (e.g., wind and rain) and secondary impacts (e.g., inland flooding and debris-related trauma).

Each storm event record in the NOAA database includes spatial information in the form of Federal Information Processing Standards (FIPS) codes that identify the county of occurrence. We retained these events as they were already geocoded at the county level and linked each one to its corresponding calendar year. This resulted in an event-level dataset, where each row represents a unique storm event in a specific county and year. County-level variables, including ICE quintile and population size, were merged into the event dataset based on the year and county of each event. We used this event-level dataset to model storm-related injuries and fatalities across time and counties. Storm-related health outcomes included counts of direct and indirect injuries and deaths. Following NOAA definitions, direct injuries and deaths are those resulting from the immediate physical forces of the storm such as structural collapse, flying debris, or drowning; while indirect outcomes include downstream or delayed effects such as carbon monoxide poisoning, electrocution, or traffic accidents during evacuation or recovery operations53.

Index of concentration at the extremes (ICE) data

To measure racialized economic concentration, we used the Index of Concentration at the Extremes (ICE), drawn from the Integrated Public Use Microdata Series (IPUMS) Contextual Determinants of Health dataset maintained by researchers at the University of Minnesota75. ICE quantifies the extent to which residents in a given county are concentrated into either privileged or disadvantaged social groups. The specific ICE metric used in this analysis reflects the distribution of households by both race and income, contrasting high-income, non-Hispanic White households with low-income, Black households.

ICE scores range from −1 (complete concentration of disadvantaged households) to 1 (complete concentration of advantaged households). A value of 0 indicates that the number of high-income non-Hispanic White households and low-income Black households within the county are equal, resulting in a numerator of zero in the ICE calculation. This measure enables a temporally nuanced assessment of structural inequality across U.S. counties by capturing the degree of racialized economic concentration within counties over time. Because ICE is calculated at the county level, it reflects population composition rather than the spatial arrangement of populations within counties.

ICE values were drawn from overlapping 5-year American Community Survey (ACS) estimates spanning 14 consecutive windows from 2005–2009 through 2018–2022 (i.e., 2005–2009, 2006–2010, 2007–2011, 2008–2012, 2009–2013, 2010–2014, 2011–2015, 2012–2016, 2013–2017, 2014–2018, 2015–2019, 2016–2020, 2017–2021, and 2018–2022). These overlapping estimates were used to ensure temporal stability and maximize coverage of county-level demographic indicators across the study period. Each storm event was linked to the ICE window whose midpoint most closely aligned with the event year. For example, storms in 2006 were assigned to the 2005–2009 ICE window, which is centered around 2007 and thus more representative of the underlying structural conditions at the time. This approach more accurately reflects the demographic context during the storm year within a one-year lag, while maintaining consistent exposure-covariate alignment across time.

Statistical analyses

We constructed an event-level dataset that integrated storm characteristics with county-level data on ICE scores and population estimates for the period from 2005 to 2022. County-level population data were obtained from the ACS and merged into each event record based on the county and year of occurrence, using consistent five-year periods for ICE measurement.

We began with descriptive analyses to examine the geographic distribution of structural inequality and storm exposure. Specifically, we generated two national maps at the county level: one displaying the modal ICE quintile (i.e., the most frequently occurring ICE quintile for each county from 2005 to 2022), and another showing the total number of storm-related events over the same period. These maps provided spatial context for understanding the relationship between structural conditions and storm exposure. They also allowed us to explore whether counties with greater structural disadvantage were more frequently exposed to storms, which is an important consideration when evaluating whether exposure patterns could confound the association between racialized economic composition and health outcomes. Because ICE data were not available for all counties in every 5-year ACS window, we used the modal ICE quintile across the entire study period to assign a representative level of racialized economic composition for each county in the maps. This approach minimized data loss and offered a stable reflection of prevailing structural conditions over time. In addition to mapping, we examined summary statistics and average rates of storm-related injuries and deaths across ICE quintiles before proceeding to multivariable modeling.

We then fitted a series of multilevel zero-inflated negative binomial regression models to estimate the association between ICE and storm-related health outcomes. Separate models were specified for each of the four outcome types: direct injuries, direct deaths, indirect injuries, and indirect deaths. This modeling strategy was selected to account for both overdispersion in count data and the large proportion of event records with zero injuries or deaths. The zero-inflated structure accommodates two processes: the probability that a county experiences no storm-related harm, and the expected count conditional on exposure. Random intercepts were included to account for repeated observations within counties and unmeasured geographic heterogeneity. Storm intensity indicators, such as maximum wind speed, rainfall totals, or surge height, were excluded from the models due to inconsistent reporting and substantial missingness in the NOAA Storm Events Database (see Discussion for detailed justification of this exclusion).

To evaluate potential nonlinear associations between ICE and storm-related outcomes, we fitted multilevel zero-inflated negative binomial models incorporating natural cubic splines with three degrees of freedom. These spline models included the same population offset and random intercept structure as the primary models. Model fit between linear and spline specifications was compared using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and likelihood ratio tests. Predicted incidence rate ratios were derived across the observed ICE range and rescaled to ICE = 0 as the reference point. Histograms of ICE distributions across counties were produced to assess data sparsity and help interpret model precision, particularly at the tails of the distribution.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the extent to which associations between ICE and storm-related injuries and deaths were independent of county-level demographic and socioeconomic characteristics. County-level percent non-Hispanic Black population, median household income, and percent of the population aged ≥65 years were obtained from the American Community Survey 5-year estimates corresponding to each ICE measurement period. These variables were selected based on their relevance to structural inequality and potential conceptual overlap with ICE. Each covariate was standardized to improve model stability and comparability of effect estimates. These variables were included in multilevel zero-inflated negative binomial models with population offsets and county-level random intercepts, consistent with the primary analysis. Because these covariates overlap conceptually with ICE, these models were treated as sensitivity analyses to evaluate robustness and clarify interpretation rather than as primary specifications.

As an additional sensitivity analysis, ICE was also modeled as a categorical variable using quintiles, with the most disadvantaged quintile specified as the reference group. These models used the same multilevel zero-inflated negative binomial framework with population offsets and county-level random intercepts. This approach allows estimation of incidence rate ratios without assuming linearity or equal spacing between ICE levels and facilitates intuitive comparison across structurally defined groups.

All results were visualized using U.S. county-level maps of ICE and storm event frequencies and incidence rate ratio plots. All data cleaning, integration, analysis, and visualization were conducted using R version 4.5.0. Multilevel zero-inflated negative binomial models were estimated using the glmmTMB package. Spline models were implemented using the splines package, and figures were generated using ggplot2 and related visualization packages. Code and data used in this study are available at (https://github.com/JTHCHEUNG-UoC/Racialized-Economic-Concentration-Predicts-Storm-Related-Injuries-and-Deaths-in-the-United-States76).

Taken together, this socioecologically grounded strategy enables us to quantify not just disparities in storm-related health outcomes, but their structural determinants. Rather than simply attributing differences to individual risk, our approach foregrounds the spatial organization of advantage and disadvantage as a key driver of public health inequities in the context of climate-driven extreme weather.
