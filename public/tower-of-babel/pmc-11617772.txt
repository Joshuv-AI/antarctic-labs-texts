Propionic Acidemia diagnosed in Amish adults and pregnancy outcomes: A case series
Authors: Ethan M. Scott, Brandon Smith, Joseph Liu, Karlee Hoffman, Jennifer Hershberger, Andew Crosby, Emma L. Baple, Olivia K. Wenger
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPropionic acidemia (PA) is an inborn error of metabolism (IEM) that typically presents in the newborn. The Amish of North America have an increased prevalence of PA due to a founder variant in the PCCB gene. The Amish PA phenotype is variable, and some individuals remain asymptomatic and undiagnosed until adulthood. Additionally, there are limited reports of pregnancy outcomes in Amish individuals with PA.MethodsA retrospective single center chart review was completed on sixty Amish individuals with PA to identify individuals diagnosed as adults (18 years or older) and pregnancy outcomes. We assessed age at diagnosis, reason for PA testing, medical history prior to diagnosis including developmental delay, seizure, protein intolerance, cardiac symptoms, and anxiety. Following the diagnosis, we assessed the prevalence of prolonged QTc and dilated cardiomyopathy. We assessed our cohort for number of pregnancies, pregnancy outcomes, and peripartum complications.ResultsNine out of sixty individuals (15 %) were diagnosed with PA as adults. A family member with PA was the most common reason to prompt genetic testing. Cardiac symptoms were present in six of nine individuals prior to diagnosis. Three individuals diagnosed as adults had dilated cardiomyopathy and one underwent cardiac transplant. There were twenty-one pregnancies in six women with eighteen successful deliveries and three miscarriages. Two women developed peripartum cardiomyopathy. There were no acute maternal decompensations.ConclusionOur work supports the consideration that all Amish newborns be screened for PA with molecular testing to enable early diagnosis. The stark difference in peripartum outcomes requires further prospective work to ensure appropriate monitoring throughout pregnancy while respecting individual values and autonomy.

Introduction

Propionic acidemia (PA) is an inborn error of metabolism (IEM) due to a deficiency in propionyl-CoA carboxylase caused by pathogenic biallelic variants in PCCA or PCCB [1]. PA individuals typically present as newborns with developmental delay, failure to thrive, seizures, or metabolic decompensation [1,2]. The Amish of North America have increased incidence of PA due to a homozygous PCCB founder variant Chr3 (GRCh38): g.136330012 A > G, NM_000532.4:c.1606 A > G; p. (Asn536Asp) [1,3,4]. Amish PA individuals can present at any age with significant variability including intellectual disability, seizure, prolonged QTc, and dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Recognition of underlying metabolic disease may be delayed due to false-negative newborn screening (NBS) or lack of newborn screening [3,4,7]. Timely diagnosis could impact management and reduce morbidity in adults, particularly in high risk states such as pregnancy [3,5,6]. There is a paucity of pregnancy outcomes in the Amish variant of PA and optimal peripartum and postnatal management is unclear [5]. This paper describes clinical characteristics leading to the diagnosis of PA in nine adult individuals due to an Amish founder variant and pregnancy outcomes from six women with PA who had a total of twenty-one pregnancies.

Materials and methods

Editorial policies and ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this project, “Propionic Acidemia Diagnosed in Amish adults: a Case Series” (#2024–013) was given by the Akron Children's Hospital Institutional Review stating that it had no fundamental ethical or legal objections to the implementation of this research project. A waiver of informed consent was granted.

Chart review

A retrospective single center chart review was completed on sixty Amish individuals with PA, homozygous for PCCB c.1606 A > G, at The New Leaf Center Clinic for Special Children in Mt. Eaton, Ohio from 2013 through 2024 to identify individuals diagnosed as adults (18 years or older) and pregnancy outcomes. We assessed age at diagnosis, reason for PA testing, medical history prior to diagnosis including developmental delay, seizure, protein intolerance, cardiac symptoms, and anxiety. Following the diagnosis, we assessed the prevalence of prolonged QTc (Bazett-corrected QT) >460 milliseconds (ms) [8] and DCM. We assessed our cohort for number of pregnancies, pregnancy outcomes, and peripartum complications. A total of nine out of sixty (15 %) of individuals were diagnosed at 18 years of age or older and included in analysis for adult PA diagnosis. Six individuals with twenty-one pregnancies were included in the pregnancy outcome analysis.

Case 1

PA1 was diagnosed at 36 years old. He presented for molecular testing after multiple 1st cousins (PA4 PA5, and PA10) were diagnosed with PA. He had a comprehensive cardiomyopathy and an arrythmia panel, which included 100 genes. He was homozygous for PCCB c.1606 A > G and no other pathogenic variants were identified. At 31 years old he had a screening echocardiogram due to a strong family history of DCM, including four siblings with DCM three of whom required cardiac transplant between 16 and 22 years old. The initial echocardiogram of PA1 demonstrated DCM with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 35–40 %. He was asymptomatic at that time. His most recent LVEF was 32 % with dilated left ventricle. His QTc has remained normal. He has remained asymptomatic, managed with a Neprilysin Inhibitor, Sacubitril/valsartan 24–26 mg twice daily and Carvedilol 12.5 mg twice daily.

Case 2

PA2 was diagnosed at 22 years old as part of a research protocol utilizing exome sequencing to explore molecular etiologies of Long QT syndrome. She initially presented at 15 years old after experiencing a sudden cardiac arrest while walking to school, with no preceding symptoms, due to ventricular fibrillation. EKG was reported to show prolonged QTc but was not available for review. Her post-arrest echocardiogram demonstrated global left ventricular dysfunction with a left ventricle shortening fraction of 17 % (z-score − 6.42). Following resuscitation, an implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) was placed. Additionally, she was managed with Metoprolol 50 mg daily. She has had two successful pregnancies and deliveries without complications. Her LVEF prior to her first pregnancy was 56 % and remained stable throughout her two pregnancies and was 60 % over one year following her second delivery. Her longitudinal strain steadily decreased during this same interval from −23 % to −15 %. She had no acute changes in the peripartum period.

Case 3

PA3 was diagnosed at 28 years old. He is a brother to PA 2. Following his sister's cardiac arrest, he had a screening EKG that demonstrated prolonged QTc at 485 milliseconds (ms). He was initiated on a beta-blocker. He had no history of cardiac symptoms including syncope. He underwent targeted molecular confirmation following his sister's diagnosis of PA. His initial echocardiogram following his diagnosis demonstrated an LVEF of 56 %. Now at 36 years old, his most recent echocardiogram demonstrated mild LV dilation, an LVEF of 54 % and global longitudinal strain (GLS) of −13 %. He is managed with Enalapril 5 mg twice daily and Atenolol 25 mg twice daily.

Case 4

PA4 was diagnosed at 24 years old. She presented for cascade molecular testing with PCCB whole gene sequencing and deletion/duplication analysis following her sister's PA diagnosis. Her sister developed end stage DCM and liver failure in the setting of recent pregnancy loss and required combined heart and liver transplant [9]. PA4 reported that during adolescence she had exercise intolerance with activities such as mowing the lawn and biking, often leading to emesis and significant fatigue. She had not sought medical care but continued to self-limit her activity. She has generalized anxiety that had worsened in 3 years preceding her diagnosis after her brother died of a sudden cardiac arrest at 28 years old (he did not have PA testing completed). Her initial echocardiogram following diagnosis demonstrated normal left ventricular size, an LVEF 53 %, GLS of 14 %. Her initial EKG showed a mildly prolonged QTc of 463 ms. She is on no cardiac medications. She has had no pregnancies or follow-up cardiology evaluations.

Case 5

PA5 was diagnosed at 26 years old. She is a sister to PA4 and underwent molecular testing with PCCB whole gene sequencing and deletion/duplication analysis after her sister's diagnosis of PA. She reported that since childhood she had self-restricted protein intake because it made her feel nauseated. She had developed chronic fatigue, palpitations, and exertional dyspnea with light housework in early adulthood. She had three years of generalized anxiety and panic attacks that had worsened in the past 2–3 years preceding her diagnosis, which is now managed with a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) and counseling. Her initial echocardiogram demonstrated mild left ventricular dilation with normal systolic function with LVEF of 58 % and her EKG showed normal sinus rhythm with a QTc of 447 ms. She is on no cardiac medications. She has had no pregnancies or follow-up cardiology evaluations.

Case 6

PA6 was diagnosed at 22 years old. He had been previously asymptomatic until he developed a 1 month history of progressive fatigue, exertional dyspnea, and orthopnea prior to admission to the ICU with severe DCM with an LVEF of 17 % and an initial prolonged QTc of 530 ms. There was no identified inciting trigger for his decompensation. His parents asked the managing team to evaluate for PA, because his younger brother had been diagnosed on NBS. Molecular testing confirmed a diagnosis of PA. He underwent a successful heart transplant, but long term follow-up information is unavailable.

Case 7

PA7 was diagnosed at 30 years old. She underwent targeted cascade molecular testing after her sister with PA developed severe isolated DCM following the delivery of her fourth child [6]. She had no preceding signs or symptoms. The EKG and echocardiogram for PA7 following her diagnosis were normal. She had two successful pregnancies and deliveries preceding her diagnosis and three after her diagnosis. During her most recent pregnancy she developed prolonged QTc of 469 milliseconds and was managed with Metoprolol 25 mg daily. She had no signs of peripartum cardiomyopathy and her LVEF has remained at 60 % or above.

Case 8

PA8 was diagnosed at 36 years old. She underwent molecular testing after her nephew was diagnosed with PA following a positive NBS. She had a panel of 200 genes common in the Amish community. There were no other pathogenic variants identified in genes associated with cardiomyopathy. Throughout early childhood and adolescence, she had exertional dyspnea, emesis and fatigue following a ¼ mile walk to school. At 27 years old, 1.5 years after her third pregnancy, she developed acute onset severe chest pain and dyspnea in the setting of pyelonephritis. Her initial echocardiogram demonstrated an LVEF of 45–50 %. She was managed with Lisinopril and Labetalol. Her systolic function normalized to 55–60 %; and she was weaned off all cardiac medications and discharged from Cardiology after 7 years of follow-up (2 years prior to her PA diagnosis). She had five pregnancies prior to her PA diagnosis and had three healthy children and two miscarriages. She has generalized anxiety that is managed with an SSRI.

Case 9

PA9 was diagnosed at 29 years old. She is a sister to PA8. She underwent cascade molecular testing with a three gene cardiomyopathy and arrhythmia panel in addition to targeted PCCB testing after her nephew was diagnosed with PA following a positive NBS. In addition to PA, she was identified as having a pathogenic variant in MYBPC3 NM_000256.3:c.3330 + 2 T > G which is associated with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [10]. She had no preceding cardiac signs or symptoms. She was diagnosed with PA in the 2nd trimester of her most recent pregnancy. Her initial EKG and echocardiogram following her diagnosis were normal with an LVEF of 57 % and normal LV size. She had a repeat EKG and echocardiogram four weeks after her delivery which demonstrated a normal LVEF of 54 % and mild LV dilation. She is on no cardiac medications. She has had a total of four pregnancies including three uncomplicated deliveries and one first trimester miscarriage. She has generalized anxiety treated with an SSRI.

Case 10

PA10 was diagnosed at 14 months old. She remained asymptomatic with normal cardiac function until her 1st pregnancy at 22 years old. Her echocardiogram prior to her first pregnancy demonstrated no LV dilation, with an LVEF of 61 %, and her QTc was 450. During the 3rd trimester she developed mild DCM with reduced systolic function with a LVEF of 52 %, and her QTc increased to 464. She had an uncomplicated delivery and there was no acute intrapartum decompensation. Six weeks following delivery her LVEF declined to 49 %, and her QTc increased to 487. She was then started on Metoprolol for QTc prolongation. She became pregnant 14 months after her 1st delivery. Her initial echocardiogram was obtained in the 1st trimester and had an LVEF of 47 % with a GLS of −12 %. During the 2nd trimester she developed moderate LV systolic dysfunction and her LVEF declined to 25 % with a GLS of −10 %. During the 3rd trimester her LVEF slightly improved to 35 % with a GLS of −11 %. She had an uncomplicated vaginal delivery. She was seen by cardiology two months after delivery and was asymptomatic and was initiated on Losartan. No further echocardiogram data is available. Her labor and delivery plan is included in supplemental table 1.

Case 11

PA11 was diagnosed at 1 year old. She remained asymptomatic with normal cardiac function throughout childhood and early adulthood. Her echocardiogram prior to her first pregnancy demonstrated no LV dilation, with an LVEF of 63 %. She had a 3rd trimester echocardiogram thashowed no LV dilation and an LVEF of 55 %. She had an uncomplicated delivery. Her delivery plan was essentially the same as PA10. Her next evaluation was about 1 year after delivery and her cardiac function was stable with no LV dilation and an LVEF of 55 %. She became pregnant about 15 months after her 1st delivery. Her 2nd trimester echocardiogram demonstrated no LV dilation and an LVEF of 64 %. Her delivery was uncomplicated. Six months following her 2nd delivery, at 26 years old, she developed moderate DCM with an LVEF of 37 %. She was started on a Metoprolol 25 mg daily and Losartan 12.5 mg daily. No further echocardiogram data is available. She has generalized anxiety managed with an SNRI.

Discussion

This case series reviewed the medical history of nine Amish PA individuals diagnosed as adults and twenty-one pregnancy outcomes for six individuals with the Amish PA variant. This work emphasizes the broad phenotype of Amish variant PA consistent with previous reports. [3,5,11] In the Amish population the prevalence of false negative NBS for PA and late presentation make this IEM an important consideration for physicians who care for the Amish population. Late-onset IEM are difficult to recognize because of the often subtle and non-specific symptoms. [[12], [13], [14]] Our cohort did not exhibit the classic early signs of PA including metabolic decompensation, failure to thrive, developmental delay, or epilepsy. However, this cohort was not universally asymptomatic at the time of diagnosis. Six out of nine developed cardiac symptoms prior to diagnosis. Cardiac symptoms prior to diagnosis included exertional dyspnea (4/9), fatigue (2/9), syncope (1/9), palpitations (1/9), orthopnea (1/9). Five individuals had a history of anxiety that had been present for years preceding the diagnosis. Symptoms such as fatigue and anxiety are easy to attribute to more common non-metabolic causes of disease, but considering the possibility of IEM in the differential diagnosis could decrease long term morbidity. In our cohort of sixty individuals, we have none who were diagnosed in adolescence, between 12 and 17 years old. PA2 presented with sudden cardiac arrest at 15 years old, but she was not diagnosed with PA until 22 years old. This absence of adolescent diagnoses may represent improved newborn screening cutoffs or improved awareness of PA in the Amish community facilitating earlier diagnosis in childhood.

Eight out of nine individuals diagnosed with PA as adults had a family member with a known diagnosis of PA. Three of our PA individuals who were diagnosed following a family member's diagnosis had long standing cardiac symptoms but had not sought out further medical care until after the family member was diagnosed. Previous studies have shown that multiple Amish adults have been diagnosed with PA only after a family member has been diagnosed. [3,5,7] Two individuals in this cohort developed isolated DCM prior to their PA diagnosis. Our work supports previous studies that PA should be evaluated in individuals with isolated DCM [3,6,15,16]. It is important to note that while genetic testing is now recommended in the international guideline, European Heart Rhythm Association (EHRA)/Heart Rhythm Society (HRS)/Asia Pacific Heart Rhythm Society (APHRS)/Latin American Heart Rhythm Society (LAHRS) Expert Consensus Statement on the state of genetic testing for cardiac diseases, following the diagnosis of DCM, not all commercially available cardiomyopathy sequencing panels include evaluation of the PCCA and PCCB genes [17].

At the time of diagnosis three out of nine individuals diagnosed as adults had DCM. Four out of nine had a prolonged QTc prior to or at the time of diagnosis. All individuals were initiated on a protein restricted died of 0.69–0.97 g of protein per kilogram per day and were on a range of levocarnitine of 1000–4000 mg per day. Due to a lack of a reliable and predictive biomarker in PA to assess disease severity biochemical studies such as propionylcarnitine or methylcitrate were not regularly obtained. No individuals at last follow-up were on a medical formula. DCM and prolonged QTc were managed by cardiology per standard guidelines.

There are limited reports of pregnancy management and outcomes in PA [7,[18], [19], [20]]. Our cohort had several uncomplicated pregnancies with successful delivery at local birthing centers and community hospitals. However, some affected women developed significant peripartum cardiomyopathy, including after previously uncomplicated pregnancies and deliveries. Two out of six women with a history of pregnancy developed peripartum cardiomyopathy (PA10, and PA11). Whereas other women demonstrated either chronic decreasing of LV function (PA2) or an acute trigger outside of the peripartum period (PA 8). Other cardiac complications in addition to reduced DCM, included QTc prolongation in one individual during her most recent pregnancy. The risk of peripartum cardiomyopathy did not appear to correlate with the number of previous pregnancies. The onset was variable from the first trimester to several months after delivery. The Amish culture values labor and delivery with minimal medical intervention [7]. The stark difference in outcomes warrants further prospective study to evaluate optimal obstetrical care in the Amish PA community that respect the values of the community without putting mother's at undue risk.

The primary limitation of this work is that it is a single center small retrospective case series. Additionally, comprehensive genetic testing was not completed on each individual, so there remains the possibility that those affected by cardiomyopathy had additional genetic risk factors for cardiac disease.

PA in the Amish population remains an important diagnostic consideration outside the newborn period. Our work supports the consideration that all Amish newborns be screened for PA with molecular testing to enable early diagnosis. [3] [5,6] We recommend clinicians strongly encourage family members to undergo genetic counseling and predictive genetic testing after the identification of an affected infant, adolescent, or adult.
