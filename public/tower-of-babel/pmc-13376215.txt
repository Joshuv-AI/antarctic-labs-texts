Realizing irradiation-resistant metallic alloys by immobilizing induced defects
Authors: Shasha Huang, Zhengxiong Su, Shihua Ma, Baichuan Xu, Haijun Fu, Xuepeng Xiang, Wenyu Lu, Ailin Yang, Zhongtao Li, Sergei L Dudarev, Chenyang Lu, Zhenggang Wu, Shijun Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaterials capable of withstanding extreme conditions are essential for advanced fission and fusion technologies. The development of irradiation-tolerant structural materials hinges on the suppression of defect evolution that could lead to mechanical degradation. In this study, we present a novel strategy to effectively immobilize irradiation-induced defects through tuning the degree of local lattice distortion, an intrinsic characteristic of concentrated solid solution alloys. Our results show that increasing the degree of local lattice distortion significantly suppresses irradiation-induced microstructural changes. Notably, the single-phase binary Ni80Mo20 alloy, which exhibits the highest recorded degree of local lattice distortion (4.82% atomic size mismatch), demonstrates markedly frozen defect motion, resulting in negligible irradiation-induced effects. Our findings suggest that irradiation-resistant materials can be developed by engineering local lattice distortion, offering an easy-to-manipulate pathway to designing irradiation-tolerant metallic alloys.Subject terms: Metals and alloys, Atomistic models

Introduction

The advancement of next-generation nuclear energy systems and applications in radiation-intense environments necessitates structural materials capable of withstanding extreme conditions over prolonged periods1–3. Irradiation damage primarily arises from the accumulation of radiation-induced defects, which aggregate into clusters, leading to microstructural degradation and mechanical failure4. Conventional mitigation strategies focus on incorporating high-density defect sinks, such as grain boundaries and secondary phases, to enhance defect recombination5,6. However, these nanostructures often experience instability under high temperatures and prolonged radiation exposure, leading to coalescence and deterioration over time7. Another relatively underexplored yet equally important approach involves the simultaneous immobilization of interstitials and vacancies at operating temperatures8. Such immobilization promotes local defect recombination, allowing defect concentrations to stabilize at a steady-state saturation level9,10. However, traditional alloys fall short in this regard, as vacancy migration typically starts below 500 K—significantly lower than the operating temperatures of nuclear reactors, not to mention the even more easily activated interstitial migration11.

Multi-principal element alloys (MPEAs) present a compelling route toward enhanced irradiation tolerance through their inherent disorder states resulting from compositional complexity12–20. The disorder in MPEAs encompasses chemical disorder attributed to the random arrangement of various highly concentrated elements and site-to-site lattice distortion caused by atomic size mismatches. It has been found that chemical disorder in MPEAs could slow down energy dissipation and create a rough defect energy landscape, both of which promote defect recombination4,21. However, the precise role of disorder remains complex and varies depending on the specific constituent elements. Alongside chemical disorder, lattice distortion is one of the four core effects in MPEAs and has been extensively validated through experimental studies12,22. More importantly, distortion critically governs the mechanical properties of MPEAs by modulating solute-dislocation interactions23–25, suggesting its potential to modify defect behavior under irradiation. By engineering lattice distortion, MPEAs may achieve irradiation resistance unattainable in conventional alloys, presenting new opportunities for radiation-tolerant material design.

Research indicates that the irradiation response of MPEAs relates to the degree of lattice distortion. Specifically, MPEAs with pronounced lattice distortion tend to form smaller dislocation loops under irradiation. However, the impact of lattice distortion on vacancy-type defects remains an ongoing debate in the literature. While some studies report the formation of smaller, denser vacancy clusters with increased lattice distortion3,26, others observe larger clusters27,28. Theoretical modeling provides further insights, suggesting that a moderate degree of lattice distortion hinders interstitial diffusion while enhancing vacancy migration3,29,30. Although the reduced mobility of interstitials aligns with experimental observations of inhibited dislocation loop growth, the expected promotion of vacancy-related defects due to lattice distortion appears inconsistent with experimental findings. These results highlight the strikingly varying effects of lattice distortion on the evolution of different types of radiation-induced defects. Such an intricate relationship underscores the need for a more systematic and comprehensive investigation of the fundamental mechanisms by which lattice distortion governs the formation, growth, and interaction of defect structures under irradiation conditions. Moreover, the ability to control interstitials and vacancies through strategic lattice distortion presents a viable pathway for improving the irradiation tolerance of MPEAs.

In this study, we demonstrate that superb irradiation-tolerant alloys can be achieved by effectively immobilizing irradiation-induced defects, including interstitials and vacancies, through maximizing lattice distortion effects. Specifically, for the developed single-phase Ni80Mo20 alloy with the highest recorded degree of lattice distortion, the irradiation-induced defect process is effectively frozen. As a consequence, the abundant dispersed point defects serve as effective recombination sites, ultimately contributing to the minimal irradiation damage. Our results offer a promising approach to designing irradiation-resistant structural materials for demanding applications in extreme environments.

Results

Experimental characterization of irradiation damage

To emphasize the role of lattice distortion, we designed four concentrated alloys to evaluate their irradiation performance: Ni, NiFe, NiCoV, and Ni80Mo20. Ni and NiFe were selected due to the abundance of existing studies on these alloys3,31, allowing for direct comparison. NiCoV is a concentrated alloy known for its excellent mechanical properties and high degree of lattice distortion32. Recently reported, Ni80Mo20 exhibits the highest recorded degree of lattice distortion among face-centered cubic (FCC) concentrated alloys33. The quantified local lattice distortion for these alloys, assessed using both radial displacement (Δd) and atomic size mismatch (δ), is summarized in Table 1. The detailed radial displacement contributions from each constituent element are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. Both Δd and δ consistently reveal a progressive increase in lattice distortion along the series: Ni <NiFe <NiCoV <Ni80Mo20.

Observations at an average irradiation dose of 8 dpa (Fig. 1c) reveal that dislocation loops in Ni and NiFe are almost exclusively glissile perfect loops, whereas a fraction of sessile faulted loops are present in NiCoV and Ni80Mo20, indicating that the growth of dislocation loops is suppressed in these two alloys. Comparing the four alloys with distinct degrees of lattice distortion, it is evident that defect cluster size continuously decreases with increasing lattice distortion. However, defect density first increases and then decreases. Thus, moderate lattice distortion can inhibit the growth of large clusters but does not curb the defect density. In contrast, in Ni80Mo20, which exhibits the highest degree of lattice distortion, both the defect cluster size and density are reduced, indicating a strong suppression of defect evolution.

Defect accumulation mechanism

To elucidate the mechanism behind the excellent irradiation tolerance of Ni80Mo20, we performed cumulative cascade damage simulations using molecular dynamics (MD) by overlaying 1 keV cascades in each alloy at 300 K. Figure 2a displays the resulting morphologies and distributions of defect clusters after 400 recoils (~0.01 dpa). The large clusters containing more than 20 point defects are encircled. The data reveal that increased lattice distortion leads to smaller clusters, which is consistent with experimental findings. Moreover, as the degree of lattice distortion increases from Ni to NiFe and NiCoV, there is a distinct shift from predominantly interstitial-type to vacancy-type defect clusters, suggesting enhanced vacancy mobility in more distorted alloys.

Figure 2b offers additional insights into the evolution of the number and size distribution of defect clusters across the four systems, with cluster size defined by the number of point defects in the cluster. In Ni, smaller clusters tend to coalesce into larger ones as the number of recoils increases. Similarly, in NiFe, large clusters emerge at high doses, although the effect is less pronounced. In this case, most clusters remain relatively small, with sizes below 30, compared to those in Ni. In comparison, NiCoV forms smaller but more densely packed clusters with sizes below 20, suggesting that while lattice distortion restricts cluster growth, it does not curb their increasing density. Meanwhile, Ni80Mo20 shows even stronger suppression of large cluster formation and exhibits a lower overall density of defect clusters compared to both NiFe and NiCoV. These simulation results align with our experimental observations, revealing a complex and non-linear relationship between lattice distortion and defect cluster formation and growth.

To understand this complex relationship, defect cluster binding energies were computed (Supplementary Fig. 2). The binding energies of interstitial clusters are the highest in Ni and the lowest in Ni80Mo20 for a given cluster size, which corresponds to the largest interstitial clusters observed in Ni and the smallest clusters found in Ni80Mo20, as shown in Fig. 1d and Fig. 2b. In contrast, the binding energies of vacancy-type clusters show no significant differences among the four alloys. Thus, the results of binding energies do not account for the observed variations in vacancy cluster distributions.

Defect cluster binding energies provide information on thermodynamics only, which governs stability at equilibrium conditions, while defect kinetics are more relevant to the dynamic defect process under irradiation. To elucidate these kinetic effects, the migration trajectories of individual interstitials and vacancies were analyzed (Fig. 2c, d, respectively). These trajectories were obtained at 1000 K, a temperature chosen to ensure sufficient migration events for statistically meaningful sampling within accessible MD timescales. Although higher than the experimental irradiation temperature, this choice enables the identification of fundamental diffusion pathways and their dependence on lattice distortion, which are expected to remain operative at lower temperatures despite reduced jump frequencies.

Figure 2c, d demonstrates that increasing lattice distortion progressively confines interstitial diffusion, causing a transition from random long-range motion to localized short-range hopping. In contrast, the increase of lattice distortion from Ni to NiCoV enhances vacancy migration (Fig. 2d), leading to a marked shift from interstitial-dominated clusters to vacancy-dominated clusters in NiCoV under irradiation (Fig. 2a). Intriguingly, further increases of lattice distortion in Ni80Mo20 strongly localize vacancy migration. These trends are also evident in the diffusion path of both interstitial- and vacancy-type clusters (Supplementary Fig. 3). Therefore, both interstitial and vacancy kinetics are strongly suppressed in Ni80Mo20 due to the presence of severe lattice distortion, leading to its smallest defect cluster size and lowest defect density in our experimental observations.

Interstitial and vacancy trapping by lattice distortions

The above results demonstrate that a higher degree of lattice distortion consistently constrains the movement of interstitials, aligning with previous studies. The reason lies in the fact that distortion roughens the energy landscape for interstitials, facilitating their trapping in low-energy configurations. Nonetheless, our findings uncover a complex and intriguing relationship between lattice distortion and vacancy diffusion. Typically, moderate lattice distortion accelerates vacancy-mediated diffusion in concentrated alloys, and this is also the case for NiCoV in our simulations, as seen from the long-range transport trajectories (Fig. 2d). However, in Ni80Mo20, which displays the highest degree of lattice distortion, vacancies start to become trapped—a phenomenon not previously observed.

We have computed the formation and migration energies of interstitials and vacancies in these alloys to understand this observation. Defect energetics are influenced by both chemical composition and lattice distortion. To decouple these factors, we compare vacancy formation energies with and without structural relaxation. In the relaxed case, both the reference and vacancy-containing cells undergo structural relaxation, while atomic positions remain fixed in the unrelaxed case. Figure 3a indicates that lattice distortion reduces vacancy formation energies by up to almost 2 eV. These findings underscore that severe lattice distortion has a more significant impact on defect energetics than chemical complexity alone.

Analysis of formation and migration energy distribution in the relaxed alloys (Supplementary Fig. 4) shows that enhanced lattice distortion broadens the range of formation energies to over 4 eV—substantially higher than the range of less than 2 eV typically reported in high-entropy alloys (HEAs)34–36. This energy broadening creates a substantial overlap between interstitial and vacancy formation energies. More importantly, we find that greater lattice distortion not only widens the distribution of vacancy migration energy barriers but also reduces their average value, in direct contrast to the behavior presented in Fig. 2d.

The reduced vacancy migration barrier and localized trajectory in Ni80Mo20 imply that severe lattice distortion induces a strong correlation effect that limits long-range diffusion. To probe this, we analyzed vacancy jumps over a 50 ns trajectory, segmenting the trajectory into discrete jumps37. The correlation factor (fv) was calculated using the mean cosine of the angle (θ) between consecutive jumps, defined as: fv=1+cos(θ)¯1−cos(θ)¯. As illustrated in Fig. 3b (with a detailed cosθ distribution in Supplementary Fig. 5), in highly distorted Ni80Mo20, the dominance of forth-and-back jumps results in the lowest fv, signifying that vacancies mainly jump between adjacent lattice sites with minimal net displacement. This behavior explains why, contrary to prior studies3,35 and expectations from the reduced vacancy migration barriers (~0.5 eV below Ni) shown in Supplementary Fig. 4i, enhanced lattice distortion in Ni80Mo20 localizes vacancy diffusion rather than facilitating it (Fig. 2d).

Mechanism of defect localization induced by lattice distortions

Immobilization of both interstitials and vacancies represents a key design strategy for developing irradiation-resistant materials. We demonstrate that lattice distortion, as an intrinsic parameter in MPEAs, provides an effective means to tune and suppress defect diffusion. To systematically highlight its potential, we designed a series of Ni-Mo alloys with adjustable lattice distortion while keeping the chemical elements the same. The considered Ni100-xMox alloys and their corresponding degrees of local lattice distortion are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Notably, the distortion levels in Ni99Mo1 and Ni90Mo10 are comparable to those in NiFe and NiCoV, respectively. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6, the diffusion trajectories of interstitials and vacancies at 1000 K in these two representative Ni100-xMox alloys mirror the behaviors seen in NiFe and NiCoV (Fig. 2c, d): vacancy mobility is higher while interstitial diffusion is slower in Ni90Mo10.

Interstitial diffusion in Ni100-xMox alloys proceeds through a chemically-biased preference for Ni-Ni dumbbells (as depicted in Fig. 3c) due to their lower formation energies—around 0.80 eV lower than Ni-Mo and Mo-Mo dumbbells in Ni80Mo20. Incorporating large-sized Mo atoms disrupts diffusion pathways through Ni channels, leading to localized interstitial jumps and a monotonic decrease in interstitial diffusivity, a trend consistent with previous studies29,38,39.

Vacancy diffusion exhibits a mobility that initially increases and then declines with increasing x in Ni100-xMox alloys (Supplementary Fig. 6). Although vacancy diffusion often correlates with melting temperature40,41, the melting points of Ni–Mo alloys in this work decrease monotonically with rising Mo content, in agreement with earlier experiments and atomic-simulation studies42,43. In MPEAs, the inherently rugged energy landscape dominantly governs vacancy‑mediated diffusion, which is in turn controlled by two competing factors—the vacancy jump frequency and the correlation factor44. While lower migration barriers enhance vacancy jump frequencies, back-and-forth jumps negate many of these jumps, leading to a lower correlation factor. These competing effects can be quantified from the statistical distributions of vacancy formation and migration energies. The migration energy distributions are characterized by their mean (μEs) and standard deviation (σEs), while formation energy distributions can be represented by the distribution of site energy differences before and after each vacancy jump (μE0 and σE0). Based on the gathered data (Supplementary Table 2), we find that as lattice distortion increases, μEs decreases, while both σEs and σE0 increase. The reduction in μEs corresponds to lower migration barriers that promote vacancy jumps, while the increase in σE0 enhances vacancy trapping at local sites. These two competing factors dictate vacancy dynamics and overall diffusivity.

Building on the species-resolved diffusion model with the input of these distributions, as detailed in our previous work44, we calculate the tracer diffusivity mediated by vacancies, as shown in Fig. 3d. The results confirm that severe lattice distortion exceeding 4.33% (Ni85Mo15) starts to inhibit vacancy diffusion. At this threshold, the effects of vacancy trapping outweigh those of enhanced vacancy jumps, leading to suppressed diffusion. Furthermore, Fig. 3d highlights that although the diffusivity increases with temperature due to the thermally activated nature of vacancy migration, the compositional trends remain consistent across the entire temperature range from 600 to 1000 K. This consistency underscores the critical influence of lattice distortion in governing defect mobility over a wide range of temperatures. Notably, the disparity in diffusivity between alloys with weak and strong lattice distortion becomes more pronounced at lower temperatures. This trend can be rationalized by the preferential activation of low-barrier migration pathways in MPEAs exhibiting a broad distribution of migration energies, which in turn amplifies vacancy trapping and correlation effects in compositionally complex systems45. These results imply that the trapping effect of lattice distortion is even stronger at the 300 K irradiation temperature. As such, the suppression of vacancy diffusion under high lattice distortion scenarios may be even more significant than depicted in Fig. 3d.

Our analysis demonstrates that moderate lattice distortion simultaneously reduces μEs while increasing σE0. Since the σE0 value remains small in this regime, the reduction in μEs dominates, resulting in enhanced vacancy diffusion. In contrast, severe lattice distortion significantly increases σE0, which diminishes the possibility of net forward diffusion, resulting in substantial local trapping of vacancies and suppressed long-range diffusion.

The decrease in μEs can be understood by the enlarged channel size at the saddle point during vacancy migration48, as depicted in Fig. 3e. The channel size is defined as the average bond length between the migrating atom at the saddle site (purple atom) and its first and second nearest neighbors (red atoms), normalized by the lattice constant (BondlengthLatticeconstant). To eliminate the effects of different chemical species, we specifically analyze cases with Ni as the migrating atom. Our results indicate that greater lattice distortion enlarges the migration channel at the transition state. This expansion is attributed to more pronounced displacements of the neighboring atoms, which shift away from the migrating species and toward the vacant sites to alleviate lattice strain. Consequently, the widened migration channel under enhanced lattice distortion directly contributes to the observed decrease in the migration barrier (μEs).

For σE0, which reflects the distribution of energy differences between two vacancy states, it is broadened continuously by lattice distortion. This trend is consistent with the widened formation energy distributions observed after considering atomic relaxations49. A higher degree of lattice distortion is typically associated with a greater structural relaxation, which leads to a significant energy release. This interpretation is supported by analyzing the displacements of the first nearest neighbors (1NN) atoms surrounding the vacancy (Supplementary Fig. 8). As lattice distortion increases, we observe both larger mean displacement and a broader distribution of 1NN atomic displacement. This behavior parallels the observed increase in σE0 with greater lattice distortion, suggesting that enhanced diversity of atomic displacements near vacancies is responsible for the wider energy distribution.

Discussion

Our findings reveal that the broadening of E0 is directly linked to the wider distribution of the increased atomic displacements around vacancies as lattice distortion intensifies. To demonstrate this further, we designed Ni80Mo10W10 alloys with a notably higher lattice distortion of 4.95%. Figure 3f illustrates the relationship between the standard deviation of E0 and the standard deviation of the displacement of 1NN atoms around vacancies in Ni80Mo10W10, along with the values for all materials studied. Supplementary Fig. 9 shows the trajectory of a single vacancy in Ni80Mo10W10 simulated at 1000 K for 5 ns, revealing more pronounced vacancy trapping compared to Ni80Mo20, thereby reinforcing our analysis.

Irradiation-resistant structural materials are essential for future nuclear energy development. Three proposed pathways for material design include selecting matrix phases with inherent irradiation tolerance, choosing materials with immobile defects, and engineering materials with high sink densities8. MPEAs with extreme chemical disorder are promising due to their inherent resistance to irradiation, which is attributed to disorder-induced slow energy dissipation and defect evolution18,30,50,51. Such disorder often diminishes the difference between interstitial and vacancy diffusion, as seen in the overlap of their migration barrier distributions, leading to enhanced defect recombination (Fig. 4a). As interstitials typically move orders of magnitude faster than vacancies, this overlap suggests that modest lattice distortion in MPEAs tends to slow down interstitials while accelerating vacancies, thereby promoting recombination.

At high lattice distortion, however, the increased 1NN displacement around vacancies raises σE0, strengthening correlation effects and localizing vacancy diffusion, which suppresses vacancy cluster growth and results in smaller clusters.

Despite these insights, the influence of lattice distortion on direct vacancy diffusion and clustering behavior remains a subject of debate. Some reports describe smaller and denser defect clusters3,26, whereas others observe larger clusters under similar irradiation conditions27,28. Table 2 summarizes the corresponding degrees of lattice distortion, irradiation conditions, and resulting distributions of interstitial and vacancy clusters from these studies3,26–28,52,53. Our results help reconcile these discrepancies by clarifying the distortion-dependent behavior of defects. At low distortion levels (δ<3%), the diffusivities of vacancies and interstitials become comparable, promoting efficient Frenkel pair recombination and thus producing smaller yet denser defect clusters. When the distortion increases to a moderate range (3% ≤ δ < 4%), interstitial diffusion is further hindered, while the widening of vacancy migration channels lowers the migration barrier, thereby enhancing vacancy mobility and promoting the formation of larger vacancy clusters, as confirmed in Table 2. Notably, these literature examples, which encompass a range of irradiation temperatures beyond room temperature as in our present work, show defect behaviors consistent with our conclusions, reinforcing the role of distortion in modulating mobility and clustering.

While increasing lattice distortion monotonically results in smaller, higher-density interstitial clusters, vacancy cluster evolution exhibits a non-monotonic dependence on distortion levels. At low distortion (δ<3%), vacancy clusters become smaller and more numerous; however, at moderate distortion (3% ≤ δ < 4%), the formation of larger vacancy clusters is observed.

Previous investigations have primarily examined alloys with low to moderate lattice distortion, where enhanced vacancy diffusion and suppressed interstitial mobility are dominant features. In contrast, our experiments and simulations reveal that once the lattice distortion exceeds 4%, a condition not previously achieved in FCC MPEAs, both vacancies and interstitials become effectively trapped, yielding smaller and sparser clusters. Notably, although severe lattice distortion lowers the vacancy migration barrier, vacancy diffusion is significantly hindered by the broad distribution of site energies (σE0) arising from pronounced 1NN displacements around vacancies, leading to strong correlation effects. This energy heterogeneity results in deep potential wells that locally trap vacancies and impede their long migration.

Taken together, our findings indicate a two-stage effect of lattice distortion on defect evolution: slight-to-moderate distortion enhances recombination by balancing defect mobilities, while severe distortion suppresses defect migration through local trapping. This unified framework reconciles the previously conflicting experimental reports and demonstrates how lattice distortion can be strategically tuned to control defect behavior in irradiated materials, as illustrated in Fig. 4b.

The immobilization of interstitials as well as vacancies in MPEAs with a high degree of lattice distortion facilitates the creation of ubiquitous point defect recombination centers, resulting in the formation of discrete point defects rather than large clusters. This aligns with our simulation results, which demonstrate that nearly all defects generated in Ni80Mo20 are point defects (Supplementary Fig. 10). To further bridge the simulation results with experimental conditions, we extended irradiation simulations to 10 dpa using the state-of-the-art creation relaxation algorithm (CRA) method54, matching experimental dose levels. These high-dose simulations reveal the same fundamental trend observed in cascade simulations: defects in Ni80Mo20 persist primarily as isolated point defects or small clusters, whereas defects in Ni readily coarsen into large aggregates (Supplementary Fig. 11). This effective defect immobilization directly mitigates irradiation-induced microstructural and mechanical degradation, including volumetric swelling and irradiation-induced hardening (as discussed in Supplementary Note VII and VIII)9,10. Therefore, our findings establish lattice distortion as a crucial design parameter for radiation-resistant MPEAs. By maximizing lattice distortion in the Ni-Mo system, the migration of both interstitials and vacancies can be suppressed. Hence, this approach offers a promising pathway for developing advanced materials for nuclear applications.

In summary, we present a design strategy for irradiation-resistant MPEAs by immobilizing both interstitials and vacancies through strategically engineered lattice distortion. Our findings reveal that while slight distortion impedes interstitial migration and enhances vacancy diffusion, increased lattice distortion restricts the diffusion of both defect types, leading to smaller and sparser defect clusters post-irradiation. Given that lattice distortion is an inherent characteristic of MPEAs, this approach provides a powerful materials design lever for irradiation-resistant materials. Therefore, our research offers a streamlined strategy for MPEA design focused on modulating lattice distortion rather than complex alloy compositions to enhance performance for extreme environment applications.

Experiment

The Ni, NiFe, NiCoV, and Ni80Mo20 alloys were fabricated via arc melting followed by drop-casting. Subsequently, the as-cast ingots were subjected to a sequence of thermomechanical treatments. This process included homogenization at 1200 °C for 24 h with water quenching, cold rolling to a thickness reduction of ~90%, and final annealing at 800 °C–1100 °C, followed by water quenching. The ion irradiation experiments were conducted using the 1.7 MV tandem accelerator at Peking University. Before ion irradiation, the samples were sequentially polished by mechanical polishing followed by electropolishing to obtain a mirror-like surface. The samples were then irradiated at room temperature with 3 MeV Ni ions to a fluence of 1 × 1016 ions/cm2 and a dose rate of approximately 1.1 × 1012 ions/cm2/s.

Cross-sectional TEM specimens were prepared using a Thermo Scientific Helios 5UX focused ion beam (FIB) system. The lamellae were sequentially thinned at an accelerating voltage of 30 kV, with beam currents tapering from 0.3 nA to 50 pA, yielding an initial thickness of approximately 160 nm. To eliminate FIB-induced artifacts, such as amorphous layers and defects caused by Ga ion milling, a rapid electrochemical “flash polishing” technique was employed. This process was carried out at 14 V for 80 ms in an electrolyte solution of 4% perchloric acid and 96% ethanol. Consequently, the lamella thickness was reduced to 80-100 nm, suitable for TEM observation. The precise lamella thickness, essential for quantifying dislocation loop densities, was verified using both FIB cross-sectional imaging and the Convergent Beam Electron Diffraction (CBED) method.

Irradiation-induced defects were characterized using an FEI Talos 200X transmission electron microscope. Quantitative analysis of dislocation loops was performed via ImageJ software, with a statistical sample size of approximately 500 dislocation loops per specimen. The damage profile and implanted Ni concentration were calculated using SRIM-2013 in full-cascade mode based on the Kinchin–Pease model (K–P mode). In this model, the number of atomic displacements (ν) is estimated from the damage energy transferred to lattice atoms, assuming a series of binary elastic collisions. Specifically, ν=0 for energies below Ed, ν=1 for energies between Ed and 2Ed, and ν=0.8E/(2Ed) for higher energies, where E is the damage energy and Ed is the displacement threshold energy. The displacement threshold energy was set to 40 eV for all constituent elements. To ensure the validity of the data, TEM observations were restricted to a depth range of 450–950 nm. This region was strategically selected to avoid the confounding influences of the surface sink effect (0–200 nm) and the injected interstitial peak (1100–1300 nm). According to SRIM predictions, this target region corresponds to a dose range of 6–10 dpa, with a nominal average dose of approximately 8 dpa.

MD simulations

MD simulations were performed using the Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS, version 2 Aug 2023)58. The interatomic interactions for Ni, NiFe, NiCoV, and Ni80Mo20 were modeled using the embedded atom method (EAM) as proposed by Zhou et al.59, with the parameters for V developed in our previous work60. To accurately capture the short-range atom repulsions in displacement cascades, the Ziegler-Biersack-Littmark (ZBL) repulsive potential was smoothly splined with the EAM potential at short interatomic distances61.

The simulation cell for all the systems contained a 50×50×50 FCC lattice with periodic boundary conditions in all directions. Within each cell, elements were randomly distributed. Each system was initially equilibrated under an NPT ensemble at 300 K for 100 ps with a timestep of 1 fs. Cumulative damage was simulated by randomly selecting a Ni PKA and imparting it with an initial kinetic energy of 1 keV. The collision cascade was simulated using an NVE ensemble, with the timestep adjusted to limit atomic displacements to a maximum of 0.01 Å per timestep. After each cascade event, the system was relaxed under the NPT ensemble to relieve stress. Subsequently, another PKA was randomly chosen, and the system was shifted to ensure that the new PKA was centered in the simulation box before initiating the next cascade. This process was repeated until a total of 400 cascades were simulated. Finally, the produced defects, including vacancies and interstitials, were identified by the Wigner-Seitz (WS) defect analysis in the OVITO software (version 3.7.8)62.

Defect formation energies and migration energies were calculated to understand defect behaviors across varying lattice distortions. These simulations were carried out in 10×10×10 FCC cells for each alloy. Defect formation energy was calculated as Ef=Ed−Ep±Ndμd, where Ed and Ep are the total energies of defective and perfect cells, respectively. Nd and μd are the number and the chemical potential of the added (-) or removed (+) element to create point defects, respectively. μd is defined as the energy of an individual atom in its most stable elemental form. Migration energies were determined by the climbing-image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method63. Simulations employed a timestep of 0.01 fs and included 15 intermediate images, with the force convergence criterion set at 1×10−6 eV/Å. Additionally, to assess defect evolution, we simulated diffusion behaviors of single vacancy, single interstitial, and their clusters using an NPT ensemble at 1000 K, allowing adequate migration events for statistically reliable sampling within MD timescales.

Creation-relaxation algorithm

The high-dose irradiation in a crystalline solid was modeled using a creation-relaxation algorithm (CRA)54, where the dpa dose is defined as ϕ=NFIN, with N the total number of atoms and NFI the accumulated Frenkel-pair insertions. At each iteration, an atom is randomly selected and displaced to a random position within the simulation cell. The perturbed configuration is then relaxed via a conjugate-gradient energy-minimization algorithm. Once the minimum is reached, the NFI is incremented, and the updated ϕ is recorded. This cycle of random atomic displacements followed by relaxation is repeated until the desired dpa level is achieved.
