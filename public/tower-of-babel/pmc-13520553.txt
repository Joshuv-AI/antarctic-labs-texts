Pore-Scale Mechanisms of CO2–WAG Enhanced Oil Recovery in Tight Conglomerate Reservoirs: A Multicomponent Multiphase Lattice Boltzmann Study
Authors: Sai Liu, Bo Zhou, Huijie Song, Wenwen Liu, Qingxiang Liu, Dian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding pore-scale displacement mechanisms of CO2–water-alternating-gas (WAG) injection in tight conglomerate reservoirs is critical for improving enhanced oil recovery (EOR) efficiency in these strongly heterogeneous systems. This study develops a three-component (oil–water–CO2) multicomponent multiphase multiple-relaxation-time lattice Boltzmann (MCMP-MRT-LBM) pore-scale numerical framework based on a digitized pore microstructure model of Class I conglomerate reservoirs in the Mahu Sag, Junggar Basin. Systematic simulations are conducted to evaluate the effects of injection mode, cycle number, injection velocity, and CO2 miscibility degree on microscopic displacement efficiency. Results demonstrate that CO2–WAG injection achieves a maximum recovery factor of 46.65%, substantially exceeding post-waterflooding CO2 flooding (35.77%) and conventional waterflooding (∼18%). Three synergistic pore-scale mechanisms underpin WAG’s superiority: alternating injection generates a Jamin-effect bubble plugging that redirects flow from macropores into low-permeability micropores; periodic fluid switching suppresses cumulative viscous fingering; and complementary CO2 extraction and water imbibition mechanisms synergistically mobilize residual oil across diverse morphologies. An optimal cycle number of four is identified, with recovery exhibiting nonmonotonic dependence on cycle frequency. Displacement efficiency peaks at moderate capillary numbers (Ca ∼10–3), while maintaining reservoir pressure above the minimum miscibility pressure (∼32 MPa) is essential for effective micropore residual oil mobilization. These pore-scale findings provide mechanistic guidance for optimizing CO2–WAG programs in the Mahu Sag and analogous tight conglomerate reservoirs worldwide.

Introduction

Global energy demand continues to grow while conventional hydrocarbon resources face accelerating depletion. In this context, tight unconventional reservoirscharacterized by matrix porosity typically below 10–12% and permeability below 1 mDhave emerged as a strategic frontier for petroleum exploration and production worldwide. In China, proven reserves of tight oil and gas have expanded substantially over the past two decades, with tight conglomerate formations now representing a critical component of national energy security. Despite their enormous resource potential, primary recovery factors from tight formations universally remain below 8–10% of original oil in place (OOIP), while conventional secondary waterflooding yields combined recovery efficiencies that rarely surpass 15–20%. The majority of residual oil remains immobilized within microto-nanoscale pore networks as clusters, thin films, and dead-end configurations, largely inaccessible to conventional displacement mechanisms owing to strong capillary forces, unfavorable wettability, and extreme pore-scale , heterogeneity. Consequently, the development of effective enhanced oil recovery (EOR) strategies for tight unconventional reservoirs has become a research priority of considerable urgency.

Among the spectrum of EOR techniques evaluated for tight formations, water-alternating-gas (WAG) injection has attracted sustained attention owing to its dual capacity to simultaneously improve volumetric sweep efficiency and microscopic displacement efficiency. , First introduced in the late 1950s, WAG involves the cyclic alternation of water and gas injection slugs: injected water suppresses gas mobility and retards viscous fingering by reducing gas-phase relative permeability, while the injected gasmost commonly CO2 or a hydrocarbon gasreduces crude oil viscosity, promotes oil swelling, lowers interfacial tension, and, under near-miscible or miscible conditions, effectively eliminates the capillary barrier separating oil from the displacing fluid. ,,,,,, .The synergistic interplay of these complementary mechanisms enables WAG to deliver incremental recovery factors of 10–30% above conventional waterflooding, a performance range documented across major field implementations including the CO2–WAG operations in the Norwegian North Sea Snorre Field, the Permian Basin miscible WAG projects in the United States, and the WAG pilot program in the Bakken Formation of the Williston Basin. , Recent experimental and simulation studies have further deepened the mechanistic understanding of imbibition dynamics and CO2–oil displacement behavior in tight and heterogeneous reservoir systems. − Comprehensive reviews of WAG field experience confirm that more than 60 commercial-scale projects have been reported globally, demonstrating consistent incremental recovery in sandstone, carbonate, and mixed lithology systems. , Nevertheless, the translation of these macroscopic successes to the design of WAG programs in strongly heterogeneous tight formations remains problematic, primarily because the pore-scale displacement physics governing WAG in such complex pore systems are insufficiently understood.

The Mahu Sag, located along the western margin of the Junggar Basin in northwestern China, hosts the world’s largest proven onshore tight conglomerate oil accumulation, with total discovered reserves in the Permian–Triassic Baikouquan Formation exceeding 5 × 108 tonnes. , Reservoir depths range from 3500 to 4500 m, with average matrix porosity of 8–12% and permeability of 0.1–1 mD, reflecting a complex diagenetic history involving compaction, cementation, and tuffaceous dissolution. , A defining characteristic of Mahu conglomerate reservoirs is their pronounced microscopic heterogeneity: high-resolution microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) and mercury intrusion porosimetry (MICP) analyses consistently reveal a bimodal pore-throat size distribution in which intergravel macropores coexist with dissolution-generated micropores hosted in tuffaceous matrix cements, with the dominant pore-throat diameter range concentrated between 0.5 and 4 μm and permeability contrasts between the two pore populations exceeding 2 orders of magnitude. , This bimodal pore architecture exerts a fundamental control on fluid displacement during any injection EOR process, as injected fluids preferentially invade the high-permeability macropore network while large volumes of oil in the low-permeability micropore population remain largely unswept. , The extreme reservoir heterogeneity is further compounded by a spatially variable mixed-wettability state: detrital grains in the Mahu Baikouquan Formation display strongly water-wet behavior (contact angle ∼30°), whereas tuffaceous interstitial cements exhibit locally oil-wet character (contact angle up to ∼140°), reflecting compositional differences in surface mineralogy and organic coatings. The coexistence of contrasting wettability domains promotes contact-line pinning at grain-scale wettability boundaries, stabilizing residual oil in complex cluster, bridge, and thin-film configurations that resist conventional mobilization.

A CO2–WAG pilot program conducted in the Mahu Sag in 2019 illustrated the severity of these technical challenges in practice. Gas breakthrough occurred at an injected pore volume (PV) of only approximately 0.35, followed by a rapid escalation of produced water cut to 68%, with ultimate oil recovery falling substantially below the design target. Post-mortem diagnostic analysis indicated that the extreme heterogeneity of the Baikouquan conglomerate promoted preferential gas invasion along high-permeability macropore pathways, generating severe viscous fingering and early gas channeling while leaving large volumes of oil marooned in the low-permeability micropore population. The mixed-wettability texture further complicated fluid redistribution by generating contact-line pinning effects at wettability boundaries, stabilizing residual oil in topologically complex configurations including ganglia, bridges, and oil-wet films. , Because reservoir core samples from the Mahu Sag are costly to acquire and conventional core-flood experiments are constrained by sample dimensions and achievable operating conditions, the fundamental pore-scale displacement mechanisms governing CO2–WAG flooding in this system have remained poorly resolvedrepresenting a critical barrier to the rational design and optimization of field-scale WAG programs.

Pore-scale numerical simulation has emerged as an indispensable complement to physical experimentation for elucidating multiphase displacement physics in complex reservoir rock. ,,, Among available pore-scale methodsincluding pore-network modeling, direct numerical simulation via volume-of-fluid or level-set approaches, and phase-field formulationsthe Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM) is particularly well suited to WAG-type problems owing to its inherent computational parallelism, straightforward treatment of arbitrarily complex solid boundaries derived from micro-CT imaging, and the ability to capture fluid interface dynamics without explicit interface-reconstruction algorithms. , The Shan–Chen multicomponent multiphase (MCMP) pseudopotential formulation of LBM provides a physically consistent framework for three-phase (water–oil–gas) systems, reproducing wetting phenomena via tunable fluid–solid interaction parameters and capturing interfacial events including snap-off, Haines jumps, and contact-line motion across mixed-wettability surfaces. , The adoption of multiple-relaxation-time (MRT) collision operatorsrather than the simpler single-relaxation-time Bhatnagar–Gross–Krook (BGK) schemehas been demonstrated to significantly suppress numerical instabilities and spurious interfacial velocities at high viscosity ratios exceeding 100:1, which are representative of realistic reservoir systems in which large viscosity contrasts exist among brine, crude oil, and supercritical CO2. , Recent GPU-accelerated MCMP-MRT-LBM implementations have further enabled three-phase displacement simulations in digitized conglomerate and sandstone microstructures at pore-throat-scale resolution, yielding relative permeability predictions and residual saturation distributions in quantitative agreement with micromodel observations and core-flood experiments. , Collectively, these methodological advances have been applied to CO2 flooding in heterogeneous sandstones, , pore-scale WAG displacement in idealized water-wet porous media, and simulations of CO2 geological sequestration with concurrent EOR; however, the systematic application of MCMP-MRT-LBM to multicycle CO2–WAG flooding in realistic bimodal, mixed-wettability conglomerate pore architecturesunder a parametric range of engineering conditions directly relevant to field operationshas not yet been reported.

The foregoing analysis identifies three critical knowledge gaps that motivate the present study: (i) no pore-scale LBM investigation has yet addressed WAG flooding within the authentic bimodal, strongly heterogeneous, mixed-wettability pore structure of tight conglomerate reservoirs of the Mahu type; (ii) existing pore-scale WAG simulations are predominantly restricted to two-phase, single-cycle displacement and do not capture the cumulative hysteretic effects of multicycle water–gas alternation on residual oil topology, relative permeability, and gas channeling behavior; (iii) the quantitative sensitivity of WAG-induced microscopic oil recovery to key operational variablesincluding injection cycle number, individual slug size, injection velocity, and degree of CO2 miscibilityhas not been systematically established at the pore scale, precluding the derivation of direct optimization guidance for field programs. To address these gaps, the present study constructs a three-phase MCMP-MRT-LBM pore-scale numerical model embedded in a digital conglomerate microstructure representative of the Mahu Sag, with a uniform wettability calibrated from measured contact angles for Mahu reservoir minerals. The model is used to conduct systematic simulations of waterflooding, gas flooding, and multicycle CO2–WAG displacement under a parametric range of injection configurations. The primary objectives of this work are (1) to quantitatively characterize the pore-scale displacement mechanismsincluding gas fingering pathways, capillary snap-off, wettability-controlled residual oil trapping, and multicycle fluid redistributionthat distinguish WAG from single-phase injection modes in Mahu conglomerate; (2) to establish the quantitative effects of injection cycle number, injection rate, and CO2 miscibility degree on microscopic recovery efficiency and residual oil morphology; and (3) to provide mechanistic pore-scale guidance for optimizing CO2–WAG injection strategies in the Mahu Sag and, by extension, in analogous tight conglomerate reservoirs worldwide.

Model Construction and Methodology

Conglomerate Microstructure Characterization

Accurate characterization of the pore structure of conglomerate reservoirs is a fundamental prerequisite for constructing representative pore-scale models and conducting pore-scale numerical simulations. Compared with sandstone or shale reservoirs, conglomerate reservoirs exhibit complex grain compositions and strong heterogeneity, making it difficult for any single characterization technique to comprehensively reveal their multiscale pore structure features. , This study takes numerous conglomerate core samples from the Mahu Sag as research objects and systematically employs multiple complementary experimental methodsincluding scanning electron microscopy (SEM), high-pressure mercury intrusion (HPMI), low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), and X-ray computed tomography (CT)to characterize key parameters such as reservoir pore types, pore size distributions, pore connectivity, and wettability. This multimethod approach ultimately provides authentic and reliable porous medium geometrical models for pore-scale Lattice Boltzmann simulations.

Basic Physical Properties and Mineral Composition

All core samples tested in this study were collected from Class I reservoir production intervals of the Mahu Sag conglomerate in the Junggar Basin, Xinjiang. Helium porosimetry and a gas permeameter were employed for conventional physical property measurements. Test results indicate that sample porosity ranges from 1.9% to 15.3% with a mean of approximately 8.24%; pore diameters range from 4.36 to 216.97 μm with a mean of 26.7 μm; pore-throat ratios range from 4 to 7 with a mean of 6.06; and permeability ranges from 0.01 to 21.72 mD with a mean of approximately 5.48 mD (coefficient of variation 0.2), indicating overall strong heterogeneity.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis indicates that the reservoir is dominated by coarse-grained clastic rocks: sandy conglomerate (39.6%), fine-gravel conglomerate (20.8%), small-pebble conglomerate (15.4%), and coarse sandstone (15.4%), with coarse lithologies cumulatively exceeding 90%. The mineral assemblage is dominated by feldspar (approximately 35–45 wt %) and quartz (approximately 20–30 wt %), accompanied by calcite (approximately 5–15 wt %), dolomite (approximately 3–8 wt %), and minor clay minerals including Illite and chlorite (approximately 5–10 wt %). Volcanic rock fragment content varies substantially among subareas (20–60 wt %), and tuffaceous interstitial cement is locally developed in some samples, forming micropore zones. The diversity and heterogeneous spatial distribution of mineral components jointly control the pore structure characteristics and wettability heterogeneity of the reservoir.

Based on analysis of numerous cast thin sections, pore types in the Mahu conglomerate reservoir are dominated by residual primary intergravel pores (equivalent radius 30–100 μm) and intragrain dissolution pores (mainly within feldspar grains, 0.5–10 μm), together accounting for approximately 93.5% of total pore volume; microfractures and shrinkage fractures are locally developed (∼5.6%); and other pore types such as intercrystalline pores account for approximately 0.9%. As shown in Figure , the pore system can be classified into two major categories: (1) intergravel macropores, controlled by the original particle packing configuration, with large pore sizes (equivalent radius 30–100 μm) and good connectivity, serving as the primary fluid flow conduits; and (2) tuffaceous dissolution micropores, mainly distributed in tuffaceous interstitial cements, typically with diameters less than 1 μm, existing in isolated or weakly connected states that contribute substantially to porosity but minimally to permeability. The contrasting volume fractions of these two pore populations constitute the pronounced bimodal pore structure characteristic of this reservoir.

Construction of the Porous Medium Model for Simulation

Based on the multiscale characterization results described above, ImageJ software was used to process representative cast thin section images and CT scan cross-sectional images to extract the microstructural morphology. The specific processing steps were: (1) contrast enhancement and 8-bit grayscale conversion; (2) median filtering for noise reduction; (3) global threshold binarization (solid/pore phase) using Otsu’s algorithm; and (4) connected component analysis to remove isolated noise pixels. With reference to the volumetric fractions and spatial distribution of intergravel macropores and tuffaceous micropores identified from CT three-dimensional reconstruction, two-dimensional binarized images were integrated and optimized with pore network parameters (mean coordination number, tortuosity, and pore-throat ratio) through geometric similarity criteria. The final simulation porous medium model consists of a 400 × 400 lattice grid at a resolution of approximately 0.5 μm/lattice unit, as shown in Figure .

Displacement comparison of single pore channel.

The selection of 400 × 400 lattice resolution was based on three criteria. First, representativeness: at 0.5 μm/lattice unit, the 200 × 200 μm2 physical domain encompasses approximately 15–20 complete intergravel macropore units along with surrounding tuffaceous micropore zones; pore-throat statistical parameters (coordination number, tortuosity, and pore-throat ratio) computed from this domain deviate less than 8% from multifield averages obtained from CT image analysis, satisfying the representative elementary volume (REV) convergence criterion. Second, grid independence: comparative simulations were conducted at 200 × 200, 400 × 400, and 600 × 600 resolutions under identical injection conditions; final recovery factors differ by less than 1.2% between the 400 × 400 and 600 × 600 cases, while the 200 × 200 case deviates by 6.8% from the 400 × 400 result, confirming that grid independence is achieved at 400 × 400. Third, computational efficiency: the 600 × 600 resolution requires approximately 2.25 times the computational cost of 400 × 400; given that the study involves more than 20 parametric simulation cases requiring systematic comparison, the 400 × 400 resolution represents the optimal balance between accuracy and efficiency.

It should be noted that all simulations in this study are performed on a two-dimensional (2D) pore structure extracted from thin sections, rather than a three-dimensional (3D) digital core. This approach is adopted based on the following justifications. First, from a computational feasibility standpoint, a 3D equivalent model at the same resolution would require approximately 2 orders of magnitude greater computational cost, making systematic parametric scanning across injection mode, cycle number, velocity, and miscibility conditions impractical within the present computational framework. Second, from a representativeness perspective, the 2D model was verified against pore network statistical parameters (coordination number, pore-throat ratio, and tortuosity) derived from 3D CT reconstruction; deviations remain below 8%, confirming that the selected 2D cross-section captures the essential structural features of the Mahu conglomerate pore system. Third, from a methodological precedent perspective, numerous published pore-scale LBM studies on CO2 flooding and WAG displacement have demonstrated that 2D models reliably capture key displacement mechanismsincluding viscous fingering suppression, capillary snap-off, and pore-blocking effectsin qualitative and semiquantitative agreement with experimental observations. ,,,, Nevertheless, three-dimensional connectivity effects, such as bypass flow around grain clusters and complex pore network topology, are not fully captured in the present 2D framework, and this constitutes a recognized limitation of the study. Extension to full 3D MCMP-MRT-LBM simulations using the digitized CT core is identified as an important direction for future work.

Construction of the MCMP-MRT-LBM Three-Phase Flow Model

The Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM) evolved from lattice gas automata and can also be derived rigorously from the continuous Boltzmann equation. The LBM equation is obtained by discretizing the Boltzmann equation in velocity, time, and space. The fundamental concept of LBM is to describe the collision and migration of particles in discrete space and to recover macroscopic fluid variables by statistical processing of the particle distribution functions. The LBM evolution equation therefore comprises two substeps: collision and streaming. Based on the number of relaxation times contained in the collision operator, LBM models are classified into the single-relaxation-time (LBGK) model and the multiple-relaxation-time (MRT) model. The MRT model improves numerical stability, increases the achievable fluid viscosity ratio, and reduces fluid–solid boundary errors, making it superior for problems involving large viscosity ratios and interphase mass transfer. To simulate the oil–water–CO2 three-phase WAG displacement process in the Mahu conglomerate reservoir, this study constructs a three-component (oil, water, and CO2) MCMP-MRT-LBM model based on the multicomponent multiphase (MCMP) Shan-Chen pseudopotential model , combined with the multiple-relaxation-time (MRT) collision operator.

Boundary Conditions

At the model inlet (left boundary), velocity boundary conditions are applied, with injection velocities for the water phase, CO2 phase, or alternating phases implemented in stages according to the injection program. At the outlet (right boundary), pressure (density) boundary conditions maintain a constant pressure. The upper and lower boundaries employ no-slip bounce-back boundary conditions (standard half-step bounce-back scheme). No-slip conditions at fluid–solid interfaces are handled using the standard halfway bounce-back (HWBB) scheme.

Initial conditions are set as follows: the pore space is fully saturated with the oil phase prior to waterflooding; after waterflooding, the state is adjusted to oil–water two-phase coexistence prior to gas injection. Injection volumes are quantified in pore volume multiples (PV), and displacement front advancement is tracked in real time through the evolution of the fluid density field.

Model Validation

Prior to conducting systematic WAG displacement simulations, the MCMP-MRT-LBM three-phase model is rigorously validated at three levels: (1) Laplace law validation (interfacial tension); (2) parameter settings and physical similarity; and (3) contact angle validation (wettability) with comparative validation against microscopic experimental results.

Laplace Law Validation

The MCMP-MRT-LBM model captures interfacial tension through fluid–fluid interaction forces, allowing the three phases to undergo spontaneous separation in the porous medium model. Parameter Settings

Conversion between dimensionless LBM parameters and physical reservoir parameters follows physical similarity criteria, with the capillary number Ca (the ratio of viscous to capillary forces, defined as the product of displacing fluid viscosity and velocity divided by interfacial tension) serving as the primary dimensionless governing parameter. , All simulations are conducted at capillary numbers consistent with Mahu field injection conditions. The spatial scale correspondence is established at 0.5 μm per lattice unit, so the 400 × 400 lattice domain corresponds to a physical area of 200 × 200 μm2. The dominant pore-throat diameters of the Mahu reservoir (0.5–4 μm, as characterized in Section ) are thereby resolved by 1–8 lattice nodes, which is sufficient to capture pore-scale interfacial dynamics with adequate fidelity. The fluid–fluid interaction parameter G cc in the Shan–Chen model directly controls the interfacial tension between fluid pairs, as confirmed by the linear calibration relationship shown in Figure b. Based on this calibration, the oil–water interfacial tension in the simulation is set to correspond to a physical value consistent with the Mahu reservoir oil–water system (∼28 mN/m at representative reservoir conditions). For CO2-oil pairs, three distinct G cc values are selected to match the three pressure and interfacial tension scenarios described below: near-zero interfacial tension for the miscible state (reservoir pressure 35 MPa, above MMP), very low interfacial tension (∼0.1 mN/m) for the near-miscible state (pressure ∼32 MPa, near MMP), and moderate interfacial tension (∼1 mN/m) for the immiscible state (pressure ∼25 MPa, below MMP). These correspondences ensure that the simulated capillary numbers and flow regimes are physically representative of the respective Mahu field injection scenarios. ,

Miscible State

Fluid interaction parameter G = 1, formation pressure 35 MPa, interfacial tension σ = 0; representing the actual conditions at the typical burial depth of the Mahu target intervals (pressure above MMP).

Near-Miscible State

Fluid interaction parameter G = 1.8, formation pressure 32 MPa, interfacial tension σ ≈ 0.1 mN/m; representing an injection scenario where injection pressure is slightly below MMP.

Immiscible State

Fluid interaction parameter G = 2, formation pressure 25 MPa, interfacial tension σ ≈ 1 mN/m; representing conditions where CO2 displaces in the gas phase due to near-wellbore pressure depletion or suboptimal injection parameters.

These parameter settings allow simulation results to directly correspond to different gas injection pressure scenarios at the Mahu field, providing a quantitative basis for optimizing the injection-production parameters.

Contact Angle Validation

Contact angle validation is accomplished by simulating droplet equilibrium morphology on a flat solid surface. , In a 400 × 100 lattice computational domain with a solid wall at the bottom, the domain is initialized with oil, and rectangular water and gas regions of side length 40 are placed symmetrically about the centerline. After evolution to steady state, the droplet profile is fitted to quantify the static contact angle θ. By adjusting the fluid–solid interaction parameter G ads, the contact angle can be controlled over the range 10°–170°. , Based on experimental results from the Mahu conglomerate, the calibrated model reproduces a water contact angle of 34° and a CO2 contact angle of 160° on the reservoir mineral surfaces, as shown in Figure .

It should be clarified that in the present model, a uniform wettability is assigned to all solid nodes, with the calibrated contact angles (34° for water, 160° for CO2) representing the overall average wettability characteristics of the Mahu conglomerate mineral surfaces. While the Mahu Baikouquan Formation is characterized by spatially variable mixed wettability in the real reservoirwith water-wet detrital grain surfaces (∼30°) coexisting with locally oil-wet tuffaceous cement regions (∼140°)the explicit spatial assignment of heterogeneous wettability was not implemented in the current 2D model. This simplification is due to the difficulty in reliably distinguishing mineral types at pore-scale resolution from thin-section images alone, and reflects the study’s primary focus on the systematic parametric effects of injection strategy variables (injection mode, cycle number, velocity, and miscibility degree) rather than wettability heterogeneity per se. The implementation of spatially distributed mixed wettability based on mineral mapping from high-resolution images is identified as an important extension for future work.

Results and Discussion

This section presents and discusses the pore-scale simulation results for all of the injection scenarios. Simulation parameter settings and the complete scenario matrix are described in Section .

Post-Waterflooding CO2 Flooding

The post-waterflooding CO2 flooding scenario serves as the baseline for all comparative analyses in this paper. In this scenario, water injection accounts for 50% of total injection volume (∼0.6 PV), followed by switching to pure CO2 injection. Oil recovery factor (EOR) curve for post-waterflooding CO2 flooding.

Fluid Distribution Evolution during the Waterflooding Stage

In the early waterflooding stage (t < 1000 ts), injected water preferentially advances along macropore channels. Due to the permeability contrast between macropores and micropores, the displacement front exhibits pronounced nonuniform advancement. In macropore-dominant zones, the water phase rapidly penetrates in the form of viscous fingering, while large volumes of oil are bypassed and trapped in the micropore network. As waterflooding progresses (1000 ts < t < 5000 ts), redistribution of capillary pressure causes some micropore oil to be slowly mobilized through Haines jump mechanisms, with residual oil gradually transitioning from continuous clusters to isolated small ganglia, dead-end oil, and corner oil configurations. At the end of waterflooding (t ≈ 10,000 ts), approximately 65% of residual oil resides in micropore zones as isolated droplets and thin films, while 35% remains in macropores as clusters. This distribution confirms that heterogeneous pore structure is the fundamental cause of limited waterflooding efficiency.

The cumulative recovery factor exhibits rapid initial growth followed by gradual deceleration. At approximately 0.3 PV injection, the waterflooding front breaks through the outlet, after which recovery depends primarily on slow capillary-driven imbibition. The waterflooding stage recovery factor is approximately 18.2%, broadly consistent with Mahu field measurements (∼17–19% at 68% water cut), further validating model reliability.

Displacement Mechanisms during the CO2 Flooding Stage

After switching to CO2 injection, the near-zero interfacial tension under miscible conditions allows CO2 to contact crude oil in a fully miscible manner, generating three synergistic displacement effects: (1) swelling effectCO2 dissolution in crude oil causes the oil phase to expand, driving cluster oil out of macropores; (2) viscosity reduction effectmiscible CO2 reduces crude oil viscosity, improves mobility ratio, and mitigates viscous fingering; and (3) extraction effectsupercritical CO2 extracts light hydrocarbon components, enabling mass transfer at the oil-CO2 contact surface. Under miscible conditions, effective capillary pressure vanishes, and CO2 can enter micropores inaccessible to conventional waterflooding, mobilizing dead-end oil and thin-film oil through combined diffusion and flow mechanisms. Simulation images show that within approximately 3000 ts of CO2 injection, some isolated oil droplets in micropores shrink appreciably and some thin-film oil is completely dissolved.

However, post-waterflooding CO2 flooding also exhibits severe gas channeling. CO2 breakthrough occurs after approximately 2000 ts of injection due to CO2’s low density and the high permeability of macropore channels. After breakthrough, CO2 flows rapidly along established pathways, stalling growth of the effective displacement area and causing the recovery curve to flatten. The final total recovery factor is 35.77%, representing an improvement of approximately 17.6% over pure waterflooding (∼18.2%). This result reveals the inherent limitations of the single-stage sequential gas flood approach in heterogeneous conglomerate reservoirs.

Comparison of Injection Modes

To elucidate the effect of different injection sequences on displacement efficiency, three injection modes are systematically compared under miscible conditions at equal total injection volume (∼1.2 PV): (1) post-waterflooding CO2 flooding (WF → GF, baseline); (2) post-gas flooding water injection (GF → WF); and (3) water-alternating-gas (WAG, 2 cycles, ∼0.3 PV per half-cycle).

Displacement Characteristics of Post-Gas Flooding Water Injection

Post-gas flooding water injection yields a final recovery factor of 40.90%, surpassing the baseline by approximately 5.13%. Simulation results are shown in Figure a,b. The advantage of prioritizing CO2 injection is that, before preferential macropore channels are occupied by water, CO2 can invade a greater fraction of macropores with lower flow resistance and rapidly mobilize oil through miscible dissolution, forming a more uniform CO2 sweep zone than in the post-waterflooding scenario.

Simulation results ((a) CO2 flooding; (b) water flooding after CO2 flooding, corresponding to post-gas flooding water injection; (c) the first cycle of WAG; and (d) the second cycle of WAG, corresponding to 2-cycle WAG).

After completing the CO2 flooding stage, switching to water injection achieves two effects: injected water advances along CO2-modified (viscosity-reduced) oil channels at lower resistance, helping to push the CO2-oil miscible front toward the outlet; simultaneously, water creates a "sealing" effect in CO2 breakthrough channels, redirecting subsequent fluid into unswept micropore zones. However, post-gas flooding water injection still encounters early gas channeling during the initial CO2 stage and declining efficiency during the later water stageindicating that sequential injection order adjustment alone cannot fundamentally resolve fingering and gas channeling.

Effect of Injection Cycle Number

Keeping total injection volume (∼1.2 PV) and miscible conditions constant, three cycle number scenarios are simulated: 2 cycles (0.3 PV per half-cycle), 4 cycles (0.2 PV per half-cycle), and 6 cycles (0.1 PV per half-cycle). Final recovery factors are 44.04%, 46.65%, and 45.49%, respectively. The differences among the three cycle scenarios are 2.61 percentage points between 4-cycle and 2-cycle WAG, and 1.16 percentage points between 4-cycle and 6-cycle WAG, representing relative changes of 5.9% and 2.5%, respectively. While modest in absolute magnitude, these differences reflect fundamentally distinct underlying mechanisms: the 2-cycle to 4-cycle improvement arises from more frequent deployment of the Jamin-effect bubble plugging and front-reconstruction mechanisms; the 4-cycle to 6-cycle decline reflects CO2 slug fragmentation and near-inlet trapping when individual slug volumes fall below approximately 0.1 PV per half-cycle, reducing the per-slug displacement reach and increasing capillary trapping losses. 4-Cycle WAG

The 4-cycle WAG achieves the highest recovery factor (46.65%), surpassing 2-cycle by approximately 2.61% and 6-cycle by approximately 1.16%. The EOR curve of 4-cycle WAG maintains the highest growth rate throughout the displacement process without a pronounced plateau, indicating the effective mobilization of new residual oil zones within each cycle. Compared with the 2-cycle scenario, 4-cycle WAG more frequently deploys the bubble plugging and fingering suppression mechanisms on shorter time scales, resealing gas channeling pathways at each switch and producing a more uniform displacement front. Simulation images at t = 20,000 ts show a more dispersed residual oil distribution and smaller oil cluster sizes in the 4-cycle scenario, reflecting more thorough expulsion of macropore cluster oil.

6-Cycle WAG

The recovery factor of 6-cycle WAG (45.49%) is lower than that of 4-cycle despite having the highest switching frequency. This nonmonotonic behavior reveals the existence of an optimal cycle range: when the injection volume per half-cycle is too small, individual CO2 slugs cannot fully diffuse through the pore space and mobilize residual oil before the next fluid switch, leading to repeated near-inlet cycling with limited contribution to far-field residual oil. Simultaneously, excessive switching wastes time on repeated front reconstructions. Furthermore, finer CO2 slugs are more prone to capillary trapping at pore throats in 6-cycle WAG, forming isolated gas-phase residuals that block subsequent fluid flow paths and reduce the CO2 utilization efficiency. The lower slope of the 6-cycle EOR curve during the middle-to-late period (t > 15,000 ts) confirms entry into a regime of diminishing returns.

Effect of Injection Velocity

Under 4-cycle WAG and miscible conditions, two injection velocity scenarios are simulated: v = 0.2 lu/ts (high velocity) and v = 0.1 lu/ts (low velocity). Final recovery factors are baseline velocity (v = 0.15 lu/ts) 46.65%, high velocity 41.99%, and low velocity 31.85%. The velocity sensitivity is markedly asymmetric: reducing injection velocity from the baseline to the low-velocity scenario causes a 14.80 percentage point drop in recovery (46.65% → 31.85%), a 31.7% relative reduction, whereas increasing velocity to the high-velocity scenario results in only a 4.66 percentage point drop (46.65% → 41.99%), a 10.0% relative reduction. This strong asymmetry demonstrates that suboptimal low capillary numbers (Ca ≈2 × 10–3) impose a far more severe penaltythrough pervasive CO2 snap-off, isolated bubble formation, and injectivity lossthan moderately elevated capillary numbers (Ca ≈4 × 10–3), which primarily accelerate fingering without inducing the catastrophic pore-blocking mechanism observed at low velocity. High-Velocity Injection

The high-velocity scenario (v = 0.2 lu/ts) yields a final recovery factor of 41.99%, approximately 4.66% below the baseline 4-cycle WAG. Although high-velocity injection increases the viscous force of the displacing fluid, in heterogeneous conglomerate pores, this primarily accelerates fluid flow in macropore channels, further exacerbating viscous fingering. The elevated capillary number causes displacing fluid to preferentially surge along macropores without sufficiently sweeping adjacent micropore zones, significantly reducing the effective sweep volume. The EOR curve shows early stage growth slightly ahead of the baseline scenario, but after CO2 breakthrough, the growth rapidly levels off. Pore-scale simulation further reveals that elevated local Reynolds numbers at macropore throats make inertial effects non-negligible, causing CO2 to bypass micropore throats without entering micropores.

Low-Velocity Injection

The low-velocity scenario (v = 0.1 lu/ts) yields the lowest final recovery factor of 31.85%, a result that appears counterintuitive since lower velocities are generally considered favorable for capillary-driven imbibition. Two adverse effects arising from the excessively low capillary number explain this outcome. First, the displacement front advances extremely slowly and injected fluid accumulates at macropore entrances without establishing a continuous front before the next fluid switch, causing each injection event to fail to develop effective continuous flow. Second, under low capillary number conditions, CO2 entering macropores is subject to strong capillary confinement and undergoes extensive snap-off at pore throats, forming large numbers of isolated CO2 bubbles. These trapped bubbles not only fail to displace oil but also block subsequent fluid flow paths and substantially reduce injectivity. Simulation images at t = 20,000 ts show numerous isolated CO2 bubbles dispersed throughout the pore network, with water flowing around them and the displacement front highly irregularconfirming severe deficiencies in stable front advancement under low-velocity injection.

Comparison of Miscible and Near-Miscible Conditions

The difference between miscible (46.65%) and near-miscible (45.57%) conditions is only approximately 1.08%, indicating that once reservoir pressure approaches or exceeds MMP, WAG sweep efficiencyrather than interfacial tensionis the dominant factor controlling recovery. This observation is consistent with recent findings on CO2–oil interaction mechanisms and imbibition-dominated displacement reported in the literature. , Provided the CO2-crude oil interfacial tension is sufficiently low (σ < 0.1 mN/m), further reducing σ to zero produces limited marginal benefit. From a pore-scale perspective, CO2 under near-miscible conditions retains strong extraction capacity and can enter most macropores and produce diffusion-driven displacement in micropores. Compared with fully miscible conditions, near-miscible CO2 leaves stronger capillary blockages for some micropore thin-film oil, but multiple WAG perturbations still mobilize most of the movable micropore oil.

Displacement Characteristics under Immiscible Conditions

The immiscible condition (40.53%) is approximately 6.12% lower than that of the miscible case, revealing the critical role of miscibility. Under immiscible conditions, distinct CO2-oil interfacial tension makes capillary forces the dominant control on pore-scale fluid distribution with two adverse consequences. First, immiscible CO2 cannot overcome pore-throat capillary resistance to enter micropores, confining its effective displacement range primarily to macropore skeleton channels. Second, the relatively large Laplace pressure of immiscible CO2 bubbles at pore throats both produces a stronger Jamin effect (beneficial for gas channeling suppression) and causes a notable injectivity decline, particularly in micropore-dense zones.

Despite lower efficiency, immiscible WAG (40.53%) still exceeds the post-waterflooding CO2 flooding baseline (35.77%) by approximately 4.76%, confirming that the sweep efficiency advantage of WAG injection persists under immiscible conditions. This is directly relevant to Mahu field operations: when the bottom-hole flowing pressure cannot be maintained above the MMP, WAG injection remains superior to continuous gas flooding. Quantitative comparison of micropore residual oil saturation at t = 20,000 ts across the three miscibility conditions confirms progressively increasing micropore residual oil from miscible to near-miscible to immiscible conditions.

The pore-scale mechanisms of micropore residual oil mobilization display strong miscibility dependence: (1) under miscible conditions, CO2 penetrates micropores via diffusion-dominated mass transfer, effectively mobilizing both thin-film and dead-end oil; (2) under near-miscible conditions, CO2 mass transfer capacity is reduced and mobilization is primarily limited to larger micropores through interface deformation and capillary pressure redistribution; and (3) under immiscible conditions, the distinct CO2-oil interface prevents CO2 from penetrating the micropore network, leaving micropore residual oil practically immobile.

The miscibility-dependent recovery gap can be precisely quantified from the simulation results: immiscible WAG (40.53%) falls 6.12 percentage points below miscible WAG (46.65%), corresponding to a 13.1% relative reduction in recovery. The near-miscible condition (45.57%) narrows this gap to only 1.08 percentage points below the miscible case, confirming that once reservoir pressure approaches MMP, the further reduction of CO2-oil interfacial tension to zero yields diminishing incremental benefit. Crucially, even immiscible WAG still surpasses the post-waterflooding CO2 flooding baseline (35.77%) by 4.76 percentage points, confirming that WAG’s mobility-control advantages persist independently of miscibility. These figures underscore that maintaining reservoir pressure above MMP (∼32 MPa) is the single most impactful operational lever for maximizing WAG recovery in the Mahu conglomerate: failure to do so costs approximately 13% of the total achievable WAG-enhanced recovery relative to the optimal miscible scenario.

The pore-scale mechanisms identified in the present study are broadly consistent with and extend recent LBM simulation findings in the literature. Wang et al. investigated immiscible CO2 injection modes in water-wet porous media using a similar MCMP-LBM framework and identified viscous fingering as the primary efficiency-limiting factor in heterogeneous pore systemsconsistent with the fingering suppression mechanism demonstrated here as the key advantage of WAG over continuous CO2 flooding. The present study extends that framework to a bimodal, uniformly water-wet conglomerate pore structure under both miscible and immiscible conditions, additionally revealing the Jamin-effect bubble plugging mechanism and the critical role of maintaining pressure above MMP for micropore oil mobilization. Furthermore, Wang et al. recently demonstrated at the pore scale that WAG injection outperforms continuous CO2 flooding in terms of microscopic displacement efficiency in water-wet mediaa conclusion that the present study confirms and substantiates within the Mahu conglomerate context, while additionally quantifying the nonmonotonic cycle-number dependence and the velocity-asymmetric sensitivity that are specific to this highly heterogeneous bimodal pore system.

In summary, recovery factor is primarily controlled by the synergistic action of four factorsinjection mode, injection cycle number, injection velocity, and miscibility degreewhose mechanisms are both mutually independent and coupled. Injection mode is the most fundamental factor, determining the spatial distribution pattern of fluids. WAG achieves displacement efficiency far superior to single-phase injection through its triple pore-scale effects, attaining a maximum recovery of 46.65% in this study. Injection cycle number exhibits a nonmonotonic effect with an optimal value of 4 cycles; field program design should optimize around this range. Injection velocity governs flow regime via the capillary number, with a clear optimal range; Mahu field operations should target moderate Ca (on the order of 10–3). Miscibility degree is decisive for micropore residual oil mobilization; maintaining reservoir pressure above MMP is essential for high-efficiency WAG.

Across all scenarios examined, recovery improvement over conventional waterflooding (∼18%) spans from +22.5 pp (immiscible 4-cycle WAG, 40.53%) to +28.7 pp (optimal 4-cycle miscible WAG, 46.65%), with the exception of the suboptimal low-velocity case (31.85%). This performance envelope confirms that CO2–WAG injection consistently delivers substantial EOR benefits across a wide range of operating conditions in the Mahu conglomerate system, provided that injection velocity is maintained within the moderate capillary number range.

Comprehensive simulation results demonstrate that for the strongly heterogeneous Mahu conglomerate reservoir, the 4-cycle, miscible, moderate-velocity WAG scenario (EOR = 46.65%) achieves the best performance in the parameter space examined, representing an improvement of approximately 28% over the field waterflooding baseline (∼18%) and approximately 10.9% over the post-waterflooding CO2 flooding baseline, confirming significant EOR potential at the pore scale. It should be emphasized that direct translation of these pore-scale findings to field-scale implementation requires further validation through core-flood experiments and reservoir-scale numerical simulations that account for large-scale heterogeneity, geomechanical effects (such as stress-dependent permeability and fracture dynamics under cyclic injection), and wellbore-to-reservoir pressure communication.

Conclusions

Based on multiscale pore structure characterization data of the Mahu Sag reservoir, this study constructed a two-dimensional digitized porous medium model incorporating authentic bimodal heterogeneous pore morphology and a representative uniform wettability calibrated from Mahu reservoir mineral contact angle measurements. A three-component (oil–water–CO2) MCMP-MRT-LBM pore-scale numerical simulation framework was developed and used to systematically simulate different injection modes, cycle numbers, injection velocities, and CO2 miscibility degrees.

The principal conclusions are: based on multiscale pore structure characterization data of the Mahu Sag reservoir, this study constructed a two-dimensional digitized porous medium model incorporating authentic bimodal heterogeneous pore morphology and a representative uniform wettability calibrated from Mahu reservoir mineral contact angle measurements. A three-component (oil–water–CO2) MCMP-MRT-LBM pore-scale numerical simulation framework was developed and used to systematically simulate different injection modes, cycle numbers, injection velocities, and CO2 miscibility degrees. The optimized scenario (4 cycles, miscible, moderate velocity, EOR = 46.65%) improves recovery by approximately 28% over the waterflooding baseline (∼18%) and approximately 10.9% over the continuous CO2 flooding baseline (35.77%), demonstrating that CO2–WAG injection is the most promising EOR strategy for the Mahu Sag. The quantitative pore-scale insights provide mechanistic foundations for optimizing WAG injection programs at the Mahu Sag and offer broad reference value for CO2-EOR in analogous tight conglomerate reservoirs worldwide, subject to further upscaling validation. The present study is based on 2D pore-scale modeling; while this approach enables systematic parametric investigation and has been validated against experimental data, full three-dimensional connectivity effects are not captured and should be addressed in future 3D simulations. At fixed total injection volume, 4-cycle WAG (approximately 0.2 PV per half-cycle) achieves the highest recovery factor (46.65%), surpassing both 2-cycle (44.04%) and 6-cycle (45.49%) scenarios. This nonmonotonic behavior confirms that too few cycles cannot repeatedly exploit the "plug-then-displace" mechanism; too many cycles produce insufficient slug volume to adequately advance the displacement front, causing CO2 to repeatedly trap near the inlet. Field program design should seek the optimal balance between thorough per-slug residual oil mobilization and repeated plugging to suppress gas channeling.

(3)Injection velocity regulates flow regime through the capillary number, with a clear optimal range. WAG displacement efficiency is highest at moderate injection velocity (Ca ≈3 × 10–3); high-velocity injection (Ca ≈4 × 10–3) exacerbates viscous fingering and causes premature macropore breakthrough (final EOR = 41.99%); low-velocity injection (Ca ≈2 × 10–3) triggers extensive CO2 snap-off at pore throats, generating isolated bubbles that block flow paths and reduce injectivity (final EOR = 31.85%). Mahu field operations should target moderate capillary numbers (Ca on the order of 10–3) to balance displacement front stability with micropore sweep efficiency.

(4)Miscibility degree is decisive for micropore residual oil mobilization. The small difference between miscible (46.65%) and near-miscible (45.57%) conditions confirms that once reservoir pressure approaches or exceeds MMP, WAG sweep efficiency dominates recovery. Under immiscible conditions (40.53%), CO2-oil interfacial tension prevents CO2 from entering low-permeability micropores, leaving the majority of micropore residual oil immobilea gap of approximately 6.12% relative to the miscible case. Even so, immiscible WAG outperforms the baseline, confirming that WAG’s mobility-control advantages persist under immiscible conditions.
