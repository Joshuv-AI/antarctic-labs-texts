Prognostic Value of Circulating NETosis Biomarkers in ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction: Citrullinated Histone H3 Outperforms Cell-Free DNA and Nucleosomes
Authors: Wenhao Xin, Hao Wang, Qianqian Feng, Shengfang Wang, Yipin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEven after successful primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI), a substantial proportion of ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) patients develop major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). Neutrophil extracellular trap formation (NETosis) contributes to atherothrombosis and coronary microvascular injury, but the prognostic value of circulating NETosis biomarkers in STEMI has not been systematically compared.MethodsWe studied 420 STEMI patients who survived index hospitalisation after PPCI. Plasma citrullinated histone H3 (H3Cit), cell-free DNA (cfDNA) and nucleosomes were measured at admission. The primary endpoint was MACE; all-cause death was secondary. Incremental prognostic value over a conventional model was assessed by ΔC-index, net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI).ResultsOver a median 545-day follow-up, 81 patients (19.3%) experienced MACE and 36 (8.6%) died. In fully adjusted Cox models, each 100ng/mL increase in H3Cit was independently associated with MACE (HR 1.511, 95% CI 1.155–1.977, P=0.003) and death (HR 1.752, 95% CI 1.203–2.552, P=0.003). Q4 vs Q1–Q3 H3Cit independently predicted MACE (HR 2.139, P=0.002) and death (HR 2.836, P=0.004). cfDNA was associated with death but not MACE after adjustment; nucleosomes lost significance after full adjustment. Adding H3Cit improved discrimination for MACE (ΔC-index 0.042; NRI 0.417; IDI 0.022) and death (ΔC-index 0.055). The prognostic association of H3Cit was directionally consistent across prespecified subgroups.ConclusionAmong three circulating NET biomarkers, H3Cit provides the strongest and most consistent prognostic information for MACE and death after STEMI and offers meaningful incremental value beyond established clinical risk factors, supporting NETosis as a potentially actionable pathway for post-STEMI risk stratification.Keywords: myocardial infarction, NETosis, citrullinated histone H3, prognosis, risk stratification

Introduction

ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) remains one of the leading causes of cardiovascular death and disability, and even contemporary primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI) does not fully eliminate residual risk after discharge.1,2 Post-STEMI patients remain vulnerable to a broad composite of ischaemic and heart-failure events, including re-infarction, stroke, ischaemia-driven revascularisation and hospitalisation for heart failure, collectively referred to as major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE).3,4 Despite guideline-directed antithrombotic and neurohormonal therapies, the residual MACE rate remains clinically meaningful, prompting active interest in biomarkers that can refine risk stratification beyond conventional clinical variables.5–7

Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) are web-like structures composed of decondensed chromatin, histones and granule proteins that are extruded by activated neutrophils in a regulated process collectively termed NETosis.8–10 Beyond their antimicrobial role, NETs have emerged as key drivers of atherosclerosis and arterial thrombosis by amplifying inflammation, activating platelets and providing a prothrombotic scaffold that is readily identified within human coronary thrombi and atherosclerotic lesions.11–15 Circulating markers of NETosis and cell death are independently associated with the severity of coronary atherosclerosis, adverse cardiac events and unfavourable fibrin clot properties,16–18 and NETosis has been implicated in the coronary microvascular consequences of acute myocardial infarction, including infarct size, ST-segment resolution and reperfusion injury.19–23 In parallel, a growing body of evidence links NETosis to the initiation and progression of heart failure, through mechanisms including NET-mediated microvascular thrombosis, impaired mitochondrial homeostasis and pro-inflammatory macrophage polarisation.24–28 Because the MACE composite after STEMI encompasses both ischaemic and heart-failure events, biomarkers reflecting NETosis are mechanistically positioned to capture residual risk from multiple pathways, and have previously been shown to predict adverse cardiovascular outcomes in atrial fibrillation.29,30

Different NETosis biomarkers differ in how specifically they reflect the extent of NETosis. Citrullinated histone H3 (H3Cit) is generated when peptidylarginine deiminase 4 citrullinates histone H3 during chromatin decondensation, a step central to NET formation, and is therefore regarded as the most NET-specific circulating marker; by contrast, cell-free DNA (cfDNA) and nucleosomes are also released during apoptosis, necrosis and general cellular turnover and are thus less specific surrogates of NETosis.31 Circulating NET markers have been examined after STEMI before, but the results have been inconsistent: some cohorts report that higher H3Cit–DNA identifies patients at increased risk of adverse events,32 whereas others find no independent prognostic value.33 Moreover, H3Cit, cfDNA and nucleosomes have rarely been measured together in the same STEMI cohort, so it remains unclear which of them, if any, best captures residual risk. Because these markers reflect different aspects of NET biology and may carry different prognostic weight, a direct, head-to-head comparison within a single cohort is clinically important to identify the most informative marker for post-STEMI risk stratification and to establish whether NETosis represents an actionable prognostic pathway.

Against this background, the present study aimed to evaluate three circulating biomarkers of NETosis — citrullinated histone H3 (H3Cit), cell-free DNA (cfDNA) and nucleosomes — measured at admission for their ability to predict MACE and all-cause death in a contemporary cohort of STEMI patients treated with PPCI, and to determine whether these biomarkers provide incremental prognostic information beyond conventional clinical risk factors.

Methods

Study Population and Blood Sampling

The study cohort and sample handling have been described in detail previously.34 In brief, 423 adult patients with STEMI who were diagnosed according to the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction and underwent PPCI were enrolled from two participating centres, Central China Fuwai Hospital and The Seventh People’s Hospital of Zhengzhou, between August 2023 and February 2025. Exclusion criteria included symptom onset-to-PCI time >12 hours, severe hepatic or renal dysfunction, active infection or recent major trauma/surgery, autoimmune disease and malignancy. Arterial blood was obtained from the arterial access site before the interventional procedure and processed into plasma that was stored at −80 °C until analysis. The present analysis was performed in the subset of patients from the original cohort who survived the index hospitalisation, after excluding three in-hospital deaths, yielding 420 patients for the present prognostic analysis.34 All participants provided written informed consent, and the protocol was approved by the ethics committees of the participating centres and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Outcomes and Definitions

The primary endpoint was MACE, defined as a composite of all-cause death, recurrent myocardial infarction, stroke, hospitalisation for heart failure and ischaemia-driven revascularisation. All-cause death was analysed separately as a secondary endpoint. Detailed component definitions are provided in the Table S1.34 All clinical endpoints were independently adjudicated by an endpoint committee of two cardiologists who reviewed the source medical records, readmission records and follow-up data and who were blinded to the circulating NETosis biomarker (H3Cit, cfDNA and nucleosome) results, with any disagreement resolved by consensus.

Measurement of NETosis Biomarkers

Plasma H3Cit, cfDNA and nucleosome levels were quantified using previously published assays.35–37 H3Cit was measured by an in-house enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in which anti-histone capture antibody from the Cell Death Detection ELISA kit (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) was coated on 96-well plates, incubated overnight at 4 °C, and developed sequentially with an anti-H3Cit detection antibody (ab5103, Abcam) and a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody; samples were developed with 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine and read at 450 nm, with results expressed in ng/mL. Samples with an absorbance below the buffer blank were assigned an H3Cit value of 0 ng/mL.35 cfDNA in plasma was quantified using the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and expressed in ng/mL.35 Nucleosomes were quantified using the Cell Death Detection ELISA PLUS kit (Roche Diagnostics) and normalised to a reference pool of plasma from five young male healthy controls to produce a multiple-of-the-median (MoM) value.35 All measurements were performed in duplicate.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous data were summarised as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables and as the median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed variables. The Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed data, while the Mann–Whitney U-test was employed for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %) and were compared using Pearson’s χ2-test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Correlations among H3Cit, cfDNA, nucleosomes and the absolute neutrophil count were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient.

For each NETosis biomarker, survival curves stratified by the upper quartile (Q4 vs Q1–Q3) were plotted using the Kaplan–Meier method, with comparisons between groups performed by the Log rank test. This empirical dichotomy at the 75th percentile follows the analytical convention established in prior cohort studies of circulating NET biomarkers.35,36 Univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were conducted to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each biomarker, entered either as a continuous variable or as the dichotomised Q4 variable. When entered as a continuous variable, H3Cit and cfDNA were modelled per 100 ng/mL increase, whereas nucleosomes were modelled per 1-MoM increase, in keeping with prior work that used the same scaling.35,36 In multivariable Cox regression analysis, Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 further adjusted for hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, natural log-transformed NT-proBNP (ln-NT-proBNP) and no-reflow.

To quantify the incremental predictive value of each NETosis biomarker, we constructed a conventional Cox model including age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, ln-NT-proBNP and no-reflow (baseline model), and separately built a new model by adding each biomarker as a continuous variable. The predictive accuracy and discriminative power of the prognostic models were evaluated using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index). To quantify the incremental predictive value of adding each biomarker to the baseline model, we calculated the change in C-index (ΔC-index) and the continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI). The ΔC-index was calculated using the bootstrap method with 2000 replicates. The NRI and IDI were computed using the R package survIDINRI (v1.1.2).

Subgroup analyses were performed for both death and MACE endpoints using H3Cit per 100 ng/mL across prespecified clinical strata (sex, age [<60 vs ≥60 years], diabetes, hypertension, smoking, dyslipidaemia and no-reflow), with interactions tested on the multiplicative scale.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The design, conduct and reporting of this study followed the RECORD (REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data) guidelines.

Results

Outcomes and Baseline Differences Between MACE and Non-MACE Patients

Over a median follow-up of 545 days (IQR 365–730), 81 patients experienced MACE, comprising 36 deaths, 16 recurrent myocardial infarctions, 5 strokes, 26 hospitalisations for heart failure and 19 ischaemia-driven revascularisations. Baseline demographic, clinical, laboratory and procedural characteristics of the overall cohort and stratified by MACE status are summarised in Table 1. Patients who developed MACE were older (61.5 ± 10.0 vs 58.4 ± 11.5 years, P=0.027), more frequently had hypertension (56.8% vs 42.8%, P=0.031) and had higher admission NT-proBNP concentrations (median 487 vs 250 pg/mL, P=0.015) than patients without MACE, while diabetes, smoking, dyslipidaemia, cholesterol fractions, haematological indices and renal function did not differ significantly between groups. Procedural characteristics, including culprit vessel distribution, use of thrombectomy and post-dilation, were broadly similar between MACE and non-MACE patients, although a non-significant trend towards a higher prevalence of no-reflow was observed in MACE patients (27.2% vs 17.1%, P=0.056). All three NETosis biomarkers were significantly higher in patients who developed MACE: H3Cit 48.0 (23.1–81.2) vs 21.7 (2.1–44.3) ng/mL (P<0.001); cfDNA 361.4 (320.9–431.6) vs 341.2 (290.4–412.4) ng/mL (P=0.034); and nucleosomes 1.42 (0.73–2.88) vs 1.18 (0.59–2.29) MoM (P=0.030).

Kaplan–Meier Survival Analyses for MACE and All-Cause Death

Kaplan–Meier curves stratified by Q4 versus Q1–Q3 of each NET biomarker are shown in Figure 3. For MACE, the Kaplan–Meier-estimated cumulative event rate at 730 days was 35.6% in the H3Cit Q4 subgroup versus 16.3% in Q1–Q3 (log-rank P<0.001). Smaller and statistically non-significant separations were observed for cfDNA (23.1% vs 20.3%; log-rank P=0.219) and for nucleosomes (28.0% vs 18.8%; log-rank P=0.078). For all-cause death at 730 days, the Q4 versus Q1–Q3 Kaplan–Meier event rates were 18.2% vs 7.2% for H3Cit (log-rank P<0.001), 12.4% vs 9.1% for cfDNA (log-rank P=0.084) and 16.2% vs 7.9% for nucleosomes (log-rank P=0.034). Across both endpoints, H3Cit demonstrated the most pronounced and statistically significant separation between the Q4 and Q1–Q3 groups, whereas differences for cfDNA and nucleosomes were more modest and variable.

NETosis Biomarkers as Predictors of MACE and Death in Cox Models

Cox regression results for each biomarker modelled as a continuous variable are presented in Table 2. H3Cit per 100 ng/mL increase was consistently associated with MACE in the univariate model (HR 1.732, 95% CI 1.356–2.211, P<0.001), in Model 1 adjusted for age and sex (HR 1.710, 95% CI 1.334–2.191, P<0.001) and in Model 2 adjusted additionally for hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, ln-NT-proBNP and no-reflow (HR 1.511, 95% CI 1.155–1.977, P=0.003). The same pattern was observed for all-cause death, with a Model 2 HR of 1.752 (95% CI 1.203–2.552, P=0.003) per 100 ng/mL increase in H3Cit. cfDNA per 100 ng/mL increase was associated with MACE in univariate and age/sex-adjusted models (Model 1 HR 1.322, 95% CI 1.053–1.660, P=0.016) but fell to borderline significance in Model 2 (HR 1.258, 95% CI 0.997–1.588, P=0.053); the association between cfDNA and death remained significant in Model 2 (HR 1.435, 95% CI 1.032–1.997, P=0.032). Nucleosomes per 1-MoM increase were modestly associated with MACE (univariate HR 1.071, P=0.032) and death (univariate HR 1.096, P=0.022) but did not retain statistical significance after full adjustment.

Incremental Prognostic Value Over a Conventional Risk Model

Table 4 presents the incremental prognostic value of each biomarker. The conventional Cox model comprising age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, ln-NT-proBNP and no-reflow achieved a C-index of 0.656 (95% CI 0.602–0.709) for MACE and 0.650 (95% CI 0.552–0.748) for death. Adding H3Cit yielded the largest gain in discrimination: for MACE, the C-index increased to 0.697 (95% CI 0.646–0.749), with a ΔC-index of 0.042 (95% CI 0.009–0.074, P=0.012), NRI of 0.417 (95% CI 0.069–0.648, P=0.015) and IDI of 0.022 (95% CI 0.001–0.065, P=0.010); for death, the C-index increased to 0.705 (95% CI 0.617–0.793), with ΔC-index 0.055 (95% CI 0.005–0.104, P=0.029) and NRI 0.531 (95% CI 0.005–0.858, P=0.045). Adding cfDNA produced only modest improvements, with a significant ΔC-index for death (0.041, P=0.033) but not for MACE (0.014, P=0.236); addition of nucleosomes did not produce statistically significant improvements in the C-index for either endpoint, with only borderline IDI gains for death (IDI 0.012, P=0.050). Overall, H3Cit provided the most consistent and clinically relevant incremental prognostic information over the conventional model for both MACE and death.

Association of H3Cit with Individual Components of MACE

To explore which components of the MACE composite contributed to the prognostic value of H3Cit, we repeated the Cox analyses for H3Cit (per 100 ng/mL and as Q4 versus Q1–Q3) against the individual components (Table S2, Table S3 and Figure S1); because stroke was infrequent (n=5), recurrent myocardial infarction and stroke were analysed as a combined endpoint. Higher H3Cit was most strongly and robustly associated with ischaemia-driven revascularisation (per 100 ng/mL: unadjusted HR 2.250, 95% CI 1.487–3.406, P<0.001; fully adjusted HR 2.047, 95% CI 1.306–3.207, P=0.002). H3Cit also showed a association with hospitalisation for heart failure in unadjusted analysis (Q4 vs Q1–Q3 HR 2.449, 95% CI 1.124–5.336, P=0.024) that was attenuated after full adjustment, whereas its association with the combined recurrent myocardial infarction/stroke endpoint was weaker and did not persist after adjustment.

Subgroup Analyses for H3Cit

Because H3Cit showed the largest incremental prognostic value, we performed subgroup analyses using H3Cit modelled as a continuous variable (per 100 ng/mL increase) for both MACE and all-cause death, with adjustment for the remaining Model 2 covariates in each stratum (Figure 4). The point estimate of the hazard ratio was >1 across all examined subgroups, including men and women, patients aged <60 and ≥60 years, patients with and without diabetes, hypertension, smoking or dyslipidaemia, and patients with or without no-reflow. Each subgroup-specific P-value and test for interaction are shown in Figure 4. Tests for interaction were non-significant for all subgroups except sex for the MACE endpoint (P for interaction=0.014), where the prognostic association of H3Cit with MACE was more pronounced in women than in men. Discussion

In this cohort of 420 STEMI patients treated with PPCI, we evaluated three circulating NETosis biomarkers — H3Cit, cfDNA and nucleosomes — for their ability to predict MACE and all-cause death. All three markers were higher in patients who developed MACE during follow-up, and each showed prognostic significance in univariate analyses. After comprehensive adjustment for age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, ln-NT-proBNP and no-reflow, however, only H3Cit remained independently associated with both MACE and death, whether modelled continuously or dichotomised at the 75th percentile. cfDNA retained an independent association with all-cause death but not with MACE, and nucleosomes were no longer statistically significant after full adjustment. Importantly, adding H3Cit to a conventional Cox model yielded meaningful gains in discrimination and reclassification for both MACE and death, whereas cfDNA and nucleosomes added only modest information.

From a clinical-practice and risk-prediction perspective, these findings support the incorporation of NETosis biomarkers, and in particular H3Cit, into risk-stratification strategies after STEMI. Current prognostic models after STEMI rely heavily on age, sex, traditional cardiovascular risk factors, natriuretic peptides and procedural variables such as no-reflow, yet residual MACE risk remains clinically meaningful.5–7 Recent work has shown that inflammatory and pyroptosis-related biomarkers such as serum IL-1β and GSDMD can independently predict no-reflow and MACE in STEMI patients treated with PPCI and add incremental value above conventional models.34,38 Our results are concordant with this literature; whereas IL-1β and GSDMD reflect the pyroptotic limb of innate immunity, H3Cit and cfDNA reflect NETosis.19 The magnitude of the incremental improvement associated with H3Cit (ΔC-index 0.042 for MACE, 0.055 for death, with significant NRI and IDI) is comparable to that reported for other novel biomarkers evaluated in STEMI and acute coronary syndrome cohorts.29,39 Because H3Cit can be quantified in a single plasma sample obtained at admission, it is operationally compatible with current STEMI workflows and could plausibly inform intensity of secondary prevention, surveillance for heart-failure hospitalisation and targeted adjunctive anti-inflammatory strategies.21

From a mechanistic standpoint, our findings are biologically plausible and consistent with a growing body of basic and translational literature on NETosis in cardiovascular disease. NETs have been shown to form within human coronary thrombi and atherosclerotic plaques, promote platelet activation and fibrin deposition, and are independently associated with coronary atherosclerosis severity and adverse cardiovascular events.11,12,14–18 In acute STEMI specifically, the coronary NET burden is a determinant of ST-segment resolution and infarct size,39 and several recent mechanistic studies have shown that endogenous triggers such as S100A12 and pathways associated with ALDH2 deficiency drive NETosis, aggravate myocardial reperfusion injury and adverse left ventricular remodelling, and correlate with clinical outcomes after PPCI.22,23 Because the MACE composite after STEMI also incorporates heart-failure hospitalisation, it is relevant that NETosis has been mechanistically linked to heart failure pathogenesis through a von Willebrand factor–SLC44A2–NET axis, KLF2-regulated neutrophil immunothrombotic dysregulation and EDIL3-dependent NET-primed macrophage polarisation.24–28 Against this mechanistic background, the stronger prognostic performance of H3Cit compared with cfDNA and nucleosomes in our cohort is biologically consistent with prior observations that H3Cit is more specific for NET formation than cfDNA or nucleosomes, whose release can also reflect apoptotic or necrotic cell death and generic cellular turnover.35,36 Taken together, our data support the view that citrullinated histone H3 integrates multiple pathophysiological contributions of NETosis — atherothrombosis, microvascular injury and post-infarction remodelling — into a single prognostically informative circulating signal.

Our results should be interpreted in the context of previous STEMI cohorts that measured circulating citrullinated histone H3, whose findings have been inconsistent. Benkhoff et al reported that H3Cit–DNA complexes measured on day 1 after STEMI (137.4 ± 100.0 µg/L) identified patients at higher risk of major adverse cardiac events (highest versus lower tertiles, HR 2.57),32 whereas Blasco et al found that admission H3Cit–DNA (median 179.30 ng/mL) did not predict death or MACE, even though classical inflammatory markers such as CRP and IL-6 did.33 The absolute H3Cit concentrations in our cohort (median 24.6 ng/mL) were substantially lower than in these reports, which is most plausibly explained by fundamental differences in what was measured and how. First, the target analyte was not the same. Both previous studies quantified H3Cit–DNA complexes using assays anchored on the DNA–histone complex; Benkhoff et al used a sandwich ELISA that captured with an antibody against citrullinated histone H3 and detected the bound DNA, calibrated against recombinant citrullinated mononucleosomes, so that only citrullinated H3 still complexed with nucleosomal DNA was quantified, and Blasco et al likewise measured H3Cit–DNA complexes. Our in-house ELISA instead captured total histones and detected the citrullinated H3 epitope directly, thereby quantifying histone-bound citrullinated H3 irrespective of its association with DNA. Because a DNA-containing nucleosomal complex and a protein epitope are chemically distinct targets measured against different calibrators, the resulting concentrations are not numerically interchangeable and would not be expected to coincide. Second, there is at present no international reference standard or common calibrator for circulating H3Cit, so absolute values are strongly assay-dependent and vary with the capture and detection antibodies, the calibration material (for example, in-vitro enzymatically citrullinated histones versus semi-synthetic or recombinant nucleosomes), the assay format and the reagent lot;31,40 concentrations obtained with different assays, antibodies and reagent batches therefore cannot be compared directly across studies. Taken together, the substantially lower median H3Cit in our cohort most plausibly reflects these differences in the analyte measured and in assay calibration rather than a genuinely lower biological NET burden.

Our findings also warrant consideration in relation to C-reactive protein (CRP), a well-established inflammatory marker after STEMI. In our cohort, conventional (non–high-sensitivity) CRP measured at admission did not differ significantly between patients who did and did not develop MACE (7.25 [2.88–11.25] versus 6.19 [2.15–10.51] mg/L, P=0.296; Table 1). This apparent divergence from reports that CRP predicts outcome after STEMI41 is most plausibly explained by assay type and sampling time: CRP rises comparatively slowly and peaks two to three days after infarction, so a single conventional-CRP value obtained in the hyper-acute phase before primary PCI may largely reflects pre-infarct baseline inflammation and may discriminates less well than high-sensitivity or peak CRP. Consistent with this, in a head-to-head STEMI study the NET marker H3Cit–DNA was not prognostic whereas CRP and IL-6 were,33 which contrasts with our observation that admission H3Cit outperformed admission conventional CRP. Together these observations suggest that H3Cit and CRP capture different and potentially complementary facets of the post-infarction inflammatory response–NETosis-driven immunothrombosis versus the systemic acute-phase reaction–and that the most informative marker may depend on the assay used and the timing of sampling. Because peak and high-sensitivity CRP were not available in our cohort, we could not perform a formal head-to-head comparison, which we acknowledge as a limitation.

This study has several limitations. First, it is an observational cohort analysis from two centres, and despite adjustment for key clinical covariates the possibility of residual confounding cannot be excluded; replication in larger, geographically diverse cohorts is required before H3Cit can be adopted into routine practice. Second, the NETosis biomarkers were measured only once, at admission, and we cannot address how dynamic changes during hospitalisation or convalescence relate to MACE. Third, direct quantification of NETs in plasma is not standardised and we therefore relied on validated ELISA-based surrogate biomarkers, which reflect but do not unequivocally prove NET formation; in particular, cfDNA and nucleosomes are influenced by non-NET cell death, which may contribute to their weaker and less specific prognostic performance relative to H3Cit. Fourth, the number of events, particularly all-cause deaths (n=36), is relatively limited, and the study findings will need to be confirmed in larger cohorts. Fifth, we did not collect imaging-based measures of microvascular injury, infarct size or left ventricular remodelling, which limits our ability to attribute the prognostic signal of H3Cit to specific intermediate phenotypes. Sixth, because our cohort consisted of STEMI patients treated with contemporary PPCI and guideline-directed medical therapy, our findings cannot be extrapolated to non-revascularised or more complex acute coronary syndrome populations without further study. Seventh, we were unable to include peak cardiac troponin or peak/high-sensitivity CRP among the study variables. The absence of these infarct-size and inflammatory measures limited our ability to benchmark H3Cit against them, and future studies with protocolised serial sampling are needed.

Conclusion

In STEMI patients treated with PPCI, circulating H3Cit measured at admission was independently associated with both MACE and all-cause death during follow-up and provided meaningful incremental prognostic information beyond a conventional risk model, whereas cfDNA and nucleosomes added only modest information. Among three NETosis biomarkers, H3Cit emerges as the most consistent and clinically useful signal, supporting its potential role in post-STEMI prognostic risk stratification.
