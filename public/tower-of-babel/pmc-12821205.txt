The multipathogen profiles and co-infection characteristics obtained from comprehensive surveillance of patients with acute respiratory infections in the post-COVID-19 era in Shenzhen, China
Authors: Dandan Niu, Qiuying Lv, Yuan Bai, Zhen Zhang, Renli Zhang, Yanxiao Gao, Zhongyao Xu, Honglin Wang, Xiaomin Zhang, Feng Sha, Yingluan Zhang, Tong Li, Tengyingzi Liu, Dan Wang, Xiaolu Shi, Yongchao Guo, Jinling Tang, Tiejian Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe long-term impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the distribution pattern of respiratory pathogens in China was unknown. Our study aimed to determine the multipathogen profiles and co-infection characteristics among patients with acute respiratory infections (ARIs) in the post-COVID-19 era in Shenzhen, China.MethodsThis multi-center cross-sectional study was conducted among patients with ARIs from five sentinel hospitals from November 1, 2022 to June 30, 2023 in Shenzhen, China. The collected respiratory samples were subjected to targeted next-generation sequencing for high-throughput screening of 108 respiratory pathogens. Multi-stage logistic regression models were built to infer interactions between pathogens at the individual level.ResultsThe median [IQR] age of 1890 patients included was 31.8 [23.7, 45.2] years. The proportions of positivity on any pathogen and co-infection were 84.0% (1587/1890) and 41.4% (782/1890). Children < 5 years and school-age children have the highest viral (78.0%, 99/127) and bacterial positive proportion (65.5%, 114/127). Influenza virus (IFV), SARS-CoV-2, and human rhinovirus (HRV) were the three leading viral pathogens, and Haemophilus influenzae (H. influenzae), Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), and Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumoniae) were the three leading bacterial pathogens. The three leading co-infection pathogens were S. pneumoniae combined with H. influenzae (4.6%, 86/1890), IFV with S. aureus (4.5%, 85/1890) and with H. influenzae (3.3%, 62/1890). The proportions of positivity on IFV, enterovirus, SARS-CoV-2, H. influenzae, S. aureus, and S. pneumoniae in outpatients were higher than those in inpatients, while the proportions of positivity on human parainfluenza virus (HPIV), human metapneumovirus (HMPV), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, and Legionella pneumophila in inpatients were higher than those in outpatients. RSV, IFV, HPIV, human adenovirus, and HRV were the top five viral pathogens among hospitalized children before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic in Shenzhen. Virus‒virus interactions, such as IFV combined with SARS-CoV-2 (OR = 20.3 [95% confidence interval (CI): 7.2–57.0]) and with HRV (OR = 7.2 [95% CI: 3.5–15.0]) exhibited competitive effects. Bacteria-bacteria interactions exhibited synergistic effects such as Moraxella catarrhalis combined with S. pneumoniae (OR = 0.3 [95% CI: 0.2–0.5]).ConclusionsThere were differences in the pathogen profiles among patients with different ages, pneumonia groups, and case types. Virus‒virus interactions presented competitive effects, while bacterium‒bacterium interactions exhibited synergistic effects.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12350-y.

Background

Acute respiratory tract infections (ARIs), a worldwide public health issue with high morbidity and mortality, rank fourth among global causes of mortality and second among low-income countries according to the World Health Organization. Lower respiratory tract infections including pneumonia caused nearly 3 million deaths and remained the deadliest infectious disease in 2019 worldwide [1, 2], and have become the main cause of hospitalization and deaths in children, especially in low- and middle-income countries with relatively scarce medical resources [3]. In recent decades, several pathogens associated with ARIs have emerged such as SARS-CoV-2 worldwide, leading to infectious disease outbreaks and epidemics [4]. ARIs posed great challenges and threats to human health, and long-term continuous surveillance was essential for the prevention and control of respiratory infectious diseases [5].

Due to non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) during the COVID-19 pandemic, the distribution of respiratory pathogens in some regions showed different epidemiological characteristics, such as Italy and Egypt [6]. The prevalence of several respiratory pathogens has significantly decreased during the short period soon after the COVID-19 pandemic in China. The detection rate of human adenovirus (HAdV) decreased from 8.9% in 2019 to 2.1% in 2020 among hospitalized children with ARIs in Shenzhen [7]. However, the long-term impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the distribution pattern of respiratory pathogens in China was unknown. Furthermore, the national influenza monitoring network only covered limited ARI pathogens in China [8, 9], and more comprehensive surveillance was needed in the future [10]. In 2022, Shenzhen took the initiative in carrying out comprehensive surveillance pilots nationwide after the COVID-19 pandemic, and aimed to determine the multipathogen profiles and co-infection characteristics among patients with ARIs in the post-COVID-19 era in Shenzhen, China.

Methods

Patient recruitment and sample collection

A cross-sectional study utilizing active sentinel surveillance was conducted among patients with ARIs at fever clinics and inpatient departments across five hospitals in four districts of Shenzhen (Bao’an, Nanshan, Longhua, and Longgang) between November 1, 2022 and June 30, 2023. The five sentinel hospitals in this study were randomly selected from 68 secondary and tertiary healthcare institutions in Shenzhen, with consideration given to covering different districts to enhance the representativeness of the surveillance sites. Outpatients and inpatients were included when they were preliminarily diagnosed with ARIs and/or pneumonia from the respiratory department, emergency department, fever clinic, and intensive care unit in hospitals without age restriction. The definitions of patients with ARIs and pneumonia were according to the 2016 clinical practice guidelines by the Chinese Thoracic Society [11]. Patients who were diagnosed with non-infectious respiratory diseases such as asthma and tumors were excluded.

Respiratory samples including nasopharyngeal swabs, sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, or pleural effusion were collected before treatment from patients within 72 h of presentation [12]. Patients with upper respiratory tract infection were given priority to collect nasopharyngeal swabs, and patients with lower respiratory tract infection including pneumonia were given priority to collect bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. At least five samples were collected per week per hospital and at least one sample on Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday to ensure distribution balance and representativeness of samples, and if the sample size was small, it will be taken as full as possible. Meanwhile, clinical information of patients was collected according to the questionnaire detailed in Table S1, which comprised five parts: patients information, clinic departments, clinical symptoms, sample types, informed consent.

Laboratory testing

The collected samples from the recruited patients were subjected to targeted next-generation sequencing (tNGS) for high-throughput screening of 108 respiratory pathogens (59 bacteria, 39 viruses, and 10 fungi, detailed in Table S2). The tNGS workflow for high-throughput pathogen screening involved three general steps as illustrated in Fig. S1, and the detailed Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) and bioinformatics analysis pipeline were provided as Supplementary Material 2. Laboratory testing was carried out in accordance with the Regulations on Biological Safety Management and the SOP of National Pathogenic Microbial Laboratories.

Statistical analysis

The questionnaire data were entered by two people into a standardized database using Epidata 4.6 and Microsoft Excel 2010, and missing data were supplemented by contacting the related doctors. Descriptive analysis was conducted using frequency and percentage for qualitative variables, and mean ± standard deviation (normal distribution), or median and interquartile range (skewed distribution) were used for quantitative variables. We classified age variable into four groups for analysis: children (< 5 years old), school-age children (5–17 years old), adults (18–59 years old), and the elderly (≥ 60 years old). The proportions of positivity on pathogens among different groups were compared through Pearson χ2 test and bonferroni method. Join-point regression analysis (JPR) was used to determine trends in the proportions of positivity on different pathogens with age [13].

To infer virus-virus, bacteria-bacteria and virus-bacteria interactions at the level of individuals, we applied multi-stage logistic regressions model to avoid selection bias and obtain robust conclusions [9]. For the first stage, we conducted logistic regressions analysis with the detection of any pathogen as explanatory variable. For the detection of each study pathogen (‘Y’) as response variable, the detection of another ‘non-Y’ pathogen was used as explanatory variable ‘X’ in turn. The age (qualitative variable), sex, illness severity (with or without pneumonia), and disease seasonality (month of the year, qualitative variable) were used as covariates for model adjustment. By this way, all the 190 pairwise interactions of key 20 pathogens (10 viruses and 10 bacteria) between each other were revealed as interactions without adjusting for multi-pathogens. For the second stage, for the detection of each study pathogen (‘Y’), the detection of all the remaining ‘non-Y’ pathogens was used as explanatory variable ‘X’, and the regressions were performed with every pathogen regarded as independent variables in turn, as these data did not allow for inferences of the direction of interactions. The age, sex, illness severity and disease seasonality were also adjusted. The logistic regression results were used without multiple comparison correction for p values. When the logistic regression results between ‘X’ and ‘Y’ were biphasic and statistically significant, it was considered that there was an interaction between ‘X’ and ‘Y’. For the third stage, the significant results from two step logistic regressions were aligned and those consistent were considered as the final robust results. Odds ratios (ORs) were used to determine the direction of interaction between pathogens. If OR value was greater than 1, it was considered as a negative competitive effect, instead it was positive synergistic effect. Statistical analysis was implemented in MATLAB R2023b. A two-sided p value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Ethics statement

This surveillance project for obtaining consent was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Center for Disease Control and Prevention (QS2023040013). Informed consent was obtained from all recruited patients for sample and demographic data collection. For patients younger than 18 years of age, informed consent was obtained through their guardians.

Results

Participants

A total of 2252 patients with ARIs were recruited from five Shenzhen sentinel hospitals, of which 362 patients were excluded due to late/repeated sampling or insufficient information, and 1890 patients were ultimately enrolled (Fig. 1). The median [IQR] age was 31.8 [23.7–45.2] years, and 53.2% (1006/1890) were male. About 28.5% (539/1890) were hospitalized patients, and 21.6% (409/1890) were diagnosed with pneumonia.

The proportions of positivity on pathogens

The proportion of positivity on any viral pathogen among patients with ARIs was 66.5% (1256/1890), varying among different age groups (χ2 = 13.123, p = 0.004). Pairwise comparisons showed that the proportion of positivity on any virus in children under five (78.0%, 99/127) was higher than that in adults (64.3%, 864/1344). The proportion of positivity on any virus in outpatients was higher than that in inpatients (70.1%, 947/1351;57.3%, 309/539; p < 0.001) (Table 1).

The proportion of positivity on any bacterial pathogen among patients with ARIs was 48.4% (915/1890), differing significantly among the different age groups (χ2 = 41.503, p < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons showed that the proportions of positivity on any bacteria in children under five (59.8%, 76/174), school-age children (65.5%, 114/127), and the elderly (54.7%, 134/245) were higher than that in adults (44.0%, 591/1344) (Table 1).

The proportion of positivity on any viral or bacterial pathogen among patients with ARIs was 84.0% (1587/1890), and there was a significant difference among the different age groups (χ2 = 13.911, p = 0.003). Pairwise comparisons showed that the proportion of positivity on any pathogen in children under five (92.1%, 117/127) was higher than that in adults (82.4%, 1107/1344). The proportion of positivity on any pathogen in outpatients (86.3%, 1166/1351) was higher than that in inpatients (78.1%, 421/539) (p = 0.001) (Table 1).

The proportion of positivity on two or more respiratory pathogens in patients was 49.3% (782/1587), and there was a statistically significant difference among the different age groups (χ2 = 51.969, p < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons showed that the proportions of positivity on two or more pathogens in children under five (55.9%, 71/117, χ2 = 12.830, p < 0.001), school-age children (65.6%, 103/157, χ2 = 27.380, p < 0.001), and the elderly (62.1%, 128/206, χ2 = 24.625, p < 0.001) were higher than that in adults (43.4%, 480/1107). The proportions of positivity on two, three-four, and five or more pathogens in patients were 27.5% (437/1587), 18.7% (296/1587), and 3.1% (49/1587), respectively. There were statistical differences in the distribution of the number of pathogens in co-infections among different age groups (χ2 = 111.547, p < 0.001). The proportions of positivity on one, two, three-four, and five or more pathogens in the elderly were 66.7% (78/206), 40.2% (47/206), 50.4% (59/206), and 18.8% (22/206), respectively (Table S3, Fig. 2).

Pathogen profiles

The proportions of positivity on the 108 respiratory pathogens among patients with ARIs were shown in Table S2, among which 58 pathogens were detected, including 29 bacteria and 29 viruses. From the perspective of pathogen attribute, the proportion of positivity on Class B legally reported infectious disease pathogens was 17.2% (325/1890), including four pathogens SARS-CoV-2, measles virus, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Bordetella pertussis with the proportions of positivity on 16.1% (305/1890), 0.1% (1/1890), 0.8% (15/1890), and 0.2% (4/1890), respectively. There were also several types of pathogens related to Class C legally reported infectious diseases, including influenza virus (IFV), enterovirus (EV), and Escherichia coli with the proportions of positivity on 27.5% (519/1890), 0.9% (17/1890), and 0.4% (8/1890), respectively. In addition, one recurrent pathogen for Shenzhen (Chlamydia psittaci) was detected with the proportion of positivity of 0.2% (4/1890). Four common opportunistic pathogenicity pathogens were also detected, including Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) (342/1890, 18.1%), human herpes simplex virus (HSV) (61/1890, 3.2%), cytomegalovirus (CMV) (29/1890, 1.5%), and human herpes virus type 6 (HHV-6) (18/1890, 1.0%).

The pathogen profiles for 20 key respiratory pathogens revealed that IFV, SARS-CoV-2, and human rhinovirus (HRV) were the three viruses with the highest proportions of positivity on 27.5%, 16.1%, and 5.4%, respectively. In children under five, the top four viruses were IFV (23.6%), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) (15.0%), HRV, and human parainfluenza virus (HPIV) (10.2%), and school-age children and adults shared the top three viruses (IFV, SARS-CoV-2, and HRV), and SARS-CoV-2 (22.9%), IFV (9.0%), and HPIV (2.9%) was the highest pathogens in the elderly (Fig. 3, Table S4).

IFV, SARS-CoV-2, and HRV were the top three viruses with the highest proportions of positivity in patients with and without pneumonia (Fig. 4, Table S4). For children under five with pneumonia, the two most common viruses were RSV (26.7%) and HPIV (13.3%), and their proportion of positivity on IFV (6.7%) was much lower than that in non-pneumonia patients (28.9%) (p = 0.012). Among school-age children with pneumonia, the proportion of positivity on SARS-CoV-2 ranked first (28.6%) surpassing that of IFV (14.3%), and the proportion of positivity on IFV in non-pneumonia patients (35.9%) was higher than that in pneumonia patients (14.3%) (p = 0.048). The two most common pathogens in adults and the elderly patients with pneumonia and non-pneumonia were IFV and SARS-CoV-2. (Table S5).

The top three bacteria (including bacteria and fungus) with high proportions of positivity were Haemophilus influenzae (H. influenzae), Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), and Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumoniae), with the proportions of positivity on 16.6%, 14.2%, and 13.0%, respectively. The three most common bacteria detected in children under five were S. pneumoniae (29.9%), H. influenzae (26.0%), and Moraxella catarrhalis (M. catarrhalis) (20.5%). In school-age children and adults, the top three bacteria were H. influenzae (35.6%), S. aureus (24.7%), and S. pneumoniae (23.0%), and the ranking order was different in the elderly with S. pneumoniae (12.7%), H. influenzae (12.2%), S. aureus (11.0%), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) (11.0%). The proportions of positivity on P. aeruginosa (11.0% vs. 2.4%, p = 0.048), Acinetobacter baumannii (A. baumannii) (6.1% vs. 0.0%, p < 0.001), and Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) (2.4% vs. 0.8%, p = 0.016) in the elderly were higher than those in children under five (Fig. 3, Table S4).

The top three bacteria were H. influenzae, S. aureus, and S. pneumoniae among patients with and without pneumonia (Fig. 4, Table S4). H. influenzae (26.7% vs. 25.8%) and S. pneumoniae (26.7% vs. 30.9%) were the top two bacteria detected in children under five with and without pneumonia. The top three bacteria detected in school-age children with pneumonia were H. influenzae (23.8%), S. pneumoniae (14.3%), and Mycoplasma pneumoniae (M. pneumoniae) (14.3%), respectively, which changed to H. influenzae (37.3%), S. aureus (26.8%), and S. pneumoniae (24.2%) in school-age children without pneumonia. The proportion of positivity on M. pneumoniae in school-age children with pneumonia (14.3%) was significantly greater than that in patients without pneumonia (0.7%) (p = 0.003). The top three bacteria detected in adults with and without pneumonia were consistent but different in ranking. The top three bacteria detected in adults with pneumonia were S. aureus (10.2%), H. influenzae (9.8%), and S. pneumoniae (8.6%), which changed to H. influenzae (15.0%), S. aureus (13.6%), and S. pneumoniae (10.5%) in adults without pneumonia,. The proportion of positivity on P. aeruginosa (6.5%) was greater in adults with pneumonia than that in adults without pneumonia (1.5%, p < 0.001). The top three bacteria in the elderly with pneumonia were S. aureus (12.4%), H. influenzae (9.7%) and P. aeruginosa (9.7%), which changed to S. pneumoniae (15.9%), H. influenzae (14.4%) and P. aeruginosa (12.1%) in the elderly without pneumonia (Table S5).

The three most common co-infection pathogens amomg patients with ARIs were S. pneumoniae with H. influenzae (4.6%, 86/1890), IFV with S. aureus (4.5%, 85/1890), and IFV with H. influenzae (3.3%, 62/1890) (Fig. 5, Table S6). Virus‒virus interactions exhibited negative competitive effects, such as IFV combined with SARS-CoV-2 (OR = 20.3 [95% confidence interval (CI): 7.2–57.0], p < 0.001), HRV (OR = 7.2 [95% CI: 3.5–15.0], p < 0.001), SARS-CoV-2 combined with HRV (OR = 9.4 [95% CI: 3.2–27.7], p < 0.001) and HPIV (OR = 7.7 [95% CI: 1.7–35.9], p = 0.009). Bacteria-bacteria interactions had a positive synergistic effect, such as M. catarrhalis combined with S. aureus (OR = 0.6 [95% CI: 0.3-1.0], p = 0.034) and S. pneumoniae (OR = 0.3 [95% CI: 0.2–0.5], p < 0.001), and M. pneumoniae combined with Group A Streptococcus (GAS) (OR = 0.2 [95% CI: 0.0–1.0], P = 0.043). Virus-bacteria interactions had competitive or synergistic effects, such as IFV combined with P. aeruginosa (OR = 7.2 [95% CI: 2.1–24.3], p = 0.001), SARS-CoV-2 combined with H. influenzae (OR = 1.8 [95% CI: 1.2–2.7], p = 0.009), M. catarrhalis (OR = 3.4 [95% CI: 1.3–8.8], p = 0.012) and M. pneumoniae (OR = 4.4 [95% CI: 1.2–16.0], p = 0.024) showed synergistic effects, while M. catarrhalis combined with HAdV (OR = 0.2 [95% CI: 0.1–0.4], p < 0.001) and human metapneumovirus (HMPV) (OR = 0.1 [95% CI: 0.0–1.0], p = 0.013) showed positive synergistic effects (Fig. 5; Table 2, Table S7).

Pathogen profiles by age, gender, and case type

The proportion of positivity on IFV decreased with age in patients aged > 45 years [Annual percent change (APC)=-5.9]. The proportion of positivity on SARS-CoV-2 increased with age in children under 10 years old (APC = 26.1), and then decreased with age in patients 10–55 years (APC=-2.5), and then increased with age in patients over 55 (APC = 8.8). The proportion of positivity on RSV decreased with age in patients (APC=-8.3). The proportion of positivity on EV decreased with age in patients aged 30–45 years (APC=-38.7). The proportions of positivity on IFV, EV, and SARS-CoV-2 in outpatients were higher than those in inpatients, while the proportions of positivity on HPIV and HMPV in inpatients were higher than those in outpatients. No difference was found in the distribution of viral profiles between different genders (Fig. S2, Table S8).

The proportions of positivity on H. influenzae, S. aureus, and GAS decreased with age in patients (APC=-1.7, -1.7, and − 11.3). The proportion of positivity on S. pneumoniae decreased with age in patients aged 0–20 years (APC=-6.6). The proportion of positivity on P. aeruginosa increased with age in patients aged 0–20 years (APC = 51.1). The proportion of positivity on K. pneumoniae increased with age in patients (APC = 7.9). The proportions of positivity on H. influenzae, S. aureus, and S. pneumoniae in outpatients were higher than those in inpatients. while the proportions of positivity on P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii, M. pneumoniae, and Legionella pneumophila (L. pneumophila) in inpatients were higher than those in outpatients. No difference was found in the distribution of bacterial profiles between different genders (Fig. S2, Table S8).

Comparison of pathogen profiles before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic in Shenzhen

Published literature have reported the pathogen profiles among hospitalized children with ARIs before and during the COVID-19 pandemic in Shenzhen, and our study indicated those after the COVID-19 pandemic in Shenzhen. He Y, et al. reported IFV, RSV, HRV, and HPIV were the top four pathogens among 2025 hospitalized children (89.6% < 4 y old) during Jul. 2007 to Jun. 2010 in Shenzhen using luminex platform and multiplex xTAGTM respiratory viral panel assay [14]. Wang H, et al. reported RSV, HAdV, HPIV, and IFV were the top four pathogens among 30,443 hospitalized children (83.0%≤3y old) during Jan. 2012 to Dec. 2015 using direct immunofluorescence assay [15]. Li L, et al. reported HRV, HPIV, HAdV, and RSV were the top four pathogens among 5696 hospitalized children (< 14 y old) in Sept. to Dec. 2019, and HRV, RSV, HPIV, and HMPV were the top four pathogens in Sept. to Dec. 2020 using polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) [7]. Our study reported RSV, IFV, HPIV, and HRV were the top four pathogens among 41 hospitalized children (< 5 y old) during Nov. 2022 to Jun. 2023 in Shenzhen using tNGS technology, and RSV, IFV, HPIV, MP, and HRV were the top five pathogens among 52 hospitalized children (< 14y old) (Figs. 6 and 7).

Discussion

Our study reported the characteristics of the pathogen profiles and co-infection among patients with ARIs in the post-COVID-19 era in Shenzhen. The proportion of positivity on any respiratory pathogen was 84.0%, and the co-infection proportion was 41.4%. IFV, SARS-CoV-2, and HRV were the three leading viral pathogens, and H. influenzae, S. aureus, and S. pneumoniae were the three leading bacterial pathogens. There were differences in the pathogen profiles among patients with different ages, pneumonia groups, and case types. Virus‒virus interactions showed competitive effects, while bacterium‒bacterium interactions showed synergistic effects. RSV, IFV, HPIV, HAdV, and HRV were the top five viral pathogens among hospitalized children before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic in Shenzhen.

The proportion of positivity on any pathogen among patients with ARIs in the post-COVID-19 era in Shenzhen was 84.0%, and the co-infection proportion was 41.4% through tNGS technology. The negative detection rate of 16% may be caused by non-standard sampling, low viral load of pathogens, or the presence of other than 108 pathogens, which can be further identified by metagenomic technology in the future. The detection of pathogens related to notifiable infectious diseases showed the advantages of tNGS technology, that is, routine detection of 108 respiratory pathogens including pathogens related to notifiable infectious diseases by tNGS technology can achieve the purpose of early detection and early intervention of infectious diseases [16]. The high proportion of positivity on EBV may reflect the unique epidemiological characteristics in Shenzhen, the increased detection sensitivity, or the lack of clinical knowledge. Future studies combined with viral load, host immune status and molecular typing were recommended to clarify the true role of EBV in ARIs and provide a basis for precise diagnosis and treatment [17].

In the immediate post-COVID-19 era, IFV, SARS-CoV-2, and HRV were the three leading viral pathogens, and H. influenzae, S. aureus, and S. pneumoniae were the three leading bacterial pathogens among patients with ARIs in Shenzhen. This finding highlighted the evolving landscape of respiratory pathogens in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic and underscored the complex interplay between viral and bacterial co-circulation in ARIs. The prominence of IFV and HRV aligns with global reports of the resurgence of seasonal respiratory viruses following the relaxation of NPIs [18]. The rapid rebound of IFV suggested a potential “immunity gap” due to reduced exposure during pandemic restrictions, as observed in other regions [19, 20]. Meanwhile, the persistent detection of SARS-CoV-2 reflected its transition from a pandemic to an endemic pathogen, warranting continued surveillance alongside other respiratory viruses. The dominance of HRV, known for its high transmissibility and limited susceptibility to NPIs, further emphasized its resilience in diverse epidemiological settings [21]. On the bacterial side, the prevalence of H. influenzae, S. aureus, and S. pneumoniae suggested that bacterial co-infections or secondary infections remained a significant concern in ARI management. These pathogens were well-documented contributors to respiratory morbidity, and their co-detection with viral pathogens may exacerbate disease severity [22]. The post-pandemic era may have altered microbial colonization patterns due to changes in antibiotic use, immune responses, or viral-bacterial interactions, necessitating further investigation into these dynamics.

There were differences in the pathogen profiles among patients with different ages, pneumonia groups, and case types. Children under 5 years old had the highest viral positive proportion compared with other age groups, and school-age children have the highest bacterial positive proportion, which was consistent with the characteristics before the COVID-19 pandemic [9]. The high proportions of positivity on RSV in children and SARS-CoV-2 in the elderly in Shenzhen were consistent with the overall characteristics in China [9, 23], and the children and elderly individuals should receive more targeted measures including health education and timely vaccination [24]. The high proportion of positivity on IFV in school-age children was also consistent with previous results [9, 25–27], and possible reasons may include low immune levels, higher exposure risk due to crowded school environment, and weak awareness of self-hygiene protection. It will be necessary to further analyze the subtypes of IFV and implement precise interventions in the future such as research and development of subtype-specific vaccines, key interventions for subtype-specific high-risk populations, etc. The age turning points of pathogens showed the age-specific risk, which was relatively consistent with those in China [9], also indicating that age-specific interventions should be implemented in the practice of prevention and control of infectious diseases.

The risk of pneumonia in school-age children infected with IFV or M. pneumoniae was higher than that in children without pneumonia, and the risk of pneumonia in the elderly infected with P. aeruginosa was higher than that in children without pneumonia, which revealed distinct age-specific patterns in the risk of pneumonia associated with different pathogens, highlighting the importance of targeted prevention strategies across populations. For IFV, the increased risk may be attributed to the virus’s ability to damage respiratory epithelium and impair mucociliary clearance, thereby predisposing to secondary bacterial invasion and more severe lower respiratory tract involvement [28]. Similarly, M. pneumoniae was known to cause direct epithelial injury and provoke intense local inflammatory responses that can progress to pneumonia, particularly in school-age children who represented the peak demographic for this pathogen [29].

Our study revealed significantly higher proportions of positivity on HPIV, HMPV, P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii, M. pneumoniae, and L. pneumophila among inpatients compared to outpatients. The increased prevalence of respiratory viruses (HPIV and HMPV) in hospitalized patients may be attributed to their propensity to cause severe lower respiratory tract infections, particularly in vulnerable populations. These viruses were known to induce significant airway inflammation and epithelial damage, which can progress to bronchiolitis or pneumonia requiring hospitalization [30]. For bacterial pathogens, the predominance of P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii among inpatients aligned with their well-documented role as opportunistic nosocomial pathogens. These organisms thrived in healthcare environments and frequently colonized patients with prolonged hospital stays, invasive devices (e.g., mechanical ventilation), or broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure [31]. The higher proportion of positivity on M. pneumoniae in inpatients may reflect both its potential to cause severe atypical pneumonia requiring hospitalization and possible diagnostic biases (e.g., more frequent testing in hospitalized cases). Similarly, L. pneumophila’s association with inpatient status likely mirrored its tendency to cause severe pneumonia (Legionnaires’ disease), particularly in elderly patients or those with comorbidities, often necessitating hospitalization for specialized management [32].

All virus‒virus interactions showed competitive effects, all bacterium‒bacterium interactions exhibited synergistic effects, and virus‒bacterium interactions exhibited competitive or synergistic effects among patients with ARIs. Co-infection with SARS-CoV-2 and IFV was not common in our study, which was consistent with the results in Russia [33]. Viral competitive interactions may suppress co-infecting viruses through three principal mechanisms, including competition for limited intracellular resources, modulation of host immune responses, and direct interference mediated by viral proteins [34–37]. Our study revealed that all analyzed bacterium-bacterium interactions exhibited synergistic effects, suggesting that microbial cooperation may be a critical ecological driver in the respiratory tract during infection. This striking pattern has been validated in previous studies [38–40], while differed from the balanced mix of synergistic and antagonistic interactions typically observed in healthy airway microbiomes [41], implying that disease conditions may select for cooperative microbial strategies. Three key mechanisms may explain this phenomenon, including resource-based synergism [42], collective virulence enhancement [43], and immune evasion collusion [44]. Especially, M. catarrhalis tended to be co-detected with other pathogens, whether this co-detection has certain consequences on the severity of the disease in clinical patients still needed more targeted attention and in-depth research.

RSV, IFV, HPIV, HAdV, and HRV were the top five viral pathogens among hospitalized children before, during, and after the COVID-19 pandemic in Shenzhen. The persistent prevalence of these viruses underscored their significant burden on children’s health, regardless of the extraordinary public health measures implemented during the pandemic. Notably, the stability in the ranking of these pathogens suggested that NPIs such as mask-wearing, social distancing, and school closures—while highly effective against SARS-CoV-2—had limited long-term suppressive effects on other common respiratory viruses. This observation aligned with previous studies reporting the resilience of viruses like RSV and HRV to NPIs, possibly due to their high transmissibility, environmental stability, or diverse transmission routes [45]. The rapid resurgence of these viruses post-pandemic further supported their epidemiological adaptability. Interestingly, while some global studies reported temporary declines in IFV and RSV activity during peak COVID-19 restrictions [46], our data indicated their swift return to pre-pandemic levels, emphasizing the need for robust pediatric vaccination strategies, particularly for influenza and RSV (with emerging monoclonal antibodies and vaccines). The sustained circulation of HAdV and HPIV, which were less susceptible to seasonal fluctuations, also called for heightened clinical awareness and improved multiplex diagnostic approaches in pediatric care.

Strengths and limitations

Our study was the first pilot study of comprehensive surveillance of 108 respiratory pathogens in China after the COVID-19 pandemic [47]. Compared with metagenomic sequencing technology, tNGS technology in our study has lower cost and broader pathogen spectrum coverage than multiple PCR detection technology, which effectively solved the problem of simultaneous surveillance of multiple respiratory pathogens by Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and could be considered for routine use in future surveillance. At the same time, the rapid detection of relatively rare pathogens in clinical diagnosis and treatment also provided key evidence for the early diagnosis and treatment of diseases [48]. A quantitative study of cost-effectiveness analysis of this tNGS technology could be considered in the future.

This study also has certain limitations. First, opportunistic pathogens were not necessarily invasive, and further data such as quantitative pathogen identification results, pathogens culture, and laboratory test results were needed to verify clinical diagnosis [49]. Second, the discovery of interactions between pathogens were revealed by their statistical relationships, and further research on the mechanism and impact on clinical outcome of their interactions was needed. Third, this study did not consider the effect of sample type on pathogen positive proportion, which may affect the results of the study. However, in order to reduce the bias, patients with upper respiratory tract infection were given priority to collect nasopharyngeal swabs, and patients with lower respiratory tract infection were given priority to collect bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. Fourth, due to lacking continuous annual data, the pathogen profile distribution and co-infection results in this study can only reflect the characteristics of patients with ARIs in winter and spring in Shenzhen. Fifth, the distribution of pathogen profiles in patients with or without comorbidities was not analyzed in this study due to missing information on patient comorbidities. Sixth, the comparisons of pathogens proportions of positivity were only made in children without adults due to limit literature data. Seventh, the limited sample size in this study may have introduced selection bias. Future studies with larger samples are recommended to enhance representativeness.

Conclusion

Our study revealed IFV, SARS-CoV-2, and HRV were the three leading viral pathogens, and H. influenzae, S. aureus, and S. pneumoniae were the three leading bacterial pathogens. There were differences in the pathogen spectrum among patients with different ages, pneumonia groups, and case types, indicating age-specific and pathogen-specific interventions should be implemented in the practice of prevention and control of infectious diseases. Virus‒virus interactions showed competitive effects, while bacterium‒bacterium interactions showed synergistic effects, which underscored the importance of multipathogen testing in clinical practice. RSV, IFV, HPIV, HAdV, and HRV among hospitalized children in Shenzhen should be given more attention and further research.
