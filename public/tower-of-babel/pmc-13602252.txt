Myoid gonadal stromal tumor: A rare testicular sex cord stromal neoplasm – a case report and review of the literature
Authors: Ferhat Çoban, Esra Betül Tunce
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myoid gonadal stromal tumor (MGST) is an exceptionally rare benign testicular sex cord–stromal neoplasm with limited published experience. We report a 30-year-old man presenting with a painless right testicular mass and normal serum tumor markers. Ultrasonography and magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a small enhancing intratesticular lesion suspicious for malignancy. Radical inguinal orchiectomy was performed. Histopathological and immunohistochemical evaluation was consistent with MGST, showing spindle-cell proliferation with diffuse smooth muscle actin, S100, and vimentin positivity and focal inhibin expression. No recurrence or metastasis occurred during 12 months of follow-up. This case highlights the diagnostic challenge and rarity of MGST.

Introduction

Testicular tumors account for approximately 1% of all malignancies in men and represent the most common solid malignancy among young adults aged 15–40 years. Approximately 90–95% of testicular neoplasms are germ cell tumors, whereas sex cord–stromal tumors constitute only 5–10% of all primary testicular tumors. This heterogeneous category includes Leydig cell tumors, Sertoli cell tumors, granulosa cell tumors, mixed sex cord–stromal tumors, and several uncommon spindle-cell neoplasms, each demonstrating distinct clinicopathological characteristics, biological behavior, and prognostic implications. Accurate pathological classification is therefore essential because therapeutic management and clinical outcomes differ substantially among these entities.1

Myoid gonadal stromal tumor (MGST) is one of the rarest members of the sex cord–stromal tumor family. It remains an exceedingly uncommon pathological diagnosis, with only a limited number of cases reported in the literature. Owing to accumulating clinicopathological evidence, it was subsequently recognized as a distinct entity in the 2016 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Tumors of the Urinary System and Male Genital Organs and retained in the most recent WHO classification.1 Histologically, MGST is characterized by a proliferation of bland spindle-shaped cells arranged in fascicular, storiform, or haphazard growth patterns. Immunohistochemically, tumor cells typically exhibit diffuse expression of smooth muscle actin (SMA) and S100 protein, often accompanied by variable inhibin positivity, supporting both myoid and gonadal stromal differentiation. Immunohistochemical evaluation is also essential for excluding morphologically similar spindle-cell neoplasms of the testis, including leiomyoma, fibrothecoma, and unclassified sex cord–stromal tumors.2,3

From a clinical perspective, MGST usually presents as a slowly enlarging, painless unilateral intratesticular mass. Serum tumor markers, including alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), are generally within normal reference ranges. Nevertheless, neither clinical presentation nor imaging findings are sufficiently specific to distinguish MGST reliably from malignant testicular neoplasms before surgery. As a consequence, most reported patients have undergone radical inguinal orchiectomy because definitive diagnosis can only be established following histopathological and immunohistochemical examination.3,4

Herein, we report a case of myoid gonadal stromal tumor of the testis in a 30-year-old man who underwent radical inguinal orchiectomy for a suspicious intratesticular mass. We describe the clinical, radiological, histopathological, and immunohistochemical findings and review the currently available literature, with particular emphasis on the differential diagnosis, emerging molecular insights, and management of this exceptionally rare testicular neoplasm.

Case presentation

A 30-year-old man presented to our urology outpatient clinic with a painless, palpable hard mass in the right testis that he had noticed during self-examination. He had no history of scrotal trauma, lower urinary tract symptoms, constitutional complaints, cryptorchidism, infertility, or previous scrotal surgery. Physical examination revealed a firm, well-circumscribed intratesticular nodule in the right testis without evidence of inguinal lymphadenopathy.

At the patient's initial presentation, serum tumor markers were within the normal reference ranges: AFP 2.83 ng/mL (reference: 0–9 ng/mL), β-hCG 0.20 mIU/mL (reference range for males: 0-5 mlU/mL), and LDH 170 U/L (reference: 0–248 U/L).

Scrotal ultrasonography demonstrated a 12 × 8.5 mm hypoechoic solid intratesticular lesion with internal vascularity extending toward the tunica albuginea. To further characterize the lesion, contrast-enhanced scrotal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed. MRI revealed an approximately 11 × 7 mm well-circumscribed intratesticular mass that appeared hypointense on T1-weighted images and hyperintense on T2-weighted images. Following contrast administration, the lesion demonstrated avid enhancement and diffusion restriction on diffusion-weighted imaging (Fig. 1A and B). The patient subsequently underwent right radical inguinal orchiectomy without perioperative complications.

Gross pathological examination revealed a well-circumscribed intratesticular tumor measuring 1.0 × 1.0 × 0.8 cm with a solid, whitish cut surface. Microscopically, the lesion consisted of spindle-shaped cells arranged predominantly in short fascicles and storiform patterns (Fig. 2A and B). Tumor cells exhibited bland cytological features with elongated nuclei and inconspicuous nucleoli. No significant nuclear atypia, mitotic activity, tumor necrosis, lymphovascular invasion, or perineural invasion was identified. The tumor was confined to the testicular parenchyma without involvement of the rete testis, tunica albuginea, epididymis, or spermatic cord. All surgical margins were negative. Immunohistochemically, the tumor cells showed diffuse positivity for S100 protein and SMA (Fig. 2C and D). In addition, the tumor cells were diffusely positive for vimentin and focally positive for inhibin, while they were negative for Melan-A, calretinin, desmin, CD34, pancytokeratin, synaptophysin, calponin, and H-caldesmon. The postoperative course was uneventful. During 12 months of follow-up, no evidence of local recurrence or distant metastasis was observed on clinical, radiological, or laboratory evaluation. At the most recent follow-up visit, the patient remained disease-free.

Discussion

MGST is among the rarest testicular sex cord–stromal tumors and remains one of the least frequently encountered entities in urological pathology. Although initially described several decades ago, it was only after its inclusion in the 2016 WHO classification that MGST became recognized as a distinct clinicopathological entity. Since then, published experience has remained extremely limited.2,4

The most comprehensive review to date was published by Dieckmann et al., in 2024, compiling 23 previously reported cases together with their own case. To the best of our knowledge, no additional cases have been reported in the literature since that review. Available evidence indicates that MGST most commonly affects young to middle-aged adults, with a median age of approximately 37 years and a median tumor size of 20 mm. Patients typically present with a painless unilateral testicular mass and normal serum tumor markers. Notably, no local recurrence or distant metastasis has been documented during follow-up in any reported patient, supporting the consistently benign biological behavior of MGST. Nevertheless, given the very limited number of reported cases, its complete clinicopathological spectrum, radiological characteristics, molecular profile, and long-term biological behavior remain incompletely defined. Beyond corroborating the clinicopathological features described in previous reports, an important contribution of the present case is the detailed radiological characterization and diagnostic evaluation of MGST. Despite its small size and well-circumscribed appearance, the lesion demonstrated internal vascularity on ultrasonography and avid contrast enhancement with diffusion restriction on MRI, features that raised concern for malignancy. Thus, this case further illustrates the substantial radiological overlap between MGST and malignant intratesticular neoplasms and highlights the continuing difficulty of establishing a confident preoperative diagnosis based on imaging alone.

Our patient closely resembled previously reported cases with respect to age, clinical presentation, tumor size, serum tumor markers, histopathological findings, and immunophenotype. Similar to the majority of published cases, the lesion was discovered after the patient noticed a palpable testicular mass, while serum AFP, β-hCG, and LDH levels remained within normal limits. Histologically, the tumor demonstrated the characteristic spindle-cell proliferation with storiform architecture and lacked cytological atypia, mitotic activity, necrosis, lymphovascular invasion, or invasive growth. The immunohistochemical profile of diffuse SMA, S100, and vimentin positivity with focal inhibin expression was also highly consistent with previously published reports.3,4

The histogenesis of MGST remains incompletely understood. Based on ultrastructural and immunohistochemical findings, the tumor is believed to originate from peritubular myoid cells or primitive gonadal mesenchymal cells capable of myogenic differentiation. The co-expression of smooth muscle markers and gonadal stromal markers supports a hybrid differentiation pattern that distinguishes MGST from conventional smooth muscle neoplasms of the testis.19

From a molecular perspective, knowledge regarding MGST remains scarce because of the limited number of reported cases. Unlike adult granulosa cell tumors, which frequently harbor FOXL2 mutations, molecular analyses of MGST have not identified recurrent pathogenic mutations or gene fusions. However, recurrent chromosome-level copy number gains have been reported, suggesting that MGST may harbor characteristic genomic alterations despite the absence of recurrent mutations or gene fusions. Nevertheless, the small number of molecularly characterized cases precludes definitive conclusions, and additional genomic and transcriptomic studies are needed to further clarify the molecular pathogenesis of MGST.20

The magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) characteristics of MGST remain poorly defined because of the extremely limited number of reported cases. In the present case, MRI demonstrated a small, well-circumscribed intratesticular lesion that was hypointense on T1-weighted images, hyperintense on T2-weighted images, showed contrast enhancement, and exhibited diffusion restriction. These findings raised suspicion for a testicular neoplasm but were not sufficiently specific to establish a preoperative diagnosis. Malignant testicular germ cell tumors, particularly seminomas, typically demonstrate low-to-intermediate T2 signal intensity, homogeneous enhancement, and restricted diffusion, whereas non-seminomatous germ cell tumors frequently exhibit more heterogeneous signal characteristics due to hemorrhage, cystic degeneration, or necrosis. Although our lesion appeared relatively small and well circumscribed without imaging evidence of hemorrhage or necrosis, its enhancement and diffusion restriction substantially overlapped with the imaging features of malignant testicular tumors. Therefore, MRI alone could not reliably differentiate MGST from malignant intratesticular neoplasms, and radical inguinal orchiectomy remained the appropriate diagnostic and therapeutic approach. Additional reports describing the MRI appearance of MGST are needed to better define its radiological spectrum and determine whether any imaging features may facilitate preoperative recognition in the future.4,15

The differential diagnosis of MGST includes several spindle-cell lesions of the testis, particularly leiomyoma, fibrothecoma, unclassified sex cord–stromal tumors, and solitary fibrous tumor. Histopathological examination alone may be insufficient because these entities can share overlapping morphological characteristics. Consequently, immunohistochemical evaluation plays a pivotal role in establishing the diagnosis. The characteristic combination of diffuse SMA and S100 expression, together with variable inhibin positivity and the absence of markers such as CD34, pancytokeratin, Melan-A, and desmin, represents the most useful diagnostic profile for distinguishing MGST from its histological mimickers.3,15,19

With regard to management, radical inguinal orchiectomy remains the standard treatment in most reported cases because malignancy cannot be excluded preoperatively. Nevertheless, accumulating evidence indicating uniformly benign behavior has raised interest in testis-sparing surgery for selected patients. Ercolino et al. reported successful organ-preserving management of an MGST with no evidence of recurrence during follow-up, suggesting that conservative surgery may represent a feasible option when clinical, radiological, and intraoperative findings are favorable. However, current evidence remains insufficient to recommend a routine testis-sparing approach, and larger series with long-term follow-up are required before definitive treatment recommendations can be established.15

Conclusion

MGST is an exceptionally rare testicular sex cord–stromal neoplasm characterized by distinctive histopathological and immunohistochemical features and an apparently benign clinical course. Despite increasing recognition following its inclusion in the WHO classification, the clinicopathological spectrum of this entity remains incompletely defined because of the limited number of reported cases. Our case further supports the growing evidence that MGST typically presents as a small intratesticular mass with normal serum tumor markers and favorable postoperative outcomes. However, preoperative distinction from malignant testicular neoplasms remains challenging because clinical and radiological findings are largely nonspecific. Additional well-documented cases with long-term follow-up and comprehensive molecular characterization are required to better define the biological behavior, radiological spectrum, and optimal management strategies for this rare tumor.
