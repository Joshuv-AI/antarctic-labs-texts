Molecular mechanisms involved in the anticancer activities of probiotics and postbiotics
Authors: Maria Teresa Rocchetti, Domenica Mangieri, Giuseppe Spano, Daniela Fiocco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProbiotics promote host health and have shown potential as both prophylactics and adjuvants for the treatment of several human diseases. Therefore, medical interest in these microorganisms has increased rapidly. Recently, probiotics along with postbiotics, i.e., their inanimated counterparts, have been explored for their capacity to treat cancer, a vast group of diseases characterized by uncontrolled cell proliferation, which represents a major global health burden and is the second leading cause of death worldwide. The present review collects and discusses recent preclinical studies addressing the anticancer action of probiotics and postbiotics from lactic acid bacteria and bifidobacteria, and describes the molecular mechanisms by which they hamper some major hallmarks of tumor cells, i.e., through stimulation of apoptosis, blockade of proliferation and the cell cycle, inhibition of metastasis, metabolic interference, activation of the host anti-cancer immune response. The medical use of probiotics should rely on an extensive understanding of the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying their anticancer activities. Accordingly, this review aims to provide insights into the advancements and limitations of the current research in this field, which is strongly needed to provide solid rationales for the prospective clinical use of probiotics in cancer.Keywords: Lactobacilli, Bifidobacteria, Colorectal cancer, Apoptosis, Short chain fatty acid, Immune surveillance, Cell cycle

Introduction

Probiotics are live microorganisms that confer health benefits to the human host when they are consumed in adequate amounts [1]. As such, they are being investigated for an increasing number of medical applications, including the treatment of gastro-intestinal disorders, skin wounds, burns, mucosal and urinary tract infections, obesity, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, and lately cognitive impairments and cancer [2–7]. Recently, postbiotics, consisting in inanimated microbial cells, have attracted much interest because they exert beneficial effects similar to those of the probiotics from which they are derived, in addition to being safer and more stable [8].

Cancer, a multifactorial disease involving a complex intersection of cellular and molecular events, represents the second leading cause of death worldwide [9]. Despite advancements in therapeutic treatments, the survival rate of cancer patients remains very poor. Furthermore, current therapeutic interventions are not always effective and often cause side effects that heavily affect the lifestyle of cancer patients. Consequently, alternative approaches, novel anticancer agents and more effective therapeutic protocols are the subject of intense research [10].

The human gut microbiota, i.e., the complex microbial community colonizing the intestine, is vital for several host functions [11]. Alterations in the composition and functions of the gut microbiota, referred to as gut dysbiosis, have been linked to the pathogenesis of various human diseases, including cancer [12, 13]. Documented associations, so-called oncomicrobiome signatures, have been found between dysbiosis and cancer risk and progression [14]. Moreover, the prevalence of specific gut microbiota for different cancer types has been described [15].

By modulating the gut microbiota, diet and some nutritional factors can affect the risk of several types of cancer [16, 17]. The administration of probiotics is known to promote eubiosis, i.e., a healthy and balanced microbial community of the gut microbiota [18], which, in turn can influence the development and prognosis of cancer. Therefore, several studies have pointed to the chemiopreventive potential of probiotics against cancer [19]. Indeed, a number of in vivo studies and clinical trials has confirmed that probiotics, as well as postbiotics, can control the growth and proliferation of cancer cells [7, 20–22].

Like for any other therapeutic application, the use of probiotics as anticarcinogenic agents should rely on an in-depth understanding of the molecular and cellular mechanisms underlying their cancer-protective effects [23]. Comprehension of the mechanistic action of probiotics is critical for a rational selection of microbial strains and their successful clinical application. This narrative review aims to summarize the current knowledge on the molecular mechanisms underlying the anticancer effects of probiotics and postbiotics from lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and bifidobacteria. Here we report relevant in vitro and in vivo studies investigating the molecules and the cellular pathways involved. This overview will shed light on the anticancer action of probiotics and postbiotics, contributing to identifying gaps in related research and providing a more robust rationale for the current and future clinical use of probiotics in cancer therapy.

Several published studies highlight the connections between gut microbiome and cancer, analysing how variations in complexity and richness might contribute to tumor onset and development. Moreover, most reviews on the anticancer activity of probiotics analyze a single species, a microbial consortium or a certain class of probiotic-derived compounds, which are usually investigated in the context of a specific tumor type. In this article, we aim to offer a more comprehensive overview, by exploring the anticancer mechanisms related to two major groups of classical, safely used and widely marketed probiotics, considering their activity as both live cells and postbiotics. We report the general anticancer activity, encompassing different types of tumor. Moreover, we emphasize the mechanistic nature, as we report the specific molecular players, of both host cells and probiotics, which are involved in the antitumor effect. Though this approach prevent us from describing a multitude of clinical and animal studies on the protective and therapeutic action of probiotics, we try not to neglect this aspect, providing some hints on clinical trials testing probiotics as adjuvants, and mentioning some animal studies that underscore molecular details.

Probiotics and postbiotics

Probiotics are live microbes that promote host health [1]. Well-known and broadly used probiotics are Gram-positive bacteria belonging to the LAB group and to the genera Bifidobacterium and Bacillus. Escherichia coli and the yeast Saccharomyces spp. also comprise well-studied probiotic strains. Moreover, there is an emerging trend in the exploration of so-called next-generation probiotics (NGPs), i.e., recently isolated gut-commensal bacteria, such as Akkermansia muciniphila, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, and members of the genus Bacteroides, which might play a role in the onset and progression of some diseases. While “traditional” probiotics are usually regulated as “food-supplements” and mainly enhance the gastrointestinal balance, NGPs are exclusively gut-symbionts, have been identified on the basis of clinical data, exhibit targeted mechanisms that link them to metabolic and inflammatory disorders, neurological conditions and even cancer. For these reasons, they could be developed as specific “live drugs” in the context of precision medicine [24].

Interestingly, cell viability may not be necessary to achieve health effects; indeed, also postbiotics can have beneficial properties. Even though the definition is still debated, according to the International Scientific Association of Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP), the term “postbiotic” indicates “a preparation of inanimate microorganisms and/or their components that confers a health benefit on the host” [25], and may include inactivated probiotic cells, bacterial cell components, crude cell extracts, with or without metabolites. Given the safety issues regarding the use of live microbes (e.g., risk of infection in immunocompromised patients), as well as the challenges in manufacturing products containing live cells, postbiotics are considered promising alternatives to probiotics, especially for food supplements and pharmaceuticals targeted to vulnerable populations, including cancer patients [8, 26].

Upon oral ingestion, probiotic microorganisms can colonize the gut. Therefore, the gastrointestinal tract is the main organ that directly benefits from these microbes [18]. Prebiotics, defined by ISAPP as substrates that are “selectively utilized by host microorganisms conferring a health benefit” [27], can enhance the efficacy of probiotics, by improving their gut colonization capacity. Several studies, including clinical trials and meta-analyses, have supported the beneficial effects imparted by probiotics [28]. Probiotic activities are strain-specific [1], however, some are broadly shared, e.g., most probiotics strengthen gut barrier integrity, enhance gastro-intestinal immunity, and restore the balance of the intestinal microbiota [29]. Because the gut, with its resident microbiota, is connected through complex, bidirectional networks with many different organs, the positive impact of probiotics and their metabolites may involve other organs and systems [30–33]. Hence, in addition to gastrointestinal disorders, their therapeutic potential encompasses other diseases, including obesity, skin conditions, allergies, neurological pathologies, and cancer [28, 34].

LAB and bifidobacteria

Conventional probiotics such as lactobacilli and bifidobacteria are the bioactive ingredients of popular functional foods and dietary supplements [35, 36].

Lactobacilli constitute a large and diverse subgroup of LAB (phylum Firmicutes) that are traditionally used to ferment food [37]. They are widespread in soil and food-related niches. Moreover, they can colonize the human body, particularly the mucosa of the oral cavity, vagina and the gastrointestinal tract. Interestingly, while lactobacilli are only minor and generally transient members of the human gut microbiota [38], they can have a relevant impact on host health [39, 40]. Bifidobacteria, phylum Actinobacteria, are among the dominant bacterial populations in the human gastrointestinal tract and are considered key health-promoting commensals, making major contributions to gut homeostasis, immune system development, and nutrient assimilation [41]. The probiotic activities of bifidobacteria are well documented in the scientific literature and encompass both intestinal and extraintestinal conditions [42, 43], with broad applications, especially in pediatrics [44].

Epidemiological and experimental studies have investigated the prophylactic and therapeutic effects of LAB and bifidobacterial probiotics against cancer, particularly colorectal cancer (CRC) [22, 45–47]. Many clinical trials have evaluated the efficacy of probiotics as adjuvants to reinforce conventional therapies and/or attenuate their adverse side effects, thus aiming to ameliorate the response to treatment, recovery and quality of life in patients with cancer (Table 1).

Cancer and features of cancer cells

The term “cancer” refers to a large group of complex and dynamic diseases characterized by an uncontrolled cell proliferation [79], which can present benign or malignant features [80]. Typically, benign phenotypes develop slowly, and the growing cells form a mass that, although reaching considerable sizes, remains delimited by a layer of connective tissue [80]. In addition, benign tumors retain the characteristics of the original tissue and do not invade the surrounding organs [80, 81]. Conversely, malignant tumors not only evolve rapidly, but also acquire an infiltrative phenotype, invading the surrounding tissues, and eventually, through lymphatic or blood circuits, they can colonize distant tissues, forming metastases [82].

Cancer cells exhibit various genetic and epigenetic alterations that affect the expression of genes controlling growth, senescence, survival, DNA repair systems, motility and cell surface properties. During a multistep process, such mutations accumulate; hence, cancer cells acquire significant dysregulation and distinctive biological properties, including i) the loss of functionality of major cell cycle checkpoints and the insensitivity to growth-modulating signals (with consequent aberrant proliferation), ii) defective apoptosis, iii) the ability to escape immune surveillance (e.g., by altering surface antigen presentation and/or by inhibiting immune cell activity), iv) metabolic alterations (e.g., increased demand for glucose and auxotrophy for specific nutrients), v) genomic instability, vi) dedifferentiation and migration capacity (resulting in cancer cell spreading and metastasis) vii) multidrug-resistance [83].

The oncomicrobiome

The oncomicrobiome (i.e., the tumor-associated microbial community) is currently recognized as a key constituent of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [84]. It comprises a diverse, multi-kingdom community of microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, and viruses, along with their collective genomes and gene products. These tumor-associated microorganisms may originate from adjacent normal tissues, the circulatory system, or the gastrointestinal tract, and can co-migrate with tumor cells facilitating the metastatic dissemination [85].

Characterized by a high heterogeneity, the oncomicrobiome exhibits distinct microbial signatures across different tumor types and even among subtypes of the same cancer, underscoring its potential as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target [86]. Far from passive bystanders, these microbial communities actively shape the TME and act on tumorigenesis, invasion, and metastasis by producing toxins and metabolites that destabilize DNA, induce epigenetic alterations and sustain proliferative signaling [87]. Moreover, in a context-dependent fashion, the oncomicrobiome modulates immune cell infiltration, orchestrates local immune responses and/or mechanisms of immune evasion, thereby either promoting immunosuppression or enhancing antitumor immunity. In this context, the oncomicrobiota appears as a key determinant of cancer immunotherapy efficacy, representing a promising therapeutic target [86]. Probiotics and postbiotics not only can contibute to restore a balanced microbiome in the gut, but also act as modulators of the intratumoral microbiome and TME, potentially enhancing anti-tumor immune response and, thus, immunotherapy outcomes [88]. Consequently, they are considered emerging tools for microbial-based therapies.

Mechanisms involved in probiotic and postbiotic anticancer effects

Probiotics and postbiotics can influence the process of carcinogenesis through mechanisms that impact several peculiar features of cancer cells, i.e., by interfering with their proliferation and invasiveness, by enhancing apoptosis or other types of death, by affecting their distinctive metabolism and/or by promoting the immune response to facilitate their clearance (Fig. 1).

Anti-proliferative and anti-metastatic activity

In addition to immortality, abnormal growth regulation, sustained angiogenesis and enhanced migration ability are major hallmarks of neoplastic cells [162]. High motility-invasive properties underlie metastatic colonization and represent the main requisite of tumor development and progression [163]. Probiotics and postbiotics have been found to affect signalings that sustain cancer cell proliferation and to modulate genes related to growth, motility and invasiveness. Most of the in vitro studies testing probiotic anticancer properties use human colon cancer cells (Table 2), which differ in their genetic background and propensity to grow and migrate. Cytotoxicity assays have been largely used to demonstrate the anti-proliferative activity of several LAB against different types of cancer cells [118, 164–168]. These studies evaluate the activity of either live probiotic cells, or their postbiotics, such as cell-free culture supernatants (CFSs), i.e., mixtures of potentially bioactive molecules released into the medium during bacterial growth. Some research papers have also attempted to investigate the molecular mechanism underlying the observed antiproliferative effect. For example, the CFS of lactobacilli induced cell cycle arrest and reduced CRC cell proliferation by downregulating the expression of ERBB-2 and ERBB-3 [92, 94]. The ERBB genes encode a family of plasma membrane receptor tyrosine kinases, which, upon stimulation, activate intracellular signals that promote cell growth and proliferation [169]. The overexpression and hyperactivation of ERBB genes, with consequent aberrant signaling, have been observed in a variety of human tumors, making these receptors attractive targets in cancer chemotherapy [170, 171].

Proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) is another intriguing molecular target for broad-spectrum chemotherapy drugs [172]. PCNA is a multifunctional protein involved in the synthesis and repair of DNA. PCNA proteins associate around DNA to form a ring structure that is essential for its replication. As cancer cells are fast growing, PCNA is indispensable for their survival. The exopolysaccharide (EPS) isolated from Lactiplantibacillus plantarum−12 was shown to hamper colon cancer cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner and by inhibiting PCNA expression [95].

The proliferation of colon cancer cells and their chemoresistant counterparts was decreased by culture supernatants from food-isolated LAB species, which induced a higher production of mitochondrial ROS. In addition, these postbiotics potentiate the effect of the chemotherapeutic agent doxorubicin (DOXO) on HT-29 cells, reversing their chemoresistance [96]. Furthermore, CFS from L. plantarum OC01 reduced IL-6-induced proliferation of 2D- and 3D-spheroid CRC cells through inhibiting the ERK and mTOR/p70S6k pathways, whose dysregulation typically sustains the uncontrolled growth of tumor cells [97]. The CFS also inhibited the epithelial-like to neural-like cadherin switch, thus hampering the process of epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT) [97], i.e., a phenotypic reprogramming that modifies cell shape, adhesiveness and motility, enhancing the migratory abilities of cancer cells [173]. Impaired in vitro migration of CRC cells was also observed upon treatment with heat-killed Lacticaseibacillus paracasei and L. plantarum [138], which decreased tyrosine phosphorylation of FAK, i.e., an enzyme that modulates cell motility by remodelling the cytoskeleton and focal adhesions [174].

The VEGF-MMP axis plays a critical role in cancer expansion and metastasis: VEGF stimulates angiogenesis within the tumor mass, thereby fueling its growth, whereas matrix metalloproteases (MMPs) are essential for extracellular matrix remodeling and cell migration, promoting cellular invasion and tumor expansion [175, 176]. L. plantarum YYC-3-derived CFS, which inhibits the expression, secretion and activity of MMP-2 and MMP-9, and reduces the level of VEGF-A, has been shown to have antimetastatic effects on colon cancer cells [93]. Interestingly, compared with other lactobacilli, L. plantarum YYC-3 CFS showed the greatest ability to inhibit cancer cell invasion and migration, thus revealing the different compositions and biological activities of CFSs from different LAB [93]. In a previous study, CFS from Lacticaseibacillus casei and L. rhamnosus GG (LGG) (i.e., a probiotic strain widely studied in relation to cancer) reduced the infiltration and invasion properties of metastatic colon cancer cells by decreasing MMP-9 activity while increasing the level of the tight junction protein ZO-1, i.e., a marker of cellular adhesion that restrains migration [90]. Through CFS fractionation, the cell invasion inhibitory activity could be attributed to a macromolecule, possibly a protein or apolysaccharide, that is released by probiotics into their growth medium.

Cell cycle arrest

The cell cycle comprises a series of events that occur in a cell as it prepares to divide. The progression through each step of the cell cycle (i.e., G1, S, G2, and M) is tightly controlled by specific checkpoint proteins, i.e., cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), which form complexes with their regulatory partner cyclins [177]. In normal cells, the activity of cyclin-CDK complexes is finely regulated by both internal and external signals, allowing proliferation only under specific conditions, e.g., lack of DNA damages and the presence of growth promoting factors. In fact, the disruption of cyclin-CDK complexes profoundly affects cell division, often causing hyperproliferation with consequent neoplastic transformation [178].

Probiotics and/or postbiotics can inhibit the growth and proliferation of cancer cells by inducing cell cycle arrest. CFSs from probiotic lactobacilli were found to stop colon cancer cells in G0/G1 and to downregulate genes encoding the cyclins required for the G1 to S transition [94, 99]. Similarly, HT29 cell growth was inhibited by CFS from L. plantarum S2T10D, which induced decreased mRNA and protein levels of cyclin B1 and D1, in parallel with cell cycle arrest in the G2/M phase [100]. Since the antiproliferative effect was associated with the strain-specific butyrogenic capability, butyrate might be involved in the cell cycle regulatory mechanism [100]. Indeed, short chain fatty acids (SCFAs) including butyrate, can bind and stimulate G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) on the plasma membrane of colonocytes [179]. Moreover, they are known to regulate the expression of human genes through chromatin remodeling, i.e., by inhibiting histone-deacetylase (HDAC) enzymes [135]. Because SCFAs are produced via anaerobic bacterial fermentation within the gut, both of these activities seem to underlie their capacity to protect against colorectal carcinogenesis [180].

SCFAs can cause growth arrest and differentiation in human colon carcinoma cells, which is accompanied by increased expression of the P21 cell cycle inhibitor, downregulation of cyclin B1, as well as increased expression of the differentiation marker intestinal alkaline phosphatase [89]. Later transcriptomic studies corroborated the significant downregulation of genes that are necessary for DNA replication and cell cycle progression [91]. The antiproliferative action of SCFAs has also been studied in the context of other tumor types. For example, in human non-small cell lung cancer cells, butyrate was found to block growth by modulating the expression of cell-cycle regulators, i.e., increasing the levels of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CKIs), such as P21, P27 and P16, through a P53-dependent or P53-independent mechanism [160]. Similarly, sodium butyrate arrested HPV-positive carcinoma cells in the G1/S transition, with upregulation of CKIs and complete loss of CDK2 activity [161].

Proapoptotic activity

Upon neoplastic transformation, cells become recalcitrant to apoptosis, i.e. the major type of regulated cell death, which ensures tissue homeostasis and allows the elimination of faulty or damaged cells [181]. Many probiotics and postbiotics stimulate apoptosis of cancer cells, inducing both the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways. Although the modes of action are not yet fully understood, probiotics appear to affect key apoptosis-related signaling pathways, modulate the levels of caspases and control the balance between pro- and antiapoptotic BCL-2. Moreover, probiotics can stimulate cancer cell death through autophagy and pyroptosis (Fig. 2).

Probiotics and postbiotics stimulate cancer cell death by different mechanisms. Apoptosis is promoted via both the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways, e.g. through activation of procaspases, by enhancing the release of cytochrome C, by modulating the expression and activity of pro- and antiapoptotic BCL-2 proteins, by inducing oxidative stress, and by modulating MAPK, JNK and ER stress-related pathways. Autophagy occurs by suppression of the mTOR pathway, reduction of β-catenin, and enhanced levels of proteins associated to the biogenesis of lysosomes and autophagosomes. Extracts from heat-killed L. plantarum 06CC2 induced apoptosis in intestinal cancer cells by activating the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress response and JNK/P38 MAPK signaling [114]. Indeed, the extract increased the levels of cleaved caspase-9 and − 3 and BIM while decreasing the protein and mRNA expression of the antiapoptotic BCL-2, suggesting the activation of mitochondria-mediated apoptosis. Moreover, increased levels of phosphorylated eIF2α, ATF4 and CHOP, suggest the activation of ER stress signaling. Consistently, the JNK/P38 cascade, which is also associated with the ER stress response, was activated, resulting in elevated levels of phosphorylated JNK (p-JNK), P38 (p-P38), ATF2 (p-ATF2) and c-JUN (p-c JUN) [114]. Heat-killed lactobacilli induce apoptosis in colon adenocarcinoma cells, by increasing the mRNA expression of BAX, CASP-3 and − 9 while reducing BCL-2 levels [115]. Similarly, CFSs from lactobacilli and bifidobacteria promoted apoptosis in different cancer cell lines, including colon, gastric and melanoma cells, and this effect was associated with the modulation of mitochondrial signaling and the caspase pathway [98, 118, 155]. Viable or heat killed bifidobacteria exert similar effects on colon cancer cells [121].

Several commercial LAB and bifidobacteria induce CRC cell death by modulating apoptosis- and tumor progression-related markers, such as CASP-9, BCL-6 and the proto-oncogene c-JUN [117]. L. plantarum was found to induce apoptosis in human oral cancer cells, upregulating the tumor suppressor PTEN, and downregulating MAPK [151]. Inactivation of the MAPK pathway is known to cause apoptosis in cancer cells [182]. Moreover, the overexpression of SMAC sensitizes neoplastic cells to apoptotic death [183]. In oral cancer cells, L. plantarum culture supernatants increased SMAC mRNA, while decreasing Survivin, i.e., an apoptosis inhibitor that is overexpressed in most cancers [19]. Moreover, L. plantarum secondary metabolites reduce the expression of antiapoptotic genes in breast cancer cells, as well as in the model organism Drosophila melanogaster, where a mixture of L. plantarum metabolites increased the expression of proapoptotic genes, suggesting the progression of cancer cells toward apoptosis [147].

Proapoptotic activities of molecules isolated from probiotics

Several types of molecules isolated from probiotic LAB and bifidobacteria may exert antitumor effects by inducing the apoptosis of cancer cells. Among them, exopolysaccharides (EPSs) are carbohydrate homo- or hetero-polymers secreted by bacteria [184]. EPSs exposed on the cell surface of lactobacilli have strain-specific characteristics and are known to contribute to various health benefits, including cancer protection. The anticancer activity of EPS most likely depends on mechanisms such as immunomodulation and the induction of cancer cell apoptosis. EPSs purified from different lactobacilli trigger apoptosis in various types of cancer cells, mostly by modulating the expression of apoptosis-related genes, such as caspases, BAX, BCL-2, CYT C and Survivin [95, 153, 185]. EPSs exhibit a broad biochemical diversity, and some studies have highlighted the relevance of their molecular structure and sugar composition for their proapoptotic ability [112, 153], suggesting that this may be mediated by the interaction of specific sugars with Toll-like receptors (TLRs) on cancer cells. Accordingly, in mouse intestinal cancer cells, L. plantarum EPSs were observed to induce extrinsic apoptosis by activating the c-JUN dependent FAS/FASL-mediated pathway, possibly through stimulation of TLR-2 [109].

Probiotic lactobacilli and bifidobacteria can produce SCFAs through fiber fermentation. SCFAs, particularly butyrate and propionate, are known to stimulate mitochondria-mediated apoptosis in human colon cancer cells [186]. In Caco-2 cells, butyrate was found to activate the caspase cascade, increasing the levels of caspase-9 and cleaved caspase-3 and −1 [102]. Since BCL-2 proteins control the release of CYT C by modulating the permeability of the mitochondrial outer membrane, the authors assumed that butyrate causes a shift in the ratio of proapoptotic to antiapoptotic BCL-2, thus increasing the cytosolic level CYT C, which results in caspase-9-directed activation of effector caspases [102].

Butyrate can promote programmed cell death in vitro, in various types of cancer cells including breast cancer [187], glioma [188], and mesothelioma cell lines [189]. For example, butyrate has been shown to exert proapoptotic effects on human hepatoma cells [152], where loss of the mitochondrial membrane potential and modulation of caspases and BCL-2 proteins were accompanied by the degradation of β-catenin and of the cell cycle regulators PRB and cyclins D. Likewise, in breast cancer cells, butyrate induced cell cycle arrest and extrinsic apoptosis through the activation of the FAS system [150].

Another molecular player involved in the antitumor activity of butyrate is neuropilin-1 (NRP-1), i.e., a transmembrane VEGF receptor that is regarded as a potential prognostic biomarker in cancer. In colon cancer cells, NRP-1 is typically upregulated, thus promoting migration and resistance to apoptosis in response to VEGF. Butyrate caused the apoptosis of CRC cells by downregulating both NRP-1 and VEGF [103]. By acting as an HDAC inhibitor, butyrate may increase the acetylation of some key regulatory genes as well as transcription factors, including SP1, which in turn controls the expression of factors regulating the cell cycle and apoptosis. For instance, in colon cells, increased acetylation of SP1 reduces its binding to the promoters of specific target genes, thus enhancing the expression of the proapoptotic protein BAK and the CKI P21, thereby promoting apoptosis and cycle arrest [190].

Like butyrate, even propionate can inhibit HDACs and promote histone hyperacetylation, hence leading to epigenetic regulation [89]. A transcriptomic study revealed that in colon cancer cells, propionate downregulates the histone arginine methyltransferase PRMT1, stimulating apoptosis through inhibition of the mTOR pathway [113].

Probiotics can also produce conjugated linoleic acids (CLAs), i.e., functional lipids consisting of heterogeneous, geometric isomers derived from linoleic acid. CLAs, which occur naturally in dairy products and in ruminant meat, have beneficial properties, including anticarcinogenic activity [191]. L. plantarum CLA (LPCLA) induced the apoptosis of human breast cancer cells through the down regulation of the NF-қB pathway [146]. Specifically, LPCLA stimulated CYT C release into the cytoplasm, increased the level of the proapoptotic protein BAX and hindered the nuclear translocation of P65 by blocking the phosphorylation of its inhibitor IқBα [146]. In unstimulated or resting cells, IқBα is associated with cytoplasmic NF-қB, thereby inhibiting its translocation into the nucleus, where it induces the expression of proinflammatory and cell proliferation genes [146, 192]. Interestingly, two different conjugated α-linolenic acid (CLNA) isomers from L. plantarum, namely, CLNA1 and CLNA2, induced pyroptosis in colon cancer cells [116]. Pyroptosis is a proinflammatory form of programmed death that kills cells through plasma membrane perforation and involves specific inflammatory caspases and the pore-forming proteins gasdermin [193]. This type of cell death is also triggered by some chemotherapy drugs [194]. The authors reported that L. plantarum CLNAs were ineffective at regulating key apoptosis-related proteins; instead, CLNA1 activated caspase-1 and gasdermin-D, whereas CLNA2 promoted the cleavage of caspase-4, caspase-5 and gasdermin-D, thus activating the classical and non-canonical pyroptosis pathways, respectively [116]. Moreoever, CLNA treatment increased lipid peroxidation, suggesting that this oxidative insult could further increase pyroptosis.

Ferrichromes, siderophores produced by bacteria, are hydrophilic metal-chelating agents with low molecular weights. Ferrichrome captures metallic elements, especially iron, thus playing important roles in the homeostasis of metals and in heavy metal tolerance [195]. Ferrichrome isolated from the CFS of probiotic L. casei inhibited colon cancer progression in vitro and in a xenograft model, by activating the JNK-DDIT3-mediated apoptotic pathway. Cancer cells treated with ferrichrome presented activated JNK and increased mRNA expression of DDIT3, a transcription factor involved in the ER stress response pathway. Notably, the anticancer effect of ferrichrome is greater than that of conventional anticancer drugs, such as 5-FU or cisplatin [107]. Moreover, the probiotic-derived ferrichrome was shown to activate the proapoptotic JNK-DDIT3 pathway in other tumor types, including gastric [140], pancreatic [196] and esophageal [141] cancers. The same proapoptotic mechanism was confirmed in vivo, in an azoxymethane/dextran sulfate sodium (AOM-DSS) mouse model of colitis-associated tumorigenesis, and in patient-derived organoids for sporadic colorectal neoplasms [108]. These findings suggest that lactobacilli-derived ferrichrome could be a valid candidate antitumor drug.

Other molecules produced by probiotic LAB are bacteriocins, i.e., peptides that exhibit antimicrobial activity [197]. Among them, plantaricins are synthesized by L. plantarum species. Plantaricin BM-1, obtained from a probiotic L. plantarum strain, induced the apoptosis of CRC cells in a dose-dependent manner and increasing the levels of cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP-1 (i.e., a key enzyme involved in genomic stability) [120].

Over the last few decades, nanotechnology has been increasingly used in medicine, including applications for diagnosis, treatment, and tumor targeting in a safer and more effective manner. Nanoparticle (NP)-based drug delivery systems provide an innovative solution to overcome the limitations of conventional cancer therapies. These systems enhance drug delivery by improving the precision, efficiency, and safety of treatment. Engineered nanoparticles specifically target tumor cells, reducing off-target effects and enhancing therapeutic outcomes [198]. Key types of nanoparticles include liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, metallic nanoparticles, dendrimers, and quantum dots, which could effectively improve the delivery of various drugs used in chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy, offering more precise and effective treatment options [199]. Probiotics offer distinct advantages as microbial factories for the synthesis of metallic nanoparticles with anti-cancer activity, such as silver nanoparticles (AgNPs). AgNPs exhibit unique physicochemical properties depending on the producing bacterial species, and have different structures, sizes, and shapes, which allow them to penetrate cancer cells and interact with their cellular components. L. acidophilus AgNps modulate the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway, upregulating AMPK expression while suppressing mTOR, MMP-9, BCL-2, and α-SMA expression, which leads to increased apoptosis and autophagy in human liver cancer cells [154].

Proapoptotic mechanisms in animal models

Animal models are extremely useful for cancer research, allowing investigations of tumor initiation and development, as well as the efficacy and mechanims of therapeutics [200]. Animal models of CRC can be generated by injecting dimethyl-hydrazine (DMH), or azoxymethane/dextran sulfate sodium (AOM-DSS) (i.e., to induce colitis-associated CRC) into mice or rats. Experiments on such models point to LGG as a promising biotherapeutic agent for treating carcinogenesis. Indeed, oral administration of LGG to DMH rats, in addition to reducing inflammation, tumor incidence, size and progression, increased the expression of proapoptotic proteins CASP-3, BAX and P53, while decreasing the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2 in colon tissue [105].

Using an AOM/DSS mouse model, Lee and collaborators [106] compared the anticancer effect of live L. plantarum nF1 (pLp) with that of its nanosized counterpart (nLp), i.e., a dead, granular form of pLp. The administration of nLp was more effective at reducing the serum levels of inflammatory markers, increasing the expression of P53 and the proapoptotic protein BAX, and reducing the antiapoptotic protein BCL-2 in colon tissue. In a colitis-associated CRC mouse model, dietary intake of L. casei BL23, a probiotic strain with anti-inflammatory and anticancer properties, protected against CRC development and exhibited an antiproliferative effect in which the mRNAs encoding the executioner caspase-7, initiator caspase-9 and proapoptotic BIK were upregulated in colon tissue. In selected mice with high CASP-7 expression, cleaved caspase-7 increased, confirming the activation of intrinsic apoptosis in vivo. Moreover, the level of colonic IL-22 (which promotes cancer cell proliferation) is decreased in L. casei-treated mice [110].

When orally administered to DMH-treated rats, L. plantarum AdF10 shifted the level of proteins involved in the P53-mediated apoptotic pathway towards that of healthy controls [111]. In AOM/DSS mice, oral intake of L. plantarum−12 modulated the intestinal microbiota and promoted colon cancer cell death, downregulated PCNA and upregulated BAX [119]. Finally, in orthotopicallly transplanted CT-26 CRC mice, supplementation with microencapsulated L. plantarum LAB12 reduced the tumor volume, inhibited angiogenesis, and increased the protein levels of the tumour suppressor P53 and the proapoptotic protein caspase-3 [122].

Stimulation of autophagy

Autophagy consists of the lysosome-driven degradation of unnecessary or damaged cellular components. This catabolic recycling is essential for the control of cell growth and for tissue homeostasis; therefore, its dysregulation contributes to the pathogenesis of many diseases, including cancer [201]. A proteomic analysis revealed that the anti-cancer effects of L. acidophilus 606 were due to its cell-bound EPSs. In vitro, such EPSs inhibited the proliferation of colon cancer cells and activated autophagy mediated by beclin-1 and GRP78, and by regulating BCL-2 and BAK [123]. More recently, proautophagic and proapoptotic actions have also been attributed to L. plantarum EPSs. In CRC cells, EPSs suppress the mTOR pathway while augmenting the expression of autophagy markers such as AMPKa, an energy sensor that stimulates autophagy; ULK1, which initiates the autophagic process in response to starvation; and the LC3-II/I protein ratio, which increases with autophagosome formation [125]. These tumor-suppressive properties were also confirmed in vivo in a murine CRC model.

Some in vitro studies have demonstrated that Lactobacillus-derived metabolites can induce autophagy in cancer cells by interfering with WNT/β-catenin signaling, which is dysregulated in most colorectal cancers. In this pathway, β-catenin is the downstream effector that translocates into the nucleus to promote the transcription of genes involved in cell proliferation and motility [202]. In the complex cross-talk between the WNT pathway and autophagy, β-catenin suppresses autophagy in cancer cells [203]. CFS or sodium butyrate from L. plantarum OC01 promoted autophagy in 3D spheroids of colon cancer cells, which was associated with increased levels of autophagosomal and lysosomal markers, and decreased levels of free β-catenin [97]. The autophagy-promoting effect of butyrate was also observed in CRC cells bearing mutations in β-catenin and APC, i.e., a tumor suppressor involved in β-catenin activation [204]. Even in this genetic setting, butyrate causes autophagy-mediated degradation of β-catenin, thus resulting in the inhibition of cell growth. However, the proautophagic activity of postbiotics has not always been substantiated. For example, CFS from another L. plantarum strain reduced colon cancer cell viability, but this effect was accompanied by inhibited autophagy and downregulated expression of autophagy-related markers, including ATG proteins, beclin-1 and LC3-II [124]. Furthermore, the anticancer effect of CFSs was potentiated by chloroquine, a well-known autophagy inhibitor. These contrasting findings can be explained by the assumption that cancer cells may enhance autophagy to obtain energy and nutrients. Moreover, autophagy is a complex process, orchestrated by multiple signalling pathways, and its exact role in promoting or inhibiting cancer depends on the context and tumor stage, which has not yet been fully elucidated [205].

Immune response regulation

During carcinogenesis, neoplastic cells acquire the ability to escape from immunosurveillance, a feature that further contributes to tumor growth. This is accomplished by different mechanisms, e.g., within the tumor microenvironment, cancer and/or other noncancerous cells can release signals that deactivate immune cells, inducing them to become tumor tolerant or even tumor promoting. Moreover, alterations in the antigen processing machinery, immune-editing and/or downregulation of MHC class I molecules result in impaired antigen presentation on tumor cells, allowing them to escape T-cell recognition [206]. Probiotic LAB modulate the immune system in a strain-specific manner, affecting cellular, humoral and nonspecific immunity [207]. Gut commensal LAB induce dendritic cell (DC) maturation, activate and promote the proliferation and cytolytic activity of NK cells, and modulate cytokine secretion [208]. Depending on their concentration and time-space expression, cytokines can either inhibit or promote tumor development, e.g. by contributing to the chronic inflammation frequently underlying cancer pathogenesis [209]. Different gut-derived LAB arouse diverse DC-mediated NK cell responses through differential production of cytokines, such as IL-12, which promotes amplification of a type-1 response (desirable in infection and cancer) via stimulation of IFN-γ production [210].

Rodent cancer models are fundamental tools for understanding how probiotics enhance immune responsiveness to tumors. Various lactobacilli exhibit immunomodulatory properties in vitro and in vivo in CRC animal models. The administration of such probiotics to rodents bearing colon cancer was found to influence the tumor microenvironment, i.e., by decreasing leukocyte infiltration [130, 131] and proinflammatory cytokine expression, and by suppressing NF-κB and WNT signaling [130], resulting in overall anti-inflammatory and antiproliferative effects. Oral administration of milk-derived L. plantarum has been shown to exert a chemopreventive effect in mice with intestinal carcinoma, retarding cancer cell growth, increasing CD8 + and NK cell tumor infiltration, up-regulating IFN-γ, and promoting DC maturation to induce polarization of CD4 + T cells toward a Th1 response [104].

In a rat model of breast cancer, oral intake of the human gut commensal L. plantarum LS/07, alone or in combination with prebiotic inulin, before and after cancer induction, exerted preventive and curative effects, suppressed the frequency of carcinomas, reduced TNF-α serum levels and increased CD4(+) helper and CD8(+) cytotoxic T cells in the tumor tissue, thereby stimulating the local immune response [148]. These immunomodulatory activities were confirmed even in a different rat model of breast cancer [149].

Heat-killed L. plantarum (H-Lp) induced cytokine production in vitro in Peyer’s patch cells [159]. Moreover, its oral administration suppressed Meth-A tumors in mice, augmenting the IFN-γ level in splenocytes and the number of CD3(+)T cells in the peripheral blood. The authors hypothesized that H-Lp could activate macrophages and DCs in the Peyer’s patches, which trafficked to the spleen, acquiring anti-tumoral activity via the mesenteric lymph nodes, and then migrated to the tumor site [159].

The probiotic LGG can also modulate DC activity and maturation. Indeed, coculture of LGG with bone marrow-derived DCs promoted T-cell activation and increased TNF-α and IL-12 expression. Compared with DCs directly treated with LGG, DCs cocultured with neutrophils and activated with LGG produced higher IL-12p70 and TNF-α levels. Furthermore, LGG-treated DCs and neutrophils, cocultured with T cells, activated them to produce IFNγ. Interestingly, lower LGG doses resulted in stronger antitumor effects, as if DCs could undergo exhaustion at high LGG doses [158]. The anti-tumor and immunostimulating properties of LGG have also been confirmed in vivo. For example, in murine models of gut cancer, LGG administration decreased the tumor burden and augmented colonic CD8 + T cell responses, which depended on TLR-2-mediated DC activation [132]. Notably, as LGG is a human intestinal commensal, these findings support the association between the presence of probiotic lactobacilli in the gut and enhanced antitumor immune responses. Moreover, given these immunomodulatory capacities, probiotic lactobacilli can be considered for improving the efficacy of immunotherapy.

The same also applies to bifidobacteria. In murine melanoma models, oral administration of commensal bifidobacterial species improved tumor control to the same extent as monoclonal antibody-based immunotherapy [156]. Indeed, DC function was activated, inducing tumor-specific T cells and intratumoral CD8 + T-cell accumulation, which indicates favorable outcomes in patients with solid tumors. Furthermore, when probiotics are combined with immunotherapeutic drugs, tumor outgrowth is nearly abolished [156].

Immune response regulation by probiotic-derived molecules

Anticancer immunity can be enhanced by components of the bacterial cell envelope, such as EPSs and lipoteichoic acids (LTAs), which are considered key immunogenic compounds of probiotic LAB [211, 212]. Both types of molecules are displayed on the cell surface and can be released into the growth medium, resulting in being components of CFS or other postbiotic preparations. LTAs from LGG were shown to modulate the phenotype of lymph node-resident DCs and the homing of migratory DCs, reducing skin tumor development in mice [157]. Similarly, they activated DCs and T cells both in vitro and in the gut-associated lymphoid tissues of mice [213]. The stimulation of immune cells by LTA from LAB seems to be mediated by their interaction with TLR-2, lipopolysaccharide-binding protein, and CD14 [214], resulting in differential activation of MAPK signaling [211]. The sugars contained in EPS can be recognised by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including TLRs and C-type lectin receptors (CLRs) of DCs, leading to NF-κB activation and the modulation of cytokines that regulate a variety of adaptive and innate immune responses [212, 215].

SCFAs also have immunomodulatory properties, which can influence the tumor microenvironment. Indeed, SCFAs can regulate leukocyte activity in rodents [216], modulate cytokine pattern and suppress NF-κB signaling in human neutrophils [217], whereas, in monocyte-derived DCs, they can reduce the release of proinflammatory chemokines, with propionate and butyrate potentially modulating leukocyte trafficking and exerting a strong anti-inflammatory effects [218]. Moreover, SCFAs downregulate the production of proinflammatory effectors in intestinal macrophages, thereby contributing to hyporesponsiveness to commensal bacteria and tolerance to the intestinal microbiota [219]. The reprograming of cytokines/chemokines expression by SCFAs seems to depend on the activation of TLRs, HDAC inhibition and subsequent epigenetic changes [220]. SCFAs augment the population and function of colonic T reg cells [221]. In colon cancer cells, propionate was shown to upregulate the surface expression of MHC class I chain-related protein A and B (MICA/B), which can activate immune cells, thus enhancing immune surveillance against tumor, along with elimination of faulty cells [126]. Moreover, butyrate can strengthen innate immunity against tumor cells by upregulating TLR-4 expression and by promoting MAPK and NF-κB phosphorylation, as demonstrated in vitro, in colon cancer cells [128]. Accordingly, in a mouse model of CRC liver metastasis, butyrate supplementation decreased the metastatic capacity and improved the immune response by increasing the levels of natural killer T cells and T helper 17 cells [129]. Overall, these findings are consistent with observational human studies showing a positive correlation between the fecal SCFA concentration and treatment efficacy in patients undergoing immunotherapy [222].

Recent studies have confirmed that postbiotics can boost immunosurveillance against neoplastic cells. Ferrari et al. [134] described a molecular mechanism by which microbial metabolites may augment the clinical response to immunotherapy based on immune checkpoint inhibitors. Such therapy relies on the elimination of cancer cells by cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), which recognize abnormal cells through tumor-specific antigens presented on their surface by HLA class I proteins. As mentioned above, downregulation of HLA class I is an immune escape mechanism observed in several cancer types. The authors reported that L. paracasei phytosphingosine upregulates HLA class I expression in different cancer cells, thereby enhancing the exposure of tumor antigens and facilitating their recognition by CTLs. This observation was corroborated in vivo, where L. paracasei postbiotics combined with immunotherapy significantly attenuated tumor growth in both breast cancer and CRC murine models [134].

Modulation of cancer cell metabolism

Probiotics and postbiotics can exert indirect anticancer activities in the gut, i.e., by modulating the metabolism of the gut microbiota, reducing carcinogen levels via absorption and degradation, preventing DNA damage by fostering repair systems, and inactivating ROS via the secretion of antioxidant enzymes and molecules [223, 224]. Moreover, probiotics can impair several peculiar metabolic traits that support the survival and growth of cancer cells.

A well-established probiotic effect is the reduction of cancer-promoting enzymes, as well as the detoxification of putrefactive metabolites, which can originate in the gut from both human and bacterial metabolism [225–227]. Polyamines are essential for cell growth and DNA synthesis. Alterations of their intracellular levels have been associated with cancer and could represent a marker for neoplastic proliferation, all of which could lead to the targeting of polyamine metabolism via anticancer strategies [228]. In this context, the cytoplasmic fraction of LGG homogenates was shown to inhibit the proliferation of colonic adenocarcinoma and gastric cancer cells [168]. This antiproliferative effect was later attributed to the postbiotic capacity to affect polyamine metabolism, i.e., decreasing the amount of intracellular polyamine, and reducing the expression and activity of ornithine decarboxylase (essential for polyamine biosynthesis) while increasing those of spermidine/spermine N1-acetyltransferase (involved in polyamine catabolism) [142].

Owing to the Warburg effect, cancer cells obtain ATP primarily via glycolysis rather than through mitochondrial oxidation. Consequently, they produce energy with lower efficiency, but can more easily incorporate nutrients into new biomass [229]. Normal colonocytes metabolize butyrate to acetyl-CoA, which sustains histone acetyltransferase activity. In contrast, in cancerous colonocytes, butyrate accumulates, functioning as an HDAC inhibitor. Interestingly, although either condition increases chromatin acetylation, different target genes are modulated, leading to differential effects. Indeed, while butyrate stimulates the proliferation of normal colonocytes, it prevents that of cancerous colonocytes under the Warburg effect [135]. Moreover, a combined metabolomics-proteomics study revealed that butyrate can activate a glycolytic pyruvate kinase, thereby inducing a metabolic reprogramming that suppresses CRC cell proliferation [136].

Another metabolic characteristic of fast growing cancer cells is that they may exhibit specific nutrient auxotrophy. In fact, compared with normal cells, they have higher demands for some substrates. Consequently, the deprivation of essential or semiessential amino acids can starve them to death, with potential for cancer therapy. Accordingly, arginine depletion strategies have been explored in different tumor types, including CRC [230]. Reducing the dietary intake of arginine or treating with arginine-depleting enzymes, e.g. arginine deiminase (ADI), can restrain cancer [231]. In this context, ADIs from probiotic LAB were found to arrest the cell cycle and induce apoptosis in stomach cancer cells [143, 144], suggesting that this might contribute to the chemopreventive properties of commensal microbes in vivo, within the host gut. Accordingly, the cytostatic activity of CFS from a dairy Lactococcus lactis strain was attributed to its ADI enzymes, which are released into the growth medium. Indeed, L. lactis CFS increased the proportion of CRC cells blocked in the G0/G1 phase while decreasing the levels of c-MYC (a key cell cycle regulator) and phosphorylated P70 S6 kinase (an effector kinase of the mTOR complex 1, which is sensitive to the depletion of glucose and arginine) [101].

Reuterin, a fermentation byproduct of the gut commensal Limosilactobacillus reuteri, is gaining attention for its cancer-preventive effects [232]. The anticancer mechanism of reuterin was described in detail by Bell et al. [137]. Owing to its electrophilic nature, reuterin generates oxidative stress and specifically oxidizes proteins involved in ribosome biogenesis, which hampers protein synthesis causing the death of colon cancer cells [137]. This antitumor property was also confirmed in vivo, as the administration of reuterin-producing L. reuteri to mice inhibited CRC growth [137]. Interestingly, reuterin was effective against several types of cancer lines, whereas it did not kill normal colon epithelial cells. This selective cytotoxicity could depend on a stronger oxidative action on malignant cells because of their altered metabolism [233].

Alterations in Ca2+ homeostasis may occur in cancer cells [234]. Store-operated Ca2+ entry (SOCE) is a ubiquitous mechanism allowing the influx of calcium ions into the cytoplasm, where they mediate a variety of physiological processes. In CRC cells, SOCE and its key molecular components, i.e., ORAI1 and STIM1, are aberrantly enhanced, thus supporting distinctive cancer hallmarks, such as invasiveness and angiogenesis. Recently, heat-killed L. paracasei and L. plantarum were found to attenuate SOCE function and the in vitro migration of CRC cells. These postbiotics significantly impaired Ca2+ influx by downregulating ORAI1 and STIM1 expression. Intriguingly, this activity was selective for adenocarcinoma cells, as SOCE was unaffected in normal colonocytes [138].

Concluding remarks and future perspective

Owing to their beneficial impact on host health and given their therapeutic potential, probiotics have drawn notable medical interest. In addition to playing a role in complex conditions, probiotics have been attributed anticarcinogenic properties. The evidence from preclinical studies indicates that probiotics and postbiotics can suppress tumors; however, the precise mechanisms, host cell targets and probiotic molecules involved are not always straightforward. Most of the articles reviewed here attempt to describe the molecular basis underlying the effects of probiotics and postbiotics against CRC, followed by long-distance by those dealing with other types of malignancies. Consistently, the gastrointestinal tract is the main target of probiotic activities, although microbial postbiotics can cross the gut-vascular barrier and, through blood circulation, can influence even other distant organs.

Overall, probiotics and postbiotics affect multiple aspects of carcinogenesis, interfering with several key cellular hallmarks and dysregulated signaling pathways that specifically sustain the growth and development of tumors. The stimulation of apoptosis in cancerous cells is the most common process involved in the anticancer effects of probiotics and postbiotics. Furthermore, enhancing the antitumor immune response is a well-studied anticancer mechanism. Indeed, probiotics can act directly on the tumor microenvironment and promote the recognition of cancer cells or indirectly, i.e., by modulating the gut microbiota, which, in turn, contributes to shaping host immunity [235]. Accordingly, gut oncomicrobiome signatures have been identified on the basis of the taxon abundance in the intestinal microbiota of cancer patients with poor or good immunotherapy response [236].

In addition to targeting the mechanisms of carcinogenesis, probiotics can improve the efficacy of chemotherapy and mitigate the toxicity and detrimental side effects of conventional anticancer treatments. Moreover, probiotics have recently been explored for innovative cancer immunotherapy applications, which are based essentially on living drug systems and bionanotechnologies, and have culminated in the development of oral tumor vaccines, engineered probiotics and therapeutic nanoplatforms. For example, engineered L. plantarum strains were tested for their ability to generate live vector vaccines that might enhance the immune response against tumors [237]. Similarly, recombinant probiotics could act as living drug factories in bio-immunotherapy [238]. Such studies are the premises for advanced therapeutic applications that hold great promise for the medical use of probiotics and postbiotics in the future.

Despite such potential, the current clinical use of probiotics in cancer is rather limited. While human studies, including interventions and observational research, indicate that the regular assumption of probiotics can reduce the risk of developing different types of cancer [239–243], the majority of clinical trials has investigated the use of probiotics to manage the side effects of conventional anticancer therapies and/or improve quality of life in patients, e.g., as adjunctive treatments to counteract gut dysbiosis (with related gastrointestinal disorders) and attenuate immune suppression. In fact, the potentiality of these biotherapeutics is much greater, but far from being validated as anticancer therapies. Well-designed, large-scale human studies would help to fulfill such potential, however, the low number of clinical trials investigating probiotics in cancer patients is ethically understandable. To overcome this lack, further mechanistic studies and robust in vivo animal studies, shedding light on the anticancer action of probiotics, are essential for precise clinical trial design and safe and effective interventions.

Although the probiotics selected for human studies comply with established safety criteria, some potential risks and limitations in their use should be considered, especially in vulnerable populations, including immunocompromised patients, children and elderly, whose immune system may be impaired, not fully developed and weakened, respectively. In these people, the use of live probiotics should be carefully evaluated to avoid systemic infections or an excessive immune stimulation. Since existing data are discordant, extra and more precise patients’monitoring should be conducted in trials involving these populations, in order to better evaluate the occurrence of adverse events related to probiotic use [244]. Another critical aspect of probiotics is their intended use, which is crucial for establishing their efficacy and safety requirements. Indeed, the regulation of probiotic products varies significantly according to their intended use. Probiotics marketed as foods or dietary supplements are a category of products developed to support the diet of the generally healthy population, whereas probiotics intended to treat or prevent diseases should be regulated as biological medicinal agents [245]. Therefore, development priorities may shift toward dietary supplement pathways because of their shorter timelines and less stringent regulatory demands, albeit at the cost of limiting the therapeutic claims.

Finally, probiotics themselves cannot cure cancer, yet the identification of the microbial components endowed with anticancer activity could lead to precise anticancer formulations being used in clinical trials as novel drugs. Anyway, owing to the molecular complexity and heterogeneity of probiotics and postbiotics, multiple cellular pathways may be elicited, thus resulting in a synergistic antitumor effect, which is perhaps the main strength of these biological agents.
