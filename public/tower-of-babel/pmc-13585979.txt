Prevalence of neuropsychiatric and seizure disorders in neurofibromatosis type 1: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Elena Moreno-Charco, Carlos Pascual-Morena, Celia Álvarez-Bueno, Blanca Zuheros-Lara, Irene Martínez-García, Pilar Martínez-Sánchez, Miguel Contreras-Molina, Silvana Patiño-Cardona
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is associated with intellectual disability (ID), autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), emotional and seizure disorders. However, published prevalence estimates vary widely. The aim of this meta-analysis was to estimate the prevalence of ID, ASD, ADHD, epileptic seizures and emotional disorders in NF1. A systematic search was conducted in MEDLINE, Scopus and Web of Science from inception to April 2026. Studies that estimated the proportion of patients with these disorders were included. Where possible, genotype was considered, i.e., NF1 general versus NF1 microdeletions. Random-effects meta-analyses with their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were performed. Fifty studies were included. The prevalence of ID was 8% (95% CI 6–11, n = 17) in NF1 and 41% (95% CI 29–52, n = 2) in NF1 microdeletion; the prevalence of ASD was 11% (95% CI 7–14, n = 17) in NF1; the prevalence of ADHD was 33% (95% CI 26–40, n = 29) in NF1 and 45% (95% CI 25–65, n = 2) in NF1 microdeletion; the prevalence of seizures was 11% (95% CI 8–14, n = 13); the prevalence of epilepsy was 9% (95% CI 6–11, n = 16); and the prevalence of depression and anxiety was 13% (95% CI 3–23, n = 11) and 12% (95% CI 5–20, n = 10). NF1 is associated with neuropsychiatric and seizure disorders, reinforcing existing recommendations for neuropsychiatric screening in this population. Introduction

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is a multisystemic, autosomal dominant genetic disorder caused by germline mutations in the NF1 gene [1, 2]. This gene encodes neurofibromin, a key negative regulator of the RAS/MAPK signalling pathway [3, 4]. With an estimated incidence of one in 2500 to 3000 live births, NF1 is classified as a RASopathy and exhibits highly variable clinical expressivity [5, 6]. Loss of neurofibromin function leads to RAS hyperactivation, resulting in peripheral nerve sheath tumours, skeletal dysplasias and alterations to brain architecture [7, 8]. The most prevalent complications include neuropsychiatric disorders such as neurodevelopmental disorders, including intellectual disability (ID), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and mood disorders, including depression and anxiety, and seizure disorders including epilepsy [9–12]. Current clinical management is predominantly symptomatic, integrating pharmacological, psychopedagogical and surgical interventions aimed at alleviating neurological complications and improving patients’ quality of life [5, 11, 13].

Identifying these neuropsychiatric disorders is crucial, as they account for the majority of non-tumoral morbidity in the NF1 population. Dysregulation of the RAS/MAPK pathway affects cell growth and alters critical processes such as neuroplasticity, myelination and the excitatory–inhibitory balance in specific synaptic circuits. These pathophysiological mechanisms underlie the observed cognitive and behavioural deficits, which influence socio-cognitive development and academic performance from an early age [9, 10, 14]. Understanding the extent to which this genetic condition affects neurodevelopment is essential for designing early screening strategies and monitoring protocols that enable personalised intervention and optimise long-term functional outcomes [1, 13].

Despite the well-established association between NF1 and an increased risk of neuropsychiatric and seizure disorders, prevalence estimates reported in the scientific literature vary considerably, hindering consensus on the true burden of these conditions. This variability is commonly attributed to differences in assessment instruments, sample sizes and selection biases across the studied cohorts. Quantitative synthesis is therefore needed to provide reliable estimates for this population. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the prevalence of neurodevelopmental disorders (ID, ASD and ADHD), emotional disturbances (depression and anxiety) and seizure disorders in individuals with NF1.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis adhered to the Meta-Analyses of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, as well as the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [15–17]. The protocol was registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420261463533).

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus and Web of Science from inception to April 2026. The search terms followed the PEO (Population/Exposure/Outcome) framework, including terms related to neurofibromatosis type 1 as the population/exposure, and terms related to neurodevelopmental disorders (ID, ASD and ADHD), emotional disorders (depression and anxiety) and seizure disorders as outcomes. A complementary search was also conducted in Google Scholar, OpenGrey and the Networked Digital Library of Theses and Dissertations. Where full texts were unavailable, attempts were made to contact the corresponding authors. Finally, the reference lists of included articles and previous reviews were also screened. The search strategy is detailed in Appendix S1.

The systematic search was performed independently by two authors (EM-C and CP-M), and disagreements were resolved by consensus or by a third author (IM-G).

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Population/Exposure: individuals with a clinical or genetic diagnosis of NF1; (2) Outcomes: prevalence of neuropsychiatric disorders, including neurodevelopmental disorders (ID, ASD and ADHD) and emotional disorders (depression and/or anxiety symptomatology), and seizure disorders (seizures and epilepsy). ID was operationalised as defined in each primary study. In the current classifications (DSM-5-TR and ICD-11), the intellectual criteria correspond to a performance level of approximately two standard deviations (SD) below the population mean (full-scale IQ below 70). This level was historically used to define the now obsolete term ‘mental retardation’; these labels were therefore mapped onto a single endpoint. As most of the primary studies predated the DSM-5/ICD-11 reformulation, and did not systematically assess adaptive functioning or age of onset, the pooled estimate reflects ID as ascertained in the source studies (predominantly an IQ below 70), rather than a formal DSM-5-TR/ICD-11 diagnosis [18–20]; (3) Design: observational studies (e.g., longitudinal, cross-sectional and case–control); (4) Language: no restrictions were applied.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Population: studies including samples with other syndromes where NF1 data could not be isolated; (2) Outcomes: studies lacking sufficient data to calculate the prevalence of the outcomes of interest.

Study selection was performed independently by two authors (EM-C and CP-M), and disagreements were resolved by consensus or by a third author (IM-G).

Data extraction

An ad hoc data extraction table was created, including the following variables: (1) reference (author and year); (2) country; (3) study design (prospective, retrospective, cross-sectional or case–control); (4) sample characteristics (sample size, age and percentage of females); (5) NF1 diagnosis (clinical or genetic); and (6) outcomes and the instruments used to measure them.

Data extraction was performed independently by two authors (EM-C and CP-M), and disagreements were resolved by consensus or by a third author (SP-C).

Quality assessment of studies

The quality of the studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Studies Reporting Prevalence Data [21]. This tool evaluates studies based on nine criteria relating to sampling, measurement, and statistical reporting. Each item was rated as ‘met’, ‘not met’ or ‘unknow’, and the overall quality was classified according to the number of items met: ‘high quality’ for equal or more than seven items, ‘moderate quality’ for five to six items, and ‘low quality’ for fewer than five items.

Quality assessment was performed independently by two authors (EM-C and CP-M), and disagreements were resolved by consensus or by a third author (SP-C).

Statistical analyses

A narrative synthesis was conducted and the results were summarised in an ad hoc table. Results were expressed as proportions ranging from 0 to 1 (i.e., 0% to 100%), with their 95% confidence interval (CI), applicable to both systematic review and meta-analyses.

Where two or more studies were available, random-effects meta-analyses were performed using the Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman model [22]. This main model was used with an untransformed (raw) scale, and the between-study variance (τ2) was estimated. Study-level 95% confidence intervals were computed using the Wald method. Unlike the classic metaprop command, in which zero events were not meta-analysable, in current versions of Stata, which use the commands meta esize d n, random (sjonkman) studylabel (Reference) (in this study) and meta forestplot, Stata assigns a subject with an event in order to be meta-analysed. When two or more reports were suspected of describing the same cohort (i.e. if they shared investigators or a recruitment centre, had a coincident recruitment period, or had matching baseline and outcome data), only one estimate per outcome (i.e. the most recent or most complete) was entered into the corresponding meta-analysis. Non-overlapping outcomes from each report were retained.

Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, categorised as not important (< 30%), moderate (30%–50%), substantial (50%–75%) or considerable (> 75%) [17, 23]. Given the large, combined sample sizes, heterogeneity was interpreted primarily through the magnitude of I2 and τ2 rather than through the statistical significance of the heterogeneity test. In addition, 95% prediction intervals were calculated for outcomes with at least three studies to describe the expected range of prevalence in a future comparable study. Meta-analyses were separated a priori into two subgroups: studies including individuals with NF1 in general, and studies including exclusively participants with NF1 microdeletions.

Secondary analyses included visual assessment of funnel plots and the Egger test to explore small-study effects. Given the mathematical dependence between the mean and the variance of a proportion on the untransformed scale, the test was computed on this scale acknowledging that asymmetry may reflect this artefact, as well as between-study heterogeneity or methodological differences, rather than publication bias per se [24]. Funnel-plot asymmetry was considered present at p < 0.10. As reliable assessment of small-study effects generally requires at least ten studies, results for outcomes below this threshold were regarded as exploratory. The trim-and-fill method was applied as an exploratory sensitivity analysis to examine the potential influence of asymmetry on the pooled estimate; its output was not interpreted as a bias-corrected prevalence [25]. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding each study individually to assess whether any single study exerted a disproportionate influence on the magnitude of the pooled estimate or on heterogeneity. Galbraith plots were used to identify outlier studies by plotting the standardised effect size against estimated precision [26].

A reanalysis of the main meta-analyses was also done using the Freeman-Tukey double-arcsine transformation, with back-transformation based on the harmonic mean of the study sample sizes [27]. Meta-regressions were performed with age and percentage of females as covariates. In addition, subgroup analyses were performed according to the age of the study participants, the diagnosis of comorbidities (i.e., interviews/assessment, self-reported, screening scales, and records/registries), the design of the studies (prospective vs. non-prospectives), and the quality of the studies (high quality vs. non-high quality studies).

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata SE, version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

Overall, 4932 records were identified (4928 from database searches across PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science, and 4 from other sources), of which 2635 (2631 from PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, and 4 from other sources) remained after removal of 2297 duplicates (all from PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science). Of these, 59 reports were sought for retrieval (55 from PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, and 4 from other sources), all of which were successfully obtained. Following full-text assessment, 9 reports were excluded for the following reasons: design not of interest (n = 5), outcome not of interest (n = 3) and population not of interest (n = 1) (all from PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science) (Supplementary Table S1). Fifty studies were ultimately included in both the systematic review and the meta-analyses (46 from PubMed, Scopus and Web of Science, and 4 from other sources) (Fig. 1) [28–77], although one study was excluded from the meta-analyses of ASD and seizure disorders due to a total overlap of cohorts (Supplementary Table S2) [56].

Characteristics of the included studies

The included studies spanned four continents, with investigations conducted in the Americas (United States, Canada and Argentina), Europe (United Kingdom, France, Denmark, Netherlands, Germany, Serbia, Italy and Belgium), Asia (Turkey and Saudi Arabia) and Oceania (Australia and New Zealand). In terms of design, 29 studies used a cross-sectional approach or collected data cross-sectionally, 14 were retrospective, 5 prospective and 2 had mixed designs. Sample sizes ranged from 24 to 8579 participants. The analysed populations showed an approximately equal sex distribution (close to a 1:1 ratio) and mean ages ranging from 4.5 to 43.3 years. Studies included samples with a clinical or genetic diagnosis of NF1, with a priori subgroup classifications for participants with general NF1 manifestations or specific genetic variants, namely microdeletions. Regarding neurodevelopmental disorders, the prevalence of ID ranged from 0.03 to 0.42 across the included studies, with the highest values observed exclusively in patients with NF1 gene microdeletions. For ASD, prevalence estimates ranged from 0.02 to 0.26 in the general NF1 population. For ADHD, prevalence in the general NF1 population ranged from 0.03 to 0.72, whilst in the microdeletion subgroup estimates ranged from 0.33 to 0.55.

Regarding psychiatric and emotional manifestations, the prevalence of depression ranged from 0.01 to 0.55, and that of anxiety from 0.02 to 0.38. Studies reporting combined depression and/or anxiety yielded prevalence estimates ranging from 0.02 to 0.36. Finally, with respect to seizure disorders, both seizures and epilepsy showed prevalence estimates ranging from 0.03 to 0.21 across the evaluated samples.

Quality assessment of studies

Using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Studies Reporting Prevalence Data, 43 studies (86%) were rated as high quality and 7 (14%) as moderate quality, with no study rated as low quality. The checklist items least frequently satisfied were the adequacy of the sample size and the adequacy of the response rate, the latter often unreported, followed by insufficient coverage of the sample in the data analysis and incomplete description of the study subjects and setting (Supplementary Table S5).

Meta-analyses

The meta-analysis yielded a pooled prevalence of 0.08 (95% CI 0.06–0.11; I2 = 80.06%) for ID, 0.11 (95% CI 0.07–0.14; I2 = 91.71%) for ASD and 0.33 (95% CI 0.26–0.40; I2 = 97.31%) for ADHD. For ID, the prevalence estimate in the NF1 microdeletion subgroup was higher, at 0.41 (95% CI 0.29–0.52; I2 = 0.29%) (Figs. 2, 3, 4).

Pooled prevalence estimates for depression, anxiety and combined depression/anxiety were 0.13 (95% CI 0.03–0.23; I2 = 99.08%), 0.12 (95% CI 0.05–0.20; I2 = 95.72%) and 0.17 (95% CI 0.07–0.26; I2 = 94.06%), respectively (Supplementary Fig S1). For seizure disorders, pooled prevalence estimates were 0.11 for seizures (95% CI 0.08–0.14; I2 = 69.00%) and 0.09 for epilepsy (95% CI 0.06–0.11; I2 = 91.05%) (Supplementary Fig S2).

Secondary analyses

Funnel plots revealed asymmetry consistent with small-study effects, as determined by the Egger test, for the following conditions: ASD (p = 0.023, n = 17), ADHD (p < 0.001, n = 29), anxiety (p < 0.001, n = 10), depression/anxiety (p = 0.001, n = 7), seizures (p = 0.035, n = 13), and epilepsy (p = 0.002, n = 16). As an exploratory sensitivity analysis, the trim-and-fill method yielded the following adjusted estimates: ASD (0.07; 95% CI 0.02–0.11); ADHD (0.27; 95% CI 0.19–0.34); seizures (0.10; 95% CI 0.07–0.13); and epilepsy (0.07; 95% CI 0.04–0.09). These estimates should be interpreted as sensitivity analyses rather than as bias-corrected prevalence estimates (Supplementary Table S6and Supplementary Fig S3).

Pooled estimates were consistent between the untransformed (raw) and Freeman-Tukey double-arcsine analyses across all outcomes, with a slight reduction in I2 under the transformed scale. Between-study variance (τ2) and 95% prediction intervals for each outcome are presented in Supplementary Table S7. Prediction intervals were wide for most outcomes, particularly for ADHD (0.02–0.73), depression (0.00–0.63), combined depression/anxiety (0.00–0.59) and anxiety (0.00–0.49), indicating that the prevalence observed in a future comparable study could span a broad range. Narrower intervals were obtained for ID (0.02–0.19), seizures (0.03–0.22) and epilepsy (0.01–0.21). Prediction intervals could not be estimated for the two microdeletion subgroups, which comprised fewer than three studies (Supplementary Table S7).

Meta-regression identified a positive association between the percentage of females in the cohorts and the prevalence of depression (p = 0.018). In addition, the subgroup analysis showed a trend towards a lower prevalence of ASD, ADHD, and anxiety through the use of records/registries, while there was a trend towards a higher prevalence of depression with the use of screening scales (Supplementary Table S7).

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that sequential exclusion of individual studies did not materially alter the magnitude of any pooled prevalence estimate or its heterogeneity (Supplementary Fig S4). Finally, Galbraith plots identified the following studies as potential outliers: Coutinho V et al. (2016) for ID, Serur Y et al. (2025) for ADHD and Boulanger JM et al. (2005) for seizures (Supplementary Fig S5).

Discussion

Main findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the prevalence of several neuropsychiatric disorders in the NF1 population, including ID, ASD, ADHD and emotional disorders, and seizure disorders. Our results indicate a prevalence of 8% for ID (41% in the NF1 microdeletion subgroup), 11% for ASD, 33% for ADHD (45% in the microdeletion subgroup), 9%–11% for seizures and epilepsy, and 12%–13% for anxiety and depression symptoms. Furthermore, meta-regression identified a positive association between the proportion of females in the cohort and the prevalence of depression.

Interpretation

The pooled prevalence of ID was 8%, with considerable heterogeneity, individual point estimates ranging from 3 to 23% and a pooled confidence interval of 6%–11%. Despite this variability, all figures were higher than those observed in the general population: 0.5% in adults and 1.8% in children and adolescents [78]. The two studies with the lowest prevalence estimates present methodological limitations that favour underestimation, in addition to relatively small sample sizes [60, 62]. One of them had a particularly low proportion of ID due to the study’s objective (diagnosis of specific learning disorders), the small sample size, and the inclusion of a participant with an unusually high IQ (104.6) [60]. The other study (3%) did not administer psychometric intelligence tests, but instead obtained ID data exclusively through parental report of previous diagnoses, a method that tends to underestimate true prevalence; the authors themselves, however, do not consider their figure anomalous, placing it at the lower end of the 4%–8% range reported in the literature [62]. At the opposite extreme, the 23% prevalence reported in is accounted for by the absence of neurological exclusion criteria (the sample included patients with central nervous system tumours, optic pathway gliomas and epilepsy) and by the bias inherent to recruitment at a highly specialised tertiary referral centre (Hôpital Pédiatrique Trousseau, Paris), which concentrates cases of greatest clinical complexity [36].

Secondly, the genotypic composition of the cohorts may have influenced the risk of ID. Two studies specifically including patients with NF1 microdeletions reported ID prevalences of 42% and 38%, respectively, figures substantially higher than those of the remaining cohorts [44, 75]. This difference has a well-characterised biological basis: whereas the majority of patients with NF1 harbour point mutations affecting exclusively the NF1 gene, NF1 microdeletions involve the simultaneous loss of up to 14 functional genes in chromosomal region 17q11.2, generating extensive haploinsufficiency with disproportionate cognitive impact [40, 76]. Among the co-deleted genes, two have been specifically implicated in the intellectual phenotype: OMG, which encodes an oligodendrocyte myelin glycoprotein involved in the regulation of synaptic plasticity [79], and RNF135, an E3 ubiquitin ligase whose deficiency is associated with ID and neurodevelopmental deficits. The co-deletion of both genes may explain the higher prevalence of ID observed in microdeletion carriers, compared to the general NF1 population. This could also account for the elevated pooled estimates when this genotype is included in cohorts [8, 75, 80–82].

The pooled prevalence of ASD in the NF1 population was 11%, higher than the estimate for the general population (0.76%) [83], although with considerable heterogeneity (range of estimates: 2%–26%). In addition, comparability with estimates in the general population is limited. The variability in out meta-analysis reflects substantial methodological differences across studies. Studies with lower estimates present limitations leading to systematic underestimation: retrospective designs based on electronic records without active case-finding [32], screening with tools of limited sensitivity and low tolerability in children with severe NF1 [36], or interpretation of the autistic profile as a statistical artefact of ADHD and internalising symptomatology [57]. Conversely, studies with higher estimates (25%–26%) employed two-stage prospective methodology with gold-standard diagnostic instruments (ADI-R, ADOS) and accounted for diagnostic masking arising from attributing social difficulties to NF1 per se or to ADHD [42, 63].

The pooled prevalence of ADHD was 33%, with a range of 3%–72% in our systematic review, likewise higher than the general population estimate (3.4%) [84]. In the NF1 microdeletion subgroup, estimates of ASD and ADHD were based on only one and two studies, respectively. These results should therefore be regarded as exploratory descriptive figures. While these estimates did not appear to be higher than those in non-deletion NF1, the small number of studies and wide confidence intervals mean that it is not possible to infer equivalence. No adequately powered comparison between subgroups was possible. Whether contiguous-gene deletion modifies the attentional or autistic burden, as it does for ID, therefore remains unresolved and cannot be established from the present data.

With regard to emotional disorders, the pooled prevalence of depression was 13%, ranging from 1 to 55% across studies, with considerable heterogeneity. The highest value was reported by a study that used diagnosis via the CES-D, a method that tends to overestimate prevalence relative to clinical assessment [35]. Similarly, a prevalence of 35% was reported based on the CBCL [60]. The pooled prevalence of anxiety was 12%, with estimates ranging from 2 to 38%. The highest values were associated with instruments such as the semi-structured, DSM-5-based KSADS-5 interview and the CBCL [60, 68]. Conversely, the lowest values were derived from retrospective sources with limited sensitivity, such as clinical records or ICD-8/10 codes [44, 47]. This considerable heterogeneity therefore reflects the disparity between screening tools and standardised clinical diagnoses.

Furthermore, meta-regression analysis revealed that the percentage of females in the sample was a statistically significant moderator (p = 0.018). For every 1% increase in the proportion of females, the prevalence of depression increased by approximately 1.1% (coefficient = 0.011). This finding is consistent with the previously reported sex differences in depression [85]. One possible explanation is that the higher prevalence of depression in females is due to a combination of biological (e.g. hormonal fluctuations and greater vulnerability of mood-regulating circuits), psychological (e.g. greater rumination and lower self-esteem) and social (e.g. greater exposure to adversity and caregiving burden) factors. These mechanisms, which have been widely described in relation to the general population, may also contribute to the higher prevalence of depression observed in females with NF1 [86].

The pooled prevalence of seizure disorders was 11% for seizures and 9% for epilepsy, with individual study ranges of 3% to 21%. These prevalences were higher than in the general population, where active epilepsy affects approximately 0.64% of people at any given time and 0.76% of people over their lifetime [87]. However, direct comparison is limited as these external figures derive from populations with different age distributions, diagnostic definitions and assessment methods. Moreover, our estimates were statistically comparable to the 5.4% lifetime epilepsy reported by a 2020 meta-analysis and the 8.1% reported by another 2024 meta-analysis. Although our estimates tended to be higher, the confidence intervals overlapped, so statistically significant differences cannot be confirmed [88, 89].

Some of the observed heterogeneity may be explained by the recruitment setting: tertiary care centres tend to report higher prevalence rates due to the concentration of patients presenting with more severe neurological symptoms. Age of the cohort also has an influence, as cumulative prevalence tends to be higher in adult samples than in paediatric cohorts, given the longer time available for diagnosis to accrue. However, this could not be confirmed [43]. Furthermore, changes to the ILAE diagnostic criteria and the use of more sensitive tests, such as video EEG compared to conventional EEG, affect case detection. The highest value was reported in a study conducted at a tertiary-level centre. This can be explained by the potential selection bias inherent to such settings, the high tumour burden of the cohort and the fact that it was a paediatric neurology clinic, to which cases with pre-existing neurological problems are preferentially referred [33].

The subgroup analyses according to the diagnostic method provides a coherent interpretation of some of the observed heterogeneity. The lower prevalence of ASD, ADHD, and anxiety obtained from administrative records is expected, since these sources only capture cases that access specialized care and are coded, thus filtering out mild, subthreshold, or primary care-managed cases, and predictably underestimating the true prevalence. This estimate should therefore be interpreted as a conservative lower bound. Conversely, in depression, screening scales tend to overestimate prevalence, a pattern consistent with general methodological evidence: the cut-off points of these instruments are calibrated to maximize sensitivity, so the false-positive rate is proportionally higher the lower the baseline prevalence of the disorder. This applies to depression in NF1 and not to outcomes with a higher baseline rate, such as ADHD or autistic traits [90].

As shown above, the prevalence of these disorders tends to be higher than published general-population estimates, although these are not directly comparable. Therefore, it is necessary to address the genetic and molecular mechanisms underlying this excess risk. The increased risk of ID, ASD, ADHD and seizure disorders lies in the aetiology of NF1 itself, which is caused by germline mutations in the NF1 gene. This gene encodes neurofibromin, a key negative regulator of the RAS/MAPK signalling pathway [91]. The loss of neurofibromin function results in RAS hyperactivation, which could alter brain architecture and cell growth. In the nervous system, the dysregulation of the RAS/MAPK pathway affects critical processes such as neuroplasticity and myelination. Crucially, in relation to seizure disorders, it also affects the excitatory–inhibitory balance in specific synaptic circuits, which may be the underlying cause of an individual’s susceptibility to seizures. However, this molecular mechanism does not account for the full clinical picture. NF1-associated epilepsy is heterogeneous and frequently secondary to demonstrable structural pathology, including brain tumours, mesial temporal sclerosis, and cortical malformations. The latter have been identified as independent predictors of persistent, drug-resistant seizures [89]. Therefore, intrinsic molecular and clinically demonstrable structural causes should be regarded as distinct contributors. This underlying imbalance interferes with development from an early age, causing cognitive and behavioural deficits [92].

With regard to disorder specificity, existing genotype–phenotype studies suggest that the attentional and autistic burden may largely be driven by haploinsufficiency of the NF1 gene itself [93]. However, our data do not allow us to establish this, and the contribution of contiguous-gene effects to these specific phenotypes remains unclear. Conversely, the higher risk of ID observed in certain patients could be explained by a broader genetic mechanism. NF1 microdeletions involve the simultaneous loss of up to 14 contiguous functional genes, including OMG, which is involved in the regulation of synaptic plasticity. Another gene affected by NF1 microdeletions is RNF135, an E3 ubiquitin ligase deficiency of which is associated with ID and profound neurodevelopmental deficits [94, 95].

These findings have implications for both clinical practice and research. From a clinical perspective, the high prevalence of ID quantifies a burden that reinforces existing NF1 surveillance recommendations, such as the developmental and neuropsychiatric assessment already advised by current guidelines [2, 96]. Furthermore, this is in line with other RASopathies [97]. This is because early detection enables timely intervention and improves patients’ quality of life. From a research perspective, larger studies that integrate molecular and genetic data alongside clinical variables are needed to allow more accurate genotype–phenotype associations to be established for neuropsychiatric outcomes. While individual estimates suggest that ADHD prevalence may be higher in the NF1 microdeletion subgroup (33%−55%) than in the general NF1 population (3%−72%), the small number of studies focusing specifically on this subgroup prevents firm conclusions from being drawn. Therefore, future research should specifically address the neuropsychiatric profile of patients with microdeletions, as this subgroup may be at differential risk not only of ADHD, but also of the other disorders addressed in this review. However, prevalence estimates alone do not determine the optimal screening instrument, interval, cost-effectiveness or treatment benefit; these require dedicated studies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The majority of the included studies had an observational design (cross-sectional, retrospective or cohort), which precludes the establishment of definitive causal or temporal relationships between NF1 and the evaluated disorders, due to the potential influence of uncontrolled confounding factors in such designs. In addition, several of the included studies had a small sample size (in some cases fewer than 30–50 participants). However, this limitation is largely inherent to the rare disease status of NF1, which hinders the recruitment of large samples. As a meta-analysis of prevalence from a single arm, this study does not provide a matched comparison with the general population. The differences described are contextual rather than estimates of relative risk. Furthermore, considerable variability was observed across studies in terms of geographical origin, culture, healthcare systems, and participant age. These factors may have contributed to the overall heterogeneity of the estimates. Although meta-regression allowed partial control for the effects of age and sex, it was not possible to adjust for the remaining contextual variables. Furthermore, the diagnostic tools used, particularly for ID, varied across studies. In some cases, they relied on self-report or parental report rather than standardised psychometric assessment. This is a particularly relevant limitation as it introduces a greater risk of under- or overestimating true prevalence. In addition, as the studies included span several decades, the diagnostic criteria for the assessed disorders, specially ID, have changed substantially. The general-population reference estimates against which our results are contextualised have also evolved during this period. This introduces intrinsic diagnostic variability across studies and limits the temporal comparability of the pooled estimates. Moreover, the outcomes were ascertained using different methods across the studies. These methods ranged from standardised diagnostic interviews to screening scales, administrative registries, and self- or parent-report. As these approaches could not capture identical constructs, the pooled estimates should be read as summaries of clinically heterogeneous evidence rather than as single diagnostic prevalences. For example, screening scales tend to overestimate prevalence, particularly for depression, whereas registry-based data tend to underestimate it. In addition, despite a systematic cohort-level overlap assessment, some degree of partial participant overlap between studies drawing on shared registers or consortia may remain that could not be identified from the published reports. Consistent with this, there was high between-study heterogeneity for most outcomes, and the corresponding prediction intervals were wide. This indicates that the prevalence expected in a future, comparable study may vary substantially, and that the pooled point estimates should be interpreted with caution. Finally, funnel-plot asymmetry suggestive of small-study effects was observed for several outcomes. This should not be equated with publication bias, as it may also stem from heterogeneity or methodological differences. For outcomes with fewer than ten studies, these analyses were exploratory.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis assessed the prevalence of the main neuropsychiatric and seizure disorders (ID, ASD, ADHD, emotional disoders and seizure disorders) in the NF1 population. The results showed a prevalence of 8% for ID (41% in the microdeletion subgroup), 11% for ASD, 33% for ADHD (45% in the microdeletion subgroup), 9–11% for seizures and epilepsy, and 12–13% for anxiety and depressive symptoms. These prevalence rates were higher than published general-population estimates, although the latter derive from populations with different age distributions, diagnostic definitions and ascertainment methods and are therefore not directly comparable. Furthermore, meta-regression data suggest that a higher proportion of females in the sample is associated with an increased risk of depression. These findings quantify the neuropsychiatric burden in NF1 patients and support existing surveillance recommendations, particularly with regard to ID, while emphasising the need to improve our understanding of the specific neuropsychiatric phenotype of patients with NF1 microdeletions. Taken together, these results reinforce the need for a multidisciplinary approach to managing NF1, with a focus on the early detection and personalised treatment of its neuropsychiatric complications. However, the considerable heterogeneity across studies and the noted methodological limitations highlight the need for new, larger prospective studies.
