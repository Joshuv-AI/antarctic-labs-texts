Two‐Handed Knot Tying Technique for Arthroscopic Surgeries
Authors: Chuan Zhang, Ming‐Ming Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKnot tying is a widely used and essential technique in arthroscopic tissue repair and fixation. Although it shows perceived simplicity, its technical complexity exceeds that of open surgery due to the challenges of suture fixation within confined intra‐articular spaces and demanding execution requirements. Current literature predominantly focuses on variations in knot configurations, yet few studies address the technical description of arthroscopic knot tying execution details—despite their status as foundational components. This article describes an arthroscopic 2‐handed knot tying technique applicable not only for arthroscopic tissue repair but also for deep tissue repairs during open surgical procedures. In this knot tying process, the combination of a knot pusher with the surgeon's hand holding it allows for a shorter stroke, akin to those found in a compact engine piston stroke. Coordination of the surgeon's both hands leads to a higher efficiency and fast tying processes.

Although knotless suture anchors have gained increasing clinical adoption, arthroscopic knot‐tying remains a fundamental and indispensable skill in orthopaedic surgery. 1 , 2 Proper knot‐tying technique is essential for achieving optimal tissue apposition during arthroscopic procedures. 3 , 4 Multiple arthroscopic sliding knot techniques have been described, each presenting unique benefits and drawbacks. 1 , 5 , 6

The optimal knot tying technique should be easy to apply, be reproducible, and allow the knot should to slide easily through arthroscopic cannulas or portals. The lock and initial security should be reliable, with the knot profile as small as possible. 7 Current research predominantly examines knot design variations, with limited emphasis on efficient intraoperative execution. 8 , 9 We describe an arthroscopic knot tying technique that offers clinical advantages: a shorter knot pusher stroke; coordination of both hands tying to save the operative time; improved technical simplicity; and enhanced reproducibility.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

The surgical technique is shown in Video 1.

The 2 strands retrieved from the cannula are pulled to confirm whether they can slide; the strand close to the surgeon is designated as the post strand and is threaded through the knot pusher, while the other strand is referred to as the loop strand and held in the right hand. The post strand is wrapped around the left ring finger and index finger, with its end clamped using a hemostat. Any tangles between strands and twists of the post strand on the knot pusher are removed. The post strand is elevated rightward along with the shaft of the knot pusher to maintain tension using the left index finger. The left middle finger is left free. The left middle finger hooks the loop strand to the left to establish a crossed over configuration. The right hand routes the loop strand in a clockwise direction around both the pusher shaft and post strand, directing it toward the lower‐left quadrant of the crossed loop while aligning it along the dorsal surface of the hooked tip of the left middle finger. The left middle finger then lifts the loop strand dorsally through this crossed loop to transfer it to the right hand. The cross loop slides down to the cannula; simultaneously, the left hand withdraws the pusher slightly just out of the cannula. Following pusher readvancement the cross loop is worked down onto the tissue. This underhand half‐hitch is repeated twice. The fourth inverted overhand half‐hitch is thrown. The left ring finger positions the loop strand posteriorly and to the left side of the post strand and the pusher shaft, resulting in a crossed configuration. Subsequently, the right hand maneuvers the loop strand in an anticlockwise direction around both the pusher and post strand, positioning it within the upper‐left quadrant of the crossover loop. The tip of the middle finger then guides the loop strand through the crossing loop posteriorly and transfers it to the right hand. The pusher is partially withdrawn just out of the cannula, while the right hand slips the throw down to the cannula and over the tip of the knot pusher. The knot pusher is readvanced to push this hitch to the knot stack. Past‐pointing technique and looping the pusher tip around the loop strand is applied to secure the hitches. Finally, more alternating throws are performed to complete the knot. The 2 strands are cut and the knot is checked for its security. This is the knot tying process in surgery. Strand Identification

The 2 strands of the single suture are retrieved from either the same cannula or a single soft tissue portal. The suture strands are pulled to confirm whether the suture can slide or not, the tying technique is most applicable for a nonsliding suture (Figure 1A,B). The suture strand close to the left hand or the surgeon is designated as the post strand, and the other strand is referred to as the loop strand (Figure 1C).

Preparation for the arthroscopic two‐handed knot‐tying technique. (A,B) A single No. 5 suture (half white, half black) is wrapped around two rubber tubes. Both limbs are then retrieved out of the cannula and pulled to confirm whether it can slide. (C) The white strand, which is close to the left hand or the surgeon is designated as the post strand and threaded onto the knot pusher. (D) The post strand is secured by wrapping it around the surgeon's ring finger and index finger, with its end clamped using a hemostat; simultaneously, the knot pusher is advanced downward and held in position by the left thumb. Any tangles between the two strands are resolved prior to tying the knot.

A hemostat is applied to the post strand that has been threaded through the knot pusher; subsequently, the knot pusher is advanced down along this post strand to confirm that no tangles between strands exist, and any twist of the post strand on the knot pusher is removed. Meanwhile control over both the hemostat and post strand (which wraps around both the ring finger and index finger) is maintained by the left hand (Figure 1D). Following this, the post strand is elevated rightward along with the pusher shaft to maintain tension using the left index finger, while the pusher is stabilized by the left thumb through the pusher ring. Throughout this entire knot‐tying process, it is essential for the post strand to remain aligned with the pusher shaft (Figure 2A). The loop strand is held by the right hand to maintain proper tension during knot formation. The left middle finger remains free of any sutures, allowing for coordination with the right hand to properly handle the loop strand.

Knot‐Tying

Underhand Loop

The left middle finger hooks the loop strand to the left, positioning it anterior to the post strand and the knot pusher, thereby establishing a crossed over configuration with the post strand and the knot pusher shaft (Figure 2B). The right hand subsequently routes the loop strand in a clockwise direction around both the pusher shaft and post strand, directing it toward the lower‐left quadrant of the crossover loop while aligning it along the dorsal surface of the hooked tip of the left middle finger (Figure 2C). The left middle finger then lifts the loop strand dorsally through this crossed loop to transfer it to the right hand (Figure 2D,E).

Controlled tension is applied by the right hand on the loop strand to facilitate slipping of this crossed loop over the tip of the pusher, simultaneously, the left hand withdraws the pusher slightly just out of the portal or cannula (Figure 2F,G). Following pusher readvancement, the cross loop is worked down to the repaired tissue, and the past‐pointing technique is employed to complete a secure underhand half‐hitch using a push‐pull methodology (Figure 2H,I).

This underhand knot tying procedure is repeated twice in the same way to complete a 3‐hitch friction knot (Figure 2J,K). During the advancement of the knot pusher on the crossed loop, the 2 strands should not be over tensioned to impair the prior hitch.

Overhand Loop

To establish a locking mechanism, after the 3‐hitch base knot set, the fourth inverted half‐hitch is thrown. The left ring finger positions the loop strand posteriorly and to the left side of the post strand and pusher shaft, resulting in a crossed configuration between the loop strand, post strand, and the knot pusher shaft (Figure 3A,B). Subsequently, the right hand maneuvers the loop strand in an anticlockwise direction around both the pusher and post strand, positioning it in the upper‐left quadrant of the crossover loop (Figure 3C‐E). Then the loop strand is guided by the tip of the middle finger through the crossing loop posteriorly, and it is transferred to the right hand (Figure 3F,G).

Step‐by‐step sequence for tying the arthroscopic overhand hitch. (A,B) With the knot pusher maintained on the knot stack to keep the post strand under tension, the left ring finger positions the loop strand posteriorly and to the left of the post strand and pusher shaft, thereby creating a crossed configuration. (C) The right hand maneuvers the loop strand in a counterclockwise direction around both the knot pusher and post strand, directing it toward the upper‐left quadrant of the crossover loop. (D‐H) The tip of the left middle finger then guides the loop strand posteriorly through the center of the crossing loop. The strand is subsequently transferred to the right hand, completing the formation of an overhand half‐hitch. (I,J) The knot pusher is partially withdrawn just out of the cannula while the right hand slips the throw over the tip of the pusher onto the portal. (K) With the loop strand converging to the post strand, the knot pusher is readvanced to push the hitch onto the knot stack. (L) The post strand is looped anticlockwise around the loop strand to the left, and past‐pointing technique is applied, so that the post strand is flipped to secure the overhand hitch.

Coordinated movements ensue: the pusher is partially withdrawn just out of the portal while the right hand slips the throw over the tip of the pusher onto the portal (Figure 3H‐J). This action culminates in readvancing the pusher to push down the hitch toward the knot stack (Figure 3K). Past‐pointing technique and looping both the tip of the pusher and the post strand around the loop strand are applied to secure the overhand hitch, then the original loop suture strand and the post suture strand are flipped after this execution (Figure 3L).

Finally, 2 or more alternating throws are performed to completely secure a 6‐throw Surgeon's knot. 10 , 11 The post strand and the knot pusher are controlled by the left hand all throughout the tying process, and the stroke of the surgeon's left hand combined with the knot pusher is very short, being only from the knot stack to the portal or the cannula.

DISCUSSION

Numerous knots have been described for arthroscopic labral and rotator cuff repair. 12 Among these procedures, a reliable and reproducible knot‐tying technique is critical to the success of these procedures, particularly given that arthroscopic knot tying is a complex process in which technical error at any step may lead to failure. 13 Specifically, common intraoperative failures include suture entanglement, premature locking, and initial loosening, whereas postoperative failures typically involve knot slippage or breakage. 1 We describe the two‐handed Knot‐tying technique, which functions in a manner analogous to the piston stroke of a compact engine. This approach uses a short knot pusher stroke and coordinated bimanual manipulation, thereby reducing operative time while enhancing technical simplicity and reproducibility (Table 1). Notably, simplicity and effectiveness are generally mutually reinforcing. Guided by this principle, the simplicity of this knot‐tying method may increase the success rate by minimizing subtle errors associated with more complex techniques.

Pearls and Pitfalls

An ideal arthroscopic knot should be simple and reproducible to perform, maintain a low profile, preserve tissue apposition tension (loop security) during tying, and ensure knot integrity. 14 , 15 Therefore, the two‐handed Knot‐tying technique—a simple, reproducible method with high initial loop security—may reduce arthroscopic knot failure rates, and it could save operative time due to the short knot pusher stroke and 2‐handed manipulation (Table 2).
