Abstract
. A classical theorem of Macbeath states that for any integers d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , n ≥ d + 1 n\geq d+1 , d d -dimensional Euclidean balls are hardest to approximate, in terms of volume difference, by inscribed convex polytopes with n n vertices. In this paper we investigate normed variants of this problem: we intend to find the extremal values of the Busemann volume, Holmes-Thompson volume, Gromov’s mass and Gromov’s mass ∗ of a largest volume convex polytope with n n vertices, inscribed in the unit ball of a d d -dimensional normed space.

52A21, 52A40, 52A27

1.
Introduction

Approximation of a convex body by convex polytopes is a long-standing problem of convex geometry (see e.g. the surveys
[
5
,
9
,
14
]
or the book
[
13
]
). In this problem, given a convex body
K
, our goal is to find a member of a fixed family of polytopes with minimal ‘distance’ from the given body, i.e. which minimizes a given error function depending on the body and the polytope.

In this paper we investigate a classical variant of this problem: we intend to find convex polytopes contained in the body with minimal volume difference; that is, having maximal volume. One of the first results in this regard is possibly due to Blaschke
[
7
, 49-53]
, who proved in 1923 that among unit area plane convex bodies, the area of a maximum area inscribed triangle is minimal for Euclidean disks. This result was generalized by Sas
[
26
]
in 1939, proving the same statement for inscribed convex
n
-gons for any fixed integer
n\geq 3
in place of triangles. The
d
-dimensional case of this problem with
d\geq 3
was settled by Macbeath
[
17
]
, who, in a classical paper in 1957, proved that in any dimension, Euclidean balls are hardest to approximate, in terms of volume difference, by inscribed convex polytopes with
n
vertices for any
n\geq d+1
.
The goal of this paper is to find an analogue of this problem in finite dimensional normed spaces. We note that other variants of the theorem of Macbeath appeared in the recent manuscripts
[
6
,
8
]
, and that our approach to find normal variants of the problem of Macbeath was used also in
[
16
]
for a different problem.

Our investigation is carried out in the
d
-dimensional real vector space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. During this, we use the standard notation
\inter(\cdot)
,
\bd(\cdot)
,
\conv(\cdot)
for the interior, boundary and convex hull of a set, and denote by
[x,y]
the closed segment with endpoints
x,y
.
We denote the family of
d
-dimensional convex bodies, and the subfamily consisting of the origin-symmetric elements, by
\mathcal{K}^{d}
and
\mathcal{K}^{d}_{o}
, respectively.
If
B\in\mathcal{K}^{d}_{o}
, the normed space with
B
as its unit ball is denoted by
\mathcal{B}
. The norm in this space is denoted as
||\cdot||_{B}
. We also equip
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the usual inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, and the induced Euclidean norm
|\cdot|
. The Euclidean unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
o
as its center, is denoted by
\mathbf{B}^{d}
, and we set
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}=\bd\mathbf{B}^{d}
and

\kappa_{d}=\lambda_{d}(\mathbf{B}^{d})
, where
\lambda_{d}(\cdot)
denotes
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. We denote the polar of a set
S
by
S^{\circ}
.
Finally, for simplicity, we call a plane convex body a
disk
, and by a convex
k
-gon we mean a convex polygon with at most
k
vertices.

Recall the well-known fact that any finite dimensional real normed space can be equipped with a Haar measure, and that it is
unique up to multiplication of the standard Lebesgue measure by a scalar.
Depending on the choice of this scalar, one may define more than one version of normed volume.
We investigate the four variants of volume that are most regularly used in the literature.
The
Busemann
and
Holmes-Thompson volume
of a set
S
in a
d
-dimensional normed space with unit ball
B
, is defined as

(1.1)

\vol^{Bus}_{B}(S)=\frac{\kappa_{d}}{\lambda_{d}(B)}\lambda_{d}(S)\,\hbox{and}\,\vol^{HT}_{B}(S)=\frac{\lambda_{d}(B^{\circ})}{\kappa_{d}}\lambda_{d}(S),

respectively.
Note that the Busemann volume of the unit ball, and the Holmes-Thompson volume of its polar, are equal to that of a Euclidean unit ball.
For
Gromov’s mass
, the scalar is chosen in such a way that the volume of a maximal volume cross-polytope, inscribed in the unit ball
B

is equal to
\frac{2^{d}}{d!}
, and for
Gromov’s mass*
(or
Benson’s definition of volume
), the volume of a smallest volume parallelotope, circumscribed about
B
, is equal to
2^{d}
.
We denote the two latter quantities by
\vol^{m}_{B}(S)
and
\vol^{m*}_{B}(S)
, respectively. For more information on these concepts of volume, the reader is referred to
[
3
,
4
]
.

Now we are ready to define the main problem investigated in this paper.

Definition 1.1
.

For any
B\in\mathcal{K}^{d}_{o}
and
\tau\in\{Bus,HT,m,m^{*}\}
, we set

(1.2)

\mu_{n}^{\tau}(B)=\{\vol_{B}^{\tau}(Q):Q\hbox{ is a polytope inscribed in }B\hbox{ with at most }n\textit{ vertices}\}.

Problem 1.2
.

For every
d\geq 2
,
n\geq d+1
and
\tau\in\{Bus,HT,m,m^{*}\}
, find

m^{\tau}(n,d)=\inf\left\{\mu_{n}^{\tau}(B):B\in\mathcal{K}^{d}_{o}\right\},\hbox{ and }M^{\tau}(n,d)=\sup\left\{\mu_{n}^{\tau}(B):B\in\mathcal{K}^{d}_{o}\right\}.

Remark 1.3
.

We observe that for any
d\geq 2
,
n\geq d+1
,
B\in\mathcal{K}^{d}_{o}
, nondegenerate linear transformation
L:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
n
, and
\tau\in\{Bus,HT,m,m^{*}\}
,
\mu_{n}^{\tau}(L(B))=\mu_{n}^{\tau}(B)
. Thus, by the compactness of affine classes of
o
-symmetric convex bodies (see
[
27
]
), the extremal values of the functionals in Problem
1.2
are attained at some convex bodies in
\mathcal{K}^{d}_{o}
.

In the paper, for any
B\in\mathcal{K}^{d}_{o}
, we denote by
I(B)
and
C(B)
a maximum volume cross-polytope inscribed in
B
, and a minimum volume parallelotope circumscribed about
B
, respectively. Furthermore, for any
n\geq d+1
, by
Q_{n}(B)
we denote a maximum volume convex polytope in
B
with at most
n
vertices.

Remark 1.4
.

For any
d\geq 2
,
n\geq d+1
and
B\in\mathcal{K}^{d}_{o}
, we have

(1.3)

\begin{split}\mu_{n}^{Bus}(B)=\frac{\lambda_{d}(Q_{n}(B))}{\lambda_{d}(B)}\kappa_{d},\quad\mu_{n}^{HT}(B)=\frac{\lambda_{d}(Q_{n}(B))\lambda_{d}(B^{\circ})}{\kappa_{d}},\\
\mu_{n}^{m}(B)=\frac{\lambda_{d}(Q_{n}(B))}{\lambda_{d}(I(B))}\cdot\frac{2^{d}}{d!},\quad\mu_{n}^{m*}(B)=\frac{\lambda_{d}(Q_{n}(B))}{\lambda_{d}(C(B))}\cdot 2^{d}.\end{split}

The remaining part of the paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we present our results about the Busemann volumes of inscribed polytopes. In Sections
3
-
5
we investigate the Holmes-Thompson volume, Gromov’s mass and Gromov’s mass
∗
of inscribed polytopes, respectively. Finally, in Section
6
we collect our additional remarks and open questions.

2.
Extremal values of the Busemann volumes of inscribed polytopes

In this section we intend to find the values of
m^{Bus}(n,d)
and
M^{Bus}(n,d)
for
d\geq 2
and
n\geq d+1
. In other words, we need to find the extremal values of

\mu_{n}^{Bus}(B)=\frac{\lambda_{d}(Q_{n}(B))}{\lambda_{d}(B)}\kappa_{d}.

over all
B\in\mathcal{K}^{d}_{o}
. We start with some simple observations.

Remark 2.1
.

By the result of Macbeath
[
17
]
mentioned in the introduction,
for any
d\geq 2
and
n\geq d+1
,
\frac{\lambda_{d}(Q_{n}(B))}{\lambda_{d}(B)}
is minimal if
B=\mathbf{B}^{d}
. Thus,
m^{Bus}(n,d)=\mu_{n}^{Bus}(\mathbf{B}^{d})
for all possible values of
d
and
n
. From the result of Sas
[
26
]
it also follows that
m^{Bus}(n,2)=\mu_{n}^{Bus}(B)
implies that
B
is an ellipse.

Remark 2.2
.

If
n\geq 2d
, then there are
o
-symmetric polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with at most
n
vertices, implying that in this case
M^{Bus}(n,d)=\kappa_{d}
, and
M^{Bus}(n,d)=\mu_{n}^{Bus}(B)
implies that
B
is an
o
-symmetric polytope with at most
n
vertices.

In the remaining part of this section we investigate
M^{Bus}(n,d)
under the condition that
d+1\leq n\leq 2d-1
.

Remark 2.3
.

Let
T
be a triangle, and
o\in T
. Then an elementary computation shows that for
B=\conv(T\cup(-T))
, we have
\lambda_{2}(B)=2\lambda_{2}(T)
. On the other hand,
B
is an
o
-symmetric convex hexagon if and only if
B=\conv(T\cup(-T))
for some triangle
T
satisfying
o\in T
. Thus,
M^{Bus}(3,2)=\frac{\pi}{2}
, and
\mu_{3}^{Bus}(B)=\frac{\pi}{2}
if and only if
B
is a (possibly degenerate)
o
-symmetric hexagon.

Our main result in this section is Theorem
2.4
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
d\geq 3
, with
d=4m+r
for some integers
m,r
, where
0\leq r\leq 3
. Then

(a)

M^{Bus}(d+1,d)=\frac{\kappa_{d}}{\binom{d}{\lfloor\frac{d}{2}\rfloor}}
, and
M^{Bus}(d+1,d)=\mu_{n}^{Bus}(B)
for
B\in\mathcal{K}^{d}_{o}
if
B=\conv(S\cup(-S))
for some regular simplex centered at
o
.

(b)

If
0\leq r\leq 1
, then

M^{Bus}(d+2,d)=\frac{\kappa_{d}}{\binom{2m+1}{m}\cdot\binom{2m+r-1}{m}}.

Furthermore,
M^{Bus}(d+2,d)=\mu_{n}^{Bus}(B)
for
B\in\mathcal{K}^{d}_{o}
if
B=\conv(S_{1}\cup S_{2}\cup(-S_{1})\cup(-S_{2}))
, where
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are
o
-symmetric regular simplices, of dimensions
2m+1
and
2m+r-1
, respectively, lying in orthogonal linear subspaces;

(c)

If
2\leq r\leq 3
, then

M^{Bus}(d+2,d)=\frac{\kappa_{d}}{\binom{2m+1}{m}\cdot\binom{2m+r-1}{m+1}}.

Furthermore,
M^{Bus}(d+2,d)=\mu_{n}^{Bus}(B)
for
B\in\mathcal{K}^{d}_{o}
if
B=\conv(S_{1}\cup S_{2}\cup(-S_{1})\cup(-S_{2}))
, where
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are
o
-symmetric regular simplices, of dimensions
2m+1
and
2m+r-1
, respectively, lying in orthogonal linear subspaces.

The proof of Theorem
2.4
is based on the properties of shadow systems (also called
linear parameter systems
), introduced by Rogers and Shephard
[
24
]
and studied by many authors.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a nonzero vector, and for any
x\in X
, let
\lambda_{x}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then the
1
-parameter family of sets

C(t)=\conv\{x+t\lambda_{x}v:x\in X\},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}

is called a
shadow system
.

We use the following result of Rogers and Shepard, stated as
[
24
, Lemma 1]
.

Lemma 2.6
.

With the notation in Definition
2.5
, the function
t\mapsto\lambda_{d}(C(t))
is convex.

To prove Theorem
2.4
, we need some additional lemmas.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a family of finitely many hyperplanes in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where the intersection of all hyperplanes is
\{o\}
. Let
X=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)^{k}
be a finite sequence of points. Let
X_{H}
denote the closure of the
k
-element point-sequences in
\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)^{k}
that can be obtained from
X
by applying finitely many subsequent projections onto hyperplanes in
\mathcal{H}
to each element of
X
simultaneously. Then
(o,o,\ldots,o)\in X_{H}
.

Proof.

First, observe that since
X_{H}
is closed and bounded, it is compact. In addition, for any projection
\proj_{H}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
onto a hyperplane
H\in\mathcal{H}
, we have
\proj_{H}(X_{H})\subseteq X_{H}
. Indeed, if
Y=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{k})
can be obtained from
X
by finitely many orthogonal projections onto hyperplanes from
\mathcal{H}
, then the same is true about
\proj_{H}(Y)=(\proj_{H}(y_{1}),\proj_{H}(y_{2}),\ldots,\proj_{H}(y_{k}))
. Similarly, if
Y=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{k})
can be obtained as the limit of a sequence of such point sequences
Y^{m}=(y_{1}^{m},y_{2}^{m},\ldots,y_{k}^{m})
, then the same is true about
\proj_{H}(Y)
.

Let us define the function
f(Y)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}|y_{i}|
on
\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)^{k}
for any sequence
Y=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{k})
. This function is clearly continuous, and thus, it attains its minimum
\delta
on
X_{H}
. If
\delta=0
, then we have
(o,o,\ldots,o)\in X_{H}
. Suppose for contradiction that
\delta>0
. Then, if
\sum_{i=1}^{k}|y_{i}|=\delta
for the sequence
Y=(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{k})\in X_{H}
, we have that there is some
H\in\mathcal{H}
that does not contain at least one of the
y_{i}
. But in this case the orthogonal projection
\proj_{H}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
onto
H
strictly decreases the value of
f(Y)
, i.e. we have
f(\proj_{H}(Y))<f(Y)
, a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

For any integers
d\geq 3
and
1\leq k\leq\frac{d}{2}
, let

A_{d}(k)=\binom{k}{\left[\frac{k}{2}\right]}\cdot\binom{d-k}{\left[\frac{d-k}{2}\right]},

and let
A_{d}=\min\left\{A_{d}(k):1\leq k\leq\frac{d}{2}\right\}
.
Let
d=4m+r
for some
r\in\{0,1,2,3\}
. Then

(1)

for
r=0
,
A_{d}=\binom{2m-1}{m-1}\cdot\binom{2m+1}{m}
, with equality iff
k=2m-1
;

(2)

for
r=1
,
A_{d}=\binom{2m}{m}\cdot\binom{2m+1}{m}
, with equality iff
k=2m-1
or
k=2m
;

(3)

for
r\in\{2,3\}
,
A_{d}=\binom{2m+1}{m}\cdot\binom{2m+r-1}{m+r-2}
, with equality iff
k=2m+1
.

Proof.

Consider the case that
k\leq\frac{d}{2}-2
. Then an elementary computation yields that

A_{d}(k+2)=\frac{(k+2)\cdot(k+1)\cdot\lfloor\frac{d-k}{2}\rfloor\cdot\lceil\frac{d-k}{2}\rceil}{\lfloor\frac{k+2}{2}\rfloor\cdot\lceil\frac{k+2}{2}\rceil\cdot(d-k)\cdot(d-k-1)}\cdot A_{d}(k).

Let
q_{d}(k)=\frac{A_{d}(k+2)}{A_{d}(k)}
.
We show that
q_{d}(k)<1
for all values of
k
and
d
. We distinguish four cases depending on the parity of
k
and
d
. If both
k,d
are even, then

q_{d}(k)=\frac{(k+1)(d-k)}{(k+2)(d-k-1)}.

From this,
(k+1)+(d-k)=(k+2)+(d-k-1)
and
0<k+1<k+2<d-k-1<d-k
implies that
q_{d}(k)<1
. If
k
,
d
or both are odd, a slight modification of this computation yields the same inequality.

Now, we prove the assertion. If
r=0
, then, by the previous consideration,
A_{d}(k)
is minimal at
k=2m-1
or
k=2m
. On the other hand,

\frac{A_{d}(2m)}{A_{d}(2m-1)}=\frac{2m+2}{2m+1}>1,

implying the statement in this case. In the cases
r=1
,
r=2
and
r=3
a similar computation proves the statement.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

The proof is based on a symmetrization procedure, similar to the one in
[
1
]
, using shadow systems.

To prove (a), since the investigated quantity is affine invariant, we can clearly assume that
Q_{d+1}(B)
is a translate of a regular simplex
S
centered at the origin
o
, and
B=\conv\left((x+S\cup(-x-S)\right)
, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Consider the function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,
f(x)=\lambda_{d}(B)=\lambda_{d}(\conv\left((x+S\cup(-x-S)\right))
. Moving
x
at a constant speed at any fixed direction induces a linear parameter system on the vertices of
B
. Thus, by Lemma
2.6
,
f
is a convex function. On the other hand,
f
is invariant under any symmetry of
S
. In particular, it follows that the set of points where
f
is minimal, is a convex set whose symmetry group contains that of
S
. Thus,
f
is minimal at
o
. We note that the formula for the volume of
B
in this special case can be found, e.g. in
[
25
]
.

Now we deal with (b) and (c). Let
Q=Q_{d+2}(B)
. Then we may assume that
B=\conv(Q\cup(-Q))
. Using the characterization of
d
-dimensional convex polytopes with
d+2
vertices (see e.g.
[
28
]
and the references therein), for some integer
1\leq k\leq d-1
,
Q
can be written in the form
\conv\left((x_{1}+S_{1})\cup(x_{2}+S_{2})\right)
, where

(i)

S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are simplices centered at
o
, of dimensions
k
and
d-k
, respectively;

(ii)

the affine hulls of
x_{1}+S_{1}
and
x_{2}+S_{2}
intersect in a singleton, which is contained in both simplices.

Using the affine invariance of the problem, we may assume that
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are regular simplices, and their affine hulls are orthogonal to each other. In this case the orthogonal projection of
Q
onto the affine hull of
x_{1}+S_{1}
is
x_{1}+S_{1}
, and the intersection of
Q
with the affine hull of
x_{2}+S_{2}
is
x_{2}+S_{2}
. Thus, by Lemma of
[
25
]
, the volume of
Q
is

\lambda_{d}(Q)=\frac{1}{\binom{d}{k}}\lambda_{d}(S_{1})\lambda_{d}(S_{2}),

independently of the position of the intersection point of
x_{1}+S_{1}
and
x_{2}+S_{2}
relative to
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
. Let us define

V(x_{1},x_{2})=\lambda_{d}\left(\conv\left((x_{1}+S_{1})\cup(x_{2}+S_{2})\cup(-x_{1}-S_{1})\cup(-x_{2}-S_{2})\right)\right).

We intend to find the quantity

V_{\min}(k)=\min\{V(x_{1},x_{2}):x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},(x_{1}+S_{1})\cap(x_{2}+S_{2})\neq\emptyset\}.

Note that
V_{\min}(k)\geq\min\{V(x_{1},x_{2}):x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
. Thus, to find
V_{\min}(k)
it is sufficient to show that
V(x_{1},x_{2})
is minimal over
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
if
x_{1}=x_{2}=o
. Let
H
be the bisector of an edge of any of the simplices
S_{1}
or
S_{2}
. Let
\proj_{H}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to H
denote the orthogonal projection of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
onto
H
. Let
v
denote a unit normal vector of
H
.

We show that
V(x_{1},x_{2})\geq V(\proj_{H}(x_{1}),\proj_{H}(x_{2}))
. To do it, we define a shadow system. Let the signed distance of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
from
H
be
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
, respectively, where the sign is determined by
v
. For any
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
i\in\{1,2\}
, set
x_{i}(t)=x_{i}+\lambda_{i}(t-1)v
, and

K(t)=\conv\left((x_{1}(t)+S_{1})\cup(x_{2}(t)+S_{2})\cup(-x_{1}(t)-S_{1})\cup(-x_{2}(t)-S_{2})\right).

Then
K(t)
is a shadow system, and thus, the function
t\mapsto\lambda_{d}(K(t))
is convex by Lemma
2.6
. On the other hand, this function is an even function of
t
, as for any value of
t
,
K(-t)
is the reflection of
K(t)
to
H
. Thus,
\lambda_{d}(K(t))
is minimal if
t=0
, implying, in particular, that
\lambda_{d}(K(1))\geq\lambda_{d}(K(0))
. But
\lambda_{d}(K(1))=V(x_{1},x_{2})
and
\lambda_{d}(K(0))=V(\proj_{H}(x_{1}),\proj_{H}(x_{2}))
, which yields our statement.

Now, let
\mathcal{H}
denote the family of the bisectors of all edges of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
. Note that the only common point of these hyperplanes is the origin
o
. Then, by Lemma
2.7
, starting with the pair
(x_{1},x_{2})
, the pair
(o,o)
belongs to the closure of the set of pairs that can be obtained by finitely many projections onto elements of
\mathcal{H}
. This, our previous argument, combined with the observation that
V(x_{1},x_{2})
is a continuous function of
(x_{1},x_{2})
, yields that
V(x_{1},x_{2})\geq V(o,o)
for any
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, or in other words,
V_{\min}(k)=V(o,o)
.

Next, we note that by
[
25
]
, we have

V(o,o)=\frac{1}{\binom{d}{k}}\lambda_{d}(\conv(S_{1}\cup(-S_{1})))\lambda_{d}(\conv(S_{2}\cup(-S_{2}))).

Furthermore, again by
[
25
]
, we have

\lambda_{d}(\conv(S_{1}\cup(-S_{1})))=\binom{k}{\left[\frac{k}{2}\right]}\lambda_{d}(S_{1}),\hbox{ and}

\lambda_{d}(\conv(S_{2}\cup(-S_{2})))=\binom{d-k}{\left[\frac{d-k}{2}\right]}\lambda_{d}(S_{2}).

Combining these inequalities, we obtain that for any
o
-symmetric convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\mu_{d+2}^{Bus}(K)\leq\frac{\kappa_{d}}{\binom{k}{\left[\frac{k}{2}\right]}\cdot\binom{d-k}{\left[\frac{d-k}{2}\right]}}

for some
1\leq k\leq d-1
.
Thus the assertion in (2) and (3) of Theorem
2.4
follows from Lemma
2.8
.
∎

3.
Extremal values of the Holmes-Thompson volumes of inscribed polytopes

In this section, we intend to find the extremal values of

\mu_{n}^{HT}(B)=\frac{\lambda_{d}(Q_{n}(B))\lambda_{d}(B^{\circ})}{\kappa_{d}}.

over all
B\in\mathcal{K}^{d}_{o}
.

First, we collect our planar results.

Theorem 3.1
.

We have the following.

(1)

For any even integer
n\geq 4
, we have
M^{HT}(n,2)=\frac{n^{2}}{\pi}\sin^{2}\frac{\pi}{n}
, and

4.
Extremal values of the Gromov’s masses of inscribed polytopes

In this section, we intend to find the extremal values of

\mu_{n}^{m}(B)=\frac{\lambda_{d}(Q_{n}(B))}{\lambda_{d}(I(B))}\cdot\frac{2^{d}}{d!}.

over all
B\in\mathcal{K}^{d}_{o}
.

First, we collect our planar results.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let

. Then the following holds.

(1)

We have
m^{m}(3,2)=1
, and
m^{m}(3,2)=\mu_{3}^{m}(B)
for
B\in\mathcal{K}^{2}_{o}
if
B
is a parallelogram.

(2)

If

, then
m^{m}(n,2)=2
, and
m^{m}(n,2)=\mu_{n}^{m}(B)
for

if

We have

, and

for any

.

(3)

If

is even, and

denotes the largest positive integer with

and

, then

-gon.

To prove (1), note that for every

.

To prove (2), observe that if

\mu_{n}^{m}(B)\geq\frac{\lambda_{2}(I(B))}{\lambda_{2}(I(B))}\cdot 2=2.

The statement in (3) follows from Lemma
3.2
. Now we prove (4).

Since

is even, by Lemma
3.2

is an
o
-symmetric convex
n

\mu_{n}^{m}(B)=\frac{2\lambda_{2}(Q_{n}(B))}{\lambda_{2}(I(B))}\leq\frac{2\lambda_{2}(Q_{n}(B))}{\lambda_{2}(I(Q_{n}(B)))},

with equality if

. In other words, we may assume that
B
is an
o
-symmetric convex
n
-gon. From now on, we follow the proof of (2) of Theorem
3.1
. We only sketch the proof.

Let
\hat{B}\in\mathcal{K}^{2}_{o}
be a maximizer of the functional
\mu_{n}^{m}(\cdot)

If
\hat{B}
has a vertex

,

\mu_{n}^{m}(\hat{B})=\mu_{n}^{m}(B^{\prime})

of

For any convex polytope

Proof.

To prove (1), note that

\mu_{3}^{m*}(B)=\frac{4\lambda_{2}(Q_{3}(B))}{\lambda_{2}(C(B))}\leq\frac{4\lambda_{2}(Q_{3}(C(B)))}{\lambda_{2}(C(B))}=2.

Similarly, to prove (2) observe that

\mu_{n}^{m*}(B)\leq\frac{4\lambda_{2}(B)}{\lambda_{2}(B)}\leq 4,

and we have equality if
B
is a parallelogram.

For (3), we note that by Lemma
3.2
:

\mu_{4}^{m*}(B)=\frac{4\lambda_{2}(I(B))}{\lambda_{2}(C(B))}\geq 2,

and we have equality if
\bd(B)
is a Radon curve (see
[
21
]
).

Now we prove (4). Assume that
B\in\mathcal{K}^{2}_{o}
satisfies
\mu_{n}^{m*}(B)=m^{m*}(n,2)
, and
B
has minimum area among these convex disks.
We show that
\bd(B)
is an equiframed curve; i.e. every point of
\bd(B)
lies on a side of a minimum area parallelogram circumscribed about
B
.
Note that for any such parallelogram
P
, the midpoints of the sides of
P
belong to
B
.

Suppose that there a supporting line
L
of
B
that is not a sideline of such a parallelogram, and note that by symmetry,
-L
has the same property. Let
[p,q]=L\cap B
, where we possibly have
p=q
. If one of the endpoints, say
p
is the midpoint of a side of a minimum area circumscribed parallelogram
P
, then replacing the sidelines of
P
through
p
and
-p
by
L
and
-L
we again obtain a minimum area circumscribed parallelogram, which contradicts our assumption that
L
is not a sideline of such a parallelogram. Thus, neither
p
nor
q
is the midpoint of a side of a minimum area circumscribed parallelogram. But then, slightly moving
L
and
L^{\prime}
towards
o
we obtain some
o
-symmetric convex disk
B^{\prime}\subsetneq B

with
\lambda_{2}(C(B^{\prime}))=\lambda_{2}(C(B))
, a contradiction. This shows that
\bd(B)
is an equiframed curve.

Equiframed curves are investigated in
[
21
]
, in particular, in Section 5 of
[
21
]
the authors describe a construction by which every equiframed curve can be constructed. Without detailing their construction, from that we just deduce the following statement: If
\bd(B)
is an equiframed curve with
B\in\mathcal{K}^{2}_{o}
, then there is some
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
in
\mathcal{K}^{2}_{o}
such that
\bd(B^{\prime})
is a Radon curve, and
\lambda_{2}(C(B^{\prime}))=\lambda_{2}(C(B))
, implying the statement.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

Note that for any
n\geq d+1
and
B\in\mathcal{K}^{d}_{o}
,

\mu_{n}^{m*}(B)=\frac{\lambda_{d}(Q_{n}(B))}{\lambda_{d}(C(B))}\cdot 2^{d}\leq 2^{d}.

On the other hand, if
n\geq 2^{d}
and
B
a parallelotope, then

, implying that in this case

.

6.
Additional remarks and open questions

The variant of Problem
1.2
for Busemann volume leads to the following question:

Problem 6.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
d+3\leq n\leq 2d-1
. Find the minimum volume of an
o
-symmetric convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that contains a unit volume convex polytope with
n
vertices.

For our next problem, note that since for every
B\in\mathcal{K}^{2}_{o}
, the sequence

\{\mu_{n}^{HT}(B)\}
is increasing, the sequence
\{m^{HT}(n,2)\}
is also increasing. On the other hand, the example of a parallelogram
P
shows that for any
n\geq 4
,

m^{HT}(n,2)\leq\frac{\lambda_{2}(P)\lambda_{2}(P^{\circ})}{\pi}=\frac{8}{\pi}.

Observe that for any
B\in\mathcal{K}^{2}_{o}
, if
n
is sufficiently large, then
\lambda_{2}(Q_{n}(B))\approx\lambda_{2}((B))
, implying that
\mu_{n}^{HT}(B)\approx\frac{\lambda_{2}(B)\lambda_{2}(B^{\circ})}{\pi}
. On the other hand, a classical result of Mahler shows that
\lambda_{2}(B)\lambda_{2}(B^{\circ})\geq 8
for any
B\in\mathcal{K}^{2}_{o}
, with equality for parallelograms. This suggests that if
n
is sufficiently large, then
m^{HT}(n,2)\approx\frac{8}{\pi}
.

We conjecture the following, which implies that
m^{HT}(n,2)=\frac{8}{\pi}
for all
n\geq 7
.

Conjecture 1
.

For any
B\in\mathcal{K}^{2}_{o}
, we have

\lambda_{2}(Q_{6}(B))\lambda_{2}(B^{\circ})\geq 8,

with equality if
B
is a parallelogram.

We note that the ‘dual’ of this problem, stating that the quantity

, where

, is maximal for ellipses, is equivalent to a conjecture of Makai, Jr.
[

]

]
.
