Introduction

The Monteggia fracture-dislocation, first described by Giovanni Battista Monteggia in 1814, is characterized by an ulnar fracture with concomitant dislocation of the radial head from both the proximal radioulnar and radiocapitellar joints [1]. Jose Luis Bado further classified this injury into four main types based on the orientation of the radial head dislocation. Beyond these classic types, Bado also introduced the term Monteggia Equivalent lesion or variant, encompassing a broad spectrum of complex fracture patterns and dislocations involving the proximal ulna and radius, where different injuries substitute the ulnar injury. These variants are rare and often diagnostically challenging, particularly in adults, and can be easily missed if not specifically sought [2]. The infrequent occurrence of these injuries, especially in adults, often leads to diagnostic delays.

Monteggia equivalent injuries compromise the stability of both the forearm and the elbow. An inaccurate or delayed diagnosis can lead to significant long-term morbidity, including chronic pain, instability, nonunion, malunion, and post-traumatic arthritic changes that severely restrict forearm rotation [3], [4].

The primary objective of treatment for Monteggia injuries is the anatomical realignment of the fractured bone, which typically reinstates the stability of the radiocapitellar joint [5], [6]. This report presents two cases of Type I Monteggia Equivalent fractures in adult patients, one of which involved a significant delayed presentation, to highlight the principles of effective management and to demonstrate that excellent functional outcomes can be achieved through appropriate surgical intervention, even in challenging scenarios. This case series has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist [7].

Case presentation

Case 1

A 20-year-old, right-hand dominant male student presented to our outpatient orthopedic clinic with a one-month history of persistent pain, a visible deformity, and functional impairment of his left forearm following a fall. He reported no significant past medical, surgical, or family history and no known drug allergies. Upon examination, there was significant tenderness and a palpable deformity over the proximal radius. Elbow and forearm motion were severely restricted and painful. Anteroposterior (AP) forearm and dedicated true lateral elbow radiographs confirmed a displaced fracture of the proximal radial shaft with an associated frank anterior dislocation of the radial head (Fig. 1). The diagnosis was a delayed Type I Monteggia Equivalent fracture. The patient underwent ORIF of the radius via an anterior surgical approach (Henry Approach) under general anesthesia. The radial fracture was anatomically reduced and stabilized with a 3.5 mm dynamic compression plate (DCP) and screws (DePuy Synthes). Following rigid fixation, the radiocapitellar joint reduced spontaneously and was confirmed stable under fluoroscopy. No specific intraoperative challenges related to the chronic nature of the injury, such as significant soft tissue contractures requiring extensive release or difficulty achieving reduction, were encountered beyond what would be expected in an acute case, demonstrating the efficacy of precise radial length restoration. The procedure was performed at a university-affiliated tertiary care hospital by a senior orthopedic surgeon with over 10 years of experience in trauma surgery. There were no intraoperative complications.

Follow-up and outcomes

At the 6-month follow-up, the patient reported being pain-free with a full range of motion (0–140° flexion-extension, 85° supination, and 80° pronation). Radiographs showed solid bony union with the hardware in situ and maintained joint reduction (Fig. 2). Clinical photographs further demonstrated the excellent functional recovery and range of motion (Fig. 5). His Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS) was 100, indicating an outstanding outcome. Case 2

A 24-year-old, left-hand dominant male manual laborer presented to our clinic two days after a fall onto an outstretched hand (FOOSH). He had no relevant past medical history or known allergies. He complained of severe pain and swelling in his left forearm. Examination revealed notable tenderness and swelling over the proximal radius with a globally restricted, painful range of motion. AP and lateral forearm radiographs confirmed a displaced fracture of the proximal radial shaft, with an associated anterior subluxation of the radial head demonstrated by a disrupted radiocapitellar line (Fig. 3). Discussion

Monteggia equivalent fractures represent a formidable challenge in orthopedic trauma, particularly within the adult population, owing to their infrequent occurrence and the substantial risk of adverse long-term sequelae if inadequately managed [3], [4]. This case series robustly demonstrates that anatomical reduction and rigid internal fixation of the radial fracture are paramount for achieving superior functional outcomes in Type I Monteggia Equivalent lesions. This holds even in delayed presentations. The successful operative management of a one-month-old injury in Case 1 underscores that excellent clinical results are attainable with precise surgical intervention, highlighting the inherent capacity of the radiocapitellar joint for spontaneous reduction once radial length and alignment are meticulously restored.

The foundational principle, positing that stable osteosynthesis of the primary fractured bone facilitates spontaneous radial head reduction, constitutes a cornerstone of Monteggia fracture management [5], [6]. Our observations are congruent with established literature that advocates for this surgical paradigm. The excellent functional outcomes, quantified by Mayo Elbow Performance Scores (MEPS) of 100 in both reported cases, are consistent with published data concerning successful Monteggia treatment [15].

However, the inherent complexity of Monteggia equivalent lesions frequently stems from their protean and often diagnostically elusive presentations [3], [4], [9], [10]. Our presented cases, specifically involving displaced proximal radial shaft fractures with concomitant radial head dislocation, represent a distinct variant within this spectrum. While overarching principles of Monteggia management are applicable, the precise surgical strategy can diverge from that employed for classical Monteggia fractures, where the ulna is the primary site of injury. The current body of literature about adult Type I Monteggia equivalent lesions with isolated radial shaft fractures is comparatively less extensive than that dedicated to pediatric cases or typical Monteggia fracture patterns [2], [14]. This report thus augments the existing knowledge base by presenting compelling evidence of successful outcomes in adults afflicted with this specific injury configuration, particularly emphasizing the management of delayed presentation (Case 1) and its implications.

A salient observation, which adds novel insight to existing literature, is the consistent achievement of spontaneous radial head reduction even in a delayed setting (Case 1). While delayed presentations of Monteggia injuries are recognized to elevate surgical complexity and the propensity for suboptimal outcomes [8], [11], our chronic case definitively illustrates that meticulous anatomical reduction of the radius can effect stable joint reduction without recourse to direct radial head manipulation or extensive soft tissue releases, as might be inferred from certain pediatric literature on chronic radial head dislocations [12], [13]. This finding is significant as it provides compelling evidence that a primary focus on restoring radial anatomy is often sufficient for radial head reduction even after a substantial delay, challenging the assumption that chronic dislocations invariably necessitate more intricate soft tissue releases or complex radial head reconstructive procedures in adults. The lack of intraoperative challenges in Case 1, despite the one-month delay, highlights that the inherent stability mechanisms of the adult radiocapitellar joint can often be restored simply by correcting the underlying bony deformity.

This case series significantly enhances our understanding of Type I Monteggia Equivalent fractures in several key ways. First, it validates the importance of anatomical open reduction and internal fixation for managing radial fractures, demonstrating that this approach reliably achieves spontaneous and stable reduction of the radiocapitellar joint. Second, the successful recovery of a patient who presented one month after the injury highlights that excellent functional outcomes can still be achieved despite delayed diagnosis, provided that meticulous surgical techniques are employed. This is particularly relevant given the common challenges of misdiagnosis or delayed recognition of these injuries. The absence of specific intraoperative challenges in the chronic case (Case 1), beyond the standard surgical difficulty for an acute case, suggests that in adult Type I Monteggia equivalents involving the radial shaft, restoration of radial length and alignment is often sufficient for reduction regardless of delay, a point of novelty compared to more complex chronic Monteggia patterns. Furthermore, both patients in this series achieved a Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS) of 100, indicating complete functional recovery and emphasizing the effectiveness of the surgical method in restoring pain-free motion. Lastly, by focusing on adult cases, this series provides valuable data on a less common injury pattern, differentiating it from the more frequently discussed pediatric Monteggia variants [1]. While Case 2, an acute presentation, demonstrates the standard successful outcome with ORIF, its inclusion here serves to provide a comparative context to the delayed presentation and reinforces the consistent efficacy of the chosen surgical approach across both acute and delayed scenarios of this specific injury pattern in adults. The innovation lies in the robust demonstration of consistently excellent outcomes with a standardized radial ORIF technique across both acute and significantly delayed presentations of adult Type I Monteggia equivalent radial shaft fractures.

Conclusion

This case series unequivocally demonstrates that meticulous anatomical open reduction and internal fixation of the radial shaft fracture constitute a highly effective and reliable treatment strategy for Type I Monteggia Equivalent lesions in adults. This approach consistently yields excellent functional outcomes, characterized by spontaneous and stable radiocapitellar joint reduction, even in instances of significantly delayed presentation. Key clinical takeaways include the imperative for a high index of suspicion for this complex injury, the prioritization of anatomical radial fixation as the primary therapeutic goal to restore elbow stability, and the realization that exceptional results are attainable even when initial diagnosis and intervention are protracted. Our findings underscore the resilience of the elbow joint and the profound impact of precise surgical technique on patient recovery, ultimately contributing to improved health and well-being and supporting sustainable reintegration into productive societal roles. This series offers valuable evidence, particularly concerning the successful management of delayed adult presentations, enriching the existing literature on this rare injury.
