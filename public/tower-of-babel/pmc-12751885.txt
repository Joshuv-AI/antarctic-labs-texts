Improved stroke risk stratification in carotid atherosclerosis: integrating intraplaque neovascularization into Carotid Plaque-RADS
Authors: Zhe Huang, Xue-Qing Cheng, Rui-Rui Lu, Xiao-Jun Bi, Ya-Ni Liu, You-Bin Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis study evaluates whether IPN, as assessed by contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), enhances stroke risk stratification within the Carotid Plaque-RADS framework.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 157 stroke patients between July 2020 and January 2021. IPN grade was assessed using CEUS, and patients were categorized based on the Carotid Plaque-RADS. The recurrence-free survival (RFS) rates were calculated, and the incremental value of incorporating IPN grades into Carotid Plaque-RADS was analyzed.ResultsDuring a follow-up period of 35 ± 10 months, 44 out of the 157 stroke patients experienced recurrent strokes. There was significant difference in the incidences of stroke recurrence between IPN Grade 1 and Grade 2 (12.6% vs. 57.4%, p < 0.001). Integration of IPN grading with Carotid Plaque-RADS resulted in disparities in RFS rates between RADS 2 and RADS 3, as well as between RADS 3 and RADS 4 (both P < 0.05), which did not exist in the Carotid Plaque-RADS score alone. Furthermore, significant difference in RFS rates were noted between Carotid Plaque-RADS ≤ 3 patients with IPN Grade 2 and those with IPN Grade 1 (P < 0.001). Among patients initially categorized under Carotid Plaque-RADS ≤ 3, 7.7% (8/104) were accurately reclassified, demonstrating a net reclassification improvement of 12.0% after incorporating IPN grades into the Carotid Plaque-RADS. Additionally, the incorporation of IPN grades with Carotid Plaque-RADS led to a substantial increase in the chi-square value (chi-square difference = 20.62; likelihood ratio test P < 0.001).ConclusionIntegration of the presence of IPN Grade 2 into Carotid Plaque-RADS significantly enhanced its ability for stratifying stroke recurrence risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-025-02077-2.

Introduction

In recent years, stroke is a serious public health problem and is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with atherosclerosis being the fundamental underlying factor [1, 2]. Most cerebrovascular events are triggered by vulnerable atherosclerotic plaques that may not be stenotic, and surprisingly, patients with significant carotid stenosis (over 70%) often remain symptom-free and may not need invasive treatments. Hence imaging techniques to identify when a carotid plaque of any size is actually vulnerable and therefore more likely to cause cerebrovascular events are needed to direct appropriate therapeutic measures on the basis of risk. To address this clinical challenge, 51 international experts collaboratively developed the Carotid Plaque-Reporting and Data System (Carotid Plaque-RADS) [3]. This system standardizes the terminology and structure for reporting characteristics of carotid plaques, thereby enhancing understanding among physicians and patients, and promoting better communication, collaboration, and management across healthcare disciplines. The classification integrates multiple imaging modalities—carotid ultrasound, computed tomography angiography(CTA), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and Positron Emission Tomography-Computed Tomography—to assess high-risk plaque characteristics and enhance patient risk profiling. It underscores crucial features of carotid plaques such as maximum wall thickness, calcification, thick, thin or ruptured fibrous cap, lipid-rich necrotic core, intraplaque hemorrhage, ulceration, and intraluminal thrombus, which are essential for stroke risk evaluation. This system lays the groundwork for early identification and appropriate treatment of high-risk plaques while minimizing invasive treatments based solely on the degree of luminal stenosis. Our previous research has demonstrated that Carotid Plaque-RADS significantly enhances stroke risk stratification compared to traditional stenosis grading, particularly in cases of mild to moderate stenosis [4].

Furthermore, intraplaque neovascularization (IPN), an important indicator of plaque instability, is critical for assessing the vulnerability of plaques in both symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals [5–7]. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) has been used as a key non-invasive tool in the detection of carotid plaque IPN, aiding in the assessment of plaque vulnerability [8–10]. By identifying plaque neovascularization, CEUS can help predicting future cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events [6, 7, 11].

However, the Carotid Plaque-RADS system currently does not incorporate IPN information as evaluated by CEUS. While IPN is recognized as an ancillary finding within the Carotid Plaque-RADS, the efficacy of including IPN as a primary characteristic has not been fully validated. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to determine whether carotid IPN as assessed with CEUS can help enhance Carotid Plaque -RADS for stroke risk stratification.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by Institutional Review Board and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The need for informed consent was waived.

Patients

This was a retrospective observational study conducted at our hospital. A total of 172 patients who experienced an ischemic stroke within the past month and who underwent evaluations including carotid MRI, CTA, ultrasound (US), and CEUS between July 2020 and January 2021 were enrolled in our study (Fig. 1). Specifically, patients with carotid atherosclerotic plaques larger than 2.0 mm underwent CEUS examination of the carotid plaque. Ischemic strokes were characterized by focal neurological deficits persisting for more than 24 h, confirmed by cerebral infarction on computed tomography and/or MRI [12]. Additionally, if plaques were present, there was at least one atherosclerotic plaque in the carotid region corresponding to the cerebral infarction. Patients were typically referred for a comprehensive evaluation of their cardio-cerebrovascular health by the Departments of Neurology or Cardiovascular Medicine/Cardiovascular Surgery, often due to symptoms indicative of cardio-cerebrovascular diseases. Others came from various departments for the investigation of neck bruits and nonspecific complaints like transient headaches or migraine-like symptoms. The exclusion criteria were patients with cardioembolism, small-vessel occlusion, strokes of other determined etiologies, strokes of undetermined etiologies, those with a history of carotid revascularization, malignancy or past neck radiation therapy, and those with calcified carotid plaques that caused sound shadowing in US images (Supplemental Figure S1). All examinations were conducted within 14 days following the stroke event.

Clinical information

Clinical data were retrieved from electronic medical records at the time of MRI, CTA, and US/CEUS imaging. This included demographic details (gender, age), smoking status, systolic blood pressure, medical history (e.g., diabetes, atrial fibrillation, coronary heart disease), and laboratory results (cholesterol, HDL, LDL levels).

Standard carotid US and CEUS

Standard carotid ultrasound and CEUS were conducted within two weeks of patient admission. Maximum plaque thickness was measured, and carotid stenosis was classified as mild, moderate, or severe based on NASCET guidelines [13]. CEUS results were categorized into two grades based on enhancement patterns: Grade 1, indicating no enhancement or enhancement confined to the plaque’s shoulder and adventitial side, and Grade 2, indicating core enhancement or extensive contrast enhancement throughout the plaque [14]. Details of the Standard Carotid US and CEUS procedures are provided in Supplemental Materials and Methods 1.

MRI and CTA

MRI utilized a 3.0-T scanner to provide high-resolution imaging of the carotid bifurcation. CTA imaging was conducted using multi-detector CT systems, focusing on carotid plaque morphology and vascular features. Details of the MRI and CTA procedures are provided in Supplemental Materials and Methods 2.

Carotid Plaque-RADS categories

To ensure unbiased evaluation, patient identities were anonymized, and their images were assessed independently by two vascular radiologists (5 and 6 years of experience). Discrepancies were resolved by a senior vascular radiologist (30 years of experience). Key carotid plaque characteristics were analyzed based on the Carotid Plaque-RADS guidelines using ultrasound, MRI, and CTA [3]. Details of the Carotid Plaque-RADS categories are provided in Supplemental Materials and Methods 3.

Follow-up

Following the carotid artery examinations and exclusion of 3 patients (Supplemental Figure S1), the remaining 169 patients underwent follow-up either through medical record reviews or direct phone contact with the patients themselves or their relatives, particularly for those impaired by cognitive or language barriers. Follow-up began from the date of the most recent stroke onset, with stroke recurrence as the primary endpoint. During follow-up, 12 patients (7.1%) were lost to follow-up, primarily due to relocation or inability to be reached by phone, leaving 157 patients (92.9%) for final analysis. As of March 2024, the mean follow-up duration was 35 ± 10 months (range, 12–60 months). Stroke recurrence was confirmed in 132 cases using diffusion MRI, while 25 cases were diagnosed based on clinical symptoms due to lack of MRI data. Among the 132 recurrent stroke cases confirmed by MRI, ischemic strokes accounted for 96.2%, while hemorrhagic strokes accounted for 3.8%. A total of 17 deaths (11/157, 7.0%) occurred during the observation period, mainly due to recurrent stroke (n = 5), cardiovascular events (n = 5), or non-cerebrovascular causes (n = 1).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using standard statistical methods. Count data were presented as frequency (%) and analyzed with the chi-square test. Normally distributed variables (mean ± SD) were assessed with Student’s t-test, while skewed data (median, IQR) were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Survival analysis was conducted with Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests. Predictors of recurrent stroke were identified with univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, estimating HRs with 95% CIs. Inter- and intra-observer agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa. Improvements in stroke prediction by incorporating IPN grades into Carotid Plaque-RADS were evaluated using C-statistics, net reclassification improvement, and likelihood ratio tests. Model fit was assessed with Akaike and Bayesian Information Criteria. All statistical analyses were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Values of P < 0.001 were interpreted as highly significant. Interobserver agreement for IPN grading was evaluated using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient (κ). The interpretation of κ values follows the standard classification: <0.20 (slight agreement), 0.21–0.40 (fair agreement), 0.41–0.60 (moderate agreement), 0.61–0.80 (substantial agreement), and 0.81-1.00 (almost perfect agreement). All statistical analyses were conducted using Prism 6.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA), SPSS Statistics (version 23.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), and Stata (version 16, StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Imaging characteristics and survival outcomes

In the 157 patients, carotid plaques were observed with various characteristics detailed in patients with stroke recurrence or without recurrence (Table 2). Of 157 patients, 52 (33.1%) presented with mild stenosis, 95 (60.5%) with moderate stenosis, and 10 (6.4%) with severe stenosis (Table 2). Notably, the group that experienced stroke recurrence showed a significantly higher percentages of IPN Grade 2 (70.5% vs. 20.3%, P < 0.001) and intraluminal thrombus (13.6% vs. 3.5%, P = 0.02) than that without recurrence. Moreover, recurrence group also displayed a higher prevalences of various plaque characteristics, including a MWT of ≥ 3 mm, LRNC with thin FC, healed ulcerated plaques, IPH, ruptured FC, and severe stenosis, although these differences did not reach statistical significance (all P > 0.05). The interobserver agreement in the assessment of image features, indicated by κvalues results, consistently exceeded 0.80, demonstrating excellent interobserver reliability. Specifically, κ values (95% confidence intervals) were 0.834 (0.756–0.911) for MWT ≥ 3 mm, 0.832 (0.722–0.911) for LRNC with intact thick FC, 0.823 (0.725–0.907) for LRNC with thin FC, 0.812 (0.689–0.903) for healed ulcerated plaque, 0.856 (0.751–0.927) for IPH, 0.832 (0.721–0.923) for ruptured FC, and 0.806(0.686–0.902) for intraluminal thrombus.

The 1-, 2-, and 3-year RFS rates were 100.0%, 100.0%, and 100.0%, respectively for Carotid Plaque-RADS 1, 100.0%, 98.0%, and 92.0%, respectively for Carotid Plaque-RADS 2, 100%, 93.5%, and 73.9%, respectively for Carotid Plaque-RADS 3 and 98.1%, 83.0%, and 60.4%, respectively for Carotid Plaque-RADS 4 (Table 1). Significant differences in RFS rates were noted across Carotid Plaque-RADS categories (P = 0.001, Fig. 1A). However, comparisons between groups showed that significant differences in RFS rates existed only between Carotid Plaque-RADS 2 and Carotid Plaque-RADS 4, as well as between Carotid Plaque-RADS 1 and Carotid Plaque-RADS 4 (P < 0.001, P = 0.029, respectively, Fig. 1A).

Analysis of IPN

In the study of 157 patients, CEUS findings were categorized into two grades: 54 individuals with IPN Grade 2, and 103 individuals with IPN Grade 1. Table 1 delineates the baseline clinical characteristics, imaging features, and stroke recurrence events in patients with IPN Grade 2 and IPN Grade 1. The percentage of IPN Grade 2 was significantly greater than that of IPN Grade 1 in patients with Carotid Plaque-RADS 4 and significantly lower in those with Carotid Plaque-RADS 1,2, and 3 (Table 1), indicating the consistency for stroke risk assessment between Carotid Plaque-RADS score and IPN grading. Thirty-one stroke recurrence occurred during the follow-up period in patients with IPN Grade 2 of contrast enhancement of carotid plaque (57.4%), whereas only 13 stroke recurrence occurred in patients within Grade 1 (12.6%, P < 0.001, Table 1). The elevated incidences of stroke recurrence in patients with IPN Grade 2 were mainly among individuals identified as high-risk (Carotid Plaque-RADS 4) and those with mild to moderate stenosis (both P < 0.001, Table 1). The RFS rates at 1, 2, and 3 years were 100.0%, 93.2%, and 89.3%, respectively, for patients with IPN Grade 1, and 83.3%, 66.7%, and 51.9%, respectively, for those with IPN Grade 2 (all P < 0.001, Table 1). There were significant differences in RFS rates between IPN Grade 1 and IPN Grade 2 (P = 0.001, Fig. 1B). Both univariable and multivariable analyses revealed a significant correlation between the risk of recurrent stroke and IPN grade 2, as well as Carotid Plaque-RADS 4 (Table 3).

Interobserver agreement

The interobserver agreement for IPN grading between the two observers was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient. Theκvalue was 0.844 (95% CI 0.4–0.923), indicating strong agreement between the two observers in classifying IPN as Grade 2 or Grade 1.

Integrating IPN into Carotid Plaque-RADS

To evaluate the value of inclusion of IPN as assessed with CEUS in Carotid Plaque-RADS score for stroke risk stratification, we integrate IPN grading with the Carotid Plaque-RADS, resulting in the formation of the IPN + Carotid Plaque-RADS system (Fig. 2). In this system, cases categorized as IPN Grade 2 are designated as IPN + Carotid Plaque-RADS 4. Significant differences in RFS rates were noted across IPN + Carotid Plaque-RADS categories (P < 0.001, Fig. 1C). This reclassification notably showed disparities in RFS rates between IPN + Carotid Plaque-RADS 2 and IPN + Carotid Plaque-RADS 3, as well as between IPN + Carotid Plaque-RADS 3 and IPN + Carotid Plaque-RADS 4 (P < 0.05, Fig. 1C), which did not exist in the Carotid Plaque-RADS score alone (Fig. 1A). The C-index for the IPN + Carotid Plaque-RADS categories model is 0.7253 (CI: 0.673–0.764). The same model also achieves a Gönen & Heller K of 0.665 (SE = 0.026). This model outperforms the basic Carotid Plaque-RADS categories model in predicting stroke recurrence, recording a Harrell’s C-index of 0.654 (CI: 0.622–0.695) and a Gönen & Heller K of 0.598 (SE = 0.196). Compared to patients with low and median risk (Carotid Plaque-RADS ≤ 3) (20/104, 19.2%), those classified as high risk (Carotid Plaque-RADS 4) demonstrated a significantly increased risk of stroke recurrence (24/53, 45.3%), with a hazard ratio (HR) of 2.927 (95% CI: 1.616–5.303, log-rank P < 0.001, Fig. 1D). When IPN grading was incorporated into the Carotid Plaque-RADS categorization, the HR for stroke recurrence in the high risk versus low and median-risk groups increased to 4.443 (95% CI, 2.285–8.637, Fig. 1E) and significantly greater than that observed in the analysis based solely on Carotid Plaque-RADS (2.927; 95% CI, 1.616–5.303), suggesting the importance of incorporating IPN with Carotid Plaque-RADS scores for refined risk stratification. Furthermore, significant difference in RFS rates was evident between Carotid Plaque-RADS ≤ 3 patients with IPN Grade 2 and those with IPN Grade 1 (P < 0.001, Fig. 3), indicating that integrating IPN grading with Carotid Plaque-RADS scores significantly improves stroke risk stratification. Among patients initially categorized under Carotid Plaque-RADS ≤ 3, 7.7% (8 out of 104) were accurately reclassified, demonstrating a net reclassification improvement of 12.0% after incorporating IPN grades into the Carotid Plaque-RADS scores (Supplemental Table 1). As detailed in Supplemental Table 2, the integration of IPN grades into the Carotid Plaque-RADS scores model significantly enhanced model fit, as evidenced by the lowest Akaike Information Criterion and Bayesian Information Criterion values. Additionally, the incorporation of IPN grades with Carotid Plaque-RADS scores led to a substantial increase in the chi-square value (chi-square difference = 20.62; likelihood ratio test P < 0.001, Fig. 4).

Discussion

In our study, we integrated IPN grading into the Carotid Plaque-RADS system to improve stroke recurrence risk stratification. Patients with IPN Grade 2 had significantly lower RFS than those with Grade 1. Incorporating IPN significantly increased the hazard ratio and predictive accuracy, especially among patients with Carotid Plaque-RADS ≤ 3. Notably, 7.7% of patients were reclassified into a higher-risk group, with a net reclassification improvement of 12.0%. This approach enhances risk prediction and enables more precise identification of high-risk patients for targeted intervention.

Of the 147 patients presenting with mild to moderate stenosis, 39 (26.5%) experienced a recurrence of stroke, with 29 of these patients having a CEUS IPN grade of 2. Historically, clinical focus has predominantly been on patients with severe symptomatic stenosis, often overlooking those with mild to moderate stenosis [15]. In this study, we noted a significant incidence of stroke recurrence among patients with mild and moderate stenosis. Patients with mild and moderate stenosis who experienced future stroke recurrence exhibited higher grades of IPN (Grade 2), indicating increased plaque vulnerability. Camp-Renm and colleagues have demonstrated that, even after adjusting for stenosis severity, the assessment of neovascularization through CEUS remains statistically significant in predicting stroke recurrence [16].

The Carotid Plaque-RADS scoring system was developed to achieve a standardized and uniform classification of carotid artery plaques, aiming to enhance communication between imaging experts, physicians, and researchers. In the scoring system, the primary characteristics of carotid artery plaques include maximum wall thickness, calcification, fibrous cap thickness, thinning or rupture, lipid-rich necrotic cores, intraplaque hemorrhage, ulcers, and intraluminal thrombi. It is well known that IPN is a risk feature of vulnerable plaques. However, the Carotid Plaque-RADS terminology does not account for characteristics associated with IPN, particularly those detected through CEUS. IPN significantly amplifies the inflammatory response within plaques, which, in turn, spurs the further growth of IPN. This cycle of escalation between IPN and inflammation not only accelerates plaque progression but also plays a crucial role in the advancement and rupture of vulnerable plaques [17]. In a pivotal study, Li et al. highlighted that high-grade contrast enhancement, as determined through CEUS, is an independent predictor of ischemic stroke or recurrent transient ischemic attack in transient ischemic attack patients [18]. Additionally, IPN has been recognized for its ability to independently forecast significant and complex coronary artery disease [6, 19]. Our findings further corroborate the significance of IPN, demonstrating that the presence of IPN Grade 2 is markedly more common among patients with recurrent strokes than those without recurrences.

Yan Song’s research findings indicate that IPN detected by CEUS is common among patients who experience stroke recurrence, with a notably higher prevalence of IPN Grade 2 in these patients compared to the non-recurrence group [20]. We conducted a qualitative analysis of CEUS results using a straightforward, reproducible binary method. Additionally, our investigation featured an extended follow-up duration and employed perfluorobutane microbubble as contrast agent which has a long persistent contrast effect and facilitates more thorough IPN observation compared to the previous studies [21, 22]. More importantly, our research builds upon these previous observations by integrating IPN into the Carotid Plaque-RADS system, significantly enhancing its diagnostic utility. This incremental value of integration of IPN grades into Carotid Plaque-RADS score is demonstrated by the observed increase in HR for predicting stroke recurrence and net reclassification improvement in our cohort. The likelihood ratio test demonstrated that IPN grading significantly improved the accuracy of Carotid Plaque-RADS scores in assessing patient risk. Those findings suggests that incorporating CEUS-assessed IPN grades into the Carotid Plaque-RADS score provides a more comprehensive and effective method for assessing stroke risk.

Beyond diagnostic accuracy, the convenience and accessibility of an imaging modality are critical determinants of its clinical utility. In this context, CEUS offers practical advantages for evaluating carotid plaques. Compared with MRI or CT angiography, CEUS can be performed at the bedside or in an outpatient setting without radiation exposure or nephrotoxic contrast agents, making it particularly suitable for patients with renal impairment or contraindications to iodinated or gadolinium-based contrast. The examination is relatively quick, cost-effective, and repeatable, allowing dynamic and real-time assessment of intraplaque neovascularization. However, CEUS remains operator-dependent, and interobserver variability can affect diagnostic consistency, highlighting the importance of standardized acquisition and interpretation protocols.

Our study has limitations. We evaluated only the thickest plaque in the ipsilateral lesion, potentially introducing selection bias. CEUS, being a two-dimensional technique, may miss neovascularization in certain plaques, and its grading system is subjective despite good interobserver reliability. Second, the relatively short follow-up and modest sample size, particularly the low sample size in the subgroup which may result in insufficient statistical power, may limit long-term outcome assessment and generalizability. Additionally, our study was conducted at a single center, which may limit the external validity and applicability of our findings to broader populations. As a retrospective study, it also carries a risk of selection bias, highlighting the need for larger prospective studies to validate these findings. Last, although diffusion-weighted MRI was used to confirm stroke recurrence in most patients, 15 individuals did not undergo MRI examination and were diagnosed based on clinical presentation alone. This may have led to potential misclassification of stroke etiology, such as strokes caused by intracranial vascular disease, or underestimation of competing intracranial sources. Future studies incorporating comprehensive intracranial vascular imaging-such as MR or CTA-are warranted to better delineate the causal relationship between carotid plaque characteristics and stroke recurrence.

Conclusion

Adding CEUS IPN Grade 2 to the Carotid Plaque-RADS score improved its ability to predict stroke recurrence risk, allowing for better identification of high-risk patients.
