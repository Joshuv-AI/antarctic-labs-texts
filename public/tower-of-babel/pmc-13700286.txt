Online EMG onset detection during sit-to-stand motion using the time derivative of sample entropy
Authors: Louise Scherrer, Abderrahmane Kheddar, Gauthier Desmyttere, Gilles Dusfour, Christian Jorgensen, Sofiane Ramdani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLow-latency detection of muscle activation onset is essential for online movement analysis and for many assistive and rehabilitation applications, enabling early inference of user motion intent. Surface electromyography (sEMG) are promising signals for this purpose. However, prior work has highlighted that designing robust online sEMG onset detectors remains challenging due to substantial inter- and intra-subject variability, and many approaches are primarily evaluated offline, limiting their applicability in real-world settings. As a step toward addressing these challenges, we propose an online sEMG onset detector designed for demanding activities of daily living and capable of handling a wide range of signal characteristics. The method is evaluated using a pseudo-online framework providing near real-life testing conditions.MethodsThe proposed detector relies on the time derivative of fuzzy sample entropy (SampEn) computed on sliding sEMG windows. Validation used two datasets collected for this study: (i) experimental sEMG comprising 1676 samples from 335 sit-to-stand motions recorded from six healthy participants, and (ii) synthetic sEMG signals generated by a physiologically-based model with controlled baseline noise. Evaluation followed a pseudo-online procedure, emulating real-time windowing on streaming data and accounting for filter-induced latency, without prior knowledge of signal amplitude or baseline noise. Performance was compared with two established baseline detectors, respectively based on the Extended Teager-Kaiser Energy Operator (ETKEO) and the fuzzy SampEn, both reimplemented under identical pseudo-online constraints. Metrics included detection delay, missed and false detection rates, and working ranges across signal and noise conditions.ResultsOn experimental data, muscle activation was detected 36±70 ms before motion onset on average, with pre-motion detection in 77% of samples. Detection delay was reduced by at least 13% (up to 71%) compared with the online-compatible baselines, and missed detections were reduced from 4% to 1% compared to a non-derivative SampEn detector. Across both datasets, the proposed method exhibited the widest working range across signal amplitudes and baseline noise levels.ConclusionsOur method enables earlier and more robust online sEMG onset detection for knee extensor activity under near real-life variability, supporting integration into EMG-based online systems. Both datasets and the real-time implementation are released open-source.Keywords: Surface EMG, Muscle activation, Online onset detection, Fuzzy sample entropy, Sit-to-stand motion

Background

Surface electromyography (sEMG) is widely used to study human movement and muscle coordination across a variety of applications, including biomechanics, motor control, and rehabilitation research [1, 2]. Depending on the application, sEMG enables the analysis of muscle activation patterns and can support both low-level estimation of muscle force or joint kinematics and higher-level detection of movement intent [2].

However, processing sEMG signals remains challenging due to their complexity and inherent noise [3, 4], as sEMG signals arise from the summation of depolarization potentials from hundreds to thousands of muscle fibers, transmitted through various tissue layers to the sensor [5]. sEMG signals exhibit substantial intra- and inter-subject variability, which complicates reliable signal analysis, particularly in online applications.

A critical step in sEMG analysis is the detection of muscle activation onset, defined as the instant when a muscle transitions from a relaxed state to contraction. Due to the EMD, this onset typically occurs 10 to 100 ms before force production [6, 7]. Accurate and timely onset detection is therefore essential for the study of neuromuscular activation timing in dynamic movements and for applications requiring early inference of motion intent.

Furthermore, online onset detection in EMG data is important for these intended applications, but introduces additional constraints since data are available as a stream [8]. As a consequence, global information about the full recording, such as overall (SNR) or baseline statistics, cannot be used a priori to tune detector parameters. A relevant literature review by Carvalho et al. [3] has highlighted that many EMG onset detection methods are primarily designed or evaluated in offline settings, while standardized evaluation protocols for online validation remain lacking. Accordingly, we focus on a pseudo-online testing protocol designed to emulate streaming deployment as closely as possible. To clarify terminology, we use online to refer to pseudo-online processing of streamed data, whereas real-time denotes computational throughput sufficient to keep pace with acquisition. Detection delays reported throughout the study account for algorithmic latency introduced by filtering and windowing.

Various methods for EMG onset detection have been explored [3]. Classical amplitude-based methods threshold low-pass filtered EMG signals [9]. They perform reliably on high-quality recordings with clear activation bursts and can be partly adapted, to some extent, to reduced signal quality, e.g., by tuning threshold(s) based on recent signal values [10].

However, sEMG signal amplitude is highly sensitive to experimental conditions, particularly to motion artifacts and changes in the skin-sensor interface, motivating preprocessing strategies such as whitening filters followed by a nonlinear operator [11], or the Teager-Kaiser Energy Operator (TKEO) [12]. This simple operator, defined as , improves the SNR [13, 14] and remains widely used, e.g. in [14] for the control of an upper-arm exoskeleton designed to assist manipulation tasks and using a double-threshold strategy. The Extended TKEO (ETKEO) generalizes TKEO (as a matrix determinant) to signal segments of arbitrary length and improves onset detection accuracy [15]. However, this method remains vulnerable to some noise types, such as spikes, which can result in false detections.

To address these limitations, alternative approaches have been explored, focusing on the spectral content of the sEMG signal using time-frequency representations, such as the continuous wavelet transform [16–18]. Compared to amplitude-based methods, these approaches increase the amount of information by considering multiple frequency bands and resolutions. Although promising, even for low SNR signals, they are computationally demanding and primarily suited for offline processing.

Other methods instead rely on statistical analyses of the signal. They exploit the fact that relaxed and contracted muscles produce signals with distinct behaviors, translating into different statistical properties before and after onset [19]. A method based on the generalized likelihood ratio, identifying the point that best separates the pre- and post-onset probability density functions (PDFs), was proposed in [20]. Two offline methods applied to low-pass filtered EMG signals preprocessed with TKEO were proposed in [4]: one based on scree-plot elbow detection applied to the cumulative sum of occurrences of rectified TKEO values exceeding a threshold, and the other based on maximum profile likelihood estimation. More recently, an online-compatible method inspired by the likelihood ratio was developed to detect changes in a heteroskedasticity model of the signal [8].

Machine learning algorithms have also been investigated for onset detection [21, 22], motivated by the success of multi-muscle EMG-based motion recognition, primarily for prosthesis control [23]. Promising onset detection was achieved using a long short-term memory (LSTM) neural network [24]. However, machine learning approaches require large labeled datasets for training, which may not be available for all applications.

An alternative class of EMG onset detectors relies on entropy-based measures, quantifying the rate of information generation (Chapter 11.4 in [25]). Indeed, during muscle activation, the EMG signal transitions from irregular baseline activity, dominated by muscle and instrumentation noise, to structured activity driven by motor-unit firings [19]. Among entropy metrics, sample entropy (SampEn) [26] is widely used in EMG processing [27, 28]. Using a fixed similarity tolerance r makes the metric sensitive to both complexity and amplitude changes, producing a sharp variation around onset [29, 30]. It has successfully been applied to EMG onset detection, e.g., for hand- or arm-motions [29, 31, 32]. Fuzzy versions of SampEn have been used to replace the discrete similarity decision by a smooth similarity function, making it more suitable for the study of temporal variations of SampEn [27]. Such fuzzy functions include exponential [27, 33] and sigmoid [28, 34]. Across studies, SampEn showed superior performances on signals with low SNR or exhibiting spike-like artifacts.

Functional movements provide a practical basis for evaluating online onset detectors. Among demanding weight-bearing activities of daily living, sit-to-stand requires large knee extensor moments at movement initiation, primarily generated by the Rectus Femoris (RF), Vastus Lateralis (VL), and Vastus Medialis (VM) muscles [35]. Their activity exhibits predominantly monophasic activation patterns with a sharp rising phase [36] (see an example in Fig. 1), making fast onset detection important to capture the most dynamic part of the EMG signal, and thus making this motion a relevant benchmark for onset detection.

The contribution of this study is twofold. First, we propose a novel online EMG onset detector based on the time derivative of fixed-tolerance fuzzy SampEn, enabling earlier and more robust onset identification than SampEn thresholding. Second, to evaluate the proposed detector under near-real-life variability, we collected an experimental dataset of 1676 sEMG samples from the RF, VL, and VM muscles of both legs from six healthy participants performing sit-to-stand motions under a wide range of conditions. Complementarily, we generated a dataset of physiologically realistic synthetic EMG signals with known ground-truth onsets to assess sensitivity to sensor noise. The performance in terms of detection delay, percentage of missed detection, false detection rates and working range across signal and baseline noise conditions is compared with established sEMG onset detectors, respectively based on the Extended Teager-Kaiser Energy Operator (ETKEO) [15] and the fuzzy SampEn [34] reimplemeted for an online-compatible setting.

Methods

EMG onset detection algorithm

The proposed onset detector relies on the time derivative of the fixed-tolerance fuzzy SampEn computed over sliding windows of the signal. Intuitively, the derivative of SampEn captures the rate of change in signal complexity, enabling detection of transitions before the entropy itself crosses a threshold. Moreover, because SampEn rises steeply at contraction onset (see Fig. 1), its derivative forms a sharp burst immediately after onset that can be efficiently thresholded with reduced risk of false detections. This effect is further accentuated by the fast-rising profile of knee extensor EMG during demanding sit-to-stand motions, which is reflected in the SampEn values. The fuzzy template similarity mapping yields smoother variations of the entropy estimate across consecutive windows when signal properties fluctuate [28]. Surface EMG recordings

Participants

In accordance with the ethical agreement,1 we gathered six healthy adults (3 females and 3 males; age: 39 ± 11 years; weight: 68 ± 15 kg; height: 1.70 ± 0.08 m, BMI: 18–32). Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years and absence of known neurological or musculoskeletal disorders affecting the lower-limb. Participants were recruited from the laboratory community. All participants provided written informed consent prior to the experiments. Data collection was conducted on the CARTIGEN motion analysis platform2 at the University Hospital of Montpellier over one year starting November 2024. Each participant completed two sessions lasting about 2 hours each, which included 30 to 45 minutes of setup followed by approximately one hour of data collection. The study size was determined to obtain a sufficient number of trials for evaluating detector performance under varying signal conditions.

Experimental protocol

During each session, participants performed sit-to-stand-to-sit movements (starting from a seated position, standing up, and then sitting back down) under various conditions. These conditions included different chair heights, carried loads, and rising speeds, simulating daily activities requiring different effort levels. Muscle fatigue was minimized by spreading trials over two sessions and incorporating frequent breaks based on participant feedback.

EMG sensors (Trigno Avanti, Delsys Inc., Boston, MA) were placed on the RF, VL, VM, Biceps Femoris and Semitendinosus of each leg following SENIAM guidelines [40], on skin rubbed with 70% alcohol. Raw signals were sampled at rate Hz. A tubular medical band worn on both legs secured the sensors to minimize slippage. Lower-limb kinematics was captured at 240 Hz using an 8-camera motion capture system (Qualisys AB, Göteborg, Sweden). Reflective markers were positioned on participants’ lower limbs and pelvis according to Qualisys marker sets [41], with two additional markers placed on the iliac crests in the frontal plane to facilitate hip segment reconstruction during partial occlusions of the hip markers (mostly due to the added load). Marker trajectories were processed using Qualisys Track Manager (v2023.1) to retrieve body segment poses.

Each leg also carried one Trigno Avanti sensor in accelerometer mode, positioned laterally at mid-thigh height and recording 3D acceleration of the thigh segment at 963 Hz to detect sit-to-stand motion onsets. Ground reaction forces under each foot were recorded at 1000 Hz by performing the sit-to-stand motions on a stationary instrumented split-belt treadmill (Treadmetrix, Park City, UT, USA). All sensors were synchronized via a Delsys Trigger Module. The experimental setup is illustrated in Fig. 2.

The resulting 24 settings were randomized for each session. During each session, participants performed five repetitions of the baseline setting {chair height: 45 cm; rising speed: spontaneous; carried load: none} and one repetition of each of the other settings. This resulted in a maximum of 56 sit-to-stand motions recorded per participant over two sessions if no attempt failed. Participants were instructed to sit normally with their feet positioned comfortably apart and not to move their feet once the motion began. The initial foot position was not standardized to better reflect real-life conditions. To reduce variability due to arm movements, participants were asked to cross their arms over their chest or place them on their hips. Participants were guided through the settings with verbal cues at each sit-to-stand motion following a change in settings. Rest periods between rising motions and the timing of each rise were left to the participants’ discretion. The goal was to collect voluntary movements representative of daily activities while avoiding anticipatory muscle contractions. For the most challenging settings, participants were instructed to rise as much as possible before sitting back down if they could not complete the motion.

After recording, EMG data were visually inspected; channels with missing data (e.g., due to detached electrodes) or inconsistent signals (e.g., loose electrodes) were discarded. Recordings were then segmented into individual sit-to-stand-to-sit cycles, totaling 335 usable motions.

Motion onset was determined using the thigh accelerometers, which provide a higher sampling frequency and better synchronization with the EMG sensors (down to 1 ms error) than the motion capture system. The vertical component of acceleration was obtained by projection onto the gravity direction, estimated from baseline sitting acceleration prior to motion. A threshold equal to 15 times the standard deviation of baseline acceleration was used to define motion onset.

General description

Incomplete or failed trials were excluded, resulting in 1676 EMG samples from the RF, VM, and VL muscles of both legs (data from the Biceps Femoris and Semitendinosus were not used for onset detection). Maximum contraction amplitudes ranged from 18 to 2800 μ V (mean: 340 μ V, SD: 500 μ V). Baseline RMS noise – measured during sitting periods at least 200 ms prior to motion onset – ranged from 1.0 to 99 μ V (mean: 4.1 μ V, SD: 5.2 μ V). SNRs spanned 12-69 dB (mean: 32 dB, SD: 7 dB). Substantial variability in signal characteristics was observed across chair heights, loads, and subject morphologies.

Synthetic EMG generation

In spontaneous motion, ground truth EMG onset timing is unknown, and only the electromechanical delay can be estimated. Moreover, studying the effects of parameters such as signal-to-noise ratio, maximum amplitude, activation profile, and fiber recruitment is challenging, as these factors are not directly or independently controllable. Synthetic EMG signals allow independent manipulation of these parameters, making them valuable for evaluating onset detection methods, provided they reproduce the signal features that influence detector performance.

Three main strategies have been used to generate synthetic EMG signals:

Semi-synthetic signals: Constructed from experimental signals by concatenating baseline and contraction segments, creating partially natural signals with known artificial onset timing [29, 42]. However, the resulting onset exhibits an abrupt and unrealistic transition, making this approach not suitable.

Amplitude-modulated Gaussian noise: Since EMG signals can be approximated as Gaussian noise [19], fully synthetic contraction bursts can be generated via amplitude modulation (e.g., truncated Gaussian envelopes [11]). This approach allows the influence of burst shape to be studied, but Gaussian noise lacks the complexity required to evaluate methods relying on time-varying dynamics.

Biophysical models: Generate signals from detailed muscle fiber activation and volume-conduction models [15, 16]. When tuned to a specific muscle type and contraction profile, these models can produce realistic EMG signals. Although computationally demanding, recent open-source implementations have made this approach more accessible.

Based on these considerations, we adopted a physiologically based approach using the open-source semgsim package [43], selected among advanced open-source options [43, 44]. Given a defined system geometry – comprising muscle, adipose tissue, and skin –, muscle fiber electrical and mechanical properties, and a target muscle force profile, the simulator generates individual muscle fiber action potentials and their 3D propagation through tissue layers. Summed potentials recorded at the skin surface via virtual electrodes produce realistic differential EMG signals [5]. The underlying model follows the formulation in [45] and subsequent extensions.

Generation

semgsim simulates ideal EMG transmission to a noiseless surface sensor. Each synthetic contraction comprised 2 s of baseline (zero-amplitude, noiseless signal) followed by 2 s of active EMG. Simulation times ranged from 30 to 90 min on a Dell Precision laptop (Intel Xeon E, 12 cores @2.80 GHz).

Signals were generated at 1074 Hz to reduce computational load. They were subsequently low-pass filtered at 450 Hz and upsampled to 2148 Hz using polyphase filtering to match the experimental data. Ground-truth onset was defined as the first sample at which the noiseless signal exceeded times its maximum absolute amplitude. A total of 150 synthetic contractions was generated.

To simulate sensor noise, white Gaussian noise was added with RMS values up to 15 μ V (Delsys’ upper limit of acceptable signal quality). Five noise levels (1, 3, 7, 10, and 15 μ V) were used to study the influence of noise. The resulting noise amplitudes and corresponding SNR values (ranging from 2 to 74 dB and computed following [49]) covered and exceeded the experimental range (see Sec. 2.3.3). The resulting synthetic signals closely matched the experimental amplitude and frequency characteristics. Following Delsys’ recommendation that signals above 1.2 dB SNR are acceptable, samples below this threshold at 15 μ V of added noise were discarded. The final dataset contained 840 samples from 140 unique contractions, each evaluated at five noise levels. All samples were processed identically to the experimental signals.

Validation protocol

We evaluated the proposed detector through a two-stage validation protocol using the synthetic and experimental datasets presented in Sec. 2.3 and 2.4. First, we conducted a method characterization step to assess the effect of the main design choices, including an ablation study comparing SampEn, smoothed SampEn, single-threshold dSampEn, and the final double-threshold dSampEn; a sensitivity analysis of the threshold parameters; and a leave-one-subject-out cross-validation study. Second, the proposed method was compared with baseline detectors in terms of detection delay, missed detections, false detections, and robustness to baseline noise. Noise robustness was evaluated on the synthetic dataset, where the imposed baseline noise level was controlled, to determine each method’s working range.

The comparison was performed relative to two established methods: ETKEO [15] and SampEn [34]. SampEn provides the conceptual basis for our approach, while ETKEO is recognized for fast and reliable EMG onset detection.

Although ETKEO is computationally efficient and compatible with online applications, it is often used offline with thresholds retrospectively adjusted to each sample’s SNR and baseline noise [13, 15]. False detections are mitigated either by adding a duration threshold to the amplitude threshold [13] or, equivalently through post-processing by rejecting detections shorter than 30 ms [11, 15].

Since our focus is online operation, ETKEO detections were performed using an online configuration, with a fixed threshold across samples and a short-duration rejection threshold.

While this constraint may reduce the performance of the evaluated methods compared with offline implementations, particularly for high-SNR signals, it was imposed to ensure a fair comparison under identical online deployment conditions. Accordingly, the comparison should be interpreted as assessing performance and robustness under realistic online operating constraints rather than as an absolute benchmark obtained under individually optimized offline tuning.

No post-processing was applied to any of the evaluated methods. In particular, for the experimental dataset, where no ground truth exists for minor pre-motion activations (e.g., postural adjustments of small torso shifts), any detection not directly associated with the rising motion was conservatively counted as a false detection.

Valid detections

Detection delay depends on ground truth availability. For synthetic data, delay was measured relative to the ground-truth EMG onset of the noiseless signals. A detection was considered valid if it occurred after the detector’s minimum response time (induced by temporal thresholding and filtering) and before an arbitrary 500 ms timeout.

For experimental data, delay was measured relative to motion onset and reported as positive when detection preceded movement. Valid detections were limited to 200 ms prior to motion onset, corresponding to the upper bound of electromechanical delay in knee extensors [50, 51]. The same 500 ms timeout was applied.

If multiple detections occurred within the valid window, only the first detection was retained, while subsequent detections were ignored. This ensures a single onset event per trial and is consistent with the intended application: the onset detection event is designed to signal the start of the contraction, whereas its behavior during the contraction itself is not relevant.

Earlier detections were considered false detections and expressed relative to the duration of the pre-onset baseline segment, yielding a false detection rate of events per second and expressed in Hz. Because the tested detectors can trigger multiple times per motion and operate at different update rates, this metric enables fair comparison across methods and datasets, whereas a traditional false-positive percentage cannot be consistently defined under this counting scheme. Conversely, movements without any valid detection were reported as missed detections (percentage over the total number of movements).

Method tuning procedure

Parameters were tuned over the entire datasets, and the same parameter set was applied identically to all samples from either the experimental or the synthetic dataset. Specifically, for the experimental dataset, no subject-specific or muscle-specific tuning was performed. This setup was designed to imitate a realistic use of the detector, where calibration would typically be performed once prior to deployment over a period encompassing varying signal amplitudes and baseline noise levels. All methods were manually tuned on the experimental dataset to minimize detection delay and missed detections while maintaining acceptable false detection rates. Across 27 min of cumulative pre-onset baseline data, we empirically found that the false detection rate could not be reduced below 0.4 Hz without degrading delay performance or increasing missed detections.

This rate was considered manageable by a rejection mechanism, which would be implemented in a subsequent decision layer for a practical application. Indeed, it corresponds to the detector’s raw single-channel output and should not be interpreted as the expected false detection rate of a complete control system. Possible strategies include enforcing temporal consistency across multiple muscles, increasing persistence requirements, or combining information from both SampEn and dSampEn.

For fair comparison, parameters were tuned such that false detection rates were matched across methods (within 5%). Both ETKEO and dSampEn achieved 1% missed detections. Results

Method characterization

To characterize the detector, the performances in terms of detection delay, rate of false detections and percentage of missed detections were evaluated with three complementary analyses. First, an ablation study was conducted to show the benefits of derivation and our double-threshold on the original SampEn single thresholding detection method. Second, a sensitivity analysis to threshold variations was performed around the selected set of parameters. Finally, a leave-one-subject-out cross-validation analysis was performed to assess the generalizability of the selected parameters across subjects.

Ablation study

The parameters of each detector variant were optimized using a grid search around manually identified operating regions. The search was performed over the SampEn window length and detection thresholds parameters.

The evaluated variants were: the original single-threshold SampEn detector, the same detector with Savitzky-Golay smoothing, a single-threshold detector applied to the SampEn derivative, and the proposed double-threshold dSampEn detector.

Table 1 summarizes the resulting performance on the experimental dataset. Smoothing the SampEn signal did not improve detection timing and slightly increased the missed-detection rate. In contrast, using the SampEn derivative improved detection speed and substantially reduced the percentage of missed detections (from 4.0 % to 1.6 %) while maintaining comparable detection performance under the same false-detection constraint, indicating that dSampEn more effectively captures onset-related transitions. The proposed double-threshold dSampEn detector further reduced the missed-detection rate to 1 % while preserving similar detection timing. Overall, these results suggest that the main improvement arises from the derivative representation, whereas the double-threshold criterion provides an additional gain in robustness by rejecting weaker or shorter transient fluctuations.

Overall, the sensitivity analysis illustrates the expected tradeoff between early detection and robustness to erroneous detections. Lowering or makes the detector more sensitive to small or brief increases in the dSampEn signal. This generally improves detection timing, with detections occurring earlier with respect to motion onset, but also increases the probability of false detections from noise fluctuations. Conversely, increasing thresholds makes the detector more conservative: false detections are reduced, but the detection delay and the missed detection rate are adversely affected.

The duration threshold has a complementary effect. Increasing improves robustness against transient fluctuations, at the cost of an additional temporal delay and increasing the missed detection rate, since it may reject short but valid onset-related derivative bursts.

The selected parameter set lies in a region where the False and Missed detections are within the predefined limits, while retaining a good detection delay. Importantly, some neighboring parameter values produce comparable results, indicating that the performance of the method is not the consequence of a narrowly tuned parameter combination, but rather corresponds to a stable operating region of the detector.

Cross-validation

In order to assess the generalizability of the selected parameters to unseen data, a leave-one-subject-out cross-validation analysis was performed. For each fold, the method was tuned using data from five of the six participants and evaluated on the remaining subject. This subject-wise validation strategy is particularly relevant given the marked morphological differences across participants, as it assesses whether parameters selected on one set of morphologies can transfer to a previously unseen individual.

Parameter tuning was performed using a randomly sampled subset of the parameter grid over the previously identified working ranges for the window length and the three detection thresholds. Specifically, 100 candidate parameter sets were evaluated, including the previously identified best-performing set, 30 neighboring parameter sets, and 69 additional samples drawn from the wider parameter space. Consequently, the selected parameter set in each fold should be interpreted as a near-optimal operating point within a broader region of similarly performing parameter combinations, as supported by the sensitivity analysis.

Discussion

Generalization of parameters

The detector was evaluated using a single fixed parameter set for all experimental and synthetic samples to reproduce a generalized online-use scenario. In this setting, movement onset must be detected from incoming EMG signals without sample-specific tuning. The results therefore illustrate how the method performs under fixed-parameter conditions. In this section, we discuss parameter transferability, the influence of inter-subject variability, and the extent to which a common configuration can serve as a practical default for online detection.

The full-dataset tuning validation showed that a single parameter set provided reliable detection across the complete dataset, despite substantial variability across muscles, subjects, sessions, and conditions. The selected configuration therefore appears suitable for a broad range of EMG signal characteristics without sample-specific adjustment. The sensitivity analysis further supports this conclusion by showing that good performance was not restricted to a narrow optimum but belonged to a relatively stable operating region. This interpretation is also supported by the successful transfer of parameters selected on experimental recordings to synthetic signals, suggesting that the selected operating point was not specific to the calibration samples when comparable signal amplitude and baseline-noise ranges were represented.

The leave-one-subject-out cross-validation further showed that parameter sets obtained from the training subjects broadly transferred to unseen subjects. For most held-out subjects, the transferred parameters provided acceptable to good detection performance, with low false-detection rates and limited missed detections. However, the balance between detection delay, false detections, and missed detections remained dependent on individual signal characteristics. Differences in EMG amplitude, baseline noise, electrode placement, morphology, and SNR can make the same absolute thresholds more or less permissive, which is expected given the large variability among participants.

Subject 6 represented the main limitation of this cross-subject transfer. This participant exhibited the largest EMG amplitudes and the lowest baseline noise in the dataset. When this signal profile was absent from the tuning dataset, the transferred thresholds became too permissive, leading to increased false-detection rates and earlier detections. These observations are consistent with the sensitivity analysis, which showed that the threshold parameters primarily control the trade-off between detection latency and robustness.

To better understand this result, a complementary leave-one-subject-out analysis was performed excluding Subject 6. Despite retaining substantial inter-subject variability (height: 1.70± 0.04,m; BMI: 25± 5), the resulting cross-validation performance was more homogeneous. This supports the interpretation that the less favorable results observed in the six-subject analysis were mainly driven by the distinctive signal characteristics of Subject 6 rather than by a general lack of parameter transferability.

Overall, the results suggest that reliable fixed-parameter operation requires a tuning dataset that adequately represents the signal characteristics expected during deployment. In practice, this may involve tuning the detector using a calibration sequence recorded from the target subject when available, or from subjects with similar morphology and EMG characteristics. More generally, the full-dataset tuning, sensitivity analysis, and cross-validation results indicate that the proposed method operates within a stable parameter region, while markedly different signal profiles may benefit from limited additional calibration.

Detection delay results

On the experimental dataset, all three methods achieved satisfactory performance for the intended application. The main differences concerned the proportion of pre-motion detections, which was highest for dSampEn, and robustness to baseline noise, as highlighted by the synthetic data analysis.

The proposed algorithm was evaluated against online re-implementations of two existing approaches. These online-oriented adaptations led to observations that differ from some previous reports. In particular, when tuned for minimal detection delay, SampEn became more sensitive to perturbations such as motion artifacts, contrary to observations reported in [29, 31]. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in signal conditions and tuning objectives, including the higher SNR range of our datasets, the use of shorter windows, and the adoption of global rather than contraction-specific parameters.

While the delay improvement of dSampEn over SampEn was modest, dSampEn substantially reduced missed detections. This advantage became more pronounced in the synthetic dataset, which included higher baseline-noise levels. These results suggest that SampEn degrades more rapidly with increasing noise than either dSampEn or ETKEO. Consistently, most SampEn missed detections occurred when baseline noise remained continuously above the detection threshold, preventing threshold crossings.

All methods exhibited a relatively wide dispersion of detection delays, reflecting the large variability in contraction profiles. On the synthetic dataset, mean delays were substantially larger than median delays because slow ramp-up contractions (up to 1.2 s) reached detectable amplitudes much later than faster ramps (as low as 0.2 s), thereby skewing the mean despite otherwise successful early detections.

For dSampEn, mean onset detection occurred approximately 100 ms after ground truth on the synthetic dataset and 36 ms before motion onset on the experimental dataset. These values are consistent with reported EMD ranges of approximately 100-200 ms during demanding dynamic movements, indicating that detection generally occurred before or near the onset of force generation.

On the experimental recordings, some events classified as false detections may correspond to genuine EMG activity unrelated to the sit-to-stand onset itself, such as anticipatory postural adjustments or hip-flexion activity involving the biarticular RF. Consistent with this interpretation, the RF accounted for 47% of all false detections. Moreover, muscle activations generally occurred nearly simultaneously, with the RF tending to activate slightly earlier than the VL and VM, although these differences were not statistically significant (p>0.05).

Resilience to sensor noise on synthetic data

The synthetic dataset deliberately over-represents high baseline noise and low-SNR conditions to probe detector limits, which explains the higher false-detection rates observed relative to the experimental data. Across the tested noise range, dSampEn maintained the most consistent balance between detection delay and erroneous detections.

A notable difference between datasets lies in how false detections evolve with increasing noise amplitude. In the experimental data, trends were broadly similar across methods, whereas the synthetic data revealed method-specific behavior. For dSampEn, false detections increased gradually with baseline noise (from 7 μ V onward), whereas ETKEO exhibited a more abrupt increase at the highest noise levels, eventually leading to detection failure. This discrepancy likely reflects characteristics of real EMG signals that are not fully captured by the synthetic model.

To further assess the effect of baseline noise, ETKEO was retuned to match the false-detection rate of dSampEn at 7 μ V. Under this configuration, ETKEO achieved delays approaching those of dSampEn, although delays remained significantly higher up to 3 μ V (p ≤ 0.02) and became comparable only at 7 μ V (p=0.6). Beyond this level, false detections increased sharply.

Overall, ETKEO can achieve delays comparable to dSampEn when tuned for minimal latency, but over a narrower range of baseline noise conditions. Extending this operating range requires higher thresholds, which increase detection delay. In contrast, dSampEn exhibits a more gradual degradation with increasing noise, resulting in more predictable performance across the tested conditions.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the proposed detector may exhibit reduced performance for slow, ramp-like contractions, as these produce less pronounced transitions in SampEn and its derivative. However, such activation profiles are uncommon in quadriceps activations during sit-to-stand movements, which typically involve relatively rapid recruitment.

Second, the simulator does not reproduce several physiological and experimental perturbations commonly encountered in practice. These include anticipatory muscle activations, incidental quadriceps recruitment during postural control, skin-electrode interface changes, motion artifacts, and inter-muscle cross-talk. Incorporating such effects into future simulation frameworks would enable a more comprehensive assessment of detector robustness and facilitate the development of targeted false-detection mitigation strategies.

Future work will focus on integrating explicit false-detection rejection mechanisms and evaluating the proposed detector within a real-time lower-limb exoskeleton control framework. Extending the evaluation to more complex locomotor tasks, such as stair ascent and descent, would also help assess the generalizability of the approach beyond sit-to-stand movements.

Conclusions

This study presents an online-compatible EMG onset detection method based on the time derivative of fuzzy SampEn, specifically designed for detecting knee-extensor activation during sit-to-stand movements. The method does not require prior knowledge of maximum contraction amplitude or precise baseline-noise characteristics.

Compared with online-compatible baseline methods, the proposed approach achieved earlier detections while maintaining a near-zero missed-detection rate and a low incidence of false detections across a wide range of signal amplitudes and baseline-noise conditions. Validation on a large experimental dataset, complemented by controlled synthetic data analyses, demonstrated robust performance across substantial inter- and intra-subject variability.

These results suggest that the proposed detector constitutes a practical solution for low-latency EMG onset detection in online applications. In particular, its robustness and limited calibration requirements make it well suited for integration into assistive-device control frameworks, such as lower-limb exoskeletons, where timely onset detection can contribute to responsive and transparent assistance from the initiation of movement.

The concept of entropy was originally defined in the context of nonlinear time series analysis and chaotic dynamics [25]. Entropies (such as Kolmogorov-Sinai and Renyi entropies) quantify the rate of information generation from a given signal. Various numerical methods have been proposed over the past four decades to estimate these measures. The approximate entropy (ApEn) was introduced in [52] as a statistical measure of time-series complexity and predictability, with the advantage of being applicable to both nonlinear deterministic and stochastic systems. It was later refined by the introduction of SampEn for cardiovascular data analysis [26, 53]. SampEn is an unbiased estimator of Renyi entropy of order 2 [25, 54]. It is more robust for short sequences and improves the consistency of regularity estimations [26, 55, 56].

SampEn quantifies the regularity of a signal by counting repeating signal patterns (templates) within a window and assessing whether matching templates of small length keep matching when slightly extended. If repeating templates can be found and continue to appear even when slightly extended, it indicates a certain degree of regularity in the signal. In [53], SampEn is defined as “the negative natural logarithm of the conditional probability that a dataset of length N, having repeated itself within a tolerance r for m points, will also repeat itself for m+1 points, without allowing self-matches”. Originally applied to physiological signals to analyze their complexity over time (see, e.g., [26–28]), the potential of SampEn as an onset detection metric was revealed by fixing the tolerance parameter r across all windows. This modified fixed SampEn, used in the present work, makes it sensitive to both complexity and amplitude variations.
