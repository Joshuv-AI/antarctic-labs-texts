The effect of combination therapy on Bevacizumab, Carmustine, and Metformin chemotherapy drugs on the fluctuating performance expression of TLR2, TLR6, and IL-6 genes in the cell line of brain glioblastoma
Authors: Seyedeh Elham Norollahi, Bahman Yousefi, Shahrokh Yousefzadeh-Chabok, Fatemeh Nejatifar, Mohammad Samadian, Mehdi Evazalipour, Mohamad Eftekhary, Masoud Faghih Akhlaghi, Ebrahim Mirzajani, Ali Rashidy-pour, Ali Akbar Samadani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveGlioblastoma, one of the most aggressive brain tumors, is distinguished by its resistance to standard treatments. So, studying practical therapeutic methods is of great importance. In this project, the effects of combined chemotherapy drugs, including bevacizumab, carmustine, and metformin, on the expression of IL-6, TLR2, and TLR6 in the glioblastoma cell line of U87MG were investigated.MethodsU87MG cells were treated with bevacizumab, carmustine, and metformin alone or in combined groups. Cell viability was measured using the MTT assay. Apoptotic rates and cell cycle were analyzed by flow cytometry. Gene and protein expression levels of TLR2, TLR6, and IL-6 were evaluated by Real-Time PCR and Western blotting, respectively. In addition, bioinformatic analyses, including protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction and pathway enrichment, were performed using Cytoscape (GeneMANIA) and R (ClusterProfiler).ResultsThe triple-drug combination(bevacizumab, carmustine, and metformin )significantly reduced cell viability and induced a substantial increase in apoptosis and G0/G1 cell cycle arrest. Treatment also led to a marked downregulation of TLR2, TLR6, and IL-6 gene expression. Bioinformatic analysis revealed these genes to be central in pathways associated with immune regulation, inflammation, and tumor progression.ConclusionThe combination of bevacizumab, carmustine, and metformin exerts a potent synergistic antitumor effect in U87MG glioblastoma cells through modulation of inflammation-associated genes, induction of apoptosis, and disruption of cell cycle progression. These findings provide a mechanistic rationale for multi-targeted combination therapy in glioblastoma and support further preclinical evaluation.

Introduction

Glioblastoma is acknowledged as one of the deadliest and most aggressive types of brain cancer [1]. Glioblastoma presents formidable treatment problems due to its invasive growth patterns and fast proliferation. The disease has a poor prognosis, with a median survival span of about 15 months [2]. The poor success rate of current therapies, such as radiation, chemotherapy, and surgical resection, underscores the pressing need for novel and efficient therapeutic approaches. In this way, combination therapy is one of the most important types of therapeutic strategies besides the routine method. So, knowing the effect of different drugs in combination therapy is the first line in choosing the drugs. In this account, the Bevacizumab drug, which is a comprehensive chemical combination in glioblastoma therapy, has a strong effect. Correspondingly, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), a crucial protein involved in angiogenesis, the process by which new blood capillaries form from old ones, is the target of the monoclonal antibody bevacizumab. Excessive VEGF synthesis in tumors causes aberrant blood vessel formation, which promotes tumor growth and metastasis. Bevacizumab starves the tumor of oxygen and nutrients by blocking VEGF, which lowers blood flow to the tumor [3]. In patients with recurrent glioblastoma, its anti-angiogenic activity can enhance progression-free survival by slowing tumor development. Bevacizumab’s effect on overall survival is still controversial, despite its potential to increase progression-free survival rates.Another important drug in glioblastoma treatment is carmustine. Carmustine is an alkylating drug that damages cancer cells’ DNA directly to achieve its effects. DNA strands cross-link as a result of the addition of alkyl groups to DNA [4]. Damage like this breaks down the cell cycle and causes apoptosis, or programmed cell death, especially in tumor-like cells that divide quickly. Because carmustine is so efficient against malignant cells, it is frequently used in conjunction with other therapies for glioblastoma. Nevertheless, its usage may be restricted because of its significant adverse effects, which include myelosuppression [5]. The third drug used in this project was metformin. Although its primary use in treating type 2 diabetes is well recognized, metformin has recently drawn interest due to its possible anti-cancer effects. A crucial modulator of cellular energy homeostasis, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is stimulated by metformin [6]. This activation can cause apoptosis and limit the growth of cancer cells. Metformin may also lower insulin levels and increase insulin sensitivity, both of which can benefit tumor metabolism. Because metformin can increase the effectiveness of conventional chemotherapy while reducing adverse effects, its use as an adjuvant medication in the treatment of glioblastoma is particularly promising [7]. The effects of combination therapy on the different signaling pathways, like toll-like receptors (TLRs) with eclectic genes, are of great importance. TLRs a key players in the recognition of pathogen-associated molecular patterns and the initiation of inflammatory responses and are part of the innate immune system [8]. TLR2 is one of the key genes of this signaling pathway. Through inflammatory pathways, this receptor is frequently linked to the promotion of tumor growth. Increased synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines that aid in tumor survival and proliferation may result from its activation. Another gene is TLR6. Like TLR2, TLR6 is involved in immune response modulation. Its role in inflammation can create an environment that is conducive to tumor growth. TLR2 and TLR6 have been associated with immune response control within tumors, which affects the body’s interaction with cancerous cells. Another important cytokine is interleukin-6 (IL-6). IL-6 has several functions in the pathobiology of cancer. Patients with glioblastoma frequently have elevated IL-6 levels, which are associated with a poor prognosis. IL-6 contributes to an inflammatory milieu that aids in the growth of tumors by encouraging the survival and multiplication of tumor cells. A method to slow the growth of tumors and enhance patient outcomes might be to target IL-6 signaling pathways [9]. Therapies may be able to tip the scales from a pro-tumorigenic environment to one that boosts anti-tumor immunity by blocking IL-6 activity. The combination therapy of bevacizumab, carmustine, and metformin may have synergistic effects on TLR2, TLR6, and IL-6 gene expression levels [10]. By reducing inflammatory signals in the tumor microenvironment, the combined effects of these medications may result in the downregulation of TLR2 and TLR6 expression. The pro-tumorigenic effects linked to these receptors may be lessened by this lowering. When bevacizumab and carmustine are combined with metformin, the drug’s capacity to reduce IL-6 levels may be strengthened. This combination may aid in the reduction of inflammation that encourages tumor growth by inhibiting signaling pathways or IL-6 production [11]. This combination treatment’s ability to modify TLR expression may cause the immune system to become less supportive of tumor growth and more supportive of anti-tumor immunity. This change may enhance the immune system’s ability to identify and destroy cancer cells. An essential step towards understanding the mechanisms of action of combination therapy for glioblastoma multiforme is to examine the effects of these drugs on the expression of TLR2, TLR6, and IL-6 genes [12]. While the protumorigenic roles of TLR2, TLR6, and IL-6 in glioblastoma have been documented, there is limited evidence on how these inflammatory mediators respond to novel combination therapies, including bevacizumab, carmustine, and metformin. Understanding whether these therapeutic regimens can suppress TLR-mediated immune evasion may provide a new rationale for combination approaches in glioblastoma treatment [13]. Comprehending these interplays not only advances our understanding of the biology of glioblastoma but also opens the door to the creation of more potent therapeutic approaches that target the tumor cells as well as the surrounding milieu. In the end, these developments, particularly combination therapy with chemotherapy drugs, could lead to higher survival rates and better quality of life for those suffering from this terrible illness [14, 15]. In this study, we investigated the impact of combined therapy with the chemotherapeutic drugs bevacizumab, carmustine, and metformin on the expression fluctuations of TLR2, TLR6, and IL-6 genes in the glioblastoma cell line.

Materials and methods

1- Preparation of the desired cell line

This study was approved under the ethics code IR.SEMUMS.REC.1402.035. The human glioblastoma cell line U87MG was obtained from the Pasteur Institute of Iran. Cells were cultured in dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C, 5% CO₂, and 95% humidity under standard conditions. Once the culture reached approximately 80–90% confluence, adherent cells were detached using 0.05% trypsin-EDTA, centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in fresh medium. Cell viability and number were determined by trypan blue exclusion using a Neubauer chamber. All procedures were performed under sterile conditions.

2- MTT test for the cell line and calculating the IC50

The cytotoxic effects of bevacizumab, carmustine, and metformin, individually and in combination, were assessed by the MTT assay. U87MG cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well and incubated for 24 h for attachment. Drug treatment groups were as follows: group 1: bevacizumab, group 2: metformin, group 3:carmustine, group 4: carmustine + bevacizumab, group 5: carmustine + metformin, group 6: bevacizumab + metformin, group 7: bevacizumab + carmustine + metformin, and group 8: control. After 24 and 48 h of treatment, MTT solution (Promega, USA) was added to each well (50 µL/well) and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in 200 µL DMSO, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an ELISA microplate reader. Cell viability (%) was calculated relative to the control group, and the IC₅₀ (drug concentration required to inhibit 50% of cell proliferation) was determined from dose-response curves. To evaluate drug interactions, the Fractional Inhibitory Concentration (FIC) index was calculated using the formula: FIC (B with the compound in A substance IC50)/(A substance IC50)+ (A with the compound in B substance IC50)/(B substance IC50).

Statistical analyses for IC₅₀ and cell viability were performed using SPSS software (t-test or one-way ANOVA), with significance set at p < 0.05.

3- Examining the rate of cell proliferation

The MTT assay was also used to assess cell proliferation rates. After seeding 1 × 10⁴ cells/well in 96-well plates and allowing 24 h for adhesion, cells were treated with the indicated drug concentrations and combinations for 24 and 48 h. Following treatment, the media were removed, wells were washed with PBS, and MTT reagent was added and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. Formazan crystals formed by metabolically active cells were solubilized in DMSO (200 µL/well), and absorbance was read at 570 nm. The absorbance values reflected the metabolic activity of viable cells, and the percentage of proliferation was calculated as the ratio of treated to control absorbance. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

4- Determination of apoptotic cells by flow cytometry (cell apoptosis)

Apoptosis in glioblastoma (U87MG) cells was evaluated using the Dead Cell Apoptosis Kit with Annexin V-FITC (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. Cells (1 × 10⁵ per well) were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with each drug or its combinations at concentrations corresponding to the respective IC₅₀ values for 24 and 48 h. After treatment, cells were washed twice with cold PBS, resuspended in 500 µL of binding buffer, and incubated with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) in the dark at room temperature for 15 min. Fluorescence signals were measured using a flow cytometer (BD FACScan, USA) equipped with CellQuest software. Cells positive for Annexin V and negative for PI were considered early apoptotic, whereas Annexin V⁺/PI⁺ cells were categorized as late apoptotic. The percentage of apoptotic cells was calculated for each treatment group.

5- Checking the cell cycle (cell cycle)

Cell cycle distribution was analyzed by propidium iodide (PI) staining followed by flow cytometry. U87MG cells (1 × 10⁴ per well) were cultured in 96-well plates and treated with single or combined drugs for 24–48 h. After treatment, cells were collected, fixed in cold 80% ethanol for 15 min at 4 °C, and then washed twice with PBS. Fixed cells were incubated with PI/RNase staining buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 30 min on ice. Samples were analyzed by flow cytometry at 488 nm excitation and 570 nm emission wavelengths. The proportion of cells in G₀/G₁, S, and G₂/M phases was determined using FlowJo software (version 10) based on DNA content histograms.

6- Preparation for examining gene expression

For gene expression studies, 200,000 U87MG cells were cultured in 6-well plates. After the cells were attached to the bottom of the flask, they were treated with the indicated drugs at IC₅₀ concentrations for 24 and 48 h. In the end, cells were trypsinized and transferred into free RNAse and DNAse microtubes and washed with PBS buffer. After centrifugation, RNA extraction agent solution was added to the precipitate.

7- RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and quantitative analysis

Total RNA was extracted from treated and control U87MG cells using the RNX-Plus reagent (EX6101, SinaClon, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 1 × 10⁶ cells were lysed in 1 mL of RNX-Plus reagent, and the RNA phase was isolated by chloroform extraction and precipitation with isopropanol. The RNA pellet was washed with 75% ethanol, air-dried, and Dissolved in RNase-free water. RNA concentration and purity were assessed spectrophotometrically using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and samples with A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 were used for further analysis. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yekta Tajhiz, Iran) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The reverse transcription reaction was carried out at 42 °C for 60 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 70 °C for 10 min. The synthesized cDNA was stored at − 20 °C until further use.

8- Primers

Specific primers for TLR2, TLR6, IL-6, and the internal control gene GAPDH were designed using AlleleID v6.0 software and synthesized commercially (Macrogen, South Korea). The sequences of the primers are presented in Table 1. GAPDH was used as an endogenous reference to normalize gene expression levels.

9- Real-time PCR

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the SYBR Green Master Mix (Yekta Tajhiz, Iran) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Each 20-µL reaction mixture contained 10 µL of SYBR Green Master Mix, 1 µL of forward primer, 1 µL of reverse primer, 2 µL of cDNA template, and 6 µL of nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, followed by 40 amplification cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 60 °C for 20 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. Melting curve analysis was performed from 72 °C to 95 °C to verify product specificity. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the internal reference gene. All reactions were conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

10- Western blot analysis

Western blotting was performed with minor modifications to previously described protocols [16]. U87MG cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors, and lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford Protein Assay Kit (DNAbioTech, Iran). Equal amounts of protein (20 µg per lane) were mixed with 2× Laemmli sample buffer, boiled for 5 min, and separated on SDS-PAGE gels. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA) and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in 0.1% Tween 20 for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against IL-6, TLR2, TLR6, and β-actin (Abcam, UK), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system and quantified by densitometry using Gel Analyzer software (NIH, USA). Protein expression levels were normalized to β-actin and expressed as fold changes relative to the control group [17].

11- Protein-Protein Interaction Analysis (PPI)

To explore the potential molecular interactions of the studied genes, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using Cytoscape software (version 3.102) and the GeneMANIA plugin. The target genes TLR2, TLR6, and TLR7 were used as input queries. Interactions were predicted based on co-expression, physical interactions, shared pathways, co-localization, and common protein domains. The top 20 interacting proteins with the highest connection scores were selected for further analysis. The final PPI network consisted of 23 nodes and 381 edges, highlighting the central role of the selected genes in immune and inflammatory pathways.

12- Enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler package in R software (version 4.31) to identify Gene Ontology (GO) terms associated with the genes in the PPI network. Enrichment was assessed across three categories: Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF), using a significance threshold of p < 0.05. The enrich GO function was used to identify significantly enriched terms, and results were visualized using dot plots generated in R. These analyses provided insights into the key biological processes and signaling pathways involving TLR2, TLR6, and TLR7 and their associated proteins.

Typically, the flow cytometry chart for this group would show a low percentage of apoptotic cells, as no therapeutic agents were applied to induce cell death. The majority of cells would be in the viable (non-apoptotic) region of the chart, with only a small number of cells displaying early or late apoptosis or necrosis. Bevacizumab

The chart for bevacizumab treatment indicates a slight increase in apoptotic cell population compared to the control (Fig. 2-b). Bevacizumab is not directly cytotoxic but works by starving the tumor of nutrients and oxygen, which can lead to apoptosis over time. The flow cytometry chart showed an increase in both early and late apoptotic cell populations, but likely not as high as cytotoxic drugs.

Metformin

The metformin treatment chart indicates a slight increase in apoptotic cells, suggesting that metformin may effectively induce apoptosis in glioblastoma cells (Fig. 2-c). Metformin can induce apoptosis through pathways involving AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation and inhibiting mitochondrial respiration, which causes energy stress in cancer cells. The effect may not be as strong as conventional chemotherapy, but increased apoptosis would be expected.

Carmustine

The carmustine chart shows a notable rise in apoptosis, reflecting its effectiveness as a chemotherapeutic agent (Fig. 2-d). Carmustine’s mechanism involves DNA alkylation, which can lead to cell cycle arrest and, subsequently, cytotoxicity, leading to both early and late apoptosis in tumor cells.

Bevacizumab + Metformin

In this group, the flow cytometry chart showed a higher percentage of apoptotic cells compared to either treatment alone (Fig. 2-e). The combination leads to synergistic effects, where the metabolic and angiogenic pathways are simultaneously targeted, leading to increased apoptosis.

Bevacizumab + Carmustine

This chart displays a substantial increase in apoptosis, indicating that the combination of bevacizumab and carmustine may be particularly effective (Fig. 2-f). The dual action of reducing tumor blood supply and inducing DNA damage can significantly enhance apoptosis in glioblastoma cells.

Metformin + Carmustine

The combination of metformin and carmustine is expected to induce a substantial increase in apoptotic cell death (Fig. 2-g). Metformin’s metabolic disruption, combined with carmustine’s direct DNA damage, acted synergistically to induce higher apoptosis rates. The flow cytometry chart for this group showed a significant population of both necrotic and late apoptotic cells.

Metformin + Bevacizumab + Carmustine

This chart illustrates the highest level of apoptosis among all treatment groups (Fig. 2-h). The triple combination therapy appeared to maximize the apoptotic effects, leveraging the strengths of all three drugs to target multiple pathways involved in glioblastoma cell survival. Each drug targets a different aspect of tumor physiology: bevacizumab inhibits angiogenesis, metformin induces metabolic stress, and carmustine causes direct DNA damage. Conclusively, the flow cytometry chart for this group showed a marked increase in necrotic, early, and late apoptotic cells, with significantly fewer viable cells remaining. In summary, the apoptosis analysis demonstrates that all treatments, particularly the combinations, significantly enhance apoptosis in glioblastoma tumor cells compared to the control. The findings suggest that combining therapies may offer an effective strategy for treating glioblastoma.

Discussion

Glioblastoma is among the most aggressive and complex malignancies, presenting significant therapeutic challenges. Key obstacles include the inability of targeted drugs to effectively penetrate the blood-brain barrier and the uncontrolled proliferation of cancer cells driven by extensive angiogenesis. Comprehensive investigations into the efficacy of approved monotherapies and combination therapies, alongside their impact on the expression of various genes and proteins involved in key signaling pathways associated with Glioblastoma, have yielded promising insights. In this study, we examined the effects of a combined treatment regimen consisting of bevacizumab, carmustine, and metformin on the expression of TLR2, TLR6, and IL-6 at both the gene and protein levels within the TLR signaling pathway in glioblastoma cell lines. Our findings indicate significant alterations in gene and protein expression, induction of apoptosis, and modulation of the cell cycle, highlighting the therapeutic potential of this combination strategy [18]. Moreover, according to other studies in comparison with our study, both gene expression analyses and Western blot results underscore the enhanced efficacy of combination therapy compared to monotherapies [19–22]. Notably, bevacizumab, an anti-angiogenic agent, significantly downregulated TLR2, TLR6, and IL-6 protein levels relative to the control group (p < 0.001). Importantly, our results are confirmed according to other results of different studies [13, 23]. However, the moderate degree of suppression suggests that while bevacizumab effectively targets vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), its impact on inflammatory signaling remains limited when administered as a monotherapy, exactly like in our study [24]. These findings align with its well-established role in reducing tumor vascularization [25]. Further studies are required to determine its precise role in immune modulation. Among the investigated drugs in this study, carmustine exhibited the most potent inhibitory effect among single-drug treatments, significantly reducing the expression of TLR2, TLR6, and IL-6 (p < 0.001).Interestingly, the obtained results of our study match those of similar studies [22, 26]. This strong suppression highlights carmustine’s dual function as a cytotoxic agent and an inhibitor of tumor-promoting inflammatory signaling. The profound inhibitory effect further supports its application in glioblastoma therapy, although its toxicity remains a clinical concern [27, 28]. Metformin, another agent examined in this study, led to a moderate reduction in TLR2, TLR6, and IL-6 levels (p < 0.01). While metformin’s primary function as a metabolic modulator limits its independent impact, its combination with other drugs demonstrated significant synergistic effects. This result from our study notably fits with the results of similar studies [19–21, 29, 30]. These findings align with previous reports on metformin’s ability to modify the tumor microenvironment through AMPK activation. Combination therapies demonstrated synergistic effects in significantly reducing TLR2, TLR6, and IL-6 expression [31]. The triple-drug combination of bevacizumab, metformin, and carmustine resulted in the most substantial suppression (p < 0.001), exceeding the effects of single-drug treatments. Additionally, the combination of metformin and carmustine exhibited comparable efficacy, with a significant reduction in inflammatory markers. The triple-drug therapy achieved the most pronounced suppression, nearly abolishing the expression of TLR2, TLR6, and IL-6 (p < 0.001). This outcome underscores the potential of multi-drug regimens to effectively target inflammatory pathways and disrupt the tumor microenvironment. Comparative analyses between treatment groups confirmed the superior efficacy of combination therapies, particularly the triple-drug regimen, in reducing tumor-promoting inflammatory signaling. However, further in vivo evaluation is necessary to confirm the impact of these findings within the complexity of the glioblastoma tumor microenvironment. Flow cytometry data provided crucial insights into apoptotic responses and cell proliferation in glioblastoma cells under different treatment conditions. Monotherapy outcomes varied, with bevacizumab inducing a slight increase in apoptotic cell populations, reflecting its role in depriving the tumor of nutrients and oxygen. However, its limited pro-apoptotic effect suggests that angiogenesis inhibition alone may be insufficient to induce substantial cell death [32]. Carmustine induced a significant increase in apoptosis, consistent with its cytotoxic mechanism via DNA alkylation, more advanced supporting its role in glioblastoma therapy. Metformin exhibited a moderate apoptotic effect, likely due to its ability to disrupt mitochondrial function and induce metabolic stress. While metformin is less potent as a standalone therapy, its potential as an adjunctive agent in combination treatments is evident. Dual-drug combinations yielded significant improvements in apoptotic responses compared to monotherapies. The complementary mechanisms of bevacizumab (anti-angiogenic), carmustine (cytotoxic), and metformin (metabolic modulation) likely contributed to these effects. Notably, the triple-drug combination exhibited the highest level of apoptosis induction, leading to a substantial increase in both early and late apoptotic populations. These findings emphasize the importance of targeting multiple pathways simultaneously to maximize therapeutic efficacy. Cell cycle analysis further validated these results. All treatments induced varying degrees of G0/G1 arrest, indicating growth suppression. The triple-drug therapy resulted in the most substantial arrest (81.89% of cells in G0/G1), demonstrating comprehensive disruption of the cell cycle. A significant decrease in the S-phase population further emphasized the combination therapy’s ability to suppress DNA replication and tumor proliferation [33]. Bioinformatics analysis provided additional insights into the observed therapeutic effects. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis identified key genes such as MYD88, TLR4, and CXCL8, which play central roles in inflammatory and immune pathways. Gene ontology enrichment further highlighted the involvement of TLR2, TLR6, and IL-6 in NF-kB signaling and cytokine-mediated responses. The substantial reduction in these genes and their interacting proteins by combination therapies suggests a shift in the tumor microenvironment towards reduced inflammation and enhanced immune surveillance. This is particularly relevant for glioblastoma, where immune evasion contributes to therapy resistance. Overall, these findings highlight the promising potential of combination therapies in glioblastoma management. The triple-drug combination offers a multi-targeted approach that simultaneously suppresses tumor cell survival and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. This strategy is particularly relevant for glioblastoma, where monotherapies often fail due to the tumor’s aggressive and heterogeneous nature. While preclinical results appear promising, translating these findings into clinical applications requires careful evaluation. Potential toxicity concerns, particularly side effects associated with carmustine and bevacizumab, must be carefully weighed against their therapeutic benefits. Clinical trials are essential to optimize dosing regimens and assess long-term outcomes [34]. In conclusion, this study demonstrates the potential of bevacizumab, carmustine, and metformin combination therapy in suppressing inflammatory signaling, inducing apoptosis, and arresting the cell cycle in glioblastoma. Given the key roles of these genes in inflammation and tumorigenesis, the observed synergistic effects suggest that combination therapy enhances overall treatment efficacy. This approach not only improves therapeutic outcomes but also significantly reduces tumor growth and enhances treatment response compared to monotherapies. The triple-drug regimen stands out due to its potent efficacy, offering a promising multi-targeted strategy for glioblastoma treatment. Importantly, in vivo studies and clinical trials will be crucial in confirming these findings and optimizing their clinical application. A limitation of our current study is the use of a single established Glioblastoma cell line. Future studies should include primary patient-derived Glioblastoma cells with different molecular backgrounds, including IDH mutation status, MGMT promoter methylation, and EGFR amplification, to improve translational relevance. The most effective treatments appear to be the combinations of bevacizumab, metformin, and carmustine, especially when used together, suggesting a potential strategy for enhancing therapeutic outcomes in tumor management. The significant downregulation of these inflammatory markers may play a crucial role in mitigating tumor progression and improving patient prognosis. Overall, this data provides valuable insights into the potential mechanisms of action of these drugs and their combinations in tumor biology.
