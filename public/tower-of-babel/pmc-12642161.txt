Examining predictors of substance use and treatment adequacy among formerly incarcerated individuals with a history of opioid and cocaine use: research protocol
Authors: Tawandra Rowell-Cunsolo, Meghan Bellerose, George Musa, Katherine Curtis, David Long, Elwin Wu, Keely Cheslack-Postava, Carl Hart
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOver 600,000 individuals are released from correctional institutions annually in the United States. Substance use disorders (SUDs) are highly prevalent in this population, and only a small proportion receive adequate treatment during incarceration. Post-incarceration, substance use increases considerably, and the risk of drug overdose is higher among formerly incarcerated individuals than among their counterparts in the general population, especially among individuals who use illicit opioids. However, significant gaps exist in our understanding of individual- and environment-level pathways of post-incarceration substance use, limiting treatment effectiveness. For those who receive substance use treatment services post-incarceration, research suggests that these services are not medically appropriate and/or responsive to their unique needs – thereby jeopardizing substance use outcomes.ObjectivesThe Addiction Treatment Adequacy Post-Incarceration (ATAP) Study aims to examine individual and environmental factors associated with changes in substance use and substance use treatment adequacy over a one-year period post-incarceration among formerly incarcerated individuals in New York City (NYC). We will examine the extent to which participants receive appropriate substance use treatment services, as recommended by their scores on the American Society of Addiction Medicine patient placement criteria (ASAM PPC).MethodsWe will use innovative spatially informed multi-level modeling strategies to investigate individual- and environment-level factors that influence substance use among individuals released from correctional institutions and residing in the NYC metropolitan area. Guided by social ecological theory, this study will address the following specific aims: 1) Characterize substance use over a 12-month period (every three months) among 350 formerly incarcerated individuals who have been released in NYC during the past three years; 2) Identify individual (e.g., race, gender, income, mental health) and environmental (e.g., proximity to drug purchasing sites, neighborhood characteristics, residential mobility, access to treatment services) risk factors for drug use among formerly incarcerated individuals; and 3) Investigate discrepancies between ASAM PPC composite scores (a measure of addiction severity) generated and levels of substance use treatment.Potential implicationsThe proposed research will identify substance use treatment gaps and influential factors that are amenable to intervention in the interest of informing effective responses to the current opioid crisis.

Background

Substance use following incarceration

Approximately600,000 incarcerated individuals are released each year in the US, and a significant proportion (65%) have been diagnosed with a Substance Use Disorder (SUD). Incarcerated individuals are seven times more likely than members of the general population to have an SUD (The National Center on Addiction and Substance Abuse 2010) and 40% of individuals incarcerated for violent crimes report that they were under the influence of drugs when they committed the offense (Bronson 2007). Despite the need for drug treatment services and evidence that treatment during incarceration reduces drug use levels and risk of recidivism (American Psychological Association 2004), only 11% of those with SUDs receive services (The National Center on Addiction and Substance Abuse 2010)– and services received often are not appropriate for the unique needs of this population (Taxman et al. 2007, 2013).

The period following release from incarceration is one of the most hazardous as formerly incarcerated people who use drugs attempt to rebuild their lives (Binswanger 2012). These individuals often have exhausted their support systems (Power 2003) and struggle with maintaining their sobriety, particularly if reunited with substance-using friends or family members (Binswanger 2012; Colbert 2013). Illicit drug use, particularly of heroin and other opioids, increases dramatically (Binswanger 2011a; NIDA 2013; Adams 2011; Stuart 2018), resulting in substantially higher risks of re-incarceration (Stahler 2013), contracting or transmitting infectious diseases such as HIV and Hepatitis C (Adams 2011; Wilson 2012), and overdose (Binswanger 2011a).

Drug overdose death rates among formerly incarcerated individuals during the post-release period are 9–17 times higher than among members of the general population (Binswanger 2007). Formerly incarcerated individuals are over 14 times as likely as members of the general population to overdose on opioids (Ranapurwala 2018). Substantial reduction of substance use frequency during incarceration may weaken individuals’ tolerance for illicit substances post-incarceration (Binswanger 2007; Strang 1996; Merrall 2010), resulting in higher overdose rates (Binswanger 2012). Opioids carry a high risk of overdose and death, in part, due to their frequent consumption in combination with other substances, such as cocaine (Cone 2003; Kosten 2003). Use of cocaine laced with fentanyl has increased 420% in the past three years; traces of fentanyl have been identified in over half of overdose cases in at least 10 States (O’Donnell et al. 2017). Such multi-drug combinations increase potential negative effects – e.g., declines in cognitive functioning, poor treatment engagement, hospitalization, overdose mortality (Binswanger 2011a; Colell 2018; Dillon et al. 2003; John et al. 2001; Coffin 2003).

Although opioid-related deaths have received more attention as a public health problem, cocaine use and overdose rates are increasing disproportionately among Blacks and Latinos (Jalal 2018; Shiels 2018; Smart 1991), who also tend to be vastly overrepresented in the criminal justice system (Kaeble 2016). In 2015, the rate of cocaine-related overdose deaths among Black men was almost identical to the rate of opioid-related overdoses among White men (7.6 vs 7.9 per 100,000 person-years) (Shiels 2018). In New York City (NYC), our proposed study setting, opioid-related deaths remain at an epidemic level, and opioids are involved in > 80% of overdose deaths (New York City Department of Health 2017); yet cocaine use is also rising (Hughes 2014). Because users of these substances frequently interact with the criminal justice system (Boutwell 2007, 2007), identifying predictors of drug use and treatment gaps is essential to informing treatment programs to improve substance use treatment outcomes.

Substance use post-incarceration, although associated with an increased risk of death as well as repeat incarceration, is rarely studied as an outcome itself, and return to drug use is often understood as a result of an existing SUD rather than of multiple co-occurring post-release risk factors. Research has reported associations between ongoing substance use and individual characteristics including age (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration 2018), race and ethnicity (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration 2016), gender (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration 2018; Evans and Reynolds 2015), income (Galea and Vlahov 2002), and educational level (Merline 2004; Martins 2015). Income is a driver of use of illicit drugs, including opioids (Martins 2017; Margaret 2012). The rise in opioid-related overdose deaths has been attributed partly to increased availability and low cost compared to other substances (Ciccarone et al. 2017), such as alcohol and cocaine. Heroin, in particular, is cheaper and more easily accessible (Cicero 2014)– attractive features for formerly incarcerated individuals who tend to have limited resources and earning capacity (Visher et al. 2011). However, individual characteristics have limited explanatory power (Mcleroy 1988), and environmental factors such as living environment may offer a more comprehensive understanding of how to respond to the opioid crisis (Rhodes 2003).

The Department of Corrections largely determines residences to which released individuals return (New York State Division of Parole 2010), and only 40% return to their former residences (Harding et al. 2013). Formerly incarcerated individuals typically are concentrated in areas that are resource-deprived and offer few opportunities for successful reentry into the community. To comply with community supervision requirements, reintegration often mandates abstinence or substantial reductions in illicit drug use (Mallik-Kane 2008). But, formerly incarcerated individuals may resettle in communities with high drug exposure, proximity to purchasing sites (Binswanger 2011a; Mkuu et al. 2019), and inadequate addiction support (Mallik-Kane 2008; Fagan et al. 2002; Cadora 2003; Sampson and Loeffler 2010; Clear 2007)– challenges to maintaining sobriety. Substance use post-incarceration may be cyclical, with reductions during periods of relative calm and relapses in reaction to unpleasant events or circumstances (Mowen and Visher 2015). Recently released individuals may initiate or increase drug use to help manage numerous stressors, increasing the likelihood of negative health outcomes such as blood-borne infections related to injection drug use, overdose, accidental injury, and suicide (Fazel and Baillargeon 2011; Hakansson and Berglund 2013). Without longitudinal studies, longer-term substance use patterns and their impacts have not been understood in this population.

Substance use treatment

Because access to effective substance use treatment programs is limited during incarceration (TheNational Center on Addiction and Substance Abuse 2010), engaging formerly incarcerated individuals in appropriate programs and/or interventions is crucial to addressing the opioid crisis. Although the need for extensive support for substance use treatment in this population is widely acknowledged (Binswanger 2011a; Mears 2003), the adequacy of community-based drug treatment services and support post-release is largely unknown (Chandler et al. 2009). The criminal justice system may stipulate treatment as a condition of probation or pretrial release or monitor sobriety (NIDA 2018; Belenko et al. 2013). However, many of the techniques used to monitor treatment success are not empirically supported and are widely used to penalize drug users for consumption (Marlowe 2011). For instance, random drug testing and mandated drug treatment often results in re-incarceration due to noncompliance (Petersilia 1992). Limited information exists about whether individuals are properly screened and referred to the most appropriate treatment services post-incarceration. Because their drug preferences, use, and needs may change over time, ongoing screening may be needed to inform drug treatment placement. Such “treatment matching” – if appropriate – is associated with a number of improved health outcomes including treatment engagement (Sharon 2003; Magura 2003), reduced substance use, and increased likelihood of long-term recovery success (Magura 2003).

The American Society of Addiction Medicine patient placement criteria (ASAM PPC) (Mee-Lee 1996) screening assessment is used to inform service planning and placement, continued stay, transfer, and discharge of patients with SUDs and co-occurring conditions. It has strong inter-rater reliability (.77 correlation coefficient) (Baker and Gastfriend 2004), convergent validity (based on alignment between ASAM recommendations and clinically higher psychiatric and addiction severity scores (p < 0.001) (Stallvik and Nordahl 2014), and predictive validity (based on fewer mean bed-days of care and improved substance use outcomes for patients matched to treatment based on the ASAM PPC vs mismatched) (Sharon 2003; Magura 2003; Angarita 2007). Using the ASAM PPC to perform treatment matching among US adults diagnosed with SUDs has been associated with better treatment retention and fewer readmissions compared to under-matching, or receiving lower levels of recommended care (Sharon 2003; Magura 2003). In contrast, placement mis-matching has been associated with excessive hospital utilization by SUD patients (Sharon 2003) and disengagement in care of heroin and cocaine users (Angarita 2007). Although 57% of US drug treatment programs use some aspect of the ASAM PPC, research on whether formerly incarcerated individuals have access to or use those programs is limited. In addition, although ASAM PPC matching has shown promise in alcohol and marijuana treatment, treatment outcomes for heroin and cocaine are understudied (Kampman and Jarvis 2015).

Study purpose & aims

Previous research demonstrates that substance use is elevated among formerly incarcerated individuals compared to the general population after adjusting for social and economic characteristics (Adams 2011; Merrall 2010; Binswanger 2007, 2011b), and death from drug overdose is substantially higher during the immediate post-release period (Binswanger 2007; Ranapurwala 2018). Although drug treatment often is noted as a substantial post-release need (Binswanger 2011a; Mears 2003), significant gaps remain in understanding pathways of return to drug use (Chandler et al. 2009). In addition to a lack of knowledge regarding risk factors for use of substances such as cocaine and heroin, the literature on post-release overdose has focused primarily on individual characteristics rather than characteristics of the environment to which formerly incarcerated individuals return.

An examination of changes in substance use and individual- and environment-level risk for opioid and cocaine use are important given (1) the need for effective substance use interventions and treatment during the immediate post-release period; (2) limited research on appropriate substance use treatment screening needed to ensure proper placement in programs; and (3) the substantial role of correctional authorities in determining the environment in which formerly incarcerated individuals will reside post-incarceration, limiting their capacity to control their exposure to drugs. Evidence-based and policy-relevant knowledge is desperately needed to help prevent consequent adverse events in this vulnerable and high-risk population.

We will use novel spatially informed multi-level modeling strategies to investigate individual and environmental factors that influence substance use and treatment access among individuals released in NYC from institutions in the New York State Department of Corrections and Community Supervision (NYSDOCCS), one of the largest U.S. criminal justice correctional agencies (Kaeble 2016). Guided by social ecological theory, we have the following Specific Aims:(1) Characterize substance use over a 12-month period among those recently released from incarceration. We will measure substance use frequency (# of times used per day and type[s] of substance[s] used [i.e., opioids, cocaine]) at five time points (every three months) among 350 formerly incarcerated individuals who have been released in NYC during the past three years. (2) Identify individual- and environment-level predictors of post-incarceration substance use. These will include individual (e.g., race, gender, income, mental health) and environmental (e.g., proximity to drug purchasing sites, neighborhood characteristics, residential mobility, access to treatment services) risk factors for drug use among formerly incarcerated individuals; and 3) Investigate discrepancies between ASAM PPC composite scores (a measure of addiction severity) generated and levels of substance use treatment over time. We are especially interested in determining whether participants receive the level of substance use treatment recommended by ASAM PPC composite score generated at five time points over the preceding 12 months and assessing individual and environmental factors associated with receiving lower than ASAM PPC-recommended levels of care.

Methods

Study overview

The 5-year study will assess individual- and environment-level factors that contribute to cocaine and/or opioid use by recently incarcerated individuals over a one-year period and the appropriateness of post-incarceration substance use treatment received. We will collect longitudinal data from participants using the ASAM PPC screening assessment and a survey comprising other standardized measures. We will use the Timeline Follow Back (TLFB) method, which utilizes a blank calendar to help participants recall events and encourages accurate substance use reporting, and urinalysis to confirm self-reported substance use from 350 formerly incarcerated individuals who have transitioned from custody to NYC communities during the previous three years. To meet Aim 1, we will follow participants for 12 months, conducting survey assessments and urinalysis at baseline and 3, 6, 9, and 12 months to determine substance use trajectories. For Aim 2, we will analyze data on individual and environmental determinants to identify predictors of post-incarceration substance use. We will produce spatial indices related to participants’ risk landscape (e.g., locations in which drug use is more prevalent) as well as the care landscape (e.g., distribution of drug treatment resources in communities) to generate environmental predictor variables for the analysis. To address Aim 3, we will administer the ASAM-PPC at each time point to assess whether participants are receiving the level of care consistent with their identified needs. Identifying barriers to treatment success could help facilitate treatment retention and improvements in treatment outcomes.

Conceptual framework

The Social Ecological Model (SEM) (Bronfenbrenner 1983) will guide the proposed research due to its recognition of the important relationships among individual characteristics and environment. This model suggests that individual behavior is situated within multiple levels of influences organized hierarchically. Substance use treatment screening protocols commonly rely on individual and social characteristics to identify those with the greatest risk of drug dependence or abuse. However, evidence indicates that, for marginalized groups and those living in resource poor areas, environmental factors may be stronger predictors of drug use and drug-related problems (Rogers 2012). The adapted SEM model will enable us to perform a comprehensive examination of a range of factors associated with substance use among formerly incarcerated individuals, including those operating at environmental levels (see Fig. 1). This model is uniquely suited to study substance use among vulnerable populations, as it recognizes that the contributions of individual and environmental factors are not just additive, but interact and influence one another reciprocally. For instance, housing stability could be driven by NYSDOCCS policies that dictate where formerly incarcerated individuals can reside; they may not be allowed to live in residences with an individual convicted of a felony, but able to do so in single-room occupancy housing (SROs), homeless shelters, and other forms of congregate housing (Chavira et al. 2018). Although intended to protect formerly incarcerated individuals from deleterious peer influences, such restrictions may cause them to remain in temporary housing or become homeless, ultimately contributing to greater risk of drug use.

Study sample

This study has the following inclusion criteria: 1) ≥ 18 years of age; 2) self-reported history of illicit opioids or cocaine use; 3) English-speaking; and 4) released from a correctional institution within the NYC metropolitan area during the past three years having served a sentence of at least six months (based on research suggesting that it takes at least six months for incarcerated individuals to lose ties to their communities (Mackenzie and Goodstein 1985). Participants’ incarceration release date will be verified by NYSDOCCS discharge paperwork. If a potential participant does not have paperwork available, we will access the NYSDOCCS Inmate Population Information Search website, which contains information on release dates and offense(s) committed. We included the eligibility criterion of release within the previous three years because this is a vulnerable period for formerly incarcerated individuals, when re-incarceration rates due to drug use are reportedly elevated (Binswanger 2011a; Visher 2007). Although we expect a quarter of our study population to be Hispanic, we have restricted the study to English-speaking adults because the ASAM PPC is not currently available in Spanish and over half of New York City residents over age 5 who report that Spanish is the primary language spoken in their homes report speaking English “very well” (American Community Survey 2018). We will have staff on our Field Team that are proficient in Spanish to insure we make every attempt to include Latinx participants whose English meets our inclusion criteria yet have questions that can be addressed in Spanish.

Based on our preliminary research and current incarceration trends, we expect the majority of our study participants to be low-income men of color who are unstably housed. Because women are a substantially smaller proportion of the incarcerated population (only 7% of those recently released from the NYSDOCCS), they are largely underrepresented in behavioral research on criminal justice populations (Tuchman 2010). However, because formerly incarcerated women typically have higher rates of engaging in high-risk sexual and substance use behaviors than men (Rowell-Cunsolo 2018), we will include them in the study, over-sampling them to ensure adequate representation. We will use previously proven strategies, e.g., monetary incentives for participants who refer women in their networks, frequent presentations at agencies that cater to formerly incarcerated women in the NYC area, and reminders to collaborating agencies to include women when referring clients.

Recruitment

Participants will be recruited on a continual basis from community-based agencies that administer services to individuals engaged with the legal system in the NYC area, including those offering health services, substance abuse treatment, housing, and employment services. Approximately 26,000 offenders are released in New York State each year, half from NYC (New York State Department of Corrections and Community Supervision 2018). Many formerly incarcerated individuals in NYC receive social services and support from these agencies, which play a critical role in assisting them with accessing community-based services and mandated programming. Recruitment flyers and advertisements with basic study information will be posted at these agencies and given to staff to distribute. Flyers will list a phone number for individuals to call if they are interested in participating in the study, allowing them to select a private time to contact the researchers to schedule a screening visit. When we visit agencies to distribute flyers, we will ask about opportunities to speak to groups of formerly incarcerated individuals (e.g., during educational classes, community reentry events, and roundtables) to promote participation and increase awareness of the study. However, we will not explicitly ask individuals if they meet inclusion criteria or directly approach potential participants during these visits. We will also enlist the assistance of our recruitment sites to refer participants. We will provide participants with recruitment coupons to give to others who may meet the study’s inclusion criteria. Similar to recruitment flyers, these coupons will contain basic information about the study and a phone number to call to learn more and to schedule a baseline interview. Participants will receive an additional $5 for each person successfully recruited. Similar to procedures in our prior research, we will make biweekly trips to recruitment sites to ensure that awareness of this study is continually being raised. We have successfully used this approach, with recruitment flyers posted at more than 30 different agencies throughout NYC, during the principal investigator’s previous research.

Study enrollment

After a brief screening questionnaire to determine eligibility, potential participants will provide informed consent prior to participating in the longitudinal study. Participants will be informed that none of the information shared with us will be shared with criminal justice personnel, including parole or probation officers.

Incentives

Individuals who meet study inclusion criteria and wish to enroll will complete a baseline interview and receive $30 in compensation. Participants will be compensated $40 for completing the 3-month follow-up assessment, $50 for the 6-month follow-up assessment; $60 for the 9-month follow-up assessment; and $70 for the 12-month follow-up assessment. This graduated compensation is comparable to that offered in research with this population (Reznick 2013).

Assessments

The ASAM PPC is an online substance use treatment screening assessment that will be verbally administered to participants at each time point. Study staff will participate in web-based courses and training on administering these instruments sponsored by the ASAM or the NYS Office of Alcoholism and Substance Abuse Services. Data collection will take place in a private room on the Columbia University Irving Medical Center/New York State Psychiatric Institute campus, which is easily accessible by public transportation.

Individual characteristics

Socio-demographic characteristics include age, gender, race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, marital status, education, income, and employment status. To measure degree of social support after release, we will use the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) (Zimet 1988), a highly validated (Zimet 1988) scale (Cronbach’s alpha of.88 for internal reliability,.85 for test–retest validity) that has been widely used, including among formerly incarcerated individuals with SUD (Valera and Boyas 2019; Staton-Tindall et al. 2007).

Substance use outcomes and screening

The primary outcome will be opioid and/or cocaine use over the past three months as measured by drug use frequency and the number/types of drugs used concurrently. We opted for these measures based on the following prior research: 1) substance use frequency is substantially reduced during incarceration, which may weaken formerly incarcerated individuals’ tolerance to illicit substances post-incarceration (Binswanger 2007; Strang 1996; Merrall 2010) and result in a higher risk of overdose (Binswanger 2012); 2) formerly incarcerated individuals may “make up for lost time” by increasing the frequency of drug use early after release and experimenting with a range of illicit substances, which increases their overdose risk (Seal 2007; Milloy 2009); and 3) more frequent illicit substance use and a greater number of drugs used are associated with greater consequences, including unintentional overdose among opiate or cocaine users (Strang 1999; Galea 2006). Measuring substance use in this manner over the course of a year will enable us to characterize trajectories of use and relate them to individual and environmental-level factors. This information can inform targeted interventions that are appropriate for this population and the point at which such interventions should be administered post-incarceration.

Participants will be asked to disclose which drugs they used and the number of days they used drugs over the past three months – the timeframe recommended by NIDA's National Drug Abuse Treatment Clinical Trials Network (CTN) Treatment Effect and Assessment Measures (TEAM) Task Force (Rosa et al. 2012; Clinical Trials Network Steering Committee 2010). For each substance used, participants will be asked to report how frequently they used it over the past three months, route of administration, and physical location where they acquired it and used it, with answers ranging from 0 (never) to 6 (daily or almost daily). At each of the five time-points, outcomes will be measured as a) presence or absence of use (opioids, cocaine, polydrug use) within the interval; b) frequency of use for opioids, cocaine, and polydrug use; and c) total number of different drugs used within the interval. Participants will be considered polydrug users if they report using more than one illicit drug during the three-month measurement period (Brecht 2008).

We will use the TLFB method to help participants recall their substance use. The TLFB allows retrospective assessment of substance use using a calendar for prompting, has shown strong test–retest reliability (Cronbach’s alpha of.73 to 1.00 for 30 day and.77 to 1.0 for 90 day) (Carey 2004, 1997) and typically is used to collect information when participants may have difficulty recalling their behaviors. Since substance use may affect memory, we expect TLFB to result in more accurate reporting over past 3 months (Hjorthoj et al. 2012; Chavira 2017). This method has been used to measure substance use in our previous research (El-Bassel 2011; Witte 2011) and in research on housing stability among formerly incarcerated individuals (Chavira 2017).

ASAM PPC composite score and level of care

The ASAM PPC (Mee-Lee 1996) examines the following dimensions to inform placement decisions: 1) acute withdrawal and/or withdrawal potential; 2) biomedical conditions and complications; 3) emotional, behavioral, or cognitive conditions and complications (including mental health diagnoses); 4) readiness to change; 5) relapse, continued use, or continued problem potential; and 6) recovery/living environment. Due to its strong empirical support and cost effectiveness, the ASAM PCC has been widely used since 2015 to inform addiction treatment authorizations for Medicaid-funded programs and 30 state systems (Program 2017). In addition to the ASAM PPC, New York State created the Level of Care for Alcohol and Drug Treatment Referral (LOCADTR)(O 2018). However, the LOCADTR is used to screen for more acute safety risks but does not generate a composite score. Since the ASAM PPC has been widely used and has strong empirical support(Sharon 2003; Magura 2003; American Society of Addiction Medicine 2017; Haley 2014), we opted to use that measure.

To determine the level of addiction treatment needed, the ASAM PPC generates a composite score summarizing the participant’s needs that can be used to recommend one of four levels of care (Mee-Lee 1996): Level I) outpatient treatment (attending meetings such as Alcoholics Anonymous); Level II) intensive outpatient/partial hospitalization (counseling and education about substance use issues); Level III) residential/inpatient treatment (treatment and assistant are provided on a 24-h basis); or Level IV) medically-managed intensive inpatient treatment (patients reside in a fully staffed facility equipped with medical services).

Urinalysis

Participants will provide a urine sample for urinalysis to measure illicit drug use objectively at baseline and 3-, 6-, 9-, and 12-months follow-up. At these assessment visits, they will be asked to urinate in a labeled specimen cup using a nearby bathroom and return the specimen cup to the research assistant. Participants will be informed that results from these tests (or any other data collected during this study) will not be shared with any criminal justice personnel. Because criminal justice-involved populations routinely undergo random or scheduled urinalysis to meet the terms of their community supervision, we believe that this process will not be seen as overly intrusive. Samples will be discarded after laboratory testing results are recorded. We will use the following test kits—CLIAwaived, Inc. 14 Panel—IDTC II”; “K2 Spice Rapid Test”; “ETG Urine Alcohol Ethyl Glucuronide Test Dip to identify opiates, methamphetamine, cocaine, and marijuana. These are the most commonly-used illicit drugs in this population (Rowell-Cunsolo 2018; Rowell 2012); the results will be compared to self-reported substance use. This will enable us to have an objective measure of drug use, although self-report and objective measures of substance use have been reported to be highly correlated (Hjorthoj et al. 2012). We will examine concordance between urinalysis results and self-reported drug use (Kappa statistics) and identify additional drugs that were unreported (including unintentionally by those unaware of substances they ingested–a common occurrence with fentanyl analogues (Daniulaityte 2019)). When a discrepancy in drug use reporting is identified, we will use the objective measure for our analysis.

Current level of care and substance use treatment accessibility

Participants will be asked to report the extent (frequency, duration) to which they participate in every ASAM PPC-specified level of care (support groups, counseling) and location of care. For instance, we will ask if participants are attending support groups to reduce substance use or maintain sobriety. If so, we will ask the name and location of support groups and current treatment site(s). For those unable to remember the exact location, we will perform a web-based search for the group or site visited and record the address provided online. We will also ask participants to indicate whether their treatment was recommended by a healthcare professional and/or mandated by the NYSDOCCS.

Criminal justice history

Participants will be asked the length of their most recent incarceration, discharge date, offense(s) committed, number of previous incarcerations, and current legal status (probation, parole). They will be asked whether they have been arrested and/or reincarcerated during each follow-up assessment. Any participant incarcerated during the study period can continue participation upon release. Up to 40% of incarcerated individuals in NYS are reincarcerated within three years after being released (New York State Department of Corrections and Community Supervision 2018), but many of these rearrests result in short incarceration stays (New York State Department of Corrections and Community Supervision 2018). By allowing them to re-enroll in the study post-incarceration, we will be able to assess how/whether their most recent incarceration affected their substance use. We will not collect data from participants during periods of incarceration but will ask them post-release whether they used drugs and received substance use treatment services while incarcerated.

Housing

Participants will be asked to provide their complete current home address. Because research indicates that formerly incarcerated individuals live in more disadvantaged communities post-incarceration than they did pre-incarceration (Warner 2015), we will also ask them to provide their most recent addresses pre- and post-incarceration, the amount of time spent in each residence, and whose residence it is (Weir 2007). We will ascertain housing stability as number of times a participant changes residences over their 12-month study enrollment (Herbert 2017). People who move more than once a year typically are viewed as being unstably housed (Harding et al. 2013; Herbert 2017; Herbert et al. 2015). We will use the four broad categories of homelessness defined by the US Department of Housing and Urban Development’s (HUD): participants will be asked whether they consider themselves as being homeless or as having a stable place to live, are at imminent risk of homelessness, are homeless under federal status, or are fleeing domestic violence (Weir 2007). They also will be asked whether their current residence was recommended or mandated by the NYSDOCCS. Although some of these data may be available from the NYSDOCCS, asking participants to indicate their living arrangements and movement may be more accurate because they may not disclose all of their activities to NYSDOCCS, especially if they are violating community supervision guidelines or engaging in illegal behavior.

Exposure to drugs and drug-purchasing sites

We will ask participants to estimate the number of people who use drugs with whom they live. They will be asked to report an approximate location where they typically purchase each type of illicit substance(s) that they consume or how the substance was obtained if not purchased, being as specific as possible. This information will be used to determine distance traveled to drug purchasing locations: proximity to drug-purchasing sites from their current residence will be measured using spatial network analysis tools. Our previous experiences interviewing people who use drugs in NYC suggest that many are open to sharing information about drug purchasing sites. However, we will also geocode data of drug arrests and overdose deaths in NYC as others have done (Barnum 2017; McCord and Ratcliffe 2007; Xia et al. 2021), to provide a second data source for those who refuse to share or cannot remember the exact location.

Neighborhood environment

Participants’ neighborhoods will be defined by census block-group that can be linked to spatial contextual data pertaining to their risk and care landscapes. Additionally, based on findings from our previous research study findings, we expect there to be enough variability in the block-groups to permit statistical analysis of environmental factors while protecting patient confidentiality (Jarlais 2011). We will maintain participant confidentiality through aggregate analysis of tracts and reporting neighborhood characteristics, not specific locations.

We will draw on primary and secondary data to develop several neighborhood-level variables that will reflect the risk and care landscapes of participants’ living environments, including the following: Racial/Ethnic Segregation:The neighborhood’s racial/ethnic segregation will be calculated using data from the US American Community Survey (ACS) at the block-group level. Based on Spatial Information Theory (Reardon 2008; Reardon and O’Sullivan 2004), indices for white-Black, white-Latinx, and white-other/mixed will be created to measure Racial/Ethnic Segregation. Spatially weighted racial composition indices account for the spatial relationships and overcomes the limitations of the widely used dissimilarity index, which does not measure the deviation from randomness.

An index of neighborhood quality will be created based on NYC Complaints (e.g., complaints regarding: disorderly youths, derelict vehicles, rodents, noise, air quality, acceptably clean streets/sidewalks/parks and playgrounds, etc.) (Musa 2021). Lack of Green/Open Space (percent of Tract covered by green/open space) and count of Amenities (parks, playgrounds, churches, healthcare facilities, drug treatment sites, schools, etc.) will be calculated at the tract level. Availability of Alcohol and Illegal Substances:Addresses of all alcohol outlets (e.g., liquor stores, bars) obtained from the State Liquor Authorities (NY, NJ, CT) (New York Liquor Authority 2021). As with residential addresses, these locations will be geocoded in ArcGIS. Locations of arrests for possession of, sale of, or intent to sell a controlled substance will be obtained from NYC Open Data. Counts of Alcohol Outlets and Drug Arrests will be summarized to Census Tract. Housing Consistency will be measured by the number of residences the participant reports and if their address changed (obtained at each assessment). Variables from self-report measures of neighborhood violence and crime will include Neighborhood Characteristics (Sampson, et al., 1997), Exposure to Community Violence (Bird, 2006); (Richters, and Martinez, 1993) and Collective Efficacy (Sampson et al. 1999).

Care landscape

We will rely on two variables to measure environmental forces that enhance a participant’s ability to access care intended to reduce substance use – support groups and treatment sites. Participants will be asked whether they have participated in a support group or drug use treatment. To characterize the presence of support groups and accessibility (location, capacity) to treatment sites, we will use secondary data from the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) National Directory of Drug and Alcohol Use Treatment Facilities and the NYC Department of City Planning Facilities Database, obtained from the NYC Office of Addiction Services and Supports (OASAS). Neighborhood characteristics are derived from participants’ geocoded addresses (using ArcGIS) at each assessment point. Neighborhood composite variables, using traditional data reduction techniques such as Principal Component Analysis, Factor Analysis, Latent Class Analysis, will be computed for each wave at the Census Block-group level (Schulz et al. 2002; Krieger 2003), using GIS and objective data obtained from the US Census, NYC Dept. of Planning, and NYC Police Department. If necessary, data will be disaggregated to the Block-group using Cadastral-based Expert Dasymetric System (CEDS) (Maantay et al. 2007), a method of disaggregating areal data, to improve the specificity of index.

Risk landscape

We will focus on four primary variables to measure environmental forces that place participants at greatest risk of substance abuse.

Area Deprivation Index (ADI)

We will use a composite measure of neighborhood deprivation, created by Amy Kind at UW in collaboration with staff at the Applied Population Laboratory, as a measure of neighborhood SES (Kind 2018). A composite index of SES is desirable because of the high correlations typically observed between separate indicators of neighborhood SES, which, if used simultaneously in multivariate analyses, would result in multicollinearity and thus violate the assumptions of regression modeling. Neighborhood crime: We will compute an index of crime at the neighborhood (Census Tract) using variables obtained at the point level (exact location) from the NYC OpenData portal (https://nycopendata.socrata.com), including police incident and criminal offense data (e.g., robbery, felonious assault, forcible rape, major felony, murder and non-negligent manslaughter, burglary, structural fires, non-structural fires, civilian fire fatalities) (Musa 2013; Kind 2018) and aggregated to the Tract level. Data reduction techniques (as described above) will be performed to generate an index.

Availability of drugs

As with neighborhood crime data, data on drug arrests will be obtained from the NYC OpenData portal (https://nycopendata.socrata.com) and geocoded. Using Getis-Ord Gi* and kernel density estimation techniques, we will identify hotspots exhibiting a high concentration of crimes related to drug sales at each of the five assessment points. These drug sale hotspots will be compared with data from the in-person quantitative interviews in which respondents are asked where they can readily purchase illegal drugs. Euclidian distance and transportation network distance to the participants’ residences at each time point will be computed for both the hotspots and the participants’ reported drug purchase locations.

Neighborhood features associated with drug arrests

In addition to the above risk landscape measures described above, we have added the following environmental features associated with drug purchasing and arrests in the United States: check-cashing centers, pawn shops, liquor stores, and vacant storefronts. A study examining the connection between drug arrests and environmental features found that drug arrests commonly occur within a few blocks of check-cashing centers, pawn shops, homeless shelters, drug treatment centers, subway stations, and beer establishments, with the last two showing a significant association after adjusting for socio-demographics (McCord and Ratcliffe 2007). More recently, two studies found that grocery stores, foreclosures, and vacant buildings were predictive of drug arrests for all types of drugs, but there is some “market specialization” for each type of drug (Barnum 2017; Xia et al. 2021). These locations will be identified using methods described above.

Retention

Retention is one of the primary challenges in longitudinal research studies with people who use drugs (Gilmore and Kuperminc 2014). To keep participants engaged and enrolled, we will use successful techniques from our previous research studies and those that have been shown to promote retention in health-based interventions generally (Brueton 2014; Rowell-Cunsolo et al. 2020; Abshire 2017) – e.g., mailed birthday cards and monthly reminder phone calls, e-mails, text messages. Mailing birthday cards to participants will allow us to maintain contact, check mailing addresses, and remind them of their next scheduled appointment (which will be scheduled at each follow-up visit). Each participant will be given an appointment card with the study contact information should they need to contact the research team. We will collect detailed contact and locator information during each visit and place monthly phone calls to remind participants of the scheduled follow-up assessments. During our monthly phone calls, participants will be asked whether they have any additional questions about the study or would like to update any of their contact information. Participants also are compensated more as their time engaged with the study increases. The Study Coordinator also will perform regular searches of the NYDOCCS database to detect whether any participants have been imprisoned since the last time they were in contact with the research team.

Statistical analyses

General aspects

Before performing statistical analysis, we will examine all variables at each time point for implausible values, outliers, and inconsistencies. Variables will be transformed (i.e., log-transform, centering, normalization) as appropriate to facilitate interpretation. Models will be checked for multi-collinearity, influential observations, and outliers. All hypothesis tests will be two-sided at level α = 0.05.

Addressing variation by sex/gender (including sex as a biological variable)

We will assess heterogeneity of associations and trends by sex/gender by including product interaction terms in regression models and compute stratum (sex) specific estimates when warranted by evidence of heterogeneity. We intend to oversample women to ensure that we are able to characterize substance use trajectories and treatment gaps among this high-risk population.

Analyses for AIM 1: Characterize substance use over a 12-month period among those recently released from incarceration

We will measure substance use frequency (number of days used), substance use location, and type(s) of substance(s) used (i.e., opioids, cocaine, polydrug use, etc.) at five time points (every three months) over a 12-month period among 350 formerly incarcerated individuals who have been released in NYC from correctional institutions within the NYSDOCCS over the past three years. Drug use at each time period will be summarized by means (SD) and percentiles of frequency of use for each drug, the number of drugs used, and the prevalence of use. The primary outcome will be frequency of opioid use. We will also explore secondary outcomes such as presence of opioid use, frequency and presence of other drugs (cocaine, polydrug use), as well as the total number of drugs used. To identify distinct latent trajectories of drug use we will use latent growth curve mixture modeling (LGMM). We expect that there will be four latent classes (Fischer 2010): class one will be occasional substance use (i.e., no use or use during less than one month in the past three months); class two will be moderately regular/monthly substance use; class three will be moderately regular/weekly substance use; class four will be frequent substance use. We will select a model based on minimizing the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) (Nylund et al. 2007).

Analyses for AIM 2: Identify individual and environmental risk and care factors related to opioid and/or cocaine use among formerly incarcerated individuals

We will identify risk factors for specific latent classes of drug use patterns; we will also explore potential moderation effects between individual and environmental factors for the outcome measures. We hypothesize that a) opioid and/or cocaine use will be higher among participants who are male, have less than a high school education, and have lower incomes; b) drug use will be higher among participants who report closer proximity to their drug purchasing sites, estimate a larger number of household members who use drugs, move residences more frequently and live further from drug treatment sites. We will build upon the generalized linear mixed effect models proposed in Aim 1. We will first remove potential nonsignificant time trend effect from the model and then refit the models. Significant effects 3 indicate significant individual risk factors and significant effects 4 indicates significant environmental risk factors on outcome measures. Moderation effects between individual and environmental factors will be assessed through same-level or cross-level interactions. We will also determine individual and environmental characteristics associated with specific latent classes of drug use patterns by re-estimating the selected LGMM models, including key hypothesized risk and care factors (time varying as well as time invariant) as covariates. This will produce estimates (magnitude and significance) of their association with each latent class. Association between individual and environmental factors and latent class membership will also be examined in latent multinomial log-linear regression models. Measures of neighborhood risk factors first will be examined individually to assess the relationship of each with substance use outcomes, adjusting for covariates. Subsequently, all neighborhood risk variables that were individually associated (p < 0.05) with a given outcome will be jointly included in a mutually adjusted model (also adjusting for covariates) to determine which aspects of neighborhood risk are independently associated with the outcome.

Analyses for AIM 3: Investigate discrepancies between ASAM PPC composite score generated and substance use treatment level of care received

AIM 3.1

We will investigate whether participants are receiving the appropriate level of care recommended by the ASAM PPC composite score generated at five time points over 12 months. We hypothesize that participants’ score of ASAM PPC level of care they received will be lower than the score of level of care they would have needed. We will first examine frequencies in each ASAM PPC domain. Participants’ score of ASAM PPC level of care they should receive is measured at four levels of care: 1) outpatient treatment; 2) intensive outpatient/partial hospitalization; 3) residential/inpatient treatment; or 4) medically managed intensive inpatient treatment. The ASAM PCC will also generate a composite score that summarizes the participants’ needs of treatment at four levels. Repeated paired outcomes measured at each time point over the 12-month study period (baseline, 3-month, 6-month, 9-month, and 12-month) will be modeled as a linear function of time and outcome indicator (two levels: PPC level of care received vs PPC-recommended level of care) using generalized linear mixed effects model to account for the clustering effect of each paired observation nested within each participant. Significant effect of the outcome category will indicate that drug treatment support to be received is not consistent with substance use treatment to be needed.

AIM 3.2

To investigate individual (e.g., race, gender, income, mental health) and environmental characteristics (e.g., proximity to drug purchasing sites, neighborhood characteristics, residential mobility, access to treatment services) of participants receiving lower than PPC-recommended level of care, we will construct a dichotomized outcome (two levels: under treatment vs treatment need is met) created by comparing the PPC levels of care received and that of PPC-recommended level of care at each time point over the 12 month (baseline, 3-month, 6-month, 9-month, and 12-month).

Repeated outcomes will be modeled as a linear function of time and participant and environmental characteristics using similar generalized linear mixed effects model 1. In model 1, the outcome Ytij is the dichotomized outcome indicating whether the ith participant from census tract j at time t received lower than recommended ASAM PPC level of care or not. A significant effect 2 indicates effect of characteristics that might change over the time (such as alcohol use). A significant effect 3 indicates effect of baseline measure of participant characteristics (such as age, race, gender); a significant effect 4 indicates effect of environmental characteristics.

Power and sample size

We will recruit 350 participants (50 women, 300 men). We assume that by the end of the study, we will have lost 30% of participants, based on previous longitudinal research with criminal justice-involved populations (Goshin and Byrne 2012). From baseline to the end of the study, assuming a linear loss of participants by time, the average attrition rate will be 8.5% per wave. As we are using multi-level models, all data at each time points will be analyzed, and thus it results in an average of about 295 participants per wave with an average attrition rate of 8.5% throughout the study period. Based on our preliminary data, those 350 participants will come from about 225 tracts. Assuming ICC = 0.05 within each tract. After considering design effect of participants nested within tract, the sample size is about 286 (i.e., 295/1.03). Assuming alpha = 0.05 and 80% power, we will be able to within-participant detect temporal differences of 0.17 standard deviations of the substance use outcome measures. We will also examine associations of personal and environmental characteristics with substance use. Assuming alpha = 0.05, 80% power, and correlation of 0.1 to 0.7 for observations from the same participants, we calculated minimum detectable effect sizes (d) for a range of exposure prevalences. We estimate d = 0.29–0.49 for an exposure with prevalence 10% (i.e., an uncommon exposure), d = 0.20–0.34 for an exposure with prevalence 25%, and d = 0.18–0.30 for an exposure of prevalence 40% (a common exposure). These effect sizes are all in the range considered “small” to “medium-small”(Cohen 1988).

Potential challenges

Retention

Retention is a common challenge in longitudinal research with high-risk populations. In addition to employing previously successful retention strategies, we will continuously monitor our retention rates to ensure that we are exploring all appropriate options to keep participants engaged. Our previous retention strategies with this population enabled us to achieve high retention rates, and we expect retention rates to be similar in this study. We will record who has dropped out of the study to determine whether there are any trends in characteristics.

Measurement

We will measure drug use using self-reported measures. Although we also will use urinalysis results, we recognize that drugs metabolize differently and that all drugs may not be detected by urinalysis. Although some results have varied (Large 2012; Magura and Kang 1996), a meta-analysis reported high agreement between urinalysis results and self-report (Hjorthoj et al. 2012). While participants may underreport the magnitude of their drug use and other stigmatizing behaviors and provide more socially desirable responses (Hunt 2015), our previous research with this population has shown that they are willing to share their drug use experiences. Because our research team members have had success in building rapport with this population, we are confident that our techniques will facilitate honest reporting. During the informed consent process, we will emphasize to participants that we have a certificate of confidentiality to protect their privacy and remind them that we will not share their responses or urinalysis results with criminal justice personnel. We also will inform them that, although we will be asking about their drug use and substance use treatment utilization, we will not share any of the information they provide with healthcare providers, employers, shelter managers, or criminal justice officers.

Generalizability

We will use a variety of data sources to examine factors associated with and predictors of opioid and/or cocaine use, including longitudinal survey research and spatially referenced primary and secondary data, which will enable us to identify temporal trends and spatial patterns that could be useful in addressing substance misuse in this population. Although our results may not be generalizable to all substance-using formerly incarcerated individuals, we believe that the findings will lay the groundwork for future intervention development in this area.

Potential implications

Our study is designed to provide insight into what substance use trajectories and access to adequate addiction treatment resources among people who use drugs (opioids and cocaine, in particular) and return to the New York City area. Identifying substance use treatment gaps and influential factors that are amenable to intervention is essential to informing effective responses to substance misuse. We hope that our study will lay the foundation for interventions that connect formerly incarcerated people who use drugs to appropriate levels of treatment and support as they transition back to their communities.
