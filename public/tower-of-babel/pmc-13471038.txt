An Embedded Model of Care to Address Depression in the In‐Home Aged Care Setting
Authors: Tanya E. Davison, Marissa Dickins, Julie Kelly, Sunil Bhar, Karen Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveDepression is increasingly common among older Australians receiving home care, yet symptoms are often under‐recognised and access to evidence‐based psychological treatment remains limited. This paper introduces EMBED, a new model of care designed to improve detection, escalation and management of depression within routine home care service delivery.MethodsWe describe the development of an evidence‐informed model of care for the Australian home care setting. The model draws on clinical guidelines, published evidence on late‐life depression and psychological treatment, implementation evidence from aged care and consultation with frontline staff, managers, senior leaders and consumer representatives.ResultsThe EMBED model comprises four integrated components: (i) workforce training to improve the knowledge, attitudes and self‐efficacy of home care workers; (ii) routine screening to support early identification of symptoms and deterioration; (iii) structured clinical assessment to guide escalation and treatment planning; and (iv) tailored psychological intervention using lifestyle therapy and behavioural activation. The intervention was designed to be stratified according to symptom severity, responsive to older people's preferences and feasible for delivery within aged care services and through health service collaboration.ConclusionsThe EMBED model offers a practical approach to addressing a major gap in current home care practice by embedding mental health capability into usual aged care service delivery. Further research is required to determine its effectiveness and cost‐effectiveness, as well as implementation requirements in the Australian home care context to enable providers to better support the growing population of older people with symptoms of depression.

Mental Health of Older Adults Receiving Home Care

In recent years, there has been a rapid increase in the number of Australians receiving funding for aged care services, such as personal care, domestic support, nursing and allied health [1], enabling more older people to remain in their own home rather than relocate into a residential aged care home. The clinical complexity of this population has increased, including rising rates of common mental health conditions such as depression [2]. One in four Australians assessed for home care in 2021–2022 had an identified mental health condition—an increase of 60% over 4 years [3]. Accounting for the growth in population size during this period, the absolute numbers of home care clients with mood disorders in Australia increased by 253% [4].

These data likely underestimate the prevalence of depression, given the common misattribution of depressive symptoms to the ‘normal’ ageing process, which impacts on recognition of depression in older people [5], and the presence of mental health stigma, which is a significant barrier to reporting and help‐seeking for older people [6]. Internationally, home care recipients are at least twice as likely as other older adults in the community to have a mental health disorder [7]. A recent survey of Australian home care recipients reported that 52% experienced at least mild symptoms of depression, while 16% reported symptoms consistent with Major Depressive Disorder [8].

Existing Approaches Fail to Address the Needs of Older Australians

Treatment guidelines for depression recommend psychological therapy as the first‐line treatment for people with mild‐to‐moderate conditions, which can be combined with pharmacological treatments for more severe cases [9]. Psychological treatments should be considered before pharmacological treatments [9, 10]. However, only 3% of Australians receiving home care access mental health services under the Medicare Benefits Schedule and < 1% receive psychology services [11]. Instead, there is an over‐reliance on psychotropic medications, prescribed to 40% of all Australians aged 85 years or older in 2023–2024 (compared to 18% of the broader Australian population) [12]. This is particularly concerning for older adults with multiple medical comorbidities and polypharmacy, given the higher risk profile of antidepressants for this group [13]. Several factors likely contribute to poor uptake of mental health services among home care recipients, including mental health stigma, low perceived need and poor awareness of treatment options, financial constraints, an insufficient number of clinicians with expertise in late‐life mental health conditions and difficulty travelling to clinics [14, 15].

There are significant consequences of the failure to address rising mental health conditions in older Australians. Inadequately treated depression in later life is associated with increased disability and mortality risk [16, 17], increases the likelihood of an older person being admitted to a residential aged care home [18] or hospital [19] and may lead to higher levels of home care service utilisation [20]. Poor management of the mental health needs of older Australians therefore impacts both health and aged care expenditure, as well as older people's quality of life.

The Role of the Aged Care Sector in Supporting Aged Mental Health

Home care providers could play a more significant role in detecting, monitoring and responding to late‐life mental health conditions. Aged care workers visit older people at home regularly and often develop trusting relationships with them. They are well‐placed to observe indications of mental health deterioration, such as decline in the person's self‐care and reduced engagement in activities. The regularity of contact provides valuable opportunities to intervene early, before symptoms are typically identified by healthcare providers and prevent symptom escalation.

While aged care services are designed to support older people's general well‐being, Australian aged care providers have not tended to consider older people's mental health as ‘core business’. This omission was recognised in the Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety's conclusion that the needs of older Australians with mental health conditions are inadequately addressed in aged care [21]. In response, Strengthened Aged Care Quality Standards now place greater responsibility on Australian aged care providers to meet older people's mental health needs, through identifying changes in their mental health, actively promoting mental health and wellbeing, responding supportively to mental health symptoms and deterioration, and escalating concerns (in particular, Standard 5.5.6) [22]. However, currently, mental health training and screening are not mandatory in aged care, and aged care workers lack knowledge of how to recognise and respond effectively to mental health deterioration [5]. Hence, the mental health needs of home care clients remain poorly supported by aged care providers. This gap in care is likely to continue without a deliberate design that integrates such elements into usual aged care practices.

An Embedded, Multicomponent Model of Care for Depression in Home Care

We propose a new model of care for depression, designed specifically for the home care setting, aligned with treatment guidelines and informed by evidence of what works with older people in other settings. The Enhanced Management of home‐Based Elders with Depression (EMBED) model of care aims to address rising rates of depression through early detection of older people with symptoms of depression and improved access to effective treatment. It was developed through consultation with frontline staff, senior staff and consumer representatives, and comprises four components to address critical gaps in care: workforce training, routine screening, clinical assessment and tailored psychological treatment (Figure 1). Each of the components of EMBED has been validated in research but not yet synthesised into a comprehensive model of care and integrated into routine home care service delivery in Australia. We describe each component in detail below, along with recommended implementation approaches.

The EMBED model of care.

Workforce Training in Depression

There is clear evidence that training can improve knowledge of depression among aged care workers and increase their self‐efficacy in caring for older people experiencing depression [23]. Yet, the Australian aged care workforce is inadequately trained to identify and support older people with depression [5, 24]. A recent national survey found that only 17% of aged care workers had received training in mental health, loneliness or social isolation in the past 12 months, while 31% reported an imminent need for such training [25].

In the EMBED model of care, training provides the workforce with information on the signs and symptoms of depression, and aims to dispel commonly held misconceptions, such as depression being a natural consequence of ageing [24]. It also includes content on anxiety, reducing mental health stigma and ageism, and cultural considerations. The training aims to build skills in how to engage older people in conversation about their mood and well‐being, addressing the hesitancy expressed by many aged care workers about ‘open[ing] up a whole Pandora's Box’ [5, p. 61]. Practical guidance on how to respond to disclosure of symptoms is included, as well as clear steps on what aged care workers should do if they have concerns about the mental health of an older person, in line with their organisational policies and procedures related to clinical deterioration and escalation. Senior, coordinating and clinical staff (e.g., care partners, nurses, allied health professionals) receive additional training in the use of standardised assessment tools, referral procedures and in supporting their teams in responding to client mental health distress.

The training developed for EMBED is an entry‐level program suitable for all home care workers, which is completed in 70–90 min and is freely available for aged care organisations upon request [26]. An online, micromodule training approach has been adopted to best meet workforce schedules. Brief, 10‐min modules can be accessed by staff out on the road using their existing device (e.g., smartphone or tablet), and either scheduled into their workplan or completed opportunistically; for example, if they finish a home visit early or have a cancelled appointment. This training program has been piloted in a large home care provider organisation and found to be feasible and acceptable [27]. Findings indicated significant improvements in staff attitudes towards late‐life depression, less ageist attitudes and greater confidence in caring for older people with depression following the training.

Routine Screening for Depression

The routine use of screening tools is a key component to increase the detection of depression in home care. Screening is critically important in this setting, given the reluctance of many older people to disclose symptoms and seek help spontaneously [5]. Few Australian home care providers currently screen for mental health symptoms during service provision. In the EMBED model of care, all new clients are screened on entry to the service as well as during routine client reviews, to detect deterioration. Embedding depression screening questions into existing screening protocols communicates to both care workers and older people the importance of mental health as well as physical health needs in care provision. Screening tools can also be used on an ad hoc basis; for example, when a care worker is concerned about the mental well‐being of a client.

Screening tools that have been validated for use with older adults, with established cut‐off points, and which can be administered by a non‐clinical workforce are essential. Screening tools should also be brief to ensure they are feasible for routine use in care delivery, such as the 2‐item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ‐2) [28], which is used in a variety of healthcare settings. Specific training should be provided to ensure those using the tools are confident in how to ask the questions and respond appropriately.

Escalation procedures should be in place for older people who screen positive for depression [29]. Research examining the implementation of a national depression screening program in Australian residential aged care homes concluded that the absence of a protocol for responding to high scores limited its potential to meaningfully improve outcomes [30]. In EMBED, it is recommended that the older person is escalated to an appropriately trained clinician within the aged care service or the older person's general practitioner (GP) for further assessment and consideration of treatment options.

Clinical Assessment

While screening indicates those who are at potential risk of depression, a more detailed assessment is required to guide treatment and management plans. Although the diagnosis of a mental health disorder is typically out of scope for aged care professionals [5], nurses and allied health professionals working in the home care setting can be trained in the use of structured assessment tools. In organisations without available clinicians, the EMBED model of care recommends that older people be escalated to their GP for further assessment. Instruments recommended for use with older adults include the Geriatric Depression Scale‐15 item [31], which is simple to administer, as well as the 9‐item Patient Health Questionnaire [32], which also provides an indication of suicidal ideation, which is common in this population. The Cornell Scale for Depression in Dementia [33] is suitable for aged care recipients with cognitive impairment and dementia, although it requires an interview with an informant familiar with the older person, and assessors should be well‐trained in the use of the scale [30].

Communication of scores on validated instruments to the older person's GP and other healthcare professionals involved in their care may help to address the barrier previously noted by aged care providers in accessing external treatment and support for their clients [5]. Sharing any information relevant to the impact of depressive symptoms on the older person's day‐to‐day functioning and duration of symptoms, if available, would also be helpful for treating practitioners.

Psychological Treatment for Depression

Psychological treatment is recommended as a first‐line treatment for older people with mild‐to‐moderate symptoms of depression [13], with evidence of its effectiveness for subthreshold conditions as well as for Major Depressive Disorder [34, 35]. Responding early to indications of mild depression is important to prevent escalation to more severe or intractable disorders [36]. While there is less evidence in home care [37] and an absence of Australian research, clinical trials have supported the use of multiple types of psychological therapy for depression in residential aged care homes, with outcomes maintained for up to 6 months [38].

Behavioural approaches appear particularly promising for the home care setting, including behavioural activation, which involves the gradual introduction of pleasurable and meaningful activities into the person's daily routine. This therapy has established effectiveness for late‐life depression [39] and has been effectively delivered to older people by non‐specialist healthcare personnel [40] and lay counsellors [41], including via telephone [42] and videoconference [41]. There is also growing interest in the use of ‘lifestyle interventions’ for depression, which support older people to adopt healthier lifestyle behaviours, focussing on aspects such as diet, sleep, physical activity and social connection. These approaches may be more acceptable than traditional forms of ‘talk therapy’ to the large proportion of older people who do not acknowledge being depressed and who may be reluctant to engage in formal mental health treatment [43, 44, 45]. Lifestyle therapy is recommended in several treatment guidelines for depression [9, 10, 45, 46].

The EMBED model of care includes a brief psychological intervention that was developed specifically for the home care setting, based on lifestyle therapy and behavioural activation. Both approaches can be delivered as part of low‐intensity interventions, including in self‐help programs, guided by trained non‐mental health specialists. Encouraging older people to select preferred treatment goals and strategies is likely to increase treatment engagement and motivation [47], and so the EMBED intervention is tailored to older people's symptoms, needs and preferences.

While we lack evidence around the ideal number of treatment sessions for home care recipients, in a recently published Cochrane review that found support for psychological interventions in residential aged care homes, interventions ranged from two to 10 sessions [38], indicating that brief interventions can be suitable for older people. Recognising the high prevalence of depression in the home care setting and the shortage of available workforces—both mental health and aged care—it is important to tailor treatment intensity to level of severity. The EMBED model employs a stratified care approach, where those with more severe symptoms are assigned to a more intensive intervention, which was found to be preferable to a stepped care approach [48]. Following an assessment, home care recipients with mild symptoms of depression select their preferred lifestyle module (from social connection, physical activity, diet, sleep hygiene, mindfulness and relaxation training) and are provided with a set of self‐help resources. They also receive guidance and support from an EMBED clinician over two sessions plus two brief telephone check‐ins. Those with moderate symptoms also select a lifestyle module, plus participate in a behavioural activation program, with a total of six sessions with an EMBED clinician. For older people with more severe symptoms of depression, up to 10 sessions are provided, with an additional focus on collaboration with their GP or other health professionals.

Psychological treatments can be delivered by primary and specialist healthcare providers in the broader community. Delivery via telehealth addresses the difficulties many older people experience accessing face‐to‐face therapy and has been found to be effective with home‐bound older adults [41]. Telephone‐delivered psychological therapy is acceptable among older adults [49] and at least as effective as face‐to‐face delivery modes [36, 50]. An EMBED digital app, which provides older people with access to a broad range of digital resources, including links to educational videos, mood tracking and activity reminders, is also available for those who welcome this approach. The emergence of digital approaches to mental health care offers promise as a cost‐effective and scalable approach to address depression within home care [51]. However, digital solutions should be tailored to the specific characteristics of the older home care population and aligned with older people's digital literacy and access to technology, and technical support should be readily available.

The potential for psychological therapy to be directly delivered by aged care providers in Australia requires exploration. There are significant advantages of embedding psychological therapy into routine aged care service delivery, with many older people finding it acceptable to receive emotional support from their aged care provider in their own home, rather than visit a healthcare professional [44]. Nurses and allied health professionals, such as social workers and occupational therapists, already provide government funded aged care services in Australia and could be trained to deliver simple psychological interventions such as lifestyle programs and behavioural activation as part of standard care. There is evidence to support this approach in both the United States and United Kingdom, where researchers have successfully trained lay counsellors and non‐specialist support workers to deliver behavioural activation programs to older people via telehealth and demonstrated reductions in symptoms of depression [42].

Implementation of an Embedded Model of Care

While the individual components of the EMBED model of care have an established evidence base, research is required to evaluate their effectiveness and cost‐effectiveness in the Australian home care setting. Key enabling factors and barriers need to be determined for this context and barriers addressed prior to scaling up. An integrative implementation approach—involving collaboration between aged care and healthcare providers—may be of greatest benefit. Multicomponent collaborative care programs for depression have been successfully implemented into home care settings in the United States and Canada, where nurses or social workers conduct screening and provide psychoeducation and simple psychological interventions, such as goal setting and behavioural activation during routine visits, with input from a psychiatric specialist or GP, and/or referral and linkage to healthcare services as required [52, 53, 54]. Positive outcomes have been reported, including reduced hospitalisations in depressed home healthcare recipients [52]. Implementation of a similar approach in Australia could provide a much‐needed and viable care pathway for the growing population of depressed older people not currently identified or managed appropriately by healthcare providers.

Conclusions

Depression is an increasing concern among older Australians receiving home care, yet current systems do not routinely identify symptoms or enable timely access to evidence‐based treatment. As this population becomes more clinically complex, aged care providers need scalable models that support early detection, appropriate escalation and care tailored to older people's symptoms, needs and preferences. The EMBED model addresses this gap by integrating workforce training, routine screening, clinical assessment and brief psychological intervention into usual home care delivery. Further research is needed to evaluate its effectiveness, cost‐effectiveness and implementation requirements of EMBED in the Australian home care context. If successful, this model could offer a feasible pathway for aged care providers and health services to work together to improve the recognition and management of depression among older people living at home.
