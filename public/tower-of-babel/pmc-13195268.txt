Concurrent Vulvar and Cutaneous Melanoma in a 40-Year-Old Woman: A Rare Case of Multiple Primary Melanomas
Authors: Samantha J. Leite, Julia Asada, Tori Smith, Jonathan Jeter, Christopher M. Tarney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Rare case of synchronous vulvovaginal and cutaneous melanoma in a 40-year-old woman.•Negative 27-gene germline panel reinforces that significant melanoma risk can arise without high-penetrance variants.•Distinct clinicopathologic features indicate multiple primaries rather than metastatic disease.•Management required multidisciplinary coordination and repeat excision for margin control.•Selective use of Mohs surgery aided in tissue preservation for multifocal vulvar melanoma in situ.

Introduction

An estimated 104,960 new cases of invasive melanoma were diagnosed in the United States in 2025, with cutaneous melanoma comprising the vast majority. (SEER. Cancer Stat Facts: Melanoma of the Skin: National Cancer Institute;, 2026) Vulvovaginal melanoma is a rare and distinct subclass, with a reported incidence of 0.136 cases per 100,000 women, accounting for fewer than 1% of all melanomas and approximately 5–10% of vulvar malignancies, yet associated with disproportionately poor survival outcomes compared to cutaneous melanoma. (Wilhite et al., 2024) Women with vulvar melanoma have five-year survival rates ranging from 63% to as low as 27% when the vaginal mucosa is involved. (Wohlmuth et al., 2020) While cutaneous melanoma is closely linked to ultraviolet (UV) exposure, the etiologic drivers of mucosal melanoma are less well understood. (Wilhite et al., 2024) Outcomes are further worsened in the approximately 4.5% of melanoma patients who develop multiple primary melanomas (MPMs). These lesions may arise either sequentially or synchronously, and are more common in individuals with a family history of melanoma, who tend to be diagnosed at an earlier age. (Spadafora et al., 2025, Ferrone et al., 2005) We present a case of synchronous vulvovaginal and cutaneous melanomas in a 40-year-old woman without a family history of cancer, highlighting the diagnostic, surgical, and multidisciplinary complexities of this rare presentation.

Case Presentation

A 40-year-old Caucasian woman presented for routine cervical cancer screening. She denied a personal or family cancer history but reported extensive tanning bed use and remote tobacco exposure. Gynecologic exam revealed multiple sub-centimeter irregularly pigmented macules involving the left labia minora and posterior fourchette with extension medial to Hart’s line (Fig. 2A). Punch biopsies of the vulvar lesions demonstrated melanoma in situ (MIS) with focal invasive dermal nests, severe cytologic atypia, and pagetoid spread (Fig. 1A–B). She was promptly referred to dermatology for full body skin examination, during which a 6 mm brown papule with an irregularly shaped dark-brown macule centrally was noted on the left buttock (Fig. 2B). Shave biopsy demonstrated invasive superficial spreading melanoma, Breslow depth 0.3 mm (pT1a), with Melan-A and PRAME immunostaining confirming melanocytic origin. After multidisciplinary discussion, the typical histological features of a thin cutaneous superficial spreading melanoma on the buttock and extensive in situ mucosal disease on the vulva, combined with the absence of visceral or distant nodal metastasis on staging PET/CT, supported interpretation of two synchronous primary melanomas rather than epidermotropic metastatic disease. Pelvic MRI demonstrated mildly prominent inguinal lymph nodes and PET/CT showed vulvar hypermetabolism, raising concern for occult stromal invasion not fully captured on limited biopsy sampling. Notably, the posterior fourchette biopsy did demonstrate focal invasive dermal nests, suggesting that the degree of invasion in the broader lesion field may have been underrepresented. Because definitive wide excision of the multifocal midline disease would likely disrupt lymphatic channels and impair future sentinel node mapping, inguinal sentinel lymph node biopsy was discussed in a multidisciplinary setting and pursued at the time of surgery through shared decision-making with the patient.

Because the vulvar pigmentation was multifocal, clinically extensive, and extended across midline mucosal structures including the posterior fourchette, bilateral modified radical vulvectomy with inguinal sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) was performed by gynecologic oncology. Final surgical pathology demonstrated melanoma with Breslow depth 0.9 mm (pT1b) with persistent positivity at central margins, confirming occult invasive disease and prompting immediate re-excision (Fig. 1C–D). All sentinel nodes and one pathologically enlarged lymph node were negative for melanoma or carcinoma.

Three months postoperatively, a new pigmented area on the right mons and right superior labia was biopsied and again showed MIS (Fig. 2D). A right simple vulvectomy with excision of the mons returned benign histology, suggesting the lesion had been fully removed by the biopsy. Given persistent concern for residual in situ disease in the right superior labia, the patient was referred for MMS to optimize margin control while preserving vulvar anatomy. MMS did not reveal any residual MIS and verified negative margins.

Comprehensive germline panel testing of 27 hereditary cancer genes, including CDKN2A, BAP1, MITF, and BRCA1/2, identified no pathogenic variants. Given the patient’s dual primary melanomas and mucosal involvement, surveillance was adapted from NCCN cutaneous melanoma guidelines for stage IIB-equivalent risk, incorporating quarterly dermatologic and pelvic examinations and groin ultrasound every 3–6 months, with interval PET/CT imaging reserved for concerning findings. The patient has been followed for approximately 10 months since definitive vulvar surgery and remains without evidence of recurrent invasive disease.

Discussion

Synchronous Primaries Versus Metastasis

Distinguishing synchronous multiple primary melanomas from metastatic disease is a key diagnostic challenge when patients present with more than one melanocytic lesion, as the distinction directly determines treatment approach. In this case, several clinical and histopathologic features supported the interpretation of two independent primaries rather than metastatic spread. The posterior fourchette biopsy demonstrated a predominantly junctional atypical melanocytic proliferation with focal dermal invasion, extensive pagetoid spread, and diffuse PRAME positivity (Fig. 1A–B), consistent with a primary mucosal melanoma arising de novo. The buttock lesion, in contrast, was a thin pT1a cutaneous superficial spreading melanoma arising in association with a pre-existing dermal nevus (Fig. 2B), a common pattern in primary cutaneous disease and not a feature of metastatic spread. These patterns differ from epidermotropic metastatic malignant melanoma (EMMM), a rare phenomenon in which metastatic deposits track along the epidermis and may histologically mimic primary melanoma in situ. (Abernethy et al., 1994) Reported cases of EMMM often occur in patients with advanced melanoma and widespread disease, including deep dermal involvement and visceral metastases. This patient had no visceral or distant nodal disease on staging PET/CT and the single mildly prominent inguinal node on MRI was benign. The laterally distinct and anatomically separated lesions, their divergent histologic patterns, and the absence of systemic metastatic disease collectively make synchronous multiple primaries far more likely than metastatic disease in this presentation.

Epidemiology and Risk Factors

Mucosal vulvovaginal melanoma represents a distinct biological entity with a poorer prognosis than its cutaneous counterpart. Most women are postmenopausal at time of diagnosis with an average age of 62, making this early 40 s presentation unusual. (Albert et al., 2020) Tanning bed use before age 45 is associated with a markedly increased risk of cutaneous melanoma and likely contributed to this patient’s left buttock lesion. (Ting et al., 2007) Unlike cutaneous disease, where intermittent sun exposure and sunburns are well-established risk factors, the etiologic drivers of mucosal melanoma are not fully understood. (Boer et al., 2019).

Genetic predisposition is an important consideration in MPMs as CDKN2A mutations carry high lifetime penetrance in mutation-positive families. However, many patients with early-onset MPMs have negative germline panel testing, indicating that environmental influences, polygenic risk, or uncharacterized genetic factors likely contribute. (Ferguson et al., 2019) The negative 27-gene hereditary panel in this case, encompassing CDKN2A, BAP1, MC1R, MITF, BRCA1/2, and TP53 among others, aligns with this pattern and reinforces that substantial melanoma risk can exist in the absence of identifiable high penetrance germline variants. In this case, somatic tumor genomic profiling of the lesions was not performed, consistent with current practice in which molecular profiling is reserved for patients with advanced or metastatic disease being considered for systemic therapy. In the setting of recurrence or unresectable disease, tumor profiling for BRAF, NRAS, KIT, and NF1 mutations, as well as mismatch repair status, would be pursued to guide systemic therapy selection.

Vulvar Melanoma Management Considerations

The management of vulvovaginal melanoma poses unique challenges due to its biologic behavior and the anatomic complexity of the vulvar region. (Dika et al., 2021) Although early-stage vulvar melanoma is managed surgically, routine molecular profiling of vulvar melanoma is likely to become increasingly important, both to identify candidates for targeted or immunotherapy in the event of recurrence and to refine prognostic assessment. Incorporating genomic data into multidisciplinary decision making may eventually allow more individualized surveillance schedules and adjuvant treatment strategies for patients with high-risk features or MPMs. (Wilhite et al., 2024, Dika et al., 2021).

Surgical excision remains the primary treatment for localized disease, yet achieving margin-negative resection is difficult due to subclinical spread, multifocal in situ disease, and the proximity of lesions to critical urinary and sexual structures, all of which contribute to a high risk of margin positivity even with planned wide margins. (Straubhar et al., 2023) The repeated need for re–excision in this patient illustrates the practical difficulty of achieving durable local control without resorting to more radical and morbid surgical approaches. Against this backdrop of challenging local control and substantial functional morbidity, margin–directed approaches that maximize tissue preservation are particularly important.

Role of Mohs Micrographic Surgery and Lymph Node Evaluation

MMS has emerged as a potential adjunct in selected patients with melanoma in situ or thin melanomas in anatomically sensitive regions. (Falcicchio et al., 2022) Its ability to provide complete circumferential peripheral and deep margin assessment while sparing uninvolved tissue is particularly valuable in the vulvar region, where radical excisions can carry significant functional and psychosocial morbidity. Dermatology was involved from the time of initial diagnosis, and MMS was discussed as part of the initial management planning. However, given the location of the vulvar lesions and anticipated need for deeper soft-tissue resection, the multidisciplinary team determined that gynecologic oncology-led resection was the most appropriate primary approach. MMS was subsequently employed for focal persistent in situ disease of the right superior labia and mons, where the more confined distribution allowed staged margin-controlled excision with preservation of vulvar architecture. While evidence for MMS in mucosal vulvovaginal melanoma remains limited, this case supports its selective use for persistent or recurrent in situ disease in anatomically sensitive regions.

SLNB remains central to staging invasive melanomas and is recommended for lesions greater than 0.8 mm in Breslow depth or for thinner tumors with high-risk features per ASCO/SSO guidelines. (Wong et al., 2018) In this case, SLNB was not pursued for a pre-invasive indication. Rather, the preoperative clinical picture, including focal dermal invasion on the posterior fourchette biopsy, vulvar hypermetabolism on PET/CT, and mildly prominent inguinal nodes on MRI, raised sufficient concern for occult invasion to warrant sentinel node evaluation at the time of definitive surgery through multidisciplinary discussion and shared decision-making. Final pathology confirming Breslow depth 0.9 mm validated this concern. In vulvar melanoma, SLNB carries important prognostic value given the higher likelihood of regional nodal spread; the negative nodal results in this patient were reassuring, though nodal negativity alone does not eliminate the substantial risk of local recurrence inherent to mucosal melanoma. (Straubhar et al., 2023).

Surveillance and Prognosis

For cutaneous melanoma, NCCN guidelines for stage IIB disease recommend physical examination every 3–6 months for the first two years, then every 3–12 months in years 3–5, with annual examinations thereafter. (Wong et al., 2018) Imaging, including groin ultrasound every 3–6 months and consideration of PET/CT every 3–12 months based on clinical concern, is recommended for high risk patients during this period. Vulvar-specific surveillance guidelines are not well established, and many centers adapt protocols from vulvar squamous cell carcinoma management, including pelvic examinations every 3–4 months for the first two years and biannually in years 3–5. In this case, given the diagnosis of multiple primary melanomas and high-risk features of mucosal involvement, surveillance was adapted from NCCN stage IIB cutaneous melanoma framework. The patient has been followed by both gynecologic oncology and dermatology in an integrated multidisciplinary fashion, with a subsequent right superior labial lesion identified on full-body skin exam, underscoring the value of this coordinated approach.

The long-term prognosis for this patient must be considered in the context of both vulvar melanoma and MPMs. Vulvar melanoma is associated with significantly lower survival rates than cutaneous melanoma, even at early stages. (Albert et al., 2020) Patients with MPMs have worse outcomes than those with a single primary tumor, likely reflecting cumulative mutagenic burden and shared environmental exposures. (Ferrone et al., 2005) MPMs in a young woman without a high penetrance germline variant are highly unusual and raise important questions about shared etiologic pathways and the adequacy of current surveillance frameworks for this population.

Multidisciplinary Care Gaps

This case illustrates a broader system-level challenge: the fragmented nature of care for patients with vulvar melanoma, who may present to gynecology, dermatology, or both, often without a structured pathway for cross-specialty coordination. To supplement this case report, a targeted narrative review of English-language literature from 2000 to 2025 was performed using joint gynecologic and dermatologic search terms to identify themes in multidisciplinary management of vulvar melanoma. The available literature consists predominantly of case series and small retrospective cohorts, with only a limited number of studies addressing integrated multidisciplinary care outcomes. Dermatology literature tended to focus on epidemiology and case-specific surgical management, while gynecologic oncology literature emphasized staging, survival and rarer malignancies, with comparatively little overlap or joint authorship between specialties. This bibliographic divide reflects a real clinical gap: in primary vulvar melanoma, prompt referral for coordinated diagnostic work-up remains inconsistently implemented, despite evidence suggesting that multidisciplinary collaboration may reduce time to diagnosis, improve surgical planning, and enhance patient satisfaction. (Cebolla-Verdugo et al., 2024) This patient benefited from early and ongoing dermatologic involvement, coordinated pathologic review with dermatopathology consultation on all specimens and joint management by gynecologic oncology and dermatology throughout her entire disease course. However, this level of integration is not universally routine. Best practices call for increased awareness of this care gap and the development of structured pathways that incorporate dermatologic assessment, gynecologic oncologic expertise, dermatopathology, and when appropriate, medical oncology and radiation oncology from the time of initial diagnosis.

Conclusions

This case illustrates an uncommon presentation of synchronous vulvar and cutaneous melanoma in a young woman, emphasizing the complex interplay of vulvovaginal melanoma biology, UV-associated cutaneous melanoma, and the broader phenomenon of multiple primary melanomas. Extensive tanning bed use likely contributed to the early-onset cutaneous lesion, while negative germline testing of 27 hereditary cancer genes reinforces that significant melanoma risk can arise without identifiable high penetrance variants. The pathological finding of focal dermal invasion on preoperative biopsy, despite an initial clinical impression of in situ disease, highlights the importance of thorough histopathological sampling and the potential for occult invasion to be underrepresented on limited biopsy alone. The difficulty achieving clear surgical margins, coupled with multifocal in situ disease requiring two separate Mohs procedures, reflects the anatomic and biologic challenges inherent to vulvar melanoma and supports the selective use of MMS to optimize margin control with maximal tissue preservation. Given the worse prognosis associated with both vulvar melanoma and multiple primary melanomas, structured long-term surveillance coordinated across gynecologic oncology and dermatology is essential. The limited evidence base for vulvar melanoma highlights an urgent need for integrated, specialty-spanning guidelines. Future research should aim to refine risk stratification and develop collaborative care models that can improve outcomes for patients with complex presentations such as synchronous vulvar and cutaneous primary melanomas.
