Effects of iron and silver nanoparticles on Ctenopharyngodon idella’s swimming performance, their bioaccumulation, histological changes, and oxidative stress
Authors: Mian Adnan Kakakhel, Nishita Narwal, Heba Mahboub, Feras Darwish Elhajji, Salman Khan, Shi Xiaotao, Mutwakel Dabiellil, Muhammad Sarfaraz Iqbal, Esmael M. Alyami, Omar A. Almohammed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanoparticles (NPs) are small-sized particles that have various applications; however, these metallic particles are found to be toxic against aquatic organisms. Therefore, the current study observed the toxicity of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) and iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs) using Ctenopharyngodon idella by swimming behavior, bioaccumulation, histopathological alterations, and oxidative stress. Fish were exposed to AgNPs and IONPs (5.16 mg/L) for 10 days to assess their possible effects on behavioral and physiological changes. Results showed that both NPs significantly decreased the critical swimming speed (Ucrit), and found a significant correlation between body size and swimming speed. The NPs were significantly accumulated in the intestine, followed by muscles, gills, liver, and brain (p < 0.05), which resulted in histological alterations, i.e., necrosis in gill lamella and grey matter disruption in brain. In addition, the NPs resulted in oxidative stress, i.e., the superoxide dismutase enzyme in the gill was significantly increased (291.66 ± 4.04) in the IONPs group. The possible mechanisms include NP-induced gill damage and altered gill morphology, which may impair respiratory efficiency, ROS, and osmoregulation, leading to reduced swimming performance.

Introduction

Nanotechnology is the interdisciplinary field involving the design, synthesis, characterization, and application of nano-sized (1–100 nm) materials [1], known as nanoparticles (NPs) [2]. NPs have a diversity of applications [3], i.e., silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have been documented as strong antibacterial agents due to the antibacterial resistance, for food preservation, textile coatings, wastewater treatment [4–6], and fish disease treatment. In contrast, iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs) are being widely utilized in the biomedical field, targeted drug delivery [7, 8], and environmental remediation [9, 10]. However, the toxicity, which is caused by these NPs in aquatic ecosystems remain a matter of intense debate. The increasing release of silver and iron oxide nanoparticles into aquatic environments poses a significant ecological threat to freshwater fish. However, limited research exists on their chronic sublethal effects on swimming performance, tissue accumulation, and histology in Ctenopharyngodon idella, highlighting a critical knowledge gap that this study aims to address.

The nanoparticles are not only directly released into aquatic ecosystems [11], for instance, AgNPs are released during washing processes and enter the water stream [12]; however, other NPs used in sunscreens and cosmetics, are washed off during bathing introduced into water bodies, which are the common sources of contamination in aquatic environments [13, 14]. Studies indicate that wastewater treatment plants serve as significant sources of NPs in waterbodies globally [15]. For example, the urban river deltas of Chilean Rivers found 17.6–22.0 µg/L of Titanium oxide nanoparticles (TiO2 NPs), black sea coastal areas (TiO2NPs 0.1–1.0 µg/L), moreover, a Chithrapuzha, in the India is contaminated by IONPs (0.88–0.92 µg/L) [16–18], and AgNPs (1–5 ng/L) globally in different rivers [19]. A study reported that Laizhou Bay, China, was found mostly contaminated by Ti-based NPs (1.78 × 108 particles/L) [20]. Additionally, the Yellow River Estuary, Lanzhou, China, has been identified as one of the high-risk zones for NPs, specifically AgNPs and TiO2NPs, as a result of industrial inputs [21]. The above-mentioned studies highlight the significant prevalence of NPs in freshwater ecosystem, further studies are warranted.

In contaminated water, the fish ingest NPs from the surrounding environment in the food chain [22], resulting in severe toxicity in fish. For example, affecting fish physiology and metabolism [23, 24]. A previous study for instance demonstrated a significant effects of AgNPs on hematological parameters of Oncorhynchus mykiss, revealing notable reductions in red blood cells (RBC) counts, hemoglobin level, and hematocrit values [25]. IONPs has been reported in Labeo rohita fingerlings to cause reactive oxygen species (ROS) with increasing superoxide dismutase (SOD) catalase (CAT), and malate dehydrogenase (MDH) respectively [26]. Recently, Clarias gariepinus (50.71 ± 3.85 g) was exposed to AgNPs, which showed abnormal behavior such as swimming movement, restlessness, and aggressiveness [27]. However, there is still a significant scientific gap in studying the effects of NPs on fish swimming behavior and swimming performance.

The grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella) is a potamodromous fish species that is primarily prevalent in China and is a member of the Cyprinidae family [28]. The C. idella is a model fish organism that is mainly used in behavioral and toxicological studies. Subsequently, the juvenile C. idella were used in the current study to assess the effect of IONPs and AgNPs on fish swimming performance, bioaccumulation, and histological alterations. The bioaccumulation and histological changes were observed in the gills, intestine, brain, and muscles. Unlike AgNPs and IONPs bioaccumulation, tissue specific effects, and particularly impact on critical behaviors like swimming performance in the fish are limited; therefore, accumulation with histological alterations were measured with a direct correlation with fish swimming impair performance.

Materials and methods

Before starting the experiment, all the tanks were well washed with potassium permanganate (KMnO4) and table salt (NaCl). The NPs was locally purchased from Guangdong Yinna Technology Co., Ltd, China. The IONPs and AgNPs were characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD) (Rigaku; SmartLab) and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) (Model: JEOL; JSM-6010PLUS/LV).. The NPs were further added to deionized water and sonicated to treat fish for their possible behavioral and physiological toxicity.

Fish culture and experimental setup

Various sizes (4–7 cm) of Ctenopharyngodon idella were purchased from a local fish market in Yichang, China. The fish were randomly distributed across three identical 30-L tanks equipped with continuous aeration and filtration systems. They were acclimated for one week under laboratory conditions to ensure their adaptation. During acclimation, fish were fed twice daily (08:00 and 20:00 h) and maintained at an ambient temperature of 26–28 °C, pH 7.5–8.0, dissolved oxygen ~ 6.0 mg/L, and a 10:14 h light:dark photoperiod [29]. The test concentration of 5.16 mg/L for both AgNPs and IONPs was selected following a preliminary 96-h range-finding bioassay conducted under the same laboratory conditions as the definitive study. In this assay, juvenile Ctenopharyngodon idella were exposed to nominal concentrations of 1.0, 2.5, 5.0, and 7.0 mg/L of each NPs type. At 5.16 mg/L, no mortality was recorded after 96 h, whereas a statistically significant reduction in swimming activity (total distance moved and time spent active) was observed compared with the control group, without severe morphological abnormalities or loss of equilibrium. In contrast, concentrations ≥ 7.0 mg/L resulted in mortality and pronounced behavioral impairment (e.g., loss of equilibrium and erratic swimming), which would have compromised the reliable assessment of critical swimming speed (Ucrit), tissue bioaccumulation, histopathology, and oxidative stress biomarkers over the 10-days exposure period. Consequently, 5.16 mg/L was chosen as the lowest observed effect concentration (LOEC) for swimming behavior that permitted 100% survival and enabled an integrative multi-biomarker evaluation.

The fish were divided into three groups: the control group (n = 14; 0.0 mg/L), the IONPs group (n = 14; 5.16 mg/L), and the AgNPs group (n = 14; 5.16 mg/L). Thirty fish were included in each cohort, of which 14 individuals per group were assessed for swimming performance. The fish were exposed to nanoparticle-contaminated water for 10 days to evaluate behavioral and physiological changes.

Bioaccumulation of IONPs and AgNPs

The isolated organs were thoroughly washed, dried, and placed in a muffle furnace at 500 °C for 2 h, which was allowed to complete ash. After digestion, 10 mL of 6 mM HCl was added to 1 g of the organ’s ash and completely dissolved in HCl. Furthermore, the solution was filtered using Whatman filter paper, and the filtrate was subjected to analytical assessment using Inductively Coupled Plasma Atomic Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-MS, Agilent Technologies, 5100) to check the deposition of IONPs and AgNPs in fish organs. Additionally, to understand the adsorption mechanism of NPs in the fish body, the Langmuir and the Freundlich isotherm models were applied using a mathematical formula.\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Adsorption capacity \left(qe\right)=\frac{\left(Ci-Cf\right)V}{m}$$\end{document}where Ci is the initial concentration and Cf is the final concentration of NPs. V is the volume, and m is the moles of solution.

The use of Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms in bioaccumulation analysis provides a mechanistic framework for understanding how nanoparticles interact with fish tissues. The Langmuir model assumes homogeneous binding sites and monolayer adsorption, helping to estimate maximum bioaccumulation capacity, while the Freundlich model accounts for heterogeneous surface binding and describes the affinity and intensity of nanoparticle accumulation. Together, these isotherms clarify whether bioaccumulation occurs through specific, saturable binding or through non-specific, multilayer interactions, offering insights into the underlying toxicokinetic processes and tissue retention mechanisms.

Oxidative stress biomarkers

Three fish per group were used to measure the catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) enzymes in liver and gills tissues using a commercially available enzyme assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

Histological observation caused by NPs

The isolated tissues, including gills, intestine, brain, and muscles, were used for histological analysis associated with NPs exposure. Fresh tissue samples (e.g., liver and muscle) were carefully excised and immediately fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24–48 h. The fixed tissues were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin wax. Sections of approximately 4–5 μm thickness were prepared using a microtome and mounted on glass slides. The sections were subsequently stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to examine general tissue architecture by following a previous study [35]. Microscopic observations were performed using a light microscope, and histopathological changes were documented and compared across samples. The prepared samples were visualized with a digital microscope, BA210 Digital. Additionally, the tissues were measured using eosin and hematoxylin under a light microscope. The illustration was further analyzed and measured using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All the experimental data were conducted in replicates and were tested for normality and presented as means with standard deviation (SD). The swimming movement exhibited by C. idella could be influenced by their body size; therefore, linear regression was applied to identify the relationship between body length (cm) and swimming speed (BL/s) using GraphPad Prism 10. Additionally, one-way ANOVA followed by post-hoc was used to find out the variation in swimming speed between the control and treatment groups. The p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The current study is of considerable significance for multiple reasons. It examines the environmental impact of NPs present in aquatic ecosystems as a result of industrial runoff and their effect on fish swimming behavior. The swimming behavior and swimming performance of fish are regarded as crucial aspects of their migration for ecology and diversity [37, 38]. Fish may ingest NPs, leading to accumulation in various organs and resulting in histological alterations, particularly in the brain, which induce neurotoxic effects. The neurotoxicity caused by exposure to NPs could be the possible reason for impaired swimming performance.

C. idella swimming test

The sensitivity of the fish behavior to contaminants is essential for evaluating toxic responses and their impact on fish health, i.e., poor swimming performance of fish is an early indicator of a substantial health risk [39, 40]. Additionally, critical swimming speed (Ucrit) is adopted as an indicator of the swimming capability of fish [41]. Therefore, two types of NPs were used to assess the possible effects on Ucrit, bioaccumulation, and histological changes. Initially, the swimming speed was assessed by following exposure to NPs, revealing that both types of NPs influenced the fish swimming performance and behavior; however, the AgNPs significantly hindered the swimming speed in comparison to IONPs (p < 0.05). The behavioral changes observed in this study, i.e., irregular swimming performance, are consistent with findings from other studies that report a significant impact of NPs on fish behavior. For instance, a previous study reported a significant decrease in Salmo trutta after exposure to graphene-based NPs [42]. The NPs are toxic by nature [43]; hence, the NPs may induce hypoxia in fish [43], resulting in reduced muscle functioning and a change in swimming performance [44]. Copper II NPs (CuONPs) showed a significant decrease in swimming performance in Danio rerio by 34% and 55% at 100 and 200 µg/L respectively [45]. Conclusively, the impaired swimming performance means ecological fitness reduction of fish, which is likely pose a threat to fish ecology and biodiversity.

Bioaccumulation of nanoparticles

The behavioral factor of fish possibly be affected by the bioaccumulation of contaminants in various organs, such as the brain, which can cause neurotoxicity in fish [46], leading to poor swimming performance [47]. Another possible reason is likely to be the activation of NF-KB and pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α pathway, which results in tissue inflammation and necrosis, particularly in the liver [48]. In the current study, various amount of NPs were bioaccumulated in different organs such as the intestine, muscles, gills, and brain (p = 0.05). In comparison, the AgNPs were more accumulated than IONPs, consequently, the AgNPs also reduced the swimming performance. In our previous study, the animal blood-mediated silver NPs (B-AgNPs) were mostly bioaccumulated in the liver, followed by the intestine [49]. Another study observed a contrast result in Cyprinus carpio, for instance, IONPs were mostly accumulated by the liver instead of the intestine, which damaged the liver capsule [50]. Previous studies have extensively reported the toxic effects of AgNPs in different fish species, including C. idella. For instance, research indicated that AgNPs accumulate significantly in gills, intestine, and muscles of C. idella, resulting in oxidative stress and histological alterations [51], which align with our current findings. In our study, the bioaccumulation of NPs was further analyzed using a kinetics model. Both the Freundlich and Langmuir models may significantly evaluate the adsorption behavior with specific interaction of NPs with fish organs [52]. In conclusion, the differential bioaccumulation of nanoparticles across fish organs, particularly the higher accumulation of AgNPs, significantly impairs physiological functions, including swimming behavior and tissue integrity. These findings highlight the role of nanoparticle-induced oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways in driving organ-specific toxicity, emphasizing potential ecological risks.

Biomarker enzymes

The stress in any organism is represented by the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [53], which is likely results in cell damage and apoptosis [54]. Hence, the oxidative stress can be confirmed by the enzymes in fish [55]. Additionally, the liver is a central organ for detoxification, metabolism, and NPs sequestration in fish [56]. Therefore, oxidative stress biomarker in the liver provides critical insights into the systemic toxicity and the capacity of fish to respond to NPs-induced stress [57]. In addition, gills act as the primary interface between fish and waterborne contaminants, and their thin epithelial structure and high vascularization make them vulnerable to ROS-mediated damage [58]. Oxidative stress in gills reflects early toxicological responses and barrier dysfunction [59]. In this study, the findings showed that SOD (291.66 ± 4.04) and CAT (1.24 ± 0.05) were found significantly increased in C. idella’s liver after exposure to AgNPs. Furthermore, the SOD (61.79 ± 8.21) in gills was significantly decreased in AgNPs group and CAT (0.14 ± 0.01) in IONPs. These enzymes play a critical role in the antioxidant defense system of fish and mitigate oxidative stress [60]. The NPs penetrate the cell membrane, leading to disruption of normal cellular function and causing oxidative stress [61]. The role of ROS generation by NPs in disrupting cellular redox balance, impairing mitochondrial function in muscle tissue [62], the possible reason for the poor swimming performance in C. idella.

Histological changes caused by NPs

Nanoparticles may lead to significant damage in fish tissues by several mechanisms [63]. Studies showed that the NPs reach higher concentrations in different organs, which can lead to toxicity and histological alterations [64.] Similarly, in this study, the IONPs and AgNPs were initially accumulated and resulted in histological alterations, for instance, increased lymphocyte production in the intestinal tissue by AgNPs. Histological studies have shown that exposure to NPs may result in cellular damage, such as necrosis and degeneration of tissue [65], which were also reported in our study by the exposure of AgNPs and IONPs. Some NPs cross the blood barrier, reach the brain, and accumulate [66] resulting in neurotoxic effects and behavioral change [67]. Which was found consistent with current results.

Effects of NPs on the length

Exposure to NPs resulted in pronounced alterations in fish tissue. Such as variations in muscle fiber size, thickness of the intestinal mucosal layer, and thickness of central white matter layer. The findings showed a significant variation in the sizes of organs between the control and treatment groups. For example, ID 1 (1616.38 µm; control), (2120.32 µm; IONPs), and (2125.55 µm; AgNPs) in the gill of grass carp. Herein, the AgNPs significantly increased the size of the gill lamella, followed by IONPs. The gill is the major site for gaseous exchange, exposed to environmental pollutants [68], which exhibit structural changes, i.e., hypertrophy [69]. The brain was also analyzed, for instance, (86.23 µm; control), (71.58 µm; IONPs), and (57.70 µm; AgNPs) were observed in the thickness of the central white matter layer. The thickness of brain meninges was also found to be different between the treatment and control groups, as given in ID 10 (6.37 µm; control), (8.10 µm; IONPs), and (7.10 µm; AgNPs) in Fig. 8. Exposure to NPs may ROS, resulting in oxidative stress [70]. The stress by ROS can damage the oligodendrocytes and, therefore, decrease the thickness of the white matter layer in the brain [71].

Future recommendations and possible unexpected results

Despite a significant bioaccumulation and histological alterations in different organs were less severe than predicted, this could indicate compensatory mechanisms. For example, mucus secretion mitigating direct epithelial contacts or delayed pathology requiring longer exposure time. Additionally, although not initially analyzed; however, the gender discriminative results may be linked to reproductive physiology, which needs further investigation. Furthermore, future research should focus on elucidating the mechanism of toxicity associated with various-sized NPs and composition, as well as their long-term effects on fish health and ecosystem dynamics. Moreover, studies should explore the potential for mitigating these effects using dietary interventions, i.e., the inclusion of antioxidants or probiotics, to enhance the resilience of fish against NPs exposure. In addition, the study found some methodological limitations such as single exposure concentration, which limit the ability to assess dose-dependent effects and determine the threshold levels of toxicity. Secondly, without post-exposure depuration phase, it is unclear whether observed effects are reversible after NPs exposure conditions.

Conclusion

Based on the study results, silver and iron oxide NPs significantly affected the grass carp swimming performance, particularly AgNPs, which profoundly reduced the fish's swimming speed. The AgNPs and IONPs accumulated in different organs, including gills, liver, and brain, which may increase ROS production, resulting in oxidative stress in grass carp. Consequently, the grass carp showed a significant decrease in swimming performance, which is likely due to the oxidative stress caused by ROS generation. Further research is required to assess on long-term exposure of NPs on fish’s health and aquatic ecosystems.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that exposure to silver and iron oxide nanoparticles significantly impairs swimming performance, induces tissue-specific bioaccumulation, and causes notable histological alterations in C. idella. The application of Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms revealed that nanoparticle accumulation follows a heterogeneous, multilayer binding pattern, suggesting complex tissue interactions. These findings collectively address the study objectives by confirming that chronic nanoparticle exposure poses sublethal physiological risks, highlighting the need for stricter regulatory oversight of nanomaterial discharge into aquatic ecosystems.
