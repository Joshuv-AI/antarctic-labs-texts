Barrier identification and strategic design for hypertriglyceridemia management in the post-acute coronary syndrome patient population at a large integrated health system
Authors: Zachary M. Salvati, Nicole L. Walters, Andrew Brangan, Benjamin Keiser, Alicia Johns, Stephen J. Voyce, Luciano Kolodny, Sephy Philip, Laney K. Jones, Scott A. LeMaire, Caroline deRichemond
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesPatients on statin therapy with persistent hypertriglyceridemia (HTG) (TG > 150 mg/dL) post-acute coronary syndrome (ACS) are at increased risk of residual cardiovascular disease (CVD). Despite recommendations to incorporate icosapent ethyl (IPE) to reduce recurrent CV events, HTG care is not standardized. We aimed to 1) co-design a strategy package to improve CV risk management in post-ACS patients with HTG, and 2) assess TG care changes following the pilot.MethodsA pre-implementation survey assessed clinician perspectives of standardizing HTG management. Contextual inquiries informed a deliberative engagement session to co-design implementation strategies. One cardiology clinic piloted the final package. Resource use and electronic health record data were assessed for outcomes and care patterns.ResultsSurvey responses (N = 16) indicated that a standardized TG management approach is acceptable, appropriate, and feasible. Clinician interviews (N = 7) highlighted lack of priority in HTG care. IPE is favored for CV benefit, but coverage concerns persisted despite established institutional routinization. A strategy package to overcome identified barriers was finalized following deliberative engagement. 50% (20/40) of invited clinicians attended the pilot presentation, and 7.5% used additional resources and workflow strategies. Medication use was consistent among eligible patients, but 1.1% received IPE, limited to severe HTG (>500 mg/dL).ConclusionOperationalized clinician strategies had varied uptake, and IPE use remained minimal. An educational presentation from experts demonstrated importance of intervention but did not translate to clinician motivation. Findings highlight areas for improvement and inform early development of a standardized clinical pathway to reduce CVD risk in post-ACS patients with HTG.

Introduction

High-intensity statins remain the first-line therapy for patients following acute coronary syndrome (ACS) [1], [2], [3], with the goal of significantly reducing low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and mitigating recurrent major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) associated with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). However, patients on statin therapy with hypertriglyceridemia (HTG) (≥150 mg/dL) post-ACS are still at an increased residual cardiovascular risk [1], [2], [3].

Factors associated with atherogenic dyslipidemia include HTG, low HDL, and increased abundance of small, dense LDL particles. Isolated atherogenicity of persistent HTG has been noted, especially in mild to moderate cases of HTG [4], [5], [6]. HTG stimulates intimal inflammation, causing endothelial damage from foam cell formation and subsequent atherosclerotic plaque development [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]. Recent data suggest that TG-lowering therapies with proven CV risk reduction are underutilized in patients with HTG [7], [8].

Persistent HTG is a known risk factor for ASCVD [9], [10], and icosapent ethyl (IPE) can be used as a complementary treatment for this high-risk patient population who are already on first-line statin therapies without significant TG reduction [11]. The REDUCE-IT trial [12] highlighted a 25% MACE reduction in this patient population, serving as the primary catalyst for NLA to review the use of IPE for ASCVD risk reduction. The most recent joint statements [13], [14], [15] incorporate guidance similar to European guidelines [16], supporting targeted TG-lowering therapies for post-ACS patients on maximally tolerated statins with persistent HTG.

Despite evidence and guidelines to support TG-lowering therapies, caring for these patients is not institutionally standardized [7]. However, optimized care for this population has shown to significantly improve renal outcomes and all-cause mortality [17]. This study sought to improve clinician management of TG post-ACS through a mixed-methods implementation study by 1) designing and iterating an implementation strategy package for a pilot study, and 2) assessing the feasibility of the implementation strategy package in a cardiology practice.

Materials and methods

Site characteristics

Geisinger Health System is an integrated healthcare system serving ~1.2 million people in rural Pennsylvania with more than 26,000 employees, including 1600 employed physicians [18], and 10 hospital campuses across three distinct service areas. Geisinger's Medication Therapy Disease Management (MTDM) program [19] leverages the pharmacotherapy expertise of pharmacists to improve patient health outcomes related to chronic disease management.

Human-centered design and implementation strategies

Selection of an implementation strategy package and assessing the feasibility of the strategies were completed using aspects of human-centered design (HCD), a methodology used to improve healthcare interventions [20]. HCD studies often incorporate characteristics of empathy-building, user-driven inquiry, ideation, and further refinement through iteration [20], [21]. A portfolio of methods, including a pre-implementation survey, contextual inquiries encompassing site observations and semi-structured interviews, and a deliberative engagement meeting, was conducted to determine clinician readiness, synthesize the current state of post-ACS care in cardiology practices, identify care pathway barriers to HTG management, and tailor selected implementation strategies before pilot launch. This case study research was led by ZS, who chose a social constructivist interpretive framework to better understand the multiple realities constructed from clinician perspectives. Intervention penetration and any changes in post-ACS care were assessed using the electronic health record (EHR) following the pilot.

A priori implementation strategies were proposed upon study launch based on preliminary data, providing the study team with time to develop resources for the pilot (Table 1). Pre-implementation survey

A survey to understand the acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility of lipid management for post-ACS patients was constructed utilizing open-ended questions and the Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM), and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM) [23]. This validated survey was used to assess readiness for adoption of the implementation strategy package. Geisinger adult cardiology healthcare professionals, including physicians, advanced practice providers, and pharmacists, were invited to participate via email. The survey remained open for four weeks, with one reminder sent after two weeks. Survey respondents were offered a $10 gift card upon completion. Study data were collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at Geisinger [24]. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize quantitative data.

Contextual inquiries

Site observations and interviews were conducted to understand the current state of, and potential barriers to, lipid management for post-ACS patients at Geisinger. Geisinger adult cardiology healthcare professionals, including physicians, advanced practice providers, and pharmacists, were invited to participate via email. Participants could choose to complete observation only, interview only, or both.

Study staff conducted half-day observations in clinic sites throughout the healthcare system. Clinicians obtained verbal authorization from any patients before allowing study staff to join their appointment per clinical care procedures. Observations were documented using a templated form to capture general notes about the clinic, healthcare professional, and each visit observed, including management options, screening, and medications. Documentation was reviewed to contextualize findings from interviews and surveys.

A semi-structured interview guide was created to assess pre-implementation outcomes. Interviews took place virtually following the observation portion of the study, if applicable, and were conducted by study staff (ZS). Demographic questionnaires were completed at the end of each interview. Participants were offered a $50 gift card upon completion of the interview. Interviews were recorded, transcribed verbatim, and de-identified prior to analysis. A codebook guided by Proctor's Conceptual Model of Implementation Research (CMIR) was developed by a study team member with experience in qualitative methods and implementation science (ZS) [25]. The coding team was comprised of two study team members (ZS and NW). A deductive coding approach was used, with each coding team member independently coding one transcript to confirm the presence of CMIR constructs and refine the codebook. The remaining transcripts were split among the coding team for primary coding, followed by review and secondary coding by the other coder. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion among the coding team until consensus was reached [26]. ATLAS.ti (version 8.4.15.0) was used to segment the data by code [27]. Segmented data were reviewed to construct themes for each implementation outcome. Constructed themes were refined through coding team discussion and comparison among the implementation outcomes.

Alongside thematic coding, ZS created visual artifacts called process maps [28] to illustrate the current state of treating TG in the post-ACS patient population. These maps served as a baseline to visualize care pathways, helping identify process barriers to overcome and points of intervention to improve upon. Process maps were used to synthesize adoption data, readily conduct cross-case comparisons, and clearly present complex data at the deliberative engagement meeting.

Deliberative engagement meeting

A deliberative engagement meeting was held with separate clinicians outside of those interviewed to triangulate collected data, ensure generalizability of emerging themes, discuss ideas on how to overcome barriers to post-ACS lipid management, and present the implementation strategies developed by the study team for feedback and refinement. Geisinger healthcare professionals selected by the study team as potential champions were invited to participate via email. A semi-structured guide was created, and the discussion was facilitated by two members of the study team (ZS, CdR). The 60-min virtual meeting was recorded, transcribed verbatim, and de-identified. Participants were offered a $50 gift card upon completion of the meeting. Subsequent analysis of this transcript, partnered with the data analyzed from contextual inquiries, synthesized the main findings in the form of common themes as well as final direction on which elements to prioritize in an implementation strategy package. Qualitative data were analyzed using the same methodology as described for the interviews.

Pilot implementation

One cardiology clinic at Geisinger was selected to serve as the pilot implementation site. All cardiology healthcare professionals at that site, including physicians, advanced practice providers, and pharmacists, were invited to participate via email. The study team held an educational presentation on the current state of post-ACS care, guideline-based recommendations for hypertriglyceridemia management, and plans for the pilot during a regularly scheduled department meeting, which all healthcare professionals at the clinic were invited to attend. Attendance at the presentation was documented, and the presentation was recorded for those unable to attend. Following the presentation, all healthcare professionals at the pilot clinic received an email to access and download the implementation strategies and complete a baseline survey. The baseline survey consisted of demographics, a question on intent to use the implementation strategy package, and measures from the Implementation Outcomes Questionnaire [29]. Access to the implementation strategies and baseline survey closed two weeks after the educational presentation, and a reminder was sent one week prior to closing. No incentive was provided for completion.

Twelve weeks following the launch of the pilot, all healthcare professionals received an invitation to participate in a post-implementation survey via email. The post-implementation survey consisted of demographics, closed- and open-ended questions regarding use of and feedback on the implementation strategy package, and the measures from the Implementation Outcomes Questionnaire to reassess acceptability, feasibility, and appropriateness, and assess penetration of the implementation strategy package [29]. The post-implementation survey was open for two weeks, and a reminder was sent after one week.

Following pilot implementation, EHR data pulls were conducted to assess changes in post-ACS hypertriglyceridemia care. Patients with an ACS diagnosis, as defined by ICD-10 codes, who completed an encounter with one of the healthcare professionals at the pilot clinic site during the implementation period, were identified for inclusion. Data included patient demographics, medical history, encounters, laboratory orders and results, and prescriptions for relevant medications before and after the encounter of interest. Analysis was conducted by a biostatistician on the study team (AJ) using SAS Enterprise Guide Version 8.3. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for eligible clinicians who utilized the implementation strategies. Additionally, frequencies and percentages were presented for labs and medications for post-ACS patients who had an encounter with eligible clinicians.

The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) 32-item checklist [30] guided reporting and ensured validity. This study was designated exempt under Geisinger IRB#2023-1796.

Contextual inquiries

Contextual inquiries incorporated both observations and semi-structured interviews with clinician stakeholders. Observational data are seen primarily as supplemental in this study since 17 observations were conducted, but no patients were seen with a history of ACS.

Seven interviews were conducted with 3 Physician Assistants (PA-C), 2 Nurse Practitioners (NP), 1 Cardiologist (MD), and 1 Pharmacist (PharmD) with varying levels of experience practicing, but who all had some clinic time scheduled per week (minimum 9–16 h). Five of the 7 clinicians see patients following up with ACS management a few times a week, 1 sees patients in this cohort daily, and 1 sees these patients about once a month.

Data visualization artifacts in the form of process maps were constructed to synthesize the current state of treating HTG in the post-ACS patient population. Of the 7 clinicians we interviewed, 3 maps were constructed to showcase different approaches to HTG care pathways for this patient cohort. Interviews revealed distinct care pathways that were stratified by provider type. Pilot engagement

Department-wide presentations were mentioned in both the contextual inquiries and the deliberative engagement session as a viable strategy to communicate the clinical importance of an initiative and subsequently drive change. The pilot presentation took place on November 12, 2024, during a regional cardiology staff meeting with 50% (20/40) of invited clinicians in attendance. Presentation slides can be found in the supplemental materials, but the content primarily included problems surrounding HTG care in the post-ACS patient population (e.g., “Persistent HTG demonstrates a significant association with recurrent MACE in post-ACS patients, etc.) [7], co-morbidities known to be associated with untreated HTG, known medical therapies, and a call to action to improve outcomes.

Pilot outcomes

The primary outcome of this intervention was to identify penetration of the implementation strategy package, and the secondary outcome was to assess any changes to post-ACS care management following pilot launch.

Following the pilot presentation, clinicians were sent accompanying resources and a baseline survey to determine intent on using the provided resources. 17.5% (7/40) of eligible clinicians completed the baseline survey, and all participants who answered communicated interest in using the provided strategies to help standardize HTG care in the post-ACS patient population. 7.5% (3/40) of eligible clinicians downloaded other implementation strategies, including the “Top 10 Things to Know” document, the clinical algorithm for HTG care, and the associated SmartPhrase, according to REDCap database tracking. None of the eligible clinicians completed the post-intervention survey at the 12-week benchmark.

Cross-sectional EHR data pulls over three months showed that of the 176 post-ACS patients with TG values pre- and post-encounter with an eligible clinician, 99.4% (175/176) of eligible patients received medication throughout. Only 1.1% (2/175) of those patients were found to be on IPE. Both patients were noted to have severe HTG (>500 mg/dL) pre-encounter and had no prescription changes pre- and post-encounter with an eligible clinician.

Despite the lack of changes in patient health outcomes pre- and post-intervention, the deliberative engagement session and pilot presentation provided opportunities for CdR to meet with department leadership and clinical champions to begin developing a standardized clinical pathway for HTG management in the post-ACS patient population.

Discussion

This study sought to improve clinician management of TG in the post-ACS patient population through designing and iterating an implementation strategy package for a pilot and assessing the feasibility of the strategy package in a cardiology practice. The suite of mixed methods provided a stepwise approach to curate and iterate on an implementation strategy package that focused primarily on clinician education via presentation from a lipid specialist expert (CdR) and supplemental resources. Prior findings [7] identified the lack of TG-lowering therapies used at the host organization. Study participants corroborated that by communicating a lack of priority to treat HTG as compared to other atherogenicities and comorbid diseases.

An implementation strategy package was developed from clinician input following semi-structured interviews and a deliberative engagement session, highlighting a greater emphasis on clinician education through presentations from lipid experts to address clinicians' lack of priority in treating HTG compared to LDL-C and other comorbidities. Literature [31], [32] suggests clinician education via subject matter experts can be one of many interventions to change clinician behavior and support long-term practice changes. Simultaneously, the study team operationalized clinician strategy recommendations by creating and sharing the requested resources. There was modest clinician attendance at the pilot presentation, but minimal penetration of other strategies. There were no significant changes in clinician medication ordering patterns pre- and post-pilot, with minimal IPE use reserved for patients with severe HTG >500 mg/dL. Prior findings [7] showed a similar percentage of patients on IPE (0.8%) with one or more TG-lowering therapies post-ACS between 2015 and 2021, and a recent real-world cohort study in Italy found that patients undergoing cardiac rehabilitation showed a similar lackluster uptake in IPE use due to strict reimbursement guidelines [33].

Clinicians reportedly favor the use of IPE over other TG-lowering therapies due to greater cardiovascular benefit but cited cost and insurance barriers as the main barriers to management. Fenofibrates were seen as a potential alternative to IPE by some clinicians, though evidence to support the cardiovascular benefit of adjunct fibrates to statin-therapy is lacking [34], [35] and can be misrepresented [36].

Despite evidence [10], [13], [37], [38], [39] and backing from professional guidelines [9], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16] to support the supplemental use of IPE following statin therapy for a high-risk patient cohort post-ACS, cost and coverage barriers were cited as reasons why clinicians might opt to start with fenofibrates and non-IPE omega-3, such as over-the-counter fish oil supplements. Cost-utility analyses seen elsewhere [40], [41], [42] have shown the cost-effectiveness of IPE in both primary and secondary prevention settings, but perceived barriers identified from this study show a disconnect between ideal treatments and the current state of managing HTG at a large integrated health system. However, the deliberative engagement session and pilot presentation presented new opportunities for study team members to engage with clinical champions and share this disconnect, leading to prototyping a standardized clinical pathway to reduce CV risk in post-ACS patients with HTG.

Limitations

This study should be interpreted through the lens of several limitations. This study had a short follow-up window post-intervention at just 12 weeks, limiting our ability to detect gradual changes over time. However, the 3-month assessment gave an early indication that the current strategy package requires further refinement. The low response rate for post-intervention survey completion forced reliance on tracking resource downloads and EHR data pulls rather than self-reported strategy use. Longitudinal data are needed to adequately assess patient outcomes and ensure intervention effectiveness and sustainability. The small sample size and purposive single-site design may limit the transferability to other clinical settings and organizational structures. However, the purpose of this work is to identify local barriers to inform strategy design that are applicable to a specific context rather than establish broad generalizability. The hope is to develop strategy packages that are transferable to other settings, but the clinician cohort with whom we spoke gave detailed approaches to overcoming site-specific barriers. More studies are needed to assess clinician perspectives on the current state of, and noted barriers to, managing cardiovascular risk in post-ACS patients with HTG.

Conclusions

Perceived barriers to prescribing IPE and lack of priority to managing CV risk in HTG patients were identified as areas to intervene with the implementation strategy package, establishing standardized care pathways for testing and treating HTG post-ACS. Despite operationalizing feedback from clinicians, knowledge obtained from educational sessions did not translate to changed behavior. Implementation strategies were seldom used outside of the pilot clinician education presentation. Rather, this study's primary contribution highlighted specific barriers to post-ACS care and the development of preliminary implementation strategies. This project has mobilized the MTDM clinic pharmacists to create a standardized pathway, and further discussions are underway to iterate the strategy package to feature interactive educational content for clinicians and other digital tools.
