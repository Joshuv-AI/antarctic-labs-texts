Debate: Young people are living in unprecedented times – too much chaos or too little resilience?: No Pain, No Gain? Why articulating distress marks youth resilience
Authors: Levi van Dam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRising levels of anxiety and depression among children and adolescents are increasingly cited as evidence that today's youth are growing up in a world of unprecedented chaos and diminishing resilience. Yet, this interpretation rests on a fragile assumption: that greater expression of distress necessarily signals weaker coping. In this debate article, we argue the opposite. Drawing on long‐term epidemiological trends, developmental theory, and recent evidence from the COVID‐19 pandemic, we suggest that young people today may not be less resilient than previous generations, but more capable of recognizing, articulating, and responding to emotional strain. In this sense, the growing visibility of distress may mark not failure, but progress: a generation that has learned to give language to pain as part of adaptive development.Keywords: Population mental health, children and adolescents, global burden of disease, prevention

A compelling narrative

The idea that today's children and adolescents have inherited a world of ‘unprecedented chaos’ has become a powerful narrative in discussions of youth mental health. Climate change, global conflict, digital acceleration, and social fragmentation are often cited as cumulative stressors that would overwhelm the current generation. Rising reports of anxiety, stress, and emotional distress among young people are then interpreted as evidence that resilience is eroding.

This narrative is compelling, but it deserves closer scrutiny. It rests on two assumptions: first, that the world has become objectively more chaotic than before; second, that rising expressions of distress necessarily signal declining resilience.

Both assumptions are more contestable than they initially appear.

Is the world objectively more chaotic?

If chaos is defined as exposure to material deprivation, physical danger, and uncontrollable adversity, the long‐term global picture is positive rather than uniformly negative. Over recent decades, extreme poverty has declined substantially, disaster‐related mortality has fallen dramatically over the past century, and social protection systems have expanded across most regions of the world. Many children today grow up with greater physical safety, longer life expectancy, and higher access to education than previous generations.

At the same time, progress is uneven. Armed conflict, climate‐related disruption, forced displacement, and inequality remain profound realities for millions of children. One of the most alarming global indicators is education: Despite increased access to educational facilities, a large proportion of children worldwide still fail to reach minimum proficiency in reading and mathematics, undermining future opportunity and wellbeing.

What this combination of trends suggests is not ‘unprecedented chaos’ as a universal condition. Importantly, many young people now grow up in environments that are safer and more predictable than those of their parents or grandparents. If so, why is the narrative of ‘unprecedented chaos’ so appealing?

Why ‘unprecedented chaos’ feels self‐evident

Part of the appeal of the ‘unprecedented chaos’ narrative may lie less in objective change than in how humans perceive and process information. Authors such as Steven Pinker (2018) and Bregman (2020) have repeatedly shown that people systematically overestimate decline and underestimate long‐term progress. Negative events are more salient, emotionally charged, and media‐visible than gradual improvements, creating a cognitive bias toward pessimism. The Gapminder n.d. initiative has demonstrated this empirically: when asked about global trends in poverty, violence, education, or health, experts and laypeople alike consistently perform worse than chance, assuming deterioration where data show sustained improvement.

This bias is currently reflected in youth mental health debates (McGorry et al., 2024). When progress in safety, education, parenting practices, and child protection becomes the baseline, remaining challenges loom disproportionately large. Rising visibility of distress may then be misread as evidence of increasing chaos or failing resilience, rather than as a by‐product of improved awareness, reduced stigma, and greater emotional articulation. Recognizing this perceptual bias does not deny real risks faced by young people; it sharpens interpretation. It invites us to distinguish between genuinely worsening conditions and changing ways of recognizing, expressing, and responding to adversity.

Pandemic distress as adaptive emotion regulation

A major collective adversity was the COVID‐19 pandemic; it therefore offers an instructive stress test for claims about declining resilience. Across the three decades preceding the pandemic, prevalence estimates of mental disorders remained largely stable in major international sources such as the Global Burden of Disease studies (Piao et al., 2022) and long‐running population cohorts using instruments such as the ASEBA did not show systematic increases in mental health problems (Vekety et al., n.d.). In contrast, during the pandemic, clear rises emerged specifically in anxiety and depressive symptoms (Liu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). From a resilience perspective, this pattern is best understood not as dysfunction, but as adaptive emotion regulation. Fear, sadness, and demoralization are functional signal emotions: they orient attention to threat, loss, and uncertainty, mobilizing coping and social support. As articulated in multilevel resilience frameworks (van Harmelen, n.d., accepted), emotion regulation is a central mechanism through which individuals adapt to adversity, dynamically calibrating emotional responses to environmental demands. In the context of a genuine global disruption characterized by isolation, unpredictability, and perceived danger, increases in anxiety and low mood reflect psychological attunement to reality rather than impaired resilience. The observation that distress intensified during an objectively chaotic period, but not during the preceding decades, supports the interpretation that this generation is not emotionally brittle but responsive: emotional systems appear context‐sensitive, increasing signal strength when adversity is real and receding when stability returns.

Rising distress as a sign of adaptation?

How, then, should we interpret the well‐documented rise in self‐reported emotional distress? One possibility is that this reflects not declining resilience but changing modes of expression Van Dam, Van Os, 2025.

Self‐report measures are sensitive to emotional awareness, language, and social norms. Over the past decades, young people have been increasingly encouraged to reflect on their feelings, recognize stress signals, and articulate inner experiences. Mental health literacy has improved, stigma has decreased, and talking about emotions has become more acceptable.

From this perspective, higher rates of reported anxiety or stress may indicate that young people are better attuned to their internal states and more willing to communicate about them. This is a core component of emotion regulation, which is widely recognized as a central building block of resilience. The ability to notice distress early, name it, and seek support is not a failure of coping, but a prerequisite for adaptive regulation.

In other words, rising distress signals do not necessarily imply that young people are overwhelmed; they may indicate that distress is no longer hidden, normalized as ‘just part of growing up’, or expressed through maladaptive behaviors such as aggression.

From silence to speech: A developmental shift

Historical comparisons help illuminate this point. Previous generations experienced fear, sadness, and uncertainty as well, but often lacked the language or social permission to articulate them. Mental health problems were more stigmatized, emotional vulnerability less visible, and help‐seeking more constrained.

Today's youth appear to inhabit a different emotional culture. They speak more openly with peers and adults about psychological struggles, and they do so earlier. This openness can easily be misinterpreted as fragility, especially when viewed through a diagnostic lens. Yet, it may equally reflect developmental progress in emotional communication, supported by families, schools, and wider cultural change.

This shift may also explain why expanded access to mental health services has not been accompanied by declining reports of distress. Greater availability of care increases awareness and recognition. As with physical health, improved diagnostic sensitivity initially raises prevalence estimates without implying a true increase in pathology.

Rethinking resilience in context

These observations point to the need for a more nuanced concept of resilience. Resilience is not a fixed personal trait, nor simply the absence of distress. Contemporary developmental science conceptualizes resilience as a dynamic, multilevel process, arising from interactions between individual capacities and supportive environments.

From this ecological perspective, resilience can coexist with distress. Feeling anxious, sad, or overwhelmed in response to challenges does not mean resilience has failed; it means the system is responding. The critical question is whether young people have access to the relational, educational, and societal resources that allow them to regain balance.

Seen in this light, many of the same societal changes often blamed for youth vulnerability may also be strengthening resilience: warmer parenting, reduced violence, greater emotional openness, and preventive interventions in schools.

Implications for the CAMH debate

If the world is not unequivocally more chaotic, and if resilience has not demonstrably declined, the central challenge for child and adolescent mental health may lie elsewhere. Rather than asking whether young people are ‘too fragile’, we should ask whether our systems are misinterpreting adaptive signals as pathology.

Conclusion

The world inherited by today's youth is complex, unequal, and rapidly changing, but not self‐evidently more chaotic than the worlds of previous generations. At the same time, there is growing evidence that many aspects of young people's behavioral functioning have improved, and that emotional awareness and expression have increased.

Taken together, these trends suggest that the current generation may not suffer from too little resilience, but from a mismatch between new forms of resilience and old ways of assessing mental health (‘problems’) and interpreting the data. The challenge for researchers, clinicians, and policymakers is to recognize this shift and to ensure that systems designed to support young people do not inadvertently undermine the very capacities they seek to protect.
