Artificial intelligence for oral cancer diagnosis: a systematic review and meta-analysis of image-based and non-imaging models
Authors: Shima Soleimani Sardou, Raziyehsadat Rezvaninejad, Fatemeh Sadat Rezvaninejad, Amir Hossein Nekouei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly recognized as a valuable tool for the early detection and prognosis of oral cancer, addressing the challenge of high mortality due to late diagnosis. Artificial intelligence based diagnostic models have the potential to improve accuracy in differentiating between malignant, premalignant and benign oral lesions. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the diagnostic performance of non-imaging and image-based artificial intelligence models and narratively synthesized evidence on prognostic and risk stratification applications in oral cancer. MethodsThis study follows PRISMA guidelines to ensure quality and reproducibility. A systematic search across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Google Scholar and Scopus identified studies from 2010 to 2024 on artificial intelligence applications in oral cancer diagnosis. Sixteen eligible studies met predefined inclusion criteria, including AI-based screening compared to histology. Data extraction and bias assessment were conducted independently using QUADAS-2. The findings highlight AI’s potential in early detection and prognosis, emphasizing the need for further validation and clinical integration to enhance diagnostic accuracy.ResultsA total of 801 studies were initially identified, with 53 undergoing further review, ultimately selecting 16 studies. Sample sizes varied from 70 to 44,000, allowing a broad evaluation of AI’s diagnostic performance. Artificial intelligence models showed wide range of sensitivity (42%–100%), specificity (63%–100%), and accuracy (63%–100%). Meta-analysis revealed a pooled sensitivity of 0.90 (95% CI: 0.81–0.98), specificity of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.84–0.95), and accuracy of 0.89 (95% CI: 0.83–0.95), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 100%). Image-based models had higher pooled sensitivity (0.94 vs. 0.76, P = 0.320), specificity (0.93 vs. 0.79, P = 0.025), and accuracy (0.93 vs. 0.81, P = 0.042).ConclusionsArtificial intelligence models show promising diagnostic performance for oral cancer based on retrospective clinical data. Although image-based models, particularly convolutional neural networks, demonstrated higher pooled sensitivity and specificity than non-imaging models, these differences were not statistically significant. Results should be interpreted with caution due to substantial heterogeneity. Advances reported in the literature, such as multimodal approaches and data augmentation, may improve non-imaging model performance and help narrow the gap between methodologies. Background

Oral cancer (OC) ranks among the six most common malignancies worldwide, with an estimated 354,000 new cases and 177,000 deaths reported in 2018 alone [1–3]. Early diagnosis of oral cancer significantly enhances the chances of successfully treating of associated lesions [4] however, over 50% of oral and pharyngeal cancers are diagnosed at advanced stages, leading to poor prognoses.; for example, the five-year survival rate for these cancers in the United States is only 52% [5–8]. Several factors complicate early diagnosis, including the heterogeneity of symptoms and the difficulty in distinguishing between malignant, premalignant, benign, and normal oral mucosal features through visual examination alone [9–12]. Consequently, the current approach has not yielded satisfactory outcomes, underscoring the need to explore alternative and supplementary methods [13]. In the oral cavity, the most likely areas to cause malignant lesions are the lower lip, the front of the floor of the mouth, and the lateral sides of the tongue, and the appearance of these lesions can be red, white, or a combination of both [14]. Also, their surface can be smooth, verrucose or ulcer-like [14].

Artificial intelligence (AI) encompasses computational systems capable of mimicking human cognitive functions, such as pattern recognition, learning, and decision-making, and has shown promise in aiding the diagnosis of oral lesions, commonly approached through image-based or non-imaging models [15, 16]. Machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), subfields of AI, utilize data-driven algorithms to perform tasks such as prediction and classification. DL, a more advanced subset of ML, leverages multi-layered neural networks to model complex patterns, especially in image and language data, and has been widely applied in medical sciences for disease prognosis and cancer diagnosis [15]. One of the unique features of artificial intelligence is its ability to classify various types of data, including text, numerical, and different images, into specific labeled groups (supervised classification) [17, 18]. Subsequently, this aids in early detection of oral cancer and, as a result, significantly contributes to increasing the survival rate and quality of life for individuals suffering from this disease [15].

In recent years, several machine learning studies have been conducted on the diagnosis, survival rates, and prognosis of oral lesions [19–22]. However, these studies reported varying accuracy levels across different artificial intelligence models, and none have systematically compared non-imaging and image-based approaches. Given the significant impact of early detection on treatment outcomes, quality of life, and survival, as well as the growing body of literature with diverse findings, we undertook a comprehensive review to evaluate the performance of non-imaging and image-based artificial intelligence in the early diagnosis and prognosis of oral cancer.

We hypothesized that image-based models would outperform non-imaging models due to their ability to capture spatial features critical to lesion characterization. This study aims to provide a clearer understanding of the diagnostic potential of machine learning in oral oncology and, to our knowledge, is the first meta-analysis comparing both modalities for prognosis assessment. Ultimately, our findings may help guide the integration of AI into clinical decision-making for oral cancer.

Data sources and search strategies

To identify eligible studies, searches were conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus and Google Scholar. The searches included titles, abstracts, and keywords using both free terms and database-specific thesaurus terms, such as Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and Embase Subject Headings (Emtree), to ensure comprehensive coverage. Google Scholar was also searched to identify relevant grey literature, acknowledging that it is not a conventional scientific database. To manage scope and avoid duplication with other sources, only the first 200 results sorted by relevance were screened. Additionally, a manual search of relevant bibliographies and key journals was performed. Table 1 outlines the summary of related studies for AI application in oral cancer. Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of the identified studies, and the selected papers were then re-assessed in full-text. In addition, to enhance the thoroughness of the review, a hand search was carried out across select bibliographies and relevant journals.

Selection of studies

First, two independent reviewers conducted screenings of articles based on MeSH terms found in the titles. After conducting an initial search, the results were compared, and any discrepancies were discussed. The articles were then imported into EndNote 20 software (Clarivate Analytics, Thomson Reuters, USA) for further review. Duplicate, irrelevant, and non-original articles were excluded. Next, the abstracts and full texts of the remaining articles were thoroughly examined to assess eligibility against the predefined inclusion criteria. Any disagreements regarding study inclusion or data extraction were first discussed between the two reviewers to reach consensus. If consensus could not be achieved, the matter was referred to a third senior reviewer, whose decision was considered final. This multi-step resolution process was implemented to minimize selection bias and ensure consistency in study inclusion. Following this process, 16 articles were selected for inclusion in this study.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers extracted data from each of the selected full-text articles. Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through consensus. The extracted data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel and Word (version 2023), with results presented as percentages and frequencies.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS-2) tool. This tool evaluates the risk of bias across four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing [24]. We anticipated variability in reference standards across studies and therefore evaluated this domain carefully using QUADAS-2, particularly where histopathology was not explicitly reported.

Ethical considerations

As this review involved only the analysis of previously published research, no ethical approval was required. The study adhered to ethical standards for systematic reviews and meta-analyses.

Meta analysis

This research adhered to established guidelines from previous diagnostic test accuracy studies to determine the accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of the studies included [25]. These values and their precision were summarized, with standard errors accounted for, and 95% confidence intervals calculated to quantify uncertainty. Forest plots were generated to visually represent the variability and heterogeneity across the included studies. A random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood estimation (REML) was applied to calculate pooled estimates of sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy [26].This approach was selected to account for potential heterogeneity among studies and provide more generalized estimates across diverse studies methods and protocol for assessing the diagnostic power of AI models. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17. All data analyses were performed using R and package metafor [27].

Ultimately, 16 studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in the meta-analysis (see Fig. 1). The selected articles encompassed a variety of methodologies and sample sizes, ranging from as few as 70 samples to as many as 44,000 samples. This diversity allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of artificial intelligence’s effectiveness in determining accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC. All of the studies indicated positive outcomes regarding the effectiveness of AI in the early identification and prognostic evaluation of oral cancer. This encompassed a broad spectrum of sensitivity (ranging from 42% to 100%), specificity (from 63% to 100%), accuracy (from 63% to 100%), and AUC (from 67% to 99%). These performance metrics were comparable to the predictions made by specialists in oral and dental diseases as well as medical students, as demonstrated in the articles that conducted such comparisons. Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the QUADAS-2 tool, which assesses risk of bias across four domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. Among the 16 included studies, risk of bias in patient selection was high in most cases, often due to retrospective designs, convenience sampling, or limited sample descriptions. Most studies had a low risk of bias in the index test domain, owing to the use of validated machine learning models and appropriate performance metrics. In the reference standard domain, several studies either relied on expert clinical diagnosis rather than histopathology or did not clearly report their reference standard, resulting in an unclear or high risk of bias in this domain for many studies. Additionally, we acknowledge the potential for reporting bias across studies included in the meta-analysis. While a formal analysis of publication bias (e.g., funnel plot, Egger’s test) was not conducted due to the limited number of studies and diverse reporting formats, we recognize that positive-result bias may have contributed to overrepresentation of favorable outcomes. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the pooled estimates of diagnostic performance.

Prognostic findings

Four studies [28–30, 39] evaluated AI models for prognostic or risk stratification purposes rather than diagnostic classification. These studies primarily focused on predicting malignant transformation risk, treatment outcomes, or disease progression using structured clinical variables. Reported outcomes included accuracy, sensitivity, and AUC for risk prediction rather than time-to-event outcomes such as overall survival or recurrence-free survival. Due to heterogeneity in outcome definitions, follow-up periods, and performance metrics, quantitative pooling of prognostic outcomes was not feasible; therefore, findings were synthesized narratively. Overall, these studies demonstrated that AI models could stratify patients into higher- and lower-risk groups with moderate-to-high predictive performance, suggesting potential clinical utility for prognosis support.

Certainty of evidence

We did not formally apply the GRADE framework; however, the overall certainty of the evidence was considered moderate based on consistency across studies and generally low risk of bias in model performance reporting. The main factors reducing certainty were high heterogeneity (I² = 100%) and limited external validation across studies. Future research should include standardized reporting and external datasets to enhance confidence in the outcomes.

This review was not registered in a public database such as PROSPERO. No predefined protocol was published. Despite this, the methodology adhered to PRISMA 2020 guidelines, and all steps (including search strategy, inclusion criteria, and data synthesis) were conducted transparently and reproducibly.

Discussion

The early diagnosis and prognosis of oral cancer remain critical challenges in improving patient outcomes, given the aggressive nature and high mortality rate associated with advanced stages of the disease. Recent advancements in AI, particularly in non-imaging and image-based models, have demonstrated significant potential in augmenting clinical decision-making. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the efficacy and accuracy of these AI approaches, revealing promising results in terms of sensitivity, specificity, and overall diagnostic performance. However, despite these advancements, challenges such as variability in datasets, model interpretability, and integration into clinical workflows underscore the need for further research and refinement. These findings highlight the pivotal role AI can play in transforming oral cancer management while emphasizing the need to address existing limitations for broader clinical adoption. Our meta-analysis results show that artificial intelligence has higher accuracy in diagnosing types of oral lesions using image data compared to text data. There are several reasons why non-imaging data may yield lower diagnostic accuracy in AI applications.

Complexity and Ambiguity of Medical Language [41]: Medical text data includes complex terminologies, varied descriptions, and may often contain ambiguous expressions that are challenging for AI to interpret accurately. This can lead to inconsistencies, especially if the AI lacks sufficient training on diverse and detailed clinical narratives.

Lack of High-Quality and Structured Text Data [42]: Medical centers often prioritize imaging data over textual information for archiving, leading to an insufficiently annotated and structured text dataset. Many healthcare systems do not use standardized language across reports, which can limit AI’s ability to generalize across cases.

Dense and Interconnected Information in Images [43]: Images naturally encode multiple features simultaneously, such as color, texture, shape, spatial relationships, and patterns. These features often follow well-defined structures, making them more amenable to machine learning models. For example, a convolutional neural network (CNN) can identify edges, contours, or other meaningful patterns within an image and use these as building blocks to understand higher-level representations. This inherent richness and interconnection of data make image analysis more straightforward for models compared to text.

Feature Extraction and Representation [43]: In images, features are spatially localized and can often be extracted with relatively straightforward transformations. With text, features such as semantics and sentiment are more abstract and need to be learned through complex embeddings like word vectors, making the task harder and more error-prone.

These challenges collectively contribute to the observed lower accuracy of non-imaging AI diagnoses, underscoring the need for standardized and enriched text data to improve outcomes in clinical AI models.

To bridge the accuracy gap between image-based and non-imaging AI models, several strategies can be employed to improve the performance of text models. First, improving data quality through increased data diversity and volume is essential [46]. Just as image models benefit from large, diverse datasets, text models can be trained on vast and varied corpora. Augmenting training data with paraphrasing, translations, or synthetic examples enhances robustness [47]. Data augmentation techniques for text, such as generating paraphrased or synthetic clinical narratives, can diversify training data, similar to image-based augmentations like flipping or cropping. More advanced approaches now include the use of generative adversarial networks (GANs) to synthesize clinically relevant textual data, improving the variety and balance of training inputs [48]. Additionally, large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-based systems can be fine-tuned on domain-specific corpora to create synthetic notes, symptom descriptions, or rare case scenarios, helping mitigate data sparsity and improve generalization [49]. Collaboration between medical centers to build shared, structured text databases further strengthens model training, especially for clinical natural language processing (NLP) tasks [50]. Finally, hybrid multimodal models that combine textual and visual data can leverage the complementary strengths of both input types. By implementing these strategies, non-imaging AI models can approach the performance of image-based models, especially in tasks that demand deep contextual understanding and generalization [49].

Although image-based models demonstrated higher pooled sensitivity compared to non-imaging models, the subgroup differences were not statistically significant. It is important to note that the number of studies evaluating non-imaging models was lower than that for image-based models, which may have limited the statistical power to detect significant differences. Moreover, both subgroups exhibited high heterogeneity, contributing to wider confidence intervals and, consequently, reducing the precision of the estimates. This increased variability also decreases the power of statistical tests to identify true differences between subgroups. These factors together suggest that, despite the apparent trend favoring image-based models, the results should be interpreted with caution.

Several studies [34–37, 40], leveraged high-resolution photographic images as a primary source for training and testing AI algorithms in the detection of oral cancer. These images encompassed a range of modalities, including intraoral photographs, close-up images of lesions, and magnified views of suspicious areas. The choice of photographic imaging reflects the practicality and ubiquity of these modalities in routine clinical settings, making them valuable for seamless integration into existing diagnostic workflows.

The AI algorithms employed in these studies, such as convolutional neural networks and deep learning models, demonstrated commendable performance in the analysis of photographic images. Jubair et al. [36], for instance, applied a CNN to intraoral photographs, achieving a sensitivity of 87% and specificity of 85% in detecting early signs of oral cancer. The consistent success across multiple studies underscores the robustness of AI algorithms in interpreting visual cues from photographic data for accurate diagnosis.

In addition, Panigrahi et al. [51], have emphasized the pivotal role of pathological imaging in harnessing the capabilities of artificial intelligence for the diagnosis of oral cancer. This study focused on the analysis of histopathological slides using transfer learning for the early diagnosis of oral cancer [51]. The study reported commendable results, with the ResNet 50 demonstrating a sensitivity of 97% and an impressive specificity of 97%. The emphasis on histopathological slides showcases the significance of microscopic examination in the accurate detection of oral cancer lesions and these images provide detailed insights into cellular and tissue-level changes, offer a valuable source of information for training and validating AI algorithms.

Also, several studies [30–33], analyzed textual information such as patient symptoms, medical history, and reported complaints and showed that the AI model showcased a heightened ability to discern nuanced patterns associated with early signs of oral cancer. This approach not only complements existing imaging modalities but also provides a more comprehensive understanding of the patient’s clinical journey, contributing to a more robust diagnostic framework.

With respect to prognosis, only a small subset of studies evaluated AI-based risk stratification rather than diagnostic classification. These studies showed heterogeneity in outcomes and follow-up durations, precluding quantitative meta-analysis; therefore, findings were synthesized narratively.

The substantial heterogeneity observed in this meta-analysis likely arises from multiple clinical and methodological sources. First, the reference standards varied across studies: while several studies clearly used histology [30, 37], others relied on oral and maxillofacial surgeons’ diagnosis [40] or did not explicitly report the reference standard [28, 29, 34]. Second, sample sizes ranged widely from 70 to 44,000, which may influence the stability and precision of model performance estimates. Third, substantial variation exists within the image-based subgroup, including clinical photographs, histopathology slides, hyperspectral images, and optical coherence tomography, each with distinct characteristics and diagnostic information. These variations likely contribute to differences in sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy across studies. Additionally, the QUADAS-2 assessments revealed unclear risk of bias in the reference standard domain for a majority of studies, further highlighting the methodological diversity and potential limitations in pooling results. Collectively, these factors underscore the need for cautious interpretation of pooled estimates and suggest that findings should be regarded as exploratory trends rather than definitive evidence. Future research should prioritize standardized reference standards, larger and more homogeneous sample sets, and harmonized imaging modalities to reduce heterogeneity and improve generalizability. Although substantial heterogeneity was observed, formal exploration of heterogeneity (e.g., meta-regression or sensitivity analyses) was not conducted. This was primarily due to the limited number of included studies per subgroup and inconsistent reporting of important study-level covariates, which precluded reliable quantitative investigation. Future studies with more standardized reporting and larger datasets are needed to enable such analyses.

The primary benefits of our research include the incorporation of diverse study types assessing non-imaging data, photographic images, and histopathologic images. Our study also compares the results of AI in the detection of oral lesions in text- based and image- based data. Notably, this study includes a subset of investigations evaluating AI-based prognostic and risk stratification models in oral oncology, although quantitative synthesis of prognostic outcomes was not feasible due to methodological heterogeneity. Furthermore, we extensively searched four major databases to identify relevant articles. However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, our inclusion criteria were restricted to studies reporting Accuracy, Sensitivity, Specificity, and AUC, neglecting other variables that demonstrate the efficacy of AI in diagnosing oral cancer. Secondly, due to access constraints to certain databases in Iran, our search did not encompass other databases. In addition, a major limitation of this meta-analysis is the extreme heterogeneity observed across all pooled estimates (I² ≈ 100%), which substantially restricts the interpretability and generalizability of summary performance measures. Such heterogeneity likely reflects variations in study design, population characteristics, data modalities, reference standards, and machine learning architectures. Consequently, pooled estimates should be interpreted as indicative trends rather than precise quantitative benchmarks. Similarly, subgroup differences between image-based and non-imaging models, although statistically significant for specificity and accuracy, are not sufficiently robust to support definitive conclusions regarding modality superiority and should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory findings. Although histopathology is considered the gold standard for oral cancer diagnosis, several included studies used expert clinical diagnosis or did not explicitly report their reference standard. These studies were retained to ensure broader coverage of real-world AI applications in screening contexts, but this methodological variability likely contributed to between-study heterogeneity and uncertainty in pooled estimates. In addition, this review was not prospectively registered in a public database such as PROSPERO, and no predefined protocol was published. Although the methodology followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines, the lack of registration may introduce a risk of selective reporting and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Future research should prioritize standardized methodologies and subgroup analyses to better understand sources of heterogeneity and improve the precision of diagnostic performance estimates. A limitation of this study is that no formal analysis was conducted to identify the sources of heterogeneity, as numerous factors—including dataset characteristics, model architectures, and evaluation protocols—may influence AI performance, and collecting all relevant variables was beyond the scope of this review. Dedicated studies focusing on heterogeneity in AI outcomes are therefore needed. Additionally, exclusion of studies that did not report diagnostic performance metrics may have resulted in publication bias, as negative or inconclusive findings are less likely to be published. Formal publication bias assessment was not feasible because each subgroup contained fewer than 10 studies, below the Cochrane-recommended threshold for reliable funnel plot analyses. Future reviews should consider including grey literature and preprints to mitigate this risk.

Conclusion

Artificial intelligence shows promising potential in diagnosing and predicting oral cancer using retrospective clinical data. Although image-based models demonstrated higher numerical performance than non-imaging models, the difference in sensitivity was not statistically significant, likely due to the limited number of non-imaging studies, high heterogeneity, and wide confidence intervals. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution. Innovative multimodal approaches and data augmentation may help improve non-imaging accuracy and narrow the performance gap between modalities. Overall, while AI shows promise in enhancing early detection and prognosis of oral cancer, further high-quality, standardized research is required before widespread clinical implementation.

This study primarily provides quantitative evidence on the diagnostic performance of AI models in oral cancer, while prognostic applications were explored narratively due to heterogeneity in reported outcomes.
