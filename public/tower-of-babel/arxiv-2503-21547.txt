Rings Whose Non-Invertible Elements are Strongly Weakly Nil-Clean
Peter Danchev, Mina Doostalizadeh, Omid Hasanzadeh, Arash Javan, Ahmad Moussavi

Abstract
. The target of the present work is to give a new insight in the theory of strongly weakly nil-clean rings, recently defined by Kosan and Zhou in the Front. Math. China (2016) and further explored in detail by Chen-Sheibani in the J. Algebra Appl. (2017). Indeed, we consider those rings whose non-units are strongly weakly nil-clean and succeed to establish that this class of rings is strongly π \pi -regular and, even something more, that it possesses a complete characterization in terms of the Jacobson radical and sections of the 2 × 2 2\times 2 full matrix ring. Additionally, some extensions like Morita context rings and groups rings are also studied in this directory.

1.
Introduction and Basic Concepts

The motivation of this paper is the class of
strongly weakly nil-clean
rings as well as results based on a recent work by Danchev et al. on GSNC rings (cf.
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
). In fact, we will combine in what follows the used by us approach developed in
[
10
]
in order to obtain a complete description of a widely more large class of rings.

Everywhere in the current paper, let
R
be an associative but
not
necessarily commutative ring having identity element, usually stated as
1
. Standardly, for such a ring
R
, the letters
U(R)
,
Nil(R)
and
Id(R)
are designed for the set of invertible elements (also termed as the unit group of
R
), the set of nilpotent elements and the set of idempotent elements in
R
, respectively. Likewise,
J(R)
denotes the Jacobson radical of
R
, and
Z(R)
denotes the center of
R
. The ring of
n\times n
matrices over
R
and the ring of
n\times n
upper triangular matrices over
R
are, respectively, denoted by
M_{n}(R)
and
T_{n}(R)
. We also denote by
R[x]
and
R[[x]]
the polynomial ring and power series ring over
R
, respectively. Standardly, a ring is said to be
abelian
if each of its idempotents is central.

Historically, Nicholson introduced in
[
22
]
and
[
23
]
the notion of
clean
and
strongly clean
rings. A ring is called (strongly) clean if every its element can be written as sum of an unit element and an idempotent element (that commute). In the past two decades, there has been intense interest in this class of rings as well as certain generalizations of their induced variants were produced.

Later on, as a different variant of clean rings, Koşan et al. introduced
[
17
]
the notion of
weakly clean
rings in general aspect (note that the commutative variant of weak cleanness was firstly defined by Ahn-Anderson in
[
1
]
). A ring
R
is called (strongly) weakly clean if every its element can be written as sum or difference an unit element and an idempotent element (that commute). The authors in
[
17
]
proved many fundamental properties of weakly clean rings.

Likewise, Diesl introduced in
[
15
]
the notion of
nil-clean
rings. A ring is said to be (strongly) nil-clean if, for each
r\in R
, there are
q\in Nil(R)
and
e\in Id(R)
such that
r=q+e
(
qe=eq
). He obtained many fundamental properties as well as developed a general theory of (strongly) nil-clean rings.

As a different variant of nil-clean rings, Danchev and McGovern introduced in
[
14
]
the notion of weak nil-cleanness for commutative rings. A commutative ring
R
is called
weakly nil-clean
if, for each
r\in R
, there are
q\in Nil(R)
and
e\in Id(R)
such that
r=q+e
or
r=q-e
. The authors proved many fundamental properties of commutative weakly nil-clean rings. They also provided a complete characterization theorem of commutative weakly nil-clean rings and proved a characterization theorem of commutative weakly nil-clean group rings. Furthermore, Breaz et al. defined in
[
4
]
the notion of weak nil-cleanness for arbitrary rings as that in
[
14
]
, and they also obtained a decomposition theorem for abelian rings to be weakly nil-clean. They also proved that, for a division ring
D
and a natural number
n
,
M_{n}(D)
is weakly nil-clean if, and only if, either
D\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, or
D\cong\mathbb{Z}_{3}
and
n=1
.

As a common generalization of the abelian weakly nil-clean rings, Kosan and Zhou introduced in
[
18
]
the concept of
strongly weakly nil-clean
rings. A ring
R
is called strongly weakly nil-clean if, for each
r\in R
,
r=q+e
or
r=q-e
, where
q\in Nil(R)
,
e\in Id(R)
and
qe=eq
. In that article both the structural theorems about commutative weakly nil-clean rings and abelian weakly nil-clean rings were somewhat extended.

In the other vein, a ring
R
is called
UU
if all of its units are unipotent, that is,
U(R)\subseteq 1+{\rm Nil}(R)
(and so
1+{\rm Nil}(R)=U(R)
). This notion was introduced by Călugăreanu
[
5
]
, and next examined in more details by Danchev and Lam in
[
13
]
. They showed that
R
is strongly nil-clean if, and only if,
R
is an exchange
UU
ring. Besides, in a more general context, a ring
R
is called
weakly UU
, and abbreviated as
WUU
, if
U(R)={\rm Nil}(R)\pm 1
. This is equivalent to the condition that every unit can be presented as either
n+1
or
n-1
, where
n\in{\rm Nil}(R)
(see the original source
[
9
]
too).

Due to Danchev et al. (see
[
10
]
), a ring
R
is called
generalized strongly nil-clean
, or just briefly abbreviated by GSNC, if every non-invertible element in
R
is strongly nil-clean. The authors in
[
10
]
proved many fundamental properties of GSNC rings. They also provided a characterization theorem of semi-local GSNC rings and proved a characterization theorem of GSNC group rings. One of the results in
[
10
]
states that a ring
R
is strongly nil-clean if, and only if,
R
is strongly clean and
UU
.

Now, considering the strongly weakly nil-clean rings from
[
6
]
and
[
18
]
, in parallel to the aforementioned GSNC rings, we introduce in a way of similarity the GSWNC rings, i.e., those rings whose non-invertible elements are strongly weakly nil-clean. These rings are simultaneously a non-trivial generalization of both strongly weakly nil-clean rings and GSNC rings, and also is a proper subclass of the class of strongly weakly clean rings as mentioned above.

So, the leitmotif of the present article is various non-elementary expansions of these rings to be studied in the sequel in-depth. Our central achievemnets are Theorems
2.24
,
2.36
and
[
theorem2.36
]
, respectively. Some applications of these results to GSWNC group rings are provided in Theorems
3.13
and
3.8
as well.

2.
GSWNC rings

We begin here with our key instrument.

Definition 2.1
.

A ring
R
is called
generalized strongly weakly nil-clean
(or just
GSWNC
for short) if every non-invertible element in
R
is strongly weakly nil-clean.

We now need a series of preliminaries.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
R
be a ring and
a\in R
be a strongly weakly nil-clean element. Then,
-a
is strongly weakly-clean.

Proof.

Assume
a=q\pm e
is a strongly weakly nil-clean representation. If
a=q+e
, then we have,
-a=(1-e)-(q+1)
where
1-e
is an idempotent and
q+1
is an unit in
R
. If
a=q-e
, then we have
-a=-(1-e)+(1-q)
where again
1-e
is an idempotent and
1-q
is an unit in
R
. Thus,
-a
has a strongly weakly-clean decomposition and hence it is strongly weakly-clean element.
∎

An automatic consequence is the following obvious claim.

Corollary 2.3
.

Every GSWNC ring is strongly weakly-clean.

In other words, GSWNC rings lie between strongly weakly nil-clean rings and strongly weakly clean rings. However, it is worthwhile noticing that
\mathbb{Z}_{3}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3}
and
M_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{3})
and
M_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{2})
are all GSWNC rings that are manifestly not strongly weakly nil-clean, while
\mathbb{Z}_{2}[[x]]
and
\mathbb{Z}_{3}[[x]]
are strongly weakly-clean rings that are not GSWNC. Also,
M_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{3})
and
\mathbb{Z}_{3}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{3}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
are all GSWNC rings, but are not GSNC.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
R_{i}
be a ring for all
i\in I
. If
\prod^{n}_{i=1}R_{i}
is GSWNC, then each
R_{i}
is GSWNC.

Proof.

Let
a_{i}\in R_{i}
, where
a_{i}\notin U(R_{i})
, whence
(1,1,\ldots,a_{i},1,\ldots,1)\notin U(\prod^{n}_{i=1}R_{i})
. So,
(1,1,\ldots,a_{i},1,\ldots,1)
is strongly weakly nil-clean, and hence
a_{i}
is strongly weakly nil-clean. If, however,
a_{i}
is not strongly weakly nil-clean, we clearly conclude that
(1,1,\ldots,a_{i},1,\ldots,1)

is not strongly weakly nil-clean and this is a contradiction.
∎

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
R
and
S
be rings. If
R\times S
is GSWNC, then
R
and
S
are strongly weakly nil-clean.

Proof.

Let
a\in R
is an arbitrary element, so
(a,0)\in R\times S
is not unit. Then by hypothesis we have
(a,0)=(q,0)\pm(e,0)
where
(q,0)
is a nilpotent and
(e,0)
is an idempotent in
R\times S
and
(q,0)(e,0)=(e,0)(q,0)
. Hence
a=q\pm e
where
q
is a nilpotent and
e
is an idempotent in
R
and
qe=eq
. So
a
is a strongly weakly nil-clean element. Then
R
is strongly weakly nil-clean ring. Similarly
S
is strongly weakly nil-clean ring.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

The direct product
\prod_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}
is GSWNC for
n\geq 3
if, and only if, each
R_{i}
is strongly weakly nil-clean and at most one of them is not strongly nil-clean.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
). Assume
\prod_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}
is a GSWNC ring. Therefore, with Proposition
2.5
at hand,
\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}R_{i}
and
R_{n}
are strongly weakly nil-clean rings. Thus, according to
[
6
, Theorem 2.2]
, without loss of generality, we can assume that for each
1\leq i\leq n-2
,
R_{i}
is a strongly nil-clean ring. Again, since
\prod_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}=(R_{1}\times\cdots\times R_{n-2})\times(R_{n-1}\times R_{n})
, Proposition
2.5
, implies that
R_{n-1}\times R_{n}
is strongly weakly nil-clean. Therefore, by
[
6
, Theorem 2.2]
, we can assume that
R_{n-1}
is strongly nil-clean and
R_{n}
is strongly weakly nil-clean.

(
\Leftarrow
). It is clear owing to Theorem 2.2 proved in
[
6
]
.
∎

strongly weakly nil-clean.

L

For any

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
R
be a ring. Then, the following are equivalent:

(i)

R
is strongly nil-clean.

(ii)

{\rm T}_{n}(R)
is strongly weakly nil-clean for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(iii)

{\rm T}_{n}(R)
is strongly weakly nil-clean for some
n\geq 3
.

(iv)

{\rm T}_{n}(R)
is GSWNC for some
n\geq 3
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). This follows employing
[
15
, Theorem 4.1]
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii)
\Rightarrow
(iv). These are trivial.

(iv)
\Rightarrow
(i). Let
a\in R
. Choose

A=\begin{pmatrix}a&&&\ast\\
&-a_{1}&&\\
&&0&\\
&&&\ddots&\\
0&&&&0\end{pmatrix}\in{\rm T}_{n}(R).

It is clear that
A
is non-invertible in
{\rm T}_{n}(R)
. By hypothesis, we can find an idempotent

E=\begin{pmatrix}e_{1}&&&\ast\\
&e_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
0&&&e_{n}\end{pmatrix}
and a nilpotent

Q=\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}&&&\ast\\
&q_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
0&&&q_{n}\end{pmatrix}
such that

A=\pm\begin{pmatrix}e_{1}&&&\ast\\
&e_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
0&&&e_{n}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}&&&\ast\\
&q_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
0&&&q_{n}\end{pmatrix}

and
EQ=QE
. It now follows that
a=e_{1}+q_{1}
or
a=e_{2}-q_{2}
,
e_{1}q_{1}=q_{1}e_{1}
and
e_{2}q_{2}=q_{2}e_{2}
. Clearly,
e_{1}
,
e_{2}
are idempotents and
q_{1}
,
q_{2}
are nilpotents in
R
. Thus,
a
has strongly nil-clean decomposition, proving (i).

∎

.

The ring
T_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{3})
is GSWNC, but
\mathbb{Z}_{3}

not

Lemma 2.12
.

If
R
is GSWNC, then
J(R)
is nil.

Proof.

Choose
j\in J(R)
. Since
j\notin U(R)
, we have
e=e^{2}\in R
and
q\in Nil(R)
such that
j=q\pm e
where
qe=eq
. Therefore,
1-e=(q+1)-j\in U(R)+J(R)\subseteq U(R)
or
1-e=(1-q)+j\in U(R)+J(R)\subseteq U(R)
, so
e=0
. Hence,
j=q\in Nil(R)
, as required.
∎

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
R
be a ring, and let
a\in R
. Then, the following are equivalent:

(i)
R
is a GSWNC ring.

(ii) For any
a\in R
, where
a\notin U(R)
,
a\pm a^{2}\in{\rm Nil}(R)
.

Proof.

The proof is quite analogous to
[
6
, Theorem 2.1]
.
∎

A ring
R
is said to be
strongly
\pi
-regular
, provided that, for any
a\in R
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a^{n}\in a^{n+1}R
.

The following observation is crucial.

Corollary 2.14
.

Every GSWNC ring is strongly
\pi
-regular.

Proof.

Let
R
be a GSWNC ring. Let
a\in R
. If
a\in U(R)
, then
a
is a strongly
\pi
-regular element. If, however,
a\notin U(R)
, then, by Proposition
2.13
, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(a\pm a^{2})^{m}=0
, so we have
a^{m}=a^{m+1}r
for some
r\in R
. Thus,
R
is a strongly
\pi
-regular ring, as claimed.
∎

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
R
be a ring. Then:

(i) For any nil-ideal
I\subseteq R
,
R
is GSWNC if, and only if,
R/I
is GSWNC.

(ii) A ring
R
is GSWNC if, and only if,
J(R)
is nil and
R/J(R)
is GSWNC.

Proof.

(i) Let
R
is a GSWNC ring and
\overline{R}:=R/I
, where
\bar{a}\notin U(\overline{R})
. Then,
a\notin U(R)
, which insures, in view of Proposition
2.13
, that
a\pm a^{2}\in{\rm Nil}(R)
, so
\bar{a}\pm\bar{a}^{2}\in{\rm Nil}(\overline{R})
.

Conversely, suppose
\overline{R}
is a GSWNC ring. If
a\notin U(R)
, then
\bar{a}\notin U(\overline{R})
, and Proposition
2.13
yields that
\bar{a}\pm\bar{a}^{2}\in{\rm Nil}(\overline{R})
. Therefore, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(a\pm a^{2})^{k}\subseteq I\subseteq{\rm Nil}(R)
.

(ii) Using Lemma
2.12
and part (i) of the proof, the proof is clear.
∎

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
R
be a ring and
0\neq e\in{\rm Id}(R)
. If
R
is GSWNC, then so is
eRe
.

Proof.

Let
a\in eRe
where it is not unit in
eRe
. We have
a=ea=ae=eae
. If
a\in U(R)
, then there exists
b\in R
such that
ab=ba=1
, which implies
a(ebe)=(ebe)a=e
, a contradiction. Therefore,
a\notin U(R)
. Hence, by Proposition
2.13
, we have
a\pm a^{2}\in{\rm Nil}(R)\cap eRe\subseteq{\rm Nil}(eRe)
, as required.
∎

Let
R
be a ring and
M
a bi-module over
R
. The trivial extension of
R
and
M
is defined as

T(R,M)=\{(r,m):r\in R\text{ and }m\in M\},

with addition defined componentwise and multiplication defined by

(r,m)(s,n)=(rs,rn+ms).

Corollary 2.17
.

Let
R
be a ring and
M
a bi-module over
R
. Then, the following are equivalent:

(i)

R
is a GSWNC ring.

(ii)

T(R,M)
is a GSWNC ring.

Proof.

Set
A={\rm T}(R,M)
and consider
I:={\rm T}(0,M)
. It is not too hard to verify that
I
is a nil-ideal of
A
such that
\dfrac{A}{I}\cong R
. So, the result follows directly from Proposition
2.15
.
∎

Let
\alpha
be an endomorphism of
R
and
n
a positive integer. It was defined by Nasr-Isfahani in
[
21
]
the
skew triangular matrix ring
like this:

{\rm T}_{n}(R,\alpha)=\left\{\left.\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&a_{1}&a_{2}&\cdots&a_{n-1}\\
0&a_{0}&a_{1}&\cdots&a_{n-2}\\
0&0&a_{0}&\cdots&a_{n-3}\\
\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&a_{0}\end{pmatrix}\right|a_{i}\in R\right\}

with addition point-wise and multiplication given by:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&a_{1}&a_{2}&\cdots&a_{n-1}\\
0&a_{0}&a_{1}&\cdots&a_{n-2}\\
0&0&a_{0}&\cdots&a_{n-3}\\
\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&a_{0}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}b_{0}&b_{1}&b_{2}&\cdots&b_{n-1}\\
0&b_{0}&b_{1}&\cdots&b_{n-2}\\
0&0&b_{0}&\cdots&b_{n-3}\\
\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&b_{0}\end{pmatrix}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}&c_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&c_{n-1}\\
0&c_{0}&c_{1}&\cdots&c_{n-2}\\
0&0&c_{0}&\cdots&c_{n-3}\\
\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\ddots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&c_{0}\end{pmatrix},

where

c_{i}=a_{0}\alpha^{0}(b_{i})+a_{1}\alpha^{1}(b_{i-1})+\cdots+a_{i}\alpha^{i}(b_{i}),~~1\leq i\leq n-1.

We denote the elements of
{\rm T}_{n}(R,\alpha)
by
(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1})
. If
\alpha
is the identity endomorphism, then
{\rm T}_{n}(R,\alpha)
is a subring of upper triangular matrix ring

.

Corollary 2.18
.

Let
R
be a ring. Then, the following are equivalent:

(i)

R
is a GSWNC ring.

(ii)

{\rm T}_{n}(R,\alpha)
is a GSWNC ring.

Proof.

Choose

I:=\left\{\left.\begin{pmatrix}0&a_{12}&\ldots&a_{1n}\\

Then, one easily verifies that

and
\dfrac{{\rm T}_{n}(R,\alpha)}{I}\cong R
. Consequently, Proposition
2.15
applies to get the wanted result.
∎

Let
\alpha
be an endomorphism of
R
. We denote by

the

whose elements are the polynomials over

, the addition is defined as usual, and the multiplication is defined by the equality

for any

. Thus, there is a ring isomorphism

\varphi:\dfrac{R[x,\alpha]}{\langle x^{n}\rangle}\rightarrow{\rm T}_{n}(R,\alpha),

\varphi(a_{0}+a_{1}x+\ldots+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\langle x^{n}\rangle)=(a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{n-1})

with

,

. So, one finds that

, where

is the ideal generated by

. Also

denotes the ring of skew formal power series over

; that is all formal power series in

with coefficients from

for all

. On the other hand we know that,
\dfrac{R[x,\alpha]}{\langle x^{n}\rangle}\cong\dfrac{R[[x,\alpha]]}{\langle x^{n}\rangle}

Theorem 2.24
.

For any ring
R\neq 0
and any integer
n\geq 3
, the ring
{\rm M}_{n}(R)
is not GSWNC.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
{\rm M}_{3}(R)

Theorem 2.36

Lemma 2.40
.

Let
R
be a GSWNC ring with
2\in U(R)
and, for every
u\in U(R)
, we have
u^{2}=1
. Then,
R
is a commutative ring.

Proof.

For any
u,v\in U(R)
, we have
u^{2}=v^{2}=(uv)^{2}=1
. Therefore,
uv=(uv)^{-1}=v^{-1}u^{-1}=vu
. Hence, the invertible elements commute with each other.

Now, we will show that
R
is abelian. Indeed, for every idempotent
e
in
R
and
a\in R
, we know
2e-1\in U(R)
and
(1+ea(1-e))\in U(R)
. Since the invertible elements commute with each other, we have
2ea(1-e)=2(1-e)ae=0
. Since
2\in U(R)
, we derive
ea(1-e)=(1-e)ae=0
, which insures
ea=eae=ae
. Therefore,
R
is abelian.

On the other side, since
1+\text{Nil}(R)\subseteq U(R)
and the invertible elements commute with each other, the nilpotent elements also commute with each other.

Now, let
x,y\in R
. We distinguish the following four basic cases:

(i)

x,y\in U(R)
: Since the invertible elements commute with each other, it is clear that
xy=yx
.

(ii)

x,y\notin U(R)
: Since
R
is a GSWNC ring, there exist
e,f\in\text{Id}(R)
and
p,q\in\text{Nil}(R)
such that
x=q\pm e
and
y=p\pm f
. Then, we have four cases for
x
and
y
. If
x=q+e
and
y=p+f
Thus,

xy=(q+e)(p+f)=qp+qf+ep+ef=pq+fq+pe+fe=(p+f)(q+e)=yx.

In the other three cases we will have similarly,
xy=yx
.

(iii)

x\in U(R)
and
y\notin U(R)
: In this case, there exists
e\in\text{Id}(R)
and
q\in\text{Nil}(R)
such that
y=q\pm e
. Since
x(1+q)=(1+q)x
, we have
qx=xq
. Therefore,

xy=x(q\pm e)=xq\pm xe=qx\pm ex=(q\pm e)x=yx.

(iv)

x\notin U(R)
and
y\in U(R)
: Similarly to case (iii), we can show that
xy=yx
.

Then,
R
is a commutative ring.
∎

,

be two rings and

-bi-module and

NM
are nilpotent
\Longleftrightarrow s
is a nilpotent.

Corollary 2.43
.

Let
R
be a ring and
s\in Z(R)\cap Nil(R)
. If
K_{s}(R)
is a GSWNC ring, then
R
is a strongly weakly nil-clean ring. The converse holds, provided
R
is a strongly nil-clean ring.

Following Tang and Zhou (cf.
[
26
]
), for any
n\geq 2
and for
s\in Z(R)
, the
n\times n
formal matrix ring over
R
defined by
s
, and denoted by
M_{n}(R;s)
, is the set of all
n\times n
matrices over
R
with usual addition of matrices and with multiplication defined below:

For
(a_{ij})
and
(b_{ij})
in
M_{n}(R;s)
,

(a_{ij})(b_{ij})=(c_{ij}),\quad\text{where}~~(c_{ij})=\sum s^{\delta_{ikj}}a_{ik}b_{kj}.

Here,
\delta_{ijk}=1+\delta_{ik}-\delta_{ij}-\delta_{jk}
, where
\delta_{jk}
,
\delta_{ij}
,
\delta_{ik}
are the Kroncker delta symbols.

Corollary 2.44
.

Let
R
be a ring and
s\in Z(R)\cap Nil(R)
. If
M_{n}(R;s)
is a GSWNC ring, then
R
is a strongly weakly nil-clean ring. The converse holds, provided
R
is a strongly nil-clean ring.

Proof.

If
n=1
, then
M_{n}(R;s)=R
. So, in this case, there is nothing to prove. Let
n=2
. By the definition of
M_{n}(R;s)
, we have
M_{2}(R;s)\cong K_{s^{2}}(R)
. Apparently,
s^{2}\in Nil(R)\cap Z(R)
, so the claim holds for
n=2
with the help of Corollary
2.43
.

To proceed by induction, assume now that
n>2
and that the claim holds for
M_{n-1}(R;s)
. Set
A:=M_{n-1}(R;s)
. Then,
M_{n}(R;s)=\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&R\end{pmatrix}

is a Morita context, where

M=\begin{pmatrix}M_{1n}\\
\vdots\\
M_{n-1,n}\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad N=(M_{n1}\dots M_{n,n-1})

with
M_{in}=M_{ni}=R
for all
i=1,\dots,n-1,
and

\displaystyle\psi:N\otimes M\rightarrow N,\quad n\otimes m\mapsto snm

\displaystyle\phi:M\otimes N\rightarrow M,\quad m\otimes n\mapsto smn.

Besides, for
x=\begin{pmatrix}x_{1n}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n-1,n}\end{pmatrix}\in M
and
y=(y_{n1}\dots y_{n,n-1})\in N
, we write

xy=\begin{pmatrix}s^{2}x_{1n}y_{n1}&sx_{1n}y_{n2}&\dots&sx_{1n}y_{n,n-1}\\
sx_{2n}y_{n1}&s^{2}x_{2n}y_{n2}&\dots&sx_{2n}y_{n,n-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
sx_{n-1,n}y_{n1}&sx_{n-1,n}y_{n2}&\dots&s^{2}x_{n-1,n}y_{n,n-1}\end{pmatrix}\in sA

and

yx=s^{2}y_{n1}x_{1n}+s^{2}y_{n2}x_{2n}+\dots+s^{2}y_{n,n-1}x_{n-1,n}\in s^{2}R.

Since
s
is nilpotent, we see that
MN
and
NM
are nilpotent too. Thus, we obtain that

\frac{M_{n}(R;s)}{J(M_{n}(R;s))}\cong\frac{A}{J(A)}\times\frac{R}{J(R)}.

Finally, the induction hypothesis and Proposition
2.41
yield the claim after all.
∎

A Morita context
\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\end{pmatrix}
is called
trivial
, if the context products are trivial, i.e.,
MN=0
and
NM=0
. We now have

\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\end{pmatrix}\cong T(A\times B,M\oplus N),

where

\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\end{pmatrix}
is a trivial Morita context consulting with
[
16
]
.

Corollary 2.45
.

If the trivial Morita context

\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\end{pmatrix}
is a GSWNC ring, then
A
,
B
are strongly weakly nil-clean rings. The converse holds if one of the rings
A
or
B
is strongly nil-clean and the other is strongly weakly nil-clean.

Proof.

It is apparent to see that the isomorphisms

\begin{pmatrix}A&M\\
N&B\end{pmatrix}\cong T(A\times B,M\oplus N)\cong\begin{pmatrix}A\times B&M\oplus N\\
0&A\times B\end{pmatrix}

are fulfilled. Then, the rest of the proof follows combining Corollary
2.17
and Proposition
2.5
.
∎

3.
GSWNC Group Rings

Let
R
be a ring,
G
a group, and, as usual, we denote by
RG
the group ring of
G
over
R
. Recall that a group
G
is a
p
-group
if every element of
G
has order that is a power of the prime number
p
. Also, we recollect that a group is
locally finite
if each finitely generated subgroup is finite.

The homomorphism
\varepsilon:RG\rightarrow R
, defined by
\varepsilon(\displaystyle\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}g)=\displaystyle\sum_{g\in G}a_{g}
, is called the
augmentation map
of
RG
and its kernel, denoted by
\Delta(RG)
, is called the
augmentation ideal
of
RG
.

Our first three technicalities here are the following.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
RG
is a GSWNC ring, then
R
is GSWNC.

Proof.

We know that
RG/\Delta(RG)\cong R
. Therefore, by Proposition
2.15
, it follows that
R
must be a GSWNC ring, as stated.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
R
be a GSWNC ring with
p\in Nil(R)
and let
G
be a locally finite
p
-group, where
p
is a prime. Then, the group ring
RG
is GSWNC.

Proof.

By
[
8
, Proposition 16]
, we know that
\Delta(RG)
is a nil-ideal. Thus, since
RG/\Delta(RG)\cong R
, Proposition
2.15
allows us to infer that
RG
is a GSWNC ring.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f:R\to S
be a non-zero epimorphism of rings with
{\rm Ker}(f)\cap{\rm Id}(R)=\{0\}
. Then,
R
is a GSWNC ring if, and only if,
S
is a GSWNC ring and
{\rm Ker}(f)
is a nil-ideal of
S
.

Proof.

Assume that
R
is GSWNC. According to the relation
S\cong R/Ker(f)
and Proposition
2.15
, it is enough to prove that
Ker(f)
is a nil-ideal. Now, let
a\in Ker(f)
. Thus,
a\notin U(R)
, so there exist
e\in Id(R)
and
q\in Nil(R)
with
a=q\pm e
and
eq=qe
. Then, we have,
0=f(a)=f(q)\pm f(e)
, and hence

f(e)\in Id(S)\cap Nil(S)=\{0\}
. This shows that
e\in Id(R)\cap Ker(f)=\{0\}
, so
a=q\in Nil(R)
, therefore,
Ker(f)
is a nil-ideal.

The converse is obvious by again Proposition
2.15
.
∎

A valuable consequence is the following one.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
R
be a ring and
G
be a group, where
\Delta(RG)\cap Id(RG)=\{0\}
. Then,
RG
is a GSWNC ring if, and only if,
R
is a GSWNC ring and
\Delta(RG)
is a nil-ideal of
RG
.

Proof.

This is clear by an epimorphism
\varepsilon:RG\to R
with
Ker(\varepsilon)=\Delta(RG)
and Lemma
3.3
.
∎

Our next three technicalities are as follows.

Lemma 3.5
.

[
27
, Lemma
2
]
.
Let
p
be a prime number with
p\in J(R)
. If
G
is a locally finite
p
-group, then
\Delta(RG)\subseteq J(RG)
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
R
be a ring and let
G
be a locally finite
p
-group, where
p
is a prime number and
p\in J(R)
. Then,
RG
is a GSWNC ring if, and only if,
R
is a GSWNC ring and
\Delta(RG)
is a nil-ideal of
RG
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
R
be a GSWNC ring and let
G
be a group such that
\Delta(RG)\subseteq J(RG)
. Then,
RG/J(RG)
is a GSWNC ring.

Proof.

We know that
RG=\Delta(RG)+R
, because
\Delta(RG)\subseteq J(RG)
, which implies that
RG=J(RG)+R
. Therefore,

R/(J(RG)\cap R)\cong(J(RG)+R)/J(RG)=RG/J(RG),

and since
R
is GSWNC, the quotient
R/(J(RG)\cap R)
is too GSWNC by Proposition
2.15
. Then, we conclude that the factor
RG/J(RG)
is a GSWNC ring, as required.
∎

An element
a\in R
is called an
involution
if
a^{2}=1
. An element
w\in R
is an unipotent if
1-w
is a nilpotent. A ring
R
is said to be an
IU
-ring if, for any
a
in
R
, either
a
or
-a
is the sum of an involution and an unipotent.

Our main statement here is the following.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
R
be a ring such that
2\notin U(R)
, and let
G
be a group such that
RG
is a GSWNC ring. Then,
G
is a
2
-group, where
2\in Nil(R)
.

Proof.

Suppose
RG
is a GSWNC ring. Invoking Lemma
2.32
, we have that either
RG
is a GSNC ring or
RG
is a strongly weakly nil-clean ring. If
RG
is a GSNC ring, one concludes from
[
10
, Theorem 3.7]
that
G
is a
p
-group, where
p\in Nil(R)
. If
p
is odd, this obviously contradicts
2\in U(R)
, so it must be that
p=2
.

If
RG
is a strongly weakly nil-clean ring, then
[
2
, Theorem 1.15]
riches us that either
R
is a strongly nil-clean ring and
G
is a
2
-group, or
R
is an
IU
ring and
G
is a
3
-group. If
R
is a strongly nil-clean ring and
G
is a
2
-group, there is nothing to prove, because from
[
15
, Proposition 3.14]
we have
2\in Nil(R)
. However, if
R
is an
IU
ring,
[
6
, Lemma 4.2]
tells us that
2\in U(R)
, which is a contradiction.
∎

The next construction is helpful to understand the complicated structure of GSWNC group rings.

Example 3.9
.

Let
G
be a non-trivial locally finite group and
2\notin U(R)
. Then,
\mathbb{Z}_{m}G
is a GSWNC ring if, and only if,
m=2^{k}
for some positive integer
k
and
G
is a
2
-group.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathbb{Z}_{m}G
is a GSWNC ring, then
\mathbb{Z}_{m}G
is GSNC or strongly weakly nil-clean by Lemma
2.32
. If
\mathbb{Z}_{m}G
is GSNC, then
G
is a
p
-group, where
p\in Nil(R)
. If
p
is odd, this contradicts
2\in U(R)
, so
p=2
. Also, we have
m=2^{k}
, because otherwise if for example
m=6
,
R=\mathbb{Z}_{6}
and,
G=C_{2}
, then clearly the ring
RG
is not GSNC (since if
RG
is GSNC, then
\mathbb{Z}_{6}
is GSNC by
[
10
, Proposition 2.7]
and this is contradiction). Now if
\mathbb{Z}_{m}G
is strongly weakly nil-clean then by
[
2
, Example 1.17]
, we have three cases:

(1)
m=2^{k}
for some positive integer
k
and
G
is a
2
-group.

(2)
m=3^{k}
for some positive integer
k
and
G
is a
3
-group.

(3)
m=2^{k}3^{l}
for some positive integers
k
,
l
and
G
is trivial.

According to the assumptions, cases (2) and (3) will not happen. So only case (1) happens and the result is obtained.

Conversely, let
m=2^{k}
for some positive integer
k
and
G
is a
2
-group. Then
\mathbb{Z}_{m}G
is a strongly weakly nil-clean ring by
[
2
, Example 1.17]
and hence it is GSWNC. ∎

We, hereafter, denote the center of group
G
by
Z(G)
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
R
be a ring with
2\notin U(R)
and
G
be a group. If
RG
is a GSNC ring, then
Z(G)
is a
2
-group.

Proof.

Take
g\in Z(G)
. We know that every GSNC ring is GNC. So, by the
[
11
, Proposition 2.6]
and hypothesis,
2\in Nil(RG)
. There is a ring epimorphism
RG\rightarrow(R/J(R))G
, so
(R/J(R))G
is GSNC. Hence, without loss of generality, we can assume
J(R)=0
. As an image of
RG
,
R
is GSNC, so we have
2=0
in
R
. Also,
1-g\in\Delta(RG)
, which implies that
1-g
is not invertible. Therefore, there exist
e=e^{2}\in RG
and
q\in Nil(RG)
such that
1-g=e+q
and
eq=qe
. Since
Z(G)
is abelian, we have

1-e=g(1+g^{-1}q)\in Id(RG)\cap U(RG),

which guarantees that
e=0
. Thus,
1-g=q\in Nil(RG)
. Hence, there is a natural number
k
such that
{(1-g)}^{2^{k}}=0
. Since
char(RG)=2
, it must be that
g^{2^{k}}=1
. So,
Z(G)
is a
2
-group, as claimed.
∎

The hyper-center of a group
G
, denoted by
H(G)
, is defined to be the union of the (transfinite) upper central series of the group
G
.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
R
be a ring with
2\notin U(R)
and
G
be a group. If
RG
is a GSNC ring, then
R
is a GSNC ring and the hyper-center
H(G)
is a
2
-group.

Proof.

Utilizing a completely similar method as that of Theorem 2.6 in
[
24
]
, the claim follows.
∎

Recall that a group
G
is called
nilpotent
if it has a normal series

1=G_{0}\leq G_{1}\leq\dots\leq G_{n}=G

such that

G_{i+1}/G_{i}\leq Z(G/G_{i}),

for
i=0,1,\dots,n-1
. Moreover, such a series is called a
central series
for
G
.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
R
be a ring with
2\notin U(R)
and
G
be a nilpotent group. Then,
RG
is a GSNC ring, if, and only if,
R
is a GSNC ring and
G
is a
2
-group.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
. It follows immediately from Lemma
3.11
.

(\Leftarrow)
. The hypothesis and
[
11
, Proposition 2.6]
yields that
2\in Nil(R)
. In accordance with
[
8
, Proposition 16]
, we know that
\Delta(RG)
is a nil-ideal.
Now the isomorphism
RG/\Delta(RG)\cong R
and
[
10
, Proposition 4.7(i)]
allows us to get that
RG

is a GSNC ring.
∎

We are now prepared to proceed by establishing the following affirmation.

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
R
be a ring such that
2\notin U(R)
, and
G
be a nilpotent group. Then,
RG
is a GSWNC ring if, and only if,
R
is a GSNC ring and
G
is a
2
-group.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
). Suppose
RG
is a GSWNC ring. From Lemma
2.32
, we deduce that either
RG
is a GSNC ring or
RG
is a strongly weakly nil-clean ring. If, foremost,
RG
is a GSNC ring, one concludes from Lemma
3.12
that
R
is a GSNC ring and
G
is a
2
-group. If
RG
is a strongly weakly nil-clean ring, then
[
2
, Theorem 1.15]
riches us that either
R
is a strongly nil-clean ring and
G
is a
2
-group, or
R
is an
IU
-ring and
G
is a
3
-group. If
R
is a strongly nil-clean ring and
G
is a
2
-group, there is nothing left to prove, because from
[
15
, Proposition 3.14]
, we have
2\in Nil(R)
. However, if
R
is an
IU
-ring and
G
is a
3
-group, we derive with the aid of
[
6
, Lemma 4.2]
that
2\in U(R)
, which is a contradiction.

(
\Leftarrow
). It follows directly from Lemma
3.12
.
∎
