Intravascular Ultrasound to Assess Transcatheter Heart Valve Geometry During Aortic Valve-In-Valve Procedure
Authors: Radosław Pracoń, Maciej Dąbrowski, Yusuke Kobari, Ania Pyłko, Arif Khokhar, Jacek Kądziela, Karol A. Sadowski, Michał Kania, Marcin Demkow, Ole De Backer, Łukasz Kalińczuk
Published: 2026

A 68-year-old man with a degenerated 23-mm Perimount surgical aortic bioprosthesis (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, California) was accepted for transfemoral valve-in-valve transcatheter aortic valve replacement. Although a 23-mm Navitor transcatheter heart valve (THV) (Abbott, Santa Clara, California) would provide 10% oversizing relative to the 21-mm true inner diameter, planned Perimount fracture with a 24-mm Atlas Gold balloon (Becton Dickinson, Tempe, Arizona) prompted selection of a 25-mm Navitor. Because THV underexpansion after valve-in-valve transcatheter aortic valve replacement is common and may impair hemodynamics, intraprocedural assessment of the final result may be clinically relevant.1

Navitor stent-frame dimensions were measured by angiography and intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) at inflow, leaflet nadir, and leaflet coaptation; Perimount ring dimensions were also measured. IVUS was performed using a 15-MHz OptiCross 35 catheter (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, Massachusetts) over the standard 0.035-inch stiff guidewire and measured inner-frame minimum diameter (Dmin), maximum diameter, cross-sectional area (CSA), and geometric orifice area (GOA). Attention was paid to alignment parallel to the THV long axis because oblique acquisition may preferentially affect maximum diameter and CSA, whereas Dmin is less affected.2

After Navitor implantation, the available angiographic projection showed subnominal frame dimensions. Compared with IVUS Dmin, angiographic diameters were larger by 2.62–3.60 mm, corresponding to 10.5–14.4 percentage-point differences in calculated expansion (Figure 1). After Atlas postdilatation, these differences narrowed to 0.61–1.07 mm and 2.4–4.3 percentage points, respectively. At inflow, the circular-equivalent area inferred from angiography differed from directly measured IVUS CSA by only 0.4 percentage points. Concordance was not uniform across THV levels: at the leaflet nadir, angiography implied 75.3% area expansion vs. 67.6% by IVUS, whereas at coaptation it implied 84.5% vs. 93.3%, corresponding to 7.7-point and 8.8-point discrepancies (Figure 2). Dynamic IVUS further showed that GOA increased from 250.88 mm2 to 366.77 mm2 (+46.2%) and leaflet folding decreased after postdilatation. Angiography remains essential for device position and gross frame expansion; however, even orthogonal projections depict only 2-dimensional frame deformation and cannot directly visualize the leaflet-defined systolic orifice. This limitation may be amplified in valve-in-valve procedures by superimposition of the surgical and transcatheter frames. In this case, postdilatation substantially improved angiography–IVUS diameter concordance, yet tomographic IVUS continued to demonstrate level-dependent, nonuniform geometry. More importantly, dynamic IVUS showed that near-nominal coaptation-level frame expansion did not translate into an equivalently large systolic leaflet opening. Thus, a satisfactory frame silhouette does not establish the size or configuration of the functional orifice. If angiography and hemodynamics alone had been used, stopping after Atlas would have been reasonable; conversely, residual lower-frame constraint could have prompted further optimization. IVUS therefore added mechanistic information in this procedural gray zone, supporting completion without further balloon escalation, although this case cannot establish that management would have differed without IVUS. GOA is a geometric determinant, not a direct measurement, of transvalvular flow; the favorable follow-up gradient provides complementary functional corroboration. Prospective evaluation is required to establish clinical benefit and cost-effectiveness.
