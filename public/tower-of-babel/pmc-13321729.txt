Cultural background and pandemic context as moderators of the association between expressive suppression and sleep quality in healthy adults: a systematic review and meta‑analysis
Authors: Wang Donglin, Wang Xuewei, Sharifah Zubaidiah Syed Jaapar, Asrenee Ab Razak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExpressive suppression is a widely used emotion regulation strategy, but studies have reported mixed findings regarding its association with sleep quality in healthy adults. This meta-analysis aimed to synthesize the evidence on the association between expressive suppression and sleep quality in non-clinical adult samples and examine the moderating effect of cultural background and pandemic context.MethodsElectronic databases including PubMed, EBSCO, and Web of Science were systematically searched from database inception to May 2025 using predefined search terms related to emotion regulation and sleep quality. Studies were included if they: (1) involved non-clinical adult samples; (2) assessed expressive suppression with the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ); (3) used standardized self-report measures of sleep quality (e.g., PSQI, ISI); and (4) reported zero-order Pearson correlations. A total of twenty-three independent samples (N = 13,636) met the inclusion criteria. A random-effects model was used to estimate the pooled effect size, and pre-specified subgroup and moderation analyses were conducted.ResultsExpressive suppression showed a small but significant positive association with poorer sleep quality (r = .14, 95% CI [0.12, 0.16], p < .0001). Between-study heterogeneity was low (I² = 28.21%). The association was stronger in studies explicitly focused on COVID-19–related stress than in studies conducted in general contexts, whereas cultural background, type of sleep measure, study design, and publication period did not significantly moderate the effect.ConclusionHabitual use of expressive suppression appears to be associated with poorer sleep quality in healthy adults, and this association may be stronger under conditions of major psychosocial stress such as the COVID-19 pandemic. Interventions that reduce reliance on suppression or promote more flexible emotion regulation may help support sleep health, particularly during periods of widespread stress.Trial registrationThis study has been registered with the PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251059053).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04527-0.

Introduction

Sleep problems have become a major public health concern worldwide [1, 2]. Studies show that about one-third of adults worldwide have varying degrees of sleep problems, such as difficulty falling asleep, sleep maintenance disorders, or decreased subjective sleep quality [3, 4]. Sleep quality is central to both physical and psychological functioning and has been linked to cognitive performance, emotion regulation, and broader psychological well-being [5–7]. In everyday life, many healthy adults report persistent difficulties falling asleep, staying asleep, or feeling rested, often in the context of chronic stress and heavy mental workload [8, 9].

A growing body of literature has linked persistent sleep problems not only to cognitive decline and weakened immune function, but also to heightened vulnerability to mental health disorders such as depression and anxiety [10–12]. Against this backdrop, researchers have increasingly turned to emotion regulation as a key mechanism underlying individual differences in sleep quality [13–15].

Emotion regulation refers to the processes through which individuals influence their emotional experience, expression, and behavior when facing internal and external emotional challenges [16]. Expressive suppression differs from strategies such as cognitive reappraisal that operate earlier in the emotion regulation process. It is a strategy that is deployed after an emotional response has been activated and involves consciously inhibiting the outward display of emotion [17].

Although expressive suppression is often viewed as appropriate or even necessary in many cultures (particularly those that emphasize emotional restraint) [18, 19], research has suggested that frequent reliance on this strategy may be associated with increased physiological arousal, activation of the sympathetic nervous system, and a mismatch between internal experience and external expression, which may contribute to increased tension and disrupt sleep architecture [20, 21]. For example, in social situations, a person may feel intense anger or anxiety but chooses to suppress these emotions to avoid conflict, even though the emotional response remains strong internally. Similarly, an employee facing unfair criticism from a supervisor may suppress their frustration to maintain workplace harmony. Individuals who habitually rely on suppression tend to report greater interpersonal tension, lower well-being, and more frequent insomnia symptoms [22–24].

While empirical interest in the link between expressive suppression and sleep quality has grown in recent years, findings remain inconsistent. Some studies suggest that suppression negatively affects sleep by increasing rumination and physiological arousal [25, 26], while other studies have reported weak or non-significant associations, and this inconsistency may be attributable to methodological and contextual factors, such as sample characteristics (e.g., cultural background), measurement tools, or the specific stressors individuals face [27, 23, 28].

Despite growing evidence of a link between expressive suppression and sleep, no prior meta-analysis has systematically quantified this association in non-clinical adult populations or examined contextual factors that might shape it. Little is known about whether cultural background or large-scale stressors such as the COVID-19 pandemic moderate the strength of this association. Therefore, the present meta-analysis was conducted to address these gaps. Our primary objectives were twofold: first, to provide a robust estimate of the association between expressive suppression and sleep quality in healthy adults; and second, to systematically investigate key moderators, including cultural background, study design, and the stress context related to the COVID-19 pandemic, that may explain variability in this relationship.

Method

Search criteria

We conducted a systematic literature search in line with standard recommendations for meta-analytic reviews [29–31]. Electronic searches were conducted in PubMed, EBSCO, and Web of Science from database inception to May 2025. The search strategy employed the following keywords and Boolean operators: (“emotion regulation” OR “cognitive reappraisal” OR “expressive suppression”) AND (“sleep quality” OR insomnia OR “sleep disturbance” OR psqi). The full database-specific search strategies are provided in Supplementary Material S1. This initial search yielded 1,204 records, which were subsequently screened for eligibility. In addition, the reference lists of included studies were manually screened to identify any potentially relevant articles not captured by the database search. The entire literature search and screening process is summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were eligible if they (a) were published in peer-reviewed journals; (b) quantitatively examined the association between expressive suppression and sleep quality; and (c) assessed suppression using the expressive suppression subscale of the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ), including translated or culturally validated versions.

Although “cognitive reappraisal” was also included in the search strings, this was done solely to capture studies administering both ERQ subscales. During screening and data extraction, only data for expressive suppression were retained. Sleep quality had to be measured with standardized, psychometrically validated self-report instruments (e.g., PSQI, ISI). To ensure comparability across studies, only zero-order correlations between expressive suppression and sleep quality were extracted. This strategy minimizes bias introduced by differing sets of covariates across studies and follows established meta-analytic recommendations [32, 30]. Authors of studies lacking zero-order correlations were contacted where feasible to request unadjusted data, though not all responded.

Finally, the sample populations were restricted to non-clinical adult participants (aged ≥ 18). Studies that included mixed samples (e.g., adolescents and adults) were retained only if separate results for participants aged 18 or older could be extracted. This restriction helps ensure group homogeneity and enhances the generalizability of the findings to healthy adult populations.

Data extraction and quality assessment

All eligible studies that satisfied the inclusion standards (refer to Table 1) were distributed to two independent researchers (WDL and WXW) for systematic coding. Each article was evaluated based on the following study-level variables: (a) publication details, including the first author and year; (b) total number of independent samples and corresponding sample sizes; (c) reported zero-order correlation coefficient (r) between expressive suppression and sleep quality; (d) average age and age range of participants; (e) national or regional context of the study; (f) suppression measurement instrument used (limited to the ERQ or its adaptations); (g) sleep assessment tool (e.g., PSQI, ISI); and (h) research design type (cross-sectional, longitudinal, or intervention-based).

Methodological quality was assessed using design-appropriate tools: cross-sectional studies and intervention studies were appraised with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist [33], and longitudinal studies were evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies [34]. Two researchers (WDL and WXW) conducted the assessments independently, and disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (AAR).

After completing the preliminary coding of their assigned articles, each coder cross-checked the coding results of the studies responsible for other coders. For the coding results with disagreements, consensus was reached through collective discussion to ensure the accuracy and consistency of the data.

The overall certainty of evidence was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) framework, which evaluates evidence across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The quality of evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low based on these criteria [35].

To further explore the potential sources of heterogeneity, this study set up five meta-moderators based on meta-analysis for meta-regression analysis. These variables were selected based on the theoretical basis of existing literature and methodological considerations, as follows:

Cultural background was included as a meta-moderating variable to explore whether there are systematic differences in the effect size between expressive suppression and sleep quality in different cultural systems. Previous studies have suggested that the use and social meaning of expressive suppression may differ across cultural contexts. For example, in collectivist cultures (such as China and Japan), expressive suppression is more common and has a higher social acceptance, and may not carry the same interpersonal implications as in more individualistic contexts; while in individualistic cultures (such as the United States and Germany), expressive suppression is often regarded as a maladaptive emotional coping style, which may be associated with poor psychological and physiological outcomes. Therefore, this study divided the culture into “collectivist culture group” and “individualistic culture group” according to the country of the subject sample. The cultural classification is based on the mainstream division of cultural dimensions in existing literature [36], which helps to test whether cultural factors regulate the strength of the correlation between expressive suppression and sleep quality. Consistent with previous meta-analyses [37, 38], cultural classification was operationalized using Hofstede’s Individualism (IDV) index. Countries with comparatively higher IDV scores were coded as individualistic, whereas those with comparatively lower IDV scores were coded as collectivistic, consistent with prior cross-cultural emotion-regulation research. Samples were classified according to the primary national context in which participants were recruited. None of the included studies reported multicultural, immigrant-majority, or mixed-country samples that required separate cultural coding [39].

Publication period was also examined as a potential moderator, aiming to assess whether effect sizes differed between recently published and earlier studies. The median publication year was 2021 (range: 2016–2025). Accordingly, studies published in 2020 or earlier were categorized as Time 1 (n = 9), while those published in 2021 or later were categorized as Time 2 (n = 14). This classification helps identify any temporal trends in effect sizes, particularly considering the increasing number of studies on this topic in recent years.

This study included study design as a meta-moderator to examine whether different methodological approaches influence the effect size between expressive suppression and sleep quality. Based on the temporal structure reported in the original studies, research designs were classified into three categories: cross-sectional (k = 19), longitudinal (k = 2), and intervention studies (k = 2). This classification aimed to assess the potential moderating role of study design. However, given the very small number of studies in the longitudinal and intervention categories, a formal statistical meta-regression across the three design types was deemed inappropriate and was therefore not performed. The overall effect size is thus predominantly driven by the large cross-sectional subgroup.

Sleep quality measurement was included as one of the meta-moderating variables to examine whether the type of instrument used influenced the strength of the association between expressive suppression and sleep quality. Studies were grouped based on the specific standardized sleep measures employed, including the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI; Buysse et al., [40]) and the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI; Bastien et al., [41]). Only studies that reported global scores or representative subscale scores from these instruments were included. Measures that were used in only a single study or lacked validation in peer-reviewed literature were excluded from the meta-moderation analysis. Ultimately, studies using PSQI or ISI were included in the subgroup analysis to assess whether the sleep quality measure moderated the observed effect sizes.

The final meta-moderating variable examined whether the study’s explicit research focus was the COVID-19 pandemic. This moderator was designed to test if the psychological context of a major, salient stressor altered the strength of the association. Studies were categorized into the “COVID-19 Focus” group if they explicitly framed their investigation around pandemic-related stressors (e.g., mentioning “COVID-19” in the title, abstract, or as a central theme of the introduction). Studies that did not have this explicit focus were categorized into the " Non-COVID-19 Focus” group. Of the 23 included samples, 4 were identified as having a COVID-19 focus, while the remaining 19 had a general, non-pandemic-specific focus. This classification allows for a test of whether the link between expressive suppression and sleep quality is amplified when individuals are assessed within the specific psychological context of the pandemic. This coding approach serves as an effective proxy to differentiate studies conducted under typical circumstances from those capturing a context of heightened and pervasive psychosocial stress, thereby allowing for a direct test of stress as a contextual moderator.

Statistical analyses

To integrate the quantitative data of each study, this study used the Metafor package in R version 4.5.0 [42] to conduct a meta-analysis of the random effects model [43]. Due to its good statistical properties and easy interpretation [44], this study selected the Pearson correlation coefficient (Pearson’s r) as the effect size indicator. Given that higher scores on the included sleep quality instruments (e.g., PSQI, ISI) reflect poorer sleep quality, a positive correlation (r > 0) indicates that greater use of expressive suppression is associated with worse sleep quality. Conversely, a negative correlation would suggest that expressive suppression is linked to better sleep quality.

Since Pearson’s r is not normally distributed, all correlation coefficients were converted to Fisher’s z values before merging the effect sizes of each study, and then converted back to r values after calculating the overall effect for interpretation [44]. The Cochran’s Q test was used to assess heterogeneity between studies to determine whether the effect size variation exceeded the fluctuations that may be caused by sampling error. At the same time, the I² statistic was reported to quantify what proportion of the inter-study effect differences were due to true heterogeneity rather than sampling error [45]. To further explore the source of heterogeneity, this study pre-set multiple meta-moderating variables for analysis, including sleep quality measurement tools (PSQI or ISI), cultural background (Individualistic or Collectivistic), publication period (≤ 2020 or ≥ 2021), study design (cross-sectional study, longitudinal study, or intervention study) and COVID-19 context. To comprehensively assess potential publication bias and the risk of selective reporting, we employed a combination of methods. These included visual inspection of funnel plots, complementary quantitative diagnostics using Egger’s regression test and Begg’s rank correlation test, and the trim-and-fill method to estimate the impact of potentially missing studies.

Results

Study characteristics and quality of literature

Information for all studies included in this meta-analysis is presented in Table 1. A total of 23 independent samples were included, comprising 13,636 participants in total. The average sample size per study was 582 participants, with individual sample sizes ranging from 53 to 1,929. The observed correlation coefficients ranged from 0.06 to 0.278. Among the 23 effect sizes, 20 reported statistically significant associations (p < .05).

The quality of included cross-sectional and intervention studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist, with scores ranging from 6 to 8, indicating generally high methodological quality.

Longitudinal studies were evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies, with total scores ranging from 7 to 8, indicating high quality. Further details are provided in Supplementary Material S2 and S3.

Meta-analysis estimation

A random-effects model was conducted to estimate the overall association between expressive suppression and sleep quality across 23 independent samples (k = 23). The analysis yielded a significant average correlation coefficient of r = .143, (SE = 0.011, z = 13.05, p < .0001, 95% CI [0.122, 0.165]), suggesting a small positive association between expressive suppression and poorer sleep quality. Higher levels of expressive suppression were associated with higher scores on sleep disturbance scales, indicating poorer sleep quality, as seen in the forest plot in Fig. 2. The Q statistic for heterogeneity was non-significant (Q (22) = 29.64, p = .13), indicating that the observed variance among effect sizes may be largely attributable to sampling error. The I² statistic was 28.21%, suggesting low to moderate heterogeneity across studies. Given the relatively low heterogeneity, these findings suggest a small but statistically significant positive association between expressive suppression and poorer sleep quality across adult non-clinical samples.

First, cultural background does not significantly moderate the relationship between expressive suppression and sleep quality. Regardless of whether the research samples come from individualistic culture or collectivist culture, a consistent positive association was observed in both cultural subgroups, indicating that no significant moderation was observed across the countries included in the studies.

Second, in terms of sleep quality measurement tools, although the included studies used different scales (such as PSQI and ISI), meta-moderation analysis showed that the type of measurement tool did not significantly affect the effect size between expressive suppression and sleep quality. This indicates that the association is robust across different measurement instruments.

Third, the publication time of the study did not have a significant moderating effect on the relationship. Whether it is early (2020 and before) or recent (2021 and later) studies, the correlation between expressive suppression and sleep quality remains consistent, indicating that this effect is stable over time.

Fourth, the association between expressive suppression and sleep quality was similar across cross-sectional, longitudinal, and intervention designs, and differences in effect size were not statistically significant.

Finally, the study focus (COVID-19 vs. General Context) was confirmed to be a significant moderating variable. The analysis revealed that the positive correlation between expressive suppression and poorer sleep quality was significantly stronger in studies with an explicit COVID-19 focus compared to those with a general focus.

Overall, a consistent positive correlation was observed between expressive suppression and poorer sleep quality across most subgroups. Although this association proved robust across different cultures, measurement instruments, and study designs, the most theoretically significant finding of this study was the moderating role of the research context. The analysis confirmed that this positive correlation was significantly stronger in studies with an explicit COVID-19 focus. In contrast, cultural background, type of sleep quality measure, publication period, and study design did not show a significant moderating effect on this relationship. These results indicate that the link between expressive suppression and sleep quality is stable under most conditions but is significantly amplified in contexts of major, widespread psychosocial stress.

Publication bias

Egger’s regression test indicated marginal funnel plot asymmetry (z = 1.964, p = .050), suggesting the presence of potential publication bias. However, this result should be interpreted with caution given the limitations of Egger’s test, particularly its sensitivity to heterogeneity and varying standard errors when the number of studies is relatively small [62, 63]. As a complementary diagnostic, Begg’s rank correlation test was also conducted, yielding a significant result (Kendall’s tau = 0.388, p = .010), which further indicates a risk of selective reporting.

The funnel plot appeared slightly asymmetrical, and the trim-and-fill procedure estimated that four studies were missing on the left side of the funnel plot (SE = 3.22) [64]. After imputing these studies, the random-effects model was re-estimated using 27 studies (k = 27). The adjusted effect size was r = .137 (95% CI [0.115, 0.158]), still statistically significant (z = 12.55, p < .001). Although the adjusted effect remained statistically significant, the pooled effect size remained small (Fig. 3).

Certainty of evidence

Using the GRADE framework, the overall certainty of evidence for the association between expressive suppression and poorer sleep quality was rated as very low. Because the pooled estimate was derived primarily from cross-sectional observational studies, the certainty of evidence began at low and was downgraded by one additional level due to suspected publication bias. Further details are provided in Supplementary Material S4.

Discussion

Summary

The present findings indicate that expressive suppression is consistently linked to poorer sleep quality in healthy adults. Although the overall effect size was small (r = .143), this pattern was generally consistent across different samples, measures, and study designs.

At the same time, our findings indicate that this association is not entirely context-free. The effect size was significantly larger in studies that explicitly focused on COVID-19 related stress compared with studies conducted in more general contexts. This pattern is consistent with the idea that situations characterized by pervasive and prolonged stress may intensify the costs of relying on suppression. During the pandemic, individuals were exposed to heightened uncertainty, health concerns, and social isolation, while often having fewer opportunities to use adaptive coping strategies [65, 66]. In such conditions, suppressing emotional expression may be both more frequent and more effortful, thereby exerting a stronger impact on sleep.

The observed association aligns well with Gross’s Process Model of Emotion Regulation [67], which conceptualizes expressive suppression as a strategy used after an emotional response has been activated. In contrast to antecedent focused strategies such as reappraisal, suppression does not substantially change the emotional meaning of a situation [68, 69]. Instead, maintaining control over outward expression may require sustained physiological arousal and cognitive effort, which are incompatible with sleep initiation and maintenance [70, 71, 13, 72].

A complementary account comes from models of perseverative cognition: suppression is associated with greater rumination and worry, which are well-established predictors of insomnia symptoms [73, 74, 25, 24]. By inhibiting expression without resolving underlying affect, suppression may prolong hyperarousal, an effect that could be amplified under chronic and uncontrollable stressors such as the COVID-19 pandemic [75, 76].

No significant moderation was observed across the countries and regions represented in the included studies with respect to the association between expressive suppression and sleep quality. This finding may appear somewhat unexpected, given that expressive suppression is often described as more socially sanctioned in many collectivistic cultural contexts, where emotional restraint is valued for the sake of group harmony [77–79]. One interpretation is that cultural norms may shape the social meanings and interpersonal consequences of suppression, but do not fully eliminate its physiological and cognitive costs. Even when suppression is normative, the autonomic arousal and mental effort required to inhibit emotional expression may still interfere with sleep [80–82]. At the same time, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Our cultural coding was based on broad country-level classifications, which may not fully capture individual-level cultural orientation, and the number of cultural contexts represented in the included studies was limited. Accordingly, the present results should be interpreted as indicating that no statistically significant moderation was observed across the included countries and regions, rather than as evidence that cultural influences are absent.

Taken together, the present findings suggest that expressive suppression is not merely a benign interpersonal strategy but a psychological style that carries health-relevant costs, particularly for sleep. These costs appear to be relatively small at the individual level, but they may accumulate over time and become more pronounced under conditions of sustained psychosocial stress. This interpretation is further supported by the randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and intervention studies included in our review [51, 59]. While the majority of our samples were cross-sectional, the intervention subgroup demonstrated a notably stronger association (r = .247) between emotion regulation and sleep quality. The two RCTs included in this review both evaluated mindfulness-based interventions and provide preliminary experimental support for the possibility that modifying maladaptive emotion-regulation tendencies can improve sleep outcomes; however, the small number of these studies precludes drawing strong causal conclusions. From a clinical and public health perspective, the results highlight the importance of considering how people cope with their emotions when designing interventions to improve sleep.

Methodological limitations

First, most of the included studies used cross-sectional designs, which limits our ability to draw causal conclusions. Given the suspected publication bias, the overall certainty of the current evidence was rated as very low according to the GRADE framework. Although higher levels of expressive suppression were consistently associated with poorer sleep quality, the temporal direction of this relationship remains unclear. It is possible that suppression contributes to sleep problems, but it is also plausible that chronic sleep disturbance undermines prefrontal control and emotion regulation capacity, thereby increasing reliance on suppression [83, 84]. Bidirectional processes are therefore likely. Longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to clarify temporal precedence and to test potential mediating mechanisms, such as rumination or physiological hyperarousal.

Second, the meta-analysis focused exclusively on zero-order correlations to enhance consistency across studies by avoiding the variability introduced by different covariate adjustments. While this enhances the comparability across studies, it may have led to the exclusion of potentially relevant findings from studies that reported only adjusted estimates. Future reviews may consider reaching out to authors to obtain unadjusted data where necessary [30, 31].

Third, publication bias remains a concern. Although Egger’s regression test was only marginally significant and should be interpreted cautiously, the significant Begg’s test and the asymmetry observed in the funnel plot suggest that studies with small or null effects may be underrepresented in the published literature, a pattern commonly interpreted as indicative of potential publication bias [63, 85]. The trim-and-fill analysis indicated that the adjusted effect remained statistically significant but slightly reduced (r = .137). Although the association persisted after adjustment, the small magnitude of the pooled effect and the potential influence of selective reporting suggest that the findings should be interpreted with caution. Taken together, these diagnostics indicate that the observed effect may be partially influenced by publication bias.

Future directions

Although interest in this topic has increased in recent years, most available studies still rely on cross-sectional designs (k = 19 out of 23 samples), limiting our ability to make causal inferences. Given that the overall certainty of evidence remains very low, a primary direction for future research is to prioritize the use of longitudinal and experimental designs to establish causality and identify mediating mechanisms (e.g., rumination, hyperarousal). Cultural and contextual factors, such as social norms around emotional expression, also warrant further exploration. Although our analysis did not find a significant moderating effect of cultural background, this finding should be interpreted with caution. The common individualistic-collectivistic dichotomy, which we employed, is a significant oversimplification that may mask more nuanced differences [86, 87]. Therefore, future meta-analyses may benefit from larger, more culturally diverse datasets [88].

Additionally, researchers should strive to use objective sleep measures such as actigraphy or polysomnography and include more demographically diverse samples to enhance generalizability. Transparent reporting and publication of null results are essential for minimizing bias and promoting cumulative knowledge building in this field [89].

Given the consistent association between suppression and poorer sleep, intervention programs based on cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) or mindfulness approaches such as Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) or Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) may help individuals reduce habitual reliance on suppression and foster greater emotional flexibility [90, 91]. Future research should also investigate whether improvements in emotion regulation serve as a mediating pathway through which such interventions exert beneficial effects on sleep, using longitudinal or experimental mediation designs to clarify causal mechanisms.

Conclusion

The current meta-analysis synthesized 23 independent samples (N = 13,636) to quantitatively examine the relationship between expressive suppression and sleep quality in healthy adults. A small but significant positive correlation was found, indicating that greater use of suppression was associated with poorer sleep outcomes. This association remained stable across different study designs, measurement instruments, and the countries and regions represented in the included studies, but appeared stronger in studies conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic. These findings suggest that expressive suppression is a modest correlate of poorer sleep quality, particularly under conditions of chronic stress. Future research should further examine the underlying mechanisms and evaluate intervention strategies aimed at reducing reliance on suppression to promote better sleep and overall well-being.
