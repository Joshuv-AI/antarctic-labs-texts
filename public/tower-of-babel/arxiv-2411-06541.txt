Counterexamples to a Weitz-Style Reduction for Multispin Systems
Kuikui Liu, Nitya Mani, Francisco Pernice

Abstract
In a seminal paper, Weitz [ Wei06 ] showed that for two-state spin systems, such as the Ising and hardcore models from statistical physics, correlation decay on trees implies correlation decay on arbitrary graphs. The key gadget in Weitz’s reduction has been instrumental in recent advances in approximate counting and sampling, from analysis of local Markov chains like Glauber dynamics to the design of deterministic algorithms for estimating the partition function. A longstanding open problem in the field has been to find such a reduction for more general multispin systems like the uniform distribution over proper colorings of a graph. In this paper, we show that for a rich class of multispin systems, including the ferromagnetic Potts model, there are fundamental obstacles to extending Weitz’s reduction to the multispin setting. A central component of our investigation is establishing nonconvexity of the image of the belief propagation functional, the standard tool for analyzing spin systems on trees. On the other hand, we provide evidence of convexity for the antiferromagnetic Potts model.

Introduction

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
and an alphabet of size
q
, a
q
-
spin system
(or
pairwise Markov random field
) is a probability distribution over configurations
\sigma\in[q]^{V}
where the probabilities are determined by the pairwise interactions of states
\sigma(u)
,
\sigma(v)
for neighboring vertices
(u,v)\in E
(see
Definition

1
). Important examples include the Ising model from statistical physics, and random solutions to well-studied constraint satisfaction problems like independent sets and proper colorings in graphs. Given such a distribution

, the following three algorithmic tasks, which are essentially equivalent
[

]

T.1

Sampling:
Generate a random

.

T.2

Counting:
Estimate the normalizing constant for the distribution

(see

).

T.3

Marginalization:
Given a vertex

for

One of the main problems in the field of counting and sampling has been to determine for a given

In this direction, a powerful intuition coming from statistical physics has been that such questions should be reducible, at least in many important cases, to analogous questions on trees which are typically easier to analyze. This has led to the following meta-question, which has been pursued by many works

,

,

,

,

,

]
:

Meta-Question 1
.

When can one reduce algorithmic questions about
Tasks

T.1
,
T.2
and
T.3
on general graphs to structural questions about the corresponding distributions on trees?

The general idea above is best illustrated with an example. Given a

on a graph

is the probability distribution

for all independent sets

. One of the early seminal results in this area

]

are computationally tractable for the hardcore model on the class of graphs of maximum degree

\Delta

-spin systems, i.e. all pairwise Markov random fields where each vertex can be in one of at most two possible states. This extension was later used by

While 2-spin theory is by now relatively well-developed, there is a scarcity of techniques in the multispin (

Conjecture 1

(Informal; Jerrum

]
)

Fix

It is known that if

(and

-colorings exists unless

[

. However, the algorithmic side of this question remains open. As in the hardcore model, the conjectured threshold
\Delta+1
for the number of colors is the critical value of

above which the uniform distribution over proper
q
-colorings exhibits correlation decay in the infinite
\Delta
-regular tree. This motivates a natural two-step approach to the conjecture:

S.1

Reduce the problem to proving (a sufficiently strong version of) correlation decay on trees.

Establish this correlation decay property on trees by analyzing belief propagation.

The second part of this program was nearly resolved in recent work

]
after earlier, weaker results due to

) was established on trees of maximum degree

,

,

. This discrepancy is due to the absence of a suitable reduction for general

For general
q
-spin systems with

, there is no Weitz-style gadget that reduces computation of vertex marginals in general graphs to trees.

Our results suggest that a genuinely new idea is required to resolve

1.1
Background and Main Results

We begin by defining the relevant objects, reinterpreting Weitz’s reduction, and formally stating our results.

Notation.

Throughout the rest of the paper, we let
G=(V,E)
be a graph of maximum degree
\Delta
, and we use
d
to refer to the degree of a specific vertex in question; hence
d\leq\Delta
. If

, we let
G-v
denote the graph where vertex

has been removed together with all its incident edges. We will write
[q]
for the space of colors (or spins), and use letters
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{c}

[q]
. The space of probability distributions over

. We let

Let
\mu
be a probability distribution over

\mathcal{U}

be the law of

\mu,\nu
are distributions on state spaces

for the product distribution over

(\mu\otimes\nu)(\omega,\sigma)=\mu(\omega)\nu(\sigma)
for all
(\omega,\sigma)\in\Omega\times\Sigma
. Finally, if
\mu
is a probability distribution on
[q]^{\mathcal{U}}
and
\bm{\lambda}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathcal{U}\times[q]}_{\geq 0}
is an

, we let

be the distribution on

defined as follows:

\displaystyle(\bm{\lambda}\star\mu)(\sigma)

\displaystyle\propto\mu(\sigma)\cdot\prod_{i\in\mathcal{U}}\bm{\lambda}_{i,\sigma(i)},\qquad\forall\sigma\in[q]^{\mathcal{U}}.

Since we are working with graphical models, depending on the context, we will typically use
\mathcal{U}=V
or
\mathcal{U}=[d]
, the latter of which should be thought of as the neighborhood of a vertex. If we wish to highlight the dependence of

on

.

Definition 1
.

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, a number of colors
q
, a symmetric
interaction matrix

A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q\times q}_{\geq 0}
, we define the Gibbs distribution of the associated
q
-spin system
as the following probability distribution
\mu
on
[q]^{V}
:

\displaystyle\mu(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z}\prod_{uv\in E}A_{\sigma(u),\sigma(v)}

where
Z
is the normalizing constant guaranteeing
\sum_{\sigma}\mu(\sigma)=1
; we call
Z
the
partition function
.

Oftentimes, an external field will also be applied to these distributions. We remark that the hardcore model over independent sets and the uniform distribution over proper colorings are examples of spin systems, with interaction matrices
A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{bmatrix}
and
A=\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}-I
, respectively.

Given a general
q
-spin system on a graph
G
and a vertex
v
with neighborhood
N(v)
, one can compute the marginal law
\mu_{v}
as a function of the joint law
\mu_{N(v)}
in the graph where

has been removed.
The following is a well-known generalization of the so-called belief propagation equations to general graphs (see, e.g.
[

, Proposition 6]
).

Lemma 1.1

(Recursion for General Graphs)
.

For every
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
, symmetric interaction matrix
A\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}^{q\times q}
, graph
G=(V,E)
, and vertex
v\in V
with
d
neighbors
N(v)=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{d}\}
, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{G,v}=F_{A,d}\left({\mu_{G-v,N(r)}}\right),

where the function
F_{A,d}
is defined as follows: for an arbitrary distribution
\nu
over
[q]^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle F_{A,d,\mathfrak{c}}(\nu)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathsf{def}}{=}\frac{G_{\mathfrak{c}}(\nu)}{\sum_{\mathfrak{b}\in[q]}G_{\mathfrak{b}}(\nu)},\qquad\forall\mathfrak{c}\in[q]

(1)

\displaystyle G_{A,d,\mathfrak{c}}(\nu)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathsf{def}}{=}{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau\sim\nu}\left[{\prod_{i=1}^{d}A_{\mathfrak{c},\tau(i)}}\right],\qquad\forall\mathfrak{c}\in[q].

(2)

Remark 1
.

One can also establish an analogous recursion in the presence of external fields. Throughout this paper, we drop the subscripts
A,d
when they are clear from context. In particular, even though belief propagation for the interaction matrix
A
is formally described by the
family
of functions
\{F_{A,d}\}_{d\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, we sometimes abuse notation and simply write
F
. We apply the same convention to
G
.

To keep this paper self-contained, a short proof of

1.1
is provided in
Appendix

A
. Note that when
T
is a tree rooted at

, with corresponding subtrees
T_{i}
rooted at

for each

, then
\mu_{G-r,N(r)}
factorizes as the product measure
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}\mu_{T_{i},u_{i}}
. In particular, restricting

1
to product measures
\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}\nu_{i}
recovers the more familiar form

F_{\mathfrak{c}}(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{d})=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{\mathfrak{b}\in[q]}A_{\mathfrak{c},\mathfrak{b}}\nu_{i}(\mathfrak{b})}{\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in[q]}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{\mathfrak{b}\in[q]}A_{\mathfrak{c},\mathfrak{a}}\nu_{i}(\mathfrak{a})}

(3)

of the belief propagation functional. It is well-known that correlation decay on trees (see
Step

S.2
) can be readily deduced from contraction of
F
in the space of product measures with respect to a suitable (pseudo)metric. In terms of
F
, the goal of obtaining a Weitz-style reduction for

S.1
can be viewed as reducing computation of

on a general measure

to computing

on product measures whose marginals are obtained from
\mu
by conditioning and marginalization. This leads to the following abstract reintepretation of Weitz’s general reduction.

Proposition 1.2

(
[
Wei06
]
)
.

Let
A\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}^{2\times 2}
be an arbitrary
2
-spin interaction matrix with spin set
\{0,1\}
. For every

, there exists a single

\bm{\lambda}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{\lambda}^{(d)}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}^{[d]\times\{0,1\}}

\displaystyle F(\mu)=F\left({\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}\left({\bm{\lambda}^{(i)}\star\mu}\right)_{i}}\right).

We postpone the proof to

[
Wei06

-spin systems like the hardcore and Ising models. In view of

Question 1

Is it the case that for every pair of positive integers

(i.e. not depending on the input measure

.

In this work, we construct a wide class of spin systems for arbitrary

where the answer to

is negative.

Theorem 1.3
.

For every pair of positive integers

, there exists a family of symmetric interaction matrices where the answer to

1
is negative for any monotone potential

.

In

, we explicitly describe a class of spin systems for which

1
has a negative answer. Our counterexamples include the ferromagnetic Potts model and a natural model of partial coloring in a

-spin system, with a special

’st non-interacting spin

To certify a negative answer to

for a specific interaction matrix

, we construct a pair of probability distributions

for which there is no such

simultaneously. We highlight that the witnesses

On the Image of Belief Propagation

Our results also have consequences for the structure of the image of the set of product measures under
F
.

Definition 2
.

For integers
d,q\geq 2
and a symmetric interaction matrix
A\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}^{q\times q}
, we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{A,d}^{\mathsf{prod}}\overset{\mathsf{def}}{=}\left\{F_{A}(\nu):\nu\text{ is a product measure over }[q]^{d}\right\}\subseteq\triangle_{q},

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{A,d}\overset{\mathsf{def}}{=}\left\{F_{A}(\mu):\mu\text{ is an arbitrary measure over }[q]^{d}\right\}\subseteq\triangle_{q}.

(6)

As we show in
Lemma

A.1
, these two sets are intimately related, with the latter being the convex hull of the former, i.e.
\mathcal{F}_{A,d}=\conv\left({\mathcal{F}_{A,d}^{\mathsf{prod}}}\right)
. These families of measures
\mathcal{F}_{A,d}^{\mathsf{prod}},\mathcal{F}_{A,d}
naturally appear in our study of Weitz-style reductions for
q
-spin systems, with the measures appearing on the right hand side of
Eq.

4
lying in
\mathcal{F}_{A,d}^{\mathsf{prod}}
. A key ingredient in our main result,
Theorem

2.1
, is to certify that for
B
as in
Eq.

8
, the set
\mathcal{F}_{B,d}^{\mathsf{prod}}
is not convex and thus for such spin systems,
\mathcal{F}_{B,d}\supsetneq\mathcal{F}_{B,d}^{\mathsf{prod}}
; see
Lemmas

2.2
and
3
. In particular, there are distributions
\mu
on
[q]^{d}
where
F_{B}(\mu)
cannot be achieved as
F_{B}(\nu)
by any single product measure
\nu
. This lack of convexity is crucial for
Theorem

1.3
.

The interaction matrices we construct to prove
Theorem

1.3
all have at least two positive eigenvalues. In other words, the presence of ferromagnetic interactions obstructs convexity of the set
\mathcal{F}_{A,d}^{\mathsf{prod}}
and yields a negative answer to
Question

1
. In light of this, it is natural to wonder if repulsion restores convexity of
\mathcal{F}_{A,d}^{\mathsf{prod}}
(i.e. equality of sets
\mathcal{F}_{A,d}=\mathcal{F}_{A,d}^{\mathsf{prod}}
), which could then lead to a positive answer to
Question

1
for antiferromagnetic models like colorings. We present evidence that this is the case by showing that for the finite-temperature
antiferromagnetic Potts model
, “most” distributions
\mu
on
[q]^{d}
one would encounter “in the wild” are such that
F(\mu)=F(\nu)
for some product measure
\nu
. One can interpret this as saying that for the antiferromagnetic Potts interaction matrix
A=\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}-(1-\beta)I
where
0\leq\beta\leq 1
, the set
\mathcal{F}_{A,d}^{\mathsf{prod}}
at least has a “large convex bulk”. For convenience, we will work in the range of temperatures
\beta
in which the resulting Gibbs distribution exhibits correlation decay on the infinite
(d+1)
-regular tree, although our result below can be made to accommodate a larger range of temperatures.

Theorem 1.4
.

Fix an integer
q\geq 2
, and suppose
d\geq 2q
. Consider the antiferromagnetic Potts interaction matrix
A=\mathbf{1}_{q}\mathbf{1}_{q}^{\top}-(1-\beta)I_{q}
in the uniqueness regime with respect to the infinite
(d+1)
-regular tree, i.e.
\max\left\{{0,1-\frac{q}{d+1}}\right\}\leq\beta\leq 1
. If
\mu
is any probability distribution over
[q]^{d}
satisfying the lower tail bounds

\displaystyle\forall\mathfrak{c}\in[q],\qquad\Pr_{\tau\sim\mu}\left[{\#\{i\in[d]:\tau(i)=\mathfrak{c}\}\leq\gamma_{*}\cdot\frac{d}{q}}\right]\leq\epsilon,

(7)

where
\gamma_{*}=\min\left\{{1-\sqrt{\beta}+\epsilon,\frac{1}{10}}\right\}
and
\epsilon\leq\frac{1}{4}
is arbitrary, then there exists a product measure
\nu
over
[q]^{d}
such that
F(\mu)=F(\nu)
.

One should interpret
\gamma_{*}
as an “effective lower bound” on the number of neighbors colored
\mathfrak{c}
as a fraction of the completely balanced allocation
d/q
, for any given color
\mathfrak{c}\in[q]
. Also, note that
1-\sqrt{\beta}\leq 1-\beta\leq\frac{q}{d+1}
, which should be thought of as small since we fix
q
and let
d\geq 2q
grow.

Remark 2
.

We emphasize that these tail bounds are quite weak. To illustrate the applicability of
Theorem

1.4
, observe that if
\mu=\mu_{G-r,N(r)}
is the marginal distribution over the neighborhood of a vertex
r
in a graph
G
of maximum degree
d+1
, then
\mu
must satisfy the marginal lower bound

\displaystyle\mu_{i}(\mathfrak{c})\geq\frac{\beta^{d}}{q-1+\beta^{d}}

for each neighbor
u_{i}\in N(r)
, which is attained by pinning the entire neighborhood of
u_{i}
to the color
\mathfrak{c}
. Hence, the law of
\alpha(\mathfrak{c})\overset{\mathsf{def}}{=}\#\{i\in[d]:\tau(i)=\mathfrak{c}\}
stochastically dominates the random variable
\sum_{i=1}^{d}X_{i}
, where
X_{i}=1
with probability
\frac{\beta^{d}}{q-1+\beta^{d}}
and
X_{i}=0
with probability
\frac{q-1}{q-1+\beta^{d}}
. In particular, by taking
\varepsilon=c/d
for some sufficiently small
c<1
, we have that

\gamma_{*}\leq 1-\sqrt{\beta}+\varepsilon\leq 1-\beta+\varepsilon\leq\frac{q}{d+1}+\frac{c}{d},

so that
\gamma_{*}\cdot\frac{d}{q}<1
. This yields

\displaystyle\Pr_{\tau\sim\mu}\left[{\alpha(\mathfrak{c})\leq\gamma_{*}\cdot\frac{d}{q}}\right]=\Pr_{\tau\sim\mu}\left[{\alpha(\mathfrak{c})=0}\right]\leq\left({1-\frac{\beta^{d}}{q-1+\beta^{d}}}\right)^{d}.

Since
q
is fixed and
\beta\geq 1-\frac{q}{d+1}
, we have
1-\frac{\beta^{d}}{q-1+\beta^{d}}\leq c_{q}
for a constant
0<c_{q}<1
independent of
d
, and so we certainly have
\Pr_{\tau\sim\mu}\left[{\alpha(\mathfrak{c})=0}\right]<\epsilon
even for moderate
d
(and certainly for large
d
).

Prior Works

As mentioned in the introduction, the relation between the belief propagation equations and complexity-theoretic questions for general graphs has been heavily studied in the literature. After Weitz’s seminal paper

Wei06
]
, Sly

(see

\Delta

\displaystyle A^{-1}G(\mu)^{1/d}\propto A^{-1}\diag(\bm{v})\left[{{\mathbb{E}}_{\alpha\sim\xi}\left[{\left({1-\bm{y}(\mathfrak{c})}\right)^{\alpha(\mathfrak{c})}}\right]^{1/d}}\right]_{\mathfrak{c}\in[q]}.

It follows that (1) is equivalent to the right-hand side lying in

. Now observe that we can write

\displaystyle A^{-1}\diag(\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\diag(\bm{v})^{-1}\diag(\bm{v})\left({\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\top}-D}\right)^{-1}\diag(\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\diag(\bm{v})^{-1}\left({\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}-\diag(\bm{y})}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\diag(\bm{v})^{-1}\left({-\diag(1/\bm{y})-\frac{\diag(1/\bm{y})\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\diag(1/\bm{y})}{1-\sum_{\mathfrak{c}\in[q]}\bm{y}(\mathfrak{c})^{-1}}}\right).

Hence, (1) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left({-\diag(1/\bm{y})-\frac{\diag(1/\bm{y})\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\diag(1/\bm{y})}{1-\sum_{\mathfrak{c}\in[q]}\bm{y}(\mathfrak{c})^{-1}}}\right)\cdot\left[{{\mathbb{E}}_{\alpha\sim\xi}\left[{\left({1-\bm{y}(\mathfrak{c})}\right)^{\alpha(\mathfrak{c})}}\right]^{1/d}}\right]_{\mathfrak{c}\in[q]}\geq 0

entrywise. Rearranging precisely yields (2).

1

Definition 3

(Bounded Potential)

We say a function

Remark 4

It will be clear in a moment why these boundedness assumptions are necessary. In practice, they often only need to be enforced on a smaller interval in

\displaystyle F_{A,d}^{\circ\varphi}\left({\bm{m}_{1},\dots,\bm{m}_{d}}\right)\overset{\mathsf{def}}{=}\varphi\left({F_{A,d}\left({\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}\varphi^{-1}(\bm{m}_{i})}\right)}\right)

Proof of
Lemma

B.3
.

Again, we follow the proof in
[

]
, which established an analogous identity in the case where the graph is a tree (so that there is no outer expectation with respect to
\xi

agnostic
to the specific form of belief propagation; all that matters is that
Question

1

For variables
\bm{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{V\times[q]}
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{G}(\bm{t})

\displaystyle\overset{\mathsf{def}}{=}\log\sum_{\sigma:V\to[q]}\left({\prod_{uv\in E}A_{\sigma(u),\sigma(v)}}\right)\cdot\exp\left({\sum_{v\in V}\bm{t}_{v,\sigma(v)}}\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{G,r,\mathfrak{c}}(\bm{t})

\displaystyle\overset{\mathsf{def}}{=}\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{r,\mathfrak{c}}}\mathcal{F}_{G}(\bm{t}).

Note that
\mathcal{F}_{G}(\bm{t})
is the log-partition function for the tilted measure
e^{\bm{t}}\star\mu
, and
\mathcal{P}_{G,r,\mathfrak{c}}(\bm{0})=\mu_{G,r}(\mathfrak{c})
. Now observe that

\displaystyle\varphi\left({\mathcal{P}_{G,r}(\bm{t})}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\left({\bm{\lambda}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{\lambda}^{(d)}}\right)\sim\xi}\left[{\varphi\left({F_{A}\left({\bigotimes_{i=1}^{d}\left({\bm{\lambda}^{(i)}\star e^{\bm{t}}\star\mu}\right)_{i}}\right)}\right)}\right],\qquad\forall\bm{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{V\times[q]}.

Crucially, universality of the mixture
\xi
means that
\xi
does not depend on
\bm{t}
whatsoever
. Hence, for each
\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{c}\in[q]
, if we differentiate entry
\mathfrak{c}
of the left-hand side with respect to the variable

for some
v\in V
and
\mathfrak{b}\in[q]
, we may push the differential inside the expectation by linearity. In particular,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{v,\mathfrak{b}}}\varphi\left({\mathcal{P}_{G,r,\mathfrak{c}}(\bm{t})}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\left({\bm{\lambda}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{\lambda}^{(d)}}\right)\sim\xi}\left[{\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{v,\mathfrak{b}}}F_{A,\mathfrak{c}}^{\circ\varphi}\left({\bm{m}_{1}\left({\bm{\lambda}^{(1)},\bm{t}}\right),\dots,\bm{m}_{d}\left({\bm{\lambda}^{(d)},\bm{t}}\right)}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\left({\bm{\lambda}^{(1)},\dots,\bm{\lambda}^{(d)}}\right)\sim\xi}\left[{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in[q]}\left({\partial_{i,\mathfrak{a}}F_{A,\mathfrak{c}}^{\circ\varphi}}\right)\left({\overrightarrow{\bm{m}}}\right)\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{v,\mathfrak{b}}}\varphi\left({\bm{p}_{i,\mathfrak{a}}\left({\bm{\lambda}^{(i)},\bm{t}}\right)}\right)}\right]

where
\bm{p}_{i,\mathfrak{a}}\left({\bm{\lambda}^{(i)},\bm{t}}\right)=\left({\bm{\lambda}^{(i)}\star e^{\bm{t}}\star\mu_{G-r}}\right)_{u_{i}}(\mathfrak{a})
and
\bm{m}_{i,\mathfrak{a}}\left({\bm{\lambda}^{(i)},\bm{t}}\right)=\varphi\left({\bm{p}_{i}\left({\bm{\lambda}^{(i)},\bm{t}}\right)_{\mathfrak{a}}}\right)
for each

and
\mathfrak{a}\in[q]
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{v,\mathfrak{b}}}\log\mathcal{P}_{G,r,\mathfrak{c}}(\bm{t})\Big|_{\bm{t}=\bm{0}}=\Psi_{\mu_{G}}^{r\to v}(\mathfrak{c},\mathfrak{b})

and so simultaneously, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{v,\mathfrak{b}}}\varphi\left({\mathcal{P}_{G,r,\mathfrak{c}}(\bm{t})}\right)\Big|_{\bm{t}=\bm{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{v,\mathfrak{b}}}\left({\varphi\circ\exp}\right)\left({\log\mathcal{P}_{G,r,\mathfrak{c}}(\bm{t})}\right)\Big|_{\bm{t}=\bm{0}}

\displaystyle=\Phi\left({\mathcal{P}_{G,r,\mathfrak{c}}(\bm{0})}\right)\cdot\mathcal{P}_{G,r,\mathfrak{c}}(\bm{0})\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{v,\mathfrak{b}}}\log\mathcal{P}_{G,r,\mathfrak{c}}(\bm{t})\Big|_{\bm{t}=\bm{0}}

\displaystyle=D_{r}(\mathfrak{c},\mathfrak{c})\cdot\Psi_{\mu_{G}}^{r\to v}(\mathfrak{c},\mathfrak{b}).

By the same token,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{v,\mathfrak{b}}}\varphi\left({\bm{p}_{i,\mathfrak{a}}\left({\bm{\lambda}^{(i)},\bm{t}}\right)}\right)\Big|_{\bm{t}=\bm{0}}=D_{i}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a})\cdot\Psi_{\bm{\lambda}^{(i)}\star\mu_{G-r}}^{u_{i}\to v}(\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}).

Substituting these calculations back in, aggregating them into a matrix over all
\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{c}\in[q]
, and writing everything as matrix-matrix products then finishes the proof.
∎

(depending on
\beta>1
), we can guarantee that

\displaystyle(q-k)(k+1)<(\beta+k)(q-k-1+\gamma).

For such a
1-\epsilon<\gamma<1
, the constant term is strictly negative for every

, and so we are done.
∎

Appendix D
Verifying Numerical Inequalities

Proof of
Claim

3.2
.

Write
f(q,\beta)=\frac{q-1}{\log\frac{1}{\beta}}\log\frac{q-1}{(q-1)-(1-\beta)}
. First, we claim that for all
0\leq\beta\leq 1
,
f
is monotone decreasing in
q
on
[1,\infty)
with
\lim_{q\to\infty}f(q,\beta)=\frac{1-\beta}{\log\frac{1}{\beta}}
. If we can show this, then we are done, since

\displaystyle\frac{q-1}{\log\frac{1}{\beta}}\log\frac{q-1}{(q-1)-(1-\beta)}\geq\frac{1-\beta}{\log\frac{1}{\beta}}\geq\sqrt{\beta},

where in the final step we used the inequality
\log x\leq\frac{x-1}{\sqrt{x}}
for
x\geq 1
.

Now, observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial q}f(q,\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\beta}}\log\frac{1}{1-\frac{1-\beta}{q-1}}-\frac{q-1}{\log\frac{1}{\beta}}\cdot\frac{1-\beta}{(q-1)^{2}\cdot\left({1-\frac{1-\beta}{q-1}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\beta}}\cdot\left({\log\frac{1}{1-\frac{1-\beta}{q-1}}+1-\frac{1}{1-\frac{1-\beta}{q-1}}}\right).

This is negative by applying the inequality
\log x\leq x-1
with
x=\frac{1}{1-\frac{1-\beta}{q-1}}
, and by using
0\leq\beta\leq 1
. To compute the limit, we apply L’Hôpital’s Rule to obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{q\to\infty}f(q,\beta)=\frac{1}{\log\frac{1}{\beta}}\cdot\lim_{q\to\infty}\frac{1-\beta}{(q-1)^{2}\cdot\left({1-\frac{1-\beta}{q-1}}\right)}\cdot\frac{1}{\frac{-1}{(q-1)^{2}}}=\frac{1-\beta}{\log\frac{1}{\beta}}

as desired.
∎
