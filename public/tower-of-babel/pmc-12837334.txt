Cost-effectiveness of antihistaminics in adults’ patients with allergic rhinitis
Authors: Jefferson Antonio Buendía, Diana Guerrero Patino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAllergic rhinitis (AR) is highly prevalent worldwide, often leading to substantial healthcare costs and diminished patient quality of life. Although guidelines frequently recommend intranasal corticosteroids, oral second-generation antihistamines remain commonly used in many low- and middle-income countries. Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR) constitutes a major public health concern worldwide, with prevalence estimates ranging from 5% to 50%, depending on diagnostic methods and the characteristics of the populations studied [1]. This variability is particularly evident in developing countries, where notably high rates have been observed—for instance, approximately 18% in Africa and 17.3% in Latin America [2]. Colombia exemplifies this burden by ranking fifth globally, with 25.2% of AR cases occurring among adolescents [3]. These figures underscore the significant epidemiological impact of AR and help explain the high volume of medical consultations attributed to this condition, as documented by multiple national and international surveys.

Despite current clinical practice guidelines consistently favoring intranasal corticosteroids (ICS) over antihistamines for their superior efficacy in reducing nasal obstruction [4], oral H₁ antihistamines remain widely employed in many regions due to their over-the-counter availability, lower cost, and relatively favorable side-effect profile, particularly in their second-generation forms [5]. First-generation antihistamines cross the blood–brain barrier and cause sedation; second-generation agents are more selective and minimally sedating [6] In Spain, for example, a multicenter study reported that up to 66% of allergic rhinitis (AR) prescriptions combined ICS with second-generation antihistamines, whereas 20% relied on ICS alone and 4% used other antihistamines [6]. A retrospective analysis of more than four million prescriptions in Australia likewise revealed that 75.9% of AR treatments involved oral antihistamines and only 16.6% involved ICS [7]. Meanwhile, data from South Korea (2010–2018) show a decrease in first-generation antihistamine use but a rise in second-generation combinations, underscoring a global trend toward more tolerable agents [8]; and a Colombian cohort reported 75% of patients being managed primarily with oral antihistamines [5]. These trends underscore that, despite the greater efficacy of intranasal corticosteroids—particularly for nasal obstruction—antihistamines remain the predominant option in many regions.

First-generation antihistamines (e.g., diphenhydramine, hydroxyzine) have historically been used for allergic rhinitis management [9]; however, these older compounds display poor receptor selectivity and readily cross the blood–brain barrier, leading to central nervous system (CNS) side effects—most notably sedation and impaired psychomotor function [10]. Sedation rates with first-generation agents can exceed 20–30% in certain patient populations [11], and first-generation antihistamines has been implicated in a significant proportion of transportation-related injuries (e.g., detected in 11% of fatally injured aviation pilots) [12]. In contrast, newer-generation H1 antagonists, such as cetirizine, loratadine, desloratadine, fexofenadine, and rupatadine, demonstrate substantially reduced sedation rates (often < 10%) and minimal anticholinergic effects [13]. Although second-generation antihistamines can be slightly more expensive at the pharmacy counter, evidence suggests that their improved safety profile may offset this cost difference through decreased adverse-event–related visits, reduced accident risk, and fewer productivity losses [14]. In the United States alone, annual medication expenditures for rhinitis surpass USD 1.3 billion; thus, selecting safer, better-tolerated agents can yield long-term cost-benefits for both patients and health systems [15]. In this sense, it is relevant for health insurers whether this extra benefit offered by new antihistamines is compensated by this additional cost. Answering these types of questions is also relevant in other low- and middle-income countries, or in low-resource settings in developed countries. This study aims to evaluate the cost-utility of newer-generation H1 antagonists in adult patients with allergic rhinitis in Colombia.

Materials and methods

Model structure

A cost-utility analysis was conducted to compare seven antihistamines (Loratadine 10 mg per day, Fexofenadine 120 and 180 mg per day, Cetirizine 10 mg per day, Olopatadine 5 mg per day, Desloratadine 5 mg per day, Levocetirizine 5 mg per day, Rupatadine 10 and 20 mg per day) to treatment of adults with allergic rhinitis in Colombia. The population of interest consisted of adults aged 18 years and older with allergic rhinitis treated in an outpatient setting. The time horizon was fixed at 28 days. This analysis used quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) and costs from a third-payer perspective. The time horizon was set as 28 days because the efficacy of most of the drugs in the clinical trials mentioned in the study was evaluated on day 28 [16].The authors agreed that it would be more suitable to use a shorter time horizon to capture the short-term outcomes following similar models rather than increase the uncertainty of the results by extrapolating [17]. We use an adaptation of decision tree model from Nakagawa et al. used to evaluate cost Cost-effectiveness Analysis of Second-Generation Antihistamine previously [17], see Fig. 1. This decision tree model illustrates the cost-effectiveness assessment of seven different antihistamines for allergic rhinitis in Colombia. Each medication branch (e.g., Loratadine 10 mg, Fexofenadine, etc.) begins with a chance node that splits into two primary health outcomes: “Reduction in Total Symptom Score (TSS) and nasal congestion score” and “Non- reduction in Total Symptom Score (TSS) and nasal congestion score”.

Decision tree for allergic rhinitis

From each of those outcomes, Fig. 1, the model further branches into two additional nodes that consider the presence or absence of side effects. Hence, each initial medication branch leads to four possible terminal nodes: (1) Effective + drug adverse events, (2) Effective + non-drug adverse events, (3) non-effective + drug adverse events, and (4) Non-effective + Non-drug adverse events. At these terminal nodes, both the total costs (e.g., drug acquisition, treatment of adverse events) and the QALYs (quality-adjusted life years) associated with each scenario are calculated. By comparing this cost and QALY results across all seven antihistamine branches, the model allows for a comprehensive evaluation of which medication represents the cost-effective option for managing allergic rhinitis in the Colombian context.

Model inputs

The effectiveness for each treatment were extracted from recent systematic review and network meta-analysis of 18 randomized controlled trials that evaluate the efficacy of different oral H1antihistamine treatments on patients with allergic rhinitis [16]; Table 1. Because the network meta-analysis by Hong et al. reports only mean changes in Total Symptom Score (TSS) and not responder proportions, we derived the probability of achieving clinically meaningful symptom reduction using a validated distribution-based approach. For trials where responder proportions were available (cetirizine 10 mg and levocetirizine 5 mg), n/N values were extracted directly. For all other antihistamines, responder probabilities were derived, not extracted, by assuming an approximately normal distribution of TSS change and applying a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of − 0.55, consistent with prior psychometric analyses of TSS. Detailed computation steps are provided in Supplementary Table S1. To evaluate the validity of this approach, we compared observed versus derived responder rates in the two drugs with original n/N data; the absolute difference ranged from 3 to 7% points, supporting the internal consistency of the transformation within this short-term horizon. A sensitivity analysis varying the MCID between − 0.40 and − 0.70 demonstrated minimal impact on treatment ranking.

Data input summary

The probabilities of drug adverse events were extracted from recent systematic review of 95 randomized controlled trials that evaluate different treatments on patients with allergic rhinitis [18]. The probabilities of adverse events were obtained by recalculating event rates [18]. For each antihistamine, the number of individuals experiencing somnolence was divided by the total sample size (n/N) to derive drug-specific AE probabilities. A detailed breakdown of the calculations is provided in Supplementary Table S2. All included clinical inputs correspond to adults with intermittent allergic rhinitis, consistent with ARIA definitions [19].

All health-state values refer to EQ-5D-5L utility scores were extracted from decision tree model of Nakagawa et al. [17] Table 1.

QALYs were estimated by assigning EQ-5D-5L utility weights to each of the four terminal health states in the decision tree (effective with AE, effective without AE, non-effective with AE, and non-effective without AE). Utility values were extracted from the study by Nakagawa et al., where patient-reported EQ-5D-5L utilities were mapped to health states reflecting symptom control and presence of somnolence. This 28-day interval reflects the duration used in the clinical trials informing the model. Utilities were applied only within this 28-day horizon, without extrapolation, consistent with prior short-term economic models in allergic rhinitis. A detailed mapping of utilities to health states is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Cost associated with drug adverse events and Non-effectivity of each treatment were extracted from a previous economic evaluation of treatments for allergic rhinitis made by health ministry and Institute for Health Technology Assessment in Colombia [18]. The drug acquisition ‘cost of each treatment were extracted from National Drug Price Information System [20]. We use US dollars (currency rate: US$1.00 = COP$ 4,400) to express all costs in the study [21]. All cost were updated to 2024 using national consumer price index [22]. Any treatment was considered cost-effective if the incremental cost-utility ratio was below US$ 5180 per QALY gained following recent estimations of willingness to pay for QALY in Colombia [23].

Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses

All inputs were subjected to probabilistic sensitivity analysis as detailed below as recommended by the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) Statement [24].

A one-way deterministic sensitivity analysis was conducted by varying each parameter across its 95% confidence interval, using Net Monetary Benefit (NMB) as the primary outcome. The tornado diagram (Supplementary Figure S1) shows that the model is most sensitive to three parameters: (1) probability of symptom reduction, (2) drug acquisition cost, and (3) utility of the “effective without adverse events” state. Other parameters—including adverse event probabilities and non-effectiveness costs—had considerably smaller influence on NMB. The probabilistic sensitivity analysis (10,000 Monte Carlo simulations) confirmed the robustness of the base-case findings: fexofenadine 180 mg consistently achieved the highest probability of being cost-effective across a wide range of willingness-to-pay thresholds, while cetirizine 10 mg remained competitive at lower thresholds. The model was built probabilistically to take account of the uncertainty around input parameter point estimates, Table 1. All probability distributions were parameterized to preserve the reported means and appropriate variance using standard Beta and Gamma formulations (see Supplementary Table S3 for formula derivations).

TreeAge Healthcare® was used in all analyses. Ethics approval was not required as the data used in this study came from publicly available data. A full CHEERS-compliant table summarizing all model inputs, parameter sources, and justification of distribution assumptions is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Results

In the cost-effectiveness analysis of antihistamines for allergic rhinitis in Colombia, cetirizine 10 mg and fexofenadine 180 mg emerged as the only undominated therapies, Table 2. Cetirizine 10 mg demonstrated favorable values for both total cost (22.15 USD) and effectiveness (0.0718), leading to an acceptable incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of 349.62 USD per additional unit of effectiveness, as well as a net monetary benefit (NMB) of 308.67 USD. Fexofenadine 180 mg showed a slight increase in cost (22.77 USD) and delivered a modest improvement in effectiveness (0.0725), resulting in an ICER of 820 USD and a higher NMB of 352.90 USD. All other agents—loratadine 10 mg, fexofenadine 120 mg, desloratadine 5 mg, olopatadine 5 mg, levocetirizine 5 mg, rupatadine 10 mg, and rupatadine 20 mg—were absolutely dominated, with higher costs and lower effectiveness.

Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analysis

A one-way deterministic sensitivity analysis was performed varying each model parameter across its 95% confidence interval. Net monetary benefit (NMB) was used as the primary outcome to facilitate interpretation. Results are presented in Supplementary Figure S1. The parameters exerting the greatest influence on the model were: (1) probability of symptom reduction, (2) drug acquisition cost, and (3) utility for the ‘effective without AE’ state. The tornado diagram illustrates (Supplementary Figure S1) the results of the one-way deterministic sensitivity analysis, showing how variations in individual model parameters influence the Net Monetary Benefit (NMB) of the evaluated antihistamine strategies. The length of each horizontal bar reflects the magnitude of change in NMB resulting from varying each parameter across its 95% confidence interval, while the central vertical line represents the base-case NMB. Among all evaluated parameters, the probability of response (treatment effectiveness) exerted the greatest impact on NMB, with variations ranging from substantial decreases to considerable increases in economic value. This indicates that the model’s conclusions are particularly sensitive to uncertainty in clinical effectiveness estimates derived from the network meta-analysis. The drug acquisition cost was the second most influential parameter, demonstrating that even relatively small changes in medication prices can meaningfully affect cost-effectiveness outcomes within a short 28-day horizon. Utility values for the effective without adverse events state also showed a noticeable impact on model results, underscoring the importance of health-related quality-of-life assumptions in short-term cost-utility analyses. In contrast, parameters related to adverse-event probability, cost of non-effectiveness, and cost of adverse events had comparatively smaller effects on NMB. These findings suggest that, although adverse events and treatment failure contribute to overall costs and utilities, they are not major drivers of the model’s economic conclusions for intermittent allergic rhinitis. Overall, the tornado diagram confirms that the model’s outcomes are robust to most parameter variations, with the key determinants being treatment effectiveness, drug costs, and utility values. Importantly, variations in these parameters do not alter the fundamental conclusion that cetirizine 10 mg and fexofenadine 180 mg remain the most economically favorable options.

The scatterplot, Fig. 2, illustrates the results of 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations evaluating the cost versus effectiveness (measured in QALYs) of various antihistamines for allergic rhinitis in Colombia. Medications like Cetirizine 10 mg (yellow triangles) and Fexofenadine 180 mg (gray squares) cluster in the lower-cost, higher-effectiveness region, indicating their superior cost-effectiveness and minimal variability in outcomes. Conversely, medications such as Rupatadine 20 mg (pink circles) are positioned in the high-cost, low-effectiveness region, highlighting their inefficiency. Other options, such as Olopatadine 5 mg (brown diamonds) and Levocetirizine 5 mg (green crosses), exhibit greater variability in both cost and effectiveness, suggesting uncertainty in their performance.

The cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs), Fig. 3, illustrate the probability of each antihistamine being cost-effective across a range of willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds in USD. Fexofenadine 180 mg (gray squares) dominates consistently, achieving the highest percentage of each iteration of the PSA to estimate the cost-effectiveness of each therapy across all WTP values. This highlights its robustness as the most economically efficient option. Cetirizine 10 mg (yellow crosses) is highly competitive at lower WTP thresholds, but its cost-effectiveness declines as the WTP increases. Medications such as Fexofenadine 120 mg (red squares) and Desloratadine 5 mg (cyan stars) show moderate performance, but they are clearly outperformed by Fexofenadine 180 mg at all thresholds. In contrast, Rupatadine 10 mg and Rupatadine 20 mg exhibit negligible probabilities of being cost-effective, regardless of the WTP threshold. The CEAC confirms Fexofenadine 180 mg as cost-effective choice for allergic rhinitis treatment in Colombia, maintaining its dominance across varying resource availability. While Cetirizine 10 mg is a reasonable alternative at lower WTP levels, the economic inefficiency of other options, particularly Rupatadine, is evident. These findings are consistent with previous deterministic and probabilistic analyses, reinforcing the recommendation of Fexofenadine 180 mg as the optimal choice in resource allocation for allergic rhinitis management.

Discussion

Cetirizine and fexofenadine were the only cost-effective (undominated) options, based on their lower costs and higher effectiveness relative to competing alternatives. These findings were reinforced by both deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses, wherein cetirizine 10 mg and fexofenadine 180 mg consistently exhibited favorable incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), net monetary benefits (NMB), and probabilities of being cost-effective. Compared to other antihistamines that were absolutely dominated (i.e., more expensive and less effective), these two agents demonstrated notable economic value within the assessed 28-day time horizon. Such evidence contributes to the growing body of literature highlighting the overall efficiency of newer-generation H1 antagonists, which balance clinical effectiveness and safety with pharmacoeconomic benefits.

To our knowledge, this study represents the first cost-utility analysis of antihistamines in the adults’ patients with allergic rhinitis in developing countries, providing valuable insights for healthcare decision-making. Interesting variations emerge when comparing these results with similar studies in other asthma phenotypes. In contrast with the present study in Colombia, which identified cetirizine 10 mg and fexofenadine 180 mg as the cost-effective options, the investigations by Nakagawa et al. [17]. in Japan concluded that ebastine (10 mg) offered the best economic value when also considering the traditional Kampo medicine shoseiryuto. Methodological differences largely account for these divergent findings: whereas our study employed a third-payer perspective, QALYs based on total symptom score (TSS), and a specific willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold suited to the Colombian context, Nakagawa et al. evaluated “overall improvement” (encompassing both symptoms and clinical assessment) using Japan’s Medical Fee Index (2020), different cost structures, and a substantially higher WTP threshold (about US $48,815/QALY). Additionally, our analysis incorporated a broader range of second-generation antihistamines in a short-term (28-day) model calibrated with local cost and epidemiologic data, while Nakagawa et al. focused on six SGAs plus shoseiryuto in their local Japanese population. Consequently, variations in healthcare financing, population characteristics, outcome definitions, and cost parameters between Colombia and Japan underscore the inherent context dependence of pharmacoeconomic evaluations.

In contrast with our study—which centered on comparing newer-generation oral antihistamines and identified cetirizine 10 mg and fexofenadine 180 mg as dominant options—Colombia’s 2022 therapeutic positioning report adopted a broader scope by evaluating multiple medication classes (including nasal antihistamines, corticosteroids, and leukotriene receptor antagonists) and focused on avoiding adverse events (rather than strictly measuring cost per QALY gained) [18]. This expanded set of interventions, different clinical outcomes (e.g., epistaxis, somnolence, and neuropsychiatric effects), and varied target populations (e.g., children aged 6–11 years, adolescents, and adults with intermittent or persistent rhinitis) introduce significant methodological distinctions. For example, the 2022 analysis used a six-month decision-tree model built around the probability of switching therapies due to adverse events, whereas our study employed a 28-day horizon focusing on total symptom score and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). Further, their reliance on short-term adverse-event avoidance rather than effectiveness outcomes, such as symptom relief, diverges from the QALY-based approach in our work. These differences in model structures, clinical endpoints, and included populations help explain why the Colombian report highlighted multiple cost-effective alternatives (e.g., fluticasone propionate, mometasona, cetirizine, loratadine) across numerous “evidence groups” rather than isolating a single preferred antihistamine as our narrower analysis did. In this study, a third panel of experts review and synthesize the findings of the efficacy, safety, and economic evaluations. During this panel, a bi-stage voting process was used to rank available therapeutic options for each population subgroup based on their average preference scores. The final ranking confirmed that, in the case of adults with intermittent allergic rhinitis, fexofenadine was positioned first and cetirizine second—precisely matching our own study’s conclusion. This alignment further reinforces the robustness of the pharmacoeconomic evidence supporting these two agents as the most preferred choices for adults with intermittent allergic rhinitis in Colombia.

Our probabilistic sensitivity analysis underscored the robustness of fexofenadine 180 mg and cetirizine 10 mg as cost-effective options for adults with intermittent allergic rhinitis, consistently placing them in the lower-cost and higher-effectiveness quadrant across a wide spectrum of willingness-to-pay thresholds. Fexofenadine 180 mg maintained the highest probability of cost-effectiveness at virtually all threshold values, while cetirizine 10 mg emerged as a strong contender at lower thresholds. These findings not only validate our deterministic results but also suggest that the conclusions remain stable under parameter uncertainty—thereby enhancing the generalizability of our recommendations. Specifically, the application of these results to other settings with similar healthcare structures or resource constraints appears feasible, as the probabilistic models account for variations in costs and outcomes that often characterize different regions’ health systems.

A key feature of our findings is their alignment with current clinical practice trends, where newer-generation H1 antagonists are favored for their reduced sedation and better safety profiles. Although several clinical guidelines advocate for intranasal corticosteroids (ICS) as first-line therapy, our results suggest that, in contexts where ICS may not be readily available or accepted, cetirizine 10 mg and fexofenadine 180 mg offer a pragmatic, cost-effective alternative. These findings add nuance to existing knowledge by quantifying cost-effectiveness within a defined time span (28 days) and using local Colombian cost data, thus filling an evidence gap where clinical guideline recommendations do not always incorporate economic analyses.

Nonetheless, some limitations should be noted. First, the 28-day time horizon may not capture long-term variations in patient adherence or shifts in symptom severity that can occur with chronic allergic rhinitis. Second, although the decision tree model draws from recent and robust systematic reviews, any network meta-analysis is subject to potential heterogeneity in study populations, outcome measures, and dosing regimens. Moreover, our perspective—focused on the third-party payer—might exclude out-of-pocket expenditures and indirect costs (such as lost productivity), potentially underestimating the full economic impact on patients and society. These constraints suggest that while our conclusions are robust within the given model parameters, additional research incorporating diverse perspectives and longer follow-up periods could further refine cost-effectiveness estimates. A key limitation is the use of utility values derived from a small Japanese sample (n = 34), which may restrict generalizability to Colombian patients. Additionally, the disutility associated with somnolence may overestimate its real-world impact, as the underlying study assumed persistent daily impairment throughout the 28-day period. Switching of therapy after early AE onset—common in routine practice—was not modeled due to the short 28-day horizon used in clinical trials and prior economic evaluations on which our structure is based. Future evaluations with longitudinal data should incorporate dynamic treatment switching and culturally relevant utility weights. Additional limitations include: (1) use of Japanese utility weights, which may not reflect Colombian preferences; (2) transformation of continuous outcomes (TSS) into binary responses, which may reduce precision; and (3) independence assumptions between clinical effectiveness and adverse events, which may not hold if individuals with stronger symptom relief are more susceptible to somnolence. An additional limitation is that probabilities of clinical response for most antihistamines were derived indirectly from continuous TSS change scores, relying on assumptions of approximate normality, bounded scale behavior, and a fixed MCID. Although validation against observed responder rates showed reasonable concordance, this method has not been previously applied in allergic rhinitis economic models.

In terms of generalizability, Colombia has a middle-income healthcare context, where budget constraints and access to medications can vary regionally [25–38]. The methodology, however, is adaptable to other low- and middle-income settings, provided locally relevant cost data and willingness-to-pay thresholds are applied [39–50]. Moreover, although our model reflects Colombian pricing structures and clinical practice patterns, the pharmacoeconomic advantages of newer-generation antihistamines may hold true in similar health systems, especially where these drugs are readily available over the counter.

Finally, these findings carry ethical and policy implications: ensuring equitable access to the cost-effective treatments could reduce the burden of allergic rhinitis in resource-constrained settings. By prioritizing cost-effective antihistamines like cetirizine 10 mg and fexofenadine 180 mg, health systems can direct resources toward other pressing healthcare needs, thereby promoting more sustainable and equitable care. Future policy discussions and practice guidelines may benefit from integrating such economic evaluations, ensuring that clinical decisions reflect both therapeutic efficacy and responsible allocation of healthcare resources.

In this cost-utility analysis of oral H1 antihistamines for adults with intermittent allergic rhinitis in Colombia, both cetirizine 10 mg and fexofenadine 180 mg emerged as the cost-effective options, offering high net monetary benefits and remaining undominated in deterministic and probabilistic analyses. Notably, fexofenadine 180 mg consistently demonstrated the highest probability of cost-effectiveness across willingness-to-pay thresholds, reinforcing its value as a preferred therapy. The study’s findings provide robust evidence for decision-makers seeking to optimize resource allocation in clinical practice, as these two agents present an effective balance of clinical outcomes, safety, and economic benefits.

Implications for future research

Further work should explore longer time horizons, broader patient populations (e.g., pediatric or geriatric), and real-world adherence data to capture the full spectrum of chronic disease management. Future studies might also incorporate societal perspectives, accounting for indirect costs such as loss of productivity and patient-centered measures of symptom burden. Additionally, head-to-head trials or expanded modeling that compares these antihistamines alongside intranasal corticosteroids can offer more comprehensive insights into the optimal treatment pathways for allergic rhinitis. Such research would further support evidence-based policymaking and refine clinical guidelines on antihistamine use in diverse healthcare settings.
