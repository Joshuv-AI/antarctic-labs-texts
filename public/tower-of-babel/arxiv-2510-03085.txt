Studying QED 3 \textrm{QED}_{3} with radial quantization on the lattice – I. Free limit Preprint: FERMILAB-PUB-25-0712-T
Peter A. Boyle, Richard C. Brower, George T. Fleming, Emanuel Katz, Nobuyuki Matsumoto, Rohan Misra

Abstract
To investigate the three-dimensional quantum electrodynamics in the radial quantization on the lattice, the lattice action is constructed and the free limit is studied on S 2 × ℝ S^{2}\times\mathbb{R} . With the overlap fermion, it is numerically verified that the important symmetries of the theory can be realized on the lattice. The analytic correlators are derived and compared to the lattice results, which agree including the overall normalization. The O ⁡ ( a 2 ) O(a^{2}) -scaling is confirmed toward the analytic value in the continuum limit, and the number of reproduced descendant states is estimated heuristically for the first few refinement levels. Our study helps us identify the features of the theory that we can study on the icosahedral lattice without fine-tuning.



I
Introduction

Conformal field theory (CFT) and lattice field theory have been successful in deepening the non-perturbative understanding of quantum field theory.
Indeed, many analytic results are derived in the 2D CFT
Belavin:1984vu
, while the lattice study today gives predictions of quantum chromodynamics (QCD) with competitive precision to experiments
Gross:2022hyw
.
To further improve the theoretical and phenomenological understanding of non-perturbative physics, the Quantum Finite Element project
Brower:2016moq
aims to study CFTs in higher dimensions from the lattice.
To cure the notorious finite-volume effects, we employ the radial quantization
Fubini:1972mf
;
Cardy:1984epx
;
Cardy:1985lth
, where the only wrap-around effect is in the radial (or temporal) direction that is exponentially suppressed and does not change the eigenstates of the dilation operator.
The difficulty in higher dimensions is then formulating the lattice theory on a curved manifold.
The former applications of the method include the 3D Ising model
Brower:2012vg
and the
\phi^{4}
theory
Brower:2014daa
;
Brower:2020jqj
.

This paper takes a step toward studying the three-dimensional quantum electrodynamics (
\textrm{QED}_{3}
) in this framework as a prototype of conformal gauge theories, which have phenomenological applications in beyond-Standard-Model physics (see, e.g.,
LatticeStrongDynamics:2023bqp
;
Hasenfratz:2022qan
;
Butt:2024kxi
for recent studies).

{\rm QED}_{3}
has collected interests from various directions for its rich features depending on the number of fermion flavors
N_{f}
(counted in two-component spinors).
In fact, the theory has parity anomaly for odd
N_{f}

Deser:1981wh
;
Redlich:1983kn
;
Redlich:1983dv
;
Appelquist:1986qw
, while it holds a conformal window for even
N_{f}
.
Recently, the conformal bootstrap was applied to the
N_{f}=4
theory assuming the conformality
Albayrak:2021xtd
(see the reference therein for a summary of the window predictions).
It is further known to have an application to superconductivity
DoreyMavromatos1990
;
DoreyMavromatos1992
;
Aitchison:1992ik
;
Aitchison:1994un
;
Farakos:1997qi
,
and lattice calculations have been performed to study chiral symmetry breaking
Dagotto:1988id
;
Dagotto:1989td
;
Alexandre:2001pa
;
Lee:2002id
;
Hands:2002dv
;
Hands:2004bh
, phase diagram
Azcoiti:1993ey
;
Fiore:2005ps
;
Strouthos:2007stc
,
\beta
-function
Raviv:2014xna
, and conformal properties
Karthik:2015sgq
;
Karthik:2016ppr
;
Karthik:2019mrr
;
Karthik:2020shl
, all of which are closely related, where the interesting physics lies in the zero fermion mass limit.
The radial picture helps us scrutinize these subjects further because the curvature of spacetime induces a mass in the propagators of the massless fields.
These two features—exponentially suppressed wrap-around effects and a massive fermion propagator—make the radial quantization for CFTs highly desirable.

We in this paper thus aim to establish the free limit.
We derive the coupling constants for the Wilson fermion and the Gaussian gauge action by extending the formulas for simplicial lattices developed in random lattice theory
Christ:1982ck
;
Christ:1982ci
;
10.1143/PTPS.86.322
with the radial direction.
The overlap fermion can then be written straightforwardly with the constructed Wilson fermion
Karthik:2016ppr
, which preserves important symmetries of the theory as a result of the Ginsparg-Wilson relation.

To confirm that our lattice action gives the correct continuum limit, the lattice correlators are compared to the analytic continuum formulas.
The lattice correlators can be evaluated without Monte Carlo simulations thanks to the bilinear form of the action.
As for the gauge sector, in particular, we calculate lattice correlators without gauge fixing by using the standard conjugate gradient method, but with a projection that removes the zero modes associated with the gauge symmetry.
We show that the lattice results agree with the analytic formulas including the overall normalization, which is further supported by confirming the
O(a^{2})
-scaling of the lowest operator dimension in the correlators.
Our derived analytic formula further allows us to heuristically estimate the number of descendant states that are reproduced for a given refinement level.
The result suggests that the first few descendants can be reproduced on the icosahedral lattice without refinement, which affirms the expectation that a qualitative result is obtainable with the coarse triangulation of the sphere
Lao:2023zis
.
The icosahedral lattice can thus serve as an adequate first step to simulate the interacting theory on
S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
by circumventing the complications of non-perturbative fine-tuning for anisotropic lattices.

We describe the discretization of
S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}

The numerical results are given in Sec.

II
QED3 in radial quantization

We consider even
N_{f}
flavors of two-component Dirac fermions
\psi_{f}
(
f=1,\cdots N_{f}
) on
S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
.
The flavor-symmetric continuum action on
{S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}}
is

\displaystyle S_{\rm cont}

\displaystyle\equiv\int dV\Big[\frac{1}{4g^{2}}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+\sum_{f=1}^{N_{f}}\bar{\psi}_{f}\sigma^{a}e_{a}^{\mu}({\nabla}^{S}_{\mu}+iA_{\mu})\psi_{f}\Big]

(II.1)

\displaystyle\equiv S_{g}+S_{N_{f}},

(II.2)

where
dV\equiv\sqrt{g}\,d^{3}x
is the volume element,
F_{\mu\nu}\equiv\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}
is the field strength of the
U(1)
gauge field
A_{\mu}
,
\sigma^{a}
are the Pauli matrices, and
\nabla_{\mu}^{S}\equiv\partial_{\mu}+\omega_{\mu}^{S}
is the covariant derivative for the spinor fields (see Appendix
A
for conventions).
We use
(x^{\mu})=(\theta,\phi,t)
for the coordinates on
S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
and
(y^{\alpha})=(\theta,\phi)
on
S^{2}
.
The metric on
S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
is

\displaystyle ds^{2}\equiv g_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}\equiv dt^{2}+d\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta d\phi^{2},

(II.3)

where the radius is set to unity.
It is convenient to take the vierbein as

\displaystyle e_{\mu}^{1}\equiv\partial_{\mu}\theta,\quad e_{\mu}^{2}\equiv\sin\theta\,\partial_{\mu}\phi,\quad e_{\mu}^{3}\equiv\partial_{\mu}t.

(II.4)

The fermion action can be rewritten with four-component spinors.
For
f=1,\cdots,N_{f}/2
, we define

\displaystyle\Psi_{f}\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\psi_{f}\\
\psi_{f+N_{f}/2}\end{array}\right),\quad\bar{\Psi}_{f}\equiv(\bar{\psi}_{f},-\bar{\psi}_{f+N_{f}/2}).

(Recall that
\Psi_{f}
and
\bar{\Psi}_{f}
are independent in the path integral.)
We then have

\displaystyle S_{N_{f}}=\sum_{f=1}^{N_{f}/2}\bar{\Psi}_{f}\gamma^{a}e_{a}^{\mu}(\nabla^{S^{\prime}}_{\mu}+iA_{\mu})\Psi_{f},

(II.7)

where
S^{\prime}
stands for the four-component spinor representation of the local Lorentz group.
We use the diagonal basis for the gamma matrices:

\displaystyle\gamma_{a}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sigma_{a}&\\
&-\sigma_{a}\end{array}\right)\quad(a=1,2,3)

with

\displaystyle\gamma_{4}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}&\mathbb{1}_{2}\\
\mathbb{1}_{2}&\end{array}\right),\quad\gamma_{5}\equiv-\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\gamma_{3}\gamma_{4}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}&-i\mathbb{1}_{2}\\
i\mathbb{1}_{2}&\end{array}\right).

We can define chiral transformations with the hermitian generators
\gamma_{4}
,
\gamma_{5}
, and
\gamma_{4,5}\equiv i\gamma_{4}\gamma_{5}
, which can be seen as the isospin Pauli matrices
\tau_{i}
:

\displaystyle\gamma_{4}=\mathbb{1}_{2}\otimes\tau_{1},\quad\gamma_{5}=\mathbb{1}_{2}\otimes\tau_{2},\quad\gamma_{4,5}=\mathbb{1}_{2}\otimes\tau_{3}.

(II.14)

SU(N_{f})
flavor symmetry in the two-component language is translated in the four-component language as the combination of the
SU(N_{f}/2)\times SU(N_{f}/2)
flavor symmetry that rotates the flavors independently for each two-component block and the
SU(2)
chiral symmetries that mix the two-component blocks
Pisarski:1984dj
.

Other important symmetries are the parity and time-reversal symmetries:

\displaystyle P:x=(\theta,\phi,t)\to x_{P}\equiv(\pi-\theta,\phi+\pi,t),

(II.15)

\displaystyle T:x=(\theta,\phi,t)\to x_{T}\equiv(\theta,\phi,-t).

(II.16)

For a vector field
A^{\mu}(x)
,

\displaystyle P:A^{\mu}(x)\to(-1)^{\delta^{\mu}_{\theta}}A^{\mu}(x_{P}),

(II.17)

\displaystyle T:A^{\mu}(x)\to(-1)^{{\delta^{\mu}_{\theta}}}A^{\mu}(x_{T}),

(II.18)

and for a local Lorentz vector field
e^{a}(x)
,

\displaystyle P:e^{a}(x)\to(-1)^{\delta^{a}_{1}}e^{a}(x_{P}),

(II.19)

\displaystyle T:e^{a}(x)\to(-1)^{\delta^{a}_{3}}e^{a}(x_{T}).

(II.20)

As for the spinor fields, in the two-component formalism, we define the parity as
Appelquist:1986fd

\displaystyle P:\psi(x)\to\sigma_{1}\psi(x_{P}),\quad\bar{\psi}(x)\to-\bar{\psi}(x_{P})\sigma_{1},

(II.21)

and the time-reversal as

\displaystyle T:\psi(x)\to\sigma_{3}\psi(x_{T}),\quad\bar{\psi}(x)\to-\bar{\psi}(x_{T})\sigma_{3}.

(II.22)

It is easy to see that the action (
II.1
) is symmetric under
P
and
T
.
The transformation laws for
\bar{\psi}
are formally the same as for
\psi^{\dagger}\sigma_{2}
.

In the four-component formalism, we replace the transformation law for the fermion under parity with

\displaystyle P^{\prime}:\Psi(x)\to\gamma_{5}\gamma_{1}\Psi(x_{P})=i(\sigma_{1}\otimes\tau_{1})\Psi(x_{P}),\quad\bar{\Psi}(x)\to\bar{\Psi}(x_{P})\gamma_{1}\gamma_{5}=-i\bar{\Psi}(x_{P})(\sigma_{1}\otimes\tau_{1}),

(II.23)

and under time-reversal with

\displaystyle T^{\prime}:\Psi(x)\to\gamma_{5}\gamma_{3}\Psi(x_{T})=i(\sigma_{3}\otimes\tau_{1})\Psi(x_{T}),\quad\bar{\Psi}(x)\to\bar{\Psi}(x_{T})\gamma_{3}\gamma_{5}=-i\bar{\Psi}(x_{T})(\sigma_{3}\otimes\tau_{1}).

(II.24)

We can see from Eq. (
II.7
) that
P^{\prime}
and
T^{\prime}
are symmetries of the action.
In fact,
P^{\prime}
can be seen as
P
and a chiral rotation in succession:

\displaystyle i(\sigma_{1}\otimes\tau_{1})\Psi(x_{P})

\displaystyle=(i\mathbb{1}_{2}\otimes\tau_{1})(\sigma_{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{2})\Psi_{f}(x_{P}),

(II.25)

\displaystyle-i\bar{\Psi}(x_{P})(\sigma_{1}\otimes\tau_{1})

\displaystyle=\bar{\Psi}_{f}(x_{P})(-\sigma_{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}_{2})(i\mathbb{1}_{2}\otimes\tau_{1}).

(II.26)

Therefore, when the chiral symmetry is broken on the lattice,
P
and
P^{\prime}
do not hold simultaneously.
The same argument holds for
T
and
T^{\prime}
.

III
Lattice on
S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}

In this section, we tessellate the sphere with a refined icosahedron
Brower:2014daa
;
Brower:2016vsl
and introduce geometric quantities that are used in the later sections.
For each of the twenty triangles that make up the icosahedron, we divide the edges into
L
segments of equal length.
By drawing lines parallel to the edges, we obtain a triangular lattice on each of the twenty original faces.
The piecewise-flat, simplicial lattice is then projected onto
S^{2}
, which results in the curved lattice with
N_{V}=10L^{2}+2
sites,
N_{E}=30L^{2}
links, and
N_{F}=20L^{2}
faces on the sphere (see Fig.
1
).

Figure 1:
The tessellated
S^{2}
with the refinement level
L=2

By construction, the isometry of the lattice is the full icosahedral group
I_{h}
, which includes parity (
II.15
).

We write the geodesic that runs from
y_{1}
to
y_{2}
on
S^{2}
as
\gamma_{y_{1}y_{2}}
.
With the affine parameter
s
on
\gamma_{y_{1}y_{2}}
, which increases from
y_{1}
to
y_{2}
, we can write the directional vector of unit length as
e^{\alpha}_{y_{1}y_{2}}\equiv dy^{\alpha}/ds
.
The lattice spin connection matrix, which is the Wilson line for the local Lorentz group, is given by

\displaystyle\Omega_{y_{1}y_{2}}=\cos\frac{\omega_{y_{1}y_{2}}}{2}+i\sigma_{3}\sin\frac{\omega_{y_{1}y_{2}}}{2},

(III.1)

where

\displaystyle\omega_{y_{1}y_{2}}\equiv\int_{\gamma_{y_{1}y_{2}}}ds\,e^{\alpha}_{y_{1}y_{2}}\omega_{\alpha}^{12}=-\int_{\gamma_{y_{1}y_{2}}}ds\,\frac{d\phi(s)}{ds}\cos\theta(s)

(III.2)

with the spin connection
\omega_{\alpha}^{ab}
calculated in the
(\theta,\phi)
coordinates.
We can see from Eq. (
III.2
) that the angular variable
\omega_{y_{1}y_{2}}
changes sign under parity (
II.15
):

\displaystyle\omega_{y_{1}^{P}y_{2}^{P}}=-\omega_{y_{1}y_{2}}.

(III.3)

We write the geodesic length as
\ell_{y_{1}y_{2}}
and the average of
\ell_{y_{1}y_{2}}
as
\bar{a}_{s}
, which defines the spatial cutoff scale.
The dual point of a triangular plaquette is defined by projecting the circumcenter of the triangle onto
S^{2}
.
We write the area factor for a site
y
as
A_{y}
, which is the area of the dual plaquette that encircles
y
.
Similarly, the area factor
A_{y_{1}y_{2}}
for a link
y_{1}y_{2}
is the sum of the two areas of the spherical triangles that are made with the link
y_{1}y_{2}
and the nearby dual sites (see Fig.

The area factors for (Left) the site

, and (Right) the link
y_{1}y_{2}

.

We find that the cutoff effects are generically smaller with the spherical formulas than the flat formulas for the simplicial lattice.

The temporal direction can be added by simply copying the tessellated sphere
L_{t}
times and
introducing the temporal lattice spacing
a_{t}
.
We write as
T\equiv a_{t}L_{t}
the physical length in the temporal direction.
The quantities that are quadratic in
\bar{a}_{s}
and
a_{t}
are written as
O(a^{2})
for brevity.
Note that, in the radial picture, the physical roles of the two lattice spacings
\bar{a}_{s}
and

From the operator-state correspondence, the spatial lattice spacing
\bar{a}_{s}
defines the cutoff scale for the state profile that is realized on the sphere, while the temporal lattice spacing

can be seen as a resolution scale for the propagation of the states in the radial direction.
Note also that the dimensionful quantities are always measured with respect to the sphere radius, which we have set to unity.

y_{i}y_{i+1}

IV.1
Wilson fermion

The one-flavor massless Wilson fermion
S_{W}\equiv\bar{\psi}D_{W}\psi
can be written as a radial extension from the
S^{2}
case
Brower:2016vsl
:

\displaystyle S_{W}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{t}\sum_{y_{1},y_{2}}\kappa_{y_{1}y_{2}}\bar{\psi}_{y_{1},t}\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\big(1-e_{y_{1}y_{2}}^{a}(y_{1})\sigma_{a}\big)U_{y_{1},t;y_{2},t}\Omega_{y_{1}y_{2}}\psi_{y_{2},t}+\frac{1}{2}\psi_{y_{1},t}\Big]

\displaystyle+\sum_{t}\sum_{y}\kappa^{\prime}_{y}\bar{\psi}_{y,t}\Big[-\frac{1}{2}(1-\sigma_{3})U_{y,t;y,t+1}\psi_{y,t+1}-\frac{1}{2}(1+\sigma_{3})U_{y,t;y,t-1}\psi_{y,t-1}+\psi_{y,t}\Big],

(IV.1)

where
\kappa_{y_{1}y_{2}}
are the spatial hopping parameters that are nonzero only if
(y_{1},y_{2})
are nearest neighbors,
\kappa^{\prime}_{y}
the temporal hopping parameters, and
e_{y_{1}y_{2}}^{a}\equiv e_{y_{1}y_{2}}^{\mu}e_{\mu}^{a}
.
In the free limit, the couplings can be chosen as

\displaystyle\kappa_{y_{1}y_{2}}=\frac{2A_{y_{1}y_{2}}}{\bar{a}_{s}\ell_{y_{1}y_{2}}},\quad{\kappa}^{\prime}_{y}=\frac{A_{y}}{\bar{a}_{s}a_{t}}.

(IV.2)

The spatial coupling is the same as the simplicial lattice formula in Ref.
Christ:1982ci
.
The temporal coupling is chosen such that we have the continuum limit with the correct spinor structure in each fundamental volume.

To see that the above choice gives the correct continuum limit, we observe that the lattice action can be rearranged into a sum over prisms
(\triangle,t)
:

\displaystyle S_{W}=\sum_{(\triangle,t)}\bar{\psi}(C^{(\triangle,t)}+B^{(\triangle,t)})\psi,

(IV.3)

where
\bar{\psi}C^{(\triangle,t)}\psi
is the naive fermion term:

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}C^{(\triangle,t)}\psi

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{i=1,2,3}\frac{\ell^{*}_{i,i+1}}{2\bar{a}_{s}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{\psi}_{i,t}\,\Omega_{i,i+1/2}\,e_{i,i+1}^{a}(y_{i+1/2})\sigma_{a}\,\Omega_{i+1/2,i+1}\,\psi_{i+1,t}\\
+\bar{\psi}_{i+1,t}\,\Omega_{i+1,i+1/2}\,e_{i+1,i}^{a}(y_{i+1/2})\sigma_{a}\,\Omega_{i+1/2,i}\,\psi_{i,t}\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1,2,3}\frac{\tilde{A}_{i}}{4\bar{a}_{s}a_{t}}\Big\{\bar{\psi}_{i,t}\sigma_{3}(\psi_{i,t+1}-\psi_{i,t-1})-(\bar{\psi}_{i,t+1}-\bar{\psi}_{i,t-1})\sigma_{3}\psi_{i,t}\Big\},

(IV.5)

and the remainder
\bar{\psi}B^{(\triangle,t)}\psi
is the Wilson term.
We abbreviate
\psi(y_{i},t)\equiv\psi_{i,t}
for simplicity, and
y_{i+1/2}
is the intersection of the simplicial and dual links, which is the midpoint of
y_{i}y_{i+1}
up to the
O(\bar{a}_{s}^{2})
curvature effect.
We evaluate the action for a smooth field
\psi(x)
that can be expanded around the center of the prism.
For each edge, we apply

\displaystyle\psi_{i+1,t}=\Big(1+\frac{1}{2}\ell_{i,i+1}e_{i,i+1}^{\mu}(y_{i+1/2})\,\partial_{\mu}\Big)\psi(y_{i+1/2},t)+O(\bar{a}_{s}^{2}).

(IV.6)

See Ref.
Brower:2016vsl
for a further argument using a linear finite element.
For the expanded coefficients, the simplicial formula
Christ:1982ck
;
Christ:1982ci
;
10.1143/PTPS.86.322
can be applied:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1,2,3}\ell_{i,i+1}\ell_{i,i+1}^{*}e^{a}_{i,i+1}e^{b}_{i,i+1}=A_{\triangle}\delta^{ab}\cdot\big(1+O(\bar{a}_{s}^{2})\big),

(IV.7)

where the relative
O(\bar{a}_{s}^{2})
error is the curvature effect.
We thus obtain

\displaystyle C^{(\triangle,t)}=\frac{1}{\bar{a}_{s}a_{t}}\cdot A_{\triangle}a_{t}\cdot\sigma^{a}e_{a}^{\mu}\overleftrightarrow{\nabla}_{\mu}^{S}\cdot\big(1+O(a^{2})\big),

(IV.8)

where
\bar{\psi}\overleftrightarrow{\nabla}_{\mu}^{S}\psi\equiv(1/2)[\bar{\psi}\nabla_{\mu}^{S}\psi-\bar{\psi}\overleftarrow{\nabla}_{\mu}^{S}\psi]

and

\bar{\psi}\overleftarrow{\nabla}_{\mu}^{S}\equiv\partial_{\mu}\bar{\psi}-\bar{\psi}\omega^{S}_{\mu}
.
The Wilson term is
O(a^{2})
and has a zero at the physical pole and is nonzero at the doubler poles.
For the regularly shaped lattices of our interest,
B^{(\triangle,t)}
is bounded from below and removes the doublers sufficiently.

IV.2

Following Ref.

;

), we define the one-flavor massless overlap fermion
S_{\rm ov}\equiv\bar{\psi}D_{\rm ov}\psi
with

(IV.10)

where
X\equiv D_{W}-M
.

\displaystyle 0<M<\alpha M_{0},\quad M_{0}\equiv{\rm min}\Big(\frac{4}{\sqrt{3}},\frac{\sqrt{3}{\bar{a}_{s}}}{a_{t}}\Big)

(IV.11)

with the safety factor, say,
\alpha=0.9
, to take into account the slightly skewed shape of the triangles.
Under the condition
\bar{a}_{s}/a_{t}\geq 4/3
, this means we can use the conventional range
0<M<2
.

\displaystyle(\bar{a}_{s}a_{t})\big[D_{\rm lat}\big]_{xx^{\prime}}\Leftrightarrow\sqrt{g(x)}\,d^{3}x\,\cdot\big[D(x)\delta^{3}(x-x^{\prime})d^{3}x^{\prime}\big].

IV.3
Global symmetries for the lattice fermions

In this section, following Refs.
Karthik:2015sgq
;
Karthik:2016ppr
, we define the multi-flavor lattice action and summarize its global symmetries.
Though we mainly focus on parity, the argument holds in parallel for time-reversal symmetry.

We recall from Sec.
II
that the continuum Dirac operator
D(x;A)\equiv\sigma^{a}e_{a}^{\mu}({\nabla}^{S}_{\mu}+iA_{\mu})
satisfies

\displaystyle-\sigma_{1}D(x_{P};A_{P})\sigma_{1}=D(x;A),

(IV.14)

where
A_{P}^{\mu}
is the parity-transformed field (
II.17
), making
P
a symmetry of the action.
For simplicity, the dependence on the gauge field will be suppressed below.
The counterpart of Eq. (
IV.14
) for the Wilson-Dirac operator
D_{W}
is
Karthik:2015sgq
;
Karthik:2016ppr

\displaystyle-\sigma_{1}D_{W}|_{x\to x_{P}}\sigma_{1}=-D^{\dagger}_{W},

(IV.15)

where we use

\displaystyle e^{a}_{y_{1}^{P}y_{2}^{P}}(y_{1}^{P})=(-1)^{\delta^{a}_{1}}e_{y_{1}y_{2}}^{a}(y_{1})

(IV.16)

and [see Eq. (
III.3
)]

\displaystyle\Omega_{y_{1}^{P}y_{2}^{P}}=\Omega^{\dagger}_{y_{1}y_{2}}.

(IV.17)

The appearance of
-D^{\dagger}_{W}
rather than
D_{W}
on the right-hand side of Eq. (
IV.15
) signifies the breaking of
P
for the Wilson fermion.

On the other hand, the breaking is minimal for the overlap fermion.
To see this, we first observe that the unitarity of
X/\sqrt{X^{\dagger}X}
implies that

\displaystyle D_{\rm ov}^{-1}=\mathbb{1}-(D_{\rm ov}^{\dagger})^{-1},

(IV.18)

where
\mathbb{1}
is the Kronecker delta in the coordinate space and its appearance is a characteristic of the Ginsparg-Wilson fermion (see, e.g., Ref.
Kikukawa:1998pd
).
We therefore have for the overlap operator

\displaystyle-\sigma_{1}(D_{\rm ov}|_{x\to x_{P}})^{-1}\sigma_{1}=-(D^{\dagger}_{\rm ov})^{-1}=D_{\rm ov}^{-1}-\mathbb{1},

(IV.19)

which shows that the parity symmetry holds for the overlap propagator up to the contact term.

We now include the flavor, and define the sign-definite multi-flavor action as
Karthik:2015sgq
;
Karthik:2016ppr

\displaystyle S_{N_{f},{\rm lat}}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{f=1}^{N_{f}/2}\Big[\bar{\psi}_{f}D_{\rm lat}\psi_{f}-\bar{\psi}_{f+N_{f}/2}D_{\rm lat}^{\dagger}\psi_{f+N_{f}/2}\Big]

(IV.20)

\displaystyle=\sum_{f=1}^{N_{f}/2}\bar{\Psi}_{f}{\cal D}_{\rm lat}\Psi_{f},

(IV.21)

where we have introduced the four-component lattice Dirac operator:

\displaystyle{\cal D}_{\rm lat}\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}D_{\rm lat}&\\
&D^{\dagger}_{\rm lat}\end{array}\right).

One can confirm that
P^{\prime}
and
T^{\prime}
symmetries in the four-component formalism, Eqs. (
II.23
) and (
II.24
), are preserved exactly on the lattice
Karthik:2015sgq
;
Karthik:2016ppr
.

As described in Eqs. (
II.25
) and (
II.26
), an
SU(2)
chiral rotation relates the spacetime symmetries in the two- and four-component formalisms.
Accordingly, the breaking of the two-component parity
P
for the Wilson fermion can be understood from the four-component perspective in terms of chiral symmetry breaking.
In fact, since
D_{W}^{\dagger}\neq-D_{W}
, a subgroup of the
SU(2)
chiral rotations generated by
\gamma_{4}=\mathbb{1}_{2}\otimes\tau_{1}
and
\gamma_{5}=\mathbb{1}_{2}\otimes\tau_{2}
is broken for the four-component Wilson-Dirac operator
{\cal D}_{W}
.
(The
\gamma_{4,5}=\mathbb{1}_{2}\otimes\tau_{3}
rotation is a phase rotation with different signs for the two-component blocks, and the corresponding symmetry is preserved trivially for the diagonal operator (
IV.3
).)
As a result, the
SU(N_{f})
flavor symmetry is broken to
SU(N_{f}/2)\times SU(N_{f}/2)
for the Wilson fermion
Karthik:2015sgq
;
Karthik:2016ppr
.

On the other hand, as already mentioned above, the preservation of the spacetime symmetries for the bulk part of the overlap propagator is a property of the Ginsparg-Wilson fermion.
Indeed, the four-component overlap operator
{\cal D}_{\rm ov}
satisfies the Ginsparg-Wilson relation
Ginsparg:1981bj
(see Ref.
Bietenholz:2000ca
for the study in odd dimensions):

\displaystyle\Gamma{\cal D}_{\rm ov}+{\cal D}_{\rm ov}\Gamma={\cal D}_{\rm ov}\Gamma{\cal D}_{\rm ov}

(IV.24)

for
\Gamma=\gamma_{4},\gamma_{5}
.
As in four dimensions
Luscher:1998pqa
, we can define the lattice chiral transformation:

\displaystyle\Psi\to e^{i\alpha\hat{\Gamma}}\Psi,\quad\bar{\Psi}\to\bar{\Psi}e^{i\alpha\Gamma},

(IV.25)

where
\hat{\Gamma}\equiv\Gamma(1-{\cal D}_{\rm ov})
.
Together with the
SU(N_{f}/2)\times SU(N_{f}/2)
flavor rotations and the
\gamma_{4,5}
rotation, they generate the exact lattice
SU(N_{f})
symmetry for the overlap fermion (see Ref.
Karthik:2016ppr
for a further proof in the operator formalism).

IV.4
Gauge action

A generic gauge-invariant Gaussian action
for the non-compact
U(1)
field on the lattice is

\displaystyle S_{g,{\rm lat}}\equiv\sum_{\triangle,t}\frac{\beta_{\triangle}}{2}\Big(\sum_{m\in p(\triangle,t)}\eta_{m}\theta_{m}\Big)^{2}+\sum_{l,t}\frac{\beta_{l}}{2}\Big(\sum_{m\in p(l,t)}\eta_{m}\theta_{m}\Big)^{2}.

(IV.26)

p(\triangle,t)
is the spacelike plaquette with the triangle
\triangle
on the timeslice
t
, while
p(l,t)
is the temporal plaquette with the link
l
on the timeslice
t
.
The angular link variable
\theta_{m}
is defined in terms of the gauge field
A_{\mu}
as

\displaystyle\theta_{m}\equiv\int_{\gamma_{m}}dx^{\mu}A_{\mu},

(IV.27)

where the integral is over the geodesic
\gamma_{m}
that forms the link
m
.
The sign factor
\eta_{m}=\pm 1
in Eq. (
IV.26
) is the relative orientation of
\gamma_{m}
in the plaquette.
In the free limit, the coupling constants can be chosen as

\displaystyle\beta_{\triangle}=\frac{1}{g^{2}}\frac{a_{t}}{A_{\triangle}},\quad\beta_{l}=\frac{1}{g^{2}}\frac{2A_{l}}{\ell_{l}^{2}a_{t}}.

(IV.28)

Again, the spatial coupling is the same as the simplicial lattice formula in Ref.
Christ:1982ci
,
and the temporal coupling
\beta_{l}
is given such that it reproduces the correct tensor contractions of
F_{\mu\nu}
in the continuum limit.

To confirm the tensorial structure, note that the integral of the one-form
A_{\mu}dx^{\mu}
around a plaquette
p
is

\displaystyle\oint_{\partial p}A_{\mu}dx^{\mu}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{p}dV\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu},

(IV.29)

where
\epsilon^{\mu\nu}
is the invariant volume tensor on the two-dimensional surface
p
.
For a smooth gauge field
A_{\mu}
such that the field strength
F_{\mu\nu}
is almost constant in
p
, Eq. (
IV.29
) can be approximated as

\displaystyle\oint_{\partial p}A_{\mu}dx^{\mu}\simeq\frac{1}{2}A_{p}\cdot\epsilon^{\mu\nu}\,F_{\mu\nu},

(IV.30)

where
A_{p}
is the area of
p
.
For a spatial plaquette
p=p(\triangle,t)
,

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\alpha\beta}\epsilon^{\gamma\delta}=h^{\alpha\gamma}h^{\beta\delta}-h^{\alpha\delta}h^{\beta\gamma},

(IV.31)

which leads to

\displaystyle\Big(\oint_{\partial p(\triangle,t)}A_{\mu}dx^{\mu}\Big)^{2}\simeq\frac{A_{\triangle}^{2}}{2}\cdot h^{\alpha\gamma}h^{\beta\delta}F_{\alpha\beta}F_{\gamma\delta}.

(IV.32)

For a temporal plaquette
p=p(\ell_{i,i+1},t)
,

\displaystyle\epsilon^{\mu\nu}=e_{i,i+1}^{\mu}e_{3}^{\nu}-e_{3}^{\mu}e_{i,i+1}^{\nu},

(IV.33)

giving

\displaystyle\Big(\oint_{\partial p(\ell_{i,i+1},t)}A_{\mu}dx^{\mu}\Big)^{2}\simeq(\ell_{i,i+1}a_{t})^{2}\cdot e_{i,i+1}^{\mu}e_{i,i+1}^{\rho}e_{3}^{\nu}e_{3}^{\sigma}F_{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\sigma}.

(IV.34)

By using Eq. (
IV.7
), we obtain for each base triangle

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{\ell_{i,i+1}^{*}}{\ell_{i,i+1}a_{t}}\Big(\oint_{\partial p(\ell_{i,i+1},t)}A_{\mu}dx^{\mu}\Big)^{2}\simeq A_{\triangle}a_{t}\cdot h^{\mu\rho}e_{3}^{\nu}e_{3}^{\sigma}F_{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\sigma}.

(IV.35)

With the
2+1
decomposition of the metric:

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}=h_{\mu\nu}+e^{3}_{\mu}e^{3}_{\nu},

(IV.36)

we can assign the following factor for a triangular prism
(\triangle,t)
:

\displaystyle\frac{A_{\triangle}a_{t}}{4}g^{\mu\rho}g^{\nu\sigma}F_{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\sigma}\simeq\frac{1}{2}\Big[\frac{a_{t}}{A_{\triangle}}\Big(\oint_{\partial p(\triangle,t)}A_{\mu}dx^{\mu}\Big)^{2}+\sum_{i}\frac{\ell_{i,i+1}^{*}}{\ell_{i,i+1}a_{t}}\Big(\oint_{\partial p(\ell_{i,i+1},t)}A_{\mu}dx^{\mu}\Big)^{2}\Big].

(IV.37)

By summing over all prisms,
we obtain
the lattice action (
IV.26
)
with the coupling constants (
IV.28
)
under the identification (
IV.27
).

V.2
Current correlator for the gauge sector

For the gauge sector, we calculate the correlator of the conserved current:

\displaystyle J^{\rho}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\rho\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}.

(V.9)

In particular,

\displaystyle J^{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}F_{\theta\phi}.

(V.10)

From Eq. (
IV.30
), we can identify the lattice operator for
J^{t}
to be

\displaystyle J^{t}\simeq\frac{1}{A_{\triangle}}\sum_{m\in p(\triangle,t)}\eta_{m}\theta_{m}\equiv J^{t}_{\rm lat}.

(V.11)

The analytic continuum expression for the temporal two-point function of
J^{t}
is derived in Appendix
C.2
to be

\displaystyle G_{g}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{g^{2}}\langle J^{t}(0,0,t)J^{t}(0,0,0)\rangle

(V.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8\pi}\sum_{n\geq 1}\sqrt{n(n+1)}(2n+1)e^{-\sqrt{n(n+1)}|t|}.

(V.13)

The non-integer spacing in the exponent for the conserved current implies that the pure gauge theory (
N_{f}=0
) is not conformal.

The corresponding lattice correlator:

\displaystyle G_{g,\rm{lat}}(t)\equiv\frac{1}{g^{2}}\langle J^{t}_{\rm lat}(0,0,t)J^{t}_{\rm lat}(0,0,0)\rangle

(V.14)

can be evaluated without Monte Carlo simulation because the gauge action (
IV.26
) is of the Gaussian form:
S_{g,{\rm lat}}=(1/2)\theta_{m}M_{mn}\theta_{n}
.
Note, however, that the zero modes associated with the gauge symmetry exist, which parametrize the gauge-orbit direction of the configuration space.
For gauge-invariant expectation values, the zero modes are canceled between the numerator and the denominator because both the action and the operator do not have an overlap with the zero modes.
Therefore, knowing that the correlator (
V.14
) is gauge invariant, for each pair of
(\theta_{m},\theta_{n})
in the correlator, we can assign the element
\tilde{M}^{-1}_{mn}
of the pseudo-inverse matrix
\tilde{M}^{-1}
, which is the inverse of
M
in the subspace without the zero modes.
This subspace can be understood as the quotient space where the original lattice configuration space is divided by the gauge symmetry.
This assignment does not contradict Elitzur’s theorem
Elitzur:1975im
because the cancellation between the numerator and the denominator does not hold for a non-gauge-invariant operator in the first place.

The projection can be performed numerically in combination with the standard conjugate gradient (CG) algorithm. By setting the initial guess for the solution
\chi
to be
\chi_{0}\equiv 0
, CG derives a polynomial
p(M)
that represents the inverse matrix for a given source vector
b
in the form:

\displaystyle\chi=\tilde{M}^{-1}P\,b\approx p(M)\,b\equiv(p_{1}M+p_{2}M^{2}+\cdots)\,b,

(V.15)

where we formally insert the projector
P
that eliminates the zero modes of
M
.
(Since the multiplication of
M
removes the zero modes by definition, the Krylov space does not include the zero modes.)
For the residual to converge to zero, however, the zero modes need to be projected out from the source vector in advance.
This can be numerically performed again with CG by a prior multiplication of
M
:

\displaystyle P\,b=\tilde{M}^{-1}M\,b.

(V.16)

Therefore, as advertised, we can assign a numerical value to
\langle\theta_{m}\theta_{n}\rangle
in the projected quotient space by locating the point source
b_{(m)}
on the link
m
, whose
m
-th component is one and otherwise zero, perform the projection (
V.16
) by using CG for the inversion:
b^{\prime}_{(m)}\equiv P\,b_{(m)}
, and perform the main inversion again with CG:
\chi=\tilde{M}^{-1}\,b^{\prime}_{(m)}
.
The
n
-th component of the solution vector,
\chi_{n}
, gives the desired two-point function.

Together with the fermion result, we thus expect a qualitative CFT study to be feasible for low-dimensional operators with the coarse lattices.

VI
Discussion

In this paper, we study the free limit of QED
3
in radial quantization on the lattice.
The lattice action is constructed and confirmed to give the correct continuum limit by comparing the lattice correlators to the derived analytic formulas.
The
O(a^{2})
-scaling for the lowest operator dimension in the correlators supports the claim.
By using the overlap fermion, we further show that the important global symmetries can be preserved on the lattice even in the radial picture.
Having established a theoretical ground in the free limit, we can readily start calculations for the interacting theory.
Hybrid Monte Carlo calculations are running for
N_{f}=2,4,6
with the overlap fermion, and a crude conformal window study will follow.
The development of high-performance code is further in progress with Grid
Boyle:2015tjk
.

It is known that anisotropic lattices require a non-perturbative fine-tuning of the coupling constants
Karsch:1982ve
;
Burgers:1987mb
(see also Refs.
Edwards:2008ja
;
HadronSpectrum:2008xlg
).
In our case, a condition for the fine-tuning will be to require the full spherical symmetry (see Refs.
Brower:2020jqj
;
Brower:2024otr
for improvement schemes).
We consider machine-learning-based optimizations
BFSM2025
to be helpful at large refinement levels
L
to deduce a large number of coupling constants from the correlator data.

Once the coupling constants are given in a systematically improvable way, a quantitative first-principles study becomes possible.
Our estimate for the reproduced descendant levels is smaller for the gauge sector, which is up to level 2 without refinement
(L=1)
and up to level 7 with the first refinement
(L=2)
.
Since the pure gauge theory is not conformal, this estimate is crude and the actual value can be closer to the fermion result, which is up to level 6 without refinement
(L=1)
and up to level 10 with the first refinement
(L=2)
.
(Note that the lowest-dimension state is
n=1
in the gauge correlator and
n=0
in the fermion propagator.)
In this regard, it will be interesting to compare lattice results with the fuzzy sphere formulation
Zhu:2022gjc
;
Fardelli:2024qla
;
Lauchli:2025fii
, where the number of states in the system is controlled differently and the descendants can be filled exactly up to a given level.
The complementary advantages of the two methods can help probe unexplored conformal theories in higher dimensions.

Another interesting direction is to consider the odd-flavor theory.
As mentioned in Introduction, the theory is considered to induce a Chern-Simons term in the effective action that violates parity
Redlich:1983kn
;
Redlich:1983dv
;
Narayanan:1997by
;
Kikukawa:1997qh
.
Despite the fact that the odd number of flavors results in a sign-indefinite determinant on the lattice, since the parity anomaly can be seen as a counterpart of the chiral anomaly in four dimensions, clarifying the theoretical picture as well as its phenomenological consequences would be helpful to understand quantum field theory from non-perturbative perspectives.

To study the interacting theory (regardless of even or odd
N_{f}
) in full theoretical rigor, it is attractive to use the domain-wall formulation
Kaplan:1992bt
;
Shamir:1993zy
;
Furman:1994ky
;
Neuberger:1997bg
(see Refs.
Hands:2015dyp
;
Hands:2015qha
for the study in 3D).
Though the block-diagonal four-component overlap operator
{\cal D}_{\rm ov}
can be obtained as a limit of the domain-wall fermion with the Wilson kernel
{\cal D}_{W}
in Möbius formulation
Brower:2012vk
, it is not straightforward to single out the two-component overlap operator
D_{\rm ov}
for the interacting theory under finite
L_{s}
(the extent of the fictitious warped direction) due to an off-diagonal gamma matrix
\Gamma=\gamma_{4}
or
\gamma_{5}
in the hopping term in the warped direction (unless with a trick such as taking the square root).
Recently, it was proposed to formulate a chiral fermion by utilizing nontrivial manifolds
Clancy:2024bjb
;
Kaplan:2024ezz
(see also Ref.
Aoki:2022aez
;
Golterman:2024ccm
;
Yamamoto:2025ehe
).
It is therefore interesting to further introduce nontrivial geometry in the warped direction to define the system in the domain-wall formulation.
For exploratory studies, on the other hand, the quenched calculation with twisted-mass Wilson fermion
Frezzotti:2000nk
;
Frezzotti:2003ni
can be a viable option to remedy the breaking of chiral and accompanying discrete spacetime symmetries.

Studies along the above lines are in progress and will be reported elsewhere.

On a generic manifold in

\displaystyle e_{\mu}^{a}\,e_{\nu}^{b}\,g^{\mu\nu}=\delta^{ab}.

(A.1)

\displaystyle dV

\displaystyle\equiv(1/D!)\,\epsilon_{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{D}}\,dx^{\mu_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{\mu_{D}}

(A.2)

\displaystyle=e^{1}_{\mu_{1}}\cdots e^{D}_{\mu_{D}}dx^{\mu_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{\mu_{D}},

(A.3)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\theta\phi t}=\det e^{a}_{\mu}=\sqrt{g}.

(A.4)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}^{R}\phi^{R}\equiv(\partial_{\mu}+\omega^{R}_{\mu})\phi^{R},

\displaystyle{\omega}^{R}_{\mu}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\omega_{\mu}^{ab}M^{R}_{ab}.

(A.6)

We define the (plain) covariant derivative
\nabla_{\mu}
to act on both vector and local Lorentz indices.
The vierbein satisfies the parallel transport equation:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}e^{a}_{\nu}=\partial_{\mu}e^{a}_{\nu}-\Gamma_{\mu\nu}^{\gamma}e_{\gamma}^{a}+\omega_{\mu\,b}^{a}e_{\nu}^{b}=0.

(A.7)

Along a geodesic

\displaystyle e_{xy}^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}^{R}\phi^{R}=0,

(A.8)

where

\displaystyle\phi^{R}(x)=\Omega_{xy}^{R}\phi^{R}(y).

(A.9)

\displaystyle\Omega_{xy}^{R}\equiv{\cal P}\exp\left(\int_{\gamma_{xy}}ds\,e_{xy}^{\mu}\omega_{\mu}^{R}\right),

(A.10)

where
{\cal P}
stands for the path-ordering that runs from
x
to

along
\gamma_{xy}
from left to right, which is the direction of increasing
s
.

For a spinor representation

\gamma_{a}
that satisfy
\{\gamma_{a},\gamma_{b}\}=2\delta_{ab}

In the main text, we have two representations in this class,

\sigma_{a}
as the generators for

\gamma_{a}
given in Eq. (

The corresponding

\displaystyle M_{ab}^{S}\equiv\frac{1}{4}[\gamma_{a},\gamma_{b}].

(A.11)

From

Appendix B
Calculation of the lattice spin connection
\Omega_{y_{1}y_{2}}

In this appendix, we summarize a method to analytically calculate the lattice spin connection matrices
\Omega_{y_{1}y_{2}}
on
S^{2}
.
Starting from the geodesic equations:

\displaystyle\tan(\phi-\phi_{0})=\iota_{\phi}k\tan(s-s_{0}),

(B.1)

\displaystyle\cos\theta=-\iota_{\theta}\sqrt{1-k^{2}}\sin(s-s_{0}),

(B.2)

one can eliminate
s-s_{0}
to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tan^{2}\theta}=\Big(\frac{1}{k^{2}}-1\Big)\sin^{2}(\phi-\phi_{0}).

(B.3)

The constants
k>0
and
\phi_{0}\in[0,2\pi)
parameterize the shape of the geodesic.
The sign factor
\iota_{\phi}\equiv\pm 1
determines the sign of
\dot{\phi}
, and the sign factor
\iota_{\theta}\equiv\pm 1
in Eq. (
B.2
) determines the sign of
\dot{\theta}
.
Given the shape parameters and the directional sign factors,
s_{0}
determines the initial point of the curve, while the endpoint of the curve is at
s=s_{0}+\ell
with the arclength
\ell
.
When
\theta
is not monotonic, the curve needs to be divided into two patches at the extremum of
\theta
at
s-s_{0}=\pm\pi/2
mod
2\pi
.

With the analytic formula for the geodesic
\gamma_{y_{1}y_{2}}
, we can readily calculate the Wilson line (
A.10
).
In
D=2
, the path-ordering is trivial,
giving Eqs. (
III.1
) and (
III.2
).
In practice, it is convenient to avoid coordinate singularities for numerical calculations.
For this, we tilt the polar axis slightly such that the pole points do not lie on top of the sites or the links.
Performing such a rotation corresponds to choosing a different gauge for the local Lorentz frame.

We remark that the coordinate singularities of
\omega^{S}_{\alpha}
are related to the double-sheet structure of the fermion field on
S^{2}
.
Using the
(\theta,\phi)
coordinates, we have for a closed curve
\gamma
on
S^{2}
:

\displaystyle\oint_{\gamma}dy^{\alpha}\omega^{S}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{i\sigma_{3}}{2}\Big(\int dV-2\pi\eta(\gamma)\Big).

(B.4)

The second term is due to the nonzero divergence at each pole, where
\eta(\gamma)=1
if the curve
\gamma
encircles the north (south) pole in the positive (negative)
\phi
direction and is 0 if
\gamma
does not include a pole in the interior.
The additional divergence term in the local curvature formula (
B.4
) can be canceled by introducing a cut
\gamma_{c}
that runs from the north pole to the south pole and does not lie on top of the lattice sites.
The antiperiodic boundary condition for
\psi
and
\bar{\psi}
is then imposed in the
\phi
-direction by assigning the minus sign when the link crosses
\gamma_{c}
.
The parallel-transported variable (
A.9
) acquires a minus sign when it crosses
\gamma_{c}
, precisely canceling the divergence term.

The antiperiodic boundary condition can be reinterpreted as a sign factor for
\Omega_{y_{1}y_{2}}
. In this case, Eq. (
A.13
) and Eq. (
B.4
) without the
\eta
term can be identified as the defining properties of the lattice spin connection.
See Ref.
Brower:2016vsl
for more on this view and an algorithm to compute
\Omega_{y_{1}y_{2}}
as an optimization problem.

Appendix C
Derivation of the continuum correlators

In this appendix, we derive the analytic expressions for the correlators quoted in Sec.
V
.
The free propagators for the fermion and gauge fields can be expressed by the eigenvalues and the eigenfunctions of the kernel matrices.
The line of argument for solving the eigenvalue problem follows Ref.
Abrikosov:2002jr
, which addressed the Dirac equation on
S^{2}
.
For completeness, we provide a full explanation in our context with corrections to the fermion result on
S^{2}
.
Note that the two Killing vectors,
\partial_{\phi}
and
\partial_{t}
, commute with each other and
with the kernel matrices for the propagators, and thus they can be simultaneously diagonalized.
The solutions are obtained in the simultaneously diagonalizing basis as they resolve the degenerate eigenvalues.

C.1
Free fermion propagator

To invert the free Dirac matrix
D\equiv\sigma^{a}e_{a}^{\mu}{\nabla}^{S}_{\mu}
, we solve the eigenvalue problem:

\displaystyle D\psi=i\lambda\psi.

(C.1)

The Dirac matrix is antihermitian with respect to the inner product:

\displaystyle(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\equiv\int dV\,\psi_{1}^{\dagger}(x)\psi_{2}(x).

(C.2)

The eigenfunctions of
D
are a subset of the eigenfunctions of the square of the Dirac matrix, which can be rewritten with the Lichnerowicz formula:

\displaystyle D^{2}\psi=\Big(g^{\mu\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla^{S}_{\nu}\psi-\frac{1}{4}R\Big)\psi,

(C.3)

where
R
is the Ricci scalar.
We first consider the eigenvalue problem for the squared matrix, which reads on
S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle\Big[\partial_{t}^{2}+\partial_{\theta}^{2}+\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}\Big(\partial_{\phi}-\frac{i}{2}\cos\theta\sigma_{3}\Big)^{2}+\cot\theta\partial_{\theta}-\frac{1}{2}\Big]\psi=-\lambda^{2}\psi.

(C.4)

We first drop the
t
-dependence and derive the solution on
S^{2}
.
We set
z\equiv\cos\theta
and take the ansatz:

\displaystyle\psi(\theta,\phi)=e^{im\phi}u_{m}(z),

(C.5)

where
m\in\mathbb{Z}+1/2
since
\psi
is antiperiodic in
\phi
(see Appendix
B
).
Equation (
C.4
) reduces to

\displaystyle\Big[\frac{d}{dz}(1-z^{2})\frac{d}{dz}-\frac{m^{2}-\sigma_{3}mz+z^{2}/4}{1-z^{2}}+\lambda^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\Big]u_{m}(z)=0.

(C.6)

The expression in the bracket is diagonal, and the differential equation can be solved independently for each component.
Writing the eigenvalues of
\sigma_{3}
as
\iota_{3}\equiv\pm 1
and separating the sign part of
m
as
m\equiv\iota_{m}|m|
, we see that each component obeys the same differential equation with the variable
\iota_{3}\iota_{m}z
for a given
|m|
.
We write the solution as
\xi_{|m|}(\iota_{3}\iota_{m}z)
.
By factoring out the singular part, the differential equation for
\xi_{|m|}(z)
reduces to a hypergeometric form, and the relevant solution can be easily found:

\displaystyle\xi_{|m|}(z)

\displaystyle\propto(1-z)^{\frac{1}{2}(|m|-1/2)}(1+z)^{-\frac{1}{2}(|m|+1/2)}F\Big(-|\lambda|,|\lambda|;\frac{1}{2}+|m|;\frac{1-z}{2}\Big),

(C.7)

where
F(a,b;c;x)
is the hypergeometric function.
We impose regularity at
z=-1
, whose value can be calculated by Gauss’s theorem:

\displaystyle F(a,b;c;1)=\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(c-a-b)}{\Gamma(c-a)\Gamma(c-b)}.

(C.8)

The condition implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+|m|-|\lambda|\equiv-n,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},

(C.9)

which leads to the expression

\displaystyle\xi_{|m|,n}(z)

\displaystyle=(1-z)^{\frac{1}{2}(|m|-1/2)}(1+z)^{-\frac{1}{2}(|m|+1/2)}P^{(|m|-1/2,-|m|-1/2)}_{n+|m|+1/2}(z),

(C.10)

where
P_{j}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(z)
is the Jacobi polynomial:
1
1

1

Conventionally, Jacobi polynomials are defined for
\alpha,\beta>-1
to ensure the integrability of the weight function, which is not the case in our usage.
Nevertheless, Rodrigues’ formula is still valid in our application:

\displaystyle P_{j}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{j}}{2^{j}j!}(1-z)^{-\alpha}(1+z)^{-\beta}\frac{d^{j}}{dz^{j}}\Big[(1-z)^{\alpha+j}(1+z)^{\beta+j}\Big]

(C.11)

because
j
,
\alpha+j
,
\beta+j
are nonnegative integers in all occasions.
Accordingly, well-known formulas such as normalization and orthogonality relations mostly apply in the generalized case
szego75
.
The only exception that appears in our study is in the zero modes of the gauge field, which can be easily distinguished by a singular integral.

\displaystyle P_{j}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(z)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1+j)}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)j!}F\Big(-j,1+\alpha+\beta+j;\alpha+1;\frac{1-z}{2}\Big).

(C.12)

From the Rodrigues’ formula (
C.11
), the expansion around
z=1
for
\alpha\geq 0
is

\displaystyle P_{j}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\alpha+j)!}{\alpha!\,j!}\Big(1+O(1-z)\Big),

(C.13)

and the expansion around
z=-1
for
\beta<0
is

\displaystyle P_{j}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(z)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{j-\beta}2^{\beta}\frac{(\alpha+j)!}{(-\beta)!(\alpha+\beta+j)!}(1+z)^{-\beta}\Big(1+O(1+z)\Big).

(C.14)

The function
\xi_{|m|}(z)
thus simplifies at the edge points:

\displaystyle\xi_{|m|,n}(1)=\frac{\delta_{|m|,1/2}}{\sqrt{2}},\quad\xi_{|m|,n}(-1)=0.

(C.15)

To obtain the eigenfunctions of
D
, we pair the spinor components which we have solved independently for the squared matrix in Eq. (
C.6
).
Note the
\sigma_{3}
-hermiticity:
For
D\psi(y)=i\lambda\psi(y)
,
D\sigma_{3}\psi(y)=-i\lambda\sigma_{3}\psi(y)
.
The result is (the symbol
\iota_{3}=\pm 1
is reused as a label):

\displaystyle D\psi_{m,n,\iota_{3}}=\iota_{3}i\lambda_{|m|,n}\psi_{m,n,\iota_{3}},

(C.16)

\displaystyle\lambda_{|m|,n}\equiv n+|m|+1/2,

(C.17)

where

\displaystyle\psi_{m,n,\iota_{3}}(\theta,\phi)=\frac{e^{im\phi}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\xi_{|m|,n}(\iota_{m}z)\\
\iota_{3}\iota_{m}i(-1)^{n}\xi_{|m|,n}(-\iota_{m}z)\end{array}\right).

The eigenfunctions are orthogonal:

\displaystyle(\psi_{m,n,\iota_{3}},\psi_{m^{\prime},n^{\prime},\iota_{3}^{\prime}})=c_{|m|,n}^{2}\,\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{\iota_{3}\iota_{3}^{\prime}},

(C.20)

where

\displaystyle c_{|m|,n}^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{2n+2|m|+1}\frac{n!(n+2|m|)!}{(n+|m|-1/2)!(n+|m|+1/2)!}.

(C.21)

To add the radial direction, we note that, for the free theory, the purely imaginary eigenvalues form complex conjugate pairs, though we do not have
\sigma_{3}
-hermiticity in three spacetime dimensions:
For
D\psi(x)=i\lambda\psi(x)
,
D\sigma_{3}\psi(x_{T})=-i\lambda\sigma_{3}\psi(x_{T})
.
By following the same steps, the solution can be obtained as

\displaystyle D\psi_{k,m,n,\iota_{3}}=\iota_{3}i\lambda_{|k|,|m|,n}\psi_{k,m,n,\iota_{3}},

(C.22)

\displaystyle\lambda_{|k|,|m|,n}\equiv\sqrt{|k|^{2}+(n+|m|+1/2)^{2}}.

(C.23)

The eigenfunctions are given by

\displaystyle\psi_{k,m,n,\iota_{3}}(\theta,\phi,t)\equiv\frac{e^{\iota_{3}ikt}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{e^{im\phi}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\times\begin{cases}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\cos(\gamma_{|k|,|m|,n}/2)\,\xi_{|m|,n}(\iota_{m}z)\\
\iota_{3}\iota_{m}i(-1)^{n}\sin(\gamma_{|k|,|m|,n}/2)\,\xi_{|m|,n}(-\iota_{m}z)\end{array}\right)\quad(k>0),\\
\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\sin(\gamma_{|k|,|m|,n}/2)\,\xi_{|m|,n}(\iota_{m}z)\\
\iota_{3}\iota_{m}i(-1)^{n}\cos(\gamma_{|k|,|m|,n}/2)\,\xi_{|m|,n}(-\iota_{m}z)\end{array}\right)\quad(k<0),\end{cases}

(C.24)

where the angular variable
\gamma_{|k|,|m|,n}
is defined by

\displaystyle\cos\gamma_{|k|,|m|,n}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{|k|}{\lambda_{|k|,|m|,n}}.

(C.25)

The eigenfunctions satisfy the orthogonality relation:

\displaystyle(\psi_{k,m,n,\iota_{3}},\psi_{k^{\prime},m^{\prime},n^{\prime},\iota_{3}^{\prime}})=c_{|m|,n}^{2}\,\delta(k-k^{\prime})\,\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{\iota_{3}\iota_{3}^{\prime}}.

(C.26)

By collectively writing the eigenfunctions of
D
as
\psi_{K}
:

\displaystyle D\psi_{K}

\displaystyle=i\Lambda_{K}\psi_{K},

(C.27)

and using the differential equation for the propagator
G(x,x^{\prime})
:

\displaystyle D(x)G(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g(x)}}\delta^{3}(x-x^{\prime}),

(C.28)

the continuum propagator can be readily given as

\displaystyle G(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{K}\frac{\psi_{K}(x)\psi_{K}^{\dagger}(x^{\prime})}{i\Lambda_{K}c_{K}^{2}}.

(C.29)

We calculate as an example the propagator in the temporal direction.
Because of Eq. (
C.15
), the expression simplifies by choosing
x=(0,0,t)
and
x^{\prime}=(0,0,0)
.
The resulting function
G(t)
is

\displaystyle G(t)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{3}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{n+1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{2\pi i}\frac{ke^{ikt}}{k^{2}+(n+1)^{2}}

\displaystyle={\rm sign}(t)\,\sigma_{3}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{n+1}{4\pi}\,e^{-(n+1)|t|},

(C.30)

as quoted in the main text.
The tower of integer-spaced exponents shows the expected spectrum of a conformal field theory with the primary operator with dimension
\Delta=1
.

C.2
Free gauge propagator

To calculate the free gauge propagator analytically, we add the gauge-fixing term:

\displaystyle S_{g}+S_{gf}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{g^{2}}\int dV\,\Big(\frac{1}{4}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{2}(\nabla_{\mu}A^{\mu})^{2}\Big)

(C.31)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2g^{2}}\int dV\,A^{\mu}(-g_{\mu\nu}\nabla^{\rho}\nabla_{\rho}+R_{\mu\nu})A^{\nu}.
