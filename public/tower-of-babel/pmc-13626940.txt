Myogenic differentiation-derived small extracellular vesicles as a therapeutic strategy for miR-193b-3p-mediated muscle regeneration
Authors: Sujin Choi, Min-Ji Sung, Hyun-Ju An, Soonchul Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The regeneration of skeletal muscle involves precisely regulated intercellular communication, including extracellular vesicle (EV)-mediated processes. In this study, we show that during in vitro myogenic differentiation of human skeletal muscle progenitor cells (myoblasts), miR-193b-3p accumulates within small EVs (sEVs) in a temporally regulated manner. Both sEV-mediated and synthetic delivery of miR-193b-3p enhanced myogenic differentiation in vitro and promoted histological recovery in a murine volumetric muscle loss model. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that miR-193b-3p directly suppresses adapter-related protein complex 2 subunit mu 1 (AP2M1), a clathrin-associated adapter protein that has not been well characterized in muscle biology, and identify it as a negative regulator of myogenic progression. AP2M1 knockdown phenocopied the effects of miR-193b-3p, suggesting that this regulatory axis may contribute to muscle regeneration. In addition, transcriptomic analyses of aged rodent and sarcopenic human datasets revealed conserved upregulation of AP2M1 in degenerative muscle states, supporting its potential clinical relevance. Collectively, these findings reveal a myogenic differentiation-dependent, sEV-mediated miRNA regulatory mechanism that contributes to muscle regeneration. Based on these results, we propose the miR-193b-3p-AP2M1 axis as a potential therapeutic target for muscle-wasting disorders.

Introduction

Skeletal muscles are characterized by a strong capacity for regeneration, primarily mediated by quiescent satellite cells that are rapidly activated in response to injury to initiate a tightly orchestrated cascade of proliferation, lineage commitment, and terminal myogenic differentiation.1,2,3 Although this endogenous regenerative program efficiently restores muscle structure following minor injuries, it is often insufficient under severe pathological conditions such as volumetric muscle loss (VML), chronic trauma, and age-associated degenerative diseases such as sarcopenia.4,5,6 These limitations highlight the need to identify molecular drivers that can enhance or restore myogenic potential, ultimately contributing to the development of therapeutic strategies.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as important regulators of skeletal muscle biology, functioning as fine-tuners of gene expression programs that dictate the fate of satellite cells, myotube formation, and regenerative capacity.7 Classical muscle-enriched miRNAs, such as miR-1, miR-133, and miR-206, have been established to modulate key signaling pathways involved in lineage specification and fusion during myogenesis.8,9,10 For example, miR-206 promotes satellite cell differentiation and accelerates muscle regeneration following acute injury,11,12,13 whereas miR-1 and miR-133 play key roles in balancing proliferation and differentiation.8,13,14 However, although these studies highlight the therapeutic potential of miRNA-based modulation of muscle repair, the repertoire of miRNAs involved in coordinating muscle regeneration, particularly those acting as paracrine factors, remains incompletely understood.

Among the emerging mediators of intercellular communication, extracellular vesicles (EVs), particularly small EVS (sEVs), have gained increasing attention.15,16 These vesicles are nano-sized particles released by donor cells that serve as carriers of bioactive molecules, including miRNAs, proteins, and lipids, enabling them to influence the behavior of recipient cells.16,17 Recent studies have shown that sEVs derived from mesenchymal stem cells or endothelial progenitor cells can enhance angiogenesis, attenuate fibrosis, and promote muscle regeneration, supporting their reparative potential.18,19 However, despite these advances, the dynamic sEV-mediated signaling that occurs during the myogenic differentiation of myoblasts, which models aspects of the physiological process of muscle repair, remains poorly understood.

Stage-specific sEV-associated miRNAs, whose expression is dynamically regulated during myogenic differentiation, are of particular interest given their potential roles as paracrine mediators of muscle regeneration.20,21 Systematic identification of these miRNAs may reveal previously uncharacterized molecular mechanisms governing muscle repair and provide novel therapeutic targets for muscle-wasting conditions. In this study, we profiled sEV-associated miRNA cargo released during myogenic differentiation and assessed their functional relevance in skeletal muscle regeneration. We identified a previously uncharacterized sEV-associated miRNA as a regulator of myogenesis. These findings enhance our understanding of sEV-mediated muscle regeneration and provide a basis for the development of miRNA-based therapeutic strategies.

sEVs were isolated from conditioned media collected no differentiation (sEV-ND, 60 h in growth medium), early differentiation (sEV-ED, 0–60 h in DM), and late differentiation (sEV-LD, 60–120 h in DM) conditions. In all vesicle groups, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed vesicles with a typical exosome-like morphology (Figure 1C). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed distinct differences in particle size distributions among vesicle types, with modal diameters of 92.7 ± 6, 56.0 ± 8.5, and 78.2 ± 15.2 nm for sEV-ND, sEV-ED, and sEV-LD, respectively (Figure 1D). Western blot analysis confirmed that all vesicle preparations were positive for the canonical sEV markers CD9, CD63, and CD81 (Figure 1E). Overall, vesicles collected at each time point met the established criteria for sEVs based on TEM, DLS, and marker analyses.

To assess the pro-myogenic effects of different sEV fractions, naive myoblasts were exposed to each vesicle type at two concentrations (50 and 100 μg/mL) during early differentiation. All three sEV types induced dose-dependent increases in MYF5, MYOD1, and MYOG expression. Notably, at both concentrations, sEV-LD elicited the highest expression levels, indicating stronger pro-myogenic effects compared with earlier-stage sEVs (Figure 1F). To further evaluate the morphological consequences of differentiation, F-actin immunofluorescence staining was performed following treatment with 100 μg/mL sEVs. Under differentiation conditions, treatment with sEV-ED and sEV-LD promoted elongation-like morphological changes compared with DM alone, with the most pronounced effects observed in the sEV-LD group. Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in cell aspect ratio following sEV-ED and sEV-LD treatment (Figure 1G). To compare the differentiation-inducing effects of sEVs with those of differentiation medium alone, HSkMCs were treated with sEVs under growth conditions. Although sEV treatment induced elongation-like morphological changes, these were less pronounced than those observed under differentiation conditions (Figure S1), suggesting that sEV treatment alone is insufficient to induce complete myogenic differentiation. Overall, these results demonstrate that sEVs released at different stages of differentiation vary in their capacity to promote pro-myogenic responses.

sEVs secreted during myogenic differentiation enhance skeletal muscle regeneration following volumetric injury

To evaluate the regenerative potential of sEVs secreted at distinct stages of myogenic differentiation, we incorporated sEV-ND, sEV-ED, and sEV-LD into collagen scaffolds and implanted these into VML defects in the tibialis anterior (TA) muscle of C57BL/6 mice, and at 2 weeks post-implantation, the tissues were harvested for histological and molecular analyses (Figure 2A). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed that compared with the muscles treated with bare scaffolds, which were characterized by a disrupted architecture within the defect region, those treated with sEV-loaded scaffolds displayed improved tissue organization (Figure 2B). Furthermore, consistent with active myogenesis, regenerating myofibers with centrally located nuclei were observed more frequently in the sEV-treated groups than in the scaffold-only group, with the sEV-LD group showing the most pronounced histological improvements.22 In addition, relative to the scaffold-only group, Masson’s trichrome (MT) staining indicated reduced collagen deposition in sEV-treated muscles, with the smallest fibrotic area observed in the sEV-LD group (Figure 2B). Consistent with these observations, quantitative analyses based on measurements of the cross-sectional fiber area (CFA) revealed an induced increase in myofiber size in the sEV-treated groups, with the most pronounced increase being detected in the sEV-LD group (Figure 2C). In addition, compared with the scaffold-only group, quantitative analysis of MT staining confirmed a marked reduction in fibrotic area in sEV-treated muscles (Figure 2D), whereas in immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses, we observed increases in the staining intensities and distributions of Myod1 and Myog in sEV-treated defects, with the strongest signals again being detected in the sEV-LD group (Figure 2B). For both these markers, quantitative assessments revealed stage-dependent increases in the IHC-positive areas, reaching the highest levels in the sEV-LD group (Figure 2E), and the findings of RT-qPCR analysis similarly indicated a more pronounced upregulation of Myod1 and Myog expression following sEV-LD treatment compared with that observed in response to earlier stage sEVs (Figure 2F). Collectively, these data indicate that differentiation-stage sEVs contribute to enhancing histological and myogenic marker outcomes following volumetric muscle injury, with late-stage sEVs being characterized by the strongest effects. miR-193b-3p as a key sEV-associated miRNA enriched during myogenic differentiation that promotes skeletal myogenesis

To identify candidate sEV miRNAs associated with the pro-myogenic effects observed during myogenic differentiation, we performed small RNA sequencing for sEV-ND, sEV-ED, and sEV-LD isolated from HSkMCs. A total of 2,588 miRNAs were detected in sEVs collected at the different stages of myogenic differentiation, among which, we sought to determine those showing differential expression (differentially expressed miRNAs [DEMs]) among groups, defined based on thresholds of a |log2 fold change| ≥ 1 and p value ≤0.01, as illustrated in volcano plots (Figures 3A and 3B) and summarized in Tables S2 and S3. In addition, to identify miRNAs that are dynamically modulated during differentiation, we compared DEMs between sEV-ED and sEV-ND and sEV-LD and sEV-ND, which revealed 16 commonly differentially expressed miRNAs (15 upregulated and one downregulated) (Figure 3C; Table S4). To further assess the potential functional relevance of miR-193b-3p in myogenic differentiation, HSkMCs were transfected with miR-193b-3p mimics, with subsequent RT-qPCR analysis revealing a marked increase in miR-193b-3p expression in the miR-193b-3p-transfected cells compared with those transfected with the miR-Cont sequence (Figure 3E). Functional assays examining myogenic differentiation revealed that the overexpression of miR-193b-3p was associated with increases in the expression of myogenic markers, including MYF5, MYOD1, and MYOG (Figure 3F), with elevations in the corresponding protein levels (Figure 3G). In addition, to assess whether miR-193b-3p contributes to the pro-myogenic effects of sEV-LD, we co-treated HSkMCs with sEV-LD and either inhibitor-miR-Cont or inhibitor-miR-193b-3p, and under these conditions, found that the inhibition of miR-193b-3p was associated with an attenuation of the sEV-LD-induced increases in MYF5, MYOD1, and MYOG protein expression (Figure S2). Collectively, these findings suggest that miR-193b-3p accumulates in differentiation-stage sEVs and may play a role in promoting myogenic differentiation during myogenesis.

AP2M1 as a candidate downstream target of miR-193b-3p

To identify downstream targets via which miR-193b-3p may influence myogenic differentiation, we used three miRNA target prediction algorithms (miRDB, DIANA, and TargetScan). Cross-referencing the outputs from these platforms identified 17 consensus candidate genes (Figure 4A). Among these, WD repeat domain 82 (WDR82), adapter-related protein complex 2 subunit mu 1 (AP2M1), and tyrosine 3-monooxygenase/tryptophan 5-monooxygenase activation protein zeta (YWHAZ) showed reduced mRNA expression following miR-193b-3p mimic transfection in HSkMCs, as assessed by RT-qPCR (Figure 4B). Notably, however, only AP2M1 showed a corresponding reduction at the protein level, indicating that it is a potential downstream target of miR-193b-3p (Figure 4C). To establish whether there is a direct interaction between miR-193b-3p and AP2M1, we conducted dual-luciferase reporter assays, which revealed that co-transfection of miR-193b-3p mimics with a reporter construct containing the 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR) of wild-type AP2M1 was associated with a reduction in luciferase activity. In contrast, mutating the predicted miR-193b-3p-binding site abolished this effect, thereby providing evidence for a direct interaction between miR-193b-3p and the AP2M1 3′ UTR (Figures 4D and 4E). Consistent with these findings, inhibition of endogenous miR-193b-3p was found to result in an increase in AP2M1 protein expression (Figure 4F). To further characterize the potential downstream mechanisms associated with the miR-193b-3p-AP2M1 axis, we examined the signaling pathways associated with receptor trafficking and the regulation of myogenesis, among which, the overexpression of miR-193b-3p was found to lead to a selective reduction in NOTCH1-NTM protein levels, whereas the levels of p-EGFR and β-catenin remained largely unchanged (Figure S3). AP2M1 expression is inversely associated with the expression of myogenic differentiation markers

To assess whether the suppression of AP2M1 is involved in the pro-myogenic effects associated with miR-193b-3p, we silenced AP2M1 in HSkMCs using siRNA. AP2M1 knockout was associated with increased expression of the myogenic markers MYF5, MYOD1, and MYOG at both the mRNA and protein levels (Figures 5A and 5B), a pattern similar to that observed following miR-193b-3p overexpression. In addition, to further examine the response of AP2M1 under a mimicked injury condition, we utilized a barium chloride (BaCl2)-induced muscle injury model. HSkMCs exposed to BaCl2 were found to be characterized by higher levels of AP2M1 expression, with concurrent reductions in the expression of myogenic markers (Figures 5C and 5D). Taken together, these findings provide evidence that AP2M1 may serve as a downstream target of miR-193b-3p and function as a negative modulator of myogenic differentiation. AP2M1 expression shows increasing age- and sarcopenia-associated trends in human and rodent skeletal muscles

To examine whether AP2M1 is associated with the degeneration of muscles, we analyzed human and rodent skeletal muscle datasets. In the GEO: GSE111016 human dataset, which includes skeletal muscle samples derived from 20 healthy individuals and 20 patients with sarcopenia, the expression of AP2M1 was found to be significantly higher in sarcopenic muscles than in those of the healthy controls (p = 0.024; Figure 6A). In an independent human dataset, GEO: GSE111006, which includes skeletal muscle samples obtained from 28 healthy individuals and four patients with sarcopenia, AP2M1 showed a similar upward tendency in sarcopenic samples, although this difference did not reach the level of statistical significance (p = 0.29; Figure 6B), which may be attributable to the small sample size of the sarcopenic group. To establish whether this pattern of expression is conserved in other species, we analyzed the GEO: GSE38077 rat dataset profiling tibialis anterior (TA) muscles extracted from young (6-months old) and aged (24-months old) Sprague-Dawley rats. Ap2m1 expression in aged muscles showed an increase compared with that in young controls, although again this difference was non-significant (p = 0.22; Figure 6C). Collectively, the patterns discerned in these datasets indicate that within the muscles of young and healthy individuals, AP2M1 tends to be more highly expressed in aged or sarcopenic skeletal muscles in different mammalian species, with statistically significant elevation observed in one human cohort and consistent trends in other datasets. miR-193b-3p promotes skeletal muscle regeneration in vivo, partly via a suppression of AP2M1

To assess the therapeutic relevance of miR-193b-3p in skeletal muscle repair, we employed a TA VML injury mouse model. Following injury, animals received intramuscular injections of either miR-Cont or miR-193b-3p mimic sequences, and TA muscles were collected 14 days later for histological and molecular analyses (Figure 7A). Compared with those treated with miR-Cont, H&E staining revealed a marked reduction in necrotic muscle fibers and a greater prevalence of regenerating fibers characterized by centrally located nuclei in the miR-193b-3p-treated muscles. In addition, MT staining indicated a reduction in the deposition of collagen in the miR-193b-3p group, with tissue morphology more closely resembling uninjured muscle, whereas more extensive fibrosis was observed in miR-Cont-treated tissues (Figure 7B). These findings were further supported by quantitative analyses. Specifically, measurement of CFA revealed a significant increase in myofiber size in miR-193b-3p-treated muscles compared with miR-Cont-treated muscles (Figure 7C). Additionally, quantitative assessment of MT staining demonstrated a notable reduction in fibrotic area in the miR-193b-3p-treated group relative to the control group (Figure 7D). IHC analysis further revealed that the administration of miR-193b-3p was associated with increases in expression of the myogenic markers Myod1 and Myog, along with reduced expression of its putative downstream target, AP2M1 (Figure 7E), with consistent findings being obtained based on RT-qPCR analyses, revealing upregulation of miR-193b-3p (Figure 7F). In addition, compared with the miR-Cont group, we detected elevated levels of Myod1 and Myog mRNA in the miR-193b-3p group, whereas in contrast, those of Ap2m1 mRNA were reduced (Figure 7G). Collectively, these data provide in vivo support that miR-193b-3p contributes to the regeneration of skeletal muscle following volumetric injury, which is mediated, at least in part, via suppression of AP2M1. Discussion

In this study, we identified a previously understudied sEV-mediated regulatory axis in which miR-193b-3p is associated with skeletal muscle regeneration through the repression of AP2M1, a negative regulator of myogenesis. We found that miR-193b-3p progressively accumulates in sEVs released by differentiating myoblasts, and that these vesicles exert pro-myogenic effects in vitro and in vivo. These findings suggest an additional layer of temporal regulation during myogenesis and indicate that AP2M1 may represent a potential molecular target for muscle-wasting conditions.

miRNAs are well-established key regulators of muscle development and regeneration, with canonical examples such as miR-1, miR-133, and miR-206 being extensively characterized.8,9 In recent years, however, attention has shifted to the miR-193 family, with studies reporting that by targeting endocytosis-related genes and signaling pathways, miR-193b has antiproliferative effects in cancer and metabolic disorders.23,24 Emerging evidence suggests that miR-193b-3p may also play roles in muscle biology. For example, this miRNA has been reported to promote skeletal muscle cell proliferation via IGF2BP1, thereby indicating its potential involvement in muscle regeneration.25 However, these studies have tended to focus primarily on intracellular interactions, whereas its role in intercellular communication remains unclear. Our findings suggest that sEV-associated miR-193b-3p is upregulated during myogenic differentiation and is associated with increased expression of myogenic markers and morphological changes in myoblasts. Notably, we identified an sEV-mediated miR-193b-3p-AP2M1 axis that may represent a potential mechanistic link between intercellular communication and receptor trafficking.

Contrastingly, some previous studies have reported that under pathological conditions such as type 2 diabetes, miR-193b may impair muscle growth by targeting the PDK1/Akt signaling pathway.26 This apparent discrepancy may be explained by several factors. First, the miR-193b precursor can undergo strand-specific processing to generate miR-193b-3p and miR-193b-5p, and differences in strand usage may contribute to context-dependent functions in skeletal muscle. Second, differences in experimental contexts, including pathological versus normal conditions, may influence the functional outcome of miR-193b signaling. Third, whereas previous studies have focused on intracellular functions, we describe an sEV-mediated role of miR-193b-3p, suggesting that its function may vary depending on its mode of delivery and cellular context.

A key finding of this study was our identification of AP2M1, a component of adapter protein complex 2, as a direct target of miR-193b-3p. The AP2 adapter complex consists of α- and β2-adaptins in association with the μ2 and σ2 subunits. AP2M1 encodes the μ2 subunit, which is essential for clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) and the internalization of diverse signaling receptors.27,28 CME has been established to play a central role in modulating the availability and trafficking of receptors involved in key developmental and regenerative pathways, including Notch, Wnt/β-catenin, and EGFR.29,30 AP2M1 has also been implicated in neurological processes and disorders, reflecting its key role in receptor trafficking and synaptic function.31,32 Notch and Wnt/β-catenin signaling, in particular, are well-established regulators of satellite cell activation and postnatal myogenesis.33 These observations thus suggest that through its role in CME, AP2M1 may contribute to the regulation of signaling pathways essential for muscle regeneration.

Consistent with this, our data suggest that AP2M1 may act as a negative regulator of myogenesis, and that its suppression phenocopies the effects of miR-193b-3p, supporting the involvement of the miR-193b-3p-AP2M1 axis in this process. Given the central role of endocytic trafficking in fine-tuning receptor-mediated signaling, reduced AP2M1 activity may contribute to the modulation of pro-myogenic signaling inputs. In line with this, our experimental data further showed that overexpression of miR-193b-3p and knockdown of AP2M1 reduced NOTCH1-NTM protein levels, suggesting that AP2M1 may modulate Notch signaling.

The in vivo relevance of this regulatory circuit was evaluated using a murine VML model. Local administration of miR-193b-3p was associated with enhanced regenerative features, reduced fibrosis, and increased expression of key myogenic transcription factors in regenerating muscle tissues, accompanied by a decrease in AP2M1 levels.34 These findings suggest that AP2M1 may function as a regulatory modulator that constrains muscle repair and indicate the potential relevance of targeting this pathway.35,36

In addition to providing mechanistic insights, our findings may have potential translational relevance. Given that currently adopted interventions for muscle degeneration, including physical therapy, nutritional support, and pharmacological modulation, often yield variable outcomes,37,38,39 the therapeutic delivery of miR-193b-3p, potentially in combination with sEV-based approaches, could represent a complementary strategy.40,41,42 The observed pro-myogenic effects suggest that miR-193b-3p may have additive or potentially synergistic effects with existing modalities, thereby contributing to a multi-pronged approach to support muscle repair. Future studies are warranted to investigate the applicability of such combined therapeutic strategies in preclinical models and to assess their potential additive or synergistic benefits.15,38

To explore the potential relevance of our findings, we analyzed transcriptomic datasets derived from aged rodents and human patients with sarcopenia. Across multiple cohorts, AP2M1 expression showed an upward trend in degenerative muscle tissues, suggesting a conserved role in age-associated muscle decline. Moreover, these observations further support the potential involvement of the miR-193b-3p-AP2M1 axis in this process.

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations. First, the upstream regulatory mechanisms controlling miR-193b-3p expression during satellite cell activation, as well as the downstream signaling pathways influenced by AP2M1 repression, remain to be fully elucidated. In addition, the complexity of sEV cargo should be considered, as other RNA species, including mRNAs, long non-coding RNAs, and additional candidate miRNAs, may also contribute to the observed biological effects, and the relative contribution of miR-193b-3p within this network remains to be defined. In this study, we focused on miRNAs due to their well-established roles in regulating gene expression during myogenesis and their ability to be directly linked to downstream targets and functional outcomes. Importantly, the in vivo sEV and miR-193b-3p experiments were designed to address sequential rather than comparative questions. The former established the stage-dependent regenerative activity of differentiation-derived sEVs, whereas the latter examined the functional relevance of miR-193b-3p identified from their cargo. Therefore, these experiments should not be interpreted as a direct comparison of regenerative efficacy between whole sEVs and miR-193b-3p. Rather, our findings indicate that miR-193b-3p represents one functionally relevant component contributing to the pro-myogenic activity of differentiation-derived sEVs, while the overall regenerative effects of sEVs likely reflect the combined actions of multiple bioactive cargos. However, although treatment with sEVs and miR-193b-3p increased the expression of myogenic markers in vivo, the magnitude of this effect was relatively modest. Therefore, further studies are needed to enhance the in vivo efficacy of these approaches and clarify their functional significance. Moreover, while histological and molecular markers of regeneration were assessed, long-term functional evaluations of muscle recovery, such as muscle strength or endurance, were not included and warrant future investigation.

In summary, we identified an sEV-mediated regulatory axis in which miR-193b-3p is associated with skeletal muscle regeneration via repression of AP2M1. Our findings contribute to an improved understanding of sEV-mediated intercellular communication during myogenesis and suggest that the miR-193b-3p-AP2M1 interaction may represent a potential target for muscle-wasting conditions. The enrichment of miR-193b-3p in sEVs derived from differentiating myoblasts, together with its pro-myogenic effects observed in vitro and in vivo, supports its potential functional relevance. Collectively, these findings provide a basis for further investigation into miRNA- and sEV-based approaches for skeletal muscle repair. In particular, sEV-mediated delivery of regenerative miRNAs may provide a basis for developing therapeutic strategies for promoting muscle regeneration in conditions such as traumatic muscle injury or muscle-wasting disorders.

Materials and methods

Cells

Primary human skeletal muscle cells (HSkMCs: PCS-950–010; ATCC) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in mesenchymal stem cell basal medium supplemented with a Primary Skeletal Muscle Growth Kit (PCS-950–040; ATCC) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (15140122; Gibco) at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. Myogenesis was induced by seeding 2 × 105 HSkMCs per well in six-well plates, and culturing them in skeletal muscle DM (Skeletal Muscle Differentiation Tool, PCS-950–050; ATCC) for 60 or 120 h, with medium changes at 2- to 3-day intervals.43

sEV isolation

sEVs were isolated from HSkMCs using ExoQuick-TC (EXOTC50A-1; System Biosciences), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, 5 × 106 HSkMCs were seeded in 150-mm dishes and incubated in growth medium or DM. sEVs were collected at defined time points corresponding to the growth conditions: sEV-ND, 60 h in growth medium; sEV-ED, 0–60 h in DM; and sEV-LD, 60–120 h in DM. The total sEV yield at each time point was quantified using a BCA protein assay. At the indicated time points, culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 30 min to remove cellular debris. The clarified supernatants were mixed with ExoQuick-TC in a 1:5 ratio, incubated overnight at 4°C, and then centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 30 min. The supernatants were discarded after centrifugation, and the sEV pellets were resuspended in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), stored at −80°C, and used within 1 month. The protein concentrations of HSkMC-derived sEVs were determined using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225; Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For subsequent experiments, sEV preparations were normalized to give comparable stock concentrations (1 mg/mL), equal amounts of which (50 or 100 μg/mL) were used for treatment.

Morphology and size distribution of sEVs

sEV morphology and size distribution were analyzed using TEM (JEM 1011 transmission electron microscope; JEOL) and DLS (ELSZ-2000ZS; Otsuka Electronics). For TEM, the sEVs were adsorbed onto formvar/carbon-coated mesh grids, negatively stained with 2% uranyl acetate, and imaged. For DLS, sEV suspensions were diluted to 1 μg/mL in PBS and measured in duplicate after temperature stabilization. Raw DLS intensity data were processed using R (version 4.3.0) with the ggplot2, dplyr, purrr, and gridExtra packages. To exclude interference from large aggregates and debris, particle size distributions were restricted to a range of 30–1,000 nm to exclude large aggregates and debris, fitted using a log-normal model, and normalized as intensity (%).44 Modal diameters (mode) were calculated and expressed as the mean ± SEM of two independent replicates.

Western blot analysis of sEV surface markers

Western blot analysis was performed to detect surface marker proteins on sEVs. Proteins were extracted from sEVs using PRO-PREP Protein Extraction Solution (17081; iNtRON Biotechnology). Equal amounts of protein (20 μg) were separated on 8% SDS-PAGE gels at 100 V for 1 h and transferred onto PVDF membranes at 95 V for 1 h. After blocking with 5% skimmed milk in TBST for 1 h at 25°C, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: CD9 (1:1,000: sc-13118; Santa Cruz), CD63 (1:1,000: sc-5275, Santa Cruz), CD81 (1:1,000: MA5-13548; Invitrogen), and β-actin (1:2,000: sc-47778; Santa Cruz). After washing, the membranes were incubated for 2 h at 25°C with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse IgG or anti-rabbit IgG [both 1:4,000: ACE BioLabs]). Protein bands were visualized using an EZ-Western Lumi Pico kit (DG-WP; DoGENBio).

Ethical statement and animals

This study complied with all relevant ethical guidelines for animal research and was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the CHA Laboratory Animal Research Center (approval no. #200076). Male C57BL/6N mice were purchased from KOATECH and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at 23°C ± 1°C and 50% ± 10% humidity, with a 12-h light/dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum.

Volumetric TA muscle loss injury model

To assess the therapeutic effects of sEVs and miRNA mimics on skeletal muscle regeneration, we performed two independent VML injury experiments using the TA muscles of mice. In both experiments, a full-thickness muscle defect (3 mm in diameter and 2 mm in depth) was created using a biopsy punch after fur removal and an anterior incision under anesthesia. Each experimental group consisted of five mice. After treatment, the skin was sutured and 2 weeks later, TA muscle samples were harvested for regeneration analysis.45 The mice were euthanized via CO2 inhalation at 30% volume/min flow rate and death was confirmed by the absence of breathing and eye discoloration.

sEV treatment

To evaluate the regenerative potential of HSkMC-derived sEVs during myogenic differentiation, mice were divided into two groups: (1) the sEVs group, in which mice received a collagen sponge loaded with 100 μg sEV suspended in 10 μL of PBS and implanted into the VML site, and (2) the Sham group, in which the mice received a collagen sponge without sEVs implanted into the corresponding defect site. The sEVs used for group 1 were classified into three types according to the stage of HSkMC differentiation: (1) sEV-ND; (2) sEV-ED; and (3) sEV-LD. The surgical site was sutured following implantation, and the mice were euthanized 2 weeks later, with TA muscle tissues being collected for assessments of regeneration.

miRNA mimic treatment

To assess the effects of miR-193b-3p on muscle regeneration, mice were divided into two groups: (1) the miR-193b-3p mimic group, in which mice were intramuscularly injected with 10 μg of miR-193b-3p mimic complexed with 1.2 μL of in vivo-jetPEI transfection reagent (201-50G; Polyplus-transfection SA) in a final volume of 50 μL, and (2) the negative control group, in which mice received the same amount of control mimic mixed with transfection reagent using the same protocol. Prior to injecting into the VML site, the mixture was incubated for 15 min at 25°C. The mice were euthanized 2 weeks after treatment and TA muscle tissues were harvested for assessments of regeneration.

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis

TA muscle tissues were resected and fixed in 10% formalin for 24 h at 4°C for subsequent histological and immunohistochemical analyses. Tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm thickness, deparaffinized with xylene, and rehydrated using a graded ethanol series. H&E and MT staining were performed for histological evaluation. For IHC analysis, sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval by microwaving using Antigen Retrieval Buffer (ab93678; Abcam) for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited by treatment with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 30 min at 25°C, followed by a 15-min incubation in 3% bovine serum albumin at 25°C. Thereafter, the slides were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against Myod1 (1:100: MA5-12902; Thermo Fisher Scientific), Myog (1:100: CMA5-11486, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and Ap2m1 (1:100: NBP2-67762; Novus Biologicals), and then treated with HRP-conjugated anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:200: A1012S; ACE Biolabs) or anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:200: A1013S; ACE Biolabs) for 20 min at 25°C. After washing with PBS, immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen for 10 min at 25°C. Images were acquired using a ZEISS Axioscan 7 microscope in bright-field scanning mode (Zeiss GmbH).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from HSkMCs and dissected TA muscle tissues using TRIzol reagent (15596018; Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For mRNA and miRNA expression analysis, 1 μg total RNA was reverse-transcribed to complementary DNA (cDNA) using a Reverse Transcription Master Mix (EBT-1542; ELPIS-BIOTECH) for mRNA or an HB miR Multi Assay Kit (SYSTEM I; HeimBiotek) for miRNA. The reverse transcription conditions were 42°C for 30 min followed by 94°C for 5 min for mRNA synthesis, and 37°C for 1 h followed by 95°C for 5 min for miRNA synthesis. RT-qPCRs for mRNA and miRNA were conducted using an EzAmp qPCR 2× Master Mix (EBT-1801; ELPIS-BIOTECH) for mRNA or an HB SYSTEM I miR Multi Assay Kit for miRNA, following the respective protocols. Thermocycling conditions for mRNA quantification included an initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95°C for 10 s, 60°C for 10 s, and 72°C for 30 s. For miRNA detection, the conditions included an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 40 s. The expression of mRNAs was normalized to that of the GAPDH internal control, and miRNA levels were normalized to those of RNU6B. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Relative expression values were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCq method.46 Details regarding the sequences of primers used for amplification are shown in Table S1.

Western blot analysis of cellular proteins

Western blotting for HSkMCs was conducted using the same protocol described in “Western blot analysis of sEV surface markers” section. Briefly, cells were either transfected with RNA oligonucleotides or subjected to BaCl2-induced injury, harvested in PBS, and lysed using a PRO-PREP Protein Extraction Solution. Equal amounts of protein (20 μg) were loaded onto 10% SDS-PAGE gels, followed by PVDF membrane transfer and blocking with 5% skimmed milk. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against MYF5 (1:1,000: sc-518039; Santa Cruz), MYOD1 (1:1,000), MYOG (1:500), AP2M1 (1:1,000), and β-actin (1:2,000: sc-47778; Santa Cruz). After washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied, and the bands were detected as described in Section above.

Small miRNA sequencing and differentially expressed miRNA analysis

To profile the miRNA cargoes in sEVs, we sequenced small RNAs. Sequencing was conducted commercially by E-Biogen Inc. (Seoul, Republic of Korea) to analyze DEMs. Libraries were prepared using an NEBNext Multiplex Small RNA Library Prep Kit (New England BioLabs, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA samples were ligated to adapters, reverse-transcribed using adapter-specific primers, and PCR amplified. The PCR products were purified using a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN, Düsseldorf, Germany) and AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Pasadena, CA, USA). Library quality and fragment size distribution were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer in conjunction with a high-sensitivity DNA assay (Agilent Technologies). High-throughput sequencing was performed using the Illumina NextSeq500 platform with single-end 75 bp reads (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequencing reads were aligned to mature miRNA reference sequences from miRBase (v.22.1) using Bowtie2 (v.2.4.2) and read counts were quantified using BEDTools (v.2.25.0) and the Bioconductor packages miRBase.db (v.1.2.0) and miRNA (v.1.58.0) in R (v.3.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Analysis of differential expression was performed between groups, with miRNAs showing a |log2(fold-change)| ≥1 and a p value <0.01 being defined as DEMs. Visualization was carried out in R using the ggplot2, VennDiagram, and pheatmap packages, and volcano plots were generated to illustrate the distribution of DEMs. Venn diagrams were used to identify overlapping and unique DEMs between comparisons, and hierarchical clustering heat maps were constructed following log transformation and normalization of the expression values. Clustering was performed using correlation-based distances with complete linkages. For candidate screening, DEMs were ranked according to their statistical significance and magnitude of fold-change, with the top-ranked miRNAs being selected for downstream experimental validation.

Transfection with RNA oligonucleotides

For transfection experiments, we used a range of RNA oligonucleotides, including miRNA mimics, inhibitors, and siRNAs. An miR-193b-3p mimic with the sequence 5′–AACUGGCCCUCAAAGUCCCGCU–3′ (obtained from the miRBase database [version 22.1; https://www.mirbase.org]) was synthesized as an RNA duplex by Genolution. The control miRNA (miR-Cont) sequence, similarly synthesized by Genolution, was 5′–CCUCGUGCCGUUCCAUCAGGUAGUU–3′. miRNA inhibitors were synthesized by BIONICS as 2′-O-methyl-modified oligoribonucleotide single strands, with the following sequences: inhibitor-miR-193b-3p (5′–AGCGGGACUUUGAGGGCCAGUU–3′) and inhibitor-miR-Cont, (5′–CAGUACUUUUGUGUAGUACAA–3′). Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting AP2M1 (si-AP2M1; sc-60184), WDR82 (si-WDR82: sc-78161), and YWHAQ (si-YWHAQ: sc-29586), as well as control siRNA (si-Cont: sc-37007), were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. For transfection, RNA oligonucleotides were combined with the G-fectin transfection reagent (Genolution) to give a final concentration of 40 nM, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The transfection mixture was added to 5 × 104 HSkMCs per well seeded in six-well plates and incubated at 37°C for 48 h.

miRNA target gene selection

Putative target genes of miR-193b-3p were predicted using selected miRNA target prediction tools, namely, miRDB version 6.0 (https://www.mirdb.org), TargetScan release 8.0 (https://www.targetscan.org), and DIANA-MICROT version 4 (https://dianalab.e-ce.uth.gr/html/universe/index.php?r=microtv4). Candidate genes were selected based on their identification as common targets using all three prediction algorithms.

Luciferase assays

The potential binding sites of miR-193b-3p within the 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR) of the AP2M1 gene were predicted using the TargetScan tool (https://www.targetscan.org). Fragments of the AP2M1 3′ UTR containing the predicted binding sites were cloned into a pGL3UC vector by Macrogen Inc. (provided by Kim, Seoul National University, Seoul, South Korea). To generate mutant reporters, nucleotide sequences lacking the miR-193b-3p-binding site were synthesized and inserted into the pGL3UC vector by Macrogen. HSkMCs (5 × 104 cells per well) were seeded in 24-well plates, incubated for 24 h, and then co-transfected with 100 ng reporter plasmid or empty pGL3UC, 100 ng Renilla luciferase plasmid, and 40 nM miR-Cont or miR-193b-3p using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000008; Thermo Fisher Scientific). After a 48-h incubation at 37°C, the cells were lysed using 1× Passive Lysis Buffer (E1910, Promega Corporation) to release the Firefly and Renilla luciferase enzymes. The luciferase activity of both the Firefly and Renilla enzymes was measured using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (E1910, Promega Corporation) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to that of Renilla luciferase.

BaCl2-induced in vitro muscle injury model

To establish an in vitro model of muscle injury, we used BaCl2, which has been widely applied in both in vivo and in vitro skeletal muscle injury models,47,48 as a chemical stimulus that induces membrane disruption and cytotoxic effects in muscle cells. HSkMCs (2 × 105 cells per well) were seeded in six-well plates and cultured with the DM for 48 h to induce myogenic differentiation. Following differentiation, the medium was replaced with fresh growth medium containing 0.012% BaCl2 (202738, Sigma-Aldrich) and incubated for 3 h at 37°C to induce chemical injury. At the indicated time points after injury, the cells were harvested for protein and RNA extraction for western blot and RT-qPCR analyses, respectively. Control cells were treated under the same conditions without exposure to BaCl2.

Transcriptomic analysis of AP2M1 expression in rodent and human skeletal muscles

To evaluate the pattern of AP2M1 expression in skeletal muscle, we retrieved transcriptomic datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. For the rodent model, we analyzed the GSE38077 dataset, which contains gene expression profiles from the TA muscles of 6- and 24-month-old Sprague-Dawley rats (n = 5 per group), whereas for human skeletal muscle, we obtained two independent GEO datasets, namely, GSE111006 (skeletal muscle biopsies from 28 healthy individuals and four patients with sarcopenia) and GSE111016 (20 healthy and 20 sarcopenic muscle samples). Normalized expression data were obtained from the GEO database. Boxplots were generated using R (v.3.6.0) with the ggplot2 package, and statistical differences between groups were assessed using the Student’s t test.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism Software 8 (Dotmatics). Differences between two groups were evaluated using unpaired Student’s t tests, whereas comparisons among more than two groups were assessed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Data are presented as the means ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with statistical significance being defined as a p value <0.05.
