Outcomes of Intentional Celiac Artery Coverage During Thoracic Endovascular Aortic Repair for Infective Native Aortic Aneurysms: A Case Series
Authors: Muhammad Aizat Tamlikha Ismail, Karthigesu Aimanan, Michael Arvind, Putera Mas Pian, Hanif Hussein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntentional celiac artery (CA) coverage during thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) may be required to achieve an adequate distal landing zone; however, this strategy remains controversial owing to concerns regarding visceral ischemia, particularly in patients with infective native aortic aneurysms with compromised physiology and hostile anatomy. We retrospectively reviewed three patients who underwent TEVAR with planned CA coverage after angiographic assessment of collateral circulation using balloon occlusion testing and selective superior mesenteric artery angiography, with CA embolization. All patients demonstrated robust collateral perfusion, underwent CA embolization, and subsequently TEVAR with intentional CA coverage. One patient developed a type Ib endoleak on surveillance imaging without sac expansion. No hepatic, gastric, or splenic ischemic events occurred during follow-up. This case series suggests that intentional CA coverage may be feasible in carefully selected patients when guided by meticulous collateral assessment, offering a pragmatic option in urgent or anatomically constrained settings.Keywords: Endovascular aneurysm repair; Celiac artery; Aneurysm, infected; Aortic aneurysm, thoracic; Collateral circulation

INTRODUCTION

Thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) has become the preferred treatment for many descending thoracic aortic pathologies, offering lower perioperative morbidity and mortality than open repair. Current Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines emphasize meticulous preoperative planning with high-resolution computed tomography angiography (CTA) to ensure adequate proximal and distal landing zones and minimize endoleak-related failure [1].

In selected patients, inadequate distal landing zones necessitate extension of the stent graft across the celiac artery (CA) [2]. The CA supplies critical splanchnic organs, and intentional coverage has traditionally been avoided because of concerns regarding hepatic, gastric, and splenic ischemia. Although collateral pathways from the superior mesenteric artery (SMA), particularly via the pancreaticoduodenal and gastroduodenal arteries, may maintain visceral perfusion, collateral adequacy is variable and cannot be assumed. A systematic review and meta-analysis reported clinically relevant rates of visceral ischemia, endoleaks, reintervention, and early mortality following CA coverage during TEVAR, underscoring the need for careful patient selection and assessment of collateral circulation [3].

Advanced endovascular and hybrid strategies, including fenestrated or branched endografts and hybrid visceral revascularization, have been developed to preserve visceral branch perfusion [4,5]. However, these approaches are frequently impractical in patients with infective native aortic aneurysms, who often present with sepsis, physiological frailty, and hostile anatomy that limit the feasibility of complex reconstruction [5]. Infective native aortic aneurysms pose technical and physiological challenges: inflammatory periaortic changes and friable landing zones frequently necessitate extended stent graft coverage to achieve an adequate seal, thereby increasing the likelihood of unavoidable CA coverage. Septic physiology and marginal hemodynamics further increase the susceptibility to low-flow visceral ischemia, rendering ischemic complications potentially catastrophic.

Data specifically addressing intentional CA coverage in infective native aortic aneurysms are limited. Therefore, we present a case series of three patients who underwent TEVAR with intentional CA coverage following angiographic collateral assessment. The requirement of institutional review board approval was waived because of the retrospective nature of this case series. Consent for publication was obtained from all patients.

1) Case 1

A 75-year-old female with diabetes and pulmonary tuberculosis in the continuation phase of treatment presented with fever, lethargy, and epigastric discomfort. CTA demonstrated a contained rupture of a suprarenal saccular aortic aneurysm, with less than 10 mm between the aneurysm and the CA origin, precluding an adequate distal landing zone (Fig. 1).

(A) Saccular aneurysm (arrow) in close proximity to the celiac artery (CA). (B) Adequate distance between the aneurysm and the superior mesenteric artery (SMA). (C) Selective SMA angiography demonstrated robust retrograde collateral perfusion to the gastric and hepatic circulation. (D) Completion angiography confirmed patent SMA following CA embolization and TEVAR.

Intraoperative balloon occlusion testing (BOT) of the CA with selective SMA angiography revealed robust retrograde hepatic and gastric perfusion. The CA was embolized using an Amplatzer vascular plug (AVP 2, Abbott; 12 mm), followed by the deployment of a 32–28×150 mm thoracic stent graft (Valiant Captivia, Medtronic). Completion angiography showed no endoleak. BOT, CA embolization, and TEVAR were performed in a single operative session. Blood cultures grew multidrug-resistant Staphylococcus spp., and treatment for concomitant pulmonary tuberculosis was continued; both infections were treated with antimicrobial therapy. The patient remained clinically stable with preserved visceral perfusion, and follow-up CTA at 1 and 6 months demonstrated satisfactory graft position without ischemic complications or aneurysm progression. She received prolonged antibiotic therapy as advised by the infectious disease team.

2) Case 2

A 72-year-old female with hypertension, rheumatoid arthritis on immunosuppressive therapy, and interstitial lung disease presented with epigastric pain 3 days after laparoscopic cholecystectomy. CTA revealed a right-sided thoracoabdominal saccular aortic aneurysm with perianeurysmal inflammatory changes, with only approximately 12 mm between the aneurysm and the CA origin, precluding an adequate distal landing zone (Fig. 2). Blood cultures grew Burkholderia pseudomallei. Pre-TEVAR visceral angiography with CA balloon occlusion confirmed adequate collateral perfusion through the pancreaticoduodenal arcade. Collateral assessment, CA embolization, and TEVAR were performed in a single operation session. The CA was embolized using an Amplatzer vascular plug (AVP 4; 8 mm), followed by deployment of a tapered 26–22×150 mm thoracic stent graft (Valiant Captivia), with the distal end of the graft landing proximal to the SMA. The postoperative course was complicated by left lower-limb deep vein thrombosis, which was treated with anticoagulation. No visceral ischemic events were observed. She completed 6 weeks of intravenous ceftazidime, followed by long-term oral trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. Follow-up at 1 year showed no recurrent sepsis or evidence of graft infection.

(A) The neck of the saccular aneurysm was adjacent to the celiac artery (CA). (B) Adequate distance between aneurysm and superior mesenteric artery (SMA). (C) Three-dimensional reconstruction demonstrating the saccular aneurysm (arrow) in relation to the CA and SMA. (D) Balloon occlusion testing demonstrated robust retrograde visceral collateral perfusion.

3) Case 3

A 66-year-old male with severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, who was receiving long-term oxygen therapy, presented with abdominal pain. CTA demonstrated a distal thoracic saccular aneurysm with extrinsic compression causing narrowing of the CA, leaving less than 10 mm for an adequate distal sealing zone (Fig. 3). A staged approach was adopted. Preoperative visceral angiography with BOT of the CA confirmed adequate retrograde perfusion through the pancreaticoduodenal arcade. CA embolization was subsequently performed using an Interlock coil (Boston Scientific; 6 mm×10 cm) and pushable Nester coils (Cook Medical; 4 mm×14 cm and 5 mm×14 cm), followed by a deliberate 2-day interval before TEVAR to allow hemodynamic redistribution through the collateral arcade prior to aortic coverage. TEVAR was then performed with deployment of a 26–22×150 mm thoracic stent graft (Valiant Captivia). Blood cultures grew Salmonella spp., and the patient was treated with prolonged antibiotic therapy. Surveillance CTA showed a type Ib endoleak without sac expansion. The patient declined further intervention and remained clinically stable, without visceral ischemia, through 2 years of follow-up.

(A) Saccular aneurysm in close proximity to and compressing the celiac artery (CA), with a patent superior mesenteric artery (SMA). (B) Three-dimensional reconstruction demonstrating the saccular aneurysm in relation to the CA and SMA, with extrinsic compression of the CA (arrow).

DISCUSSION

Intentional CA coverage during TEVAR remains controversial because of the potential for visceral ischemia, including hepatic, gastric, and splenic ischemia [3]. These concerns are amplified in infective native aortic aneurysms, which often present with sepsis, hemodynamic instability, or rupture and may require urgent intervention. In this setting, advanced endovascular strategies, such as fenestrated or branched endografts, are often impractical because of device availability, manufacturing delays, and procedural complexity. Inflammatory periaortic changes may necessitate extended distal stent-graft coverage to achieve a durable seal, thereby increasing the likelihood of unavoidable CA coverage. Mehta et al. [6] reported that approximately 2 cm of healthy aorta is required for reliable distal fixation during TEVAR. In infective native aortic aneurysms, visceral ischemic complications such as hepatic infarction, gastric ischemia, or pancreatitis can be catastrophic and compromise both infection control and survival [7,8]. Septic physiology and poor hemodynamics may further increase the susceptibility to low-flow ischemia.

The physiological basis for CA coverage lies in the pancreaticoduodenal arcade, which provides collateral communication between the SMA and CA territories [6]. Retrograde flow through the pancreaticoduodenal and gastroduodenal arteries can maintain hepatic and gastric perfusion after CA interruption. However, collateral adequacy is variable and cannot be assumed, underscoring the unpredictability of visceral perfusion following CA coverage [9].

1) Collateral assessment protocol

Static CTA alone may not adequately predict functional collateral sufficiency, particularly in patients who are acutely ill or septic. Dynamic angiographic assessment using BOT of the CA with selective SMA angiography provides real-time evaluation of retrograde hepatic perfusion and may be useful for procedural planning [10]. In our series, BOT was performed in all 3 patients via femoral access using 6-Fr and 4-Fr sheaths, with a 0.035-inch guidewire used to cannulate the CA and SMA. The CA was occluded using a 6 mm×20 mm balloon for 3 minutes while selective SMA angiography was performed to evaluate retrograde opacification of the CA territory. The adequacy of collateral perfusion was judged qualitatively from the angiographic flow pattern, specifically prompt retrograde opacification of the common hepatic and gastroduodenal arteries. No intra-arterial pressure measurements (e.g., stump pressure) were obtained in this series; however, quantitative pressure-based criteria may provide additional objective evidence of collateral sufficiency and should be considered when angiographic findings are equivocal.

Whether BOT is mandatory in all patients or selectively useful when CTA demonstrates SMA–CA collateral pathways remains debatable. Even when collaterals are apparent on preoperative CTA, static imaging cannot predict their hemodynamic adequacy under conditions of acute CA interruption, particularly in patients with sepsis and hemodynamic compromise. Therefore, BOT may be useful and should be considered when intentional CA coverage is planned. However, its routine use in all cases requires validation in a larger series.

2) Role of pre-TEVAR CA embolization and timing

Pre-TEVAR CA embolization serves a dual purpose. First, it is intended to reduce the risk of retrograde type II endoleak arising from the CA after stent-graft deployment, which is an important consideration in the infective setting, where endoleak-driven sac pressurization may compromise aneurysm exclusion and increase the risk of secondary rupture. Second, embolization may facilitate hemodynamic redistribution of mesenteric flow through the pancreaticoduodenal arcade prior to aortic coverage.

In Cases 1 and 2, CA embolization and TEVAR were performed in the same operative session, reflecting the urgency of presentation and our confidence in the collateral adequacy confirmed on BOT. In Case 3, a deliberate 2-day interval between CA embolization and TEVAR was chosen to allow hemodynamic redistribution through the collateral arcade before complete aortic coverage. This staged approach may theoretically reduce ischemic risk in patients with borderline collaterals or greater physiological frailty. The optimal timing strategy remains undefined, and larger studies are needed to determine whether a staged approach confers a measurable ischemic benefit over concomitant embolization and TEVAR.

In addition to BOT and selective SMA angiography, several adjunctive methods for assessing the risk of visceral ischemia after CA coverage have been described. Intra-arterial stump pressure measurement following CA balloon occlusion may provide a quantitative surrogate of hepatic perfusion pressure; however, no universally accepted pressure threshold has been validated for TEVAR with CA coverage. Preprocedural assessment of SMA patency is essential because unrecognized SMA stenosis may critically impair collateral flow after CA coverage [3]. In our series, SMA patency was confirmed in all patients before TEVAR.

3) Infection, pathogen characteristics, and visceral function

The pathogens identified in our series—multidrug-resistant Staphylococcus spp. (Case 1), B. pseudomallei (Case 2), and Salmonella spp. (Case 3)—represent a microbiologically heterogeneous spectrum of infective native aortic aneurysms, including pathogens encountered in tropical and subtropical regions [8]. Each pathogen poses distinct systemic challenges: Staphylococcus spp. may be associated with aggressive bacteremia and septic embolic phenomena; B. pseudomallei, the causative organism of melioidosis, can produce profound systemic inflammation and multi-organ involvement; Salmonella spp. require prolonged antimicrobial therapy because recurrence or persistent infection may occur if treatment is inadequate. Despite these microbiological differences, no patient developed clinically evident hepatic, gastric, splenic, or intestinal ischemia after CA coverage. The small sample size precludes firm conclusions regarding the pathogen-specific susceptibility to visceral ischemia.

The type Ib endoleak identified in Case 3 warrants particular discussion. In the context of infective native aortic aneurysms, a persistent endoleak is clinically important because continued sac pressurization may compromise aneurysm exclusion, complicate infection control, and increase the risk of aneurysm expansion or secondary rupture [7]. Although the patient declined further intervention, close surveillance was essential. Early reintervention should be considered if sac expansion, persistent or worsening endoleak, graft migration, or clinical signs of ongoing infection are identified. Potential reintervention options include distal stent graft extension, adjunctive endovascular procedures, or open surgical conversion, depending on the patient’s fitness, anatomy, and extent of infection.

Given the infective etiology and the dependence of visceral perfusion on SMA-to-CA collateral pathways after CA coverage, continued clinical and imaging surveillance is warranted, with particular attention to endoleak, sac enlargement, graft migration, recurrent infection, and SMA patency.

4) Published evidence and comparative context

Published studies evaluating CA coverage during TEVAR encompass heterogeneous aneurysm etiologies and clinical settings, including infected aneurysms. Planned CA coverage with confirmed SMA-to-CA collateral circulation and preserved SMA inflow has been reported to be feasible in selected patients [6]. However, CA coverage is not universally benign, and systematic reviews have reported non-trivial rates of visceral ischemia, spinal cord ischemia, mortality, and endoleaks [3]. These adverse outcomes may be related to inadequate collateral perfusion, untreated SMA stenosis, extensive aortic coverage, or endograft-related failure.

In patients with infective native aortic aneurysms who are unfit for open repair at the time of presentation, TEVAR may serve as a bridging or definitive strategy when combined with meticulous planning, source control when feasible, and prolonged antimicrobial therapy [7,8]. Infection does not necessarily preclude intentional CA coverage, but it mandates heightened physiological assessment and close surveillance. Alternative strategies for preserving CA perfusion, including chimney techniques, fenestrated or branched endografts, and hybrid bypass procedures, may be appropriate for selected patients. However, in infective native aortic aneurysms, these approaches are frequently limited by urgency, physiological frailty, anatomical constraints, cost, and device availability.

Based on the available evidence and our institutional experience, intentional CA coverage in infective native aortic aneurysms should be considered only when the following conditions are met: (1) CA coverage is necessary to obtain an adequate distal landing zone; (2) robust SMA-to-CA collateral circulation is demonstrated angiographically, preferably with BOT; (3) significant SMA stenosis is excluded or corrected; and (4) alternative revascularization strategies are considered when collateral adequacy is uncertain.

In summary, intentional CA coverage during TEVAR may be feasible in carefully selected patients with infective native aortic aneurysms when guided by meticulous angiographic assessment of collateral circulation and preserved SMA inflow. In urgent or anatomically constrained settings where complex branched technologies are not available, this strategy may offer a pragmatic and effective option; however, larger studies are needed to better define its safety and durability.
