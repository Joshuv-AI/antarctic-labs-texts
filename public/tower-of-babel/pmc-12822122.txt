Brucellosis complicated by aortitis and thyroiditis: a case report
Authors: Tamer R. Abuelsamen, Hala Alzaghloul, Hussam H. Alhawari, Hiba Abbasi, Tareq Massimi, Moaath Alsmady, Akram A. Saleh, Qussay Alsabbagh, Azmy Hadidy, Malik E. Juweid, Marwan H. Adwan, Khaled Al-Salahat, Faris G. Bakri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBrucellosis is a neglected and common zoonotic disease. Infections in humans result in a granulomatous disease that can affect many organs with various presentations and complications. Aortitis and thyroiditis are rare complications with limited data. This case discusses the successful medical therapy alone in the management of aortitis and the development of Brucella thyroid disease through autoimmune mechanisms.Case presentationThis is a 58-year-old male patient from Jordan. He presented with prolonged fever and was found to have brucellosis associated with abdominal aortitis, subacute thyroiditis, bilateral sacroiliitis, and lumbar spondylodiskitis. We review his clinical course with an emphasis on the complications of aortitis and thyroiditis. The aortitis was managed conservatively with antibiotics alone, avoiding surgical or endovascular intervention. The subacute thyroiditis was associated with autoimmune markers and was detected in the setting of an underlying pre-existing multinodular goiter. After a long follow-up period of three years, the patient continued to do well.ConclusionPhysicians should be aware of the complications of aortitis and thyroiditis in patients with brucellosis. Medical therapy alone can be an option for some patients with aortitis who are at high surgical risk. In addition, the development of subacute thyroiditis in the setting of brucellosis might be secondary to an autoimmune response.

Background

Brucellosis is a zoonotic infection caused by small, Gram-negative, coccobacilli of the genus Brucella. In low-income countries, it is endemic and neglected. Endemic regions are located primarily in the Middle East, Mediterranean, and Latin America. Transmission to humans occurs mainly through the ingestion of unpasteurized dairy products. Human brucellosis is a granulomatous disease that can affect many organs and have variable presentations and complications [1–3]. Among these complications are: spondylodiskitis, sacroiliitis, aortitis, and thyroid disease.

Osteoarticular involvement is one of the most common complications of brucellosis and varies in rate from 10% to 85% of patients. It may present as sacroiliitis, spondylitis, osteomyelitis, peripheral arthritis, bursitis, or tenosynovitis. The most common osteoarticular involved sites in adults are the sacroiliac (up to 80%) and spinal (up to 54%) joints [4]. Cardiovascular involvement, such as endocarditis, myocarditis, pericarditis, or endarteritis, is rare with a rate of 3% of patients. Aortic and iliac involvement is even more rare in brucellosis [1]. Aortitis caused by Brucella is a life-threatening condition and is best managed by surgical or endovascular intervention in addition to antibiotics [5]. Disregarding surgical intervention is even considered a fatal oversight [5]. Thyroid gland involvement is an extremely rare condition and is reported almost exclusively as case reports [6].

Here, we present a male patient, who was found to have brucellosis complicated by abdominal aortitis, subacute thyroiditis, bilateral sacroiliitis, and lumbar spondylodiskitis. This patient is unique because (a) he presented with several simultaneous localized complications due to brucellosis; (b) he did not undergo any surgical intervention for the aortitis, because of comorbid contraindications, and instead was managed with antibiotics alone. He subsequently responded to antibiotics and showed complete clinical and radiological recovery; and (c) he developed subacute thyroiditis accompanied by the presence of autoimmune markers which shows the ability of Brucella to cause autoimmune thyroid reaction. These observations offer valuable insights on the pathogenesis and management of aortitis and thyroiditis in human brucellosis.

Case presentation

A 58-year-old Jordanian man was admitted in August 2022 for fever, chills, rigors, and sweating, all predominantly happening at night, fatigue, and back pain primarily in the upper back and both shoulders with less intensity in the lower back for two months. At home, he recorded a temperature of 39 °C. He took paracetamol at night with mild benefit. He did not take any antibiotics. One month prior to admission, he sought care from a physician for the same symptoms but had no benefit. The patient had dyslipidemia, ischemic heart disease, and a history of coronary artery bypass graft surgery. He was not known to have hypertension or diabetes. He quit smoking 2 years ago, but before that, he had a smoking history of 40 pack-year. He has no travel history and works as a lawyer. He was on atorvastatin 20 mg once daily and aspirin 300 mg once daily.

Upon physical examination, he looked mildly ill. His blood pressure was 114/69 mmHg, pulse rate 90/minute, temperature 38 ○C, respiratory rate 18/minute, and pulse oximeter 97%. He had no rash, no lymphadenopathy, no murmurs, no hepatosplenomegaly, no nail changes, no meningeal signs, and no joint changes. He had no abdominal or back tenderness. His neurological and musculoskeletal exams were completely intact. Laboratory studies showed normal complete blood count, elevated liver enzymes, and elevated inflammatory markers (Table 1). Possible causes of prolonged fever in this setting included malignancy, autoimmune disorders, and infections —ranging from general infectious conditions such as endocarditis, deep-seated abscesses, and tuberculosis to regionally relevant infections such as brucellosis.

Chest computed tomography (CT) showed a markedly enlarged thyroid consistent with multinodular goiter causing a mass effect, without evidence of abscess formation. An abdominal CT scan showed abdominal aortic wall irregularity with asymmetrical thickening on the left side of the aortic wall. Three days after admission, blood cultures grew Brucella melitensis, and Brucella serum agglutination titers (SAT) came back markedly positive (Table 1). On subsequent questioning, the patient mentioned the following remarks: that, in the last six months, he used to drink unpasteurized milk from a local shop; that he did not complain of any neck swellings, hotness, or tenderness; that his mother had goiter for which she underwent surgical removal; and that his sister had just been diagnosed with brucellosis. Also, on subsequent clinical examination, the patient was found to have a palpable goiter.

Thyroid ultrasound showed multiple heterogeneous nodules of TIRADs (Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data Systems) classifications 3 and 4. Thyroid function tests (TFTs) showed low thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) and a normal thyroxine (T4) (Table 1). Testing for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) was negative. An echocardiogram was normal and did not show any evidence of endocarditis. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the dorsal and lumbosacral spine showed abdominal aortitis with abnormal surrounding enhancement indicating inflammation (Fig. 1A), spondylodiskitis at L3-L4 vertebrae (Fig. 1B), and bilateral sacroiliitis (Fig. 1C).

The patient was started on intravenous gentamicin, oral rifampin, and oral doxycycline. Shortly after that, the patient experienced fever defervescence and improvement in his general condition. The patient was offered the option of surgical intervention. However, after being informed of the high surgical risk and the absence of indications for urgent intervention, he refused the surgical procedure and preferred the option of treatment with antibiotics and being observed clinically. He was discharged three weeks after starting therapy.

Two months after discharge, the TFTs became normal (Table 1). Testing for anti-thyroglobulin was positive, and for anti-thyroid peroxidase was negative (Table 1). Fine-needle aspiration of the thyroid nodules showed benign follicular nodules bilaterally with numerous macrophages, neutrophils, and lymphocytes (Bethesda 2 according to The Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology (TBS-RTC).

One year after discharge, the patient remained symptom free, except for mild back pain. His Brucella SAT became negative. His ESR, CRP, TSH, and T4 became normal (Table 1). The thickening of the aortitis had decreased with emergence of focal aneurysmal dilatation of an ulcerative lesion (Fig. 2A), the para-aortic lymphadenopathy had regressed (Fig. 2A), and the sacroiliitis had improved (Fig. 2C). Subsequently, his antibiotics were stopped.

Two years after discharge, he continued to do clinically well. His Brucella SAT was negative. His CRP and ESR were normal (Table 1). The patient remained euthyroid, and the goiter persisted. His TFTs were normal (Table 1). Antibodies for anti-thyroglobulin and for anti-thyroid peroxidase were negative. Tests for rapid plasma reagin, HLA-B27, and serum antineutrophil cytoplasmic autoantibodies (ANCA) were all negative (Table 1). HLA typing showed A2/A33, B40/B51, Cw15/Cw15, DR17(3)/DR16, and DQ2/DQ5(1) haplotypes. The MRI and CT showed moderate improvement with areas of persistent inflammation (Fig. 3) and (Fig. 4). Therefore, because of the concern that the infection was still present, the patient was restarted on antibiotics. However, three months after restarting the antibiotics, an F-18 FDG-positron emission tomography/computed tomography (FDG-PET/CT) scan was done, and showed no evidence of hypermetabolic lesions and therefore the antibiotics were subsequently discontinued.

At the three-year follow-up after discharge, the patient remained clinically well. His treatment course included oral rifampin (600 mg once daily), oral doxycycline (100 mg twice daily), and intravenous gentamicin (300 mg once daily) during the first two weeks of therapy. This was followed by continued rifampin and doxycycline for one year. He was then off therapy for one year, after which retreatment with rifampin and doxycycline was administered for three months. The patient has been off therapy for the past nine months.

Discussion

The patient here presented with a prolonged fever and was eventually found have brucellosis due to B. miltensis. He was also found to have abdominal aortitis, subacute thyroiditis, bilateral sacroiliitis, and lumbar spondylodiscitis. Of note, the diagnosis of these complications including the aortitis and thyroiditis might have been missed if the diagnosis of brucellosis had been established, by the Brucella blood culture and SAT results, prior to performing the CT scan. In such a case, the fever would likely have been attributed to brucellosis, and additional imaging may not have been pursued. Previous reports similarly have indicated that aortitis due to brucellosis carries a higher incidence of missed diagnosis [1, 7].

Brucella aortitis is a rare and life-threatening condition, with 166 cases reported to date [5, 7–16] and a mortality rate of 12.3% [8]. Interestingly, the incidence of Brucella aortitis is likely underestimated in developing countries because hyperendemic regions, such as the Middle East and Latin America, are underrepresented in the published reports. Such underestimation may result from limited diagnostic capabilities and scientific or financial barriers to publication [1, 8].

Risk factors for aortitis include male sex, age >50 years, atherosclerosis, diabetes, congenital vascular malformation, history of syphilis, cystic medial necrosis, immunocompromised status, smoking, hypertension, alcohol consumption, steroid treatment, and chemotherapy [17, 18]. Infective aortitis can originate from the bloodstream, septic emboli, or infected surrounding tissues [9, 19]. Brucellosis may cause severe inflammatory reaction by infection of abnormal vascular tissues, such as intimal defect caused by atherosclerosis, activating endothelial cells, up-regulating adhesion molecules, and secreting pro-inflammatory chemokines, leading to severe inflammatory reactions. As a result of infection, artery endothelium is damaged, and the middle layer is exposed to the blood flow, then leading to the development of arterial ulcers, aortic dissection, aortic aneurysm, and aortic rupture [20]. Here, the patient had several risk factors for aortitis, including male sex, age, smoking, and atherosclerotic disease. The aortitis presented as an arterial wall inflammation with an ulcerative lesion and most likely resulted from contiguous spread of infection from the adjacent lumbar spondylodiscitis.

The differential diagnosis for fever, aortitis, and spondylodiscitis, aside from Brucellosis, includes tuberculosis, pyogenic spondylodiscitis, syphilis, axial spondyloarthropathy, and ANCA associated vasculitides [19]. Brucella was favored as the causative agent based on the compatible clinical presentation, isolation of the Brucella from blood, favorable response to therapy, and decrease in the inflammatory markers. Negative RPR ruled out syphilis [19], negative HLA-B27 along with insufficient criteria excluded axial spondyloarthropathy [19, 21], and negative ANCA excluded ANCA-associated vasculitides [19].

Brucella aortitis is typically managed with antibiotics and surgery. Indications for surgical intervention include hemodynamic instability, limb ischemia, and impending aneurysmal rupture [22]. However, both open surgery and endovascular repair for infected aneurysms are associated with higher complication rates [5]. In this patient, antibiotic treatment alone with close monitoring was preferred because of the absence of clear indications for surgery and because of the high surgical risk due to the underlying cardiovascular comorbidities. The patient ultimately achieved recovery, demonstrated by a progressive reduction in inflammation on serial CT and MRI scans, and confirmed, at two years after starting therapy, by a negative FDG PET/CT scan. In aortic aneurysm, FDG PET/CT scan has proven valuable for detecting infection, monitoring the effectiveness of antibiotics, and determining the optimal treatment duration [23].

Thyroid involvement in Brucella is an extremely rare complication, and fewer than 20 patients have been reported thus far in the literature [6, 24]. Presentations were mostly reported as thyroid abscess and, to a lesser extent, as acute, subacute, chronic suppurative, recurrent thyroiditis, or acute non-suppurative thyroiditis [6]. Here, we suggest that Brucella infection triggered subacute thyroiditis, in a pre-existing multinodular goiter, resulting in transient thyrotoxicosis. The anti-thyroglobulin antibodies were initially positive and became negative within a few months, indicating a brief autoimmune response secondary to the infection. Subacute thyroiditis is a self-limiting disease caused by viral or postviral agents, and a potential cause could be linked to an overactive immune response [25]. In this patient, Brucella infection may have initiated such a response. Previous studies have shown that Brucella can trigger multiple immunologic reactions, which in some cases can result in severe systemic complications or even death [26]. Furthermore, the presence of HLA-A2, HLA-B40, and HLA DR17 alleles might have predisposed the patient to this autoimmune response [27, 28].

This report is limited by being a single case, making it difficult to generalize our findings regarding the treatment of infected aneurysms with antibiotics alone. We therefore continue to emphasize the important role of surgical intervention in the management of such cases.

Conclusion

Physicians should be aware of the complications of aortitis and thyroiditis in patients with brucellosis as these complications are serious and can be missed. For patients with aortitis, medical therapy alone with close observation can be an option for those who are at high surgical risk. In addition, the development of subacute thyroiditis in the setting of brucellosis might be secondary to an autoimmune response.
