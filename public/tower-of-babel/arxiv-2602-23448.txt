Additive One Approximation for Minimum Degree Spanning Tree: Breaking the O ⁡ ( m ​ n ) O(mn) Time Barrier
Sayan Bhattacharya, Ermiya Farokhnejad, Haoze Wang

Abstract
We consider the “minimum degree spanning tree” problem. As input, we receive an undirected, connected graph G = ( V , E ) G=(V,E) with n n nodes and m m edges, and our task is to find a spanning tree T T of G G that minimizes max u ∈ V ⁡ deg T ⁡ ( u ) \max_{u\in V}\deg_{T}(u) , where deg T ⁡ ( u ) \deg_{T}(u) denotes the degree of u ∈ V u\in V in T T . The problem is known to be NP-hard. In the early 1990s, an influential work by Fürer and Raghavachari presented a local search algorithm that runs in O ~ ​ ( m ​ n ) \tilde{O}(mn) time, and returns a spanning tree with maximum degree at most Δ ⋆ + 1 \Delta^{\star}+1 , where Δ ⋆ \Delta^{\star} is the optimal objective. This remained the state-of-the-art runtime bound for computing an additive one approximation, until now. We break this O ⁡ ( m ​ n ) O(mn) runtime barrier dating back to three decades, by providing a deterministic algorithm that returns an additive one approximate optimal spanning tree in O ~ ​ ( m ​ n 3 / 4 ) \tilde{O}(mn^{3/4}) time. This constitutes a substantive progress towards answering an open question that has been repeatedly posed in the literature [Pettie’2016, Duan and Pettie’2020, Saranurak’2024]. Our algorithm is based on a novel application of the blocking flow paradigm.

Technical Overview

The
[
5
]
algorithm starts with an arbitrary spanning tree
T
of the input graph
G
, and then in each successive iteration updates
T
to reduce its number of nodes with maximum degree. At all times, it is ensured that
T
remains a spanning tree of
G
. We, however, approach the problem from a different perspective: We start with a valid forest
\mathcal{F}
where each node is a singleton component, and
E\left(\mathcal{F}\right)=\emptyset
. In each successive iteration, we update
\mathcal{F}
(by inserting/deleting some edges in it) so as to reduce the number of components of
\mathcal{F}
. Throughout our algorithm, we ensure that
\mathcal{F}
remains a valid forest. We terminate when
\mathcal{F}
consists of one component, i.e., when
\mathcal{F}
becomes a spanning tree of
G
.

The rest of this section is organized as follows. In
Section

3.1
, we present and analyze the
[
5
]
algorithm from our new perspective. Subsequently, in
Section

3.2
, we present a key technical insight under a simplifying assumption, which hints at the possibility of obtaining a “polynomial improvement” over the runtime bound of
[
5
]
. Finally, in
Section

3.3
, we provide a very high-level sketch of several ideas that are needed to get rid of the simplifying assumption.

3.1
The
[
5
]
Algorithm (From a New Perspective)

The algorithm starts with a trivial valid forest
\mathcal{F}
, where each node
v\in V
is a singleton component and
E(\mathcal{F})=\emptyset
. Subsequently, it runs for
n-1

iterations
. Each iteration invokes
Lemma

3.1
to reduce the number of components of
\mathcal{F}
by one, while ensuring that
\mathcal{F}
continues to remain a valid forest. At the end of the last iteration, there is only one component in
\mathcal{F}
, and hence
\mathcal{F}
becomes a spanning tree of
G
with maximum degree at most
\Delta^{\star}+1
. Since each iteration takes
\tilde{O}(m)
time and there are
n-1

\tilde{O}(mn)
.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
5
]
)
.

There exists a deterministic algorithm that, given a valid forest
\mathcal{F}
(which is not a spanning tree), modifies
\mathcal{F}
(by inserting/deleting some edges) so that
\mathcal{F}
continues to remain a valid forest, but with one fewer component than before. The algorithm runs in
\tilde{O}(m)
time.

Proof.

(Sketch)
We consider a trivial molecular decomposition
\mathcal{M}_{\text{tr}}
of
\mathcal{F}
where each component of
\mathcal{F}
is a special molecule, and there is no normal molecule.
We compute all the
\mathcal{M}_{\text{tr}}
-atoms.
Now, we say that an edge

u
and
v

\mathcal{M}_{\text{tr}}
-atoms in two different components of
\mathcal{F}
.
By iterating over all edges of

, we find a suitable edge

.
Next, we apply

-atoms containing

.
Finally, we insert the edge into

, and terminate.
It is easy to verify that

To prove the correctness of the algorithm, it now remains to show that we can always find an augmenting edge

Correctness.

For the sake of contradiction, suppose that there exists a valid forest
\mathcal{F}
with
f>1

Let

Observe that if we delete all the nodes in

-atom.
Now, consider the collection

-atoms of
\mathcal{F}

Since

|\mathcal{C}|\geq f+\Delta^{\star}\cdot|N|.

(3.1)

Since we have assumed that there is no augmenting edge, there is no edge in

\mathcal{F}

-atoms within the same component of

Now, consider the optimal spanning tree

\Delta^{\star}\cdot|N|\geq|\mathcal{C}|-1\geq f+\Delta^{\star}\cdot|N|-1.

Intuitively,
3.2
says that the size (in terms of the number of nodes) of every component in the forest
\mathcal{F}
is at most a constant times the average size of a component. Within this context,
Lemma

3.3
captures a key technical insight, and points out how to implement a phase.

Breaking the
O(mn)
Time Barrier.

We start with a valid forest
\mathcal{F}
with
f=n
components and
E(\mathcal{F})=\emptyset
. Suppose that we get extremely lucky, in the sense that
3.2
continues to hold all the time. Then, as long as
f\geq n^{0.51}
, we invoke
Lemma

3.3
to reduce the number of components in
\mathcal{F}
by
\Omega(f^{2}/n)=\Omega\left(n^{0.02}\right)
, which takes
\tilde{O}(m)
time. In other words, we spend
amortized

\tilde{O}\left(m/n^{0.02}\right)
time to reduce the number of components of
\mathcal{F}
by one. This is precisely the source of our “polynomial advantage” over the
[
5
]
algorithm. Overall, the total time we spend until
f
becomes smaller than the threshold
n^{0.51}
is at most
\tilde{O}\left(mn/n^{0.02}\right)=\tilde{O}\left(mn^{0.98}\right)
. Subsequently, when
f
becomes smaller than
n^{0.51}
, we switch back to the
[
5
]
algorithm as outlined in
Section

3.1
. From this point onward, we spend
\tilde{O}(m)
time per iteration, to reduce the number of components of
\mathcal{F}
by one. So, overall it takes
\tilde{O}\left(mn^{0.51}\right)
time to reduce the number of components of
\mathcal{F}
all the way down to one, at which point our algorithm terminates. Clearly, the overall runtime of the entire procedure is at most
\tilde{O}\left(mn^{0.98}\right)+\tilde{O}\left(mn^{0.51}\right)=\tilde{O}\left(mn^{0.98}\right)
.

In fact, a more refined analysis here would imply that this algorithm actually runs in
\tilde{O}(m\sqrt{n})
time.
We, however, skip this more refined analysis, since (i) our goal in this technical overview is only to highlight the key ideas that lead us to break the
O(mn)
time barrier, and (ii) if we remove our simplifying assumption, then we get a slightly weaker guarantee than that of
Lemma

3.3
, which eventually gives us a runtime bound of
\tilde{O}\left(mn^{3/4}\right)
.

Lemma 3.3
.

There is a deterministic algorithm that, given a valid forest
\mathcal{F}
satisfying
3.2
as input, modifies
\mathcal{F}
(by inserting/deleting some edges) in such a way that
\mathcal{F}
continues to remain a valid forest, but with
\Omega(f^{2}/n)
fewer components than before. The algorithm runs in
\tilde{O}(m)
time.

Lemma 3.5

Lemma 3.6

Proof.

(Sketch)
Immediately after we invoke the degree-reduction subroutine (see

3.3
Overview of Our Algorithm (Without Any Simplifying Assumption)

Let us highlight three crucial properties that we used to derive the algorithm for
Lemma

3.3
.

1.

Under
3.2
, the process of degree reduction of an
\mathcal{M}
-reducible node
u
(see
Lemma

2.1
) only affects
O(n/f)
nodes of the forest
\mathcal{F}
, as the component of
\mathcal{F}
containing
u
has size
O(n/f)
.

2.

We compute a
blocking set
of augmenting edges in
\tilde{O}(m)
time (see
Lemma

3.5
). Specifically, there does
not
exist any augmenting edge at the end of the last round of the algorithm for
Lemma

3.3
.
This is reminiscent of the classical blocking flow algorithm for the maxflow problem.

3.

Any
arbitrary
blocking set of augmenting edges (not necessarily the one returned by the algorithm) is of size
\Omega(f^{2}/n)
.
This follows from
Lemma

3.5
.

In the remainder of this section, we present a high-level overview of the main challenges that arise while attempting to ensure these properties in the general case (when
3.2
might not hold), and explain how we overcome these challenges.

Challenge I: Non-Uniform Components.
In the absence of
3.2
, we need to deal with the fact that some components of the initial forest
\mathcal{F}
might not be of size
O(n/f)
.
As a result, a single call to the degree-reduction subroutine (see
Lemma

2.1
) might even affect
\Omega(n)
nodes if the concerned component of
\mathcal{F}
happens to be of size
\Omega(n)
.

To address this challenge, we chop up every “large” (of size
\Omega(n/f)
) component
C
of
\mathcal{F}
into a collection of mutually node-disjoint “small” molecules, each of size
O(n/f)
.
We call the resulting structure a
\theta
-molecular decomposition
(see
Section

5
), with
\theta=O(n/f)
.
We work with the atoms w.r.t. these small molecules.
Thus, even if a component
C
of
\mathcal{F}
is large, when we reduce the degree of a node
u
in
C
, it only affects at most
O(n/f)
nodes in the molecule containing
u
, and it remains possible in future to reduce the degrees of other nodes that are in different small molecules of
C
.

Challenge II: Size of a Blocking Set of Augmenting Edges.

Unfortunately, we can no longer lower bound the size of a blocking set of augmenting edges as in
Lemma

3.5
, for the following reason.
It might happen that a node
v
in a component
C
of
\mathcal{F}
is reducible w.r.t. the entire component
C
, but is
not
reducible w.r.t. the concerned small molecule of size
O(n/f)
which contains
v
in our
\theta
-molecular decomposition.
As a result, lots of augmenting edges (as per
Definition

3.4
) can no longer be considered as augmenting.

To address this challenge, we define an intricate object called an
augmenting chain
(see
Definition

4.2
and
Definition

4.4
). To get some intuition behind this definition, consider a non-forest edge
(u,v)\in E-E(\mathcal{F})
between an
\mathcal{M}
-reducible node
u
, and an
\mathcal{M}
-non-reducible node
v
which lies outside of the component of
u
.
Let us refer to such an edge
(u,v)
as a
bad
edge. Note that if
we encounter a bad edge
(u,v)
, then we can try to perform the following operations.

Tiny Swap:

1.

Insert the edge
(u,v)
into the forest
\mathcal{F}
.

2.

Consider a sub-tree
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{xv}
of
\mathcal{F}
, where
(x,v)\in E(\mathcal{F})
. Delete the edge
(x,v)
from
\mathcal{F}
, so that the degree of
v
remains
\leq\Delta^{\star}+1
and the tree
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{xv}
gets disconnected from the rest of the forest
\mathcal{F}
.

3.

Try to search for edges that can reconnect
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{xv}
back to the rest of the forest
\mathcal{F}
.

Morally, an augmenting chain consists of a sequence of tiny swaps as described above, until we find an edge connecting two molecules which is an augmenting edge.

We derive an analogue of
Lemma

3.5
, by considering augmenting chains of length
O(n/f)
.
More precisely, we show that any
blocking set
of
O(n/f)
-length augmenting chains has size at least
\Omega(f^{3}/n^{2})
(see
Lemma

5.2
).
Here, the phrase “blocking set” refers to the property that we can
not
find any augmenting chain of length
O(n/f)
, after we have successively applied all the chains in to the concerned set.
Note that this bound is slightly weaker than
Lemma

3.5
. This is because applying an augmenting chain of length
\Omega(n/f)
can affect
\Omega(n^{2}/f^{2})
nodes, but applying an augmenting edge only affects
O(n/f)
nodes.
But this weaker guarantee is still sufficient for us to break the
O(mn)
time barrier.

Challenge III: Finding a Blocking Set of Augmenting Chains Algorithmically.

Our definition of an augmenting chain is quite complex, and searching for such objects is non-trivial, especially since we need to find a blocking set of them within a limited time.
Moreover, after improving the forest
\mathcal{F}
by some tiny swaps, the validity of the remaining augmenting chains may be cast into doubt.

We provide an intricate algorithm (see
Section

7
) that searches for augmenting chains by exploring potential tiny swaps sequentially for
n/f
layers, until it finds augmenting chains of length
\simeq n/f
.
The algorithm is involved, since we do not know in advance which layer of tiny swaps an edge
(u,v)
might contribute to.
Along the way, we need to prove some interesting structural properties of augmenting chains.
The resulting procedure which addresses Challenge III runs in
\tilde{O}(mn/f)
time, where the extra
n/f
factor overhead in runtime (in comparison with
Lemma

3.3
) arises because the augmenting chains are now of length
O(n/f)
.

Putting Everything Together.

As per the above discussions, we bypass
3.2
, and provide an algorithm with guarantees analogous to that of
Lemma

3.3
. In
\tilde{O}(mn/f)
time, this allows us to reduce the number of components of the forest
\mathcal{F}
by
\Omega(f^{3}/n^{2})
.
Overall, this leads to an additive one approximate minimum degree spanning tree algorithm that runs in
\tilde{O}(mn^{3/4})
time (see
Section

5
).

Alternating and Augmenting Chains

Our definitions of alternating and augmenting chains are a bit intricate. To ease into them, we start with a few helpful terminologies. Consider any
valid
forest
\mathcal{F}
, and any molecular decomposition
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathcal{F}
. For every pair of distinct nodes

within the same component of
\mathcal{F}
, we refer to

as an
\mathcal{M}
-block
iff the following two conditions hold:

•

is either
\mathcal{M}
-non-reducible, or the root of a normal
\mathcal{M}
-molecule.

•

is contained in an
\mathcal{M}
-molecule.

Observation 4.1
.

Consider any molecular decomposition
\mathcal{M}
of a valid forest
\mathcal{F}
. For every
\mathcal{M}
-atom
C
and
\mathcal{M}
-block

. In other words, an
\mathcal{M}
-block cannot have a non-trivial intersection with an
\mathcal{M}
-atom.

Proof.

If the node
y
is the root of a normal
\mathcal{M}
-molecule, then the observation is trivial.
Thus, for the rest of the proof, suppose that the node
y
is
\mathcal{M}
-non-reducible and belongs to some molecule

of
\mathcal{M}
.
Each
\mathcal{M}
-atom in
T
is a connected sub-tree of

, and any two distinct
\mathcal{M}
-atoms are mutually node-disjoint. By definition, the node

is not in any of these
\mathcal{M}
-atoms.
This implies that

can not have a non-trivial intersection with any
\mathcal{M}
-atom.
∎

Throughout the rest of this paper, we say that a triple
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{M},D)
is a
valid configuration
iff
\mathcal{F}
is a
valid
forest,
\mathcal{M}
is a molecular decomposition of
\mathcal{F}
, and

is a

of
\mathcal{M}
-free nodes satisfying the following condition: Every node

. We refer to the nodes in

as

(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{M},D)
.

Our algorithm works by repeatedly applying a sequence of augmenting chains to reduce the number of components in a valid forest by a large additive factor. Before describing the algorithm, we need to introduce a couple of concepts – (i) “

\theta

We say that a valid configuration
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{M},D)
is a
t

(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{M},D)
is at least
t
.
Next, fix any integer
\theta\in[1,n]
, and consider any molecular decomposition
\mathcal{M}
of a valid forest
\mathcal{F}
. We say that
\mathcal{M}
is a
\theta
-molecular decomposition
of
\mathcal{F}
iff the following conditions hold.

1.

Every component of
\mathcal{F}
with at most
2\theta

\mathcal{M}
-molecule.

2.

For every normal
\mathcal{M}
-molecule
M
, we have
|V(M)|\leq\theta
;

3.

For any arbitrary molecule
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{x\leftarrow y}
of
\mathcal{F}
which is not part of
\mathcal{M}
and
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{x\leftarrow y}
contains at least one
\mathcal{M}
-free node, we must have

.

Intuitively, we can compute a
\theta
-molecular decomposition
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathcal{F}
in near-linear time in a bottom-up manner, starting from the leaves in
\mathcal{F}
.
To see how this works, say that a component of
\mathcal{F}
is
small
if it contains at most
2\theta
nodes, and

otherwise.
We start by designating every small component of
\mathcal{F}
as a special
\mathcal{M}
-molecules, and every leaf node in a big component of
\mathcal{F}
as a singleton normal
\mathcal{M}
-molecule.
Subsequently, as long as there exists an edge
(u,v)\in E(\mathcal{F})
in a big component of
\mathcal{F}
such that
\left|V\left(T_{uv}^{\mathcal{F}}\right)\right|\leq\theta
and

is an
\mathcal{M}
-molecule for all neighbors

\left\{T_{wu}^{\mathcal{F}}\right\}_{w\in\psi_{\mathcal{F}}(u)\setminus\{v\}}
from
\mathcal{M}
and insert the molecule

into
\mathcal{M}
.
The procedure terminates when no more molecules can be added to
\mathcal{M}
in this manner.

This is correct for the initial

Now, assume that it is violated for the first time while the procedure removes

5.1
Description of Our Algorithm

We start by initializing
\mathcal{F}
to be a forest with
n
components and an empty edge-set, i.e. with
E(\mathcal{F})\leftarrow\emptyset
and
V(\mathcal{F})\leftarrow V
. Subsequently, our algorithm consists of two stages.

Stage I.

Let
f
denote the number of components of the valid forest
\mathcal{F}
.
As long as
f\geq n^{3/4}
, we keep implementing the next
round
of our algorithm. Each round consists of the following steps.

•

Set
H:=\lceil 20n/f\rceil
,
\theta=\lceil 20n/f\rceil
,
\mathcal{F}^{(0)}:=\mathcal{F}
, and
D^{(0)}:=\emptyset
.

•

Compute a
\theta
-molecular decomposition
\mathcal{M}^{(0)}
of
\mathcal{F}^{(0)}
.

(Observe that
(\mathcal{F}^{(0)},\mathcal{M}^{(0)},D^{(0)})
is a
1
-configuration.)

•

For every
\ell\in[1,H]

–

Call the subroutine from
Lemma

5.3
on the
\ell
-configuration
\left(\mathcal{F}^{(\ell-1)},\mathcal{M}^{(\ell-1)},D^{(\ell-1)}\right)
, which returns an
(\ell+1)
-configuration
(\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)},\mathcal{M}^{(\ell)},D^{(\ell)})
after applying a sequence of
s_{\ell}
augmenting chains (each of length
\ell
) w.r.t.
(\mathcal{F}^{(\ell-1)},\mathcal{M}^{(\ell-1)},D^{(\ell-1)})
.

•

At the end of the above
for loop
, we clearly have an
(H+1)
-configuration
(\mathcal{F}^{(H)},\mathcal{M}^{(H)},D^{(H)})
, which is derived by applying a sequence of
\sum_{\ell=1}^{H}s_{\ell}
augmenting chains w.r.t.
(\mathcal{F}^{(0)},\mathcal{M}^{(0)},D^{(0)})
. We now reset
\mathcal{F}\leftarrow\mathcal{F}^{(H)}
, and terminate this round.

Stage II.
We initiate this stage when we observe that the current valid forest
\mathcal{F}
has
f<n^{3/4}
components at the end of a given round.
We implement this stage in
iterations
. Each iteration reduces the number of components of
\mathcal{F}
by one, while ensuring that
\mathcal{F}
remains valid, by invoking
Lemma

3.1
.
This stage ends when
\mathcal{F}
consists of only one component, and hence is a spanning tree of
G
with maximum degree at most
\Delta^{\star}+1
.
Our algorithm returns this spanning tree as the output.

5.2
Analysis of Our Algorithm: Proof of
Theorem

1.1

From the description of our algorithm, it is obvious that it returns a spanning tree of maximum degree at most
\Delta^{\star}+1
.
Indeed,

Lemma

5.3
and
Lemma

3.1
guarantee that
\mathcal{F}
always remains a
valid
forest (i.e., has maximum degree at most
\Delta^{\star}+1
), and we terminate when
\mathcal{F}
has only one component. We devote the rest of this section towards analyzing the runtime of our algorithm.

To begin with, it is easy to see that Stage II of our algorithm takes
\tilde{O}(mn^{3/4})
time. This is because Stage II consists of at most
n^{3/4}
iterations, and each iteration takes
\tilde{O}(m)
as per
Lemma

3.1
. It now remains to show that Stage I of our algorithm also takes at
\tilde{O}(mn^{3/4})
time.

Consider any given round in Stage I. The round starts with a valid configuration
(\mathcal{F}^{(0)},\mathcal{M}^{(0)},D^{(0)})
, where
D^{(0)}=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{M}^{(0)}
is a
\theta
-molecular decomposition of
\mathcal{F}^{(0)}
.
The round ends with an
(H+1)
-configuration
(\mathcal{F}^{(H)},\mathcal{M}^{(H)},D^{(H)})
, after applying a sequence of
\sum_{\ell=1}^{H}s_{\ell}
augmenting chains (each of length
\leq H
) w.r.t.
(\mathcal{F}^{(0)},\mathcal{M}^{(0)},D^{(0)})
. Accordingly, by
Lemma

5.2
, there are at least
\sum_{\ell=1}^{H}s_{\ell}\geq f^{3}/(10^{5}\cdot n^{2})
many augmenting chains in this sequence. In other words, each round reduces the number of components in
\mathcal{F}
by at least
f^{3}/(10^{5}\cdot n^{2})
. Furthermore, by
Lemma

5.3
, each round takes
\tilde{O}(mH)=\tilde{O}(mn/f)
time. This holds because the concerned
for loop
in any given round runs for
H
iterations, and each iteration makes one call to the subroutine guaranteed by
Lemma

5.3
.

To summarize, each round takes
\tilde{O}(mn/f)
time, and reduces the number of components of
\mathcal{F}
from
f
to at most
f-f^{3}/(10^{5}\cdot n^{2})
.
Suppose that Stage I lasts for exactly
t\geq 1
rounds.
For each
i\in[1,t]
, let
f_{i}
denote the number of components of the forest
\mathcal{F}
just after the
i^{\text{th}}
round.
Let
f_{0}=n
.
Thus, by definition, we have
f_{i}\geq n^{3/4}
for all
i\in[0,t-1]
and
f_{t}<n^{3/4}
.
From the above discussion, it follows that
f_{i+1}\leq f_{i}-f_{i}^{3}/(10^{5}\cdot n^{2})
for all
i\in[0,t-1]
.
We now derive that

\frac{1}{f_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{f_{i+1}}\cdot\left(1-\frac{f_{i}^{2}}{10^{5}n^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{f_{i+1}}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{10^{5}\sqrt{n}}\right),

where the last inequality holds because
f_{i}\geq n^{3/4}
for all
i\in[0,t-1]
.
Hence, we get

\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\frac{1}{f_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{f_{t-1}}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{1}{10^{5}\sqrt{n}}\right)^{i}\leq\frac{1}{n^{3/4}}\cdot(10^{5}\sqrt{n})=O\!\left(n^{-1/4}\right).

Accordingly, we infer that the total time spent during Stage I is at most

\tilde{O}(\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}mn/f_{i})=\tilde{O}(mn^{3/4})
.
This gives us the desired runtime bound of
Theorem

1.1
.

6.2
A Potential Function Argument

To prove

Lemma 6.2

Lemma 6.3
.

Consider any index

denote the set of

-avoiding molecules of

that are

2.

\left|\mathcal{M}(W(t))\right|\geq f/2+(\Delta^{\star}-1)\cdot|W(t)|

\left|\mathcal{M}(W(t))-\mathcal{M}_{\texttt{atom}}(W(t))\right|\leq f/4

Proof.

From

, we infer that

\displaystyle\Delta^{\star}\cdot|W(t+1)|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{M}_{\texttt{atom}}(W(t))\right|+|W(t)|-1

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{M}(W(t))\right|-\left(\left|\mathcal{M}(W(t))-\mathcal{M}_{\texttt{atom}}(W(t))\right|\right)+|W(t)|-1

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle f/2+(\Delta^{\star}-1)\cdot|W(t)|-f/4+|W(t)|-1

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle f/4-1+\Delta^{\star}\cdot|W(t)|

\displaystyle f/5+\Delta^{\star}\cdot|W(t)|.

. This concludes the proof of the corollary.
∎

Proof of

.

\Delta^{\star}\cdot|W(H)|\geq fH/5+\Delta^{\star}\cdot|W(0)|\geq fH/5.

Running Time (Implementation Details)

Time Spent on Constructing Layers.

Recall the labels ‘scanned’ and ‘unscanned’ for the nodes.
Assume
x\in Z_{t}
and we want to scan the sub-tree
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{x}
.
We can easily determine the set of unscanned nodes inside
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{x}
by maintaining a label for each node
y\in V
that indicates whether or not
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{y}
is scanned.
As a result, in order to iterate over the set of unscanned nodes inside
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{x}
, we can do a simple DFS, and whenever we reach a node
y
such that
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{y}
is already scanned, we ignore the entire sub-tree
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{y}
within
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{x}
.

This concludes that the running time of scanning the nodes in
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{x}
is actually proportional to the number of unscanned nodes in there, i.e.,
O(1+\#\{\text{unscanned nodes in }T^{\mathcal{F}}_{x}\})
.
According to
7.6
, we consider each subtree
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{x}
at most once for every fixed node
x
.
Hence, the total time for scanning nodes during the construction of the layers is
\tilde{O}(n)
.

For every unscanned node
u
that is contained in an atom of
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{x}
, we iterate over all neighbors
v\in\psi_{G}(u)
only once.
For every neighbor
v\in\psi(u)
, we must check whether
v\in V(T^{\mathcal{F}}_{u\leftarrow x})
.
This property can be easily performed in
\tilde{O}(1)
time by simple data structures on rooted trees.
More precisely, we maintain the depth of nodes (according to their depth from roots of molecules to leaves), and save the ancestors of depth
(\text{depth}(x)-2^{i})
for each
i\geq 0
.
Then we can find the unique ancestors of
u
and
v
at depth
\text{depth}(x)+1
in
\tilde{O}(1)
time.
These ancestors are equal if and only if
v\in V(T^{\mathcal{F}}_{u\leftarrow x})
.

As a result, the total time spent for constructing the layers
Z_{0},Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{\ell-1}
is at most
\tilde{O}(m)
.

Time Spent on the Pruning Procedure.

In the pruning procedure of
Z_{t}
, some of the nodes in
Z_{t}
might be removed from this set.
We can simply charge the running time of removing the node
v
from
Z_{t}
to the edge
(u,v)
that makes the algorithm put
v
into
Z_{t}
.
All of these edges are distinct according to the argument in the previous paragraph.
As a result, the total time spent in Phase I of the algorithm is at most
\tilde{O}(m)
.

Time Spent on Searching For
(w_{\ell-1},z_{\ell})
.

Since the algorithm for searching the edges
(w_{\ell-1},z_{\ell})
is similar to what it does for constructing the layers, the running time of this part is
\tilde{O}(m)
as well.

Time Spent on Finding Potential Effective Ancestors in the Backward Search.

Recall the labels ‘effective’ and ‘ineffective’ for the nodes.
Consider a
t\in[0,\ell-1]
and a
w_{t}
inside a call at layer
t
of the backward search.
Below, we describe how to efficiently iterate over effective ancestors of
w_{t}
in
Z_{t}
, i.e., effective nodes
x\in Z_{t}
such that
w_{t}\in V(T^{\mathcal{F}}_{x})
.

We maintain a rooted forest
\mathcal{F}(Z_{t})
w.r.t.
Z_{t}
as follows;

•

There are exactly
|Z_{t}|
non-leaf nodes in
\mathcal{F}(Z_{t})
, each corresponding to a unique node in
Z_{t}
.

•

The leaves of
\mathcal{F}(Z_{t})
correspond to
\mathcal{M}
-atoms that are contained in at least one sub-tree
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{x}
for some
x\in Z_{t}
.

•

For each leaf
v
in
\mathcal{F}(Z_{t})
corresponding to the
\mathcal{M}
-atom
C_{v}
, the parent of
v
in
\mathcal{F}(Z_{t})
is the least ancestral node
x\in Z_{t}
such that
V(C_{v})\subseteq V(T^{\mathcal{F}}_{x})
.

•

For each non-leaf node in
\mathcal{F}(Z_{t})
corresponding to
x\in Z_{t}
, the parent of
x
in
\mathcal{F}(Z_{t})
is the least ancestral ancestor of
x
that is contained in
Z_{t}
.

•

Each node in
\mathcal{F}(Z_{t})
is either ‘effective’ or ‘ineffective’.

Note that we can construct these data structures for all
t\in[1,\ell-1]
simultaneously by performing only one DFS call at the end of Phase I.

Now, once we have a node
w_{t}
in an
\mathcal{M}
-atom corresponding to a leaf node
v
in
\mathcal{F}(Z_{t})
, we can efficiently iterate over the effective ancestors of
w_{t}
in
Z_{t}
as follows.
The algorithm considers the ancestors of
w_{t}
in the decreasing order of their depth (w.r.t. the rooted molecules), and marks them ineffective as long as no augmenting chain is found.
As a result, while considering the ancestors of an
\mathcal{M}
-atom
C
in
\mathcal{F}(Z_{t})
, if we reach an ancestor
x\in Z_{t}
which is already marked ‘ineffective’, we can stop searching for the rest of the ancestors of
C
.
The reason is that there must be another
\mathcal{M}
-atom
C^{\prime}
which satisfies
V(C^{\prime})\subseteq V(T^{\mathcal{F}}_{x})
and
x
is marked ‘ineffective’ because of
C^{\prime}
.
But, in this case, all of the ancestors of
x
in
Z_{t}
must also become ‘ineffective’ because of
C^{\prime}
.
If this is not the case, an augmenting chain must have been found with
C^{\prime}
being one of its critical
\mathcal{M}
-atom, and both of the
\mathcal{M}
-atoms
C
and
C^{\prime}
(which are in the same
\mathcal{M}
-molecule) will become free nodes, and the algorithm no longer considers
C
as an
\mathcal{M}
-atom.
Hence, the algorithm can efficiently iterate over all effective ancestors of an
\mathcal{M}
-atom in
Z_{t}
.

Number of Times a Node is Considered a Potential Ancestor.

Assume
x\in Z_{t}
is considered as a potential ancestor of
w_{t}
.
For every effective edge
(x,y)
, there might be a recursive backward search at layer
t-1
.
If this recursive call is unsuccessful, the edge triggering the call will become ineffective and will never be scanned again.
If the recursive call is successful, it means that
y
must be inside an atom, and after applying the augmenting chain
y
becomes a free node.
Hence, the next time that the edge
(x,y)
is scanned, it will become ineffective.
Hence, the total number of times that a node
x
can be considered as a potential ancestor of some node throughout the entire Phase II is at most
n_{x}=O(\deg_{\mathcal{F}^{(0)}}(x))
.

Time Spent on Exploring Potential Effective Edges.

Now, consider a node
x\in Z_{t}
as a potential ancestor of
w_{t}
.
According to the previous explanations, the effective edges incident on
x
will be scanned at most twice.
As a result, the total time spent on searching for effective edges
(x,w_{t-1})
for each fixed
x
is at most
O(n_{x}+\deg_{\mathcal{F}^{(0)}}(x))
.
Finally, by summing up these values for all
x
, we conclude that the total time spent on searching for augmenting chains in Phase II of the algorithm is at most
\tilde{O}(m)
.

Time Spent on Applying Augmenting Chains to Configurations.

Consider an augmenting chain
A=(w_{0},z_{1},w_{1},\ldots,w_{\ell-1},z_{\ell},)
.
The time spent on the degree-reduction subroutine of
w_{i}
(and possibly
z_{\ell}
if it is contained in an
\mathcal{M}
-atom) is at most
\tilde{O}(m_{w_{i}})
according to
Lemma

2.1
, where
m_{w_{i}}
is the number of edges in the
\mathcal{M}
-atom containing
w_{i}
.
Since the atoms containing
w_{i}
s are all distinct among all augmenting chains that the algorithm finds, we conclude that the total time spent on the degree-reduction subroutine is at most
\tilde{O}(m)
throughout the entire Phase II.

Finding the child
y_{i}
of
z_{i}
that satisfies
T^{\mathcal{F}}_{w_{i}\leftarrow z_{i}}=T^{\mathcal{F}}_{y_{i}\leftarrow z_{i}}
can be done easily by maintaining simple data structures on rooted trees.
The only important note here is that we do not update the data structures after changing the forest.
All of these searches can be performed according to the very initial forest
\mathcal{F}
given as input.
The reason is that the molecules containing
w_{0},w_{1},\ldots,w_{\ell}
and possibly
z_{\ell+1}
are not affected at the time of applying
A
, and the edges
(w_{i},z_{i+1}),(y_{i},z_{i})
coincide in the forest at that time and the initial forest
\mathcal{F}
.
Hence, we initialize these data structures only once.
This argument also shows that the total number of edges that we change in the forest by applying all of the augmenting chains is at most
\tilde{O}(m)
(because each edge appears in at most one augmenting chain in Phase II).
Updating
\mathcal{M}
and
D
trivially takes at most
\tilde{O}(n)
time.

Appendix C
Getting Rid of the Prior Knowledge of
\Delta^{\star}

In this section, we show how to run our algorithm without prior knowledge of
\Delta^{\star}
.

We do a binary search on the value of
\Delta^{\star}
using variable
k\in[1,n]
.
We use the generalization of our algorithm for the BDST problem with input
b(u)=k
for all
u\in V
.
If our algorithm finds a spanning tree of maximum degree
k+1
, we reduce the value of
k
according to the binary search step.
If our algorithm was unsuccessful in finding a spanning tree of degree at most
k+1
, we increase the value of
k
according to the binary search step.
Eventually, we consider the smallest value of

, where the algorithm has successfully returned a spanning tree of maximum degree at most

.
We show that
k^{\star}+1\leq\Delta^{\star}+1
.

For the sake of contradiction, assume that
k^{\star}>\Delta^{\star}
.
In this case, define
k:=k^{\star}-1\geq\Delta^{\star}
and consider the execution of the algorithm for bounds
b(u)=k
for all
u\in V

Since
k\geq\Delta^{\star}
, there exists a spanning tree
T^{\star}
satisfying
\deg_{T^{\star}}(u)\leq b(u)
for all
u\in V
, and our algorithm would successfully return a spanning tree of maximum degree
k+1
.
This is in contradiction with the assumption on the minimality of
k^{\star}
.

As a result, our algorithm eventually returns a spanning tree of maximum degree
\leq\Delta^{\star}+1
where
\Delta^{\star}
is the maximum degree of the optimal spanning tree.

Note that it is NP-Hard to find the value of
\Delta^{\star}
.
Our binary search does not find the exact value of

.
The final

\Delta^{\star}
