A machine learning-based interpretable model for predicting pancreatic cancer in chronic pancreatitis patients with focal pancreatic lesions
Authors: Jin-Hui Yi, Teng Wang, Ya-Wei Bi, Jin-Jie Xu, Miao Liu, Guang-Ming Chen, Zhao-Shen Li, Dan Wang, Ning-Li Chai, Liang-Hao Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic cancer (PC) is deadly and distinguishing it from inflammatory conditions in chronic pancreatitis (CP) patients is challenging. We aimed to develop machine learning models to predict PC in CP patients with focal pancreatic lesions.MethodsFor this bicentric retrospective study, CP patients with indeterminate focal pancreatic lesions discovered through contrast-enhanced computed tomography scans were enrolled. Final diagnosis of focal pancreatic lesions was established by surgical pathology or follow-up outcomes. We used Boruta algorithm for feature screening, and conducted six machine learning models (Logistic Regression, Random Forest, eXtreme Gradient Boosting, Light Gradient Boosting Machine, K-Nearest Neighbors and Naive Bayes). The input data this study used were clinical information and laboratory data. Finally, SHAP was employed for interpretation. Receiver operating characteristic curve, area under curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity and specificity were used to evaluate model performance.ResultsA total of 187 participants were enrolled, and 44 patients (23.5%) were diagnosed as PC. Six important features were identified by the Boruta algorithm. Among the six machine learning models based on these features, Logistic Regression had the best diagnostic performance (AUC: 0.875 (95% CI: 0.801–0.949) in the training set and 0.908 (95% CI: 0.818–0.997) in the testing set). The ranking of SHAP variables importance from highest to lowest were carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9, hemoglobin, alanine transaminase, carcinoembryonic antigen, aspartate transaminase and the maximum diameter of focal pancreatic lesions.ConclusionSix features-based machine learning models, especially the Logistic Regression, had satisfactory performance in predicting PC in CP patients with focal pancreatic lesions. This approach was crucial for enhancing early detection rate and reducing mortality associated with PC.Keywords: Pancreatic cancer, Chronic pancreatitis, Machine learning, SHAP

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC), a highly aggressive malignancy with a poor prognosis and a dismal 5-year survival rate, ranks as the sixth leading cause of cancer-related death globally [1]. Chronic pancreatitis (CP) is a progressive fibro-inflammatory disease caused by genetic, environmental or other risk factors, leading to pain, pancreatic exocrine and endocrine dysfunction, and even PC [2]. Approximately 0.7%-6.6% of CP patients would develop to PC [3–5]. Additionally, among CP patients, 27%-50% have a focal pancreatic lesion, which may indicate PC or inflammatory conditions such as mass-forming CP, focal autoimmune pancreatitis or groove pancreatitis [6]. Differentiating PC from these inflammatory conditions in CP patients remains a significant clinical challenge due to overlapping imaging findings, symptoms, and clinical presentations in the early stages.

Several methods have been employed to distinguish PC from inflammatory conditions. Computed tomography (CT) is the first-line diagnostic method, though its accuracy depends on the imaging equipment and the doctor’s experience. Tumor markers such as carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9) and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) are non-invasive diagnostic tools [7]. CA19-9 levels are elevated in over 80% of patients with advanced PC, while more than 40% CP patients with inflammatory conditions also experience elevated CA19-9 levels due to obstructive icterus [8]. Moreover, tumor markers may remain within normal limits until PC reaches an advanced stage. In recent years, endoscopic ultrasound-guided tissue acquisition has been recommended to diagnose pancreatic neoplasms, while the sensitivity for pancreatic-mass lesions in the setting of CP was only 53.5%-73.9% [9, 10]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop a noninvasive, convenient, and highly accurate diagnostic test to reliably identify PC in patients with CP, thereby avoiding the risk of diagnostic delay from false-negative results.

Machine learning (ML) has been applied to predicting diagnosis, prognosis, and outcomes in various medical fields, including heart failure, pressure injury, and Alzheimer’s disease [11–14]. It is a burgeoning field for medical researchers to analyze large datasets and identify associations between clinical data and outcomes by using algorithms, enabling predictions of future events [15]. However, ML models are often limited in medical decision-making due to “black box” trait. This means understanding how these models function and which specific features contribute to a given prediction is difficult [16]. To address this critical issue of model explainability, Lundberg et al. introduced SHapley additive exPlanations (SHAP) in 2017 to provide an intuitive explanation for ML models [17]. It is based on the SHAP value that is calculated by the individual characteristics in the context of a particular prediction to explain the contribution (positive or negative) of factors to a model’s prediction.

The primary objective of this bicentric retrospective study was to develop and validate robust ML models by using clinical information and laboratory data for predicting PC in CP patients presenting with focal pancreatic lesions. Furthermore, by integrating the SHAP framework, we aimed to furnish transparent, interpretable explanations for the models’ predictions. This approach was intended to yield a reliable and intelligible decision-support tool to facilitate future clinical translation and research.

Methods

Patients

This bicentric retrospective study included patients admitted to Changhai Hospital from December 2012 to September 2022, and patients admitted to the First Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital from January 2012 to December 2024. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of ChangHai Hospital (CHEC2025-151) and informed consent was obtained from each patient. CP patients with indeterminate focal pancreatic lesions discovered through contrast-enhanced CT scans were enrolled. For patients who had undergone several contrast-enhanced CT scans, the first scan that demonstrated a focal pancreatic lesion for each patient was chosen as baseline, and all other input data were also collected at the same time point. The diagnostic criteria for CP were based on the Asia-Pacific consensus [18].

Participants were excluded from the study if they met any of the following criteria: incomplete clinical data; no surgical pathology results for the focal pancreatic lesions and loss to follow-up, indicating that a final diagnosis of focal pancreatic lesions could not be established, diagnosis of pancreatic pseudocyst or pancreatic cystic neoplasm.

Data collection

Clinical data including age, symptoms, leukocyte, erythrocyte, hemoglobin, D-Dimer, albumin, serum bilirubin, alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), gamma glutamyltransferase (GGT), CEA and CA 19 − 9, were recorded. Details from contrast-enhanced CT scans, such as pancreatic stone(s), the maximum diameter of focal pancreatic lesions, and the location of focal pancreatic lesions, were collected.

The final diagnosis of focal pancreatic lesions was established by surgical pathology results or follow-up outcomes. For patients who underwent surgery, the final diagnosis was determined by the surgical pathology result of the resected specimen. Otherwise, the final diagnosis was confirmed by follow-up outcomes, which comprehensively considered both the clinical manifestations and additional pathological assessments obtained via endoscopic ultrasound-guided tissue acquisition or cytology brushing. Each of the patients was followed for at least 1 year or until death. If the lesions showed spontaneous shrinkage or patients did not exhibit exacerbated clinical manifestations such as anemia, weight loss, icterus or death caused by PC-related complications, the diagnosis was non-PC. If additional pathological assessment revealed PC, or if the patient died of clinically diagnosed PC, or if the patient presented with typical imaging features, such as hypodense mass with arterial phase hypoenhancement, pancreatic ductal dilation, vascular encasement or invasion, the diagnosis was PC. Notably, given the considerably high false-negative rate associated with endoscopic ultrasound-guided tissue acquisition or cytology brushing, a negative pathological finding did not permit a diagnosis of non-PC, whereas a positive finding confirmed the diagnosis.

ML model

The flow diagram for the development of ML model was displayed in Fig. 1A. Boruta algorithm was used to screen the features for ML analysis to identify all relevant features while being robust to collinearity. Boruta algorithm is a wrapper-based feature selection method built around the random forest algorithm, which systematically compares the importance of original features with randomly generated “shadow features” to identify all relevant predictors [19]. The analysis was performed in the R software using the “Boruta” package. The Boruta algorithm was executed with a maximum of 500 iterations (maxRuns = 500) using the default random forest algorithm, with the random seed set to 123 to ensure reproducibility. Based on the importance of features relative to the shadow attributes, all features were categorized as “Confirmed”, “Tentative” or “Rejected”.

The dataset was partitioned prior to model construction. Specifically, 70% of the samples were designated as the training set using the createDataPartition function from the “caret” package, while the remaining 30% were allocated to the testing set. This research employed a combination of six ML models, including Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) and Naive Bayes. For each model, hyperparameters were optimized and early stopping was employed during training where possible, using 5-fold cross-validation on training data. The main predictive performance of ML models was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) with the mean area under curve (AUC) and 95% confidence interval (CI). In addition, accuracy, specificity, and sensitivity were calculated for each model. The maximum Youden’s index was used to define an optimal cut-off value and to distinguish PC patients from non-PC patients.

We introduced SHAP to provide explanations for ML model predictions, which was based on an R package named “shapviz”. SHAP values indicated whether each feature contributes positively or negatively to the prediction. Feature importance was visualized using a SHAP summary plot, ranked by average absolute SHAP values. Individual patient predictions were explained using force plots.

Statistical analysis

For parameters with continuous variables, the normal distribution was expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and the skewed distribution was presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). The categorical variables were described using case numbers (percentage). Continuous variables following a normal distribution were compared using the 2-tailed Student’s t-test, while those with a skewed distribution were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test in different groups. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis and data visualization were conducted using R software (version 4.5.2) and SPSS 27.0 (SPSS, Chicago, Illinois, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 412 CP patients with indeterminate focal pancreatic lesions discovered through contrast-enhanced CT scans were enrolled (201 patients admitted to ChangHai Hospital, and 211 admitted to the First Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital). After screening, 225 patients were excluded (114 patients had incomplete clinical data records; 43 patients did not have final diagnosis for the focal pancreatic lesions, and 68 were diagnosed as pancreatic pseudocyst or pancreatic cystic neoplasm). Finally, 187 patients were enrolled, of whom 44 patients (23.5%) were detected PC. Among these 44 patients, 35 (79.5%) were confirmed by surgical pathology, and the remaining 9 (20.5%) patients were diagnosed based on follow-up outcomes. LR model formula

Based on the LR model, the risk score for predicting PC can be calculated using the following formula: Risk score = 3.9161 + 0.000848 × Diameter (mm) − 0.042685 × Hemoglobin (g/L) + 0.007127 × ALT (U/L) − 0.004160 × AST (U/L) + 0.115948 × CEA (ng/mL) + 0.007470 × CA19-9 (U/mL).

The predicted probability of PC was then obtained by applying the sigmoid function: P(PC) = e(Risk score) / (1 + e(Risk score)).

Discussion

Focal pancreatic lesions in CP patients encompass a diverse array of conditions, which can typically be categorized into PC or non-PC conditions [20]. Non-PC lesions are classified into mass-forming CP, focal autoimmune pancreatitis and groove pancreatitis. In our study, approximately 23.5% of patients were diagnosed as PC, which emphasized the critical need for precise differentiation between PC and non-PC focal pancreatic lesions in the setting of CP.

At present, the similarity of clinical presentation, imaging and laboratory results between them presents a challenge for clinical practice. For example, CA19-9 is currently one of the most used tumor markers for diagnosing PC with the sensitivity around 79%-81% [21, 22]. However, among CP patients, due to reasons such as the lack of Lewis enzyme and fucosyltransferase, as well as a low tumor burden, CA19-9 may yield false-negative results [23, 24]. Su et al. analyzed 34 studies and revealed that even in the absence of an elevated CA19-9, CP patients still had a 24% likelihood of developing PC [25]. Moreover, endoscopic ultrasound-guided tissue acquisition is a reliable procedure to obtain tissue diagnosis, and it has been recommended for diagnosing pancreatic malignancy. Unfortunately, the presence of acoustic shadowing from pancreatic stone(s) or extensive vascularization significantly influence its diagnostic performance in CP patients with focal pancreatic lesions [9, 10]. A meta-analysis involved 593 CP patients who underwent endoscopic ultrasound guided fine‑needle aspiration for solid pancreatic lesions and showed that the pooled sensitivity was only 65% [26]. A higher rate of missed diagnosis may result in delayed diagnosis, leading to the undiagnosed PC becoming unresectable rapidly, which ultimately causes the high mortality rates.

There is an urgent need to develop a noninvasive, readily accessible method with high diagnostic accuracy for identifying PC in patients with CP. It is well-known that the prediction for PC is seldom driven by a single factor. In this study, the Boruta algorithm was adopted to assess the relative importance of multiple variables. Eventually, 6 features including CA19-9, hemoglobin, AST, the maximum diameter of focal pancreatic lesions, ALT and CEA were selected as important features. These selected features incorporated clinical information and laboratory results, providing robust support for constructing a predictive model for PC in CP patients presenting with focal pancreatic lesions. In recent years, ML has proven to be particularly well-suited for medical predictive tasks. In our study, we constructed six ML models and evaluated their predictive performance. Among these, LR model demonstrated the best performance (AUC = 0.908). LR is a classical linear model based on probability estimation, characterized by its interpretability, computational efficiency, and robustness to overfitting compared to more complex algorithms such as decision trees or neural networks. To our knowledge, this is the first study in the world to establish and validate ML models for predicting PC in CP patients who have focal pancreatic lesions. The ML model developed in this study exhibited satisfactory diagnostic performance and held particular importance in assisting gastroenterologists in making optimal clinical decisions.

Compared with the commonly used diagnostic methods at present, the ML models we developed in this study has several advantages. Firstly, the ML models achieved excellent sensitivity both in the training set and testing set. As we all know, surgery is the only potentially curative treatment for PC, capable of elevating the 5-year survival rate to between 10% and 25% [27]. Given this, accurate and timely diagnosis of PC in its early stage is crucial for formulating the subsequent treatment strategy. A high-sensitivity diagnostic method could significantly reduce the chance of missed diagnosis, thereby providing as many patients as possible with the opportunity for surgical resection at an early stage. Secondly, the ML models integrated clinical information and laboratory results for detecting PC in the setting of CP. These features were all common in clinical practice and easy to collect, making the model particularly suitable for primary medical institutions that may lack sufficient personnel, expertise and infrastructure. Thirdly, we introduced SHAP values in our research to help clinicians decode ML models. While LR coefficients and odds ratios provided global feature importance rankings, SHAP offers complementary value in two aspects. First, it provided local interpretability. Second, using a unified SHAP framework across all six models enabled consistent comparison of feature importance without switching interpretability approaches. To validate SHAP’s reliability for LR, we compared SHAP-derived importance with absolute coefficients. The two showed strong agreement (Spearman’s ρ = 0.943, p = 0.0167), confirming that SHAP did not introduce bias when applied to LR. Thanks to SHAP, we can identify intuitively that the CA19-9, hemoglobin, ALT, CEA, AST and the maximum diameter of focal pancreatic lesions were critical predictive factors for diagnosis of focal pancreatic lesions, ranked in that order of influence [5, 28]. More importantly, by visualizing individual‑level predictions, SHAP allowed clinicians to progress from merely understanding which variables hold statistical importance to analyzing the dynamic interplay through which these factors collectively influence the final diagnostic decision. Fourthly, the patients in this study were recruited from two major tertiary medical centers in China over a period of more than ten years. This approach not only expanded the sample size but also enhanced the generalizability of the findings and reduced single-center bias, thereby providing a preliminary internal validation environment for assessing the generalizability of the developed ML model.

Several limitations also should be considered in our study. Firstly, given that this was a retrospective study and patient data were collected over a span of ten years, the absence of data and potential bias were inevitable. Some potential features, such as smoking and alcohol status, pain pattern, body mass index, alpha fetoprotein and interleukins, were not included in our analysis. Secondly, although we made efforts to exclude the influence of pancreatic cystic neoplasms in this study, for patients whose final diagnosis depended on follow-up result, the lack of imaging or pathological evidence may have resulted in some cases of pancreatic cystic neoplasms not being excluded. Thirdly, as this study lacked an external validation cohort from other independent medical centers, future research involving multicenter collaborations with larger sample sizes is warranted to further validate the generalizability of these findings. Fourthly, main pancreatic duct diameter was not included in our prediction model. Main pancreatic duct dilation has been consistently identified as an independent predictor of pancreatic malignancy [29, 30]. The exclusion of this important predictor from our model may limit its predictive performance. Future studies incorporating main pancreatic duct diameter are warranted to validate and potentially improve our model. Fifthly, feature selection was performed using the full dataset rather than strictly within the training set. This approach may introduce data leakage, potentially leading to optimistic estimation of model performance. However, given our relatively limited sample size (n = 187), we prioritized feature selection stability. Future studies with larger cohorts should perform feature selection exclusively within the training set to avoid this limitation. Last but not least, multiple comparison correction was not performed in this study, as the primary analysis focused on predictive modeling using ML algorithms rather than hypothesis testing of multiple independent comparisons. The reported p-values for baseline characteristics should be interpreted as descriptive rather than confirmatory.

In conclusion, we developed and evaluated six ML models that integrated clinical information and laboratory results to predict the diagnosis of focal pancreatic lesions in CP patients, and LR model had the best performance. The key features contributing to the model included CA19-9, hemoglobin, ALT, CEA, AST and the maximum diameter of pancreatic focal lesions. This model may aid in the early detection of PC by identifying high-risk CP patients with focal lesions who should undergo endoscopic ultrasound-guided biopsy or surgical evaluation, potentially reducing diagnostic delay and improving outcomes.
