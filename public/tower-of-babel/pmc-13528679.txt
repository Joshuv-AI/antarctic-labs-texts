Inequalities in injury burden in the Americas, 2000–2021: A systematic analysis
Authors: Ian Richard Hambleton, Britta Baer, Ricardo Pérez-Núñez, Selvi Jeyaseelan, Roberta Caixeta, Silvana Luciani, Ramon Martinez, Anselm Hennis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective.To assess mortality and disability due to injuries in the Americas, with a focus on subregional and gender disparities, and changes over time.Methods.Data from the 2021 World Health Organization Global Health Estimates were analyzed, covering all injury causes (ICD-10: V01–Y89) across 35 countries in the Americas from 2000 to 2021. Age-standardized mortality and disability-adjusted life year (DALY) rates were calculated by sex, cause, and subregion. Inequalities were quantified using absolute differences, relative ratios, and the index of disparity (IDisp).Results.In 2021, injuries caused over 662 000 deaths and 40.6 million DALYs in the Americas, with men accounting for nearly three-quarters of the burden. While age-standardized injury rates declined in most subregions, marked inequalities persisted. Interpersonal violence was the leading cause of injury-related mortality and disability, followed by road injuries, especially among men and boys aged 15–39 years. Self-harm increased substantially in both sexes, and falls rose in association with population aging. Interpersonal violence and drowning showed the highest between-country disparities, with IDisp values of 85.3 and 88.9, respectively, in 2021.Conclusions.Although some progress has been made in reducing injury burden in parts of the Americas, substantial challenges, including in some cases widening inequalities, remain. A renewed commitment to violence and injury prevention – rooted in equity, intersectoral action, and regional collaboration – is essential to reducing avoidable harms across the life course.

Injuries and violence are a leading cause of death and disability in the Region of the Americas, disproportionately affecting young people, especially men, and disadvantaged populations. In the two decades since 2000, the absolute number of deaths from injuries in the Americas increased, with violence, road traffic, and self-harm accounting for a substantial share of the burden (1).

The Region of the Americas continues to face a substantial and unequal burden from homicide, suicide, and road traffic injuries. Recent regional and global evidence shows that injuries remain a major cause of premature mortality and disability, disproportionately affecting young people – especially men – and reflecting persistent social, economic, and geographic inequalities (1–4). Road traffic injury remains a leading cause of death among children and young adults globally, and recent World Health Organization (WHO) reporting suggests that progress in reducing road traffic mortality has been too slow, including in the Americas, despite long-standing commitments to a safe-system approach and the availability of effective interventions (5). Together, these patterns underscore that injuries are not random events but important and preventable markers of wider system frailties in safety, equity, and access to timely prevention and care. This persistent and unequal burden underscores the importance of placing injury and violence prevention at the center of public health action. Injuries intersect with multiple Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3.6 (halving road traffic deaths), SDG 5.2 (eliminating violence against women and girls), SDG 11.2 (safe, accessible transport systems), and SDG 16 (reducing violence and related deaths) (6, 7). The SDG framework further recognizes that broader goals – such as poverty reduction, gender equality, education, urban safety, and access to justice – are also relevant to reducing the incidence and consequences of injuries (8, 9).

In the Americas, injury and violence prevention is a long-running concern. Since the 1990s, Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) Member States have adopted numerous resolutions and strategies to address injuries and violence (10). Yet the scale of the problem remains considerable, and with increasing migration in the region, socio-political-economic changes, as well as health system challenges including the COVID-19 pandemic, population vulnerabilities are increasing along with underlying risk factors such as social inequalities, alcohol and drug use, and other household stressors.

Injuries arise from a wide spectrum of external causes – including road traffic injuries, falls, drowning, burns, poisoning, suicide, and interpersonal violence. While these are often studied and managed as separate categories, they share common determinants and risk factors (such as alcohol use, environmental hazards, and social inequality), prevention strategies (such as multisectoral approaches and strong regulation), robust health system response networks especially for emergency services, and data needs (11). A public health approach that acknowledges these interconnections is essential to building more integrated and efficient prevention and management for violence and injuries.

Importantly, the burden of injury is rarely equitably distributed. Marked differences by sex, age, socioeconomic status, and geography persist. Young men, especially in low- and middle-income countries, face disproportionately high risks of homicide and road traffic injuries (5, 6). Women and children are more likely to experience domestic and sexual violence (12). Indigenous, Afro-descendant, migrant, and rural populations often face structural barriers to prevention and care (13). These inequalities point to failures in health, justice, education, and social protection systems – but also reveal opportunities for cross-country learning and regional cooperation.

This article presents a systematic analysis of trends and inequalities in the burden of injuries in the Americas between 2000 and 2021, using available regional data to identify disparities by age, sex, cause, and country. In doing so, we aim to inform regional policy dialogue, highlight effective responses, and support renewed momentum to reduce the preventable toll of injuries and violence through a data-driven, equity-focused public health response.

METHODS

Data sources

The WHO Global Health Estimates (GHE) are comparable cause of death and disability estimates from the year 2000 onward. These estimates draw on data submitted by WHO Member States, and on the results of the Global Burden of Disease (2021) (14). The GHE represent the best estimates from WHO, based on the evidence available up to May 2024, rather than the official estimates of Member States. Although the GHE methods documentation used in this study was released in 2024, the most recent year of country-comparable mortality and disability-adjusted life year (DALY) estimates available in that release was 2021. From the GHE data we extracted information on all injuries (International Classification of Diseases, ICD: V01–Y89). Injuries are classified into unintentional and intentional. Unintentional injuries include road injuries, poisonings, falls, fire, heat and hot substances, drowning, exposure to mechanical forces, and natural disasters. Intentional injuries include self-harm, interpersonal violence, and collective violence. Four unintentional poisoning codes (ICD: X41, X42, X44, X45) are associated with alcohol and commonly misused drugs, are assigned to the drug or alcohol use disorder category and are not included in this review. Full details of the WHO GHE are available at the WHO Global Health Observatory (15, 16).

Countries and subregions of the Americas

The WHO GHE are available for WHO Member States. For this analysis we grouped the included countries of the Americas into eight subregions, as defined by PAHO: North America (2 countries: Canada, United States of America), Central America (7 countries: Belize, Costa Rica, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, Panama), Andean Area (5 countries: Bolivia [Plurinational State of], Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Venezuela [Bolivarian Republic of]), Southern Cone (4 countries: Argentina, Chile, Paraguay, Uruguay), Latin Caribbean (3 countries: Cuba, Dominican Republic, Haiti), non-Latin Caribbean (12 countries: Antigua and Barbuda, Bahamas, Barbados, Dominica, Grenada, Guyana, Jamaica, Saint Kitts and Nevis, Saint Lucia, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, Suriname, Trinidad and Tobago), Brazil as a separate country, and Mexico as a separate country.

Metrics

We used two metrics from the WHO GHE. Number of deaths: for each country, we extracted the number of deaths by injury cause, age, sex, and year. Disability-adjusted life years (DALYs): the DALY is a summary measure that combines time lost through premature death and time lived in states of less-than-optimal health, loosely referred to as “disability.” So, the DALY adds together the years of life lost (YLL) and the years of lost good health, also called the years lived with disability (YLD). One DALY can be thought of as one lost year of “healthy” life. Because the DALY is an aggregation of premature mortality and years lived with disability, it offers a more direct link than mortality alone to the associated burden on healthcare systems. We present age-standardized rates per 100 000 population for mortality and disability, calculated by the direct method using the WHO World Standard Population (17). For deaths and disability, we present the change over time as the percentage difference between values in 2000 and in 2021.

Measuring injury inequalities

This study presents differences in injury mortality and disability between women and men, and between subregions or countries of the Americas, providing quantitative estimates of injury inequality between subpopulations. We provide three measures of injury inequality, and following methods described by WHO we present absolute and relative measures (18). Absolute and relative measures of inequality capture different dimensions of disparity. Absolute measures show the magnitude of difference between groups (e.g., injury rate difference), while relative measures show the proportional difference (e.g., injury rate ratio). Both are important: absolute measures highlight the public health burden, while relative measures reveal the extent of inequality regardless of overall rates. Presenting both offers a more complete picture of health disparities.

To describe gender inequality, we use two simple measures. The gender difference is the absolute gender inequality for mortality or disability; that is, the value for men minus the value for women. The gender ratio is the relative inequality for mortality or disability; that is, the value for men divided by the value for women. To describe country-level inequality, we use the index of disparity (IDisp), a relative inequality for mortality or disability. We use the IDisp to capture the extent of mortality or disability inequality across countries of the Americas. We compare each country’s rate to the overall average for the Americas. The IDisp therefore presents the average percentage difference from this regional reference, with the following interpretation: a value of 0% means no disparity, while higher values indicate greater inequality. This index allows comparisons over time or between populations, even when group sizes or overall rates differ (19).

RESULTS

General injury trends

The overall burden of injuries in the Americas in 2021 – numbers and rates of deaths and DALYs – are summarized in Table 1, and the trend in DALY rates since 2000 is visualized in Figure 1.

In 2021, injuries remained a major cause of mortality and disability across all subregions of the Americas, with stark differences by sex and geography. As shown in Table 1, men consistently bore a greater burden of injury-related deaths and DALYs than women. Across the Region, there were 663 thousand deaths from injuries – 502 thousand among men and 162 thousand among women – and over 40.6 million DALYs (30.4 million in men, 10.3 million in women). The highest absolute number of deaths occurred in North America (225 thousand) and Brazil (161 thousand), while the highest age-standardized death rates were reported in Central America (130.3 per 100 000 men), Brazil (119.7 per 100 000 men), and the non-Latin Caribbean (117.2 per 100 000 men). Between 2000 and 2021, age-standardized death rates declined in most subregions, with notable reductions in the Andean Area (–34.4% among men, –29.8% among women) and Southern Cone (–33.3% among men, –24.8% among women). Conversely, North America and the Latin Caribbean saw modest increases among women and men (North America 6.4% among men, 7.4% among women; Latin Caribbean 4.4% among men, 6.9% among women).

Burden of injuries, unintentional injuries, and intentional injuries between 2000 and 2021 in eight subregions of the Americas, measured using disability-adjusted life year (DALY) rate per 100 000

The burden of DALYs followed similar patterns. DALY rates per 100 000 people were highest in Central America (7 603 for men) and Brazil (7 361 for men), while the most substantial rate reductions were observed in the Andean Area (–32.8% women and men combined) and Southern Cone (–25.9% women and men combined). Most subregions reported DALY rate reductions although again the picture was mixed, with the Latin Caribbean notably reporting higher age-standardized DALY rates in 2021 compared to 2000 particularly among women (17.3% higher among women, 5.8% among men), underscoring regional variability.

Figure 1 reinforces these findings by illustrating a general downward trend in age-standardized DALY rates from injuries between 2000 and 2021 across all subregions, with a persistent excess burden among men. Trends in unintentional injuries broadly mirrored the overall pattern, although the Latin Caribbean experienced a sharp spike in 2004 due to flash floods across Hispaniola, particularly Haiti, and in 2010 due to the Haitian earthquake – in 2010 DALY rates in the Latin Caribbean temporarily touched 26 000 among men and 39 000 among women. In contrast, intentional injury rates declined more slowly and unevenly, with Central America maintaining comparatively high burdens throughout. Together, these data point to meaningful progress in reducing age-standardized injury burdens in some contexts, with persistent concerns about the sustainability of efforts and related disparities.

Interpersonal violence

The burden of injuries from interpersonal violence in the Americas is summarized in Supplementary Table 1. In 2021, interpersonal violence remained the leading cause of injury-related deaths and disability in the Americas, accounting for nearly 200 thousand deaths and over 12 million DALYs. The overall mortality rate from interpersonal violence has declined only slightly since 2000 – down by 1% to 19 deaths per 100 000 people. The numbers mask persistent disparities between sexes and subregions. Men continue to bear the heaviest burden, with a death rate of 33.6 per 100 000, around seven times higher than the 4.8 per 100 000 observed in women. This pattern is mirrored in the years of healthy life lost: men lost 2 083 DALYs per 100 000 compared to 314 for women. Although there was a modest overall drop in the regional DALY rate since 2000 – down by 1.2% to 1 205 years per 100 000, some regions, particularly Mexico and the non-Latin Caribbean, saw large increases.

These summaries reflect the enduring and gendered nature of interpersonal violence, which includes injuries intentionally inflicted by another person, excluding war or lawful interventions. While further disaggregation – such as identifying cases of intimate partner violence – is needed to better inform prevention strategies, the available data underscore the importance of addressing interpersonal violence as a public health priority across the Region.

Road injuries

The burden of road injuries in the Americas is summarized in Supplementary Table 2. In 2021, road injuries remained a major public health concern across the Americas, accounting for 145 275 deaths – more than three-quarters of which occurred among men. This translates to a mortality rate of 13.5 per 100 000 population, down 17% from 2000. Although overall rates declined, particularly among women (−25.4%), the burden remains unequally distributed: men continue to face disproportionately high rates of both death (21.2 per 100 000 among men compared to 5.9 per 100 000 among women) and disability (1 317 per 100 000 among men compared to 403 per 100 000 among women). This sex disparity has persisted for over two decades. The Region also reported 8.9 million DALYs due to road injuries, with three-quarters of this burden borne by men. The Latin Caribbean is the subregion with the highest road injury mortality (21.3 deaths per 100 000 people) and years of healthy life lost (1 321 DALYs per 100 000 people): increases of 11% and 12% since 2000, respectively.

Self-harm

The burden of injuries from self-harm in the Americas is summarized in Supplementary Table 3. In 2021, self-harm accounted for over 100 000 deaths and nearly 5 million years of healthy life lost in the Americas, reflecting an emerging and substantial public health issue. While men experienced the majority of deaths (79%) and lost more years of healthy life than women, the past two decades have seen notable and similar increases in both mortality and DALYs among women and men – with mortality up 59.7% and disability up 55.2%. Regionally, North America, Mexico, and Brazil saw substantial increases in both self-harm deaths and DALYs from 2000 to 2021, and North America bore the highest mortality and DALY rates in 2021. Despite some regional declines, especially in Central America and the Caribbean, the self-harm death rate rose by 17% since 2000, reaching 9.2 deaths per 100 000 in 2021. This pattern reflects global trends, where self-harm is a leading cause of injury, death, and disability, particularly among young adults.

Falls

The burden of injuries from falls in the Americas is summarized in Supplementary Table 4. In 2021, falls accounted for over 85 000 deaths and 5.6 million years of healthy life lost in the Americas – representing a dramatic increase in this injury burden since 2000, up 187% and 49%, respectively. The age-standardized mortality rate rose 64% since 2000 to 5.7 per 100 000, but the similarly standardized disability rate fell marginally, from 460 per 100 000 in 2000 to 445 per 100 000 in 2021, reflecting an older population in 2021 driving the increasing burden from falls (20). While absolute numbers have risen, regional trends varied, with many subregions experiencing declines in age-standardized rates. Nevertheless, population aging across the Americas continues to elevate the burden. Although older adults suffer the greatest burden of fatal falls, injuries from falling are common across the life course, and reducing fall injuries requires systemic strategies – including safer built environments, proactive health and social policies, and community-level interventions – to mitigate risk and protect health at every age. Recently, for example, a systems approach has been advocated that moves beyond individual behavior and provides for environments, policies, and awareness that prioritize safety, creating buffers so that falls are either avoided or made less serious because of protections in place (21).

To better distinguish injuries that primarily cause premature mortality from those that produce a greater burden through nonfatal disability, we examined the composition of DALYs into years of life lost (YLLs) and years lived with disability (YLDs) in 2021 (Supplementary Table 12). Clear differences were observed by injury type. Interpersonal violence, self-harm, and drowning were overwhelmingly driven by premature mortality, with YLLs accounting for 94.0%, 98.1%, and 99.1% of total DALYs, respectively. In contrast, falls were predominantly disability-driven, with YLDs contributing 70.8% of total DALYs overall, rising to 77.8% among women. Road injuries showed a more mixed profile, although premature mortality still accounted for most DALYs (82.3% overall). At the aggregate level, unintentional injuries had a substantially larger disability component than intentional injuries (38.1% versus 5.7% of DALYs due to YLDs), highlighting important differences in the types of prevention, trauma care, rehabilitation, and long-term support required across injury categories.

Injury burden across the life course

The changing injury profile in 2021 (using DALYs) across five broad age groups – early childhood (under 5s), school-aged or adolescent (5–19 years), young adults (20–39 years), middle adulthood (40–64 years), and older adults (over 65 years) – is visualized in Figure 2, with years of injury disability presented in Supplementary Table 11. In 2021 alone, injuries accounted for over 40.6 million DALYs in the Americas, with distinct patterns across the life course. Children under 5 experienced the lowest burden (1.2 million DALYs), primarily due to drowning (139 thousand DALYs) and road injuries (190 thousand DALYs). This likely reflects environmental risks in and around the home, limited supervision, and early exposure to hazards without safety infrastructure.

Among children and adolescents aged 5–19 years, the burden rose sharply to nearly 5 million DALYs, driven by interpersonal violence (1.7 million), road injuries (1.1 million), and self-harm (628 thousand). This increase may reflect the convergence of risk-taking behaviors, increased independence and mobility, and social determinants such as poverty, exclusion, and exposure to violence across the life course. Among perpetrators, interpersonal violence is often a learned behavior – frequently first observed in childhood and sometimes repeated later in life.

The peak burden occurred among young adults aged 20–39 years, who accounted for almost half the regional total (17.3 million DALYs). Interpersonal violence (7.3 million), road injuries (4.0 million), and self-harm (2.5 million) were dominant, reflecting heightened exposure to occupational and interpersonal risks, economic pressures, and mental health challenges.

Age-specific burden of injuries between 2000 and 2021 in the Americas, measured using disability-adjusted life year (DALY) rate per 100 000

In adults aged 40–64 years, the burden declined to 11.8 million DALYs. Road injuries (2.8 million), interpersonal violence (2.6 million), falls (1.9 million), and self-harm (1.5 million) remained prominent, possibly due to cumulative exposure to injury risk at work and at home, and the persistence of mental health stressors.

For adults aged 65 and older, the burden fell to 5.5 million DALYs, and falls became the leading cause (2.5 million DALYs). This reflects physiological vulnerability, reduced balance, and environmental hazards, underscoring the growing need for age-friendly environments and fall prevention.

These evolving patterns highlight how injury risks shift with age, making a life course approach essential. Public health responses must be tailored to the specific vulnerabilities, exposures, and opportunities for prevention at each stage of life.

Inequalities in injury deaths and disability across the Americas

Inequalities in the injury burden between countries are summarized in Supplementary Table 13. Marked disparities in injury-related mortality and disability persist across the Americas. In 2021, drowning displayed the highest between-country inequality (mortality IDisp = 88.9; DALY IDisp = 86.1), likely driven by geographic exposure and uneven access to safe environments and rescue services. Interpersonal violence showed high inequality in both mortality (IDisp = 85.3) and disability (IDisp = 74.6), reflecting entrenched structural vulnerabilities – such as social and gender inequality, crime, and weak governance.

Overall inequality in injury burden changed little between 2000 and 2021 (for example, the all-injury DALY IDisp rose from 29.0 to 31.8), but trends varied by cause. Inequality in fall-related mortality declined substantially to 2006 – possibly due to improvements in elder care – before rising slightly. In contrast, road injury disparities grew (mortality IDisp from 36.8 to 40.8), reflecting inconsistent implementation of transport safety policies and emergency response capacity.

Intentional injuries (self-harm and violence) consistently showed greater inequality than unintentional injuries, pointing to the influence of social determinants and health service gaps. As shown in Figure 1, men in Central America and the Latin Caribbean experienced the highest intentional injury burdens. The 2010 Haitian earthquake caused a sharp spike in unintentional injury DALYs in the Latin Caribbean, highlighting how disasters can magnify health system vulnerabilities.

These findings reveal persistent – and in some areas, worsening – inequalities in injury burden across countries and subregions. Addressing them will require targeted, context-specific strategies that require close collaboration between clinical response services, and action to tackle “upstream” structural and social drivers.

Gender inequality

Across all subregions of the Americas, men experience a substantially higher injury burden than women for all five major injury categories: interpersonal violence, road injuries, self-harm, falls, and drowning. As shown in Figure 3, the male-to-female DALY rate ratio consistently exceeded 1.0. The disparity was greatest for interpersonal violence, with men experiencing more than nine times the burden of women in Brazil and the Andean Area, with the gender ratio more than 6 across the Americas a whole. For road injuries, male DALY rates were between two and five times those of females across all subregions, with peak ratios of 4.7 in Brazil, 4.2 in Mexico, and 4.1 in Central America. Even for falls, often seen as more age-related than gendered, the male burden surpassed the female burden across all subregions, with gender ratios ranging from 1.1 to 2.3.

Figure 4 shows all countries above the parity line, confirming higher male DALY rates across all injury types. However, the spread of countries varies by cause. For interpersonal violence and self-harm – the intentional injuries – points are widely dispersed, suggesting large, country-specific gender gaps that may require tailored national strategies. In contrast, falls and road injuries show tight clustering, indicating more consistent gender differences across the region – potentially amenable to coordinated, regional approaches. The degree of clustering reflects not just the size of the gender gap, but whether common or context-specific factors are driving it.

DISCUSSION

This new analysis on injuries and violence in the Americas highlights the enduring and unequal burden of all types of injuries across the Americas, with sharp disparities by sex, subregion, and cause. While regional age-standardized rates of injury-related deaths and DALYs declined between 2000 and 2021, these improvements mask persistent and, in some cases, growing inequalities. Central America, Brazil, and the non-Latin Caribbean consistently recorded some of the highest male injury death rates, underscoring the urgent need for localized, equity-oriented strategies. These trends echo global findings that men – particularly young men – bear the brunt of fatal injuries in low- and middle-income settings due to socioeconomic disadvantage, limited services, and higher-risk exposures (22, 23).

Interpersonal violence remains one of the Region’s most alarming injury profiles. With nearly 200 000 deaths and over 12 million DALYs in 2021, the Americas continue to suffer the highest homicide rates globally (6). Despite the availability of evidence-based solutions, there has been only a marginal decline in interpersonal violence mortality rates since 2000. Perhaps most concerning, some subregions – Mexico, the Caribbean, and Central America – experienced rising rates. These trends reflect deeper structural drivers, such as social exclusion, organized crime, weak law enforcement, increasing regional migration, and gender and social mores that normalize the use of violence. Interpersonal violence often begins early and may escalate over time, creating an urgent need to prioritize timely intervention among at-risk groups and life-course factors. While health services are well-positioned to facilitate early identification, truly sustainable efforts involve intersectoral approaches spanning health, education, policing, justice, and community empowerment (12).

Rates of road injuries, although declining overall, continue to exact a heavy toll – especially among men. Nearly 9 million DALYs, 145 thousand deaths in 2021, and a percentage mortality rate reduction of around 17% signal that the Region is far from achieving the SDG 3.6 target to halve road traffic deaths by 2030. Male rates are consistently triple those of females, with particularly high burdens in the Latin Caribbean and Central America. The Decade of Action for Road Safety 2021–2030 was launched in response to stagnation, and calls for accelerated investment in infrastructure, regulation, and enforcement (24). While some progress has been observed, key targeted interventions, including speed management, safer vehicles, and post-crash care, remain under-implemented in many countries.

Self-harm represents an increasingly prominent public health concern in the Americas. While still under-discussed, suicide and self-harm accounted for over 100 000 deaths and 5 million DALYs in 2021, with both women and men showing sharp increases since 2000. This reflects growing psychosocial stressors – including economic hardship, social isolation, and untreated mental illness – that demand an urgent scaling up of mental health services and suicide prevention programs (25).

The dramatic rise in fatal falls – up 187% since 2000 – signals a demographic shift as aging populations become increasingly vulnerable to environmental hazards and physiological decline. Although age-standardized disability rates from falls have plateaued, absolute numbers continue to rise. A system-level approach to fall prevention is now essential, with strategies encompassing built environment safety, elder care, and universal design (26).

For unintentional drowning, while some improvements have been observed in total number of deaths and DALYs, this issue remains an important burden especially among children and adolescents, and one external cause with important inequalities within the Americas. The first ever United Nations resolution on drowning prevention, adopted in 2021, is an important policy document to address this burden, along with the World Health Assembly resolution in 2023 to accelerate action on global drowning prevention (27, 28).

Finally, this analysis confirms entrenched gender and geographic inequalities in injury burden. Male-to-female DALY rate ratios exceed 2.0 for most injury types and reach 6.0 or more for interpersonal violence. Country-level disparities remain high and have worsened for several causes, including road injuries and interpersonal violence. Tackling these inequities demands a shift from reactive to preventive public health models – centered on equity, multisectoral collaboration, and continuous regional learning (29).

The policy implications of these findings differ across injury categories and levels of action. Road injuries and falls showed comparatively tighter clustering in gender-specific DALY rates across countries, suggesting that these injuries may be shaped by more consistent systems-level determinants across the Region and may therefore benefit from coordinated regional strategies, including shared road safety standards, trauma and rehabilitation system strengthening, common surveillance indicators, and exchange of best practices in safer transport and age-friendly environments. The relatively tighter clustering for these injury types may also be consistent with the influence of broader regional policy alignment during the study period, although our descriptive analyses do not allow causal attribution. By contrast, interpersonal violence and self-harm showed much wider cross-country dispersion, indicating that their burden is more strongly shaped by context-specific social, cultural, economic, and institutional factors. For these intentional injuries, regional frameworks remain important, but meaningful progress is likely to depend on tailored national and subnational programs that address local patterns of inequality, insecurity, social norms, and mental health system capacity. Together, these findings support a layered model of injury prevention in the Americas, combining regional coordination where determinants are shared with context-sensitive implementation where determinants are more heterogeneous. The injury burden in the Americas remains unacceptably high, with deep-rooted inequalities that continue to shape who is most affected and where progress has been slowest. Yet the evidence also points to meaningful gains in several areas and subregions, showing that change is possible. These successes offer a foundation to build on – through better data and more consistent investment in cross-sector prevention. By prioritizing equity, sharing lessons across countries, and aligning efforts with broader social and health goals, the Region can move closer to broader injury reduction.
