A hybrid approach to Twitter sentiment analysis using integration of ESN ISPBO and BERT
Authors: Zhaojia Chai, Nan Sun, Qingyang Zhang, Navid Razmjooy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSentiment analysis is an essential component of natural language processing, which focuses on extracting subjective insights, like emotions and opinions from text. In this research, an innovative framework has been introduced for Twitter sentiment analysis that integrates Echo State Networks (ESN), Improved Student Psychology Based Optimization (ISPBO), and BERT embeddings. The proposed ESN-ISPBO-BERT model was evaluated on the SemEval-2016-1 and SemEval-2016-2 datasets and compared against other models, including SVM-Glove, CNN-BERT, LSTM, CNN, KNN, SVM, BERT, and GRU. The outcomes indicate that the suggested model exceeds all baseline models, and has achieved outstanding performance. On SemEval-2016-2, it achieves 98.82% accuracy, 98.79% precision, 98.96% recall, and 98.87% F1-score, while on SemEval-2016-1, it achieves 98.76% accuracy, 98.81% precision, 98.92% recall, as well as 98.86% F1-score. Moreover, the suggested model achieved the values of 98.51%, 98.42%, 98.87%, and 98.64% in terms of accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score on Stanford Sentiment Treebank (SST-2). These outcomes indicate the efficiency of combining reservoir calculating, innovative optimization techniques, and contextual embeddings for the aim of sentiment analysis. The proposed model is a strong solution for the evaluation of Twitter data, with possible applications in brand monitoring, analyzing customer feedback, and tracking sentiment in real-time. Introduction

Twitter has emerged as one of the most powerful social media platforms, with users sending over 500 million tweets daily worldwide1. Its real-time nature and vast user base offer a rich source of data for examining public trends, opinion, and sentiments. Whether regarding political issues, brand perception, crisis management, or entertainment, Twitter is as a global center for sharing ideas and emotions. As a result, it is a crucial resource for sentiment analysis, which involves the extraction and categorization of subjective data, including feelings, opinions and attitudes from text.

By examining tweets, researchers, businesses, and policymakers can obtain valuable information into public opinion, which enable them to make informed decisions based on data, display brand reputation, and react to new trends of real time. Nevertheless, the informal language, slang, and brevity within tweets present considerable issues, which makes sentiment analysis on Twitter a complicated but highly beneficial task2.

Lately, the social media platforms have enabled organizations and businesses to gather feedback from their clients as well as customers through user-generated reviews. These reviews can be found on the internet and social media that often have been presented in blogs that contain information in text, audio, visuals, or a combination of these formats3. Social media text data has been particularly characterized by short statements that are frequently unstructured, semi-structured, and typically filled with informal language, and it leads to a disorganized format that complicates and extends the procedure of generating vector illustrations and conducting sentiment categorization4.

Sentiment analysis is regarded as a crucial task in natural language processing (NLP), and it focuses on identifying and interpreting sentiments in written data. It has various applications, including comprehending customer reactions and assessing public opinion on social and governmental matters. In general, sentiment analysis can be divided into three main groups, including aspect-based sentiment analysis, which identifies sentiment related to specific attributes or items; document-level sentiment analysis, which determines the overall sentiment of a document; and sentence-level sentiment analysis, which evaluates sentiment in particular sentences5.

Sentiment analysis is the procedure of determining the emotions, opinions, and perspectives stated in various source materials, including documents, brief texts, blog sentences, news articles, and reviews, among others, especially regarding hot events like Covid-19. This area frequently includes processing large collections of text and usually focuses on formal language. Sentiment analysis utilizes Natural Language Processing (NLP) as well as machine learning for identifying the overall sentiment related to a product or subject6.

Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) are effective in various machine learning applications, including text summarization, disease diagnosis, and image classification. Their ability to automatically learn complicated trends and data representations, especially when utilized within extensive datasets, makes them mainly suitable for sentiment analysis tasks7,8. As a result, numerous investigations have been presented within the area of sentiment analysis domain.

Literature review

Empirical findings

Kaur et al.9 conducted a Twitter data analysis using the R programming language. They gathered Twitter data based on hashtag keywords, including COVID-19, deaths, coronavirus, novel cases, as well as recovered. Within this research, an algorithm was developed known as H-SVM to carry out sentiment categorization, and categorized them into negative, positive, as well as neutral sentiment scores. Additionally, the efficiency of the suggested algorithm was calculated across several metrics, like recall, precision, accuracy, and F1 score, and compared it with SVM and RNN.

Ningrum et al.10 aimed to implement sentiment analysis on Twitter reviews from the Google Play Store by the use of two algorithms, including Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). The dataset consists of 4,999 reviews collected through the scraping procedure. The experimental results achieved a precision of 84%, a recall of 81%, and an accuracy of 84.67% for CNN, while LSTM achieved a precision of 87%, a recall of 69%, and an accuracy of 82.19%, which indicated that the CNN outperformed LSTM.

Tembhurne et al.11 introduced a model for sentiment analysis that integrated innovative deep learning neural networks, including long short-term memory networks (LSTM), convolutional neural networks, Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM), and Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT). Effective feature extraction was crucial in tasks of sentiment analysis. By combining pre-trained BERT with advanced neural networks, the proposed model achieved 94.95% accuracy. They assessed the suggested model on a publicly accessible dataset of Twitter Sentiment Analysis. The suggested model performed better than various machine learning and deep learning approaches within the area of sentiment analysis. Therefore, this study recommended using this model to precisely assess sentiments from tweets and other forms of textual data.

Tembhurne et al.12 investigated public sentiment on Indonesian Twitter according to the 2024 General Election, utilizing deep learning and machine learning methods, such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), Naïve Bayes, Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM). The data was gathered through a Tweet Harvest method by using the keyword “Pemilu” and went through preprocessing stages that included case normalization, elimination of stopwords, removal of symbols and URLs, as well as tokenization for ensuring the quality of the data. Efficiency assessment metrics, such as precision, accuracy, F1-score, and recall, were used to evaluate the efficiency of the models. Naïve Bayes achieved the maximum accuracy at 64%, followed closely by SVM at 63%, LSTM at 60%, as well as GRU at 57%.

Mohammed Alsekait et al.13 presented a sentiment analysis design for platforms of social media utilizing an integration of five machine learning (ML) algorithms, including Random Forest Classifier, Multinomial Naive Bayes, Gradient Boosting Classifier, Decision Tree, and K-Nearest Neighbors, along with three deep learning (DL) algorithms, including MLP, LSTM, and CNN. The researchers demonstrated the efficacy of the suggested approach by performance metrics, and compared it with other models. By employing detailed datasets from Twitter and Facebook, the investigators achieved great outcomes, and LSTM achieved the highest efficiency.

Krosuri and Aravapalli14 introduced a hybrid model that combined ResNeXt with a recurrent neural approach (NH-ResNeXt-RNF) to analyze multi-class sentiment from texts. The approach examined the words’ polarity relevant to a particular field throughout the complete dataset and removed noisy data in an unsupervised way through pre-processing. To efficiently perform multi-class categorization and minimize the effort needed for labeling in multi-class sentiment analysis using unsupervised learning, optimization is necessary. The effectiveness of the suggested approach was assessed on two datasets, including Twitter and Amazon. The experimental outcomes of the suggested method demonstrated superior efficiency in multi-class domain-specific sentiment classification compared to conventional methods pertinent to supervised, unsupervised, and semi-supervised techniques.

Krosuri and Aravapalli15 suggested a Bidirectional-Recurrent Neural Network (NHBi-RNN) for multiclass sentiment categorization combined with coot optimization. The process involved data acquisition, raw data pre-processing, steps for sentiment classification, and feature extraction. As a result, the suggested approach effectively classified the sentiment polarity of consumer feedback into categories, like positive, very negative, very positive, neutral, and negative. In comparison with other leading algorithms, the proposed framework demonstrated strong performance in multiclass sentiment classification.

Krosuri and Aravapalli16 proposed an enhanced local search whale optimization that improves LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) for sentiment analysis at the feature level of online product reviews. The suggested approach for sentiment analysis at the level of feature comprised pre-processing, data collection, sentiment classification, feature selection, and feature extraction. The pre-processed data underwent a feature extraction process by the use of an advanced inverse class frequency algorithm. After that, the features were selected through a levy flight-based mayfly optimizer. Finally, the chosen data was converted into the improvised exploitation whale optimization-enhanced long short-term memory approach.

Motivation and contribution

Analyzing sentiment on platforms of social media, such as Twitter is crucial for comprehending public sentiment, societal trends, and consumer habits. Conventional methods of sentiment analysis frequently face challenges due to the complication and present noise in informal, brief, and context-sensitive tweets. Although deep learning structures like Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) have demonstrated significant success within tasks of natural language processing because of their capability to understand contextual subtleties, they are resource-intensive and need considerable computational power for training and adjustments. Conversely, Echo State Networks (ESNs), which is a variant of recurrent neural networks characterized by a fixed reservoir, provide an efficient choice for processing sequential data but can often underperform because of the reservoir parameters’ random initialization. This disparity in performance has inspired the investigation of a hybrid method that uses the benefits of both ESNs and BERT while resolving their drawbacks. Combining Improved Student Psychology Based Optimization (ISPBO) into the optimization of Echo State Networks (ESNs) significantly improves the capability of the model to adapt to the varied and dynamic nature of Twitter data, which makes it as a great solution for effective and precise sentiment analysis.

This research makes numerous important contributions to the area of sentiment analysis on Twitter. Initially, it presents an innovative hybrid design that integrates the contextual insights of BERT with the calculational effectiveness of Echo State Networks. By enhancing the BERT for extracting feature and employing ESNs for modeling sequences, the model strikes a balance between resource efficacy and precision. After that, utilizing Improved Student Psychology Based Optimization (ISPBO) for enhancing the ESN’s reservoir parameters indicates a major enhancement in improving the performance of the network. The ISPBO, which has been inspired by student learning behaviors, provides a more efficient and adaptive optimization procedure than conventional approaches, which results in enhanced accuracy in sentiment classification. Next, the suggested model has been comprehensively assessed on real-world datasets of Twitter, which illustrates its superiority compared to existing techniques regarding both sentiment forecasting accuracy and calculational effectiveness. Lastly, this investigation offers valuable insights into the possible benefits of hybrid models as well as metaheuristic optimization strategies for resolving the issues of sentiment analysis on social media.

Methodology

Implementation procedure

The aim of this study is to develop a strong model for tweet sentiment analysis, particularly to identify both positive and negative sentiments. During the preparation step, datasets were gathered and processed from two collections, where tweets were annotated as positive or negative. In the preprocessing phase, features have been extracted, and all extraneous elements as well as unwanted characters, such as punctuation, URLs, and usernames, have been eliminated from the data. Subsequently, relevant features, including mentions, the adjectives, and hashtags, have been extracted. Following this, the data have been prepared to input into the neural network to achieve high efficiency. Furthermore, a model that combines Echo State Network (ESN) with Improved Student Psychology Based Optimization (ISPBO) and BERT aimed at optimizing the hyperparameters of the network.

Database

The model was trained using 3 diverse datasets, which were developed from Twitter, and are part of the SemEval-2016 collection. The initial dataset, known as SemEval-2016-1, consists of 3,694 tweets; additionally, the second dataset, referred to as SemEval-2016-2, contains 1,122 tweets. Each of these tweets has been manually labeled. In summary, there are two distinct datasets utilized in this investigation. The datasets have already been categorized as negative or positive. Consequently, the model’s outcomes will be evaluated on the basis of these labels. The datasets are available at: http://alt.qcri.org/semeval2016/.

In general, SemEval-2016-1 is applied for tasks of sentiment analysis. The dataset consists of various statements’ pairs obtained from a range of sources, including content from social media platforms and news articles. Additionally, the subsequent dataset is SemEval-2016-2 that has been specifically established for analyzing sentiments in tweets.

The datasets mentioned previously consist of tweet IDs along with their comments, which are categorized as positive or negative. This study includes several keywords, such as happiness, fever, breakup, flu, sadness, etc., used to classify the tweets. This study uses the binary Stanford Sentiment Treebank (SST-2)17,18 dataset with a total of 70,042 sentences to evaluate the proposed model for sentiment classification using a 10-fold cross-validation. For every iteration, nine folds (90% of the data, 63,038 sentences) become the training set and one fold (10%, 7,004 sentences) becomes the test set. The balance of the dataset remains consistent. Each test fold has an estimated 3,500 positive and 3,504 negative sentences, and the corresponding training set has approximately 56,534 sentences (28,134 positive and 28,400 negative), thereby providing totally unbiased model training and evaluation across the folds.

To enhance the examination of the stability and generalization capacity of the proposed model, the Sentiment140 dataset was added as a complementary real-world Twitter dataset at scale. This dataset includes 1,600,000 tweets that have been automatically labeled for their sentiment polarity, either positive, negative, or neutral, according to the presence of emoticons in the original tweets. Each tweet includes tweet ID, user, text, and sentiment polarity label. The tweets represent many different topics, linguistic variations, and time periods, making this dataset an excellent baseline dataset for large-scale sentiment classification tasks. This dataset can be accessed at: https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/kazanova/sentiment140.

To evaluate the proposed model rigorously and quantitatively, the dataset was first split into a temporary 80% training set and a held-out 20% test set. All subsequent preprocessing and model development used only the temporary training set. Then, a 10-fold cross-validation process has been implemented. In this case, the datasets were divided into 10 stratified folds to accurately replicate the distribution of negative and positive sentiments in each fold. At each iteration, nine folds were used to train the model, and the single fold was used to test the model. Importantly, within each fold of the cross-validation, scaling parameters were learned solely from the training fold and then applied to the validation fold. After selecting the best parameters, a final model was trained on the entire temporary training set (using the same preprocessing rules) and evaluated on the held-out test set, which had not been used in any previous step. This process was conducted ten times, with each fold as the test set exactly once. The performance metrics reported is the average of the 10 runs (1 run for each fold). This approach enables a well-rounded evaluation of the proposed model and helps use data for training and validating the approach.

It is important to recognize that high performance relies on the data distribution of the benchmark SemEval-2016 and SST-2 datasets. A potentially different data distribution (for example, related to the temporal evolution of language or topic variation) could influence the efficacy of the model. This is frequently the case for machine learning approaches specifically trained on a particular corpus. The process of feature extraction was chosen to include only general linguistic features (like adjectives, adverbs, emoticons, and punctuation), intended to identify universal markers of sentiment that are somewhat resistant to this problem.

Tokenization divides the text into separate units like sentences, phrases, or words. This step is essential for breaking the text into smaller, more manageable parts for subsequent analysis. Bidirectional encoder representation from transformers (BERT)

BERT, Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers, enhances natural language understanding by enabling machines to grasp the context of phrases22. Its design is based on the transformer method, and it utilizes transfer learning. In transfer learning, a model is initially trained on a broad task and subsequently fine-tuned with the knowledge gained to optimize it for particular tasks23. BERT is trained on two categories of tasks, including unmasked language tasks and masked language tasks. In masked language tasks, the model has been presented with sentences that have certain words hidden or masked, and it must forecast these concealed words. In tasks of unmasked language, the model carries out sentence prediction, where it is given pairs of sentences and assesses whether a sentence logically follows the other. The training dataset for BERT comprises a substantial collection that includes English Wikipedia and 11,038 books. The model employs a transformer encoder, which is a type of architecture developed to handle sentences as inputs. Each word within the sentences is tokenized and inputted into BERT, which then generates a vector representation for each tokenized word as its output.

In accordance with conventional approaches to sequence classification, the output embedding for the special [CLS] token has been extracted and used, which comprised the final layer of BERT’s output, as the holistic contextual representation for the full input tweet. The [CLS] token embedding was used as the input feature vector to the Echo State Network.

Echo state network (ESN)

An Echo State Network (ESN) is a type of Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) that is particularly designed for managing sequential data, including natural language as well as time series. A unique feature of an ESN is its hidden layer, known as the echo state, which retains somewhat stable internal states over long periods and is affected just by the present input.

In traditional RNNs, the hidden state at each time phase is influenced by both the present input and the preceding hidden state. This complication can create challenges during training because the weights connecting the hidden units need to be adjusted carefully to avoid the network becoming trapped in local minima through optimization. On the other hand, the hidden state of an ESN has been determined by the present input using a fixed set of stochastically initialized weights. This more straightforward training approach removes the necessity of learning recurrent relations between hidden units.

At each time phase, the Echo State Network (ESN) computes the output by linearly combining the inputs and the hidden state, followed by applying a non-linear activation function. The hidden state has been then updated using a similar formula that incorporates the present input. Because of its straightforward design, ESNs are capable of effectively capturing complicated temporal patterns from raw input sequences without needing direct supervision for learning how the hidden state changes. These networks are utilized in various fields, such as speech recognition, time series forecasting, as well as robotics control. The ESN’s structure procedure can be demonstrated in Fig. 3.

Improved student psychology based optimizer (ISPBO)

Inspiration

Within competitions of student, the individual who achieves the maximum exam scores has been known as the optimal or top individual. In general, other individuals want to improve their performance to become the top individual. Das et al. introduced a novel optimization algorithm known as the Student Psychology Based Optimizer (SPBO)25. For becoming the best candidate in a competition, every candidate must achieve well scores compared to their classmates. So, the candidates should demonstrate high performance in entire subjects. Furthermore, it is important to know that natural talents of a candidate in specific subjects, along with their skills and level of interest. Some candidates may enhance their efforts by comparing themselves to top candidates, while others concentrate on achieving this goal by trying to improve relative to their classmates’ average scores. Taking into account that candidates’ efforts are influenced by their interest in certain subjects, those who consistently invest more effort in particular areas tend to attain better results. Improved student psychology based optimizer (ISPBO)

This study’s validation shows that although the SPBOA typically performs well for many optimization issues, it sometimes falls short in finding optimal solutions because of the issues like inconsistent outcomes, a tendency to get stuck in local optima, as well as premature convergence. In this investigation, two enhancements are implemented to improve and update the algorithm to address its current limitations.

One enhancement is the implementation of the Opposition-Based Learning (OBL) approach. This technique is inspired by the concepts found in Oriental philosophy26,27. The OBL identifies that for every potential solution, there exists a consistent opposing position. This technique is an effective enhancement for enhancing the algorithm’s exploration (global search) abilities. In this process, every solution generates its opposite position that is then assessed in comparison with its opposite, with the superior one chosen as the new position for the solution.

Performance metrics

At this stage, the metrics have been explained. The evaluation has been conducted using the most widely utilized effectiveness metrics in the classification task, which include accuracy, F1-score, precision, and recall. These metrics are computed in the following way:

where, True Positive (TP) indicates the number of positive tweets that have been correctly identified as positive. False Positive (FP) refers to the number of negative tweets that have been mistakenly categorized as positive. Additionally, True Negative (TN) and False Negative (FN) function similarly to FP and TP but for the negative classes. Within the current research, various evaluation metrics are employed, such as precision, accuracy, recall, and F1-score. Precision measures the ratio of true positive categorizations or predictions relative to all of the positive forecasts made by the network, with a high precision rate signifying a low proportion of false positives, thereby minimizing inaccurate positive predictions or categorizations. Recall aims to quantify the ratio of true positive forecasts against entire actual positive samples, where a high recall rate indicates that the model is capable of identifying most real positive instances, thus reducing missed recognitions.

The F1-score has been the harmonic average of precision and recall. It has been mainly useful while dealing with datasets that are imbalanced. A high F1-score indicates a good equilibrium between recall and precision. Ultimately, accuracy aims to measure the ratio of accurate forecasts, which includes true positives and negatives, out of all the predictions. A high accuracy value signifies that the model can effectively predict a large number of instances.

Prevention of data leakage

In order to protect against data leakage that could artificially inflate performance metrics and ensure the integrity of the results, a strict cross-validation protocol has been conducted that did not allow any data leakage between training and testing data. Preprocessing, feature extraction (including user-level statistics such as tweet counts and sentiment scores) and BERT tokenization all occurred independently in each fold of the 10-fold cross-validation. Notably, for the Twitter datasets (SemEval-2016), it was ensured that all tweets from one user were in one fold. This user-level isolation would prevent the model from being trained on a user’s data and then being tested on another tweet from the same users that would be a major source of leakage.

Additionally, optimization of the ESN reservoir parameters using the ISPBO algorithm was carried out exclusively on the fold’s training data, which included an internal validation split. The model for each fold was only evaluated on the previously untouched test data fold. The consistent high performance observed across all folds and, importantly, on the entirely separate Stanford Sentiment Treebank (SST-2) dataset, which originates from a different source (movie reviews), strongly indicates that the results are due to the model’s generalizable capabilities and not a artifact of data leakage.

Statistical significance test results

In order to assess the significance of the higher performance of the newly proposed ESN-ISPBO-BERT model, statistical analysis was performed. A statistical paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (an appropriate nonparametric test for comparing two matched groups) were conducted simulating the results of 10-fold cross-validation, by reporting the mean accuracy and standard deviation per model. The null hypothesis for each test was that the median difference in performance between the proposed model and a baseline model is zero. The alternative hypothesis was that the proposed model’s performance is greater. The results of the statistical tests have conducted only on SemEval-2016-1 in order to show that the suggested model could have performed better than other, which has been represented in Table 15.

The results of the statistical analysis provide strong evidence to reject the null hypothesis for all pairwise comparisons. The p-values for all tests are considerably less than the significance threshold of α = 0.05. This indicates that the observed performance gains of the ESN-ISPBO-BERT model over every listed baseline model are statistically significant and are extremely unlikely to have occurred by random chance. The proposed model does not only outperform these baselines by a small amount, but it does so with high statistical confidence. The degree of analysis establishes that the conjunction of Echo State Networks and the Improved Student Psychology Based Optimizer with BERT embeddings establishes a uniquely powerful architecture for Twitter sentiment analysis and provides improvements that are both practically significant and statistically valid.

Error analysis

While the ESN-ISPBO-BERT framework has clearly demonstrated excellent performance for all datasets, a thorough error analysis has been presented, where various significant limitations have been identified. The majority of tweets were misclassified because they contained sarcasm, irony, or mixed sentiment. The meaning of these tweets could not be captured entirely through lexical or semantic embeddings because of the lack of contextual cues. For example, there are cases where humorous tweets, tweets that included cultural idioms, or tweets that had figurative language were misinterpreted. In such scenarios, BERT’s contextual embeddings would assign neutral polarity to a statement, whereas the contextual polarity was negative or positive.

Ambiguous or domain-specific vocabulary, which is related to vocabulary involving emergent slang or abbreviations shared within particular online communities, poses additional challenges. BERT embeddings and the feature set are fixed since the model does not adapt to Twitter. For real-life datasets in Twitter, sentiment distributions are commonly imbalanced; positive tweets will generally dominate the sentiment distributions. Even with cross-validation and multiple datasets, the sentiment imbalances can lead to a slight bias toward the classes that have greater representation.

With respect to optimization, the ISPBO component will sometimes exhibit sensitivity to hyperparameter initializations, which will result in slight variances in the convergence speed or final performance across runs. While the hybridization with ESN improves computational efficiency, the memory footprint of BERT remains relatively high, which may restrict deployment in low-resource or real-time mobile environments.

Conclusion

Sentiment analysis, a branch of natural language processing (NLP), concentrates on identification and extraction of subjective data from textual feelings and opinions. In this research, we employed two well-known datasets, SemEval-2016-1, SemEval-2016-2, and Stanford Sentiment Treebank (SST-2). The suggested ESN + ISPBO + BERT model was evaluated with several leading models. On SemEval-2016-2, the network obtained accuracy value of 98.82%, precision value of 98.79%, recall value of 98.96%, and F1-score value of 98.87%. Likewise, on SemEval-2016-1, it accomplished accuracy value of 98.76%, precision value of 98.81%, recall value of 98.92%, and F1-score value of 98.86%. The suggested model achieved the values of 98.51%, 98.42%, 98.87%, and 98.64% in terms of accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score on Stanford Sentiment Treebank (SST-2). These findings underscored the efficacy of merging ESN (Echo State Networks), ISPBO (Improved Student Psychology Based Optimization), and BERT embeddings, illustrating the model’s capability to capture sequential and contextual nuances in tweets. The suggested model offered a strong and efficacious architecture for sentiment analysis, especially when dealing with Twitter data, where grasping informal and nuanced language is essential. Its impressive performance indicated applicability in real-life contexts, like monitoring brand sentiment, analyzing political opinions, and assessing customer feedback. Moreover, the effectiveness of the hybrid method highlighted the promise of integrating reservoir calculating, advanced optimization algorithms, and pre-trained models of language for NLP tasks. The primary limitation of this study is that its effectiveness may decrease when applied to tweets from different time periods, cultural contexts, topics, or other social media platforms due to potential distribution shift. For future research, the architecture could be applied to other datasets of sentiment analysis from various languages or domains to assess its versatility. Secondly, further optimization of the ISPBO or the examination of alternative metaheuristics could improve the model’s effectiveness. Ultimately, implementing the network in real-life uses and assessing its efficacy in actual environments would yield valuable information regarding its practical usefulness.
