Sonophotocatalytic efficiency of nickel and copper supported ferrite nanoparticles doped MWCNTs nanocatalyst as a novel catalyst for amoxicillin degradation
Authors: Hossein Alizadeh, Zohreh Poozesh, Mahshad Sadat Sadr, Yashar Fadaei Khamseh Motlagh, Mohadeseh Rezaei, Nezamaddin Mengelizadeh, Haniyeh Mirshekar, Davoud Balarak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The main objective of this study was to evaluate the potential for amoxicillin (AMX) degradation in a sonophotocatalytic system employing a nickel–copper supported ferrite nanoparticle-doped MWCNTs nanocatalyst (NCFM). Our target catalyst was prepared based on the coprecipitation method, and its synthesis was verified by undergoing various techniques, including FTIR, XRD, FESEM/EDX, BET, and TEM. Best results in terms of AMX, COD, and TOC removal efficiencies were recognized in the optimum values of the parameters so that their removal efficiencies were 100%, 95%, and 93%, respectively. The observed optimum values were 25 mg. L⁻¹ AMX, pH of 3, photocatalyst mass of 0.6 g.L⁻¹, time of 40 min, ultrasonic wave frequency of 35 kHz, and UV intensity of 24 W. According to kinetic studies, the sonophotcatalytic process in the presence of NCFM was found to be more successful in degrading AMX compared to other assessed processes, i.e., sonolysis, photolysis, photocatalysis, and sonocatalysis. Moreover, hydroxyl radical (•OH) and hole (h+) were proven to be effective in AMX degradation, based on scavenging tests. A 5-run cycle test evaluating the stability and reusability of our studied catalyst confirmed an efficiency of 95.2% for the last run. Employing our evaluated process was associated with producing degradable effluents (according to high BOD5/COD and BOD5/TOC ratios) and improved results in terms of reducing toxicity (based on results of Escherichia coli (E. coli) growth inhibition rate). Moreover, the utilized system exhibited the ability to mineralize AMX to NH4+, NO3−, SO42−, CO2, and H2O. The above observations could be considered confirmative data for the successful application of the studied system with NCFM for degrading AMX.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28253-6.

Introduction

Recent anthropogenic activities have introduced various emerging chemical pollutants, among which antibiotics are of major concern due to their extensive use in human and veterinary medicine1,2. Once released into the environment, antibiotics can cause chronic toxicity and promote bacterial resistance3. Amoxicillin (AMX), a broad-spectrum β-lactam antibiotic belonging to the penicillin group, is frequently detected in domestic and industrial wastewaters4,5. Its persistence in aquatic environments can lead to microbial resistance, unpleasant odors, and disruption of biological treatment processes6. Moreover, AMX exhibits strong resistance to conventional treatment systems, highlighting the necessity for advanced and efficient removal technologies7.

Following the Covid-19 outbreak, antibiotic consumption in Iran has substantially increased, leading to elevated concentrations of these compounds in wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs). A recent study in Isfahan reported a noticeable rise in antibiotic levels during this period. Globally, β-lactam antibiotics represent more than 65% of the total antibiotic usage, while in Iran they account for approximately 32.6%, with penicillin, amoxicillin, and ampicillin being the most frequently prescribed agents6. Among them, amoxicillin (AMX) is widely misused and overprescribed due to its easy availability and low cost. This situation, intensified by the Covid-19 pandemic and seasonal infections, has resulted in the excessive release of AMX into aquatic environments, making it a critical pollutant of concern8.

Given the associated environmental and health risks, the effective removal of antibiotics from aqueous systems is essential9. Various treatment methods, such as coagulation, adsorption, membrane filtration, electro-oxidation, and chemical oxidation, have been explored. However, these approaches often suffer from drawbacks such as incomplete mineralization, sludge generation, and high operational costs10. In contrast, photocatalytic processes have emerged as promising technologies due to their high mineralization efficiency, low energy requirements, and eco-friendly nature11. Among the studied catalysts, TiO₂ has demonstrated strong activity but is limited by the rapid recombination of photogenerated electron–hole pairs12. Consequently, alternative semiconductors including ZnO, SnO₂, Co₃O₄, Mn₃O₄, Cu₂S, and ZnS have been investigated, although their long-term stability is often hindered by photocorrosion and reduced recyclability13,14.

Considering these drawbacks, the use of bimetallic nanocomposites composed of two different transition metals has gained significant attention due to their enhanced photocatalytic lifetime and the formation of nano-heterojunctions15. Among them, magnetic ferrites (MFe₂O₄; M = Cu, Co, Ni, Zn) have emerged as promising candidates because of their tunable electronic and magnetic properties, high stability, and facile recovery16,17. In particular, mixed ferrites exhibit narrow band gaps, excellent chemical stability, and efficient charge transfer, which improve pollutant degradation performance18,19.

Previous studies have confirmed the superior activity of various mixed ferrites such as CeMnFe₂O₄, Mg-CoFe₂O₄, and CoFe₂O₄ in dye and antibiotic degradation20–22. Among these materials, nickel–copper ferrite (Ni–CuFe₂O₄) demonstrates outstanding surface area, magnetic properties, and structural stability, enabling efficient electrostatic adsorption and charge separation23. The synergistic combination of NiFe₂O₄ and CuFe₂O₄ enhances electron–hole (e⁻/h⁺) transfer, coercivity, and saturation magnetization, while the presence of Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ ions ensures excellent recyclability and structural integrity under experimental conditions24.

Despite their excellent photocatalytic properties, nanocomposites often suffer from particle aggregation, which limits their active surface area and reduces degradation efficiency25. To overcome this drawback, researchers have proposed the use of support materials to immobilize nanoparticles and prevent agglomeration26. Among various supports, MWCNTs have attracted significant attention due to their high surface area, strong adsorption capacity, and excellent electrical conductivity27. MWCNTs facilitate charge transfer, suppress electron–hole recombination, and provide a porous structure that enhances pollutant adsorption and subsequent degradation28.

The addition of ultrasound (US) has become a popular improvement in photocatalytic processes, as it reduces the time required for the complete mineralization of pollutants29. By introducing the mentioned process, cavitation occurs in the aqueous solution, causing the emergence of sonoluminescence and ‘hot spots’. The ultrasonic cavitation generates hot spots that may cause water molecules in contact with the surface of the acoustic catalyst to undergo pyrolysis, which results in the generation of •OH and •H30. The catalyst can subsequently be excited by the light generated with a wide range of wavelengths, facilitating the formation of charge carriers and the generation of electron-hole pairs in CB and VB31. In addition, the implementation of this procedure aids in dispersing the nanoparticles throughout the solution, resulting in more effective treatment of contaminated solutions32. The use of ultrasound in sonophotocatalysis offers the added benefit of continuously cleaning the surface of the photocatalyst, allowing for long-term activity. The combination of light and ultrasound waves in sonophotocatalysis leads to heightened production of reactive radicals, attributed to the role of ultrasonic waves in enhancing the separation of electron-hole pairs. Furthermore, ultrasonic waves can also amplify the mass transfer of pollutants to the surface of the photocatalyst33,34. In a study, by using a photocatalytic system operated with CuO-TiO2/rGO in combination with the US, the efficiency of degrading methylene orange can be significantly increased. In fact, the efficiency can be 3.7 times higher than when using US or photocatalysis alone35.

The present study provides a comprehensive evaluation of AMX degradation using a novel NCFM under a sonophotocatalytic system. This work is distinctive in its breadth and systematic approach, encompassing not only catalyst synthesis and characterization but also detailed investigations of operational parameters, scavenger experiments to identify active oxidative species, toxicity and biodegradability assessments using E. coli bioassays, photocatalyst reusability, mineralization levels and the determination of specific energy consumption. Furthermore, the performance of the sonophotocatalytic process was extensively compared with other treatment pathways, including photocatalysis, sonocatalysis, adsorption, photolysis, and sonolysis, providing a holistic understanding of the efficiency and mechanism of AMX degradation. Overall, this study presents a thorough and integrated approach to evaluating both the physicochemical and biological aspects of an advanced oxidation system based on the NCFM nanocomposite.

The main objective of this study was to investigate the degradation of AMX using a sonophotocatalytic system operated with the synthesized NCFM. The study focused on evaluating the influence of key operational parameters, identifying active oxidative species, assessing catalyst stability and reusability, and determining mineralization, toxicity, and biodegradability of the treated effluents.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

MWCNTs were supplied by an Indian Nanomaterials Pioneers Company (purity > 95%, length = 10–30 μm, inside diameter = 5–10 nm, and outside diameter = 20–30 nm), FeCl₃·6 H₂O, CuCl₂·2 H₂O, NiCl₂·6 H₂O, NaOH, and H₂SO₄ were provided by Merck (Germany). Other chemicals including AMX, isopropyl alcohol (IPA), Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1, 4-benzoquinone (BQ), K₂S₂O₈, and NaN₃ were purchased from Sigma Aldrich, USA. Furthermore, for preparing and diluting the solutions, deionized water was utilized.

Synthesis of the NCFM

The nanocomposite NCFM was synthesized via a co-precipitation method (Fig. 1)23. Initially, 1.0 g of MWCNTs was dispersed in 50 mL of deionized water using ultrasonication for 20 min. Subsequently, 0.50 g of FeCl₃·6 H₂O, 0.25 g of CuCl₂·2 H₂O, and 0.25 g of NiCl₂·6 H₂O were added into the suspension and stirred for 90 min. The pH of the solution was adjusted to 12 using NaOH. The mixture was then maintained in a water bath at 100 °C under continuous stirring for 6 h. The resulting precipitate was collected using a 0.22 μm membrane filter, thoroughly washed with deionized water, and dried at room temperature. Lastly, the calcination step (400 °C, 2 h) was performed in a closed crucible inside a muffle furnace under limited oxygen exposure to prevent any degradation or oxidation of the MWCNTs.

Experiments

The sonophotocatalytic experiments were conducted in a 250 mL cylindrical Pyrex batch reactor equipped with a quartz window to allow UV irradiation. The reactor was partially immersed in an ultrasonic bath (Sunshine SS-6508T, China) operating at adjustable frequencies (20–50 kHz) with an acoustic power intensity of 1–2 W·cm⁻². A Philips mercury UV lamp (λ = 254 nm, 8–32 W) was positioned vertically above the reactor at a fixed distance of 5 cm to provide uniform illumination. The reactor was fitted with a magnetic stirrer to ensure homogeneous suspension of the photocatalyst and prevent sedimentation. The solution temperature was maintained at 25 ± 2 °C by continuous water circulation through the ultrasonic bath. The catalyst (0.6 g L⁻¹) was dispersed in 100 mL of AMX solution of desired concentration and stirred in the dark for 30 min to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium before illumination. 0.1 NaOH, and H₂SO₄ were used to adjust the pH. At known times, 1 mL sample was withdrawn from the AMX solution during its mixing. The AMX level of samples was estimated based on absorption measurement through the employment of a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (DR5000, Hach, Canada) at λmax = 280 nm. The changes in the efficiency of the sonophotocatalytic process were monitored under different operational conditions, such as catalyst mass, AMX concentration, pH, UV intensity, and US frequency. The experiments in the current research were carried out three times, and fortunately, the pollutant removal percentage was consistent in different repetitions. Each of the photolysis, sonolysis, adsorption, sonocatalytic, photocatalytic, and sonophotocatalytic experiments was conducted separately under optimized conditions for 60 min. As a instance, the adsorption experiment was performed solely in the presence of the catalyst, without light irradiation or ultrasonic assistance.

In addition, our studies also included investigations into adsorption, sonolysis, photolysis, photocatalysis, and sonocatalysis. This was undertaken to provide further information about the evaluated systems in our study and to gain a greater understanding of the degradation mechanism. The conditions considered for these studies were an AMX concentration of 50 mg.L⁻¹, a pH of 5, and a catalyst mass of 0.6 g.L⁻¹. Moreover, our evaluated catalyst was exposed to tests of catalytic stability based on AMX degradation in five consecutive cycles in the presence of the catalyst. The catalyst was then employed in the next cycle after collection, washing with double-distilled water and ethanol, and drying in an oven. For all cycles, the experimental conditions were similar. The active species involved in the degradation were identified based on scavenging tests using 1 mM of commonly used scavengers (IPA, EDTA, and BQ). Employed analyses and analytical methods are shown in Section S1 (Supplementary Information).

Toxicity test

The toxicity of the effluent from the studied process was measured using E. coli. For this purpose, in an autoclave, test tubes containing broth lactate were sterilized, and E. coli was incubated on an EMB agar medium (at 37 °C for 24 h). A control test was also included in this part of the study. The toxicity test tubes contained one microbe loop + 1 mL reactor outlet + 10 mL of culture medium. At the end of incubation (37 °C), test tubes were assessed in terms of absorbance using UV–Vis spectrophotometry at λ = 600 nm.

Results and discussion

Specification prepared photocatalyst

The surface morphology of the MWCNTs and NCFM was examined through SEM imaging (Fig. 2a–b). In Fig. 2b, morphological characteristics obtained based on SEM were indicative of the loading NCF particles onto the MWCNTs, which was associated with a comparatively even dispersal. Based on Fig. 2c (TEM image), the scattering of NCF particles on the outer walls of the MWCNTs is noticeable. Moreover, the average diameter was in the range of 3–14 nm, according to the histogram related to size distribution based on the TEM image (Fig. 2d). EDX analysis was utilized to verify the elemental composition of the catalyst (Fig. 2e). The detection of Cu, Ni, Fe, and O elements, along with the primary component C, in the synthesized catalyst, indicates the successful integration of NCF on MWCNTs.

The crystalline structure of the synthesized NCFM nanocomposite was further confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Fig. 3a). The diffraction pattern exhibited distinct peaks at 2θ values of approximately 16.7°, 32.5°, 46.3°, 57.2°, and 66.8°, which were indexed to the (111), (220), (400), (440), and (511) planes, respectively. These reflections are characteristic of a cubic spinel structure, confirming the formation of Ni–Cu mixed ferrite (NiCuFe₂O₄). The observed diffraction peaks are in good agreement with the standard JCPDS/PDF cards No. 10–0325 for NiFe₂O₄ and No. 34–0425 (or 25–0283) for CuFe₂O₄, indicating the successful incorporation of Ni²⁺ and Cu²⁺ ions within the spinel lattice (space group Fd3m)36. The absence of additional peaks related to Fe₂O₃, NiO, or CuO phases demonstrates the high phase purity of the obtained ferrite. The average crystallite size of the NiCuFe₂O₄ phase, calculated using the Scherrer equation (Kλ/βcosθ), was found to be in the nanometric range and consistent with TEM observations (3–14 nm), confirming the formation of highly crystalline spinel nanoparticles uniformly anchored on the surface of the MWCNTs.

The apparent disappearance of the MWCNTs peaks at 25° and 42° (corresponding to the (002) and (100) planes of graphitic carbon) after ferrite deposition does not imply the complete destruction of MWCNTs, but rather partial amorphization and loss of graphitic order due to nanoparticle anchoring and mild oxidation during synthesis. Similar observations have been reported for ferrite-decorated MWCNTs nanocomposites, where XRD peaks of MWCNTs weaken or vanish owing to surface coverage and structural distortion37,38.

The FT-IR spectra of MWCNTs and NCFM are shown in Fig. 3b. In the spectrum of pristine MWCNTs, several characteristic absorption bands are observed. The broad band centered around 3440 cm⁻¹ corresponds to the stretching vibration of surface hydroxyl (–OH) groups and adsorbed water molecules. The peaks appearing near 1600 cm⁻¹ and 1380 cm⁻¹ are assigned to N–H bending and C–OH bending vibrations, respectively, confirming the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups such as carboxyl and hydroxyl groups on the CNT surface36. The weak band at ~ 1120 cm⁻¹ is attributed to C–O stretching vibrations from carboxylic or epoxy functionalities, which serve as anchoring sites for the deposition of metal ions during the co-precipitation process. After the incorporation of Ni–Cu ferrite nanoparticles, several changes are evident in the FT-IR spectrum of the NCFM composite. The broad O–H stretching band becomes more intense and slightly shifts, suggesting increased hydroxyl interactions and the formation of hydrogen bonds between the ferrite nanoparticles and functionalized MWCNTs. Moreover, the overall intensity of the oxygenated functional group bands decreases, indicating partial consumption of these groups during the coordination and nucleation of metal ions onto the MWCNTs surface. Most notably, a new and prominent absorption band appears between 500 and 600 cm⁻¹, which is characteristic of the Fe–O stretching vibration in the tetrahedral and octahedral sites of a spinel ferrite structure37. The appearance and enhancement of this band in the NCFM spectrum confirm the successful formation of the Ni–Cu ferrite phase on the MWCNTs framework. It should be emphasized that the weak feature occasionally observed around 560 cm⁻¹ in pristine MWCNTs does not correspond to ferrite formation; rather, it arises from trace amounts of metallic catalyst residues (Fe, Ni, or Co) that typically remain from the chemical vapor deposition (CVD) synthesis of MWCNTs. The significant increase in intensity and definition of the 500–600 cm⁻¹ band in NCFM unambiguously verifies the presence of the spinel ferrite structure anchored onto the MWCNTs surface38.

Moreover, the BET analysis (Fig. 3c) revealed a remarkably high specific surface area of 121.09 m².g⁻¹ for the synthesized NCFM nanocomposite, which is highly favorable for enhancing photocatalytic performance due to the abundance of accessible active sites. The N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherm of the NCFM nanocomposite is characteristic of a mesoporous material and corresponds to IUPAC Type IV behavior, indicating capillary condensation within mesopores. Closer inspection of the hysteresis loop shows a nearly vertical adsorption branch at intermediate relative pressures and an open, non-closing desorption branch that is more consistent with an H3-type hysteresis. H3 loops are commonly associated with aggregates of plate-like particles and slit-shaped pores (or non-rigid slit-like pores) and typically do not show a limiting adsorption at high p/p₀, which matches the observed isotherm shape here39. Furthermore, the pore size distribution curve (Fig. 3d) is in good agreement with the TEM image (Fig. 2c), confirming that the majority of pores in NCFM are smaller than 10 nm.

To further confirm the improved charge separation and reduced electron–hole recombination in the synthesized NCFM nanocomposite, photoluminescence (PL) spectra were recorded at an excitation wavelength of 320 nm, as shown in Fig. 4a. The pristine Ni–Cu ferrite displayed a strong emission peak centered around ~ 430 nm, which corresponds to the radiative recombination of photoinduced charge carriers. However, after the incorporation of MWCNTs, the PL intensity of the NCFM composite significantly decreased37. This reduction in emission intensity indicates that the MWCNTs effectively acted as an electron acceptor and transporter, facilitating the migration of photogenerated electrons and thereby suppressing e⁻/h⁺ recombination. The observed quenching in PL emission confirms that the heterojunction interface between Ni–Cu ferrite and MWCNTs promotes efficient charge separation, which correlates well with the enhanced photocatalytic and sonophotocatalytic performance of the NCFM catalyst.

The optical properties of the synthesized NCFM nanocomposite were further evaluated using UV–Vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (DRS), and the obtained spectra are illustrated in Fig. 4b. The absorption edge of NCFM appeared around 560 nm, which corresponds to a typical semiconductor behavior. Using the Tauc plot method (Fig. 4c), the band gap energy (Eg) of the NCFM was estimated to be 2.1 eV, indicating a noticeable reduction compared to pristine ferrite nanoparticles (2.5 eV). This narrowing of the band gap can be attributed to the strong interfacial interaction between Ni–Cu ferrite and the conductive network of MWCNTs, which facilitates electron delocalization and promotes light absorption in the visible region. The lower band gap enhances the generation of photoinduced charge carriers under UV–Vis irradiation, thereby improving the photocatalytic efficiency of the NCFM catalyst. Such behavior demonstrates that coupling MWCNTs with Ni–Cu ferrite significantly improves optical response and accelerates charge transfer, leading to superior sonophotocatalytic degradation performance toward AMX40.

Effect of operating factors

Effect of pH

As known, solution pH influences the characteristics of the photocatalyst and the contaminant surface charges and affects the adsorption behavior. Based on this, its evaluation in the range of 3–11 was considered in our study for degrading AMX in the presence of NCFM (Fig. 5a). The tests related to the studied parameter were carried out with 0.6 g.L− 1 NCFM, 100 mg.L− 1 AMX, 32 W intensity, and 35 kHz ultrasound frequency. Our observations were indicative of a reduction in AMX degradation efficiency (100 to 65.11%) by changing pH from 3 to 11. The description of this event is based on alterations of the surface charge of the pollutant and photocatalyst. Besides, for NCFM, the point of zero charge was obtained to be 7.1 (Fig. 5b). Hence, a positive surface is expected for particles at pH values lower than 7.1, while it will be negative for pH values higher than 7.1. In addition, AMX possesses three ionizable functional groups with pKa values of approximately 2.4 (carboxylic acid), 7.4 (amino group), and 9.6 (phenolic hydroxyl) (Fig. 5c)12. Therefore, AMX exists mainly in its cationic form at pH < 2.4, as a zwitterion in the intermediate range (2.4 < pH < 7.4), and becomes predominantly anionic at pH > 7.441,42. Consequently, at acidic pH values, electrostatic attraction between the positively charged catalyst surface and the deprotonated carboxyl group of AMX enhances adsorption, whereas under alkaline conditions, electrostatic repulsion between negatively charged species dominates. Another point that should be considered is the high production of OH− at an alkaline pH range; this restricts the significant adsorption of AMX by the catalyst. This observation aligns with findings in another study conducted to evaluate the photocatalytic process in removing AMX21.

Effect of catalyst dosage

The changes in degradation efficiency of AMX were evaluated by applying different catalyst masses in the studied system (Fig. 6a). For this purpose, the following conditions were utilized in operating the system: AMX concentration of 25 mg.L− 1, US of 35 kHz, UV of 8 W, and pH of 3. In this part, as higher catalyst masses were utilized, an improvement in the efficiency of the system for removing AMX was obtained. For catalyst concentrations of 0.1 and 0.6 g.L− 1, removal efficiencies of 57.2% and 100% were detected, respectively. The observed event can be the result of the increase in the surface active sites of the catalyst, which are able to carry out the adsorption process along with the sonocatalytic process simultaneously43. Raising the amount of catalyst in the reaction mixture results in a higher yield of oxidizing radicals, as more light becomes available. Furthermore, augmenting the catalyst concentration to the optimal level boosts the activity of the holes by generating extra nuclei. According to Samarghandi et al.40, the efficiency of removing acid blue 113 dye through photocatalytic degradation was went up from 45% to 90% as the dose of ZnO-GAC catalyst was improved from 0.2 g to 1 g.L− 1. However, a further increase in the dosage of the catalyst resulted in a decrease in the removal percentage. This decrease can be attributed to the overlapping of the active sites of the catalyst surface. Also, a high catalyst dose will cause turbidity and interference in light transmission. Furthermore, the levels of produced cavitation bubbles are boosted in the presence of more active sites; this develops photodegradation by improving the production of reactive species44. Soomro et al.45 and Sun et al.46 have observed similar trends for the mentioned parameter, where higher catalyst mass resulted in higher pollutant degradation efficiency due to the accessibility to a higher active surface area and higher cavitation activity.

Effect of UV light intensity

The intensity of UV light, as another parameter affecting the degradation efficiency, was evaluated in the degradation of AMX (Fig. 6b). To do this, optimal conditions were considered at NCFM mass of 0.6 g.L− 1, AMX amount of 100 mg.L− 1, a pH of 3, and US of 35 kHz. Our observations were representative of the enhancement in AMX degradation from 74.5% to 100% for UV intensities from 8 to 32 W. The result may arise from abundance of excited e−/h+ pairs, which leads to enhanced levels of free radicals. When there are abundant levels of pairs, the chance of AMX oxidation occurs due to the improvement the reaction rate between the active sites and pollutant molecules47. Reza et al.48 further highlighted the enhancement in degradation efficiency, attributing it to the increased generation of reactive species. This phenomenon occurs through the heightened production of protons, necessary for facilitating the transfer of electrons from the valence band level to the conduction band level.

Effect of ultrasonic frequency

Assessing the degradation of AMX under various US frequencies (20, 35, and 50 kHz) was also undertaken as another aspect of our investigation. This segment involved utilizing an AMX concentration of 100 mg.L− 1, a dosage of 0.6 g.L− 1 for NCFM, maintaining a pH of 3, and employing UV with a power of 24 W (Fig. 6c). Considering our observations, frequencies from 20 to 35 kHz could be effective in enhancing the AMX degradation efficiency from 91.2% to 100%. The increase in cavitation flow, enhancing the mass transfer rate in the aqueous phase and the release rate of reactive species, could have caused the above-mentioned enhancement49. The high US waves are capable of better cleaning the surface of the studied catalyst; this may participate in improving its catalytic activity50. Cavitation governs the generation of •OH and the development of h+ on the catalyst (surface or inner) in our evaluated system51. Moreover, as the frequency is boosted, as the frequency is boosted, a rise in temperature and a decline in the ability of nanoparticles for adsorption are observed. Due to this phenomenon, the adsorption levels of contaminant molecules on active sites of the catalyst are reduced, which leads to declining degradation52. Nevertheless, there was no noteworthy change in efficiency and kinetic rate at 50 kHz. Accordingly, 35 kHz was considered as US wave intensity for the subsequent experiments. Effect of initial AMX concentration

System efficiency was also evaluated based on variations in AMX concentration (Fig. 6d). The experiments pertaining to this factor were carried out at a pH of 3, using a 0.6 g.L− 1 catalyst mass, and subjected to UV radiation at 32 W and US at 35 kHz. According to our observations, for low concentrations of AMX, degradation efficiencies were higher, while they decreased at higher concentrations. This might be explained as follows: Fixed levels of e−/h+ pairs are generated for contaminant degradation under constant catalyst mass, UV, and US conditions54. In the presence of high AMX concentrations, the rate of the photon absorption (used in the photocatalytic reaction) by the catalyst surface is boosted, which reduces the degradation of pollutants due to declining radical generation55. The reduction in effectiveness may be linked to factors such as an insufficient photocatalyst-to-AMX ratio, generation of excessive product amounts (which compete with the primary molecule for reactive species consumption), and elevated solution turbidity (which hinders the penetration of light into the reaction medium)56. Ji et al.57 and Liu et al.58 also reported similar findings regarding the effect of the evaluated parameter.

Catalyst activity in different treatment systems

In this section, we compared the results obtained from photolysis, sonolysis, adsorption, sonocatalytic, photocatalytic, and sonophotocatalytic used for eliminating AMX (Fig. 7a). These processes were operated under optimal conditions. As depicted by the results, degradation efficiencies of 30.2% and 18.2%, after 60 min, were detected for the utilization of sonolysis and photolysis, respectively. In the adsorption process, a remarkable improvement in AMX removal (48.9% after 60 min) was recognized by increasing time, which is ascribed to the pHzpc of the catalyst and pKa of AMX. The efficient adsorption of the AMX molecules by the catalyst occurred at a pH of 3. In addition, good adsorption can be caused by the interaction of active sites, positively charged surface of CNTs, and carboxylate group (-COO-) of AMX in acidic pH59.

Adding the catalyst to photolysis and sonolysis reactors results in a remarkable enhancement in degradation efficiency. In the sonocatalysis process, better dispersion of the catalyst and the effect of ultrasound on the surface of the catalyst (cleaning of the surface) are considered as reasons for increasing the efficiency. Furthermore, it is able to create cavitation bubbles for decomposing water molecules and the formation of free radicals60. Improving efficiency in a photocatalysis reactor involves the absorption of photon energy on the catalyst’s surface, resulting in the generation of h+ through the excitation of electrons from the valence band to the conduction band. The generated h+ actively contributes to the production of additional •OH by decomposing water molecules27. Finally, in the case of the sonophotocatalytic process (US/UV/NCFM), AMX was observed to be degraded completely at 40 min; for this process, there is a synergistic effect by the evaluated system for improved production of •OH, after calculating the PFO constant rate, as represented in Fig. 7b. Accordingly, sonophotocatalytic degradation system exhibits a 1.28-fold synergistic effect. The capability of the ultrasound in producing H2O2, followed by the decomposition of H2O2 in presence of UV for generating •OH, may be attributed as the reason for AMX degradation in the hybrid process. In addition, US intensifies the collision between pollutant molecules and catalyst particles during the adsorption–desorption process occurring in the reactor61.

For all processes, the regression coefficient exceeded 0.9, as shown in Fig. 7b. The regression coefficients (R²) for the photolysis, sonolysis, adsorption, sonocatalytic, photocatalytic, and sonophotocatalytic experiments were 0.991, 0.992, 0.998, 0.981, 0.978, and 0.987, respectively. The reaction rate constant or K for AMX degradation using photocatalysis and sonophotocatalysis processes are 0.089 and 0.133 min− 1, respectively, indicating a degradation rate 1.49 times faster for sonophotocatalysis compared to photocatalysis. In addition, the reaction rate coefficients for adsorption, photolysis, sonolysis, and sonocatalysis processes were 0.0085, 0.0027, 0.0047, and 0.0147, respectively. The comparison of the reaction rate coefficient shows a high degradation rate with the sonophotocatalysis process compared to adsorption, photolysis, sonolysis, and sonocatalysis. Specifically, its reaction rate is 15.6 and 49.25 times greater than adsorption and photolysis, respectively, and 28.2 and 9.05 times greater than sonolysis and sonocatalysis.

The comparison made in Table S1 provides comprehensive data on the potential of the evaluated system, i.e., NCFM. Based on this, the removal percentages of 88 to 100 could be recognized for different systems mentioned in Table. The difference in efficiencies might be ascribed to dissimilarities in operating conditions and the structural features of catalyst and studied contaminants. The superior performance of our utilized catalyst in the removal of AMX can be attributed to the synergistic effects of Fe2O4, facilitating rapid electron transfer (for generating O2•−), and the accelerated participation of h+ (for generating •OH). In addition, our system, in a short time, could offer high removal efficiency compared to other systems cited in Table S1, which is representative of the positive role of the US on the catalytic systems. highlighting the positive role of ultrasound (US) in catalytic systems. According to available reports, the efficiency enhancement achieved by US can be attributed to the following mechanisms: (1) the breakdown of water molecules by ultrasound, producing more reactive species; (2) improved mass transfer between the aqueous phase and the photocatalyst surface, as well as better particle dispersion through cavitation; and (3) cleaning of the catalyst surface and regeneration of its active sites by acoustic cavitation19. Adsorption using only carbon nanotubes resulted in a 47% removal rate within 60 min, which exceeded that of the studied nanocomposite. This is attributed to the fact that carbon nanotubes have a higher adsorption capacity compared to the nanocomposite. However, a fundamental issue with using carbon nanotubes is the challenge of collecting and reusing them. In a study conducted by Samy et al.62 to remove 1,4 dioxane by Cu–Fe–ZnO catalyst, the results showed that the 1,4 dioxane removal by the Cu–Fe–ZnO/biochar composite reached 93.1 ± 8.7% at 0.6 g.L− 1 within 90 min, whereas 1.2 g. L− 1 of bare biochar achieved only 42.7 ± 3.3% removal in 210 min.

Identifying oxidative agents

Identifying the main oxidative agents responsible for AMX degradation in the evaluated sonophotocatalytic system was performed through a series of scavenging experiments using EDTA, IPA, and BQ (Fig. 8). The addition of these scavengers to the reaction medium resulted in a decline in degradation efficiency, confirming the activity of multiple reactive species in the oxidation process. In the absence of scavengers, the degradation efficiency reached 100%. The highest decrease in AMX removal (from 100% to 64.1%) was observed in the presence of IPA, indicating that photogenerated hydroxyl radicals (•OH) played the most significant role in the oxidation of AMX. The introduction of EDTA reduced the degradation efficiency to 75.2%, highlighting the key contribution of h⁺, while the presence of BQ caused only a minor reduction (to 84.1%), suggesting a limited role of O₂•⁻ in the process.

To further elucidate the mechanism, additional scavenging experiments were carried out to evaluate the influence of electron (e⁻) and non-radical species. K₂S₂O₈ (1 mM) were employed as electron scavengers, while sodium azide (NaN₃, 1 mM) was used as a singlet oxygen (¹O₂) quencher. The presence of K₂S₂O₈ slightly increased AMX degradation efficiency to 10%, due to their effective capture of photogenerated electrons and suppression of e⁻/h⁺ recombination, which enhances the production of oxidative species such as •OH and h⁺. In contrast, the addition of NaN₃ reduced the degradation efficiency from 100% to approximately 89.3%, confirming the formation and participation of singlet oxygen (¹O₂) as a non-radical oxidant. Although •OH and h⁺ remain the dominant active species, the involvement of ¹O₂ provides additional oxidation capacity and improves the selectivity of the process19.

These observations, along with previous findings, confirm that the NCFM/UV/US system operates through a synergistic mechanism involving both radical and non-radical (¹O₂) oxidative pathways, which collectively contribute to the efficient degradation and mineralization of AMX molecules. Gholami et al.61 discovered that the main active species responsible for the sonophotocatalytic degradation of pollutants in the MMH/g-C3N4/UV/US system are OH, h+, and O2•−, achieved by introducing EDTA, BQ, and TBA to the system.

To clarify the photocatalytic degradation mechanism of AMX in the NCFM/UV/US system, the experimental results of scavenger tests, mineralization data, and previous reports were considered. The main reactive species involved were identified as •OH, photogenerated h⁺, and O₂•⁻. Figure 9 proposed degradation pathway of amoxicillin (AMX) in the NCFM/UV/US system, showing the main intermediates and reactive species (•OH, h⁺, O₂•⁻).

Upon simultaneous irradiation with UV light and ultrasound, the NCFM catalyst absorbs photons, and electrons (e⁻) are promoted from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB) of Ni–Cu ferrite, leaving h⁺ behind (Eqs. 6 and 7)37. The photogenerated electrons are rapidly transferred to the MWCNTs network, which acts as an electron sink and transport channel, effectively suppressing e⁻/h⁺ recombination. The electrons in the CB react with dissolved oxygen molecules to generate superoxide radicals (O₂•⁻), while the photogenerated holes oxidize surface-adsorbed water or hydroxide ions to form •OH (Eqs. 8–11). These reactive species act synergistically to attack and oxidize AMX molecules adsorbed on the catalyst surface19. The generated •OH and h⁺ species are primarily responsible for the oxidative degradation of AMX. The β-lactam ring and aromatic amino groups in the AMX molecule are highly susceptible to radical attack, leading to cleavage of the amide bond and hydroxylation of the aromatic ring. This process results in the formation of several short-chain intermediates such as hydroxy-amoxicillin, aminophenol, hydroquinone, p-hydroxybenzoic acid, and small organic acids (e.g., oxalic, acetic, and formic acids). These intermediates are subsequently mineralized into inorganic end-products including CO₂, H₂O, NO₃⁻, NH₄⁺, and SO₄²⁻.

Therefore, the overall mechanism involves: (1) Photoexcitation of NCFM under UV/US irradiation, generating e⁻/h⁺ pairs; (2) Charge separation enhancement via MWCNTs, which suppresses recombination; (3) Formation of reactive radicals (•OH, h⁺, O₂•⁻) responsible for oxidation; and (4) Stepwise degradation of AMX molecules through hydroxylation, deamination, and decarboxylation, leading to mineralization. This proposed mechanism demonstrates the synergistic action between photocatalytic and sonochemical effects and is consistent with the observed high efficiency and mineralization performance of the Stability and reusability of the catalyst

Another critical characteristic for the effective deployment of a catalyst in real-scale sonophotocatalytic systems is its stability and reusability65. Thus, they were considered as a sub-objective of our study and were studied by conducting five consecutive reaction cycles in the presence of NCFM (Fig. 10a); these investigations were done under optimal conditions obtained from previous experiments. Our findings suggested a modest decrease in the system’s effectiveness (from 100% to 95.2%), likely attributed to the saturation of certain active catalyst sites and the loss of some catalysts in the course of the reuse process66. Nevertheless, an efficiency of over 95.2% is indicative of sufficient reusability of new NCFM nanocomposite in degrading using the evaluated process. Similarly, co-doped TiO2@functionalized SWCNTs utilized in a study conducted by Isari et al.56 represented a substantial reusability, maintaining high degradation efficiency even after five consecutive cycles. Improving the recyclability of the composite depends on several factors such as increasing the production of reactive radicals and improved mass transfer of pollutants to the photocatalyst surface. Within the sonophotocatalyst process, microbubbles undergo expansion as they absorb energy from the ultrasonic field, ultimately giving rise to the generation of a substantial quantity of hydroxyl radicals. This process involves the excitation of the photocatalyst by both light and ultrasonic radiation, inducing the formation of more active radicals. Importantly, this excitation not only serves to cleanse the active sites of particles by eliminating attached byproducts but also fosters the enhancement of mechanisms such as adsorption, mass transfer, and particle dispersion. The interplay of these mechanisms in the photocatalysis process contributes to the ongoing stability of the composite material55.

Toxicity, mineralization and UV–Vis absorption analysis

Due to the importance allocated to mineralization and biodegradability of effluents by various studies, investigating these factors was also performed for the outlets of evaluated sonophotocatalytic process employed for degrading AMX. For this purpose, the levels of removal efficiencies of TOC, COD, BOD5/TOC, and BOD5/COD obtained by the NCFM/US/UV system were recorded (Fig. 10b). According to findings, after 120 min, a significant decrease in the removal of COD and BOD5 was observed; based on this, the mineralization process for the studied pollutant is done rapidly and in a short time. Furthermore, the AMX degradation in the evaluated system in the presence of NCFM could provide high efficiencies, which is due to the facts that our prepared catalyst could offer extraordinary adsorption levels and the high potential of the electron-hole pair for generating active radicals37. Although complete degradation of AMX molecules was achieved within 40 min, total mineralization required a longer reaction time (up to 120 min). This distinction arises because degradation refers to the breakdown of the parent AMX structure into intermediate organic compounds, whereas mineralization denotes the complete conversion of these intermediates into inorganic end-products (CO₂, H₂O, NO₃⁻, and SO₄²⁻). Therefore, the 40-min reaction represents full pollutant removal, while the 120-min period corresponds to complete mineralization and detoxification of the solution.

The BOD₅/COD and BOD₅/TOC ratios shown in the inset of Fig. 10b provide further evidence of the effectiveness of the sonophotocatalytic process in enhancing the biodegradability of the AMX-containing solution. Significantly, a notable enhancement in both BOD5/COD and BOD5/TOC ratios was identified after 120 min, with values shifting from 0.22 to 0.75 for BOD5/COD and 0.6 to 1.2 for BOD5/TOC. Wastewater is considered non-biodegradable when the BOD5/COD ratio is below 0.4 and the BOD5/TOC ratio is below 0.8. In this study, the transformation of non-biodegradable wastewater into biodegradable wastewater was successfully achieved. Tan et al.67 and Vigneshwaran et al.52 have similar trends for the mentioned parameter so that for higher time, higher BOD5/COD and BOD5/TOC ratios are noted, indicating the conversion of non-biodegradable wastewater into biodegradable wastewater.

The mentioned findings are considered as evidence for confirming the reduction of the toxicity and improvement of the biodegradability. Providing more confirmative evidence for the above results was done by conducting toxicity tests; for this, the growth inhibition percentage of E. coli (Eq. 12) was inspected in different processes, represented in Fig. 10c. Accordingly, bacterial growth inhibition represented a gentle reduction (19%) in the direct photolysis process after 180 min. Generating remarkable levels of toxic substances in the photolysis system leads to representing small changes in growth inhibition. However, this rate was diminished from 56.1 to 18.7% after adding NCFM to the photolysis system. The mentioned findings are confirmed by the obtained values such as the highest AMX (100%) and COD (93%) removal efficiencies. The highest reduction in inhibition percentage was reported for the sonophotocatalytic process (56.1 to 7.2%). According to our observations, the sonophotocatalytic degradation process could exhibit the capability of application before the biological treatment, as a pretreatment method, for providing the conditions to degrade the resistant compounds. The UV–Vis absorption spectra of amoxicillin (AMX, 25 mg·L⁻¹, pH = 3) during sonophotocatalytic degradation using NCFM dose (0.6 g·L⁻¹ catalyst, 35 kHz ultrasound frequency, and 24 W UV light) are presented in Fig. 10d.

At the beginning of the reaction (0 min), a strong absorption band centered at λmax ≈ 280 nm was observed, which is attributed to the π→π* electronic transitions of the aromatic ring and β-lactam functional groups in the AMX molecule. As the reaction proceeded, the absorbance intensity at 280 nm gradually decreased with increasing reaction time (10–120 min), indicating the progressive decomposition of AMX molecules in the reaction medium. After 120 min, the characteristic absorption peak of AMX nearly disappeared, confirming the complete degradation of the parent antibiotic compound. The continuous decline of the absorption band demonstrates the breakdown of the conjugated chromophoric system responsible for light absorption in the AMX structure. Additionally, the absence of any new peaks in the visible region (300–400 nm) indicates that no stable or colored intermediate species were formed during the degradation process.

This spectral evolution strongly supports the kinetic results and confirms the effective photocatalytic and sonochemical synergy of the NCFM/UV/US system. The simultaneous generation of •OH radicals and photogenerated holes (h⁺) leads to enhanced oxidation of AMX molecules and their conversion into non-aromatic, non-absorbing species. The complete disappearance of the absorption peak at 280 nm after 120 min serves as direct spectroscopic evidence of the total degradation and mineralization of AMX under the optimal sonophotocatalytic conditions.

Identification and change of inorganic ions

When a contaminant is exposed to the mineralization process in the presence of carbon, sulfur, and nitrogen atoms, the production of CO2, SO42−, and nitrogenous compounds (such as NH4+ and NO3−) is expected. Based on this, measuring the released values of NH4+, NO3−, and SO42− was considered as the basis for determining the AMX mineralization level during the sonophotocatalytic process (Fig. 10e). According to observations, the levels of NH4+ (0 to 5.1 mg. L− 1) and NO3− (0 to 1.97 mg.L− 1) enhanced during the studied AMX degradation process (10 to 120 min), which is indicative of the likelihood of combination with intermediates or loss as N2 gas69. Moreover, an enhancement in the SO42− levels (0 to 1.1 mg.L− 1) is detectable after 120 min, which represents that at the initial stages of the process, there are no significant changes in the –SO3− group of AMX, and its transformation into SO42− ions is done slowly. The potential of producing SO42−, NH4+, NO3−, and NO2− during the 90-hour degradation of AMX was also documented by Benacherine et al. who assessed the mineralization of AMX via a photocatalytic process70.

Conclusion

Degrading AMX in a sonophotocatalytic system was evaluated in the presence of our synthesized nanocomposite, i.e., NCFM. This nanocomposite was found to be effective in the degradation of AMX and was significantly stable in the conducted process. The NCFM/UV/US system revealed the potential for complete degradation of AMX, which might be ascribed to activities of the generated reactive species (e.g., O2•−, h+, and •OH). Based on the results of five consecutive reaction cycles for evaluating the prepared nanocomposite stability, it demonstrated remarkable recyclability, with a minimum reduction in efficiency detected after the completion of cycles. As observed, removal efficiencies of TOC, BOD₅/TOC, and BOD₅/COD were used to report the levels of mineralization and biodegradability of the system’s effluent. Based on it, a decline was recognized for COD and TOC after 120 min, and an increase in the BOD5/COD ratio (0.22 to 0.75) was observed; these observations can be introduced as evidence for the progression of biodegradability in the process effluents. The suitability and effectiveness of the studied system were also confirmed by a toxicity test and an evaluation of the levels of inorganic ions (e.g., NH4+, NO3−, and SO42−) in the treated solution.
