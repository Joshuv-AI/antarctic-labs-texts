On the Age of Information in Single-Server Queues with Aged Updates
Fernando Miguelez, Urtzi Ayesta, Josu Doncel, Maria Dolores Ugarte

Abstract
The Age of Information (AoI) is a performance metric that quantifies the freshness of data in systems where timely updates are critical. Most state-of-the-art methods typically assume that packets enter the monitored system with zero age, neglecting situations, such as those prevalent in multi-hop networks or distributed sensing, where packets experience prior delays. In this paper, the AoI is investigated when packets have a non-zero initial age. We derive an expression for the average AoI in this setting, showing that it equals the standard AoI plus a correction term involving the correlation between packet age and inter-departure times. When these variables are independent, the expression simplifies to an additive correction equal to the mean initial age. In cases where the dependency structure is unknown, we also establish lower and upper bounds for the correction term. We demonstrate the applicability of our approach across various queueing scenarios, such as forwarding, tandem, and retrial queues. Additionally, we explore the accuracy of the derived bounds on a tandem composed of several queues, a model that has not yet been analytically solved from an age perspective.



Keywords
: Age of Information, aged updates, single-server queue, tandem queues, retrial queues

Introduction

The Age of Information (AoI) is a relatively new metric that measures the freshness of the knowledge we have about the status of a remote system. More specifically, the AoI of a source is defined as the time elapsed since the generation of the last successfully received packet containing information about that source. The emergence of AoI in the seminal paper
[
1
]
, has triggered an interest in its analysis in the context of queueing theory, which has been notably motivated by the fact that policies that optimise classical performance metrics, such as throughput, delay or package-loss probability, do not necessarily minimise the AoI.

Most of the research in AoI considers the case in which the age of the packets to be delivered is zero, which corresponds to packets entering the communication channel as soon as they are generated. In this paper, the focus is on cases where packets to be delivered have a non-zero age. This situation arises when packets have already spent time in another system before reaching the final link, which is common in multi-hop networks, distributed sensing, and edge computing. For instance, in a wireless network, updates may be generated at a sensor, relayed through multiple intermediate nodes, and eventually delivered to a destination. By the time they enter a specific queue, they have already accumulated some age from prior hops. Another example is found in a sensor network, in which sensors collect and locally process data before transmitting it to a remote server. In such cases, the AoI at the receiver reflects both the local processing time and the transmission delays. Figure
1
(a) depicts an elementary example of this model with some potential application scenarios. Between the packet’s generation time and its arrival at the system queue, some delay exists, which may be due to various causes, such as intermediate buffers (b.1), network latency or preprocessing (b.2), or sensor remoteness in a multi-hop network (b.3). This delay produces a sequence of initial ages on the incoming packets.

The main result provides an expression for the Average Age of Information (AAoI) when packets have a non-zero initial age (
\Delta^{A}
), relating it directly to the AAoI in the standard case where packets arrive with zero age (
\Delta^{0}
). Specifically, it is proven that
\Delta^{A}=\Delta^{0}+\lambda^{e}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{n}A_{n-1}\right],
where
\lambda^{e}
is the effective arrival rate of packets, and
A_{n-1}
and
Y_{n}
refer to the sequences of packet arrival ages and interdeparture times, respectively. In the case the age process and inter-departure times are statistically independent (which, as shown in Section
4
, may occur in various scenarios), the equation reduces to
\Delta^{A}=\Delta^{0}+\mathbb{E}\left[A_{n-1}\right].
For more complex systems where the dependency structure is unknown or cannot be studied analytically, specific bounds can be established to determine the range within which the final value of
\Delta^{A}
lies. The applicability of the main result is illustrated in various settings, including a forwarding queue, tandem queues and retrial queues. Additionally, the validity of interval estimation for
\Delta^{A}
is tested in some of these scenarios, and its practical usefulness is highlighted in an application to a multi-queued tandem.

2
Model Description

2.1
System model

Consider a communication system through which a destination node receives updates about a process of interest. At epochs
g_{n}
, information updates are generated at the source and transmitted to the destination as packets. However, unlike standard models, we assume that updates do not enter the system immediately upon generation. Instead, they arrive at the queueing system after incurring a certain delay.

Packets enter the queueing system at epochs
t_{n}
according to an arrival process of rate
\lambda
with i.i.d. inter-arrival times
X_{n}=t_{n}-t_{n-1}
. Each arriving packet has a corresponding initial age
A_{n}\geq 0
, defined as the time elapsed between the packet’s generation and its arrival at the system. The initial ages
\left\{A_{n}\right\}
are modelled as a stationary and ergodic sequence of identically distributed, but not necessarily independent, variables. Importantly, we allow
A_{n}
to statistically depend on the inter-arrival times
X_{n}
and the inter-departure times
Y_{n}
. This is a key aspect that introduces additional complexity in the model. Still, it also reflects a wide variety of realistic scenarios, enhancing the usefulness and relevance of this framework. The stationarity assumption on
A_{n}
ensures that the process remains bounded in expectation and avoids pathological cases of ever-increasing update ages.

Upon arrival, a packet is either discarded, queued for future processing, or served immediately, depending on the queue size, the packet management policy, and the system’s state. Packets that are not discarded enter the system and complete the service. These are referred to as
effective arrivals
, and the effective inter-arrival times are denoted by
X_{n}^{e}
. Once a packet enters the server, it stays in service for a
\mu
-rate exponential time
S_{n}\sim\textsl{exp}\left(\mu\right)
, independent of the inter-arrival time. The service completion times are denoted by
t^{\prime}_{n}
, and define the sequence of departures. The
system time
of packet
n
is
T_{n}=t^{\prime}_{n}-t_{n}
, and the
inter-departure time
is
Y_{n}=t^{\prime}_{n}-t^{\prime}_{n-1}
.

The timeliness of information at the destination node can be quantified using the metric AoI, which is discussed in the next section.

2.2
Age of Information with aged updates

Define the counting process of delivered packets

N(s)=\max\left\{n:t^{\prime}_{n}\leq s\right\}.

(1)

At any given time
s
, the timestamp of the last packet received at the destination is
g_{N(s)}
. The AoI is thus defined as the random process

\Delta(s)=s-g_{N(s)},\ s>0.

(2)

Figure 2:
Example of age profile in a model with aged updates. The packet arriving at epoch
t_{3}
carries a high age. When the packet is delivered at
t^{\prime}_{3}
, it produces an upper jump on the age at the monitor. Disjoint areas
H_{n}
are used to calculate the Average Age of Information.

A graphical illustration of the AoI evolution in this model is shown in Figure
2
. The process evolves piecewise linearly, with occasional jumps at delivery times
t^{\prime}_{n}
. While it still maintains the typical sawtooth shape characteristic of such functions, some updates may enter the system with a high initial age. At the time of delivery of these updates, the age at the destination may increase instead of resetting to a lower value. This behaviour is a distinctive feature of models with aged updates and is not seen in standard AoI models with zero-aged updates
[
11
]
.
Specifically, at times
t^{\prime}_{n}
, the AoI resets from the peak value
A_{n-1}+X_{n}+T_{n}
to
A_{n}+T_{n}
. Thus, if
A_{n}
exceeds the threshold
X_{n}+A_{n-1}
, the delivery of this packet leads to an upper jump in the AoI function, as illustrated by packet 3 in Figure
2
. We term these ”far updates” due to their similarity to distant status updates in asynchronous monitoring.

3
Main results

The following theorem states the main result of this article.

Theorem 1
.

For the single-server queue with aged updates described in Section
2.1
, the AAoI is given by

\Delta^{A}=\Delta^{0}+\lambda^{e}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{n}A_{n-1}\right],

where
\Delta^{0}
is the AAoI in the corresponding system with zero-aged updates,
\lambda^{e}
is the effective arrival rate,
Y_{n}
is the
n
-th inter-departure time, and
A_{n-1}
is the initial age of the
(n-1)
-th packet.

Proof
.

The graphical method is adopted, a widely used technique for computing the AAoI via geometric area decomposition
[
6
,
12
,
11
]
. Let us examine Figure
2
, which illustrates the evolution of the AoI in a system with aged updates, including a sample far update, and suppose that the process is observed during an interval
[0,T]
. Without loss of generality, it can be assumed that deliveries occur at both the initial time
t=0
and the final time
t=T
, that is,
t^{\prime}_{0}=0
and
t^{\prime}_{n}=T
for some
n\leq N(T)
, where
N(T)
is the number of delivered updates at the end of the observed period, as defined in (
1
). This is a harmless assumption since the only consequence of leaving it out is the addition of two finite areas that, nonetheless, will vanish in the limit. Also, for simplicity, assume that after the delivery at
t=0
, the system becomes empty.

Under the ergodicity assumption, the average of the process
\Delta^{A}(t)
along the observed interval can be calculated as the time average

\Delta_{T}^{A}=\dfrac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Delta^{A}(t)\textrm{d}t

(3)

However, the integral in Equation (
3
) can be replaced by the sum of the disjoint polygonal areas
H_{n}
, for
n=0,1,\ldots,N(T)-1
. Each of these areas consists of the sum of a triangular area and a rectangular area,

H_{n}=\dfrac{Y_{n+1}^{2}}{2}+Y_{n+1}(A_{n}+T_{n}).

(4)

Hence, Equation (
3
) can be written as

\Delta_{T}^{A}=\dfrac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N(T)-1}H_{n}=\dfrac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N(T)-1}\left(\dfrac{Y_{n+1}^{2}}{2}+Y_{n+1}(A_{n}+T_{n})\right).

(5)

The AAoI is then computed taking (
5
) to the limit as
T\to\infty
,

\Delta^{A}=\lim_{T\to\infty}\Delta_{T}^{A}.

(6)

By the Global Balance Principle, the flow out of a set of states is equal to the flow into the same set. By applying this principle to the entire set of states, it follows that the effective arrival rate and the departure rate are equal. Additionally, the ratio between the number of delivered packets and the interval length approaches the departure rate. Thus,

\lambda^{e}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle def}}{{=}}1/\mathbb{E}\left[X^{e}\right]=1/\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]=\lim_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{N(T)}{T}.

The limit in Equation (
6
) can then be expanded as

\displaystyle\Delta^{A}

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N(T)-1}\left(\dfrac{Y_{n+1}^{2}}{2}+Y_{n+1}(A_{n}+T_{n})\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{N(T)}{T}\ \lim_{T\to\infty}\dfrac{1}{N(T)}\sum\limits_{n=0}^{N(T)-1}\left(\dfrac{Y_{n+1}^{2}}{2}+Y_{n+1}(A_{n}+T_{n})\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{e}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{n+1}^{2}\right]/2+\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{n+1}T_{n}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{n+1}A_{n}\right]\right).

The first two terms in the parentheses are recognised as those defining the standard AAoI with zero-age updates
[
11
,
6
]
, and the proof is complete
\qquad{}_{\square}
.

A well-known identity in probability says that the expected value of the product of two random variables can be expressed as the sum of their covariance and the product of their means. Moreover, the coefficient of variation of a random variable is defined as the ratio between the standard deviation and the mean, while the correlation coefficient between two random variables is the ratio between their covariance and the product of the standard deviations. Because of this, the age correction term in Theorem
1
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\lambda^{e}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{n}A_{n-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}{}\left[Y_{n},A_{n-1}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[A_{n}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{n}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{n}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[A_{n}\right]+\dfrac{r_{Y,A}\cdot\sigma_{Y}\cdot\sigma_{A}}{\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{n}\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[A_{n}\right]+r_{Y,A}\cdot\kappa_{Y}\cdot\sigma_{A},

(7)

where
r_{Y,A}
is the correlation coefficient between
Y_{n}
and
A_{n-1}
, and
\sigma_{*}
and
\kappa_{*}
stand, respectively, for the standard deviation and the coefficient of variation of the corresponding random variable.

Remark
.

The above expression states that, given the marginal distributions of
Y_{n}
and
A_{n-1}
, the dependence between them affects the correction term only through the correlation coefficient. From another perspective, (
7
) can also be interpreted as implicitly limiting the correlation between
Y_{n}
and
A_{n-1}
. Indeed, since the correction term is positive, the correlation coefficient must satisfy

\mathbb{E}\left[A_{n}\right]+r_{Y,A}\cdot\kappa_{Y}\cdot\sigma_{A}>0\ \Longrightarrow\ r_{Y,A}>-\kappa_{A}^{-1}\kappa_{Y}^{-1}.

This inequality implies specific tendencies for the correlation: it tends to be positive if either variable shows high variability or negligible mean (i.e.,
\kappa_{Y}\to\infty
or
\kappa_{A}\to\infty
). Conversely, a strong negative correlation is only possible when, for given means, at least one of them is highly stable (
\sigma_{A}\to 0
or
\sigma_{Y}\to 0
), or, for given standard deviations, at least one of them exhibits very large average values (
\mu_{A}\to\infty
or
\mu_{Y}\to\infty
).

The following corollary is also a direct consequence of equation (
7
).

Corollary 1
.

In any queueing system with inter-departure times
Y_{n}
and initial ages
A_{n}
, the correction term in Theorem
1
is bounded by

\mathbb{E}\left[A_{n}\right]-d/2\leq\lambda^{e}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{n}A_{n-1}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[A_{n}\right]+d/2,

where
d=2\kappa_{Y}\sigma_{A}
is the interval width.

The lower and upper bounds provided above depend exclusively on the marginals of
Y_{n}
and
A_{n}
, which makes them particularly helpful for anticipating the correction term’s value in scenarios where the dependence structure between
Y_{n}
and
A_{n-1}
is unknown or not analytically tractable.

4
Applications

In this section, Theorem
1
is applied to several queueing scenarios to motivate the versatility and relevance of the aged update framework. Initially, the simple case is examined where each update experiences a fixed delay before entering the system. Next, a system where retransmissions occur after transmission errors is considered. Here, the delay from repeated attempts implicitly induces a process of initial ages. The computation of the AAoI at the output of tandem queueing systems is also analysed, where the time spent in previous nodes naturally contributes to the age at the final queue. Both a classical case involving identical queues and a more general setting with heterogeneous queues are addressed, highlighting the flexibility of the proposed approach across different network topologies. Finally, an example of a retrial queue is considered, in which the time spent in orbit is interpreted as the initial age for updates in an analogous model. When deemed interesting, the tightness of the bounds provided in Corollary
1
will be assessed analytically and numerically.

4.1
Independency

The model analysis greatly simplifies when the sequence
\{A_{n}\}
of initial ages is independent of the sequence of inter-departure times
\{Y_{n}\}
. In this case, the cross term of Theorem
1
factorizes as
\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{n}A_{n-1}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{n}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[A_{n-1}\right]
, and the AAoI can be then expressed as

\Delta^{A}=\Delta^{0}+\mathbb{E}\left[A_{n}\right].

An elementary and illustrative example occurs when each arriving packet undergoes a fixed delay
A>0
before entering the system. In this setting, all updates have the same initial age
A_{n}=A
, leading to

\Delta^{A}=\Delta^{0}+A.

This case is relevant in real-world applications where sources and monitors are not perfectly synchronised. For instance,
[
13
]
reports observations of fixed delays in practical communication systems due to clock shifts. Our framework naturally incorporates such delays, allowing for a direct and simple quantification of their impact on information freshness.

The potential of the independent case also emerges when the correction term is examined in the single-server FCFS queue under traffic’s limiting conditions. In particular, its behaviour in the
heavy traffic
(HT) regime, in which the server is nearly saturated, and the
light traffic
(LT) regime, in which the server utilisation rate is close to zero, allows for the anticipation of the effect of aged updates on freshness in stressed or idle environments. An approximation of the value of the correction term under these two regimes is discussed next.

4.1.1
Heavy traffic

In the HT regime, the single-server FCFS queue operates nearly continuously, with negligible idle periods between jobs. Moreover, the Lindley-type equation

Y_{n}=S_{n}+(X^{e}_{n}-T_{n-1})^{+},

(8)

where
x^{+}=\max\{0,x\}
, defines the inter-departure times as the sum of the service and the idle times. As a result, under HT conditions, the inter-departure times asymptotically converge in distribution to the service time distribution, and therefore become independent of the arrival process and initial ages. Consequently, in Equation (
7
),
r_{Y,A}\to 0
, and the correction term collapses to
\mathbb{E}\left[A_{n}\right]
. Furthermore, since
\kappa_{Y}\to 1
, the range of the interval defined in Corollary
1
approaches
d\to 2\sigma_{A}
, resulting in a tighter interval for stable age processes.

4.1.2
Light traffic

The LT regime occurs when the effective arrival rate approaches zero. Together with the Global Balance Principle, this feature is captured by the condition

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{n}^{e}\right]\to\infty.

For a given standard deviation, this implies that the coefficient of variation
\kappa_{Y}
goes to zero. Consequently, the second term in the right-hand side of equation (
7
) and the interval width in Corollary
1
become negligible, which leads to the independent case with very tight bounds.

4.2
Forwarding

The computation of AAoI in error-prone models, where there is a forwarding protocol after a failed transmission, can be performed using the scheme of aged updates. Intuitively, a failed transmission has a dual effect on the age process. On one hand, a failure does not improve the age process because the information has not been received correctly. On the other hand, a forwarded packet has already spent some time in the system, which makes it indistinguishable, from the perspective of information timeliness, from a packet that arrives with an initial age.

To illustrate this, a zero-wait model is considered, in which the source is aware of the system’s state and can generate a fresh packet as soon as a delivery occurs. This model is idealised, but also insightful in capturing the best-case AoI performance
[
11
, Section III.B]
. In particular, because of the source responsiveness, the time variables in the zero-wait model are identically distributed,

X_{n+1}=T_{n}=Y_{n}=S_{n}\sim\textsl{exp}\left(\mu\right).

(9)

Suppose further that the communication link is not completely reliable, and a packet arrives corrupted at the destination with probability
\alpha
. The monitor provides acknowledgement (ACK) or negative acknowledgement (NACK) feedback after each update, which is received by the source instantaneously. Upon successful transmission, the source sends a fresh packet; otherwise, it retransmits the same packet –in particular, with the same timestamp– until an ACK is received. Figure
3
a) shows how the age evolves in this model. Successful deliveries are marked with a blue triangle and produce a downward jump in the age function. Instead, deliveries marked with a red triangle do not affect the age process, as these deliveries are not completed. Figure
3
b) presents an equivalent error-free aged-update model, where failed transmissions in the error-prone model can be viewed as triggering an independent initial age process
A_{n}
.

4.3
Tandem Queues

Aged updates also prove useful for computing the AAoI in general queueing networks. In such configurations, the age of a packet upon arrival reflects the time spent at earlier nodes. A particularly illustrative case is that of a tandem queueing system, where the final AAoI can be obtained by separately evaluating the AAoI at the last queue and then adding a correction term, as indicated by Theorem
1
, for the correlation between the final inter-departure times and the initial ages.

The following examples illustrate the usefulness of this idea. In the first example, a tandem of two infinite-capacity M/M/1 is studied. While the final AAoI in this setup is already known, it validates the application of the aged-update framework in the presence of statistical dependence between the inter-departure times and the initial ages. In the next example, the previous tandem is extended to an arbitrary number of queues, to highlight the practical advantages of Corollary
1
. The last example addresses a tandem formed by two heterogeneous queues, a system barely explored in the literature compared to those composed of identical queues.

Throughout these examples, superscripts
(1)
and

4.4
Retrial Queues

A Retrial Queue is a type of queueing model where an arrival from the source —referred to as a
primary arrival
— temporarily leaves the system when it finds the server busy and returns later. While waiting to re-enter, the packet is said to be in an
orbit
. When the server becomes available, both the source and the orbit compete for access, each submitting packets based on their own renewal processes. This process is depicted in Figure
6
. There are two key differences between a standard queue and the orbit in retrial queues. First, packets in the orbit cannot monitor the server’s state; they can only check their status by attempting to re-enter the system, an event known as a
retrial
. Furthermore, after a service is completed, there is a period during which the server is idle until a new update arrives, whether it be a primary arrival or a packet from the orbit. Secondly, to better reflect the real-life systems it is intended to represent (customers repeatedly calling a store until they receive service), packets in the orbit generally do not follow any specific order; thus, the service discipline is random.

Figure 6:
Basic model for a retrial queue (from
[
18
, Section 3.5]
).

Retrial queueing models are generally difficult to analyse, and the model described here is one of the few that can be solved analytically. Therefore, it is not surprising that, to the best of our knowledge, the AoI has not yet been studied in this class of models. However, using the aged-updates scheme, the calculation of AAoI in these models can be significantly simplified, enabling the derivation of either an exact expression or reasonable bounds. For clarity, the specific model considered in this example will first be described, followed by an explanation of how to apply the scheme of aged updates to determine the AAoI for the model.

4.4.1
Retrial queueing model example

Consider a M/M/1 retrial queue with single re-attempts

2

. Primary arrivals and retrials follow Poisson processes with rates

Similarly, if an update attempts to re-enter the system from the orbit and finds the server busy, it returns to the orbit. This system can be modelled using a Continuous Time Markov Chain with the state space

5
Conclusions

In this work, we relax the idealised assumption that all updates enter the system with zero age, and develop a more general framework to account for scenarios where updates may already be aged upon arrival. This approach models a situation that arises naturally in multi-hop networks, sensor systems with preprocessing delays, and retransmission protocols.

Our main result is a closed-form expression for the AAoI in single-server queues with aged updates. We demonstrate that the AoI for this system is equal to that of a system with zero-age packets, augmented by a correction term that captures the interaction between the packet’s initial age and the inter-departure times. This formulation enables us to recover simpler results under independence and to deduce analytical bounds when complex dependencies are present. We illustrate the applicability of the proposed framework by deriving well-known and new results across a variety of queueing systems, including retransmissions, tandem queues of different complexity, and retrial systems. Additionally, we conduct simulation tests to assess the validity of the theoretical results in a network characterised by a complex dependence structure, highlighting both the generality and practical relevance of the approach.

Several lines for future research emerge from this work. One natural direction is to extend the focus from single-server systems to more complex and realistic scenarios, such as multi-source systems, where aged updates may compete for server resources. Another extension would be to explore the impact of relaxing the assumption of i.i.d. arrivals, where the correlation between queueing times and initial ages becomes more confusing. It could also be interesting to investigate optimal scheduling policies that explicitly account for the initial age of updates. The analysis could also be extended to consider negative initial ages, which may arise in systems where updates remain valid for a certain period before they begin to age. This approach would require revisiting the graphical method used in the proof of the main result, as the current one assumes non-negative initial ages. Another promising research direction is the application of the aged-updates framework to alternative age metrics, including the Age of Incorrect Information or the Query AoI. Finally, it could be valuable to justify the theoretical findings empirically by measuring the age on real data, assessing the practical accuracy of the expressions and bounds derived in this work.
