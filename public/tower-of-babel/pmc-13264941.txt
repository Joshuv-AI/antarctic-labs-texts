Comparative screening accuracy of the Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised with Follow-Up (M-Chat-R/F) for early autism spectrum disorder: a systematic review
Authors: Aaron Siong Fatt Tsen, Mohammad Azri Bantalani, Muhammad Nazrin Ab Rahim, Siti Zuraina Ain Amat, Khalid Mokti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEarly screening for autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is critical for timely intervention, making the Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised with Follow-Up (M-CHAT-R/F) a widely adopted tool globally. However, a recent systematic synthesis of its comparative accuracy is lacking. This review aimed to evaluate the screening accuracy of the M-CHAT-R/F against other ASD screening instruments.DesignA systematic review was conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. A narrative synthesis was employed to integrate findings across diverse study designs and methodological quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist.Eligibility criteriaPeer-reviewed, English-language observational studies published between January 2020 and October 2025 that compared the accuracy metrics of the M-CHAT-R/F with alternative screening tools.Information sourcesScopus, PubMed, ScienceDirect and ProQuest.ResultsFrom 172 identified articles, six high-quality studies encompassing 12 633 participants aged 12–48 months were analysed. The synthesis revealed a complex performance profile for the M-CHAT-R/F. While its sensitivity for detecting potential cases was highly inconsistent, its negative predictive value remained consistently high, reliably identifying children who screened negative. Conversely, the tool demonstrated variable specificity and a persistently low positive predictive value, indicating a substantial false-positive burden and reduced confidence in positive results. Furthermore, the M-CHAT-R/F’s accuracy was highly age-dependent, with performance degrading outside the optimal 16–30-month window compared with other specialised tools.ConclusionsThe M-CHAT-R/F may not be an optimal instrument in every clinical setting. Its clinical utility may be limited by variable sensitivity and a lower positive predictive value, potentially creating a higher false-positive burden. Clinicians and health systems should consider adopting age-specific, tiered screening protocols to improve diagnostic efficiency and better allocate intervention resources.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterised by ongoing difficulties in social interactions and communication, as well as limited and repetitive behavioural patterns, interests, or activities. The impact of ASD globally is significant; as of 2021, around 61.8 million people were diagnosed with autism, resulting in a worldwide age-adjusted prevalence rate of 788.3 for every 100 000 individuals. Notably, ASD is listed among the top ten contributors to non-fatal health issues for those under 20 years old.1 Data from the CDC’s Autism and Developmental Disabilities Monitoring Network demonstrates an increase in the estimated prevalence of ASD, rising from 27.6 per 1000 children (1 in 36) in the 2020 surveillance year to 32.2 per 1000 children (1 in 31) in the 2022 surveillance year.2

This growing prevalence is also reflected regionally. In Malaysia, the count of autistic children registered with the Department of Social Welfare has skyrocketed by 663% over the past ten years, increasing from 6991 in 2013 to 53 323 in 2023.3 This exponential increase is largely attributed to heightened public awareness, broader diagnostic criteria, and improvements in diagnostic screening practices rather than a true biological increase in incidence alone.4 In parallel, the prevalence within Malaysian schools has risen, with the highest rates reported in the Federal Territories of Labuan and Kuala Lumpur, where 2022 data showed prevalence rates of 17.36 per 1000 children (1 in 58) and 15.42 per 1000 children (1 in 65), respectively.5

However, substantial obstacles exist in the realm of early diagnosis. This delay is concerning, as late diagnosis is often linked to limited public knowledge and varying levels of autism awareness among primary care and family physicians, who are often the first point of contact.6 The urgency of early detection is underscored by the broader context of youth mental health; recent data highlights the escalating burden of neurodevelopmental and mental health disorders among children globally, and particularly in Asia, making effective primary care screening an imperative public health priority.7

Recognising that comprehensive early intervention, especially before 24 months of age—such as Naturalistic Developmental Behavioural Interventions, targeted speech therapy and structured play-based therapies—can significantly enhance cognitive and adaptive skills. Hence, public health initiatives must prioritise timely and effective screening.8 To address this need, the Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised with Follow-Up (M-CHAT-R/F) has become a prominent two-stage, parent-dependent screening tool. It consists of 20 yes/no questions that take approximately 5 to 10 min for a caregiver to complete, designed for toddlers aged 16 to 30 months.9 The revision aimed at lowering false-positive results while ensuring high sensitivity. Recent systematic reviews validate its strong psychometric attributes, showing a sensitivity rate of 78% (95% CI 0.57 to 0.91) and a specificity rate of 0.98 (95% CI 0.88 to 1.00), making it highly recommended for early ASD detection.10

The M-CHAT-R/F is a key screening tool, distinct from the comprehensive diagnostic instruments required for a formal evaluation. This diagnostic landscape includes gold-standard tools like the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (sensitivity 87%, specificity 75%), the Childhood Autism Rating Scale (sensitivity 89%, specificity 79%) and the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised diagnostic interview.10 While all are part of the assessment pathway, the M-CHAT-R/F stands out for its suitability for widespread primary care use because it is a concise, parent-completed tool.

Despite the M-CHAT-R/F’s broad acceptance, including its adaptation for diverse cultural contexts like Malaysia,11 there are still significant gaps in the compiled evidence. Current research shows a considerable variation in outcomes, attributable to differences in study designs, characteristics of the samples and methods of confirming diagnoses. This inconsistency poses challenges for healthcare professionals and policymakers in establishing screening programmes that are based on solid evidence.

Several important gaps call for a new comprehensive review. First, although the American Academy of Pediatrics advises for autism-specific screenings during 18-month and 24-month checkups, there is a lack of synthesised information regarding the best timing to implement the M-CHAT-R/F in regular practices. Second, its screening performance in various cultural and demographic settings remains uncertain.11 Additionally, while recent systematic reviews examining general autism screening exist,10 a detailed search revealed a lack of recent synthesis (2020–2025) strictly evaluating the direct, head-to-head comparative screening accuracy of the M-CHAT-R/F against other emerging screening instruments.

The main goal of this systematic review is to analyse how the M-CHAT-R/F screening performance stacks up against other well-known screening methods for the early identification of ASD. To fulfil this objective, the review will evaluate the screening accuracy (including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV)) of the M-CHAT-R/F alongside other ASD screening instruments across various groups and environments.

This systematic review is highly significant, providing a solid evidence foundation for policymakers and healthcare providers to enhance national screening recommendations, boost the implementation of the M-CHAT-R/F, and support clinical decision, especially in countries such as Malaysia.11 From a public health viewpoint, synthesising this evidence aids in achieving the broader goal of reducing the age for diagnosis, thereby expanding the window for essential early interventions.

Methodology

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses 2020 guidelines.12 It was carried out between October and November 2025.

As this study is a systematic review of previously published data, ethical approval and informed consent were not required. The review protocol was not registered in advance on PROSPERO, which is acknowledged as a methodological limitation.

The search was limited to studies published from January 2020 to October 2025 to capture the most contemporary screening landscape, specifically focusing on how the M-CHAT-R/F compares with newly developed and digitally enhanced screening tools that have emerged recently.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted across four databases: Scopus, PubMed, ScienceDirect and ProQuest. The search was finalised on 9 October 2025.

To ensure reproducibility, the systematic literature search used specific Boolean search strings tailored to each database. For Scopus, PubMed and ProQuest, the following full search string was applied: (“toddler” OR “child” OR “children” OR “preschool” OR “childhood”) AND (“modified checklist for autism in toddlers” OR “M-CHAT” OR “MCHAT” OR “M-CHAT-R” OR “M-CHAT-R/F”) AND (“Screening tool” OR “questionnaire” OR “clinical judgement” OR “without screening”) AND (“sensitivity” OR “specificity” OR “predictive value” OR “validity” OR “diagnostic accuracy”).

For ScienceDirect, the search string was adapted as follows: (“toddler” OR “child” OR “children” OR “preschool” OR “childhood”) AND (“Screening tool” OR “questionnaire” OR “clinical judgement” OR “without screening”) AND (“modified checklist for autism in toddlers” OR “M-CHAT” OR “MCHAT” OR “M-CHAT-R” OR “M-CHAT-R/F”) AND (“sensitivity” OR “specificity” OR “predictive value” OR “validity”).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The selection of studies was made using predetermined eligibility criteria. Filters were applied to limit results to English-language peer-reviewed articles published between 1 January 2020 and 9 October 2025. Eligible studies must also have used an observational study approach (cohort, cross-sectional or case–control) and directly compared the screening accuracy of the M-CHAT-R/F to that of other screening tools. Exclusion criteria were letters, case studies, case reports, editorials, or reviews. Additionally, research that emphasised the M-CHAT-R/F method alone without any comparison to other methods or focused on screening for ASD was not included.

Study selection and data extraction

The study selection and data extraction process was conducted by four independent reviewers working in pairs. Each pair independently screened assigned titles, abstracts, and full texts, and extracted data. Any disagreements within pairs were resolved through discussion and full group consensus. The purpose of this form is to collect information about (1) key study characteristics, including author, publication year, country, region, study design, sample size and participant age and (2) all relevant screening accuracy metrics, such as sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV for both the M-CHAT-R/F and its comparator tools. Only publicly accessible data were used in the review; the authors were not reached for any missing or erroneous details.

Screening metrics were defined as follows: Sensitivity (ability to correctly identify true cases), Specificity (ability to correctly identify non-cases), PPV (probability that a positive screen is a true case) and NPV (probability that a negative screen is not a case).13 Based on established guidelines from the American Academy of Paediatrics, developmental screening instruments should demonstrate minimum sensitivity and specificity levels of 70% to 80% to be considered acceptable.14 Because predictive values are strictly dependent on population prevalence, rigid percentage cut-offs are not designated for these metrics. Instead, clinical acceptability is evaluated against established validation benchmarks; the foundational validation of the M-CHAT-R/F established a PPV of 47.5% for ASD,15 while current meta-analyses establish a pooled PPV benchmark of 51% and a pooled NPV benchmark of 99% for screening in general populations.9

Quality assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias for each included study were assessed independently by all four reviewers using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for Diagnostic Test Accuracy Studies. This 10-item tool addresses key domains, including the appropriateness of the patient sample, the unbiased interpretation of the index test, the validity of the reference standard, and the thoroughness of the follow-up.16

Data synthesis

A narrative synthesis of the extracted data was conducted, as statistical meta-analysis was not deemed appropriate due to the substantial variation in the comparator tools, study populations, and participant age groups. The findings were organised and presented in a summary table to facilitate comparison. Key screening metrics (sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV) and their 95% CIs were extracted as reported. The synthesis focused on the comparative screening accuracy of the M-CHAT-R/F, with findings grouped by the specific tools being compared and participant age groups.

Patient and public involvement

No patient involved.

Result

Search result

A thorough exploration of various databases revealed 172 entries. After eliminating 23 duplicates, 149 titles were reviewed, which led to the dismissal of 99 entries. The abstracts of the 50 remaining articles were evaluated for their eligibility, resulting in 36 articles being discarded. This left 14 full-text articles for eligibility assessment. Out of these, eight articles were ruled out due to a lack of direct comparison between the M-CHAT-R/F and an alternative screening tool. Eventually, six studies fulfilled all the criteria for inclusion and were considered in the final analysis (figure 1).

All six included studies demonstrated high methodological quality with a low risk of bias across key JBI domains, ensuring that the synthesised screening accuracy metrics were derived from robust empirical data. Consequently, the observed variations in screening performance were attributed primarily to contextual factors, such as cohort age and specific study designs, rather than underlying methodological flaws.

Characteristics of included studies

The six included studies, all published between 2021 and 2025, represented a total participant pool of 12 633 children. These studies showed significant diversity in both their methods and geographical locations. The methodologies consisted of two prospective cohort studies, three cross-sectional studies and one prospective case-control study. Research came from various regions worldwide, such as the Americas (USA, Ecuador), the Western Pacific (Singapore, China) and Southeast Asia (India). The ages of the children at the time of screening ranged from 12 months to 48 months. Each of the six studies was independently evaluated and found to have high methodological rigour in accordance with the JBI critical appraisal criteria (table 1).

In the context of broad population screening, where the priority was to identify all children who may be at risk, instruments were evaluated on their sensitivity and NPV. In this domain, the M-CHAT-R/F’s performance was highly variable. On one hand, it demonstrated considerable strength in several studies. Mata-Iturralde et al found it achieved perfect sensitivity (1.00) and NPV (1.00),17 which was reinforced by Mukherjee et al, who recorded a high sensitivity of 0.95 and NPV of 0.99.18 Wong et al also found the M-CHAT-R/F was the most sensitive tool at all ages tested.19 However, this high sensitivity was not universal. The Sturner et al study found the M-CHAT-R/F performed poorly, with a low sensitivity of 0.36. In that same study, it was outperformed by the Quantitative Checklist for Autism-10 Item (Ordinal) (Q-CHAT-10-O), which achieved a sensitivity of 0.63.20 Furthermore, even when the M-CHAT-R/F performed well, it was outperformed in the Mukherjee et al study by both the Rastriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram–Autism Specific Questionnaire and the Trivandrum Autism Behavioural Checklist, which both achieved perfect 1.00 metrics.18

In the subsequent context of referring children for formal diagnosis, where the priority shifted to minimising false positives using high specificity and PPV, evaluations consistently revealed the M-CHAT-R/F’s primary weakness. A recurrent finding across multiple studies was a low PPV, which indicated a high false-positive rate. This was reported by Zhang et al (PPV 0.31) in a large cohort, Sturner et al (PPV 0.36), and Mata-Iturralde et al (PPV 0.42).17 20 21 In these direct comparisons, other specialised tools proved more effective. Choueiri et al found the Rapid Interactive Screening Test for Autism in Toddlers achieved perfect specificity (1.00) and PPV (1.00),22 while Mata-Iturralde et al found the Autism Detection in Early Childhood (Guayaquil Spanish) (ADEC-GU) achieved near-perfect metrics (PPV 0.95, specificity 1.00).17 In the Zhang et al study, the Binomial Observation Test (BOT) showed a slightly better but still low PPV of 0.38,21 and in the Sturner et al study, the Q-CHAT-10 provided a superior PPV (0.54).20

The longitudinal study by Wong et al demonstrated that comparative accuracy was not static. At 12 months, the M-CHAT-R/F (sensitivity 0.83) was the only viable high-sensitivity screener, as both the ASDetect (sensitivity 0.08) and the Social Attention and Communication Surveillance, Revised (SACS-R) (sensitivity 0.33) indicated clinically poor sensitivity. By 30 months, however, this dynamic had reversed. The SACS-R emerged as the superior tool, matching the M-CHAT-R/F’s sensitivity (0.72) while exceeding its performance on all other metrics, including specificity (0.90 vs 0.77) and PPV (0.68 vs 0.48).19

Summary of findings

This systematic review concluded that the M-CHAT-R/F did not demonstrate consistent, universal utility as a primary ASD screener. Its performance varied dramatically across cohorts, with a persistent limitation being a low positive predictive value that indicated a high burden of false positives. The evidence clearly illustrated that a singular screening approach centred on the M-CHAT-R/F was suboptimal. Several alternative instruments were identified that offered a more clinically useful balance of psychometric properties, often outperforming the M-CHAT-R/F for both broad, high-sensitivity detection and more precise, high-specificity identification. Furthermore, the evidence underscored that screening accuracy was highly dynamic; the optimal tool was critically dependent on context, particularly the child’s developmental stage, mandating an age-specific and context-aware approach to instrument selection.

Discussion

The core findings demonstrate that the M-CHAT-R/F functions as a highly sensitive, yet low specificity, screening tool for ASD, a profile that necessitates careful consideration in clinical implementation. While all included studies were rated as high quality based on the JBI checklist, it is important to acknowledge the substantial variation in sample sizes, ranging from 81 to over 11 000 participants. As such, greater weight was placed on findings from large-scale prospective cohorts which provided highly robust, population-level evidence regarding the M-CHAT-R/F’s lower positive predictive value compared with smaller cross-sectional snapshots, reinforcing a cautious interpretation of the tool’s performance.

The American Academy of Paediatrics recommends that effective developmental screening instruments exhibit sensitivity and specificity of at least 70%–80%.23 The evidence synthesised in this review reveals that the M-CHAT-R/F’s performance did not consistently meet these standards. Although the tool’s validation across multiple languages underscores its cultural adaptability and broad global applicability, the primary weakness identified was its persistently lower specificity and PPV compared with alternative instruments.11 15 24 25 The review’s results indicate that this profile, marked by inconsistent sensitivity and low PPV, suggests that while the M-CHAT-R/F can be effective for broad early detection, it comes at the cost of an unacceptable burden of false positives, thereby underscoring the need for mandatory multi-stage validation protocols. Similar findings from a meta-analysis of the M-CHAT-R/F revealed a pooled sensitivity of 82.5% and a positive predictive value of 51.2% in low-risk populations, indicating that roughly half of the children with a positive screen will ultimately receive an ASD diagnosis.26

However, the M-CHAT-R/F’s reliance on subjective parental or caregiver reports introduces inherent assessor-dependent limitations. Parents may inadvertently underreport symptoms due to a lack of awareness regarding developmental milestones, or they may exhibit leniency in symptom acknowledgement due to cultural stigmas surrounding developmental delays, thereby increasing the risk of false negatives.9

Conversely, false positives can also be driven by parental reporting styles. Parents experiencing high anxiety or over-awareness of certain isolated behaviours may tend to over-report a child’s symptoms. Furthermore, first-time parents or those whose children exhibit highly inconsistent skills often report difficulty in accurately completing the M-CHAT-R/F questionnaire, contributing to decreased specificity.26 27

While earlier seminal reviews have firmly established the M-CHAT-R/F as a massive improvement over baseline clinical judgement,15 the synthesis of recent literature suggests an evolving landscape. As newer, often observation-based or digitally integrated tools emerge, the comparative advantage of the M-CHAT-R/F’s predictive value is increasingly challenged.

Recent advancements suggest that parental assessment can be significantly improved through digital technology.28 A digital version of the M-CHAT-R/F equipped with automated risk assessment has been shown to improve the overall quality of screening. For instance, the SenseToKnow mobile autism screening application displays strategically designed movies and a bubble-popping game on a caregiver’s screen while recording the child’s behavioural responses through the device’s front-facing camera and sensors. These responses are then subsequently analysed automatically using computer vision and machine learning.29

A key factor influencing the tool’s effectiveness is that the M-CHAT-R/F’s screening accuracy is reduced as the child’s age increases, particularly beyond the validated window of 16 to 30 months. Sensitivity reduces and specificity increases for the M-CHAT-R/F as the children grow older. Younger children with general developmental delays may fail certain M-CHAT items even though they are not autistic, as they tend to acquire developmental skills gradually over time.30 31 The M-CHAT-R/F’s capacity to detect true positives decreases as the tool becomes less appropriate with developmental milestones relevant for older children.32 As children grow older, some autism-related behaviours may become less obvious or be masked by learnt behaviours, reducing the likelihood that parents will accurately report the symptoms. Children with milder forms of ASD may acquire compensatory abilities as they grow older, resulting in negative outcomes on an autism screening test.32 This phenomenon underscores the importance of early and repeated screening to capture evolving symptom presentations, as well as the potential for diagnostic drift in older children for further intervention.33 The review’s results indicate that this profile, marked by inconsistent sensitivity and low PPV, suggests that while the M-CHAT-R/F can be effective for broad early detection, it may come at the cost of a potentially high burden of false positives.

Furthermore, methodological differences across studies, particularly the use of cross-sectional designs, likely contribute to an overestimation of the M-CHAT-R/F’s sensitivity and specificity values in some published findings. Higher sensitivity in cross-sectional studies is often due to incomplete follow-up on screen negatives and differences in case ascertainment methodology, which prospective studies systematically address. Thus, sensitivity can appear overestimated in cross-sectional studies for M-CHAT R/F. A cross-sectional study conducted among a high-likelihood population increases the chances that positives were truly positive, hence yielding a high sensitivity result.34 The cross-sectional design resulted in incomplete follow-up of screen-negative cases, as only screen-positive children were referred for diagnostic confirmation.17 22 This may have led to an overestimation of sensitivity and the omission of potential false-negative cases.34

Strengths and limitations

The methodology employed in this review exhibits several notable strengths, ensuring the rigour and comprehensive nature of the synthesised evidence. A comprehensive and systematic approach was used to ensure methodological rigour, transparency, and reproducibility of the findings. Furthermore, the review utilised direct comparative analysis, which enabled a clearer understanding of the relative diagnostic performance of the M-CHAT-R/F tool rather than evaluating the tool in isolation. The inclusion of recent, high-quality evidence enhances the reliability of the synthesised findings. Finally, adopting a global perspective and contextual insights allowed for a cross-cultural comparison of M-CHAT-R/F validity and implementation, offering valuable insights for context-specific adaptation of the screening instrument.

Despite these strengths, the review is subject to several methodological limitations. The limited number of comparative studies restricted the statistical generalisability of the results. A significant challenge was the heterogeneity across studies, stemming from variability in study design, population age range, and diagnostic standards, which complicated the ability to make uniform comparisons. Consequently, this high heterogeneity led to a lack of quantitative synthesis whereby meta-analysis was not feasible. Moreover, the review faced potential publication and language bias because only English-language articles were included, which may have led to the omission of relevant studies published in other languages. Additionally, a notable methodological limitation of this study is that the review protocol was not registered in advance on PROSPERO.

Recommendations

Effective public health practice necessitates the adoption of structured, evidence-based guidelines for autism screening. As a recommendation, health systems might explore evidence-based tiered screening pathways. This suggested tiered strategy could be beneficial for optimising detection while effectively managing the potential burden of false positives. This approach involves universal parent-report screening, such as the M-CHAT-R/F or a validated Q-CHAT variant, conducted at 18 to 24 months. This initial tier must be followed by structured clinician-administered observation, utilising tools like the ADEC, for children who screen positive. Only those children positive at both tiers should proceed to comprehensive diagnostic evaluation. This tiered strategy is crucial for optimising detection while effectively managing the burden of false positives and allocating resources efficiently.

In addition, health systems must mandate rigorous cultural adaptation and validation before deploying screening instruments in new linguistic or cultural contexts. Proper adaptation requires substantial investment in comprehensive processes, including forward and back translation, cognitive interviewing, pilot testing and validation studies that use adequate sample sizes and blinded diagnostic verification. Deploying a tool based on superficial translation alone risks introducing measurement error and promoting inequitable outcomes.

Successfully establishing screening programmes also requires an investment in implementation infrastructure. This infrastructure includes proportionate funding for training, seamless workflow integration, clear follow-up protocols and sufficient diagnostic capacity to match screening coverage. Training must extend beyond mere administration to cover result interpretation, sensitive family communication and cultural sensitivity.

Furthermore, systems must establish monitoring and evaluation systems to track key implementation outcomes. Tracking metrics such as screening and follow-up completion rates, the time from screening to diagnosis, false positive rates and age at diagnosis enables iterative refinement of the programme. Population-based follow-up studies are also essential for gathering valuable data on long-term programme effectiveness.

Current recommendations are limited by several critical evidence gaps, highlighting key priorities for future research. One major gap is the lack of comparative effectiveness research. There remains a need for large-scale, head-to-head studies that directly compare different screening tools and pathways within the same populations. Research must examine various tiered screening strategies and implementation approaches, assessing their impact on the age at diagnosis, access to intervention and long-term outcomes to strengthen the evidence base for screening policy.

Additionally, research focusing on long-term outcomes is critical for establishing the ultimate value of screening programmes. Prospective studies are necessary to determine whether early detection achieved through screening actually translates into earlier intervention initiation, improved developmental trajectories and better long-term outcomes. These studies should also investigate potential differential benefits across various demographic and socioeconomic groups to assess equity impacts. Ultimately, economic evaluation is urgently needed to inform resource allocation. Rigorous cost-effectiveness analyses must compare screening strategies by incorporating all associated costs, such as screening, diagnostic evaluation and intervention costs, alongside the benefits derived from earlier detection and improved outcomes. Such analyses must be tailored to consider diverse health system contexts and varying ASD prevalence rates.

Conclusions

Due to the limited number of included studies and their substantial heterogeneity, the resulting conclusions must be interpreted with caution. Although the findings highlight notable limitations in the M-CHAT-R/F’s performance, they are currently insufficient to support definitive policy-level recommendations against its universal application. Clinicians and health systems might consider exploring age-specific, tiered screening pathways to account for the observed variability in the tool’s performance across different developmental stages. However, because robust empirical evidence directly supporting the effectiveness of such strategies remains limited, further targeted research is highly warranted.
