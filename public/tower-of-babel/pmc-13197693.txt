Extranodal marginal zone lymphoma presenting as a paraspinal mass and pleural effusion
Authors: Brent Cami, Heather Corveleyn, Karolien Donckerwolcke, Shula Staessens, Vincent De Pauw, Shane Hanon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
MALT-lymphomas are a subtype of non-Hodgkin lymphomas that typically present in the gastro-intestinal tract (50% of cases), the salivary glands and lung. Pleural involvement, however, is exceedingly rare with only a few cases described in the literature to date. Here we present a case of pleural MALT-lymphoma in a 71-year-old woman presenting with progressive dyspnea since 5-6 months without cough or sputum. The CT scan showed paravertebral bilateral pleural thickening (right > left), with associated right sided pleural effusion. Positron emission tomography (PET) scan revealed FDG-uptake limited to the thickened areas of the pleura. Pleural fluid analysis was consistent with a pleural exudate with marked predominance of lymphocytes on flow cytometry (93% lymphocytes) with negative aerobic, anaerobic and mycobacterial cultures. A thoracoscopic biopsy of the right pleural mass revealed massive invasion of the pleura by a mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma. The final diagnosis was a stage IV MALT-lymphoma for which systemic therapy with rituximab-bendamustine was administered. Follow-up FDG-PET-CT scan after 4 chemotherapy cycles confirmed complete radiological remission. The case is particularly remarkable because there was no known pre-existing pleural inflammation, which is typically a hallmark of MALT lymphoma in extranodal sites. Immunohistochemical characterization of pleural fluid lymphocyte subtypes might have provided a clue to the diagnosis. In pleural exudates with lymphocytic predominance this diagnostic step should not be overlooked.

Case report

Background

Marginal zone lymphomas (MZL) account for 7-8% of non-Hodgkin lymphomas and can be categorized into three subtypes respectively: mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT), nodal and splenic [[1], [2], [3]]. MALT lymphomas often have an indolent course and typically occur in tissues with chronic inflammation due to infection or autoimmune disease [3]. The most common sites are the gastro-intestinal tract (50% of cases), the salivary gland, lung, head and neck, ocular adnexae, skin, thyroid and breast [[3], [4], [5]]. Pleural involvement, however, is exceedingly rare with only a few cases described in the literature to date [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. Here, we describe a MALT lymphoma presenting as a bilateral pleural mass with right sided pleural effusion.

Case

A 71-year-old woman was referred to the pneumology department for progressive dyspnea since 5-6 months without cough or sputum. She has a medical history of tuberculosis at age 4, for which she had received 9 months of antibiotic treatment. There was no history of asbestos exposure and the previous chest CT scan in 2019 was normal. The new CT scan showed bilateral pleural thickening (right > left), with associated right-sided pleural effusion. Laboratory studies showed a normal WBC count and normal biochemical findings including normal CRP, calcium, lactate dehydrogenase, protein count and liver enzymes. Serologic tests including HIV, HBV, HCV, EBV and CMV were all negative. Additionally, IgG, IgM and IgG titers were within the normal range. Analysis of the pleural fluid obtained by thoracentesis was consistent with a pleural exudate with marked predominance of lymphocytes on flow cytometry (93% of white blood cells). Mycobacterial PCR, aerobic and anaerobic cultures as well as mycobacterial culture remained negative. No malignant cells were found upon microscopical examination.

A thoracoscopy with biopsy of the right pleural mass was performed and unexpectedly revealed massive invasion of the pleura by a mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma. Subsequent bone marrow biopsy showed no morphologically or immunohistochemical signs of invasion by this lymphoma. Upon histopathology, lymphoid tissue with an expansion of abnormal follicular dendritic cells and barely no rests of germinal centers (BCL6/CD10) was observed. The whole tissue is taken in by marginal zone lymphoma (MZL) (CD20 positive, MUM1/IgD/CD43 some positive). The inflammatory component consisted of small T-lymphocytic inflammation with mainly plasma cells (Fig. 2). The fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) testing reported an increase in the number of copies of chromosome regions 3q (BCL6) and 18q (MALT1). Further NGS detected only a tumor necrosis factor, alpha-induced protein 3 (TNFAIP3) stop codon mutation (NM_001270508.1(TNFAIP3):c.1097_1098del (p.(Glu366Glyfs∗22)).Fig. 2VATS-guided biopsy of the pleura. After consulting with a multidisciplinary team, the final diagnosis was a stage IV MALT-lymphoma presenting as a bilateral pleural mass. Systemic therapy with rituximab-bendamustine was administered. Follow-up FDG-PET-CT scan after 4 chemotherapy cycles confirmed remission and complete radiological response.

Discussion

Pleural involvement with MALT-lymphoma is rare, with only a few cases in literature to date [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. The development of any type of primary malignant lymphoma in the pleura is rare as well, and typically associated with chronic inflammation such as the pyothorax associated lymphoma (PAL) [13,14]. PAL is highly related with longstanding pleural effusion due to chronic tuberculous infection, which was not the case in our patient. Moreover, PAL usually shows more aggressive types of lymphoma such as high-grade or diffuse large-cel B-phenotype [9,13,[15], [16], [17]] and is less commonly associated with MALT-lymphomas.

It can sometimes be difficult to differentiate MALT-lymphoma from other indolent lymphomas (i.e. lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma or follicular lymphoma) since their histological findings can be indistinguishable from each other [18], which is why additional genetic analysis was performed. In this case there was an increase in the number of copies of MALT1 (Mucosa-Associated Lymphoma Translocation 1) on chromosome 18q21 [19] and a TNFAIP3-mutation [19,20] which is most suggestive for the presence of a MALT-lymphoma.

Prior immunohistochemical characterization of pleural fluid lymphocyte subtypes could have provided a clue in the earlier stage to the diagnosis. In pleural exudates with lymphocytic predominance this diagnostic step should not be overlooked.

In conclusion, we presented a patient with a bilateral pleural mass caused by a MALT-lymphoma. The case is particularly remarkable because there was no known pre-existing pleural inflammation.
