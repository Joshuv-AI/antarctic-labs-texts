Subspace and DOA estimation under coarse quantization
Sjoerd Dirksen, Weilin Li, Johannes Maly

Abstract
We study direction-of-arrival (DOA) estimation from coarsely quantized data. We focus on a two-step approach which first estimates the signal subspace via covariance estimation and then extracts DOA angles by the ESPRIT algorithm. In particular, we analyze two stochastic quantization schemes which use dithering: a one-bit quantizer combined with rectangular dither and a multi-bit quantizer with triangular dither. For each quantizer, we derive rigorous high probability bounds for the distances between the true and estimated signal subspaces and DOA angles. Using our analysis, we identify scenarios in which subspace and DOA estimation via triangular dithering qualitatively outperforms rectangular dithering. We verify in numerical simulations that our estimates are optimal in their dependence on the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of the target matrix. The resulting subspace estimation guarantees are equally applicable in the analysis of other spectral estimation algorithms and related problems.

Remark 2.6

The introduction of

Instead, it may be better to use a greater number of low precision sensors, but utilize them in a smarter way, such that accurate estimation of

is still possible.

Spectral estimation is intrinsically linked to subspace estimation. This stems from the fact that estimating the “signal subspace” on which

An accurate deterministic perturbation and resolution analysis of ESPRIT has been worked out in

{\rm md}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta})\lesssim_{s}\dist(\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}},\boldsymbol{U}).

(3.1)

{\rm md}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\min_{{\pi}}\max_{k}|\theta_{k}-\widehat{\theta}_{{\pi(k)}}|_{\mathbb{T}},

where the minimum is taken over all permutations

From the analysis of ESPRIT in the unquantized setting

Theorem 3.4

Suppose Assumptions
2.7

4.1
An adversarial example

In this example, we will consider an adversarial situation that illustrates the inconsistency of direct rounding for deterministic signals. We first select an arbitrary real
\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{O}^{p\times s}
that will be fixed throughout the experiment. In our simulations,
\boldsymbol{U}
is a single random draw according to the Haar measure, and from performing simulations numerous times, we observe that its choice does not affect the following reported rates of approximation.

In this experiment, we set
p=32
,
s=8
, and will vary
n
. For each
k\in[n]
, let
k^{\prime}=k\mod s
, and we define the clean samples
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
such that
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
is the
k^{\prime}
-th column of
\boldsymbol{U}
. Although
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
cycle through the same
s
samples in a periodic manner, they obviously provide enough information to fully recover the subspace
\boldsymbol{U}
.

We draw real i.i.d. samples
\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{e}_{n}\in\mathcal{U}(-\nu,\nu)^{p}
with
\nu=0.01

\boldsymbol{y}_{k}=\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\boldsymbol{e}_{k}

b=2
and
b=4
bits, and direct rounding with
b=2
and
b=4
bits. Each quantizer uses the same
\lambda
parameter that controls the range of inputs, which in this experiment, is chosen as
\lambda=2
.

For quantized samples generated by either rectangular or triangular dithering, we have already discussed how to compute
\boldsymbol{U}_{n}^{\square}
and
\boldsymbol{U}_{n}^{\triangle}
, as summarized in
Algorithm

1
. If
\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{q}_{n}
are quantized measurements computed via direct rounding, we let
\boldsymbol{U}_{n}^{R}
be the leading left singular space of the matrix
[\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{q}_{n}]
.

To make this comparison among these five quantizers fair, we normalize by the total bits used. A quantizer that uses
b
bits per real scalar will use
bpn
bits for
n
unquantized measurements in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. This means that rectangular, 2-bit triangular, and 2-bit direct rounding can utilize twice as many measurements compared to 4-bit triangular and 4-bit direct rounding.

We perform this routine for 100 trials. Note that the only stochastic component in this experiment is the noise since

is drawn once and fixed in this experiment.

Under the same simplifications as in

\dist(\boldsymbol{U}^{\square}_{n},\boldsymbol{U})\lesssim_{p,\lambda,K}\frac{1}{\lambda_{r}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{x}})\sqrt{n}}.

If we increase

and enforce the scaling

for

, then the upper bound for the sin-theta distance will:

(i)

remain constant at the critical exponent
\beta=1/2
,

(ii)

decrease in
n
when
\beta<1/2
,

(iii)

eventually achieve the trivial upper bound of 1 when
\beta>1/2
.

4.3

Here we verify an important claim from
Theorem

2.10
for the triangular dither. In high signal-to-noise situations where
\mu^{2}+\nu^{2}\leq\lambda_{s}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{x}})
, we have for probability

,

\dist(\boldsymbol{U}_{n}^{\triangle},\boldsymbol{U})\leq C\sqrt{\frac{\nu^{2}+\mu^{2}}{\lambda_{s}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{x}})}}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha p}{n}}=\frac{C\sqrt{(\nu^{2}+\mu^{2})\alpha p}}{\sigma_{s}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})}.

To test the dependence on
\sigma_{s}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})
, we consider the following sequence of deterministic signals. We pick 32 logarithmically spaced integers between
10^{3}
and
10^{7}
. Letting
N
denote any such number, define the vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots\boldsymbol{x}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
for

, where

\boldsymbol{x}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}\,\cos\left(\frac{16\pi(k-1)}{N}\right)&\sin\left(\frac{16\pi(k-1)}{N}\right)&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

That is, the first two components of
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots\boldsymbol{x}_{N}
are equally spaced samples from eight rotations of the unit circle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, while the remaining entries are zero. Clearly,
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N}
are samples from a subspace
\boldsymbol{U}
of dimension

4.4
Spectral estimation example: well-separated case

Here, we consider the quantized spectral estimation problem in the well-separated case where
\Delta(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq\frac{2}{p}
and for deterministic amplitudes. As discussed earlier, this is a highly favorable situation since (
3.2
) shows that
\sigma_{s}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})\simeq\sigma_{1}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})\simeq\sqrt{p}
. Provided that the assumptions in
Corollaries

3.3
and
3.5
hold, the estimators
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}^{\square}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}^{\triangle}
both decay at rates of
O(1/\sqrt{n})
.

To numerically verify these results, we set
p=32
and
s=4
. We create an arbitrary
\boldsymbol{\theta}
that satisfies
\Delta(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq\frac{2}{p}
by setting
\theta_{k}=\frac{4k}{p}+\tau_{k}
where
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{s}\in\mathcal{U}(-\frac{1}{p},\frac{1}{p})
are independent. Here,
\tau
is only used as a random perturbation in order to avoid any number theoretic properties that may have some special interactions with the Fourier transform. Even though
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is partly chosen in a stochastic manner, it is fixed throughout the subsequent experiment. We let the amplitudes
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{a}_{n}
cycle through the
s
canonical basis vectors for
\mathbb{R}^{s}
, where is
n
is always chosen to be a multiple of
s
. In this case,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{a}}=\boldsymbol{I}
and
\lambda_{s}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{a}})=1
.

For each trial, we draw independent
\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{e}_{N}\sim\mathcal{U}_{\mathbb{C}}(-\nu,\nu)^{p}
where
\nu=0.01
, and form the noisy unquantized measurement
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{a}_{k}+\boldsymbol{e}_{k}
for each
k\in[N]
. These samples are quantized using five different quantizers: rectangular dithering with
4
bits, triangular with
b=4
and
b=8
bits, and direct rounding with
b=4
and
b=8
bits. Each quantizer uses
\lambda=6
.

To set up a fair comparison, we normalize by the total bits used. Recall that
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
is a complex vector, so the number of bits used is multiplied by a factor of 2; for example,
4
-bit rectangular dithering uses 2 bits per real scalar and 2 bits per imaginary scalar. A quantizer that uses
b
-bits per complex scalar entry uses
B=bpn
bits for
n
measurements in
\mathbb{C}^{p}
. Since
p
is fixed throughout this experiment, the total number of bits
B
is proportional to
n
.

For each of these five quantizers, we generate quantized samples
\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{q}_{n}
and then proceed to estimate
\boldsymbol{\theta}
using their associated covariance estimators in ESPRIT (see
Algorithm

2
). We repeat the above procedure for 100 trials and report the median matching distance error. Again, this is averaged over 100 draws of the noise, while
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and the amplitudes are fixed. The results are shown in Figure
5
.

Figure 5:
ESPRIT using five different quantizers. The black dashed line is
1/{\sqrt{\rm Bits}}
.

As expected, ESPRIT is not a consistent estimator of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
when given directly rounded measurements because the analog measurements
\{\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{a}_{k}\}_{k\in[n]}
cycle through the same
s
elements in
\mathbb{C}^{p}
and direct rounding is unable to take advantage of additional measurements. On the other hand, the simulations verify our claim that the dithered estimators are consistent and the matching distance error decays at a rate of
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{B}})
as
B\to\infty
, or equivalently,
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
as
n\to\infty
. Triangular dithering improves as we increase the number of bits since this effectively reduces the quantizer resolution
\mu
in the term
\sigma^{2}=\mu^{2}+\nu^{2}
.

This example may seem artificial since the amplitudes cycle through the canonical basis vectors. On the other hand, this is the ideal scenario for spectral estimation. When
\boldsymbol{a}_{k}
is a canonical basis vector, then we collect information from just a single frequency per time snapshot. This is in contrast to a more complicated and realistic scenario where all entries of the amplitude vector are nonzero, and in which case, we would collect a super-position of waves that would need be disentangled. Yet, even for this ideal situation, the performance of direct rounding quickly saturates, which illustrates a significant advantage for dithered quantizers when many samples can be collected.

4.5
Pushing the limits of quantization

Here, we consider the most extreme scenarios that our theory offers. We again look at 4-bit rectangular and triangular dithering for spectral estimation. This is the fewest bits that our methods can use, since we must work with complex measurements, see also Remark
2.2
. We examine the super-resolution scenario whereby the minimum separation is allowed to be arbitrarily small and much smaller than
\frac{1}{p}
. In this setting, spectral estimation is highly sensitive to noise and errors from quantization. We consider the case of deterministic amplitudes for simplicity.

More concretely, we set
p=32
and parameterize the minimum separation by varying
\varepsilon\in[\frac{1}{32p},\frac{1}{p}]
. Consider sources located at
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{0,\varepsilon,\frac{1}{2}\}
so that
\Delta(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\varepsilon
and
s=3
. This is an important example, which corresponds to the scenario whereby two frequencies/objects/sources are close together and difficult to distinguish, while a third frequency/object/source is far away from the others and serves as a decoy. In this scenario, we have
\sigma_{s}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})\geq C\sqrt{p}\,(p\varepsilon)
for some numerical
C>0
, and this estimate is sharp in
\varepsilon
and
p
, see
[
31
]
. If one is interested in replicating the experiment with more complicated choices of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, one can find a corresponding expression for
\sigma_{s}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})
in
[
31
]
.

For large enough
N
, we select vectors
\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{a}_{N}\sim\mathcal{U}_{\mathbb{C}}(-1,1)^{s}
independently and keep them fixed through the rest of this experiment. We also vary the number of measurements
n
while keeping
p
fixed, so the total number of bits
B
used for either 4-bit rectangular or triangular dithering is
4pn
. Since we treat
p
as a constant,
B\simeq n
. For each trial, we generate
\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{e}_{N}\sim\mathcal{U}_{\mathbb{C}}(-\nu,\nu)^{p}
independently with
\nu=0.01
. For
n\leq N
, the unquantized complex samples are
\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}_{n}
, where
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{a}_{k}+\boldsymbol{e}_{k}
for each
k\in[n]
. These are quantized using either 4-bit rectangular or 4-bit triangular dithering with parameter
\lambda=5
.

Figure 6:
Phase transition plots.

For a single trial, we say ESPRIT is successful using rectangular (resp. triangular) dithering if
{\rm md}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}^{\square},\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq\frac{1}{4}\Delta(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon
(resp.
{\rm md}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}^{\triangle},\boldsymbol{\theta})\leq\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon
). Here, the
\frac{1}{4}
factor was chosen arbitrarily, but since
\varepsilon
is the minimum separation of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, success should be quantified relative to
\varepsilon
. For each pair
(\varepsilon,B)
we repeat the above procedure 500 times for each quantizer and report the fraction of trials for which ESPRIT is successful. The noise is the only stochastic component of this experiment.

Our results are shown in Figure
6
. For both quantizers, we see that there is a phase transition region whereby the method appears to go from failure to success. This region is a strip under logarithmic scale. We say
(\varepsilon,B)
is usually successful if at least 95% of 500 trials for that pair of parameters are successful. For each
\varepsilon
, we find the smallest
B
for which
(\varepsilon,B)
is usually successful, and fit these data points with the best possible line in the least squares sense. The slopes of these lines are
-1.6814
and
-1.7193
for 4-bit rectangular and 4-bit triangular, respectively. Hence, the empirical results suggest that
B\geq C(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{1.6814}
and
B\geq C(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{1.7193}
are enough for rectangular and triangular dithering to succeed at least 95% over these trials.

Our theorems cannot fully explain these results. For triangular dithering, using
Theorem

3.4
together with
\sigma_{s}(\boldsymbol{\Phi})\geq C\sqrt{p}\,(p\varepsilon)
and
B=4np
, with fixed probability,

{\rm md}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}^{\triangle},\boldsymbol{\theta})\lesssim_{s,p,\nu,\lambda_{s}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{a}})}\frac{1}{\varepsilon\sqrt{B}},

which would require
B\geq C(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{4}
for usually successful recovery.
For rectangular dithering the gap is even worse. Using
Theorem

3.2
and greatly simplifying the resulting expression, with fixed probability,

{\rm md}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}^{\square},\boldsymbol{\theta})\lesssim_{s,p,\nu,\lambda_{s}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{a}})}\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}\sqrt{B}},

which would require
B\geq C(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{6}
for usually successful recovery.

\lambda_{r}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{x}})=n^{-1}\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})
. Recalling the properties of
\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{n}
from

2.8
, we see that
\sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{n}^{*}
has independent subgaussian rows with
\psi_{2}
-norm bounded by a universal constant, and the entries of
\boldsymbol{\Xi}
are uncorrelated, and have mean zero and unit variance.

From this rescaling, we see that
Theorem

2.10
(i) is a consequence of the following theorem which is a high probability version of
[
4
, Theorem 3]
generalized to our noise setting. One just has to identify
p_{1}
with
n
,
p_{2}
with
p
,
r
with
s
,
\tau
with
K
,
\boldsymbol{X}
with
\frac{1}{\sigma}\boldsymbol{X}_{n}^{*}
,
\boldsymbol{Z}
with
\frac{1}{\sigma}\boldsymbol{\Xi}_{n}^{*}
,
\boldsymbol{Y}

\frac{1}{\sigma}(\boldsymbol{Q}_{n}^{\triangle})^{*}
,
\boldsymbol{V}
with
\boldsymbol{U}
, and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}
with
\boldsymbol{U}_{n}^{\triangle}
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\tau>0
, and
p_{1},p_{2},r\in\mathbb{N}
with
r\leq\min\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
. Suppose
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{F}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}
has rank
r
and the rank
r
truncated SVD of
\boldsymbol{X}
is
\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}
. Assume
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathbb{F}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}
is a random matrix whose rows
\boldsymbol{z}_{1}^{*},\dots,\boldsymbol{z}_{p_{1}}^{*}
are independent, isotropic,
\tau
-subgaussian random vectors. Let
\boldsymbol{Y}:=\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{Z}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{S}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}}^{*}
be the rank
r
truncated SVD of
\boldsymbol{Y}
. There is a an absolute constant
C>0
and constants

and

depending only on

such that for any

, with probability at least

,

\|\sin(\boldsymbol{V},\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}})\|^{2}\leq\min\left\{1,\frac{C\alpha\,p_{2}\,(\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})+p_{1})}{\sigma_{r}^{4}(\boldsymbol{X})}\right\}.

We follow the same steps as in the proof of
[
4
, Theorem 3]
, which requires that
\boldsymbol{Z}
additionally has i.i.d. entries.
The main point of deviation is

5.6

.
We first recall a well known
\varepsilon
-net result.

Lemma 5.4
.

There are absolute constants
C,c>0
such that the following holds. Suppose
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{F}^{r_{1}\times r_{2}}
is a random matrix. Then for all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}(\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\geq ct)\leq C^{r_{1}+r_{2}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{F}^{r_{1}},\,\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}=1\\
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{F}^{r_{2}},\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v}|\geq t).

Proof.

The real case of this lemma is well known, see also
[
5
, Lemma 5]
. For the complex case, we split
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{A}_{1}+\mathsf{i}\boldsymbol{A}_{2}
into its real and imaginary components. First note that
\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\leq 2\|\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\|+2\|\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\|\leq 4\max\{\|\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\|,\|\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\|\}
, where
\|\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\|
and
\|\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\|
are the
\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}
operator norms acting on real vectors.
Thus, for an absolute constant
c>0
and any
t>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}(\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\geq 4ct)\leq\mathbb{P}(\|\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\|\geq ct)+\mathbb{P}(\|\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\|\geq ct).

The final two quantities can be controlled using the
\varepsilon
-net statement for real matrices applied to
\boldsymbol{A}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{A}_{2}
. Hence,

\mathbb{P}(\|\boldsymbol{A}\|\geq 4ct)\leq C^{r_{1}+r_{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{2}\,\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}},\,\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}=1\\
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{2}},\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}_{k}\boldsymbol{v}|\geq t).

Finally, if
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}
are real, a calculation shows that
|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v}|\geq\max\{|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}|,|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}_{2}\boldsymbol{v}|\}
, which establishes that for
j\in\{1,2\}
,

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}},\,\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}=1\\
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{2}},\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}_{j}\boldsymbol{v}|\geq t)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}},\,\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}=1\\
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{2}},\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v}|\geq t)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{r_{1}},\,\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}=1\\
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{r_{2}},\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v}|\geq t).

Combining the above completes the proof.
∎

Next, we establish some basic concentration bounds related to the matrix
\boldsymbol{Z}
.

Lemma 5.5
.

There are absolute constants
C,c>0
such that the following holds. Assume
\boldsymbol{Z}\in\mathbb{F}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}
is a random matrix whose rows
\boldsymbol{z}_{1}^{*},\dots,\boldsymbol{z}_{p_{1}}^{*}
are independent, isotropic,
\tau
-subgaussian random vectors. For any fixed matrices
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{F}^{r_{1}\times p_{1}},\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{F}^{p_{2}\times r_{2}}
and
\boldsymbol{C}\in\mathbb{F}^{p_{2}\times r_{1}}
, we have for all
t>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\|\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{B}\|\geq t)\leq C^{r_{1}+r_{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{ct^{2}}{\tau^{2}\|\boldsymbol{A}\|^{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\|\boldsymbol{C}^{*}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}-p_{1}\boldsymbol{I}_{p_{2}})\boldsymbol{B}\|\geq t)

\displaystyle\leq C^{r_{1}+r_{2}}\exp\left(-c\min\left\{\frac{t}{\tau^{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\|\|\boldsymbol{C}\|},\,\frac{t^{2}}{p_{1}\tau^{4}\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}\|\boldsymbol{C}\|^{2}}\right\}\right).

Proof.

Fix unit vectors
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{F}^{r_{1}},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{F}^{r_{2}}
. Observe that

\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{v}=\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{A})=\sum_{k=1}^{p_{1}}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{*}\boldsymbol{w}_{k},

where we let
\boldsymbol{w}_{k}
denote the
k
-th column of
\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}
. Since the rows of
\boldsymbol{Z}
are independent and the entries of
\boldsymbol{Z}
have mean zero, the previous expression is a sum of
p_{1}
independent mean zero random variables. Since
\boldsymbol{z}_{k}
is
\tau
-subgaussian and
\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}
has rank one,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{p_{1}}\|\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{*}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{\psi_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tau^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{p_{1}}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{k}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\tau^{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\tau^{2}\|\boldsymbol{A}\|^{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}.

Thus, by Hoeffding’s inequality (see, e.g.,
[
47
, Theorem 2.6.2]
), we get

\mathbb{P}(|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{v}|>t)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{ct^{2}}{\tau^{2}\|\boldsymbol{A}\|^{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}}\right).

Using the
\varepsilon
-net result
Lemma

5.4
, we obtain the first claimed inequality.

For the second part of this lemma, using
\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{*})=\boldsymbol{I}_{p_{2}}
yields

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{C}^{*}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}-p_{1}\boldsymbol{I}_{p_{2}})\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{p_{1}}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{C}^{*}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{*}-\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{*})\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{p_{1}}\Big(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{*}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{C}^{*}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}-\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{*}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{C}^{*}\boldsymbol{z}_{k})\Big).

Due to the assumption that the rows of
\boldsymbol{Z}
are independent, the right hand side is a sum of independent, mean-zero random variables. Next, we see that

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{*}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{C}^{*}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\|_{\psi_{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{z}_{k}^{*}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{\psi_{2}}\|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{C}^{*}\boldsymbol{z}_{k}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\tau^{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\|\|\boldsymbol{C}\|.

Thus, by Bernstein’s inequality (see, e.g.,
[
47
, Theorem 2.8.1]
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|\boldsymbol{u}^{*}\boldsymbol{C}^{*}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}-p_{1}\boldsymbol{I}_{p_{2}})\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{v}|>t)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-c\min\left\{\frac{t}{\tau^{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\|\|\boldsymbol{C}\|},\,\frac{t^{2}}{p_{1}\tau^{4}\|\boldsymbol{B}\|^{2}\|\boldsymbol{C}\|^{2}}\right\}\right).

The
\varepsilon
-net result in
Lemma

5.4
completes the proof.
∎

From Lemma
5.5
, we further derive the following concentration bounds. We use some additional notation to simplify the presentation. For a matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
, let
P_{\boldsymbol{A}}
be the orthogonal projection onto the range of
\boldsymbol{A}
. For
\boldsymbol{V}\in\mathbb{F}^{p_{2}\times r}
with orthonormal columns, let
\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}\in\mathbb{F}^{p_{2}\times(p_{2}-r)}
be a matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for the orthogonal complement of the subspace spanned by
\boldsymbol{V}
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Under the same notation and assumptions of
Theorem

5.3
, there is an absolute
C>0
and constants
C_{\tau},c_{\tau}>0
depending only on
\tau
such that for any
t>0
,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\big(\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V})\leq(\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})+p_{1})(1-t)\big)\\
&\qquad\leq\exp\left(Cr-c_{\tau}(\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})+p_{1})\min\{t,t^{2}\}\right),\end{split}

(5.4)

and

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\big(\sigma_{r+1}^{2}(\boldsymbol{Y})\geq p_{1}(1+t)\big)\\
&\qquad\leq\exp\left(Cp_{2}-c_{\tau}p_{1}\min\{t,t^{2}\}\right).\end{split}

(5.5)

If additionally,
\sigma_{r}(\boldsymbol{X})\geq C_{\tau}p_{2}
, then for any
t>0
,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}\left(\|P_{\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}}\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}\|>t\right)\\
&\leq\exp\left(Cp_{2}-c_{\tau}\min\left\{t^{2},t\sqrt{\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})+p_{1}}\right\}\right)\\
&\qquad+\exp\left(-c_{\tau}(\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})+p_{1})\right).\end{split}

(5.6)

Proof.

We use the shorthand notation
\sigma_{k}:=\sigma_{k}(\boldsymbol{X})
. Throughout,
C,c
are absolute constants while
C_{\tau},c_{\tau}
are constants that only depend on
\tau
. Their values may change from one line to another. Define the
r\times r
diagonal matrix,

\boldsymbol{M}=\diag\left((\sigma_{1}^{2}+p_{1})^{-1/2},\dots,(\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1})^{-1/2}\right).

Setting
\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}:=\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}
, we see that

\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}_{1}\boldsymbol{Y}_{1})=\boldsymbol{S}^{2}+p_{1}\boldsymbol{I}_{r}\quad{\rm and}\quad\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}_{1}\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M})=\boldsymbol{I}_{r}.

Proof of (
5.4
)
. Observe that

\displaystyle\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{1})

\displaystyle\geq\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{M}^{-1})\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M})

\displaystyle\geq(\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1})\big(1-\|\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{I}_{r}\|\big).

Some algebraic manipulations show that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{I}_{r}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\boldsymbol{X}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{M}+\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{M}

\displaystyle\quad+\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}-p_{1}\boldsymbol{I}_{p_{2}})\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{M}.

Note that
\|\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{M}\|=\|\boldsymbol{M}\|\leq(\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1})^{-1/2}
and
\|\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{M}\|=\|\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{M}\|\leq 1.
We use
Lemma

5.5
for each term to see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\|\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\boldsymbol{X}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{M}\|\geq t/3)

\displaystyle\leq C^{r}\exp\left(-\frac{ct^{2}}{\tau^{2}}(\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1})\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\|\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{M}\|\geq t/3)

\displaystyle\leq C^{r}\exp\left(-\frac{ct^{2}}{\tau^{2}}(\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1})\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\|\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}-p_{1}\boldsymbol{I}_{p_{2}})\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{M}\|\geq t/3)

\displaystyle\leq C^{r}\exp\left(-c\min\left\{\frac{t}{3\tau^{2}}(\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1}),\,\frac{t^{2}}{9p_{1}\tau^{4}}(\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1})^{2}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C^{r}\exp\left(-c_{\tau}(\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1})\min\left\{t,t^{2}\right\}\right).

This now implies

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}(\|\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{I}_{r}\|\geq t)\\
&\leq\exp\left(Cr-c_{\tau}(\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1})\min\{t,t^{2}\}\right).\end{split}

(5.7)

Combining these inequalities yields (
5.4
).

Proof of (
5.5
)
.
By the Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem,

\displaystyle\sigma_{r+1}(\boldsymbol{Y})

\displaystyle=\min_{\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{A})\leq r}\|\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{A}\|

\displaystyle=\min_{\operatorname{rank}(\boldsymbol{A})\leq r}\|\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{Z}-\boldsymbol{A}\|\leq\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|,

where the final inequality follows by taking
\boldsymbol{A}=\boldsymbol{X}
. Hence, using that
\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z})=p_{1}\boldsymbol{I}_{p_{2}}
, we find

\sigma_{r+1}^{2}(\boldsymbol{Y})\leq\|\boldsymbol{Z}\|^{2}=\sigma_{1}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z})\leq p_{1}+\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}-\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z})\|.

We now use
Lemma

5.5
to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(p_{1}^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}-\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z})\|>t)

\displaystyle\leq C^{p_{2}}\exp(-c_{\tau}p_{1}\min\{t,t^{2}\}).

Hence, with probability at least
1-\exp(Cp_{2}-c_{\tau}p_{1}\min\{t,t^{2}\})
, we have
\|\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}-\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z})\|\leq p_{1}t
and in particular

\sigma_{r+1}^{2}(\boldsymbol{Y})\leq p_{1}(1+t).

Proof of (
5.6
)
. We start with the calculation

\displaystyle\|P_{\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}}(\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp})\|

\displaystyle=\|(\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M})(\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M})^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp})\|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{r}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M})\|\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}\|.

We treat these two terms separately. For the first, note that
\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M})=\sigma_{r}(\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M})\geq 1-\|\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{I}_{r}\|
. Using (
5.7
) with
t=\frac{1}{2}
and the assumption that
\sigma_{r}(\boldsymbol{X})\geq C_{\tau}p_{2}
hence
\sigma_{r}(\boldsymbol{X})\geq C_{\tau}r
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M})<\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\|\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M}-\boldsymbol{I}_{r}\|>\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(Cr-c_{\tau}(\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1}))

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-c_{\tau}(\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1})).

For the second term, since
\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}=\boldsymbol{0}
, we see that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\boldsymbol{X}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}+\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}-p_{1}\boldsymbol{I}_{p_{2}})\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}.

Recall that
\|\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{M}\|\leq 1
and
\|\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{M}\|\leq(\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1})^{-1/2}
. Note that
\|\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}\|\leq 1
. By
Lemma

5.5
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\|\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\boldsymbol{X}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}\|>t/2)

\displaystyle\leq C^{p_{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{ct^{2}}{\tau^{2}}(\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1})\right),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\|\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}(\boldsymbol{Z}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}-p_{1}\boldsymbol{I}_{p_{2}})\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}\|>t/2)

\displaystyle\leq C^{p_{2}}\exp\left(-c\min\left\{\frac{t}{\tau^{2}}\sqrt{\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1}},\frac{t^{2}}{p_{1}\tau^{4}}(\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1})\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C^{p_{2}}\exp\left(-c\min\left\{\frac{t}{\tau^{2}}\sqrt{\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1}},\frac{t^{2}}{\tau^{4}}\right\}\right).

Thus, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\|\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}\|>t)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(Cp_{2}-c_{\tau}\min\left\{t^{2},t\sqrt{\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1}}\right\}\right).

To finish the proof, we note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\|P_{\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}}(\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp})\|>t)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{r}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M})\|\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}\|>t)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma_{r}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M})\|\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}\|>t,\,\sigma_{r}^{-2}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M})\leq 2\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma_{r}^{-2}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M})>2\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\|\boldsymbol{M}^{*}\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}\|>\frac{t}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\sigma_{r}^{-2}(\boldsymbol{Y}_{1}\boldsymbol{M})>2\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(Cp_{2}-c_{\tau}\min\left\{t^{2},t\sqrt{\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1}}\right\}\right)+\exp(-c_{\tau}(\sigma_{r}^{2}+p_{1})).

∎

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem
5.3
.

Thanks to the trivial estimate
\|\sin(\boldsymbol{V},\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}})\|\leq 1
, it suffices to prove the result if

\sigma_{r}^{4}(\boldsymbol{X})\geq{C}\alpha\,p_{2}\,(\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})+p_{1}).

(5.8)

Observe that this holds if and only if

(\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})-\sigma_{+})(\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})-\sigma_{-})\geq 0,

where

\sigma_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left({C}\alpha p_{2}\pm\sqrt{{C^{2}}\alpha^{2}p_{2}^{2}+4{C}\alpha p_{1}p_{2}}\right).

Since
\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})\geq 0\geq\sigma_{-}
, we see that

\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})\geq\sigma_{+}\geq{C}\alpha p_{2}.

(5.9)

By
[
4
, Proposition 1]
, if
\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}\in\mathbb{F}^{p_{2}\times(p_{2}-r)}
is such that
[\boldsymbol{V}\ \boldsymbol{V}_{\perp}]
is an orthogonal matrix
and
\sigma_{r}(\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V})>\sigma_{r+1}(\boldsymbol{Y})
, then

\|\sin(\boldsymbol{V},\widehat{\boldsymbol{V}})\|^{2}\leq\min\left\{1,\frac{\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V})\|P_{\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}}(\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp})\|^{2}}{(\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V})-\sigma_{r+1}^{2}(\boldsymbol{Y}))^{2}}\right\}.

Throughout this proof,
C^{\prime},c^{\prime}
, etc. are absolute constants, while
C_{\tau},c_{\tau}
are constants that only depend on
\tau
. The value of these constants may change from one line to another.
For
t=\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})/{4}(\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})+p_{1})
consider the event

A_{t}=\{\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V})\geq(\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})+p_{1})(1-t)\}.

Using (
5.4
) and
\min\{t,t^{2}\}=t^{2}
as
0<t\leq 1
, we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{t}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\exp({C^{\prime}}r-(\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})+p_{1})\min\{t,t^{2}\})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left({C^{\prime}}r-c_{\tau}\frac{\sigma_{r}^{4}(\boldsymbol{X})}{\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})+p_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-c_{\tau}\alpha p_{2})

by (
5.8
), provided that
\alpha_{\tau}
is large enough.
For
s=\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})/({4}p_{1})
consider

B_{s}=\{\sigma_{r+1}^{2}(\boldsymbol{Y})\leq p_{1}(1+s)\}.

By (
5.5
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(B_{s}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\exp({C^{\prime}}p_{2}-c_{\tau}p_{1}\min\{s,s^{2}\})

\displaystyle=\exp\left({C^{\prime}}p_{2}-c_{\tau}p_{1}\min\left\{\frac{\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})}{p_{1}},\frac{\sigma_{r}^{4}(\boldsymbol{X})}{p_{1}^{2}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left({C^{\prime}}p_{2}-c_{\tau}\frac{\sigma_{r}^{4}(\boldsymbol{X})}{\sigma_{r}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X})+p_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-c_{\tau}\alpha p_{2})

by (
5.8
), provided that
\alpha_{\tau}
is large enough.
Finally, for
u=\sqrt{\alpha p_{2}}
consider

C_{u}=\{\|P_{\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}}(\boldsymbol{Y}\boldsymbol{V}_{\perp})\|\leq u\}.

By (
5.6
),
