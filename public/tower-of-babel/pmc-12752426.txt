The relationship between flourishing, social support, and regulatory emotional self-efficacy in maintenance hemodialysis patients: a network analysis
Authors: Xiaoqing Zeng, Yan Fan, Hongli Yan, Jie Yang, Jing Wu, Jing Chu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients receiving maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) face significant physical and psychological challenges due to the chronic nature of their condition as well as the demands of treatment regimens. Mental health plays a crucial role in their overall well-being, yet the interplay between psychological factors such as social support, regulatory emotional self-efficacy, and flourishing remains underexplored.ObjectiveThis study aims to investigate the complex relationships between social support, regulatory emotional self-efficacy, and flourishing among patients receiving MHD using advanced network analysis to identify key factors that could inform targeted interventions to enhance their mental health and quality of life.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 376 patients receiving MHD from tertiary and secondary hospitals in Shanghai. Data were collected through standardized questionnaires assessing flourishing, perceived social support, and regulatory emotional self-efficacy. Network analysis was used to explore the associations and centrality of these variables related to the psychological well-being of patients receiving MHD.ResultsThe network analysis revealed that positive emotion is a central node, strongly connected to other dimensions of flourishing, social support, and emotional self-regulation. Accomplishment and meaning were also closely intertwined, indicating their pivotal roles in the well-being of patients receiving MHD. Notably, family support was negatively associated with engagement, highlighting potential areas of conflict within familial caregiving dynamics.ConclusionsThis study underscores the importance of positive emotion, accomplishment, and meaning in the psychological well-being of patients receiving MHD. The findings suggest that interventions aimed at enhancing emotional self-regulation and strengthening social support networks, could improve the mental health outcomes for this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07624-3.Keywords: Maintenance hemodialysis, Flourishing, Social support, Regulatory emotional self-efficacy, Network analysis

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a global health issue affecting approximately 10% of the population worldwide [1]. Kidney failure, defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 15 ml/min/1.73 m², represents the final stage of CKD requiring renal replacement therapy to sustain life [1]. With approximately 4.59 million patients undergoing renal replacement therapy worldwide in 2023 and projections suggesting an increase to 5.4 million by 2030 [2], hemodialysis remains the predominant form of treatment [3].

Patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) face significant psychological challenges alongside their physical symptoms. These patients typically experience multiple symptoms post-dialysis, including emotional disturbances that significantly impact their quality of life [4]. Depression and anxiety have been identified as substantial risk factors for mortality in this population [5], highlighting the critical relationship between mental health and survival outcomes [6].

While traditional research has focused primarily on negative psychological states in patients receiving MHD, positive psychology has redirected attention toward flourishing and well-being [7, 8]. Flourishing, conceptualized by Martin Seligman through the PERMA model (Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, and Accomplishment), represents an optimal state of mental health beyond the mere absence of psychological distress [9]. For patients receiving MHD managing a chronic condition with significant treatment demands, understanding the components that contribute to flourishing is particularly relevant.

Social support and regulatory emotional self-efficacy emerge as two critical factors potentially associated with flourishing in patients receiving MHD. Social support, defined as beneficial interpersonal interactions that shield individuals from stress [10], provides essential resources for patients navigating a demanding treatment regimen. Studies demonstrate that patients receiving MHD with strong perceived support from family, healthcare providers, and friends maintain better mental health [11] and demonstrate greater treatment adherence in managing their condition [12].

Regulatory emotional self-efficacy—an individual’s confidence in their ability to express positive emotions and manage negative ones [13]—directly influences coping capacity and psychological outcomes [14]. For patients receiving MHD facing ongoing stressors, the ability to effectively regulate emotions is vital for maintaining psychological well-being while managing the chronic nature of their disease.

According to Conservation of Resources Theory, individuals strive to obtain, retain, and protect valuable resources, including social support and emotional resilience [15]. For patients receiving MHD facing chronic stressors, these psychological resources may be crucial in maintaining well-being despite their health challenges. However, the complex interrelationships between flourishing, social support, and regulatory emotional self-efficacy remain underexplored in this population.

While previous studies have examined these factors individually, there is a specific gap in understanding how they interact within a network structure. Network analysis provides a sophisticated methodology for mapping complex relationships between variables, with nodes representing elements and edges representing connections [16, 17]. By measuring the centrality of nodes, researchers can determine their relative influence within the system [18], potentially identifying key intervention targets.

Therefore, the study aims to investigate the network structure of flourishing, social support, and regulatory emotional self-efficacy among patients receiving MHD. By examining which aspects of social support and regulatory emotional self-efficacy are central to flourishing, we seek to identify priority targets for interventions designed to enhance mental health outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Participants and procedures

Patients receiving MHD were selected as the study subjects. Inclusion criteria: (1) Meeting the International Society of Nephrology’s kidney failure diagnostic criteria which is defined as eGFR less than 15 mL/min/1.73 m² [19] (2) Age ≥ 18 years; (3) Dialysis duration ≥ 3 months (aligning with the Kidney Disease guideline definition of kidney failure with replacement therapy as maintenance dialysis for 90 days or more [2]), with no hospitalization in the past month, absence of acute complications and consistent laboratory values within normal ranges for dialysis patients; (4) Clear consciousness, no cognitive impairment, and proficient in Mandarin Chinese with the ability to comprehend study questions and provide responses independently; (5) Signed informed consent and voluntary participation. Exclusion criteria: (1) Recent major surgery; (2) Severe comorbidities such as heart failure, myocardial infarction, or cancer; (3) History of mental illness, severe organic mental disorders, or personality disorders.

Data were collected via paper-and-pencil tests and electronic questionnaires between October and November 2022. Using convenience sampling, patients receiving MHD meeting the study requirements were selected from three tertiary hospitals and one secondary hospital in Shanghai. Contact was made with hospitals and relevant departments to explain the study’s purpose and obtain consent. Research tools were distributed on-site to eligible patients receiving MHD, the study’s purpose and content were explained, and informed consent was obtained before conducting the survey. Completed questionnaires were collected on the spot, checked for missing or multiple responses, and verified with the patients. Missing data were handled by complete-case analysis under the assumption that they were missing completely at random (MCAR), given their expected low proportion. A sensitivity analysis was planned to compare the baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants to validate this approach.

Measurements

Socio-demographic variables

A socio-demographic questionnaire was designed by the researchers after a literature review, including gender, age, education level, marital status, primary caregiver, employment status, and dialysis duration.

PERMA profiler (Chinese Version, PP-C)

The PERMA Profiler was developed by Butler and Kern based on Seligman’s PERMA theory of flourishing and was adapted into Chinese by the research team to assess the flourishing level of the population [20]. The scale consists of 23 items across 9 dimensions, including 15 items forming five dimensions (Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationship, Meaning, Accomplishment) and 8 supplementary items assessing overall well-being (1 item), negative emotion (3 items), physical health (3 items), and loneliness (1 items). Each item is scored on a scale of 0–10, with 0 representing a lower level and 10 representing a higher level. The total flourishing score is the sum of the scores of the five dimensions and overall well-being. The Cronbach’s α of the scale in this study was 0.895.

Perceived social support scale (PSSS)

The Perceived Social Support Scale was developed by Zimet et al., and was revised by Huang Li et al., to assess the overall level of perceived social support [21]. The PSSS consists of 12 items across three dimensions: family support, friend support, and other support. Scored using a 7-point Likert scale, total social support score is the sum of the scores of all items. The higher the score, the higher the level of perceived social support. The Cronbach’s α of the scale in this study was 0.872.

Regulatory emotional self-efficacy scale (RES)

The Regulatory Emotional Self-Efficacy Scale was developed by Caprara et al., and revised by Shufeng Wen et al. [22]. The RES consists of 12 items across three dimensions: perceived self-efficacy in expressing positive affect (POS), perceived self-efficacy in managing despondency (DES), and perceived self-efficacy in managing anger (ANG). Scored using a 5-point Likert scale, the higher the score, the stronger the individual’s regulatory emotional self-efficacy. The Cronbach’s α of the scale in this study was 0.875.

Statistical analysis

Sample size adequacy for network analysis

Prior to conducting the analyses, we ensured that our sample size (N = 376) was adequate for reliable network estimation. Although power analysis for network models is complex, our sample size was evaluated against established methodological recommendations [23, 24]. The empirical adequacy of our sample size was further confirmed by the high stability of the resulting network, as indicated by a correlation stability coefficient (CS-coefficient) of 0.673 for strength centrality—far above the recommended threshold of 0.50 [23].

Descriptive statistics

The data were analyzed in SPSS 26.0. Descriptive statistics are presented as the mean, standard deviation (SD), frequency, and percentage (%). Furthermore, we calculated the correlation of dimensions and plotted the correlation matrix between the variables using the R package ‘corrplot’ in R Studio.

Network estimation

All network analyses were conducted in RStudio. Initially, we employed the Gaussian graphical model (GGM) to construct the network structure for continuous data. In the GGM, the structure is represented as interacting nodes, where nodes symbolize variables, and edges between nodes represent the strength of associations between variables, accounting for all other variables in the network. If two nodes are connected in the resulting graph, it indicates that these nodes are conditionally dependent after controlling for all other variables. The absence of an edge implies conditional independence between the nodes. The GGM is typically estimated using the graphical lasso, a method that applies regularization to avoid estimating false edges, resulting in a sparse network that adequately describes the data [25]. Specifically, we utilized the ‘estimateNetwork’ function from the R package ‘bootnet’ and the R package ‘qgraph’ to construct and evaluate the network.

Network inference

We used the R package ‘bootnet’ to compute centrality indices for the estimated network to determine the importance of each node. There are three primary measures of centrality: strength, which indicates how well a node is directly connected to other nodes; closeness, which measures average value of distance between nodes and other nodes; and betweenness, which quantifies the number of times a node acts as a bridge along the shortest path between two other nodes [26]. A node with strong direct connections to other nodes is deemed important and should be centrally positioned in the graph; a node with closer indirect connections to others is more susceptible to network changes; a node that frequently serves as a bridge in pairwise relationships is crucial to the overall network structure. Recent studies have shown that closeness and betweenness indices are relatively unstable [23], which our analysis also confirms. Therefore, we will focus on node strength in the remainder of the article.

Network stability

We used the R package ‘bootnet’ to assess the stability of the network structure. The stability of centrality indices was evaluated using case-dropping bootstrapping (1,000 bootstrap samples), and stability was quantified using correlation stability coefficients (CS-coefficients). An acceptable stability CS coefficient is greater than 0.25, a value above 0.5 indicates strong stability, while a value below 0.25 signifies instability [23].

Results

Among the 406 questionnaires collected, 376 were deemed valid, resulting in an effective response rate of 92.61%. Questionnaires were excluded if respondents had undergone dialysis for less than three months, provided incomplete responses, or exhibited inconsistent answers.

Centrality indices (Within variable)

The standardized estimates of the centrality indices for strength, closeness, and betweenness are shown in Fig. 3. Strength centrality quantifies the degree of direct connections a node has with other nodes. Considering strength, the nodes with the most connections were Positive emotion (P), Meaning (M), perceived self-efficacy in managing despondency (DES), and Accomplishment (A). Closeness centrality considers a node’s distance to other nodes in the network, reflecting its position and role within the entire network. Considering closeness, the nodes with the highest inverse sum of the shortest paths were Positive emotion and POS. Flourishing, with the highest strength centrality, was positive emotion, while regulatory emotional self-efficacy, with the highest strength centrality and betweenness, was managing despondency. These findings indicate the strong impact of positive emotion and perceived self-efficacy in managing despondency in the network.

Network stability

Stability analyses indicated that the network was accurately estimated, with small to moderate confidence intervals around the edge weights. The results are shown in Fig. 4. These coefficients were obtained using a 100% sample of individuals and a subset that tapers off as the percentage of cases decreases. The correlation-stability coefficient for strength centrality was 0.673, while closeness and betweenness were higher than 0.25. The recommended threshold for stable estimation does not fall below 0.25, preferably surpassing 0.5. Consequently, emphasis was placed on interpreting strength.

Discussion

The present study offers a novel perspective on the concurrent associations among flourishing, three types of regulatory emotional self-efficacy, and three types of social support in a sample of 376 patients receiving MHD. The results reveal that positive emotion is the main pathway linking the network constructs, while perceived self-efficacy in managing despondency and family support significantly impact flourishing. These findings may be useful for future prevention and intervention efforts to promote mental health in patients receiving MHD.

Positive emotion is the core of the network structure

The constructed network reveals the unique relationships between different types of social support, emotional self-regulation efficacy, and flourishing. Positive emotion is the main pathway connecting the three constructs’ mutual influences. Positive emotions are feelings produced when goals are achieved or positively evaluated by others [27]. Fredrickson proposed the “Broaden-and-Build” theory of positive emotions, suggesting that positive emotions expand cognitive processes, enabling individuals to more effectively acquire and analyze information and take creative actions, thereby building and enhancing personal resources [28]. This is caused by the virtuous interaction between the positive meanings inherent in events and the positive outcomes they produce. Positive emotions need to be accurately conveyed by the individual to bring about beneficial effects [29].Individuals with high self-efficacy beliefs are more likely to take appropriate actions to cope with negative life events [30]. Therefore, patients receiving MHD with a high sense of self-efficacy in expressing positive emotions may be more willing to share positive emotions with others in social interactions, thus experiencing a sense of well-being. The PERMA theory defines flourishing from five aspects. This study found that positive emotions are positively correlated with all other aspects. From a social functionality perspective, positive emotional experiences support intimate relationships by promoting behaviors that encourage reciprocity and bonding [31]. A longitudinal study also found that positive emotions are positively correlated with a sense of accomplishment, and individuals with positive emotions perform better in experiments [32]. Patients receiving MHD may receive various types of social support in interpersonal relationships, Social support provides patients with resources to cope with the disease, such as financial support, emotional support, and daily care, leading to positive experiences [33]. For example, healthcare workers used Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy to intervene in kidney failure patients and found that it could alleviate psychological distress and improve mental health in these patients [34]. These effects were achieved through the increase in social resources, such as healthcare workers’ interventions.

Beyond expanding cognitive processes, positive emotions also play a crucial role in alleviating risk factors and helping patients cope with negative aspects of their condition. Chloe Tulip et al. found that positive emotion interventions can alleviate patients’ negative emotions [35]. The meta-analysis by Selda Koydemir et al. confirms that positive psychological interventions show promise in effectively promoting subjective well-being [36]. Horner et al. indicated that positive emotions facilitate more effective problem-solving strategies and social support-seeking behaviors rather than avoidance tactics, complementing our finding that individuals with high self-efficacy are more likely to take appropriate actions when confronting negative life events [37]. Additionally, research by Nicolò Granata and Laura Duque demonstrated that positive emotions correlate with better disease self-management and treatment adherence, which is essential for patients receiving MHD [38, 39]. While our study emphasizes positive psychological aspects, these findings suggest that enhancing positive emotions and self-efficacy can help patients receiving MHD more effectively manage disease-related negative factors, potentially reducing their psychological distress and improving their quality of life.

The importance of positive emotions for patients receiving MHD extends beyond general psychological benefits to addressing their specific clinical challenges. patients receiving MHD face unique stressors including time-consuming treatment sessions, dietary and fluid restrictions, vascular access complications, and profound fatigue, which collectively contribute to reduced quality of life and heightened psychological distress. Positive emotions are particularly valuable for these patients as they can enhance coping mechanisms necessary for long-term treatment adherence. Research by Fatemeh Sabouri et al. and colleagues demonstrated that positive psychological states in patients receiving MHD correlate with better phosphate control and improved interdialytic weight gain management—two critical clinical parameters in renal care [40]. Additionally, Untas et al. showed that positive psychological interventions effectively reduce death anxiety in this population [41], while Kyungmin Kim et al. found that psychosocial factors significantly impact quality of life in end-stage renal disease patients [42]. These findings suggest that the benefits of positive emotions extend beyond psychological well-being to potentially influence survival outcomes. The network analysis identifying positive emotion as a central node underscores its potential as a strategic intervention target that could simultaneously improve multiple aspects of well-being in patients receiving MHD, including treatment compliance, interpersonal relationships, and overall resilience when facing this challenging chronic condition.

Accomplishment is closely intertwined with meaning

Among the correlation matrices, accomplishment and meaning had the strongest correlations, the same as the results of the network analysis. Moreover, meaning ranked second in the centrality indices. The strong correlation between accomplishment and meaning in the network for patients receiving MHD is likely due to the way these individuals derive a sense of purpose from their ability to achieve personal goals, even in the face of adversity. Patients receiving MHD face numerous physical and psychological challenges, these achievements can be as simple as adhering to treatment regimens, maintaining personal care, or engaging in social activities despite the limitations imposed by their condition [43, 44]. Moreover, the daily struggles that patients receiving MHD experience often led them to cognitive reappraisal and redefine what life meaning is to them. ‘Live in the moment’ serves as a coping strategy, helping them to stay motivated and engaged in their treatment and daily life [45]. This strategy is closely linked to their ability to maintain a positive outlook on life. This is especially critical for patients who must navigate the constant challenges of dialysis and the accompanying physical and emotional burdens. Studies have shown that patients who find meaning in their lives and set achievable goals are better able to cope with the demands of their illness, leading to improved psychological outcomes [46]. Patients receiving MHD often face a limited ability to control many aspects of their lives, the ability to accomplish goals-whether related to health, social interactions, or personal projects-provides a crucial sense of control and self-determination [47].

Comprehensive social support is important

Most edges in the network are positive, but family support and engagement were negative, which is differs from the results of correlation analysis. Engagement refers to being attracted, interested, and involved in an activity or the world. Patients need to maintain high concentration to complete a task, temporarily forgetting their patient role and experiencing intrinsic well-being [20]. Patients receiving MHD often experience fatigue, making it challenging to maintain long-term concentration on tasks [48]. Patients are no longer able to contribute to daily household chores as they did before, which places greater responsibilities on their families [49]. Therefore, social support is often needed during fatigue to help complete daily tasks [50]. Among different types of social support, friend support is more prominent, possibly due to the lower proportion of unemployed individuals in the surveyed population, leading to more social interactions [51]. Patients with kidney failure experience a high symptom burden, which has been negatively associated with their interpersonal relationships [52]. The prolonged disease treatment often leaves patients and their families feeling overwhelmed. This imbalance in family roles can easily lead to conflicts [49]. The caregiving burden on family caregivers can easily affect the quality of care [53]. Therefore, social support patients receiving MHD cannot rely solely on one form of support; other forms of support from friends, healthcare workers, and fellow patients are equally important.

Practical implications

Positive emotion plays a central role in the network. Clinicians can utilize the characteristics of patients’ flourishing for psychological interventions, such as promoting positive emotions and helping them adjust emotional states. Studies show that writing about positive emotions helps individuals face trauma and stress, leading to more positive emotions [54]. Setting achievable goals in daily life enhances the sense of meaning and accomplishment. Providing more external social support is also beneficial. Healthcare professionals can encourage and assist patients in understanding disease-related information, creating an environment for expressing positive emotions, educating about emotional regulation methods, organizing group support activities, and ensuring disease treatment and care.

Limitation and further studies

Participants were recruited from three tertiary hospitals and one secondary hospital in Shanghai, limiting generalizability to other geographic regions or healthcare settings. The relatively modest sample size may have affected statistical power and network stability. Additionally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences about the relationships between variables in the network structure, while self-report measures utilized may introduce reporting bias, particularly given the potential influence of social desirability among patients receiving treatment. Future research should employ larger, more diverse samples across multiple regions to enhance representativeness, implement longitudinal designs to examine network dynamics over time and provide stronger evidence regarding causal relationships between flourishing, social support, and regulatory emotional self-efficacy, and consider mixed-methods approaches to provide deeper insights into patients’ experiences. Further studies might also investigate how treatment duration, comorbidities, and demographic factors influence network structures, potentially identifying subgroups that might benefit from targeted interventions.

Conclusion

The present study explored the network structure of flourishing, social support, and regulatory emotional self-efficacy among patients receiving MHD. Findings suggest that positive emotion serves as a significant node within this network, demonstrating positive connections with multiple factors. Analysis revealed that family support was negatively associated with engagement, while perceived self-efficacy in managing despondency exhibited positive associations with flourishing components. These identified nodes represent potential targets for clinical interventions. Network analysis demonstrated a robust connection between accomplishment and meaning, indicating that fostering achievement recognition may enhance sense of purpose in this population. Clinical approaches incorporating structured goal-setting that acknowledges patients’ capabilities within treatment constraints may prove beneficial, including establishing attainable objectives and recognizing incremental progress. This research provides preliminary insights into the interconnected nature of psychological well-being factors in the MHD population, establishing a foundation for targeted mental health support strategies.
