Rehabilitation physical activity service systems for people with disabilities in Korea, the United States, Germany, and Japan: paradigm review and policy directions for Korea
Authors: TaeEung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAdults with disabilities engage in substantially less physical activity and experience elevated risks of secondary conditions relative to adults without disabilities. This narrative comparative review examined how rehabilitation physical activity systems are organized across governmental, healthcare, and community service structures in Korea, the United States, Germany, and Japan, and derived policy directions for addressing fragmentation, access limitations, and service sustainability in Korea.MethodsA narrative, policy-oriented comparative review integrating peer-reviewed studies, laws, and official documents was conducted between January 2010 and November 2025.ResultsKorea is transitioning toward a rehabilitation exercise-and-sport delivery model supported by rehabilitation sports public services and data-driven community platforms, yet governance fragmentation and limited community capacity persist. Across the four countries, a concurrent shift toward biopsychosocial and rights-based paradigms, stronger community anchoring, workforce professionalization, and increasing digitalization was identified, although the institutional forms and implementation gaps differed by country.ConclusionsKorea should strengthen an integrated rehabilitation physical activity system that expands sustained opportunities for physical activity and exercise through coordinated community pathways, stable public financing, disability-competent workforce development, interoperable data systems, and co-production with people with disabilities and their families.

Introduction

Adults with disabilities are significantly less likely to meet aerobic physical activity recommendations and have higher rates of cardiometabolic, musculoskeletal, and mental health problems than adults without disabilities [1, 2]. Large-scale syntheses indicate that people living with disabilities are 16% to 62% less likely to meet physical activity guidelines, yet demonstrate clinically significant improvements in cardiovascular fitness, strength, and cardiometabolic risk factors when they participate in structured exercise [1]. Evidence from adults with chronic disease but without disability, such as those with coronary heart disease or heart failure, also shows that exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation reduces hospitalizations and improves health-related quality of life, underscoring the broader relevance of rehabilitation exercise paradigms [3, 4]. Community-based rehabilitation (CBR) programs for community-dwelling older adults in Asian countries similarly report improvements in balance, gait speed, and functional mobility [5].

In Korea, the development of a rehabilitation exercise and sports service-delivery model, the progressive institutionalization of publicly supported rehabilitation exercise and sports services, and the introduction of smart living laboratories together reflect a substantive transition from fragmented, project-based initiatives toward a more integrated and sustainable public service system [6–10]. These initiatives aim to connect medical rehabilitation with community-based exercise programs, supported by rehabilitation exercise professionals and digital platforms that monitor participation and outcomes. Although community-based physical activity and exercise programs may yield substantial health benefits, their integration with medical rehabilitation is essential for addressing condition-specific functional limitations, ensuring clinical safety and appropriateness, and facilitating continuity of care from supervised rehabilitation to sustained participation in community settings, while also reflecting a balanced consideration of both medical and social models of disability. Nevertheless, studies and implementation reports continue to identify gaps in accessibility, intersectoral coordination, workforce capacity, and sustainable financing [6, 7, 9]. These Korea-specific challenges, particularly fragmentation across health, welfare, and sport sectors and limited continuity between clinical and community services, provide the immediate policy rationale for the present review.

Accordingly, this narrative review compared public, quasi-public, and community-linked rehabilitation physical activity structures for adults with disabilities in Korea, the United States, Germany, and Japan and identified policy directions for Korea in light of internationally observed trends and Korea-specific barriers related to fragmentation, access, and service continuity. Although the primary focus is on adults with disabilities, we incorporate evidence from adult rehabilitation populations without disabilities (e.g., cardiac rehabilitation and community-based rehabilitation in older adults) when it provides transferable insights for service design.

Methods

Review design and conceptual orientation

This study was conducted as a narrative, policy-oriented comparative review. Narrative reviews are particularly suitable when the aim is to integrate heterogeneous sources, such as empirical studies, laws, policy documents, and organizational reports, and to construct an interpretive understanding of complex service systems across countries rather than estimate pooled intervention effects [11, 12]. The approach followed general guidance for narrative and configurative reviews, emphasizing transparent reporting of the search strategy, explicit inclusion criteria, and systematic, framework-guided synthesis, while retaining flexibility to incorporate key gray literature and legal texts [11, 12, 22].

The overarching objective was to compare rehabilitation physical activity service systems for adults with disabilities in Korea, the United States, Germany, and Japan, as well as to derive implications for the future direction of the Korean system. The United States, Germany, and Japan were selected as comparator countries because they represent distinct yet influential policy, healthcare, and community-based rehabilitation contexts, thereby providing a meaningful basis for cross-national comparison with Korea in the organization and delivery of rehabilitation physical activity services for adults with disabilities. In line with this objective, we prioritized documents that described system architecture across governmental, healthcare, and community sectors, including governance, financing, community infrastructure, and workforce arrangements. Evidence from adult rehabilitation populations without disabilities (e.g., cardiac rehabilitation and CBR in older adults) was treated as a transferable evidence base to inform disability-inclusive system design, not as direct evidence on people with disabilities [3–6].

Eligibility criteria for inclusion

Documents were eligible if they focused on at least one of the four target countries or presented comparative data including at least one of them; addressed adults with disabilities (≥ 18 years), with inclusion of non-disabled adult rehabilitation populations when paradigmatically relevant; and contained information on policy, service delivery, financing, or workforce structures [3–10, 12–19].

Selection proceeded in three stages. First, titles and abstracts (or executive summaries) were screened for relevance. Second, full texts of potentially relevant records were assessed against the criteria, with particular attention to governance, financing, delivery platforms, and workforce content [6–10, 12–19]. Third, reference lists of included documents were hand-searched (snowballing) to identify additional foundational or policy sources that were not captured initially [11, 22]. When eligibility was ambiguous, documents were retained only if they clarified the evolution of current systems or were repeatedly cited as key references in more recent work [12].

Synthesis and methodological considerations

Synthesis followed a two-step process. First, within-country narrative profiles were developed for Korea, the United States, Germany, and Japan, describing the evolution and current configuration of rehabilitation physical activity systems, cross-checked against official policy documents and recent syntheses where available [1, 2, 6–10, 12–19]. Second, a framework-based comparative analysis was conducted using four predefined analytic dimensions: (1) underlying paradigm (medical vs. biopsychosocial/rights-based); (2) degree of community anchoring; (3) level and form of workforce professionalization; and (4) degree of digitalization and data-driven governance. These analytic dimensions were developed a priori from the review’s policy-oriented comparative framework and informed by the main domains of data extraction, namely governance, financing, workforce, service-delivery platforms, and digital/data systems, as these represented the most recurrent and policy-relevant features across the four country cases. Key elements of the review, the research questions, eligibility criteria, core search strategy, data-charting template, and analytic dimensions, were specified a priori to enhance transparency and rigor, consistent with recommendations for narrative reviews [11, 12]. No formal risk-of-bias assessment was conducted because the primary objects of analysis were system descriptions and policy frameworks rather than effect estimates. Limitations of this design include the possibility of missing non-English and non-Korean sources, incomplete capture of rapidly evolving policy initiatives, and the interpretive nature of the comparative analysis [11, 12, 22].

Document selection and characteristics

In total, 508 records were identified through databases and grey-literature searches. After removal of 48 duplicates, 460 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 360 records were excluded as clearly unrelated to rehabilitation physical activity or to at least one of the four target countries. The full texts of 100 documents were assessed for eligibility, leading to exclusion of 52 documents for the following primary reasons: not focused on one of the four target countries (*n* = 18); no clear link to rehabilitation physical activity or disability/rehabilitation policy (*n* = 16); pediatric-only populations (*n* = 11); and elite or high-performance sport without a rehabilitation or health-promotion context (*n* = 7).

The final sample included in the qualitative synthesis comprised 48 documents, of which 27 were peer-reviewed journal articles and 21 were policy or official documents (government reports, white papers, framework agreements, or legal texts). Results: country profiles of rehabilitation physical activity service systems

Korea

Korea has recently shifted from ad hoc disability sports projects toward an integrated rehabilitation exercise and sports policy. Kim et al. developed a multi-layered service-delivery model that links medical rehabilitation, community centers, and sports facilities through coordinated assessment, individualized exercise prescription, and referrals managed by rehabilitation exercise specialists [6]. The model emphasizes continuity from hospital discharge to community participation and positions rehabilitation exercise as a health-promotion strategy for people with disabilities rather than a purely therapeutic intervention.

Building on this conceptual work, Park et al. used a Delphi method to establish consensus on the structure and functions of RSPS, defining critical roles for physicians, rehabilitation exercise professionals, coordinators, facility managers, and government agencies [7]. A subsequent study by the same group applied service design thinking to create a national rehabilitation sports public service information system that supports physical activity management after hospital discharge, integrating user journeys, digital touchpoints, and stakeholder responsibilities [9].

Complementing these policy-level developments, Lee et al. and Moon et al. described a data-driven smart living lab platform equipped with accessible exercise devices, sensors, and information systems to deliver and evaluate rehabilitation exercise and sports programs in local communities [8, 10]. This platform generates standardized data on participation and outcomes, providing an empirical basis for refining service models and scaling effective practices.

Despite these advances, empirical and policy reports highlight persistent challenges: limited accessibility of sports facilities, regional disparities in service availability, insufficient numbers of rehabilitation exercise professionals, and weak linkages between residential facilities, health providers, and community sports organizations [6, 7, 9]. These issues reflect broader concerns in Korean rehabilitation and disability policy regarding fragmentation and sustainability.

United States

In the United States, rehabilitation physical activity is embedded within a broader rights-based and community-oriented disability policy environment. The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans specify that adults, including those with disabilities, should accumulate at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, plus muscle-strengthening activities on two or more days [12]. Surveillance analyses show that adults with mobility disabilities engage in significantly less leisure-time physical activity and are less likely to participate in muscle-strengthening exercise than adults without disability, underscoring the need for tailored interventions [24].

Rather than a single national rehabilitation sport entitlement, the United States relies on multiple complementary infrastructures: national guidelines, disability-focused health-promotion initiatives, and disease-specific rehabilitation programs. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and NCHPAD support inclusive health promotion by providing technical assistance, resources, and implementation models such as the Inclusive Community Implementation Process and “inclusive healthy communities” approaches, which help local coalitions redesign environments, programs, and policies [2, 14].

A notable feature of the United States paradigm is workforce professionalization; however, the specific occupational groups emphasized within each system differ according to national policy traditions and service-delivery structures. In the United States, professionalization has been supported through inclusive and adaptive fitness training and certification pathways for exercise and fitness professionals, although these credentials are generally voluntary rather than mandatory and their uptake remain variable across practice settings. Germany and Japan likewise rely on broader and more heterogeneous workforces that extend beyond sport coaches alone. Overall, the cross-national comparison suggests that workforce development remains uneven across countries, with persistent gaps in standardization, implementation, and coordination between top-down institutional frameworks and bottom-up adoption in community settings. The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) and NCHPAD have developed an inclusive fitness specialist credential that trains fitness professionals to design adapted exercise programs, address environmental barriers, and collaborate with healthcare providers for adults with functional limitations [23]. This aligns with broader literature on exercise recommendations for adults with specific conditions (e.g., spinal cord injury), which emphasizes individualized assessment, intensity prescription, and risk management while remaining consistent with recommendations for the general adult population [3, 4].

Research on exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation and chronic disease management among adults without disability further illustrates the value of structured, supervised programs integrated into standard care, with demonstrated reductions in hospitalizations and improvements in functional capacity and quality of life [3, 4]. Such evidence provides a transferable foundation for designing rehabilitation physical activity services that also meet the needs of adults with disabilities.

Germany

Germany operates one of the most institutionalized rehabilitation physical activity systems worldwide through Rehabilitations sport and Functions training. Under the Social Code Book IX, these are supplementary rehabilitation benefits that physicians can prescribe and finance through statutory health, pension, or accident insurance [13]. The federal framework agreement specifies eligible indications, standard durations (e.g., 50 sessions over 18 months for many conditions), quality criteria, and responsibilities of insurers and providers [13, 15].

Rehabilitations sport is typically delivered as group-based exercise in certified sports clubs and associations, often linked to disability sports organizations. Health insurers, such as IKK classic, explicitly describe its aims as strengthening endurance, strength, coordination, flexibility, and self-management, while fostering long-term transition into independent physical activity and mainstream club sports [15, 16]. For adults with chronic diseases, including cancer survivors or individuals with musculoskeletal conditions, Rehabilitations sport serves as a bridge between medical rehabilitation and community participation.

This review conceptualizes rehabilitation exercise as one component of a broader continuum encompassing community-based physical activity, inclusive exercise, and sport participation for health, autonomy, enjoyment, and social inclusion, thereby avoiding an exclusive reliance on the medical model of disability [1, 2, 17–20]. In this context, therapy-related services and therapist competencies are relevant insofar as they support the safe prescription, supervision, and progression of structured exercise for individuals with condition-specific functional limitations or chronic health needs, but they do not exhaust the meaning or purpose of physical activity for people with disabilities [3, 13]. Recent qualitative work by Schuber et al. examined therapist competencies required in group-based exercise therapy within German rehabilitation facilities [13]. Patients and therapists emphasized the importance of technical expertise, patient-centered communication, motivational strategies, and the ability to manage heterogeneous groups, competencies that are equally relevant for adults without disabilities undergoing rehabilitation for chronic conditions [3, 13].

Japan

Japan’s policy paradigm is shaped by disability and sport legislation and by the strategic role of para-sport. The Japanese government’s Fourth Basic Programme for Persons with Disabilities sets out a comprehensive policy framework to promote independence, social participation, and the elimination of discrimination, explicitly referencing the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities [17]. In parallel, the Basic Act on Sport, enacted in 2011, establishes sport as a right of all citizens, including persons with disabilities, and clarifies the responsibilities of national and local governments to create inclusive sporting environments [18, 19].

Within this legal framework, the JSA has assumed a central role in promoting sports for people with disabilities. Yukishita describes JSA initiatives to increase para-sport participation, improve facility accessibility, strengthen community-based programs, and develop human resources such as coaches and support staff [20]. Policy documents emphasize linkages between rehabilitation, daily living support, and continuous opportunities for exercise and sport in community settings [17–20].

Compared with Germany’s insurance-based scheme, the Japanese model is more strongly oriented toward sport policy and social participation; however, both systems share an emphasis on long-term engagement, community settings, and multi-level governance.

Results: cross-country comparative synthesis

Shared paradigm shifts identified across the four countries

We observed a cross-national shift from predominantly medical models toward biopsychosocial and rights-based frameworks; however, this transition should not be interpreted solely as a right to receive rehabilitation, but more broadly as a right to participate in sport, exercise, and physical activity as integral components of health, autonomy, inclusion, and community life [1, 17–20]. In this context, rehabilitation remains an important pathway for supporting safe and equitable participation, particularly for individuals with condition-specific functional limitations, but it does not exhaust the meaning or value of physical activity for people with disabilities [1, 13, 17–20]. Accordingly, disability and sport policies across the four countries increasingly recognize not only the rehabilitative function of structured exercise, but also the intrinsic and social value of sport and physical activity in their own right [17–20].

Consolidation of community-anchored rehabilitation exercise

In Germany, rehabilitations sport is deliberately located in sports clubs; in the United States, community coalitions and health-promotion programs extend beyond clinical settings; in Japan, community para-sports and local programs are central to policy; and in Korea, smart living labs are explicitly designed as community hubs [5, 6–10, 14, 13, 15–20]. Evidence from CBR in older adults and from cardiac rehabilitation in adults without disability supports the effectiveness of community-based, supervised exercise in sustaining functional gains and independent living [3–5].

Professionalization and accreditation of the exercise workforce

Germany recognizes rehabilitation sports instructors through standardized qualifications linked to the BAR framework agreement; the United States promotes inclusive fitness specialists via the ACSM/NCHPAD; Japan invests in para-sport coaches and support personnel; and Korea is beginning to define competencies and roles for rehabilitation exercise specialists within RSPS and smart living labs [6–10, 23, 13, 20]. This convergence reflects a recognition that high-quality rehabilitation physical activity for both adults with and without disability requires specialized knowledge and interprofessional collaboration.

Increasing digitalization and data-driven governance

Korea’s smart living lab and RSPS information system, NCHPAD’s technology-enabled dissemination, and digital monitoring tools used by insurers and providers in Germany illustrate the importance of data for monitoring utilization, outcomes, and equity [9, 10, 8, 14, 13]. Digital tools also support personalized feedback and remote support for adults with and without disability, although concerns regarding digital access and literacy remain.

At the same time, country-specific facilitators and limitations were evident. Germany benefits from statutory financing and club-based delivery but remains shaped by indication-specific eligibility and formal program structures; the United States benefits from rights-based guidance and community innovation but shows uneven implementation and largely voluntary credential uptake; Japan benefits from strong sport-policy leadership and social participation framing but has less explicit insurance-based rehabilitation exercise entitlement; and Korea has advanced rapidly in model development and digital innovation but continues to face fragmentation, regional inequities, and unstable financing [6–10, 13–20, 23].

Discussion: strategic directions for Korea’s rehabilitation physical activity services

Building on the cross-country synthesis presented above, the Discussion translates these findings into actionable strategic directions for Korea while delineating practical barriers, enabling conditions, and governance considerations that may influence feasibility, scalability, and equity.

Korea’s RSPS and smart living lab initiatives provide a foundation for a life-course rehabilitation ecosystem that links acute care, subacute rehabilitation, community health centers, sports facilities, and workplaces [6–10]. Evidence from exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation and CBR centers among older adults suggests that long-term, supervised programs followed by supported transition to community settings are crucial for maintaining functional capacity and reducing hospital utilization among adults without disability [3–5]. In conclusion, Korea has entered a pivotal transition in which rehabilitation physical activity for adults with disability is being reframed from a discretionary welfare service to a rights-based public service supported by formal models, digital platforms, and community infrastructures. Comparative analysis with the United States, Germany, and Japan indicates that Korea’s evolving system already incorporates key global paradigms—community anchoring, workforce professionalization, and digitalization—yet important gaps remain in legal entitlements, sustainable financing, workforce capacity, data systems, and co-production with users.

By institutionalizing publicly financed rehabilitation exercise benefits, investing in a competent and stable rehabilitation exercise workforce, expanding data-driven and digital service platforms, and embedding co-production with adults with disability and their families, Korea can develop a robust rehabilitation physical activity ecosystem. Such an ecosystem will not only advance the health and participation of adults with disability but also provide a comparative policy framework that may inform context-sensitive adaptation in other countries facing similar fragmentation, access, and coordination challenges.
