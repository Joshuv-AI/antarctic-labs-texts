Mixed Multiple Orthogonal Laurent Polynomials on the Unit Circle
Edmundo J. Huertas 1, Manuel Mañas 2

Abstract
. Mixed orthogonal Laurent polynomials on the unit circle of CMV type are constructed utilizing a matrix of moments and its Gauss–Borel factorization and employing a multiple extension of the CMV ordering. A systematic analysis of the associated multiple orthogonality and biorthogonality relations, and an examination of the degrees of the Laurent polynomials is given. Recurrence relations, expressed in terms of banded matrices, are found. These recurrence relations lay the groundwork for corresponding Christoffel–Darboux kernels and relations, as well as for elucidating the ABC theorem. The paper also develops the theory of diagonal Christoffel and Geronimus perturbations of the matrix of measures. Christoffel formulas are found for both perturbations.

1.
Introduction

Defined with respect to multiple weight functions or measures, multiple orthogonal polynomials form a distinctive class of polynomials that play an important role in fields such as numerical analysis, approximation theory, and mathematical physics. Unlike traditional orthogonal polynomials associated with a single weight function, these polynomials satisfy simultaneous orthogonality conditions, making them valuable for tackling complex analytical problems. For a comprehensive introduction to multiple orthogonal polynomials, see
[
ismail
]
and
[
Nikishin-Sorokin
]
, while
[
afm
]
explores their connections with integrable systems. Their significance also extends to the study of Markov processes and generalized random walks beyond the standard birth-and-death framework, as shown in
[
bidiagonal2
,
Reference10
,
Reference11
,
Reference12
]
.

The concept of mixed multiple orthogonal polynomials was first introduced by Sorokin in 1994
[
sorokin1
]
and later expanded in 1997 in collaboration with van Iseghem
[
Sorokin_Van_Iseghem_1
]
, as they explored matrix orthogonality of vector polynomials, see also
[
Sorokin_Van_Iseghem_2
,
Sorokin_Van_Iseghem_3
]
. This framework re-emerged in 2004
[
Evi_Arno
]
within the study of multiple non-intersecting Brownian motions, where the term “mixed multiple orthogonal” was formally coined. Further discussions can be found in
[
mixto_FUG
]
,
[
afm
]
, and
[
Ulises-Sergio-Judith
]
. In these studies, a rectangular
q\times p
matrix of weights, each of rank 1 at every point in the support, was constructed. However, the most relevant frameworks for the present discussion are found in
[
Sorokin_Van_Iseghem_1
]
and
[
Ulises-Sergio-Judith
]
, where the
q\times p
rectangular matrix consists of functionals or measures.

Mixed multiple orthogonal polynomials play a significant role in the spectral theory of banded operators. For certain banded matrices that admit a positive bidiagonal factorization, it has been demonstrated that a Favard-type spectral theorem holds in this extended framework, generalizing the classical tridiagonal case of Jacobi matrices; see
[
bidiagonal1
,
bidiagonal2
,
bidiagonal3
]
.

Orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle have been the subject of extensive study; see, for example,
[
Simon
]
. A major development emerged with the introduction of the Cantero–Moral–Velázquez (CMV) ordering and the discussion of CMV orthogonal Laurent polynomials
[

,
Simon
,
am
]
. These ideas have since been extended to various settings, such as matrix orthogonality
[
am2
]
and multivariate Laurent orthogonal polynomials on tori
[
am3
]
. Additionally, the Christoffel and Geronimus perturbations have been examined in these extended cases
[
amt
,
am4
,
GMM
]
, see also
[
am3
]
. In the matrix case, the Riemann–Hilbert problem was discussed in connection with the matrix discrete Painlevé II equation
[
CM
]
.

However, to date, almost only two papers have explored possible constructions of multiple orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle: the seminal paper
[
MV-CA08
]
and later
[
CDR-JCAM15
]
. Since then, there has been little further development. In January 2024, the first author announced at the Biennial Meeting of The Spanish Royal Mathematical Society the results presented in this paper; see this
link
. As we were completing the final revisions of this paper, two new papers on the subject appeared
[
KM
,
k

.

This paper develops the framework of mixed multiple orthogonal Laurent polynomials, beginning with an introduction to CMV mixed multiple Laurent orthogonality in Section 2. This section establishes foundational concepts, including the construction of the moment matrix and its Gauss–Borel factorization, which are essential to understanding the properties and structure of these polynomials.

Section 3 explores mixed multiple orthogonal Laurent polynomials in depth, covering their orthogonality properties, recurrence relations, Szegő recurrence matrices, and various recurrence structures specific to both matrix and scalar cases. Additionally, this section introduces the Christoffel–Darboux kernels, which are central to the analysis within this framework, as well as second kind functions.

The study of perturbations begins in Section 4, which addresses diagonal Christoffel perturbations. This section examines the connector matrix, connection formulas, and the Christoffel formulas for key polynomial functions, analyzing how these perturbations modify and extend the classical structure.

Section 5 then explores Geronimus perturbations, presenting foundational tools such as the connector matrix and connection formulas in this context. This section also includes the study of diagonal Geronimus perturbations with singular parts, as well as Christoffel–Geronimus formulas for diagonal Geronimus transformations.

CMV mixed multiple Laurent orthogonality in the unit circle

2.1.
The moment matrix and its Gauss–Borel factorization

A measure
\mu
in unit circle

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbb{T}&\coloneq\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\},&z&=\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{i}\theta},&\theta&\in[-\pi,\pi],\end{aligned}

can be always split into its absolutely continuous part with respect to the normalized Lebesgue measure
\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}
and its singular part
\mu_{s}
,

\displaystyle\mu(\theta)=w(\theta)\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}+\mu_{s}(\theta).

Notice that on
\mathbb{T}
we have
\mathrm{d}z=\mathrm{d}\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{i}\theta}=\operatorname{i}\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{i}\theta}\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{2\pi\operatorname{i}z}.

Hence, we can write for
z\in\mathbb{T}

\displaystyle\mu(z)=w(z)\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{2\pi\operatorname{i}z}+\mathrm{d}\mu_{s}.

We assume that the support of the measure
\mu
is a infinite set in
\mathbb{T}
.
Given two function
f,g
over
\mathbb{T}
two possible inner products can be considered

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\overline{f(\theta)}g(\theta)\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta),&&\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}{f(\theta)}\overline{g(\theta)}\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta),\end{aligned}

where the overline denotes the complex conjugate.
In terms of integrals in the unit circle these integrals are written

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\mathbb{T}}\bar{f}(z^{-1})g(z)\mathrm{d}\mu(z),&&\int_{\mathbb{T}}{f(z)}\bar{g}(z^{-1})\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta),\end{aligned}

respectively.

Further, as we are going to discuss mixed multiple orthogonal systems in the unit circle we need to
consider a
q\times p
rectangular matrix,
q,p\in\mathbb{N}
, with
q
rows and
p
columns,
of Borel complex measures, with support having infinite points, on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}

(1)

\displaystyle\mu(z)

\displaystyle\coloneq\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{1,1}(z)&\cdots&\mu_{1,p}(z)\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\mu_{q,1}(z)&\cdots&\mu_{q,p}(z)\end{bmatrix}

with its splitting in terms of absolutely continuous and singular parts is

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\mu(z)=w(z)\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{2\pi\operatorname{i}z}+\mathrm{d}\mu_{s},

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} w&=\begin{bmatrix}w_{1,1}(z)&\cdots&w_{1,p}(z)\\

w_{q,1}(z)&\cdots&w_{q,p}(z)\end{bmatrix},&\mu_{s}&=\begin{bmatrix}\mu_{s,1,1}(z)&\cdots&\mu_{s,1,p}(z)\\

The Fourier series of this rectangular matrix of measures is

\displaystyle F_{\mu}(u)\coloneq\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{n}u^{n},

for absolutely continuous measures,
\mathrm{d}\mu(\theta)=w(\theta)\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}

satisfies

\displaystyle F_{\mu}(\theta)=w(\theta).

Proposition 2.35

(Szegő type recurrence relations)
.

The left (correspondingly right) mixed type multiple orthogonal polynomials
on the step line
B(z)
and
A(z^{-1})
(correspondingly
\mathcal{B}(z)

and
\mathcal{A}(z^{-1})
) satisfy as well the following recurrence-type
relations

\displaystyle RB(z)

\displaystyle=\bar{\mathscr{A}}(z^{-1}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(z)R

\displaystyle=\bar{A}(z^{-1}),

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}\bar{\mathscr{A}}(z)

\displaystyle=B(z^{-1}),

\displaystyle\bar{A}(z)\mathscr{R}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}(z^{-1}),

\displaystyle S\bar{\mathscr{A}}(z^{-1})

\displaystyle=zB(z),

\displaystyle\bar{A}(z^{-1})S

\displaystyle=z\mathcal{B}(z),

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}B(z^{-1})

\displaystyle=z\bar{\mathscr{A}}(z),

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(z^{-1})\mathscr{S}

\displaystyle=z\bar{A}(z).

Proof.

The action of
R
on the matrix biorthogonal polynomials
B(z)
is as follows

\displaystyle RB(z)=\bar{\mathscr{L}}\eta_{[q]}L^{-1}LZ_{[q]}(z)=\bar{\mathscr{L}}\eta_{[q]}Z_{[q]}(z)=\bar{\mathscr{L}}Z_{[q]}(z^{-1})=\bar{\mathscr{A}}(z^{-1}).

The action of
R
on the matrix biorthogonal polynomials
\mathcal{B}(z)
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(z)R=Z_{[p]}^{\top}(z)\mathcal{U}\mathcal{U}^{-1}\eta_{[p]}\bar{U}=Z_{[p]}^{\top}(z)\eta_{[p]}\bar{U}=Z_{[p]}^{\top}(z^{-1})\bar{U}=\bar{A}(z^{-1}).

Correspondingly, the action of
S
on
\mathcal{B}(z)
yields

\displaystyle S\bar{\mathscr{A}}(z^{-1})=L\nu_{[q]}\bar{\mathscr{L}}^{-1}\bar{\mathscr{L}}Z_{[q]}(z^{-1})=L\nu_{[q]}Z_{[q]}(z^{-1})=zLZ_{[q]}(z)=zB(z)

and finally

\displaystyle\bar{A}(z^{-1})S=Z_{[p]}^{\top}(z^{-1})\bar{U}\bar{U}^{-1}\nu_{[p]}\mathcal{U}=Z_{[p]}^{\top}(z^{-1})\nu_{[p]}\mathcal{U}=zZ_{[p]}^{\top}(z)\mathcal{U}=z\mathcal{B}(z).

∎

3.
Christoffel perturbations

In this section, we will examine the theory of Christoffel transformations of the
q\times p
matrix of measures
\mathrm{d}\mu(z)
defined in (
1
). It means that we will discuss the effect of the multiplication of
\mathrm{d}\mu(z)
by certain appropriate
r\times r
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
diagonal matrices
W_{[r]}(z)
with Laurent polynomials at the entries of the main diagonal, which we will define in detail a little later. Since we are working with matrices, let us observe that these transformations can come from the left hand side (LHS for short in the sequel) multiplication of
\mathrm{d}\mu(z)
, namely
W_{[q]}(z)\mathrm{d}\mu(z)
, and also in a dual manner they can come from the right hand side (RHS for short in the sequel) multiplication of
\mathrm{d}\mu(z)
, namely
\mathrm{d}\mu(z)W_{[p]}(z)
. We will begin with a thorough development of the LHS Christoffel perturbations of
\mathrm{d}\mu(z)
, and towards the last part of the section, as the results are expected to be similar, we will provide a less detailed presentation of the results for the RHS Christoffel perturbations of
\mathrm{d}\mu(z)
.

First of all, we will start by introducing some structures that will be
useful to us in the development of the main ideas. Thus, let us define the
following matrices based on the identity matrix. For
a,r\in\mathbb{N}
,
a\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
,
\nu=0,1,2,\ldots
, the matrix
I_{[r]}^{(a)}
will
be a semi-infinite diagonal matrix with ones just at the positions
(r\nu+a,r\nu+a)
in the main diagonal, and zeros everywhere else. For a given
r
, these matrices satisfy

(65)

\displaystyle I_{[r]}^{(a)}I_{[r]}^{(b)}=\delta_{a,b}I_{[r]}^{(a)},

for
\delta_{a,b}
being the Kronecker delta, and also we find

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{r}I_{[r]}^{(a)}=I

for
I
being the semi-infinite identity matrix. For example, for
r=2
we
have

\displaystyle I_{[2]}^{(1)}

\displaystyle\coloneq\operatorname{diag}(1,0|1,0|1,0,\dots),

\displaystyle I_{[2]}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\coloneq\operatorname{diag}(0,1|0,1|0,1|\dots).

From (
65
) we know that

(66)

\displaystyle I_{[r]}^{(a)}I_{[r]}^{(a)}=\left(I_{[r]}^{(a)}\right)^{2}=I_{[r]}^{(a)},

and also observe that

(67)

\displaystyle I_{[r]}^{(a)}Z_{[r]}(z)=Z_{[r]}^{(a)}.

In this framework, from (
27
), we can define the following
r
projector matrices
\Upsilon_{[r]}^{(a)}
,
a\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, such that

(68)

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{[r]}^{(a)}=\Upsilon_{[r]}I_{[r]}^{(a)}=I_{[r]}^{(a)}\Upsilon_{[r]},

satisfying

(69)

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{r}\Upsilon_{[r]}^{(a)}=\Upsilon_{[r]}\sum_{a=1}^{r}I_{[r]}^{(a)}=\Upsilon_{[r]}I_{[r]}=\Upsilon_{[r]}.

The action of these projector matrices
\Upsilon_{[r]}^{(a)}
on the
monomial matrices (
2
) is such that
\Upsilon_{[r]}^{(a)}
multiplying
Z_{[r]}(z)
yields
z
by a monomial matrix
Z_{[r]}^{(a)}(z)
of size
\infty\times r
, which are all zeros except the
a
-th colum, satisfying

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{r}Z_{[r]}^{(a)}(z)=Z_{[r]}(z).

Moreover, the action of these matrices on a semi-infinite column vector of the monomial matrix
Z_{[r]}(z)
can be seen as follows

(70)

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{[r]}^{(a)}Z_{[r]}^{(b)}(z)=z\delta_{a,b}Z_{[r]}^{(b)}(z).

Notice that these columns
Z_{[r]}^{(a)}
are previously defined in (
3
), and note also the following subtle detail: with this notation,
and taking (
66
) into account, we find that

(71)

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{[r]}Z_{[r]}^{(a)}(z)=\Upsilon_{[r]}I_{[r]}^{(a)}Z_{[r]}(z)=\Upsilon_{[r]}\left(I_{[r]}^{(a)}\right)^{2}Z_{[r]}(z)=\Upsilon_{[r]}^{(a)}Z_{[r]}^{(a)}(z).

Although these semi-infinite projection matrices
\Upsilon_{[r]}^{(a)}
have a countable infinity number of columns identically zero, it
is a simple matter to check that if we restrict their action just to their own subspace, the transpose of
\Upsilon_{[r]}^{(a)}
is also its inverse, that is,
\left(\Upsilon_{[r]}^{(a)}\right)^{\top}=\left(\Upsilon_{[r]}^{(a)}\right)^{-1}
.

For reasons that will become clear a little further ahead, here we are going
to analyze perturbations of a very specific form. Similar perturbations were
described for the first time in
[
amt
]
, and there they were called
“
prepared Laurent polynomials
”.
To describe the particular kind of modifications in which we are interested
here, we first define a
balanced Laurent polynomial
as follows

(72)

\displaystyle W(z)=c(z-z_{1})(z-z_{2})\cdots(z-z_{2d})z^{-d}=\sum_{i=-d}^{d}\omega_{i}z^{i},

for which
\deg^{\pm}W(z)=d
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, with
W(z)
having exactly
2d
different complex zeros. Let us
define now a
r\times r
diagonal matrix

(73)

\displaystyle W_{[r]}(z)\coloneq\operatorname{diag}\left(W_{1}(z),\ldots,W_{r}(z)\right),

with all the entries in its main diagonal being well-poised Laurent polynomials of the same degrees
\pm d
, and different zeros. By extension, we will refer to
W_{[r]}(z)
as
matrix balanced Laurent polynomial
. Under this framework,
W_{[r]}(z)
has a total of
2dr
different and simple zeros, and given a matrix of measures (
1
), in what follows we will consider the new perturbed matrix of measures

(74)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\hat{\mu}(z)\coloneq W_{[q]}(z)\mathrm{d}\mu(z),

where
W_{[q]}(z)
is a matrix prepared Laurent polynomial. The
corresponding Christoffel
W
-modified CMV left (
\mathcal{\hat{M}}
) (resp.
right (
\tilde{\mathcal{\hat{M}}}

(75)

\displaystyle\mathcal{\hat{M}}=\oint_{\mathbb{T}}Z_{[q]}(z)\mathrm{d}\hat{\mu}(z)\,Z_{[p]}^{\top}(z^{-1})=\oint_{\mathbb{T}}Z_{[q]}(z)\,W_{[q]}(z)\mathrm{d}\mu(z)\,Z_{[p]}^{\top}(z^{-1})

and

(76)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{\hat{M}}}=\oint_{\mathbb{T}}Z_{[q]}(z^{-1})\mathrm{d}\hat{\mu}(z)Z_{[p]}^{\top}(z)=\oint_{\mathbb{T}}Z_{[q]}(z^{-1})\,W_{[q]}(z)\mathrm{d}\mu(z)Z_{[p]}^{\top}(z).

Next, with the notation

(77)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}=\sum_{b=1}^{q}W_{b}\left(\Upsilon_{[q]}^{(b)}\right),

we prove the following result concerning the connection between the Gram

Proposition 3.1

The Christoffel
W

\mathcal{\hat{M}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{\hat{M}}=\oint_{\mathbb{T}}Z_{[q]}(z)\,W_{[q]}(z)\mathrm{d}\mu(z)Z_{[p]}^{\top}(z^{-1})=\mathcal{W}\oint_{\mathbb{T}}Z_{[q]}(z)\mathrm{d}\mu(z)Z_{[p]}^{\top}(z^{-1})=\mathcal{WM}.

\displaystyle\left(\Upsilon_{[r]}\right)^{k}Z_{[r]}(z)=z^{k}\,Z_{[r]}(z)=Z_{[r]}(z)\,z^{k},\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

This in turn implies that, for any Laurent polynomial

\displaystyle p\left(\Upsilon_{[r]}\right)Z_{[r]}(z)=Z_{[r]}(z)\,p(z).

In particular, for any given matrix prepared Laurent polynomial

we get

\displaystyle W_{[q]}\left(\Upsilon_{[q]}\right)Z_{[q]}(z)=Z_{[q]}(z)W_{[q]}\left(z\right).

The right hand side above can be written as

\displaystyle Z_{[q]}(z)W_{[q]}(z)=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}Z_{[q]}^{(1)}&Z_{[q]}^{(2)}&\cdots&Z_{[q]}^{(q)}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}W_{1}(z)&&\\

Every entry in the above vector is, in fact, a semi-infinite column vector with Laurent polynomials in their entries, and taking (

\displaystyle Z_{[q]}^{(a)}W_{a}(z)=I_{[q]}^{(a)}Z_{[q]}(z)W_{a}(z),

\displaystyle W_{[q]}\left(\Upsilon_{[q]}\right)Z_{[q]}(z)=

&\ddots&\\
&&W_{q}(\Upsilon_{[q]})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}Z_{[q]}^{(1)}&Z_{[q]}^{(2)}&\cdots&Z_{[q]}^{(q)}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}W_{1}(\Upsilon_{[q]})Z_{[q]}^{(1)}&W_{2}(\Upsilon_{[q]})Z_{[q]}^{(2)}&\cdots&W_{q}(\Upsilon_{[q]})Z_{[q]}^{(q)}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle W_{a}(\Upsilon_{[q]})Z_{[q]}^{(a)}=W_{a}(\Upsilon_{[q]})I_{[q]}^{(a)}Z_{[q]}(z)=W_{a}(\Upsilon_{[q]}^{(a)})Z_{[q]}(z),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(W_{1}\left(\Upsilon_{[q]}^{(1)}\right)+W_{2}\left(\Upsilon_{[q]}^{(2)}\right)+\cdots+W_{q}\left(\Upsilon_{[q]}^{(q)}\right)\right)Z_{[q]}(z)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{b=1}^{q}W_{b}\left(\Upsilon_{[q]}^{(b)}\right)\right)Z_{[q]}(z).
