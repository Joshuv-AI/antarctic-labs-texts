Microbiology Leaders Evolving and Accountable to Progress (MicroBio-LEAP) project: educating, training, and inspiring leaders within the microbial sciences
Authors: Carla Y. Bonilla, Christine M. Pribbenow, Lee Hughes, C. Phoebe Lostroh, Iyiola Oladunjoye, Douglas Johnson, Daniel McArdle, Shaundra Branova, Shannon Vassell, Amy L. Kullas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGiven their formal positions, microbiology leaders have the capability to influence policies and practices that affect the culture of their workplaces and organizations. The Microbiology Leaders Evolving and Accountable to Progress (MicroBio-LEAP) project Training of Leaders program provided scholars with the tools, knowledge, and confidence needed to lead meaningful change and inspire others to do the same. The project goals were to expand awareness, promote inclusion, transform practices, and encourage microbiology leaders to engage in activities that foster awareness, eliminate discrimination, and cultivate a welcoming environment where all can thrive and succeed within the microbial sciences. The project engaged leaders by (i) promoting deep individual introspection, (ii) facilitating peer group exchanges, and (iii) providing a space in which participants applied new knowledge and skills to ensure the training moved from awareness to action. Building a community of change agents will help to develop a positive and welcoming culture for all in the microbial sciences and broader STEM fields.

PERSPECTIVE

Building leadership capacity is essential for institutions and programs to serve the needs of its membership. In particular, scientific societies must develop an inclusive leadership culture that can meet the demands of an increasingly diverse global community in order to ensure science serves society in a just and equitable way. In 2020, the American Society for Microbiology (ASM) Diversity, Equity and Inclusion (DEI) Task Force conducted a Member Perception Survey and found that members perceive the microbial sciences as lacking in diversity in leadership positions and that traditional hierarchical traditions prevent inclusive participation (1). These findings presented an opportunity for a cultural shift among the leadership of ASM to create a climate of authentic inclusion and reject approaches built on oppressive and exclusionary—therefore ineffective—practices.

ASM has a decades-long tradition of contributing to the growth of a diverse population of microbiologists. Such initiatives include establishing fellowships aiming to close the gap in participation of scientists from historically excluded groups (HEGs) (e.g., the 1980 Watkins Fellowship), as well as the formation of various volunteer groups to address the status of HEGs (e.g., the Subcommittee on the Status of Historically Excluded Groups, the Subcommittee on the Status of Women in Microbiology, and the Progress, Impact, Access and Accountability [PIAA] Committee). Significantly, in 2000, ASM was recognized with the Presidential Award for Excellence in Mentoring Underrepresented Minorities in STEM for its partnership with the National Institutes of Health to manage the Annual Biomedical Research Conference for Minoritized Scientists (ABRCMS) (1, 2). Altogether, ASM has contributed to expanding the participation of members from HEGs in various areas of microbiology.

Nevertheless, leadership development among the ASM membership has been an unmet opportunity. With funding from the National Science Foundation Leading Culture Change Through Professional Societies of Biology, ASM sought to train microbiology leaders who wanted to affect cultural change within their spheres of influence. Given their formal positions, microbiology leaders have the capability to influence policies and practices that affect the culture of their workplaces and organizations (3, 4). Microbiology Leaders Evolving and Accountable to Progress (MicroBio-LEAP) complemented the work of ABRCMS, mBio Early Career Editorial Board, and other similar programs with their “bottom-up” approach toward change by taking a “top-down” approach and focusing on the leaders with the strongest influence on practices within their institutions and organizations (Fig. 1). ASM believes that furthering intercultural awareness and sustainable change in the microbial sciences ultimately requires the engagement and commitment of STEM leaders at multiple levels.

ASM's multi-faceted approach to culture and climate shift to engage with early career scientists and leaders in the microbial sciences community using inclusive diversity with equity, access, and accountability practices. The existing programs described are representative but not encompassing of all ASM programs.

RELATIONSHIP TO PRIOR WORK AND MAKING A CASE FOR THE MicroBio-LEAP PROJECT

ASM collected a significant amount of data through the 2020 Membership Perception Survey, shared within the ASM DEI Task Force report, which included diversity-, equity-, and inclusion-related membership perception data about public policy, research, and education affecting HEGs in the microbial sciences (1). These findings collectively confirm the need for change, primarily in the area of leaders’ growth in intercultural awareness and skill development (5).

Sustainable culture change can happen when leaders take purposeful actions to achieve broader participation and inclusion within their organization and then provide ongoing support to others. The MicroBio-LEAP project focused on leaders because research shows that as “frontline representatives of the organization,” leaders play a significant role in modeling their organization’s practices, commitment, and strategies for achieving broader participation and inclusion (6). Within the microbial sciences, leaders influence the policies and practices for advancement within academia (e.g., promotion and tenure review policies), in clinical laboratories, and in the biotechnology and pharmaceutical sectors, publishing in scholarly journals, and opportunities to present one’s work at meetings and conferences (7, 8).

There is extensive literature that supports inclusive diversity as essential for every organization’s or field’s success. For example, the McKinsey Report Series, “Diversity Wins,” builds a strong case for diversity as a means of greater productivity (9). The McKinsey Report shows that the likelihood of outperforming peers on service, quality, and success correlates with diversity, while the penalties are becoming steeper for workplaces lacking diversity. Furthermore, a more diverse organization promotes discovery and innovation (10), while the benefits of diverse groups in academic and organizational settings have also been documented (11).

UNDERSTANDING AND ADDRESSING COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS OF DEI

DEI initiatives are essential for fostering inclusive and productive environments in various sectors, including education, healthcare, and corporate settings. As institutions work to foster more inclusive, equitable, and collaborative environments, common misconceptions about DEI can stall or undermine progress, hindering effective implementation of policies and practices. Clarifying these misconceptions is crucial for organizations and individuals to recognize and maximize the benefits of DEI practices. The following are a few assumptions, along with research that debunks them.

Misconception 1: DEI initiatives lower standards

One of the most persistent myths about diversity efforts is the belief that they lower standards, suggesting that DEI initiatives compromise organization standards and quality by prioritizing diversity over merit (12). It is important to understand that DEI efforts focus on enhancing organizational excellence by ensuring all individuals have equitable opportunities to contribute based on their qualifications and abilities. In fact, when organizations create fair and transparent systems that allow advancement opportunities to all, standards are often raised. Expanding the talent pool brings in new perspectives, fresh ideas, and lived experiences that can spark innovation, creativity, and better problem-solving (12). This approach not only maintains organizational standards but also often elevates them by incorporating a wider range of perspectives and experiences, giving a competitive edge in today’s marketplace (12).

Misconception 2: DEI only benefits HEGs

Another common assumption is that DEI initiatives are only meant to help certain groups, typically those who have been historically excluded from equal participation in organizations (13). However, the reality is that when organizations invest in inclusive and welcoming environments, everyone benefits. Workplaces and academic settings that prioritize inclusion tend to be more collaborative, creative, and productive. By inviting different perspectives and encouraging open dialog, teams make better decisions and avoid the pitfalls of groupthink. When all voices are heard and respected, people feel more engaged, valued, and connected to the organization’s mission (13). This sense of belonging translates into better outcomes across the board, from stronger research teams to more innovative problem-solving. Inclusion benefits everyone involved, expanding opportunities and engagement for all. In short, DEI efforts increase productivity and profitability by reducing groupthink and encouraging diverse perspectives (13).

Misconception 3: DEI is a one-time effort and creates division

DEI work can be misunderstood as a one-time event or campaign. However, building inclusivity takes ongoing effort, reflection, and willingness to adapt over time (14). This could mean revisiting policies, re-evaluating leadership structures, or responding to new challenges as they arise and making consistent progress. Continuous assessment and adaptation are necessary to address emerging challenges and ensure that DEI efforts remain effective and relevant.

Additionally, some believe that DEI efforts foster division by emphasizing differences among individuals. DEI can be misunderstood as something that draws lines between people instead of building bridges. However, DEI promotes understanding and collaboration by acknowledging and valuing diverse experiences and different perspectives (14). Doing so leads to a more cohesive and innovative organizational culture along with overall improved results. Studies show that recognizing and valuing individuals’ unique backgrounds not only builds trust and mutual respect, but also fosters stronger and more connected communities (15). When people feel seen and supported, they are more likely to collaborate and contribute, which benefits everyone (15).

GOALS AND APPROACH OF THE MicroBio-LEAP PROJECT

The goal of the MicroBio-LEAP project was to design a program that expands awareness, transforms practices, builds community, and encourages microbiology leaders to engage in activities that foster awareness, eliminate discrimination, and create a welcoming environment where all can thrive and succeed within the microbial sciences. Specifically, the MicroBio-LEAP project aimed to educate, train, and inspire ASM and Association of Medical School Microbiology and Immunology Chairs (AMSMIC) leaders through a cultural transformation strategy that increased intercultural awareness to promote access and accountability across all levels of ASM and AMSMIC to ultimately create an inclusive climate where everyone can fully participate and feel they belong. The project supported scholars by providing incentives and resources to stimulate change within their circles of influence across the discipline and in a variety of settings.

The project worked with leaders by (i) promoting deep individual introspection, (ii) facilitating peer group exchanges, and (iii) providing a space where participants can practice knowledge and skills to ensure the training goes beyond awareness to action. The MicroBio-LEAP project distinguished itself from traditional knowledge and skill-building training through its community of practice approach. To provide a structure for long-term sustainability and engagement, the project centered on a Training of Leaders (ToL) model. The project promoted change through the tenets of planning, participation, adaptive management, and evaluation, which are essential to its theory of change (Fig. 2). Ultimately, the goal was sustainable culture change, which requires integrative and systematic programming efforts to create an environment where all stakeholders feel included and can thrive (16).

MicroBio-LEAP TASK FORCE

ASM held a 4-week open call for the MicroBio-LEAP Task Force from February to March of 2023. Thirty-two applicants responded to nine open-ended questions detailing their interest in the position, as well as their experience in equity and inclusion advocacy. Six Task Force members were selected after thorough review of all applicants by ASM’s PIAA Committee and staff team, and ultimately selected the Task Force members.

The MicroBio-LEAP Task Force and an external consultant helped develop and implement the ToL curriculum for the pilot project. During the implementation of the program, they revised the ToL curriculum and resources as needed to adapt the program specifically for microbiologists. Additionally, the Task Force provided iterative feedback about the modules through surveys and by debriefing after each module’s programming. Through lectures, group discussions, and coaching, the Task Force assisted the 24 ASM ToL scholars to increase their awareness and develop institutional best practices and activities that foster awareness, eliminate discrimination, create openness, and build skills within the microbial sciences.

RECRUITMENT OF SCHOLARS

ASM tested the ToL concept by piloting its curriculum with a heterogeneous group of 24 ASM members representing academia, government, and industry who hold leadership positions at ASM in governance, scholarly publishing, and conference planning. To recruit ASM members from academia who could influence institutional policies and practices, ASM partnered with AMSMIC, holding an open call from mid-September to mid-October of 2023. In the application, candidates completed multiple choice and free response questions focused on leadership, experiences within the field of microbiology, and their personal and professional goals for the ToL Scholars Program. After four rounds of rigorous review, the MicroBio-LEAP Task Force, in collaboration with members of the PIAA Committee and staff team, selected 24 scholars from 89 applicants. Scholars were selected with the intent of building a representative cohort, exemplifying the breadth across professional sectors (i.e., industry, government, academic, and non-profit), career stage, leadership experience, employment role, and experience within professional organizations and institutions. Additionally, organizational membership was taken into consideration to ensure that membership from ASM and AMSMIC organizations was represented in the cohort.

CURRICULUM AND LOGISTIC COMPONENTS

The MicroBio-LEAP project distinguished itself from traditional knowledge and skill-building training through its community of practice format, which included reflective, interactive, and collaborative peer groups. The ToL program in this design phase consisted of 17 sessions taking place approximately every other week (i.e., biweekly) over the course of 10 months. The ToL program was longer in duration than many other programs to intentionally facilitate community building. A recent meta-analysis found that lengthier trainings have a greater impact on participants, particularly when complemented by other initiatives and targeting both awareness and skill development, as was the case with MicroBio-LEAP (17). For their participation in this design-based, pilot program, the scholars received a modest stipend, distributed in four installments over the program’s duration, to compensate for the additional time needed to fully participate in the program.

The Task Force, PIAA Committee and staff team, and an external consultant collaborated to design a comprehensive, integrative, systematic, and engaging curriculum and program. The program was structured to challenge and expand participants’ intercultural awareness and skills for increasing diversity, advancing equity, and ensuring inclusion. The ToL modules covered five topics: empathic leadership, practical solutions for reducing unconscious bias and addressing barriers to participation, perspective taking, psychological safety, and inclusive leadership/achieving sustainable inclusion (Table 1). Each section topic had three sessions: (1) a consultant led the 2- to 3-hour foundational seminar for each of the five topics; (2) scholars engaged in collaborative learning pods reflecting on the topics in small groups; four check-ins were scheduled with the consultant and the small groups to provide greater clarity and answer any questions; and (3) all scholars participated in large-group reflection exercises to further discuss the topics and solicit thoughtful feedback and reflections from members of the MicroBio-LEAP Task Force.

One component of the ToL Scholars developmental process included learning pods (e.g., small reflection/processing groups). The program utilized the PathLMS online learning platform to engage in live sessions (seminars), interactive chats, and asynchronous learning, along with having the ability to share the recorded videos and resource materials. Each scholar was placed in a small group of individuals representing different programmatic areas of ASM. They worked as a team to reflect upon, process, and co-design some of their learning experiences. Dialog and small-group experiences can increase inclusion and belonging in STEM if participants intentionally engage in action-oriented conversations centered on DEI (18). These groups remained the same throughout the program to build cohesion, safety, and trust.

To fully engage and be present during the sessions, along with developing a “safe space” in the program, the ToL scholars developed “ground rules” in order to hold themselves and their colleagues accountable (Table 2).

IN-PERSON ToL SCHOLARS SYMPOSIUM

A crucial aspect of the ToL Scholars Program was the in-person MicroBio-LEAP ToL Scholars summit. This summit occurred near the midpoint of the program, and over 20 scholars gathered to engage in rich discussion, absorb in-depth knowledge around best practices, listen and learn from practitioners and champions, and collaborate on the development of their final deliverables for the program. Scholars reported that the summit inspired and energized ToL scholars to continue their work in the program and beyond. The in-person component also served to strengthen the professional bonds the scholars had developed through the virtual sessions.

FINAL DELIVERABLES OF THE SCHOLARS

At the program’s conclusion, the scholars submitted individual deliverables, some of which were completed with group collaboration. The scholars were asked to identify a DEI-related challenge impacting a “space” in the microbial/immunological sciences. This space could be a department, a laboratory, an advisory or review board (e.g., editorial boards), a task force, a committee, or other microbial/immunological sciences-related spaces. After identifying the challenge, the scholars proposed a solution to mitigate the issue and explained how this solution aligns with ASM’s values and addresses the identified challenge. Additionally, some scholars described how this solution could be scaled to be implemented in the broader microbial sciences and STEM community at large.

There were opportunities to share and work on their deliverables with their colleague scholars in the small-group collaborative learning pods and in thought partnership with the MicroBio-LEAP Task Force. These deliverables enabled scholars to demonstrate their growth and development throughout the program, develop a plan for introducing relevant policies and/or practices at the scholar’s institution or organization, and provide feedback for the continued development of the ToL Scholars Program and its curriculum for future implementation. The final deliverables were shared and reflected upon in their collaborative learning pods through short, pre-recorded videos and feedback from the MicroBio-LEAP Task Force and other scholars in the program, which continued peer-to-peer knowledge sharing and co-design. The scholar deliverables fell into two categories: (i) identifying challenges and (ii) proposing solutions.

PROGRAM EVALUATION

Formative and summative evaluation was conducted by an external evaluator throughout the implementation of this design project. Mixed methods of data collection included pre-/post-assessments (i.e., the Intercultural Development Inventory [19]), surveys, and individual interviews. These methods and corresponding findings provided the development team with current information to make changes to the curriculum while in process and were used to identify participant outcomes immediately post-participation.

All of the scholars who participated in interviews (n = 23; 96%) would recommend this program to their peers. When they applied for this program, they noted that their goals were to learn how to build inclusive and welcoming STEM environments, gain a deeper understanding of DEI, connect to others within ASM, learn about themselves, and apply new ideas into practice. Ninety-five percent of the interviewees said the program met their expectations. In particular, they noted that the five content areas were appropriate and appreciated the modules about “psychological safety” and “unconscious bias” the most. Using retrospective post-test methodology, their greatest skill gains were in practical solutions and “techniques to reduce unconscious bias” and “modeling empathic leadership.” Their greatest knowledge gains were in “understanding the concepts of diversity, equity, inclusion, and belonging ” and “types of unconscious bias.” Pre- and post-assessments using the Intercultural Development Inventory indicate one level increase in Global Mindset for 55% of the scholars, one level decrease for 10% of the scholars, and no movement for 35% of the scholars. When asked if they felt motivated to make changes and to be an agent of change moving forward, 100% of the scholars indicated they were, indicating that the primary objective of this program was met.

All of the participants felt that the programming allowed them to ask questions and learn in a safe environment. They felt that the collaborative learning pods and small group sessions were the most valuable aspects of this program. Recommendations for future iterations include providing overall learning objectives and more structure in the initial phase of the program, increasing active learning opportunities, and more time for small group sessions.

CONCLUSIONS

The ToL Scholars program achieved the goal of encouraging microbiologists to embrace inclusive and equitable leadership practices by expanding their understanding of unconscious bias, DEI principles, and psychological safety. ASM envisions that after the ToL Scholars complete their projects, they will inspire more change agents and champions when they train students, postdoctoral fellows, and other employees, thereby creating inclusive and diverse environments where all are welcome and thrive in their respective careers. Ultimately, the individual behaviors of ASM’s leaders and ASM’s practices are better aligned with an authentic equity framework, which aims to create learning communities that center on the strengths of all members of the STEM community (20).

The pedagogical approach of learning communities was also successful, as scholars reported they felt safe to engage with small learning pods. Significantly, scholars felt empowered to operate as agents of change at their institutions, which is the primary and long-term goal of ASM’s multi-pronged approach to create a cultural shift in the microbial sciences.

The Training of Scholars program comes at a critical time when microbiologists can play a role in protecting the collective well-being through advocacy and leadership. Scientific civic engagement is needed now, and microbiologists can promote equitable and just discourse of the ways that scientific research benefits all members of society. Empowering microbiologist leaders with the tools to advocate for equity and justice will strengthen their ability to implement successful scientific strategies and policies that promote health and scientific progress in society.
