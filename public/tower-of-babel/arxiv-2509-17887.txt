Coxeter-Dynkin algebras of canonical type
Daniel Perniok

Abstract
. We propose a definition of Coxeter-Dynkin algebras of canonical type generalising the definition as a path algebra of a quiver as it is given for instance in [ LdlP11 ] . Moreover, we construct two tilting objects over the squid algebra [ Rin90 ] – one via generalised APR-tilting and one via one-point-extensions and reflection functors – and identify their endomorphism algebras with the Coxeter-Dynkin algebra. This shows that our definition gives another representative in the derived equivalence class of the squid algebra, and hence of the corresponding canonical algebra. Finally, we explain how these Coxeter-Dynkin algebras illustrate the connection to Saito’s classification of marked extended affine root systems [ Sai85 ] .

It is well-known that indecomposable representations of a tame hereditary algebra split into three classes: the preprojective component, the preinjective component and the tubes (see e. g.

A recurring question in this context concerns the parametrisation of the tubes. While in general this might be an arbitrarily hard number-theoretic problem, over an algebraically closed field the answer is known. The closed points of the projective line
\mathbbm{P}^{1}_{k}
serve as a parameter set, which hints at a hidden geometry behind the tubular family, even in the general case. Over an algebraically closed field, this led to the definition of

[

]

of coherent sheaves on a weighted projective line

and the module category of a canonical algebra in the sense of
[

]

In order to develop the theory without assumptions on the base field, Ringel gave a sophisticated definition of canonical algebras generalising the previous one
[
Rin90
]
. He also defined another algebra, called

, over which he constructed a tilting module whose endomorphism algebra is a canonical algebra. Building on this, Lenzing and de la Pe

[
LdlP99
]
. Furthermore, they showed that this condition is equivalent to the existence of a certain hereditary category
\mathcal{H}
which – over an algebraically closed field – can be identified with
\Coh(\mathbb{X})
in the sense of
[
GL87
]
.

An axiomatic characterisation of these categories can be found in
[

]

over

[

In a recent work, Burban provides a geometric description of the category

by constructing – up to Morita-equivalence – a ringed space

, called

, which is the desired generalisation of Geigle-Lenzing’s weighted projective lines
[

Our goal is to look at yet another representative in the derived equivalence class of the canonical algebra and the squid algebra, the so-called
Coxeter-Dynkin algebra of canonical type

one can find a definition of Coxeter-Dynkin algebras as path algebras of quivers modulo relations, motivated by connections to singularity theory. A generalisation of this definition to all cases which can occur over non-algebraically closed fields is the first goal of this work. Moreover, we establish in two different ways that the Coxeter-Dynkin algebra

Subsequently, we apply the idea of reflection functors and one-point-extensions to canonical algebras to give a detailed proof of Ringel’s tilting theorem for the squid algebra and the canonical algebra and offer a new perspective on the definition of canonical algebras.

Finally, we will discover that Coxeter-Dynkin algebras of canonical type illustrate the connection between the theory of canonical algebras and exceptional curves on one side and geometric group theory on the other side. It turns out that Coxeter-Dynkin algebras are closely related to the diagrams that Saito used to classify extended affine root systems of codimension

if the following conditions are satisfied (see e. g.

(CT1)

has projective dimension at most 1,

(CT2)

\Ext_{A}^{1}(T,T)=0
,

(CT3)

there exists an exact sequence

0\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow A_{A}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow T_{0}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow T_{1}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow 0

with

.

Note that one can find deviating definitions in the literature. Often one requires only that

has finite projective dimension and that there is an
\add(T)
-coresolution of
{}_{A}A
of arbitrary finite length
[
BB07
]
.
Let us also recall a consequence of the definition. Defining

, it is clear that

becomes a

-bimodule and we obtain a functor

\displaystyle\Hom_{A}(T,-):\qquad\mathsf{mod-}A\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathsf{mod-}B,

which restricts to an equivalence
\add(T)\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathsf{proj-}B
of additive categories. In particular, we get an equivalence between the bounded homotopy categories and a diagram

\textstyle{\mathcal{K}^{b}(\add(T))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{\mathcal{K}^{b}(\mathsf{proj-}B)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{\mathcal{D}^{b}(\mathsf{mod-}A)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\mathcal{D}^{b}(\mathsf{mod-}B)}

where the right vertical functor is an equivalence because

has finite global dimension. Moreover, the left vertical functor is fully-faithful by condition (CT1) and (CT2) and
its essential image contains complexes of projectives by (CT3).
Hence, it is also an equivalence since

. In other words, in this situation we obtain an equivalence (of triangulated categories) between

and

and we say that

.

One approach to construct tilting modules is to start with the direct sum of the indecomposable projectives and replace one summand by a different indecomposable module. One instance of this is the following result due to Brenner and Butler which generalises the concept of

Theorem 2.1

Let

\displaystyle T=(1-e)A\oplus\tau^{-1}(S)\quad\in\mathsf{mod-}A

From now on, let

We will apply Theorem

3.
Derived equivalence via one-point-extensions

The goal of this section is to establish another perspective on the derived equivalence between the squid algebra and the Coxeter-Dynkin algebra. More precisely, we construct a different equivalence
\mathcal{D}^{b}(A)\cong\mathcal{D}^{b}(B)
which does not come from a classical tilting module as in Section
2
but from a
tilting complex
. The construction of this tilting complex makes use of reflection functors for hereditary algebras and the fact that all our algebras can be seen as one-point extensions of hereditary algebras.

Following
[
Ric89
]
we call a bounded complex of projective modules
T\in\mathcal{K}^{b}(\mathsf{proj-}A)
a
tilting complex
if the following two conditions are satisfied:

(T1)

\Hom_{\mathcal{K}^{b}(\mathsf{proj-}A)}(T,T[i])=0
for all
0\neq i\in\mathbb{Z}
and

(T2)

\add(T)
generates
\mathcal{K}^{b}(\mathsf{proj-}A)
as a triangulated category.

Sometimes we will also use this terminology to denote a bounded complex of (not necessarily projective)
A
-modules if it is quasi-isomorphic to a complex of the above form.
It is proven in
[
Ric89
, Theorem 6.4]
that the existence of such a tilting complex
T
with
\End(T)\cong B
is equivalent to
\mathcal{K}^{b}(\mathsf{proj-}A)
and
\mathcal{K}^{b}(\mathsf{proj-}B)
being equivalent as triangulated categories. More concretely, in this case there is an equivalence of triangulated categories sending the tilting complex
T
to the free module
B_{B}
viewed as a complex concentrated in degree zero.
By
[
Ric89
, Prop. 9.1]
the above statements are equivalent to their dual versions using left modules.

It turns out that for our purposes it is more convenient to work with left modules for a moment.
Let
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
be finite-dimensional algebras which are derived equivalent, i. e. we have equivalences of triangulated categories

\textstyle{\mathcal{K}^{b}(A_{0}\mathsf{-proj})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{\mathcal{K}^{b}(B_{0}\mathsf{-proj})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{\qquad\Phi_{0}:}
\textstyle{\mathcal{D}^{b}(A_{0}\mathsf{-mod})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{\mathcal{D}^{b}(B_{0}\mathsf{-mod}).}

Note that the vertical functors are in general fully faithful, and equivalences if and only if
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
are of finite global dimension. Then by Rickard’s theorem there exists a tilting complex
T_{0}\in\mathcal{K}^{b}(A_{0}\mathsf{-proj})
with
\End(T_{0})^{\mathsf{op}}\cong B_{0}
such that
\Phi_{0}(T_{0})={}_{B_{0}}(B_{0})
. The following result – proven by Barot and Lenzing in a slightly more restricted setting – shows that the derived equivalence between
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
extends to a derived equivalence between certain one-point-extension algebras. For convenience, we give a full proof even though it works analogously to the one given in
[
BL03
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
G
be a finite-dimensional division algebra and
N
an
A_{0}
-
G
-bimodule such that
\Phi_{0}(N)
is isomorphic in
\mathcal{D}^{b}(B_{0}\mathsf{-mod})
to a complex concentrated in degree zero, in other words we can identify
\Phi_{0}(N)
with a left
B_{0}
-module
N^{\prime}
. Then
N^{\prime}
becomes a
B_{0}
-
G
-bimodule with a right
G
-action induced by that on
N
. Let

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{0}&N\\
0&G\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}B_{0}&N^{\prime}\\

be the corresponding one-point-extension algebras of
A_{0}
resp.
B_{0}
and

\displaystyle e_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1_{A_{0}}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad e_{G}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\

\textstyle{G^{\mathsf{op}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{\End_{\mathcal{D}^{b}(A_{0}\mathsf{-mod})}(N[0])\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\scriptstyle{\Phi_{0}}
\textstyle{\End_{B_{0}\mathsf{-mod}}(N^{\prime})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\textstyle{\End_{\mathcal{D}^{b}(B_{0}\mathsf{-mod})}(N^{\prime}[0])}

which induces a morphism
G^{\mathsf{op}}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\End_{B_{0}\mathsf{-mod}}(N^{\prime})
and hence a

-

Recall that we have an adjoint pair

\textstyle{A_{0}\mathsf{-mod}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{\perp}

\displaystyle Ae_{0}\otimes_{A_{0}}-:\quad\mathcal{K}^{b}(A_{0}\mathsf{-proj})\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}^{b}(A\mathsf{-proj}).

\displaystyle\Hom_{\mathcal{K}^{b}(A\mathsf{-proj})}(Ae_{0}\otimes T_{0},Ae_{0}\otimes T_{0}[n])\cong\Hom_{\mathcal{K}^{b}(A_{0}\mathsf{-proj})}(T_{0},T_{0}[n])\cong\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}B_{0}^{\mathsf{op}},&\text{if }n=0,\\

\displaystyle\Hom_{\mathcal{K}^{b}(A\mathsf{-proj})}

\displaystyle(Ae_{G}[0],Ae_{0}\otimes T_{0}[n])=0\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}.

\displaystyle\Hom_{\mathcal{K}^{b}(A\mathsf{-proj})}

\displaystyle(Ae_{G}[0],Ae_{G}[n])\cong\Ext_{A}^{n}(Ae_{G},Ae_{G})\cong\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}G^{\mathsf{op}},&\text{if }n=0,\\

\add(T_{0})
generates

as a triangulated category and the functor

, it follows that
\add(Ae_{0})[0]
is contained in

Ae_{G}[0]
is a direct summand of

Definition 5.1

Remark 5.5
.

Note that we have used left modules, since this was more convenient for identifying

with a canonical bilinear lattice. By the last theorem we know that passing to right modules (in other words passing to the opposite algebra) does not change the bilinear lattice up to isomorphism. Regarding the squid algebra, it is clear that its opposite algebra is not a squid algebra, but it is derived equivalent to an ordinary squid algebra with (possibly) different input data. The same holds true for Coxeter-Dynkin algebras of canonical type. When extracting the symbol from the minimal model, the weights and the exceptional points, one has to keep in mind that (

Building on the previous calculations, we want to change our perspective to describe a link to geometric group theory. We can view finite-dimensional algebras as a source of bilinear forms, looking at the associated (symmetrised) Euler form. Given such a symmetric bilinear form, one can introduce the notion of
(generalised) root systems
and study the corresponding reflection groups (or
Weyl groups
). Since domestic canonical algebras are derived equivalent to tame hereditary algebras
[
Rin90
, §2.7]
, the Weyl groups are affine Coxeter groups. In the case
\delta=0
, the symmetrised Euler form is still positive semidefinite, but has a
2
-dimensional radical. The associated Weyl groups belong to a different interesting class of reflection groups, the so-called
extended affine Weyl group
(or
elliptic Weyl group
). In particular, Saito studied
extended affine root systems
and classified them via Dynkin diagrams. The Dynkin diagrams that show up for the extended affine root systems of codimension
1

[
Sai85
, Table 1]
look very similar to the underlying weighted graphs (
1.6
) of tubular Coxeter-Dynkin algebras, with dotted edges in Saito’s Dynkin diagrams indicating relations in the algebra. Comparing
[
Sai85
, §8.2]
with Lemma
5.6
, this connection becomes even clearer, namely the basis consisting of the classes of simple
B
-modules corresponds to the basis of simple roots associated to the vertices of Saito’s Dynkin diagrams.

Concerning the representation type, we recall that a finite-dimensional algebra is called
tame concealed
if it arises as the endomorphism algebra of a preprojective tilting module over a tame hereditary algebra
[
Rin84
, §4.3]
. Canonical algebras of domestic type are tame concealed by
[
Rin90
, §2.7]
. We note that domestic squid algebras are not tame concealed since a corresponding tilting module over a tame hereditary algebra will always contain a direct summand which is not preprojective but lies in a tube. A Coxeter-Dynkin algebra
B
is clearly wild whenever
\delta>0
since it is a one-point-extension of a wild hereditary algebra
B_{0}
. Moreover, it must be tame for
\delta=0
by the derived equivalences that we established. In the
domestic case we expect the Coxeter-Dynkin algebras to be representation-finite. It is already known that there is a representation-finite algebra in the derived equivalence class of every tame hereditary algebra except those of type
\tilde{A}_{p,1}
(meaning type
\tilde{A}_{p}
with an orientation such that there is a unique sink and source at adjacent vertices). Namely, this follows from
[
Rin84
, §4.2(8)]
where Ringel proved that the endomorphism algebra of a tilting module
T
over a tame hereditary algebra is representation-finite, if
T
admits both preprojective and preinjective direct summands. However, the statement for domestic Coxeter-Dynkin algebras of canonical type does not seem to be obvious, since we do not have an explicit description for the tilting from a tame hereditary algebra to the Coxeter-Dynkin algebra.
