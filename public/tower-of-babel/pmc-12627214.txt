Microbiota-responsive oral nano-amifostine enables colorectal-specific radioprotection and tumor immunity via gut microenvironment reprogramming
Authors: Kaichao Song, Ling Ren, Xiuping Guo, Gang Ren, Yanan Sun, XiaoLian Tian, Chujuan Hu, Mingyu Pan, Quanyong Yu, Zhigang Luo, Yumei Hao, Peng Lei, Haoyang Yu, Sitong Yang, Qingbo Chen, Yuanbin Li, Shan Wang, Yingying He, Zhouguang Hui, Wensheng Zheng, Jiandong Jiang, Lulu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Radiotherapy for abdominal malignancies is limited by intestinal toxicity and secondary colorectal cancers. Here, we develop an oral, microbiota-responsive nano-amifostine (CS/PEC-AMF NPs) system that achieves site-specific radioprotection in the colorectum without compromising antitumor efficacy. By conjugating amifostine to pectin and encapsulating it with chitosan, the nanoparticles enable pH- and microbiota-triggered release in the large bowel, safeguarding drug bioactivity during gastrointestinal transit. In murine models, CS/PEC-AMF NPs attenuate both acute and chronic radiation-induced bowel injury, restore epithelial integrity, preserve stem cell populations, and promote tight junction repair. Integrated metagenomic and metabolomic analyses reveal that the system normalizes gut microbiota diversity and composition, increases short-chain fatty acid production, and facilitates macrophage polarization towards the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. Notably, the formulation synergistically enhances tumor suppression and extends survival in orthotopic colorectal tumor models undergoing radiotherapy and reduces the incidence of secondary colorectal tumors post-irradiation. Mechanistically, transcriptomic analysis demonstrates the suppression of proinflammatory pathways and the promotion of DNA repair programs. This study provides a paradigm for leveraging functional nanomaterials to orchestrate precise, tissue-specific radioprotection and immune modulation, addressing a key challenge in abdominal cancer therapy.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s42114-025-01492-x.

Introduction

Radiotherapy is a cornerstone in the treatment of various abdominal tumors, with more than 50% of patients with abdominal cancer worldwide undergoing radiation therapy[1, 2]. Despite significant advancements in imaging and treatment technologies that have improved the precision and accuracy of radiation delivery[3], radiotherapy-induced toxicity to intestinal tissues remains a significant concern. This toxicity not only compromises the efficacy of cancer treatment but also negatively impacts the quality of life for patients[1, 4]. Of particular concern are secondary malignancies, especially in the colon and rectum, which pose a growing risk for long-term survivors of abdominal radiotherapy. A 29-year follow-up study reported an increased incidence of colon cancer in patients with a history of abdominal radiation compared to the general population[5]. A meta-analysis of 16reports similarly indicated that radiotherapy increased the risk of subsequent rectal cancer in patients treated for prostate cancer[6]. In a study of 23,112 cervical cancer patients, second malignancies were diagnosed in 2545 (11.0%) cases, with a significantly higher risk of developing cancers in the colon, rectum, and anus[7]. Contributing factors to this phenomenon may include low cellular turnover, larger surface areas, exposure to environmental factors, genetic and molecular variations, and pre-existing inflammatory bowel conditions. These findings underscore the urgent need to develop strategies that specifically protect the large intestine from radiation-induced injuries.

The intestinal barrier, a critical component of gastrointestinal health, is key in maintaining overall digestive function. Radiation therapy can directly damage the intestinal epithelium, the inner lining of this barrier, leading to DNA breakage and cell death, disrupting mucus production and tight junction integrity. As a result, essential processes such as digestion, nutrient absorption, and defense against harmful agents are impaired. Furthermore, radiation exposure affects intestinal immune cells—including lymphocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells—diminishing their capacity to monitor the intestinal environment, detect and eliminate pathogens, and regulate immune responses. The gut microbiota, an essential component of the intestinal environment, undergoes significant alterations along the gastrointestinal tract. In the large intestine, bacterial populations reach trillions and include at least 1000 distinct species. Abdominal radiotherapy profoundly disrupts this microbial ecosystem, leading to changes in the composition and function of gut bacteria. Imbalances in the gut microbiota have been linked to increased susceptibility to infections, gastrointestinal disorders, inflammation, and impaired immune responses[8]. Effective management of radiotherapy-induced toxicity depends on addressing the interdependence of these crucial components, each of which plays a unique and indispensable role in preserving intestinal health during treatment.

Traditional radioprotective strategies primarily focus on restoring intestinal epithelial integrity but face persistent challenges related to the lack of specific protection for the colorectal region, disruption of gut commensal microbiota, and dysregulated mucosal immune responses. This study aims to develop an effective system to safeguard the microenvironment of the large bowel. Amifostine (Ethyol or WR-2721, AMF) is a radioprotective agent designed to shield healthy cells from the toxic effects of radiotherapy. Its selective action leverages the metabolic differences between normal and cancer cells (Fig. Sup 1A). Consistent with its relationship to alkaline phosphatase levels (Fig. Sup 1B, C), AMF is shown not only to protect colonic epithelial cells (Fig. Sup 2) but also to preserve intestinal immune cells and gut microbiota without reducing the cytotoxic effects of radiotherapy on tumor cells (Fig. Sup 3). In 1995, the FDA approved AMF for clinical use in reducing side effects in patients with head and neck cancer undergoing postoperative radiation therapy[9]. Since then, AMF has been investigated for its potential to protect other normal tissues, with clinical studies demonstrating its effectiveness in reducing radiation-induced gastrointestinal toxicities through intravenous injection[10, 11]. However, the drug’s use is limited by its administration route and potential side effects. Oral AMF undergoes extensive metabolism in the upper intestines, leading to low bioavailability at the target site. By contrast, intravenous administration at high doses can cause adverse effects such as nausea, vomiting, flushing, skin reactions, hypotension, injection site reactions, altered taste, and allergic responses. Intra-colorectal administration has been explored as an alternative delivery method to prevent radiation-induced bowel injury, with numerous studies confirming its feasibility, tolerance, and radioprotective efficacy, with minimal systemic toxicity[12, 13].. Nevertheless, oral administration remains more favorable in clinical practice due to patient compliance. While efforts have been made to develop oral formulations of AMF[14–16], there remains a lack of formulations specifically targeting the colorectum to prevent radiation enteropathy. Developing an orally administered, colorectal-targeted AMF formulation holds substantial clinical significance for mitigating radiation-induced bowel injuries.

Prebiotics, commonly found in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, are known for their ability to modulate gut microbiota. These non-digestible dietary supplements selectively stimulate the growth and activity of beneficial intestinal bacteria, offering protective effects against radiation-induced damage[17]. Pectin, a complex polysaccharide in plant cell walls[18], has been reported to protect the gastrointestinal barrier by promoting the growth of beneficial microbiota and stimulating the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs). Additionally, it enhances the intestinal immune barrier by interacting with immune receptors[19]. Pectin’s resistance to enzymatic digestion in the stomach and small intestine and its specific degradation by colonic microbiota[20, 21] make it an ideal material for colon-targeted drug delivery systems. Chitosan, a natural polysaccharide derived from chitin, offers antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and wound-healing properties[22]. In our previous work, pectin-chitosan nanoparticles were developed to enhance the modulatory effects of berberine on gut flora. These nanoparticles enabled microbiota-specific release of berberine in response to pH and enzymatic conditions in the colon[23]. In the present study, a pectin-amifostine hybrid (PEC-AMF) was synthesized to address the sensitivity of AMF to the upper intestines. In addition, chitosan-encapsulated pectin-amifostine nanoparticles (CS/PEC-AMF NPs) were designed to achieve precise protection against colorectal injury during radiotherapy.

In summary, this study utilized natural materials—specifically prebiotics and chitosan—as functional carriers to formulate an orally administered nanomedicine. This formulation targets the colorectal region, restoring the intestinal microenvironment, including the epithelium, immune system, and microbiota. As a result, it alleviates bowel injuries and reduces the risk of secondary malignancies in patients undergoing abdominal radiotherapy for primary cancers. The proposed hypothesis was tested through experiments, revealing the underlying mechanisms.

Results

Preparation and characterization of CS/PEC-AMF NPs

The PEC-AMF conjugate was synthesized via amidation, involving the reaction between the carboxylic acid group of pectin and the amino group of AMF (Fig. 1A, Fig. Sup 4A). The 1H NMR spectrum confirmed the presence of characteristic peaks for both AMF and pectin and indicated the formation of a new amide bond (Fig. 1B, Fig. Sup 4B). Additional confirmation of PEC-AMF synthesis was provided by FT-IR spectra and DSC analysis (Fig. Sup 4C, D). The degree of substitution (DS) for PEC-AMF was determined to be 23.4%, indicating 0.234 mol of AMF per mol of pectin monomer (Fig. Sup 5A). TEM analysis revealed that chitosan was essential for aggregating PEC-AMF into spherical structures, facilitating AMF transport and protection within the gastrointestinal tract (Fig. Sup 5B). The screening and optimization process for CS/PEC-AMF NPs is presented in Table Sup 6–8 and Fig. Sup 6. The resulting CS/PEC-AMF NPs had an average size of 297 ± 15.3 nm, with a PDI of 0.154 ± 0.03 and a zeta potential of −8.54 ± 1.13 mV (Fig. 1C,). TEM images revealed that the NPs were spherical and uniform (Fig. 1E). The NPs exhibited a measured drug loading capacity of 13.7 ± 1.7% and stability in distilled water for up to 1 week (Fig. 1F). The pH- and enzyme-responsive properties of CS/PEC-AMF NPs are critical for effective delivery to the colonic site (Fig. 1G). Our formulation logic aligns with reports that plant-derived saponins self-assemble into micellar nanoparticles with pH-dependent release and favorable biocompatibility, supporting oral, microenvironment-responsive delivery[26]. Stability testing in simulated intestinal fluids (pH 6.8) over a 6-h period confirmed that the nanoparticles remained intact (Fig. 1 H and I). In contrast, upon exposure to simulated colonic fluid (pH 7.4, 4.5% w/v caecal contents), the nanoparticles rapidly disintegrated, indicating that the NPs release AMF specifically in response to the colonic environment.

A fluorescent molecule, Cy5, was conjugated to PEC to assess drug distribution and absorption, yielding CS/PEC-Cy5 NPs, which exhibited similar size and zeta potential to CS/PEC-AMF NPs (Fig. Sup 7). After oral administration, these formulations followed the typical gastrointestinal transit: through the stomach (pH 1.5–2.5, 1–2 h), small intestine (pH 5.5–6.8, 3–6 h), and finally reaching the large intestine (pH 6.4–7.8, microbiota-rich, 12–24 h) (Fig. 1J)[27]. The release and absorption profile of AMF or Cy5-containing formulations was then examined (Fig. 1K, Fig. Sup 8). Formulations were placed in dialysis tubes, which were subsequently immersed in artificial gastric fluid for 2 h, artificial intestinal fluid for 3 h, and artificial colonic fluid with 4.5% w/v mouse caecal contents for 6 h. The results indicated that free AMF was primarily released in simulated gastric fluid, with minimal release in simulated colonic fluid. PEC-AMF and a mixture of CS, PEC, and AMF (CS/PEC/AMF) primarily released their drug content in simulated intestinal fluid, with only trace amounts reaching the colon. In contrast, CS/PEC-AMF NPs exhibited no release in simulated gastric fluid but released a significant amount of AMF upon reaching simulated colonic fluid (Fig. Sup 9). This demonstrates that the nanoparticles are capable of pH- and microbiota-responsive release, making them well suited for colon-specific drug delivery. Subsequent experiments demonstrated that CS/PEC-Cy5 NPs, following colon-targeted drug release, were effectively taken up by FHC cells (Fig. 1L, M). Moreover, the active form of AMF (1065, m/z 135.0945) was detected in FHC cells co-cultured with colonic fluid from the CS/PEC-AMF NP group, establishing the basis for the selective protective effect of the drug (Fig. 1 N and O).

Evaluation of radiation protection of CS/PEC-AMF NPs in vitro

To simulate in vivo conditions more accurately, FHC cells were co-incubated with artificial colonic fluid (as prepared in the final dissolution step mentioned above) for 2 h before undergoing X-ray exposure. An X-ray dose of 3 Gy was selected to induce reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and cause cell damage (Fig. Sup 10B). As shown in Fig. 2A, B and Fig. Sup 11A, irradiation significantly increased the ratio of apoptotic and dead cells. However, treatment with CS/PEC-AMF NPs substantially improved cell viability. These findings were further confirmed by Calcein-AM/PI staining for cell death (Fig. Sup 11B) and γ-H2AX staining for DNA double-strand breaks (Fig. 2C). The assessment of intracellular ROS levels across different treatment groups aligned with these observations (Fig. Sup 11C). Furthermore, the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), interleukin 1β (IL-1β), and interleukin 6 (IL-6), were markedly elevated in the irradiated group compared to the control (Fig. 2D). CS/PEC-AMF NP treatment significantly suppressed ROS production and inflammatory cytokine expression (Fig. 2E, F). Although CS/PEC/AMF also exhibited protective effects, the difference was insignificant. Notably, CS/PEC-AMF NPs protected both immune cells (Figs. 2G–−2J, Fig. Sup 12) and gut microbiota (Fig. 2 K and L). In vivo bio-distribution of CS/PEC-AMF NPs

In vivo fluorescence imaging following intragastric administration (Fig. 3 A and B) demonstrated higher Cy5 intensity in the abdominal region of mice treated with CS/PEC-Cy5 NPs compared to the Cy5 group. Notably, imaging of the dissected gastrointestinal (GI) tract (Fig. 3 C and D, Fig. Sup 13) revealed that fluorescence intensity in the Cy5 and PEC-Cy5 groups was predominantly localized in the upper GI tract, with minimal distribution in the colorectal region. In contrast, the CS/PEC-Cy5 NP group exhibited enhanced and concentrated fluorescence signals, specifically in the colorectal area. Tissue fluorescence analysis (Fig. 3E) further confirmed that the fluorescence intensity in the CS/PEC-Cy5 NP group was significantly higher than in the free Cy5 and PEC-Cy5 groups, likely due to the improved stability conferred by the chitosan coating. Air flow–assisted desorption electrospray ionization mass spectrometry imaging (AFADESI-MSI) was employed to assess whole GI tract samples, achieving better visualization of the bio-distribution and higher targeting efficacy of CS/PEC-AMF NPs. This novel technique provides in situ spatial distribution information for active compounds across the entire GI tract (Video 1). A representative mass spectrum of 1065 (m/z 135.0945), the active form of AMF detected by AFADESI-MSI in positive ion mode, is shown in Fig. 3 F and G. As illustrated in Fig. 3 H and I, AMF was almost undetectable in the colorectal region at different time points in the AMF group, likely due to significant degradation in the digestive tract. In contrast, the CS/PEC-AMF NP group exhibited high levels of 1065 in the colorectum at 6 h post-administration. This can be attributed to the protective role of pectin in the upper GI tract, which shields AMF from degradation and facilitates its release in the colorectum after enzyme-mediated degradation by gut microbiota. Moreover, the chitosan encapsulation further enhanced stability and minimized premature AMF release before reaching the colorectum, explaining the stronger 1065 signal in the CS/PEC-AMF NP group compared to the PEC-AMF group. This observation was further corroborated by AMSI analysis (Fig. 3J). In summary, the CS/PEC-AMF NPs developed in this study protect AMF as it transits through the upper GI tract while facilitating its accumulation in the colorectum, achieving sufficient drug levels to exert a radioprotective effect. Given their superior in vitro protective effects and higher drug accumulation in the large bowel, CS/PEC-AMF NPs were selected for subsequent in vivo evaluations.

CS/PEC-AMF NPs alleviate acute radiation-induced bowel injury

The radioprotective effects of CS/PEC-AMF NPs against acute intestinal injury were evaluated in healthy C57BL/6 mice 3 days after irradiation. As outlined in Fig. 4A and Fig. Sup 14, different formulations were administered orally (at an AMF dose of 200 mg/kg) 6 h before radiation exposure. Colorectal tissue was collected for further analysis following the sacrifice of the mice. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed severe intestinal damage in the X-ray group, characterized by crypt destruction, necrotic epithelial cells, and an enlarged gap between the submucosa and muscle layer (Fig. 4B). Treatment with AMF showed slight improvement, though epithelial cell shedding was still evident. Both CS/PEC-AMF NPs and the CS/PEC/AMF mixture alleviated radiation-induced bowel damage. The colon structure in the CS/PEC-AMF NP–treated group closely resembled that of the control group, with no apparent pathological injury. Radiation disrupts the intestinal epithelium, including stem cells, secretory cells, and enterocytes[28]. As depicted in Figs. 4E–-H, radiation caused a significant reduction in Lgr5+ intestinal stem cells (ISCs), which was effectively reversed by CS/PEC-AMF NPs, indicating strong protection of ISCs. Furthermore, CS/PEC-AMF NPs significantly counteracted the loss of goblet cells (marked by Mucin 2, Fig. 4 E and I) and Paneth cells (marked by lysozyme, Fig. 4E and J) induced by radiation. In addition to reducing ROS content (Fig. Sup 15A, B), CS/PEC-AMF NPs improved the proportion of live cells (Fig. 4K-–M, Fig. Sup 15C-E), enhanced tight junction integrity (Fig. 4N, O and Fig. Sup 16), and protected gut microbiota (Fig. 4P). Notably, the protective effect on intestinal integrity was more pronounced in the CS/PEC-AMF NP group compared to the AMF and CS/PEC/AMF treatments.

These findings suggest that CS/PEC-AMF NPs effectively prevent radiation-induced intestinal cell damage, promote the proliferation and differentiation of ISCs, maintain tight junction structures, and facilitate intestinal barrier restoration. The enhanced protection may be attributed to the synergistic effects of AMF, prebiotics, and chitosan.

CS/PEC-AMF NPs modulate gut microbiota and immunity

Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of gut microbiota in maintaining intestinal health. This study employed high-throughput 16S rRNA gene sequencing to investigate the protective effects of CS/PEC-AMF NPs on the intestinal microbiota. Alpha diversity analysis revealed a significant reduction in microbial abundance and diversity following radiation exposure (Fig. 5A, Fig. Sup 17). Treatment with CS/PEC-AMF NPs significantly restored the alpha diversity index compared to the irradiated group. Furthermore, principal component analysis (PCA) of beta diversity demonstrated that radiation induced substantial shifts in the intestinal microbiota composition compared with the control group (Fig. 5B (-a), Fig. Sup 17). The microbiota composition in the CS/PEC-AMF NP–treated group closely resembled that of the control group. This observation was further corroborated by non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) (Fig. 5B (-b)) and principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) (Fig. 5B (-c)), confirming that CS/PEC-AMF NPs effectively restored intestinal flora composition. Subsequent analysis of microbial composition at different taxonomic levels revealed further insights (Fig. 5C, Fig. Sup 18). At the phylum level (Fig. 5C (-a)), radiation caused an increase in Firmicutes and decreased Bacteroidota, which were normalized following treatment with CS/PEC-AMF NPs. Additionally, CS/PEC-AMF NPs increased the abundance of Verrucomicrobiota, a microorganism involved in food digestion, intestinal health maintenance, immune regulation, and protection against harmful microorganisms[29]. At other taxonomic levels (Figs. 5D–-G), the abundance of Akkermansiaceae, Murinus, and Turicibacter was significantly elevated post-radiation. The rise in Akkermansiaceae, a mucus-degrading bacterium, is likely associated with radiation-induced intestinal damage[30]. Turicibacter has been linked to intestinal diseases[31], while Murinus is known to suppress anti-tumor immunity through tryptophan metabolism[32]. However, treatment with CS/PEC-AMF NPs significantly reduced the levels of these potential pathogenic microorganisms. In addition, CS/PEC-AMF NPs markedly enhanced the relative abundance of beneficial bacteria, such as Muribaculaceae and Alistipes, demonstrating a more pronounced regulatory effect on gut microbiota than AMF alone. Similar patterns were observed at the class, order, and family levels (Figs. 5E–-G). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was performed to identify key bacterial species exhibiting differential abundance between the groups. Targeted metabolite analysis was conducted to quantify the levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) across different groups. As shown in Fig. 5 I and J, the concentrations of six SCFAs in the X-ray group were significantly lower than those in the control group. Treatment with CS/PEC-AMF NPs effectively restored SCFAs levels, particularly propanoic acid and butyric acid, while AMF treatment and the blank carrier exhibited no significant impact on SCFAs content.

Radiation-induced excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) damage the intestinal mucosal barrier, triggering the recruitment of inflammatory cells into the lamina propria and promoting inflammation. Macrophages can polarize into two types—M1 and M2—depending on inflammatory stimuli. M1 macrophages are pro-inflammatory, releasing various inflammatory factors that exacerbate tissue damage. As shown in Fig. 5K, radiation led to a marked accumulation of M1 macrophages in the colonic tissue. However, treatment with CS/PEC-AMF NPs significantly reduced the number of M1 macrophages while increasing the proportion of M2 macrophages. The enhanced M2/M1 ratio indicates that CS/PEC-AMF NPs promote the polarization of macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 type to the anti-inflammatory M2 type, thereby supporting tissue repair and mitigating inflammation (Fig. 5K and Fig. Sup 20). Additionally, CS/PEC-AMF NPs significantly reduced the infiltration of monocytes and neutrophils while enhancing dendritic cell populations (Fig. Sup 21). Radiation notably increased the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 in colonic tissue. AMF treatment alone did not significantly alleviate the elevated cytokine levels. In contrast, both the CS/PEC-AMF NPs and carrier groups showed substantial reductions in the expression of these inflammatory cytokines, with CS/PEC-AMF NPs demonstrating a more pronounced effect (Fig. 5 L and M). This is likely due to the capacity of pectin to downregulate the expression of cytokines such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 in the colon, thereby reducing inflammation and enhancing the immune barrier of the intestinal mucosa.

CS/PEC-AMF NPs mitigate radiation-induced chronic bowel injury

CS/PEC-AMF NPs significantly alleviate radiation-induced colonic atrophy, with the mean colonic length recovering from 6.30 ± 0.16 cm to 8.13 ± 0.70 cm (Fig. 6A, B). Apoptosis of intestinal cells was reduced following exposure to CS/PEC-AMF NPs (Fig. 6C, D, Fig. Sup 22). Compared to the control group, the radiation group exhibited a marked increase in ROS levels within colon tissues. Treatment with CS/PEC-AMF NPs notably decreased oxidative stress levels (Fig. 6E–-G). The CS/PEC/AMF treatment group displayed only a minimal reduction in ROS, which was not statistically significant. As illustrated in Fig. 6H, X-ray radiation significantly decreased the number of goblet cells in colon tissue and resulted in noticeable thinning of the mucus layer compared to the control group. Although the AMF group demonstrated a slight increase in mucus layer thickness, the number of goblet cells remained low. By contrast, the CS/PEC-AMF NP group showed a significantly higher density of goblet cells, with mucus layer thickness restored to levels comparable to those of the control group. Immunofluorescence staining of tight junction proteins in colon tissue sections (Fig. 6I) revealed significantly lower expression levels of these proteins in the radiation group compared to the control group. AMF treatment exhibited slight improvement in Occludin expression but did not significantly increase ZO-1 levels. By contrast, both the CS/PEC-AMF NPs and CS/PEC/AMF groups exhibited a significant enhancement in the expression of Occludin and ZO-1, with the CS/PEC-AMF NP group demonstrating superior improvements. These findings were further supported by q-PCR (Fig. 6J) and Western blot analysis (Fig. 6K, Fig. Sup 23). Subsequently, Masson’s staining was employed to assess collagen deposition in colon tissues across various treatment groups. As shown in Fig. 6L, the control group exhibited negligible collagen presence, whereas the radiation group displayed substantial collagen deposition in the submucosal layer, indicative of fibrosis. Treatment with CS/PEC-AMF NPs significantly reduced collagen content, while the AMF group showed no marked improvement. The CS/PEC/AMF group demonstrated a moderate reduction in collagen deposition, likely attributable to the properties of pectin and chitosan in mitigating radiation-induced late-stage enteric fibrotic changes. Consistent results were observed with immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 6M), qPCR (Fig. 6N), and Western blot analysis (Fig. 6K, Fig. Sup 23). The levels of various inflammatory factors in colon tissues were evaluated post-radiation. q-PCR analysis results (Fig. 6O) indicated that, compared to the control group, levels of inflammatory factors, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, remained elevated in the colon tissues of mice after radiation exposure. All treatment groups exhibited varying degrees of reduction in tissue inflammatory factors, with the CS/PEC-AMF NP group showing the most significant decrease, as evidenced by a notable reduction in fluorescence intensity corresponding to different inflammatory factors. Effects of CS/PEC-AMF NPs on the radiotherapy of orthotropic colorectal cancer

CS/PEC-AMF NPs protect against radiation toxicity in an orthotropic colorectal mouse model without compromising tumor treatment. The effects of CS/PEC-AMF NPs on tumor radiotherapy were further evaluated. Male Nod-SCID mice with orthotropic colon tumors were treated with CS/PEC-AMF NPs via gavage and subjected to 12-Gy whole-abdomen irradiation (WAI) (as illustrated in Fig. 7A, Fig. Sup 24, and Video 2). Comparison groups included X-ray + PBS and X-ray + AMF and groups that did not undergo radiotherapy. As shown in Fig. 7 B and C, all three irradiated groups exhibited significant inhibition of tumor growth compared to their non-irradiated counterparts, highlighting the critical anti-cancer effects of radiotherapy. However, CS/PEC-AMF supplementation mitigated radiation-induced colorectal inflammatory hyperplasia (Fig. 7 C and D). In addition, body weight and survival rates were significantly reduced in the irradiated groups treated with PBS and AMF compared to the non-irradiated group (Fig. 7E and F), consistent with previous research[33]. Conversely, treatment with CS/PEC-AMF NPs effectively improved both body weight recovery and survival rates in irradiated mice. Interestingly, pulmonary metastasis was observed in the untreated group but diminished in the CS/PEC-AMF NP group (Fig. 7G, Fig. Sup 25). Similar results were obtained in treating orthotropic rectal tumors (Figs. 7H–N, Fig. Sup 26, Video 3). As depicted in Fig. 7 I, J and M, N, CS/PEC-AMF NPs mitigated radiation-induced intestinal shortening without compromising the rectal tumor-suppressive effect. Furthermore, body weight recovery and survival rates improved in orthotropic rectal mice post-irradiation with CS/PEC-AMF NPs (Fig. 7K, L). Notably, the protective effect of CS/PEC-AMF NPs was markedly superior to that of AMF, aligning with results observed in healthy mice. These findings demonstrate that CS/PEC-AMF NPs can protect the normal intestine and enhance quality of life without compromising the therapeutic efficacy against colorectal tumors. Following WAI, which simulates treatment for primary abdominal tumors, mice were intraperitoneally injected with AOM (10 μg/g) and subsequently provided drinking water supplemented with 1% DSS, which accelerates the onset of secondary colorectal cancer[34]. To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms that CS/PEC-AMF NPs protect against colorectal damage, mRNA sequencing of colorectal tissues from the mice was conducted. The resulting volcano plots visually demonstrated that CS/PEC-AMF NPs significantly reversed radiation-induced gene expression changes. Using the criteria of |fold change|≥ 2 and P-value ≤ 0.05, 691 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between the control and X-ray groups, comprising 168 upregulated and 106 downregulated genes. Conversely, only 27 DEGs were found between the control and CS/PEC-AMF NP groups (Fig. 8G, Fig. Sup 27). Heatmap analysis further revealed that treatment with CS/PEC-AMF NPs effectively reversed radiation-induced gene expression changes and promoted the restoration of homeostasis (Fig. 8H).

Subsequently, KEGG pathway and GO enrichment analyses were performed to better understand the functions of the identified DEGs. GO enrichment analysis indicated that the DEGs between the control and radiation groups were primarily involved in biological processes such as the inflammatory response, immune response, and neutrophil chemotaxis (Fig. 8I). Furthermore, signaling pathways, including IL-17, TNF, NF-κB, and NOD-like receptor interactions, were also enriched (Fig. 8J). However, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that IL-17, NF-κB, Toll-like receptor, and NOD-like receptor signaling pathways were not enriched in the CS/PEC-AMF NP group. In contrast, signaling pathways associated with DNA replication and repair were significantly enriched (Fig. Sup 28). Notably, the base-excision repair (BER), mismatch repair (MMR), and nucleotide-excision repair (NER) pathways were enriched in the CS/PEC-AMF NP group (Fig. 8K). These pathways have been reported to be involved in DNA damage repair[35]. Further analysis indicated that CS/PEC-AMF NP intervention substantially decreased ROS levels (Fig. 8 L and M) and alleviated pro-inflammatory lymphocytes while increasing the populations of dendritic cells (DCs) and M2 macrophages (Figs. 8N–Q). Moreover, the elevated expression of pro-inflammatory factors in the colonic tissues and circulation of the untreated and X-ray groups was ameliorated by CS/PEC-AMF NP treatment (Fig. Sup 29).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates the efficacy of chitosan-encapsulated pectin-amifostine (CS/PEC-AMF) nanoparticles in mitigating radiation-induced bowel injury while preserving the therapeutic efficacy of radiotherapy against colorectal tumors[36, 37]. A key aspect of this approach is the combination of natural materials, which facilitates targeted drug delivery and contributes to restoring gut microbiota and immune homeostasis.

Radiation therapy plays a pivotal role in the treatment of malignant abdominal pelvic tumors; however, it often inflicts collateral damage on normal tissues. The incidence of secondary colon and rectal malignancies is a growing concern for long-term survivors who have undergone abdominal radiation for primary malignancies. Thus, there is an urgent need to develop more effective radioprotective strategies to safeguard the large bowel and enhance radiotherapy outcomes.

Conventional radioprotective strategies primarily focus on restoring epithelial integrity. For instance, crypt cell apoptosis has long been utilized to measure intestinal radiation toxicity[38]. However, gut flora and immune cells play integral roles in the bowel microenvironment, contributing to both the initiation and healing processes of bowel damage. It is increasingly evident that radiation-induced bowel injury arises from a complex interplay of epithelial injury, alterations in the enteric immune system, and changes in the gut microbiome[39].

Amifostine (Ethyol or WR-2721) is a radioprotective agent specifically designed to shield healthy cells from the toxic effects of radiation therapy. Its mechanism of action is related to the differences in cellular metabolism between normal and cancer cells. Healthy cells possess higher levels of alkaline phosphatase compared to cancer cells. This enzyme converts amifostine into its active form, WR-1065, which acts as a free radical scavenger and antioxidant, protecting cells from the damaging effects of radiation-generated reactive oxygen species. The preferential conversion of amifostine in healthy cells offers them greater protection against radiation-induced damage (Supplementary Information and Fig. Sup 1A-C). This study first demonstrated that amifostine protects colorectal epithelial cells and safeguards intestinal immune cells and gut bacteria without diminishing the cytotoxicity of radiotherapy on cancer cells, as indicated by alkaline phosphatase levels (Fig. Sup 2,3). Currently, amifostine is one of the few approved selective radioprotectors for clinical use; however, its application may be limited by factors such as a lack of colon specificity and potential side effects. Therefore, developing an orally targeted colorectal formulation of amifostine is clinically significant for preventing radiation-induced bowel injuries. To specifically target the colon environment and protect amifostine, this study synthesized a pectin-amifostine conjugate through amidation reactions, followed by the preparation of CS/PEC-AMF NPs via electrostatic interactions. Morphological and stability assays demonstrated that chitosan is necessary for aggregating pectin-amifostine into spherical shapes conducive to targeted delivery and protection of AMF.

To further understand the biodistribution and targeting efficacy of CS/PEC-AMF NPs in the gastrointestinal tract, a novel patent-pending method—AFADESI-MSI—was employed with whole gastrointestinal imaging (Video 1). This method provides in situ spatial distribution information on active reagents and their metabolites throughout the gastrointestinal tract. The results indicate that CS/PEC-AMF NPs facilitate pH/microbiota-responsive drug release in the large intestine, thereby achieving specific protective capabilities in this region. Consequently, levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), a primary cause of radiation-induced tissue damage, were significantly reduced. This reduction in ROS levels correlated with decreased cell death, thereby protecting the intestinal lining from acute and chronic radiation injury.

The gut microbiota plays a vital role in maintaining intestinal health and function through competitive exclusion, enhancement of barrier function, immunomodulation, and metabolic regulation. Abdominal radiotherapy has detrimental effects on gut bacteria, significantly altering the composition and function of the gut microbiota. Disruption of the delicate balance within the gut microbiota has been linked to various complications, including increased susceptibility to infections, gastrointestinal disorders, inflammation, and compromised immune function. A clinical study revealed significant remodeling of the microbial community structure in the gut following pelvic radiotherapy[40]. Individuals who developed radiation enteritis exhibited a marked decrease in alpha diversity within their gut flora, with specific bacterial changes closely associated with this condition[41, 42]. This dysbiosis of the gut microbiota can further affect the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which play a critical role in radiation-associated bowel injuries by regulating immune homeostasis[43, 44]. Modulating the gut microbiota presents a potential strategy for mitigating radiation toxicities. Some studies have explored fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) as a therapeutic approach to restoring gut microbiota composition following radiotherapy. Results from these investigations have demonstrated that FMT effectively alleviates radiation-induced gastrointestinal toxicities and enhances the survival rates of mice in irradiation models[45, 46]. Prebiotics, which are non-digestible fibers or compounds found in natural foods, promote the growth and activity of beneficial microorganisms. Pectin, a complex linear polysaccharide derived from plant cell walls, serves as a prebiotic. It can protect the gut from damage in a microbiota-dependent manner by enhancing the abundance of commensal microbiota and stimulating SCFA production. Additionally, pectin directly strengthens intestinal immune barrier functions through interactions with immune receptors[47]. Research by Yang et al. demonstrated that pectin could prevent radiation-induced ileal fibrosis and reduce epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition mediated by altered SCFA concentrations.[48] Furthermore, Sureban et al. demonstrated that pectin protects against high-dose radiation-induced deletion of intestinal stem cells and promotes crypt regeneration, thereby improving overall survival in mice.[43] Chitosan, a natural polysaccharide derived from chitin, possesses properties such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and wound healing effects.

An essential aspect of this approach is the combination of pectin and chitosan, which facilitates targeted delivery and contributes to restoring gut microbiota and immune homeostasis. The prebiotic nature of pectin supports beneficial gut bacteria, often disrupted by radiation therapy. Chitosan, a natural biopolymer, complements this effect by stabilizing the microbiota and enhancing immune responses. Analysis of the 16S rRNA confirmed that CS/PEC-AMF NPs maintained the composition of the intestinal flora. Beta diversity analysis indicated that X-ray exposure significantly altered the intestinal microbiota composition; however, the microbiota in the CS/PEC-AMF NP group closely resembled that of the control group. Notably, Bacteroidales, major producers of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), were increased in the CS/PEC-AMF NP mice. The dominant enterobacterial species in the CS/PEC-AMF NP group included Muribaculaceae, Alistipes, Oscillospiraceae, Lachnospiraceae, Bacteroidales, and Clostridia. Previous literature indicates that Muribaculaceaecan competitively inhibit the growth of various pathogenic bacteria, such as Clostridium difficile[49]. Additionally, the increase in Alistipeshas a positive regulatory role in intestinal diseases, consistent with prior reports[50]. The increased abundance of Lachnospiraceaebenefits SCFA production, contributing positively to the alleviation of colitis[51]. Moreover, targeted metabolite analysis of SCFAs demonstrated that the levels of seven SCFAs in the X-ray group were significantly lower than those in the control group. In contrast, treatment with CS/PEC-AMF NPs effectively restored the abundance of SCFAs, particularly propanoic acid and butanoic acid. These results align with previous studies that establish the crucial role of SCFAs in regulating susceptibility to radiation-induced diseases[52]. Consequently, CS/PEC-AMF NPs effectively reduced neutrophil infiltration in colonic tissues, increased the number of anti-inflammatory dendritic cells, and facilitated macrophage polarization from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2, hence reducing tissue inflammation. Furthermore, CS/PEC-AMF NPs reduced the expression levels of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 in colonic tissues, thereby alleviating inflammation.

One of the most significant findings was that CS/PEC-AMF NPs could protect against immediate radiation damage and mitigate the long-term risk of secondary malignancies, a major concern in radiotherapy. This study demonstrated that CS/PEC-AMF NPs extended the survival rate of mice with orthotopic colon and rectal tumors undergoing radiotherapy without compromising the tumor suppression effect. This protective mechanism addresses notable challenges in existing radiotherapy practices, where protecting healthy intestinal tissues often compromises tumor control. Furthermore, the incidence of AOM/DSS-induced colorectal cancer in mice exposed to radiation was eliminated by CS/PEC-AMF NP intervention. These results highlighted the therapeutic potential of CS/PEC-AMF NPs in enhancing both safety and efficacy. The molecular mechanisms underlying these protective effects were elucidated through mRNA sequencing analysis, which revealed that CS/PEC-AMF NPs inhibited the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby preventing the cytokine storms often induced by radiation. Additionally, these NPs enhance intrinsic cellular repair processes, promoting DNA repair mechanisms and further protecting healthy tissues. These findings indicated that the proposed strategy effectively alleviates the adverse effects of pelvic radiotherapy. By protecting the intestinal microenvironment and reducing the risk of secondary cancers, CS/PEC-AMF NPs potentially improved the quality of life for patients undergoing treatment for pelvic tumors. Future clinical studies are warranted to translate these preclinical findings into therapeutic protocols. Long-term investigations will ensure the safety and tissue compatibility of CS/PEC-AMF NPs (Fig. Sup 30).

While the findings are promising, this study has certain limitations. The murine model, though informative, does not fully capture the complexities of human physiology and tumor biology. Future research should involve larger animal models and ultimately progress to clinical trials to validate the efficacy and safety of CS/PEC-AMF NPs in human subjects. Furthermore, comprehensive investigations into the long-term effects and potential toxicity of these nanoparticles are warranted.

Conclusions

The development of CS/PEC-AMF NPs signifies a substantial advancement in radioprotection, providing dual benefits of safeguarding healthy intestinal tissues while preserving anti-tumor efficacy. This innovative strategy holds the potential to redefine radiotherapy for abdominal and pelvic tumors, enhancing both safety and effectiveness.
