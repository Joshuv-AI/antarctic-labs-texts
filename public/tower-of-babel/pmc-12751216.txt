Effects of exercise with probiotics, exercise alone, and probiotics alone on physical, motor, affective, and cognitive functions in stroke patients: a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Adekola B Ademoyegun, Emmanuel T Ojo, Wasiu A Rasaq, David O Fabuluje, Mosimiloluwa C Akinsole, Adedayo A Awotipe, Taofeek O Awotidebe, Chidozie E Mbada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical and neuropsychiatric disturbances often persist after stroke, necessitating innovative rehabilitation strategies. Combining exercise with probiotics may synergistically enhance recovery by leveraging neuroplasticity and modulation of the gut-brain axis. This study evaluated the individual and combined effects of exercise and probiotics in stroke patients.MethodsNinety-six patients (32 per group) within 1 year post-stroke were randomized to exercise with probiotics (EWP) (90 min/session multimodal exercise, 3 times weekly, plus probiotics, 1 capsule/day, 3.3 billion CFU Lactobacillus rhamnosus and Bifidobacterium lactis, for 8 weeks), exercise alone (EA), or probiotics alone (PA). The primary outcomes of physical disability (modified Rankin Scale [mRS]) and depression (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale [HADS]-depression sub-scale) and secondary outcomes of motor function (Supplemental Motor Scale of National Institute of Health Stroke Scale [SMSNIHSS]), anxiety (HADS-anxiety sub-scale), and cognition (Montreal Cognitive Assessment [MoCA]) were assessed at baseline, 4th week, and 8th week.ResultsEWP showed significantly greater improvements in mRS (p < 0.05, η² = 0.26 at week 4, 0.46 at week 8), SMSNIHSS (p < 0.05, η² = 0.08 at week 4, 0.24 at week 8), HADS-D (p < 0.05, η² = 0.24 at week 8), HADS-A (p < 0.05, η² = 0.07 at week 4, 0.26 at week 8), and MoCA (p < 0.05, η² = 0.18 at week 4, 0.46 at week 8) compared to EA and PA at weeks 4 and 8, except for HADS-D at week 4 (p > 0.05, η² = 0.04). At week 8, EA significantly outperformed PA in mRS, SMSNIHSS, and MoCA (p < 0.05), but not HADS-D or HADS-A (p > 0.05). Transient stomach upset occurred in 9.4% (PA) and 6.3% (EWP), resolved with water. EA reported no adverse events.ConclusionEWP showed superiority compared to either intervention alone in improving physical and motor functions, mood, and cognition in stroke patients. EA outperforms PA in physical, motor, and cognitive outcomes but shows comparable effects on depression and anxiety. Minimal adverse events support EWP’s feasibility. These findings underscore the possibility of integrating exercise with probiotics to optimize post-stroke recovery in multiple domains.Trial registrationPan African Clinical Trial Registry (PACTR202408541705549), 13/08/2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01446-2.Keywords: Stroke, Probiotics, Cognition, Affective function, Exercise

Introduction

Globally, stroke is one of the leading causes of death and disability-adjusted life years [1]. The prevalence of stroke and its burden are increasing, especially in developing countries [2–4]. In Nigeria, for instance, the 30-day fatality rate of stroke is about 40% [4, 5]. Meanwhile, many stroke survivors are presented with physical, motor, emotional, and cognitive deficits requiring interventions [6]. Effective rehabilitation post-stroke is imperative not only to improve functional abilities but also to enhance well-being and quality of life. One of the common intervention protocols in stroke management is exercise intervention, and different exercise protocols have been shown to positively modify various stroke-related sequelae and promote neuroplasticity and clinical recovery, including motor recovery, cardiovascular health, mood, and emotions [7–10]. Exercise promotes stroke recovery through multiple mechanisms, such as enhancing cardiovascular fitness, improving muscle strength, stimulating neuroplasticity, reducing inflammation, releasing endorphins, boosting brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), promoting neural repair, improving psychological well-being, etc [7–16].

Despite these benefits, post-stroke recovery is still suboptimal, necessitating the exploration of adjunctive therapies that may augment exercise benefits. Recently, the “microbial-gut-brain axis” has emerged as a possible therapeutic target in stroke rehabilitation [17]. A normal synergy between gut and brain in stroke patients enhances positive immune response, promotes normal endocrine environment, and induces and promotes neuro-regeneration, thereby resulting in better clinical outcomes [17, 18]. However, an incidence of stroke disrupts microbial composition balance, called dysbiosis, worsens the stroke outcome, and thereby provides rationale for the inclusion of probiotics as a complementary intervention in stroke rehabilitation [17–19].

Probiotics are supplements composed of living, safe microorgaisms that are beneficial to human health through modulation of gut microbiota [17, 18, 20, 21]. Although many of the studies investigating the importance of probiotics in stroke are undertaken using stroke animal models [17, 18, 22], several authors have reported the benefits of probiotics administration in stroke recovery, such as positive modulation of the gastrointestinal environment, promotion of anti-inflammatory and suppression of pro-inflammatory processes after stroke, including improved clinical outcomes [22–24]. For instance, studies have shown that probiotics enhance neurogenesis and cognition and reduce symptoms of anxiety in animal models [25, 26]. Similarly, among humans, probiotics supplementation has been associated with a reduction in depression and cognitive impairment in both clinical and community samples [27, 28].

Meanwhile, despite the promising theoretical and initial clinical outlook, the impact of combining physical exercise with probiotic supplementation on multidimensional clinical outcomes in patients with stroke remains underexplored. Together, both interventions have the potential to modulate overlapping pathways, including inflammatory processes or signaling, gut microbiota composition, neurotrophic factor expression, and stress regulation [25, 29]. While some studies have explored the effects of these interventions separately, none, to our knowledge, have investigated their potential synergistic impact, particularly in relation to physical, motor, and neuropsychiatric aspects of stroke recovery. To address this gap, we conducted a randomized controlled trial investigating the effects of combined exercise with probiotics, exercise alone, and probiotics alone on physical, motor, affective, and cognitive functions in stroke patients. We hypothesized that the combined interventions would be more effective and yield superior outcomes compared to either intervention alone. By examining both separate and potential synergistic effects of structured exercise and probiotics supplementation, we aim to apprise evidence-based, novel, safe, and effective rehabilitation approaches for enhancing post-stroke recovery.

Methods

Study design

This study was a three-arm, parallel-group, randomised controlled trial conducted to examine the comparative effects of exercise combined with probiotics, exercise alone, and probiotics alone on physical, motor, affective, and cognitive functions in patients with stroke. The trial was conducted at the Osun State University Teaching Hospital and the State Specialist Hospital, Osogbo, Nigeria. The ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of the Osun State University Teaching Hospital, Osogbo, Nigeria (Approval No: UTH/REC/2024/12/1083). The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013), and all participants provided written informed consent. The trial was prospectively registered on 13th August, 2024, at the Pan African Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No: PACTR202408541705549).

Participants

Participants for this study, recruited between December 2024 and June 2025, were adults (≥ 18 years) with a confirmed diagnosis of ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke, as verified by radiological imaging (Computed Tomography or Magnetic Resonance Imaging) and clinical assessment. Inclusion criteria were having the ability to walk with or without aid or perform seated exercises, with stroke duration of ≤ 1 year, and the ability to give informed consent. The exclusion criteria included major communication and/or cognitive problem, pre-stroke severe psychiatric conditions (e.g., psychosis), unstable cardiovascular conditions, pre-stroke modified Rankin Score (mRS) other than 0 or any physical disability before stroke, other neurological problems (e.g., Parkinson’s disease), repeat stroke, current use of probiotics supplement or any other medication/supplement that could affect microbome or gastrointestinal motility within four weeks before enrollment, gastrointestinal disorder (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease), enrollment in another interventional trial, or consent refusal. During the pendency of the trial, 112 patients were assessed for eligibility, out of which 16 were excluded on account of stroke duration > 1 year (n = 9), major communication problem (n = 2), recurrent stroke (n = 4), and refused consent (n = 1). Participants were consecutively recruited into this randomized controlled trial. No participant was lost to follow-up. The participants’ CONSORT flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. Randomisation and blinding

Patients who fulfilled the inclusion criteria were randomized in a 1:1:1 ratio into the exercise alone (EA), probiotics alone (PA), and exercise with probiotics (EWP) groups using a simple balloting procedure. An equal number of allocation labels (‘A’, ‘B’, and ‘C’; 32 each) were prepared on slips of paper, sealed in identical opaque envelopes, and placed in a non-transparent box. For each participant, an independent research assistant (blinded to group identity and not involved in intervention or assessment) randomly drew one envelope from the box without replacement. The patient was then allocated to EA, PA, or EWP according to the label drawn (‘A’, ‘B’, or ‘C’). The use of non-replacement ensured balanced group sizes across the trial arms while maintaining allocation concealment. Due to the nature of the intervention, physiotherapists delivering the treatments and the participants could not be blinded. However, outcome assessors were blinded to group allocation throughout the study. To maintain blinding, assessors were not involved in the randomization or intervention process and were only provided with coded participant IDs without access to allocation lists. Participants were instructed not to disclose their intervention to assessors, and assessments were conducted in a separate setting from the intervention sessions. Periodic checks were performed by the trial coordinator to ensure that blinding was preserved, and no instances of unblinding were reported. All clinical assessments were carried out by experienced physiotherapists who were trained in the standardized administration of all outcome measures.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated a priori using Cohen’s Table [30] for one-way ANOVA with three groups. Assuming a large effect size (f = 0.40), a significance level of α = 0.05, and power of 0.80, a minimum sample of 78 participants (26 per group) was required. To account for an estimated 20% attrition, the final target sample size was set at 96 participants (32 per group). The choice of a large effect size (f = 0.40) was informed by previous stroke rehabilitation trials that reported moderate-to-large improvements following exercise-based interventions [31, 32]. However, to evaluate the robustness of this assumption, a sensitivity analysis was performed using the final sample size (N = 96). The analysis showed that with 96 participants, statistical power would be approximately 0.72 for a moderate effect size (f = 0.30) and 0.58 for a small effect size (f = 0.25). This indicates that while the study was adequately powered to detect large effects, it may have been underpowered to detect modest treatment effects. Therefore, the present trial also provides preliminary effect size estimates to inform future larger-scale studies. The sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome (HADS-D) at the 8th week.

Baseline assessment

Immediately after randomization, baseline data of the participants, including socio-demographic data (age, sex, weight, height, body mass index, marital status, educational status, and income status) and residential address and contact phone numbers, were obtained. Participants with no or less than a tertiary education were categorized as low education status, while income was classified as low (< #70,000), moderate (#70,000–250,000), and high (>#250,000), using the Nigerian national minimum wage. The paretic side, time since stroke, stroke type, and prescribed medications were also assessed. Stroke duration was categorised as early sub-acute (≤ 3 months), late sub-acute (4–6 months), and early chronic (˃6 ≤ 12 months) stage [33]. Furthermore, the number and type of stroke risk factors or co-morbidity (e.g., hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, ischaemic heart disease or previous myocardial infarction or angina pectoris; diabetes; urinary tract infection; and respiratory infection were assessed [34]. The smoking and alcohol habits of the participants were also assessed.

The baseline stroke severity of each participant was assessed by the use of the 11-item National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), with each item scoring between 0 and 4. A higher score is indicative of a higher level of impairment. The maximum possible score is 42, with the minimum score being 0 [35]. The NIHSS score was categorized as mild (1–4 scores), moderate (5–15 scores), moderately severe (16–20 scores), and very severe (≥ 21 scores) stroke [35]. The baseline physical, motor, affective (depression and anxiety), and cognitive functions of the participants were assessed with mRS, Supplemental Motor Scale of NIHSS (SMSNIHSS), Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), respectively.

Interventions

Exercise alone group

Participants in this group received a multimodal, progressive, supervised, 8-week, 90-minute per session exercise intervention three times weekly, amounting to 24 sessions of 90-minute exercise or 36 h of exercise in total. The exercise sessions included range of motion (ROM) exercises, strengthening exercises (proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation techniques, Theraband exercises), balance and flexibility exercises (step-ups, chair rises, wall exercise, marching, toe rises, ball kicking, sudden stop and turn on motion), upper limb functional exercises (opening of drawers, writing, hand exerciser, picking and counting), and aerobic exercises (treadmill exercise, riding a stationary bicycle ergometer, stepping exercise) [36–38].

The ROM, strengthening, balance, flexibility, and upper limb exercises were individualized but followed a standardized progression protocol. Participants typically performed 2–3 sets of 5–10 repetitions. Progression was applied when participants could complete all prescribed repetitions with correct form and without undue fatigue for two consecutive sessions. In such cases, repetitions were increased by 2–3 per set, or an additional set was added. Exercise intensity for strengthening was set initially at 20%–30% of one-repetition maximum (1RM) and progressed gradually, while aerobic intensity was prescribed at 50%–70% of maximum heart rate (MHR). Progression of endurance and aerobic exercises was achieved by incrementally increasing speed, resistance, or duration while maintaining the target intensity range. This approach allowed consistency across participants while accommodating individual differences in recovery.

The exercise intervention was on alternate days with at least one day of rest. The exercise was supervised by licensed physiotherapists. The participants in this group received no placebo capsules. Exercise adherence was monitored with direct supervision of a physiotherapist, exercise diary/logs, calls/texts, and caregivers/family.

Probiotics alone group

Participants in this group received probiotics supplementation for eight weeks. Participants were required to take a daily capsule of probiotics containing 3.3 billion CFU Lactobacillus rhamnosus and Bifidobacterium lactis (Morepen Laboratories Ltd., Himachal Pradesh, India) for 8 weeks, totaling 56 capsules. Participants were asked to take the supplement after breakfast [17, 39, 40]. Participants were instructed to maintain their usual activity levels and to avoid initiating/participating in a new exercise programme to control for physical activity. Participants’ adherence was monitored with calls/texts, reminders by family/caregivers, by a diary, and by inspecting the blister packs of consumed capsules once every week during physical meetings [40].

Exercise with probiotics group

Participants in this group received both multimodal, progressive, supervised 8-week, 90-minute exercise intervention three times weekly and a daily capsule of probiotics containing 3.3 billion CFU Lactobacillus rhamnosus and Bifidobacterium lactis, concurrently (i.e., 12 sessions of 90-minute exercise or 36 h of exercise plus 56 capsules of probiotics). In this group, if falling on exercise intervention day, the participants took the daily dose of the probiotics at least 30 min before the exercise intervention [36]. The combined strategies for adherence in the EA and PA groups were employed in this group.

Safety and nutritional control

Adverse events such as exercise-related injuries, stomach/gastrointestinal discomfort, etc., were monitored every week by the study team. Contact information was provided to each participant to report concerns or adverse events. All participants continued to receive standard stroke care, including nutritional counseling. Participants were instructed to maintain a consistent diet throughout the study and to avoid foods or supplements known to alter gut microbiota or influence study outcomes (e.g., omega-3 fatty acids, non-study probiotics, prebiotics, or high-sugar diets). Adherence was monitored using weekly dietary checklists and structured participant interviews conducted by research staff. Any reported deviations were documented and reviewed. This approach has been validated in similar microbiota intervention studies as a feasible method of minimizing dietary confounding [41, 42].

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes

The primary outcomes for each group in this study were physical disability and depression assessed at baseline, 4th week, and 8th week of intervention. The physical function was assessed by mRS, a validated measure of physical disability after stroke. The mRS is a tool measured on a seven-point Likert scale from 0 (no disability), 1 (no significant disability), 2 (slight disability), 3 (moderate disability), 4 (moderately severe disability), 5 (severe disability), and 6 (death). The higher score indicates greater disability [34]. The participants’ symptoms of depression were assessed by the HADS depression subscale. This instrument has seven items/questions on the depression subscale rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 3. The HADS depression subscale has a maximum score of 21, with a higher score indicating more symptoms of depression [43].

Secondary outcomes

Motor impairment, anxiety, and cognition were the secondary outcomes in this trial, also assessed at baseline, 4th week, and 8th week of the study. The SMSNIHSS was used to assess motor function. The SMSNIHSS has a six-point grade to assess the motor function of both limbs from no movement (score of 5) to normal movement (score of 0) [44], with a minimum to maximum score being 0 to 40. A higher SMSNIHSS score indicates a severe motor impairment after stroke [44]. The anxiety symptoms were evaluated with the HADS anxiety subscale. This tool has seven items on the anxiety subscale scored on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 to 3. The HADS anxiety subscale has a maximum score of 21, with a higher score suggesting greater anxiety symptoms [43]. The MoCA was used to assess cognitive functions in the areas of memory, language, abstraction, executive function, etc. This instrument has a maximum score of 30, with a lower score indicating greater cognitive impairment [45].

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics of mean, median, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage, as appropriate, were used to summarise socio-demographic and stroke-related characteristics and baseline outcome measures. Repeated measures ANOVA were used to compare the effects of different interventions within the group across baseline, 4th, and 8th weeks of the study. Furthermore, one-way ANOVA was used to evaluate between-group comparisons of physical, motor, cognitive, and affective functions at the 4th and 8th weeks. Post hoc tests with Bonferroni adjustment were employed to identify group differences where a significant difference was observed. Effect sizes were reported using partial eta squared (η²), with magnitude defined as small (0.01), medium (0.06), and large (0.14) [46]. The alpha level was set at p < 0.05. No imputation for missing data was required as all participants completed the intervention and follow-up assessments. All analyses were conducted on an intention-to-treat basis. The intention-to-treat and per-protocol analyses were identical, as there were no dropouts. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 21.0.

Results

All 96 patients (32 per group) completed the 8-week intervention. In the PA group, three participants (3/32; 9.4%) reported transient stomach upset during the first week of probiotics administration, which resolved after drinking water. In the EWP group, two participants (2/32; 6.3%) reported similar transient stomach upset during the first week, which also resolved with water intake, with no interference in their exercise schedule. No further adverse events were reported in either group throughout the intervention. The EA group reported no adverse events during or after exercise sessions.

The baseline socio-demographic and stroke-related characteristics are presented in Table 1. The mean age of the total cohort was 58.6 ± 8.83 years, with more male participants (67.7%). The majority of participants had a high level of education (54.2%), while about half (49.0%) had a history of alcohol intake. The most common stroke type observed in this cohort was ischaemic stroke (70.8%), while the majority was in the sub-acute stroke phase (65.6%) with a mean stroke duration of 5.72 ± 3.46 months. The participants had mild to moderate stroke severity with NIHSS mean scores of 3.75 ± 2.32. The results showed no significant differences in the examined socio-demographic and stroke-related features across EA, PA, and EWP groups (p > 0.05). Similarly, there were no significant differences in the baseline outcome measures (physical disability, motor impairment, depression, anxiety, and cognitive impairment) across the three groups (Table 2), indicating that participants in the three groups had comparable baseline outcome measures.

Discussion

This randomised controlled trial aimed to investigate the comparative effects of exercise combined with probiotics (EWP), exercise alone (EA), and probiotics alone (PA) on physical, motor, affective (depression and anxiety), and cognitive (attention, executive function, memory, language, visuospatial ability, conceptual thinking, calculation, and orientation) functions in patients with stroke. To our knowledge, this is the first study to provide empirical data on the potential synergistic effects of exercise and probiotics in stroke patients. The findings from within-group analysis indicated all three interventions had significant effects on physical disability, motor impairment, depression, anxiety, and cognition across baseline, 4th week, and 8th week, except in physical disability and motor function, where the effects of PA were not significant. Previous studies have established the efficacy of physical exercise [7–11] and probiotics supplementation [22–24, 47, 48] in improving clinical outcomes in stroke.

Exercise promotes stroke recovery through multiple proven mechanisms [7–16]. Although most data on probiotics supplementation in stroke come from animal studies or non-stroke clinical populations, probiotics might promote stroke recovery via reduced neuroinflammation, improved systemic health, positive modulation of neurotransmitters, and enhanced neurogenesis, gut-brain axis, and BDNF [22–24, 49–52]. In short, probiotics primarily influence systemic inflammation and neural function through the modulation of the gut microbiota, and psychological health through the gut-brain axis. The significant improvement in depression, anxiety, and cognition among patients in the PA group is consistent with the findings of earlier studies that support the role of the gut-brain axis in neuropsychiatry recovery [40, 47, 48, 51, 53, 54].

However, the non-significant improvement in physical disability and motor function observed among patients in the PA group across the study’s time points may indicate limited direct influence of probiotics alone on physical symptoms or insufficiency of probiotics alone to drive motor recovery, highlighting the importance of physical exercise in stroke rehabilitation. Furthermore, the intervention period of 8 weeks may not be sufficient enough for probiotics to exert significant neuromuscular adaptations, as evidence implied that it takes long-term administration of probiotics for the translation of probiotic-mediated modulation of inflammation and the gut–brain axis into functional benefits [55]. Also, the dosage and the specific strains used in this study may not have strong direct effects on motor recovery pathways, which are primarily driven by task-specific and exercise-induced neural plasticity and neuromotor stimulation (7–10, 12–16).

The within-group results further showed that the greatest effect sizes were observed in the EWP group for the outcome measures, suggesting a possible synergistic effect of exercise and probiotics on post-stroke physical, motor, affective, and cognitive recovery. The results of between-group comparisons showed that patients in the EWP group had significantly higher improvement across all outcome measures compared to the EA and PA groups at both the 4th and 8th week, except in depressive symptoms at the 4th week, where the improvement was not significantly higher. This corroborates the hypothesis that combining exercise with probiotics improves stroke recovery across multiple domains. The findings further suggest potential synergistic effects of exercise and probiotics, by which exercise’s promotion of neuroplasticity, motor and functional recovery, and cardiovascular fitness, combined with probiotics’ positive modulation of neuroinflammation and gut-brain axis, led to greater clinical benefits compared to either intervention alone.

The lack of significant between-group differences in depressive symptoms at the 4th week, despite significant improvements at the 8th week, implies a delay in the possible synergistic influence of EWP on affective outcome, which is consistent with previous evidence. Previous studies have shown that the anti-depressant effects of exercise require several weeks of consistent training before becoming clinically meaningful [56, 57]. Similarly, results of systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that although the ideal time to observe the effects of probiotics on psychiatric symptoms is unknown, the effects of probiotics supplementation on mood may take weeks to appear in non-clinical populations [55].

The findings also indicated that the combined effect sizes of EWP on outcome measures in this study increased over time from week 4 (η²=0.04–0.26) to week 8 (η²=0.24–0.46) of intervention. This implies that the potential synergistic benefits of EWP appear in a short time, accumulate and strengthen over time as both interventions target complementary neural pathways, reinforcing the combined intervention’s superiority compared to either intervention alone. The temporal increase in effect sizes also indicate the necessity for a longer period of combined intervention to observe greater additive benefits for future trial designs.

The findings further showed that at the 4th week, there were no significant differences between the impact of EA and PA across all outcome measures. However, at the 8th week, the effects of EA on physical disability, motor function, and cognition were significantly better than PA, while the effects of EA on depression and anxiety were not significantly different from those of PA. Summarily, the effects of EA were substantially better than those of PA only at the 8th week in terms of physical disability, motor, and cognitive functions. These findings highlight the critical role of exercise intervention in enhancing physical, motor, and cognitive recovery, while probiotics showed comparable effectiveness in affective outcomes. The superiority of EA over PA for physical, motor, and cognitive functions at week 8 may result from the direct impact of exercise on some neurophysiological parameters, including direct stimulation of BDNF, enhancing hippocampal neurogenesis, promotion of vascular endothealia growth factor, supporting neural repair, and increasing cerebral blood flow, which subsequently promote motor recovery, executive function, attention, memory, etc [11, 14, 18, 40, 48, 54, 58, 59].

Although probiotics also promote BDNF and reduce neuroinflammatory processes through the gut-brain axis, it appears that the cognitive benefits of probiotics are less robust compared to multimodal exercise intervention within 8 weeks. It is possible that the translation of microbiota into meaningful post-stroke cognitive benefits requires more time, therefore shows slower benefits compared to exercise. However, the very large MoCA effect size at 8 week in the EWP group (η²=0.46) indicates that probiotics augment the cognitive benefits of exercise, potentially through synergistic inflammation reduction [18, 22, 40, 48, 51, 54, 60]. The comparable benefits between EA and PA on symptoms of depression and anxiety at the 8th week indicate possible overlapping mechanisms of alleviating affective symptoms between the two interventions, including positive neurotransmitter modulation (e.g., serotonin, GABA) and reduction in neuroinflammation [14, 18, 22, 40, 48, 51, 53, 60].

The transient stomach upset reported in the first week by patients in PA (3/32; 9.6%) and EWP (2/32; 6.3%) groups, which was resolved with water and not reoccurring, suggest a self-limiting, mild gastrointestinal discomfort associated with probiotics supplementation (e.g., Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium). The non-interference with exercise schedule in the EWP group and no reported adverse reaction in the EA group indicate the excellent tolerability and safety of the structured multimodal exercise intervention employed in this study. Previous research has reported the safety of structured exercise (aerobic at 50–70% MHR, strength/flexibility at 20–30% 1RM) in sub-acute or chronic stroke patients [14–16]. The safety profile of interventions across all groups with high adherence support their clinical applicability and integration of probiotics with exercise in stroke rehabilitation.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study provide important insights for post-stroke rehabilitation. One, a multimodal stroke rehabilitation approach combining physical exercise and probiotics supplementation may provide better improvements in a wide spectrum of stroke recovery, including physical and neuropsychiatric sequelae, than traditional rehabilitation methods. Two, although exercise remains a major integral for physical and motor recovery in stroke rehabilitation, probiotics appear as a safe, cost-effective, and simple adjunct therapy for mental and cognitive recovery. Three, the observed improvements in the affective and cognitive functions even among patients receiving only probiotics indicate that interventions targeting gut–brain interactions may be particularly beneficial in relieving post-stroke neuropsychiatric symptoms. Four, considering probiotics’ tolerability, accessibility, and safety, it can be readily integrated into standard stroke care, especially in centres where access to mental health support is limited. Finally, these findings support the integration of nutritional interventions as part of stroke rehabilitation, especially during the sub-acute and early chronic stages, when neuroplasticity and psychological adjustment post-stroke are critical.

Limitations of the study

While this study yielded promising insights, certain limitations must be acknowledged. First, the lack of data on some biomarkers, such as inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6), neurotrophic factors (e.g., BDNF), and neurotransmitter systems (e.g., 5-HT, dopamine), as well as faecal microbiota composition, limits understanding of the mechanisms of synergistic efficacy of exercise combined with probiotics and relative contributions of exercise and probiotics. Second, this study is limited to probiotics dosage and strain (1 capsule per day, 3.3 billion CFU Lactobacillus rhamnosus and Bifidobacterium lactis, for 8 weeks). Higher doses and other probiotics strain/multi-strain formulations over a longer period may be better optimized to post-stroke physical, motor, affective, and cognitive functions. Similarly, the exercise regimen (3 times weekly, aerobic at 50–70% MHR, strength/flexibility at 20–30% 1RM, for 8 weeks) may limit the strength of improvement, as more sessions (e.g., 5–7 times weekly) or extended duration (e.g., 12–16 weeks) could further promote better recovery. Third, the modest sample size (n = 96) may reduce the ability to detect smaller but clinically significant between-group differences. Fourth, while baseline distribution was balanced across groups, the inclusion of both ischaemic and haemorrhagic stroke patients may limit the generalizability of the findings due to the heterogeneity in recovery patterns between stroke subtypes. Thus, future studies should investigate subtype-specific responses. Fifth, the adherence to probiotic supplementation was monitored using participant diaries, reminder calls, and weekly blister pack checks, which may not fully confirm daily intake. This could introduce reporting bias and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Finally, the mild-to-moderate baseline stroke deficits may have introduced ceiling effects, hindering detectable improvements, especially in the PA group. Despite these limitations, this study’s robust design presents empirical data on the novel integration of exercise and probiotics in stroke patients and provides evidence on the potential synergistic benefits of EWP on post-stroke motor and neuropsychiatric recovery, coupled with high tolerability and mild adverse effects.

Conclusion

This three-arm, parallel, randomised controlled trial demonstrates that combining multimodal exercise with probiotics supplementation for 8 weeks significantly improved physical disability, motor function, depression, anxiety, and cognition in stroke patients of one year duration, with increasing effect size from 4 to 8 weeks, compared to exercise or probiotics alone. Exercise alone significantly outperformed probiotics alone in enhancing physical disability, motor function, and cognition at week 8, while both interventions showed comparable benefits on affective (depression and anxiety) symptoms. The minimal adverse effects and tolerability of combining exercise with probiotics supplementation support its feasibility in clinical practice. These findings underscore the possibility of integrating exercise with probiotics, particularly in the sub-acute to early chronic phase of stroke rehabilitation, to optimize recovery in multiple domains. Future research should explore more frequent exercise regimens, higher probiotics doses, patients with severe deficits or longer post-stroke duration, longer intervention periods, and biomarkers (e.g., IL-6, BDNF) to examine the synergistic mechanisms and enhance stroke recovery.
