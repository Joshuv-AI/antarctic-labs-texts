Evaluating the Accuracy, Empathy, and Readability of Generative AI Versus Registered Nurses in Discharge Planning: A Vignette‐Based Study
Authors: Yike Wang, Meiyan Ji, Xinghua Bai, Yuan Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo compare the multidimensional performance of discharge instructions generated by generative AI (GPT‐4) versus those created by clinical registered nurses across three dimensions—accuracy, empathy and readability—and to explore the impact of patient.DesignA prospective, double‐blind, vignette‐based cross‐sectional study.MethodsFive standardized multidisciplinary discharge scenarios were constructed. Discharge instructions were generated independently by five registered nurses and GPT‐4. Fifteen clinical experts conducted blinded assessments of accuracy, while 38 patients conducted blinded assessments of empathy and readability. Objective text features were extracted using natural language processing. Paired t‐tests or Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests were used to compare differences between groups, and a generalized linear mixed model was constructed to analyse factors influencing the acceptability of AI‐generated text.ResultsAI outperformed nurses in information comprehensiveness, but experts identified safety risks in AI‐generated texts, whereas no such issues were found in nurse‐produced texts. Nurses significantly outperformed AI in both empathy and readability, and objective NLP analysis confirmed that AI‐generated texts exhibited higher syntactic complexity and terminology density. The generalized linear mixed model indicated that advancing age and lower educational attainment were associated with reduced acceptance of AI‐generated texts. These findings derive from standardized vignettes under controlled experimental conditions and require further validation in real clinical settings.ConclusionGenerative AI offers value as a drafting aid for ensuring information completeness in discharge instructions; however, its safety risks, empathy deficits, and linguistic complexity currently limit its standalone application. AI may be better positioned as an assistive tool for nurses rather than an independent communication tool, and its deployment should address potential digital divide issues among patient populations.Implications for the Profession and/or Patient CareThese findings support positioning artificial intelligence as an information completeness tool requiring mandatory nurse review before patient delivery. Nurses remain essential for ensuring clinical safety, providing empathetic communication, and adapting language complexity to individual patient needs. Healthcare organizations should establish protocols requiring nurse verification of all artificial intelligence‐generated discharge content, with particular attention to medication dosages and contraindications. The identification of a digital divide necessitates that deployment strategies include health literacy assessment and tiered delivery approaches to prevent technological advances from exacerbating health communication inequalities among vulnerable populations.Impact What problem did the study address? ○Generative artificial intelligence tools are increasingly proposed for clinical documentation, yet limited evidence exists comparing their discharge instruction quality against registered nurses—the professionals primarily responsible for discharge education—across multiple dimensions relevant to patient safety and comprehension. Introduction

Discharge planning serves as a critical transitional link between inpatient care and home‐based rehabilitation, and registered nurses are widely recognized as the primary professionals responsible for coordinating and delivering discharge education in clinical practice (Kang et al. 2020). Effective discharge instructions help patients understand their follow‐up medication regimens, key points for self‐monitoring, and follow‐up appointment schedules, thereby reducing the risk of unplanned readmissions. However, numerous studies have shown that the communication of information during the discharge process often faces significant challenges. It has been reported that approximately 19.6% of patients are readmitted within 30 days of discharge for various reasons, a significant proportion of which is attributed to inadequate communication during the discharge process (Donzé et al. 2023; Seol et al. 2025). Kripalani et al. (2007) similarly noted in their systematic review that a lack of understanding of information is a common issue among inpatients at discharge, which is closely related to the quality of discharge instructions, language complexity, and the time healthcare providers have available for education. In clinical practice, registered nurses typically bear the primary responsibility for discharge education; however, increasing workloads and administrative pressures may limit the comprehensiveness and personalization of this education (Kang et al. 2020). Consequently, how to support nurses in balancing the accuracy of information with patients' emotional needs and comprehension abilities remains an ongoing focus in the field of discharge planning and a core concern of nursing science.

The complexity of discharge planning is further amplified in patients with highly complex clinical pathways. Polytrauma patients, for example, frequently require prolonged hospitalization, multidisciplinary coordination, rehabilitation planning, extensive caregiver education, and complex discharge pathways before returning home (Biz et al. 2016). The discharge management of such patients illustrates one of the most demanding scenarios for nursing practice, requiring substantial clinical judgement, individualized patient education, and coordination across disciplines that extend far beyond the provision of standardized written instructions. This example underscores the critical importance of accurate, empathetic, and individualized discharge communication—skills that have long been cultivated within the nursing profession.

In recent years, the application of generative large language models (LLMs), exemplified by GPT‐4, in the healthcare sector has garnered widespread attention. These models are capable of rapidly generating structured medical text based on natural language prompts, demonstrating significant potential for use in scenarios such as clinical documentation assistance and the creation of patient education materials. A study by Ayers et al. found that ChatGPT's responses to patients' online inquiries scored higher than those of physicians in terms of both information quality and empathy, sparking discussions about whether AI can assist healthcare professionals in communication roles under specific circumstances (Ayers et al. 2023). Meanwhile, a systematic review on the ethics and governance of large language models in the medical field, published by Bedi et al. (2024), also noted that while exploring the clinical value of these tools, attention must be paid simultaneously to their potential safety risks and equity issues. These developments raise a question of direct relevance to nursing: if generative AI can produce discharge‐related text, how does its performance compare with that of registered nurses—the very professionals who deliver this education at the bedside?

The aforementioned studies collectively indicate that there is a pressing need for rigorous evaluation of large language models' performance in generating medical text. However, a review of the existing literature reveals several critical gaps that are particularly relevant to nursing practice and nursing science. First, regarding the selection of comparison subjects, most studies contrast AI outputs with physicians' responses (Aydın et al. 2024; Haltaufderheide and Ranisch 2024), while paying insufficient attention to nurses—the professional group that bears primary responsibility for discharge education and patient communication. Given that nurses and physicians differ substantially in communication style, emotional engagement, use of plain language, and patient‐centered approaches, a direct comparison between AI and nurses is essential to determine whether AI tools can genuinely support nursing workflows. This gap represents a significant limitation in the current evidence base for nursing informatics and practice innovation. Second, regarding evaluation dimensions, previous studies have largely focused on information accuracy or single‐dimensional quality scores (Shool et al. 2025), while paying relatively insufficient attention to empathy and readability—factors that are central to the nursing model of holistic, patient‐centred care. Discharge instructions must not only be medically accurate but also make patients feel cared for and ensure they can truly understand and follow the recommendations (Keçeci et al. 2025). These relational and communicative competencies represent core elements of nursing expertise that any AI tool must be evaluated against.

Regarding evaluation methods, some studies rely on the subjective judgements of researchers or a small number of experts, lacking a multi‐dimensional design that combines professional assessment with patient‐perspective evaluation (Powell et al. 2020). Meanwhile, in terms of experimental control, most studies directly use real online Q&A records or clinical documents as evaluation materials. However, the clinical backgrounds and information complexity of patients associated with different texts vary significantly, which may introduce confounding factors into direct comparisons between AI and human outputs (Reddy et al. 2026). Using standardized clinical scenarios (vignettes) as a uniform input may be a viable strategy to enhance comparability across studies.

Furthermore, regarding the study population, current research is primarily conducted in English‐language contexts, and evaluation data for Chinese medical texts remains limited. As a non‐alphabetic language, Chinese has different factors contributing to reading difficulty compared to English, and the Chinese patient population may exhibit significant heterogeneity in health literacy levels (Fu et al. 2025). Whether these factors influence patients' acceptance of AI‐generated content remains to be fully explored through empirical research. This is particularly relevant for nursing practice in Chinese healthcare settings, where nurses must tailor discharge education to patients with diverse literacy backgrounds. Based on the above considerations, this study aims to employ a prospective, double‐blind, standardized‐scenario‐based cross‐sectional design to systematically compare the performance of generative AI (GPT‐4) and registered clinical nurses in the generation of discharge plans across three dimensions: accuracy, empathy and readability. Unlike previous studies that compared AI with physicians, this study specifically selects registered nurses as the comparator to directly address the question of whether AI can match nursing competencies in discharge communication—a question with immediate implications for how AI tools should be integrated into nursing workflows. This study adopts a multi‐perspective approach combining expert and patient evaluations, supplemented by natural language processing (NLP) techniques to conduct objective quantitative analysis of text features. Additionally, the study explores whether patients' demographic characteristics (such as age and educational level) influence their acceptance of AI‐generated text. The findings are intended to provide an evidence‐based reference for the appropriate positioning of AI within nursing practice, specifically as a potential drafting aid rather than a replacement for nurses' clinical judgement and relational communication skills, and to inform the development of tiered, equitable approaches to AI‐assisted discharge planning.

Methods

Study Design

This study employed a prospective, double‐blind, vignette‐based cross‐sectional study design. Its primary aim was to compare the multidimensional performance of generative AI large language models and registered nurses (RNs) in producing written discharge instructions. The study protocol design and reporting adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening of Epidemiological Reporting of Observational Studies) guidelines (von Elm et al. 2007) and incorporated the core principles of the DECIDE‐AI (Decision‐Making for the Evaluation of AI Interventions in Healthcare) framework (Vasey et al. 2022).

Participant Recruitment and Inclusion Criteria

This study was conducted at a Grade III Level A hospital in Northeast China. Three groups of participants were independently recruited to generate the text materials and conduct a double‐blind evaluation.

The first group comprised five registered nurses recruited through purposive sampling to draft discharge instructions. Inclusion criteria: (1) Hold a valid registered nurse licence; (2) Possession of ≥ 5 years of continuous full‐time clinical experience in relevant specialties (cardiology, endocrinology, obstetrics, gastrointestinal surgery, geriatrics); (3) Daily duties include independently conducting patient discharge education. These criteria were designed to ensure that the text generated by registered nurses represents current high‐quality clinical nursing standards.

The second group consisted of 15 medical experts, including specialist attending physicians and nursing administrators. Inclusion criteria: Associate senior‐level professional title or higher, or ≥ 10 years of clinical experience, and familiarity with current clinical guidelines. Additionally, 40 adult patients or primary caregivers who had recently (within the past 6 months) been hospitalized were recruited via convenience sampling. Inclusion criteria: Age ≥ 18 years, basic literacy, and no severe cognitive impairment.

Vignette Development and Text Generation

Vignette Construction and Validation

The research team constructed five standardized multidisciplinary discharge vignettes based on de‐identified real electronic medical records (EMRs) (see Supporting Information S1 for details). The five clinical scenarios were purposively selected to represent high‐volume discharge conditions across five major clinical departments: cardiology (acute myocardial infarction post‐percutaneous coronary intervention), endocrinology (newly diagnosed Type 2 diabetes with insulin initiation), obstetrics (caesarean section postpartum), gastrointestinal surgery (post‐laparoscopic cholecystectomy) and geriatrics (community‐acquired pneumonia in an elderly patient with multiple comorbidities). The selection was guided by two criteria: (1) each scenario should represent a frequently encountered discharge condition within its respective department based on hospital discharge statistics from the preceding year; and (2) the five scenarios collectively should encompass varying levels of discharge complexity, including differences in medication management requirements, activity restrictions, wound care needs, and follow‐up urgency. Although these scenarios cover common clinical fields, they do not encompass all possible discharge settings (e.g., psychiatry, paediatrics, palliative oncology), and this is acknowledged as a limitation.

All scenarios underwent rigorous de‐identification to remove all personally identifiable health information (PHI) that could potentially identify real patients. The content validity of the scenarios was assessed by three independent clinical experts (one physician and two senior nursing specialists not otherwise involved in the study), yielding a content validity index (S‐CVI) of 0.94.

Text Generation

For each of the five scenarios described above, five registered nurses and a generative AI system independently generated written discharge instructions.

Nurse‐Generated Text

In a controlled environment free from external interference, the five nurses were each assigned one scenario corresponding to their respective specialties. Using their professional expertise and standard hospital resources, they drafted discharge instructions for patients in accordance with their daily clinical practices. The word count was limited to 500–800 words. Each nurse produced a single version of the discharge instructions without iterative revision or selection from multiple drafts. This design was intentional: it reflects real‐world clinical conditions in which nurses typically compose discharge instructions once under time constraints, and the study aimed to evaluate routine nursing practice output rather than an optimized or polished product.

AI‐Generated Text

This study selected GPT‐4 (version gpt‐4‐0613), a representative large language model currently in use, as its tool. The research team employed standardized prompt engineering to control variables (the complete prompt is provided in Supporting Information S2 for full reproducibility). To ensure the study's reproducibility, the researchers used the OpenAI API interface to limit the model's divergence parameter (setting Temperature = 0.2 to reduce output variability and minimize medical hallucinations). The following standardized prompt was uniformly provided to the model: ‘As a registered nurse with extensive clinical experience, please draft a comprehensive, safe, easy‐to‐understand, and empathetic discharge instruction guide for the patient based on the clinical information provided below. Please limit the text to 500–800 words. The patient's clinical information is as follows: [scenario text]’. The complete output text generated by the model on the first attempt was selected, without any manual intervention or secondary prompts.

To assess the reproducibility of AI outputs, the research team conducted three additional independent test runs for each scenario (not included in the final analysis). Content comparison by two researchers confirmed that outputs were highly consistent in structure, key information points, and overall recommendations (> 95% semantic overlap), supporting the reproducibility of the generation process at the specified temperature setting. Nevertheless, because large language models are inherently non‐deterministic and subject to version updates, exact replication with different model versions or API updates cannot be guaranteed; this is acknowledged as a limitation.

Blinding and Measurement Instruments

The 10 discharge instruction texts generated in this study (5 generated by nurses and 5 by AI) were uniformly formatted by research assistants (stripping original formatting and standardizing font, font size and paragraph spacing) and randomly coded (e.g., A1, B1). Double‐blind method was maintained: neither evaluators nor data analysts were aware of whether a given text originated from AI or a registered nurse.

Evaluation tasks were distributed according to the professional requirements of each dimension. A panel of medical experts assessed accuracy, while a patient group assessed empathy and readability. The measurement tools for each dimension are described below.

Accuracy

To comprehensively assess the reliability of medical information, the expert panel used a scoring tool based on the following two dimensions. The Modified Global Quality Scale (GQS) was used to evaluate clinical accuracy (Bernard et al. 2007). This scale ranges from 1 to 5 points (1 point = contains fatal errors or is severely misleading; 3 points = generally correct but omits key details; 5 points = information is extremely accurate, comprehensive, and highly consistent with current specialty care guidelines). The content validity index (S‐CVI) of this modified scale is 0.92. Experts must specifically review all assessed texts for fabricated medical information (i.e., ‘medical hallucinations’ commonly found in AI contexts, such as inventing nonexistent medications or incorrect dosages). Additionally, a binary forced‐choice method is employed. Based on extensive clinical experience, experts must answer: ‘If a patient fully follows the home care instructions in this text, would it lead to mild, moderate, or severe adverse clinical events?’ If the answer is ‘yes’, experts are required to specifically identify the risk paragraphs that trigger the red flag warning.

Empathy

This study utilized the Consultation and Relational Empathy (CARE) Measure (Mercer 2004). The original CARE scale is widely used to assess healthcare provider empathy in face‐to‐face clinical settings. For this study, we extracted and adapted core items designed to evaluate written communication, covering key aspects of emotional empathy (e.g., ‘Made me feel cared for’, ‘Showing care and compassion’). The scale was streamlined to a total of 5 items, using a 1–5‐point Likert scale (total score range: 5–25 points). In a preliminary pilot phase (N = 15 patients), the adapted scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.89), supporting its use in the main study.

Readability

Given the variations in health literacy among patient populations, this study employed a dual‐verification approach combining computer algorithms with patients' subjective perceptions.

For the objective component, natural language processing (NLP) was performed using Python (version 3.9) and the jieba Chinese word segmentation library. A series of linguistic and structural features were extracted from each text (see Table S2 for the complete metric set). Three core parameters were selected for the main analysis: (1) Average Sentence Length; (2) Medical Terminology Density: calculated by comparing the text against the *List of Common Words in Modern Chinese* to determine the proportion of rare medical terms in the full text; (3) a Chinese reading comprehension difficulty index based on word frequency (a higher index indicates a higher level of education required to comprehend the text).

For the subjective component, the Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool (PEMAT‐P) (Shoemaker et al. 2014) was employed, which evaluates two independent dimensions: ‘Understandability (17 items)’ and ‘Actionability (7 items)’. Patients were asked to assess whether sentences were easy to understand and whether specific action instructions were provided. Strictly following the scoring guidelines of the U.S. Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), and after excluding ‘Not Applicable (N/A)’ items, the scores for both dimensions were converted to percentages ranging from 0% to 100%; a higher percentage indicates that the material is easier to understand and act upon.

Sample Size Calculation and Data Collection

Given that this study employs a paired design to compare score differences between AI and RN, a prior power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1 software. Assuming a medium effect size (Cohen's d = 0.5), α = 0.05, and a power of 0.80 (1 − β), calculations indicated that at least 34 patients were required per group. Ultimately, 40 patients were recruited to account for a 15% dropout or invalid questionnaire rate. The size of the medical expert panel (N = 15) was based on the standard panel size for medical intervention evaluation studies to ensure the reliability of the evaluation and a multidisciplinary perspective.

It should be noted that the patient sample was recruited via convenience sampling from a single tertiary hospital rather than through random selection from a defined population. Consequently, the power analysis serves to determine the minimum number of raters needed to detect a meaningful paired difference with adequate statistical power, rather than to support inferences about population‐level prevalence or generalizability. The implications of this sampling approach for external validity are discussed in the Limitations section.

Data collection was conducted via a secure online survey platform (e.g., WJX). The system utilized a randomization module to scramble the presentation order of scenarios and A/B texts to eliminate order effects. Two attention check questions were covertly inserted into the questionnaire (e.g., ‘To confirm that you are reading carefully, please select option 2 for this question’). Questionnaires that failed the attention check were deemed invalid and excluded from the data. At the end of the questionnaire, evaluators were asked to guess for each text: ‘Do you think this guidance was written by a registered nurse or by AI?’ This served to verify the effectiveness of the blind method and the degree of anthropomorphism of the AI.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed by a member of the research team with formal training in biostatistics, with results independently verified by a second researcher. Data were double‐entered and cross‐checked. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 software, with a significance level set at a two‐tailed p < 0.05. Invalid questionnaires that were incomplete or failed the attention test were directly excluded. For missing values in valid questionnaires, if the missing rate was < 5%, multiple imputation methods were used for data handling.

Continuous data (scores for each dimension) were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s) if they followed a normal distribution; otherwise, they were expressed as median and interquartile range (M, IQR). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Since AI‐generated text and nurse‐generated text in the same clinical scenario were scored back‐to‐back by the same group of evaluators, paired t‐tests or Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests were used to compare differences in scores across dimensions.

All tests for differences reported effect sizes (Cohen's d or r values) (0.2 = small, 0.5 = moderate, 0.8 = large). Differences in safety risks (proportion of red flags) were analysed using the McNemar test. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to assess inter‐rater reliability among multiple expert and patient raters; an ICC > 0.75 was considered to indicate good reliability.

Generalized Linear Mixed Models (GLMM) were constructed to investigate whether patient demographic characteristics influence understandability ratings of AI‐generated texts. The GLMM was selected for three reasons: (1) the data have a hierarchical structure, with multiple ratings nested within individual patients who each evaluated multiple vignettes; (2) random intercepts for participant ID account for individual baseline differences in rating behaviour, while random intercepts for vignette ID account for variability in inherent scenario difficulty; and (3) the model permits simultaneous estimation of fixed effects (age, education level, gender, participant role) while appropriately handling the non‐independence of repeated observations from the same rater. Age and educational level were incorporated as fixed‐effect covariates based on prior literature indicating their association with health literacy and digital health acceptance (McAnally and Hagger 2023; Norman and Skinner 2006).

For the objective NLP metrics (Panel D), which were computed across only five paired text samples, readers should note that these analyses should be interpreted as descriptive comparisons. Although statistical tests were performed for completeness, the small number of text pairs limits the robustness of inferential conclusions, and the reported p‐values should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the ethics committee. All registered nurses involved in drafting the text, as well as the medical experts and patients who participated in the scoring, were fully informed and signed electronic informed consent forms prior to the start of the study. All case scenarios used in the study are fictional or deeply de‐identified and do not pose any risk of privacy breaches involving real patients.

Results

Participant Characteristics and Data Quality Assessment

The study protocol and vignette‐based evaluation process are illustrated in Figure 1. Of the 40 patients and caregivers initially recruited, two were excluded for failing the embedded attention check; the final valid patient sample comprised 38 individuals (validity rate: 95.0%). Together with 15 medical experts and 5 registered nurses, the study included 58 valid participants in total (Table S1 presents the sociodemographic characteristics of each group). The patient group had a mean age of 55.4 ± 14.2 years; 63.2% were patients themselves, and 36.8% were primary caregivers. The expert panel (N = 15) included nursing managers (66.7%) and specialist attending physicians (33.3%), with a mean clinical experience of 16.8 ± 4.5 years.

Study flow diagram and vignette‐based evaluation process.

Inter‐rater reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients. Among the 15 experts evaluating accuracy, reliability was excellent (ICC = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.82–0.91). Among the 38 patients evaluating empathy and readability, agreement was good (ICC = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.73–0.85), indicating acceptable data reliability. In the blinding verification assessment administered at the end of the questionnaire, only 42.5% of patients correctly identified whether a text was written by AI or a nurse—a rate approaching chance level—supporting the effectiveness of the double‐blind procedure.

Accuracy

Overall, the AI's accuracy score was slightly higher than that of registered nurses (4.75 ± 0.32 vs. 4.48 ± 0.55, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.61). This advantage stemmed primarily from the AI's higher score on the comprehensiveness/no omissions dimension (4.88 vs. 4.12, p < 0.001).

On the clinical safety/no hallucinations dimension, however, nurses outperformed AI (4.90 ± 0.28 vs. 4.52 ± 0.61, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = −0.81). Using a binary forced‐choice method, experts identified 16.0% (12/75) ‘red flags’—errors that could lead to moderate‐to‐severe adverse events—in 16.0% (12/75) of AI‐generated text evaluations. Examples included incorrect medication dose inferences and absent warnings for specific contraindications.

By contrast, no red flags were identified in any nurse‐generated text (0/75). The McNemar test confirmed a statistically significant difference between groups in the incidence of critical safety errors (p = 0.002).

These findings should be interpreted in light of the limited number of clinical scenarios evaluated; whether the observed safety risk rate generalizes to a broader range of discharge conditions warrants further investigation.

Empathy

In assessments based on patients' subjective experiences, registered nurses demonstrated significantly higher emotional and communication scores (Table 1, Panel B; Figure 2). On the CARE Empathy Scale, the total scores for discharge instructions generated by nurses were higher than those generated by AI (22.1 ± 2.4 vs. 16.8 ± 2.6, p < 0.001), demonstrating a large effect size (Cohen's d = −2.05). Patients perceived the nurses' texts as better at ‘making them feel cared for’ (4.6 vs. 3.2) and ‘demonstrating empathy’ (4.7 vs. 3.1). Given that empathy was assessed through written text alone, these scores reflect perceived empathy in a static reading context and may not fully capture the dynamics of empathetic communication in live clinical encounters.

Readability

Patient‐assessed readability showed a clear advantage for nurse‐generated content (Table 1, Panel C). On the PEMAT‐P understandability subscale, nurses scored 89.6% ± 8.2% compared with 68.4% ± 12.5% for AI (p < 0.001). A similar pattern emerged for actionability—the extent to which the text provides clear behavioural guidance—where nurse‐generated instructions (92.5% ± 7.6%) again outperformed AI‐generated text (71.2% ± 14.3%, p < 0.001).

Natural Language Processing Validation

To validate the reasons behind patients' subjective ratings, this study extracted objective natural language processing features from the text using Python (Table 1, Panel D; Table S2). Because these metrics were computed across only five paired text samples per group, the following results should be interpreted as descriptive comparisons; the reported p‐values are provided for reference and should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size.

The analysis confirmed that AI‐generated text exhibits greater structural and syntactic complexity. Despite being subject to the same prompt length constraint, AI‐generated text was longer than that produced by nurses (684.2 vs. 595.4 words, p = 0.024). At the syntactic level, the average sentence length of AI‐generated text (24.6 words/sentence) was nearly double that of the nurses' text (12.8 words/sentence) (p = 0.003), and it employed a higher density of rare medical terminology (15.6‰ vs. 11.2‰, p = 0.015). The Chinese Reading Comprehension Difficulty Index, which reflects reading thresholds, indicated that AI‐generated text was more difficult (82.4 ± 6.5), whereas text produced by registered nurses was more accessible (45.6 ± 8.2); this difference was statistically and clinically significant (p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 5.01). Although these differences are large in magnitude, the limited number of text samples means that generalizability to other clinical scenarios remains to be confirmed.

The model results (marginal R 2 = 0.31, conditional R 2 = 0.58) suggest the potential existence of a digital divide. Age was a significant negative predictor (β = −0.58, p = 0.001), meaning that for every additional year of age, the average score for understanding the AI text decreased by 0.58 points. Conversely, educational attainment showed a positive effect: compared to those with a junior high school education or lower, evaluators with a high school/vocational school education (β = 11.2, p = 0.021) and a bachelor's degree (β = 18.65, p < 0.001) demonstrated higher acceptance of the AI text. Furthermore, the participant's role was an independent influencing factor: primary caregivers scored higher on the comprehension of complex AI texts than the patients themselves (β = 6.42, p = 0.045), while gender had no significant effect on the scores.

Although these associations are statistically significant, the moderate marginal R 2 (0.31) indicates that substantial variance in AI text acceptance remains unexplained by the demographic variables assessed, and the confidence intervals for the master's degree subgroup are wide due to limited representation (n = 2). These estimates should therefore be confirmed in larger, more educationally diverse samples.

Discussion

This study employed a double‐blind, cross‐over design to conduct a multidimensional comparison of the accuracy, empathy, and readability of generative AI and clinical registered nurses in the creation of discharge plans. Overall, the results reveal a notable tension: while AI demonstrates certain advantages in the comprehensiveness of information coverage, significant gaps remain between it and experienced registered nurses in terms of clinical safety, emotional communication, and the clarity of language.

Before examining the specific dimensional results, it is important to position this study's contribution within the existing evidence base. While several studies have compared AI with healthcare professionals in generating medical text, this study offers meaningful additional insights in four respects. First, the selection of registered nurses—rather than physicians—as the comparator directly addresses the question most relevant to clinical nursing: whether AI can replicate the communication competencies that nurses employ in discharge education. Prior work by Ayers et al. (2023) compared AI with physicians whose online responses were often brief and impersonal, making AI appear superior by contrast.

By using experienced discharge education nurses as the comparison standard, this study provides a more clinically meaningful benchmark. Second, the simultaneous evaluation of accuracy, empathy, and readability within a single study reveals the multidimensional nature of discharge communication—a holistic perspective consistent with nursing's patient‐centered philosophy that single‐dimension studies cannot capture. Third, the incorporation of patient evaluators alongside expert assessors provides the patient voice that is central to nursing practice but absent from most AI evaluation studies. Fourth, the identification of demographic moderators (age and education level) through GLMM analysis offers novel empirical evidence regarding the digital divide in AI acceptance—a finding with direct implications for equitable nursing care delivery. Collectively, these methodological choices enable this study to provide confirmatory evidence regarding AI's information comprehensiveness advantage while generating new insights regarding AI's limitations in the specific communicative competencies that define nursing practice.

In terms of accuracy, AI achieved higher scores for comprehensiveness and completeness than registered nurses. This finding aligns with work by Ayers et al., who reported that ChatGPT's responses to patient questions surpassed physicians' brief replies in both quality and information content (Ayers et al. 2023). One possible explanation is that large language models benefit from their vast training corpora, enabling them to systematically extract and integrate medical information from multiple sources, thereby demonstrating greater consistency in terms of information completeness.

However, this study also found a certain proportion of ‘red flags’ in the AI‐generated text, primarily involving inappropriate inferences regarding drug dosages or the omission of specific contraindications. This aligns with the ‘hallucination’ phenomenon in large language models summarized by Sallam in his systematic review, wherein models tend to generate content that appears reasonable but lacks factual basis (Sallam 2023). Ji et al. (2023) conducted an in‐depth analysis of the mechanisms underlying hallucinations, pointing out that the root cause of this phenomenon lies in the model's reliance on statistical probability to predict the next token, rather than reasoning based on medical causal logic. This implies that even if the model's output appears grammatically and structurally flawless, the factual accuracy of its content may still contain unpredictable biases.

Notably, the registered nurses in this study committed no critical safety errors. This likely reflects the clinical reasoning and situational judgement that nurses develop through sustained bedside practice—capacities that probability‐based language models currently lack. The finding supports the position advanced by Meskó and Topol that healthcare AI should always operate under human oversight rather than as an autonomous decision‐making endpoint (Meskó and Topol 2023). On the other hand, the relative shortcomings in comprehensiveness observed among nurses also warrant attention. This phenomenon may be related to time pressures and information overload in clinical practice. Rushton et al. noted that nurses in high‐intensity clinical environments often need to complete multiple tasks within a limited timeframe, which may compromise the completeness of discharge instructions (Schlak et al. 2022). This is precisely the area where AI, as a tool for verifying information completeness, could provide added value. The findings of this study may offer a reference framework for clinical practice, positioning AI as a tool to assist nurses in drafting routine documentation rather than as a replacement. It appears necessary for all AI‐generated medical content to undergo review by a professional before reaching the patient.

Regarding empathy, patients' emotional resonance with nurse‐generated text was substantially greater, and the effect size was large. At first glance, this finding appears to conflict with the results of Ayers et al., in which evaluators rated ChatGPT's responses as more empathetic than those of physicians (Ayers et al. 2023). However, there are key methodological differences between the two studies that may help explain this inconsistency. Ayers et al.'s study compared brief responses from AI and physicians on an online forum, where physicians' replies in that context were often extremely concise or even cold; in contrast, the control group in this study consisted of specialized nurses with extensive experience in discharge education, who inherently assume a significant role in providing emotional support to patients in their daily work. As Baek et al. noted, patient‐centered care—of which relational communication is a core component—is fundamentally dependent on the human dynamics of nursing practice (Baek et al. 2023). The emotional warmth reflected in the nurses' texts in this study may be a written projection of this implicit clinical experience. In contrast, AI's expressions of empathy rely more heavily on templated polite phrases (such as ‘Wishing you a speedy recovery’), and this superficial linguistic mimicry may struggle to be perceived by patients as genuine care. A systematic review published in 2024 also noted that large language models are currently better at simulating the form of empathy rather than conveying its substance (Sorin et al. 2024).

However, the AI's absolute empathy score (16.8/25, with an average of approximately 3.2/5.0 per item) was not at the bottom of the scale, but rather in the upper‐middle range. This hypothesis is indirectly supported by the blinded validation results of this study: only 42.5% of patients were able to correctly identify the source of the text, a rate close to that of random guessing. This suggests that AI‐generated text already possesses certain anthropomorphic characteristics in its surface form, although patients can still perceive differences from registered nurses in terms of deeper emotional experiences. Whether the AI's emotional expression capabilities will gradually narrow this gap through continuous model iteration and affective fine‐tuning for medical scenarios remains an open question worthy of ongoing investigation.

On readability, both objective NLP metrics and patients' subjective assessments converged on a consistent pattern: AI‐generated discharge instructions exhibited markedly greater syntactic complexity and terminology density than nurse‐authored counterparts. This finding is concordant with work by Shukla et al., who reported that ChatGPT‐generated patient education materials typically exceed the recommended sixth‐grade reading level (Shukla and Sun 2025). One possible explanation is that the training corpora of large language models contain a vast amount of academic literature and clinical guidelines, which naturally leads to outputs that tend to be formal and technical. In contrast, when writing discharge instructions, registered nurses appear to spontaneously employ communication strategies developed through long‐term health education practice—specifically, using short sentences, lists, and everyday language to convey key information. Effective discharge communication is not merely the transmission of information; it is a process that requires dynamic adaptation based on the patient's cognitive level.

It should be noted that, as a non‐alphabetic language, the factors contributing to reading difficulty in Chinese differ from those in English. Chinese lacks mature, standardized readability assessment tools similar to the Flesch–Kincaid score, and the visual complexity of Chinese characters and the polysemy of vocabulary pose additional challenges to readability assessment. Meng et al., in their discussion of the readability of health information in Chinese, pointed out that the applicability of existing Chinese readability formulas in medical contexts still requires further validation (Ji et al. 2021). Although the word‐frequency‐based Chinese reading difficulty index adopted in this study provides a valuable quantitative reference, the psychometric properties of this metric itself require further validation in a larger cohort of Chinese patients. Nevertheless, the NLP data from this study suggest that future optimizations of AI models for patient education scenarios should incorporate ‘lowering the reading threshold’ and ‘enhancing the actionable nature of instructions’ as core fine‐tuning objectives.

The findings regarding both AI's safety risks and nurses' empathic communication advantage gain additional clinical significance when considered in the context of patients with highly complex discharge needs. Polytrauma patients, for instance, often require prolonged hospitalization, coordinated multidisciplinary management including nursing care, rehabilitation planning, caregiver training, and social support before returning home (Biz et al. 2016). Discharge instructions for such patients extend far beyond standardized written recommendations; they require nurses to exercise substantial clinical judgement, to anticipate individual barriers to recovery, and to communicate with both patients and family caregivers in an empathetic, adaptive manner. In these high‐complexity scenarios, the safety risks identified in AI‐generated text (such as inappropriate medication dose inferences) could carry particularly severe consequences, and the emotional support needs of patients and caregivers far exceed what templated AI‐generated phrases can provide.

This context reinforces the position that AI‐generated discharge instructions should undergo rigorous professional review before delivery to patients, and that for complex cases, AI may be most appropriately positioned as an information completeness checklist for nurses rather than a primary text generator. Future studies should specifically evaluate AI performance in these high‐complexity discharge scenarios to determine the boundaries of safe AI application in nursing practice.

The GLMM analysis reveals a finding that merits attention at the policy level. Older patients and those with lower educational attainment demonstrated reduced comprehension and acceptance of AI‐generated text—a result consistent with McAnally et al.'s meta‐analysis linking health literacy to health outcomes (McAnally and Hagger 2023). Norman and Skinner (2006) had already warned in their conceptual framework of eHealth literacy that the promotion of digital health tools, if it ignores baseline differences in user literacy, may inadvertently widen rather than narrow health inequalities. In the Chinese clinical context, this issue may be particularly pronounced. A survey by Fu et al. (2025) on health literacy among middle‐aged and elderly Chinese adults revealed that health literacy levels are significantly lower in rural areas and among groups with lower educational attainment. This implies that if healthcare institutions directly provide AI‐generated discharge instructions to these groups without adequate adaptation, they may exacerbate rather than alleviate inequalities in information comprehension.

The observation that primary caregivers rated AI‐generated text more favourably than patients themselves suggests that involving family members in the information dissemination process may be a productive strategy, particularly for older adults or patients with cognitive decline. More broadly, the results indicate that medical AI deployment should not follow a uniform approach. Instead, healthcare systems should account for population heterogeneity and develop stratified, personalized health communication strategies that ensure technological advances benefit patients from diverse backgrounds equitably.

An important contextual consideration for interpreting these findings is that this study evaluated written discharge instructions generated under controlled experimental conditions rather than real bedside nurse–patient interactions. In actual clinical practice, discharge education is a dynamic, interactive process involving verbal communication, clarification of patient doubts, nonverbal cues such as facial expressions and tone of voice, emotional reassurance, and continuous adaptation to patient responses and comprehension levels (Tobiano et al. 2019). These interactional dimensions cannot be captured in text‐based evaluations. Consequently, the empathy and readability advantages demonstrated by nurses in this study may actually underestimate the full extent of nursing communication competencies in real‐world discharge encounters. Conversely, AI's performance in this study reflects an optimistic scenario, as AI‐generated text was not subjected to the real‐time questioning, clarification requests, and emotional dynamics that characterize actual discharge conversations. Future research should move beyond text comparison to evaluate AI‐assisted discharge processes in prospective clinical settings, measuring patient outcomes such as comprehension at discharge, medication adherence, and readmission rates rather than text quality alone.

Limitations

This study has the following limitations, which should be considered when interpreting the results. First, this study was conducted as a single‐centre trial, with all participants recruited from a Grade A tertiary hospital in Liaoning Province via convenience sampling. The non‐random sampling approach means that the patient evaluators may not be representative of the broader patient population, and the power analysis served to determine the minimum sample needed for paired comparisons rather than to support population‐level inference. The generalizability of the findings to primary care facilities, community health service centres, and regions with different cultural and linguistic backgrounds requires validation through future multicentre studies with probability‐based sampling. Second, this study developed five standardized, cross‐specialty scenarios. Although these covered common fields such as cardiology, endocrinology, obstetrics, gastrointestinal surgery and geriatrics, they did not encompass all clinical settings (e.g., psychiatry, paediatrics, palliative oncology and polytrauma rehabilitation). The limited number of scenarios means that the objective NLP analyses (Panel D) are based on a small number of text pairs and should be interpreted descriptively. Discharge instructions across different specialties may vary in terms of information complexity and emotional needs, and caution is required when generalizing the findings.

Third, this study tested only a single model (GPT‐4, gpt‐4‐0613). Given the rapid iteration in the field of large language models (such as the continuous emergence of new models like GPT‐4o, Claude 3.5 and Gemini), the conclusions of this study may be time‐sensitive. Future research should include cross‐comparisons of multiple models. Fourth, written assessments based on standardized scenarios differ fundamentally from bedside discharge education in real‐world hospital settings. The latter involves nonverbal communication dimensions such as tone of voice, facial expressions and body language—dimensions that cannot be captured by text‐based studies. Consequently, the empathy assessment results in this study may underestimate the advantages nurses possess in real‐world scenarios. Finally, although the sample size met the requirements of the a priori power analysis, only two patients (5.3%) held a master's degree or higher. This may have led to unstable estimates for this subgroup in the GLMM model, resulting in wide confidence intervals and a lack of statistical significance (p = 0.072). Future studies may consider expanding the sample size of the highly educated subgroup to obtain more precise estimates.

Implications for Nursing Practice

The findings of this study carry several practical implications for the integration of generative AI into nursing discharge education workflows.

First, AI is an information completeness tool. The demonstrated advantage of AI in generating comprehensive discharge information suggests that nurses can leverage AI as a drafting assistant to ensure that no critical elements (medication schedules, follow‐up appointments, warning signs) are inadvertently omitted during time‐pressured discharge encounters. In practice, this could involve nurses generating an AI draft as a starting template, then applying professional judgement to verify clinical accuracy, remove potentially unsafe content, and personalize the language.

Second, mandatory safety review by nurses. The 16.0% ‘red flag’ rate identified in AI‐generated text underscores that AI‐produced discharge content cannot be delivered directly to patients without professional review. Healthcare organizations implementing AI‐assisted discharge tools should establish protocols requiring nurse verification of all AI‐generated medical content, with particular attention to medication dosages, contraindications, and activity restrictions. Third, humanizing AI‐generated content. Given AI's significantly lower empathy scores, nurses play an irreplaceable role in adding empathetic, personalized language to AI‐generated drafts. Training programmes could equip nurses with skills to efficiently review and enhance AI drafts—preserving the information completeness while infusing the text with warmth, encouragement, and individualized care messages that reflect the nurse–patient relationship.

Fourth, readability adaptation for diverse populations. The finding that AI‐generated text exhibits excessive syntactic complexity and terminology density highlights the need for nurses to simplify AI‐generated content before delivery, particularly for older patients and those with lower educational attainment. Institutions could develop tiered delivery protocols: a comprehensive AI‐generated draft for the medical record, and a nurse‐adapted, plain‐language version for patient education. What's more, addressing the digital divide. The GLMM findings demonstrating reduced AI text acceptance among older and less‐educated patients have direct implications for equitable nursing care. When AI‐assisted discharge tools are deployed, nurses should assess individual patients' health literacy levels and provide additional verbal explanation, teach‐back verification, or caregiver involvement for vulnerable populations to ensure that technological innovation does not exacerbate health communication inequalities. Finally, the finding that caregivers demonstrated higher comprehension of AI‐generated text than patients themselves suggests that discharge education strategies for older adults or patients with cognitive decline should systematically involve family caregivers as information intermediaries—a practice that nurses are uniquely positioned to facilitate.

Conclusion

Through a double‐blind, vignette‐based study design, this research provides an empirical comparison of the multidimensional performance of generative AI and clinical registered nurses in generating written discharge instructions under controlled experimental conditions. The results suggest that AI demonstrates advantages in information comprehensiveness but carries clinically significant safety risks from ‘hallucinations’ that cannot be overlooked. In terms of empathy and readability, experienced registered nurses maintain a clear advantage, reflecting communication competencies developed through sustained clinical practice that current language models do not replicate.

Furthermore, patients' age and educational level significantly influence their acceptance of AI‐generated text, indicating that the deployment of AI‐assisted discharge tools must account for potential digital divide issues to ensure equitable care delivery.

Given current technological limitations, generative AI may be best positioned as an ‘intelligent drafting assistant’ for registered nurses rather than an independent patient communication tool. This positioning preserves the essential nursing role in ensuring clinical safety, providing empathetic communication, and adapting language to individual patient needs, while leveraging AI's capacity for comprehensive information aggregation.

Several directions for future research emerge from these findings. First, multi‐centre, multi‐model validation studies should be conducted to test the robustness and generalizability of these findings across diverse clinical settings and evolving AI platforms. Second, three‐arm comparative studies should evaluate ‘raw AI output’, ‘AI‐drafted and nurse‐edited output’, and ‘nurse‐only output’ to determine whether human‐AI collaboration can effectively balance information comprehensiveness with emotional warmth and plain‐language clarity—while also investigating whether nurses reliably identify and correct AI hallucinations during the editing process. Third, prospective clinical trials should move beyond text quality evaluation to measure patient outcomes including discharge comprehension, medication adherence, and readmission rates. Finally, for populations with lower health literacy, automated simplification algorithms or tiered delivery strategies for AI‐generated content should be developed and evaluated to ensure that the application of technology in nursing practice reduces—rather than exacerbates—health communication inequalities.
