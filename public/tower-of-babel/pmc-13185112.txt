Structured Electrodes Induce Local pH as a Primary Determinant of CO2 Reduction Selectivity
Authors: Mingxuan Zhu, Daniel R. Morphet, Yiming Wang, Zhifei Yan, Daniel G. Nocera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highly structured electrodes permit high operating currents to be achieved as well as enhanced selectivity, especially for the carbon dioxide reduction (CDR) reaction. An understanding of the origins of CDR selectivity is complicated by the intricate internal structure of high surface area electrodes, which may modify the microenvironment in which catalysis occurs. We report here enhanced faradaic efficiency of the reduction of CO2 to CO (FE­(CO)) on photolithographically fabricated Au microwire (MW) electrodes as compared with their planar counterpart. We demonstrate that this selectivity arises from the local pH gradient at the electrode–solution interface, as a result of the electrode microstructure. By employing confocal microscopy with a ratiometric pH sensor, we can construct in situ maps of the local pH at the electrode–solution interface. When normalized for local pH, the same dependence of FE­(CO) is observed for the fabricated MW and planar electrodes, and the selectivity is a direct result of the HCO3 –/CO3 2– equilibrium.

Electrocatalysis benefits from large current densities derived from high surface area electrodes per geometric area. Beyond this obvious enhancement, the added complexity of higher surface area electrodes arising from micro- and nanostructuring has been shown to improve the activity and selectivity of small molecule conversions , and in particular for the CO2 reduction (CDR) reaction. , The selectivity of the CDR reaction, as measured by the faradaic efficiency (FE) of the carbon product, is eroded when current is diverted to the parasitic hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) due to the varying pH dependencies of HER and CDR. − Electrode micro- and nanostructuring has been phenomenologically shown to mitigate HER and, in doing so, improve the selectivity of CDR. , The origins of this enhanced CDR selectivity have been ascribed to a variety of factors, including electric field-induced ion gradients, − thermal gradients, concentration gradients of substrates, and mass transport considerations. Additionally, the effect of pH at the electrode surface must be considered as an important parameter in determining CDR selectivity − as the reaction consumes protons and is thus governed by proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET). − Noting that the local pH may vary significantly from the bulk pH in electrocatalytic reactions, , measurement of how the local pH is affected by electrode structure would appear to be crucial to defining the impact of electrode micro- and nanostructuring on the selectivity of CDR reactions.

We now report the proclivity of microstructured electrodes to alter the local pH and consequently CDR selectivity. We examine the reduction of CO2 to CO on Au electrodes, as this CDR reaction has been thoroughly studied owing to an established chemistry that allows CO to be translated into liquid fuels by syngas processing. − Moreover, it has been established that structured Au electrodes can suppress the HER and consequently promote CDR with high selectivity. ,,− Photolithographic techniques are employed here to fabricate Au microwire (MW) arrays of varying roughness, determined by the height of the Au MW. By coupling confocal microscopy with a self-referencing pH dye, we define the local pH within the MW arrays and correlate it with the FE­(CO). As has been observed previously, the FE­(CO) is observed to indeed increase with the degree of electrode microstructuring. However, when normalized for local pH, there is no difference in CDR selectivity between the MW Au arrays as compared to a planar Au electrode, showing that the sole effect of the electrode microstructure on selectivity is due to increasing the local pH and thus suppressing the HER. This result highlights the need for measuring local pH in the interpretation of electrocatalytic reactions that involve PCET. Accordingly, we expect that the conclusions presented herein may extend to many other electrocatalytic reactions that have employed micro- and nanostructured electrodes.

Based on the SEM images for each electrode, roughness factors (RFs) were calculated to be 1, 1.45, and 2.23 for planar, MW-s, and MW- l Au electrodes, respectively (Table S1). We also employed electrochemical surface area (ECSA) measurements as a complementary determination of RF; the RFs of MW-s and MW- l were normalized to that of the planar Au electrode and determined to be 1.31(9) and 2.2(2), respectively (Table S1 and Figure S4). Three ECSA measurements, conducted on different regions of the same wafer (Figure S5), indicate a uniform structure across the entire wafer. Furthermore, the agreement between experimental and theoretical RF values indicates that the surface roughness of the electrodes is due to the geometric microstructure as opposed to roughness of the conformal coating.

To assess the impact of electrode microstructuring on CDR electrocatalysis, electrogenerated CO and H2 for each electrode were analytically quantified by a gas chromatograph (GC) interfaced to a custom designed electrochemical cell (Figure S6). Figure shows the FE­(CO) for the H2 and CO generation. Following CDR, SEM imaging confirmed that the Au-coated electrodes remained structurally intact (Figure S3). The bell-shaped FE­(CO)–potential curves in Figure are consistent with the characteristics of Au CDR electrocatalysis. , As indicated by the partial current density plots in Figure S7, at applied potentials (E appl) positive of −1.3 V, the current for CO generation increases with increasing overpotential, balanced by a shallower potential dependence of the HER current density, leading to the observed increase in FE­(CO) in Figure . At E appl more negative than −1.3 V, FE­(CO) decreases owing to the increased dominance of HER with increasing overpotential, coupled to possible limitations imposed by CO2 mass transport and the irreversible consumption of CO2 by reaction with OH–. These studies establish that increased structuring enhances FE­(CO), as reflected by the shift in the FE­(CO) curve and its maximum toward more positive potentials.

Figure A shows a schematic of the confocal microscope setup (Figure S8) used to image the pH within the MW array. A vertical scan of the cathode was taken between MWs to avoid blocking the confocal laser by the MWs. The imaging region was scanned from the base of the electrode to 56 μm above the electrode base (light gray rectangle in Figure A), thus encompassing regions within and above the MW array. The region in which pH measurements were recorded was from 1 to 4 μm (dark gray rectangle in Figure A) above the electrode base. Quantification of pH was achieved with the dye 6,8-dihydroxy-1,3-pyrenedisulfonic acid disodium salt (DHPDS), shown in Figure B. A ratiometric calibration curve (Figure B) was constructed as previously described by measuring the emission from the dye produced from exciting two distinct absorption peaks with intensities changing in opposite directions upon varying pH (Figure S10), as required for a ratiometric pH dye. ,, For the DHPDS calibration curve in Figure B, the excitation wavelengths were 405 and 473 nm and the emission detection window was between 490 and 590 nm. To examine differences arising from convection for the vertical (GC) and horizontal (confocal) cell configurations, chronoamperometry traces were recorded for each cell. The current density for the confocal cell was slightly reduced compared to that of the GC cell (Figure S9). This difference in current density between the two experiments translates to a small pH difference of 0.2 unit, with the GC cell exhibiting the higher ΔpH = 0.2.

With local pH measurements in hand, FE­(CO) was analyzed in light of the local pH. Figure C plots the FE­(CO) measured at E appl = −1.108 V (Figure ) versus the local pH measurement for the three electrodes. Figure extends this analysis to other potentials using the FE­(CO) data in Figure and the local pH measurements shown in Figure S11. FE­(CO) increases as local pH increases, and the selectivity for CDR is the same for the planar and the MW structured electrodes at a given local pH. Thus, local pH alone is sufficient to explain the increased FE­(CO) of the MW electrodes.

The FE­(CO) vs pH curve in Figure follows that of the HCO3 –/CO3 2– equilibrium. Though the midpoint of the HCO3 –/CO3 2– equilibrium is conventionally taken as 10.3, with increasing ionic strength, the equilibrium shifts to lower pH values with a midpoint of 9 observed for media of very high ionic strength (0.7 M). Within this limiting range, our midpoint value of 9.2 for solutions of 0.1 M ionic strength is reasonable for the HCO3 –/CO3 2– equilibrium. The midpoint value differs by 0.1 pH unit between the horizontal (GC) and vertical (confocal) electrode configurations (Figure S12). Noting that HCO3 – is the proton source for HER, , the suppression of HER and correspondent increase in FE­(CO) are simply determined by the pH dependence of the HCO3 –/CO3 2– equilibrium. Under our conditions of a large CO2-saturated electrolyte reservoir and forced convection, as we have previously described, the speciation of the equilibrium is controlled by hydration reactions of CO2 and acid–base reactions of carbonic acid and bicarbonate. In summary, our results demonstrate that increased electrode microstructuring determines the local pH that establishes the HCO3 –/CO3 2– equilibrium.

Disentangling the role of highly structured electrodes in electrocatalysis is helpful to advance next generation electrochemical device development. The results reported herein show that at modest overpotentials where water is not the proton donor of HER, CDR selectivity of microstructured Au electrodes is primarily due to the establishment of a local pH and that it is the HCO3 –/CO3 2– acid–base equilibrium that governs the FE­(CO) for CDR on Au electrodes. More complicated electrode architectures may affect CO2 mass transport and delivery, , thus altering the local pH and its coupling to the HCO3 –/CO3 2– equilibrium. Notwithstanding, the results reported herein highlight the need to quantify local pH in the interpretation of the activity and selectivity of electrocatalytic reactions when performed within the nano- and mesostructures of high surface area electrodes.
