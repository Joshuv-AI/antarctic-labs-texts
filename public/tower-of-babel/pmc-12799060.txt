INTRODUCTION

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a major global public health concern, driving chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), with an estimated 820,000 deaths annually[1]. Vietnam, as a hyper-endemic region, reports hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) prevalence above 8%, corresponding to nearly 10 million chronic carriers[2], highlighting the need for effective screening and management. HBsAg is the hallmark of active infection, whereas hepatitis B surface antibody (anti-HBs) indicates protective immunity, either from vaccination or resolved infection. Conventionally, HBsAg and anti-HBs are considered mutually exclusive, with successful seroconversion to anti-HBs serving as a key therapeutic goal in chronic HBV management. However, rare cases demonstrate simultaneous positivity of HBsAg and anti-HBs, a phenomenon termed a serological paradox. The clinical implications of this remain uncertain, with potential explanations including transient seroconversion, assay cross-reactivity, or circulating viral immune escape variants that evade antibody neutralization[3]. Emerging evidence suggests that such cases may be associated with advanced liver disease or high viral replication, underscoring the need for careful evaluation[4,5]. This article reports a case of a 54-year-old male with chronic HBV infection and compensated cirrhosis exhibiting concurrent HBsAg and high-titer anti-HBs positivity, aiming to provide insights into its clinical significance, underlying mechanisms, and implications for disease monitoring and management in high-risk patients.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 54-year-old male farmer presented with persistent bloating and dyspepsia. He had no history of alcohol abuse, hepatotoxic drug intake, or substance use. Physical examination was unremarkable, without jaundice. Initial laboratory screening excluded hepatitis C virus (HCV) co-infection (anti-HCV S/Co 0.2). Serological testing revealed a paradoxical profile: highly reactive HBsAg (S/Co 1438.5) alongside a high-titre anti-HBs (136.6 mIU/ml). Virological assessment showed elevated HBV deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) (4.3 ×106 copies/ml), indicating active viral replication. Liver enzymes were mildly elevated (aspartate aminotransferase 70.8 U/l; alanine aminotransferase 78.3 U/l), Gamma-glutamyl transferase was elevated (114 U/l), platelets were reduced (90 ×109/l), and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) was markedly increased (104 ng/ml), suggesting advanced liver pathology.

Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated regional fatty liver infiltration, and multiple hypoechoic hepatic nodules. Transient elastography confirmed significant fibrosis (F4), consistent with compensated cirrhosis (Fig. 1).

Based on the combination of serological paradox, high viral load, transaminitis, and imaging-confirmed cirrhosis, a diagnosis of active-phase chronic hepatitis B with compensated cirrhosis was established. Treatment with tenofovir 300 mg daily was initiated, with routine outpatient monitoring planned to assess viral suppression, liver function, and surveillance for hepatocellular carcinoma. After 1 month of treatment, the patient’s symptoms improved, levels of liver enzymes decreased slightly but remained unstable, and the viral load had not yet been reassessed. The patient continued regular antiviral therapy. After 4 months of treatment, the patient experienced complete resolution of clinical symptoms. Laboratory tests showed more stable liver function and enzymes, a slight improvement in platelet count, and a marked reduction in HBV DNA viral load. AFP returned to within the normal range, and abdominal ultrasound revealed no lesions suggestive of hepatocellular carcinoma. DISCUSSION

This case highlights the rare serological paradox of simultaneous HBsAg and anti-HBs positivity in a patient with active chronic hepatitis B and compensated cirrhosis. While HBsAg and anti-HBs are typically mutually exclusive, recent reports indicate that coexistence, although uncommon (1–7% prevalence)[6,7], may occur more frequently in patients with high viral loads or specific S gene mutations[5]. Our patient had high HBV DNA (4.3 ×106 copies/ml), elevated transaminases, thrombocytopenia, and markedly raised AFP, underscoring a high-risk phenotype for advanced liver disease and hepatocellular carcinoma.

The underlying mechanism likely involves viral immune escape, where S gene mutations alter HBsAg antigenicity, rendering anti-HBs antibodies non-neutralizing. Partial host immune dysfunction may further permit active viral replication despite robust antibody production[8]. The coexistence of HBsAg/anti-HBs in this context should not be interpreted as benign or indicative of imminent seroclearance; rather, it may serve as a biomarker of complex, uncontrolled infection requiring intensive monitoring.

Concurrent HBsAg and anti-HBs positivity in chronic hepatitis B indicates complex immune changes and potential liver disease progression. Recent studies have shown that patients with this serological profile often have higher HBV DNA loads, prolonged elevation of liver enzymes, and increased rates of cirrhosis or HCC compared with those who are positive for HBsAg alone[9,10].

Limitations of this report include single-patient observation, and short follow-up, preventing definitive conclusions regarding prognosis or treatment response. Nonetheless, the paradoxical simultaneous positivity of HBsAg and anti-HBs represents a complex serological phenomenon in chronic hepatitis B. This case report illustrates that this phenotype can be strongly correlated with high-risk clinical parameters, including high viral replication and advanced liver disease (compensated cirrhosis). The underlying pathology is highly suspected to involve the development of viral immune escape mutants or severe immunological dysfunction, which prevents effective antibody-mediated viral clearance. Identification of this unusual serological pattern mandates heightened clinical suspicion and intensive follow-up to effectively manage the associated significant risks of liver disease progression and malignant transformation.
