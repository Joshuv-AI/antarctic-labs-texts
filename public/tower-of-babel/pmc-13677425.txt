An interim analysis of acute treatment adverse events and patient reported quality of life for functional imaging guided salivary gland sparing radiation therapy using diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Katelyn Cahill, Catriona Hargrave, Kane Miller, Alexandria Leppien, Claudia Chapple, Jasmine Meaghan, Michelle Phan, Mark Denham, Patrick O'Connor, Alan Ho, David Hogan, Joanne Castelli, Zack Y. Shan, Myo Min
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeEvolving evidence suggests that regional sparing of the parotid gland during radiation therapy could reduce radiation-induced xerostomia in head and neck cancer. This interim analysis investigates the functional relationship between the diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (DW-MRI) derived parotid gland biological at-risk volume (BRV) dose and acute adverse events, including patient-reported quality of life (QoL).Materials and methodsTwenty-four head and neck cancer patients underwent MRI-simulation to delineate parotid gland BRVs using DW-MRI derived apparent diffusion coefficient measurements. Dose was optimised, sparing the parotid gland BRV. Clinician-reported acute adverse events (xerostomia, dysphagia, oral pain) were recorded and patient-reported QoL was assessed using University of Washington QoL Questionnaire v4.1 with Radiation Therapy Oncology Group modification. Adverse events were quantified using area under the curve and time to side effect peaks were calculated and analysed.ResultsA moderate positive correlation trend was observed between contralateral BRV mean dose and xerostomia area under the curve, although this did not reach statistical significance (r = 0.34, p = 0.13). There was a strong relationship between the contralateral BRV sector and days to peak dysphagia (r = 0.56, p = 0.005). Low- and high-dose grouped analysis revealed a trend toward significance for time to peak xerostomia (p = 0.059). There were no significant associations with the ipsilateral parotid gland BRVs.ConclusionFindings suggest a potential functional-dose relationship between the contralateral BRV mean dose and acute xerostomia. Ongoing follow-up will determine whether the suggestive trends observed in the acute phase strengthen with long-term recovery time.

Introduction

Radiation Therapy (RT) plays a pivotal role in the definitive management of locoregionally advanced squamous cell carcinoma of the head and neck (HN) [1]. As tumour control and survival improve, efforts increasingly focus on preventing and managing long-term radiation effects in head and neck cancer (HNC) survivors [2]. This cohort commonly experiences side effects including xerostomia, sticky saliva, dysphagia, speech difficulty and HN pain [3]. These treatment-related complications are closely linked with reduced quality of life and treatment decision regret [4].

Volumetric modulated arc radiotherapy (VMAT) has transformed modern RT; this has been particularly important in complex sites such as the HN [5]. This has resulted in the development of parotid-sparing techniques which are essential in minimising dose, and consequently, xerostomia side effects [6]. Despite advanced techniques, approximately 40% of HNC patients that receive RT continue to experience irreversible parotid gland (PG) damage [7], [8]. Such patient outcomes suggest that anatomical sparing alone may not be sufficient to preserve function.

Evidence indicates heterogeneous radiosensitivity within the PG, providing biological justification to move beyond PG mean dose constraints [9], [10], [11], [12]. A subsequent investigation has reported that major PG ducts contain an abundance of stem cells, critical to its radiation response. Irradiation of this stem-cell-rich (SCR) region reduces salivary function and impairs repair and regeneration, suggesting that damage to this region compromises PG recovery following RT [13]. Stem-cell sparing RT has been introduced clinically [14], [15] with reports of split volume and superficial parotid-sparing optimisation techniques reporting promising xerostomia reduction [16], [17], [18]. These findings support the rationale of using biological subvolumes for parotid-sparing RT and may contribute to further reductions in xerostomia.

Building on this evidence, there is a strong rationale to investigate whether the SCR region can be identified using functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). The integration of MRI in RT is advancing through functional imaging techniques such as diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (DW-MRI). DW-MRI can provide personalised biological information at a cellular level, displaying the restriction of water diffusion corresponding to a highly cellular environment and reduced extracellular space [19]. Quantification using the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) demonstrates that a higher cellular density is associated with a lower voxel-based ADC value [20]. Due to the non-uniform distribution of stem cells in the PG [13], it is hypothesised that regions of low ADC may correspond to the SCR region. This approach has been used to delineate a PG biological at-risk volume (BRV), supporting investigations into functional imaging-guided salivary-gland sparing RT (FIGS-RT) [21].

Preliminary work within our group demonstrated the feasibility of incorporating the PG BRV into treatment planning. The BRV derived from the 30th ADC percentile was a more suitable sparing volume than the 20th, achieving greater PG dose reduction while maintaining target coverage and plan quality. This study translates prior findings to evaluate the impact of this novel technique. Therefore, the aim of this prospective pilot study was to assess whether dose reduction to the PG BRV improves adverse events and patient-reported quality of life (QoL). This interim analysis provides preliminary insights into acute treatment-related side effects and QoL.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

Eligible FIGS-RT patients were consented for radical or high dose palliative RT for HNC, with or without chemotherapy and an intent to spare the PGs. Patients were required to be ≥18 years of age and have no contraindications to MRI, to allow for MRI Simulation (MRI-Sim). Exclusion criteria included previous RT to the PG and pre-existing loss of salivary function, as determined from the patient's clinical history. Local human research ethics committee approval was granted for this study (HREC/2019/QPCH/57691). Twenty-four patients were included in the final analysis.

Planning CT and MR simulation acquisition

A planning computed tomography (CT) scan was acquired on a SOMATOM CT scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) at 120 kV and 2 mm slice thickness. Patients were positioned in a 9-point HN thermoplastic mask with head, neck and shoulder vac-bag. All MRI-Sim images were acquired on a 1.5 Tesla MAGNETOM Aera (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). Patients were scanned on the INSIGHT™ MR flat-top overlay (CQ Medical, Avondale, USA) in their thermoplastic mask with a body 18-channel long flex coil attached to the MRI bridge. Two large 4-channel flex coils were positioned around the INSIGHT™ MRI head coil holder and a 32-channel spine coil [22]. T2-weighted and DW-MRI sequences were acquired using optimised RT specific MRI-Sim protocols. The MRI acquisition parameters are reported in Supplementary Table 1. Geometric distortions were minimised using B0 shimming during DW-MRI application and the ADC map was automatically generated on the scanner console from a voxel-wise linear regression across the log-transformed signal of all three b-values.

Parotid gland biological at-risk volume (BRV) delineation

Ipsilateral and contralateral PGs were delineated on the registered T2-weighted image and transferred to the ADC map. Following the previously described methodology [21], the BRV30 was defined as the subvolume derived from voxels below the 30th percentile of the ADC distribution within each PG (See Supplementary Fig. 1). Each PG was split into eight anatomical sectors using a common intersection point [21]. This approach was not intended to define functional subvolumes, but to evaluate the spatial distribution of the BRV30 within each PG. Therefore, the BRV30 was divided into the following regions: SupAnteroLateral, SupPosteroLateral, SupAnteroMedial, SupPosteroMedial, InfAnteroLateral, InfPosteroLateral, InfAnteroMedial, and InfPosteroMedial. The anatomical sector with the largest percentage distribution of BRV30 is denoted as the BRV30 sector. All PG and BRV contours were exported to Monaco v6.1.2 (Elekta AB, Stockholm, Sweden) with the planning CT and ADC map. Target volumes were completed by the attending RO as per international guidelines [23], [24], [25] and HN eviQ protocols [26].

Radiation therapy dose optimisation and treatment delivery

VMAT plans were optimised using the Monte Carlo algorithm in Monaco using a simultaneous integrated boost technique. Dose-volume histogram (DVH) constraints for HN organs of interest were defined according to guidelines [26]. This included a ≤ 26 Gy mean planning objective for all PG and BRV contours (Parotid_L, Parotid_R, Parotid_L_BRV30, Parotid_R_BRV30, Parotid_L_sector_BRV30, Parotid_R_sector_BRV30). Target coverage was prioritised during optimisation, with BRVs used as additional optimisation structures alongside conventional PG sparing objectives. PlanIQ™ (Sun Nuclear Corporation, Melbourne, FL, USA) was used to assess dose constraint feasibility for organs of interest. Two full arcs were used for bilateral disease and two 210-degree partial arcs for unilateral. Patient-specific quality assurance was performed with an Elekta iView electronic portal imaging device using a 3%/2 mm gamma criterion. A pre-treatment cone beam CT (CBCT) was acquired, with tumour volumes prioritised during online matching. External-contour changes and the PGs were also reviewed and local procedures were followed when consistent changes were identified.

Adverse event assessment

A clinician-led evaluation was performed using Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0 for xerostomia, dysphagia and oral pain. Assessments were collected at baseline (before RT), weekly from week two and at follow-up appointments one and three months after RT. Long-term follow-up is planned for five years post-treatment. For analysis, the maximum grade was recorded and the time to peak adverse event (days) was calculated. The week-to-week grade change relative to the preceding assessment was documented, allowing the trajectory of side effects to be characterised. Area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each patient using the trapezoidal rule. This was used as an exploratory metric to quantify the cumulative burden of treatment-related adverse events during treatment and throughout the overall acute period, including up to three-months follow-up. Longitudinal CTCAE scores were plotted against time where the x-axis represented the number of days from the start of RT and the y-axis represented the CTCAE grade.

Head and neck patient-reported quality of life questionnaire

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) were assessed using The University of Washington Quality of Life (UW-QOL v4.1) Questionnaire, with Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) modifications. This was self-administered electronically with no input from trial co-ordinators. The questionnaire was completed at baseline, during week three and the final week of treatment, followed by assessments at one and three months after treatment. Scores for each response were scaled according to the guideline for the UW-QOL questionnaire where 0 was the worst possible response and 100 was the best. Totals were calculated for each domain as well as all additional general questions, where a higher score was indicative of a better outcome.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise plan quality, DVH, organ of interest metrics, acute adverse events and QoL scores. Mean dose to the whole PG, BRV30 and the anatomical sector BRV30 for the ipsilateral and contralateral PGs were further examined. A total of 72 statistical tests were performed, comprising 36 correlation analyses and 36 two-sample t-tests across six PG contours and the three adverse event symptoms. Each was evaluated using two outcome measures (overall AUC and time to peak adverse event). To assess the relationship between PG dose and adverse events, the mean PG dose was analysed as continuous and grouped variables. Continuous outcomes (overall AUC and time to peak adverse event) were analysed using Pearson or Spearman correlation based on Shapiro-Wilk normality testing. Pearson correlation with linear regression was applied when both variables were normally distributed, otherwise Spearman's Rank-Order correlation was used. Correlation coefficients, p-values and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported for Pearson correlations, with significance defined as p < 0.05. For the grouped analysis, the mean dose to each PG contour was classified as high or low based on the median. Two-sample t-tests compared high and low PG dose groups for adverse event AUC, days to peak and PROMs. Statistical data analysis was performed in RStudio Version 4.3.3 [27].

Parotid gland BRV analysis

The 30th percentile ADC value (reported in ×10−3 mm2/s) was extracted for each PG for all patients. The mean ADC values were 0.855 ± 0.138 (Range 0.674–1.213) for the ipsilateral PG and 0.848 ± 0.155 (Range 0.580–1.124) for the contralateral PG. The mean volume of the ipsilateral and contralateral BRV was 17.6 cm3 and 17.5 cm3, occupying a mean percentage of 64.3% and 64.8% for each whole PG respectively. The largest BRV distribution was observed in the SupAnteroLat sector for both ipsilateral and contralateral PGs, occurring in 70.83% and 37.5% of patients respectively. Dosimetry analysis

The prescribed dose varied across patients with a maximum of 70 Gy in 35 fractions and a minimum of 60 Gy in 30 fractions with elective nodal dose ranging from 54 to 56 Gy. The high dose mean PTV coverage (D98%) was 98.4% ± 1.2% (range 95.9–99.9%). Four patients were prescribed an intermediate dose level (63 Gy) where the mean PTV coverage was 95.6% ± 5.3% (range 88.3–99.6%) and the low dose (54–56 Gy) mean PTV coverage was 97.5% ± 1.0% (range 95.0–99.0%). The ipsilateral BRV30 received a mean dose 3.1 Gy less than the whole ipsilateral PG, with a further 10.1 Gy reduction for the ipsilateral BRV30 sector. Reductions were also seen across the contralateral PG where the BRV30 and the BRV30 sector were 1.4 Gy and 4.7 Gy lower than the whole PG, respectively. A summary of all dose, PG and other organ of interest statistics is provided in Table 2. Reduced external contour separation was noted in four cases due to weight loss. CTCAE assessment

Analysis of the acute CTCAE assessments was performed for xerostomia, dysphagia and oral pain. AUC values represent cumulative side effects over the acute study period (overall) and during RT (treatment). All twenty-four patients were assessed at baseline and for most treatment and follow-up time points, reduced sample sizes were observed at week four (n = 22), week six (n = 22), 1-month follow-up (n = 23) and 3-month follow-up (n = 22). To preserve the study size, the data were analysed using a last-observation-carried-forward approach in week 6 for two separate patients. Of the two patients with missing three-month follow-up assessments, one was recorded outside of the allowable time range and the other was not available as the patient passed away prior to this time. The median peak clinician-reported CTCAE assessment was grade two for xerostomia, grade three for dysphagia and grade two for oral pain. Grade two xerostomia was observed in 50% of patients at three-months follow-up. The mean xerostomia AUC from baseline to the final week of RT was 45.5 ± 32.3. When extended to include up to the three-month follow-up, the overall mean AUC increased to 202.1 ± 78.3. The increase in overall AUC indicates persistence of xerostomia beyond the completion of RT; however the variation seen in the standard deviation shows considerable inter-patient variability in the severity and duration of xerostomia.

Parotid mean dose analysis

No statistically significant correlations were observed between the mean dose to the PG contours and acute adverse events. Although a moderate association was suggested for xerostomia, with a weak, non-significant positive trend for the contralateral PG mean dose (r = 0.34; p = 0.106; 95% CI = −0.08 to 0.65) and the contralateral BRV30 mean dose (r = 0.32; p = 0.13; 95% CI = −0.10 to 0.64) (Fig. 2). Given the modest cohort size and feasibility design of this pilot study, individual observations may have disproportionately influenced some correlation analyses, particularly at a higher dose range. The fitted linear regression model demonstrated a positive dose-response relationship, estimating xerostomia AUC increasing by approximately 3 units per 1 Gy increase in mean dose. This reflects the numerical slope of the model rather than a statistically significant dose-response relationship. A significant association was noted between the contralateral BRV30 sector mean dose and time to dysphagia peak (r = 0.56; p = 0.005), indicating that a higher dose to this region was associated with a later peak in dysphagia severity. There were no other correlations between dose and time to peak side effects.

Low and high dose grouped data analysis

Normal distribution of the grouped high and low mean dose data was confirmed for all acute events, as such, t-tests were performed between groups. There were no statistically significant differences found, although a trend was observed for xerostomia and the contralateral PG (p = 0.078). Other endpoints did not reveal any significant associations with dose.

Higher contralateral BRV30 sector dose was significantly associated with increased days to dysphagia peak (p = 0.008). Similar, non-significant associations were observed for contralateral BRV30 sector for xerostomia peak (p = 0.059) and ipsilateral BRV30 sector mean dose for oral pain (p = 0.092). Discussion

This interim analysis examined the relationship between the PG BRV mean dose and acute outcomes, including patient-reported QoL. As the initial phase of the ongoing FIGS-RT pilot study, these findings provide early insight into potential dose-response relationships within the PG. Although statistically significant associations were not observed for xerostomia AUC with any BRV30 contours, emerging trends suggest the importance of continued follow-up to assess whether dose-related effects resolve over time.

The potential relationship between the contralateral PG regions (whole gland and BRV contours) and acute xerostomia reiterates the importance of sparing the contralateral side during dose optimisation. The suggestive trends observed support the concept of preserving the microstructure and functional integrity of the unaffected contralateral PG, indicating that a higher mean dose to the contralateral BRV30 may impair saliva production. Similarly, patient-reported QoL scores show a significant association with contralateral BRV30 mean dose, highlighting a prominent acute dose-QoL relationship.

The persistence of xerostomia three-months after RT is consistent with previously reported parotid-sparing RT outcomes [7]. This indicates that side effects persist during early post-treatment recovery despite personalised parotid-sparing. Patients receiving higher contralateral BRV30 sector dose demonstrated an indication toward delayed dysphagia (significant) and xerostomia (weak) peaks. This region is thought to be highly radiosensitive; therefore, the low-dose category was expected to demonstrate longer time to peak. This suggests a complex dose-adverse event relationship is being observed. An explanation is that the BRV30 sector retains some regenerative capacity, prolonging symptoms and delaying the xerostomia peak. Alternatively, delayed peaks may reflect compensation from minor salivary glands or individual differences in radiosensitivity rather than true BRV30 recovery; longitudinal data will clarify this. The shared biological mechanisms of xerostomia and dysphagia are evident. Acute presentations of both predict late-stage dysphagia [28], and xerostomia significantly influences perceived dysphagia [29], [30]. The acute data presented are consistent with these reports and demonstrate an association with dose to the contralateral BRV30 sector and a delayed peak in both endpoints. This suggests that dose-dependent functional changes in this region could predict the trajectory of side effects, allowing for earlier intervention.

The ipsilateral PG typically receives higher dose to preserve target coverage, leading to earlier functional decline, whereas the contralateral is generally spared due to its distance from the high dose volume. The results of this study indicate functional, partial sparing could lead to slower cumulative damage which is evident with the association seen within the contralateral BRV30 sector. Therefore, regional dose effects may influence the magnitude and timing of side effects. A similar finding was reported in a study sparing the PG SCR region where dose to the contralateral SCR region was a strong predictor at 12 and 24-months after RT [15]. These outcomes suggest this interim analysis will be better validated with continued long-term follow-up, including classification of any relationship between the DW-MRI derived BRV and the previously described SCR region.

This study has several limitations that must be acknowledged. The small sample size reduces the statistical power to detect significant associations; further studies with larger cohorts are necessary. Additionally, mid-treatment PG shrinkage correlates with the planning mean dose [31]. Although PG shrinkage evaluation was not included, it is acknowledged that treatment-related volumetric changes may alter delivered dose and geometry of the BRV and therefore affect the observed relationships. This represents an important area for future work and will be considered in subsequent studies. Results also suggest a disconnect between subjective experience and physiological function, supporting the use of saliva flow measures alongside patient-reported outcomes to better characterise the biological relationships explored in this study. Salivary stimulation is commonly used in studies evaluating the impact of PG function [15], [20], [32], [33], [34].

Continued long-term follow-up will be essential to determine any association with sparing the BRV30 of the PG and functional recovery. Based on similar studies, it is expected that xerostomia will continue to recover up to two years after RT [18]. Similarly, Steenbakkers et al. reported that the association between dose to the SCR region and xerostomia increased over time [15]. This supports the continuation of this study whereby future directions include the collection of long-term adverse events and patient-reported QoL. This pilot study provides a foundation for a phase II/III or multi-centre trial and offers preliminary insight into acute adverse events and QoL. These results will guide future study designs to ensure adequate power and validate preliminary associations.

To conclude, the observed correlations in the acute phase of the FIGS-RT study did not demonstrate any consistent associations. A non-significant, positive trend was observed between high contralateral BRV30 mean dose and increased acute xerostomia and significantly lower QoL three months after RT. This exploratory work suggests a potential dose-response relationship within the functionally critical region of the contralateral PG. Long-term follow-up will determine whether the suggestive trends observed strengthen with continued recovery time.
