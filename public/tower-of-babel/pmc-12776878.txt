Timing Matters: Caution and Opportunity in Early Vasopressin for Septic Shock
Authors: Habib Md R Karim, Ankur Khandelwal, Bheemas Atlapure, Azeez Aspari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHow to cite this article: Karim HMR, Khandelwal A, Atlapure B, Aspari A. Timing Matters: Caution and Opportunity in Early Vasopressin for Septic Shock. Indian J Crit Care Med 2025;29(12):1058.Keywords: Distributive shock, Early sepsis, ICU outcomes, Vasopressin

Dear Editor,

We read Bhattacharjee et al.'s systematic review and meta-analysis (SRMA) exploring the influence of vasopressin initiation timing on outcomes in septic shock patients with interest.1 For all critical care physicians, the question—when to start vasopressin is crucial. Thus, the SRMA exploring the little-known aspects of septic shock management is commendable. The SRMA is methodologically robust, focused on including the studies. Further, trial sequential analysis (TSA) increased statistical reliability. The pooled findings of early (<6 hours) vasopressor initiation on intensive care unit (ICU) mortality, hospital stay, and need for renal replacement therapy (RRT) are also clinically encouraging for adoption in practice. Although these results resonate with the vasopressin-sparing benefits hinted at in the VASST trial and the renal protection suggested by the VANISH trial; a few aspects still merit careful interpretation before practice recommendations.2,3

First, only one included study was a randomized controlled trial (RCT); the remainder were retrospective analyses with moderate-to-serious risk of bias, making confounding by indication almost inevitable. Second, the 6-hour “early” threshold, though pragmatic, remains arbitrary. The definition of timing varied—from shock onset to first norepinephrine administration or hospital presentation—each reflecting distinct physiological and logistical contexts. Early vasopressin started in the Emergency Department may differ profoundly in impact and safety from early use within 6 hours of ICU admission.

Third, safety data are limited.4 Vasopressin is a strong vasoconstrictor, and some clinically relevant complications, such as digital or mesenteric ischemia, were inconsistently reported and remain to be investigated. Furthermore, although the SRMA met conventional significance, the TSA line indicates that the evidence for benefits remains inconclusive. These findings reiterate the similar outcomes and limitations to a previous analysis with 23 studies (only 2 RCTs) conducted in 2023.5

Therefore, we believe that future research should focus on: (A) adequately powered pragmatic RCTs stratified by both time since shock onset and norepinephrine dose thresholds; (B) integration of perfusion-based markers such as diastolic arterial pressure, diastolic shock index, or vasopressor-to-norepinephrine ratio index (VNERi) to guide personalized timing; and (C) systematic reporting of ischemic and renal adverse effects. Such designs would clarify whether early vasopressin truly alters the organ dysfunction trajectory or merely reflects clinician preference and patient phenotype.

Until such data are available, we should interpret early vasopressin signals as promising yet hypothesis-generating, balancing potential catecholamine-sparing and RRT advantages against unquantified ischemic risk. Nevertheless, the Bhattacharjee et al.'s work and previous SRMA aptly rekindles the debate—not only on how much, but on how soon?.1,5 The timing may even be as early as an hour of norepinephrine initiation, when the required dose has exceeded a certain level along with ongoing fluid therapy.
