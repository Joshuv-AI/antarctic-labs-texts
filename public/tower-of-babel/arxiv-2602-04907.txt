Physics as the Inductive Bias for Causal Discovery
Jianhong Chen, Naichen Shi, Xubo Yue

Abstract
Causal discovery is often a data-driven paradigm to analyze complex real-world systems. In parallel, physics-based models such as ordinary differential equations (ODEs) provide mechanistic structure for many dynamical processes. Integrating these paradigms potentially allows physical knowledge to act as an inductive bias, improving identifiability, stability, and robustness of causal discovery in dynamical systems. However, such integration remains challenging: real dynamical systems often exhibit feedback, cyclic interactions, and non-stationary data trend, while many widely used causal discovery methods are formulated under acyclicity or equilibrium-based assumptions. In this work, we propose an integrative causal discovery framework for dynamical systems that leverages partial physical knowledge as an inductive bias. Specifically, we model system evolution as a stochastic differential equation (SDE), where the drift term encodes known ODE dynamics and the diffusion term corresponds to unknown causal couplings beyond the prescribed physics. We develop a scalable sparsity-inducing MLE algorithm that exploits causal graph structure for efficient parameter estimation. Under mild conditions, we establish guarantees to recover the causal graph. Experiments on dynamical systems with diverse causal structures show that our approach improves causal graph recovery and produces more stable, physically consistent estimates than purely data-driven state-of-the-art baselines.

Introduction

Understanding cause-effect relationships is fundamental for explaining complex real-world phenomena, such as gene regulation in genetics

8
]
, anthropogenic climate change in earth science
[

, and brain influence processes in neuroscience

However, RCTs can be prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, or even infeasible in many settings. Consequently, inferring causal structure from purely observational data or interventional data, known as
causal discovery
, has attracted much attention
[
10
]
.
Traditional causal discovery methods admit intrinsic ambiguities: without additional structure, multiple causal models are statistically indistinguishable. Classical approaches partially mitigate this ambiguity by imposing inductive biases such as acyclicity
[
24
]
, non-Gaussianity
[
32
]
, or temporal precedence
[
40
]
, typically yielding identifiability up to an equivalence class. While these assumptions have enabled substantial algorithmic progress, they are largely agnostic to the data-generating process and often misaligned with the mechanisms governing real physical systems. As a result, causal discovery algorithms may succeed statistically yet fail to recover mechanisms that are interpretable and generalizable.

In contrast, physical systems are not arbitrary stochastic processes; they are constrained by conservation laws, symmetries, and dynamical principles that induce structure far stronger than generic statistical regularities. Differential equations, ordinary or partial, provide a compact representation of such constraints by encoding how variables evolve through causal interactions over time and space. This observation motivates a natural question:

Can physical laws be formalized as an inductive bias for causal discovery, and if so, what are the theoretical consequences?

Despite the fact that both tools serve as powerful approaches for understanding real-world processes, there remains a clear need for a framework that bridges the causal discovery with available physical knowledge.

An Intuitive Solution.
Perhaps a seemingly natural approach is to incorporate physical knowledge into causal discovery by first fitting an ODE model using known mechanisms, and then applying a standard causal discovery algorithm to the residuals or remaining variables. While intuitive, this strategy treats physical modeling and causal discovery as two separate stages, implicitly assuming that the known dynamics and unknown causal structure can be cleanly decoupled. In practice, this separation is often problematic: model mismatch, unmodeled feedback, and stochastic effects can confound residual-based causal signals, leading to unstable or spurious discoveries. Moreover, existing causal discovery methods are not designed to account for uncertainty in the physical model itself, nor to jointly reason about mechanistic and causal parameters. These limitations highlight the need for a unified framework in which physical constraints and causal structure are learned simultaneously. Indeed, existing approaches can struggle even in seemingly benign settings, as we demonstrate below.

A Motivating Example.

In this example, we assume that the
available
mechanistic knowledge is minimal such that, for a subset of variables, physics specifies only their intrinsic dynamics, which we represent as self-loops in the graph (as illustrated in the top panel of Fig.

S1.2
.
In our formulation, the unknown causal structure is encoded in the diffusion term, and we define the target graph by the sparsity pattern of cross-variable couplings in
A
. The key task is to infer the unknown cross variable couplings (the green edges) between variables with unspecified dynamics and the mechanistic subsystem.
Crucially, even when partially specified ODE dynamics are available, they provide mechanistic constraints on the system’s evolution, which can shrink the space of plausible interactions and reduce ambiguity relative to purely data-driven causal discovery.

From this example, we can see that existing causal discovery frameworks are not designed to systematically incorporate mechanistic information, which leads to several challenges.

First
, many existing methods impose an acyclicity
assumption
, which can be overly restrictive for modeling real-world dynamical mechanisms.
Second
, there is a lack of a unified framework that can integrate these two sources of information. More specifically, data-driven causal discovery is often formulated via a structural causal model (SCM), such that
x_{i}(t)=f_{i}\left(x_{\mathrm{pa}(i)}(t)\right)
, whereas mechanistic prior knowledge is typically expressed through a differential equation, such that
\dot{x}_{i}(t)=g_{i}\left(t,\bm{x}(t),\bm{\gamma}\right)
. Since these formulations impose constraints at different levels (static functional assignments versus continuous time dynamics), it is nontrivial to combine them within a single learning objective without introducing an explicit bridge between mechanistic constraints and causal graph estimation.

Our key modeling idea is to use stochastic differential equations (SDEs) to combine mechanistic knowledge and statistical knowledge. The key reason is that SDEs provide a natural extension of ODE models by accounting for process noise and unmodeled inputs.
In particular, the Brownian motion component of an SDE (i.e.,
W_{t}
) provides a mathematically tractable representation of accumulated, small-scale random perturbations, and induces a distribution over trajectories rather than a single deterministic path. This is crucial for real data, where uncertainty must be
propagated through time and across coupled variables
. Moreover, the diffusion term separates intrinsic dynamical randomness from measurement noise, enabling principled likelihood-based inference and uncertainty quantification.

Our Contribution:
In this paper, we propose a framework that bridges the known dynamics with causal discovery, supported by theoretical guarantees and empirical validation. Our key contributions are: (1) We introduce an SDE-based causal discovery framework that integrates mechanistic (physics-based) dynamics with data-driven graph learning. In our formulation, the drift term encodes known ODE dynamics, while the diffusion term captures unknown cross-variable causal couplings beyond the prescribed physics. (2) We propose
SCD
, a scalable gradient-based method for causal graph recovery from observed trajectories by optimizing a tractable quasi-likelihood induced by Euler-Maruyama discretization. (3) We prove that, given
p
variables, for each node
i\in[p]
, if
n\gtrsim\max\{s_{i}^{3}\log p,\;s_{i}^{4}\}
and the regularization level is chosen as
\lambda_{n}\gtrsim\sqrt{\log p/n}+\sqrt{s_{i}/n}
, where
s_{i}
is the number of parent nodes in causal graph, then
SCD
recovers the true graph with high probability. To our knowledge, this is among the first identifiability analyses that simultaneously accommodates multiplicative SDE noise and nonconvexity, without imposing acyclicity or additive-noise assumptions. (4) We validate
SCD
on time series data generated by SDEs with both acyclic and cyclic directed structures on diffusion by varying the dimension
p
and the type of available ODE knowledge, and compare against four benchmark methods.

2
Literature Review

Causal Discovery from Time-series Data.
Our work is related to causal discovery from observational time-series data, as it studies causal structure in dynamical systems. A comprehensive survey of time-series causal discovery can be found in
Gong et al. 2024
. Existing approaches are typically categorized into (i) constraint-based methods relying on conditional independence tests, (ii) score-based structure optimization, and (iii) Granger-causality-based methods grounded in predictability. While our framework departs from these paradigms by incorporating mechanistic ODE knowledge as an inductive bias, it nevertheless operates on time-series observations. For completeness, we briefly review this literature in Appendix (see §
S1.1
).

Interpretation of Differential Equations From Causal Perspective.
One line of work that is seemingly related to our problem involves reinterpreting differential equation models from a causal perspective. For instance,
Mooij et al. 2013
show that, under suitable regularity conditions, the equilibrium behavior of a first-order ODE system can be mapped to a structural causal model, providing a causal interpretation of equilibrium relations, even in the presence of feedback cycles. Moreover,
Hansen & Sokol 2014
formalize how causal interventions act on SDEs by explicitly defining postintervention SDEs. Their framework shows that intervening on an SDE corresponds to the continuous-time limit of interventions in structural equation models, and establishes conditions under which postintervention distributions are identifiable from the observational SDE. While these works clarify how causality is defined and interpreted in differential equation models, they do not address the problem of learning causal graph structure from observational data, which is the focus of our work.

SDE-based Causal Discovery.
The closest methodological line to our work is SDE-based causal discovery, which models causal structure through stochastic dynamical systems.
Bellot et al. 2022
propose
NGM
based on the additive-noise SDE model
dx(t)=f(x(t))dt+dW(t)
, which uses Neural ODEs
[
5
]
for mean process
f(x(t))
and extracts graphical structure from the first layer of
f(x(t))
. This formulation assumes data drawn from a stationary distribution induced by additive noise, which constrains its applicability to a limited class of dynamical systems.
To address this limitation,
Wang et al. 2024
propose
SCOTCH
, a variational-inference framework that infers posterior distributions over candidate graphs. It adopts a general SDE formulation
dx(t)=f(x(t))\,dt+g(x(t))\,dW(t)
and uses neural networks to parameterize both
f
and
g
, with the graph defined by the induced dependencies among state variables. However, their emphasis is on continuous-time modeling and forecasting from irregular observations. Although they discuss identifiability and consistency, their method mainly learns a latent continuous-time representation to improve prediction rather than performing classical structural identification. In addition, their diffusion term
g
is constrained to a nonzero diagonal form.
Related efforts also include constraint-based causal discovery formulated in SDE form
[
20
]
, which is currently limited to directed acyclic graphs, and optimal-control-based approaches such as graphical continuous Lyapunov models (GCLMs)
[
38
,
6
]
, which rely on additive-noise dynamics.

Although these SDE-based works provide a flexible continuous time description of stochastic dynamics, existing approaches often restrict attention to additive-noise formulations, acyclicity, and frequently rely on stationarity-type conditions for analysis or inference. Moreover, ODEs and SDEs are often used primarily as data-generation tools, with limited discussion of how mechanistic ODE structure relates to the corresponding SDE representation. Importantly, in many real-world systems, ODE-level physical mechanisms constitute reusable domain knowledge. Causal discovery should be grounded in reliable prior knowledge and, when possible, refined into reusable knowledge that supports future discoveries and interventions
[
31
]
.
How to systematically incorporate such partial mechanistic information as an inductive bias for causal structure learning remains an open challenge.

3
Causal Coupling Beyond Known Physical Dynamics

3.1
Problem Setup

In this study, we view the observed
p
-dimensional trajectory
\bm{x}(t)
as a (discretely sampled) realization of an underlying stochastic process
\bm{X}(t)=(X_{1}(t),\ldots,X_{p}(t))
on
[0,T]
. We approximate the SDE dynamics on a time grid
I=\{t_{0},t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\}\subset[0,T]
using the Euler-Maruyama scheme. Specifically, for each component
i\in[p]
and time index
k\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
, with step size
\Delta t_{k}=t_{k+1}-t_{k}
, we have

x^{i}_{k+1}=x^{i}_{k}+g_{i}(t,\bm{x}(t),\bm{\gamma})\Delta t_{k}+\big(A_{i}^{\top}x_{k}\big)\sqrt{\Delta t_{k}}\,z^{i}_{k+1},

(1)

where
z^{i}_{k+1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Here,
g_{i}(t,\bm{x}(t),\bm{\gamma})\equiv\frac{d}{dt}x_{i}(t)
denote deterministic ODE (i.e., without uncertainty) for component
x_{i}(t)
and define the drift term, where
\bm{\gamma}
denote ODE parameters. In contrast,
A_{i}^{\top}
collects the coefficients associated with multiplicative noise and defines the diffusion term. We further define
\Delta W_{k}^{i}\coloneqq\sqrt{\Delta t_{k}}z_{k+1}^{i}
. Therefore, we have
\Delta W_{k}^{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Delta t_{k})
, which corresponds to the Euler-Maruyama discretization of the continuous-time SDE for each
i\in[p]
:

dX_{i}(t)=g_{i}(t,\bm{x}(t),\bm{\gamma})dt+\big(A_{i}^{\top}X(t)\big)dW_{i}(t)

(2)

where
\{W_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{p}
are independent standard Brownian motion. As a result, in
p
-dimensional we can have the following SDE:

\begin{split}&d\bm{X}_{t}=\bm{g}(t,\bm{x}(t),\bm{\gamma})dt+S_{A}(\bm{X}_{t})dW_{t},\\
&S_{A}(\bm{X}_{t})=\mathrm{diag}\big([A\bm{X}_{t}]_{1},[A\bm{X}_{t}]_{2},\ldots,[A\bm{X}_{t}]_{p}\big).\end{split}

(3)

We next derive a principled link between the causal graph and known physics (even partially), showing that the induced dynamics admit an SDE formulation.

3.2
Characterization of Causal Influence

Building on the previous section, we interpret the diffusion term as an
uncertain information channel
whose impact is modulated by the current system state. In an idealized setting where the system is fully understood and all relevant mechanisms are correctly modeled, this stochastic component may be negligible. In that case, deterministic dynamics (i.e., physics-based information) can be sufficient for prediction.
Consequently, Eq.(
3
) provides a unified formulation that recovers deterministic physics while incorporating causal uncertainty when unmodeled or stochastic influences are present. Under this intuition, we can define causality as in Definition
1
.

Definition 1

(Causal Coupling Beyond Known Dynamics)
.

With a known deterministic dynamics for
\bm{X}_{t}
,
an external event (or information input) exerts a
causal influence beyond the known dynamics
at time
t
if and only if
S_{A}(\bm{X}_{t})\neq 0
. Equivalently,
no such additional causal interaction
is present if and only if the system is
causally closed
under the known dynamics (i.e.,
S_{A}(\bm{X}_{t})=0
) in which case the evolution remains fully described by the known ODE.

This definition characterizes causality as additional dynamical coupling not explained by the known ODE. It aligns with an information-flow interpretation of causality, but it captures higher-order (rather than pairwise) effects. More precisely, Granger causality
[
11
]
is defined operationally via incremental predictability and admits a natural interpretation as directed information flow (e.g., in terms of conditional mutual information over time-lagged histories).In our work, we leverage the availability of a partially known ODE model and define causal influence structurally: if a variable appears in the governing equations, then perturbations to that variable can propagate through the dynamics and induce changes in downstream state trajectories, thereby establishing a causal effect within the system. Under this definition, we do not need to separately impose an acyclicity assumption to improve causal interpretability. Below we provide an illustrative example based on a physical system. In addition to the conceptual discussion, §
S1.4
presents a rigorous mathematical derivation of our interpretation.

Example: Predator-prey behavior.

Within a predator-prey system (e.g., foxes and rabbits), whose population dynamics can be modeled by the Lotka-Volterra (LV) equations. In an idealized setting with no additional external drivers, the dynamics are fully determined over long time horizons given the initial conditions and fixed parameters. In practice, however, exogenous factors inevitably enter the system, for example, human hunting that reduces the predator population, habitat changes, or the introduction of a new species. These interventions can be interpreted as causal inputs that perturb the original dynamics. Consequently, the original LV model alone is no longer sufficient to capture the observed behavior, and the system must be augmented to account for these new causal factors. In our formulation, the dynamics are assumed to be known and are represented by
\bm{g}(t,\bm{x}(t),\bm{\gamma})
(e.g., the LV component in the predator-prey example). The additional causal coupling that is not captured by the known ODE is encoded by
A
.

4
Causal Graph Estimation

Before moving to the learning objective, we first define the graph formally.
We consider a directed graph
G\coloneqq(V,E)
, where
V\coloneqq\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}\}
with
v_{i}\coloneqq x_{i}
. We define
e_{ij}\coloneqq(v_{j}\to v_{i})
and include
e_{ij}\in E
if and only if
a_{ij}\neq 0
. The associated graph representation follows directly from this affine form. Clearly, the graph structure is determined by ODE knowledge and
A
. In this problem, our main task is to learn the causal graph and new edges from the unknown mechanism variables to ODE variables, encoded by the matrix
A
, and we assume that the graph is sparse. Given the trajectory
\bm{x}(t)
with
n
time steps of
\bm{X}_{t}
, we can have a graph lasso optimization.
Moreover, it can be solved by negative log of the Euler–Maruyama Gaussian transition and using the Gaussian quasi-likelihood, as follows:

\begin{split}\hat{A}=&\arg\min_{A}\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\Big[\\
&\,\log\big((a_{i}^{\top}\bm{x}(k))^{2}+c\big)+\frac{r_{i,k}^{2}}{(a_{i}^{\top}\bm{x}(k))^{2}+c}\Big]+\lambda\|A\|_{1},\\
&r_{i,k}\coloneqq\frac{x_{i}(k+1)-x_{i}(k)-g_{i}(t,\bm{x}(t),\bm{\gamma})\Delta t_{k}}{\sqrt{\Delta t_{k}}},\end{split}

(4)

where we define
\bm{x}(k+1)\coloneqq\bm{x}(t_{k+1})
, and introduce a
stabilization constant

c>0
to ensure numerical stability and to make the objective well-defined, which in turns facilitates our theoretical analysis about the optimization landscape.
Moreover, we can see that the diffusion estimator depends on the drift through
r_{i,k}
. Any misspecification of the ODE knowledge introduces an additive bias term
\Delta t_{k}e_{i,k}^{2}
in
\mathbb{E}[r_{i,k}^{2}\mid\bm{x}(k)]
, which inflates the estimated diffusion magnitude. In cases where ODE is not known or specified accurately, we also provide a learning procedure for the ODE component (see §
S1
) with a numerical study under ODE misspecification.

5
Main Theoretical Results

As shown in the previous section, we consider that data are generated from a discretized SDE whose distribution may be non-Gaussian due to multiplicative noise. This setting allows us to relax several standard assumptions that are commonly adopted in causal graph inference; however, it also creates fundamental technical challenges for both the formulation and the theoretical analysis.

Since SDEs have a well-developed mathematical theory, we avoid imposing ad hoc assumptions directly on the observed data and instead derive the required regularity from the underlying SDE dynamics. A basic requirement is
well-posedness
: the SDE must admit a unique (strong) solution on the observation horizon
[0,T]
; otherwise the data-generating mechanism is not identifiable and causal graph recovery is ill-defined. In the theoretical analysis, we assume the underlying ODE is linear (or locally linearizable), so that its dynamics can be written approximately as an affine mapping
\dot{x}(t)\approx Bx(t)+b
. We therefore impose standard well-posedness conditions: (i) local Lipschitzness and linear growth of the drift and diffusion, and (ii) a square-integrable initial condition, stated precisely in §
4
. Beyond existence and uniqueness, for finite-sample analysis on
[0,T]
(without assuming stationarity), we also require control of the solution’s moments over time. Fortunately, Lyapunov stability provides exactly the control we need. We therefore impose the following condition.

Assumption 1
.

Assume the SDE satisfy the Lyapunov stability. Then, there exists a unique matrix
\Sigma\succ 0
such that

B\Sigma+\Sigma B^{\top}+A\Sigma A^{\top}=M,

(5)

where
M
is a constant matrix with
M\prec 0
. Then, we further assume that there exist constants
C,c>0
such that
\|\Sigma_{t}-\Sigma\|_{2}\leq Ce^{-ct}\|\Sigma_{0}-\Sigma\|_{2}
, for all
t\geq 0
and
\Sigma_{t}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[X_{t}X_{t}^{\top}]
. Moreover, there exists constants
C_{0},c_{0}>0
such that for all
t\geq 0
and
h\geq 0
,

\|\mathrm{Cov}(X_{t},X_{t+h})\|_{2}\leq C_{0}e^{-c_{0}h}.

(6)

Notably, this stability assumption is
weaker than stationarity, since it does not require an invariant distribution and is compatible with time-inhomogeneous dynamics.

Remark 1
.

We note that Assumption.
1
can be verified under standard Lyapunov (mean-square exponential) stability conditions for linear SDEs. See Section 6.3 of
Khasminskii 2012
. The Eq.(
6
) is also known as exponential decay of cross-time covariances. This can be proved under standard Lyapunov exponential mean-square stability conditions for linear SDE. See section 6.4 of
Särkkä & Solin 2019
.

Moreover, Assumption.
1
provides many good properties which are essential for controlling the random design induced by the SDE. We present these properties in Proposition.
1
,

Proposition 1
.

Let the SDE satisfy the assumptions from §
4
and
T<\infty
(i.e. locally Lipschitz, linear growth condition, and the square-integrable initial condition). Then, the SDE defined in Eq.(
3
), admits a unique strong solution with continuous sample paths. If Assumption.
1
holds, there exist constants
\beta>0
and
C>0
such that
\mathbb{E}\|X_{t}\|^{2}\leq Ce^{-\beta t}\|X_{0}\|^{2}
, for all
t\in[0,T]
(see Definition 2.1 of
Higham et al. 2003
). Then, for any
K>0
, we have
\mathbb{P}(\|X_{t}\|>K)\leq K^{-2}Ce^{-\beta t}\mathbb{E}\|X_{0}\|^{2}
.

Remark 2
.

This proposition implies that, for any fixed
K>0
, the upper bound on
\mathbb{P}(\|X_{t}\|>K)
decays exponentially in
t
; hence the deviation event

\{\|X_{t}\|>K\}
becomes less likely as
t

With these well-posedness and Lyapunov stability conditions in place, we now turn to our main statistical guarantees. In particular, we establish finite-sample error bounds for stationary points of the empirical objective under the SDE-induced random design, and we characterize the resulting implications for causal graph recovery.

Given the optimization problem in Eq.(
4
), one may ask whether it is possible to identify the true structure by solving Eq.(
4
). We consider it as parent selection problem, and use
\theta_{i}
to represent the parent vector of node
i
(i.e., the i-th row of
A
). For more details, see §
S2.1
. In high-dimensional statistics, a common structural condition used to ensure identifiability is sparsity. Let
V_{phy}\coloneqq\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}\}\subseteq V
denote the set of variables for which physics information is available. We then state the following assumption.

Assumption 2

(Sparsity)
.

There exists some
\kappa\in[0,1)
for all node
j\in V\setminus{V_{phy}}
such that

\lim_{|V|\to\infty}\max_{j\in V\setminus{V_{phy}}}|pa(j)|=O(|V|^{\kappa})

(7)

Without this assumption, minimizing Eq.(
4
) does not identify the parents.
Thus, sparsity is necessary for identifiable and statistically tractable recovery in high-dimensional statistics.

Even under sparsity, exact parent selection is not guaranteed in the presence of strong correlations among variables. In particular, if some non-parent variables are highly correlated with the true parent set, they can explain the same variation in the data and lead to spurious edge selection under
\ell_{1}
regularization. To rule out these extreme cases and enable support recovery, we consider a standard incoherence condition, which is widely used in the analysis of Lasso and related graph recovery methods
[
21
,
17
]
. The incoherence condition is typically imposed on the Fisher information matrix (i.e., the Hessian of the population loss evaluated at the ground truth
\theta_{i}^{*}
). We first define the (population) Fisher information matrix as
Q^{*}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{i}^{\ast};X)\right]
. For index sets
A,B\subseteq V
, we denote the corresponding sub-block by
Q^{*}_{AB}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\nabla_{A}\nabla_{B}\mathcal{L}(\theta_{i}^{\ast};X)\right]
, where
\nabla_{A}
denotes the gradient restricted to coordinates in
A
. Let
V_{i}\coloneqq\mathrm{pa}(i)
be the parent set of node
i
, and let
V_{i}^{c}\coloneqq V\setminus V_{i}
denote its complement. We then impose the following condition.

Assumption 3

(Incoherence)
.

There exists
\alpha\in(0,1]
such that
\|Q^{*}_{V^{c}_{i}V_{i}}(Q^{*}_{V_{i}V_{i}})^{-1}\|_{\infty}\leq 1-\alpha
.

Under these conditions, the high-dimensional parent selection problem becomes statistically tractable. Under Assumption.
1
and Assumption.
3

4

7
Conclusion

We propose a novel and theoretically justified framework that incorporates known physics knowledge as an inductive bias for causal discovery, while explicitly representing uncertainty in the inferred causal graph via a principled stochastic modeling foundation based on SDEs. To the best of our knowledge, this is
the first
causal discovery framework for nonstationary time series that does not rely on a DAG assumption and does not require committing to a specific SDE noise structure or function class such as diagonal diffusion and additive noise. We hope this work will motivate continued exploration of frameworks that integrate mechanistic knowledge and causal reasoning for complex, nonstationary dynamical systems.

Impact Statement

This work advances causal discovery by incorporating physical knowledge as an inductive bias, improving reliability and interpretability in dynamical systems. Ethically, grounding causal discovery in explicit physics helps reduce spurious conclusions and promotes transparent, accountable use of causal models. In the long term, more trustworthy causal discovery methods may positively impact scientific decision-making and system design across engineering and natural sciences, while emphasizing responsible deployment and interpretation of causal claims.

Constraint-based methods infer causal structure via conditional independence tests. PCMCI

S1.4
Mathematical Formulation for Physics As Inductive Bias

Let
\tilde{\bm{u}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the subset of
d\leq p
variables for which partial mechanistic knowledge is available. We model the known physics using an affine ODE of the form
\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{\bm{u}}(t)=\tilde{B}\tilde{\bm{u}}(t)+\tilde{\bm{b}}
, where
\tilde{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\tilde{\bm{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This representation can be interpreted as an exact affine mechanism when appropriate, or as a first-order (locally affine) surrogate for more complex nonlinear dynamics. To align dimensions with
x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we define the augmented vectors and matrices

\bm{u}(t)\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{u}}(t)\\
0\end{bmatrix},\quad B\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{B}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad b\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bm{b}}\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

(14)

We model the remaining (unknown) relationships via a causal graph, while retaining the mechanistic component as an explicit term in the dynamics. Building on this mechanistic component, we decompose each state variable as

x_{i}(t)=\sum_{j\in pa(i)}a_{ij}x_{j}(t)+u_{i}(t).

(15)

In later sections, we will use the shorthand such that
x_{i}(t)=[A\bm{x}(t)]_{i}+u_{i}(t)
or equivalently
x_{i}(t)=a_{i}^{\top}\bm{x}(t)+u_{i}(t)
, where
a_{i}^{\top}
denotes the
i
-th row of
A
(so that
[A\bm{x}(t)]_{i}=a_{i}^{\top}\bm{x}(t)
). Noted that
A
is full matrix and each row vector
a_{i}
contains
a_{ij}
for
j\in pa(i)
and
a_{ik}=0
for
k\notin pa(i)
. Since ODEs can be described as the rates of change over time, we can take derivative respected
t
. Since
A
is fully unknown, we introduce a latent stochastic term
\bm{\epsilon}(t)\coloneqq[\epsilon_{1}(t),\epsilon_{2}(t),\ldots,\epsilon_{p}(t)]^{\top}
that modulates the unknown component through a multiplicative relation to account the uncertainty for this additional coupling component. Consequently, we model the dynamics as

\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}{\bm{x}}(t)=A\bm{\dot{x}}(t)\odot\bm{\epsilon}(t)+B^{\top}\bm{x}(t)+\bm{b}\end{split}

(16)

where
\bm{\epsilon}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is Gaussian white noise vector such that
\bm{\epsilon}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{p})
, and
\odot
is the elementwise multiplication of two vectors. Moreover, additional structural information about the dynamics of the unknown variable is unavailable. We therefore work with a surrogate dynamics and set
\frac{d}{dt}\bm{x}(t)\approx\bm{x}(t)
. The substitution is used solely to obtain a
closed, state-measurable
diffusion model when
\dot{x}
is not directly observed. It should be viewed as a
surrogate feature choice
rather than an assertion of the true dynamics. More generally, any proxy computed from available information (e.g.,
\dot{x}=g(t,x;\gamma)
from the known ODE) yields a closed diffusion parameterization and leads to the same diffusion-coupling interpretation. This is reasonable because real-world time series variables are typically non-degenerate and exhibit intrinsic self-dynamics. By multiplying the
dt
on both side and
\epsilon_{i}(t)d(t)\equiv W_{i}(t)
, we can observe that it is in the form of Eq.(
2
). Then, the new differential equation is composed by a known ODE and an causal information part defined by
A
with uncertainty.

S1.10
Future works

Beyond these results, we recognize several promising directions for future work. First, although our main theory is developed under stable SDE assumptions, our empirical results indicate that the method can also perform well on unstable SDEs. This motivates a deeper theoretical study of our optimization and statistical guarantees in the unstable regime, which we believe is both important and technically interesting. Second, because SDEs form a broad and flexible modeling class, accommodating general drift and diffusion structures, we believe our study offers practical guidance on using SDEs as a principled tool for physics-informed causal discovery. An important and realizable extension is to incorporate richer mechanistic knowledge by moving beyond standard ODEs to more general dynamical forms, such as delay differential equations and ODEs with time-varying parameters, as well as more general nonlinear functional specifications from a mathematical (not purely engineering) perspective. Third, stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) provide a natural next step for causal learning from spatiotemporal datasets, where both spatial interactions and temporal evolution are essential. Finally, building on the same foundational machinery, particularly It
\hat{o}
’s lemma, a promising direction is to extend the framework to causal representation learning by introducing an observation (measurement) function on top of the latent SDE dynamics with known physics.

Finally, we reiterate that our original motivation is to bring physics-informed inductive biases into causal discovery as a causal structure is reliable only insofar as the real-world phenomenon of interest can be meaningfully mapped to it. We believe that broader physics perspectives, beyond differential equation formulations alone, should be explored, as they can further improve the reliability and applicability of causal discovery in scientific settings. While this is a compelling direction, developing a general and systematic integration of richer physical principles is inherently challenging and remains an important topic for future work.
