Dear Editor,

We thank the authors for their insightful comments on our scoping review, “Machine Learning and Deep Learning Models for Early Sepsis Prediction.” Their observations primarily focus on translational and implementation challenges—an important dimension that complements the evidence-mapping objective of our review.

Scope of Our Review

Our review synthesized methodological trends in early sepsis prediction models and identified persistent gaps, such as inconsistent variable sets, limited prospective validation, and inadequate explainability. The translational roadmap presented in the letter aligns with these findings but extends the discussion toward operational deployment, which is indeed the next critical step for the field.

Minimal, Standardized Datasets

While we agree with the need for low-burden standardized datasets, we emphasize the necessity of multicenter consensus building and benchmarking frameworks that allow reproducible comparisons across algorithms. Establishing a national minimum dataset for AI-enabled sepsis prediction could significantly accelerate model evaluation and adoption in Indian ICUs.

Explainability and Uncertainty

We concur that explainability and uncertainty quantification remain underdeveloped components in current research. Beyond SHAP-based approaches, journals should increasingly expect robust calibration assessment, transparent risk communication, and clear documentation of model behavior in edge cases, as recommended in recent critiques of XAI in health care.1

Prospective, Workflow-integrated Evaluation

We agree that retrospective studies alone are insufficient. Future work should mandate prospective, workflow-integrated evaluations where algorithms are tested in real-time clinical settings, aligned with sepsis bundles and EWS pathways. Such studies should clearly differentiate algorithmic performance from clinical impact measures—a distinction highlighted as essential for the translation of ML in health care.2

Additional Recommendations for IJCCM

Adoption of Standardized AI Reporting Requirements

Indian Journal of Critical Care Medicine (IJCCM) may consider mandating an adapted checklist based on CONSORT-AI and SPIRIT-AI, requiring explicit details on dataset characteristics, preprocessing, feature handling, external validation, drift monitoring, and clinician–AI interaction design.5 Submissions should also clarify how model outputs influence decision-making or patient outcomes.5 This would ensure the completeness and transparency of AI studies and help with the replicability of the study in real-world settings.

Clear Separation of Technical Performance and Clinical Utility

Journals should require authors to report algorithmic metrics (AUROC, calibration curves) separately from clinical utility outcomes (time-to-antibiotics, length of stay, resource use). Such separation is critical to avoid overclaiming algorithmic capability and to evaluate real-world readiness.2

Performance Error Reporting and Risk Mitigation

It is essential to document and analyze performance errors and failure cases to ensure safe AI deployment. Unforeseen errors can pose significant risks at scale; therefore, authors should report representative failure cases, explain contributing factors, and outline risk-mitigation strategies. Clear interpretation of these errors—especially regarding specific populations or clinical contexts—helps define when and for whom the AI intervention can be safely implemented.5

Conclusion

The letter to the editor advances an important conversation on translating sepsis prediction models into real-world clinical environments. Our review offers the foundational mapping of existing methodologies, while the operational recommendations presented above highlight critical next steps for ensuring safe, explainable, and clinically meaningful AI deployment in LMIC critical care settings.
