Sustainability in endoscopy: switching from sterile water to tap water
Authors: Christine Li, Michael Guo, Ahmer Karimuddin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Use of sterile water in endoscopy is common practice but is not based on available evidence, as the literature suggests that the use of tap water in endoscopy is safe and appropriate, even for advanced procedures. We sought to examine the financial and environmental cost savings at our institution after switching from sterile to tap water for endoscopy.Methods:We conducted a comparative analysis of a period of use of sterile water irrigation (October 2022) in endoscopy to an equivalent period of use of tap water irrigation (October 2023). Advanced and invasive procedures continued to use sterile water during both study periods. Our analysis focused on the financial and environmental impact of switching to tap water.Results:The number of procedures remained stable, with 922 procedures during the sterile water phase and 905 procedures during the tap water phase. Introduction of tap water led to a substantial reduction in sterile water usage, from 336 to 192 bottles per month, saving $310.70 per month. The cost of irrigation water per procedure decreased from $0.80 to $0.47, representing a 41.2% cost reduction. Transitioning to tap water eliminated the need for 144 bottles per month, resulting in a monthly waste reduction of 17.45 kg. The estimated carbon footprint was 54.18 kg of carbon dioxide equivalents from production alone, excluding sterilization, transportation, and waste management.Conclusion:Use of tap water in endoscopy has been proven to be safe and effective. An additional benefit is the financial and environmental cost savings of decreasing sterile water use. Implementation of tap water protocols in endoscopy units can be a simple step toward increasing sustainability in endoscopy.

Health care is a major contributor to environmental degradation, generating substantial waste and greenhouse gas emissions. In endoscopy, the use of sterile water for irrigation remains common despite strong evidence that tap water is a safe and effective alternative, with multiple studies confirming the microbiological safety of potable tap water for routine gastrointestinal endoscopy, with no increase in infection-related complications.1,2

Despite this evidence, sterile water continues to be used out of an abundance of caution or institutional habit. This practice generates excess plastic waste, increases carbon emissions, and contributes to rising health care costs. In an era of climate crises and constrained health care budgets, simple operational changes can have a meaningful impact.

We sought to evaluate the financial and environmental impact of switching from sterile water to tap water in an endoscopy unit at a tertiary care hospital in British Columbia. We hypothesized that this change would yield meaningful cost savings and environmental benefits with no negative effect on procedural volume or safety.

Methods

We conducted a comparative, retrospective analysis using data from the endoscopy unit at St. Paul’s Hospital in Vancouver, BC. We analyzed data from 2 periods: October 2022 (sterile water irrigation) and October 2023 (tap water irrigation). The transition to use of tap water occurred in late 2022, with quality-control monitoring maintained throughout. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) and other procedures requiring strict sterility continued to use sterile water in both periods. We obtained data on the number of procedures, volume, and cost of sterile water used, cost per procedure, number of sterile water bottles used, and plastic waste reduction. We estimated carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) emissions by the quantity of polyvinyl chloride (PVC) plastic used in the sterile water containers. We determined the amount of PVC plastic per container by measuring the mean weight of 10 empty sterile water bottles used at our institution. We calculated total CO2e emissions by multiplying the estimated PVC mass by the published emission factor for PVC production from the United Kingdom Department for Business, Energy, and Industrial Strategy.3

Results

The total number of procedures was similar across both periods, with 922 procedures in October 2022 and 905 procedures in October 2023. In October 2022, the unit used 336 bottles of sterile water, averaging $0.80 per procedure. In October 2023, with tap water used for most procedures, only 192 sterile bottles were required, and the cost per procedure dropped to $0.47. Monthly water cost savings totalled $310.70. The switch to tap water led to a reduction of 144 bottles per month. This equated to 17.45 kg of plastic waste avoided and 54.18 kg CO2e in production emissions avoided. Discussion

Our findings support a growing body of evidence that potable tap water is safe and effective for use in gastrointestinal endoscopy. Three decades ago, Wilcox and colleagues1 found that no microbiologic contamination occurred in procedures performed with tap water, and no increase in adverse patient outcomes was observed. Similarly, Puterbaugh and colleagues2 confirmed the microbiological safety of tap water used during flexible endoscopy in a clinical setting, advocating for its broader use. Despite this, use of sterile water has persisted as the norm in many institutions, driven largely by institutional inertia, regulatory ambiguity, and clinician preference rather than empirical necessity.

Beyond being safe, the transition to tap water in our study resulted in measurable financial and environmental benefits. With a cost reduction of 41.2% per procedure, our single-site results translate to potential savings of more than $3700 annually, not accounting for larger systems or regional networks. The potential savings could also increase if tap water were used for all endoscopy procedures (including ERCP). This aligns with Rockey’s4 early observations that endoscopy is an area where opportunities for cost efficiency remain underexploited. It is estimated that more than 1.7 million endoscopic procedures are performed each year in Canada. 5 Given current fiscal pressures in health care, particularly with rising procedure volumes and staffing costs, even minor operational changes can yield important aggregate effects.

Environmental sustainability is also a core value in modern health care. Our intervention prevented the use of 144 sterile water bottles per month — equivalent to a reduction of nearly 17.5 kg of plastic waste and more than 54 kg CO2e emissions from manufacturing. These numbers are conservative, as they do not include emissions related to transportation, sterilization, or downstream waste processing. These factors can cause wide-ranging variability in the true total emissions from the use of sterile water bottles and are institution-specific. Many studies have highlighted the substantial life-cycle environmental impact of production and disposal of plastic water bottles, making such substitution initiatives critical in reducing the sector’s ecological footprint. 6 In our unit, the irrigation system is designed to attach to the neck of a sterile water bottle, necessitating the use of 1 sterile bottle per room at the start of each day. This bottle was subsequently refilled with tap water for the remainder of nonsterile procedures, excluding ERCP and other sterile scope rooms. Further reductions in waste could be achieved through adoption of fully nonsterile irrigation systems or compatible alternative containers.

Water-assisted endoscopy studies have consistently shown safe patient outcomes with the use of tap water for colonoscopy preparation and irrigation. Moreover, innovations such as underwater endoscopic mucosal resection and water exchange colonoscopy rely on large volumes of water, further reinforcing the need for cost-effective, sustainable water sources. Despite these advantages, uptake of tap water protocols is inconsistent. Institutional policies, perceptions about safety, and lack of interdepartmental coordination often serve as barriers to change. Implementation requires proactive leadership, education, and appropriate quality-assurance protocols to monitor for potential infectious complications, although the literature consistently reports none when municipal potable water is used appropriately. In our institution, successful implementation required multidisciplinary engagement, including hospital leadership and infection prevention and control, both of which supported the transition and identified no mandate for use of sterile water in routine endoscopy.

Limitations

This was a single-centre analysis regarding the implementation of tap water for irrigation within a single endoscopy unit. There may be additional institutional or systems limitations applicable to other centres that were not addressed in our study.

Conclusion

This study shows that adopting tap water protocols can support institutional sustainability targets and contribute to responsible resource stewardship in surgical practice. To promote broader implementation, health authorities should consider national guidelines to standardize use of tap water in endoscopy where clinically appropriate, environmental audits of endoscopy and surgical units, and inclusion of sustainability metrics into hospital quality-improvement frameworks. Multicentre and prospective studies could explore longitudinal outcomes, cost trends, and potential barriers to implementation across diverse health care settings.
