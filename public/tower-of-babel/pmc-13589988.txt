Evaluating the Zero-Markup Drug Policy’s Effects on Financial Burden and Pharmaceutical Channel Migration: A Structural Variation and Interrupted Time Series Analysis from Hubei, China
Authors: Yang Zhou, Kunhe Lin, Li Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study evaluates the impact of China’s zero-markup drug policy (ZMDP) on patients’ financial burden and pharmaceutical purchasing channel migration, using Yichang City in Hubei Province as a case study.MethodsWe analyzed data from 108 hospitals (January 2016 – December 2019) and monthly medical insurance reimbursement records. Structural variation analysis (SVA) and interrupted time series analysis (ITSA) with month-of-year fixed effects and counterfactual predictions were employed to assess changes in outpatient costs, inpatient costs, and pharmacy purchase outcomes (total expenditure, purchase volume, and average cost per purchase) before and after the policy implementation (August 2017).ResultsDrug cost proportions declined from 41.67% to 37.14% for outpatient and from 34.97% to 27.60% for inpatient services. ITSA revealed a marginally significant immediate outpatient cost reduction (β2 = −4.431, p = 0.094) and a significant monthly inpatient cost decline (β3 = −49.144, p = 0.031). However, diagnostic and procedural costs increased, with limited shift toward professional fees. Pharmacy data showed a coherent pattern: total expenditure significantly decreased (β2 = −953.507 thousand ¥, p = 0.024), purchase volume increased (β3 = 1353.214, p = 0.034), and average cost per purchase declined (β3 = −0.291, p < 0.001) – indicating successful price reduction with expanded pharmacy access.ConclusionThe ZMDP effectively reduced hospital drug expenditures but triggered cost substitution toward diagnostic services and pharmaceutical channel migration to retail pharmacies. For healthcare leaders, these findings suggest that vertical price regulation without horizontal integration across care settings may simply relocate costs. Future reforms should incorporate integrated prescription monitoring and complementary pricing reforms to achieve sustainable financial protection.Strengths and Limitations of This StudyThis study has several strengths. The use of ITSA with month-of-year fixed effects and counterfactual predictions controls for seasonal patterns and quantifies policy effect magnitudes. Structural variation analysis complements this by measuring expenditure redistribution across service categories. Placebo tests further support the robustness of findings for most outcomes. Limitations include the single-group ITSA design without a concurrent control group and the single-city setting, which limit causal attribution and generalizability. The study period ends in 2019 due to the COVID-19 pandemic, constraining long-term observation.

Background

The global public health sector is confronting the dual challenges of population aging and increasing chronic disease burden,1–3 driving sustained growth in healthcare expenditures. Global health spending is projected to increase from $10 trillion in 2015 to approximately $20 trillion by 2040.4 This trend manifests as persistent pressure on healthcare financing systems in developed countries,5–7 while creating tensions between health service coverage expansion and cost containment in developing countries.8

In China, healthcare cost escalation has been particularly pronounced. Between 2015 and 2019, China’s total health expenditures grew at an average annual rate exceeding 10%, with per-capita inpatient costs in public hospitals rising by 19.1% over this period.9 Evidence suggests pharmaceutical expenditures constitute a key driver of healthcare cost growth,10,11 influenced by both market mechanisms12,13 and systemic characteristics of the healthcare system, including distribution channel markups,14,15 hospital drug price premiums,16 and physician overprescribing due to various incentives.17,18 In 2015, drug costs accounted for 47.3% of average outpatient expenses and 36.8% of average inpatient expenses in Chinese hospitals.9

Given the dominance of public hospitals in China’s healthcare system, government policies play a crucial role in regulating hospital pricing.16,19 To curb the unreasonable growth of pharmaceutical costs, the Chinese government mandated in 2017 that all public hospitals eliminate drug markups by the end of September.19 This policy aimed to sever the direct financial link between hospitals and drug sales, thereby reducing patients’ pharmaceutical expenses and alleviating their financial burden.

Theoretically, the ZMDP may induce shifts in hospital revenue composition through several mechanisms.20,21 First, the elimination of the 15% drug markup reduces revenue from pharmaceutical sales, while the policy’s compensation mechanism—80% from service fee adjustments and 20% from government subsidies—creates a structural incentive to reallocate service volumes toward diagnostic and procedural categories. Second, diagnostic tests and medical supplies are characterized by higher revenue per unit and greater billing flexibility compared to professional service fees, making them a more readily adjustable component of hospital revenue mix.22 Third, expanding diagnostic and procedural services entails fewer administrative and regulatory barriers than upward adjustment of professional service fees, which would require comprehensive fee schedule reforms and formal recognition of healthcare workers’ labor value.23 These theoretical predictions—diagnostic substitution and potential channel migration—inform the empirical framework of this study.

Current research on China’s ZMDP has examined multiple dimensions, including policy background, theoretical foundations, international experience, and practical effects.24,25 The policy has achieved some positive results: it effectively curbed inflated drug prices and reduced patients’ pharmaceutical expenses,16,26 and promoted adjustments in hospital revenue structures, forcing hospitals to focus on improving medical service quality rather than relying on drug sales for profit.21,27

However, concerns remain that the ZMDP may not have fully positive effects on cost structure.28 Studies have found that hospitals may compensate for reduced pharmaceutical revenue by increasing diagnostic and procedural costs such as examination fees and medical supply costs.26,27,29 Although drug costs have significantly decreased, this has not necessarily led to a real reduction in patients’ financial burden. Some studies suggest that, in response to revenue pressure, hospitals may reduce drug procurement volumes or restrict the use of specific medications,27,30 potentially leading patients to purchase drugs from external sources, which could affect drug accessibility and introduce safety risks.

In 2017, Yichang City, Hubei Province, implemented the ZMDP in its public hospitals. This study selects Yichang City as a representative sample to comprehensively analyze the policy’s impact on three aspects: medical cost structure, cost control effectiveness, and pharmaceutical channel migration to retail pharmacies. Specifically, we evaluate: (1) the policy’s impact on medical expense composition, assessing whether it alleviated patients’ pharmaceutical burden while triggering shifts toward diagnostic costs; (2) the effectiveness of cost control mechanisms by analyzing expenditure trends before and after policy implementation; and (3) whether the policy has led healthcare institutions to shift drug sales to retail pharmacies.

Materials and Methods

Study Setting

Yichang City, situated in Hubei Province, Central China, boasted a GDP of 446.082 billion CNY in 2019, ranking third in the province. The permanent population was 4.1379 million, and the registered population was 3.9094 million. 3,812,200 people participated in Urban Basic Medical Insurance. Social insurance coverage reached 98%. The city’s medical infrastructure comprises a three-tier network of medical services, consisting of 108 hospitals with 6721 physicians, 11,528 nurses and a total of 22,905 beds.

In September 2012, Hubei Province launched the zero-markup drug policy reform, initially implementing the removal of drug markups in a pilot selection of public hospitals. The policy was expanded on October 30, 2015, when all 64 county-level public hospitals participating in Hubei’s comprehensive healthcare reform abolished drug markups, including designated districts and counties within Yichang City. Finally, on July 31, 2017, the reform was extended to urban public hospitals in Yichang City and nine other prefecture-level cities, with the sole exemption of traditional Chinese herbal decoctions. This milestone marked the full elimination of drug markups across all public hospitals in Hubei Province.

Before the implementation of zero-markup of drugs, the compensation channels of public hospitals in China were mainly composed of “drug income, medical service income and financial subsidy income”, which were reduced to the current “medical service income” and “financial subsidy income” after the reform. According to the compensation subject, it is mainly divided into public financial funds, medical insurance funds and patients (Shown in Figure 1). Therefore, in order to maintain the balance of hospital income and expenditure and reduce the economic burden of patients, it is necessary to increase the service income of hospitals and increase financial subsidies. Combined with the zero-markup reform experience of other cities in China, the price of medical services, which had been in a state of low growth or even loss for a long time in order to maintain the public welfare of public hospitals, has ushered in adjustments. For the new batch of reform cities established in Hubei Province, the provincial price bureau also proposed the structural adjustment of medical service prices by local price authorities on the basis of comprehensive consideration of the number of local hospital projects, medical service project income, drug income and other data, in accordance with certain principles, considering the government compensation policy. The price adjustment policy allows reasonable price differences between different regions, and covers all medical service items, so that the reform is more relevant to local realities.

Study Design and Data Sources

This study uses the health statistics data from the Health Commission and the patient compensation data of Medical Security Bureau of Yichang City in Hubei Province for empirical analysis. The health statistics cover the operation data of 108 hospitals (as of the end of 2019) in the city from January 2015 to December 2019. From these records, we obtained the outpatient and inpatient visits of each hospital, the income breakdown of outpatient and inpatient visits, the number of doctors, the number of nurses and the number of beds.

Since January 2018, the Urban and Rural Residents Basic Medical Insurance and the New Rural Cooperative Medical Insurance in Yichang City have been integrated, so the patient compensation data are only for the Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance. To exclude the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, the data span was set to January 2016 to December 2019. At the same time, since the zero-markup policy for drugs implemented in Yichang City in 2017 was only for urban public hospitals, we further screened the medical institutions for settlement, and only retained the data in the urban area of Yichang City. Therefore, the sample for the actual analysis of medical insurance reimbursement is the monthly settlement data of various medical institutions of Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance in Yichang City from January 2016 to December 2019, including the total cost of the month and the number of persons in the month, including 1361 reimbursement records of general outpatient service, 1293 reimbursement records of general inpatient service, and 26,276 reimbursement records of drug purchase in pharmacies.

We separately analyzed the changes and trends of the average cost of outpatients and inpatients, the total cost of pharmacies, the number of drug purchasers and the average cost of drug purchasers after the implementation of the zero-markup policy. Specifically, we assessed trends before and after the reforms to determine whether the zero-markup policy had an impact over time on patients’ medical spending in hospitals and on actual total spending. Through this approach, we aim to gain an in-depth understanding of whether the zero-markup policy has produced practical help in reducing the burden on patients.

Outcome Variables

The aim of this study was to investigate the effect of zero-markup policy on hospital medical expenditure and actual medical expenditure of outpatients and inpatients. This exploration provides insights into the impact of the implementation of zero-markup policies on alleviating the medical burden of outpatients and inpatients. Therefore, the outcome variables of this study include two dimensions. The medical cost variables, including the average cost of outpatient and inpatient visits and their composition, elucidate the changes in the financial burden of outpatient and inpatient visits after the implementation of the zero-markup policy and the reasons for them. Pharmacy-related expenditure variables—including total expenditure at pharmacies, average cost per purchase, and their derivative variable, number of patients purchasing medications. The variables of the two dimensions jointly reflect the changes in the actual economic burden of patients after the implementation of the zero-markup policy.

Statistical Analysis

Calculation of the Value of Structural Variation

Value of structural variation (VSV) refers to the difference between the proportion of the final value and the initial value of an item at a given time. When VSV>0, it indicates that the proportion of items increases over time, otherwise it shows a decreasing trend. The basic formula is as follows: VSV=Xi1-Xi0 (i indicates the serial number of a cost item; 0 is the initial stage, 1 is the final stage). Degree of structure variation (DSV) is defined as the sum of absolute changes in each cost component during a specific period, can effectively capture the dynamic shifts in cost composition ratios over time. The expression is as follows: DSV=Σ|Xi1–Xi0| (i=1, 2,……5). Contribution of Structure Variation (CSV) is used to indicate the extent to which certain cost items contribute to changes in the overall structure of health expenditure. It represents the absolute VSV of each cost item as a percentage of the total DSV. The expression is as follows: CSV=|Xi1–Xi0|/DSV×100% (i=1, 2,……5).

ITSA Analysis

We used ITSA, a robust quasi-experimental method, to evaluate the impact of the ZMDP on outpatient costs, inpatient costs, and pharmacy-related outcomes (total pharmacy expenditure, number of pharmacy purchases, and average cost per pharmacy purchase). Using Stata 17 software, we included monthly outcome variables from January 2016 to December 2019. The zero-markup reform was launched on July 31, 2017; therefore, we designated August 2017 as the reform boundary. Specifically, we assigned a value of 0 for the pre-reform period (January 2016 – July 2017) and 1 for the post-reform period (August 2017 – December 2019). where Yt is the outcome variable at time point t; Tt is a continuous time trend (1 to 48 months); Xt is a binary indicator for the post-policy period (0 before August 2017, 1 from August 2017 onward); Tt × Xt is the interaction term representing the post-policy slope change; Mm,t are month-of-year fixed effects (February through December, with January as the reference) to control for seasonal fluctuations; and ε_t is the error term. In this specification, β0 represents the baseline level at t=0, β1 estimates the pre-policy trend, β2 captures the immediate level change at the reform point, and β3 captures the change in slope after the reform; thus, β1+ β3 represents the post-policy trend.

To account for autocorrelation and heteroskedasticity inherent in time-series data, we estimated the model using Newey–West standard errors with one lag.

To address seasonality, we incorporated month-of-year fixed effects into the ITSA regression. This approach absorbs systematic annual fluctuations (eg, Lunar New Year effects, year-end billing cycles) that could otherwise bias the policy coefficient estimates.

To enhance interpretability, we estimated counterfactual trajectories—ie, the expected outcome values had the ZMDP not been implemented—by setting Xt and Tt × Xt to zero while retaining the estimated time trend and seasonal components. The difference between actual and counterfactual predictions at each post-policy time point represents the cumulative policy effect attributable to the ZMDP.

All monetary values are reported in nominal Chinese yuan (CNY) at the time of occurrence, with US dollar (USD) equivalents provided for international reference using annual average exchange rates of the respective years. To isolate policy effects from price fluctuations, monetary values were converted to constant 2016 CNY using monthly healthcare-specific Consumer Price Index (CPI). Exchange rates: 2016: US$1 = ¥6.6423; 2017: US$1 = ¥6.7518; 2018: US$1 = ¥6.6174; 2019: US$1 = ¥6.8985 (People’s Bank of China, 2020).

Analysis of Structural Variation

During the period of 2015–2019 years, the years with higher DSV were 2015–2016 and 2016–2017, with outpatient accounted for 4.62% and 4.45%, and inpatient accounted for 7.54% and 6.03%. The degree showed a slight decreasing trend over the following years. Moreover, the DSV of outpatient and inpatient medical expenditures within the years 2015–2019 in total was 9.41% and 18.13%, respectively.

The results reveal that the proportion of structural variation contribution of each cost item between the several year periods showed a different orientation. While in total, for both outpatient and inpatient services, the highest proportion of structural variation contribution from 2015 to 2019 was constituted by drug costs, accounting for 48.10% and 40.66% respectively, especially in 2016–2017 and 2017–2018, which was the year when the reform was launched in 2017. Next both are laboratory and medical supply costs. The contribution rates of these two items in outpatient expenses were 14.93% and 17.83% respectively, and in inpatient expenses were 14.39% and 15.17%. From 2015 to 2019, the two professional fees, treatment cost and surgery cost, aggregately accounted for only 14.48% and 11.03% in outpatient and inpatient services, respectively. Thus, in an overall perspective, the main factors affecting the changes in outpatient and inpatient medical service costs in Yichang City are drug costs, laboratory costs, and medical supply costs. Discussion

Structural variation analysis revealed a significant decline in the proportion of drug costs for both outpatient (41.67%→37.14%) and inpatient (34.97%→27.60%) services following the ZMDP. ITSA results further showed a marginally significant immediate outpatient cost reduction (β2= −4.431, p = 0.094), while inpatient costs transitioned to a significant monthly decline (β3= −49.144, p = 0.031). These findings are consistent with previous studies.21,28,29 By severing the financial link between hospitals and drug sales, the policy directly lowered patients’ pharmaceutical expenses.16,19

However, we also observed a notable increase in diagnostic and procedural costs—particularly laboratory tests and medical supplies—consistent with findings from other studies.18,27,29,31 This substitution effect can be explained by hospitals’ strategic response to revenue loss: laboratory tests and medical supplies offer higher profit margins and greater billing flexibility,32,33 making them a relatively accessible compensatory mechanism given limited government subsidies.16,33,34 Critically, the shift toward professional fees (treatments and surgeries) was minimal, accounting for only 14.48% and 11.03% of total structural changes in outpatient and inpatient services, respectively. This deviates from the policy’s original intent to rebalance hospital revenue toward service-based income.21,35 The asymmetry likely reflects implementation complexity: expanding diagnostic services requires fewer systemic changes than increasing professional service fees, which necessitate comprehensive fee schedule reforms.36

The ITSA results demonstrate that the ZMDP exerted differential effects on cost containment.21 For outpatient costs, the policy induced a marginally significant one-time reduction (β2= −4.431, p = 0.094) without altering the post-policy trend. For inpatient costs, however, a significant post-policy decline emerged (β3= −49.144, p = 0.031), indicating sustained cost control in the inpatient setting. Counterfactual predictions showed cumulative effects ranging from -¥4.73 (outpatient) to -¥1480 (inpatient) at 24 months post-policy. This differential effect may reflect inpatient care’s greater drug utilization and more complex service bundles, offering more substantial cost reduction potential.

The pharmacy-related outcomes revealed a coherent pattern: total pharmacy expenditure significantly decreased (β2= −953.507, p = 0.024), pharmacy purchase volume increased (β3= 1353.214, p = 0.034), and average cost per purchase declined (β3= −0.291, p < 0.001). This triad suggests that the policy successfully lowered drug prices while simultaneously expanding patient access to pharmacy services—lower per-purchase costs incentivized more frequent visits, yet aggregate expenditure still declined as price reductions outweighed volume increases. The significant pre-policy upward trend in average cost per purchase (β1= 0.441, p < 0.001) reversing to a significant decline post-policy (β3= −0.291, p < 0.001) further supports successful price control.

For healthcare leaders and policymakers, these findings offer three actionable implications. First, hospital executives should implement internal cost monitoring systems that track both drug and non-drug expenditure categories to prevent cost substitution. Second, City Health Commissions should establish integrated surveillance systems that monitor medication purchases across both hospital and retail settings—currently tracked separately—to detect hidden cost transfers and regulatory blind spots.18,37 Third, the limited shift toward professional fees suggests that medical service price adjustments alone may be insufficient to rebalance hospital cost structures; complementary strategies such as value-based payment reforms and enhanced government subsidies are needed to better align hospital incentives with patient-centered care.38

This study has several strengths, including comprehensive data coverage (108 hospitals, 496 annual reports, and monthly insurance settlement data) and robust quasi-experimental methods (ITSA with month-of-year fixed effects and counterfactual predictions). However, several limitations warrant consideration.

First, the single-city design limits generalizability. Future research should employ multi-city data to enhance external validity. Second, the single-group ITSA design cannot definitively rule out contemporaneous regional shifts or other co-occurring policies. Future research using control regions or synthetic control methods could strengthen causal attribution. Third, the study period ended in 2019; the 29-month post-policy window may be insufficient to capture long-term adaptive responses. Future studies should extend the observation period. Fourth, while pharmacy data capture expenditure and frequency, we did not measure patient out-of-pocket costs or clinical outcomes. Future research should link expenditure data with patient-level outcomes. Fifth, placebo tests for outpatient cost and average cost per pharmacy purchase remained significant (p < 0.01), suggesting more complex cyclical patterns not fully captured by month-of-year fixed effects. Future research using more sophisticated seasonal adjustment methods or longer time series could better isolate seasonal effects.

Conclusions

This study evaluated the ZMDP’s impacts on financial burden and pharmaceutical channel migration using SVA and ITSA with month-of-year fixed effects and counterfactual predictions. The policy effectively reduced hospital drug expenditures, with outpatient costs showing a marginally significant immediate reduction and inpatient costs transitioning to a significant monthly decline. However, the policy triggered unintended consequences, including cost substitution toward diagnostic services and pharmaceutical channel migration from hospitals to retail pharmacies. The pharmacy data revealed a coherent pattern: total expenditure declined, purchase volume increased, and per-purchase costs decreased—suggesting the policy successfully lowered drug prices while expanding patient access to pharmacy services. For healthcare leaders, these findings suggest that vertical price regulation without horizontal integration across care settings may simply relocate costs rather than reduce them. Future reforms should incorporate integrated prescription monitoring, transparent hospital-pharmacy financial disclosure, and complementary medical service pricing reforms to achieve comprehensive financial protection and sustainable healthcare delivery.
