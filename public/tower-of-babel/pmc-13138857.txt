Enhancing precision in limb-salvage surgery
Authors: Sam Dhaene, Milan Roelens, Léonard Geudin, Gwen Sys
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsAchieving tumour-free margins and precise prosthetic alignment remains an important challenge in limb-salvage surgery (LSS). Malalignment can compromise limb function and implant longevity, whereas inadequate margins increase the risk of local recurrence. Therefore, this study aims to develop a novel patient-specific guide (PSG) concept that integrates both osteotomy and prosthetic alignment into a single, user-friendly platform.MethodsA multifunctional PSG was developed, composed of two primary components: a patient-specific insert that is precisely contoured to the patient’s bone anatomy, and a reusable universal frame that envelops the patient-specific element. The universal frame serves as a central platform to attach auxiliary instrumentation guides that facilitate osteotomy, reaming, and prosthetic alignment. A proof-of-concept study was conducted on ten cadaveric femora. Following CT imaging, virtual preoperative planning was performed in a 3D environment. The patient-specific parts of the guide were 3D-printed based on the virtual osteotomy and prosthetic alignment planes. The guide system was sequentially applied to perform the osteotomy and prosthesis placement on each specimen. Accuracy was assessed by comparing postoperative CT scans to the preoperative virtual plans, quantifying alignment and resection errors across all planes.ResultsThe PSG achieved sub-degree accuracy in coronal and sagittal alignment, with median deviations of 0.4° in both planes and a median rotational deviation of 1.2° (IQR 0.2° to 2.2°). Resection height deviation was 2.4 mm (IQR 1.9 to 3.6), with 90% of cases within a 6 mm margin. No significant correlation was observed between angular and translational errors, indicating no cross-dimensional propagation of error.ConclusionThis cadaveric proof-of-concept study demonstrates that this innovative multifunctional PSG enables precise intraoperative execution of virtual preoperative plans. By integrating both resection and prosthetic alignment into a single, user-friendly, and cost-effective platform, it addresses an unmet need in limb salvage surgery and could support precision-driven standardization in oncological limb reconstruction.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2026;15(5):451–460.

Introduction

Malignant bone tumours remain a major challenge in orthopaedic oncology. Despite representing only 0.2% of all cancers, these aggressive neoplasms (most commonly osteosarcoma, Ewing’s sarcoma, and chondrosarcoma) predominantly affect children, adolescents, and young adults, and often require complex, multimodal medical and surgical therapy.1 Amputation was long regarded as the gold-standard treatment, however advances in diagnostic imaging, effective multiagent chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, and improvements in reconstructive techniques have shifted the paradigm toward limb salvage surgery (LSS) as the preferred approach for most patients.2

LSS aims to achieve oncological control while simultaneously preserving and restoring limb functionality.2 When tumour-free resection margins are obtained, LSS provides overall and disease-specific survival rates equivalent, or even superior to amputation, offering significantly better postoperative function and quality of life (QoL).2-6 Securing adequate surgical margins is critical, as it is the single-most important prognostic factor for local tumour control and survival.7 On the other hand, overly aggressive resection of healthy tissue in pursuit of wider margins can needlessly kill bone and soft-tissue, complicating reconstruction and impairing limb function.

At present, limb preservation is recommended for up to 90% of patients with malignancies affecting the distal femur.8 After tumour resection, the resulting bone defect is typically reconstructed using a modular endoprosthesis (especially for large juxta-articular defects) or biological material, such as bone grafts.8,9

Despite these advances, LSS procedures remain highly complex and challenging. Their prolonged operation times increase the risk of perioperative complications (e.g., infection, major blood loss, soft-tissue damage) which can significantly impair postoperative limb functionality. With improving patient survival rates, mid- to long-term implant-related complications, such as periprosthetic fracture, mechanical failure, instability, and especially aseptic loosening, have become increasingly important, as they can culminate in implant failure and the need for revision surgery.4,5,10-12 Aseptic loosening, the leading cause of late prosthetic failure, is often a consequence of malalignment, which induces uneven load distribution and subsequent progressive bone resorption at the implant-bone interface.13-15 Therefore, achieving optimal alignment of the prosthetic component in all planes is crucial to maximize implant longevity and limb functionality.

Yet in current practice, resection and reconstruction are often performed ‘freehand’, relying largely on the surgeon’s experience. Even for expert surgeons, anatomical variability, loss of normal landmarks following resection, and impaired visualization often hinder accurate execution of the operative plan.16 Studies show that freehand distal femur osteotomies can deviate by up to 15 mm from the intended plan, potentially leading to unintended intralesional excisions.17,18 Furthermore, when multiple osteotomies are required, as is often the case in LSS, small inaccuracies can accumulate and compound the overall deviation. Accurate prosthetic alignment presents an additional technical challenge, particularly in the absence of standardized guidelines for rotational positioning of tumour endoprostheses. Surgeons often use the linea aspera as an anatomical landmark for the true posterior aspect of the femur, however, recent studies have demonstrated that its reliability is inconsistent and subject to considerable anatomical variation.19-21 As a result, implant alignment in LSS is often performed by approximation, potentially compromising limb function and risking long-term implant failure.

In light of these challenges, various computer-assisted tumour surgery (CATS) technologies have been explored to improve intraoperative accuracy. Computer-assisted navigation and augmented reality (AR) can enhance surgical precision. However, these methods also face limitations, such as high costs, steep learning curves, radiation exposure, and technical malfunctions.16,17,22-25 There remains a clear need for a user-friendly solution that ensures consistent accuracy in tumour resection and prosthesis placement without such drawbacks. Patient-specific guides (PSG) have demonstrated improved osteotomy accuracy and are widely adopted in conventional TKA. (e.g., Zimmer Biomet’s Signature ONE).17,18 However, existing PSG implementations are typically restricted to osteotomy guidance and neglect accurate prosthetic positioning, which limits their use case in reconstructive surgery following tumour resection. To bridge this gap, this proof-of-concept (POC) study introduces a novel, multifunctional PSG that accurately reproduces the preoperatively defined surgical plan. The innovation of this approach lies in integrating precise guiding for both the osteotomy and prosthetic alignment into a single modular device, streamlining each surgical step. This novel surgical guide is designed to enhance surgical precision, safeguard oncological margins, preserve surrounding healthy tissue, facilitate optimal prosthetic positioning, and ultimately aims to improve patient outcomes.

Methods

This proof-of-concept study was conducted with the approval of the University Ethics Committee (internal reference number ONZ-2024-0381; study ID 19740). Ten Thiel-embalmed male cadaveric femora were obtained through the institution’s anatomical donation programme. Specimens were selected based on the absence of significant anatomical deformity that would preclude a standard endoprosthetic reconstruction.

Prototype guide design

The key innovation is the patient-specific guide’s multicomponent design: 1) a disposable patient-specific insert, 2) a reusable universal frame, and 3) attachable auxiliary instrumentation guides (Figure 1).

Stepwise assembly and workflow of the multifunctional patient-specific guide.

The patient-specific insert is circumferentially conformal to the topography of the distal femur at the level of the planned osteotomy site. This ensures that the guide can only be seated in one unique position, constraining both rotational and axial degrees of freedom. The insert is custom-fabricated for each case using 3D printing technology. The universal frame, CNC-machined from aluminium, functions as a reusable central platform that envelops the patient-specific insert. This platform features standardized mounting points that accommodate interchangeable auxiliary instrumentation guides, including sawblade guides for osteotomy, reamer guides for canal preparation, and alignment guides for prosthesis positioning. Because this design preserves a single reference frame throughout the entire procedure, it enables attachment of the auxiliary guides in a known spatial position relative to the bone. This in turn allows precise multiplanar positioning of the prosthesis.

Virtual planning

CT imaging was performed for each specimen (120 kVp, 200 mAs, 0.875 × 0.875 mm in-plane resolution, 0.3 mm slice thickness). A virtual preoperative plan was created using 3D Slicer (version 5.2.2, Harvard Medical School, USA) with custom developed extensions.

For each specimen a 3D bone model was generated through semi-automated segmentation of the CT data, followed by manual refinement. The osteotomy plane was defined at 12 cm proximal to the joint line to evaluate guide positioning under more challenging geometrical conditions, as this level provides a less distinctive cortical surface than more metaphyseal regions. The anterior reference point was defined as the intersection between 1) the anterior cortex and 2) the plane perpendicular to the posterior condylar line that passes through the centre point of the femoral cross-section at the osteotomy level. A custom-programmed software module automatically positioned the virtual guide model at the planned location on the femur, enabling virtual rehearsal of all surgical steps: osteotomy execution, canal reaming, and prosthesis alignment. The final guide geometry was generated by performing a Boolean subtraction of the femoral surface from the guide model incorporating a uniform 1.5 mm offset. This offset ensured anatomical conformity of the guide by accounting for the periosteum, segmentation uncertainties, and manufacturing tolerances.

Experimental setup

The PSG system was subsequently used to perform the osteotomy and distal femur prosthesis placement on all ten specimens. For this proof-of-concept study, 3D-printed surrogate prostheses were used to replicate a standard distal femur reconstruction implant. The surrogate stems were intentionally undersized relative to the endomedullary canal, ensuring sufficient latitude for deviation across all anatomical planes. In laboratory setting, each specimen was secured in a mounting apparatus that replicated standard operative positioning. The surgical workflow followed standard oncological limb salvage protocols, with all steps guided by the PSG system (Figure 2). First, the patient-specific insert was positioned on the distal femur, according to the preoperative plan. Next, the universal frame was assembled and secured around the insert. For the osteotomy, the saw guide attachment was mounted to the universal frame and resection was performed through the predefined cutting slot. After completing the osteotomy, the saw guide was removed and the reaming guide was attached to the same universal frame, maintaining alignment with the pre-planned femoral canal axis. Subsequent reaming prepared the intramedullary canal to accommodate the prosthetic stem. For prosthesis placement, an alignment guide was affixed to guide insertion of the surrogate endoprosthesis. A secondary alignment cap was used to control the rotational orientation and joint line height of the prosthesis during cementing. Following final prosthesis fixation, the entire guide system was removed.

Accuracy evaluation

The goal of the analysis is to assess the accuracy of reproducing the planned prosthetic orientation using the novel guide concept. Surgical accuracy was evaluated by comparing postoperative CT scans to the corresponding preoperative plans. Key geometrical variables were measured to quantify deviation between intended and achieved surgical outcomes: 1) angular inaccuracies, including rotational alignment error (angular deviation around the femoral axis), coronal plane alignment error (varus/valgus), and sagittal plane alignment error (flexion/extension); and 2) translational inaccuracies including axial proximal-distal resection height offset.

To decouple these metrics, planned and postoperative models were co-registered using rigid body alignment in 3D Slicer for independent quantification. Each variable was calculated by comparing the spatial coordinates of eight reproducible reference points between the planned and postoperative models. Saw-blade width (1.5 mm) was accounted for in all height-offset calculations.

Statistical analysis

Angular errors (°) and translational errors (mm) are presented as medians and IQRs. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess relationships among the error metrics and to determine whether systematic error propagation occurred across different planes. For all statistical analyses, p < 0.05 was considered significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R v. 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria).

Results

All ten cadaveric distal femur reconstructions were completed successfully using the novel patient-specific guide system. The patient-specific insert demonstrated anatomical conformity and a secure fit in all cases. The complete guide system was removed smoothly after each prosthesis cementing without complications.

Postoperative CT analysis revealed high precision in osteotomy height. The median resection height deviation was 2.4 mm (IQR 1.9 to 3.6). A height deviation of ≤ 6 mm was observed in nine out of the ten specimens (90%), with one specimen exhibiting an outlier deviation of 7.9 mm.

Final femoral component alignment demonstrated excellent concordance with preoperative planning (Figure 3 overlay). Median errors were 0.4° (IQR 0.2° to 0.9°) in the coronal plane and 0.4° (IQR 0.2° to 2.2°) in the sagittal plane. Additionally, the in-plane median rotational error of the endoprosthesis was 1.2° (IQR 0.2° to 2.2°), with only two specimens exceeding 3° of rotational error. The modular design facilitated seamless transitions between surgical steps (osteotomy, canal reaming, and prosthesis alignment) without requiring intraoperative readjustments or repositioning. The sawblade guide slot consistently produced clean, flat osteotomy surfaces with no evidence of blade wobble or cutting artifacts. The universal frame design enabled efficient workflow, maintaining alignment references throughout all surgical phases without manual readjustment.

Discussion

This proof-of-concept study demonstrates that the novel multifunctional PSG system can reliably reproduce the preoperative surgical plan. The guide achieved high precision in both osteotomy and endoprosthetic alignment, with minimal linear and angular deviations from the plan. Median angular alignment errors were 0.4° in both coronal and sagittal planes, the median rotational deviation was 1.2°, and the median resection height deviation was 2.4 mm. These results represent a substantial improvement over freehand resection techniques, which have been reported to result in resection height deviations of up to ~ 15 mm and rotational errors exceeding 10°.17,18,21 No significant correlations were found between translational and angular errors, suggesting that inaccuracies in one dimension did not systematically propagate to others. This indicates independent control of each surgical step during the procedure.

The accuracy improvements afforded by this guide concept could have important clinical implications. In limb salvage orthopaedic oncology, the primary goal is to achieve adequate tumour-free surgical margins while preserving as much healthy tissue as possible. The minimal height deviations achieved by our guide system can give surgeons confidence that the planned resection boundaries are being accurately reproduced intraoperatively. This precision is vital, as achieving wide tumour-free margins is the single-most important prognostic factor for local tumour control and survival.7 Inadequate margins substantially increase local recurrence risk: for example, a marginal resection (osteotomy through the tumour’s reactive zone) is associated with recurrence rates up to 70%, while an intralesional resection (osteotomy through tumour tissue) approaches a 100% local recurrence rate.26,27

The single outlier observed (7.9 mm height deviation) likely reflects a guide-positioning error or anatomical variation not fully accounted for during planning. This underscores the continued need for intraoperative vigilance and verification even when using patient-specific instruments.

Beyond oncological safety, the guide system’s precise endoprosthetic alignment is expected to have meaningful implications for implant longevity and limb functionality. Even small implant malalignments produce uneven mechanical loading at the bone-implant interface, triggering cortical bone resorption, and ultimately aseptic loosening, the primary cause of late prosthetic failure.14 By achieving excellent multiplanar alignment accuracy, our system should optimize load distribution and minimize stress concentrations on the implant and surrounding bone, thereby mitigating the mechanical drivers contributing to aseptic loosening. In turn, this improved alignment may promote better restoration of limb mechanics and function.

When benchmarked against alternative technologies in the field, PSGs offer distinct advantages in accessibility and workflow efficiency. Computer-assisted navigation systems can achieve comparable accuracy (0.3 to 4.0 mm error) but they are limited by costly equipment requirements, steep learning curves, and substantially increased operating times (up to 30% longer procedures).16,22,28 They also carry additional risks such as registration errors and line-of-sight interference that can disrupt tracking.16,22 Augmented reality (AR) approaches likewise show promise for improving accuracy, but current AR systems face limitations including surgeon visual fatigue, tracking inaccuracies, and high costs.24

Unlike navigation systems that provide real time intraoperative feedback, conventional PSI instruments lack intraoperative adjustability once manufactured. Our PSG design addresses this limitation through a modular architecture, in which only the small, patient-specific insert is customized for each case, while the larger universal frame and auxiliary guides remain standardized. The uniform design of the universal frame allows the surgeon to become familiar with its handling, potentially improving ease of use and reducing the learning curve across cases. The insert features full bone annular surface contouring, which constrains the guide to a unique anatomical position, preventing major placement errors and protecting surrounding neurovascular structures. The modular design also enables efficient production of multiple contingency inserts (Plan A, B, and C) for each patient at minimal additional expense. Such intraoperative flexibility is paramount in oncological surgery, where the true tumour extent may differ from preoperative imaging. Furthermore, the design facilitates seamless transitions between osteotomy, reaming, and prosthesis placement without repositioning and does not require the use of K-wires or Kirschner pins. This continuity could help reduce operating time.

Existing PSI solutions generally address only the resection phase. In contrast, our PSG uniquely combines osteotomy and prosthesis alignment into a single workflow, delivering comprehensive support for limb salvage reconstruction. With its ability to reproducibly achieve sub-degree and millimetric fidelity to the surgical plan, the pivotal question becomes not whether precise alignment is possible, but what the optimal targets are.

Despite these promising results, several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, as a cadaveric study, it did not replicate the full complexity of a live surgical environment. The absence of soft tissues, bulky tumours, and intraoperative bleeding could lead to an overestimation of accuracy. Our modular design’s ability to provide alternate inserts for different scenarios may help mitigate some of these challenges, but this has yet to be validated clinically. Second, although the guide is designed to minimize exposure, limited soft-tissue dissection with periosteal preservation is necessary to seat the guide accurately in vivo. Third, the sample size was small (n = 10, all male), which limits generalizability across sexes, ages, and anatomical variations. Fourth, the 3D-printed surrogate implants replicated stem geometry, but not cement interdigitation or mechanical properties of commercial devices. Additionally, all osteotomies in this study were performed at fixed distances on normal bones, whereas clinical tumours vary in size, location, and surrounding pathology. The selected osteotomy level (12 cm proximal to the joint line) corresponds to a femoral segment with a more cylindric cross-section, which may underestimate alignment variability seen at more distal levels. The study did not assess operating time, radiation exposure, learning curves, cost-effectiveness, stability of the guide under surgical forces, cement quality, functional outcomes, or long-term survival. Future clinical trials must evaluate these parameters to establish clinical utility.

Building on these promising results, several avenues warrant further investigation. Extending the guide system to also assist with the tibial component alignment represents the logical next step, as optimal knee reconstruction requires precise positioning of both femoral and tibial components. Technical refinements in the guide’s design, such as reducing its overall footprint and incorporating a secure snap-fit or clamping closure mechanisms, could enhance intraoperative handling and flexibility. Integration with AI-driven planning algorithms may further streamline the guide design process and reduce interoperator variability in surgical planning. Most critically, prospective in vivo clinical trials are needed to validate the PSG systems’ performance in clinical settings. Such studies should evaluate not only alignment metrics, but also oncological outcomes, functional metrics, and complication rates compared to standard techniques. Cost-benefit analyses comparing this modular PSG approach against freehand techniques and navigation-assisted surgery will also be important for determining its value and encouraging widespread adoption. Lastly, the principles of this guide system may be applicable to other complex procedures that demand high-precision bone reconstructions.

In summary, this proof-of-concept study demonstrates that this novel multifunctional PSG system can accurately translate preoperative virtual planning into intraoperative execution. Unlike existing patient-specific instruments, which are limited to osteotomy guidance, this system uniquely integrates both tumour resection and endoprosthetic alignment into a single modular platform, providing support throughout the entire limb salvage procedure. The modular design – featuring interchangeable patient-specific inserts, a reusable universal frame, and attachable auxiliary instrumentation guides – enables efficient, cost-effective manufacturing while achieving millimetric and sub-degree accuracy. This level of surgical precision should improve oncological safety, optimize implant positioning, and contribute to better functional outcomes, enhanced long-term prosthesis survival, and reduced complication rates.

Ultimately, integrating such precision tools into routine oncological practice may help redefine technical standards in limb salvage reconstruction.
