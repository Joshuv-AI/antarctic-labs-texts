Molecular insight into microtubule nucleation by the XMAP215/γ-TuRC module
Authors: Collin T. McManus, Sophie M. Travis, Philip D. Jeffrey, Rui Zhang, Sabine Petry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
It has become increasingly evident in recent years that nucleation of microtubules from a diverse set of microtubule organizing centers (MTOCs) requires both the γ-tubulin ring complex (γ-TuRC) and the microtubule polymerase XMAP215. Despite their essentiality, little is known about how these nucleation factors interact and work together to generate microtubules. Using biochemical domain analysis of XMAP215 and structural approaches, we find that the XMAP215 C-terminal region interacts broadly with γ-TuRC, involving a sixth TOG domain which binds γ-tubulin. Moreover, TOG6 is required for XMAP215 to promote nucleation from γ-TuRC to its full extent. Interestingly, we find that XMAP215 also depends strongly on TOG5 for microtubule lattice binding and nucleation. We further report a cryo-EM structure of TOG5 bound to the microtubule lattice that reveals promotion of lateral interactions between tubulin dimers. While XMAP215 constructs’ effects on nucleation are generally proportional to their effects on polymerization, formation of a direct complex with γ-TuRC allows cooperative nucleation activity. Thus, we propose that XMAP215’s C-terminal TOGs 5 and 6 play key roles in nucleation by promoting formation of longitudinal and lateral bonds in nascent γ-TuRC-templated microtubules at cellular MTOCs.

Introduction

Microtubules are hollow, cylindrical protein polymers assembled from ɑβ-tubulin heterodimers. They are involved in a range of essential cellular functions, including intracellular organization and transport, resilience to mechanical stress, cell motility, and chromosome segregation. Although purified tubulin will form, or nucleate, microtubules spontaneously at sufficient concentrations in vitro, cells regulate where and when microtubule nucleation occurs through localization of nucleation factors1,2. These factors aid nucleation by promoting assembly of tubulin oligomers. Essential for the nucleation of microtubules in a eukaryotic cell is the microtubule nucleation module consisting of the γ-tubulin ring complex (γ-TuRC) and Xenopus microtubule assembly protein of 215 kDa (XMAP215)/ch-TOG3–6.

γ-TuRC promotes nucleation by templating tubulin assembly in a geometry roughly matching the helical parameters of a microtubule7–11. It presents a ring of 14 γ-tubulin monomers, each supported by one of five γ-tubulin complex proteins (GCPs 2-6). Importantly, γ-TuRC’s overall architecture is splayed open compared to the microtubule, such that the template is imperfect with low activity in vitro10,12,13. Nevertheless, computational modeling, nucleation assays using purified components, and structural studies have demonstrated that assembly of tubulin onto the γ-TuRC template provides sufficient lateral bond energy to close the γ-TuRC ring, a conformation in which spoke 14 overlaps and occludes spoke 110,12,14,15. γ-TuRC thus consistently nucleates and caps 13 protofilament microtubules14–16. γ-TuRC was thought to be the only essential nucleator until it was shown that XMAP215 is equally important, and moreover acts synergistically with γ-TuRC, an activity that had been posited nearly two decades earlier4,17,18.

XMAP215 and its orthologs are plus-end tracking proteins that promotes microtubule polymerization by recruiting soluble tubulin19–23. Tubulin binding by XMAP215 orthologs is achieved by a variable number of tumor overexpressed gene (TOG) domains; arrays of two TOG domains have been identified in yeast, three in C. elegans, and five in other eukaryotes like D. melanogaster and vertebrates24. Crystal structures from yeast, C. elegans, D. melanogaster, and human orthologs provide a full complement of structural models of the five XMAP215 TOG domains25–32. These domains share similar architectures, containing 12 helices in six HEAT repeats (HRs) A-F that stack to form a 60 Å alpha-solenoid paddle. Conserved residues in the intra-HR loops mediate binding to tubulin on the surface that is exposed on the microtubule exterior25–28,32,33. Despite their homology, the TOG domains in pentameric XMAP215 orthologs have structural differences conserved at their positions in the TOG array that are thought to establish XMAP215’s orientation on the microtubule plus end and allow for processive polymerization25,29–31. Polymerization activity has been further linked to microtubule lattice affinity conferred by the flexible linkers following TOG2 and TOG430,33–35.

More recently, it was found that XMAP215 and its homologs in lower eukaryotes are also required for microtubule nucleation, that XMAP215 directly binds to γ-TuRC, and that both components form a module that synergistically nucleates a microtubule in the cell4–6,10,36–38. Although co-recruitment may often be transient, XMAP215 has also been reported to colocalize with γ-TuRC in vitro and at centriolar subdistal appendages, where it may play a role in anchoring microtubules4,5,12,17,20. This anchoring activity is consistent with XMAP215’s ability to nucleate microtubules when plus-end tracking is impeded by tethering to resin via C-terminal tags or immobilization on a glass surface18,23. However, precisely how XMAP215 contributes to microtubule nucleation with γ-TuRC and generates the microtubule cytoskeleton of the cell is yet to be uncovered.

In this study, we characterize the structures and activities of multiple domains to reveal how XMAP215 helps nucleate microtubules with γ-TuRC. We show that there is indeed a TOG6 domain C-terminal to TOG5 in XMAP215, and that, while TOG6 plays a role that specifically enhances the efficiency of nucleation, TOG5 functions to promote both nucleation and polymerization. Our work indicates general rules of how polymerization and nucleation are linked and helps explain how the microtubule cytoskeleton is formed.

Results

XMAP215’s C-terminal region contains a TOG domain with unique architecture

Since XMAP215’s polymerase activity relies on its N-terminal TOG domains, which are interspersed by flexible linkers, we wondered whether domains might exist in its C-terminal region which facilitate its co-nucleation activity with γ-TuRC. Based on secondary structure prediction and solution NMR data of the D. melanogaster XMAP215 ortholog39,40, we hypothesized that a sixth TOG domain (TOG6) resides within XMAP215’s C-terminal domain, which may have evolved a nucleation-specific role.

First, we used AlphaFold241 to predict the structure of the Xenopus laevis full length XMAP215 (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Consistent with prior structural data, the prediction includes five canonical TOG domains, each connected by flexible linkers24,25,28–31. Strikingly, downstream of TOG5 in what has previously been referred to as the XMAP215 C-terminal domain, an additional domain was predicted. It is also composed of 6 pairs of alpha helices arranged in a paddle, consistent with general TOG morphology, and thus provides further support for the putative TOG6 domain (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1a, b), though it is the most divergent from the other XMAP215 TOG domains (Supplementary Fig. 1a, c). To assess this prediction, we crystallized TOG6 and collected a low-resolution dataset (~6 Å), which we solved by molecular replacement (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1c and Supplementary Table 1). This revealed density consistent with the helices in the TOG6 prediction. In addition, a four-helix bundle (FHB) stands out against the unstructured linkers and individual alpha-helices of the rest of the C-terminal domain prediction (Fig. 1a). This domain occurs 18 amino acids downstream of TOG6 and resembles the last 4 helices of some TOG domains (Supplementary Fig. 1e). To assess whether the TOG6 domain is evolutionarily conserved, we performed AlphaFold2 predictions for XMAP215 orthologs across species. We found TOG6 domains within the C-terminal regions of flies, humans, and mice (Fig. 1c, d). In the C. elegans ortholog ZYG-9, which has only three previously described TOG domains, and whose TOG3 is structurally homologous to XMAP215 TOG528,29, a TOG4 domain was also predicted with analogous fold to XMAP215 TOG6 (Fig. 1c, d). This suggests that this domain has a meaningful and specific function requiring its structural conservation on par with the other TOG domains. Moreover, the FHB downstream of TOG6 is also conserved across these species (Fig. 1e), suggesting a conserved function.

Given that XMAP215’s TOG domains have unique structures that are conserved with respect to their positions in the TOG array, we asked how TOG6 compares to XMAP215’s other TOG domains. For comparison, we aligned the six TOG domains from the X. laevis XMAP215 AlphaFold2 prediction (Supplementary Fig. 1a) on the best conserved HRs A-C (Fig. 1f)29. The most extreme difference between TOG6 and the other TOG domains is in the kink in the alpha solenoid stack that occurs between HRs C and D (Fig. 1f). When viewed from the non-tubulin binding surface, TOGs1, 2, and 3 exhibit similar kinks, with planes generated from HRs A-C and D-F intersecting at approximately −28° (Supplementary Fig. 1d). TOG4 exhibits a kink in the opposite direction but of similar magnitude with an angle of +22.8°. The kink in TOG5 of −7.6° is negligible, giving it an overall straight geometry as previously noted28,29. The kink in TOG6 is greatly exaggerated at +68°, strongly differentiating it from the N-terminal TOGs (Supplementary Fig. 1d). We also noticed that TOG6 HRs A, B, and C appear more staggered than in the other TOG domains, with a shorter HR A sitting higher, and the longer HRs B and C reaching lower (Fig. 1f). In sum, the unique structures of TOG domains 4, 5 and 6 imply they may have unique functions.

XMAP215’s C-terminal domains are required for its function as a co-nucleator with γ-TuRC

We next wondered whether the domains identified in XMAP215’s C-terminal region are involved in its nucleation activity with γ-TuRC. To address this, we used total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy and time lapse imaging to capture the effects of various C-terminal deletion constructs of XMAP215 on nucleation of single microtubules from γ-TuRCs tethered to passivated coverslips (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Movie 1)10,12,13,15. Plots of microtubule mass under these conditions show that the C-terminal deletions reduce total microtubule signal, but this could be due to changes in nucleation rates, polymerization rates, or both (Fig. 2c). In comparison to full-length XMAP215, the XMAP215ΔTOG6 construct, which is missing only the amino acids comprising TOG6, nucleates roughly 2-fold slower. Further deletion of both TOG6, the FHB, and the intervening linker in the XMAP215ΔTOG6ext construct caused a roughly 4-fold decrease in nucleation compared to the full-length protein, on par with the TOG1-5 construct which lacks the C-terminal domain entirely (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 2b). Compared to full-length XMAP215, all constructs produce similar microtubule growth rates, achieved comparable maximum microtubule lengths, and cause polymerization faster than the buffer condition (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Although the microtubule polymerization rate appeared slightly lower for TOG1-5 than the other XMAP215 constructs, this difference was only significant when compared with XMAP215ΔTOG6 and could possibly be attributed to the use of a bacterial expression system for TOG1-5 as opposed to the eukaryotic expression system used for the others (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Tubulin’s GTPase activity can abort inefficient microtubule nucleation, as can catastrophe factors43–45. If a direct interaction were to allow XMAP215 to localize to a naked γ-TuRC, it could recruit soluble tubulin to the nucleation template and help overcome cellular barriers to nucleation. Thus, we hypothesized that stronger localization to γ-TuRC would correlate with more efficient microtubule nucleation, so we next dissected the impact of our XMAP215 C-terminal deletions on microtubule minus end localization. Specifically, we analyzed localization of XMAP215-GFP constructs in anonymized kymographs of γ-TuRC nucleated microtubules. While all the constructs tested tracked the plus end, we detected four behaviors of XMAP215-GFP constructs with regards to minus end localization (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 2d). (i) GFP localization to the origin of nucleation could occur before tubulin signal was visible then depart as the microtubule began elongating (before), (ii) GFP signal could coincide with the appearance of tubulin signal and remain at the minus end following nucleation (after), (iii) GFP could localize before tubulin signal appeared and also remain bound after the microtubule began polymerizing (both), (iv) and other times no minus end localization was observed (neither) (Fig. 2e). We interpret the GFP signal in each of these behaviors to represent multiple molecules of a given XMAP215 construct, as the entire population was tagged in these experiments, and a single molecule cannot exhibit two simultaneous localizations. In the after and both cases, we submit that molecules remaining at the minus end may have been involved in nucleation but failed to begin plus-tracking when polymerization began, instead remaining anchored at the capped minus-end. Quantifying the frequency of each localization behavior, we observed full length XMAP215 localized to the minus end before and after nucleation with frequencies of 73% and 49% respectively, similar to what has been previously reported12. These frequencies were higher than for the C-terminal deletion constructs (Fig. 2f, g), and the frequency of localization to the minus end after nucleation also appeared to correlate with the size of each C-terminal deletion (Fig. 2g). Accordingly, cases in which the XMAP215 construct exhibited no localization to the minus end were less frequent for the full-length construct than the C-terminal deletions (Fig. 2h). These results are consistent with a model in which XMAP215 relies on its C-terminal domains to localize to γ-TuRCs where it can then strengthen longitudinal and lateral interactions, which facilitate nucleation46, such that formation of a critical nucleus of tubulin dimers outpaces catastrophe.

Since deletion of TOG6 and the FHB impeded nucleation and minus-end localization, we wondered whether these domains contribute to binding γ-tubulin and γ-TuRC. We designed several GFP fusions of XMAP215 truncation and deletion constructs (Supplementary Fig. 3a) and assessed their binding to recombinant human γ-tubulin, αβ-tubulin, and native γ-TuRC from Xenopus laevis. Using a pull-down assay, we found that constructs which contained the TOG6 domain bound γ-tubulin significantly better than the negative GFP control, that this domain on its own was sufficient to bind, and deleting TOG6 through the FHB causes a failure to pull down significantly more γ-tubulin than the GFP control (Supplementary Fig. 3b, d). Moreover, binding to γ-tubulin appears unique to TOG6, though all TOG domains showed some binding (Supplementary Fig. 3c, d), and TOG6 fails to bind αβ-tubulin by gel filtration unlike TOGs 1, 2, 3, and 4 (Supplementary Fig. 3e). Interestingly, TOG5 also showed little binding to αβ-tubulin (Supplementary Fig. 3e), consistent with the previous observation that mutating TOG5 does not affect the number of tubulin dimers bound by full-length XMAP21533. Using a pull-down assay to assess binding to intact γ-TuRC, the physiologically relevant interaction partner, we found that TOG6 is sufficient to bind the complex while the N-terminal TOGs show no detectable interaction. While deletion of TOG6 from the C-terminal domain (CΔTOG6) did not weaken the interaction, a larger deletion in CΔTOG6ext did reduce binding, though it still bound more tightly than the GFP negative control (Supplementary Fig. 3f–i). These results suggest that XMAP215’s C-terminal interaction with γ-TuRC is not mediated by a single domain but rather involves multiple regions, including but not limited to TOG6 and the FHB. Previously, the affinities of yeast homolog Alp14’s TOG1 and TOG2 for tubulin were found to be highly salt-dependent, with affinity on the order of 100 nM at 100 mM KCl and 1–3 μM at 200 mM KCl. These are consistent with the physiological necessity for the polymerase to quickly bind and release tubulin32, and the presence of multiple weak interactions may similarly be the basis for XMAP215’s transient localization to γ-TuRC and subsequent release to begin its plus-tracking activity.

TOG5 localizes XMAP215 to the assembling microtubule lattice to drive nucleation and polymerization

Albeit to a lesser extent than full length XMAP215, γ-TuRC mediated microtubule nucleation is promoted by the presence of TOG1-5 (Fig. 2b–d, Supplementary Fig. 2b and Supplementary Movie 1). We thus sought to account for how this occurs in the absence of a direct interaction between γ-TuRC and the TOG1-5 construct. We considered that two nucleation factors, if they bind orthogonal tubulin surfaces, could promote assembly of the same tubulin oligomer through formation of an indirect complex23,35. We thus examined whether TOG1-5 is sufficient for XMAP215’s activity as an independent nucleation factor. Although nucleation cannot be observed as closely without tethered γ-TuRCs, we used TIRF microscopy to visualize spontaneously nucleated microtubules, which fell to the coverslip as a proxy. Over a five-minute period, roughly equivalent numbers of microtubules were detected in the presence of either XMAP215 or TOG1-5, and this number was greater than the buffer control (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b and Supplementary Movie 2). We therefore conclude that XMAP215 can promote spontaneous microtubule nucleation independently of γ-TuRC in vitro, as previously shown18,23,25,35, and TOG1-5 is sufficient for this activity. Moreover, since TOG1-5 is also sufficient for XMAP215’s polymerization activity (Supplementary Fig. 2c), our data support the previous suggestion that XMAP215’s polymerase and nucleation activity are linked35.

What region of XMAP215, then, could be responsible for binding the nascent microtubule lattice to form an indirect complex between TOG1-5 and γ-TuRC? We narrowed our search to the linker4 region and the TOG5 domain due to the following. Firstly, we suspected the linker4 region, which is comprised of the disordered 100 amino acids between TOG4 and TOG5 and is rich in lysine (15%), serine (20%), and proline (14%) residues, because it had been suggested to promote microtubule lattice binding and polymerization due to its high isoelectric point33–35. TOG5 was implicated in experiments in Drosophila S2 cells when point mutation analysis showed it contributed to microtubule localization of linker4-TOG5 constructs29,34. Additionally, in in vitro experiments with full-length XMAP215, mutations in the tubulin binding-surface of TOG5 did not affect tubulin binding by XMAP215, but did negatively affect microtubule polymerization rates33. This suggested that TOG5 contributes to microtubule polymerization in some way other than recruitment of soluble tubulin.

We therefore compared TOG1-5 with further XMAP215 truncations TOG1-4 and TOG1-linker4 for their ability to bind microtubule seeds stabilized with the slowly hydrolysable GTP analog, GMPCPP43. Although the tubulin assembly intermediates bound by XMAP215 during nucleation likely differ from microtubule seeds in conformation and curvature, such intermediates cannot be easily stabilized, so seeds must be used as a substitute. While inclusion of the linker4 microtubule binding domain barely improved the localization to stabilized microtubules compared to the TOG1-4 construct, TOG1-5 strongly localized to GMPCPP seeds (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). We exclude that this might be due to an avidity effect from the number of TOG domains because a TOG2-5 construct maintained the same stronger microtubule binding as TOG1-5 while having only four TOG domains, like TOG1-4 and TOG1-linker4. These data are consistent with a role of TOG5 in localizing XMAP215 to the nascent microtubule lattice during nucleation.

Does TOG5 positively impact the ability of XMAP215 to nucleate a microtubule? To address this question, we assessed γ-TuRC-dependent microtubule nucleation in the presence of TOG1-5, TOG1-linker4, and TOG1-4. Whereas TOG1-linker4 and TOG1-4 nucleated at similar rates, TOG1-5 stimulated nucleation roughly 2.5-fold faster (Fig. 3a–c, Supplementary Fig. 5a, and Supplementary Movie 3). Polymerization rates and maximum microtubule length promoted by these constructs reflected the trend observed for nucleation, and all constructs exhibited typical plus-end tracking (Fig. 3d, e and Supplementary Fig. 5b). Therefore, while all the constructs tested appear to be functional polymerases, the presence of TOG5 enhances both XMAP215’s polymerase activity and its ability to nucleate microtubules with γ-TuRC, and supports a model where XMAP215 constructs lacking the C-terminal region promote nucleation by interacting with γ-TuRC indirectly through αβ-tubulin assembling on the γ-tubulin template. These results could perhaps be explained if the deletion of TOG5 decreased overall recruitment of soluble tubulin to microtubules or to γ-TuRC, and it is possible that TOG5 has some affinity for soluble tubulin. Given the evidence, however, that TOG5 does not bind soluble tubulin as well as the other N-terminal TOG domains, and given our finding that TOG5 can help drive XMAP215 localization to tubulin lattice, in agreement with others29,33, we favor the explanation that TOG5’s contribution to microtubule nucleation and polymerization is through tubulin lattice recognition. We next wondered whether TOG5’s lattice binding or TOG6’s γ-TuRC binding ability might also promote microtubule nucleation from γ-TuRC in isolation. Previous works have established that arrays of multiple TOG domains are necessary for XMAP215 constructs to promote microtubule polymerization, though individual TOGs have been shown to influence the lag phase in light-scattering microtubule growth assays29,47. Because individual TOG domains should therefore lack the canonical polymerase activity of wild-type XMAP215, we used a higher concentration of tubulin (10 μM) to bias microtubule dynamic instability more towards growth and added TOG1, TOG4, TOG5, and TOG6 each at the high concentration of 1 μM so that we might detect subtle effects on microtubule assembly. We found that TOG1, TOG4, and TOG6 did not appreciably affect generation of microtubule mass when compared to the buffer condition, but TOG5 caused a marked increase (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b and Supplementary Movie 4). Accordingly, when we measured polymerization rates and microtubule lengths, only TOG5 caused significant increases relative to the other conditions (Supplementary Fig. 6e, f). TOG5 also caused about a 2.5-fold increase in microtubule nucleation rate, while TOG1 and TOG6 did not affect the rate, and TOG4 caused a more subtle, approximately 1.5-fold increase (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). Thus, our data suggest that although TOG6 contributes to XMAP215’s interaction with γ-TuRC, it does not change γ-TuRC’s conformation to induce nucleation. On the other hand, TOG5’s, and to a lesser extent TOG4’s, increase in nucleation from γ-TuRC is consistent with the previous finding that they decrease the lag phase in bulk polymerization assays29.

Notably, both TOG1 and TOG5 exhibited plus end tracking behavior (Supplementary Fig. 6g), suggesting that not only does XMAP215 track the plus end through a ratcheting mechanism involving serial incorporation of tubulin dimers27,32,47,48, but specifically recognizes tip-specific features through its TOG domains. To better understand how this single domain of XMAP215 aids in microtubule assembly, we next turned out attention to how TOG5 interacts with microtubule lattices and what features of the plus end specific lattice it recognizes.

Structure of TOG5 bound to the GMPCPP microtubule lattice

To study the structure of TOG5 bound to a tubulin lattice, we used single particle cryogenic-electron microscopy (cryo-EM). Given the challenges presented by structural study of heterogeneous targets like microtubule plus-ends and given that TOG5 helped recruit XMAP215 constructs to GMPCPP seeds, which are relatively homogeneous and stable, we chose to study TOG5 bound to GMPCPP stabilized microtubules (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). First, we applied microtubule seeds to holey carbon grids. We then washed them twice with highly concentrated TOG5-GFP having been desalted into EM buffer (see methods) to promote saturation of the microtubule lattice (Supplementary Fig. 7). After data collection, we applied a seam-search protocol for accurate particle alignment during processing, then generated a C1 reconstruction of the microtubule (Fig. 4a)49,50. The resulting 3.33 Å reconstruction (Supplementary Fig. 7) revealed the familiar density of the GMPCPP tubulin lattice in which ɑ- and β-tubulin can be distinguished by the microtubule luminal S9-S10 loop, which is longer in ɑ-tubulin. We next sought to better resolve the TOG5 density through further processing. We performed symmetry expansion using the measured helical parameters in our C1 reconstruction, then sorted the expanded particles according to TOG5 occupancy after signal subtraction of the tubulin density. Following subsequent local refinement using a mask around two tubulin dimers plus the TOG5, we obtained a reconstruction refined to 2.89 Å by GSFSC. This resolution estimation was driven largely by the tubulin density in which sidechains and helices are clearly resolved, while the TOG5 domain displayed more moderate local resolution (Supplementary Fig. 7). We are able to assign density to each helix of TOG5 and identify slightly higher resolution features nearer to the tubulin interface, including ordered loops and several bulky sidechains. This reconstruction was therefore sufficient to build two tubulin dimers, unambiguously dock TOG5 from an AlphaFold2 multimer prediction of XMAP215 TOG5 with αβ-tubulin, and adjust some TOG5 sidechains before coordinate refinement (Fig. 4b–e and Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). The microtubule exterior is recognized by TOG5’s intra-HR loops (Fig. 4b–d), a binding mode consistent with that observed for TOGs 1 and 2 from yeast orthologs (Stu2 and Alp14) bound to soluble tubulin26,27,32. This binding occurs primarily to a single tubulin dimer, burying 477 Å2 of the β-tubulin surface and 443 Å2 of the ɑ-tubulin surface, roughly the same magnitude observed for other TOG/tubulin structures (Supplementary Fig. 8c). TOG5’s unique HR0 buries 121 Å2 of a neighboring β-tubulin surface orthogonal to the primary binding site, an interaction which resembles previous predictions and is only possible in the tubulin lattice29.

Bulky sidechains resolved in our symmetry expanded map help provide atomistic detail regarding TOG5’s interaction with the tubulin lattice. The lateral contact formed by TOG5’s unique HR0 loop appears to involve a cation-π interaction between TOG5 R1222 and a secondary β-tubulin H307 (Fig. 4e). TOG5’s substantial interaction with α-tubulin is mediated by HRE and the second helix of TOG5 HRF. HRE’s contribution involves hydrogen bonding between T1413 and N1417 with the α-tubulin backbone, and R1416 with α-tubulin T309. In HRF, we observe K1444 forming a salt bridge with α-tubulin E113, M1448 involved in hydrophobic packing, and a pair of arginine residues R1452 and R1455 interacting with α-tubulin E414 and E420, respectively (Fig. 4e). To survey the possible contribution of other less well resolved residues in our reconstruction, we searched our model for TOG5 residues within 5 Å of tubulin side chains. The search yielded several highly conserved TOG5 residues in common between our structure and an AlphaFold2 multimer prediction of a TOG5/ɑβ-tubulin complex (Supplementary Fig. 8b, d–f and Supplementary Fig. 9a). Notably, F1250 in the TOG5 HRA loop packs against the backbone in the ɑ11’-ɑ12 loop of β-tubulin (Supplementary Fig. 8g). This contrasts with the analogous tryptophan residue which occurs in the other canonical XMAP215 TOG domains and binds the β-tubulin ɑ3 helix in TOG1 and TOG2 structures (Supplementary Fig. 11a)24,26,27,32. This altered binding site may help to explain the divergence of this residue in TOG5 from tryptophan to phenylalanine across orthologs. The HRB loop contributes to primary β-tubulin binding through H-bonding involving TOG5 T1289 and S1292, and finally HRD H-bonds with the β-tubulin backbone through S1371 and its own backbone (Supplementary Fig. 8g). We note that our reconstruction does not support an interaction between K1335 and β-tubulin Y98, unlike the analogous basic residue in HRC of e.g., Stu2 TOG1, R116 (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Thus, K1335 in HRC of TOG5 does not seem important for binding the microtubule and may offer an explanation for the weak effects of a TOG1-55AA (F1250A K1335A) mutant on microtubule nucleation Xenopus egg extract in a previous study4.

In a microtubule binding assay, a double mutant of HR0 R1222E and HRA F1250E (TOG5RFEE) abrogated microtubule binding compared to our wildtype TOG5 (TOG5WT) construct, as we expected from previous mutation analysis of TOG5 (Supplementary Fig. 10A, B)25,29. Similarly, two HRF double mutant constructs K1444E M1448E (TOG5MKEE) and R1452E R1455E (TOG5RREE), also abrogated localization to microtubule seeds, while a wildtype TOG1 (TOG1WT) construct exhibited weak localization (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). These results underscore the role for the last HRF helix of TOG5 as well as the HR0 and HRA loops in recognizing the microtubule lattice, and indicate that this interaction is significantly hampered by the introduction of negative charges, which repel the microtubule surface.

In examining our cryo-EM reconstructions, we noticed additional density near TOG5, which was positioned consistently with the C-terminal tubulin tails. These disordered tails are rich in glutamate residues, giving them a negative charge. By plotting Coulombic potential on the surface of our TOG5 model, we see that the tails are revealed at low contour near patches of positive charge on TOG5 (Supplementary Fig. 10c). This is consistent with XMAP215 having lower affinity for subtilisin treated microtubules21. Nonspecific electrostatic interactions with tubulin tails are therefore likely contributing to TOG5 microtubule localization in addition to specific sidechain interactions.

Having gained greater insight into how TOG5 interacts with tubulin, we wondered how it is able to discriminate between tubulin’s straight, lattice conformation and its bent, soluble conformation. We aligned TOG1/tubulin structure (PDB 4FFB) from the yeast XMAP215 ortholog Stu2 to our TOG5 model using the β-tubulin subunits as alignment anchors (Fig. 4e). In doing so, the differences between the bent and straight conformations of tubulin become clear, where the ɑ-tubulin subunit curves up towards the TOG domain by about 9 Å. Looking down the axis of the microtubule towards the minus end, we noticed that the TOG1 crosses over TOG5 (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 11a). From our chosen perspective, the loops of N-terminal HRs A and B of TOG1 bind to the right of TOG5’s, intermediate HRs C and D are relatively well aligned on the microtubule circumferential axis, and the C-terminal repeats E and F of TOG1 bind to the left of TOG5’s (Supplementary Fig. 11a). When comparing residues which are in analogous positions on TOG1 and TOG5 and which are within 5 Å of tubulin, we find mostly lateral displacements of magnitudes between 5 and 9 Å for HRs A, B, and E, mostly longitudinal displacements between 1 and 6 Å for HRs C and D, and a large 11 Å displacement for the HRF loops. Aligning the TOG1 and TOG5 models and generating a structure-based sequence alignment, we see that many of these residues in analogous positions are conserved (Supplementary Fig. 11b, c). This is curious given their differing binding positions and suggests that changes in fold can alter the binding site. Cumulatively, the differing binding site, the smaller kink in the alpha-solenoid of TOG5 compared to TOG1, and the bending of ɑ-tubulin in the soluble structure result in a severe clash with TOG5’s HRs E-F (Fig. 4f, g). Analogously, TOG1 interacts less with straight ɑ-tubulin (90 Å2 buried area) versus bent ɑ-tubulin (350 Å2 buried area) in our aligned structure (Fig. 4f), explaining its weaker microtubule localization in our GMPCPP seed binding assay (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). Thus, the features of TOG5 that allow it to bind ɑ- and β-tubulin in the straight conformation preclude it from binding bent tubulin, and the features that allow e.g., TOG1 to bind bent tubulin prevent it from strongly binding both monomers in lattice tubulin.

TOG5 senses nucleotide state and may prefer a tubulin conformation of intermediate curvature

We next investigated how TOG5 independently tracks the microtubule plus-end and considered whether nucleotide sensing might play a role, given what we know about MAPs EB1/3 and TPX2. EB1/3 tracks microtubule plus-ends by recognizing tubulin in the GDP-Pi hydrolysis transition state, which lies behind the GTP cap and is mimicked by the nucleotide analogue GTPγS51–53. Its preference for the compacted conformation of this lattice is strong enough to induce hydrolysis of GMPCPP54,55. TPX2 is also able to plus track at low concentrations, but instead prefers the GMPCPP microtubule lattice, which is expanded. GMPCPP microtubules are very stable due to their expansion and favorable arrangement of the nucleotide E-site, similar to Taxol microtubules, and have therefore historically been inferred to represent a GTP-like state23,43,54,56,57. Thus, given that XMAP215 and EB1 exhibit discrete localizations on the microtubule lattice52, we hypothesized that TOG5 would exhibit a lattice preference more similar to TPX2 than EB1.

To assess this prediction, we examined binding of TOG5 to microtubules stabilized by GMPCPP and GTPγS, each labelled with orthogonal fluorophores. We found that TPX2 and EB1 behaved as expected, preferring GMPCPP and GTPγS, respectively. Unexpectedly, TOG5 preferred the GTPγS microtubule lattice, though to a lesser extent than EB1, given its near 0 Pearson’s coefficient with GMPCPP and smaller magnitude coefficient with GTPγS (Fig. 5a, b). This suggests that TOG5 is sensitive to tubulin nucleotide state and lattice compaction, but that it may prefer an intermediate state between that represented by the GMPCPP and GTPγS lattices. Growing microtubule ends are not blunt but are understood to consist of tapered tubulin sheets and protofilaments with intermediate curvature between fully bent, unpolymerized tubulin and ultimately straightened, lattice-incorporated tubulin60. Sensing of tubulin curvature can thus also contribute to microtubule plus-end tracking, and although we have shown TOG5 contributes to localization of XMAP215 to straight microtubule seeds (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d and Supplementary Fig. 10a, b), its independent plus-end tracking could be explained if its ideal binding substrate is tubulin lattice with intermediate curvature. If this is the case, TOG5 should be sufficient to catalyze depolymerization of microtubule seeds by biasing exposed dimers towards a curvature less compatible with the microtubule lattice. Indeed, catalyzing depolymerization is a known property of full-length XMAP215 which is not the case for other microtubule assembly proteins such as CKAP2 or TPX221,23,57,61. We therefore assayed the stability of microtubule seeds in the presence of TOG1, TOG5, and a buffer control. Compared to the buffer condition in which GMPCPP microtubules are almost entirely stable over a 20-minute period, both TOG1 and TOG5 at 1 μM concentration catalyze depolymerization, though TOG5 does so about 3-fold faster (Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Movie 5). Thus, we conclude that while TOG5 can promote lattice localization of XMAP215, its ideal binding site is likely tubulin sheets of intermediate curvature at the microtubule plus end. We also surmise that although TOG1 binds a more fully curved tubulin conformation than TOG5, it catalyzes depolymerization more slowly due to weaker localization to the microtubule seed (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). This also suggests that TOG5 aids lattice formation by stabilizing an intermediate state during the necessary bent to straight conformational change, and our cryo-EM structure thus represents a post-catalysis state.

Binding to γ-TuRC is required for specific nucleation versus polymerization

An outstanding question in the field is what differentiates microtubule nucleation from polymerization60. By using an approach which allows us to closely approximate initial microtubule nucleation rates and directly measure polymerization rates, we report that all XMAP215 truncation and deletion constructs that lack TOG6 or the entire C-terminal domain, including TOG5 alone, display a microtubule polymerization rate that is proportional to their nucleation rate (Fig. 5e). Strikingly, this linear relationship is broken by the full-length XMAP215 protein which exhibits the most robust interaction with γ-TuRC. Instead of stimulating nucleation 3-fold faster than buffer, according to the linear trend, we measured an increase of 21.0 ± 7.7-fold. Thus, the ability to directly bind to γ-TuRC is specifically linked to promotion of microtubule nucleation.

Finally, we wondered whether the XMAP215 C-terminal region would still promote γ-TuRC mediated nucleation with impaired polymerase activity by perturbation of TOG1. We employed a TOG2-C construct, which contains TOG2 through the very C-terminus of XMAP215 (lacking TOG1 and the proceeding linker), and a TOG1-51AA construct, which contains TOG1 through TOG5 (lacking the C-terminal region) and whose TOG1 domain is mutated to weaken tubulin binding4,33. Deletion of TOG1 in TOG2-C reduced the microtubule polymerization rate roughly 2-fold relative to full-length XMAP215, and the nucleation rate about 4-fold. Mutation of TOG1 and the absence of the C-terminal region in TOG1-51AA caused an approximately 1.5-fold reduction in polymerization rate relative to XMAP215, but a roughly 14-fold decrease in nucleation rate, while the mutants exhibit normal plus tracking behavior (Supplementary Fig. 12a–g and Supplementary Movie 6). Thus, although TOG2-C polymerized microtubules more slowly and produced shorter microtubule than TOG1-51AA, it nucleated roughly 3.5-fold faster (Supplementary Fig. 12a–f and Supplementary Movie 6). This is consistent with our conclusion that the presence or absence XMAP215’s C-terminal region does not affect microtubule polymerization but is important for promoting γ-TuRC mediated nucleation. Otherwise, TOG2-C would nucleate more slowly than TOG1-51AA given its slower microtubule polymerization rate. Moreover, we observe that deletion of TOG1 in TOG2-C appears more deleterious for polymerization than the TOG1 alanine substitutions introduced in TOG1-51AA 26,28,33. Together, these results support a model in which XMAP215’s C-terminal region localizes the polymerase to γ-TuRC to enhance its promotion of nucleation from the γ-tubulin template.

Discussion

XMAP215 has been well studied as a microtubule polymerase: the unique features and conserved positions of each TOG domain in the pentameric array in vertebrate XMAP215 orthologs were proposed to orient XMAP215 on the growing microtubule to sequentially recruit soluble tubulin, aid its lattice incorporation, and stabilize the newly formed lattice25,29–31. In this work, we gain further insight into how XMAP215 facilitates microtubule nucleation and discuss its implications.

There has been an ongoing debate about how microtubule nucleation and polymerization relate to one another. Calculating initial nucleation rates from directly counted individual nucleation events and comparing them with polymerization rates measured from the same reactions allowed us to better understand this relationship. By plotting the fold change in polymerization rate versus fold change in nucleation rate, we revealed a linear trend for XMAP215 constructs with C-terminal deletions and therefore disrupted γ-TuRC binding. A linear relationship was previously reported in vitro using microtubule seeds and in cells in which tubulin sequestration slowed both nucleation and polymerization44. Thus, we extrapolate more broadly that proteins which aid in the association of GTP tubulin with the microtubule plus end will proportionally affect rates of both nucleation and polymerization. However, nucleation activity is specifically promoted when XMAP215 and γ-TuRC directly interact with one another. Thus, there exists a separation of functions between XMAP215’s N- and C-termini, in which the N-terminal TOGs engage in processes which promote microtubule assembly in general, and the C-terminus constitutes the molecular basis for the cooperativity between XMAP215 and γ-TuRC.

Consistent with XMAP215/γ-TuRC’s function as a nucleation module throughout the cell, work by Ali and colleagues demonstrated that ch-TOG is involved in γ-TuRC mediated nucleation at centrosomes and at the Golgi5. Interestingly, they report that while the ch-TOG C-terminal region is sufficient for its own localization to interphase centrosomes, the full-length protein is necessary for co-recruitment of γ-TuRC. Given the separation of function we describe in this work, it is possible that recruitment of tubulin to the γ-TuRC template by ch-TOG/XMAP215 is necessary for targeting of the nucleation module to certain MTOCs in cells. Nevertheless, ch-TOG’s interaction with γ-TuRC is transient when microtubules are allowed to polymerize5, consistent with our in vitro observation that XMAP215 will often localize to γ-TuRC before nucleation and then depart, tracking the plus-end after nucleation (Fig. 2e–g and Supplementary Fig. 2d). Likewise, we observed minus-end localization after nucleation was less frequent than before nucleation (Fig. 2f, g), though this activity may be relevant in cells for anchoring microtubules to MTOCs, such as centrosomal subdistal appendages5, and likely involves the interactions of TOG5, 6, and the broader C-terminal region as described in this work. Characterizing the dynamics of XMAP215/γ-TuRC binding will be interesting to pursue in the future.

Curiously, while genetic duplication events seem to have given rise to homologous TOG domain pairs, TOG1-2 and TOG3-4, TOG5 has remained without a partner62. While some NMR characterization of a sixth TOG domain was performed previously39,40, here we obtained crystallographic evidence for the predicted structure of the TOG6 domain that is conserved across orthologs. This domain was sufficient to bind γ-tubulin and γ-TuRC, and its deletion reduced XMAP215’s co-nucleation activity. TOG6 therefore appears to facilitate the nucleation-specific function of XMAP215’s C-terminal region, either as part of its multicomponent interaction with γ-TuRC or as an essential steric spacer. Thus, we propose that the use of pentameric TOG array be revised to hexameric TOG array for XMAP215 and its homologues, with TOG5 finally having found its partner domain.

It will be further interesting to explore to which spokes and with what stoichiometry XMAP215 binds γ-TuRC. Since TOGs 1 and 2 bind β-tubulin via their intra-HR loops in HRs A-D26,27,32, we asked whether TOG6 might bind similarly to γ-tubulin. We therefore examined the charge and sequence conservation of the surfaces that would be involved if this were the case (Supplementary Fig. 13). TOG6’s HRA-C loops produce a negatively charged surface. These would likely repel the negatively charged surface of γ-tubulin which is exposed on the outside of γ-TuRC (Supplementary Fig. 13, γ-tubulin outer view, TOG6 bottom view). Moreover, the residues of these HR loops are not well conserved, unlike in other TOG domains (Supplementary Figs. 8f and 9)24,31. AlphaFold 3 multimer predictions of TOG6 and exhaustive γ-tubulin/GCP complexes failed to provide any models of high confidence, or which seemed plausible63. Thus, we posit that the TOG6/γ-tubulin interface is divergent from canonical TOG/tubulin interactions and may have driven evolution of TOG6’s divergent solenoid architecture (Fig. 1b, f and Supplementary Fig. 1d). In γ-TuRC, The alpha-helical extensions of the GCP3 GRIP2 domain obstruct the outer surface of γ-tubulin which we suspect would preclude interaction with any binding partners, but this site remains free at GCP2, 4, 5, and 69–11. These 9 available sites are distributed on γ-TuRC in an interesting way, namely at every other spoke in the case of the more symmetric γ-TuSC portion (spokes 1-8), and/or at every spoke on the metazoan-specific portion (spokes 9-12). It may be that XMAP215 can bind all 9 of these available sites, or that specific interactions with other γ-TuRC subunits bias it towards one region of the ring over another. Because only between 4 and 7 tubulin dimers are needed to assemble on γ-TuRC to trigger nucleation10,12,15, and even a single XMAP215 molecule bound to γ-TuRC should improve tubulin recruitment, it may bind with variable stoichiometry over a range of possible binding sites and still improve nucleation efficiency. Ultimately, the precise nature of XMAP215’s interaction with γ-TuRC will be best characterized through structure determination of the complex which may require additional factors to stabilize their interaction such as the TACC3 TAC domain.

In this work, we also explore how the TOG5 domain contributes to γ-TuRC dependent microtubule nucleation. In our in vitro TIRF experiments, we found that TOG5 drives XMAP215 localization to microtubules and that the domain promotes nucleation and polymerization both independently and as part of larger TOG arrays. Interestingly, we noted that both TOG1 and TOG5 exhibited the ability to independently track microtubule plus-ends, though we expect the mechanism differs. TOG1 has a well-characterized preference for the curved conformation of tubulin and has been previously used to assay the presence of curved dimers at the ends of stabilized microtubules26,27,64. Its plus tracking is therefore likely due to its recognition of curved tubulin subunits in solution which then bind the growing microtubule end, straighten upon polymerization, and consequently release TOG1. A similar mechanism for plus tracking could apply to TOG5, however this is contradicted by the observations that mutation of TOG5 does not affect the amount of soluble tubulin bound by XMAP215, and the solenoid structure of TOG5 causes a steric clash when it is aligned to TOG structures bound to bent tubulin29,33. Importantly and in contrast to TOG1, the simultaneous increases in polymerization and nucleation rates caused by TOG5 indicate that its binding promotes microtubule assembly (Supplementary Fig. 6c–e). These effects and observations could be explained if TOG5 recognizes unique features belonging to the assembling tubulin lattice, either at plus ends or on γ-TuRC, where its binding energy helps to stabilize the lattice and promote its formation.

When further investigating what plus-end specific features TOG5 might be recognizing, we determined that TOG5 is sensitive to the lattice’s nucleotide state, but also that lattice curvature may be the dominant feature involved. Considering the behavior of other MAPS, TPX2 has been reported to localize strongly to curved microtubules and track plus-ends independently of nucleotide, doing so in the presence of both GTP and GTPγS23. Additionally, the plus-end tracking activity of doublecortin (DCX) has also been attributed to its recognition of longitudinal curvature65. Thus, when we observed that TOG5 could both strongly bind to stabilized seeds and trigger their depolymerization (Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Fig. 10a, b), we reasoned that these behaviors and its plus tracking could all be explained by a preference for tubulin lattices of intermediate curvature. Indeed, this is consistent with the predictions of others that TOG5, with its unique geometry, helps transition tubulin from bent to straight conformations during lattice incorporation29,46.

Through our cryo-EM structure, we establish the structural basis for how TOG5’s unique architecture enables it to bind the microtubule lattice. We observe that it substantially interacts with ɑ-tubulin within the straight tubulin lattice via its C-terminal HRs E-F. We also confirm that TOG5’s characteristic N-terminal HR0 contributes a minor interaction with the β-tubulin of a laterally associated dimer, as previously predicted29. Stabilization of lateral interactions may allow TOG5 to help zipper up neighboring protofilaments and decrease tubulin off-rates. Taken together, we propose that TOG5 helps to direct XMAP215 to the nascent or growing microtubule plus-end and agree with previous models that TOG5 binding promotes assembly of the microtubule lattice by facilitating the conformational transition from bent to straight during lattice incorporation.

Nevertheless, in addition to its lattice stabilizing role, TOG5 may retain some affinity for soluble tubulin and aid in tubulin recruitment just as TOG1 displays some affinity for microtubule lattices (Supplementary Fig. 10A–B). TOG5’s lattice binding may also explain its recently characterized requirement for XMAP215’s cross linking of the actin and microtubule cytoskeletons66–68, and it will further be interesting to determine through the solving of additional microtubule-bound TOG structures whether CLASPs, another family of TOG proteins that stabilize microtubules, interact with microtubules in analogous or contrasting ways to XMAP21524,69,70. The role of the TOG4 domain also merits further investigation as it has been hypothesized to bind multiple tubulin dimers29, and we found it had a subtle effect in promoting nucleation from γ-TuRC (Supplementary Fig. 6c), raising the possibility that it aids in plus-end tracking when tethered adjacent to TOG5.

While TOG5 and TOG6 are conserved across metazoan orthologs of XMAP215, they are absent from yeast Stu2/Alp14 (Fig. 1c). How then is microtubule nucleation achieved in these species? While the microtubule polymerase is required for nucleation, an additional factor, namely the TACC3 ortholog Spc72/Alp7, promotes Stu2/Alp14’s assembly with γ-TuSC through its CM1 domain, and thus promotes microtubule nucleation in the absence of a γ-TuRC binding C-terminus36,37. Moreover, acetylation of Stu2 TOG2 was shown to allow it to bind γ-tubulin and may further promote its co-nucleation activity with yeast γ-TuSC complexes71. In these species, TOG5 is perhaps rendered dispensable by the avidity effects of dimerization throughStu2/Alp14’s coiled-coil domain, bringing together two disordered microtubule binding C-terminal linker regions (Fig. 1c)72.

Throughout this work, our experiments were designed within the framework that XMAP215 functions as an array of tethered TOG domains27. This model is supported by myriad studies in which at least some level of microtubule polymerase activity is retained despite swapped TOG domain positions, scrambled linker sequences, and domain truncations4,17,27,29,30,33–35,47. This model also requires that adjacent TOG domains exhibit overlapping or redundant functions. Consistent with this model, we observe retained, though reduced, polymerase activity of our truncation constructs, including TOG2-C and TOG1-4, which indicate that TOG1 is not solely responsible for soluble tubulin binding and that the linker4-TOG5 region is not solely responsible for microtubule localization. Nevertheless, the tethered TOG array model is limited in that it is agnostic to the possibility of higher order assemblies formed between multiple XMAP215 TOG domains which could coordinate tubulin recruitment and microtubule binding. For instance, it is conceivable that TOG1, TOG2, TOG3, and TOG4, which are each capable of binding soluble tubulin albeit with differing affinities, could assemble into an arrangement analogous to the square-shaped structure observed for the four TOG domains present in the yeast Alp14 dimer32. Future work examining the conservation of this phenomenon in metazoan XMAP215 orthologs would be instrumental in further establishing our understanding of how XMAP215 facilitates microtubule nucleation and polymerization.

Taken together, we propose the following model for microtubule nucleation. XMAP215 and γ-TuRC interact directly via the γ-tubulin specific TOG6 domain and the C-terminal region which likely interacts with other γ-TuRC subunits. This direct binding gives rise to the synergy between the two nucleators. The N-terminal TOG5 domain is positioned to interact with the first tubulin dimers assembling onto the γ-tubulin ring template, facilitates adoption of a straight lattice conformation, and stabilizes their lattice interactions. Therefore, both γ-TuRC and TOG5 together enable lateral tubulin interactions which promotes γ-TuRC closure and a switch from nucleation to polymerization. The N-terminal TOG domains 1-4 recruit soluble tubulin to the nascent microtubule lattice. Thus, the unique features and conserved positions of each TOG domain in the hexameric array in vertebrate XMAP215 orthologs orient XMAP215 to recruit soluble tubulin, aid its lattice incorporation, and stabilize the lattice to promote both microtubule nucleation and polymerization (Fig. 5f).

Protein construct cloning, expression, and purification

XMAP215 truncation constructs

TOG1-5 (aa1-1,460), TOG1-51AA (aa1-1,460: W21A, K102A), TOG1-4 (aa1-1,090), TOG2-C (aa264-2,065), and TOG5-C (aa1,191-2,065) were generated for a previously published work by subcloning into an ampicillin resistant pST50 vector with C-terminal GFP, 7xHis-StrepII tags4,73. For this work, TOG1(aa1-240), TOG2 (aa264-515), TOG3 (aa556-808), TOG4 (aa846-1,090), TOG5 (aa1,191-1,460), TOG1-linker4 (aa1-1,192), and TOG2-5 (aa264-1,460) were subcloned into the same pST50 with C-terminal GFP, 7xHis-StrepII tags using Gibson Assembly (NEB). TOG5RFEE (R1222E F1250E), TOG5KMEE (K1444E M1448E), and TOG5RREE (R1452E R1455E) were made with the Q5 site directed mutagenesis kit (NEB) and the previously mentioned wildtype TOG5 construct as template. XMAP215 C-term (aa1,461-2,065) was generated for a previously published work by subcloning into a similar pST50 vector but with N-terminal StrepII-6xHis and GFP tags4 and was used to generate CΔTOG6 (aa1,461-1,559 + 1,840-2,065) CΔTOG6ext (aa1,461-1,559 + 1,932-2,065) with the Q5 site directed mutagenesis kit (NEB). TOG6 (1,560-1,839) and TOG6ext (1,560-1,932) were subcloned into a pET-28a vector with N-terminal 6xHis, GFP, HRV3C, and StrepII tags by Gibson Assembly (NEB). For crystallization, TOG6 (1,560-1,839) was subcloned into the ampicillin resistant pST50 vector with an N-terminal 7xHis tag by Gibson Assembly (NEB). Oligonucleotide primers were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) and are provided in a Supplementary Data file.

All constructs were transformed into Rosetta 2 (DE3) pLysS E. coli cells for expression. Cells were grown under antibiotic selection with chloramphenicol and either ampicillin or kanamycin in 1–2 L Luria Broth cultures at 37 °C to an optical density (OD) at 595 nm of 0.6–0.8. Cultures were then chilled on ice at 4 °C before induction with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), whereupon they were incubated for 16–18 h at 16 °C. Cultures were then pelleted, flash frozen, and stored at −80 °C until they were thawed for purification. Unless otherwise specified, all protein handling during and after purification was performed on ice or at 4 °C.

For purification of N-terminal constructs (anything containing some or all of TOG1-TOG5), cell pellets were thawed, resuspended in Strep Binding Buffer (280 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 100 mM Tris, 6 mM beta-mercaptoethanol (BME), 0.2 mM phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), pH 7.7) supplemented with 1 µg of DNase from bovine pancreas and 1 dissolved tablet of cOmplete EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche). Pellets were then homogenized with a Biospec Tissue Tearor (Dremel), and lysed with an Emulsiflex C3 (Avestin) at 10,000–15,000 psi. Lysates were clarified by ultracentrifugation for 35 min at 4 °C at 70,000 × g in a 45Ti rotor (Beckman Coulter). Constructs were then subjected to affinity purification using StrepTactin SuperFlow resin (IBA) using Strep Binding Buffer (280 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 100 mM Tris, 6 mM BME, 0.2 mM PMSF, pH 7.7) for lysis, binding, and washing. Elution was performed in Strep Binding Buffer supplemented with 3.3 mM desthiobiotin. TOG1, TOG2, TOG3, TOG4, and TOG5 were then dialyzed overnight in gel filtration buffer (10 mM HEPES, 100 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM ethylene glycol-bis(β-aminoethyl ether)-N,N,N’,N’-tetraacetic acid (EGTA), 10% sucrose, 6 mM BME, 0.2 mM PMSF, pH 7.5). TOG1-5, TOG1-51AA, TOG1-linker4, and TOG2-C were further subjected to gel filtration on a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva), TOG2-5, TOG1-4, and TOG5-C on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) in gel filtration buffer, and TOG5RFEE, TOG5KMEE, and TOG5RREE on a Superdex 75 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) in gel filtration buffer. Peak elution fractions from affinity and size exclusion chromatography were analyzed for purity by SDS-PAGE and staining with Coomassie blue. The desired fractions’ concentrations were estimated using a Bradford assay against a bovine serum albumin (BSA) standard before aliquoting and flash freezing in liquid nitrogen before storage at −80 °C.

For purification of C-terminal constructs (C-term, CΔTOG6, CΔTOG6ext, TOG6, TOG6ext, and the TOG6 crystallization construct), pellets were similarly resuspended, homogenized, and lysed, but instead using His Binding Buffer (300 mM NaCl, 50 mM NaPO4H, 10 mM Imidazole, 6 mM BME, 0.2 mM PMSF, pH 7.9) supplemented with 1 µg of DNAse from bovine pancreas and 1 dissolved tablet of cOmplete EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche). Lysates were incubated with Ni-NTA agarose (Qiagen), washed with His Wash Buffer (His Binding Buffer with 40 mM imidazole), and eluted with His Elution Buffer (His Binding Buffer with 250 mM imidazole). All constructs were subject to gel filtration on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column in gel filtration buffer, except the crystallization construct which was run on a Superdex 75 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) in HBS (10 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), pH 7.5). These were similarly evaluated by SDS-PAGE, Coomassie staining, and Bradford assay concentration quantitation before aliquoting, snap freezing, and storage at −80 °C.

XMAP215 full length, TOG6, and TOG6ext deletion constructs

XMAP215ΔTOG6 (aa1-1,559 + 1,840-2,065) and XMAP215ΔTOG6ext (aa1-1,559 + 1,932-2,065) were created as derivatives of a pFastBac1 vector containing full-length XMAP215 (1-2,065) with C-terminal TEV, GFP, 7xHis, and StrepII (12) using the Q5 Site Directed Mutagenesis Kit (NEB). Peak fractions were aliquoted and snap frozen before storage at −80 °C.

γ-Tubulin and γ-TuRC

Human γ-tubulin with C-terminal TEV, StrepII, 7xHis tags had been produced in the Petry Lab for a previous publication and was expressed and purified using the Bac-to-Bac Sf9 expression system, nickel resin, and gel filtration4. Endogenous X. laevis γ-TuRC was also purified according to a published method13. Briefly, a human γ-TuNA (γ-TuRC nucleation activating) peptide from CDK5RAP2 with N-terminal StrepII, 6xHis, TEV, HaloTag, and HRV3C tags was expressed from a pST50 vector in Rosetta2 (DE3) pLysS E. coli in 2 L of Terrific Broth and purified using StrepTactin Superflow resin. X-ray crystallography

Crystallization

A construct of X. laevis XMAP215 TOG6 (aa1563-1839) was crystallized by hanging-drop vapor diffusion at 20 °C after mixing 1 μL of protein (7.5 mg/mL) with 1 μL of well buffer (75 mM Tris, 25 mM MES, 600 mM NaCl, 4% PEG 8000 (w/v), 30% Ethylene Glycol (v/v), pH 6.75). Crystals exhibited needle morphology with a hexagonal cross section and were roughly 0.3–0.4 × 0.1 × 0.1 mm and grew within a week. Crystals were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen without need for additional cryoprotectant. X-ray diffraction data were collected at the U.S. National Synchrotron Light Source II AMX Beamline.

Molecular replacement and refinement

Data were processed using XDS. The AlphaFold (version2) prediction for the crystallized construct was then used for molecular replacement in PHASER. Successful molecular replacement required in silico deletion of the first alpha-helix of the first HR and therefore did not appear in the resulting map, but subsequent attempts to purify a construct without this helix revealed greatly decreased its solubility and resulted in the majority of protein precipitating during purification. Further rigid body refinement was performed using LORESTR. The composite-omit map was in Fig. 1B was generated in Phenix using default settings.

Protein binding assays

GFP pull-downs

Pulldowns were performed using GFP-Trap Magnetic Agarose Resin (Chromotek) for each reaction. Resin was first equilibrated in bulk with three 1 mL washes of gel filtration buffer (10 mM HEPES, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 100 mM KCl, 6 mM BME, 10% sucrose (w/v), pH 7.5), then aliquoted such that 5 μL of packed resin could be used for each pull down. 100 μL of each GFP-tagged bait construct in gel filtration buffer was then coupled to the equilibrated resin rotating for 1 h. To ensure saturation, sufficient amounts of each bait construct were used to have 40 μg equivalents of GFP present, twice the capacity for 5 μL of resin. The resin was then washed once with 200 μL of gel filtration buffer, then resuspended in 200 μL blocking buffer (10 mM HEPES, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 100 mM KCl, 6 mM BME, 2% sucrose (w/v), 0.05% Tween (v/v), 1% bovine serum albumin (w/v), pH 7.5) and incubated rotating for 3 h. The blocking buffer was subsequently removed and replaced with 100 μL of 1 μM Human γ-tubulin in blocking buffer or 90 μL blocking buffer plus 10 μL peak Xenopus γ-TuRC fraction and allowed to bind rotating for 1 h. Blocking buffer was supplemented with 2 mM GTP for these binding reactions. The resin was then washed 3 times with 200 μL of wash buffer (10 mM HEPES, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, 100 mM KCl, 6 mM BME, 2% sucrose (w/v), 0.05% Tween (v/v), pH 7.5) and finally resuspended in 100 μL of 1xSDS-PAGE sample buffer (6 mM DTT) and boiled for 10 min.

Pull down samples were then run on 4–12% Bis-Tris denaturing gels (GenScript), then analyzed by Coomassie staining to evaluate coupling of bait proteins to the resin and Western blotting to assay coprecipitation of prey protein. For Western blotting, membranes were probed using a mouse GCP5 Antibody (E-1) (sc-365837, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) diluted 250-fold and/or a mouse γ-tubulin antibody GTU-88 (T6557, Sigma) diluted 1000-fold and a secondary sheep anti-mouse antibody (#NA931, Amersham) at a 5000-fold dilution. For quantification, background was removed from images of gels and blots through subtraction of corresponding Gaussian-blurred images, then integrated band intensity was measured using the Plot Lanes function in FIJI ((FIJI Is Just) ImageJ 2.16.0/1.54p; Java 1.8.0_202 [64 bit])73. Intensity for each band was normalized to the peak for each corresponding gel or blot, and the normalized γ-tubulin intensity divided by the normalized bait intensity was reported.

Size exclusion chromatography

For size exclusion assays we used an AKTA PURE SYSTEM with Superdex 75 10/300 GL (Cytiva) column, which was equilibrated with BRB80 (80 mM PIPES, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, pH 6.8) supplemented with 0.05% Tween-20 (v/v), and the same was used to dialyze TOG-GFP constructs. TOG-GFP constructs were dialyzed for 1 h using a 3500 MW Slide-A-Lyzer Mini Dialysis Cassette (Thermo Scientific). Bovine brain tubulin (PurSolutions) was diluted 1 to 10 in cold BRB80, then both the tubulin and dialyzed TOG-GFP were separately precleared by ultracentrifugation at 247,000 × g in a TLA100 rotor (Beckman Coulter) for 10 minutes at 2 °C. The concentrations of the clarified proteins was estimated by Bradford (BioRad) assay against a bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma) standard curve. 100 μL samples were mixed containing 5 μM TOG-GFP construct, 5 μM tubulin, or both in BRB80 plus 0.05% Tween-20 and 1 mM GTP and incubated on ice for 10 min before loading onto the column. Absorbance at 280 nm was used to track the protein peak. Theoretical traces were determined by summing the absorbance from the independent TOG-GFP and tubulin runs. For compiled depiction in Supplementary Fig. 3e, the theoretical peak represents the average of all calculated theoretical peaks, and the observed traces are adjusted such that their maxima are accurately displaced from the maximum of the average theoretical peak.

In vitro TIRF assays

γ-TuRC mediated microtubule nucleation

These assays were performed as established in previous publications10,12 and modified by Rale and coworkers (2023). High Precision 22 × 22 mm No 1.5H coverslips (Deckglaser) coverslips were etched by sonication in 3 M NaOH, rinsed in MiliQ water, then further sonicated in Piranha solution made from 3 parts 30% H2O2 and 5 parts sulfuric acid. After rinsing again with MiliQ water and drying, the coverslips were treated with 3-glycidyloxypropyl trimethoxysilane (GOPTS, Sigma) at 75 °C for 30 min. Unreacted GOPTS was removed with two washes in acetone then coverslips were dried under nitrogen gas. 10% biotin-PEG (w/w) (9 parts HO-PEG-NH2, 1 part biotin-CONH-PEG-NH2, Rapp Polymere) powder was then sandwiched between two coverslips and melted at 75 °C. Air bubbles were expressed with gentle pressure, and coverslips were incubated at 75 °C overnight. Sandwiches were then separated and washed in MiliQ water before spin drying and storage at 4 °C. Coverslips were used within three months of preparation. Imaging chambers were made fresh for each day of experiments with two pieces of parallel double-sided tape on a slide passivated with an application of 2 mg/mL PLL-g-PEG (SuSOS AG) in MiliQ water which was allowed to dry in air. Channels were rinsed with MiliQ water and dried under nitrogen gas. Functionalized coverslips were cut into quarters using a diamond pen, then attached to the tape face-down to close the chamber.

Before imaging, chambers were blocked with 5% v/v Pluronic F-127 and casein buffer (BRB80 with 30 mM KCl, 1 mM GTP, 0.075% w/v methylcellulose 4000 cp, 1% w/v D-glucose, 0.02% w/v Brij-35, 5 mM BME, 0.05 mg/mL κ-casein). NeutrAvidin (ThermoFisher) at 0.05 mg/mL in casein buffer was then flowed in and used to attach biotinylated γ-TuRC diluted 1:100 in BRB80 to the coverslips.

Reaction mixes were prepared with the desired αβ-tubulin concentrations (7 μM or 10 μM) containing 5% Alexa 568-tubulin plus 1 mg/mL BSA (Sigma) in assay buffer (BRB80 with 30 mM KCl, 1 mM GTP, 0.075% w/v methylcellulose 4000 cp, 1% w/v D-glucose, 0.02% w/v Brij-35, 5 mM BME). XMAP215-GFP constructs or control gel filtration buffer (10 mM HEPES, 100 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM EGTA, 10% sucrose, 6 mM BME, pH 7.5) were added to the desired concentration before clarification by ultracentrifugation at 247,000 × g for 10 min at 2 °C in a TLA100 (Beckman Coulter). Glucose oxidase (GmbH) was then added to the clarified mix at 0.68 mg/mL and catalase (Sigma) at 0.16 mg/mL before flowing into the imaging chamber. Imaging was performed on a Nikon Ti-E inverted stand (RRID:SCR_021242) with an Apo TIRF 100x oil objective (NA = 1.49) and an Andor iXon DU-897 EM-CCD camera with EM gain set to 300, with the objective heated to 33.5 °C using a heated collar (model 150819–13, Bioptechs). Imaging began after precisely 1 min. A single field of view was imaged every 3 s for 5 min.

Microtubule mass was quantitated after first using FIJI’s ((FIJI Is Just) ImageJ 2.16.0/1.54p; Java 1.8.0_202 [64 bit])74 Subtract Background function with a rolling ball radius of 50 pixels on all frames, measuring the mean intensity of the tubulin fluorophore for each frame, then subtracting the value of the first frame from all subsequent frames. To measure microtubule nucleation and dynamics, we cropped each movie to the middle 256 × 256 pixels and 150 s. We used the Stackreg or HyperStackReg plugins to correct translational drift75,76, then manually selected each microtubule using the tubulin channel only for the Reslice operation. The generated kymographs were analyzed to determine the frame in which nucleation occurred, microtubule growth rate, and maximum microtubule length. Spontaneous microtubule nucleation

Imaging chambers for spontaneous microtubule nucleation assays were prepared identically to those described in the previous section, but without NeutrAvidin or γ-TuRC steps. Reaction mixes were prepared using 5% Alexa 568 labeled 15 μM tubulin plus XMAP215-GFP construct or control gel filtration buffer, clarified, and imaged using the same microscopy setup as above. We imaged 9 fields of view in a 3 × 3 array every 30 s for five minutes using the NIS-Elements AR program (Nikon, version 5.02.01-Build 1270), and manually counted the number of microtubules in all fields of view in every other frame for plotting.

Microtubule localization, lattice preference, and depolymerization

To assay binding of constructs to the microtubule lattice, we made Guanosine-5’-[(α,β)-methyleno]triphosphate (GMPCPP, Jenna Bioscience) stabilized microtubule seeds using a standard protocol. A polymerization mix of 20 μM bovine brain tubulin (PurSolutions) containing 10% biotin- and 10% Alexa 568-labeled tubulin was prepared in BRB80 (80 mM PIPES, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, pH 6.8) with 1 mM GMPCPP. This mixture was incubated on ice for 5 min before ultracentrifugation at 247,000 × g in a TLA100 rotor for 10 min at 2 °C and subsequent incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. The resulting microtubule seeds were pelleted by centrifugation at 17,000 × g for 15 min at room temperature, then resuspended in one reaction volume of warm BRB80 using a wide orifice pipette tip.

For the lattice preference assay, we generated dual-labelled seeds by polymerizing GTPγS microtubules off GMPCPP microtubules generated as above, but using Atto 647 N (ATTO-TEC Gmbh) labeled tubulin. In the final resuspension step, the GMPCPP seeds were resuspended in 10% Alexa568-labeled 50 μM tubulin in modified BRB80 buffer containing 0.2 mM MgCl2 and 1 mM guanosine 5’-O-[γ-thio]triphosphate (GTPγS) and similarly polymerized for 1 h at 37 °C. These seeds were pelleted again and resuspended in 0.2 mM MgCl2 BRB80 supplemented with 1 mM DTT. EB1-GFP and GFP-TPX2 constructs were expressed and purified as previously described77,78.

Imaging chambers were prepared as for the γ-TuRC mediated nucleation assays, though the PLL-g-PEG step was skipped and seeds diluted 1:500–1:1000 in warm BRB80 were attached to coverslips in place of γ-TuRC. For the localization experiments in Supplementary Fig. 4c, d, reaction mixes were prepared with XMAP215-GFP constructs at 200 nM in localization assay buffer (BRB80 with 75 mM KCl, 1% w/v D-glucose, 5 mM BME, 0.1 mg/mL BSA, 0.34 mg/mL glucose oxidase, 0.08 mg/mL catalase), flowed into imaging chambers, and imaged after a 10-minute incubation at room temperature. For the localization experiments in Supplementary Fig. 10a, b and lattice preference experiments in Fig. 5a, b, imaging channels were blocked with a stronger casein buffer (1 mg/mL κ-casein) and reaction mixes were prepared with TOG-GFP constructs at 100 nM, EB1 at 80 nM, or TPX2 at 3.5 nM in a supplemented CSF-XB buffer (with 5% w/v sucrose, 1% w/v D-glucose, 5 mM BME, 0.34 mg/mL glucose oxidase, and 0.08 mg/mL catalase). Imaging for Supplementary Fig. 4c, d was performed on a Nikon Ti-E inverted stand (RRID:SCR_021242) with an Apo TIRF 100 x oil objective (NA = 1.49) and a Hamamatsu ORCA-Fusion BT Digital CMOS camera. Other experiments used the same imaging setup as the live nucleation assays. Images were captured in a 3 × 3 array using the NIS-Elements AR program (Nikon, version 5.02.01-Build 1270). Depolymerization assays in Fig. 5c, d were performed as for the binding experiments in Supplementary Fig. 10a, b, but we instead imaged a single field of view every 30 s for 20 min.

For processing, we used custom FIJI ((FIJI Is Just) ImageJ 2.16.0/1.54p; Java 1.8.0_202 [64 bit]) macros74. To segment microtubules for localization analysis, we thresholded the tubulin channel using the Otsu method, performed the open operation followed by the dilate operation to remove puncta and encompass all the microtubule signal. We accepted regions of interest (ROI) with a 0.75 μm2 minimum particle area. The mean fluorescence intensity in both the tubulin and XMAP215 channels for each ROI was recorded. To account for background fluorescence, we created an inverse mask by again automatically thresholding, dilating once, and inverting the selection to exclude all microtubules. We measured the mean intensity in both channels over the entire remaining area and subtracted the resulting value for each channel from the corresponding values for each segmented microtubule. The difference was used to calculate the MAP/MT values reported. Steps were taken to manually exclude protein aggregates from intensity measurements. For lattice preference assays, we segmented by thresholding the GMPCPP and GTPγS channels then combined the resulting binary masks using the OR image calculator operation and followed by a dilate, dilate, erode, erode sequence to promote recognition of continuous microtubules of multiple lattice types. We then accepted ROIs with a minimum area of 0.25 μm2 and circularity between 0 and 0.5 and combined them into a single ROI. Finally, we analyzed colocalization of MAP and tubulin signal using the Coloc 2 built-in plugin and reported the Pearson’s correlation coefficient for each reaction.

Depolymerization rates were also measured using a custom macro in which kymographs were generated using the Reslice operation and rates determined from the slope created by shrinking microtubule signal.

Cryo-electron microscopy

Sample preparation

GMPCPP stabilized microtubule seeds were generated using a slightly modified protocol in which we skipped the microtubule pelleting step. A polymerization mixture containing 20 μM bovine brain tubulin (PurSolutions) in BRB80 (80 mM PIPES, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA, pH 6.8) plus 1 mM GMPCPP was incubated on ice for 5 min before preclearing by ultracentrifugation at 247,000 × g for 10 min at 2 °C. The supernatant was then incubated at 37 °C for 1 hour, then finally diluted 1:2 in warm BRB80. Meanwhile, purified TOG5-GFP construct was desalted into EM Buffer (BRB80 plus 5 mM BME and 0.05% Tween-20 (v/v)) using a 0.5 mL 7 K MWCO Zeba Spin Desalting Column (Thermo Scientific). After desalting, we estimated the TOG5 concentration to be 66 μM by Bradford Assay. Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 400-square mesh Cu grids (Quantifoil) were glow-discharged for 1 min at 10 mA using a PELCO easiGLow glow discharging system (Ted Pella) and loaded onto a Leica EM GP2 plunge freezer (Leica Microsystems) with the chamber set to 20 °C and 85% humidity. 3 μL of diluted microtubule seeds were applied to the grid, manually blotted away with Whatman No. 1 blot paper, then washed twice with 3 μL of TOG5 in EM buffer. A 30 s incubation took place after each application of microtubules or TOG5 to the grid. The grid was then front blotted for 3 s using the Leica EM GP2 and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane and transferred to a grid box under liquid nitrogen where it was stored until data collection.

Cryo-EM data collection

Data were collected using a Titan Krios G3 cryo Transmission Electron Microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 300 kV with a K2 Summit direct electron detector camera equipped with a GIF Bio-Quantum energy filter (Gatan). Collection of 32-frame movies was automated using EPU (Thermofisher, version 2.12.1.2782REL) in EFTEM mode with a 4.1 s exposure time, total dose of 30 e-/Å2, a pixel size of 1.114 Å (later binned to 1.405 Å during processing), and a defocus range of −2 to −3.5 μm. We collected 2486 movies total.

Cryo-EM data processing

The microtubule data processing (Supplementary Fig. 7) was done using a similar protocol as previously described49,50,55. Briefly, CTF of micrographs was estimated using CTFfind version 4 and microtubule segments were automatically picked using ‘Filament Tracer’ in CryoSPARC (version 4.6.0)79. The microtubule particles with 8-nm step size were subject to 2D classification and supervised 3D classification (13-protofilaments vs 14-protofilaments) in CryoSPARC. Both 13- and 14-protofilament microtubules exhibited clear TOG5 densities. The 14-protofilament microtubule particles, comprising 69.4% of the total, were used for subsequent data processing. We used a previously established protocol to determine the seam location for each MT particle involving Relion (version 3.1.0) and Frealign (version 9)49,50, and then did symmetry expansion using the 3-start symmetry (rise 9.0 Å, twist −25.8°) in CryoSPARC, which effectively rotates/translates all the pseudo-helical symmetry related tubulin subunits to the same location for further local 3D classification and local refinement. We performed unsupervised 3D classification (6 classes) on tubulin-signal subtracted particles in CryoSPARC using a mask encompassing a single TOG5 molecule. One class (334,570 particles) exhibiting well-defined TOG5 density was selected for final 3D reconstruction. Subsequent local refinement in CryoSPARC, using a soft-edged mask covering two tubulin dimers and one TOG5 domain, achieved a local resolution of 2.9 Å.

TOG5/GMPCPP model building

We started with the undecorated GMPCPP microtubule model PDB 6DPU and used UCSF ChimeraX (version 1.8)80 and COOT (version 0.9.8.5)81 to independently rigid body fit two ɑ- and two β-tubulin chains, as well as a model of TOG5 from an AphaFold2 multimer prediction of our TOG5 construct in complex with bovine tubulin into the corresponding density of our unsharpened cryo-EM map. To prevent side chains from wandering into neighboring tubulin density during refinement, we also rigid body fit six tubulin monomers and their associated ligands to form a perimeter around the two main dimers. We mutated the main tubulins from the porcine sequence to the bovine and performed minor local real space refinement in COOT before performing a global real_space_refine at a nominal resolution of 2.9 Å in PHENIX (version 1.21-5207). Perimeter tubulin monomers were then removed from the model and validation_cryoem was used to calculate refinement statistics for the two-tubulin one TOG5 final model (Supplementary Table 2). Molecule and map depictions presented in this work were generated using UCSF ChimeraX unless otherwise stated80.
