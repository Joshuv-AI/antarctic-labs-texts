Divergent amino acid profiles in sarcopenic obesity versus non-obese sarcopenia: A prospective cohort study in community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Ting Zhang, Yafei Wu, Shuyi Li, Jason Leung, Timothy Kwok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe roles of branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) and aromatic amino acids (AAAs) in sarcopenia remain conflicting, largely due to the overlooked impact of obesity. Whether these AAs differentially associate with sarcopenia without obesity (sarcopenia-only) versus sarcopenic obesity (SO) is unknown. This study evaluated their cross-sectional and prospective associations and explored dietary determinants.MethodsThis prospective study included 2994 community-dwelling Chinese older adults. Serum BCAAs and AAAs were quantified at baseline. Multivariable multinomial logistic regression examined cross-sectional (baseline and 14-year) and exploratory prospective (4-year and 14-year) associations with incident sarcopenia-only and SO. Dietary intake was assessed using a validated food frequency questionnaire.ResultsCross-sectionally, each 1‑SD increment in BCAAs and AAAs was associated with lower sarcopenia-only risk (all ORs < 1, P < 0.05), whereas higher valine (OR = 1.195, P = 0.032) and isoleucine (OR = 1.221, P = 0.016) were associated with increased SO risk. The 14‑year cross‑sectional analysis confirmed the positive association between BCAAs and SO (ORs: 1.604–1.668, all P < 0.01). Prospectively over 4 years, higher baseline BCAAs and tyrosine were associated with lower sarcopenia-only risk (all ORs < 1, P < 0.05). Conversely, higher baseline isoleucine and phenylalanine emerged as short-term risk factors for incident SO (ORs: 1.233–1.331, P < 0.05). Exploratory 14‑year analyses showed directionally consistent associations, with valine inversely associated with sarcopenia‑only (OR = 0.628, P = 0.024) and BCAAs positively associated with SO (ORs: 1.555–1.693, P < 0.01). Dietary patterns differed, with sarcopenia-only showing lower fiber/vegetable intake but higher red/processed meat consumption (all P < 0.05), whereas SO showed elevated hs‑CRP (P < 0.05) and tended to have a higher dietary inflammatory index.ConclusionsIn community‑dwelling older adults, serum BCAAs and AAAs were inversely associated with sarcopenia-only but positively associated with SO. The consistency of these associations across time points supports their utility as potential concurrent metabolic markers for phenotype differentiation. These distinct profiles, alongside dietary correlates, caution against uniform AA supplementation and suggest phenotype-specific nutritional strategies in older adults.

Introduction

Aging increases the risk of sarcopenia, a geriatric syndrome characterized by progressive loss of muscle strength, mass, and function, leading to impaired quality of life and increased mortality [1]. With rising obesity among older adults [2], sarcopenic obesity (SO), the coexistence of sarcopenia and high adiposity, has emerged as a distinct condition with synergistic adverse health impacts [[3], [4], [5]]. First described in 1996 [6], SO was formally defined in 2022 [7,8] and affects over 10% of older adults worldwide [2], increasing risks of metabolic dysfunction, falls and mortality. Although both sarcopenia and SO involve dysregulated muscle metabolism, their pathophysiological pathways may differ, making accurate differentiation critical for targeted clinical management.

Amino acids (AAs), particularly branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs: valine, leucine, isoleucine) and aromatic amino acids (AAAs: tryptophan, phenylalanine, tyrosine), are critical for protein synthesis. However, BCAA efficacy in promoting muscle protein synthesis is often reduced in insulin resistance (IR), a hallmark of SO. Age-related muscle loss and fat infiltration contribute to IR [9]. Intramuscular fat deposition, a risk factor for impaired glucose metabolism and reduced muscle mass in type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [10,11], correlates with increased BCAA levels [12]. BCAA accumulation impairs glucose oxidation [13] and exacerbates IR via mTOR activation [14], while IR disrupts BCAA catabolism, perpetuating elevated BCAA levels [13,15]. Animal protein may also activate mTORC1, promoting oxidative stress, inflammation and IR [16]. Elevated AAAs have also been associated with IR [17].

Thus, while BCAAs/AAA supplementation may benefit sarcopenia alone, it could paradoxically exacerbate IR and chronic inflammation in SO. The gut microbiota may mediate the link between diet, AA metabolism and musculoskeletal health [[17], [18], [19]]. Diets high in animal protein but low in fiber may promote proteolytic bacteria that compete for dietary AAs, reducing their availability for muscle synthesis, whereas fiber-rich diets may support saccharolytic bacteria, potentially enhancing host AA availability [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. The protein-fiber balance may thus influence host AA pools and muscle protein anabolism.

Therefore, using a large community-based cohort of older adults with long-term follow-ups, this study aimed to: a) characterize serum BCAAs and AAAs across normal, sarcopenia-only, obesity-only and SO groups; b) evaluate cross-sectional associations between these AAs and the risk of these conditions, with prospective associations examined as exploratory analyses; and c) explore potential dietary factors underlying observed associations. We hypothesize that BCAAs and AAAs would exhibit distinct patterns that may help elucidate the metabolic divergence between sarcopenia and SO.

Methods

Study population

This study used data from the prospective Mr. OS and Ms. OS (Hong Kong) cohort. The study design and methodology have been previously detailed [22]. A total of 4000 community-dwelling Chinese older adults (≥65 years) were recruited between August 2001 and March 2003. The cohort included 2000 men and 2000 women. A stratified sampling strategy was used to enroll approximately 33% of participants from each age group: 65–69, 70–74, and ≥75 years. After excluding participants with missing muscle data (n = 1), serum BCAA and AAA data (n = 1002), or dietary data (n = 3), 2994 individuals were finally included in the cross-sectional analyses of baseline sarcopenia-only and SO. Of these, 878 participants were included in the 14-year cross-sectional analysis. For the prospective analyses, after further excluding participants with baseline diseases and those lost to follow-up, 2451 participants were included in the 4-year (short-term) prospective analysis, and 772 in the 14-year (long-term) exploratory analysis for incident sarcopenia-only and SO. Notably, sample sizes for the 14‑year analyses varied due to differing inclusion criteria: the cross‑sectional analyses included participants with available AA and phenotyping data at that time point (n = 878); the prospective analyses further excluded those with baseline sarcopenia or SO and required baseline AA data (n = 772); and the AA stability analysis (as described in Section 2.4) required both baseline and follow‑up AA measurements, irrespective of phenotyping data (n = 860). This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of The Chinese University of Hong Kong. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Measurements of sarcopenia

Muscle parameters, including muscle mass, handgrip strength, gait speed and five‑time chair stand test, were assessed at baseline and at the 4‑ and 14‑year follow‑ups. Body composition was evaluated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA; Hologic QDR 4500 W, Waltham, MA, USA). Appendicular lean muscle mass (ALM) was derived as the sum of lean muscle mass in both arms and legs. ALM index (ALMI) was calculated as ALM divided by the square of height (ALM/height²). All scans were performed by certified operators following standardized protocols.

Handgrip strength was measured using a JAMAR Hand Dynamometer (5030JO; Sammons Preston, Bolingbrook, IL, USA). Two trials were performed for each hand, and the maximum value from either hand was retained for analysis. Gait speed was determined as the time required to walk a 6-meter straight line at a usual pace. For the chair stand test, the time to complete five rises from a seated position was recorded. Both tests were performed twice, with the better result used for analysis. According to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 criteria [1], sarcopenia was diagnosed when the following criteria were met: (1) low muscle mass (DXA-derived ALMI: Men <7.0 kg/m²; Women <5.4 kg/m²) as a necessary component; and (2) either low muscle strength (handgrip <28 kg for men; <18 kg for women) or low physical performance (five-time chair stand test ≥12 s, or 6-meter gait speed <1.0 m/s), or both.

Definition of sarcopenic obesity

According to the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) and European Association for the Study of Obesity (EASO) consensus statement [7,8], SO is defined as a clinical condition characterized by the co-existence of obesity (excess percent body fat [PBF]) and sarcopenia (low muscle mass, low handgrip strength and/or low physical performance). The diagnosis necessitates the positive identification of both components. The American Association of Clinical Endocrinology (AACE) and American College of Endocrinology (ACE) recommend the use of the WHO PBF thresholds of ≥25% for men and ≥35% for women for the diagnosis of obesity [23]. Based on their sarcopenia and obesity status, participants were therefore classified into four groups: normal controls, sarcopenia alone (AWGS-defined sarcopenia without high PBF), obesity alone (high PBF without sarcopenia) and sarcopenic obesity (co-existence of both conditions), respectively.

Serum amino acid analysis

Fasting blood samples were collected and centrifuged to isolate serum, which was then stored at −80 °C until analysis. Baseline serum levels of BCAAs (leucine, isoleucine, valine) and AAAs (tryptophan, phenylalanine, tyrosine) were quantified as previously described [24], using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) on a Shimadzu LC-20ADXR Prominence LC system (Kyoto, Japan) coupled to a Sciex QTRAP5500 mass spectrometer with a Turbo V ion source and TurbolonSpray probe (Framingham, MA, USA).

Serum AA levels were also measured at the 14-year and 20-year follow-ups, but using different analytical platforms. At the 14-year follow-up, serum AAs were quantified using proton nuclear magnetic resonance (¹H NMR) spectroscopy, and at the 20-year follow-up, using the HM700 high-throughput targeted metabolomics. The 14‑year panel included valine, leucine, isoleucine, phenylalanine, and tyrosine (but not tryptophan), whereas the 20‑year panel included valine, leucine, isoleucine, tryptophan, and phenylalanine (but not tyrosine).

Because absolute AA concentrations are not directly comparable across different platforms, we assessed long‑term stability using rank‑order analyses based on within‑time‑point relative rankings (above vs. below the median) rather than absolute values. This approach may help reduce inter‑platform variability and allowed assessment of whether individuals’ relative positions persisted over time. Specifically, 860 participants with available AA measurements were included in the 14‑year stability analysis, and 246 in the 20‑year analysis. Specifically, 860 participants with both baseline and 14‑year AA measurements were included in the 14‑year stability analysis, and 246 with both baseline and 20‑year measurements were included in the 20‑year analysis.

Assessment of dietary intake

At baseline, dietary assessment was conducted with a locally validated 280-item food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) [25], querying the frequency and portion sizes of food consumption in the previous year. Although this FFQ has been validated for this population, it relies on self-reported intake over the preceding year and is subject to recall bias. The energy and nutrient composition of food items were derived from the Chinese Medical Sciences Institute and McCance and Widdowson’s databases. Nutrient intakes were derived by multiplying the reported consumption of each food item by its nutrient content per portion, followed by energy adjustment using the residual method.

Total protein intake was calculated as the sum of animal-based and plant-based protein. Animal protein sources included red meat, processed meat, fish, seafood, poultry, dairy products, eggs and others. Plant protein sources comprised whole and refined grains, fruits, vegetables, soy products, legumes, nuts and others. The dietary inflammatory index (DII) was calculated for each participant using data from the FFQ. The DII, which was originally developed by Shivappa et al. [26] based on 45 food parameters linked to inflammatory markers, was calculated in this study using 30 available parameters, with methodological details published elsewhere [27].

Covariates

At baseline, data on demographics, lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking, drinking, physical activity, living status), medical history of chronic diseases, and medication use (statins, corticosteroids) were collected using validated questionnaires by trained research assistants. Assessed chronic conditions included diabetes mellitus, hypertension, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and cancer. We assessed physical activity using the Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE), adapted for the Chinese population in Hong Kong [28]. Following a standardized protocol, participants underwent duplicate measurements of body weight and height while wearing light indoor clothing and without shoes. A Physician Beam Balance Scale (Healthometer McCook, IL, USA) and a stadiometer (Holtain Ltd., Crosswell, UK) were used for weight and height, respectively. Body mass index (BMI) was derived as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²), using the average values for analysis. Serum hs-CRP levels were quantified using the Vitros Fusion 5.1 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Vitros Chemistry Products, Rochester, NY) and was performed by PathLab Co. Ltd. (Council Bluffs, IA). Triglycerides (TG) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were measured at the 14-year follow-up using ¹H NMR spectroscopy, and the TG/HDL-C ratio was calculated as a potential surrogate indicator of IR [29].

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical variables as percentages. For continuous variables that were not normally distributed, group comparisons were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post‑hoc tests and Bonferroni correction. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests with Bonferroni correction.

For baseline cross-sectional analyses, multivariable multinomial logistic regression was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between each AA (per SD increase) and the outcome, with the normal group as reference. Two models were constructed: Model 1 (crude) and Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, smoking, drinking, physical activity, total energy and protein intake, living status, diabetes, cancer, hypertension [HTN], heart failure [HF], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD], statin and corticosteroid use). To evaluate the consistency of these cross-sectional associations at a later stage, we repeated this analysis using 14-year follow-up data (Table S3). In a sensitivity analysis, we additionally adjusted for the TG/HDL‑C ratio as an indicator of IR (Table S4).

For prospective analysis, logistic regression was used to assess the exploratory associations between baseline AAs and incident sarcopenia-only and SO at 4-year (short-term, Table 3) and 14-year (long-term, Table S5) follow-ups. To evaluate the longitudinal stability of AA measurements, we examined the rank-order stability of individual AA levels over time, with the outcome defined as having a high (above the median) concentration of the corresponding AA at the 14-year (n = 860) and 20-year (n = 246) follow-ups (Table S2). Assessment of the nonlinear relationship between serum AA levels and the outcome was analyzed using restricted cubic splines (RCS) in the fully adjusted regression model. The model was fitted with three knots at the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles of AAs. The curve showed the adjusted OR and 95% CI, with the median concentration set as the reference (OR = 1). A heatmap was generated using Z-scores of median dietary component levels to visualize dietary patterns across groups. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 31.0) and R software (version 4.4.3). A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In terms of inflammatory markers, hs-CRP levels were significantly higher in the SO group (1.60 mg/L, IQR 0.90−3.60) than in the normal group (1.30 mg/L, IQR 0.60−2.70, P < 0.05). Baseline and long-term stability of serum amino acid profiles

Baseline serum BCAA and AAA profiles differed significantly across the four groups (Fig. 1). The sarcopenia-only group had lower levels of BCAAs (valine, leucine, isoleucine) and AAAs (phenylalanine, tyrosine) than the normal group (all P < 0.05), whereas the SO group showed elevated valine and isoleucine (both P < 0.01) and a trend toward higher other AAs. Direct comparison confirmed significantly higher BCAAs, phenylalanine, and tyrosine in the SO versus sarcopenia-only group (all P < 0.05). Baseline AA concentrations demonstrated significant rank-order stability over time, predicting corresponding relative rankings at the 14- and 20-year follow-ups (Table S2). The fully adjusted ORs for maintaining high concentrations (≥ median) per 1‑SD increment in baseline were 2.265 for valine, 2.141 for leucine, 2.726 for isoleucine, 1.831 for phenylalanine, and 2.295 for tyrosine at 14 years. A similar pattern was observed at 20 years, with ORs ranging from 1.575 to 3.041 (Table S2).

Cross-sectional associations between AAs and the risks of sarcopenia alone and sarcopenic obesity

Multivariable logistic regression demonstrated distinct associations between serum AA levels and the risks of sarcopenia-only and SO (Table 2). In the fully adjusted model (Model 2), each 1‑SD increment in BCAAs and AAAs was significantly associated with a lower risk of sarcopenia-only, with ORs ranging from 0.673 (leucine, 95% CI: 0.576−0.785) to 0.845 (tryptophan, 95% CI: 0.736−0.970) (all P < 0.05). In contrast, valine (OR = 1.195, 95% CI: 1.015−1.406, P = 0.032) and isoleucine (OR = 1.221, 95% CI: 1.038−1.436, P = 0.016) were positively associated with SO. Discussion

This study provides novel evidence for contrasting associations of serum BCAAs and AAAs with sarcopenia alone and SO, identifying a divergent pattern characterized by widespread depletion of these BCAAs and AAAs in sarcopenia-only but significant elevation of specific BCAAs in SO. This distinct metabolic signature helps clarify previous seemingly contradictory reports and underscores the necessity of accounting for adiposity in sarcopenia phenotyping. Cross-sectional analyses at baseline and 14 years confirmed the robust positive associations between BCAAs and SO, supporting their utility as concurrent metabolic biomarkers for phenotype differentiation.

The consistent inverse associations between baseline AAs and the risk of developing sarcopenia-only over 4-year follow-up support a potential role of AA deficiency in the pathogenesis of sarcopenia alone. Maintaining adequate serum AA levels may be crucial for muscle health, potentially by ensuring a continuous substrate supply for muscle protein synthesis. However, the causality cannot be established from observational data, and the observed associations could be influenced by other factors such as gut microbiota or metabolic health. Both BCAAs and AAAs are important substrates for muscle protein synthesis and thus hold therapeutic promise for sarcopenia [[30], [31], [32]]. However, lower serum levels in sarcopenia-only cannot be explained by inadequate dietary protein intake, as this group had non-significantly higher intake than controls. Instead, this finding implicates non-dietary factors, such as alterations in host metabolism or the gut microenvironment [33,34], as the underlying cause. Previous studies in sarcopenia reported decreased abundance of Prevotella copri and reduced microbial potential for BCAA metabolism in affected individuals [33]. Another microbiome study also reported depletion of AAA biosynthesis pathway in sarcopenia [34]. These studies suggest that gut microbial dysregulation may contribute to AA deficiency and sarcopenia-only development.

Notably, the inverse association between AAs and sarcopenia-only observed at baseline was no longer significant at the 14‑year cross‑section. This attenuation likely reflects a healthy survivor effect. Participants with persistently low AAs and more severe sarcopenia at baseline may have experienced higher morbidity and mortality over the 14‑year interval, leaving a healthier subgroup whose AA levels were no longer significantly depleted. This also suggests a potential shift in the biological significance of AA levels with advancing age. In younger-old adults, low AAs may primarily reflect substrate deficiency for muscle protein synthesis. However, among the oldest-old, elevated AAs, particularly BCAAs, may represent a more prominent indicator of chronic metabolic dysregulation.

Conversely, elevated baseline BCAAs and certain AAAs were associated with SO, suggesting a role for dysregulated AA metabolism in its pathogenesis, with consistent evidence at 14 years supporting BCAAs as concurrent metabolic markers. Additionally, the relative ranking of individuals’ AA levels demonstrated stability over long-term follow-ups across different analytical platforms. This consistency suggests that baseline measurements may reflect long-term metabolic status and that the observed associations are unlikely to be driven merely by transient changes. This long-term pattern may reflect progressive metabolic dysregulation, where chronic inflammation and IR, characteristic of SO, lead to sustained suppression of BCAA catabolism.

Aging and obesity can promote pathological intramuscular fat accumulation [35], which correlates with elevated BCAA levels [12], and fosters chronic inflammation [5], thereby contributing to IR and muscle loss [35]. IR is associated with reduced activity and expression of key enzymes involved in BCAA catabolism, such as the branched-chain keto acid dehydrogenase (BCKDH) complex, in peripheral tissues like skeletal muscle and adipose tissue [13,15]. Downregulation of BCAA catabolic enzymes were also characterized in individuals with high HOMA-IR [36]. This impairment leads to BCAA accumulation, which in turn may exacerbate metabolic dysfunction. For example, accumulated BCAAs can inhibit pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) activity, impairing glucose oxidation and further contributing to IR [13]. These findings are supported by associations between elevated BCAA levels and IR across multiple experimental models [14,37]. Collectively, these mechanisms may create a vicious cycle that simultaneously promotes fat accretion and impairs muscle quality [5,35]. It is plausible that a similar mechanism contributes to the associations we observed. However, causality cannot be established from observational data, and we lacked direct IR measures, precluding definitive mechanistic interpretation.

In addition to IR, age-related alterations in skeletal muscle AA handling also warrant consideration. Aging is associated with anabolic resistance, a reduced muscle protein synthetic response to protein intake [38]. This diminished anabolic responsiveness may be further compounded by age-related impairment in skeletal muscle uptake of AAs [39]. Moreover, older adults exhibited delayed postprandial AA absorption following protein ingestion, with peak essential and branched-chain AA concentrations occurring later than in younger adults, potentially contributing to suppressed or delayed protein synthesis in senescent muscle [40]. Recent human data suggest that the exaggerated postprandial AA response in older adults after high-protein meals may reflect reduced AA uptake by skeletal muscle, potentially related to lower muscle mass [41]. These age-related changes may further affect AA profiles and their associations with sarcopenia phenotypes, independently of IR. Reduced uptake would limit substrate availability for muscle protein synthesis, while anabolic resistance would impair the efficiency of utilizing available AAs for protein synthesis [[38], [39], [40], [41]]. However, lack of direct measurements of muscle AA uptake and protein synthetic capacity prevents examination of these pathways.

This study found distinct dietary patterns across sarcopenia phenotypes. Sarcopenia-only showed higher intake of red and processed meat but lower consumption of fiber and vegetables. Although FFQ-based dietary assessment and lack of microbiota data warrant caution, these dietary patterns may influence AA availability through gut microbial modulation, consistent with prior evidence that dietary composition can affect microbial AA metabolism [42]. Protein-rich diets may promote proteolytic bacteria that utilize AAs, whereas fiber-rich diets may support saccharolytic bacteria, some of which are capable of AA synthesis [21,42]. Additionally, SO showed a higher DII, which may correlate with elevated systemic inflammation and muscle loss observed in this group [43]. The risk of sarcopenia and SO may relate not only to protein quantity but also to dietary context and quality. However, the above dietary findings remain exploratory and require future validation with objective measures and microbiome analyses.

This study has several strengths. These included a large community-based cohort with longitudinal sarcopenia assessments, phenotype stratification revealing divergent AA associations, dietary data integration, and rigorous confounder adjustment. Several limitations should also be considered. First, different analytical platforms across time points precluded comparison of absolute AA concentrations and assessment of longitudinal changes. Although the long-term rank-order stability supports the relative stability of these measures, short-term influences (e.g., diet, exercise, acute illness) cannot be excluded. Second, the 14-year prospective analysis was limited by reduced sample size and potential survival bias; findings are therefore exploratory.

Third, the single baseline FFQ is subject to recall bias and cannot capture long-term dietary changes. Moreover, the absence of microbiome sequencing data prevents direct validation of the microbial role. Fourth, residual confounding by IR cannot be excluded, as we lacked direct IR measures and adjusted only for diabetes. Fifth, we lacked data on age-related changes in muscle AA uptake capacity, an IR-independent pathway that could affect circulating AA levels. Future studies incorporating repeated objective dietary assessments, microbiota profiling, IR and muscle AA uptake measurements are warranted to confirm these findings. Finally, the observational design precludes causal inference; interventional and mechanistic studies are needed to validate these associations and evaluate phenotype-targeted interventions. Additionally, this cohort consisted of older Hong Kong Chinese adults, and generalizability to other populations warrants further investigation.

Conclusions

In this longitudinal cohort study of community‑dwelling older adults, serum BCAAs and AAAs showed divergent associations with sarcopenia-only and with SO. Lower levels of these AAs were associated with higher sarcopenia-only risk, whereas higher levels, particularly of BCAAs, correlated with elevated SO risk over both short- and long-term follow-up. These metabolic differences were accompanied by distinct dietary patterns, with sarcopenia-only associated with lower intake of fiber and vegetables, while SO linked to a higher dietary inflammatory potential.

Collectively, these findings suggest distinct metabolic profiles that may require phenotype-specific nutritional strategies in older populations. While BCAA/AAA supplementation may hold therapeutic promise for sarcopenia-only, its potential risks in SO warrant caution. The observed paradox cautions against indiscriminate amino acid supplementation in older adults. Further mechanistic and interventional studies are warranted to establish causality and evaluate phenotype-tailored approaches.
