The Larkin Mass and Replica Symmetry Breaking in the Elastic Manifold
Gérard Ben Arous, Pax Kivimae

Abstract
This is the second of a series of three papers about the Elastic Manifold model. This classical model proposes a rich picture due to the competition between the inherent disorder and the smoothing effect of elasticity. In this paper, we analyze our variational formula for the free energy obtained in our first companion paper [ 16 ] . We show that this variational formula may be simplified to one which is solved by a unique saddle point. We show that this saddle point may be solved for in terms of the corresponding critical point equation. Moreover, its terms may be interpreted in terms of natural statistics of the model: namely the overlap distribution and effective radius of the model at a given site. Using this characterization, obtain a complete characterization of the replica symmetry breaking phase. From this we are able to confirm a number of physical predictions about this boundary, namely those involving the Larkin mass [ 54 , 6 , 53 ] , an important critical mass for the system. The zero-temperature Larkin mass has recently been shown to be the topological trivialization threshold, following work of Fyodorov and Le Doussal [ 37 , 38 ] , made rigorous by the first author, Bourgade and McKenna [ 13 , 12 ] .

Introduction

This paper is the second in a series of works focusing on the elastic manifold, a paradigmatic model in the study of disordered elastic systems. Our first paper
[
16
]
, computed the asymptotic quenched free energy of the model in the large-
N
limit in terms of a variational problem over a certain Parisi functional. In this paper, we show that the asymptotic quenched free energy may be computed in terms of a simpler variational problem, which we show is solved by a unique saddle point pair
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
. The critical points equations for this pair are explicit, and thus allow for a concrete method to compute the value of the free energy.

Next, we show that both entries of the saddle point may be interpreted as the asymptotic values of important statistics from the Gibbs measure in the large-
N
limit. In particular, we show that
\zeta_{c}
is given by the limiting overlap distribution of two samples from the Gibbs measure, taken at any given site. In addition, we show that
q_{c}
is the square of the effective radius of the model, in the sense that Gibbs measure, which is supported on a Euclidean space, concentrates in a neighborhood of a product of spheres of radius
\sqrt{Nq_{c}}
. In particular, our equations also give a method to relate and compute both of these quantities.

Employing this, we study the replica symmetry breaking in this model. We find a complete characterization of the boundary between the replica symmetry breaking phases (RSB) and replica symmetric (RS), which we use to confirm a number of partial characterizations in the physics literature. In particular, we confirm that the zero-temperature critical mass is precisely the Larkin mass. We further show that the positive temperature Larkin mass is a critical boundary, as predicted, but show that the Larkin equation, which captures local stability of the RS-solution, does not always coincide with the RS-RSB phase boundary. Moreover, in the RS-phase, we explicitly compute both
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
and the value of the asymptotics quenched free energy. In addition, we show a series of analogous results for a family of spherical models with elastic interaction.

\bm{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{N}

For an introduction to the physical predictions about such phases, we recommend the work

This phase is also expected to be matched by an abundance of meta-stable states in the energy landscape, thought to cause a notable difference in the equilibrium behavior of the model. 1.2
Results on the Replica Symmetric phase

We now turn to our results characterizing the Replica Symmetric (RS) phase of the model. We say that the model is RS, for fixed choice of
(\beta,\mu,t,L,d)
, if the corresponding Parisi measure
\zeta_{c}
is a Dirac mass. This phase tends to have fairly simple behavior compared to the Replica Symmetry Breaking (RSB) phase where the Parisi measure
\zeta_{c}
is supported on more than one point.

Our main result provides an explicit analytic characterization of the RS-phase, as well as the value of the limiting free energy, and the pair
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
in this phase.

Theorem 1.7

(Characterization of The RS-Phase)
.

Fix a choice of
(\beta,\mu,t,L,d)
. Then the model is RS if and only if

\sup_{0<s<R_{1}(\mu;t)}g_{\beta}(s)=g_{\beta}(R_{1}(\mu;t)),

(1.22)

where here

g_{\beta}(s)=\beta^{2}B\left(\frac{2s}{\beta}\right)+\beta sK(s;t)-\frac{1}{L^{d}}\log(K(s;t)I-t\Delta)-s\left(2\beta B^{\prime}\left(\frac{2}{\beta}R_{1}(\mu;t)\right)+\mu\right).

(1.23)

Moreover, if we define for
i\geq 1

R_{i}(\mu;t)=\mathrm{tr}(\mu I-t\Delta)^{-i},

(1.24)

then when the model is RS, the free energy is given by

\mathcal{P}_{\beta,q_{c}}(\zeta_{c})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\log\left(\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\right)+\frac{1}{L^{d}}\log\det(\mu I-t\Delta)+\beta^{2}\left(B(0)-B\left(\frac{2}{\beta}R_{1}(\mu;t)\right)\right)\right),

(1.25)

and if we define
q_{L,\beta}:=-2B^{\prime}\left(\frac{2}{\beta}R_{1}(\mu;t)\right)R_{2}(\mu;t)
, the saddle point pair is given by

(\zeta_{c},q_{c})=(\delta_{q_{L,\beta}},q_{L,\beta}+\beta^{-1}R_{1}(\mu;t)).

(1.26)

From this and Theorem
1.6
, we note the following immediate corollary.

Corollary 1.8
.

When the model is RS, for any
x\in\Omega
we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\langle\|\bm{u}(x)-\bm{u}^{\prime}(x)\|^{2}_{N}\rangle=\beta^{-1}R_{1}(\mu;t).

(1.27)

Note that the expression on the right is independent of the disorder term. In particular, the per-site distance between two samples in the RS-phase is the same if one sets
B=0
. In this case, the Hamiltonian is deterministic, and coincides with the Hamiltonian of the massive discrete Gaussian free field, where this formula is true for all
N
by a quick computation.

Our next corollary compares the formula for the quenched free energy to that of the annealed free energy. The annealed free energy is defined as
N^{-1}L^{-d}\log\mathbb{E}Z_{N,\beta}
, and is easily computed to be

N^{-1}L^{-d}\log\mathbb{E}Z_{N,\beta}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\log\left(\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\right)+\frac{1}{L^{d}}\log\det(\mu I-t\Delta)+\beta^{2}B(0)\right).

(1.28)

By Jensen’s inequality we have that

N^{-1}L^{-d}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,\beta}\leq N^{-1}L^{-d}\log\mathbb{E}Z_{N,\beta},

(1.29)

so the annealed free energy always serves as an upper bound for the quenched free energy, but often this inequality is strict. Our result gives an explicit formula for this gap.

Corollary 1.9
.

When the model is RS, we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(N^{-1}L^{-d}\log\mathbb{E}Z_{N,\beta}-N^{-1}L^{-d}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,\beta}\right)=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}B\left(\frac{2}{\beta}R_{1}(\mu;t)\right).

(1.30)

In particular,

\lim_{\mu\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(N^{-1}L^{-d}\log\mathbb{E}Z_{N,\beta}-N^{-1}L^{-d}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,\beta}\right)=0.

(1.31)

Our above result explicitly gives the AT-line for the elastic manifold model, which we may now compare to results in the physics literature. The most famous of these is the zero-temperature boundary (i.e.

Theorem 1.10

Fixing

For our next result, we fix a positive temperature
\beta>0
. Then varying
\mu

\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)

4B^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{2}{\beta}R_{1}(\mu;t)\right)R_{2}(\mu;t)=1.

(1.33)

This appears in the original work of Mézard and Parisi
[

as (4.8) with

[

g_{\beta}^{\prime}(R_{1}(\mu;t))=0

, one computes

g_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(R_{1}(\mu;t))=4B^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{2}{\beta}R_{1}(\mu;t)\right)-R_{2}(\mu;t)^{-1}.

(1.34)

In particular, (
1.33

g_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(R_{1}(\mu;t))=0
, and so marks the boundary of the regime where
R_{1}(\mu;t)
is a local maximum of
g_{\beta}
(i.e.
g_{\beta}^{\prime\prime}(R_{1}(\mu;t))>0
).

However, while being a local maximum is necessary to be a global maximum, it is not sufficient. In particular, we know that the model is RSB if

4B^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{2}{\beta}R_{1}(\mu;t)\right)R_{2}(\mu;t)<1,

(1.35)

\beta_{DW}(t)\geq 0

\beta\geq\beta_{DW}(t)
, and for
\beta\leq\beta_{DW}(t)
, the system is RS for any choice of

However, for temperatures below

1.33

\beta\geq\beta_{DW}(t)

\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)
is indeed the largest critical mass. In particular, global stability is implied by the local stability for masses above
\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)
.

Theorem 1.11
.

Fixing
(t,B,L,d)
, and considering the behavior of the model at parameters
(\beta,\mu)
, the following statements hold:

1.

If
\beta\leq\beta_{DW}(t)
, then for any
\mu
, the model with parameters
(\beta,\mu)
is RS.

2.

If
\beta>\beta_{DW}(t)
, and
\mu\geq\mu_{Lar}(\beta)
, then the model with parameters
(\beta,\mu)
is RS.

However, these results leave open the behavior below the Larkin mass at positive temperature. This problem is discussed by
[

, particularly in their Appendix E, and the region of ambiguity appears as the grey region of their Figure 10.

In essence, this boundary is resolved by our Theorem

[

, we show that a similar failure can occur for the continuum model with

and

1.3
Results for the Spherical Model

We now introduce an additional model, which is similar to the above except that instead of taking an isotropic field on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
in the presence of a quadratic potential, we consider an isotropic field on the sphere. Such isotropic fields are more well known, as they form the family of spherical spin glass models, which have seen extensive study.

In particular, we fix, for each
N\geq 1
, a choice of centered Gaussian random field
H_{N}:S_{N}\to\mathbb{R}
with isotropic covariance given for
\sigma,\tau\in S_{N}
by

\mathbb{E}[H_{N}(\sigma)H_{N}(\tau)]=N\xi((\sigma,\tau)_{N}),

(1.36)

where
\xi:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is some fixed function. As before, it is a result of Schoenberg
[
66
]
that for such a family to exist for all
N\geq 1
it is necessary and sufficient that
\xi
is an analytic function on
[-1,1]
with positive coefficients. That is, there are coefficients
\beta_{p}\geq 0
such that

\xi(x)=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\beta_{p}^{2}x^{p},

(1.37)

and such that
\xi(1)<\infty
. We will assume as before that for some sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
,
\xi(1+\epsilon)<\infty
, so that
\xi
extends to an analytic function on
(-1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)
.

We then take a sequence of i.i.d copies of
(H_{N,x})_{x\in\Omega}
. We define the spherical version of our Hamiltonian by taking, for
\bm{u}\in S_{N}^{\Omega}

\mathcal{H}_{N,\rm{Sph}}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}t\Delta_{xy}(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(y))+\sum_{x\in\Omega}H_{N,x}(\bm{u}(x))+\sqrt{N}h\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{u}_{1}(x).

(1.38)

Associated with this, we may define as well the partition function

Z_{N,\beta,\rm{Sph}}=\int_{S_{N}^{\Omega}}e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}_{N,\rm{Sph}}(\bm{u})}\omega(d\bm{u}),

(1.39)

where here
\omega(d\bm{u})
is the surface measure on
S_{N}^{\Omega}
induced by the inclusion
S_{N}^{\Omega}\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Omega}
. As before, in
[
16
]
we gave a computation for free energy
N^{-1}L^{-d}\log Z_{N,\beta,\rm{Sph}}
.

Theorem 1.12

(
[
16
]
, Theorem I.1.6)
.

We have a.s. that

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}L^{-d}\log Z_{N,\beta}=\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}L^{-d}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,\beta}=F_{\Omega,\mathrm{Sph}}(\beta,t),

(1.40)

where here
F_{\Omega,\mathrm{Sph}}(\beta,t)
is an explicit deterministic quantity.

As in the case above, our main goal again is to make the formula for
F_{\Omega,\mathrm{Sph}}(\beta,t)
more tractable.

Given a choice of
\zeta\in\mathscr{Y}(1):=\mathscr{Y}
, we define for
s\in[0,1]

\delta(s)=\int_{s}^{1}\zeta([0,u])du.

(1.41)

Furthermore, let us take a choice of
q_{*}>0
such that
\zeta([q_{*},1])=0
. Given this data, we may define the functional

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}_{\beta}(&\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\log\left(\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\right)+\beta h^{2}K(\beta\delta(0);t)^{-1}+\beta(1-q_{*})K(\beta(1-q_{*});t)\\
&-\frac{1}{L^{d}}\log(K(\beta(1-q_{*});t)I-t\Delta)+\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\beta K(\beta\delta(u);t)du+\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\zeta([0,u])\xi^{\prime}(u)du\bigg).\end{split}

(1.42)

A similar remark as to Remark
1.3
implies the choice of
q_{*}
doesn’t affect the value of the function. Our first result here is a computation of the quenched free energy.

Theorem 1.13

(A New Spherical Parisi Formula)
.

We have that

F_{\Omega}(\beta,t,\mu)=\inf_{\zeta\in\mathscr{Y}}\mathcal{B}_{\beta}(\zeta).

(1.43)

We give this result in the form a minimizer here, as in the case of
L^{d}=1
, one may check that it coincides with the general formula for the limiting quenched free energy of the normal spherical spin glass model obtained in the even case by Talagrand
[
73
]
and the general case by Chen
[
24
]
. As in the case of
[
73
]
, we may also use the convexity of the functional to obtain a similar characterization of the unique minimizer.

Theorem 1.14

(Identification of the Overlap Distribution)
.

The functional
\mathcal{B}_{\beta}
is strictly convex on
\mathscr{Y}
with a unique minimizer,
\zeta_{c}
, which is the unique solution to the following equation:

\zeta\left(\big\{s\in[0,1]:f_{\xi}(s)=\sup_{0<s^{\prime}<1}f_{\xi}(s^{\prime})\big\}\right)=1.

(1.44)

where for
s\in(0,1)
, we define

f_{\beta,\xi}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}F_{\beta,\xi}(u)du,\;\;F_{\beta,\xi}(s)=-h^{2}K(\beta\delta(0);t)^{-2}K^{\prime}(\beta\delta(0);t)+\xi^{\prime}(s)+\int_{0}^{s}K^{\prime}(\beta\delta(u);t)du.

(1.45)

and
\delta
is as in (
1.41
).

Finally, we have a partial result which characteristics
\zeta_{c}
in terms of the model. Unfortunately, we will need an additional assumption on
\xi
in this case to obtain this characterization, as indeed, even in the one-site case (i.e. when
|\Omega|=1
), a general characterization is not known.

The condition we will need is the following: a mixing function
\xi
is generic if

\text{span}\{x^{p}:p\in\mathbb{N},\text{ such that }\beta_{p}\neq 0\}

(1.46)

is dense in
C([-1,1],\mathbb{R})
with respect to the uniform topology. Under this assumption, we obtain a version of Theorem

, which additionally provides an interpretation of the Parisi measure in terms of the Gibbs measure. In this case, this is given by

\langle f(\bm{u})\rangle_{\rm{Sph}}=\frac{1}{Z_{N,\beta,\rm{Sph}}}\int_{S_{N}^{\Omega}}e^{-\beta\mathcal{H}_{N,\mathrm{Sph}}(\bm{u})}\omega(d\bm{u}).

(1.47)

Essentially, we again find that
\zeta_{c}
encodes the law of the limiting overlap distribution.

Theorem 1.15
.

Then if
\xi
is generic, then we have for any continuous function
f:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
and

that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\langle f((\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}(x))_{N})\rangle_{\rm{Sph}}=\int_{0}^{1}f(r)\zeta_{c}(dr).

(1.48)

Finally, we have a similar characterization of the RS-phase in this case, though in this case the results are less explicit.

Theorem 1.16
.

If the model is RS for some choice of
\beta
then the free energy is given by

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\inf_{q\in[0,1)}\bigg(&\log\left(\frac{2\pi}{\beta}\right)+\beta h^{2}K(\beta(1-q);t)^{-1}+\beta K(\beta(1-q);t)\\
&-\frac{1}{L^{d}}\log\det(K(\beta(1-q)-t\Delta))+\beta^{2}(\xi(1)-\xi(q))\bigg),\end{split}

(1.49)

and the Parisi measure is given by
\zeta_{c}=\delta_{q_{*}}
, where
q_{*}
is the minimizer of this expression.
Moreover, a model is RS if and only if the minimizer
q_{*}
satisfies

\sup_{q_{*}\leq s\leq 1}g_{\beta}(s)=g_{\beta}(q_{*}),

(1.50)

where here

\begin{split}g_{\beta}(s)=\beta^{2}\xi(s)+\beta(1-s)K(\beta(1-s);t)-\frac{1}{L^{d}}\log(K(\beta(1-s);t)-t\Delta)\\
-s\left(\beta^{2}\xi^{\prime}(q_{*})-\beta K(\beta(1-q_{*});t\right).\end{split}

(1.51)

1.4
Overview

We now provide an outline of our proofs, as well as a structural overview of the paper. The core of our method can be explained in how we show that the expression for
F_{\Omega}(\beta,t,\mu)
in Theorem
1.1
is equal to the expression given in Theorem
1.5
. The reason for this is that in the course of proving this result, the methods we employ can be generalized to show most of the other main results.

To outline this method, we need to discuss the expression for
F_{\Omega}(\beta,t,\mu)
derived in Theorem
1.1
. The exact version of this is recalled below in Theorem
4.1
. However, to explain the key improvement, we only need to recall that it expresses the limiting free energy as an optimization problem of the form

F_{\Omega}(\beta,t,\mu)=\sup_{\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}}\left(\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q})}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\right).

(1.52)

The functional
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
is not important now, as our main focus is on the domains of these optimizations. Already the supremum is over
\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
, a space of dimension
L^{d}
. Moreover, the infimum is over pairs
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
, and while
\zeta\in\mathscr{Y}(q_{av})
where
q_{av}
is the average of the values of
\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
, the other parameter is a continuous function
\bm{\Phi}:[0,q_{av}]\to\prod_{x\in\Omega}[0,\bm{q}(x)]
, subject to some conditions. In particular, both domains we are optimizing over are enormous, and growing in
L
.

In effect, Theorem
1.5
shows that we may obtain the same value for
F_{\Omega}(\beta,t,\mu)
by restricting the supremum to values for which
\bm{q}
is the constant vector, and the infimum to the case where
\bm{\Phi}
is the identity. Not only does this shrink the domain of optimization massively to a now
L
-independent domain, but when evaluated on this domain, the expression for
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\beta,\bm{q}}
simplifies significantly to the
\mathcal{P}_{\beta,q}(\zeta)
given above.

We perform this restriction in two steps. In the first, we show that if we assume that
\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
is a constant vector, then we may restrict the infimum to the case where
\bm{\Phi}
is the identity. We will show that this effectively a special case of the same question for the spherical model. In particular, this restriction follows directly from our formula for the spherical model, Theorem
1.13
.

This is done in Section
2
, which roughly provides the proofs for Theorem
1.13
, as well as Theorems
1.14
and
1.15
. For the first theorem, we use a continuity argument in
\xi
to reduce ourselves to the generic case. For such models, the gradient of the Parisi functional in the coefficients of
\bm{\xi}
provide sufficient information to identity any measure which is an minimizer of
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
, the spherical version of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}
. However, when combined with the inherent symmetry of the model, all of these derivatives coincide, which essentially forces the minimizer to be symmetric. This simultaneously shows the first two theorems. The final theorem then is an exercise in the variational calculus.

The next step in showing Theorem
1.5
, is to show that the supremum over
\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
occurs on a constant vector. This we do over two sections. First, we have Section
3
, which establishes the existence of minimizers of
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
, as well as a set of formulas obeyed by them. The method used here is just a mixture of variational calculus and analysis of the resulting minimization equations. These results immediately give formulas for the minimizers of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}
as well.

Next, in Section
4
, we complete the proof of Theorem
1.5
and Theorem
1.4
as well. The main result is Proposition
4.6
, which shows that the
\bm{q}\mapsto\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}(\bm{q})}\mathcal{P}_{\beta,\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
is concave and has a unique solution. The main tool are some identifications between subsets of the domains
\mathscr{Y}(\bm{q})
as one varies
\bm{q}
. Employing these identifications, the functional
\mathcal{P}_{\beta,\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
is essentially linear in the data of
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
. The main difficulty is showing that we may restrict the domains for the minimization of
\mathcal{P}_{\beta,\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
, locally around a given point, to use these identifications uniformly. For this, we use the identities satisfied by the minimizers obtain in Section
3
. This is enough to establish concavity, but not strict concavity, so we are left with the problem of showing there is unique maximizing
\bm{q}
. For this we use the convexity to show that the set of maximizers forms a non-empty convex set. Thus if there is not a unique maximizer, this set will contain a line of maximizers. Differentiating along this line, and using the above identifications, this implies an additional equation which contradicts the equations for minimizers established before.

Now we have Section
5
, which establishes Theorem
1.6
. After reducing to the case of
h=0
, the proof of (
1.20
), which identifies
q_{c}
in terms of the effective squared radius, essentially follows from uniqueness of the maximizer of the previous section, as we show that one may identify
\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}(\bm{q})}\mathcal{P}_{\beta,\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
with the contribution to the partition function from configurations whose squared radii are roughly
\bm{q}
. However (
1.21
) is much more complicated. Morally, this result should follow in the same way one obtains the spherical result, Theorem
1.15
, as in view of (
1.21
) one may imagine that Gibbs measure is essentially concentrated on a product of spheres of radius
\sqrt{q_{c}}
. If this were true, you could use the fact that the restricted Hamiltonian turns out to be a generic spin glass, and obtain it from Theorem
1.15
. However, justifying this replacement is beyond our means. Instead, we show that we may replace the Gibbs measure with one supported on thin annuli of radius roughly
\sqrt{q_{c}}
. When this radius is small enough, we may attempt to mimic the proof of Section
2
by finding certain centered Gaussian fields on these annuli and correlate them to our restricted field. Once we produce enough of these, and send the thickness to zero, we are able to proceed in the same spirit as Section
3
.

Finally, we complete the paper with Section
6
, which establishes our results for the RS-regime. In particular, it is where we prove Theorems
1.7
,
1.10
,
1.11
and
1.16
. These follow from direct analysis of the saddle point equations which specify
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
, and in particular, is essentially self-contained.

1.5
History and Related Works

We will now review some additional related works, beginning with those involving the one-site case (i.e. when
|\Omega|=1
), where much more is known. Starting with the spherical model (
1.38
), an expression for the quenched free energy given by Crisanti and Sommers
[
28
]
, employing the replica-symmetry breaking scheme pioneered by Parisi
[
63
]
compute the quenched free energy of Ising spin glass model. A rigorous derivation of these formulas in the even case was obtained by Talagrand
[
73
,
74
]
, building upon interpolation method used by Guerra to obtain the upper bound in the Ising case
[
44
]
. The general Ising case was later obtained by Panchenko
[
61
]
, with the general spherical case soon after being obtained by Chen
[
24
]
.

We also mention that a number of computations for the free energy of different multi-site models have recently been obtained. Many of these rely on the foundational work of Panchenko
[
62
]
, who computed the free energy of the multi-species Ising spin glass model in the convex case. Following this, Bates and Sohn
[
8
,
7
]
computed the free energy of the multi-species spherical spin glass model, again in the convex case. We also mention the work of Ko
[
51
]
, which computes the free energy in the spherical case with contained overlaps.

As we have mentioned above, another approach to studying this model is through its topological complexity. The computation of the annealed complexity of the elastic manifold model done by Fyodorov and Le Doussal
[
37
,
38
]
, and later made rigorous through the work of Bourgade, McKenna, and the first author
[
13
,
12
]
. The works
[
37
,
38
]
also build on related annealed complexity computations of Fyodorov et. al.
[
33
,
40
,
35
,
39
]
. Many of these results were later made rigorous, and extended, by Auffinger and Zeng
[
5
,
4
]
.

Much more is known about the complexity in the one-site spherical case. In particular, the annealed complexity in this case was computed in works by Auffinger, Černý, and the first author
[
3
,
2
]
. These results match those obtained earlier in physics literature
[
1
,
23
]
, for which they correspond to the zero-temperature case of a more general computation for the complexity of TAP states.

However, outside of the topological trivialization regime, computation of the annealed complexity only yields a lower bound on the ground state energy. Moreover, this bound is known not to be tight in general (see
[
26
]
). So in general, to obtain the correct bound, one must compute the quenched complexity. A remarkable result of Subag
[
68
]
, later extended by Subag and Zeitouni
[
71
]
, that in the case of the pure
p
-spin glass model, the annealed and quenched complexity coincide. This was further extended to the quenched complexity of low-lying local minima for a certain class of
1
RSB mixed spin glass models by Subag, Zeitouni, and the first author in
[
17
]
. Not only does this lead to a computation of the ground state energy, but such results have led to a more complete understanding of the geometry of the low-temperature Gibbs measure, as shown in follow-up works by Subag and Zeitouni
[
69
,
70
]
. In addition, these have played an important role in studying the associated Langevin dynamics, as in the work of Gheissari and Jagannath
[
41
]
. In addition, the work of Jagannath and the first author
[
14
]
discusses further ramifications of this study to concepts such as shattering and metastability.

However, outside of these special cases, such results are scarce, with the only full extension being to the non-gradient generalization of the spherical pure
p
-spin model
[
49
]
, some partial results on the spherical bipartite model
[
50
]
, both by the second author, and a general result for the total local minima of the mixed spin glass model by Belius and Schmidt
[
10
]
, though with no control on their energy values. In all cases, one simply shows that the annealed and quenched complexity coincide, as no rigorous method to compute the quenched complexity outside of this case is known.

Finally, we mention that identification of the parameters arising in Parisi-type variational formulas has also been studied in a number of spin glass models. In one-site models, this was approached in the works of Talagrand
[
72
]
and Panchenko
[
59
]
. Moreover, recent work by Bates and Sohn
[
9
]
used these methods of identification to simplify the Parisi-formula for the balanced Potts spin glass model. Following this was a recent result of Issa
[
47
]
which did the same for a symmetric version of the multi-species spin glass model. With an added genericness assumption, both of these works also allow one to identify this measure with an appropriate limiting overlap distribution. In addition, both use a method similar to the one we employ in Section
2
to simplify a symmetric multi-site variational formula to a single parameter.

2
Proof of Theorems
1.13
,
1.14
, and
1.15

In this section we establish our simplified formula for the spherical model, and in particular give the proofs for Theorems
1.13
,
1.14
, and
1.15
. This establishes all of our results for the spherical model except for Theorem
1.16
, which describes the model’s RS phase. Before proceeding, we note that it is clear that it suffices to demonstrate our results in the case of
\beta=1
, which we will assume for the remainder of the section. In addition, we will write
Z_{N,\mathrm{Sph}}:=Z_{N,1,\mathrm{Sph}}
and
\mathcal{B}:=\mathcal{B}_{1}
.

We now outline the main steps in our method. To prove Theorem
1.13
, we will first only work in the case of generic
\xi
(obtaining the general case later by a continuity result). We then recall a more general inhomogeneous Parisi functional, which gives the free energy for a more general class of inhomogeneous models recalled below. We show, though, that if one differentiates the infimum of this Parisi functional in the model parameters, then one can obtain certain statistics of any minimizer of the Parisi functional. When the model is generic, these are enough to uniquely determine the minimizer.

Moreover, the infimum of this Parisi functional is the limit as
N\to\infty
of these models average free energy. Using this, we are able to relate the statistics of a minimizer to the limits of certain derivatives of the average free energy, which we relate to certain statistics of the Gibbs measure. As these will obviously be independent of
x\in\Omega
for the original spherical model considered above, this shows that the unique minimizer of the Parisi functional must also be independent of
x\in\Omega
. This is enough to demonstrate Theorems
1.13
and
1.15
, and in effect does so simultaneously in the generic case. After this all that is left is to Theorem
1.14
, which is followed by a direct computation.

For one-site models, differentiability of the infimum of the Parisi functional goes back to work of Talagrand
[
72
]
. This was followed by work of Panchenko
[
60
]
, who used a similar method as outlined above to show the one-site version of Theorem
1.15
. We essentially follow their line of reasoning, however in our case it allows us to simplify our formula considerably in the generic case, and in the general case as well by continuity.

We now recall some results and notations from our companion paper
[
16
]
. Let us fix a finite set
\Omega
and a positive semi-definite matrix
D\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
. Next, take a collection,
\bm{\xi}=\{\xi_{x}\}_{x\in\Omega}
, such that each
\xi_{x}
is a mixing function as in (
1.37
), and let
\{H_{N,x}\}_{x\in\Omega}
be an independent family of functions with
H_{N,x}
associated to the mixing function
\xi_{x}
as in (
1.36
). Finally, let us fix a vector of external field strengths
\bm{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
. We then define a Hamiltonian, generalizing (
1.38
) by

\mathcal{H}_{N,D,\bm{\xi},\bm{h},\rm{Sph}}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}D_{xy}(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(y))+\sum_{x\in\Omega}H_{N,x}(\bm{u}(x))+\sqrt{N}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{h}(x)\bm{u}_{1}(x).

(2.1)

Associated with this, we may define as well the partition function

Z_{N,\rm{Sph}}(D,\bm{\xi},\bm{h})=\int_{S_{N}^{\Omega}}e^{-\mathcal{H}_{N,D,\bm{\xi},\bm{h},\rm{Sph}}(\bm{u})}\omega(d\bm{u}).

(2.2)

The free energy of this partition function was computed in Theorem I.1.6. To state this result, we need to set more notation First, if one has
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
and
D\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
, we will use the shorthand
D+\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
to denote the matrix
[D+\bm{u}]_{xy}=D_{xy}+\delta_{xy}\bm{u}(x)
. When
A
and
B
are symmetric matrices, we will use the notation
A>B
to denote that
A-B
is positive definite.

Next, we will need some functions associated with
D
. Existence and claims about these functions were shown in Appendix C of
[
16
]
. When
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
is such that
D+\bm{u}>0
, we define
\bm{R}^{D}(\bm{u})\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
by

R_{x}^{D}(\bm{u})=[(D+\bm{u})^{-1}]_{xx}\text{ for }x\in\Omega.

(2.3)

Next, we define
\bm{K}^{D}
as the functional inverse of
\bm{R}^{D}
, so that for
\bm{u}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
,

[\left(D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u})\right)^{-1}]_{xx}=\bm{u}(x)\text{ for }x\in\Omega.

(2.4)

We also define for
\bm{u}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
,

\Lambda^{D}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\left(\sum_{x\in\Omega}K_{x}^{D}(\bm{u})\bm{u}(x)-\log\det(D+\bm{K}^{D}(u))\right),

(2.5)

This is in fact the Legendre transform of the concave function
\bm{u}\mapsto\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\log\det(D+\bm{u})
, so that in particular
|\Omega|\nabla\Lambda^{D}(\bm{u})=\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{u})
.

Next we focus on the domain of our functional. We first take a measure
\zeta\in\mathscr{Y}
. Then, for each
x\in\Omega

Definition 2.3

For a finite set

3
Minimization Identities for the Parisi Functional

In this section, we will study the derivative of the functional
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{D,\bm{\xi},\bm{h}}
, recalled in the previous section, with respect to a certain family of perturbations. These perturbations essentially amount to a form of measure re-parametrization. While this family typically does not form a complete basis of tangent directions in the domain, they will give a number of important facts.

The first is the existence of global minimizers for
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{D,\bm{\xi},\bm{h}}
, an important technical fact used above. The second is that if
\xi^{\prime}_{x}(0)\neq 0
for all
x\in\Omega
, then any minimizer, say
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
, is such that
\text{supp}(\bm{\Phi}_{*}(\zeta))\cap\partial[0,1]^{\Omega}=\varnothing
. Roughly speaking, this shows that the (possibly plural) Parisi measures do not have support at either
0
or
1
, and this result will be used crucially in our proof of concavity in Section
4
. Finally, it gives explicit formulas that any minimizer must satisfy, which will be important in showing concentration of the radius of the model.

We note the methodology of this section, especially Lemma
3.1
, owes to the work of
[
7
]
, who compute the minimizers of similar functionals in the case of certain multi-species spherical spin glass models.

To begin, let us fix
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}
. Then, choosing some additional coordinate-wise non-decreasing absolutely continuous function
\bm{\chi}:[0,1]\to[0,1]^{\Omega}
, and we define
\bm{\Psi}=\bm{\chi}-\bm{\Phi}
. We consider the family

\bm{\tilde{\Phi}}^{\epsilon}=\bm{\Phi}+\epsilon\bm{\Psi}=(1-\epsilon)\bm{\Phi}+\epsilon\bm{\chi}.

(3.1)

We wish to compute the derivative of
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{D,\bm{\xi},\bm{h}}
in the family
\bm{\tilde{\Phi}}^{\epsilon}
, but as it may not live in
\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}
due to violating the condition (
2.6
), we need to introduce a slight reparametrization. Namely, define

\alpha_{\epsilon}(q)=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\tilde{\Phi}^{\epsilon}_{x}(q)=q(1-\epsilon)+\epsilon\sum_{x\in\Omega}\chi_{x}(q),

(3.2)

which is a continuous, strictly increasing function
\alpha_{\epsilon}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
. In particular, this shows that
\alpha_{\epsilon}
is a homeomorphism from
[0,1]
onto
[\alpha_{\epsilon}(0),\alpha_{\epsilon}(1)]
, so we may define for
q\in[\alpha_{\epsilon}(0),\alpha_{\epsilon}(1)]
,

\bm{\Phi}^{\epsilon}=\bm{\tilde{\Phi}}^{\epsilon}\circ\alpha_{\epsilon}^{-1},

(3.3)

and otherwise define it by the linear interpolations

\bm{\Phi}^{\epsilon}(q)=\frac{q}{\alpha_{\epsilon}(0)}\tilde{\bm{\Phi}}^{\epsilon}(0),\;q\in[0,\alpha_{\epsilon}(0)),\;\;\bm{\Phi}^{\epsilon}(q)=\frac{q-\alpha_{\epsilon}(1)+\tilde{\bm{\Phi}}^{\epsilon}(1)(1-q)}{1-\alpha_{\epsilon}(1)},\;q\in(\alpha_{\epsilon}(1),1].

(3.4)

We define
\zeta^{\epsilon}
as the push-forward of
\zeta
under
\alpha_{\epsilon}
, so that

\zeta^{\epsilon}([0,u])=\begin{cases}1,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\ u>\alpha_{\epsilon}(1)\\
\zeta([0,\alpha_{\epsilon}^{-1}(u)]),\;\;\;u\in[\alpha_{\epsilon}(0),\alpha_{\epsilon}(1)]\\
0,\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;u<\alpha_{\epsilon}(0)\end{cases}.

(3.5)

It is then easily checked that
(\zeta^{\epsilon},\bm{\Phi}^{\epsilon})\in\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}
.

We will now prepare to compute the derivative of
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{D,\bm{\xi},\bm{h}}
in this family at
\epsilon=0
. There is a slight technicality, in that this only defines a family
\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}
for positive
\epsilon>0
, so we may only consider the derivative in the positive direction. On the other hand if both
\Psi_{x}(0)=0
and
\Psi_{x}(1)=1
for all
x\in\Omega
, one may actually check the above construction defines a family for
\epsilon\in(-\delta,\delta)
for sufficiently small
\delta>0
. Thus in this case the derivative itself may be considered. For brevity, we will omit this from the notation, as it will not affect the value of the derivative, though we will return to this technicality in the proof of Lemma
3.3
below, as local minimization only implies that the derivative is non-negative in the prior case, while it implies actual vanishing in the latter.

The computation of this derivative will be given by the following lemma, for which we will introduce some notation to make its statement simpler. Let consider the function
\bm{\delta}:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)^{\Omega}
associated to
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
by (
1.41
). We first introduce the following constants, indexed by

\mathcal{J}_{y}=\sum_{x,z\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{\delta}(0)))^{-1}]_{xy}[(D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{\delta}(0)))^{-1}]_{zy}\bm{h}(x)\bm{h}(z).

(3.6)

Next, we introduce three families of functions, again indexed by
y\in\Omega

\mathcal{G}_{y}(q)=\xi^{\prime}_{y}(\Phi_{y}(q))+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\int_{0}^{q}\nabla_{y}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(u))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(u)du\right)+\mathcal{J}_{y},

(3.7)

\mathcal{H}_{y}(q)=K^{D}_{y}(\bm{\delta}(q))+\int_{q}^{1}\zeta([0,u])\xi^{\prime\prime}_{y}(\Phi_{y}(u))\Phi_{y}^{\prime}(u)du,

(3.8)

\mathcal{F}_{y}(q)=\mathcal{H}_{y}(q)+\zeta([0,q])\mathcal{G}_{y}(q).

(3.9)

Lemma 3.1
.

With notation as above, and for any
q_{*}
such that
\zeta([0,q_{*}))=1
and
\Phi_{x}(q_{*})<1
for all

, we have that

\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{D,\bm{\xi},\bm{h}}(\zeta^{\epsilon},\bm{\Phi}^{\epsilon})=\frac{1}{2|\Omega|}\sum_{y\in\Omega}\left(\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\mathcal{F}_{y}(q)\Psi_{y}^{\prime}(q)dq-\mathcal{F}_{y}(q_{*})\Psi_{y}(q_{*})+\mathcal{H}_{y}(0)\Psi_{y}(0)\right).

(3.10)

Proof.

We restrict to
\epsilon
is small enough that we still have for each
x\in\Omega

\Phi_{x}^{\epsilon}(\alpha_{\epsilon}(q_{*}))=\tilde{\Phi}_{x}^{\epsilon}(q_{*})<1.

(3.11)

We also denote by
\bm{\delta}^{\epsilon}
as the
\bm{\delta}
-function defined with respect to
(\zeta^{\epsilon},\bm{\Phi}^{\epsilon})
. We observe that

\delta_{x}^{\epsilon}(\alpha_{\epsilon}(1))=\Phi_{x}^{\epsilon}(1)-\Phi_{x}^{\epsilon}(\alpha_{\epsilon}(1))=1-\tilde{\Phi}^{\epsilon}_{x}(1)=-\epsilon\Psi_{x}(1),

(3.12)

and that

\delta_{x}^{\epsilon}(\alpha_{\epsilon}(q))=\delta_{x}(\alpha_{\epsilon}(1))+\int_{\alpha_{\epsilon}(q)}^{\alpha_{\epsilon}(1)}\zeta([0,q])(\Phi^{\epsilon}_{x})^{\prime}(q)dq=-\epsilon\Psi_{x}(1)+\int_{q}^{1}\zeta([0,q])(\tilde{\Phi}^{\epsilon}_{x})^{\prime}(q)dq.

(3.13)

Finally note that for
q\in[0,\alpha_{\epsilon}(0)]
we have that
\delta_{x}^{\epsilon}(q)=\delta_{x}^{\epsilon}(\alpha_{\epsilon}(0))
.

Now we note that by using
\zeta^{\epsilon}([0,\alpha_{\epsilon}(q_{*})))=1
and (
3.11
), we may evaluate the functional using the
\epsilon
-dependent endpoint
\alpha_{\epsilon}(q_{*})
. To do this, we note that

\int_{0}^{\alpha_{\epsilon}(q_{*})}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{\epsilon}(q))(\Phi_{x}^{\epsilon})^{\prime}(q)dq=K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{\epsilon}(0))\Phi_{x}^{\epsilon}(0)+\int_{0}^{q_{*}}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{\epsilon}(\alpha_{\epsilon}(q)))(\tilde{\Phi}_{x}^{\epsilon})^{\prime}(q)dq.

(3.14)

Using these results we may compute that

\begin{split}\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\int_{0}begin^{\alpha_{\epsilon}(q_{*})}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{\epsilon}(q))(\Phi_{x}^{\epsilon})^{\prime}(q)dq=\Psi_{x}(0)K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(0))+\int_{0}^{q_{*}}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(q))\Psi_{x}^{\prime}(q)dq\\
+\sum_{y\in\Omega}\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\nabla_{y}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(q))\bigg(-\Psi_{y}(1)+\int_{q}^{1}\zeta([0,u])\Psi_{y}^{\prime}(u)du\bigg)\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq.\end{split}

(3.15)

To contend with the final term we note that

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\nabla_{y}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(q))\bigg(\int_{q}^{1}\zeta([0,u])\Psi_{y}^{\prime}(u)du\bigg)\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq=\\
\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\zeta([0,u])\left(\int_{0}^{u}\nabla_{y}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(q))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq\right)\Psi_{y}^{\prime}(u)du+(\Psi_{y}(1)-\Psi_{y}(q_{*}))\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\nabla_{y}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(q))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq.\end{split}

(3.16)

Next we note that

\delta^{\epsilon}_{x}(\alpha_{\epsilon}(q_{*}))=\Phi^{\epsilon}_{x}(1)-\Phi^{\epsilon}_{x}(\alpha_{\epsilon}(q_{*}))=1-\epsilon\Psi_{x}(q_{*})

(3.17)

so we may conclude that

\frac{d}{d\epsilon}|\Omega|\Lambda^{D}(\bm{\delta}^{\epsilon}(\alpha_{\epsilon}(q_{*})))=-\sum_{x\in\Omega}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(q_{*}))\Psi_{x}(q_{*}).

(3.18)

In conclusion, and noting the terms involving
\Psi_{y}(1)

\begin{split}\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\left(|\Omega|\Lambda^{D}(\bm{\delta}^{\epsilon}(\alpha_{\epsilon}(q_{*})))+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\int_{0}^{\alpha_{\epsilon}(q_{*})}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{\epsilon}(q))(\Phi_{x}^{\epsilon})^{\prime}(q)dq\right)=\\
\sum_{y\in\Omega}\Psi_{y}(0)K^{D}_{y}(\bm{\delta}(0))-\sum_{y\in\Omega}\Psi_{y}(q_{*})\left(\sum_{x\in\Omega}\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\nabla_{y}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(q))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq+K^{D}_{y}(\bm{\delta}(q_{*}))\right)\\
+\sum_{y\in\Omega}\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\left(\sum_{x\in\Omega}\zeta([0,u])\left(\int_{0}^{u}\nabla_{y}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(q))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq\right)+K^{D}_{y}(\bm{\delta}(u))\right)\Psi_{y}^{\prime}(u)du.\end{split}

(3.19)

Next, noting that
\zeta^{\epsilon}([0,\alpha_{\epsilon}(0)))=0
, we see that

\int_{0}^{1}\zeta^{\epsilon}([0,q])\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\Phi_{x}^{\epsilon}(q))(\Phi_{x}^{\epsilon})^{\prime}(q)dq=\int_{0}^{1}\zeta([0,q])\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\tilde{\Phi}_{x}^{\epsilon}(q))(\tilde{\Phi}_{x}^{\epsilon})^{\prime}(q)dq+\xi_{x}(1)-\xi_{x}(\tilde{\Phi}^{\epsilon}_{x}(1)),

(3.20)

so we compute as above that

\begin{split}\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\int_{0}^{1}\zeta^{\epsilon}([0,q])\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\Phi_{x}^{\epsilon}(q))(\Phi_{x}^{\epsilon})^{\prime}(q)dq=\\
\int_{0}^{1}\zeta([0,q])\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(q))\Psi_{x}^{\prime}(q)dq+\int_{0}^{1}\zeta([0,q])\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(q))\Psi_{x}(q)\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq-\xi_{x}^{\prime}(1)\Psi_{x}(1).\end{split}

(3.21)

Using integration by parts, we rewrite the second term as

\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{q}^{1}\zeta([0,q])\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(q))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq\right)\Psi_{x}^{\prime}(u)du+\left(\int_{0}^{1}\zeta([0,q])\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(q))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq\right)\Psi_{x}(0).

(3.22)

We note that as
\zeta([0,q])=1
for
q\in[q_{*},1]

\int_{q_{*}}^{1}\left(\zeta([0,q])\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(q))+\int_{q}^{1}\zeta([0,u])\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(u))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(u)du\right)\Psi_{x}^{\prime}(q)dq=\xi_{x}^{\prime}(1)(\Psi_{x}(1)-\Psi_{x}(q_{*})).

(3.23)

Thus combined we see that

\begin{split}\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\int_{0}^{1}\zeta^{\epsilon}([0,q])\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\Phi_{x}^{\epsilon}(q))(\Phi_{x}^{\epsilon})^{\prime}(q)dq=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\zeta([0,q])\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(q))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq\right)\Psi_{x}(0)\\
+\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\left(\zeta([0,q])\xi^{\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(q))+\int_{q}^{1}\zeta([0,u])\xi^{\prime\prime}_{x}(\Phi_{x}(u))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(u)du\right)\Psi_{x}^{\prime}(q)dq-\xi^{\prime}(1)\Psi_{x}(q_{*}).\end{split}

(3.24)

Now finally we contend with the external field term to get

\begin{split}\frac{d}{d\epsilon}[(D+\bm{K}^{D}(\delta^{\epsilon}(0)))^{-1}]_{xy}=\\
-\sum_{z\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{K}^{D}(\delta(0)))^{-1}]_{xz}[(D+\bm{K}^{D}(\delta(0)))^{-1}]_{zy}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\zeta([0,q])d\Psi_{z}(q)-\Psi_{z}(1)\right).\end{split}

(3.25)

Now we note that for
q\geq q_{*}
, we have that

\mathcal{F}_{y}(q)=K^{D}_{y}(\bm{\delta}(q))+\xi^{\prime}_{y}(1)+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\int_{0}^{q}\nabla_{y}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(q))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq\right)+\mathcal{J}_{y}.

(3.26)

Altogether, we obtain the desired result.
∎

To further make use of this computation, we will want the following result.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let us fix arbitrary
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
as above, and assume
q_{*}<1
is such that
\zeta([0,q_{*}))=1
and
\Phi_{x}(q_{*})<1
for all
x\in\Omega
. Then for any smooth compactly supported function
f:(0,q_{*})\to\mathbb{R}
and
y\in\Omega
, we have that

\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{F}_{y}(q)f(q)dq=-\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(q)\mathcal{G}_{y}(q)\zeta(dq).

(3.27)

Proof.

By integration by parts, we have that

\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(q)K^{D}_{y}(\bm{\delta}(q))dq=\int_{0}^{1}f(q)\zeta([0,q])\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\int_{0}^{q}\nabla_{y}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(u))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(u)du\right),

(3.28)

and that

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}&f^{\prime}(q)\zeta([0,q])\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\int_{0}^{q}\nabla_{y}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(u))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(u)du\right)dq\\
=&-\int_{0}^{1}f(q)\zeta([0,q])\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\int_{0}^{q}\nabla_{y}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(u))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(u)du\right)dq\\
&-\int_{0}^{1}f(q)\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\int_{0}^{q}\nabla_{y}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(u))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(u)du\right)\zeta(dq).\end{split}

(3.29)

Canceling a common term, we see that

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(q)\left(K^{D}_{y}(\bm{\delta}(q))+\zeta([0,q])\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\int_{0}^{q}\nabla_{y}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(u))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(u)du\right)\right)dq\\
&=-\int_{0}^{1}f(q)\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\int_{0}^{q}\nabla_{y}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(u))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(u)du\right)\zeta(dq).\end{split}

(3.30)

Similarly, we have that

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(q)\left(\xi^{\prime}_{y}(\Phi_{y}(q))+\zeta([0,q])\left(\int_{q}^{1}\zeta([0,u])\xi^{\prime\prime}_{y}(\Phi_{y}(u))\Phi_{y}^{\prime}(u)du\right)\right)dq\\
&=-\int_{0}^{1}f(q)\left(\int_{q}^{1}\zeta([0,u])\xi^{\prime\prime}_{y}(\Phi_{y}(u))\Phi_{y}^{\prime}(u)du\right)\zeta(dq).\end{split}

(3.31)

Combining these with the trivial observation

\mathcal{J}_{y}\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(q)\zeta([0,q])dq=-\mathcal{J}_{y}\int_{0}^{1}f(q)\zeta(dq),

(3.32)

completes the proof.
∎

Combining these lemmas, we will obtain a fairly tractable set of equations for any minimizers. However, as we have not even yet shown that any minimizer exists, we first consider the functional restricted to certain compact subsets of
\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}
. In particular, we define for
\epsilon\in(0,1)

\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{\epsilon}=\{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}:\Phi_{x}(\sup\text{supp}(\zeta))\leq 1-\epsilon\text{ for all }x\in\Omega\}.

(3.33)

We note that this set is non-zero and compact. In particular,
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{D,\bm{\xi},\bm{h}}
must possess a global minimizer over this set. The following result gives some useful identities for any such minimizer.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
be any local minimizer of
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{D,\bm{\xi},\bm{h}}
over
\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{\epsilon}
. Then for
y\in\Omega
, and any
q\in\text{supp}(\zeta)\cap(0,q_{*})
, we have
\mathcal{G}_{y}(q)=0
. Finally, if
0\in\text{supp}(\zeta)
, then
\mathcal{G}_{y}(0)\leq 0
and if
q_{*}\in\text{supp}(\zeta)
, then
\mathcal{G}_{y}(q_{*})\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let
f:[0,1]\to[0,1]
be a strictly increasing function such that
f(0)=0
and
f(q)=\Phi_{y}(q)
for
q\in[q_{*},1]
. Consider the Lemma
3.1
with respect to
\chi_{y}(q)=f(q)
and
\chi_{x}(q)=0
for
x\in\Omega\setminus\{y\}
. In this case,
\bm{\Phi}^{\epsilon}
lies in
\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{\epsilon}
for
\epsilon\in(-\delta,\delta)
with
\delta>0
small, so Lemma
3.1
yields that

\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\mathcal{F}_{y}(q)df(q)-\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\mathcal{F}_{y}(q)\Phi_{y}^{\prime}(q)dq=0.

(3.34)

As this holds for all choices of
f
, this implies that
\mathcal{F}_{y}
is constant a.e. on
[0,q_{*}]
.

Next, let
f
be a smooth function on
[0,q_{*}]
with
f(0)=f(q_{*})=0
. By continuity, we see that Lemma
3.2
yields that

\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\mathcal{G}_{y}(q)f^{\prime}(q)\zeta(dq)=-\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\mathcal{F}_{y}(q)f(q)dq=0,

(3.35)

where in the last equality we have used that
\mathcal{F}_{y}(q)
is constant a.e.
In particular, we see that
\zeta(\{q\in(0,q_{*}):\mathcal{G}_{y}(q)\neq 0\})=0
. As
\mathcal{G}_{y}
is continuous though, this implies that it vanishes on
\text{supp}(\zeta)\cap(0,q_{*})
.

Note as well that
\mathcal{F}_{y}
is clearly continuous away from the atoms of
\zeta
. As
\mathcal{G}_{y}
vanishes on
\text{supp}(\zeta)\cap(0,q_{*})
though, this implies that
\mathcal{F}_{y}
is continuous and thus constant on
(0,q_{*})
.

We now turn our attention to the final claims. We observe that taking
f:[0,1]\to[0,1]
to be a strictly increasing function such that
f(0)\geq 0
and
f(q)\leq\Phi_{x}(q)
for
q\in[q_{*},1]
, we may study the derivative as above. However, in this case, the family is only in the domain for
\epsilon\geq 0
, so we only obtain from Lemma
3.1
that

\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\mathcal{F}_{y}(q)f(q)dq-\mathcal{F}_{y}(q_{*})f(q_{*})+\mathcal{H}_{y}(0)f(0)\geq\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\mathcal{F}_{y}(q)\Phi_{y}^{\prime}(q)dq-\mathcal{F}_{y}(q_{*})\Phi_{y}(q_{*}).

(3.36)

If we let
\mathcal{F}_{y,t}
denote the value taken by
\mathcal{F}_{y}
on
(0,q_{*})
, we may write (
3.36
) as

(\mathcal{F}_{y,t}-\mathcal{F}_{y}(q_{*}))(f(q_{*})-\Phi_{y}(q_{*}))+(\mathcal{H}_{y}(0)-\mathcal{F}_{y,t})f(0)\geq 0.

(3.37)

We see from this that

\mathcal{F}_{y,t}\leq\mathcal{F}_{y}(q_{*})\text{ and }\mathcal{F}_{y,t}\leq\mathcal{H}_{y}(0).

(3.38)

We note however that

\mathcal{F}_{y,t}=\lim_{q\to 0}\mathcal{F}_{y}(q)=\mathcal{H}_{y}(0)+\zeta(\{0\})\mathcal{G}_{y}(0),

(3.39)

\mathcal{F}_{y,t}=\lim_{q\to q_{*}}\mathcal{F}_{y}(q)=\mathcal{F}_{y}(q_{*})-\zeta(\{q_{*}\})\mathcal{G}_{y}(q_{*}).

(3.40)

Thus if
\zeta(\{0\})\neq 0
, then
\mathcal{G}_{y}(0)\leq 0
as desired. However, if
\zeta(\{0\})=0
and
0\in\text{supp}(\zeta)
, we see that
0
is in the closure of
\text{supp}(\zeta)\cap(0,q_{*})
, and so
\mathcal{G}_{y}(0)=0
by continuity. In either case, if
0\in\text{supp}(\zeta)
, we see that
\mathcal{G}_{y}(0)\leq 0
. A similar argument establishes the claim for
\mathcal{G}_{y}(q_{*})
.
∎

We may use this result to guarantee the existence of a global minimizer for
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{D,\bm{\xi},\bm{h}}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

The functional
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{D,\bm{\xi},\bm{h}}
has at-least one minimizer over
\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}
. Moreover, there exists some small
c>0
, such that any such minimizer, say
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
, satisfies

\text{supp}(\bm{\Phi}_{*}(\zeta))\in[0,1-c]^{\Omega}.

(3.41)

Additionally, if
\xi^{\prime}_{x}(0)\neq 0
for each
x\in\Omega
, then for possibly smaller
c>0
, any minimizer
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
satisfies

\text{supp}(\bm{\Phi}_{*}(\zeta))\in[c,1-c]^{\Omega}.

(3.42)

Proof.

First two claims follow immediately by combining Lemma
3.3
and Lemma I.3.11.

For the remaining claims, fix a minimizing pair
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
. We define
q_{m}=\inf(\text{supp}(\zeta))
, and observe that
\bm{\delta}(q_{m})=\bm{\delta}(0)
. So we have that

\mathcal{G}_{y}(q_{m})=\xi^{\prime}_{y}(\Phi_{y}(q))+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\nabla_{x}K^{D}_{y}(\bm{\delta}(0))\Phi_{x}(q)+\mathcal{J}_{y}.

(3.43)

If
q_{m}=0
, our minimization conditions imply that
0\leq\mathcal{G}_{y}(0)=\xi^{\prime}_{y}(0)+\mathcal{J}_{y}
. However, by our assumptions, we have that
\xi^{\prime}_{y}(0)+\mathcal{J}_{y}>0
, which is a contradiction. Thus we must have
q_{m}>0
, and so
\mathcal{G}_{y}(q_{m})=0
for all
y\in\Omega
. Using the formula for
\nabla_{x}K_{y}^{D}
, we may rewrite this system of equations as

\Phi_{x}(q_{m})=\sum_{y\in\Omega}[(D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{\delta}(0)))^{-1}]_{xy}^{2}(\xi_{y}^{\prime}(\Phi_{y}(q_{m}))+\mathcal{J}_{y}),

(3.44)

We note that the sum on the right is bounded below by

[(D+\bm{K}^{D}(\bm{\delta}(0)))^{-1}]_{xx}^{2}(\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\Phi_{x}(q_{m}))+\mathcal{J}_{x})=\delta_{x}(0)^{2}(\xi_{x}^{\prime}(\Phi_{x}(q_{m}))+\mathcal{J}_{x})\geq\delta_{x}(0)^{2}\xi^{\prime}_{x}(0).

(3.45)

By the above work we have that
\delta_{x}(0)\geq 1-\Phi_{x}(q_{M})\geq c
, so we see that for each
x\in\Omega

\Phi_{x}(q_{m})\geq\xi^{\prime}_{x}(0)c.

(3.46)

So by shrinking
c
by an amount only dependent on
\min_{x\in\Omega}\xi_{x}^{\prime}(0)>0
, we obtain the second claim.
∎

4
The Euclidean Model and Concavity

We now focus our attention on the Euclidean model. The goal of this section will be to show a certain function is concave with a unique minimizer, but to begin we will need to recall some more relevant results from our companion paper
[
16
]
. We also return from the above case of a general finite set
\Omega
to specifically the case
\Omega=[[1,L]]^{d}
and
D=-t\Delta
. However, we note that all of our results hold in the setting of the previous sections.

To start, we will need a functional. Begin by fixing a choice
\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
and define
q_{t}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{q}(x)
. Next we take a measure
\zeta\in\mathscr{Y}(q_{t})
. Then, for each each
x\in\Omega
, we choose a continuous, coordinate-wise non-decreasing function
\Phi_{x}:[0,q_{t}]\to[0,\bm{q}(x)]
, which much be such that for each
s\in[0,q_{t}]
we have that

\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{q}(x)^{-1}\Phi_{x}(s)=q_{t}^{-1}s.

(4.1)

We will assume as well that there is
0<q_{*}<q_{t}
is such that
\zeta([0,q_{*}))=0
and
\Phi_{x}(q_{*})<\bm{q}(x)
for each
x\in\Omega
. We will denote the set of pairs
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
satisfying these conditions as
\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q})
. We observe that when
\bm{q}(x)=1
for all
x\in\Omega
, this coincides with the
\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q})
considered above.

Given these choices we define the continuous coordinate-wise non-increasing function
\bm{\delta}:[0,q_{t}]\to[0,\infty)^{\Omega}
by

\delta_{x}(s)=\int_{s}^{q_{t}}\zeta([0,u])\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(u)du.

(4.2)

With all these choices made, we define the functional

\begin{split}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\log\left(2\pi\right)+\Lambda^{-t\Delta}&(\bm{\delta}(q_{*}))+\frac{1}{L^{d}}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bigg(-\mu\bm{q}(x)+\int_{0}^{q_{*}}K_{x}^{-t\Delta}(\bm{\delta}(q))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq\\
&-2\int_{0}^{q_{t}}\zeta([0,u])B^{\prime}(2(\bm{q}(x)-\Phi_{x}(u)))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(u)du\bigg)\bigg).\end{split}

(4.3)

We showed in
[
16
]
that this paper may be used to calculate the limiting free energy.

Theorem 4.1

(Theorem I.1.3)
.

We have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}L^{-d}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}=\sup_{\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}}\left(\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q})}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\right).

(4.4)

The main result of this section will be to show that map

\bm{q}\mapsto\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q})}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})

(4.5)

is concave, and has a unique maximizer. By symmetry, this maximizer must be a constant vector. However, first we need more preliminary results which will make out work on the spherical model useful here. As may be clear from the similarities between their forms, this functional can be quite simply related to a spherical case of the spherical one studied above.

Indeed, for
(\zeta^{\bm{q}},\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{q}})
in
\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q})
, we may define
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}
by
\Phi_{x}(s)=\bm{q}(x)\Phi_{x}^{\bm{q}}(q_{t}^{-1}s)
and
\zeta^{\bm{q}}([0,s])=\zeta([0,q_{t}^{-1}s])
. This mapping gives a bijection between
\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q})
and
\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}
.

Next, define a matrix
D_{\bm{q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega\times\Omega}
such that
[D_{\bm{q}}]_{xy}=\sqrt{\bm{q}(x)\bm{q}(y)}t\Delta_{xy}
and let
\bm{h}=0
. Further, let us define the mixing function
B_{q}(r)=B(2q(1-r))
, and form the family
\bm{B}_{\bm{q}}=(B_{\bm{q}(x)})_{x\in\Omega}
.
Then, following Lemma I.2.3, we have the relation

\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\frac{h^{2}}{2\mu^{2}}+\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(-\mu\bm{q}(x)+\log(\bm{q}(x))\right)+\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{D_{\bm{q}},\bm{B}_{\bm{q}},\bm{0}}(\zeta^{\bm{q}},\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{q}}).

(4.6)

This correspondence will allow us to translate results from
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}
to
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}
.

We note that the external field term does not appear in the functional, only as a constant term. That is, we may effectively view the associated spherical model as if it doesn’t have an external field. The reason for this is due to a change of variables which one may make in the case of the Euclidean model, which renders the external field fairly inert. In particular, we have the following remark, which follows from Lemma I.2.1.

Remark 4.2
.

If we include for the moment the choice of
h
, in the notation
Z_{N}(h)
, for the partition function, we have that

N^{-1}|\Omega|^{-1}\log Z_{N}(h)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}N^{-1}|\Omega|^{-1}\log Z_{N}(0)+\frac{h^{2}}{2\mu}.

(4.7)

We now begin translating the above results for spherical models to this functional. First, by noting that
B_{q}^{\prime}(0)=-2qB^{\prime}(2q)>0
we see that Lemma
3.4
yields the following.

Corollary 4.3
.

For each
\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
, the functional
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}
possesses a minimizer on
\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q})
.

Moreover, for any
\bm{q}_{0}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
, one may find a small
\epsilon,\delta>0
so that if one takes
\bm{q}\in\prod_{x\in\Omega}[\bm{q}_{0}(x)-\delta,\bm{q}_{0}(x)+\delta]
, and any minimizer
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}
over
\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q})
, then
\text{supp}(\bm{\Phi}_{*}(\zeta))\subseteq[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]^{\Omega}
.

For the next corollary, we need to introduce following variant of the family of functions

defined above: For

and

\mathcal{G}_{y}^{\bm{q}}(s)=-2B^{\prime}(2(\bm{q}(y)-\Phi_{y}(s)))+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\nabla_{y}K^{-t\Delta}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(u))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(u)du\right).

(4.8)

Then we obtain the following from Lemma
3.3
and Corollary
4.3

Corollary 4.4
.

For each
\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
, and any minimizer

of

Finally, we collect the following result from Theorems

Corollary 4.5

If one chooses

and forms the constant vector

\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q})}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\inf_{\zeta\in\mathscr{Y}(q)}\mathcal{P}_{q}(\zeta).

(4.9)

Moreover, the right-hand side is strictly convex in
\zeta

given by the unique solution the equations

\zeta\left(\big\{s\in[0,q]:f_{q}(s)=\sup_{0<s^{\prime}<q}f_{q}(s^{\prime})\big\}\right)=1,

(4.10)

where for

f_{q}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}F_{q}(u)du,\;\;\;\;F_{\beta,q}(s)=-2B^{\prime}(2(q-s))+\int_{0}^{s}K^{\prime}(\delta(u);t)du,

(4.11)

where

1.10
).

With all of these results collected, we define the function

\widehat{\mathscr{P}}(\bm{q})=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q}_{0})}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}).

(4.12)

We may now state the main result of this section.

(4.13)

where

.

Before discussing the proof of this, we observe the following corollary of Corollary

Corollary 4.7

For fixed

We now begin preparing for the proof of Proposition
4.6
, for which the first major step will be the following.

Lemma 4.8
.

The function
\bm{q}\mapsto\widehat{\mathscr{P}}(\bm{q})
is concave in
\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
.

As we know there is at least one maximizer of
\widehat{\mathscr{P}}(\bm{q})
(see Corollary I.2.9), we note that if Lemma
4.8
established instead strict convexity, then Proposition
4.6
would follow immediately. Instead, we show this uniqueness after establishing Lemma
4.8
, analyze the behavior of
\widehat{\mathscr{P}}(\bm{q})
around a symmetric maximizer.

We now proceed to the proof of Lemma
4.8
. To do this, we will show that, for fixed
\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
, there are minimizers of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}
which lie in a certain subset of the domain. We then show that for a small neighborhood of
\bm{q}^{\prime}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
around
\bm{q}
, we may naturally identify this subset of the domain with subsets of the domain of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}^{\prime}}
, which both contain in the minimizer, and are such that
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}^{\prime}}
becomes a linear function in
\bm{q}^{\prime}
. Thus locally, we may view
\widehat{\mathscr{P}}(\bm{q})
as the infimum over a fixed set of linear functions of
\bm{q}
, which obviously implies concavity.

In the case where
\bm{\Phi}=\bm{I}
, these restrictions and mapping are quite simple. For
\epsilon>0
, we relate a measure
\zeta
on
[0,q]
to one
\zeta^{\prime}
on
[0,q+\epsilon]
by letting
\zeta^{\prime}([0,s+\epsilon])=\zeta([0,s])
for
s\in[0,q]
and
\zeta^{\prime}([0,\epsilon])=0
. Of course, we may also go the other direction, and associate a measure
[0,q+\epsilon]
to one on
[0,q]
as long as the support of this measure is disjoint from
[0,\epsilon]
. Under these mappings, one may show that
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}
is linear in
\epsilon
.

Unfortunately, a number of issues make this picture more complicated for general
\bm{\Phi}
. We begin by defining mappings between the domains of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}^{\prime}}
. To begin, fix
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q})
. Given an additional choice of
\bm{r}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
we define an injection
\varphi_{\bm{q},\bm{q}+\bm{r}}:\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q})\to\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q}+\bm{r})
. Intuitively, we should like to push the existing measure data from
\prod_{x\in\Omega}[0,\bm{q}(x)]
to
\prod_{x\in\Omega}[\bm{r}(x),\bm{q}(x)+\bm{r}(x)]
, by translation, filling the remaining portions with zero measure. That is, letting
r_{t}=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{r}(x)
, we would like to define

\tilde{\Phi}_{x}(s)=\Phi_{x}(s-r_{t})+\bm{r}(x)\text{ for }s\in[r_{t},q_{t}+r_{t}],\;\;\tilde{\Phi}(s)=s\left(\frac{\bm{r}(x)}{r_{t}}\right)\text{ for }s\in[0,r_{t}],

(4.17)

\tilde{\zeta}([0,s])=\zeta([0,s-r_{t}])\text{ for }s\in[r_{t},q_{t}+r_{t}],\;\;\tilde{\zeta}([0,r_{t}))=0.

(4.18)

Unfortunately this choice need not preserve (
4.1
). To rectify this, we define a function
\alpha:[0,q_{t}+r_{t}]\to[0,q_{t}+r_{t}]
by

\alpha(s)=\frac{q_{t}+r_{t}}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}(\bm{q}(x)+\bm{r}(x))^{-1}\tilde{\Phi}_{x}(s).

(4.19)

This clearly defines a monotone increasing bijection, and we see that
\bm{\Phi}^{\prime}=\tilde{\bm{\Phi}}\circ\alpha^{-1}
satisfies (
4.1
) by definition. Similarly, we define
\zeta^{\prime}
by letting
\zeta^{\prime}([0,s])=\tilde{\zeta}([0,\alpha^{-1}(s)])
. Finally we define
\varphi_{\bm{q},\bm{q}+\bm{r}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=(\zeta^{\prime},\bm{\Phi}^{\prime})
.

Our next step will relate
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}+\bm{r}}(\varphi_{\bm{q},\bm{q}+\bm{r}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}))
and
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
. We will also use
\bm{\delta}
and
\bm{\delta}^{\prime}
to denote the functions respective to
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
and
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}^{\prime}}(\varphi_{\bm{q},\bm{q}+\bm{r}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}))
. We first note that

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{q_{t}+r_{t}}\zeta^{\prime}([0,s])B^{\prime}\left(2(\bm{q}(x)+\bm{r}(x)-\Phi_{x}^{\prime}(s))\right)(\Phi_{x}^{\prime})^{\prime}(s)ds=\\
&\int_{0}^{q_{t}+r_{t}}\tilde{\zeta}([0,s])B^{\prime}\left(2(\bm{q}(x)+\bm{r}(x)-\tilde{\Phi}_{x}(s))\right)(\tilde{\Phi}_{x})^{\prime}(s)ds=\\
&\int_{0}^{q_{t}}\zeta([0,s])B^{\prime}\left(2(\bm{q}(x)-\Phi_{x}(s))\right)\Phi_{x}^{\prime}(s)ds.\end{split}

(4.20)

We similarly observe that for
s>r_{t}

\delta^{\prime}_{x}(\alpha(s))=\int_{s}^{q_{t}+r_{t}}\tilde{\zeta}([0,s])(\tilde{\Phi}_{x})^{\prime}(s)ds=\delta_{x}(s-r_{t}),

(4.21)

while for
s\leq r_{t}
we have that
\delta^{\prime}_{x}(\alpha(s))=\delta_{x}(0)
. Noting that if
\zeta([0,q_{*}))=1
and
\Phi_{x}(q_{*})<\bm{q}(x)
for each
x\in\Omega
, then we have that
\zeta^{\prime}([0,\alpha(q_{*}+r_{t})))=1
and
\Phi_{x}^{\prime}(\alpha(q_{*}+r_{t}))<\bm{q}(x)+\bm{r}(x)
. So using this point, we see that

\int_{0}^{\alpha(q_{*}+r_{t})}K_{x}^{-t\Delta}(\bm{\delta}^{\prime}(q))(\Phi_{x}^{\prime})^{\prime}(q)dq=\int_{0}^{\alpha(r_{t})}K_{x}^{-t\Delta}(\bm{\delta}(0))(\Phi_{x}^{\prime})^{\prime}(q)dq+\int_{r_{t}}^{q_{*}+r_{t}}K_{x}^{-t\Delta}(\tilde{\bm{\delta}}(q))\tilde{\Phi}_{x}^{\prime}(q)dq=

(4.22)

K_{x}^{-t\Delta}(\bm{\delta}(0))\tilde{\Phi}_{x}(r_{t})+\int_{r_{t}}^{q_{*}+r_{t}}K_{x}^{-t\Delta}(\tilde{\bm{\delta}}(q))\tilde{\Phi}_{x}^{\prime}(q)dq=K_{x}^{-t\Delta}(\bm{\delta}(0))\bm{r}(x)+\int_{0}^{q_{*}}K_{x}^{-t\Delta}(\bm{\delta}(q))\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(q)dq.

(4.23)

Noting similarly that
\Lambda^{-t\Delta}(\delta^{\prime}(\alpha(q_{*}+r_{t}))))=\Lambda^{-t\Delta}(\delta(q_{t}))
, we see that

\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}+\bm{r}}(\varphi_{\bm{q},\bm{q}+\bm{r}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}))=\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})+\frac{1}{2L^{d}}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{r}(x)K^{-t\Delta}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(0)).

(4.24)

In particular, fixing
\bm{q}
, the function
\bm{r}\mapsto\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}+\bm{r}}(\varphi_{\bm{q},\bm{q}+\bm{r}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}))
is linear.

Recalling that the pointwise infimum of a family of concave functions is concave, as well as the fact that concavity is a local property, we see that Lemma
4.8
follows if we show the following.

Lemma 4.9
.

Fix
\bm{q}^{*}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
. Then there is some
\bm{q}_{0}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
and
\epsilon>0
, with
\bm{q}^{*}\in\prod_{x\in\Omega}(\bm{q}_{0}(x),\bm{q}_{0}(x)+\epsilon)
, and such that for all
\bm{q}\in\prod_{x\in\Omega}[\bm{q}_{0}(x),\bm{q}_{0}(x)+\epsilon]

\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q})}\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\inf_{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q}_{0})}\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}_{0}}(\varphi_{\bm{q}_{0},\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})).

(4.25)

To do this we will study the image of
\varphi_{\bm{q}_{0},\bm{q}}
by roughly defining a partial inverse function. However this will not be a true partial inverse. Instead it will be up to an equivalence relation that respects the values of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
, which we now define.

Definition 4.10
.

Fix
\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}
, and a probability measure
\zeta
on
[0,q_{t}]
. We will say two functions
\bm{\Phi},\bm{\Phi}^{\prime}:[0,q_{t}]\to\prod_{x\in\Omega}[0,\bm{q}(x)]
are
\zeta
-equivalent if
\bm{\Phi}(s)=\bm{\Phi}^{\prime}(s)
for

. We will say pairs
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
and
(\zeta^{\prime},\bm{\Phi})
are
\mathcal{P}
-equivalent if
\zeta=\zeta^{\prime}
and
\bm{\Phi}
is
\zeta
-equivalent to
\bm{\Phi}^{\prime}
.

With this definition, we see that (
4.6
) and Lemma
2.8
above implies that if
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
and
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}^{\prime})
are
\mathcal{P}
-equivalent then
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}^{\prime})
.

We will now define our partial inverse
\varphi_{\bm{q},\bm{q}+\bm{r}}
, up to this equivalence relation, which we will denote
\hat{\varphi}_{\bm{q}+\bm{r},\bm{q}}
, with domain given by the subset
\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q}+\bm{r})
defined by

\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{\bm{r}}(\bm{q})=\{(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}(\bm{q}+\bm{r}):\bm{\Phi}(r_{t})=\bm{r},\;\;\zeta([0,r_{t}))=0\}.

(4.26)

We define

\hat{\Phi}_{x}(s)=\Phi_{x}(s+r_{t})-\bm{r}(x)\text{ and }\hat{\zeta}([0,s])=\zeta([0,s+r_{t}])\text{ for }s\in[0,q_{t}].

(4.27)

We further define
\beta:[0,q_{t}]\to[0,q_{t}]
by

\beta(s)=\frac{q_{t}}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{q}(x)^{-1}\hat{\Phi}_{x}(s).

(4.28)

Then letting
\bm{\Phi}^{\prime\prime}=\hat{\bm{\Phi}}\circ\beta^{-1}
and
\zeta^{\prime\prime}([0,s])=\hat{\zeta}([0,\beta^{-1}(s)])
, we define
\hat{\varphi}_{{\bm{q}+\bm{r},\bm{q}}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=(\zeta^{\prime\prime},\bm{\Phi}^{\prime\prime})
.

Next, let us denote by
\bm{\delta}^{\prime\prime}
and
\bm{\delta}
as the functions corresponding to
(\zeta^{\prime\prime},\bm{\Phi}^{\prime\prime})
and
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
, respectively. Then as
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})\in\mathscr{Y}_{\bm{r}}(\bm{q})
we see for

that
\bm{\delta}(s)=\bm{\delta}(r_{t})
and for

and

\delta^{\prime\prime}_{x}(\beta(s))=\delta_{x}(s+r_{t}).

(4.29)

From this, it is easy to check, as in (

\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\hat{\varphi}_{\bm{q}+\bm{r},\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}))=\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})-\frac{1}{2|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{r}(x)K^{-t\Delta}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(0)).

(4.30)

We now verify that
\varphi_{\bm{q},\bm{q}+\bm{r}}\circ\hat{\varphi}_{\bm{q}+\bm{r},\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi}):=(\zeta^{0},\bm{\Phi}^{0})
is
\mathcal{P}
-equivalent to
(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})
, so that in particular

\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}+\bm{r}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})=\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}+\bm{r}}(\varphi_{\bm{q},\bm{q}+\bm{r}}\circ\hat{\varphi}_{\bm{q}+\bm{r},\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})).

(4.31)

If we denote
\gamma(s)=\beta^{-1}(\alpha^{-1}(s)-r_{t})+r_{t}
, then it is clear that for

, we have
\bm{\Phi}^{0}(s)=\bm{\Phi}(\gamma(s))
. Note that as both
\bm{\Phi}^{0}
and
\bm{\Phi}
satisfy (
4.1
), we see that in fact
\gamma(s)=s
for

, so that
\bm{\Phi}^{0}(s)=\bm{\Phi}(s)
on this domain. We also see that
\zeta^{0}([0,s])=\zeta([0,\gamma(s)])=\zeta([0,s])
, and as
\zeta([0,r_{t}))=0
, we conclude that
\zeta^{\prime\prime}=\zeta
. Combining this with the previous result, that
\bm{\Phi}^{0}(s)=\bm{\Phi}(s)
for

, we see that
\bm{\Phi}^{0}
and
\bm{\Phi}
are
\zeta
-equivalent, giving the desired claim.

The next result we will need is a similar composition law. Take
\bm{r},\bm{r^{\prime}}\in[0,\infty)^{\Omega}

\varphi_{\bm{q},\bm{q}+\bm{r}+\bm{r}^{\prime}}(\zeta,\bm{\Phi})

With the proof of Lemma

The main idea starts by noting that the set of maximizers of
\widehat{\mathscr{P}}(\bm{q})
is non-empty and convex. Moreover by transitivity of

(see Definition

) we may also find some

\bm{q}^{*}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}

. We then apply the functions
\varphi
and

to move the minimizing pair of

to another maximizer

. We show that maximization condition on
\bm{q}^{*}
implies that the value of the functional will not change after we do this. However, this shows that the pushed measure is infact a minimizer of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}^{\prime}}
. Thus both satisfy the condition of Corollary

at these different points, which when combined with Corollary

, we show is impossible.

.

(\zeta^{\bm{\epsilon}},\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{\epsilon}})=(\widetilde{\zeta}^{\bm{\epsilon}}\circ\alpha_{\epsilon}^{-1},\widetilde{\bm{\Phi}}^{\bm{\epsilon}}\circ\alpha_{\epsilon}^{-1}).

(4.39)

Letting
\bm{\delta}^{\bm{\epsilon}}
and
\bm{\delta}

and

in the respective definitions

(4.40)

Now noting that

and
\widetilde{\zeta}^{\bm{\epsilon}}([0,\epsilon_{*}+\epsilon_{t}))=0

and

(4.41)

In particular by (

) and (

) we see that

\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}^{*}+\bm{\epsilon}}(\zeta^{\bm{\epsilon}},\bm{\Phi}^{\bm{\epsilon}})=\mathcal{P}_{q^{*}}(\zeta)+\frac{1}{2|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{\epsilon}(x)\left(K_{x}^{-t\Delta}(\bm{\delta}(0))-\mu\right).

(4.42)

Now with these preliminaries aside, fix
q^{*}
to be such that
\bm{q}^{*}
maximizes
\widehat{\mathscr{P}}
. Fixing
\zeta
as well to be the measure which minimizes
\mathcal{P}_{q^{*}}
, we note that by Theorem
2.4
we have that
(\zeta,\bm{I})
is a maximizer of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}^{*}}
with

\mathcal{P}_{q^{*}}(\zeta)=\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}}(\zeta,\bm{I}).

(4.43)

In particular, by Corollary
4.3
, we have that
0\notin\text{supp}(\zeta)
. Thus we see by (
4.42
) that for
\bm{q}^{*}
to indeed maximize
\mathcal{Q}
, we must have that
K^{-t\Delta}(\delta(0))=\mu
, so that
\zeta
satisfies

\delta(0)=\int_{0}^{q^{*}}\zeta([0,u])du=R_{1}(\mu;t).

(4.44)

Now let us assume that the set of maximizers of
\widehat{\mathscr{P}}
contains something other than
\bm{q}^{*}
. As this set is convex, we may find some
\bm{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
such that
\bm{q}^{*}+t\bm{r}
maximizes
\bm{Q}
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Let
r_{*}=-\min_{x\in\Omega}\bm{r}(x)
. As
0\notin\text{supp}(\zeta)
, we may find small
\epsilon_{0}>0
so that
\zeta([0,\epsilon_{0}r_{*}])=0
. Thus we may apply the above computations with
\bm{\epsilon}=\epsilon_{0}\bm{r}
to get that

\widehat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}^{*}+\epsilon}(\zeta^{\epsilon},\bm{\Phi}^{\epsilon})=\mathcal{P}_{q^{*}}(\zeta).

(4.45)

However, as
\mathcal{P}_{q^{*}}(\zeta)=\widehat{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{q}^{*})=\widehat{\mathcal{P}}(\bm{q}^{*}+\bm{\epsilon})
, as they both maximizers of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
, we see that
(\zeta^{\epsilon},\bm{\Phi}^{\epsilon})
is a minimizer of
\hat{\mathcal{P}}_{\bm{q}^{*}+\epsilon}
. Thus by Corollary
4.4
we have that
\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{\bm{q}^{*}+\bm{\epsilon}}(u)=\bm{0}
for
u\in\text{supp}(\zeta^{\epsilon})
. Employing the above equations, we see equivalently that

\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{\bm{q}^{*}+\bm{\epsilon}}(\alpha_{\epsilon}(u+\epsilon_{t}))=\bm{0}\text{ for }u\in\text{supp}(\zeta).

(4.46)

To study this equation, note that the function
\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{\bm{q}^{*}}
associated to
(\zeta,\bm{I})
are all copies of some fixed function
\mathcal{G}^{q^{*}}
by symmetry. Using Corollary
4.4
again we see that for
u\in\text{supp}(\zeta)

\mathcal{G}^{q^{*}}(u)=0
. Now we compute
\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{\bm{\epsilon}}(\alpha_{\epsilon}(u+\epsilon_{t}))
. For any
y\in\Omega
and
u\in[\epsilon_{*},q^{*}]
we have

\mathcal{G}_{y}^{\bm{\epsilon}}(\alpha_{\epsilon}(u+\epsilon_{t}))=-2B^{\prime}(2(q^{*}+\epsilon(x)-\widetilde{\Phi}^{\epsilon}_{x}(u+\epsilon_{t})))+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\int_{0}^{u+\epsilon_{t}}\nabla_{y}K^{-t\Delta}_{x}(\bm{\delta}^{\epsilon}(\alpha^{\epsilon}(s))d\widetilde{\Phi}_{x}^{\epsilon}(ds)\right)=

(4.47)

-2B(2(q^{*}-u))+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(\int_{\epsilon_{*}}^{u}\nabla_{y}K^{-t\Delta}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(s))ds+(\epsilon_{t}+\epsilon_{*})\left(\frac{\bm{\epsilon}(x)+\epsilon_{*}}{\epsilon_{t}+\epsilon_{*}}\right)\nabla_{y}K^{-t\Delta}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(0))\right)=

(4.48)

\mathcal{G}(u)+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\left(-\epsilon_{*}\nabla_{y}K^{-t\Delta}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(0))+(\bm{\epsilon}(x)+\epsilon_{*})\nabla_{y}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(0))\right)=

(4.49)

\mathcal{G}(u)+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{\epsilon}(x)\nabla_{y}K^{-t\Delta}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(0)).

(4.50)

Now if
u\in\text{supp}(\zeta)
we have that
\mathcal{G}_{y}^{t}(\alpha_{\epsilon}(u+\epsilon_{t}))=\mathcal{G}(u)=0
. In particular, we have that for all
y\in\Omega

\sum_{x\in\Omega}\bm{\epsilon}(x)\nabla_{y}K^{-t\Delta}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(0))=0.

(4.51)

Now recall that

\nabla_{y}K^{D}_{x}(\bm{\delta}(0))=-[(\delta(0)I-t\Delta)^{-1}]_{xy}^{2}.

(4.52)

As
\delta(0)I-t\Delta
is positive definite, the matrix
-((\delta(0)I-t\Delta)^{-1})^{2\odot}
is negative definite by the Schur Product Theorem, and in particular, this matrix is invertible. Thus as
\bm{\epsilon}\neq\bm{0}
the equation (
4.51
) yields a contradiction. Thus we have shown that the minimizer
\bm{q}^{*}
is unique.

All that is left is to show that
q^{*}
is the only value of
q\in(0,\infty)
for which the minimizing measure
\zeta_{q}
satisfies (
4.44
). To do so, choose another such value
q^{\prime}
. Note by the work proceeding (
4.44
) (in particular (
4.42
)) this would imply that
\bm{q}^{\prime}
is in fact a local maximum of
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
. By convexity this means it would be a global maximum, but as we have just shown this is unique, we must have that
q^{\prime}=q^{*}
.
∎

In this section, we will prove Theorem

Remark 5.1
.

For the moment, let us denote the dependence of the Gibbs measure associated to
\mathcal{H}_{N}
by writing
\langle*\rangle_{h}

\bm{e}_{1}\in(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Omega}

\langle f(\bm{u})\rangle_{h}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\left\langle f\left(\bm{u}-\sqrt{N}\frac{h}{\mu}\bm{e}_{1}\right)\right\rangle_{0},

(5.1)

and moreover this distributional equality holds jointly over all choices of

This illustrates that the strange behavior in Theorem

in the case of

comes from working with
\bm{u}
and not
\bm{u}-\sqrt{N}\frac{h}{\mu}\bm{e}_{1}

1.6
in the case of

Proposition 5.2
.

Assume that
h=0
, and let
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
be the pair from Corollary
4.7
. Then for any
x\in\Omega
and any continuous

of sub-exponential growth, we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left\langle f(\|\bm{u}(x)\|^{2}_{N})\right\rangle=f(q_{c}),

(5.2)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left\langle f((\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}(x))_{N})\right\rangle=\int_{0}^{q_{c}}f(r)\zeta_{c}(dr).

(5.3)

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

From Remark
5.1
this is equivalent to showing, in the case that
h=0
, that we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left\langle\left|\left\|\bm{u}-\sqrt{N}\frac{h}{\mu}\bm{e}_{1}\right\|^{2}_{N}-\left(q_{c}+\frac{h^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\right|\right\rangle=0,

(5.4)

and that for any bounded continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left\langle f\left(\left(\bm{u}-\sqrt{N}\frac{h}{\mu}\bm{e}_{1},\bm{u}^{\prime}-\sqrt{N}\frac{h}{\mu}\bm{e}_{1}\right)_{N}\right)\right\rangle=\int_{0}^{q}f\left(r+\frac{h^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right)\zeta_{c}(dr).

(5.5)

We note that

\left\|\bm{u}(x)-\sqrt{N}\frac{h}{\mu}e_{1}\right\|^{2}_{N}=\|\bm{u}(x)\|^{2}+\frac{h^{2}}{\mu^{2}}+\frac{2h}{\mu}\left(\frac{\bm{u}_{1}(x)}{\sqrt{N}}\right),

(5.6)

and that

\left(\bm{u}-\sqrt{N}\frac{h}{\mu}\bm{e}_{1},\bm{u}^{\prime}-\sqrt{N}\frac{h}{\mu}\bm{e}_{1}\right)_{N}=(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}(x))_{N}+\frac{h^{2}}{\mu^{2}}+\frac{h}{\mu}\left(\frac{\bm{u}_{1}(x)}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\bm{u}_{1}^{\prime}(x)}{\sqrt{N}}\right).

(5.7)

By continuity of
f
, both of these results would follow if we prove that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left\langle\left|\left(\frac{\bm{u}_{1}(x)}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\right|^{2}\right\rangle=0.

(5.8)

However note that, for each fixed
x\in\Omega
, the marginal measure of
\mathbb{E}\langle*\rangle
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is isotropic, as the underlying Hamiltonian is when
h=0
. Using this and Proposition

, we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left\langle(\bm{u}_{1}(x))^{2}\right\rangle=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left\langle\|\bm{u}_{1}(x)\|^{2}_{N}\right\rangle=q_{c},

(5.9)

completing the proof.
∎

We now move onto the proof of Proposition
5.2
, which will compose the remainder of this section. Thus for the remainder of the section we will always assume that we are in the case of
h=0
. We will begin by setting up for the proof of (
5.2
). For this it is useful to establish a variety of notation. First, for any subset
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
, we will denote by

Z_{N}(A)=\int_{A}e^{-\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{u})}d\bm{u},\;\;\;\;\langle f(\bm{u})\rangle_{A}=\frac{1}{Z_{N}(A)}\int_{A}f(\bm{u})e^{-\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{u})}d\bm{u},

(5.10)

the partition function and Gibbs measure of
\mathcal{H}_{N}
on the subset
A
.

Next, for
q\in(0,\infty)
, let us define the annulus

D_{N}^{\epsilon}(q)=\{\bm{u}\in(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Omega}:|\|\bm{u}\|^{2}_{N}-q|\leq\epsilon\}.

(5.11)

The free energy for restrictions of this type were also computed in
[
16
]
. In particular, by Corollary I.2.8 we for
\epsilon\in(0,q_{c})
that

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}|\Omega|^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}(D_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q}_{c}))=\sup_{\bm{q}\in(q_{c}-\epsilon,q_{c}+\epsilon)^{\Omega}}\widehat{\mathscr{P}}(\bm{q}),

(5.12)

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{-1}|\Omega|^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}\left(D_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q}_{c})^{c}\right)=\sup_{\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}\setminus(q_{c}-\epsilon,q_{c}+\epsilon)^{\Omega}}\widehat{\mathscr{P}}(\bm{q}).

(5.13)

By Proposition
4.6
we see that

\sup_{\bm{q}\in(q_{c}-\epsilon,q_{c}+\epsilon)^{\Omega}}\widehat{\mathscr{P}}(\bm{q})=\widehat{\mathscr{P}}(\bm{q}_{c}),

(5.14)

and if we define

\widehat{\mathscr{P}}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q}_{c}):=\sup_{\bm{q}\in(0,\infty)^{\Omega}\setminus(q_{c}-\epsilon,q_{c}+\epsilon)^{\Omega}}\widehat{\mathscr{P}}(\bm{q}),

(5.15)

that we also have that

\widehat{\mathscr{P}}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q}_{c})<\widehat{\mathscr{P}}(\bm{q}_{c}).

(5.16)

From this and some concentration results, we will obtain the following useful estimate.

Lemma 5.3
.

For
\epsilon>0
define

G_{N}(\epsilon):=\left\langle I\left(\|\bm{u}\|_{N}^{2}\notin D_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q}_{c})\right)\right\rangle.

(5.17)

Then there is a continuous function
l(\epsilon)>0
such that for sufficiently large
N
(possibly dependent on
\epsilon
)

\mathbb{P}\left(G_{N}(\epsilon)\geq\exp(-Nl(\epsilon))\right)\leq 2\exp(-Nl(\epsilon)).

(5.18)

In particular, for sufficiently large
N
, we have that
\mathbb{E}G_{N}(\epsilon)\leq 3\exp(-Nl(\epsilon))
.

Proof.

The key is to note that

G_{N}(\epsilon)=\frac{Z_{N}(D_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q}_{c})^{c})}{Z_{N}},

(5.19)

and then employ a number of concentration results to use the above results. For this, let us define
\gamma(\epsilon):=\widehat{\mathscr{P}}(\bm{q}_{c})-\widehat{\mathscr{P}}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q}_{c})>0
. Convexity of
\widehat{\mathscr{P}}
implies that it, and thus
\gamma(\epsilon)
is continuous. Now we define the events

\mathcal{E}_{1}(\epsilon)=\{||\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\log Z_{N}(D_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q}_{c})^{c})-|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}(D_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q}_{c})^{c})|>\gamma(\epsilon)/4\},

(5.20)

\mathcal{E}_{2}(\epsilon)=\{||\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\log Z_{N}-|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}|>\gamma(\epsilon)/4\}.

(5.21)

Then by Corollary I.A.3, we have for

that

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}_{i}(\epsilon)\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{N|\Omega|\gamma(\epsilon)^{2}}{64B(0)}\right).

(5.22)

Conditional on

we have that

|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\log G_{N}(\epsilon)\leq\frac{\gamma(\epsilon)}{2}+|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\log\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}(D_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q}_{c})^{c})}{\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}}\right).

(5.23)

But by the above computations we have that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\left(\frac{\gamma(\epsilon)}{2}+|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\log\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}(D_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q}_{c})^{c})}{\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N}}\right)\right)=-\frac{\gamma(\epsilon)}{4}.

(5.24)

Thus we obtain the desired result by taking

\delta(\epsilon)=\min\left(\frac{\gamma(\epsilon)}{4},\frac{N|\Omega|\gamma(\epsilon)^{2}}{64B(0)}\right).

(5.25)

∎

\mathbb{E}\langle I(\|\bm{u}(x)\|^{2}_{N}\geq r)\rangle\leq\mathbb{E}\langle I(\bm{u}\in A^{N}_{max}(\sqrt{r}))\rangle=\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{N}(A^{N}_{max}(\sqrt{r}))/Z_{N}\right].

By Remark I.2.7 there is some small

and large enough

\mathbb{E}Z_{N}(A^{N}_{max}(\sqrt{r}))\leq e^{-Ncr}.

(5.29)

\mathcal{E}(r)=\left\{N^{-1}\log Z_{N}>\frac{cr}{2}\right\}.

(5.31)

Thus we see there is some small

|\mathbb{E}\langle f(\|\bm{u}(x)\|^{2}_{N})\rangle-\mathbb{E}\langle f(\|\bm{u}(x)\|^{2}_{N})I(|\|\bm{u}(x)\|^{2}_{N}-q_{c}|\leq\epsilon)\rangle|

For any

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\log|\mathbb{E}\langle f((\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime})_{N})\rangle-\mathbb{E}\langle f((\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime})_{N})\rangle^{q_{c},\epsilon}|=0.

|\mathbb{E}\langle f((\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime})_{N})\rangle-\mathbb{E}\langle f((\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime})_{N})I(\|\bm{u}\|_{N}^{2}\in D_{\epsilon}(\bm{q}_{c}))\rangle|\leq\mathbb{E}\langle I(\|\bm{u}\|_{N}^{2}\notin D_{\epsilon}(\bm{q}_{c}))\rangle,

With this result, we are now able to describe the rest of our method to establish (
5.3
), beginning with a heuristic motivation.

We define
S_{N}(q)=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\|u\|_{N}=q\}
. Associated to this are the quantities

Z_{N}(q_{c})=\int_{S_{N}(q)^{\Omega}}e^{-\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{u})}\omega(d\bm{u}),\;\;\;\;\langle f(\bm{u})\rangle^{q_{c}}=\frac{1}{Z_{N}(\bm{q}_{c})}\int_{S_{N}(q_{c})^{\Omega}}f(\bm{u})e^{-\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{u})}\omega(d\bm{u}),

(5.40)

where
\omega
is surface measure induced by the inclusion
S_{N}(q_{c})^{\Omega}\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Omega}
. One may imagine that when
\epsilon
is very small,
\mathbb{E}\langle f((\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime})_{N})\rangle^{q_{c},\epsilon}
should be approximated by the
\mathbb{E}\langle f((\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime})_{N})\rangle^{q_{c}}
.
So we in particular, the problem is now to characterize minimizer of
|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,q_{*}}
in terms of the Gibbs measure
\langle*\rangle_{q_{c}}
, reducing us to a spherical model.

The key realization is that the restriction of the isotropic field
V_{N}
given by (
1.3
) to any sphere
S_{N}(q)
has a generic mixing function for any
q
. We show this below, but it is also fairly clear from the description of
B
in (
1.4
). In particular, the identification of the minimizer should follow essentially from the methods in Section
2
.

Unfortunately, we are unable to make this approach work directly, as we are not able to justify the approximation of
\mathbb{E}\langle f((\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime})_{N})\rangle_{q_{c},\epsilon}
by
\mathbb{E}\langle f((\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime})_{N})\rangle_{q_{c}}
. Instead, we take an alternative approach. First we decompose
V_{N}
into a number of factors on
S_{N}(q_{c})
, and then extend these factors to
D_{N}^{\epsilon}(q_{c})
as a whole. Adding a small multiple of one of these terms to
D_{N}^{\epsilon}(q_{*})
, one can consider the free energy of this perturbed partition function, and show that if one sends
N\to\infty
and then
\epsilon\to 0
, one may apply the same differentiation method as in Section
2
.

Thus to begin, we will study the restrictions of a single copy of our original isotropic field
V_{N}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
(
1.3
). As
V_{N}
is an isotropic field on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the restriction of
V_{N}
to
S_{N}(q_{c})
is also isotropic, and thus the restriction coincides with some mixed
p
-spin model (on a sphere of non-standard radius) with mixing function
B_{q}(r)=B(2(q-r))
. Recall (
[
3
]
) the pure
p
-spin model,
H_{N,p}
, which is a homogeneous polynomial of degree-
p
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, which is also a centered Gaussian function with covariance given for
u,u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
by

\mathbb{E}H_{N,p}(u)H_{N,p}(u^{\prime})=N(u,u^{\prime})_{N}^{p}.

(5.41)

We note that

B_{q}(r)=B(2(q-r))=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{(-2)^{p}}{p!}\right)B^{(p)}(q)r^{p}.

(5.42)

It is then standard to show (see
[
73
]
) that as the restriction of
V_{N}
to
S_{N}(q_{c})
is a centered Gaussian function with covariance
B_{q}
, there is a family of pairwise independent pure
p
-spin models
\{V_{N,p}(u)\}_{p\geq 0}
(with non-standard normalization) such that for
u\in S_{N}(q_{c})
we have

V_{N}(u)=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}V_{N,p}(u).

(5.43)

Their normalization is given so that

\mathbb{E}V_{N}(u)V_{N}(u^{\prime})=\gamma_{p}(2q_{c})^{2}N(u,u^{\prime})_{N}^{p},

(5.44)

where
\gamma_{p}(q)\geq 0
is given by

\gamma_{p}(q)^{2}=\left(\frac{(-2)^{p}}{p!}\right)B^{(p)}(q).

(5.45)

Using the decomposition of
B
given by (
1.4
) we see that for
p\geq 1

(-1)^{p}B^{(p)}(q)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-\lambda^{2}q)\lambda^{2p}\nu(d\lambda)>0,

(5.46)

so that
\gamma_{p}(q)
is strictly positive for all
q\in[0,\infty)
.

Now we stress that as (
5.43
) only holds for
u\in S_{N}(q_{c})
. However,
V_{N,p}
is simply a random homogeneous polynomial of order
p
, and canonically extends to
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Thus we may consider both
V_{N}
and
V_{N,p}
as coupled Gaussian functions on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. To understand them, we compute their covariance.

Lemma 5.5
.

For
u,u^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we have that

\mathbb{E}V_{N}(u)V_{N,p}(u^{\prime})=N(u,u^{\prime})_{N}^{p}\gamma_{p}(q_{c}+\|u\|^{2}_{N})^{2}.

(5.47)

Proof.

We first show this in the case where for some
\lambda>0
we have that

B(x)=\exp(-\lambda^{2}x).

(5.48)

In this case we have that

\gamma_{p}(q)^{2}=\left(\frac{(2\lambda^{2})^{p}}{p!}\right)\exp(-\lambda^{2}q).

(5.49)

In this case, it is easy to explicitly construct a model for the field
V_{N}
. For this, observe that we may write

N\exp(-\lambda^{2}\|u-u^{\prime}\|^{2}_{N})=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{(2\lambda^{2})^{p}}{p!}\right)N(u,u^{\prime})_{N}^{p}\exp(-\lambda\|u\|^{2}_{N})\exp(-\lambda\|u^{\prime}\|^{2}_{N}).

(5.50)

Thus if we take a family pairwise independent standard pure
p
-spin models
\{H_{N,p}(u)\}_{p\geq 0}
, we have that as functions on
\mathbb{R}^{N}

V_{N}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\left(\sqrt{\frac{(2\lambda^{2})^{p}}{p!}}\right)H_{N,p}(u)e^{-\lambda^{2}\|u\|^{2}_{N}}.

(5.51)

From this we see that

V_{N,p}(u)=\left(\sqrt{\frac{(2\lambda^{2})^{p}}{p!}}\right)H_{N,p}(u)e^{-\lambda^{2}q}.

(5.52)

As the functions
H_{N,p}
are pairwise independent, we have that

\mathbb{E}V_{N}(u)V_{N,p}(u^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}H_{N,p}(u)H_{N,p}(u^{\prime})\left(\frac{(2\lambda^{2})^{p}}{p!}\right)e^{-\lambda^{2}(\|u\|^{2}_{N}+q)}=N(u,u^{\prime})^{p}\gamma_{p}(\|u\|_{N}^{2}+q)^{2}.

(5.53)

This establishes the case
B(x)=\exp(-\lambda^{2}x)
. The case where
B(x)=c_{0}
is trivial. We observe however that by taking the sum of independent isotropic fields, both sides of (
5.47
) are linear in the choice of
B
. In particular, this establishes the result when
B
is of the form

B(x)=c_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\exp(-\lambda^{2}_{i}x)=c_{0}+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda^{2}x}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\delta(x-\lambda_{i})dx\right),

(5.54)

for any choice of non-negative
c_{i}
and positive
\lambda_{i}
.

However, positive linear combinations of Dirac measures are dense in the space of finite measures on
(0,\infty)
under the total variation norm, as is seen by taking the quantile transform. Thus we only need to show that both the left- and right-hand sides of (
5.47
) are continuous with respect to the total variation norm. As the function
\lambda\mapsto e^{-\lambda^{2}q}\lambda^{2p}
is continuous and uniformly bounded on
[0,\infty)
when
q>0
, we see that
B^{(p)}(q)
, and thus
\gamma_{p}(q)
is thus continuous with respect to the total variation norm. This shows that the right-hand side of (
5.47
) is continuous with respect to the total variation norm. Similarly, as we have that

|\mathbb{E}V_{N}(u)V_{N,p}(u^{\prime})|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}V_{N}(u)^{2}\mathbb{E}V_{N,p}(u^{\prime})^{2}}=\sqrt{NB(0)N\gamma_{p}(2q_{c})^{2}},

(5.55)

we see that the left is continuous with respect to the total variation norm as well.
∎

Now if we fix

Z_{N,\epsilon,q_{c}}(\beta,p)=\int_{D_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q_{c}})}\exp\left(-\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{u})-\sum_{x\in\Omega}\beta V_{N,p,x}(\bm{u}(x))\right)d\bm{u},

Lemma 5.6
.

Fix any

\begin{split}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\bigg|&|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\partial_{\beta}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,\epsilon,q_{c}}(\beta,p)\\

Proof.

We first observe that

\partial_{\beta}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,\epsilon,q_{c}}(\beta,p)=\sum_{x\in\Omega}\mathbb{E}\langle-V_{N,p,x}(\bm{u}(x))\rangle^{q_{c},\epsilon}_{\beta,p}=|\Omega|\mathbb{E}\langle-V_{N,p,y}(\bm{u}(y))\rangle^{q_{c},\epsilon}_{\beta,p}

(5.58)

where we have used transitivity of the model in the last equality. To compute this let us define the function

(5.59)

For
\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Omega}

\mathrm{Cov}\left(V_{N,p,y}(\bm{u}(y))(\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{u}^{\prime})+\sum_{x\in\Omega}\beta V_{N,p,x}(\bm{u}^{\prime}(x)))\right)

(5.61)

\langle V_{N,p,y}(\bm{u}(y))\rangle^{q_{c},\epsilon}_{\beta,p}=\frac{\int_{D_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q_{c}})}V_{N,p,y}(\bm{u}(y))\exp\left(-\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{u})-\sum_{x\in\Omega}\beta V_{N,p,x}(\bm{u}(x))\right)d\bm{u}}{\int_{D_{N}^{\epsilon}(\bm{q_{c}})}\exp\left(-\mathcal{H}_{N}(\bm{u})-\sum_{x\in\Omega}\beta V_{N,p,x}(\bm{u}(x))\right)d\bm{u}},

(5.62)

then by Gaussian integration by parts we have that

N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\langle-V_{N,p,y}(\bm{u}(y))\rangle^{q_{c},\epsilon}_{\beta,p}

(5.63)

=\mathbb{E}\langle\|\bm{u}(y)\|_{N}^{2p}C_{\beta,p}(\|\bm{u}(y)\|^{2}_{N})\rangle^{q_{c},\epsilon}_{\beta,p}-\mathbb{E}\langle(\bm{u}(y),\bm{u}^{\prime}(y))^{p}_{N}C_{\beta,p}(\|\bm{u}^{\prime}(y)\|^{2}_{N})\rangle^{q_{c},\epsilon}_{\beta,p}.

(5.64)

The function

is smooth, so by Taylor’s theorem there is some

such that

|\mathbb{E}\langle(\bm{u}(y),\bm{u}^{\prime}(y))^{p}_{N}C_{\beta,p}(\|\bm{u}^{\prime}(y)\|^{2}_{N})\rangle^{q_{c},\epsilon}_{\beta,p}-(\bm{u}(y),\bm{u}^{\prime}(y))^{p}_{N}C_{\beta,p}(q_{c})\rangle^{q_{c},\epsilon}_{\beta,p}|

(5.65)

(5.66)

Similarly we have that

|\mathbb{E}\langle\|\bm{u}(y)\|_{N}^{2p}C_{\beta,p}(\|\bm{u}(y)\|^{2}_{N})\rangle^{q_{c},\epsilon}_{\beta,p}-(q_{c})^{p}C_{\beta,p}(q_{c})|\leq C\epsilon(q_{c}+\epsilon)^{p}.

(5.67)

As we have that

(depending continuously on

\left|\Omega|^{-1}N^{-1}\partial_{\beta}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,\epsilon,q_{c}}(\beta,p)-(1+\beta)\gamma_{p}(2q_{c})^{2}\left(q^{p}-\mathbb{E}\langle(\bm{u}(y),\bm{u}(y))_{N}^{p}\rangle^{q_{c},\epsilon}_{\beta,p}\right)\right|\leq C\epsilon,

(5.68)

which gives the desired result.

Proof of (

\begin{split}\hat{g}_{\beta,q}(s)=&\beta^{2}B(2(q-s))+\beta(q-s)K(\beta(q-s);t)-\frac{1}{L^{d}}\log(K(\beta(q-s);t)-t\Delta)\\
&-s\left(-2\beta^{2}B^{\prime}(2(q-q_{*}))-\beta K(\beta(q-q_{*});t)\right).\end{split}

, we see that this implies that

. This shows the potential pair can only have the claimed form, so we only need to check that (

\hat{g}_{\beta,q}(q-\beta^{-1}s)=\beta^{2}B\left(\frac{2s}{\beta}\right)+sK(s;t)-\frac{1}{L^{d}}\log(K(s;t)-t\Delta)

+(q-\beta^{-1}s)\left(2\beta^{2}B^{\prime}\left(\frac{2}{\beta}R_{1}(\mu;t)\right)+\beta\mu\right).

(6.20)

Removing some constant terms from the second line then yields the desired result.
∎

1.10

1.11
