Abstract
We describe a framework of modeling a central chilled water plant (CCWP) that consists of an aggregate cooling coil, a number of heterogeneous chillers and cooling towers, and a chilled water-based thermal energy storage system. We improve upon existing component models from the open literature using a constrained optimization-based framework to ensure that the models respect capacities of all the heat exchangers (cooling coils, chillers, and cooling towers) irrespective of the inputs provided. As a result, the proposed model has a wider range of validity compared to existing models; the latter can produce highly erroneous outputs when inputs are not within normal operating range. This feature is essential for training learning-based controllers that can choose inputs beyond normal operating conditions and is lacking in currently available models. The overall plant model is implemented in Matlab and is made publicly available. Simulation of a CCWP with closed loop control is provided as an illustration.

I
Introduction

Central chilled water plants (CCWPs) consist of chillers, cooling
towers, and increasingly thermal energy storage (TES) systems. They
are variously called “chiller plants”, “central plants”,
“district cooling energy plants”, etc. CCWPs are widely used
in campuses to provide cooling to a cluster of
buildings. Optimal
control of CCWPs have attracted quite a bit of research. Apart from
model-based optimal control such as MPC
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
,
there has recently been considerable interest in learning-based control
methods, especially reinforcement
learning
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]

and extremum-seeking
[
11
,
12
]
.

Learning-based model-free controllers, such as reinforcement learning
(RL) and extremum seeking controllers, need to explore the action
space to learn the optimal control policy. Such exploration can drive
the system outside its normal and safe operating
conditions. Additionally, training such a controller for a complex system like a CCWP typically requires a long and costly training phase.
Due to the risk and cost of learning directly from experiments
conducted on a physical CCWP, these “model-free”
controllers are typically designed by conducting numerical experiments on a realistic simulator. There
has been substantial work on modeling CCWPs and their
components - chillers, cooling towers and TES systems - spanning decades;
see
[
13
]
and references therein. Some of the well-established
component models are now incorporated into EnergyPlus
[
14
]
and the Modelica Buildings Library (MBL)
[
15
]
. There are works specifically on modeling CCWPs by combining existing equipment models, and controller development is an usual motivation behind these works
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
, as it is in our case.

However, there is currently a lack of easy-to-use open-source models
of CCWPs that can be used for training learning-based controllers. One of the
main reasons is the
limited region of validity
of these
models. They often produce poor predictions when inputs are chosen
outside normal operation range. For studies with classical control
algorithms, this feature is not a serious hurdle since control
commands can be chosen to be within normal operating range. However, the
situation is quite different in case of an RL controller. An RL controller
implicitly learns system constraints by getting poor rewards when its
actions lead to constraint violations, such as a lack of adequate
cooling to buildings. Therefore the simulation model must allow operation outside normal
operating conditions and predict poor performance of the plant therein. For example, if a small condenser water flow
rate is used under a high cooling load condition, the model must
predict that the plant is unable to reject the heat from the buildings
and the cooling load is not serviced. This is also true for
extremum seeking, though to a lesser degree. However, existing models of
chillers and cooling towers, and thus of CCWPs composed of them,
can predict utterly unreasonable outputs
when the inputs are outside the designed operating conditions. We show an example of
such behavior - limited region of validity - of a commonly used chiller model in Sec.
I-A
.

This paper presents an open-source model for the simulation of
CCWPs that fills the gap mentioned above. The schematic of the
CCWP for which the proposed model is developed is shown in
Fig.
1
. The components of the central plant (cooling coil, TES, chiller, and cooling tower) are modeled by
modifying existing models from the open literature, some of which are already
part of EnergyPlus and MBL. Our key innovation is to formulate some of the
equipment models as solutions to constrained
optimization problems so that the capacities of all the heat exchangers
(the cooling coil, chillers, and cooling towers) are respected. As a
result, the proposed CCWP model is able to generate reasonable
outputs when inputs are chosen outside the designed operating
conditions of the CCWP. Matlab implementation is available in
https://gitlab.com/pbarooah/ccwp_model
.

The rest of the paper is organized as
follows. Section
I-A
provides evidence of
the limited range of validity of an existing chiller model, which is the motivation behind
the paper. Section
I-B
summarizes contributions
over prior art. The model of each equipment in a CCWP is described
in Section

. The interconnection of these component
models to construct the complete model of a CCWP is described in
Section
III
. Simulations of an illustrative CCWP model are reported in Section
IV
. The paper concludes with some comments on future work in Section

.

I-A

Evidence of limited region of validity

The
Electric:EIR
model of a vapor compression
centrifugal chiller has been widely used in many
studies
[
19
,
20
]
. It is
currently available in
EnergyPlus
[
21
]
and the
MBL
[
15
]
. In this section we
present a scenario in which the existing
Electric:EIR
model predictions are dramatically incorrect when subjected to
inputs that are outside normal operating conditions. This
failure is the motivation for the new approach to modeling
proposed in this paper.

An
Electric:EIR
chiller from the MBL is put into a
simulation test environment where all inputs to a chiller can
be varied; see Figure
2(a)
. The chiller has
a rated capacity of 471.2 kW, and is of type

McQuay_WSE_471kW_5_89COP_Vanes
. When the inputs
are at their nominal values, the output - condenser
water return temperature,
T^{\mathrm{cdwr}}
- is at its nominal value,
35\degree
C; see
Figure
2(b)
. As the condenser water
flow rate
\dot{m}^{\text{cd}}
is decreased, the predicted cooling water return temperature
increases. When the cooling water flow rate is reduced to one
tenth of its nominal value, the model predicts that
T^{\mathrm{cdwr}}
is
90\degree
C in steady state;
see Figure
2(b)
. This is
clearly incorrect; in reality no chiller would exhibit a
cooling water return temperature close to the boiling
point. In fact, the condenser return water temperature should be below
48.9\degree
C to prevent damaging water pipes and cooling towers
[
22
]
.

The culprit is the lack of a hard upper bound on the condenser
capacity in the
Electric:EIR
chiller model. The model assumes that the
cooling load is transferred to the condenser water by the refrigerant no matter how
hot the water stream becomes. In reality,
physics imposes
saturation
: if the heat exchange
capacity of the equipment is reached, the outlet temperature
will not increase further no matter the
input is. The existing
Electric:EIR
model fails to capture this behavior. The underlying chiller model in the MBL is the same as that in EnergyPlus.

I-B

Contribution Over Prior Art

Our first contribution is a novel method to ensure that the
state-dependent capacities of all the heat exchangers - cooling coils
in air handling units, evaporator and condenser in chillers, and cooling towers - are all respected at each simulation timestep. While there are research papers that have addressed
this issue for some equipment, such as
[
23
]
,
those ideas have not been translated into these open-source simulation
tools. The approach we use in this paper to enforce hard limits on heat exchanger capacities is to formulate the state propagation problem - to compute next state from current states and inputs - as a constrained optimization problem: the actual cooling
delivered
is one that is as close as possible to the cooling
demand
while staying within the heat exchanger capacities. The cooling demand is specified as problem data. Due to the availability of modern optimization modeling tools, specifying and solving the optimization problem is straightforward. This approach is inspired partially by the
iterative computations used in EnergyPlus and MBL models as well as in
the method proposed in
[
23
]
. The underlying NLP solver uses an iterative scheme to find an optimal solution satisfying the constraints when it exists, but using modern tools for optimization makes it far easier to formulate and solve the constrained optimization problem than implementing an iterative scheme manually.

Our second contribution is that a Matlab-based software
implementation of the proposed model is made publicly available
(
https://gitlab.com/pbarooah/ccwp_model
). This software can be used by researchers to test and compare multiple control
methods.

Our proposed model structure is divided into three
levels: equipment, water loop, and plant. Connecting equipment models
belonging to the same water loop results in the corresponding water
loop model and combining multiple water loops leads to the plant
model. This modular framework greatly reduces the effort for building
a customized CCWP model. For example, swapping a chiller only
changes the equipment level program; adding chillers or implementing
new pump strategies only changes the water loop level program.

A preliminary version of the model developed here was described in
[
8
,
24
]
. The model in
[
8
,
24
]
was limited to homogeneous chillers and cooling towers, while in this paper each cooling tower and each chiller has a distinct sub-model so that heterogeneous equipment can be modeled in the proposed framework. Another major difference is that in
[
8
,
24
]
, the plant model was not modular; in fact both supervisory controller computation and the plant’s state propagation was cast as one single constrained optimization problem. In contrast, each equipment sub-model’s computations in this work is separate, which makes the overall plant model to be modular and also provides a clear separation between the model’s state propagation and supervisory controller’s computations.

II
Equipment Models

We start with a few preliminaries and then describe the model of each
equipment in a CCWP. There are five major equipments in a CCWP:
the cooling coil, the thermal energy storage (TES), the chiller(s),
the cooling tower(s), and various water pumps: chilled water pumps at
the chillers and the cooling water pumps at the cooling towers. For
each equipment model, we list the control input

u
, disturbance input
w
, and state
x
. All equipment models are discrete time dynamical systems of
the form:
x_{k+1}=f(x_{k},u_{k},w_{k})
and
y_{k}=g(x_{k},u_{k})
, with
k
being the discrete time index. The sampling period is denoted by
t_{s}
.

II-A

Preliminaries

II-A
1
Variable naming convention

Variables are named with the following convention:
the
supply temperature is (nominally) lower than the return
temperature
. Since the water returning from one equipment
is often supplied to another equipment, such as cooling coil
to chiller, the words “supply” and “return” can be
confusing without providing sufficient context to indicate
whether it is “supplied by” or “supplied to”. Providing
such meticulous context becomes cumbersome, and requires too
many super/sub-scripts. The convention we follow here is
consistent and straightforward. Note that heating equipment such as boilers are not
considered here, which may require a revision of this convention.

Remark 1
.

The reason for enforcing heat transfer capacity of the
cooling coil implicitly via the maximum return
temperature of the “load water” is that, since the
cooling coil model is an aggregate of many coils, the
upper bound on its heat exchange capacity is the sum
of these individual capacities, which requires
considerable bookkeeping that is prone to error. In
contrast,
T^{\text{lwr}}_{\text{UB}}
is fairly standard, and is
thus easy to specify. According to the ASHRAE
handbook
[
26
]
this temperature
is typically maintained at
12-15\degree
C. In the sequel, we use
this approach for enforcing capacity of chiller and
cooling tower as well.

II-C

Thermal Energy Storage (TES) Model

The thermal energy storage system considered here is a chilled
water tank. The TES is modeled by two virtual sub-tanks: warm water
tank (superscript “tww”) and cold water tank (superscript
“twc”), as shown in Figure
1
. This is a
common modeling approach since a thermocline separates the
warm water and cold water in the
TES
[
27
]
. Associated with each tank are
two variables: the temperature of the waters in the sub-tanks:
T^{\text{tww}}_{k}
and

T^{\text{twc}}_{k}
, and the “fractions” of water mass in them:
S^{\text{tww}}_{k}
and

S^{\text{twc}}_{k}
. The total mass of water inside the TES is always
kept constant, i.e.,
S^{\text{tww}}_{k}+S^{\text{twc}}_{k}=1
. The amount of
increase in one sub-tank equals to the amount of decrease in
the other sub-tank. The flow rate of water into the TES is

\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}
. When
\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}>0
, this means that
\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}t_{s}
(kg) of water at a temperature
T^{\text{sw}}
(supply water from
the chillers; see Fig.
1
) is charged into
the cold water tank at time
k
(rather, during the time
interval
[(k-1)t_{s},kt_{s}]
) and
\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}t_{s}
(kg) of water
at temperature
T^{\text{tww}}
has been discharged from warm
water tank during the same time. When
\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}<0
,

\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}t_{s}
(kg) of water at temperature

(return

\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k}t_{s}
(kg) of
water at a temperature of
T^{\text{twc}}
has been discharged from
cold water tank. We assume the TES is well insulated and that
no mixing occurs between the two sub-tanks. Thus, the
temperature in each sub-tank is only effected by the
temperature and flow rate of water coming into and out of the

u^{\text{tes}}
and state

II-D

Chiller Model

We consider water-cooled vapor compression chillers; see
Fig.
4
. The refrigerant loop in the chiller transfers heat from the
chilled water stream to the cooling water stream, which
reduces the chilled water temperature from
T^{\text{chwr}}
to

T^{\text{chws}}
and increases the condenser water temperature from
T^{\mathrm{cdws}}
to
T^{\mathrm{cdwr}}
.

The proposed chiller model is modified from the

Electric:EIR
chiller model
in
[
21
]
. The
Electric:EIR
chiller model abstracts out the details of the refrigerant
loop, and assumes that all of the heat absorbed by the chilled
water at the chiller evaporator is transferred to the
condenser water at the chiller
condenser. Since it does not limit the condenser capacity,
unrealistically high condenser water return temperature
T^{\mathrm{cdwr}}

can be predicted when cooling by the condenser water is inadequate, as demonstrated in
Section
I-A
. We modify the

Electric:EIR
chiller model by enforcing saturation of the condenser
water outlet temperature at the predetermined value
T^{\text{cwr}}_{\text{UB}}
, which implicitly
defines a hard upper bound on the capacity of the condenser, i.e.,
\dot{q}^{\text{cd}}_{\text{UB}}
. Because of the saturation feature, the proposed model is called the
Electric:EIR:wSat
model.

Fig. 4:
Schematic of a water-cooled vapor compression
chiller, showing variables used in the model.

\displaystyle=[T^{\text{chwr}},\dot{m}^{\text{chw}},T^{\mathrm{cdws}},\dot{m}^{\text{cd}},T^{\text{chws}}_{\text{set}}]^{T},

\displaystyle x^{\text{ch}}

\displaystyle=[T^{\text{chws}},T^{\mathrm{cdwr}}]^{T},

(17)

where

and

are the temperature and flowrate

is the

is the temperature at the

The chilled water supply temperature

(at evaporator) is modeled based on heat
balance with a first order dynamic behavior:

\displaystyle T^{\text{chws}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=a^{\text{ch}}T^{\text{chws}}_{k}+(1-a^{\text{ch}})(T^{\text{chwr}}_{k}-\frac{\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{k}}{C_{{\text{pw}}}\cdot\dot{m}^{\text{chw}}_{k}}).

\displaystyle 0\leq\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{k}

The required cooling rate

is determined by the difference between the chilled water return temperature and the chilled water supply setpoint:

\displaystyle\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{\text{req},k}

\displaystyle=C_{{\text{pw}}}\cdot\ \dot{m}^{\text{chw}}_{k}\cdot\ (T^{\text{chwr}}_{k}-T^{\text{chws}}_{\text{set}}).

(20)

The upper bound on the evaporator heat exchange rate capacity is:

\displaystyle\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{\text{UB},k}

\displaystyle=\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{\text{nom}}\cdot\ f^{\text{CapFunT}}_{k},

(21)

where
\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{\text{nom}}
is the constant nominal capacity of the chiller (evaporator);
f^{\text{CapFunT}}_{k}
is a capacity modifier function that depends on myriad factors and is usually an empirical relationship that differs from one chiller to another. We take the empirical relationship provided for the
Electric:EIR
chiller in
[
21
]
:

\displaystyle f^{\text{CapFunT}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}T^{\text{chws}}_{k}+\alpha_{3}(T^{\text{chws}}_{k})^{2}+\alpha_{4}T^{\mathrm{cdws}}_{k}

\displaystyle+\alpha_{5}(T^{\mathrm{cdws}}_{k})^{2}+\alpha_{6}T^{\text{chws}}_{k}T^{\mathrm{cdws}}_{k},

(22)

where
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{6}
’s are empirical parameters.
For the chiller condenser, a heat balance leads to the
following dynamic model of the condenser water return
temperature
T^{\mathrm{cdwr}}
with an assumed first order response
between the input and the output (with filter coefficient
a^{\text{cd}}
):

\displaystyle T^{\mathrm{cdwr}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=a^{\text{cd}}T^{\mathrm{cdwr}}_{k}+(1-a^{\text{cd}})(T^{\mathrm{cdws}}_{k}+\frac{\dot{q}^{\text{cd}}_{k}}{C_{{\text{pw}}}\cdot\dot{m}^{\text{cd}}_{k}}),

(23)

where
\dot{m}^{\text{cd}}_{k}
is the flowrate of cooling water through the condenser, and the heat exchange at the condenser
\dot{q}^{\text{cd}}_{k}
is:

\displaystyle\dot{q}^{\text{cd}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{k}+\eta_{1}P^{\text{ch}}_{k},

(24)

where the second term
\eta_{1}P^{\text{ch}}_{k}
is due to the waste heat produced by the electricity consumed by the chiller, mainly the compressor motors. The condenser return water temperature cannot be lower than the supply (after picking up heat at the condenser), and it cannot exceed its upper bound:

\displaystyle T^{\mathrm{cdws}}_{k}

\displaystyle\leq T^{\mathrm{cdwr}}_{k}\leq T^{\mathrm{cdwr}}_{\text{UB}},

(25)

In the
Electric:EIR
model
from
[
21
]
, the electric power
consumed by the chiller,
P^{\text{ch}}_{k}
, is modeled by the
following empirical relationships:

\displaystyle P^{\text{ch}}_{k}=

\displaystyle P^{\text{ch}}_{\text{nom}}\cdot f^{\text{CapFunT}}_{k}\cdot\ f^{\text{EIRFunT}}_{k}\cdot\ f^{\text{EIRFunPLR}}_{k}\cdot\text{CR}_{k}

(26)

where
f^{\text{CapFunT}}
is defined in (
II-D
), and

\displaystyle f^{\text{EIRFunT}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}T^{\text{chws}}_{k}+\beta_{3}(T^{\text{chws}}_{k})^{2}+\beta_{4}T^{\mathrm{cdws}}_{k}

\displaystyle+\beta_{5}(T^{\mathrm{cdws}}_{k})^{2}+\beta_{6}T^{\text{chws}}_{k}T^{\mathrm{cdws}}_{k},

(27)

where
\beta_{i}
’s are empirical parameters. The
f^{\text{EIRFunPLR}}_{k}
term is the modifier due to the part load ratio:

\displaystyle f^{\text{EIRFunPLR}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}\mathrm{PLR}_{k}+\gamma_{3}\mathrm{PLR}_{k}^{2},

(28)

\displaystyle\mathrm{PLR}_{k}

\displaystyle=\min\big\{\max\{\frac{\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{k}}{\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{\text{UB},k}},\mathrm{PLR}_{\text{LB}}\},\mathrm{PLR}_{\text{UB}}\big\},

(29)

with coefficients
\gamma_{i}
’s. The last term
CR
in (
26
) is the cycling ratio:

\displaystyle\text{CR}_{k}:=\min\{\frac{\mathrm{PLR}_{k}}{\mathrm{PLR}_{\text{LB}}},\ 1.0\}

(30)

which is the modifier to account for chiller cycling on and
off under very low load condition, where
\mathrm{PLR}_{\text{LB}}
and
\mathrm{PLR}_{\text{UB}}
are lower and upper bounds for the part load ratio.

Note that given the current input and state

u_{k}^{\text{ch}},x_{k}^{\text{ch}}
, the next state
x^{\text{ch}}_{k+1}
, namely

T^{\text{chws}}_{k+1}
and
T^{\text{cwr}}_{k+1}
, cannot be uniquely
determined from these relationships since
\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{k}
is not
yet known. The intermediate variable
\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{k}
and the
next state
x_{k+1}^{\text{ch}}
will be determined together as
follows. The low-level controllers will try to run the chiller
to cool down the chilled water from
T^{\text{chwr}}_{k}
to the setpoint

T^{\text{chws}}_{\text{set}}
unless either the evaporator reaches its capacity (
\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{k}=\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{\text{UB},k}
) or the condenser reaches its capacity
(
T^{\mathrm{cdwr}}_{k+1}=T^{\text{cdwr}}_{\text{UB}}
). The actions of these low-level
controllers - along with the associated physics that enforces
heat exchanger capacities - are abstracted into the following
optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{k}}&(T^{\text{chws}}_{\text{set},k}-T^{\text{chws}}_{k+1})^{2}\\
\text{ s.t. }&\eqref{eq:TChWS}-\eqref{eq:CR}\end{split}

(31)

In summary, (
31
) - a nonlinear program
(NLP) - is the proposed chiller
model as its solution determines the next state

x^{\text{ch}}_{k+1}=(T^{\text{chws}}_{k+1},T^{\mathrm{cdwr}}_{k+1})
from
u_{k}^{\text{ch}},x_{k}^{\text{ch}}
.

Additionally, the problem data must satisfy

\displaystyle T^{\text{chws}}_{\text{LB}}

\displaystyle\leq T^{\text{chws}}_{\text{set},k}\leq T^{\text{chwr}}_{k},

(32)

\displaystyle T^{\text{cws}}_{k}

\displaystyle\leq T^{\mathrm{cdwr}}_{\text{UB}},

(33)

where (
32
) ensure
\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{\text{req},k}\geq 0
and (
33
) ensures
\dot{q}^{\text{cd}}_{k}\geq 0
. If the inputs do not satisfy one of the
constraints (
32
)-(
33
),
the chiller’s refrigerant loop will be considered inoperative, and chilled water
and condenser water will pass through the chiller with no heat exchange:
T^{\text{chws}}_{k+1}=T^{\text{chwr}}_{k}
and
T^{\mathrm{cdwr}}_{k+1}=T^{\mathrm{cdws}}_{k}
.

The electrical power consumed by the pumps that circulate the water streams in the chillers is modeled using a black-box model proposed in
[
28
]
:

\displaystyle P^{\text{chwp}}_{k}

\displaystyle=a_{1}\ln(1+a_{2}\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{chw}})+a_{3}\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{chw}}+a_{4},

(34)

where
a_{i}
are empirical parameters.

Cooling Tower Model

We consider variable speed evaporative cooling towers in this
paper; see Fig.
7
.

Fig. 7:
Cooling tower flows and their
related variables. Inlet and outlet water flow rates
are assumed same.

The inputs and state of the cooling tower model are:

\displaystyle u^{\text{ct}}

\displaystyle=[T^{\text{cwr}},\dot{m}^{\text{cw}},\dot{m}^{\text{oa}}]^{T},

(35)

\displaystyle w^{\text{ct}}

\displaystyle=T^{\text{oawb}},

(36)

\displaystyle x^{\text{ct}}

\displaystyle=T^{\text{cws}},

(37)

where
T^{\text{cws}},T^{\text{cwr}}
are the temperatures of the cooling water supply (from the cooling tower to the chiller condensers) and cooling water return (to the cooling tower); see Fig.
1
.
As in the other equipment, the actual heat exchange in the
cooling tower will be the smaller of the heat exchange desired
(decided by a setpoint to a local controller that manipulates
the fan speed) and the capacity of the heat exchanger which is
a function of the OA wet bulb and air flow rate through the tower.

The proposed cooling tower model is modified from the
variable speed evaporative cooling tower model in EnergyPlus
[
21
]
with the so-called
“YorkCalc” correlations. In the sequel, we will call the
unmodified model from EnergyPlus the
YorkCalc
model, and proposed model the

YorkCalc:wSat
model, meaning, “with saturation”. A few
intermediate variables are needed to describe the model. Define the
scope
temperature
T^{\text{sco}}
as the difference between the hot cooling water return temperature and the OA wt bulb temperature:

\displaystyle T^{\text{sco}}_{k}

\displaystyle:=T^{\text{cwr}}_{k}-T^{\text{oawb}}_{k},

(38)

which is a function purely on the inlet conditions (
u^{\text{ct}}
and
w^{\text{ct}}
). Define also the
range
temperature
T^{\text{ran}}
, and
approach
temperature
T^{\mathrm{app}}
of the cooling tower as:

\displaystyle T^{\mathrm{ran}}_{k}

\displaystyle:=T^{\text{cwr}}_{k}-T^{\text{cws}}_{k},

(39)

\displaystyle T^{\mathrm{app}}_{k}

\displaystyle:=T^{\text{cws}}_{k}-T^{\text{oawb}}_{k}.

(40)

It follows from (
38
)-(
40
) that

\displaystyle T^{\text{ran}}_{k}+T^{\text{app}}_{k}=T^{\text{sco}}_{k}.

(41)

Also,
T^{\mathrm{ran}}\in[T^{\text{ran}}_{\text{LB}},T^{\text{ran}}_{\text{UB}}]
,
T^{\mathrm{app}}\in[T^{\text{app}}_{\text{LB}},T^{\text{app}}_{\text{UB}}]
where the lower and upper bounds
are known constants for a particular cooling tower design. There are three possible scenarios:

Case 1

T^{\text{cwr}}_{k}-T^{\text{oawb}}_{k}<T^{\text{ran}}_{\text{LB}}+T^{\text{app}}_{\text{LB}}
: The temperature difference between OA (wet bulb) and cooling water return is not adequate to lose heat to the atmosphere. Meaning, water vapor cannot evaporate, and no cooling can be provided to the cooling water. Therefore,
T^{\text{cws}}_{k+1}=T^{\text{cwr}}_{k}
.

Case 2

T^{\text{cwr}}_{k}-T^{\text{oawb}}_{k}>T^{\text{ran}}_{\text{UB}}+T^{\text{app}}_{\text{UB}}
: This is the other extreme, in which the maximum possible range and approach still cannot satisfy (
41
). In this case, the range is saturated at its maximum:
T^{\text{cws}}_{k+1}=T^{\text{cwr}}_{k}-T^{\text{ran}}_{\text{UB}}
.

Case 3

T^{\text{cwr}}_{k}-T^{\text{oawb}}_{k}\in[T^{\text{ran}}_{\text{LB}}+T^{\text{app}}_{\text{LB}},T^{\text{ran}}_{\text{UB}}+T^{\text{app}}_{\text{UB}}]
:
In the YorkCalc model, the following empirical relationship is used for computing the approach for a given range
[
21
]
:

\displaystyle\widehat{T}^{\text{app}}(T^{\text{ran}},u^{\text{ct}},w^{\text{ct}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0,j=0,\ell=0}^{2}c_{(i+1)\cdot(j+1)\cdot(k+1)}(T^{\text{oawb}})^{i}(T^{\text{ran}})^{j}LGR^{\ell}

(42)

where
LGR
is the liquid gas ratio :

\displaystyle LGR_{k}

\displaystyle=\min\{\frac{FR^{\text{wat}}_{k}}{FR^{\text{air}}_{k}},LGR_{\text{UB}}\},

(43)

where
FR^{\text{air}}
and
FR^{\text{wat}}
are the fractions of air and water flowrates:

\displaystyle FR^{\text{wat}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{m}^{\text{cw}}_{k}}{\dot{m}^{\text{cw}}_{\text{nom}}},

\displaystyle FR^{\text{air}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{m}^{\text{oa}}_{k}}{\dot{m}^{\text{oa}}_{\text{nom}}}

(44)

Our proposal is to compute range and approach temperatures by solving the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\min_{T^{\text{ran}}_{k},T^{\text{app}}_{k}}(T^{\mathrm{app}}_{k}-\widehat{T}^{\text{app}}(T^{\mathrm{ran}}_{k},u^{\text{ct}},w^{\text{ct}}))^{2}\\
\text{ s.t. }&\ T^{\text{ran}}_{\text{LB}}\leq T^{\mathrm{ran}}_{k}\leq T^{\text{ran}}_{\text{UB}},\\
&\eqref{eq:CTBalance}:T^{\mathrm{ran}}_{k}+T^{\mathrm{app}}_{k}=T^{\text{sco}}_{k},\\
&T^{\text{app}}_{\text{LB}}\leq T^{\mathrm{app}}_{k}\leq T^{\text{app}}_{\text{UB}}.\end{split}

(45)

That is, we use the empirical relation (
II-E
) as an approximation. Once the range temperature
T^{\mathrm{ran}}_{k}
is computed by solving the
NLP (
45
), the cooling water supply
temperature
T^{\text{cws}}_{k+1}
is determined from:

\displaystyle T^{\text{cws}}_{k+1}=a^{\text{ct}}T^{\text{cws}}_{k}+(1-a^{\text{ct}})(T^{\text{cwr}}_{k}-T^{\mathrm{ran}}_{k})

(46)

where
a^{\text{ct}}
is a low pass filter coefficient to model a first order
response between inlet and outlet temperatures in the heat
exchanger. The heat exchanged from cooling water to the ambient in the
cooling tower is:

\displaystyle\dot{q}^{\mathrm{ct}}_{k}

\displaystyle=C_{{\text{pw}}}\dot{m}^{\text{cw}}_{k}(T^{\text{cwr}}_{k}-T^{\text{cws}}_{k})

(47)

The electrical power consumption by the fan motors at the
cooling tower is modeled using cubic fan law
[
21
]
:

\displaystyle P^{\text{ct}}_{k}

\displaystyle=P^{\text{ct}}_{\text{nom}}\cdot(FR^{\text{air}}_{k})^{3}.

(48)

The electrical power consumption by the water pumps that circulate the cooling water is modeled using a black-box model proposed in
[
28
]
:

\displaystyle P^{\text{cwp}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}\ln(1+\gamma_{2}\dot{m}_{k}^{\text{cw}})+\gamma_{3}\dot{m}^{\text{cw}}_{k}+\gamma_{4}.

(49)

where
\gamma_{i}
’s are empirical parameters.

Remark 3

(Difference between
YorkCalc
model in EnergyPlus and
MBL and the proposed
YorkCalc:wSat
model)

The
NLP (
45
) ensures that when the prediction from the
YorkCalc correlations are infeasible (such as when

\widehat{T}^{\text{app}}
is negative), range and approach
temperatures are still within pre-specified
bounds.

III
Central Chilled Water Plant Model

The widely deployed primary-secondary pumping strategy is considered
in our CCWP model
[
30
]
. Two
subsystems — chilled water loop and cooling water loop — are first
constructed by interconnecting equipments in these water loops, whose
models were described in Section
II
. The interconnection
of the two water loops leads to the full CCWP model.

Since there are multiple chillers and cooling towers in the
CCWP, the letter
i
is used as the index for each chiller
and cooling tower. On/off status of an individual equipment is denoted by the symbol

\mathbbm{1}
. For instance,
\mathbbm{1}^{\text{ch},i}=1
if chiller
i
is “on” and 0 if
“off; similarly
\mathbbm{1}^{\text{ct},i}
is used to denote on/off status
of the
i
-th cooling tower. A variable without an index is the sum
of those with indices. For example,
\dot{m}^{\text{chw},1}
is the
flowrate of chilled water through chiller 1, whereas

\dot{m}^{\text{chw}}
is the total chilled water flowrate through all the “on”
chillers.

III-A

Chilled water loop

The chilled water loop (see Fig.
1
consists of
four types of equipment: an aggregate cooling coil, a TES, chillers, and
chilled water pumps. The exogenous inputs and states of all the constituent component models form those of the chilled water loop. However, due to the interconnected nature of the system the output of an equipment, or the combination of the outputs of multiple equipments, can become the input to another equipment in this loop. So some of the inputs to component models become internal variables - that we retain as outputs - but do not count as external inputs/control commands. The commanded inputs to the chilled water loop are

\displaystyle u^{\text{chw}}=[\dot{m}^{\text{lw}},\dot{m}^{\text{tw}},T^{\text{chws}}_{\text{set}},\dot{m}^{\text{chws},i},\mathbbm{1}^{\text{ch},i},\dot{m}^{\text{cd},i}],i=1,\dots,n^{\text{ch}}.

(50)

To fully describe the interconnected CCWP model the following variables are needed that are considered as outputs since they are functions of the states and inputs:

\displaystyle y^{\text{chw}}

\displaystyle=[T^{\text{sw}},T^{\text{rw}},T^{\text{chwr}},\bar{T}^{\text{chws}},\dot{m}^{\text{sw}},\dot{m}^{\text{bp}},\dot{m}^{\text{chw}},

\displaystyle\dot{m}^{\text{cd}},\dot{q}^{\text{cc}},\dot{q}^{\text{evap}},\dot{q}^{\text{cd}},P^{\text{ch}},P^{\text{chwp}}].

(51)

To explain the components of
y^{\text{chw}}
, first note that the flowrates, heat exchange rates, and power consumptions are the sum of corresponding terms from each individual equipment. Using the terminology described at the beginning of the section, we get

\displaystyle\dot{q}^{\text{evap}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\text{ch}}}\dot{q}^{\text{evap},i}_{k},

\displaystyle\dot{q}^{\text{cd}}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\text{ch}}}\dot{q}^{\text{cd},i}_{k},

(52)

\displaystyle P^{\text{ch}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\text{ch}}}P^{\text{ch},i}_{k},

\displaystyle P^{\text{chwp}}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\text{ch}}}P^{\text{chwp},i}_{k},

(53)

where
\dot{q}^{\text{evap},i}_{k},\dot{q}^{\text{cd},i}_{k},P^{\text{ch},i}_{k},P^{\text{chwp},i}_{k}
are defined
in (
31
),(
24
), (
26
), and (
34
).

In a primary-secondary pumping system, the water flowrate through each
“on” chiller is maintained at its nominal value,

\dot{m}^{\text{chw},i}_{\text{nom}}
. As a result, the total chilled water flowrate
(
\dot{m}^{\text{chw}}_{k}
) may be more than what is required by loads and TES,
which is the
supply water
flowrate
\dot{m}^{\text{sw}}_{k}
. See
the points marked “
sw
” and “
rw
” for “supply water” and
“return water” in Fig.
1
. A
one-way bypass valve thus routes the redundant chilled water
(
\dot{m}^{\text{bp}}_{k}
) from chiller outlet directly to the chiller inlet. We
thus obtain the following

\displaystyle\dot{m}^{\text{chw}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\text{ch}}}\mathbbm{1}^{\text{ch},i}_{k}\dot{m}^{\text{chw},i}_{\text{nom}},

\displaystyle\dot{m}^{\text{bp}}_{k}=\dot{m}^{\text{chw}}_{k}-\dot{m}^{\text{sw}},

(54)

\displaystyle\dot{m}^{\text{sw}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\dot{m}^{\text{lw}}_{k}+\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k},

\displaystyle\dot{m}^{\text{rw}}=\dot{m}^{\text{sw}}.

(55)

The load water supplied to the cooling coil could be the mixture of chilled
waters from the chillers or chilled water from the TES or a mixture of the two, depending
on the commands to valve “A” in Fig.
1
. The return water could be the
water leaving the cooling coil or the TES or a mixture of the two,
depending on the commands to valve “B” in Fig.
1
. Chilled water return is a
mixture of return water and bypass water. The above relationships are summarized below, where
\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\text{ch}}}\mathbbm{1}^{\text{ch},i}\neq 0
means at least one chiller is on, and
\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\text{ch}}}\mathbbm{1}^{\text{ch},i}=0
means
all chillers are off:

\displaystyle T^{\text{lws}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}T^{\text{sw}}_{k}+\frac{\text{min}(\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k},0)}{\dot{m}^{\text{lw}}_{k}}\Big(T^{\text{sw}}_{k}-T^{\text{twc}}_{k}\Big),&\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\text{ch}}}\mathbbm{1}^{\text{ch},i}\neq 0,\\
T^{\text{twc}}_{k},&\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\text{ch}}}\mathbbm{1}^{\text{ch},i}=0,\end{cases}

(56)

\displaystyle T^{\text{rw}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}T^{\text{lwr}}_{k}+\frac{\text{max}(\dot{m}^{\text{tw}}_{k},0)}{\dot{m}^{\text{sw}}_{k}}\Big(T^{\text{tww}}_{k}-T^{\text{lwr}}_{k}\Big),&\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\text{ch}}}\mathbbm{1}^{\text{ch},i}\neq 0,\\
T^{\text{tww}}_{k},&\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\text{ch}}}\mathbbm{1}^{\text{ch},i}=0,\end{cases}

(57)

\displaystyle\bar{T}^{\text{chwr}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}T^{\text{rw}}_{k}+\frac{\dot{m}^{\text{bp}}_{k}}{\dot{m}^{\text{chw}}_{k}}(\bar{T}^{\text{chws}}_{k}-T^{\text{rw}}_{k}),&\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\text{ch}}}\mathbbm{1}^{\text{ch},i}\neq 0\\
T^{\text{rw}}_{k},&\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\text{ch}}}\mathbbm{1}^{\text{ch},i}=0,\end{cases}

(58)

where the
plantwide
chilled water supply
temperature, denoted by
\bar{T}^{\text{chws}}
, is the temperature of the chilled water streams from all chillers after mixing. It is a weighted average of
the chilled water supply temperatures of all active chillers:

\displaystyle\bar{T}^{\text{chws}}_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\dot{m}^{\text{chw}}_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\text{ch}}}\mathbbm{1}^{\text{ch},i}T^{\text{chws},i}_{k}\dot{m}^{\text{chw},i}_{k},&\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\text{ch}}}\mathbbm{1}^{\text{ch},i}\neq 0,\\
T^{\text{chwr}}_{k},&\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\text{ch}}}\mathbbm{1}^{\text{ch},i}=0,\end{cases}

(59)

From Fig.
1
, we see that the chilled water return temperature entering each chiller’s evaporator is the
plantwide
chilled water return temperature:

\displaystyle T^{\text{chwr},i}

\displaystyle=\bar{T}^{\text{chwr}},

\displaystyle i=1,\dots,n^{\text{ch}}.

\displaystyle T^{\mathrm{cdws},i}

\displaystyle=\bar{T}^{\text{cws}}

\displaystyle i=1,\dots,n^{\text{ch}}

(61)

\displaystyle T^{\text{sw}}_{k}

III-D

Software implementation

A Matlab implementation of the CCWP model is released under the MIT
license and is available in
https://gitlab.com/pbarooah/ccwp_model
. The chiller model (
31
) and the cooling tower
model (
45
) are non linear programs (NLPs). There are many powerful open source tools for posing and solving NLPs efficiently. In our Matlab implementation we use CasADi
[
31
]
and IPOPT
[
32
]
to pose and solve (
31
),(
45
). The user needs to download and install CasADi, which
is freely available under LGPL-3.0 license. All the parameters of the CCWP are defined in the function
func_defPlantParams_2Ch2CT
in the codebase.

During simulation, the control inputs
u_{k}^{p}
can be computed either by a
supervisory controller or specified a-priori. In
either case, not all possible values of the input
vector
u^{p}_{k}
are feasible. For instance, if a
chiller is “on” then there must be a non-zero
condenser water flow rate in that chiller, meaning

\mathbbm{1}^{\text{ch},1}_{k}=1
and
\dot{m}^{\text{cd},1}_{k}=0
are
not allowed. There are several such feasibility checks
that need to be done; e.g., (
7
),(
63
). A function

func_CheckInputs
in the codebase checks if

u_{k}
passes these feasibility tests, and if not, stops the simulation.

The codebase currently has the model of a CCWP that consists of an
aggregate load, a TES, two distinct chillers, two distinct cooling towers, and corresponding chilled water and cooling
water pumps. The parameters of
ElectricEIRChiller Carrier 19XR
823kW/6.28COP/Vanes
model in MBL, which has a cooling capacity
0.823 MW, are used for Chiller 1. The parameters of

ElectricEIRChiller York YT 1368kW/7.35COP/VSD
model in MBL,
which has a cooling capacity 2.1 MW, is used for Chiller 2. Both
cooling towers are of YorkCalc type, but their nominal air flowrate
and water flowrates are changed from the default to be consistent with
the chiller condenser flowrates. The design cooling capacity of the two
cooling towers combined is 2859.8 kW. The nominal power consumption of
cooling towers are modified according to MBL
references
[
15
]
. Some of the parameters of the CCWP are mentioned in Table
I
.

The state propagation function
func_f_CCWP_2Ch2CT
returns the
next state
x^{p}_{k+1}
when provided
x^{p}_{k},u^{p}_{k},w_{k}^{p}
and plant parameters as arguments. For
simulating a CCWP with a different number and type of equipment, a
new state propagation function has to be written.

Closed Loop Simulation Example

The proposed model has been used for training RL controllers for a
CCWP in
[

the control command
u_{k}
at every
k
with a goal to reduce total
electricity cost and meet the load
\dot{q}^{\text{L}}

space, the interested reader can find them in
[

]
.

, cooling delivered
\dot{q}^{\text{cc}}

period. Note that the user needs to provide these three exogenous inputs in order to perform simulations with the
CCWP model and the rule based controller. The OA wet bulb

maximum cooling load equal to 130% of the total cooling capacity of the two chillers combined. These
closed loop simulations can be recreated by running the Matlab script

simulateCCWP_withRBC.m
in the codebase.

The design COP of the two chillers used in the simulations are

V
Conclusions

The proposed model has been used for training an RL
controller for a CCWP in
[
33
]
. There are many improvements that can be made to the model and its
software implementation. As to the software, translating the codebase
from Matlab to Python or Julia will obviate the need for commercial
software with a license fee. Improvements are possible to the
mathematical model itself, such as incorporating additional dynamic
elements and transport delays.

Verification of the proposed CCWP model against experimental data is not addressed in this paper. The accuracy of the proposed model is dependent on the accuracy of the constituent equipment models. It was shown in
[
36
]
that many existing and widely used chiller models for predicting COP can work well in laboratory conditions but provide poor predictions in field conditions. The component models we use are ultimately based on existing models in the literature and may suffer from similar issues. However, since the proposed CCWP model is modular, improved component models can be incorporated in the future.
