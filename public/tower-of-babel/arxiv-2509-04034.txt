A Full-Atmosphere Model of Jupiter
Antonín Knížek, Paul B. Rimmer, Martin Ferus

Abstract
This paper presents a combined 1D photochemical-thermochemical kinetics model of Jupiter’s deeper atmosphere, troposphere and stratosphere. The model covers atmospheric pressure range from 1.1 × 10 3 1.1\times 10^{3} bar to 7.4 × 10 − 11 7.4\times 10^{-11} bar and is the first model that incorporates sulfur chemistry when spanning an atmospheric region of this extent. This model incorporates a new version of the STAND reaction network with updated NH 4 ​ SH \text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{SH} chemistry, and updated Antoine equation parameters for NH 4 ​ SH \text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{SH} and H 2 ​ S \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{S} . Validation against current models of Jupiter’s atmosphere as well as recent observational data shows that our model successfully describes Jupiter’s main observed chemical features. Since one of the focuses of the model is the chemistry on nitrogen, it correctly predicts the formation of a mixed NH 3 \text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}} - NH 4 ​ SH \text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{SH} cloud layer between 0.1 and 1 bar. It also describes the chemistry of HCN throughout the atmosphere and discovers a region in the stratosphere between 1 × 10 − 6 1\times 10^{-6} and 6.76 × 10 − 8 6.76\times 10^{-8} bar, where HCN forms through radical chemistry with maximum mixing ratio 33 ppb at 2.94 × 10 − 7 2.94\times 10^{-7} bar – a prediction testable by observations. At the same time, our model predicts a quenched N 2 \text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} mixing ratio 490 ppm up to 10 -6 bar. The model therefore successfully bridges the gap between existing models of separate regions of Jupiter’s atmosphere and makes new testable predictions of several chemical species.

Introduction

Jupiter is the largest planet of our Solar system, a failed star, and as such has always attracted considerable attention of the scientific community.
Its atmosphere is mainly composed of hydrogen and helium with no clear lower boundary
(
Guillot 1999
)
.
In addition to the two main gases, the atmosphere also contains water, methane, ammonia, hydrogen sulfide and nitrogen.

Throughout the history of studying Jupiter, observations of its atmosphere and modelling were used together to provide a picture of the gas giant in an ever-increasing detail.
In recent years, the importance of understanding Jupiter’s chemistry has grown, because the planet represents a class of exoplanets known in extrasolar systems
(
Gratton et al. 2023
)
.
Naturally, the most efficient way of collecting planetary data is sending probes to observe the planet from close-up.
The first of these for Jupiter was the Voyager 1 spacecraft, which made a flyby in 1979 and measured the temperature profile, magnetic field and observed its moons.
Recent reanalysis by
Gupta et al. 2022
shows that thermal data from this probe are still valid today.

Another important and successful probe was the Galileo probe, launched in 1989, which was dedicated to analysis of the Jupiter’s atmosphere and moons.
Models preceding its launch include the work of
Prinn and Barshay 1977
, who propose that the presence of CO in what they call the ’visible’ atmosphere is due to rapid mixing from deeper atmospheric layers with temperature reaching 1100 K.
Furthermore,
Prinn and Olaguer 1981
, who proposed nitrogen and hydrogen reactions in Jupiter’s atmosphere as an observable prediction for the probe.
That theoretical paper studied reaction kinetics and aerosol catalysis to suggest that nitrogen should be stable in the deep atmosphere of Jupiter and should manifest in the ’upper visible regions’ in abundances 0.6 - 3 ppmv without catalysis or 10 ppmv with catalysis considered.
The probe later provided a range of new information on the giant planet, including the atmospheric pressure and temperature (
pT
) profile
(
Seiff et al. 1998
)
and built, alongside Earth-based observations, the foundation for many studies dealing with Jupiter’s chemistry.
These latter studies included photochemistry and carbon chemistry
Moses et al. 2005
, Jupiter’s stratospheric chemistry with focus on carbon species
(
Moses et al. 2003
)
,
\text{PH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
photochemistry
(
Visscher et al. 2009
)
, or the chemistry of
HCN

(
Moses et al. 2010
)
.
Hydrogen cyanide itself has received significant attention, e.g. in
Tokunaga et al. 1981
;
Lellouch et al. 2006
, and lately by
Cavalié et al. 2023b
who observed
HCN
and
CO
with the ALMA telescope and retrieved spatial distribution of those species on Jupiter.

One significant event in the history of Jupiter was the impact of comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 in 1994, which marked the first observation of an extraterrestrial collision in the Solar system
(
Crawford et al. 1994
;
Zahnle and Mac Low 1994
)
.
The impact altered the chemical composition of the surrounding atmosphere for years to come, manifested e.g. through new sulfur chemistry
(
Moses et al. 1995
)
, and altered
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}

(
Bézard et al. 2002
)
, or CO,
\text{CS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and COS
(
Lellouch et al. 1995
;
Lellouch et al. 1997
)
mixing ratios.

The most recent wave of information on Jupiter’s composition and chemistry was provided by the Juno spacecraft.
For example, observations by
Bolton et al. 2017
;
Li et al. 2017
show variable ammonia levels across the planet.
The formation of mushballs and subsequent rainout have been proposed as a possible explanation
(
Guillot et al. 2020b
;
Guillot et al. 2020a
;
Grassi et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Ingersoll et al. 2017
;
Stevenson 2020
;
Brown et al. 2018
)
.
Ammonia itself is an important precursor of
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{SH}
, which is expected to form on Jupiter and likely contributes to its orange colour
(
Loeffler and Hudson 2018
)
.
Its presence was also suggested in the Giant Red Spot by
Bjoraker et al. 2018
.
Juno has also significantly improved knowledge of Jupiter’s gravity, leading to the idea that Jupiter may have a diluted central concentration of heavy elements.
The mission has also revealed wind depth in the odd zonal harmonics of the gravity field and details about the magnetic field
(
Stevenson 2020
)
.

Combination of Juno, Galileo, Voyager and ground-based observational data provides constraints on the structure of the zonal jets
(
Kaspi et al. 2020
)
, water abundance
(
Li et al. 2020
)
, carbon species abundance
(
Sinclair et al. 2017
;
Sinclair et al. 2018
)
, depletion of alkali metals
(
Bhattacharya et al. 2023
)
, or the abundance of oxygen
(
Cavalié et al. 2023a
)
.
Such information can be used for example to constrain the formation location of Jupiter, which according to
Öberg and Wordsworth 2019
formed beyond the
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
snowline and then migrated towards the Sun.

Still more is expected from the upcoming JUICE mission in the 2030s
(
Fletcher et al. 2023
)
.
The main topics of this mission will include acquiring a global perspective on Jupiter’s atmospheric science, investigation of phenomena on timescale from minutes to months, and exploration of the atmosphere at high vertical resolution.
The mission will also help answer how the atmosphere is connected to the deep circulation and composition, which is currently an aspect of the Jupiter’s atmospheric research that relies heavily on assumptions about the deeper atmosphere.
Yet, it is at the same time understood that for example the distribution of ammonia is tied to deep atmosphere dynamics
(
Stevenson 2020
)
or that the distribution of carbon species is heavily dependent on the deep water content
(
Visscher et al. 2010
)
.

Most models of the Jupiter’s atmosphere focus on specific questions, such as the behaviour of a single species, constraint on a single parameter, or specific region.
Some notable works include
Ingersoll and Cuzzi 1969
, who showed that the light and dark bands on Jupiter have different temperatures.

Fegley and Lodders 1994
then assumed thermochemical equilibrium and a dry adiabatic thermal profile, and constructed a model of Jupiter and Saturn without any probe data.
In the same year,
Guillot et al. 1994
challenged the view that giant planets are adiabatic and fully convective below 4000 K.
The authors later reevaluated their work
(
Guillot et al. 2006
)
, and as discussed in
Freedman et al. 2008
, the use of correct pressure-broadened alkali opacities (not known at the time of the original publication) leads to the conclusion that the original fully convective hypothesis still holds, which goes on to show that the use of up-to-date data and models is vital for successful description of planetary atmospheres.
It is nowadays generally recognized that Jupiter’s deeper atmosphere is in thermodynamic equilibrium.
Many studies devoted to interior studies were performed, one of the latest being
Rensen et al. 2023
.
There, Juno data were used to model elemental and molecular abundances in the deeper atmosphere.
Other models, such as
Miguel et al. 2022
or
Howard et al. 2023
reveal important information about the distribution of some heavier elements and the structure of the planetary interior.
These papers use thermodynamic equilibrium codes, such as GGchem
(
Woitke et al. 2018
)
.
A significant advantage of these codes is the usage of Gibbs free energy minimization to calculate the equilibrium composition based on thermodynamic properties of selected species.
These calculations are fast and efficient and allow for the exploration of a large parameter space quickly.

On the other hand, as one progresses upwards, the atmosphere quickly departs from equilibrium, and kinetics and photochemistry become an important aspect
(
Moses 2014
)
.
For example,
(
Moses et al. 2011
)
discusses carbon, nitrogen and oxygen chemistry on two hot Jupiters and shows that disequilibrium processes enhance the presence of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
and
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
above equilibrium due to quenching.
Both species are then photochemically removed in the upper atmospheric layers.
Such disequilibrium chemistry also enhances the mixing ratios of unsaturated hydrocarbons (mainly
\text{C}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
), nitriles (HCN in particular), and radicals (OH,
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
,
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
).
Similarly,
Hu 2021
shows that photodissociation of
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
in presence of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
on temperate gas giant planets leads to increased mixing ratio of
HCN
.
At the same time, photodissociation of
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
in the presence of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
leads to CO and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and suppresses the synthesis of
HCN
.
They also show that
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
is photochemically removed from Jupiter’s atmosphere and once turned to
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
, the vast majority condenses out.
The effect of vertical winds and eddy diffusion on the distribution of ammonia is explored in another disequilibrium model of Jupiter’s deeper atmosphere and stratosphere that covers the same atmospheric extent as our model
(
Tsai et al. 2021
)
.

What all these studies show is that disequilibrium processes are very important in atmospheres of giant planets.
Moreover, lack of direct observations from the deeper atmosphere further cements the need for correct understanding of both thermochemistry and chemical kinetics in planetary atmospheres as deeper abundances of species are often constrained by observations in the upper atmospheric layers and subsequent modelling
(
Visscher et al. 2010
;
Wang et al. 2015
;
Wang et al. 2016
)
.

In this paper, we employ a 1D photochemical-thermochemical kinetics model ARGO to describe the chemistry in equilibrium and the disequilibrium processes in a combined code (Section
2
).
The model constructed in this paper is intended to bridge the existing gap between the deeper and upper regions of Jupiter’s atmosphere and provide a tool for the prediction of observable features in Jupiter’s atmosphere, many of which depend on deeper atmospheric properties.
The implemented gas phase chemistry is described in Section
3
, the ammonia and ammonium hydrosulfide are shown in Section
4
, the deep tropospheric chemistry is discussed in Section
5
, and the chemistry of HCN is described in Section
6
.

2
Chemical model

The basis of the code ARGO used in this paper was extensively described in
Rimmer and Helling 2016
.
In brief, it is a 1D photochemical-thermochemical code for atmospheric chemistry modelling.
The code combines photochemistry, kinetics, diffusion, condensation, evaporation and outgassing. The thermochemical portion of the code includes reverse reaction rate coefficients following a thermodynamic Gibbs free energy treatment.
The code was described in detail in
(
Rimmer and Helling 2016
)
.
Necessary inputs for this code include:

•

combined pressure (
p
, bar), temperature (
T
, K), and eddy diffusion coefficient (
K_{\mathrm{zz}}
, cm
2
s
-1
) profile

•

initial atmospheric composition at one of the boundaries, here at the bottom of the atmosphere

•

actinic flux at the top of the atmosphere

•

chemical network

At each atmospheric level, the code solves a set of coupled 1D continuity equations which describe the time-dependent atmospheric composition at that level:

\frac{\delta n_{i}}{\delta t}=P_{i}-L_{i}-\frac{\delta\Phi_{i}}{\delta{z}}

(1)

[cm

This flux includes both eddy (

Implementation of eddy diffusion and molecular diffusion is described in

2.1
Pressure-temperature profile

In this paper, we use a combined (
p,T
) profile, where the upper part from 6.7 bar (altitude -63.7 km) to the top is taken from
Moses et al. 2005
.
That profile reasonably agrees with a profile published by

The lower part of the (

The bottom of the profile temperature was chosen to be 1400 K, because below that the code has convergence issues and the atmosphere follows equilibrium.

For the calculation,

The specific heat capacity was estimated as:

2.2
Initial conditions

The initial conditions to this model are supplied as elemental abundances at the bottom of the (

) profile.
The elemental abundances of H, He, N, C, O and S were taken from
Rensen et al. 2023

\sim

The abundances were converted to mixing ratios and scaled to 1.
We also note that

We still choose to use their values because they are to most up-do-date values.

Since ARGO uses mixing ratios as initial conditions, the 5% margin was applied to the mixing ratios directly, i.e.,

n_{i}
is the mixing ratio of species

is the mixing ratio of that species + 5% margin.

The calculated values compare Jupiter’s deep atmosphere vs solar photosphere and therefore differ from what is commonly observed in the literature (e.g.

\nu_{j}
being positive for products and negative for reactants.
In the code, as well as in the database, there exist two sets of polynomial coefficients, one for temperatures up to 1000 K and the other for 1000 – 6000 K.
There exists a 15
th
coefficient in the Burcat database, which we do not use.
In our case, we use an expression for the true equilibrium constant, which we define as

\begin{split}K=\exp&\left(\Delta a_{1}(1-\ln T)+\frac{\Delta a_{2}T}{2}+\frac{\Delta a_{3}T^{2}}{6}\right.\\
&\left.+\frac{\Delta a_{4}T^{3}}{12}+\frac{\Delta a_{5}T^{4}}{20}+\frac{\Delta a_{6}}{T}+a_{7}\right)\end{split}

(11)

The 7 term polynomial coefficients for
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{SH}
that are used in this code are not available.
Instead, we calculate them using the available coefficients for
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{S}
and the expression for the equilibrium constant from
Lewis 1969
:

\ln K=14.82-\frac{4705}{T}

(12)

The constant in
Lewis 1969
is valid in the temperature range 180–300 K
(
Carlson et al. 1987
)
, and we extrapolate to the whole temperature range.
Also, this expression is derived for the reaction

{}{}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{4}}}\mathrm{SH}\hskip 0.86108pt{}{}\mathrm{(}\mathrm{s}\mathrm{)}{}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.89444pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.89444pt}{$\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\leftarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}$}}{}{}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}{}{}\mathrm{(}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{)}{}+{}{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{S}\hskip 0.86108pt{}{}\mathrm{(}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{)}

(13)

In this reaction,
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{SH}
forms in the solid phase.
Our approach adopts that but the code also includes condensation and evaporation (described below) which quickly establish equilibrium between the condensed and evaporated

.

The rate constant
k_{f}
for the reaction
8
was derived from the kinetic theory of gases.
From
Draine 2011
, for a reaction

{}\mathrm{A}{}+{}{}\mathrm{B}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}{}\mathrm{products}

(22)

the reaction rate per unit volume is

k=n_{A}n_{B}\langle\sigma v\rangle_{AB}

(23)

where
\langle\sigma v\rangle_{AB}
is the two-body collisional rate coefficient, and
n_{A}
and
n_{B}
are number densities of reactants A and B, respectively, in units [cm
-3
].
Then from the kinetic theory of gases, we take

E_{r}=\frac{3}{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T

(24)

where the energy of an ideal gas is proportional to the temperature of the gas and
k_{\mathrm{B}}
is the Boltzmann constant.
From
Libretexts Inc. 2022
, the collisional rate constant can be expressed as

\langle\sigma v\rangle_{AB}=\langle v_{\mathrm{r}}\rangle\sigma_{AB}e^{-\frac{E_{r}}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}}

(25)

where
v_{\mathrm{r}}=\vec{v_{\mathrm{a}}}-\vec{v_{\mathrm{b}}}
is the relative velocity of the colliding particles,
\sigma_{AB}
is the averaged sum of hard-sphere collision cross-sections of particles A and B in units [m
2
],
E_{r}
is the relative kinetic energy.
Since we consider gas with zero internal energy, the total energy can be expressed as the kinetic energy

E_{r}=\frac{1}{2}\mu v_{\mathrm{r}}^{2}

(26)

where
\mu
is the reduced mass of the reacting particles.
By comparing Eq.
24
and Eq.
26
, we obtain

v_{\mathrm{r}}=\sqrt{\frac{3k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\mu}}

(27)

and finally

\langle\sigma v\rangle_{AB}=\sqrt{\frac{3k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\mu}}\sigma_{AB}e^{-\frac{3}{2}}

(28)

The molar masses of
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{S}
,
M_{\mathrm{{}\mathrm{NH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}}}
and
M_{\mathrm{{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{S}}}
are 17.031 g mol
-1
and 34.08 g  mol
-1
, respectively.
Hence,

\mu=\frac{M_{\mathrm{H_{2}S}}M_{\mathrm{NH_{3}}}}{M_{\mathrm{H_{2}S}}+M_{\mathrm{NH_{3}}}}\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{A}}}=1.886\times 10^{-26}\mathrm{kg}

(29)

where
N_{\mathrm{A}}
is the Avogadro constant.

The kinetic diameters of
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{S}
are 260 pm and 360 pm, respectively
(
Matteucci et al. 2006
;
Breck 1973
)
, so
\sigma_{AB}
is then calculated as

\sigma_{AB}=\pi\left(\frac{d_{A}d_{B}}{2}\right)^{2}=3.019\times 10^{-15}\mathrm{cm}^{2}

(30)

A new reaction type was introduced in the code.
The reaction rate is calculated from

k=k_{f}n_{A}n_{B}

(31)

where the reaction rate constant,
k_{f}
, is calculated as:

k_{f}=c_{1}T^{c_{2}}N_{\mathrm{HTOT}}

(32)

where by comparison with Eq.
28

c_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{3k_{\mathrm{B}}}{\mu}}\sigma_{AB}e^{-\frac{3}{2}}=3.16\times 10^{-12}\mathrm{cm}^{3}\mathrm{s}^{-1}

(33)

c_{2}=\frac{1}{2}

(34)

and
N_{\mathrm{HTOT}}
is the number density of the atmosphere which accounts for the fact that the reaction in fact needs a third body to carry away excess energy.

The reverse reaction rate constant is calculated in a similar manner as described in
Rimmer and Helling 2016
and
Visscher and Moses 2011
.
In short, the expression for the rate constant is

k_{r}=k_{f}e^{\frac{-\Delta G_{\mathrm{r}}}{RT}}\left(RT\right)^{\Delta v}

(35)

where
\Delta G_{\mathrm{r}}
is the Gibbs free energy of the reverse reaction and
\Delta v=\sum_{j}v_{j}
, where
j
are the stoichiometric coefficients of the reaction, positive for products and negative for the reactants.

.
Similarly to their paper,
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OH}
formation is dominated by reaction

{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}{}+{}{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}{}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{OH}{}+{}{}\mathrm{H}

(40)

below the quench level.
However, our model shows that the quench level occurs at around 320 bar, where the formation is dominated by reaction

{}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{O}{}+{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}{}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{OH}{}+{}{}\mathrm{H}

(41)

and balanced by destruction from reaction

{}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{OH}{}+{}{}\mathrm{H}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}{}+{}{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}

(42)

Above that, we observe an increasing mixing ratio of methanol, again from reaction

{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}{}+{}{}{}\mathrm{H}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}\mathrm{O}{}\mathrel{\mathop{\makebox[0.0pt]{\rightarrow\displaystyle}}\limits}{}{}{}\mathrm{CH}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{3}}}\mathrm{OH}{}+{}{}\mathrm{H}

(43)

and then a stable quenched mixing ratio above 170 bar.
One possible difference in the models are different rate constants for the reactions in question.
Another possible explanation is a different vertical resolution of the model.
Our model uses a set vertical resolution 1 km, while
Visscher et al. 2010
use resolution of ”at least 20 altitude levels per scale height”.
At
\sim
450 bar, the scale height is
\sim
144 km, which means that with our resolution, we have 144 altitude levels per scale height.

Ammonia and ammonium hydrosulfide

The model was used to explore the chemistry of nitrogen in the Jupiter’s deeper atmosphere, the troposphere and the stratosphere.

The model contains the presence of ammonium hydrosulfide, a compound likely present on Jupiter and contributing to its orange-ish colour
(

.
As Figure

\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{SH}

Notably,
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{S}
does not condense by itself, but is consumed to produce
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{SH}
.
Its mixing ratios therefore also drops sharply at this level, but the condensate does not appear.
Since
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{S}
is the major sulfur-bearing species in the deeper atmosphere and since absolute majority of it is consumed to produce
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{SH}
, most of the sulfur is effectively trapped below the
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{SH}
condensation level, although some
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{S}
is again photochemically formed in the upper stratospheric layers around
10^{-7}-10^{-9}
bar.
On the contrary, although ammonia condenses at the
10^{0}-10^{1}
bar atmospheric level, it begins to photochemically form again at
5\times 10^{-5}
bar and reaches as much as
3.5\times 10^{-11}
mixing ratio at
2\times 10^{-7}
bar before being finally photochemically removed from the atmosphere.
The temperatures and mixing ratios of both
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{S}
and
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
are sufficiently low in the stratosphere that no formation of
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{SH}
is observed.
Similar reasoning is also employed in
Hu 2021
.

Bolton et al. 2017
present
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
mixing ratio map constructed from the initial results from the Juno spacecraft.
As they show in their Figure 3, ammonia abundance at
10^{2}
bar is around 340-360 ppm across all latitudes and this abundance decreases with the decreasing pressure up until its condensation level.
The decrease is different for different latitudes.
Near the equator, the mixing ratio is rather constant and explained by rapid mixing with deeper layers, pointing to and effect similar to the Hadley cell.
At higher latitudes, the decrease in ammonia mixing ratio is slower and smoother.
In all cases, condensation occurs around 0.7 bar.
Our model predicts ammonia mixing ratio at
10^{2}
bar 740 ppm, but rapidly decreasing with the decreasing pressure, down to 320 mbar at 1.55 bar where condensation starts to occur.
The behaviour of our model is therefore qualitatively similar to the observational data, although the condensation levels and the pre-condensation amounts are slightly different.

The most likely explanation is that the thermal profile needs to be updated down do deeper levels, although the measured Galileo probe data extend down to 6.7 bar.
Recently,
Gupta et al. 2022
have shown that Voyager radio occultation measurements give temperature at 1 bar up to 4 K different to the Galileo data with a possible variation as much as 7 K.
Differences in thermal profile would influence both the differences in ammonia abundance and condensation level, although chemical abundances at larger pressures would not be significantly affected
(
Rensen et al. 2023
)
.
Another possible explanation for the higher amount of ammonia is the fact that some reactions in the ARGO model are not reversed, as mentioned above.
A third possible explanation is that the deep atmosphere amount of N in this model is too high.

As discussed, the condensation level is predicted slightly deeper than was observed.
Condensation in this model is governed by the Antoine equation.

The condensation chemistry on Jupiter is complicated to simulate.
The condensate clouds probably include not only pure ices of

Bolton et al. 2017

A last, most exciting option is that there is an unknown chemical process in the atmosphere, which could explain the abundance.

Deep tropospheric chemistry

Figure

shows the mixing ratios in Jupiter’s deep atmosphere up to the troposphere.
In comparison with
Rensen et al. 2023
, our model predicts similar amounts of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
He
,
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{O}
,
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{S}
,
CO
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
- the main carriers of H, O, S and C in the deeper atmosphere.
The species behaviour in general is similar although the mixing ratios are slightly different.
Note that for graphical clarity, we did not plot the maximum and minimum values of our model.

There is a significant difference in the mixing ratio of
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
.
Our model predicts a relatively constant value of 11 ppm from the bottom of the atmosphere up to
10^{-6}
bar where it starts to drop off.
On the other hand,
Rensen et al. 2023
show a gradual decrease of
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
mixing ratio from
\sim 8.5\times 10^{-4}
at 1000 bar to below
10^{-10}
at a few bar level.
This is due to the fact that the authors use a thermochemical equilibrium model.
However, quenching maintains a relatively constant amount of nitrogen even in the higher atmosphere.
Quenching is a process where the timescale for vertical transport becomes important, because as temperature decreases, thermochemical reactions slow down to the point where vertical transport is faster, species stop reacting and remain at a constant level throughout the atmosphere above.
This is exactly the case of
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, which gradually stops converting to
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
(as it would in equilibrium) and remains at a fixed value
\sim
11ppm up until
10^{-6}
bar where it begins to photochemically decompose, making it an important disequilibrium species in the Jupiter’s atmosphere.
Similar behaviour for
\text{N}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
was described by
Moses et al. 2010
.

6
Hydrogen cyanide

One interesting outcome of our model is the formation of
HCN
in the stratosphere.

HCN
on Jupiter is a molecule of great interest, because it is a molecule which plays a role in prebiotic chemistry
(
Sutherland 2016
;
Ferus et al. 2017
)
, is a relevant tracer for nitrogen chemistry, can potentially be used to trace impacts
(
Rimmer et al. 2020
)
, and because its polymers may be contributing to the planet’s colour
(
Woeller and Ponnamperuma 1969
;
Matthews 1991
)
.

HCN

Tokunaga et al. 1981
detected the R-branch lines of the

column density
5\times 10^{-3}

The authors also claim that if condensation was considered in their work, the upper limit would increase to 2 ppb.
Most recently, these upper limits were refined by
Davis et al. 1997
to 0.93 ppb if
HCN
condenses in the upper troposphere or 0.16 ppb if it is uniformly mixed throughout the troposphere and stratosphere.
In 1994, the impact of comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 induced large changes in the Jupiter’s atmosphere concerning
HCN
.

HCN
quickly spread through the atmosphere which remains altered by the impact to this day
(
Moreno et al. 2003
;
Lellouch et al. 2006
)
.

Moses et al. 2010
model Jupiter’s tropospheric nitrogen chemistry in an attempt to explain its low observational upper limit.
At the same time, more recently,
Hu 2021
predict low (
~10^{-9}
) mixing ratios of
HCN
throughout Jupiter’s atmosphere.
They observe formation of
from

\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}

\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}

7
Conclusion

Jupiter’s deeper atmosphere, troposphere and stratosphere are for the first time combined in a single 1D chemical kinetics model.
The model was benchmarked and stands relatively well in comparison to existing region-specific models in the literature.
The model code is based on the ARGO code published in
Rimmer and Helling 2016
.
This current iteration of the code contains a new edition of the STAND reaction network with
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{SH}
chemistry as well as updates to Antoine equation parameters for condensation of
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{SH}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{S}
.
We discuss throughout the paper that thermal profile and initial conditions at the lower boundary are the most important effects on the resulting mixing ratios and quench levels of atmospheric species.
The formation and condensation of
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{SH}
from
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{S}
and
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
traps the majority of sulfur at the lower levels of the atmosphere.
Both ammonia and
\text{NH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}\text{SH}
condense and form cloud layer between 0.1 and 1 bar atmospheric level, likely contributing to the planet’s orange-ish colour.
Comparison with recent observations by Juno shows that our model best represents behaviour of the mixing ratio of ammonia near equatorial latitudes, where rapid mixing and massive updrafts are the dominant atmospheric effect, as compared to other latitudes, where heterogeneous chemistry, global circulation and aerosols play a more important part.
By comparison with thermochemical equilibrium model by
Rensen et al. 2023
, we also show the importance of quenching for the atmosphere.
Our model predicts
HCN
formation between
1\times 10^{-6}
and
6.76\times 10^{-8}
bar with a maximum mixing ratio of 33 ppb at
2.94\times 10^{-7}
and details the mechanism of its formation through radical chemistry and photochemistry.
This is an important prediction which can be tested by observations.
