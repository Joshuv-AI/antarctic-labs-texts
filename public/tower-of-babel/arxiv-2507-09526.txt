An order-theoretic characterization of JB-algebras
Mark Roelands, Samuel Tiersma

Abstract
We give an order-theoretic characterization of the JB-algebras among the complete order unit spaces in terms of the existence of an order-anti-automorphism of the interior of the cone that is homogeneous of degree − 1 -1 . More geometrically, we characterize JB-algebras as those complete order unit spaces for which the interior of the cone is a symmetric Banach–Finsler manifold under Thompson’s metric. Furthermore, we show that two order unit spaces are isomorphic if there exists a gauge-reversing bijection between them, thus answering a question raised by Noll–Schäfer. These results have previously been established for finite-dimensional resp. reflexive order unit spaces by Walsh and Lemmens–R.–Wortel.



Keywords:
Jordan algebras, JB-algebras, order unit spaces, order-anti-isomorphisms, gauge-reversing bijections, Thompson’s metric, symmetric Banach–Finsler manifolds

Subject Classification: 17C50, 58B20, 46B40, 17C36

Introduction

Jordan algebras form a fecund class of nonassociative real algebras. Originating in the work of P. Jordan, J. von Neumann, and E. Wigner
[
JvNW34
]
on the foundations of quantum mechanics in the 1930s, Jordan algebras have since been connected to a vast range of fields including optimization (cf.
[
AG03
]

[
Fay97
]
), statistics (cf.
[
Mal94
]
), complex and differential geometry (cf.
[
Koe99
]
,
[
Ber00
]
), Lie theory (cf.
[
FF77
]
,
[
Jac71
]
), and the study of symmetric spaces (cf.
[
Loo69
]
,
[
Kau02
]
,
[
Upm85
]
,
[
Sat80
]
). More specifically, we consider a unital Jordan algebra
A
which is simultaneously a Banach space for a norm satisfying
\lVert xy\rVert\leq\lVert x\rVert\lVert y\lVert
and
\lVert x\rVert^{2}\leq\lVert x^{2}+y^{2}\rVert
for all
x,y\in A
; such pairs
(A,\lVert\cdot\rVert)
are known as
JB-algebras
. The purpose of this paper is to establish a conjectured correspondence between JB-algebras and certain so-called order unit spaces.

An
order unit space

(V,V_{+},v)
is a partially ordered vector space
V
, with positive cone
V_{+}
, together with a choice of archimedean order unit
v\in V
, see
Section

2.4
. One defines intrinsically a norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v}
on
V
deemed the
order unit norm
. The
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v}
-interior of
V_{+}
is the open cone

V^{\circ}_{+}:=\{x\in V:x\geq\delta v\text{ for some }\delta>0\}.

If
A
is a JB-algebra with unit element
e
and one sets
A_{+}:=\{x^{2}:x\in A\}
, then the triple
(A,A_{+},e)
is an example of an order unit space. Conversely, the question how to characterize those order unit spaces that arise in this way from a JB-algebra has received sustained attention. We provide an answer based on the following observation. The
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{e}
-interior
A^{\circ}_{+}
of
A_{+}
consists precisely of the squares having a Jordan inverse. The Jordan inverse map
i\colon A^{\circ}_{+}\to A^{\circ}_{+}
,
i(x)=x^{-1},
is a bijection having the properties of being an order-anti-isomorphism and homogeneous of degree
-1
, that is
x\leq y\iff i(x)\geq i(y)
and
i(\lambda x)=\lambda^{-1}i(x)
for all
x,y\in A^{\circ}_{+}
and
\lambda>0
. This observation motivates the following general definition.

Let
(V,V_{+},v)
be an order unit space. A bijection
\Phi:V^{\circ}_{+}\to V^{\circ}_{+}
is called
gauge-reversing
if it is an order-anti-isomorphism and is homogeneous of degree
-1
. (For an alternate definition in terms of so-called
gauge functions
, see
Section

2.6
.)
Our main result is the following order-theoretic characterization of JB-algebras among the order unit spaces, which resolves
[
LvG18
, Conjecture 2]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(V,V_{+},v)
be a complete order unit space. Then there exists a structure of Jordan algebra on
V
such that
(V,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v})
is a JB-algebra with unit element
v
and cone of squares
V_{+}
if and only if there exists a gauge-reversing bijection
V^{\circ}_{+}\to V^{\circ}_{+}
.

We will prove this theorem as
Theorem

5.33
.

Let us now compare
Theorem

1.1
with other known characterizations of JB-algebras. This line of research has its origins in the Koecher-Vinberg theorem (
[
Koe57
]
and
[
Vin60
]
) asserting that a cone in a finite-dimensional real vector space corresponds to a JB-algebra (or what is equivalent, a Euclidean Jordan algebra
[
FK94
, Ch. III, Sec. 1; Ch. VIII, Sec. 4]
, or a formally real Jordan algebra
[
HOS84
, Cor. 3.1.7 and 3.3.8]
) if and only if the cone is
symmetric
, i.e. homogeneous and self-dual for some inner product. This result has been extended to characterize unital JH-algebras
[
Chu17
]
, which are precisely the reflexive JB-algebras (see
[
LRW25
, Thm. 2.8]
). A characterization of general, not necessarily reflexive, JB-algebras, however, cannot involve the existence of an inner product and has been obtained by Walsh.

Walsh has shown that an open cone in a finite-dimensional vector space is symmetric if and only if it admits a gauge-reversing self-bijection
[
Wal18
]
. Combined with the Koecher-Vinberg theorem, Walsh’s result proves
Theorem

1.1
in case
V
is finite-dimensional. This result has been extended to characterize spin factors
[
LRvI17
]
and JH-algebras
[
LRW25
]
, relying on the aforementioned extension of the Koecher-Vinberg theorem to infinite dimension by Chu. However, in the general case a different method is required to construct the algebra multiplication.

This method is provided by the foundational work of Springer on
j
-structures
[
Spr73
]
. He defined a
j
-structure
, roughly speaking, to be a birational involution on a finite dimensional vector space
V
that is homogeneous of degree
-1
and satisfies the weak Hua identity

j(v+x)+j(v+j(x))=v=j(v).

Springer showed that
j
-structures are equivalent to finite-dimensional Jordan algebras in characteristic
\neq 2
. McCrimmon adopted the definition of a
j
-structure to what he coined an
h
-structure
, which is required to satisfy a stronger form of Hua’s identity
[
McC77
, Sec. 3]
. It is this notion of an
h
-structure which we will implicitly use, although we will not use this term.

JB-algebras have also been studied from the perspective of symmetric spaces. In
[
KU77
]
and
[
BKU78
]
it has been shown that an open cone
C
in a Banach space has a JB-algebra structure (in an equivalent norm) if and only if the tube domain
T=V\oplus\mathrm{i}C\subset V^{\mathbb{C}}
over
C
admits a symmetry
s
, i.e. a holomorphic involution
s\colon T\to T
with an isolated fixed point, equivalently, if and only
T
is biholomorphic to a bounded symmetric domain.

One obtains a so-called
Finsler metric

\nu\colon C\times C\to[0,\infty)
on the open cone
C=V_{+}^{\circ}
of an order unit space
V
from a suitable assignment to each point in
C
of a norm on
V
which is equivalent to
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v}
. An example of an
\Aut(C)
-invariant Finsler metric on
C
is
Thompson’s metric

d_{T}\colon C\times C\to[0,\infty)
defined by

d_{T}(x,y):=\log\inf\{\lambda\geq 1:\lambda^{-1}y\leq x\leq\lambda y\}.

A
\nu
-symmetry
of
C
at a point
p\in C
is an involutive
\nu
-isometry having
p
as an isolated fixed point. We say that
(C,\nu)
is a
symmetric Banach–Finsler manifold
if there exists a
\nu
-symmetry around each point of
C
. It has been shown in
[
LRW25
, Thm. 3.5]
that any
d_{T}
-symmetry is a gauge-reversing bijection.

It is known that the open cone of each JB-algebra is a symmetric Banach–Finsler manifold. In fact, Chu has proved in
[
Chu21
]
that
V
corresponds to a JB-algebra if and only if there exist an
\Aut(C)
-invariant Finsler metric
\nu
and analytic
\nu
-symmetries turning
(C,\nu)
into a symmetric Banach–Finsler manifold. Without assuming any continuity or smoothness of the symmetries, we provide a new proof of Chu’s result in case
\nu=d_{T}
, thus answering in the affirmative
[
LvG18
, Conjecture 1]
. The following theorem follows from the preceding discussion and
Theorem

1.1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
V
be a complete order unit space and let
C=V_{+}^{\circ}
be its open cone. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

there exists a
d_{T}
-symmetry
S_{p}:C\to C
at some point
p\in C
;

(2)

we have that
(C,d_{T})
is a symmetric Banach–Finsler manifold;

(3)

there exists a JB-algebra structure on
(V,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v})
with cone of squares
V_{+}
.

We will prove this theorem as
Theorem

5.35
.

An overview of the proof of Theorem
1.1
will now be given. Throughout this paper, we work in the following, more general, context. We assume
(V,V_{+},v)
and
(W,W_{+},w)
are two complete order unit spaces, with open cones
C=V_{+}^{\circ}
and
D=W_{+}^{\circ}
, and that
\Phi\colon C\to D
is a gauge-reversing bijection.

In
Section

3
we define cones
C_{usc}
and
C_{lsc}
consisting of upper respectively lower semicontinuous, affine functions on the state space of
V
, which are obtained by adjoining to
C
all limits of decreasing respectively increasing nets in
C
. The map
\Phi\colon C\to D
is extended to a gauge-reversing bijection
\Phi_{usc}\colon C_{usc}\to D_{lsc}
. We establish that the cone
C_{usc}
is
strongly atomic
, i.e. each element in
C_{usc}
is the supremum of the extremal vectors that it dominates. In fact, we show that under
\Phi_{usc}
the extremal vectors of
C_{usc}
correspond to the pure states on
W
, whose ample existence is guaranteed by the Krein–Milman theorem.

In
Section

4
several consequences of strong atomicity of
C_{usc}
are deduced by scrutinizing the ordering. First, we show that
\Phi
is Gateaux-differentiable in all positive directions in
Theorem

4.7
. Second, we establish a strong form of Hua’s identity in
Theorem

4.10
. Third, we
reprove
[
NS77
, Theorem B]
asserting that a gauge-preserving bijection
\Psi\colon C\to D
extends to a linear isomorphism from
V
onto
W
in the special case that
C_{usc}
be strongly atomic in
Theorem

4.9
.

In
Section

5
we prove that
\Phi
is Fréchet-differentiable and that for each point
x\in C
the negative of the derivative at
x
defines an isomorphism
-D\Phi(x)\colon(V,V_{+},x)\to(W,W_{+},\Phi(x))
of order unit spaces. We show that
S_{x}:=-[D\Phi(x)]^{-1}\circ\Phi
is a
d_{T}
-symmetry of
C
at
x
. Specifically, we let
j:=S_{v}
be the prospective inverse map of the Jordan algebra structure on
V
with identity element
v
. We define the quadratic representation of an element
x\in C
by
P(x):=-[Dj(x)]^{-1}
and show that
P
extends to a quadratic polynomial map
P\colon V\to L(V)
. As a consequence we prove that every gauge-reversing bijection
\Phi\colon C\to D
is bianalytic. The map
P
is shown to satisfy an alternative axiomatization of JB-algebras in terms of the quadratic representation (see
Definition

2.41
), finishing the proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

As a byproduct of the proof, we answer in the affirmative a question raised by Noll and Schäfer
[
NS77
]
whether two possibly non-complete order unit spaces connected by a gauge-reversing bijection are necessarily isomorphic.

Theorem 1.3
.

If
V
and
W
are order unit spaces such that there exists a gauge-reversing bijection
\Phi\colon C\to D
, then
V
and
W
are isomorphic.

Once more, in case
V
and
W
are finite-dimensional this was shown by Walsh
[
Wal18
]
. We prove
Theorem

1.3
in
Section

5.8
as
Theorem

5.38
, by showing that a gauge-reversing bijection
\Phi\colon C\to D
extends to a gauge-reversing bijection
\hat{\Phi}\colon\hat{C}\to\hat{D}
, where
\hat{C}
and
\hat{D}
are the open cones of the norm completions of
V
resp.
W
. We have also included a version of
Theorem

1.1
for non-complete order unit spaces in that section.

Preliminaries

2.1
Differentiable maps

In this section, we recall the definition of Gateaux- and Fréchet-differentiable maps, and prove a chain rule for the Gateaux-derivative.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(X,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X})
and
(Y,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Y})
be Banach spaces. Let
F\subset X
be a subset and
f\colon F\to Y
a function.

(1)

Given a point
x\in F
and a vector
p\in X
, we say that
f
is
Gateaux-differentiable
at
x
in the direction
p
if for sufficiently small
\mu>0
we have
x+\mu p\in F
, and there is a vector
q\in Y
such that

\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\mu^{-1}\lVert f(x+\mu p)-f(x)-\mu q\rVert_{Y}=0.

Plainly, the vector
q
is unique if it exists, and called the
Gateaux-derivative
of
f
at
x
in the direction
p
. We emphasize that, due to the right limit in the definition, our notion of Gateaux-derivative is one-sided. Nonetheless, if
\lambda\geq 0
, then
\lambda q
is the Gateaux-derivative of
f
at
x
in the direction
\lambda p
.

(2)

Let
x
belong to the interior of
F
. We say that
f
is
Fréchet-differentiable
at
x
if there exists a continuous linear map
A\colon X\to Y
such that

\lim_{h\to 0}\lVert h\rVert_{X}^{-1}\lVert f(x+h)-f(x)-A(h)\rVert_{Y}=0.

Again, the linear map
A
is unique if it exists. We call
A
the
Fréchet-derivative
of
f
at
x
and denote it
Df(x)
. Note that if
f
is Fréchet-differentiable at
x
, then
f
is continuous at
x
.

We will use the following well-known chain rule for Fréchet- and Gateaux-derivatives.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
X,Y,Z
be Banach spaces. Let
f\colon F\to G
and
g\colon G\to Z
be functions, defined on subsets
F\subset X
and
G\subset Y
. Let
x\in F
and set
y:=f(x)\in G
.

(1)

If
f
is Fréchet-differentiable at
x
, and
g
is Fréchet-differentiable at
y
, then
g\circ f
is Fréchet-differentiable at
x
with Fréchet-derivative given by the chain rule

D(g\circ f)(x)=Dg(y)\circ Df(x).

(2)

Let
p\in X
,
q\in Y
and
r\in Z
be vectors such that
q
is the Gateaux-derivative of
f
at
x
in the direction
p
, and
r
is the Gateaux-derivative of
g
at
y
in the direction
q
. Assume additionally that
g
is Lipschitz on a neighbourhood of
y
in
G
. Then
r
is the Gateaux derivative of
g\circ f
at
x
in the direction
p
.

Proof.

Part (1) is standard, so we only show part (2).

Since
g
is Lipschitz on a neighborhood of
y
in
G
, there exist
\delta>0
and
L>0
such that
\lVert g(y_{1})-g(y_{2})\rVert_{Z}\leq L\lVert y_{1}-y_{2}\rVert_{Y}
whenever
y_{i}\in G
with
\lVert y_{i}-y\rVert_{Y}<\delta
for
i=1,2
. For
\mu>0
sufficiently small we have
\lVert\mu q\rVert_{Y}<\delta/2
and
\lVert f(x+\mu p)-f(x)-\mu q\lVert_{Y}<\delta/2
, hence
\lVert f(x+\mu p)-y\lVert<\delta
and
\lVert f(x)+\mu q-y\rVert<\delta
. Since
g
is
L
-Lipschitz on
\{y^{\prime}\in G:\lVert y^{\prime}-y\rVert_{Y}<\delta\}
, for sufficiently small
\mu>0
we have

\displaystyle\lVert g(f(x+\mu p))-g(f(x)+\mu q)\rVert_{Z}\leq L\lVert f(x+\mu p)-f(x)-\mu q\rVert_{Y}

and hence by the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\lVert(g\circ f)(x+\mu p)-(g\circ f)(x)-\mu r\rVert_{Z}

\displaystyle\leq L\lVert f(x+\mu p)-f(x)-\mu q\rVert_{Y}

\displaystyle\hskip 8.2511pt+\lVert g(f(x)+\mu q)-g(f(x))-\mu r\rVert_{Z}.

As
q
is the Gateaux-derivative of
f
at
x
in the direction
p
and
r
is the Gateaux-derivative of
g
at
y
in the direction
q
, it follows that
r
is the Gateaux-derivative of
g\circ f
at
x
in the direction
p
.
∎

2.2
Analytic maps

Let
(X,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{X})
and
(Y,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{Y})
be Banach spaces. The
norm
of a multilinear map
\phi\colon X^{k}\to Y
is given by

\lVert\phi\rVert:=\sup\{\lVert\phi(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})\rVert_{Y}:x_{i}\in X,\lVert x_{i}\rVert_{X}\leq 1\}\in[0,\infty].

We say
\phi
is
continuous
if
\lVert\phi\rVert<\infty
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. We say a map
c\colon X\to Y
is a
(continuous) homogeneous polynomial map of degree

k
if it is defined by a (continuous) symmetric multilinear map
\overline{c}\colon X^{k}\to Y
via

c(x):=\overline{c}(x,x,\ldots,x).

The map
\overline{c}
is uniquely determined by
c
according to the following formula, taken from
[
Boc70
, p. 274]
, which follows readily from the inclusion–exclusion principle:

\displaystyle\overline{c}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\epsilon_{1}=0,1\\
\cdots\cdots\\
\epsilon_{k}=0,1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{k-\epsilon_{1}-\ldots-\epsilon_{k}}c\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\epsilon_{i}x_{i}\right).

(1)

Definition 2.4
.

A map
f\colon U\to Y
defined on a neighborhood
U
of a point
x\in X
is said to be
analytic at
x
if there exist
r>0
with
\{z\in X:\lVert z-x\rVert_{X}<r\}\subset U
and for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
a continuous homogeneous polynomial map
c_{k}\colon X\to Y
of degree
k
such that
\limsup_{k}\lVert\overline{c_{k}}\rVert^{1/k}\leq r^{-1}
and for all
h\in X
with
\lVert h\rVert_{X}<r
one has

f(x+h)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_{i}(h).

(2)

We say
f
is
analytic
if
f
is analytic at each point of its domain
U
.

Many classical results about analytic functions of a single variable extend to the Banach space setting, among which we record for later reference the identity principle.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
U
be a connected open subset of the Banach space
X
, and let
f,g\colon U\to Y
be analytic maps. Suppose there exists a nonempty open subset
U_{0}
of
U
such that
f(x)=g(x)
for each
x\in U_{0}
. Then
f
and
g
are identically equal on
U
.

Proof.

See
[
Whi65
, Corollary, p. 1080]
.
∎

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
B
be a Banach algebra with identity. Then the set
G:=\{b\in B:b\text{ is invertible}\}
is open and the inverse map
G\to G
,
b\mapsto b^{-1}
is analytic.

Proof.

This is shown in
[
Con90
, Theorem VII.2.2]
.

2.3
Semi-continuous functions

In this section we recall some facts concerning (upper or lower) semicontinuous functions, referring to
[
Bee93
]
for proofs.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
X
be a topological space.

(1)

A function
g\colon X\to[-\infty,\infty)
is called
upper semicontinuous
if for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
the set
g^{-1}([-\infty,\lambda))
is open in
X
.

(2)

A function
h\colon X\to(-\infty,\infty]
is called
lower semicontinuous
if for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
the set
h^{-1}((\lambda,\infty])
is open in
X
.

(3)

A net
(g_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
of functions
g_{\alpha}\colon X\to(-\infty,\infty]
is called
almost nondecreasing
if for every
\epsilon>0
there exists an index
\alpha_{0}
such that for all indices
\alpha\geq\alpha^{\prime}\geq\alpha_{0}
one has
g_{\alpha}\geq g_{\alpha^{\prime}}-\epsilon
.

(4)

A net
(h_{\beta})_{\beta}
of functions
h_{\beta}\colon X\to[-\infty,\infty)
is called
almost nonincreasing
if
(-h_{\beta})_{\beta}
is an almost nondecreasing net.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
X
be a topological space.

(1)

Let
(g_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
be an almost nonincreasing net which consists of upper semicontinuous functions
g_{\alpha}\colon X\to[-\infty,\infty)
. Then the function
g\colon X\to[-\infty,\infty)
defined

is upper semicontinuous and equal to the pointwise limit of the net

.

(2)

Let
(h_{\beta})_{\beta}
be an almost nondecreasing net which consists of lower semicontinuous functions
h_{\beta}\colon X\to(-\infty,\infty]
. Then the function
h\colon X\to(-\infty,\infty]
defined by
h(x):=\limsup_{\beta}h_{\beta}(x)
is lower semicontinuous and equal to the pointwise limit of the net
(h_{\beta})_{\beta}
.

Proof.

See
[
Bee93
, Theorem 1.3.1]

∎

.

Let

(1)

Let

(2)

2.4
Order unit spaces

In this section we introduce order unit spaces and state Kadison’s theorem giving a functional representation of complete order unit spaces as
Theorem

2.11
. We conclude with a discussion of norm completions.

Let
V_{+}
be a proper cone in a real vector space
V
, i.e.
V_{+}+V_{+}\subset V_{+}
and
\lambda V_{+}\subset V_{+}
for all
\lambda\geq 0
, as well as
V_{+}\cap-V_{+}=\{0\}
. A partial order
\leq
on
V
is defined by

x\leq y\iff y-x\in V_{+}.

We call such a pair
(V,V_{+})
a
partially ordered vector space
. It is called
archimedean
if for every
x\in V
the set
\{\lambda x:\lambda\geq 0\}
has an upper bound only if
x\leq 0
. An element
v\in V_{+}
is called an
order unit
if for every
x\in V
there exists
\lambda>0
such that
x\leq\lambda v
. A triple
(V,V_{+},v)
is called an
order unit space
if
(V,V_{+})
is an archimedean partially ordered vector space with order unit
v
.

Let
(V,V_{+},v)
be an order unit space. Its
order unit norm
is the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v}
on
V
defined by

\lVert x\rVert_{v}:=\{\inf\lambda>0:-\lambda v\leq x\leq\lambda v\}.

Its
open cone
is the
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v}
-interior
V^{\circ}_{+}
of
V_{+}
, given by

V^{\circ}_{+}=\{x\in V:\epsilon v\leq x\text{ for some }\epsilon>0\}.

The elements of
V^{\circ}_{+}
are precisely the order units of the partially ordered vector space
(V,V_{+})
. If
v^{\prime}\in V^{\circ}_{+}
, then the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v^{\prime}}
equivalent to
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v}
. We call
(V,V_{+},v)
a
complete order unit space
if
(V,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v})
is a Banach space.

Example 2.10
.

Let
K
be a compact convex subset of a locally convex space
E
. We write
A(K)
for the real linear space of continuous affine functions
a\colon K\to\mathbb{R}
. We give
A(K)
the pointwise ordering, so that
A(K)_{+}=\{a\colon K\to[0,\infty)\text{ affine continuous}\}
. The triple
(A(K),A(K)_{+};\mathbbm{1}_{K})
is a complete order unit space, where
\mathbbm{1}_{K}:K\to\mathbb{R}
is identically equal to
1
.

We now recall a fundamental theorem of Kadison, stating that each complete order unit space is isomorphic to
A(K)
for a certain compact convex subset
K
of
V^{*}
, called the state space.

Let
(V,V_{+},v)
be an order unit space. Its
state space
is the set
K
of positive linear functionals
\rho\colon V\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\rho(v)=1
. Since each state is
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v}
-continuous,
K
is a subset of the continuous dual
V^{*}
of
(V,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v})
. We endow
V^{*}
with the wk*-topology. It follows from the Banach–Alaoglu theorem that
K
is a wk*-compact convex subset of
V^{*}
.

Theorem 2.11

(Kadison)
.

Let
(V,V_{+},v)
be a complete order unit space. For
x\in V
define
\tilde{x}\colon K\to\mathbb{R}
by
\tilde{x}(\rho):=\rho(x)
for all
\rho\in K
. Then there exists an isomorphism of order unit spaces

\displaystyle(V,V_{+},v)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}(A(K),A(K)_{+},\mathbbm{1}_{K}),

(3)

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto\tilde{x}.

Proof.

We refer to
[
Alf71
, Theorem II.1.8]
.
∎

Remark 2.12
.

If
V
is a, possibly non-complete, order unit space, then the assignment
x\mapsto\tilde{x}
defines an isometric isomorphism of
V
onto a norm dense subspace of
A(K)
, namely the space

consisting of all functions on

that extend to a continuous real-valued affine function on

(see again
[

, Theorem II.1.8]
). In particular, the norm completion
\hat{V}
of

is identified as
\hat{V}\cong A(K)
.

Of fundamental importance in the theory of compact convex sets is the Krein–Milman theorem, ensuring the existence of extremal points. Recall that if
L
is a convex subset of a vector space
E
, then an
extremal point of

L
is a point
\rho\in L
such that for all
0<\lambda<1
and
\sigma,\tau\in L
with
\rho=(1-\lambda)\sigma+\lambda\tau
one has
\sigma=\tau=\rho
, i.e.,
\rho
is not a proper convex combination of points in
L
different from
\rho
. The set of extremal points of
L
is denoted
\delta_{e}L
.

Theorem 2.13

(Krein–Milman)
.

Let
L
be a compact convex subset of a locally convex space. Then
L
is the closed convex hull of the set
\delta_{e}L
of extremal points of
L
.

Proof.

We refer to
[
Con90
, Theorem V.7.4]
.
∎

An extremal point of the state space
K
of an order unit space
(V,V_{+},v)
is called a
pure state
. So, the set of pure states of
V
is denoted
\partial_{e}K
. It follows from the Krein–Milman theorem that if
a,a^{\prime}\in A(K)
agree on
\delta_{e}K
, then

holds; see
Lemma

3.8
for a more general statement.

We conclude with a lemma on the completion of an order unit space, which will be applied in
Section

.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
(V,V_{+},v)
be an order unit space. Let
\hat{V}
be the completion of the normed space
(V,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v})
, and let
\hat{V}_{+}
be the closure of
V_{+}
in
\hat{V}
. Then
(\hat{V},\hat{V}_{+},v)
is a complete order unit space and the order unit norm on
\hat{V}
coincides with the extension of the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v}
on
V
to its completion.

Proof.

By
Remark

2.12
, we may assume that
V=A(K,E)
for some locally convex space
E
and compact convex subset
K
of
E
, so that
\hat{V}=A(K)
. Let us verify that the closure of
A(K,E)_{+}
inside
A(K)
equals
A(K)_{+}
. Plainly the closure of
A(K,E)_{+}
is contained in
A(K)_{+}
. Conversely, let
x\in A(K)_{+}
. Since
A(K,E)
is norm dense in
A(K)
we can find a sequence
(x_{n})_{n}
in
A(K,E)
converging to
x
in
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v}
, i.e. uniformly on
K
. Therefore, the sequence
(\rho_{n})_{n}
defined by
\rho_{n}:=\inf_{K}x_{n}
converges to
\inf_{K}x\geq 0
. It follows that the sequence
(y_{n})_{n}
defined by
y_{n}:=x_{n}+\max(0,-\inf x_{n})v
belongs to
A(K,E)_{+}
and converges to
x+\max(0,-\inf_{K}x)v=x
. We conclude that
x
is contained in the closure of
A(K,E)_{+}
, as desired.

The final assertion about coincidence of the two norms on
\hat{V}_{+}
holds because both norms agree with the supremum norm on
A(K)
.
∎

In the forthcoming section, we will identify
x
with
\tilde{x}
and write
x(\rho)
instead of
\tilde{x}(\rho)=\rho(x)
, for
x\in V
and
\rho\in K
.

2.5
Gauges and Thompson’s metric

Let
(V,V_{+},v)
be an order unit space. On the open cone
C=V^{\circ}_{+}
there exists a natural metric
d_{T}
called Thompson’s metric. We will show that
d_{T}
is locally bi-Lipschitz to the metric associated with the order unit norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v}
. Moreover, we define gauge functions, which measure or gauge how small or large one element of
C
is compared to another.

In the following definition, observe that since
C
consists of the order units of
(V,V_{+})
, for any two
x,y\in C
there exists
\lambda>0
such that
x\leq\lambda y
.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
(V,V_{+},v)
be an order unit space and
C=V_{+}^{\circ}
its open cone.

(1)

We define
Thompson’s metric

d_{T}\colon C\times C\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by

d_{T}(x,y)=\inf\{\lambda>0:x\leq\lambda y\text{ and }y\leq\lambda x\}.

(4)

(2)

We define the gauge-functions
L\colon C\times C\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
M\colon C\times C\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
by

m(x,y):=\sup\{\lambda>0:\lambda y\leq x\}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0},

(5)

and

M(x,y):=\inf\{\mu>0:x\leq\mu y\}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}.

(6)

In terms of the gauge functions, Thompson’s metric is given by

d_{T}(x,y)=\log\max\{M(x,y),M(y,x)\}.

(7)

One deduces that
d_{T}
satisfies the triangle inequality and is nonnegative from the inequality

M(x,z)\leq M(x,y)M(y,z),

(8)

valid for all
x,y,z\in C
.

Example 2.16
.

Using Kadison’s
Theorem

2.11
, we may view elements
x,y\in C
as continuous functions from the state space
K
to
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Their quotient
x/y\colon K\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
\psi\mapsto x(\psi)/y(\psi)
is a continuous function on
K
as well. The minimum and maximum values of
x/y
over
K
are given by the gauge function values
m(x,y)
resp.
M(x,y)
, that is

m(x,y)=\min_{\psi\in K}\frac{x(\psi)}{y(\psi)}\text{ and }M(x,y)=\max_{\psi\in K}\frac{x(\psi)}{y(\psi)}.

(9)

The gauge functions have the following elementary properties.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
x,y\in C
and
\lambda>0
. Then

\displaystyle m(y,x)

\displaystyle=M(x,y)^{-1},

(10)

\displaystyle M(\lambda x,y)

\displaystyle=\lambda M(x,y)

(11)

\displaystyle M(x,\lambda y)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}M(x,y)

(12)

\displaystyle m(y,\lambda x)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}m(y,x)

(13)

\displaystyle m(\lambda y,x)

\displaystyle=\lambda m(y,x)

(14)

\displaystyle d_{T}(\lambda x,\lambda y)

\displaystyle=d_{T}(x,y).

(15)

Proof.

Left to the reader.
∎

Lemma 2.18
.

Let
\lambda>0
. For all
x,y\in C
with
x,y\geq\lambda^{-1}v
we have

d_{T}(x,y)\leq\lambda\lVert x-y\rVert_{v}.

Proof.

For each state
\psi
on
(V,v)
we have, because
y(\psi)\geq m(y,v)\geq\lambda^{-1}
, that

\frac{x(\psi)}{y(\psi)}=1+\frac{x(\psi)-y(\psi)}{y(\psi)}\leq 1+\frac{\lVert x-y\rVert_{v}}{m(y,v)}\leq 1+\lambda\lVert x-y\rVert_{v}.

This shows that
M(x,y)\leq 1+\lambda\lVert x-y\rVert_{v},
and the same holds with
x
and
y
interchanged. We conclude that

d_{T}(x,y)\leq e^{d_{T}(x,y)}-1=\max\{M(x,y),M(y,x)\}-1\leq\lambda\lVert x-y\rVert_{v}.

∎

Lemma 2.19
.

Let
\lambda>0
. For all
x,y\in C
with
x,y\leq\lambda v
we have

\lVert x-y\rVert_{v}\leq\lambda d_{T}(x,y).

Proof.

Let
\psi
be a state on
(V,v)
. We have
y(\psi)\geq e^{-d_{T}(x,y)}x(\psi)
because
e^{d_{T}(x,y)}y\geq M(x,y)y\geq x
. Using that
1-e^{-a}\leq a
for each
a\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain

x(\psi)-y(\psi)\leq(1-e^{-d_{T}(x,y)})x(\psi)\leq\lambda d_{T}(x,y).

By interchanging
x
and
y
we find that
|x(\psi)-y(\psi)|\leq\lambda d_{T}(x,y)
. The desired inequality follows since
\psi
is an arbitrary state on
(V,v)
.
∎

As a consequence of the two preceding lemmata, Thompson’s metric
d_{T}
and the metric associated with the order unit norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v}
are bi-Lipschitz with constant
\lambda
on the Thompson’s metric ball
\{x\in V:d_{T}(v,x)\leq e^{\lambda}\}=[\lambda^{-1}v,\lambda v]
around
v
of radius
e^{\lambda}
for each
\lambda>0
, hence these metrics are locally bi-Lipschitz. In particular,
d_{T}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v}
induce the same topology on
C
.

2.6
Gauge-reversing bijections

In this section we define gauge-preserving bijections and gauge-reversing bijections. We show they are isometries for Thompson’s metric and, consequently, are locally bi-Lipschitz with respect to the order unit norms. Moreover, we introduce
d_{T}
-symmetries, and state that these are gauge-reversing bijections.

Definition 2.20
.

Let
(X,\leq)
and
(Y,\leq)
be partially ordered sets, and let
\Phi\colon X\to Y
be a map. We say that
\Phi
is
order-preserving
(resp.
order-reversing
) if for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
with
x_{1}\leq x_{2}
one has
\Phi(x_{1})\leq\Phi(x_{2})
(resp.
\Phi(x_{1})\geq\Phi(x_{2})
). We say
\Phi
is an
order-isomorphism
(resp.
order-anti-isomorphism
if
\Phi
is bijective and both
\Phi
and
\Phi^{-1}
are order-preserving (resp. order-reversing).

In the remainder of this section,
(V,V_{+},v)
and
(W,W_{+},w)
denote order unit spaces, and

and

their open cones.

Definition 2.21
.

Let
\Phi\colon C\to D
be a bijection. We say that
\Phi
is

(resp.

) if for all

m(\Phi(x),\Phi(y)=m(y,x)

(16)

and

m(\Phi(x),\Phi(y))=m(x,y).

(17)

Since the functions

and

are multiplicatively inverse by (
31
), one arrives at an equivalent definition if condition (
16
) is replaced by
M(\Phi(x),\Phi(y))=M(x,y)
and condition (
17
) by
M(\Phi(x),\Phi(y))=M(y,x)
.

.

For a bijection
\Phi\colon C\to D
, the following statements are equivalent:

is gauge-reversing;

(2)

\Phi

See
[

, Lemma 2.5]

∎

Proposition 2.23
.

(2)

Theorem 2.24
.

Let

Proposition 2.25
.

Each gauge-reversing or gauge-preserving bijection

Proof.

Suppose first that

Definition 2.26
.

We define a

.

Let

Proposition 2.28
.

Let

. For all

(18)

. Likewise, since

Theorem 2.29
.

2.7
Jordan algebras

In this section we first define classical, i.e. linear, Jordan algebras. We then provide an alternative axiomatic framework devised by McCrimmon, and show each quadratic Jordan algebra is a linear Jordan algebra. The theory in this section is purely algebraic and is valid over any field of characteristic
\neq 2
with at least four elements. However, for simplicity we work over the field of real numbers
\mathbb{R}
. Throughout this paper, all our (Jordan) algebras are assumed to have an identity element.

Definition 2.30
.

A
Jordan algebra
is a real linear space
A
equipped with a commutative, but not necessarily associative,
\mathbb{R}
-bilinear product that satisfies the so-called
Jordan-identity

\displaystyle x\circ(y\circ x^{2})

\displaystyle=(x\circ y)\circ x^{2}

(J)

and has a unit element
e
, i.e.
x\circ e=x
, for all
x,y\in A
.

Example 2.31
.

Let
(A,\cdot)
be an associative real algebra with identity
e
. Define on
A
the following
Jordan product

\circ
by

x\circ y:=\tfrac{1}{2}(x\cdot y+y\cdot x).

Then
(A,\circ)
is a Jordan algebra with identity element
e
.

For a real vector space
V
, we denote
L(V)
the real algebra of
\mathbb{R}
-linear endomorphisms of
V
.

Definition 2.32
.

Let
(A,\circ)
be a Jordan algebra, and let
x\in A
be an element.

(1)

We define the
linear representation
of
x
to be the endormorphism
T(x)\in L(A)
given by

T(x)(y):=x\circ y=y\circ x.

(2)

The
quadratic representation
of
x
is defined in terms of the linear representation as

U(x):=2T(x)^{2}-T(x^{2})\in L(A).

(19)

The map
T\colon A\to L(A)
is linear, but the quadratic representation map
U\colon A\to L(A)
is a quadratic map in the sense of the following definition.

Definition 2.33
.

Let
A
and
B
be real vector spaces. We say a map
U\colon A\to B
is
quadratic
if it is homogeneous of degree
2
, i.e.
U(\lambda x)=\lambda^{2}U(x)
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in A
, and the map
U:A\times T\to B
defined by

U(x,y):=\tfrac{1}{2}(U(x+y)-U(x)-U(y))\in B

is bilinear.

Since
U(x,e)y=x\circ y
for all
x,y\in A
, the bilinear product
x\circ y
can be reconstructed from the quadratic product
U(x)y=2x\circ(x\circ y)-x^{2}\circ y
. In fact, McCrimmon has expressed the axioms for a Jordan algebra in terms of the quadratic representation.

Definition 2.34
.

A
(unital) quadratic Jordan algebra
is a triple
(A,U,e)
consisting of a real vector space
A
, a vector
e\in A
and a quadratic map
U\colon A\to\End(A)
satisfying the following axioms. Let
U(\cdot,\cdot)\colon A\to\End(A)
be the symmetric bilinear map given by
U(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}(U(x+y)-U(x)-U(y))
. Then for all
x,y,z\in A
, we impose that

\displaystyle U(e)

\displaystyle=\Id_{A},

(QJ1)

\displaystyle U(x)U(y,z)x

\displaystyle=U(U(x)y,x)z,

(QJ2)

\displaystyle U(U(x)y)

\displaystyle=U(x)U(y)U(x).

(QJ3)

If
A
is a classical Jordan algebra with unit element
e
and quadratic map
U:A\to L(A)
, then
(A,U,e)
is a quadratic Jordan algebra, as is shown in
[
Jac69
, §1.2]
. Conversely, a quadratic Jordan algebra
(A,U,e)
gives rise to a classical Jordan algebra with identity element
e
and quadratic representation map
U
. This result is proved in
[
Jac69
, §1.3 and §1.4]
, among a list of other identities. To make our exposition more self-contained, we reproduce his derivation in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.35
.

Let
(A,U,e)
be a quadratic Jordan algebra. Then
A
is a Jordan algebra with unit element
e
in the product
a\circ b:=U(a,b)e
.

Proof.

It is clear that the product
\circ
is bilinear and commutative. For every
a\in A
set

T(a)=U(a,e)\in\End(A).

Take
x=e
in (QJ2) to find using (QJ1) that

T(y)z=U(y,e)z=U(y,z)e=y\circ z,

(20)

so
T(y)
is the operation of multiplication by
y
. In particular,
T(e)=U(e,e)=U(e)=\Id_{A}
, so
e
is the multiplicative identity element. Moreover,
U(x)e=U(x,x)e=x\circ x=:x^{2}
.

Next we show that for every
a\in A
we have

U(a)=2T(a)^{2}-T(a^{2}).

Let
\lambda
be real number, and take
x=a+\lambda e
and
y=e
in (QJ3). This gives, since
U(e)=\id_{A}
, that

U(a+\lambda e)^{2}=U((a+\lambda e)^{2}),

which expands, using (QJ1) again, as

(U(a)+2\lambda U(a,e)+\lambda^{2}\Id_{A})^{2}=U(a^{2}+2\lambda a+\lambda^{2}e).

Comparison of coefficients at
\lambda^{2}
gives

2U(a)+4U(a,e)^{2}=4U(a)+2U(a^{2},e).

Dividing by
2
and substitute
T(a)=U(a,e)
and
T(a^{2})=U(a^{2},e)
to obtain
U(a)=2T(a)^{2}-T(a^{2})
.

Finally, we prove the Jordan identity by showing that
T(x)
and
T(x^{2})
commute. Take
z=e
in (QJ2) to find using (
20
) that

U(x)U(x,e)y=U(x)U(y,e)x=U(U(x)y,x)e=U(x,e)U(x)y,

i.e.
U(x)
and
U(x,e)=T(x)
commute. Since we have shown that
T(x^{2})=2T(x)^{2}-U(x)
, it follows that
T(x)
and
T(x^{2})
commute.
∎

If
A
and
B
are Jordan algebras, then a linear map
\phi:A\to B
is a Jordan homomorphism if and only if
\phi(1_{A})=1_{B}
and
U_{B}(\phi(x))\circ\phi=\phi\circ U_{A}(x)
.
It follows that the categories of Jordan algebras and quadratic Jordan algebras are equivalent (see also
[
Jac69
, §1.4]
).

Definition 2.36
.

An element
x\in A
is called
invertible
if its quadratic representation
U(x)
is invertible in the Banach algebra
L(A)
.

Remark 2.37
.

This notion of invertibility agrees with the one commonly used in the theory of linear Jordan algebras, where an element
x\in A
is called invertible if there exists
y\in A
such that
x\circ y=e
and
x^{2}\circ y=x
. Indeed, such element
y
exists if and only if
U(x)
is invertible, in which case
y=U(x)^{-1}
. The element
x^{-1}:=U(x)^{-1}x
is called the
inverse
of
x
, and has quadratic representation
U(x^{-1})=U(x)^{-1}
.

2.8
JB-algebras

In this section we define JB-algebras, which are Jordan algebras with a compatible norm. The compatibility conditions are customarily stated in terms of the linear product, however, may equivalently be formulated in terms of the quadratic representation operators. This observation allows to present a definition of JB-algebra referencing only the quadratic product.

Definition 2.38
.

A
JB-algebra
is a Jordan algebra
(A,\circ,e)
that is simultaneously a Banach space in a norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
satisfying for all
x,y\in A
the conditions

\displaystyle\lVert x\circ y\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert x\rVert\lVert y\rVert,

(21)

\displaystyle\lVert x^{2}\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert x\rVert^{2},

(22)

\displaystyle\lVert x^{2}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert x^{2}+y^{2}\rVert.

(23)

It follows from (
22
) that
\lVert e\rVert=1
provided
A\neq 0
. Each JB-algebra is an order unit space per the following proposition.

Proposition 2.39
.

Let
A
be a JB-algebra with norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
and identity element
e
.

(1)

The set
A_{+}:=\{x^{2}:x\in A\}
of squares in
A
is the positive cone of an order unit space
(A,A_{+})
with order unit
e
. Moreover, the order unit norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{e}
coincides with the given norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
.

(2)

For every
x\in A
the map
U(x):A\to A
is positive and has norm
\lVert U(x)\rVert=\lVert x\rVert^{2}_{e}
.

Proof.

For (1) see
[
HOS84
, Lemma 3.3.7 and 3.3.10]
; for
(2) see
[
AS03
, Thm. 1.25]
.
∎

The following theorem provides a converse.

Theorem 2.40
.

Let
(A,A_{+},e)
be a complete order unit space which is simultaneously a Jordan algebra that has
e
as its identity element. Suppose for each
x\in A
, the quadratic representation
U(x)\in L(A)
of
x
is positive and has
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{e}
-operator norm
\lVert U(x)\rVert_{e}=\lVert x\rVert^{2}
. Then
A
is a JB-algebra in the order unit norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{e}
with cone of squares
A_{+}=\{x\in A:x^{2}\}
.

Proof.

From
[
AS01
, Lemma 1.80]
it follows that
\lVert x\circ y\rVert_{e}\leq\lVert x\rVert_{e}\lVert y\rVert_{e}
for all
x,y\in A
and that all squares are positive. Conversely, by [ibidem, Lemma 1.73] each positive element is a square. Since
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{e}
is monotone on
A_{+}=\{x^{2}:x\in A\}
, we have
\lVert x^{2}\rVert_{e}\leq\lVert x^{2}+y^{2}\rVert_{e}
for all
x,y\in V
. Finally, according to [ibidem, Theorem 1.81] we have
\lVert x^{2}\rVert_{e}=\lVert x\rVert_{e}^{2}
for each
x\in A
. We have shown that the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{e}
satisfies (
21
), (
22
) and (
23
). We conclude that
A
is a JB-algebra in the order unit norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{e}
with cone of squares
A_{+}
.
∎

Theorem

2.35
,
Proposition

2.39
and
Theorem

2.40
combine to show the equivalence of the standard definition of JB-algebras encountered in the literature, to the following alternative definition, which we will employ exclusively in the remainder of this article.

Definition 2.41
.

A
JB-algebra
is a complete order unit space
(A,A_{+},e)
together with a quadratic map
U\colon A\to L(A)
satisfying (QJ1-QJ3) such that for every
x\in A
the quadratic representation
U(x)\in L(A)
is positive and has
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{e}
-operator norm
\lVert U(x)\rVert_{e}=\lVert x\rVert^{2}_{e}
.

The only if direction of
Theorem

1.1
is a known result in the theory of JB-algebras.

Theorem 2.42
.

Let
(A,A_{+},e,U)
be a quadratic JB-algebra, and let
\Omega:=A_{+}^{\circ}
be its open cone. For
x\in\Omega
denote
i(x):=x^{-1}(=U(x)^{-1}x)
the inverse of
x
. Then the map
i\colon\Omega\to\Omega
is Fréchet-differentiable with derivative

Di(x)=-U(x)^{-1}.

Moreover,
i
is an involutive gauge-reversing bijection satisfying
i(e)=e
and
Di(e)=-\Id_{A}
.

Proof.

The Fréchet-derivative of
i
is determined in
[
FK94
, Prop. II.3.3(i)]
for Euclidean Jordan algebras; the proof is also valid for JB-algebras.

It is clear that
i
is involutive and homogeneous of degree
-1
. In
[
AS03
, Lemma 1.31]
it is shown that
i
is order-reversing. Since
i=i^{-1}
this entails that
i
is an order-anti-isomorphism. We conclude that
i
is an involutive gauge-reversing bijection. Finally,
U(e)=\Id_{A}
yields that
i(e)=U(e)^{-1}e=e
and
Di(e)=-U(e)^{-1}=-\Id_{A}
.
∎

3
Extended cones of semicontinuous affine functions

It will be useful to extend the given gauge-reversing bijection to certain larger cones of semicontinuous affine functions. We will introduce these cones in
Section

3.1
, and construct the extension in
Section

3.2
. The technical advantage of these extended cones is that they possess a wealth of extremal structure, as will be discussed in
Section

3.3
.

3.1
Extension of the cones

Throughout this section, we let
(V,V_{+},v)
be a complete order unit space. We let
K
be its state space, always considered in the wk*-topology. Recall Kadison’s
Theorem

2.11
stating that there exists an isomorphism
(V,V_{+},v)\cong(A(K),A(K)_{+},\mathbbm{1}_{K})
sending
x\in V
to the function
\tilde{x}:K\to\mathbb{R}
given by
\tilde{x}(\rho):=\rho(x)
. We will identify each element
x\in V
with the affine continuous function
\tilde{x}
on
K
it defines, thus dropping the tilde in
\tilde{x}
. Hence, we will write
x(\rho)
for the value
\rho(x)=\tilde{x}(\rho)
.

We denote the open cone by
C:=V_{+}^{\circ}
. Since
C=\{x\in V:x\geq\delta v\text{ for some }\delta>0\}
and
K
is compact, we have

C=\{a\colon K\to(0,\infty)\text{ affine continuous}\}.

The set of all
[0,\infty]
-valued affine functions on
K
is denoted

A(K)_{+}^{\infty}:=\{g\colon K\to[0,\infty]\text{ affine}\}.

We endow
A(K)_{+}^{\infty}
with the pointwise ordering given by
a\leq b
if
a(\rho)\leq b(\rho)
for every
\rho\in K
.
We remark that
A(K)_{+}^{\infty}
is a
monotone complete
partially ordered set: each nonincreasing net in
A(K)^{\infty}_{+}
has an infimum, and each nondecreasing net in
A(K)_{+}^{\infty}
has a supremum.

Let us also define a transitive relation
\prec
on
A(K)^{\infty}_{+}
by
a\prec b
if
a(\rho)<b(\rho)
for every
\rho\in K
. Note that for
a,b\in A(K)_{+}
we have
a\prec b
if and only if
b-a\in C
, whereas
a\leq b
if and only if
b-a\in A(K)_{+}
.

Moreover, on
A(K)_{+}^{\infty}
we define a pointwise addition and multiplication by scalars in
[0,\infty]
, using the convention that
0\cdot\infty=0
. In this way,
A(K)_{+}^{\infty}
becomes a semimodule over the semiring
[0,\infty]
.

We now introduce two cones in
A(K)_{+}^{\infty}
containing
C
, which will figure prominently in the sequel:

C_{usc}:=\{g\colon K\to[0,\infty)\mid\text{ affine upper semicontinuous}\}

(24)

and

C_{lsc}:=\{h\colon K\to(0,\infty]\mid\text{ affine lower semicontinuous}\}.

(25)

We remark that
C_{lsc}
is closed under addition and multiplication by scalars in
(0,\infty]
and that
C_{usc}
is closed under addition and multiplication by scalars in
[0,\infty)
. Furthermore, we have that

C_{usc}\cap C_{lsc}=\{g\colon K\to(0,\infty)\text{ affine continuous}\}=C.

Example 3.1
.

Let
F
be a nonempty closed face of
K
. Define the function
I^{\infty}_{F}\colon K\to[0,\infty]
by

I^{\infty}_{F}(\rho)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\rho\in F,\\
\infty&\text{if }\rho\not\in F.\end{cases}

(26)

We have
I^{\infty}_{F}\in C_{usc}
with
\min I^{\infty}_{F}=1
. In particular, if
\psi\in\delta_{e}K
is a pure state, then
I^{\infty}_{\{\psi\}}\in C_{usc}
.

Remark 3.2
.

It would have been equivalent to define
C_{usc}
and
C_{lsc}
as cones of affine semicontinuous functions on the dual cone of
V_{+}
. Indeed, let
V^{*}_{+}
be the dual cone of
V_{+}
in
V^{*}
, consisting of all positive linear functions on
V
, so that
K=\{\rho\in V^{*}_{+}:\rho(v)=1\}
. Restriction defines an order- and
[0,\infty]
-semimodule-isomorphism

\displaystyle\{\tilde{g}\colon V^{*}_{+}\to[0,\infty]\text{ affine}\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}A(K)^{\infty}_{+},

(27)

\displaystyle\tilde{g}

\displaystyle\mapsto\tilde{g}|_{K},

(28)

because each
g\in A(K)^{\infty}_{+}
has a unique affine extension
\tilde{g}\colon V^{*}_{+}\to[0,\infty]
given by

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(\rho)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }\rho=0,\\
\rho(v)g(\rho(v)^{-1}\rho)&\text{if }\rho\neq 0.\end{cases}

Moreover,
g
is upper or lower semicontinuous if and only if
\tilde{g}
is so, whence the isomorphism (
27
) given by
\tilde{g}\mapsto\tilde{g}|_{K}
restricts to order-isomorphisms

\{\tilde{g}:V^{*}_{+}\to[0,\infty)\text{ affine upper semicontinuous}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}C_{usc}

and

\{\tilde{g}:V^{*}_{+}\to(0,\infty]\text{ affine lower semicontinuous}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\to}}C_{lsc}.

It follows from this alternative description of
C_{usc}
and
C_{lsc}
, that if
v^{\prime}\in C
is a second order unit and
C^{\prime}_{lsc}
and
C^{\prime}_{usc}
are the cones defined by (
25
) and (
24
) but with
K
replaced by the state space
K^{\prime}:=\{\rho\in V^{*}_{+}:\rho(v^{\prime})=1\}
of
(V,V_{+},v^{\prime})
, then there exist canonical cone isomorphisms
C_{usc}\to C^{\prime}_{usc}
and
C_{lsc}\to C^{\prime}_{lsc}
given by
g\mapsto\tilde{g}|_{K^{\prime}}
which respect all structure.
In conclusion, the cones
C_{usc}
and
C_{lsc}
are independent of the choice of the order unit
v\in C
for
(V,V_{+})
.

Our next theorem shows that
C_{lsc}
is the closure of
C
inside
A(K)_{+}^{\infty}
under the formation of suprema of nondecreasing nets. Similarly,
C_{usc}
consists precisely of the infima of nonincreasing nets in
C
. We will deduce these approximation results from two lemmata.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
K
be the state space of a complete order unit space
(V,V_{+},v)
. Consider an affine upper semicontinuous function
g\colon K\to[-\infty,\infty)
, and set

\mathfrak{A}:=\{a\in A(K):a\succ g\}.

(1)

For all
a_{1},a_{2}\in\mathfrak{A}
there exists
a\in\mathfrak{A}
with
a\leq a_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.

(2)

For every
\rho\in K
we have
g(\rho)=\inf\{a(\rho):a\in\mathfrak{A}\}
.

Proof.

Part (1) follows from
[
Alf71
, Cor. I.1.4]
and part (2) follows from [ibidem, Cor. I.1.2].
∎

We now make a remark about our language concerning nets. Let
E
be a nonempty subset of
A(K)^{\infty}_{+}
such that for any two
h_{1},h_{2}\in E
there exists
h\in E
with
h\geq h_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Then the inclusion map
E\to A(K)^{\infty}_{+}
is a nondecreasing net indexed by the directed partially ordered set
(E,\leq)
. By abuse of language, we will speak of the nondecreasing net
E
. Similarly, if
D
is a nonempty subset of
A(K)^{\infty}_{+}
such that for any two
g_{1},g_{2}\in D
there exists
g\in D
with
g\leq g_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, then we will view
D
as nonincreasing net indexed by itself.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(V,V_{+},v)
be a complete order unit space, with open cone
C:=V_{+}^{\circ}
.

(1)

Each nonincreasing net in
C_{usc}
has an infimum in
C_{usc}
, which is given pointwise.

(2)

Each nondecreasing net in
C_{lsc}
has a supremum in
C_{lsc}
, which is given pointwise.

Proof.

(1) Note that the empty set (or net) has infimum
0
in
C_{usc}
. Let
(g_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
be a nonempty nonincreasing net in
C_{usc}
, and denote by
g:K\to[0,\infty)
its pointwise infimum, which is given by
g(\rho)=\inf_{\alpha}g_{\alpha}(\rho)
for each
\rho\in K
. Then
g
is affine and upper semicontinuous by
Lemma

2.8
(1), whence
g\in C_{usc}
. It is then clear that
g
is the infimum of the net
(g_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
in
A(K)^{\infty}_{+}
, hence in
C_{usc}
.

(2) This is shown in a similar way using
Lemma

2.8
(2).
∎

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
(V,V_{+},v)
be a complete order unit space, with open cone
C:=V_{+}^{\circ}
.

(1)

Each
g\in C_{usc}
is the pointwise infimum of the nonincreasing net
\{a\in C:g\prec a\}
.

(2)

Each
h\in C_{lsc}
is the pointwise supremum of the nondecreasing net
\{b\in C:b\prec h\}
.

Proof.

(1) In
Lemma

3.3
we have seen that
g
is the pointwise infimum of the nonincreasing net
\mathfrak{A}:=\{a\in A(K):a\succ g\}
. Now for
a\in A(K)
we have that
a\succ g
and
g\geq 0
imply
a\succ 0
. Therefore
\mathfrak{A}\subset C
and the proof is complete.

(2) Let
\mathfrak{B}:=\{b\in A(K):b\prec h\}
. Applying
Lemma

3.3
(1) with
g=-h
yields that for all
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathfrak{B}
there exists
b\in\mathbb{B}^{\prime}
with
b\geq b_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Since
0\in\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}
holds, the subnet
\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}:=\{b\in C:b\prec h\}=\{b\in A(K):0\prec b\prec h\}
is cofinal in
\mathfrak{B}
. Therefore by part (2) of that theorem,
h(\rho)=\sup\{b(\rho):b\in\mathfrak{B}\}=\sup\{b(\rho):b\in\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}\}
for every
\rho\in K
.
∎

Part (1) of the following lemma asserts that any nonincreasing net in
C_{usc}
with infimum, say
g\in C_{usc}
, is cofinal in a suitable sense with respect to the net
\mathfrak{A}=\{x\in C:x\prec g\}
considered in the previous. Part (2) contains the analogous cofinality assertion for a nondecreasing net in
C_{lsc}
. The lemma is a version of Dini’s theorem for semicontinuous functions.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
(V,V_{+},v)
be a complete order unit space, with open cone
C:=V_{+}^{\circ}
.

(1)

Let
(g_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
be any nonincreasing net in
C_{usc}
with infimum
g\in C_{usc}
. Let
f\in C
with
f\succ g
. Then there exist an index
\alpha_{0}
and
\epsilon>0
with
g_{\alpha_{0}}\prec f-\epsilon
.

(2)

Let
(h_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
be any nondecreasing net in
C_{lsc}
with supremum
h\in C_{lsc}
. Let
f\in C
with
f\prec h
. Then there exist an index
\alpha_{0}
and
\epsilon>0
with
h_{\alpha_{0}}\succ f+\epsilon
.

Proof.

(1) Since
f
is continuous, the functions
g_{\alpha}-f
and
g-f
are upper semicontinuous. From
g\prec f
it follows by
Lemma

2.9
(1) that
\max(g-f)<0
, so there exists
\epsilon>0
with
g-f\prec-\epsilon
. For every index
\alpha
the set
U_{\alpha}:=\{\rho\in K:g_{\alpha}(\rho)-f(\rho)<-\epsilon\}
is open since
U_{\alpha}=(g_{\alpha}-f)^{-1}((-\infty,\epsilon))
. We claim that
(U_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is an open cover of
K
. Indeed, because for each
\rho\in K
we have that
\inf_{\alpha}(g_{\alpha}(\rho)-f(\rho))=g(\rho)-f(\rho)<-\epsilon
, there exists an index
\alpha
such that
g_{\alpha}(\rho)-f(\rho)<-\epsilon
, that is,
\rho\in U_{\alpha}
. By compactness of
K
we can find finitely many indices
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
such that
K=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}U_{\alpha_{i}}
. Since the net
(g_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is downward directed, there exists an index
\alpha_{0}
such that for each
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
we have
g_{\alpha_{i}}\geq g_{\alpha_{0}}
, in particular,
U_{\alpha_{i}}\subset U_{\alpha_{0}}
. It follows that
K=U_{\alpha_{0}}
. We conclude that
g_{\alpha_{0}}\prec f-\epsilon
, as desired.

(2) This is shown analogously to part (1).
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
K
be the state space of the complete order unit space
(V,V_{+},v)
.

(1)

Let
g\colon K\to[-\infty,\infty)
be affine and upper semicontinuous. Then there exists a pure state
\psi\in\delta_{e}K
such that
\psi(g)=\sup g
.

(2)

Let
h\colon K\to(-\infty,\infty]
be affine and lower semicontinuous. Then there exists a pure state
\psi\in\delta_{e}K
such that
\psi(h)=\inf h
.

Proof.

(1) Let
F=\{\rho\in K:g(\rho)=\sup g\}
.
Lemma

2.9
asserts that
g
attains its supremum, so
F
is nonempty. Moreover,
F
is a face of
K
. Because
F=g^{-1}([\sup g,\infty))
and
g
is upper semicontinuous,
F
is closed. We conclude that
F
is a nonempty, closed face of
K
. The Krein–Milman
Theorem

2.13
gives that
F
is the closed convex hull of
\delta_{e}F
; in particular
\delta_{e}F\neq\emptyset
. Since
F
is a face of
K
, we have
\delta_{e}K\cap F=\delta_{e}F\neq\emptyset,
as was to be shown.

(2) Just like in part (1) one shows that
F=\{\rho\in K:h(\rho)=\inf h\}
is a nonempty, closed face of
K
, which therefore contains an extremal point of
K
.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
h,h^{\prime}\colon K\to(-\infty,\infty]
be lower semicontinuous affine functions. If
h(\psi)\leq h^{\prime}(\psi)
for each pure state
\psi\in\delta_{e}K
, then
h\leq h^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Theorem

3.5
furnishes an increasing net
(h_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
of continuous affine functions on
K
such that
h(\rho)=\sup_{\alpha}h_{\alpha}(\rho)
for each
\rho\in K
. Now by replacing
h
with an arbitrary member
h_{\alpha}
of the net, which again satisfies
h_{\alpha}(\psi)\leq h^{\prime}(\psi)
for each
\psi\in\delta_{e}K
, we may and do assume that
h
is continuous.

The function
h_{0}=h^{\prime}-h
is affine lower semicontinuous and satisfies
h_{0}(\psi)\geq 0
for all
\psi\in\delta_{e}S
. It follows from
Lemma

3.7
(2) that
\inf h_{0}\geq 0
, whence
h^{\prime}\geq h
.
∎

We close this section by extending the gauge functions
L
and
M
defined in
Definition

2.15
on
C
to
A(K)^{\infty}_{+}
, and establishing some of their elementary properties.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
x,y\in A(K)_{+}^{\infty}\setminus\{0,\infty\}
. We define the quantities

\displaystyle m(y,x):=\sup\{\lambda\in[0,\infty):\lambda x\leq y\}\in[0,\infty),

(29)

and

\displaystyle M(x,y):=\inf\{\mu\in(0,\infty]:x\leq\mu y\}\in(0,\infty].

(30)

Note that

m(y,x)=M(x,y)^{-1}

(31)

which is understood to include the assertion that
m(y,x)=0\iff M(x,y)=\infty
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
x,y\in A(K)_{+}^{\infty}
such that
x\succ 0
. If
\lambda\in[0,m(x,y))
then
x-\lambda y\succ 0
.

Proof.

It is to be shown for every
\rho\in K
that
x(\rho)-\lambda y(\rho)>0
. If
y(\rho)=0
then we have
x(\rho)-\lambda y(\rho)=x(\rho)>0,
since
x\succ 0
. Suppose next that
y(\rho)>0
. Then
m(x,y)y\leq x
implies
x(\rho)\geq m(x,y)y(\rho)
. Therefore
x(\rho)-\lambda y(\rho)\geq(m(x,y)-\lambda)y(\rho)>0
, as
m(x,y)-\lambda>0
by assumption.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
x,y\in A(K)_{+}^{\infty}
with
0\neq x\neq\infty
and
y\prec\infty
. Let
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
,
\beta\in[0,\infty)
and
\gamma\in[0,\alpha m(x,y))
. Then we have

\displaystyle M(\alpha x+\beta y,x)

\displaystyle=\alpha+\beta M(y,x),

(32)

\displaystyle m(\alpha x+\beta y,x)

\displaystyle=\alpha+\beta m(y,x),

(33)

\displaystyle m(\alpha x-\gamma y,x)

\displaystyle=\alpha-\gamma M(y,x),

(34)

\displaystyle M(\alpha x-\gamma y,x)

\displaystyle=\alpha-\gamma m(y,x).

(35)

Proof.

We content ourselves with proving formula (
34
), because the other formulae are obtained by similar arguments. Since
0\neq x\neq\infty
we have
m(\alpha x,x)=\alpha
so (
34
) holds if
\gamma=0
. Now if
0<\gamma<\alpha m(x,y)=m(\alpha x,y)
, then
\alpha x-\gamma y\in A(K)^{\infty}_{+}
by
Lemma

3.10
. Now for
\lambda\in[0,\alpha/\gamma]
we have

\alpha-\gamma\lambda\leq m(\alpha x-\gamma y,x)\iff(\alpha-\gamma\lambda)x\leq\alpha x-\gamma y\iff y\leq\lambda x\iff\lambda\geq M(y,x).

Using (the contrapositive of) this equivalence and the fact that
m(\alpha x-\gamma y,x)\leq m(\alpha x,x)=\alpha
and
\alpha/\gamma>m(x,y)^{-1}=M(y,x),
we conclude that

\displaystyle m(\alpha x-\gamma y,x)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\alpha-\gamma\lambda:\lambda\in[0,\alpha/\gamma],\alpha-\gamma\lambda>m(\alpha x-\gamma y,x)\}

\displaystyle=\alpha-\gamma\sup\{\lambda\in[0,\alpha/\gamma]:\lambda<M(y,x)\}

\displaystyle=\alpha-\gamma M(y,x),

which is this formula (

3.3

Definition 3.16
.

Let

x=\sup\{p\in\ext E\colon p\leq x\}\text{ in }E.

We continue with our assumption that
(V,V_{+},v)
and
(W,W_{+},w)
be complete order unit spaces, with state spaces

resp.

, and open cones

and

. The set of extreme vectors

Let

(1)

(2)

Proof.

As to (1)

The equivalence (2)

Recall that we defined

as the set of pure states in

Let

\displaystyle p

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\{\lambda p\colon 0\leq\lambda\leq 1\}}}

Theorem 3.19
.

Let
\Phi\colon C\to D
be a gauge-reversing bijection. Then
\Phi
is convex, i.e. for all

and
\lambda\in[0,1]
one has

\Phi((1-\lambda)x_{1}+\lambda x_{2})\leq(1-\lambda)\Phi(x_{1})+\lambda\Phi(x_{2}).

(43)

Proof.

Let
\psi\in\delta_{e}S
be a pure state of
W
. We will show that the function
\psi\circ\Phi\colon C\to(0,\infty)
is convex.
Proposition

3.18
grants us an extremal vector
p\in\ext_{v}(C_{usc})
such that
\psi(\Phi(z))=M(p,z)=m(z,p)^{-1}
for every
z\in C
. It suffices to show that the map
m(\cdot,p)\colon C\to(0,\infty)
is concave, because the inverse of a strictly positive concave function is convex.

We claim that
m(\cdot,p)
is homogeneous of degree

and superadditive. Plainly, for all
\lambda>0
and

we have
m(\lambda z,p)=\lambda m(z,p)
. Let now

. Then
m(z_{1},p)p\leq z_{1}
and
m(z_{2},p)p\leq z_{2}
hold, so
(m(z_{1},p)+m(z_{2},p))p\leq z_{1}+z_{2}
, whence
m(z_{1},p)+m(z_{2},p)\leq m(z_{1}+z_{2},p)
. This proves our claim. It follows that the function

is concave, since

m((1-\lambda)x_{1}+\lambda x_{2},p)\geq m(1-\lambda)x_{1},p)+m(\lambda x_{2},p)=(1-\lambda)m(x_{1},p)+\lambda m(x_{2},p).

Consequently,
\psi\circ\Phi=m(\cdot,p)^{-1}

\psi\in\delta_{e}S
was arbitrary, we conclude that
\Phi
is convex by

3.8
.
∎

Theorem 3.20
.

Let
(V,V_{+},v)
and
(W,W_{+},w)
be complete order unit spaces such that there exists a gauge-reversing bijection
\Phi\colon C\to D
. Then the cone
C_{usc}
is strongly atomic.

Proof.

Let us show that condition (3) of
Lemma

3.17
is satisfied. Let
g,g^{\prime}\in C_{usc}
be such that
m(g,p)\leq m(g^{\prime},p)
, equivalently
M(p,g)\geq M(p,g^{\prime})
, for each

. Set

and
h^{\prime}=\Phi_{usc}(g^{\prime})
. Let
\psi\in\delta_{e}S
be arbitrary. By
Proposition

3.18
there exists a unique

such that
\Phi_{usc}(p)=I^{\infty}_{\{\psi\}}
. Moreover, according to (
42

and

.
It follows that

Now

Corollary 3.21
.

Let
y,z\in C_{usc}
. Then

\lVert y-z\rVert_{v}\leq\sup\{|m(y,p)-m(z,p)|:p\in\ext_{v}C_{usc}\}.

Denote the supremum on the right by
\nu
. We claim that

. Indeed, for each

4
Consequences of strong atomicity

In
Theorem

3.20
of the previous section we have established that the extended cone
C_{usc}
is strongly atomic. Each of the three subsections of this section derives a distinct consequence of strong atomicity of
C_{usc}
. In the first subsection we prove that the gauge-reversing bijection
\Phi\colon C\to D
is Gateaux-differentiable in positive directions, and that the negative of the Gateaux derivative at a point
x\in C
extends to an isomorphism
(V,V_{+},x)\cong(W,W_{+},\Phi(x))
. In the second subsection, we give an alternative proof of
Theorem

2.24
, stating that a gauge-preserving bijection
\Psi\colon C\to D
extends to a linear isomorphism between

and

, under the additional assumption of strong atomicity of
C_{usc}
. Finally, in the third subsection, we prove a strong form of Hua’s identity, which provides an explicit formula for
D\Phi
in terms of
\Phi
.

4.1

In this section we will show that the gauge-reversing bijection
\Phi_{usc}\colon C_{usc}\to D_{lsc}
extending

has a (one-sided) Gateaux derivative at every point

in each direction

is order-reversing. We show that the map

.

4.2
Noll-Schäfer theorem for strongly atomic cones

Let
(V,V_{+},v)
and
(W,W_{+},w)
be complete order unit spaces. By a theorem of Noll and Schäfer,
[
NS77
, Theorem B]
, if
\Psi\colon C\to D
is a gauge-preserving bijection with
\Psi(v)=w
, then
\Psi
extends to an isomorphism of order unit spaces
(V,V_{+},v)\cong(W,W_{+},w)
. To make our exposition more self-contained, we will give an independent proof of this theorem under the assumption that the cone
C_{usc}
be strongly atomic. Note that by
Theorem

3.20
the latter condition is satisfied if there exists a gauge-reversing map
\Phi\colon C\to D
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
(V,V_{+},v)
and
(W,W_{+},w)
be complete order unit spaces, and suppose that
C_{usc}
is strongly atomic. Let
\Psi\colon C\to D
be a gauge-preserving bijection with
\Psi(v)=w
. Then
\Psi
extends to an isomorphism of order unit spaces
(V,V_{+},v)\cong(W,W_{+},w)
.

Proof.

Let
\Psi_{usc}\colon C_{usc}\to D_{usc}
be the gauge-preserving bijection extending
\Psi
that has been constructed in
Proposition

3.15
. We will show that
\Psi_{usc}(x+y)=\Psi_{usc}(x)+\Psi_{usc}(x)
for all
x,y\in C_{usc}
.

First assume that
x\in C
. For each
p\in\ext C_{usc}
there exists
q\in\ext D_{usc}
such that for each
\lambda\geq 0
one has
\Psi_{usc}(x+\lambda p)=\Psi(x)+\lambda q
. Then for
\lambda\geq 0
and
\mu>0
, we have

x+\lambda p\leq x+\mu y\iff\Psi(x)+\lambda q=\Psi_{usc}(x+\lambda p)\leq\Psi_{usc}(x+\mu y).

It follows that

m(y,p)=m(\mu^{-1}(\Psi_{usc}(x+\mu y)-\Psi(x)),q).

Since the left-hand side does not depend on
\mu
, neither does the right-hand side. By
Corollary

3.21
we conclude that for all
\mu>0
one has

\mu^{-1}(\Psi_{usc}(x+\mu y)-\Psi(x))=\Psi_{usc}(x+\mu y)-\Psi(x).

(53)

Now if
x\in C_{usc}
is arbitrary, we may take a nonincreasing net
(x_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
in
C
with infimum
x
to see that (
53
) holds for all
x,y\in C_{usc}
and
\mu>0
. We obtain that

\displaystyle\Psi_{usc}(x+y)

\displaystyle=\mu^{-1}\Psi_{usc}(\mu x+\mu y)

\displaystyle=\mu^{-1}(\Psi_{usc}(\mu x+\mu y)-\Psi_{usc}(\mu x))+\mu^{-1}\Psi_{usc}(\mu x)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{usc}(\mu x+y)-\Psi_{usc}(\mu x)+\Psi_{usc}(x).

Taking the infimum over all
\mu>0
gives that

\Psi_{usc}(x+y)=\Psi_{usc}(x)+\Psi_{usc}(y).

We conclude that
\Psi_{usc}
is additive on
C_{usc}
, so in particular
\Psi
is additive on
V_{+}
.

Since
V=V_{+}-V_{+}
there exists a unique linear map extending
\Psi
, which we denote
\Psi\colon V\to W
as well. Similarly,
\Psi^{-1}\colon D\to C
extends to
\Psi^{-1}\colon W\to V
. It is clear that
\Psi^{-1}\circ\Psi=\id_{V}
and
\Psi\circ\Psi^{-1}=\id_{W}
, so
\Psi
is a linear bijection. Moreover,
\Psi(C)=D
implies that
\Psi(V_{+})=W_{+}
. We conclude that
\Psi\colon(V,V_{+},v)\to(W,W_{+},w)
is an isomorphism of order unit spaces.
∎

4.3
Hua’s identity

The principal result of this subsection is that every gauge-reversing bijection
\Phi
automatically satisfies Hua’s identity. This identity will play a central role in all subsequent subsections. As a corollary we obtain a formula for the Gateaux-derivative
D\Phi
of
\Phi
solely in terms of
\Phi
itself, which will be instrumental in establishing higher-order smoothness properties of
\Phi
.

Theorem 4.10

(Hua’s identity)
.

Let
\Phi\colon C\to D
be a gauge-reversing bijection between the open cones of complete order unit spaces
(V,V_{+},v)
resp.
(W,W_{+},w)
. Then for all
x,y\in C
we have

\Phi(x)-\Phi(x+y)=-D\Phi(x)(\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(x)+\Phi(y))).

(54)

Proof.

In view of
Remark

3.2
, we may and do assume that
x=v
and
\Phi(x)=w
, so our objective becomes to show the equation

w-\Phi(v+y)=-D\Phi(v)(\Phi^{-1}(w+\Phi(y))).

Both members reside in
D
, since
w-\Phi(v+y)\geq w-\Phi(v+m(y,v)v)=(1-(1+m(y,v))^{-1})w
and because
-D\Phi(v):(V,V_{+},v)\to(W,W_{+},w)
is an isomorphism of order unit spaces by
Theorem

4.7
.

By
Theorem

3.20
it suffices to prove for each
q\in\ext_{w}D_{usc}
that

m(w-\Phi(v+y),q)=m({-D\Phi(v)}(\Phi^{-1}(w+\Phi(y))),q).

(55)

Let
p\in\ext_{v}C_{usc}
and
q\in\ext_{w}D_{usc}
be a pair of extremal vectors corresponding to one another as in
Theorem

4.3
(1), i.e. such that for each
\lambda>0
we have

\Phi(v+\lambda p)=w-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+1}q.

(56)

To calculate the left-hand side of (
55
), observe that for each
\lambda>0
it holds that

\lambda p\leq y\iff v+\lambda p\leq v+y\iff w-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+1}q\geq\Phi(v+y)\iff\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+1}q\leq w-\Phi(v+y).

Because we defined
m(y,p)=\sup\{\lambda>0:\lambda p\leq y\}
, this shows that

m(w-\Phi(v+y),q)=\frac{m(y,p)}{m(y,p)+1}.

We now turn to the right-hand side of (
55
). One sees from (
56
) that the Gateaux-derivative of
\Phi_{usc}
at
v
in the direction of
p
equals
D\Phi_{usc}(v)(p)=-q
. Since
-D\Phi_{usc}(v):C_{usc}\to D_{usc}
is a gauge-preserving bijection by
Theorem

4.7
, for each
g\in C
we have that

M(q,{-D\Phi}(v)(g))=M({-D\Phi_{usc}}(v)(p),{-D\Phi_{usc}}(v)(g))=M(p,g).

Moreover,
Proposition

3.18
grants us a state
\psi
on
(W,W_{+},w)
such that
M(p,g)=\Phi(g)(\psi)
for each
g\in C
. An application of these results to
g=\Phi^{-1}(w+\Phi(y))
yields

M(q,{-D\Phi(v)}(\Phi^{-1}(w+\Phi(y))))=M(p,\Phi^{-1}(w+\Phi(y)))=w(\psi)+\Phi(y)(\psi)=1+M(p,y).

Because
L
and
M
are multiplicatively inverse, we have
m(y,p)=M(p,y)^{-1}
and

m(w-\Phi(v+y)/q)M(q,{-D\Phi(v)}(\Phi^{-1}(w+\Phi(y))))=\frac{m(y,p)}{m(y,p)+1}(1+M(p,y))=1.

For the same reason this equality is equivalent to (
55
), whence the proof is complete.
∎

We obtain the following formula for the Gateaux derivative

of

.

5.1

In

The space

of all bounded linear operators

\lVert A\rVert_{v,w}:=\sup\{\lVert Ax\rVert_{w}:x\in V,\lVert x\rVert_{v}\leq 1\}.

Lemma 5.1
.

For each

we have

\lVert A\rVert_{v,w}\leq 3\sup\{\lVert Ax\rVert_{w}:x\in C,\lVert x\rVert_{v}\leq 1\}.

Every

. The lemma follows since

∎

Lemma 5.2
.

For all

\lVert D\Phi(x)\rVert_{v,w}\leq M(v,x)^{2}.

Proof.

The isomorphism of order unit spaces

\lVert\cdot\rVert_{w}\leq M(\Phi(x),w)\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\Phi(x)}

\lVert D\Phi(x)y\rVert_{w}\leq M(\Phi(x),w)\lVert D\Phi(x)y\rVert_{\Phi(x)}=M(v,x)\lVert y\rVert_{x}\leq M(v,x)^{2}\lVert y\rVert_{v}

\lVert\Phi(x)-\Phi(y)\rVert\leq\lambda^{2}\lVert x-y\rVert_{v}.

\lVert(D\Phi(x)-D\Phi(y))u\rVert_{w}=2\lambda\lVert u\rVert_{v}\cdot\lVert D\Phi(x)\hat{u}-D\Phi(y)\hat{u}\rVert_{w}\leq 2\lambda\lVert u\rVert_{v}\cdot 3\lambda^{2}\lVert x-y\rVert_{v}=6\lambda^{3}\lVert x-y\rVert_{v}\lVert u\rVert_{v}.

Since

∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let

. For all

As in the proof of

for

In

we have shown that

is the Gateaux-derivative of

at

.

5.3

Let us first show that the map

be the continuous linear map given by evaluation in

We have

5.4
Analyticity

Recall from
Definition

5.11
that we defined the quadratic representation map

to be the composition

. Since

is homogeneous of degree
-1
, the map

is homogeneous of degree

, and so

has degree

. In the present section we extend

to a quadratic polynomial map

, using the same derivation as in
[

, Theorem 3.4]
. As a consequence, we prove that gauge-reversing bijections are analytic.

We will now deduce from
Theorem

5.13
and

that

is a quadratic map

Theorem 5.18

(cf.
[
McC77
, Theorem 3.4]
)
.

extends to a continuous quadratic polynomial map

.

Proof.

First fix

and let

. We will show that

,

extends to a quadratic polynomial map on

.

Replacing
x
by
tx
in

gives

(t^{2}P(x)-P(y))j(tx+y)=tx-y.

Differentiating with respect to

gives

(74)

Differentiate again at

to find that

2P(x)z=P(y)[D^{2}j(y)](x)(x).

Since

is twice continuously differentiable, its second differential

. Therefore, formula (

and linearity of

.

The map

being a quadratic polynomial, it satisfies for all

By virtue of this identity, it follows from

, one deduces that

is continuous on all of

is a continuous quadratic polynomial map.

.

Each gauge-reversing bijection

. For each

Let us define the bilinear map

Lemma 5.21

∎

Proposition 5.22

The quadratic map

On linearizing (

.

The quadratic map

5.6
Verification of the quadratic JB-algebra axioms

In the previous section, we have shown that
(V,V_{+},v,P)
is a quadratic Jordan algebra. To show this quadruple is a quadratic JB-algebra in the sense of

2.41
, it remains to be shown that

is positive with norm
\lVert P(x)\rVert_{v}=\lVert x\rVert_{v}^{2}
for each

.

Theorem 5.25
.

The linear map
P(x)
is positive for each
x\in V
.

Proof.

We have already seen for each

that

is an automorphism of
(V,V_{+})
in

5.11
(2). Fix an arbitrary

. Since

is the closure of

, it suffices to show that
P(x)(j(y))\in V_{+}
for all

. In view of (
75
) and the positivity of

, we need only show that
[D^{2}j(y)](x)(x)\in V_{+}
. Owing to Kadison’s
Theorem

2.11
, it is equivalent to prove that
\psi([D^{2}j(y)](x)(x))\geq 0
for each state
\psi\in K
.

So let
\psi\in K
, and define the function
f\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
by
f(t):=\psi(j(y+tx))
on the open interval
I:=\{t\in\mathbb{R}:y+tx\in C\}
around

. Because
\Phi
is convex by
Theorem

3.19
and
\psi
is a positive functional,

is convex as well. Since
\psi
is a continuous linear functional and

is twice continuously differentiable by

5.17
, so is the map
f
, and
f^{\prime\prime}(0)=[D^{2}(\psi\circ j)(y)](x)(x)=\psi([D^{2}j(y)](x)(x))
. Since

is convex, it follows that
\psi([D^{2}j(y)](x)(x))=f^{\prime\prime}(0)\geq 0
, as desired.
∎

The formula in the following theorem has been demonstrated in
Theorem

5.13
for all
x,y\in C
, and will now be extended to its natural domain of definition.

Lemma 5.26
.

For all
x,y\in V
with

one has

(P(x)-P(y))j(x+y)=x-y.

(83)

Proof.

Let
U=\{(x,y)\in V\times V\colon x+y\in C\}
. Then
U
is a connected open subset of the Banach space
(V^{2},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{(v,v)})
. The mapping
f\colon U\to V
,
f(x,y):=(P(x)-P(y))j(x+y)-(x-y)
is analytic on

by

5.19
. By

5.13
we have

. The identity principle for analytic functions (

) implies that

.
∎

We have shown that the map

is analytic in
Theorem

5.19
. We now show its power series expansion around

is given by the familiar geometric series, following
[

, Theorem 4.4]
.

Proposition 5.27
.

There exists
0<r\leq 1
such that for all
h\in V
with
\lVert h\rVert_{v}<r
one has

j(v-h)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}h^{k}.

(84)

Proof.

Since
j
is analytic by
Theorem

5.19
, there exist
0<r\leq 1
and for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
a continuous symmetric homogeneous polynomial map
c_{k}\colon V\to V
such that for all
h\in V
with
\lVert h\rVert<r
one has

j(v+h)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}c_{k}(h).

We proceed to show for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and
h\in V
that

c_{k}(h)=(-1)^{k}h^{k}.

If
h=0
then
c_{0}(0)=v=h^{0}
and
c_{k}(0)=0=h^{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. Now assume
h\neq 0
. Let
t
be a real number with
|t|<\lVert h\rVert_{v}^{-1}
. Then
v+th\in C
holds, so
Lemma

5.26
with
x=v
and

gives
(I-t^{2}P(h))j(v+th)=v-th.

If in addition
t^{2}\lVert P(h)\rVert_{v}<1
, then
I-t^{2}P(h)
is invertible in
L(V)
. The powers of
h
in the Jordan algebra

being given by
h^{2k}=P(h)^{k}v
and
h^{2k+1}=P(h)^{k}h
, it follows that whenever
|t|<\min(\lVert h\rVert_{v}^{-1},\lVert P(h)\rVert_{v}^{-1/2})
one has

j(v+th)=(I-t^{2}P(h))^{-1}(v-th)=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}t^{2k}P(h)^{k}\right)(v-th)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}t^{k}h^{k}.

Comparison of both series for

yields

, whence (

).
∎

Remark 5.28
.

We will shortly see in

5.31
that
\lVert P(x)\rVert_{v}=\lVert x\rVert_{v}^{2}
for each

, which implies that

holds with

.

Lemma 5.29
.

For all
x\in V
with
\lVert x\rVert_{v}<1
we have

v-x^{2}=2j(j(v-x)+j(v+x)).

Proof.

Let
r>0
be as in
Proposition

5.27
. Because
x\mapsto x^{2}=P(x)v
is continuous by
Theorem

5.18
, there exists
0<r_{1}\leq r
such that
\lVert x\rVert<r_{1}
implies
\lVert x^{2}\rVert<r
for each

. Let
x\in V
with
\lVert x\rVert<r_{1}
. Then for each

we have

, hence the power series expansion (

) is valid. We obtain that

j(v-x)+j(v+x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x^{k}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}x^{k}=2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x^{2k}=2j(v-x^{2}).

Now apply

to obtain

provided that
\lVert x\rVert<r_{1}
.

Both sides of this equation are analytic functions of

on the open unit ball, and have just been shown to be equal on the open ball of radius

∎

Lemma 5.30
.

For all
x\in V
with
-v\leq x\leq v
we have

.

Proof.

First assume that
\lVert x\rVert_{v}<1
. By convexity of

Upon using

, we obtain

.
∎

Proposition 5.31
.

For each
x\in V
we have
\lVert P(x)\rVert_{v}=\lVert x\rVert^{2}_{v}
.

Proof.

Since

is positive, we have

Thus our objective is to show that
\lVert x^{2}\rVert_{v}=\lVert x\rVert_{v}^{2}
for each

. Since both members of this equation are homogeneous of degree

in

.

Suppose that

-v\leq x\leq v
. Then

gives

. Conversely suppose that
\lVert x^{2}\rVert_{v}\leq 1

-v\leq x^{2}\leq v

-v\leq\tfrac{1}{2}((x+v)^{2}-x^{2}-v)=x=\tfrac{1}{2}(x^{2}+v-(x-v)^{2})\leq v,

∎

In this section we collect the main results of this paper concerning complete order unit spaces. In particular, we complete the proof of

The if direction of

Theorem 5.33
.

Remark 5.34

of

at

.

Proof.

The gauge-reversing bijections

. Part (3) is shown in

. To ease notation set

. In

. In

Suppose that

We proceed to prove

5.8
Gauge-reversing bijections on non-complete order unit spaces

In this section we will prove
Theorem

1.3
, which asserts that two order unit spaces are necessarily isomorphic if there exists a gauge-reversing bijection between their open cones. For
complete
order unit spaces this has been shown in
Theorem

4.7
; in order to reduce to this case, we will extend a gauge-reversing bijection between possibly non-complete order unit spaces to their completions. Finally, we exploit this extension to deduce from
Theorem

5.33
that a non-complete order unit space that admits a gauge-reversing bijection to a second order unit space is a norm dense subalgebra of a JB-algebra.
Let
(V,V_{+},v)
be a, possibly non-complete, order unit space. In
Lemma

2.14
we constructed a complete order unit space
(\hat{V},\hat{V}_{+},v)
such that
(\hat{V},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v})
is the completion of the normed space
(V,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{v})
.

Lemma 5.36
.

Let
(V,V_{+},v)
be an order unit space, and let
(\hat{V},\hat{V}_{+},v)
be its completion. The inclusion map
i\colon(C,d_{T})\to(\hat{C},d_{T})
is a completion of the metric
(C,d_{T})
.

Proof.

The map
i
is an isometry, because the ordering on
V
is the restriction of the ordering on
\hat{V}
to
V
. Hence it suffices to show that
C
is
d_{T}
-dense in
\hat{C}
. Indeed, let
x\in\hat{C}
be given and pick
\epsilon>0
such that
x\geq\epsilon v
. Let
(y_{n})_{n}
be a sequence in
V
such that
\lVert y_{n}-x\rVert_{v}\to 0
. Set
x_{n}:=y_{n}+\lVert y_{n}-x\rVert_{v}v
for each
n
, and note that
x_{n}\geq x
. Consequently
x_{n}\geq\epsilon v
, so
Lemma

2.18
gives that
d_{T}(x_{n},x)\leq\epsilon^{-1}\lVert x_{n}-x\rVert_{v}\leq 2\epsilon^{-1}\lVert y_{n}-x\rVert_{v}
. We conclude that
d_{T}(x_{n},x)\to 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Since
x\in\hat{C}
was arbitrary, this shows that
C
is
d_{T}
-dense in
\hat{C}
as desired.
∎

Lemma 5.37
.

Let
\Phi\colon C\to D
be a bijective

-isometry between the open cones of order unit spaces

resp.
W
. Then there is a unique bijective

-isometry
\hat{\Phi}\colon\hat{C}\to\hat{D}
extending
\Phi
. If
\Phi
is gauge-preversing (resp. gauge-reserving), then
\hat{\Phi}
is also gauge-preversing (resp. gauge-reserving).

Proof.

By the universal property of the completion of a metric space, there is a unique bijective isometry
\hat{\Phi}\colon(\hat{C},d_{T})\to(\hat{D},d_{T})
extending
\Phi
.

Suppose that
\Phi
is order-reserving. Let
x,y\in\hat{C}
be such that
x\leq y
. We shall show that
\hat{\Phi}(x)\geq\hat{\Phi}(y)
. As in the proof of
Lemma

5.36
we can find sequences
(x_{n})_{n}
and
(y_{n})_{n}
in
C
which

-converge to
x
resp.

, and satisfy
x_{n}\leq x\leq y\leq y_{n}
for each

. Since
\Phi
is order-reversing, we have
\Phi(x_{n})\geq\Phi(y_{n})
for each
n
. Moreover, we have that
d_{T}(x_{n},x)=d_{T}(\Phi(x_{n}),\hat{\Phi}(x))\to 0
, so

gives that
\lVert\Phi(x_{n})-\hat{\Phi}(x)\rVert_{w}\to 0
as

. Similarly, we have
\lVert\Phi(y_{n})-\hat{\Phi}(y)\rVert_{w}\to 0
as

. Because the cone
\hat{V}_{+}
is the closure of
V_{+}
, it follows that
\hat{\Phi}(x)\geq\hat{\Phi}(y)
, which establishes that
\hat{\Phi}
is order-preserving. Similarly, one may show that
\hat{\Phi}
is order-preversing whenever
\Phi
is order-preversing.

Assume that
\lambda>0
and
\mu>0
are such that
\mu\Phi(x)=\Phi(\lambda x)
for all

. The map

given by

is a bijective

-isometry. We may reformulate our assumption as

. It is plain that
\widehat{m_{C,\lambda}}=m_{\hat{C},\lambda}
, whence
m_{\hat{D},\mu}\circ\hat{\Phi}=\hat{\Phi}\circ m_{\hat{C},\lambda}
. We conclude that
\mu\hat{\Phi}(x)=\hat{\Phi}(\lambda x)
for all

. Applying this result with

arbitrary and

(resp.

) shows that
\hat{\Phi}
is homogeneous of degree

(resp. of degree

) whenever

is.

We conclude using
Proposition

2.22
and
Proposition

2.23
.
∎

Theorem

1.3
will now be demonstrated.

.

Lemma

5.37

-D\hat{\Phi}(v)\colon(\hat{V},\hat{V}_{+},v)\to(\hat{W},\hat{W}_{+},w),

whose inverse is given by
-D(\hat{\Phi}^{-1})(w)\colon(\hat{W},\hat{W}_{+},w)\to(\hat{V},\hat{V}_{+},v).

Let us show that
D\hat{\Phi}(v)[V]\subset W
. First, consider
z\in C

z\leq\tfrac{1}{2}v
. Then we obtain that
\Phi(z)-w\geq\Phi(\tfrac{1}{2}v)-w=2w-w=w,

\Phi(z)-w\in D
. Applying

Theorem

4.10

x=v

\Phi(v+y)-D\hat{\Phi}(v)(\Phi^{-1}(\Phi(v)+\Phi(y)))=\Phi(v).

Since
\Phi(v)+\Phi(y)=\Phi(z)
and
\hat{\Phi}
extends
\Phi
, we obtain

-D\hat{\Phi}(v)(z)=w-\Phi(v+y)\in D.

Since
\{z\in C:z\leq\tfrac{1}{2}v\}
spans
V
and
-D\hat{\Phi}(v)
is linear, it follows that
-D\hat{\Phi}(v)[V]\subset W
. Similarly one shows that
-D(\widehat{\Phi}^{-1})(w)[W]\subset V
. We conclude that
-D\widehat{\Phi}(v)
restricts to an isomorphism of order unit spaces from
(V,V_{+},v)
onto
(W,W_{+},w)
.
∎

Theorem 5.39
