Towards Robust Process Reward Modeling via Noise-aware Learning
Bin Xie, Bingbing Xu, Xueyun Tian, Yilin Chen, Huawei Shen

Abstract
Process Reward Models (PRMs) have achieved strong results in complex reasoning, but are bottlenecked by costly process-level supervision. A widely used alternative, Monte Carlo Estimation (MCE), defines process rewards as the probability that a policy model reaches the correct final answer from a given reasoning step. However, step correctness is an intrinsic property of the reasoning trajectory, and should be invariant to policy choice. Our empirical findings show that MCE producing policy-dependent rewards that induce label noise, including false positives that reward incorrect steps and false negatives that penalize correct ones. To address above challenges, we propose a two-stage framework to mitigate noisy supervision. In the labeling stage, we introduce a reflection-aware label correction mechanism that uses a large language model (LLM) as a judge to detect reflection and self-correction behaviors related to the current reasoning step, thereby suppressing overestimated rewards. In the training stage, we further propose a N oise- A ware I terative T raining framework that enables the PRM to progressively refine noisy labels based on its own confidence. Extensive Experiments show that our method substantially improves step-level correctness discrimination, achieving up to a 27% absolute gain in average F1 over PRMs trained with noisy supervision.

3.1
Monte Carlo Estimation

Given a generative policy
\pi
, MCE samples
K
future continuations from step
s_{t}
, producing trajectories
\{\tau^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, and compares their final answers
\mathrm{a}(\tau^{(k)})
with the ground-truth answer
a^{*}
. Two common estimation strategies are used:

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{t}^{\text{SE}}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbbm{1}\bigl(\mathrm{a}(\tau^{(k)})=a^{*}\bigr),

(1)

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{t}^{\text{HE}}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\max_{k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}}\mathbbm{1}\bigl(\mathrm{a}(\tau^{(k)})=a^{*}\bigr),

(2)

where
\mathbbm{1}(\cdot)
denotes the indicator function.
Soft Estimation (SE)
assigns a probabilistic label, while
Hard Estimation (HE)
yields a binary label indicating whether at least one sampled continuation reaches the correct answer.

3.2
Discriminative Process Reward Modeling

Given a problem
P
and a reasoning trajectory
\tau=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{T})
, a discriminative process reward model (PRM)
R_{\theta}
assigns a correctness score to each step conditioned on its prefix:

r_{t}=R_{\theta}(P,s_{\leq t}),\quad r_{t}\in(0,1).

(3)

Let
y_{t}\in[0,1]
denote the step-level correctness label, which can be either binary (e.g., from HE) or continuous (e.g., from SE).
PRMs are typically trained using a cross-entropy objective:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[y_{t}\log r_{t}+(1-y_{t})\log(1-r_{t})\right].

(4)

This formulation enables PRMs to act as discriminative classifiers that evaluate the validity of intermediate reasoning steps.

3.3
Policy-Dependent Label Noise

A fundamental limitation of MCE is that the estimated label
\hat{y}_{t}(\pi)
depends on the
future behavior of the sampling policy
\pi
, rather than the intrinsic correctness of step
s_{t}
. This policy dependence introduces label noise, including: (i)
False Positives (FP)
, where incorrect steps receive high rewards due to downstream self-correction (As shown in Figure
2
Left), and (ii)
False Negatives (FN)
, where correct steps are penalized because later reasoning fails (As shown in Figure
2
Right). Mitigating such policy-induced noise is the central motivation of this work.

4
Method

In this section, we propose a two-stage framework for training robust discriminative PRMs under noisy process supervision. Our method addresses policy-dependent noise introduced by MCE through (i) reflection-aware label correction during data annotation and (ii) noise-aware iterative training with selective label refinement. The overall workflow is illustrated in Figure
3
.

4.1
Reflection-Aware Label Correction

As discussed in Section
3
, MCE assigns step-level labels by sampling
K
trajectories from a generative policy conditioned on a given reasoning prefix. However, this procedure may produce false positives when an initially incorrect step is later corrected within a trajectory that still reaches the correct answer.

To mitigate this issue, we introduce a reflection-aware correction mechanism that operates at the
trajectory level
prior to MCE aggregation. For each sampled trajectory
\tau^{(i)}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{T})
, we apply a large language model to identify whether the correctness of the final answer is achieved by explicitly revising or negating an earlier step
s_{t}
. The prompt template is shown in Appendix
B
. Formally, for a trajectory
\tau^{(i)}
that reaches the correct final answer, we define a binary reflection indicator:

\textsc{Reflect}(\tau^{(i)},t)\in\{0,1\},

(5)

which evaluates whether any downstream step
s_{k}
(
k>t
) explicitly corrects the content of
s_{t}
. If
\textsc{Reflect}(\tau^{(i)},t)=1
, the trajectory is considered invalid evidence for the correctness of step
s_{t}
, and is re-labeled as unsuccessful with respect to
s_{t}
, even though it reaches the correct final answer.

After applying reflection detection to all sampled trajectories, MCE aggregation is performed on the corrected trajectory outcomes. This mechanism is designed as a high-precision filter that selectively removes spurious success trajectories caused by self-correction. As a result, it substantially reduces false positives introduced by MCE.

4.2
Noise-Aware Iterative Training

While reflection-aware correction substantially reduces false positives, residual label noise persists, particularly false negatives arising from limited policy capability. Directly correcting such cases via judgment-based supervision is inherently ambiguous, as downstream failures do not provide observable evidence that a preceding step is incorrect. To robustly train PRMs under this residual noise, we propose a noise-aware iterative training framework.

We first train an initial PRM
R_{\theta^{(0)}}
on the filtered dataset with labels
\{\tilde{y}_{t}^{(0)}\}
using standard cross-entropy loss. At iteration
k
, the model
R_{\theta^{(k)}}
re-evaluates each step and outputs a continuous correctness score:

r_{t}^{(k)}=R_{\theta^{(k)}}(P,s_{\leq t}),\quad r_{t}^{(k)}\in(0,1).

(6)

For each step, we refine its supervision signal based on the discrepancy between the model prediction and the current label. Given a threshold
\delta
, the refined label
\tilde{y}_{t}^{(k+1)}
is defined as:

\tilde{y}_{t}^{(k+1)}=\begin{cases}r_{t}^{(k)},&\text{if }|r_{t}^{(k)}-\tilde{y}_{t}^{(k)}|>\delta,\\
\tilde{y}_{t}^{(k)},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(7)

This selective refinement strategy preserves the majority of original labels while attenuating the influence of potentially corrupted ones. Under the empirically observed low-noise regime after reflection-based filtering, strong disagreement between the model prediction and the original label is more likely to indicate label noise than model error. The refinement and retraining procedure is repeated for multiple iterations to obtain the final PRM. The complete training procedure is summarized in Algorithm

5.2
Label Quality Evaluation (RQ1)

This section evaluates whether the proposed reflection-aware label correction can effectively reduce label noise introduced by MCE.

Main Results.

We evaluate label quality on the PRM800K test set using human-annotated step-level correctness labels, reporting Accuracy and F1. As shown in Table
2
, reflection-aware correction consistently outperforms standard MC-HE across all generators. Compared to MC-HE, F1 improves by 3.3–16.4 points (e.g., 0.521
\rightarrow
0.685 for
7B-Inst.
), with concurrent gains in Accuracy (e.g., 0.610
\rightarrow
0.710 for
7B-Inst.
). Across most settings,
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
matches or slightly surpasses
Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct
in F1.

Analysis.

These results support our hypothesis that downstream self-corrections are a reliable signal for identifying policy-induced false positives in MCE. By filtering such trajectories prior to aggregation, reflection-aware correction substantially improves precision, leading to large F1 gains while maintaining comparable recall. This high-precision behavior reduces label noise without sacrificing coverage of correct steps, yielding cleaner hard supervision. Overall, the improved label quality provides a stronger foundation for training robust PRMs in the subsequent noise-aware iterative training stage.

5.4
Further Analysis and Insights

We further analyze the behavior of NAIT from two complementary perspectives: the effect of training data scale and the dynamics of iterative noise-aware optimization.

Effect of Training Data Scale.

We study the impact of training data scale on PRM performance under the
NAIT
framework by training models on increasingly larger subsets of
MCRD-7B_7B
(16K–128K), with all other settings fixed. As shown in Figure
4a
, step-level F1 improves consistently across all ProcessBench benchmarks as data scale increases, indicating stronger discrimination between correct and incorrect reasoning steps. For example, F1 on GSM8K rises from 57.2 to 77.9, while MATH and OmniMath improve from 44.6 to 64.6 and from 34.1 to 57.4, respectively, as training data scales up. These gains transfer directly to test-time reasoning. In Best-of-
N
evaluation (Figure
4b
, PRM@8 accuracy increases steadily with more training data, e.g., from 89.2 to 95.6 on GSM8K and from 74.4 to 82.1 on MATH, reflecting more reliable trajectory ranking and improved search guidance across tasks. These results demonstrate that the remaining gap to Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B is largely attributable to data scale rather than
NAIT
framework limitations.

Effect of Iterative Training in NAIT.

We analyze the effect of iterative optimization in
NAIT
by evaluating PRMs across training stages (Stage 0–3) on the full

with progressively smaller thresholds (
\tau=0.3,0.2,0.1

Conclusion

In this paper, we study the problem of noisy supervision in process reward modeling and analytically show that Monte Carlo Estimation inevitably introduces policy-dependent errors that fundamentally hinder PRM training. We propose a sophisticated, two-stage solution that seamlessly combines reflection-aware label correction during data construction with Noise-Aware Iterative Training (NAIT) during optimization, effectively enabling PRMs to precisely correct both deceptive false positives and overlooked false negatives without costly additional supervision. Extensive and rigorous experiments demonstrate that our approach consistently and significantly improves step-level discrimination and robust test-time reasoning performance across diverse datasets and varying model scales, while achieving remarkable data efficiency.

During the training of PRM, we continued the language modeling approach. Specifically, we introduced three additional special tokens into the model’s vocabulary: [PRM], [POS], and [NEG]. When calculating the loss, the model masked the original text tokens and only output the logits corresponding to the last token of each step, namely [POS] and [NEG]. Then, we used Equation

[Problem]

A nurses’ station orders bandages in bulk packs of 50. On the first day, the nurses used 38 bandages and ordered one bulk pack of bandages. On the second day, they used ten fewer bandages. On the third day, they ordered two bulk packs of bandages and only used half a pack. They had 78 bandages left at the end of the third day. How many bandages did they start with on the first day?

[Response]

To determine how many bandages the nurses started with on the first day, we need to follow these steps:
[
PRM: 99.6, GT: Correct
]

•

1. Calculate the number of bandages used each day:

\bullet

First day:
The nurses used 38 bandages.

\bullet

Second day:
They used 10 fewer bandages than the first day, so they used
38-10=28
bandages.

\bullet

Third day:
They used half a bulk pack of bandages. Since each bulk pack contains 50 bandages, half a pack is
50/2=25
bandages.

[
PRM: 94.1, GT: Correct
]

•

2. Determine the total number of bandages used over the three days:

\text{Total bandages used}=38+28+25=91

[
PRM: 98.4, GT: Correct
]

•

3. Calculate the total number of bandages ordered:

\bullet

First day:
They ordered 1 bulk pack, which is 50 bandages.

\bullet

Third day:
They ordered 2 bulk packs, which is
2\times 50=100
bandages.

\bullet

Total bandages ordered:

50+100=150
.

[
PRM: 83.2, GT: Correct
]

•

4. Determine the total number of bandages available at the beginning:

\bullet

At the end of the third day, they had 78 bandages left.

\bullet

Let
B
be the initial number of bandages.

\bullet

The total number of bandages used plus the remaining bandages equals the initial number of bandages:

B=\text{Total bandages used}+\text{Remaining bandages}

B=91+78=169

[
PRM: 0.5, GT: Incorrect
]

Thus, the nurses started with
169
bandages on the first day.
[
PRM: 29.4, GT: Incorrect
]

PRM Evaluation Case 2

Generator:

Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct

Process Reward Model:

Qwen2.5-Math-7B-NAIT

[Problem]

Kim sleepwalks, to monitor her sleeping hours, she installs a camera in her room. Kim usually goes to sleep at 10 p.m. and wakes up at 6 a.m. She is a sleepwalker. One day, after reviewing the cameras, she finds that she woke up that day and was sleepwalking from 2:15 to 2:38 am. Also, that day she woke up 5 minutes earlier than usual to go to the bathroom. How many minutes did she sleep on her bed that day?

[Response]

To determine how many minutes Kim slept on her bed that day, we need to account for her usual sleep schedule, the time she spent sleepwalking, and the additional time she woke up early.
[
PRM: 99.6, GT: Correct
]

•

1. Calculate the total sleep time if there were no interruptions:

\bullet

Kim normally sleeps from 10 p.m. to 6 a.m., which is:

8\text{ hours}=480\text{ minutes}

[
PRM: 99.6, GT: Correct
]

•

2. Account for the time she woke up early:

\bullet

Kim woke up 5 minutes earlier than usual. This means she was awake for an additional 5 minutes before her usual wake-up time of 6 a.m. Therefore, she lost 5 minutes of sleep:

480\text{ minutes}-5\text{ minutes}=475\text{ minutes}

[
PRM: 88.3, GT: Correct
]

•

3. Subtract the time spent sleepwalking:

\bullet

Kim was sleepwalking from 2:15 a.m. to 2:38 a.m., which is:

2:38\text{ a.m.}-2:15\text{ a.m.}=23\text{ minutes}

\bullet

Since sleepwalking does not count as sleep on her bed, we subtract these 23 minutes from the remaining sleep time:

475\text{ minutes}-23\text{ minutes}=452\text{ minutes}

[
PRM: 92.6, GT: Correct
]

Therefore, the number of minutes Kim slept on her bed that day is
452
.
[
PRM: 99.3, GT: Correct
]
