Optical genome mapping enhanced by refined variant interpretation in pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Anna Bekő, Borbála Péterffy, Alex Hughes, Janka Sára Jakab, Irén Haltrich, Kristóf Balázs Árvai, Gábor Bedics, Katalin Csonka, Gergő Papp, Dóra Kapczár, Bettina Aranka Bohusné Barta, Lajos László Hegyi, Gábor Szalóki, Gábor Barna, András Matolcsy, Bálint Egyed, Zsuzsanna Hevessy, Anne Benard‐Slagter, Sander Palit, Suvi Savola, Csaba Bödör, Donát Alpár
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractReliable detection of structural variants (SVs) and copy number variations (CNVs) is crucial in the contemporary diagnostics of pediatric B‐cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B‐ALL). However, limitations of commonly used conventional and molecular cytogenetic methods may hinder the accurate genetic characterization of patients. Optical genome mapping (OGM) offers a reliable alternative by enabling high‐resolution, genome‐wide detection of CNVs and SVs. Chromosomal aberrations were screened using OGM in 51 children with B‐ALL. The results were compared with those of karyotyping, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), digital multiplex ligation‐dependent probe amplification (digitalMLPA), and targeted RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq). OGM data showed high congruency with karyotyping and FISH findings, detecting clinically relevant variants beyond G‐banding results and unraveling a complex KMT2A fusion undetected by FISH. Gene fusions involved in complex ETV6::RUNX1 translocations, but not detected by RNA‐seq, were confirmed using FISH. Normalization of OGM copy number values with DNA‐index‐improved concordance with FISH‐derived copy numbers in near‐tri/tetraploid cases. In the peripheral regions of OGM variants (fringe‐zones), a novel evaluation strategy called ‘FriZone’ was applied, which significantly improved the concordance between OGM and digitalMLPA. In addition, a co‐segregation analysis revealed strong associations between ETV6::RUNX1 fusion and deletions of ETV6, RAG2, and NR3C2. OGM uncovered complex rearrangements undetected by widely used methods in 15% of cases, improving genetic classification and risk stratification in 10% of the patients. The FriZone analysis and normalization by DNA‐index provide a refined, more accurate approach to OGM variant interpretation, facilitating the efficient application of OGM in clinical diagnostics. © 2026 The Author(s). Introduction

B‐cell precursor acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B‐ALL) is the most common type of hematological malignancy in children [1, 2]. The 5‐year survival rate of pediatric B‐ALL reaches 85–90% in developed countries, attributable to significant advances in the clinical disease management, increasingly aided by molecular diagnostic and monitoring strategies [3]. The advent of next‐generation sequencing enabled the comprehensive molecular characterization of B‐ALL, uncovering novel disease subtypes with clinically relevant genomic and transcriptomic features [4]. However, accurate diagnostics still requires a combined application of multiple techniques, for example conventional karyotyping, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), multiplex ligation‐dependent probe amplification (MLPA), or single‐nucleotide polymorphism array, as well as DNA‐ and RNA‐based sequencing [5, 6]. In addition, reliable cytogenetic characterization and genetic subgroup assignment of patients remain essential for accurate risk stratification and clinical disease management [7, 8]. Known limitations of such cytogenetic and molecular cytogenetic methods, which are still widely used in clinical diagnostics, may hamper genetic subtyping in a nonnegligible subset of patients [9].

Optical genome mapping (OGM), a DNA‐based, digital cytogenomic technology, allows for the simultaneous, genome‐wide detection of copy number variations (CNVs) and structural variants (SVs) greater than 500 base pairs (by de novo analysis), also identifying the specific genes involved [10]. In recent years, it has been highlighted that, in addition to the detection of routinely screened aberrations, OGM enables the analysis of SVs with higher resolution than is achievable by karyotyping [11, 12]. Hence, the technology seems to be suitable for screening the World Health Organization (WHO) subgroup‐defining [7] and most other subtype‐defining variants in B‐ALL [4], as well as for identifying potential novel biomarkers [13]. Although OGM enables high‐resolution detection of all SV classes in a single, rapid workflow, its widespread application is still hampered by challenges in data interpretation [14].

In this study, we assessed the feasibility, diagnostic applicability, and added value of OGM in a real‐word cohort of patients with pediatric B‐ALL. Besides comparing OGM data with results obtained by routinely applied conventional and molecular cytogenetic techniques, the performance of the method in detecting CNVs and subgroup‐defining rearrangements was evaluated by benchmarking against digital multiplex ligation‐dependent probe amplification (digitalMLPA) and targeted RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq). Notably, we have developed a novel analysis strategy facilitating aneuploidy and deletion detection with higher accuracy, which can contribute to an improved variant interpretation, aiding the efficient implementation of OGM in clinical diagnostics.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and patient consent

Ethical approval (45563‐2/2019/EKU) from the Medical Research Council of Hungary and written informed consent from the patients and/or from their parents or guardians were obtained for the study, which was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [15].

Patient samples

Diagnostic bone marrow or peripheral blood samples from 51 children with pediatric B‐ALL were analyzed, with a real‐world distribution of B‐ALL subgroups represented by the cohort (supplementary material, Table S1). The median age at diagnosis was 5.5 years (0–18 years), with a male‐to‐female ratio of 1.32:1. Flow cytometry was used to assess the leukemic blast percentage in the specimens (average 81.5%, range: 26–97%) [16, 17] (supplementary material, Table S1) and to determine patients' ploidy status based on the DNA‐index [18, 19] (supplementary material, Table S1). Diagnosis and genetic profiling were performed based on morphological and immunophenotypic features, as well as cytogenetic abnormalities identified using karyotyping and FISH (supplementary material, Table S2) [7]. Fusion transcripts were screened using a targeted RNA‐seq panel (TruSight RNA Pan‐Cancer Panel, Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), as detailed in the Supplementary Materials and Methods. Fusion transcripts were called as previously described [20].

Optical genome mapping

Besides the regularly performed methods, all samples were further analyzed by OGM (Bionano, San Diego, CA, USA). Ultra‐high molecular weight genomic DNA was extracted from the bone marrow or peripheral blood samples (supplementary material, Table S3), fluorescently labeled using the Bionano Prep Direct Label and Stain Kit, and loaded into the Bionano Saphyr Chip's flow cells (G2.2 or G3.3) following the manufacturer's protocols and as detailed in the Supplementary Materials and Methods. Data analysis was performed using the Rare Variant Analysis Pipeline (Bionano Solve version 3.8.2 [21, 22]), and variants were interpreted and filtered in the Bionano Access software version 1.8.2 [22, 23], as described in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

digitalMLPA

Further validation of CNVs was performed in all but one sample using the digitalMLPA probemix D007 (test version D007‐X7, preliminary D007‐B1, MRC Holland, Amsterdam, the Netherlands). This probemix contains a total of 951 probes genome‐wide, including 528 target probes, 251 karyotyping probes (supplementary material, Table S4), and over 160 control probes and fragments. digitalMLPA protocol and data analysis were performed as previously described and detailed in the Supplementary Materials and Methods [24, 25, 26].

Enhanced OGM variant interpretation

To improve OGM variant interpretation, two refinement strategies were newly developed and implemented. First, normalization by the DNA‐index of OGM‐derived copy numbers (CNs) was introduced to optimize CN accuracy in near‐tri/tetraploid cases. To perform the CN value normalization with the DNA‐index, patients were classified according to DNA‐index into ploidy subgroups: hypodiploid, diploid, hyperdiploid (low hyperdiploid and high hyperdiploid), and near tri‐/tetraploid [27, 28]. These ploidy categories were subsequently considered at downstream analyses. Deviations of original OGM CN values from FISH CN values were calculated as (OGM CN value) − (FISH CN value). DNA‐index‐based normalization of OGM CN values was performed by multiplying the original OGM CN value by the DNA‐index (OGM CN value × DNA‐index), and deviations of the normalized OGM‐derived CN values from FISH‐derived CN values were then calculated as follows: (Normalized OGM CN value) − (FISH CN value) (see supplementary material, Figure S1). Second, to enhance the interpretation of peripheral regions of OGM variants, known as ‘fringe‐zones’, a method refinement called FriZone was established and applied. Fringe‐zones are defined as regions on the periphery of variants, spanning from the last, still captured OGM label to the first undetected label on the patient's DNA molecule (Figure 1). At both ends of the variants, the distance between the last captured label on the reference molecule and the corresponding digitalMLPA probe was compared with the size of the non‐deleted DNA fragment, defined as the difference between the coordinates of the labels delimiting the variant on the detected molecule. If the size of the non‐deleted DNA fragment was larger than the distance to the digitalMLPA probe, the probe was considered as non‐deleted. Otherwise, the probe was considered as being affected by the deletion, as shown in Figure 1. Detailed workflow description of FriZone is presented in the supplementary material, Figure S2.

At both ends of the variant, the size of the non‐deleted DNA fragment (5,943 bp) was compared to the distance between the last captured label on the reference molecule (A and D) and the respective digitalMLPA probe. In the case of probe S019897, located in the fringe‐zone, the probe's distance from the last captured label exceeds the size of the non‐deleted DNA fragment, and hence the target locus falls within the deleted region, reflected by a ratio of 0.5.

Statistical analyses

Distributions of SVs detected in at least three individuals were compared across disease subgroups using the Kruskal–Wallis test and visualized with box plots. The distribution of the original and normalized signed deviations of OGM CN values from FISH CN values was compared using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test and visualized using violin plots. The concordance between the results of digitalMLPA and OGM was evaluated by calculating interrater reliability (Cohen's kappa) across all genes designated as ‘target gene’ by digitalMLPA. The difference between the original OGM analysis and the FriZone in CN interpretation was assessed by evaluating the concordance of CN values between digitalMLPA and each OGM data analysis method over digitalMLPA probes overlapping the fringe‐zones using the McNemar test. Statistical analyses and figure generation were performed using SPSS version 29.0.1.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA), and Inkscape version 1.4.2 software [29]. Co‐segregation analysis was conducted with Fisher's exact test using the SciPy 1.13.1 Python library (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA). Prefiltered gene alterations using a virtual gene panel (supplementary material, Table S5), clusters of variants detected by OGM, and B‐ALL subgroup‐defining aberrations were included in the co‐segregation analyses, as outlined in the Supplementary Materials and Methods and supplementary material, Figure S3. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

OGM, successfully performed on all 51 leukemic samples, identified 2,160 SVs. Following application of the predefined filtering criteria, 1,599 SVs were retained, including 996 deletions, 392 insertions, 24 inversions, 160 translocations, and 27 duplications, corresponding to an estimated false discovery rate of 26% for the OGM SV pipeline. Additionally, 126 large subchromosomal CNVs and 148 whole‐chromosome CNVs were detected. The highest frequency of SVs was observed in the hypodiploid and ETV6::RUNX1 subgroups, with a median of 52.5 (range: 35–70) and 42 (range: 28–93) variants, respectively. The distribution of detected SVs across B‐ALL subgroups is shown in the supplementary material, Figure S4. Considering the large subchromosomal CNVs, ETV6::RUNX1‐positive patients had a significantly higher number of variants compared to patients with high‐hyperdiploidy (Kruskal–Wallis test, p = 0.013), with no other significant difference observed among the disease subgroups. As expected, whole‐chromosome gains and losses were mostly detected in high‐hyperdiploid (HHD) and hypodiploid patients.

Comparison of OGM data with conventional and molecular cytogenetic results

Karyotyping was performed on all 51 pediatric B‐ALL patient samples; however, cell culturing was unsuccessful in 41% of the cases (n = 21), and a normal karyotype was observed in 33% (n = 17). Among the 13 cases with identified chromosomal aberrations, OGM fully confirmed the findings of conventional karyotyping in 10 patients, partially validated the G‐banding results in two cases (P_34 and P_45), and showed complete discordance in a single case (P_10) with near‐tetraploidy (Figure 2A). In five cases where G‐banding reported a normal karyotype, the DNA‐index exceeded 1.16, or FISH results suggested increased chromosome numbers. In all but one of these cases, OGM fully established the HHD status by uncovering the specific chromosomal gains, with case P_37 being discordant due to its near‐triploid status. Overall, OGM detected 27 variants (gains or losses) larger than 10 Mbp and three variants with lengths between 5 and 10 Mbp not described by G‐banding. All 30 of these variants were confirmed by a third independent method, digitalMLPA, except for a 6.7‐Mbp deletion on 8q affecting a region not covered by the applied digitalMLPA probemix. OGM successfully generated digital karyotypes for all 21 cases where G‐banding failed due to a lack of dividing cells or nonevaluable metaphases.

Regarding the CNVs, an agreement of 90% was observed between FISH and OGM, while the concordance for rearrangements was 99%. The discordance between FISH and OGM was primarily driven by near‐tri/tetraploid (P_37, P_42, and P_10) cases, with a concordance of 97% between FISH and OGM results among the remaining patients. In these 48 cases, OGM only failed to validate three FISH‐derived CN alterations. At two out of three of these discordant data points, OGM findings were corroborated by digitalMLPA (Figure 2B). In patient P_36, OGM clarified a previously misinterpreted gain of the 3’ KMT2A FISH probe (supplementary material, Figure S5). In addition to the variants detected by FISH, OGM identified 12 CNVs, including six prognostically significant CDKN2A deletions, all of which were validated by digitalMLPA. OGM copy number normalization by DNA‐index

In diploid cases, original and normalized OGM CN values showed minimal differences compared with FISH CN values, with mean absolute deviations (MADs) of 0.073 and 0.079, respectively, and without significant differences between the two OGM evaluation strategies (mean of pairwise differences in absolute deviations – MPDAD: −0.006, Wilcoxon: p = 0.720, Figure 3A), In hypo‐ and hyperdiploid cases, the original OGM CN values were closer to FISH results (MAD: 0.201 and 0.108, respectively) than the normalized OGM CN values (MAD: 0.301 and 0.373, respectively), with a MPDAD of −0.100 and −0.265, respectively (Wilcoxon, p < 0.001, Figure 3B,C). However, in near‐tri/tetraploid cases, normalization substantially reduced deviations from FISH data (MAD = 0.326), outperforming the original OGM CN analysis (MAD = 1.251), with a MPDAD of 0.925 (Wilcoxon, p < 0.001, Figure 3D). Agreement with FISH was 5.3% (2/38 datapoints) with original OGM and 81.6% (31/38 datapoints) with normalized OGM analysis. Lists of CN values obtained by FISH, as well as by OGM before and after normalization with DNA‐index are presented in the supplementary material, Table S7.

Identification of translocations and gene fusions

Targeted RNA‐seq detected subgroup‐defining rearrangements in 51% of cases (n = 26), as shown in the supplementary material, Table S8. OGM identified 92% of these rearrangements, while two IGH translocations (IGH::DUX4 in P_46 and IGH::EPOR in P_49) detected with low read count by RNA‐seq (four spanning reads in both cases) were not recognized by OGM. In patient P_46, FISH did not identify the IGH rearrangement either. However, OGM confidently identified the t(6;14)(p22.3;q32.33) (IGH::ID4) translocation in patient P_13, with the breakpoint on chromosome 6 located 23 kb centromerically to the ID4 gene, covered by the RNA‐seq panel and exhibiting overexpression. As expected, P2RY8::CRLF2 fusions (n = 3) were detected as deletions in the PAR1 region by OGM, with subsequent visual inspection confirming the presence of the gene fusion (supplementary material, Figure S7). No IGH::CRLF2 rearrangements were detected in our cohort by either OGM or RNA‐seq.

Altogether, 160 intrachromosomal fusions and interchromosomal translocations were identified by OGM, with 15 of the translocations being reciprocal and counted as a single event (supplementary material, Table S9). Of the OGM‐detected variants, 35% (n = 56) were covered by and, thus, analyzable with the RNA‐seq panel. Targeted RNA‐seq, however, confirmed only 55% (31/56) of these alterations, with the discordance between OGM and RNA‐seq affecting 17/51 patients. In 9 out of 17 patients, ETV6 and/or RUNX1 genes were involved in variants not identified by RNA‐seq; therefore, these aberrations were further scrutinized.

In patient P_31, the signal pattern of the ETV6::RUNX1 dual‐fusion FISH probe suggested a reciprocal translocation, while RNA‐seq only reported the ETV6::RUNX1 transcript. OGM revealed an additional deletion of the rearranged chromosome 12 segment, conferring a RUNX1::LRP6, instead of the RUNX1::ETV6 fusion (supplementary material, Figure S8). In patient P_50, deletion of the 12p13.2p12.1(11736073–24446506) region and concurrent ETV6::SOX5 fusion on the allele not involved in the ETV6::RUNX1 fusion were identified by OGM. RNA‐seq only detected the reciprocal ETV6::RUNX1 translocation, with FISH showing slightly atypical signal pattern (supplementary material, Figure S9). In seven cases, complex ETV6::RUNX1 translocations were detected by OGM. In five out of seven patients, the atypical FISH patterns confirmed the rearrangements, with only a subset of the newly occurring fusions showing transcriptional activity in individual patients as revealed by RNA‐seq (Figure 4). In patient P_51, in‐depth variant analysis was performed by comparing the fusion extra‐signal FISH probe pattern and the RNA‐seq data, revealing RUNX1::SLC30A7 besides the ETV6::RUNX1 fusion (supplementary material, Figure S10). In patient P_25, RNA‐seq validated the additional, transcribed RUNX1 rearrangement identified by OGM, as presented in the supplementary material, Figure S11.

Validation of CNVs identified by OGM using digitalMLPA

Altogether, 424 variants were detected by both OGM and digitalMLPA, representing a concordance of 94% between the two methods. Regarding whole chromosome gains and losses (n = 147), the two techniques showed a concordance of 99%, with a single subclonal gain of chromosome 10 revealed exclusively by OGM (patient P_20). Among 271 subchromosomal variants detected by OGM and 260 by digitalMLPA, 254 CNVs were identified by both methods, demonstrating a high overall concordance (Cohen's kappa = 0.93). The kappa value for digitalMLPA target genes was 1 (perfect agreement) in 118/153 genes covered by the probemix, with all other, lower kappa values being presented in supplementary material, Figure S12. In nine patients, both OGM and digitalMLPA detected IKZF1 loss, while in P_47, a subclonal IKZF1 deletion was only reported by OGM. Additionally, six patients with IKZF1 loss harbored deletions of CDKN2A/B, PAX5 genes, or the PAR1 region in the absence of an ERG deletion (IKZF1 plus genotype), with two of them having a hypodiploid chromosome set. In patients P_16, P_23, and P_39 (HHD), no further deletions fulfilling the criteria of IKZF1 plus status were identified. TP53 deletions, commonly associated with adverse prognosis, were observed as part of larger CN losses in four patients: two of them having hypodiploid karyotype with 17 loss (P_09 and P_30), P_10 showed a 17p loss, while P_04 had a partial 17p loss resulting from an unbalanced t(17;20)(p11.2;q11.23) translocation. Discordances were conferred by (i) variants in the subtelomeric and pericentromeric regions, (ii) subclonal variants identified exclusively by OGM, or (iii) the overlap of digitalMLPA probes with the fringe‐zones of the OGM variants.

FriZone analysis of variants refines OGM data interpretation

Considering all variants detected by OGM, digitalMLPA probes overlapped with fringe‐zones 191 times in total. Based on the original analysis, concordance between the CN value of these digitalMLPA probes and OGM deletions was only observed at 74/191 data points [38.7%, positive predictive value (PPV) = 0.39]. The newly introduced FriZone approach significantly increased the concordance (McNemar test, p < 0.001, Figure 5A), with 145/191 (75.9%, PPV = 0.89) genomic loci showing congruent digitalMLPA and OGM CN values. Following re‐evaluation of fringe‐zone areas, Cohen's kappa values altered in 11 genes covered by digitalMLPA. For seven genes, an increase in the kappa value was observed; for three other genes, the value decreased; and in one case, re‐analysis did not change the level of interpretation (Figure 5B). The list of variants and corresponding digitalMLPA probes located in fringe‐zones is provided in supplementary material, Table S10.

Co‐occurrence of aberrations unraveled by OGM

Simultaneous presence of alterations was investigated in the entire patient cohort to reveal positive and negative associations between individual genetic lesions. Pair‐wise gene‐level scrutiny identified 151 statistically significant co‐occurrences between genes never affected by the same lesion (supplementary material, Table S11). In addition, 240 positive associations were observed between genomic clusters, defined as described earlier (supplementary material, Table S12). Significant co‐occurrences were found between ETV6::RUNX1 fusion and deletions of 12p (including ETV6), 11p12 (RAG2), 6q21 (FOXO3), and 4q31.23 (NR3C2), as well as between FHIT deletion and 12p losses. Beyond the positive gene‐level associations, nine significant co‐occurrences were observed between clusters not including any coding genes. Furthermore, the simultaneous presence of an ETV6::RUNX1 fusion and deletion of 6q22.31 (MCM9 and ASF1A genes), as part of larger 6q deletions or shorter SVs, was found in six patients. In nine out of 13 ETV6::RUNX1‐positive cases, at least two of the significantly co‐occurring events were detected (supplementary material, Figure S13). No statistically significant, mutually exclusive interactions were observed in our patient cohort.

Complex and clinically relevant KMT2A rearrangement resolved by OGM

P_41, an infant of 4 months, presented at the clinic with anemia, thrombocytopenia, and elevated white blood cell count (620 × 109/L). Flow cytometry of the blood sample showed 94% B lymphoblasts with an immunophenotype characteristic of KMT2A‐rearranged B‐ALL (CD19+, CD45+, CD34+, CD10−, and CD20−). Karyotyping was unsuccessful due to the absence of in vitro metaphases, and KMT2A FISH test with break‐apart probe showed a normal signal pattern (Figure 6A). Despite the negative FISH finding, clinical data and flow cytometry supported the presence of KMT2A translocation. Therefore, OGM was performed and revealed a KMT2A::AFF3 gene fusion (Figure 6B). In addition, the in‐depth scrutiny of the OGM pattern uncovered a 355‐kb‐long inversion on chromosome 11, conferring a KMT2A::TMPRSS4 fusion (Figure 6C). The 5' breakpoint of the KMT2A gene co‐localized with the 3' breakpoint of the AFF3 gene, while the 5' part of the AFF3 gene fused with the 3' portion of the DLG2 gene (Figure 6D). The KMT2A::AFF3 and DLG2::AFF3 fusions were validated by RNA‐seq, while the KMT2A::TMPRSS4 rearrangement by DNA sequencing. The FISH test failed since the 710‐kb‐long 5' part of the applied probe set covered the inversion over its whole extent. The more comprehensive OGM data unraveled the clinically relevant KMT2A::AFF3 gene fusion, which was not detectable by the more focused FISH assay, and thus enabled the enrollment of the patient in the Interfant‐21 clinical study.

Discussion

The heterogeneous genetic background of pediatric B‐ALL underscores the need for careful diagnostic assay selection to ensure accurate molecular subtyping and efficient risk assessment. Besides established molecular methods, cytogenetic techniques remain integral to the clinical workflow [5]. Although karyotyping is still widely used, it is inherently limited by factors such as low genomic resolution, dependence on dividing cells, and typically poor morphology of leukemic blast‐derived chromosomes, leading to overrepresentation of normal cells among the evaluated metaphases in some subgroups of the disease [30, 31]. Popular complementary methods also have significant limitations; for example, (i) FISH only allows for the simultaneous interrogation of two to four loci, (ii) array technologies are unable to detect balanced aberrations [32], and (iii) affordable next‐generation sequencing protocols typically include amplification of the input material, potentially conferring bias in the readout, and require distinct DNA and RNA assays for CNV and fusion gene detection [33].

OGM offers a remarkable technological advancement by enabling genome‐wide, PCR‐free screening for SVs and CNVs, with relatively high resolution and without a need for dividing cells and metaphase preparation [14]. Recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility and clinical utility of OGM in the context of hematological malignancies, with high concordance to karyotyping, FISH, and microarray for detecting recurrent aberrations [10, 11, 12].

This study provides a thorough performance comparison between OGM and various conventional and molecular cytogenetic methods, supplemented by in‐depth analysis of translocations validated through RNA‐seq. Since relative CN calculation normalized to the diploid genome, and genomic regions sparsely covered by fluorescent labels are characteristic limitations of OGM, we introduced methodological refinements during our technical benchmarking, thereby improving data interpretation.

Karyotyping and FISH were performed as required by the diagnostic workflow of B‐ALL [5]. However, G‐banding was unsuccessful in 21 cases, resulting in a failure rate higher than previously reported [34]. Additionally, in five cases with DNA‐index > 1.16, normal karyotype was observed, presumably attributable to the lower in vitro mitotic index of the leukemic cells [30]. While the DNA‐index estimates ploidy status without defining modal chromosome number or identifying specific chromosomal alterations that may further influence the prognosis of children with HHD B‐ALL [24, 35, 36], OGM accurately detected whole‐chromosome gains in all HHD cases, underscoring its added value even in genetic subgroups with generally favorable prognoses.

Of note, OGM effectively determined the IKZF1 status (normal, IKZF1 del, or IKZF1 plus) [37] in our patient cohort via a single analysis, reinforcing its utility as a standalone alternative to contemporarily utilized multiple, more conventional assays in determining IKZF1 status [37] and corroborating previous reports [38]. In addition, the current study demonstrates the capacity of the technique to simultaneously detect recurrent alterations conferring different prognosis, for example, high hyperdiploidy and IKZF1 loss, in the same patient, enabling a refined risk assessment that may support patient stratification [39].

Deletions involving CDKN2A/B genes and KMT2A translocations are associated with adverse clinical outcomes [4, 37, 40] and, in principle, are detectable by FISH [41]. However, a notable subset of CDKN2A deletions remained undetected in our cohort, consistent with previous observations by Savola et al [42]. Moreover, in one case, OGM revealed a KMT2A::TMPRSS4 fusion previously unreported in ALL, as part of a complex KMT2A rearrangement also involving KMT2A::AFF3, which was confirmed by RNA‐seq but missed by FISH due to its limited resolution. These findings highlight the intrinsic resolution constraints of FISH and underscore the need for higher‐resolution approaches, such as OGM, digitalMLPA, or RNA‐seq, to avoid overlooking clinically relevant, potentially targetable variants, particularly in the era of menin inhibitors [43].

Subgroup‐defining fusions detected by RNA‐seq were highly concordant with rearrangements identified by OGM in our cohort. However, two IGH rearrangements detected by RNA‐seq with low spanning read counts were not identified by OGM, despite Guermouche et al reporting successful OGM detection of IGH translocations in multiple myeloma [44]. Notably, t(6;14)(p22;q32), a recurrent translocation in B‐ALL was identified in one patient, with breakpoints located centromeric to the ID4 gene and within the IGH region, leading to overexpression of ID4 [45]. We observed discrepancies between OGM and RNA‐seq in detecting rearrangements driven by enhancer‐hijacking mechanisms, albeit at a lower frequency than reported by Ok et al [46]. Differences in RNA‐seq methodology and panel design, OGM variant filtering strategy, and cohort size and composition likely account for this divergence. While our cohort is limited to draw definitive conclusions about OGM's utility for detecting IGH rearrangements, our findings may suggest that IGH rearrangements are indeed detectable by OGM, though complementary techniques may be required, especially when no subgroup or other risk group defining alteration is identified.

ETV6::RUNX1 gene fusion is one of the most frequent genetic lesions in pediatric B‐ALL and is associated with favorable prognosis [47]. Three‐way ETV6::RUNX1 translocations are known, though rarely reported events in pediatric B‐ALL and are associated with worse event‐free survival compared to the reciprocal ETV6::RUNX1 translocation [48]. Surprisingly, more than half of the ETV6::RUNX1‐positive patients in our cohort harbored a complex translocation, confirmed by FISH or RNA‐seq. These findings suggest that OGM may serve as a valuable technique for identifying complex rearrangements, which may be more prevalent than previously recognized and warrant further investigation to explore their potential prognostic relevance.

Ploidy alterations affecting the entire genome, such as near‐tri/tetraploidies, are recurrent events in pediatric B‐ALL associated with favorable outcomes [49]. The limitation of OGM in accurately detecting these aberrations was previously reported, since the technology normalizes CN data to a diploid baseline. Although atypical CNV variant allele frequency (VAF) patterns may indicate the presence of near‐tri/tetraploidy, confirmation by orthogonal methods remains warranted [14, 50]. To improve the delineation of accurate modal chromosome number and the number of individual chromosomes affected, we introduced a DNA‐index‐based correction of the OGM‐derived CN values. While the DNA‐index estimates ploidy, it remains an approximation, necessitating additional methods to determine modal chromosome number and specific chromosomes affected, thereby enabling accurate cytogenetic diagnosis. By normalizing the OGM CN results with the DNA‐index in cases with unbalanced chromosomal CN changes, detectable in principle by OGM, the normalization had no positive effect. However, in cases exhibiting whole‐genome ploidy changes, which are undetectable by OGM, the adjusted CN values for individual chromosomes more closely approximated the actual chromosome numbers, suggesting the benefit of this enhanced method to achieve more accurate subgroup classification in pediatric B‐ALL. Consequently, a complementary reference method for absolute CN assessment is important when OGM is implemented in clinical diagnostics. This can be achieved by DNA‐index flow cytometry or by alternative cytogenetic techniques that provide absolute CN counts like FISH. The DNA‐index appears particularly essential when atypical CNV VAF patterns are detected, and OGM is used as the sole cytogenetic technique. However, if alternative methods for absolute CN assessment are available, normalization using the DNA‐index primarily serves as a supportive tool rather than a mandatory component of the diagnostic workflow.

Variant calling in OGM is based on analyzing modifications in the labeling pattern, such as missing or additional labels, as well as differences in molecule size between detected labels, in comparison with the reference molecule's labeling pattern. Consequently, certain regions between two labels may not be characterized with the desired resolution. Boer et al investigated these regions and confirmed the involvement of specific genes in the variants using Cas9‐directed long‐read sequencing, thereby clarifying the precise loci overlapping the tested variants [51]. Comparing the size of the non‐deleted DNA segments and the position of the loci of interest (in our scenario, digitalMLPA probes), relative to OGM labels, allows for a more accurate depiction of the variants and overlapping regions, also reducing the likelihood of false positive findings. By implementing our novel FriZone analysis, exons involved in a deletion could be identified with higher confidence, which is clinically relevant, since, for example, deletions spanning exons 2–8 of the IKZF1 gene are associated with an even worse prognosis than exon 4–7 or 1–8 losses in B‐ALL [52]. Similarly, deletions of individual genes such as TP53, known to be associated with poor prognosis [53], can be characterized with enhanced accuracy, akin to results reported by Boer et al [51], using, however, a single method only.

OGM identified a higher frequency of SVs in the ETV6::RUNX1 subgroup compared to HHD and KMT2A rearranged cases. A previous study by Brandes et al also reported differences between HHD and ETV6::RUNX1‐positive patients [54], confirming that secondary events are required for the leukemic transformation of ETV6::RUNX1‐positive pre‐leukemic cells, since the gene fusion alone is insufficient to induce clinically manifest disease [55, 56, 57]. Our co‐segregation analysis supports the necessity of these secondary events by showing a strong association between ETV6::RUNX1 fusion and deletions of recurrently affected genes, such as ETV6, RAG2, NR3C2, and FOXO3 [55, 58]. While a higher prevalence of 6q21 deletions was previously reported in the B‐other and HHD subgroups, our data demonstrate a strong association between these deletions and the ETV6::RUNX1 subtype [58].

Although the size of our current cohort is comparable to those in previous OGM studies, we acknowledge that future research involving larger patient populations is needed to confirm and validate certain findings regarding the prevalence and distribution of variants identified by OGM. In diagnostic settings where OGM is implemented, FriZone may support variant interpretation, and OGM has the potential to replace conventional methods such as karyotyping, FISH, array‐based genomic profiling, or MLPA for copy number and SV analyses, whereas the selection of complementary or alternative assays should be guided by laboratory infrastructure, sample throughput, and the required resolution and depth of analysis. While the novel FriZone analysis may improve OGM variant interpretation and thereby increase the inter‐method concordance, complementary methods remain necessary to mitigate the clinical risk posed by potential false‐negative findings. Furthermore, to obtain a comprehensive cytogenetic, genomic, and transcriptomic profile of patients, OGM should be combined with DNA and RNA‐seq to detect single‐nucleotide variants, small insertions or deletions, and transcriptomic changes, especially in cases where no clear subgroup or prognostic group defining alterations are identified. Finally, our validation was not performed using techniques enabling highly granular genome‐wide analysis, for example, whole‐genome sequencing (WGS), which remains the gold standard for detecting all classes of genetic variants across the entire genome and could serve not only as an optimal verification method but also as a more comprehensive diagnostic tool for identifying point mutations, SVs, and CNVs. Nevertheless, it is important to note that OGM may more efficiently resolve complex aberrations in some cases than short‐read sequencing, commonly employed in clinical‐grade WGS workflows [59].

In conclusion, our results highlight the value of optical genome mapping in the diagnostic workflow of pediatric B‐ALL and demonstrate that in certain scenarios, RNA‐seq remains instrumental in identifying translocations, especially if the affected genomic regions are challenging to map by OGM. Notably, our novel CNV and SV analysis strategies improve variant interpretation, enabling a more informative and efficient integration of OGM into clinical diagnostics, eventually facilitating an enhanced genetic characterization and risk stratification in pediatric B‐ALL.
