QSyncFold: quantum neural network for multidimensional sync-discovery in protein folding
Authors: Jinjing Shi, Peng Du, Wenwu Zeng, Wenxuan Wang, Shaoliang Peng, Xuelong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuantum computing provides alternative encoding and sampling paradigms for protein structure prediction (PSP), but existing quantum-PSP methods are often limited by resource-scaling issues and by discrete or inefficient encodings for continuous coordinates. To address these limitations, we propose QSyncFold, a hybrid quantum–classical neural network framework that combines quantum superposition with differentiable learning. QSyncFold employs ProtaQode to simultaneously achieve reversible continuous-space encoding of residue coordinates and parameterized interaction modeling. This is realized by encoding residue–pair interactions in superposition via a decomposable Any-State RY (ASRY) operator that is efficient for a limited qubit budget. Algorithmically, QSyncFold trades register size for iteration count, reducing the qubit requirement for each iteration from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $O(N)$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $3+\lceil \log _{2} N \rceil $\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $N$\end{document} is the number of residues. This design ensures the framework is experimentally viable under NISQ constraints. On short peptide structure prediction, QSyncFold achieved a 5.25-fold improvement in the lDDT metric compared with the Variational Quantum Eigensolver baseline and demonstrated a clear trade-off between qubit budget and convergence speed. While using quantum baselines as the primary comparison, the method performance approaches AlphaFold2 in the short peptide domain, with classical methods serving as background reference. This study advances the precision and methodology of quantum computing in PSP, illustrating a viable pathway for quantum algorithms in biomolecular modeling.

Introduction

Protein structure prediction (PSP) is the task of inferring 3D conformation from its amino acid sequence [1, 2]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, this process culminates in the polypeptide chain folding to form the tertiary structure, which may further assemble into a quaternary structure, making it an ideal challenge for deep learning methodologies. It remains a central problem in computational biology due to structure largely dictates function, which in turn enables inference of function [3–6] and communications [7–9]. Classical machine-learning methods, such as advanced deep learning models like AlphaFold [10–12], RoseTTAFold [13], and ESMFold [14], have achieved remarkable practical success and provide a natural benchmark for applied PSP research. Nevertheless, the underlying combinatorial explosion of residue–residue interaction patterns and the high-dimensional continuous search over atomic coordinates pose fundamental challenges, as the scale of the conformational space grows exponentially with sequence length and degrees of freedom. It has spurred the development of new representation and prediction paradigms beyond classical deep learning systems.

Protein folding process and prediction pathways.

Quantum machine learning (QML) is one such novel paradigm [15, 16]. By exploiting quantum superposition and entanglement, QML can represent and manipulate exponentially large amplitude vectors with few qubits, offering a fundamentally different mechanism for addressing data explosion and high-dimensional search problems at a theoretical level. This capability has been widely applied in fields such as computer vision [17–20], graph theory [21, 22], natural language processing [23, 24] and software engineering [25]. Early efforts to apply QML to PSP have taken several approaches, including Hamiltonian-based formulations using Variational Quantum Eigensolvers (VQE) to minimize energy-like objectives [26], Grover-inspired search schemes adapted to folding constraints [27, 28], and hybridizations of classical deep models with quantum walks or other quantum subroutines [29]. Figure 2 shows the lattice network encoding and the QNN workflow employed by existing QML approaches. Although these works confirm the feasibility of quantum approaches on toy or medium-sized instances, they simultaneously expose recurring practical limitations regarding scalable PSP on near-term devices.

Previous QML encoding and QNN learning methods.

Concretely, we identify three interrelated limitations of existing PSP quantum computing methods that hinder their extensibility to realistic modeling tasks. First, resource scaling remains a challenge, as many formulations require a number of qubits that grows linearly with sequence length, such as VQE needing at least \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $2(n-3)$\end{document} qubits and Grover algorithm requiring \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $3n$\end{document} qubits for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $n$\end{document} residues. This is incompatible with current NISQ hardware and prevents the exploration of moderate-length proteins. Second, lattice-based models failed in representing amino acid spatial arrangements in continuous space, deviating from natural conformations. Third, residue interactions are confined to nearest and next-nearest neighbors in VQE, neglecting long-range effects, while static Hamiltonian parameters hinder adaptability across diverse protein structures. These constraints jointly limit both the fidelity of encoded structures and the practical applicability of PSP quantum methods beyond simple proof-of-concept instances.

Quantum neural networks (QNNs), which are hybrid architectures that parameterize quantum circuits and optimize parameters with classical optimizers, offer a promising route to mitigate these limitations [30, 31]. QNNs are differentiable, can encode continuous variables via parameterized rotations, and permit architectural design that trades qubit budget for quantum circuit and iterative computation.

In this paper, we introduce Quantum Neural Network Multidimensional Sync-Discovery in Protein Folding (QSyncFold), a QNN-based model featuring the Protein Quantum Encoder (ProtaQode) with sub-components Quantum Interactive Residue-Pair Encoder (QIRE) and Quantum Continuous Space Encoder (QCSE). QIRE employs a novel Any-State RY (ASRY) gate to represent residue–pair interactions within superposition systems, while QCSE enables continuous space representation, departing from lattice constraints. Protein quantum encoder ProtaQode

ProtaQode is an encoder designed to map the information of protein into quantum states, facilitating their use as inputs for QNNs. This encoder encompasses the coding of positional information as well as the encoding of interaction intensities. Quantum interactive residue-pair encoder, QIRE, is an innovative technique employing superposition states to denote the interactive strength between residues. Protein is a biological macromolecule composed of amino acids as its basic building blocks. The interactions among various residues determine the 3D structure and functionality of proteins [32]. However, the interactions of natural states change based on environmental conditions, residue types, sequence positions, and the impact of adjacent residues. Consequently, employing learnable parameters to denote interaction intensities can encapsulate the dynamic behavior of proteins across varying environments. The adaptability is not provided by conventional static models. At the same time, the superposition states can significantly reduce qubits and contribute to the subsequent prediction of structures. Model workflow of QSyncFold

The model is a classical-quantum hybrid that implements parameter processing and post-processing on a classical computer, encoding data and optimizing the residue structure on a quantum computer, and fine-tunes parameters using adjoint differentiation and gradient descent methods during training. The model primarily contains four components as shown in Fig. 7. The initial component is the random structure generation module, which employs the QCSE approach. In QCSE, prior knowledge of the training set is utilized to initialize the random structure effectively, detailed in Supplementary E. By randomly generating dozens of structure batches for optimization, the jitter of the results can be eliminated to a certain extent. The second module calculates the parameters necessary for the quantum circuit. Alongside the pertinent values presented in QIRE and QCSE, other auxiliary parameters, including direction vectors and distance maps, are also computed. The subsequent component is the quantum circuit. Each residue \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $r_{i}$\end{document} utilizes the relative position and interaction strength of other residues to optimize the positioning of residue \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $r_{i}$\end{document}. The optimal protein structure can ultimately be predicted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $N-1$\end{document} iterations for each residue (where the coordinates of residue \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $r_{0}$\end{document} are meaningless and fixed at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $(0,0,0)$\end{document}). The final stage is post-processing. Having been normalized before inputting, the coordinate data have to be restored during the measurement stage. Secondly, for some extreme value data, the first \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $25\%$\end{document} and the last \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $25\%$\end{document} of the dataset are eliminated, and the mean is computed to derive the ultimate relative orientation, which is subsequently transformed into Cartesian coordinates of the spatial structure.

Execute the aforementioned calculations for each interaction and coordinate. In training step, Adam optimizer [33] and the Adjoint quantum circuit differentiation method [34] are used. Results

This experiment assesses the performance of four protein-folding models, including VQE [26], QAOA [37], AlphaFold2 [12], and QSyncFold, using four metrics: RMSD [38], GDT-TS [39], lDDT [40], and TM-score [41], across two alignment methods (SVD and F3KP in Supplementary G for detailed algorithms). Single-chain small proteins were filtered from the RCSB PDB database [42] to constitute the dataset. This dataset was partitioned into training and testing sets using an 8:2 ratio, with training validated through five-fold cross-validation. MSA-only searches with ColabFold did not identify additional homologous sequences for any test protein.

QSyncFold achieves superior performance to prior quantum models

Performance comparison. The results under SVD alignment are presented in Fig. 8, while the results under F3KP alignment are shown in Supplementary H. Detailed data are provided in Table 1. QSyncFold demonstrates superior performance among quantum models (VQE and QAOA). Across GDT-TS, lDDT, and TM-score, QSyncFold significantly outstrips VQE and QAOA, highlighting its effectiveness in leveraging quantum computing for PSP. For instance, QSyncFold achieves up to a 5.25-fold improvement over VQE in the lDDT metric using the F3KP alignment method. Compared with earlier quantum algorithms, the QNN-based QSyncFold exhibits substantial improvements, effectively harnessing quantum computing properties to excel in specific problem domains. The analysis results shows that AlphaFold2 only maintain a slight edge over QSyncFold even though it outperforms previous quantum models across all metrics. On all metrics, the gap between QSyncFold and AlphaFold2 is significantly smaller than that between VQE and AlphaFold2. Specifically, on the lDDT metric, QSyncFold differs from Alphafold2 by merely 3.1%.

Resource consumption. A theoretical study on resource consumption was conducted to demonstrate the low resource requirements of QSyncFold compared with other QML models. When encoding coordinate information, the quantum coordinate state encoder (QCSE) uses a constant of 3 qubits. Additionally, for quantum interaction residue encoding (QIRE) with a maximum supported length of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ N $\end{document}, the use of quantum superposition reduces the required number of qubits to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ \lceil \log _{2}{N} \rceil $\end{document}. As a result, the total number of qubits used in the quantum circuit per iteration is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ 3 + \lceil \log _{2}{N} \rceil $\end{document}. Figure 12 highlights the significant advantage of QSyncFold in quantum resource utilization, particularly in adapting to the limitations of the noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era by conserving qubit resources. However, this efficiency comes at the cost of a linear increase in the number of iterations, which corresponds to the frequency of executing the quantum circuit.

Analysis of the biological significance of QSyncFold

QIRE encodes more interactions. Figure 14 demonstrates the superiority of our QIRE approach. The heatmap, with axes representing the protein sequence (PDB ID 7n2i), illustrates interaction strengths between residue pairs. Adjacent residues, primarily linked by peptide bonds, exhibit weak interactions with limited influence on global structure. In contrast, second-nearest neighbors display strong interactions, as corroborated by the Miyazawa–Jernigan matrix contact energy map [45]. Notably, QIRE also captures long-range interactions, which are essential for accurate PSP [46]. In summary, QSyncFold effectively integrates local and long-range interaction data, with QIRE enhancing ability of the model to extract critical information for precise structural predictions.

Significance of interacting pairs. Although QSyncFold does not explicitly model biochemical properties, its quantum interaction states reveal biologically meaningful residue-level patterns. Quantum state analysis in Fig. 15 reveals the dominance of specific residue pairs across spatial scales. At hop=1, pairs V-K, A-K, and W-K exhibit the strongest interactions, whereas at hop=8, only W-K remains significant. This prevalence of short-range interactions highlights their critical role in protein folding dynamics. Lysine’s positively charged \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\epsilon $\end{document}-amino group facilitates electrostatic interactions, such as salt bridges and hydrogen bonds with acidic residues, stabilizing tertiary structures and enabling DNA recognition via phosphate backbone interactions [47]. Its posttranslational acetylation, often mimicked by glutamine substitution, modulates interaction specificity [48]. Glutamine complements these effects through polar amide networks, particularly in proteins with Q/K-rich motifs, where transglutaminase catalyzes covalent cross-linking via isopeptide bonds [49, 50]. Together, these residues cooperatively enhance structural stability and functional regulation.

Conclusion

We presented QSyncFold, a hybrid quantum–classical neural framework for PSP that explicitly targets three key limitations of prior quantum-PSP proposals: inefficient resource scaling, poor continuous-space encoding, and limited learnability. The core encoder, ProtaQode, couples the reversible QCSE for real-valued coordinate encoding with the QIRE that embeds adaptive interaction strengths in superposition via the ASRY gate. Algorithmically, QSyncFold realizes a practical resource–iteration trade-off: per-iteration qubit usage is reduced to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ 3 + \lceil \log _{2}{N} \rceil $\end{document} for an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $N$\end{document}-residue chain.

Empirically, on our short-peptide dataset, QSyncFold substantially improves prediction quality relative to representative quantum baselines (VQE and QAOA), demonstrating concrete progress within the current landscape of quantum-based PSP methods. Depending on the alignment and evaluation settings, QSyncFold leads prior quantum methods across all metrics and achieves a 5.25-fold lead in the lDDT metric compared with the VQE method. Future work may explore integrating evolutionary information into quantum PSP, e.g. via quantum search algorithms such as Grover’s method.

In summary, QSyncFold demonstrates that QNN can provide a resource-aware and interpretable path for quantum-native approaches to biomolecular modeling. While significant engineering and algorithmic work remains before wide applicability on realistic proteins is achieved, our results clarify the theoretical and practical trade-offs involved and point to concrete next steps for scaling quantum PSP as quantum hardware and algorithms continue to evolve.
