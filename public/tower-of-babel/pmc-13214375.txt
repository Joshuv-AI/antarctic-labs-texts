Impaired removal of dying brain cells by microglia in Gpr34 deficient mice
Authors: Diana G. Bohannon, Ana Geller, Sean K. Simmons, John A. Cintron, William E. Martenis, Katherine Stalnacker, Autumn Masse, Min Jee Kwon, Yan-Ling Zhang, Joshua Z. Levin, Prabhat S. Kunwar, Morgan Sheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
GPR34, a G protein-coupled receptor present selectively in microglia and other myeloid cells, is highly expressed in homeostatic microglia but is downregulated in disease associated microglia (DAM) such as those found in Alzheimer’s disease brain. However, little is known about GPR34’s role in microglia function or brain development. Here, we studied Gpr34 knockout (KO) mice at postnatal 18-day (P18) and 3 months (3 mo) age. In the brains of P18 Gpr34 KO mice, there were elevated numbers of neurons, oligodendrocytes and microglia, many of which were IHC-positive for the cell death markers cleaved-caspase 3, phospho-RIP3, or annexin V. P18 KO animals showed a reduced localization of microglia in areas of high cell death, while the increased evidence of cell death way largely resolved in 3 mo animals. While the uptake of bacterial particles was not impacted ex-vivo, KO microglia showed increased intracellular accumulation of endogenous cargo, myelin basic protein (MBP) and synaptosomal-associated protein 25 (SNAP25), suggesting altered handling of apoptotic debris without a global phagocytic defect. Notably, transcriptomic analysis revealed persistent impact of immune pathways even at 3 months, at the stage where KO mice also displayed sustained hypolocomotion. Collectively, these results indicate that murine Gpr34 contributes to early clearance of dying cells and may influence a long-lasting impact on microglia state and behavior.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03777-4.

Introduction

The role of the innate immune system in neurological diseases has become prominent over the last decade, and single-cell RNA sequencing of brain resident immune cells, particularly microglia, in disease states has revealed many microglial genes of biological significance and potential therapeutic value. Multiple studies have compared microglia in normal and diseased states and identified a transcriptionally distinct subset of microglia in animal models and patients (especially for Alzheimer’s disease [AD]) termed “Disease Associated Microglia” (DAMs) [1, 2]. Additionally, there is a “Homeostatic” microglial subset which is depleted in the brains of AD patients and AD mouse models [1, 2]. Several microglial specific receptors have been proposed to regulate these state transitions and to distinguish homeostatic and disease associated microglia including GPR34 [3–5]. GPR34, (an X-linked gene selectively expressed in microglia and other myeloid cells) encodes a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) of the P2Y12-like subclass [3, 4]. Many RNA-seq studies find that GPR34 is highly expressed in all major populations of homeostatic microglia, but strongly decreased in DAM states [1–5]. Because it encodes a druggable microglia-specific target and its expression is profoundly suppressed in many neurodegenerative diseases, GPR34 has become a target of interest in understanding and modulating microglial function [1–8]. While most studies focused on the role of GPR34 in disease models, its role in microglia function during development is not well understood.

To understand the role of GPR34 during development, it is important to consider its signaling mechanisms and cellular function. Although GPR34 could be considered an orphan receptor, the phospholipid lysophosphatidylserine (LysoPS) has been proposed to be its endogenous ligand [6–11]. LysoPS is produced by the removal of one of the lipid chains of phosphatidylserine (PS) by enzymes like PS-specific phospholipase A1 (PS-PLA1) [9–17]. PS is a common phospholipid found on the inner leaflet of the cell membrane, and LysoPS can be formed when PS is flipped to the outer leaflet of a dying cell and interacts with extracellular PS-PLA1 [3, 5, 6, 13]. Additionally, LysoPS has been identified as a component of degrading myelin, and GPR34 may allow microglia to detect damaged myelin for removal [5, 18]. The presence of LysoPS in myelin may explain why the brain shows particularly high levels of LysoPS compared to other organs [14, 19, 20]. While the function of GPR34 in microglia is still unclear, these findings suggest that microglia may use GPR34 to detect and respond to dying cells and damaged myelin in the brain initiating a signaling cascade to support their clearance [4, 5, 8, 18].

Microglia, the major resident immune cells of the brain, make up about 10–15% of the cells in the brains of mice and humans [21, 22]. While microglia can proliferate in the brain and may occasionally repopulate from the bone marrow, the majority of microglia are seeded into the brain prenatally between embryonic day 8.5 (E8.5) and E12.5 [21–24]. Recent studies have established that microglia are essential for the developmental cell attrition, the brain patterning and organization, and that alterations in microglia function can impact brain development [24–30]. In adult mice, microglia have been implicated in maintenance of neuronal health, synaptic activity and connectivity, and are thought to contribute to many neurological disorders [2, 25–27, 30–39]. Given these important microglial roles in development, understanding how GPR34 contributes to microglia function in this context is highly relevant.

To investigate GPR34 function during brain development, we characterized Gpr34 knockout (KO) mice. We focused on postnatal day 18 (P18) as this period is characterized by major waves of cell death which require a particularly high level of support from microglia, the cells primarily responsible for the removal of dead cells from the brain [28, 29, 32, 40]. We compared these findings with 3-month-old brains, when the developmental cell death is largely resolved Through transcriptomic, functional and behavioral measures, we also examined the impact of GPR34 on clearance process, microglia state and behavior.

Materials and methods

Animals

Housing and care

Female Gpr34 ± mice on a 129/SvEv-C57BL/6 mosaic background (Taconic Biosciences; TF1866) were backcrossed to male C57BL/6 J mice (Jax) until confirmed to be genetically inbred on a C57BL/6 J background by miniMuga (Transnetyx). 12 wk old female Gpr34 ± mice were then bred with a 12 wk old C57BL/6 J male (Jax) mouse resulting in litters of Gpr34 ± or +/+ female and Gpr34 -/Y and +/Y male pups. Female mice were not used in this study, while Gpr34 -/Y mice were labeled Gpr34 KO and Gpr34 +/Y mice were labeled as Gpr34 Wt. Additionally, 2 outbred wild-type male mice (8 wks old) and 2 male 5xFAD mice (9 mo old) were used as high and low neuroinflammation controls to produce training images for image analysis algorithms. All animals were housed at the Broad Institute’s AAALAC-approved facilities on a 12-h light/dark cycle, with food and water ad libitum. All procedures involving Gpr34 mutant mice were approved by the Broad Institute IACUC (Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee) and conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All pups were genotyped by tail snip and tattooing at 7 days of age and returned to their parental breeder cage. Where applicable, mice were weaned at 21 days into home cage monitoring cages (detailed below) in sex and genotype matched littermate pairs receiving pellet and supplemental gel food and water ad libitum for the first two weeks followed by pellets and bottled water alone. Weaned mice were housed in a behavioral cubical on a 12 h non-inverted light/dark cycle on a rack with up to 80 other cages of gender and genotype diverse mice.

Euthanasia and necropsy

P18ay old male mice were removed directly from their litters, while 3 month old male mice were removed directly from their home cage monitoring cage and placed under deep anesthesia (4% Isoflurane). While under deep anesthesia, cardiac puncture exsanguination was performed and the blood was placed in an EDTA coated tube for downstream processing. Afterwards, the lower abdominal artery was cut, and mice were then perfused with 20 mL of ice-cold Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) or Phosphate-buffered saline (HBSS) followed by 20 mL 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at a rate of 2 mL/min for histological studies only. Spleens and hind leg bones were then collected for further processing, and brains were removed and separated into hemispheres before being placed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) at RT overnight. Mice used for RNA sequencing were similarly treated with the exception that they were perfused with ice cold PBS followed by HBSS instead of PFA and the brain hemispheres were frozen over liquid nitrogen vapors before being stored at −80 °C.

Histology

After PFA perfusion, whole brains were removed from skull and bisected into hemispheres which were placed in labeled 10% NBF jars RT overnight. These samples were then processed (Pegasus; Leica Biosystems) and embedded (HistoCore Arcadia H Embedding Station & HistoCore Arcadia C Cold Plate; Leica Biosystems) into paraffin blocks. 5 um sagittal sections were then cut from these blocks using a (HistoCore AutoCut Rotary Microtome; Leica Biosystems) microtome and mounted onto slides. One slide from the first sagittal slice underwent H&E staining (Slide scanner: Aperio Versa 8 Scanning System; Leica Biosystems), others were stained via immunofluorescence for further analysis as previously described [41, 42]. Briefly, sections were baked overnight at 60*C before being deparaffinated and rehydrated in xylene and serial alcohol baths (2 × 5 min each). Sections then underwent citrate-based antigen retrieval (Vector) in the microwave for 20 min before resting at room temperature (RT) for another 20 min. Slides were then washed in PBS with 2% fish skin gelatin (Millipore Sigma) (PBS/FSG) 3 times for 3 min each on a rocker hence forth described as the wash step. Washed slides were then permeabilized using a solution of 0.1% Triton X-100 (Fisher Scientific) in PBS at RT for 1 h. Sections were then washed and incubated with 5% natural goat serum (Fisher Scientific) with 1% tween 20 (Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at RT. After tapping off serum, primary antibodies were then applied at concentrations listed in Supplemental Table 1a at RT for 1 h. After washing off primary antibodies, secondary antibodies made in goat (Jackson Immuno) were applied at 1:500 at RT for 1 h. Secondary antibodies were then washed off, and primary and secondary antibody steps were repeated as needed. After all antibodies were washed off, slides were incubated with DAPI (Fisher Scientific) at 1:10,000 RT for 5 min before being washed an incubated with Vector TrueVIEW Autofluorescence Quenching Kit for 5 min at RT. Slides were then washed with water, and mounted with VectaShield hard set antifade mounting media (Vector) and left to dry at 4*C overnight. 12–24 h after mounting, slides were loaded into an AxioScan slide scanner (Zeiss) and whole sagittal slices were scanned at either 20X or 40X using a combination of DAPI, AF488, AF555, and AF647 appropriate filters. Scans were then saved in a de-identified manner such that genotype could not be discerned by image name.

Image analysis

De-identified scanned images were then pooled into a folder based on staining parameters. Training images from 2 outbred wild-type male mice (8 wks old) and 2 male 5xFAD mice (6 mo old) were then analyzed using QuPath open-source software following previously validated and published methodologies [32, 41, 42]. In short, scanned slices were manually divided into cortex, hippocampus, thalamus, and hind brain (pons and medulla) based on 14 day or 30 day old mouse brain maps from the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas according to the diagram presented in supplemental Fig. 1a [43]. Percent area detections were established by by setting thresholding parameters for each marker of interest by electing a value that appropriately distinguished positive and negative staining in both Wt and 5xFAD test mice in all brain regions. This threshold was then universally applied to all brain sections stained with this marker and the resulting positive area was divided by the total area of that region to calculate percent area.

Positive cell counts were quantified using QuPath’s positive cell detection tool to identify nuclei using DAPI channel with a pixel size of 0.1um and nuclear identification parameters of 8um background radius, 10-400um2 area, and a threshold of 100. Single threshold positive detection intensities were set in an identical method to the percent area thresholds based upon Wt and 5xFAD test tissues and universally applied across groups. Once parameters had been set using training data, protocols were applied across all samples and regions of interest identically in a double blinded manner and the results were exported as a csv file. Region area, DAPI number, positive cell number, and positive area data were then exported to Excel where they were compiled into a master worksheet that re-identified the exported data. Statistical variability amongst populations of interest were then graphed and assessed in GraphPad Prism 9. Statistical significance in differences between groups were analyzed by performing an unpaired t-test (multiple or single) between Wt and KO mice of the same age group in the same brain regions. Differences between brain areas or animals of different ages were not assessed. These results were reported as nominal significance p < 0.05 *, p < 0.01 **, p < 0.001 ***, p < 0.0001 ****.

Bulk RNA sequencing

HBSS perfused left brain hemispheres were frozen over liquid nitrogen vapors and stored at −80*C in preparation for RNA extraction and bulk RNA sequencing. The somatosensory cortex, motor cortex, hippocampus, thalamus, and hind brain (pons and medulla), were micro-dissected over dry ice. Each brain region from each animal was then separately prepared for bulk RNA sequencing. RNA was isolated from tissues using the Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit per manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, tissue samples were lysed and homogenized through use of Qiashredders, then placed into columns, and bound to RNeasy silica membrane. Contaminants were washed away, and columns were treated with DNase (Qiagen provided) to digest any residual DNA, and concentrated RNA was eluted in water. The RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer and RNA integrity (RIN) was measured with RNA pico chips (Agilent technology) using a 2100 Bioanalyzer Instrument (Agilent). Purified RNA was stored at −80 C until library preparation for bulk RNA-sequencing analysis. Bulk sequencing libraries were prepared using Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA Kit (Catalog #20,020,595) per manufacturer’s instructions. 300 ng of total RNA from each sample was used and the concentration of resulting cDNA library was measured with High Sensitivity DNA chips (Agilent) using a 2100 Bioanalyzer Instrument (Agilent). A 5 nM normalized library was pooled, and sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq X 25B (Illumina) with 50 bases each for reads 1 and 2 and 8 bases each for index reads 1 and 2. The depth of sequencing for each individual library ranged from 30.5 million to 645 million before downsampling, with a median of 49 million reads.

Bulk RNA sequencing analysis

For processing of raw reads, we used our in-house pipeline as reported previously [7]. In particular, reads were mapped to the mm10 genome with STAR v2.7.9a [44] and QC was calculated on the resulting BAM file with PICARDTools CollectRNASeqMetrics v2.27.5 [45]. Quantification was performed with Salmon v1.6.0 [46] with the arguments -l A –posBias –seqBias –gcBias –validateMapping and a reference based on the GTF and FASTA used for the mm10 10X Cell Ranger reference, with the full genome as decoys. Next, tximport v1.18.0 [47] as used to import the data into R v 4.0.3 [48], and the count data were downsampled to 50 million reads per sample using the rbinom command in R (for samples with < 50 million reads no downsampling occurred). The count data were then loaded into a DESeqDataSet object using DESeq2 v1.30.1 [49]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed by using the vst and PlotPCA functions in DESeq2. One sample was removed that clustered with the wrong age group in PC space and PCA was recalculated. For each brain region and age, the samples from that brain region and age were extracted. Genes that did not have at least 5 reads in 3 samples were excluded. Normalized counts were extracted from the DESeq object and the top 2 surrogate variables (SVs) were calculated with the svaseq command in sva v3.38.0 [50], and the DESeq command was used to calculated DE genes between KO and WT, with the top 2 SVs included as covariates. This was followed by shrinkage of the logFC with lfcShrink with type = ”normal”. The results without shrinkage were also extracted for further analysis, see below. Next, gene set enrichment analysis was performed with fgsea v1.16.0 [51]. For the GO analysis, the gene sets were downloaded with AnnotationDbi v1.52.0 [51]. For the DAM and Homeostatic gene sets, the gene sets were extracted from differential expression from a previous publication [2]. These gene sets were fed into fgsea, with the genes ordered by the stat column in the DESeq DE tables. The KO and WT labels for each mouse were permuted 20 times at random, and for each permutation the DE genes for all brain regions and ages were calculated as described above, except without shrinkage. TRADE v0.1.0 [52] was then used to calculate the TWI score for the permuted and unpermuted datasets, and a permutation p-value was calculated. Supporting information can be found in supplemental Fig. 2.

Microglia phagocytosis assays

In vivo phagocytosis intracellular flow cytometry

Right brain hemispheres of P18 and 3 mo Gpr34 KO and Wt animals were collected after HBSS perfusion and processed into a single cell suspension using an adult mouse brain dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotechnologies) including debris removal and red blood cell lysis. Samples were then split in half and one half was stained with Zombie NIR (Biolegend) for 20 min in the dark before fixation and permeabilization (CytoFix/Perm, Biolegend). Samples were then stained for 20 min in the dark with antibodies against CD45, CD11b, CX3CR1, myelin basic protein (MBP), and Snap25 (Supplemental Table 1b). Samples were then washed and imaged on a Sony ID7000 along with appropriate single color, unstained, and fluorescent minus one (FMO) controls. Unmixing was performed by loading unstained and single-color control samples from each tissue type. Unmixed samples and controls were then exported to FCS Express for further analysis. Analyzed flow cytometry results were exported to Excel and graphing and statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Statistical significance in differences between samples were assessed by two-way ANOVA. These results were reported as nominal significance p < 0.05 *, p < 0.01 **, p < 0.001 ***, p < 0.0001 ****.

Ex vivo E. coli pHrodo Red phagocytosis assay

Half of the single-cell suspensions from the right hemisphere of P18 and 3mo Gpr34 KO and Wt mice were incubated with pHrodo red tagged E. coli according to manufacturer’s direction and incubated at room temperature for one hour. After one hour, pHrodo Red was washed out three times and cells were stained with Zombie NIR, CD45, CD11b, CX3CR1 for 20 min in the dark (Supplemental Table 1b). Samples were then washed and imaged on a Sony ID7000 along with appropriate single color, unstained, and FMO controls. Unmixing was performed by loading unstained and single-color control samples from each tissue type. Unmixed samples and controls were then exported to FCS Express for further analysis. Analyzed flow cytometry results were exported to Excel and graphing and statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Statistical significance in differences between samples were assessed by two-way ANOVA. These results were reported as nominal significance p < 0.05 *, p < 0.01 **, p < 0.001 ***, p < 0.0001 ****.

Behavior

Home cage monitoring system

A Digital Ventilated Cage (DVC) monitoring system (Techniplast) was used to monitor the behavior of mice from the 3 month old cohort from 21 days to necropsy at 3 months. Pairs of genotype matched littermate male mice were placed into a DVC rack with a 12 h light/dark cycle, food and water availability consistent to what is found in their breeding colony in accordance with the animal housing and care described above. With two animals per cage, only the locomotion index readout is considered to be reliable for reporting by Techniplast. Therefore, locomotion index data were exported by hour for light and dark periods separately. The data were subsequently cleaned to remove any hours in which the cage was removed from the rack during that recording period, and datapoints were exported into GraphPad Prism 9 for statistical analysis. Statistical significance in differences between samples were assessed by determining the area under the curve for each Wt and KO cages. The areas under the curve for each group were then compared by a t-test to determine whether reported differences were statistically significant. These results were reported as nominal significance p < 0.05 *, p < 0.01 **, p < 0.001 ***, p < 0.0001 ****.

Open field test

At two months of age, mice in home DVCs were removed from their racks and placed in a closed room containing open field chambers to habituate for 30 min. After habituation, mice were removed from their cages one at a time and placed in Omnitech Electronics Inc SuperFlex Open Field apparatus (cleaned with 70% ethanol) and allowed to freely explore the lit (white light) open field box (16 inch × 16 inch dimensions) for 60 min. Mice were then returned to their pair housed cages, which were replaced on the DVC racks, and behavioral data were exported into Excel for further analysis. Features of interest were then exported into GraphPad Prism 9 for statistical analysis. Flow cytometry

Blood was collected via cardiac puncture and placed in a tube containing EDTA. Hind legs and spleens were collected from P18 and 3mo old mice after perfusion with ice cold PBS under anesthesia and prior to perfusion with PFA. Bone marrow was collected by cleanly removing the femur and tibia and spinning them down at 1000 G in an empty Eppendorf tube for 30 s. Spleen was mashed through a 70 um strainer, and cells were collected in 1 mL of PBS. Cells were washed in 2 mL ice cold PBS then cells from all 3 sample types were counted and 10 million cells were added to clean flow staining tubes. Remaining cells were pooled by sample type and used for unstained, single color control, and fluorescence minus one (FMO) controls. CD32/CD16 block (Biolegend) was applied to all tubes for 5 min, before Zombie NIR was added for another 10 min. Zombie NIR positive control tube was placed in the oven at 90*C for 2 min to kill a portion of the cells. Appropriate antibodies (listed in Supplemental Table 1c) were then added to each tube and briefly vortexed before being incubated in the dark for 20 min. After staining, 1 mL of 1 × red blood cell lysis buffer (BD) was added to each tube and incubated at 4*C in the dark for 10 min. Tubes were then spun down at 700 G and washed 3 times with 3 mL ice cold PBS at 700 G for 10 min each.

Samples were then transferred into a deep well plate and immediately run on an ID7000 spectral flow analyzer (Sony) until sample wells were empty. Unmixing was performed by loading unstained and single-color control samples from each tissue type. Where necessary, minimal compensation was applied to supplement Sony’s spectral unmixing algorithm. Unmixed samples and controls were then exported to FCS Express for further analysis. Bone marrow progenitor populations were analyzed along with mature immune populations from all sample types to identify populations defined in Supplemental Table 2. FMO controls were used to determine where gate placement should be assigned when necessary. Population numbers were then exported into Excel where both number of cells per million and percent of parent quantifications were assessed for populations of interest. Statistical variability amongst populations of interest were then graphed and assessed in GraphPad Prism 9. Statistical significance in differences between groups were analyzed by performing a multiple unpaired t-test with Holm-Sidak correction for multiple comparisons between Wt and KO mice of the same age group in the same organs. Differences between organs or animals of different ages were not assessed. These results were reported as FDR corrected significance p < 0.05 *, p < 0.01 **, p < 0.001 ***, p < 0.0001 ****.

Results

Increased numbers of cells with programmed cell death markers in Gpr34 KO brain at P18

We evaluated the brains of P18 old Gpr34 KO mice by histology, during a postnatal period when there are waves of cell attrition which rely on microglial clearance [4, 5, 8, 18]. As LysoPS, the proposed ligand of GPR34, can be produced in part by dying cells or dystrophic myelin, we investigated cell death related proteins by immunostaining in brain sections from of P18 Gpr34 KO and WT mice [5, 9, 14, 18, 53, 54]. To capture cells in the process of programmed cell death, we stained for cleaved caspase 3 (c-Casp3, a marker of apoptosis) and phosphorylated Receptor-interacting protein kinase 3 (p-RIP3, a marker of necroptosis) as well as Annexin V (Anx5, which binds externalized cell surface PS during cell death) [55–57] (Fig. 1 a-f). By measuring the colocalization of c-Casp3, p-RIP3, and Anx5 with cell type specific markers, we found that a twofold higher percent of neurons were immunoreactive for p-RIP3 in the cortex and a 3.5-fold higher in the hind brain of Gpr34 KO animals (Fig. 1 g,h) Additionally, we observed a twofold increase in Anx5 percent area colocalization with neuronal microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2) in the hind brain of P18 KO mice (Fig. 1i). Oligodendrocytes showed a twofold increase in the percent of cells with c-Casp3 immunoreactivity in the cortex of KO mice, and ANX5 showed increased colocalization to myelin basic protein (MBP) percent area at an approximately twofold higher level in all brain regions except hind brain which had a 1.5-fold increase in P18 KO mice (Fig. 1 j-l). Pu.1 positive macrophages showed increased levels of p-RIP3 colocalization in KO mice with a fourfold increase in the cortex, 1.5-fold increase in the thalamus, and twofold increase in the hind brain (Fig. 1 m, n). A corresponding increase in the percent of microglia that colocalize with Anx5 was observed with a fivefold increase in the cortex, and ninefold increase in the hind brain of Gpr34 KO mice (Fig. 1 o). Finally, no significant differences were observed in c-casp3, p-RIP3, or Anx5 colocalization with astrocyte markers when Gpr34 KO and WT mice were compared (Fig. 1 p-r). Collectively these results demonstrate an increase in the number of cells with cell death markers in KO animals at P18, particularly in the cortex and hind brain with more macrophages and neurons showing evidence of necroptosis and more oligodendrocytes showing evidence of apoptosis. Increased density of neurons, microglia, and oligodendrocytes in Gpr34 KO brain at P18ays

Since an increase in the steady state number of cells showing positive markers for programmed cell death could be due to elevated cell death, or reduced clearance of dying cells in Gpr34 deficient mice, we sought to better understand the cell type composition in brain regions of interest. We measured the density of neurons and observed that NeuN density was reduced by 15% in the cortex and increased by 25% in the hind brain of P18 Gpr34 KO animals (Fig. 2a). Similarly, the density of MAP2-positive cells was reduced by 20% in the cortex, but increased by twofold in the hind brain of KO mice (Fig. 2b). The density of Sox10 + oligodendrocyte nuclei was 1.8-fold higher in the cortex of KO animals, and the percent area of MBP was increased by threefold in the cortex, twofold in the hippocampus and thalamus, and 1.5-fold in the hind brain of Gpr34 KO mice (Fig. 2 c, d). The density of Pu.1 + macrophage nuclei density was increased by approximately 2.5-fold in both the cortex and hind brain, and TMEM119 density was increased by twofold in the cortex and 2.5-fold in the hind brain of Gpr34 KO mice (Fig. 2 e, f). No statistically significant alterations in astrocyte density were observed between KO and WT Gpr34 mice (Fig. 2 g, h). Transcriptomic changes in Gpr34 KO mouse brains highlight reduced immune function and chemotaxis related pathways

To investigate molecular changes in Gpr34 KO brain by a more unbiased approach, we performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on dissected brain regions of P18 and 3 mo old Gpr34 KO mice versus Wt littermate controls. The bulk RNA-seq confirmed that Gpr34 RNA expression was eliminated in all brain regions and age groups (Supplemental Fig. 3a). As measured by Transcriptome-wide Analysis of Differential expression (TRADE) [52] or by the number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with adjusted p value of less than 0.05, the hind brain was the most affected region in P18 Gpr34 KO mice (Fig. 5a, Supplemental Fig. 3b) (Supplemental Document 1). Graphing the top five most differentially regulated genes in each brain area and age confirmed the loss of Gpr34 as the most differentially regulated gene in all brain areas, but additionally identified some age and region specific DEGs which may be of interest (Fig. 5b, Supplemental Fig. 3c).

In P18 animals, several other genes which are implicated in microglial function were lower in KO mice in at least one brain region including zinc finger protein 691(Znf691), purinergic receptor P2Y12 (P2ry12), and phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit delta (Pik3cd). In contrast, adhesion G protein-coupled receptor D1 (Adgrd1) also known as GPR133, was higher in the thalamus of KO mice. Several brain regions of 3-month-old KO mice had lower levels of P2ry12 and C-X3-C motif chemokine receptor 1 (Cx3cr1), both of which are selectively expressed in microglia. In addition to microglia-related genes, several genes with known neuronal functions showed higher mRNA levels in P18 KO mice in at least one examined brain region, including vesicle amine transport 1 (Vat1), prodynorphin (Pdyn), protocadherin gamma subfamily B, 1 (Pcdhgb1), neurotrophic receptor tyrosine kinase 2 (Ntrk2), and argininosuccinate synthase 1 (Ass1). Others, such as cerebellin 3 precursor (Cbln3), and Purkinje cell protein 2 (Pcp2) were lower in P18 KO animals compared to controls. Both Pcp2 and paired like homeobox 2 A (Phox2a) were lower in 3mo KO mice than controls in at least one brain area.

Discussion

Our study examined the role of microglial-enriched GPCR, GPR34, during a critical postnatal developmental period characterized by cell attrition, apoptotic cellular clearance and circuit patterning [3–18]. In contrast to previous studies focused on the disease models, we investigated the GPR34 function in this development context. We found that the loss of Gpr34 at P18 resulted in increased accumulation of dying cells, and elevated neural and glia cells, along with reduced proximity of microglia to the dying cells (Fig. 1–3). We also showed that bacterial phagocytosis is not affected, and the intracellular accumulation of endogenous cargo indicates altered processing (Fig. 6). Although these early cellular phenotypes resolved by 3 months (Fig. 4), transcriptomic analyses show persistent dysregulation of immune and disease related pathways, along with a sustained hypolocomotion phenotype (Fig. 5). Our findings support a model that GPR34 regulates cellular turnover and microglial interactions with dying cells during early development, with potential long-term consequences for microglia state and behavior.

At P18, we find Gpr34 ko mice showed increased cell death markers accompanied by elevated densities of neurons, microglia and oligodendrocytes. This is a developmental time window, where extensive programmed cell attrition and turnover happen, processes that are essential for proper developmental, behavioral and circuit outcomes. Given this context, our findings suggest that the lack of Gpr34 leads to impaired or delayed resolution or clearance of the dead cells, rather than simply increased production of cells or increased cell death alone. However, we cannot definitively resolve this issue without more direct kinetic analyses of cell proliferation and death. Microglia are the key mediators for clearing apoptotic cells and other endogenous debris during this developmental window and they do so by appropriately sensing, locating and engaging with the targets [21–39]. Notably, we find the reduced proximity of microglia to dying cells in the brain of Gpr34 KO animals. The fact that GPR34 is known to be activated by LysoPS, a lipid molecule generated during apoptosis [8–15], it is reasonable to expect that the loss of GPR34 may impair microglial sensing and/or response to apoptotic materials. Together, we suggest that GPR34 facilitates efficient microglial interactions with dying cells during early development. Further work will be needed to directly address this mechanism. Importantly, we find that the early cellular abnormalities are largely resolved by three months of age, suggesting a temporally restricted window of functional need or alternatively the emergence of potential compensatory mechanisms afterward. However, the early disruptions could still cause the long-lasting consequences. Indeed, we observe persistent alterations in microglial transcriptional state and motor behaviors in adulthood.

Our key finding that Gpr34 loss disrupts the clearance of dead brain cells during early development directly implicates defective microglial function particularly phagocytosis. However, phagocytosis is not a unitary process but rather a complex and highly context and substrate dependent [8, 40, 63–66]. Different substrates engage distinct receptors and intracellular cascade events. Efferocytosis, which is most relevant in our context, involves clearance of dying cells through recognition of “eat-me” signals such as phosphatidylserine exposed on apoptotic cells. While clearance of endogenous materials like myelin debris relay on complement receptor 3 (CR3), the bacterial clearance is enabled by recognition of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) through Toll-like receptors, Fc receptors, and complement pathways [64–66].

Our experiments addressed only selected aspects of microglial phagocytosis. We find that uptake of E. coli bioparticles is preserved in Gpr34 KO microglia, indicating intact PAMP-dependent bacterial phagocytosis and core engulfment machinery, and potentially arguing against a global defect in phagocytic uptake. However, increased intracellular accumulation of endogenous neural cargo, including MBP and SNAP25 in Gpr34 KO microglia should not be taken as enhanced phagocytic activity. This can be explained by the increase in vivo load or impaired lysosomal degradation and/or altered internal processing. This takeaway aligns with growing evidence that GPR34 regulates context dependent and substrate specific aspects of phagocytosis rather than global impact on phagocytosis. Prior studies, including our recent one, suggest context dependent effects on myelin, beta-amyloid and microbial substrates [8, 40, 63, 64].Importantly, because efferocytosis and degradation of endogenous substrates were not directly assessed in this study, defects in the apoptotic cell phagocytosis and/or downstream processing of endogenous materials cannot be ruled out and remain important areas for future investigation.

At the behavior level, the Gpr34 knockout resulted in a persistent hypolocomotion phenotype which was evident even at three months of age. This raises the question how early cellular defects could cause the persistent motor deficits. While there could be many possibilities, developmental transcriptomic analyses could provide us a mechanistic insight. Despite the early cellular death phenotypes being normal by adulthood, dysregulation of immune- and disease-associated pathways, evidenced by increased DAM signatures and a corresponding loss of homeostatic programs across brain regions is evident in three-month-old Gpr34 KO animals. It is possible that early cellular defects may initiate long-lasting changes in transcriptional state, including in microglia across the brain regions including the ones involving motor circuits.

The motor circuits, which include basal ganglia, cortico-striatal, and cerebellar regions, may depend on the continuous support of microglia for the proper functioning. Therefore, the compromised motor circuits due to dysfunction of microglial state may lead to the sustained hypolocomtioon phenotype observed in Gpr34 KO animals. This is consistent with other findings from Cx3cr1 and P2ry12 deficient mice in which neurodevelopmental abnormalities and altered cortical development resulted in generalized hypolocomotion, suggesting that a conserved mechanism may be at play with Gpr34 deficient mice [63, 67–74]. When considered together, our findings point to a unifying developmental cascade which has been observed in other models of developmental microglia deficits, but the direct causal relationship between immune or microglial state and motor behavior was not tested and remains an important direction for future studies.

Our work on Gpr34 knockout brains indicate that clearance of dying cells is impaired at P18, but this phenotype is resolved by 3 months of age. We hypothesize that in this context, GPR34 may be acting as a receptor to allow microglia to recognize and approach dying cells efficiently. GPR34 deficiency also led to similar neurodevelopmental pathologies associated with other key microglia gene knockouts, including low brain weight, cortical structural abnormalities, and hypomobility. We suggest that loss of GPR34 may have reduced the rate at which microglia are able to target dying cells resulting in their accumulation at P18, but eventual clearance by 3 months. Our findings highlight a role of GPR34 in developmental clearance of dying cells, and suggest overarching implications for the role of DAMs in neurodevelopment and in neurological disorders.
