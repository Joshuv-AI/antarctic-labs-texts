Variability in the regulation of medically important antimicrobials among countries: A One Health approach
Authors: Akinbode Okunola, Elliott Dennis, Matthew Hille
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe One Health approach recognizes that antimicrobial monitoring and regulations must jointly address the risks of antimicrobial resistance in human and animal health for effective strategy and policy to achieve global public health. We operationalize this principle by integrating the World Health Organization's human importance classifications with the World Organization for Animal Health's veterinary classifications to create a hierarchical framework that prioritizes human health. This framework underpins our unique “One Health Score” (OHS), to evaluate regulatory compliance of country-specific drug residue limits with international standards of Codex. Codex, the European Union, and the United States are identified as leaders of establishing maximum residue limits to which other countries defer to. Among pairs with medical classifications, 53% fall under the pairing of Highly Important Antimicrobials (H3) for humans and Veterinary Critically Important Antimicrobials (V1) for animals. The One Health Score is non-negative for at least 50% of countries at each medical importance level. However, 21% of the countries with H3V1 MRLs are laxer than Codex. Drugs with a high market demand in animal agriculture are the most commonly regulated among countries. This suggests drugs used less in food animals may not be adequately regulated currently, despite, in some cases, being highly important for human health. Country officials must balance economic and health priorities when setting or modifying drug residue limits.Keywords: One Health, Antimicrobial use, Antimicrobial resistance, Global health, Animal production

Introduction

One Health is an integrated approach to public health that recognizes the interconnectedness impact of antimicrobial use across humans, animals, plants, and the environment [1], [2]. It is used by international organizations as a framework to address complex global challenges such as antimicrobial resistance (AMR). AMR is one growing public health threat, driven in part by the misuse and overuse of antimicrobials in both human medicine and animal agriculture [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9].

Evidence from various surveillance studies highlights the relationship between antimicrobial use (AMU) in food-producing animals and the emergence of resistance in zoonotic bacteria [10]. For example, joint reports by the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), and European Medicines Agency (EMA) found statistically significant associations between the use of fluoroquinolones in livestock and resistance in E. coli, Salmonella spp., and Campylobacter jejuni. A similar link was found between macrolide use and resistance in Campylobacter coli [11], [12], [13]. Additional evidence comes from country-specific experiences. In the United Kingdom, resistance of Campylobacter jejuni isolates from retail poultry meat to ciprofloxacin and nalidixic acid increased substantially between 2001 and 2015 [14]. In the United States, enrofloxacin use in poultry was prohibited in 2005 following evidence linking fluoroquinolone-resistant Campylobacter spp. to retail poultry products [15]. Similarly, in Canada, the withdrawal of ceftiofur in poultry production corresponded with increases in ceftiofur-resistant Salmonella Heidelberg among both chickens and humans [16].

These documented links between veterinary antimicrobial use and human health outcomes have prompted antimicrobial governance, surveillance, and regulatory responses worldwide. Yet the effectiveness and consistency of these measures remain poorly understood. Globally, food-animal and aquaculture sectors account for approximately 73% of total antimicrobial consumption, raising concern about the transmission of resistant bacteria through food, soil, and water [17], [18], [19], [20].

One approach countries use to mitigate the risk of AMR in humans is through establishing maximum residue limits (MRLs) for veterinary drugs in animal products as part of their Sanitary and Phytosanitary (SPS) measures, with Codex Alimentarius (Codex), the joint Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and World Health Organization (WHO) body responsible for establishing science-based international standards for veterinary drug residues, pesticides, and contaminants in food, providing the international benchmark for global compliance. These MRLs contribute to food safety with providing a buffer against actual or suspected harmful effects of antimicrobial residue in food products, even at low levels. The process of setting these MRLs by country is expected to be based on scientific risk assessment and/or aligned with international standards set by Codex [21], although political economy considerations often influence the implementation and adoption of MRLs. While such considerations are important, this study focuses on patterns of regulatory alignment and divergence within the existing international framework.

Codex sets veterinary drug MRLs based on risk assessments performed by the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives, (JECFA) prioritizing public health [22]. However, scientific data regarding drug residues is often lacking, leading to gaps in veterinary drug residue regulations that, in some cases, can lead to antimicrobials being used in animals that have no defined international standard exist [21]. This practice has the potential to threaten human health if excessive drug residues are present in the final consumed animal products [6], [7], [23], [24]. Recognizing these risks, the Quadripartite One Health Joint Plan of Action (OHJPA) – a collaborative initiative of WHO, WOAH, United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) and the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) – has advocated for coordinated global governance and enhanced intersectoral cooperation to mitigate AMR [25].

Despite science-based approaches to setting national MRLs and the availability of international standards, an important gap remains in AMU governance. Existing antimicrobial classification systems developed by the WHO and WOAH provide guidance for preserving antimicrobial efficacy in human and veterinary medicine, respectively. However, these frameworks are applied independently and do not provide a mechanism for evaluating antimicrobial significance across both sectors simultaneously. As a result, there is currently no systematic framework for assessing whether national drug residue regulations reflect the principles of One Health. Consequently, little is known about whether countries apply greater regulatory scrutiny to antimicrobials that are critical to both human and animal health when establishing veterinary drug residue standards.

This gap is particularly relevant within the One Health paradigm. Antimicrobials that are indispensable for treating serious human infections while also playing important roles in animal disease management present unique stewardship and surveillance challenges. Inadequate regulatory controls for such drugs may increase the risk of AMR and undermine global efforts to preserve their long-term effectiveness. Yet there is limited understanding of the combined significance of antimicrobial classes and individual drugs across human and veterinary medicine, and whether this joint importance is reflected in national residue regulations.

To address this gap, we examine the prevalence of veterinary drug MRLs and their alignment with Codex standards through a One Health lens. Using WHO and WOAH antimicrobial classifications, we first develop a hierarchical framework that jointly characterizes the medical importance of antimicrobials across human and veterinary medicine. We then assess the prevalence of veterinary drug MRLs within each One Health importance category and evaluate the extent to which national standards align with Codex recommendations across countries, antimicrobial classes, and animal groups.

Methods

Human medical importance classification of antimicrobials

The WHO supports governments in managing AMR globally using the Access, Watch, and Reserve (AWaRe) classification system to guide global antimicrobial stewardship and surveillance. Together with the WHO Essential Medicines List (EML), these tools provide a framework that categorizes antimicrobials into human-only, human and animal use, and animal-only drugs, with human-only drugs being critical last-resort options for multidrug-resistant infections [26].

The classification system of WHO [26] evaluates drugs used in both human and animal medicine based on two criteria: Criterion 1 (CRH1) examines whether a drug class is critical for treating serious bacterial infections in humans, and Criterion 2 (CRH2) assesses its role in zoonotic infections or potential for the transfer of resistance genes. Drugs are further classified by priority: PF1 if a drug in the class is on the WHO EML and categorized as Watch or Reserve in the AWaRe list, and PF2 if the class treats infections linked to resistant strains, frequent invasiveness, and life-threatening risks.

Based on these criteria and additional prioritization factors, drugs are categorized into five levels of importance: Highest Priority Critically Important Antimicrobials (H1) [CRH 1&2 + PF 1&2], Critically Important Antimicrobials (H2) [CRH 1&2 + PF 1 or 2], Highly Important Antimicrobials (H3) [Either CRH 1 or 2], Important Antimicrobials (H4) [Neither CRH 1 nor 2], and Not Medically Important Antimicrobials (NIA).

Veterinary medical importance classification of antimicrobials

The WOAH evaluates antimicrobials' veterinary importance based on their use in food-animal production, considering all such antimicrobials as important [27]. WOAH employs two primary criteria for classification: Criteria 1 (CRV1) if more than 50% of responding veterinarians recognize its importance for veterinary use and Criteria 2 (CRV2) if the drugs within a class are used to treat specific bacterial infections, and no suitable alternatives are available. These criteria create three classification levels: Veterinary Critically Important Antimicrobials (V1) meet both criteria, Veterinary Highly Important Antimicrobials (V2) satisfy either criterion, or Veterinary Important Antimicrobials (V3) if neither are met. This classification system specifically excludes drug classes or subclasses restricted to human use, as well as those used as growth promotants [27].

Data

Veterinary drug MRLs were obtained from FoodChain ID Inc. (FoodChain) and formatted according to the methodology described in Okunola et al. [21] to get country-level MRLs for all drug-product pairs. This results in MRLs for 406 drugs used in 164 products across 60 commodity groups and 88 countries, as well as MRLs set by Codex. For this study, Codex is treated as a “country” to determine how other country's standards align. Approximately 55% of the drug-product MRL pairs have both WHO and WOAH classifications.

We then filter these results by the union of drugs that are listed by WHO or WOAH. This results in 679,139 drug–product pairs across all countries and Codex that included 344,676 (51%) of the total possible pairs used in both human and animal medicine, 30,546 (4%) as human-only, and 72,330 (11%) as animal-only. The remaining 231,587 country pairs (34%) in the database were left unclassified and removed from further consideration in the study. These substances primarily included environmental-use compounds, such as crop pesticides (e.g., Carbofuran, Sodium pentachlorophenate), and substances for which sufficient classification information could not be identified.

We further classified the 344,676 drug–product pairs based on their importance for human medicine as defined by WHO and veterinary purposes as defined by WOAH. Drug names were harmonized across FoodChain, WHO, and WOAH databases to ensure consistency. For example, Sulfamethazine and Sulfadimidine were treated as a single compound, Enramycin and Enduramycin were standardized as Enramycin, and Benzylpenicillin-related variants were consolidated under Benzylpenicillin. Approximately 191,160 pairs (55%) had published WHO and WOAH classifications, 67,284 pairs (20%) WHO only, 11,593 pairs (3%) WOAH only, and 74,639 (22%) no medical importance classification by either WHO or WOAH. The final pairs are classified based first on their human importance defined by WHO (i.e. H1–H4) and then on their veterinary importance defined by WOAH (i.e. V1–V3) giving 12 unique pairings (i.e. H1V1, H1V2, …, H4V3). Fig. 2 displays this filtering process. Thus, the final dataset consisted of 191,160 pairs across 88 countries and Codex, 164 products, and 76 drugs which are classified into 12 unique pairings.

Results

Regulatory characteristics of medically important veterinary drug MRLs

The cumulative distribution of medically important drug-product MRL pairs by country is shown in Fig. A1 in Appendix A. A majority – 60 countries (67%) – defer to external regulatory authorities for their MRLs. These include 41 countries that defer to Codex, six to the EU, five to the United States, and eight that rely on combinations of external standards (See Table A4 in Appendix A). The remaining 33% of countries establish their own national MRLs, but regulatory influence remains concentrated. Notably, just ten countries (34%) account for 82% of all medically important MRL pairs. The US, EU & Codex are the leading regulators of MRLs. Five countries defer to the US, six to the EU, and 41 to the Codex. When including countries that defer to them, the EU, US, and Codex collectively regulate 55% of the pairs, with the EU leading at 39%, followed by the US (13%) and Codex (3%).

By medical importance

As reported in Fig. 3, approximately 53% of the regulated medically important drug-product pairs belong to the H3V1 medical importance level – the 7th most important classification. H1V1 and H2V1 – 1st and 2nd importance classifications – accounted for 11% and 21% of total MRL pairs, respectively. The top three important antimicrobials for human health (H1−H3) that are also the most crucial for veterinary medicine (V1) account for 85% of the total pairs.

By animal group

We categorized the 60 commodity groups into 14 animal groups based on species' relatedness and obtained the prevalence of residue regulations by animal groups (see Fig. B1 in Appendix B). Avians are the most regulated, accounting for 30% of the medically important drug-product pairs followed by Bovidae (20%), Fish (11%), Camelid (8%) and Swine (6%) groups. The prevalence of medically important antimicrobials by drug class is presented in Fig. C1 in Appendix C. Globally, sulfonamides are the most regulated class, accounting for 22% of the total medically important drug-product pairs. Other prominent classes are penicillins (17%), quinolones (13%) and macrolides (11%) (see Table C2 in Appendix C for the distribution of drug classes across each medical importance levels).

By country

Fig. 4 illustrates the hierarchical distribution of medically important antimicrobials across the different importance levels for the US, EU, and Codex. In the US, approximately 25% of the MRL pairs fall within the H2V1 medical importance level, while 50% are in the H3V1 category. In the EU, around 12% of MRL pairs are classified as H1V1, 19% as H2V1, and 55% as H3V1. Codex shows a similar pattern, with about 28% of MRL pairs in the H2V1 category and 57% in H3V1.

United States

Data on antimicrobial sales (kg) by class and target animal biomass (TAB in kg) for 2020 were sourced from the US Food and Drug Administration [29]. Antimicrobial intensity (mg/TAB) was calculated using FDA methodology by normalizing class-specific antimicrobial sales using target animal biomass (TAB) for cattle, poultry, and swine. Tetracyclines represent the most consumed drugs, with an intensity of 45.1 mg/TAB, followed by penicillin (8.7 mg/TAB), macrolides (4.9 mg/TAB), aminoglycosides (3.7 mg/TAB), and sulfonamides (3.2 mg/TAB). These five classes all belong to the H3V1 medical importance level, accounting for approximately 57% of all the US MRL pairs.

European Union

Data on antimicrobial sales (kg) by class and animal biomass is available for each country in the EU and reported by the European Medical Association [30]. Because MRLs are established at the EU level, antimicrobial sales and population correction units (PCU) were aggregated across member states prior to calculating class-specific antimicrobial intensity. Penicillins (31.7 mg/PCU), tetracyclines (26.9 mg/PCU), and sulfonamides (9.9 mg/PCU) are the most intensely used antimicrobial classes in the EU. These are followed by macrolides (8.9 mg/PCU) and aminoglycosides (5.4 mg/PCU). Notably, these top five most intensely used veterinary drug classes are the same top five (71.8%) most regulated classes in the EU's food-animal production system.

Both the US and EU exhibit a strong alignment between antimicrobial sales intensity and regulatory oversight, with their top five most consumed veterinary drug classes also being the most regulated. In the US, tetracyclines lead usage (45.1 mg/TAB), while in the EU, penicillins dominate (31.7 mg/PCU). Despite differences in consumption patterns, both regions prioritize MRL regulations for tetracyclines, penicillins, macrolides, aminoglycosides, and sulfonamides, which collectively account for a large share of the MRL pairs.

One Health Scores for medically important veterinary drug MRLs

Country-level MRLs are used to calculate a “One Health Score” as an aggregate indicator of the country's regulatory compliance to the international standard Codex. Countries that fully defer to Codex were excluded from the OHS analysis because their score is mechanically equal to zero, while countries fully deferring to the EU or United States inherit the regulatory profile of those jurisdictions. Therefore, only the 28 countries who set their own national MRLs were examined. This results in 97,850 drug-product MRL pairs accounting for about 51% of the total medically important pairs. Of the 97,850 pairs, Codex standards exist for 10,315 pairs, or approximately 11% (see Figure D in Appendix D). The One Health Score is estimated only for pairs for which both country and Codex MRLs exist using Eqs. (1), (2), (3).

Discussion

This study highlights disparities in the classification and regulation of antimicrobials for human and veterinary use. Despite identifying both WHO and WOAH classifications for 51% of the drug–product pairs, nearly half of the dataset lacks classification. This gap underscores limitations in interagency coordination between WHO and WOAH, which hampers comprehensive assessments of the potential AMR risk and its implications for global One Health. Strengthening collaboration between these agencies is essential for a unified and effective One Health strategy.

Countries predominantly regulate antimicrobials with high veterinary importance: H1V1 (11%), H2V1 (21%), and H3V1 (53%). The higher relative prevalence of H3V1 drug-product pairs suggests prioritization of widely used antimicrobials over those most critical for both human and veterinary medicine (H1V1), likely due to resource constraints and market factors. U.S. and EU case studies support this conclusion, showing that the most consumed antimicrobial classes (penicillins, tetracyclines, and sulfonamides) are also the most regulated. This correlation demonstrates how market demand influences regulatory behavior, establishing a direct relationship between antimicrobial use intensity and regulatory stringency in both jurisdictions.

According to WOAH, V1 drugs that also fall under WHO's H1 classification require stringent oversight, as they are essential for both animal and human health. H1V1 drugs (11%) should not be used for prevention without clinical assessment, as first-line treatments without justification, or as growth promotants. The relative underrepresentation of critically important drugs (H1V1) in global residue limits raises concerns about the adequacy of current MRL regulation strategies. While widely used antimicrobials receive the most regulatory attention, gaps remain in setting residue limits for drugs that are crucial for both human and veterinary health. Addressing these gaps requires a more comprehensive regulatory approach that balances public health priorities with the realities of antimicrobial use in food-animal production.

Avians are the most regulated animal group accounting for 30% of medically important drug–product pairs. This aligns with the intensive antimicrobial use in poultry production, driven by high global demand for poultry products and the nature of current poultry production systems where birds are grown in confinement with high bird densities. Bovidae (20%) and fish (11%) follow, reflecting their importance in global food systems. In contrast, the relatively lower regulation of swine (6%) – despite swine being the world's most consumed meat and the highest consumer of antimicrobials, especially in Asia [18], [31] – indicates a critical gap in policy. If misuse or overuse of antimicrobials intensifies within swine production, this gap could have far-reaching public health implications. These findings highlight the importance of an animal group-specific regulatory approach to effectively regulate the use of antimicrobials, consistent with the principles of One Health.

The dominance of the US, EU, and Codex as global veterinary drug regulators is evident, with 5, 6, and 41 countries deferring to them, respectively. National policies that rely on MRLs set by the US, EU, or Codex create a potential gap in drug residue regulations since the relative importance of specific drugs among countries may vary depending on current, and potentially even more importantly, emerging disease trends within the local human population. This reliance underscores the disproportionate influence these groups have in shaping global AMR mitigation efforts.

Codex standards cover only 11% of the drug–product pairs for which countries have established national MRLs. The One Health Score analysis reveals variability in regulatory stringency across countries, especially in H2V1 and H3V2. At least 50% of countries achieve non-negative One Health Scores at each medical importance level, implying that countries tend to set MRLs that are compliant with Codex. Regulatory laxity in H2V1 is concerning with half of countries, on aggregate, having MRLs lower than Codex for drugs in this class of importance. The relatively stringent regulation of drugs used in avian species and specific drug classes such as cephalosporins (3rd-, 4th-, and 5th- generation), Aminocyclitols, and quinolones indicate the need for strong policy attention to reduce potential AMR risks to drug classes such as macrolides and tetracyclines that are the most consumed drugs in both humans and animals.

This study has a couple of limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the analysis is based on a cross-sectional dataset and therefore cannot capture changes in regulatory behavior over time. Regulatory priorities, veterinary drug residue standards, and the medical importance classifications published by WHO and WOAH evolve in response to emerging scientific evidence, disease patterns, and antimicrobial resistance risks. As a result, the study provides a snapshot of the current regulatory landscape rather than an assessment of how countries adapt their regulations over time. Second, the analysis relies on Codex maximum residue limits as the international benchmark for evaluating regulatory compliance. However, Codex standards are available for only a subset of veterinary antimicrobials, limiting the scope of comparative analysis. Third, the case study examining the relationship between veterinary drug use and regulatory prevalence uses antimicrobial sales data to estimate AMU intensity. While sales data provide a useful and widely available indicator of use, they may not fully reflect actual farm-level antimicrobial administration across species, production systems, or regions. Consequently, the observed relationship between antimicrobial demand and regulatory behavior should be interpreted as indicative rather than causal.

Conclusion

This study underscores the pivotal role of market demand in shaping veterinary drug regulations, particularly in resource-constrained settings. While focusing on more commonly used antimicrobials may increase the likelihood of detecting MRL violations, it may also inadvertently deprioritize drugs that are less commonly used in animal production systems but are nonetheless critical from a One Health perspective. Policymakers are tasked with balancing the health needs of humans and animals with, in some cases, limited science – all within a fixed national budget. The variability in MRLs among countries described herein, particularly those crucial to the well-being of both humans and animals, highlights a lack of data to support scientifically proven MRLs that are widely accepted globally. Enhanced international collaboration is crucial to enable more comprehensive assessments of potential AMR risks, fostering a robust One Health strategy that addresses antimicrobial use, particularly in the context of growing global food-animal production systems. The funding and execution of well-designed drug residue studies to fill these gaps in science are crucial to achieving this future goal.
