Can artificial intelligence-derived bone mineral density outperform traditional morphometric measurements in opportunistic osteoporosis screening using pelvic radiographs? A multicenter comparative diagnostic study
Authors: Chia-Che Lee, Xi-Qian Heng, Hung-Kuan Yen, I-Hsin Chen, Tzu-Hao Tseng, Hong-Da Lu, Hung-Chun Lee, Ruey-Feng Chang, Chen-Yu Wang, Shau-Huai Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo investigate whether an artificial intelligence (AI) model could demonstrate high diagnostic accuracy in identifying dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) confirmed osteoporosis, outperform traditional human-read metrics, and deliver substantial clinical net benefit.MethodsThis retrospective multicenter cohort study included 382 patients with hip fractures who had both preoperative pelvic radiographs and postoperative DXA scans. An AI model analyzed the intact contralateral proximal femur to predict both bone mineral density (BMD) values and T-scores. Diagnostic performance was evaluated against DXA-confirmed osteoporosis (T-score ≤−2.5) and compared with manual cortical thickness index (CTI) and femoral neck width (FNW) measurements using Spearman rank-order correlation analysis, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, the DeLong test, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsThe AI model showed a strong correlation with DXA-derived BMD values for both the femoral neck (ρ = 0.76, R2 = 0.55, mean absolute error (MAE) = 0.06 g/cm2) and the total hip (ρ = 0.69, R2 = 0.51, MAE = 0.08 g/cm2). Correspondingly, the AI-predicted T-scores correlated well with DXA-derived T-scores for the femoral neck (ρ = 0.76, R2 = 0.55, MAE = 0.47) and the total hip (ρ = 0.69, R2 = 0.51, MAE = 0.65). The AI model demonstrated excellent discriminative performance, achieving an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.88 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.83–0.93) for the femoral neck and 0.87 (95% CI 0.82–0.92) for the total hip, both significantly higher than CTI (0.72 [95% CI 0.65–0.79]; P < 0.001) and FNW (0.53 [95% CI 0.44–0.62]; P < 0.001). DCA illustrated that the AI models provided greater clinical net benefit than manual measurements, remaining clinically advantageous up to a threshold probability exceeding 0.90.ConclusionsDeep learning AI offers a superior, automated alternative to traditional manual morphometric measurements for identifying osteoporosis on routine pelvic radiographs in patients with hip fractures, potentially facilitating timely secondary fracture prevention.

Introduction

Osteoporosis, a pervasive and progressive skeletal disorder characterized by low bone mineral density (BMD) and a deteriorated bone microstructure, leads to compromised bone strength and an increased risk of fractures [1,2]. Despite its growing prevalence in an aging society, osteoporosis remains significantly underdiagnosed and undertreated, even among patients who have already sustained a fragility fracture [3,4]. For patients presenting with a hip fracture, identifying underlying osteoporosis is critical to initiating secondary prevention strategies, as these individuals are at a markedly elevated risk for subsequent, often devastating, contralateral fractures [5]. While dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is the established clinical standard for diagnosing osteoporosis [1], its utility as a screening tool is hindered by limited medical accessibility, the cost of dedicated equipment, and low screening utilization rates [[6], [7], [8]]. In contrast, standard pelvic radiographs are universally obtained during the acute management and follow-up of proximal femur fractures. Leveraging these existing radiographs to assess systemic BMD opportunistically presents a vital opportunity to reduce the substantial rates of osteoporosis underdiagnosis without requiring additional radiation exposure, separate clinical appointments, or excess healthcare expenditure [9].

Historically, orthopedic surgeons have attempted to estimate regional bone quality and fracture risk from these routine radiographs using manual morphometric measurements, such as the cortical thickness index (CTI) and femoral neck width (FNW) [[10], [11], [12]]. However, these traditional human-read parameters are limited by their subjectivity, reliance on two-dimensional structural approximations, and notable interobserver variability [13]. Recently, artificial intelligence (AI)—particularly deep learning—has demonstrated remarkable proficiency in extracting complex, subclinical quantitative features directly from standard medical imaging [[14], [15], [16]]. Consequently, AI-based interpretation of plain radiographs has emerged as a highly practical alternative for opportunistic osteoporosis screening [9,17].

However, while these deep learning models have been successfully validated in general, asymptomatic populations, their external clinical validity within high-risk hip fracture cohorts remains unexplored. Furthermore, it remains unknown whether a purpose-built AI model can provide more accurate opportunistic assessments of bone health than established, traditional human-evaluated radiographic indices when analyzing the same pelvic radiographs of these patients. Addressing this knowledge gap is essential to determine whether AI can reliably augment or replace human visual assessment to navigate strict insurance reimbursement criteria for bone-protective therapies, facilitate earlier intervention, and prevent secondary fragility fractures.

We therefore asked: (1) What is the diagnostic accuracy of an AI model derived from routine pelvic radiographs for identifying DXA-defined osteoporosis in a fracture cohort? (2) Do AI models demonstrate superior discriminative performance compared to traditional human-read CTI and FNW measurements in predicting osteoporosis? (3) Do AI models provide greater clinical net benefit than traditional manual measurements across varying diagnostic risk thresholds?

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective, multicenter cohort study was conducted using data from the National Taiwan University Hospital, Taipei main branch, and the National Taiwan University Hospital, Yunlin Branch. We focused on patients who presented with fragility hip fractures and had both a standardized pelvic radiograph and a DXA scan available for analysis. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the National Taiwan University Hospital, which waived the requirement for direct patient informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the imaging and data analysis (IRB No. 202512025DINE). Furthermore, this research adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [18].

Study population

We queried our institutional electronic medical records to identify patients who underwent surgical intervention for a proximal femur fracture over a 5-year period (from June 2020 to June 2025). To be included in the study cohort, patients were required to meet the following specific criteria: (1) presentation with a fragility-related hip fracture (femoral neck, intertrochanteric, or subtrochanteric fracture); (2) availability of a standardized, preoperative anteroposterior (AP) pelvic radiograph; and (3) completion of a postoperative DXA scan within 3 months after the fracture to serve as the diagnostic reference standard for systemic BMD.

Patients were excluded if their fractures were the result of high-energy trauma (eg, motor vehicle collisions) or if their radiographic images were of insufficient diagnostic quality due to severe patient positioning errors, excessive hardware artifact from prior surgeries, or incomplete capture of the contralateral (unfractured) proximal femur. The final study population enrolled 382 patients who met all inclusion and exclusion criteria (Fig. S1) [Supplementary Materials].

Baseline characteristics

Demographic and clinical variables were extracted from the medical records, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), study site, fracture anatomical classification, and the brand of the DXA machine utilized.

Manual radiographic morphometry

To serve as the traditional human-read comparison arm, manual morphometric measurements were performed on the preoperative AP pelvic radiographs. Specifically, the CTI and FNW were evaluated by two orthopedic surgeons (C.-C. L. and S.-H.F.), each with over 10 years of clinical experience.

The CTI was measured according to the established reference method described by Dorr et al. [11]. The total diaphyseal width (DW) and intramedullary canal width (FW) were manually measured 10 cm distal to the center of the lesser trochanter, and the CTI was calculated using the formula: (DW − FW)/DW. The FNW was determined by measuring the outer diameter at the narrowest width of the femoral neck, utilizing the structural analysis principles initially developed by Beck et al. [19]. To ensure measurement consistency, both intra-observer and inter-observer reliability were formally assessed for these parameters. The manual measurements demonstrated high reproducibility and excellent agreement within and across the observers’ evaluations, confirming the reliability of the traditional radiogrammetry arm.

To eliminate anatomical distortion, cortical disruption, and hardware artifacts resulting from the acute fracture, all radiographic assessments—both human-read and AI-derived—were strictly performed on the intact, contralateral (unfractured) proximal femur.

Artificial intelligence model

To autonomously predict BMD values directly from the two-dimensional pelvic radiographs, we deployed a commercially available AI software-as-a-medical-device (SaMD), the DeepXray platform (Alpha Intelligence Manifolds, Taipei, Taiwan) [20,21]. The DeepXray system has received US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) 510(k) clearance (K223621) [20] as an automated radiologic image-processing application, and its BMD estimation modules are approved by the Taiwan Food and Drug Administration (TFDA) [21]. The software was utilized in its original, unmodified form.

Constructed within the PyTorch framework, the algorithm operates via a three-stage deep learning pipeline. Initially, a YOLOv5-based object detection model localizes and delineates the region of interest (ROI)—specifically, the intact contralateral proximal femur. Following localization, an anatomical landmark detection network utilizing a High-Resolution Transformer (HRFormer) architecture achieves precise spatial alignment and extracts relevant structural features. In the final stage, a RegNetY-16GF convolutional neural network executes a quantitative regression on these geometric parameters and image features to estimate regional BMD.

The foundational AI model was trained using over 10 000 paired radiograph and DXA datasets sourced from external institutions independent of this validation study. No patient data or images from our participating institutions were included in the original training dataset, ensuring zero risk of data leakage. Therefore, this study serves as a strict, independent external validation. For our cohort, the algorithm was deployed in its original state to output predicted T-scores for the femoral neck and total hip, with no supplementary local training, fine-tuning, or parameter modifications. The DeepXray algorithm [20,21] was initially trained using imaging data from GE Lunar Prodigy and iDXA densitometers as the reference standard. Because most of our institutional DXA scans were also performed using GE systems, the AI-predicted values were analyzed directly against the institutional DXA reports without mathematical cross-calibration or vendor-specific conversion equations.

DXA examinations and reference standards

To ensure the reliability of our diagnostic reference standard, all DXA examinations were performed by International Society for Clinical Densitometry (ISCD)-certified technologists. Scans were acquired at the participating institutions using a variety of densitometers, predominantly GE (eg, Lunar iDXA) systems, along with a smaller number of Norland, DMS, and Hologic systems. BMD was quantified specifically at the intact, contralateral proximal femur, with data extracted for both the femoral neck and total hip. In strict accordance with ISCD guidelines, regions exhibiting prior fractures, metallic hardware, or severe degenerative artifacts that could compromise DXA validity were excluded from the analysis. T-scores were systematically derived using reference means and standard deviations from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database [22]. Routine quality assurance procedures were maintained at each clinical site in accordance with ISCD Official Positions [[23], [24], [25]]. However, direct mathematical conversion of raw BMD values between scanner brands was not performed in this analysis.

Study outcomes

This study evaluated three distinct diagnostic and clinical outcomes. First, to establish foundational validity, we assessed the correlation between AI-predicted and DXA-measured absolute BMD values, as well as the diagnostic accuracy of the AI-derived T-scores in identifying underlying osteoporosis. For this comparison, osteoporosis was rigidly defined using the World Health Organization criteria as a DXA-derived T-score of ≤–2.5 at any site (femoral neck or total hip). Second, as the primary comparative objective of this study, we evaluated the AI model's discriminative performance relative to traditional, human-read morphological parameters (CTI and FNW) for predicting the same DXA-confirmed diagnosis. Finally, to assess real-world clinical applicability, we evaluated the net clinical benefit of the AI models relative to traditional measurements across varying diagnostic risk thresholds.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to present continuous data as medians with interquartile ranges, and categorical data as frequencies and percentages. To evaluate the continuous correlation between AI-derived predictions and DXA-derived reference standards (including both absolute BMD and T-scores), we used simple linear regression (R2), mean absolute error (MAE), and Spearman rank-order correlation coefficient (ρ). The correlation was considered weak if the absolute value of ρ fell between 0.1 and 0.3, moderate if between 0.3 and 0.5, and strong if greater than 0.5 [26].

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to assess the clinical utility of the AI-derived BMD and traditional morphometric measurements (CTI and FNW) against the binary outcome of DXA-confirmed osteoporosis. The area under the ROC curve (AUROC) assesses a model's discriminative performance—its ability to distinguish patients with the condition of interest (such as DXA-confirmed osteoporosis) from those without. AUROC typically ranges from 0 to 1, where 0.5 represents random guessing and 1.0 a perfect prediction. Generally, a model with an AUC ≥ 0.7 is considered to demonstrate acceptable discriminative ability and is potentially suitable for clinical application [27]. Furthermore, the DeLong test was used to compare AUROCs [28].

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of the models, several metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and overall accuracy, were calculated at the optimal cut-off point determined by Youden's index. Youden's index is used in ROC analysis to achieve an optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity by identifying the point on the ROC curve that maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity, which is considered the optimal cut-off point in this study [29]. Additionally, for the AI-derived T-scores, these diagnostic metrics were evaluated using the standard clinical threshold of ≤−2.5. We included this specific threshold alongside Youden's index to demonstrate the AI model's practical performance when applied to the universally recognized diagnostic criteria for osteoporosis, ensuring the results are applicable to current real-world clinical guidelines [1].

To determine whether using the predictive models to inform treatment decisions would do more good than harm in real-world practice, we conducted a decision curve analysis (DCA) [30]. The DCA plots the clinical net benefit—which weights the true-positive rate against the false-positive rate—of making decisions based on the models’ predictions across all possible risk thresholds (or cost-to-benefit ratios) in relation to the two default strategies of treating all patients or treating no patients [30]. A clinically useful predictive model should yield a higher net benefit than these default strategies across a range of acceptable threshold probabilities. Individual care providers can set an acceptable risk threshold for the proposed intervention and assess the corresponding net benefit to determine whether consulting the model is superior to adopting default approaches.

Statistical analyses were conducted on a complete-case basis for each anatomical site, accounting for varying sample sizes due to data availability. All statistical tests were two-sided, and exact P-values were reported. Statistical significance was defined a priori as P < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

This study included 240 (62.8%) and 142 (37.2%) eligible patients from two independent institutes, the National Taiwan University Hospital main branch in Taipei and the National Taiwan University Hospital Yunlin Branch, respectively. Among 382 eligible patients, 77.5% (N = 296) were female with a median age of 81 years (interquartile range [IQR] 73 to 86 years). The median BMI of the cohort was 21.7 kg/m2 (IQR 19.5 to 25.1 kg/m2). The most frequent injury presentation was a femoral neck fracture (73.3% [N = 275]), followed by intertrochanteric fractures (24.3% [N = 91]) and subtrochanteric fractures (2.4% [N = 9]). The median DXA-derived BMD was 0.486 g/cm2 (IQR 0.426 to 0.546 g/cm2) at the femoral neck and 0.529 g/cm2 (IQR 0.449 to 0.631 g/cm2) at the total hip. In comparison, the median AI-derived BMD was 0.512 g/cm2 (IQR 0.472 to 0.553 g/cm2) at the femoral neck and 0.494 g/cm2 (IQR 0.439 to 0.545 g/cm2) at the total hip. Correspondingly, the median DXA-derived minimum T-score of the cohort was −3.5 (IQR –4.0 to −2.9), with regional median T-scores of −3.1 (IQR –3.6 to −2.6) at the femoral neck and −3.4 (IQR –4.0 to −2.5) at the total hip. In comparison, the median AI-derived minimum T-score was −3.7 (IQR –4.1 to −3.2), with regional median T-scores of −2.9 (IQR –3.2 to −2.6) at the femoral neck and −3.7 (IQR –4.1 to −3.2) at the total hip. Traditional radiogrammetry measurements yielded a median CTI of 0.50 (IQR 0.46 to 0.54) and a median FNW of 34.5 mm (IQR 32.4 to 37.2). Based on the diagnostic criteria of a DXA-derived T-score ≤−2.5, 87.2% (N = 333) of patients had osteoporosis at any site. Diagnostic accuracy of the artificial intelligence model in identifying osteoporosis

Continuous variable analysis demonstrated a positive linear correlation between the AI-predicted values and the DXA assessments. When evaluating BMD, the AI-derived predictions yielded a ρ of 0.76, an R2 of 0.55, and a MAE of 0.06 g/cm2 for the femoral neck (N = 374, excluding 8 with incomplete AI extraction) (Fig. 1). At the total hip (N = 317, further excluding 57 Norland DXA scans lacking total hip reference values), the AI-predicted BMD achieved a ρ of 0.69, an R2 of 0.51, and a MAE of 0.08 g/cm2 (Fig. S2) [Supplementary Materials]. Correspondingly, the AI-predicted T-scores also demonstrated robust accuracy for the femoral neck (N = 374), the AI-derived T-scores yielded a ρ of 0.76, an R2 of 0.55, and a MAE of 0.47 (Fig. 2). Discriminative performance of artificial intelligence versus traditional morphometric measurements

The AI models showed higher discriminative performance when compared directly to traditional human-read radiographic indices. Manual morphometric measurements resulted in lower overall diagnostic accuracy, with the CTI achieving an AUC of 0.72 (95% CI 0.65–0.79) (Fig. 4) and the FNW achieving an AUC of 0.53 (95% CI 0.44–0.62) (Fig. 5). Paired ROC comparisons demonstrated the superiority of the deep learning algorithms compared to manual metrics. The AUC of the AI femoral neck model was statistically higher than both CTI (P < 0.001) and FNW (P < 0.001). Correspondingly, the AUC for the AI total hip model was statistically higher than those of both CTI (P < 0.001) and FNW (P < 0.001). Clinical utility and decision curve analysis

To evaluate real-world applicability, DCA demonstrated that the AI models yielded a greater clinical net benefit than traditional morphometric measurements across varying diagnostic risk thresholds. Specifically, both the AI femoral neck and total hip predictors consistently provided the highest net benefit across almost the entire range of threshold probabilities, remaining well above the default “treat all” and “treat none” baselines up to a threshold exceeding 0.90. In contrast, the net benefit of the traditional CTI began to decline after a threshold probability of approximately 0.40. It intersected with and dropped below the “treat all” baseline at a probability threshold near 0.65. Discussion

Identifying underlying bone loss in older adults who have sustained an initial fragility fracture is paramount for initiating medical therapy and preventing secondary fractures. While deep learning models for BMD estimation have been successfully validated in general, asymptomatic screening populations, their clinical utility and comparative diagnostic performance in high-prevalence, fracture-specific cohorts remain underexplored. Therefore, the primary novelty and contribution of this study is the external clinical validation of a commercially available AI model specifically in a high-risk hip fracture cohort, evaluating its performance directly against traditional human-read morphometric indices (CTI and FNW). Our findings indicate that the AI model successfully reproduces DXA-derived classifications and provides a substantially higher clinical net benefit than traditional manual evaluations. By leveraging existing preoperative imaging, clinicians can rapidly identify high-risk individuals at the point of care, justify timely DXA referrals, and effectively optimize secondary fracture prevention pathways.

Diagnostic accuracy of the artificial intelligence model

Our findings demonstrate that deep learning algorithms can achieve high diagnostic accuracy when evaluating the contralateral proximal femur on routine preoperative radiographs, thereby establishing AI as an effective tool for opportunistic screening of bone loss, especially in high-risk populations. These findings align with large-scale studies such as Hsieh et al. [9], who reported similar predictive performance (AUC 0.88) when using deep learning to predict BMD from plain pelvic radiographs in an Asian screening population. Our study extends their work by proving that high diagnostic accuracy is maintained even when analyzing the contralateral hip in an acute fracture setting.

Furthermore, recent literature has demonstrated similarly high AI predictive accuracy across different anatomical sites, including the lumbar spine [31] and chest radiographs [32]. Given that our study found no statistically significant difference in discriminative performance between the femoral neck and total hip, future research could explore extending our AI model to other anatomical sites to further broaden its clinical utility. Despite this strong correlation and discriminative capacity, the AI model currently remains a screening tool rather than a definitive diagnostic replacement. To establish the AI model as a non-inferior diagnostic standard, prospective randomized clinical trials are needed to demonstrate that AI-guided treatment initiation yields equivalent or superior long-term reduction in secondary fracture risk compared to the traditional DXA-guided care pathway.

Discriminative performance of artificial intelligence versus traditional morphometrics

Direct comparison revealed that the deep learning model demonstrated significantly superior discriminative performance compared with traditional human-read morphometric parameters (CTI and FNW). While these traditional indices are sometimes viewed as historical, they remain highly relevant for assessing structural bone health. Relying solely on BMD is clinically insufficient; approximately 70% of osteoporotic fractures occur in individuals who do not meet the BMD criteria for osteoporosis [33]. Epidemiological data from the Dubbo Osteoporosis Epidemiology Study demonstrated that over half of low-trauma fragility fractures occur in patients with non-osteoporotic BMD, with approximately 12% presenting with entirely normal BMD T-scores [34]. This highlights that geometric, structural, and biomechanical factors, beyond areal BMD, profoundly influence susceptibility to fragility fractures. Recognizing this structural importance, the 2019 ISCD Official Position guidelines endorse the use of cortical index measurements on plain radiographs as formal indications for preoperative DXA evaluation and as reliable predictors of osteoporosis and postoperative fractures [35]. Similarly, FNW remains independently associated with hip fracture risk even after adjusting for BMD [36].

In rural or resource-limited areas where DXA facilities are frequently unavailable, routine pelvic radiographs represent the primary opportunistic screening modality. Before the widespread integration of AI-assisted visual analysis, traditional manual morphometrics were the primary screening method available to clinicians in these environments. While AI models have increasingly been applied to plain films, no previous studies have directly evaluated whether deep learning can outperform these specific, conventional structural X-ray metrics.

By demonstrating that an automated AI system can instantaneously extract subclinical pixel-level features and outperform manual evaluations, our study suggests that local physicians no longer need to rely on or undergo training for tedious geometric measurements. While our AI model is currently optimized to estimate DXA-derived BMD, traditional parameters such as CTI and FNW assess bone health from entirely different geometric and structural dimensions. Therefore, to definitively establish that our AI model can completely replace these structural parameters, our future research will focus on advancing the model's capabilities beyond BMD estimation to perform direct, longitudinal fracture risk prediction, thereby identifying and stratifying high-risk patients who require immediate bone-protective medications.

Clinical utility and decision curve analysis

DCA confirmed that implementing AI models yields a higher clinical net benefit across realistic diagnostic thresholds than traditional morphometrics or default treatment strategies. Critics might argue that investigating opportunistic screening in a fracture cohort is clinically redundant. Certain progressive clinical guidelines, such as the Dachverband Osteologie (DVO) guidelines adopted in Germany [37], automatically classify low-energy hip fractures as clinical osteoporosis, thereby warranting immediate pharmacological intervention regardless of DXA results. However, this perspective overlooks the complex reality of global healthcare policies and disparate insurance reimbursement criteria. In many healthcare systems, including Taiwan, National Health Insurance (NHI) regulations rigidly require a documented T-score ≤−2.5 on a DXA scan to approve reimbursement for specific, high-cost anti-osteoporotic medications, even in the undeniable presence of a fragility fracture [38]. Therefore, our AI model possesses profound clinical utility: it provides an immediate, highly accurate preliminary assessment that justifies and accelerates referral for DXA, thereby navigating stringent insurance frameworks.

Furthermore, quantifying BMD in a fracture cohort is scientifically crucial because BMD severity directly stratifies the risk of subsequent fractures. A recent comprehensive systematic review by Ren et al. [39] identified reduced BMD at the hip, femoral neck, and intertrochanteric regions, as well as clinical osteoporosis, as major risk factors. Similarly, a prospective cohort study by Yang et al. [40], which closely mirrors our patient population of patients aged ≥65 years with low-energy hip fractures, demonstrated that a lower T-score (−3.1 vs. −2.0, P < 0.001; odds ratio = 1.45) is a significant predictor of contralateral hip fractures. This underscores the critical role of osteoporosis management in preventing contralateral fracture. These findings are strongly supported by Wang et al. [41] and Berry et al. [42], who established baseline BMD as a critical, independent risk factor for experiencing a second hip fracture. By accurately estimating BMD severity, our AI model not only flags patients who meet reimbursement criteria but also helps clinicians identify those at highest risk of imminent secondary fractures, enabling targeted and aggressive secondary prevention.

Limitations

When interpreting these findings, readers must carefully consider several crucial limitations regarding the generalizability of our model. Primarily, our cohort was restricted exclusively to patients who had already sustained a proximal femur fracture, resulting in an exceptionally high prevalence of clinical osteoporosis (87.2%). Because predictive metrics such as PPV and NPV are inherently prevalence-dependent, these specific results may not be directly generalizable to lower-risk, asymptomatic community populations. Although our AI model was evaluated specifically on the unfractured, contralateral proximal femur—demonstrating its ability to assess underlying bone architecture independent of the acute fracture line—we did not include a true non-fracture control group to empirically validate its performance in a strictly asymptomatic population. Therefore, while the clinical utility demonstrated by our DCA is highly applicable to secondary fracture settings, extrapolation to general population-based screening should be approached with caution. To directly address this limitation, a prospective cohort study specifically evaluating the model's performance in a distinct, non-fracture population (eg, patients with severe hip osteoarthritis) is currently underway.

Additionally, our analysis is constrained by a relatively small sample size of approximately 400 patients. Consequently, we lacked the statistical power to conduct robust subgroup analyses, preventing us from exploring whether the AI model's predictive accuracy varies across specific demographic or clinical subpopulations. Furthermore, geographic and demographic homogeneity must also be considered. The study cohort is drawn exclusively from the Taipei and Yunlin branches and consists entirely of an older, Chinese demographic. Because AI models are highly sensitive to ethnic variations, the algorithm's predictive accuracy may decline if applied indiscriminately to populations with different genetic or geographical backgrounds without prior recalibration.

Another notable limitation of this study is the heterogeneity of DXA densitometer brands used across the participating institutions. The AI algorithm was originally trained using GE systems as the gold standard, and while GE machines comprised the vast majority of our institutional dataset, a subset of patients was scanned using Hologic, DMS, or Norland systems. Because reliable, universally accepted cross-calibration equations are primarily established between major manufacturers such as GE and Hologic [43], whereas mathematical standardization for smaller brands like DMS and Norland remains less clinically validated, we opted to analyze the raw, unconverted T-scores directly. Although using raw data reflects real-world opportunistic screening conditions, the lack of mathematical standardization introduces potential systemic variance, as absolute bone density measurements vary across hardware vendors. Providing the distribution of DXA brands as a reference variable highlights this real-world variability. Still, future prospective trials should ideally utilize a single standardized DXA vendor to eliminate this confounding factor.

Furthermore, we must acknowledge the “black-box” nature of deep learning algorithms, which inherently limits clinical interpretability. The use of proprietary, commercially available software precluded us from generating custom explainability visualizations, such as Grad-CAM heatmaps, to pinpoint the exact pixels driving the AI's predictions. However, since absorptiometric features such as raw image intensity and brightness were deliberately suppressed during initial training [44], we infer that the AI relies primarily on spatial, textural, and morphologic bone patterns—such as trabecular microarchitecture and cortical thickness—rather than simple radiographic density. Nonetheless, until these specific microarchitectural features are explicitly mapped, the precise radiographic drivers of the model's output remain inferential.

Finally, our study shares the limitations inherent to all retrospective imaging research, including potential assessment bias and reliance on historical DXA data that may not perfectly align chronologically with the timing of the injury. Despite these limitations, our study remains highly valuable because it provides robust comparative evidence that AI models can significantly outperform traditional manual measurements using the same standard preoperative radiographs.

Conclusions

Deep learning AI offers a superior, automated alternative to traditional manual morphometric measurements for identifying underlying osteoporosis on routine pelvic radiographs. By analyzing the unfractured contralateral hip, this technology serves as a robust triage tool that meets strict healthcare reimbursement policies and optimizes secondary fracture prevention pathways in high-risk orthopedic cohorts. Future research should investigate the model's capacity to prospectively predict imminent secondary fractures, enabling precise risk stratification for patients who urgently require bone-protective therapies.
