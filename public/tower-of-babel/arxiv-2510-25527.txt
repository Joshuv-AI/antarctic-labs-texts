Enhanced quality factors at resonance in acoustofluidic cavities embedded in matched elastic metamaterials
Valdemar Frederiksen, Henrik Bruus

Abstract
We show that by embedding liquid-filled acoustofluidic cavities in a metamaterial, the quality factor of the cavity at selected acoustic resonance modes can be enhanced by 2 to 3 orders of magnitude relative to a comparable conventional cavity by matching the coarse-grained elastic moduli of the metamaterial to the acoustic properties of the liquid.



I
Introduction

The quality factor
Q
of an acoustic resonance mode in a liquid-filled acoustofluidic cavity embedded in an elastic solid is limited by the dissipation in the liquid, mainly due to the large stresses in the thin viscous boundary layers near the elastic walls of the cavity. For MHz ultrasound resonance modes in typical water-filled acoustofluidic cavities with kinematic viscosity
\nu
at angular frequency
\omega
,
Q
-factors are often in the range 10 - 500
Barnkob et al. 2010
, a value set by the relevant length scale of the cavity relative to the thickness
\delta=\sqrt{\frac{2\nu}{\omega}}
of the viscous boundary layer
Bach and Bruus 2018
. For a long straight box-shaped cavity of length
L
, width
W
, and height
H
, with
H<W\ll L
, it is found that
Q=\frac{H}{\delta}
. Given a typical height
H=160~\textrm{\textmu{}m}
and a standing half-wave along the width
W=375~\textrm{\textmu{}m}
with a resonance frequency of 2 MHz, we have the boundary-layer width
\delta=0.4~\textrm{\textmu{}m}
, and thus
Q=400
.

In the above example, the boundary layer is formed by the viscous friction in the liquid, as the acoustic velocity of the liquid is changing from its bulk value
v_{1}
to zero at the nearly rigid wall over a distance of
\delta
. The smallness of
\delta
is the reason that the dissipation
\dot{W}_{\mathrm{BL}}\approx\eta\big(\frac{v_{1}}{\delta}\big)^{2}
in the boundary layer dominates the total dissipation in the system. The
Q
-factor may be increased considerably simply by removing the boundary layer. In this paper we show that the boundary layer may be removed or at least strongly suppressed by embedding the acoustic cavity not in a conventional elastic solid, denoted by superscript ”
\mathrm{sl}
”, but instead in an elastic metamaterial denoted by superscript ”
\mathrm{mm}
”. By tuning the coarse-grained elastic moduli of the metamaterial, it is possible at a given resonance to match at the solid-liquid interface of the cavity the vibrational velocity
\partial_{t}\bm{u}
of the metamaterial to the acoustic velocity
\bm{v}_{1}
of the liquid. This matching will suppress the boundary layer, and the dissipation of the system will then be limited by the small bulk dissipation of the liquid, and the
Q
-factor increases by a factor 200 to
\sim 10^{5}
.

Theory

II.1
The 2D model system

Our proposed system is assumed to be translational invariant in the length direction
L
and with the rectangular cross section sketched in Fig.
1
. In this cross section a fluid-filled cavity
\Omega^{\mathrm{fl}}
circumscribing a rectangle of width
W^{\mathrm{fl}}
and height
H^{\mathrm{fl}}
is surrounded by a solid material region
\Omega^{\mathrm{sl}}
which is subdivided into four domains. The base
\Omega^{\mathrm{sl}}_{\mathrm{base}}
is a large rectangular block of regular solid material of width
W^{\mathrm{sl}}
and height
H^{\mathrm{sl}}
and with a free surface to the left. The lid
\Omega^{\mathrm{sl}}_{\mathrm{lid}}
is a thin rectangular region of regular solid material of width
W^{\mathrm{sl}}_{\mathrm{lid}}
with a prescribed time-harmonic displacement
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{phys}}=d_{0}\cos(\omega t)\>\bm{e}_{y}
on its rightmost boundary. The remaining two rectangular regions
\Omega^{\mathrm{mm}}_{\mathrm{up}}
and
\Omega^{\mathrm{mm}}_{\mathrm{down}}
above and below the cavity consist of a mechanical metamaterial, where the small-scale geometry has been optimized such as to make the metamaterial move in sync with the fluid in the cavity. We treat all acoustic fields
g_{1}
to be complex-valued with a harmonic time dependence
\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}
, such that the real part corresponds to the true physical field
g^{\mathrm{phys}}
,

g^{\mathrm{phys}}(\bm{r},t)=\mathrm{Re}[g_{1}(\bm{r})\>\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}].

II.2
The fluid domain

To model the acoustic behavior of the coupled fluid-solid system, we first consider the governing equations in the fluid domain
\Omega^{\mathrm{fl}}
in terms of the pressure
p
, velocity
\bm{v}
, and density
\rho^{\mathrm{fl}}
, namely the continuity and Navier–Stokes equation,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho^{\mathrm{fl}}

\displaystyle=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\rho^{\mathrm{fl}}\bm{v}),

(2a)

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathrm{fl}}\partial_{t}\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{fl}}-(\rho^{\mathrm{fl}}\bm{v}\!\cdot\!\bm{\nabla})\bm{v},

(2b)

where the fluid stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{fl}}
is given in terms of
p
,
\bm{v}
, and the respective shear and bulk viscosity
\eta
and
\eta^{\mathrm{b}}
,

\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{fl}}=\big[-p+\big(\eta^{\mathrm{b}}-\tfrac{2}{3}\eta\big)\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v}\big]\bm{I}+\eta\big[\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}+(\bm{\nabla}\bm{v})^{\textsf{T}}\big].

(3)

At the fluid-solid interface, which is the boundary
\partial{\Omega^{\mathrm{fl}}}
of the fluid domain
{\Omega^{\mathrm{fl}}}
with normal vector
\bm{n}
, the boundary conditions are continuity in velocity and normal stress,

\displaystyle\bm{v}

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\bm{u},

\displaystyle\text{ at }\partial{\Omega^{\mathrm{fl}}},

(4a)

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{fl}}\cdot\bm{n}

\displaystyle=\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{sl}}\cdot\bm{n},

\displaystyle\text{ at }\partial{\Omega^{\mathrm{fl}}},

(4b)

where
\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{sl}}
and
\partial_{t}\bm{u}
is the stress and velocity of the material surrounding the fluid domain. We solve the above equations by applying perturbation theory in terms of the acoustic Mach number assuming fields to become progressively weaker with increasing orders. For the zeroth-order solution, we assume the fluid to be at rest
\bm{v}_{0}=\bm{0}
with constant pressure
p_{0}
and density
\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}
. We then assume the first-order fields to be time-harmonic,
g_{1}=g_{1}(\bm{r})\>\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}
, and relate the first-order pressure and density,
p_{1}(\bm{r})=c^{\,2}_{0}\rho_{1}^{\mathrm{fl}}
, by assuming a constant compressibility
\kappa_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}=\big[\rho^{\mathrm{fl}}_{0}(c_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}})^{2}\big]^{-1}
, where
c_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}
is the speed of sound. Finally, we truncate the perturbation expansion and keep only zeroth-, first-, and time-averaged second-order fields,

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathrm{fl}}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}+\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}\kappa_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}p_{1}(\bm{r})\>\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}\omega t}}+\rho_{2}^{\mathrm{fl}}(\bm{r}),

(5a)

\displaystyle p(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=p_{0}+p_{1}(\bm{r})\>\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}\omega t}}+p_{2}(\bm{r}),

(5b)

\displaystyle\bm{v}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=\bm{v}_{1}(\bm{r})\>\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}\omega t}}+\bm{v}_{2}(\bm{r}).

(5c)

Inserting this expansion into Eq. (
2
), we obtain the first-order governing equations,

\displaystyle i\omega\kappa_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}p_{1}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v}_{1},

(6a)

\displaystyle i\omega\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}\bm{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}p_{1}-\eta\nabla^{2}\bm{v}_{1}-\big(\eta^{\mathrm{b}}-\tfrac{2}{3}\eta\big)\bm{\nabla}(\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v}_{1}),

(6b)

which we solve numerically, and which we work with analytically moving forward.

Numerically, we also solve for the second-order fields in
{\Omega^{\mathrm{fl}}}
, which are governed by the equations
Bach and Bruus 2018
,

\displaystyle\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{v}_{2}+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\big\langle\rho_{1}^{\mathrm{fl}}\bm{v}_{1}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=0,\;

\displaystyle\text{ in }{\Omega^{\mathrm{fl}}},

(7a)

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{2}^{\mathrm{fl}}-\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}\bm{\nabla}\cdot\big\langle\bm{v}_{1}\bm{v}_{1}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\bm{0},\;

\displaystyle\text{ in }{\Omega^{\mathrm{fl}}},

(7b)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{2}+\big\langle(\bm{u}_{1}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\bm{v}_{1}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\bm{0},\;

\displaystyle\text{ at }\partial{\Omega^{\mathrm{fl}}},

(7c)

where the bracket
\big\langle A_{1}B_{1}\big\rangle
represents the time average over one oscillation period of the product of any two complex-valued first-order fields
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
,

\big\langle A_{1}B_{1}\big\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\re\big[A_{1}B^{*}_{1}\big],

(8)

with the asterisk being the complex conjugate.

To characterize the utility of acoustic modes with regard to focusing microparticles by acoustophoresis, we calculate for each mode three useful quantities: The time-averaged acoustic energy density
E_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\mathrm{fl}}
in the fluid,

E_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\mathrm{fl}}=\frac{\kappa_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}}{2}\big\langle p_{1}p_{1}\big\rangle\!+\!\frac{\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}}{2}\big\langle{v}_{1,i}{v}_{1,i}\big\rangle=\frac{\kappa_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}}{4}|p_{1}|^{2}\!+\!\frac{\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}}{4}|\bm{v}_{1}|^{2},

(9a)

the Rayleigh streaming speed
v_{2}^{\mathrm{Rayl}}
defined by,

v_{2}^{\mathrm{Rayl}}=\frac{3E_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\mathrm{fl}}}{2\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}c_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}},

(9b)

and the spatial average
\big\langle\!\big\langle v_{2}\big\rangle\!\big\rangle
of the magnitude of the streaming velocity
\bm{v}_{2}
,

\big\langle\!\big\langle v_{2}\big\rangle\!\big\rangle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{V}^{\mathrm{fl}}}\int_{{\Omega^{\mathrm{fl}}}}|\bm{v}_{2}|\>\mathrm{d}V.

(9c)

If we imagine placing a spherical particle with radius
a
inside an acoustic cavity, the particle is subject to an acoustic radiation force
F_{\mathrm{rad}}\sim a^{3}k^{\mathrm{fl}}E_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\mathrm{fl}}
pushing it towards the nearest pressure node (or anti-node)
Gorkov 1962
;
Bach and Bruus 2020
;
Winckelmann and Bruus 2023
. Simultaneously, a drag force
F_{\mathrm{drag}}\sim 6\pi a\eta v_{2}
associated with the acoustic streaming field
\bm{v}_{2}
will tend to instead pull this particle around in a vortex motion. Consequently, there exists a critical radius
a_{\mathrm{c}}\sim\>\delta\sqrt{v_{2}/v_{2}^{\mathrm{Rayl}}}
, for which
F_{\mathrm{drag}}\sim F_{\mathrm{rad}}
, and below which particle focusing ceases. For polystyrene particles in a the box-shaped 2-MHz cavity described in the introduction, this radius is roughly
a_{\mathrm{c}}\sim 1~\textrm{\textmu{}m}

Muller et al. 2012
;
Barnkob et al. 2012a
. For strong focusing of small particles one therefore needs
E_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\mathrm{fl}}
to be as high as possible while keeping the streaming field
\bm{v}_{2}
as low as possible.

II.3
The solid domains

In the solid domains, we apply linear elastodynamics to solve for the displacement field
\bm{u}(\bm{r},t)
, such that the strain is
\bm{s}=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\nabla}\bm{u}+(\bm{\nabla}\bm{u})^{\textsf{T}})
and the Hookean stress
\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{sl}}
in terms of the stiffness tensor
\bm{C}
is linear in
\bm{s}
,

\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{sl}}=\bm{C}:\bm{s}=C_{ijkl}s_{kl}.

(10)

The second equality employs index-notation, where summation over repeated indices is implied. For isotropic materials,
\bm{C}
can be written in terms of the Voigt coefficients
C_{11}
and
C_{44}
as

C_{ijkl}=(C_{11}-2C_{44})\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+C_{44}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}).

(11)

Consequently, the governing equation for the time-harmonic displacement field
\bm{u}
of the elastic solid is the Cauchy momentum equation,

\displaystyle\bm{u}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle=\bm{u}_{1}(\bm{r})\>\mathrm{e}^{-{\mathrm{i}\omega t}},

(12a)

\displaystyle-\omega^{2}\rho^{\mathrm{sl}}\>\bm{u}_{1}

\displaystyle=\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{sl}},\;\text{ in }{\Omega^{\mathrm{sl}}}.

(12b)

On the boundary of the solid domain, three types of boundary conditions apply: (1) continuity of velocity on the fluid-solid boundaries
\partial\Omega^{\mathrm{fl}}
, which supplement the continuity of stress Eq. (
4b
), (2) a prescribed time-harmonic displacement with amplitude
d_{0}
on the actuated part
\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{osc}}^{\mathrm{sl}}
of the outer boundary, and (3) zero stress on the remaining free part
\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{free}}^{\mathrm{sl}}
of the outer boundaries,

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{\omega}\;\bm{v}_{1},

\displaystyle\text{ at }\partial\Omega^{\mathrm{fl}},

(13a)

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{1}

\displaystyle=d_{0}\;\bm{n},\quad

\displaystyle\text{ at }\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{osc}}^{\mathrm{sl}},

(13b)

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}^{\mathrm{sl}}\cdot\bm{n}

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

\displaystyle\text{ at }\partial\Omega_{\mathrm{free}}^{\mathrm{sl}}.

(13c)

We end this section by defining the time-averaged acoustic energy density
E_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\mathrm{sl}}
within the solid as

E_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\mathrm{sl}}=\frac{1}{2}\big\langle\big(C_{ijkl}s_{1,ij}\big)s_{1,kl}\big\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\rho^{\mathrm{sl}}\omega^{2}\big\langle u_{1,i}u_{1,i}\big\rangle.

(14)

The total time-averaged acoustic energy
\bar{U}_{\mathrm{ac}}
of the device, the time-averaged acoustic power
\bar{P}_{\mathrm{in}}
supplied to the device, and the quality factor
Q_{n}
of the
n
th resonance modes at frequency
\omega_{n}
of the device are thus,

\displaystyle\bar{U}_{\mathrm{ac}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega^{\mathrm{fl}}}E_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\mathrm{fl}}\>\mathrm{d}V+\int_{{\Omega^{\mathrm{sl}}}}E_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\mathrm{sl}}\>\mathrm{d}V,

(15a)

\displaystyle\bar{P}_{\mathrm{in}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial{\Omega^{\mathrm{sl}}}}\big\langle(-\mathrm{i}\omega_{n}\bm{u}_{1})\cdot(\bm{\sigma}_{1}^{\mathrm{sl}}\cdot\bm{n})\big\rangle\>\mathrm{d}A,

(15b)

\displaystyle Q_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{n}\bar{U}_{\mathrm{ac}}}{\bar{P}_{\mathrm{in}}}.

(15c)

II.4
The metamaterial domains

The metamaterial domains consist of
N_{y}\!\times\!N_{z}
-arrays of the elongated hexagonal unit cell specified in Fig.
2
and Table
1
. Like the regular solid domains, the metamaterial domains are governed by the Cauchy equation. In a full numerical model this is implemented straightforwardly. However, when making analytical coarse-grained approximations, we describe a given metamaterial as a homogeneous anisotropic elastic solid governed by the Cauchy equation with effective values (superscript ’mm’) of the mechanical fields, such as
\bm{C}\rightarrow\bm{C}^{\mathrm{mm}}
and
\bm{u}\rightarrow\bm{u}^{\mathrm{mm}}
. Most of the effective quantities are assumed to be local spatial average values of their exact counterparts with the single exception of
\bm{C}^{\mathrm{mm}}
, which instead is defined to satisfy the course-grained version of Eq. (
10
) as follows. On a single unit cell (see the inset of Fig.
2
) we a apply a constant strain
s_{yy}=d_{0}/W_{\mathrm{cell}}
by prescribing a displacement of
\bm{u}=d_{0}\bm{e}_{y}
on the rightmost edge
\mathcal{E}_{R}
, a zero displacement
\bm{u}=\bm{0}
on the leftmost edge
\mathcal{E}_{L}
, and the symmetry condition
u_{z}=0
on the top and bottom edges
\mathcal{E}_{T}
and
\mathcal{E}_{B}
. Then the metamaterial values of the elastic coefficients are given by the respective average curve integrals as
C^{\mathrm{mm}}_{yyyy}=\frac{1}{H_{\mathrm{cell}}}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{L}}\sigma_{yy}\mathrm{d}\ell/s_{yy}
and
C^{\mathrm{mm}}_{yyzz}=\frac{1}{W_{\mathrm{cell}}}\int_{\mathcal{E}_{T}}\sigma_{zz}\mathrm{d}\ell/s_{yy}
.

III.3
An approximate analytical solution

As stated earlier, the goal of this paper is to create an acoustofluidic mode without viscous boundary layers so that
\bm{v}_{1}
is irrotational
Bach and Bruus 2018
. In this case, the first-order governing equations reduce to a Helmholtz equation for
p_{1}
, while the velocity field
\bm{v}_{1}
is proportional to
\bm{\nabla}p_{1}
,

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}p_{1}

\displaystyle=-(1+\mathrm{i}\gamma^{\mathrm{fl}})\frac{\omega^{2}}{(c_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}})^{2}}\>p_{1},

(16a)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\mathrm{i}}{\omega\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}}\bm{\nabla}p_{1}

(16b)

where
\gamma^{\mathrm{fl}}=\omega\kappa_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}(\eta^{\mathrm{b}}+\frac{4}{3}\eta)
is the dimensionless bulk damping coefficient of the fluid, which for
MHz
-frequencies in water is of order
10^{-5}
.
A mode is thus synchronized with the wall motion, if this curl-free bulk velocity along the entire boundary of the fluid domain satisfies the no-slip condition (
4a
), which in terms of
p_{1}
is

\frac{1}{\omega^{2}\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}}\bm{\nabla}p_{1}=\bm{u}_{1},\;\text{ at }\partial{\Omega^{\mathrm{fl}}}.

(17)

This condition gives us a strong hint about how to construct a cavity with a wall-synchronized resonance mode. We start by assuming that the cavity contains an ideal standing pressure half-wave along the
y
-direction,

p_{1}=p_{1}^{0}\sin(k^{\mathrm{fl}}y),\;\text{ with }\;k^{\mathrm{fl}}=\frac{\pi}{W^{\mathrm{fl}}}.

(18)

Neglecting
\gamma^{\mathrm{fl}}
, this mode obeys Eq. (
16a
) for
\omega=c_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}k^{\mathrm{fl}}
. Next, to satisfy the wall-synchronization condition (
17
), the displacement
\bm{u}^{\mathrm{mm}}_{1}
in the two metamaterial regions
\Omega^{\mathrm{mm}}_{\mathrm{up}}
and
\Omega^{\mathrm{mm}}_{\mathrm{down}}
may be chosen to be,

\bm{u}^{\mathrm{mm}}_{1}=\frac{p_{1}^{0}k^{\mathrm{fl}}}{\omega^{2}\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}}\;\cos(k^{\mathrm{fl}}y)\>\bm{e}_{y},\;\text{ in }\;\Omega^{\mathrm{mm}}_{\mathrm{up}}\;\text{ and }\;\Omega^{\mathrm{mm}}_{\mathrm{down}}.

(19)

Finally, one way to ensure that the displacement field
\bm{u}_{1}
in the isotropic base solid
{\Omega^{\mathrm{sl}}}

has a vanishing amplitude along the base-metamaterial interface at
y=-\frac{1}{2}W^{\mathrm{fl}}
, while maintaining a non-vanishing normal stress, is to assume that
\bm{u}_{1}
takes the form of a standing longitudinal displacement wave with a node at
y=-\frac{1}{2}W^{\mathrm{fl}}
,

\bm{u}_{1}=u_{1}^{0}\;\sin\big[k^{\mathrm{sl}}(y+\tfrac{1}{2}W^{\mathrm{fl}})\big]\;\bm{e}_{y},\;\text{ in }\;\Omega^{\mathrm{sl}}_{\mathrm{base}}.

(20)

From the Cauchy equation (
12b
) and the stress continuity boundary conditions Eqs. (
4b
) and (
13c
), this assumption results in the amplitude and wavenumber relations,

\displaystyle u_{1}^{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{1}^{0}}{k^{\mathrm{sl}}C_{11}},

(21a)

\displaystyle k^{\mathrm{sl}}

\displaystyle=k^{\mathrm{fl}}c_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}\sqrt{\frac{\rho^{\mathrm{sl}}}{C_{11}}}=k^{\mathrm{fl}}\frac{c_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}}{c_{0}^{\mathrm{sl,L}}},

(21b)

the quarter-wave width condition,

W^{\mathrm{sl}}_{\mathrm{base}}=\frac{\pi}{2k^{\mathrm{sl}}}=\frac{c_{0}^{\mathrm{sl,L}}}{2c_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}}W^{\mathrm{fl}},

(22)

and the following set of conditions on the coarse-grained metamaterial parameters
\bm{C}^{\mathrm{mm}}
and
\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{mm}}
,

C^{\mathrm{mm}}_{iyyy}=\frac{1}{\kappa_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}}\delta_{iy},\quad C^{\mathrm{mm}}_{izyy}=\frac{1}{\kappa_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}}\delta_{iz},\quad\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{mm}}=\rho_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}.

(23)

In the limit where the lid thickness
W^{\mathrm{sl}}_{\mathrm{lid}}
tends to zero, and the device height
H^{\mathrm{sl}}
tends to infinity, this system has a near-perfectly synchronized resonance mode at frequency
f=c_{0}^{\mathrm{fl}}/W^{\mathrm{fl}}
. In the following section we show that even in the case of a geometry with finite values of
W^{\mathrm{sl}}_{\mathrm{lid}}
and
H^{\mathrm{sl}}
, the above conditions serve as en excellent starting point for numerical determination of metamaterials that support well-synchronized modes.

IV.1
Optimizing the system for maximum Q value

Our numerical optimization procedure for the metamaterial is divided into two steps. First, we find a metamaterial which satisfies the three criteria listed in Eq. (
23

L_{2}

IV.3
Streaming

To better understand the usefulness of the synchronized metamaterial cavities for doing acoustophoresis, we study the acoustic streaming field
\bm{v}_{2}
, as this will put a lower bound on the size of the particles that can be focused inside the cavity. For this reason, the streaming field of the optimized
(N_{y}\times N_{z})=(5\times 10)
metamaterial cavity is compared to that of the reference cavity. On one hand, one would expect the synchronized cavity to produce less streaming than the reference cavity, since its boundary layers are considerably weaker. On the other hand, the many corners along the fluid-solid interface of the synchronized cavity tend to amplify the streaming
Ovchinnikov et al. 2014
. That the former effect dominates is revealed by Table
2
, where it is seen that the normalized streaming
\big\langle\!\big\langle v_{2}\big\rangle\!\big\rangle/v_{2}^{\mathrm{Rayl}}
is monotonically suppressed (nearly
\propto N_{y}^{-1}
) by more than an order of magnitude when going from the reference cavity up to the
(N_{y}\times N_{z})=(10\times 20)
synchronized metamaterial cavity. Such a
10
-fold reduction in the streaming speed implies a
\sqrt{10}
-fold decrease in the critical radius
a_{c}
from
\sim 1~\textrm{\textmu{}m}
to
\sim 0.3~\textrm{\textmu{}m}
, allowing significantly smaller particles to be focused.

We note in Fig.
5
that the streaming field of the synchronized metamaterial cavity is not only quantitatively, but also qualitatively different from that in the reference cavity. Whereas the streaming field in the reference cavity is dominated by the conventional four large rolls filling up the entire cross section, the streaming field in the synchronized cavity instead contains many smaller streaming vortices of various shapes and sizes, resulting in a less uniform streaming pattern, but clearly with more suppression of the streaming
\big\langle\!\big\langle v_{2}\big\rangle\!\big\rangle\sim\frac{1}{50}v_{2}^{\mathrm{Rayl}}
in the middle third of the channel,
-\frac{1}{6}H^{\mathrm{fl}}<z<\frac{1}{6}H^{\mathrm{fl}}
, such that
a_{c}
drops down to
\sim 0.15~\textrm{\textmu{}m}
in this region.

IV.4
Particle focusing

A remarkable addition to the discussion of the critical particle size
a_{c}
is that Table
2
reveals how the fluid pressure field
p_{1}^{\mathrm{mm}}
in the synchronized metamaterial device scales with
p_{1}^{\mathrm{ref}}
of the reference device and the Q-factors,
p_{1}^{\mathrm{mm}}\sim(Q^{\mathrm{mm}}/Q^{\mathrm{ref}})\>p_{1}^{\mathrm{ref}}

(Q^{\mathrm{mm}}/Q^{\mathrm{ref}})^{2}\sim 4\times 10^{4}

\tau\propto\frac{\eta(W^{\mathrm{fl}})^{2}}{a^{2}E_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\mathrm{fl}}}

Barnkob et al. 2012b

\tau^{\mathrm{mm}}\propto\left(\frac{Q^{\mathrm{ref}}}{Q^{\mathrm{mm}}}\right)^{2}\>\frac{\eta(W^{\mathrm{fl}})^{2}}{a^{2}E_{\mathrm{ac}}^{\mathrm{fl},ref}}

4\times 10^{4}

a^{\mathrm{mm}}\sim 0.01a^{\mathrm{ref}}

V
Discussion

The fraction of acoustic energy stored inside the fluid
\bar{U}^{\mathrm{fl}}_{\mathrm{ac}}/\bar{U}_{\mathrm{ac}}\approx 0.2
for the synchronized cavities. With the bulk damping factors
\gamma^{\mathrm{fl}}\approx 2\times 10^{-5}
and
\gamma^{\mathrm{sl}}\approx 6\times 10^{-7}
, the theoretical upper bound for
Q
should therefore be
Hahn and Dual 2015

Q^{\mathrm{bulk}}=\frac{\bar{U}_{\mathrm{ac}}}{\gamma^{\mathrm{fl}}\bar{U}^{\mathrm{fl}}_{\mathrm{ac}}+\gamma^{\mathrm{sl}}\bar{U}^{\mathrm{sl}}_{\mathrm{ac}}}\approx 2.2\times 10^{5}.

(25)

In Table
2
we see that the highly resolved cavities tend to approach this theoretical upper bound quite nicely, and we may thus conclude that this synchronization manages to remove the dissipation associated with the viscous boundary layers rather reliably.

Looking ahead, if we were to speculate how one may reduce this dissipation even further and thus obtain still higher
Q
-factors, theoretically it should be possible to construct acoustic modes in such a way that the fluid contains less than a half-wave across the channel at resonance, but still contains a pressure node. This would greatly lower the bulk dissipation within the fluid.

We note that the proposed cavities have a harder time suppressing the streaming velocities compared to how easily
Q
-factors are raised. As mentioned earlier, we attribute this to be caused by the many corners around the fluid channel
Ovchinnikov et al. 2014
, even though these corners have been rounded a bit to avoid generating too much streaming. One may also attempt to construct a cavity with fewer convex corners, or apply one of the methods proposed for suppression of acoustic streaming in previous work
Bach and Bruus 2020
;
Karlsen et al. 2018
;
Winckelmann and Bruus 2021
. Clearly, there are many ways to suppress the remaining streaming further.

With regard to how such synchronized cavities might be fabricated, one possibility is to rely on drawing towers such as the ones used for the fabrication of photonic crystal optical fibers. We note that such drawing towers already are designed to shape fused silica glass with a feature length scale lower than the length scales considered in this work
Tajima et al. 2004
. Another possibility is to employ two-photon 3D-printing and fabricate the metamaterial structure directly, a possibility that has been demonstrated recently on comparable intricate micro- and nanoscale structures in glass
Bauer et al. 2023
.

VI
Conclusion

By using metamaterials in acoustofluidic systems, it is possible to reduce the acoustic boundary layers dramatically by synchronizing the motion of the fluid to that of the adjacent material. This will in turn remove the viscous friction near the fluid-solid interface, and it may also, but to a lesser extent, reduce the acoustic streaming. This technique of using acoustic metamaterials to remove viscous friction could help increase
Q
-factors in a wider range of microelectromechanical systems. Specifically for acoustophoretic focusing of particles, synchronized metamaterial cavities may lead to faster focusing of smaller submicron particles compared to the current state-of-the-art of using cavities in conventional materials.
