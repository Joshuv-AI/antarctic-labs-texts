Vertex-Minimal Triangulation of Complexes with Homology
Jon V. Kogan

Abstract
For a given pair of numbers ( d , k ) (d,k) , we establish the minimal number of vertices in pure d d -dimensional simplicial complexes with non-trivial homology in dimension k k . Furthermore, we solve the problem under the additional constraint of strong connectivity with respect to any intermediate dimension.



1
Introduction

Simplicial complexes are a natural generalization of graphs and have been studied extensively in recent decades- whether as clique complexes of graphs, triangulations of manifolds, or random models for topological spaces.
Given the strong interest in extremal graph theory, it is natural to ask extremal questions in the context of simplicial complexes, specifically those that involve the number of faces within these complexes.

A central result in this area is the Kruskal–Katona theorem (see
[
7
]
), which provides a description of the possible face-vectors of a simplicial complex.
Subsequently, Björner and Kalai related matters to topology in
[
2
]
, by showing the relationship between the face vector and Betti vector of a simplicial complex.

In this paper, we alter the question in
[
2
]
by adding
geometric requirements that the simplicial complex must satisfy.
For instance, it is plain to see that the smallest number of vertices of a complex with non-trivial
k
-homology is
k+2
(in
\partial(\Delta^{k+1})
), and for a pure
d
-dimensional complex the minimum is
d+1
.
Combining these requirements turns out to result in a more interesting problem.

In
Section

3
we prove:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
0\leq k\leq d
. Any pure
d
-dimensional simplicial complex with nontrivial
H_{k}
has at least
\left\lceil\frac{(d+1)(k+2)}{k+1}\right\rceil
vertices, and this bound is tight.

Additionally, we explore strong connectivity (defined in
Definition

2.2
), an equivalence relation on simplexes, whose components are crucial for understanding the cohomological structure of the entire complex, as well as cohomology operations such as the cup product and Steenrod operations (see
Claim

6.1
).
We prove the following result:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
1\leq k\leq d
. Any strongly-connected
d
-dimensional complex with non-trivial
H_{k}
has at least
d+1+\left\lceil\frac{d}{k}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{d(k+1)}{k}+1\right\rceil
vertices, and this bound is tight.

This is a special case of a more general theorem on relative strong connectivity, proven in
Section

5
:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
1\leq k\leq d
and
X
a pure
d
-dimensional complex, strongly connected w.r.t dimension
m>d+1-\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rceil
, such that
H_{k}(X)\neq 0
. Then
X
has at least
d+1+\left\lceil\frac{m}{k}\right\rceil
vertices, and this bound is tight.

The importance of strongly connected components w.r.t dimension
m
stems essentially from the fact that these components determine collapsability to dimension
m-1
(see
Claim

6.2
).

Finally, in
Section

6
we relate these results to other work in the field.

Preliminaries

Notation
.

We use the following conventions:

•

By

(or simply

•

By

•

For a simplicial complex

Definition 2.1
.

A simplicial complex is called
pure
d
-dimensional
if any simplex in the complex is contained in a
d
dimensional face. Note that this implies that
d
is the dimension of all maximal faces.

Definition 2.2
.

For a simplicial complex

, define a relation
\sim
on its
d
-dimensional faces by having
\sigma\sim\tau
if they share a
d-1
-dimensional face.
This is obviously reflexive and symmetric. Complete this relation under transitivity and call it
\sim^{\prime}
. A
strong connectivity component
or
strongly connected component
of
C
is an equivalence class of
d
-dimensional faces of
C
under this relation.
A pure-
d
-dimensional simplicial complex is called
Strongly Connected
if all of its
d
-dimensional faces are in the same component.

A simplicial complex where all maximal simplexes are of dimension greater or equal to
d
is called
Strongly Connected with respect to dimension
d
if its
d
-skeleton is strongly connected.
Equivalently, a complex is strongly connected w.r.t dimension
d
if its maximal faces form a single component under the relation of sharing
d-1
dimensional faces.

Definition 2.3
.

For a simplicial complex
X
strongly connected w.r.t dimension
d
, an

is the attachment
X\cup_{A}\Delta^{m}
of a new face to
X
, such that
m\geq d
and
A
contains a
(d-1)
-face (thus the new complex is also strongly connected w.r.t dimension
d
).

In this paper, we will discuss strongly connected components as resulting from expansion operations. Expansion operations fit into a
growth process
, i.e a series of subcomplexes
A_{0}\subset A_{1}\subset...A_{k}=A
, where
A_{0}
is a simplex and
\dim(A_{0})\geq d
,
A
is any strongly connected
d
-complex,
A_{i+1}
has exactly one maximal face more than
A_{i}
and all
\{A_{i}\}
are strongly connected w.r.t dimension
d
.

Claim 2.4

(from the preprint
[
6
]
)
.

For any

strongly connected w.r.t dimension

Proof.

Define a graph

, where

is the set of maximal faces of

The strong connectivity w.r.t dimension

For a covering of a topological space

Theorem 2.9

(Leray’s nerve theorem,
[

, 4G exercise 4]
)
.

Let

.

A complex

that has non-trivial

has at least

.

Proof.

By assumption,

entails that

as well.
We conclude that

has at least

vertices in

that do not span a simplex.

.

.
∎

Relative Strong Connectivity

Claim 5.1
.

The complex
MH_{d,k}
from
Example

3.1
is strongly connected w.r.t dimension
d-\left\lceil\frac{d-k}{k+1}\right\rceil=d+1-\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rceil=\left\lfloor\frac{(d+1)k}{k+1}\right\rfloor
.

Proof.

Recall that
MH_{d,k}
is defined using vertex sets
s_{0},...,s_{r}
which contain
\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rceil
,
s_{r+1},...,s_{k+1}

which contain
\left\lfloor\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rfloor
, and
z
which contains
1
unless
k+1|d+1
(where it is empty).

MH_{d,k}
has
k+2
maximal faces
f_{0},...,f_{k+1}
, where
f_{i}=\bigcup_{j\neq i}s_{j}\bigcup_{j=i}^{r}z
.

In the case where
k+1|d+1
, each pair of maximal faces share
\frac{(d+1)(k+2)}{k+1}-2\frac{d+1}{k+1}=\frac{(d+1)k}{k+1}
vertices, and thus also strongly connected w.r.t this dimension.
Otherwise, for the maximal faces
f_{i},f_{j}
, we divide into cases:

•

If Both
i,j\leq r
,

|f_{i}\cap f_{j}|=\left\lceil\frac{(d+1)(k+2)}{k+1}\right\rceil-|s_{i}|-|s_{j}|=\left\lceil d+1+\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rceil-2\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rceil=d+1+\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rceil-2\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rceil=

d+1-\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rceil=d+1-\left\lceil\frac{d+1+k-k}{k+1}\right\rceil=d+1-\left\lceil 1+\frac{d-k}{k+1}\right\rceil=d-\left\lceil\frac{d-k}{k+1}\right\rceil

•

If
i,j>r
:

|f_{i}\cap f_{j}|=\left\lceil\frac{(d+1)(k+2)}{k+1}\right\rceil-|s_{i}|-|s_{j}|-|z|=\left\lceil d+1+\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rceil-2\left\lfloor\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rfloor-1=

d+1+\left\lfloor\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rfloor+1-2\left\lfloor\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rfloor-1=d+1-\left\lfloor\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rfloor\geq d+1-\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rceil

•

Finally, if
i\leq r<j
(the other case is symmetric):

|f_{i}\cap f_{j}|=\left\lceil d+1+\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rceil-|s_{i}|-|s_{j}|-|z|=d+1+\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rceil-\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rceil-\left\lfloor\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rfloor-1=

d+1-\left\lfloor\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rfloor-1=d+1-\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rceil

We conclude that every pair of maximal faces share
d+1-\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rceil
vertices, and that faces
f_{i},i\leq r
are not in the same strongly connected component w.r.t dimensions larger than
d+1-\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{k+1}\right\rceil
.
∎

An element in ”
\mathfrak{C}_{\infty}
” (in our case it is enough to consider
\mathfrak{C}_{d}
) is said to be a
staggered dimensionwise minimal example
for the properties defining
\mathfrak{C}_{-1}
.

\mathfrak{C}_{-1}
is non empty (by
Example

5.2
) and the image of any
F_{i}
always has a minimum. Therefore,
\mathfrak{C}_{d}
is non-empty, and a staggered dimensionwise minimal example
C
exists.

C
is strongly connected w.r.t dimension
m
, so we may chose a growth process
\Delta^{d}=C_{0}\subset...\subset C_{n}=C
.

C_{n-1}
has fewer
d
-faces than
C
, so by staggered dimensionwise minimality
H_{k}(C_{n-1})=0
.
If
C=C_{n-1}\cup_{D}\Delta^{d}
, by Mayer–Vietoris
H_{k-1}(D)\neq 0
, and thus by
Lemma

2.10

D
has at least
k+1
maximal faces with empty intersection-
D_{0},...,D_{k}
coming from maximal faces
D_{0}^{\prime},...,D_{k}^{\prime}
of
X
.

D
also contains
m
vertices which comprise a simplex (by strong connectivity), which we denote by
V=\{v_{1},...,v_{m}\}
. Denote the other vertices by
W=\{w_{m+1},...,w_{d+1}\}
. Applying
Lemma

2.10
again we may assume WLOG that
V\subset D_{0}
.

If
|D_{i}\cap V|>\frac{m(k-1)}{k}
for all
i
, by the pigeonhole principle there must be a vertex common to all
D_{i}
, contradicting our choice of
D_{i}
.
Therefore,
min_{i}|D_{i}\cap V|\leq\frac{m(k-1)}{k}
(assume WLOG that this minimum is obtained with
D_{1}
), and:

|D_{1}^{\prime}\setminus D|\geq|D_{1}^{\prime}|-|D_{1}\cap V|-|D\setminus V|\geq d+1-\frac{m(k-1)}{k}-(d+1-m)=\frac{m}{k}.

Since
C
contains both
D_{1}^{\prime}\setminus D
and the
d
-face attached along
D
, and they are disjoint, this gives the desired bound. Notice that this recreates the results in
Theorem

1.1
and
Theorem

1.2
for appropriate choices of

.

By
Example

5.2
the bound is also tight.

6
Discussion

The importance of strong connectivity to topology is seen in the following:

Claim 6.1
.

(proved in
[
6
, Lemma 2.7]
)
For a simplicial complex
X
:
H^{d}(X)
,
Im(\vee:H^{*}(X)\times H^{*}(X)\to H^{d}(X))
and steenrod operations landing in
H^{d}(X)
are non-zero only if there exists a
d
-dimensional strong-connectivity component on which they are non-zero.
If
X
has
d
as the maximal dimension, this is an if and only if for
H^{d}(X)
.

A corollary to
Claim

6.1
is that
Theorem

1.2
my be used as a lower bound for any simplicial complex where the image of the cup product or some steenrod operation in
H^{d}
is non-trivial.

This should be compared to the bound in
[
1
, §16]
, which says that if a complex
X
has non-trivial
\cup:H^{k}(X)\times H^{d-k}(X)\to H^{d}(X)
, then
|X|\geq d+1+max(k,d-k)+1
(by having vertex disjoint
\Delta^{d}
and
\Delta^{max(k,d-k)}
as subcomplexes).
Of course,
d+1+max(k,d-k)+1
is much bigger than
d+1+\frac{d}{k}
in all cases except
k=1
.

The importance of relative strong connectivity is seen from its influence on collapsability:

Claim 6.2
.

(to appear in a different article)
A simplicial complex
C
collapses onto dimension
d
if and only if all strong connectivity components of
C
w.r.t dimension
d+1
collapse onto dimension
d
.

Regarding the theorems in this paper– while they do not take into account the whole Betti-vector, they do produce an upper bound on the minimal number of vertices needed:

Example 6.3
.

let
b=(b_{1},b_{2},...,b_{k})
be a list of non-negative integers, and
d\geq k
. Then there exists a pure
d
-dimensional (or strongly connected w.r.t dimension
d
) complex with a number of vertices behaving asymptotically (in
d,b_{1},b_{2},...,b_{k}
) as

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sqrt[i+2]{b_{i}}\frac{(d+1)(i+2)}{i+1}\gamma_{i}-(d+1)(|I|-1),

or in the strongly connected case as

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sqrt[i+1]{b_{i}}\frac{(d+1)(i+1)}{i}\gamma_{i-1}-(d+1)(|I|-1).

The constant
\frac{1}{e}\leq\gamma_{i}\leq 1
stems from Stirling’s approximation, and
I
is the set of indexes for which
b_{i}\neq 0
.
These are obtained by repeating the constructions in
Example

3.1
(
Example

4.2
) for a larger ”basic cycle” of
sk_{i}(\Delta^{n})
(
sk_{i-1}(\Delta^{n})
and then suspending), with
n
determined by
b_{i}
, and then gluing the resulting complexes along a
d
-face.

Compare
Example

6.3
to
[
2
, Theorem 1.3]
which states that a Betti-vector
b=(b_{1},b_{2},...,b_{k})
is realizable using
n+1
vertices if and only if
b
is the face vector of a Sperner family (anti-chain) of subsets of
[n]
.

The Lubell–Yamamoto–Meshalkin inequality states that a Sperner family with a face-vector
(a_{0},...,a_{n})
supported on
[n]
must satisfy

\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}i!(n-i)!\leq n!

We thus see that
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}i!(n-i)!b_{i}\leq n!
. Asymptotically approximating (following
[
3
, formula 67]
):

n\approx\Gamma^{-1}(n!)=\theta\left(\frac{ln(n!/\sqrt{2\pi})}{W_{0}(\frac{1}{e}ln(\frac{n!}{\sqrt{2\pi}})}\right)

W_{0}
being the (main branch of) Lambert W function. Substituting the approximation from
[
4
, 4.11]
we get:

n\approx\Gamma^{-1}(n!)=\theta\left(\frac{ln(n!/\sqrt{2\pi})}{ln(\frac{1}{e}ln(\frac{n!}{\sqrt{2\pi}})-lnln(\frac{1}{e}ln(\frac{n!}{\sqrt{2\pi}})}\right)=\theta\left(\frac{ln(n!/\sqrt{2\pi})}{ln(\frac{1}{e}ln(\frac{n!}{\sqrt{2\pi}})}\right)=\Omega\left(\frac{ln(S/\sqrt{2\pi})}{ln(\frac{1}{e}ln(S)}\right)

and we get
n=\Omega(\frac{ln(S)}{ln(ln(S))})
.
While the LYM inequality is not tight in non-trivial cases, if
n=o(S^{\epsilon}),\epsilon>0
for minimal examples, we may conclude that the requirements of purity of dimension and strong connectivity seem to require an asymptotically greater number of vertices.
