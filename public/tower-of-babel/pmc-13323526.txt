Gilteritinib response in acute myeloid leukemia harboring a rare FLT3 juxtamembrane domain mutation with subsequent clonal evolution
Authors: Fumihiko Nakao, Takahiro Shima, Ken Takigawa, Yuichiro Semba, Fumiaki Jinnouchi, Takuji Yamauchi, Jun Odawara, Yoshikane Kikushige, Yasuo Mori, Koji Kato, Takahiro Maeda, Koichi Akashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMutations in the FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) gene are among the most clinically relevant molecular abnormalities in acute myeloid leukemia (AML); however, the therapeutic significance of rare non-canonical FLT3 mutations involving the juxtamembrane domain (JMD) remains poorly defined. We report a 34-year-old woman with acute monocytic leukemia harboring a rare FLT3-JMD missense mutation (V579A) who experienced early relapse following standard induction and consolidation chemotherapy. Targeted next-generation sequencing revealed the presence of FLT3 V579A along with a concurrent truncating ARID1A mutation. Salvage therapy with the type I FLT3 inhibitor gilteritinib induced rapid hematologic remission within three weeks, allowing successful bridging to haploidentical allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Three months after transplantation, the patient relapsed, and genomic analysis demonstrated loss of the FLT3-mutated clone with the emergence and expansion of TP53-mutated independent clones, indicating clonal evolution and an apparent shift away from FLT3-dependent disease biology. This case suggests that AML harboring rare FLT3-JMD point mutations may exhibit transient dependence on FLT3 signaling and may respond to FLT3 inhibition despite the absence of canonical FLT3 alterations. These findings highlight the potential value of extended molecular profiling and longitudinal genomic assessment to identify potential therapeutic targets and mechanisms of resistance in relapsed AML.Keywords: Acute myeloid leukemia, FLT3 V579A, Gilteritinib

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a biologically heterogeneous hematologic malignancy characterized by recurrent genetic alterations that influence disease behavior and therapeutic response [1, 2]. Among these, mutations in the FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3) gene represent some of the most clinically relevant molecular abnormalities, occurring in approximately 30% of patients with AML. The two canonical FLT3 mutation types—internal tandem duplications (ITD) and tyrosine kinase domain (TKD) point mutations—are well-established therapeutic targets [3–5], and the type I FLT3 inhibitor gilteritinib has demonstrated efficacy in relapsed or refractory AML harboring these alterations [6–9].

Beyond these canonical alterations, rare non-ITD, non-TKD FLT3 mutations involving the juxtamembrane domain (JMD) have been increasingly recognized through the use of expanded next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels [10, 11]. These mutations, which include missense substitutions and small deletions, account for approximately 2% of AML cases. Such mutations are thought to disrupt the autoinhibitory function of the juxtamembrane domain [12], resulting in constitutive FLT3 activation and enhanced leukemic proliferation [11, 13]. The clinical significance of FLT3-JMD mutations, however, remains incompletely defined. Retrospective analyses suggest that patients with these mutations may experience high relapse rates and unfavorable outcomes comparable to those observed in FLT3-ITD-positive AML [11]. Importantly, although in vitro studies have shown that both type I and type II FLT3 inhibitors, including gilteritinib, can suppress the growth of AML cells carrying FLT3-JMD mutations, clinical evidence supporting the therapeutic efficacy of FLT3 inhibitors in this molecular subset is extremely limited [10, 11, 13]. Consequently, AML patients lacking canonical FLT3 mutations are generally not considered candidates for FLT3-targeted therapy, potentially overlooking a subset of patients who may derive benefit.

Herein, we report a case of chemotherapy-refractory AML harboring a FLT3-JMD missense mutation (V579A) in which gilteritinib monotherapy induced rapid hematologic remission and enabled bridging to allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. This case provides hypothesis-generating insights into the potential therapeutic vulnerability of FLT3-JMD-mutated AML and illustrates the dynamic clonal evolution underlying subsequent resistance, highlighting the importance of comprehensive molecular profiling and longitudinal genomic assessment in precision AML therapy.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old Japanese woman with no significant past medical or family history presented to our hospital in April 2022 with a two-week history of persistent fever and generalized fatigue. Initial laboratory evaluation revealed a white blood cell count of 10.8 × 109/L with 31% blasts, a hemoglobin level of 12.3 g/dL, and a platelet count of 138 × 109/L. Lactate dehydrogenase was elevated to 534 U/L, and there was no evidence of disseminated intravascular coagulation. Physical examination was unremarkable. Bone marrow examination demonstrated a hypercellular marrow with 83.2% blasts, predominantly composed of monoblasts and promonocytes. Cytochemical analysis showed that the blasts were myeloperoxidase-negative and naphthyl butyrate esterase-positive, with inhibition by sodium fluoride, consistent with monocytic lineage differentiation (Fig. 1A-D). Flow cytometric analysis revealed an expanded CD45dim blast population (91.8%) expressing CD4, CD11c, CD33, CD56, and HLA-DR, and lacking expression of CD7, CD13, CD14, CD34, cytoplasmic (cy) CD3, cyCD79a, cyMPO, and cyTdT. Cytogenetic analysis revealed a normal karyotype (46,XX). Molecular screening at diagnosis was negative for recurrent AML fusion genes, FLT3-ITD/TKD mutations, and NPM1 mutations. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with acute monocytic leukemia according to the WHO 2022 classification. The patient received standard induction chemotherapy consisting of idarubicin (12 mg/m2 on days 1–3) and cytarabine (100 mg/m2 on days 1–7), achieving hematologic complete remission with a reduction in bone marrow blasts to 2.0%. She subsequently underwent consolidation therapy with high-dose cytarabine (3 g/m² every 12 h on days 1–3). However, bone marrow examination performed after completion of consolidation revealed early relapse, with blasts accounting for 24% of nucleated cells.

During consolidation therapy, results became available from a targeted next-generation sequencing panel encompassing 398 genes recurrently mutated in hematologic malignancies, which had been performed using the diagnostic bone marrow specimen. This analysis identified a missense mutation in the FLT3 juxtamembrane domain (V579A) with a variant allele frequency (VAF) of 0.28, as well as a truncating ARID1A mutation (Q439*; VAF 0.24) (Table 1). Although canonical FLT3-ITD and TKD mutations were absent, previous preclinical studies have demonstrated the oncogenic potential and FLT3 inhibitor sensitivity of FLT3-JMD mutations, including V579A [11, 13]. The early relapse following intensive chemotherapy suggested limited benefit from conventional salvage chemotherapy regimens. Given the reported activity of type I FLT3 inhibitors against FLT3-JMD mutations, gilteritinib was selected as salvage therapy.

As the use of gilteritinib in AML lacking canonical FLT3-ITD/TKD mutations constitutes off-label administration, thorough informed consent was obtained from both the patient and her family members prior to initiating treatment. Throughout the treatment course, the patient was closely monitored, and we were prepared to initiate salvage therapy promptly if needed. Gilteritinib was initiated at a reduced dose of 120 mg once daily owing to concomitant administration of posaconazole. The patient achieved hematologic complete remission within three weeks, with the bone marrow blast percentage decreasing from 24% to 3.6%. However, multi-color flow cytometric analysis of this remission sample detected an abnormal CD33 + CD45dimCD56+ population comprising 0.44% of total cells, suggesting the presence of minimal residual disease. Following this response, the patient proceeded to allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation using peripheral blood stem cells from an HLA-haploidentical sibling donor. The conditioning regimen consisted of total body irradiation (12 Gy) and fludarabine (90 mg/m²). Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) prophylaxis included post-transplant cyclophosphamide (100 mg/kg), tacrolimus, and mycophenolate mofetil. Neutrophil engraftment was achieved on day 17, and platelet recovery (> 20 × 109/L) was achieved on day 21. Bone marrow examination on day 30 demonstrated complete remission with full donor chimerism, without detection of abnormal clones by flow cytometric analysis. The post-transplant course was complicated by cytomegalovirus (CMV) colitis. No clinically significant acute GVHD was observed. Although post-transplant maintenance therapy with gilteritinib had been planned, it could not be initiated because of persistent CMV infection.

Approximately three months after transplantation, myeloblasts reappeared in peripheral blood, and bone marrow examination confirmed hematologic relapse with 52% blasts. Gilteritinib was promptly reintroduced; however, no clinical response was observed. Cytogenetic analysis and targeted sequencing performed on the relapsed bone marrow specimen revealed marked clonal evolution. AML clones carrying a complex karyotype had emerged, and molecular analysis demonstrated disappearance of the FLT3 V579A mutation and acquisition of a distinct ARID1A frameshift mutation (G323fs; VAF 0.23) along with two TP53 mutations (R273H; VAF 0.24 and Y126fs; VAF 0.28) (Table 1). Subsequently, venetoclax plus azacitidine therapy was administered, followed by salvage chemotherapy with high-dose cytarabine and mitoxantrone with the intent of achieving disease control for a second transplantation. However, the leukemia proved refractory, and the clinical course was further complicated by several viral infections. The patient ultimately did not undergo a second transplant and died 314 days after the initial allogeneic transplant. The overall clinical course is summarized in Fig. 2.

Discussion

This case offers clinically relevant insight into the potential therapeutic vulnerability and subsequent clonal evolution of AML harboring FLT3-JMD mutations. While the oncogenic and drug-sensitive nature of FLT3-JMD mutations has been demonstrated in preclinical models, clinical evidence supporting the efficacy of FLT3 inhibitors in this molecular subset has been extremely limited. Our observation suggests that FLT3-JMD mutations, such as V579A, may represent a therapeutically relevant molecular subset in selected clinical contexts. At diagnosis, the FLT3 V579A mutation was detected at a relatively high variant allele frequency, suggesting its presence in a major leukemic clone. Despite achieving initial remission with standard induction chemotherapy, the patient experienced early relapse after consolidation therapy, indicating an intrinsically treatment-resistant disease course. Previous studies have shown that FLT3 V579A leads to constitutive FLT3 signaling and enhanced leukemic proliferation, and AML patients harboring FLT3-JMD mutations experience unfavorable outcomes [12, 13]. Consistent with these findings, the rapid and robust response to gilteritinib observed in our patient supports the hypothesis that the relapsed leukemia remained functionally dependent on FLT3 signaling at that time point. Importantly, the clinical course of this patient suggests a transient “FLT3-dependent phase” of disease biology. Gilteritinib monotherapy induced a second hematologic remission within weeks, enabling successful bridging to allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. However, at post-transplant relapse, gilteritinib was completely ineffective. Comprehensive genomic analysis at second relapse demonstrated loss of the original FLT3 V579A clone and emergence of TP53-mutated independent clones, indicating a shift in the dominant leukemic population. This pattern is consistent with previous reports in FLT3-ITD-positive AML, in which selective pressure from FLT3 inhibition or transplantation leads to clonal evolution and outgrowth of FLT3-independent, genetically complex subclones [14, 15].

Notably, a small subset of AML patients without canonical FLT3-ITD/TKD mutations has been reported to respond to gilteritinib [16], raising the possibility that FLT3 pathway dependency may exist even in the absence of canonical FLT3 alterations. Although functional analyses of FLT3 activation were not performed in this case, the clinical course of this patient is consistent with the possibility that the AML cells harbored functional dependence on FLT3 signaling. As gilteritinib is a multikinase inhibitor, off-target antitumor effects such as AXL inhibition cannot be excluded. However, mRNA and protein expression levels of AXL and other relevant targets were not assessed in this case, which we acknowledge as an additional limitation of this report.

Several factors may have contributed to this rapid clonal shift. Given the short-term interval between transplantation and the emergence of a TP53-mutated dominant clone, the 5% variant allele frequency detection threshold of our NGS panel, and the presence of residual MRD prior to transplantation, the existence of a low-frequency TP53-mutated clone at the time of initial diagnosis cannot be entirely excluded. Prior treatment and post-transplant immune surveillance may have preferentially suppressed FLT3-mutated clones, facilitating the selective expansion of TP53-mutated populations with intrinsic resistance to both targeted therapy and graft-versus-leukemia effects. Although it must be acknowledged that inferring clonal architecture from VAF is inherently limited, and the potential for sampling bias introduced by single-site bone marrow aspiration cannot be definitively excluded, these observations underscore the dynamic nature of AML clonal architecture and the context-dependent efficacy of targeted therapies.

The concurrent presence of ARID1A mutations further complicates the interpretation of leukemogenic drivers in this case. Although it remains unclear whether the initial ARID1A and FLT3 mutations resided within the same clone, the acquisition of a distinct ARID1A frameshift mutation at relapse suggests ongoing genomic instability and clonal diversification. ARID1A alterations have been implicated in disease progression and therapeutic resistance in AML [17], and their emergence alongside TP53 mutations likely contributed to the aggressive, treatment-refractory phenotype observed after transplantation. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have reported an association between ARID1A mutations and sensitivity to gilteritinib or other FLT3 inhibitors.

This case has several important clinical implications. First, it highlights the potential value of extended molecular profiling in patients classified as “FLT3-negative” by routine testing, as rare FLT3-JMD point mutations may be missed yet remain therapeutically relevant. Second, it suggests that FLT3 inhibitors may be effective even in the absence of canonical FLT3-ITD or TKD mutations in cases where leukemic cells may retain dependence on FLT3 signaling. Finally, it suggests the need for longitudinal genomic monitoring, particularly in the setting of relapse and post-transplantation, to guide rational therapeutic decision-making.

This report has several limitations. First, as this is a single-patient report and mutational profiling was not performed on bone marrow specimens obtained at first relapse, definitive establishment of FLT3 V579A as the primary leukemogenic driver is not possible. Second, functional analyses such as phospho-FLT3 and STAT5 activation were not performed using patient-derived samples, and therefore FLT3 pathway dependence was not directly demonstrated. Third, as gilteritinib is a multikinase inhibitor, off-target effects cannot be excluded as contributing factors to the clinical response. Nevertheless, the temporal association between gilteritinib administration and clinical response, followed by loss of the FLT3-mutated clone at resistance, is consistent with a biologically meaningful role of this mutation. It should be noted that current evidence supporting the efficacy of FLT3 inhibitors in AML harboring FLT3-JMD mutations remains limited, and their use in this context constitutes off-label administration. Prospective studies and larger case series are warranted to determine the prevalence, prognostic impact, and optimal therapeutic strategies for AML patients harboring FLT3-JMD mutations.
