RADAR in COPD: From Risk Prediction to Clinical Action
Authors: Myriam Calle Rubio, Soha Esmaili, Juan Luis Rodríguez Hermosa
Published: 2026

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) remains a major clinical challenge because of its burden, heterogeneity, and the difficulty of assessing patients’ clinical status longitudinally in routine care. Beyond assigning a severity category or clinical phenotype, the central challenge is to recognize when clinical control is no longer maintained, identify potentially modifiable drivers of poor control, and select interventions whose effects can be reassessed over time.1, 2 Although tools such as the CAT are useful for assessing symptoms and health status, they do not fully operationalize clinical control in COPD.

The concept of clinical control has emerged as a dynamic target in COPD management. It integrates the patient's current clinical impact with recent stability and therefore requires repeated reassessment rather than a single classification assigned at diagnosis.1, 3 This requires a brief, reproducible, and clinically actionable tool that can be incorporated into routine follow-up. RADAR was designed to fill this gap by combining key routine clinical domains into a simple score that supports longitudinal monitoring and treatment decisions. RADAR was developed as a pragmatic instrument to quantify this construct using four routinely available variables: dyspnea, rescue medication use, recent exacerbations, and physical activity. The resulting score ranges from 0 to 8 points, with scores of 0–1, 2–3, and ≥4 corresponding to good, partial, and poor clinical control, respectively.4

RADAR emerged from the development of a quantitative clinical-control score within the EPOCONSUL audit, grounded in the established GesEPOC criteria for COPD control.4 Its subsequent prospective validation in a specialist-care cohort showed that RADAR-based control categories were associated with future adverse clinical events and 12-month health status.5 This step was important because it provided prognostic validation for a concise measure that translates a previously qualitative clinical-control framework into an operational tool for routine follow-up.

Longitudinal evidence from primary care has extended the evidence base for RADAR beyond specialist settings. In a prospective multicentre observational cohort of 706 patients receiving maintenance triple therapy, poor baseline control (RADAR ≥4) was associated with a higher 3-month risk of severe exacerbations and composite clinical events. When modelled as a continuous measure, each 1-point increase in RADAR was also associated with a higher rate of moderate exacerbations. Changes in both RADAR and CAT scores were observed over follow-up, supporting the interpretation of COPD control as a dynamic construct that merits repeated assessment in routine care.6 These findings extend the evidence for longitudinal change, however, its validation in other national and international populations is still needed.

A clinically important limitation of routine COPD follow-up is the discordance between structured assessment of control and physician perception. In a multicentre cross-sectional primary care cohort (n = 988), 45.7% had poor RADAR-defined control. Overall agreement between physician-assessed control and RADAR classification was 49.3%, and physician assessment overestimated control in 34.0% of cases.7 Although this observation cannot establish a causal link with therapeutic inertia, it identifies a clinically relevant perception gap. Consistent with this interpretation, the EPOCONSUL audit – using qualitative GesEPOC control criteria rather than RADAR – found that, among patients classified as uncontrolled, physician classification of the disease as controlled was independently associated with therapeutic inertia (OR 3.37, 95% CI 2.33–4.88).8 These complementary observations support the use of structured assessment to make poor control visible and to prompt a systematic evaluation of potentially modifiable drivers during routine follow-up.

The analysis of independent determinants of poor control provides a further practical message. In the same primary care cohort, exacerbator phenotypes were the strongest correlates of poor RADAR-defined control; however, several potentially modifiable factors were also independently associated with poor control, including active smoking, lower adherence, high dosing frequency, and a greater inhaler burden.7 These findings indicate that poor control reflects not only underlying disease severity but also behavioral and treatment-related factors that may be amenable to intervention. Although the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, a high RADAR score may therefore serve as a signal to review smoking exposure, adherence, and regimen complexity, rather than being interpreted solely as an indication for pharmacological escalation.

Evidence from a prospective multicentre observational cohort illustrates how RADAR can describe changes following treatment simplification. Among 684 patients switching from multiple-inhaler triple therapy to single-inhaler triple therapy, the 3-month switch was associated with reduced regimen complexity, a more favourable adherence profile, and a lower prevalence of poor RADAR-defined control. Higher baseline RADAR burden was associated with a greater likelihood of clinically meaningful improvement, whereas severe airflow limitation was associated with a lower likelihood of response. These observations should be interpreted cautiously, because higher baseline scores confer greater scope for improvement and the observational design cannot establish a causal effect of simplification on clinical control. Nevertheless, repeated RADAR assessment may be useful for documenting clinical evolution after a treatment change and identifying patients whose control warrants closer review.9

Further supportive evidence comes from the REDACT study, a multicentre, prospective, single-arm real-world study of patients with uncontrolled COPD treated with aclidinium/formoterol. At 12 weeks, 849 (83.7%) patients achieved control according to the original COPD control criteria, accompanied by significant improvements in lung function and health status. In a post hoc analysis, RADAR classifications also shifted significantly towards the good-control category. Given the uncontrolled design and post hoc nature of the RADAR analysis, these findings should be interpreted as showing that RADAR can describe short-term changes in clinical status during follow-up, rather than as formal evidence of score responsiveness or of a drug-specific effect.10

Therapeutic inertia is likely to arise when clinical control is judged informally or when symptom burden is considered in isolation. RADAR offers a practical way to counter this problem by standardizing the assessment of control, highlighting patients with persistent instability, and supporting a more systematic review of treatment adequacy and modifiable barriers. Although prospective implementation studies are needed, this structured approach may help close the gap between recognition of poor control and treatment adjustment. Current evidence supports a broader role for RADAR beyond risk stratification. By providing a structured and repeatable measure of clinical control, the score may identify patients who require a focused review of potentially modifiable contributors to poor control, including smoking, adherence, and regimen complexity, together with exacerbation history and the adequacy of the current treatment strategy. RADAR should not be used as a stand-alone treatment algorithm or as a trigger for automatic pharmacological escalation. RADAR has the potential to move COPD follow-up beyond passive risk description towards more structured clinical decision-making. Its principal value may lie in making poor control visible, supporting a focused review of potentially modifiable contributors, and enabling reassessment after treatment optimisation. Whether RADAR-guided follow-up improves patient-centred outcomes or reduces therapeutic inertia will require prospective implementation studies. Nevertheless, its simplicity and prognostic value make it a promising tool to support more individualized and consistent COPD care.

RADAR has shown promising prognostic validity in prior cohorts, and the present findings extend its longitudinal interpretation by showing measurable change over time; however, it is important to distinguish prognostic validity from longitudinal change and from true responsiveness to treatment. The current evidence supports the first two, whereas the latter requires prospective intervention studies.
