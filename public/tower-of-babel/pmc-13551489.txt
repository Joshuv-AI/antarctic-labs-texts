Introduction

Septic shock is one of the leading causes of admission and death in ICUs and remains a significant global health challenge (Arbous et al. 2024). The core pathophysiological alterations of septic shock lie in vasodilation and capillary leakage, which disturbances precipitate a rapid decline in effective circulating volume and consequent impairment of microcirculatory perfusion. Fluid resuscitation constitutes the foundational, time‐sensitive intervention for restoring hemodynamic stability and reversing shock progression (Chiu and Legrand 2021). Fluid therapy is a continuous and highly dynamic process characterized by distinct phases throughout the clinical course. In clinical practice, intravenous access is primarily chosen during the early stage of fluid therapy, with central venous catheters (CVCs) typically being the first choice. ‘static access management’ is common: a CVC is often kept in place until treatment ends, the patient leaves the ICU, or complications arise, rather than being adjusted based on the patient's changing hemodynamic needs. However, this often overlooks ongoing hemodynamic needs, fluid management goals, and infection risks, and lacks clear criteria for de‐escalation, resulting in unnecessarily extended CVC dwell. The existing consensus regarding intravenous access management in critically ill patients statements emphasize the safe use of central venous catheters (CVCs) and core management strategies, which encompass standardized operational protocols, infection prevention and control measures and evidence‐based technical selection (Slama et al. 2020; Pinelli et al. 2025). Strategies for venous access selection and the timing of access transitions have been inadequately addressed. There exists a lack of evidence‐based criteria and operational standards to guide critical clinical decisions concerning the initiation, de‐escalation and discontinuation of access. Current management of venous access in septic shock lacks a scientific framework to guide access type, maintenance and timing based on the diverse pathophysiological stages. This leads to reliance on personal experience, causing practice variability, inconsistent care quality and increased risk of complications like catheter‐related bloodstream infections. This study examines the characteristics of fluid therapy across various stages of septic shock and the corresponding needs for venous access devices. It proposes a phased intravenous access management strategy tailored to the treatment of septic shock, aligning access needs with the specific phases of treatment. This approach aims to reduce the risk of catheter‐related bloodstream infections, improve patient outcomes and optimize the utilization of medical resources.

Background

Critical illness patients need venous access devices for various clinical situations (emergency, ICU and surgery) and purposes (blood samples, medications, fluids, contrast medium and haemodynamic monitoring) (Abdulelah et al. 2025). ICU patients typically receive twice as many IV drugs as non‐ICU patients and often require resuscitation and haemodynamic support via IV vasopressors (Barlow et al. 2020). The selection of appropriate vascular access devices is critical to the management of critically ill patients, as suboptimal decision‐making may result in patient harm and poor clinical outcomes (Woller et al. 2016). The process of selecting and deciding on vascular access for critically ill patients presents significant challenges in clinical practice. ICU patients often need multiple‐lumen catheters for high infusion rates and incompatible drugs. Central venous catheters (CVCs) provide this with their wider lumen compared to peripheral catheters. Additionally, many medications for severe illnesses, like certain antibiotics, require central venous infusion, highlighting the essential role of CVCs for these patients (Borgonovo et al. 2023; Quici et al. 2025). It has been reported that more than 78% of patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) require the insertion of a central venous catheter (CVC) (Tamblyn et al. 2022). However, the use of CVADs carries inherent risks, including catheter‐related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs), which can lead to significant morbidity, mortality and increased healthcare costs (Baldauf et al. 2025). The Infusion Therapy Standards of Practice were published by Infusion Therapy Standards indicated that the appropriate vascular access device (VAD) is selected on the basis of the prescribed therapy or treatment regimen, anticipated duration of therapy, vascular pathway, patient's age, comorbidities, history of infusion therapy and vascular access, patient preference for VAD type and location and overall vascular health (history of difficult intravenous access, vessel and skin health at the insertion site) (Nickel et al. 2024). However, the Infusion Therapy Standards of Practice generally designed for a wide audience and cover a wide range of topics. Clinicians recognize that the management of critically ill patients in emergent situations differs significantly from the treatment of stable patients in controlled environments. Before determining the optimal type and placement of a venous access device, it is imperative to evaluate the patient's requirements for intravenous fluid therapy (Scorer et al. 2025).

In general, sepsis patients are treated in four phases: resuscitation, optimization, stabilization and evacuation. In most cases, patients who require fluid resuscitation will enter this (R.O.S.E.) conceptual framework during the rescue phase (Hoste et al. 2014). The new fluid therapy framework emphasizes personalized fluid assessment, timely administration and frequent reassessment. Each therapy phase has specific goals, requiring adaptable intravenous access devices. For instance, during the early septic shock, timely and adequate fluid resuscitation is vital for tissue perfusion. The treatment's success depends on the venous access flow rate. Poor access choice can lead to volume overload and tissue edema. Insufficient flow rate limits fluid replacement, delaying shock reversal, while high‐flow catheters without careful monitoring risk overload. Thus, aligning access capabilities with treatment needs is crucial (Bentzer et al. 2016; Mouncey et al. 2015).

This study introduces ‘phased dynamic management of vascular access,’ creating an indicator system by integrating vascular access management into the four fluid therapy phases for septic patients. It highlights ‘access‐requirement matching degree’ as a key evaluation metric, promoting proactive decisions at critical points, considering escalation during the ‘optimization’ phase and de‐escalation during the ‘stabilization/weaning’ phase. This approach transforms vascular access management into a continuous optimization process, elevating ‘Vascular Access Management’ from basic catheter maintenance to strategic planning and dynamic optimization, thus advancing critical care nursing. This assessment indicator system provides ICU nurses and clinical teams with a structured framework to standardize vascular access device selection, reducing decision‐making variability. It serves as essential content for critical care and vascular access education, helping nurses understand the relationship between fluid therapy in septic shock and vascular access needs, while reinforcing knowledge of catheter risks and management. Additionally, it offers evidence‐based guidance for hospital infection protocols, nursing quality standards and vascular access management policies. The assessment indicator system formulated in this study is expected to decrease the dwell time of central venous catheters, reduce the incidence of catheter‐related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs), enhance patient outcomes and optimize the allocation of medical resources in future clinical practice.

Methods

Aims

To determine a consensus on the evaluation criteria for venous access tools appropriate for each stage of septic shock using the Delphi method.

Methods

Study Design

The Delphi method was employed to develop a comprehensive assessment indicator system for evaluating the appropriateness of venous access devices in addressing the infusion needs of patients with septic shock. The Delphi method, referred to as the expert consultation technique in China, is a systematic decision‐support framework commonly employed to address uncertain and incomplete issues through the integration of collective expert opinions (Drumm et al. 2022). It leverages the extensive wisdom and experience of experts and is applicable in domains such as economics, development planning, finance and healthcare (Nasa et al. 2021). The Delphi method is extensively employed in the development of diverse evaluation systems and the identification of specific indicators, owing to its simplicity, intuitiveness and harnessing of collective wisdom (Waldmüller et al. 2020). The reporting of this study was informed by the Guidance on Conducting and Reporting Delphi Studies (CREDES) in palliative care, which comprises recommendations derived from a methodological systematic review (EQUATOR research reporting checklists, see File S1) (Jünger et al. 2017).

Formation of a Research Team

The research team consisted of ICU medical and nursing leaders, a clinical pharmacist, an ultrasound technologist, a dietician, an IV specialist nurse, an ICU specialist nurse and three nursing master's students. All members are familiar with the study content and are responsible for the following key tasks: literature retrieval, preparation of inquiry letters, expert communication and consultation, data collection and statistical analysis and revising the indicators according to the experts.

Construction of an Initial Draft

The study was initiated with a literature review, followed by a content analysis of the literature to extract evidence related to the selection and assessment of vascular access devices (VADs). To improve the practical applicability of clinical protocols, the research team systematically characterized the four stages of fluid therapy for patients with septic shock and established evaluation criteria for intravenous access devices according to the specific requirements and evidence‐based guidelines of each stage. The subsequent sections provide a detailed description of these four stages (Malbrain et al. 2020).

Resuscitation (R): Severe shock, life saving, fluid management and the use of vasopressor medications cannot be tapered and/or incrementally increased. Optimization (O): Unstable, organ rescue, maintenance of tissue perfusion and stabilization of vasopressor drug administration. Stabilization (S): Stable, organ support, aimed for zero or negative fluid balance, no vasopressor drug use or a decreasing trend of drug administration. Evacuation (E): Recovery, organ recovery and no vasopressor drug use. The draft hinged on the (R.O.S.E.) conceptual framework of fluid resuscitation in septic shock patients.

The research team generated an original needs assessment indicator system consisting of 4 indicators of the first level, 10 indicators of the second level and 35 indicators of the third level. The first level indicators reflect the core requirements for venous access devices at each stage of fluid therapy; the second level indicators are subdivisions of the first level indicators; and the third level indicators specify the details of the needs assessment. The initial draft was verified for readability and feasibility by two experienced experts in intravenous therapy nursing.

Delphi Process

Expert Selection and Inclusion Criteria

To ensure diverse research subjects, Delphi consultants should number between 15 and 30 (Dijkstra et al. 2021). The expert consultation was conducted over three rounds. The initial round involved 19 experts from Zhejiang Province and six Grade A tertiary hospitals, with ages ranging from 36 to 53 years. In the subsequent rounds, only one expert failed to meet the questionnaire deadline. This attrition rate of one expert per round was significantly below the acceptable threshold reported in similar expert consultation studies. Furthermore, the consistency of opinions among the remaining experts was sufficient to ensure the reliability of the final consultation outcomes. A limitation of this study is the exclusive use of a Chinese expert panel, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Nonetheless, this study represents a valuable initial step in addressing a significant gap in the literature. Inclusion criteria were as follows:(1) worked in third‐grade class general teaching hospitals at least 10 years; (2) must have engaged in ICU clinical practicing, Nursing education or intravenous clinical specialist nursing; (3) bachelor's degree or above; (4) Title of Mid‐level or above; (5) voluntarily participated in and promised to participate in all rounds of consultation; (6) informed consent. Exclusion criterion: missing the questionnaire deadline.

Preparation of the Expert Consultation Questionnaire

The expert consultation questionnaire had five parts: (1) Preface, detailing the study's significance, content, purpose and completion instructions; (2) Main section, where experts rated each needs assessment indicator's importance using a Likert scale from 1 (completely unimportant) to 5 (very important). Columns are available for indicators and revision comments, allowing experts to share ideas and suggestions. (3) Experts' general information: gender, age, educational, area of professional focus, title and years of experience.(4) Index judgement based on expert familiarity with the study. (5) Appendix: References and research team contact details.

Delphi Consulting and Feedback Cycle

Between June and August 2024, consultation questionnaires were emailed or sent via WeChat, with each expert consultation round lasting 2 weeks. Experts did not interact with each other. After the first round, researchers compiled feedback of each expert and revised the questionnaire for the next round to further confirm accuracy of the assessment index system. Indicators with a mean importance score above 3.5 and a coefficient of variation below 0.25 were retained (Wei et al. 2022). Researchers reviewed expert opinions on missing indicators and decided on their inclusion or exclusion. The assessment indicator system was finalized after three rounds of expert consultation.

Statistical Analysis

Data entry and analysis were performed using Excel 2019 and SPSS 25.0. Expert positivity was measured by the questionnaire response rate, with over 70% indicating high motivation (Jiang et al. 2023). The authority coefficient (Cr), calculated as Cr = (Ca + Cs)/2, reflects the expert's judgement basis (Ca) and familiarity (Cs) with the research. A Cr of 0.70 or higher suggests strong expert authority and reliable advice (Zhai et al. 2020). Experts' opinion coordination was measured using Kendall's W and the coefficient of variation (CV), with lower CVs indicating higher consistency. Index importance was shown as mean ± standard deviation, with significance at p < 0.05. We calculated each index's weight coefficient using SPSSAU software.

Quality Control

A systematic literature review was initially conducted to define the research topic and framework. In this study, the experts were selected from the specialized fields of critical care medicine, nursing and intravenous therapy with a view to avoiding potential biases in the results that might be caused by experts from the same backgrounds. During the consultation, the research team promptly addressed experts' questions and verified results. Afterward, they reviewed the returned questionnaires and compiled expert opinions. If more than 10% of the questionnaire items were left unanswered, the questionnaire would be deemed invalid and subsequently excluded from the analysis. The data were double‐checked and entered by two researchers via Excel 2019 software.

Ethical Approval

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, China (approval number: IIT20240271B‐R1) and conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, who could withdraw at any time.

The Formation Process of the Assessment Index System

The study included three consultation rounds, during which the indicator system was refined based on screening criteria, expert opinions and research group discussions (Figure 2).

Flow diagram of consultation process.

Round 1

Following the initial expert correspondence, a discussion with the research team resulted in the addition of one second‐level and four third‐level indicators. Twelve experts emphasized that patients remain in shock during resuscitation and optimization, underscoring the importance of peripheral veins despite this condition. As the disease progresses, central venous access should be prioritized, with a focus on its safety to ensure necessary infusions. Experts recommend evaluating the safety of emergency central venous catheters by verifying if they meet puncture specifications and determining whether replacement is needed within 48 h. They also highlight the importance of assessing the skin around the puncture site, noting that using different drugs in the same pathway can increase catheter failure rates due to interactions, and emphasizing the impact of catheter fixation on drug delivery efficiency and the risk of accidental removal. The second‐level indicator ‘Assess the safety of central venous access during emergencies (4.72 ± 0.46)’ was added. Third‐level indicators now include: ‘evaluate catheter placement for potential reset within 48 hours (4.78 ± 0.43)’, ‘inspect the puncture site (bleeding, oozing, skin injury) (4.78 ± 0.43)’, ‘prevent tube occlusion by assessing drug compatibility (4.67 ± 0.49)’ and ‘check catheter fixation to avoid kinking or accidental removal (4.78 ± 0.43)’. Round 2 commenced after these modifications.

Round 2

In round 2, three new third‐level indicators were introduced and two first‐level indicators were revised. Three experts emphasized that the assessment indicators should reflect different stages of disease progression. During resuscitation and optimization, multiple interventions such as drug administration, high‐flow rehydration and haemodynamic monitoring may occur concurrently, raising concerns about the adequacy of intravenous access devices. During evacuation, most patients have relatively simple infusion needs and are nearing transfer or discharge. However, some patients may still require parenteral nutrition or specialized medications, necessitating an evaluation of their infusion requirements for general treatment. ICU staff must prepare for vascular access transitional care and reassess infection risks associated with central venous devices before discharge. We introduced three new third‐level indicators: ‘timely drug delivery access (3.89 ± 0.58)’, ‘general drug use services (4.56 ± 0.62)’ and ‘catheter retention risk assessment prior to ICU transfer (4.61 ± 0.50)’. Additionally, we revised two first‐level indicators: ‘evaluation and optimization of intravenous access devices (4.44 ± 0.51)’ and ‘further assessment of the patient's venous access device (4.61 ± 0.50)’. These indicators aim to capture the evolving infusion needs during septic shock treatment in the ICU. A new consultation questionnaire was developed, incorporating 57 indicators into round 3.

Round 3

In the third consultation round, experts provided no additional comments, indicating a high level of consensus on each indicator. Over three Delphi study rounds, the assessment index system was progressively refined, culminating in a final system comprising 4 first‐level, 11 second‐level and 42 third‐level indicators (Table 4).

Discussion

Reliability and Scientific Analysis

The evaluation index system for venous access devices in patients with septic shock, developed in this study and oriented towards fluid therapy needs, has been demonstrated to be scientifically robust and reliable. The recall rates for the first and second consultation questionnaires were 100% and 94.74%, respectively. Constructive opinions were provided by 63.16% and 27.78% of experts. This shows high motivation and support from the experts. The authority coefficients of the three consultation rounds were 0.869, 0.886 and 0.931, respectively, which indicated a high level of expert familiarity with critical nursing content. A limitation of this study, lies in the adoption of a self‐evaluation tool for expert assessment, which results in the absence of an objective evaluation of the experts involved the Kendall's W values in this study (0.307–0.323) indicates a low level of agreement. Furthermore, the Kendall's W value combined with the finding of statistical significance (p < 0.001) (Table 3)—confirms that the evaluation indicator system possesses a certain degree of reliability. Similar critical care Delphi studies support the acceptability of low‐to‐moderate Kendall's W values (Yu et al. 2025). That study used the Delphi method to develop nursing quality indicators for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, reporting Kendall's W values of 0.111–0.145 over two rounds, confirming that values between 0.10 and 0.33 are suitable for critical care research. Combining the Delphi method with AHP enhances the objectivity and reliability of the results. During the resuscitation phase, the criteria of ‘ensuring sufficient access for adequate fluid resuscitation (high flow rates and power‐injection capability)’ and ‘the appropriateness of hemodynamic monitoring’ received relatively high scores (4.44 ± 0.51 and 4.67 ± 0.49, respectively). This indicates that venous access must fulfil the performance requirements for early rapid volume expansion and circulatory monitoring. These findings align with the principle of ‘initiating fluid resuscitation within one hour’ as emphasized in the Surviving Sepsis Campaign (Evans et al. 2021). During the optimization phase, experts prioritized the safety of emergency central venous access. Indicators like ‘evaluating catheter potential reset within 48 h’, ‘Inspect the puncture site’ and ‘Check catheter fixation to avoid kinking or accidental removal’ scored 4.78, indicating strong expert agreement. This highlights the need for systematic short‐term evaluation and maintenance to prevent complications. Additionally, the indicator for ‘preventing catheter occlusion by assessing drug compatibility’ scored 4.67, underscoring the importance of chemical compatibility in maintaining access patency. This shift reflects a move from focusing solely on catheter placement to emphasizing ongoing maintenance. In the stable phase, high scores were given to ‘Meet the needs of contrast injection during the CT scan (power‐injection capability)’ (4.78 ± 0.43), ‘Evaluate peripheral intravenous catheters (PIVCs) function’ (4.67 ± 0.49) and ‘ Evaluate the risk of catheter retention or removal’ (4.72 ± 0.46), showing their importance and the need for thorough venous access evaluation. In contrast, ‘ Using diverse and complex medicines safely’ and ‘Assess for signs of phlebitis, extravasation/leakage’ scored lower (both 3.56 ± 0.51) but remain clinically significant, highlighting the need for ongoing attention to drug interactions and catheter safety. During the Evacuation phase, the item ‘4.3.3 Remove unnecessary central venous catheters’ scored highest (4.83 ± 0.51), aligning with the Infusion Nursing Standards of Practice's advice to remove non‐essential catheters early to reduce infection risk. Including indicators like ‘drug compatibility assessment’ highlights that venous access safety involves systematic clinical decisions, not just technical issues. These findings create a dynamic, detailed venous access management pathway with important clinical guidance implications.

Impact of De‐Escalation Catheter Management on Preventing Catheter‐Related Bloodstream Infections

Catheter‐associated bloodstream infections are common in healthcare settings, negatively affecting patient outcomes and increasing costs (Chovanec et al. 2021). Factors influencing these infections include assessment, catheter placement, nursing care and patient management (Hussain et al. 2021). Critical ill patients are highly susceptible to infections, making careful vascular access selection crucial for their safety. In practice, it is essential to evaluate the pros and cons of catheter types. Central venous catheters (CVCs) are preferred for rapid fluid replacement due to their high blood flow and multiple lumens, but they carry a higher infection risk compared to peripheral venous catheters (Pitiriga et al. 2022). A retrospective study found that infection rates significantly increased in patients with central venous catheters (CVCs) left in place for over 21 days, unlike those with peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs). This suggests that catheter type and duration influence infection risk. Therefore, after the critical illness phase, catheters should be replaced with safer options as the disease progresses to minimize infection risk. However, in practice, healthcare professionals often overlook the importance of catheter indwelling time, especially in critically ill patients, and should pay more attention to this factor (Pitiriga et al. 2025). Many guidelines stress the importance of standardized catheter insertion and maintenance to prevent central line associated bloodstream infections (CLABSI). However, there is limited guidance on assessing and selecting suitable intravenous devices and adjusting venous access devices to reduce CLABSI (Nickel et al. 2024; Ling et al. 2016; Chaves et al. 2018; Rosenthal et al. 2025), but provide limited guidance on the evaluation and appropriate selection of venous access devices, as well as on the timely adjustment of these devices based on assessment outcomes. This issue has persisted unresolved until now. In this study, an expert consultation questionnaire was developed based on the characteristics of fluid therapy at different stages of septic shock and the varying requirements for venous access devices. Introduces a phased intravenous access management strategy for the treatment of septic shock, which aligns access requirements with the distinct phases of treatment. The strategy underscores the importance of catheter de‐escalation and timely removal during the Evacuation phase, with the potential to reduce catheter indwelling time, decrease the risk of catheter‐related bloodstream infections, enhance patient outcomes and optimize the utilization of medical resources.

Analysis of Evidence Application in Clinical Practice

This study aimed to evaluate the rational and safe selection of intravenous devices for fluid therapy in septic shock patients across different stages. Appropriate intravenous access is essential for effective fluid administration in these patients. Matching the progression of the disease with the choice of venous access can optimize patient outcomes by reducing the risk of errors. The index system for evaluating the requirements of venous access devices developed in this study comprehensively encompasses the characteristics of fluid therapy at different stages of septic shock. (1) Resuscitation: In severe shock, rapid fluid resuscitation and timely administration of vasopressors are critical to achieving adequate perfusion pressure. Continuous hemodynamic monitoring is essential, and prompt, evidence‐based decision‐making is required. Vasopressors should be initiated via peripheral access to avoid delays in treatment, prioritizing the restoration of mean arterial pressure despite the risk of drug extravasation (Nickel et al. 2024; Evans et al. 2021; Paxton and Lemieux 2021). In emergencies, two peripheral venous accesses (18G or larger) are required for proper fluid resuscitation (Jünger et al. 2017). If peripheral access fails after two attempts, central venous access is established immediately (Nickel et al. 2024). Furthermore, in scenarios where patients encounter life‐threatening conditions such as cardiac arrest and immediate intravenous access cannot be established, it is advisable to promptly initiate an intraosseous route to ensure timely medication administration (Soar et al. 2015). (2) Optimization: Hypovolaemia has been resolved, but haemodynamic instability persists. The primary objective is to support organ function and maintain adequate tissue perfusion through personalized and regularly reassessed fluid therapy. It is crucial to review the safety of existing venous access devices, as emergency placements are frequently suboptimal. Assess all peripheral venous access points and remove any catheters that pose a safety risk, such as those inserted non‐sterilely, dysfunctional catheters, those causing phlebitis or suspected of infection (Nickel et al. 2024; Pittiruti et al. 2023; Tran et al. 2020; Ray‐Barruel et al. 2020). Replace upper extremities promptly if peripheral access is needed (Nickel et al. 2024). Non aseptic emergency access should be changed or removed within 48 h (Pittiruti et al. 2023; Selby et al. 2021; O'Grady et al. 2011). Use multilumen catheters for incompatible drug infusions (Nickel et al. 2024; Chopra et al. 2015). Catheter‐related bloodstream infections are more common at sites with skin injuries and fluid leakage (Pittiruti et al. 2023; Tran et al. 2020; Selby et al. 2021). Improperly fixed central venous catheters can cause accidental extubation, impacting drug delivery efficiency. (3) Stabilization: This phase of fluid therapy focuses on replacing ongoing losses, supporting organs and replenishing water and electrolytes. It is crucial to thoroughly evaluate IV access devices, considering factors like the therapy duration, drug properties, need for hemodynamic monitoring, vascular conditions, disease factors and patient preferences (Chopra et al. 2015; Moureau 2019). This helps optimize venous access to ensure safe fluid infusion and prevent catheter‐related infections. Due to the disease's stability, a multidisciplinary approach, especially involving clinical pharmacists, is advised (Jacobi 2020). (4) Evacuation: Prior to transfer, the patient was in a step‐down treatment phase aimed at reducing fluid overload. To minimize infection risk, it is essential to remove unnecessary catheters based on the patient's evolving needs (Nickel et al. 2024). The choice of central venous catheters for critically ill patients depends on weighing risks and benefits (Chinese Abdominal Intensive Care Association and Asia Society of Emergency and Critical Medicine 2022). The peripheral venous system poses a lower risk of bloodstream infections, is safer, and easier to establish (Nickel et al. 2024; Carr et al. 2016; Manrique‐Rodríguez et al. 2021). Effective transitional care should encompass comprehensive venous access management to ensure seamless transfers of critically ill patients from the ICU. In addition, In the ICU, choosing the right first‐line antibiotics for septic shock is crucial. Research indicates that early use of broad‐spectrum antibiotics at the maximum recommended dose improves outcomes by enhancing tissue penetration (Zelenitsky et al. 2013). Highlight that factors like diluent, pH and infusion rate impact medication safety in critically ill patients. Selecting the appropriate vascular access device (VAD) can prevent complications such as chemical phlebitis. For septic shock patients, clinicians should consider antibiotic properties, administration methods and patient status to tailor treatment strategies that optimize drug delivery and minimize adverse effects (Borgonovo et al. 2023).

Comprehensive and Limitations Analysis

The MAGIC guidelines emphasize the appropriate use of peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) in hospitalized patients and delineate the justified indications for their placement (Woller et al. 2016). The ERPIUP consensus standardizes the comprehensive management of peripheral venous access processes (Pittiruti et al. 2023). Meanwhile, the GAVeCeL T consensus offers evidence‐based practices and safety strategies for central venous access in critically ill patients (Pinelli et al. 2025). In alignment with the R.O.S.E. concept of fluid resuscitation for patients experiencing septic shock, this study focused on the evolving venous access requirements across various clinical stages. A staged, de‐escalating approach was implemented to align with diverse therapeutic needs, transforming general safety principles into a dynamic, precise and real‐time assessment tool. This framework is more attuned to the clinical realities of critical care environments, enhancing the scientific rigour and safety of venous access selection, reducing catheter‐related complications and optimizing the quality of diagnosis and treatment. These patients, particularly in emergency settings, differ significantly from those in stable conditions. Consequently, there is a need for specialized standards and training materials to enhance clinical practice skills in this critical area. This study delineates four stages of infusion therapy for sepsis patients, enhancing clinical implementation and operability. It also highlights key aspects of intravenous access management for each stage, aiding nurses in improving process management within a holistic nursing framework. However, the choice of intravenous access device is not solely determined by fluid therapy needs. Numerous factors must be considered when selecting an appropriate venous access device, including patient characteristics, handling conditions, and treatment duration. Even experienced clinical operators find it challenging to make the optimal decision.

Clinical guidelines cannot encompass every possible outcome. This study aimed to develop a standardized approach for managing septic shock patients and to establish protocols for evaluating venous access in emergency settings. The findings can be applied to clinical practice, training and management for critical care nurses.

Owing to limitations in time and available manpower, this study employs a self‐evaluation tool without objective expert assessment. All experts are from the same region in China and primarily from tertiary hospitals, introducing potential bias in their perspectives. However, their extensive experience in critical care, nursing, and intravenous therapy across various fields like clinical medicine and nursing education ensures diverse and comprehensive consultation outcomes, reducing potential biases. The study only selected 19 experts to conduct the Delphi survey and most experts were from tertiary hospitals in Zhe Jiang Province, Therefore, the results of this study may be biassed. The evaluation indicators of this study focused exclusively on whether the current venous access devices can meet the fluid resuscitation needs and infusion safety of patients with septic shock at different stages. Other factors, such as techniques for catheter tip position, establishing venous access and puncture site selection, were not considered. Catheter tip position serves as a critical risk factor for catheter‐associated thrombosis (Caers et al. 2005). This study primarily focuses on the assessment of venous access device requirements in patients with septic shock. Consequently, there may be limitations in the appropriateness of the selected venous access devices. The indicator system developed in this study has not undergone rigorous testing for reliability and validity, nor has it been empirically applied and its validity and applicability in clinical settings need further evaluation.

Nursing Management and Implications for Clinical Practice

Fluid therapy is central to septic shock resuscitation, with intravenous access playing a crucial role. The choice and upkeep of infusion devices impact medication delivery and treatment plans. The study's evaluation criteria, combined with the patient's diagnosis, treatment stage and disease progression, help identify and address risks early, improving patient safety and reducing preventable adverse outcomes. The safe management of venous access in critically ill patients extends beyond the sole responsibility of ICU nurses; it necessitates a multidisciplinary approach to ensure effective clinical implementation and patient safety. It is recommended that evaluation indicators for venous access devices, specifically tailored for patients with septic shock, be integrated into the hospital information system to facilitate clinical promotion and application. Simultaneously, a computer‐aided decision‐making module should be implemented to assist clinical staff in the safe management of venous access.

ICU nurses, who frequently manage venous access in critically ill patients, should make informed and safe choices. Despite this, unsafe practices are common. In clinical practice, patient‐specific factors greatly impact vascular access decisions. For those on continuous renal replacement therapy or haemodialysis, dedicated catheters and vascular protection strategies are crucial to conserve limited access resources. Avoid using the subclavian site in haemodialysis and advanced kidney disease patients to prevent vein stenosis. Catheters are a major cause of bacteraemia in these patients. For temporary dialysis access expected to last over 3 weeks, opt for a tunnelled cuffed catheter instead of a non‐cuffed one, even in the ICU. Burn patients, due to damaged vessels and poor skin, with their necrotic tissue and bacteria, are high‐risk for infection. Catheters in or near burns (≥ 25 cm2) have a 1.79 times higher risk of bacterial colonization and a 5.12 times higher risk of bacteraemia than those placed elsewhere. Avoid placing venous catheters in burn‐injured areas or within 25 cm2 of burns. In emergencies, if no unburned sites are available, catheters may be placed near burns but must be removed within 72 h and monitored for infection (American Society of Anesthesiologists Task Force on Central Venous Access 2020). Patients on anticoagulants or with bleeding disorders have a higher bleeding risk. Therefore, difficult‐to‐compress sites like the subclavian vein should be avoided (Mumoli et al. 2025). Instead, trained specialists should perform punctures under ultrasound guidance, with blood transfusions given as needed. Thus, adjustments should be tailored to the patient's condition and treatment stage. Given their close patient contact, ICU nurses should enhance their clinical innovation.

Conclusion

In critical nursing practice, after three expert consultations, a valid and reliable assessment index system was created. This system, based on the ROSE fluid therapy model for septic shock patients, includes indicators for intravenous access devices suited to various disease progression stages (Zampieri et al. 2023). The assessment index system aims to ensure patient safety, venous health and efficient emergency treatment. However, it remains theoretical, and further empirical research is needed to verify its practicality and feasibility.
