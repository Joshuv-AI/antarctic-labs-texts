Prevalence of Workplace‐Related Musculoskeletal Disorders Among Nurses and Midwives in a Tertiary Healthcare Facility: A Descriptive Cross‐Sectional Survey
Authors: Iddrisu Sisala Mohammed, Mohammed Hardi Abdulai, Mudasir Mohammed Ibrahim, Hannah Buasilenu, Issahaka Abdulai Baako, Brenda Abena Nyarko, Abubakari Wuni, Camillus Buunaaisie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo assess the prevalence of workplace‐related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs) among Nurses and Midwives in a Tertiary Healthcare Facility in Northern Ghana.MethodsThe study utilised a descriptive cross‐sectional study design using the STROBES guidelines. Standardised Nordic musculoskeletal questionnaire was used to gather data from 309 nurses and midwives. The selection of respondents was done using a simple random sampling method. The data were analysed using SPSS version 27.0.ResultsFrom the 259 included respondents, 195 (75.3%) experienced WMSDs in the last 12 months. 138 (70.7%) highlighted their low back (lumbar/sacral) as the affected region and 119 (61.0%) indicated experiencing WMSDs during the first 5 years of clinical practice. Key job‐related risk factors contributing to WMSDs development included working in the same positions (such as standing, bending over, sitting, kneeling) for long periods (176 [68.0%]), treating an excessive number of patients in 1 day (142 [54.8%]), continuing to work while injured or hurt (139 [53.7%]), bending or twisting the back in an awkward way (140 [54.1%]) and not enough rest breaks or pauses during the workday (130 [50.2%]). Regarding coping strategies, the most frequently employed method was getting someone else to help handle a heavy patient (83 [42.5%]). The multivariate regression analysis revealed that respondents with fewer than 5 years of experience were markedly more susceptible to reporting WMSDs compared to those with over 10 years of experience (AOR = 3.73, 95% CI [1.03, 13.42], p = 0.043).ConclusionThe overall prevalence of WMSDs among nurses and midwives was high. This study also revealed that the most commonly affected body region was their low back. Working in the same position for long periods was identified as a major occupational risk factor contributing to these disorders. Despite this risk, a significant number of nurses and midwives consistently sought assistance when handling heavy patients. The current study suggests that hospitals should provide comprehensive ergonomic training to healthcare professionals, emphasising proper body mechanics, posture and techniques for patient moving and handling.Patient or Public ContributionPatients or public were not involved in setting the research objectives, the design and preparation of the manuscript. Nurses and midwives working the tertiary hospital answered the research questionnaires.

Background

Work‐related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs) are well known for causing significant and long‐lasting pain and physical disability, affecting millions of people worldwide (Akodu and Ashalejo 2019). Occupational characteristics have been recognised to impact the health, safety and well‐being of individuals (Thinkhamrop and Laohasiriwong 2015).

The term ‘work‐related musculoskeletal disorders’ encompasses a wide range of inflammatory and degenerative discomforts and disorders affecting muscles, tendons, ligaments, joints, peripheral nerves and supporting blood vessels. These disorders develop when workers are exposed to risk factors from work activities and conditions or when the existing symptoms are aggravated by the working environment (Jin et al. 2011; Tinubu et al. 2010). WMSDs include various conditions like sprains, strains, lacerations, back pain, carpal tunnel syndrome and muscle/connective tissue diseases, which are often caused by repetitive actions, excessive force, bending, grasping and twisting (Lin et al. 2020).

Over the past few decades, nursing professionals have consistently comprised approximately 33% of the hospital workforce. Unfortunately, they face significant risks, accounting for 60% of reported occupational injuries (Akodu and Ashalejo 2019). Nursing professionals hold a crucial role in healthcare service delivery across the globe and their daily tasks vary significantly between nursing units. These tasks often involve moving, pulling or pushing patients, leading to excessive bending, turning, lifting and supporting patients during various activities. Due to this diversity in their work, they are exposed to different risk factors, leading to various WMSDs (Bediako et al. 2021; Lin et al. 2020; Nurhayati et al. 2022). The prevalence of WMSDs among nursing professionals is high, making it one of the most common occupational injuries among care workers (Thinkhamrop and Laohasiriwong 2015).

Numerous countries worldwide have reported high rates of WMSDs among nursing populations. For example, in China, the prevalence was 77.4% (Yan et al. 2017), while in Nigeria, it was 84.4% (Tinubu et al. 2010). Similar trends were observed in other countries like Zimbabwe (95.7%) (Chiwaridzo et al. 2018), India (89.1%) (Anap, Iyer, and Rao 2013), Kenya (70.8%) (Olutende et al. 2022), Bulgaria (97.5%) (Stanchev and Vangelova 2022), Thailand (91.7%) (Jin et al. 2011), Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (63.8%) (Tariah et al. 2020), Cameroon (77.9%) (Buh et al. 2021), Iran (58.2%) (Kaydani, Zarea, and Soltanzadeh 2022) and Malaysia (97.3%) (Krishnan, Raju, and Shawkataly 2021). Several studies have documented the annual prevalence of WMSDs in different regions, ranging from 40% to 95% in Asian and Western populations. The most commonly affected areas were the lower back (29%–64%), neck (34%–63%) and shoulders (17%–75%) (Azma et al. 2016). Notably, the back region was found to be the most prevalent site for musculoskeletal disorders among the working population (Harcombe et al. 2014; Clari et al. 2021).

Various workplace activities have been identified as risk factors for WMSDs, including prolonged standing, heavy lifting, repetitive tasks, fixed neck postures, excessive rotation, long working hours and uncomfortable working positions. Additionally, certain demographic features, such as the increasing age, body mass index (BMI) and work experience, as well as psychological factors like depression, are significant contributors to WMSDs (Saberipour et al. 2019; Stanchev and Vangelova 2022; Olutende et al. 2022). Lifestyle choices, like inadequate physical exercise and insufficient sleep, have also been associated with an increased risk of low back pain (Mirmohammadi and Yazdani 2014).

WMSDs are a significant contributing factor to early labor market exits, leading to rising compensation and healthcare costs, reduced productivity and a lower quality of life (Yan et al. 2017). Additionally, WMSDs have been shown to impact family roles negatively (Balakrishnan, Chellappan, and Changalai 2016). Numerous studies have extensively examined WMSDs in various occupational settings, including offices, manufacturing and healthcare (Amin et al. 2020).

In Ghana, nursing and midwifery professionals are particularly vulnerable and WMSDs have emerged as one of the most common occupational injuries within the medical service industry. Recent surveys in Ghana reported high prevalence rates of WMSDs among different categories of nurses, ranging from 18.8% to 79.0% (Boakye et al. 2018) and a subsequent survey in 2021 revealed a 94% prevalence of WMSDs among nursing population (Bediako et al. 2021).

The causes of WMSDs are complex and multifactorial (Lin et al. 2020). Some common perceived job risk factors include lifting or transferring dependent patients, inadequate injury prevention training and working in awkward or cramped positions (Boakye et al. 2018). However, despite these issues, research on WMSDs among nurses and midwives in the Northern Region of Ghana remains scarce compared to other regions as well as studies assessing both nurses and midwives. Furthermore, the lack of proper infrastructure and equipment in several hospitals in the Northern Region has contributed to the overall decline in the health of nurses and midwives, which could lead to an increased prevalence of WMSDs. Hospitals in the Northern Region of Ghana face significant challenges, including malfunctioning equipment, crumbling infrastructure and inadequate facility maintenance (Adatara et al. 2021). The layouts, furniture and equipment in the working environments are not optimised for comfort and safety, lacking adequate ergonomic support. Moreover, the insufficient availability of modern medical equipment and technology compels nurses and midwives to perform tasks manually (Krishnan, Raju, and Shawkataly 2021). They are often required to engage in frequent physical handling and lifting due to the absence of lifting aids or mechanised support, placing strain on their musculoskeletal system (Khudhir et al. 2017). This strain could potentially lead to the development of WMSDs, such as lower back pain and shoulder injuries. No studies have been conducted on the prevalence of WMSDs in general or on specific aspects of WMSDs among nurses and midwives in Northern Ghana. The negative effects of poor posture and musculoskeletal disorders are likely to persist unless appropriate measures are taken to identify and address them (Olutende et al. 2022). Therefore, this study assessed the prevalence of WMSDs among nurses and midwives in a Tertiary Healthcare Facility in Northern Ghana. By addressing the occupational health status, perceptions of job risk factors, coping strategies and associated factors with WMSDs, this research aimed to shed light on the pressing issues and pave the way for potential solutions.

Methods

Study Design

A descriptive cross‐sectional study with the STROBES guidelines was used to collect data from nurses and midwives working in the Tamale Teaching Hospital (TTH).

Study Setting

The study was conducted in TTH, it is a government tertiary hospital situated in ‘Dohinayili’ community, a suburb of Tamale Metropolis in the Northern Region of Ghana. It is the third oldest and largest teaching hospital in Ghana after the Korle Bu Teaching Hospital and Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital. It is also a referral hospital for the five regions of Northern Ghana.

The hospital has a bed capacity of over 800 and serves as a tertiary referral center for the regions, as well as neighbouring Burkina Faso. The University for Development Studies' School of Medicine and Health Sciences and other Nursing and Midwifery training colleges in and around Tamale, Ghana, uses it as a clinical training facility for its nursing and medical students. The hospital has a total nursing and midwifery population of 1357 which constituted the target population.

Study Population

The population of interest in the study was all nurses and midwives working in the Tamale Teaching Hospital. Those nurses and midwives who engaged in patient care in their daily work and had more than 1 year of work experience were included in the study. The exclusion criteria were nurses and midwives in administrative, educational or research roles in the hospital. Also, nurses and midwives who were on annual leave or not present during the study period were excluded.

Sample Size Determination and Sampling Technique

A total sample of 309 nurses and midwives were recruited for the study. The sample size was calculated using the Slovin (1960) formula.

The Slovin's formula is calculated as follows; n = N/(1 + Ne 2).where n is the sample size. N is the target population (1357). e is the margin of error. The margin of error for the study was 5%. We opted for a 5% margin of error to achieve a balance between sample size efficiency and precision.

This implies n = 1357/(1 + 1357[0.05]2) = 308.9≈309.

A simple random sampling technique was employed to recruit respondents for the study. Within each ward, we compiled a list of eligible respondents, assigning numbers to their names in sequential order from the first name to the last name. Afterward, this compiled list was imported into the SAS JMP Professional software. By employing the random assignment function feature of the software, we generated random numbers. Respondents whose names corresponded to these numbers were then given the questionnaires to fill out.

Data Collection Instrument

Data collection was done using a structured questionnaire adapted from Tinubu et al. (2010). The instrument is a Standardised Nordic musculoskeletal questionnaire which was developed by Kuorinka et al. (1987). The questionnaire consisted of four (4) sections: Section A, 12 multiple choice questions collected data on sociodemographic of respondents. Section B comprised 11 multiple choice questions collected data on occupational health in nursing practice. Section C consisted of 17 10‐point Likert scale questions collected data on perceptions on job risk factors that may contribute to development of WMSDs. The last Section D included nine multiple choice questions and collected data on coping strategies towards reducing the risk for development of WMSDs.

Instrument Validity and Reliability

The structured instrument was reviewed by a number of health experts to ensure its items reflect the true picture of study specific objectives. A pilot study was then conducted at Tamale Central Hospital with 15 respondents. During this pilot phase, it became evident that certain questions within the adopted questionnaire were irrelevant or incongruent with the study's objectives. A thoughtful decision was made to omit these specific questions to ensure the survey aligns more accurately with the targeted research objectives. The instrument showed a good internal consistency with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.89. Cronbach alpha is a way of assessing reliability of an instrument and values of 0.7 or higher indicate acceptable internal consistency (Nunnally and Bernstein 1994).

Data Collection Procedure

Data collection began after approval was obtained from the Research and Development Unit of TTH. Prior to data collection, respondents were informed about the study's purpose and provided written consent. They were assured that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without consequences. Data were collected through face‐to‐face interviews conducted by the researchers using a structured questionnaire designed to gather information on the study topic. The questionnaire was administered to respondents who agreed to take part in the study on a one‐on‐one basis in private, after obtaining both verbal and written consent. Respondents were instructed to complete the questionnaires at their convenience. Questionnaires were administered to all respondents within a duration of 4 weeks. Out of the 309 initially consenting respondents, 259 returned completely filled questionnaires, while the other 50 respondents were unable to complete them due to time constraints. Ultimately, the cooperation rate, representing the proportion of respondents who completed the questionnaires successfully, was 84.0%. Retrieved questionnaires were organised in envelopes to minimise the risk of damage during handling and transportation.

Study Variables

The dependent variable in this study is the prevalence of WMSDs, defined as the occurrence of WMSDs among respondents within the last 12 months and measured as a binary variable (presence or absence). The independent variables include sociodemographic factors such as age, body mass index, sex and marital status, as well as workplace factors, which encompass department, profession, rank, work experience and nursing qualification.

Statistical Analysis

Data were entered, cleaned and analysed using SPSS version 27.0. Descriptive statistics were employed to summarise the data gathered from the study respondents regarding sociodemographic factors and other relevant variables. The results were presented in the form of proportions, percentages, means and standard deviations. A binary logistic regression model was used to control for confounding variables and determine the factors associated with the prevalence of WMSDs, at a 95% confidence interval. The significance level was set at p‐value below 5%.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Research and Development Unit of the hospital with reference number REDACTED. Additional permission was obtained from the ward managers of the selected wards and respondents were informed about the nature of the study, including its purpose and procedures and provided written consent prior to their involvement. Respondents were assured of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty. The respondents were informed and guaranteed confidentiality and anonymity throughout the study.

Results

Sociodemographic Characteristics

A total of 309 nurses and midwives were invited to participate in the study from T.T.H. Out of these, a total of 259 individuals participated, resulting in a response rate of 84.0%. The respondents' mean age was 30.9 years (SD: 4.6), with 157 (60.6%) of them being between the ages of 30 to 39 years old. The respondents' mean body mass index was 25.0 kg/m2 (SD: 5.5) with 137 (52.9%) of them having normal weight. The majority of respondents were females 153 (59.1%), married 161 (62.2%) and 181 (69.9%) were working in theatre‐related departments. The majority of the respondents were nurses 187 (72.2%), staff nurses 105 (40.5%) and had worked for < 5 years 139 (53.7%). In this study, the bivariate binary logistic regression analysis showed that, among all the sociodemographic factors, work experience was significantly associated with the prevalence of WMSDs. Even after controlling for other variables, nurses with < 5 years of work experience remained significantly more likely to have the WMSDs compared to those with more than 10 years of experience (AOR = 3.73, 95% CI [1.03, 13.42], p = 0.043) (Table 5).

Discussion

This is the first cross‐sectional study to assess the prevalence of WMSDs among nurses and midwives in Northern Ghana. As we expected, the 12‐month prevalence of WMSDs was high at 75.3%. This rate could be influenced by the workplace where the respondents are employed. Being a tertiary hospital, it deals with a wide range of often complex cases requiring frequent patient transfers and complicated medical procedures, making the work that nurses and midwives do physically demanding. This aligns with the findings of previous research conducted by Buh et al. (2021), Tinubu et al. (2010) and Sharma et al. (2022), all of which indicated that the prevalence rates of WMSDs among the nursing population were 77.9%, 78.0% and 80.0% respectively. However, the prevalence observed in our study is higher than that reported by (Kaydani, Zarea, and Soltanzadeh 2022) (58.2%) and (Thinkhamrop and Laohasiriwong 2015) (47.8%). The study suggests that healthcare institutions implement wellness programs, incorporating physical exercise, stress management and mental health support, to address the vulnerability experienced in early‐career healthcare professionals. Additionally, the implementation of training programs focusing on proper body mechanics, ergonomic principles and safe patient‐handling techniques could further help mitigate susceptibility during this career stage.

The research findings indicated that a significant proportion (70.7%) of respondents specifically mentioned that their lower back area (lumbar/sacral region) was predominantly affected, in comparison to other body regions. This observation agrees with the findings of investigations conducted by Bediako et al. (2021) and Chiwaridzo et al. (2018), both of which highlighted that the most commonly experienced WMSDs with respect to body regions was the lower back (73.3% and 67.9% respectively). In contrast, Jin et al. (2011) and Lin et al. (2020) reported that their studies identified shoulders (64.4%) as the most frequently affected body area respectively. A separate survey carried out by Azma et al. (2016) indicated that the neck (48.9%) was the prevailing region of the body affected by WMSDs. This diversity in findings highlights the multifaceted nature of musculoskeletal disorders and the need to consider a variety of factors such as work tasks, ergonomic conditions when interpreting and addressing their prevalence.

In the present study, a significant proportion of respondents (61.0%) indicated experiencing WMSDs during the first 5 years of clinical practice. Similarly, other studies by Akodu and Ashalejo (2019) and Tinubu et al. (2010) reported that a greater number of respondents experienced their first episode of a WMSDs within the first 5 years of clinical practice. Of the respondents who reported WMSDs, a noteworthy 63.6% sought treatment from a health professional and agreed with the findings of study conducted by Tinubu et al. (2010). This alignment across studies underscores the vulnerability of healthcare professionals in the nascent stages of their careers to the development of musculoskeletal issues, potentially due to the learning curve associated with adapting to demanding work tasks and postures.

This study revealed that working in the same positions for long periods (such as standing, bend over, sitting, kneeling) 176 (68.0%) is a job risk factor contributing to the development of WMSDs. This finding is consistent with the research conducted by Akodu and Ashalejo (2019), Boakye et al. (2018) and Anap, Iyer, and Rao ( 2013). Additionally, other job risk factors associated with the development of WMSDs were treating an excessive number of patients in 1 day (54.8%) and showed agreement with Boakye et al. (2018); continuing to work while injured or hurt (53.7%) and was consistent with the study by Tinubu et al. (2010); bending or twisting the back in an awkward way (54.1%) and was in accordance with the studies by Clari et al. (2021) and Sharma et al. (2022); not enough rest breaks or pauses during the workday (50.2%) and was in agreement with the study by Chiwaridzo et al. (2018). This study suggests that recognising and mitigating these identified risk factors, workplaces can enhance the health and productivity of nurses and midwives, fostering a more supportive and sustainable work environment.

In addition to the job risk factors associated with WMSDs, respondents adopted a variety of strategies to mitigate the risk of developing this condition. Within the context of this study, a higher percentage of respondents (42.5%) reported getting someone else to help handle a heavy patient. This corroborates with other studies by Boakye et al. (2018) and Anap, Iyer, and Rao ( 2013) where getting help from colleagues in handling heavy patients was the most used coping strategy by the respondents. Furthermore, a greater number of respondents employed additional coping strategies. These included modifying patient's position or nurse position (37.4%). This aligns with the conclusion drawn from the study conducted by Akodu and Ashalejo (2019), where modifying patient or nursing positions was identified as a coping strategy by respondents. Another coping strategy employed by a significant number of respondents, 29.7% to be precise, modified nursing procedure in order to avoid stressing an injury. This practice concurs with the findings of Boakye et al. (2018). In essence, beyond the identifiable factors contributing to the occurrence of WMSDs within their profession, respondents demonstrated resourcefulness by employing diverse coping strategies to mitigate the risks.

This result of the study showed that work experience was significantly associated with the prevalence of WMSD (p = 0.011). This finding is in accordance with earlier studies such as Nasiri‐Ziba, Nosrati, and Hanani (2017), Thinkhamrop and Laohasiriwong (2015) and Chiwaridzo et al. (2018), all of which established a significant relationship between working experience and occurrence of WMSDs. This collective body of research aligns to emphasise the pivotal role that accumulated work experience plays in influencing the occurrence and development of WMSDs. As professionals navigate their roles over time, the intricate interplay between repetitive tasks, postural demands and ergonomic considerations becomes increasingly pronounced, contributing to the heightened risk of musculoskeletal issues.

Study Limitations

Firstly, the study's cross‐sectional design limits its ability to establish causality, only allowing for the identification of associations between variables. An important limitation of this study is the utilisation of self‐administered questionnaires to assess the prevalence of WMSDs among the respondents. While self‐reporting is inherently convenient, straightforward and time‐efficient, it carries the potential for bias. Also, this study included only one hospital, which may not be representative even though it is the only tertiary healthcare institution and source of referral for the five regions in Northern Ghana. Additionally, there is a possibility of non‐response bias, potentially limiting the generalisability to other nurses and midwives. Sixteen percent of respondents declined to participate in the study and they may possess unique experiences, viewpoints or traits not accounted for in the analysis. Finally, there might also be unmeasured potential confounding factors influencing the prevalence of WMSDs, such as specific working conditions, personal health status and ergonomic factors that were not accounted for in the study.

Implication for Nursing Practice

The high prevalence of WMSDs among nurses and midwives in Northern Ghana necessitates several key implications to enhance nursing practice. A critical step is the implementation of comprehensive and regular ergonomic training. Educating healthcare professionals on proper body mechanics, safe patient handling techniques and the use of assistive devices is essential to minimising physical strain and reducing the risk of WMSDs. Such training can greatly contribute to safer work practices and better physical health for nurses and midwives.

Again, it is critical to improve the work atmosphere in healthcare facilities. Prioritising ergonomic enhancements, such as adjustable beds, lift aids and well‐designed workspaces, can help nursing staff cope with the physical demands of their jobs. Furthermore, the development and implementation of effective injury prevention initiatives is critical. These programs should include clear standards for safe patient handling, regular safety audits and rapid treatment of any musculoskeletal issues, resulting in a safer and more supportive work environment.

Advocating for policies that enforce ergonomic standards and involving nurses and midwives in policy development can ensure that their needs are adequately addressed. Moreover, fostering a culture that supports the reporting of musculoskeletal issues without fear of reprisal is essential for early intervention and effective management.

Conclusion

The study concludes that there was a high prevalence of WMSDs among nurses and midwives in the last 12 months, primarily affecting their lower back (lumbar/sacral). The study revealed that most of these professionals experienced their first WMSD within their first 5 years of clinical practice. Working in the same positions for long periods (such as standing, bending over, sitting, kneeling) was identified as a major occupational risk factor contributing to these disorders. Despite this risk, a significant number of nurses and midwives consistently sought assistance when handling heavy patients. The study also showed that nurses and midwives with fewer than 5 years of experience were markedly more susceptible to reporting WMSDs compared to those with over 10 years of experience. The current study's results highlight the importance of hospitals providing comprehensive ergonomic training to healthcare professionals, emphasising proper body mechanics, posture and techniques for patient handling. This knowledge will empower nurses and midwives to perform their duties with reduced physical strain and risk of injury.
