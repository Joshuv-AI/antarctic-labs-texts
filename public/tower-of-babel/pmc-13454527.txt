Development and Validation of an LC-MS/MS Method for Simultaneous Determination of 12 Antibiotics in Human Serum
Authors: Shuai Ruan, Jiayuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo develop and validate a high-throughput LC-MS/MS method for simultaneous quantification of 12 clinically important antibiotics, addressing current limitations in multi-class therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM), particularly for β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations.MethodsA validated LC-MS/MS method was established using isotope-labeled internal standards. A total of 200 clinical serum samples were analyzed. Method performance was evaluated in accordance with CLSI C62 guidelines, including linearity, lower limits of detection (LLOD), lower limits of quantification (LLOQ), selectivity, accuracy, precision, matrix effects, carryover, interference, dilution effect and stability.ResultsAll analytes showed excellent linearity (R2 ≥ 0.9938), with acceptable accuracy (87.01%–112.73%) and precision (<10% CV). Significant matrix-related interference was observed for imipenem, tigecycline, and vancomycin under hemolytic conditions, highlighting analyte-specific limitations. Clinical TDM results revealed that only 51.5% of patients achieved target therapeutic concentrations, while 48.5% were outside the recommended range.ConclusionThis method provides a robust analytical platform for multi-antibiotic TDM. Importantly, clinical data demonstrate substantial variability in empirical dosing, supporting the need for concentration-guided antimicrobial therapy. However, further external validation and outcome-based studies are required before routine clinical implementation.

Introduction

Antimicrobial agents are indispensable for the treatment of bacterial infections; however, their pharmacokinetics vary considerably among hospitalized and critically ill patients.1 Standard dosing may therefore result in subtherapeutic exposure or drug accumulation, leading to treatment failure, toxicity, and the emergence of antimicrobial resistance.2 Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) has consequently become an important strategy for optimizing individualized antimicrobial therapy. In parallel, β-lactamase inhibitors have gained increasing clinical importance because they restore the activity of β-lactam antibiotics against resistant Gram-negative pathogens and constitute essential components of modern combination regimens.3,4

Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) is the preferred analytical platform for antimicrobial TDM owing to its high sensitivity, specificity, and capability for simultaneous multi-analyte quantification. Although several multiplex LC–MS/MS methods have been reported, most focus on conventional antibiotics and do not adequately support current combination therapies involving β-lactamase inhibitors. For example, Feliu et al developed a method covering 15 antimicrobial agents without including β-lactamase inhibitors,5 whereas Lu et al expanded the analytical panel to 18 agents but likewise excluded these compounds.6 Cheng et al incorporated avibactam into a sensitive LC–MS/MS assay; however, the method was limited to the ceftazidime–avibactam combination.7 Similarly, a recently published LC–MS/MS method (2024) remained focused on conventional antibiotics without integrating clinically important enzyme inhibitors. Consequently, simultaneous monitoring of widely used combination regimens, such as ceftazidime–avibactam and cefoperazone–sulbactam, remains insufficiently addressed in routine TDM.

To address this unmet need, we developed and validated a rapid LC–MS/MS method for the simultaneous quantification of twelve antimicrobial agents in human serum, encompassing β-lactam antibiotics, carbapenems, β-lactamase inhibitors, glycopeptides, tetracyclines, oxazolidinones, fluoroquinolones, and sulfonamides. A key feature of the assay is the simultaneous determination of avibactam and sulbactam together with their companion β-lactam antibiotics. The method was validated according to CLSI C62 recommendations and subsequently applied to routine clinical TDM samples. Furthermore, exploratory analyses of pharmacological subgroups were performed to demonstrate its real-world clinical applicability. By integrating multiple antimicrobial classes with key β-lactamase inhibitors into a single analytical workflow, the proposed method provides a practical platform for individualized antimicrobial therapy and precision dosing in contemporary clinical practice.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

Reference standards of avibactam, ceftazidime, sulbactam, imipenem, tigecycline, vancomycin, meropenem, linezolid, cefoperazone, sulfamethoxazole, piperacillin, and moxifloxacin, together with their corresponding stable isotope-labeled internal standards, were obtained from certified commercial suppliers.

LC-MS-grade methanol, acetonitrile, and formic acid were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 3-(N-morpholino)propanesulfonic acid (MOPS), bovine hemoglobin, triglycerides, and other analytical-grade reagents were obtained from commercial sources and used without further purification.

Preparation of Calibration Standards and Quality Controls

The 12 antibiotic reference standards were accurately weighed. Stock solutions of imipenem, meropenem, and ceftazidime were prepared at a concentration of 10 g/L using pure water. Stock solutions of avibactam, sulbactam, cefoperazone, and piperacillin were prepared at 10 g/L using a mixture of DMSO and acetonitrile (1:1, v/v). Stock solutions of tigecycline, vancomycin, moxifloxacin, linezolid were prepared at 10 g/L using a mixture of DMSO and methanol (1:1, v/v). Stock solution of sulfamethoxazole was prepared at 50 g/L using a mixture of DMSO and methanol (1:1, v/v). The isotopic internal standard was dissolved in methanol to prepare a 1 g/L stock solution.

The individual stock solutions of the 12 antibiotics were mixed in appropriate proportions and subsequently diluted with 50% methanol to create working solutions for a 7-point calibration curve. These working solutions were stored at −20°C. The calibration working solutions were diluted 10‑fold to obtain calibration standards. Two levels of quality control (QC) samples were prepared using the same procedure. The internal standard stock solutions of antimicrobial agents were mixed proportionally and diluted with methanol–acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) to prepare an internal standard working solution with a concentration of 0.2 mg/L for each internal standard.

Sample Preparation

Serum samples, calibration standards, QC samples, and internal standard (IS) working solutions were thawed and equilibrated to room temperature before use. Aliquots of 40 μL of patient serum, calibration standards, or QC samples were transferred into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes, respectively. Then 160 μL of IS working solution was added. After mixing, the mixture was centrifuged at 14,000×g for 5 min. An aliquot of 100 μL supernatant was transferred into a 96-well plate, followed by the addition of 200 μL purified water. After vortex mixing for 5 min, the sample was injected into the LC-MS/MS system.

LC-MS/MS Analysis

The LC-MS/MS analysis was performed using an AB SCIEX ExionLC™ AC System (AB Sciex, USA) coupled with an AB Sciex Triple Quad™ 4500MD mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, USA).

Chromatographic separation was performed on a Discovery HS C18 column (5 cm × 4.6 mm, 3 μm; Merck, Germany) maintained at 30°C. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (Mobile Phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (Mobile Phase B). A constant flow rate of 0.5 mL/min was used with a gradient elution program as detailed in Table 1. The injection volume was 5 μL, and the autosampler temperature was set at 4°C. Method Validation

The validation criteria for this method were established based on CLSI C628 and Consensus of method development and validation of liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry in clinical laboratories.9 The method validation included linearity, lower limits of detection (LLOD), lower limits of quantification (LLOQ), selectivity, accuracy, precision, matrix effects, carryover, interference, dilution effect and stability.

Calibration curves were established by plotting the analyte-to-internal standard peak area ratio against the nominal concentration using weighted linear regression. Linearity was evaluated across the calibration range for each analyte. Sensitivity was assessed by determining the LLOD and LLOQ. The LLOD was defined as the concentration corresponding to a signal-to-noise ratio of 3, while the LLOQ was defined as the lowest concentration that met the predefined accuracy and precision requirements.

Selectivity was evaluated using serum samples obtained from five healthy individuals. Blank serum samples were analyzed to assess endogenous interference at the retention times of each analyte and its corresponding internal standard.

Accuracy was evaluated using serum samples spiked at low, medium, and high concentration levels and expressed as recovery. Precision was assessed by analyzing QC samples at three concentration levels within a single day (intra-day) and across multiple analytical batches (inter-day).

Matrix effects were evaluated using normal serum and alternative matrices by calculating the internal standard-normalized matrix factor (MF). Matrix effects were considered acceptable when the normalized MF and corresponding variability remained within the predefined limits.

Stability was evaluated using low- and high-concentration serum samples under conditions representative of routine clinical practice, including room temperature (12 h), refrigerated storage (2–8°C, 3 d), three freeze–thaw cycles, and processed sample storage in the autosampler (48 h). Stability was assessed by comparing measured concentrations with freshly prepared samples.

Carryover was evaluated by injecting a blank serum sample immediately after the highest calibration standard to assess residual analyte and internal standard signals.

Dilution integrity was evaluated using serum samples with concentrations above the upper limit of quantification (ULOQ). Samples were diluted 2-, 5-, 10-, 20-, and 50-fold with blank pooled human serum and analyzed following the standard sample preparation procedure. The diluted samples were evaluated to verify accurate quantification after dilution.

Interference studies were performed using hemolyzed and lipemic serum matrices prepared from pooled human serum supplemented with bovine hemoglobin or triglyceride emulsion to simulate clinically relevant interference conditions. Low- and high-concentration QC samples were prepared in both normal and interference matrices and analyzed in six replicates following the standard sample preparation procedure. Interference was evaluated by comparing the measured concentrations in hemolyzed or lipemic samples with those obtained from the corresponding normal serum matrix.

Clinical Application

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) / Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval No. 2026‑0164), and written informed consent was not required. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Residual serum samples from hospitalized patients receiving antimicrobial therapy were collected between January 2026 and March 2026.

The inclusion criteria for serum samples subjected to drug concentration analysis were as follows: (1) administration of standard drug doses; (2) continuous medication for ≥7 days; and (3) age ≥18 years. The exclusion criteria were: (1) confirmed drug resistance; (2) women who were preparing for pregnancy or were pregnant; and (3) incomplete clinical information.

For sulfamethoxazole, peak concentration samples were collected 2–3 h after administration.10,11 For the other 11 antibiotics, trough concentrations were sampled 0.5 h before the next dose administration. Venous blood was collected using vacuum tubes containing inert separator gel and coagulation promoter. Serum was separated by centrifugation. For imipenem and meropenem patient serum samples, an equal volume of MOPS buffer12 was added at a 1:1 ratio after serum separation. High-concentration samples were diluted with purified water into the linear range prior to sample preparation.

A total of 200 clinical serum samples were included for method application and therapeutic drug monitoring assessment.

Results

Method Development

A mixture of the 12 target antibiotics was analyzed by LC-MS/MS in MRM mode. The developed method achieved baseline separation of all 12 antibiotics within 8.5 minutes, as shown in Figure 1. Despite the substantial physicochemical diversity of the included antimicrobial classes, satisfactory peak symmetry and chromatographic resolution were obtained for all analytes. These results demonstrate the capability of the developed method to simultaneously quantify structurally diverse antimicrobial agents within a single analytical platform, supporting its suitability for routine therapeutic drug monitoring applications. Linearity, LLOD and LLOQ

Excellent linearity was observed for all twelve analytes across their respective calibration ranges, with correlation coefficients (R2) ranging from 0.9938 to 0.9999 (Table 3). The LLOD and LLOQ were sufficiently low to cover clinically relevant concentration ranges encountered during antimicrobial therapy. These findings indicate that the assay provides reliable quantification across a broad concentration spectrum, allowing accurate assessment of both subtherapeutic and potentially toxic drug exposures. Selectivity

Selectivity was evaluated using serum samples obtained from five independent individuals. No endogenous interference was observed at the retention times of any analyte or internal standard.

The absence of matrix-derived interference confirms the high analytical specificity of the method and supports its application in complex clinical serum samples.

Dilution Effects

These samples were diluted 2-, 5-, 10-, 20-, and 50-fold with blank serum samples (n=6). Precision (CV, %) was found between 0.64 and 9.10% while accuracy results were ranging from 89.68%–113.20% for all the antimicrobial agents. These findings demonstrate that the developed LC-MS/MS assay maintains acceptable analytical performance following substantial sample dilution. Consequently, samples with concentrations exceeding the calibration range can be accurately quantified after appropriate dilution, thereby extending the reportable range of the method and reducing the need for repeat sample collection.

Accuracy and Precision

Accuracy was assessed by performing five replicate analyses at three different concentrations for each antibiotic. The recovery rates for the 12 antibiotics ranged from 87.01% to 112.73%, meeting the required performance criteria.

The precision results for the 12 antibiotics are shown in Table 4. Both intra- and inter-day precision (expressed as %RSD) were less than 10% at all three concentration levels. The observed precision and accuracy results indicate that the assay provides reproducible and reliable quantitative measurements suitable for clinical therapeutic drug monitoring. Stability

The stability of the compounds was evaluated under various conditions: processed samples in the autosampler (4°C) for 48 hours; clinical samples at room temperature for 12 hours, at 2–8°C for 3 days, and after three freeze-thaw cycles. The stability data, presented in Table 5, showed that the deviations in serum concentrations for the 12 antibiotics remained within ±15%, confirming adequate stability under the tested conditions. These findings demonstrate adequate analyte stability throughout sample handling, storage, and analytical processing, thereby supporting the practicality of the method for routine laboratory implementation. Matrix Effect

The normalized matrix factor for the 12 compounds ranged from 87.63% to 112.69%, with corresponding CV values between 2.92% and 9.53%. These results indicate that the matrix effect was negligible and acceptable. These results indicate minimal matrix-related ion suppression or enhancement following internal standard normalization. The low variability observed among different serum matrices further demonstrates the robustness of the assay when applied to heterogeneous clinical specimens.

Interference Test

The interference study was performed using lipemic and hemolyzed samples at both low and high quality control levels. The accuracy bias for ten of the antimicrobial agents remained within ±15%, indicating that severe lipemia and hemolysis had minimal impact on quantitative performance. However, for imipenem, tigecycline, and vancomycin in hemolyzed samples, the biases were −19.38%, −71.93%, and −18.30%, respectively. Vancomycin also showed a bias of 17.91% in severely lipemic samples. Although the observed deviations for imipenem and vancomycin slightly exceeded the predefined ±15% acceptance criterion, they remained below ±20%, whereas tigecycline exhibited a substantially larger negative bias. Overall, these findings indicate that the developed assay is robust against common endogenous interferences for most analytes. However, quantitative results for imipenem, tigecycline, and vancomycin should be interpreted with caution when severe hemolysis is present, and additional evaluation or repeat sampling may be warranted in clinical practice. These results highlight an important methodological consideration for multi-class LC-MS/MS assays: matrix effects may not be uniform across chemically diverse antibiotics and should be evaluated on an analyte-specific basis.

Carryover

The ratios of peak area at the retention time of compounds in blank matrix to that in 20% LLOQ samples were 0.01–0.39, and the ratios of IS peak area to that in 5% LLOQ samples were 0.01–0.47. All values remained well below the predefined acceptance criteria, indicating negligible carryover. These findings confirm that the method is suitable for continuous analysis of clinical samples with widely varying antimicrobial concentrations without introducing significant analytical contamination.

Discussion

This study developed and validated a high-throughput LC-MS/MS method for simultaneously quantifying 12 antibiotics from 8 classes in human serum. The method addresses a critical clinical need by enabling efficient TDM for patients on multi-drug regimens for severe infections, overcoming the limitations of single- or class-specific assays.

Methodological success was achieved through systematic optimization, including chromatographic/mass spectrometric conditions and time-segmented multiple reaction monitoring, achieving baseline separation of all analytes in 8.5 minutes. For unstable β-lactams (eg, imipenem, meropenem), immediate stabilization with MOPS was implemented.12,16 Full method validation confirmed excellent sensitivity, accuracy, precision, and robustness against matrix effects. Compared with many previously reported LC–MS/MS methods,17–21 which mainly emphasized routine analytical validation, the present study additionally included a dedicated interference assessment using hemolyzed and lipemic specimens. Such evaluation is particularly relevant for antimicrobial TDM because critically ill patients frequently present with compromised sample quality. The interference study therefore provides additional evidence supporting the robustness of the proposed method under real-world laboratory conditions while also identifying analyte-specific limitations that should be considered during clinical interpretation.

The principal innovation of this study lies not simply in increasing the number of analytes, but in integrating clinically important β-lactamase inhibitors into a comprehensive routine TDM workflow. As the clinical use of β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations continues to expand, simultaneous quantification of both antibacterial agents and enzyme inhibitors may offer additional value for individualized antimicrobial therapy and future PK/PD-guided precision dosing.

Application of the method to routine clinical samples revealed substantial variability in antimicrobial exposure among different pharmacological classes. The pseudo-subgroup analysis demonstrated that therapeutic target attainment differed considerably across antimicrobial categories, suggesting that standard dosing may not achieve adequate exposure for all patients. In particular, β-lactamase inhibitors exhibited greater variability than conventional β-lactam antibiotics, with nearly equal proportions of subtherapeutic and supratherapeutic concentrations. These findings indicate that exposure of enzyme inhibitors cannot be reliably inferred from the concentrations of their companion β-lactam antibiotics alone.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study, and the clinical validation cohort was relatively limited, particularly for individual antimicrobial agents and β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations. Larger multicenter studies are required to further evaluate the generalizability of the proposed method.3 Second, the present clinical application focused primarily on measured drug concentrations, whereas detailed patient characteristics, including renal function, intensive care unit (ICU) status, severity of illness, and clinical outcomes, were not incorporated into the analysis. Therefore, exposure–response relationships and PK/PD target attainment could not be comprehensively assessed.1,2 Third, although the analytical performance satisfied CLSI C62 recommendations, noticeable interference was observed for imipenem, tigecycline, and vancomycin in severely hemolyzed samples, it should be noted that this study did not perform mechanistic experiments to fully characterize the origin of matrix interference, which represents a limitation of the current work. Finally, because this study was designed as an analytical method validation, the proposed pharmacological subgroup should be regarded as exploratory. Future prospective studies integrating larger patient cohorts, clinical outcome data, and population pharmacokinetic or Bayesian dose optimization strategies are warranted to further establish the clinical utility of this assay for precision antimicrobial therapy.22

Conclusion

In conclusion, a rapid and robust LC–MS/MS method was successfully established and validated for the simultaneous quantification of twelve antimicrobial agents, including the clinically important β-lactamase inhibitors avibactam and sulbactam. Compared with previously reported methods, the present assay extends routine therapeutic drug monitoring from conventional antibiotics to contemporary β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combination therapy. Although satisfactory analytical performance was achieved, matrix interference was observed for several analytes under severely hemolyzed conditions, and the method has not yet been externally validated or compared with other established analytical platforms. Therefore, further multicenter studies incorporating external validation and outcome-based clinical evaluation are warranted to confirm its broader clinical utility. Nevertheless, the proposed method provides a practical analytical platform for individualized antimicrobial therapy and precision dosing.
