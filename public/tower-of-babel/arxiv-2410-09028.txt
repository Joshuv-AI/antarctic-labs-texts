Anomalously extended Floquet prethermal lifetimes and applications to long-time quantum sensing
Kieren A Harkins, Cooper Selco, Christian Bengs, David Marchiori, Leo Joon Il Moon, Zhuo-Rui Zhang, Aristotle Yang, Angad Singh, Emanuel Druga, Yi-Qiao Song, Ashok Ajoy

Abstract
Floquet prethermalization is observed in periodically driven quantum many-body systems where the system avoids heating and maintains a stable, non-equilibrium state, for extended periods. Here we introduce a novel quantum control method using off-resonance and short-angle excitation to significantly extend Floquet prethermal lifetimes. This is demonstrated on randomly positioned, dipolar-coupled, C 13 {}^{13}\textrm{C} nuclear spins in diamond, but the methodology is broadly applicable. We achieve a lifetime T 2 ′ ≈ 800 T_{2}^{\prime}{\approx}800 s at 100 K while tracking the transition to the prethermal state quasi-continuously. This corresponds to a > {>} 533,000-fold extension over the bare spin lifetime without prethermalization, and constitutes a new record both in terms of absolute lifetime as well as the total number of Floquet pulses applied (here exceeding 7 million). Using Laplace inversion, we develop a new form of noise spectroscopy that provides insights into the origin of the lifetime extension. Finally, we demonstrate applications of these extended lifetimes in long-time, reinitialization-free quantum sensing of time-varying magnetic fields continuously for ∼ {\sim} 10 minutes at room temperature. Our work facilitates new opportunities for stabilizing driven quantum systems through Floquet control, and opens novel applications for continuously interrogated, long-time responsive quantum sensors.

I
Introduction

Floquet prethermalization – the arrest of thermalization in periodically driven many-body quantum systems – has garnered significant recent attention
Abanin et al. [2015]
,
Kuwahara et al. [2016]
,
Weidinger and Knap [2017]
,
Else et al. [2017]
. Periodic kicks induce the system to enter a long-lived metastable state rather than heating rapidly, enabling applications that exploit many-body couplings before the system ultimately thermalizes to infinite temperature
Singh et al. [2019]
,
Beatrez et al. [2021]
,
Peng et al. [2021]
,
Rubio-Abadal et al. [2020]
. Among these are the formation of non-equilibrium states like prethermal time crystals
Choi et al. [2017]
,
Zhang et al. [2017]
,
Rovny et al. [2018]
,
Kyprianidis et al. [2021]
,
Beatrez et al. [2023a]
,
Moon et al. [2024a]
, stabilized topological phases
Potirniche et al. [2017]
,
Ye et al. [2021]
,
Zhang et al. [2022]
, and the creation of stabilized nanoscale spin textures
Harkins et al. [2023]
. It has also been exploited for quantum sensing
Sahin et al. [2022a]
, wherein the prethermal state is rendered responsive to external fields; the long-lifetime boosting sensitivity and resolution.

A key focus for these applications, especially in sensing, is on developing strategies to extend prethermal lifetimes via Floquet control
Dong et al. [2008]
,
Fleckenstein and Bukov [2021]
,
Zhao et al. [2022]
, and understanding the origins of heating processes during different stages of thermalization
Ho et al. [2023]
,
Weidinger and Knap [2017]
,
Mallayya et al. [2019]
,
Beatrez et al. [2021]
,
Birnkammer et al. [2022]
.

Consider nuclear spins in a solid coupled via magnetic dipolar interactions. When prepared in a superposition state,
\rho_{0}{\propto}I_{x}
, where
I
is the net spin-1/2 Pauli operator, the state rapidly decays to infinite temperature
\rho_{\infty}{\sim}\mathds{1}
with a characteristic time-scale
T_{2}^{*}
, driven by the interactions. However, suitable Floquet control can reengineer the dipolar Hamiltonian to
\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{eff}}
, where
[\mathcal{H}_{\textrm{eff}},I_{x}]{=}0
, inducing prethermalization, and significantly extending the state’s survivial. For
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclei in diamond, we demonstrated a lifetime extension from
T_{2}^{*}{\approx}1.5
ms to
T_{2}^{\prime}{=}90
s
Beatrez et al. [2021]
. Lifetimes are typically estimated from a
1/e
-intercept of the decay profiles, but this approach, while useful, is simplistic and incomplete.

Several key questions arise: (i) can these lifetimes be further extended, (ii) can broader insights into the thermalization process be gained by examining the heating profiles to infinite temperature, and (iii) can this be leveraged for sensing? In this study, using optically hyperpolarized
13
C nuclei and new Floquet control strategies, we address these aspects.

Notably, we observe that prethermal lifetimes can be significantly increased through tailored off-resonant excitation combined with short-angle pulses (nominally
\vartheta{<}10^{\circ}
), extending the
1/e
-lifetime to
T_{2}^{\prime}{>}800
s
– a
>
533,000-fold increase over
T_{2}^{*}
under identical conditions. This corresponds to over 7M control pulses applied to the spins, setting a new record for both the duration of prethermalization and the number of Floquet pulses sustained (see detailed table of comparison in Figure
S1
). Remarkably, however, the extension itself is achieved with only a small number of pulses per
T_{2}^{*}
period, leading us to term this an
anomalously
extended prethermal lifetime.

By leveraging the high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and rapid data sampling (millions of data points) enabled by hyperpolarization and instrumentation advances, we develop a powerful approach based on Laplace inversion (LI)
Song et al. [2002]
,
Venkataramanan et al. [2002]
to quantify individual decay channels in the thermalization process. This provides more detailed insights than simple
1/e
intercepts and enables a deeper understanding of lifetime extensions via Floquet control.

Building on this, we demonstrate practical applications in long-time, continuously interrogated AC magnetometry with hyperpolarized
13
C nuclei
Sahin et al. [2022a]
. Magnetic fields are measured continuously for
{\approx}
10min post a single initialization step, marking the longest such demonstration of quantum sensing to our knowledge.

II.4
Application: Long-time continuously interrogated AC magnetometry

While already fundamentally interesting, the extended prethermal lifetimes can be also exploited for quantum sensing. We consider, in particular, the sensing of time-varying (AC) magnetic fields
B_{\textrm{sens}}(t)=B_{\textrm{AC}}\cos(2\pi f_{\textrm{AC}}t+\phi)
using the hyperpolarized
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclei
Sahin et al. [2022a]
. Figure
4
A shows the protocol, here at room temperature (RT); the
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
spins are exposed to the AC field along
\hat{\textbf{z}}
with the Floquet sequence from Figure
1
C, while being quasi-continuously interrogated. The AC field does not need to be synchronized with the sequence. The sensing principle, previously described
Sahin et al. [2022a]
, involves
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclei prethermalizing to a time-varying Hamiltonian that includes the AC field; the spin micromotion dynamics then reports a direct imprint of
B_{\textrm{sens}}(t)
.
This results in oscillatory deviations
\Delta S
on top of the signal
S
in Figure
1
D and Figure
3
A, with
\Delta S\propto B_{\textrm{AC}}
, and frequency at harmonics of
f_{\textrm{AC}}
(dominantly
f_{\textrm{AC}}
or
2f_{\textrm{AC}}
).

Sensing is effective over a relatively wide bandwidth
f_{\textrm{AC}}\in[
10 Hz,1 MHz
]
; the upper limit determined by the shortest interval between pulses. This sensing bandwidth lies in a blind-spot
Moon et al. [2024b]
between vapor cell magnetometers
Budker and Romalis [2007]
,
IJsselsteijn et al. [2012]
,
Kurian et al. [2023]
and electron-based sensors such as NV centers
Maze et al. []
,
Kuwahata et al. [2020]
,
Sahin et al. [2022a]
, and is therefore in a practical useful range.

Figure
4
B shows the RT sensing result for a single-tone AC field at
f_{\textrm{AC}}{=}50
Hz with
B_{\textrm{AC}}{=}82\mu
T. In contrast to other quantum sensing platforms
Degen et al. [2017]
, only a single-shot of initialization (hyperpolarization) is applied here. Signal deviations
\Delta S
are then measured continuously, without sensor reinitialization, for
t{\sim}10
min. For clarity, data in Figure
4
B shows
\Delta t{=}100
ms windows at intervals of 1min, 3min, 5min, and 9.6min respectively. Two cases are compared: Figure
4
B(i) shows on-resonant pulses with flip-angle
\vartheta{=}90^{\circ}
(green), and Figure
4
B(ii) shows
\Delta\omega{=}5
kHz off-resonant pulses with nominal
\vartheta{=}10^{\circ}
(yellow). As is evident,
\Delta S
reflects a direct imprint of
B_{\textrm{AC}}
; the relative weights of the
f_{\textrm{AC}}
and 2
f_{\textrm{AC}}
components depends on the offset. In Figure
4
B, data is shown by the light lines, while the darker lines are a fit. The numbers marked in grey indicate the measured SNR of the AC field signature over the noise floor in a
1
s interval for each window.

Physically, for sensing, one is interested in the excursions of the spin vector away from the originally defined prethermal axis due to the AC field; measuring its projection on the
\hat{\textbf{x}}{\text{-}}\hat{\textbf{y}}
plane. While the on-resonance signal
S
might have a large absolute (DC) magnitude (not shown in Figure

\Delta S
is, which is in fact larger in the off-resonance case. In addition, the on-resonance
\Delta S
signal decays
significantly faster, as is evident moving rightwards in Figure
4
B. By
t{=}580

B(ii)) shows an SNR that is almost an order of magnitude larger than the on-resonance case (Figure
4
B(i)).

For a more comprehensive view into the sensing gains using small-angle off-resonance pulses, Figure

C-D contrasts
\vartheta{=}90^{\circ}
(green) and
\vartheta{=}15^{\circ}
(orange), showing the

Finally, we comment that while Figure

III
Discussion

This work demonstrates long-time, reinitialization-free quantum sensing by exploiting extended prethermal lifetimes with small-angle off-resonance pulses. The absolute signal level
S
of the prethermal state, so long as it is above the noise floor, does not affect the sensing, allowing full exploitation of the long
T_{2}’
times (Figure
4
C-D). The achieved
T_{2}’{\approx}800
s values themselves, asymptotically approaching
T_{1}
, are significant, and constitute a record value (see Figure
S1
). Moreover, experiments in Figure
4
show sensing for
{\sim}10
min with no re-initialization, also setting a record in both duration and the number of pulses (
{\sim}
7M) employed. Even in its current form, this opens applications in sensitive detection in a blind-spot region of ULF and VLF fields
Moon et al. [2024b]
and for underwater magnetometry
Page et al. [2021]
.

We envision several promising future directions. Further
T_{2}’
extension is expected by lowering the temperature to 4K, where at
B_{0}{=}10
T, NV and P1 center electrons are polarized, suppressing electron-mediated relaxation to the
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclei
Takahashi et al. [2008]
,
Jarmola et al. [2012]
. The protocols developed here are more broadly applicable than just
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
spins in diamond. We envision similar approaches being applied to ensembles of electronic spins, e.g. NV or P1 centers
Williams and Ramanathan [2023]
. Particularly compelling may be applications to polarized
1
H nuclear spins in organic solids, especially pentacene-doped naphthalene, where long
T_{1}
times, due to diamagnetic singlet ground states, can exceed 100hr
Eichhorn et al. [2014]
,
Quan et al. [2019]
,
Singh et al. []
. We anticipate long-time, reinitialization-free quantum sensing for several tens of hours in this system. This portends new applications for nuclear-spin clocks
Hodges et al. [2013]
, gyroscopes
Ajoy and Cappellaro [2012]
,
Ledbetter et al. [2012]
,
Jaskula et al. [2019]
,
Jarmola et al. [2021]
, relayed NMR sensors for proximal analytes
Meriles [2005]
,
Sidles [2009]
, and spin-based magnetometers akin to levitating compass needles
Jackson Kimball et al. [2016]
, which can be continuously responsive to external magnetic fields, while capable of being tracked in three-dimensions on the Bloch sphere
Sahin et al. [2022b]
.

The Laplace inversion approach introduced here offers a novel means of discerning individual mechanisms for nuclear spin relaxation. We observe that the Laplace peaks are organized into discrete peaks (Figure
1
E, Figure
2
A) likely reflecting different mechanisms of nuclear decay, such as dipolar coupling, electron-nuclear interactions, and phonon processes
Jarmola et al. []
,
Beatrez et al. []
. The transition to mono-exponential behavior via off-resonant excitation (Figure
3
C) suggests the ability to tailor the spectral density profiles experienced by the nuclei
Ajoy et al. []
. This anticipates future work controlling individual decay channels via quantum control or changes in temperature or magnetic field
Takahashi et al. [2008]
.

Materials and Methods

Materials –
The sample used in this work is a single-crystal diamond measuring 3
{\times}
3
{\times}
0.3 mm, containing a natural abundance of
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
nuclei and NV centers at 1 ppm concentration. This same sample has been characterized in prior studies on Floquet prethermalization
Beatrez et al. [2021]
, allowing for direct comparisons to the lifetime extensions observed. The sample is oriented parallel to the
B_{\text{pol}}\approx 27
mT magnetic field, ensuring simultaneous hyperpolarization of the four NV center axes.

Experimental Setup –
Instrumentation for hyperpolarization and
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
readout follows previous works, and we refer the reader to those studies for detailed descriptions
Ajoy et al. [2018]
. Data here is taken with two apparatus: one at 100K and 9.4T (Fig.
1
-Fig.
3
) and another at room temperature and 7T (Fig.
4
). Polarization in both cases is carried out at low fields,
B_{\text{pol}}\approx 30
mT, driven by optically excited NV centers and chirped microwave (MW) excitation. The polarization mechanism involves successive Landau-Zeener anti-crossings in the rotating frame
Ajoy et al. [2018]
,
Ajoy et al. [2021]
,
Pillai et al. [2023]
.

The low-temperature DNP apparatus uses a shuttled cryostat design described in Ref.
Harkins et al. [2023]
. The sample is polarized in a cryostat at the
B_{\text{pol}}
center, located in the fringe field above the
B_{0}{=}9.4
T superconducting magnet, with a laser impinging from below. The cryostat is then shuttled to
B_{0}
in
{\sim}
90s for NMR measurements. This cryostat hosts a dual NMR-hyperpolarization probe with a MW coil for DNP and an NMR saddle coil for inductive readout of the
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
precession signal.

{}^{13}\textrm{C}
spin control and readout –

{}^{13}\textrm{C}
interrogation is performed using a high-speed arbitrary waveform transceiver (Proteus), as described in Ref.
Moon et al. [2024a]
. This device features a high memory capacity (16GB) and a large sampling rate (1GS/s), enabling continuous interrogation of
{}^{13}\textrm{C}
spin precession in windows between pulses. In typical experiments, we apply 4-12M pulses. The readout window is typically
{\approx}16\mu
s, but for long acquisitions (
{>}
300s), we shorten it to
{\approx}4\mu
s to conserve memory.

The entire Larmor precession is sampled in these windows, allowing us to discern both amplitude and phase information of the spins in the rotating frame. This is particularly useful in magnetometry experiments where the signal of interest is imprinted in both amplitude and phase. For AC magnetometry, the spins are exposed to a weak magnetic field applied via a secondary coil, parallel to the microwave excitation loop and positioned within the NMR probe. The field strength is calibrated by observing the induced shift in the
13
C Larmor frequency when a DC field is applied of different strengths.

S4
Validation of the Laplace Inversion Fitting

As a starting point we assume that the experimental signal
s(t)
is well-represented by a sum of exponentials with different weights and decay times:

s(t)=\sum_{j}w_{j}e^{t/T_{2}^{j}}+r(t),

(S 1)

where
r(t)
is the residual error of the model. The signal is then suitable for a LI analysis yielding the various weights
w_{j}
for relaxation times
T_{2}^{j}\in{[}0,{T_{2}^{\rm max}]}
in a suitable interval. In practice the LI is performed numerically by performing a regularised
\ell^{2}
optimization on the following objective
Song et al. [2002]
,
Venkataramanan et al. [2002]

\min_{W}||S-KW||_{2}^{2}+\alpha||W||_{2}^{2},

(S 2)

where
S
and
W
are discretized vectors of the signal and weights, respectively.
K_{ij}=e^{-t_{i}/T_{2}^{j}}
is a matrix representation of the exponential kernel function, and
\alpha
is a Tikhonov regularization parameter.

Typically, numerical LI is limited by experimental noise and low sampling rates. However, in our case, these issues are addressed by our high initial polarization levels and high sampling rates of 1GS/s providing a large SNR.
These factors are unique to our experimental apparatus, enabling us to utilize the full power of the LI algorithm. The resulting fits are robust against noise, and are qualitatively insensitive to changes in the length of measurement. Additionally, the regularization parameter
\alpha
displays a wide window of acceptable
\alpha
values that capture the same qualitative information.

In Fig.
S5
, we demonstrate the robustness of the LI algorithm against noise using the same data as Fig.
2
A from the main text. Fig.
S5
(A)-(C) shows an on-resonance decay (similar to Fig.
1
(D) from the main text) with different amounts of noise added to it. Fig.
S5
(A) shows a single time domain trace of the raw data, while Fig.
S5
(B)-(C) show single time domain traces of the raw data plus a number randomly sampled from a Gaussian distribution of amplitude 0.01 (Fig.
S5
(B)) or 0.03 (Fig.
S5
(C)) added to each point. Fig.
S5
(A)-(C) is meant to give some intuition to the reader as to how much noise was added to each data set. Below each of these decay curves, Fig.
S5
(D)-(F) shows the corresponding LI map similar to Fig.
2
(A) in the main text. As can be seen in each of the three color plots, the LI map looks extremely similar in each one, therefore demonstrating that the LI algorithm is robust against large amounts of noise added to the data.

S4.2
Effect of Changing Fitting Parameter
\alpha

The LI algorithm obtains a fit to the data by minimizing a matrix equation
Song et al. [2002]
,
Venkataramanan et al. [2002]
. In order to prevent over-fitting such as fitting to noise in the data, a regularization parameter

,
Venkataramanan et al. [2002]

S5
Spin relaxation under periodic driving

S5.1
Relaxation model

A qualitative insight into the relaxation behaviour of the system under periodic driving may be gained following the approach by
Rhim et al. [1978]
, but adopted to the case of non-cyclic evolution. For simplicity we assume an idealised Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle H_{0}(t)=\omega_{z}\sum_{i}I_{iz}+\omega_{x}(t)\sum_{i}I_{ix}+\sum_{i<j}(d_{ij}-\kappa_{ij}(t))T^{ij}_{20},

(S 3)

within the rotating-frame. Here,
\omega_{z}
is the carrier offset frequency (in rad/s) assumed to be uniform across the ensemble. The radio-frequency field is described by
\omega_{x}(t)
. The secular part of the dipolar interaction is described by the spherical tensor operators
T^{ij}_{20}
quantised along the space-fixed
z
-axis. In Cartesian coordinates the secular part is given by

\displaystyle T^{ij}_{20}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(2I_{iz}I_{jz}-(I_{ix}I_{jx}+I_{iy}I_{jy})).

(S 4)

The dipolar coupling constants
d_{ij}
are modulated randomly in time via
\kappa_{ij}(t)
. The modulations may be caused by lattice vibrations for example.

For the current case a
\theta_{x}
pulse of length
\tau_{1}
is repeated once every period
T=\tau_{1}+\tau_{2}

\displaystyle{[}\theta_{x}(\tau_{1})-\tau_{2}{]}-{[}\theta_{x}(\tau_{1})-\tau_{2}{]}-\dots.

(S 5)

As a consequence the rf-Hamiltonian is periodic in time

\displaystyle H_{\rm rf}(t+T)=H_{\rm rf}(t).

(S 6)

The rf-propagator then admits a Floquet form

\displaystyle U_{\rm rf}(t)=P(t)e^{-iH_{\rm eff}t}.

(S 7)

The operator
P(t)
captures the micro-motion of the system

\displaystyle P(t+T)=P(t),P(0)=\mathbbm{1}.

(S 8)

The effective Hamiltonian
H_{\rm eff}
describes the macro-motion taking the system from
nT
to
(n+1)T
. The rf-propagator belongs to rotation group SO(3). It is then convenient to choose the Euler representation for
P(t)

\displaystyle P(t)=R(\Lambda_{t})=R_{z}(\alpha_{t})R_{y}(\beta_{t})R_{z}(\gamma_{t}).

(S 9)

The Euler angles are required to be periodic
\Lambda_{t+T}=\Lambda_{t}
in time. For the effective Hamiltonian we may choose the axis-angle representation

\displaystyle H_{\rm eff}=\omega_{\rm eff}\{R_{z}(\phi_{\rm eff})R_{y}(\theta_{\rm eff})I_{z}R^{\dagger}_{y}(\theta_{\rm eff})R^{\dagger}_{z}(\phi_{\rm eff})\},

(S 10)

where
(\theta_{\rm eff},\phi_{\rm eff})
define a new quantization axis of the system. The effective angles are given by

\displaystyle\theta_{\rm eff}=\cot^{-1}\{\cos(\gamma/2)\cot(\eta)+\cot(\psi/2)\csc(\eta)\sin(\gamma/2)\},

(S 11)

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm eff}=-\frac{\gamma}{2},

with

\displaystyle\psi=\tau_{1}\sqrt{\omega_{x}^{2}+\omega_{z}^{2}},\quad\gamma=\tau_{2}\omega_{z},\quad\eta=\tan^{-1}(\omega_{x},\omega_{z}).

(S 12)

The relaxation dynamics are analysed within the interaction frame generated by
U_{\rm rf}(t)
. The stochastic part takes the form

\displaystyle Q(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\kappa_{ij}(t)U^{\dagger}_{\rm rf}(t)T^{ij}_{20}U_{\rm rf}(t)

(S 13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\kappa_{ij}(t)e^{+iH_{\rm eff}t}P^{\dagger}(t)T^{ij}_{20}P(t)e^{-iH_{\rm eff}t},

\displaystyle Q(t)=\sum_{i<j}\sum_{m}\kappa_{ij}(t)e^{+iH_{\rm eff}t}T^{ij}_{2m}e^{-iH_{\rm eff}t}D^{2}_{m0}(\Lambda^{-1}_{t}),

(S 14)

where
D^{2}_{mn}(\Lambda)
are Wigner D-matrix elements. We express the spherical tensor operators
T^{ij}_{2m}
in terms of spherical tensor operators
B^{ij}_{2m}
quantized along
n_{\rm eff}

\displaystyle T^{ij}_{2m}=\sum_{n}B^{ij}_{2n}q_{nm}.

(S 15)

We may then express
Q(t)
as follows

\displaystyle Q(t)=\sum_{i<j}d_{ij}(t)\sum_{n,m}B^{ij}_{2n}q_{nm}D^{2}_{m0}(\Lambda^{-1}_{t})e^{+in\omega_{\rm eff}t}.

(S 16)

The Wigner D-matrix elements may be expanded as a Fourier series with angular frequency
\Omega=2\pi/T

\displaystyle Q(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\kappa_{ij}(t)\sum_{k,n,m}B^{ij}_{2n}q_{nm}c_{mk}e^{+ik\Omega t}e^{+in\omega_{\rm eff}t}

(S 17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\kappa_{ij}(t)\sum_{k,n}B^{ij}_{2n}(\sum_{m}q_{nm}c_{mk})e^{+ik\Omega t}e^{+in\omega_{\rm eff}t},

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}\kappa_{ij}(t)\sum_{k,n}B^{ij}_{2n}b_{nk}e^{+ik\Omega t}e^{+in\omega_{\rm eff}t}.

For Gaussian statistics the relaxation superoperator in the interaction frame may be written as follows
Ernst et al. [1990]
,
Breuer and Petruccione [2007]

\displaystyle\hat{\Gamma}^{I}=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\overline{\hat{Q}(t+\tau)\hat{Q}(t)}d\tau.

(S 18)

The overline indicates the stochastic average, the hat indicates a commutation superoperator (
\hat{A}B=AB-BA
). Within the rotating-wave approximation we then find

\displaystyle\hat{\Gamma}^{I}\simeq

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\overline{\kappa^{2}_{ij}(0)}\sum_{n,k}|b_{nk}|^{2}J(n\omega_{\rm eff}+k\Omega)\hat{B}^{ij}_{2n}(\hat{B}^{ij}_{2n})^{\dagger}

(S 19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\overline{\kappa^{2}_{ij}(0)}\sum_{n}c_{n}\hat{B}^{ij}_{2n}(\hat{B}^{ij}_{2n})^{\dagger},

where we assume the modulations between two different pairings of spins to be uncorrelated. The spectral density
J(\omega)
is given by a Lorentzian characterised by a correlation time
\tau_{\rm c}

\displaystyle J(\omega)=\frac{\tau_{\rm c}}{1+(\omega\tau_{\rm c})^{2}}.

(S 20)

If we assume that the dipolar interactions are sufficiently averaged by the pulse sequence we may further approximate

\displaystyle\hat{L}^{I}\simeq\hat{L}^{I}_{\rm dd}+\hat{\Gamma}^{I},\quad\hat{L}^{I}_{\rm dd}=-i\sum_{i<j}d_{ij}\hat{B}^{ij}_{20},

(S 21)

where the first term represents the average dipolar Hamiltonian quantized along the effective axis. The magnetisation after an initial
\pi/2
-pulse may be decomposed with respect to the effective axis
n_{\rm eff}

\displaystyle\rho^{I}(0)\propto\sum_{m=-1}^{+1}a_{m}(\theta_{\rm eff},\phi_{\rm eff})\sum_{i}p^{i}(0)B^{i}_{1m}.

(S 22)

The expansion coefficients are given by

\displaystyle a_{-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\cos(\theta)\cos(\phi)-i\sin(\phi))=-a^{*}_{+1},

(S 23)

\displaystyle a_{0}=\sin(\theta)\cos(\phi).

In general each spin may have a different initial polarisation, but on the time scale of the relaxation process these differences are equalised by spin diffusion effects. In the interaction frame we thus assume equal polarisation for all magnetisation modes

\displaystyle\rho^{I}(t)\simeq\sum_{m=-1}^{+1}a_{m}(\theta_{\rm eff},\phi_{\rm eff})p_{m}(t)\sum_{i}B^{i}_{1m},

(S 24)

which will approximately relax with rates given by

\displaystyle R^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sum_{i}B^{i}_{10}|\hat{\Gamma}^{I}|\sum_{i}B^{i}_{10})}{\sum_{i}(B^{i}_{10}|B^{i}_{10})}

(S 25)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta M_{2}}{4}\sum_{n}n^{2}\sum_{k}|b_{nk}|^{2}J(n\omega_{\rm eff}+k\Omega),

\displaystyle R^{{}^{\prime}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sum_{i}B^{i}_{11}|\hat{\Gamma}^{I}|\sum_{i}B^{i}_{11})}{\sum_{i}(B^{i}_{11}|B^{i}_{11})}

(S 26)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta M_{2}}{8}\sum_{n}(6-n^{2})\sum_{k}|b_{nk}|^{2}J(n\omega_{\rm eff}+k\Omega),

\displaystyle\Delta M_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i<j}\overline{\kappa^{2}_{ij}(0)},

(S 27)

where
\Delta M_{2}
is the change in the second moment of the dipolar spectrum. Within the rotating-frame the signal is approximately given by

\displaystyle s(t)\simeq 2|a_{1}(\theta_{\rm eff},\phi_{\rm eff})|^{2}e^{-R^{{}^{\prime}}_{2}t}+|a_{0}(\theta_{\rm eff},\phi_{\rm eff})|^{2}e^{-R^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}t},

(S 28)

which displays, within the limitations of the model, a bi-exponential decay.

S5.3
Small angle off-resonant lifetime elongation

1/R^{{}^{\prime}}_{1}

1/R_{1}
. A simplified picture of the anomalous lifetime elongations may be given as follows. Within the interaction frame of the micro-motion operator

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(t)=H_{\rm eff}+V(t),

(S 29)

where the time-independent Floquet Hamiltonian

represents the random perturbation superimposed with a periodic modulation due to

. Consider for simplicity the two-level system shown in Fig.

\tilde{\alpha}
and
\tilde{\beta}
depends on the spectral density

, which is only

kHz for the current case. The experimental sampling rate on the other hand is at least

and, as a first approximation, ignore the higher sampling harmonics

. For small flip angles

leading to efficient relaxation. Intuitively this follows from the fact that even small resonant kicks eventually tip the
