Time Symmetry, Retrocausality, and Emergent Collapse: The Tlalpan Interpretation of Quantum Mechanics Preprint: APS/123-QED
Alejandro Frank

Abstract
Quantum mechanics has remained conceptually puzzling since its inception. While its mathematical formalism provides predictions of unparalleled accuracy, the interpretative framework underpinning measurement and collapse has never been fully clarified. The Tlalpan Interpretation (QTI) proposes a shift: the wavefunction collapse is not a primitive, axiomatic rule but an emergent phenomenon, a spontaneous breaking of time symmetry triggered by amplification and record creation. The novelty of QTI lies in its embedding of collapse within the conceptual language of critical phenomena in statistical physics. The theory introduces measurable parameters (the amplification fraction χ \chi , the record asymmetry O, and the retrocausal coherence time τ RC \tau_{\mathrm{RC}} ) that serve as order parameters describing the quantum–classical transition. Collapse is thus characterized not as a mysterious fiat but as a phase transition, with thresholds and scaling laws analogous to magnetization in ferromagnets or condensation in superfluids. This perspective allows the interpretation to retain microscopic time symmetry and spatial locality, while attributing quantum correlations and Bell-type violations to retrocausal boundary constraints rather than instantaneous action at a distance. Collapse corresponds to the granularization of trajectories: the conversion of smooth, time-symmetric quantum evolution into discrete, irreversible records. Most importantly, QTI is testable. It predicts: (1) sharp threshold-like disappearance of interference when amplification exceeds a critical value, (2) anomalously fast decay of reversibility in chaotic optical cavities compared with regular ones, and (3) qualitatively new interference fringes in a time-symmetric generalization of Moshinsky’s diffraction in time. If confirmed, these predictions would place collapse squarely in the realm of thermodynamic processes, removing the last “mystical” postulate from quantum theory.



I
Introduction

I.0.1
The measurement problem

Since the early 20th century, quantum mechanics has been haunted by the so-called “measurement problem.” Schrödinger’s wave mechanics and Heisenberg’s matrix formulation were shown to be mathematically equivalent, but the question of what the wavefunction means and how it connects to the definiteness of observed reality has remained unresolved.

In the orthodox Copenhagen interpretation, advanced most prominently by Niels Bohr and Werner Heisenberg, the wavefunction is assumed to evolve deterministically according to the Schrödinger equation until an ill-defined event called “measurement” occurs. At that moment, the system is said to “collapse” into one definite outcome. This collapse is not described by the Schrödinger equation itself; it is introduced as a new rule — outside the theory’s fundamental dynamics.

This pragmatic view worked well for guiding calculations and designing experiments, but it left physicists uneasy. Einstein, along with Podolsky and Rosen (1935), argued that such a framework must be incomplete. They pointed to “spooky action at a distance”: correlations between distant particles that seemed to defy locality. Their challenge anticipated John Bell’s theorem, which later proved that no purely local, forward-causal hidden-variable model could reproduce all the predictions of quantum mechanics.

I.0.2
Alternative interpretations

In response, a variety of alternative interpretations have been proposed, each solving part of the puzzle while raising new difficulties:

•

Bohmian mechanics (1952): introduces hidden variables guided by a pilot wave. Determinism is restored, but at the cost of explicit spatial nonlocality.

•

Many-Worlds (Everett, 1957): denies collapse altogether. The universal wavefunction branches into countless worlds, each realizing one outcome. The price is ontological extravagance: why should we experience just one branch, and how does probability emerge?

•

Transactional interpretation (Cramer, 1986): employs time-symmetric “handshakes” between advanced and retarded waves. Elegant, but it stops short of explaining how irreversibility and outcome uniqueness appear.

•

Two-State Vector Formalism (Aharonov, 1964 onward): emphasizes that quantum systems are described by both forward- and backward-evolving states. This yields surprising insights, especially regarding weak measurements, but does not solve the collapse problem.

•

Decoherence theory (Zurek, Joos, Zeh, 1970s–2000s): explains why interference is effectively suppressed when systems entangle with uncontrolled environments. Decoherence shows why the world looks classical, but it cannot explain why one outcome occurs rather than a mixture, nor why the arrow of time consistently points forward.

Each of these perspectives highlights important features of quantum mechanics, but the central enigma remains: why does a time-symmetric microdynamics give rise to a time-asymmetric macroreality?

I.0.3
The time symmetry puzzle

Microscopic laws — Newton’s mechanics, Maxwell’s electrodynamics, and above all Schrödinger’s equation — are fundamentally invariant under time reversal. If a process is allowed, its temporal mirror image is equally possible. Yet the macroscopic world is filled with irreversible processes: entropy increases, records accumulate, and memory points to the past.

The central puzzle of quantum foundations is that collapse seems to introduce irreversibility at the most fundamental level, despite the underlying equations being reversible. Most interpretations have accepted this mismatch as an axiom, or postponed it to cosmology.

I.0.4
The QTI proposal

The Tlalpan Interpretation offers a different path. Its central commitments are:

1.

Microscopic time symmetry is exact. The Schrödinger equation admits both forward and backward solutions; unmeasured systems evolve according to these reversible laws.

2.

Retrocausal sensitivity exists. Quantum probabilities can depend not only on past preparation but also on future boundary conditions, as encoded in two-time formalisms. This dependence, however, is statistical and non-signaling.

3.

Collapse is emergent. It is triggered by amplification and record creation, which act like phase transitions. Once microscopic fluctuations are amplified irreversibly, time symmetry is spontaneously broken, and advanced influences are quenched.

4.

Quantitative parameters define the transition. The amplification fraction
\chi
, the record asymmetry O, and the retrocausal coherence time
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}
provide measurable order parameters. Collapse is not vague or metaphysical but experimentally accessible.

Thus, in QTI, the wavefunction’s collapse is not a primitive law but a collective instability of time symmetry. Amplification plays the role that temperature does in magnetism: driving a system across a critical threshold where symmetry breaks.

The interpretation is named Tlalpan from the Nahuatl roots

(earth) and
pan

The name also carries a cultural resonance: situating a universal physical idea within a local intellectual tradition, as a reminder that science is both global and rooted.

II
Core Principales of the Tlalpan Interpretation

II.0.1
Time Symmetry as a Fundamental Property

At the microscopic level, quantum dynamics are governed by the Schrödinger equation:

i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=H\Psi

Here
\Psi(x,t)
is the wavefunction, describing the quantum state at position
x
and time
t
. The operator
H
is the Hamiltonian, representing the total energy (kinetic plus potential).
\hbar
is Planck’s constant divided by
2\pi
.

If
\Psi(x,t)
is a solution to this equation, then its time-reversed partner
\Psi^{*}(x,-t)
is also a solution, provided
H
is time-reversal invariant (for example, no external magnetic fields that explicitly break time symmetry).

This means that microscopic quantum laws are reversible: they make no distinction between past and future.

Yet, in daily life, the world is clearly asymmetric: we remember the past, not the future; detectors register outcomes that persist; entropy increases. In standard quantum mechanics, this asymmetry is inserted by hand in the form of the collapse postulate.

QTI’s proposal: collapse is not a separate axiom but a spontaneous breaking of time symmetry. Just as a ferromagnet chooses a direction of magnetization below its Curie temperature, amplification in quantum measurements forces the system to pick a temporal direction: forward.

II.0.2
The Two-Time Boundary Formalism

If time symmetry is real, then states should not depend only on past initial conditions but also on future boundaries. This is formalized in the Aharonov–Bergmann–Lebowitz (ABL) rule, introduced in 1964:

P(a_{k}|i,f)=\frac{\left|\braket{f|P_{k}|i}\right|^{2}}{\Sigma_{j}\left|\braket{f|P_{j}|i}\right|^{2}}

where
\ket{i}
is the prepared initial state,
\ket{j}
is a final state selected at some later time (post-selection), and
P_{k}
is the projection operator corresponding to the intermediate outcome
a_{k}
.

This formula shows that the probability of obtaining outcome ak depends on both how the system was prepared and how it will end.

The interpretation is that quantum systems in unmeasured regimes are sensitive to both past and future boundaries.

This “retrocausal sensitivity” does not allow paradoxes or communication to the past, because the correlations appear only statistically, across ensembles, not in individual controllable events.

This echoes Wheeler and Feynman’s absorber theory of electrodynamics, which also employed both advanced and retarded waves.

II.0.3
Amplification as Spontaneous Symmetry Breaking

In practice, quantum measurements always involve some form of amplification. A microscopic event —for example, a single photon hitting a photodiode— triggers a cascade: electron avalanches, current pulses, digital signals, stored data. A single radioactive decay in Schrödinger’s cat experiment would release energy that triggers a Geiger counter, which in turn sets off a macroscopic poison mechanism.

These cascades are thermodynamically irreversible. Once they occur, the time-reversed solution is practically impossible to realize.

QTI’s central claim: amplification acts like a phase transition.

•

Below threshold: the system remains time-symmetric, retrocausal influences possible.

•

At threshold: the system undergoes a spontaneous breaking of time symmetry, and collapse occurs.

•

Above threshold: the system is classical, with irreversible, forward-pointing records.

This explains why collapse is absent in isolated systems but inevitable in macroscopic ones.

II.0.4
Control and Order Parameters

To make these ideas testable, QTI introduces quantitative measures.

(a) Amplification fraction (
\chi
):

\chi=\frac{N_{\mathrm{measured}}}{N_{\mathrm{total}}},

where
N_{\mathrm{measured}}
is the number of events that have been irreversibly amplified into records, and
N_{\mathrm{total}}
is the total number of events.

\chi=0
means no amplification (fully quantum, reversible).

\chi=1
means all events are amplified (fully classical).

This parameter is directly tunable: for instance, by adjusting the gain of a photomultiplier tube or by coupling an interferometer more or less strongly to detectors.

(b) Record asymmetry (O):

Defined using the Kullback–Leibler divergence (DKL), a measure of how different two probability distributions are.

O=\frac{1}{2}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\mathrm{forward}}||p_{\mathrm{backward}})+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\mathrm{backward}}||p_{\mathrm{forward}})\right]

where:

•

p_{\mathrm{forward}}(r)
: distribution of records
r
obtained when the system is allowed to evolve normally forward in time.

•

p_{\mathrm{backward}}(r)
: distribution of records obtained in a “time-reversal” or echo protocol, where one attempts to reverse the dynamics (for example using spin echoes, phase-conjugated light, or microwave time-reversal mirrors).

The Kullback–Leibler divergence is defined as:

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p||q)=\sum_{r}p(r)\log\frac{p(r)}{q(r)}

Interpretation:

If
p_{\mathrm{forward}}
and
p_{\mathrm{backward}}
are identical, then
O=0
, i.e. time symmetry preserved.

If
O>0
, the forward and backward distributions differ, hence irreversibility has emerged.

Thus,
O
functions as an order parameter for collapse, analogous to magnetization in ferromagnets or the density difference in liquid–gas transitions.

(c) Retrocausal coherence time (
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}
):

This is the maximum timescale over which retrocausal sensitivity to future boundaries survives. It can be estimated in delayed-choice or quantum eraser experiments by measuring how long interference remains affected by future measurement settings.

•

For
\chi<\chi_{C}
,
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}
is finite: advanced effects can influence interference patterns.

•

At
\chi=\chi_{C}
,
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}
collapses to zero: advanced effects are quenched.

•

For
\chi=\chi_{C}
,
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}=0
: retrocausal sensitivity is gone, the system is fully classical.

This plays the role of a correlation length in phase transitions, except here it is a temporal correlation length.

II.0.5
Collapse as a Critical Transition

QTI proposes that collapse follows the same mathematical structure as critical phenomena in condensed matter.

•

Subcritical regime (
\chi<\chi_{C}
): Interference visibility
V
remains high,
O=0
,
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}>0

•

At the critical point (
\chi=\chi_{C}

V

•

Supercritical regime (
\chi>\chi_{C}

The transition is characterized by scaling laws:

\displaystyle O\sim(\chi-\chi_{c})^{\beta}\quad\text{(growth of irreversibility)}

\displaystyle V\sim|\chi-\chi_{c}|^{\nu}\quad\text{(loss of interference visibility)}

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}\sim|\chi-\chi_{c}|^{-\gamma}\quad\text{(divergence of retrocausal coherence time)}

Here
\beta

\nu

This means that collapse is not arbitrary but follows universal laws. Measuring these exponents across different platforms (optical, atomic, superconducting circuits) would establish whether collapse indeed behaves as a critical phenomenon.

III

A distinctive feature of the Tlalpan Interpretation (QTI) is that it is not limited to a philosophical framework, but rather formulates clear and falsifiable physical predictions. If collapse is an emergent phenomenon—a critical transition in time symmetry—then it should leave experimental signatures distinct from the predictions of standard decoherence or purely ontological interpretations such as Many-Worlds.

QTI identifies three main scenarios:

1.

Comparison between optical or microwave cavities with regular and chaotic geometry (quantum billiards).

2.

A T-symmetric extension of the classical Moshinsky time diffraction experiment.

3.

Scaling protocols that allow measuring the parameters
\chi
,
O
, and
\tau_{\text{RC}}
and verifying whether collapse follows universal critical laws.

III.0.1
Chaotic vs. Regular Cavities: Loschmidt Echoes

Quantum billiards systems are cavities in which particles or photons bounce multiple times. The shape of the cavity determines whether the corresponding classical trajectories are integrable (regular) or chaotic.

Rectangular or circular cavity: regular trajectories.

Stadium-shaped cavity (developed by Bunimovich): chaotic trajectories.

In the quantum regime, these classical differences manifest themselves in interference patterns and stability to time reversal.

The Loschmidt Echo

To test for reversibility, the time reversal fidelity (Loschmidt echo) is defined:

F_{TR}(t)=\left|\braket{\Psi(0)|U^{\dagger}(t)U(t)|\Psi(0)}\right|^{2}

where:

•

\ket{\Psi(0)}
is the initial state.

•

U(t)
describes the forward evolution in time.

•

U^{\dagger}(t)
is the operation that attempts to reverse the evolution (e.g., using phase-conjugate mirrors in optics, or spin-echo protocols in nuclear spins).

•

F_{TR}(t)=1
means perfect reversibility.

III.0.2
Standard Predictions vs. QTI

Standard Decoherence: In the absence of environmental coupling,
F_{TR}
should remain very high in both regular and chaotic cavities. Differences only appear when the system interacts with an environment.

QTI: Even without an environment, chaoticity itself acts as an internal amplifier. Small fluctuations in initial conditions are exponentially amplified in chaotic cavities, so that the system crosses the collapse threshold (
\chi_{c}
) more quickly.

Central Prediction:

Regular cavities → persistent Loschmidt echo, high reversibility, until external coupling causes collapse.

Chaotic cavities →
F_{TR}
decays anomalously fast, much faster than standard decoherence explains.

This can be verified with microwaves, acoustics, optics, and single photons. Experiments with time-reversal mirrors already show that reversal is much more fragile in chaotic geometries.

A perhaps simpler additional way to test this hypothesis is to couple a one-photon-at-a-time double slit experiment succesively to the regular cavity and the chaotic one. QTI predicts that interference will persist for the regular cavity and will didappear for the chaotic one. A clear cut result. This experiment is being prepared at the quantum optics lab of Alfred U’Ren at the Nuclear Science Institute at UNAM.

III.0.3
Time-Symmetric Diffraction in Time

The Moshinsky Problem

In 1952, Marcos Moshinsky formulated the phenomenon of diffraction in time: a temporal analogue of optical diffraction. A particle beam strikes a shutter, which suddenly opens at
t=0
.

The standard solution, considering only delayed components, is:

\psi_{\mathrm{ret}}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\exp[i(kx-\omega t)]\cdot\operatorname{erfc}\left(-(x-vt)/\sqrt{2i\hbar t/m}\right)

Here:

•

k
is the wavenumber,

•

\omega=\hbar k^{2}/2m
is the associated frequency,

•

v=\hbar k/m
is the group velocity,

•

\operatorname{erfc}
is the complementary error function.

This result predicts oscillations in time, equivalent to Fresnel fringes in space. These oscillations have been observed in experiments with neutrons and ultracold atoms.

The QTI Extension

QTI proposes introducing a future boundary condition: the shutter not only opens at
t=0
, but also closes again at
t=\tau
. This introduces an advanced component:

\psi_{\mathrm{adv}}(x,t;\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\exp[{i(kx-\omega(t-\tau))}]\\
\cdot\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{-(x-v(t-\tau))}{\sqrt{2i\hbar(t-\tau)/m}}\right)

valid for
t<\tau
.

The full wavefunction is:

\psi(x,t)=\psi_{\mathrm{ret}}(x,t)+\alpha(\kappa)\psi_{\mathrm{adv}}(x,t;\tau)

The factor
\alpha(\kappa)
depends on the amplification intensity
\kappa
:

•

For
\kappa<\kappa_{C}
(weak amplification),
\alpha\approx 1
and the two terms interfere.

•

For
\kappa>\kappa_{C}
(strong amplification),
\alpha\rightarrow 0
and only the delayed contribution remains.

Distinctive prediction

In the subcritical regime, additional fringes appear, shifted in phase with respect to the standard Moshinsky oscillations. These fringes depend on the future boundary conditions.

In the supercritical regime, they disappear, and the pattern reduces to that of the standard theory.

This prediction is unambiguous: the appearance or disappearance of these additional fringes distinguishes QTI from other interpretations.

IV
Philosophical Implications

The Tlalpan Interpretation (QTI) not only proposes a physically testable framework, but may also reconfigures several of the deepest assumptions about time, causality and observation.

IV.0.1
Time, Causality, and Human Intuition

Microscopic physics is, in essence, reversible:

In classical mechanics, if a trajectory is possible, its time-reversed version is also possible.

In electrodynamics (without dissipation), Maxwell’s solutions allow for advanced and retarded waves.

In quantum mechanics, the Schrödinger equation is symmetric under time reversal.

However, human experience and macroscopic reality are dominated by one-way time: we remember the past, not the future; entropy grows; records always accumulate in the same direction.

This contrast generates the basic paradox: how do we reconcile reversible dynamics with an irreversible world?

QTI answers: time is not fundamentally asymmetric; asymmetry emerges as a result of a phase change. Collapse is not an externally imposed process, but a spontaneous transition of temporal symmetry brought about by amplification.

In the subcritical regime (without amplification), the system can depend on conditions from both the past and the future: retrocausality without paradoxes.

In the supercritical regime (with macroscopic amplification), this sensitivity is extinguished, and only forward causality survives.

Thus, causality is contextual:

Subcritical: time-symmetric, sensitive to past and future conditions.

Supercritical: time-asymmetric, forward causality.

IV.0.2
Collapse as Symmetry Breaking

In the Copenhagen tradition, collapse was a postulate without dynamic explanation. In Many-Worlds, collapse is eliminated at the cost of multiplying realities.

QTI offers a third path: collapse is an emergent phenomenon.

Amplification functions as a control parameter (similar to temperature in magnetism).

The log asymmetry
O
acts as an order parameter.

The transition occurs at a critical value
\tau_{c}
, accompanied by critical exponents
\beta
,
\nu
,
\gamma
.

The metaphor is clear: just as a magnet spontaneously magnetizes below the critical temperature, the quantum world spontaneously collapses when amplification exceeds a threshold.

Ontology: Collapse is not a fundamental law, but a collective process. Irreversibility is not in the equations, but in the dynamics of the amplified logs.

IV.0.3
The Observer

The role of the observer has historically been controversial:

In Copenhagen, “observation” is linked to the act of a human experimenter. This led to debates about whether consciousness was necessary for collapse.

In Many-Worlds, the observer is divided into multiple branches, each witnessing a result.

QTI proposes: the observer is secondary.

Collapse occurs when amplification converts microscopic fluctuations into irreversible records.

A human consciousness is not necessary: a detector, a cat, or any macroscopic system with many degrees of freedom already collapses the state.

The human mind only records a fait accompli.

Example: In the Schrödinger’s cat experiment, the cat is not in a true superposition state of “dead and alive.” The amplification of radioactive decay to the poison mechanism has already produced a result before anyone opens the box. The human observer does not cause the collapse: he merely observes it.

This eliminates the anthropocentrism of collapse and returns physics to its status as a natural, not a psychological, phenomenon.

IV.0.4
Retrocausality and Free Will

The idea of retrocausality provokes skepticism because it seems to threaten temporal consistency or free will.

QTI limits its domain:

Forward effects only operate in the subcritical regime (without amplification).

As soon as an irreversible record appears, forward causality dominates.

Retrocausal correlations are statistical: they do not allow sending messages or manipulating the past.

Thus, human freedom is not affected. The future can influence the past at a microscopic level, but those effects are erased as soon as a collapse occurs.

In philosophical language:

At the microscopic level, reality resembles the ”block universe” of relativity, where past and future are on equal footing.

At the macroscopic level, experience resembles the ”flow of time” because the emergence of collapse freezes a forward causal order.

QTI, therefore, integrates both views within a single framework.

IV.0.5
Historical Continuity

QTI falls within a tradition of attempts to reconcile temporal symmetry with quantum physics:

Wheeler–Feynman (1940s): electrodynamics of action at a distance with advanced and retarded waves.

Cramer (1986): the Transactional Interpretation, with temporal ”handshakes” between states.

Aharonov et al. (1960s–): two-state-vector formalism, useful for pre- and postselection experiments.

Huw Price (1990s): philosophical defense of retrocausality as a natural way out of quantum nonlocality.

What distinguishes QTI is that it introduces the language of critical phenomena:

It not only admits temporal symmetry, but also explains how and when it is broken.

It provides measurable parameters and scaling laws.

It offers concrete predictions that allow the idea to be tested.

V
Conclusions and Outlook

V.0.1
Summary of the Proposal

The Tlalpan Interpretation (QTI) reformulates the foundations of quantum mechanics. It retains the microscopic time symmetry of the Schrödinger dynamics but explains the macroscopic arrow of time as an emergent feature. Collapse is not primitive; it arises through spontaneous symmetry breaking when microscopic fluctuations are amplified into irreversible records.

This framework introduces control and order parameters — the amplification fraction (
\chi
), record asymmetry (
O
), and retrocausal coherence time (
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}
). These quantities define the quantum–classical transition as a critical phenomenon, enabling the use of scaling laws and renormalization group (RG) methods.

Thus, QTI bridges the gap between quantum theory and statistical physics: collapse becomes analogous to magnetization in ferromagnets, superconductivity, or other critical transitions where microscopic symmetries break spontaneously.

V.0.2
Key Predictions

QTI makes testable predictions that differ from decoherence theory or other interpretations:

1.

Threshold collapse of interference: Visibility in interferometers should remain robust until amplification reaches a critical value
\tau_{c}
, then collapse sharply, instead of decaying smoothly.

2.

Chaos-dependent irreversibility: In chaotic optical or microwave cavities, reversibility should decay far more rapidly than in regular cavities, even in isolation, because chaos itself acts as an amplifier.

3.

Time-symmetric diffraction in time: Experiments extending Moshinsky’s shutter problem should reveal additional interference fringes, sensitive to future boundaries, that vanish once amplification exceeds
\tau_{c}
.

These signatures distinguish QTI from both Many-Worlds (which predicts no collapse) and decoherence-only accounts (which predict gradual, environment-dependent suppression).

V.0.3
Conceptual Implications

Measurement problem: Collapse emerges dynamically, eliminating the need for an ad hoc postulate.

Arrow of time: Irreversibility is not fundamental but the result of spontaneous breaking of time symmetry through record creation.

Causality: Below threshold, retrocausal influences are physical but non-signaling; above threshold, only forward causality survives.

Ontology: QTI retains a single realized world, avoiding the proliferation of Many-Worlds and the observer-centric axiom of Copenhagen.

V.0.5
Outlook

If confirmed, the Tlalpan Interpretation would remove the last “mystical” axiom from quantum theory. Collapse would be understood as a thermodynamic, collective phenomenon rather than an unexplained primitive.

QTI is more than an interpretation: it is a research program. It translates foundational puzzles into empirical tests and quantitative physics, aligning the theory of measurement with the broader framework of phase transitions and complexity.
