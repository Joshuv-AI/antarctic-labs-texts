Enhancing Clinical Models with Pseudo Data for De-identification
Paul Landes, Aaron J. Chaise, Tarak N. Nandi, Ravi K. Madduri

Abstract
Many models are pretrained on redacted text for privacy reasons. Clinical foundation models are often trained on de-identified text, which uses special syntax (masked) text in place of protected health information. Even though these models have increased in popularity, there has been little effort in understanding the effects of training them on redacted text. In this work, we pretrain several encoder-only models on a dataset that contains redacted text and a version with replaced realistic pseudo text. We then fine-tuned models for the protected health information de-identification task and show how our methods significantly outperform previous baselines. The contributions of this work include: a) our novel, and yet surprising findings with training recommendations b) redacted text replacements used to produce the pseudo dataset c) pretrained embeddings and fine-tuned task specific models d) freely available 1 1 1 https://github.com/APPFL/cpbert pseudo training dataset generation and model source code used in our experiments

Introduction

De-identification is the process of modifying a corpus so that any personal or
private information is either redacted (masked data), replaced with from the
same corpus (anonymized data), or replaced from an external source (synthetic
data). An example of a de-identified dataset is Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III Version 1.4
Johnson et al. 2016
, which uses
masked tokens that have a particular syntax and identify the masked/redacted
text. For example,
[**Doctor Last Name**]
are masked tokens that replace a physician’s last
name as shown in
Figure 0b
. In this work, ‘‘masked’’
tokens or text refer to de-identified redacted text rather than mask tokens in
masked language models such as BERT
Devlin et al. 2019
. This clarification becomes more important
in the discussion of the mask language pretrained embeddings in

Section
4.1.3
.

Clinical data is governed by Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act laws, which protect patients’ health
records. De-identified corpora do exist, such as the Cerner Real-World Data
Ehwerhemuepha et al. 2022

and
Cerner Health Facts
2
2

2

https://uthsc.edu/cbmi/data/cerner.php
, but these datasets take significant effort to procure. However,
Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III is publicly available and easily accessible
3
3

3

Access to the
Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III corpus
requires

creating a PhysioNet account and finishing a training course.
, which is the
reason it is the most popular corpus for clinical text research. Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III has
been used to pretrain many Large Language Models and
masked language models
Yang et al. 2022
;
Huang et al. 2020
;
Alsentzer et al. 2019
.

Given the popularity of Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III and that a significant portion of the corpus is
redacted (11,204,493 masked tokens or 6% of the corpus), we believe it warrants
investigation to determine the negative effects of redacted mask tokens. Our
primary research questions for this work are

a)

to determine if masked language models are negatively effected by redacted text

b)

if they are, can they be improved by pretraining on substituted realistic
pseudo data

We hypothesize that named entities in language models lack the necessary
entropy, and instead, saturate over the redacted tokens in de-identified
corpora. We believe that other tasks, such as named entity recognition and entity linking, are
also negatively affected for the same reasons.

To address these questions, we processed Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III’s entire two million free text
notes written by medical professionals. These clinical notes were processed
for unsupervised pretraining with masked text left intact
(see
Figure 0b
); we call this the “masked dataset”. This corpus
was copied with masked text replaced by pseudo text, which resulted in the
“pseudo dataset” (see
Figure 0c
). We then used the original
masked dataset and pseudo dataset to self-supervised train several encoder-only masked language models and save
each as new pretrained checkpoints. Each checkpoint was then used to train
and evaluate new fine-tuned models on a de-identification dataset.

The pseudo data generation techniques are covered in
Section
4.1.1
,
and the methods to create the pseudo dataset are covered in
Section
4.1.2
.
The de-identification models, architecture and method of training are detailed
in
Section
5
. We explain how our pretraining methods led to better
fine-tuned de-identification models with a surprising finding in

Section
6
and discuss trade-offs in
Section
7
.

Related Work

As the state-of-the-art of general domain Large Language Models outpaced that of clinical
Large Language Models
DeepSeek-AI et al. 2025
;
Anil et al. 2024
;
Touvron et al. 2023
,
their necessity was
challenged
Agrawal et al. 2022
;
Kartchner et al. 2023
.
However, recent studies show that clinical domain Large Language Models and masked language models have better
performance and are more efficient at biomedical domain specific
tasks
Wang et al. 2024
;
Hager et al. 2024
;
Ankit Pal 2024
;
Wornow et al. 2023
;
Lehman et al. 2023
.

Others have used pseudo tokens in pretrained language models.

Tan et al. 2022
used pseudo tokens to train new BERT
layers for contrastive learning. However, they use a generative method for
semantically similar sentences where ours uses classification models to
determine pretrained embedding models’ performance on a biomedical task.
Recently, Large Language Models have been leveraged to produce pseudo data, such as rare
molecule
discovery
Chen et al. 2024
;
Guo et al. 2024

and open-ended responses
generation
Zenimoto et al. 2024
.

The de-identification of clinical corpora using contemporary deep learning methods
started with the work of

Liu et al. 2015
, who used character
level neural networks, and
Dernoncourt et al. 2017
, who used bi-directional long-short term memory with
non-contextual word
embeddings
Mikolov et al. 2013b
;
Mikolov et al. 2013a
;
Pennington et al. 2014
.
Similarly,
Liu et al. 2017
used a bi-directional long-short term memory with a conditional random field output,
and
Hartman et al. 2020
used an recurrent neural network with a conditional random field to classify
protected health information. Soon after,
Johnson et al. 2020
utilized masked language models, such as BERT for the task.

We also use masked language models, but train our own checkpoints rather than use off-the-shelf
models. Our goal differs in that we endeavor to discover how check-pointing
pretrained embeddings on masked and pseudo tokens affect the downstream
fine-tuning task of de-identification.

Pretrained

Both the masked and pseudo datasets are derived from Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III Version 1.4
Johnson et al. 2016
. This corpus is taken from the Beth
Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts and contains 58,976
hospital admissions. This dataset includes both structured (such as patient
vitals) and unstructured data (such as free text notes by medical
professionals). Our datasets are based on the MIMIC-III unstructured data
and consists of 2,083,180 notes from 46,520 patients who were admitted to the
intensive care unit surgical, medical, and neonatal departments.

De-identification

The 2014 i2b2/UTHealth de-identification dataset
Stubbs and Uzuner 2015
that reports on 296
patients in 1,304 medical records. This dataset was de-anonymized by replacing
protected health information with hand crafted pseudo data, which is somewhat similar to our method.
The redacted data was then demarcated with lexical spans annotated with one of
23 labels indicating the type of replaced data, such as “date”, “hospital”,
etc.

4.1
Pretraining

The pretraining phase included creating the masked and pseudo datasets, and then training embeddings.

4.1.1
Pseudo Database

The first preprocessing step was to create a pseudo database. This database
includes lists of data that is used to derive the pseudo dataset. These lists and their
counts include:

Hospitals by state (4,826)

Companies (500)

Universities (2,074)

US states names and abbreviations (51)

First names by gender (28,516)

Last names (162,252)

The first name, gender and popularity distribution comes from a collection of
sources. First and last names include popularity statistics as a count
recorded by year. The last name popularity distribution comes from the 2010 US
census. The first name popularity distribution was drawn from the years
between 1960 and 2020 to better represent those that would be found in the
MIMIC-III corpus.

4.1.2
Derived Datasets

The masked dataset was created by formatting the clinical text as a single flat newline
separated file with normalized sentences. This was accomplished by tokenizing
and sentence chunking the contents of the free text clinical

NOTEEVENTS
table from Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (see
Section
3
) using
ScispaCy
Neumann et al. 2019
library. We also reconstructed
enumerated and itemized lists (
i.e.
vitals) heuristically.

The pseudo dataset was then created by replacing the masked tokens with realistic text by
sampling from the pseudo database (see
Section
4.1.1
) and randomized data
generation methods. For each line of the masked dataset, tokens were parsed for
protected health information “tags” with MIMIC-III mask regular expressions. The tag (such as
a first name, hospital, medical record number, etc.), was then used to select
how the pseudo data was generated for the replacement. For example, “Doctor
First Name” would be parsed from
[**Doctor Last Name**]
using a regular expression, which
in turn was mapped to the tag
DOCTORLASTNAME
. A list of data
generators and the tag masked token tags associated with them is given in

Table
2
.

Once a generator was selected, it was used to create the pseudo text. For
example, the last name generator mapped from the
DOCTORLASTNAME
tag
would sample the pseudo database for the replacement text, and substitute it in
place of the MIMIC-III masked tokens. The pseudo data replacement of first and
last names were sampled from the database using the popularity of the name as
the probability distribution. Some tags include the gender of the name, or
chosen randomly if the tag was missing.

Much of the generated data comes from randomized data rather than lists.
Examples include dates, identifiers such as medical record numbers
(
NUMERICID
) and phone numbers (
PHONE
), and patient ages
(
AGE
).

Not all Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III masked tokens have a generator as the text often does not follow a
conventional surface text pattern. In these cases we use the clinical Large Language Model GatorTron
Yang et al. 2022
to fill in the missing token
using the entire sentence as input. The model was also used to replace
ambiguous tags such as
NAME
, which in MIMIC-III, can name a person,
place or thing.

Discussion and Error Analysis

Figure 4:
Confusion matrices of the
RoBERTa Large fine-tuned models on Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act re-categorized labels.
Top (a): masked. Bottom (b): pseudo.

Our hypothesis that pseudo models outperform masked models holds for
XLM-RoBERTa but not RoBERTa large.

Figure
4
shows that the pseudo model incorrectly
predicted
LOCATION
36 times more than the masked model. These
(re-categorized
LOCATION
) false positives are attributed to 34
additional i2b2 corpus non-protected health information (“other”
O
) tokens categorized as

HOSPITAL
(see
Appendix
A

Figure
6
).

Many of the misclassified locations include named entities and medical terms
such as Medtronic (medical device company) and TSH (thyroid stimulating
hormone). Generally, the model performed better with context words that helped
in clarifying the terminology such as “clinic” and “north” in hospital
names.

Given the high number of
LOCATION
misclassifications we calculated the
intersection of hospital names in the i2b2 dataset with the pseudo list
and found that only 29 (7.2%) are shared, or 35 (8.91%) if “hospital” and
“clinic” are stripped. Yet, this tag was the second most replaced data type
in the pseudo dataset (see
Table
2
).

We see a similar pattern with non-protected health information tokens predicted as
NAME
24
times more often in the pseudo model. We also see that
NAME
is
mislabeled as
LOCATION
23 more times in the pseudo model. The
majority of the misclassifications are the i2b2
PATIENT
false positives,
which are often confused with doctor names. This speaks to a larger problem of
confusion with medical terms and human names.

However, the pseudo model was better with diseases and medical terms named after
individuals. For example, a mention of the last name “Gleason”, which as a
prostate cancer pathology scoring system
Gleason 1992
, was
correctly labeled in the pseudo model but not the masked.

The performance results in shown in
Table
8

support the these findings.
NAME
is shown to under perform in the
RoBERTa pseudo models except for the XLM-RoBERTa models. In fact, we see
that the XLM-RoBERTa pseudo models consistently outperform the masked models
(with the exception of the
AGE
label). See models 13, 14 in

Appendix
A

Figure
6

for confusion matrices.

This is partly due to the sensitivity of the score given the low count of the
label (824 in the training set). Another factor is almost certainly the single
numeric-only values replaced in the pseudo dataset, such as
AGE
tags (see

Table
2
). Furthermore, the i2b2 corpus includes plural morphology
(“40’s”) other, descriptors such as years and month (“49y7.7m”), which were
not addressed when constructing the pseudo data generators.

The high performance, despite potentially low entropy or confusing pseudo data,
highlights the robustness of the XLM-RoBERTa model. We see a significant
improvement in the pseudo over the masked models for
PROFESSION
,

NAME
and
LOCATION
. We believe the XLM-RoBERTa model is more
capable with these labels and Latin-rooted medical terminology because of its
exposure to foreign names, locations and romance languages.

8
Conclusion and Future Work

Our hypothesis that pseudo dataset masked language model training yields fitter downstream
fine-tuned de-identification models is congruent with the multi-lingual
XLM-RoBERTa. Multi-lingual trained masked language models are better adept at continued
learning on lower entropy pseudo data (such as foreign names). However, the
quality of generated pseudo data can negatively impact downstream fine-tuned
model performance. Furthermore, there is no one-size-fits-all for all tasks as
any Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III trained model will generate masked tokens. However, clinical
models still have a place as they, thus far, continue to outperform general
domain foundational
models
Hu et al. 2024
;
Wornow et al. 2023
.

The pseudo data trained models improved results and implies real clinical
training would increase performance even further for the de-identification
task.
Subsequent work includes self-supervised training Large Language Models on our datasets and
supervised fine-tuning new clinical task models from the pretrained
checkpoints.
