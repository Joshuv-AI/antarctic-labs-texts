Surfing the SWAVES: Lifecycle-aware Service Placement in MEC
Federico Giarrè, Holger Karl

Abstract
In Multi-access Edge Computing (MEC) networks, users covered by a mobile network can exploit edge clouds (ECs) , computational resources located at the network’s edge, to execute virtual network functions (VNFs) . ECs are particularly useful when deploying VNFs with strict delay and availability requirements. As users roam in the network and get handed over between cells, deployed VNFs must follow users to retain the benefits of edge computing. Yet, having VNFs ready at the closest EC can be challenging: (i) ECs are not usually powerful enough to store and run any combination of VNFs simultaneously; (ii) if a VNF is not available at the needed EC , a series of time-consuming operations has to be performed before the VNF becomes operational. These limitations can be addressed by proactively starting VNFs instances at (likely) future locations, balancing better latency properties against higher resource usage. Such proactive deployment does need forecasting of user movements, but these will be imperfect, creating yet another tradeoff. We present our approach to this service provisioning problem, SWAVES. When compared on the ratio of users’ unsuccessful packets, SWAVES improves such metric by orders of magnitude with respect to other proposed heuristic.



I
Introduction

We consider a scenario where mobile users access services provided by a
MEC
infrastructure, with maximum
end-to-end (E2E)
latency in the milliseconds and high availability requirements. These services are deployed via
VNFs
running in computing premises situated close to
base station (BS)
, i.e.
ECs
.
In
MEC
, users can deploy
VNFs
at
ECs
to comply with their
quality of service (QoS)
requirements thanks to proximity, reducing latency.

In scenarios where
QoS
requirements are relaxed,
VNF
placement can be flexible and optimized for resource usage and load balancing, even if distant from the user, as shown is
Figure 1
.
But what if latency requirements become tight and users are mobile?

VNFs
deployment need to follow users’ mobility to maintain the benefits of
MEC
.
As users connect to new
ECs
, however, the needed
VNF
may not be available yet; there might not even be the software for it. One solution is to reroute user traffic to a running instance of the
VNF
in another location, increasing experienced delay
[
1
]
while starting the
VNF
at the new location. But this approach easily violates strict
QoS
requirements of certain classes of services.
Another approach could be to have every
VNF
always running at every
EC
but, by definition,
ECs
have constrained computational resources, rendering this option unfeasible.
To support such services, it is necessary to pre-emptively start
VNFs
instances along user paths to retain proximity and benefit from
MEC
. But to start a
VNF
, a sequence of time-consuming operations has to be performed, known as service
lifecycle

[
2
,
3
]
: from the retrieval and building of the executable if not available, to the start of a
virtual machine (VM)
or container if virtualized, and finally responding to requests.
These operations need variable time depending on the type of service, executable type, and network conditions, delaying the effectiveness of service provisioning decisions up to tens of seconds.

To address service lifecycle during provisioning along users’ path, exact knowledge about future users’ connections are needed. However, such knowledge is not obtainable in the real world. A key helper function to deal with this problem is forecasting of user behavior, in particular location
[
4
]
.
This can be exploited to pre-emptively deploy users’ required
VNFs

in time
and
at the right location

[
5
]
.
On the other hand, we cannot expect forecasts to be always accurate, especially as the randomness in user movements increases
[
1
]
.
To tackle this problem, instead of forecasting the exact next location of users, we can look at the likelihood of a service being needed in a cell further in the future (as shown in
Figure 2
).
These forecasts can be finally used to commence
VNFs
’ startup operations where needed, preparing them where they will be (likely) needed and, most importantly, removing them where they are not useful anymore.
From a logical viewpoint, a service spreads to nearby
ECs
starting from users requesting it, creating a
software wave
, hence the name of our approach, SWAVES.

Our contributions in this paper are the following:

•

We characterize service lifecycle via a
finite state machine (FSM)
, describing the sequence of states a generic
VNF
has to go through and the time it takes before being operational. We then consider such
FSM
when placing services, including their installation, startup and usage.

•

We introduce SWAVES, a lifecycle-aware service provisioning approach, robust to the randomness of user movement.

•

We experimentally validate SWAVES with respect to optimal and heuristic solvers under different
QoS
strictness and mobility randomness scenarios.

As in

[

13

[
12
]
. Conversely, users and

each either receive or send packets.

Finally, given

\underbrace{\frac{1}{\mu_{u}-\lambda_{u}}}_{\text{wireless link queuing}}+\underbrace{2(|\mathcal{L}|+1)t_{p}}_{\text{processing time}}+\underbrace{\sum^{\mathcal{L}}_{l}\frac{1}{\mu_{l}}+\frac{\rho_{l}}{2\mu_{l}(1-\rho_{l})}}_{\text{queuing per wired link}}.

Service Model

We consider a set
\mathcal{V}

; each instance can serve multiple users across the network. Each instance

to run.

is a vector of fixed values, one per type of resource (e.g., CPU, memory, disk),
and is enough for the services to comply with the required service level agreement (SLA)
2
2

2

.

Stateless
VNFs

\underbrace{\sum^{\mathcal{L}}_{l}\frac{V_{\text{mem}}}{\mu_{l}}}_{\text{transmission delay}}+\underbrace{2(|\mathcal{L}|+1)t_{p}}_{\text{processing time}}+\underbrace{\sum^{\mathcal{L}}_{l}\frac{1}{\mu_{l}}+\frac{\rho}{2\mu_{l}(1-\rho)}}_{\text{queuing per wired link}},

(2)

where

is the series of links to be traversed to exchange the user context.
We assume that the service cannot serve the user during user context migration, as it would update the context to be migrated.

IV
Problem Formulation

With the objective of meeting users’ strict
QoS
requirements, we want to minimize the ratio of users’
unsuccessful packets
.
A packet sent by users is considered unsuccessful if any of the following three conditions holds:

(i)

The packet is lost anywhere, in particular, on the wireless link,
4
4

4

Packet losses are not evaluated in our example scenarios to maintain focus on key points, though they would surely render a packet unsuccessful.
or

(ii)

when packet arrives at the EC serving the request VNF, that VNF is not in the running state,

(iii)

or the VNF is running, but currently migrating user state (only relevant for stateful VNFs),

(iv)

or the running VNF processes the packet but fails to meet the deadline imposed by the service’s
QoS
requirements.

We use
p_{u}(t)=1
to denote that the packet sent by user
u
at time
t
is unsuccessful (and
p_{u}(t)=0
, else).

\min\;\lim_{T\to\infty}\sum^{T}_{t}\sum^{\mathcal{U}}_{u}p_{u}(t),

(3a)

Let
z^{t}_{e,v,\psi}
the binary variable set to 1 if

v
at

(3b)

\sum_{\psi}^{\text{FSM}}z^{t}_{e,v,\psi}=1\quad\forall e\in\mathcal{B},v\in\mathcal{V},t\in T

(3c)

\sum^{\mathcal{\mathcal{V}}}_{v}R^{e}_{v}\leq R_{\text{tot}}\quad\forall e\in\mathcal{B},

(3d)

\sum^{\mathcal{\mathcal{B}}}_{e}c^{t}_{u,e}=1\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{U},\forall t\in\mathcal{T}.

V
Proposed approach

We want to deploy
VNFs
along users’ paths to reduce unsuccessful packets but, due to their lifecycle, deployment must begin early to ensure readiness when needed. Moreover, given
ECs
’ constrained resources, over-provisioning of
VNFs
is not possible. Consequently,
VNFs
must be deployed in time and only where there is a reasonable chance they will be needed in the near future.

V-A

Service deployment

Fig. 5:
SWAVES inference chain.

We present our approach, SWAVES. By assessing the likelihood of a user connecting to different
BSs
, we can change the required
VNFs
state in the respective
ECs
to

(i)

get
VNFs
as close to the running state as possible where users will likely connect, and

(ii)

remove
VNFs
where users are not going to connect, freeing
ECs
for other
VNFs
to use.

While a
VNF
near to the running state is not able to serve users, shortening the time to the running state can sensibly reduce unsuccessful packets.
We assume knowledge of two probabilities (cmp. Section
V-B
):

1.

The probability density function
f(L)
of a user following a particular path
L\in\mathcal{L}_{u}

2.

The probability
P(b\,|\,l)
of a user
u
connecting to
BS

b
given its location
l
.

From that, we compute the probability of the user
not
connecting to a cell
b
through the entirety of a time horizon
h
by conditioning on which path
L
a user actually follows, using the law of total probability, and then checking whether the user connects to
b
at that particular location. In more detail:

P_{b_{u}\neq b}=\int_{L\in\mathcal{L}_{u}\atop L=<l(t),l(t+1),\ldots>}\prod_{i=1}^{h}\left[1-P(b\,|\,l(t+i))\right]\cdot f(L)\;dL.

(4a)

To consider service multi-tenancy, given the
VNF

v
to deploy and the subset
\mathcal{U}_{v}
of users needing
v
, the probability of a service being needed at a certain cell
b
is equal to

P_{v,b}=1-\prod_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{v}}P_{b_{u}\neq b}

(4b)

Computing
P_{v,b}
for each
VNF
gives us knowledge of where
VNFs
are likely to be needed. We can exploit such knowledge during service deployment operations to prepare
VNFs
in time, reducing users’ unsuccessful packets.

V-B

Estimating probablities

To
estimate the probabilities
P(b\,|\,l)
and
f(L)
needed in equation (
4a
) in a real system, we employ the inference chain shown in
Figure 5
. Forecasting of user location is an autoregressive process where, given an input of the size of the horizon
h
containing the last
h
positions, the model outputs the next location. This process is repeated by sliding the input window and containing the previous cycle’s output, resulting in a prediction of a path
L
.

But users’ connection to
BSs
does not solely depend on their location: it is heavily influenced by randomness and other environmental factors. Consequently, we cannot expect to be exact in predicting to which
BS
a user will connect given its position. Therefore, we use a classifier to output, given the user’s location, the probabilities of one
BS
to be a candidate for the handover. Finally, resulting probabilities are used in equations (
4a
) to predict the likelihood of a service being needed at a certain cell within the time horizon, influencing service placement decisions.

We consider COST 231 Hata loss model

P_{u}=P_{b}\frac{\absolutevalue{h}^{2}}{L_{p}},

(6)

|h|^{2}

The data rate achievable by user

\mu_{u}=B\>\log_{2}(1+\frac{P_{u}}{N}),

with

VI-E

Comparison cases

We use the previously discussed modeling and experimental assumptions in a simulator. Parameters for the performed simulations can be found in
Table III
.
We execute simulations of 10 minutes simulated time, during which 50 users move in the network using the same mobility model, uniformly requiring one of 10 available
VNFs
.
We compare the SWAVES approach to three others:

(i)

A static heuristic;

(ii)

A reactive heuristic;

(iii)

An oracle.

Literature on this topic has thoroughly explored the problem of service placement using a variety of methods and solvers. With the aim of demonstrating the impact our approach has on the provisioning itself, we perform
VNF
placement and user connection decisions using an
Integer Linear Programming (ILP)
solver. As such, the approaches differ in
when
the problems are solved, and whether future knowledge is used.

The
static heuristic
solves the placement problem only once. In this case, no
VNF
changes state, and no user is ever migrated between instances throughout the simulation. It is intended as almost trivial to realize, but likely a worst-case comparison. The
reactive heuristic
solves the problem s every time an event takes place. Two types of events are used as triggers: a
VNF
is ready or a handover happens. SWAVES works the same, but periodically perform forecasts for all users, and considers them for the placement and connections problems. Finally,
the oracle
acts the same as SWAVES, but it has perfect future knowledge.

These approaches are compared on the ratio of unsuccessful packets under different network conditions, like the randomness of user movement and the delay limit required by
VNFs
. In the following sections, results for the described approaches are shown. To improve readability, each plot shows only one oracle line, obtained from simulations with
\alpha=0.9
.

must
be located where users are connected.
Due to the time
VNFs
take to change state, it is paramount for the user to find the required
VNF
already in a state that is as close as possible to running at the new
EC
.
Figure 7
shows a cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the ratio of unsuccessful packets per user.

The static heuristic shows that, in such conditions, up to 90% packets can be unsuccessful. The reactive heuristic shows how constantly adapting the service location can enhance this metric by up to two orders of magnitude with respect to the static heuristic.
The reactive heuristic not having any information of where the user may be headed results in setting up
VNFs
in advanced enough states in nearby
ECs
. This comes at the cost of filling
ECs
with unused
VNFs
, requiring time to shut them down and remove them to make space for useful ones; but these still need additional time to be started.

This is the main advantage of SWAVES. Thanks to forecasting, it is possible to use resources in high-likelihood
ECs
, ensuring that
VNFs
are prepared only where there is a reasonable chance for them to be useful.
Intuitively, SWAVES’s performance is affected by the randomness of user movement. Still, the main gain of SWAVES is restricting where
VNFs
are prepared, resulting in at most 15% difference between SWAVES runs with different
\alpha
, converging in the best and worst case. Likewise, the gap between SWAVES and the oracle is between 10% and 20%, depending on
\alpha
.
This gap is given by handovers being not deterministic, so even if SWAVES can predict the eligible
BSs
for the users to connect to, the uncertainty results in more resource consumption from which the oracle does not suffer.

Medium delay limit case

In this scenario, the acceptable delay requirement is relaxed compared to the previous case. In particular, services can now tolerate a
E2E
delay of up to 2 ms. Now
VNFs
do not
need
to be deployed at the exact location where users are connected. Conversely, user requests comply with such a delay limit when connected to
ECs
that are part of the same
M_{1}
cluster.

The distance between SWAVES and oracle can be attributed again to the randomness of the handover process, which could connect users to

VII
Conclusions & Outlook

Our approach, SWAVES, is able to leverage forecasts of user movement to prepare
VNFs
at
the right place and time
, sensibly reducing service disruption during their movement.
This is possible thanks to several factors. First, knowing likely future users connection helps freeing resources and spending less time in
VNF
deployment.
This allows users to fully exploit the proximity to the required
VNF
, ensuring compliance with their strict
QoS
requirements. Consequently, users experience disruptions mostly only due to stateful migrations, reducing unsuccessful packets by orders of magnitude with respect to other heuristics.
Even when forecasts are inaccurate, SWAVES has been proven beneficial in enforcing service proximity to fewer cells, leading to an overall improvement concerning the reactive heuristic.
In this paper, the effectiveness of SWAVES in the context of single
VNF
deployment and the importance of addressing the service lifecycle during service placement operations have been addressed.
Future work on SWAVES will include the application to service chains and graphs, focusing on more complex service deployment scenarios.
