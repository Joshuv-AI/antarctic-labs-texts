Factors influencing the turnover intention for disease control and prevention workers in Northeast China: an empirical analysis based on logistic-ISM model
Authors: Ruiqian Zhuge, Yanping Wang, Yiran Gao, Qunkai Wang, Yuxuan Wang, Nan Meng, Yu Cui, Qiujin Huang, Kexin Wang, Qunhong Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to determine the current turnover intention among workers at Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDCs) in Northeast China and to investigate the factors contributing to this phenomenon.Materials and methodsThe cross-sectional study was conducted in May 2023 across four northeastern provinces of China. The study included a total of 11,912 valid participants who were CDC workers selected using a stratified cluster sampling method. The study assessed demographics, turnover intention, work resources, work perceptions, and psychological support through online questionnaires. The binary logistic regression analysis identified the factors associated with turnover intention for CDC workers, while the Interpretative Structural Modelling (ISM) revealed the hierarchical relationship between the influencing factors.ResultsThe study found that 28.8% of the respondents reported high turnover intention. The binary logistic regression suggested that the risk factors of turnover intention among employees included gender, age, education, and several work-related, organizational, and psychological factors. The work-related factors were daily working hours, job burnout, and role overload. The organizational factor was unit support for training, unit’s infrastructure conditions, leadership style, remuneration package and performance appraisal and promotion system. The psychological support factors were family support, professional identity, and organizational commitment. The ISM analysis results showed that the primary factors influencing CDC workers’ intention to leave were internally connected and grouped into four categories according to their level of impact: surface, transition, deep, and essential factors. Notably, the essential factors were professional identity and organizational commitment.ConclusionNearly one-third of the respondents expressed a strong desire to resign from their employment. Turnover intention among CDC workers was subject to diverse influences. Early identification, detection, and targeted multidisciplinary interventions should be introduced to address the array of factors that affect staff, with particular emphasis on boosting the professional identity and organizational commitment of CDC workers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11738-x.

Introduction

Turnover intention is a serious issue among workers of the Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) worldwide, as it threatens the quality and stability of public health services. [1, 2] 47% of state and 39% of local public health workers in the US governmental sector intended to retire or quit their jobs. [2, 3] In Korea, the turnover rate of field epidemiologists was 40% in the 2016–2017 cohort [4]. Similarly, Ethiopia is also facing a high turnover rate among its healthcare professionals. A study in Addis Ababa showed that the top five departments with the highest termination rates were nursing (15.6%), internal medicine (12.5%), public health (10.4%), pediatrics (9.4%), and surgery (9.4%) [5]. The global shortage of healthcare workers is projected to increase to 12.9 million by 2035 [1].

Disease prevention and control has played a significant role in increasing China’s average life expectancy by 26.6 years between 1950 and 2010, contributing 77.9% to this achievement [6]. Despite this critical contribution, high turnover has also been a prevalent issue among public health professionals in China. Data from the China Health Statistics Yearbook from 2007 to 2017 show that the number of CDC workers in China initially increased to 206,485 in 2005 following the SARS pandemic but then decreased to 194,425 by 2020 [7]. This period also witnessed a notable decline, particularly in the loss of professional and technical talents within the CDCs.

The issue of CDC workers’ turnover intention deserves special attention for several reasons. First, the frequent turnover of CDC workers can compromise the delivery of primary care services [8]. Since 2000, countries around the world have been strengthening their public health systems to address new infectious diseases, terrorism, and other public health threats [9]. The high turnover of CDC workers can hamper these efforts and endanger public health. Second, the loss of CDC workers can impair the functionality of the public health system. High turnover among CDC workers can result in an insufficient supply of health services, which can limit people’s access to healthcare and undermine the quality and safety of their health [10]. Third, the adverse consequences of CDC workers’ turnover can include financial loss and poor organizational practices [11]. Previous studies have shown that the high turnover rate entails substantial managerial costs, such as replacement, recruitment, selection, and training of new staff, and loss of organizational knowledge [12–14].

According to the theory of planned behavior, behavioral intentions are reliable predictors of actual behavior [15]. Therefore, turnover intention is a more insightful measure than the turnover rate, as it precedes the act of resignation and has a stronger predictive capability [16]. A review by Steel and Ovalle found that several studies reported a correlation coefficient of 0.50 between turnover intention and actual turnover [17]. Another longitudinal study conducted among family physicians in England found that those with a strong intention to resign were 4.48 times more likely to actually resign after a period of 5 years than those without such intentions [18]. Employee intention to leave indicates potential issues such as low morale, absenteeism, or poor performance. By identifying the factors that contribute to employee’s intention to resign, organizations can take proactive measures to prevent or reduce turnover [8]. Thus, focusing on the factors that affect intention to resign, rather than on turnover itself, can yield more efficient results.

Many studies have revealed the influencing factors of turnover intention. Among them was occupational identity, which Van Dick et al. had found to be associated with lower turnover intention in a study of four different occupational groups. [19, 20] They had defined organizational identity as the perceived overlap between employees’ self-concept and the norms, values, and goals of the organization. Meanwhile, Griffiths’ meta-analysis had found that organizational commitment and team cohesion predicted lower staff turnover rates [21]. In addition, related studies had also found that turnover intention was influenced by the satisfaction of economic, material, and psychological rewards. [22, 23] However, some studies may have some limitations. First, the sample size in some studies is insufficient, which affects the validity of the results. Second, some studies may have only focused on factors like remuneration packages and job satisfaction, without considering other factors like career progression and working conditions. Third, some studies may have only described the correlation between the inclination to depart and specific factors, without examining the underlying mechanisms involved. Fourth, the conclusions of some outdated studies may no longer reflect the current situation and are not comparable. Moreover, there is limited data on the turnover intention of Chinese CDC workers.

In summary, this study aims to explore the turnover intention of Chinese CDC workers based on their self-reports and identify the potential factors associated with it. This study extends the existing research on turnover intention among CDC employees in China. Using a large sample size of 11,912 workers from CDCs in the Heilongjiang, Jilin, Liaoning, and Inner Mongolia Autonomous Regions, the study employed Logistic-ISM models to examine the determinants of CDC workers’ turnover intention and the hierarchical structure of influential factors. Understanding the factors that influence the intention of CDC workers to leave their job is crucial in the context of healthcare reforms that aim to strengthen grassroots health organizations in China. By doing so, it will help to ensure the success of healthcare reforms while retaining a skilled and dedicated workforce.

Methods

Data collection and sample size

This cross-sectional study was conducted through an online survey in May 2023. A stratified cluster sampling method was used to distribute electronic questionnaires to workers at provincial, municipal, and district levels in Heilongjiang Province, Jilin Province, Liaoning Province, and Inner Mongolia Autonomous Regions, excluding non-professionals. This research, which involved human participants, was approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Harbin Medical University. Before the start of the study, each participant provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards stated in the Declaration of Helsinki (2008). Volunteers were recruited to participate in the study, and they had the right to withdraw from the study at any time. Anonymity was ensured by using anonymous questionnaires to collect survey data, thus protecting the privacy of the participants. A total of 12,150 CDC workers completed the questionnaire. The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) participants volunteered to participate; (2) participants had read and acknowledged the informed consent form on the front page of the online questionnaire; (3) participants provided assurances about the accuracy of the information they provided. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) multiple unanswered items in the questionnaire; (2) contradictions in the responses; (3) completion times that were too short or too long. After excluding invalid data (n = 238), the final sample consisted of 11,912 participants (validity rate: 98.04%). The validity of the questionnaire was tested by the factor analysis function of SPSS26.0 software, and the KMO value was 0.934, indicating that the validity was high and suitable for factor analysis.

Variables

Outcome Variable

The primary outcome measure was the turnover intention among CDC workers. To assess the level of turnover intention, each participant completed a self-reporting questionnaire. The question asked was, “Currently, do you have a desire to leave your work unit or not work at the CDC?” with responses rated on a five-point Likert scale (ranging from 1 = Not at all likely to 5 = Very likely). For the purposes of logistic regression modeling, and in conjunction with a comprehensive literature review and expert panel input, these options were ultimately dichotomized into two categories: “high willingness” (scores of 4–5) or “low willingness” (scores of 1–3).

Explanatory variable

The independent variables incorporated in the model included demographic characteristics (age, gender, educational background, organizational level), work-related factors (daily working hours, job burnout, role overload), work capacity (knowledge stock, public health professional competence), development of a learning organization (unit support for training, satisfaction with pre-service training, satisfaction with on-the-job training, frequency of knowledge or skills updating activities, frequency of collective learning or group discussion activities), working conditions (unit’s information technology construction, unit’s infrastructure conditions), leadership management (leadership style, management style), work rewards (remuneration package, performance appraisal and promotion system), and psychological support (family support, team cohesion, professional identity, organizational commitment).

Age was divided into two categories: those 40 years or younger and those older than 40 years. Education level was considered an ordered categorical variable, ranging from college or lower, to bachelor’s degree, to master’s degree. Unit level was classified into three groups: provincial, municipal, and district/county level. Daily working hours were grouped into two categories: those working 8 h or less and those working more than 8 h. Job burnout and role overload were assessed using the question, “Do you feel burned out/overloaded during your workday?” with response options measured on a 5-point Likert scale (never, rarely, occasionally, often, daily), which were then collapsed into two groups: “no” (never, rarely, and occasionally) and “yes” (often and daily). The frequency of knowledge or skills updating activities and collective learning or group discussion activities were initially measured using a 5-point Likert scale (the higher the score, the higher the frequency), later categorized into two groups: “low” (score of 3 or less) and “high” (score above 3). The assessment question for psychological support was “Please rate your family support/team cohesion/professional identity/organizational commitment on a scale of 1–10, with a higher score indicating better alignment with your situation.” In the modelling process, response scores were divided into “low” and “high” groups based on the average score of participants.

All remaining variables, including knowledge stock, professional competence in public health, unit support for training, satisfaction with pre-service training, satisfaction with on-the-job training, unit’s information technology construction, unit’s infrastructure conditions, leadership style, management style, remuneration package, performance appraisal and promotion system, were assessed in terms of participants’ level of satisfaction. This was initially measured on a 5-point Likert scale (very dissatisfied, dissatisfied, average, more satisfied, very satisfied), which was then divided into two groups: “dissatisfied” (responses of very dissatisfied, dissatisfied and average) and “satisfied” (responses of more satisfied and very satisfied). To assess work competence, we asked the question, “Are you satisfied with your knowledge base/professional competence in public health?” To assess leadership and management styles, we asked the question, “Are you satisfied with the leadership/management style of your unit?” where leadership style is defined as a leader’s pattern of behavior linked to work environment, experience and personality, and management style is defined as a general term for the principles and ways in which the unit adheres to the management process. (Please refer to the supplementary material for more information on the questionnaire).

Statistical analysis

In this study, we employed two statistical methods: descriptive statistics and binary logistic regression analyses were performed on the survey data using SPSS V.27.0, while interpretative structural models (ISM) were constructed using MATLAB V.9.0. First, we used the χ2 test to examine the association between CDC workers’ turnover intention and each predictor variable. Variables that were significant in the χ2 test were then included in the regression analysis. Second, we used binary logistic regression models to investigate the relationship between turnover intention and the study variables. We adopted a significance level of p < 0.05 to indicate statistical significance. To assess the model fit, we applied the Durbin-McDonald Watson test. We estimated odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to evaluate the effect of the predictors on CDC workers’ turnover intention. Finally, we used ISM to classify the significant variables in all binary logistic regression models into different levels of impact.

The binary logistic regression model is useful in identifying the key factors that affect the turnover intention of CDC workers. However, it does not reveal the interconnections and hierarchical structure among these factors. To overcome this limitation, we used the ISM to explore the significant factors identified by the binary logistic regression model. The specific steps involved in this process are as follows:

Results

Self-reported turnover intention and participant characteristics

Out of the 11,912 respondents, 3,436 (28.8%) CDC workers expressed a high turnover intention. Table 1 shows the general characteristics of the participants. Among the 11,912 interviewees, 8,036 were women, accounting for more than two-thirds (67.5%). The age distribution was relatively balanced, with 54.9% of individuals being under 40 years old. The education level was mainly bachelor’s degree (62.1%) and 8.7% held a master’s degree or higher. The respondents’ work experience was distributed as follows: 32.2% worked for 0–5 years, 12.9% worked for 6–10 years, 10.4% worked for 11–15 years, and 44.5% worked for 16 years or more. Univariate analysis revealed that all factors in the table except for the unit level had a significant association with the participants’ turnover intention (p < 0.05).

Discussion

In this study, over 28% of the participants expressed a high intention to turnover. The results of this study showed that the factors influencing the turnover intention of CDC workers were a typical multi-step structural model. The mechanism of turnover intention was as follows: surface factors directly drove CDC workers to generate turnover intention, intermediate factors and deep factors indirectly influenced surface factors and ultimately acted on the target layer, and essential root factors were the most fundamental causes for CDC workers to generate turnover intention. In general, the turnover intention of CDC workers could be summarized by two main paths.

“Psychological drive-cultural competence and experience accumulation-job promotion-economic return” constituted the first path influencing the turnover intention of CDC workers. Specifically, it consisted of professional identity, organizational commitment → gender, age, educational background → performance appraisal and promotion system → remuneration package and other factors. In this path, remuneration package was the basic factor, which reflected the material reward for the work value and achievement of CDC workers [24]. Remuneration package was constrained by the performance appraisal and promotion system, which determined and regulated their remuneration level and growth rate by considering the work quality and quantity of CDC workers, as well as their positions and responsibilities within the organization. The fairness and rationality of the performance appraisal and promotion system depended to a certain extent on the full consideration of the basic cultural competence and experience accumulation of CDC workers. [20, 21] Gender, age and educational background were objective personal characteristics, which reflected the ability and experience of CDC workers, and also affected their work attitude and behavior. For example, gender might lead to different work preferences and styles of CDC workers, age might affect their work enthusiasm and stability, and educational background might affect their work skills and knowledge. These factors might cause differences in performance level and promotion potential, so the performance appraisal and promotion system should distinguish them appropriately, so as to evaluate and reward the work performance and contribution of CDC workers fairly and effectively. Finally, the deep psychological identification promoted the improvement of the basic cultural competence and experience accumulation, because it stimulated the learning intention, work involvement and career development of CDC workers. [22, 23] Professional identity and organizational commitment determined whether CDC workers were satisfied and proud of their work, whether they identified and supported the goals and values of their organization [23]. When CDC workers had low professional identity and organizational commitment, they were more likely to be dissatisfied and disloyal to their work, thus generating the idea of leaving.

The second path was “psychological drive-leadership support-environmental support-workload reduction and recognition by others”, which consisted of professional identity and organizational commitment → leadership style → unit infrastructure conditions, unit support for training → role overload, daily working hours and family support and other factors. In this path, role overload, daily working hours and family support were the key factors influencing the turnover intention of CDC workers, which reflected the degree of work pressure and psychological support of CDC workers. Workload reduction and recognition by others were moderated by environmental support, which determined the work efficiency and work satisfaction of CDC workers. Environmental support included unit infrastructure conditions and unit support for training, which reflected the degree of investment and improvement of the unit on the work conditions and work ability of CDC workers. Unit infrastructure conditions involved the workplace, equipment, materials and other aspects of CDC workers, which affected their work safety and convenience. Unit support for training involved the training opportunities, training content, training effect and other aspects of CDC workers, which affected their work skills and innovation. And the level of environmental support was influenced by leadership support. Leadership style determined to a certain extent the allocation and optimization of the work environment and work resources of the unit for CDC workers, and also affected their work trust, work participation and work responsibility. Finally, psychological drive was the motivational factor, when CDC workers had high professional identity and organizational commitment, they were more willing to work hard and loyal for their work and organization, thus obtaining the support of leadership and environment, and then achieving workload reduction and recognition by others. [7–25]

Based on the regression analysis, among the surface factors, the remuneration package emerged as the most significant factor. Previous studies have indicated that if all workers were satisfied with their wages and job conditions, only 7.4% would consider leaving, which is less than half of the current rate of 17.7% [24]. Dissatisfaction with salary level can significantly affect employees’ work attitudes and behaviors, as observed in the study by GAN et al. [12] When employees’ actual income falls below their expected level and personal expectations are not met, they experience a sense of imbalance, resulting in decreased work motivation and an increased tendency to leave. In 2009, China implemented a performance-based pay system reform for CDC workers that has been in operation for over a decade. However, despite its implementation, the reform has not been successful in retaining the workforce. In 2012, local CDCs faced a government funding shortfall of 28.9%. Additionally, the cancellation of administrative fees, including the “three fees” such as preventive examination fees, health examination fees, and health prevention service fees in 2017, further impacted the income of CDC workers. According to the Social Comparison Theory, when staff teams compare themselves with others, it influences their career choices [7]. In China, there is a significant income disparity between CDC workers and clinicians. CDC workers, especially at the grassroots level, are treated less favorably than their counterparts in public hospitals. It is noteworthy that while salary plays a role, it is not the sole determinant of turnover and job satisfaction. CDC managers interested in improving retention and job satisfaction should focus on enhancing employee satisfaction in areas such as leadership, organizational commitment, and professional identity.

The ISM model suggests that professional identity and organizational commitment are the key factors influencing CDC workers’ turnover intention. According to the iceberg model theory, these factors can be divided into two categories: “above the ice” factors that are easily observable, understandable, and measurable, such as salary, benefits, and working conditions; and “below the ice” factors that are more difficult to observe and measure, often related to intrinsic motivation, self-value, and personal traits. Current evidence indicates that intrinsic motivation plays a vital role in job retention [25]. Professional identity can effectively reduce burnout and job stress among staff members. Moreover, research has suggested that professional identity is important for understanding one’s career and decision-making processes, as individuals with a strong sense of professional identity tend to take pride in their work, achieve personal satisfaction, and experience career growth [26]. Similarly, the research indicates that there is a negative correlation between organizational commitment and the intention of CDC workers to leave their jobs. This can be explained by the fact that workers who feel a sense of belonging and responsibility towards their organization are more likely to stay with their current unit. [19–27] Organizational commitment creates a positive emotional experience for CDC workers and intrinsically motivates their career development. [28, 29]

Our research reveals that CDC workers’ turnover intention varies depending on individual characteristics. First, male CDC workers have a stronger turnover intention than female ones. [30, 31] This may be attributed to men’s higher levels of achievement motivation and risk-taking [27, 32]. Moreover, in the traditional Chinese culture, men often bear more family and social obligations than women, and they may also seek more financial rewards, which motivates them to look for another job [33]. Second, CDC workers with higher education levels tend to have a higher turnover intention. Higher education is associated with job expectations, and those with higher education may have a mismatch between their expectations and their actual job roles, resulting in dissatisfaction and lower intrinsic job satisfaction [34]. Third, our findings suggest that younger CDC workers are more prone to have higher turnover intentions, a pattern that has also been observed in earlier research [35]. Compared to their older counterparts with more experience and service, the younger workforce values more several attributes that significantly affect their job satisfaction and propensity to leave, such as personal growth and development opportunities, compensation levels, task clarity, job variety, and job autonomy. [36, 37] Additionally, research has indicated that older employees are less likely to quit their positions due to several factors. [38, 39] These factors include greater invested human capital, higher wages, increased autonomy, power, or status, more involvement in decision-making, and a clearer self-concept. These factors enhance job satisfaction and organizational commitment, which in turn reduces the desire for turnover [40–42].

Based on the Job Demands-Resources theory, employees who face a situation where their job demands surpass their available resources may experience a state of health deterioration. This implies that high job demands can make employees feel stressed and exhausted, leading them to withdraw from their job as a self-protection strategy. [43, 44] This could explain why employees with long working hours, job burnout, and role overload tend to have a higher intention to leave their job. Numerous studies have demonstrated that job burnout and role overload increase employees’ job stress and decrease their commitment to the organization, which can ultimately result in turnover [45]. However, employees who receive sufficient family support are less likely to quit their job. For instance, the spouses and parents of CDC workers can be supportive by taking care of their children and assuming more family responsibilities, reducing CDC workers’ work-family conflict and consequently lowering the probability of employees quitting their job.

Our research also confirms the influence of other factors on CDC workers’ turnover intention. First, leadership style plays a vital role in shaping CDC workers’ turnover intention. [46, 47] Leadership style reflects how a leader behaves and performs tasks, which can affect the psychological needs of employees to different degrees [48]. Previous studies have indicated that a suitable leadership style that creates a harmonious organizational atmosphere can increase employees’ organizational commitment and trust in the unit, effectively lowering their turnover intention. [49, 50]

Second, unit support for training has a positive effect on reducing turnover [51]. By undergoing training, CDC workers can gain enhanced professional knowledge and skills and improve work efficiency and quality. [52, 53]It is noteworthy that according to a qualitative survey conducted by the CDC, field epidemiology training programmes were introduced in China in 2001. While these programmes have been successful in many regions, the effectiveness and quality of these training programmes vary across regions [9]. The main reasons for the lack of success in this area include limited financial support, insufficient leadership attention, inadequate teacher resources, and only partial participation in field practicum training due to excessive daily workload [54].

Third, our findings show that good infrastructure conditions within units contribute to reducing the turnover intention of CDC workers [55]. Comfortable office spaces, suitable air quality, and the necessary working equipment and resources needed by CDC workers help in reducing workload and burnout, enhancing work efficiency, and lowering the willingness to leave. Following the 2003 SARS epidemic, the Chinese government invested 10 billion yuan in rebuilding CDC facilities [56]. However, many studies have revealed regional disparities in the distribution of resources and the working environment of CDCs, which may be attributed to uneven levels of economic development and infrastructure [57]. Therefore, the government should increase support for impoverished county-level CDCs and enhance their ability to attract talent through more supportive policies, transfer payments from the central government, enhanced salaries and special subsidies, and improved management. Moreover, the government should boost investment in instruments and equipment for CDCs, upgrade the hardware facilities of CDCs, and rationalize the allocation of software facilities such as intelligent and information-based systems.

Fourth, our results suggest that a reasonable performance appraisal and promotion system contribute to reducing the turnover intention [58]. This may be attributed to narrow promotion channels and an unreasonable appraisal system that fail to guarantee CDC workers’ rewarding performance and implicitly reduce their income level, leading to a higher turnover intention. Additionally, Maslow’s hierarchy of needs theory categorizes human needs from the lowest to highest as physiological needs, safety needs, belonging and love needs, respect needs, and self-actualization needs [59]. Title promotion can elevate CDC workers’ status, reflecting a direct increase in their self-worth, and better satisfies the higher-order needs of CDC workers.

Based on our findings, we recommend the following actions: (1) CDC institutions should create a positive work environment and establish a fair system to protect employee welfare, thereby increasing professional identity and organizational commitment. (2) Health administrative bodies should offer specific provisions to safeguard CDC workers’ labor rights, such as ensuring reasonable workloads and working hours. (3) Employers must consider the unique characteristics of CDC workers and provide opportunities for their career advancement while also meeting their legitimate needs.

Study limitations and future prospects

Despite the numerous findings from this investigation, there are several noteworthy limitations. Firstly, the data on turnover intention was collected through a self-report questionnaire administered to CDC workers. It is possible that reported intentions to leave and actual departures differ, leading to potential biases in our estimates if certain groups tend to overestimate or underestimate their true intentions more than others. Secondly, all participants in this study were recruited from Heilongjiang province, Jilin province, Liaoning province, and Inner Mongolia Autonomous Regions in China. Therefore, the findings of this study may not be generalizable to populations in other geographical areas. Thirdly, given that this was a cross-sectional study, we were unable to establish causal relationships based on the results. Hence, it is imperative to interpret the results of this investigation with caution, and future research should aim to expand its geographical scope and incorporate additional methodologies to support the findings of the self-assessment report.

Conclusion

Based on research data from CDCs in four northeastern provinces of China, this study utilizes binary logistic models and ISM analysis to explore the influencing factors of CDC workers’ turnover intention and their stratification structure. Our findings reveal that nearly one-third of CDC workers in these provinces exhibit a high turnover intention. This intention is driven by a range of factors, including gender, age, educational background, daily working hours, job burnout, role overload, unit support for training, unit’s infrastructure conditions, leadership style, remuneration package, performance appraisal and promotion system, family support, professional identity, and organizational commitment. The study uncovers thirteen key factors that impact CDC workers’ intention to quit their jobs, which are both interconnected and independent. These factors are categorized into four levels: surface factors, transition factors, deep factors, and essential factors. The essential factors that drive the turnover intention are professional identity and organizational commitment. The study recommends that the government and CDCs should collaborate to provide CDC workers with adequate welfare benefits. Additionally, psychological support should be offered to address the psychological needs of CDC workers and reduce their willingness to leave their jobs.
