Ex vivo bradykinin as a functional biomarker for angioedema with normal C1-inhibitor
Authors: Jinguo Chen, J. Joanna Yu, Joseph Chiao, Yunkou Wu, Mark D. Scarupa, Lili Wan, H. Henry Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBradykinin-mediated angioedema (AE-BK) remains diagnostically challenging particularly in patients with normal C1-inhibitor, where diagnosis primarily relies on clinical criteria. Routine laboratory tests can identify complement deficiency in hereditary angioedema due to C1-inhibitor deficiency (HAE-C1INH) but not directly assess kallikrein-kinin system dysregulation underlying these conditions.ObjectiveWe sought to investigate potential associations between BK generation following ex vivo cold activation and clinical phenotypes of AE-BK, with or without C1-inhibitor deficiency.MethodsFunctional total BK generation ex vivo was quantified using a validated LC-MS/MS assay in cold-activated EDTA blood samples from 181 individuals: 46 with HAE-C1INH, 68 with recurrent angioedema and normal C1-inhibitor clinically suspected to be AE-BK, and 67 controls. A subset of patients in both angioedema groups was receiving long-term prophylaxis with plasma kallikrein inhibitors.ResultsElevated total BK following ex vivo activation was observed in most patients not on long-term prophylaxis (90.3% of HAE-C1INH; 57.4% of suspected AE-BK). Serial measurements demonstrated marked BK increases during acute angioedema episodes and stable levels during remission. Total BK levels correlated with plasma kallikrein activity and were reduced toward the reference range in patients receiving long-term prophylaxis with plasma kallikrein inhibitor therapy in both AE-BK subtypes.ConclusionsThe measurement of ex vivo BK generation by LC-MS/MS is associated with clinical features of AE-BK and with pharmacologic inhibition of kallikrein-kinin system. These observations suggest that this assay may have a role in pathophysiology-based patient stratification and treatment monitoring for AE-BK, though further prospective multicenter studies are warranted to clarify its diagnostic performance and clinical utility.

Angioedema (AE) is characterized by transient, localized swelling of subcutaneous or submucosal tissues resulting from increased vascular permeability, as defined by the 2023 international DANCE (Definition, acronyms, nomenclature, and classification of angioedema) consensus.1 The 2 most common subtypes are mast cell–mediated AE and bradykinin-mediated angioedema (AE-BK).1,2 Differentiating these subtypes is critically important, because AE-BK carries a 45-fold higher mortality risk, primarily from laryngeal edema and asphyxiation.3 Tragically, diagnostic delays are common, and patients with hereditary angioedema due to C1 esterase inhibitor (C1INH) deficiency (HAE-C1INH) often wait 3.2 to 13.7 years for a correct diagnosis.4,5 These delays contribute to preventable mortality, underscored by a Brazilian study in which 87% of fatal cases were undiagnosed before death.6

HAE-C1INH is the prototype of AE-BK. Its pathology is not driven by complement dysfunction, but by the dysregulated activation of the contact and kallikrein-kinin system (KKS).7, 8, 9 This system—comprising coagulation factor XII, factor XI, plasma prekallikrein, and high-molecular-weight kininogen—generates BK when inadequately controlled. Although C1INH is the primary endogenous regulator, its deficiency alone is insufficient to cause angioedema; additional factors, including overexpression of the contact system, are required.10 In cases of angioedema with normal C1INH (nC1IHN), whether hereditary or nonhereditary, growing evidence indicates that the contact system / KKS can also be aberrantly activated.8,11,12 This shared pathophysiology highlights the necessity of assessing the contact system pathway in suspected AE-BK, yet current diagnostic practice relies on the complement system (C1INH antigen/function, C4, C1q, autoantibodies against C1INH)9 and, in selected cases, genetic testing.13 These approaches have notable limitations, including only 70% sensitivity for HAE-C1INH,14,15 and they fail entirely to diagnose AE-BK with normal C1INH.

Several emerging biomarkers, including soluble glycoprotein 120,11 cleaved high-molecular-weight kininogen,16 and a few inflammatory and microvascular endothelial cell-surface receptors,17,18 show promise but remain investigational and are not available for clinical use. Although at least 8 genes have been associated with HAE with normal C1INH (nC1INH), known variants account for only a minority of cases.12,19 Most patients with AE with nC1INH lack identifiable mutations and have no validated biochemical diagnostic markers. They do not have family history and are unresponsive to high-dose antihistamines. Their etiology is unknown and currently classified as AE-UNK (angioedema due to unknown cause).1

Direct measurement of BK has been historically challenging due to its extremely short half-life and susceptibility to ex vivo degradation. However, recent advances in LC-MS/MS now enable robust quantification of BK and key metabolites.20,21 Incorporation of ex vivo cold activation has further enhanced analytical performance, improving assay sensitivity by 100-fold while minimizing preanalytical variability related to specimen collection and handling.10

Contemporary treatment strategies for HAE-C1INH include on-demand and long-term prophylaxis (LTP) therapies.22 Most guidelines recommend individualized LTP across all HAE subtypes.23 A major challenge persists, however, because there is no validated biomarker to monitor the efficacy of these treatments.

We hypothesize that many AE-UNK cases represent AE-BK, and that total BK generation ex vivo following cold activation, quantified using a validated LC-MS/MS assay in cold-activated blood, serves as a useful tool for this condition. This study aimed to evaluate whether ex vivo BK generation is associated with clinical phenotypes of AE-BK and assesses its potential for monitoring therapeutic responses to LTP across AE-BK subtypes. To this end, 2 patient groups are included: individuals with HAE-C1INH and those with AE-UNK and nC1INH but clinically suspected AE-BK, operationally defined as AE-BK with nC1INH in this study.

Methods

Study subjects

This prospective, noninterventional diagnostic study was approved by the Salus Central Institutional Review Board (protocol: Virant-A0001). A total of 181 individuals were enrolled from an Angioedema Centers of Reference and Excellence–certified center: 114 patients with AE without urticaria and 67 control subjects with no history of AE. Written informed consent was obtained from the study participants or their legally authorized representatives.

Diagnostic criteria and patient classification

All patients with AE underwent standardized evaluation, including detailed clinical history, laboratory testing for C1INH protein levels and function, and genetic testing for SERPING1 variants by whole-exome sequencing where clinically indicated. A subset of patients with AE from both cohorts who were receiving long-term prophylaxis with plasma kallikrein inhibitors (LTP-PKIs) was specifically analyzed here. The effect of long-term prophylactic C1INH replacement therapy on BK levels is currently under investigation, and the findings will be reported separately when available.

Quantification of total BK

Whole blood samples were subjected to standardized cold incubation to induce ex vivo activation of the contact system, followed by LC-MS/MS quantification of BK and its metabolites as reported previously.10 The resulting total BK values reflect functional contact system reactivity under controlled ex vivo conditions rather than direct measurement of circulating in vivo BK concentrations. Accordingly, results are interpreted as a surrogate marker of KKS dysregulation. The procedure is described briefly as follows.

Sample preparation and cold activation

Whole blood was collected in EDTA tubes and aliquoted in duplicates. (Using paired frozen plasma samples, we obtained comparable results; data not shown.) To promote contact system activation ex vivo, samples were subjected to cold incubation at 4°C for 3 days. The reaction was stopped by ethanol precipitation.

Sample extraction

A stable isotope-labeled internal standard mix was added to each sample. BK peptides were then extracted with C18 solid-phase extraction cartridges.

LC-MS/MS analysis

BK and its key metabolites (BK1-5, BK1-7, BK1-8, and BK1-9) were quantified using a validated LC-MS/MS method on SCIEX Triple Quad 5500+ QTRAP MS/MS coupled with a Shimadzu Nexera 40 HPLC system (AB SCIEX, Framingham, Mass).10 Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Kinetex Biphenyl column, and analytes were detected using a scheduled Multiple Reaction Monitoring. An 8-point calibration curve (0.1-1000 ng/mL) and quality control samples were included in each run. All samples were run in duplicate.

Data analysis and reporting

Data were processed using Analyst and SCIEX OS software (AB SCIEX). Concentrations were calculated from standard curves after normalization with the internal standard. Total BK was defined as the sum of the concentrations of the 4 measured peptides (BK1-9, BK1-8, BK1-7, and BK1-5). The reference interval for total BK was established as 0.1 to 9.1 ng/mL, with values higher than 9.1 ng/mL considered elevated.

Plasma kallikrein activity assay

Plasma kallikrein activity (PKa) was measured using a modified chromogenic assay. To eliminate interference from activated factor XII and ensure specificity for plasma kallikrein, plasma samples were preincubated with a specific inhibitor, PKSI-527 (trans-4-aminomethylcyclohexanecarbonyl-phenylalanyl-4-carboxymethylanilide). The rate of substrate cleavage was measured by monitoring absorbance at 405 nm using a Spark microplate reader (Tecan, Morrisville, NC) and analyzed with Magellan software (Tecan). All samples were run in duplicate. The raw PKa (nmol/min/mL) was converted to final results (mU/mL) by a constant factor 1.92 as described in the literature.25

Statistical analysis

Numerical values are reported as mean ± SEM. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using Kruskal-Wallis test (nonparametric 1-way ANOVA), followed when appropriate by pairwise Wilcoxon rank sum test. Qualitative variables were compared between normal or elevated PKa and BK using Fisher exact test. A portion of the statistical analyses was performed using SRplot online platform (Shanghai Jiaotong University, China).26

Results

Total BK is elevated in both HAE-C1INH and AE-BK

Total BK levels were measured across 181 subjects categorized into 3 cohorts (Table I). All patients in the HAE-C1INH group had C1INH/C4 tests (45 of 46 with abnormal C1INH function) and genetic testing (45 of 46 with positive SERPING1 pathogenic mutations). Among patients with HAE-C1INH not receiving LTP, 90.3% (28 of 31) demonstrated elevated total BK, with a mean ± SEM level of 258.9 ± 35.2 ng/mL (Table II and Fig 1, A). Notably, this included 1 patient with a classic HAE-C1INH phenotype but no identifiable SERPING1 mutation who exhibited a BK level of 276.7 ng/mL, and 1 patient with a confirmed SERPING1 mutation but normal C1INH function and C4 levels who had a BK concentration of 235.9 ng/mL. AE-BK with unknown etiology group (n = 68) displayed a female predominance (79.4%), consistent with previous report in HAE-nC1INH study.8 Among 61 subjects in this cohort not on LTP, 57.4% exhibited elevated total BK levels, with a mean ± SEM of 135.8 ± 25.0 ng/mL, approximately 45-fold higher than that for controls (cutoff value, 9.1 ng/mL; Table II and Fig 1, A). Among the 18 subjects who had elevated BK levels and underwent genetic testing, no pathogenic SERPING1 mutations and none of 8 genes associated with HAE-nC1INH12 were identified. Variants of uncertain significance were identified in 2 individuals (Table I).

Acute attacks in AE-BK are associated with BK surges

To investigate BK dynamics during acute attacks, serial measurements were obtained in 2 patients with AE-BK with nC1INH. In both cases, total BK levels, which remained within the normal range (below 9.1 ng/mL) during asymptomatic periods, exhibited sharp surges of 330 ng/mL and 425 ng/mL during acute swelling episodes (Fig 1, B). This pattern of dynamic fluctuation mirrors the well-established pathophysiology seen in HAE-C1INH.27,28

Chronically stable elevation of total BK in HAE-C1INH even during remission

HAE-C1INH is recognized as a systemic activation process of the contact system with local manifestations.29 To investigate whether the contact system / KKS overactivation and BK production are persistent or transient, serial measurements of total BK were performed in 10 individuals with HAE-C1INH not on LTP during periods with no symptoms and signs of AE (remission). The results exhibited chronically elevated, yet stable total BK levels in peripheral blood over a 3-year period (Fig 1, D), supporting a model of sustained, aberrant systemic activation of the contact/KKS even outside acute attacks and underscoring the reliable diagnostic utility of total BK in AE-BK.

Total BK levels strongly correlate with PKa in AE-BK

Elevated BK levels may result from contact system activation, fibrinolysis, or impaired degradation. To investigate whether BK elevation was linked to excessive contact system activation, both BK and PKa were measured in 51 patients with AE-BK with nC1INH. A strong positive correlation was observed between PKa and BK levels (P < .001, Fig 2). LTP-PKIs normalize BK levels

PKIs provide sustained suppression of PKa throughout the dosing interval.22 Among patients with HAE-C1INH on LTP-PKIs (n = 15) evaluated during attack-free visits, 80.0% (12 of 15) achieved near-normal BK levels (mean ± SEM, 14.1 ± 0.4 ng/mL), representing a significant reduction compared with untreated patients (P < .001; Table II and Fig 1, A). Two individuals with persistently elevated total BK levels (123.7 and 276.7) after treatment had documented histories of medication nonadherence.

A similar effect was observed in the AE-BK with nC1INH cohort, where 85.7% (6 of 7) of patients on LTP-PKIs achieved complete normalization of BK (mean ± SEM, 4.3 ± 0.4 ng/mL). In both diagnostic groups, paired before-and-after analyses from representative individuals demonstrated a clear temporal association between LTP-PKI initiation and reduction of total BK toward the normal range (Fig 1, C).

Discussion

This single-center, hypothesis-generating study demonstrates that ex vivo BK generation following cold activation is associated with clinical features of AE-BK and with pharmacologic inhibition of PKa. Importantly, more than half the angioedema cases of unknown etiology (AE-UNK)—but clinically suspected AE-BK with nC1INH—showed a similar pattern of BK dysregulation with HAE-C1INH, directly supporting a BK-mediated mechanism. The effectiveness of LTP-PKIs in these patients further reinforces this pathogenic pathway. The performance metrics are intrinsically limited by the lack of a criterion-standard reference for AE-UNK and reflect the current diagnostic challenge.

Our findings contribute evidence relevant to an important gap in the current diagnostic paradigm for AE-BK.13 The conventional diagnostic approach, focused on complement markers (C1INH, C4), does not assess the core pathophysiology of contact system / KKS activation and is therefore insufficient for the full spectrum of AE-BK. This is particularly problematic for AE-UNK with nC1INH, where current diagnostics often yield inconclusive results.12 Here, direct BK quantification provides a potential tool, enabling clinicians to move beyond diagnostic uncertainty and objectively distinguish between BK-mediated and non–BK-mediated AE subtypes, a distinction with direct therapeutic implications.

It is well documented that contact system / KKS activation plays the central role in HAE-C1INH pathophysiology.9 In our study, the strong association between BK and PKa in patients with AE-BK with nC1INH solidified the shared pathophysiology with HAE-C1INH. Although the exact genetic etiology in many of these patients remains elusive, potentially involving de novo mutations or undiscovered genes,5,24 BK measurement offers an immediate and practical solution for pathophysiology-based patient stratification. It provides biochemical evidence of contact system / KKS dysregulation irrespective of the underlying genetic defect.

PKIs (eg, lanadelumab or berotralstat) are recommended for prophylaxis use to prevent HAE-C1INH acute attacks.30 Given the prolonged half-life (t½ = 14 days) of lanadelumab, PKa remains inhibited by more than 90% throughout the dosing interval.22 Its pharmacodynamic effect is expected to suppress BK generation. In this study, more than 80% of patients with HAE-C1INH and 85.7% of patients with AE-BK receiving LTP-PKIs outside of attack periods achieved normalization or near-normalization of BK levels. From a therapeutic standpoint, this is a pivotal finding. It confirms the on-target pharmacodynamic effect of these agents in both HAE-C1INH and AE-BK with nC1INH and strongly supports total BK as a reliable biomarker for monitoring treatment response. This provides an objective tool to guide personalized therapy and optimize long-term control, moving beyond reliance on subjective patient recall of attack frequency or symptoms.12

Our findings should be interpreted in light of the study’s limitations, including its single-center design, reliance on clinical case definitions in the absence of a criterion-standard biomarker, and the limited availability of paired pretreatment and acute-phase samples. Prospective studies with serial plasma collections are warranted to characterize the dynamic fluctuations of total BK in relation to disease activity and to define its clinical utility for long-term monitoring. In addition, in vivo suppression of BK in patients with nC1INH following LTP-PKI treatment remains to be clinically demonstrated to confirm its therapeutic relevance.

Conclusion

Measurement of total BK ex vivo after cold activation, a surrogate marker of contact system / KKS dysregulation, could help address the limitations of conventional complement testing in the diagnosis and management of AE-BK, especially for clinically suspected cases with unknown etiology (AE-UNK) and nC1INH. It enables pathophysiology-based stratification of AE-BK subtypes and provides a potential objective biomarker for monitoring treatment response.Clinical implicationsBK assay may help objectively diagnose AE-BK and monitor therapy, particularly for patients with nC1INH levels.
