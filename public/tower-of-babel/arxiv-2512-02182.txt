Efficient and Intuitive Two-Phase Validation Across Multiple Models via Principal Components
Sarah C. Lotspeich, Cole Manschot

Abstract
Two-phase sampling offers a cost-effective way to validate error-prone measurements in observational databases or randomized trials. Inexpensive or easy-to-obtain information is collected for the entire study in Phase I. Then, a subset of patients undergoes cost-intensive validation to collect more accurate data in Phase II. Critically, any Phase I variables can be used to strategically select the Phase II subset, often enriched for a particular model of interest. However, when balancing primary and secondary analyses in the same study, competing models and priorities can result in poorly defined objectives for the most informative Phase II sampling criterion. We propose an intuitive, easy-to-use solution that balances and prioritizes explaining the largest amount of variability across all models of interest. Using principal components to succinctly summarize the inherent variability of the error-prone covariates for all models. Then, we sample patients with the most “extreme” principal components (i.e., the smallest or largest values) for validation. Through simulations and an application to data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), we show that extreme tail sampling on the first principal component offers simultaneous efficiency gains across multiple models of interest relative to sampling for one specific model. Our proposed sampling strategy is implemented in the open-source R package, auditDesignR .

1
Introduction

Data are rarely collected for a single limited purpose in research. Observational databases, like electronic health records (EHR), compile troves of information on extensive samples, providing new opportunities to explore various novel scientific interests with a single data source. Randomized experiments, such as clinical trials, attempt to simultaneously address the primary endpoint alongside multiple secondary objectives. Moreover, data from both observational and randomized studies can require validation in their own ways.

1.1
Necessity of Validation in Observational Studies and Clinical Trials

For observational databases, data are generally routinely collected with other purposes (e.g., providing good clinical care) at the forefront. They may have errors resulting from a variety of sources, such as improper collection, transcription, or a lack of data cleaning. Thus, when these data are repurposed for research
[
25
]
, called “secondary uses,” they may exhibit measurement error or inaccuracies
[
12
]
and bias downstream analyses
[
11
]
. Manual chart reviews, wherein clinical experts compare research data to clinical source documents (like patients’ electronic charts), can help to validate observational datasets. This practice has gained popularity across disciplines, including global HIV research
[
7
,
17
]
and learning health systems
[
30
,
19
]
, but it is usually implausible to conduct chart reviews for entire studies. Partial validation (i.e., of a subset of patients) is more feasible.

Source document verification (data validation) has long been standard in clinical trials
[
39
]
, making data collected therein of generally high quality in terms of typos and other human errors. When data quality concerns are identified, there are methods to incorporate these audit findings into statistical models (e.g.,
[
31
]
). Further, under random assignment, the exposure of interest (generally a treatment) should be perfectly collected. Still, trial outcomes and biomarkers must be measured, and procedures and tests for them may be imperfect. Thus, validation in clinical trials can involve the re-measurement using more accurate instruments to account for measurement error
[
20
,
32
,
13
]
. Again, the more accurate measures are usually invasive or expensive, making them unreasonable to obtain for all trial participants.

For either observational or randomized studies, validation is necessary, and partial validation is often the most feasible option, given logistical constraints like time and funding. Partial validation studies are a special type of two-phase design
[
40
]
. In Phase I, inexpensive or readily available information is collected for all
N
patients in the study (e.g., from EHR or using less-accurate, less-invasive diagnostics). In Phase II, a subset of
n
patients (
n<N
) is chosen for validation, such that more accurate measurements are collected (e.g., from expert chart reviews or more-accurate, more-invasive testing). The sampling strategy for Phase II can leverage any information available for all patients in Phase I.

1.2
Informative Sampling Strategies for Partial Validation

Often, the Phase II subset is enriched for a particular model (e.g., to minimize the variance of a model coefficient of interest). This enrichment depends on the type of model and the estimation procedure (e.g., linear regression using multiple imputation to overcome missing validation data for the rest of Phase I). Essentially, the identification of which patients would be most informative to validate depends on what we plan to analyze about them and how.

For design-based estimators, like inverse probability weighting (IPW) and mean score, a variety of informative sampling designs exist for partial validation in a two-phase schema to minimize the variances of a particular model coefficient. Model-based estimators, like multiple imputation and maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), allow for more design flexibility than design-based estimators, like inverse probability weighting (IPW), since patients can be excluded from consideration (i.e., given zero validation sampling probability). Their variances do not lend themselves to intuitive, analytical solutions for optimal designs, but many have been proposed (e.g.,
[
22
,
34
,
3
,
18
]
). While not theoretically optimal, sampling the patients with extreme values of their outcome (called outcome dependent or extreme tail sampling [ETS]) is typically more efficient than simple random sampling (SRS)
[
9
,
1
,
26
,
2
,
42
,
15
]
. ETS on other quantities, including (weighted) residuals
[
19
,
34
,
21
]
and influence functions
[
3
]
, can offer sizable efficiency gains, as well.

1.3
Balancing Multiple Models and Objectives

Most informative two-phase sampling designs assume that estimating a single coefficient well (e.g., with great precision) is the study’s priority. Yet, post-hoc and exploratory analyses can result in competing objectives when designing a validation study. Balancing multiple analytical goals leads to an ill-defined optimization problem. Instead of a two-phase design with a clear objective, having multiple models and competing objectives complicates the optimal approach for validation sampling. Of course, a “silver bullet” would maximize all objectives (unrealistic in practice), whereas maximizing just one leads to a sub-optimal system solution. For instance, focusing on a single objective may give us good performance for that model at the detriment of all others. Various approaches exist to the multi-objective problem, but none have been applied for two-phase designs
[
6
]
.

We forgo the existing complex approaches, which are dependent upon functions of the moments of the coefficients of interest (often unknowable in practice) across multiple models, for a simpler approach. Principal component analysis (PCA), a popular nonparametric dimension reduction technique, offers a promising way to adapt existing informative sampling designs to accommodate multiple modeling objectives. The components succinctly summarize the variability within the data, and, conveniently, applying PCA on centered and scaled data (or on the data’s covariance matrix) inherently resolves the issue of differences in the variables’ scales. We explore how PCA can be used to capture information across multiple error-prone variables and guide targeted sampling strategies for partial validation.

1.4
National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey

To motivate the use of our proposed PCA-driven sampling strategy in practice, we consider the accuracy of food recall logs and dietary measurements
in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES)
[
10
]
. For the 2021–2023 cohort, two dietary recalls were conducted via phone interviews;
individuals reported the foods and drinks they had consumed within the past
24
hours.

While convenient, even the gold standard method of food recall logs is subject to recall bias, under- or over-reporting, and fallibility of portion size estimation (see
[
35
]
and references therein).
The additional step of converting food intake into nutrients can be complicated by product differences in nutrient density when individuals oversimplify food items (e.g., the amount of protein in “yogurt”).
We estimate the relationship between nutrient intake and relevant health outcomes, incorporating partial validation data.

1.5
Overview

In this paper, we propose a new sampling strategy for partial validation studies (two-phase designs) that balances multiple modeling objectives simultaneously. Validation sampling depends on the first principal component of all observed covariates, and the outcome variables for the multiple models may be shared or unique.
Our design is simple to implement and balances competing analytical objectives in an intuitive and easy-to-understand approach.
We compare the efficiency (i.e., the inverse of the variance) of our design under various measurement error scenarios and find it outperforms both SRS and ETS on a single covariate. In Section
2
, we outline our proposed validation study design and chosen estimator. In Section
3
, we present a robust collection of simulations showing the performance of our sampling strategy. In Section
4
, we consider our data application in the NHANES study. In Section
5
, we conclude with a discussion on the practicality and limitations of our approach.

Methods

2.1
Model and Data

Suppose we are conducting a study of
N
patients on whom
P
separate statistical models are of interest. For each model
j
, we have a continuous outcome
Y_{ji}
, an exposure
X_{ji}
, and a vector of other confounders
{\mathbf{Z}}_{ji}
(
j\in\{1,\dots,P\}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
). They are assumed to be related through the usual normal linear regression model:

Y_{ji}=\beta_{0j}+\beta_{1j}X_{ji}+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2j}{\mathbf{Z}}_{ji}+\epsilon_{ji},

(1)

where the random errors
\epsilon_{ji}
are independent and identically distributed following a normal distribution with mean
=0
and variance
=\sigma_{j}^{2}
. We are most interested in estimating
\beta_{1j}
from each model, but all the exposures interest
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}=(X_{i1},\dots,X_{iP})^{\top}
are difficult to measure.

Instead, we observe error-prone versions of these exposures
\boldsymbol{X}^{*}_{i}=\boldsymbol{X}_{i}+\boldsymbol{U}_{i}
for all
N
patients, where
\boldsymbol{U}_{i}=(U_{i1},\dots,U_{iP})^{\top}
is the vector of additive measurement errors for all
P
models. We are planning a partial validation study, such that
n
patients (
n<N
)
will
have their true covariates
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
measured. Let
R_{i}=1
if the
i
th subject is validated (i.e.,
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
is observed), and
R_{i}=0
otherwise. Under the two-phase design framework, we control the assignment of the validation indicator
R_{i}
. To satisfy the missing at random (MAR) assumption
[
16
]
for our ultimate analyses,
R_{i}
can depend only on information that is fully observed (i.e., the outcomes, error-prone exposures, and confounders). Without loss of generality, we will assume that if
R_{i}=1
, then
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
is fully observed (i.e., the covariates are fully validated).

We want to use these data to estimate the
P
separate models, all of which have equal priority in this study. In Section
2.2
, we discuss various validation sampling designs to select the subset of
n
patients for
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
collection. In Section
2.3
, we outline how multiple imputation can be used to estimate the models after only partially validating the study data (i.e., overcoming missing
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
for the
N-n
unvalidated patients). Throughout, our priority is to strategically design the validation study to promote the efficiency of the coefficient estimates
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{1}=(\hat{\beta}_{11},\dots,\hat{\beta}_{1P})^{\top}
, obtained via multiple imputation, accompanying the exposures
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
.

2.2
Validation Sampling Designs

Traditionally, targeted validation sampling approaches focus on a single model
j
, and the goal is to identify which
n
patients, if validated, would minimize the variance of
\hat{\beta}_{1j}
corresponding to the covariate
X_{ji}
in that model (e.g.,
[
30
,
19
,
13
,
22
,
34
,
3
,
18
,
43
]
).
While effective for improving the estimation of
\hat{\beta}_{1j}
, as designed, these strategies do not consider the estimation of the coefficients from the other models of interest.

Many cited works suggest that optimal designs for multiple coefficients are possible with appropriate optimality criteria, like D-optimality (e.g., minimizing the determinant of the variance-covariance matrix). However, most of these designs are already complex for a single coefficient; extending them to consider multiple simultaneously would be challenging.
Therefore, we propose a novel strategy for partial validation in a multi-objective study based on the ETS design (already a common strategy for single-objective studies) and PCA.

In exploratory analyses or feature creation, PCA is a common approach to dimension reduction. It seeks to explain variability in the original data through a smaller subset of newly created variables (called principal components or loadings). This property of explaining variability in a reduced dimension motivates our use of PCA in designing a validation study. We apply PCA to all the models’ error-prone exposures
\boldsymbol{X}^{*}
to create a single variable that captures the variability between them. In particular, we take the first principal component
PC_{1}^{*}
, which will always explain the most variability in
\boldsymbol{X}^{*}
out of all the estimated principal components. Then, we prioritize patients for validation based on
PC_{1}^{*}
, rather than focusing only on a single
X_{j}^{*}
or trying to balance all of
\boldsymbol{X}^{*}
simultaneously. This strategy balances the reduction in variance across all
P
models without adding computational burden, and it seamlessly integrates with existing single-objective validation sampling methods, like ETS.

In our simulations and application to NHANES, we consider three sampling strategies.
Simple random sampling (SRS):
The
n
patients are chosen from the original study of
N
completely at random (i.e., with equal probability). This design ignores all available information, making
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
missing completely at random
[
16
]
.

Extreme tail sampling on the
j
th model’s error-prone exposure (ETS-
X_{j}^{*}
):
The original
N
patients are ordered ascendingly by
X_{j}^{*}
, and those with the
n/2
largest and
n/2
smallest values are chosen for validation. In practice, the model
j
would be chosen a priori. For demonstration, we use
j=1
.
Extreme tail sampling on the first principal component of all error-prone exposures (ETS-
PC_{1}^{*}
):
The original
N
patients are ordered ascendingly by
PC_{1}^{*}
, and those with the
n/2
largest and
n/2
smallest values are chosen for validation.

The intuition behind ETS designs comes from the belief that extreme observations are most informative. For example, by validating patients following the ETS-
X_{1}^{*}
design, we correct the measurement error in the most extreme error-prone exposures and expect better estimation for the linear regression of
Y_{1}
on
X_{1}
(controlling for
{\mathbf{Z}}_{1}
) as these extreme values may be more influential.
Our ETS-
PC_{1}^{*}
design is similarly motivated.
After expressing the entirety of
\boldsymbol{X}^{*}
in a lower dimension (as
PC_{1}^{*}
), we seek to validate patients with the most extreme values of
\boldsymbol{X}^{*}
and improve estimation across all
P
linear regression models.

The advantages of the ETS-
PC_{1}^{*}
design hinge upon how much variability in
\boldsymbol{X}^{*}
is captured by
PC_{1}^{*}
. When the error-prone exposures
\boldsymbol{X}^{*}
are correlated,
PC_{1}^{*}
should perform well. Accordingly, we anticipate that the average variance across
\hat{\beta}_{1}
should be lower using ETS-
PC_{1}^{*}
than ETS-
X_{j}^{*}
or SRS. However, even with uncorrelated
\boldsymbol{X}^{*}
, most models’ estimates will have similar variability with ETS-
PC_{1}^{*}
, ETS-
X_{j}^{*}
, or SRS. (The one exception would be the
j
th model, which would see much better gains under an ETS-
X_{j}^{*}
design.)

2.3
Imputation Procedure

In our validated subset, we can estimate the
j
th linear regression model in (
1
) using the observed exposure
X_{ji}
directly. However, for the rest of the study, we will instead replace their missing
X_{ji}
value with an informative placeholder. Specifically, we adopt a single deterministic imputation framework in the Simulations (Section
3
), wherein unvalidated patients contribute predicted exposures
\widehat{X}_{ji}
to the analysis models. (Single imputation is computationally quicker than multiple, and we focused on the estimates’ empirical variability.) However, multiple imputation is preferred if estimated variances are desired (e.g., for inference), in which case the imputation models would need to be modified.
R
code for imputation under each design can be found online at
https://github.com/sarahlotspeich/ETS_PCA
.

2.3.1
Different Imputation Models Under Different Validation Study Designs

For an unvalidated patient,
\widehat{X}_{ji}
comes from an imputation model fit to the validation study. Any fully available information that helps estimate
X_{ji}
can be incorporated, and any variables used in the validation study design
must
be included
[
43
]
.

Under an SRS design, the imputed values are defined simply from the error-prone exposure
X_{ji}^{*}
and the additional confounders
{\mathbf{Z}}_{i}
(presumed to be error-free and fully observed) as

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{ji}=\widehat{\alpha}_{0j}+\widehat{\alpha}_{1j}X^{*}_{ji}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2j}{\mathbf{Z}}_{i}.

(2)

When
X_{ji}
is numeric, the estimated coefficients in (
2
) can be obtained via normal linear regression using only data from the validation subset. Of course, different regression models could instead be used to impute other types of exposures, like binary or categorical.

If the validation study was selected under an ETS-
X_{j}^{*}
design, the error-prone exposure
X_{j}^{*}
used in the design must be included in
all
exposures’ imputation models. Let
j^{\prime}
index which model was used in the design (
j^{\prime}\in\{1,\dots,P\}
). Then, the imputed values
\widehat{X}_{ji}
are:

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{ji}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\widehat{\alpha}_{0j}+\widehat{\alpha}_{1j}X^{*}_{ji}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2j}Z_{i}\text{ if }j=j^{\prime}\text{, and }\\
\widehat{\alpha}_{0j}+\widehat{\alpha}_{1j}X^{*}_{ji}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{2j}Z_{i}+\widehat{\alpha}_{3j}X_{ji}^{*}\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

(3)

Note that the coefficients will take on different values in the first versus second case in (
3
).

Under our proposed ETS-
PC_{1}^{*}
design, the first principal component
PC_{1}^{*}
must be included in all imputation models. The imputed values
\widehat{X}_{ji}
are then defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{ji}

\displaystyle=\widehat{\alpha}_{0j}+\widehat{\alpha}_{1j}X^{*}_{ji}+\widehat{\alpha}_{2j}Z_{i}+\widehat{\alpha}_{3j}PC^{*}_{i1}.

(4)

As with the SRS design, estimating the imputation models under either ETS design depends on the type of covariates, but can be done using standard regression techniques. For ease of notation,
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
are the estimated coefficients for any of the imputation models in (
2
)–(
4
).

Single imputation following (
2
)–(
4
) has been called
deterministic imputation
, since missing values are replaced with a single model prediction
\widehat{X}_{ji}
and treated as fixed in the subsequent analyses
[
8
]
. This approach can be implemented using existing software to (i) fit the imputation model and (ii) calculate the predictions. Alternatively, the
mice
function in the
mice

R
package can be used with argument
method = "norm.predict"

[
38
]
.

2.3.2
Modifications for Multiple Imputation

When using multiple imputation, the imputation models need to be modified one step further. Due to the stochastic nature of multiple imputation, which incorporates variability about the imputation procedure itself, the analysis model outcomes
Y_{j}
must be included in the imputation models. Otherwise, the imputation models will not be
congenial
with the analysis model, and the resulting multiple imputation estimates will be biased
[
8
,
23
]
.

There are various ways one might incorporate randomness into the multiple rounds of imputation.
One of the earliest and most common methods is to (i) resample the coefficients
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
from a distribution based on the fitted ones
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
in the validation study and (ii) generate the imputed values
\widehat{X}_{ji}
from the distribution dictated by
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

[
27
]
. For example, if
X_{ji}
is continuous, then in each imputation the parameters
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and
\tilde{\sigma}
will first be randomly drawn from a Normal distribution with mean
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and standard deviation
\hat{\sigma}
. Then, patients without
X_{ji}
will have their imputed values
\widehat{X}_{ji}
simulated from a Normal distribution with mean based on the imputation model (but evaluated at
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
) and standard deviation
\tilde{\sigma}
. This process is repeated many times, the analysis model is fit to each imputed dataset, and the estimates from each fitted model are ultimately pooled using Rubin’s rules
[
27
]
.

We can again use the
mice
R package for implementation. Specifically, the
mice
function can be used with argument
method = "norm"
, and it will include the outcome
Y_{j}
in the imputation model by default. After multiple rounds of imputation, only two more lines of code are needed to fit the analysis model to each and pool the resulting estimates.

4
Application to the Error-Prone Dietary Intake Exposures

Having established the relative advantages of our proposed ETS-
PC_{1}^{*}
validation study design in synthetic data (Section
3
), we further examined its performance in more realistic data. It was not feasible for us to conduct multiple validation studies under different design strategies in practice. Instead, we adopted a real-world dataset and added errors to it. Importantly, using real data, we no longer control the covariance between exposures, which heavily influences how best to select our validation subset. Comparing the statistical efficiency of
\widehat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}_{1}
between same-size validation studies with different design strategies is our primary objective.

4.1
About the Survey and Data

The NHANES includes various demographic, examination, laboratory, and questionnaire data.
We focus on the dietary interview portion, called the “What We Eat in America” (WWEIA), which is conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics in collaboration with the U.S. Department of Agriculture and the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. WWEIA captures individual-level daily consumption of
64
nutrients and food components and is an incredibly valuable source for dietary intake data
[
14
]
.

In the most recent WWEIA (
2021

2023

24

1

[
37
]
. The NHANES data set reports the cumulative intake of these nutrients for each recall.

4.3
Measurement Error in Dietary Intake

The NHANES dietary interview uses the United States Department of Agriculture Automated Multiple-Pass Method (AMPM) which is considered the most sophisticated methodology for 24-hour food recall
[
35
]
. ADPM begins with a list of all foods and beverages consumed, followed by probing questions, collecting the time and occasion of meals, detailed descriptions, and a final nudge for anything else consumed.

Two main limitations of the AMPM exist. First, it requires multiple days of recalls to obtain accurate
mean
measurements of intake, which is both costly and time-consuming. Second, it can induce self-report bias from psycho-social behaviors that result in under or over estimation of nutrient and energy intake, though underreporting is most common (see
[
35
]
and references therein).
Weighed food records are the gold standard for recording portions and the most accurate measurements, but has a high burden on participants.

[
41
]
.
Weighed food records require measuring all portions prior to consumption and any food waste that remains after a meal.
A large-scale study such as the NHANES cannot implement weighed food records due to the size, cost, and burden.
It is possible that measurement error may occur during the food recall and may be considered to impact assessment on the relationship of dietary intake with other biomarkers.

We treat the available dietary intake exposures from the NHANES dataset as the error-free versions
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4},X_{5})
.
Calcium (
X_{1}
) and saturated fat (
X_{3}

X_{5}

X_{2}

4.4
Partial Validation of Dietary Intake Covariates

For our purposes, all
N=2388

\boldsymbol{Y}=(Y_{1},Y_{2},Y_{3},Y_{4},Y_{5})

\boldsymbol{X}^{*}

n=250

observed. We considered three strategies to select this subset of validated patients.

First, we included the naive SRS design, where the
250
individuals were chosen for validation from the original sample of
2388
with equal probability (i.e., ignoring all information).
While theoretically valid, this design leads to the widest 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1}
. It leads to model estimates that are asymptotically consistent (i.e., converge in probability to the true coefficients) as both sample sizes (
N
and
n

Second, we considered an ETS-
X_{1}^{*}
design that focuses on the error-prone dietary intake exposure,
X_{1}^{*}
(calcium), from the first model. This approach might appeal if we were primarily interested in this model, so that we tailored our validation sampling strategy to focus on it. However, we may later decide that the other four models are also relevant, and then re-purpose the validation study data to estimate them.
The ETS-
X_{1}^{*}
design should offer the narrowest 95% CI for Model 1, but it may not even outperform SRS for Models 2–5.

Third, we applied our proposed ETS-

35\%

\boldsymbol{X}^{*}

(calcium) and the smallest of

For comparison, we calculated the average percent variability explained by

When varying the covariance structures for

Based on the correlation matrix for the NHANES data (Web Appendix), their covariance structure resembled the unequal dependence scenario; the

5
Discussion

Extreme tail sampling on the first principal component offers an accessible, intuitive way to balance multiple, competing analytical priorities when choosing which individuals to validate in studies with exposure measurement error. This strategy amplifies the benefits of a conventional single-objective two-phase design by avoiding the expenses of conducting separate, targeted validation samples for distinct models. It does so without incurring computational complexity, and we provide a user-friendly
R
package.

In simulations, our proposed sampling strategy improved the overall efficiency of the two-phase estimates across multiple models of interest under mild assumptions. The improvement was evident under varying covariance structures, measurement error severities, and validation proportions. Models where the error-prone exposure was strongly correlated with the others saw the greatest improvement. Still, when the exposures’ variability was relatively small compared to the outcomes’, any ETS procedure (on a single exposure or principal component) will see decreased benefits as the improved precision of covariates will not substantially improve the precision of the overall effect estimates.

We demonstrate the design’s advantages in multiple imputation analyses, where model specification is critical to achieving unbiased estimates. In practice, selection procedures (e.g., based on information criteria) can help select the “best-fitting” imputation model
[
28
]
. However, the advantages of sampling on the first principal component should also manifest if applied to other model-based estimation procedures
[
18
]
, which opens up possibilities for more robust analytical approaches, like semiparametric MLE
[
33
]
.

Toward broader applicability of the proposed sampling strategy, there are multiple interesting directions for future work. First, investigations into efficiency gains with other common outcome types (including binary, count, or time-to-event) are needed. Second, adopting existing informative or even optimal validation study designs for a dimension-reduced version of the error-prone exposures (like the first principal component) could offer further advantages. Third, modifications to other error settings (e.g., error-prone outcomes) are promising; in particular, the set of variables contributing to the PCA will need to be revisited. Finally, approaches considering more than the first principal component (e.g., splitting the validation study into
k
further subsamples, and choosing
n/k
patients from the extremes of the first through
k
th principal component).
