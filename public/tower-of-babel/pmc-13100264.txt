Atypical ocular and vascular manifestations of Klebsiella liver abscess syndrome
Authors: Sadia Ahsan, Karan Desai, George Horani, Ivan Mercado, Hasan Munshi, Utku Ekin, Yusuf Mubarak, Rahed Mohammed, Ali Zahran, Konstantinos Leou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Klebsiella liver abscess syndrome is an invasive syndrome associated with hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKp) characterized by pyogenic liver abscesses with potential for metastatic dissemination. This usual colonizer of the gastrointestinal flora has been the culprit of community-onset disease worldwide. We present a unique case of a 33-year-old male with poorly controlled diabetes who developed a liver abscess complicated by hepatic vein thrombophlebitis and exudative retinal detachment without vitritis. Although hypermucoviscosity testing was not performed, this clinical scenario of a community-onset invasive infection with ocular and vascular metastatic dissemination is characteristic of an hvKp strain. This case aims to broaden the recognized radiologic and clinical spectrum of this potentially devastating syndrome and highlights the critical role of imaging in diagnosing its atypical manifestations.

Introduction

Klebsiella liver abscess syndrome (KLAS) is defined by pyogenic liver abscess formation often caused by hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (hvKp), frequently associated with hematogenous and/or thromboembolic spread to distant organs. First described in East Asia, it is now reported globally in both immunocompetent and immunocompromised hosts. The syndrome most commonly metastasizes to the eyes, central nervous system, and lungs, with endogenous endophthalmitis representing its most vision-threatening complication [1]. We report a case of KLAS in a young diabetic patient presenting with several rare complications: exudative retinal detachment without vitritis and septic hepatic vein thrombosis resulting in septic pulmonary emboli and thromboembolic stroke syndrome.

Case presentation

A 33-year-old African male with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus (HbA1c 11.4%) presented with 1 week of fever, fatigue, epigastric pain, and jaundice. He denied alcohol use or recent travel but reported herbal tea consumption for glycemic control. On arrival, he was febrile with a temperature of 39.3°C, tachycardic, and normotensive; the physical exam was remarkable for jaundice and right upper quadrant tenderness.

Laboratory findings

Initial complete blood count showed a normal white blood cell (WBC) count of 10.2 × 10³/mm³ (4.5-11) and thrombocytopenia with platelet count 68 × 10³/mm³ (140-440); however, later developed worsening leukocytosis with WBC counts up to 25.2 × 10³/mm³ and nadir platelet count 47 K/mm³. Complete metabolic panel revealed hyponatremia 122 mEq/L (135-145), elevated total bilirubin 19.6 mg/dL (0.3-1.1), elevated alkaline phosphatase 140 U/L (34-104), AST 167 U/L (13-39), and ALT 295 U/L (7-52). Renal function was normal. Blood cultures were obtained, and the patient was started on broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Abdominal imaging and microbiology

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen showed hepatomegaly with multiple poorly defined hypoattenuating lesions in both hepatic lobes, the largest 3.2 cm, consistent with abscesses (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Magnetic resonance imaging confirmed multiple T2-hyperintense, rim-enhancing lesions with restricted diffusion and revealed a filling defect in a middle hepatic vein branch suggestive of septic thrombophlebitis (Fig. 5). Blood cultures subsequently grew Klebsiella pneumoniae, reported as a mucoid strain. Pulmonary findings and interventions

His course was further complicated by acute hypoxic respiratory failure, and computed tomography imaging of the chest demonstrated septic emboli, bilateral pleural effusions, diffuse bilateral ground-glass opacities, multifocal consolidations, and mild interstitial edema (Fig. 3A and B). A decision was then made to proceed with computed tomography-guided aspiration of the right hepatic lobe abscess, which yielded 4 mL of purulent material. Transthoracic echocardiogram showed normal left ventricular systolic function, mild to moderate tricuspid regurgitation, and no vegetations; transesophageal echocardiogram was considered but deferred due to patient instability.

Clinical course and outcome

Serial labs showed persistent hyperbilirubinemia (peak 33.8 mg/dL), fluctuating leukocytosis, and anemia requiring transfusion. Ferritin was monitored daily for both inflammation and iron status, given K. pneumoniae’s reliance on iron acquisition [2,3]. After 1 week, hemodynamics stabilized, but his vision loss persisted. He was then transferred to another facility for ophthalmologic evaluation with a retinal specialist.

Discussion

K. pneumoniae is a gram-negative bacillus of the Enterobacteriaceae family, part of the normal flora of the human oral cavity and gastrointestinal tract. Virulence in hvKp is associated with capsular polysaccharide serotypes (notably K1 and K2), the hypermucoviscous phenotype, and plasmid-encoded siderophore systems [4,5]. Hypervirulent, hypermucoid strains are strongly associated with Klebsiella liver abscess syndrome (KLAS), an invasive infection characterized by primary pyogenic liver abscess with metastatic spread, most frequently to the eyes, lungs, and central nervous system [6]. While initially described in East Asia, KLAS is now increasingly reported worldwide, including among immunocompetent hosts [7]. HvKp is also carried in the gastrointestinal tract, serving as a reservoir that facilitates community spread and healthcare transmission, which likely contributes to its global emergence [8].

Diabetes mellitus is a well-established independent risk factor for hvKp infection, with poor glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 9%) associated with a higher likelihood of metastatic complications [1]. Hyperglycemia promotes capsular polysaccharide overproduction, enhancing resistance to opsonophagocytosis [1]. Hyperglycemia also impairs early innate cell recruitment and signaling in respiratory K. pneumoniae infection, reducing CXCL1/CXCL2, IL-1β, and TNF-α responses and disrupting TLR2/TIRAP pathways, thereby increasing susceptibility in diabetic hosts [9]. Additionally, siderophore systems, particularly aerobactin, are key virulence factors that facilitate iron acquisition and systemic dissemination [2,10,11]. While hyperferritinemia in this setting largely reflects systemic inflammation rather than iron overload, excess bioavailable iron can enhance bacterial growth and virulenc [12].

Our microbiology lab reported blood and liver abscess cultures as a “mucoid strain.” A limitation is that hypermucoviscosity testing and molecular virulence profiling were not performed. Clinical laboratories currently cannot reliably differentiate classical K. pneumoniae (cKp) from hypervirulent strain (hvKp), and hypermucoviscosity alone is not a reliable marker [8]. The hypermucoviscous phenotype is defined by a positive string test (≥ 5 mm string when lifting a colony with a loop) [13], but some cKp strains display this feature while some hvKp strains do not. Likewise, the presence of the aerobactin receptor gene or K1/K2 capsule cannot independently define hvKp [8]. Instead, a highly suggestive clinical scenario where community-onset invasive infection with metastatic dissemination in an otherwise healthy individual is accepted as consistent with hvKp [8]. Our patient, a young otherwise healthy male who presented from the community with a liver abscess complicated by metastatic ocular and vascular involvement, fits this clinical definition and is therefore consistent with hvKp despite the absence of confirmatory phenotypic or molecular confirmation.

This case is notable for several uncommon, radiologically confirmed complications: exudative retinal detachment without vitritis, hepatic vein thrombophlebitis, septic pulmonary emboli, and multifocal embolic strokes. Metastatic endophthalmitis is the most devastating complication of KLAS, which was the primary concern given our patient’s acute monocular vision loss. However, the presentation was atypical. Classic endogenous endophthalmitis usually involves purulent vitreous infection, which was not evident here. Instead, the patient developed a total exudative retinal detachment. The likely mechanism was not a single embolus but rather microembolization of Klebsiella to the highly vascular choroid, producing localized inflammation, disruption of the blood–retina barrier, and leakage of protein-rich fluid into the subretinal space [14]. Progressive accumulation of this fluid separated the retina from the choroid, leading to detachment and loss of photoreceptor function. Intense intraocular inflammation also precipitated rapid, complete cataract formation, further contributing to vision loss and obscuring retinal evaluation. The absence of pre-existing diabetic retinopathy supported this theory. Together with septic hepatic vein thrombophlebitis and multifocal embolic strokes, these findings expand the recognized metastatic spectrum of hvKp infection.

Early recognition and risk stratification are essential in diabetic patients to prevent metastatic spread. Prognostic factors include elevated SOFA/APACHE scores, hemodynamic instability, gas-forming abscesses, and existing metastatic infection [15]. Initial evaluation should consist of contrast-enhanced computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging to characterize abscess morphology and assess for vascular involvement [16]. Any visual complaints should prompt urgent ophthalmologic evaluation with ocular ultrasonography [17].

Source control is central to management. Unilocular abscesses >5 cm should be drained; catheter drainage is preferred over single aspiration, particularly for multiloculated cavities. Surgical intervention should be considered when percutaneous drainage is not feasible [3]. Empiric therapy for susceptible strains includes extended-spectrum cephalosporins; for extended-spectrum β-lactamase-producing isolates, carbapenems are recommended per IDSA guidelines. Carbapenem-resistant hvKp warrants escalation per infectious diseases consultation. In endogenous endophthalmitis, urgent intravitreal antibiotics (eg, ceftazidime-based regimens) with vitreous tap and, often, vitrectomy are indicated [18]. Antimicrobial therapy is typically continued for 4-6 weeks, guided by clinical response and imaging. In this patient, intravenous meropenem was administered for a total duration of 6 weeks, guided by clinical stabilization and radiographic improvement of the hepatic abscesses.

Optimal glycemic control is critical to reduce the risk of recurrence. Given the association between hvKp and septic thrombophlebitis, patients should be monitored for venous thromboembolism and anticoagulated when clinically safe. This case underscores the importance of early hvKp consideration in any patient with uncontrolled diabetes who presents with liver abscess and systemic symptoms. Cross-sectional imaging plays a pivotal role in detecting not only abscesses but also vascular and metastatic complications, and prompt ocular imaging is warranted for any new visual deficits.

Conclusion

This case highlights Klebsiella liver abscess syndrome complicated by exudative retinal detachment and septic hepatic vein thrombosis with thromboembolic sequelae. These findings broaden the recognized spectrum of hvKp infection. Cross-sectional imaging is essential for early detection of both primary and metastatic disease to reduce morbidity.
