A test method for assessing chronic oral toxicity of a pesticide to solitary nesting orchard bees, Osmia spp. (Hymenoptera: Megachilidae)
Authors: Ana R Cabrera, Joshua Zuber, Niklas Hamaekers, Allen Olmstead, Pamela Jensen, Prema Karunanithi, Daniel R Schmehl, Nina Exeler
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrchard bees of the genus Osmia Panzer are important pollinators of fruit trees in various regions of the world, with some species commercially available in the United States and Europe. In addition to their pollination services, Osmia lignaria, Osmia cornifrons, Osmia bicornis, and Osmia cornuta have been identified as potential model species for solitary bees in pesticide risk assessment and have been used for the development of new methods to test acute lethal effects via contact and oral routes of exposure. Our goal was to expand the available methodology to characterize the toxicity of pesticides for these solitary bees through a chronic oral test for adult bees. Chronic oral toxicity of pesticides to orchard bees has been reported, but methods differ among research groups. In our study, O. lignaria, O. cornifrons, O. bicornis, and O. cornuta female bees had access to sucrose solution ad libitum in separate, species-specific 10-day tests. Mean body mass, mean daily consumption, and survival differed among the studied bee species. The dose–response test design was validated with dimethoate, a reference toxic compound, and chronic toxicity endpoints were estimated for the 4 Osmia species. The median lethal daily doses normalized by weight for O. lignaria, O. bicornis, O. cornuta, and O. cornifrons were within the same order of magnitude at 0.23, 0.26, 0.49, and 0.61 µg dimethoate/g bee/day, respectively. The methodology described here was aligned as much as possible with the available honey bee and bumble bee standard methods to facilitate the comparison of chronic toxicity profiles among bee species.

Introduction

The Pollinator Risk Assessment process is a critical step in the registration and regulation of plant protection products, which has frameworks in place in various regions, including North America (USEPA et al. 2014, USEPA 2016), the European Union (EFSA 2023), and Brazil (IBAMA 2017). While there are some differences among these frameworks, in essence, they evaluate risk to pollinators following a tiered approach, initiating at a screening level with toxicity and exposure characterization under worst-case scenarios and refining these 2 components of risk towards more realistic scenarios (USEPA, PRMA, EFSA, and IBAMA). These frameworks are also based on the honey bee as the representative pollinator species, although some frameworks also include other bee species (EFSA 2023).

Under these regulatory frameworks, toxicity characterization is conducted with a number of honey bee standardized methods described in guidelines and guidance documents for acute contact (OECD 1998a, USEPA 2012), acute oral (OECD 1998b), and chronic oral (OECD 2017a) tests with the adult stage, and acute and chronic tests with the larval stage (OECD 2013, 2021). Toxicity refinements for the honey bee colony are conducted in tunnel settings (OECD 2007), semi-field colony feeding studies, and field studies with free-foraging colonies (USEPA et al. 2014, USEPA 2016). Inclusion of other bee species in risk assessment frameworks has been a major driver for the development of standard methodology with bumble bees and solitary bees (EFSA 2023). Currently, there are regulatory guidelines for acute contact (OECD 2017b) and acute oral (OECD 2017c) tests for bumble bees, and a chronic oral test protocol was validated via a multilaboratory effort organized by the International Commission for Plant-Pollinator Relationships (hereafter ICPPR, Steeger 2018). The validation of protocols for guideline development by ICPPR has followed the evaluation by technical experts of test results shared by the independent laboratories evaluating a specific test protocol. Comparable results from dose–response tests and an acceptable control survival provide confidence in the evaluated protocol. While the acute contact and oral test protocols have been validated for solitary bees of the genus Osmia in a similar approach by ICPPR (Roessink et al. 2020), OECD guidelines have not yet been published. Currently, there are no standardized methods to characterize chronic oral toxicity for adult solitary bees.

Chronic oral toxicity to orchard bees has been characterized for Osmia bicornis with a number of chemicals in recent years; however, each reported test was conducted with a different methodology, and this limits the interpretation and comparison of results. For example, Azpiazu et al. (2019) conducted a test with O. bicornis to evaluate the chronic oral toxicity of acetamiprid, imidacloprid, myclobutanil, and their mixture combinations observing the bees until all died, with a test duration over 50 days, exchanging sucrose solution every 3–4 days, and providing pollen (Table 1). Azpiazu et al. (2022) followed a similar method to test sulfoxaflor, fluxapyroxad, and their mixture at different concentrations in the sucrose solution provided to O. bicornis bees, with some bees surviving more than 30 days, but no pollen was supplied in the test system (Table 1). The number of replicates (experimental units) per treatment group in both studies varied among treatments due to an exclusion step of the non-feeder bees during the first 2–3 days (Azpiazu et al. 2019) or 1 day of the test (Azpiazu et al. 2022). A flower petal attached to the syringe containing the feeding solution was included in 5 of the published experiments (Sgolastra et al. 2018, Azpiazu et al. 2019, 2022, 2023, Martins et al. 2023), but a silk petal was used by Heard et al. (2017) and a yellow square-cut sponge cloth by Mokkapati et al. (2022) (Table 1). Furthermore, Spurgeon et al. (2016) and Heard et al. (2017) conducted tests with O. bicornis to evaluate chronic oral toxicity with several compounds during a 10-day period, which is the duration of the honey bee (OECD 2017) and bumble bee (Steeger 2018) standardized chronic tests. The food consumption was measured at 3 timepoints (48, 96, and 240 h); the number of replicates in the study was lower than the above-mentioned bee protocols; and the study design included both male and female bees (Spurgeon et al. 2016, Heard et al. 2017, Table 1). Mokkapati et al. (2022) followed a test design of 8 days of pesticide exposure followed by 8 days of feeding the study bees with untreated sucrose solution (Table 1). Furthermore, low control survival was reported in one of the chronic tests (65%–75%, Heard et al. 2017), although a minimum control survival is typically identified as a validity criterion in standardized methods.

Comparison of methods described in the literature to characterize chronic oral toxicity of pesticides to Osmia spp

The objective of our work was to develop a method to characterize chronic oral toxicity of pesticides to 4 species of orchard bees (Osmia spp.) based on the principles of the honey bee (OECD 2021) and bumble bee (Steeger 2018) standard tests. A standardized and harmonized approach across bee test protocols will enable the characterization of chronic oral toxicity to orchard bees that can facilitate the interpretation of results and conduct inter-species sensitivity comparisons.

Materials and Methods

Orchard Bee Species, Sources, Maintenance, and Incubation Conditions

Orchard bee cocoons from Osmia lignaria and Osmia cornifrons were sourced from Watt’s Solitary Bees (Bothell, WA, USA). Osmia bicornis and Osmia cornuta cocoons were sourced from WAB-Mauerbienenzucht (Konstanz, Germany). Upon receipt, cocoons were stored refrigerated (3–5 °C) until the incubation phase. Incubation of cocoons to promote the emergence of male and female bees occurred in environmentally controlled chambers at 22 ± 2 °C, 60 ± 10% RH, and a 16:8 h light:dark cycle. Cocoons were set in a dark box connected to a clear (plexiglass) box by a tube to allow the emerged bees to move to the clear box and facilitate daily collections. As adult bees emerged, they were placed in ventilated containers (10 cm × 10 cm × 7.5 cm), separating males and females, and kept at 3–5 °C and 60 ± 10% RH until the experiments initiated. Female bees that emerged within a 4-day period prior to the experiment initiation were used after a selection step based on body mass and then randomly assigned to treatment groups at each experiment. Incubation dates for O. lignaria and O. cornifrons cocoons were 22–29 March 2021 (cage type evaluation), 29 March–6 April 2021 (food quantification), and 14–15 April 2021 (mating evaluation). Cocoons for the dimethoate dose–response tests were incubated from 25–31 March 2022 for O. lignaria, 26 April–1 May 2022 for O. cornifrons, 1–11 June 2022 and 19–29 April 2021 for O. cornuta, and 3–13 May 2023 for O. bicornis. Cocoons for the solvent evaluation were incubated on 25 March–3 April 2023 for O. lignaria (pausing incubation between 27 and 28 March 2023), and 25 April–1 May 2023 for O. cornifrons.

Selection of a Cage Design to Meet Survival Criterion and Quantify Diet Daily Consumption

Two cage designs were evaluated as a first step in the development of a chronic oral test. This initial evaluation was conducted in the United States with O. lignaria and O. cornifrons bees. There were 2 types of cages evaluated: one using roller queen cages (also known as Nicot cages) (Mann Lake Ltd., Hackensack, MN) that were used for the acute oral ring test (Roessink et al. 2020), and one based on a round 5.5-oz condiment cup (6 cm height × 7 cm top diameter × 5 cm bottom diameter). For both cages, the food was delivered through a brown cell cup (Mann Lake Ltd., Hackensack, MN), and a 50% w/v sucrose solution was provided ad libitum but not quantified. This delivery device was part of the setup along with the roller queen cage for the acute oral test validation (Roessink et al. 2020), which was our reference point to initiate the method development for a chronic oral test. One unmated female bee was placed inside each cage, and survival was observed for 10 days. Follow-up preliminary test modifications of the condiment cup cage evaluated the replacement of the queen cup with a 3-ml syringe inserted on the side, the syringe tip removed, and the syringe resting on the cage base, sustained by a rubber band kept in place at the union of the condiment cup and the lid (Fig. 1). This arrangement enabled the exchange of the syringes to quantify daily consumption and facilitated observations of survival and sublethal effects. Preliminary observations also led to the inclusion of an empty brown cell cup, maintained on its side inside the condiment cup cage, to provide an object for the Osmia spp. bees to hold or stay while not actively feeding. The rubber band portion inside the cage, from securing the feeding syringe, provided another surface for the bees to hold and rest.

Cage designs utilized during the chronic oral tests for A) O. cornifrons and O. lignaria (in the USA laboratory) and B) O. bicornis and O. cornuta (in the Germany laboratory).

The cage design selection and validation were conducted in 2021. An independent research effort recently reported by Azpiazu et al. (2023) described methodology for acute and chronic tests for solitary bees utilizing similar approaches to the ones described in this work. However, there are some differences to the work described here, including the addition of a petal in the test system, and references to previous chronic evaluations from the same research group (see Azpiazu et al. 2019, 2022) indicate the chronic test is intended for a period longer than 10 days. While they conducted acute toxicity oral tests with O. cornuta exposed to dimethoate, they did not report any dimethoate chronic oral toxicity endpoint (Azpiazu et al. 2023).

The cage and basic test design described here were initially evaluated with O. lignaria and O. cornifrons, measuring their daily consumption and survival of a new cohort of unmated female bees, assessed daily for 10 days. The bees were fed untreated 50% w/v sucrose solution, and consumption was measured by recording the weight of the syringe containing the feeding solution before and after each feeding event, every 24 ± 2 h. Three evaporation controls were included to correct consumption estimates. Thirty bees of each species were used in this evaluation step. Prior to starting the 10-day feeding, the individual bee weight was measured by introducing an individual bee in an empty preweighed roller queen cage with a rubber stopper, measuring the cage containing the bee, and then estimating the weight of the bee by subtraction. The bees were then transferred to their final condiment cup cage. This cage design was selected and used in the subsequent chronic oral tests with O. lignaria and O. cornifrons using different test substances conducted in 2022 and 2023.

Method evaluation with European Osmia species was conducted in Germany. The cage was slightly modified with local materials for their dimethoate tests conducted in 2022 and 2023 with O. bicornis and O. cornuta (Fig. 1). The plastic container was rectangular (10 cm × 8 cm × 5 cm), with a punctured lid to allow passive ventilation and an opening closed with a rubber stopper to introduce the bees. The syringe fits through the side of the cage without the need for a rubber band to keep it in place resting on the bottom of the cage. In addition to a brown cell cup, a small folded piece of wire mesh was included to provide more surface for the bees to hold.

Evaluation of a Mating Step

The consumption and survival were compared among presumed unmated (hereafter “unmated”) and presumed mated (hereafter “mated”) female bees. This step was included in this evaluation because a mating step was included in the acute oral test protocol for Osmia spp. bees organized by ICPPR (Roessink et al. 2020) to promote the feeding of the study bees, and thus, it was considered as a potential protocol element in the chronic oral test design development. The emerged female bees were split into 2 groups: one was held in a ventilated cage (24 cm × 24 cm × 24 cm) without food for 24 h and without male bees, and another group of female bees was held under similar conditions, but male Osmia bees of their corresponding species were included at a 2:1 male:female ratio. After this mating period, 1 female bee was introduced per cage, 30 bees per mating status group (“unmated,” “mated”). This step was evaluated with O. lignaria and O. cornifrons in 2021. Bee weights and food consumption were measured following methods described in “Selection of a Cage Design to Meet Survival Criterion and Quantify Diet Daily Consumption” section.

Dose–Response Chronic Oral Test with Dimethoate

Dimethoate (Pestanal) was sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (Batch No. BCCF3993, St. Louis, MO, USA) and used in all dose–response tests. Dimethoate is a highly bee-toxic insecticide used for the development of bee toxicity protocols and is included as a toxic reference treatment group in regulatory guidelines (i.e., OECD 213, OECD 214, OECD 246, and OECD 247). Dose–response tests with dimethoate were conducted with O. lignaria (2022), O. cornifrons (2022), O. bicornis (2023), and O. cornuta (2022, 2023) for a total of 5 dose–response tests with 4 orchard bee species. The 2 tests with O. cornuta were conducted to generate information on the replicability of the test results. The selected dimethoate concentrations in sucrose solution were 0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 mg dimethoate/kg of diet or parts per million (ppm), informed from range finder tests conducted in 2021 with O. lignaria and O. cornifrons. A stock solution was first prepared with dimethoate dissolved in water without the aid of a solvent, and then this stock solution was used to prepare the feeding solutions. There were 30 bees (replicates) per control and in each dimethoate test level, with daily food exchange, consumption measurements, sublethal (i.e., affected, moribund), and survival observations. Bee weights and food consumption were measured following methods described in “Selection of a Cage Design to Meet Survival Criterion and Quantify Diet Daily Consumption” section. Tests with O. lignaria and O. cornifrons were conducted in Chesterfield, MO, USA, using 2 environmentally controlled chambers. The O. bicornis and O. cornuta tests were conducted in Monheim am Rhein, Germany.

Evaluation of One Solvent and One Suspension Agent

Potential test items hard to solubilize in sucrose solution may benefit from the addition of a solvent or suspension agent to maintain a homogeneous distribution between feeding solution exchanges. Acetone (solvent) and xanthan gum (suspension agent) are commonly used in these situations with honey bee and bumble bee tests. These test materials were evaluated with O. lignaria and O. cornifrons in 2023 but not with O. bicornis or O. cornuta. The test design with each bee species included one control (referred as untreated or UTC) group, acetone at 2.5% v/v (AC_2.5), acetone at 5% v/v (AC_5), xanthan gum at 0.05% w/w (XA_0.05), and xanthan gum at 0.1% w/w (XA_0.1) concentration in the sucrose solution. These levels were selected based on concentration levels safe for honey bees (Tomé et al. 2023). There were 30 bees (replicates) per control and in each acetone and xanthan gum test level, with daily food exchange, consumption estimate, and survival observations. Bee weights and food consumption were measured following methods described in “Selection of a Cage Design to Meet Survival Criterion and Quantify Diet Daily Consumption” section. Three evaporation controls with 50% w/v sucrose solution, 3 with 5% acetone, and 3 with 0.1% xanthan gum were included in the test to correct daily consumption estimates for their corresponding treatment groups (control, acetone, and xanthan gum). Consumption, survival, and other sublethal effects were evaluated daily for 10 days.

Environmental Conditions

All orchard bee tests were maintained in controlled growth chambers (Thermo Scientific, Forma 3900 Series, USA; RUMED, Rubarth Apparate GmbH, CPS-P1060, Germany). Environmental conditions were set and maintained at 22 ± 2 °C, 60 ± 10% relative humidity and a 16:8 h Light:Dark cycle. The only source of light was that emitted by the light bulbs in the growth chamber models (Osram Biolux L 18w/965, USA; Rhenac-Greentec, Germany). The bees were not exposed to UV light.

Dimethoate Solutions Sample Collection and Chemical Analysis

Stock solution samples were taken at preparation, and feeding solution samples were taken at the preparation of feeding solutions on days 0 and 5 for the dimethoate dose–response chronic oral tests conducted with O. lignaria and O. cornifrons. No sample analysis was conducted for the O. bicornis and O. cornuta tests, but information on the test substance purity and process was documented to support the feeding solution preparation.

Approximately 0.5 ml of stock and 2 ml of feeding solutions were collected and stored at −20 °C until analyzed. Dimethoate residues were quantified in the Residue Laboratory at Bayer CropScience (Chesterfield, MO, USA). Dimethoate residue levels in the stock solutions and feeding solutions were analyzed via LC-MS/MS. Linearity of the residues was determined in a range of 0–100 ng/ml (= μg/L or ppb). Untreated and spiked control samples were included in the analyzed set for validation. The method level of quantification was 5 ppb (parts per billion), and the level of detection was 0.3 ppb. Stock solutions were diluted 10,000-fold prior to the extraction process via serial dilution. Sample preparation consisted of taking a 0.5 g aliquot of the sample and transferring it to a 15-ml tube, then adding 50 μl of standard, quality control) or acetonitrile (ACN), the later for unknown and blank samples into a conical tube. This was followed by the addition of Internal Standard solution to each tube, with the exception of blank samples that received ACN only. Ten ml of 1:1 ACN:Water was delivered to each tube, which was subsequently mixed using a SPEX Geno/Grinder 2010 (SPEX SamplePrep, Metuchen, NJ) for 5 min at 1,200 cycles per minute. Afterward, the tubes were centrifuged for 5 min at 4500 × g, and following this step, an aliquot of 1 ml was dispensed to a 96-well plate. Ten μl was injected into the LC-MS/MS for analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the statistical software R, version 4.2.2 (R Core Team 2022), with packages “multcomp,” “tidyverse,” “car,” “clinfun,” and “drc.” For continuous endpoints (food consumption and body weight), significant departures from either normality (Shapiro Wilk, α = 0.01) or variance homogeneity (Levene, α = 0.05) were routinely observed. Therefore, these endpoints were evaluated using nonparametric statistical methods.

Mating Step Evaluation

The mean daily consumption (mg sucrose solution/bee/day) of the mated and unmated bees was compared via a 2-sided Wilcoxon rank sum exact test in each of the 2 tests, conducted with O. lignaria and O. cornifrons. Differences in survival between treatment groups were evaluated using a Fisher exact test.

Dimethoate Dose–Response Chronic Oral Tests

The weight of bees randomly assigned to the treatment groups was analyzed via a 2-tailed Kruskal Wallis to evaluate for potential bias. The mean daily sucrose solution consumption for each Osmia test was analyzed via a one-tailed Jonkheere-Terpstra trend test. Survival-response data were fit with a 3-parameter log-logistic model using either nominal treatment concentrations or daily doses (i.e., median lethal concentration [LC50] or media lethal dietary dose [LDD50]). The daily doses were estimated by factoring the daily consumption estimate for each individual bee and the nominal concentration of dimethoate in the feeding solution. The no observable effect concentration (NOEC) and the no observed effect dietary dose (NOEDD) were identified via Cochran-Armitage Trend tests (OECD 2006). The dose-based endpoints for each Osmia species are reported as μg dimethoate/bee/day and normalized by weight as μg dimethoate/g bee/day using the average bee weight for each Osmia species.

Chronic Oral Tests with Acetone and Xanthan

Mean daily food consumption was first rank transformed, and treatments were compared to the control using a Dunnett’s t-test. Starting body weights among treatments were compared using a Kruskal–Wallace test. Survival in treatments compared to the control was evaluated using a Fisher exact test.

Results

Evaluation of a Mating Step

A mating step was evaluated to determine if the survival and daily food consumption were different among unmated and mated bees. The mean daily consumption of O. lignaria mated bees (63 mg/bee/day) was over 30% higher than for unmated bees (47 mg/bee/day) and was statistically significant (P = 0.015, Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S4). The mean daily consumption of O. cornifrons mated bees (86 mg/bee/day) and unmated bees (76 mg/bee/day) was not significantly different (P = 0.34, Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S4). Survival of O. cornifrons at day 10 was 100% for both mated and unmated bees, while survival of O. lignaria at day 10 was 83% for mated and 67% for unmated bees. Based on these results, and considering that mating was only assumed but not corroborated, this step was removed from further consideration in the protocol.

Analytical Results

The nominal concentrations for 5 stock solutions and 6 feeding solutions were confirmed via residue analysis (Supplementary Table S1). All recoveries were within 70%–120%, considered acceptable in regulatory testing (USEPA 1996), thus confirming the test levels for the chronic tests with O. lignaria and O. cornifrons.

Weight of Bees

The average bee weight and standard deviation were 105 ± 12 mg for O. lignaria, 71 ± 8 mg for O. cornifrons, 88 ± 11 mg for O. bicornis, 129 ± 16 mg (2022), and 128 ± 18 mg (2023) for O. cornuta (Supplementary Fig. S1, Table 2 and Supplementary Table S2). No statistically significant differences were observed in starting test weights among treatment groups for O. cornifrons (P = 0.997), O. cornuta 2022 (P = 0.731), and O. cornuta 2023 (P = 0.908); however, this was not the case for O. lignaria (P = 0.018) and O. bicornis (P = 0.044) (Supplementary Table S4).

Mean Daily Sucrose Solution Consumption

Treatment-related effects on food consumption were observed in all studies except those with O. lignaria (P = 0.387) and O. bicornis (P = 0.070, Fig. 3, Supplementary Tables S2 and S4). Mean daily food solution consumption in the control groups was 85 ± 25 mg/bee/day for O. cornifrons, 47 ± 24 mg/bee/day for O. lignaria, 47 ± 27 mg/bee/day for O. bicornis, 151 ± 46 mg/bee/day for O. cornuta in the 2022 test, and 121 ± 99 mg/bee/day for O. cornuta in the 2023 test (Supplementary Table S2). A NOEC of 0.5 ppm was observed with O. cornifrons; consumption NOECs were ≥ 2 ppm for O. lignaria and O. bicornis; and a NOEC of 0.125 ppm was observed in the O. cornuta 2022 study, while in the 2023 test, the NOEC observed was 0.25 ppm (Supplementary Table S4). O. cornuta exhibited the highest sensitivity for consumption of dimethoate-treated feeding solution.

Mean daily consumption (mg sucrose solution/bee/day) of orchard bees in the control and 5 dimethoate treatment groups (0.125 mg/kg, 0.25 mg/kg, 0.5 mg/kg, 1 mg/kg, and 2 mg/kg dimethoate in the feeding solution), in each of the 5 chronic tests. Tests were conducted with Osmia cornifrons, O. lignaria, O. bicornis, and O. cornuta; the latter was tested twice during 2022 and 2023. The lowest observable effect concentration (LOEC) is the lowest test level that exhibited statistically significant differences from the control; the NOEC is the test level right below the LOEC and is the highest test level that does not significantly differ from the control.

Mortality

The NOEC for mortality in the O. cornifrons study was 0.25 ppm with an LC50 of 0.56 (0.46–0.66) ppm and the LDD50 estimated at 42 (37–48) µg dimethoate/bee/day (Table 2; Supplementary Table S4). For O. lignaria, the NOEC was 0.25 ppm with an LC50 of 0.41 (0.35–0.47) ppm and an LDD50 of 24 (19–29) µg dimethoate/bee/day (Table 2). The NOEC for O. bicornis was also 0.25 ppm with an LC50 of 0.64 (0.34–0.95) ppm, and the LDD50 was estimated at 23 (15–32) µg dimethoate/bee/day (Table 2; Supplementary Table S4). For O. cornuta, NOECs of 0.25 ppm and 0.5 ppm were observed in 2022 and 2023, respectively (Table 2; Supplementary Table S4). LC50 values for O. cornuta were 0.68 (0.57–0.80) ppm in the 2022 study and 0.65 (0.51–0.79) ppm in the 2023 study, while LDD50 values were 62 (53–71) µg dimethoate/bee/day in the 2022 study and 69 (33–104) µg dimethoate/bee/day in the 2023 study (Table 2; Supplementary Table S4). Notably, the data from the 2023 test with O. cornuta had a poor fit to the log-logistic model per visual inspection, and thus, the results are presented but not further interpreted.

Use of a Solvent and a Suspension Agent for Chronic Oral Tests with Osmia spp.

Acetone is the most common solvent used in oral toxicity tests with bees to aid in the solubilization of some test substances. We evaluated 2 acetone concentrations added to the sucrose solution, 2.5% and 5%, the same concentrations that have been used with other bee species. No significant differences in food consumption were observed for any acetone or xanthan gum concentration and species except for the 2.5% acetone treatment with O. lignaria (P = 0.041, Supplementary Table S4). However, this finding is not considered to be treatment-related as the higher treatment of 5% acetone was not significantly different from the control (P = 0.996, Supplementary Table S4). No significant differences in mortality were observed in either study.

Discussion

Our protocol and test design enabled the characterization of chronic oral toxicity of dimethoate to orchard bees and offer a method that is based on the honey bee (OECD 2017a) and bumble bee (Steeger 2018) chronic oral tests. The cage design allowed the quantification of daily food consumption which is critical in regulatory chronic oral bee tests to estimate dose-based endpoints. Furthermore, the test setup resulted in high control survival (≥80%) in 4 of the 5 dose–response tests, the exception being the control survival in the O. cornuta 2023 test (70%, Table 2). While ≥80% control survival is lower than the validity criteria identified for the honey bee and bumble bee chronic oral tests (i.e., ≥85% control survival), this level is the same proposed for the acute oral test with orchard bees (Roessink et al. 2020). The methodology was transferrable among 4 orchard bee species (O. lignaria, O. cornifrons, O. bicornis, and O. cornuta) and across 2 laboratories located in 2 different continents (USA and Germany). This is an indication of the robustness of the methodology and that it can serve as the basis for a standardized test.

Dimethoate was selected for the development of this test design because it has been historically used for this purpose with other bee toxicity tests and is often included in bee toxicity guidelines as a positive control. Our results identified dimethoate LC50s for O. lignaria, O. cornifrons, O. bicornis, and O. cornuta at 0.41, 0.56, 0.64, and 0.68 ppm, respectively. This test design is labor-intensive, and including a positive control treatment group to a potential dose–response test to evaluate another molecule will add to the labor needs. However, if dimethoate is included as a positive control group, our results indicate that dimethoate at ≥1 ppm will achieve >50% mortality, which is a validity criterion in bee test guidelines (OECD 2017a, 2017b, 2017c). Validity criteria are included in guidelines to ensure that the data are representative of the larger population and the test system and cages are suitable to sustain the test organism for the duration of the test, which is the case of a control validity criterion. In contrast, a positive control provides a point of reference that the system is working as expected and that the test organisms are of representative sensitivity to a known toxic compound. Validity criteria in current OECD Guidelines are based on the survival endpoint, but a criterion for the consumption parameter integrated in an exclusion step could be considered for this test design with Osmia spp. For example, we observed several bees in our tests that did not feed or consumed very little feeding solution during the first few days (Figs. 2, 3, and 4) and eventually died. Alternately, an extended acclimation period that allows the selection of bees that survive and feed for a few days prior to pesticide exposure could be incorporated to minimize the number of non-feeder bees during the exposure phase.

Our study also adds to the growing body of work addressing species sensitivity in bees. Our results showed that O. lignaria could be considered as the most sensitive species of the 4 species tested to dimethoate on a concentration- and dose-basis normalized by weight; however, those endpoints were within 2× difference on a concentration basis and within 3× difference on a body weight-dose basis (Table 2). Moreover, given the alignment of the test designs between the honey bee and orchard bees, when comparing the dimethoate chronic oral toxicity endpoints generated for the 4 orchard bees in our test (Table 2) with the honey bee endpoints (OECD 2017d), the LC50s for O. cornifrons, O. bicornis, O. cornuta, and O. lignaria were comparable to the honey bee chronic dimethoate LC50 (0.48 ± 0.15 ppm). Dimethoate dose-based endpoints incorporated the individual daily consumption, which varied between the 4 Osmia species, and the endpoints were further normalized on a weight basis (expressed as μg dimethoate/g bee/day) based on the average weight of the test bees, which also varied between species (Table 2). Based on the dose-based normalized LDD50s, the sensitivity of the orchard bee species observed was O. lignaria > O. bicornis > O. cornuta > O. cornifrons. However, these endpoints were above the dose-based normalized LDD50 for the honey bee, which we estimated at 0.15 μg dimethoate/g bee/day considering the reported LDD50 of 0.015 ± 0.003 μg dimethoate/bee/day (OECD 2017d) and an assumed body weight of 100 mg (Thompson and Pamminger 2019).

We also evaluated 2 acetone and 2 xanthan gum test levels with O. cornifrons and O. lignaria following the presented 10-day test design. The objective of this evaluation was to expand the flexibility of the test design to address the need for certain test substances to maintain them in solution or suspension when they are difficult to solubilize in a sucrose solution. We found that acetone can be used to solubilize other molecules up to a level of 5% v/v in the sucrose solution, and xanthan gum can be used to maintain suspension up to a level of 0.1% w/w in the sucrose solution without affecting the daily consumption and survival of both Osmia species, which is the case for honey bees too (Tomé et al. 2023). Since acetone and xanthan gum have not been evaluated with O. bicornis and O. cornuta, it is recommended to test them before conducting a test for a test substance that needs a solvent or a suspension agent.

The study bees in our experiments during 2021–2023 were sourced from the same suppliers in the United States and Germany, albeit the exact conditions for larval development and nutritional value of their pollen balls are unknown. Notably, there was variability in the average weight of the 2 North American species in different years but not in the average weight of O. cornuta, the only European species with datasets generated in 2 different years (Supplementary Fig. S2).

While we randomly allocated study bees to all treatments with the goal of removing bias due to body weight, there were statistical differences in the O. lignaria and O. bicornis groups; however, the variance in treatment means for these 2 studies was relatively small compared to the within treatment variance, and it is unlikely that these small differences impacted the overall results of the study. The practice of including the body weight measurement is valuable to enable the calculation of dosage-based endpoints (i.e., µg a.i./g bee/day rather than µg a.i./bee/day). In addition, the mean daily food intake of O. cornifrons fed with untreated sucrose solutions during the 2023 test (solvent and suspension agent) was lower than that observed in the previous 2 years (Supplementary Fig. S2). These observations highlight the inherent variability from working with bees that are not yet mass-reared under more controlled conditions but rather trapped in their naturally occurring areas or agroecosystems.

Taken together, our work presents a novel chronic oral toxicity test design with orchard bees of the genus Osmia, aligned to the honey bee and bumble bee chronic oral tests. The harmonized protocol elements include replication, test duration, criteria for control survival, and daily measurement of food consumption. The measurement of individual bee weights and normalization of endpoints by weight facilitates the comparison of chronic oral toxicity endpoints among Osmia species and with other bee species, such as honey bees and bumble bees. For the molecule tested in this project, dimethoate, our results showed that the honey bee is protective of 4 orchard bee species. Our aim is that this protocol is considered for further validation and contributes to the development of a standard, validated method to test chronic oral toxicity of compounds with solitary bees to facilitate results interpretation and comparison with the model species for risk assessment, the honey bee.
