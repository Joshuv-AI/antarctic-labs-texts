Formulation and Performance of a Rhamnolipid-Based Nanoemulsion for Enhanced Heavy Oil Recovery in Offshore Reservoirs
Authors: Jiangang Li, Bo Deng, Yunpu Tang, Yingcheng Wang, Erlong Yang, Yinsong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heavy oil resources are abundant, yet their high viscosity imposes severe difficulties in both recovery and pipeline transport. Conventional viscosity reducers suffer from inherent limitations, including poor environmental compatibility, insufficient stability, and low efficiency. In this work, we developed an eco-friendly nanoemulsion system that can efficiently reduce heavy oil viscosity, offering a new pathway for the green exploitation of heavy oil resources. This study employed the hydrophilic–lipophilic balance (HLB) method to screen surfactant formulations. A composite surfactant system consisting of rhamnolipid and AEO-9 at a mass ratio of 2:1 (HLB = 20.44) was used in 0.5 mol/L CaCl2 aqueous solution, with glycerol as the cosurfactant at a surfactant/cosurfactant mass ratio (Km ) of 3 and palm oil as the oil phase, to prepare microemulsion precursor #5. Upon 500-fold dilution, this precursor yielded nanoemulsion #5–500. Dynamic light scattering characterization revealed a narrow particle size distribution centered at 60–80 nm, with a zeta potential of – 52.8 mV. At 55 °C, the nanoemulsion reduced the viscosity of heavy crude oil by 91.85% and lowered the oil–water interfacial tension to 0.15 mN/m. The contact angle on an oil-wet surface decreased from 106° to 74°, crossing the 90° threshold, indicating a wettability reversal from oil-wet to water-wet. Core flood experiments demonstrated that sequential injection of the diluted nanoemulsion followed by the neat formulation at 55 °C enhanced oil recovery by 68.79%. This green nanoemulsion system offers a feasible and environmentally friendly technological pathway for efficient heavy oil recovery.

Introduction

Heavy oil accounts for a substantial share of global oil resources. Statistics indicate that heavy oil represents approximately 50% of the world’s proven petroleum reserves, offering considerable development potential. − However, owing to its high density (0.92 g/cm3), high viscosity (50 mPa·s), and elevated contents of resins and asphaltenes, heavy oil exhibits poor flowability, which poses major challenges to its recovery and transportation. , Particularly in offshore heavy oil fields, the viscosity of crude oil further increases after cyclic steam stimulation (CSS), resulting in impaired flowability and a significant decline in recovery efficiency. Given the sustained growth in global energy demand and the progressive depletion of conventional oil resources, the efficient development of heavy oil has become a critical focus for the petroleum industry. −

Heavy oil recovery predominantly relies on thermal methods, including cyclic steam stimulation (CSS), steam flooding (SF), and steam-assisted gravity drainage (SAGD). − However, the application of these conventional thermal techniques on offshore platforms faces major challenges, such as high energy consumption, large space requirements, substantial capital investment, and significant greenhouse gas emissions. More critically, owing to the limited space and stringent environmental regulations on offshore installations, traditional thermal recovery methods cannot adequately satisfy both economic and environmental requirements.

Chemical enhanced oil recovery (EOR) techniques, particularly surfactant flooding and nanoemulsion flooding, have been regarded as promising alternatives for offshore heavy oil development owing to their advantages of simple equipment, flexible operation, and environmental friendliness. − In recent years, nanoemulsions have attracted considerable attention in the field of heavy oil EOR because of their unique colloidal properties. Compared with conventional emulsions, nanoemulsions typically have droplet sizes below 100 nm and exhibit exceptional kinetic stability, enormous specific surface area, and ultralow interfacial tension. They can efficiently reduce oil–water interfacial tension, modulate reservoir rock wettability, and significantly lower heavy oil viscosity through in situ emulsification, thereby improving the mobility ratio and enhancing microscopic sweep efficiency. − However, the construction of highly stable nanoemulsions often requires high dosages of synthetic surfactants (sometimes up to 10 wt % of the total system), which not only substantially increases operational costs but also introduces potential ecotoxicological risks and environmental burdens, severely restricting the large-scale field application of nanoemulsions in oilfields.

Given the current interest in and demand for green, sustainable, and environmentally friendly materials, − the use of renewable, biodegradable biosurfactants to replace conventional petroleum-based surfactants has emerged as a potential solution to overcome the above bottlenecks. , On this basis, a green and environmentally friendly nanoemulsion was prepared using the pseudoternary phase diagram method of microemulsions. The optimal microemulsion formulation was determined by systematically investigating the effects of surfactant type, oil phase, cosurfactant, surfactant/cosurfactant mass ratio, and CaCl2 aqueous solution concentration on the microemulsion region. The nanoemulsion was then obtained via the dilution method, and its properties were characterized in terms of droplet size, zeta potential, surface tension, interfacial tension, and wettability, as well as its viscosity-reducing effect on heavy oil. Core flooding experiments were conducted to evaluate the EOR performance under different injection sequences (diluted solution followed by stock solution vs stock solution followed by diluted solution) and temperatures (55 and 65 °C). This study aims to provide both an economically and environmentally efficient development strategy and a theoretical basis for offshore heavy oil reservoirs.

Methods

Preparation of the Viscosity Reducer

The surfactant system was screened using the hydrophilic–lipophilic balance (HLB) method. The HLB method is based on the principle that the required HLB of the system to be emulsified should be as close as possible to that of the mixed surfactant system; optimal emulsification is achieved when the two values are equal. Two surfactants, rhamnolipid and AEO-9, were mixed at mass ratios of 2:1, 1.5:1, 1:1, 1:1.5, and 1:2. The HLB value of each mixture was calculated using the following formula 1 HLB=∑(Xi×HLBi) where HLB i is the HLB value of an individual surfactant, and Xi is its mass fraction.

The mixed surfactant system was used to emulsify coconut oil, olive oil, and palm oil, respectively. The emulsification volume was measured to identify the most compatible mixed surfactant system and oil phase. The specific procedure was as follows: 1 mL of oil phase and 1 mL of deionized water were added to a 25 mL graduated centrifuge tube with a stopper, followed by the addition of 2 mL of the mixed surfactant system. The mixture was vigorously agitated using a vortex mixer and then allowed to stand. The total volume of the emulsion (V W) and the volume of the emulsified layer (V L) were recorded. The emulsifying capacity (E C) was calculated using the following formula EC=VLVW×100% 2 The pseudoternary phase diagrams of microemulsions were constructed using an oil titration method. The mixed surfactant and two different cosurfactants were blended at mass ratios (Km ) of 1:1, 2:1, 3:1, and 4:1. The resulting surfactant/cosurfactant mixture was treated as a single pseudocomponent and subsequently combined with deionized water at various mass ratios (1:9, 2:8, 3:7, 4:6, 5:5, 6:4, 7:3, 8:2, and 9:1) to prepare a series of binary systems. Each sample had a total mass of approximately 2–3 g and was placed in a 50 mL beaker. The oil phase was added dropwise using a microsyringe at increments of 0.05 g per addition. After each addition, the mixture was vortexed at 3000 r/min for 2 min using a vortex mixer until the system turned from clear to turbid. Qualitative assessment was performed by visual inspection: microemulsions exhibited low viscosity and clear transparency, with no visible light path when a parallel laser beam from a laser pointer was incident; in contrast, conventional emulsions appeared turbid and opaque. Each boundary point was determined by three parallel titrations, and the average mass of oil added at the phase inversion point was recorded. The oil mass at the critical phase transition was documented, and pseudoternary phase diagrams were plotted using Origin 2021. The microemulsion region area was calculated using the Area tool embedded in Origin 2021.

To expand the microemulsion region, deionized water was replaced with CaCl2 aqueous solution. A selected point within the microemulsion region was subjected to fixed-volume dilution, followed by ultrasonic oscillation at a frequency of 60 kHz for 5 min to obtain the nanoemulsion.

Droplet Size Distribution and Zeta Potential

The droplet size distribution of the bionano oil displacement agent system was measured and analyzed under different nanoparticle concentrations using a Bettersize2600 laser particle size analyzer. For size measurements, the nanoemulsion was directly diluted with deionized water to minimize multiple scattering effects. Zeta potential measurements were performed by dispersing the nanoemulsion in 0.5 mol/L CaCl2 solution at 25 °C. Each sample was measured three times, and the average value was reported as the Zeta potential.

Surface Tension Measurement

Surface tension of the nanoemulsion systems was determined using a Krüss K100 fully automatic tensiometer via the Wilhelmy plate method with a platinum plate. Prior to measurement, each sample was equilibrated in a constant-temperature water bath at 25 °C for 30 min. An appropriate volume of the equilibrated sample was then transferred into a clean measuring vessel. The platinum plate, which had been cleaned by flame heating to remove contaminants, was vertically immersed into the liquid to a depth of approximately 2 mm. The maximum force exerted on the plate by the liquid film was automatically recorded by the instrument and converted into surface tension values. For each sample, five parallel measurements were performed. Outliers were discarded, and the arithmetic mean of the remaining values was taken as the final result, with the relative standard deviation controlled within 5%. Throughout the entire experimental procedure, the ambient temperature was maintained constant, and disturbances from air currents and vibrations were carefully avoided.

Interfacial Tension Measurement

The oil–water interfacial tension (IFT) was measured using a TX-500C spinning drop tensiometer. The quartz tube was thoroughly cleaned and filled with viscosity reducer solutions at varying concentrations. A droplet of heavy crude oil was then injected into the quartz tube, allowing the oil droplet to remain suspended within the viscosity reducer solution. The quartz tube was subsequently placed in the measurement chamber of the TX-500C tensiometer. Upon reaching the desired temperature, the density difference between the sample solution and the heavy crude oil was monitored, and the IFT was measured at a rotational speed of 5000 r/min. Data were recorded every minute until three consecutive readings stabilized, and the average value was taken as the measured interfacial tension.

Wettability Evaluation

The surface wettability of the core samples was evaluated using a dynamic contact angle meter. The contact angle on the untreated core surface was measured initially. Subsequently, the core was immersed in the viscosity-reducing system at 55 °C for 24 h. After removal and drying, the contact angle on the treated core surface was measured again.

Viscosity Reduction Rate Measurement

The viscosity reduction performance of #5 and #5–500 was evaluated using a Brookfield DV2TLVTJ0 rotational viscometer. The experimental procedure for determining the viscosity reduction rate was as follows: (i) S-1 heavy crude oil was placed in a constant-temperature water bath and maintained at 55 °C for 1 h. After removing free water and air bubbles by stirring, the initial viscosity (μ0) at 55 °C was measured within 20 s using the Brookfield rotational viscometer. (ii) A 70 g aliquot of the S-1 heavy crude oil was transferred into a beaker, and 30 g of viscosity reducer was added. A stirring paddle was positioned at the center of the beaker, 2–3 mm above the bottom, and the mixture was stirred at 250 r/min under constant temperature for 2 min. (iii) The viscosity of the treated crude oil (μ) was measured rapidly within 20 s using the Brookfield DV2TLVTJ0 rotational viscometer. The viscosity reduction rate (f) was calculated using the following formula 3 f=μ0−μμ0×100% where μ0 is the viscosity of the S-1 oilfield heavy crude oil sample (mPa·s), and μ is the viscosity of the crude oil after treatment with the viscosity reducer (mPa·s).

Core Flooding Experiments

The core samples used in this study were 2.5 cm in diameter and 30 cm in length, with a permeability of 2000 mD. The enhanced oil recovery performances of microemulsions #5 and #5–500 were investigated under different injection modes and reservoir temperatures using a standard core flooding apparatus. Prior to the experiments, the geometric parameters and dry weight of each core were measured. The cores were then vacuum-saturated (vacuum degree <10 Pa, held for 30 min) to determine the pore volume. Formation brine, the neat viscosity reducer, and its diluted solutions were preheated in a constant-temperature oven before use. After mounting the core in the holder, an overburden pressure of 10 MPa was applied, and the system was maintained at the target temperature for 2 h. The core was then saturated with crude oil at an injection rate of 0.5 mL/min until continuous oil production was observed at the outlet. The irreducible water volume was measured, and the initial oil saturation was calculated accordingly. Subsequently, water flooding was performed at the same rate of 0.5 mL/min, with oil production and pressure drop recorded every 10 min. When the water cut reached 98%, the injection fluid was sequentially switched to microemulsion #5 and then #5–500. The cumulative fluid production, cumulative oil production, and pressure variations were continuously recorded throughout the process. The final cumulative enhanced oil recovery was calculated based on the measured data.

Results and Discussion

Preparation of the Viscosity Reducer

Screening of Surfactant and Oil Phase

Surfactants can form a stable monolayer at the oil–water interface, thereby reducing interfacial tension and promoting emulsion formation. The HLB value of a surfactant is a key parameter determining the microemulsion type; higher HLB values indicate stronger hydrophilicity and a greater tendency to form oil-in-water (O/W) microemulsions. To obtain an appropriate mixed surfactant system, three biosurfactantsrhamnolipid, sophorolipid, and lipopeptidewere separately combined with the nonionic surfactant AEO-9 at various mass ratios. Preliminary experiments showed that the combination of rhamnolipid and AEO-9 performed better than the other biosurfactant-based systems. Therefore, the anionic biosurfactant rhamnolipid (HLB = 22–24) and the nonionic surfactant AEO-9 (HLB = 13.3) were selected as the mixed surfactant system. The two surfactants were blended in mass ratios of 2:1, 1.5:1, 1:1, 1:1.5, and 1:2, yielding calculated HLB values of 20.44, 19.72, 18.65, 17.58, and 16.86, respectively (Table ). This HLB range is suitable for emulsifying heavy crude oils containing polar components, which typically require higher HLB values than those for pure hydrocarbon emulsification (10–14) because of polar constituents such as asphaltenes and resins.

Three common vegetable oilscoconut oil, olive oil, and palm oilwere selected as the oil phase due to their economic viability. The emulsifying capacity (EC) of mixed surfactant systems with varying HLB values for each oil phase was measured, and the results are presented in Figure . The experimental results indicate that the HLB value of the mixed surfactant system significantly influences the emulsification performance for different oil phases. Previous studies have suggested that the hydrophobic chain length of the surfactant should match the carbon chain length of the oil phase to effectively reduce interfacial tension. When a significant mismatch in chain length exists, the hydrophobic tail cannot effectively embed into the oil phase structure, leading to reduced adsorption efficiency and increased interfacial tension. Consequently, for a given vegetable oil, surfactants with different HLB values exhibit varying degrees of interfacial tension reduction. Similarly, for a given surfactant system, the area of the single-phase region varies among different vegetable oils.

Emulsification capacity of oil phase with different HLB values.

As shown in Figure , the emulsifying capacity of the mixed surfactant system for coconut oil was generally low, exhibiting only a slight increase with increasing HLB value, though the overall performance remained unsatisfactory. For olive oil, the emulsifying capacity initially increased and then decreased with increasing HLB value of the mixed surfactant system. Optimal emulsification of olive oil was achieved at an HLB value of 17.58, with the resulting emulsion exhibiting no phase separation. In contrast, the mixed surfactant system demonstrated the highest emulsifying capacity for palm oil. At an HLB value of 20.44, the emulsifying capacity for palm oil reached 66%, and further increases in HLB value resulted in a relatively stable emulsification performance. These results indicate that the HLB value of the mixed surfactant system significantly influences the emulsifying capacity for different oil phases, with the mixed system at an HLB of 20.44 exhibiting the best emulsification performance for palm oil. Considering both emulsification efficiency and economic feasibility, palm oil was selected as the oil phase, and the mixed surfactant system with an HLB value of 20.44 was chosen for subsequent nanoemulsion preparation.

Screening of Cosurfactant and Optimization of Mixing Ratio

The pseudoternary phase diagrams of the four-component system comprising mixed surfactant, cosurfactant, palm oil, and deionized water were determined at 40 °C. The mass ratios of surfactant to cosurfactant (Km ) were set at 1:1, 2:1, 3:1, and 4:1, using glycerol and n-butanol as cosurfactants, respectively. The phase inversion points of the microemulsion region were identified via the oil titration method, and the areas of the microemulsion regions were calculated using Origin 2021 software. The resulting phase diagrams are presented in Figures and . In these diagrams, the white regions represent two-phase zones, while the colored regions correspond to the microemulsion zones. The stability and solubilization capacity of the microemulsion systems are primarily reflected by the area of the microemulsion region.

As shown in Figure , the area of the microemulsion region in the pseudoternary phase diagram initially increased and then decreased with increasing Km value. The maximum microemulsion area of 0.06195 was achieved at Km = 3. This trend can be attributed to the following considerations. When the Km value is relatively low, the cosurfactant is insufficient relative to the surfactant, and the excess surfactant cannot be fully incorporated into the oil–water interfacial film, thereby disrupting the compact structure of the microemulsion and reducing its stability. Conversely, when the Km value is too high, the excess surfactant tends to generate a large number of bubbles during the preparation process, which interferes with the accurate determination of the oil phase inversion point. Consequently, the microemulsion area exhibits a pattern of initial increase followed by a decrease.

As shown in Figure , when n-butanol was used as the cosurfactant, the variation pattern of the microemulsion region area with increasing Km value was similar to that observed for the glycerol system, exhibiting an initial increase followed by a decrease. The maximum microemulsion area was also achieved at Km = 3; however, the difference in area between Km = 3 and Km = 2 was only 0.00167. From an industrial application perspective, a Km value of 3 requires a higher surfactant dosage, which may pose increased safety risks and higher costs. Therefore, given the comparable microemulsion areas, Km = 2 represents a more economical and practical choice.

It is generally accepted that alcohol-based cosurfactants can modify the surface activity and hydrophilic–lipophilic balance of surfactants during micelle formation, as well as adjust the polarity of the aqueous and oil phases. In this study, the role of the cosurfactant is to participate in the distribution and arrangement of surfactant molecules at the oil–water interfacial film, thereby altering the interfacial properties of the system. The addition of alcohol reduces the critical micelle concentration, facilitating the formation of the microemulsion region. Alcohols with different carbon chain lengths exhibit varying abilities to soften the oil–water interfacial film. As the carbon chain length increases, the molecular volume of the alcohol becomes larger, resulting in greater resistance to incorporation into the interfacial film. This reduces the flexibility of the interfacial film while increasing its rigidity, which hinders the curvature of the interfacial film and consequently impedes the formation of microemulsions.

In the pseudoternary system of mixed surfactant, cosurfactant, palm oil, and water, the microemulsion region formed by glycerol at a Km value of 3 is larger than that formed by n-butanol, primarily because the carbon chain length of n-butanol is greater than that of glycerol. As the carbon chain length of the alcohol increases, the proportion of cosurfactant required to achieve the maximum microemulsion region gradually decreases. Based on the above analysis, glycerol was selected as the cosurfactant in this study, and the mass ratio of mixed surfactant to glycerol was set at Km = 3 for the subsequent preparation of nanoemulsions.

Preparation of Nanoemulsion Viscosity Reducer

Based on the selection of glycerol as the cosurfactant and a mass ratio of mixed surfactant to cosurfactant of Km = 3, the effect of CaCl2 concentration on the formation of the microemulsion region was investigated at 40 °C. Aqueous solutions with CaCl2 concentrations of 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, and 1.0 mol/L were prepared, and the pseudoternary phase diagrams of ·the mixed surfactant + glycerol + palm oil + CaCl2 aqueous solution system were determined. The areas of the microemulsion regions were calculated, and the results are shown in Figure .

The triangular axes separately denote the mass fraction of component O, component W, and combined component S+A in the ternary microemulsion system.

As shown in Figure , with increasing CaCl2 concentration, the area of the microemulsion region first increased and then decreased. The maximum microemulsion area was observed at a CaCl2 concentration of 0.5 mol/L. It is proposed that in the low-concentration range, Ca2+ forms a structured water layer through strong hydration, significantly enhancing the solvation capacity of the water core and promoting expansion of the microemulsion region. When the concentration exceeded 0.5 mol/L, competitive hydration between Ca2+ and the polar groups of the surfactant occurred, inducing a micellar dehydration effect. This resulted in a loss of hydrophilicity at the surfactant head groups, a corresponding decrease in cloud point, and a reduction in the water core radius, ultimately leading to a decrease in the microemulsion region area.

Based on the screening results described above, the formulation of each component in the microemulsion was determined as follows: the mixed surfactant consisted of rhamnolipid and AEO-9 at a mass ratio of 2:1, the mass ratio of the mixed surfactant to the cosurfactant (glycerol) was Km = 3, the aqueous phase was a 0.5 mol/L CaCl2 solution, and the oil phase was palm oil. A pseudoternary phase diagram was constructed using this formulation, and the composition corresponding to point A in the diagram was selected as the microemulsion formulation for the preparation of nanoemulsions, as shown in Figure . This microemulsion was designated as #5, and the nanoemulsion was prepared using the microemulsion dilution method.

Particle Size Distribution and Zeta Potential

The size distributions of nanoemulsions obtained by diluting the microemulsion at various dilution multiples are presented in Figure . Overall, the mean droplet size of the nanoemulsions exhibited a decreasing trend with increasing dilution ratio. At low dilution ratios, the surfactant concentration remained substantially above the critical micelle concentration (CMC), which helped preserve the structural integrity of the droplets. Although dilution increased the interdroplet spacing, the high surfactant level effectively suppressed droplet aggregation, resulting in only minor changes in particle size. As the dilution ratio was further increased, the surfactant concentration gradually declined, accompanied by a corresponding reduction in droplet dimensions. At a 500-fold dilution, the resultant nanoemulsion displayed a relatively narrow size distribution centered in the range of 60–80 nm, with a zeta potential of −52.8 mV, the absolute value of which was well above 30 mV, indicating sufficient electrostatic repulsion for colloidal stability. Accordingly, the nanoemulsion prepared at 500-fold dilution was designated as #5–500 and selected for subsequent experiments.

Particle size distribution of nanoemulsions at different dilution ratios.

Wettability Evaluation

The wettability alteration performance of the nanoemulsion #5–500 viscosity-reducing system on rock surfaces was investigated, and the contact angle measurements of the core before and after treatment are presented in Figure . As observed, the contact angle decreased from 106.0° to 74°, indicating a transition of the rock surface from oil-wet to water-wet, which facilitates the detachment of heavy oil from the core surface. Therefore, during the enhanced oil recovery process for heavy oil, the wettability reversal effect of the viscosity-reducing system also contributes synergistically to the improvement in heavy oil recovery. The viscosity-reducing surfactant molecules align directionally at the oil–water interface, with hydrophobic groups oriented toward the oil phase and hydrophilic groups toward the aqueous phase, thereby reducing the interfacial tension and consequently enhancing the dispersion, emulsification, and viscosity-reduction performance for heavy oil.

Surface Tension and Interfacial Tension

As shown in Figure , the surface tension of the microemulsion gradually decreases with increasing concentration. This is due to the synergistic effect of the surfactant, cosurfactant, and oil phase in the microemulsion, which increases the spreading density at the solution surface as the microemulsion concentration rises. When the dilution concentration of #5 reaches 0.2 wt %, the surface tension decreases to 27 mN/m; further increasing the microemulsion concentration results in little change in surface tension. This trend indicates that the adsorption of surface-active species at the solution surface has reached saturation, and stable micellar structures begin to form inside the solution.

Surface tension of microemulsions with different concentrations.

Interfacial tension between #5 at various concentrations and S–1 crude oil was measured using a TX-500C tensiometer. Figure presents the interfacial tension as a function of #5 concentration. As the microemulsion concentration increased from 0 to 0.4 wt %, the interfacial tension decreased rapidly at first and then leveled off. #5 reached a minimum of 0.15 mN/m at 0.2 wt %. The surfactant adsorbs at the oil–water interface, reducing interfacial tension and yielding the observed low values. Lower interfacial tension favors both displacement of crude oil from reservoir pores and emulsion stabilization.

Evaluation of Viscosity Reduction Performance

The viscosity reduction rate is an important performance indicator for evaluating the viscosity-reducing effect of microemulsion #5. Therefore, the viscosity reduction effects of #5 and #5–500 on S-1 heavy oil were evaluated. To further investigate the viscosity reduction performance of the nanoviscosity reducer at different concentrations, the viscosity reduction rates of #5 and #5–500 on heavy oil were measured at 55 °C. The results showed that the viscosity reduction rate of #5 was 85.17%, while that of #5–500 reached 91.85%, indicating a superior viscosity reduction effect. This is because the original solution has a high concentration of active components, which tends to form multilayer adsorption or micellar aggregation at the oil–water interface, leading to increased rigidity of the interfacial film and hindering the formation of low-viscosity oil-in-water emulsions. Under appropriate dilution conditions, surfactant molecules can arrange more efficiently at the oil–water interface, forming a stable emulsion system and thereby significantly reducing the viscosity of heavy oil. The addition of the nanoviscosity reducer #5–500 enables the formation of an oil-in-water emulsion between the heavy oil and the surfactant solution. A water film can form around the heavy oil, greatly reducing its viscosity.

Core Flooding Experiments

The above findings indicate that nanoemulsion #5–500 exhibits a pronounced viscosity-reducing effect on S-1 heavy oil. To further evaluate the applicability of this technology in actual oilfield operations, core flooding experiments were conducted to assess the potential of microemulsion #5 and its nanoemulsion counterpart (#5–500) for enhancing oil recovery after waterflooding. To this end, four sets of core flooding tests were designed and performed, systematically investigating the displacement performance of the two viscosity reducers (microemulsion #5 and nanoemulsion #5–500) on S-1 heavy oil under different injection modes and temperature conditions. The increase in temperature reduces the viscosity of the crude oil to some extent, but simultaneously accelerates surfactant desorption and reduces interfacial activity, resulting in a slightly lower overall recovery than that achieved at 55 °C.

Displacement effect of diluted solution followed by undiluted solution at 65 °C.

As shown in Figure , at 65 °C, a sequential injection mode of stock solution followed by diluted solution was adopted. After water flooding reached a water cut of 98%, the recovery degree was 14.29%. Subsequently, 1 PV of undiluted #5 was injected, resulting in an increase in recovery degree of 30.56% over that of water flooding. Following this, 1 PV of #5–500 diluted solution was injected, leading to a further increase in recovery degree of 28.81% from the previous stage. Compared with water flooding alone, the final enhanced oil recovery reached 59.37%. The disadvantage of the “undiluted first, diluted later” sequence becomes more pronounced at elevated temperatures, as the adsorption loss of the high-concentration system increases, and the subsequent diluted solution is difficult to fully compensate, resulting in a further reduction in the incremental oil recovery.

Conclusion

Based on the comprehensive results from a series of experiments including preparation, characterization, and physical simulation, the following conclusions are drawn:1.When rhamnolipid and nonionic surfactant AEO-9 were mixed at a mass ratio of 2:1, the hydrophilic–lipophilic balance (HLB) value of the mixed surfactant was 20.44. Under this condition, the mixed surfactant exhibited excellent emulsifying performance, with an emulsification capacity of 66% for palm oil. Through pseudoternary phase diagram analysis, glycerol was identified as the optimal cosurfactant, and the best performance was achieved at a mass ratio of mixed surfactant to cosurfactant of Km = 3. A novel green nanoemulsion viscosity reducer was successfully prepared.2.Both the original viscosity reducer formulation (#5) and its diluted form (#5–500) demonstrated excellent viscosity reduction performance for heavy oil, achieving viscosity reduction rates of 85.17% and 91.85%, respectively, at 55 °C.3.Core displacement experiment results indicated that the viscosity reducer exhibited optimal displacement performance at 55 °C. Regardless of whether the diluted solution was injected first followed by the original solution, or vice versa, the enhanced oil recovery exceeded 65%. Overall, the nanoemulsion viscosity reducer exhibits promising application potential under laboratory conditions, providing a technical reference for chemical flooding development in offshore heavy oil fields.
