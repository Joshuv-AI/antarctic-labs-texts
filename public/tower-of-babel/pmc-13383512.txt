Background

Knots can occasionally loosen unexpectedly, particularly when suturing at awkward angles and when visibility or depth perception is limited [1], especially during cardiac valve replacement surgery. A loosened knot can be a potential obstacle and may require reintervention, which adds additional time and leads to severe consequences such as pledgets falling into the cardiac cavity, increasing the risk of cerebrovascular accidents [1–2]. Knotting on the annulus remains a major challenge that may result in complications such as paravalvular leaks (PVLs) [3–4]. However, reviews and reports on immediate and effective strategies for managing a loosened knot during cardiac surgery are scarce. Therefore, we describe a series of techniques for addressing this issue in emergency situations.

The following four practical and convenient surgical techniques can be performed when a loosened knot on the annulus is encountered during cardiac surgery. Line drawings are presented to provide details regarding the key steps of these techniques.

The titanium clip method

Typically, approximately 5–6 knots are tied on the sewing ring. If a knot loosens, particularly the first or second knot (Fig. 1A), then the stitches should be continuously wound several times using a wire hook, followed by the application of one or two medium-sized of titanium clips either on the knot below or on the bottom, to secure the loosened knot and fix it in place (Fig. 1B). We have performed this method, which we refer to as the titanium clip method, during surgery (Fig. 1C). An insightful and vivid visualization of this technique is provided in Additional file 1.

PVL repair technique

A loosened knot often leads to stitch-wide leakage, which can be addressed using techniques for repairing PVLs. Our team has summarized a series of techniques for managing various sizes of PVLs, including the supravalvular repair method (Fig. 2A), folding-type sealing method (Fig. 2B), complex supravalvular and subvalvular repair method (Fig. 2C), and pericardium patch repair method (Fig. 2D). These techniques have been published in the Journal of Cardiac Surgery [5–6]. Surgeons can select the most appropriate technique based on the PVL diameter and specific clinical situations.

(A-D) The line drawings show four techniques for managing various sizes PVLs

Direct suturing of the polyester fabric of the sewing ring

If a knot cannot be tightened during surgery, especially during minimally invasive surgery, then surgeons can use an additional stitch to suture directly onto the polyester fabric of the sewing ring (Fig. 3A). Ensure that the stitch is made through the polyester fabric rather than attempting to tie another knot adjacent to the loosened knot.

(A) The line drawing shows using an additional stitch to suture directly onto the polyester fabric of the sewing ring. (B) The line drawing shows skipping one knot from the loosened knot and suturing with another stitch

Skipping and suturing with another stitch

If a knot cannot be tightened during surgery, specifically the first knot, then surgeons can place an additional stitch while skipping one or two knots from the loosened knot (Fig. 3B). Suturing the loosened knot directly with the subsequent knot will pull the loosened knot further and fail to tighten it; therefore, one or two knots should be skipped before suturing to allow tightening.

Discussion

Loosened knots are highly undesirable events for surgeons. Nowadays, self-tying devices are commonly used, which bring great convenience to operation [7]. However, cardiac surgeries, such as valve replacement surgeries, often involve awkward angles and require many knots. Therefore, loosened knots are inevitable whether manual tying or using knot instruments, especially some complex procedures. Such incidents necessitate additional operative time for reintervention, considerably prolonging the intraoperative cardiopulmonary bypass time, thereby increasing the risk of complications. Moreover, undetected or inadequately addressed loosened knots can most commonly lead to PVLs and affect long-term postoperative outcomes [8]. Thus, we comprehensively propose these techniques as emergency solutions to prevent more serious consequences.

Originally, we summarized these simple yet practical techniques for surgeons to manage the unexpectedly loosened knots in emergencies. However, we must acknowledge that the potential risk of clip displacement does exist. Thus, we treat these techniques as emergency management. And the device is not designed for tightening a knot indeed, if surgeons adhere to the principle can only abandon this method. In addition, several other issues remain to be discussed. These emergency techniques are based on our clinical experience, currently lacking the evidence regarding methodological rigor and safety validation, nevertheless they have been applied in clinical practice and achieved satisfactory results. We suggest that surgeons select the most suitable alternative techniques based on the specific intraoperative conditions. These techniques can be used during both general open-heart and minimally invasive surgeries, and we believe they can save considerable time and reduce unnecessary complications.

Conclusion

These techniques offer cardiac surgeons some simple yet practical alternatives for managing unexpectedly loosened knots and their associated complications. By avoiding reintervention, we believe these techniques are highly beneficial in intraoperative emergencies.
