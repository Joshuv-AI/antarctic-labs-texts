Palmatine Attenuates Lipopolysaccharide-Induced Acute Lung Injury Via Suppression of NLRP3 Inflammasome Activation, Pyroptosis, and Metabolic Remodeling
Authors: Pu Ning, Jing Wu, Qiuyue Long, Yaolin Zheng, Jialing Gan, Xiaoyi Hu, Shixu Song, Xiaomin Li, Mingzheng Jiang, Hongli Ye, Wenyi Yu, Yukun He, Yali Zheng, Zhancheng Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute lung injury (ALI) is a critical condition characterized by uncontrolled inflammation, respiratory insufficiency, and tissue damage, often triggered by pneumonia or sepsis. Aberrant activation of the NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain associated protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and subsequent pyroptosis are key drivers of ALI pathogenesis. Palmatine (PAL), a naturally derived isoquinoline alkaloid with diverse pharmacological effects, was investigated for the therapeutic potential against lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced ALI in this study, focusing on NLRP3 inflammasome, pyroptosis, and metabolic regulation. Our findings showed that PAL significantly suppressed NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis in LPS/adenosine triphosphate (ATP)-stimulated THP-1 macrophages and inhibited M1 macrophage polarization. In C57BL/6J mice subjected to intratracheal LPS challenge, PAL alleviated lung histopathological injury, decreased tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin (IL)-6, IL-1β, and IL-18 levels in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, and reduced lung wet-to-dry ratio and lung tissue myeloperoxidase activity. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that PAL markedly attenuated LPS-induced upregulation of NLRP3 and Gasdermin-D (GSDMD). PAL also downregulated the mRNA expression of Caspase-1, Apoptosis-associated speck-like protein (Asc), High-mobility group box 1 (Hmgb1), Il1b, and Il18, as well as the protein levels of cleaved Caspase-1 (p20), GSDMD-N and Caspase-11 in lung tissue. Metabolomic profiling indicated PAL-driven metabolic reprogramming involving the oxidation of branched-chain fatty acids and very long-chain fatty acids. Integrated multi-omics analysis highlighted cytosolic DNA-sensing and NOD-like receptor signaling as key pathways underlying PAL’s effects. Collectively, PAL mitigates ALI by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation, suppressing pyroptosis, and reprogramming metabolism, supporting its potential as a therapeutic candidate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-025-02358-6.Keywords: Acute lung injury, Palmatine, Macrophages, NLRP3 inflammasome, Pyroptosis, Metabolomics

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI) is a common acute inflammatory respiratory disorder with high incidence and mortality [1]. Its more severe manifestation, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), remains a major global health challenge, affecting about three million people with a 30–40% mortality rate [2]. Patients with ALI present with uncontrolled pulmonary inflammation, respiratory distress, acute hypoxemia, disruptions of the alveolar-capillary barrier function, and non-cardiac respiratory failure. Although recent advances such as lung-protective ventilation, prone positioning, and extracorporeal support have improved outcomes, no effective pharmacological treatments for ALI are currently available [3]. Therefore, clarifying its pathological mechanisms and developing novel therapeutic agents remains crucial.

The infiltration of inflammatory cells and upregulation pro-inflammatory cytokines are key pathophysiological hallmarks of ALI [4]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome plays a pivotal role in ALI [5, 6]. The NLRP3 inflammasome, a cytoplasmic multiprotein complex, comprised of NLRP3 protein, apoptosis-associated speck-like protein (ASC), and pro-caspase-1, is activated by diverse pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), leading to the maturation and secretion of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-18 [7]. Pyroptosis is a form of programmed cell death, mediated by inflammasome-induced activation of caspase-1 and caspase-11, which cleaves Gasdermin D (GSDMD) to form membrane pores [8], causing cell swelling and rupture. While appropriate activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome is critical for host defense against pathogens, excessive activation induces severe lung tissue damage and organ dysfunction [9]. Targeting inflammasomes and their downstream pyroptotic pathways has attracted growing interest as a potential therapeutic strategy for ALI [10–13].

Palmatine (PAL) is an isoquinoline alkaloid primarily found in Phellodendron amurense Rupr [14]., Coptis chinensis Franch [15]., and Corydalis yanhusuo W. T. Wang ex Z. Y. Su & C. Y. Wu [16]. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated PAL exhibits a wide range of pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, anti-bacterial, immune-enhancing, and anti-tumor effects [17]. It has been investigated for therapeutic potential in cancer [18], diabetes [19], atherosclerosis [20], gouty [21], and chronic colitis [22]. Recent studies show that PAL ameliorates lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced sepsis-associated encephalopathy in mice [23] and alleviates LPS-induced ALI by suppressing the activation of bone marrow-derived macrophages and alveolar macrophages through the NF-κB and p38 MAPK signaling pathways [24]. PAL also mitigates particulate matter-induced ALI by inhibiting pyroptosis via Nrf2 signaling pathway activation [25]. Additionally, PAL improves LPS-induced ALI by suppressing M1 macrophage polarization through NAMPT/TLR2/CCR1 signaling axis [26]. Despite these advances, the precise roles and underlying mechanisms of PAL in LPS-induced ALI remain incompletely understood. Notably, no studies have explored the therapeutic mechanisms of PAL in ALI from a metabolomics perspective.

In this study, we investigated whether PAL exerts its protective effects against LPS -induced ALI by targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome and pyroptosis. To clarify its deeper mechanisms, integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were performed in a mouse model of LPS-induced ALI.

Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

Palmatine (HPLC ≥ 98%, HY-N0110A), phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, HY-18739), MCC950 sodium (HY-12815 A), and adenosine triphosphate (ATP, HY-B2176) were purchased from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). LPS (Escherichia coli 055: B5, L2880) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, CK-04) was from Dojindo Laboratories (Shanghai, China). BCA Protein Assay Kit (23227), human interferon-γ (IFN-γ) (AF-300-02), IL-4 (200-04), and IL-13 (200 − 13) were from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Primary antibodies, including NLRP3 (30109-1-AP), caspase-1 (22915-1-AP), ASC (10500-1-AP), GSDMD (20770-1-AP), HMGB-1 (10829-1-AP), iNOS (22226-1-AP), Arg-1 (16001-1-AP), and GAPDH (81640-5-RR) were from Proteintech (Wuhan, China); cleaved caspase-1 p20 (4199T) from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA); cleaved N-terminal GSDMD (ab215203) and caspase-11 (ab180673) from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA). RNA was extracted using the SteayPure Universal RNA Extraction Kit II (AG21022, Accurate Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Hunan, China). Reverse transcription and qPCR were performed with Takara kits (RR047A and RR820A) (Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Japan). Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (A0423), Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (A0473), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) cytotoxicity assay kits (C0016) were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Myeloperoxidase (MPO) assay kit (BC5715) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits for tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α, SEKM-0034), interleukin-6 (IL-6, SEKM-0007), IL-1β (SEKM-0002 and SEKH-0002), and IL-18 (SEKM-0019 and SEKH-0028) were purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China). Tribromoethanol (M2910) was sourced from Lab Animal Technology Development Co., Ltd (Beijing, China).

Cells Culture and Treatment

A human monocyte leukemia cell line (THP-1) was obtained from the Cell Resource Center of Peking Union Medical College (Beijing, China) and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA), 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, USA), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution (Sigma, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Cells were differentiated into macrophage-like cells by treatment with 100 ng/ml PMA for 24 h [27].

Then the cells were divided into six groups: (I) Control, (II) LPS, (III) LPS + MCC950, (IV) LPS + PAL-L (50 µM), (V) LPS + PAL-M (100 µM), and (VI) LPS + PAL-H (200 µM). The concentrations of PAL were based on previous literature [28] and our CCK-8 results. Except for the control group, cells were first primed with LPS (1 µg/mL) [29] and treated with MCC950 (1 µM) [30] or PAL for 12 h, followed by ATP (5 mM) stimulation for 1 h [31]. Then the cells were collected for further analysis.

CCK-8 Assay

THP-1 cells (1 × 10⁶ cells/mL) were seeded into 96-well plates, incubated with PMA for 24 h, and then exposed to different concentrations of PAL (0–400 µM) for 24 h, with untreated cells as control. After rinsing with phosphate-buffered saline, cells were incubated with 200 µL of culture medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent for 3 h at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Multiskan FC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Cell viability was calculated as: cell viability=(Aexperiment-Ablank)/(Acontrol-Ablank)×100%.

LDH Cytotoxicity Assay

Cell culture supernatants were collected and analyzed using the LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 1 h prior to the scheduled time point, 10% of the culture volume was transferred to the wells designated for maximum enzyme activity. After incubation at 37 °C for 1 h, the culture plates were centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min. Then, 120 µL of the supernatant from each well was transferred into a new 96-well plate, mixed with 60 µL of freshly prepared LDH detection working solution, and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm (reference wavelength: 600 nm) using a microplate reader (Multiskan FC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Immunofluorescence Analysis

THP-1 cells (2 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded into 24-well plates, treated with 100 ng/ml PMA for 24 h, and then divided into four groups: (I) control, (II) LPS, (III) LPS + MCC950, and (IV) LPS + PAL. Except for the control group, cells were stimulated with LPS (1 µg/mL) and treated with MCC950 (1 µM) or PAL (200 µM), followed by ATP (5 mM) for 1 h. After fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min and blocked with 5% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against NLRP3 (1:400), ASC (1:400), and caspase-1 (1:100), followed by Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:500) or Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:500) for 2 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained with DAPI overnight at 4 °C, and images were acquired using an FV1000 confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at 100× magnification.

Macrophage Polarization and Treatment

THP-1 cells (5 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated with 100 ng/ml PMA for 24 h. LPS (100 ng/mL)/IFN-γ (20 ng/mL) or IL-4 (20 ng/mL)/IL-13 (20 ng/mL) [32], with or without PAL (200 µM), were added to stimulate M1 or M2 polarization for 48 h. Cells were then collected for subsequent analysis.

Molecular Docking

To verify the effects of PAL on NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, molecular docking was used to evaluate the affinity of NLRP3 and caspase-1 with PAL. The 3D crystal structure of NLRP3 (PDB ID: 7VTQ) was gained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) database (https://www.rcsb.org), and caspase-1 was retrieved from AlphaFold 3. The 2D structure of PAL was obtained from PubChem. Water molecules and original ligands in the NLRP3 orcaspase-1 structures were removed using PyMOL (Version 3.0.3). The target proteins were then prepared in AutoDock Tools (Version 1.5.7) for hydrogenation, charge calculation, and non-polar hydrogen combination, and the result was stored in PDBQT format. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina (Version 1.2.3) [23, 33], and the results were visualized with PyMOL and LigPlot (Version 2.2.9).

Animals Experiment Protocol

Male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks, 20–25 g) were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Guangdong, China; license number SYXK (Yue) 2023 − 0341). Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at 23 ± 2 °C, 55 ± 10% humidity, and a 12 h light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water.

A total of 40 mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 10): (I) control, (II) LPS, (III) LPS + MCC950, and (IV) LPS + PAL. Randomization was performed using a random number generator to minimize allocation bias. On Day 1, mice in the MCC950 and PAL groups were pretreated with MCC950 (10 mg/kg) [34] via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection or PAL (100 mg/kg) [25, 35] via intragastric (i.g.) administration, respectively. One hour later, mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal tribromoethanol (0.2 mL/10 g body weight) and then received intratracheal instillation of either sterile PBS or LPS (5 mg/kg) [36]. Above treatments continued once daily for subsequent two days, and mice were euthanized 72 h after LPS administration. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and lung tissues were collected for analysis.

To investigate the effects of PAL treatment on the transcriptomic and metabolomic profiles compared to LPS exposure, an additional cohort of 12 male mice was randomly divided into three groups (n = 4 per group): control, LPS, and LPS + PAL (100 mg/kg). Treatment and sample collections were performed as described above.

Lung Wet-to-Dry (W/D) Weight Ratio

The W/D ratio of the lung was determined to evaluate pulmonary edema. Briefly, surface moisture of the left lung was gently blotted, and the wet weight was immediately measured using an analytical balance. The tissue was then dried in an oven at 60 °C for 72 h until a constant weight was achieved. The dry weight was subsequently recorded, and the W/D ratio was calculated to assess the extent of lung water content.

Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (BALF) Collect

Mice were anesthetized and intubated via the trachea. A total volume of 1 mL sterile PBS was instilled into the lungs through the endotracheal tube [37]. The bronchoalveolar space was gently lavaged three times, and the recovered BALF (0.7–0.8 mL per mouse) was collected for analysis.

Protein Concentration Measurement

BALF were centrifuged at 1,500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected. The total protein concentration in the BALF supernatant was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The reaction plate was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, and the absorbance was measured at 562 nm using a microplate reader.

Myeloperoxidase (MPO) Measurement

MPO activity was measured using a commercially assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. One unit of MPO activity was defined as the amount of enzyme required to degrade 1 µM of hydrogen peroxide per gram of tissue per minute at 37 °C under the assay conditions.

Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) Staining and Histological Observation

Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by graded ethanol dehydration and paraffin embedding. Paraffin sections were subsequently stained with H&E using standard protocols. Histopathological changes were observed under an optical microscope at 200 × magnification, and representative images were captured for analysis. For each specimen, three slides were analyzed. Ten fields of view (200×) per slide were randomly selected for observation. The histopathological changes were visualized by two experienced investigators which were blinded to group arrangement.

ELISA

Cell culture supernatants were centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the concentrations of secreted IL-1β and IL-18 were quantified using ELISA kits, following the manufacturers’ instructions. The levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-18 in BALF supernatants were similarly determined. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was extracted from cell lysates and lung tissues using the SteayPure Universal RNA Extraction Kit II. The concentration and purity of RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). One microgram of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and qRT-PCR was performed on StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Primers for target genes (NLRP3, Caspase-1, ASC, IL-1β, IL-18, GSDMD, High-mobility group box 1 (HMGB-1), iNOS, Arg-1, CD86, CD206) and reference genes (β-actin, GAPDH) were synthesized by Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The specific primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method normalized to β-actin or GAPDH.

Western Blot

Total protein was extracted from cell lysates and lung tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined by the BCA method. Equal amounts of protein (25 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore, Massachusetts, USA). After blocking with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China) and imaged with Tanon 4600 system (Shanghai Tanon Science & Technology Co., Ltd.). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA) and normalized to GAPDH. All analysis was carried out by a colleague blinded to the experimental group.

Transcriptome Sequencing and Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from lung tissues using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, California, USA). RNA quality and comcentration were evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Libraries were constructed from 1 µg RNA per sample and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform to produce 150 bp paired-end reads. Before analysis, sequencing reads were subjected to strict quality control (QC) to ensure the accuracy of subsequent analyses. The following filtering steps were applied: (1) Reads containing adapter sequences were removed; (2) Low-quality reads were filtered out, including those with more than 10% unknown nucleotides or with more than 50% of bases having a quality score ≤ 10. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) analysis between different groups was performed using the DESeq2 package in R (version 4.4.2). P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Genes with FDR < 0.05 and |log₂ FC| ≥ 1 were defined as DEGs. Volcano plots and heatmaps were generated using ggplot2 and pheatmap packages. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed with clusterProfiler package in R, and the results were visualized using ggplot2. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis and Venn diagram generation were using OmicShare tools (https://www.omicshare.com/tools/) [38]. Pathways with an adjusted FDR < 0.05 after Benjamini–Hochberg correction were considered significantly enriched.

Ultra-High Performance Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) Analysis

Lung tissue was homogenized in 400 µl precooled methanol and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant (200 µl) was mixed with 200 µl water, freeze-dried, resuspended, and prepared for LC-MS analysis. UHPLC–MS/MS experimental details, including instrument parameters and data analysis methods, are provided in the Supplementary Information. QC samples were analyzed to ensure system repeatability and stability. Metabolites with > 80% missing values or coefficient of variation (CV) > 30% in QC samples were excluded. Differential metabolites (DMs) were identified by principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/). A 200-time permutation test was performed with SIMCA 16.1, and the Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) value was calculated using cross-validation. Metabolites with VIP ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05 were selected as DMs. Volcano plots were generated by ggplot2 and pheatmap packages in R. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis and integrated transcriptomics - metabolomics analysis [39] were conducted using MetaboAnalyst 6.0. Unadjusted P-values < 0.05 were used to identify potentially relevant pathways. Heatmaps illustrating DMs were generated using the OmicShare tools.

Statistics and Analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (mean ± SEM). Normal distribution of the data was assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test was applied for multiple group comparisons. For comparisons between two groups, two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test was used. The sample sizes (n) are indicated in the figure legends. All experiments were repeated independently three times with similar results. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

PAL Suppresses LPS/ATP-Stimulated IL-1β and IL-18 Secretion in THP-1 Macrophages

To assess the cytotoxicity of PAL on THP-1 cells, cell viability was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 1a, pretreatment with varying concentrations of PAL had no significant effect on THP-1 cell viability. Thus, concentrations of 50, 100, and 200 µM were selected for subsequent experiments. Using an LPS + ATP inflammasome activation model, ELISA results revealed that LPS/ATP stimulation markedly elevated IL-1β (Fig. 1b) and IL-18 (Fig. 1c) secretion, whereas PAL treatment significantly and dose-dependently reduced both proinflammatory cytokines.

PAL Inhibits NLRP3 Inflammasome Activation and Pyroptosis in THP-1 Macrophages

Given that PAL significantly reduced IL-1β and IL-18 secretion following LPS/ATP stimulation, we further investigated its effect on NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis. LPS/ATP markedly upregulated NLRP3 mRNA (Fig. 1d) and protein levels (Fig. 1g), which were dose-dependently suppressed by PAL. Similarly, PAL significantly suppressed the mRNA and protein expression levels of Caspase-1 (Fig. 1e and h) and ASC (Fig. 1f and i) in a dose-dependent manner, suggesting inhibition of inflammasome activation. PAL reduced the LPS/ATP-induced mRNA expression of GSDMD, HMGB-1, and IL-1β (Fig. 2a-c) in THP-1 macrophages, and the protein expression of GSDMD, cleaved GSDMD-N, and HMGB-1 (Fig. 2d-f), as shown in representative immunoblot (Fig. 2g). Furthermore, PAL significantly decreased LDH release, indicating a reduction in membrane rupture and pyroptotic cell death (Fig. 2h).

Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed these findings. LPS/ATP stimulation enhanced the expression of NLRP3, ASC, and caspase-1 (Fig. 3a-b) in THP-1 macrophages, which was markedly suppressed by PAL treatment (Fig. 3c-e). PAL also reduced the co-localization of NLRP3 with ASC, as well as caspase-1 with ASC (Fig. 3f-g), suggesting disruption of inflammasome complex formation. Interestingly, treatment with the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 did not significantly diminish the co-localization of NLRP3 and ASC. The results implied that PAL may interfere with NLRP3 inflammasome assembly through mechanisms beyond simple NLRP3 inhibition.

Molecular docking was used to assess the interaction between PAL and the NLRP3 inflammasome. The binding affinity was evaluated with a threshold of −5.0 kcal/mol, with lower values indicating more stable binding. The results showed that PAL bound to NLRP3 and Caspase-1 with binding energies of −5.783 kcal/mol and − 7.513 kcal/mol, respectively. PAL binds to NLRP3 by forming hydrogen bonds with residues TRP956 and ASP980, and hydrophobic interactions with THR985 (Fig. 3h-i). The interaction between PAL and Caspase-1 primarily involved hydrogen bonding—SER18 and ARG339, π-cation interactions—ARG381, and hydrophobic interactions—TRP338, LEU346, and ARG381 (Fig. 3j-k). These results indicate that PAL has strong binding activities with NLRP3 inflammasome pathway.

PAL Inhibits M1 Polarization in THP-1 Macrophages

Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome is closely associated with macrophage polarization. We found PAL alone did not affect macrophage polarization toward either the M1 or M2 phenotype at either the mRNA or protein level. Under LPS/IFN-γ stimulation, PAL dose-dependently downregulated CD86 mRNA expression (Fig. 4a), and PAL (200 µM) significantly suppressed iNOS mRNA expression (Fig. 4b). Under IL-4/IL-13 stimulation, PAL increased CD206 mRNA expression (Fig. 4c), and at 200 µM, enhanced Arg-1 mRNA expression (Fig. 4d). At the protein level, PAL inhibited the expression of iNOS (Fig. 4e and g), whereas did not significantly affect Arg-1 (P > 0.05) (Fig. 4f and h). These findings suggest that PAL primarily M1 macrophage polarization, potentially contributing to its beneficial effects.

PAL Protects Mice against LPS-Induced ALI and Attenuates Pulmonary Inflammation

To assess the protective effects of PAL in vivo, mice were pretreated with PAL before LPS intratracheal instillation (Fig. 5a). The lung W/D ratio, BALF protein concentration, and lung tissue MPO activity were evaluated as key indicators of pulmonary edema, microvascular permeability and neutrophil infiltration. LPS challenge significantly increased all three parameters, whereas treatment with PAL or the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 markedly reduced these elevations (Fig. 5b-d). Lung tissues H&E staining (Fig. 5e) revealed that LPS caused severe lung injury, including thickened alveolar walls, extensive inflammatory cell infiltration, and widened interstitial spaces, indicating the onset of acute inflammation and structural disruption, whereas PAL pretreatment markedly attenuated these pathological changes.

ELISA analysis revealed that LPS markedly increased TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-18 in BALF, whereas PAL treatment significantly suppressed these cytokines (Fig. 5f-i). Interestingly, although MCC950 alleviated histological lung injury, it failed to reduce TNF-α and IL-18 levels in BALF. Collectively, these results suggest that PAL exerts protective effects against LPS-induced ALI by mitigating pulmonary edema, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and preserving lung tissue integrity.

Transcriptome Sequencing Identifies Nlrp3 and Gsdmd as Key Genes of PAL in the Treatment of ALI

To elucidate the underlying anti-inflammatory mechanisms of PAL in ALI, transcriptomic profiling of mice lung tissue was performed. PCA revealed a clear separation among the control, LPS, and PAL-treated groups (Fig. 6a), indicating substantial transcriptomic alterations induced by LPS and partial restoration of gene expression patterns following PAL treatment.

Between LPS and control groups, 563 DEGs (|log₂ FC| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05) were detected, including 266 upregulated and 297 downregulated genes (Fig. 6b). In the PAL vs. LPS comparison, 304 DEGs were identified, with 209 genes upregulated and 95 downregulated. Volcano plots illustrated the distribution of DEGs (Fig. 6c-d). Notably, Nlrp3, Caspase-1, and Gsdmd were significantly upregulated in LPS-induced ALI but markedly downregulated following PAL treatment, suggesting suppression of inflammasome-related pyroptosis. Venn network diagram analysis revealed an overlap of seven DEGs between the LPS vs. control and PAL vs. LPS comparison groups (Fig. 6g), among which Nlrp3 and Gsdmd were identified as potential key targets mediating the therapeutic effects of PAL in ALI.

Transcriptome Analysis Revealed that the Therapeutic Effect of PAL on ALI Related to Inflammatory Response and Pyroptosis Pathways

To identify the pathways potentially affected by PAL treatment, DEGs (|log₂FC| ≥ 1.5 and FDR < 0.05) were analyzed by KEGG. Enrichment results were visualized in circular (Fig. 7a-b) and bubble diagrams (Fig. 7c-d). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that LPS-induced ALI primarily involved Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, Th17 cell differentiation, natural killer cell mediated cytotoxicity, cytokine and cytokine receptor interactions, antigen processing and presentation, T cell receptor signaling pathway and arachidonic acid metabolism. These pathways are closely associated with the inflammatory response, tissue damage, and fatty acid metabolism dysregulation in ALI. PAL treatment significantly modulated several of these pathways.

GO enrichment analysis revealed that LPS challenge activated pathways related to lymphocyte-mediated immunity, cell killing, regulation of immune effector processes, inflammatory response, and the canonical inflammasome complex (Fig. 7e). In contrast, PAL altered genes involved in lymphocyte-mediated immunity, immune response, the canonical inflammasome complex, and the granzyme-mediated programmed cell death signaling pathway (Fig. 7f), which is associated with cell pyroptosis. These findings suggest that LPS induces a robust inflammatory and immune response, particularly involving immune-mediated cytotoxicity mechanisms by natural killer cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes, whereas PAL mitigates immune effects by regulating inflammasome complex and immune cell death.

PAL Reverses ALI by Inhibiting the NLRP3 Signaling and Pyroptosis Pathway in Mice

The mRNA expression levels of lung tissue were assessed by qPCR to verify the transcriptomics results. PAL pretreatment significantly reduced the mRNA levels of Nlrp3, Caspase-1, Asc, Gsdmd, Hmgb1, Il1b and Il18 (Fig. 8a-g). Consistently, western blot analysis (Fig. 8h-n and r) revealed that PAL notably reduced the protein expression of NLRP3, Caspase-1, ASC, GSDMD, and HMGB-1, and the levels of cleaved Caspase-1 (p20) and cleaved GSDMD-N, indicating suppression of the canonical NLRP3-Caspase-1-GSDMD pyroptosis pathway. Interestingly, PAL also downregulated Caspase-11 and cleaved Caspase-11 (Fig. 8o-p and s), which may suggest inhibition of the noncanonical pyroptosis pathway. Notably, both mRNA and protein analyses showed that MCC950 did not reduce GSDMD expression, nor did it decrease the protein levels of HMGB-1 and GSDMD-N.

To further investigate the potential implications of the observed metabolite changes, metabolites with a VIP > 1 and FDR < 0.05 were selected for metabolic pathway analysis. A total of 109 metabolites were significantly dysregulated by LPS, with 100 metabolites upregulated and 9 downregulated. KEGG enrichment analysis identified pathways including glutathione metabolism, beta-alanine metabolism, the Warburg effect, the urea cycle, ammonia recycling, the pentose phosphate pathway, and amino acid metabolism (Fig. 9d). These results suggest that LPS-induced ALI induces substantial metabolic reprogramming, particularly affecting energy metabolism, oxidative stress response, and amino acid metabolism. PAL treatment mainly affected the oxidation of branched-chain fatty acids and beta-oxidation of very long-chain fatty acids (Fig. 9e), implying the modulation of energy and lipid metabolism and cell membrane stability, thereby alleviating LPS-induced inflammation and metabolic imbalances.

Differential metabolites (|log₂FC| > 1 and FDR < 0.05) were selected for visualization in the volcano plot (Fig. 9f-g). To further identify the most important and core DMs, selection criteria were applied based on a covariance (absolute p) > 0.1 in the S-plot (Supplementary Fig. S2), VIP > 1, and FDR < 0.05 in the comparison between the LPS and control groups. Ten metabolites were found to be significantly dysregulated in the LPS group. The relative intensities of sulfuric acid, thromboxane B₂, Lyso PE 16:0, 3-hydroxybutyric acid, acetyl-L-carnitine, DL-norleucine, palmitoylcarnitine, Lyso PC 16:0, and ACar 18:1 were increased, whereas the level of caffeine was decreased (Fig. 9h). In PAL-treated mice, the relative intensities of 2-amino-1,3,4-octadecanetriol, acetyl-L-carnitine, Lyso PC 16:0, eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), D-mannose 6-phosphate, and docosapentaenoic acid (DPA) in lung tissues were reduced (Fig. 9i).

Multi-Omics Integration Identifies Key Metabolic and Immune Pathways Modulated by PAL in ALI

The integrated analysis of transcriptomic and metabolomic datasets was conducted (Fig. 9j-k and Table S4-S5) and revealed that the therapeutic effects of PAL in ALI were primarily associated with steroid hormone biosynthesis, linoleic acid metabolism, ether lipid metabolism, and glycerophospholipid metabolism, as well as Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation. Notably, the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway and the NOD-like receptor signaling pathway were also implicated, suggesting a close association with inflammasome activation and pyroptotic processes.

Discussion

ALI is a common life-threatening disease with poor prognosis, triggered by multiple factors. NLRP3 inflammasome plays a pivotal role in initiating inflammatory cascade in ALI/ARDS [13]. Suppressing excessive inflammation and inhibiting inflammasome activation are key strategies for the treatment of ALI. PAL has shown anti-inflammatory and protective effects in animal models of ALI [24, 25]. In the present study, we demonstrated that PAL protects THP-1 macrophages subjected to LPS and ATP stimulation by inhibiting the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome–associated pyroptotic pathway, and alleviated murine ALI through the NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptotic pathway and modulating the metabolic state in vivo, thereby exerting its protective effects against ALI.

Pyroptosis is a pro-inflammatory form of programmed cell death mediated by inflammatory caspases activated by inflammasomes. It primarily occurs through two pathways: the canonical NLRP3–caspase-1 pathway and the noncanonical caspase-4/5/11 pathway. Unlike non-inflammatory cell death forms such as apoptosis, necroptosis, or ferroptosis, pyroptosis involves membrane pore formation, cell lysis, and robust inflammatory responses. As illustrated in Fig. 10, at the priming phase, LPS, as the prototypical class of PAMPs, is recognized by Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), activating nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signaling and inducing pro-IL-1β, pro-IL-18, and NLRP3 protein expression. At the activation phase, diverse stimuli, including extracellular ATP, crystallization, potassium (K+) efflux [40], lysosomal damage, and mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) [41], trigger NLRP3 assembly. Activated NLRP3 cleaves caspase-1 cleavage, leading to IL-1β/IL-18 maturation and GSDMD-dependent pore formation, culminating in pyroptotic death. In parallel, noncanonical pyroptosis mediated by caspase-4/5 in human and caspase-11 in mice is also critical in endotoxemia [42]. Intracellular LPS directly activates caspase-4/5/11, leading to GSDMD cleavage and pore formation independently of caspase-1. This pathway contributes to cytokine release, endothelial barrier disruption [43], and vascular leakage in ALI/ARDS, as well as coagulation abnormalities during sepsis. In the present study, we focused on the canonical inflammasome pathway and demonstrated that PAL suppressed NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis both in vitro and in vivo, highlighting its therapeutic potential in attenuating ALI. Notably, we also observed that PAL reduced Caspase-11 expression, suggesting that its protective effects may also involve the noncanonical pyroptosis pathway.

Proposed mechanism of PAL in attenuating ALI by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis

Consistent with previous study [26], our results showed that PAL treatment could downregulate the levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-18 in BALF, alleviated histopathological changes, including interstitial edema, inflammatory cells infiltration, and increased vascular permeability. PAL also significantly reduced lung tissue MPO activity, a marker of neutrophil infiltration, which promotes inflammation and oxidative stress [44]. Recent evidence supports the link between oxidative stress, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and the potential modulatory role of PAL. Oral administration of PAL (50 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg) attenuated particulate matter-induced lung inflammation and ALI by activating the Nrf2-related antioxidant pathway, suppressing NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis, and reducing intracellular ROS production [25]. Additionally, PAL reduced apoptosis and oxidative stress through BAX/Bcl-2 and Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathways, alleviating LPS-induced depressive-like behavior [45]. This multi-layered regulatory network involving the “inflammation-oxidative stress-cell death” pathways suggests that PAL exerts multi-target therapeutic effects against ALI.

Previous studies have demonstrated that NLRP3 inflammasome activation is an important factor in M1 polarization of macrophages and macrophage pyroptosis. Inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome activation could reduce TMAO-stimulated M1 features [46]. Moreover, pyroptosis inhibitors could affect the direction of alveolar macrophages polarization in mice with ALI caused by sepsis [47]. In our study, PAL treatment profoundly inhibited M1 macrophages markers (iNOS and CD86) expression in THP-1 cells. Previous research has shown that PAL alleviates intestinal inflammation through inhibition of M1 macrophage polarization, reduces levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and enhances tight junction protein ZO-1 expression, thereby restoring intestinal barrier dysfunction in DSS-induced experimental colitis [22]. These findings suggest that PAL modulates macrophage polarization and exerts anti-inflammatory effects, which may contribute to its therapeutic potential in ALI.

In this study, we employed lung tissue metabolomics to investigate the mechanisms underlying PAL’s therapeutic effects on ALI. Key metabolic pathways involved in PAL’s action on ALI were identified, including the oxidation of branched-chain fatty acids and beta-oxidation of very long-chain fatty acids. We observed a reduction in the relative intensity of Lyso PC 16:0 in the PAL-treated group. As a structural component of cell membranes, alterations in Lyso-PCs can disturb cell function and signaling by promoting positive curvature and facilitating pore formation. Thus, the reduction observed here may represent a protective mechanism against pyroptotic cell death. Additionally, acetyl-L-carnitine is rapidly consumed to fuel mitochondrial β-oxidation and the TCA cycle, while EPA and DPA can be catabolized for energy or converted into pro-resolving lipid mediators [48]. Their reduction in PAL-treated group may suggest metabolic reprogramming in injured lungs, where these substrates are mobilized to meet energy demands and restore homeostasis. Although our present study identified key metabolic pathways and potential sentinel metabolites associated with PAL treatment in ALI, targeted UHPLC-MS/MS quantification and functional validation of these metabolites need be performed in future studies. Integrative transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses indicated that PAL treatment engaged the cytosolic DNA-sensing and NOD-like receptor signaling pathway. Recent study has shown that the cytosolic DNA-STING-NLRP3 axis plays a crucial role in murine ALI induced by LPS [49]. The mechanisms by which PAL influences the NLRP3 inflammasome through the cytosolic DNA-STING pathway warrant further investigation.

Overall, our study demonstrated that PAL inhibits the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in LPS-induced ALI, thereby suppressing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and metabolic reprogramming, ultimately alleviating lung damage. However, several limitations should be noted. First, NLRP3- or caspase-1-knockout mice should be employed in our future studies to further validate the mechanistic role of PAL and to identify more definitive therapeutic targets for ALI/ARDS. Second, the LPS-induced ALI model mainly represents endotoxin-driven injury and reflects Gram-negative bacteria-mediated inflammation. It remains unclear —and warrants further investigation—whether the protective effect of PAL observed in current model can be generalized to other forms of lung injury, such as those induced by Gram-positive bacteria, viral infections, or sterile injuries like ventilator-induced or ischemia/reperfusion injury. Third, the potential side effects and toxicity associated with prolonged PAL usage requires comprehensive evaluation.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that PAL effectively inhibits the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and its downstream pyroptotic cascade, while concurrently modulating multiple metabolic pathways to restore metabolic homeostasis and attenuate inflammation in ALI. These results underscore the therapeutic potential of PAL in ALI and provide a strong foundation for further mechanistic investigations and the development of PAL-based therapeutic strategies.
