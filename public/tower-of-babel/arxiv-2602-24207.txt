The Stability of Online Algorithms in Performative Prediction
Gabriele Farina, Juan Carlos Perdomo

Abstract
The use of algorithmic predictions in decision-making leads to a feedback loop where the models we deploy actively influence the data distributions we see, and later use to retrain on. This dynamic was formalized by 39 in their work on performative prediction. Our main result is an unconditional reduction showing that any no-regret algorithm deployed in performative settings converges to a (mixed) performatively stable equilibrium: a solution in which models actively shape data distributions in ways that their own predictions look optimal in hindsight. Prior to our work, all positive results in this area made strong restrictions on how models influenced distributions. By using a martingale argument and allowing randomization, we avoid any such assumption and sidestep recent hardness results for finding stable models. Lastly, on a more conceptual note, our connection sheds light on why common algorithms, like gradient descent, are naturally stabilizing and prevent runaway feedback loops. We hope our work enables future technical transfer of ideas between online optimization and performativity.



1
Introduction

Social prediction is an inherently dynamic affair. The predictions we make inform decisions that actively shape the data we see. These influenced outcomes are then fed back into the datasets used to train models, leading to a continual feedback loop between prediction systems and their encompassing social contexts.

Once recognized, we start to see this impact, or
performativity
, of social prediction everywhere. Predicting individuals’ health outcomes influences their behavior and hence their realized outcomes. A bank’s choice of credit risk model influences the features that people strategically report to the system. Link prediction algorithms in professional networks determine who we know, and hence what links will end up forming in the future. The reader can certainly come up with more examples.

39
introduced a formal, learning-theoretic framework called
performative prediction
to reason about learning algorithms in these dynamic environments. In their work, they identify a natural solution concept termed
performative stability
. A model is performatively stable if it actively shapes data in a way that its own predictions look optimal in hindsight.

The motivation behind stability is that it prevents a runaway feedback loop where learners repeatedly update their models because they are not risk-minimizing on the observed data.
In more detail, performativity often surfaces as a type of unexpected distribution shift. Decision-makers often respond to this shift by retraining their models on the most recent examples. Performatively stable models have the desirable property that if you deploy them and then retrain on their induced distributions, you get the same model back. There is no incentive to deviate via retraining. See
Figure

1
for a visual illustration.

It seems intuitive that to guarantee performative stability, one needs tight control over the stability of the feedback loop. In that vein, all positive results known today rest on the assumption that the response of the world is a contraction (i.e., the feedback loop has Lipschitz constant
<
1). In a precise sense, this is not avoidable, as shown by recent impossibility results
(
4
)
. However, in a fortunate twist, we show that by moving the focus from a single model to a mixture, stability can always be ensured without making any assumptions on the distributional response. In particular, we establish an unconditional reduction from regret minimization to performative stability. In particular, a corollary of our reduction implies that gradient descent based retraining always leads to performatively stable models, and we achieve the first stability guarantees for weakly convex, or non-smooth, losses (or retraining in finite-samples).

As a result, our work significantly expands the space of problems for which one can efficiently achieve performative stability and highlights how classical supervised learning algorithms are naturally equilibrating when deployed in these more dynamic domains. Looking forward, we hope our results will inspire future work at the intersection of performativity and online learning.

1.1
Contributions

To discuss our results, we give a brief overview of the framework of performativity.

Performative prediction extends supervised learning to account for the fact that deploying predictive models actively shapes the distribution of future data. More formally, if the learner makes predictions using a model
f_{\theta}
where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes a vector of parameters, then they see examples
z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)
drawn i.i.d. from the distribution
\mathcal{D}(\theta)
induced by the model.
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
is the distribution map, a function from model parameters
\theta
to distributions over points in a set
\mathcal{Z}
. Typically, we let
z=(x,y)
consist of features
x\in\mathcal{X}
and outcomes
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
In this case, the distribution map
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
captures how deploying models can influence both the marginal distribution over features as well as the conditional distribution over outcomes. Performative prediction therefore strictly subsumes the setting known as strategic classification
(
19
)
.

Given the dynamic effects of prediction, the following stability notion is one of the key desiderata within performativity:

Definition 1
.

Given a loss function
\ell(z;\theta)
, a model
\theta_{\mathrm{PS}}
is
\varepsilon
-performatively stable if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{\mathrm{PS}})}\ell(z;\theta_{\mathrm{PS}})\leq\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{\mathrm{PS}})}\ell(z;\theta)+\varepsilon.

More generally, we say that a mixture
\mu_{\mathrm{PS}}
over models
\theta
is
\varepsilon
-performatively stable if

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu_{\mathrm{PS}}}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)}\ell(z;\theta)\leq\min_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu_{\mathrm{PS}}}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)}\ell(z;\theta^{\prime})+\varepsilon.

Performatively stable models are the fixed points of the following retraining procedure,

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\mathrm{argmin}_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{t})}\ell(z;\theta).

(1)

This is a natural dynamic that arises in recommender systems, strategic classification, and even traffic prediction where new models are trained on data that was influenced by previous models.

Our main result shows that any online algorithm that guarantees sublinear external regret against the stochastic losses
\ell(z_{t};\theta_{t})

\mathcal{D}(\cdot)

It is by now a celebrated fact that online algorithms have good generalization properties in supervised learning

. Here, we show that when data is no longer drawn i.i.d. from a fixed distribution, but is instead

As we will discuss, this connection significantly expands the kinds of problems for which one can guarantee performative stability. As easy corollaries of this main result, we can show that by taking a mixture over their iterates, common no-regret algorithms such as gradient descent and retraining (follow the leader) converge to performative stability under substantially weaker assumptions on the loss function
\ell
and the distribution map
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
than those previously used in the performative prediction literature. Readers already familiar with prior work can jump to
Table

1

Crucially, earlier analyses required that predictions only had a very weak and smoothly varying influence on the induced distribution. Formally they assumed that
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
is Lipschitz (see
Definition

3
). More than just a technical condition, this assumption ruled out stability in important domains like medicine or education where people take actions that influence outcomes by thresholding model predictions (see e.g.
41

Moreover, recent work by
4

\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
and strongly convex and smooth

By working with mixtures over models, we sidestep these complexity barriers, greatly expand the settings in which stability can be achieved efficiently, and provide a simple theoretical justification for why widely used learning practices prevent runaway feedback loops in social prediction.

The Power of No-Regret.

Our reduction joins a long line of results that reveal deep and sometimes surprising connections between regret minimization and seemingly disparate areas of research.
In game theory, regret-minimizing dynamics are known to be intimately tied to equilibrium. The web of connections in that space is far too pervasive to be exhaustive. It spans a multitude of solution concepts, including coarse correlated and correlated equilibria
(
21
;
9
;
38
)
, Nash equilibria in zero-sum games, and more (including
\Phi
-equilibria, see
18
), across a variety of models including normal-form games, extensive-form games (e.g.,
12
;
13
), harmonic games
(
28
)
, general concave games
(
10
)
, among others.
In a similar vein, calibrated forecasting can also be famously reduced to no-regret learning
(
16
;
17
)
. Blackwell’s approachability theorem has been shown to be algorithmically equivalent to online linear optimization, yielding a tight reduction between approachability and no-regret learning
(
1
)
.

In nonlinear optimization, recent reductions from nonsmooth, nonconvex optimization (in the sense of Goldstein stationarity) to online learning have led to optimal stochastic algorithms
(
8
)
.
In statistics and probability theory, sequential testing and anytime-valid inference can be reduced to no-regret algorithms, giving rise to e-processes and confidence sequences
(
36
;
44
)
. Deterministic regret inequalities have been shown to be equivalent to sharp martingale tail bounds and associated concentration inequalities
(
43
)
.
Finally, multiplicative-weights and related online learning algorithms yield conceptually simple proofs of von Neumann’s minimax theorem
(
37
;
14
;
15

(

, and certain results in derandomization
(

Preliminaries and Technical Background

Before presenting our main results, we provide some further context on definitions and prior results in performative prediction.
In addition to performative stability, there is a second main solution concept in performative prediction known as
performative optimality
.

Definition 2
.

A model
\theta_{\mathrm{PO}}
is performatively optimal if,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{\mathrm{PO}})}\ell(z;\theta_{\mathrm{PO}})\leq\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)}\ell(z;\theta).

Here,
\mathrm{PR}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)}\ell(z;\theta)
is known as the
performative risk
of the model specified by
\theta
. Optimal models minimize the performative risk. If we denote by
\mathrm{DPR}(\theta,\mathcal{D}(\theta^{\prime}))=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta^{\prime})}\ell(z;\theta)
the
decoupled
performative risk, we can relate optimality and stability as follows,

\displaystyle\textit{(Stability)}\;\;\mathrm{PR}(\theta_{\mathrm{PS}})\leq\min_{\theta}\mathrm{DPR}(\theta,\mathcal{D}(\theta_{\mathrm{PS}}))\quad\quad\textit{(Optimality)}\;\;\mathrm{PR}(\theta_{\mathrm{PO}})\leq\min_{\theta}\mathrm{DPR}(\theta,\mathcal{D}(\theta))

Both solution concepts are in general distinct.
39
showed how stable models need not be performatively optimal and optimal models need not be stable.

Given its connection to the retraining feedback loops we discussed in the introduction, the question of when and why do various algorithms converge to stability has been one of the main focus areas of the performative prediction literature. The bulk of prior work analyzed convergence to performative stability under following Lipschitz condition on the distribution map:

Definition 3
.

A distribution map
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
is
\varepsilon
-sensitive (or Lipschitz) if for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}\big(\mathcal{D}(\theta),\mathcal{D}(\theta^{\prime})\big)\leq\varepsilon\cdot\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}.

Here,
\mathcal{W}_{1}
is the earthmover’s or Wasserstein-1 distance between the distributions
\mathcal{D}(\theta)
and
\mathcal{D}(\theta^{\prime})
.

Intuitively, the sensitivity condition formalizes the notion that small changes in predictions lead to small changes in the induced distribution.

A canonical example where this assumption holds is in models of strategic classification
(
19
)
, where people manipulate their features to game a classifier. In particular, some works assume that if a linear classifier with parameters
\theta
is deployed, individuals modify their features from
x
to
x-\varepsilon\theta
in an effort to move to the positive side of the decision boundary.
Using this sensitivity condition (
Definition

3
), prior work in this area established the following result.

Theorem 2

(
39
;
32
)
.

Assume that
\ell(z;\theta)
is
\beta
-smooth, and
\gamma
-strongly convex in
\theta
for every
z
. Furthermore, assume that the distribution map
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
is
\varepsilon
-sensitive with
\rho=\varepsilon\beta/\gamma<1
. Then, retraining,

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\mathrm{argmin}_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{t})}\ell(z;\theta)

converges to a unique stable point
\theta_{\mathrm{PS}}
at a linear rate,
\|\theta_{T}-\theta_{\mathrm{PS}}\|\leq\mathcal{O}(\exp(-T))
. Additionally, if
\nabla_{\theta}\ell(z;\theta)
is
\beta
-Lipschitz as a function of
z
for every
\theta
, then stochastic gradient descent,

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{\theta}(z_{t},\theta_{t}),\quad z_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{t}),

converges to a unique stable point at a polynomial rate,
\mathbb{E}\|\theta_{t}-\theta_{\mathrm{PS}}\|^{2}\leq\mathcal{O}(1/T)
.

In other words, if the loss function is well-conditioned and the distribution map is Lipschitz with a small Lipschitz constant then repeatedly retraining overcomes performativity and strategic behavior. While promising, the regime in which these results hold is limited. Prior work gives examples showing how retraining and gradient descent diverge if losses are only convex, but not strongly convex, non-smooth, or if
\rho=\varepsilon\beta/\gamma>1
. More importantly, they illustrate how things can quickly destabilize if the distribution map
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
is not continuous.

Discontinuous
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
commonly arise in practice whenever decision-makers choose actions by thresholding a prediction. To give a concrete example, public schools often assign interventions to students whose predicted probability of graduation,
f_{\theta}(x)
, falls below a cutoff.
41

f_{\theta}(x)
is below this cutoff:

\Pr[y=1\mid f_{\theta}(x)>\tau]\ll\Pr[y=1\mid f_{\theta}(x)\leq\tau].

These discrete jumps are not just common in practice, they also pose a technical barrier for convergence analyses. To illustrate this, we recall the following example from

3
Main Results

The convergence to stability in the previous example is not an accident, but rather a reflection of a more general result that we will now present. Before proving our main theorem, we recall the definition of a no-regret learning algorithm.

Definition 4
.

Consider the online learning protocol, where at every time step
t
, the Learner selects a vector
\theta_{t}\in\Theta
, and then having seen
\theta_{t}
, Nature selects a loss
\ell_{t}:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. An algorithm guarantees no regret if at every round
t
it selects
\theta_{t}\in\Theta
such that no matter the choice of function
\ell_{t}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(\theta_{t})-\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(\theta)\leq\mathrm{Regret}(T),

where
\mathrm{Regret}(T)
is a function that is
o(T)
.

As mentioned in Section
1.2
, online algorithms are popular not just because they have guarantees in adversarial settings, they also serve as a powerful template for algorithm design in more structured domains. In that vein, our main result shows that if the
\ell_{t}
are equal to the stochastic losses
\ell(z_{t},\cdot)
then the uniform mixture over the iterates of any no-regret algorithm is performatively stable.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{T}
be the sequence of iterates produced over
T
rounds by an online algorithm that achieves sublinear regret on the sequence of losses
\ell_{t}(\theta)=\ell(z_{t},\theta)
where
z_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{t})
and
\ell
is bounded. Let
\mu
be the uniform distribution over
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{T}
. Then, in expectation over the draws from
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
, the mixture
\mu
is
\mathrm{Regret}(T)/T
-performatively stable,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)}\ell(z;\theta)\leq\min_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)}\ell(z;\theta^{\prime})+\frac{\mathrm{Regret}(T)}{T}.

Proof

Let
\theta_{\star}
be any vector in
\Theta
. The goal is to upper bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu,z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)}\left[\ell(z;\theta)-\ell(z;\theta_{\star})\right].

We start out by expanding the first expectation over
\mu
. Since
\mu
is chosen to be the uniform distribution over
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{T}
, this expression then becomes:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{t})}\left[\ell(z;\theta_{t})-\ell(z;\theta_{\star})\right].

Next, we add and subtract
\ell(z_{t};\theta_{t})-\ell(z_{t};\theta_{\star})
where
z_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{t})
for every
t
. Rearranging, the expression becomes

\displaystyle\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{t})}\left[\ell(z;\theta_{t})-\ell(z;\theta_{\star})-(\ell(z_{t};\theta_{t})-\ell(z_{t};\theta_{\star}))\right]}_{\text{small by a martingale argument}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[\ell(z_{t};\theta_{t})-\ell(z_{t};\theta_{\star})\right]}_{\text{small by regret}}.

In particular, if we define
V_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{t})}\left[\ell(z;\theta_{t})-\ell(z;\theta_{\star})\right]-[\ell(z_{t};\theta_{t})-\ell(z_{t};\theta_{\star})]
. Then,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{t})}\left[\ell(z;\theta_{t})-\ell(z;\theta_{\star})-(\ell(z_{t};\theta_{t})-\ell(z_{t};\theta_{\star}))\right]=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}V_{t}.

Here,
V_{1},\dots,V_{T}
is a martingale difference sequence. In more detail, conditioned on the events up until time
t-1
,
z_{t}
is an independent draw from
\mathcal{D}(\theta_{t})
. Hence,
\mathbb{E}[V_{t}|V_{1},\dots,V_{t-1}]=0
. Furthermore,
\mathbb{E}[|V_{t}|]<\infty
since losses are bounded. Therefore,
\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=1}^{T}V_{t}]=0
where this expectation is taken over the randomness in
z_{1},\dots z_{t}
. Moving onto the second term,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(z_{t};\theta_{t})-\ell(z_{t};\theta_{\star})\leq\mathrm{Regret}(T),

by guarantee of the online algorithm. Stitching these two together, we get the main result since the argument holds for any
\theta_{\star}
.

On a technical level, the proof is an online-to-batch conversion that relies on a martingale argument. The main difference relative to standard online-to-batch analyses is that at every time step, we draw samples
z_{t}
from
\mathcal{D}(\theta_{t})
instead of a fixed distribution as in supervised learning. This makes critical use of the fact that online algorithms can guarantee low regret against adaptive adversaries that choose
\ell_{t}
as a function of
\theta_{t}
. This allows us to set
\ell_{t}(\theta)=\ell(z_{t},\theta)
where
z_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{t})
is drawn from the distribution induced by the iterate at time
t
.

The statement is non-asymptotic (it holds for all
T\geq 1
), and in line with prior work
(
32
;
11
)
, establishes stability in expectation over the sampling procedure. One can in principle extend this result to hold with high probability over the draws from
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
using standard machinery (e.g. Freedman’s inequality and a covering argument, see
26
;
7
).

Comparison to Prior Work.

Prior work in the performative prediction literature has also explored connections to online learning.
11
show that under the assumptions of
Theorem

3
, namely that losses are
\beta
-smooth,
\gamma
-strongly-convex, and
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
is
\varepsilon
-sensitive with
\varepsilon<\gamma/\beta
, then a single model equal to the average of the iterates
\bar{\theta}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\theta_{t}
produced by an online algorithm is performatively stable. We on the other hand show that the mixture over iterates is performatively stable, but do not require smoothness, strong convexity, or that
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
is continuous (much less Lipschitz).

A more recent result by
40
shows that in the
outcome
performative setting where features
x
are sampled from a fixed distribution
\mathcal{D}_{x}
, and a binary outcome y is sampled from a (possibly discontinuous) performative distribution
\mathcal{D}_{y}(x,f_{\theta}(x))
that depends on the learner’s forecast,
f_{\theta}(x)
, any algorithm that guarantees online multicalibration with respect to a particular set of functions can be converted to a (randomized) performatively stable model if
\ell
is equal to the squared loss. By anchoring performativity on the model
\theta
instead of the specific prediction
f_{\theta}(x)
, we achieve stability for general distribution maps
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
where
\theta
influences the
joint
distribution over
z=(x,y)
and our result holds for any loss where one can guarantee low regret.

4
Implications of Main Result

Theorem

3
is a general reduction showing we can extract a performatively stable mixture from the iterates produced by any no-regret learning algorithm. Here, we instantiate this reduction for specific choices of loss functions and algorithms, illustrating how
Theorem

3
can be used to show that popular procedures are indeed stable in previously unknown settings. While prior work showed convergence to stability of different algorithms using tailored, bespoke arguments, our work provides a simple, unifying analysis showing convergence to stability by leveraging known regret bounds.

Throughout this section, we will assume that losses have gradients that are uniformly bounded by
G
and that the compact, convex domain
\Theta
has diameter bounded by
D
:

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathcal{Z},\theta\in\Theta}\|\nabla_{\theta}\ell(z;\theta)\|_{2}\leq G,\quad\sup_{\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq D.

Finite-Sample Guarantees for Retraining.

Retraining, as defined in
Equation

1
, is the most commonly studied approach to finding performatively stable solution. All analyses of this procedure
(a)
worked in the standard model with
\rho<1
, and
(b)
considered only an idealized procedure where the learner finds the risk-minimizing solution at the population level at every time step. The finite sample results in
39
are exponential in the problem dimension.

However, retraining is exactly equal to the classical follow-the-leader algorithm. Using standard regret bounds, along with
Theorem

3
, we can establish the first proof that smoothed retraining (where the learners retrains and then randomizes over the iterates) efficiently converges to stability in finite samples without making any assumptions on
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
. We recall the following lemma:

Lemma 1

(
46
;
22
;
2
)
.

Assume that
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{T}
is a sequence of functions that are all
\gamma
-strongly convex. If
\theta_{t}
are chosen according to the follow-the-leader algorithm,

\displaystyle\theta_{t}\in\mathrm{argmin}_{\theta}\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\ell_{j}(\theta).

(2)

Then, the iterates satisfy:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(\theta_{t})-\min_{\theta}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(\theta)\leq\frac{G^{2}}{2\gamma}(\log(T)+1).

Using this result, we can establish the following convergence for repeated retraining:

Corollary 1
.

Let
\ell
be a
\gamma
-strongly convex loss with gradients bounded by
G
over the domain
\Theta
. Assume that the learner runs the following repeated retraining procedure,

\displaystyle\theta_{t}\in\mathrm{argmin}_{\theta}\sum_{j=1}^{t-1}\ell(z_{j};\theta)\text{ where }z_{j}\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{j}).

Then, the uniform mixture over
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{T}
is performatively stable:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)}\ell(z;\theta)\leq\min_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)}\ell(z;\theta^{\prime})+\frac{G^{2}}{2\gamma}\cdot\frac{(\log(T)+1)}{T}.

This result makes two adjustments relative to the standard definition of retraining as per
Equation

2
. First,
Equation

1
, defines
\theta_{t}
to be the population risk minimizer on
\mathcal{D}(\theta_{t-1})
. Here, at every timestep the learner finds the risk minimizing solution over the entire history of samples
z_{t}
where each
z_{t}
is drawn from a
different
distribution. Furthermore, at the end, the learner randomizes across models instead of deploying the final iterate
\theta_{T}
. As discussed in
Section

2
, this modification is necessary to both to avoid hardness (or non-existence) of deterministic performatively stable models and to guarantee stability while making no assumptions on
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
.

Improved Guarantees for Gradient Descent.

Apart from retraining, prior work also studied whether the stochastic gradient method also converges to stability. As outlined previously,
32
and
11
proved that gradient descent is indeed stable if loss is again smooth, strongly convex, and
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
is Lipschitz with
\rho<1
. Using our reduction, we can remove any assumptions on
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
and establish the first performativity stability guarantees of any algorithm for losses that are non-smooth or just weakly convex.

To do this, we recall the standard regret bounds for online gradient descent:

Lemma 2

(
50
,
22
)
.

Assume that
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{t}
is a sequence of convex functions. If
\theta_{t}
are chosen using online gradient descent

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\mathrm{Proj}_{\Theta}\left[\theta_{t}-\eta_{t}\nabla\ell_{t}(\theta_{t})\right],\text{ where }\eta_{t}=\frac{D}{G\sqrt{t}},

then the iterates satisfy the following regret bound:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(\theta_{t})-\min_{\theta}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(\theta)\leq\frac{3}{2}GD\sqrt{T}.

If losses
\ell_{t}
are additionally
\gamma
-strongly convex and we set
\eta_{t}=1/(G\gamma t)
then,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(\theta_{t})-\min_{\theta}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(\theta)\leq\frac{G}{\gamma}(1+\log T).

Tying this lemma together with
Theorem

3
, we get the following corollary:

Corollary 2
.

Assume that the learner runs the following (greedy) gradient descent algorithm,

\displaystyle\theta_{t+1}=\mathrm{Proj}_{\Theta}\big[\theta_{t}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{t}(z_{t}\;\theta_{t})\big],\text{ where }\eta_{t}=\frac{D}{G\sqrt{t}},\;z_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{t}),

and that the loss
\ell
has gradients bounded by
G
over the domain
\Theta
. If the losses are convex,
\eta_{t}=D/(G\sqrt{t})
, then the uniform distribution over
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{T}
satisfies,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)}\ell(z;\theta)\leq\min_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)}\ell(z;\theta^{\prime})+\frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{GD}{\sqrt{T}}.

If the losses are
\gamma
-strongly convex, and
\eta_{t}=1/(G\gamma t)
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)}\ell(z;\theta)\leq\min_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)}\ell(z;\theta^{\prime})+\frac{G}{\gamma}\cdot\frac{1+\log(T)}{T}.

Ignoring quantities that depend on the diameter of the space
\Theta
, the bounds we get for gradient descent in the strongly-convex case match those from
32
and
11
up to a
\log(T)
factor. However, we achieve this guarantee for general distribution maps
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
. This result holds for what
32
refer to as
greedy
gradient descent where the learner performs a model update and redeploys at every time step.

It’s an interesting direction for future work to understand if by leveraging other online algorithms (e.g. the primal dual analysis in
46
) one could also prove that
lazy
versions of gradient descent where the learner performs many updates before redeployment also lead to stability, while reducing the total number of deployed models.

Guaranteeing Performative Stability in New Regimes.

As a final corollary, we highlight how the reduction to online learning can be used to guarantee stability in new domains (or in old domains at faster rates). In particular, to get a
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/T)
rate for stability, prior work required that the losses
\ell
were
pointwise
strongly convex. That is for every
z
,
\ell(z;\theta)
is a strongly convex function in
\theta
. If we don’t add a regularizer, this fails for the two most common losses in machine learning: log loss
\log(1+\exp(-y\cdot\theta^{\top}x)
and squared loss
(y-\theta^{\top}x)^{2}
.

These losses are however exp-concave and one can achieve
\log(T)
regret for these problems using second-order methods. Using this connection, we can establish the first positive results for performative stability in these common settings (and with fast rates).

Corollary 3
.

Let
\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{T}
be a sequence of losses that are
\alpha
-exp-concave for
\alpha>0
. That is, for every
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\nabla^{2}\ell_{t}(\theta)-\alpha\nabla\ell_{t}(\theta)\nabla\ell_{t}(\theta)^{\top}
is a positive semi-definite matrix. There exists an efficient online algorithm, the online Newton step algorithm, that guarantees that for
T\geq 4
:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(\theta_{t})-\min_{\theta}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_{t}(\theta)\leq 2\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}+GD\right)\cdot d\cdot\log(T).

Consequently, if the learner produces iterates
\theta_{t}
by running online Newton step on the losses
\ell(z_{t},\theta)
for
z_{t}\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta_{t})
. The uniform distribution over iterates
\theta_{1},\dots\theta_{T}
is performatively stable:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)}\ell(z;\theta)\leq\min_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)}\ell(z;\theta^{\prime})+2\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}+GD\right)\cdot\frac{d\cdot\log T}{T}.

We point the reader to the excellent monograph
23
for details on the online Newton step algorithm. The corollary follows again by tying together known regret bounds
(
22
)
with
Theorem

3
.

Lastly, we note that we do not intend this section to be an exhaustive list of corollaries. Online optimization is by now a very rich and mature field with sophisticated algorithms that in many ways improve on the ones we present here (in particular on their dimension, and
G,D
dependence). We chose these due to their simplicity and to highlight some of their consequences for performativity.

5
Discussion and Future Work

In this work, we establish a reduction between no-regret learning in online optimization and performative stability. As a consequence of this connection, we expand the regimes of problem where one can achieve this natural solution concept, and provide an explanation behind why common learning procedures do not produce runaway feedback loops in dynamic, social environments.
We believe that there are a number of exciting directions to pursue in future work.

First, as we discussed in
Section

2
, performatively stable models are fixed point of retraining, but they do not necessarily minimize the performative risk. Prior work by
33
and
40
show that, there exists worst case instances where stable solutions in fact
maximize
the performative risk. This begs a natural question: what structural conditions can we impose on
\mathcal{D}(\cdot)
to ensure that stable points are also optimal? Moreover, are there notions of regret that would imply performative
optimality
instead of performative
stability
?

Furthermore, our results hold for the standard performativity setting where there is a single learner that deploys a model
\theta
and then observes samples
z\sim\mathcal{D}(\theta)
. In many settings, like traffic prediction, the data we see is not a function of a single model but is rather jointly shaped by many models. This is known as the multiplayer performative prediction problem
(
35
)
. It would be interesting to know: if each player runs a no-regret algorithm and then randomizes across their models, is the resulting joint set of models stable in the multiplayer setting?

A similar question is also interesting for the stateful performative prediction problem
(
5
)
, where the data we see is not just a function of the mostly recently deployed model, but of the entire history of deployed models. Are online algorithms also performatively stable in this setting? Can one establish a similar connection between stability and no (dynamic) regret algorithms? We believe our results only begin to explore the space of questions at the intersection of online learning and performativity.
