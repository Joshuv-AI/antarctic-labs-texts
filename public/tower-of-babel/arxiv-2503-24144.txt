𝖯 {\mathsf{P}} -completeness of Graph Local Complementation
Pablo Concha-Vega

Abstract
Local complementation of a graph G G on vertex v v is an operation that results in a new graph G ∗ v G*v , where the neighborhood of v v is complemented. This operation has been widely studied in graph theory and quantum computing. This article introduces the Local Complementation Problem, a decision problem that captures the complexity of applying a sequence of local complementations. Given a graph G G , a sequence of vertices s s , and a pair of vertices u , v u,v , the problem asks whether the edge ( u , v ) (u,v) is present in the graph obtained after applying local complementations according to s s . The main contribution of this work is proving that this problem is 𝖯 {\mathsf{P}} -complete, implying that computing a sequence of local complementation is unlikely to be efficiently parallelizable. The proof is based on a reduction from the Circuit Value Problem, a well-known 𝖯 {\mathsf{P}} -complete problem, by simulating circuits through local complementations. Aditionally, the complexity of this problem is analyzed under different restrictions. In particular, it is shown that for complete and star graphs, the problem belongs to 𝖫𝖮𝖦𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 {\mathsf{LOGSPACE}} . Finally, it is conjectured that the problem remains 𝖯 {\mathsf{P}} -complete for the class of circle graphs.

Introduction

Graph transformations are fundamental in graph theory, providing frameworks
for studying structural properties, characterizing graph classes, and modeling
dynamic processes
[
20
,
25
,
5
,
22
]
. Operations
such as vertex deletion, edge contraction, and graph complement play a crucial
role in identifying graph invariants and designing classification theorems

[
7
,
25
,
21
]
. These transformations also appear in
applications ranging from combinatorial optimization to network dynamics and
quantum information
[
24
,
6
,
15
]
.

Beyond pure graph theory, graph transformations have significant implications
in computational complexity theory and algorithm design. Many computational
problems involve modifying graphs through local or global operations, with
applications in problems such as graph isomorphism, vertex cover, and
coloring
[
9
,
2
,
23
,
17
]
.
Transformations often serve as a tool for proving hardness results, designing
efficient algorithms, or establishing connections between different computational
problems (see for example
[
12
]
).

One such transformation is local complementation, which modifies a graph
G

by complementing the edges in the neighborhood of a selected vertex while leaving
the rest of the structure unchanged. Formally, the local complementation of an
undirected graph
G
at a vertex
v
produces a new graph
G*v
, where the
induced graph on the neighborhood of
v
is replaced by its complement, while
the adjacency relations with the rest of the graph remain unchanged (see
Figure
1
). This operation has been widely studied in various
contexts, including graph theory, coding theory, and quantum
computing
[
11
,
5
,
3
,
4
,
10
,
18
,
16
,
8
]
.

G

G*0

Figure 1:
Example of local complementation.

This work investigates the
Local Complementation Problem
(
\mathcal{LCP}
),
a decision problem that captures the complexity of computing the effect of
successive local complementations. Given an undirected graph
G
, a sequence of
vertices
s
, and two vertices
u,v
, the problem asks whether the arc
(u,v)
is
present in
G*s
, where
G*s
denotes the graph obtained after applying local
complementation sequentally acording to
s
.

The main result establishes that
\mathcal{LCP}
is
{\mathsf{P}}
-complete, placing it among the
hardest problems that can be solved efficiently on a deterministic sequential
machine. This classification suggests that efficiently parallelizing the computation
of local complementations is unlikely unless
{\mathsf{P}}={\mathsf{NC}}
.

1.1
Previous work

Two graphs are
locally equivalent
if one can be obtained from the other
through a sequence of local complementations. A graph
H
is a
vertex-minor

of a graph
G
if it is an induced subgraph of a graph locally equivalent to
G
.
Naturally, the operation of local complementation has been extensively studied
within the framework of vertex-minors theory. A comprehensive compendium of results
on this topic can be found in
[
19
]
.

A fundamental result on vertex-minors theory is the characterization of circle
graphs via forbidden vertex-minors
[
5
]
. A circle graph is defined by
a chord diagram where vertices correspond to chords and two vertices are adjacent
if and only if their corresponding chords intersect (see Figure
2

for an example). The theorem states that a graph is a circle graph if and only if
none of its vertex-minors is isomorphic to any of the graphs shown in Figure
3
.
Furthermore, circle graphs are recognizable in polynomial time
[
3
,
13
]
.

From a computational complexity perspective, it is known that determining whether
two graphs are locally equivalent can be done in
\mathcal{O}(n^{4})
time
[
4
]
.
Interestingly, deciding whether a graph is a vertex-minor of another graph or
isomorphic to a vertex-minor of another graph is an
{\mathsf{NP}}
-complete
problem
[
9
]
. Previously, the same authors demonstrated that counting the
number of graphs locally equivalent to a given graph is
\#{\mathsf{P}}
-complete, even
when restricted to circle graphs
[
8
]
.

Beyond classical graph theory, local complementation also plays a significant role
in quantum computing, particularly in the study of
graph states
. A graph
state is a type multi-qubit state that can be represented with a graph. In this
setting, local Clifford operations on graph states can be fully described using
local complementation
[
27
]
. As a result, local complementation has been
widely studied in quantum computing as it represents a powerful tool for quantum
computing theorists. Notable works on this topic include
[
1
,
16
,
26
]
.

2
Preliminaries

In this article, all graphs are simple.
Let
H
be a graph. The set of vertices of
H
is denoted
V(H)
, and the set
of edges by
E(H)\subseteq V(H)\times V(H)
(
\subseteq{V(H)\choose 2}

for undirected graphs).

let
G
be an undirected graph. The
local complementation
of
G
at a
vertex
v
is an operation resulting in a graph
G*v
obtained by complementing the
adjacencies of all the neighbors of vertex
v
. In other words, two vertices

u,u^{\prime}\in V(G)
are adjacent in
G*v
if and only if exactly one of the following holds:

•

(u,u^{\prime})\in E(G)

•

(u,v),(u^{\prime},v)\in E(G)

Applying local complementation to a graph
G
over a sequence of vertices

s=(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
is inductively defined as

G*s=G*(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})=G*v_{1}*(v_{2},\dots,v_{k})
and
G*\epsilon=G
.

2.1
Boolean Circuits

A
Boolean circuit

C
is a labeled directed acyclic graph in which
edges carry unidirectional logical signals and the vertices compute elementary logical
functions. The entire graph computes a Boolean function from the inputs to the outputs
in a natural way. Every vertex
v\in V(C)
has a type from
\{I,\band,\bor,\bnot\}
. A vertex
v
with type
I
has indegree
0
and is called an
input
.
The inputs of
C
are given by a tuple
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
of different vertices.
When computing a circuit, every input vertex has to be assigned a Boolean
value, these values are denoted by
(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\in\{\bfalse,\btrue\}^{n}
.
A vertex
v
with outdegree
0
is called an
output
.
The outputs of
C
are given by a tuple
(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})
of different vertices.
A vertex that is neither input nor output is called a
gate
.
The size of
C
, denoted by
\size(C)
, is the number of vertices in
C

The depth of
C
, denoted by
\depth(C)
, is the length of the longest path in
C

from an input to an output.

In this work, adjacency matrices are regarded as the standard encoding of graphs,
for both directed and undirected. The
adjacency matrix
of a graph
G

is given by
A(G)=(a_{ij})
where:

a_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }(v_{i},v_{j})\in E(G)\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Given a circuit
C
, its encoding consists of two structures: the adjacency matrix

A(C)
of the underlying graph, and A sequence
t(C)\in\{I,\band,\bor,\bnot\}^{\size(C)}
storing the type of each vertex. The encoding of
C
is
denoted by
\overline{C}=(A(C),t(C))
.

2.2
The Circuit Value Problem

The Circuit Value Problem (
\mathcal{CVP}
) is a widely studied decision problem
in
{\mathsf{P}}
-completeness theory and is considered the
{\mathsf{P}}
-complete
analogue of the Satisfiability Problem in
{\mathsf{NP}}
-completeness theory.
The problem is defined as follows: Given an encoding
\overline{C}
of
a Boolean circuit
C
, input values
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
and a
designated output
y
, determine the value of
y
on the given input.
Formally:

Circuit Value Problem (
\mathcal{CVP}
)

Input:
An encoding
\overline{C}
of a Boolean circuit
C
, input values

(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
, a designated output
y
.

Question:
Is output
y
of
C

\btrue
on input values
(b_{1},\dots b_{n})
?

Analogously to Satisfability,
\mathcal{CVP}
has many variants that are

{\mathsf{P}}
-complete. One of them is the Topologically Ordered
\mathcal{CVP}
.
A
topological ordering
of a circuit is a numbering of its
vertices such that
u
is less than
v
for every (directed) edge
(u,v)
.
Similarly, restraining the type of gates only to
\{\band,\bnot\}
also
leads to a
{\mathsf{P}}
-complete problem. Interestingly, combining both
restrictions, also leads to a
{\mathsf{P}}
-complete version of
\mathcal{CVP}
.
Moreover, an even more restricted version considering only circuits of
fanout at most
2
, is also
{\mathsf{P}}
-complete
[
14
]
.
Altought these restricted versions of the
\mathcal{CVP}
have their own names
in the literature, this latter restricted
\mathcal{CVP}
version will henceforth
be refered to simply as
\mathcal{CVP}
.

2.3
The Local Complementation Problem

The Local Complementation Problem (
\mathcal{LCP}
) is defined as follows: given
a graph
G
, a sequence of vertices
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
, and a pair
of vertices
u,v\in V(G)
, decide whether the edge
(u,v)
belongs
to the graph obtained after applying the local complementation operation
on
G
at each vertex in the given sequence in order. Formally:

Local Complementation Problem (
\mathcal{LCP}
)

Input:
An undirected graph
G
, a sequence of vertices
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
of
G
,
a pair of vertices
u,v\in V(G)
.

Question:
Does
(u,v)
belong to the graph
G*(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
?

Note that the
\mathcal{LCP}
is clearly in
{\mathsf{P}}
, as it can be solved by
a naive algorithm that iteratively applies the sequence of local
complementation to
G
.

3
{\mathsf{P}}
-completeness of
\mathcal{LCP}

This Section contains the main result of this article,
i.e.,
that

\mathcal{LCP}
is
{\mathsf{P}}
-complete. This is proven by reducing the
\mathcal{CVP}
to
\mathcal{LCP}
.
Roughly, it means that an arbitrary instance of the
\mathcal{CVP}
can be transformed
into an instance of
\mathcal{LCP}
, which implies that any algorithm solving
\mathcal{LCP}

can solve
\mathcal{CVP}
. The latter is achieved by constructing a series of

gadgets
to simulate circuits.
From now on, when reducing from the previously defined restricted version
of the
\mathcal{CVP}
, it is assumed that circuits have a topological ordering,
are composed only of
\band
and
\bnot
, and have fanout at most
2
.

3.1
Circuit Gadgets

Although the aim is to simulate circuits, the nature of the proof needs not
only
\band
and
\bnot
gadgets but also the inclusion of a
\copyg
gadget
and a
\dupl
gadget. Every gadget is composed by a graph, a sequence of
vertices, inputs and outputs. The main idea is that a gadget must compute
its task by ordlerly applying the local complementation operation over the
vertices of its sequence. It is important to note that, in order to prove the
main result, the sequence for each gadget must remain constant; that is,
it cannot be computed arbitrarily for a given input. Instead, the same
sequence must function correctly for any possible input to the gadget.

Inputs and outputs in the circuit are represented by the existance or absence
of predefined edges in the graph, therefore, for every input
x_{i}
of a
circuit
C
, a pair of vertices
v_{i},v^{\prime}_{i}
is created and they are connected
by an edge if and only if its Boolean value
b_{i}=\btrue
.

In this same fashion,
\copyg
of a
\btrue
variable
consists in “turning on” another predefined edge through a sequence of local
complementations. On the other hand, if the variable has a
\bfalse
value,
the same sequence of local complementations will not turn on the designated
edge. In particular, this gadget is a quadruple

(G_{\smallcopy},s_{\smallcopy},in,out)
such that:

•

V(G_{\smallcopy})=\{0,1,2,3\}
,

•

E(G_{\smallcopy})=\{(0,2),(1,3)\}
,

•

s_{\smallcopy}=(0,1,0)
,

•

in=(0,1)
represents the input of the gadget,

•

out=(2,3)
represents the output of the gadget.

3.2
Combining Gadgets

With the toolkit of gadgets now defined, the next step is to describe
how to combine them to simulate circuits.

A first step is describing how to combine two gadgets together.
In order to accomplish the latter, one must replace the output
(u,u^{\prime})

of a gadget with the input
(v,v^{\prime})
of a second gadget by collapsing

u
(resp.
u^{\prime}
) with
v
(resp.
v^{\prime}
) into one vertex
p
(resp

p^{\prime}
) while keeping all their edges,
i.e.
, for every edge
(s,u)

and
(t,v)
(resp.
(s^{\prime},u^{\prime})
and
(t^{\prime},v^{\prime})
) the edges
(s,p)
and

(t,p)
(resp.
(s^{\prime},p^{\prime})
and
(t^{\prime},p^{\prime})
) have to be created. Also,
the sequence
s_{1}
of the first gadget has to be concatenated with
the sequence
s_{2}
of the second gadget, resulting in
s_{1}s_{2}
.
An example is depicted in Figure
9
.
Note that none of the gadgets consider applying local complementation
on their output vertices, therefore concatenating their sequences
does not leave to any kind of malfunctioning.

A
\bnot
gadget and a
\dupl
gadget combined. Local complementing
on the sequence
(0,1,0,4,2,3,2,5,6,5,2,6,2)
computes the duplication
of the negation of the variable encoded in the adjacency relation
of vertices
0
and

.

3.3
Building Circuits

This subsection presents the construction of an arbitrary circuit by
combining the previously defined gadgets. Additionaly, the correctness
of this construction is established. An example of this construction
is ilustrated in Figure
10
.

Figure 10:

Left: example of a Boolean circuit with outdegree
2
. This circuit
is composed only of
\band
and
\bnot
gates. Right: The construction
of a graph that can simulate the circuit by applying local
complementation. The sequence correspond to the concatenation of
the sequences of the gadgets by layer.

Given a circuit
C
a graph-sequence structure (GSS)
(G,s,u,v)
is constructed
where that
G
is an undirected graph,
s
a sequence of vertices of
G

and
u,v\in V(G)
represent the output of
C
. The construction is now presented.

The process starts with an empty graph-sequence structure.
First, for every input
x_{i}
of a circuit
C
, a pair of
vertices
v_{i},v^{\prime}_{i}
is created. The edge
(v_{i},v^{\prime}_{i})
is added if and
only if its associated Boolean value
b_{i}=\btrue
.
This structure is referred to as the
input layer
.
After the input layer, duplication layers alternate
with logic layers, concluding with a final logic layer that contains
the circuit’s output. Note that the sequence asociated to the input
layer is the empty sequence, then nothing has to be added to
s
.

A
duplication layer
is composed of
\dupl
and
\copyg
gadgets.
Depending on whether a Boolean value is used once or twice, one of these
two gadgets is selected and attached. Duplication layers are initially
placed after the input layer and subsequently after each logic layers.
Despite their placement, the construction process remains the same in
both cases. For every pair of vertices
v,v^{\prime}
representing a Boolean
variable in the previous layer, whether originating from the input
layer or from the output of a logic layer, a
\dupl
or a
\copyg
gadget
is attached. The sequences of all the gadgets used in a layer are
concatenated one after the other in no particular order and then
concatenated at the end of
s
.

On the other hand, a
logic layer
is composed of
\band
and

\bnot
gadgets. Similarly as before, the outputs of the previous
layer are the inputs of a logic layer as specified in the circuit
C
.
Again, the sequences of all the gadgets used in a layer are concatenated
one after the other in no particular order and then added to
s
. The
last layer of this construction is a logic layer that contains the
pair of vertices
u,v
representing the output of
C
.

The correctness of this structure is now proven.

Lemma 1
.

Given a circuit
C
, input values
(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
and the GSS of

C
as
(G,s,u,v)
, then
(u,v)\in G*s
if and only if

C
outputs
\btrue
on inputs
x
.

Proof.

First of all, notice that the sequences associated to the gadgets
exclude the vertices defined as gadget outputs. This ensures that
during the simulation, applying local complementation on the
vertices specified by
s
within a particular layer never results
in edges connecting to vertices in the subsequent layers.

The proof proceeds by induction on the layers of the GSS representing
the circuit. The base case establishes that the input layer combined
with the first duplication and logic layers, correctly simulates the
first layer of gates of
C
.

For proving the base case, local complementation is applied
to the vertices given by
s
corresponding to the first three layers
of the GSS. Since the input layer works simply as the representation
of the inputs
x
, the process begins by local complementing the vertices
specified by
s
on the first duplication layer. This step preserves the
behavior of the
\dupl
and
\copyg
gadgets in their original form,
ensuring they work as intended.

Attention then shifts to the first logic layer. Although the gadgets
in this layer are no longer in their original form, due to the input
vertices having additional edges, applying local complementation on the
vertices specified by
s
results in irrelevant edges with the
preceding layers and in the desired edges from the original functioning
of the gadgets. The edges connecting previous layers are irrelevant
because those vertices will not undergo further local complementation.
This behavior is present across all the subsequent layers.

For the induction step, the assumption is made that the simulation works
as expected up to the the
k
-th logic layer. The goal is to prove that
the subsequent duplication and logic layers also behave as intended.

First, consider the
k+1
-th duplication layer. By the induction
hypothesis, the
k
-th logic layer correctly represent the outputs of
the corresponding gates of
C
. The gadget inputs at duplication layer

k+1
are these outputs. Similarly as before, applying
local complementation as specified by
s
transforms the
\copyg

and
\dupl
gadgets as intended but with extra edges connecting
the previous layers of the GSS. Therefore, the
k+1
-th duplication
layer functions as intended.

Finally, consider the
k+1
-th logic layer. Gadget inputs in this
layer are the outputs of the
k+1
-th duplication layer. Therefore,
applying local complementation on the vertices specified by
s

work as intended. Thus, the correctness of the construction extends
to logic gate
k+1
, completing the induction step.
∎

3.4
Computing the GSS of a Circuit in
{\mathsf{LOGSPACE}}

Lemma
1
proves that simulating a
circuit using its GSS correctly computes its output. If the GSS of a
circuit can be computed in
{\mathsf{LOGSPACE}}
, it implies that
\mathcal{CVP}
reduces to

\mathcal{LCP}
under
{\mathsf{LOGSPACE}}
reductions. Consequently, proving this fact
demonstrates the main result of this article, showing that
\mathcal{LCP}
is

{\mathsf{P}}
-complete.

The GSS of a circuit can be indeed computed in
{\mathsf{LOGSPACE}}
due to its
structured and modular nature (the graph and the sequence are
composed of fixed-size objects). The graph is constructed by
“gluing” predefined, fixed-size graphs corresponding to the
components of the circuits. This operation only requires keeping
track of the components and their connections, which can be achieved
in logarithmic space.

Similarly, the sequence associated with the GSS is formed by
concatenating the fixed sequences of the gadgets. Since this
concatenation is performed directly based on the circuit’s structure
and it does not depend on global information, it can also be
managed in
{\mathsf{LOGSPACE}}
. Finally, the two special vertices of the GSS
representing the output of the circuit are identified during the
construction with minimal usage of space. The following theorem is
then concluded.

Theorem 2
.

\mathcal{LCP}
is
{\mathsf{P}}
-complete.

3.5
Further Observations

An interesting variation of the
\mathcal{LCP}
extends the allowed operations
to both local complementation and vertex deletions, making it the
“vertex-minor counterpart” of the
\mathcal{LCP}
. In this case, given
a graph
G
, a sequence
s
of pairs
(v,operation)
, where each pair
consists of a vertex
v
and an operation (either local complementation
or vertex deletion), and a pair of special vertices
u,u^{\prime}
, the problem
asks whether
u
and
u^{\prime}
are connected in the graph obtained after
applying the sequence of operations from
s
. This variant remains

{\mathsf{P}}
-complete, which directly follows from the
{\mathsf{P}}
-completeness
of the
\mathcal{LCP}
.

Subgraph complementation
is a local transformation also
studied in the literature (see
[
12
]
for example).
A subgraph complement of a graph
G
is
a graph obtained by complementing one of its induced subgraphs.
Thus, local complementation can be seen as a special case of subgraph
complementation. A variation of the
\mathcal{LCP}
in which the sequence
of vertices is replaced by a sequence of vertex subsets to apply subgraph
complementation, would also be
{\mathsf{P}}
-complete.

Another noteworthy observation comes from restricting the
\mathcal{LCP}
to complete
graphs and star graphs. The set of graphs
locally equivalent to a complete graph is quite restricted: these are exactly
the star graphs of the same size (i.e., with the same number of vertices), and
vice versa (see Figure
11
). As a result, to decide the
problem, one only needs to track whether the graph is a complete graph or a
star graph, and if it is a star graph, which vertex serves as the center of the
star. Since this information can be stored in a single integer, the problem can
be categorized in
{\mathsf{LOGSPACE}}
. This shows that, for these specific graph classes,
the complexity of the problem is significantly reduced.

4
Conclusions

In this work the
\mathcal{LCP}
is introduced and proved to be
{\mathsf{P}}
-complete.
This problem captures the complexity of the operation of local complementation
by asking whether the graph resulting from applying this operation following a
given sequence contains or not a specific edge. This gives valuable insights,
as calculating a single edge through a series of
given
local complementations
is unlikely to be done with more efficiency unless
{\mathsf{P}}
equals a lower
complexity class such as
{\mathsf{NC}}
or
{\mathsf{LOGSPACE}}
.

In subsection
3.5
, it was observed that variations of the

\mathcal{LCP}
present interesting challenges, with their computational complexity varying
significantly. These variations might involve considering different operations,
such as pivoting or switching,
instead of local complementation, or combinations of operations as mentioned earlier
(e.g., local complementation combined with vertex deletions). The complexity of
such variants can be influenced by restricting the number of times an operation
can be applied to a vertex or by limiting the graph class under consideration,
as seen in the case of complete graphs and star graphs.

A particularly interesting class of graphs are circle graphs, which serve as a
canonical example of a class closed under local complementation. It is worth
noting that all the gadgets presented in this paper are circle graphs, which is
not difficult to verify (a task left to the reader). I conjecture that the problem
remains
{\mathsf{P}}
-complete when restricted to circle graphs, though this remains an
open question.

The results of this paper also have implications in the context of graph states in
quantum computing. As local complementation plays a crucial role in the manipulation
of graph states, the
{\mathsf{P}}
-completeness of the
\mathcal{LCP}
suggests that knowing whether
two qubits are entagled after a series of operations is a challenging task even for
graph states.
