Histopathological, immunohistochemical, and molecular characterization of Coenurus cerebralis infection in sheep with evaluation of cerebrospinal cytokine responses
Authors: Marwa M Attia, Hend Ibrahim Mohamed, Abuelhassan Elshazly Younis, Abdelrahman M Magdy, Sara S Barsoum, Sohila M El-Gameel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoenurosis is a significant zoonotic parasitic disease caused by Coenurus cerebralis, the larval stage of the cestode Taenia multiceps. The parasite invades the central nervous system (CNS) of intermediate hosts, particularly sheep and goats, leading to neurological disorders, severe economic losses, and death in advanced cases. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of C. cerebralis infection in sheep in Egyp between March 2024 and August 2024 and to characterize its histopathological, immunohistochemical, and molecular features, with a focus on cytokine gene expression in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). A total of 200 sheep presenting with neurological signs were examined, of which 10% were confirmed to be infected. Post-mortem examination, parasitological identification, histopathology, and immunohistochemistry targeting cluster of differentiation 3 (CD3) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) were performed. Molecular confirmation was achieved by PCR amplification of the mitochondrial cox1 gene. Additionally, quantitative PCR was used to assess the expression of cytokine genes (IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, and IL-12) in CSF samples compared with healthy controls. Cystic lesions containing multiple scolices were mainly detected in the cerebrum and cerebellum. Histopathological examination revealed granulomatous encephalitis and perivascular cuffing. Immunohistochemical analysis showed strong CD3⁺ T-cell infiltration and TNF-α expression surrounding cyst margins. Molecular sequencing demonstrated 100% identity with reference C. cerebralis strains (GenBank: LC271726, LC271732, LC271737). Notably, IL-6, IL-10, and IL-12 were significantly upregulated (p < 0.001) in infected sheep, indicating a mixed pro-inflammatory and regulatory immune response. These findings provide valuable insights into host–parasite interactions in ovine coenurosis and support the use of integrated molecular and immunological approaches for improved diagnosis and disease management.Keywords: Taenia multiceps, Coenurus cerebralis, immunohistochemistry, qPCR, IL-6, TNF-α

Introduction

Taenia multiceps is a pathogenic cestode that affect CNS of livestock worldwide. Adult worms usually inhabit the small intestine of domestic and wild carnivores. Coenurus cerebralis is metacestode larval stage which found mostly in the central nervous systems (CNS) of ruminants (Scala and Varcasia 2006; Varcasia et al. 2022). The intermediate hosts become infected via ingestion of food contaminated with Taenia multiceps eggs (Soulsby 1986). This disease occurs as acute or chronic forms, primarily affecting young sheep at day 10 to 33. At day 33 to 40 days the onchosphere has piriform vesicle (about 8 mm). At two months, it reaches cherry size (about 2 cm). The chronic phase (the most common form) begins at 3 months where the onchosphere has well formed protoscoleces, and ends at 8 months where C. cerebralis reaches 6 cm and contain about 23 cluster protoscoleces. It is associated with displacement and pressure atrophy in brain tissue resulting from oncospheres traveling through the CNS (Varcasia et al. 2022). Because of decreased output, treatment expenses, and mortality ratesT. multiceps might result in large financial losses for cattle producers (Wu et al. 2012). As nations impose stringent laws to stop the disease from spreading to people, it may also have an effect on export and trade markets. As T. multiceps is zoonoticit emphasizes the need for thorough control measures to safeguard the health of both humans and animals. It’s important to take into account the expense of caring for and discarding unhealthy animals. The 50–60 mm diameter coenurus cerebralis is a fluid-filled cyst encased in a connective tissue membrane (Varcasia et al. 2009). According to (Oryan et al. 2015) and (Varcasia et al. 2016), the cyst has a thin, transparent membrane with 400–500 protoscoleces invaginated from the inner membrane. These protoscoleces are clustered into varying numbers of clusters, ranging from one to 23; they appear as white spots linked to the internal membrane. Post-mortem examination of the brains from infected sheep revealed signs of hyperemia, swelling of the meninges, and the presence of characteristic cysts, as reported by (Mohammadi et al. 2021). Histological analysis further demonstrated widespread meningoencephalitis, marked by the infiltration of multinucleated inflammatory cells—primarily eosinophils and neutrophils—within both the meninges and brain tissue. These inflammatory changes were accompanied by perivascular cellular infiltration, tissue necrosis, and damage attributed to the migratory activity of the parasite (Oryan et al. 2015). The inflammatory response due to coenurosis, induces severe nervous manifestation. Multiple investigations have identified areas of liquefactive necrosis surrounding cerebral cysts, attributed to degenerative processes involving satellitosis, neuronophagia, and widespread microgliosis, which collectively lead to the formation of microglial nodules (Achenef et al. 1999).

Although certain clinical symptoms may suggest coenurosis, a definitive diagnosis typically relies on the detection of parasitic cysts within the cerebral tissue (Sharma and Chauhan 2006). According to (Scott 2012), approximately 80% to 90% of cysts localize to the cerebral hemispheres, with 5% to 10% occurring in the cerebellum. Additionally, molecular diagnostics using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) have shown that both natural and experimental infections in sheep and goats can be identified with 100% accuracy (Oryan et al. 2015; Abera et al. 2016; Amrabadi et al. 2015; Ajaj et al. 2021).

Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) play key roles in driving Th1-mediated cellular immunity. This type of immune response is crucial for suppressing parasitic infections and activating latent stages, as observed in Toxoplasma gondii and Neospora caninum, where it contributes to cyst development (Soliman et al. 2019). In cases of chronic cerebral coenurosis, histological analysis has shown features such as perivascular accumulation of mononuclear cells linked to cellular inflammation. Additional inflammatory findings include demyelination, areas of liquefactive necrosis, and various degenerative changes such as neuronophagia, satellitosis, and extensive microgliosis, all contributing to the formation of microglial nodules (Sharma et al. 1998).

This study was undertaken to provide an in-depth evaluation of the pathological and immunomolecular aspects of Taenia multiceps metacestode (Coenurus cerebralis) infections in naturally affected sheep. The core objectives included:

Determine the prevalence and anatomical distribution of C. cerebralis cysts in clinically affected sheep; characterize the histopathological alterations in the central nervous system associated with cyst development; evaluate the local immune response through immunohistochemical detection of key inflammatory markers (CD3 and TNF-α) in brain tissues; confirm the molecular identity and phylogenetic relationship of the parasite using COX1 gene sequencing; and explore the cytokine gene expression profile in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) to reveal the host’s systemic immunological response to neurocoenurosis. By integrating clinical, pathological, molecular, and immunological data, this study aims to enhance the current understanding of host–parasite interactions in ovine coenurosis and to identify potential biomarkers for early diagnosis and immunotherapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

Case history

A total of 200 sheep (120 males and 80 females), aged between 7 months and 9 years, were clinically examined between March and August 2024. The animals belonged to 15 sheep herds located in peri-urban districts of Cairo and Giza provinces, Egypt. All sheep were subjected to thorough clinical and neurological examinations. Animals showing clinical signs suggestive of coenurosis—such as circling behavior, head pressing, blindness, and ataxia—were selected for further investigation. Consent was obtained from herd owners prior to the collection of post-mortem samples from slaughtered animals.

Necropsy

The heads of animals suspected of infection were subjected to gross examination at the Parasitology Department, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Cairo University, to identify Coenurus cerebralis cysts. Cysts found within the brain tissue of infected sheep were meticulously removed and washed using physiological saline. The isolated cysts were preserved in 70% ethanol to maintain the structural integrity of parasite tissues and to prevent enzymatic degradation of nucleic acids prior to molecular analysis. Ethanol fixation is widely used for parasitological specimens because it effectively preserves DNA quality while inhibiting microbial growth and tissue decomposition. This preservation method ensures that genomic DNA remains stable during storage and transport, thereby allowing accurate downstream molecular identification, including PCR amplification and sequencing of target genes such as mitochondrial cox1.

Parasitological examination

After being cleaned in phosphate buffered saline, the obtained brain cysts had all of their morphological characteristics recorded. Each cyst’s protoscolices were kept in 70% ethanol. Each scolex’s small and large hooks were numbered and measured in order to determine the cyst’s species (Oryan et al. 2015).

Molecular identification of C. cerebralis cyst

DNA extraction and PCR analysis

The cysts previously preserved in ethanol were thoroughly rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Genomic DNA was then extracted from the entire cyst using a commercial kit (Thermo Scientific Gene JET Genomic DNA Purification Kit) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The extracted DNA was stored at − 20 °C until used for amplification. The mitochondrial COX1 gene was targeted for PCR using specific forward (5′-CTTTGAGTGCGTGGTTGTTG-3′) and reverse (5′-AGAACCTACAGTGCACACAAT-3′) primers, as described by (Amer et al. 2017). Amplification was carried out in a Nexus Gradient Eppendorf thermal cycler at an annealing temperature of 53 °C for 35 s. The resulting amplicons were visualized on 1.5% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide (0.5 mg/ml) and examined under UV transillumination.

PCR product sequencing

The PCR products were purified using the Big-Dye Xterminator Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the supplier’s instructions. Sequencing was conducted using the Big Dye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit, and samples were analyzed with the 3500 Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Bidirectional sequencing was performed using the same forward and reverse primers designed for the ITS1 nuclear gene. The resulting electropherograms were reviewed and edited using FinchTV software (v1.4.0, Geospira Inc©) to ensure quality. Sequence comparison was carried out using the BLAST tool (blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), followed by alignment with known E. granulosus sequences from GenBank using Clustal W and BioEdit software. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the MEGA 7 software. The finalized sequences from this study were submitted to GenBank for accession.

Histopathological examination

Samples from both the brain and cerebellum were preserved in 10% neutral buffered formalin. These tissues then underwent standard histological processing and were stained using hematoxylin and eosin (HandE) to facilitate microscopic examination, following the methods described by (Bancroft and Gamble 2008) and (Prophet et al. 1992).

Immunohistochemistry

To assess the inflammatory response associated with parasitic lesions, brain tissue surrounding cyst lesions were subjected to immunohistochemical staining using the streptavidin-biotin-peroxidase complex (ABC) technique—an established approach for detecting specific antigens in histological samples (Hsu et al. 1981). Primary antibodies included monoclonal rabbit anti-human CD3 and TNF-α (both diluted 1:150; Invitrogen, USA). A polyclonal biotinylated goat anti-polyvalent antibody (TP-125-BN, Lab Vision, USA) served as the secondary antibody. The stained slides were examined under a light microscope at 40× magnification, selecting five random fields per slide for analysis. Both the intensity and spatial distribution of the stained cells were evaluated according to standardized immunohistochemical protocols (Shi et al. 2001; Ramos-Vara 2005).

The immunostained sections were examined using a light microscope at 40× magnification. Five non-overlapping high-power fields (HPFs) were randomly selected from each tissue section. Field selection focused primarily on areas adjacent to the parasitic cysts and regions exhibiting perivascular inflammatory infiltration. The number and distribution of immunopositive cells were assessed in each selected field, and the staining intensity was qualitatively evaluated based on the presence of brown chromogenic signals in the cell membrane (CD3) or cytoplasm (TNF-α). This approach allowed consistent assessment of the inflammatory cellular response associated with Coenurus cerebralis lesions in the examined brain tissues.

Analysis of cerebrospinal fluid using gene expression

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples were collected aseptically from sheep by lumbar puncture using sterile disposable needles and syringes, following standard veterinary neurological procedures. Immediately after collection, the samples were transferred to sterile RNase-free tubes and stored on ice to preserve RNA integrity. Total RNA was extracted from CSF samples using a commercial RNA extraction kit (e.g., GeneJET RNA Purification Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity and concentration of the extracted RNA were evaluated using spectrophotometric analysis, and the RNA samples were stored at − 80 °C until further use for complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis and quantitative real-time PCR analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was used to quantify the mRNA levels of four key cytokines: interleukin-4 (IL-4), IL-6, IL-10, and IL-12, employing gene-specific primers (Table 1) and SYBR Green chemistry. The expression of each target gene was normalized to the GAPDH housekeeping gene, and the relative fold-change was calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). These analyses provided a sensitive and accurate measure of cytokine gene regulation in response to C. cerebralis infection and offered critical insights into the neuroimmune status of affected sheep.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 29; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard error (SE). Differences in cytokine gene expression levels between infected and healthy control sheep were evaluated using the independent samples Student’s t-test. When comparisons involved more than two variables, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to determine significant pairwise differences. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical signs

Out of the 200 sheep examined, 20 (10%) exhibited characteristic neurological signs, such as ataxia, head tilting, circling, and blindness. The symptoms were more pronounced in animals between 1 and 2 years of age.

Macroscopic results

Cysts were predominantly located superficially in the cerebrum (80%) and cerebellum (20%). Each cyst contained numerous white scolices grouped in clusters. Infected brain tissues showed meningeal congestion, edema, and localized swelling.

Molecular Results

Phylogenetic analysis confirmed that the isolate clustered within the Taenia multiceps clade, supported by high bootstrap values. The amplified DNA from the C. cerebralis cysts corresponded to a 447 bp product. This partial COX1 gene sequence was aligned with reference sequences using the BLAST tool. All sequence comparisons were further validated using the MEGA7 software to construct phylogenetic trees, which corroborated the initial BLAST alignment. The sequence analyzed in this work (GenBank accession no. PP838575) displayed complete homology with the reference sequences mentioned above (LC271726, LC271732, and LC271737); Fig. 1.

Histopathological Findings

Microscopic evaluation of brain tissue demonstrated granulomatous inflammation accompanied by perivascular cuffing. Additional features included the presence of multinucleated giant cells, necrotic zones, and gliosis. Extensive liquefactive necrosis was also observed, along with signs of satellitosis and neuronophagia (Fig. 2).

Immunohistochemistry

Staining for CD3 and TNF-α revealed distinct immune cell localization. TNF-α–positive cells exhibited dark brown staining within the cytoplasm, while CD3 + T lymphocytes showed strong membrane staining of a similar hue. These positively stained cells—particularly macrophages and larger immune cells—were primarily located around blood vessels and in close proximity to the parasitic cysts. Quantitative assessment of staining intensity ranked CD3 higher than TNF-α, indicating that T lymphocytes were more abundant. This predominance suggests an adaptive immune response driven mainly by T cells (Figs. 3 and 4).

The percentage of positively stained cells was calculated relative to the total number of cells in each field. CD3-positive T lymphocytes showed a significantly higher proportion of immunoreactive cells compared with TNF-α-positive cells in the perivascular and pericystic inflammatory regions. Cytokine Expression in CSF

Quantitative PCR analysis of cerebrospinal fluid from symptomatic sheep showed significantly elevated mRNA levels of IL-6 and IL-10. IL-12 expression was moderately increased, reflecting a Th1-type immune response, while IL-4 expression remained low and did not differ significantly from control animals (p = 0.07). Overall, the cytokine expression pattern indicated a combination of pro-inflammatory and regulatory immune activity within the central nervous system, with IL-6, IL-10, and IL-12 all showing statistically significant upregulation (p < 0.05) in affected cases (Fig. 5; Table 3).

Discussion

Coenurosis presents a significant threat to small ruminant production, particularly in developing countries, where it leads to high rates of illness and death—especially among young animals (Soliman et al. 2022; Scala and Varcasia 2006; Abera et al. 2016; Amer et al. 2017). In the current study, the prevalence of coenurosis among examined sheep was found to be 10%. This rate is notably higher than previous reports from Dakahlia Governorate, Egypt, where (El Beskawy et al. 2017) recorded 3.48%, and (Amer et al. 2017) reported 3.03% in Ashmoun and El Sadat, El Menoufia Province. Similar lower rates were observed in Iraq (7.3%; Karim 1979), Jordan (3%; Achenef et al. 1999; Abo-Shehada et al. 2002), and Italy (0.35%; Scala et al. 2007). Conversely, higher prevalence rates were reported in Turkey (15.5%; Gicik et al. 2007), Iran (18.7%; Tavassoli et al., 2011), and Tanzania (44.4%; Miran et al. 2015). These variations may stem from diverse geographical, environmental, and socio-economic conditions, as suggested by (Sharma and Chauhan 2006) and (Ajaj et al.2021) .

To date, no detailed microscopic investigations have explored the specific immune cell types involved in the inflammation surrounding coenurosis lesions in the central nervous system. The CD3 antigen plays a crucial role in initiating both cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CD8+) and helper T cells (CD4+) (Rahsan et al. 2018). As a reliable immunohistochemical marker, CD3 is expressed on the membranes of all mature T-cells and is commonly present in cases of T-cell leukemia and lymphoma. Research by (Kmiecik et al. 2013) has shown that a higher presence of CD3 + immune cells infiltrating brain tumors correlates with improved prognosis and longer survival in patients with glioblastoma. (Chai et al. 2024) reported an increased distribution density of CD3+ T cells in M. benedeni. (Caballero and Pamer 2015) found that in Eimeria vermicularis infection, the number of Intraepithelial lymphocytes (IEL) increases sharply when the number of coccidium oocysts increases. Macrophages, known for their functional plasticity, can switch between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory phenotypes. (Rodriguez-Cruz et al. 2019) identified a novel subset of macrophages expressing CD3, which can be further divided into CD3+TCRαβ + and CD3+TCRαβ − types. The latter subgroup is capable of secreting TNF-α, which may account for the elevated expression of this cytokine detected in the current study’s immunohistochemical analysis. TNF-α itself is a well-characterized pro-inflammatory mediator, commonly associated with several autoimmune and inflammatory disorders such as rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis, and Crohn’s disease.

and in parasitic infections such M. benedeni as recorded by (Chai et al. 2024). In affected sheep, immunohistochemical staining revealed distinct cellular markers: CD3-positive cells exhibited intense membrane-bound brown coloration, while TNF-α–positive cells showed dark brown cytoplasmic staining. These immune cells, including macrophages and other large cell types, were primarily concentrated near the parasitic cysts and within perivascular cuffs. (Rahsan et al. 2018) also employed immunohistochemistry to identify T lymphocytes (CD3), B lymphocytes (CD79), macrophages (CD163), and neurodegenerative markers (GFAP), highlighting their distribution around lesion margins and within brain tissue. GFAP expression indicated reactive astrocytic responses, as previously noted by (Papparella 2004).

Quantitative comparison of staining intensity in this study ranked CD3 expression higher than TNF-α, aligning with (Rahsan et al. 2018), who observed that macrophages (CD163+) were the predominant immune cells in granulomatous inflammation. The intensity of CD3, CD79α, and CD163 staining further supports that macrophages are the principal cell type in chronic parasitic infections such as coenurosis.

Moreover, studies by (Del Brutto et al. 2015) and (Dametto 2016) suggest that TNF-α levels can rise significantly due to the degeneration of cysticerci, whether naturally or induced by anthelmintic treatment. (Williams 1983) characterized granulomatous inflammation as a chronic response primarily involving macrophages, epithelioid cells, and multinucleated giant cells, typically accompanied by lymphocytes, plasma cells, and fibroblasts. However, newer research (Prendergast and Anderton 2009) demonstrates that activated immune cells can traverse the blood brain barriers(BBB). In coenurosis, the nature of the host immune response depends on factors such as cyst location, disease stage, and parasite load (Gonzales et al. 2016).

A robust cellular immune response can delay disease progression (Garcia et al. 2014), while cytokine signaling plays a pivotal role in managing inflammation and neural tissue damage. These cytokines stimulate astrocyte activation and gliosis, prompting a rapid response from astrocytes and nearby endothelial cells, as detailed by (Fleury et al. 2015). Further histopathological and parasitological studies are needed to deepen our understanding of coenurosis.

The cytokine expression profile observed in this study provides important insight into the host immune response during cerebral coenurosis. The significant upregulation of IL-6 suggests an active inflammatory response within the central nervous system, as IL-6 plays a key role in mediating acute inflammatory reactions and promoting immune cell recruitment to sites of infection. Concurrently, the elevated expression of IL-10 indicates the presence of regulatory mechanisms aimed at limiting excessive inflammation and protecting neural tissues from immune-mediated damage. The increased IL-12 expression further supports the activation of a Th1-mediated immune response, which is commonly associated with defense against parasitic infections and stimulation of cell-mediated immunity. In contrast, the relatively low expression of IL-4 suggests a limited Th2-type response in the examined cases. Collectively, these findings indicate that cerebral coenurosis in sheep is characterized by a predominately Th1-oriented immune response accompanied by regulatory cytokine activity that may help modulate inflammation within the central nervous system.

This investigation reports a 10% prevalence of cerebral coenurosis in sheep from Cairo and Giza, aligning with earlier findings from Egypt (Amer et al. 2017) but varying widely from international data. These discrepancies suggest that environmental, ecological, and geographic variables significantly influence disease burden.

Histological evidence of granulomatous inflammation and liquefactive necrosis corroborates the chronic and destructive progression of C. cerebralis in the central nervous system. These patterns are consistent with observations from prior research by (Oryan et al. 2015) and (Sharma et al. 1998) reinforcing the role of persistent antigenic exposure and parasitic damage in shaping the local immune environment.

The predominance of CD3 + T lymphocytes in immunohistochemical assays point to a strong cell-mediated immune response as a major driver of pathogenesis. This parallels similar immune responses observed in central nervous system diseases such as gliomas, where increased CD3 + infiltrates are associated with more favorable immune engagement (Kmiecik et al. 2013). The detection of CD3+TCRα; β– macrophages, known producers of TNF-α, may explain the concurrent expression of this cytokine in affected tissues.

The cytokine expression data derived from CSF qPCR analysis further supports this immunological profile. Elevated levels of IL-6 and IL-10suggest an ongoing inflammatory response coupled with a regulatory signaling to mitigate tissue damage. IL-12 expression signifies a Th1-skewed immune response, essential for eliminating intracellular pathogens. In contrast, the low expression of IL-4 indicates a minimal Th2 response. Molecular verification using COX1 gene sequencing confirmed the identity of C. cerebralis with high confidence, as also supported by previous reports (Ajaj et al. 2021). This finding underscores the reliability of PCR-based molecular diagnostics in detecting coenurosis, particularly when imaging and serological tests are not available.

Study limitations

Despite the valuable findings obtained in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the number of infected animals included in the molecular and immunological analyses was relatively limited, which may restrict the generalization of the results. Second, the study was conducted in sheep herds located only in the peri-urban districts of Cairo and Giza, and therefore the findings may not fully represent the epidemiological situation in other regions of Egypt. Additionally, cytokine expression was assessed only in cerebrospinal fluid, and further investigations including peripheral blood or brain tissue cytokine profiling could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the host immune response. Finally, although molecular confirmation using the cox1 gene was performed, additional genetic markers and larger sample sizes would strengthen phylogenetic analyses and improve our understanding of the genetic diversity of Taenia multiceps in Egypt. Future studies incorporating broader geographic sampling and expanded immunological analyses are therefore recommended.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that Coenurus cerebralis infection in sheep causes significant neurological and immune alterations. Through combined molecular, histological, and immunohistochemical analyses, the research confirms the parasite’s identity and highlights its pathogenic impact on the central nervous system. The predominance of CD3⁺ T cells and TNF-α-positive cells indicates a strong cellular immune response. While elevated IL-6, IL-10, and IL-12 expression reflects both inflammatory and regulatory immune activity. Overall, these findings emphasize the importance of molecular and immunological markers for accurate diagnosis, disease monitoring, and the development of targeted immunotherapies or vaccines against ovine coenurosis.
