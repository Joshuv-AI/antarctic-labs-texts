Efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant therapy combined with immunotherapy in MMR‑proficient/microsatellite stable non‑metastatic rectal cancer: a systematic review and meta‑analysis
Authors: Yuegang Li, Chengcheng Han, Jianqiang Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo comprehensively evaluate the efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant therapy combined with immunotherapy in patients with MMR-proficient/microsatellite stable (pMMR/MSS) non-metastatic rectal cancer.MethodsMajor databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, ASCO, and ESMO, were systematically searched for studies on neoadjuvant therapy combined with immunotherapy in pMMR/MSS non-metastatic rectal cancer, up to April 2025. Statistical analysis utilized Stata 18 software, calculating outcomes with pathological complete response (pCR), major pathological response (MPR), clinical complete response (cCR), R0 resection, anal preservation rate, and the incidence of adverse events. Heterogeneity and publication bias were evaluated using I² and funnel plots.ResultsA total of 21 trial cohorts from 18 studies were included in the meta-analysis. The overall pooled pCR, MPR, and cCR rates were 35% (95% CI: 30–40%, I2 = 48.8%, p < 0.001), 58% (95% CI: 51–64%, I2 = 69.0%, p < 0.001), and 19% (95% CI: 11–26%, I2 = 86.2%, p < 0.001), respectively. An R0 resection rate of 99% (95% CI: 99–100%, I2 = 51.8%) and an anal preservation rate of 84% (95% CI: 79–90%, I2 = 75.1%) were also achieved. Regarding safety, grade ≥ 3 immune-related adverse events (irAEs) and surgery-related adverse events (srAEs) occurred at rates of 1% and 6%, respectively. Notably, subgroup analyses demonstrated that short-course chemoradiotherapy (SCRT) yielded significantly higher pCR (46% vs. 31%, p < 0.001) and MPR (66% vs. 53%, p = 0.02) rates compared to Long-course chemoradiotherapy (LCRT), with a trend toward superior cCR (25% vs. 15%, p = 0.08). Comparison among neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) protocols revealed comparable pCR rates across consolidation (36%), induction (34%), and concurrent (35%) strategies (p = 0.97), although the MPR rate differed significantly (p < 0.05). Additionally, subgroup analysis based on immune checkpoint inhibitor type demonstrated similar efficacy between monotherapy (pCR: 36%) and dual therapy with PD-1 plus CTLA-4 inhibitors (pCR: 29%), with no statistically significant differences observed across endpoints.ConclusionNeoadjuvant therapy combined with immunotherapy in pMMR/MSS non-metastatic rectal cancer demonstrates promising efficacy—with high response rates, excellent R0 resection, and favorable anal preservation outcomes—and an acceptable safety profile. The improved outcomes observed with short-course radiotherapy underscore its potential role in optimizing treatment. However, the heterogeneous nature of current evidence and study designs highlight the need for further high-quality randomized trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-025-06387-4.Keywords: Neoadjuvant therapy, Immunotherapy, Rectal cancer, pMMR/MSS, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Rectal cancer remains a significant global health challenge, accounting for a substantial proportion of colorectal cancer morbidity and mortality worldwide, with many patients requiring multimodal treatment strategies to achieve optimal outcomes (Bray et al. 2024). Traditional neoadjuvant radiotherapy combined with surgery and chemotherapy typically yields a pCR rate of only approximately 15% (Kim et al. 2023; Conroy et al. 2024; Moretto et al. 2024). Recently, the introduction of total neoadjuvant therapy has improved pCR rates to over 30% (Bahadoer et al. 2021; Kagawa et al. 2024), yet these advances still fall short of meeting the clinical demand for higher organ preservation and enhanced survival, particularly in the pMMR/MSS population where effective treatment options remain limited. In the field of immunotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors have revolutionized treatment paradigms, especially in Microsatellite Instability-High (MSI-H) tumors where they demonstrate remarkable efficiency (Le et al. 2015). In contrast, the majority of rectal cancers present as pMMR/MSS, which are characterized by limited immunogenicity and poor responses to conventional immunotherapeutic strategies (Hajj et al. 2023). Furthermore, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in pMMR/MSS rectal cancer poses additional challenges to eliciting robust antitumor immunity.

Recent studies suggest that combining immunotherapy with radiotherapy, particularly SCRT, may help overcome these obstacles. SCRT has the potential to modulate the tumor microenvironment, enhance antigen presentation, and boost tumor immunogenicity, thereby sensitizing tumors to immunotherapy. Moreover, differences in treatment protocols—such as the timing and sequencing of immunotherapy relative to radiotherapy, and the choice of immune checkpoint inhibitors could significantly influence clinical outcomes (Wu et al. 2023).

Given these uncertainties, a systematic synthesis of existing evidence is urgently needed to delineate the true value of neoadjuvant therapy combined with immunotherapy in pMMR/MSS rectal cancer. In this context, our meta-analysis aims to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant therapy combined with immunotherapy in this patient population through rigorous subgroup analyses, including comparisons between SCRT and LCRT as well as assessments based on different NAT protocols. This work seeks to provide a unified perspective on current outcomes and to guide future research directions and clinical decision-making. This research adhered to the TITAN Guidelines 2025 and did not employ any AI-based tools (Agha et al. 2025).

Methods

Literature search

This study was conducted in strict accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, ensuring a comprehensive and transparent review process. In addition, the methodology has been documented following the AMSTAR (Assessing the Methodological Quality of Systematic Reviews) standards (Page et al. 2021; Shea et al. 2017). Notably, this study protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews) under registration number CRD420251042248. We systematically searched the literature related to the application of neoadjuvant therapy combined with immunotherapy in non-metastatic pMMR/MSS rectal cancer. The databases used for the search included PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Embase, as well as conference abstracts from ASCO and ESMO to obtain the latest research data. The search was conducted up to April 2025. To ensure a comprehensive retrieval, keywords and their synonyms—such as “neoadjuvant therapy”, “immunotherapy,” “pMMR/MSS,” and “rectal cancer”—were employed. Details of the search methodology could be available in Supplementary 1. Eligible studies included clinical trials (single-arm, cohort, or randomized controlled trials) investigating neoadjuvant therapy combined with immunotherapy in patients with non-metastatic pMMR/MSS rectal cancer. For randomized trials, only the experimental arms receiving immunotherapy were included. Studies were required to report at least one primary outcome of interest (e.g., pCR, MPR, cCR, R0 resection rate, or anal preservation rate). Exclusion criteria included reviews, case reports, editorials, animal studies, studies without original clinical data, and duplicate publications. Title and abstract screening, as well as full-text review, were independently performed by two reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion to ensure consistency and objectivity. A third reviewer was consulted when necessary.

Outcome measures

The studies included in this analysis focused on multiple clinical endpoints, including pCR, MPR, cCR, R0 resection rate, and anal preservation rate. Additionally, to assess treatment safety, data on adverse events were systematically collected, with particular attention given to the incidence and severity of irAEs and srAEs.

Statistical analysis and heterogeneity assessment

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 18 software, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Pooled outcomes were expressed as percentages. Inter-study heterogeneity was quantitatively evaluated using the I² statistic and further confirmed via the Cochran Q chi-square test. When heterogeneity was substantial (I2 ≥ 50%), a random-effects model was employed; for minimal heterogeneity (I2 < 50%), a fixed-effects model was used. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore variations in clinical outcomes across different treatment protocols and patient characteristics. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, which were constructed to visually evaluate potential bias based on asymmetry. Additionally, Egger’s and Begg’s tests were performed to quantitatively assess potential publication bias for pCR and MPR outcomes.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Full-text data were extracted collaboratively by two independent reviewers, who also carried out a strict quality assessment of the included studies. Given that some data were sourced from ASCO and ESMO conference abstracts—which may not be as comprehensive as peer-reviewed articles—special consideration was given to the limitations of these sources during data interpretation and discussion, ensuring the scientific robustness and reliability of the conclusions. Given the predominance of single-arm trials, study quality was assessed using the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS), which provides a structured scoring system for non-randomized designs. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of pooled effect sizes. A leave-one-out approach was applied, whereby each study was excluded in turn and the meta-analysis was repeated.

Results

Literature search results and quality assessment

After screening titles, abstracts, and full texts, our search initially identified 18 studies. Among these, 5 were available only as conference abstracts, and 6 were randomized controlled trials. However, due to the diverse treatment approaches across these studies, we extracted only the cohorts that specifically involved neoadjuvant therapy combined with immunotherapy for rectal cancer. As a result, the final meta-analysis was based on data from 21 distinct trial cohorts. Table 1 provides a summary of the key characteristics and detailed information for all included cohorts, and Fig. 1 illustrates the study selection process. Given that most studies included in this meta-analysis were single-arm trials, we employed the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) for quality evaluation. MINORS provides a structured framework for assessing non-randomized studies. Studies were scored on a scale, with 0–12 indicating lower quality and 13–16 representing higher quality. Any conflicts during the assessment were thoroughly discussed and resolved collaboratively to ensure reliability and consensus.

Meta-analyses

A total of 21 trial cohorts from 18 studies were included in the meta-analysis. The pooled pCR rate was 35% (95% CI: 0.30–0.40, I2 = 48.8%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2a). Data from 17 cohorts yielded a pooled MPR rate of 58% (95% CI: 0.51–0.64, I2 = 69.0%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2b), while analysis of 10 cohorts revealed a cCR rate of 19% (95% CI: 0.11–0.26, I2 = 86.2%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2c). Moreover, 11 cohorts demonstrated a remarkably high R0 resection rate of 99% (95% CI: 0.99–1.00, I2 = 51.8%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2d), and the pooled anal preservation rate from 14 cohorts was 84% (95% CI: 0.79–0.90, I2 = 75.1%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2e). To further assess the robustness of the pooled estimates, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were performed for pCR and MPR. Corresponding plots are provided in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2.

Forest plot of pCR rate (a). Forest plot of MPR rate (b). Forest plot of cCR rate (c). Forest plot of R0 resection rate (d). Forest plot of anal preservation rate (e)

Data on grade 1–2 irAEs were extracted from 5 trial cohorts, with a combined incidence of 45% (95% CI: 0.28–0.61; I2 = 85.9%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3a). In parallel, grade ≥ 3 irAEs had a pooled incidence of 1% (95% CI: −0.01–0.03; I2 = 54.2%, P = 0.17) (Fig. 3b). For TRAEs, data on grade 1–2 events were taken from 4 cohorts. Owing to low heterogeneity (I2 = 27.4%, P < 0.001), a fixed-effects model was applied, yielding a pooled incidence of 13% (95% CI: 0.08–0.18) (Fig. 3c). Additionally, the pooled incidence for grade ≥ 3 TRAEs was 6% (95% CI: 0.06–0.603; I2= 0.00%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3d).

Subgroup meta-analyses

To explore the impact of different treatment strategies on the efficacy of neoadjuvant therapy, we conducted detailed subgroup meta-analyses focusing on radiotherapy regimens and NAT protocols. In the comparison of radiotherapy regimens, patients receiving SCRT achieved a pCR rate of 46% (95% CI: 0.39–0.52, p < 0.001), which was significantly higher than the 31% observed in the LCRT group (95% CI: 0.27–0.35, p < 0.001). The difference between the two groups was statistically significant (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4a). Similarly, the MPR rate was 66% (95% CI: 0.61–0.72, p < 0.001) in the SCRT group compared with 53% (95% CI: 0.43–0.63, p < 0.001) in the LCRT group, with the intergroup difference reaching statistical significance (p = 0.02) (Fig. 4b). In addition, the cCR rate in the SCRT group was 25% (95% CI: 0.18–0.33, p < 0.001), compared to only 15% (95% CI: 0.07–0.24, p < 0.001) in the LCRT group; although the difference did not reach conventional statistical significance, it showed a trend (p = 0.08) (Fig. 4c).

Subgroup analysis based on radiotherapy regimens: pCR comparison of SCRT versus LCRT(a). MPR comparison of SCRT versus LCRT(b). cCR comparison of SCRT versus LCRT(c)

In immunotherapy-type NAT, the treatment protocols were categorized into three groups—consolidation, induction, and concurrent. Regarding pCR, the consolidation group achieved a rate of 36% (95% CI: 0.29–0.42, p < 0.001), the induction group 34% (95% CI: 0.24–0.45, p < 0.001), and the concurrent group 35% (95% CI: 0.27–0.43, p < 0.001); comparisons among these groups revealed no significant differences (p = 0.97) (Fig. 5a). MPR rates were 52% (95% CI: 0.40–0.64, p < 0.001) for the consolidation group, 53% (95% CI: 0.43–0.63, p < 0.001) for the induction group, and 68% (95% CI: 0.61–0.74, p < 0.001) for the concurrent group, with differences among groups reaching statistical significance (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5b). Lastly, cCR rates were 17% (95% CI: − 0.02–0.36, p = 0.08) in the consolidation group, 23% (95% CI: 0.11–0.35, p < 0.001) in the induction group, and 16% (95% CI: 0.01–0.32, p = 0.04) in the concurrent group, though the intergroup differences were not significant (p = 0.78) (Fig. 5c).

Subgroup analysis of immuno-neoadjuvant therapy protocols: pCR comparison among consolidation, induction, and concurrent treatment groups (a). MPR comparison among consolidation, induction, and concurrent treatment groups (b). cCR comparison among consolidation, induction, and concurrent treatment groups (c)

Furthermore, subgroup analyses based on the type of immune checkpoint inhibitors revealed that pCR rates were 36% for monotherapy with either PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors, while the dual immunotherapy combination of PD-1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors achieved a pCR rate of 29% (95% CI: 0.11–0.48, p < 0.001) (Fig. 6a). The corresponding MPR rates were 57% (95% CI: 0.51–0.64, p < 0.001) for PD-1, 61% (95% CI: 0.41–0.81, p < 0.001) for PD-L1, and 46% (95% CI: 0.19–0.73, p < 0.001) for the combined PD-1 plus CTLA-4 approach (Fig. 6b). Finally, the cCR rates were 18% (95% CI: 0.10–0.26, p < 0.001) for PD-1 monotherapy and 33% (95% CI: 0.03–0.64, p = 0.03) for the combination therapy (Fig. 6c). Importantly, tests for subgroup differences across all three endpoints yielded p values greater than 0.05, indicating that the observed numerical differences among the groups are not statistically significant.

MPR comparison among PD-1, PD-L1, and combined PD-1 plus CTLA-4 therapies (b). cCR comparison among PD-1 monotherapy and combination therapy (c)

Publication bias

To evaluate the potential publication bias in this study, we generated funnel plots for the three key outcomes—pCR, MPR, and cCR (Fig. 7a to Fig. 7c). Visual inspection of these funnel plots revealed relatively symmetric distributions. Nonetheless, given that some of the included studies had small sample sizes and varying quality, the possibility of selective reporting or small-sample effects cannot be entirely ruled out. Overall, the available evidence suggests that publication bias had a limited impact on the results of this meta-analysis. However, caution is warranted when interpreting these findings, and future high-quality studies are recommended to further validate these conclusions. Egger’s and Begg’s tests were performed for pCR and MPR outcomes. For pCR, Egger’s test yielded P = 0.9379 and Begg’s test P = 0.7858. For MPR, Egger’s test yielded P = 0.1038 and Begg’s test P = 0.1275. None of the tests indicated statistically significant publication bias.

Discussion

This study presents the first systematic meta-analysis specifically focused on neoadjuvant immunotherapy in non-metastatic pMMR/MSS rectal cancer, integrating efficacy, safety, and treatment timing across diverse clinical cohorts. Overall, the data demonstrated a pCR of 35%, an MPR of 58%, an impressive R0 resection rate of 99%, and a satisfactory anal preservation rate; regarding adverse events, most were Grade I–II, indicating that the treatment was well tolerated. These results are consistent with the findings reported in the meta-analysis by Zhang et al., which indicated a pCR rate of 37% (Zhang et al. 2025), further confirming the significant effect of neoadjuvant therapy combined with immunotherapy in improving local pathological responses. Additionally, a meta-analysis by Anwaar Saeed et al. (Ansab et al. 2025). evaluated PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors combined with neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy in locally advanced rectal cancer and included subgroup analyses by radiotherapy type. However, their study encompassed a broader patient population and did not specifically focus on non-metastatic pMMR/MSS disease or explore immunotherapy timing and combination strategies. Our analysis builds on this by providing more targeted subgroup comparisons to inform treatment optimization in this specific cohort.

In comparing radiotherapy regimens, we observed that patients treated with SCRT achieved significantly higher pCR and MPR rates than those undergoing LCRT. This benefit likely results from SCRT’s ability to deliver a high radiation dose in a short time, rapidly inducing tumor apoptosis and shrinkage to enhance the pathological response (Park et al. 2015; Han et al. 2023). Although the improvement in cCR was only marginal—possibly due to variations in assessment criteria, treatment parameters, and patient differences—the overall advantage is clear. Moreover, while combination therapy has boosted pCR rates, its effect on survival outcomes has been documented in only two studies. Notably, the Japanese VOLTAGE-A and NRG-GI002 studies presented long-term follow-up data at the 2023 ASCO GI meeting. In VOLTAGE-A, among MSS patients, the immunotherapy combination group achieved a 3-year overall survival (OS) rate exceeding 97.4%. Similarly, in NRG-GI002, the immunotherapy group reported a 3-year OS of 95%, markedly higher than the 87% observed in the chemoradiotherapy-only group. Therefore, selecting a radiotherapy regimen should be individualized to balance short-term efficacy with long-term survival outcomes.

After categorizing neoadjuvant immunotherapy protocols by their timing—namely consolidation, induction, and concurrent—we observed that while pCR and cCR did not significantly differ among the groups, the concurrent strategy demonstrated a clear advantage in achieving a higher MPR. This benefit is likely attributable to radiotherapy’s ability to rapidly induce local immune activation. Studies have shown that high-dose radiation promptly triggers tumor cell apoptosis and the release of tumor-associated antigens, which in turn activates dendritic cells and promotes the priming of tumor-specific T cells. This process facilitates the recruitment and expansion of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) within the tumor microenvironment, enhances the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and may contribute to the formation of long-term immune memory. These mechanisms collectively enhance the antitumor immune response and may explain the superior pathological outcomes observed with SCRT (Zhu et al. 2022). Furthermore, when comparing immune checkpoint inhibitor strategies—using PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors as monotherapy versus in combination with CTLA-4 inhibitors—we found that although short-term efficacy did not reach statistically significant differences, the combination approach showed a numerical advantage. This suggests that the synergistic effects of the combined regimen might further potentiate antitumor immunity. Consequently, large-scale, randomized controlled trials are urgently needed to determine the optimal immunotherapy protocol and elucidate its underlying mechanisms, ultimately offering more tailored treatment strategies for patients with rectal cancer.

In terms of safety, our analysis indicates that both immune-related and surgery-related adverse events were predominantly low-grade (grade I–II), with a low incidence of grade 3 or higher events. This safety profile appears more favorable compared to that observed with conventional neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. However, it is important to note that the adverse events associated with immunotherapy differ from those induced by chemoradiotherapy. Unlike the early cytotoxic effects of chemotherapy, irAEs often have a delayed onset, persist for longer, and may recur during treatment (Emiloju and Sinicrope 2023). Additionally, while chemotherapeutic agents primarily affect metabolically active cells, irAEs can involve any tissue or organ system—such as the skin and endocrine system—and may result in specific toxicities like pneumonitis, colitis, and myositis (Wang et al. 2024). Since the type and incidence of adverse events are frequently influenced by patients’ baseline conditions and combination therapies, enhanced monitoring and individualized management are essential in clinical practice.

Although this study integrated data from multiple cohorts to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant therapy in rectal cancer, it has certain limitations. First, most of the included studies were single-arm trials, and their design along with relatively small sample sizes may introduce bias. Second, differences in treatment protocols, patient selection, and evaluation criteria across studies resulted in heterogeneity, affecting the generalizability and applicability of the findings. Moreover, the predominance of East Asian cohorts may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Consequently, more high-quality, multicenter randomized controlled trials are needed to further validate and optimize neoadjuvant treatment strategies. Furthermore, due to limited reporting across studies, biomarker-based subgroup analyses—such as PD-L1 expression, tumor mutational burden (TMB), and immune infiltration profiles—could not be performed, which may have constrained our ability to identify predictive factors for treatment response.

In summary, our study not only confirms the benefit of neoadjuvant therapy in improving local pathological response and surgical outcomes in rectal cancer but also strengthens the existing body of literature. Future studies should build on larger sample sizes and improved trial designs to further elucidate the molecular mechanisms behind the synergistic effects of radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy, ultimately providing more precise and individualized treatment options for rectal cancer patients.
