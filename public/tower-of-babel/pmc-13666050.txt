TIME FOR COFFEE coordinates phase separation and chromatin regulation for salt tolerance
Authors: Wenwen Tian, Xinheng Lyu, Grace R. Qian, Xuehua Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The circadian clock enhances plant fitness by aligning internal timing with the external photoperiod, but how it coordinates spatiotemporal regulation with chromatin dynamics to improve salt tolerance is not well understood. Here, we show that the clock component TIME FOR COFFEE (TIC) forms biomolecular condensates in response to salt stress, but not to osmotic stress alone. Consistently, tic mutants were hypersensitive to salt stress yet displayed enhanced salt tolerance under 21-hour light-dark cycles matching their shortened circadian period, highlighting the importance of circadian clock in salt tolerance. Salt stress increases H3K9ac levels at the salt stress–responsive genes and induces their expression in a time-of-day–dependent manner in wild-type plants, whereas both H3K9ac level and transcriptional activation are attenuated in tic mutants. TIC physically associates with HD2-type histone deacetylases, and a subset of HD2 partially colocalizes with TIC condensates in the nucleolus under salt stress. Genetic and biochemical analyses showed that salt stress promotes TIC condensate formation, accompanied by reduced HD2 occupancy at salt stress–responsive loci, thereby elevating H3K9ac levels and promoting gene expression. Together, these findings uncover a mechanism by which salt-induced TIC condensate formation links chromatin regulation with circadian timing, thereby promoting plant adaptation to salt stress.

INTRODUCTION

Earth’s rotation leads to rhythmic fluctuations in environmental conditions (1). To fully exploit these predictable oscillatory factors for better fitness, plants have evolved an intricate endogenous timing system called the circadian clock (2). The circadian clock plays an important role in coordinating the endogenous molecular regulation pathway to better fit the environment (3). TIME FOR COFFEE (TIC) is a crucial nuclear regulator of the circadian clock in Arabidopsis, influencing various biological processes, including hormone signaling, metabolic homeostasis, and environmental stress responses (4–7). TIC interacts with CIRCADIAN CLOCK ASSOCIATED 1 and LATE ELONGATED HYPOCOTYL to form a morning complex that negatively regulates PHYTOCHROME A signaling, thereby repressing hypocotyl elongation under far-red light (8, 9).

Biomolecular condensates are increasingly recognized as a fundamental mechanism for organizing cellular compartments (10, 11). Many biomolecular condensates form through liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS), which is driven by multivalent interactions often mediated by intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) (12, 13). LLPS is highly sensitive to the physicochemical properties of the surrounding conditions (14). Environmental factors, including temperature (15, 16), molecular crowding solvent (17), and osmotic stress (18, 19) promote or dissolve the biomolecular condensates in cells. Recent studies have highlighted a pivotal role of biomolecular condensates in cellular response and adaptation to molecular crowding SEUSS (SEU) and Decapping 5 (DCP5) (20, 21). Very few studies in plants have addressed the role of diverse salt conditions, beyond molecular crowding and osmotic effects, in driving condensation formation during stress adaptation (22).

Hyperosmotic and salt stresses are known to alter chromatin states through changes in histone modifications (23). Sodium chloride (NaCl) treatment has been reported to affect histone acetylation levels, particularly histone H3 lysine 9 acetylation (H3K9ac), which is generally associated with active transcription (24–26). Salt stress can induce dynamic changes in H3K9ac at stress-responsive genes, thereby facilitating transcriptional reprogramming required for stress adaptation (27). These chromatin changes are thought to be mediated by the coordinated regulation of histone acetyltransferases and histone deacetylases (HDACs), thereby linking salt stress signaling with epigenetic control of gene expression (28).

In plants, HDACs are categorized into three families: RPD3, SIR2, and the plant-specific HD2 family (29–33).The HD2 family has been identified to be nucleolar located (34–36) and exhibits functional redundancy and coordination among its members (33, 37–39). The Arabidopsis genome encodes four HD2 members: HISTONE DEACETYLASE 2A (HD2A), HISTONE DEACETYLASE 2B (HD2B), HISTONE DEACETYLASE 2C (HD2C), and HISTONE DEACETYLASE 2D (HD2D), each participating in fundamental processes such as seed germination, root and leaf development, floral transition, and responses to abiotic and biotic stresses (36, 40). Among them, HD2A and HD2C have been shown to regulate global H3K9ac levels (38, 41), indicating a role for HD2 family proteins in chromatin regulation. In addition, HD2C and HD2D positively regulate salt tolerance, although the transcript levels of HD2 family genes are repressed by salt stress (36, 42). Together, these findings suggest that multiple HD2 family members may contribute to the regulation of H3K9ac under salt stress.

In this study, we reveal that salt stress induces TIC condensation, which promotes plant adaptation in coordination with the endogenous circadian rhythm. Upon salt stress, TIC forms condensates that promote the partial partitioning of HD2-type proteins into TIC condensates, accompanied by increased H3K9 acetylation and transcriptional activation of salt stress–responsive genes. Our results reveal a mechanism linking salt-induced biomolecular condensation and chromatin regulation with time-of-day dependent of plant responses to salt stress.

RESULTS

The N-terminal IDR region contributes to the intrinsic condensation propensity of TIC

Prediction of IDRs using IUPred (43) and prion-like regions using PLAAC (44) showed that nearly the entire TIC protein is intrinsically disordered, with residues 824 to 1550 predicted to form a prion-like region (Fig. 1A). To examine whether TIC forms condensates, we generated a TIC-GFP fusion protein and observed speckles in the nuclei of Nicotiana benthamiana cells (Fig. 1B). To identify TIC regions that contribute to condensation, we generated a series of truncation constructs based on sequence and structural predictions. TIC (824-1550)-GFP was designed to include the predicted prion-like domain (44) and TIC (661-1550)-GFP was generated because residues 660 to 677 were predicted to form an α-helical region (fig. S1A) (45). AlphaFold analysis further suggested that residues 458 to 460 may form local contacts and engage in transient intramolecular interactions with residues around 365 (fig. S1B) (45). Therefore, we generated TIC (1-460) and TIC (461-1550) to preserve this predicted interaction, whereas the TIC (1-422) construct excludes this region, allowing us to assess its contribution to TIC condensation (fig. S1B). Both the N-terminal domain of TIC (amino acids 1 to 422) and TIC (1-460) alone were able to form condensates in the nucleus, while TIC (824-1550), TIC (661-1550), TIC (461-1550), and TIC (461-823) failed to form nuclear condensates (Fig. 1B). These results demonstrated that the TIC (1-422) has an intrinsic capacity to form condensates in N. benthamiana, whereas neither the prion-like regions (824-1550) nor the middle region (461-823) showed this capacity. Next, we performed photobleaching experiments and found that TIC condensates were dynamic molecular exchange between TIC condensates and the surrounding nucleoplasm, as the GFP signal recovered from 0.27 to 0.81 within 50 s after photobleaching (Fig. 1, C and D).

To evaluate the intrinsic capacity of TIC to undergo condensate formation in a minimal and well-controlled system, we purified His-GFP and His-TIC (1-422)-GFP from Escherichia coli. (fig. S2A), which was shown to be sufficient for condensate-like puncta formation in N. benthamiana (Fig. 1B). In contrast, purification of full-length TIC was challenging in bacteria, likely due to its large molecular weight (164.5 kDa). The His-TIC (1-422)-GFP solution rapidly became turbid following dilution into buffer containing 50 mM NaCl but the GFP alone did not (Fig. 1E). Turbidity measurements using a spectrophotometer revealed increased absorbance in the His-TIC (1-422)-GFP solution (Fig. 1F). The His-TIC (1-422)-GFP condensates rapidly fused into larger droplets, demonstrating their liquid-like behavior (Fig. 1G and movie S1). TIC condensates exhibited fluorescence recovery to a plateau within 17 min after photobleaching, demonstrating that the protein molecules are dynamically mobile and continuously exchange with the surrounding phase (Fig. 1, H and I, and movie S2). Upon centrifugation, His-TIC (1-422)-GFP condensates sedimented, leaving a clear supernatant (Fig. 1, J to L), indicating the formation of sedimentable assemblies under these conditions. The formation of His-TIC (1-422)-GFP condensates depended on both protein and salt concentrations, with higher protein concentrations or low NaCl concentrations promoting phase separation (fig. S2B). Together, these results indicate that the 1-422 region contributes to the intrinsic condensation propensity of TIC.

Salt stress induces TIC phase separation in Arabidopsis

To examine whether TIC forms biomolecular condensates in Arabidopsis, we generated a CRISPR-Cas9-edited tic mutant as previously described (46) and also analyzed the T-DNA insertion mutant tic-2 (8, 9). In the CRISPR-Cas9–edited tic mutant, a single cytosine deletion was introduced in the third exon, corresponding to the 865th nucleotide of the genomic DNA from the ATG start codon (fig. S3A). Sanger sequencing confirmed that this deletion caused a frameshift mutation, leading to altered downstream amino acids and a premature stop codon (fig. S3, A and B). Next, we generated a GFP-TIC transgene driven by the native TIC promoter and introduced it into a guide RNA– and Cas9-free tic mutant obtained after backcrossing to Col-0. Both the CRISPR-Cas9–edited tic and tic-2 mutants exhibited comparable late-flowering phenotypes, and the GFP-TIC transgene fully rescued the late-flowering phenotype of CRISPR-Cas9–edited tic mutant (fig. S3C) (7), confirming its functionality.

In contrast to the constitutive formation of TIC condensates in N. benthamiana (Fig. 1B), GFP-TIC condensates were not observed in Arabidopsis under normal growth conditions (Fig. 2A). We next examined whether abiotic stresses or pharmacological treatments could induce TIC condensate formation. Nevertheless, TIC did not form nuclear speckles following cold, heat, gibberellic acid (GA), abscisic acid (ABA), or trichostatin A (TSA) treatments (fig. S4A). GFP-TIC formed speckles upon NaCl treatment at both ZT1 and ZT13 (Fig. 2A and fig. S4B), suggesting that salt-induced TIC condensation is not restricted to a specific time of day. TIC-GFP also formed condensates following NaCl treatment, indicating that this response is independent of GFP tag position (fig. S4C). To identify the domains responsible for NaCl-induced condensates, we constructed a series of TIC domain deletion transgenes driven by the native promoter and introduced back to tic mutant in Arabidopsis, which had already been tested in N. benthamiana (Fig. 1B). Upon NaCl treatment, TIC (1-823) formed condensates, whereas neither TIC (1-422) nor TIC (∆423-823) did so (Fig. 2A), suggesting that the region spanning residues 423 to 823 is required for NaCl-induced condensate formation in Arabidopsis. AlphaFold structure prediction (45) further revealed that TIC (302-317) and TIC (660-677) form two α-helical regions connected by a flexible linker (fig. S1A). On the basis of this prediction, we generated TIC (∆423-660)/tic and TIC (423-660)/Col-0 transgenic lines and found that neither construct formed condensates upon NaCl treatment (Fig. 2A and fig. S4D), suggesting that the 423-660 region is required but not sufficient for salt induced condensate formation and that the N-terminal 1-422 region also contributes to this process (Fig. 2A). Thus, intrinsic condensation propensity of the N-terminal 1-422 region alone is insufficient to drive salt-responsive condensate formation in vivo, and the 423-660 region is additionally required, suggesting that intrinsic condensation propensity and salt-responsive condensation represent related but distinct properties of TIC.

Upon treatment with 0.25 M NaCl, visible TIC condensates appeared within approximately 1 min (Fig. 2A). In contrast, under 0.15 M NaCl treatment, TIC condensates were not detectable at early time points but became apparent after approximately 20 to 30 min (Fig. 2B). Because salt stress also imposes osmotic stress (47), we included nonionic osmotic treatments to distinguish salt-specific effects from osmotic effects. Specifically, we compared 0.15 M NaCl with 0.3 M mannitol and 24% PEG-8000 (Fig. 2C) and used 0.5 M mannitol and 0.5 M sorbitol as nonionic osmotic controls for the higher-concentration salt treatments (Fig. 2D). On the basis of the expected numbers of dissolved particles, 0.5 M mannitol and 0.5 M sorbitol are approximately iso-osmotic with 0.25 M monovalent salts, including LiCl, RbCl, NH4NO3, KCl, and NaCl, and are broadly comparable in theoretical osmolarity to 0.15 M MgCl2, Na2SO4, and K2SO4. Under these conditions, all tested salts induced TIC condensate formation, whereas mannitol, sorbitol, and PEG-8000 did not (Fig. 2, C and D). These results indicate that TIC condensation is not simply a consequence of nonionic osmotic stress. Moreover, TIC condensation was reversible, as the condensates rapidly dissolved upon transfer to water after NaCl or KCl treatment (Fig. 2E). The condensates were also disrupted by 1,6-hexanediol treatment (Fig. 2E), suggesting that weak hydrophobic interactions contribute to their formation (48). Consistently, fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) analysis showed that GFP-TIC fluorescence rapidly recovered in the bleached region, with the normalized fluorescence intensity increasing from 0.1059 immediately after photobleaching to 0.5738 within 250 s, corresponding to a recovery fraction of 52.3% (Fig. 2, F and G), indicating a dynamic exchange of TIC molecules within the condensates. Together, these findings indicate that TIC undergoes dynamic and reversible phase separation in response to diverse salt conditions.

TIC condensates regulate plant tolerance to salt stress

To assess the physiological relevance of TIC condensates, we tested the salt tolerance and found that both the CRISPR-Cas9–generated tic mutant and the tic-2 (T-DNA) mutant exhibited significantly reduced survival rate under salt stress (Fig. 3, A and B). We then examined plant growth under different photoperiods, given the established role of TIC in circadian regulation (9). The growth defect of tic under salt stress was partially alleviated when plants were grown under 21-hour cycles (10.5 hours light/10.5 hours dark), compared with 24-hour cycles (12 hours light/12 hours dark) or constant light conditions (fig. S5, A and B). This is likely attributable to the shortened circadian period (∼21 hours) of the tic mutant under free-running conditions (9, 49), and is consistent with the notion that plants better tolerate stress when the external cycle matches with their endogenous circadian period (50). TIC (Δ423-660)/tic also showed improved survival rates under salt stress when grown under 21-hour cycles, similar to the tic mutant, compared with seedlings grown under 24-hour cycles or constant light (fig. S5, A and B). Notably, TIC (1-823)/tic still exhibited salt tolerance comparable to that of the full-length TIC (fig. S6, A to C), despite lacking a substantial C-terminal portion of TIC. The tic mutant also showed reduced tolerance to Na2SO4 or KCl stress (fig. S7, A to C and F) but exhibited wild-type–like tolerance to sorbitol- or mannitol-induced nonionic osmotic stress (fig. S7, D to F). This aligns with our finding that TIC forms condensates in response to NaCl, Na2SO4, K2SO4, or KCl stress, but not to sorbitol- or mannitol-mediated nonionic osmotic stress (Fig. 2, C and D). Consistent with its reduced survival rates, the tic mutant showed substantially increased H2DCFDA fluorescence after salt stress, indicating elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, whereas Col-0 showed no obvious change (Fig. 3, C and D). Correspondingly, the tic mutant exhibited greater inhibition of primary root growth under salt stress (Fig. 3, E and F). The defect in the tic mutant was largely rescued by TIC or TIC (1-823), but only partially rescued by TIC (Δ423-660) (Fig. 3, E and F). These findings are consistent with the survival assays and further support that TIC condensates positively regulate plant tolerance to salt stress.

To investigate the molecular basis of this regulatory role, we examined the expression of three salt stress–responsive genes: NAC domain containing protein 19 (NAC019) (20, 51), Responsive to Dehydration 29A (RD29A) (52), and Responsive to Dehydration 20 (RD20) (53). NAC019 and RD29A exhibited robust circadian expression, with their transcript levels significantly reduced in the tic mutant at their respective peak times, ZT13 and ZT9 (fig. S8, A and B). In contrast, RD20 showed weaker rhythmic oscillation, yet its expression was also reduced in tic during the night (fig. S8C). We further found that salt-induced expression of the clock-controlled NAC019 and RD29A was higher when treatment was initiated at the peak than at the trough of gene expression in Col-0, whereas fold induction was markedly reduced in tic at both time points (fig. S8, D and E). In comparison, the weakly rhythmic RD20 displayed a similar response to salt stress at ZT1 and ZT13 but again showed a reduced fold induction in tic (fig. S8F). Under salt stress, the tic mutant showed reduced expression of NAC019, RD29A, and RD20 compared with Col-0 and GFP-TIC/tic plants (fig. S9A). Expression of TIC (1-823)-GFP in the tic mutant largely restored the expression of these genes, whereas expression of TIC (Δ423-660)-GFP resulted in substantially weaker transcriptional activation (fig. S9A). These results indicate that the 423-660 region is required for TIC-mediated transcriptional regulation. Consistently, salt-induced H3K9ac levels at these loci were reduced in tic mutant, largely restored by expression of TIC (1-823)-GFP in the tic background, and remained substantially lower in plants expressing TIC (Δ423-660)-GFP. Together, these results suggest that the 423-660 region is necessary for TIC condensate-associated H3K9ac deposition and transcriptional activation of salt-responsive genes.

TIC interacts with HD2 proteins

Prior proteomic analyses suggested that TIC may associate with HD2B and HD2C, as several peptides from these proteins were detected in TIC purification, and reciprocal purification of HD2C recovered a few TIC peptides (9, 39). However, direct interaction between TIC and HD2C, as well as other HD2 family proteins, had not been confirmed. To address this, we performed split luciferase assays in N. benthamiana epidermal cells by coexpressing cLUC-TIC with HD2A/HD2B/HD2C/HD2D fused to nLUC (Fig. 4A), as well as bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays by coexpressing nYFP-TIC with HD2A/HD2B/HD2C/HD2D fused to cYFP (Fig. 4B). Both assays demonstrated that TIC interacts with HD2A, HD2B, HD2C, and HD2D (Fig. 4, A and B). Next, we coexpressed TIC-Flag with HD2A-MYC, HD2B-GFP, HD2C-mCherry, and HD2D-HA in N. benthamiana epidermal cells and found that TIC associates with each HD2 protein in vivo (Fig. 4C). We then recombinantly expressed and purified mCherry-HD2A, HD2B-mCherry, mCherry-HD2C, and mCherry-HD2D (fig. S10A). However, none of the HD2 proteins formed detectable condensates on their own in vitro (fig. S10B). By contrast, in the presence of TIC (1-422)-GFP, each HD2 protein displayed condensate-like signals that colocalized with TIC (1-422)-GFP droplets (Fig. 4D), suggesting that HD2 proteins partition into TIC (1-422) condensates rather than independently driving droplet formation in vitro. Consistently, His pull-down and BiFC assays showed that HD2A, HD2B, HD2C, and HD2D each interact with TIC (1-422) (fig. S11, A to E), supporting the notion that the enrichment of each HD2 protein in TIC (1-422) droplets in vitro involves specific protein-protein interactions rather than solely passive partitioning. In Arabidopsis, TIC did not show obvious colocalization with HD2 proteins under normal conditions (fig. S12, A to L). However, a subset of TIC condensates was observed in the nucleolus and showed limited spatial association with HD2 proteins under salt stress (fig. S12, A to L). These findings suggest that salt stress promotes TIC phase separation and positions a subset of TIC condensates in the nucleolus near HD2 proteins, providing a potential spatial context for their functional association.

HD2-type family proteins associate with each other

Given that HD2C and HD2B form both homo- and hetero-oligomers (39), and that HD2A physically interacts with HD2B and HD2C (37, 38), we next investigated whether HD2D also associates with each of HD2A, HD2B, and HD2C. In agreement with these findings, BFP-HD2A, HD2B-GFP, and HD2C-mCherry showed overlapping localization within the nucleolus, whereas HD2D-GFP colocalized with HD2A-mCherry, HD2B-mCherry, or HD2C-mCherry in pairwise assays (Fig. 5A). Using in vitro pull-down assays, we found that purified GST-HD2A and GST-HD2D each interact with all HD2 family members (Fig. 5, B and C), suggesting that HD2A and HD2D engage in both homotypic and heterotypic interactions. These findings are consistent with the interaction patterns observed for HD2B and HD2C and with the IP-MS results showing that HD2C co-immunoprecipitated with HD2A, HD2B, HD2C, and HD2D (39). Co-immunoprecipitation experiments further confirmed that HD2A and HD2D each self-associate (Fig. 5, D and E) and interact with the other HD2-type proteins (Fig. 5, F and G). Consistently, BiFC assays showed that all pairwise combinations of HD2 proteins interacted in the nucleus (Fig. 5H), and coexpression of mCherry- and GFP-tagged HD2 protein pairs revealed their colocalization in Arabidopsis root tip nuclei (fig. S13, A to F). Collectively, these results indicate that HD2 family proteins extensively interact with one another.

TIC activates salt stress–responsive genes partly via HD2-mediated H3K9 acetylation

Given that HD2 family members interact with one another and have been implicated in salt stress responses, including enhanced salt tolerance in HD2D overexpression lines (36) and increased salt sensitivity in the hd2c loss-of-function mutant (42), we next asked whether the TIC-HD2 interaction contributes to the transcriptional regulation of salt stress–responsive genes known to be regulated by H3K9ac (54, 55). We generated an amiR-HD2A hd2b hd2c hd2d (hd2) quadruple mutant and a tic hd2 quintuple mutant using an artificial miRNA to knock down HD2A expression (fig. S14). In the hd2 and tic hd2 mutants, basal transcript levels of NAC019, RD20, and RD29A were elevated before salt treatment (Fig. 6, A to C). Following salt stress, their expression in the hd2 mutant reached Col-0-like levels but remained higher than in the tic hd2 mutant (Fig. 6, A to C). These results indicate that the hd2 mutation partially restores the reduced expression of NAC019, RD20, and RD29A in tic under salt stress. These results suggest that TIC regulates the tested salt-responsive genes partially through HD2 proteins, although additional HD2-independent mechanisms may also contribute. Consistently, before salt treatment, H3K9ac levels at these genes were increased in the hd2 and tic hd2 relative to Col-0 but remained unchanged in tic (Fig. 6, D to F), suggesting that HD2 proteins normally restrict H3K9ac accumulation at these loci. Upon salt treatment, H3K9ac levels at these genes increased in Col-0 but were attenuated in tic (Fig. 6, D to F), consistent with the impaired transcriptional activation observed in tic (Fig. 6, A to C). Introduction of the hd2 mutation into tic partially restored H3K9ac levels at the tested genes under salt stress (Fig. 6, D to F), paralleling the corresponding changes in gene expression (Fig. 6, A to C). The reduced H3K9ac levels at NAC019, RD20, and RD29A in tic under salt stress were associated with impaired salt-induced dissociation of HD2 proteins from these loci rather than altering their overall nuclear localization (figs. S15 and S16). Collectively, these findings show that TIC regulates salt-responsive genes, at least in part, through HD2-associated modulation of H3K9ac.

DISCUSSION

For proper growth and development, seedlings must precisely and rapidly coordinate cellular activities to echo diverse environmental cues in a timely and spatially controlled manner. TIC, a component of the circadian clock, integrates temporal information and forms salt stress–induced speckles, highlighting a mechanism by which plants orchestrate cellular processes in a time- and compartment-dependent manner to cope with abiotic stress. In this study, we propose a model in which TIC integrates with HD2-mediated chromatin regulation to optimize plant responses to salt stress. Specifically, salt stress induces TIC condensate formation and is accompanied by spatial association with a subset of HD2 proteins, reducing HD2 occupancy at salt stress–responsive loci. This may reduce HD2-mediated H3K9ac removal, leading to increasing H3K9ac levels and promoting the expression of circadian-controlled salt stress–responsive genes, ultimately enhancing plant salt tolerance (Fig. 6G).

According to the IUPred predictions, most regions of TIC are intrinsically disordered (Fig. 1A) (43). Domain deletion analysis showed that TIC (1-422) is also predicted to be intrinsically disordered and is capable of undergo LLPS in vitro and N. benthamiana (Fig. 1), whereas the TIC (423-660) region is required for salt stress–induced TIC condensate formation in Arabidopsis (Fig. 2A). In vitro, elevated NaCl directly alters the physicochemical environment of TIC and may modulate the intermolecular interactions that support TIC (1-422) condensation, as salt concentration can profoundly influence protein phase behavior through both electrostatic and nonionic interactions (56). In vivo, however, salt triggers broader cellular responses, including changes in ion homeostasis and intracellular signaling, as well as changes in protein interactions and potentially posttranslational regulation (57). Collectively, these cellular changes may reshape the physicochemical and molecular environment that governs biomolecular condensate behavior (58). Together, these findings suggest that TIC’s intrinsic phase-separation capacity and salt stress–induced condensate formation are related but distinct properties that depend on different regions of the protein. Condensates of the osmotic-stress sensor DCP5 became prominent after approximately 15 min under 0.15 M NaCl, whereas maximal condensate formation under 0.3 M NaCl was observed much later, at approximately 60 min after treatment; some condensates even spontaneously dispersed during prolonged stress (21). This example illustrates that stress-induced condensate formation can exhibit complex, condition-dependent temporal dynamics rather than a simple linear relationship between salt concentration and condensation rate. Accordingly, the rapid appearance of TIC condensates under the 0.25 M condition should not be interpreted as evidence for comparable condensation kinetics under lower salt concentrations or different ionic conditions (Fig. 2, B and D). The condensate formation of SEU and DCP5 is primarily driven by molecular crowding associated with osmotic stress (20, 21). In contrast, TIC rapidly forms condensates under diverse salt conditions but not under nonionic osmotic treatments, thereby promoting plant survival under salt stress (Figs. 2 and 3 and figs. S5 to S8). Notably, the tic mutant has been reported to exhibit enhanced drought tolerance (7), whereas no obvious survival defect was observed under mannitol- or sorbitol-induced osmotic stress in our assays (fig. S7). Collectively, these results suggest that TIC has distinct roles in drought, osmotic, and salt stress responses. In addition, the timing of the external light-dark cycle strongly influences plant growth, as the tic mutant shows delayed flowering compared with the wild type under a 16-hour light/8-hour dark cycle, but not under an 8-hour light/16-hour dark cycle (7). When plants are grown under conditions in which their endogenous circadian period is mismatched with the external light-dark cycle, productivity is reduced (59, 60). A mismatch between the endogenous circadian period and the external light-dark cycle reduces productivity and may further compromise adaptation to abiotic stress (59–61). Consistent with this, the tic mutant, which has a shorter circadian period of approximately 21 hours under free-running conditions, shows improved adaption to salt stress under a 21-hour light-dark cycle (9, 49) (fig. S5), under which its endogenous clock is better aligned with the external cycle. The rhythmic expression and time-of-day–dependent salt induction of NAC019 and RD29A may enable plants to optimize their responses to salt stress across the day-night cycle (fig. S8, A, B, D, and E) (62, 63).

Genome-wide studies have shown that diurnal oscillations of H3K9ac are associated with the rhythmic regulation of approximately 20 to 30% of expressed genes (64). Notably, genes with morning-phased H3K9ac peaks and evening-phased troughs are enriched for salt-stress responses, suggesting that H3K9ac links the circadian clock with salt stress regulation (64). Similarly, the rice clock component Oryza sativa pseudo-response regulator 73 recruits HDAC10 to deacetylate H3K9ac at OsHKT2;1, thereby repressing its transcription and positively regulating rice sodium tolerance (65). HD2-type proteins, which interact with one another (Fig. 5 and fig. S13), partitioned into TIC (1-422)-GFP droplets (Fig. 4D) in vitro. Moreover, HD2 proteins interacted with TIC (Fig. 4, A to D, and fig. S11), whereas in Arabidopsis, only limited spatial association between HD2 proteins and TIC condensates was observed under salt stress (fig. S12) (9, 39). This difference may reflect the greater molecular complexity of the nucleolar environment in vivo (66). HD2 proteins predominantly localize to the nucleolus, and their subnuclear distribution can be dynamically regulated in response to environmental signals (33, 35). Thus, the in vitro and in vivo observations are not necessarily inconsistent, but rather indicate that TIC-HD2 association is more readily detected in the simplified reconstituted system than in the cellular context (Fig. 4D and fig. S12). Together, these results support an association between TIC and HD2 proteins under salt stress, while the extent of HD2 enrichment within TIC condensates in vivo remains to be fully resolved. Our data support a model in which salt stress promotes H3K9 acetylation, which is associated with the rapid activation of salt stress–responsive genes (fig. S9). Specifically, increased H3K9ac levels at the tested salt stress–responsive loci appear to relieve transcriptional repression, at least in part through the reduced occupancy of HD2 proteins at these genes (fig. S16). Because the salt-induced reduction in HD2 occupancy is compromised in the tic mutant, TIC likely functions upstream in regulating HD2 association with the tested salt stress–responsive loci. Consistent with this idea, salt-induced TIC condensates show limited spatial association with HD2 proteins (fig. S12), which may contribute to reduced HD2 occupancy at the target loci without broadly altering their nuclear localization (figs. S15 and S16). This spatial redistribution would be expected to reduce HD2-mediated deacetylation, preserve H3K9ac, and favor transcriptional activation under salt stress. The broader significance of this mechanism is supported by prior reports showing that H3K9ac increases under salt stress in both maize and Arabidopsis (25, 26, 67); that H3K9ac levels are elevated in hd2a, hd2c, and hd2b hd2c mutants (38, 41, 68); and that salt represses the expression of multiple HD2 genes (42). Together, these findings suggest that TIC acts as a salt stress–responsive link between circadian regulation and HD2-associated chromatin control to fine-tune plant adaptation to salt stress. The fact that TIC also functions with TSA-sensitive HDAC activity in the circadian regulation of predawn PHYA transcription and far-red light–mediated hypocotyl growth (8) further indicates that TIC integrates multiple deacetylase pathways, rather than acting solely through HD2-type enzymes, to shape gene expression programs in response to environmental cues.

Collectively, our study reveals a regulatory network in which salt-induced TIC condensates link circadian timing to HD2-associated chromatin regulation, highlighting how the circadian clock and membraneless compartments coordinate the spatiotemporal control of histone acetylation to enhance plant adaptation to salt stress.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and growth conditions

For seedling growth, Arabidopsis seeds were surface-sterilized and sown on ½ MS (Murashige-Skoog) medium supplemented with 1% sucrose and 0.7% agar. After stratification at 4°C for 3 days, the plates were transferred to a growth chamber set at 22°C with a 16-hour light/8-hour dark cycle. The tic-2 (CS833656), hd2c (SALK_129799), and hd2d-1 (CS369104) mutant seeds were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC), while the hd2b mutant was generated using the CRISPR-Cas9 system, as described before (39). Similarly, the tic mutant was generated using CRISPR-Cas9, following the method described by Liu et al. (46). Two single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were designed to target TIC using the CRISPR primer design tool (http://skl.scau.edu.cn/) (69). The tic mutant was backcrossed to the Col-0 background to remove the Cas9 transgene and generate an sgRNA- and Cas9-free tic mutant line. Because of the lethality of hd2a hd2b null double mutants (70), an amiR-HD2A line was used to achieve partial suppression of HD2A. Artificial microRNAs targeting HD2A were designed using WMD3 and introduced into the miR319a backbone by overlapping PCR (71). The amiR-HD2A constructs were cloned into a binary vector driven by the CaMV 35S promoter. The amiR-HD2A hd2b hd2c hd2d (hd2) and amiR-HD2A hd2b hd2c hd2d tic (hd2 tic) lines were generated by introducing the amiR-HD2A construct into the hd2b hd2c hd2d and hd2b hd2c hd2d tic backgrounds, respectively, via Agrobacterium tumefaciens–mediated floral dip transformation. The primers used in the plasmid construct are listed in table S1.

Construction of vectors and generation of transgenic plants

The 1-kb upstream promoter of TIC was amplified and cloned into the pCAMBIA-1300 vector using Eco RI and Sac I restriction sites. The genomic sequence of TIC, including domain deletion variants, was subsequently cloned into the vector using Kpn I and Sal I restriction sites. Promoters of all HD2 family genes were cloned into the pCAMBIA-1300 vector using EcoRI and SacI restriction sites. Coding sequences of HD2A and HD2D were fused with GFP, mCherry, MYC, or HA tags and cloned into the vector using BamHI and SalI restriction sites. HD2B-GFP and HD2B-mCherry constructs were generated using KpnI and SalI, whereas HD2C-GFP and HD2C-mCherry were generated using SacI and SalI. Transgenic lines expressing HD2A-GFP, HD2B-GFP, HD2C-GFP, and HD2D-GFP under their native promoters were generated in the tic mutant background and then backcrossed to Col-0 to obtain lines carrying the same transgene insertion sites in both the Col-0 and tic backgrounds.

Assay for condensate formation in Arabidopsis thaliana

Condensate formation assays were performed in A. thaliana seedlings expressing GFP-TIC and domain deletion variant proteins. Seedlings were grown on 1/2 MS medium supplemented with 1% sucrose under long-day conditions (16 hours light/8 hours dark) for 7 days. For salt-induced condensate formation experiments, GFP-TIC/tic seedlings were treated with NaCl (0.15 M) for 30 min, or with NaCl (0.25 M), Na2SO4 (0.15 M), KCl (0.25 M), LiCl (0.25 M), RbCl (0.25 M), NH4NO3 (0.25 M), MgCl2 (0.15 M), CaCl2 (0.15 M), or K2SO4 (0.15 M) for 1 min. For osmotic stress used to assess TIC condensate formation, GFP-TIC/tic seedlings were treated with PEG-8000 (24%), or mannitol (0.3 M) for 30 min, or with sorbitol (0.5 M) and mannitol (0.5 M) for 1 min. For hormone and inhibitor treatments used to assess TIC condensate formation, seedlings were treated with GA (100 μM), TSA (2 μM) for 30 min, or ABA (50 μM) for 5 min. For temperature treatments used to assess TIC condensate formation, GFP-TIC/tic seedlings were incubated at 4°C or 37°C for 30 min. Fluorescence signals were observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope at laser powers of 1 or 2%, respectively. Colocalization among HD2-type proteins and between HD2-type proteins and TIC was assessed by comparing fluorescence intensity profiles along selected lines using ImageJ. For quantitative analysis, Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) was calculated for regions of interest (ROIs) using SPSS. At least three biological replicates were analyzed.

Protein extraction and precipitation assays

Co-immunoprecipitation assays were performed via Agrobacterium-mediated transient expression in 5-week-old N. benthamiana leaves. For the first set of experiments, HD2A-HA or HD2D-HA was coexpressed with HD2A-GFP, HD2B-GFP, HD2C-GFP, or HD2D-GFP. For the second set, TIC-FLAG was coexpressed with HD2A-MYC, HD2B-GFP, HD2C-mCherry, or HD2D-HA. Leaves were ground in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in ice-cold protein extraction buffer containing 50 mM tris-Cl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% (v/v) Nonidet P-40, 1 mM dithiothreitol, 100 μM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (cOmplete, Roche). Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 15,000g for 15 min at 4°C. Protein extracts were incubated with anti-HA magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 88836) or anti-FLAG magnetic beads (Sigma-Aldrich, M8823) for 2 hours to form the immunoprecipitation complexes. After washing the beads three times with a buffer containing [50 mM tris-Cl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% Nonidet P-40 (v/v), and 100 μM PMSF], the immunoprecipitated complexes were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The target proteins were detected by immunoblotting using anti-GFP (Abcam, ab290), anti-HA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 26183), anti-FLAG-HRP (Sigma-Aldrich, A8592), anti-MYC (Abcam, ab32), and anti-mCherry antibodies (Abcam, ab205402,) respectively.

Pull-down experiment

The HD2A and HD2D coding sequence was cloned into the pGEX-4T-1 vector to generate a glutathione S-transferase (GST)–HD2A and GST-HD2D fusion protein under the control of the T7 promoter for bacterial expression. The TIC (1-422)-GFP coding sequence was cloned into the pET28a vector using BamHI and SalI restriction sites to generate a His-tagged fusion protein for bacterial expression. These plasmids were transformed into the Escherichia coli Rosetta (DE3) strain, and protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG at 16°C for 16 hours. Following induction, cells were lysed in a buffer containing 50 mM tris-Cl (pH 8.0), 300 mM NaCl, 100 μM PMSF, and a 1× protease inhibitor cocktail. Next, lysozyme (1 mg/ml; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added, and the mixture was incubated for 30 min at 4°C with rotation. The lysate was then sonicated using a 5-s on/10-s off cycle for 8 min and centrifuged at 13,000g for 1 hour. The resulting supernatant was filtered through 0.2-μm filters and incubated with glutathione agarose (Cytiva, 17-0756-01) or Ni-NTA agarose (Qiagen, 30210) for 2 hours at 4°C with rotation. The beads were washed three times with a buffer comprising 50 mM tris-Cl (pH 7.5), 300 mM NaCl, 100 μM PMSF, and 20 mM imidazole. Last, the purified glutathione beads bound to GST, GST-HD2A, and GST-HD2D fusion proteins were incubated with equal amounts of protein extract from N. benthamiana leaves expressing either HD2A-GFP, HD2B-GFP, HD2C-GFP, or HD2D-GFP. The purified Ni-NTA agarose bound to His-GFP and His-TIC (1-422)-GFP infusion proteins were incubated with equal amounts of protein extracts from N. benthamiana leaves expressing HD2A-HA, HD2B-mCherry, HD2C-mCherry, or HD2D-HA. After three additional washes, the GST- and His-tag proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) staining, and the pulled-down proteins were detected by immunoblotting using the indicated antibody.

In vitro droplet assay

His-TIC (1-422)-GFP, His-GFP, His-mCherry, His-mCherry-HD2A, His-HD2B-mCherry, His-mCherry-HD2C, and His-mCherry-HD2D proteins were induced and purified as described in the pull-down experiment and were eluted with buffer containing 50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 400 mM NaCl, and 500 mM imidazole. The eluate was concentrated and desalted five times with buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5) and 300 mM NaCl] using an Amicon Ultra-0.5 centrifugal filter (Sigma-Aldrich, UFC503024) to remove imidazole. In vitro droplet formation assays were carried out in 384-well plates in 50 mM tris-Cl buffer containing different concentrations of NaCl. Droplets were visualized using a Leica STELLARIS 5 confocal microscope.

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching

FRAP experiments on His-TIC (1-422)-GFP, TIC-GFP, or GFP-TIC condensates were performed using a Lecia STELLARIS 5 confocal microscope equipped with either a 10×/0.40 numerical aperture (NA) dry objective or a 40×/1.25 NA oil immersion objective, together with the Leica FRAP module and a HyD detector. Imaging was performed with a resolution of 1024 by 1024 pixels and a scan speed of 100 or 400 Hz. Bleaching settings were configured as follows: fly mode-off, zoom in-on, change bleach format-off, set background to zero-on, delete bleach images after scan-off, and use laser settings for all ROIs-on. Photobleaching at 488 nm was set to 30 or 100% power, with a prebleach image acquired, followed by photobleaching and subsequent postbleach image capture. The fluorescence recovery rate was calculated as previously described (72).

BiFC assay

For the BiFC assay, HD2A, HD2B, HD2C, and HD2D coding sequences were fused to nYFP or cYFP using Bam HI and Sal I, respectively. TIC and its truncated variant TIC (1-422) were fused to nYFP using the same restriction sites. N. benthamiana leaves were coinfiltrated with Agrobacterium strains harboring the corresponding plasmid combinations, with H2B-mCherry serving as a nuclear marker. Fluorescence signals were captured 72 hours postinfiltration using a confocal microscope equipped with a 40×/1.25 NA oil-immersion objective, at a resolution of 1024 by 1024 pixels and a scan speed of 100 Hz.

Split luciferase complementation assay

The cLUC-TIC and corresponding HD2A-nLUC, HD2B-nLUC, HD2C-nLUC, and HD2D-nLUC vectors were cotransfected into N. benthamiana leaf cells via Agrobacterium-mediated infiltration using a buffer containing 10 mM 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid, 10 mM MgCl2, and 100 μM acetosyringone. After 72 hours of incubation, the infiltrated leaf cells were treated with 200 μM luciferin, and the luciferase signal was detected with a charge-coupled device camera using a 5-min exposure time.

RNA extraction and RT-PCR

Total mRNA was extracted from 10-day-old seedlings, which were stratified for 3 days at 4°C, using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For mRNA expression analysis, 10 μg of total RNA was treated with the TURBO DNA-free Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, AM1907) to remove any contaminating DNA. Then, 1 μg of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using an oligo(dT) primer and ProtoScript II reverse transcriptase (NEB, no. M0368L). Quantitative PCR was performed on a CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad) with SYBR Green Master Mix (Bio-Rad). The transcript levels were normalized to UBQ10 as the internal reference gene.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays for H3K9ac were performed as previously described (73, 74). Briefly, 10-day-old seedlings were ground into a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. Approximately 2 g of tissue powder was resuspended in nuclei isolation buffer [Hepes (10 mM), sucrose (1 M), KCl (5 mM), MgCl2 (5 mM), Trition X-100 (0.6%), PMSF (0.4 mM), and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail] and incubated at 4°C for 20 min. Formaldehyde was then added to a final concentration of 1% to cross-link proteins to DNA, and samples were incubated at room temperature for 20 min with gentle rocking. Cross-linking was quenched by adding glycine to a final concentration of 120 mM and incubating at 4°C for 15 min. The homogenate was filtered through Miracloth, and nuclei were pelleted and resuspended in 0.3 ml of nuclear lysis buffer and 1.7 ml of ChIP dilution buffer [Trition X-100 (1.1%), EDTA (1.2 mM), tris-HCl (16.7 mM, pH 8.0), NaCl (167 mM), and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail]. Chromatin was sheared by sonication. After centrifugation at 12,000g for 10 min, the supernatant was incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-H3K9ac antibody (Abcam, ab32129) or anti-H3 antibody (Abcam, ab1791) conjugated beads or GFP-Trap Magnetic Agarose (ChromoTek, catalog no. GTMA) with rotation. DNA-protein complexes were eluted using elution buffer (0.1 M NaHCO3 and 1% SDS), and cross-links were reversed by incubation at 65°C for 6 hours. Samples were then treated with RNase and proteinase K, and DNA was purified for subsequent qPCR analysis.

Seedlings survival rate analysis and root elongation measurements

Seedlings were grown on ½ MS agar plates supplemented with 1% sucrose, with or without NaCl (0.15 M), KCl (0.125 M), sorbitol (0.25 M), or mannitol (0.3 M), under long-day conditions (16 hours light/8 hours dark) for 16 days. For Na2SO4 treatment (75 mM), seedlings were grown for 10 days under the same conditions. Survival rates were then recorded at the indicated time points. To examine the effect of different photoperiods for survival rate under NaCl (0.15 M) stress, seedlings were grown under 10.5 hours light/10.5 hours dark, 12 hours light/12 hours dark, or constant light (12 hours light/12 hours light), and survival rates were recorded at 10, 12, 14, and 16 days after germination under NaCl (0.15 M) treatment. All seedlings were grown at 22°C. At least three independent biological replicates were used for statistical analysis. For primary root length measurements, seeds were grown on ½ MS agar plates supplemented with 1% sucrose in the presence or absence of 0.1 M NaCl for 10 days. Primary root length was measured using ImageJ software.

Live imaging of ROS in root tips

Seven-day-old seedlings were treated with or without 0.15 M NaCl for 60 min and then stained with 50 μM 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA; Invitrogen, catalog no. D399) for 20 min. After four washes with distilled water, root tips were imaged using a 20×/0.70 NA oil-immersion objective on a Leica STELLARIS 5 laser-scanning confocal microscope. H2DCFDA fluorescence was excited with a 488-nm laser at 0.01% power, and emission was collected between 500 and 550 nm using a HyD detector. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software and statistically analyzed using SPSS software.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates. Data are presented as the mean ± SE. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 17.0. Differences between two groups were evaluated using Student’s t test, whereas multiple group comparisons were performed by one-way ANOVA followed by Fisher’s LSD post hoc test, as appropriate. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. All experiments yielded consistent results across independent replicates.
