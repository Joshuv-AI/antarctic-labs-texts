A mathematical framework for pre-mass detection of renal tissue anomalies from computed tomography scans: exploration of a biomechanical signature based on enhancement kinetics
Authors: Amina El Ghazi, Abdessadek Aaroud, Abdelmajid Bessate
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly detection of renal cancer remains challenging, as current imaging techniques predominantly identify tumors only after a visible mass has formed. Lesions smaller than 1 cm are particularly difficult to detect reliably using routine computed tomography (CT). This study presents a novel mathematical framework for detecting early renal tissue anomalies before visible mass formation, grounded in the hypothesis that tumorigenesis induces subtle biomechanical alterations in tissue enhancement patterns that are observable on CT imaging.MethodsWe introduced the Local Biomechanical Index (LBI), a voxel-wise metric quantifying local deviations in Hounsfield unit values relative to an intra-patient renal reference, and a volumetric enrichment ratio (Rt/g) measuring the spatial concentration of extreme LBI values. The single-phase framework was evaluated on a random sample of 20 cases from the KiTS19 dataset using fixed parameters: a 3-mm local radius, the 99th percentile threshold, a 10 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm three-dimensional (3D) window, and an enrichment threshold of 2.0. Voxel-level recall, precision, F1-score, and Rt/g were calculated against the reference tumor masks and summarized using means, standard deviations (SDs), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsAcross the 20 cases, the mean recall was 0.575 (SD, 0.231; 95% CI: 0.474–0.676), the mean precision was 0.449 (SD, 0.292; 95% CI: 0.321–0.577), and the mean F1-score was 0.431 (SD, 0.221; 95% CI: 0.334–0.528). The mean Rt/g was 5.223 (SD, 5.427; 95% CI: 2.842–7.604). Recall exceeded 0.50 in 11 of 20 cases (55%), while Rt/g exceeded 2.0 in 14 cases (70%), indicating that candidate anomalies were frequently concentrated within tumor-associated regions. Because KiTS19 provides tumor annotations but does not annotate all other renal abnormalities, the reported precision represents agreement with the reference tumor masks rather than definitive precision for detecting all renal anomalies.ConclusionsThese findings provide preliminary evidence that early tumor-related biomechanical alterations may be detectable through spatial analysis of enhancement deviations in CT images. The proposed framework operates without tumor segmentation, relies on interpretable statistical measures, and can be fully automated. Although further validation on large longitudinal datasets is required, this approach opens a promising research direction for segmentation-free early detection of renal cancer and for integrating biomechanical signatures into radiological screening workflows.Keywords: Early cancer screening, kidney, computed tomography (CT), biomechanical signature, segmentation-free index

Introduction

Clinical context and diagnostic problem

Kidney cancer represents a major public health burden, with high incidence and significant mortality when diagnosed at advanced stages (1). Although computed tomography (CT) is the primary diagnostic tool for kidney cancer, it is effective only once a visible mass has already formed and does not reliably identify pre-mass tissue transformations (2). In clinical practice, the diagnostic grey zone for lesions smaller than 1 cm means that most renal masses are diagnosed only when they have reached 2 cm or larger (3).

Limitations of existing approaches and methodological gap

Artificial intelligence (AI)-based technologies have recently shown progress in the detection and automatic segmentation of visible tumor masses, including through evaluation on reference datasets such as KiTS19 (4).

However, most of these methods rely on supervised learning using expert-curated datasets in which tumor boundaries are manually and precisely delineated as ground-truth annotations. Although these annotations are not required as inputs during inference, the models learn predominantly from clearly identified and carefully segmented lesions. Consequently, their performance may decline substantially when applied to heterogeneous, unsegmented clinical CT examinations, particularly when lesions are subtle, poorly defined, or differ from those represented in the training data (4).

State of the art

Cancer is a major public health issue as its incidence is rising and too frequently has a poor prognosis when diagnosed late. CT is the first-line imaging modality for diagnosis but has poor utility in screening since reliable detection of very small (typically <1 cm) lesions is fundamentally compromised by the technology’s severe limitations in its capability to detect such lesions. Because of this technology-related limitation on reliable diagnosis of kidney cancer at very small sizes, the clinical implications are immediate. Ultrasound, in the current clinical setting, is unable to detect sub-centimeter lesions, and CT technology has inherent limitations in terms of sensitivity as well as poor reliability due to artifacts and misinterpretation of noise. In this context, there are clear and reliable diagnoses for lesions <5 mm and those >10 mm using both CT and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), but the 5–10 mm range is a gray area because MRI, while much better at detection, is rarely used for screening. In this regard, it can be said that lesions less than 5 mm and greater than 10 mm are clear for diagnosis; however, those ranging between 5 and 10 mm constitute a gray zone, as the MRI, despite being more sensitive, is not often used in the screening stage. Given this reality, it’s no shock that by the time most renal masses are diagnosed in clinical practice they’re already 2 cm or larger; and prognosis is most favorable for lesions much smaller (i.e., localized) (3).

In clinical practice, radiological analysis relies primarily on two-dimensional (2D) images from CT or MRI, with each slice corresponding to an isolated anatomical section. The radiologist must therefore manually examine a large volume of data, potentially reaching 500 to 1,000 slices for a complete thoraco-abdomino-pelvic CT scan, and mentally reconstruct the three-dimensional (3D) structure of the kidney from these successive slices. This procedure generates a substantial cognitive load, induces visual fatigue, and may contribute to inter-observer variability, particularly for microlesions whose interpretation remains challenging (5).

The development of more precise detection methods, easy to integrate into routine medical practice and suitable for preliminary examinations, is therefore essential to improve early diagnosis. Hence, the clinical need for sensitive and early detection tools which could be easily incorporated into a busy radiological workflow still persists.

This raises the possibility of an entirely new approach: what if the first observable signature were not morphological, but biomechanical? Oncophysics has discovered that the cancer transformation of a tissue is driven by early micro-architectural changes—hypercellularity, increase in extra-cellular matrix, and solid stress—which generate local compaction and change the mechanical response of a tissue many orders-of-magnitude before we can detect an invading cancer mass (6). The hypothesis is that this compaction leaves a subtle but detectable imprint during routine CT scanning, manifesting as a regional and statistically significant deviation from expected Hounsfield unit (HU) density.

These biomechanical changes, including increased cellularity and solid stress, have been shown to precede morphological evidence and can be detected by techniques such as MRI elastography, which can quantify mechanical differences between renal tumors and renal parenchyma (7).

CT provides indirect information on electron density, which is correlated with mass density and tissue composition (8), by measuring X-ray attenuation in tissue. We hypothesize that early mass effects on the density of tissue associated with tumor transformation might thus leave a subtle but detectable mark in local HUs when a distinct mass entity cannot be clearly seen.

Here, we present a mathematical theory of Local Biomechanical Index (LBI) for “segmentation-free” screening, which formalizes this conjecture in this paper.

The biomechanical hypothesis: the birth of a paradigm?

This remark invites consideration of a much broader paradigm: what if the first observable signature was not morphological, but biomechanical? Work in oncophysics has demonstrated that early alterations of the tissue microenvironment (hypercellularity, compaction and mechanical stress modifications) precedes morphologically detectable transitions during tumorigenesis (6,7). The first of these is CT, which allocates tissue electron density [essentially a composite measure that correlates with mass density and chemical composition (8)] based on X-ray attenuation data. Our hypothesis in this study is that those tissue compaction events associated with early stages of tumorigenesis would then be able to stamp into locally resolved HUs but without the requirement for a morphologically defined mass as well.

Proposition: a mathematical formula on the biomechanical spatial concentration

Our contribution addresses this methodological gap. We propose to quantify, at every parenchymal point, the degree of deviation from biomechanical normality. The specificity is based on characterizing the local spatial spread of this deviation, at the voxel level, by an LBI, but especially through an analysis of the spatial concentration of extreme signatures. We aim to distinguish the focal cluster of tissue instability (end-product of an emerging lesion) from random physiological background, by studying the spatial location of these deviations in the renal parenchyma. Importantly, this approach is segmentation-free, as it requires neither a trained model nor prior learning of the target lesion.

Methods

Dataset and preprocessing

Experimental validation was performed on cases from the public KiTS19 database (4). Cases were selected for their representativeness of real clinical challenges, including notably small tumors (case 00120) and complex anatomical presentations (case 00000). For each case, the 3D CT volume and its associated reference segmentation (background: 0; healthy parenchyma: 1; tumor: 2) were used. No additional preprocessing (registration, global intensity normalization) was applied, to preserve native radiomic information and test method robustness under realistic conditions. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Link to clinical practice: from biomechanical signal to lesion characterization

Translating this biomechanical signature into a clinically relevant tool requires establishing a clear bridge between the quantitative measurement of enhancement (HU) and the radiologist’s diagnostic reasoning. The added value of our approach lies precisely in this correspondence: the voxel-wise analysis of HU, formalized by the LBI index, makes it possible to capture the same pathophysiological characteristics that underpin the clinical interpretation of renal lesions. Indeed, as highlighted by numerous studies in renal imaging, diagnosis relies on the evaluation of specific enhancement profiles in multiphasic CT (9,10). For example, the absence of significant enhancement (HU <10) is the marker of simple cystic lesions (Bosniak I) (11), while rapid and intense enhancement in the corticomedullary phase (high wash-in rate) associated with rapid wash-out constitutes the typical signature of hypervascular clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) (10,12). Conversely, weak and progressive enhancement points to a hypovascular tumor such as papillary carcinoma (9,13). Our LBI, by quantifying the deviation from normal local enhancement, operationalizes this clinical reading grid. A voxel or region with a high LBI does not signal a mere abstract “anomaly”; it objectifies an area whose enhancement kinetics resemble, for example, those of a hypervascular tumor. Thus, the conceptual framework we propose does not simply detect a statistical deviation; it provides a probabilistic map whose interpretation can be directly correlated with established diagnostic categories (cystic, hypervascular solid, hypovascular solid, etc.), thereby addressing a fundamental clinical need: moving from simple detection to assistance in early lesion characterization. By demonstrating that the spatial concentration of extreme LBI values corresponds to foci whose enhancement behavior is pathognomonic, we lay the groundwork for a tool that could, in the long term, not only alert to the presence of a pre-mass anomaly but also suggest its nature, bridging the gap between tissue physics and medical reasoning.

Implementation

All the algorithms were run in Python (version 3.8) using common scientific libraries (NumPy and SciPy). 3D neighborhood and windowing operations were implemented in a single convolution form. The calculations were performed on a single workstation. LBI maps and candidate masks are stored in NIFTI files for subsequent visualization, analysis and processing using external software (3D Slicer, ITK-SNAP).

Multicase validation on 20 KiTS19 cases

To determine whether the observations obtained from the initial illustrative cases could be reproduced across a broader sample, the complete single-phase experimental pipeline was applied to a random sample of 20 cases from the KiTS19 dataset. The same retained parameter configuration was used for all cases: a local radius of r=3mm, the 99th percentile threshold (Q99), a 10 mm × 10 mm × 10 mm 3D window, and an enrichment threshold of Rmin=2.0.

For each case, the candidate mask generated without using the tumor annotation was compared with the reference tumor mask. Voxel-level recall, precision, F1-score, and the tumor-to-global enrichment ratio Rt/g were calculated. The tumor annotation was used exclusively for performance evaluation and was not involved in candidate generation. Aggregate results are presented as the mean, standard deviation, and 95% confidence interval (CI) across the 20 cases.

The mean number of false-positive candidate voxels, defined as candidate voxels located outside the reference tumor mask, was 3,052.7 per case (95% CI: 1,489.8–4,615.6). This is a voxel-level measure and should not be interpreted as the number of false-positive lesions or false-positive patients.

Overall, the multicase evaluation confirms that the spatial enrichment signal was not restricted to the initially selected favorable examples. The mean Rt/g substantially exceeded 1, and 70% of the cases presented an enrichment ratio greater than 2, indicating that candidate anomalies were frequently concentrated within tumor-associated regions rather than being uniformly distributed throughout the kidney. Nevertheless, the moderate mean precision and F1-score, together with the variability observed across cases, demonstrate a substantial false-positive burden in the present single-phase implementation. It should also be noted that KiTS19 provides annotations for renal tumors but not for all other renal abnormalities; therefore, candidate regions outside the tumor mask are systematically counted as false positives, even though some may correspond to real but unannotated abnormalities, and the reported precision should consequently be interpreted as tumor-mask precision rather than as the definitive precision of the method for detecting all renal anomalies.

These results therefore support the quantitative feasibility of the spatial enrichment principle, but they do not constitute definitive diagnostic or clinical validation.

Discussion

Scientific interpretation of preliminary results

The results of these exploratory experiments are consistent with the working hypothesis: a local deviation in enhancement (HU), interpreted as a potential biomechanical signature, appears to concentrate nonrandomly in the tumor regions of the analyzed cases. On the slice studied in EXP02, all extreme voxels are located in the tumor, and in 3D (EXP04), the enrichment ratio Rt/g is markedly greater than 1 (3.64). These observations suggest that the concept of spatial concentration of biomechanical anomalies could be relevant for distinguishing a pathological focus from physiological background noise.

Independence from tumor segmentation: the approach circumvents a major limitation of supervised methods by not requiring annotation of the target lesion. It is designed to detect potential anomalies, including subtle ones.

Synthesis and clarification of the study scope

We have extended the validation to a random sample of 20 cases from KiTS19 [mean recall =0.575 (0.474–0.676), mean R(t/g) =5.22 (2.84–7.60)], performed a sensitivity analysis of the parameters, clarified the fundamental distinction between LBI (a mathematical deviation index without direct diagnostic value) and ΔHU (a precise physiological selector of vascular anomalies), and strengthened the discussion on robustness against protocol variations. We wish to reaffirm that the present study constitutes a proof of concept aimed at validating the quantitative core of the proposed framework using the single-phase KiTS19 database. This deliberate choice, which corresponds to the case where p=0 (i.e., the absence of a native pre-injection phase), does not undermine the generality of the mathematical formulas presented. All equations—including ΔI(p), μΔ,local(p), μΔ,global(p), LBI, ρW, and Rt/g—are defined for an arbitrary phase p and thus remain fully valid. The results obtained on KiTS19, while encouraging, do not represent final conclusions. They pave the way for the next phase of the project, which will consist of applying the complete framework including pre- and post-contrast acquisitions (the case where p corresponds to a post-injection phase with real ΔHU). This future validation on the multiphasic UCSF RMaC database will allow evaluation of the diagnostic potential of ΔHU as a precise selector, with the goal of reducing vascular false positives and improving detection performance. This subsequent work will be the subject of a distinct article.

Limitations and research perspectives

This proof-of-concept study, although encouraging, has limitations that open the way for numerous future works.

Current methodological limitations

Empirical parameterization: the parameters (r, percentile, Rmin) are set heuristically. Systematic optimization on a training set is necessary.

Restricted validation: the results are limited to a few cases from KiTS19. Extensive validation on the entire database, and then on independent multicenter cohorts, is imperative to confirm the robustness and generalizability of the approach.

Conclusion and potential implications

This study lays the theoretical and computational foundations for a new conceptual approach for early detection of kidney cancer. By proposing to map the deviation from “normal” biomechanics via enhancement kinetics in multiphasic CT scans, it offers a possible avenue to address the pre-mass detection challenge. Its relative algorithmic simplicity and interpretability make it potentially adaptable for future research.

Our proof-of-concept work suggests that the biomechanical signature is indeed present and measurable in the analyzed tumors. The next steps, including parameter optimization, spatial filtering, and validation on large cohorts, aim to transform this conceptual framework into a more mature research tool, capable of being evaluated for potential assistance in early diagnosis.

Conclusions

This study presents a conceptual exploration for the early recognition of kidney cancer by CT scan. By breaking away from the morphological segmentation paradigm, we proposed a mathematical framework based on the local biomechanical signature of tissue, via the LBI and the analysis of spatial concentration of anomalies by the Rt/g ratio.

The application of this framework to selected cases from the KiTS19 challenge provided an encouraging proof-of-concept: the studied tumor regions exhibit an enrichment in biomechanical anomalies and their spatial concentration correlates with the presence of the mass. The method is independent of tumor segmentation, interpretable, and based on robust intra-patient normalization.

This work is a first step. It establishes the foundations for a new research avenue for pre-mass detection. Short-term perspectives include parameter optimization, integration of spatial clustering techniques to reduce false positives, and, above all, extensive validation on large cohorts, ideally longitudinal, to assess the true early detection potential of the approach. In the longer term, after rigorous validation, this type of conceptual framework could be considered as a warning module in the radiological workflow, complementing existing tools.
