The impact of pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading on peri-operative outcomes: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials
Authors: Ka Ting Ng, Tee Hang Chia, E. Shan Heng, Mohd Fitry Bin Zainal Abidin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsPre-operative oral carbohydrate loading is widely adopted in enhanced recovery after surgery protocols, yet its clinical benefits remain uncertain. This review aimed to determine the effect of pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading on the incidence of post-operative nausea and vomiting (PONV) and other peri-operative outcomes in surgical patients.Methods:MEDLINE, EMBASE, and CENTRAL databases were systematically searched from database inception to 31 August 2025. Randomised controlled trials examining the effects of pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading with conventional fasting or placebo in adult patients were included.Results:Ninety-eight trials involving 9,824 patients were included. The main pooled analysis showed lower incidence of PONV with oral carbohydrate loading prior to surgery [risk ratio (RR) 0.74, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.58–0.94, P = 0.02, trial sequential analysis = inconclusive]. Significant reductions were also found in the carbohydrate group for pre-operative hunger [mean difference (MD) -1.56, 95% CI -2.57 to -0.55, P = 0.003], thirst (MD -1.61, 95% CI -2.35 to -0.88, P < 0.001), post-operative glucose level (MD -3.45, 95% CI -6.64 to -0.25, P = 0.03), insulin resistance (MD -1.21, 95% CI -1.70 to -0.72, P < 0.001), post-operative pain (MD -0.43, 95% CI -0.71 to -0.15, P = 0.003), and length of stay (MD -0.40, 95% CI -0.59 to -0.21, P < 0.001).Conclusion:Pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading may reduce PONV. However, this benefit was not confirmed in the sensitivity analysis restricted to low-risk bias studies. It also reduces pre-operative hunger and thirst, post-operative pain, and the length of hospital stay, although the certainty of evidence for these outcomes is low. The absolute effect sizes for post-operative pain and length of stay were small and of uncertain clinical importance. These findings should be interpreted with caution due to substantial heterogeneity, publication bias, and lower certainty of evidence.

INTRODUCTION

Post-operative nausea and vomiting (PONV) remains one of the most common anaesthetic complications. It is associated with patient discomfort, delayed recovery, and prolonged hospital stay.[1] Surgical patients have traditionally been instructed to undergo overnight fasting to minimise the risk of pulmonary aspiration during anaesthesia, but overnight fasting also worsens thirst, hunger, and peri-operative metabolic stress. Surgical trauma also provokes a neuroendocrine stress response characterised by peripheral insulin resistance, transient hyperglycaemia, and post-operative catabolism.[2]

Pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading has been proposed to improve patient comfort pre-operatively and reduce the risk of PONV without increasing the risk of pulmonary aspiration. The idea of pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading up to 2 h before anaesthesia was also incorporated into several enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways for selected elective surgical patients.[345] However, recent studies have reported conflicting findings.[67] Beyond PONV, the effects of pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading on peri-operative comfort, metabolic, safety, and recovery outcomes also remain incompletely defined. Although several systematic reviews on this topic have been published, most have restricted to specific surgical populations, pooled a comparatively small number of trials, or have not incorporated trial sequential analysis and formal Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) certainty assessment. Therefore, an updated systematic review and meta-analysis such as the present meta-analysis that synthesised all relevant trials published up to August 2025, standardised outcome definitions, and assessed the certainty and robustness of the cumulative evidence was necessary.

The current review evaluated adult surgical patients who received pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading compared with placebo or conventional fasting. We hypothesised that pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading reduces the incidence of PONV in adult patients undergoing surgery. The primary objective of this review was to evaluate the impact of pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading in the reduction of PONV. The secondary objectives were to examine the impact of pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading on pre-operative hunger and thirst scores, post-operative pain scores, post-operative serum glucose level, insulin resistance, residual gastric volume, and length of hospital stay.

METHODS

The Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions was used in conducting this review.[8] Prior to the literature search, the review protocol was published in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) on 3 May 2025 (CRD420251036146). A population-intervention- comparison-outcomes (PICO) approach was used to generate the research questions [Supplementary Table 1].

CENTRAL (1947 - 31 August 2025)

Inclusion criteria included: (a) RCTs examining the effects of oral carbohydrate loading versus conventional fasting or placebo before surgery and (b) trials involving adult patients (≥18 years old).

Exclusion criteria were: (a) observational studies, case series, case reports, and trials published as abstracts; (b) trials examining intravenous carbohydrate loading and a combination of oral carbohydrate with other confounders (chewing gum, whey protein, antioxidant); and (c) trials with healthy volunteers.

The primary outcome was the incidence of PONV. Secondary outcomes included length of hospital stay, residual gastric volume, pre-operative hunger, pre-operative thirst, post-operative pain, glucose level, and insulin resistance. Subgroup analysis was also conducted for the carbohydrate group, comparing it with the conventional fasting group and the placebo group for the incidence of PONV.

Two authors (TC, EH) screened the title and abstract of each study independently. Discussion was made with the third author (KN) to resolve any discrepancy. Both authors (TC, EH) independently screened the full texts, and again, any conflict was resolved by the third author (KN). The final studies were included based on consensus among all three authors. A standardised data extraction form was used to extract data from all included studies. Both authors (TC, EH) independently used the Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment tool to assess the risk of bias for included RCTs.[9] Disagreements were resolved via a discussion with the third author (KN). In the case of missing data, the corresponding author of the individual trial was contacted in an attempt to retrieve the data.

Review Manager version 5.4 was used for statistical analyses.[10] A two-tail P value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. Findings were reported as Mantel–Haenszel risk ratio (RR) and mean difference (MD) for binary and continuous outcomes, respectively. For patient-reported outcomes measured using different numerical scales including hunger, thirst, and pain scores, data were harmonised by converting eligible scores to a common 0–10 scale before pooling. Mean and standard deviations were also transformed using the same conversion factor. Due to variation of study population, types of surgery, and dosage of carbohydrate load, a random-effects model was used to pool estimates. Heterogeneity was assessed using I2 statistic, with values of <40%, 40–60%, and >60% categorised as low, moderate, and substantial, respectively. Sensitivity analysis was conducted for the primary outcome based on low risk of bias studies to ensure robustness of the findings. A funnel plot was also created to assess potential publication bias for the primary outcome.

The GRADE assessment of evidence and summary of findings were independently performed by two authors (TC, EH) using GRADEpro/GDT software.[11] The certainty of evidence was assessed based on five criteria (risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias). Any discrepancy was resolved by the third author (KN). Trial sequential analysis was performed for the primary outcome to assess the statistical reliability of the cumulative evidence and the risk of random errors due to sparse data and repeated significance testing in the meta-analysis using the trial sequential analysis viewer version 0.9.5.5 Beta (Copenhagen Trial Unit, 2017).[12] The required information size was calculated with 5% risk of type 1 error, a power of 80%, and model variance-based heterogeneity correction.

Twenty-one RCTs (n = 1709) measured PONV outcome, and pooled analysis demonstrated that pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading significantly reduced the incidence of PONV (RR 0.74 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.58–0.94, P = 0.02, I² = 58 %, level of evidence = low) [Figure 2]. The funnel plot for PONV shows asymmetry, suggesting small-study effects or publication bias [Supplementary Figure 2].

In the subgroup analysis of overnight fasting and oral placebo, our pooled data showed similar significant reduction in PONV in oral placebo groups (11 studies, n = 795 patients, RR 0.62 95% CI 0.42–0.92, P = 0.02, I² = 61%) [Figure 3]. However, in the overnight fasting group, although still favouring pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading in reducing PONV, the result was no longer statistically significant (13 studies, n = 1018 patients, RR 0.79 95% CI 0.59–1.07, P = 0.13, I² = 52%) [Figure 3]. Sensitivity analysis of RCTs with low risk of bias did not demonstrate a significant effect of carbohydrate loading on PONV (7 studies, n = 490 patients, RR = 1.06 95% CI 0.84–1.33, P = 0.63, I2 = 8%) [Supplementary Figure 3].

Twenty RCTs (n = 1802) reported pre-operative hunger score. Statistically significant reduction was found in the carbohydrate group from the pooled data analysis (MD -1.56 95% CI -2.57 to -0.55, P = 0.003, I² = 100%, level of evidence = low) [Supplementary Figure 4]. Nineteen RCTs (n = 1642) examined pre-operative thirst score. Data revealed a statistically significant reduction in the carbohydrate group compared to the control group (MD -1.61 95% CI -2.35 to -0.88, P < 0.001, I² = 99%, level of evidence = low) [Supplementary Figure 5]. Nineteen RCTs (n = 1756) measured post-operative pain score. Pooled data analysis revealed statistically significant difference between the carbohydrate versus control groups (MD -0.43 95% CI -0.71 to -0.15, P = 0.003, I² = 92%, level of evidence = low) [Supplementary Figure 6].

Forty-six RCTs (n = 4787) examined the length of hospital stay after surgery. The combined data revealed statistically significant reduction of hospital stay in the carbohydrate group (MD -0.40 95% CI -0.59 to -0.21, P < 0.001, I² = 85%, level of evidence = low) [Supplementary Figure 7]. Twelve RCTs (n = 1528) examined residual gastric volume. Pooled data analysis found no significant difference in the residual gastric volume between the carbohydrate versus control groups (MD 1.02 95% CI -1.37 to 3.41, P = 0.40, I² = 84%, level of evidence = very low) [Supplementary Figure 8].

Thirty RCTs (n = 2267) examined post-operative serum glucose level, which was statistically significant between the carbohydrate and control groups (MD -3.45 95% CI -6.64 to -0.25, P = 0.03, I² = 78%, level of evidence = moderate) [Supplementary Figure 9]. Fifteen RCTs (n = 1026) reported insulin resistance using the homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) equation. The carbohydrate group was found to have significantly reduced insulin resistance compared to the control group (MD -1.21 95% CI -1.70 to -0.72, P < 0.001, I² = 96%, level of evidence = moderate) [Supplementary Figure 10].

DISCUSSION

The current meta-analysis demonstrated that pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading was associated with a lower incidence of PONV in the main pooled analysis. In subgroup analysis, the reduction in PONV remained statistically significant in the placebo group but was not statistically significant in analysis comparing carbohydrate loading with conventional fasting. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis produced a neutral pooled estimate and did not support a significant reduction in PONV with pre-operative carbohydrate loading. In terms of secondary outcomes, carbohydrate loading reduced pre-operative thirst and hunger, as well as post-operative pain, insulin resistance, and serum glucose levels. However, no significant difference was detected in the residual gastric volume. The level of evidence ranged from very low to moderate, due to potential risk of bias, indirectness, imprecision, inconsistency, and dose–response relationship.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the most comprehensive review summarising the clinical evidence on the effect of pre-operative carbohydrate loading on the incidence of PONV in patients undergoing elective surgery. The main strengths of this review include an exhaustive literature search, and all included RCTs underwent rigorous appraisal of methodology and evidence quality using the GRADE approach. Compared with previous reviews by Ricci et al.,[111] Cheng et al.,[112] and Noba et al.,[113] our analysis included a larger body of evidence with additional trials published up to 2025. Earlier syntheses reported inconsistent findings, which may be attributable to significant degree of heterogeneity in outcome definitions and measurement approaches, including the use of non-uniform endpoints such as nausea alone, vomiting alone, or composite outcomes assessed at different time points.[1726307385111112] By applying a standardised definition of PONV and standardising outcome reporting to a single 0–24 h incidence per study, we aimed to reduce variability and minimise duplication of outcome data across time points to improve comparability across studies.

For the purpose of this review, PONV is defined as any nausea, retching, or vomiting within 24 h of anaesthesia. It is multifactorial and may involve serotonin-mediated vagal signalling. Volatile agents and peri-operative opioids are established risk factors for PONV and may activate central emetic pathways involving the area postrema and nucleus of the tractus solitarius.[114] Surgical handling of the bowel can further trigger neurogenic and inflammatory pathways within the intestinal wall, contributing to impaired gastrointestinal motility leading to post-operative ileus.[115116] Pre-operative carbohydrate loading is believed to attenuate the metabolic stress response, which can dampen inflammatory signalling in the gut wall. In addition, patients who are less metabolically stressed typically require lower peri-operative opioid doses, removing another well-known trigger for emesis.[2] However, given the non-significant sensitivity analysis, the conventional fasting subgroup, and the inconclusive trial sequential analysis due to the accrued sample size representing only 83.7% of the required information size, the extent to which carbohydrate independently reduces PONV remains uncertain. These inconsistent findings may be partly explained by the smaller number of studies included in the sensitivity analysis and by clinical and methodological heterogeneity across trials, including differences in surgical populations, types of surgery, anaesthetic choice, opioid exposure, carbohydrate regimen, and antiemetic prophylaxis.[334447557791110] In some studies, PONV may have been driven predominantly by factors such as spinal morphine, peri-operative opioids, volatile anaesthesia, or procedure-related inflammatory responses, which may not be directly modified by carbohydrate loading.[334455] Whether any observed effect is mediated by carbohydrate content itself, reduced fasting duration, altered gastric emptying, reduced opioid exposure, or attenuation of surgical stress response could not be established from the current evidence.

The very high statistical heterogeneity observed for several pooled outcomes, namely, incidence of hunger, thirst, and insulin resistance (I² >90%), further reduces confidence in the summary estimates and most likely reflects genuine clinical heterogeneity in surgical population, different procedures, carbohydrate formulation, concentration, volume and timing of administration, anaesthetic and analgesic technique, and antiemetic prophylaxis, rather than chance alone. As many of the included trials were embedded within enhanced recovery pathways, the observed effects may represent the combined influence of shortened fasting, peri-operative fluid intake, and other co-interventions rather than the carbohydrate load in isolation, and the independent contribution of carbohydrate cannot be disentangled from the present data. The pooled estimates should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating and interpreted at the level of the overall intervention strategy rather than as precise, transferable effect sizes.

The effects of pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading on lowering pre-operative hunger and thirst scores are consistent with earlier reviews.[89112113] Notably, those syntheses detected no difference in thirst when pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading was compared with plain or flavoured water. Taken together, the evidence indicates that any fluid is sufficient to relieve thirst, whereas the presence of carbohydrate is required to reduce the sensation of hunger. For post-operative pain, our result differed from a previous review of 22 trials,[116] which found no evidence of oral carbohydrate loading in reducing post-operative pain. A RCT by Singh et al.[50] reported lower pain scores in patients who received pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading, which could possibly be due to reduction in surgical stress response as well as improvement in general well-being. However, this result should be interpreted with caution as the overall quality of evidence is very low. Although pain scores were harmonised and converted to a standardised 10-point scale where possible, the included trials used different rating scales including visual analogue score, numeric rating, and verbal rating scales, assessed at non-uniform time points, and involved procedures with very different baseline pain burdens and analgesic protocols, contributing to substantial degree of heterogeneity. Most studies were small and only partially blinded, so the observed benefit could reflect expectation or placebo effects rather than a true analgesic action. Moreover, the pooled reduction in pain score was small (an MD of less than half a point on a 10-point scale) and is unlikely to be clinically important in isolation, particularly given the low certainty of evidence. Our review also demonstrated that patients who received pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading were associated with a shorter length of hospital stay. While this result was similar to those of earlier studies, some confounding factors including patient’s demographic profile, comorbidities, and social situation could affect the total duration of hospitalisation.[111112117] The absolute reduction in length of stay was also modest (approximately 0.45 day); whether a difference of this magnitude is clinically meaningful for individual patients or health services is uncertain and should not be over-interpreted on the basis of statistical significance alone.

Several studies concluded no significant difference in residual gastric volume between the carbohydrate and control groups.[113118119] This finding was consistent with our review and suggested that it is safe to provide pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading 2 h before surgery. In our study, patients who received pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading had lower post-operative glucose levels and insulin resistance, which was in line with findings from previous reviews.[111] Surgical trauma induces a neuroendocrine stress response characterised by a surge of catecholamine release, hepatic glycogenolysis, and insulin resistance,[2] and pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading is thought to attenuate this response by maintaining patients in a metabolically “fed” state. It replenishes hepatic glycogen stores, promotes a modest increase in circulating insulin, and thereby reduces catecholamine-driven gluconeogenesis that typically follows prolonged fasting. As a result, peri-operative blood glucose levels may remain closer to euglycaemia, potentially blunting the insulin resistance response.[214] These metabolic effects provide a plausible explanation for the observed improvements in clinical outcomes. However, given the variability in evidence certainty across outcomes, this relationship should be interpreted with caution.

One major limitation of this meta-analysis was the marked heterogeneity in pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading regimens which may obscure any dose–response relationship in the pooled estimates. The asymmetry seen in the funnel plot for the primary outcome [Supplementary Figure 2] further raises the possibility of publication bias or small-study effects, which may have exaggerated the apparent benefit of pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading on PONV. It reinforces the need for cautious interpretation of this outcome. Potential confounding factors could not be fully adjusted for, including type of surgery, peri-operative care, anaesthetic regimens, patient baseline characteristics, and adjunctive use of different types of anti-emetics or opioids. Future trials should explore the use of pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading in diabetic patients with regular peri-operative glycaemic reading monitoring. As the present review was designed to compare carbohydrate loading versus placebo or conventional fasting, it could not determine the optimal carbohydrate regimen. Future trials and network meta-analyses comparing different formulations, concentrations, doses, and timing of pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading are needed to determine the most effective regimen. We were also unable to undertake several potentially informative subgroup analyses such as major versus minor surgery, abdominal versus non-abdominal surgery, enhanced recovery versus conventional pathways, and different pre-operative oral carbohydrate formulations due to inconsistent reporting across the included trials. These comparisons should be prioritised in future studies to help explain the observed heterogeneity and to identify the patient populations most likely to benefit. Long-term outcomes beyond discharge such as quality of life, return to normal activity, and readmission rates could be explored, and formal cost-effectiveness analysis could be performed to support health policy decisions.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, pre-operative oral carbohydrate loading may reduce the incidence of PONV and improve pre-operative hunger and thirst, post-operative pain, and the length of hospital stay. However, given the low level of evidence, high degree of heterogeneity, non-significant sensitivity analysis, potential publication bias, and inconclusive trial sequential analysis, the effect on PONV should be interpreted cautiously and was not confirmed when the analysis was restricted to studies at low risk of bias. Several statistically significant secondary outcomes, including post-operative pain and length of stay, showed small absolute effects of uncertain clinical importance. Further adequately powered studies are warranted to standardise the dose and concentration of oral carbohydrate on homogeneous surgical patients.
