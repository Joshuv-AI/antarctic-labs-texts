Full shuffle and p-rectified semi-inner powerful IoU-based chili pepper flower recognition with environment-aware YOLO11
Authors: Cui-Ping Zhang, Zhi-Yong Wang, Xue-Wei Wang, Zhi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As a common greenhouse-grown commercial crop, chili pepper yields have always been a focus of attention. Effective management is essential for both environmental sustainability and high productivity in greenhouses. Whether for growth monitoring, yield estimates, and automated production, precise identification of chili flowers is critical. To this goal, this paper works upon the YOLO11 object detection model, focusing on the precise detection of chili flowers. The diversity of samples across various scenarios is increased by utilizing a self-built dataset of chili flowers in greenhouses and integrating data augmentation. To address YOLO11’s limitations in this task, this paper proposes a unified framework that integrates three modules: (1) full shuffle to enhance information exchange between model channels and optimize weights; (2) Since YOLO11 cannot incorporate external environmental data during training, the environment-aware C3k2 is introduced; and (3) The proposed p-rectified Semi-inner Powerful IoU accelerates model convergence and flexibility. Experiments show that our method obtains 84.8% mAP50, 50.2% mAP50-95, 81.1% Precision, and 75.6% Recall, outperforming the baseline YOLO11’s 77.3% by 7.5 percentage points, as well as YOLOv8, YOLO12, RTDETR, and Faster R-CNN under the same experimental conditions. Therefore, in terms of performance, our method generally surpasses existing algorithms for chili flower detection. Research on detecting chili flowers in greenhouses remains limited. The study offers valuable insights for the advancement of smart agriculture. Future work will further explore the model’s generalization capabilities across multiple varieties and growth stages, as well as deploy it on embedded devices for practical application.

Introduction

As one of the major commercial crops, chili peppers are highly compatible with greenhouse environments’ temperature, light, water, and air control capabilities, with their biological characteristics of being heatloving, moderately light-demanding, having shallow root systems, and being highly sensitive to water and nutrients. As a result, greenhouse cultivation of chili peppers represents a high-value agricultural production system worldwide. To boost yields, in addition to meticulous management of growth factors, artificial pollination must be well-executed, especially in enclosed environments like greenhouses with low air circulation and few insects, as this may dramatically improve fruit set rates. However, due to the small and dense nature of chili flowers, traditional manual pollination is almost impossible in large-scale greenhouses. Conventional methods are labor-intensive and time-consuming, with pollination outcomes entirely dependent on the farmer’s experience—a practice unsustainable in high-density facility agriculture.

Smart agriculture is using disruptive technologies to reconstruct crop management paradigms as conventional agriculture transforms into digitalization. Smart agriculture is a new mode of agricultural production that integrates contemporary information technology to intelligentize the entire chain, including management, operation, processing, storage, transportation, sales, and service. As an important component of agricultural intelligence, monitoring crop growth processes significantly impacts yield prediction and field management. The foundation of automated pollination lies in equipping machines with the ability to perceive and locate their target—the flower. This study builds a chili flower detection algorithm to support agricultural production management in greenhouses.

The calyx diameter of chili flowers is typically 5–8 mm, which is white or light yellow and has low contrast against a green background. This small-scale characteristic is prone to missed detection in routine inspections. Additionally, water droplets easily form in greenhouse environments with high humidity, distorting petal textures. Due to high-density planting, approximately 35% of the flowers are only partially visible, making it challenging for standard algorithms to locate targets reliably. Light reflections on the petal surface form highlight noise, severely interfering with color feature extraction.

The rapid development of AI has brought a glimmer of hope, with deep learning-based image detection shining brightest. There are many types of methods, including ViT, Swin Transformer, and YOLO (You Only Look Once) (Redmon et al., 2016), which is the most representative. YOLO’s single-stage architecture balances the accuracy and speed of recognition, employing feature pyramids and multi-scale prediction mechanisms to improve its capacity to withstand interference in complex agricultural scenarios.

A review of the existing literature reveals that current research primarily focuses on 1) crop detection, including fruits, seeds, branches, leaves, and diseases; The methods employed vary widely, ranging from traditional machine learning approaches such as SVM and Bayesian methods to deep learning algorithms like R-CNN and YOLO. However, flower detection is relatively uncommon. 2) Among the limited research on flower detection, most studies focus on flower detection in open-field crops, while research on chili flower detection in greenhouse environments is relatively rare.

Based on the above analysis, the task of detecting chili flowers in complex greenhouse environments essentially involves examining the combined effects of multiple factors, including high-density plant, small target size (5-8mm), low contrast with leaves and the surrounding environment, approximately 35% partial occlusion, and lighting interference. This study enhances YOLO’s adaptability to chili flower detection circumstances, increases accuracy, and provides data assistance for precision pollination, water and fertilizer management, and other agricultural activities. Related work

Agricultural vision

Flower recognition in greenhouses has also evolved alongside advancements in machine vision. Early traditional image processing methods combined color, shape, and texture information with machine learning, such as SVM (support vector machines) (Sri and Sridhar, 2025) and Bayesian (Dembla et al., 2025; Arellano et al., 2025) classifiers, to perform basic recognition in stable environments. However, these approaches were susceptible to illumination and occlusion, resulting in a weak generalization ability. Deep learning, such as Faster R-CNN (Rahim and Mineno, 2020) and SSD (Tian et al., 2019), shows significantly superior performance in flower detection for crops like tomatoes (Li and Zhu, 2024), cucumbers (Tang et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2023) and strawberries (Zhou et al., 2023) compared to classic machine learning. However, existing models still have room for improvement in both accuracy and real-time performance under conditions of light fluctuation, dense plant growth, complex backgrounds, and severe occlusions.

Crop detection utilizing computer vision technologies in greenhouses has been a long-term development. Traditional machine vision involves locating regions of interest (ROIs) in input images, extracting feature vectors, predicting object categories within ROIs via region classification and boundary regression, and outputting detection results. SVM (Support vector machines) (Sri and Sridhar, 2025), Bayesian classifiers Gobi and Sharma, 2023), and K-means clustering (Jiang et al., 2023) are the most often used approaches. However, in the complex context of high-density greenhouse cultivation, these methods suffer from low detection accuracy and high computational demands, making real-time detection challenging. Deep learning-based object detection methods are characterized by high detection speeds and accuracy, primarily falling into two categories: region proposal-based methods and regression-based methods. Region proposal-based methods, such as R-CNN, detect in two stages: the first selects candidate regions, and the second employs deep learning methods for classification and regression. In contrast, the regression-based methods, like YOLO, are end-to-end, enabling faster processing speeds suitable for real-time applications. However, it suffers from lower detection accuracy and suboptimal performance.

Small-object detection

In computer vision, small-object detection has always been difficult because of the limited number of target pixels, the absence of feature information, and the downsampling-induced loss of features. Researchers have made significant efforts to address this challenge. We noticed that existing small-object detection methods mainly employ six key technologies to enhance model performance, including multiscale feature fusion, attention mechanism improvement, self-attention mechanisms (Transformers), and data augmentation & sample balancing. Multi-scale feature fusion, exemplified by Feature Pyramid Networks (FPNs) (Lin et al., 2017), is capable of preserving both spatial details and semantic information. The FPN–ISResNet–Faster RCNN model was presented by Hou et al. (2023) for the detection, evaluation, and validation of apple leaf diseases. Experimental results show that this model exhibits high accuracy and generalization capabilities in detecting apple diseases. However, in situations where real-time is crucial, this method necessitates careful evaluation due to its much higher computing needs. Attention mechanisms (Vaswani et al., 2017) and transformers both essentially fall into the category of feature enhancements, as they can strengthen feature representations in small-object regions and suppress irrelevant background noise. Representative attention mechanisms include CBAM (Woo et al., 2018), ECA (Wang et al., 2020), and SA (Zhang and Yang, 2021). Representative transformer architectures include DETR (Carion et al., 2020), VIT (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020), and their variations (Zhu et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2021; Srivastava et al., 2025; Siddiqui et al., 2025). Cai et al. (2025) combined attention with C2f DCNv2 in YOLOv8 to adapt to complex traffic scenes, and the novel EMAM was designed to enhance the recognition and representation of small, occluded objects. Experimental results on the KITTI and COCO-traffic datasets showed average accuracy of 97% and 69%, respectively. Miri Rekavandi et al. (2025) gathered over 60 studies from 2020 to 2023 on the application of transformers in small-object detection, along with 12 large-scale datasets spanning various scenarios such as medical, aerial images, and video, to serve as a reference for future research. While attention and transformers offer certain advantages in terms of accuracy, their computational complexity is significantly higher than that of CNNs. Data augmentation and sample balancing are data-wise strategies to mitigate the scarcity of samples for small objects and class imbalance by artificial expansion. These methods include real-time and offline strategies. Real-time strategies include Mosaic, MixUp (Zhang et al., 2023b), and Random Affine, while offline strategies include affine transformations, oversampling, and generative methods (GANs). This kind of method is the simplest to implement but is typically used for preprocessing.

YOLO

YOLOs drive breakthroughs in real-time detection performance via architectural innovation. YOLOv1 (Redmon et al., 2016), proposed by Redmon et al. in 2016, has undergone continuous structural and performance optimization through multiple revisions over nearly a decade, with widespread application across diverse fields. It transforms object detection into a regression challenge, employing neural networks to predict both the category and bounding box coordinates of objects within images. YOLOv2 (Redmon and Farhadi, 2017) builds upon the optimized YOLOv1 by introducing anchor and multi-scale training to enhance small object detection capabilities. YOLOv3 (Redmon and Farhadi, 2018) significantly improves the accuracy by integrating the feature pyramid network (FPN) to better adapt to changes in object sizes. YOLOv5 (Jocher, 2020), a milestone in YOLOs, utilizes CSPDarknet53 as its backbone and integrates Mosaic and Mish, making it the most widely applied object detection algorithm. YOLOv8 (Jocher et al., 2023) replaced v5 due to its significantly improved inference speed, particularly in fine-grained detection within complex scenes. The evolution of YOLO continues unabated. The YOLO12, centered on attention, demonstrates its strong vitality. However, its ecosystem is still underdeveloped, which severely limits application. This study is based on YOLO11 (Khanam and Hussain, 2024), mainly for the following reasons: Firstly, YOLO11 has been available longer than v12, resulting in a more mature ecosystem, which provides convenience for applied research. Secondly, YOLO11 is more advanced than YOLOv1-v10, achieving a relatively balance between accuracy and speed. Finally, most of the current applications are based on the earlier version of YOLO, which provides a broader prospect for our research.

Table 1 shows a brief history of the YOLO series. A fundamental dilemma is evident in the evolution of YOLO: simplicity means real-time performance, yet complexity leads to high accuracy. Almost every version releases multiple variants (n/s/m/l/x) based on the same architecture to meet the diverse deployment requirements of different scenarios. The differences among these variants primarily lie in their complexity (i.e., depth, width, and input). Generally speaking, the nano and tiny are better suited for agricultural vision tasks due to their small parameter count, low power consumption, and high real-time performance. Bakirci (2025) evaluated the suitability of tiny-, nano-, and small- models for real-time object detection on resource-constrained platforms, further confirming that although the accuracy is lower than that of other variants, this shortcoming can be mitigated through targeted improvements. Therefore, YOLO11n was selected as the baseline.

Notwithstanding these advances, current research shows that the majority of agricultural detection methods still rely on earlier frameworks and fail to fully leverage the latest backbone network structures. Meanwhile, current techniques struggle to tackle the challenges specific to greenhouse environments, such as intricate backgrounds, dense planting, and small target sizes, and are limited in using environmental semantic priors to enhance feature extraction. To address these deficiencies, we provide a unified framework based on YOLO11 that incorporates an environment-aware C3k2 for embedding semantic priors, a full shuffle to enhance information exchange and mitigate overfitting, and an optimized IoU variant for efficient localization.

Environment-aware C3k2

YOLO11 detection for chili flowers is significantly hampered in greenhouses by factors including small flower targets, low color contrast, uneven lighting, and severe occlusion. Incorporating environmental data and even time parameters into YOLO11 is important to improving the extraction and reducing the effect of unnecessary redundant data on identification accuracy. C3k2 is one of the key blocks in YOLO11. Through replacing the 1 × 1 conv layer in the C2 module with two consecutive 3 × 3 conv layers, it effectively expands the receptive field with significantly increasing computational complexity. However, when applied to chili flower detection in greenhouse scenarios, C3k2 exhibits two issues:

Chili flowers exhibit diversity in size and shape due to different shooting distances and angles. However, C3k2 employs a uniform standard to extract information, resulting in the loss of objects that occupy a small portion of the image during the process.

Throughout the development of object detection, Intersection over Union (IoU) has consistently served as the primary metric for evaluating the alignment between predicted and ground-truth bounding boxes. It is defined by Equation 9.

where Bg and Bg are the predicted and ground-truth bounding boxes, respectively. Since IoU was initially applied to object detection scenarios, it has undergone many improvements: GIoU (Rezatofighi et al., 2019), DIoU (Zheng et al., 2020), CIoU, SIoU (Gevorgyan, 2022), WIoU (Tong et al., 2023), alpha IoU (He et al., 2021), Focal S/G/D/E/CIoU (Zhang et al., 2019), and Inner-IoU (Zhang et al., 2023a), Focaler-IoU (Zhang and Zhang, 2024), Powerful IoU (Liu et al., 2024), MPDIoU (Siliang and Yong, 2023). These improvements have evolved from a single overlap metric into complex loss functions that take into consideration various geometric relationships—such as position and shape—thereby enhancing both accuracy and convergence speed. However, the variety of the real world is not well addressed by the current IoU, especially in complex greenhouses. By focusing on the interior of bounding boxes, Inner-IoU reduces interference from external backgrounds, thereby improving detection speed. This characteristic is particularly important for detecting chili flowers in greenhouses. However, Inner-IoU does not account for factors such as shape, position, and relative size. Minimizing PIoU guides the predicted box to move toward the GT box along an approximate straight-line path. PIoU incorporates object size as an adaptive penalty factor p, effectively mitigating slow convergence caused by unnecessary expansion of predicted boxes during training. Building upon the design principles of Inner-IoU and Powerful IoU, Inner Powerful IoU is proposed. Its concept involves calculating inner bounding boxes within both predicted and ground truth (GT) boxes, with IoU computation entirely based on inner boxes. This approach accelerates model convergence, reduces external interference, and accounts for factors like shape and position. However, computing two inner boxes increases the computational complexity of IoU. In this study, we propose Semi-inner Powerful IoU (see Figure 4), which accelerates model convergence and enhances chili flower recognition performance by more flexible ratio settings.

On the contrary, when r↘, then CxI↗, CyI↗, so (|WG−WP|CxI+|HG−HP|CyI)↘. Therefore, the loss rises with increasing r and falls with decreasing r.

Model training

This study builds upon YOLO11n with enhancements. Loading pre-trained YOLO11n weights from the MS COCO dataset is a reasonable option for accelerating convergence, as it leverages prior knowledge in extracting general features. Input images are sized at 640 × 640. To mitigate initial oscillation, learning_rate = 0.001 is employed alongside a warm-up strategy. The warm-up is set to 5 epochs, with cosine annealing applied to gradually decay the learning rate, ensuring smooth convergence toward the optimum. Training is conducted for 1000 epochs. Within the limits of GPU memory, batch size is set to 4 to stabilize gradient descent. At the same time, the Adam optimizer is selected. To prevent overfitting, given the small batch size (4), we adopt an early stopping strategy with a patience of 50 epochs based on the validation loss. We also employ 10% of the dataset as a validation set and conduct repeated experiments to ensure result stability.

Conclusion and discussion

Greenhouses represent a classic setting in modern agriculture, serving as a main battleground for smart agriculture due to their controllable environments. However, the complexity of conditions, as well as high crop density, pose persistent challenges for efficient, precise object detection. Chili peppers, a popular greenhouse cash crop, have long been considered inaccessible for yield enhancement through artificial pollination, primarily due to their tiny, densely clustered flowers. Automatic pollination requires improved detection accuracy. Building on YOLO11, this paper presents three strategies: Full shuffle overcomes the drawback of channel shuffle, where information exchange is incomplete when shuffling based on the entire channel position. By dividing feature maps into regions, full shuffle achieves complete information exchange while minimizing complexity, significantly improving model performance. The text-described external conditions enter the processing workflow via TinyBert encoding, enriching the feature maps and enhancing robustness against environment changes. Semantic priors prevent overfitting to spurious correlations through spatial regularization in full shuffle and refined localization in improved powerful IoU, thereby achieving precise spatial coordinate mapping. Unlike the CIoU, which includes computationally intensive aspect ratio, our variant significantly reduces computational burden while preserving the bounding box localization accuracy advantages of powerful IoU, enhancing flexibility—a critical consideration in resource-limited agricultural applications.

We acknowledge the following limitations regarding the dataset and the scope of the experiment: All images were taken in a single show hall in Shouguang, Shandong. Environmental factors (lighting, humidity) may differ from those in other greenhouses or open fields, potentially influencing model generality. This dataset primarily consists of fully bloomed flowers. Early buds or senescent flowers have not been labeled. Performance on those stages remains untested. This dataset covers only one kind of chili pepper. Flowers from different types may differ in color, size, and petal arrangement. Our model has not yet been verified on other varieties. While the dataset adequately illustrates the effectiveness of the proposed method under controlled conditions, its scope is limited. Therefore, results should be interpreted with caution when considering deployment of this method in different greenhouse environments, varieties, or imaging conditions. In addition, the dataset and data-splitting strategy might provide a risk of data leaks.

In future work, we plan to improve our method’s generalizability by collecting data from different locations, varieties, and seasons. Furthermore, we will systematically test our method on edge platforms to fully evaluate the model’s feasibility for deployment in commercial greenhouse environments.
