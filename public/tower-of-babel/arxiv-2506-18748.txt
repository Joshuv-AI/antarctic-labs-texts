Fast State-Augmented Learning for Wireless Resource Allocation with Dual Variable Regression
Yiğit Berkay Uslu, Navid NaderiAlizadeh, Mark Eisen, Alejandro Ribeiro

Abstract
We consider resource allocation problems in multi-user wireless networks, where the goal is to optimize a network-wide utility function subject to constraints on the ergodic average performance of users. We demonstrate how a state-augmented graph neural network (GNN) parametrization for the resource allocation policy circumvents the drawbacks of the ubiquitous dual subgradient methods by representing the network configurations (or states) as graphs and viewing dual variables as dynamic inputs to the model, viewed as graph signals supported over the graphs. Lagrangian maximizing state-augmented policies are learned during the offline training phase, and the dual variables evolve through gradient updates while executing the learned state-augmented policies during the inference phase. Our main contributions are to illustrate how near-optimal initialization of dual multipliers for faster inference can be accomplished with dual variable regression, leveraging a secondary GNN parametrization, and how maximization of the Lagrangian over the multipliers sampled from the dual descent dynamics substantially improves the training of state-augmented models. We demonstrate the superior performance of the proposed algorithm with extensive numerical experiments in a case study of transmit power control. Finally, we prove a convergence result and an exponential probability bound on the excursions of the dual function (iterate) optimality gaps.

A common method for solving such problems is to parametrize the resource allocation policies and move to the Lagrangian dual domain. Assuming the duality gap of the parametrized problem is sufficiently small, the Lagrangian can be maximized over the primal variables and minimized over the dual variables, with each dual variable corresponding to a constraint in the original resource allocation problem. This alternating maximization and minimization scheme leads to a
dual gradient descent (DGD) algorithm
that
does not necessarily converge to a feasible, deterministic policy
but is proven to generate trajectories of policy iterates (solutions) that are
sampled from a stochastic policy that is near-optimal and almost-surely feasible
. As such,
the DGD algorithm lacks a stopping criterion with feasibility guarantees in finite iterations. Moreover, the need for online computation of Lagrangian maximizing primal variables for every dual iterate is an important limitation
[
20

state-augmented
algorithms has been proposed to address the aforementioned challenges of DGD
[
20
,
21
,
15
]
. In the context of wireless networks, a resource allocation policy with state-augmented parametrization augments the input network states with corresponding dual variables and is trained to maximize an expected Lagrangian over a distribution of training network states and dual variables. Iterative dual variable updates during the online execution of the learned policy follow a(n) (stochastic) descent direction for the parametrized Lagrangian. Consequently, the state-augmented algorithm emerges as a practical algorithm to approximately sample from a feasible and near-optimal policy akin to the aforementioned DGD algorithm for any given network state (configuration).

In this paper, we propose a novel
state-augmented primal-dual learning algorithm with dual variable regression
for resource allocation problems in wireless networks. Our method differs from prior implementations of state augmentation
[
21
,
20
]
in two aspects. First, we introduce a dual-regression module that initializes the dual multipliers close to their optimal levels instead of initializing them at zero during online execution. The dual variable regression training leverages the trained state-augmented policy to optimize a regression loss. Here, the regression targets are the near-optimal dual multipliers that are estimated by rolling out state-augmented dynamics that descend the state-augmented Lagrangian over the training dataset of network configurations. The regression inputs are the corresponding individual network configurations and additional easy-to-evaluate regression features extracted for each network configuration. Second, we introduce a subroutine to the state-augmented training algorithm that samples dual multipliers from dual descent dynamics rolled out for each given training network configuration. By matching the dual descent trajectories, we resolve the need for a judicious choice of dual multiplier sampling distributions and train state-augmented models with superior performance.

Aside from the algorithmic novelty, the secondary contribution of this work is the derivation of i) a convergence result stating that the dual function iterates obtained by the stochastic DGD algorithm visit a small neighborhood of a dual optimum value regularly and ii) an excursion result stating that large deviations between two consecutive visits are highly unlikely. We leverage ergodic complementary slackness to characterize the suboptimality of an arbitrary dual multiplier initialization. Combining these results, we provide theoretical insights into the dual descent dynamics and a justification for the proposed dual multiplier initialization method.

We demonstrate the proposed state-augmented algorithm with dual variable regression in a power control setup, resembling that of
[
21
]
, where the objective is to maximize the network-wide sum rate, subject to per-user minimum-rate requirements. We utilize graph neural network (GNN) architectures for both resource allocation policy and dual variable regression (initialization) functions, referred to as primal-GNN and dual-GNN, respectively. Both architectures view the dual variables and resource allocation vectors as graph node signals and process a graph representation of the wireless network state (configuration), given by the set of channel gains. In particular, the primal-GNN is trained to output the transmit power levels at each time step by processing the network state and dual variables as the input graph edge weights and node features, respectively. The dual-GNN, on the other hand, is trained to output a dual multiplier vector by processing the same network configuration and certain regression features.

We verify the fast learning of the dual dynamics and the optimal policies under the proposed approach by comparing against the state-augmented algorithm without optimal initialization and several other baselines. We demonstrate how a state-augmented algorithm exhibits a policy switching (randomization) behavior to sample a stochastic, near-optimal and feasible policy where none of the sampled (deterministic) policies can be guaranteed to be strictly feasible individually. We highlight the ability of the GNN-parametrized state-augmented resource allocation policy and the dual-regression policies both to transfer to unseen network configurations.

II
Wireless Resource Allocation Problem

We consider a general problem of allocating network resources (e.g., frequency bands, transmit powers, scheduling decisions, etc.) to maximize a network-wide utility function (e.g., sum-rate) subject to utility constraints (e.g., minimum-rate requirements) defined for each network agent.

II-A

Network Setup and Optimization Problem

Consider a wireless network comprised of
N
agents and operating over a series of time steps
t\in\{0,1,2,\dots,T-1\}
, where at each time step
t
, we denote by
{\mathbf{H}}_{t}\in{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
the
instantaneous network state
, comprising the set of time-varying channel gains across the network. The underlying randomness and variation in the network state can be attributed to shifts in network dynamics over time and the stochastic geometry of the network. Accordingly, we decompose the network state at time step
t
into two components:

(i)

A large-scale (slow-varying) component
{\mathbf{H}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
, also called the
network configuration
, determined primarily by the large-scale fading effects and the network geometry (e.g., physical locations of network agents), both of which are assumed to be constant throughout the
T
time steps.

(ii)

A small-scale (fast-varying) component
{\mathbf{H}}^{s}_{t}\in{\mathcal{H}}
that varies over time due to fast-varying fading effects.

To allocate the resources, we assume access to only the large-scale network configuration measurements. For a given
{\mathbf{H}}
, we define the resource allocation function (policy)
{\mathbf{p}}:{\mathcal{H}}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, and
{\mathbf{p}}({\mathbf{H}})
denotes the vector of resource allocation decisions. The resource allocation decisions subsequently lead to the network-wide ergodic performance vector
{\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{p}}({\mathbf{H}})\big)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, with
{\mathbf{r}}:{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
denoting the performance function. The ergodic performance vector is defined through the instantaneous performance vector,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{p}}({\mathbf{H}})\big)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}_{t}|{\mathbf{H}}}\left[{\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}}_{t},{\mathbf{p}}({\mathbf{H}})\big)\right]

(1)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}}_{t},{\mathbf{p}}({\mathbf{H}})\big),\quad{\mathbf{H}}_{t}={\mathbf{H}}\odot{\mathbf{H}}^{s}_{t},

where, with a slight abuse of notation, we use
{\mathbf{r}}
to represent both ergodic and instantaneous performance functions.

Given a concave network-wide objective utility
f_{0}:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, and a concave constraint utility
{\mathbf{f}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and any
{\mathbf{H}}
, we write an unparametrized resource allocation problem as

\displaystyle P^{*}({\mathbf{H}})=

\displaystyle\maximum_{{\mathbf{p}}({\mathbf{H}})}

\displaystyle f_{0}\left({\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{p}}({\mathbf{H}})\big)\right),

(2a)

subject to

\displaystyle{\mathbf{f}}\left({\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{p}}({\mathbf{H}})\big)\right)\geq{\mathbf{0}}.

(2b)

In (
2
), we wrote
{\mathbf{f}}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{N})^{\top}
to denote the vector-valued function whose
i
th entry
f_{i}
corresponds to the constraint utility of
i
th agent. Note that the objective and the constraints are derived based on the
ergodic average
network performance vector, rather than the instantaneous performance vector [c.f. (
1
)]. The goal of the resource allocation problem is, therefore, to derive the optimal vector of resource allocation decisions
{\mathbf{p}}^{*}({\mathbf{H}})
that maximizes the objective utility
f_{0}
while satisfying the constraint utility
{\mathbf{f}}
for any given
{\mathbf{H}}
.

II-B

Parametrized Dual Problem

Solving (
2
) entails an infinite-dimensional search over the set of all resource allocation functions
{\mathbf{p}}
for any given
{\mathbf{H}}
. This is practically infeasible, even in the case of a single network configuration. Therefore, we consider a family (distribution) of network configurations
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{H}}}
and restrict the search space to
parametrized
resource allocation functions
{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta})
indexed by a finite-dimensional set of parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, e.g., a neural network with learnable weight and bias parameters.

We cast the parametrized resource allocation problem as

\displaystyle P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}=

\displaystyle\maximum_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}}~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{H}}}}\left[f_{0}\Big({\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta})\big)\Big)\right],

(3a)

subject to

\displaystyle{\mathbf{f}}\Big({\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta})\big)\Big)\geq{\mathbf{0}},\quad\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{H}}}\text{-a.e.}

(3b)

where, different from (
2
), the maximization is now performed over the set of parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}
and the utility constraints are to hold for almost all network configurations
{\mathbf{H}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{H}}}
. The problem defined in (
3
) is not convex in
\boldsymbol{\theta}
in general, and obtaining approximate maximizers with feasibility guarantees through gradient-ascent-based methods is challenging. We work around this challenge by moving to the Lagrangian dual domain. Introducing the nonnegative dual multiplier functionals
\boldsymbol{\lambda}:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{N}_{+}
, we define the parametrized Lagrangian functional for the problem in (
3
) as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{H}}}}\bigg[{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\lambda}({\mathbf{H}});{\mathbf{H}}\big)\bigg],

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}({\mathbf{H}})=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}
is the vector of nonnegative dual multipliers corresponding to the constraints for each given
{\mathbf{H}}
in (
3b
), and
{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\lambda}({\mathbf{H}});{\mathbf{H}})
is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\lambda}({\mathbf{H}});{\mathbf{H}})

\displaystyle\coloneqq f_{0}\Big({\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta})\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\boldsymbol{\lambda}({\mathbf{H}})\cdot{\mathbf{f}}\Big({\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta})\big)\Big).

(5)

We note that since the constraints must hold uniformly across all
{\mathbf{H}}
and the objective is a summation over all
{\mathbf{H}}
, our problem exhibits a separable structure. This leads to a decomposition of the Lagrangian function in (
4
) such that we can equivalently solve the subproblems indexed by each individual network configuration
{\mathbf{H}}
independently. For this reason, and to facilitate a simplified notation, we break down our original problem into subproblems indexed for each
{\mathbf{H}}
and simply write
{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
in place of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\lambda}({\mathbf{H}});{\mathbf{H}}\big)
.

We define a Lagrangian maximizer as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}}{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\lambda}),

(6)

and write the parametrized dual function as

\displaystyle g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\coloneqq{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),\boldsymbol{\lambda}\big)=\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}}{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\lambda}).

(7)

The dual function
g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
upper bounds
P^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
for any
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\succcurlyeq{\mathbf{0}}
, and the parametrized dual problem seeks the tightest upper bound by minimizing the dual function, i.e.,

\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}\coloneqq g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})=\min_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}).

(8)

In (
8
),
D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is the optimal dual value and is attained by an optimal dual multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
.
We note that both the Lagrangian maximizers
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
and the optimal dual multipliers
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
are a set in general. In particular,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
will refer to either the set of all optimal dual multipliers or an arbitrary element of this set, with due clarification whenever the distinction is necessary.

The dual problem is appealing because finding the Lagrangian maximizers entails an unconstrained optimization problem. Moreover, the dual function is convex in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
although the Lagrangian in (
4
) or the dual problem in (
8
) are generally non-convex in
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. This follows from the dual function being the pointwise infimum over a family of affine functions
[
22
, p. 81]
. Furthermore, a subgradient of the dual function at a given
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, denoted by
{\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\in\partial g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
, can be evaluated by virtue of Danskin’s theorem
[
23
]
as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\coloneqq{\mathbf{f}}\Big({\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\big)\Big),

(9)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}
denotes a Lagrangian maximizer as defined in (
6
). Since the minimization in (
8
) is a convex problem and (
9
) is a descent direction for the dual function, a solution to the dual problem can be obtained by a dual descent method that alternates between the inner primal maximization step and a dual subgradient descent step. Although the inner maximization problem is non-convex, it is unconstrained—deep neural networks trained with stochastic gradient descent algorithms have shown huge empirical success in tackling such non-convex optimization problems by finding good minima.

Remark 1
.

The duality gap of the parametrized problem
D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-P^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is nonzero in general, and we incur a loss of optimality by working in the dual domain. However, the underlying unparametrized, functional problem, e.g., (
2
), can be shown to exhibit zero duality gap under certain mild conditions, and solving the parametrized problem over a sufficiently rich function class leads to a small duality gap
[
24
]
.

II-C

Dual Gradient Descent (DGD) Algorithm

Given an initial dual multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}
, an iteration index
k=0,1,\ldots
, and a step size
\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, a dual (sub)gradient descent algorithm is described by the iterative scheme given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}}{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}),

(10a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\Big[\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}-\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})\Big]_{+},

(10b)

where
[\cdot]_{+}\coloneqq\max(\cdot,{\mathbf{0}})
is the projection to the nonnegative orthant. The sequence of iterates
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})_{k\geq 0}
generated by (
10
) asymptotically satisfies the constraints in (
3b
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}{\mathbf{f}}\left({\mathbf{r}}\Big({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})\big)\Big)\right)\geq{\mathbf{0}},\;\,a.s.,

(11)

and attains an objective value that is near-optimal, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{K\to\infty}\!\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}f_{0}\left({\mathbf{r}}\Big({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})\big)\Big)\right)\right]\geq D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\mathcal{O}}(\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),

(12)

for any
{\mathbf{H}}
under the assumptions of bounded subgradients and the existence of strictly feasible primal variables. This result has been proven several times in
[
25
,
21
,
20
]
under different setups, hence we omit the proof. For future reference, we denote by
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger})
the dual multiplier trajectories induced by the dual updates in (
10b
).

Important to note is that the sequence of DGD iterates need not converge to a pair of optimal variables
(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})
—optimality and feasibility guarantees hold for the sequential execution of the generated trajectories and not for any individual iterates or their averages [c.f. Fig.
4
]. In fact, the DGD algorithm solves a sequence of deterministic, unconstrained problems to generate a trajectory of primal variables (and induced policies) to solve a convex relaxation of the original problem (see
[
21
, Appendix B]
). An algorithmic implication of this is that the DGD method cannot be stopped at an arbitrary iteration and should be run online to ensure the optimality of trajectories which, although possible, is challenging. The state-augmented primal-dual learning algorithm we introduce in the next section leverages policy randomization over time to sample resource allocation decisions that are near-feasible and near-optimal in the ergodic regime.

III
State-Augmented Resource Allocation with Dual Variable Regression

III-A

State-Augmented Parametrization

First proposed in
[
20
]
, a
state-augmented parametrization
interprets the dual multipliers as part of the input space and is trained to learn Lagrangian maximizing policies for given dual multipliers
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and network configurations
{\mathbf{H}}
jointly. A resource allocation function with a state-augmented parametrization
{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}:{\mathcal{H}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}_{+}\times\boldsymbol{\Phi}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
can be represented as
{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}({\mathbf{H}},\boldsymbol{\lambda};\boldsymbol{\phi})
where
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\boldsymbol{\Phi}
denotes a finite-dimensional set of parameters.

For given
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and
{\mathbf{H}}
, we define the Lagrangian function with a state-augmented parametrization as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{H}})

\displaystyle=f_{0}\Big({\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}({\mathbf{H}},\boldsymbol{\lambda};\boldsymbol{\phi})\big)\Big)

\displaystyle+\boldsymbol{\lambda}\cdot{\mathbf{f}}\Big({\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}({\mathbf{H}},\boldsymbol{\lambda};\boldsymbol{\phi})\big)\Big).

(13)

Considering a joint probability distribution over the dual multipliers and network configurations
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{H}}}
, an
optimal state-augmented parametrization
is defined as a maximizer of the
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{H}}}
-expectation of the Lagrangian defined in (
13
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}

\displaystyle\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\boldsymbol{\Phi}}\;\mathbb{E}_{(\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{H}})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{H}}}}\big[{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{\phi};\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{H}})\big].

(14)

An optimal
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}
that solves (
14
) approximates the resource allocation decisions made by the DGD iterates
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
given in (
10a
), i.e., we have
{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k},{\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})\approx{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})\big)
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*};\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})\approx{\mathcal{L}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})\big)
, provided that the optimization is performed over a sufficiently expressive state-augmented parameter class
\boldsymbol{\Phi}
, and that the training distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{H}}}

adequately covers the range of the dual multiplier dynamics of (
10b
). The former condition is formalized through use of near-universal parametrizations while for the latter, we propose an effective sampling scheme in Section
III-B
.

Definition 1

(
\nu
-universal parametrizations
[
21
, Def. 1]
)
.

A state-augmented parametrization
{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}({\mathbf{H}},\boldsymbol{\lambda};\boldsymbol{\phi})
for
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\boldsymbol{\Phi}
is near-universal with degree
\nu>0
, or equivalently,
\nu
-universal, for functions
{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}))
if there exists
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\boldsymbol{\Phi}
such that

\displaystyle\hskip-5.0pt\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger})}\|{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}({\mathbf{H}},\boldsymbol{\lambda};\boldsymbol{\phi})-{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big({\mathbf{H}},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\big)\|\leq\nu,\;\,{\mathcal{H}}\text{-a.e.}

(15)

State-augmented learning eliminates the need for online computation and memorization of optimal primal and dual variable pairs
\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})\}
by introducing a single parametrization
\boldsymbol{\phi}
. The expected Lagrangian maximization step in (
14
) is performed jointly for all possible networks and dual multipliers drawn from
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},{\mathbf{H}}}
during offline training whereas the
dual dynamics are run only during online execution
. For a given
{\mathbf{H}}
and a dual multiplier initialization
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}
(e.g.,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}\equiv{\mathbf{0}}
), we utilize the state-augmented resource allocation policy parametrized by
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}
to replace the two-step update sequence in (
10
) with (stochastic) dual descent updates of

\displaystyle\hskip-5.0pt\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k+1}\!=\!\left[\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}\!-\!\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}{\mathbf{f}}\Bigg(\!\!\frac{1}{T_{0}}\!\!\!\!\sum_{t=kT_{0}}^{(k+1)T_{0}-1}\!\!\!\!\!\!{\mathbf{r}}\Big({\mathbf{H}}_{t},{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}({\mathbf{H}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k};\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})\Big)\!\Bigg)\!\right]_{+}\!\!\!\!.

(16)

In (
16
), trained state-augmented parametrization executes Lagrangian maximizing policies
{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}({\mathbf{H}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\lfloor t/T_{0}\rfloor};\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})
at each time step
t
, and the dual dynamics descend the parametrized Lagrangian. Here,
k\in\{0,1,\dots,K-1\}
is an iteration index with
K=\lfloor T/T_{0}\rfloor
, and
T_{0}
denotes the time duration between consecutive dual variable updates. We note that the state-augmented dual dynamics exhibit the Markovian property and constraints are evaluated through stochastic estimates of the ergodic performance function given by the
T_{0}
-average of the instantaneous performance vectors [c.f. (
1
)].

Optimality and feasibility guarantees, analogous to those of the DGD algorithm [cf. (
11
) and (
12
)], have been proven for the state-augmented trajectories generated by (
16
), as formalized in the following theorem.
1
1

1

We note that the state-augmented policies in
[
21
]
operate on the instantaneous network states
{\mathbf{H}}_{t}
whereas our policies operate on the large-scale network configuration
{\mathbf{H}}
[c.f. (

)]. However, the theoretical analysis remains the same due to unbiased iterates assumption.

Theorem 1
.

[
21
, Thm. 2]

Assuming the boundedness and strict feasibility of the constraints, expectation-wise Lipschitzness of the network-wide objective utility function
f_{0}
and the constraint utility
{\mathbf{f}}
, and the
\nu
-universality of the state-augmented parameterization
{\mathbf{p}}^{\boldsymbol{\phi}}({\mathbf{H}},\boldsymbol{\lambda};\boldsymbol{\phi})
, and that the expected values of the objective and utility constraints within each iteration provide unbiased estimates of the objective and constraints in (
3
), the sequence of decisions made by the state-augmented algorithm in (
16
) is feasible, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}{\mathbf{f}}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathbf{r}}\Big[{\mathbf{H}}_{t},{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\left({\mathbf{H}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\lfloor t/T_{0}\rfloor};\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}\right)\Big]\right)\geq{\mathbf{0}},\quad a.s.,

(17)

and near-optimal, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[f_{0}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathbf{r}}\Big[{\mathbf{H}}_{t},{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\left({\mathbf{H}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\lfloor t/T_{0}\rfloor};\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}\right)\Big]\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\mathcal{O}}(\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})-{\mathcal{O}}(\nu).

(18)

Theorem
1
shows that the state-augmented policies are almost surely feasible and near-optimal given long enough run time. Moreover, the objective utility attained is within a constant additive gap of
D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
which can be made arbitrarily small when a sufficiently small dual step size
\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and near-universal parametrizations are utilized. Nonetheless, in a practical deployment where the network dynamics
{\mathbf{H}}_{t}
shift quickly over time, using an arbitrarily small dual step size
\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
with an improper initialization will be ineffective for meeting the optimality and feasibility guarantees in finite time/iterations. This underscores the need for initializing the dual dynamics close to the optimal dual multipliers
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}({\mathbf{H}})
for a given
{\mathbf{H}}
.

III-B

Proposed Sampling & Initialization of Dual Multipliers

Departing from prior state-augmented approaches where dual multipliers are drawn from a uniform prior during training and initialized at zero during inference, we propose to train the state-augmented policy
\boldsymbol{\phi}
with dual multipliers sampled from the associated dual descent trajectories for each training network configuration
{\mathbf{H}}
and initialize the dual multipliers at their near-optimal levels during inference for any given
{\mathbf{H}}
.

Our proposed dual multiplier sampling scheme periodically rolls out state-augmented dual dynamics at designated training checkpoints. The generated trajectories are saved to a buffer, from which dual multipliers are subsequently sampled until the next checkpoint. Starting with initial dual multiplier sampling distributions
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\mathbf{H}})
, e.g., uniform distribution for all
{\mathbf{H}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{H}}}
, the resulting procedure gradually adjusts the sampling distributions
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\mathbf{H}})
to match the dual descent trajectories
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\phi})
, and aims to settle at an optimal policy
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}
and training distributions
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\mathbf{H}})\approx\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})\approx\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger})
. We discuss the details of the proposed sampling scheme and the training algorithm overall in the Supplementary Materials.

Given
{\mathbf{H}}
and a trained state-augmented policy
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}
, we propose to estimate an optimal dual multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}({\mathbf{H}})
by generating trajectories of dual dynamics and computing the expected time-average of the dual multiplier iterates,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})=

\displaystyle\lim_{K\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})}\Bigg[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}({\mathbf{H}})\Bigg],

(19)

where we denote by
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})
an optimal dual multiplier estimate, and the expectation is over
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})
, which denotes the randomness of the state-augmented dynamics
\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}({\mathbf{H}})\}_{k\geq 0}
in (
16
) generated by
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}
for a given
{\mathbf{H}}
. In a practical implementation of (
19
), we roll out a batch of trajectories for sufficiently large
K
and
\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. This allows for a fast initialization of the online dynamics for any given
{\mathbf{H}}
. In Section
IV
, we prove that the DGD counterpart of (
19
) where the expectation is over
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger})
, yields an estimate that can be made arbitrarily close to an optimal dual multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}({\mathbf{H}})
. Assuming
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})\approx\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger})
for an optimal state-augmented policy
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}
, (
19
) serves as a theoretically grounded method for near-optimal initialization of state-augmented dynamics.

In the next section, we introduce a secondary supervised learning algorithm—which we call the dual variable regression or
dual-regression (DR) algorithm
for short—that succeeds the state-augmented training algorithm and is trained to learn
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})
for all
{\mathbf{H}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{H}}}
. This way, we learn the near-optimal multipliers for network configurations seen during training and generalize to unseen ones during inference.

III-C

Dual-Regression Parametrization

We define a parametric regression function
{\mathbf{d}}_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{H}}\times\boldsymbol{\Psi}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{c}_{+}
where
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\boldsymbol{\Psi}
is a finite-dimensional set of parameters and
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{F_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}}
denotes the set of
F_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}
-dimensional regression features. We denote this regression function by
{\mathbf{d}}_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}({\mathbf{X}}({\mathbf{H}}),{\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\psi})
and refer to it as
dual-regression function (parametrization)
. Note that the regression features
{\mathbf{X}}({\mathbf{H}})
depend on the network configuration
{\mathbf{H}}
similar to the near-optimal dual multiplier estimates
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})
.

For a given
{\mathbf{H}}
, we define the regression loss function as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}\big({\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{H}},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*};\boldsymbol{\psi}\big)=\big\|{\mathbf{d}}_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\big({\mathbf{X}},{\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\psi}\big)-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}\big({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}\big)\big\|^{2}_{p},

(20)

where
\|.\|_{p}
denotes the
L^{p}
vector norm. In (
20
), near-optimal dual multiplier estimates
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})
serve as regression targets (labels) and an
optimal dual-regression parametrization
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*}
is a solution of the risk minimization problem given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*}\in

\displaystyle\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\;\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{H}}\sim\mathcal{D}_{{\mathbf{H}}}}\left[{\mathcal{F}}\big({\mathbf{X}}({\mathbf{H}}),{\mathbf{H}},\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*};\boldsymbol{\psi}\big)\right].

(21)

At the start of the online execution of the state-augmented algorithm, for any given
{\mathbf{H}}
, we can utilize an optimal dual-regression parametrization
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*}
to initialize the dual multipliers close to their optimal levels by setting

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}({\mathbf{H}}):={\mathbf{d}}_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\big({\mathbf{X}}({\mathbf{H}}),{\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\psi}^{*}\big),

(22)

and run the online dual updates in (
16
) as before. Note again that the regression features
{\mathbf{X}}
might depend on the regression input
{\mathbf{H}}
, the state-augmented parametrization
\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*}
, or a (learnable) external feature extractor. The choice is application-dependent, and we discuss our choice of
{\mathbf{X}}
in Section
V
after we introduce the power allocation problem [c.f. (
40
) and (
41
)].

IV
Convergence and Excursion

In this section, we present theoretical insights into the dual dynamics of the DGD algorithm from which we developed the state-augmented algorithm. Similar to our treatment of the parametrized dual problem and the DGD algorithm in Section
II
, we break down the Lagrangian into subproblems indexed by each network configuration
{\mathbf{H}}
and work with a given
{\mathbf{H}}
in our theoretical analysis [c.f. (
II-B
)].

IV-A

Preliminaries

We first present several definitions and assumptions regarding the dual function and its stochastic subgradients that allow us to study the convergence behavior of the dual dynamics.

Assumption 1

(Unbiased stochastic subgradients with bounded expected norms)
.

For any
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, the stochastic subgradient
\widehat{{\mathbf{s}}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
is an unbiased estimate of a true subgradient, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{{\mathbf{s}}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})]={\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\in\partial g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
. Furthermore, the second moment of the norm of the subgradients is bounded, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\left\|\widehat{{\mathbf{s}}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right\|_{2}^{2}]\leq S^{2}
for some
S>0
.

Assumption 2

(Strictly feasible policy exists.)
.

For any
{\mathbf{H}}
, there exists a strictly feasible primal variable
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}
and a constant
\xi>0
such that we have
{\mathbf{f}}\left({\mathbf{r}}\big[{\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger})\big]\right)\geq\xi\mathbf{1}
.

Definition 2
.

For a given
\epsilon>0
, we define an
\epsilon
-ball of near-optimality for parametrized dual function iterates as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon}\coloneqq\Big\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}\;\,|\,\;g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})-D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\leq\epsilon\Big\},

(23)

and the
\epsilon
-ball of near-optimal dual multipliers as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\epsilon}\coloneqq\Big\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{+}\;\,|\,\;\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\|\leq\epsilon\Big\},

(24)

where
\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\|
is understood as the distance of
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
to the closest optimal dual multiplier, i.e., to the set
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{*}_{0}
.

Assumption
2
asserts the existence of a strictly feasible solution. Although the unbiasedness and bounded expected norm of stochastic subgradients in Assumption
1
are commonplace in convergence analyses of stochastic gradient descent algorithms, the former might not hold in practice since the dual update window
T_{0}
is finite. However, it is reasonable to expect that for a suitable choice of an update window
T_{0}
, the bias can be made sufficiently small, and the stochastic subgradients are likely to be descent directions (e.g., see
[
20
, Appendix II]
). The second moment assumption can be guaranteed, e.g., if the utilities
f_{i}
are assumed to be bounded on
\mathrm{dom}\,f_{i}
.

One motivation for defining
{\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon}
as the
\epsilon
-near-optimality ball of dual function iterates is due to the following lemma:

Lemma 1
.

Given Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
, any dual multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon}
is contained in the ball

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\epsilon-f_{0}\left({\mathbf{r}}\left({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{p}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}\big)\right)\right)}{\xi},

(25)

where
\xi
is the feasibility gap and
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger}
are the strictly feasible model parameters as defined in Assumption
2
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A
.

An immediate implication of Lemma
1
is that the set of optimal dual multipliers
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{*}
is bounded. By the equivalence of norms, Lemma
1
asserts that whenever the current dual function iterate
g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
is within an
\epsilon
-neighborhood of
D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, the current dual iterate
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is also close to an optimal multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
.

We formally state these as corollaries and finish with a lemma which establishes that the dual dynamics indeed perform a stochastic subgradient descent on the dual function.

Corollary 1
.

Under Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
, the set of optimal dual multipliers
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{*}_{0}
is bounded.

Corollary 2
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, for any given
\epsilon>0
, there exists a
B_{\epsilon}
-ball of near-optimal dual multipliers that contains
{\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon}
, i.e.,
{\mathcal{G}}_{\epsilon}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{B_{\epsilon}}
.

Lemma 2
.

For any
k\geq 0
, the dual iterates of the stochastic DGD algorithm satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k+1}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\right\|^{2}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\right\|^{2}+\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{2}S^{2}-2\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\left[g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})-D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right].

(26)

Proof.

See Appendix
B
.

Lemma
2
implies that whenever the dual function optimality gaps
g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})-D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
are larger than
\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}
, dual dynamics tend to decrease the optimality gap in expectation and vice versa. We will establish in Proposition
2
and Theorem
2
that stochastic dual dynamics drive the dual function iterates to regularly visit a near-optimality neighborhood
\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}}
, and tend not to drift far away between two consecutive visits, respectively.

IV-B

Ergodic Complementary Slackness and Near-Optimality

We first prove a result that characterizes the near-optimality of the average of the dual iterates. We will use this result as a starting point to motivate our pursuit of near-optimal initialization of the state-augmented inference algorithm.

Proposition 1
.

Given a network configuration
{\mathbf{H}}
and initial dual multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}
, let
\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be the sequence of dual multipliers generated by the stochastic DGD algorithm in (
10
) and define
\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{K}\coloneqq(1/K)\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}
as the time/iteration-average of the dual multipliers up to time/iteration
K
and define expected dual multiplier averaged over
K
iterations as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}_{K}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\dagger})}\left[\bar{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{K}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}\right],

(27)

where the expectation is over the trajectories generated by the DGD algorithm [c.f. (
19
)]. Then under Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
, the dual function evaluated at
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}_{K}
satisfies

\displaystyle g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}_{K}\big)-D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\leq\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}+\frac{\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\right\|^{2}}{2\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}K},

(28)

and there exists some positive constant
B_{\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}}
such that

\displaystyle\big\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}_{K}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\big\|\leq B_{\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}}\left(1+\frac{\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\right\|^{2}}{K\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{2}S^{2}}\right).

(29)

Proof.

See Appendix
C
.

We gain important insights for the finite-time behavior of the dual dynamics through Proposition
1
. Since the optimality gaps are proportional to the dual step size
\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, we ideally should choose a small step size. However, if the distance of the initial multiplier to the set of optimal multipliers
\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\right\|
is large and
\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is small, then the second term on the near-optimality bound decays relatively slowly and the DGD algorithm generates suboptimal decisions for a longer period at the beginning of inference. This presents a trade-off between finite-time optimality and feasibility while also offering an opportunity for significant improvement by initializing the dual multipliers close to their optimal levels.

By taking the liminf of both sides in (
29
), it follows that

\displaystyle\liminf_{K\to\infty}\;\big\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}_{K}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\big\|\leq B_{\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}}.

(30)

Assuming the limit
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}=\lim_{K\to\infty}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}_{K}
exists, the expected time-averaged dual multiplier
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}
satisfies
||\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}||\leq B_{\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}}
, or equivalently,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}\in\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{B_{\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}}}
.
Similarly, the expected time average of the dual function increments converges to
{\mathcal{G}}_{\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}}
in the limit, i.e.,

\displaystyle\liminf_{K\to\infty}\;g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}_{K}\big)-D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\leq\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}.

(31)

Asymptotic near-optimality of the sequence of decisions generated by (
10a
) is proven by an appeal to the ergodic complementary slackness result (see e.g.,
[
21
]
). However, the convergence claims of Proposition
1
do not paint the complete picture. The existence of arbitrarily bad realizations of dual descent trajectories that never generate near-optimal decisions is not in contradiction with Proposition
1
, which could render our efforts of near-optimal initialization of dual multipliers futile. Fortunately, the convergence and excursion results we state next show that this is not the case. For almost every realization, the dual function iterates (resp. dual multiplier iterates) enter a small neighborhood of
D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
(resp.
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{*}_{0}
) infinitely often and deviations therefrom are highly unlikely.

IV-C

Almost-sure Convergence of Best Iterates

Before stating the stochastic convergence result, it is instructive to analyze the deterministic case. Assume access to an oracle that replaces the stochastic subgradients with their unbiased expectations. By an appeal to recursion, we have

\displaystyle\min_{k=0,\ldots,K}\big[g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})-D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big]\leq\frac{\left\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}\right\|^{2}+K\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{2}S^{2}}{2\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}K}.

(32)

The convergence of the best dual function optimality gap follows since the right hand side of (
32
) approaches
\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}
as
K\to\infty
. Since the stochastic subgradients
\widehat{{\mathbf{s}}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
oscillate around a true subgradient, it is reasonable to expect a similar convergence behavior in the stochastic case. The following proposition from
[
25
, Theorem 1]
confirms this intuition by leveraging ideas from supermartingale theory.

Proposition 2
.

Consider the stochastic DGD algorithm given in (
10
). Given any
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}
and that Assumption
1
holds, the best dual function iterate
g^{\text{best}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(K\,|\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0})\coloneqq\min_{k=0,\ldots,K}\big[g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})\big]
satisfies

\displaystyle\liminf_{K\to\infty}\;g^{\text{best}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(K\,|\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0})-D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\leq\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}\quad\text{a.s.}

(33)

Proof.

See Appendix
D
.
∎

Proposition
2
asserts that for almost all realizations and arbitrary
\delta>0
,
g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})\leq D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}-\delta
occurs eventually for some
k
. Moreover, by a time-shift argument,
g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})\leq D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}-\delta
happens infinitely many times. By Corollary
2
, an analogous statement holds for the dual iterates, i.e.,

\displaystyle\liminf_{K\to\infty}\;\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\text{best}}(K\,|\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0})\leq B_{\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}}\quad\text{a.s.},

(34)

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\text{best}}(K\,|\,\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0})\coloneqq\min_{k=0,\ldots,K}\big\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\big\|
.

IV-D

Excursions of Dual Function Iterates

Although Proposition
2
assures that the dual function iterates enter a neighborhood of optimality at least once, it does not claim anything about the behavior of optimality gaps between visits to the optimality neighborhood. It could happen that the dual iterates stray away from the optimality neighborhood arbitrarily far with a small but nonzero probability, and we refer to such events as excursions. A linear probability bound on the excursions of the dual (function) iterates follows under minimal assumptions based on the tightness of the probability measure induced by the dual dynamics
[
21
, Appendix A]
and Lemma
2
is an intermediate step in the proof. By introducing additional assumptions on the curvature of the dual function inside the excursion domain of interest, we derive an exponential probability bound on the deviations from the optimality neighborhood. We formally define an excursion and state additional assumptions before stating the main theorem.

Definition 3

(Excursion of optimality gaps)
.

For
k\geq 0
, let
G_{k}\coloneqq g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k})-D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
be the dual function optimality gaps as before and define a stopping time
L\coloneqq\min\{l>0\,|\,G_{k_{0}+l}<G_{k_{0}}\}
. We define the sequence
\{G_{k_{0}},\ldots,G_{k_{0}+L}\}
as an excursion of the dual function optimality gaps and
G^{\dagger}_{k_{0}}=\max_{0\leq l\leq L}G_{k_{0}+l}
as the worst excursion gap.

Assumption 3

(Local Lipschitz-smoothness and strong-convexity)
.

There exist constants
\lambda_{\max},G_{\max},m,L>0
and an excursion subregion
{\mathcal{E}}
such that
\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\|\leq\lambda_{\max}
for any
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
and the dual function is
L
-Lipschitz smooth on
{\mathcal{E}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hskip-3.00003ptg_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})\!-\!g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1})

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1})\!+\!\frac{L}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}\|^{2},

(35)

\forall\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}\in{\mathcal{E}}
and
m
-strongly convex on
{\mathcal{E}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hskip-5.0ptg_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2})\!-\!g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1})

\displaystyle\geq{\mathbf{s}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1})\!+\!\frac{m}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1}\|^{2},

(36)

\forall\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{1},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{2}\in{\mathcal{E}}
, where the excursion subregion
{\mathcal{E}}
is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}\coloneqq\big\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{c}_{+}\,|\,G_{\min}\leq g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})-D^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\leq G_{\max}\big\},

(37)

where
G_{\min}\coloneqq\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{L}{2m}\right)
denotes the inner excursion boundary and we note that it is a constant multiple of
\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\frac{S^{2}}{2}
.

Assumption
3
is a technical condition we introduce on the dual function so that the gradients grow sufficiently fast within an excursion subregion. These are mild assumptions compared to assuming that the dual optimality gap function exhibits global strong-convexity and Lipschitz-smoothness. Although there are results showing that the dual function is strongly concave or satisfies local strong convexity in a neighborhood of an optimal solution (see, e.g.,
[
26
]
), these results generally pertain to certain classes of convex optimization problems and are too restrictive in our case. Next, we present the main excursion theorem that provides a probability bound on large deviations that exponentially decays within the subregion
{\mathcal{E}}
where local strong convexity and Lipschitz smoothness of
G_{k}
are assumed, and exhibits linear decay outside
{\mathcal{E}}
.

Theorem 2

(Excursion Bound for Dual Function Optimality Gaps)
.

Consider a sequence of dual function optimality gaps
\{G_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
with
G_{0}=(1+\rho)G_{\min}
for any
\rho>0
, and suppose Assumptions
1
-
3
hold. Then the probability of
\gamma
-excursions of the dual function optimality gaps is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big[G^{\dagger}_{0}\geq\gamma\;|\;G_{0}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{e^{-\beta\big(\min\big\{\gamma,G_{\max}\big\}-G_{0}\big)}}{1+\min\big\{0,\gamma-G_{\max}-1\big\}},

(38)

with
\beta\coloneqq\frac{\rho L}{S^{2}\left(1+\rho^{2}\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{2}\frac{L^{2}}{4}\right)}>0
.

Proof.

See Appendix
E
.
∎

The idea behind the proof is that the sequence of optimality gaps
G_{k}
exhibits a supermartingale property. The linear probability bound is obtained through Markov’s inequality for (super)martingales, while the exponential bound follows from an exponential transform of
G_{k}
that preserves the supermartingale property within
{\mathcal{E}}
. In summary, the dual function optimality gaps
G_{k}
given by the dual dynamics tend to enter a neighborhood of optimality and rarely drift far away.

V
Numerical Results: Optimal Power Control

We test the proposed state-augmented learning algorithm in a power control problem in a setting similar to
[
21
]
. To briefly summarize the setup, all wireless network realizations are sampled from a family of network configurations comprised of
N
users, i.e., transmitter-receiver pairs
\{(\mathsf{Tx}_{i},\mathsf{Rx}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, dropped uniformly within an
R\times R
square area with an average user density of
\rho=N/R^{2}
users/
\text{\,}{\mathrm{km}}^{2}
. Each transmitter communicates with a single designated receiver, and each receiver treats the incoming interference from all but its associated transmitter as noise. The (instantaneous) network state at time step
t
,
{\mathbf{H}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}_{+}
, and the large-scale (slow-varying) network state, i.e., network configuration,
{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}_{+}
, contain the magnitude of all complex channel gains, where instantaneous and long-term channel gains between transmitter
\mathsf{Tx}_{i}
and receiver
\mathsf{Rx}_{j}
are denoted by
h_{ij,t},h_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}
.

Denoting the maximum transmit power by
P_{\max}
, resource allocation vectors
{\mathbf{p}}\in[0,P_{\max}]^{N}
represent the transmit power levels of the transmitters. Given network configuration
{\mathbf{H}}
and resource allocation vector
{\mathbf{p}}={\mathbf{p}}({\mathbf{H}})
, the instantaneous performance vector
{\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}}_{t},{\mathbf{p}}\big)
represents the receiver rates, and the rate for each receiver
\mathsf{Rx}_{i}
at timestep
t
is given by

\displaystyle\!\!r_{i}({\mathbf{H}}_{t},{\mathbf{p}})=\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{p_{i}\left|h_{ii,t}\right|^{2}}{WN_{0}+\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{N}p_{j}\left|h_{ji,t}\right|^{2}}\right),

(39)

where
W
and
N_{0}
denote the channel bandwidth and noise power spectral density (PSD), respectively, and it is assumed that capacity-achieving codes are used. The ergodic performance vector
{\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}},{\mathbf{p}}({\mathbf{H}})\big)=(1/T)\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}}_{t},{\mathbf{p}}({\mathbf{H}})\big)
accordingly represents the ergodic receiver rates and is evaluated over a horizon of
T
time steps [c.f. (
1
)]. We can write the power control problem for any given
{\mathbf{H}}
as

\displaystyle P^{*}({\mathbf{H}})=

\displaystyle\maximum_{{\mathbf{p}}({\mathbf{H}})}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbf{1}_{N}^{\top}{\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}}_{t},{\mathbf{p}}({\mathbf{H}})\big),

(40a)

subject to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}{\mathbf{r}}\big({\mathbf{H}}_{t},{\mathbf{p}}({\mathbf{H}})\big)\geq\mathbf{1}_{N}f_{\min},

(40b)

where we imposed a minimum ergodic rate requirement of
f_{\min}
bps/Hz for all receivers by considering a minimum-rate constraint utility

VI
Conclusion

We considered a resource allocation problem in multi-user wireless networks where the goal is to optimize a network-wide utility subject to constraints on the ergodic performance of the users over a distribution of network configurations. We proposed a state-augmented policy with dual variable regression for fast sampling of near-optimal and feasible resource allocations during inference. Leveraging GNN parametrizations, we demonstrated the scalability and transferability of the learned power control policies across various families of wireless network graphs. In addition to experimental verification, we proved several convergence and excursion results for the dual function (iterate) optimality gaps.

and obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big[G^{\dagger}_{0}\geq\gamma\,|\,G_{0}\big]=\mathbb{P}\big[H_{L^{*}}>e^{\beta\gamma}\,|\,H_{0}\big].

(82)

We define the stopped process

\displaystyle H_{l\wedge L^{*}}:=\begin{cases}H_{l}&\quad\text{if $l<L^{*}$},\\
H_{L^{*}}&\quad\text{if $l\geq L^{*}$}.\end{cases}

(83)

Since
L^{*}
is a.s. finite, the stopped process is a martingale by an appeal to Doob’s optional stopping theorem
[
33
]
. In fact, the stopped process
H_{l\wedge L^{*}}
inherits the supermartingale property of
H_{l}
. When
l<L^{*}
, the stopped sequence (
83
) is identical to the transformed sequence and for
l\geq L^{*}
, all values of the stopped process are equal to
H_{L^{*}}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[H_{(l+1)\wedge L^{*}}\,|\,H_{l\wedge L^{*}}\big]

\displaystyle\leq H_{l\wedge L^{*}},\quad l=0,1,\ldots,

(84)

and in particular, applying (
84
) recursively yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[H_{L^{*}}\,|\,H_{0}]\leq H_{0}.

(85)

Finally, we combine (
82
) and (
85
) through Markov’s inequality to obtain the exponential probability bound of

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big[G^{\dagger}_{0}\geq\gamma\,|\,G_{0}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[H_{L^{*}}>e^{\beta\gamma}\,|\,H_{0}]

(86)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[H_{L^{*}}\,|\,H_{0}]e^{-\beta\gamma}

(87)

\displaystyle\leq H_{0}e^{-\beta\gamma}=\exp\left(-\beta(\gamma-G_{0})\right).

(88)

We finish by combining the exponential probability bound on the deviations within
{\mathcal{E}}
with a linear bound outside
{\mathcal{E}}
.
∎

E.3
Implementation of GNN Architectures

Both GNNs operate over a graph representation of a given network configuration
{\mathbf{H}}
, denoted by
{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathbf{H}}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{E}},{\mathcal{W}},{\mathcal{Y}})
, where

•

{\mathcal{V}}=\{1,\ldots,N\}
denotes the set
of nodes with each node representing a user (a transmitter-receiver pair).

•

{\mathcal{E}}={\mathcal{V}}\times{\mathcal{V}}
denotes the set of directed edges where each edge from node
i
to node
j
corresponds to the channel gain between
i
th transmitter and
j
th receiver.

•

{\mathcal{W}}:{\mathcal{E}}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
is a weighting function that returns the log-normalized channel gains
e_{ij}=\frac{1}{Z}\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{P_{\max}|h_{ij}|^{2}}{WN_{0}}\right)
where
Z>0
is a normalization constant.

•

{\mathcal{Y}}
denotes the input node features. For the primal-GNN, the node features vary over time and at each time step
t
, we set them to the dual multipliers
{\mathcal{Y}}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\lfloor t/T_{0}\rfloor}({\mathbf{H}})
. For the dual-GNN, we always set the input node features to the DR features, i.e.,
{\mathcal{Y}}={\mathbf{X}}({\mathbf{H}})
.

We work with an equivalent matrix representation of
{\mathcal{W}}
. We denote by
{\mathbf{W}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\mathcal{V}}|\times|{\mathcal{V}}|}
the weighted adjacency matrix with
W_{ij}=e_{ij}
for all
i,j\in{\mathcal{V}}
, and describe the GNN operation with the concept of a
graph shift operator (GSO)
. A GSO captures the underlying graph topology and is defined as any real and symmetric matrix
{\mathbf{S}}
that satisfies
({\mathbf{S}})_{ij}=0
iff
(i,j)\notin{\mathcal{E}}
. The (weighted) adjacency matrix
{\mathbf{W}}
we defined is a valid GSO and another typical example of which is the Laplacian matrix
{\mathbf{L}}={\mathbf{D}}-{\mathbf{A}}
where
{\mathbf{D}}=\mathbf{diag}\left({\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{1}\right)
is the node degree matrix and
{\mathbf{A}}
is (weighted) adjacency matrix.

Both GNNs share the same backbone and process graph data through a cascade of
L
graph convolutional network (GCN) layers. Each GCN layer
\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{(\ell)}
is comprised of a graph convolutional filter and a pointwise nonlinearity
\varphi
(e.g., relu), and maps input node embeddings, i.e.,
{\mathbf{Z}}^{(\ell-1)}
, and the GSO
{\mathbf{S}}
to output node embeddings by

\displaystyle\hskip-6.99997pt{\mathbf{Z}}^{(\ell)}\!=\!\boldsymbol{\Psi}^{(\ell)}\left({\mathbf{Z}}^{(\ell-1)};{\mathbf{S}},\Theta^{(\ell)}\right)\!=\!\varphi\left[\,\sum_{k=0}^{K}\;{\mathbf{S}}^{k}{\mathbf{Z}}^{(\ell-1)}\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}^{(\ell)}\right]\!.

(95)

In (
95
),
\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(\ell)}=\{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{k}^{(\ell)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{F_{\ell-1}\times F_{\ell}}\}_{k=0}^{K}
is a set of weights (graph filter coefficients) that we learn during training. To be more precise,
\boldsymbol{\phi}
and
\boldsymbol{\psi}
include all the respective weights
\{\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{(\ell)}\}_{1\leq\ell\leq L}
plus any additional learnable parameters, e.g., parameters of the normalization and embedding layers.

E.4
Other Practical Considerations & Training Details

We first train the primal-GNN for
N_{\mathrm{SA}}=100
epochs on the training dataset
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{H}}}
. Each training epoch iterates over
{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{H}}}
with a mini-batch size of
B_{{\mathbf{H}}}=8
networks, i.e., a total of
100\times 128/8=1600
iterations, and we sample
B_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
= 4 dual multipliers for each network. Dual multiplier sampling distribution is initially set to a standard uniform prior and we initialize an empty dual multiplier buffer for each training network configuration. The buffer capacity is set to 100 dual multiplier vectors per network configuration. We test the primal-GNN on both the training and validation datasets every
N^{0}_{\mathrm{SA}}=2
epochs. We roll out the dual dynamics with the same choices of
T,T_{0}
and
\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
as the ones we used in the actual test phase. That is, we roll out the dynamics for
T=200
time steps, where the dual update (iteration) window is set to
T_{0}=5
, and the dual step size is set at
\eta_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=0.2
. The initial multipliers are set to an average multiplier we compute across the buffer of each network configuration, which we use as a proxy for an optimal dual multiplier. Then the dual multiplier trajectories are added to the buffers corresponding to each network configuration, and we draw dual multipliers from this dynamically evolving buffer as the training progresses.

Training of the primal-GNN is succeeded by the optimal dual multiplier estimation phase and training of the dual-GNN. We split the training dataset further into training and validation datasets with a
7:1
ratio. For each given
{\mathbf{H}}
, the regression targets,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\dagger}({\mathbf{H}};\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*})
, are estimated by averaging dual multipliers in the corresponding dual multiplier sampling buffer. Given the regression features computed by (
41
) and regression targets (labels), we train the dual model also for
N_{\mathrm{DR}}=100
epochs with a batch size of
B_{{\mathbf{H}}}=32
.
