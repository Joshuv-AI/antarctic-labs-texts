CBL combined with MDT mode in clinical teaching of liver cancer: a randomized controlled study
Authors: Simin Zhang, Jiao Li, Cui Deng, Sheng Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiver cancer management requires multidisciplinary collaboration, yet traditional clinical teaching often fails to foster integrated clinical reasoning skills among medical students. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of combining case-based learning (CBL) with multidisciplinary team (MDT) mode in clinical teaching of liver cancer.MethodsA total of 112 postgraduate students from Dali University Clinical Medical School who completed clinical internships at The Third People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province between January 2023 and June 2025 were enrolled and randomly assigned to experimental group (n = 56) and control group (n = 56). The control group received traditional lecture-based teaching, while the experimental group received CBL combined with MDT teaching. Teaching effectiveness was evaluated through theoretical examinations, clinical skills assessments using objective structured clinical examination (OSCE), and questionnaire surveys. Independent t-tests and chi-square tests were used for statistical analysis.ResultsThe experimental group demonstrated significantly higher theoretical examination scores (87.15±5.34 vs. 81.23±6.12, t=3.41, P<0.01, Cohen's d=1.03) and clinical skills assessment scores (86.42±5.68 vs. 81.08±5.95, t=2.89, P<0.01, Cohen's d=0.91) compared to the control group. OSCE subdomain analysis revealed superior performance in history taking (17.82±1.56 vs. 16.94±1.78, P=0.017), diagnostic test interpretation (17.65±1.72 vs. 16.51±1.93, P=0.012), treatment planning (17.93±1.48 vs. 16.32±1.85, P=0.002), and doctor-patient communication (17.58±1.63 vs. 16.21±1.89, P=0.008). Background

Primary liver cancer (hereafter referred to as hepatocellular carcinoma, HCC) ranks as the fourth most common malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality in China, imposing a substantial disease burden on public health [1, 2]. Data from the National Cancer Center indicate approximately 389,000 new cases and 336,000 deaths annually nationwide [3]. The clinical management of HCC involves multiple disciplines, including hepatology, hepatobiliary surgery, interventional radiology, medical oncology, diagnostic imaging, and pathology, making it challenging for any single specialty to comprehensively address all aspects of patient care [4, 5]. With rapid advancements in targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and interventional techniques, HCC treatment has evolved toward precision medicine and individualized multidisciplinary comprehensive therapy [6, 7]. Consequently, cultivating multidisciplinary collaborative competence and integrated clinical reasoning skills among medical students has become a critical priority in oncology education.

Case-based learning (CBL) is a student-centered pedagogical approach that utilizes clinical cases as vehicles for learning, guiding students to analyze authentic or simulated cases to develop problem-identification, analysis, and resolution skills [8, 9]. CBL has been widely implemented in medical education, with studies demonstrating its effectiveness in stimulating learning interest and bridging the gap between theoretical knowledge and clinical practice [10]. The multidisciplinary team (MDT) model, characterized by collaborative decision-making involving specialists from multiple disciplines to develop individualized treatment plans, has become the standard of care in oncology [11, 12]. Beyond its clinical utility, MDT represents a valuable educational resource, offering students direct observation of discipline-specific perspectives and collaborative processes [13, 14].

Recent years have witnessed growing interest in integrating CBL with MDT in oncology education [15, 16]. Song et al. [17] applied MDT combined with CBL in HCC education and found that this approach facilitated students’ development of systematic clinical reasoning frameworks. Xie et al. [18] reported that CBL combined with MDT significantly enhanced students’ comprehension of comprehensive HCC treatment strategies. However, existing studies are predominantly limited by small sample sizes and short observation periods, lacking systematic validation with adequate statistical power.

The theoretical framework underlying CBL-MDT integration draws upon constructivist learning theory and social cognitive theory. Constructivism posits that learners actively construct knowledge through interaction with authentic contexts and problems [19]. CBL provides the authentic clinical context, while MDT offers the social interaction with multiple expert perspectives. Social cognitive theory emphasizes observational learning and modeling [20], with MDT discussions enabling students to observe and internalize expert reasoning processes. The synergy between these approaches potentially creates a powerful learning environment that surpasses either method alone.

This study aimed to systematically evaluate the effectiveness of CBL combined with MDT in HCC clinical teaching using a large sample of 112 postgraduate students, with comprehensive outcome measures encompassing knowledge acquisition, clinical skills performance, and learner perceptions.

Methods

Study design and participants

A prospective randomized controlled design was employed. The study population comprised postgraduate students from Dali University Clinical Medical School who completed clinical internships at The Third People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province between January 2023 and June 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) full-time Master’s degree candidates; (2) completion of foundational courses including internal medicine and surgery; (3) voluntary participation with written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) prior systematic clinical rotations in hepatobiliary oncology; (2) inability to participate fully in the teaching program; (3) refusal to complete questionnaires.

A total of 112 eligible students were randomly allocated using a computer-generated random number table to either experimental group (n = 56) or control group (n = 56). Randomization was performed by an independent researcher not involved in teaching or assessment. Baseline characteristics including gender, age, educational level, and entrance examination scores were comparable between groups (Table 1).

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of The Third People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants were assured of their right to withdraw at any time without consequence, and data confidentiality was maintained throughout the study.

Teaching faculty

A total of 12 senior attending physicians and above participated as instructors, including 3 from hepatobiliary surgery, 2 from interventional radiology, 2 from medical oncology, 2 from diagnostic imaging, 2 from pathology, and 1 from hepatology. All instructors received standardized training on teaching protocols, including uniform teaching objectives, case selection criteria, and structured discussion formats. The same instructor team delivered both control and experimental group teaching to minimize instructor-related variability. Weekly faculty meetings were held to ensure consistency in content delivery and to address any implementation issues.

Teaching interventions

Both groups received a total of 8 teaching hours covering identical core content related to HCC diagnosis and management, aligned with the National Health Commission’s “Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Primary Liver Cancer (2024 Edition)” [1].

Control group: traditional lecture-based teaching

Students in the control group received conventional didactic instruction comprising: (1) theoretical lectures (3 h) systematically covering HCC epidemiology, etiology, pathology, clinical manifestations, diagnostic modalities, staging systems, and treatment principles; (2) clinical observation (3 h) in inpatient wards, including bedside teaching and observation of typical case presentations and physical examinations; (3) routine case discussions (2 h) where instructors presented cases and led structured discussions following traditional formats.

Experimental group: CBL combined with MDT teaching

The experimental group received an integrated CBL-MDT intervention structured as follows:

① Case Selection and Preparation: The teaching team curated five representative HCC cases from institutional clinical records, subsequently anonymized for educational use. Selected cases encompassed diverse clinical scenarios: early-stage resectable HCC, intermediate-stage suitable for interventional therapy, advanced-stage requiring palliative care, HCC complicated by cirrhosis and portal hypertension, and recurrent HCC. Each case package included comprehensive documentation: complete medical history, physical examination findings, laboratory data, imaging studies (CT, MRI, ultrasound), and pathology reports.

Outcome measures

Theoretical knowledge assessment

A standardized written examination was administered immediately following the teaching intervention. Content covered HCC etiology, pathology, clinical manifestations, imaging characteristics, diagnostic criteria, staging systems, treatment principles, and recent research advances. The examination comprised multiple-choice questions (40 points), multiple-response questions (20 points), short-answer questions (20 points), and case-based analysis questions (20 points), totaling 100 points. Questions were randomly selected from a validated item bank to ensure comparability between groups.

Clinical skills assessment

Clinical competency was evaluated using objective structured clinical examination (OSCE) with five stations: history taking (20 points), physical examination (20 points), diagnostic test interpretation (imaging and laboratory data, 20 points), treatment planning (20 points), and doctor-patient communication (20 points), total 100 points. Three assessors (associate professors or above) independently rated each student using standardized checklists, with the mean score used for analysis. Assessors were blinded to group allocation.

Questionnaire survey

An anonymous questionnaire assessed students’ perceptions of teaching effectiveness immediately after the intervention. The instrument evaluated four dimensions: learning interest stimulation, clinical thinking cultivation, team collaboration enhancement, and communication skills improvement, using dichotomous (yes/no) responses. Overall satisfaction with the teaching model was measured using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very dissatisfied to 5 = very satisfied). The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study (see Additional file 1 for the English version) and was pilot-tested for clarity and internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.86). Content validity was established through review by three senior medical educators, who evaluated each item for relevance, clarity, and comprehensiveness. Items were retained only when endorsed as “essential” or “useful” by at least two of the three experts. Construct validity was supported by the instrument’s ability to discriminate between groups, as demonstrated in Table 2 [21]. All 112 distributed questionnaires were returned and valid (100% response rate).

Statistical analysis

Sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). Based on a pilot study (n = 20 per group) showing an effect size of 0.65 for the primary outcome (theoretical examination scores), with α = 0.05 (two-tailed) and power = 0.90, the required sample size was 50 per group. Accounting for 10% potential attrition, 56 participants per group were enrolled.

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous data meeting normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent samples t-tests. Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-tailed). Effect sizes for primary outcomes were calculated using Cohen’s d (0.2 = small, 0.5 = medium, 0.8 = large).

Results

Theoretical examination performance

Students in the experimental group achieved significantly higher theoretical examination scores (87.15 ± 5.34) compared to the control group (81.23 ± 6.12), with a mean difference of 5.92 points (95% confidence interval [CI]: 3.49–8.35, t = 3.41, P = 0.001). The effect size (Cohen’s d) was 1.03, indicating a large practical significance.

Clinical skills assessment

The experimental group demonstrated superior clinical skills performance, with mean OSCE scores of 86.42 ± 5.68 versus 81.08 ± 5.95 in the control group (mean difference 5.34 points, 95% CI: 2.67–8.01, t = 2.89, P = 0.005, Cohen’s d = 0.91).

Subdomain analysis revealed that experimental group students outperformed controls in history taking (17.82 ± 1.56 vs. 16.94 ± 1.78, t = 2.43, P = 0.017), diagnostic test interpretation (17.65 ± 1.72 vs. 16.51 ± 1.93, t = 2.57, P = 0.012), treatment planning (17.93 ± 1.48 vs. 16.32 ± 1.85, t = 3.25, P = 0.002), and doctor-patient communication (17.58 ± 1.63 vs. 16.21 ± 1.89, t = 2.71, P = 0.008). Physical examination scores did not differ significantly between groups (P > 0.05). Results are summarized in Table 3.

Questionnaire survey results

The experimental group showed significantly higher endorsement rates across all evaluated dimensions (Table 2). For learning interest stimulation, 94.6% of experimental group students responded affirmatively compared to 78.6% in the control group (χ²=6.42, P = 0.011). Clinical thinking cultivation was recognized by 96.4% versus 76.8% (χ²=9.52, P = 0.002). Team collaboration enhancement was reported by 92.9% versus 71.4% (χ²=9.05, P = 0.003). Communication skills improvement was acknowledged by 94.6% versus 69.6% (χ²=12.37, P < 0.001).

Overall satisfaction (combining “very satisfied” and “satisfied” responses) was 94.6% (53/56) in the experimental group, significantly exceeding the 76.8% (43/56) in the control group (χ²=7.42, P = 0.006).

Discussion

This randomized controlled study provides robust evidence supporting the integration of CBL with MDT mode in liver cancer clinical teaching. Students exposed to the CBL-MDT approach demonstrated significantly superior performance in both theoretical knowledge acquisition and clinical skills application, with large effect sizes indicating substantial practical significance. Furthermore, the intervention positively influenced learners’ perceptions across multiple competency domains, including learning motivation, clinical reasoning, teamwork, and communication.

Interpretation of findings and comparison with previous research

The observed improvements in knowledge outcomes align with previous smaller-scale studies. Song et al. [17] reported enhanced understanding of HCC treatment strategies among 40 students using MDT-CBL integration. Xie et al. [18] similarly found improved comprehension of comprehensive HCC management in a 60-student sample. Our study extends these findings by demonstrating consistent effects in a substantially larger sample (N = 112) with rigorous methodology including randomization, blinded assessment, and comprehensive outcome measurement.

The superior clinical skills performance in the experimental group, particularly in treatment planning and communication domains, merits attention. Treatment planning requires integration of multiple information sources—tumor characteristics, liver function, patient preferences, and evidence-based guidelines—precisely the cognitive skill cultivated through MDT observation and participation. Communication skills improvement likely reflects the modeling effect of observing expert-p atient interactions and the active engagement required in case presentations and MDT discussions.

Notably, physical examination scores did not differ between groups, suggesting that this fundamental clinical skill may be less influenced by the teaching modality and may require dedicated simulation-based training. This finding underscores the importance of multimodal instructional design in comprehensive clinical education.

Mechanisms underlying educational effectiveness

The educational benefits of CBL-MDT integration can be understood through multiple theoretical lenses. From a constructivist perspective [19], learning occurs most effectively when embedded in authentic contexts. CBL provides authentic clinical scenarios, while MDT offers authentic collaborative problem-solving. Students construct knowledge by grappling with real clinical dilemmas, integrating information from multiple sources, and observing how experts navigate uncertainty and disagreement.

Social cognitive theory [20] emphasizes observational learning—learners acquire cognitive skills by observing models demonstrating those skills. MDT discussions provide powerful modeling of clinical reasoning across disciplines. Students observe how radiologists systematically analyze images, how surgeons weigh operative risks against potential benefits, how oncologists integrate molecular profiling with treatment selection. This multi-model exposure potentially accelerates development of sophisticated clinical reasoning patterns that would require years of clinical experience to acquire through traditional apprenticeship.

The collaborative learning environment inherent in MDT also fosters development of teamwork competencies essential for modern healthcare [22]. Students experience firsthand that optimal cancer care requires pooling of diverse expertise and respectful negotiation of differing perspectives—lessons difficult to convey through didactic instruction alone.

Pedagogical considerations for implementation

Successful implementation of CBL-MDT integration requires attention to several key factors. Case selection is paramount—cases should represent common clinical scenarios while encompassing sufficient complexity to generate meaningful interdisciplinary discussion [23]. Our experience suggests that five well-curated cases spanning the disease spectrum provide adequate coverage without overwhelming students.

Faculty development represents another critical consideration. Instructors must be skilled in facilitating student-centered discussions rather than reverting to didactic teaching [24]. MDT participants need orientation to their educational role, emphasizing explicit articulation of reasoning processes that experienced clinicians often perform automatically. Regular debriefing sessions among teaching faculty help maintain consistency and address emerging challenges.

Institutional support is essential given the resource-intensive nature of MDT teaching. Protected time for faculty participation, scheduling coordination across departments, and recognition of teaching contributions in academic advancement are important enabling factors [25].

Challenges and mitigation strategies

Several implementation challenges warrant consideration. ① Coordination complexity: Engaging multiple specialists simultaneously requires advance planning. Our approach involved scheduling MDT sessions at fixed times (e.g., alternate Thursday afternoons) protected in departmental schedules, and maintaining a core MDT faculty pool with backup arrangements for clinical emergencies. ② Student adaptation: The transition from passive learning to active participation may initially discomfort some students. We addressed this through pre-course orientation explaining the pedagogical rationale, gradual scaffolding of participation expectations, and ongoing faculty support. ③ Case currency: Rapid advances in HCC management require continuous case updating. We implemented quarterly case review sessions where teaching faculty discuss recent literature and modify cases accordingly. ④ Assessment validity: Comprehensive evaluation requires moving beyond traditional testing. Our OSCE approach and multi-dimensional questionnaire provide more nuanced assessment but require additional resources.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this single-center study may limit generalizability to other institutional contexts with different patient populations, faculty expertise, or educational resources. The teaching culture, student demographics, and healthcare system in China may differ from those in other countries. Multi-center replication studies across diverse geographic, cultural, and educational settings would strengthen external validity and confirm the generalizability of our findings [26].

Second, the relatively short follow-up period assessed only immediate learning outcomes. Whether educational benefits translate into sustained clinical competence during residency and independent practice remains unknown. Longitudinal studies tracking participants into clinical practice would address this gap. Future research should incorporate follow-up assessments at 6- to 12-month intervals to evaluate knowledge retention and skill durability [27].

Third, our design comparing combined CBL-MDT against traditional teaching cannot isolate the independent contributions of each component. Factorial designs comparing CBL alone, MDT alone, and combination therapy would provide mechanistic insights.

Fourth, while we attempted to blind assessors, complete blinding was challenging given institutional familiarity, as assessors may have recognized students from previous clinical rotations. However, we implemented several measures to minimize assessment bias: (1) assessors were from departments not directly involved in teaching; (2) all OSCE stations used standardized checklists with pre-defined scoring criteria; (3) each student was assessed independently by three assessors, and the mean score was used for analysis; and (4) assessors were not informed of group allocation. Nonetheless, the potential for residual bias remains a limitation [28].

Fifth, potential confounding by student motivation or prior academic achievement cannot be completely excluded despite randomization. Baseline equivalence on measured variables provides reassurance, but unmeasured confounders remain possible.

Sixth, while the same faculty team taught both groups, the potential for instructor-related variability cannot be completely excluded despite standardized training protocols. Future studies may consider video-recording sessions for fidelity assessment.

Seventh, although the questionnaire demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.86), more comprehensive psychometric validation—including confirmatory factor analysis and test-retest reliability—was not conducted. Future studies should consider using validated instruments such as the Medical Education Teaching Effectiveness Questionnaire (METEQ) or conducting formal validation of our instrument in larger samples [29].

Future research directions include: (1) multi-center randomized trials to establish generalizability [30]; (2) longitudinal cohort studies examining durable effects on clinical performance [31]; (3) qualitative studies exploring student learning processes and mechanisms [32]; (4) development and validation of more sophisticated assessment tools capturing collaborative decision-making competence [33]; (5) investigation of optimal dosing—how many CBL-MDT sessions produce maximum benefit; (6) exploration of technology-enhanced approaches such as virtual MDT simulations to reduce resource requirements [34]; (7) cost-effectiveness analysis to inform resource allocation decisions [35].

Implications for medical education

Our findings have several implications for medical education practice. First, they support incorporation of multidisciplinary collaborative learning into oncology curricula, moving beyond traditional discipline-specific teaching [36]. Second, they suggest that authentic clinical contexts and expert modeling accelerate development of complex clinical reasoning skills. Third, they highlight the value of explicit attention to teamwork and communication competencies alongside biomedical knowledge [37].

For institutions considering implementation, we recommend starting with pilot programs focusing on a single disease (e.g., liver cancer) before expanding to other malignancies [38]. Investment in faculty development and protected faculty time are essential prerequisites [39]. Partnerships with clinical MDT programs can leverage existing clinical conferences for educational purposes with appropriate modification.

Conclusions

This randomized controlled study demonstrates that combining case-based learning with multidisciplinary team teaching significantly enhances theoretical knowledge, clinical skills, and comprehensive competencies in liver cancer education compared to traditional lecture-based instruction. Students exposed to CBL-MDT integration showed superior performance in knowledge acquisition, treatment planning, diagnostic reasoning, and communication skills, with high satisfaction across multiple learning dimensions. While implementation requires coordination and resources, the substantial educational benefits justify broader adoption of this integrated pedagogical approach in oncology training programs. These findings contribute to the growing evidence base supporting innovative, learner-centered instructional strategies in medical education.
