Stability of Non-Hyperbolic Sets
Marco Martens, Liviana Palmisano

Abstract
In families of unfoldings we find codimension-one laminations consisting of maps exibiting an invariant, non-hyperbolic Cantor set containing a Collet–Eckmann point whose orbit is dense in the set. The topology of this Cantor set remains unchanged along the leaves of the lamination, ensuring codimension-one stability. Furthermore, within this lamination we identify a sublamination whose leaves contain maps with infinitely many sinks accumulating on the non-hyperbolic Cantor set containing the Collet–Eckmann point. These results provide a new perspective on the stability of chaotic non-hyperbolic dynamics in two-dimensional systems and reveal surprising coexistence phenomena that highlight the richness and complexity of such dynamics.

Introduction

This paper addresses two central questions in dynamical systems: the existence and stability of dynamically relevant sets. Given a dynamical system
(\mathcal{M},f)
where
\mathcal{M}
is a manifold and
f
an evolution acting on it, one aims to identify the regions where the dynamics effectively take place—that is, the sets on which most orbits spend most of their time. Establishing the existence of such sets can already be a challenging task. Once their existence is established, one naturally seeks to understand whether these sets, together with their dynamical properties, persist under perturbations of the system. Are these sets isolated? Are their properties robust and observable across nearby systems? In other words, are they stable? Let us now make the notion of stability more precise.

Given two dynamical systems
(M,f)
and
(M,g)
with relevant sets
A(f)
and
A(g)
, we say that they are
conjugate
if there exists a homeomorphism
h:A(f)\to A(g)
such that
h\circ f=g\circ h.

One can think of this homeomorphism as a change of coordinates. For instance, if
A(f)
and
A(g)
are Cantor sets, the conjugacy map “rearranges” the pieces of one Cantor set to produce the other. Roughly speaking, this means that the two sets share the same topological structure, they are topologically equivalent.

Within the space of dynamical systems, one can thus consider the class of maps whose relevant sets are topologically equivalent in this sense. The geometry of this class provides information about the
stability
of the corresponding sets. We say that the set
A(f)
is
codimension-
k
stable
if the class of systems conjugate to it forms a codimension-
k
submanifold of the space of dynamical systems. This manifold is referred to as the
manifold of stability
.

The sets exhibiting the strongest form of stability are sinks or closed attracting periodic orbits. Indeed, such sets are codimension-
0
stable: their topology does not change under small perturbations of the system. More interesting examples arise in one-dimensional dynamics, such as
\mathcal{C}^{3}
circle maps. In this class of systems, the topology of the relevant set (in this case, the entire circle) persists on codimension-one manifolds. The set is therefore codimension-
1
stable. This phenomenon follows from one of the most profound results in one-dimensional dynamics, namely Herman’s Theorem, see
[
28
]
; for more modern expositions, see
[
20
]
and
[
30
]
.

The stability of sets in one-dimensional dynamics is by now largely understood, and no major surprises are expected. Obtaining this complete picture required several decades of work and the efforts of many leading mathematicians. Although the main questions have been settled, a number of subtle and intriguing problems remain open.

In contrast, the situation in two dimensions is far richer and less complete. We now turn to the family of systems at the center of our study, namely the Hénon family (see Section
3
for a precise definition). In fact, in this paper we consider a broader class of families, of which the Hénon family is only one example. Specifically, we study
unfoldings of maps with a homoclinic tangency and a transversal intersection
. The precise definition is given in Subsection
2.1
.

Which relevant sets are known to exist in such systems and what do we know about their stability?

As expected, finitely many sinks can exist, and these are codimension-0 stable. Indeed, sinks are hyperbolic sets. The existence of infinitely many sinks has also been established (see
[
33
]
), together with some preliminary results concerning their stability (see
[
23
]
).
More intricate dynamics have likewise been shown to occur. In particular, the existence of a
period-doubling Cantor attractor
—an invariant, non-hyperbolic, minimal set carrying a measure with zero Lyapunov exponent—has been proved (see
[
14
,
31
]
). This set is known to be codimension-
1 stable. Furthermore, the existence of two period-doubling Cantor attractors has been demonstrated, and these are codimension-2 stable (see
[
36
]
).

Even more complex invariant sets, known as
strange attractors
, have been shown to exist in this family. Strange attractors are invariant, non-hyperbolic, and chaotic sets (they carry a measure having positive Lyapunov exponent). The seminal work establishing their existence is
[
3
]
, where a remarkably intricate and ingenious proof was developed. Similar constructions adapted to more general systems and further studies were later given in
[
7
,
4
,
6
,
27
,
32
,
43
,
44
]
. Coexistence of these sets is established in
[
5
]
.
What makes these sets chaotic is the existence of a point satisfying the
Collet–Eckmann condition
, which, roughly speaking, asserts that the derivative of the map grows at least exponentially fast along the orbit of this point (see Definition
14
for a precise formulation).

A natural question then arises: what can be said about the
stability
of strange attractors? Their chaotic nature makes this question extremely challenging. It is known that, in parameter space, there exists a positive Lebesgue measure set of maps exhibiting such attractors; however, their topology changes as the parameter varies. So far, we lack the tools to clearly address this problem.
The present paper aims to provide a partial answer to this question. A new construction and new tools are given. Furthermore, while trying to answer this question, we found new fascinating phenomena, namely the existence of maps with infinitely many sinks accumulating on a set containing a Collet-Eckmann point.

We now summarize our main results, which together describe the existence and stability of non-hyperbolic Cantor sets in families of unfoldings.”

Theorem A establishes the existence of an invariant, non-hyperbolic Cantor set that is codimension-1 stable. In particular, any two maps lying on the same leaf of the lamination are conjugate on their corresponding Cantor sets. In other words, along each leaf of the lamination, the corresponding Cantor sets are topologically conjugate, and their structure remains invariant.

These results are obtained through the introduction of dynamical partitions and the associated directed graphs. The inverse limit of these graphs is homeomorphic to the Cantor set. This combinatorial structure provides effective control over both the topological dynamics along the leaves and the measure-theoretical properties of the Cantor set. These inverse limits and directed graphs were introduced in
[
21
]
. In the present work, we adapt these ideas to the setting of families of maps exhibiting homoclinic tangencies.

To formally establish the codimension-one stability of the Cantor set, we introduce the notion of infinitely renormalizable maps (see Subsection
10.3
). For simplicity, we refer to this process as renormalization, although it is, strictly speaking, a pre-renormalization. In particular, we study the first-return maps to the sets generating the dynamical partition, without performing any rescaling. Since our main focus lies on topological properties, this pre-renormalization suffices for our purposes.
To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first attempt to formulate a generalized renormalization framework with two branches in the setting of two-dimensional systems. A formulation of renormalization for two-dimensional systems using only one branch has been explored in
[
8
,
14
,
16
,
17
,
36
]
, but our construction differs in that it employs two branches and more closely resembles generalized renormalization as used for one-dimensional systems.

Let us go back to analyze the statements in Theorem A. In particular, one should compare these results with those of
[
14
]
, where the period-doubling Cantor attractor was shown to be codimension-1 stable. In that case, the topology of the Cantor set also persists along a codimension-1 leave; however, the constructed leave is very short. Numerical experiments suggest that the period-doubling locus should, in fact, form a long global curve, but proving this remains a challenging open problem. In contrast, our approach allows for the construction of global curves that are genuine manifolds of stability.

We next strengthen these results by showing the existence of a sub-lamination such that the corresponding non-hyperbolic invariant Cantor sets contain a Collet–Eckmann point with a dense orbit in the Cantor set. These sets, which exhibit chaotic dynamics, are likewise codimension-1 stable, i.e. their topology is preserved along the leaves of the lamination. This result represents an initial step toward the study of stability of strange attractors. To the best of our knowledge, no prior results are available in this direction. The proof of the theorem relies on the dynamical structure described above, together with a detailed analytical control along the tangency loci.

In pursuing the stability of chaotic non-hyperbolic sets, we encounter a striking coexistence phenomenon that further illustrates the richness of the dynamics in these systems. We prove the existence of a sub-lamination in which each leaf contains a map with infinitely many sinks of increasing period, accumulating on a non-hyperbolic Cantor set that contains a Collet–Eckmann point. In the classical Newhouse construction, the coexistence of infinitely many sinks is produced by the persistence of homoclinic tangencies. The underlying mechanism relies on the intersection of stable and unstable Cantor sets whose thicknesses exceed one, ensuring that tangencies survive under perturbation.

Our approach is fundamentally different. To prove Theorem C, we avoid using the persistence of tangencies altogether. Instead, we introduce a quantitative condition (
2.2
) that involves only the eigenvalues of a saddle point. This spectral condition replaces the geometric thickness criterion of Newhouse and provides an explicit, verifiable rule for when the construction can be iterated. It is worth noting that, in the Hénon family, the set of maps satisfying the thickness condition and the set satisfying condition (
2.2
) intersect, but neither is contained in the other.

The proof proceeds inductively: starting from a map with a sink and a homoclinic tangency, we show that the sink persists under small parameter changes, while the tangency gives rise to a new tangency and an additional sink. Iterating this mechanism yields infinitely many sinks accumulating on a non-hyperbolic Cantor set containing a Collet–Eckmann point.

The most significant difference between our approach and Newhouse’s original method
[
33
]
lies in the creation of the secondary tangency. In Newhouse’s construction, the persistence of tangencies guarantees that every nearby map possesses a tangency somewhere, but this dependence on the map is highly discontinuous, making the analysis delicate. Our method—inspired by
[
3
,
5
]
—replaces this mechanism with the use of critical points and binding points, thereby avoiding any dependence on the persistence of tangencies.

Thus, our eigenvalue-based and constructive method offers an alternative route to the Newhouse phenomenon and clarifies how coexistence of chaos and stability can occur.

, define a critical point as a tangency between a local stable manifold and an unstable manifold of a periodic point.

Homoclinic tangencies play a crucial topological role in general. However, they are difficult to detect. That is why in varies studies, starting with
[
3
]
, there are notions of approximate critical points which share with homoclinic tangencies the property that expanding vectors are mapped into contracting ones. In particular, in
[
3
]
, critical points are rather tangencies between the unstable manifold of the saddle point and an approximate local stable manifold, not necessarily of the saddle point.
In our situation, similarly the tangent vector of the unstable manifold at the critical point
c_{t,a}
is mapped into the contracting horizontal vector at the critical value
z_{t,a}
.

The crucial fact, which comes from the fundamental property of the approximate critical points (and homoclinic tangencies), is that in a neighborhood, the return map is an Hénon-like map. The local
Hénon behavior is what is important and allows the analysis. From a technical point of view, the notion of critical point itself is less important; the local Hénon behavior is all what is needed.

Another instance where critical points occur but play only a secondary role is in the context of strongly dissipative Hénon maps at the boundary of chaos. These maps have a period doubling Cantor attractor, see
[
14
]
. The Cantor attractors are studied using renormalization zooming in to the so-called
tip
of the Cantor set as in
[
14
]
. Indeed, return maps to neighborhoods of the tip are
Hénon-like maps. A posterior one shows that the stable manifold at the tip is tangent to the direction of the neutral Lyapunov exponent. The tip plays the role of a critical point. However, this fact did not play any role during the renormalization analysis. This is another instance where, from a technical point of view, the notion of critical point is not that important.

6
Newhouse phenomenon

In this section, we identify parameters corresponding to maps that exhibit infinitely many sinks, a phenomenon referred to as the Newhouse phenomenon. Although this result itself is not new, our approach is constructive in nature and departs fundamentally from earlier methods, which in turn enables us to establish a novel and unexpected property of unfoldings.

We start by proving the existence of sinks in two-dimensional unfoldings.

6.1
Cascades of sinks

In this section we are going to prove that, for a two dimensional set of parameters, an unfolding contains maps which have a sink of high period.
Choose

6.2
Newhouse phenomenon: infinitely many sinks

We are now ready to set up an induction procedure to prove the existence of maps having infinitely many sinks. This procedure uses the so-called “Newhouse boxes”. In the first generation, the Newhouse boxes are essentially rectangles in
\mathcal{A}_{n}
whose boundary are defined by the curves of secondary tangencies, see Figure

As a consequence, the family restricted to the Newhouse boxes of second generation have two sinks and it is an unfolding of a new homoclinic tangency. We proceed by induction.
