A spatiotemporal defect-integrated deepfake video detection and forgery algorithm attribution model
Authors: Chang Liu, Xiaoxuan Wang, Yinfu Zhang, Jianli Liu, Zhenhua Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a prominent form of AI-generated content, Deepfake has aroused substantial safety concerns, as they substantially enhance the stealth of fraudulent activities and boost their success rates in real-world scenarios. Most existing research on Deepfake focuses primarily on detection tasks, and fails to fully capture the subtle manipulation traces that are unique to different forgery algorithms left during the synthesis process. Additionally, it is also crucial to attribute the specific generation algorithms of manipulated videos, which can help determine the type of forgery and reduce the negative impact of widespread misinformation dissemination. To fill this gap, this paper proposes a spatiotemporal artifact-aware framework designed to simultaneously accomplish two core tasks: Deepfake video detection and forgery algorithm attribution. Specifically, to comprehensively model the spatiotemporal information of the tampered video, the powerful local feature learning capability of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and the long-range dependency capturing capability of the Transformer are combined to mine the traces left behind by the forgery process from both the local and global information of the input dynamic image sequence. To enhance the model’s ability to capture robust forgery features, frequency-domain filter is innovatively integrated into the convolutional feature, amplifying the subtle traces carried by synthesis algorithms. Furthermore, considering the multi-scale nature of forgery traces, we utilize both middle-layer and deep-layer outputs of the backbone network to separately expose temporal defects at different feature levels. The final prediction result for input face sequences is obtained by fusing the predictions from these two components. The proposed framework is trained under the joint supervision of cross-entropy loss, triplet loss, and hard sample mining loss. This multi-loss optimization strategy effectively adjusts intra-class compactness and inter-class separability, enabling the model to learn more discriminative features for both detection and attribution. Comprehensive experiments on the FaceForensics++ dataset demonstrate that the proposed method achieves 97.86±0.18% detection accuracy and 99.81±0.11% AUC, as well as 98.42±0.15% accuracy for forgery algorithm attribution, outperforming most state-of-the-art approaches on this dataset.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The term “Deepfake” refers to an AI-driven media synthesis technology1. With the rapid iteration of generative algorithms such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), and diffusion models2, Deepfake technology has evolved from crude tampering to highly realistic synthesis3. Its accessibility has also been significantly reduced by the proliferation of user-friendly editing tools, enabling broader adoption among non-expert users4. Today, Deepfake techniques like face swapping, facial attribute modification, and expression reshaping are widely deployed, producing forgeries that are visually indistinguishable from authentic media for the average observer. As a double-edged technology, Deepfake has found legitimate applications in film and television production, game development, education, and other industries–bringing innovation, entertainment, and practical convenience to daily life. However, its misuse carries unimaginable consequences. Particularly in recent years, criminals have increasingly integrated Deepfake technology into telecom fraud, driving the “intelligent upgrading” of traditional telecom scams 5. This not only severely undermines social trust and endangers the property security of the general public but also complicates criminal investigations and case-solving processes, posing significant challenges to law enforcement agencies. A recent incident involving a Hong Kong-based enterprise highlights the growing risks of Deepfake technology in the financial sector: perpetrators used a highly convincing Deepfake video to impersonate the company’s chief financial officer and other staff, leading an employee to mistakenly transfer US25million6. Notably, such cases are not isolated–similar incidents have been reported multiple times globally7. Against this backdrop, the development of reliable Deepfake detection technologies has become a critical challenge that demands urgent breakthroughs to mitigate emerging risks. Defect detection has been widely studied in many fields, including industrial non-destructive testing 8 and visual surface inspection9. Similarly, Deepfake video detection aims to identify subtle manipulation artifacts hidden in video content.

Undoubtedly, videos convey more comprehensive and rich information compared to static images, as they encompasses a complete spatiotemporal dimension that aligns with how humans naturally perceive and process real-world events. This intrinsic consistency with human cognitive patterns makes videos more psychologically credible than individual static images10. Images can be easily manipulated and forged with open-source software, and even non-professional users without specialized technical knowledge can easily master these tools, resulting in low forgery costs and relatively limited deceptive potential due to the lack of dynamic contextual support. In contrast, the continuous flow of time and multi-frame contextual information in videos significantly enhances the public’s trust in their authenticity, as dynamic content is intuitively perceived as more difficult to tamper with without leaving traces. From a practical application perspective, this trust gap means that forged videos have greater potential harm. They are more likely to deceive viewers, spread misinformation, or facilitate malicious activities such as fraud and false information campaigns. Therefore, developing detection technologies that can effectively identify forged videos has become increasingly important and urgent to mitigate these escalating risks11.

Figure 1 illustrates the general generation process of Deepfake. First, an input source video clip is decomposed into individual frames, and a face detection tool is used to locate and crop the facial region in each frame. Next, an encoder-decoder network transforms the source face into the target face, and the synthesized fake facial image is seamlessly blended with the background of the corresponding frame. Finally, all processed frames are reassembled into a cohesive deceptive video clip. During this multi-step synthesis process, two distinct types of forgery artifacts are inevitably introduced: first, spatial artifacts, which are visual irregularities in the spatial domain arising from inherent limitations of the generation model, such as unnatural blending boundaries, patch-level inconsistencies, and localized blurring; second, temporal artifacts, which refer to dynamic defects caused by inconsistencies between consecutive frames during video assembly, primarily manifested as frame-wise incoherence over time (e.g., mismatched facial expressions, unnatural motion transitions). Notably, these artifacts are algorithm-specific, meaning different forgery algorithms leave unique and distinguishable manipulation traces during synthesis.

Specifically, spatial artifacts have long been the focus of numerous existing Deepfake detection methods. The most prevalent approach leverages CNNs to capture these spatial irregularities, capitalizing on the inherent characteristics of Deepfake generation models12–14. However, a key limitation of such frame-wise detection strategies is that each frame of a forged video is often tampered with independently, meaning there is no explicit modeling of correlations between consecutive frames. This independent processing inevitably gives rise to temporal discontinuities in the tampered facial regions, such as subtle flickering, abrupt changes in facial features, or unnatural transitions. Consequently, temporal artifacts have been widely recognized as critical and informative cues for Deepfake detection15–17. Figure 2 presents visually distinguishable examples of temporal discontinuities, all retrieved from publicly available forensic datasets (where t denotes the timestamp within the video sequence). As is apparent, forged videos generated by different Deepfake algorithms exhibit varying degrees of temporal defects between adjacent frames. However, existing temporal-aware Deepfake detection methods still encounter critical limitations: (1) Isolated spatial-temporal processing: Existing approaches often treat spatial artifacts and temporal inconsistencies as isolated cues, lacking effective fusion of their complementary information and thus limiting generalization to unseen forgeries18; (2) Coarse-grained temporal modeling: Many methods use coarse-grained temporal feature extraction, which cannot effectively capture the subtle, dynamic temporal defects induced by advanced Deepfake techniques19.

To address the critical limitations of existing frame-wise detection methods that ignore temporal correlations and overlook temporal artifact cues, as well as the insufficient exploitation of temporal cues in most inter-frame approaches, we propose leveraging the complementary nature of spatial artifacts and temporal incoherence to improve the accuracy of real-versus-forged video discrimination. Specifically, our framework integrates two core modules to target and overcome these limitations: First, to fully exploit spatial-temporal complementary cues, we first integrate a Spatial-Frequency Aware Module that fuses multi-scale spatial features with frequency domain artifacts, ensuring the model can robustly capture frame-level spatial irregularities; Second, to further mitigate the issue of independent frame processing and explicitly model inter-frame correlations, we design a Dynamic Defect Module that captures the temporal evolution of forgery traces in video sequences, effectively quantifying subtle temporal inconsistencies caused by tampering operations and capturing sequence-level temporal inconsistencies. By integrating these two types of discriminative cues, the STDFD model can capture both frame-level spatial irregularities and sequence-level temporal inconsistencies, thereby achieving more robust and reliable Deepfake detection.

Beyond verifying a video’s authenticity, identifying the specific forgery algorithm used to create a manipulated video is equally critical for forensic and risk mitigation purposes. For instance, if a forged video is traced back to Deepfakes or FaceSwap manipulations, it can be determined that the video is a face-swapped forgery, meaning the individual depicted in the video is not the original subject. In contrast, if the video is traced back to Face2Face or NeuralTextures operations, it can be confirmed that the identity of the person in the video is authentic, but elements such as facial expressions or lip movements have been manipulated. Such traceability capability serves a practical purpose: by clarifying the nature of the forgery, it helps contextualize the video’s credibility and limits its spread; in turn, this reduces the deceptive impact of Deepfake content and provides valuable clues for law enforcement and content moderation, thereby mitigating potential harms to individuals, organizations, or public trust. However, existing research primarily focuses on Deepfake model attribution and source attribution20,21, with limited efforts on fine-grained identification of specific forgery algorithms, leaving a critical gap in practical forensics. To address this limitation, our work targets precise algorithm-level forgery identification, filling this gap and enabling more comprehensive Deepfake forensic analysis.

From a technical perspective, detection and forgery algorithm attribution are highly coherent and complementary tasks. Both tasks rely on mining subtle manipulation artifacts left during the video synthesis process. Deepfake detection distinguishes real videos from fake ones by capturing common forgery traces, while algorithm attribution identifies unique traces specific to different generation methods. Since they share the same spatiotemporal and frequency-domain feature foundation, a unified dual-task framework is technically reasonable and conducive to improving the performance and robustness of both tasks.

Related works

Deepfake detection

Several methods employ specially designed network architectures to mine spatial visual artifacts, such as texture distribution discrepancies22, color variations23, and blending boundary24,25. However, with the continuous advancement and refinement of Deepfake technology, these visual artifacts have become increasingly difficult to detect. In addition, compared to image content features, counterfeit traces in the spatial domain are extremely fragile, and neural networks tend to prioritize content features over these subtle forgery cues26. Therefore, blindly using deep models to capture forgery cues does not guarantee effectiveness. Some methods also exploit the semantic artifacts of human faces in Deepfake videos. Due to the lack of global constraints in the face forgery process of generative models, this introduces anomalous manifestations and mismatched details from a global perspective, such as facial region asymmetry27, inconsistent eye colors28, and eyebrow mismatches29. Nevertheless, relying entirely on such semantic information is not always reliable. Once a forged video does not contain the specific semantic artifacts that a method depends on, the performance of that method will degrade significantly. To further capture subtle and fine-grained forgery traces, Zhao et al.30 proposed a multi-attentional network to capture fine-grained information for deepfake detection. However, it neglects frequency and temporal cues, thus limiting its generalization in video scenarios. More recently, more advanced approaches have recognized the critical role of frequency-domain cues and incorporated frequency information for face forgery detection31,32 and other computer vision tasks33. In contrast to spatial visual artifacts, frequency clues can reveal imperceptible statistical discrepancies between real and forged faces, especially in high-frequency components such as textures, edges, and noise patterns that are difficult to conceal. Typical frequency-aware methods extract forgery traces via Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) 16,34, Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)35, or learnable frequency filters36, and integrate them with spatial features to improve detection robustness and generalization. Nevertheless, as validated in recent anti-forensic studies37,38, these frequency-aware detectors could be easily bypassed by designing a specialized frequency-domain regularization or adversarial constraint in the generator loss. Such strategies effectively eliminate the inherent frequency discrepancies between real and forged faces, making frequency-based forensic traces less distinguishable and thus evading detection. In addition, to better capture global dependencies through self-attention mechanisms and enhance the ability to identify subtle global deception cues, multiple studies31,39–41 have explored the application of Visual Transformers (ViT)42 to improve deception detection performance. However, these single-dimensional modeling methods typically result in performance saturation and poor generalization.

In recent years, an increasing number of studies have incorporated the temporal dimension into video tampering detection, recognizing its significance in capturing forgery-related sequential patterns. Among these approaches, the direct application of 3D-CNNs to fake video detection stands out as a straightforward and intuitive method17,19. Nevertheless, 3D-CNNs typically encounter high computational complexity and substantial memory overhead due to their 3D kernel operations, which limits their applicability for real-time deployment on resource-constrained devices. Furthermore, RNNs (Recurrent Neural Networks), as sequence-specific models tailored for processing sequential data, have also been extensively adopted in deepfake video detection tasks15,18,43. While effective for modeling short-term sequential dependencies, these RNN-based methods often struggle to capture long-term temporal relationships and are susceptible to gradient vanishing or exploding problems when handling lengthy video sequences. ST-SBV44 leverages inherent and generalizable spatial-temporal artifacts present in deepfake videos across different generation methods, enabling detectors to learn more universal and effective feature representations. Wang et al.45 proposed a multi-task learning framework that integrates a CNN backbone with a Transformer module46, fusing spatial feature extraction with global contextual modeling. Li et al.47 developed a spatio-temporal generator to synthesize large volumes of high-quality fake videos and trained a self-supervised Transformer-based spatio-temporal capturing framework, addressing the limitation that conventional methods often rely on specific forgery traces and thus exhibit poor generalization. However, these studies still have room for improvement in integrating spatiotemporal information.

Collectively, the above advances have significantly promoted the development of deepfake detection technology, forming a research paradigm that evolves from single spatial artifact mining to multi-dimensional spatiotemporal feature fusion. However, existing methods still face critical limitations: insufficient spatiotemporal feature integration, underutilization of frequency-domain forgery traces, and weak generalization across different forgery algorithms. These gaps directly motivate the work of this paper. To address these shortcomings, we propose a spatiotemporal defect-integrated framework that fully fuses spatial-frequency artifacts and multi-scale temporal defects, effectively overcoming the deficiencies of existing methods and achieving more robust deepfake detection. Meanwhile, advanced techniques including cross-modal knowledge distillation, symmetrical knowledge transfer, quantitative regularization, and causal feature alignment also provide valuable methodological references for robust feature representation and generalization in visual forensic tasks48–51.

Deepfake attribution

In the field of digital forensics and information security, it is of great significance to conduct research on deepfake attribution. This task can effectively identify the generation source of forged content and trace its propagation path, providing vital support for the detection of deepfake-related cases and the confirmation of evidence chains. Currently, research on deepfake attribution is still in its infancy, which is mainly categorized into two typical tasks: model attribution and source attribution.

Deepfake model attribution aims to determine which generative model or framework is used to produce a given fake image or video52. This technique focuses on identifying and tracing the specific generation model, including its architecture, parameters, and training pipeline, as the key evidence for forensic analysis. Typical researches have made significant progress in distinguishing different models and architectures, which has become a major branch of digital forensics. Specifically, some studies20,53 have explored the attribution problem based on generative adversarial network (GAN) models. Considering that the model attribution method designed for GAN images cannot be extended to face-swap Deepfake videos, Jia et al.54 attempted to tackle the problem of fine-grained model attribution for face-swap Deepfake videos. To capture subtle model-specific artifacts, they proposed DMA-STA, a method integrating spatial and temporal attention for frame-level feature extraction and video-level fusion.

In contrast, Deepfake source attribution focuses on analyzing the source, origin, production environment, or specific creator behind a fake sample. It further traces the generation source, such as the device, environment, or specific user that generates the content, rather than only identifying the model type. This technology provides more detailed clues for the entire forgery traceability. Shi et al.21 constructed a new deepfake video dataset from 10 distinct camera devices and proposed a novel training-free deepfake detection framework, which can not only distinguish between real and fake videos but also trace their origins using camera fingerprints.

Compared with model attribution and source attribution, we believe it is more critical to identify the specific manipulation type in deepfake videos, namely the forgery algorithm used to generate such videos.Specifically, model attribution focuses on distinguishing the generative model architecture that produces fake content rather than the manipulation pipeline, while source attribution aims to trace the generation device, environment, or creator of fake samples instead of how the video is tampered.In contrast, the forgery algorithm attribution studied in this paper targets the specific manipulation logic and operational flow such as face swapping and expression reenactment, which directly reveals how the video is forged. This difference is of great significance because it transforms forensic analysis from judging “which model or device generates the content” to analyzing “which algorithm manipulates the content.” The necessity of conducting research on forgery algorithm attribution for deepfake videos lies in its unique forensic value. Although model attribution and source attribution can provide macro-level source information, forgery algorithm attribution can extract detailed features of underlying technical means. Different manipulation algorithms correspond to different malicious purposes and behavioral characteristics. Therefore, tracing the specific forgery algorithm not only helps to understand the attacker’s operating mechanism but also plays an important role in evidence collection, responsibility confirmation, and the design of targeted strategies. Thus, this research field, which has not been fully studied, is of great value and constitutes the core focus of this paper.

Method

The proposed multi-task learning framework, which is designed based on the joint modeling of spatial-frequency artifacts and temporal defects, is abbreviated as STDFD. The comprehensive workflow of this framework is depicted in Figure 3. Concretely, a video clip serves as the input, from which N consecutive frames are extracted. Subsequent to face detection and cropping, a sequence of N consecutive facial images is acquired, denoted as . Subsequently, X is fed sequentially through three modules: the spatial-frequency aware module, the temporal defect extraction module, and the classification module. Specifically, the spatial-frequency aware module is designed to excavate spatial artifacts and tampering traces in each facial image of X, with XceptionNet adopted as its backbone network. This network is composed of four components: the Stem layer, Entry Flow, Middle Flow, and Exit Flow. After a single facial image is processed by the Entry Flow layer, the generated feature map is transformed into the frequency domain via a frequency-domain filter–this step supplements RGB spatial information and thus enables the capture of more subtle forgery cues. Once all N facial images are processed by the spatial-frequency aware module, the output of each network layer forms a sequence feature map across the N facial images, where inter-frame inconsistencies are inherently embedded. We then leverage the advantage of the Transformer architecture in capturing long-range temporal dependencies to excavate temporal defects across the sequence. Considering the multi-scale characteristics of forgery traces, the sequential feature maps output by the middle-layer Entry Flow and deep-layer Exit Flow of XceptionNet are fed into separate Transformer branches, respectively, for extracting temporal defect features at two distinct scales. Finally, the classification module employs a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) head to predict the authenticity of the input video, with the final prediction result derived by fusing the output features of the spatial and temporal modules.

Spatial-frequency aware module

Existing deepfake content fails to faithfully capture the distribution of frequency characteristics during generation55. In other words, deep generative models prioritize the optimization of pixel-level visual realism during facial synthesis, while lacking explicit constraints and modeling capabilities for the inherent frequency distribution and spectral statistical properties of natural faces. Such models tend to suppress or distort high-frequency and mid-frequency components that are imperceptible to the human eyes but highly discriminative for forensic analysis, resulting in consistent and detectable frequency discrepancies between real and forged faces. To enhance sensitivity to subtle manipulations, we design a spatial-frequency aware module that jointly leverages spatial textures and frequency artifacts through a multi-scale extraction process. In detail, the proposed Spatial-Frequency Aware Module is composed of two parallel and complementary branches: the Spatial Feature Branch and the Frequency Feature Branch.

Dynamic defect module

To mitigate the deficiency in temporal feature modeling within current deepfake video detection approaches, the proposed STDFD model incorporates a hybrid CNN-Transformer architecture for collaborative spatiotemporal feature learning. The backbone network extracts hierarchical features at varying depths, yielding two intermediate feature sequences that serve as inputs to the Dynamic Defect Module. The first is the low-level feature sequence output by the Entry Flow, which preserves fine-grained textural details and local noise that are key indicators for identifying subtle spatial-frequency defects. We clarify that low-level features represent pixel residuals, high-frequency artifacts, local noise, and texture inconsistencies, which are widely recognized in image forensics as the most sensitive traces introduced by face forgery34,35. The second is the high-level feature sequence output by the Exit Flow, which encodes high-level semantic abstractions and global contextual information.

The selection of Entry Flow and Exit Flow features for the dual-branch Transformer design follows a multi-scale and complementary principle. The Entry Flow outputs low-level, fine-grained spatial and textural details, including subtle manipulation traces, blending artifacts, and high-frequency inconsistencies that are critical for detecting local tampering. The Exit Flow generates high-level semantic and global structural representations, which capture overall face structure, temporal coherence, and global manipulation patterns that help identify inter-frame inconsistencies and algorithm-specific artifacts. By fusing these two complementary levels of features, the model can capture both local subtle defects and global temporal patterns, leading to more comprehensive and robust detection and attribution.

To comprehensively capture forgery cues across different scales, we design the Dynamic Defect Module with two parallel, complementary branches to process these two feature sequences separately: the Low-Level Temporal Defect Branch (processing ) and the High-Level Semantic Temporal Branch (processing ). This dual-stream strategy ensures the model simultaneously detects subtle pixel-level artifacts and semantic-level temporal inconsistencies.

Loss function

Within the dynamic defect module, the embedding corresponding to the class token () output by the Transformer Encoder from deep features is denoted as e2 . This embedding is utilized to compute both the Triplet Loss and the Hard Sample Mining Loss. Concurrently, the model’s final prediction output is employed to calculate the classification loss.

Triplet Loss Conventional face forgery detection methods typically frame this task as a standard classification problem, employing Cross-Entropy (CE) Loss to supervise the network’s training process. However, training exclusively with CE Loss often fails to learn sufficiently discriminative features, particularly for samples that are inherently similar. This is because CE Loss primarily encourages features from different classes to be separated, without explicitly enforcing intra-class compactness or inter-class dispersion. This challenge is particularly acute when attributing fake samples to their generative algorithms, as different forgery types originating from the same source image often exhibit high visual similarity. This similarity complicates the detector’s decision-making process and renders it highly susceptible to adversarial sample attacks. To address this limitation and further optimize inter-class decision boundaries while accounting for the intra-class distribution characteristics of different categories, we introduce Triplet Loss. This loss function guides the network to learn subtle differences between samples, thereby enhancing its ability to distinguish between most visually similar forgeries in the FF++ dataset.

Triplet Loss is fundamentally a metric learning approach. Its core principle is as follows: for a triplet <a, p, n>, where a (anchor) represents a reference sample, p (positive) denotes a sample from the same class as a, and n (negative) denotes a sample from a different class, the goal of Triplet Loss is to iteratively optimize the feature space. The optimization aims to decrease the distance between the anchor a and the positive sample a, while simultaneously increasing the distance between the anchor a and the negative sample n. Notably, the triplet loss does not directly utilize the label information of the samples. Instead, it constrains the distance relationships among triplets by ensuring that intra-class samples are sufficiently close and inter-class samples are sufficiently distant, thereby enhancing the model’s classification performance.

Triplet samples are generally categorized into three types: easy triplets, semi-hard triplets, and hard triplets. The loss value of an easy triplet is 0; in other words, the condition d(a,n)>d(a,p)+m is satisfied even without training the model. Consequently, if a training batch contains a large number of such easy triplets, offers minimal benefit to the training process, as the model fails to learn discriminative features and its detection performance remains constrained. A semi-hard triplet is defined as a triplet where the distance between the negative sample and the anchor sample exceeds that between the positive sample and the anchor sample, yet the loss value has not yet reached 0. These triplets require further optimization during training to minimize the loss, thereby facilitating network convergence and providing substantial valid statistical information. Hard triplets are the most challenging for the network to learn, mathematically defined as d(a,p)>d(a,n). Training with such samples can significantly enhance network performance, making them particularly effective for detecting highly similar yet distinct samples.

The conventional method for calculating Triplet Loss involves randomly selecting triplet samples from a training batch. A significant drawback of this method is that the randomly selected triplets may predominantly consist of easy triplets, which severely hinders network training. To enhance the model’s ability to distinguish between real and fake classes (for detection) and to differentiate between fake classes generated by various forgery algorithms (for attribution), this work comprehensively considers the relationships between each sample and all other samples. Specifically, it iterates over all possible triplet combinations within each training batch to compute Triplet Loss, thereby ensuring coverage of all triplet types. The margin parameter setting will be detailed in the experimental section.

Hard Sample Mining Loss The aforementioned Triplet Loss assigns equal weight to all triplet combinations; however, certain hard triplets deserve prioritized attention because they correspond to samples that are most prone to causing model misclassification. For hard sample mining, we first identify all triplet combinations within the same training batch. Experiments and evaluation

Experimental dataset and settings

Dataset. The primary dataset employed in our experiments is the FaceForensics++ (FF++) dataset56, which has been extensively utilized in the field of Deepfake video detection owing to its large-scale nature, high-quality content, and diverse forgery techniques. FF++ comprises 1,000 original YouTube videos, each paired with forged versions generated via four distinct manipulation techniques: Deepfakes (DF), Face2Face (F2F), FaceSwap (FS), and NeuralTextures (NT)57–60. Each manipulation technique generates 1,000 forged videos, resulting in a total of 4,000 manipulated videos. Notably, Deepfakes and FaceSwap belong to face-swapping technologies, while Face2Face and NeuralTextures correspond to facial expression replacement technologies. This dataset further provides three levels of compression quality: C0 (lossless original), C23 (high-quality, HQ), and C40 (low-quality, LQ). In our work, we adopt the high-quality FF++ (HQ, C23) subset for all experiments. Following the standard experimental protocol adopted by most existing methods, we split the dataset into training and test sets by selecting the first 720 videos from each manipulation category for training and the remaining 280 videos for testing.

Tasks and Metrics. The proposed framework is designed to address two primary objectives: detecting Deepfake at the video level and tracing the specific algorithms used to generate them. For the binary forgery detection task, the framework outputs two values representing the probabilities that the input video belongs to the real category and the forged category, respectively. We designate this specific model configuration as STDFD-D. In attribution experiments, the framework functions as a multi-class classifier, generating five probability scores that indicate the likelihood of the input video belonging to the real, DF-forged, F2F-forged, FS-forged, and NT-forged categories, respectively. This configuration is referred to as STDFD-A. To benchmark our model against state-of-the-art techniques, we employ two standard evaluation metrics, i.e, AUC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve) and ACC (Accuracy). AUC measures the model’s ability to distinguish between positive and negative samples across different classification thresholds, with values closer to 1 indicating superior performance. ACC quantifies the proportion of correctly classified samples, reflecting the overall accuracy of the model’s predictions. These metrics enable a comprehensive assessment of the model’s detection capability in our experiments.

Implementation Details. XceptionNet serves as the backbone network for our model. Regarding data preprocessing, facial images cropped from the original videos undergo a standardized pipeline: they are first uniformly resized to 256×256, then center-cropped to 224×224, and finally standardized using the mean and variance computed on the ImageNet dataset. The STDFD method is implemented using the PyTorch framework. The training process is conducted on two NVIDIA GeForce GTX 1080 Ti GPUs, with a batch size of 8 and 200 training epochs. For optimization, Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) is adopted, with an initial learning rate of 0.001. The learning rate is decayed by a factor of 0.1 every 30 epochs, and this decay stops once the learning rate drops to 1e-7. in the binary classification task of detecting fake videos, real videos are assigned the label 0 and fake videos are assigned the label 1. For forgery algorithm attribution, the classes are encoded as follows: Real is labeled 0, while DF, F2F, FS, and NT are labeled 1 through 4 respectively. Regarding the temporal modeling configuration, the model employs two Transformer Encoders, each with 6 layers and 16 attention heads. For the two streams, the feature dimensions are set to d_model=728 and d_model=2048, respectively, while the sequence length is fixed at N=10.

Cross-manipulation evaluation

Robust generalization is essential for a high-performance face forgery detector. This section aims to evaluate the framework’s transferability across different manipulation algorithms on the FF++ dataset. Specifically, we assess the model’s performance by training it on one forgery algorithm and then testing it on other categories.

The experimental results are presented in Table 2, where gray backgrounds denote evaluations conducted within the same forgery algorithm. For comparison, we selected several representative works, including XceptionNet, Face X-ray, MultiAtt, and DCL. XceptionNet serves as the baseline for our framework; Face X-ray attempts to detect fusion boundaries resulting from hybrid operations; MultiAtt captures forgery clues from a fine-grained perspective; and DCL is a contrastive learning-based universal forgery detection scheme. Overall, as indicated by the bold values in the table, our STDFD framework achieved the highest AUC score in 9 out of 12 cross-algorithm validation settings, as well as the highest average cross-domain score. While XceptionNet performs well in internal evaluations, it exhibits limited generalization when detecting unseen forgeries. This limitation primarily stems from the model’s tendency to overfit features favorable for current classifications while neglecting common characteristics across different forgery categories. Face X-ray enhances model generalization by reconstructing training samples and forgery boundary mask labels to focus on fusion boundaries in forged images. While it outperformed the STDFD-D framework in three specific cross-validation scenarios (F2F to NT, FS to NT, and NT to FS), its performance significantly lagged behind STDFD in the remaining experiments. MultiAtt excels at distinguishing image authenticity by mining subtle local texture cues inherent to forgery algorithms, yet it suffers from limited generalization. DCL utilizes various data augmentation techniques to generate positive and negative samples, capturing discriminative representations through contrastive learning. However, experimental results indicate that its generalization capability is markedly inferior to that of STDFD. In comparison to these methods, STDFD’s better generalization primarily stems from its spatio-temporal modeling approach. By effectively integrating CNNs and Transformer architectures to simultaneously address local and global forgery artifacts in synthetic videos from the FF++ dataset, and utilizing multi-scale spatio-temporal artifact information for decision-making, STDFD effectively enhances both in-domain detection performance and cross-domain generalization capabilities for the studied forgery algorithms.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged, which are summarized as follows:

First, the proposed model only employs spatiotemporal defect features extracted from video frames for Deepfake detection and forgery algorithm attribution, without integrating multimodal information (e.g., audio and text associated with the video). Therefore, the current model struggles to handle emerging forgery techniques that may involve multimodal information. To address this issue, we will introduce audio and textual information to construct a complete multimodal forensic framework in the future, so as to further improve the detection reliability and generalization ability when facing advanced cross-modal forgery attacks.

Second, the proposed model contains 168M trainable parameters, indicating a relatively large scale. This is mainly attributed to the adoption of a hybrid CNN-Transformer architecture, dual-branch temporal modeling module, and spatial-frequency aware enhancement module. Such a structure significantly improves the feature representation capability for forgery traces but also incurs high resource consumption. Specifically, the model requires about 500 ms to infer a 10-frame video clip, and the resulting high resource consumption limits its real-time efficiency and makes it difficult to deploy directly on edge devices or in real-time monitoring scenarios. Although the model does not adopt a specialized lightweight design in this study, it still has high feasibility for model compression and acceleration, including network pruning, knowledge distillation, and quantization, which can effectively reduce parameters and speed up inference. In future research, we will explore lightweight network design and efficient inference strategies to achieve a better trade-off between detection performance and efficiency. Advanced dynamic multi-objective optimization techniques can be further adopted to balance model accuracy, complexity, and generalization69.

Third, the reliability and robustness of the proposed model may be challenged in complex adversarial environments and unseen generative scenarios. Potential threats include adversarial perturbations, subtle post-processing distortions, unknown forgery types, and emerging diffusion-based Deepfake techniques. Although our model achieves promising performance on standard GAN-based benchmark datasets, its generalization and robustness to unseen generative models still need to be further verified and improved. In future work, we will further explore the universal robustness of the Deepfake detection and attribution model. Specifically, we will design more effective structures and training strategies, and construct large-scale diffusion-based Deepfake datasets to improve the model’s stability and reliability against adversarial attacks, unknown forgery patterns, and emerging generative models, so as to enhance its practical applicability in real-world and secure forensic scenarios.

Conclusion

Compared to single static fake images, the dynamic information inherent in manipulated videos significantly enhances public trust in the content, rendering such videos potentially more harmful. Furthermore, tracing forgery algorithms is crucial for undermining the credibility of malicious fake videos and effectively mitigating the adverse consequences of their misuse. To this end, this study proposes a novel and practical framework for deepfake video detection and forgery algorithm attribution. It aims to simultaneously address both the distinction between real and fake content and the identification of video forgery generation algorithms. By leveraging the complementary strengths of CNNs and Transformer, it fully exploits spatial-frequency artifacts within video frames as well as the dynamic inconsistencies between frames. Experimental results on the FF++ dataset demonstrate that our proposed framework achieves competitive performance in distinguishing real video from Deepfake, and performs comparably or better than most existing mainstream detection models. Moreover, it achieves satisfactory attribution accuracy across four forgery algorithms on the FF++ dataset. This work provides a feasible solution for Deepfake detection and forgery algorithm attribution, and offers valuable insights for advancing research in Deepfake defense, which may have potential implications for safeguarding personal privacy in related scenarios.
