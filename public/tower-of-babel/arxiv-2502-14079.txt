Population Dynamics Control with Partial Observations
Zhou Lu, Y.Jennifer Sun, Zhiyu Zhang

Abstract
We study the problem of controlling population dynamics—a class of linear dynamical systems evolving on the probability simplex—from the perspective of online non-stochastic control. While Golowich et al. (2024) analyzed the fully observable setting, we focus on the more realistic, partially observable case, where only a low-dimensional representation of the state is accessible. In classical non-stochastic control, inputs are set as linear combinations of past disturbances. However, under partial observations, disturbances cannot be directly computed. To address this, Simchowitz et al. (2020) proposed to construct oblivious signals, which are counterfactual observations with zero control, as a substitute. This raises several challenges in our setting: (1) how to construct oblivious signals under simplex constraints, where zero control is infeasible; (2) how to design a sufficiently expressive convex controller parameterization tailored to these signals; and (3) how to enforce the simplex constraint on control when projections may break the convexity of cost functions. Our main contribution is a new controller that achieves the optimal O ~ ​ ( T ) \tilde{O}(\sqrt{T}) regret with respect to a natural class of mixing linear dynamic controllers. To tackle these challenges, we construct signals based on hypothetical observations under a constant control adapted to the simplex domain, and introduce a new controller parameterization that approximates general control policies linear in non-oblivious observations. Furthermore, we employ a novel convex extension surrogate loss, inspired by Lattimore (2024) , to bypass the projection-induced convexity issue.

Introduction

Online non-stochastic control
(
Agarwal et al., 2019
;
Hazan et al., 2020
;
Hazan and Singh, 2022
)
is an emerging framework at the intersection of online learning and control theory. By leveraging techniques from
Online Convex Optimization
(OCO), it enables the design of
no-regret
controllers that go beyond traditional assumptions, such as i.i.d. disturbances and time-invariant cost functions, in optimal control. In this work, we extend this framework to the control of
population dynamics
, a class of
Linear Dynamical Systems
(LDS) evolving on the probability simplex. These systems, which arise in epidemic modeling and evolutionary game theory, are not
strongly stabilizable
—that is, there exists no state-feedback linear controller that can drive the system asymptotically to zero in the absence of disturbances. This violates a key assumption underpinning much of non-stochastic control theory.

Golowich et al. (2024)
recently studied this problem in the
fully observable
setting, where the controller has access to the exact high-dimensional state (i.e., the full population distribution). However, in many practical applications, full observability is unrealistic due to measurement constraints. A more practical setting assumes that only a low-dimensional representation of the state is available—its dimensionality reflecting the number of effective measurements that can be made, as exemplified by the celebrated Kalman filter
(
Kalman, 1960
)
and the rich literature on inverse problems. From a technical perspective, this
partially observable
setting presents a fundamental challenge: the standard approach in non-stochastic control—designing controls as linear combinations of past disturbances
(
Hazan and Singh, 2022
)
—becomes infeasible since past disturbances can no longer be directly computed.

This paper focuses on controlling partially observable population dynamics and introduces new techniques to handle the intricate interplay between partial observability and the simplex constraint. Our contributions include (i) constructing tailored signals as alternatives to incomputable disturbances, (ii) designing a novel convex controller parameterization that approximates control policies linear in non-oblivious observations, and (iii) introducing a convex extension surrogate loss to ensure that projections are applied only during control execution, thereby preserving the convexity in parameter updates.

Technical challenges.

This problem presents unique challenges due to the interplay between population dynamics and partial observability:

•

Memory effects in non-stochastic control:
Unlike OCO, the cost function
c_{t}(y_{t},u_{t})
depends not only on
u_{t}
but on the entire history
(u_{1},\dots,u_{t-1})
through
x_{t}
. Many works assume strong stabilizability
2
2

2

There exists a
“default” controller such that

decays to zero exponentially fast without disturbances.
to mitigate this issue, reducing the problem to an OCO-like setting with logarithmic memory. However, population dynamics are only

•

Partial observability and convexity:
Classical non-stochastic control strategies parameterize

as a linear combination of past disturbances, ensuring convexity. This relies on computing

from state observations, which is impossible under partial observability.

While
Golowich et al. (2024)
and

addressed these issues separately, their combination introduces new difficulties.
Simchowitz et al. (2020)
proposed using counterfactual observations under zero control (termed

This raises two key questions: (i) how to construct suitable signals under the simplex constraint and (ii) how to ensure control feasibility without violating convexity. In particular, if we directly apply the approach of
Simchowitz et al. (2020)
to population dynamics, the resulting
u_{t}
could easily violate the simplex constraint, and a naive projection would introduce nonconvexity to the subsequent optimization problem.

Results and techniques.

We propose two controllers achieving the optimal
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
regret against different comparator classes. A simpler version competes with linear
Markov
controllers of the form

, while a more advanced version handles

1.

Convex extension surrogate loss:
We make a novel use of a convex extension surrogate loss, coupled with the

(

)
, to bypass the simplex constraint on the output of the controller. While similar ideas exist in OCO
(
Cutkosky and Orabona, 2018
)
, our specific use case in partially observable non-stochastic control is more challenging.

2.

New signal construction:
Instead of counterfactual observations under zero control, we construct signals based on a constant control policy
u_{t}=K_{0}y_{t}
, where
K_{0}
is a stochastic matrix. This enables a new convex controller parameterization tailored to the simplex constraint, which requires new techniques beyond the existing unconstrained analysis
(
Simchowitz et al., 2020
)
.

2
Minkowski Extension and Projection

To address constraint issues in population dynamics, we introduce a convex extension technique. A similar approach has been explored in OCO (Online Convex Optimization), as presented in
(
Cutkosky and Orabona, 2018
, Section 4)
. However, we extend this technique to the more complex setting of non-stochastic control.

Motivation.

Our control learning class outputs a “raw control”
\tilde{u}_{t}(M)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
, which is linear in the learning parameters
M
. In population dynamics, this raw control may not necessarily satisfy the constraint
\Delta^{d_{x}}
. A naive approach would be to project
\tilde{u}_{t}
onto
\Delta^{d_{x}}
, but this breaks the convexity: while
c_{t}(y_{t},u_{t})
is convex in
u_{t}
, it becomes non-convex in
M
after projection.

To resolve this, we separate control
updating
from
execution
. Specifically, we use a convex
surrogate loss

e_{t}
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
rather than
c_{t}
, allowing learning updates to occur in an unconstrained space. Projection is applied only at execution time, preserving convexity in parameter updates. This mirrors the philosophy of mirror descent, where updates occur in a dual space before mapping back to the primal space.

Our approach leverages a convex extension technique based on the
Minkowski functional
(also known as the
gauge function
). While the Minkowski functional is a classical concept in convex analysis
(
Rockafellar, 1970
)
, it has recently been used in projection-free online learning
(
Mhammedi, 2022
)
and bandit convex optimization
(
Fokkema et al., 2024
)
. Inspired by
Lattimore (2024)
, we construct a convex extension function using this tool.

Definitions.

Throughout the following definitions, we let
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a convex set satisfying
0\in\mathcal{K}
. We define the Minkowski functional as follows:

Definition 2

(Minkowski functional)
.

The Minkowski functional
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
associated with the set
\mathcal{K}
is defined as
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x)=\inf\{t\geq 1:x\in t\mathcal{K}\}
.

Intuitively,
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x)
is the smallest positive scaling of
\mathcal{K}
required to enclose
x
. Since we are interested in cases where
x\notin\mathcal{K}
, we enforce
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x)\geq 1
. By construction,
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}
is convex, and
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x)=1
if and only if
x\in\mathcal{K}
.
We now define two key constructs: the
Minkowski projection
and the
Minkowski extension
3
3

3

We note that the definition of the Minkowski function is not unique, as shown in
(
Lattimore, 2024
, Section 3.7)
. Here we use a simple form not considered there, but sufficient for our purpose.
.

Definition 3

(Minkowski projection)
.

The Minkowski projection to the set
\mathcal{K}
is a function mapping
\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{K}
, given by
\Proj_{\Delta^{d_{x}}}^{MP}(x)=\frac{x}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x)}
.

Definition 4

(Minkowski extension)
.

For any convex function
f:\mathcal{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
, its Minkowski extension
e_{f}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
with respect to the set
\mathcal{K}
is defined as

\displaystyle e_{f}(x)=\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x)\cdot f\left(\frac{x}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x)}\right).

(1)

Here the notation
\mathcal{K}
is omitted on the LHS for conciseness.

Key Properties

The Minkowski extension satisfies several useful properties, making it well-suited as a surrogate loss function.

Lemma 5

3.1
Our Algorithm

The algorithm
\mathtt{GPC\mathchar 45\relax PO\mathchar 45\relax Simplex}
(
Algorithm

1
) is in broad strokes similar to
\mathtt{GPC\mathchar 45\relax Simplex}

(

)

The general idea is the following: although the comparator class consists of only linear Markov policies
u_{t}=Ky_{t}
, we can’t directly learn this
K
by OCO because
y_{t}
is non-oblivious. Instead, the non-stochastic control approach reparameterizes the control

as a linear combinations of computable oblivious signals

4
Extending to Non-Markov Policy

The results in the previous section hold for Markov linear policies, where the control

u_{t}
depends only on the most recent observation
y_{t}
. However, in partially observable control problems, it is well-known that Markov policies may not be expressive enough to capture the optimal policy, even in the i.i.d. noise setting
(
Başar and Bernhard, 2008
)
. Thus, it is crucial to study whether optimal regret bounds can be achieved for a broader class of non-Markov policies.

4.1
Motivation: The Need for a Richer Policy Class

Prior work by
Simchowitz et al. (2020)
studied control with partial observations under
\ell_{2}
constraints. They noted that a richer policy class is required to capture the
H_{\infty}
control policy in the i.i.d. noise setting. To address this, they introduced the class of stabilizing linear dynamic controller (LDC):

\displaystyle s_{t+1}=A_{\pi}s_{t}+B_{\pi}y_{t},\quad u_{t}=C_{\pi}s_{t}+D_{\pi}y_{t}.

Here, the control is computed as a linear combination of the current observation
y_{t}
and a hidden state
s_{t}
, which evolves according to a linear system driven by past observations. This structure enables the policy to incorporate the full history of observations rather than relying solely on

y_{t}
.

The LDC structure originates from the optimal solution for Linear Quadratic Gaussian (LQG) control with partial observations
(
Başar and Bernhard, 2008
)
. In LQG, the key idea is to estimate an approximate state
\tilde{x}_{t}
using a Kalman filter, then compute the optimal control
u_{t}
, as if
\tilde{x}_{t}
were the true state
x_{t}
. The estimation evolves as

\tilde{x}_{t+1}=A\tilde{x}_{t}+Bu_{t}+D(y_{t+1}-C(A\tilde{x}_{t}+Bu_{t})),

where
D(y_{t+1}-C(A\tilde{x}_{t}+Bu_{t}))
is the correction term to the original system based on the current observation
y_{t+1}
. Since
u_{t}
is linear in
\tilde{x}_{t}
,
\tilde{x}_{t+1}
is simply linear in
\tilde{x}_{t}
and
y_{t+1}
, LDCs are expressive enough to capture classical LQG control with i.i.d. Gaussian noise.

4.2
Extending to Population Dynamics

For population dynamics, the optimal linear control policy under full observability was derived by
Golowich et al. (2024)
. To extend this to partial observability, we need an analogous comparator class to LDCs. Similarly we need to simulate
x_{t}
by
s_{t}
in the stochastic setting, the main difference lies in the original evolution of
x_{t}
:

x_{t+1}=(1-\gamma_{t})(Ax_{t}+Bu_{t})+\gamma_{t}w_{t}.

In contrast to classical control, where noise is Gaussian with zero mean, population dynamics involve disturbances
w_{t}
over the simplex
\Delta^{d_{x}}
with a nonzero mean
v
. A natural choice is to set

s_{t+1}=(1-\gamma_{t})(As_{t}+Bu_{t})+\gamma_{t}v.

Since
\gamma_{t}
and
w_{t}
are independent,
E[x_{t}]=E[s_{t}]
holds by the linearity of expectation when
s_{1}=x_{1}
. Thus, this system is expressive enough to capture optimal population control with stochastic noise. For simplicity, we assume
v=\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{1}_{d}
which is the most natural prior, and our analysis can be generalized to for any
v\in\Delta_{d}
.

Definition 15

(simplex-LDC)
.

Given
d_{x},d_{y}\in\mathbb{N}

\pi=(A_{\pi},B_{\pi},C_{\pi},D_{\pi})
of appropriate dimensions with the additional assumptions that

1.

s_{1}=\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\in\Delta^{d_{x}}
.

2.

\forall s\in\Delta^{d_{x}},y\in\Delta^{d_{y}}
, there holds that the next inner state and control evolved according to the simplex LDC are in the simplex, i.e.

\displaystyle s_{t+1}=(1-\gamma_{t})((1-\alpha)A_{\pi}s_{t}+\alpha B_{\pi}y_{t})+\gamma_{t}\cdot\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\in\Delta^{d_{x}},\quad u_{t}=C_{\pi}s_{t}+D_{\pi}y_{t}\in\Delta^{d_{x}}.

Unlike standard control settings where states decay to zero without noise, population dynamics maintain a unit
\ell_{1}
-norm due to the simplex constraint. Therefore, we replace strong stabilizability with a mixing time assumption.

4.3
Our Algorithm and Result

With the learning policy class defined, our new controller algorithm (deferred to appendix) is a modified version of Algorithm
1
to accommodate the new class of learning policies. Essentially, we raise the dimension of the signals to incorporate the uniform prior.

For any
\pi\in\Pi_{\tau}(\mathcal{L})
and partially observable simplex LDS
\mathcal{L}
, we let
(y_{t}(\pi))_{t}
and
(u_{t}(\pi))_{t}
denote the counterfactual sequences of observations and controls that would have been obtained by playing the policy
\pi
from the beginning of the time. We can now state our main result for controlling partially observable simplex LDS, with respect to the class of
\tau
-mixing simplex-LDC.

Theorem 18

(PO regret (
\tau
-mixing simplex-LDC))
.

Let
d_{x},d_{y},T\in\mathbb{N}
with
d_{y}\leq d_{x}
. Set
H=\lceil 2\tau\lceil\log\frac{4096\tau d_{x}^{7/2}LT^{2}}{\epsilon}\rceil\rceil
,
\eta=1/(LH^{2}d_{x}^{6}\sqrt{T})
. Then for any
\tau>0
, there exists an algorithm with the following policy regret guarantee with respect to
\Pi_{\tau}(\mathcal{L})
:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}(y_{t},u_{t})-\min_{\pi\in\Pi_{\tau}(\mathcal{L})}\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}(y_{t}(\pi),u_{t}(\pi))\leq\tilde{O}\left(L\tau^{4}d_{x}^{8}\sqrt{T}\right),

where
\tilde{O}(\cdot)
hides all universal constants and poly-logarithmic dependence in
T
.

4.4
New Technical Ingredients

Similarly to the previous setting, Theorem
18
depends on several building blocks.
First, we will show that the convex learning policy class (Definition
17
) used in our algorithm approximates
\Pi_{\tau}(\mathcal{L})
well. To incorporate the structure of population dynamics, we design a new LDC analogue

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}x_{t+1}(\pi)\\
s_{t+1}(\pi)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma_{t})A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}\begin{bmatrix}x_{t}(\pi)\\
s_{t}(\pi)\end{bmatrix}+\gamma_{t}\begin{bmatrix}w_{t}\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}y_{t}(\pi)\\
u_{t}(\pi)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}C&0\\
D_{\pi}C&C_{\pi}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{t}(\pi)\\
s_{t}(\pi)\end{bmatrix},

coupled with a change in the control
\tilde{u}_{t}
by appending the prior below
o_{t}
. The additional
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}
term is crucial to proving the desired equivalence, such that after careful decomposition and rewriting of the control, we can use the important matrix identity
X^{n}=Y^{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}X^{i-1}(X-Y)Y^{n-i}
as a bridge.

Next, we will show that the memory mismatch error is small. In this step, because our mixing assumption differs from that in the previous section, a fine-grained analysis is conducted on lower bounding
\alpha
when the mixing time of
A
is large. A key step is to bound the mixing time of
A
by the mixing time of
A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}=\begin{bmatrix}(1-\alpha)A+\alpha BD_{\pi}C&\alpha BC_{\pi}\\
B_{\pi}C&A_{\pi}\end{bmatrix}.
Because they have different dimensions, we need an auxiliary matrix
A_{compare}=\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
B_{\pi}C&A_{\pi}\end{bmatrix}
as a bridge. This requires a new analysis along with a slightly worse dependence on
d_{x}
, which comes from the
\ell_{1}
norm estimation of a key term, which is no longer in
\Delta^{d_{x}}
but lives in
\Delta^{d_{x}}-\Delta^{d_{x}}
.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we investigate the control of population dynamics in a partially observable setting. Despite its practical significance, this problem presents several nontrivial technical challenges due to the interplay between simplex constraints and partial observability.

We develop controller algorithms with
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
regret that perform effectively against both linear Markov controllers and a broader class of non-Markov linear dynamic controllers. Our results build upon the frameworks of
Golowich et al. (2024)
;
Simchowitz et al. (2020)
, incorporating key algorithmic and analytical innovations to address the unique challenges of the problem.

. By Lipschitz condition on

\displaystyle\left|f\left(\frac{u}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(u)}\right)-f\left(\frac{v}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(v)}\right)\right|\leq 4L_{f}\kappa^{3}\|u-v\|_{2}.

In addition, by

, we have that

is
\frac{1}{r}
-Lipschitz.
As a result,

\displaystyle|e(u)-e(v)|

\displaystyle=\left|\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(u)f\left(\frac{u}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(u)}\right)-\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(v)f\left(\frac{v}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(v)}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(u)f\left(\frac{u}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(u)}\right)-\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(u)f\left(\frac{v}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(v)}\right)\right|+\left|\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(u)f\left(\frac{v}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(v)}\right)-\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(v)f\left(\frac{v}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(v)}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 4|\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(u)|\cdot L_{f}\kappa^{3}\|u-v\|_{2}+\left|f\left(\frac{v}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(v)}\right)\right|\cdot\frac{1}{r}\|u-v\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4DL_{f}\kappa^{4}\|u-v\|_{2}+D_{\mathcal{K}}L_{f}\cdot\frac{1}{r}\|u-v\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(4DL_{f}\kappa^{4}+\frac{D_{\mathcal{K}}L_{f}}{r})\|u-v\|_{2},

where the second inequality uses the Lipschitz condition of

and

; the third inequality uses the fact that

Proof of Lemma

Proof.

Let
\kappa=\frac{R}{r}
such that
r\mathbb{B}^{d}\subset\mathcal{K}\subset R\mathbb{B}^{d}
. We discuss three cases of
x_{1},x_{2}
.

Case 1,
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathcal{K}
:

in this case
\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{1})=\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{2})
, and clearly
\|\frac{x_{1}}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{1})}-\frac{x_{2}}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{2})}\|_{2}=\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{2}
.

Case 2,
x_{1},x_{2}\notin\mathcal{K}
:

first we prove the following useful lemma.

Lemma 21
.

A function
f
is
L
-non-expanding, if and only if there exists some constant
C>0
, for any
x_{1},x_{2}
with
\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{2}\leq C
, we have that

\|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})\|_{2}\leq L\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{2}.

Proof.

The only if direction is straightforward. For the if direction, for any
x_{1},x_{2}
with distance
\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{2}=N>0
, denote
n=\lfloor\frac{N}{C}\rfloor+1
, and denote
y_{i}=\frac{i}{n}x_{1}+\frac{n-i}{n}x_{2}
, for
i\in[n-1]
. Then
\|x_{2}-y_{1}\|_{2},\|x_{1}-y_{n-1}\|_{2},\|y_{j}-y_{j+1}\|_{2}
are all bounded by
C
for any

. As a result,
\|f(x_{2})-f(y_{1})\|_{2},\|f(x_{1})-f(y_{n-1})\|_{2},\|f(y_{j})-f(y_{j+1})\|_{2}
are all bounded by
\frac{NL}{n}
for any

. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|f(x_{2})-f(y_{1})\|_{2}+\|f(x_{1})-f(y_{n-1})\|_{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-2}\|f(y_{j})-f(y_{j+1})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq NL=L\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{2}.

∎

Intuitively, Lemma
21
tells us that we only need to consider
x_{1},x_{2}
with a small distance for proving the theorem. In particular, since
r\mathbb{B}^{d}\subset\mathcal{K}
, which means
\|x_{1}\|_{2},\|x_{2}\|_{2}\geq r

\theta\in[0,\pi]

Denote
a=\|\frac{x_{1}}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{1})}\|_{2},b=\|\frac{x_{2}}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{2})}\|_{2}
, where

By the law of cosines, we have that

\left\|\frac{x_{1}}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{1})}-\frac{x_{2}}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{2})}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}-2ab\cos\theta}.

The above expression is convex in
a
for a fixed

and vice versa. Therefore, the derivative is monotone. Thus, it’s upper bounded by

\displaystyle\max\{\sqrt{b^{2}+b^{2}-2b^{2}\cos\theta},\sqrt{R^{2}+b^{2}-2Rb\cos\theta}\}

for any fixed
b

\displaystyle\max\{\sqrt{r^{2}+a^{2}-2ra\cos\theta},\sqrt{a^{2}+a^{2}-2a^{2}\cos\theta}\}

a
. We discuss two cases of
\theta

Case A,

in this case, we have that

which is equivalent to
\cos\theta(a-r\cos\theta)-r\sin^{2}\theta\leq 0
. As a result, for a fixed
a
, the maximum of distance is taken at
b=a
, therefore

\sqrt{r^{2}+a^{2}-2ra\cos\theta}\leq\sqrt{a^{2}+a^{2}-2a^{2}\cos\theta}\leq R\theta,

since
1-\cos\theta=2\sin^{2}\frac{\theta}{2}
, and
\sin\theta\leq\theta
for
\theta\geq 0
.

Case B,
\cos\theta>\frac{r}{a}

in this case, the estimate
\sqrt{r^{2}+a^{2}-2ra\cos\theta}
is too rough, and we will make use of the assumption to obtain a lower bound on

.
Because
\mathcal{K}
is convex, the triangle formed by

(a,0,\cdots,0),(\frac{r^{2}}{a},\frac{r\sqrt{a^{2}-r^{2}}}{a},\cdots,0),(0,0,\cdots,0)

\mathcal{K}
. Slightly abusing the notations, denote

as the first two coordinates for now. We denote

Then we have that

therefore
b\geq\|P\|_{2}=\frac{ra}{\sqrt{a^{2}-r^{2}}\sin\theta+r\cos\theta}
. As a result, inserting
b
into the law of cosines, the distance is at most

\max\{\frac{a^{2}\sin\theta}{\sqrt{a^{2}-r^{2}}\sin\theta+r\cos\theta},\sqrt{2a^{2}(1-\cos\theta)}\}\leq\frac{R^{3}\theta}{r^{2}}.

Now we only need to establish an upper bound on
\theta
. We set
C=\frac{r}{100}
in Lemma
21

, we have that

, and in this regime,

\theta\leq 4\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\leq\frac{2}{r}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{2}.

Combining the two bounds, we have that

\|\frac{x_{1}}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{1})}-\frac{x_{2}}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{2})}\|_{2}\leq\frac{2}{r}\max\{R,\frac{R^{3}}{r^{2}}\}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{2}\leq 2\kappa^{3}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{2}.

Case 3,
x_{1}\in\mathcal{K}
,
x_{2}\notin\mathcal{K}

we denote
a=\|x_{1}\|_{2}\leq R
,
b=\|x_{2}\|_{2}\geq r
and

. We make an argument based on the angle
\theta\in[0,\pi]
between
x_{1},x_{2}
. Without loss of generality, by a coordinate change, we may assume

x_{1}=\frac{x_{1}}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{1})}=(a\cos\theta,a\sin\theta,\cdots,0),\frac{x_{2}}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{2})}=(c,0,\cdots,0).

We have that

\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{2}^{2}=a^{2}+b^{2}-2ab\cos\theta,\|x_{1}-\frac{x_{2}}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{2})}\|_{2}^{2}=a^{2}+c^{2}-2ac\cos\theta.

Clearly, when

,
x_{2}^{\top}(x_{1}-\frac{x_{2}}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{2})})\leq 0
, and
\|\frac{x_{1}}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{1})}-\frac{x_{2}}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(x_{2})}\|_{2}\leq\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{2}
. Therefore we only need to consider the case when

B.2

Unlike

Lemma 26

Suppose that
(c_{t})_{t}

B_{\pi}C&A_{\pi}\end{bmatrix},\quad P_{\pi,\pi_{0}}\begin{bmatrix}C&0\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha B(D_{\pi}K_{0})C&\alpha BC_{\pi}\\
B_{\pi}C&A_{\pi}-I_{d_{x}\times d_{x}}\end{bmatrix},

which yields the nice property

A_{\pi,cl}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{A}_{K_{0}}^{\alpha}&0\\
0&I_{d_{x}\times d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}=P_{\pi,\pi_{0}}\begin{bmatrix}C&0\\
0&I_{d_{x}\times d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}.

Now, we can continue rewriting the previous equation

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{i=1}^{t}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}M^{[i]}\begin{bmatrix}y_{t-i}(\pi_{0})\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}\lambda_{t,j}C_{\pi,cl,u}A_{\pi,cl}^{i-1}P_{\pi,\pi_{0}}\begin{bmatrix}C&0\\
0&I_{d_{x}\times d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{A}_{K_{0}}^{\alpha}&0\\
0&I_{d_{x}\times d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}^{j-i-1}\begin{bmatrix}w_{t-j}\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{t}\lambda_{t,j}C_{\pi,cl,u}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}A_{\pi,cl}^{i-1}\left(A_{\pi,cl}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{A}_{K_{0}}^{\alpha}&0\\
0&I_{d_{x}\times d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}\right)\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{A}_{K_{0}}^{\alpha}&0\\
0&I_{d_{x}\times d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}^{j-i-1}\right)\begin{bmatrix}w_{t-j}\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{t}\lambda_{t,j}C_{\pi,cl,u}\left(A_{\pi,cl}^{j-1}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{A}_{K_{0}}^{\alpha}&0\\
0&I_{d_{x}\times d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}^{j-1}\right)\begin{bmatrix}w_{t-j}\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\lambda_{t,j}C_{\pi,cl,u}\left(A_{\pi,cl}^{j-1}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{A}_{K_{0}}^{\alpha}&0\\
0&I_{d_{x}\times d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}^{j-1}\right)\begin{bmatrix}w_{t-j}\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}.

Combining the two parts, we have the desired equation

\hat{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0}):=M^{[0]}\begin{bmatrix}y_{t}(\pi_{0})\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}M^{[i]}\begin{bmatrix}y_{t-i}(\pi_{0})\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\lambda_{t,i}C_{\pi,cl,u}(A_{\pi,cl})^{i-1}\begin{bmatrix}w_{t-i}\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}=u_{t}(\pi).

Step 2: low truncation error.

The next step is to show that with the mixing time assumption on
A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}
in Definition
16
, the truncation loss
\|\hat{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})-\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})\|_{2}
is small, where

\displaystyle\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0}):=M^{[0]}\begin{bmatrix}y_{t}(\pi_{0})\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}+\sum_{i=1}^{H}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}M^{[i]}\begin{bmatrix}y_{t-i}(\pi_{0})\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}

is the truncated but unprojected control parameterized by
M
(
Eq.

27
) operating on the Markov signals
y_{t}(\pi_{0})
.

By definition, we unfold
\hat{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})
and
\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})
and get

\displaystyle\|\hat{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})-\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i=H+1}^{t}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}M^{[i]}\begin{bmatrix}y_{t-i}(\pi_{0})\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i=H+1}^{t}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}C_{\pi,\mathrm{cl},u}A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}^{i-1}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha B(D_{\pi}-K_{0})&\alpha BC_{\pi}\\
B_{\pi}&A_{\pi}-I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}y_{t-i}(\pi_{0})\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}\right\|.

We have the following equivalent form for any
v_{y}\in\Delta^{d_{y}},v_{x}\in\Delta^{d_{x}}
:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\alpha B(D_{\pi}-K_{0})&\alpha BC_{\pi}\\
B_{\pi}&A_{\pi}-I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}v_{y}\\
v_{x}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{bmatrix}B(D_{\pi}v_{y}+C_{\pi}v_{x})\\
B_{\pi}v_{y}+A_{\pi}v_{x}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}K_{0}v_{y}\\
v_{x}\end{bmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+(1-\alpha)\left(\begin{bmatrix}B(D_{\pi}v_{y}+C_{\pi}v_{x})\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}K_{0}v_{y}\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}\right).

Rewrite as following for simplicity of notation:

\displaystyle v_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}B(D_{\pi}v_{y}+C_{\pi}v_{x})\\
B_{\pi}v_{y}+A_{\pi}v_{x}\end{bmatrix},\quad v_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}K_{0}v_{y}\\
v_{x}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle v_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}B(D_{\pi}v_{y}+C_{\pi}v_{x})\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix},\quad v_{4}=\begin{bmatrix}K_{0}v_{y}\\
\frac{1}{d_{x}}\mathbf{1}_{d_{x}}\end{bmatrix}.

Note that
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4}\in\Delta^{d_{x}}\times\Delta^{d_{x}}
. Recall the mixing-time assumption on
A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}
as in Definition
16
, denote
x^{\star},s^{\star}\in\Delta^{d_{x}}
as the unique pair of vectors such that

\displaystyle t^{\mathrm{mix}}(A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}})=\min_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\left\{t:\quad\forall t^{\prime}\geq t,\sup_{p\in\Delta^{d_{x}},q\in\Delta^{d_{x}}}\|A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}^{t}(p,q)-(x^{\star},s^{\star})\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{4}\right\}\leq\tau.

For simplicity, write
p^{\star}=(x^{\star},s^{\star})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d_{x}}
to be the concatenated vector.
Then, we can further bound
\|\hat{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})-\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})\|
by

\displaystyle\quad\|\hat{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})-\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i=H+1}^{t}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}C_{\pi,\mathrm{cl},u}A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}^{i-1}[(v_{1}-v_{2})+(1-\alpha)(v_{3}-v_{4})]\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\sum_{i=H+1}^{t}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}C_{\pi,\mathrm{cl},u}A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}^{i-1}(v_{1}-v_{2})\right\|+(1-\alpha)\left\|\sum_{i=H+1}^{t}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}C_{\pi,\mathrm{cl},u}A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}^{i-1}(v_{3}-v_{4})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\sum_{i=H+1}^{t}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}C_{\pi,\mathrm{cl},u}A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}^{i-1}(v_{1}-p^{\star})\right\|+\left\|\sum_{i=H+1}^{t}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}C_{\pi,\mathrm{cl},u}A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}^{i-1}(v_{2}-p^{\star})\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+(1-\alpha)\left\|\sum_{i=H+1}^{t}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}C_{\pi,\mathrm{cl},u}A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}^{i-1}(v_{3}-p^{\star})\right\|+(1-\alpha)\left\|\sum_{i=H+1}^{t}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}C_{\pi,\mathrm{cl},u}A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}^{i-1}(v_{4}-p^{\star})\right\|.

By the mixing-time assumption, choice of
H\geq 2\tau\lceil\log\frac{4096\tau d_{x}^{7/2}LT^{2}}{\epsilon}\rceil
and Lemma 18 in
[
Golowich et al., 2024
]
, we can further bound

\displaystyle\|\hat{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})-\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})\|

\displaystyle\leq 4\sum_{i=H}^{\infty}2^{-i/\tau}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{512d_{x}^{7/2}LT^{2}}

Since in the previous step we have shown that
\hat{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})=u_{t}(\pi)
, the above bound directly translates to

\displaystyle\|\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})-u_{t}(\pi)\|

\displaystyle\leq 4\sum_{i=H}^{\infty}2^{-i/\tau}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{512d_{x}^{7/2}LT^{2}}

Step 3: low projection error.

By the previous step and the Lipschitz assumptions of the operators
\phi
and
\pi
, we have that since
u_{t}(\pi)\in\Delta^{d_{x}}
,

\displaystyle\|u_{t}(M;\pi_{0})-u_{t}(\pi)\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\phi^{-1}(\frac{\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0}))}{\pi(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0}))})-\phi^{-1}(\frac{\phi(u_{t}(\pi))}{\pi(\phi(u_{t}(\pi))})\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\left\|\frac{\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0}))}{\pi(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0}))}-\frac{\phi(u_{t}(\pi))}{\pi(\phi(u_{t}(\pi))}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 64d_{x}^{7/2}\cdot\|\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})-u_{t}(\pi)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 64d_{x}^{7/2}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{512d_{x}^{7/2}LT^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{8LT^{2}}.

Note that the observation
y_{t}(M;\pi_{0}),y_{t}(\pi)
can unfold as follows as in
Eq.

11
and thus

\displaystyle\|y_{t}(M;\pi_{0})-y_{t}(\pi)\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}C((1-\alpha)A)^{i-1}\alpha B(u_{t-i}(M;\pi_{0})-u_{t-i}(\pi))\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{8LT^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{8LT^{2}}\cdot T.

By the Lipschitz assumption on the cost function
c_{t}
in Assumption
1
, we have that

\displaystyle\quad\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}(y_{t}(M;\pi_{0}),u_{t}(M;\pi_{0}))-\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}(y_{t}(\pi),u_{t}(\pi))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}|c_{t}^{y}(y_{t}(M;\pi_{0}))-c_{t}^{y}(y_{t}(\pi))|+\sum_{t=1}^{T}|c_{t}^{u}(u_{t}(M;\pi_{0}))-c_{t}^{u}(u_{t}(\pi))|

\displaystyle\leq L\cdot\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|y_{t}(M;\pi_{0})-y_{t}(\pi)\|_{1}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|u_{t}(M;\pi_{0})-u_{t}(\pi)\|_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2LT\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{8LT}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

Thus, we have proved
Eq.

25
. We move on to prove
Eq.

26
.

Proof of
Eq.

26
.

The proof follows similarly to the proof of
Eq.

16
. By the definition of
e_{t}(M)
in
Eq.

6
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}e_{t}(M)-\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}(y_{t}(M;\pi_{0}),u_{t}(M;\pi_{0}))\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})))\cdot c_{t}^{u}\left(\phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;K_{0})))}\right)\right)-c_{t}^{u}(u_{t}(M;\pi_{0}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})))-1)\cdot c_{t}^{u}\left(\phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;K_{0})))}\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}^{u}\left(\phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;K_{0})))}\right)\right)-c_{t}^{u}(u_{t}(M;\pi_{0}))\right|.

To bound the first term, note that

\displaystyle|\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})))-1|

\displaystyle=|\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})))-\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(u_{t}(\pi)))|

\displaystyle\leq 2d_{x}\cdot\|\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})-u_{t}(\pi)\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{256d_{x}^{5/2}LT^{2}},

and therefore by Lipschitz assumption of
c_{t}
,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})))-1)\cdot c_{t}^{u}\left(\phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;K_{0})))}\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{256d_{x}^{5/2}T}.

To bound the second term, we have

\displaystyle\quad\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}^{u}\left(\phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;K_{0})))}\right)\right)-c_{t}^{u}(u_{t}(M;\pi_{0}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq L\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;K_{0})))}\right)-u_{t}(M;\pi_{0})\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2L\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\|\frac{\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;K_{0})))}-\phi(u_{t}(M;\pi_{0}))\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2L\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\|\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0}))-\phi(u_{t}(M;\pi_{0}))\|_{1}}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})))}

\displaystyle\quad+2L\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(1-\frac{1}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})))}\right)\cdot\|\phi(u_{t}(M;\pi_{0}))\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2L\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})-u_{t}(M;\pi_{0})\|_{1}+2L\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;\pi_{0})))-1)

\displaystyle\leq 2LT\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{8LT^{2}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{512d_{x}^{7/2}LT^{2}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{256d_{x}^{5/2}LT^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2T}.

Combining, we have that for all
T\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}e_{t}(M)-\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}(y_{t}(M;\pi_{0}),u_{t}(M;\pi_{0}))\right|\leq\frac{3\varepsilon}{4},

and conclude the proof.
∎

D.2
Bounding the memory mismatch error

Lemma 31

(Memory mismatch error, non-Markov Policy)
.

Suppose that
(c_{t})_{t}
satisfy
Assumption

1
with Lipschitz parameter
L
. Let
\tau,\beta>0
, and suppose that
\Pi_{\tau}(\mathcal{L})
is nonempty. Consider the execution of
\mathtt{Non\mathchar 45\relax Markov\mathchar 45\relax GPC\mathchar 45\relax PO\mathchar 45\relax Simplex}
(
Algorithm

2
) on
\mathcal{L}
. If the iterates
(M_{t}^{[0:H]})_{t\in[T]}
satisfy

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq t\leq T-1}\max_{i\in[H]}\left\|{M_{t}^{[i]}-M_{t+1}^{[i]}}\right\|_{1\to 1}\leq\beta,

(28)

then for each
t\in[T]
, the loss function
\ell_{t}
computed at time step
t
satisfies

\displaystyle|c_{t}(y_{t}(M_{t};K_{0}),u_{t}(M_{t};K_{0}))-c_{t}(y_{t},u_{t})|

\displaystyle\leq O\left(Ld_{x}^{11/2}\beta\log^{2}(1/\beta)H\tau^{2}\right).

Proof.

Fix
t\in[T]
. First, by
Eq.

11
, we have that for all
M\in\mathcal{M}_{d_{x},d_{y},H}^{+}
,

\displaystyle y_{t}-y_{t}(M;K_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}C((1-\alpha)A)^{i-1}\alpha B(u_{t-i}-u_{t-i}(M;K_{0})).

(29)

We consider two cases of the mixing time
\tau_{A}
of
A
. Let
\tau_{A}=t^{\mathrm{mix}}(A)
, and
t_{0}=\lceil\tau_{A}\log(2/\beta)\rceil
.

Case 1:
\tau_{A}\leq 4\tau
.

Let
p_{A}^{\star}
satisfy
p_{A}^{\star}=Ap_{A}^{\star}
be a stationary distribution of
A
, then by adding and subtracting
p_{A}^{\star}
, we have
\forall M\in\mathcal{M}_{d_{x},d_{y},H}^{+}
,

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i=t_{0}+1}^{t-1}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}[(1-\alpha)A]^{i-1}\alpha B(u_{t-i}-u_{t-i}(M;K_{0}))\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\alpha\sum_{i=t_{0}+1}^{t-1}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}[(1-\alpha)A]^{i-1}(Bu_{t-i}-p_{A}^{\star})\right\|_{1}+\left\|\alpha\sum_{i=t_{0}+1}^{t-1}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}[(1-\alpha)A]^{i-1}(Bu_{t-i}(M;K_{0})-p_{A}^{\star})\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\sum_{i=t_{0}+1}^{t-1}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}(1-\alpha)^{i-1}\|A^{i-1}(Bu_{t-i}-p_{A}^{\star})\|_{1}+\alpha\sum_{i=t_{0}+1}^{t-1}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}(1-\alpha)^{i-1}\|A^{i-1}(Bu_{t-i}(M;K_{0})-p_{A}^{\star})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i=t_{0}+1}^{t-1}\frac{1}{2^{\lfloor(i-1)/\tau_{A}\rfloor}}\leq 2\tau_{A}\beta\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{i}}\leq C\tau\beta\log(1/\beta).

Thus, it suffices to show a bound for the first
t_{0}
terms. We have that

\displaystyle\|\tilde{u}_{t-i}-\tilde{u}_{t-i}(M_{t};K_{0})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|(M_{t-i}^{[0]}-M_{t}^{[0]})y_{t-i}(K_{0})\|_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{H}\bar{\lambda}_{t-i,j}\|(M_{t-i}^{[j]}-M_{t}^{[j]})y_{t-i-j}(K_{0})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq(H+1)i\beta\leq 2Hi\beta,

since
y_{t}\in\Delta^{d_{y}}
,
\forall t
, and
\max_{0\leq j\leq H}\|M_{t-i}^{[j]}-M_{t}^{[j]}\|_{1\rightarrow 1}\leq i\beta
by the assumption. To bound the
\|u_{t-i}-u_{t-i}(M_{t};K_{0})\|_{1}
, we note that by the non-expanding condition established in Lemma
20
, we have that

\displaystyle\|u_{t-i}-u_{t-i}(M_{t};K_{0})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{d_{x}}\left\|u_{t-i}-u_{t-i}(M_{t};K_{0})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq Cd_{x}^{7/2}\|\tilde{u}_{t-i}-\tilde{u}_{t-i}(M_{t};K_{0})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq Cd_{x}^{7/2}Hi\beta.

Thus, we can bound
\|y_{t}-y_{t}(M_{t};K_{0})\|_{1}
as the following:

\displaystyle\|y_{t}-y_{t}(M_{t};K_{0})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{t_{0}}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}C[(1-\alpha)A]^{i-1}\alpha B(u_{t-i}-u_{t-i}(M))\right\|_{1}+C\tau\beta\log(1/\beta)

\displaystyle\leq Cd_{x}^{7/2}\beta Ht_{0}^{2}+C\tau\beta\log(1/\beta)

\displaystyle\leq Cd_{x}^{7/2}\beta\log^{2}(1/\beta)H\tau^{2}

for some constant
C
.

Case 2:
\tau_{A}>4\tau
.

Similarly, we will give a lower bound on
\alpha
. Recall that

\tau=t^{\mathrm{mix}}(A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}})=\min_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\left\{t:\quad\forall t^{\prime}\geq t,\sup_{p\in\Delta^{d_{x}},q\in\Delta^{d_{x}}}\|A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}^{t}(p,q)-(x,s)\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{4}\right\},

where
A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}=\begin{bmatrix}(1-\alpha)A+\alpha BD_{\pi}C&\alpha BC_{\pi}\\
B_{\pi}C&A_{\pi}\end{bmatrix}
. Suppose (for the purpose of contradiction) that
\alpha\leq 1/(256d_{x}\tau)
. Denote

A_{compare}=\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
B_{\pi}C&A_{\pi}\end{bmatrix}
. We have that

A_{compare}-A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}=\begin{bmatrix}-\alpha(BD_{\pi}C-A)&\alpha BC_{\pi}\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}.

For any
v_{1},v_{2}\in\Delta^{d_{x}}
, we have that

\displaystyle A_{compare}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{bmatrix}-A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{t}A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}^{t-i}(A_{compare}-A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}})A_{compare}^{i-1}\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{t}A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}^{t-i}\begin{bmatrix}A-BD_{\pi}C&BC_{\pi}\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}A_{compare}^{i-1}\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Notice that
A_{compare}^{i-1}
is always of form
\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
B^{*}&C^{*}\end{bmatrix}
for
i>1
, where
B^{*}+C^{*}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{x},d_{x}}
and both
B^{*},C^{*}
are non-negative, meaning that
A_{compare}^{i-1}
always maps
\Delta^{d_{x}}\times\Delta^{d_{x}}
to itself. Therefore
A_{compare}^{i-1}\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{bmatrix}\in\Delta^{d_{x}}\times\Delta^{d_{x}}
for any
i\geq 1
.

The matrix
\begin{bmatrix}A-BD_{\pi}C&BC_{\pi}\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}
always maps
\Delta^{d_{x}}\times\Delta^{d_{x}}
to
\Delta^{d_{x}}\times\Delta^{d_{x}}-\Delta^{d_{x}}\times\Delta^{d_{x}}
. Because
A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}
also maps
\Delta^{d_{x}}\times\Delta^{d_{x}}
to itself, we have that
A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}^{t-i}
maps
\Delta^{d_{x}}\times\Delta^{d_{x}}-\Delta^{d_{x}}\times\Delta^{d_{x}}
to itself. Combining these three steps, we have that

\|A_{compare}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{bmatrix}-A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}^{t}\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\end{bmatrix}\|_{1}\leq 2\alpha d_{x}t.

Because of our mixing time assumption on
A_{\pi,\mathrm{cl}}
, we have that for the same
x,s
,

\sup_{p\in\Delta^{d_{x}},q\in\Delta^{d_{x}}}\|A_{compare}^{4\tau}(p,q)-(x,s)\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{16}+\frac{1}{16}\leq\frac{1}{4}.

As a result,
t^{\text{mix}}(A_{compare})\leq 4\tau
. However,
\tau_{A}\leq t^{\text{mix}}(A_{compare})
by definition since the first half of
A_{compare}^{t}(p,q)
is exactly
A^{t}p
and therefore
\|A^{4\tau}p-x\|_{1}\leq 1/4
, which leads to a contradiction.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\|y_{t}-y_{t}(M_{t};K_{0})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}(1-\alpha)^{i-1}\|u_{t-i}-u_{t-i}(M_{t};K_{0})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq Cd_{x}^{7/2}H\beta\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}(1-\alpha)^{i-1}\cdot i

\displaystyle\leq Cd_{x}^{11/2}H\beta\tau^{2}.

By Lipschitz condition on
c_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle|c_{t}(y_{t}(M_{t};K_{0}),u_{t}(M_{t};K_{0}))-c_{t}(y_{t},u_{t})|

\displaystyle\leq L(\|y_{t}-y_{t}(M_{t};K_{0})\|_{1})\leq CLd_{x}^{11/2}\beta\log^{2}(1/\beta)H\tau^{2}.

∎

D.3
Wrapping up the proof of Theorem
18

Before proving Theorem
18
, we establish that the loss functions
\ell_{t}
used in
\mathtt{Non\mathchar 45\relax Markov\mathchar 45\relax GPC\mathchar 45\relax PO\mathchar 45\relax Simplex}
are Lipschitz with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\Sigma}
in Lemma
32
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\Sigma}
measures the
\ell_{1}
-norm of
M=M^{[0:H]}
as a flattened vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}(d_{y}+d_{x})(H+1)}
, formally given by

\displaystyle\|M_{t}\|_{\Sigma}:=\sum_{i=0}^{H}\sum_{j\in[d_{x}],k\in[d_{y}+d_{x}]}|(M_{t}^{[i]})_{jk}|.

Lemma 32

(Lipschitzness of
e_{t}
)
.

Suppose that a partially observable simplex LDS
\mathcal{L}
and
H\geq\tau>0
are given, and
\Pi_{\tau}(\mathcal{L})
is nonempty. Fix
K_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{x},d_{y}}
. For each
t\in[T]
, the pseudo loss function
e_{t}
(as defined on Line
8
of
Algorithm

2
is
O(Ld_{x}^{11/2}\tau)
-Lipschitz with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\Sigma}
in
\mathcal{M}_{d_{x},d_{y},H}^{+}
.

Proof.

Since
e_{t}
(Line
8
of
Algorithm

2
) is a composite of many functions, we analyze the Lipschitz condition of each of its components. First, we show that the
M\mapsto y_{t}(M;K_{0})
and
M\mapsto\tilde{u}_{t}(M;K_{0})
are Lipschitz in
M
on
\mathcal{M}_{d_{x},d_{y},H}^{+}
.
In particular, fix
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{d_{x},d_{y},H}^{+}
, we will show that

\displaystyle\|y_{t}(M_{1};K_{0})-y_{t}(M_{2};K_{0})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq O(d_{x}^{7/2}\tau)\|M_{1}-M_{2}\|_{\Sigma},

\displaystyle\|\tilde{u}_{t}(M_{1};K_{0})-\tilde{u}_{t}(M_{2};K_{0})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{d_{x}}\|M_{1}-M_{2}\|_{\Sigma}.

Expanding
\tilde{u_{t}}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\tilde{u}_{t}(M_{1};K_{0})-\tilde{u}_{t}(M_{2};K_{0})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{d_{x}}\|\tilde{u}_{t}(M_{1};K_{0})-\tilde{u}_{t}(M_{2};K_{0})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{d_{x}}\left\|(M_{1}^{[0]}-M_{2}^{[0]})y_{t}(K_{0})+\sum_{i=1}^{H}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}(M_{1}^{[i]}-M_{2}^{[i]})y_{t-i}(K_{0})\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{d_{x}}\max_{0\leq i\leq H}\|M_{1}^{[i]}-M_{2}^{[i]}\|_{1\rightarrow 1}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{d_{x}}\|M_{1}-M_{2}\|_{\Sigma},

where the second inequality follows from the fact that
\ell_{2}
norm is bounded by
\ell_{1}
norm. By Lemma
9
, we have

\displaystyle\|u_{t}(M_{1};K_{0})-u_{t}(M_{2};K_{0})\|_{1}\leq O(d_{x}^{7/2})\|M_{1}-M_{2}\|_{\Sigma}.

Expanding
y_{t}
, we have that

\displaystyle\|y_{t}(M_{1};K_{0})-y_{t}(M_{2};K_{0})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\bar{\lambda}_{t,i}C[(1-\alpha)A]^{i-1}\alpha B(u_{t}(M_{1})-u_{t}(M_{2}))\right\|_{1}.

We consider two cases, depending on the mixing time
\tau_{A}:=t^{\mathsf{mix}}(A)
of
A
.

Case 1:

\tau_{A}\leq 4\tau
. Let the stationary distribution of
A
be denoted
p^{\star}\in\Delta^{d_{x}}
. Then by Lemma 22 in
[
Golowich et al., 2024
]
,
\forall i\in\mathbb{N}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\langle\mathbbm{1},v\rangle=0
,
\|A^{i}v\|_{1}\leq 2^{-\lfloor i/\tau_{A}\rfloor}\|v\|_{1}\leq 2^{-\lfloor i/4\tau\rfloor}\|v\|_{1}
. Then,

\displaystyle\|y_{t}(M_{1})-y_{t}(M_{2})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\|CA^{i-1}B(u_{t}(M_{1})-u_{t}(M_{2}))\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}2^{-\lfloor(i-1)/4\tau\rfloor}\|u_{t}(M_{1})-u_{t}(M_{2})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq Cd_{x}^{7/2}\|M_{1}-M_{2}\|_{\Sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}2^{1-\lfloor(i-1)/4\tau\rfloor}

\displaystyle\leq Cd_{x}^{7/2}\tau\|M_{1}-M_{2}\|_{\Sigma}.

Case 2:

\tau_{A}>4\tau
.
We already know from the previous discussion that
\alpha\geq 1/(256d_{x}\tau)

We have

\displaystyle\|y_{t}(M_{1})-y_{t}(M_{2})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}(1-\alpha)^{i-1}\|(u_{t}(M_{1})-u_{t}(M_{2}))\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq Cd_{x}^{7/2}\|M_{1}-M_{2}\|_{\Sigma}\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}(1-1/(256d_{x}\tau))^{i-1}

\displaystyle\leq Cd_{x}^{11/2}\tau\|M_{1}-M_{2}\|_{\Sigma}.

Here we proved that
M\mapsto y_{t}(M;K_{0})
is
O(d_{x}^{11/2}\tau)
-Lipschitz, and
M\mapsto\tilde{u}_{t}(M;K_{0})
is
2\sqrt{d_{x}}
-Lipschitz. Immediately,
c_{t}^{y}(y_{t}(M;K_{0}))
is
O(Ld_{x}^{11/2}\tau)
-Lipschitz on
\mathcal{M}_{d_{x},d_{y},H}^{+}
. It suffices to show that

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;K_{0})))\cdot c_{t}^{u}(\phi^{-1}(\frac{\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;K_{0}))}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;K_{0}))}))

is Lipschitz on
\mathcal{M}_{d_{x},d_{y},H}^{+}
. Since
\phi
is
1
-Lipschitz over
\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}+d_{x}}
, we have that
M\mapsto\phi(\tilde{u}_{t}(M;K_{0}))
is
2\sqrt{d_{x}}
-Lipschitz. By assumption
c_{t}^{u}
is
L
-Lipschitz on
\Delta^{d_{x}}
, and
\phi^{-1}
is
2
-Lipschitz over
\mathcal{K}
, we have
c_{t}^{u}\circ\phi^{-1}
is
O(L)
-Lipschitz on
\mathcal{K}
.

By Lemma
9
,

\displaystyle u\mapsto\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(u)\cdot\left(c_{t}^{u}\circ\phi^{-1}\left(\frac{u}{\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(u)}\right)\right)

is
O(Ld_{x}^{4})
-Lipschitz over
\mathcal{K}
, making
c_{t}^{u}(u_{t}(M;K_{0}))

O(Ld_{x}^{9/2})
-Lipschitz on
\mathcal{M}_{d_{x},d_{y},H}^{+}
. . Combining, we have that
e_{t}
is
O(Ld_{x}^{11/2}\tau)
-Lipschitz on
\mathcal{M}_{d_{x},d_{y},H}^{+}
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

18
.

We are ready to prove
Theorem

18
. It is crucial to check the condition in Lemma
31
. Define

\displaystyle\|M_{t}\|_{\star}:=\max_{0\leq i\leq H}\|M_{t}^{[i]}\|_{1\rightarrow 1}.

It is easy to check that
\|M\|_{\Sigma}\geq\|M\|_{\star}
. With slight abuse of notation, let
\|M\|_{2}
denote the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the flattened vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}(d_{y}+d_{x})(H+1)}
for
M\in\mathcal{M}_{d_{x},d_{y},H}^{+}
. Let
\|\cdot\|_{\Sigma}^{*}
denote the dual norm of
\|\cdot\|_{\Sigma}
. Lemma
32
implies that

Algorithm

2
guarantees

\displaystyle\quad\|M_{t}-M_{t+1}\|_{\star}\leq\|M_{t}-M_{t+1}\|_{\Sigma}\leq_{(1)}\sqrt{d_{x}(d_{y}+d_{x})H}\|M_{t}-M_{t+1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq_{(2)}\eta\sqrt{d_{x}(d_{y}+d_{x})H}\|\partial e_{t}(M_{t})\|_{2}\leq_{(3)}\eta\sqrt{d_{x}(d_{y}+d_{x})H}\|\partial e_{t}(M_{t})\|_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle\leq_{(4)}\eta(d_{x}(d_{y}+d_{x})H)^{3/2}\|\partial e_{t}(M_{t})\|_{\Sigma}^{*}\leq_{(5)}\eta(d_{x}(d_{y}+d_{x})H)^{3/2}Ld_{x}^{11/2}\tau

\displaystyle\leq 3\eta H^{5/2}Ld_{x}^{17/2},

for some constant
C
, where
(1)
and
(3)
follow from
\ell_{1}
-norm-
\ell_{2}
-norm inequality that
\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\|v\|_{2}\leq\|v\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{n}\|v\|_{2}
;
(2)
follows from the update in Line
9
of
Algorithm

2
;

(4)
follows from that
\|M\|_{\Sigma}\leq d_{x}d_{y}H\|M\|_{\Sigma}^{*}
, and
(5)
follows from Lemma
27
, the assumption that
d_{x}\geq d_{y}
, and that
H\geq\tau
.

Moreover, by the standard regret bound of OGD (Theorem 3.1 in
Hazan et al. [2016]
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}e_{t}(M_{t})-\min_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}e_{t}(M)\leq O\left(\frac{D^{2}}{\eta}+(Ld_{x}^{11/2}\tau)^{2}\eta T\right),

where
D
denotes the diameter of
\mathcal{M}_{d_{x},d_{y},H}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\Sigma}
, which is bounded in this case by

\displaystyle D=\sup_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{H}\sum_{j\in[d_{x}],k\in[d_{y}+d_{x}]}|M^{[i]}_{jk}|\right\}\leq 3(d_{y}+d_{x})H.

Take
\eta=1/(LH^{2}d_{x}^{6}\sqrt{T})
and recall that

, then we have

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}(y_{t},u_{t})-\min_{K^{\star}\in\mathcal{K}^{\triangle}_{\tau}(\mathcal{L})}\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}(y_{t}(K^{\star}),u_{t}(K^{\star}))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}(y_{t},u_{t})-\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}(y_{t}(M_{t};K_{0}),u_{t}(M_{t};K_{0}))+\sum_{t=1}^{T}c_{t}(y_{t}(M_{t};K_{0}),u_{t}(M_{t};K_{0}))-\sum_{t=1}^{T}e_{t}(M_{t})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{t=1}^{T}e_{t}(M_{t})-\min_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}e_{t}(M)+1

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}e_{t}(M_{t})-\min_{M\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}e_{t}(M)+\tilde{O}\left(\eta H^{11/2}L^{2}d_{x}^{14}T\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{O}\left(\frac{d_{x}^{2}H^{2}}{\eta}+(Ld_{x}^{11/2}\tau)^{2}\eta T+\eta H^{11/2}L^{2}d_{x}^{14}T\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{O}\left(L\tau^{4}d_{x}^{8}\sqrt{T}\right),

where the first inequality follows from Lemma

by taking
\varepsilon=1
; the second inequality follows from Lemma

by taking

and since

from Observation

, and the third inequality follows from OGD regret guarantee.
∎
