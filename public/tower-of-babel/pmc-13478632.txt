Enzymatic quorum quenching alters the phyllosphere microbiome and suppresses a bacterial-induced plant disease
Authors: Qian Zhang, Reed Jacobson, Matthew Nelson, Celine Bergonzi, Michael J Sadowsky, Mikael H Elias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial plant pathogens cause hundreds of millions of dollars in annual crop losses in the United States alone, further stressing an already strained global food supply. Numerous bacteria in the phyllosphere use N-acyl homoserine lactone (AHL)-mediated quorum sensing (QS) systems to coordinate behaviors and shape microbial communities. Strategies that interfere with bacterial QS systems employ enzymes to degrade or transform AHLs in a process called quorum quenching (QQ). This interference results in altered microbial community structure and function. We previously isolated and engineered SsoPox, a highly stable AHL-degrading lactonase that interferes with QS. Here, we evaluated the effects of SsoPox-mediated QS disruption on the Zea mays phyllosphere in a Goss’s wilt infection model caused by Clavibacter nebraskensis. In this proof-of-concept work, we found that infection significantly altered the composition and structure of the leaf microbial community. SsoPox QQ lactonase treatment substantially reduced this shift, resulting in a leaf surface community resembling the uninfected controls. qPCR experiments revealed a small, yet significant reduction of pathogen abundance on plant leaves following enzymatic treatment. As a result, the formulated QQ lactonase spray reduced disease severity. This study demonstrates the critical role of microbial signaling in the phyllosphere and highlights the potential of QQ lactonases to control plant diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53522-3.Keywords: Biocontrol, Lactonase, Quorum sensing, Quorum quenching, Corn, Maize, Goss’s wilt, Clavibacter nebraskensisSubject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Microbiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Unprecedented human population growth in the last 50 years is straining world food supplies1. While increasing crop yields and expanding agricultural croplands are two solutions to address worldwide food shortages, these approaches have high environmental costs2 and lead to competition between agricultural and urban land use3. Significant crop losses due to plant diseases are superimposed on these production issues, severely limiting crop yields and food production4.

Over the last 70 years, traditional methods for controlling plant diseases have included breeding for resistance and the use of chemical agents (e.g., bactericides, fungicides, and nematocides)5. While these approaches are often successful, it is estimated that plant diseases caused by fungi, bacteria, viruses, oomycetes, and nematodes still result in an average 10–22% loss to global food production each year6. This loss is largely caused by the evolution of resistance among plant pathogens, enhanced pathogen host range, and environmental factors promoting pathogen growth7,8. Antibiotics are commonly used to combat these diseases, but the extent of their use on crops is not well-documented globally. While it is reported that around 70,000 kg are used on crops per year in the United States9, data in other areas of the world are lacking. The most commonly used antibiotics are oxytetracycline and streptomycin, which effectively suppress plant diseases caused by Xanthomonas spp., Pseudomonas spp., and Erwinia spp. strains10. Combined with antibiotic use in other sectors, this practice is believed to have contributed to resistance among plant and human pathogens11, resulting in increased environmental and health costs. Therefore, new technologies are needed to better suppress crop diseases. While it has been postulated that the majority of plant diseases are caused by fungi12, a large number of bacteria also affect a large variety of crops, including cereal, fiber, fruit, vegetable, and spice crops, together costing billions of dollars in yearly losses worldwide13.

Many plant pathogenic bacteria communicate with each other using small diffusible molecules via quorum sensing (QS) systems to regulate pathogenicity14,15. Generally, QS is also used by bacteria to coordinate cell growth, bioluminescence, motility, virulence, and biofilm production16. This communication is mediated by chemical signal molecules, among the most studied are the acyl-homoserine lactones (AHLs) produced by many Gram-negative bacteria14. Typically, AHLs are comprised of a homoserine lactone moiety and an alkyl chain that vary in chemical structure, with typical lengths ranging from 4 to 14 carbon atoms (C4–C14). Bacterial species specificity in AHL communication is dictated by structural variation in and decorations on the acyl side chain17.

Several enzymes have been found to degrade AHLs and thereby disrupt microbial signaling. Two main classes of degrading enzymes have been described: (i) amidases (or acylases) that hydrolyze the amide bond of the AHL and break it down into the corresponding fatty acid and homoserine lactone18, and (ii) lactonases, that open-up the lactone ring19,20. Lactonases have been isolated from various sources16,21, including bacteria22–24, archaea25–28, fungi29, and mammals30–32.

The degradation of AHLs results in the interference in QS, and this phenomenon has been termed quorum quenching (QQ)33,34. QQ was shown to inhibit numerous microbial behaviors, including biofilm formation and virulence35. More importantly, QQ enzyme’s effects are broad-reaching and can impact taxa other than those that can synthesize and detect AHLs. There have been numerous studies showing that QQ enzymes can impact microbial population dynamics. Animal studies have shown that lactonases can alter microbial populations in the gut microbiomes of zebrafish36 and chickens37. In other settings, reports show QQ enzymes alter microbial populations in membrane bioreactors38,39, and affect biological processes such as biocorrosion40,41. Recent work shows QQ enzymes alter the human dental plaque microbiome and its physiology42,43. Despite the growing body of literature, these intriguing effects of QQ on microbial community structures remain poorly understood.

Previous strategies using QQ to control bacterial plant diseases include the creation of transgenic plants expressing QQ enzymes24,44, the use of lactonase-producing bacteria45,46, encapsulated lactonase enzyme47, and the topical use of purified lactonase enzyme48. One critical parameter to enable enzymatic treatment in ‘real-world’ conditions is enzyme stability. Stable lactonases from several thermophilic microorganisms have been identified and characterized22,23,25,27,49. In particular, the SsoPox lactonase from Saccharolobus solfataricus49(a facultatively anaerobic, hyperthermophilic, acidophilic, and sulfur-metabolizing crenarchaeon) exhibited exceptional properties. The catalytic properties of SsoPox against substrates C6 to 3-oxo-C12-AHL were improved by using protein engineering50, and mutants with resistance to denaturants, detergents, organic solvents, radiation, and proteases were subsequently identified51. The exceptional stability of this enzyme is illustrated by its ability to be immobilized on polyurethane52, silica beads38, coatings40,53, and remain active in crop adjuvants54. This remarkable stability in diverse conditions may enable direct application of SsoPox and other highly stable lactonases to plant surfaces, including leaves, shoots, and roots.

Lactonases were previously reported to alter microbial population structure and have effects beyond the bacteria that directly produce and use AHLs. Here, we evaluated the effects of a formulated QQ lactonase on the Zea mays phyllosphere using a Goss’s wilt infection model caused by Clavibacter nebraskensis (formerly Clavibacter michiganensis subsp. nebraskensis)55. We found that QQ lactonase treatment significantly altered the phyllosphere community structure. While C. nebraskensis is not known to utilize or respond to AHL signals, the observed community change was concomitant with a reduction of Goss’s wilt disease symptoms. This finding could suggest a complex relationship between community structure and C. nebraskensis pathogenicity.

Materials and methods

SsoPox lactonase production and purification

The mutant lactonase used in this study, SsoPox W263I, was previously described50, and is hereafter referred to as SsoPox throughout the manuscript. The protein was overexpressed in Escherichia coli strain BL21 Star (DE3)(Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) containing the pGro7 plasmid (TakaRa Bio, San Jose, CA, USA). The enzyme was produced as described56 using a 75-L fermentation system (New Brunswick Scientific, Edison, NJ, USA) by the University of Minnesota Bioresource Center (BRC). The enzyme was concentrated and its purity was evaluated using SDS-PAGE . The activity was measured against the substrate paraoxon (diethyl 4-nitrophenyl phosphate) in activity buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM CoCl2) as previously described49. The enzyme was then lyophilized under vacuum and stored.

Bacterial strain and maintenance

The Clavibacter nebraskensis strain CIC267, isolated in Minnesota, was obtained from Dr. Carol Ishimaru at the University of Minnesota. The strain was maintained and grown on nutrient broth-yeast extract (NBY) medium57.

Greenhouse study

Corn (Zea mays L.) assays were performed, in triplicate, in a greenhouse with five different treatment groups. Plant growth containers, each containing one maize plant (maize cv. Viking 40−30 UP), were set to 16 h light and 8 hour dark cycles at 20 °C. Maize leaves at the V5 stage were inoculated with C. nebraskensis at 106 CFUs/mL in phosphate buffered saline (PBS, 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4,1.8 mM KH2PO4, pH 7.4) using a modified version of the “prick-sponge” method outlined in Haugrud et al.58. The inoculation tool, a multipurpose sponge and a 4 × 4 grid of 16 pins affixed to opposite ends of a pair of tongs (Fig. S1), was dipped into the 106 CFUs/mL C. nebraskensis inoculum. This concentration is typical for infecting corn, as shown previously reported studies (105–108 CFUs/mL59,60), and is compatible with measured pathogen population in infected corn plants [often 107 CFUs/mL or above60]. Then a single leaf per plant was clamped using the tongs with the sponge on top and pins on the bottom side of the leaf to simultaneously wound and inoculate. Plant leaves that were only inoculated with bacteria and did not receive any other treatments were termed Inoculation Control (IC; n = 6). The inoculation sites on leaves in the Inoculation Control + Buffer (ICB; n = 3) treatment group were allowed to dry for 1 h and subsequently sprayed using a mist spray bottle (Fingertip 2 oz sprayer, Equate, AK, USA) with 200 µL of activity buffer (HEPES). The inoculation sites of corn leaves in the Lactonase Treatment (LT) (n = 6) group were allowed to dry for 1 h and subsequently sprayed with 200 µL of lactonase enzyme SsoPox (at 10 mg/mL). Leaves in two other treatment groups were not inoculated with C. nebraskensis. These groups were the Lactonase Control (LC; n = 3) group, which was treated with 200 µL of lactonase enzyme at 10 mg/mL, and the Buffer Control (BC; n = 3) group, which was treated with 200 µL of PBS. After inoculation, the temperature of the growth chambers was increased to 25 ˚C and remained there for the duration of the study. First infection symptoms appear after one week. After 14 days, 3 treated leaves were selected at random from the IC and LT groups, and all leaves from the other groups (LC, and BC) were photographed (Fig. S2), then all leaves (15 total) were stored at − 20 °C for subsequent microbial DNA extraction. The photographed leaves, as well as the leaves from ICB were used in 16 S sequencing, whereas all leaves were used in qPCR quantification of C. nebraskensis.

Leaf blight quantification

Corn leaf measurements were measured using Fiji ImageJ61 image processing software. The total surface area of each leaf was traced manually and measured via the software polygon tool. Leaf blight surface area of each leaf was measured in the same manner by manually tracing any areas of the leaf that were not green (brown, black, or yellow). The sum of the area of the blight was divided by the total leaf area of each individual leaf, yielding leaf blight percentage. Measurements were assessed for significance at α = 0.05 via a one-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons. Figures were produced using GraphPad Prism 8. Leaf disease was also visually quantified on a 1–10 scale (1, no infection to 10, severely infected) using previously established methods62.

Field study

To further investigate the use of the lactonase enzyme SsoPox to inhibit plant damage due to infection by C. nebraskensis, we conducted a field study at the University of Minnesota Agricultural Experiment Station at the University of Minnesota Saint Paul Campus (44° 59′ 29.2″ N 93°11′ 03.7″ W). Zea mays L. plants were swn into the field plots, approximately 6 rows x 18 plants each. A single leaf per plant at the V5 stage was infected as described above in “Greenhouse Study”: After inoculation, samples were left to air dry for 30 min and were either left untreated in the Inoculation Control group (IC), sprayed at the inoculation site with 200 µL of SsoPox at 10 mg/mL in the Lactonase Treatment group (LT), or treated with control activity buffer (HEPES) containing 10 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA, Akron Bio, USA) in the Inoculation Control + BSA group (IC + BSA). All treatments were done in three different plots (Fig. S3), and each treatment contained four replicates. First infection symptoms appear after one week. The treated corn leaves were collected after 4 weeks and photographed (Fig. S4), and disease level was quantified as described above.

DNA extraction and sequencing

The 15 leaves, three from each group (IC, ICB, LT, LC, and BC), were removed from − 20 °C, and were cut into small pieces and placed into a sterilized jar containing 45 mL of DNA extraction buffer (0.1 M ammonium phosphate, 0.1% hydrolyzed gelatin, and 0.02% Tween 20, pH 8.2) and 5 mL of 5 mm glass beads. The jar was placed on a rocker platform for 30 min, and the liquid was removed and centrifuged at 10,000 ×g for 15 min. The supernatant was discarded, and DNA was extracted from the remaining pellet using PowerSoil® DNA isolation kits (Qiagen, MO BIO Laboratories, CA, USA) through QIAcube Connect (Qiagen, CA, USA), according to manufacturer instructions. One leaf’s DNA from the BC was discarded due to poor quality.

High throughput DNA sequence analysis was used to assess the distribution and relative abundance of endophytic microbiota inhabiting the corn leaf phyllosphere. PCR was used to amplify the V4 region of 16S rRNA, by using primer set 515F (5′-GTG CCA GCM GCC GCG GTA A-3′) and 806R (5′-GGA CTA CHV GGG TWT CTA AT-3′)63. Sequencing was done by the University of Minnesota Genomics Center (UMGC, Minneapolis, MN, USA) using barcoded primers and the dual indexing method64. Amplicons were gel purified, pooled, and paired‒end sequenced at a read length of 300 nucleotides on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Quantification of Clavibacter nebraskensis on corn leaf

To determine the abundance of the C. nebraskensis pathogen in the different corn leaf samples, the extracted DNA described above was evaluated by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) using the CMN_01184 gene present in C. nebraskensis65. The qPCR was performed using the primer set 1184_qF2 (5′-CATTTCCCGATATGGGCGGT-3′) and 1184_qR2 (5′-AGGGTCTTCCTCGGTCAAGT-3′)65 and the StepOnePlus Real time system (Thermo Scientific). The reaction mixture (10 µL) contained 1SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad), 4 µM each forward and reverse primer, and 1 µL of the DNA template. PCR was performed in duplicate for each corn leaf sample, and in triplicate for the standard DNA fragment (2100 to 2106 copies/µL) under the following reaction conditions: 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of 98 °C for 15 s, 61 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 18 s. ROX was used as the passive reference dye and in this study the qPCR efficiency was 99.49% and R2 was 0.999.

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

The SHI7 software program was used to clean and trim the raw sequence data66. In brief, Nextera adaptors were first removed from the raw sequence data, the adaptor-free sequences were assembled and further trimmed to a threshold quality score of Q33. V4 primer sequences and short reads were also removed, and the high-quality sequences reads between 200 and 300 bp were kept. After data denoising, dereplication, and compaction by using the NINJA-OPS program67, the trimmed and high-quality sequence reads were aligned to SILVA database (97% similarity) and picked de novo operational taxonomic units (OTUs) by using Bowtie268,69. To remove the contamination of chloroplast and mitochondria in the phyllosphere microbiome, non-microbiota sequence reads were removed via QIIME70, and data were rarefied to 1000 reads per sample before statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis, Alpha diversity indices, as well as Good’s coverage were calculated using the Shannon index and abundance-based coverage estimate through mothur ver. 1.34.071. Differences in beta diversity were evaluated using among samples analysis of similarity (ANOSIM), which employed Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrices72,73, and principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed to ordinate the variations.

Manuscript editing Large Language Model Claude Sonnet 4.0 (Anthropic, USA) was used to improve the clarity of some portions of the manuscript.

Results and discussion

Lactonase treatment reduced Goss’s wilt and modestly reduced pathogen abundance on leaf surfaces

Lactonases have been previously reported to profoundly affect microbial population structure, with effects extending beyond bacteria that directly produce and use AHLs38,74,75. More specifically, previous reports showed that quorum quenching enzymes can significantly modulate the abundance of Gram-positive bacteria in a community76,77. Based on these observations, we hypothesized that QQ lactonase treatment could modify the phyllosphere population structure. We evaluated this in an infection model in which the pathogen does not depend on AHL signaling, with a disease induced by a Gram-positive bacteria n: Goss’s wilt caused by C. nebraskensis. We also questioned whether AHL community signal disruption would result in changes in disease levels. The lactonase SsoPox was used in this study because of its extreme stability, including protease resistance and environmental resistance49,78.

Initial greenhouse testing was conducted by spray treating infected corn leaves with lactonase and buffer control applications one hour after inoculation. Disease quantification on leaves showed that QQ lactonase treatment significantly alleviated disease symptoms, reducing average blight coverage by less than 2-fold from ~ 24% to ~ 13% (Fig. 1A, Fig. S2). To understand if symptom reduction correlated with reduced C. nebraskensis in the phyllosphere, we determined pathogen abundance on plant leaves using quantitative PCR (qPCR) to compare the treatment groups (IC, ICB, LT, LC, BC). Analysis of corn leaves infected with C. nebraskensis showed that pathogen abundance on plant leaves was reduced by 56.4% following QQ lactonase treatment with Fig. 1B showing a statistically significant reduction in copies of the CMN_1184 gene from ~ 8450 to ~ 3680 copies when comparing the IC and the LT groups. The modest decrease in pathogen abundance may suggest that a reduction in symptoms is caused by the reduction in pathogen abundance. However, it is also possible that other unknown mechanisms are involved.

Lactonase treatment altered specific bacterial taxa relative abundance in leaf surface community

Since previous studies have shown that treatment with lactonases, including SsoPox, alters complex microbial communities38,39,74,79, it is likely that disruption of AHL-based QS affected the entire leaf surface microbiome beyond C. nebraskensis. We investigated potential changes in the population structure of the phyllosphere microbiota by using Illumina MiSeq sequencing (Fig. 1C).

At the family level, sequencing data analysis revealed that Microbacteriaceae was the dominant taxon in both IC groups, which is expected since C. nebraskensis is a member of this family. QQ lactonase treatment resulted in a large and significant reduction in the relative abundance of Microbacteriaceae (from 98.63 to 8.50%; ANOVA, P < 0.05), and a corresponding large increase in Enterobacteriaceae (from 0.47 to 75.53%) and Pseudomonadaceae (from 0.17 to 17.23%). The decrease in the relative abundance of Microbacteriaceae with in the LT group is consistent with the above-mentioned reduction of the abundance of C. nebraskensis seen by qPCR, although the decrease in relative abundance is much larger. This suggests either other Microbacteriaceae species could be present, or that copy numbers of C. nebraskensis CMN_1184 and 16 S genes are not present in equal abundance.

Notably, the LT group’s community structure was most like that of BC group not inoculated with C. nebraskensis. This similarity is evidenced by comparable relative abundances of Microbacteriaceae (8.5% for LT vs. 4.3% for BC), Enterobacteriaceae (73.5% vs. 63.3%) and Pseudomonadaceae (17.2% vs. 18.7%), demonstrating that QQ lactonase treatment can shift the infected corn’s microbial community to resemble that of uninfected leaves.

Previous reports highlighted that bacterial communities on corn leaves are dominated by members of the phyla Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria80. Microbial community analysis done here confirmed this, showing that Proteobacteria comprised 90.90% of the LT and 88.8% of the BC groups. In contrast, the Actinobacteria comprised 9.0% and 6.85%, respectively (Fig. S5). Overall, microbial community analysis demonstrated that C. nebraskensis infection significantly alters leaf microbial communities by largely increasing the relative abundance of Microbacteriaceae on leaf surfaces, while this shift does not occur with QQ lactonase treatment.

Shifts in corn leaf bacterial community structure and diversity

Sequencing analysis revealed significant changes (ANOSIM, R = 0.98 P = 0.0002, IC compared to LT) in alpha diversity following C. nebraskensis inoculation and subsequent lactonase treatment, as measured by Shannon index scores (Fig. 1D; Table 1). Both of the IC and ICB groups showed low Shannon index scores (0.20 and 0.15), consistent with previous reports showing that corn leaf infection leads to reduced phyllosphere diversity81. When corn inoculated with C. nebraskensis was treated with lactonase, the Shannon diversity increased greater than 7-fold to 1.44. Although this increase was dramatic, diversity remained relatively low - below that of uninoculated BC group (2.18).

Principal component analysis (PCoA) of sequencing data revealed significant differences in bacterial beta diversity between the two consolidated groups (inoculation controls comprised of IC + ICB, and non-inoculated controls comprised of BC + LC) and LT (ANOSIM, R = 0.96, P < 0.001, Bonferroni, alpha = 0.017) (Fig. 1E). Significant changes in the leaf surface bacterial community were also observed following corn inoculation with C. nebraskensis (ANOSIM, R = 1.0, P = 0.004, Bonferroni, alpha = 0.017 compared to inoculation controls). Beta diversity also changed significantly when corn was treated with lactonase (ANOSIM, R = 0.89, P = 0.014, Bonferroni, alpha = 0.017 compared to inoculation controls). Importantly, however, no statistically significant differences in beta diversity were found between the LT and the non-inoculated controls groups (R = 0.64, P = 0.028, Bonferroni, alpha = 0.017), further supporting that QQ lactonase treatment after C. nebraskensis inoculation protects against infection-associated microbial community shifts.

Taken together, these results demonstrated that QQ lactonase treatment alters microbial community composition in the corn phyllosphere when leaves are inoculated with C. nebraskensis. Of note, the recurring observation that QQ enzymes alter microbial communities, while not a biocidal action, could trigger the emergence of resistance82. However, contrary to Quorum Sensing Inhibitors (QSI), resistance to enzymatic quenchers is still unknown82.

Lactonase treatment reduced Goss’s wilt in pilot field study

To confirm our observations obtained from growth chamber experiments, we performed a small-scale field study to investigate the ability of SsoPox lactonase to treat Goss’s wilt disease in corn caused by C. nebraskensis under field conditions. Disease levels were scaled from 1 (no infection) to 10 (severely infected), and the affected area of the leaves were quantified using image analysis. The LT group experienced significantly reduced Goss’s wilt disease symptoms from levels 6.50 to 3.08 (Tukey’s HSD, P < 0.05) (Table 2; Fig. 2A–C, Fig. S6), with no significant difference observed between the IC group and the IC + BSA treatment group (Tukey’s HSD, P > 0.05). Image analysis confirmed these observations, showing significant differences between the LT and IC and IC + BSA controls (Fig. 2D, Fig. S6; ANOVA, P < 0.05). IC and IC + BSA groups showed 29% and 26% average leaf blight coverage, respectively, while LT reduced coverage to 11%, representing a >2-fold reduction in disease symptoms.

This >2-fold reduction in symptoms is consistent with our greenhouse results, suggesting that this effect might be translatable to the field. However, the mechanism(s) underlying this symptom reduction remains unclear. Indeed, C. nebraskensis, like many Gram positive bacteria83,84, has not been reported to produce or detect AHLs or any other known QS molecules. It is not the first time that lactonase treatment is shown to affect Gram-positive bacteria74,76,85,86. Yet it is unclear if lactonases like SsoPox directly affect this pathogen via an unknown mechanism, or whether the community re-organization imposed by lactonases is responsible for a change in C. nebraskensis’ ability to infect corn plants. Alternatively, it is also possible that C. nebraskensis could detect and respond to AHLs, as genomes of Clavibacter species contain several homologous copies of the transcriptional activators REC (cd00156) and LuxR_C_like (cd06170), a regulatory two-component system activated by AHLs87. Such ability to respond to AHLs could possibly explain a response to lactonases, since these enzymes can reduce AHLs abundance levels.

Conclusions

Global food demand is increasing dramatically due to population growth and rising per capita income. While compensatory increases in crop production are necessary, they raise concerns about harmful environmental impacts. To minimize these effects while reducing crop losses, innovative and environmentally sustainable technologies are needed to suppress crop diseases. Traditional approaches using chemicals and antibiotics are no longer viable, as their use has demonstrated harmful effects to human health and ecosystem integrity11,88–91.

Here we show that lactonase treatment significantly alters the phyllosphere population structure in a corn infection model. In this model, Goss’s wilt symptoms caused by C. nebraskensis were reduced using a purified, stable lactonase enzyme. Observed symptom reduction in controlled greenhouse conditions and under pilot field conditions were approximately 3-fold. Importantly, modulations of the phyllosphere microbial community structure are concomitant to the reduced symptoms.

Our 16 S sequencing results suggest that lactonase treatment preserved the corn leaf microbial community upon infection: instead of the dramatic increase in the relative abundance of the family Microbacteriaceae (in which the pathogen C. nebraskensis is included) as seen in infected controls, lactonase treated leaf surface communities resembled the healthy plant controls. It remains unclear at this time, however, what the mechanism(s) for the control of C. nebraskensis infection symptoms in corn is (are) and it will require follow-up investigations to unravel these mechanisms. Yet, this study suggests that community changes induced by QQ lactonases are robust, can be used successfully in the field and may potentially be leveraged for the control of bacterial crop disease. More studies using larger-scale field trials across different conditions, dose-response evaluation, and temporal analysis are needed to validate the consistency and reliability of this biocontrol approach before practical implementation.

Currently, the specific mechanism explaining this observation is not elucidated. Whether the QQ lactonase directly affected C. nebraskensis or the induced changes to the phyllosphere community were unfavorable to disease development is unclear. Future work will need to focus on elucidating the precise direct or indirect mechanism of action underlying disease suppression by lactonase treatment.
