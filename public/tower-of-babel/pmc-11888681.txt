Evaluation of the Clinical Outcomes of the Berger-Tenenhaus Approach for Open Reduction and Internal Fixation of Mandibular Fractures
Authors: S Meera Petchiammal, Thiruneelakandan Sambanthan, Adalarasan Subramanian, Murugan Ranganathan, Selvakumar Rajkumar, Bhuvana Laxmi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:The aim of this study was to evaluate the clinical outcomes, such as duration of surgery, ease of surgical access for fixation, injury to branches of the facial nerve, and scar assessment encountered on using the Berger-Tenenhaus approach for open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) of subcondyle, ramus, angle, and body fractures of mandible.Materials and Methods:This was a prospective clinical study. Patients were selected based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. Seventeen patients in the age group above 18 years were included in this study. All patients were treated under general anesthesia using Berger-Tenenhaus approach for ORIF. The surgical outcomes were evaluated with follow-ups conducted at 24 hours, 1 week, 1 month, and 6 months.Results:The results indicated that the average duration of surgery was 34.47 ± 7.383 minutes with good access to fracture sites and inconspicuous scars at the 6th month. There were no cases of facial nerve paralysis.Conclusion:This study concludes that the Berger-Tenenhaus approach is an effective and safe technique for ORIF of mandibular fractures, such as subcondyle, ramus, body, and angle. This approach has less duration of surgery with good access, no morbidity to the facial nerve, and provides cosmetic results.

INTRODUCTION

Mandibular fractures are the most prevalent after nasal bone fractures. Consequently, mandibular fractures pose challenges in functional restoration and also present potential risks to airway management in severe cases. According to the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (ACS NSQIP) database, mandible fractures are the most frequently encountered isolated facial fractures.[1] The main causes of these fractures are road traffic accidents (RTAs), assaults, falls, and other pathologies.[2] In India, the prevalence of mandibular fractures is common in males (21–30 years of age), the major cause being RTAs and treated by open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) (64.8%) and closed reduction (35.2%).[3]

Choosing an appropriate surgical approach stands as the primary and essential step in a surgical procedure to effectively minimize potential complications, such as injury to the facial nerve and conspicuous scar. The selection should be made with careful consideration of key anatomical landmarks, including the facial nerve and parotid gland, and it should provide sufficient exposure of the fracture and facilitate the placement of osteosynthesis materials with minimal invasiveness. A range of surgical incisions have been described in the literature: preauricular, retromandibular, and submandibular methods. However, the risks associated with them are challenging dissection, potential damage to the facial nerve, and parotid gland. The marginal mandibular nerve (MMN) is at high risk due to its varied anatomy and its superficial course. This shows that treating mandibular fractures located between the mandibular angle and condyle is challenging due to the complexity of accessing that area while minimizing the risk of facial nerve injury.

In 2008, Dr. Berger and Dr. Tenenhaus introduced a novel transcutaneous incision for accessing the subcondylar fractures of the mandible. The incision was between the anatomic window of the MMN and buccal nerve (BN) branches of the facial nerve. They have operated on 38 cases and found excellent results without facial nerve injury and with inconspicuous scar.[4] Jacob Thayer, MD, and Brian Mailey, MD, in 2020 adopted this incision and expanded its use by treating the fractures between angle and condyle regions of the mandible.[5] They have arrived at the result that the Berger-Tenenhaus incision is safe, technically simple, and yields excellent outcomes in terms of reduction and fixation of fracture and also esthetics. They stated that this incision is best suitable for treating mandibular fractures occurring at or above the angle level. This study aims to evaluate the clinical outcomes of the Berger-Tenenhaus approach for ORIF of subcondylar, ramus, body, and angle fractures of the mandible focusing on surgical duration, ease of access, facial nerve injury, and scar assessment.

METHODOLOGY

Study design

Patients with fractures in the subcondylar, ramus, angle, or body regions of the mandible who presented to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery, Government Dental College and Hospital, Cuddalore District, were the subjects of this study. Institutional Review Board and Human Institutional Ethical approval were obtained. The entire study was conducted with strict adherence to ethical guidelines. Patients were chosen according to the inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria. An information sheet about the study was provided to each patient, and written informed consent was obtained in the regional language.

PROCEDURE

Preoperative assessment

A thorough clinical and radiological examination was conducted after obtaining standard case history. Medical and anesthetic fitness were obtained. Erich arch bar was placed as part of the preoperative preparation.

Surgical procedure

A novel, gentle curvilinear incision was made 5 mm anterior to the angle and 5 mm above and parallel to the inferior border of the mandible, with a total length of 2 cm, in the zone between MMN and BN, marked using a surgical marker [Figure 1]. Following local anesthetic injection, the skin was incised with a scalpel [Figure 2], and blunt dissection was performed through the subcutaneous fat, superficial musculoaponeurotic system (SMAS), and parotid gland in a cephalad-caudal direction, exposing the masseter muscle and the tendinous pterygomasseteric sling. The subperiosteal plane was then dissected, and a broad elevator was employed to access the mandibular angle, ramus, body, and subcondylar regions [Figure 3]. The fracture site and surrounding periosteum were cleaned and elevated using a Molt periosteal elevator.

Reduction and fixation

After the exposure, the fracture site was reduced and fixed with 2 mm titanium miniplates secured with 2*8 mm titanium miniscrews for mandibular angle, ramus, and body regions. For the subcondyle, 1.5 mm titanium miniplates and 1.5*8 mm titanium miniscrews were used [Figure 4]. Copious saline irrigation was conducted in the fracture site. The incision was closed in layers by 3-0 Vicryl and a running subcuticular stitch superficially by 4-0 Prolene [Figure 5]. If associated fractures were present, they were also anatomically reduced and fixed through an appropriate approach.

Sutured site

Follow-up assessments were conducted at 24 hours, 1 week, 1 month, and 6 months. Sutures were removed on the 7th postoperative day. Parameters, such as duration of surgery and ease of access to the fracture site, were assessed intraoperatively. House-Brackmann facial nerve grading system was used to access the facial nerve function [Figure 6], and scar assessment was conducted by the Vancouver Scar Assessment Scale [Figure 7] at each follow-up.

RESULTS

Information regarding demographics, diagnosis, duration of surgery, facial nerve function, ease of access to the fracture site, and scar assessment total score was inputted into Microsoft Excel and examined with IBM SPSS Statistics (version 20). The level of significance was determined at P < 0.05. This research involved 17 participants whose average age was 41.71 ± 13.93 years, ranging from 23 to 67 years. Most participants were men (88.2%), while only two were women (11.8%). In this study, fractures were mainly caused by RTAs (70.59%), followed by assaults (29.41%). Of 17 patients in this study, three had subcondyle fractures, two had ramus fractures, 10 had angle fractures, and two had body fractures.

The average duration from incision to exposure was 16.00 ± 3.391 minutes, ranging between 10 and 23 minutes. On average, it took about 18.47 ± 6.226 minutes for fixation to closure ranging between 13 and 35 minutes. On average, the total duration of surgery was 34.47 ± 7.383 minutes, varying between 25 and 55 minutes [Table 1]. In 16 patients (94.1%), the ease of access to the fracture site was “Good,” while in one patient (5.9%), it was “Fair,” [Table 1]. House-Brackman Grading Scale revealed all patients retained grade 1 facial nerve function at 1 day, 1 week, 1 month, and 6 months postoperatively [Table 2]. According to the Vancouver Scar Scale, all patients (100%) had score 3 in 1st week. In 1 month, 16 patients (94.1%) had score 2 and one patient had score 1. In 6-month follow-up, 14 (82.4%) patients had score 1 and three (17.6%) patients had score 2. Keloid and hypertrophic scores were not observed in this study. There was a significant statistical difference in wound healing between 1st week and 1st month (P = 0.000*), showing that significant improvements occur in this time frame. There were no statistically significant differences in scar scores between 1 month and 6 months, indicating that there is minimal change after the 1st month [Table 3].

DISCUSSION

Selecting the appropriate surgical technique is crucial, particularly for fractures involving the region between angle and condyle. In extraoral procedures, low-level mandibular fractures are typically managed with submandibular or retromandibular approaches, while the preauricular approach is preferred for high condylar fractures. The retromandibular approach, initially proposed by Hinds and Girotti, includes variations (retro-parotid and trans-parotid approach) that are associated with a significant risk of facial nerve injury (12%–48%).[6] A 3% to 48% risk of facial nerve injury is associated with the preauricular approach and 5% to 48% with the submandibular approach.[78] The trans-masseteric anteroparotid approach reduces facial nerve damage but may increase the risk of parotid fistulas due to blunt dissection of the parotid gland.[9]

The optimal surgical approach should offer straightforward access to the displaced fracture fragments, while also avoiding excessive traction from retractors in order to minimize facial nerve injuries. Batra et al.[10] documented that 88% of the MMN emerges from the inferior border of the parotid gland and runs along the angle in 52% of cases and below in 32% with an average of 1.5 cm from the angle and above the lower border in 16% of cases. Narayanan et al.[11] in 2012 described a zone called “Nerve free window” between the BN and the MMN at the anterior edge of the parotid gland. The Berger-Tenenhaus incision placed using the anatomic window between the BN and the MMN showed good results for condyle, angle, and ramus fractures without any facial nerve injury and with acceptable cosmetic effects. Moreover, this incision is placed in the anterior and central portions of the face which further facilitates easy reflection of tissues, and the exposed area can be easily retracted till the body region of the mandible in complex fractures.

The etiology of fractures in this study was assault (29.41%) and RTAs (70.59%), consistent with earlier studies.[12] This study’s gender distribution showed a male predominance with a ratio of 7.5:1, aligning with a previous study conducted by Yadav et al.[13] Patient’s ages ranged from 23 to 67 years, with a mean age of 41.71 years, reflecting the involvement of adults in outdoor activities.[14] This approach had an average surgery duration of 34.47 minutes, which was significantly shorter than others.[15] It offers a minimal working distance from the incision to the fracture site. It offers good anatomical reduction, easy placement of osteosynthesis devices, and shorter duration of surgery especially to expose angle fracture. No facial nerve branches were encountered during the procedure, and patients did not experience any facial nerve paresthesia or paralysis. This result corresponds with Thayer, who reported no incidence of facial nerve injury in his study. The positioning of the neck after neck extension in the operating table will bring MMN further downward preventing its injury.

All patients’ scars were inconspicuous after 6 months with a satisfactory outcome. This plays a major role in preventing the unsightly scarring in esthetically sensitive area of the face. All participants in this study belonged to the South Indian subset of the Asian population. Minimal postoperative swelling was encountered in all patients. Additionally, there were no instances of wound dehiscence, and trismus and parotid fistulae were observed in this study.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, the Berger-Tenenhaus approach is a technically straightforward, safe, and effective approach for ORIF of mandibular fractures at and above the level of angle region. It offers excellent access to the fracture site, minimal morbidity to the facial nerve, and satisfactory cosmetic outcome with minimal postoperative complications. Hence, the Berger-Tenenhaus incision stands as a versatile approach and can be recommended for ORIF of subcondyle, ramus, angle, and body fractures of the mandible.

Limitations

For high condylar fractures, however, this approach might not be as successful, and its effectiveness depends on the surgeon’s proficiency in handling any procedural difficulties. Future research should focus on its application for high condylar fractures and long-term outcomes to further validate its effectiveness.
