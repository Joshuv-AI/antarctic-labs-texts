Optimizing case mix for per-diem payment of mental disorders based on E-CHAID decision tree analysis
Authors: Yongqi Han, Xin Xiong, Menghan Zhang, Bo Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMental disorders impose a substantial economic burden on patients, their families, and society. Although per-diem payment is commonly used for inpatient mental health services, the current per-diem payment standards for mental disorders remain relatively crude and fail to reflect the differences in actual resource consumption among patients with different characteristics. Therefore, this study analyzed the factors associated with per-diem inpatient costs among patients with mental disorders to provide evidence for the development of a refined case mix–based per-diem payment system suitable for mental health services provided in China.MethodsA total of 25,510 inpatients with mental disorders admitted to two hospitals in Chongqing between 2020 and 2024 were included in this study, and their demographic, clinical, and cost information collected. A univariate analysis and generalized linear model were used to identify the main factors associated with per-diem inpatient costs, and the Exhaustive CHi-squared Automatic Interaction Detection (E-CHAID) decision tree model was applied to construct a case-mix classification model for per-diem payments in mental health services.ResultsThe generalized linear model showed that sex, age, insurance type, discharge mode, hospital level, readmission plan, inclusion in clinical pathways, surgical status, number of comorbidities, number of hospitalizations, length of stay (LOS), and principal diagnosis were significantly associated with per-diem inpatient costs. Upon the use of LOS, hospital level, insurance type, and number of comorbidities as splitting variables, 10 case-mix groups were generated. The grouping scheme demonstrated high reliability and robustness, with a reduction in variance of 0.43 and coefficients of variation of < 1 across all groups (0.20–0.59). The per-diem payment standards for the 10 groups were 120.06–658.16 yuan.ConclusionsLOS, hospital level, insurance type, and number of comorbidities were key factors for grouping inpatients with mental disorders, and the construction of a refined case mix–based per-diem payment system using the E-CHAID decision tree is a reasonable and appropriate approach.Keywords: E-CHAID, Decision tree, Mental disorders, Per-diem payment, Case mix

Background

The global burden of mental disorders has recently increased. According to latest World Health Organization estimates, more than 1.1 billion people worldwide – approximately one in seven of the global population – accounting for approximately 155 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), were affected by mental disorders in 2021 [1, 2]. Depression and anxiety alone are estimated to cause global economic losses of approximately US$1 trillion annually [3].

The burden of mental health disorders in China is alarming. Data from the China Mental Health Survey indicate a lifetime prevalence of mental disorders in China of 16.57% and a 12-month prevalence of approximately 9.32% [4]. In 2021, the estimated number of incident cases of mental disorders in China was 54.62 million, and mental disorders were responsible for 23.20 million person-years of DALYs [5]. Furthermore, in 2018, the total treatment expenditure for mental and psychological disorders in China amounted to 87.168 billion yuan, accounting for 2.47% of the total national disease-related healthcare expenditure and approximately 0.09% of the gross domestic product. Of the total treatment costs for mental disorders, 31.8% were borne by households, and 48.3% of caregivers of patients with mental disorders experienced substantial financial burdens [6, 7]. These findings indicate that mental disorders impose a profound economic burden on affected individuals, their families, and the society as a whole. Therefore, it is imperative for the Chinese government to implement effective measures to control healthcare expenditures.

Among the policy instruments for containing mental health service costs and improving the efficiency of resource utilization, health insurance payment mechanisms selection and design play a pivotal role. China’s health insurance payment system recently entered a new stage dominated by a prospective payment system, with multiple diseases already included in diagnosis-related group (DRG)–based payments. China primarily adopts case-based (e.g., DRG-based) and per-diem payments for inpatients with mental disorders. DRG-based payment uses the DRG as the payment unit; classifies patients into different payment groups according to diagnosis, treatment procedures, and resource use; and then sets the payment standards in advance for each group. In contrast, per-diem payment uses hospital bed days as the unit of payment and is commonly applied in situations in which resource input varies over time during hospitalization [8].

However, DRG-based payments are not well-suited for mental health treatment [9], although existing per-diem payment standards remain relatively crude [10]. For example, in regions such as Beijing, per-diem payment standards are determined mainly at the hospital level, resulting in weak alignment between payment amounts and patients’ clinical characteristics and failing to adequately reflect the healthcare needs and resource consumption of inpatients with mental disorders [11]. This is because resource utilization among patients with mental disorders often shows substantial heterogeneity in terms of length of stay (LOS), diagnosis type, comorbidity burden, and treatment intensity, leading to large differences in daily resource consumption across patients. Similarly, in other disease areas such as intracerebral hemorrhage and tuberculosis, more refined DRG or case-mix approaches have been explored to improve alignment between payment standards and resource utilization [12, 13], indicating the practical need for a refined case mix–based per-diem payment approach for mental disorders. However, related studies conducted in China are limited. Existing research has mainly focused on single disease categories of mental disorders or included a relatively limited set of grouping variables; moreover, their study designs were at an early stage. Other studies were restricted to policy feasibility discussions based on international experience and theoretical frameworks, while empirical evidence on refined case mix–based per-diem payment systems in the Chinese context remains insufficient [14–16].

Some Western countries explored refined case mix–based per-diem payments for inpatients with mental disorders and demonstrated an overall transition from extensive payment models to patient-centered and finely stratified payment approaches. Among them, the prospective per-diem payment systems for mental disorders in Switzerland, Germany, and the United States are the most relevant and representative. These systems can be broadly classified into two types. The first type is a case mix–based per-diem payment in which patients are grouped according to factors related to healthcare resource use, such as principal diagnosis, complexity, comorbidities, or treatment intensity, and different per-diem payment standards are assigned to different case-mix groups. The Swiss Tariff Psychiatry system and German Pauschalierendes Entgeltsystem in der Psychiatrie und Psychosomatik system belong to this category. The former forms basic diagnostic groups according to the patient’s principal diagnosis and further subdivides them according to complexity, secondary diagnoses, and age [17, 18]. The latter further stratifies patients based on inpatient care type and basic disease groups combined with age, complications/comorbidities, and treatment intensity, thereby establishing per-diem payment standards that more closely match patients’ healthcare resource use [19].

The second type is a per-diem payment based on a basic daily rate with adjustments for patient characteristics. This model does not usually directly establish fixed case-mix groups. Instead, it applies adjustment coefficients to a uniform base per-diem rate according to factors such as age, comorbidities, LOS, and regional variation. The United States’ Inpatient Psychiatric Facility Prospective Payment System is a typical example of this type [20]. Although payment systems and service structures differ across countries and from those in China, these models share one common feature: They all attempt to make payment standards more closely reflect the actual resource use across patients through patient stratification or payment adjustment, thereby reducing mismatch caused by crude payment standards and promoting more appropriate control of healthcare expenditures.

In summary, given the limited evidence from China and insights from the international experience, this study follows the core logic of developing refined per-diem payment groupings based on differences in patient healthcare resource use. Using large-scale multivariable inpatient data covering all categories of mental disorders, an Exhaustive CHi-squared Automatic Interaction Detection (E-CHAID) decision tree was applied to perform a case-mix classification of inpatients with mental disorders in China. This model provides evidence for exploring a more suitable case mix–based per-diem payment approach in the Chinese context and may help improve the rationality and precision of payment standards, promote a more efficient allocation of healthcare resources, reduce the financial burden on patients, and curb the unreasonable growth of medical expenditures.

Methods

Data collection and selection

The data for this study were obtained from the hospital discharge records of inpatients admitted to two specialized psychiatric hospitals in Chongqing, China, between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2024. In 2024, the two study hospitals had 1,442 open beds and 9,021 annual inpatient cases, accounting for approximately 6.48% and 12.09% of the corresponding totals in Chongqing, respectively, indicating a certain degree of representativeness of regional inpatient psychiatric services. Patients were included if they met the diagnostic criteria for mental disorders according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10; codes F00–F99). Patients were excluded if the primary diagnosis was not a mental disorder; if key variables were incomplete, such as data missing for core variables including per-diem inpatient cost and LOS; per-diem inpatient cost showed obvious logical errors; or the LOS was < 1 day. A total of 25,510 patients with mental disorders were included and their demographic, clinical, and cost information collected.

Figure 1 presents a flowchart of the participant selection process. To eliminate the impact of price fluctuations, the per-diem inpatient costs in 2020–2024 were adjusted using the healthcare consumer price index (CPI) for Chongqing using 2024 as the base year. The CPI data were obtained from the Statistical Yearbook of the National Bureau of Statistics of China.

Selection of study patients

Research variables

In this study, variable selection was informed by existing literature, inpatient management practices for mental disorders, health insurance payment system background, the availability of information in hospital discharge records, and expert opinions in the field of mental health. The variables were defined as follows.

The dependent variable was the per-diem inpatient cost derived from hospital discharge and billing records by dividing the total inpatient cost by the actual LOS. This indicator reflects the actual expenditure incurred during hospitalization rather than unit costs based on hospital cost accounting. Because the original data did not include information on hospital cost accounting or the allocation of indirect costs, no analysis of indirect cost allocation was performed.

The independent variables were classified into two categories: (1) basic information including sex, age, insurance type, admission mode, admission condition, discharge mode, source of admission, and hospital level; and (2) clinical service information including readmission plan, surgical status, number of comorbidities, number of hospitalizations, LOS, and principal diagnosis. The selected variables were as follows:

Insurance types mainly included Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance and Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance, which correspond to the two major insurance schemes covering non-employed urban and rural residents and employed or retired urban populations in China’s basic medical insurance system [21].

Hospital level was defined according to the Chinese hospital classification system, which adopts a three-tier structure to reflect differences in service capacity and resource allocation across institutions. This study was conducted in secondary and tertiary hospitals [22].

The readmission plan variable reflects planned rehospitalization shortly after discharge and is an important indicator of the continuity of care and service utilization in mental health. Previous studies reported that readmission is closely associated with psychiatric inpatient service use and patient complexity [23].

The inclusion of clinical pathways reflects whether patients have entered standardized clinical management processes. Previous studies demonstrated that clinical pathways are widely used in public hospitals in China to standardize care processes, promote quality improvement, and align with payment reform [24].

In this study, surgical status mainly referred to whether patients received electroconvulsive therapy, a representative therapeutic procedure in inpatient psychiatric services that was commonly included as an important clinical characteristic in recent studies of psychiatric hospitalization in China [25].

The definitions and classifications of the above variables were primarily based on the Notice of the Ministry of Health on Revising the Front Page of Inpatient Medical Records in China.

Statistical analysis

First, because the per-diem inpatient costs showed a skewed distribution, nonparametric tests were used in the univariate analysis. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to examine binary variables, while the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used to examine categorical variables. Second, based on the univariate analysis, variables with statistical significance were included in a generalized linear model (GLM) to identify the main factors associated with per-diem inpatient cost in which binary and multi-categorical variables were handled using binary coding and dummy variables, respectively. Finally, with per-diem inpatient cost as the dependent variable, all statistically significant variables identified in the GLM were entered into the E-CHAID decision tree model as independent variables. The E-CHAID model then automatically selects the optimal splitting variables according to its splitting criteria, thereby grouping the patients.

Case-mix classification methods

For the case-mix classification, we used the E-CHAID model for the decision tree analysis. E-CHAID is an improved decision tree method based on CHi-squared Automatic Interaction Detection (CHAID), which has been used in studies of case-mix classification and DRG grouping [26]. The grouping principle is based on the relationship between the target and predictor variables, and samples are automatically grouped into multidimensional contingency tables according to the significance levels of the chi-squared tests. A major advantage of E-CHAID is its ability to handle nonlinear data and tolerate a certain number of missing values, thereby overcoming the limitations of traditional parametric methods [27]. In addition, compared to CHAID, E-CHAID performs more exhaustive variable merging and grouping procedures, thereby more accurately identifying grouping variables [28]. The parameters for tree growth were set as follows: maximum depth, three levels; minimum number of cases in each parent node, 100; minimum number of cases in each child node, 50; and a significance level of α = 0.05 for node splitting.

Evaluation indicators of the E-CHAID decision tree model

The nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis H test and reduction in variance (RIV) were used to assess intergroup heterogeneity, while the coefficient of variation (CV) was used to evaluate intragroup homogeneity. RIV was calculated as (total sum of squared deviations − sum of squared deviations within all subgroups) divided by the total sum of squared deviations, while CV was calculated as the standard deviation divided by the mean. For the grouping model, a statistically significant Kruskal–Wallis H test, together with a larger RIV and smaller CV, indicated greater intergroup and smaller intragroup variations, reflecting better grouping performance. In this study, a RIV > 40% and CV < 1 were adopted as criteria for evaluating the grouping results [29]. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.0, and the significance level was set at P < 0.05.

Calculation of per-diem payment standards, high-cost cases, and disease weights

To reduce the influence of extreme values, the median inpatient cost per day within each group was used as the per-diem payment standard [30]. The upper cost limit was defined as the 75th percentile of the per-diem inpatient cost plus 1.5 times the interquartile range (P75 + 1.5[IQR]) to identify extremely high-cost cases; cases exceeding this limit were classified as outliers [31]. Disease weight was calculated as the ratio of the mean inpatient cost per day of a given case-mix group to the mean inpatient cost per day of all cases; a higher weight indicated greater consumption of healthcare resources [32].

GLM analysis

With the per-diem inpatient cost as the dependent variable, 14 variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analysis were included as independent variables in a GLM with a log-link function and gamma distribution. Sex, age, insurance type, discharge mode, hospital level, readmission plan, inclusion in clinical pathways, surgical status, number of comorbidities, number of hospitalizations, LOS, and principal diagnosis were statistically significant factors (χ² = 23,758.395; P < 0.001). In terms of effect size, hospital level and LOS had the most pronounced impact on per-diem inpatient costs. Compared with the reference group, patients treated in tertiary hospitals had significantly higher per-diem inpatient cost (β = 0.891; 95% confidence interval, 0.868–0.914; P < 0.001). In contrast, patients with a longer LOS had a significantly lower per-diem inpatient cost than the reference group, and the magnitude of the decrease increased as hospitalization duration increased (β = −0.354 to − 0.841; all P < 0.001). Development of decision tree–based case-mix grouping scheme

With per-diem inpatient cost as the dependent variable, all factors that were statistically significant in the GLM, including sex, age, insurance type, discharge mode, hospital level, readmission plan, inclusion in clinical pathways, surgical status, number of comorbidities, number of hospitalizations, LOS, and principal diagnosis, were entered into the E-CHAID decision tree model as independent variables. The decision-tree model automatically selects the optimal splitting variables during the recursive partitioning process based on the chi-squared splitting criterion. In the final model, only LOS, hospital level, insurance type, and number of comorbidities remained as splitting variables, resulting in 10 case-mix groups (Fig. 2).

Distribution of daily inpatient costs of inpatients with mental disorders in China (N = 25,510)

After the grouping, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was applied to examine intergroup differences in per-diem inpatient costs. Significant differences were noted in cost distributions across the 10 groups (H = 14,014.074; P < 0.001), indicating good intergroup heterogeneity and statistically significant group separation. In addition, the calculated RIV value was 0.43, further supporting the substantial intergroup heterogeneity. The CV values of the case-mix groups were 0.20–0.59, indicating small intragroup variation and satisfactory grouping performance. The detailed results are presented in Table 3.

Discussion

In this study, the E-CHAID decision tree model classified inpatients with mental disorders into 10 case-mix groups using four splitting variables, with LOS serving as the primary splitting variable. As LOS increased, the per-diem inpatient cost decreased, a finding that is consistent with the results of previous studies [33, 34]. This relationship may reflect the typical pattern of inpatient care for mental disorders characterized by more intensive resource use in the early stages and gradual stabilization in the later stages [35].

During the initial phase of hospitalization, patients are often in the acute stage and require concentrated diagnostic tests, treatments, and intensive nursing care, resulting in higher daily costs. As patients enter the stabilization and rehabilitation phases, care focuses on maintenance and functional recovery with fewer new treatment services and a lower testing frequency required, leading to a progressive decline in daily resource use [36]. Therefore, using LOS as the primary splitting variable allowed the E-CHAID model to stratify patients into relatively homogeneous per-diem cost categories corresponding to the early, middle, and late hospitalization stages. This study provides empirical evidence of the development of phased and differentiated per-diem payment standards.

In the present sample, hospital level was selected as a second-level splitting variable, and per-diem inpatient costs were higher among patients treated in the tertiary hospital than among those treated in the secondary hospital, which is broadly consistent with the direction reported in previous studies [37, 38]. This may be because higher-level medical institutions usually treat patients with mental disorders in the acute phase as well as more complex cases, including those involving acute agitation, severe behavioral problems, or concomitant physical risks, whereas patients with relatively stable conditions are more often hospitalized for treatment or rehabilitation management in lower-level medical institutions [39, 40]. Under this care structure, inpatients with mental disorders treated in higher-level medical institutions tend to have higher overall risk and greater management complexity. Accordingly, the inpatient management of these patients may require more intensive medical and nursing input and a broader range of therapeutic and monitoring services. Together, these factors may increase daily resource consumption among inpatients with mental disorders in higher-level medical institutions [41].

In the existing design of per-diem payments for mental disorders, hospital level has been incorporated into the payment standard system in some regions [42]. For example, cities such as Xuzhou and Ningbo have taken hospital level into account when setting per-diem payment standards for mental disorders. This suggests that, in current health insurance payment practice, differences between medical institutions are commonly considered when establishing per-diem payment standards for mental health services. It should be noted, however, that because this study included only two hospitals and hospital level overlapped with hospital identity, the present findings are more appropriately interpreted as indicating that hospital level had some grouping value in the current sample, rather than representing an independent effect of hospital level itself. Its stability and generalizability in broader multi-hospital datasets still require further validation.

Based on the initial stratification by LOS and hospital level, the E-CHAID decision tree further selected insurance type and number of comorbidities to refine the classification of inpatients with mental disorders. Patients covered by the Urban and Rural Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URRBMI) in China have higher per-diem inpatient costs than those covered by the Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (UEBMI), a pattern that was also reported in previous studies [43]. This may be because the population covered by the URRBMI includes a higher proportion of older adults and individuals with lower socioeconomic status who are more likely to delay seeking care and be hospitalized after disease progression, resulting in greater treatment difficulty and higher per-diem costs [44, 45]. Moreover, reimbursement levels are generally lower for URRBMI beneficiaries in China, which further contributes to the higher daily costs incurred by this group [46].

As shown in Fig. 2, the per-diem inpatient cost for Node 8 (URRBMI) was 719.12 yuan, higher than the mean per-diem inpatient cost of 594.35 yuan for Node 7 (UEBMI). When these two nodes were further subdivided in the case-mix classification, differences in healthcare-seeking behavior and insurance coverage associated with insurance type were reflected in the corresponding per-diem payment standards and weights.

Number of comorbidities was also selected as a splitting variable; as expected, patients with more comorbidities had higher per-diem inpatient costs. This may be because when patients with mental disorders present with additional diseases, hospitals increase diagnostic testing, medication use, and nursing input; thus, per-diem inpatient cost increases as the number of comorbidities increases [47]. Studies from multiple countries have confirmed that comorbid physical conditions in patients with mental disorders lead to additional healthcare resource use and cost burden [48, 49]. Therefore, the impact of comorbidities on per-diem inpatient costs should be fully considered when implementing refined per-diem payments for mental disorders. This may help avoid inappropriate case-mix classifications that could encourage hospitals to preferentially admit patients with milder conditions while discouraging the admission of patients with more severe conditions. Moreover, classifying comorbidities by number as well as type may help more accurately characterize the differences in per-diem resource consumption among inpatients with mental disorders.

Interestingly, although principal diagnosis is generally considered an important factor associated with per-diem inpatient costs, it was not retained as a final splitting variable in the E-CHAID decision tree. This may be explained by the use of broad ICD-10 diagnostic categories for the principal diagnosis, within which substantial heterogeneity still exists in terms of disease severity, functional status, cognitive impairment, and treatment intensity, thereby limiting the ability to distinguish differences in per-diem inpatient costs.

Previous studies demonstrated that reliance on coarse diagnostic groupings in mental health case-mix systems may fail to adequately capture variations in healthcare resource use [50]. Moreover, some studies reported that long-acting injectable antipsychotics may influence the overall healthcare costs through effects on readmission and resource utilization [51]. Therefore, certain medications may have uneven effects on per-diem inpatient costs across different terminal nodes of the E-CHAID decision tree. Given the data limitations, future research could further examine whether specific medication categories have different impacts on per-diem inpatient costs within particular terminal nodes under the current case-mix framework.

The case-mix classification system developed herein demonstrated good statistical performance. With only 10 groups and 25,510 inpatients with mental disorders included, the RIV reached 0.43, higher than that reported for many existing case-mix classification systems. A scoping review by Tran et al. of case-mix classification systems in community mental health facilities showed that, although various models have been developed in this field, most still have limited explanatory power for mental health resource use. Among the few better-performing models, the Australian Mental Health Classification and Costing System defines 46 groups in community settings and achieves a RIV of 0.266 [52]. In addition, the RIV observed herein was higher than that of the case-mix classification system jointly developed by the InterRAI and the Arkansas Department of Human Services Child and Adolescent Mental Health Service, which includes eight groups and reports an RIV of 0.30 [53]. However, it remained lower than that of the system constructed by Eagar et al. based on mental health service data from New Zealand, which reported an RIV of 0.67 [54].

Compared with the international findings described above, the current results are generally reasonable but require further improvement. Finally, as shown in Table 4, establishing scientifically sound per-diem payment standards and cost control thresholds for each case-mix group not only helps optimize health insurance payment incentive mechanisms and regulate healthcare service delivery, but it strengthens the management of high-cost cases and promotes more appropriate control of inpatient expenditures.

The refined case mix–based per-diem payment approach developed herein using the E-CHAID decision tree has clear decision rules, and most of the variables involved are routinely recorded in the hospital information systems. Therefore, it can be integrated into electronic health records (EHRs) or hospital information systems (HIS). Previous studies demonstrated that tasks related to case-mix classification and payment grouping can be performed through EHRs or HIS because these systems can automatically extract relevant clinical and cost data and perform grouping accordingly [55]. Based on the findings of this study, hospital level, insurance type, and number of comorbidities can be identified at an early stage of admission, whereas LOS is a more suitable dynamic indicator during hospitalization. Therefore, the application of the refined case mix–based per-diem payment model developed using the E-CHAID approach should not rely on fixed one-time grouping at triage. Instead, hospital information systems can be used to conduct an initial classification early after admission and continuously update patient stratification according to the real-time LOS information recorded in the HIS. This may support the early warning of high-resource-use cases and more refined inpatient management. Simultaneously, when setting payment standards for mental disorders, health insurance authorities may use the historical distribution of per-diem inpatient costs in each case-mix group as a reference for developing differentiated per-diem payment standards and cost control thresholds.

This study had several limitations. First, the analyzed data were obtained from one secondary hospital and one tertiary hospital in Chongqing; therefore, hospital level may overlap with hospital identity. Under this data structure, the estimated effect of hospital level in the model and its splitting role in the E-CHAID tree may reflect not only differences between levels of care but also unmeasured institution-specific factors, such as staffing ratios. Accordingly, findings related to hospital level should be interpreted and generalized with caution. Further validation is still needed using data from more hospitals. Second, owing to limitations in the availability of hospital discharge data, clinical severity indicators and functional assessment measures were not included. Patient complexity is mainly reflected indirectly by variables such as LOS and number of comorbidities, which may not fully represent the true clinical condition of patients with mental disorders. Third, this study used a retrospective design; therefore, it could not assess the dynamic effects of implementing a refined case mix–based per-diem payment model on cost control. However, these findings require further confirmation in future prospective studies. Fourth, the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic may have impacted healthcare costs and case-mix grouping among patients with mental disorders; however, its effects were not specifically evaluated in this study. Future studies should incorporate data from broader regions to assess the impact of public health events on inpatient payment groupings and payment standards for mental disorders.

Conclusions

This study provides a preliminary foundation for optimizing the case-mix grouping per-diem payments for mental disorders. It identified the key grouping variables associated with per-diem inpatient costs and applied the E-CHAID decision tree to case-mix grouping. In this two-hospital dataset, LOS, hospital level, insurance type, and number of comorbidities were the main splitting variables for constructing case-mix groups under the per-diem payment structure. Based on a large sample size, the developed case-mix system classified inpatients with mental disorders into 10 groups with relatively homogeneous per-diem costs, effectively distinguishing between different resource use levels. Overall, this study provides a useful reference for further improving refined case mix–based per-diem payment models.
