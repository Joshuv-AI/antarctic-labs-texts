Poster 180. Comparing the Efficacy of Minimal Clinically Important Difference Thresholds using Distribution-Based or Anchor-Based Estimations: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Brandon J. Allen, Jackson Woodrow, Maya S. Dubrovsky, Rachel L. Poutre, Rishi P. Earla, Jeffrey S. Mun, Stephen M. Gillinov, Bilal Siddiq, Nathan J. Cherian, Ryan E. Dean, Scott D. Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:In orthopaedic literature, the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) is a common threshold used to calculate the smallest change in a patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) that a patient would identify as an improvement after treatment. However, thresholds can vary widely and be calculated in two ways: either by distribution or anchor-based methods. The purpose of this study was to review existing literature to evaluate the MCID thresholds calculated by different methods.Methods:PubMed, Scopus, and Embase were queried in April 2025 for studies that calculated MCID values for the modified Harris Hip Score (mHHS) and the Visual Analog Pain Score (VAS). Inclusion criteria consisted of patients who underwent hip arthroscopy for the treatment of femoroacetabular impingement. Studies were excluded if they did not calculate their own MCID value. Anchor-based thresholds were defined as methods that calculated the average improvement at which patients indicated they perceived a benefit. Distribution-based thresholds were estimated from half of the standard deviation of either the preoperative PROM score (Preop) or the change in PROM scores (Delta).Results:Of the 609 unique studies identified in our search, 13 studies (5547 hips, 56% Female) were included. Both Delta and Preop distribution-based methods produced similar thresholds for mHHS at 1-year follow-up (7.70 [6.77-8.62], P=0.856). At 2-year follow-up, distribution-based estimates of MCID for mHHS (7.28 [6.84-7.72], P=0.924) and VAS (1.14 [1.06-1.23], P=0.992) were similar. Anchor-based thresholds were 4.2, 16.0, and 5.3 for 1-year mHHS, 2-year mHHS (Figure 1), and 2-year VAS (Figure 2), respectively. Furthermore, even within the same study sample, distribution-based methods yielded different MCID thresholds than the corresponding anchor-based approaches (Figure 3).Conclusions:As distribution-based methods are intended to estimate anchor-based thresholds, this study illustrates differences between MCID thresholds calculated with varying methodologies. This suggests that distribution-based methods may misrepresent patient-perceived improvement following hip arthroscopy.
