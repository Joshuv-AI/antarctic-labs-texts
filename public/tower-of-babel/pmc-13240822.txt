Supramolecular self-assembled polyphenol nanoparticles alleviate osteoarthritis by inhibiting chondrocyte ferroptosis
Authors: Zijian Wu, Guang Shi, Luhao Li, Zhenzhou Piao, Zhuowen Hao, Renxin Chen, Zouwei Li, Hanke Li, Yilong Huang, Jiayao Chen, Zheyuan Zhang, Lanhong Guo, Minchao Dong, Yi Wang, Juan Du, Jingfeng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a degenerative joint disease with no currently available curative treatments. Recent studies have implicated ferroptosis in the progression of cartilage degradation and inflammation in OA, highlighting it as a potential therapeutic target. Here, we report the synthesis of epigallocatechin gallate–proline polyphenolic nanodrugs (EP NDs), prepared via a Mannich condensation reaction between epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) and proline (Pro), followed by self-assembly, aiming to attenuate disease progression through the suppression of ferroptosis. In vitro experiments demonstrated that EP NDs possess potent free radical scavenging activity, effectively scavenging free radicals (DPPH, ABTS, ·OH, O2−·) and reducing intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in chondrocytes. Furthermore, EP NDs significantly suppressed oxidative stress-induced downregulation of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), restored iron homeostasis, attenuated lipid peroxidation, and preserved mitochondrial function, thereby alleviating metabolic dysfunction in chondrocytes under ferroptotic conditions. Additionally, EP NDs exhibited excellent anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties, promoting the polarization of macrophages from the M1 to the M2 phenotype. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that EP NDs upregulated the NRF2 signaling pathway while downregulating the MAPK pathway, contributing to ferroptosis inhibition and delayed OA progression. In a rat model of OA, EP NDs effectively mitigated ferroptosis, alleviated inflammation, and exerted significant chondroprotective effects. In general, these findings demonstrate that EP NDs enhance cartilage protection through synergistic anti-ferroptotic, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory mechanisms, thereby presenting a simple, efficient, and promising therapeutic approach for OA.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent degenerative joint disorder globally, afflicting over 500 million people worldwide. It predominantly affects middle-aged and elderly populations, with core pathological hallmarks including progressive articular cartilage damage, cartilage matrix catabolism, joint space narrowing, and secondary synovitis [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. OA frequently induces chronic pain and progressive joint dysfunction in patients, which can ultimately result in joint disability [6]. This not only severely compromises patients' quality of life but also imposes a substantial familial and socioeconomic burden [4,[7], [8], [9]]. Currently, clinical management of OA is primarily symptom-oriented, encompassing oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and intra-articular corticosteroid injections [2,[10], [11], [12]]. However, long-term NSAID administration is associated with adverse effects such as gastrointestinal injury, cardiovascular impairment, and renal dysfunction [1,4,10,13]. While intra-articular corticosteroid injections provide short-term symptomatic relief, prolonged administration may entail a potential risk of exacerbating cartilage degradation [14]. Thus, there is an unmet clinical need to develop novel therapeutic strategies that target the core pathological mechanisms of OA while integrating high efficacy and safety profiles.

The pathogenesis of OA is complex, involving the interplay of multiple factors, including mechanical, biological, genetic, biochemical, and immunometabolic factors, which collectively disrupt the balance between cartilage synthesis and degradation [4,11,15]. In addition to traditional risk factors such as aging, obesity, and joint trauma [2,7], recent studies have revealed that ferroptosis—a form of iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation-driven regulated cell death—plays a pivotal role in the pathological progression of OA [16,17]. Up to now, it has been confirmed that ferroptosis is associated with a variety of degenerative diseases (such as Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and kidney degeneration), traumatic brain injury, carcinogenesis, ischemia-reperfusion injury and stroke [5]. Distinct from necrosis, apoptosis, and autophagy, ferroptosis induces cell death through Fe2+-mediated Fenton reactions (Fe2+ + H2O2 → Fe3+ + ·OH + OH−), which catalyze lipid peroxidation in cell membranes and ultimately lead to cell death [10,18]. At the molecular level, ferroptosis is typically characterized by downregulated expression of GPX4 and upregulated expression of acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) [17,[19], [20], [21]]. As the core enzyme of the glutathione antioxidant system, GPX4 exerts an irreplaceable role in inhibiting lipid peroxidation [20,22,23]. Within the pathological microenvironment of OA, chondrocytes are chronically exposed to adverse conditions, such as excessive accumulation of ROS, disturbed iron metabolism, and reduced endogenous antioxidant defense capacity, which readily trigger the ferroptotic program [5,24]. This process not only directly exacerbates extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation and impairs cartilage homeostasis, but also amplifies inflammatory signaling cascades, forming a persistently deteriorating pathological positive feedback loop that promotes OA progression. Notably, existing studies have demonstrated that inhibiting chondrocyte ferroptosis in OA can significantly improve joint function [18,[24], [25], [26]]. Therefore, targeted inhibition of chondrocyte ferroptosis, combined with synergistic intervention in oxidative stress, iron metabolism, and inflammatory networks, is gradually emerging as a highly promising research direction in the field of OA treatment.

Bioactive compounds derived from natural plants, which are rich in antioxidant structural units, are regarded as important resources for scavenging ROS and intervening in oxidative stress-related diseases [27,28]. EGCG, one of the most abundant natural polyphenolic compounds in tea leaves, has garnered considerable attention due to its excellent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and chondroprotective activities [29,30]. Studies have indicated that EGCG not only efficiently scavenges ROS, inhibits the expression of inflammatory factors and ferroptosis, but also promotes the polarization of macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, while protecting chondrocytes against metabolic disorders [10,27]. Additionally, EGCG exhibits strong affinity with outer mitochondrial membrane proteins through hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and π−π stacking, thereby demonstrating the capacity to target mitochondria [31]. However, like other bioactive agents, EGCG suffers from inherent drawbacks including poor stability, low bioavailability, and rapid metabolism in vivo, which severely hinder its clinical translation and application [29,32]. Existing EGCG modification strategies, such as metal coordination [33,34] and oxidative coupling [35], are often associated with partial loss of bioactivity or potential biotoxicity risks. Although nanoformulations constructed by combining tea polyphenols with protein [36] or polysaccharide [37] carriers hold promise, their structural heterogeneity may induce immunogenicity and other risks [29], introducing uncertainty into practical applications. In contrast, small-molecule amino acids, as fundamental building blocks of proteins, possess advantages of structural simplicity, high stability, favorable biosafety, and low cost [38,39]. They not only maintain high bioactivity and eliminate potential immunogenicity, but also significantly reduce costs. More importantly, various small-molecule amino acids can directly regulate chondrocyte oxidative stress, ferroptosis, and inflammatory cascades within the OA pathological microenvironment, and are therefore considered important endogenous factors enabling chondrocytes to resist pathological damage [27,40,41]. This provides a novel approach for the application of EGCG-based nanodrugs in OA treatment. Pro, a key member of small-molecule amino acids, is one of the critical components of collagen. It not only directly participates in the synthesis and repair of the cartilage matrix, but also exerts a protective effect against cellular oxidative stress [[42], [43], [44]]. As a natural antioxidant, the transport and metabolism of Pro play a critical role in maintaining mitochondrial function [45]. Disruption of Pro homeostasis can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and abnormal calcium handling [46]. Furthermore, Lei et al. demonstrated that Pro suppresses inflammatory responses by reducing the infiltration of pro-inflammatory M1-type macrophages [47]. Notably, a previous study has shown that Pro-rich protein 11 (PRR11) binds to and stabilizes dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH) to confer ferroptosis resistance; its downregulation leads to increased lipid peroxidation and impaired mitochondrial morphology [48]. In summary, based on the multiple studies described above, we hypothesize that in addition to its proven antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, Pro may have a promising role in counteracting ferroptosis. Thus, the organic combination of EGCG and Pro is expected to achieve synergistic effects at the molecular and functional levels, further enhancing the antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-ferroptotic capacities of chondrocytes, thereby exerting a positive regulatory effect on ferroptosis-driven OA progression.

Herein, we report the construction of EGCG-Pro self-assembled polyphenol nanoparticle drugs (EP NDs) endowed with anti-ferroptotic, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory properties to achieve potent chondroprotective effects. Specifically, EGCG was covalently conjugated with Pro via Mannich condensation reaction to generate polyphenolic oligomeric derivatives, which subsequently underwent spontaneous self-assembled into polyphenol nanoparticles driven by intermolecular hydrogen bonding and π−π stacking effects [10,27,29]. We hypothesized that this nanosystem would not only enhance the physicochemical stability of EGCG and prolong its intra-articular retention, but also enable synergistic therapeutic intervention by integrating the biological activity of Pro with the pleiotropic pharmacological effects of EGCG, thereby targeting multiple key pathological processes of OA (Scheme 1). Mechanistically, at the cellular level, EP NDs were designed to scavenge excessive ROS, restore GPX4 expression, rebalance iron homeostasis, and suppress lipid peroxidation, ultimately inhibiting chondrocyte ferroptosis and then preserving mitochondrial function. At the level of inflammatory and immune regulation, EP NDs modulate macrophage polarization, reduce the secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators, and remodel the joint immune microenvironment. Furthermore, integrated transcriptomic analysis systematically elucidated their regulatory effects on the NRF2-mediated antioxidant pathway and the MAPK-associated inflammatory signaling cascade, providing mechanistic insight into their multi-target therapeutic actions. Finally, the overall efficacy of EP NDs in attenuating cartilage degeneration and delaying OA progression was validated in a rat model. Collectively, this study presents a green and efficient nano-therapeutic strategy based on natural product-derived polyphenols and offers new experimental evidence supporting ferroptosis-targeted intervention in OA, highlighting its promising translational potential.Scheme 1Schematic illustration of the preparation of EP NDs and their therapeutic effects on OA.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of EP NDs

Based on the high chemical reactivity of the dihydroxy or trihydroxy substitution on the B-ring and the meta-5,7-dihydroxy substitution on the A-ring of EGCG [49], multifunctional polyphenol nanoparticles were synthesized via a one-step Mannich condensation reaction. As shown in Fig. 1A, EGCG, Pro, and formaldehyde were condensed in water at room temperature to generate tea polyphenol oligomeric derivatives. Intermolecular hydrogen bonding and π−π stacking interactions enhanced the entanglement between oligomers, which ultimately self-assembled into polyphenol nanoparticles after 4–5 h of reaction. After preparation, scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Fig. 1B) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Fig. 1C) images revealed that EP NDs exhibited a uniform spherical morphology with favorable dispersibility. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDS) elemental mapping confirmed the coincident distribution of C, O, and N elements in EP NDs (Fig. 1D and Fig. S1). Subsequently, the particle size (Fig. 1E) and zeta potential (Fig. 1F) of EP NDs were characterized. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis showed that the average particle size of EP NDs was approximately 417.5 nm, with a zeta potential peak of −25.14 mV. As illustrated in Fig. 1G, the particle size increased gradually with prolonged reaction time, reaching the micrometer scale after 24 h. Meanwhile, the zeta potential remained stable within the range of −22 to −27 mV over the same period. To further verify the successful conjugation of EGCG and Pro, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis was performed (Fig. 1H). As shown in the FTIR spectra, the out-of-plane C–OH bending vibration of the phenolic ring in EGCG (700–600 cm−1) disappeared during the assembly of EP NDs. Meanwhile, the characteristic peak of Pro near 623 cm−1 was significantly weakened or shifted in the product, further confirming the condensation reaction between its amino group and the phenolic hydroxyl groups of EGCG. The deformation vibrations of Ar–H on the A-ring of EGCG at 1148, 1094, and 787 cm−1 vanished, while an asymmetric stretching vibration of aliphatic ether appeared at 1119 cm−1, indicating the addition reaction of formaldehyde with EGCG at the H-6 and H-8 positions [29]. The C–OH stretching of EGCG appeared as a broad band ranging from 1373 to 1280 cm−1. No obvious changes were observed in the stretching vibration of aromatic −C=C− within the range of 1530–1450 cm−1, suggesting preservation of the EGCG molecular skeleton—a feature favorable for the long-term application of EP NDs in vivo. In addition, the slight decrease in the peak intensity of EP NDs at 1610 cm−1 could be attributed to the asymmetric stretching vibration of the carboxyl group in Pro. Collectively, these spectral changes confirmed that the formation of EP NDs involved definite chemical bonding rather than simple physical mixing. Subsequently, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to investigate the chemical composition and bonding states of EP NDs. In the C 1s spectrum (Fig. 1I), the peak at 284.8 eV was assigned to C–C/C=C bonds, representing aromatic rings and aliphatic carbons in EP NDs. The peak at approximately 285.5 eV was attributed to C–O/C–N moieties, consistent with the abundant hydroxyl groups in EGCG and the amine groups in Pro. The intense peak at around 286.6 eV corresponded to C=O species derived from ester groups, while the contribution at a higher binding energy of approximately 289.1 eV was assigned to carboxyl groups, verifying their presence in the nanoparticles. Furthermore, a weak feature observed at approximately 292.1 eV was attributed to the π−π shake-up satellite peak, a characteristic of aromatic systems with delocalized π electrons. These results confirmed the coexistence of hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl functional groups, supporting the involvement of intermolecular hydrogen bonding and π−π interactions in the self-assembled system. In the N 1s spectrum (Fig. 1J), the peak at approximately 402.2 eV was assigned to C2N+H, and the peak at approximately 403.0 eV was assigned to C3N+H. Both further validated that hydrogen-bonding interactions stabilized the structure of the polyphenol nanoparticles. Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry was utilized to characterize the molecular structure of EP NDs. As shown in Fig. 1K, a series of regularly increasing peaks with a mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) around 470 corresponded to EGCG fragments linked by methylene bridges (m/z = 470.1 for −EGCG–CH2−). Multiple peak clusters spanning 569.3 to 621.5 in the mass spectrum were attributed to the conjugation of single-molecule EGCG and Pro. The peak cluster centered at 771.9 was probably derived from the binding of single-molecule EGCG and double-molecule Pro. The peak clusters around 941.9, 1094.2, and 1256.5 represented EGCG-conjugated dimer structures with or without Pro, respectively, while the peak cluster near 1425.2 was assigned to trimers. Additionally, signals corresponding to tetramers, pentamers, and weak hexamers were detected in EP NDs from the mass spectrum. Based on these results, a possible chemical structural formula of EP NDs obtained from the reaction between EGCG and Pro was proposed. In the 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectrum (Fig. 1L), the distinct chemical shifts between H-6 and H-8 of EP NDs confirmed that H-8 exhibited high reactivity in the addition reaction between EGCG and formaldehyde. The characteristic signals appearing at 3.6 ppm, 4.0 ppm, and 5.1 ppm were attributed to methylene bridges (Ar-CH2–Ar) and benzoxazine units (N–CH2–Ar, N–CH2–O), respectively. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern (Fig. 1M) demonstrated that EP NDs only exhibited broadened diffused peaks in the range of 10–90°, indicating the formation of an amorphous structure. This contrasted with the sharp diffraction peaks of the crystalline raw materials (Pro and EGCG), confirming that the Mannich condensation reaction disrupted the original long-range molecular order of the raw materials and facilitated the recombination of molecules into disordered nanoparticles through covalent cross-linking. Finally, the successful synthesis of EP NDs was further confirmed by ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectroscopy (Fig. 1N). After the Mannich condensation reaction, the maximum absorption peak of EP NDs was redshifted compared with that of pure EGCG, accompanied by a broadened peak shape. Moreover, the spectral profile differed significantly from that of the physical mixture of EGCG and Pro. This observation indicated that new chemical bonds were formed between EGCG and Pro, which induced changes in the molecular conjugate system and electron cloud distribution, ultimately leading to the formation of optically active nanoparticles with a novel electronic structure.

EP NDs are green nanoparticles synthesized via a one-step method using natural plant extracts and a small-molecule amino acid. Various EGCG-modifying nanoplatforms have been previously reported, including metal coordination [33,34], protein [36] or polysaccharide [37] modifications. However, EGCG–metal complexes may present potential biotoxicity or compromise the active center, whereas proteins and polysaccharides generally exhibit higher immunogenicity and cost compared to small-molecule amino acids. In contrast to other complex nanoparticle-based strategies, such as the responsive hydrogel system reported by Gan et al. [50], EP NDs offer reduced immunogenicity and batch-to-batch variability, thereby representing a green and efficient therapeutic strategy for the treatment of OA.

Biocompatibility and intracellular uptake of EP NDs

First, the effect of EP NDs on chondrocyte viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. As shown in Fig. 2A, chondrocytes were co-incubated with EP NDs at various concentrations (0–20 μg/ml) for 24 h and 72 h. No significant difference in cell viability was observed among these groups, indicating that EP NDs exhibited negligible cytotoxicity at the tested concentrations. Notably, EP NDs within the range of 0–20 μg/ml even exerted a slight stimulatory effect on chondrocyte growth. Subsequently, four concentrations of EP NDs (0 μg/ml, 5 μg/ml, 10 μg/ml, and 20 μg/ml) were selected for further co-incubation with chondrocytes for 24 h and 72 h. Optical microscopy images of cells stained with Alcian Blue revealed that the majority of cells in each group maintained a normal morphology, without obvious shrinkage or rupture. These observations confirmed the good growth status of chondrocytes, which was consistent with the results of the CCK-8 assay (Fig. 2B). Live/dead staining assay results showed that a small number of dead cells appeared and the number of live cells decreased significantly when the concentration of EP NDs exceeded 20 μg/ml (Fig. 2C and D). Furthermore, flow cytometry (FCM) analysis confirmed that chondrocytes did not undergo apoptosis when treated with EP NDs at concentrations ≤20 μg/ml. Based on these experimental results, 20 μg/ml of EP NDs was selected as the optimal concentration for subsequent experiments (Fig. 2E and S2). To assess the intracellular uptake of EP NDs by chondrocytes, EP NDs (20 μg/ml) were labeled with Cy5.5 and co-incubated with chondrocytes. Antioxidant capacity of EP NDs

Oxidative stress refers to a pathological state in which the balance between oxidative and antioxidative processes is disrupted, mainly characterized by excessive production of ROS that surpasses the body's scavenging capacity, leading to the accumulation of free radicals and subsequent tissue damage [24,51]. Under the persistent oxidative stress microenvironment of OA, excessive ROS act as second messengers to activate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and inflammatory responses, disrupting the balance between cartilage anabolism and catabolism and thereby impairing cartilage integrity [26,52]. In addition, excessive ROS accumulation can directly damage chondrocyte DNA, induce mitochondrial dysfunction, promote cellular senescence and programmed cell death, and further weaken the self-repair ability of cartilage [53]. Notably, ROS play a pivotal hub role in ferroptosis regulation. The continuous overproduction of ROS can disrupt cell membrane structure and stability by inducing lipid peroxidation, thereby driving the iron-dependent cell death process [16,18,19]. A growing body of evidence has confirmed that excessive ROS production in chondrocytes and oxidative stress-induced pathological processes are crucial for the occurrence and progression of OA [24,54]. Previous studies have demonstrated that EGCG, a natural antioxidant, can effectively scavenge ROS and inhibit oxidative stress [27,55]. Meanwhile, Pro, a member of the small-molecule amino acid family, also exerts a protective effect against oxidative stress [44]. These findings collectively lay a solid theoretical foundation for the excellent antioxidant capacity of EP NDs, which are derived from the reaction between EGCG and Pro.

To assess the antioxidant capacity of EP NDs in vitro, we first examined their scavenging effects on four distinct free radicals, including 2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) cation radical (ABTS), 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), superoxide anion (O2−•), and hydroxyl radical (•OH). The results of the free radical scavenging assays clearly demonstrated that the antioxidant activity of EP NDs was gradually enhanced with the increase of its concentration (Fig. 3A–D). Subsequently, we quantitatively analyzed the free radical scavenging capacity of EP NDs to further confirm its antioxidant potential. As shown in Fig. 3E and F, when the concentration of the polyphenol nanodrug reached 20 μg/ml, its scavenging rates against ABTS and DPPH both exceeded 90%. Furthermore, at a concentration of 40 μg/ml, the scavenging efficiencies were further improved to 99.82 ± 0.17% and 96.28 ± 1.30%, respectively. Meanwhile, as illustrated in Fig. 3G and H, 20 μg/ml EP NDs exhibited scavenging rates of 65.04 ± 0.008% and 69.17 ± 3.03% against O2−• and •OH, respectively. These findings indicate that EP NDs possess excellent antioxidant capacity by effectively scavenging free radicals. Then we employed the 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) probe to assess the intracellular ROS scavenging effect of EP NDs in chondrocytes. In this experiment, chondrocytes were stimulated with 200 μM H2O2 to mimic the OA-associated oxidative stress environment, which is characterized by excessive ROS accumulation. The injured chondrocytes were then treated with three different interventions: 20 μg/ml Pro, 20 μg/ml EGCG, and 20 μg/ml EP NDs. Intracellular ROS levels were detected and analyzed using inverted fluorescence microscopy and FCM. As presented in Fig. 3I and K, the fluorescence intensity of the images was positively correlated with intracellular ROS levels, and the H2O2-stimulated group showed significantly higher fluorescence intensity than the untreated Control group. After therapeutic intervention, the fluorescence intensities of the Pro, EGCG, and EP NDs groups were significantly reduced, among which the EP NDs group exhibited the optimal therapeutic effect, with ROS levels close to those of normal cells. Consistent with the fluorescence imaging results, FCM analysis further confirmed that the EP NDs group could effectively eliminate intracellular ROS and displayed superior scavenging efficiency compared with the other two treatment groups (Fig. 3J). In addition, to confirm that the superior antioxidant activity of EP NDs results from their chemical conjugation and nanostructural properties rather than a simple additive effect of EGCG and Pro, a physical mixture group (EP PM, equal-volume mixture of 20 μg/ml Pro and 20 μg/ml EGCG) was included. As a crucial intracellular antioxidant enzyme system, superoxide dismutase (SOD) can effectively scavenge ROS to protect cells from oxidative stress-induced damage, and changes in its activity can reflect the state of oxidative stress [56]. Adenosine triphosphate (ATP), an essential nucleotide, is primarily synthesized in mitochondria through oxidative phosphorylation, and its levels reflect mitochondrial function under oxidative stress conditions [57]. Malondialdehyde (MDA) is one of the key end products of lipid peroxidation, and its production can effectively reflect the degree of oxidative damage to cell membranes, thus serving as an important indicator for assessing oxidative stress levels [58,59]. Therefore, we further evaluated the intracellular antioxidative stress activity of the polyphenol nanoparticle drug by quantifying the expression levels of SOD, ATP, and MDA (Fig. 3L–N). The experimental results suggested that EP NDs could enhance SOD activity, increase ATP production, and inhibit MDA generation, thereby restoring cellular ROS balance and revitalizing the antioxidant system. Finally, from the perspective of cellular morphological changes, Alcian Blue staining imaging demonstrated that EP NDs could prevent cell shrinkage or abnormal swelling induced by oxidative stress (Fig. 3O). In conclusion, EP NDs possess robust antioxidative stress capacity and can effectively scavenge free radicals or ROS, thereby inhibiting the progression of OA.

Efficacy of EP NDs in inhibiting H2O2-induced ferroptosis

Ferroptosis was first clearly reported in 2012 [60] and has been widely recognized as one of the key mechanisms governing the occurrence and progression of various diseases. It plays a particularly crucial role in pathological processes including neurodegenerative diseases [61], cancer and ischemia-reperfusion injury [62]. Beyond these, numerous studies have confirmed that ferroptosis also serves as a core mechanism driving chondrocyte dysfunction and OA progression, making targeted inhibition of ferroptosis a promising therapeutic strategy for delaying disease advancement [9,16,18,19,22,24,25]. In the pathological microenvironment of OA, elevated intra-articular iron levels, lipid peroxidation, excessive ROS accumulation, and impaired antioxidant defense systems are intertwined, collectively promoting ferroptosis [1]. Chondrocyte ferroptosis exacerbates the metabolic imbalance of articular cartilage, resulting in increased expression of MMP13 and degradation of ECM components such as aggrecan (ACAN) and collagen type II (Col2), thereby accelerating OA progression [63]. Furthermore, ferroptosis can induce adverse mitochondrial alterations, including decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, increased mitochondrial membrane density, reduction or loss of cristae, and overall mitochondrial atrophy, which further exacerbates OA [60]. Emerging studies have demonstrated the considerable potential of EGCG [10,27] and Pro [48] in counteracting ferroptosis at the molecular level, thereby providing compelling evidence that EP NDs can represent a promising strategy for the treatment of ferroptosis-associated OA.

We conducted a series of experiments to validate the anti-ferroptotic capacity of the polyphenol-based nanodrug. Chondrocytes were stimulated with 200 μM H2O2 to establish a chondrocyte ferroptosis model [10]. During the ferroptosis process, Fe2+ can mediate the Fenton reaction to generate Fe3+ and •OH, which catalyzes lipid peroxidation in the cell membrane and ultimately leads to cell death [64]. The accumulation of intracellular Fe2+ was assessed using the FerroOrange probe to clarify changes in iron levels following interventions. As shown in Fig. 4A and D, the fluorescence intensity in the H2O2 model group was significantly higher than that in the control group, confirming that oxidative stress induced iron overload. After treatment with Pro, EGCG, and EP NDs, the abnormal iron accumulation was reversed in all groups, with the EP NDs group showing the strongest therapeutic effect. Furthermore, proteins such as GPX4, ACSL4, ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), and solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) are widely recognized as ferroptosis markers [20,65]. We performed immunofluorescence (IF) staining to analyze the expression changes of the key ferroptosis regulators GPX4 and ACSL4 before and after treatment. The results demonstrated that H2O2 stimulation significantly downregulated the anti-ferroptotic protein GPX4 (Fig. 4B and E) and upregulated the pro-ferroptotic protein ACSL4 (Fig. 4C and F). After treatment, all three therapeutic groups exhibited a trend of reversing this abnormal expression pattern: the fluorescence intensity of GPX4 significantly increased, while that of ACSL4 was markedly suppressed. Among them, the EP NDs group achieved the most pronounced effect, with expression levels of GPX4 and ACSL4 nearly returning to those of the normal control group. RT-qPCR analysis of ferroptosis-related genes further supported the anti-ferroptotic activity of EP NDs at the transcriptional level (Fig. 4G–I). Compared with the H2O2 group, Pro, EGCG, and EP NDs treatment significantly upregulated mRNA levels of GPX4 and FTH1, while significantly downregulating ACSL4 mRNA expression. Consistently, the effect of EP NDs was the most pronounced among them. In agreement with the above findings, Western blot analysis further validated that the nanoparticles exerted superior anti-ferroptotic activity by upregulating the ferroptosis suppressor GPX4 and downregulating the ferroptosis promoter ACSL4 (Fig. 4K and L). Moreover, as shown in Fig. S4A and S4B, the EP NDs group exhibited stronger fluorescence intensity and higher GPX4 protein expression levels compared with the EP PM group. Similarly, EP NDs exhibited a stronger inhibitory effect on ACSL4 protein expression than the EP PM group (Fig. S4C and S4D). As illustrated in Fig. 4J, EP NDs can modulate their anabolic/catabolic balance by suppressing the ferroptosis pathway. The restoration of mitochondrial function plays a pivotal role in maintaining cartilage metabolic homeostasis, thereby delaying the progression of OA [66]. Although mitochondrial dysfunction is not exclusive to ferroptosis, the occurrence of ferroptosis leads to the initiation and propagation of mitochondrial lipid peroxidation, subsequently resulting in structural damage and functional impairment of mitochondria [67]. To investigate the mitochondrial protective effects of EP NDs during chondrocyte ferroptosis, we performed JC-1 and MitoSOX Red staining. As shown in Fig. 4M and Q, H2O2 treatment decreased the mitochondrial membrane potential, converting JC-1 aggregates (red fluorescence) into JC-1 monomers (green fluorescence) and a marked reduction in the red-to-green fluorescence ratio, indicative of aggravated mitochondrial damage. Notably, EP NDs administration effectively mitigated the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, demonstrating the strongest recovery among all treated groups. FCM analysis of JC-1-stained cells further confirmed this finding (Fig. S5). MitoSOX Red, a highly selective fluorescent indicator for mitochondrial ROS (mtROS), was used to quantify mtROS levels. As presented in Fig. 4N and R, treatment significantly suppressed mtROS production in H2O2-stimulated cells, with the most pronounced reduction observed in the EP NDs group. Taken together, these results highlight the potent mitochondrial-protective capacity of EP NDs during ferroptosis in OA. Previous studies have established that aberrant iron accumulation during ferroptosis causes a burst of intracellular ROS generation and reduced activity of the GPX4-centered antioxidant enzyme system, thereby increasing chondrocyte susceptibility to oxidative stress and ultimately exacerbating metabolic disturbance in articular cartilage [1,63]. To evaluate the beneficial effect of the polyphenol-based nanodrug on maintaining cartilage metabolic homeostasis during ferroptosis inhibition, we employed IF staining, RT-qPCR, and Western blot assays. As demonstrated in Fig. 4O and S, EP NDs significantly restored the expression of the pro-anabolic protein Col2, with a fluorescence intensity notably superior to that of the Pro and EGCG groups. Similar results were observed for SOX9 IF images (Fig. 4P and T). In the MMP13 IF experiment, high expression of the pro-catabolic protein MMP13 was evident in the H2O2 group, but its fluorescence intensity was substantially attenuated following treatment (Fig. S6A and S6B). Interestingly, EP NDs exhibited stronger inhibition of MMP13 expression compared to Pro and EGCG. As shown in Fig. 4U–Y, H2O2 stimulation significantly downregulated mRNA levels of cartilage-anabolic markers (Col2, SOX9, ACAN) and upregulated those of cartilage-catabolic markers (MMP13, ADAMTS5). At the molecular level, EP NDs demonstrated the greatest capacity among the three treatment groups to restore or maintain the balance between cartilage matrix synthesis and degradation. Finally, Western blot analysis of SOX9, Col2, MMP13, COX2, and ADAMTS5 protein expression (Fig. 4Z and S7) yielded consistent conclusions, confirming that EP NDs most effectively preserve cartilage matrix homeostasis. In summary, compared to Pro or EGCG alone, EP NDs exert more pronounced effects in suppressing catabolic protein expression, and promoting anabolic protein expression to maintain chondrocyte metabolic balance.

Anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory activity of EP NDs

During the progression of OA, ferroptosis and inflammation engage in a detrimental bidirectional cycle. On one hand, lipid peroxidation products released during ferroptosis directly activate NLRP3 inflammasome and NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to the massive release of pro-inflammatory factors including IL-1β. On the other hand, inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α further suppress GPX4 expression and promote iron accumulation, establishing a “ferroptosis-inflammation” positive-feedback loop that collectively exacerbates chondrocyte death and joint destruction [[68], [69], [70]]. Within the inflammatory microenvironment of OA, macrophages predominantly adopt a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, which secretes high levels of cytokines such as IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and iNOS [71]. These pro-inflammatory factors act on chondrocytes, disrupting their structure and function. In contrast, anti-inflammatory M2-phenotype macrophages secrete cytokines such as IL-10 and Arg1 to promote tissue repair [72]. Therefore, repolarizing M1 macrophages toward the M2 phenotype represents a key therapeutic strategy to modulate the bone immune microenvironment, suppress inflammation, and facilitate tissue repair and regeneration, ultimately reversing OA pathology [56,71].

To establish an inflammatory model in vitro, M0 macrophages were stimulated with 100 ng/ml LPS and 20 ng/ml IFN-γ for 24 h to induce polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. Both ferroptosis and inflammatory responses trigger excessive ROS accumulation, and maintenance of intracellular redox homeostasis is a prerequisite for M2 phenotypic repolarization [70,73]. We first investigated the ROS-scavenging capacity of EP NDs in an inflammatory milieu by quantifying ROS levels in LPS/IFN-γ-stimulated macrophages treated with EP NDs. As shown in Fig. 5A and C, intracellular ROS levels in LPS/IFN-γ-primed macrophages were reduced by Pro treatment, further decreased in the EGCG group, and reached the lowest fluorescence intensity in the EP NDs group, verifying the potent ROS-scavenging activity of EP NDs under inflammatory conditions. We next evaluated the potential of macrophages to repolarize from the pro-inflammatory M1 to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype to assess the anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory activity of EP NDs. IF staining was performed to detect the expression of CD86 (an M1 marker) and CD206 (an M2 marker). As shown in Fig. 5B, D, and 5E, treatment with Pro, EGCG, or EP NDs downregulated CD86 expression and upregulated CD206 expression in LPS/IFN-γ-stimulated macrophages, indicating that all three agents possess anti-inflammatory activity and can promote M1-to-M2 repolarization. Among the treatments, EP NDs exhibited the strongest repolarization effect. A schematic illustration summarizes how EP NDs exert anti-inflammatory effects via immunomodulation that drives repolarization from the M1 toward the M2 phenotype (Fig. 5F). Western blot analysis further confirmed the changes in protein expression of M1 and M2 markers following different treatments (Fig. 5G, H, and 5I). The results showed that EP NDs significantly suppressed the expression of the M1-type pro-inflammatory cytokine iNOS and enhanced the expression of the M2-type anti-inflammatory cytokine Arg1, demonstrating a robust shift toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. Consistent with these findings, RT-qPCR analysis revealed that EP NDs effectively upregulated mRNA levels of M2 markers while downregulating those of M1 markers, with the strongest effects among all groups (Fig. 5J and K), confirming their potent repolarization capacity. Interestingly, we observed that Pro alone also exhibited moderate anti-inflammatory and repolarization activity, aligning with previous reports highlighting the favorable anti-inflammatory properties of small-molecule amino acids [27,40,41]. During the inflammatory cascade in OA, excessive pro-inflammatory cytokines induce sustained oxidative stress in chondrocytes, disrupting the balance between anabolism and catabolism, and ultimately leading to cell death and cartilage degeneration [1]. To evaluate the effect of EP NDs on cartilage repair and regeneration in an inflammatory microenvironment, we collected conditioned media from the previously cultured macrophages treated with Pro, EGCG, or EP NDs (designated as P-Medium, E-Medium, and EP-Medium, respectively) and used them to co-culture LPS/IFN-γ-stimulated chondrocytes. IF staining for the cartilage matrix anabolism proteins SOX9 and ACAN revealed that LPS/IFN-γ-induced inflammation markedly suppressed the anabolic activity of chondrocytes (Fig. 5L, N, 5M, and 5O). Co-culture with all three conditioned media partially reversed this inhibitory effect, with the EP Medium group exhibiting the strongest recovery. As shown in Fig. S8, the EP Medium group displayed the lowest fluorescence intensity of the cartilage catabolic protein ADAMTS5 among all treated groups, indicating that EP NDs significantly attenuated cartilage catabolism under inflammatory conditions. Subsequent quantitative RT-qPCR analysis of cartilage metabolism-related genes further demonstrated that treatments upregulated mRNA levels of the anabolic markers SOX9 and ACAN while downregulating those of the catabolic markers COX2 and MMP13 (Fig. 5P– S). Finally, Western blot analysis confirmed that EP-Medium provided the most effective regulation of cartilage matrix synthesis and degradation, thereby promoting cartilage repair and regeneration (Fig. 5T– W). In conclusion, EP NDs possess remarkable anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties. In the inflammatory microenvironment of OA, they drive macrophage repolarization toward the M2 phenotype, downregulate pro-inflammatory mediators, upregulate anti-inflammatory factors, and ultimately restore cartilage metabolic homeostasis while inhibiting cartilage degeneration.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis of H2O2-induced chondrocytes with EP NDs intervention

To elucidate the key molecular mechanisms by which EP NDs suppress ferroptosis-related OA, we performed transcriptome sequencing analysis of H2O2-induced chondrocytes (H2O2 group) and H2O2-induced chondrocytes treated with EP NDs (EP NDs group). The volcano plot of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified 2004 DEGs between the two groups, including 948 upregulated and 1056 downregulated genes (Fig. 6A). Heatmap analysis revealed that EP NDs systematically modulated gene expression compared to the H2O2 group: they significantly downregulated multiple detrimental gene clusters, including pro-ferroptotic genes (ACSL4, NCOA4, NOX1, NOX3), cartilage-catabolic genes (MMP9, MMP13, ADAMTS4, ADAMTS5, COX2), and inflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α), while simultaneously upregulating anti-ferroptotic genes (GPX4, SLC7A11, SLC3A2, FTH1) and anabolic genes (COL2, SOX9, ACAN, CSPG4), resulting in an overall gene expression profile shifted toward a protective state (Fig. 6B). Subsequently, we performed functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs to identify associated biological functions and signaling pathways. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis indicated that EP NDs suppressed ferroptosis and alleviated inflammation through synergistic regulation of multiple signaling pathways, thereby promoting cartilage repair and regeneration in OA (Fig. 6C). Among these, activation of the NRF2 pathway upregulated the expression of downstream targets including GPX4 and GSH, strengthening the cellular antioxidant defense system and directly inhibiting core processes of ferroptosis [22,70]. The MAPK signaling pathway, particularly the p38, ERK, and JNK branches, serves as a central hub linking oxidative stress, inflammation, and ferroptosis [74]. The significant enrichment of the MAPK pathway strongly suggests that EP NDs may effectively block its overactivation, thereby interrupting the vicious cycle among oxidative stress, inflammation, and ferroptosis, and ultimately contributing to anti-inflammatory, anti-ferroptotic, and cartilage-protective effects. Therapeutic efficacy of EP NDs in vivo

Building on the multifunctional synergy of EP NDs demonstrated in vitro—including antioxidant, anti-ferroptotic, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory activities—we further investigated their therapeutic potential in vivo. A rat OA model was established via anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) [24]. To determine an optimal dosing interval and the retention profile of EP NDs in the knee joint, Cy5.5 fluorescent probe alone (Cy5.5 group) and Cy5.5-labeled EP NDs (Cy5.5 + EP NDs group) were injected intra-articularly into two groups of rats, respectively. In vivo fluorescence imaging was performed at multiple time points to monitor changes in fluorescence intensity. As shown in Fig. 7A and B, the fluorescence signal in the Cy5.5 group diminished rapidly and was nearly undetectable by day 9. In contrast, the Cy5.5 + EP NDs group exhibited a much more gradual decline in fluorescence, with detectable signal still present in the knee joint 15 days post-injection. These results confirm the favorable retention and sustained presence of EP NDs in the joint, supporting a dosing interval of once every two weeks for subsequent therapeutic studies. One week after surgery, rats received intra-articular injection treatment with PBS, Pro, EGCG, or EP NDs once every two weeks for a total of eight weeks (Fig. 7C). In addition, ex vivo imaging of major organs revealed the strongest fluorescence signal in the kidneys, indicating that EP NDs were primarily metabolized and cleared via the renal route. OA is a chronic and multifactorial disease characterized by cartilage injury and degeneration, as well as pathological alterations in the subchondral bone [66]. We evaluated the therapeutic effects of EP NDs on the morphology and microstructure of subchondral bone using micro-computed tomography (micro-CT). Micro-CT imaging revealed severe joint deformation, erosive changes, prominent osteophyte formation, and subchondral bone sclerosis in the knee joints of OA rats (PBS group) (Fig. 7D and E). Quantification of osteophyte numbers demonstrated that treatment with Pro, EGCG, or EP NDs reduced osteophyte formation in the knee joint, with the EP NDs group achieving the optimal inhibitory effect (2.00 ± 0.82 osteophytes) (Fig. S11). Furthermore, histomorphometric analysis of CT images yielded a series of bone structural parameters, including bone surface-to-volume ratio (BS/BV), trabecular thickness (Tb. Th), bone volume-to-tissue volume ratio (BV/TV), trabecular pattern factor (Tb. Pf), trabecular separation (Tb. Sp), and trabecular number (Tb. N) (Fig. 7F–I and S12). BS/BV reflects bone remodeling activity, with a decrease indicating reduced bone erosion. Tb.Th and BV/TV represent trabecular bone mass and microstructure integrity. Lower Tb. Pf values indicate better trabecular connectivity, corresponding to reduced bone fragmentation. A decrease in Tb. Sp signifies less bone loss, as it reflects a reduction in inter-trabecular spacing. Meanwhile, Tb. N provides a direct quantitative measure of trabecular density [76]. The PBS group exhibited severe bone and cartilage erosion, reflected by significant decreases in Tb. Th, BV/TV, and Tb. N, along with increases in BS/BV, Tb. Pf, and Tb. Sp, indicating that bone resorption predominated over bone formation. All treatments partially reversed these osteodestructive changes, with the EP NDs group showing the most pronounced recovery of subchondral bone architecture. Compared to the PBS group, the EP NDs group showed the following changes: BS/BV decreased from 31.19 ± 0.70 to 22.99 ± 0.75, Tb. Pf decreased from 7.45 ± 0.71 to 2.73 ± 0.56, and Tb. Sp decreased from 0.36 ± 0.01 to 0.17 ± 0.01, while BV/TV increased from 0.36 ± 0.01 to 0.49 ± 0.01, Tb. Th increased from 0.051 ± 0.005 to 0.095 ± 0.005, and Tb. N increased from 3.72 ± 0.15 to 5.09 ± 0.11. Moreover, Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of rat knee joints revealed reduced joint effusion in all three treatment groups compared to the PBS group. Notably, the EP NDs group exhibited T2-weighted signal recovery nearly comparable to that of the sham group, visually demonstrating its ability to counteract OA progression (Fig. 7J).

To further assess structural changes in articular cartilage and synovium following in vivo treatment with EP NDs, we performed histological analyses using Safranin O-Fast Green (SO-FG), Toluidine Blue (TB), and Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE) staining. As shown in Fig. 7K–M, the sham group displayed intact and smooth articular cartilage surfaces, normal chondrocyte morphology, evenly distributed proteoglycans in the ECM, as well as thin and continuous synovial tissue with a smooth surface, clear boundaries, and minimal inflammatory cell infiltration. In contrast, the PBS-treated OA model exhibited severe cartilage surface erosion, fissures, fibrillation, disorganized chondrocyte arrangement, substantial proteoglycan loss, synovial hyperplasia with increased cell layers, and prominent inflammatory cell infiltration. However, EP NDs markedly reversed these characteristic OA pathological alterations, restoring the damaged articular cartilage to a state close to normal. The chondro-protective effect of EP NDs was reflected in the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) and Mankin scores, both widely used to evaluate the severity of osteoarthritic lesions [71]. Among all treatment groups, EP NDs provided the greatest therapeutic benefit, reducing the OARSI score from 6.5 ± 1.0 in the PBS group to 0.875 ± 0.25 and the Mankin score from 9.75 ± 0.96 to 2.0 ± 0.82 (Fig. 7N and S13). Similarly, EP NDs significantly improved synovial pathology, lowering the synovitis score from 5.75 ± 0.5 in the PBS group to 1.25 ± 0.5 (Fig. 7O). Analysis of glycosaminoglycan (GAG) content confirmed that EP NDs effectively inhibited proteoglycan degradation, thereby helping to maintain cartilage integrity (Fig. 7P). To sum up, EP NDs not only protect cartilage but also enhance subchondral bone remodeling, representing a promising therapeutic strategy for OA.

Integrated effects of EP NDs on ferroptosis, metabolic homeostasis, and immunomodulation in vivo

After the eight-week treatment period in the rat OA model, we performed immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of proteins related to ferroptosis, cartilage metabolism, and inflammation to evaluate the in vivo regulatory effects of EP NDs on ferroptosis, cartilage homeostasis, and anti-inflammatory activity. As shown in Fig. 8A, E, and 8F, the intensity of GPX4 staining was markedly stronger in the Sham and EP NDs groups compared to the PBS, Pro, and EGCG groups, while the staining of ACSL4 showed the opposite pattern. These results confirm that EP NDs significantly upregulate GPX4 and downregulate ACSL4, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis and protecting cartilage. For cartilage anabolic proteins (Col2, SOX9, and ACAN), EP NDs produced consistently darker brown staining, which was significantly more intense than that in other treatment groups (Fig. 8B). Quantitative analysis further verified that the expression levels of Col2, SOX9, and ACAN were notably higher in the EP NDs group compared to the Pro and EGCG groups (Fig. 8G–I). In addition, EP NDs effectively regulated cartilage catabolic proteins (MMP13 and ADAMTS5), as evidenced by significantly lighter staining in the EP NDs group relative to the PBS group (Fig. 8C). Corresponding quantitative data confirmed that EP NDs substantially downregulated the expression of these catabolic markers (Fig. 8J and K). IHC staining of IL-1β revealed the darkest brown signal in the PBS group, indicating substantial inflammatory cell infiltration in the synovium. As demonstrated in Fig. 8L, EP NDs most effectively suppressed the release of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β. Notably, IF staining of CD86 and CD206 in tissue sections highlighted the superior immunomodulatory capacity of EP NDs in the complex inflammatory OA microenvironment: among the three treatment groups, the EP NDs group exhibited the highest expression of the anti-inflammatory marker CD206 and the lowest expression of the pro-inflammatory marker CD86 (Fig. 8D and S14). In a word, these results present the robust in vivo efficacy of EP NDs in modulating ferroptosis, maintaining cartilage metabolic balance, and exerting anti-inflammatory and immunoregulatory functions. At last, to assess the in vivo biosafety of EP NDs, we performed histological analysis of the rats’ major visceral organs (including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) and knee joints, in conjunction with blood analyses. HE staining revealed no notable histopathological alterations in any of the major organs across treatment groups compared with the sham group, confirming that none of the tested materials induced organ toxicity (Fig. S15). Moreover, as shown in Fig. S16, no obvious local tissue damage was observed in the knee joints of rats following intra-articular injection of Pro, EGCG, or EP NDs. In addition, routine blood biochemistry markers and hematological parameters in all treatment groups remained within normal reference ranges relative to the sham group, indicating an absence of systemic toxicity (Figs. S17 and S18). Together, these results demonstrate that EP NDs represent a safe and reliable intra-articular therapeutic strategy with favorable biosafety in vivo for OA.

While EP NDs have demonstrated promising multi-target therapeutic effects in both in vitro and in vivo experiments for delaying the progression of ferroptosis-associated OA, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged, along with directions for future research. First, although the H2O2-induced ferroptosis model was successfully established, it does not fully replicate the complex pathological microenvironment of ferroptosis-associated OA. Therefore, validation experiments using selective ferroptosis inducers, such as Erastin or RSL3, would be beneficial. Furthermore, the inclusion of ferroptosis-specific markers tests (e.g., MDA, 4-HNE) or rescue experiments with ferrostatin-1 could further confirm successful model induction. Second, the EP PM group was included in some key in vitro experiments but not in the in vivo study. Thus, future experiments should incorporate this group to enhance the completeness of the study. Third, transcriptomic and Western blot analyses suggest that EP NDs exert their effects by activating the NRF2 pathway and inhibiting the MAPK signaling pathway. Although we have validated the key role of the NRF2 pathway in OA treatment using the NRF2 inhibitor ML385, further rescue experiments with specific inhibitors or siRNAs are required to determine whether the inhibition of the MAPK signaling pathway is a direct or indirect effect. Fourth, it should be noted that, based on in vitro cytocompatibility results, a single dose (20 μg/ml) of EP NDs was used for all in vivo experiments. Although this dose exhibited significant therapeutic effects in the rat OA model, the lack of dose-response analysis limits our understanding of the optimal therapeutic window and potential toxicity threshold. Future studies with multiple dose groups (e.g., low, medium, and high doses) are necessary to clarify the dose-response relationship and evaluate the safe concentration range of EP NDs for clinical application. Fifth, this study primarily relied on a post-traumatic OA model (ACLT) in rats, which may not fully represent the heterogeneity of human OA, particularly age-related, metabolic, or spontaneous OA. Finally, although the short-term biosafety data in rats are encouraging, the long-term safety and biodegradation profile of EP NDs in large animal models remain to be systematically evaluated.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a green and carrier-free self-assembled polyphenol nanodrug (EP NDs) via a one-step Mannich condensation between EGCG and Pro for the treatment of ferroptosis-associated OA. Through a series of in vitro and in vivo experiments, we demonstrate that Pro possesses intrinsic anti-ferroptotic activity for the first time, thereby validating our initial hypothesis. Pro can be covalently conjugated with EGCG to form a stable nanodrug, expanding the therapeutic application of small-molecule amino acids in the treatment of OA. Furthermore, EP NDs establish a multi-target therapeutic network that simultaneously activates the NRF2 pathway and inhibits the MAPK signaling cascade, achieving synergistic anti-ferroptotic, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory effects within a single nanoplatform. The entirely carrier-free, metal-free, and protein/polysaccharide-free design of EP NDs minimizes potential biotoxicity, immunogenicity, and batch-to-batch variability, offering a green, simple, and efficient strategy for OA therapy. Collectively, this work not only provides a novel approach for ferroptosis-associated OA, but also establishes a paradigm for integrating natural products and small-molecule amino acids into multifunctional nanodrugs for the treatment of complex degenerative diseases.

Experimental section

Materials

All chemicals were sourced from commercial suppliers and used without further purification. Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG, E276041), formaldehyde (F111941), and Cy5.5 (C651516) were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co. Proline (Pro, ST1500), Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, C0037), Calcein-AM/PI live/dead staining kit (C2030S), 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, S0033M), 4′,6′-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, P0131), superoxide dismutase (SOD) assay kit (S0101S), malondialdehyde (MDA) assay kit (S0131), adenosine triphosphate (ATP) assay kit (S0150), mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit with JC-1 (C2006), Trizol reagent (T718636), 4% paraformaldehyde fixative (P0099), and bovine serum albumin (BSA, ST023) were all sourced from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). In addition, the hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining kit (C0105S), saffron o–fast green (SO-FG) cartilage staining kit (C0621S), methylaniline blue (TB) staining solution (C0637), and Hoechst 33,342 (C1022) were also purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). The Cellular Ferrous Ion Detection Fluorescent Probe (FerroOrange, F374) was obtained from Dojindo (Japan). Alcian Blue staining reagent (G1560), TRITC Phalloidin (CA1610), ABTS radical scavenging assay kit (BC4775), and DPPH radical scavenging assay kit (BC4755) were supplied by Solarbio (China). Nearly all primary antibodies were procured from San Ying Biotechnology (China).

Cell culture

The immortalized mouse chondrocyte cell line ATDC5 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA), while RAW264.7 macrophages were acquired from Servicebio (China). Cells were cultured in F12/DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cells were maintained and passaged in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 as indicated, with the medium replaced every 2–3 days.

Preparation process of EP NDs

To prepare EP NDs, EGCG (183 mg) was dissolved in 40 ml deionized water under magnetic stirring until completely dissolved. After 15 min, formaldehyde (30 μl, 30% formaldehyde in water) was added to the opalescent solution with continued magnetic stirring. Five minutes later, Pro solution (3 ml, 10 mg/ml) was introduced into the opalescent solution. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 4–5 h, during which the opalescent solution turned milky, indicating successful synthesis of polyphenol nanodrugs. The polyphenol nanoparticles were collected by centrifugation (10,000 r/min, 10 min) and washed three times with deionized water. The obtained polyphenol nanoparticles were redispersed in deionized water and stored at 4 °C.

Characterization of EP NDs

The morphology of EP NDs was observed using scanning electron microscope (SEM, S-4800; Hitachi, Japan) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100; JEOL, Japan). Elemental mappings of EP NDs were performed via energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS; Phenom ProX, G5, Eindhoven, Netherlands). The size and zeta potential of EP NDs were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS; Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS, Germany). Structural analysis of EGCG, Pro, and EP NDs was conducted using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR; Thermo Fisher Nicolet 6700, USA). The chemical composition of EP NDs was examined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF/TOF) of EP NDs was acquired in reflectron mode with a CHCA matrix on a Bruker Autoflex SPEED instrument (USA). Bruker 400M (Bruker, America) was used to analyze the 1H NMR of EP NDs and EGCG. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of EGCG, Pro, and EP NDs were measured using a Bruker D8 advanced powder diffractometer (Bruker, USA). UV-vis absorption spectra were collected by a UV spectrophotometer (SHIMADZU-UV-2600 I, Japan).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed with the CCK-8 assay following culture in complete medium containing EP NDs. Chondrocytes were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated with F12 medium supplemented with varying concentrations of EP NDs (0, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80 μg/ml) for 24 and 72 h. At the end of each incubation period, each well was treated with a solution containing 10 μl of CCK-8 reagent and 90 μl of F12 medium, followed by incubation in a light-proof incubator at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (VARIOSKAN FLASH, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). All cell experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates.

Alcian blue staining

Chondrocytes were cultured in 24-well plates with complete medium containing PBS or EP NDs at concentrations of 5, 10, and 20 μg/ml for 24 and 72 h. After incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Following fixation, cells were washed 2–3 times with PBS and stained overnight with Alcian Blue (G1560, Solarbio, pH 2.5). Finally, after washing the cells 2–3 times with PBS, images were captured using an inverted microscope (Leica, Germany).

Live/dead staining

Chondrocytes were cultured in 24-well plates with F12 medium containing different concentrations of EP NDs (0, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80 μg/ml) for 24 and 72 h. Following incubation, cell viability was assessed using a Live/Dead Cell Double Staining Kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. After staining for 20–30 min in the dark, the cells were gently washed three times with PBS. Fluorescence images were then captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany).

Cell apoptosis staining

The apoptosis rate of Chondrocytes was examined via FCM after treatment with F12 medium containing different concentrations of EP NDs. Cells were exposed to 0, 5, 10, and 20 μg/ml EP NDs for 24 h, harvested by trypsinization, and subsequently subjected to apoptosis detection using an Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1062S, Beyotime) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. FCM data were analyzed with FlowJo software following standard analytical procedures.

Cellular uptake of EP NDs

After co-culturing chondrocytes with Cy5.5-labeled EP nanoparticles for 5 h, the cells were washed three times with PBS. They were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (T9284, Sigma, USA) for another 15 min. Following three additional PBS washes, the cells were stained with 100 μM TRITC Phalloidin (CA1610, Solarbio) and Hoechst 33,342 (C1022, Beyotime, diluted 1:1000) for 20 min. Finally, cellular uptake of EP NDs was visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica TCS SP8 X, Germany).

Antioxidant property analysis of EP NDs

The free radical scavenging capacity of EP NDs was assessed using DPPH, ABTS, superoxide anion (O2−•), and hydroxyl radical (•OH) assays. For the ABTS assay (BC4775, Solarbio), 0.2 ml of ABTS solution (7.4 mmol/L) was mixed with 0.2 ml of potassium persulfate solution (2.6 mmol/L) and incubated in the dark for 24 h to generate ABTS radicals. The resulting mixture was then diluted 50-fold with PBS to prepare the working solution. Different concentrations of EP NDs (0, 5, 10, 20, and 40 μg/ml) in 0.6 ml were combined with the ABTS working solution, allowed to react in the dark for 10 min, and the absorbance was measured at 734 nm using a UV spectrophotometer.

In the DPPH assay (BC4755, Solarbio), 700 μl of DPPH working solution (0.1 mM) was mixed with 100 μl of EP NDs at varying concentrations (0, 5, 10, 20, and 40 μg/ml). After incubation in the dark at 25 °C for 30 min, absorbance was recorded at 517 nm. O2−• and •OH scavenging assays were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions using commercial kits (HYF5129F and HJ19273, respectively, HUAYUN).

Intracellular antioxidant stress and protective capability of EP NDs

The oxidative stress model was established in chondrocytes by treatment with 200 μM H2O2. The cells were divided into five experimental groups: Control (untreated), H2O2 (oxidative stress only), Pro (H2O2 + 20 μg/ml Pro), EGCG (H2O2 + 20 μg/ml EGCG), and EP NDs (H2O2 + 20 μg/ml EP NDs). Following 24 h of co-culture, the medium was discarded, and the cells were gently washed three times with warm PBS. Intracellular ROS levels were detected using a 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) assay kit (S0033M, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After staining, the cells were incubated in the dark at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 30 min, washed three times with serum-free medium, and observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany). Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software for statistical analysis.

Following the described chondrocyte treatment protocol, cells were washed three times with PBS and detached using trypsin. Subsequently, cells were incubated with the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere under light-protected conditions for 30 min, followed by three additional washes. FCM analysis was then performed on the stained cells. Data were processed and analyzed with FlowJo software according to standard procedures.

ATP content in chondrocytes was measured using the ATP assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Total SOD activity was determined with a corresponding SOD assay kit. The extent of lipid peroxidation was evaluated using the MDA assay kit to assess oxidative damage across treatment groups. Furthermore, Alcian Blue staining was performed to evaluate the protective effect of EP NDs on chondrocytes under oxidative stress.

Fe2+ detection

The FerroOrange fluorescent probe (24 μg) was dissolved in 35 μl of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock solution, which was then diluted with physiological saline to obtain a working concentration of 1 μmol/L. Cells from each treatment group were incubated with the working solution for 30 min at 37 °C under 5% CO2 without washing, and subsequently observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica TCS SP8 X, Germany).

Immunofluorescence staining

Following 48 h of pre-intervention culture, cells from each treatment group were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. After washing with PBS, cells were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min, followed by another PBS wash. Non-specific binding was blocked by incubating the cells with 5% BSA for 1 h. Subsequently, cells were incubated overnight (12–16 h) at 4 °C with primary rabbit antibodies—including GPX4 (30388-1-AP, Proteintech), ACSL4 (22401-1-AP, Proteintech), Col2 (28459-1-AP, Proteintech), SOX9 (67439-1-Ig, Proteintech), ACAN (68350-1-Ig, Proteintech), and MMP13 (18165-1-AP, Proteintech)—diluted at ratios ranging from 1:200 to 1:500. After three washes with PBST (P1031, Solarbio), cells were incubated with a goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (ab150077, Proteintech) diluted 1:500 at room temperature for 2 h. Following another round of PBST washes, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI-containing mounting medium to prevent fluorescence quenching. Stained cells were imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany), and fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software.

RT-qPCR assay

Primers for RT-qPCR were designed using PrimerBank and their specificity was confirmed by NCBI BLAST analysis (Supplementary Table 1). Cells from each treatment group were trypsinized and total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed with TaqMan reagents (Takara, Japan) on a Light Cycler 480 system. The mRNA expression levels of GPX4, FTH1, ACSL4, Col2, SOX9, ACAN, ADAMTS5, MMP13, COX2, CD86, and CD206 were assessed. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as an internal reference for normalizing target gene expression.

Western blot assay

Western blot assay was performed to detect the expression levels of relevant proteins. Chondrocytes from each experimental group were collected, and total protein was extracted by using RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime) containing PMSF as a protease and phosphatase inhibitor. After centrifugation at 12,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected and mixed with loading buffer at a 4:1 ratio. The mixture was then heated at 95 °C for 5–10 min. The extracted proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) at a constant voltage of 120 V, followed by transfer onto a PVDF membrane at 400 mA in transfer buffer. After blocking with 5% BSA for 1 h, the membrane was incubated overnight (12–16 h) at 4 °C with the following primary rabbit antibodies: GPX4 (30388-1-AP, 1:2000, Proteintech), ACSL4 (22401-1-AP, 1:2000, Proteintech), SOX9 (67439-1-Ig, 1:1000, Proteintech), Col2 (28459-1-AP, 1:2000, Proteintech), MMP13 (18165-1-AP, 1:2000, Proteintech), ADAMTS5 (60178-1-Ig, 1:2000, Proteintech), COX2 (27308-1-AP, Proteintech), and β-actin (66009-1-Ig, 1:3000, Proteintech). The membrane was then washed three times with TBST (ST673, Beyotime) and incubated with a diluted goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. After three additional TBST washes, protein bands were visualized using the BeyoECL Plus kit (P0018M, Beyotime). β-Actin was used as an internal loading control for normalizing target protein expression.

Mitochondrial function analysis

Mitochondrial membrane potential was measured by using the JC-1-based assay kit (C2006, Beyotime). Following the manufacturer's instructions, chondrocytes were incubated with JC-1 staining solution at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing three times with 1× JC-1 assay buffer, samples were observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany). Mitochondrial ROS were assessed with MitoSOX™ Red reagent (S0061S, Beyotime) as per the protocol, and fluorescence images were captured using a confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8 X, Germany). Fluorescence intensities from all images were quantified with ImageJ software.

Evaluation of the repolarization of macrophages

Based on different treatments, RAW264.7 macrophages were divided into five groups: Control, LPS/IFN-γ, Pro (LPS/IFN-γ + Pro), EGCG (LPS/IFN-γ + EGCG), and EP NDs (LPS/IFN-γ + EP NDs). RAW264.7 macrophages were cultured overnight in 6-well plates. Except for the Control group, all other groups were then treated with 100 ng/ml LPS and 20 ng/ml IFN-γ for 24 h. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated for another 24 h in fresh DMEM medium or DMEM containing Pro (20 μg/ml), EGCG (20 μg/ml), or EP NDs (20 μg/ml). The protein expression levels of CD86 (13395-1-AP, Proteintech) and CD206 (18704-1-AP, Proteintech) were evaluated by IF staining, while their corresponding mRNA expression levels were assessed by RT-qPCR. In addition, protein levels of iNOS (18985-1-AP, Proteintech) and Arg-1 (16001-1-AP, Proteintech) were assessed by Western blot analysis.

Chondrocytes were divided into five treatment groups: Control, LPS/IFN-γ, P Medium (LPS/IFN-γ + P Medium), E Medium (LPS/IFN-γ + E Medium), and EP Medium (LPS/IFN-γ + EP Medium). After overnight seeding in 6-well plates, all groups except Control were stimulated with 100 ng/ml LPS and 20 ng/ml IFN-γ for 24 h. Cells were then washed with PBS and incubated for an additional 24 h in fresh DMEM or conditioned media derived from macrophage cultures: P Medium (supernatant from Pro-treated macrophages), E Medium (supernatant from EGCG-treated macrophages), or EP Medium (supernatant from EP NDs-treated macrophages). SOX9 and ACAN expression in chondrocytes was evaluated by IF staining. mRNA levels of SOX9, ACAN, COX2, and MMP13 were analyzed by RT-qPCR. Protein expression of SOX9, Col2, and ADAMTS5 was assessed by Western blotting.

Transcriptome sequencing

Chondrocytes were treated with H2O2 alone or in combination with EP NDs, resulting in two experimental groups: H2O2 and EP NDs (H2O2 + EP NDs). Total RNA was extracted and purified from chondrocytes using TRIzol reagent (Sigma, USA). RNA sequencing libraries were prepared and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) by Shanghai Meiji Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. Subsequent functional enrichment analyses—including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses—were conducted to elucidate potential biological mechanisms. A corrected p-value (padj) < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for the enrichment results of GO and KEGG analyses.

Animal study

All Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were housed under standard laboratory conditions at 23–25 °C with a 12-h light/dark cycle and had free access to water and standard laboratory chow. All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University (Approval No. WP20250350).

Biodistribution and retention of EC NPs in vivo

To assess the distribution and retention of EC NPs in vivo, rats were administered Cy5.5-labeled EC NPs or free Cy5.5 via intra-articular injection. In vivo imaging was performed at days 0, 3, 5, 7, 9, 12, and 15 post-injection using an IVIS system with the following parameters: excitation wavelength 745 nm, emission wavelength 800 nm, and exposure time 1 s.

Construction of ACLT-induced OA model

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of EP NDs against OA in vivo, an OA model was induced in SD rats by transecting the anterior cruciate ligament of the right knee. Animals were randomly divided into five groups: Sham (sham surgery), PBS (ACLT alone), Pro (ACLT + Pro), EGCG (ACLT + EGCG), and EP NDs (ACLT + EP NDs). Briefly, the ACL was exposed and transected in the right hind limb of rats in the latter four groups. In the Sham group, the ligament was exposed but left intact.

Therapy of animal models of OA

SD rats were randomly assigned to five groups: Sham, PBS, Pro, EGCG, and EP NDs. Animals in the Sham and PBS groups received an intra-articular injection of 50 μl PBS. Rats in the Pro group were injected with 50 μl saline containing Pro (20 μg/ml), the EGCG group received 50 μl saline containing EGCG (20 μg/ml), and the EP NDs group was administered 50 μl saline containing EP NDs (20 μg/ml). Since the pathological features of OA were largely established by one week after ACLT, this time point was used as the standard baseline for initiating therapeutic intervention [24,76,77]. All injections were performed once every two weeks. After 8 weeks, all rats were euthanized and knee joints were collected for subsequent analysis.

Magnetic resonance imaging

High-resolution knee joint images of SD rats after 8 weeks of treatment were acquired by using T1-T2 dual-mode MRI on a 7 T PharmaScan70/16 system (Bruker, Germany). Briefly, rats were anesthetized with a combination of medetomidine and sevoflurane, placed supine on the MRI scanning platform, and subjected to three-dimensional MRI acquisition. Relaxation rates, including r1 (or r2), were measured. For T1-weighted imaging at 7 T, the parameters were set as follows: TR = 867 ms, TE = 6.92 ms, slice thickness = 1 mm. For T2-weighted imaging, the parameters were: TR = 3000 ms, TE = 10 ms, slice thickness = 1 mm.

Micro-CT scanning and analysis

After 8 weeks of treatment, rats were euthanized, and knee joints were carefully dissected. The joints were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 3 days and then scanned on a micro-CT imaging system (SkyScan 1276, Bruker, Germany) using the following parameters: 0.25 mm aluminum filter, 45 kV voltage, 200 μA current, and 6.5 μm image resolution. The acquired data were reconstructed into three-dimensional images using NRecon software (Bruker, USA). Key parameters for evaluating knee joint repair—including BS/BV, BV/TV, Tb. Th, Tb. Pf, Tb. Sp and Tb. N—were analyzed with Data Viewer (Bruker, USA) and CTAn software (Bruker, USA).

Histological staining

After micro-CT scanning, rat knee joint specimens were decalcified in EDTA solution for 14 days, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Harvested knee joints were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 48 h, after which the right knees were decalcified in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, pH 7.4) for 7 days at 37 °C with continuous shaking at 80 rpm. Following graded dehydration, the specimens were paraffin-embedded and sectioned coronally at 4 μm thickness for histological evaluation. Articular cartilage structure was examined using SO-FG, TB and HE staining. OARSI, Mankin and synovitis scores were then applied to each stained section. Cartilage degradation was independently scored by two investigators blinded to the treatment groups.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence analyses

For IHC and IF analyses, tissue sections were first subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval followed by blocking. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the respective primary antibodies listed below. For IHC visualization, biotinylated secondary antibodies and 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) were applied. For IF staining, secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 594 were used. The primary antibodies employed included: GPX4 (30388-1-AP, Proteintech), ACSL4 (22401-1-AP, Proteintech), SOX9 (67439-1-Ig, Proteintech), ACAN (68350-1-Ig, Proteintech), Col2 (28459-1-AP, Proteintech), MMP13 (18165-1-AP, Proteintech), ADAMTS5 (60178-1-Ig, Proteintech), CD86 (13395-1-AP, Proteintech), CD206 (18704-1-AP, Proteintech), and IL-1β (16806-1-AP, Proteintech).

Biocompatibility assessment

Blood samples from rats were centrifuged for biochemical analysis to assess hepatic and renal function. Serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TBIL), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CREA) and blood uric acid (UA) were determined using an automated biochemical analyzer (TBA-2000FR, Toshiba, Japan) to evaluate potential systemic toxicity. A complete blood count (CBC) was also conducted to monitor hematological parameters (Supplementary Table 2). After 8 weeks of treatment, rats were euthanized. Major organs (liver, heart, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and processed for hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining to examine histopathological changes.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times, and representative data are presented. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± SD. (n ≥ 3) Comparisons between two groups were analyzed by Student's t-test, while comparisons among three or more groups were assessed by ordinary one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
