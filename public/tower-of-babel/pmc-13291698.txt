Comprehensive morphometric analysis of the jugular foramen and its septations in cadavers and dry skulls
Authors: Suphatsorn Hathaidechadusadee, Vilai Chentanez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe jugular foramen (JF) is a complex anatomical region transmitting cranial nerves IX–XI and the internal jugular vein, where variations in its dural and bony septations can influence tumor spread, venous drainage, and surgical access. This study examined the morphology and morphometry of the JF using 200 dry skulls and 39 formalin-fixed cadavers. Extracranial features, including bony septation (BS), jugular dome, and JF–stylomastoid foramen distance, were evaluated in skulls, while intracranial dural septation (DS), sigmoid sinus (SS) diameter, and JF–internal acoustic meatus distance was measured in cadavers. BS was identified in 42.8% of foramina, most commonly Type I (37.3%), followed by Type II (5.0%) and Type III (0.5%). Type I BS predominantly originated from the temporal bone. The jugular dome was bilaterally present in 52.5% of skulls and was significantly deeper in males. The JF was consistently larger on the right in both length and width, while the JF–stylomastoid foramen distance was greater on the left. DS was present in all cadavers, with Type I predominating (89.7%), followed by Type III (3.9%) and Type IV (6.4%), whereas Type II (absence of septation) was not observed. The SS was significantly larger on the right and in males, and the mean JF–internal acoustic meatus distance measured approximately 6 mm bilaterally. These findings highlight distinct JF morphology in the regional anatomical collections emphasizing the importance of understanding anatomical variation for improving radiologic interpretation and ensuring safe skull base surgical approaches.Keywords: Bony septation, Dura mater, Jugular foramen, Morphology, Neurosurgery

Introduction

The jugular foramen (JF) contains the glossopharyngeal nerve (cranial nerve [CN] IX), vagus nerve (CN X), and accessory nerve (CN XI), along with the internal jugular vein (IJV), making it a critical corridor at the posterior skull base. Lesions such as paragangliomas, schwannomas, and meningiomas, as well as surgical manipulation during tumor resection, can compromise these structures and lead to significant neurological deficits [1]. The JF is commonly described as being divided into pars nervosa and pars vascularis by a dural septation (DS), a configuration that influences characteristic tumor growth patterns and can be visualized on high-resolution magnetic resonance imaging [2-5]. Within the JF, CN IX, X, and XI follow an anteromedial to posterolateral arrangement, and DS variations may alter nerve compartmentalization, contributing to distinct patterns of tumor extension [3, 5, 6].

Bony septation (BS), or intrajugular projections, further subdivides the foramen and may compress adjacent CNs, influencing symptomatology and complicating surgical drilling [7, 8]. The right JF is typically larger, reflecting asymmetry in IJV caliber, but its dimensions vary by sex, side, ethnicity, and regional population [8-12]. Venous dominance patterns also correlate with transverse–sigmoid sinus asymmetry on magnetic resonance venography, a factor relevant to preoperative planning [13]. The jugular dome reflects development of the superior jugular bulb; absence of a dome suggests an underdeveloped bulb and may facilitate tumor invasion toward the middle ear, whereas a high-riding bulb reduces the operative window in translabyrinthine approaches [8, 14, 15].

The stylomastoid foramen (SF) and internal acoustic meatus (IAM) are additional landmarks frequently encountered in skull base surgery. In infratemporal approaches, facial nerve rerouting necessitates precise knowledge of the JF–SF distance [8, 16]. In posterior fossa procedures, particularly the far lateral approach, the JF–IAM relationship is essential for safe exposure of CNs VII–X [17, 18]. Only one study has evaluated JF dimensions and dome prevalence in dry skulls from northern Thailand, and no cadaveric assessments of DS or BS have been reported in this population [19]. This study aims to define the prevalence and morphology of BS in dry skulls and DS in cadavers, and to characterize JF and sigmoid sinus (SS) dimensions, dome morphology, and distances from the JF to the SF and IAM, to improve anatomical understanding and support surgical planning.

Materials and Methods

The extracranial BS pattern, dome presence, JF morphometry, and JF–SF distance were recorded from 200 dry skulls preserved in the Department of Anatomy. Intracranial DS types, SS diameter, and JF–IAM distance were obtained from 39 formalin-fixed cadavers donated for medical teaching. Dry skulls and cadavers with structural damage near the JF were excluded. Approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University (IRB No. 0187/68). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB as the cadavers were originally donated for research and education.

Dry skull observation and measurement

Sex determination

Sex was determined using nonmetric cranial traits [20] evaluated independently by two researchers. Traits included the nuchal crest, mastoid process, orbital margin (lateral to the supraorbital foramen), and glabella–supraorbital ridge contour. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Ethnicity and age of skulls were unknown.

BS classification, dome assessment, and JF measurements

BS types and subtypes, BS origin, and dome presence (Figs. 1–3) were identified by direct inspection according to definitions from a previous study [7]. A stereomicroscope (SMZ745T; Nikon) equipped with NIS-Elements software was used to measure JF diameters and the JF–SF distance (Fig. 4). Dome depth was measured with a digital Vernier caliper (Mitutoyo®; range 0–150 mm; resolution 0.01 mm).

Cadaveric observation and measurement

Cadavers were placed in prone position. The skull was sectioned circumferentially approximately 2 cm above the eyebrows to the occiput. After removal of the skull cap and brain, DS types (Fig. 5) were classified according to a previous system [6]: Type I, DS separating CN IX anteriorly from CN X–XI posteriorly; Type II, absence of DS; Type III, DS separating CN IX–X anteriorly from CN XI posteriorly; Type IV, multiple DSs between CN IX, X, and XI. High-resolution photographs were taken with a metric scale and analyzed using Pixit Pro A4 (version 9.7; Lanoptik Technologies Ltd.) to measure DS width and length, SS diameter, and the JF–IAM distance. Cadaver demographic data (sex, age, weight, height, nationality) were obtained from departmental records.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.2 (IBM Co.). The study used a significance level of 0.05. Qualitative variables were reported as frequencies and percentages; associations were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test, which was selected to account for the low or zero frequencies observed in certain classification categories. Normality of quantitative variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean, standard deviation, and range, and compared between sides and sexes using the paired and unpaired t-tests, respectively. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median and interquartile range and analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank and Mann–Whitney U tests. All measurements were performed twice by a single researcher, and the mean values were used for analysis. Correlation coefficients were calculated to assess intraobserver reliability.

Types, subtypes, and origin of bony septation

BSs were identified in 171 JF (42.8%). Bilateral presence was observed in 52 skulls (26.0%), while unilateral presence occurred in 67 skulls (right: 19.5%, n=39; left: 14%, n=28), and bilateral absence was noted in 81 skulls (40.5%). Type I BS (non-articulated) was the most common, observed in 149 JF (37.3%). Type II (articulated) occurred in 20 JF (5.0%) and Type III (multiple septations) in 2 JF (0.5%). Representative morphologies are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Prevalence and depth of the jugular dome

The jugular fossa (dome) was bilaterally present in 52.5% (105/200) of skulls. Unilateral domes occurred more frequently on the right (25.0%) than on the left (11.5%), while bilateral absence occurred in 11.0% (22/200). Median dome depth was greater in males (12.87 mm, IQR 4.09 mm) than in females (11.69 mm, IQR 3.56 mm; P=0.002). By side, depth tended to be greater on the right (12.79 mm, IQR 4.09 mm) than on the left (11.41 mm, IQR 3.85 mm), though not statistically significant (P=0.063).

Diameters of the jugular foramen

The JF was significantly longer on the right (16.79±2.08 mm) than on the left (15.38±2.16 mm; 95% CI: –1.77 to –1.06; P<0.001). Males (16.33±2.24 mm) had slightly greater lengths than females (15.83±2.19 mm; 95% CI: –0.93 to –0.06; P=0.026).

Median JF width did not differ by sex (P=0.394) but was greater on the right (9.47 mm, IQR 2.35 mm) than on the left (7.60 mm, IQR 2.32 mm; P<0.001).

Distance between JF and SF (JF–SF)

The JF–SF distance was significantly greater on the left (6.40±1.41 mm) than on the right (5.54±1.61 mm; 95% CI: 0.65–1.07; P<0.001). No sex-related difference was observed (males, 6.90±1.54 mm; females, 5.85±1.60 mm; P=0.127).

Types and dimensions of dural septation

DSs were identified in all 78 JF examined from the intracranial aspect (Fig. 5). Type II DS (absence of septation) was not observed. Type I DS (between CN IX anteriorly and CN X–XI posteriorly) predominated, present in 70 sides (89.7%; 36 right, 34 left). Type III DS (between CN IX–X anteriorly and CN XI posteriorly) occurred in 3 sides (3.9%), and Type IV (multiple septations) in 5 sides (6.4%) (Table 5). No sex-related difference was identified. Mean DS length was 3.50±0.60 mm on the right and 3.63±0.82 mm on the left (P>0.05). Mean DS width measured 1.26±0.37 mm (right) and 1.36±0.45 mm (left).

Diameter of the sigmoid sinus

The median SS diameter was larger in males (7.52 mm, IQR 2.84 mm) than in females (6.85 mm, IQR 2.10 mm; P=0.003). A right-dominant asymmetry was evident, with the right SS (8.23 mm, IQR 2.60 mm) exceeding the left (6.94 mm, IQR 1.81 mm; P=0.004).

Distance between JF and internal auditory meatus (JF–IAM)

The mean JF–IAM distance was 5.96±0.82 mm on the right and 5.95±0.90 mm on the left (P=0.895). No difference was observed between sexes (males, 5.95±0.91 mm; females, 5.96±0.80 mm; P=0.930).

Intra-observer reliability was assessed in all measurements. The analysis demonstrated excellent reliability, with intraclass correlation coefficient=0.899–0.992.

Discussion

This study delineated both intracranial DS and extracranial BS of the JF, together with morphometric data critical for skull-base surgery and radiologic evaluation.

Bony septation, jugular dome, jugular foramen dimensions and JF–SF distance

Type I BS was most prevalent (37.3%), differing from Fang et al. [7] (4%). Type II BS occurred in 5.0%, subdivided into IIa (3.0%), IIb (1.25%), and IIc (0.75%). Type III was rare (0.5%), lower than prior reports [7]. Variation in BS prevalence across different studies [7, 8, 10, 21-29] may reflect developmental differences in the ossification of bony bridges along the dural sheath during the embryonic period. Given that BS has been identified in nearly all fetal skulls by the end of development, these discrepancies suggest that genetic components likely influence the varying prevalence observed across different populations [21]. Differences in prevalence of BS subtypes, especially Type I, may be attributed to variations in the classification criteria or the resolution of the observation methods used across different studies. The classification employed here facilitates standardized reporting during imaging interpretation and surgical description. A bilateral dome was observed in 52.5% of skulls, and bilateral absence in 11.0%, consistent with Ramalho Rodrigues et al. [23]. Previous studies report bilateral presence ranging from 49%–100% and absence 0%–20% [8, 19, 24-28]. The right dome tended to be deeper, consistent with prior findings [8-10, 30-32]. Variations in the reported frequency of the dome may be attributable to observer subjectivity, as the dome represents a projection of the temporal bone. Subtle or minimally projecting bony prominences may be interpreted differently among observers, leading to inconsistencies in classification as a distinct dome structure. Clinically, recognition of a deep dome or high-riding jugular bulb is essential, as these increase the risk of venous injury during otologic or skull-base procedures [33, 34]. The JF was significantly larger on the right, both in length and width, aligning with most prior reports [19, 30-32]. Although some found no significant asymmetry [8-10, 26, 28, 29, 35], the right-dominant pattern likely corresponds to asymmetry of the SS, IJV, and brachiocephalic vein. The larger size of the right JF compared with the left, as well as the larger dimensions observed in men, is commonly attributed to right-sided dominance of the SS. This dominance is thought to be related to asymmetrical cerebral venous drainage patterns, with the superior sagittal sinus more frequently draining into the right transverse–sigmoid sinus system, resulting in a larger right IJV and JF [8-11, 27, 36]. The relatively smaller left JF, particularly in females, may predispose to neurovascular compression and complicate surgical exposure [8]. The JF–SF distance was greater than previously reported by Das et al. [8] and Kotgirwar and Athavale [9], with consistent left-greater-than-right asymmetry (P<0.001). As the SF marks the exit of CN VII, this relationship is clinically relevant for defining the operative corridor near the mastoid tip and digastric ridge.

Dural septation, sigmoid sinus diameter and JF–IAM distance

Type I DS predominated (89.7%), similar to prior studies [5, 6] but with higher prevalence. Type II DS was absent, contrasting earlier findings [5, 6]. Type III and IV DS occurred in 3.9% and 6.4%, respectively—lower than in Akdag et al. [5] and Tubbs et al. [6]. Different DS morphologies influence both tumor progression and operative strategy. Type II DS offers less impediment to meningioma extension, whereas Type IV may restrict tumor spread but complicate dissection [6]. Preoperative identification of DS configuration and extent can reduce risk of CN injury [5]. The SS was significantly larger on the right and in males, consistent with most reports [8, 29, 35]. Although measurement ranges overlap, right-side dominance remains a predictable pattern, informing venous dominance expectations in lateral skull-base surgery. The JF–IAM distance (~6 mm bilaterally) was comparable to that reported by Akdag et al. [5]. This measurement provides an important anatomic landmark during posterior fossa and petrous apex approaches.

Limitations

The absence of demographic data for dry skulls—such as age and ethnicity—limits correlation with individual variation. These findings represent observations from a regional anatomical collection and should not be interpreted as definitive population-specific characteristics. The determination of sex in dry skulls relied on the subjective assessment of non-metric cranial traits, which is inherently probabilistic and may introduce classification errors or observer bias compared to documented records. Sex-related morphometric differences should be interpreted as tendencies rather than definitive sexual dimorphism. Future studies integrating morphometric and imaging approaches would strengthen preoperative planning data.

Due to very small frequencies in some categories, the statistical comparisons were underpowered; therefore, non-significant findings should be interpreted cautiously and not as evidence of equivalence.

Conclusion

The present series demonstrated a higher prevalence of BS Types I and II and a lower frequency of Type III compared with prior work. Dome prevalence corresponded with previous findings, and JF dimensions displayed consistent right-side dominance. The absence of Type II DS distinguishes this cohort from earlier studies. Comprehensive morphometric understanding of the JF and its septations is essential for accurate radiologic interpretation and safe surgical navigation within this anatomically intricate region.
