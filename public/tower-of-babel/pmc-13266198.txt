CPT Codes for Pediatric Orthopaedics
Authors: R Dale Blasier, Kevin Neal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCPT codes are essential for standardizing medical services, billing, and reimbursement, and for ensuring clear communication among providers, insurers, and patients. The purpose of this review is to examine the history of the 33 most frequently reported CPT codes (as recorded by pediatric Orthopaedic fellowships), their shortcomings, and the challenges currently facing the pediatric orthopedist. None of the pediatric Orthopaedic CPT codes have been reviewed since 2007. Many codes lack vignettes that describe the typical work performed by the clinician. Several codes have not been assigned an RVU value in 30 years (and none have been reviewed in the last 17 years). Therefore, it is unknown whether the current wRVU for each of these codes is accurate. The number of designated inpatient visits for many pediatric Orthopaedic codes is likely inaccurate. The Pediatric Orthopaedic Society of North America could take responsibility for overseeing CPT codes used by pediatric Orthopaedic surgeons. This could be done by establishing a committee of experts to review the existing codes and make recommendations regarding code development, revision, or deletion.Key Concepts(1)There are CPT codes which are unique and important to pediatric orthopaedics.(2)These CPT codes have not recently been reviewed and may be out of date.Keywords: Coding for pediatric orthopedic procedures, CPT Codes, Current Procedural Terminology

Introduction

CPT codes, or Current Procedural Terminology codes, are a standardized system of five-digit codes used to describe medical procedures and services for documentation, billing, and reporting. They are also useful for researchers and for resource allocation in healthcare. There are thousands of CPT codes that describe tests and procedures across all medical specialties. A subset of CPT codes applies to Orthopaedic surgery, with a smaller subset dedicated to pediatric Orthopaedic surgery. This work aims to review the current state of CPT codes relevant to pediatric Orthopaedic surgery.

History of CPT and the resource-based relative value scale (RBRVS)

In 1966, the American Medical Association (AMA) organized and published the first set of CPT codes. In 1970, the code format was updated to five digits. In 1973, the AMA established the first Relative Value Scale Update Committee (RUC) to develop the RBRVS, which assigned relative value units (RVUs) linked to CPT codes [1]. The RBRVS was developed at Harvard University as part of a national study initiated in December 1985 and published in JAMA on September 29, 1988. William Hsiao served as the principal investigator, leading a multidisciplinary team of researchers—including statisticians, physicians, economists, and measurement specialists—to develop the RBRVS. In 1988, the results were submitted to the Healthcare Financing Administration for use in the American Medicare system. The Healthcare Financing Administration is now known as the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS). In December 1989, President George H. W. Bush signed the Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1989 into law, which converted Medicare to an RBRVS payment schedule. On January 1, 1992, the Medicare program introduced a new payment system for physician services based on the RBRVS [2].

The AMA and CPT codes used in medical practice

Under the current CPT/RBRVS system, billing and coding use CPT codes, which the AMA establishes, maintains, and assigns work values. The initial set of CPT codes was based on the Harvard Hsiao study and implemented by CMS in 1992. The original purpose was to review all codes periodically to ensure they are technically appropriate, reflect current surgical practices, and are correctly valued to account for the work and resources involved. Since 1991, the AMA has continually updated RBRVS. By May 2003, more than 3,500 corrections had been submitted to CMS [3].

The AMA CPT Editorial Committee is responsible for maintaining the CPT codes and descriptors. Since 1992, the AMA RUC has officially maintained the value and relativity of these codes. The RUC describes the resources required to provide physician services, which CMS considers when assigning RVUs. While the RUC offers recommendations for RVU values, CMS has final authority over payments in the Physician Fee Schedule [4].

The AMA RUC (Relative Value Update Committee) assigns value to CPT codes by evaluating the relative worth of services based on the work involved in performing procedures. This includes estimating the time and effort required for each procedure. The RUC's recommendations are typically based on data gathered from surveys of practicing physicians about the work involved for each code [5]. These recommendations are then sent to CMS, which may accept them as presented or modify them as needed before they are published in the Physician Fee Schedule.

Several factors can cause existing codes to become misvalued over time. For example, the site of service may shift from inpatient to outpatient, the way a procedure is performed may change, new technologies or devices may be introduced, the patient population undergoing the procedures may shift, and efficiency improvements may reduce the time and effort required. As a result, codes should be reviewed periodically to determine whether they are misvalued and need reassessment by the AMA RUC. However, frequently reviewing all codes (currently over 11,000 CPT codes) would be burdensome because it would require gathering updated surveys for revaluation.

In 2006, to keep codes current after their inception, the AMA RUC established the Five-Year Identification Workgroup, now called the Relativity Assessment Workgroup (RAW), to identify services that might be misvalued using objective reevaluation methods [3]. The approach used screening tools to highlight important or high-value codes for review. These codes could then be examined individually, rather than re-evaluating all codes regularly.

Various screens have been created to identify potentially misvalued codes. These include site-of-service anomalies, high-volume growth, CMS fastest-growing codes, high work intensity, services previously surveyed by one specialty but now performed by a different one, and Harvard-valued codes with high utilization. These screens are generally activated by reviewing annual Medicare claims data.

Ideally, all CPT codes should be periodically reviewed to ensure they reflect current practice and are properly valued. Codes originating in 1992 as part of the Hsiao study should have been reviewed and potentially revalued by the RUC since then. Codes developed after 1992 would have been valued by the RUC from the start, but in both cases, recent reviews may not have occurred. Because the trigger for re-review of codes depends on Medicare claims data, and very few pediatric Orthopaedic procedures are reimbursed by Medicare, these procedures are not well-represented in current screening methods. Since most children's procedures are reimbursed by Medicaid, private payors, or self-pay, nationwide claims data are unavailable to trigger screening. Consequently, pediatric Orthopaedic codes have not undergone review.

CPT codes used by pediatric orthopaedic surgeons

Pediatric orthopaedic surgeons may use any of the CPT codes in the musculoskeletal (MSK) section, ranging from 20,200 to 29,999. Additional codes outside the MSK section may also be applicable. For example, wound repair codes are in the integumentary section, and neuroplasty codes are in the nervous system section. There are two main types of MSK codes relevant to pediatric orthopaedics: 1) general-use codes that any orthopaedic surgeon may use, such as fracture treatment or hardware removal, and 2) codes specific to children, such as pinning a slipped capital femoral epiphysis or applying a clubfoot cast. CPT Codes unique to peds ortho.

There is no nationwide database that indicates which CPT codes are most important or most frequently used in pediatric orthopaedics. As a result, the decision was made to identify the most frequently used codes by reviewing case log numbers submitted to the OMeGA Medical Grants Association in 2022. Founded in 2008, OMeGA was a third-party administrator of graduate medical education (GME) grants for Orthopaedic fellowships in the United States. Its mission is to sustain Orthopaedic graduate medical education by funding the most meritorious and innovative programs, ensuring a fair, transparent, and conflict-free review process. As a condition of funding, OMeGA required an annual report of cases performed by the funded fellows in training. Case reports were reviewed to identify which CPT codes were most frequently reported.

These case numbers are likely not a perfect representation of the codes used in pediatric Orthopaedic practice for several reasons: 1) there were no stringent reporting requirements from OMeGA, 2) case types reported by fellows may have differed from those typically performed in practice, and 3) some programs emphasized spine surgery, while others did not. Nonetheless, case numbers from thirteen fellowship programs were reviewed. The programs included TSRH, Boston Children's, Children's of Atlanta, Children's Hospital of LA, Cincinnati Children's Hospital, Columbia, Johns Hopkins, Mass General, NYU, Hospital for the Ruptured and Crippled, Rady San Diego, Stanford, and Vanderbilt. The 33 most frequently reported CPT codes were identified. See Fig. 1.

The codes are ranked by frequency of reporting in the OMeGA case files for 2022. “CPT Date” lists the date of the last consideration by the CPT Editorial Committee. If the designation is 1992, the code has never been reconsidered. “RUC Rev” lists the date of the last consideration of the code value by the AMA RUC. If the designation “Harvard” appears, it indicates that the RVU values and physician times are those imputed by the Hsiao Harvard study in 1992 and have never been valued using the standard RUC survey method. “Vignette” identifies whether a vignette in the CMS database describes the physician work during the procedure for a typical patient. “Claims 2023” specifies the number of claims in the CMS database for the specified procedure in 2023. “InPTVisit” indicates the number of inpatient days designated during the global period. The number of days is imputed from the original Harvard methodology and may include half days. Codes designated as “ZZZ” are add-on codes and do not include any post-op visits.

Once the most frequently reported codes were identified, they were scrutinized to determine when each code had last been considered by the CPT Editorial Panel, the date of the last consideration of the code value by the AMA RUC for RVU valuation, whether a vignette in the CMS database describes the physician work during the procedure, the current work RVU, the number of claims in the CMS database for the specified procedure, and the number of inpatient days designated during the global period for the procedure. Issues with the pediatric orthopaedic code set

Few of these codes have been reviewed recently; none have been reviewed since 2007, and many have not been reviewed since their inception in 1992. The CPT/RBRVS system assumes that, ideally, no more than five years should pass before a code is reviewed. Because the current system uses only Medicare data, many of these codes are “stale” and potentially misvalued.

Codes recently updated in the CMS and AMA databases include vignettes for each code. Each vignette describes the typical work performed by the clinician during the pre-, intra-, and post-operative periods. Vignettes are important for code selection when billing and help auditors and payers verify correct usage. The review process involves creating vignettes, underscoring the need to review most pediatric Orthopaedic codes.

Each of these codes has been assigned a work RVU (wRVU). The wRVU values in the database are assumed correct unless a code is flagged as potentially misvalued. Because many of these codes haven't been valued in 30 years (none in the last 17 years), changes in methodology, medical practice, patient population, site of service, and performing specialty could affect the work value. Therefore, it is uncertain whether the current wRVU for each of these codes is accurate.

Unfortunately, the number of cases performed by pediatric Orthopaedic surgeons is so low compared with those in general, adult Orthopaedic surgery that the numbers may be too small to be meaningful. It is unfortunate that there is not a nationwide claims database for pediatric cases.

The count of designated inpatient visits for many pediatric Orthopaedic codes is likely inaccurate. Currently, many of these procedures (28,262, 24,579, 25,574, 27,475, 24,575, and 27,479) are performed in outpatient settings. The accurate number of hospital days and the usual site of service for each code have never been confirmed through review.

Summary/call to action

Many pediatric Orthopaedic CPT codes are outdated. Typical triggers for review haven't occurred because the CMS database doesn't track the use of pediatric procedures. Many of these codes lack vignettes that accurately describe physician work. Since none of these codes have been reviewed recently, the RVU valuations may be inaccurate, and the number of inpatient visits may be unreliable.

Although it may be tempting to overlook the limitations of CPT coding for pediatric Orthopaedic procedures and let the current reimbursement system continue, doing so risks a future revaluation of codes without input from the experts best qualified to advise and make recommendations about the process.

The Pediatric Orthopaedic Society of North America could assume responsibility for overseeing CPT codes used by pediatric Orthopaedic surgeons. This could be achieved by forming a committee of experts to review existing codes and, if there are discrepancies regarding appropriate code values, provide recommendations to the AMA RUC. The committee could also suggest new codes to the AMA CPT Editorial Committee or recommend edits or deletions of current codes.
