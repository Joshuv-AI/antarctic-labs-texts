Salivary Varicella Zoster Virus DNA Detection in Patients With Acute Abdominal Pain: Frequency, Diagnostic Value, and Implications for Enteric Zoster
Authors: Hyo-Ju Son, Seung-Beom Kim, Ji-Soo Kwon, Kyung Hwa Jung, Sang-Oh Lee, Sung-Han Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnteric zoster, a rare herpes zoster manifestation, occurs when varicella-zoster virus (VZV) reactivates enteric nervous system neurons, leading to abdominal pain and gastrointestinal symptoms without a cutaneous rash. Due to absence of the skin lesion, diagnosis is challenging. Recently, salivary VZV DNA polymerase chain reaction (PCR) has been suggested as a diagnostic test for herpes zoster. This investigation aimed to assess the prevalence of salivary VZV DNA positivity among patients with acute abdominal pain and to evaluate its diagnostic value by comparing PCR results in patients undergoing intra-abdominal surgery.MethodsWe prospectively enrolled adult patients presenting to the emergency department with acute abdominal pain between June 2023 and April 2024 at a community-based university hospital. The patients underwent detailed history reviews, blood tests, and abdominal computed tomography (CT). Salivary VZV DNA PCR was performed. Additionally, to investigate subclinical VZV reactivation under acute gastrointestinal stress, salivary VZV DNA PCR was conducted within 48 hour post-abdominal surgery as a control. Enteric zoster was inferred in individuals exhibiting positive salivary VZV DNA, identified through real-time PCR.ResultsIn total, 101 patients with abdominal pain were enrolled. Positive salivary VZV DNA PCR results were found in 5 (5.0%) patients. Two patients had acute cholecystitis, one had acute appendicitis, one had acute pancreatitis, and the remaining patient had no identifiable cause of abdominal pain on CT. All 19 patients tested negative for salivary VZV DNA PCR post-abdominal surgery within 48 hours.ConclusionAbout 5% of patients experiencing acute abdominal pain exhibited positive salivary VZV DNA PCR findings, indicating that enteric zoster may be a potential factor in both recognized and unidentified causes of acute abdominal pain. Further research is needed to determine if antiviral therapy guided by salivary VZV DNA PCR results can alleviate abdominal pain in this patient population.

INTRODUCTION

Enteric zoster is a rare manifestation of varicella-zoster virus (VZV) reactivation that occurs in the enteric nervous system.123 However, the true disease incidence may be underestimated, given the characteristic neurotropic nature of VZV and its widespread dissemination into sensory nerve fibers, including those within the enteric nervous system during primary varicella infection. Unlike the typical cutaneous presentation of herpes zoster, enteric zoster manifests with gastrointestinal dysfunction or abdominal pain, without associated cutaneous lesions.45 This condition arises when VZV, latent in enteric neurons, reactivates and causes localized inflammation.3 Despite its potential clinical significance, enteric zoster remains poorly understood and underdiagnosed because the diagnosis of enteric zoster is challenging owing to the absence of the vesicular rash observed in herpes zoster.45 Current diagnostic approaches, such as invasive tissue biopsy via endoscopy or laparoscopy, are impractical in routine clinical practice. Consequently, many cases of enteric zoster remain undiagnosed, resulting in patients without appropriate management or treatment.6 This diagnostic gap emphasizes the necessity for a reliable, non-invasive biomarker to identify VZV reactivation in the enteric nervous system.

Recently, salivary VZV DNA polymerase chain reaction (PCR) has emerged as a promising tool for detecting VZV reactivation.789 Previous studies have demonstrated that VZV DNA is absent in the saliva of healthy individuals but is detectable in patients with herpes zoster.810 Our previous study demonstrated the feasibility of salivary VZV PCR in identifying VZV reactivation in a small cohort of patients with unexplained abdominal pain at a tertiary hospital setting.5 However, that pilot investigation was limited by a narrow study population, including a high proportion of patients with cancer or prior abdominal surgery. The current study was designed to address these limitations by prospectively evaluating a broader, more generalizable population of patients presenting to a community-based emergency department with acute abdominal pain. Furthermore, we incorporated a control group of post-abdominal surgery patients to assess whether VZV reactivation could be attributed to physiological stress. Our aim was to estimate the frequency of VZV reactivation and to evaluate the diagnostic utility of salivary VZV PCR in this real-world clinical context.

METHODS

Study population

From June 2023 to April 2024, adult individuals aged 18–80 presenting with abdominal pain at the emergency department of Eulji Medical Center, a 600-bed South Korean hospital, were enrolled in a prospective study. All participants underwent abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT). The intensity of pain was assessed utilizing numerical rating scale (NRS) upon emergency room arrival. Patients taking antiviral agents including acyclovir, famciclovir, valacyclovir, and valganciclovir were excluded. As a control group, we assessed salivary VZV DNA positivity in patients who underwent abdominal surgery within 48 hours to examine VZV reactivation during acute gastrointestinal stress.

Salivary collection and nucleic acid extraction

Saliva specimens (up to 1 mL) were obtained using a commercial self-collection device (DNA Genotek, Ottawa, Canada) at least one hour after the last meal. After vigorous agitation, samples were maintained at room temperature until processing. Total nucleic acids were extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions using a silica membrane-based spin column kit (QIAamp DNA Mini Kit; Qiagen, Venlo, The Netherlands).

Real-time PCR assay for VZV DNA

Quantitative detection of VZV DNA was performed using real-time polymerase chain reaction targeting the ORF62 open reading frame, which is essential for viral gene expression. An internal amplification control was included by targeting the human β-actin gene to ensure sample quality and validate extraction efficiency. The primer and probe sets employed in this study were identical to those validated in prior studies by Ra et al.5 Fluorescently labeled TaqMan probes were used for duplex detection. PCR amplification was carried out on a real-time thermocycler (LightCycler 96; Roche, Basel, Switzerland) using a hydrolysis probe master mix optimized for low-copy target detection. To calculate viral load, cycle threshold values were plotted against a dilution series of plasmid DNA encoding the ORF62 target. The assay demonstrated a lower limit of quantification of 20 viral copies per milliliter, consistent with performance standards recommended in the Minimum Information for Publication of Quantitative Real-Time PCR Experiments guidelines.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test or χ2 test, as appropriate. For continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test or Student’s t-test was applied based on normality of distribution. All analyses were performed using SPSS (version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with P values less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

The research protocol, documents for informed consent, and materials providing information to participants received approval from the Eulji Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 2023-01-010).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

A total of 101 patients who presented to the emergency department with abdominal pain were included in this study. In addition, 19 patients who underwent intraabdominal surgery within 48 hours were included as the control group. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the groups are presented in Table 1. Among these 101 patients, 50 (49.5%) were male, and their mean age was 55.0 ± 17.1 years. Of the 19 patients who underwent abdominal surgeries, 13 (68.4%) were male, and their mean age was 56.0 ± 13.2 years. Most patients with abdominal pain (67.3%) were previously healthy. However, significant differences were observed in the prevalence of solid tumors, which were more common in the surgical group (31.6% vs. 7.9%, P = 0.003). A history of herpes zoster was reported in 12.9% of the patients with abdominal pain and in 5.3% of the patients in the surgical group (P = 0.210).

Abdominal pain characteristics

In patients with abdominal pain, 55.4% reported symptom onset between 1 and 5 days prior to presentation, and the majority (34.7%) experienced pain in more than 2 abdominal areas. Pain severity was mostly reported as an NRS score of 4 or 5 (85.1%). Associated symptoms included nausea and vomiting (33.7%) and diarrhea (22.8%), with abdominal tenderness observed in 81.2% of patients. None of the patients presented with skin lesions indicative of herpes zoster.

Salivary VZV DNA PCR results

Salivary VZV DNA PCR was positive in 5 patients (4.9%; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.72–9.18). The clinical details of the 5 PCR-positive patients are summarized in Table 2. Among these 5 patients, 2 were diagnosed with acute cholecystitis, 1 with acute pancreatitis, and 1 with acute appendicitis. One patient had no remarkable findings on abdominal imaging. PCR-positive patients had a mean VZV DNA load of 2.89 log copies/mL, with values ranging from 2.00 to 3.80 log copies/mL. All patients who underwent intraabdominal surgery within 48 hours tested negative for salivary VZV DNA.

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the prevalence of salivary VZV DNA positivity among patients presenting to the emergency department with acute abdominal pain, aiming to explore the potential association with enteric zoster. Approximately 5% of the participants tested positive for salivary VZV DNA, providing preliminary evidence that VZV reactivation in the enteric nervous system may contribute to unexplained abdominal pain in a subset of patients. These results provide important insights for our understanding of the possible contribution or potential role of enteric zoster in patients with acute abdominal pain.

Herpes zoster is typically diagnosed by the presence of characteristic skin lesions; however, their absence in enteric zoster presents significant diagnostic challenges for clinicians.6 Recent studies have highlighted the potential of salivary PCR as a non-invasive and highly sensitive diagnostic tool for VZV DNA, with a sensitivity ranging from 40% to 100% and a specificity of 100% for herpes zoster diagnosis.8111213 Compared to plasma testing, salivary PCR has demonstrated significantly greater sensitivity in identifying VZV reactivation. Our previous study demonstrated that salivary PCR achieved a sensitivity of 88% for detecting VZV DNA in patients with suspected herpes zoster, whereas plasma PCR showed a much lower sensitivity of 28%.8 These findings emphasize the clinical utility of salivary PCR in diagnosing VZV-associated conditions, particularly in clinical settings such as zoster sine herpete, central nervous system zoster, or enteric zoster.

The hidden nature of VZV infections in the viscera or vasculature requires non-invasive diagnostic methods. In a previous study, all 5 patients with unexplained abdominal pain were confirmed to have salivary VZV positivity, and one of these patients had VZV DNA and VZV protein detected in the perforated gastric ulcer tissue. The data underscore the potential role of saliva-based PCR as a supportive diagnostic modality for detecting enteric zoster. This study was based on our previous investigation of salivary VZV DNA PCR as a diagnostic tool for enteric zoster in patients presenting with acute abdominal pain. While the earlier pilot study demonstrated a 10% positivity rate in 50 patients, it was limited by its small sample size and the nature of its study population, which included a high proportion of patients with cancer or immunosuppression treated at a tertiary referral center. Such heterogeneity introduced substantial potential for confounding, making it difficult to generalize the results to the broader population. In contrast, the present study aimed to validate and expand upon those initial findings by enrolling a larger number of patients from a community-based emergency department, where the prevalence of confounding factors such as malignancy or recent abdominal surgery was substantially lower. This design allowed for a more accurate assessment of the real-world diagnostic utility of salivary VZV PCR in patients presenting with acute abdominal pain. Moreover, the larger sample size and the inclusion of patients without significant underlying conditions improved the reliability and external validity of our findings.

Given that there have been no animal models and its causal relationship between VZV saliva positivity and abdominal pain is elusive, enteric zoster could be typically considered by excluding other causes of abdominal pain to align with current literature.4 A novel observation in this study is the presence of salivary VZV DNA in patients with objectively diagnosed abdominal conditions. Among the 5 patients who tested positive for salivary VZV DNA PCR in this study, 4 were diagnosed with well-established gastrointestinal conditions, such as acute cholecystitis, pancreatitis, or appendicitis. These diseases are traditionally understood as being caused by primary local pathologies—obstruction, inflammation, or infection localized to the relevant organ. Our findings raise the possibility that VZV reactivation within the enteric nervous system may act as a previously underappreciated contributing or exacerbating factor in such conditions. VZV reactivation has been previously associated with gastrointestinal conditions, including gastric ulceration1415 and colonic pseudo-obstruction,161718 suggesting that the localized inflammatory response induced by the virus can mimic or trigger acute gastrointestinal conditions. While the causal relationship between salivary VZV DNA detection and these abdominal pathologies remains uncertain, our findings open new questions regarding the broader role of VZV in gastrointestinal symptomatology. Further investigations using histological analysis, molecular diagnostics, and possibly VZV-targeted interventions are warranted to elucidate whether these observations reflect true viral pathogenesis or incidental reactivation. If confirmed, these insights may lead to a reevaluation of diagnostic strategies and therapeutic options for common abdominal conditions.

Additionally, one of the patients with positive salivary VZV DNA PCR results had no identifiable cause of abdominal pain based on a comprehensive clinical evaluation, including CT imaging. This result strongly suggests the possibility of enteric zoster as the underlying etiology. The absence of structural abnormalities or other apparent causes of pain highlights the potential for VZV reactivation within the enteric nervous system to produce non-specific abdominal symptoms without overt radiologic findings. Such a finding emphasizes the diagnostic challenge of enteric zoster and highlights the utility of salivary VZV DNA PCR as a non-invasive tool for identifying VZV reactivation in patients with unexplained abdominal pain. This finding further supports the need for heightened clinical awareness and additional research on the role of enteric zoster in similar unexplained cases.

Previous studies have reported that VZV reactivation can occur under high-stress conditions, such as among astronauts,1920 critically ill patients,21 and patients with autoimmune diseases.22 The possibility that VZV reactivation represents a stress-induced bystander phenomenon cannot be entirely ruled out. However, other investigations in different stressful scenarios beyond usual daily life, such as during medical examinations8 or isolation in a negative pressure room because of coronavirus disease 2019,5 reported negative salivary VZV DNA results. An important observation in this study is the absence of 48 hours salivary VZV DNA PCR positivity in the control group of patients who underwent abdominal surgery despite the significant physiological and psychological stress associated with such procedures. By employing a more pathophysiologically relevant comparator than in previous studies, the present analysis demonstrated that even under conditions of significant systemic and localized gastrointestinal stress, none of the postoperative patients showed detectable salivary VZV DNA. These findings may contradict the argument that salivary VZV DNA positivity in patients with unexplained abdominal pain is unlikely to be merely a result of generalized stress-related viral reactivation. Instead, it points toward a more specific role of VZV reactivation within the enteric nervous system, supporting the existence of enteric zoster as a distinct clinical entity. This distinction is critical, as it underscores the potential utility of salivary VZV DNA PCR in differentiating VZV-associated enteric symptoms from other stress-induced conditions, providing a targeted approach to its diagnosis and management. There is no animal model for zoster reactivation. Therefore, further epidemiologic studies and more clinical studies like ours are needed in this area. Randomized clinical trials to determine if antiviral therapy guided by salivary VZV DNA PCR results can alleviate abdominal pain in this patient population will provide a more accurate causal answer for this issue.

Based on our findings, we suggest that salivary VZV DNA PCR be considered as a supplementary diagnostic tool in the evaluation of acute abdominal pain of unclear etiology. Although further validation in larger cohorts is necessary, our data support a clinical framework in which salivary VZV PCR testing may help identify patients with potential enteric zoster who may otherwise remain undiagnosed. In selected cases, a positive PCR result could inform decisions regarding empiric antiviral therapy, especially when other diagnostic avenues are exhausted. Clinical algorithms for abdominal pain assessment may benefit from integrating VZV reactivation testing as part of the differential diagnostic process in both emergency and inpatient settings.

Our study had a few limitations. First, the relatively small sample size and single-center design may have limited the generalizability of our findings. To mitigate this, we employed rigorous inclusion criteria, standardized data collection, and experimental protocols. Second, this study did not evaluate the longitudinal effects of antiviral therapy in PCR-positive patients, leaving the clinical impact of targeted treatment unclear. Future research should address these limitations by including larger multicenter cohorts and investigating the outcomes of antiviral interventions in patients with confirmed VZV reactivation.

In summary, our study demonstrated that approximately 5% of patients who presented to the emergency department with acute abdominal pain had positive salivary VZV DNA PCR results, suggesting a potential link to enteric zoster as an underrecognized etiology. These preliminary findings highlight the diagnostic utility of salivary VZV DNA PCR as a non-invasive tool for identifying enteric zoster. However, well-designed multicenter studies and randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate these observations and to determine whether targeted antiviral therapy can improve clinical outcomes in patients with confirmed VZV reactivation.
