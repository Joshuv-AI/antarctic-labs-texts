A Characterization of Functional Affine Surface Areas
Fernanda M. Baêta

Abstract
A characterization of valuations on the space of convex Lipschitz functions whose domain is a polytope in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} is obtained. It is shown that every upper semicontinuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation can be written as a linear combination of a constant term, the volume of the domain, and a functional affine surface area. In addition, dual statements for finite-valued convex functions are established. † † 2020 AMS subject classification: 52B45, 26B25, 52A41, 52A27.

Introduction and Statement of the Main Result

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex body (i.e., a non-empty compact convex set) and denote by
\bd K
its topological boundary. By a theorem of Aleksandrov (see
[
1
]
), for almost every point
x\in\bd K
with respect to the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
, there exists a paraboloid that osculates
\bd K
at
x
. By Schütt and Werner
[
47
]
, the
affine surface area
of a general (not necessarily smooth) convex body is defined by

\displaystyle\as(K)=\int_{\bd K}\kappa(x)^{\frac{1}{n+1}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x),

where the (generalized) Gaussian curvature
\kappa(x)
of
\bd K
at such a point
x
is taken as the Gaussian curvature of the corresponding osculating paraboloid. For smooth convex surfaces, this definition coincides with the classical one (see
[
9
]
).

The affine surface area
\as(\cdot)
is defined for every
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
, where
\mathcal{K}^{n}
denotes the space of convex bodies equipped with the topology induced by the Hausdorff metric. Lutwak
[
35
]
showed that
\as(\cdot)
is upper semicontinuous, that is, if
K_{m}\to K
in the Hausdorff metric, then

\as(K)\geq\limsup_{m\to\infty}\as(K_{m}).

Moreover,

\as(\cdot)
is
equi-affine invariant
, i.e., invariant under volume-preserving affine maps, and
a valuation (see
[
46
]
). More precisely, a functional
\Phi:\mathcal{K}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called a
valuation
if

\displaystyle\Phi(K\cup L)+\Phi(K\cap L)=\Phi(K)+\Phi(L)

whenever
K,L
and
K\cup L
belong to
\mathcal{K}^{n}
.

One of the most famous results
on valuations is the Hadwiger characterization theorem, which classifies all continuous and rigid motion invariant valuations on the space
\mathcal{K}^{n}
(see
[
25
]
). Specifically, it states that any such valuation can be expressed as a linear combination of the intrinsic volumes
V_{0},V_{1},\dots,V_{n}
, which include the
n
-dimensional volume
V_{n}
, the Euler characteristic
V_{0}
, and the surface area
2V_{n-1}
.
A complete characterization of affine surface area is due to Ludwig and Reitzner, representing an equi-affine
analog of Hadwiger’s theorem.

Theorem 1

(
[
30
,
33
]
)
.

A functional
\Phi:\mathcal{K}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an upper semicontinuous and equi-affine invariant valuation if and only if there exist constants
c_{0},c_{1}
and
c_{2}\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\Phi(K)=c_{0}V_{0}(K)+c_{1}\,V_{n}(K)+c_{2}\Omega(K)

for every
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}
.

Ludwig and Reitzner
[
34
]

further posed the problem of classifying all valuations on the space
\mathcal{K}_{0}^{n}
of convex bodies containing the origin in their interiors, which are invariant under volume-preserving linear transformations. They showed that any valuation
\Phi:\mathcal{K}_{0}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that is upper semicontinuous, invariant under volume preserving linear transformations, and vanishes on polytopes can be represented as

\displaystyle\Phi(K)=\int_{\bd K}\zeta(\kappa_{0}(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{K}(x),

where
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{K}(x)=\langle x,n(x)\rangle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)
is the cone measure on
\bd K,n(x)
is the exterior unit normal vector to
K
at
x\in\bd K
, and

\kappa_{0}(x)=\frac{\kappa(x)}{\langle x,n(x)\rangle^{n+1}}.

Here,
\zeta
belongs to the set

\displaystyle\conc=\left\{\zeta:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)\mid\ \zeta\ \mbox{is concave},\ \lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\zeta(t)=0,\mbox{ and }\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\zeta(t)/t=0\right\}

(1)

(see
[
34
,
31
]
), and
\langle x,y\rangle
denotes the standard inner product of
x
and
y
, either in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
or
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
depending on the context. A complete answer to Ludwig and Reitzner’s problem in the upper semicontinuous case was later provided by Haberl and Parapatits (see
[
24
]
). Further characterizations of valuations with interesting invariance properties were obtained, for example, in
[
2
,
22
,
23
,
25
,
26
,
32
,
36
,
37
]
. For results on the affine surface area of convex bodies, we refer to
[
33
,
35
,
47
]
; see also the recent survey
[
48
]
.

One important direction of generalization is the extension of valuations from convex bodies to convex functions, which has attracted significant attention in recent years; see, for instance,
[
13
,
12
,
15
,
14
]
.
Further developments and characterizations can be found in
[
41
,
16
,
17
,
27
,
28
]
, and more recently in
[

,

,

]
.
Denote by
\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the space of lower semicontinuous, convex, and proper functions (i.e., not identically
\infty
) defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let

\displaystyle\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})=\{v:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\mid\ v\text{ is convex}\}

be the space of finite-valued convex functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A functional
Z:\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called a
valuation
if

Z(u\wedge v)+Z(u\vee v)=Z(u)+Z(v)

for every
u,v\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that also
u\wedge v,u\vee v\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, where
u\wedge v
and
u\vee v
denote the
pointwise minimum and maximum of
u,v\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, respectively.

For
\epi(v)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
the epi-graph of a convex function
v:U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
U
is an open bounded convex set, the affine surface area of
\epi(v)
is given by

\displaystyle\fas(v)=\int_{U}(\det\D v(x))^{\frac{1}{n+2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

where
\D v(x)
denotes the Hessian matrix of
v
at
x
and
\D v(x)=0
if
v
is not twice differentiable at
x
(see
[
49
]
). According to Aleksandrov’s theorem
[
1
]
,
a convex function
v:U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

is twice differentiable almost everywhere. In particular, the
Hessian
\mathrm{D}^{2}v(x)
exists for almost every
x\in U
.
For further definitions of functional affine surface area, see, for example,
[
3
,
10
,
29
,
45
]
.

The set of points where the function
u\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
takes finite values is called the (effective)
domain
of
u
, denoted by

\dom u=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\ u(x)<\infty\},

and for a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we denote its interior by
\interior(A)
.
We consider the subspace of
\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\displaystyle\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\{u\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})\mid\ \dom u\mbox{ is compact},\,u\mbox{ is Lipschitz on $\interior(\dom u)$}\},

that is, the set of functions
u\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with compact domain that are Lipschitz on the interior of their domain. In particular, if the interior of
\dom u
is empty, then
u
is not required to be Lipschitz. The functional
\fas(\cdot)
is a valuation, and it satisfies the following properties

(i)

\V_{n}(\mathbb{R})

invariance
, i.e.,
\fas(u\circ\varphi)=\fas(u)
for every
u\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\varphi\in\V_{n}(\mathbb{R})
;

(ii)

translation invariance
, i.e.,
\fas(u\circ\tau)=\fas(u)

for every
u\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and translation
\tau
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

(iii)

vertical translation invariance
, i.e.,
\fas(u+c)=\fas(u)
for every
u\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and every constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
;

(iv)

dual translation invariance
, i.e.,
\fas(u+l)=\fas(u)
for every every
u\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and every linear function
l
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

A valuation
Z:\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with properties (i) and (ii) is called an
equi-affine invariant valuation
. If
Z
is both vertically and dually translation invariant, we say that
Z
is
dually epi-translation invariant
.

We equip the set
\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with the following notion of convergence: We say that
u_{k}\in~\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is
\tau
-convergent to
u\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, written
u_{k}\xrightarrow{\tau}u
, if

(i)

for every sequence
x_{k}\to x
, we have

u(x)\leq\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}u_{k}(x_{k});

(ii)

there exists a sequence
x_{k}
that converges to
x
such that

u(x)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}u_{k}(x_{k});

(iii)

the Lipschitz constants of
u_{k}
on the interior of the
\dom u_{k}
are uniformly bounded by some constant
M>0
independent of
k
.

This notion of convergence was also considered in
[
6
,
7
]
. A related formulation of condition (iii) was first considered in
[
18
,
19
]
, in the context of real-valued Lipschitz continuous maps on the unit sphere
S^{n-1}
. The convergence given by conditions (i) and (ii) is called
epi-convergence
(for more details see
[
43
]
).

We say that
Z:\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
\tau
-
upper semicontinuous
if, for every sequence
u_{k}
in
\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that is
\tau
-convergent to
u\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

Z(u)\geq\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}Z(u_{k}).

The function
t\mapsto t^{\frac{1}{n+2}}
for
t\geq 0
belongs to the set
\conc
defined in (
1
). Moreover, the restriction of
\fas(\cdot)
to
u\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is
\tau
-upper semicontinuous, as proved in
[
7
, Theorem 1]
. We consider the following subset of
\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\displaystyle\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\{\,u\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})\mid\ \dom u\text{ is a polytope}\,\}.

Our goal is to prove a functional version of Theorem
1
, thereby obtaining a characterization of the more general functional affine surface area

\displaystyle Z(u)=\int_{\dom u}\zeta(\det\D u(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

(2)

for
\zeta\in\conc
and
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. This functional version is naturally related to the geometric version in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, since the epi-graph of a convex function
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. To the best of our knowledge, Theorem
2
provides the first characterization of upper semicontinuous valuations on the space of convex functions in arbitrary dimension
n
. The one-dimensional case was established previously (see
[
6
]
), and our result extends this to the general setting.

Theorem 2
.

A functional
Z:\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
\tau
-upper semicontinuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation if and only if there are constants
c_{0},c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and a function
\zeta\in\conc
such that

\displaystyle Z(u)=c_{0}+c_{1}\,V_{n}(\dom u)+\int_{\dom u}\zeta(\det\D u(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

(3)

for every
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

By
[
7
, Theorem 1]
and
[
6
, Lemma 12]
, the functional (
2
) defines an equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation that is finite and
\tau
-upper semicontinuous for all
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. However,
[
7
, Example 1]
shows that (
2
) is not finite for every
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and every
\zeta\in\conc
if the Lipschitz condition in
\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is omitted. Furthermore, by
[
6
, Lemma 6 and Lemma 9]
, the volume of the domain is a
\tau
-continuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation. It is worth noting, however, that there exist sequences of functions with compact domains that epi-converge to a function with a compact domain, yet the volume of the domain is not continuous with respect to epi-convergence (see also
[
7
, Example 1]
). Therefore, the functional on the right-hand side of (
3
) is a
\tau
-upper semicontinuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation. We expect that additional
\tau
-upper semicontinuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuations exist on
\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
which vanish on
\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Note that the integral (
2
) is not continuous, as it vanishes on piecewise affine functions. As a consequence of Theorem
2
, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 3
.

A functional
Z:\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
\tau
-continuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation if and only if there are constants
c_{0},c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

Z(u)=c_{0}+c_{1}\,V_{n}(\dom u)

for every
u\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Note that this result is established in
\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, since every
u\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
can be approximated by functions of
\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
Z
is
\tau
-continuous.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we recall basic results and properties from convex geometry, the Monge–Ampère measure, the Legendre transform, and infimal convolution. Section
3
is devoted to the dual space of
\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
via the Legendre transform, where we derive the dual theorem corresponding to Theorem
2
and the dual corollary corresponding to Corollary
3
. Finally, in Section
4
, we present the proof of Theorem
2
, which relies on several intermediate results also established in this section.

Background

2.1
Preliminaries

Following
[
43
]
, a function
u\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is called

if
\dom u
can be written as the union of finitely many polyhedral sets, relative to each of which
u(x)
is affine. A set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
polyhedral
if it can be expressed as the intersection of finitely many closed halfspaces. We denote by
\PA(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the set of all piecewise affine functions. By
[
6
, Lemma 14]
, we obtain the following result.

Lemma 4
.

For every
u\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, there exists a sequence
u_{k}\in\PA(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that is
\tau
-convergent to
u
.

We say that
Z:\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
simple
if
Z(u)=0
for every
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\dom u
is not full-dimensional. Let
\mathcal{P}^{n}
denote the set of polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The following result from
[
6
, Lemma 13]
will be used in the proof of Theorem
2
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
Z:\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a simple,
\tau
-upper semicontinuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation. Then there exists a constant
d\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle Z(\I_{P})=d\,V_{n}(P)

for every polytope
P\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
.

Strictly speaking, Lemma 13 in
[
6
]
was stated for
\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, while here we consider the subspace of functions with polytopal domains. However, the statement remains valid in this setting.

We say that a valuation
\Phi:\mathcal{P}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is
simple
if
\Phi(P)=0
for every polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
of dimension at most
n-1
. See
[
37
, Theorem 14.1]
and the references therein for the following well-known result.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\Phi
be a translation invariant, non-negative, simple valuation on
\mathcal{P}^{n}
.
Then there exists a non-negative constant
d\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\Phi(P)=d\,V_{n}(P)

for every polytope
P\in\mathcal{P}^{n}
.

To prove Proposition
23
in Section

, we will use the following version of the Vitali covering theorem. A collection of sets
\mathcal{C}
is called a

for

if, for each
x\in B
and
\delta>0
, there exists a

such that

and

. We say that
\mathcal{C}
is a

for

if there exists a constant

\diam(U)^{n}\leq d\,V_{n}(U)

Theorem 7

(
[
20
]
, Theorem 1.10)
.

Let
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded set, and let
\mathcal{C}
be a regular family of closed sets for

that is a Vitali covering for

. Then, for any

We denote by

, that is, the smallest convex set containing

. For functions

\{f\neq g\}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(x)\neq g(x)\}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\{f=g\}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(x)=g(x)\}.

\mathcal{P}
of pairwise disjoint non-empty subsets of

The following theorem provides a concrete characterization of epi-convergence, showing its equivalence with a more familiar notion of convergence.

Theorem 8

(
[
43
]
, Theorem 7.17)
.

Let
u_{k}
be a sequence in
\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that epi-converges to

. If

has non-empty interior, the following are equivalents

(1)

(2)

.

2.2
Monge–Ampère measure

For every
u\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the
subdifferential
of
u
at
x\in\dom u
is defined by

\displaystyle\partial u(x)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid u(z)\geq u(x)+\langle y,z-x\rangle\ \mbox{for all}\ z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\},

and we set
\partial u(x)=\emptyset
for
x\not\in\dom u
.
For a convex function
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
, differentiability at a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is equivalent to the subdifferential of
u
at
x
being a singleton. Moreover,
L
is the Lipschitz constant of
u\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
in the interior of its domain if and only if
\|y\|\leq L
for every
y\in\partial u(x)
, where
\|y\|=\sqrt{\langle y,y\rangle}
is the usual Euclidean norm of
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
x\in\interior(\dom u)
(see, e.g.,
[
8
, Theorem 3.61]
).

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex body and
w\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We define the function
w\dotplus\I_{K}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle(w\dotplus\I_{K})(y)=\begin{cases}w(y),&y\in K,\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\sup\bigl\{\ell(y)\mid\ell\text{ is affine},\ \ell\leq w\text{ on }K,\ \ell(x)=w(x)\text{ for some }x\in\bd K\bigr\},&y\notin K.\end{cases}

(4)

By construction,
w\dotplus\I_{K}
is convex on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, agrees with
w
on
K
, and is the maximal convex extension of
w|_{K}
obtained by extending
w
via its supporting affine hyperplanes at boundary points.

One natural class of functions in
\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is formed by the
indicator functions
of convex bodies
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n}

\displaystyle\I_{K}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x\in K,\\
\infty,&x\notin K.\end{cases}

By formula (2.4) in
[
44
]
, the subdifferential of
\I_{K}
at each boundary point
x
of
K
is the normal cone of
K
at
x
, which is given by

N_{K}(x)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\langle y,z-x\rangle\leq 0\ \text{for all }z\in K\}.

In particular, there are functions that are Lipschitz continuous in the interior of their domains but whose subdifferentials are unbounded at boundary points. For example,
\partial\I_{[0,1]}(1)=[0,\infty)
.

An important Radon measure associated with a convex function
v:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the
Monge–Ampère measure
, defined by

\displaystyle\MA(v;B)=V_{n}(\partial v(B)),

for any Borel set
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where

\partial v(B)=\bigcup_{x\in B}\partial v(x).

The notion of Monge–Ampère measure is fundamental in the definition of weak or generalized solutions of the Monge–Ampère equation (see, e.g.,
[
21
,
50
]
).

2.3
Legendre transform

For a convex function
u\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the function

\displaystyle u^{*}(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big(\langle x,y\rangle-u(y)\big),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

is called the
Legendre transform
(or
convex conjugate
) of
u
. By
[
4
, Theorem 1.4.1]
, we have
u^{**}=u
. Note that convex conjugation is continuous with respect to epi-convergence,

u_{k}\text{ is epi-convergent to }u\,\,\Leftrightarrow\,\,u^{*}_{k}\text{ is epi-convergent to }u^{*}

(5)

for
u_{k},u\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see
[
43
, Theorem 11.43]
).

Let
u\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
\varphi\in\V_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Denote by
\tau_{y}(x)=x+y
the translation by
y
and by
l_{y}(x)=\langle x,y\rangle
the linear function associated with
y
. A straightforward computation yields

(1)

(u+c)^{*}(x)=u^{*}(x)-c
;

(2)

(u\circ\varphi)^{*}(x)=(u^{*}\circ\varphi^{-t})(x)
;

(3)

(u\circ\tau_{y}^{-1})^{*}(x)=u^{*}(x)+\langle x,y\rangle
;

(4)

(u+l_{y})^{*}(x)=u^{*}\circ\tau_{y}^{-1}(x)
.

Furthermore, if
u,v\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
are such that
u\wedge v\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
u^{*}\wedge v^{*}\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and

\displaystyle(u\wedge v)^{*}=u^{*}\vee v^{*},\qquad(u\vee v)^{*}=u^{*}\wedge v^{*}

(6)

(see, e.g.,
[
14
, Lemma 3.4 and Proposition 3.5]
).

By
[
43
, Theorem 11.14]
,
u\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is piecewise affine if and only if
u^{*}\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
is also piecewise affine.

2.4
Infimal convolution

For any two convex functions
u,v:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
, the
infimal convolution

u\,\square\,v
is defined by

\displaystyle\left(u\,\square\,v\right)(x)=\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(u(y)+v(x-y)\right),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(7)

The epi-graph of
u
is the non-empty, closed, convex set

\epi(u)=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\mid u(x)\leq t\}.

Geometrically, the infimal convolution corresponds to Minkowski sum of epi-graphs,

\epi(u\,\square\,v)=\epi(u)+\epi(v).

In particular, adding a smooth function to one whose graph contains corners produces a smooth function.

By
[
11
, Proposition 2.1 and Proposition 2.6]
, for every
u,v\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have

\dom(u\,\square\,v)=\dom u+\dom v,\quad u\,\square\,v\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

and the following relation holds

\displaystyle(u\,\square\,v)^{*}=u^{*}+v^{*}.

(8)

A particularly important example is the
Moreau envelope
, defined for
\lambda>0
and
u\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by

\displaystyle u_{\lambda}=u\,\square\,\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}

(see, e.g.,
[
5
,
43
]
). By
[
5
, Proposition 12.15]
, the infimum in (
7
) that defines
u_{\lambda}
is uniquely attained. Moreover, by
[
43
, Theorem 1.25]
,
u_{\lambda}
epi-converges to
u
as
\lambda\nearrow\infty
, and by
[
43
, Theorem 2.26]
, the Moreau envelope
u_{\lambda}
is convex and continuously differentiable.

We will work with a variant of the Moreau envelope that preserves both the compactness and the polytopal structure of the domain. Let
\lambda,\mu>0
, and denote
C=[-1,1]^{n}
.
For
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, define

\displaystyle u_{\lambda,\mu}=u\,\square\,\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}+\I_{\mu C}\right).

(9)

Note that
u_{\lambda,\mu}(x)=u_{\lambda}(x)
for
x\in\dom u+\mu C
, and
u_{\lambda,\mu}(x)=\infty
otherwise. So, in particular,
u_{\lambda,\mu}\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Now let
q(x)=\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2},x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This quadratic function is the unique function satisfying
q^{*}=q
, see, for example,
[
43
, Example 11.11]
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle q(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}(x),

where
q_{i}(x)=\frac{x_{i}^{2}}{2}
and
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
. Thus, by (
8
),

\displaystyle q=q^{*}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\right)^{*}=q_{1}^{*}\,\square\,\cdots\,\square\,q_{n}^{*}

and consequently,

\displaystyle u_{\lambda,\mu}=u\,\square\,\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(\|\cdot\|^{2}+\I_{\mu C}\right)=u\,\square\,\lambda\left(q_{1}^{*}\,\square\,\cdots\,\square\,q_{n}^{*}+\I_{\mu C}\right).

For each
i=1,\dots,n
, where
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
are the canonical basis vectors of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we compute

q_{i}^{*}(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big(\langle x,y\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}y_{i}^{2}\Big)=\begin{cases}\sup_{y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}}\big(x_{i}y_{i}-\tfrac{1}{2}y_{i}^{2}\big),&\text{if }x=x_{i}e_{i},\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

A straightforward calculation shows that

q_{1}^{*}\,\square\,\cdots\,\square\,q_{n}^{*}+\I_{\mu C}\\
=\left(q_{1}+\I_{[-\mu,\mu]\times\{0\}^{n-1}}\right)\,\square\,\left(q_{2}+\I_{\{0\}\times[-\mu,\mu]\times\{0\}^{n-2}}\right)\,\square\,\cdots\,\square\,\left(q_{n}+\I_{\{0\}^{n-1}\times[-\mu,\mu]}\right),

which implies

\displaystyle u_{\lambda,\mu}=u\,\square\,\lambda\left[\left(q_{1}+\I_{[-\mu,\mu]\times\{0\}^{n-1}}\right)\,\square\,\left(q_{2}+\I_{\{0\}\times[-\mu,\mu]\times\{0\}^{n-2}}\right)\,\square\,\cdots\,\square\,\left(q_{n}+\I_{\{0\}^{n-1}\times[-\mu,\mu]}\right)\right].

(10)

A classical construction associates to each convex body
K\in\mathcal{K}^{n+1}
a convex function with compact domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
via

\displaystyle\lfloor K\rfloor(x)=\inf_{(x,t)\in K}t

(see, for example,
[
28
]
). Conversely, for a convex function
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, one can associate the convex body

\displaystyle K^{u}=\epi(u-M_{u})\cap R_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\epi(u-M_{u}))+M_{u}e_{n+1}

(11)

where
M_{u}=\max_{\{u<\infty\}}u(x)
and
R_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
denotes reflection with respect to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

A set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is called a
zonotope
if
S=J_{1}+\cdots+J_{m}
for some line segments
J_{1},\dots,J_{m}
, with respect to Minkowski sum. If a convex body
T\in\mathcal{K}^{n+1}
can be approximated in the Hausdorff metric by zonotopes, then
T
is called a
zonoid
. By
[
44
, Corollary 3.5.7]
, every centrally symmetric convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a zonoid.

Consider the function
v(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2}+\I_{[-\mu,\mu]}(x)
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mu>0
, and let
K^{v}
be the convex body associated with
v
as in (
11
). It is straightforward to check that
K^{v}
is a centrally symmetric convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, hence a zonoid.
Therefore, there exist line segments
J_{1}^{k},\dots,J_{m_{k}}^{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
S_{k}=J_{1}^{k}+\cdots+J_{m_{k}}^{k}
converges to
K^{v}
in the Hausdorff metric as
k\to\infty
.

Let
\lfloor J_{i}^{k}\rfloor
denote the convex functions associated with these segments
J_{i}^{k}
,
i=1,\dots,m_{k}
, and set
w^{k}=\lfloor J_{1}^{k}\rfloor\,\square\cdots\,\square\,\lfloor J_{m_{k}}^{k}\rfloor\in\LP(\mathbb{R})
. Each
\lfloor J_{i}^{k}\rfloor

is affine on its domain and is one-dimensional, i.e., depends on a single variable. Moreover, the functions
w^{k}
are uniformly Lipschitz with constant at most
2\mu
, since each segment
J_{i}^{k}
lies inside the convex body
K^{v}
, whose boundary has slope at most
2\mu
. By definition of the epi-sum,

\displaystyle w^{k}=\lfloor J_{1}^{k}\rfloor\,\square\cdots\,\square\,\lfloor J_{m_{k}}^{k}\rfloor\;\;\text{is $\tau$-convergent to }v\text{ as }k\to\infty.

(12)

Using this approximation together with (
10
), we conclude that there exists a sequence of functions
w^{k}_{m_{k}}
, as in (
12
), such that

\displaystyle u\,\square\,\lambda w_{m_{k}}^{k}\xrightarrow{\tau}u_{\lambda,\mu}\quad\text{as }k\to\infty.

(13)

By (
8
), we have

\displaystyle(u_{\lambda,\mu})^{*}=\left(u\,\square\,\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}+\I_{\mu C}\right)\right)^{*}=\left(u^{*}+\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}+\I_{\mu C}\right)^{*}\right).

Since
\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}+\I_{\mu C}
epi-converges to
\I_{\{0\}}
as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, it follows from (
5
) that
\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}+\I_{\mu C}\right)^{*}
epi-converges to the constant function
l\equiv 0
. Consequently,
(u_{\lambda,\mu})^{*}
epi-converges to
u^{*}
, which is equivalent to
u_{\lambda,\mu}
epi-converging to
u
as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
with
\mu>0
. However,
u_{\lambda,\mu}
is not
\tau
-convergent to
u
as
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, since the Lipschitz constants are not uniformly bounded. To address this, instead of letting
\lambda\rightarrow\infty
, we fix
\lambda>0
and let
\mu\to 0
. In this case,
\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}+\I_{\mu C}
also epi-converges to
\I_{\{0\}}
, and in particular,
u_{\lambda,\mu}
is
\tau
-convergent to
u
as
\mu\to 0
.

Lemma 9
.

Let
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and let
\lambda,\mu>0
.
Then, for every
x_{0}\in\dom u_{\lambda,\mu}
, where
u_{\lambda,\mu}
is defined by (
9
), there exists
y_{0}\in\dom u
at which the infimum in the definition of
u_{\lambda,\mu}(x_{0})
is attained, and there exists a quadratic function

q_{x_{0}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

q_{x_{0}}(x)=u(y_{0})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|x-y_{0}\|^{2}

such that
q_{x_{0}}(x_{0})=u_{\lambda,\mu}(x_{0})
and
q_{x_{0}}(x)\geq u_{\lambda,\mu}(x)
for all
x\in y_{0}+\mu C\subset\dom u_{\lambda,\mu}
. In particular,
x_{0}\in y_{0}+\mu C
.

Proof.

By definition,

u_{\lambda,\mu}(x)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\dom u\\
x-y\in\mu C\end{subarray}}\Bigl(u(y)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}\Bigr),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Since
x_{0}\in\dom u_{\lambda,\mu}
, the set

S_{x_{0}}=\{y\in\dom u\mid\;x_{0}-y\in\mu C\}

is compact, and the function

y\mapsto u(y)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|x_{0}-y\|^{2}

is lower semicontinuous. Hence the infimum is attained, i.e., there exists
y_{0}\in S_{x_{0}}
such that

u_{\lambda,\mu}(x_{0})=u(y_{0})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|x_{0}-y_{0}\|^{2}.

Define the quadratic function

q_{x_{0}}(x)=u(y_{0})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|x-y_{0}\|^{2},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Then
q_{x_{0}}(x_{0})=u_{\lambda,\mu}(x_{0})
. Moreover, for any
x
such that
x-y_{0}\in\mu C
, that is,
x\in y_{0}+\mu C
,
we have

u_{\lambda,\mu}(x)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\dom u\\
x-y\in\mu C\end{subarray}}\Bigl(u(y)+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}\Bigr)\leq u(y_{0})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|x-y_{0}\|^{2}=q_{x_{0}}(x).

Therefore,

q_{x_{0}}(x)\geq u_{\lambda,\mu}(x)

for all
x\in y_{0}+\mu C
. Moreover, since
x_{0}-y_{0}\in\mu C
, we have
x_{0}\in y_{0}+\mu C
. This completes the proof.
∎

3
Dual Results

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a non-empty, closed convex set with support function
h_{K}:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to(-\infty,\infty]
defined by

h_{K}(x,t)=\sup_{(y,y_{n+1})\in K}\langle(x,t),(y,y_{n+1})\rangle

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Note that
h_{K}(\cdot,-1)\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
, and for any
u\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have

u^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\langle(y,-1),(x,u(x))\rangle=h_{\epi(u)}(y,-1).

(14)

For a convex function
u
with compact domain and
K=K^{u}
as in (
11
), we obtain

h_{K}(\cdot,-1)=h_{\epi(u)}(\cdot,-1).

(15)

In particular, if
\dom u
is a polytope, then the projection of
K
onto
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is also a polytope.

Define

\displaystyle\Conv_{\MAh}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})=\{h_{K}(\cdot,-1)\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})\mid\,K\in\mathcal{K}^{n+1},\,\supp(\MA(h_{K}(\cdot,-1);\cdot))\text{ is compact}\},

where supp denotes the support of a measure. We also consider the subset

\displaystyle\Conv_{\MAp}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})=\{h_{K}(\cdot,-1)\in\Conv_{\MAh}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})\mid\,\text{ the projection of $K$ onto $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is a polytope}\}.

We say that a sequence
v_{k}\in\Conv_{\MAh}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
is
\tau^{*}
-convergent to
v\in\Conv_{\MAh}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
if the following conditions hold

(i)

v_{k}
epi-converges to
v
;

(ii)

there exists a compact set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\supp(\MA(v_{k};\cdot)),\supp(\MA(v;\cdot))\subseteq C
for every
k
.

(cf.
[
7
]
). This definition also applies to
\Conv_{\MAp}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
.
The terminology
\tau^{*}
-convergence is motivated by the duality between the sets
\Conv_{\MAh}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
and
\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, established via the Legendre transform, as follows.

Lemma 10

(
[
7
]
, Lemma 3)
.

A sequence
u_{k}
in
\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is
\tau
-convergent to
u\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if and only if
u_{k}^{*}
and
u^{*}
belong to

\Conv_{\MAh}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
and
u_{k}^{*}
is
\tau^{*}
-convergent to
u^{*}
.

By Lemma
10
, together with (
14
) and (
15
), we conclude that a sequence
u_{k}

in
\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})

is
\tau
-convergent to
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if and only if
u_{k}^{*}
and
u^{*}
belong to

\Conv_{\MAp}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
and
u_{k}^{*}
is
\tau^{*}
-convergent to
u^{*}
.

Using
[
42
, Corollary 23.5.1]
, we have

\displaystyle p\in\partial v(x)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad x\in\partial v^{*}(p),

(16)

for
v\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, by
[
21
, Lemma A.29]
, we obtain the following result.

Lemma 11
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open convex set, let
u:D\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function, and let
u^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
denote its Legendre transform. Then

\partial u^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset\bar{D},

where
\bar{D}
denotes the closure of
D
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let
v\in\Conv(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
and
p\in\dom v^{*}
. By
[
21
, Lemma A.22]
, the set
\partial v(C)
is compact for every compact set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and by
[
43
, Theorem 11.8]
,
v^{*}
has bounded domain. If
p\notin\partial v(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
p\not\in\partial v(x)
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By (
16
), this implies

\displaystyle x\not\in\partial v^{*}(p)\quad\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

that is,
\partial v^{*}(p)
is empty. Hence
p
lies on the boundary of
\dom v^{*}
. Therefore, by Lemma
11
,

\displaystyle V_{n}(\dom v^{*})=V_{n}(\partial v(\mathbb{R}^{n}))+V_{n}(\dom v^{*}\setminus\partial v(\mathbb{R}^{n}))=V_{n}(\partial v(\mathbb{R}^{n}))=\MA(v;\mathbb{R}^{n})

(17)

for every
v\in\Conv_{\MAh}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
.

In
[
6
, Lemma 9]
, it is shown that the map
v^{*}\mapsto V_{n}(\dom v^{*})
is
\tau
-continuous, i.e., if
v_{k}^{*}\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is
\tau
-convergent to
v^{*}\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
V_{n}(\dom v_{k}^{*})\to V_{n}(\dom v^{*})
as
k\to\infty
.
This result is proved using Painlevé–Kuratowski convergence of the sets
\dom v^{*}
. Alternatively, it follows from (
17
). Indeed, let
v_{k}^{*}
be a sequence in

\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that is
\tau
-convergent to
v^{*}\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. By Lemma
10
, there exists a compact set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle\MA(v_{k};\mathbb{R}^{n})=\MA(v_{k};C)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\MA(v;\mathbb{R}^{n})=\MA(v;C),

and such that
v_{k}\in\Conv_{\MAh}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
is
\tau^{*}
-convergent to
v\in\Conv_{\MAh}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
. In particular,
v_{k}
epi-converges to
v
, which implies that
\MA(v_{k};\cdot)
converges weakly to
\MA(v;\cdot)
(see
[
21
, Proposition 2.6]
). That is,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\beta(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\MA(v_{k};x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\beta(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\MA(v;x)

for every
\beta\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the space of continuous functions with compact support on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Choosing
\beta\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
equal to
1
on
C
,
we deduce that
\MA(v_{k};\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\MA(v;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. By (
17
), this implies that
V_{n}(\dom v_{k}^{*})\to V_{n}(\dom v^{*})
as
v_{k}^{*}\xrightarrow{\tau}v^{*}
.

Finally, let
v\in\Conv_{\MAh}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
and
\zeta\in\conc
. Then, as shown for example in
[
7
, equation (11)]
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\zeta(\det\D v(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\dom v^{*}}\tilde{\zeta}(\det\D v^{*}(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

(18)

where
\tilde{\zeta}=\zeta(1/t)\,t
for
t>0
, and
\tilde{\zeta}\in\conc
.

We now conclude that the functional
Z^{*}:\Conv_{\MAp}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by
Z^{*}(v)=Z(v^{*})
is a
\tau^{*}
-upper semicontinuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation if and only if
Z:\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
\tau
-upper semicontinuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation. Combining this with (
17
), (
18
), (
6
), and Lemma
10
, we deduce the following result from Theorem
2
.

Theorem 12
.

A functional
Z:\Conv_{\MAp}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
\tau^{*}
-upper semicontinuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation if and only if there are constants
c_{0},c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and a function
\zeta\in\conc
such that

\displaystyle Z(v)=c_{0}+c_{1}\MA(v;\mathbb{R}^{n})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\zeta(\det\D v(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

for every
v\in\Conv_{\MAp}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
.

We obtain the following result from Theorem
12
, or equivalently, by duality from Corollary
3
.

Corollary 13
.

A functional
Z:\Conv_{\MAh}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
\tau^{*}
-continuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation if and only if there are constants
c_{0},c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

Z(v)=c_{0}+c_{1}\MA(v;\mathbb{R}^{n}),

for every
v\in\Conv_{\MAh}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R})
.

Since Theorem
2
and Theorem

are equivalent, it suffices to prove Theorem

.

4
Proof of Theorem
2

It is sufficient to prove the necessity part of the theorem, namely, that if
Z:\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
\tau
-upper semicontinuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation, then there exist constants
c_{0},c_{1}
and a function
\zeta\in\conc
such that
Z
is given by (
3
). We will adapt elements from the proof of
[
33
]
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
Z
is translation invariant, we have
Z(\I_{\{x_{0}\}})=c_{0}
for every
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, define

\displaystyle Z_{0}(u)=Z(u)-c_{0}.

Then
Z_{0}
is a
\tau
-upper semicontinuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation that vanishes on indicator functions of singletons, i.e.,
Z_{0}(\I_{\{x_{0}\}})=0
for every
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus, it suffices to prove the following proposition.

Proposition 14
.

Let
Z:\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
\tau
-upper semicontinuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation that vanishes on indicator functions of singletons. Then there exist a constant
c_{1}
and a function
\zeta\in\conc
such that

\displaystyle Z(u)=c_{1}\,V_{n}(\dom u)+\int_{\dom u}\zeta(\det\D u(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

for every
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

To prove Proposition
14
, we proceed by induction on the dimension
n
. For
n=1
, by
[
6
, Theorem 2]
, there exist a constant
d
and a function
\gamma\in\conc
such that

Z(u)=d\,V_{1}(\dom u)+\int_{\dom u}\gamma(u^{\prime\prime}(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

for every
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R})
. Thus, Proposition
14
holds for
n=1
.

Assume that Proposition
14
holds in dimension
n-1
, i.e., for every
\tau
-upper semicontinuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation

\tilde{Z}:\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

which vanishes on indicator functions of singletons, there exist a constant
d
and a function
\gamma\in\conc
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}(u)=d\,V_{n-1}(\dom u)+\int_{\dom u}\gamma(\mbox{det}_{n-1}\D u(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}\dots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{n-1}

(19)

for every
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
. Here,
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})
are coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, and
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}\dots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{n-1}
denotes the
(n-1)
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. The term
\det_{n-1}\D u
is the determinant of the Hessian of
u
restricted to
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, where the function is defined. For simplicity, we will often denote
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1})
and
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}\dots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{n-1}
collectively by
x
and
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
, respectively.

Using (
19
), we show that
Z
is
simple
.

Lemma 15
.

Let
Z
be as in Proposition
14
. Then
Z
is simple.

Proof.

Let
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a hyperplane and denote by
\mathcal{P}(H)

the set of polytopes contained in
H
. Consider the restriction

\LP(H)=\{u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})\mid\ \dom u\in\mathcal{P}(H)\},

and define
\tilde{Z}=Z|_{\mathcal{F}}
. Identifying
H\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,
we obtain that
\tilde{Z}
is a
\tau
-upper semicontinuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation on
\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
which vanishes on indicator functions of singletons and is invariant under affine transformations of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By the induction assumption (
19
),

\displaystyle Z(u)=\tilde{Z}(u)=d\,V_{n-1}(\dom u)+\int_{\dom u}\gamma(\mbox{det}_{n-1}\D u(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

(20)

for every
u\in\mathcal{F}
with some
\gamma\in\conc
and constant
d\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let
\varphi_{t}
be the affine transformation which scales by
t>0
in
H
and by
1/t^{n-1}
in the direction orthogonal to
H
. By equi-affine invariance of
Z
,

\displaystyle Z(u\circ\varphi_{t})=Z(u).

Applying (
20
) to
u\circ\varphi_{t}
, we obtain

\displaystyle Z(u)

\displaystyle=d\,V_{n-1}(\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\dom u))+\int_{\varphi^{-1}_{t}(\dom u)}\gamma(\mbox{det}_{n-1}\D u\circ\varphi_{t}(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{t^{n-1}}\,V_{n-1}(\dom u)+\int_{\varphi^{-1}_{t}(\dom u)}\gamma\left(t^{2(n-1)}\mbox{det}_{n-1}\D u(\varphi_{t}(x))\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{t^{n-1}}\,V_{n-1}(\dom u)+\dfrac{1}{t^{n-1}}\int_{\dom u}\gamma\left(t^{2(n-1)}\mbox{det}_{n-1}\D u(x)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=d\,V_{n-1}(\dom u)+\int_{\dom u}\gamma(\mbox{det}_{n-1}\D u(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Hence

\frac{d}{t^{n-1}}\,V_{n-1}(\dom u)+\dfrac{1}{t^{n-1}}\int_{\dom u}\gamma\left(t^{2(n-1)}\mbox{det}_{n-1}\D u(x)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\\
=d\,V_{n-1}(\dom u)+\int_{\dom u}\gamma(\mbox{det}_{n-1}\D u(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

(21)

for every
t>0
and
u\in\LP(H)
.

Taking
u\in\LP(H)
with
\mbox{det}_{n-1}\D u(x)=0
for all
x\in\dom u
and
V_{n-1}(\dom u)>0
, this gives

\frac{d}{t^{n-1}}\,V_{n-1}(\dom u)=d\,V_{n-1}(\dom u)

for every
t>0
, since
\gamma(0)=0
. This immediately implies that
d=0
. With
d=0
, (
21
) implies that
\gamma(t^{2})=\gamma(1)\,t
, that is,
\gamma\equiv c\sqrt{\cdot}
for some constant
c\geq 0
. Finally, consider a sequence

u_{k}(x)=kx_{1}^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}x_{i}^{2}+\I_{[0,1]^{n-1}\times\{0\}}(x),\quad k\in\mathbb{N},

which epi-converges to

u(x)=\sum_{i=2}^{n-1}x_{i}^{2}+\I_{\{0\}\times[0,1]^{n-2}\times\{0\}}(x).

Since
\dom u_{k}
and
\dom u
are not full-dimensional,
u_{k}
is
\tau
-convergent to
u
. Since
Z
is
\tau
-upper semicontinuous, we get

0\leq\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}Z(u_{k})=\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}c\,\sqrt{2^{n-1}k}\leq Z(u)=0,

and hence
c\equiv 0
. This completes the proof.
∎

By Lemma
5
, there exists a constant
c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

Z(\I_{P})=c_{1}\,V_{n}(P)
for every polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, by
[

, the functional

shares the same properties as

, with the additional property of being
\tau
-continuous. Therefore,

\displaystyle Z_{1}(u)=Z(u)-c_{1}\,V_{n}(\dom u)

(22)

defines a
\tau
-upper semicontinuous, simple, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation that vanishes on indicator functions of polytopes in
\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})

to denote this specific valuation with the above properties.

We now show that

also vanishes on another class of functions. Following
[
33
]
, recall that a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a
cylinder set
if it can be written as the Minkowski sum of an at most
(n-1)
-dimensional convex body
K
and a closed line segment
J
. We define an analogous notion for convex functions.
We say that a function
u\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a

if there exist
w\in\LC(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, a closed interval
J\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and an affine function

such that

where
\dom w
is a convex body of dimension at most

Lemma 16
.

We have
Z_{1}(u)=0
for every cylinder function
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

Let
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a cylinder function.
Thus, there exist
w\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, a closed interval
J\subset\mathbb{R}
,
and an affine function
v
such that

u=w\,\square\,(v+\I_{J}),

where
\dom w
is a convex body of dimension at most
n-1
.

Since
Z
is equi-affine and dually translation invariant, we may assume that
\dom w\subset H
, where
H
is a hyperplane containing the origin,
and that the interval
J
lies in the line
L
.
We consider only segments
J
lying in
L
. Set

\LP(H)=\bigl\{w\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})\mid\ \dom w\subset\mathcal{P}(H)\bigr\}.

Fix
w\in\LP(H)
. By the translation invariance of
Z
, the value of

Z_{1}\big(w\,\square\,(v+\I_{J})\big)

remains unchanged if the closed line segment
J
is translated along the line, since the epi-sum corresponds to the Minkowski sum of the epi-graphs. Consequently,
Z_{1}\big(w\,\square\,(v+\I_{J})\big)
depends only on the length of
J
, not on its position in
L
. Define

g_{w}(s)=Z_{1}(w\,\square\,(v+\I_{J})),\quad s=V_{1}(J).

Let
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
be line segments in
L
, whose relative interiors are disjoint, and suppose that their intersection consists of the origin such that
v(0)=0
.
Set
s_{1}=V_{1}(J_{1})
and
s_{2}=V_{1}(J_{2})
. Then

\displaystyle(w\,\square\,(v+\I_{J_{1}}))\wedge(w\,\square\,(v+\I_{J_{2}}))

\displaystyle=w\,\square\,(v+\I_{J_{1}\cup J_{2}})

\displaystyle(w\,\square\,(v+\I_{J_{1}}))\vee(w\,\square\,(v+\I_{J_{2}}))

\displaystyle=w.

Since
Z_{1}
is a translation invariant and simple valuation, we get

Z_{1}(w\,\square\,(v+\I_{J_{1}\cup J_{2}}))=Z_{1}(w\,\square\,(v+\I_{J_{1}}))+Z_{1}(w\,\square\,(v+\I_{J_{2}})),

which yields

g_{w}(s_{1}+s_{2})=g_{w}(s_{1})+g_{w}(s_{2}).

Thus,
g_{w}
is an upper semicontinuous solution of Cauchy’s functional equation. Hence, there exists a real-valued function
w\mapsto Y(w)
such that

g_{w}(s)=Y(w)\,s,

and therefore

Z_{1}(w\,\square\,(v+\I_{J}))=Y(w)V_{1}(J)

for every
w\in\LP(H)
and every line segment
J
in
L
. Note that
Y
is defined on
\LP(H)
, and it is a
\tau
-upper semicontinuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation that vanishes on indicator functions of polytopes. By the induction assumption (
19
) for
\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
, we obtain

Y(w)=\int_{P}\gamma(\mbox{det}_{n-1}\D u(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

which implies

\displaystyle Z_{1}(w\,\square\,(v+\I_{J}))=V_{1}(J)\int_{P}\gamma(\mbox{det}_{n-1}\D u(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

(23)

for every
u+\I_{P}\in\LP(H)
, every line segment
J
in
L
, and some suitable
\gamma\in\conc
.

As in the proof of Lemma
15
, choose equi-affine invariant transformations
\varphi_{t}
that dilate
by a factor of
t>0
in the direction of
H
and by a factor of
1/t^{n-1}
in the direction of
J
. Then

(w\,\square\,(v+\I_{J}))\circ\varphi_{t}

is a translate of the function
(u\circ\varphi_{t}+\I_{\frac{1}{t}P})\,\square\,(v\circ\varphi_{t}+\I_{t^{n-1}J})
. From the equi-affine invariance of
Z_{1}
and (
23
), it follows that

\displaystyle Z_{1}((u\circ\varphi_{t}+\I_{\frac{1}{t}P})\,\square\,(v\circ\varphi_{t}+\I_{t^{n-1}J}))

\displaystyle=V_{1}(J)\int_{P}\gamma\left(t^{2(n-1)}\mbox{det}_{n-1}\D u(x)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=Z_{1}(w\,\square\,(v+\I_{J}))

\displaystyle=V_{1}(J)\int_{P}\gamma\left(\mbox{det}_{n-1}\D u(x)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Hence
\gamma(t^{2(n-1)})=\gamma(t)
for every
t>0
. Since
\gamma\in\conc
, we conclude that
\gamma\equiv 0
.
∎

Proposition 17
.

Let
Z:\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
\tau
-upper semicontinuous, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation that vanishes on indicator functions of polytopes and cylinders functions. Then there is a function
\zeta\in\conc
such that

\displaystyle Z(u)=\int_{\dom u}\zeta(\det\D u(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

for every

.

From now on, let
Z
denote a valuation with the same properties as in Proposition
17
. To prove the proposition, we first establish a series of preparatory results and constructions; once these are in place, the full argument follows naturally.

Using the facts that
Z
vanishes on indicator functions of polytopes, is dually epi-translation invariant, and is a
\tau
-upper semicontinuous valuation, we obtain

\displaystyle Z(u)\geq 0

(24)

for every
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Given
a>0
, define the function
\Phi_{a}:\mathcal{P}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\Phi_{a}(P)=Z(aq+\I_{P}),

where

\displaystyle q(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(25)

By (
24
), the functional
\Phi_{a}
is non-negative. Moreover, since
Z
is a simple valuation on
\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it follows that
\Phi_{a}
is a simple valuation on
\mathcal{P}^{n}
. Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and consider the translation function
\tau_{y}(x)=x+y
. Note that

\displaystyle q\circ\tau_{y}^{-1}(x)=q(x)+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}-\langle x,y\rangle\right),

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and that
w_{y}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}-\langle x,y\rangle
is an affine function. Since
Z
is translation and dually epi-translation invariant, we obtain

\displaystyle Z(aq+\I_{P})

\displaystyle=Z((aq+\I_{P})\circ\tau_{y}^{-1})

\displaystyle=Z(aq+aw_{y}+\I_{P+y})

\displaystyle=Z(aq+\I_{P+y}).

Hence, for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\Phi_{a}(P+y)=Z(aq+\I_{P+y})=Z(aq+\I_{P})=\Phi_{a}(P).

This shows that
\Phi_{a}
is translation invariant.

By Theorem
6
, there exists a non-negative constant

such that

\Phi_{a}(P)=d\,V_{n}(P),

and consequently,

\displaystyle Z(aq+\I_{P})=d\,V_{n}(P)

(26)

for every polytope

. In particular, (

\displaystyle\dfrac{Z(aq+\I_{P_{1}})}{V_{n}(P_{1})}=\dfrac{Z(aq+\I_{P_{2}})}{V_{n}(P_{2})}

(27)

for all polytopes
P_{1},P_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

Define the function
\zeta:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\zeta(a)=\dfrac{Z(aq+\I_{C})}{V_{n}(C)},

(28)

where
a\geq 0
and
C=[-1,1]^{n}
. By (

), we then have

\displaystyle Z(aq+\I_{P})

\displaystyle=\zeta(a)V_{n}(P),

for every polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
a\geq 0
.

For a general quadratic function

\tilde{q}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\langle x,Ax\rangle+l(x)+c,

where

is symmetric and positive semidefinite,
\det A=a\geq 0
,

\displaystyle Z(\tilde{q}+\I_{P})

\displaystyle=\zeta(a)V_{n}(P),

(29)

for every polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, since
Z
is an equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation.

Throughout the text,
q
denotes the quadratic function defined in (
25

C=[-1,1]^{n}
denotes the unit cube centered at the origin with side length

The strategy for proving Proposition
17
is as follows. First, we show that

\displaystyle Z_{\zeta}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\zeta(\det\D u(x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

as

\tau
-upper semicontinuous, and that (

\displaystyle=Z(q^{a}+\I_{R})

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{m\rightarrow\infty}Z(u_{m})

\displaystyle=\limsup_{m\rightarrow\infty}\left(\left(\dfrac{s-a}{s-r}\right)Z(q_{1}^{r}+\I_{R})+\left(1-\left(\dfrac{s-a}{s-r}\right)\right)Z(q_{1}^{s}+\I_{R})\right)

Setting

\displaystyle=V_{n}(R)\left(\left(\dfrac{s-a}{s-r}\right)\zeta(2^{n}r)+\left(1-\left(\dfrac{s-a}{s-r}\right)\right)\zeta(2^{n}s)\right),

The previous lemmas establish key properties of

Lemma 20
.

Proof.

Since we have already shown that

More precisely, by induction on the dimension

Proposition 21
.

For a given
\zeta\in\conc
, there exists a unique functional

Z:\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

satisfying the following properties:

(i)

Z
is
\tau
-upper semicontinuous;

(ii)

Z
is a simple, equi-affine and dually epi-translation invariant valuation;

(iii)

Z
vanishes on indicator functions of polytopes and cylinder functions;

(iv)

Z(aq+\I_{P})=\zeta(a)V_{n}(P)
for every
a\geq 0

and polytope
P
, where
q(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}
.

A function

is called

if
\dom w
can be expressed as the union of finitely many polyhedral sets, on each of which

has the form

for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix

.

Let
P\LQ(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denote the family of convex functions of the form

u=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{m}w_{i},

where each
w_{i}
is either a piecewise linear-quadratic function or a cylinder function with polytopal domain. Since
\zeta
is defined by (
29
), and
Z
vanishes on indicator functions of polytopes, is dually epi-translation invariant, and vanishes on cylinder functions, the value of

is determined for every

. Note that
\PA(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset P\LQ(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and, since
\PA(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is dense in
\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see Lemma
4
), every
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
can be approximated by elements of
P\LQ(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The
\tau
-upper semicontinuity of

then implies that for any sequence
u_{k}\in P\LQ(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
u_{k}\xrightarrow{\tau}u
, we have

\displaystyle Z(u)\geq\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}Z(u_{k}).

(35)

To show that
Z
is uniquely determined by
\zeta
and thus prove Proposition
21
, it suffices to construct, for every
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, a sequence
u_{k}\in P\LQ(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that equality holds in (
35
), i.e.,

\displaystyle Z(u)=\sup\Bigl\{\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}Z(u_{k})\mid u_{k}\in P\LQ(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\,u_{k}\xrightarrow{\tau}u\Bigr\}.

(36)

We will show that it is enough to prove (
36
) for the special class of functions
u_{\lambda,\mu}
introduced in Subsection
2.4
.
For this, we require the following lemma.

Lemma 22
.

Let
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and let
v
be an affine function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We have

Z(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J}))=Z(u),

where
J\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a closed line segment.

Proof.

We begin by considering line segments
J\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
in a fixed line
L
.
Since
Z
is a translation invariant valuation,
Z(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J}))
depends only on the length
s=V_{1}(J)
. Thus, the function
g_{u}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)

defined by

\displaystyle g_{u}(s)=Z(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J})),\quad s=V_{1}(J),

is well-defined. As in the proof of Lemma
16
, let
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
be line segments lying on the same line, with disjoint relative interiors, and suppose that their intersection consists of a single point
p
such that
v(p)=0
. Then

\displaystyle(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J_{1}}))\wedge(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J_{2}}))

\displaystyle=u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J_{1}\cup J_{2}})

\displaystyle(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J_{1}}))\vee(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J_{2}}))

\displaystyle=u.

Let
s_{1}=V_{1}(J_{1})
and
s_{2}=V_{1}(J_{2})
. Since
Z
is a valuation, it follows that

\displaystyle Z(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J_{1}\cup J_{2}}))+Z(u)=Z(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J_{1}}))+Z(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J_{2}})),

which implies

\displaystyle g_{u}(s_{1}+s_{2})+g_{u}(0)=g_{u}(s_{1})+g_{u}(s_{2})

for every
s_{1},s_{2}\geq 0
. Hence,
g_{u}(s)-g_{u}(0)
satisfies Cauchy’s functional equation and depends upper semicontinuously on
s
. Therefore, there exists a real-valued function
u\mapsto Y(u)
such that

\displaystyle g_{u}(s)=Y(u)\,s+g_{u}(0),

for every
s\geq 0
. In particular,

\displaystyle Z(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J}))=Y(u)\,V_{1}(J)+Z(u)

(37)

holds for every
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, every affine function
v
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and every closed line segment
J\subset L
.

For
J
sufficiently long in
L
, and by definition of the epi-sum, we can find closed half-spaces
H_{1}^{+}
and
H_{2}^{-}
, orthogonal to
J
, such that

u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J})+\I_{(\dom u+J)\cap H_{1}^{+}\cap H_{2}^{-}}

is a non-degenerate cylinder function
w\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Set
J_{1}=J\cap H_{1}^{+}
and
J_{2}=J\cap H_{2}^{-}
. Note that

Z(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J}))\\
=Z(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J})+\I_{\dom(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J}))\cap H_{1}^{+}})+Z(w)+Z(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J})+\I_{\dom(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J}))\cap H_{2}^{-}}).

Since
Z
is a simple dually epi-translation invariant valuation that vanishes on cylinder functions,

\displaystyle Z(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J}))=Z(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{I_{1}+I_{2}})).

Using this together with (
37
), we obtain

Y(u)\,V_{1}(J)=Y(u)\,V_{1}(J_{1}+J_{2})=Y(u)\,(V_{1}(J_{1})+V_{1}(J_{2})),

and, since
V_{1}(J)>V_{1}(J_{1})+V_{1}(J_{2})
, it follows that

Y(u)=0.

Substituting into (
37
), we conclude that

Z(u\,\square\,(v+\I_{J}))=Z(u)

for every
u\in\LP(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and thus the proof is complete.
∎
