5G communication delay dataset for cloud-based vehicle planning and control
Authors: Xinrui Zhang, Lu Xiong, Peizhi Zhang, Haojie Feng, Junpeng Huang, Xiurong Wang, Mengjie Tian, Zhuoren Li, Dongxiao Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCloud-based intelligent connected vehicles (CICVs) rely on vehicle-to-network-to-vehicle (V2N2V) communication for cooperative planning and control (PnC), where communication delay directly influences control performance and safety. To support research on delay modeling and delay-aware control, we present CICV5G, a publicly available dataset of 5G communication delay for cloud-based CICV applications. The dataset comprises more than 150,000 synchronized records collected from field experiments at the Tongji Intelligent Vehicle Evaluation Base, covering diverse driving environments, network modes, and vehicle velocities. Each record includes network indicators (RSRP, SINR, Cell ID) and vehicle motion data (position, heading, velocity) with millisecond-level timestamps. All measurements were obtained through a real V2N2V loop using a 5G V2N2V testbed with both public and private networks. The dataset has been validated through statistical analysis of delay distributions and network consistency. CICV5G provides a reproducible foundation for developing communication delay models, delay prediction methods, and cloud-based planning and control strategies for CICVs.

Background & Summary

With the rapid development of 5G and automated vehicle technologies, Cloud-based Intelligent Connected Vehicles (CICVs) have become a central focus in Intelligent Transportation System (ITS)1. By integrating cloud computing, high-speed wireless communication, and vehicle intelligence, CICVs enable large-scale cooperative Planning and Control (PnC) that can enhance traffic efficiency, improve safety, and reduce energy consumption2,3. Demonstrations of CICVs have already been successfully conducted in controlled environments such as ports, industrial zones, and mining areas, revealing their strong potential for broader deployment4–7. In such system, real-time communication between the vehicle and the cloud-referred to as vehicle-to-network-to-vehicle (V2N2V) interaction forms the foundation for cooperative decision-making. The communication delay in this loop directly affects the responsiveness and stability of CICVs, influencing trajectory tracking, control accuracy, and overall safety performance8,9. For trajectory planning, delayed uplinked state information makes the planner operate on stale inputs, while delayed downlink trajectory create a time mismatch between the planned trajectory and the vehicle’s true execution state. This mismatch can manifest as trajectory fluctuations, increased tracking error, and reduced safety margins10. For control, random delay introduces effective input latency and irregular update intervals. These effects may manifest as step-like changes in received command signals, degrading transient performance and stability margins, and can even induce oscillations if latency is not explicitly modeled or compensated11. Therefore, understanding and modeling 5G communication delay is fundamental to evaluating and designing cloud-based PnC strategies. The delay determines how quickly cloud-computed control signals can influence vehicle motion and how accurately sensor information can be reflected in cloud decisions. When the network latency fluctuates, especially under varying signal conditions or network loads, vehicles may respond to outdated information, leading to degraded stability or even unsafe maneuvers. Consequently, accurate delay characterization is indispensable for both system-level design and simulation fidelity in CICV research.

Despite its importance, most existing PnC studies rely on idealized or stochastic delay assumptions—such as bounded random delay or Gaussian-distributed latency—without validation against real-world 5G conditions12–15. While these simplified models provide theoretical insights, they fail to capture the complex, time-varying, and context-dependent nature of delay in practical V2N2V networks, where latency is affected by vehicle mobility, network configuration, and radio signal quality. The lack of high-frequency, application-level, end-to-end delay datasets severely limits researchers’ ability to quantitatively analyze delay effects or benchmark delay-aware control algorithms.

Several representative 5G datasets have been published in recent years, such as SPEC5G16, the 5G channel and context dataset17, and the European V2X communication dataset18. These datasets have contributed greatly to understanding 5G network performance and standardization, yet their objectives and methodologies differ substantially from the requirements of CICVs. They mainly target network-level evaluation or protocol analysis, lacking the temporal alignment and vehicle-context information necessary for delay-sensitive PnC analysis. In addition, some recent studies have measured vehicular communication delay in specific testing scenarios19–22. However, these experiments are typically limited in scale, focus on low-speed conditions or limited scenarios, and record only a few communication parameters. As a result, the available data remain fragmented and scenario-specific, falling short of the large-scale, high-frequency, and context-rich datasets required to support delay modeling and validation for real-world CICV applications. To address these limitations, we developed a large-scale 5G V2N2V communication delay testbed at the Intelligent Connected Vehicle Evaluation Base of Tongji University. The testbed integrates private and public 5G networks, a vehicle–cloud computing platform, and full-scale vehicles equipped with onboard computing units and 5G Customer Premises Equipment (CPE). Using this platform, experiments were conducted across three representative driving environments—urban, arterial, and rural/off-road zones—and under diverse operating conditions including varying velocities, signal strengths, and transmission frequencies. More than 150,000 high-frequency records were collected, each containing synchronized network indicators (RSRP, SINR, Cell ID) and vehicle motion data (position, heading, velocity) with millisecond-level timestamps. The data were validated through statistical analysis of delay distributions, confirming the internal consistency and reliability of the measurements.

Methods

This section provides a complete description of the data generation process, including the 5G delay testbed construction, experimental design, and data collection. The CICV5G dataset was produced through controlled field experiments using a 5G communication delay testbed designed for cloud-based intelligent connected vehicles (CICVs).

5G Delay testbed construction

The CICV5G dataset was collected using a standardized 5G communication delay testbed established at the Tongji University Intelligent Connected Vehicle Evaluation Base (Fig. 1a). The testbed comprises three major subsystems: the vehicle platform, the 5G communication network, and the cloud platform (Fig. 1b).

Overview of Tongji University 5G communication delay testbed for CICVs. (a) Test site at the Tongji ICV evaluation base, including representative driving zones (urban, arterial, and rural/off-road) and the deployment of 5G private/public base stations and the cloud control center. (b) Vehicle-network-cloud architecture used for V2N2V delay measurement, where the instrumented vehicle (GNSS/vehicle interface, onboard platform, 5G CPE and logging module) exchanges uplink/downlink messages with the cloud via MQTT and the 5G network. (c) Delay measurement workflow, showing parameter initialization, MQTT connection, message serialization, uplink transmission, and downlink subscription for RTT logging.

The cloud platform integrates a lightweight PnC service and a Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) server for real-time data exchange. The vehicle transmits its state information—including position, heading, and velocity—to the cloud through the 5G base station, where the MQTT protocol ensures lightweight publish–subscribe communication. The cloud parses and verifies the received messages, records timestamps, and echoes the data back to the vehicle, enabling accurate measurement of the V2N2V communication delay.

The 5G communication network includes both a private network (n8) and a public network (n78) operated by commercial providers. The private network operates in the 949.5–959.5 MHz band and employs network slicing technology to establish an independent, low-interference channel for experimental use. The public network operates in the 3,400–3,500 MHz band, representing real commercial deployment conditions.

The vehicle platform is based on a modified BYD Qin Pro electric vehicle equipped with a 5G CPE, an onboard industrial computing unit, and a data acquisition module. All sensors and network interfaces are synchronized through a Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) clock to maintain consistent time references. Vehicle motion data and network indicators are encoded to ensure compact and efficient data transmission.

Scope of cloud-based PnC and measurement configuration

In this study, cloud-based PnC covers two representative CICV paradigms: (a) the cloud performs decision and trajectory planning while the vehicle conducts tracking control and motion execution, and (b) the cloud performs decision, planning, and control while the vehicle primarily handles motion execution. The V2N2V delay measurements in CICV5G are intended to characterize the end-to-end communication latency constraints shared by both paradigms. During dataset acquisition, we ran a lightweight cloud PnC service without any decision/planning/control solver; it only performs message receiving, parsing/verification and encoding/packaging before immediate echo-back for accurate delay measurement.

Experimental design

A split–split-plot design (SSPD)23 was adopted to ensure comprehensive and statistically valid sampling across multiple influencing factors. The response variable was the 5G V2N2V communication delay, measured as the round-trip time (RTT) between the vehicle and the cloud. All measurements were collected using one instrumented test vehicle equipped with the standardized onboard computing unit and 5G CPE. Data collection was conducted over a period of eight months to reduce sensitivity to short-term network conditions. To improve temporal representativeness and capture potential variations in network load, measurements were conducted at different times of day (including both daytime and nighttime sessions). This experimental design allows efficient evaluation of multi-factor interactions while maintaining experimental balance and minimizing resource requirements. Within each configuration, multiple repeated test runs were carried out. The same vehicle, onboard equipment, and cloud environment were maintained throughout all experiments to ensure consistency. During each run, delay was measured continuously while the vehicle was in motion. The SSPD framework enabled the assessment of both main effects and interaction effects among scenario, network mode, and velocity, providing a statistically balanced dataset that captures environmental and operational diversity.

To capture high-frequency delay fluctuations relevant to real-time PnC applications, data transmission was primarily conducted at 20 Hz, which served as the standard operating frequency for PnC. Additional tests were performed at 10, 33, and 100 Hz to evaluate the impact of transmission rate on communication latency and system stability. For each transmission cycle, both timestamps were generated on the same onboard computing device, ensuring that the measured RTT accurately represents the complete V2N2V communication delay without requiring time synchronization between the vehicle and the cloud. All experiments were performed under both day-time and night-time conditions to capture possible variations in network load. The final dataset includes more than 150,000 valid RTT samples, evenly distributed across all scenario–network–velocity combinations. This experimental framework ensures that the CICV5G dataset is statistically robust, physically representative, and suitable for use in modelling, prediction, and validation of 5G communication delay for cloud-based intelligent connected vehicle systems.

Data collection

The overall testing process followed a standardized procedure for message transmission, reception, and delay measurement, ensuring that each communication cycle was executed under consistent timing and network conditions. Before each test, configuration parameters such as message frequency, topic structure, and network mode were loaded into the onboard software environment. The pre-configuration module initialized the system, read configuration files, and launched three independent threads responsible for data acquisition, message transmission, and data reception. This multithreaded design enabled non-blocking data exchange and precise timestamp alignment during high-frequency operation.

Once the system was initialized, the first thread continuously acquired vehicle state information, including position, heading, and velocity, by parsing GNSS and Controller Area Network (CAN) data streams. These signals were processed and packaged into structured objects for transmission. The second thread was responsible for encoding and transmitting the vehicle status messages. It created a MQTT client, established a connection with the cloud MQTT broker through the 5G CPE, and serialized the vehicle state message data (Vsmdata) using the Google Protocol Buffers format for compact and efficient communication. If the connection attempt failed, the system automatically retried until a stable 5G link was established, ensuring continuous operation in fluctuating network environments.

After the uplink transmission, the cloud MQTT server immediately echoed the received message back to the vehicle via the downlink channel, completing one V2N2V communication cycle. The third thread on the onboard computer subscribed to the corresponding topic, listened for the echoed data, and recorded the incoming message. The timestamps for message transmission (pub_time) and reception (sub_time) were logged simultaneously by the onboard system, and the difference between them represented a single round-trip communication delay sample. This closed-loop design allowed real-time monitoring and continuous delay measurement without requiring clock synchronization between the vehicle and the cloud. The overall workflow of the data collection is illustrated in Fig. 1(c).

Ethics statement

The data used in this study were collected directly by the authors during controlled field experiments conducted for research purposes. The drivers involved in the experiments were aware that vehicle-operation and communication data were being recorded. The released dataset contains no personally identifying information, and no additional ethical concerns are associated with its public release.

Data Records

The CICV5G dataset provides real-world measurements of 5G communication delay for CICV applications. The complete dataset and accompanying documentation have been deposited in Zenodo24, which serves as the formal archival repository for this study. An identical copy of the dataset, together with the associated figures, processing tools, and analysis code, is also available on GitHub (https://github.com/zxr805/CICV5G). Together, these resources enable full reproducibility of the data acquisition, preprocessing, and analysis procedures described in this work.

According to the experimental workflow described in the Methods section, all processed data are organized within the repository under the Data/ directory, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

The Data/ directory contains all measurement logs collected using the CICV5G testbed. Each subfolder corresponds to a specific combination of driving scenario (urban, arterial, or rural/off-road) and network mode (n8 private or n78 public). In addition, a dedicated subdirectory named W2S/ contains datasets collected during test runs in which the vehicle traveled from areas with strong 5G coverage to regions of weaker signal quality. This subset provides continuous measurements of how communication delay evolves as signal strength deteriorates, offering valuable data for analyzing delay sensitivity to network conditions. Furthermore, several disconnection and large-delay samples obtained under n8 rural/off-road conditions are also included in this folder, facilitating studies on abnormal latency and link recovery behavior.

Each dataset file contains synchronized temporal, vehicular, and network variables recorded at uniform sampling intervals. These variables are organized in column format, where each row represents a single transmission cycle corresponding to a vehicle-cloud communication event. We assess (i) consistency across scenarios and network modes, (ii) a statistical characterization of delay distributions, and (iii) sampling-rate stability. The analyses document measurement quality and reproducibility.

In stable coverage areas such as urban and arterial roads, the average 5G V2N2V delay remained around 18–20 ms, with 90th-percentile delays below 25 ms. The private network (n8) consistently exhibited smaller variance and higher stability than the public network (n78). In rural and off-road zones, noticeable delay fluctuations were observed primarily under the n8 private network, where a single base station provided limited coverage and weak signal strength. In contrast, the n78 public network maintained more stable connectivity through inter-base-station handovers, effectively mitigating large latency spikes. The uniform delay levels and stable variance across multiple configurations demonstrate that the testbed architecture and acquisition process produced consistent results regardless of external environment or network mode.

Statistical characterization of delay distributions

The statistical characteristics of 5G V2N2V delay were examined through probability distribution fitting. Four representative models—Normal, Gamma, Nakagami, and Rayleigh—were fitted to delay data for each scenario–network configuration using maximum-likelihood estimation. Model performance was evaluated by Residual Sum of Squares (RSS) and the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC).

As shown in Table 4, the Gamma distribution consistently achieved the lowest RSS and AIC values across all test cases, indicating the best fit. The fitted curves reveal a positively skewed distribution, where the majority of delay samples fall between 10 and 25 ms, accompanied by a thin long tail that is consistent with communication-induced effects along the end-to-end 5G V2N2V path, such as time-varying RAN scheduling and retransmissions under fluctuating radio conditions, as well as queueing and transient congestion in the core/transport networks25.

Sampling-rate stability

To ensure that high-frequency data transmission does not degrade temporal precision, the dataset was analyzed across four transmission frequencies: 10, 20, 33, and 100 Hz. For each frequency, metrics including mean delay, standard deviation, jitter, and packet retention ratio were computed. As shown in Fig. 5, the average V2N2V delay remained stable (18~20 ms) at 10~33 Hz, with jitter below 3 ms. At 100 Hz, a slight increase in average delay (~28 ms) and variance was observed due to queue buffering, but no data loss or timestamp misalignment occurred. This demonstrates that the multithreaded MQTT-based acquisition system maintains consistent performance even under high data rates, guaranteeing that the dataset preserves full integrity across all sampling configurations.

In summary, the CICV5G dataset achieves high accuracy, completeness, and reproducibility. The data exhibit consistent patterns across diverse scenarios, statistically stable delay distributions, coherent relationships between network and motion variables, and robust sampling-rate stability. These results confirm that the dataset is a technically rigorous and physically faithful representation of real-world 5G vehicular communication performance.

Usage Notes

The CICV5G dataset provides synchronized measurements of 5G communication delay and network indicators for CICVs. It is intended for studies on delay modeling, prediction, and delay-aware PnC. The complete dataset is archived on Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.17475688) and dedicated GitHub (https://github.com/zxr805/CICV5G). The dataset therefore allows direct quantitative analysis of latency without additional preprocessing.

When using the dataset, researchers may analyze the delay values according to scenario, network mode, or sampling frequency to examine how different environments affect 5G communication performance. Typical analyses include computing descriptive statistics (mean, variance, percentiles) and visualizing delay distributions through histograms, cumulative distribution functions (CDFs), or box plots. The dataset also contains RSRP and SINR measurements, which can be combined with delay data to explore correlations between network signal quality and end-to-end latency.

The repository includes a tools/ directory containing open-source Python scripts for statistical analysis and visualization. These tools compute delay statistics, perform probability distribution fitting, analyze delay–RSRP relationships, and generate visual representations such as violin plots, ECDFs, and spatial delay maps. Each script can be customized through command-line parameters to adjust the scenario, network mode, or frequency range. All codes rely only on standard Python libraries (NumPy, Pandas, SciPy, Matplotlib), ensuring compatibility across systems and easy reproducibility of the figures presented in this paper.

The delay time-series can also be integrated into downstream applications such as cloud-based control simulations, delay-aware trajectory planning, and communication reliability evaluation. Researchers may import the dataset into simulation environments like CARLA, SUMO, or Simulink to emulate 5G-based cooperative control.

All data are anonymized and contain no privacy-sensitive information, and they are released under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) license to encourage open reuse and further contributions from the research community.
