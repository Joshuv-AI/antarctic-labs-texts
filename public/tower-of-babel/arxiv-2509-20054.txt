Generalized Li-Haldane Correspondence in Critical Free-Fermion Systems
Yuxuan Guo, Sheng Yang, Xue-Jia Yu

Abstract
Topological phenomena in quantum critical systems have recently attracted growing attention, as they go beyond the traditional paradigms of condensed matter and statistical physics. However, a general framework for identifying such nontrivial phenomena, particularly in higher-dimensional systems, remains insufficiently explored. In this Letter, we propose a universal fingerprint for detecting nontrivial topology in critical free-fermion systems protected by global on-site symmetries. Specifically, we analytically establish an exact relation between the bulk entanglement spectrum and the boundary energy spectrum at topological criticality in arbitrary dimensions, demonstrating that the degeneracy of edge modes can be extracted from the bulk entanglement spectrum. These findings, further supported by numerical simulations of lattice models, provide a universal fingerprint for identifying nontrivial topology in critical free-fermion systems.

A remarkable advance came from Li and Haldane

;
Chandran et al. 2011

Chandran et al. 2014

Specifically, for a given subsystem

\hat{K}_{A}

\hat{\rho}_{A}=\text{e}^{-\hat{K}_{A}}

The Li-Haldane conjecture states that there is a one-to-one correspondence between the entanglement spectrum (spectrum of
\hat{K}_{A}
) and the low-energy spectrum of the system’s physical boundary.

Though initially proposed for gapped phases of matter, the conjecture has demonstrated broader applicability. Recent research suggests that the Li-Haldane conjecture may be more universal and apply to even gapless states than originally conceived. For instance, Refs.
Metlitski and Grover 2011
;
Kallin et al. 2011
demonstrate that Goldstone modes in systems with continuous symmetry breaking can be extracted from the entanglement spectrum. Furthermore, recent numerical investigations reveal universal structures in the entanglement spectrum at quantum critical points in both ladder and two-dimensional (2D) quantum systems
Wang et al. 2025a
;
Wang et al. 2025b
;
Liu et al. 2025
;
Mao et al. 2025
;
Zhu et al. 2025
;
Li et al. 2024c
. Notably, the authors and collaborators recently extended the conjecture to gapless SPT states in 1D interacting spin chains
Yu et al. 2024
. Although formal results from algebraic quantum field theory
Dalmonte et al. 2022
provide some insight into the entanglement Hamiltonian with Lorentz symmetry, analytical results regarding the entanglement Hamiltonian of gapless states in higher dimensions are particularly scarce. So far, there has been no exact result for the entanglement spectrum of gapless SPT protected by global symmetries. This motivates our effort to develop entanglement Hamiltonian-based diagnostic methods for these systems in general dimensions.

This Letter serves to fill in that gap by proposing a universal fingerprint for identifying nontrivial topological degeneracies in free fermion gapless SPT states across
arbitrary dimensions
. Specifically, we analytically demonstrate an exact correspondence between the bulk entanglement spectrum and the boundary energy spectrum for critical free-fermion models in general dimensions. We show that boundary degeneracies at critical points can be extracted from the bulk entanglement spectrum, and we further verify its robustness against strong disorder and interaction. These findings, supported by lattice-model simulations, constitute a gapless generalization of the Li-Haldane bulk-boundary correspondence and, to our knowledge, the first analytical progress of such a correspondence in topological critical systems. Our work establishes entanglement-based diagnostics as a universal and distinctive fingerprint for identifying topological phenomena in critical free-fermion systems across arbitrary dimensions, offering a promising avenue for addressing fundamental challenges in the study of gapless topological phases of matter, particularly in higher dimensions.

Model and method.
—To realize gapless SPT physics in these free-fermion systems with topologically protected zero edge modes, the Hamiltonian needs to undergo a continuous phase transition between distinct topological insulating phases characterized by nonzero topological invariants
Verresen 2020
. This requires the models to possess certain global symmetries such that the Hamiltonian falls into a symmetry class characterized by a
\mathbb{Z}
-valued topological invariant in the Altland-Zirnbauer (AZ) classification
Altland and Zirnbauer 1997
. We use
\hat{H}_{\alpha}
to denote a Hamitonian with toloplogical number
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}
. Specifically, in 1D, we may choose
\hat{H}^{\text{1d}}_{\alpha}(k)=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{A,k}^{\dagger}&\hat{c}_{B,k}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\left[\cos(\alpha k)\,\sigma_{x}+\sin(\alpha k)\,\sigma_{y}\right]\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{A,k}\\
\hat{c}_{B,k}\end{pmatrix}
, which belongs to the
AIII
symmetry class. In 2D, an example is
\hat{H}^{\text{2d}}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{A,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}&\hat{c}_{B,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}[\sin(\alpha k_x)\sigma_{x}-\sin(k_y)\sigma_{y}+(1-\cos(\alpha k_x)-\cos(k_y))\sigma_{z}]\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{A,\mathbf{k}}\\
\hat{c}_{B,\mathbf{k}}\end{pmatrix}
, which belongs to
C
symmetry class.
Linear combinations of different
\hat{H}_{\alpha}
can realize phase transitions between different topological insulators or superconductors
Verresen et al. 2017
. For example,
\hat{H}^{\text{1d}}_{1}+\hat{H}^{\text{1d}}_{2}
corresponds to the critical point that separates two gapped phases with winding numbers
\omega=1
and
\omega=2
, featuring edge states in a nontrivial topological universality class
Verresen et al. 2017
;
Verresen et al. 2018
;
Verresen et al. 2021
;
Yu et al. 2022
. Similarly,
(a\hat{H}^{\text{2d}}_{0}+b\hat{H}^{\text{2d}}_{1}+c\hat{H}^{\text{2d}}_{2})/(a+b+c)
realizes a Chern insulator transition between Chern number
\mathcal{C}=1
and
\mathcal{C}=2
when
b=a+c
and
c>a
, supporting chiral edge states
Verresen 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2025
.

In the main text, we focus on 1D and 2D models as illustrative examples, while the more involved 3D case is discussed in the Supplemental Material (SM). We thus obtain a set of critical Hamiltonians constructed as linear combinations of
\hat{H}_{\alpha}
, which exhibit topologically protected edge states. Thanks to the exactly solvable nature of free-fermion models, we can easily compute the entanglement spectrum using the correlation matrix method (see Sec. I of the SM and Refs.
Chung and Peschel 2001
;
Cheong and Henley 2004
;
Peschel and Eisler 2009
for details), which enables the general analysis presented in the following sections.

Analytical result of Entanglement Spectrum and Boundary modes
—At the critical point between insulators or superconductors with topological numbers
n
and
n+1
, one can formally define the winding number as
n+\frac{1}{2}

Verresen 2020
. However, this critical winding number is unstable under perturbations and can be reduced to
n
perturbatively, highlighting the need for new diagnostics to identify nontrivial topology in such critical systems.
Meanwhile, for the ground state of a quadratic Majorana Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\frac{\text{i}}{2}\sum_{i,j}\mathcal{H}_{ij}\hat{\chi}_{i}\hat{\chi}_{j}
,
in which all negative energy modes are occupied, the single-particle entanglement Hamiltonian at some region
A
is
\hat{\rho}_{A}={\mathcal{Z}}_{A}^{-1}\exp(\frac{\text{i}}{2}\sum_{i,j\in A}(\mathcal{K}_{E})_{ij}\hat{\chi}_i\hat{\chi}_j)
described by the following flatten Hamiltonian written as

{\mathcal{K}}_{E}=-\text{i}U^{\dagger}\,\operatorname{sgn}(D)\,U,

(1)

where
U
diagonalizes
\text{i}\mathcal{H}
to
D
, and
\operatorname{sgn}(D)_{ii}=1
if
D_{ii}\leq 0
, and
-1
otherwise. This establishes the relation between the free-fermion entanglement spectrum
\xi_{k}
and the new Hamiltonian
\text{i}\mathcal{K}_{E}
with eigenvalues
\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}
through relation

\displaystyle\xi_{k}=\frac{1+\tanh\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}}{2}\,.

(2)

Now the entanglement spectrum is identified with a Hamiltonian different from the original one that gives the energy spectrum, so what is the relation between them? We can further show that the entanglement Hamiltonian
\mathcal{K}_{E}
can be symmetrically and smoothly connected to the original Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
via a quasi-local fermionic Gaussian circuit of logarithmic depth, because there is a gap of
O(1/L)
, where
L
is the size of system. This transformation does not affect the boundary modes that are exponentially localized at the edge, and we will discuss more details in Sec. II of SM. Since we did not assume any spacetime symmetry, the robust boundary state is also a robust internal symmetry-protected boundary mode of
\mathcal{K}_{E}
rather than an accident zero-energy mode, and hence
\mathcal{K}_{E}
shares same zero modes with
\mathcal{H}
. And the main result of this letter is that the zero mode of
\tilde{\epsilon}_{k}
has one to one correspondence with the localized boundary zero modes
{\epsilon}_{k}
of gapless SPTs.

Effects of disorder and interactions.
—Gapless topological phases have often been referred to as topological semimetals in the literature
Armitage et al. 2018
;
Yan and Felser 2017
.
Here we emphasize the crucial distinction between free-fermion gapless SPT states and topological semimetals.
For example, a 3D Weyl semimetal is gapless only at isolated points in momentum space, while any 2D slice that avoids these points is a gapped lower-dimensional system with nontrivial Chern numbers, giving rise to the well-known Fermi arcs that can be regarded as “topological edge states” in this context.
Thus, although topological semimetals are gapless at discrete points, their “topological edge states” are inherent from the gapped slices, in sharp contrast to our case, where the edge states occur exactly at the gapless critical points.
Moreover, topological semimetals rely on a
momentum-dependent
energy gap and therefore would be destabilized by disorder, which mixes momentum between different gapless points
Pixley and Wilson 2021
.
In contrast, the topological degeneracies of free-fermion gapless SPT states are robust against symmetry-preserving disorders, and importantly, the Li-Haldane correspondence remains valid, as confirmed numerically in a 1D disordered critical model as shown in Figs.
3
(a) and (b).

Discussion and concluding remarks.
—To summarize, we have developed a universal theory of the entanglement spectrum for identifying topological phenomena at quantum critical points in free-fermion systems across arbitrary dimensions. Specifically, for a broad class of topological free-fermion lattice models protected by global on-site symmetries, we analytically establish an exact correspondence between the bulk entanglement spectrum and the boundary energy spectrum at criticality. More importantly, we demonstrate that the bulk entanglement spectrum captures the underlying topological degeneracies of critical systems, even in the absence of open boundaries. Our analytical results are further supported by numerical simulations on lattice models in various dimensions. These findings establish an exact bulk-boundary correspondence in topological critical free-fermion systems, enabling the entanglement spectrum to serve as a universal fingerprint for detecting topological degeneracies at criticality, where conventional topological invariants used to characterize topological insulators and superconductors become ill-defined. As such, our framework represents a first step toward addressing the more challenging identification of gapless SPT states in general dimensions.

Looking ahead, it would be fascinating to explore the role of lattice symmetries—such as inversion symmetry—in topological critical systems, where subtle distinctions in bulk-boundary correspondence may manifest in the entanglement spectrum of crystalline gapless topological phases, analogous to their gapped counterparts
Turner et al. 2010
. Moreover, it is worthwhile to numerically investigate the Li-Haldane correspondence in higher-dimensional interacting gapless SPT phases, which are expected to host much richer topological phenomena beyond the 1D setting
Yu et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2024
. On the experimental side, we note that the entanglement spectrum of free-fermion models can be measured in phononic systems across different spatial dimensions
Lin et al. 2024
, offering a promising platform to test our theoretical predictions. In addition, the entanglement spectrum of interacting gapless SPT states may be efficiently accessed on state-of-the-art digital quantum platforms
Zache et al. 2022
;
Kokail et al. 2021a
;
Joshi et al. 2023
;
Kokail et al. 2021b
via entanglement Hamiltonian learning, or on analog quantum simulators through quantum variational approaches.

Supplemental Material for “Generalized Li-Haldane Correspondence in Critical Free-Fermion Systems”

I
A brief review of Gaussian state method

Fermionic Gaussian states play a fundamental role in many-body quantum physics, as they can be completely characterized by their two-point correlation functions
Chung and Peschel 2001
;
Cheong and Henley 2004
;
Peschel and Eisler 2009
. The density matrix of a fermionic Gaussian state can be expressed as:

\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\exp\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2N}\Gamma_{ij}\hat{\chi}_{i}\hat{\chi}_{j}\right),

(S1)

where
\hat{\chi}_{i}
are Majorana operators satisfying the anticommutation relation
\{\hat{\chi}_{i},\hat{\chi}_{j}\}=2\delta_{ij}
, and
\mathcal{Z}
is a normalization factor. The matrix
\Gamma_{ij}
contains information about the correlations in the state.

It is convenient to transform
\Gamma_{ij}
into a block-diagonal form via an orthogonal transformation. This results in a collection of
2\times 2
blocks of the form:

\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda_{k}\\
-\lambda_{k}&0\end{pmatrix},

(S2)

where
\lambda_{k}\geq 0
for
k=1,2,\ldots,N
. In this canonical basis, the density matrix can be rewritten as:

\hat{\rho}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\prod_{k=1}^{N}\exp\left({\mathrm{i}\lambda_{k}}\tilde{\hat{\chi}}_{2k-1}\tilde{\hat{\chi}}_{2k}\right),

(S3)

with normalization factor
\mathcal{Z}=\prod_{k=1}^{N}2\cosh(\lambda_k)
. Here,
\tilde{\hat{\chi}}_{i}=\sum_{j}O_{ij}\hat{\chi}_{j}
are the transformed Majorana operators, where
O
is the orthogonal matrix that block-diagonalizes
\Gamma_{ij}
. A key advantage of the Gaussian state formalism is that it allows for straightforward computation of information-theoretic quantities. For instance, the von Neumann entropy of
\hat{\rho}
can be expressed as:

S_{\textrm{von}}=-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left[f_{k}\ln f_{k}+(1-f_{k})\ln(1-f_k)\right],

(S4)

where
f_{k}=\frac{1}{1+e^{2\lambda_{k}}}
represents the effective occupation number of fermions.

The covariance matrix
\Gamma_{ij}
of a Gaussian state is determined by the two-point correlation functions of Majorana operators:

\Gamma_{ij}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\big(\hat{\rho}[\hat{\chi}_{i},\hat{\chi}_{j}]\big)=\tanh(\mathcal{H}_G)_{ij},

(S5)

where
[\hat{\chi}_{i},\hat{\chi}_{j}]
denotes the commutator of Majorana operators. To obtain the reduced density matrix of
\hat{\rho}
in a subregion
A
, we only need to keep terms that are supported in subregion
A
:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{A}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{A}}\exp{\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\sum_{i,j \in A}(\Gamma_{A})_{ij} \hat{\chi}_i \hat{\chi}_j\right)},

(S6)

where
\Gamma_{A}
is derived from the restriction of
\Gamma
to region
A
, and
\mathcal{Z}_{A}
is the corresponding normalization factor.

The ground state of a quadratic Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\sum_{i,j}\mathcal{H}_{ij}\hat{\chi}_{i}\hat{\chi}_{j}
can be regarded as a Gaussian state in the limit where
\Gamma=\tanh(\beta\mathcal{H})
with
\beta\rightarrow\infty
. In this zero-temperature limit, the covariance matrix simplifies to:

\Gamma=-\mathrm{i}U^{\dagger}\,\operatorname{sgn}(D)\,U,

(S7)

where we denote this
\Gamma
as the entanglement Hamiltonian
\mathcal{K}_{E}
. Here,
U
is the unitary matrix that diagonalizes
\mathrm{i}\mathcal{H}
as
U(\mathrm{i}\mathcal{H})U^{\dagger}=D
, and
\operatorname{sgn}(D)_{ii}=1
if
D_{ii}\leq 0
, and
-1
otherwise.

Upon further diagonalization of this entanglement Hamiltonian, we obtain the reduced density matrix in the following form:

\hat{\rho}_{A}=\prod_{k=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\tanh{\lambda_k}\tilde{\hat{\chi}}_{A,2k-1}\tilde{\hat{\chi}}_{A,2k}\right),

(S8)

where
\lambda_{k}
are the eigenvalues of the entanglement Hamiltonian
\mathcal{K}_{A,E}
. In numerical calculations, we can conveniently obtain the quantities
\frac{1}{2}\pm\frac{\mathrm{1}}{2}\tanh{\lambda_k}
, which we refer to as the single-particle entanglement spectrum. The equivalence between this spectrum and the boundary energy spectrum will be demonstrated in the following subsection for free-fermion critical states.

II
Detailed proof of the bulk-boundary correspondence in the entanglement spectrum

In this section, we develop the correspondence between entanglement Hamiltonian
\mathcal{K}_{E}
and physical Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
. The interpolation path connecting these two Hamiltonian is given by
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}(t)=(1-\frac{t}{T})\mathcal{H}+\frac{t}{T}\mathcal{K}_{E}
, where
t\in[0,T]
parameterizes the path and
\hat{U}=\mathcal{T}\exp{\text{i}\int_0^T{\hat{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}(t)}dt}
is the associated time evolution operator. The energy gap of the original Hamiltonian is
\Delta_{0}=\frac{c}{L}
, with
L
being the system size, while the gap of
{\mathcal{K}_{E}}
is
\Delta_{1}=1
. Since the unitary transformation between
\mathcal{H}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
does not mix positive and negative energy modes, the gap along the path is simply
\Delta(t)=(1-t/T)\Delta_{0}+(t/T)\Delta_{1}
. Mixing between eigenstates of the same energy sign (positive-positive or negative-negative) preserves the topological properties of the system, whereas time evolution processes that couple positive and negative energy states can destroy the underlying topology.

To analyze the time evolution, we proceed in the spirit of perturbation theory: starting with the initial state
\ket{\psi_n}
, we derive the dynamics of the expansion coefficients:

\displaystyle\dot{c}_{n}=\sum_{k}{c}_{k\neq n}\frac{\langle\psi_{n}|\dot{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}|\psi_{k}\rangle}{E_{n}(t)-E_{k}(t)}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}[E_{k}(t^{\prime})-E_{n}(t^{\prime})]dt^{\prime}}.

(S9)

It is conjectured that, when starting from the ground state of a many-body system, only a few states near with each other significantly contribute to this time evolution, so let us only focus on the states near the gap. Integrating this equation for
n\neq m
(where
m
denotes the ground state index), we obtain:

\hat{c}_{n\neq m}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\langle\psi_{n}|\dot{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}|\psi_{m}\rangle}{E_{n}(t)-E_{m}(t)}e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}[E_{n}(t^{\prime})-E_{m}(t^{\prime})]dt^{\prime}}dt.

(S10)

where
n
denotes one of the states slightly below the gap and
m
denotes one of the states above the energy gap.

This can be bounded as:

|\hat{c}_{n\neq m}(T)|\leq\int_{0}^{T}\frac{|\langle\psi_{n}|\dot{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}|\psi_{m}\rangle|}{E_{n}(t)-E_{m}(t)}dt\leq\frac{C}{T}\max(\lambda)\log L,

(S11)

where
C
is an
O(1)
constant and
\max(\lambda)
represents the sum of the largest eigenvalues of the single-particle Hamiltonian
H
and
\tilde{H}
, which is also
O(1)
. This suggests we can effectively control
\hat{c}_{n\neq m}(T)
when
T
is on the order of
O(\log L/\epsilon)
, where
\epsilon
is the desired relative error measuring the mix of negative energy states and positive energy states.

The original Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
denotes a ground state with few touching points (a 0-dimensional fermi surface) and the entanglement entropy is
O(L^{d-1}\log L)
.
While the ground state of
\tilde{H}
is gapped with non-area law scaling of entanglement entropy, it cannot be strictly local but the violation is mild. It is reasonable to assume it is quasi-local with an effective interaction range of
O(\log L)
. Combining these results, the evolution operator
\exp(i\int_0^T H(t) dt)
can be approximated by a local quantum circuit of depth
O(\log^{2}L)
.

Furthermore, if the boundary state of
H
is localized at the interface and satisfies the correlation decay
\langle\hat{\chi}_{a}(0)\hat{\chi}_{b}(r)\rangle<e^{-ar}
for sufficiently large
r
, then we can analyze the boundary state of
\tilde{H}
using the Lieb-Robinson bound. Since this bound establishes that a strictly local operator can only grow to a quasi-local operator of size
O(\log L)
under the dynamics, the boundary state of
\tilde{H}
must satisfy the modified correlation decay:

\langle\hat{U}^{-1}\hat{\chi}_{a}(0)\hat{\chi}_{b}(r)\hat{U}\rangle<O(L(\log L)^{4})e^{-ar}

(S12)

for large enough
r
. This indicates that the boundary state of
\tilde{H}
remains localized at the boundary, if
a
is slightly increased. Consequently,
H
and
\tilde{H}
possess the same boundary zero mode, even in the case where
H
is a Hamiltonian for critical fermions. And this interprets that there is a one-to-one correspondence between fermionic zero mode and zero mode of free-fermion entanglement spectrum.

III
Brief Review of Low-Dimensional Free Fermion gapless SPT Models

In one dimension (1D), the gapped phases of the AIII class are
\mathbb{Z}
-classified, and a topological state with winding number
\alpha=n
is described by the Hamiltonian
Verresen et al. 2018
;
Verresen 2020
;
Verresen et al. 2021
;
Yu et al. 2022
:

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\text{1d}}_{n}=\sum_{i}\hat{c}^{\dagger}_{A,i}\hat{c}_{B,i+n}+\text{h.c.}

(S13)

In momentum space, this Hamiltonian reads:

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\text{1d}}_{n}=\sum_{k}\hat{\Psi}_{k}^{\dagger}[\cos(nk)\sigma_{x}+\sin(nk)\sigma_{y}]\hat{\Psi}_{k}

(S14)

where we introduce the basis
\hat{\Psi}_{k}^{\dagger}=(\hat{c}_{A,k}^{\dagger},\hat{c}_{B,k}^{\dagger})
.

It is clear that the winding number of this Hamiltonian is
n
. A linear combination
\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}\hat{H}^{\text{1d}}_{\alpha}
becomes critical under two conditions. The first condition occurs when
\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}=0
, producing a gapless point at
k=0
. The second condition arises when
\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\alpha}c_{\alpha}=0
, resulting in a gapless point at
k=\pi
. In our main text, we focus on two specific models:
(\hat{H}^{\text{1d}}_{1}+\hat{H}^{\text{1d}}_{2})/2
and
(\hat{H}^{\text{1d}}_{2}+\hat{H}^{\text{1d}}_{3})/2
, both of which exhibit the gapless SPT characteristics described above.

shows the energy levels under open boundary conditions and the bulk entanglement spectrum for representative parameters within the gapped phases of the winding number
\nu=0
(a1-a2),
\nu=1
(b1-b2),
\nu=2
(c1-c2), and
\nu=3
(d1-d2), respectively.
It is clear that the topological zero edge modes (red circles in the plotting) can be fully reflected in both energy and entanglement spectra.

.

In 2D, class A is
\mathbb{Z}
classified Chern insulator, the lattice model corresponding to
\hat{H}^{\text{2d}}_{1}(\mathbf{k})=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{A,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}&\hat{c}_{B,\mathbf{k}}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}[\sin(k_x)\sigma_{x}-\sin(k_y)\sigma_{y}+(1-\cos(k_x)-\cos(k_y))\sigma_{z}]\begin{pmatrix}\hat{c}_{A,\mathbf{k}}\\
\hat{c}_{B,\mathbf{k}}\end{pmatrix}
reads as

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\text{2d}}_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\Big[-\text{i}\hat{c}_{A,{i+1,j}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{B,{i,j}}-\text{i}\hat{c}_{B,{i+1,j}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{A,{i,j}}+\hat{c}_{A,{i,j+1}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{B,{i,j}}-\hat{c}_{B,{i,j+1}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{A,{i,j}}

\displaystyle+\hat{c}_{A,{i,j}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{A,{i,j}}-\hat{c}_{B,{i,j}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{B,{i,j}}-\hat{c}_{A,{i+1,j}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{A,{i,j}}+\hat{c}_{B,{i+1,j}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{B,{i,j}}

\displaystyle-\hat{c}_{A,{i,j}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{A,{i,j+1}}+\hat{c}_{B,{i,j}}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{B,{i,j+1}}\Big]+\text{h.c.}

(S15)

The Chern number can be calculated using the formula:

\nu=\int_{\text{BZ}}\frac{d^{2}k}{4\pi^{2}}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu}\text{Tr}[(h^{-1}\partial_{\mu}h)(h^{-1}\partial_{\nu}h)],

(S16)

where
h
is the flattened Hamiltonian of
H
. Notably, a topological insulator with higher Chern number
n
can be readily constructed by replacing
i+1
with
i+n
in the original Hamiltonian. When we consider a linear combination
\hat{H}^{\text{2d}}=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}\hat{H}^{\text{2d}}_{\alpha}
, the system becomes gapless at
\mathbf{k}=(\pi,0)
when the condition
\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{\alpha}c_{\alpha}=0
is satisfied. For the specific case where
\alpha\in\{0,1,2\}
, the Hamiltonian
(a\hat{H}^{\text{2d}}_{0}+b\hat{H}^{\text{2d}}_{1}+c\hat{H}^{\text{2d}}_{2})/(a+b+c)
describes a topological phase transition between Chern insulators with
\mathcal{C}=1
and
\mathcal{C}=2
when
b=a+c
and
c>a
, supporting chiral edge states. Conversely, when
a<c
, the system realizes a trivial critical point.
Fig.
S2
shows the energy levels and the bulk entanglement spectrum for representative parameters within the gapped phases of
\mathcal{C}=0
(a1-a2),
\mathcal{C}=1
(b1-b2), and
\mathcal{C}=2
(c1-c2), respectively.
For
\mathcal{C}=1
and
2
, we can easily see the existence of topological zero edge modes through both energy and entanglement spectra.

Since the 2D Chern insulator belongs to class A (with
U(1)
symmetry as a physical symmetry), its
\mathbb{Z}
classification remains stable in the presence of interactions.
We therefore introduce an interacting model that is pinned to the critical state when the interaction strength is increased, given by

\hat{H}(\lambda)=\lambda\hat{H}^{\text{2d}}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\hat{H}^{\text{2d}}_{2}+\hat{H}_{\text{int}},

where
\hat{H}_{1}^{\text{2d}}
and
\hat{H}_{2}^{\text{2d}}
are dual to each other under the transformation

\hat{c}_{A,i,j}\leftrightarrow\hat{c}_{A,-i+3,j}
and

\hat{c}_{B,i,j}\leftrightarrow\hat{c}_{B,-i,j}
.
The interaction term invariant under this dual transformation reads

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{int}}

\displaystyle=U\sum_{i,j}\big[(\hat{c}_{A,i,j}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{B,i,j}+\hat{c}_{B,i,j}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{A,i,j})(\hat{n}_{A,i,j}+\hat{n}_{B,i,j})

\displaystyle\quad+(\hat{c}_{A,i,j}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{B,i+3,j}+\hat{c}_{B,i+3,j}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{A,i,j})(\hat{n}_{A,i,j}+\hat{n}_{B,i+3,j})\big].

(S17)

Under the duality transformation, the Hamiltonian satisfies

\hat{H}(\lambda)\leftrightarrow\hat{H}(1-\lambda)
.
This duality ensures that if a phase transition occurs upon varying
\lambda
,
it must be pinned at
\lambda=1/2
.

IV
\mathbb{Z}
-Classified Topological Superconductors—Gapless topological physics in the three-dimensional model

An intriguing model of
\mathbb{Z}
-classified topological superconductors of class
\rm DIII
, characterized by time-reversal symmetry
T^{2}=-1
and particle-hole symmetry
C^{2}=1

Altland and Zirnbauer 1997
, has been extensively discussed in the context of the B phase of superfluid
{}^{3}\text{He}
. In this system, fermions form spin triplet pairs, thus establishing a
p
-wave superconductor. Using a mean-field approach, we can express the BCS-pairing term by introducing appropriate order parameters:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}(\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu)\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Big[\Delta_{+1}(\mathbf{k})\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{-\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^{\dagger}+\Delta_{+1}^{*}(\mathbf{k})\hat{c}_{-\mathbf{k},\uparrow}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}\Big]

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Big[\Delta_{0}(\mathbf{k})\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^{\dagger}+\Delta_{0}^{*}(\mathbf{k})\hat{c}_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}\Big]

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Big[\Delta_{-1}(\mathbf{k})\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}^{\dagger}+\Delta_{-1}^{*}(\mathbf{k})\hat{c}_{-\mathbf{k},\downarrow}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{k},\downarrow}\Big]\,.

(S18)

The
p
-wave pairing requires
\Delta_{m}(\mathbf{k})
to be proportional to the spherical harmonics
\mathcal{Y}_{1,m}
, yielding:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\pm 1}

\displaystyle=\Delta(\pm k_{x}+ik_{y})\,,

(S19)

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle=\Delta k_{z}\,.

(S20)

This continuum model represents a nontrivial topological superconductor when
\mu>0
, as the mass term changes sign when
|\mathbf{k}|\rightarrow\infty
. To regularize this model on a square lattice, we replace
k_{i}
with
\sin k_{i}
and
k_{i}^{2}
with
4\sin^{2}\left(\frac{k_{i}}{2}\right)
, where we set the lattice constant
a=1
,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\nu=1}

\displaystyle=(3-\mu)\sum_{\mathbf{r}\sigma}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{r},\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{r},\sigma}-t\sum_{\mathbf{r},\mathbf{e_{i}},\sigma}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{r},\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{e_{i}},\sigma}+\text{h.c.}+\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\Delta\Big(\text{i}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{e}_{x},\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r},\uparrow}-\text{i}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{e}_{x},\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r},\uparrow}+\text{h.c.}\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\Delta\Big(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{e}_{y},\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r},\uparrow}-c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{e}_{y},\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r},\uparrow}+\text{h.c.}\Big)+\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\Delta\Big(-\text{i}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{e}_{x},\downarrow}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r},\downarrow}+\text{i}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{e}_{x},\downarrow}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r},\downarrow}+\text{h.c.}\Big)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\Delta\Big(c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{e}_{y},\downarrow}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r},\downarrow}-c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{e}_{y},\downarrow}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r},\downarrow}+\text{h.c.}\Big)+\sum_{\mathbf{r}}\Delta_{z}\Big(-\text{i}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{e}_{z},\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r},\downarrow}+\text{i}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{e}_{z},\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r},\downarrow}+\text{h.c.}\Big)

(S21)

For analytical convenience, we set
\mu=3
and
t={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}2}\Delta=\Delta_{z}=1
, which establishes a topological insulator characterized by winding number
\nu=1
. To analyze this system more efficiently, we introduce the Nambu basis:

\hat{\Psi}_{k}^{\dagger}=(\hat{c}_{k,\uparrow}^{\dagger},\hat{c}_{k,\downarrow}^{\dagger},\hat{c}_{-k,\downarrow},-\hat{c}_{-k,\uparrow}).

(S22)

In this representation, the effective Hamiltonian takes the elegant form:

\hat{H}_{\nu=1}(\mathbf{k})=\hat{\Psi}_{k}^{\dagger}\sum_{i}(\sin{k_i}\alpha_{i}-\cos k_{i}\beta)\hat{\Psi}_{k},

(S23)

where
\alpha_{i}=\sigma_{x}\otimes\sigma_{i}
and
\beta=\sigma_{z}\otimes\sigma_{0}
are constructed from Pauli matrices, with
\sigma_{i}
(
i=x,y,z
) and
\sigma_{0}
representing the identity matrix. The topological invariant of gapped phases of
\hat{H}(\mathbf{k})
are distinguished by the winding number

\nu=\int_{\text{BZ}}\frac{d^{3}k}{24\pi^{2}}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}\text{Tr}[(q^{-1}\partial_{\mu}q)(q^{-1}\partial_{\nu}q)(q^{-1}\partial_{\rho}q)],

(S24)

where
q(\mathbf{k})
is the off-diagonal block of the flattened Hamiltonian of
H(\mathbf{k})
. From this equation, it is straightforward to construct models with higher winding number
\nu=\alpha
starting from
\nu=1
. We simply replace
k_{z}
in the original Hamiltonian with
\alpha k_{z}
:

\hat{H}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{k})=\hat{\Psi}_{k}^{\dagger}(\sin{k_x}\alpha_{x}+\sin{k_y}\alpha_{y}+\sin{\alpha k_z}\alpha_{z}-(\cos{k_x}+\cos{k_y}+\cos{\alpha k_z})\beta)\hat{\Psi}_{k}

(S25)

In the corresponding lattice tight-binding model, this modification is equivalent to replacing
\mathbf{e}_{z}
with
\alpha\mathbf{e}_{z}
to obtain a model with higher winding number. The
\alpha
-model is a sum over all such Hamiltonian with different winding numbers. We consider the interpolations between
\nu=0,1,2
, where

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{\text{3d}}=\frac{1}{a+b+c}(a\hat{H}_{0}^{\text{3d}}+b\hat{H}_{1}^{\text{3d}}+c\hat{H}_{2}^{\text{3d}})\,.

(S26)

At
\mathbf{k}=(0,\pi,\pi)
or
(\pi,0,\pi)
, the Hamiltonian becomes gapless when
a+c-b=0
. The nature of the phase transition depends on the relative magnitudes of the parameters. If
a>c
, this represents a trivial phase transition from
\nu=0
to
\nu=1
. Other wise, if
c>a
, this represents a nontrivial phase transition from
\nu=1
to
\nu=2
. For example, the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{1}^{\text{3d}}+\hat{H}_{2}^{\text{3d}}
represents a topologically nontrivial gapless state. Additionally, there exists a tricritical point in the phase diagram at
b=2a=2c
.
