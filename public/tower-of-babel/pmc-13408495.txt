Metagenomic survey of pathogen prevalence in the infant gut
Authors: Nienke van Beek, Ahmed Bargheet, Ching Jian, Hanna T Noordzij, Alise Ponsero, Veronika Kuchařová Pettersen, Katri E Korpela
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe human microbiota impacts our health and well-being from infancy throughout our lives. Besides mutualistic and commensal strains, it also contains opportunistic pathogens. Infants may be especially vulnerable to opportunistic pathogen colonisation due to their immature immune systems and low microbial diversity.The study aims to examine associations between opportunistic pathogen prevalence and factors such as breastfeeding, antibiotic use, birth-mode, and the presence of other bacterial taxa. This study analysed 3981 publicly available shotgun metagenomes collected from 1275 infants and 415 mothers across ten countries to identify species that may be considered opportunistic pathogens in the infant gut. The prevalence of C. difficile was decreased in breastfed infants and in those carrying Faecalibacterium and Dorea spp. S. aureus carriage was negatively associated with antibiotic use and positively with skin contact and breastfeeding. K. pneumoniae was acquired later in life and was more prevalent in premature infants, and less commonplace in vaginal deliveries without antibiotics. Our findings indicate that opportunistic pathogen prevalence in the infant gut is influenced by medical and caregiving practices and may be modifiable through targeted interventions. Reducing the spread of these opportunistic pathogens could contribute to global efforts against early life infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47440-7.Keywords: Infant gut, Opportunistic pathogen colonisation, Gut microbiota, Infectious disease, Antimicrobial resistanceSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Microbiology

Introduction

The human microbiota impacts our health and well-being from infancy throughout our lives. Comprised of archaea, bacteria, bacteriophages, and fungi, it is a complex ecosystem. In addition to the commensal and mutualistic strains, the gut microbiota is a potential reservoir of opportunistic pathogens. These are species that are generally commensal but exploit host vulnerability and become infectious, hence opportunistic. Infants especially, due to their immature immune systems and low microbial diversity, may be particularly vulnerable to opportunistic pathogen colonisation. It is thought that antibiotic treatments and other perturbations increase the risk of opportunistic pathogen colonisation, and we and others have noted that caesarean born infants carry more potentially pathogenic bacteria in their gut1. However, the factors affecting the asymptomatic carriage of pathogenic organisms in the human gut are not clearly understood. Large-scale gut microbiota surveys can serve as a tool to monitor the prevalence of opportunistic pathogenic microbes in the general population. While these microbes may not cause disease in the focal individual, their prevalence in the population may indicate risk of transmission to more vulnerable groups and thus the risk of infection in the population. Furthermore, a study on the carriage of opportunistic pathogenic bacteria can help identify potential transmission routes and therefore opportunities for reducing infection burden within the population.

Factors disturbing the natural development of the infant gut microbiota may predispose to increased likelihood of being colonised with opportunistic pathogens. Intrapartum antibiotic use2, postpartum antibiotic use3, C-section birth4, and lack of breastfeeding4,5, are typical factors leading to deviations in the microbiota and affecting infant well-being in the first year1,2.

This paper uses published data from 3981 shotgun metagenomes of 1275 infants and 415 mothers from ten countries6 to identify species that contain opportunistic pathogens in the infant gut and link them to variables such as breastfeeding, antibiotic use and birth mode. In addition, we correlate the abundance of other bacterial taxa with the opportunistic pathogens to identify any putative suppressors of pathogen carriage.

Methods

Sample and data collection

This sub-study is based on data derived from a large meta-analysis of infant gut metagenomes previously published by Bargheet et al. (2025)6. Briefly: Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia, www.covidence.org) screened the literature and two independent researchers considered eligibility according to the following inclusion criteria: original reports on gut/faecal microbiota of human infants and potentially their mothers that used shotgun metagenomics, written in English and published in the last ten years. The parent study included 3981 high-quality shotgun metagenomic samples from 1275 infants and 415 mothers across 14 cohorts in 10 countries (USA, UK, Norway, Netherlands, Sweden, Finland, Luxembourg, Italy, South Africa, and Zimbabwe). All had used Illumina sequencing platforms. All data used in this sub-study were publicly available and fully anonymized; therefore, no new ethical approval was required.

The included cohorts provided longitudinal or cross-sectional sampling during the first two years of life, with a focus on early infancy. For the present analysis, a subset of infant samples with complete metadata was selected to investigate the prevalence and determinants of potentially opportunistic bacterial species. The metadata included infant age, mode of delivery (vaginal or Caesarean section), gestational age, feeding type (breastfed, formula-fed, or mixed), and antibiotic exposure prior to sample collection. Samples with missing or incomplete metadata for these variables were excluded. Metadata were curated following the protocol described by Bargheet et al. to ensure consistency across cohorts (see data processing below).

Data processing

The metagenomes were taxonomically annotated at species level using MetaPhlAn4 v.4.0.6 against the CHOCOphLAN database7. Metagenomes with more than 50% unclassified reads were excluded from the analysis to ensure taxonomic reliability.

We screened the species-level profiles to identify taxa with known opportunistic pathogenic potential, based on literature8, and computed their prevalence across the dataset.

Data on national antibiotic prescribing practices were obtained from Browne et al. (2021), which provided country-level prescription rates given as defined daily dosage (DDD), from 2010 and 2018 9 (Table 1).

Selection of opportunistic pathogens

A literature search for (opportunistic) pathogens and pathobionts in the infant gut yielded a list of infant gut opportunistic pathogens: Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, vancomycin-resistant enterococcus (VRE), Streptococcus agalactiae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Streptococcus gallolyticus, Staphylococcus aureus, Clostridioides difficile, Salmonella enterica, Listeria monocytogenes, Campylobacter jejuni, Helicobacter hepaticus, Helicobacter pylori, and Proteus mirabilis8,18,19.

We subsequently analysed all opportunistic pathogens with a prevalence above 10 cases in our dataset. Escherichia coli was excluded from analysis as it was present in almost all samples.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in R 4.3.2 (2024.09.1) within RStudio (version: 2024.09.1 + 394 “Cranberry Hibiscus” Release, for windows), using the packages: tidyverse20, reshape221, nlme22, ggeffects23, marginal effects24, lme425, and ggplot226.

When there were more than 10 unique cases of a opportunistic pathogen, Generalized Linear Mixed-Effects Regression (GLME) with a binomial distribution were used to identify associations between the opportunistic pathogen’s prevalence (number of positive cases observed) and early-life variables, including infant birth mode (caesarean section, vaginal and the use of intrapartum antibiotics), antibiotic use, infant feeding mode, and prevalence of other bacterial species. The models used a combined variable of family ID and study ID as a random effect to correct for multiple samples from the same person. The models included and adjusted for infant age, delivery method (including the use of intrapartum antibiotics), postnatal antibiotic use (a Boolean signifying whether a child had received antibiotics prior to sampling or not), prematurity, feeding mode, the number of sequencing reads, and, where geographical region had a significant effect, region. Where relevant, country-level variables, such as antibiotic prescription rates, skin-to-skin care prevalence, and GBS prevention strategies, were included in place of geographical region. To test for associated other bacterial species the bacterial species were also added to the model.

To model the association between the opportunistic pathogen and another bacterium the model only included and adjusted for infant age, antibiotics, prematurity and feeding mode and the bacterium in question.

Models were fitted separately for infant and maternal samples. However, due to the limited sample size and lower opportunistic pathogen prevalence in maternal samples, only infant samples were used for detailed statistical association testing.

The results are presented as adjusted estimates of prevalence calculated by the R package marginal effects. For the effect of the GBS prevention strategy on GBS carriage, we used empirical marginalisation to focus only on delivery for each prevention strategy. For the other predictions, we used the default marginalisation.

Carriage rates also differed by region. Infants in Africa (p < 0.001) and Europe (p < 0.001) were less likely to carry K. pneumoniae, while those in the USA were more likely to (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3E).

Certain bacterial species seemed to protect against K. pneumoniae carriage. Blautia sp. MSK_20_85 (p = 0.006) and Anaerobutyricum hallii (p = 0.011) were linked to lower carriage rates (Fig. 3H-K). Higher levels of Bacteroides ovatus (p = 0.009), C. difficile (p = 0.0019), and E. faecalis (p < 0.001) also made carriage less likely (Fig. 3L-N).

Staphylococcus aureus

Staphylococcus aureus prevalence changed with several factors. Older infants were less likely to carry this bacterium (p < 0.001, Fig. 4A). Infants born vaginally who received antibiotics during delivery also had reduced S. aureus carriage (p = 0.015, Fig. 4B).

Premature infants were more likely to carry S. aureus (p < 0.001, Fig. 4D). Breastfeeding was linked to higher S. aureus rates (p < 0.005, Fig. 4E), especially in countries with more kangaroo care (p < 0.001, Fig. 4F). Conclusion and discussion

Using an extensive metagenomic survey of 3497 faecal samples from 1275 infants, we discovered that healthy infants have a high prevalence of potential pathogens, including 14% prevalence of C. difficile, 1,88% prevalence of GBS, and almost 18% prevalence of K. pneumoniae in infants Table 2—much higher than in the maternal samples. These are likely underestimates, as the species may be present below the detection limit, and thus absence cannot be confirmed. While carriage is an obvious prerequisite to infection, most carriage does not lead to infection even in this highly susceptible population, as the CDC estimates the incidence of GBS infection at 0,2 cases per 100033 . While asymptomatic carriage of virulent GBS has been found at 20% in a hospital setting. Previous studies have reported C. difficile prevalence to be ~ 5–20% and have estimated that about half involve a toxigenic strain34–36. C. difficile infection almost always requires a predisposing antibiotic course34. However, asymptomatic colonisation is epidemiologically important as it enables the survival and further spread of the opportunistic pathogen in the population.

VRE, GBS and S. pneumoniae

Due to the low case count of VRE, GBS and S. pneumoniae (n = 12–24) the model did not identify significant associations beyond a small effect of infant age. However, these analyses are likely underpowered to detect modest or small associations. Older infants have a higher carriage change of VRE and S. pneumoniae, however GBS has a higher carriage change right after birth and the chance drops with age. This indicates that GBS is likely acquired at birth. Intrapartum antibiotics can prevent GBS infection cases when administered to mothers that carry GBS37, however country strategies for this administration differ and when clindamycin is used instead of penicillin, GBS might be able to thrive due to its resistance, rather than be eliminated38–40. It would therefore be interesting to study the effect of intrapartum antibiotics and early interventions such as kangaroo care and early breastfeeding41 on GBS carriage with a dataset containing more GBS cases.

K. pneumoniae

Our results show that K. pneumoniae is the least prevalent in vaginally born babies who did not receive antibiotics. Thus, it is one of the opportunistic pathogens associated with disrupted gut microbiota colonisation in caesarean-born and antibiotic-exposed infants1, likely acquired from the hospital environment in the absence of maternally transmitted protective bacteria. The prevalence was higher in the USA compared to other regions, but this was not explained by either the antibiotic DDD in the country nor a difference in kangaroo care.

We found that K. pneumoniae correlates negatively with several taxa, including Bacteroides, A. hallii and Blautia sp MSK. Bacteroides can limit Klebsiella through IL-36 signalling42. Bacteroides is also often missing in Caesarean born infants, where K. pneumoniae is more likely to be present1. It seems Bacteroides plays an important role in early life prevention of colonisation of this common opportunistic pathogen. These taxa are also known for their short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, which has been found to limit K. pneumoniae colonisation43–45. However, from this observational data we cannot infer whether the effects reflect potential colonisation resistance or shared ecological factors or whether there are direct effects. While there is evidence that Klebsiella may enhance C. difficile colonisation and inflammation46, we found C. difficile to be negatively associated with K. pneumoniae in infants.

S. aureus

According to our results, the infant carriage of S. aureus, a common skin bacterium that can cause infection in predominantly skin and soft-tissue47, was positively associated with breastfeeding and skin-to-skin contact. This is in line with previous studies finding S. aureus to be transferred though breastmilk48. Its prevalence was reduced by exposure to antibiotics, suggesting that it is sensitive to the commonly used antibiotics, typically beta-lactams, and might be lost from the mother’s skin to reduce transfer to the infant when intrapartum antibiotics are used. We have recently shown that S. aureus in the infant gut is strongly predictive of lower incidence of atopic dermatitis at 2 years of age, suggesting that it might have immunological benefits1. We found no commensal bacteria that inhibited the carriage of S. aureus.

C. difficile

The risk of C. difficile carriage increased with age. There was also a significantly higher prevalence in the USA compared to Europe and South Africa. Like with K. pneumoniae this difference was not explained by the country’s DDD of antibiotics nor by a difference in kangaroo care.

While C. difficile infection requires antibiotic exposure34,35, we did not see a significant effect of antibiotic exposure on C. difficile carriage. As in previous studies, breastfeeding was associated with a lower prevalence of C. difficile 4. Faecalibacterium has previously been reported to be negatively associated with C. difficile-49, and we also found F. prausnitzii relative abundance to associate negatively with C. difficile. F. prausnitzii consumes acetate and produces the SCFA butyrate and other bioactive anti-inflammatory molecules such as shikimic and salicylic acids50. We also found that two species of Dorea were negatively associated with C. difficile carriage. Dorea species have genes to metabolise primary bile acids51, which stimulate Clostridioides spore germination, to secondary bile acids, which inhibit germination34,52,53. However, while these are feasible biological explanations, our observational data does not indicate whether the presence of F. prausnitzii and Dorea spp. are indicative of a microbiome less permissible to C. difficile colonisation, or whether there are direct effects, as we can only observe correlation.

Additionally, we found that increasing relative abundance of E. faecalis and E. coli, indicative of a normal colonisation with common commensal bacteria in infants, was negatively associated with C. difficile.

Limitations

The opportunistic pathogens we found might be an underrepresentation of the actual prevalence in the infant gut, as MetaPhlAn4 is designed primarily for community profiling and may not perform optimally for detecting low-prevalence pathogens, especially at species level in a dataset with variable read depths.

The KC data are estimates per country based on studies done in one or multiple years between 2010 and 2020. The individual studies did not report on skin-to-skin contact for the infants. The ecological nature of key exposures means that unmeasured country-level confounders (healthcare systems, socioeconomic factors, cultural practices) cannot be properly controlled for in individual-level analyses.

Similarly, the DDD of antibiotics for each country was not measured at the individual level for included infants but instead reflect aggregated national policies and prescribing patterns. The KC and DDD data proxies could introduce an ecological bias if confounding factors such as access to care or maternal exposure associate with both the proxy and the outcome (opportunistic pathogen carriage). Additionally, a direction of the bias cannot be assumed. The findings using these proxies are better understood as hypothesis-generating and indicative of broader structural or healthcare system influences that warrant confirmation in datasets with directly measured individual exposures.

Additionally, it is unclear whether the bacteria identified were truly opportunistic pathogenic, as they likely include both opportunistic pathogenic and non-opportunistic pathogenic strains. Notably, these pathogens are opportunistic—the same strain can be pathogenic in certain situations, and a commensal in others. Additionally, all infants in the study were healthy, so we cannot draw conclusions about the risk of infection. However, colonisation is the first step towards infection, and one of the most effective ways to prevent infections is preventing colonisation by the opportunistic pathogen. The dataset is purely observational, and any associations observed are correlational; causal relationships cannot be inferred.
