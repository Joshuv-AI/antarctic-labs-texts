Omnidirectional motion of an untethered tripodal microrobot using radial piezoelectric actuators
Authors: Yu Gao, Jing Li, Shijing Zhang, Jipeng Yan, Yilin Zhang, Baoyi Liu, Jinghan Guan, Dehong Wang, Jie Deng, Yingxiang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMiniature robots require minimal operational space for confined environments. Here, we propose an untethered tripodal piezoelectric robot to achieve omnidirectional locomotion and narrowing its required turning space exactly to its compact size (Φ3.10 × 2.75 cm3, 12.2 g). Three radial piezoelectric actuators synthesize six circumferential driving forces, while a trajectory interpolation algorithm enables omnidirectional locomotion without attitude turning. By switching between high-frequency continuous and intermittent excitation modes, the robot achieves a wide dynamic range, spanning from a 3 cm/s macroscopic speed to a 0.56 μm microscopic step resolution, thereby enabling both rapid maze navigation and precise cross-scale microscopic observation. Integrating actuation, control, communication, five onboard sensors, and power, the robot execute various inspection tasks with the capability of autonomous navigation and multi-robot coordination. These results validate key performance attributes in achieving both high integration and omnidirectional motion to enhance the ability for inspection and exploration missions.Subject terms: Mechanical engineering, Actuators

Introduction

The ability to navigate and perform complex tasks within confined environments is a key driver for the development of miniature robots. Researchers are trying to realize the practical application capabilities in various fields, such as biotechnology1,2 and environmental exploration3,4. The design of such robots increasingly focuses on miniaturization and high system integration. However, these two goals present a fundamental trade-off: maximizing onboard functionality while minimizing physical size.

Miniaturizing the size with integration of power supply and sensors is crucial for a robot to perform complicated functions in a confined environment, and such miniaturization remains challenging. Researchers have reported miniaturized robots driven by electromagnetic motors5–9 with good mobility. However, miniature robots are limited by their transmission mechanisms and actuation principles, including structural complexity, susceptibility to electromagnetic interference and challenges in further miniaturization. The miniature robots driven by dielectric elastomers10–13 and magnetic force14–17 have been successfully developed but require severe excitation conditions (the electric field strength typically exceeds 1 × 104 V/mm) and bulky setups to generate the external magnetic field, respectively. Miniature robots actuated by light18, heat19, and shape memory alloys20,21 have been demonstrated, but usually have a long response time due to the slow energy exchange process. Piezoelectric actuators22–27 feature fast response, simple structure, and have been applied in precision motion platforms and miniature robotic systems.

A robot’s maneuverability is not only defined by its size but also by its turning radius. A robot might not be able to make turns in a space where it can easily pass. To address this, we define a dimensionless metric known as the minimum reorientation cost (RCmin) (see Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1 for a definition). Our findings indicate that the turning radius inherently increases the reorientation cost during a robot’s planar locomotion. In this context, the inevitable turning radius of microrobots driven by piezoelectric actuators imposes comparable constraints on spatial reorientation costs, thereby limiting their accessibility to confined environments. It is because most current reports on miniature piezoelectric robots use linear decoupling drive methods. Robots using this method typically possess a single linear driving force (forward and backward) and rely on reorienting their own attitude to change the direction of actuation, covering the full range (0 ~ 360°). Frequent attitude adjustments and repositioning within confined spaces further complicate the task of motion control. Previous works have partially addressed this challenge through two main strategies. The first aims to reduce the turning radius mechanically. For example, one miniature robot28 utilizes electrostatic footpads to create a pivot foot, effectively reducing the turning radius to the robot’s width. The second strategy relies on tangentially distributed force synthesis29,30 for omnidirectional motion. Although this configuration eliminates the turning radius, it is inherently optimized for generating rotational torque, which makes achieving stable linear motion difficult due to its susceptibility to undesirable yaw and path deviation. Therefore, the development of microrobots with highly integrated systems and the capability for zero-radius omnidirectional maneuverability remains a central challenge.

Here, we present an untethered miniature tripodal piezoelectric robot that utilizes three radially distributed actuation units to achieve planar locomotion without attitude adjustments. Key advancements of this work are as follows.

(i) Locomotion strategy. A miniature tripodal piezoelectric robot with three radial piezoelectric actuators (compact size: Φ3.10 × 2.75 cm3, light weight: 12.2 g) is developed. The tripodal radial actuation strategy achieves six discrete motion directions symmetrically distributed along the circumference. Combined with the trajectory interpolation algorithm, these enable omnidirectional planar locomotion through vector decomposition. The robot demonstrates H, I, and T letter trajectory drawing, bidirectional maze navigation, and gas leak inspection using an onboard ethanol sensor. (ii) Cross-scale motion. By switching between high-frequency continuous and intermittent excitation modes, the robot achieves a wide dynamic range, spanning from a macroscopic speed of 3 cm/s down to a microscopic step resolution of 0.56 μm, thereby enabling both rapid maze navigation and precise cross-scale microscopic observation. (such as wafer detection and cell specimen observation). (iii) Miniaturization and high integration. Benefiting from the integration of actuation, communication, control, sensing (five types of sensors) and power supply units into the robot’s body, the robot demonstrates untethered motion, a long endurance of over 49 min and applications of multi-sensor integration (including autonomous navigation and multi-robot coordination).

Results

Structure and actuation

As shown in Fig. 1a, the proposed piezoelectric robot consists of a tripodal structure with a power supply unit (Fig. 1ai) and an onboard circuit unit (Fig. 1aii) located at its top and bottom, respectively. The robot features three independently controlled actuation units arranged in a radial layout. Each actuation unit consists of a mass block, a thin plate, a main body (all made of aluminum alloy, 2A12), a lead zirconate titanate (PZT-4) piezoelectric ceramic plate, and a driving foot (Alumina), as shown in Fig. 1a(iii). The thin plate (0.4 mm) is designed to suppress the transmission of vibrations from the driving foot to the central cover. Three actuation units are arranged 120° apart from each other, and each unit forms a 45° angle with the ground. An actuation unit generates a radial driving force through the accumulation of high-frequency, micron-scale vibrations at the foot end. Additionally, a synthetic radial driving force can be formed between any pair of these units. This results in six primary circumferentially distributed driving forces, enabling full 360° coverage.

The power supply unit generates high-voltage square wave signals (three outputs, up to 60 Vp-p) for the actuation units and supports wireless communication to enable the robot to move untetheredly and under control. The system integrates five types of sensors, including position, attitude, gas, voltage, and temperature sensors. The tripodal configuration creates ample clearance at the base, facilitating convenient installation of the onboard circuit unit while maintaining balanced weight distribution and compact dimensions. Detailed parameters of each unit can be found in Supplementary Fig. 2. For a more detailed view of the tripodal robot, please see part I in Supplementary Movie S1.

The actuation unit uses 1st-order bending vibration as the operating mode, exciting a high-frequency in-plane oblique trajectory at the driving foot. The piezoelectric composite structure can repeatedly extend and contract, causing the driving foot to strike the ground, as shown in Fig. 1b and part II in Supplementary Movie S1. The piezoelectric ceramic is bonded to the metal elastomer and undergoes periodic expansion and contraction by altering the direction of the excitation signal, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3.

The omnidirectional motion strategy includes two key approaches. (i) Tripodal radial actuation strategy. Three radial actuation units generate driving forces along six circumferentially distributed directions (three fundamental directions and three synthetic directions). We define two operating states of an actuation unit. (i) When the excitation frequency of an actuation unit matches its resonant frequency, it operates in the optimal vibration state (OVS), achieving maximum vibration displacement. (ii) When the excitation frequency is set to a non-resonant mode to induce the high-frequency friction-reduction effect, the unit operates in the low-frequency bounce state (LBS).

The robot has two locomotion modes: the single actuation mode (Fig. 1ci) and the dual actuation mode (Fig. 1cii). The states of the driving legs are indicated by green (OVS) and blue (LBS) circles. This figure shows the robot from a lateral view, where the viewing direction is shown in the upper-left corner. Under this view, two driving legs are overlapped, and their states are represented by two stacked circles. In the single actuation mode, one of the actuation units operates in OVS, while the other two remain in LBS. In this mode, the robot moves forward along the extension line of the actuation unit in OVS. In the dual actuation mode, two of the actuation units operate in OVS, while only one remains in LBS. In this case, the optimal driving force of the robot is oriented between the two OVS units.

As a result, the robot moves in the opposite direction of the extension line of the LBS actuation unit (between two OVS units), with a displacement of ΔS2. For a detailed animation of the locomotion, see Part II in Supplementary Movie S1. Thus, the single and dual actuation modes generate three direct and three synthetic driving vectors, respectively, which collectively enable the robot to perform planar locomotion along six directions (See Supplementary Fig. 4a for a schematic diagram of the six directions).

(ii) Trajectory interpolation algorithm. Since the six actuation directions generated by the tripodal radial actuation strategy are discretely distributed, they are insufficient to provide the fine resolution required for motion along arbitrary planar trajectories. To address this limitation, we propose an interpolation-based motion method based on the tripodal radial actuation strategy. The interpolation algorithm densifies discrete data points and computes the optimal combination of two corresponding actuation unit directions (two basis vectors) for a given target trajectory. The robot can fit complex motion paths by decomposing the desired trajectory into a series of steps along the two basis vectors. For detailed algorithmic information, please refer to Supplementary Note 2 and Supplementary Fig. 4b. With six primary actuation directions enabling a full range (0 ~ 360°), the robot performs planar locomotion without attitude adjustment by dynamically switching the basis vectors for interpolation.

By combining these two aspects, the robot achieves omnidirectional planar locomotion capability without the need for attitude adjustment. We elaborate on these two approaches as follows. Two prototypes of the tripodal piezoelectric robot (Single robot, 12.2 g, Φ3.10 × 2.75 cm3, untethered) are fabricated, with a comparison to a 1-dollar coin shown in Fig. 1d. We measure the vibration modes of the three actuation units using the laser vibration measurement platform (10 Vp-p, sweep frequency mode, see Supplementary Fig. 5). All three units exhibit the first-order bending vibration mode, as shown in Fig. 1e and Supplementary Movie S2. The resonant frequencies of three actuation units are 31.51, 31.46, and 31.66 kHz, respectively, which deviate from the modal simulation frequency (31.79 kHz, detailed analysis is in the next section) by only 0.88%, 1.04%, and 0.41%, respectively. The keyframes from a motion movie are extracted into a synthetic image, as shown in Fig. 1f. The robot achieves wide-range macroscopic locomotion on a glass substrate. Under the dual actuation mode, the robot reaches an untethered speed of 29.09 mm/s (60 Vp-p), corresponding to 0.94 body lengths per second (BL/s).

Simulation analysis and performance characterization

The robot’s dimensional parameters are established based on three factors (see “Methods” section) and miniaturization requirements. The thin plate serves as a key structural component, with its primary design parameters being height and thickness. We establish a finite element method (FEM) simulation model of the robot, and the material parameters involved are provided in Supplementary Note 3. Details on the determination of the thin plate’s key parameters can be found in Supplementary Note 4.

For the finalized actuation unit, the first to fourth vibration mode shapes and the resonant frequencies are 31.79, 68.71, 117.24 and 192.70 kHz, respectively, as shown in Fig. 2a. Lower-order modes are easier to excite and require fewer piezoelectric elements and simpler fabrication processes. Therefore, the first-order bending vibration is selected as the working mode of the robot. In the transient dynamic analysis, one actuation unit is excited under OVS conditions (80 Vp-p, 31.79 kHz), with the other two units unexcited. Figure 2bi defines the orientation of the reference coordinate system, and the test point is located at the foot end. Simulation results show that the vibration amplitudes of the driving foot end along the OY and OZ directions are 11.15 and 8.83 μm, respectively, as shown in Fig. 2bii. The vibration trajectory approximates an oblique line, as shown in Fig. 2c. The foot-end displacement is mainly concentrated along the OY and OZ directions, while the displacement in the OX direction, which is perpendicular to the driving direction, is small (less than 0.1 μm). The vibration characteristics of three actuation units are measured by the laser vibration measurement platform (10 Vp-p, fixed frequency mode, see Supplementary Fig. 5), as shown in Fig. 2d. The vibration velocities are 109.16, 131.42, and 128.66 mm/s, respectively. The mean vibration velocity is 123.08 mm/s.

Under ideal conditions, when a single actuation unit operates at a high frequency, the vibration amplitude of the non-actuated unit should tend toward zero. Transmissibility is used to evaluate the vibration isolation performance of the thin plate. Specifically, we excite the robot in single/dual actuation mode and observe the vibration velocities of the remaining units. We define the reference vibration velocity as the mean vibration velocity of three actuation units (123.08 mm/s). The vibration transmissibility is defined as the ratio between the vibration velocity of the observed unit and the reference velocity. A higher transmissibility indicates a poorer vibration isolation effect. The isolation test results are shown in Fig. 2e. The results show that the maximum observed vibration velocity of 21.74 mm/s, corresponding to a transmissibility of 17.66%, occurs when leg-1 is in OVS, and leg-3 remains unactuated. The robot exhibits good vibration isolation performance, confirming the validity of the isolation structure design.

Using the micro-displacement measurement platform (see Supplementary Fig. 7), we measure the actual vibration displacement of the driving foot end (80 Vp-p). Figure 2f, g show the displacements in the OY and OZ directions, which are 10.03 and 9.31 μm, respectively. The synthesized trajectory in the OYZ plane forms an oblique line, consistent with the simulation results, as shown in Fig. 2h.

Locomotion characteristics of the untethered robot

Untethered motion ability serves as the foundation for wide-range flexible mobility. The power characteristics of the tethered robot are shown in Fig. 3a. The results demonstrate that power consumption increases with the applied voltage, rising from only 18.9 mW at 10 Vp-p to a maximum of 611.7 mW at 60 Vp-p (at 31.28 kHz). Under a constant excitation voltage, the power consumption of the driving leg initially increases and subsequently decreases with rising frequency, peaking at the resonance point. Additionally, the power consumption of a single driving leg in LBS is measured at 313.8 mW and 74.0 mW under excitation voltages of 60 Vp-p and 30 Vp-p, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8a). We analyze the motion efficacy of the robot across various directions and observe a decline in energy utilization at specific yaw angles (Supplementary Note 5). The reduced efficacy at synthesis angles results from vector cancellation combined with cumulative power consumption. Based on the power requirements, the onboard circuit unit is designed, including two vertically stacked circuit boards (the top board and the bottom board), as shown in Fig. 3bi, ii, respectively. The stacked design makes efficient use of the spatial advantages offered by the tripodal structure, allowing the integration of additional functional and sensing units. The onboard circuit unit supports three independent square-wave outputs with adjustable voltage ranging from 0 to 60 Vp-p. A lens is mounted underneath the bottom board to reflect the laser beam generated by the position sensor, requiring a clearance from the ground (~2 mm). Please see Supplementary Fig. 9 and Note 6 for the detailed working principle of the actuation part in the onboard circuit unit.

We conduct experiments to evaluate the locomotion performance of the robot in six directions on a glass substrate. The relationships between frequency and velocity in single actuation mode are shown in Fig. 3c. The excitation voltage is set to 30 Vp-p, and the motion direction is indicated in the figure. The maximum velocities are 24.23, 20.55, and 16.5 mm/s, with the corresponding excitation frequencies being 31.65, 30.95, and 30.98 kHz, respectively. To evaluate the rate of change in the excitation frequency-velocity relationship, we introduce a frequency sensitivity metric, defined as the magnitude of velocity variation per Hz. The results indicate that the sensitivity values for all three fundamental motion directions remain below 0.52 mm s−1 Hz−1. Given that the minimum frequency adjustment increment used in the tests is 10 Hz, the achievable velocity control resolution is less than 5.2 mm/s. Notably, in the dual actuation mode (with two legs in OVS), controlling velocity through frequency modulation proves difficult. Consequently, we employ voltage adjustment to regulate the robot’s motion speed. The motion characteristics under the dual actuation mode are shown in Fig. 3d. At 30 Vp-p, the robot in the dual actuation mode achieves velocities of 22.3, 23.36, and 12.74 mm/s, respectively. At 60 Vp-p, the robot reaches velocities of 30.08, 29.09, and 28.21 mm/s. The maximum speed reaches 0.97 BL/s when leg-1 and leg-2 serve as active units. According to the voltage-velocity relationship, the voltage sensitivities for the three synthetic directions are 0.26, 0.19, and 0.52 mm s−1 Vp-p−1. With a minimum adjustment increment of 30 Vp-p, the corresponding maximum velocity variations are 7.8, 5.7, and 15.6 mm/s, respectively. Details of the locomotion in single actuation mode and dual actuation mode are provided in Supplementary Movie S3 (Parts Ⅰ and Ⅱ, respectively), and both modes enable effective planar locomotion. We observe that the minimum excitation voltage required for effective motion is 30 Vp-p. This corresponds to minimum velocities of 7.73 mm/s and 12.74 mm/s for the three fundamental directions and the three synthetic directions, respectively. Therefore, the velocity regulation ranges are 7.73 ~ 24.23 mm/s for the fundamental directions and 12.74 ~ 30.08 mm/s for the synthetic directions.

By modulating the driving force ratios of the two active legs in OVS, we find that the tripodal radial actuation strategy can also synthesize six additional asymmetric motion angles. We characterize the robot’s omnidirectional motion across 12 directions in 30° increments, including six asymmetric synthetic angles (see Part III in Supplementary Movie S3). For each direction, a 90-mm trajectory segment is extracted for analysis (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Orientation stability is evaluated via yaw offset, defined as the angular difference between the theoretical target and the actual trajectory angle (ranging from 0° to 360° relative to the positive x-axis). The results show a minimum yaw offset of 0.2° during motion toward the 30° azimuth, while a maximum offset of 5.6° occurred at the 120° azimuth (Supplementary Fig. 10b). Furthermore, the trajectory deviation, including the average and maximum trajectory deviations, are employed to quantify trajectory-keeping capabilities (definitions provided in Supplementary Fig. 10c caption). The average deviation ranges from a minimum of 0.4 mm (0.4% relative to travel distance) at 0° to a maximum of 1.8 mm (2.0%) at 90°, while the maximum deviation spans from 1.4 mm (1.6%) at 0° to 5.1 mm (5.7%) at 90°. These results demonstrate that the robot, governed by the tripodal radial actuation strategy, effectively achieves planar motion across 12 discretized directions (30° resolution) with trajectory-keeping capabilities.

Beyond the glass substrate, the robot also demonstrates effective locomotion on marble and stainless-steel surfaces (see Part I in Supplementary Movie S4). The velocity characteristics across the six primary motion directions on these diverse contact interfaces are presented in Supplementary Fig. 11. The robot achieves a peak velocity of 30.66 mm/s on the glass substrate (actuation: #1 and #3; LBS: #2). In comparison, the maximum locomotion speeds on marble and stainless-steel surfaces reach 26.21 and 26.06 mm/s, respectively (actuation: #1 and #2; LBS: #3 for both cases). Furthermore, the robot exhibits a load-carrying capacity, achieving omnidirectional planar motion (six directions) while carrying a payload of approximately 13.63 times its body weight (robot: 2.2 g; total load: 30 g, including a 10 g power unit and a 20 g additional mass; see Part II in Supplementary Movie S4). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 12, the locomotion speed in all six directions follows a non-monotonic trend characterized by an initial increase followed by a subsequent decrease as the payload increases. The maximum velocity across six directions is achieved at an additional load of 10 g (peak value: 36.8 mm/s, 1.19 BL/s; actuation: #1 and #3, LBS: #2). This phenomenon is likely attributed to the frictional coupling between the driving feet and the substrate. At lower weights, the robot’s self-weight generates insufficient friction to match the driving force, leading to partial slippage during the actuation cycle. An appropriate increase in payload enhances the frictional force, thereby reducing the slip duration and increasing the velocity. However, excessive friction eventually restricts the displacement of the foot tips, resulting in a subsequent decline in locomotion speed. The payload characteristic tests demonstrate the robot’s considerable load-carrying capacity, highlighting the potential for future integration with functional manipulation units. Moreover, the robot demonstrates steady ascent at a maximum incline angle of 1.5° (see Part Ⅲ in Supplementary Movie S4). As the tilt angle increased to 2°, the robot exhibited stalling and failed to maintain movement on the incline. Furthermore, the robot generates miniature step-size displacements using an intermittent actuation method, achieving low-speed, high-resolution motion. The core method is to generate high-frequency intermittent excitation signals, placing the robot in a stepping motion state. Once the robot is set to operate in this state, the onboard circuit unit generates a signal mask based on the original continuous excitation signal (see Supplementary Fig. 13). The signal mask is periodically applied to modulate the continuous output. The key parameters are the mask frequency and duty cycle, which determine the duration of a full mask cycle and the effective signal output duration within each cycle, respectively.

We set the mask frequency to 10 Hz and measure the motion step size under various directions (three fundamental directions and three synthetic directions) and duty cycle conditions (the testing platform is shown in Supplementary Fig. 14a), as shown in Fig. 3e. The results indicate that a lower duty cycle leads to a finer step size, resulting in higher motion resolution and slower locomotion speed. We utilize the derivative of the duty cycle-step size curve to define the step sensitivity (in μm/1% duty cycle), which serves to quantify the step control precision during micro-scale low-speed motion, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 14b. The maximum average step size variation per 1% duty cycle increment is 1.38 μm, with an actual sensitivity range of 0.29 ~ 3.53 μm/1% duty cycle (actuation: #3, LBS: #1 and #2). Figure 3fi presents a representative displacement curve of the stepping motion. Notably, the robot exhibits a stepping motion pattern, with its motion frequency matching the mask frequency (10 Hz, 10 steps per second). The robot achieves micrometer-level minimum step sizes in all six directions, with the minimum step size reaching 0.56 μm (Actuation: #1 and #2; LBS: #3), as shown in Fig. 3 fii. Combined with its macroscopic locomotion ability, the robot shows potential for planar motion tasks across macro-to-micro scales. As the two modes differ only in the form of the driving signal, the switching overhead remains low, ensuring operational convenience. Moreover, powered by a 180 mAh lithium battery, the robot sustained wide-range macroscopic locomotion (30 Vp-p) for over 49 min, achieving a cumulative travel distance of 62.1 m (see Supplementary Movie S5).

Planar motion performance and applications

Leveraging multi-sensor integration and its omnidirectional planar motion, the proposed robot is capable of performing a series of diverse locomotion tasks and application-specific functions. The robot traces the letters H, I, and T on an iron substrate (see Part Ⅰ of Supplementary Movie S6). We use a vision-based tracking algorithm to label the trajectories, as shown in Fig. 4a. The robot completes each trajectory without adjusting the attitude, enabling more efficient and simplified path execution. Moreover, we construct a miniature maze with a glass substrate, beneath which five magnets are placed as checkpoints, as shown in Fig. 4b. The tripodal piezoelectric robot demonstrates the ability to traverse the maze both in the forward direction (Fig. 4bi) and in reverse (Fig. 4bii). When the user guides the robot to a checkpoint, the onboard hall sensor detects a change in the magnetic field, which is displayed on the mobile terminal (see Part Ⅱ in Supplementary Movie S6).

The proposed robot integrates an onboard ethanol gas sensor capable of detecting ethanol gas in the air. A narrow tunnel is constructed with an ethanol leak source, which consists of an ethanol atomizer used to simulate the leakage point (Fig. 4c). The robot starts from the initial position and moves forward while continuously reading the gas sensor data and transmitting it to the mobile terminal. As the robot approaches the leak source, the sensor value increases and reaches up to 74 times its initial value (see Supplementary Movie S7). When the robot moves away from the leak source, the sensor reading gradually decreases. The sensor data curve aligns well with the actual location of the leak source, demonstrating the capability to carry sensors for mobile operations.

Furthermore, the omnidirectional planar locomotion is extended from macroscopic to microscopic motion by combining the high-frequency continuous and intermittent excitation modes. The robot achieves both the wide-range, relatively fast motion and the miniature-range, low-speed, high-resolution motion. Thus, a microscopic observation platform (see Supplementary Fig. 15) is constructed to achieve scratch detection on a wafer using the microscopic locomotion of the robot (see Part Ⅰ in Supplementary Movie S8), as shown in Fig. 4di. During the inspection process, the robot is able to reposition for observations in different directions without needing to adjust its orientation. This eliminates issues commonly associated with conventional robots, such as blind spots caused by random turning radii and the need for re-localization. In addition, the robot is capable of carrying biological slides for microscopic observation of biological cells, as shown in Fig. 4dii. The robot transports both animal and plant slides, and their basis structures are observed under the microscope (see Part Ⅱ in Supplementary Movie S8).

In addition to high-resolution microscopic observation, the robot can leverage its ability to switch between microscopic high-precision and macroscopic fast-motion modes to perform cross-scale microscopic observation (see Supplementary Movie S9). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 16a, a dual-station observation platform consisting of two microscopes with different magnifications (×750 and ×1500) is established for image stitching and defect localization. First, the robot, carrying a target chip, moves rapidly to the scanning station (×750). Due to the limited field of view (FOV) inherent to high-magnification lenses, the robot utilizes its high-precision stepping mode combined with an image-stitching algorithm to reconstruct a panoramic view of the chip (Supplementary Fig. 16b). The stitched results reveal two potential defects consisting of suspected broken wires in this multi-pin wire-bonding chip. Subsequently, the robot switches to macroscopic fast-motion mode to reach the second workstation—the inspection station (×1500). Here, the robot reverts to microscopic stepping mode to precisely relocate and confirm the identified defects. Using the stitched panoramic image as a reference for planar coordinates, the robot confirms two wire-bonding fractures (Supplementary Fig. 16c). This experiment demonstrates the robot’s flexible cross-scale motion switching capability and its promising potential in fields such as micro-defect inspection.

To evaluate the robustness required for practical applications, the robot is elevated to a height of 0.6 m and released in its working posture for a free-fall impact onto the working surface (Fig. 4e). Despite flipping and rolling upon impact, the robot maintained normal power supply, communication, and locomotion functionality (see Part I in Supplementary Movie S10). Subsequently, an iron block, weighing approximately 559 times (1230 g) the original tripodal robot (2.2 g), is placed on the robot for 6 s (Fig. 4f). Although structural deformation occurred under the load, the robot partially recovers its original shape after the load is removed and retains its capability for untethered locomotion (see Part II in Supplementary Movie S10). Following the two sets of experiments, the degradation of velocity characteristics across the six primary directions is evaluated (Fig. 4g). While the robot preserves its locomotion capability in the six primary directions, varying degrees of speed reduction are observed. The maximum reduction occurs in the synthetic direction of driving legs #1 and #3 (dual actuation mode), representing a decrease of approximately 55.3%. This is followed by the single actuation mode (actuation: #3; LBS: #1 and #2), which shows a 50.9% decline. These results suggest that driving leg #3 sustains relatively more severe damage during the two tests, which alters the overall vibration characteristics.

Application of multi-sensor integration

The proposed robot demonstrates omnidirectional planar motion capabilities. However, its open-loop control scheme requires tedious command transmission and manual state determination, which restricts its utility in practical applications. We propose a motion strategy based on multi-sensor integration, where complementary sensing capabilities are coordinated to automatically determine the required driving direction vector, enabling autonomous navigation and synchronized multi-robot operation. Specifically, the user inputs the required motion trajectory (a series of key points) through a mobile control terminal, enabling the robot to autonomously navigate through designated checkpoints in order. The point-to-point (PTP) navigation process operates in a single-cycle manner, involving only the target point and the current position at each iteration.

The working principle of the PTP navigation scheme is shown in Fig. 5a. The mobile control terminal establishes transmission control protocol (TCP) communication with the robotic system, while the robot broadcasts its status information via the WebSocket protocol. The host device can issue commands through hypertext transfer protocol (HTTP), including macro/micro motion actuation and motion parameter configuration. The robot utilizes an optical position sensor and an attitude sensor to record real-time relative displacement and angular deviation (for attitude compensation), respectively. The shortest path between the start and target points is decomposed into two selected basis vectors, and the corresponding interpolation trajectory is generated. The next motion direction and the required actuation units are determined by computing the angular deviation in real time. This process primarily employs a Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) control algorithm. The PTP navigation scheme eliminates the need for attention to robot attitude calibration and actuation direction determination. The interface of the mobile control terminal (Fig. 5b), consists of four functional areas: a status bar, robot information panel, status box, and drawing area. The key features are as follows. (i) Status bar: providing system-level operational feedback. (ii) Robot information: presenting quantitative parameter information for the motion. (iii) Status box: displaying real-time robot positioning and attitude data. (iv) Drawing area: enabling users to input sequential target points through graphical interaction.

(i) Number “6”-shaped trajectory. (ii) Triangle trajectory. d The multi-robot application. Using the PTP navigation algorithm, we conduct demonstration experiments of autonomous navigation along a number “6”-shaped and a triangular path, as shown in Fig. 5ci, ii, respectively. Firstly, a series of target points forming a number “6” pattern is generated. The left panel of the image displays the actual motion trajectory recorded in real time and captured by the mobile terminal. Key target points are marked along this trajectory. The robot automatically reaches all target points in sequence and completes the number “6”-shaped trajectory (see Part I in Supplementary Movie S11). The right panel presents the actual motion process, recorded by the overhead camera of the experimental platform. It is worth noting that the robot is able to determine the current motion mode (single or dual) and the direction of locomotion, and the entire process is performed autonomously by the robot. The autonomous navigation along the triangular path (see Part II in Supplementary Movie S11) is shown in Fig. 5cii. Notably, a transformation exists between the user-defined coordinate system and the robot’s actual motion coordinate system, which is defined by scale factors along the X and Y directions. Users can perform calibration by adjusting these scale factors. We adjust the OX and OY direction scale factors separately, as shown in Fig. 5ci, ii, respectively. As a result, the actual motion trajectory undergoes corresponding deformation along the X and Y directions.

In addition, the PTP navigation system exhibits robustness and holds potential for multi-robot applications. When a robot’s position sensor is damaged or non-functional, the PTP navigation system enables robots to share position information, thereby restoring the autonomous navigation capability of the faulty robot. To demonstrate this feature, we assume a scenario in which one robot (white) operates normally, while the other robot (black) experiences a position sensor failure, as shown in Fig. 5di. Due to the loss of position information, the black robot is unable to perform autonomous motion (although open-loop actuation remains functional). The normally functioning robot shares its current position through the mobile terminal, as shown in Fig. 5dii. The black robot receives the coordinate data of the white robot from this network and synchronizes it as its own current position. The user-defined target point is shared between the two robots. Under these conditions, the black robot restores its PTP navigation capability.

We place the two robots on an iron substrate, with the mobile terminal connected only to the white robot, as shown in Fig. 5e. A set of four key target points forming a diagonal line pattern is defined and shared by both robots. The white robot sequentially reaches all target points, while the black robot utilizes the position information of the white robot to achieve synchronized autonomous navigation. Robots operate in a state of position synchronization (see Part Ⅰ in Supplementary Movie S12). Due to network latency, some positional deviation and time lag between motions are observed.

Furthermore, we increase the number of robots to three and transition to a robot vision feedback platform, demonstrating that the PTP navigation system exhibits good scalability in terms of both the number of controlled agents and the underlying platform framework (see Part II in Supplementary Movie S12). Multi-robot motion demonstration is achieved by integrating the vision feedback platform with the PTP navigation algorithm. Initially, we consider a single-robot scenario where the user can select the target robot within the video stream display area and designate a target position (Supplementary Fig. 17a). The host computer then transmits the target coordinates to the robot. Leveraging the onboard PTP navigation system, the robot autonomously calculates the required actuation ratios for its driving legs, enabling point-to-point motion without the need for users to manage orientation deflection or complex control commands. In a multi-robot scenario, three robots (ID: 0, 3, and 4) are selected as the controlled agents, with robot ID: 0 designated as the leader. Upon the user specifying a target point, the leader (ID: 0) autonomously navigates toward the goal, while the remaining robots (Followers) autonomously follow the leader (Supplementary Fig. 17b). The entire group ceases motion once all following robots have reached the target destination.

Discussion

This work presents an untethered miniature tripodal piezoelectric robot that utilizes three radial actuation units to achieve omnidirectional planar locomotion. The radial distribution of the piezoelectric actuators facilitates motion synthesis and the generation of zero rotation torque, thereby preventing unpredictable attitude deflections caused by torque imbalances. Zero-radius turning capability improves the usefulness of miniature robots for inspection and exploration tasks, especially in tight spaces. The robot demonstrates H, I, and T letter trajectory drawing, bidirectional maze navigation, and gas leak inspection using an onboard ethanol sensor. Moreover, the robot can switch between a continuous mode with speeds up to 3 cm/s for maze navigation, and an intermittent mode with a minimum step size of 0.56 μm for precision microscopic observation. This cross-scale locomotion capability enables the robot to perform wide-range, multi-point chip defect inspections. Featuring a compact and highly integrated design, the miniature prototype incorporates actuation, communication, control, power, and sensor units. The robot achieves untethered motion and a long endurance (over 49 min), enabling applications based on multi-sensor integration. The robot can carry a maximum payload of approximately 13.63 times its body weight and achieves effective omnidirectional locomotion on glass, marble, and stainless-steel surfaces. Furthermore, the robot retains its functional locomotion capabilities after sustaining a 0.6 m drop impact and a continuous compressive load of approximately 559 times its body weight, demonstrating exceptional structural robustness. Moreover, real-time switching of actuation vectors and autonomous trajectory interpolation have been achieved by the PTP navigation system, enabling the robot to achieve autonomous navigation (e.g., following a number “6”-shaped path and a triangular path). Through position data sharing enabled, a robot with a sensor failure is able to restore its autonomous navigation capability, enhancing the overall robustness of the robot. We implement three multi-robot auto-following scenarios, demonstrating the scalability and practical application potential of the PTP navigation system.

Furthermore, we evaluate the power consumption using the cost of transport (CoT), with a calculated value of 139 (see Supplementary Note 7). The CoT is compared with other similar robots5,6,13,28–35 and insects36–41, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 18. The results show that the robot achieves a good balance between high integration and power consumption. The main sources of power consumption are the boost conversion on the circuit board and the power supply requirements of the multiple onboard sensors. In addition, we compare the key performance metrics (actuation mode, size, weight, maximum motion speed, minimum motion step distance, RCmin, multiple degrees of freedom (DOFs) and endurance) of miniature robots with different actuation types5–17,20,21,28–35,42–44, as shown in Table S1. The robot combines a miniaturized form factor with a low-cost in-plane reorientation capability and the ability to perform high-resolution movements in an untethered configuration.

Besides, the proposed robot still has some limitations: (i) Currently, the robot is used solely as an object-carrying platform for microscopic observation experiments, which limits its practical application ability. Integrating active functional units (e.g., suction and gripping) can enrich the range of objects that the robot is capable of manipulating. (ii) Due to constraints in size and weight, the boost capability and output current of the onboard circuit unit are limited, which adversely affects the robot’s motion performance. (iii) Since the robot is driven by micro-amplitude vibrations of its foot tips, its operation has primarily been demonstrated on smooth, planar surfaces. Exploring and expanding the robot’s locomotion capabilities on non-uniform surfaces is essential for broadening its potential application scenarios. (iv) Manufacturing and installation errors induce parasitic torques, which, combined with the constraints in motion synthesis and trajectory interpolation precision, inevitably lead to deviations in the robot’s path.

Some possible future directions and challenges include: (i) Exploring more efficient actuation methods and piezoelectric ceramic arrangements to improve the motion performances. (ii) Integrating functional operational units (such as miniature grippers) to enable micro-gripping applications and multi-robot collaborative demonstrations. (iii) Optimizing the power management of the onboard circuit unit to reduce power consumption and further extend the operational endurance. (iv) Developing velocity smoothing algorithms and implementing closed-loop velocity control systems to further enhance the robot’s controllability and ensure the smoothness of task execution.

Methods

Robot materials and fabrication

The tripodal piezoelectric robot is made of aluminum alloy and three pieces of piezoelectric ceramic plates. The material of the driving foot is mainly alumina. The connectors and the shells are fabricated by 3D printing technology (Stereolithography, photosensitive resin). The fabrication process of the robot is shown in Supplementary Fig. 19a. We apply a thin and uniform layer of epoxy resin to one side of a piezoelectric ceramic (PZT) plate (see Supplementary Fig. 19ai, ii). A custom-designed fixture for the actuation unit is developed, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 19aiii. The fixture consists of a cover block, two bolts, two nuts, and a base. Tightening the bolts drives the cover block to apply a preload to the PZT. The preload does not require a precise value but should be appropriate and constant. This preload is maintained for 24 h (see Supplementary Fig. 19aiv).

Afterward, electrical wires are soldered to the silver-coated surface of the PZT using a soldering iron (see Supplementary Fig. 19av). All three actuation units undergo the same fabrication process. The completed actuation units are then placed into the reserved grooves of the central cover (both upper and lower parts). The upper and lower blocks are used to compress and bond the central cover with the three actuation units via epoxy resin. A preload is again applied and maintained for 24 h using bolts and nuts, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 19avi. Similarly, the driving foot of the actuation unit is fixed to the ends of the unit using epoxy resin and is left to cure for 24 h (see Supplementary Fig. 19b).

High-frequency friction-reduction effect

The high-frequency friction-reduction effect is employed to minimize the robot’s movement resistance. Under this effect, the non-actuated units of the robot remain in LBS rather than in a static state. Although the driving force generated by the actuation unit in LBS is lower than that generated in OVS, it importantly reduces the friction acting on the driving foot. This effect leverages the principle of vibration-induced friction reduction, which can be explained as follows: the driving foot periodically makes and breaks contact with the surface under the effect of vibration, transforming static loading into dynamic loading. As a result, the average normal force between the foot and the substrate is reduced, thereby decreasing the passive friction. The frequency for the LBS is uniformly set to 10 kHz in experimental tests.

Key factors in determining structural parameters

Parameters determination of the robot is mainly based on the following three factors. (i) Resonant frequency. Resonant frequency should be over 20 kHz to ensure that there is no sound noise for humans during motion. (ii) Foot-end displacement. There must be an effective displacement at the foot end. Specifically, the displacement of the actuation unit in OVS should be greater than 5 μm, which can ensure that the robot can overcome the frictional force between the foot end and the working surface. (iii) Vibration isolation. The vibrations of three actuation units must be independent, and the vibration transmission ratio of the actuated unit (OVS) to the non-actuated units must be kept below the empirically determined 20% threshold.

Experimental data acquisition and analysis

The robot operates on both glass and iron substrates, as described in the main text. We use a 30 frames per second (fps) with 1080 P resolution high-definition USB camera (Hikvision, China) connected to a tripod for overhead shooting to record the trajectory of the robot. The excitation signal required for the tethered test is provided by the switching power supply (QD-8D, China). The untethered locomotion test of the robot is a start-stop command sent by a mobile phone. All speed characteristic test videos are analyzed by self-developed software (Python), and the average speed is calculated by recording the relative positions of the robot at keyframes. The robot’s motion trajectory is tracked using a vision-based tracking algorithm. The error bars indicate the standard deviation for n = 3 sample measurements at each data point. Statistical analysis of the data is conducted using OriginPro 2024b (OriginLab, America). Note: Due to the destructive nature of certain experiments (e.g., drop and compression tests), two identical prototypes (Prototype #1 and #2) are employed for performance characterization. Unless otherwise specified, the data presented in the main text are obtained from Prototype #1. Both prototypes are fabricated using identical processes to ensure consistent mechanical behavior and reproducibility.

Experimental platform construction

The laser vibration measurement platform employs a scanning laser Doppler vibrometer (PSV-400-M2, Polytec, Germany) to measure the modal shapes and vibration velocity of the actuation unit. A power amplifier (ATA-4051, Aigtek Inc., China) is used to amplify the signals before applying them to the robot in the vibration characteristic test. We conduct a power consumption test on the tethered tripodal robot using a digital power meter (WT210, Yokogawa, Japan). The robot’s microscale step motion and the micrometer-scale vibration amplitude of the driving foot are measured using a laser displacement sensor (LK-H020, Keyence, Japan). External power to the actuation unit is supplied by an ultrasonic power supply (QD-8D, China) in the driving foot end displacement test. The high-magnification videos of the wafer and cell specimens in the robot’s microscopic observation demonstration are captured using an optical microscope (B013, Supereyes, China).
