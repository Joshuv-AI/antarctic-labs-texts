miRNA‐6236 Regulation of Postischemic Skeletal Muscle Angiogenesis
Authors: Arul M. Mani, Victor Lamin, Ronan C. Peach, Eli H. Friesen, Thomas Wong, Madhu V. Singh, Ayotunde O. Dokun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeripheral arterial disease affects >200 million people worldwide and is characterized by impaired blood flow to the lower extremities. There are no effective medical treatments available. Using the mouse hind‐limb ischemia model and miRNA sequencing, we identified a novel miRNA, miR‐6236, whose expression significantly elevated in ischemic mouse limbs compared with nonischemic limbs. The role of miR‐6236 in general or in postischemic angiogenesis is not known. Here we describe its role using in vivo and in vitro models of peripheral arterial disease.Methods and ResultsIn primary mouse and human endothelial cells, we studied the effect of simulated ischemia on miR‐6236 expression and assessed its role in cell viability, apoptosis, migration, and tube formation during ischemia. Furthermore, we developed miR‐6236 null mice and tested its role in postischemic perfusion recovery using the hind‐limb ischemia model. Lastly, using bioinformatics and gene expression analysis, we identified putative angiogenic miR‐6236 targets. In vitro simulated ischemia‐enhanced miR‐6236 expression in mouse and human endothelial cells, whereas its inhibition improved viability, migration, tube formation, and reduced apoptosis. In vivo ischemic mouse skeletal muscle tissue showed higher miR‐6236 expression compared with nonischemic muscles. Loss of miR‐6236 improved impaired postischemic perfusion recovery and poor angiogenesis associated with streptozotocin‐induced diabetes in mice. Six of the 8 miR‐6236 predicted angiogenic target mRNAs showed expression consistent with regulation by miR‐6236 in ischemic skeletal muscle.ConclusionsOur results show for the first time that miR‐6236 plays a key role in regulating postischemic perfusion recovery and angiogenesis.

Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) is a major circulatory disorder that causes reduced blood supply to the lower extremities due to obstruction of arteries as a result of atherosclerosis. 1 , 2 PAD afflicts >200 million people worldwide, 1 , 3 and it is the most common condition of arteriosclerosis after coronary heart disease and cerebrovascular diseases. 3 , 4 Ischemia‐related tissue damage and loss of perfusion are the major issues in PAD. 5 , 6 , 7 Following vessel occlusion, angiogenesis is one of the key processes involved in postischemic tissue adaptation to reestablish tissue perfusion. It is a complex process involving several pathways including metabolic, cell signaling, and inflammation. 8 , 9 , 10 Current medical management of PAD primarily focuses on reducing the risk of developing vascular atherosclerosis but does not address the critical issue of improving impaired perfusion. 11 , 12 Gene‐based therapy has emerged as a novel approach to promote angiogenesis in ischemic tissue to improve perfusion. 13 , 14 , 15 Previous studies reported that gene expression changes or gene variants have been shown to influence susceptibility to PAD. 7 , 16 , 17 , 18 There is strong evidence that perfusion of ischemic tissues can be improved through proangiogenic gene transfer 19 , 20 or manipulating regulatory mechanisms contributing to angiogenesis. 21 , 22 , 23

Recently, miRNAs have emerged as key posttranscriptional regulators of many genes. 21 , 23 , 24 miRNAs are small (19–23 nucleotide) RNA molecules that generally decrease gene expression by interfering with transcription or translation to maintain cellular homeostasis. 21 miRNAs regulate gene expression by their ability to bind 3′‐untranslated regions of target mRNA and prevent translation or cause mRNA degradation. 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 Moreover, a single miRNA can target multiple genes involved in the same pathway or biological process, making it a promising tool for modulating specific biological processes. 30

miRNAs have been shown to play a major role in the regulation of postischemic perfusion recovery in experimental models of PAD by our laboratory and others. 22 , 23 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 Prior studies identified miRNAs to study based on prediction algorithms indicating that these miRNAs target proteins involved in angiogenesis or postischemic recovery. 34 , 35 In the present study, we used an unbiased approach by performing miRNA sequencing of RNA isolated from ischemic and nonischemic hind limbs from mice following hind‐limb ischemia (HLI). We identified a novel miRNA, miRNA‐6236 (miR‐6236), whose expression was significantly elevated in the ischemic limbs when compared with nonischemic limbs. miR‐6236 is a 19‐nucleotide miRNA located on chromosome 9 in mice as an intergenic miRNA, but there is a complete lack of information about its human orthologue. Little is known about the role of miR‐6236 in general, and its role in postischemic angiogenesis has not been studied. Our literature search yielded only 2 publications related to miR‐6236, none of which was related to PAD, endothelial cells, skeletal muscles, or postischemic adaptation. The aim of the present study was to investigate whether miR‐6236 plays a role in postischemic skeletal muscle angiogenesis using in vitro and in vivo mouse models, followed by identification of its putative angiogenic targets. Moreover, we aimed to determine whether its orthologue is present in human cells and define its role in postischemic angiogenesis.

METHODS

The data and methods will be accessible to other researchers upon request for the purpose of reproducing the results or replicating the procedures.

Animals

All animal experiment protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Iowa, Iowa, in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Male and female C57BL/6 mice (catalog number 000664; Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, MA), and miR‐6236 knockout (miR‐6236KO) mice on the C57BL/6 background were used in some experiments. The miR‐6236 knockout mouse line was generated at the Genome Editing Facility, Carver College of Medicine, University of Iowa, Iowa, using a CRISPR/Cas system (Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats/CRISPR‐associated protein, a genome editing technology) by deleting 1463bp sequence (NC_000075.7:110109296–110 110 759) in chromosome 9 of C57BL/6 mice. Loss of miR‐6236 was confirmed by sequencing and genotyping. Homozygous miR‐6236−/− were obtained by breeding miR‐6236+/− heterozygous mice. It is well known that C57BL/6 mouse strain shows excellent perfusion recovery and angiogenesis following HLI. However, if made hyperglycemic, the strain showed impaired perfusion and angiogenesis. 6 , 22 , 32 , 36 Hence, to assess the role of miR‐6236 in the C57BL/6 background, it was necessary to induce hyperglycemia with streptozotocin. Thus, wild‐type and miR‐6236KO mice at 10 to 12 weeks were given 5 consecutive streptozotocin (catalog number 100557; M.P. Biomedicals, OH) daily injections (male mice: 50 mg/kg body weight; female mice: 65 mg/kg body weight) after the mice were fasted for 4 hours as previously described. 37 , 38 Fasting blood glucose and hemoglobin A1c levels were measured at 3 weeks and 1 month after the last streptozotocin injection. Mice with hyperglycemia (fasting glucose: ≥154 mg/dL and ≥8% hemoglobin A1c) were used for HLI surgery (Figure S1). All mice were kept on a normal chow diet (catalog number 7913; ENVIGO, Madison, WI) with free access to food and water.

HLI Surgery and Perfusion Recovery

HLI surgery was performed as described previously. 22 , 23 , 31 , 32 , 39 Briefly, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of xylazine (5 mg/kg), and ketamine (100 mg/kg) followed by surgical ligation and excision of the femoral artery of the left hind limb. The unoperated right hind limbs served as nonischemic control. Peripheral blood flow was measured by laser speckle contrast imaging using Perica PSI (Perimed, Las Vegas, NV). Blood perfusion images were obtained on days 0, 3, 7, 14, and 21 postsurgery. Perfusion recovery was expressed as the ratio of blood flow in the operated to the contralateral control limb as previously described. 31 All animal studies were blinded to investigators.

miRNA Sequencing

A complete gastrocnemius muscle from ischemic (day 3 post‐HLI) as well as nonischemic male C57BL/6J mice were snap‐frozen and sent to Qiagen Genomic Services (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) for custom miRNA sequencing. Briefly, total RNA was isolated using the miRNeasy kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. miRNA sequencing libraries were constructed from 100 ng of total RNA using the QIAseq miRNA library kit. The resulting library pool was then sequenced on a NextSeq500 sequencing instrument according to the manufacturer's instructions. Library preparation quality control (QC) was performed using TapeStation 4200 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). Raw data were demultiplexed, and FASTQ files for each sample were generated using the bcl2fastq software (Illumina). FASTQ data, a format for storing nucleotide sequences and quality scores were checked using the FastQC tool. Bowtie2 (2.2.2) was used for mapping the reads. Differential expression analysis was performed using the EdgeR statistical software package (Bioconductor; https://bioconductor.org/). For normalization, the trimmed mean of the M‐values (TMM) method, based on log‐fold and absolute gene‐wise changes in expression levels between samples (TMM normalization), was used.

Cell Culture and Simulated Ischemia

Mouse primary skeletal muscle microvascular endothelial cells (mSkMVECs; catalog number T4991; Applied Biological Material, Richmond, Canada), mouse primary skeletal muscle cells (catalog number C57‐6167; Cell Biologics, Chicago, IL), mouse primary aortic smooth muscle cells (catalog number C57‐6080; Cell Biologics), and pooled human aortic endothelial cells (HAECs; catalog number CC‐2535; Lonza, Morristown, NJ) were obtained from commercial sources as indicated. For the nonischemic conditions, cells were grown in their respective cell culture growth media (Endothelial Cells Growth Medium, catalog number 211–500; Cell Applications, San Diego, CA for mouse and human endothelial cells, and Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium, catalog number 11885–084, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) supplemented with a skeletal muscle medium kit (catalog number M1261‐Kit; Cell Biologics) for mouse skeletal muscle cells, and smooth muscle cell medium (catalog number M2268; Cell Biologics) supplemented with a smooth muscle cell medium kit (catalog number M2268‐Kit; Cell Biologics) for mouse aortic smooth muscle cells) at 37 °C in a humidified 95% air and 5% CO2 atmosphere. All experiments were done with cells grown between passages 4 and 8.

For simulated ischemia, cells were cultured in their respective starvation medium (endothelial starvation media, catalog number 209–250; Cell Applications), for mouse and human endothelial cells, skeletal muscle cell starvation media (catalog number 151S‐250; Cell Applications), for mouse skeletal muscle cells, smooth muscle cell medium (catalog number M2268, Cell Biologics) without growth factor kit for mouse smooth muscle cells), and hypoxia (2% oxygen, 5% CO2) using a hypoxic chamber at 37 °C for the indicated time period. Optimal dose (100 nM for mouse endothelial cells and 50 nM for human endothelial cells) of antagomir (mouse miR‐6236 antagomir, catalog number MH24534; human miR‐6236 antagomir, catalog number 4464086, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for inhibition of miR‐6236 was determined as described in Figure S2.

Cell Sorting

Endothelial cells were enriched from pooled skeletal muscles collected from the tibialis anterior, gastrocnemius muscle, and extensor digitorum longus from both the operated and unoperated hind limbs of each animal as previously described. 40 Briefly, muscle tissues were minced into small pieces and digested in 0.5% collagenase type II (Worthington Biochemical, Lakewood, NJ) for 45 minutes at 37 °C and another 45 minutes at 37 °C shaking (200 rpm). Digested tissue was passed through 70‐μm nylon filters (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ), and cells in the flow‐through were collected by centrifugation (1500g for 15 minutes). Cells were resuspended in fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (phosphate buffered saline, 0.001% NaN3, and 0.04% fetal bovine serum) and washed 3 times. Washed cells were then resuspended in 10 mL FACS buffer and counted using a hemocytometer.

To sort endothelial cells, 2×106 cells were blocked with cluster of differentiation 16/32 (CD16/CD32) monoclonal antibodies (1:400 dilution, catalog number 14–0161‐82; Invitrogen) for 15 minutes on ice and then stained with APC (Allophycocyanin)‐conjugated anti‐mouse cluster of differentiation 31 (CD31; 1:400 dilution, catalog number BDB551262; BD Biosciences) in the dark for 45 minutes. Cells were then washed 3 times with FACS buffer and resuspended in 1 mL FACS buffer. The stained cells were analyzed and sorted using a Cytek Aurora (Cytek Bioscience) fluorescence‐activated cell sorter. Cells were gated for single cells by forward and side‐scattered light, and live and dead cells were differentiated by Hoechst 33342 dye (catalog number H3570; Invitrogen) (Figure S3). Subsequently, CD31+ cells were sorted in a sterile tube for total RNA isolation and miR‐6236 expression (Figure 1F).

Identification of the miR‐6236 Human Orthologue

Using previously published human RNAseq data, 41 RNA immunoprecipitation‐based assay, 42 , 43 and stem‐loop reverse transcriptase–polymerase chain reaction, 44 we cloned, sequenced, and identified the human orthologue of miR‐6236 as described below. Human miR‐6236 is an 18‐nucleotide miRNA located on chromosome 21, and its predicted hairpin structure is depicted in Figure S4. To obtain an miRNA‐enriched RNA sample for use as a template for miR‐6236 amplification and cloning, we performed RNA immunoprecipitation assay. Briefly, human skeletal muscle cells were lysed in 500 μL RNA immunoprecipitation assay buffer, cell lysate was precleared with addition of 20 μL Protein A/G PLUS‐Agarose (catalog number SC‐2003; Santacruz, Dallas, TX), and incubated at 4 °C for 30 minutes, then centrifuged at 900g at 4 °C for 30 seconds to remove the pellet. Next, 10 μg of AGO‐2 (Argonaut‐2) antibody was added and incubated at 4 °C for an hour, followed by 20 μL Protein A/G PLUS‐Agarose (catalog number SC‐2003; Santacruz) and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Pellets were collected as above. The pellets were washed with RNA immunoprecipitation assay buffer 4 times at 4 °C, and miRNA enriched RNA was extracted from collected pellets using an miRNeasy mini kit (catalog number 2172004; Qiagen). Isolated RNA was used for amplifying human miR‐6236 by stem‐loop reverse transcriptase–polymerase chain reaction as follows. Briefly, the amount of miRNA associated with AGO‐2 was reverse transcribed by using TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcriptase kit (catalog number 4366597; Thermo Fisher Scientific) using human miR‐6236 specific primer. cDNA was used for miR‐6236 amplification using human miR‐6236‐specific primer and stem‐loop reverse transcription primer attached to MIuI (Micrococcus luteus I; catalog number R0198S; New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) and Xho (Xanthomonas holcicola I; catalog number R0146; New England Biolabs) restriction sites, respectively. The amplified polymerase chain reaction product was cloned into pGL3 basic vector and was custom sequenced to verify the presence of human miR‐6236.

TaqMan Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was isolated from the cells or muscle tissue using miRNeasy mini kit (catalog number 2172004; Qiagen). RNA concentrations were measured by Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of RNA samples (10 ng) were reverse transcribed by TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcriptase kit (catalog number 4366597; Thermo Fisher Scientific) using miR‐6236‐specific primer. cDNA was used to determine expression of miR‐6236 by TaqMan quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) (TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix, catalog number 4444554; Thermo Fisher Scientific) using miR‐6236‐specific primers (mouse miR‐6236, catalog number 474656; human miR‐6236, catalog number CT2W74X; miR‐10a, catalog number 000387; Thermo Fisher Scientific in a Quant Studio 3 thermocycler; Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific). miR‐10a expression was measured as reference miRNA. Relative change in miR‐6236 expression was determined by the ∆∆Ct method 22 , 32 , 39 and presented as fold change versus control.

Cell Transfection

Endothelial cells plated in 96‐well plates were transfected with either control oligos (Negative Control; catalog number 4464077; Thermo Fisher Scientific), miR‐6236 antagomir to mouse miR‐6236 (catalog number MH24534; Thermo Fisher Scientific), or human miR‐6236 (catalog number 4464086; Thermo Fisher Scientific) using Lipofectamine 3000 (catalog number L3000‐015; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Twenty‐four hours after transfection, cells were used for further experiments as needed.

Cell Viability

Cell viability was measured as described previously with slight modifications. 33 mSkMVECs or HAECs plated in 96‐well plates were either untransfected or transfected with control oligos or miR‐6236 antagomir to mouse or human miR‐6236 using Lipofectamine 3000. Twenty‐four hours after seeding or transfection, cells were exposed to either normoxia or simulated ischemia for indicated time periods. At the end of incubation, media were carefully replaced with MTT reagent (3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyltetraz‐olium bromide (MTT) reagent; catalog number K299‐1000; BioVision, Milpitas, CA) and incubated at 37 °C for 3 hours. After 3 hours, MTT solvent was added to each well and incubated for 15 minutes at room temperature with shaking. Absorbance was measured at 590 nm, as the measure of viable cells, and the results were presented as percent cell viability when compared with normoxia controls.

Cell Apoptosis

Cell apoptosis was measured as described previously with slight modifications. 33 mSkMVECs or HAECs plated in 96‐well plates were transfected with control oligos or miR‐6236 antagomir to mouse or human miR‐6236 using Lipofectamine 3000. Twenty‐four hours after transfection, cells were exposed to either normoxia or simulated ischemia for indicated time periods. At the end of incubation, apoptosis in cells was determined using a colorimetric TUNEL assay kit (Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling assay kit; catalog number 4822‐96‐K; TiterTACS apoptosis detection kit, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN), and the results were presented as percent cell apoptosis when compared with normoxia controls.

Cell Migration

Cell migration was measured as described previously with slight modifications. 45 mSkMVECs or HAECs plated in 6‐well plates were transfected either with control oligos or miR‐6236 antagomir to mouse or human miR‐6236 using Lipofectamine 3000. Twenty‐four hours after transfection, a wound was created by scraping/scoring the cell layer with a 10‐μL pipette tip, and cells were exposed to either normoxia or simulated ischemia in the presence of 5 mM hydroxyurea for 24 hours. Cell migration was recorded by photomicrograph (MD35A; AMScope, Irvine, CA), and the endothelial cell migration expressed as percentage of wound closure (total scraped area at 0 hours–unoccupied scraped area by cells at 24 hours per total scraped area at 0 hours×100) was calculated.

Tube Formation Assay

Tube formation assay was performed as described previously. 45 mSkMVECs or HAECs plated in 6‐well plates were transfected either with control oligos or miR‐6236 antagomir to mouse or human miR‐6236 using Lipofectamine 3000. After 24 hours of transfection, cells were trypsinized, and an equal number of cells were plated in enriched Matrigel‐coated 96‐well plates (30,00 cells per well) (catalog number 354234; Corning, Glendale, AZ) followed by exposure to either normoxia or simulated ischemia. After 6 hours, endothelial cell tube formation was digitally recorded (MD35A; AMScope), and images were analyzed by Wim Tube image analysis (Wimasis Image Analysis, Cordoba, Spain). Results were presented as the average value of the total number of tubes, total tube length, number of branching points, and number of loops in each experimental group.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry for quantification of CD31+ myofibers was performed as follows. Gastrocnemius muscle tissue samples were fixed in 10% formalin as described earlier. 22 , 32 , 39 , 46 Briefly, 10‐μM thick sections of gastrocnemius muscle was incubated with rabbit anti‐mouse CD31 antibody (1 in 400 dilutions; catalog number ab28364; Abcam) for 1 hour at room temperature. Staining was performed using the Dako EnVision+System‐HRP DAB (Agilent) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Negative and positive control slides were included in each set of staining. Slides were scanned at ×20 magnification under a fluorescence microscope (BX61; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). CD31‐stained cells and muscle fibers in the entire sections were counted, and capillary density was expressed as CD31+/myofiber.

Prediction of Functional miR‐6236 Targets

Targetscan, 47 miR‐Walk, 48 and miR‐Gate 49 target prediction algorithms were used to identify potential miR‐6236 biological targets. Among the predicted targets, those with known functions related to angiogenesis were regarded as effective miR‐6236 targets.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as mean±SEM. The exact test was implemented using edgeR package to adjust the P value in miRNA sequencing data. Statistical comparison between groups was analyzed by independent sample t tests and 1‐way ANOVA using the Tukey post hoc test by GraphPad Prism, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. For the perfusion recovery experiment, we used a 2‐way ANOVA mixed‐effects model, followed by post hoc Tukey test by GraphPad Prism. Data analyzed were within 1 standard deviation (68% coverage).

RESULTS

Increased miR‐6236 Expression in Mouse Postischemic Skeletal Muscle

We determined the miRNAs affected by ischemia in gastrocnemius muscle day 3 postischemic (HLI) and nonischemic hind limb (adjusted P<0.01; n=6; 2‐fold cutoff) of male mice. Compared with the nonischemic control limbs, the expression of 77 miRNAs was upregulated, whereas the expression of 40 miRNAs was downregulated in ischemic hind limbs. Among these miRNAs, miR‐6236 was significantly upregulated, suggesting a possible role in postischemic adaption (Figure 1A). We further validated the increase in the expression of miR‐6236 by TaqMan qPCR using the same samples of day 3 postischemic (HLI) and nonischemic hind limb. Our results confirmed that compared with the control limbs, the expression of miR‐6236 is increased in ischemic limbs (Figure 1B; ischemic 2.7±0.47 versus nonischemic 1.0±0.14; n=4; P<0.05).

Increased miR‐6236 Expression in Postischemic mSkMVECs, Mouse Skeletal Muscle Cells, and Mouse Aortic Smooth Muscle Cells

Although skeletal muscle tissue consists of multiple cell types, the endothelial cells, skeletal muscle cells, and smooth muscle cells are major cell types affected during ischemic conditions. 50 , 51 , 52 We tested which of these cells have increased expression of miR‐6236 under ischemic conditions. We exposed the above mouse cells to simulated ischemia and measured the levels of miR‐6236 expression by TaqMan qPCR. Our results show that expression of miR‐6236 increased in cultured endothelial cells under simulated ischemia in a time‐dependent manner reaching up to >4.6‐fold greater than the nonischemic control (Figure 1C). In skeletal muscle cells, the expression of miR‐6236 increased transiently peaking (6.5‐fold) at 12 hours of ischemia and returning to basal levels by 72 hours of ischemia (Figure 1D). Similarly, in smooth muscle cells, the increase in expression of miR‐6236 was robust but transient; the expression increased almost 10‐fold at 12 hours after ischemia, tapering down to 2.6‐fold after 72 hours of ischemia (Figure 1E). Thus, ischemia induced expression of miR‐6236 in the 3 major cell types present in an ischemic mouse limb.

Inhibition of miR‐6236 by Antagomir Improves Viability and Reduces Apoptosis in Ischemic mSkMVECs

Angiogenesis is a critical component of postischemic recovery following HLI, and endothelial cells play a key role. 53 We therefore investigated the role of miR‐6236 in ischemic mouse endothelial cells. First, we tested the levels of miR‐6236 in endothelial cells isolated from mouse ischemic skeletal muscle tissue compared with that in nonischemic skeletal muscle, and our results showed that expression of miR‐6236 is increased in endothelial cells isolated from ischemic skeletal muscles when compared with control nonischemic skeletal muscles (Figure 1F; ischemic 2.76±0.10 versus nonischemic 1.0±0.0.08; n=5; P<0.05). Endothelial cell survival is thought to be an essential mechanism during angiogenesis. 54 Next, we assessed viability of mouse endothelial cells in ischemic conditions in vitro and found this predictably and steadily decreased during 72 hours of simulated ischemia (Figure 2A), with about 50% viability noted at 24 hours (Figure 2B; control oligos+simulated ischemia, 54.23±5.4 versus control oligos+normoxia, 100±3.8; n=4; P<0.05). During this time, the expression of miR‐6236 increased under ischemic conditions (Figure 1D; 72 hours simulated ischemia, 4.6±0.53; 24 hours simulated ischemia, 3.3±0.99; normoxia, 1.0±0.15; n=3; P<0.05). We tested the effect of inhibiting miR‐6236 by antagomir on viability of endothelial cells under ischemia. Endothelial cells transfected with miR‐6236‐antagomir showed a significant improvement in cell viability in ischemia compared with control oligos‐treated cells (Figure 2B; miR‐6236 antagomir+simulated ischemia, 83.0±6.0 versus control oligos+simulated ischemia, 54.23±5.4; n=4; P<0.05), but treatment did not completely restore viability to the levels in normoxia (Figure 2B; miR‐6236 antagomir+simulated ischemia, 83.0±6.0 versus control oligos+normoxia, 99.9±3.8; n=4). Cell viability was unaffected in antagomir‐treated normoxic endothelial cells (Figure 2B; miR‐6236 antagomir+normoxia, 108.2±5.7 versus control oligos+normoxia, 100±3.8; n=4). Next, we tested the effect of inhibiting miR‐6236 by antagomir on apoptosis of endothelial cells under ischemia. Twenty‐four hours of simulated ischemia increased endothelial cell apoptosis when compared with normoxic cells (Figure 2C; control oligos+simulated ischemia, 157.4±6.2 versus control oligos+normoxia, 100±2.47; n=4; P<0.05). Endothelial cells transfected with miR‐6236‐antagomir showed a significant reduction of cell apoptosis compared with control oligos‐treated cells during ischemia (Figure 2C; miR‐6236 antagomir+simulated ischemia, 101.21±7.28 versus control oligos+simulated ischemia, 157.4±6.2; n=4; P<0.05). Cell apoptosis was unaffected in antagomir‐treated normoxic endothelial cells (Figure 2B; miR‐6236 antagomir+normoxia, 101.2±4.8 versus control oligos+normoxia, 100±2.47; n=4). These results suggest that loss of miR‐6236 is protective in ischemia‐mediated endothelial cell apoptosis.

Inhibition of miR‐6236 by Antagomir Improves Wound Healing in Ischemic mSkMVECs

Migration of endothelial cells toward damaged or ischemic tissue is a measure of their potential for angiogenesis 55 that can be tested in cultured cells by migration assay. We tested the effect of inhibiting miR‐6236 on endothelial cell migration in ischemic culture conditions (Figure 2D and 2E). Ischemia resulted in a significant decrease in mSkMVEC migration into the wound (scraped) area when compared with cells under normoxic condition (control oligos+simulated ischemia, 42.7±2.7 versus control oligos+normoxia, 72.02±2.7; n=6; P<0.05). Thus, inhibiting miR‐6236 improves mSkMVEC migration in ischemia. Parallel cultures of endothelial cells were grown separately during this experiment to assess the miR‐6236 antagomir efficiency and to determine if simulated ischemia enhanced miR‐6236 expression, whereas miR‐6236 antagomir significantly inhibited the simulated ischemia‐induced miR‐6236 expression (Figure S5A).

Loss of miR‐6236 Improves Perfusion Recovery and Angiogenesis in Streptozotocin‐ Induced Hyperglycemic Mice

To better understand the in vivo role of miR‐6236 in postischemic angiogenesis and perfusion recovery, we generated the miR‐6236KO mouse line in the C57BL/6 background as described in the Methods. We confirmed loss of miR‐6236 expression by evaluating its expression in several tissues in wild‐type and miR‐6236KO mice (Figure 4B). Loss of miR6236 expression was also confirmed in ischemic skeletal muscles of miR‐6236KO mice (Figure 4C). It is well known that the C57BL/6 mouse strain shows excellent perfusion recovery and angiogenesis following HLI. However, if made hyperglycemic, the strain shows impaired perfusion recovery and angiogenesis. Hence, to assess the role of miR‐6236 in the C57BL/6 background, we induced hyperglycemia with streptozotocin. We then evaluated the physiological effect of miR‐6236KO in ischemic skeletal muscles of mice treated with streptozotocin. Consistent with prior publications, 6 , 22 , 32 , 36 wild‐type C57BL/6 mice treated with streptozotocin show poor perfusion recovery (approximately 60% at day 21, Figure 4D). However, loss of miR‐6236 (miR‐6236KO) significantly improved perfusion recovery in streptozotocin‐induced hyperglycemic mice (Figure 4D and 4E; day 21 perfusion; miR‐6236KO+streptozotocin+HLI, 76.9±3.35 versus wild‐type+streptozotocin+HLI, 57.32±2.67; n=9; P<0.05). Additionally, immunohistochemistry for CD31+ cells to quantify capillary density, a measure of angiogenesis in the ischemic limbs, showed higher capillary density (capillaries/myofiber) in the ischemic gastrocnemius muscles of streptozotocin‐treated miR‐6236KO mice when compared with wild‐type C57BL/6 mice treated with streptozotocin (Figure 4F and 4G; miR‐6236KO+streptozotocin+HLI, 1.13±0.06 versus wild‐type+streptozotocin+HLI, 0.37±0.08; n=5; P<0.05). Thus, inhibiting miR‐6236 improves perfusion recovery and angiogenesis in postischemic mouse hind limbs.

Identification of Putative miR‐6236 Targets

We used a bioinformatics approach to identify genes whose expression is potentially regulated by mouse miR‐6236. We performed target prediction analyses using major public databases. Queries at miR‐Walk, miR‐Gate, and Targetscan yielded 16 177, 1582, and 99 putative targets, respectively. A Venn diagram analysis revealed 37 target genes that were common to all 3 predictions, placing higher confidence that these genes may be targets of mouse miR‐6236 (Figure S6 and Table S1). Among these, 8 genes (22%) were reported to be associated with angiogenesis (Table S2). Confirmation of 8 genes by SYBR green qPCR using specific gene primers (Table S3) showed that mRNA expression of KCTD10 (potassium channel tetramerization domain containing 2 protein; Figure S7A; ischemic 0.05±0.03 versus nonischemic 1.08±0.24; n=4; P<0.05), PDGFA (platelet‐derived growth factor α polypeptide; Figure S7B; ischemic 0.004±0.003 versus nonischemic 1.10±0.30; n=4; P<0.05), PDXP (pyridoxal [pyridoxine, vitamin B6] phosphatase; Figure S7C; ischemic 0.21±0.03 versus nonischemic 1.08±0.24; n=4; P<0.05), WWOX (WW domain containing oxidoreductase; Figure S7D; ischemic 0.03±0.01 versus nonischemic 1.03±0.13; n=4; P<0.05), EPAS1 (endothelial PAS domain protein 1; Figure S7E; ischemic 0.005±0.004 versus nonischemic 1.05±0.19; n=4; P<0.05), PAK2 (p21 protein [Cdc42/Rac]‐activated kinase 2; Figure S7F; ischemic 0.003±0.002 versus nonischemic 1.56±0.92; n=3–4; P<0.05) were reduced in ischemic tissue samples when compared to nonischemic tissue. FOXC2 (Forkhead box C2) levels were not affected in ischemic tissue when compared with nonischemic tissue (Figure S7G; ischemic 1.36±0.31 versus nonischemic 1.02±0.12; n=4; P<0.35). The level of S1PR5 (sphingosine‐1‐phosphate receptor 5) was found to be low in both groups of samples, as shown by their higher Ct (Cycle threshold) values by SYBR green qPCR. Therefore, S1PR5 is considered not expressed in mouse skeletal muscle tissue.

DISCUSSION

The pathophysiology of PAD is characterized by marked changes in gene expression and reduced angiogenesis in the ischemic limb. 57 , 58 , 59 Although miRNAs are now recognized as key posttranslational regulators of gene expression, there is limited information on the role of miRNAs in the pathology of PAD. 60 , 61 We have previously investigated the role of miRNAs in postischemic adaption by comparing miRNA expression profile in ischemic tissues from different mouse strains. 33 In this study we performed miRNA sequencing in ischemic and nonischemic gastrocnemius muscle. Our results show 77 miRNAs were upregulated, whereas the expression of 40 miRNAs were downregulated in ischemic hind limbs (Figure 1A). Among these miRNAs, miR‐6236 was chosen for further investigation due to its novelty. Our review of the literature showed only 2 publications on miR‐6236 and its nondefined role in PAD, angiogenesis, or postischemic adaption. 62 , 63 We validated the increased expression of miR‐6236 in ischemic mouse hind limbs by TaqMan qPCR and identified for the first time the human orthologue. Moreover, we defined its role in mouse and human endothelial cells as an inhibitor of different aspects of the angiogenic process in vitro. In vivo, we showed loss of miR‐6236 improved postischemic perfusion recovery. Additionally, we identified potential miR‐6236 targets by target prediction algorithms, and found 22% of identified targets were related to angiogenesis.

Although there are exceptions, most miRNAs bind to noncoding regions of their target mRNA to regulate translation resulting in decreased expression of the target protein. 64 In the present study, higher levels of miR‐6236 have been observed in ischemic skeletal muscle tissue and in CD31+ cells isolated from ischemic skeletal muscle tissue compared with nonischemic tissue or CD31+ cells from nonischemic skeletal muscles. Moreover, similar findings were observed in ischemic skeletal muscle microvascular endothelial cells, ischemic skeletal muscle cells, and ischemic vascular smooth muscle cells compared with nonischemic controls. However, our time course experiment showed the increased expression of miR‐6236 in ischemic skeletal muscle cells and vascular smooth muscle cells was transient, with peak expression occurring within 6 to 12 hours of exposure to simulated ischemia. This suggests the function of miR‐6236 in these different cell types may be different than its function in endothelial cells. This concept is consistent with previously described work that showed miRNA function can depend on cell type and or cellular compartment. This is exemplified in the studies of the miR‐17‐92 cluster, which was shown to have proangiogenic function in tumor cells 65 but antiangiogenic functions in endothelial cells. 66 The role of miR‐6236 in other cell types have not been explored, and such future studies will provide novel insight into the function of this miRNA.

Other antiangiogenic miRNAs have been previously described. 23 , 31 miR‐29a is expressed in endothelial cells, and its inhibition improved tube formation in human and mouse endothelial cells, respectively. 23 , 31 Moreover, miR‐15a/16 is expressed in endothelial progenitor cells. Elevated levels of miR‐15a/16 in proangiogenic cells of humans was associated with a severe form of PAD, 67 and its inhibition improved perfusion recovery and angiogenesis. 67 , 68 In contrast, miR‐93 and miR‐375 are examples of proangiogenic endothelial expressed miRNAs, because their overexpression attenuates ischemia‐induced apoptosis and enhanced proliferation and tube formation in human or mouse endothelial cells. 33 , 69

In our in vivo studies, we generated mice lacking miR‐6236 in the C57BL/6 background. It is well known that the C57BL/6 mice can restore perfusion recovery effectively and rapidly following HLI. 18 , 39 Hence, the effect of the loss of an antiangiogenic miRNA on perfusion recovery is not likely to be discernable in this strain. However, when rendered hyperglycemic, this strain has been shown to have impaired perfusion recovery following HLI 6 , 22 , 32 , 36 ; hence, loss of an antiangiogenic miRNA may be discernable in this setting. We therefore induced hyperglycemia in the miR‐6236 knockout mice using streptozotocin and assessed perfusion recovery and capillary density following HLI (Figure 4D and 4G). Consistent with the results from our in vitro studies, streptozotocin‐treated miR‐6236KO mice had improved perfusion recovery and increased capillary density when compared with wild‐type mice treated with streptozotocin (Figure 4D and 4G). Collectively, the above data indicate that miR‐6236 inhibition improves postischemic angiogenesis in vitro and in vivo. It is important to note that a single miRNA can regulate multiple genes, which are often genes that function within the same regulatory pathway. 70 Therefore, the loss of miR‐6236 in the present study could impact expression of multiple genes in the angiogenic pathway.

Our findings indicate that the expression levels of 6 out of 8 target mRNAs were reduced in ischemic tissue compared with nonischemic tissue. One of the miR‐6236 targets, KCTD10, a member of the KCTD (potassium channel tetramerization domain) protein family, plays a significant role in angiogenesis during early embryonic development. Homozygous KCTD10−/− mice exhibited delayed growth and died early in development, showing substantial defects in both angiogenesis and heart development. 71 Another target, PDGFA, is involved in the regulation of endothelial cell proliferation and migration, which are critical for angiogenesis and vascular repair. 72 Additionally, EPAS1 is known to promote angiogenesis by transactivating VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor) and its receptor, Flt‐1 (Fms‐like tyrosine kinase 1). It has been reported that EPAS1 plays a crucial role in hypoxic conditions, enhancing the expression of VEGF, Flt‐1, Flk‐1 (fetal liver kinase 1, a VEGF receptor 2) and Tie2 (tyrosine kinase with immunoglobulin‐like and endothelial growth factor‐like domains 2), which are essential for mature angiogenesis. 73 Furthermore, studies have shown PAK2 plays a crucial role in angiogenesis, regulating endothelial cell development and function through the Bmk1/Erk5 pathway (Big mitogen‐activated protein kinase 1/Extracellular regulated kinase 5 pathway). It promotes endothelial cell survival, proliferation, and migration. Depletion of PAK2 leads to apoptosis and angiogenesis defects, underscoring its importance in vascular development and stability. 74 Moreover, WWOX is an important tumor suppressor. It has been reported that reduced WWOX levels lead to increased angiogenesis by enhancing microvessel density and endothelial cell tube formation. In ischemic legs, the reduction of WWOX impairs its regulatory role, leading to uncontrolled or excessive angiogenesis, which can hinder proper tissue repair and vascular stability. 75 Finally, low levels of PDXP (pyridoxal phosphatase) in ischemic tissue has been attributed to cellular stress and metabolic disruption caused by reduced oxygen and nutrient supply. This leads to decreased conversion of pyridoxal to PLP (pyridoxal 5′‐phosphate), impairing angiogenesis by reducing endothelial cell proliferation and interfering with DNA replication processes. Consequently, the formation of new blood vessels is hindered, highlighting the critical role of PDXP in tissue repair and regeneration. 76 Taken together, miR‐6236 may regulate angiogenesis through multiple targets. The specific targets it regulates may be context and tissue dependent. These studies provide a foundation for future studies to identify the targets of miR‐6236 and examine its potential as a therapeutic target in humans with PAD.
