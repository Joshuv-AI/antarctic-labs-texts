Variable clinical features and delayed diagnosis in six Chinese patients with anti-interferon-gamma autoantibodies: a retrospective analysis in a university hospital in China
Authors: Junwu Zhang, Jinyao Ni, Wanzhong Kong, Jinlin Liu, Yanxia Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnti-interferon (IFN)-gamma (γ) autoantibody positivity (AIGA) is a rare cause of adult-onset immunodeficiency, leading to severe disseminated opportunistic infections with varying outcomes. Due to its rarity, the diagnosis of AIGA is often missed or delayed.MethodsWe used the hospital information system of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University to retrospectively analyze the data of patients with AIGA between January 2018 and March 2024. Clinical, laboratory, and outcome data were collected and analyzed.ResultsSix patients with AIGA were included in this study. A retrospective review of the clinical characteristics and laboratory results showed that all patients had recurrent opportunistic infections, and five patients had hypergammaglobulinemia before receiving the diagnosis of AIGA. All six patients presented with pneumonia and recurrent cough; two patients presented with recurrent skin abscesses, two presented with recurrent penile ulcers, and two presented with severe bone destruction. Of these, five patients were infected with Talaromyces marneffei (T. marneffei). After being diagnosed with AIGA, all six patients received routine anti-infection therapy. As the disease progressed, all patients presented with recurrent infections. Notably, five patients exhibited elevated serum immunoglobulin G (IgG) (median 25.06 g/L; interquartile range, 17.73–38.22) during a previous admission. One succumbed to respiratory failure at follow-up, while five survived.ConclusionThe diagnosis of AIGA is often delayed and should be considered as a differential diagnosis in patients with recurrent opportunistic infections and hypergammaglobulinemia.Keywords: Acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, Clinical features, Laboratory results, Anti-interferon- gamma autoantibodies

Introduction

Adult-onset immunodeficiency due to anti-interferon (IFN)-gamma (γ) autoantibodies (anti-IFN-γ Abs) is a distinct disorder that has recently emerged in Southeast Asia [1]. Anti-IFN-γ Abs positivity (AIGA) usually occurs in previously healthy individuals and presents as a chronic, recurrent, and difficult-to-control infection that can be effectively treated with aggressive antimicrobial therapy [2]. Neutralizing anti-IFN-γ Abs are responsible for patients’ increased susceptibility to nontuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infections and intracellular pathogens [1, 3]. The lymph nodes are the most commonly involved organs, followed by the skin. Reactive conditions coincide with direct infections and frequently present as skin manifestations such as neutrophilic dermatoses [2, 4]. However, early diagnosis of the disease is difficult because of protean manifestations in immunocompetent hosts and the absence of standard laboratory assays [2, 5]. Patients may require long-term antimycobacterial therapy because of chronic infections and high recurrence rates, even after remission.

Importantly, the diagnosis of AIGA is often delayed due to its rarity, lack of awareness, and nonspecific clinical presentation, and undiagnosed patients may develop severe opportunistic infections with unsatisfactory control, particularly in those patients who have recurrent infections [6, 7]. Therefore, clinicians should strive to diagnose this rare disease as early as possible, followed by close patient monitoring and measures to regulate the immune system. Previous research on AIGA is mainly case reports, and large-scale studies on the clinical manifestations and laboratory results of this rare disorder are lacking [5, 8]. Thus, we designed this study to investigate the clinical manifestations and laboratory results of patients with AIGA in a large university hospital in China, with the aim of identifying distinguishable clinical manifestations or laboratory parameters to diagnose this rare disease as early as possible.

Materials and methods

Patient information

Patients with positive anti-IFN-γ Abs test results recorded in the hospital information system at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University of Wenzhou, China, between January 2018 and March 2024 were included. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) anti-IFN-γ Abs positivity; (2) clinical features of immunodeficiency; (3) age: ≥18 years. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) primary/secondary immunodeficiency; (2) malignancy; (3) immunosuppressive therapy history. Six patients were included in this study based on the Hospital Information Management System database. In 2023, the resident population of the Wenzhou district in the Zhejiang Province was 9.761 million. Only a handful of similar cases have been reported in the region, underscoring the rarity of this disorder. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University with the number KY2023-R223.

Study design and data collection

The concentrations of immunoglobulin G (IgG) were measured using nephelometry by Siemens BN II (reference values:7.0–16.0 g/L) or Beckman Coulter AU5800 (reference values: 8.6–17.4 g/L). The lymphocyte subsets investigated included B cells (reference values: 5–18%), CD3+ T cells (reference values: 60–79%), CD4+ T cells (reference values: 34–52%), and CD8+ T cells (reference values: 21–39%). All lymphocyte subsets were detected using flow cytometry, and these data were extracted from the hospital information system. The laboratory was certified using ISO15189 standards. The clinical manifestations and other laboratory results of the six patients were reviewed in detail. Survival data for all patients were confirmed by telephone contact in August 2024.

Detection of AIGA

An indirect ELISA was employed to detect anti-IFN-γ Abs in pre-coated microplates. The assay involved incubating serially diluted serum samples, followed by a washing step and the addition of an enzyme-conjugated secondary antibody. The presence of autoantibodies was revealed by a chromogenic reaction, with an optical density (OD) value > 0.5 at a 1:100 dilution set as the positive threshold. Antibody titers were further stratified according to reactivity at 1:500 and 1:2500 dilutions.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 5. Continuous data are shown as median (interquartile range, IQR).

Results

All six patients with AIGA had not received a timely diagnosis in the tertiary university hospital

Due to the frequent missed/delayed diagnosis of AIGA, we used an electronic medical record system to retrospectively screen patients diagnosed with AIGA between January 2018 and March 2024 in a tertiary university hospital.

Six patients with AIGA were included in the study. A retrospective review of the clinical characteristics and laboratory results showed that all patients had recurrent opportunistic infections, and five patients presented with hypergammaglobulinemia before being diagnosed with AIGA. All six patients presented with pneumonia and recurrent cough; two patients presented with recurrent skin abscesses, two presented with recurrent penile ulcers, and two presented with severe bone destruction. Five patients were infected with Talaromyces marneffei (T. marneffei) (Table 1). As the disease progressed, the clinical symptoms of all patients worsened, leading to a final diagnosis of AIGA. Importantly, all six patients were immunocompetent before positive anti-IFN-γ Abs results were detected. Moreover, none of the six patients received a timely diagnosis of AIGA in this tertiary university hospital setting; time to diagnosis ranged from 2 months to 4 years.

All six patients with AIGA had severe opportunistic infections with unsatisfactory control

All six patients with AIGA developed severe opportunistic infections, including disseminated T. marneffei, disseminated NTM, and various bacterial infections. All patients experienced unsatisfactory infection control. Additionally, the six patients did not have underlying respiratory diseases, more frequently exhibited dissemination of systemic infections with severe pleural effusion, and were more likely to have other intracellular pathogen infections. With AIGA progressed, the clinical symptoms worsened in two patients. As of the follow-up phone calls in August 2024, one patient had succumbed to respiratory failure and the other five survivors remained alive (Table 1).

Case 1

A 60-year-old Chinese woman with no pertinent medical history was admitted to our hospital in August 2016 with a 3-month history of left chest wall redness and swelling, and intermittent fever for 2 years. During hospitalization, a left chest fluid culture confirmed disseminated T. marneffei infection. The patient was successfully treated with antifungal therapy. On a second admission in July 2017, a right lung tissue biopsy culture results revealed NTM. The patient was administered anti-mycobacterial therapy; however, treatment was discontinued after 1 month due to side effects. In May 2018, the patient presented with fever, cough, excessive sputum, and a hand abscess, and was prescribed antibiotics. High-levels of anti-IFN-γ Abs were detected in the patient’s serum, leading to a confirmed diagnosis of AIGA. Following the administration of anti-nontuberculous mycobacterial therapy, the symptoms and pulmonary infiltration gradually improved. The joint destruction and lymphadenitis persisted. Subsequent to the confirmed AIGA diagnosis, the patient developed a hip skin abscess, left clavicular lymph node enlargement, recurrent fever, and lower back pain. Documented radiological results revealed the destruction of multiple bones, including the left rib, left clavicle, sternum, bilateral scapulae, and lumbar spine. In addition, the abscess and alveolar lavage fluid cultures were positive for T. marneffei, Mycobacteria intracellular, Enterococcus raffinosus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Antibiotics, glucocorticoids, and cyclophosphamide immunotherapy were administered from August 2019 to June 2020. As of the follow-up phone call, the patient had succumbed to respiratory failure in October 2023.

Case 2

A 54-year-old man was admitted to our hospital in May 2021 presenting with fever and chills. Physical examination revealed swollen lymph nodes and penile ulcers. Computed tomography (CT) showed bilateral lung infections. The patient’s symptoms significantly improved with antibiotics, glucocorticoids, and he was discharged. The patient was readmitted several times because of a recurrent fever. Positron emission tomography (PET)/CT showed multiple lymphadenopathies in the supraclavicular area, right upper chest wall intermuscular area, mediastinal areas, right hilar lung, hepatic hilar area, retroperitoneal area, left supraclavicular area, and right inguinal lymph node area. A lymph node biopsy revealed granulomatous inflammation. Moreover, next-generation sequencing (NGS) of the lymph nodes revealed Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex and Epstein-Barr (EB) virus. The alveolar lavage culture was positive for T. marneffei. Ultimately, the patient was diagnosed with AIGA in August 2021. However, recurrent fever, cough, and lower back pain due to vertebral bone infection persisted. The patient received symptomatic anti-infection treatment, regularly received rituximab, and has survived as of the follow-up phone call.

Case 3

A 55-year-old Chinese woman with no pertinent medical history was admitted to our hospital in September 2021 with a 3-month history of intermittent cough and a 3-day fever. The patient was diagnosed with syphilis which resolved satisfactorily (as seen from pericardial fluid cultures) with antisyphilis therapy, leading to discharge. The patient was readmitted to the hospital with fever, diarrhea, and a rib mass. The rib mass exhibited myofibromatous hyperplasia and inflammatory granulation tissue formation; PET/CT revealed skull, cervical spine, sternum, ribs, thoracic spine, and sacral infections. Blood, sputum, and fecal cultures and NGS of the rib abscess revealed Salmonella typhimurium, human herpes virus, Mycoplasma hominis, T. marneffei, multiple other infections, thrombocytopenia, and anemia. Immunosuppressants, antibiotics, and corticosteroids were administered, and the symptoms improved. Ultimately, the patient was diagnosed with AIGA in February 2022. Recurrent cough, fever, bloodstream and urinary system infections, and face, sacrococcygeal and pelvic abscesses persisted as revealed during readmissions. The patient was administered antibiotics and corticosteroids, supplemented with immunomodulatory treatment (rituximab and intravenous immunoglobulin) and has survived as of the follow-up phone call.

Case 4

The patient was a 36-year-old woman with a history of hepatitis who was admitted to the hospital in April 2022 with a 1-month history of cough and malaise. Physical examination revealed the presence of multiple mouth ulcers. Radiological examination revealed multiple lymphadenopathies, including bilateral cervical lymph nodes, multiple mediastinal lymph nodes, and hepatogastric and splenic lymph node enlargement. The cervical lymph nodes showed benign hyperplasia of the lymphatic tissue. Recurrent cough and malaise did not improve despite various treatments and subsequent negative blood and bone marrow culture results. In June 2022, acid-fast bacilli were detected in multiple mediastinal lymph nodes. An acid-fast smear and NGS of the lymph node biopsy specimen revealed Mycobacterium colombiense. Ultimately, the patient was diagnosed with AIGA syndrome. The patient required repeated hospitalizations for recurrent cough and fever for which antibiotics were administered. The patient stabilized and as of the follow-up telephone call remains alive.

Case 5

A 56-year-old man was previously admitted to the hospital with gastric and colon polyps that were endoscopically removed in September 2019. In September 2022, the patient was hospitalized with complaints of abdominal pain and diarrhea. CT revealed an infection in the left lower lung lobe. Stool and blood cultures were positive for Salmonella enteritidis and NSG of the alveolar lavage fluid revealed Legionella pneumophila. Antibacterial treatment was administered. The patient’s condition improved, leading to discharge from the hospital. In June 2023, the patient was admitted to the hospital because of an 8-month history of restricted movement of the right shoulder accompanied by redness and swelling. The drainage fluid from the right shoulder joint was positive for T. marneffei. The patient underwent debridement of the right shoulder joint abscess and symptomatic anti-infection treatment. During hospitalization, the patient tested positive for anti-IFN-γ Abs, and AIGA syndrome was definitively diagnosed. The symptoms eventually improved, and the patient was discharged. However, as of the follow-up phone call, the patient’s right shoulder has remained persistently red, swollen, and painful despite various treatments.

Case 6

A 52-year-old man was admitted to our hospital in October 2022 with a 10-day history of cough and chest pain. CT revealed bilateral pleural effusions, a tissue mass in the left hilum, and multiple mediastinal and left hilum lymph node enlargements. Although the pleural effusion microbiological culture was negative, the patient continued to receive prophylactic antibiotic treatment and was discharged. In November 2022, the patient was readmitted to the hospital with cough and chest pain and was discharged with symptomatic treatment. In December 2022, the patient was readmitted with cough and hemoptysis. Sputum NGS revealed T. marneffei and pneumocystis. The patient was administered symptomatic and anti-infection treatments and was discharged. During that hospitalization, immunodeficiency was considered and subsequent diagnostic testing revealed AIGA syndrome. Subsequently, the patient experienced repeated cough, sputum, chest pain, and penile ulcers, which improved after symptomatic treatment and immunomodulatory therapy. As of the follow-up phone call, the patient remained alive.

Five patients with AIGA had hypergammaglobulinemia since the onset of various symptoms of infection

A retrospective review of the laboratory results of the patients with AIGA showed that all six patients had high C-reactive protein levels. In addition, five patients had high leukocyte counts and erythrocyte sedimentation rates, and four patients had low hemoglobin levels. Based on these results, the patients were more likely to have other intracellular pathogenic infections. Notably, five patients also had high serum IgG levels during previous admissions. The median (IQR) serum IgG level among these patients was 25.06 (17.73–38.22) g/L (reference values:7.0–16.0 g/L or 8.6–17.4 g/L) (Table 2).

Discussion

Anti-cytokine autoantibodies are an emerging disease etiology due to disturbances in the physiological functions of cognate cytokines [9]. High-titer neutralizing autoantibodies against cytokines may block cytokine activity, resulting in susceptibility to various infectious microbes. AIGA was first identified in patients with severe mycobacterial infections and was considered an autoimmune phenotype of inborn genetic errors of the interleukin (IL)−12/IFN-g axis [2]. This condition is now considered a phenocopy of inborn errors of immunity, wherein the antibody-mediated blockade of IFN-γ signaling clinically mirrors the effects of destructive genetic mutations in the same pathway [10]. Most AIGA cases have been reported in Southeast Asia. Recently, specific human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class II molecules have been found associated with these autoantibodies, which may explain the high prevalence of this immunodeficiency syndrome in Southeast Asian populations [11, 12].

IFN-γ plays a critical role in macrophage-mediated killing and granuloma formation in response to intracellular pathogens. In addition, anti-IFN-γ Abs have been associated with disseminated opportunistic infections, such as T. marneffei, Salmonella species, and varicella-zoster virus. Moreover, anti-IFN-γ Abs have also been found in several reported cases of T. marneffei infection in patients who were HIV negative. Thus, AIGA may be considered a new form of late-onset immunodeficiency conferring a predisposition to bacterial, fungal, and severe mycobacterial infections [2, 11, 13, 14]. Our study revealed that, as the disease progressed, the clinical symptoms of all patients worsened, leading to the eventual diagnosis of AIGA. Importantly, all patients were apparently immunocompetent hosts before the detection of the anti-IFN-γ Abs positivity. Moreover, six patients had not received a timely diagnosis in our tertiary university hospital; the time to diagnosis ranged from 2 months to 4 years. Therefore, patients with opportunistic diffuse infections may benefit from screening for AIGA, particularly those patients without other immunodeficiency conditions and patients of Asian descent. Treatment protocols should be tailored for each patient according to infection, autoantibody titers, and treatment response [5]. A systematic review revealed that anti-IFN-γ Abs titer suppression and immune system restoration are important for disease control [5]. However, apart from antimicrobial therapy, there is currently no standard regimen to control the production of these pathogenic autoantibodies and eliminate recurrent infections [3].

Recently, a systematic literature review of anti-IFN-γ Abs-associated diseases was conducted by Zhang et al. [5]. A total of 195 patients with AIGA were retrospectively analyzed. The authors found that AIGA can cause severe opportunistic infections with unsatisfactory control, particularly in patients with recurrent infections. Clinicians involved in the care of patients with AIGA should closely monitor this disease and provide treatments that promote immune system regulation. However, the laboratory results were not fully examined in Zhang et al.’s large retrospective study. In the present small retrospective review of the hospital records of six patients with AIGA, we found that the following laboratory parameters were associated with infection: high C-reactive protein levels, increased leukocyte counts, increased erythrocyte sedimentation rates, and low hemoglobin levels. Importantly, our case series also showed that five of the six patients had hypergammaglobulinemia since the first onset of various infection symptoms, and the median (IQR) IgG level of the patients was 25.06 (17.73–38.22) g/L. While hypergammaglobulinemia is commonly observed in chronic infections, inflammatory conditions, or autoimmune diseases, recent prospective cohort studies have highlighted its specific link to AIGA. Comparative studies in patients with T. marneffei or NTM infections have found that IgG levels are significantly higher in those with anti–IFN‑γ Abs than in AIGA-negative patients with the same underlying infections [15, 16]. These findings suggest that hypergammaglobulinemia in AIGA reflects not only persistent antigenic stimulation but also a more profound immune dysregulation. It is likely that the elevated IgG levels in AIGA may arise from at least two interconnected processes. First, persistent antigenic stimulation from opportunistic infections could drive polyclonal B-cell activation and antibody production. Second, the neutralization of IFN‑γ might disturb the Th1/Th2 equilibrium, potentially favoring a Th2-polarized response that further enhances B-cell activity and antibody class switching [17–19]. Together, these processes point to a state of generalized B-cell hyperactivity and loss of immune homeostasis, positioning hypergammaglobulinemia as a clinically relevant indicator in the evaluation of patients with suspected AIGA.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective design restricted access to comprehensive methodological protocols for anti–IFN-γ Abs testing performed by external laboratories, potentially affecting assay comparability.Second, the single-center design and modest sample size may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Third, the absence of whole-genome sequencing also precluded systematic exclusion of other primary immunodeficiencies in this adult population. Finally, the lack of prolonged follow-up data hindered assessment of long-term disease dynamics and treatment outcomes. Taken together, these limitations underscore the importance of future multi-center prospective studies with larger, well-characterized cohorts and extended follow-up to more fully elucidate the clinical and immunological features of this rare condition.

Conclusion

In conclusion, improved clinical recognition of AIGA remains essential for timely diagnosis and management. This syndrome should be suspected in patients with recurrent or disseminated opportunistic infections—particularly NTM or T. marneffei especially among individuals of Asian descent. Our case series further highlights that hypergammaglobulinemia often appears early in the disease course, even at initial infection onset, and may serve as a useful diagnostic clue when commonly recognized causes have been excluded. In such settings, testing for anti–IFN‑γ Abs should be considered promptly to establish a definitive diagnosis and guide appropriate therapy.
