Influence of Mother's Self-Efficacy, Social Support &amp; Intention on Exclusive Breastfeeding: A Systematic Review
Authors: Monika, S Malar Kodi, Prasuna Jelly, Chandrakala Sankarapandian, Anand Lakshminarasimhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Breastmilk is widely considered to be the best source of nutrition for infants. Psychosocial factors such as maternal attitude, intention, self-efficacy and social support may influence EBF practice. The study aimed to examine the influence of mothers' self-efficacy, social support, and intention on their decision to breastfeed exclusively.A systematic and comprehensive search was conducted across four databases (PubMed, Scopus, Embase, CINAHL) from 2010 to 2024. Observational studies assessing psychosocial determinants of EBF were included. The screening of studies, the methodological quality assessment of selected studies using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale, and the analysis were performed independently by four reviewers. Study selection followed PRISMA guidelines, and findings were synthesised narratively.A total of 16 studies conducted across various countries were included, revealing that the majority of mothers initiate breastfeeding within 1–6 hours. Non-working mothers had higher EBF rates, whereas employed mothers had lower rates of exclusively breastfed infants. Most studies reported positive associations between breastfeeding self-efficacy, intention, social support and EBF practices.The review highlights the need for improved antenatal education, practical support, and counselling from healthcare providers to increase breastfeeding rates and mothers' confidence.

Introduction

Breastfeeding offers both immediate and long-term health benefits for mothers and their infants, and it is generally acknowledged that breast milk is the best nutrition for infants [1]. UNICEF and WHO recommend mothers start breastfeeding as soon as possible after giving birth and do so exclusively for the first six months of the child's life [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) set a global target in 2012 to increase the percentage of women who exclusively breastfeed for the first six months to at least 50% by 2025[3]. According to recent estimates, only 37% of infants in low- and middle-income countries aged 0-5 months are exclusively breastfed [4].

The duration of breastfeeding is influenced by a variety of demographic factors, including maternal age, socio-economic status, ethnicity, cultural group, intention to breastfeed, and family support [5]. Furthermore, research indicates that low breastfeeding self-efficacy and a lack of social support are linked to unfavorable breastfeeding experiences [6].

Breastfeeding is predominantly an individual choice that mothers must make shortly after childbirth. The psychological aspect is essential, as breastfeeding, especially for first-time mothers, requires significant inner fortitude and persistence [7].

Breastfeeding self-efficacy (BSE) is one of the most important, modified factors influencing postpartum women's breastfeeding behavior, according to international research [8,9]. Bandura's concepts serve as the foundation for breastfeeding self-efficacy [10]. Breastfeeding self-efficacy (BSE) refers to a mother's belief in her capacity to breastfeed, a concept established by Dennis in 1999[11].

Breastfeeding outcomes have been found to be significantly influenced by perceived and actual support [12]. Support has a positive impact on mothers' confidence and intention to breastfeed, particularly in low-income communities. The most influential factor in a woman's decision to breastfeed is her partner [13]. Developing successful healthcare strategies to increase exclusive breastfeeding rates will require an in-depth understanding of mothers' intentions, self-efficacy, and social support, as well as how these are connected with breastfeeding practices [14]. This systematic review examined intention, self-efficacy, and social support influencing exclusive breastfeeding to inform future interventions and health policies.

Maternal self-efficacy, intention, and social support influence exclusive breastfeeding. Antenatal and postnatal assessment, partner-inclusive education, and continuous nursing support may strengthen maternal confidence, improve breastfeeding practices, and enhance maternal and infant outcomes.

Methodology

This systematic review was registered in PROSPERO (registration no. CRD42024548023). This review was structured and reported in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (Figure 1). Reviewers 1 and 2 conducted the initial screening. For the initial screening, the titles and abstracts of all the articles were reviewed. Duplications and other articles that were not related to relevant topics were excluded. We obtained all the potentially relevant articles in full text after initial screening. Articles were included for final review only if they met our inclusion criteria. All reviewers with nursing expertise reviewed the selected articles, and the final results of the study will be formulated based on the included articles.

PRISMA Flow Diagram

Search Strategy and Information Sources: A comprehensive literature search was performed across PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and CINAHL Complete databases to identify relevant studies published between January 2010 and July 2024. Both free-text keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were used, including “breastfeeding,” “exclusive breastfeeding,” “postpartum mothers,” “intention,” “self-efficacy,” and “social support.” Boolean operators (AND/OR) were applied to combine search terms.

Reference lists of included studies were also manually screened to identify additional eligible articles. Each of the included studies was meticulously searched and screened.

Eligibility Criteria: The inclusion criteria were cohort, cross-sectional, comparative, correlational, and descriptive studies that assessed exclusive breastfeeding; studies in which participants included healthy mothers who were at least 18 years of age or older and were having term & healthy infants until 6 months of age; and articles that were written and accessible in the English language and published from 2010 to 2024. Mixed-method, qualitative, and intervention studies; reviews or guidelines pertaining to exclusive breastfeeding; studies on the weaning process; studies that included newborns with any comorbidity or preterm birth (such as pyloric stenosis, cleft lip, or cleft palate); and content from books, unpublished theses/dissertations, unpublished case reports, and conferences were all excluded.

Data Extraction: Data extraction tables (Table 3) were utilized to compile essential information from the selected studies.

Assessment of Studies for Risk of Bias: The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included observational studies were independently assessed by four reviewers using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS). The scale evaluated studies based on participant selection, comparability of study groups, and outcome assessment. Discrepancies in quality assessment were resolved through discussion among the reviewers. All studies meeting the inclusion criteria were retained, and quality assessment scores are presented in Tables 1 and 2.

Results

Search Results

The selection of studies was guided by the PRISMA guidelines for study search and selection, along with the inclusion criteria. The electronic databases yielded 218 studies, but 72 of them were duplicates. The remaining 146 paper titles and abstracts were screened for relevance; 86 were deemed irrelevant and excluded. In addition, 60 full-text publications were checked for eligibility, and 16 studies that met the predetermined inclusion criteria from 2010 to 2024 were included in the systematic review.

Study Characteristics and Settings

Of 16 studies, 14 were cross-sectional & 2 were cohort studies. Most of the studies reported that initiation of breastfeeding was done within 1-6 hours after delivery [7,8,11,15–26]. In one study, 60% of mothers initiated within 12 hours after childbirth [27]. Numerous studies have shown that non-working mothers are more likely to exclusively breastfeed than employed mothers [15–23]. Another study revealed that 72.6% of mothers were employed and practised high-exclusive breastfeeding, compared with housewives [11].

Studies have also reported that educational status influences exclusive breastfeeding. Mothers who were educated at the high school, secondary school, or university level had higher breastfeeding rates and greater self-efficacy [7,8,11,15–21,23,24,27]. Similarly, mothers with prior breastfeeding experience, such as multiparous women, were more likely to breastfeed. Eight studies also reported that antenatal classes, counselling, and education were found to be predictors of exclusive breastfeeding among mothers. [7,11,16,19,21,23,25,26]

Outcome Results

Influence of Intention – Four studies have revealed an association between exclusive breastfeeding and intention. [18,20,25,27] Breastfeeding intention was reported as a significant predictor of exclusive breastfeeding for at least six months [18]. Mothers who planned to continue breastfeeding breastfed for a longer duration than those without prior breastfeeding plans (25). We have identified only a few relevant studies that meet our inclusion/eligibility criteria to examine mothers' intentions to breastfeed after childbirth.

Influence of Breastfeeding Self-Efficacy: We identified 15 studies, 11 of which reported an association between exclusive breastfeeding and breastfeeding self-efficacy. [8,11,15,16,18,19,22,23,26,27] Breastfeeding self-efficacy was found to be linked with exclusively breastfeeding [20]. Mothers who continued exclusive breastfeeding for up to 6 months had higher breastfeeding self-efficacy than those who stopped exclusive breastfeeding or switched to mixed/complementary feeding. [8,11,15,16,18,20,22,23,26] Early breastfeeding initiation was related to mothers with high maternal self-efficacy, and in addition, high self-efficacy was found to be predictive of decreasing pre-lacteal feedings [26]. 4 studies reported no association between breastfeeding self-efficacy and exclusive breastfeeding [17,21,24,25], but differences in self-efficacy were found between mothers who breastfed & those who didn't [25]. No link between breastfeeding self-efficacy & duration of exclusive breastfeeding was found. [19,21,24] In contrast to this, one study revealed a moderate level of breastfeeding self-efficacy, but in relation to exclusive breastfeeding, self-efficacy is lowest for exclusive breastfeeding. [19]

Influence of Social Support - 7 studies were found that related to the social support variable. Breastfeeding support at the time of delivery was found to be linked with early initiation of breastfeeding [26] & reported to be more likely to continue breastfeeding to 6 months [23]. One study reported no association between perceived social support and breastfeeding duration at 1 & 6 weeks, but mothers also reported that more 'hands-on' practical support would help them breastfeed for longer [24].

However, in one study, differences between the exclusive breastfeeding group & non-exclusive breastfeeding group in relation to perceived family support were observed [16]. The presence of strong social norms was found to promote exclusive breastfeeding [26]. In three studies, no relation has been observed between exclusive breastfeeding and social support. But social support is positively correlated with self-efficacy; i.e., the more social support a mother receives, the more significant her emotional state is in her breastfeeding self-efficacy. [7,11,19]

The included studies suggest that maternal intention, breastfeeding self-efficacy, and social support are associated with exclusive breastfeeding practices. Studies observed that these factors influence the mother's decision to breastfeed or to change the infant's feeding method.

Discussion

This systematic review examined how maternal intention, self-efficacy, and social support influence exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) among mothers. By analyzing multiple studies, this review gives insight into the psychological and social factors that impact a mother's decision to exclusively breastfeed her infant for the first six months of life.

This review included 16 studies (14 cross-sectional, 2 cohort) that investigated key factors influencing exclusive breastfeeding. The main finding was that mothers with higher confidence in their ability to breastfeed were more likely to continue EBF for six months. Encouragement from family, especially partners, and healthcare professionals positively impacted breastfeeding initiation and duration. Mothers who had a strong intention to exclusively breastfeed before childbirth were more likely to follow through. According to most studies in this systematic review, breastfeeding was initiated within 1 to 6 hours after delivery. [7,8,11,15–26] Consistent with the above findings, a 2021 study in China found that 46.6% of mothers initiated breastfeeding early [1]. In contrast to the abovementioned results, some research has demonstrated delayed breastfeeding initiation because of cultural beliefs, low breast milk production, being too exhausted to feed, baby sleep, caesarean sections, and lack of awareness. [28–32]

A 2023 study by Daif Allah et al. [15] found that only 32.1% of mothers had exclusively breastfed, with self-efficacy being the key contributing factor. Compared with mothers who did not exclusively breastfeed, those who did were more likely to have high self-efficacy [20]. The present study found that maternal

characteristics, specifically education level, influenced EBF practice. Earlier research conducted in developed countries consistently linked mothers who had completed college or higher to a higher prevalence of EBF. The short maternity leave period and limited knowledge may explain the non-significant relationships between maternal education and EBF reported in earlier research [1].

Mother's intention, high breastfeeding self-efficacy, and social support have been linked to early breastfeeding initiation. The results were consistent with earlier research by Sandhi A et al. in 2020[14], which found that 34% of mothers had started breastfeeding within an hour of giving birth, with breastfeeding self-efficacy being a key determinant. Similar research was conducted by Kervin EB et al. in 2010, which found that intention and support were the key determinants [12].

Breastfeeding intention emerged as a major contributing factor to exclusive breastfeeding. The outcome was consistent with an earlier study by Baumgartner T et al. 2020[33], which found that 75.9% of mothers intended to breastfeed their children exclusively. A 2018 study by Manhire et al. [5] found that a woman's mother's and partner's strong support increased breastfeeding and extended the intended duration. Most mothers reported they intended to EBF and planned to breastfeed [5]. Additional research has also shown how crucial mother and partner support is for the duration of breastfeeding, as well as how important peer, family, and partner support is for a pregnant woman's intention to breastfeed. [34–36]

In the present review, one important contributing factor to exclusive breastfeeding was found to be breastfeeding self-efficacy [37]. The outcome was similar to earlier research by Gunes OA et al. [38] and Kronborg H et al. [37], which found a correlation between BSES-SF scores and EBF duration.

In the present review, breastfeeding support was associated with a greater likelihood of continuing breastfeeding to 6 months. The outcome was consistent with the earlier research conducted in 2010 by Kervin EB et al. [12]. Saghooni et al.'s study found that breastfeeding self-efficacy was mainly impacted by social support. [39]

This review demonstrates that maternal intention, self-efficacy, and social support positively influence exclusive breastfeeding. Diverse studies, PRISMA guidance, and Newcastle-Ottawa assessment strengthen its findings. However, methodological heterogeneity, varying measurement tools, English-language restriction, and exclusion of unpublished studies limit comparability, generalizability, and causal inference. Future research should examine cultural and healthcare-system influences on breastfeeding practices.

Conclusion

This review shows that the rate of exclusive breastfeeding increases when the mother is comfortable and confident in her ability to breastfeed. The support and assistance of her spouse, other family members, and the community make such an outcome possible.

The findings confirm that maternal intention, self-efficacy, and social support significantly influence exclusive breastfeeding. The evidence suggests that these factors play a vital role in determining whether a mother initiates and sustains exclusive breastfeeding. However, more longitudinal and interventional studies are necessary for fully comprehending their long-term effects.
