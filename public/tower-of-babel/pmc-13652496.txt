Transcatheter arterial embolization enabling curative surgery for bleeding locally advanced breast cancer: a case report
Authors: Tomoe Kameyama, Tomoko Seki, Takamichi Yokoe, Aiko Nagayama, Maiko Takahashi, Tetsu Hayashida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Locally advanced breast cancer (LABC) is defined as stage IIIB or IIIC disease without distant metastasis and is characterized by extensive primary tumor invasion and/or regional lymph node involvement. Systemic therapy is the standard initial treatment, followed by surgery when feasible[1,2]. Initial management includes compression or topical hemostatic agents, whereas severe or refractory bleeding may require endovascular therapy or surgery. Among these options, transcatheter arterial embolization (TAE) is especially advantageous in patients with unstable clinical conditions because it can be performed under local anesthesia with minimal invasiveness.

Herein, we report a case of LABC in which TAE achieved effective hemostasis, thereby enabling subsequent systemic therapy and an ultimately curative surgical resection of the primary tumor.

This case has been reported in accordance with the SCARE 2025 criteria[3].

Case report

Clinical findings and diagnostic assessment

A 66-year-old woman with no significant medical history presented with progressive enlargement of a mass in the left breast. She had first noticed the mass approximately 10 years earlier but had not sought medical evaluation. Over the preceding 2 months, the tumor rapidly increased in size, resulting in two ulcerated lesions with malodorous exudate. On April 25 of Year X, sudden bleeding occurred from an ulcerated lesion, accompanied by transient loss of consciousness, prompting emergency transport to our hospital.

On admission, she was hemodynamically stable. Physical examination revealed a large, elevated 14 × 10-cm mass with a 3-cm ulcerated area on the left breast (Fig. 1A). Laboratory tests demonstrated anemia (hemoglobin 9.8 g/dL) without evidence of coagulopathy. Angiography and embolization procedure

Percutaneous access was obtained via the left brachial artery, and digital subtraction angiography was performed after the catheter was advanced to the left subclavian artery. The internal mammary artery and the lateral thoracic artery were identified as the primary tumor-feeding vessels. Two branches of the internal mammary artery and the lateral thoracic artery were selectively embolized using gelatin sponge particles (Serescue; Fig. 3). The procedure was completed without complications. Post-embolization course

Approximately 30 minutes after embolization, the ulcerated tumor surface gradually turned black, consistent with ischemic necrosis (Fig. 1B). The patient developed significant local pain, which was managed with analgesic therapy.

On May 2, radiotherapy was initiated for local control. Because the patient had no evidence of distant metastasis and remained a candidate for curative treatment, a total dose of 60 Gy in 25 fractions was prescribed to the breast to maximize durable local control. The supraclavicular region received the standard dose of 50 Gy in 25 fractions for regional nodal irradiation. Histopathological examination of a biopsy specimen obtained at admission confirmed invasive ductal carcinoma, nuclear grade I, estrogen receptor-positive (Allred score 5 + 3), progesterone receptor-positive (Allred score 4 + 2), HER2 score 1 +, and a Ki-67 index of 20%. Endocrine therapy with letrozole was initiated on May 9.

Both the primary tumor and the regional lymph node metastases gradually decreased in size. After approximately 12 months of neoadjuvant therapy, follow-up CT performed in March (Year X + 1) demonstrated that the primary tumor had decreased in size to approximately 5 cm (Fig. 1C), and definitive surgical resection was subsequently performed in May (Year X + 1). Before surgery, the patient was counseled that preoperative radiotherapy might increase operative difficulty because of treatment-related fibrosis, with potential risks of impaired wound healing and postoperative functional deficits.

Surgical findings

On May 11 (Year X + 1), a total mastectomy with axillary lymph node dissection was performed. The skin was excised in an elliptical fashion encompassing the tumor, with an additional 3-cm skin incision toward the axilla to initiate the procedure. A circumferential full-thickness skin flap was created. Marked edema and neovascularization beneath the tumor required meticulous ligation.

During the axillary dissection, dense scar tissue was observed. The thoracodorsal artery, vein, and nerve were preserved, whereas the intercostobrachial nerve and long thoracic nerve could not be identified. The surgical wound was closed successfully without the need for a skin graft (Fig. 1D).

Pathological findings and outcomes

Histological examination revealed only a small residual 1.2-mm invasive tumor, corresponding to a therapeutic response grade of 2b according to the Japanese Breast Cancer Society criteria. No residual lymph node metastasis was identified.

After surgery, letrozole was switched to tamoxifen because of arthralgia. Tamoxifen was subsequently discontinued because of suspected interstitial pneumonitis that required corticosteroid therapy. Although a concomitant herbal medicine was later considered the most likely causative agent, a causal relationship with tamoxifen could not be completely excluded. Exemestane was subsequently initiated but was also discontinued because of severe arthralgia. Given the patient’s intolerance to multiple endocrine agents and the previous episode of interstitial pneumonitis requiring corticosteroid therapy, the multidisciplinary team decided to withhold further endocrine therapy and continue careful surveillance (Supplemental Digital Content Table S1, available at: https://links.lww.com/IJSCR/A140).

Despite the inability to identify either the intercostobrachial nerve or the long thoracic nerve intraoperatively, no postoperative winged scapula, clinically significant sensory disturbance, or upper-extremity dysfunction was observed during follow-up.

The patient has undergone regular surveillance with tumor markers every 3 months and annual CT scans and bone scintigraphy. At the most recent follow-up, 7 years after the initial presentation, the patient remained clinically well, with no evidence of disease recurrence (Fig. 1E).

Discussion

Management of bleeding in LABC is challenging. When conservative measures fail, TAE provides a minimally invasive option for rapid hemorrhage control, particularly in patients with a compromised general condition or initially unresectable disease. In the present case, the patient experienced transient loss of consciousness associated with hemorrhage from a rapidly enlarging fungating tumor. Although temporary hemostasis was achieved on admission, recurrent bleeding posed a potentially life-threatening risk and precluded the initiation of definitive oncologic treatment. Because no distant metastasis was identified and curative treatment remained feasible, TAE was selected to achieve definitive hemorrhage control rather than purely palliative management. Urgent mastectomy was considered to carry a substantial risk of excessive intraoperative bleeding because of the highly vascular tumor, while radiotherapy alone was unlikely to achieve sufficiently rapid hemostasis. Our strategy was to achieve prompt hemorrhage control, administer multimodal therapy, and perform surgery after maximal tumor regression whenever feasible. TAE, therefore, best supported this treatment strategy. Because the patient’s recent massive hemorrhage and transient hemodynamic instability increased the anticipated risk of cytotoxic chemotherapy, chemotherapy was not considered the optimal initial treatment. After successful hemostasis, radiotherapy was administered with curative intent to achieve durable local control before surgery. Favorable tumor biology further supported the use of endocrine therapy once the patient’s condition had stabilized.

The breast is supplied primarily by the internal mammary artery, lateral thoracic artery, anterior intercostal arteries, and thoracoacromial artery[4]. Consistent with these anatomical findings, the internal mammary artery and lateral thoracic artery were identified as the major tumor-feeding vessels in our patient.

A PubMed search using the terms “breast,” “bleeding,” and “embolization” identified eight previously reported cases[5–11] (Table 1). Hemostasis was successfully achieved in all reported cases, demonstrating that embolization is an effective technique for controlling tumor-related hemorrhage. Most previously reported cases involved patients with recurrent or metastatic disease or those who were not considered suitable candidates for curative surgery. Consequently, embolization was used primarily as a palliative hemostatic intervention or to facilitate subsequent systemic therapy rather than as definitive surgical treatment. Although Atzori et al[9] also reported curative surgery after embolization, their patient required repeated preoperative embolization, achieved only a modest response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, and ultimately required skin graft reconstruction. By contrast, in the present case, TAE achieved immediate and sustained hemostasis, thereby allowing uninterrupted radiotherapy and endocrine therapy. This treatment strategy resulted in marked tumor regression and ultimately enabled definitive surgical resection with minimal tissue loss and without the need for skin grafting. These findings suggest that, in selected patients without distant metastasis, TAE may function as a critical bridge from emergent hemorrhage control to curative-intent multimodal treatment.

Potential complications include pain, necrosis, infection, and vascular access–related events; however, these were manageable and did not compromise subsequent treatment. Careful patient selection and multidisciplinary collaboration remain essential.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that TAE can facilitate uninterrupted multimodal therapy and subsequent curative surgical resection in selected patients with non-metastatic LABC. Beyond its established role in emergency hemostasis, TAE may also serve as an important component of curative-intent multidisciplinary treatment. Further studies are warranted to better define its optimal indications.
