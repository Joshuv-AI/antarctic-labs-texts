Genome-wide CRISPR screens in primary human natural killer cells identify countermeasures against immunosuppressive environment
Authors: Quoc Viet Nguyen, Yi-Jun Lan, Jason Cheng-Yu Chang, Hsin-An Shih, Jenifer Faustine, Cheng-Chieh Chen, Shu-Yu Ho, Ching-Wen Cheng, Tsu-Lan Chao, Steven Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNatural killer (NK) cells are promising effectors for cancer immunotherapy, but their efficacy is limited by immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments. To uncover strategies for enhancing NK cell function, we establish a CRISPR loss-of-function screening platform for primary human NK cells by combining BaEVRless-pseudotyped lentiviral transduction of sgRNA libraries with Cas9 protein electroporation. This platform enables genome-scale interrogation of gene function in non-transformed NK cells. Kinome-focused and genome-wide screens identify key regulators of NK cell proliferation, cytotoxicity, and resistance to prostaglandin E2 (PGE2)-mediated suppression. STK17B deletion enhances NK cell expansion, while loss of CCDC53 boosts degranulation and cytotoxicity. We also uncover the CRL5 complex—including RNF7, UBE2F, and CISH—as critical inhibitors of IL-2 signaling and effector function under PGE2 stress. These findings establish a scalable platform for CRISPR-based functional genomics in primary NK cells and reveal engineering targets to enhance NK cell persistence and efficacy in tumor microenvironments.Subject terms: NK cells, CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing, Genetic engineering, High-throughput screening

Introduction

Natural killer (NK) cells are cytotoxic innate lymphocytes that mediate tumor immunosurveillance through direct killing of transformed cells and secretion of proinflammatory cytokines1,2. Their activity is tightly regulated by a balance of activating and inhibitory signals, enabling rapid, antigen-independent responses. However, the tumor microenvironment (TME) imposes metabolic, physical, and immunosuppressive barriers that impair NK cell infiltration, persistence, and effector function3. Despite their critical role in early tumor control, the intracellular pathways governing NK cell activation and adaptation to the TME remain poorly understood. Unlike T cells, NK cells lack well-characterized checkpoint regulators, with CISH being among the few known4. A deeper understanding of NK cell–intrinsic regulatory mechanisms is essential to overcome tumor-induced suppression and improve immunotherapy.

CRISPR genetic screening is a powerful approach to dissect gene function and immune regulation; however, its application in primary human NK cells has been technically challenging due to inefficient sgRNA delivery and unstable Cas9 expression in short-lived cultures. Early studies relied on murine NK cells using gene subset libraries5 or NK-92 cell line6, which do not fully recapitulate the immunology of primary human NK cells. Recent advances have begun to overcome these challenges. Nikolic et al. combined a genome-wide CRISPR screen in NK-92 with a targeted sub-library screen in peripheral blood NK cells to identify UBE2F, ARIH2 and the Cullin-5-RING E3 ubiquitin ligase (CRL5) complex as negative regulators of IL-15R signaling7. Biederstädt et al. conducted genome-wide screens in human cord blood NK cells, using retroviral transduction of sgRNA and Cas9 protein electroporation, to uncover MED12, ARIH2, and CCNC as suppressors of NK cell antitumor function8. These studies demonstrate that large-scale functional genomics in primary NK cells is now achievable, providing unprecedented opportunities to delineate cell-intrinsic checkpoints and discover therapeutic targets.

Here, we present a CRISPR knockout (KO) screening platform tailored to primary human NK cells isolated from peripheral blood. We adopted the SLICE method—originally developed for primary T cells9—which combines lentiviral delivery of sgRNA libraries with Cas9 protein electroporation to achieve efficient gene disruption without requiring stable Cas9 expression (Fig. 1a). By integrating SLICE with BaEVRless-pseudotyped lentiviral vectors (BaEV-LV)10, we enabled scalable and high-efficiency CRISPR perturbation in NK cells. To overcome transduction limitations, we improved BaEV-LV production by ablating the ASCT2 receptor in HEK293T packaging cells to prevent syncytia formation, supplementing cultures with inositol hexaphosphate (IP6), and optimizing purification protocols. These improvements increased BaEV-LV titers by ~50-fold and enabled robust sgRNA delivery. Optimization of SLICE method enabled >80% KO efficiencies while maintaining NK cell viability and function.

Using this platform, we performed kinome-focused and genome-wide CRISPR KO screens in ex vivo expanded primary NK cells to systematically identify regulators of cell proliferation, degranulation, and resistance to prostaglandin E2 (PGE2)-mediated immunosuppression. Degranulation is the principal mechanism of NK cell cytotoxicity, and its impairment in the TME represents a major therapeutic barrier. Our screens uncovered genes encoding canonical regulators (UNC13D, VAV1, PIK3CD), novel inhibitory checkpoints (STK17B, CCDC53), and components of the CRL5 ubiquitin ligase complex (RNF7, UBE2F, CISH). Although the screens were conducted ex vivo, the pathways identified are fundamental to NK cell biology and provide a foundation for investigating how NK cells respond to suppressive cues in vivo. These findings provide a framework for understanding the intracellular programs that constrain NK cell activity under suppressive conditions. More broadly, this work establishes a scalable functional genomics platform and highlights candidate targets for engineering NK cells with improved persistence and anti-tumor efficacy.

Results

Optimized BaEV-LV production and SLICE-mediated gene KO in primary NK cells

Efficient CRISPR screening in NK cells requires robust delivery of sgRNA libraries and effective gene KO. VSV-G–pseudotyped lentivirus (VSVG-LV) is inefficient because NK cells lack the low-density lipoprotein receptor necessary for VSV-G binding11. BaEVRless, a modified envelope protein from Baboon endogenous retrovirus, enhances transduction by targeting the ASCT receptors widely expressed in activated NK cells10. Compared to VSVG-LV, BaEV-LV demonstrated >80% transduction efficiency in primary NK cells at low multiplicities of infection (MOI, Fig. 1b). However, BaEV-LV titers were initially ~100-fold lower than VSVG-LV due to rapid syncytia formation and cell death in virus-producing HEK293T cells (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Similar issues were previously reported10,12.

We speculated that BaEVRless bound to ASCT receptors on neighboring HEK293T cells, triggering membrane fusion (Supplementary Fig. 2a). To test this, we disrupted the ASCT1 and ASCT2 genes by electroporating Cas9 ribonucleoproteins (RNP, Supplementary Fig. 2b) and analyzed the KO clones for syncytia formation (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). ASCT1 KO produced small, fused cell patches (Supplementary Fig. 1c); whereas, ASCT2 KO completely abolished syncytia, confirming that ASCT2 is the primary BaEVRless receptor (Supplementary Fig. 1d). ASCT2-KO clone #8 increased BaEV-LV titers by six-fold (Fig. 1d), exhibited normal growth (Supplementary Fig. 2e, f), and integrated seamlessly into standard lentiviral packaging protocol. IP6 supplementation, a metabolite essential for HIV replication13, further boosted titers in a dose-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 2g). Lenti-X precipitation yielded >90% virus recovery without affecting NK cell viability (Supplementary Fig. 3a–c). Together, these optimizations increased BaEV-LV titers by 50-fold to ~5 × 108 TU/mL (Fig. 1c), enabling scalable sgRNA library delivery.

To circumvent the Cas9 expression problem, we implemented the SLICE-mediated KO method (SLICE-KO), wherein lentiviral sgRNA transduction is followed by electroporation of recombinant Cas9 protein (Supplementary Fig. 4a). Optimization of BaEV-LV transduction at low MOI (0.3) and 4 µg/mL polybrene without spinfection balanced efficiency and viability (Supplementary Fig. 3d–f). Using this protocol, we performed SLICE-KO of CD96 with 20 pmol Cas9 and MOI 0.2–0.4, and achieved ~80% KO efficiency and high viability (Fig. 1e, f; Supplementary Fig. 4b–e). Comparable efficiency for CD226 (encoding DNAM-1) further confirmed the robustness of SLICE in primary NK cells (Fig. 1g).

Pilot CRISPR kinome screen identifies key regulators of NK cell proliferation

We conducted a pilot screen using the Brunello kinome library (6,204 sgRNAs targeting 763 kinase genes)14 to identify modulators of NK cell expansion. The library was packaged in BaEV-LV using our optimized protocol. To maintain 1000-fold sgRNA coverage, we transduced 2 × 107 NK cells at MOI 0.3, followed by Cas9 electroporation. After puromycin selection and six-day expansion, sgRNAs from surviving cells were amplified for next-generation sequencing (NGS) and analyzed to identify essential kinase genes (Fig. 2a).

Packaging and transduction of sgRNA libraries were highly efficient. BaEV-LV-transduced NK cells retained 6,194 of 6,204 sgRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 5a). MAGeCK analysis confirmed uniform sgRNA distribution in kinome-edited cells (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c). As expected in a successful CRISPR KO screen, sgRNAs targeting essential genes were depleted. Notably, we observed depletion of sgRNA targeting key cell cycle kinases including CDK615, TRRAP16, and TP53RK17, a negative regulator of apoptosis in response to mitotic stress (Fig. 2b, c). Depletion of JAK3, a critical kinase downstream of the IL-2 signaling pathway essential for NK cell survival18, further validated the screen. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed significant depletion of gene sets associated with cell cycle, metabolism, JAK-STAT and p53 signaling, confirming robust CRISPR screening in primary NK cells (Fig. 2d; Supplementary Fig. 5d).

Two kinase genes were enriched, suggesting negative regulatory roles in NK cell growth (Fig. 2b, c). IRAK3 encodes IL-1 receptor–associated kinase-3, a modulator of innate inflammation that negatively regulates IL-1/TLR signaling19. IRAK3 KO in mice delays cancer growth and enhances myeloid and T cell activation20. We were particularly interested in STK17B (DRAK2), a death-associated kinase implicated in immune homeostasis and apoptosis21–23. DRAK2 inhibits IL-2-induced STAT5A phosphorylation in T cells24 and modulates TCR-mediated calcium influx25, although its mechanisms remain unclear. We disrupted STK17B by Cas9 RNP electroporation using the top-ranking sgRNA from the kinome library and confirmed editing efficiency by Sanger sequencing and Inference of CRISPR Edits (ICE) analysis and immunoblotting (Supplementary Fig. 6a, 6b). AAVS1, a genome-safe harbor locus, was used as a non-functional editing control. ICE analysis estimated ~60% KO efficiency, which corresponded to a proportional reduction in STK17B protein level. Gene-KO NK cells were rested for four days for target protein turnover, normalized by cell density, and cultured under 10 or 100 U/mL of IL-2 for three more days to assess proliferation (Fig. 2e).

STK17B-KO cells proliferated more rapidly than mock-electroporated and AAVS1-KO controls. We performed carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) staining and flow cytometry to measure NK cell proliferation. CFSELow cells, as the result of cell division, were gated based on individual mock controls to yield ~50% CFSELow cells in the mock condition. The analysis revealed a higher percentage of replicating cells in the STK17B-KO population (80−90% CFSELow) than both controls (50−60%) under both high and low IL-2 conditions (Fig. 2f). Consequently, the total number of STK17B-KO cells increased by 1.5 and 2-fold relative to controls under low and high IL-2 concentrations, respectively (Fig. 2g). Importantly, STK17B ablation did not impair NK cell cytotoxicity in vitro (Fig. 2h).

Our findings in NK cells are consistent with previous observations in T cells, where STK17B is implicated in immune homeostasis and inhibition of IL-2 signaling21–24. Our results suggest that STK17B similarly regulates NK cell proliferation and survival under cytokine-limiting conditions. Further studies are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms, which may reveal checkpoints that limit NK cell expansion and persistence in IL-2-limited environments.

Genome-wide screen uncovers regulators of NK cell degranulation

Encouraged by successful kinome screens, we performed a genome-wide CRISPR KO (GeCKO) screen to identify regulators of NK cell degranulation, the major cytotoxic mechanism for tumor cell killing. Systematic dissection using CRISPR screens enables the identification of genes that regulate this process and may be targeted to enhance anti-tumor activity. The GeCKO library26, comprising 119,461 sgRNAs targeting 20,708 genes, was packaged into BaEV-LV at high titer ( ~ 108 TU/mL) (Supplementary Fig. 7a). MAGeCK analysis confirmed >98% sgRNA representation in transduced NK cells and high library uniformity, comparable to the kinome screen (Supplementary Fig. 7b–d). GSEA revealed significant depletion of gene sets associated with ribosome function, spliceosome activity, oxidative phosphorylation, and primary immunodeficiency (Supplementary Fig. 7e–g). These findings demonstrate the efficiency and scalability of BaEV-LV for genome-wide CRISPR perturbation in primary human NK cells.

We co-cultured GeCKO-edited NK cells with HCT116 colon cancer cells to activate NK cell degranulation (Fig. 3a). CD107a surface expression was used to isolate highly degranulating (CD107ahigh) and poorly degranulating (CD107alow) NK cells by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for NGS (Supplementary Fig. 8a–c). Comparative analysis of sgRNA distributions revealed gene enrichment and depletion profiles. Top negatively ranked genes—including UNC13D, VAV1, RAPGEF1, LAMP1, PIK3CD, ORAI1, CD226, ITGAL, and CDC42—encode receptors, adaptors, and signaling kinases essential for granule polarization, calcium flux, and immune synapse formation (Fig. 3b, c)27. Notably, SKP1 (rank 10), a core component of the SKP1–Cullin1–F-box (SCF) ubiquitin E3 ligase complex, was depleted in CD107ahigh cells. The SCF complex regulates proteasomal turnover of many substrates, including the transcription factor FOXO1 (encoded by FOXO1, rank 2263), which must first be phosphorylated by Akt and then recognized by the F-box protein SKP228. The role of FOXO1 in NK cells remains unresolved. In mice, cytosolic FOXO1 was shown to mediate autophagy essential for NK cell development, protection against reactive oxygen species and antiviral responses29. In contrast in human NK cells, FOXO1 was reported to suppress maturation and antitumor activity30.

To validate SKP1, we disrupted the gene using Cas9 RNP electroporation with the two top-ranking sgRNAs from the screen. Although ICE analysis showed ~0% indels for both sgRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 6b), immunoblotting revealed a notable reduction in SKP1 protein using sgRNA #2 across two donors (Supplementary Fig. 6c). Similar discrepancies between genotyping and protein loss have been observed31–33. Unexpectedly, SKP1 loss reduced FOXO1 protein levels, opposite to the canonical model in which the SCF complex promotes FOXO1 degradation. (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). Because SKP1 and FOXO1 were identified as positive regulators of degranulation in our screen (Fig. 3b), the reduced FOXO1 protein observed in SKP1-KO NK cells is consistent with their shared functional role, even if the underlying mechanism differs from that described in other cell types. Notably, neither AKT nor SKP2, encoding the key regulators of SCF complex, were significantly depleted in our screen. These findings suggest that SKP1 may regulate NK cell degranulation through mechanisms distinct from SCF complex-mediated pathway described in cancer cells, underscoring the need to define additional SKP1 targets that modulate NK cell effector function.

CCDC53 and FAM49B are negative regulators of NK cell cytotoxicity

We were more interested in positively ranked genes, which were potential negative regulators of NK cell degranulation. Among the top hits were known immune regulators, including CALHM25, FAM49B34, PTPN1135 and TNFAIP336. Notably, CALHM2 and FAM49B have been implicated in lymphocyte function through prior CRISPR screens. CALHM2 was previously identified in primary murine NK cells, where its KO enhanced cytotoxicity and promoted tumor infiltration, including in human CAR-NK models5. FAM49B, originally discovered in Jurkat T cell line as a cytoskeletal regulator, attenuates T cell receptor signaling by modulating actin dynamics34. We also identified genes associated with calcium signaling (EZH237, ORAI238), lysosomal biogenesis (DUSP139, RAD1440), vesicular trafficking (RALGAPB41,42), and endocytic recycling (RAB11B43)—processes critical for granule polarization and release (Fig. 3d). However, the resolution of positively ranked genes in GeCKO screens tends to be lower than for depleted hits, owing to factors, such as screen scale, donor variability, and the inherent strengths of negative selection in pooled dropout assays. To overcome these limitations and increase the precision of functional validation, we designed a secondary screen using a custom sgRNA library.

Our custom library comprises 1115 positively ranked genes from the GeCKO screen, five known regulators of NK cell degranulation as positive controls (LAMP1, PIK3CD, UNC13D, CD226, ITGAL), and 905 non-targeting negative controls, totaling 11,981 sgRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 9a). MAGeCK analysis confirmed high sgRNA uniformity before and after transduction into NK cells from three new donors (Supplementary Fig. 9b, 9c). After HCT116 co-culture, CD107ahigh and CD107alow cells were isolated for analysis (Supplementary Fig. 8d). The secondary screen confirmed depletion of positive controls and reinforced enrichment of several candidates (Fig. 3e, f). GSEA further highlighted genes associated with membrane trafficking (Supplementary Fig. 9d).

We disrupted eight top-ranked gene candidates by electroporating Cas9 RNPs with two top sgRNAs per gene, alongside AAVS1 as a negative control (Supplementary Fig. 8e). Edited cells were rested for 10 days before degranulation analysis. We performed a correlation analysis between sgRNA enrichment scores from the secondary screen and CD107a expression levels measured by flow cytometry in KO cells, providing an assessment of how well the screen results predicted functional degranulation phenotypes (Supplementary Fig. 9f). The modest correlation (R² = 0.464) highlights discrepancies between the pooled screening data and individual KO phenotypes, underscoring the need for detailed functional validation.

Ablation of ACTR2, CCDC53 and WDR91 resulted in a 3–4-fold increase in surface CD107a despite donor variability (Supplementary Fig. 8e). ACTR2- and WDR91-KO cells exhibited high basal CD107a without stimulation, suggesting altered CD107a localization or vesicle trafficking (Supplementary Fig. 9g). By contrast, CCDC53-KO cells showed consistently elevated CD107a at baseline and after stimulation (Fig. 4a, c; Supplementary Fig. 6a). KO cells also demonstrated higher IFN-γ production, although the increase did not reach statistical significance due to donor variation (Fig. 4d).

To evaluate functional impact, we assessed NK cell cytotoxicity using xCELLigence Real-Time Cell Analysis (RTCA, Fig. 4b). CCDC53-KO NK cells enhanced killing of HCT116 without compromising NK cell growth (Fig. 4e, f; Supplementary Fig. 9e). FAM49B-KO cells also showed increased cytotoxicity. Although FAM49B has not been characterized in NK cells, its role in T cells—where it suppresses activation by restraining Rac activity and cytoskeletal remodeling—suggests a potential parallel function in NK cells34. Finally, although ITSN2 ranked highly in the screen, its KO caused only modest degranulation enhancement and did not improve long-term killing. Collectively, these results highlight CCDC53 and FAM49B as key negative regulators of NK cell effector function.

CCDC53 ablation enhances perforin secretion

CCDC53 is a core component of the WASH complex which regulates actin dynamics and vesicle trafficking44. WASH is essential for lytic granule polarization and degranulation in NK cells45, and its disruption in T cells impairs trafficking of key receptors, including TCR, CD28, LFA-1, and GLUT1, critical for activation and proliferation46. Prior biochemical studies showed that CCDC53, along with Strumpellin, FAM21, and SWIP, forms a regulatory subcomplex that inhibits WASH-mediated actin polymerization47. Although CCDC53 participates in these processes, its specific contribution to NK cell degranulation has not been characterized.

To determine how CCDC53 loss influences NK-cell effector functions, we first assessed the surface level of major activating and inhibitory receptors. We analyzed the KO cells before and after HCT116 stimulation by flow cytometry, on day 3 and 10 post editing to ensure complete CCDC53 turnover. We did not observe significant changes in Fas ligand and activating receptors CD16, NKp46, and NKG2D between the parental, AAVS1-KO control and CCDC53-KO cells (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Similarly, there were no change in inhibitory receptors NKG2A, PD-1, and TIGIT.

Next, we analyzed the secretion of TNF, IFN-γ, perforin and granzyme B by Luminex multiplex immunoassay, which uses antibody-conjugated and fluorescence-labeled microspheres for protein detection. On day 3, CCDC53-KO cells produced comparable levels of cytokines, perforin and granzyme B secretion relative to control groups under HCT116 stimulation and chemical activation by phorbol myristate acetate (PMA) and ionomycin (Supplementary Fig. 10b). On day 10, however, CCDC53-KO cells showed significant increase in perforin secretion in both unstimulated and HCT116-stimulated conditions (Fig. 4g). Despite donor variation, these data indicate that CCDC53 disruption did not alter receptor abundance in NK cells under these conditions, but increased perforin secretion at baseline and upon cancer cell stimulation. This increase in perforin secretion likely contributes, at least in part, to the enhanced cytotoxicity observed in CCDC53-KO NK cells.

Genome-wide screen reveals mechanisms of resistance to PGE2 immunosuppression

Solid tumors suppress immune effectors through diverse physical and chemical mechanisms, including secretion of inflammatory mediator PGE2. In T cells, PGE2 signals through Gαs-coupled EP2 and EP4 receptors to elevate intracellular cAMP, impairing IL-2 signaling by promoting IL-2Rγc degradation and disrupting receptor assembly48,49. In NK cells, PGE2 inhibits cytotoxicity, IFN-γ production, degranulation, chemotaxis and immune synapse polarization50,51. Mechanistically, PGE2 impairs IL-15 signaling by downregulating surface expression of IL-2Rγc, a critical component of the IL-15 receptor complex52. Collectively, these effects contribute to tumor immune evasion by impairing NK and T cell activation.

We leveraged GeCKO screening to investigate the genetic basis of NK cell resistance to PGE2 immunosuppression (Fig. 5a). PGE2 treatment reduced degranulation in a dose-dependent manner, with near-complete inhibition observed in one donor (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). CD107ahigh NK cells from donor 1 and 2 were isolated, and sgRNA enrichment analysis identified resistance-associated genes. MAGeCK analysis revealed enrichment of sgRNAs targeting GNAS and PRKAR1A, which encode Gαs and the type I regulatory subunit of cAMP-dependent protein kinase (PKA) respectively, implicating the Gαs–PKA pathway in mediating PGE2 responses in NK cells (Fig. 5b, c). RASA2, a known attenuator of T cell activation53, also emerged as a modulator of NK cell sensitivity to PGE2. Additionally, CCDC53 was enriched, albeit with low confidence based on a single high-rank sgRNA (Supplementary Data 9). Notably, although EP4 has been identified as the primary PGE2 receptor in NK cells54, neither EP2 nor EP4 emerged as hits, suggesting that targeting a single receptor may not fully disrupt downstream signaling.

Strikingly, RNF7, UBE2F and CISH emerged as top hits conferring PGE2 resistance (Fig. 5b, c; Supplementary Fig. 11c). RNF7 encodes RBX2, and UBE2F encodes a NEDD8-conjugating enzyme—both are core components of the CRL5 ubiquitin ligase complex55. CRL5 promotes proteasomal degradation of signaling intermediates in cytokine signaling, inflammation, and tumorigenesis. In T cells, CRL5 downregulates effector function by degrading JAK1 and JAK3 to attenuate JAK–STAT signaling56, this process involves CIS (CISH), a SOCS family protein that downregulates IL-15 signaling and anti-tumor immunity by targeting phosphorylated JAK1, JAK3 and IL-2Rβ for proteasomal degradation4,57. Consistent with prior evidence that CISH, UBE2F and RNF7 deletion augment NK cell cytokine responsiveness and anti-tumor activity7, our screens position the CRL5–CIS axis as a central checkpoint in NK-cell sensitivity to PGE2 (Fig. 5e).

These top-ranked hits demonstrated high statistical confidence and strong correlation between two donors (Fig. 5d; Supplementary Fig. 11c), underscoring the robustness of the screen. Among these candidates, RASA2 and CISH have been previously characterized, and we therefore focused our validation efforts on GNAS, UBE2F and RNF7 functions in NK-cell resistance to PGE₂.

Ablation of GNAS, UBE2F, and RNF7 improves NK cell degranulation under PGE2 suppression

We disrupted GNAS, UBE2F, or RNF7 by Cas9 RNP electroporation using the top-ranked sgRNAs from the screen, confirmed the editing outcomes by ICE and immunoblotting, and rested the cells for 10 days before functional assays (Fig. 6a). GNAS KO was modest, yielding ~30% indels with partial protein reduction (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). UBE2F KO showed a notable discrepancy in genotyping and protein loss —indel frequencies were ~30%, yet UBE2F protein was undetectable by immunoblotting. RNF7 KO was more efficient as confirmed by ~80% indels and high level of protein loss. Importantly, none of the gene perturbations affected NK cell expansion (Supplementary Fig. 11d), indicating that the observed phenotypes were not attributable to defects in cell growth or viability.

We next assessed whether these perturbations conferred resistance to PGE2 suppression of NK-cell degranulation. NK cells were exposed to 100 ng/mL PGE2 for 24 hours, stimulated by HCT116, and analyzed for surface CD107a and intracellular IFN-γ. As expected, AAVS1-KO control cells were susceptible to PGE2 suppression, showing ~50% reductions in both markers relative to untreated control (Fig. 6b, c). In contrast, GNAS-, UBE2F-, and RNF7-KO cells were more resistant to PGE2 suppression, showing only minor, insignificant reduction in CD107a compared with untreated AAVS1-KO cells (Fig. 6b). UBE2F- and RNF7-KO cells exhibited elevated IFN-γ levels at baseline and under PGE2 suppression (Fig. 6c). Under PGE2 condition, the IFN-γ levels increased to 13% in the UBE2F-KO and 21% in RNF7-KO cells, compared with 2% in AAVS1-KO control. Although donor variation in PGE₂ sensitivity was substantial as observed in both the PGE2 screen (Supplementary Fig. 11b) and this degranulation assay, the overall pattern supports a role for GNAS, UBE2F, and RNF7 in PGE2 suppression of NK cell effector function.

UBE2F- and RNF7-KO enhances IL-2 signaling

Our findings with UBE2F- and RNF7-KO cells are consistent with the enhanced cytokine responsiveness observed in the absence of CRL5-dependent negative regulation recently reported by Nikolic et al7. To further probe this mechanism, we examined IL-2-induced JAK-STAT signaling pathway in the gene-KO cells. The IL-2 receptor comprises IL2Rα (CD25), IL2Rβ (CD122) and IL2Rγ (CD132). Upon IL-2 engagement, JAK1 and JAK3 kinases associate with IL2Rβ and IL2Rγ and phosphorylate tyrosine residues within their cytoplasmic domains, enabling the recruitment and phosphorylation of STAT5A and STAT5B. Activated STAT5 dimers then translocate to the nucleus to drive transcription of cytokine-responsive genes.

We pulse-stimulated the gene-KO cells with IL-2 and monitored the phosphorylation levels of JAK1, JAK3 and STAT5 over time by immunoblotting. The native and phosphorylated forms of STAT5A and STAT5B homologous proteins were detected using pan-STAT5 antibodies. In the absence of PGE2, UBE2F- and RNF7-KO cells exhibited more sustained phosphorylation of JAK1, JAK3 and STAT5 (hereafter pJAK1, pJAK3 and pSTAT5, respectively) compared with AAVS1-KO controls (Fig. 6d; Supplementary Fig. 12a, 12b). This enhancement became more pronounced under PGE2 suppression. In PGE2-treated AAVS1-KO controls, pJAK1 and pJAK3 were nearly undetectable, and pSTAT5 was markedly diminished relative to untreated controls (Fig. 6d; Supplementary Fig. 12a, b). By contrast, UBE2F- and RNF7-KO cell maintained robust pJAK1, pJAK3 and pSTAT5 levels throughout the two-hour time course. Despite high donor variation, the overall increase in phosphorylation amplitude and persistence was observed across all three donors (Supplementary Fig. 13).

We next assessed the abundance of IL-2 receptor subunits by flow cytometry. Consistent with observations in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes58, PGE2 treatment increased IL2Rα but decreased IL2Rγ in NK cells (Fig. 6e, f). Disrupting UBE2F or RNF7 further augmented the IL2Rα levels relative to AAVS1-KO control, as measured by mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). Both KOs increased basal IL2Rγ expression and prevented its PGE2-induced downregulation (Fig. 6e, f). In prior studies7,58, IL2Rβ was detected at very low levels in primary T and NK cells, consistent with our results (Fig. 6f). We evaluated two commercial antibodies and found the IL2Rβ MFI too low for reliable interpretation. Nevertheless, the overall pattern of JAK-STAT activation and IL-2 receptor modulation we observed in the UBE2F- and RNF7-KO cells aligns with prior reports of PGE2- and CRL5-mediated suppression of IL-2 receptor expression and IL-2-dependent signaling.

UBE2F and RNF7 KO boost NK cell cytotoxicity

We wanted to assess whether the increased resistance to PGE2 in the gene-KO cells translated into enhanced anti-tumor activity. We first examined the in vitro cytotoxicity of GNAS-, UBE2F- and RNF7-KO cells under two PGE2 treatment conditions. In condition 1, NK cells were pretreated with PGE2 for one day and assayed against the cancer targets without PGE2, matching the CRISPR screen setup. In condition 2, NK cells were not pre-exposed to PGE2 but assayed in the continuous presence of PGE2. NK cell-mediated lysis of HCT116 and PANC-1 pancreatic cancer cells was monitored by RTCA for three days across three donors (Supplementary Fig. 14a−d). Prolonged PGE2 exposure (condition 2) markedly reduced NK cell cytotoxicity of NK cells against both cancer types (Fig. 7a versus 7b). UBE2F and RNF7 ablation improved cytotoxicity in both PGE2 conditions; however, substantial donor-to-donor variability was observed, and the overall enhancement did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, GNAS KO did not improve cytotoxicity. This is likely because GNAS loss primarily reduces NK cell sensitivity to PGE2, whereas the RNF7 and UBE2F KO directly boost IL-2 signaling and effector function.

Next, we tested the UBE2F- and RNF7-KO cells in a three-dimensional tumoroid assay under PGE2 condition 2. We generated GFP+ HCT116 and PANC-1 cell lines by lentiviral transduction of gfp gene, formed tumoroids in ultra-low attachment 96-well round-bottom plates, co-cultured with NK cells in the presence of PGE2, and monitored the NK-mediated tumoroid lysis by cell imagining using high-content microscopy. Tumoroid sizes were measured by GFP fluorescence. Both cancer cell lines readily formed tumoroids within 2 − 3 days.

HCT116 tumoroids were more susceptible to NK cell killing than PANC-1. In the absence of PGE2, HCT116 tumoroids were efficiently lysed by NK cells regardless of gene KO status at 24 h (Fig. 7c, d; Supplementary Fig. 15a, b). However, under PGE2 treatment, cytotoxicity of AAVS1-KO cells was markedly reduced, whereas UBE2F- and RNF7-KO retained robust killing. KO-dependent enhancement was evident in two donors but less pronounced in the third, resulting in an insignificant overall increase (Fig. 7e).

PANC-1 tumoroids expanded more slowly than HCT116, but were more resistant to NK cell killing. We generated larger PANC-1 tumoroids, approximately double the size of HCT116, and monitored NK cell cytotoxicity for one additional day. Under PGE2 treatment, AAVS1-KO cells exhibited greatly diminished cytotoxicity, resulting in <20% PANC-1 tumoroid lysis by the end of assay (Fig. 7f−h; Supplementary Fig. 15c, 15d). In contrast, UBE2F- and RNF7-KO NK cells maintained robust cytotoxic activity and progressively shrunk PANC-1 tumoroids. Notably, even under PGE2 suppression, cytotoxicity of UBE2F- and RNF7-KO NK cells approached that of untreated AAVS1-KO controls (Fig. 7f). Although these assays suggested improved cytotoxic function following UBE2F or RNF7 ablation, the overall effect did not reach statistical significance in the linear mixed-effects analysis, likely reflecting substantial donor-to-donor variability. Together, these observations suggest that disruption of the CRL5 complex may partially mitigate PGE2-mediated suppression of NK cell cytotoxicity.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a scalable CRISPR KO screening platform for primary human NK cells by integrating BaEV-LV–mediated sgRNA delivery with Cas9 protein electroporation. This approach overcame long-standing challenges—low transduction efficiency and transient Cas9 expression—enabling efficient and scalable gene disruption. To enable robust sgRNA delivery, we enhanced BaEV-LV production by ablating ASCT2 in HEK293T packaging cells, increasing lentiviral titers by ~50-fold. Coupled with the SLICE method, our system consistently achieved >80% KO efficiency while preserving NK cell viability and effector function, providing a robust foundation for high-throughput functional genomics in primary NK cells.

Using this platform, our pilot kinome screen identified both essential and negative regulators of ex vivo NK cell proliferation. Depletion of canonical cell-cycle kinases (CDK6, TRRAP) and JAK3 validated the screen’s sensitivity, while enrichment of STK17B and IRAK3 revealed novel inhibitory mechanisms. Notably, STK17B KO enhanced NK cell proliferation under low IL-2 condition without compromising cytotoxicity, suggesting that STK17B may act as a limiting factor in NK cell homeostasis and expansion. Further dissection of STK17B signaling could inform strategies to increase NK-cell ex vivo manufacturing and potentially persistence under low IL-2 availability during adoptive cell therapy.

Our genome-wide degranulation screen uncovered canonical regulators, such as UNC13D, VAV1, RAPGEF1, PIK3CD, and ORAI1, confirming the importance of granule mobilization, calcium flux, and immune synapse formation. Functional validation pinpointed CCDC53, a subunit of WASH complex, as a negative regulator of NK cell degranulation. CCDC53 KO enhanced CD107a mobilization, perforin secretion, and in vitro cytotoxicity, supporting its role as a tunable regulator of granule trafficking. While CD107a was used as the primary indicator in our screens, other degranulation-associated markers can provide complementary insights into degranulation processes. IFN-γ expression also indicates NK cell activation, as it is synthesized de novo upon stimulation. In contrast, perforin and granzyme B, the main granule cargos, are pre-formed, stored in cytolytic granules, and secreted upon activation. Although their abundance may not directly reflect degranulation activity, intracellular staining for perforin and granzyme B staining can help study granule synthesis. However, practical considerations—such as signal sensitivity, the need for cell fixation and permeabilization, and larger starting cell numbers—may limit their use in high-throughput pooled formats.

The PGE2-resistance screen provided a complementary view of NK-cell regulation under immunosuppression. We recapitulated the expected role of the Gαs–cAMP axis (GNAS, PRKAR1A) and uncovered a broader role for the CRL5 complex (RNF7, UBE2F, CISH) in modulating IL-2 signaling. Mechanistically, UBE2F and RNF7 KO preserve IL-2 signaling by preventing CRL5-mediated degradation of phosphorylated JAK1 and JAK3. Consistent with this, UBE2F and RNF7 ablation enhanced NK cell cytotoxicity under PGE2 exposure. These observations support a model in which perturbation of CRL5 components preserves IL-2 response under immunosuppressive conditions. Overall, our results align with prior studies implicating PGE2- and CRL5-mediated suppression of cytokine signaling and immune cell functions7,8,58. Together, these findings position the CRL5 complex as a critical checkpoint, and targeting this pathway may synergize with cytokine support to bolster NK cell activity in the TME.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that our optimized CRISPR platform enables genome-wide discovery in primary NK cells and reveals genetic nodes that can be leveraged to improve proliferation, effector function, and resilience to suppression. Discovery of intracellular checkpoints (STK17B, CCDC53, CRL5 components) and signaling nodes (GNAS, PRKAR1A, RASA2) supports multiplex engineering approaches—e.g., triple KO of STK17B, CCDC53, and RNF7—to enhance proliferation, degranulation, and resistance to TME suppression. This platform is readily adaptable to other functional assays, including responses to tumor ligands, metabolic constraints or other immunomodulatory cues.

This study also highlights methodological considerations. Pooled loss-of-function screens predominantly capture genes with strong single-gene phenotypes and may underestimates functionally redundant, dosage sensitive or context-specific regulators. Donor variability reflects natural biological diversity in human NK cells, influences the magnitude of certain phenotypes (as seen in our functional assay) and contributes to some heterogeneity in results. Expanding future screens to larger or defined donor cohorts may help mitigate this issue and generalize these findings. In addition, our work focused on loss-of-function screens, and incorporating CRISPR activation, interference, or combinatorial perturbations will enable a more comprehensive interrogation of NK regulatory networks. Finally, our screens were performed in feeder-and cytokine-stimulated, ex vivo expanded NK cells. Although this expansion step is technically required to achieve the scale necessary for genome-wide CRISPR screening, ex vivo NK cells may not fully mirror in vivo naïve states. Nonetheless, the pathways identified here provide a framework for understanding NK-cell regulation under controlled conditions and offer testable hypotheses for future in vivo or clinical studies.

In summary, we present a technically optimized and scalable CRISPR screening platform for primary human NK cells and illustrate its utility across proliferation, degranulation, cytokine signaling, and anti-cancer activity. By enabling systematic functional genomic discovery, this resource provides a foundation for rational engineering of next-generation NK-cell therapies with enhanced potency and durability.

Methods

Reagents and antibodies

All chemicals were purchased from Merck and cell culture reagents from Thermo Fisher Scientific, unless specified otherwise. Antibodies used for flow cytometry and immunoblotting are listed in Supplementary Data 1.

Cell culture

HEK293T (# CRL-3216), K562 (# CCL-243), Capan-2 (# HTB-80), U-251 MG (# HTB-14), HCT116 (# CCL-247) and PANC-1 (# CRL-1469) cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and maintained according to the ATCC protocols. Briefly, HEK293T, Capan-2 and U-251 MG were maintained in the Complete DMEM medium containing DMEM with high glucose (Corning # 10-013-CM) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Corning # 35-010-CV), 25 mM HEPES (Gibco # 15630080), 1X GlutaMAX (Gibco # 35050061) and 1X penicillin-streptomycin (Pen-Strep, Gibco #15140122). K562 was maintained in RPMI-1640 ATCC modification (Gibco # A1049101) supplemented with 12.5% heat-inactivated FBS, 25 mM HEPES, 1X GlutaMAX, and 1X Pen-Strep. For routine passage, the adherent cells were dissociated by Trypsin-EDTA solution (Gibco # 25200072). The dissociated cells were diluted in fresh media at 1:4 to 1:8 ratio. Cell density and viability during routine passage were determined by Trypan blue staining in the Countess II cell counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All cell lines were maintained in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2 and routinely checked for Mycoplasma contamination using the EZ-PCR detection assay kit (Biological Industries, # 20-700-20). All cells were tested negative for mycoplasma contamination before any experiment. Genetically modified K562 was used as feeder cells to stimulate the ex vivo proliferation of primary NK cells. The modified K562 was generated by transduction with VSVG-LV expressing 4-1BBL and membrane-bound IL-21 (mIL-21)59. K562 feeder cells were expanded in RPMI medium containing 2 μg/ml of puromycin (Merck # SI-P8833) to maintain transgene expression and then irradiated at the dosage of 100 Gy to terminate cell proliferation. Irradiated K562 cells then were washed and used immediately for NK stimulation or cryopreserved at 3 × 106 cells/vial in liquid nitrogen for future use.

Gene KO by Cas9 RNP electroporation

Recombinant Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 protein was expressed and purified from Escherichia coli and single-guide RNA (sgRNA) were synthesized by in vitro transcription as described60. The sgRNA sequences and oligonucleotides for sgRNA synthesis are listed in Supplementary Data 2. Cas9 RNP electroporation in primary NK cells using Lonza 4D Nucleofector was detailed in Huang et al., 202133. Briefly, 40 pmol of Cas9 protein and 48 pmol of sgRNA were mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min to form Cas9 RNP. The RNP was then mixed with 4 × 105 primary NK cells in 20 μL of P3 buffer (Lonza # V4XP-3024). After gentle pipetting, the cell mixture was transferred into 16-well strip-format nucleofection cuvettes. Electroporation was performed using the Lonza 4D Nucleofector X-unit with CM137 pulse code. Immediately after electroporation, 100 μL of pre-warmed NK culture medium was added to each well to recover the cells at 37 °C for 15 min. Following recovery, NK cells were transferred to a 24-well culture plate containing 500 μL of medium per well and maintained under the standard culture condition.

ASCT1- and ASCT2-targeting sgRNAs were designed using CRISPR Design tool on Benchling website (www.benchling.com). Cas9 RNP electroporation in HEK293T consisted of 2 × 105 HEK293T cells in 20 μL of SF buffer (Lonza # V4XC-2024) and 2 μL of 20 μM Cas9 RNP (equivalent to 40 pmol final concentration). The cell mixture was then loaded into the 16-well nucleofection strips (Lonza) and electroporated by pulse code DS150. Immediately after electroporation, 100 μL of pre-warmed Complete RPMI 1640 medium was added to each well for cell recovery in a 37 °C incubator for 15 min. Subsequently, the cells were transferred to 24-well culture plates filled with 1 mL of pre-warmed Complete DMEM medium. ASCT1- and ASCT2 editing were analyzed 72 h post-electroporation.

For validation, candidate genes from the CRISPR screens were disrupted by Cas9 RNP electroporation using the top-ranking sgRNAs identified in the libraries and the protocol described above. Gene-KO NK cells were allowed to recover for 3–10 days, as noted in the figure legends, in Complete NK MACS medium supplemented with 100 U/mL IL-2 (Pepro Tech # 200-02) before functional analysis. No feeder cells were added to stimulate the gene-KO NK cells.

KO analysis by Sanger sequencing and ICE

KO efficiency was determined by Sanger sequencing and ICE analysis using the default parameters (https://ice.synthego.com). Briefly, KO cells were collected by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min and washed with DPBS once. Cell pellets were lysed by QuickExtraction solution (Lucigen # QE09050) at 65 °C for 15 min, 98 °C for 5 min and 4 °C for 10 min to extract the genomic DNA. One hundred ng of genomic DNA was used for PCR amplification of the target loci using KAPA HiFi HotStart PCR kit (Roche # KK2502) and the primer sets in Supplementary Data 2. After validated by DNA gel electrophoresis, the PCR product was purified by Zymoclean Gel DNA Recovery Kit (Zymo Research # D4008), eluted in H2O and subjected to Sanger sequencing at the Institute of Biomedical Sciences, Academia Sinica. The % indel was determined on the Synthego website by Inference of CRISPR Edits tool (ICE) using the default setting (https://www.synthego.com/products/bioinformatics/crispr-analysis).

ASCT1 and ASCT2 immunoblotting

ASCT1 and ASCT2 expression were validated by immunoblotting. The KO HEK293T cell pellet was dissolved in 50 μL of SDS loading dye containing 80 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8 (Fisher BioReagents # BP152-1), 2% β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma Aldrich # M3148), 4% SDS (Fisher BioReagents # BP166-500), 15% glycerol (Fisher BioReagents # G/0650/17) and 0.01% Orange G (Acros Organics # 229820250). The cell lysate was heated at 95 °C for 5 min, and resolved by electrophoresis in 10% SDS-PAGE gel at 100 V for 1.5 h. The proteins were transferred from SDS-PAGE to 0.2 μm PVDF membrane (Millipore # ISEQ85R) in Tris-Glycine Transfer buffer (Bio-Rad # 1610771) by Trans-Blot SD Semi-Dry Transfer (Bio-Rad) at 100 mA for 1.5 h. The membrane was incubated in TBST buffer consisting of 50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl (VWR # 0241) and 0.1% Tween-20 (Fisher BioReagents # BP337-500) at pH 7.6 with 5% (w/v) skim milk (BD Biosciences # 232100) for 1 h at room temperature. After blocking, the membrane was incubated with the primary antibodies in TBST with 5% skim milk overnight at 4 °C. The membrane was washed three times with TBST and incubated with the secondary antibodies in TBST with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature. The membrane was washed three times with TBST. The target proteins were visualized by Western Lightning PLUS-ECL (Perkin Elmer # NEL105001EA) or Ultra kit (Perkin Elmer # NEL113001EA). The images were acquired by Azure Biosystems C300 or Thermo Fisher Scientific iBright FL1000, and processed by ImageJ version 1.54 g.

Flow cytometry and FACS

Flow cytometry was performed on CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter) at the Flow cytometry core facility in the Institute of Biological Chemistry, Academia Sinica. NK cells were harvested by centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 min and washed once with 1 mL of ice-cold Flow buffer consisting of DPBS (Corning # 21-031-CM) supplemented with 2% FBS, 25 mM HEPES, and 0.5 mM EDTA (VWR # 0105-1KG). For surface protein detection, the cells were stained with antibody solution at the manufacturer’s recommended ratio in the dark on ice for 15 min. After staining, the cells were washed with 1 mL of Flow buffer, pelleted at 500 × g for 5 min, resuspended in 200 μL of Flow buffer, and transferred to a 5 mL Falcon polystyrene flow tube with a cell strainer snap cap (Corning # 352325). Modified K562 feeder cells were prepared similarly to monitor the expression the 4-1BBL and mIL-21. The ASCT1- and ASCT2-KO HEK293T cells were single cell-sorted by FACSAria III FACS system (BD Biosciences) and FACSDiva version 8.0.3 software (BD Biosciences) at the flow cytometry core facility in the Institute of Biomedical Sciences, Academia Sinica. The isolated cells were collected in 96-well plates at one cell per well in 200 μL of Complete DMEM medium and expanded by the standard culture method. The expanded cells were validated for the ablation of ASCT1 and ASCT2 protein expression by immunoblotting before storing in liquid nitrogen. All data were analyzed using FlowJo version 10.6.2 (BD Biosciences) and CytExpert version 2.3 (Beckman Coulter).

Construction of BaEVRless envelope plasmid

BaEVRless envelope plasmid was generated by replacing the VSV-G gene in pMD2.G plasmid (Addgene #12259) with the BaEVRless sequence reported previously10. The PCR primers are listed in Supplementary Data 3. Briefly, the BaEVRless gene was codon-optimized and synthesized as a gBlock fragment from IDT DNA. The BaEVRless sequence was amplified from the gBlock by KAPA HiFi HotStart PCR kit (Roche) using primer 1 and 2. The pMD2.G vector backbone without the VSV-G gene was amplified by primer 3 and 4. The PCR products were resolved by DNA gel electrophoresis, extracted by QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen # 28106), and ligated by NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly (NEB # E2621S) per the manufacturer’s protocol. The plasmid was transformed and maintained in E. coli Stbl3 strain (Thermo Fisher Scientific # C737303) and purified using Qiagen plasmid extraction kit. All plasmids were validated by Sanger Sequencing in DNA Sequencing Core Facility at the Institute of Biomedical Sciences at Academia Sinica. BaEVRless plasmid map (pMD2.G_BaEVRless) is attached as Genbank file in Supplementary Data 11. Plasmids generated in this study are available by contacting the corresponding author.

Construction of GFP lentiviral plasmid

Lentiviral transfer plasmids encoding turboGFP were constructed into the pHR vector (modified from Addgene #79125) by NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly (NEB) using the same approach as BaEVRless plasmid. The PCR primers are listed in Supplementary Data 3. The PCR reactions were performed using KAPA HiFi HotStart PCR kit (Roche). Briefly, to make pHR_turboGFP plasmid, the turbogfp gene was amplified from pmaxGFP plasmid (Addgene # 177825) using primer 5 and 6, and ligated with the pHR vector amplified by primer 7 and 8. The plasmid was validated by Sanger sequencing. The plasmid map is attached as GenBank file in Supplementary Data 12.

Construction of sgRNA lentiviral plasmids for SLICE

sgRNAs targeting the CD96 and CD226 genes were ligated into pLAS2W.Ppuro lentiviral transfer plasmid (C6 RNAiCore facility) by NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly (NEB). The PCR reactions were performed using KAPA HiFi HotStart PCR kit and the primers are listed in Supplementary Data 3. Briefly, the CD96-sgRNA expression cassette consisting of U6 promoter, spacer sequence and sgRNA scaffold was amplified from CD96-sgRNA Dual Expressing Plasmid61 using primer 9 and 10, and assembled with pLAS2W.Ppuro vector amplified by primer 11 and 12. The CD226-sgRNA cassette was amplified from CD96-sgRNA Dual Expressing Plasmid using primer 9 and 13 and assembled with pLAS2W.Ppuro vector amplified by primer 11 and 14. The CD226-targeting sgRNA spacer sequence was encoded in primer 9. All plasmids were validated by Sanger sequencing. The plasmid maps are attached as GenBank files in Supplementary Data 13 and 14.

Lentiviral production

HEK293T and the ASCT KO cells were maintained as described above. The cells were passaged every 2 − 3 days at the confluency of 80 − 90%. To ensure consistent and high lentiviral titer, it is recommended not to use cells that have been passaged more than 30 times. On day 0, 8.5 − 9.0 × 106 cells were seeded on a 10 cm dish in 10 mL of lentiviral packaging medium consisting of Opti-MEM medium (Gibco # 22600134) with 5% heat-inactivated FBS, 1X GlutaMAX, 1X sodium pyruvate (Gibco # 11360070) and 1X MEM non-essential amino acids solution (Gibco # 11140035). Approximately 16 − 18 h after seeding, the cells were transfected with 8 μg of transfer plasmid, 7 μg of packaging plasmid pCMVdeltaR8.91 (C6 RNAiCore facility), 3 μg of envelope plasmid pMD2.G (Addgene #12259) or pMD2.G_BaEVRless (this study) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen # L3000015) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. The medium was removed 6 h after transfection, and replenished with fresh lentiviral packaging medium supplemented with IP6. The 30 mM stock IP6 solution was prepared by dissolving IP6 powder (Merck # P8810) in DPBS. IP6 solution was adjusted to pH 7, filter-sterilized and stored at 4 °C. IP6 was added to the packaging medium at the concentrations indicated in Supplementary Fig. 2g or at the optimal concentration of 150 μM. At 48–50 h post transfection, the lentivirus-containing medium was collected in a 15 mL conical tube and centrifuged at 650 × g for 5 min to remove cell debris. The supernatant was passed through low-protein binding 0.45 µm syringe filter (Millipore # SLHPR33RB). The lentivirus was purified by two different methods for comparison. In the first method, lentivirus was precipitated from the supernatant using Lenti-X Concentrator (Takara # 631232) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In the second method, lentivirus was pelleted by centrifugation in 20% sucrose cushion at 4500 × g at 4 °C for 20 h. In both methods, the lentivirus pellet was suspended at 1/50 volume of the cell culture supernatant in the basal NK MACS medium (Miltenyi # 130-114-429) or Opti-MEM. The sgRNA-encoding lentivirus was prepared by the same method. The purified lentivirus was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen as aliquots and store at −80 °C.

For the lentiviral vectors carrying the gfp reporter gene, functional titer of lentivirus was determined by transducing HEK293T cells and measuring transduction efficiency by flow cytometry based on GFP expression. Briefly, 5 × 104 HEK293T cells were seeded in 500 μL of Complete DMEM medium containing 8 μg/mL of polybrene in 24-well plate. Different volumes of lentivirus ranging from 0.1 to 5 μL were added to transduce HEK293T cells for 2 days. Gene expression was determined by flow cytometry and functional titer was calculated based on the samples that gave rise to 10 − 30% transduction efficiency following Equation (1) below:

For the vectors harboring puromycin resistance gene, including those for SLICE-KO and all CRISPR sgRNA libraries, the transduced HEK293T cells were treated with 2 μg/mL of puromycin on day 2 after transduction. On day 4, 100% of non-transduced cells should be killed by puromycin. Transduction efficiency was determined based on the number of live cells in the transduced group by Trypan Blue staining (Gibco # 15250061) compared to the cell number in non-transduced group without puromycin treatment. Functional titer was calculated from the samples that gave rise to 30% transduction efficiency.

Imaging analysis of BaEVRless-induced syncytia

The parental, ASCT1-KO (clone #3) and ASCT2-KO (clone #8) HEK293T cells were transfected by Lipofectamine 3000 with lentiviral packaging plasmids encoding the VSV-G or BaEVRless envelope gene and a transfer plasmid encoding the turbogfp gene as described above. No IP6 was added. At 48 hours post transfection, bright-field and green fluorescent images of the parental cells and KO clones were taken on an Olympus CKX41 inverted microscope and processed by ImageJ.

Ex vivo expansion of primary NK cells

CD3 negatively enriched and cryopreserved human peripheral blood NK cells were purchased from Lonza and HemaCare. All experiments on human cells were conducted according to the human experimental guidelines approved by the Institutional Review Board on Biomedical Science Research, Academia Sinica (case # AS-IRB01-23065N). It will not be necessary nor possible to collect individual informed consents. On day 0, the cryopreserved NK cells were thawed in a 37 °C water bath with gentle agitation until no ice was observed inside the tube. NK cells were gently transferred to a 15 mL conical tube containing 8 mL of 4 °C RPMI 1640 (ATCC modification) medium supplemented with 10 U/mL DNaseI (Merck # 10104159001). The cryovial was rinsed with 1 mL of the RPMI medium to transfer the remaining NK cells to the 15-mL tube. After centrifugation at 400 × g for 10 min, the cell pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of prewarmed Complete NK MACS medium containing NK MACS Medium, 5% EliteGro-Adv human plate lysate (EliteCell # EPA-500), 1% GlutaMAX, and 1% Pen-Strep. The medium was also supplemented with 10 U/ml of DNaseI and 2 ng/mL of recombinant IL-15 (Peprotech # 200-15). The cell suspension was transferred to a T25 flask (Corning # 430639) and maintained in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. On day 1, NK cells were collected from the flask and the cell density was determined to set up the feeder-dependent expansion. Approximately 5 × 106 NK cells were co-cultured with 1 × 107 of the modified and irradiated K562 feeder cells in 40 mL of Complete NK MACS medium supplemented with 100 U/mL of recombinant IL-2 in a T75 flask (Corning # 430641U). The final NK cell density was 1.25 × 105 cells/mL in 40 mL of medium. On day 3 and 5, NK cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min. Twenty mL of the supernatant was carefully removed from the top layer and replaced with 20 mL of fresh Complete NK MACS medium with 100 U/mL IL-2. NK cells were gently resuspended and transferred back to the flask. On day 7, NK cells were collected by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min, washed once with DPBS and cryopreserved in CryoStor CS10 (STEMCELL # 7930) at 3 × 106 NK cells per vial for future use. The purity of primary NK cells was analyzed by flow cytometry based on CD3 and CD56 expression.

For CRISPR screens, the expanded NK cells were thawed and re-stimulated with K562 feeder cells at ratio 1:1 as described above. NK cells were allowed to recover under the standard culture condition for 3 days prior to CRISPR screens. For gene validation and functional assays, NK cells were maintained at a concentration below 3 × 106 cells/mL and were passaged every 2 − 3 days by dilution in fresh Complete NK MACS medium.

NK cell transduction

About 5 × 104 of primary NK cells were suspended in 100 μL of fresh Complete NK MACS medium per well of the 96-well flat-bottom plate. Lentivirus was added at the MOI indicated in the experiment. Polybrene, also known as hexadimethrine bromide (Merck # H9268), was dissolved in H2O to 8 mg/mL and sterilized by passing through a 0.2-μm filter. Polybrene solution was stored at –80 °C as aliquots to prevent repeated freezing and thawing. Polybrene was supplemented in the medium at concentrations as specified in Supplementary Fig. 3d, f or at the optimal concentration of 4 μg/mL for all other experiments. Spinfection was performed at 1200 × g for 90 min at 32 °C. Afterward, the cells were resuspended by gentle pipetting and incubated at 37 °C overnight. One day after transduction, the cells were washed and resuspended in 100 μL fresh Complete NK MACS medium. The transduced cells were analyzed by flow cytometry to determine the transduction efficiency at the specified time.

Respiration assay

Cellular respiration was determined by WST-1 assay (Abcam # ab65473) using the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, parental and the ASCT2-KO HEK293T cells were seeded in a 12-well plate at 1 × 104 cells in 1 mL of Complete DMEM medium per well, and set up three wells per cell type. After one day of incubation, 100 µL of the medium was removed from each well, and 100 μL of WST-1 reagent was gently added to the cell culture to avoid disturbance of the cells. The plate was gently rocked to evenly mix WST-1 reagent with the culture medium, and then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After incubation, 100 μL of culture supernatant was transferred to a transparent 96-well plate and the absorbance at 440 nm and 650 nm (reference) was measured in Infinite M1000 Pro microplate reader (Tecan).

Kinome CRISPR KO screen for NK cell proliferation

The kinome CRISPR KO screen was performed in primary NK cells using the SLICE (sgRNA lentiviral infection with Cas9 protein electroporation) approach developed for primary T cells9. Human Kinome CRISPR KO Library was from Addgene (#1000000082) and prepared as described14. The kinome CRISPR sgRNA library was packaged into BaEV-LV in the ASCT2-KO HEK293T cells using our standard protocol. The viral titer was determined in HEK293T cells by puromycin selection as described above. On day 0, 2 × 107 of the expanded NK cells were transduced with the kinome-encoding BaEV-LV at MOI 0.3. Recombinant Cas9 protein was prepared as detailed in Lin et al., 202260. At 24 h after transduction (day 1), Cas9 was electroporated into NK cells using Lonza 4D nucleofection system and pulse code CM137 in 100-μL electroporation cuvettes. The reaction contained 400 pmol of Cas9 protein and 1 × 107 of the transduced NK cells in 100 μL of P3 solution (Lonza). Immediately after electroporation, 500 μL of prewarmed Complete NK MACS medium was added directly into the cuvette to recover the NK cells for 15 min at 37 °C. Next, NK cells were transferred to a T25 flask at 1 × 106 cells/mL, and incubated at 37 °C incubator. After 24 h (day 2), the NK cells were collected by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min, and resuspended in the Complete NK MACS medium supplemented with 1 µg/mL of puromycin to select for the sgRNA-transduced cells for 48 h. On day 4, NK cells were collected by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min, washed once with DPBS, and resuspended in the puromycin-free Complete NK MACS medium. NK cells were cultured for 6 more days to allow cell proliferation and protein turnover, during which fresh medium was added every 2–3 days to maintain cell density at 5 × 105 to 2 × 106 cells/mL. The cell suspension was split into multiple flasks when needed. Total cell number was maintained at 6 × 106 cells to ensure >1000X sgRNA coverage. On day 10, the surviving NK cells were collected for genomic DNA extraction, sgRNA amplification and NGS to determine the sgRNA representation in the NK cell population.

Genome-wide CRISPR KO screen for NK cell degranulation

Genome-wide KO screens were performed using GeCKO library set A and B from Addgene (#1000000049). GeCKO sgRNA library set A and B were individually packaged into BaEV-LV using our standard protocol, and the viral titers were determined as for the kinome library. The procedure for genome-wide KO screen was similar to the kinome screen with some modifications. The experimental workflow and timeline are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 8a. On day 0, the expanded NK cells were divided into two groups of approximately 1.2 × 108 cells and were transduced separately with GeCKO library set A- or B-encoding BaEV-LV at MOI 0.3 to ensure >500X coverage. On day 1, NK cells were then electroporated with Cas9 protein to initiate SLICE KO. On day 2, NK cells were selected with puromycin for 48 h. On day 4, NK cells were collected by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min, washed once with DPBS, and resuspended in the puromycin-free Complete NK MACS medium. Cells treated with the two library sets were maintained and processed independently throughout the screening assay. On day 13, 2 × 107 of NK cells were collected as “GeCKO-edited cells” for NGS analysis. On day 14, NK cells were co-cultured with HCT116 cells to stimulate degranulation. In a T75 flask, 4 × 107 NK cells were incubated with 4 × 107 HCT116 in 40 mL of Complete DMEM medium without IL-2 and in the presence of 100 μL PE-conjugated anti-CD107a (Supplementary Data 1) to a final dilution factor of 1/400. After one hour of co-culture, Brefeldin A (BioLegend # 420601) and Monesin solution (BioLegend # 420701) were added at 1X to the cells and incubated for two more hours. Cells were washed once with DPBS and stained with APC-conjugated anti-CD56 antibody (Supplementary Data 1) at a dilution factor of 1/100 for 20 min on ice. Stained cells were resuspended in Flow buffer containing DAPI (BioLegend # 422801) as viability dye and sorted by FACS. Viable NK cells were separated from HCT116 by gating for DAPI−CD56+ and were further sorted by CD107a expression into CD107ahigh and CD107alow cells. The cell sorting procedure and gating strategy for three independent donors are shown in Supplementary Fig. 8b, c. Gating parameters were adjusted slightly for each donor to isolate approximately 2 × 106 NK cells of each CD107a-defined population. Sorting was completed within one hour to preserve cell integrity. The cells were harvested in Complete NK MACS medium, pelleted by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min and washed once with DPBS. Cell pellets were frozen and stored at –80 °C for subsequent genomic DNA extraction, sgRNA amplification and NGS analysis.

Genome-wide CRISPR KO screen for PGE2-mediated immunosuppression

PGE2 screen followed the same BaEV-LV transduction and puromycin selection as GeCKO screen. The procedure is outlined in Supplementary Fig. 8a. On day 13, 4 × 107 of SLICE-KO NK cells were incubated with 100 ng/mL of PGE2 (Tocris Bioscience # 2296) for 24 h. On day 14, the PGE2-treated cells were washed once with DPBS and incubated with HCT116 to stimulate degranulation as described above. The gating strategy for CD107ahigh population for three independent donors are shown in Supplementary Fig. 11b. Gating parameters were adjusted slightly for each donor to isolate approximately 1 × 106 of CD107ahigh NK cells from each library set as “PGE2+CD107ahigh” cells for NGS. Sorting was completed within one hour to preserve cell integrity.

Preparation of genomic DNA for NGS

The genomic DNA of NK cells were purified using Quick DNA Miniprep Plus kit (Zymo Research # D4069) according to manufacturer’s protocol. Amplification and bar-coding of the sgRNA sequences from genomic DNA were performed using a two-step PCR reaction as described9. The PCR primers are listed in Supplementary Data 4. The reaction consisted of 50 μL of NEBNext High Fidelity 2X PCR Master Mix (NEB # M0541L), 4 μg of genomic DNA, 2.5 μL each of the 10 μM NG-Lib-Fwd and NGS-Lib-KO-Rev primers, and water to 100 μL total. The thermocycler setting was: 3 min at 98 °C, followed by 10 s at 98 °C, 10 s at 66 °C, 25 s at 72 °C, for 23 cycles; and a final 2 min extension at 72 °C. The PCR amplicons were purified by DNA gel electrophoresis and extracted using Zymoclean Gel DNA Recovery kit. The concentration and size of the amplicons were determined by Qubit DNA quantification (Thermo Fisher Scientific # Q32851) and Fragment Analyzer (Agilent). The Nextera XT Index Kit v2 (Illumina # FC-131-2001) was used to add the dual-barcoded adaptors. The molar concentration of the amplicon from each sample was normalized and then sequenced on a NextSeq 500/550 instrument (Illumina). The data were processed using HiSeq Analysis Software version 0.9. The purified CRISPR KO Library plasmids were also PCR-amplified and sequenced using the same procedure to verify the integrity of sgRNA library.

Synthesis of custom sgRNA library

A series of filters were implemented to select the gene candidates for custom library. First, the top positive ranked genes were chosen from the GeCKO screening results with P-value < 0.05 and FDR < 0.4. Next, all miRNA genes and genes with low expression levels in human primary NK cells were removed. Endogenous gene expression in human NK cells was analyzed based on the RNA Seq data from Levy et al., 202162. Genes with average TPM ≤ 2.0 were considered low expression and were removed. Genes without RNA Seq data were also removed. Enriched gene list was then further trimmed down by removing genes whose negative rank was higher than positive rank. Finally, we selected 1115 genes for custom library as listed in Supplementary Data 5. A custom sgRNA library was designed by extracting all sgRNAs from the Bassik library (Addgene #101926 to 101934) corresponding to our gene list. Non-targeting sgRNAs (451) and safe-targeting sgRNAs (454) were added as controls. In addition, sgRNAs targeting known positive regulators of degranulation (LAMP1, CD226, ITGAL, PIK3CD and UNC13D) were included as depletion controls. Each gene was targeted by ~10 sgRNAs. The final library set consisted of 11981 sgRNAs as listed in Supplementary Data 5.

The custom sgRNA library was constructed using a protocol previously described63. Briefly, single-stranded DNA oligonucleotide pool, encoding the sgRNA sequences, was obtained from Twist Biosciences. The sgRNA sequences were amplified as per manufacturer’s protocol using the following primers:

Forward: 5’-GTAACTTGAAAGTATTTCGATTTCTTGGCTTTATATATCTTGTGGAAAGGACGAAACACC-3’ Reverse: 5’- ACTTTTTCAAGTTGATAACGGACTAGCCTTATTTTAACTTGCTATTTCTAGCTCTAAAAC-3’

The PCR-amplified DNA was resolved by electrophoresis in 2% agarose gel and extracted by Zymoclean Gel DNA Recovery Kit. Purified DNA fragments were cloned into lentiGuide-Puro vector (Addgene #52963) by NEB HiFi DNA Assembly. The assembled DNA was precipitated by isopropanol, washed twice with 70% ethanol, and dissolved in TE buffer. The purified DNA was electroporated into ElectroMax Stbl4 competent cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific # 11635018) for plasmid library amplification. Multiple electroporation reactions were conducted to ensure > 200X coverage of the custom library size. Plasmid DNA was extracted using ZymoPURE Plasmid Midiprep Kit (Zymo Research # D4201) and library integrity was assessed by NGS. Custom screening was performed in three independent NK donor cells using the same procedure as described in GeCKO screens for degranulation under normal culture condition. At least 2 × 107 custom library-edited NK cells were used for degranulation assay and approximately 1 × 106 cells from each CD107ahigh and CD107alow population were isolated by FACS for NGS analysis. The cell sorting data are shown in Supplementary Fig. 8d.

Bioinformatic analysis of CRISPR KO screens

The enriched and depleted genes in the kinome screen were identified using the MAGeCK software version 0.5.964. The read count table of each sgRNA was determined from raw fastq sequencing files by the default MAGeCK “count” module. The “–trim-5” parameter was used to retrieve sgRNA with offset design during the count table construction. To obtain gene level enrichment, the MAGeCK “test” module was performed with default settings using the alpha-robust rank aggregation algorithm. For the NK cell degranulation screening under normal condition in both GeCKO library and the custom library, the “–paired” argument is applied to increase statistical power. The non-targeting control sgRNA in each library was used to normalize the size factor between each donor. The distribution of individual sgRNA from the top-ranking genes is visualized using R. The output of gene and sgRNA level enrichment from MAGeCK analysis are included in Supplementary Data 6–10. The original NGS data files are available at the NCBI Sequence Read Archive: kinome screen (PRJNA1078135), GeCKO degranulation and PGE2 screens (PRJNA1256701), and secondary screen using custom sgRNA library (PRJNA1263446).

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA, version 4.3.3) using the GSEAPreranked tool, as described by Subramanian et al., 2005. Log-fold change (LFC) values from gene-level MAGeCK analysis were used as input for ranking. KEGG and Reactome pathway gene sets (Homo sapiens) were used as references, with only those containing between 5 and 500 genes included. Enrichment scores were calculated using 1000 permutations to generate a robust null distribution. Pathways with nominal p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Immunoblotting for KO validation

Cells were pelleted and lysed in SDS lysis buffer (125 mM Tris pH 6.8, 4% SDS, 20% glycerol, 10% β-mercaptoethanol, 0.01% Orange G) and denatured at 95 °C for 10 min. Lysates were resolved by electrophoresis on 4–20% SDS polyacrylamide gels (OmicsBio # P20420-015) and transferred onto 0.2 μm PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature with 5% (w/v) skim milk in TBST (50 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20, pH 7.6), except for FAM49B and GNAS, which were blocked with 0.5% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA; GoldBio # A-420-50). Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by three washes in TBST and incubation with HRP-conjugated anti-IgG secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Antibody dilution ratios are specified in Supplementary Data 1. Signals were detected using Thermo iBright FL 1500 with Western Lightning PLUS-ECL for β-actin and Western Lightning Ultra (Perkin Elmer/Revvity # NEL113001EA) for other proteins. For re-probing, membranes were stripped with RapidStrip Western Blot Stripping Buffer (OmicsBio # AR0001) for 10 minutes at room temperature, washed twice with TBST, and re-incubated with antibodies as described above. All antibodies were diluted in 5% skim milk in TBST except anti-FAM49B and anti-GNAS, which were diluted in 0.5% BSA in TBST. β-actin served as the loading control and was run on the same blot. After probing for target proteins, the membrane was stripped and reprobed for β-actin. Semi-quantitative analysis of SKP1 and FOXO1 protein levels from the immunoblot was performed using ImageJ. SKP1 and FOXO1 band intensities were first normalized to their matching β-actin controls.

Cell proliferation measurements

NK cell proliferation was assessed using Precision Cell Count Beads (BioLegend # 424902) as previously described33 and also by carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) staining. CFSE powder (BioLegend # 423801) was dissolved in 36 μL of DMSO (Corning # 25-950-CQC) to prepare 5 mM stock solution and diluted in DPBS to 0.5 μM working solution. Four days post Cas9 RNP electroporation, 4 × 105 NK cells were collected by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min, resuspended in 500 μL of 0.5 μM CFSE, and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. The reaction was quenched with 1 mL DPBS, and cells were centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and resuspended in pre-warmed Complete NK MACS medium. Half of the cells were analyzed immediately (Day 0) by flow cytometry (FITC channel) to measure CFSE intensity. The remaining cells were cultured for 3 days before analysis to assess proliferation by CFSE dilution.

Degranulation assay and intracellular IFN-γ staining

NK cells were rested for 10 days after gene KO to allow for turnover of the target protein. For activation, 1 × 105 NK cells were co-cultured with 1 × 105 HCT116 cells in 100 μL of Complete DMEM containing 20 μg/mL anti-CD107a antibody. Plates were centrifuged at 200 × g for 3 min to promote cell contact and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Monensin and Brefeldin A were then added at 1X final concentration to block endosomal recycling and Golgi transport, followed by a 2-hour incubation at 37 °C. For intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using 100 μL of Cyto-Fast Fix/Perm Buffer Set (BioLegend # 426803) at room temperature for 20 minutes, washed twice with Cyto-Fast Perm Wash Solution, and stained with 0.5 μL each of anti-IFN-γ and anti-CD56 antibodies (Supplementary Data 1) in 50 μL of Perm Wash Solution. After a 20 min incubation, cells were centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 minutes, resuspended in 100 μL flow buffer, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Calcein-AM cytotoxicity assay

This assay was performed as described in Huang et al., 202165. Briefly, cancer cells were dissociated by Trypsin-EDTA, neutralized by Complete DMEM, pelleted at 200 × g for 3 min and washed once with DPBS. Cells were then resuspended at 1 × 106 cells/mL in Complete DMEM containing 1.6 μL of 10 μM Calcein-AM (BioLegend # 425201) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After staining, cells were washed three times with DPBS and adjusted to 1 × 105 cells/mL in RPMI-1640 ATCC modification. NK cells were pelleted at 300 × g for 5 min, resuspended in RPMI-1640, and serially diluted in U-bottom 96-well plates to achieve desired effector-to-target (E:T) ratios. Stained cancer cells (100 μL) were added to each well. Plates were centrifuged at 120 × g for 3 min to promote cell contact, then incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Thirty minutes before analysis, 2% Triton X-100 (Fisher BioReagents # BP151-500) was added to the target cell-only and medium-only control groups to determine maximal and background release. After co-culture, plates were centrifuged at 120 × g for 3 min, and 100 μL of supernatant was transferred to a 96-well Opti-plate (Basic Life # BL6051) for fluorescence measurement (488/520 nm) using an Infinite M1000 pro (Tecan). Luminex multiplex immunoassay

CCDC53 gene was disrupted by Cas9 RNP electroporation as described, and the KO NK cells were recovered in Complete NK MACS medium supplemented with 100 U/mL of IL-2. On Days 3 and 10 post editing, 5 × 104 CCDC53-KO NK cells and 1 × 105 HCT116 cells were co-cultured in 200 μL of Complete DMEM in 96-well U-bottom plates (TPP AG # 92097). Plates were centrifuged at 120 × g for 3 min to promote cell contact and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 3 h . After incubation, plates were centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and the supernatants were collected and stored at -80 °C until analysis. Multiplex immunoassays were performed using the Luminex 200 system (Luminex) and xPONENT version 4.3 software (Luminex) at the Inflammation Core Facility, Institute of Biomedical Sciences, Academia Sinica, supported by the Academia Sinica Core Facility and Innovative Instrument Project (AS-CFII-113-A9). Frozen supernatants were thawed, mixed well, centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 5 min to remove debris, and then diluted 1:4 with PBS containing 1% BSA. Levels of TNF, IFN-γ, perforin and granzyme B were quantified using antibody-conjugated magnetic beads. Briefly, 50 μL of diluted sample, standard, or control was incubated with pre-mixed capture beads for 2 h on an orbital shaker at room temperature, followed by three washes and incubation with 40 μL of biotinylated detection antibody for 1 h. After washing, 50 μL of streptavidin–phycoerythrin (BD Pharmingen # 554061) was added and incubated for 30 min at room temperature, protected from light. Fluorescence intensity was measured on the Luminex 200 instrument, and cytokine concentrations were determined from standard curves.

xCELLigence real-time cell analysis

Long-term cytotoxicity of gene-KO NK cells was evaluated using xCELLigence real-time cell analysis (RTCA) system and RTCA software Pro version 2.1.0 (ACEA Biosciences), a label-free, impedance-based platform that continuously monitors cell proliferation and viability. HCT116 cells were seeded at 1 × 105 cells per well in 100 μL DMEM without IL-2 into 16-well E-plates equipped with microelectrodes (ACEA Biosciences) and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for cancer cell attachment and proliferation. Electrical impedance changes, recorded as cell index values, were recorded every 15 min to monitor cancer cell growth. After 24 h, 5 × 104 or 1 × 105 NK cells in 100 μL DMEM supplemented with 200 U/mL IL-2 were added to each well to achieve an E:T ratio of 1:2 or 1:1, respectively. Cytotoxicity was monitored for an additional ~72 h with data acquired at 15-minute intervals. Cell index values were normalized to the time point immediately before NK cell addition. Each condition was assayed in technical duplicates and repeated independently with NK cells from three donors. PANC-1 assays were performed similarly with an adjusted E:T ratio. Briefly, 2 × 104 PANC-1 cells were seeded per well in 100 μL RPMI without IL-2, and after 24 h, 1 × 104 NK cells in 100 μL RPMI containing 200 U/mL IL-2 were added to achieve an E:T ratio of 1:2. Percent cancer lysis was calculated using Equation (3): % cancer lysis = (1−En/Tn) × 100, where En represents the cell index of the NK cell-containing well and Tn represents that of the cancer-only control at the same time point.

Analysis of JAK-STAT signaling

On day 10 post editing, approximately 2 × 106 gene-KO NK cells were washed and cultured overnight in 2 mL of fresh Complete NK MACS medium supplemented with 100 U/mL IL-2, with or without 100 ng/mL PGE2. On day 11, the cells were washed twice with DPBS and incubated in basal NK MACS medium without IL-2 for 4 h at 37 °C to reset JAK and STAT phosphorylation to baseline. Starved cells were then pulse-stimulated with 1 mL Complete NK MACS medium containing 500 U/mL IL-2 for 30 min, washed with DPBS to remove IL-2, resuspended in basal NK MACS medium, and maintained at 37 °C for the indicated durations (0, 30, 90 and 120 min) to assess phosphorylated JAK1, JAK3, and STAT5 levels. At each time points, cells were pelleted, washed once with DPBS, and lysed immediately in 40 μL of SDS lysis buffer with Orange G. Lysates were denatured at 95 °C for 10 min and stored at −80 °C until subsequent SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting analyses as described above.

Tumoroid assay

Target cells (HCT116 or PANC-1) were transduced with a lentiviral vector encoding nuclear-localized GFP, and GFP⁺ populations were enriched by FACS using FACSAria III (BD Biosciences) at the Flow Cytometry Core Facility, Institute of Biomedical Sciences, Academia Sinica. To generate tumor spheroids, GFP⁺ target cells were dissociated with Trypsin-EDTA, counted, and seeded into 96-well U-bottom ultra-low attachment plates (Corning # 7007) in Complete DMEM. A total of 3000 GFP⁺ HCT116 or 20,000 GFP⁺ PANC-1 cells were plated in 50 μL medium per well, centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 48–72 h to allow spheroid formation. Once spheroids were established, gene-KO NK cells were added in 100 μL of Complete DMEM supplemented with 300 U/mL IL-2, with or without 100 ng/mL PGE2, at E:T ratios indicated in the figure legends. Plates were centrifuged at 130 × g for 10 min to promote NK–tumor contact and imaged using a scanR High-Content Screening Station (Olympus) at 30-minute intervals for 5 days. Image acquisition and analysis were performed with scanR Acquisition and Analysis version 3.4.1 (Olympus), and GFP fluorescence intensity of tumor spheroids was quantified to assess NK cell–mediated cytotoxicity.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were performed by GraphPad Prism 10 using statistical methods specified in the figure legends. Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) with the number of independent donor cells indicated. All statistical tests are two-tailed and test methods are indicated in figure legends. For RTCA and tumoroid assays, technical replicates were averaged prior to analysis, and NK cells from each donor were treated as one biological replicate. Data were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with time and treatment as within-subject factors, followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test to compare treatment groups with the designated control at the time points specified in the figures. P-values ≤ 0.05 were considered significant.
