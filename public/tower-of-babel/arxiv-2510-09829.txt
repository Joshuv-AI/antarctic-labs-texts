Spectrum of the wave equation with Dirac damping on a compact star graph
Mikuláš Kučera

Abstract
. We consider the wave equation with a distributional Dirac damping and Dirichlet boundary conditions on a compact interval. It is shown that the spectrum of the corresponding wave operator is fully determined by zeroes of an entire function. Consequently, a considerable change of spectral properties is shown for certain critical values of the damping parameter. We also derive a definitive criterion for the Riesz basis property of the root vectors for an arbitrary placement of a complex-valued Dirac damping. Finally, we consider a generalisation of the problem for compact star graphs and provide insight into the essence of the critical damping constant.



1.
Introduction

To model playing harmonics on a string, in
[
2
]
, Bamberger, Rauch, and Taylor introduced the following wave equation:

(1.1)

u_{tt}(x,t)-u_{xx}(x,t)+\alpha\delta(x-a)u_{t}(x,t)=0,\quad x\in[0,\pi],\quad t\geqslant 0,

where
u:\left[0,\pi\right]\times[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
is the displacement of the string,
a\in\mathbb{(}0,\pi)
, subject to the Dirichlet boundary conditions

u(0,t)=0=u(\pi,t),\quad t\geqslant 0.

By detailed analysis of the contraction semigroup of the corresponding wave operator acting on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\dot{H}_{0}^{1}(0,\pi)\times L^{2}(0,\pi)
, the authors show that in the case of central placement
a=\pi/2
, the optimal damping constant (i.e. such that ensures the fastest decay of non-harmonic modes) is
\alpha=2
.

Further research was carried out in
[
3
]
by Cox and Henrot. Using the ‘shooting function’ method, they characterised the eigenvalues as roots of an entire function. It was also established that in the special case
a=p\pi/q
, with
p
and
q
being coprime integers, and
\alpha\in[0,+\infty)\setminus\left\{2\right\}
, the root vectors comprise a Riesz basis for
\mathcal{H}
. However, the analysis remained incomplete for
\alpha=2
and arbitrary placement of the damping as well as a general complex damping
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
.

This paper aims to solidify the ‘shooting function’ method. Reasoning with the poles of resolvent in the spirit of
[
1
]
, it is shown that algebraic multiplicities of eigenvalues are exactly their multiplicities as roots of the function in question. For rational placement of the damping, this characterisation consequently allows us to determine whether or not the root vectors form a Riesz basis in
\mathcal{H}
. The importance of this result lies in the fact that it enables the simple spectral solution of
[
1
]
to the optimal damping problem proposed in
[
2
]
.

Similarly to the non-compact star graph model of Krejčiřík and Royer
[
5
]
, using a simple symmetry observation, we extend the results to admit arbitrary complex Dirac dampings. The generalisation for an unrestricted placement is then achieved using a result of Krejčiřík and Lipovský
[
4
]
. The authors calculated the spectral determinant of the wave operator and, as expected, confirmed its singular behaviour at
\alpha=\pm 2
.

Finally, some insight is provided into the appearance of the values
\alpha=\pm 2
as critical points of the model. Following the footsteps of
[
5
]
, we analyse the wave equation on a compact star graph in the sense of
[
14
]
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
vertices. It is shown that the abrupt change of spectral properties happens precisely for
\alpha=\pm n
.

This behaviour of the wave equation subject to a non-regular damping is not unprecedented. We refer to the 2020 article
[
6
]
. The authors consider the singular damping of the form

u_{tt}(x,t)-u_{xx}(x,t)+\frac{\alpha}{x}u_{t}(x,t)=0,\quad x\in(0,1),\quad t\geqslant 0,

with Dirichlet boundary conditions and
\alpha>0
. It was shown that the otherwise infinite spectrum suddenly shrinks to
n-1
eigenvalues whenever
\alpha=2n
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, for
\alpha=2
, the spectrum becomes empty. Moreover, in this concrete setting with
\alpha=2
, all solutions of the wave equation are shown to vanish at finite time.

The present model has another, perhaps less apparent, possible application reaching into relativistic quantum mechanics. The traditional spectral approach to the wave equation (
1.1
) lies in considering
\psi=(u,u_{t})^{T}
and rewriting it as

A(a,\alpha)\psi=\partial_{t}\psi,\quad A(a,\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
\partial_{xx}&-\alpha\delta_{a}\end{pmatrix},

where
A(a,\alpha)
is the generator of the corresponding semigroup. However, one can take
\phi=(u_{t},u_{x})^{T}
and rewrite (
1.1
) as

\mathrm{i}D(a,\alpha)\phi=\partial_{t}\phi,\quad D(a,\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{i}\alpha\delta_{a}&-\mathrm{i}\partial_{x}\\
-\mathrm{i}\partial_{x}&0\end{pmatrix}.

The one-dimensional Dirac-type operator with a highly localized potential
D(a,\alpha)
is self-adjoint whenever
\alpha\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
. As discovered in
[
7
,
8
]
, unitary equivalence exists between
A(a,\alpha)
acting in
\dot{H}_{0}^{1}(0,\pi)\times L^{2}(0,\pi)
and
\mathrm{i}D(a,\alpha)
considered in
L^{2}(0,\pi)\times L^{2}(0,\pi)
. This observation further emphasizes the importance of accounting for a complex damping parameter.

The paper is organised as follows. Section
2
introduces the model and its basic known properties. Our main results concerning the spectrum and basis of root vectors are also formulated here. Section
3
provides proofs and outlines of calculations of the general properties of the wave operator needed for our analysis. The results concerning Riesz basis of root vectors are proven in Section
4
. In Section
5
, we explain the appearance of the mysterious damping constant
\pm 2
by considering the wave equation with Dirac damping on a compact star graph.

2.
The model and main results

2.1.
The damped wave equation and the wave operator

Our setting is the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=\dot{H}_{0}^{1}(0,\pi)\times L^{2}(0,\pi)
endowed with the inner product

\left\langle{\phi},{\psi}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\phi_{1}^{\prime}},{\psi_{1}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+\left\langle{\phi_{2}},{\psi_{2}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}.

Setting
\psi=(u,u_{t})
, the wave equation (
1.1
) can be reformulated as

\displaystyle A(a,\alpha)\psi=\psi_{t},\quad A(a,\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
\partial_{xx}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\psi(x,0)=\psi_{0}(x),

\displaystyle\mathsf{dom}\,A(a,\alpha)=

(2.1)

\displaystyle\left\{\psi\in\left(\dot{H}_{0}^{1}(0,\pi)\cap H^{2}(0,a)\cap H^{2}(a,\pi)\right)\times\dot{H}_{0}^{1}(0,\pi)\mid\psi_{1}^{\prime}(a+)-\psi_{1}^{\prime}(a-)=\alpha\psi_{2}(a)\right\}

with Cauchy data
\psi(x,0)=\psi_{0}(x)
, where
\psi\in\mathcal{H}
. The jump condition in the domain is to be understood in the sense of the absolutely continuous function in the given equivalence class of
\dot{H}_{0}^{1}(0,\pi)
.

It is shown in
[
2
]
that the operator
A(a,\alpha)
defined above is maximally dissipative for
\alpha\geqslant 0
. It then follows from the Lumer-Phillips theorem
[
9
, Theorem 4.3]
that
A
gives rise to the contraction semigroup
\exp(tA)
which is a suitable solution to (
1.1
).
By
[
2
]
, the operator has a compact resolvent for all values
a\in(0,\pi)
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
; therefore, it also has purely discrete spectrum. We will replicate and improve the result by showing that the inverse is even Hilbert-Schmidt and computing its Hilbert-Schmidt norm in Section
3
.

It was further discovered by Bamberger, Rauch, and Taylor that the harmonic spectrum (i.e. purely imaginary eigenvalues) is non-empty if and only if
a
is a rational multiple of
\pi
. They also found out that all eigenvalues are geometrically simple. When it comes to determining algebraic multiplicity, the following result is pivotal.

Theorem 2.1
.

\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is an eigenvalue of
A(a,\alpha)
if and only if it is a root of the entire function

(2.2)

S(\lambda;a,\alpha)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\sinh(\lambda\pi)+\alpha\sinh(\lambda a)\sinh(\lambda(\pi-a))\right).

Additionally, the algebraic multiplicity of the eigenvalue
\lambda
is exactly its multiplicity as a root of
S(\lambda;a,\alpha)
.

As a corollary, all eigenvalues are shown to be algebraically at most double.

Since the analysis for
\alpha>0
,
\alpha\neq 2
has to some extent been done by Cox and Henrot, here we focus especially on the case
\alpha=2
as well as, perhaps most notably, arbitrary
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
.

2.2.
The root vectors

Recall that a sequence in a Hilbert space is called a Riesz basis if it is the image of an orthonormal basis under a bounded isomorphism. In pursuit of proving or disproving the Riesz basis property for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
and
a\in(0,\pi)
, we first note the following symmetrical relationship between
A
and its adjoint:

Proposition 2.2
.

For any
a\in(0,\pi)
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, the adjoint operator of
A(a,\alpha)
is
A^{\ast}(a,\alpha)=-A(a,-\overline{\alpha})
.

Since both of the operators
A
and
A^{\ast}
possess compact resolvents, their systems of root vectors can be ordered and normalized to form biorthogonal sequences. In other words, if
\left\{\psi_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is the system of root vectors of
A
and
\left\{\phi_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
the system of root vectors of
A^{\ast}
, we can demand
\left\langle{\phi_{m}},{\psi_{n}}\right\rangle=\delta_{mn}
. This observation allows us to determine the Riesz basis property based on the following criterion.

Theorem 2.3
.

[
10
, Ch. 1, Theorem 9]
.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and
\left\{\psi_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{H}
. Then
\left\{\psi_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a Riesz basis in
\mathcal{H}
if and only if it is total in
\mathcal{H}
and Bessel and possesses a biorthogonal sequence
\left\{\phi_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
that is also total and Bessel.

Recall that by
[
10
]
a sequence
\left\{\psi_{n}\right\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is Bessel if and only if

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|\left\langle{\psi_{n}},{\psi}\right\rangle\right|^{2}<+\infty,\quad\forall\psi\in\mathcal{H}.

A subset of
\mathcal{H}
is said to be total (or complete) if its linear span is dense in
\mathcal{H}
.

The verification of the condition in Theorem
2.3
was done in
[
3
]
for positive
\alpha\neq 2
and rational placement of the damping
a=p\pi/q
. In Section
4
we show the derivation of the general condition:

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
a\in(0,\pi)
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
. The root vectors of
A(a,\alpha)
form a Riesz basis in
\mathcal{H}
if and only if
\alpha\neq\pm 2
.

3.
General properties of the wave operator

3.1.
The adjoint operator

For further use, it is convenient to know the formula for the adjoint of
A
. We strive to prove Proposition
2.2
.
Let
\phi\in\mathsf{dom}\,A^{\ast}
,
\eta\coloneqq A^{\ast}\phi
. Then we have

(3.1)

\left\langle{\phi_{1}^{\prime}},{\psi_{2}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+\left\langle{\phi_{2}},{\psi_{1}^{\prime\prime}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=\left\langle{\phi},{A\psi}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\eta},{\psi}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\eta_{1}^{\prime}},{\psi_{1}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+\left\langle{\eta_{2}},{\psi_{2}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

for any
\psi\in\mathsf{dom}\,A
.

Setting
\psi_{1}=0
and
\psi_{2}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(0,a)
or
C_{0}^{\infty}(a,\pi)
in (
3.1
) yields
\phi_{1}^{\prime}\in\dot{H}^{1}(0,a)\cap\dot{H}^{1}(a,\pi)
(the choice is consistent with the domain of
A
using the fact that
\psi_{2}(a)=0
). Next, choose
\psi_{1}\in H^{2}(0,\pi)
and
\psi_{2}=0
(the choice is again consistent with
\mathsf{dom}\,A
since the derivative of
\psi_{1}
is continuous on
(0,\pi)
whenever
\psi_{2}(a)=0
). Hence, we have

\left\langle{\phi_{2}},{\psi_{1}^{\prime\prime}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=-\left\langle{\eta_{1}},{\psi_{1}^{\prime\prime}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}.

Making use of the surjectivity of the Dirichlet Laplacian on
(0,\pi)
and the fact that the choices of
\psi_{1}
cover precisely its domain, we conclude that
\phi_{2}=-\eta_{1}
in the distributional sense and thus also almost everywhere. It follows that
\phi_{2}\in\dot{H}_{0}^{1}(0,\pi)
.

For any
\phi\in\mathsf{dom}\,A^{\ast}
and
\psi\in\mathsf{dom}\,A
, we can now calculate:

\displaystyle\left\langle{A^{\ast}\phi},{\psi}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\phi},{A\psi}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\phi_{1}^{\prime}},{\psi_{2}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}+\left\langle{\phi_{2}},{\psi_{1}^{\prime\prime}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=-\psi_{2}(a)\left(\overline{\phi_{1}^{\prime}(a+)}-\overline{\phi_{1}^{\prime}(a-)}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\overline{\phi_{2}(a)}\left(\psi_{1}^{\prime}(a+)-\psi_{1}^{\prime}(a-)\right)-\left\langle{\phi_{1}^{\prime\prime}},{\psi_{2}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}-\left\langle{\phi_{2}^{\prime}},{\psi_{1}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\psi_{2}(a)\left(\overline{\phi_{1}^{\prime}(a+)}-\overline{\phi_{1}^{\prime}(a-)}+\alpha\overline{\phi_{2}(a)}\right)+\left\langle{\begin{pmatrix}0&-I\\
-\partial_{xx}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}\\
\phi_{2}\end{pmatrix}},{\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}\\
\psi_{2}\end{pmatrix}}\right\rangle.

This already forces
\phi_{1}^{\prime}(a+)-\phi_{1}^{\prime}(a-)=-\overline{\alpha}\phi_{2}(a)
and consequently, indeed
A^{\ast}(a,\alpha)=-A(a,-\overline{\alpha})
. We have proven Proposition
2.2
.

3.2.
The resolvent

Next, we provide explicit construction of the resolvent. Consider the equation

(A-\lambda I)\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}f\\
g\end{pmatrix}

for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
(u,v)^{T}\in\mathsf{dom}\,A
and
(f,g)^{T}\in\mathcal{H}
.
This gives us
v=\lambda u+f
and
u^{\prime\prime}-(\lambda+\alpha\delta_{a})v=g
. Substituting the first equation into the second, we have the Sturm-Liouville problem

(3.2)

u^{\prime\prime}-\lambda(\lambda+\alpha\delta_{a})u=(\lambda+\alpha\delta_{a})f+g.

The approach is standard. We choose solutions
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
to (
3.2
) with zero right-hand side such that

u_{1}(0)=0=u_{2}(\pi),\quad u_{1}^{\prime}(0)=1,\quad u_{2}^{\prime}(\pi)=-1.

The Green function is of the form

\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x,y)=-\frac{1}{u_{1}(\pi)}\begin{cases}u_{1}(x)u_{2}(y),&0\leqslant x\leqslant y\leqslant\pi,\\
u_{1}(y)u_{2}(x),&0\leqslant y\leqslant x\leqslant\pi.\end{cases}

The solution to (
3.2
) is then given by

(3.3)

u(x)=\int_{0}^{\pi}\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x,y)[(\lambda+\alpha\delta_{a}(y))f(y)+g(y)]\,\textrm{d}y\eqqcolon G_{\lambda}\left[(\lambda+\alpha\delta_{a})f+g\right](x),

where
G_{\lambda}
is the corresponding Green operator. Combined with the equation for
v
, we arrive at the following result.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
a\in(0,\pi)
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
,
\lambda\in\rho(A(a,\alpha))
. The resolvent of
A
in
\lambda
is

(3.4)

(A-\lambda I)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}G_{\lambda}(\lambda+\alpha\delta_{a})&G_{\lambda}\\
I+\lambda G_{\lambda}(\lambda+\alpha\delta_{a})&\lambda G_{\lambda}\end{pmatrix},

where
G_{\lambda}
is the Green operator defined in (
3.3
).

We will now show that
A^{-1}
is Hilbert-Schmidt by directly calculating its Hilbert-Schmidt (HS) norm. Note that setting
\lambda=0
in (
3.4
), the inverse simplifies to

A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}G_{0}(\alpha\delta_{a})&G_{0}\\
I&0\end{pmatrix}.

To calculate the HS norm, we will use the orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions of the unbounded operator
A_{0}
with
\alpha=0
. In such case, the operator is skew-adjoint, as follows from Proposition
2.2
.

We have

A_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
\partial_{xx}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathsf{dom}\,A_{0}=H_{0}^{2}(0,\pi)\times H_{0}^{1}(0,\pi).

Solving

A_{0}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}

gives
v=\lambda u
and so
u^{\prime\prime}-\lambda^{2}u=0
with Dirichlet boundary conditions.
Therefore,

\lambda_{n}=\mathrm{i}n,\quad\omega_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{n\sqrt{\pi}}\sin(nx)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\mathrm{in}\end{pmatrix},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},

where
\omega_{n}
are the normalized eigenfunctions. It is straightforward to check that the system
\left\{\omega_{n}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}
forms an orthonormal basis in
\mathcal{H}
.

Back to the HS norm calculation; clearly

(A^{-1}\omega_{n})(x)=\frac{1}{n\sqrt{\pi}}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\sin(na)\mathcal{G}_{0}(x,a)+\mathrm{i}n\int_{0}^{\pi}\mathcal{G}_{0}(x,y)\sin(ny)\,\textrm{d}y\\
\sin(nx)\end{pmatrix}.

To find
\mathcal{G}_{0}
, we must solve for
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
. A simple calculation yields
u_{1}(x)=x
,
u_{2}(x)=\pi-x
. Therefore,

\mathcal{G}_{0}(x,y)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\begin{cases}x(\pi-y),&0\leqslant x\leqslant y\leqslant\pi,\\
y(\pi-x),&0\leqslant y\leqslant x\leqslant\pi.\end{cases}

Consequently,

\partial_{x}\mathcal{G}_{0}(x,y)=\begin{cases}-\frac{\pi-y}{\pi},&0\leqslant x<y\leqslant\pi,\\
\frac{y}{\pi},&0\leqslant y<x\leqslant\pi\end{cases}

and

\int_{0}^{\pi}\mathcal{\partial}_{x}G_{0}(x,y)\sin(ny)\,\textrm{d}y=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\int_{0}^{x}y\sin(ny)\,\textrm{d}y-\int_{x}^{\pi}(\pi-y)\sin(ny)\,\textrm{d}y\right)=-\frac{\cos(nx)}{n}.

We obtain

\left(A^{-1}\omega_{n}\right)_{1}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{n\sqrt{\pi}}\left[\alpha\sin(na)\partial_{x}\mathcal{G}_{0}(x,a)-\mathrm{i}\cos(nx)\right].

The
\dot{H}_{0}^{1}
norm is bounded by

\displaystyle\left|\left|\left(A^{-1}\omega_{n}\right)_{1}^{\prime}\right|\right|^{2}_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{\pi n^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}|\alpha|^{2}\sin^{2}(na)(\partial_{x}\mathcal{G}_{0}(x,a))^{2}+\cos^{2}(nx)\,\textrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi n^{2}}\left(\left|\alpha\right|^{2}\frac{a(\pi-a)}{\pi}\sin^{2}(na)+\frac{\pi}{2}\right).

Note the for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain equality.

Overall, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left|A^{-1}\omega_{n}\right|\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\left|(A^{-1}\omega_{n})_{1}^{\prime}\right|\right|^{2}_{L^{2}}+\left|\left|(A^{-1}\omega_{n})_{2}\right|\right|^{2}_{L^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\pi n^{2}}\left(\left|\alpha\right|^{2}\frac{a(\pi-a)}{\pi}\sin^{2}(na)+\pi\right).

Taking the Fourier expansion of
x(\pi-x)
on
(0,\pi)
, one has

x(\pi-x)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos(2nx)}{n^{2}}=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^{2}(nx)}{n^{2}}.

Finally, the HS norm is

\left|\left|A^{-1}\right|\right|_{\text{HS}}^{2}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\left|\left|A^{-1}\omega_{n}\right|\right|^{2}\leqslant\left(\frac{|\alpha|a(\pi-a)}{\pi}\right)^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}.

Realising that
a(\pi-a)\leqslant\pi^{2}/4
, we can also obtain a bound independent of
a
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
a\in(0,\pi)
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
. The inverse
A^{-1}(a,\alpha)
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator with norm satisfying

(3.5)

\left|\left|A^{-1}\right|\right|_{\text{HS}}^{2}\leqslant\left(\frac{|\alpha|a(\pi-a)}{\pi}\right)^{2}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{3}\leqslant\pi^{2}\left(\frac{|\alpha|^{2}}{16}+\frac{1}{3}\right).

The first inequality becomes equality if
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, the second one for
a=\pi/2
.

It follows that the inverse is compact and
A
has discrete spectrum.

3.3.
The characteristic function

We are now fully equipped to find the characteristic function of
A
and show how it determines the spectrum – this constitutes the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Recall that the index of an eigenvalue
\lambda\in\sigma(A)
is defined as

\iota(\lambda)\coloneqq\min\left\{k\in\mathbb{N}\mid\mathsf{ker}(A-\lambda I)^{k}=\mathsf{ker}(A-\lambda I)^{k+1}\right\}.

For convenience, we set
\iota(\lambda)=0
whenever
\lambda\notin\sigma_{p}(A)
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
A
be a densely defined operator in a Banach space that possesses a compact inverse and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Then the index
\iota(\lambda)
is equal to the order of
\lambda
as pole of the resolvent.

Proof.

Denote
T\coloneqq A^{-1}
the compact inverse. Then
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
A
if and only if
1/\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
T
. Moreover, both geometric and algebraic multiplicities are the same and so are the indices.
[
12
, Theorem 9.2.3]

Assume arbitrary
\mu\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and denote

P_{\mu}\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\gamma}(T-zI)^{-1}\,\textrm{d}z.

If
\mu
is an eigenvalue of
T
, then
P_{\mu}
is the Riesz projection onto the root subspace associated with
\mu
. Here
\gamma
is an arbitrary positively oriented Jordan curve in the resolvent set
\rho(T)
such that no eigenvalue lies in its interior
D
except possibly
\mu
. Then its index is

\iota(\mu)=\min\left\{k\in\mathbb{N}\mid(T-\mu I)^{k}P_{\mu}=0\right\}<\infty.

Thanks to the functional calculus, we have

(T-\mu I)^{k}P_{\mu}=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\gamma}(z-\mu)^{k}(T-zI)^{-1}\,\textrm{d}z,\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Therefore,
\iota(\mu)
is the lowest
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
(z-\mu)^{k}(T-zI)^{-1}
is holomorphic in
D
. In other words,
\iota(\mu)
is equal to the order of the pole
\mu
of
(T-zI)^{-1}
.

It remains to be shown that the order of the pole
1/\lambda
of
(T-zI)^{-1}
is precisely the order of the pole
\lambda
of
(A-zI)^{-1}
. This follows from the simple observation that

(A-zI)^{-1}=-\frac{1}{z}T\left(T-\frac{1}{z}I\right)^{-1},\quad z\in\mathbb{\rho}(A)\setminus\{0\}.\qed

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
A
be a densely defined operator in a Banach space that possesses a compact inverse. Let
\lambda
be a geometrically simple eigenvalue of
A
. Then the algebraic multiplicity of
\lambda
is equal to its order as pole of the resolvent.

Note that by
[
2
]
all eigenvalues of
A
are geometrically simple. As we have already found the resolvent in Proposition
3.4
, we can see that the order of its pole
\lambda
is the order of
\lambda
as a pole of the Green operator
G_{\lambda}
and, as a consequence, of the Green function
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
. The only possible singularity of
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
appears in the denominator
u_{1}(\pi).
Solving (
3.2
) with zero right-hand side,
u_{1}(0)=0
, and
u_{1}^{\prime}(0)=1
, one obtains the solution

u_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\begin{cases}\sinh(\lambda x),&0\leqslant x\leqslant a,\\
-\left(\cosh(\lambda\pi)+\alpha\sinh(\lambda a)\cosh(\lambda(\pi-a))\right)\sinh(\lambda(\pi-x))\\
+\left(\sinh(\lambda\pi)+\alpha\sinh(\lambda a)\sinh(\lambda(\pi-a))\right)\cosh(\lambda(\pi-x)),&a\leqslant x\leqslant\pi.\end{cases}

It follows that the order of the pole of
(A-zI)^{-1}
in
\lambda
is exactly its multiplicity as root of

(3.6)

S(\lambda;a,\alpha)\coloneqq u_{1}(\pi)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\sinh(\lambda\pi)+\alpha\sinh(\lambda a)\sinh(\lambda(\pi-a))\right).

Theorem
2.1
is thus proven.

Differentiating the function twice with respect to
\lambda
, it is simple matter to show that no root of
S(\cdot;a,\alpha)
is of higher multiplicity than 2.

Proposition 3.5
.

All roots of
S(\cdot;a,\alpha)
are at most double.

Proof.

Let
F(\lambda)\coloneqq\lambda S(\lambda;a,\alpha)
for simplicity. Note that

F(\lambda)=\sinh(\lambda\pi)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\cosh(\lambda\pi)-\frac{\alpha}{2}\cosh(\lambda(\pi-2a)).

We have

(3.7)

F^{\prime}(\lambda)=\pi\cosh(\lambda\pi)+\pi\frac{\alpha}{2}\sinh(\lambda\pi)-(\pi-2a)\frac{\alpha}{2}\sinh(\lambda(\pi-2a))

and for the second derivative

\displaystyle F^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\pi^{2}\sinh(\lambda\pi)+\pi^{2}\frac{\alpha}{2}\cosh(\lambda\pi)-(\pi-2a)^{2}\frac{\alpha}{2}\cosh(\lambda(\pi-2a))

\displaystyle=\pi^{2}F(\lambda)+2a\alpha(\pi-a)\cosh(\lambda(\pi-2a)).

Suppose for contradiction that
F(\lambda_{0})=F^{\prime}(\lambda_{0})=F^{\prime\prime}(\lambda_{0})=0
for some
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
. Then necessarily

\cosh(\lambda_{0}(\pi-a))=0\implies\lambda_{0}=\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\pi-2a},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Simultaneously,

(3.8)

0=F(\lambda_{0})=\sinh(\lambda_{0}\pi)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\cosh(\lambda_{0}\pi)=\sinh(\mathrm{i}\omega)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\cosh(\mathrm{i}\omega)=\mathrm{i}\sin\omega+\frac{\alpha}{2}\cos\omega,

where we denoted
\omega\coloneqq\frac{\pi^{2}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\pi-2a}
.

If
\mathsf{Re}\,\alpha\neq 0
, then
\cos\omega=0
and consequently also
\sin\omega=0
– a contradiction.

If
\alpha\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
, the equation (
3.7
) for
F^{\prime}(\lambda)=0
yields
\cos\omega=\cosh(\lambda_{0}\pi)=0
by taking the real part. However, (
3.8
) then forces also
\sin\omega=0
giving us the same contradiction as above.
∎

Combined with the result of
[
2
]
, this immediately leads to the following conclusion.

Corollary 3.6
.

All eigenvalues of
A(a,\alpha)
are algebraically at most double and geometrically simple.

4.
Basis of root vectors

4.1.
Rational placement of the damping

First, we will discuss the special case
a=p\pi/q
, where
p
and
q
are coprime positive integers. In
[
3
]
, Cox and Henrot noticed that the characteristic function
S
can be rewritten as

(4.1)

S(\lambda;p\pi/q,\alpha)=-\frac{1}{4\lambda}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\pi}P_{\alpha}(\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda\pi/q}),

where

(4.2)

P_{\alpha}(z)\coloneqq(2-\alpha)z^{q}+\alpha z^{p}+\alpha z^{q-p}-(2+\alpha).

Note that
1
is a simple root of
P_{\alpha}
.

From here it can be observed more closely why the model shows unpredictable behaviour at
\alpha=\pm 2
. Denoting
\zeta_{k}=|\zeta_{k}|\mathrm{e}^{i\theta_{k}}
the roots of
P_{\alpha}
, with convention
\zeta_{1}=1
, we arrive at the system of eigenvalues

\displaystyle\lambda_{1,n}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}qn,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},

(4.3)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k,n}

\displaystyle=-\frac{q}{2\pi}(\ln|\zeta_{k}|+\mathrm{i}(\theta_{k}+2\pi n)),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},\,k\in\{2,\dots,\mathsf{deg}\,P_{\alpha}\}.

If we let
\psi_{k,n}
denote the corresponding eigenvectors of
A(p\pi/q,\alpha)
, we find

\displaystyle\psi_{1,n}

\displaystyle=\sin(nqx)\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\mathrm{i}nq\end{pmatrix},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},

\displaystyle\psi_{k,n}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}u_{k,n}\\
\lambda_{k,n}u_{k,n}\end{pmatrix},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},\,k\in\{2,\dots,\mathsf{deg}\,P_{\alpha}\},

where

u_{k,n}(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\sinh(\lambda_{k,n}(\pi-a))\sinh(\lambda_{k,n}x),&\text{for }0\leqslant x\leqslant a,\\
\sinh(\lambda_{k,n}a)\sinh(\lambda_{k,n}(\pi-x)),&\text{for }a\leqslant x\leqslant\pi.\end{cases}

Similarly, if
\lambda_{k+1,n}=\lambda_{k,n}
, we obtain the generalised eigenvector by solving the equation
(A-\lambda_{k,n}I)\tilde{\psi}_{k,n}=\psi_{k,n}
as

\psi_{k+1,n}\equiv\tilde{\psi}_{k,n}=\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{u}_{k,n}\\
u_{k,n}+\lambda_{k,n}\tilde{u}_{k,n}\end{pmatrix},

where

\tilde{u}_{k,n}=\begin{cases}x\sinh(\lambda_{k,n}(\pi-a))\cosh(\lambda_{k,n}x)+(\pi-a)\sinh(\lambda_{k,n}x),&0\leqslant x\leqslant a,\\
-x\cosh(\lambda_{k,n}a)\cosh(\lambda_{k,n}(\pi-x))+a\cosh(\lambda_{k,n}a)\sinh(\lambda_{k,n}(\pi-x)),&a\leqslant x\leqslant\pi.\end{cases}

By Proposition
2.2
,
A^{\ast}(a,\alpha)=-A(a,-\overline{\alpha})
; therefore, the spectrum is
\sigma(A^{\ast})=\sigma_{p}(A^{\ast})=\{\overline{\lambda}_{k,n}\}_{k,n}
and the corresponding eigenvectors
\phi_{k,n}
can be found in a similar fashion.

Combined with proper normalisation, we have found biorthogonal sequence to the sequence
\{\psi_{k,n}\}_{k,n}
of eigenvectors of
A
. In
[
3
]
, it is shown that when normalised as
\psi_{k,n}/\lambda_{k,n}
, the sequence is Bessel, i.e. for all
\psi\in\mathcal{H}
holds

\sum_{k,n}\left|\left\langle{\psi},{\psi_{k,n}/\lambda_{k,n}}\right\rangle\right|^{2}<+\infty.

The same can be done for the biorthogonal sequence. To decide whether the systems are total, Cox and Henrot invoked the Livšic criterion:

Theorem 4.1
.

(Livšic,
[
11
, Theorem V.2.1]
).
Let
T
be a compact operator in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Suppose
\mathsf{Re}\,T\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}(T+T^{\ast})
is dissipative and trace-class. Then

\mathsf{Tr}\,(\mathsf{Re}\,T)\leqslant\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{p}(T)}\mathsf{Re}\,\lambda

with eigenvalues repeated according to their algebraic multiplicity. Equality holds if and only if the root vectors of
T
are total in
\mathcal{H}

An analogous statement holds for accretive operators with the opposite inequality.

The dissipativness of
A(a,\alpha)
for
\alpha>0
was established in
[
2
]
. Let us extend the result for a general
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}.

Proposition 4.2
.

Operator
A(a,\alpha)
is

(1)

maximal dissipative if and only if
\mathsf{Re}\,\alpha\geqslant 0
,

(2)

maximal accretive if and only if
\mathsf{Re}\,\alpha\leqslant 0
,

(3)

skew-adjoint if and only if
\mathsf{Re}\,\alpha=0
.

Proof.

Let
\psi\in\mathsf{dom}\,A
. Integrating by parts, we have

\left\langle{\psi},{A\psi}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\psi_{1}^{\prime}},{\psi_{2}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}-\left\langle{\psi_{2}^{\prime}},{\psi_{1}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}-\alpha|\psi_{2}(a)|^{2}.

Therefore,

\mathsf{Re}\,\left\langle{\psi},{A\psi}\right\rangle=-\mathsf{Re}\,\alpha|\psi_{2}(a)|^{2}.

The claim for dissipativness and accretivness follows.

The maximality is a consequence of Theorem
2.1
. Clearly, for
\mathsf{Re}\,\alpha\geqslant 0
and
\lambda>0
, we have

\mathsf{Re}\,S(\lambda;a,\alpha)>0.

Therefore,
(0,+\infty)\subset\rho(A(a,\alpha))
. We proceed analogously for
\mathsf{Re}\,\alpha\leqslant 0
to show that
(-\infty,0)\subset\rho(A(a,\alpha))
.

The characterisation of skew-adjointness is an immediate consequence of Proposition
2.2
.
∎

It is left to apply the criterion to the compact inverse
T\coloneqq A^{-1}
. As
\mathsf{Re}\,A^{-1}
is a one-dimensional operator, calculation of the trace is simple and for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
done in
[
3
]
. The extension for all complex
\alpha
is straightforward an we omit the proof:

Proposition 4.3
.

\mathsf{Tr}\,\left(\mathsf{Re}\,A^{-1}(a,\alpha)\right)=-\frac{\mathsf{Re}\,\alpha(\pi-a)a}{\pi}
.

For
\alpha>0
,
\alpha\neq 2
, Cox and Henrot found that also

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda}=-\frac{\alpha(\pi-a)a}{\pi}

and thus arrived at the result that the root vectors are complete by Theorem
4.1
. Combined with the Bessel property and the same two properties of the biorthogonal sequence, the root vectors form a Riesz basis in
\mathcal{H}
due to Theorem
2.3
.

Here, we will provide detailed calculation for
\alpha=2
and extend the result for
\alpha=-2
. Recall that for
\alpha=2
, the degree of the polynomial (
4.2
) is
r\coloneqq\max\{p,q-p\}<q
. Also note that, as pointed out in
[
3
]
, simply by differentiating the characteristic function (
2.2
) from definition, we obtain the Taylor expansion

(4.4)

S(\lambda;a,\alpha)=\pi+\alpha a(\pi-a)\lambda+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2}).

On the other hand, the following equality holds:

Proposition 4.4
.

S(\lambda;p\pi/q,2)=\pi-\pi\lambda\left(\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda}-\frac{\pi(q-r)}{q}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{2})
.

Proof.

Note that
P_{2}(z)=2(z^{r}+z^{q-r}-2)
. Then using (
4.1
), we have

\lambda S(\lambda;p\pi/q,2)\equiv F(\lambda)=-\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\pi}P_{2}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda\pi/q}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\pi}\prod_{k=1}^{r}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda\pi/q}-\zeta_{k}\right),

where
\zeta_{k}
are the roots of
P_{2}
,
\zeta_{1}=1
. Differentiating
F
, we have

F^{\prime}(\lambda)=-\frac{\pi}{2}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\pi}\prod_{k=1}^{r}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda\pi/q}-\zeta_{k}\right)+\frac{\pi}{q}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\pi(1-2/q)}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\prod_{k\neq j}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda\pi/q}-\zeta_{k}\right).

Therefore,

F^{\prime}(0)=\frac{\pi}{q}\prod_{k=2}^{r}\left(1-\zeta_{k}\right)=\pi.

Differentiating again, we obtain

\displaystyle F^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\pi}\prod_{k=1}^{r}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda\pi/q}-\zeta_{k}\right)+\frac{2\pi^{2}}{q^{2}}(q-1)\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\pi(1-2/q)}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\prod_{k\neq j}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda\pi/q}-\zeta_{k}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{2\pi^{2}}{q^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda\pi(1-4/q)}\sum_{i,j=1}^{r}\prod_{k\neq i,j}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda\pi/q}-\zeta_{k}\right).

At
\lambda=0
this becomes

\displaystyle F^{\prime\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi^{2}(q-1)}{q^{2}}\prod_{k=2}^{r}\left(1-\zeta_{k}\right)-\frac{2\pi^{2}}{q^{2}}\sum_{j=2}^{r}\prod_{k\neq 1,j}(1-\zeta_{k})=\frac{2\pi^{2}(q-1)}{q}-\frac{4\pi^{2}}{q}\sum_{k=2}^{r}\frac{1}{1-\zeta_{k}}

(4.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{q}\left[\sum_{k=2}^{r}\frac{\zeta_{k}+1}{\zeta_{k}-1}+q-r\right].

At the same time, (
4.3
) gives us

\frac{1}{\lambda_{k,n}}=-\frac{2\pi}{q}\frac{\ln|\zeta_{k}|-\mathrm{i}(\theta_{k}+2\pi n)}{\ln^{2}|\zeta_{k}|+(\theta_{k}+2\pi n)^{2}}

for
k\in\{1,\dots,\mathsf{deg}\,P_{\alpha}\}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore, the real parts are

\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda_{k,n}}=-\frac{\ln|\zeta_{k}|}{2\pi q}\frac{1}{\frac{\ln^{2}|\zeta_{k}|}{4\pi^{2}}+\left(n+\frac{\theta_{k}}{2\pi}\right)^{2}}.

Note the for the sum of real parts, we may ignore the imaginary eigenvalues
\lambda_{1,n}
.

To proceed with the proof, we need to know the sum of the following series.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\beta,\,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
\beta\neq 0
. Then

(4.6)

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{(n+\gamma)^{2}+\beta^{2}}=\frac{\pi}{2\beta}\frac{\sinh(2\pi\beta)}{\cosh^{2}(\pi\beta)-\cos^{2}(\pi\gamma)}.

Proof.

It is simple to verify the assumptions for Poisson summation
[
13
, Theorem 2.4]
. The Fourier transform of
f(x)=\frac{1}{(x+\gamma)^{2}+\beta^{2}}
is

\hat{f}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\xi x}}{(x+\gamma)^{2}+\beta^{2}}\,\textrm{d}x=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\xi\gamma}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\xi a}}{x^{2}+\beta^{2}}\,\textrm{d}x=\frac{\pi}{\beta}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\xi\gamma-2\pi\mathrm{i}|\xi|\beta}

using residues. Poisson summation then yields

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{f}(n)=\frac{\pi}{\beta}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}(n\gamma-|n|\beta)}=\frac{\pi}{\beta}\left(\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\gamma-2\pi\beta}}+\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\gamma-2\pi\beta}}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2\beta}\frac{\sinh(2\pi\beta)}{\cosh^{2}(\pi\beta)-\cos^{2}(\pi\gamma)}\qed

Let us get back to the proof of Proposition
4.4
. Setting
\gamma\coloneqq\theta_{k}/2\pi
and
\beta\coloneqq\ln|\zeta_{k}|/2\pi
in (
4.6
), it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda_{k,n}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\ln|\zeta_{k}|}{2\pi q}\frac{\pi^{2}}{\ln|\zeta_{k}|}\frac{\sinh\left(\ln|\zeta_{k}|\right)}{\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{\ln|\zeta_{k}|}{2}\right)-\cos^{2}(\theta_{k}/2)}=\frac{\pi}{q}\frac{1-|\zeta_{k}|^{2}}{|\zeta_{k}|^{2}-2\mathsf{Re}\,\zeta_{k}+1}

(4.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{q}\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{\zeta_{k}+1}{1-\zeta_{k}}.

Comparing with (
4.5
), we have

\mathsf{Re}\,F^{\prime\prime}(0)=-2\pi\sum_{k=2}^{r}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda_{k,n}}+\frac{2\pi^{2}(q-r)}{q}

completing the proof since
F^{\prime\prime}(0)
is real.
∎

Comparing Proposition
4.4
and (
4.4
), we can conclude with the following

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
a=p\pi/q
,
\alpha=\pm 2
. Then

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda}=-\frac{\alpha(\pi-a)a}{\pi}\pm\frac{\pi}{q}(q-r)=\begin{cases}-\frac{\alpha(\pi-a)a}{\pi}\pm a,&\text{for }0<a\leqslant\pi/2,\\
-\frac{\alpha(\pi-a)a}{\pi}\pm(\pi-a),&\text{for }\pi/2\leqslant a<\pi.\end{cases}

Proof.

For
\alpha=-2
, thanks to Proposition
2.2
, holds
\mathsf{Re}\,A^{-1}(a,-2)=-\mathsf{Re}\,A^{-1}(a,2)
.
∎

Using Proposition
4.3
, we can see that for
\alpha=2
holds

\mathsf{Tr}\,\left(\mathsf{Re}\,A^{-1}\right)<\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda}.

Analogously, for
\alpha=-2
we have

\mathsf{Tr}\,\left(\mathsf{Re}\,A^{-1}\right)>\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda}.

Thanks to the Livšic criterion (Theorem
4.1
), we can state the desired result for
\alpha=\pm 2
:

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
a=p\pi/q
and
\alpha=\pm 2
. Then the root vectors of
A(a,\alpha)
are not total in
\mathcal{H}
. Consequently, they do not form a Riesz basis.

4.2.
Complex damping parameter

By careful analysis of the proof of Proposition
4.4
, we are able to determine the Riesz basis property also for an arbitrary
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{\pm 2\}
.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{\pm 2\}.
Then

\mathsf{Re}\,S^{\prime}(0;p\pi/q,\alpha)=-2\pi\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda}.

Proof.

Note the for
\alpha\neq 2
, the polynomial
P_{\alpha}
is of degree
q
. As a consequence, in (
4.5
), we obtain

(4.8)

F^{\prime\prime}(0)=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{q}\sum_{k=2}^{q}\frac{\zeta_{k}+1}{\zeta_{k}-1}.

Since the calculation (
4.7
) does not depend on
\alpha
, we can compare it with (
4.8
) to conclude

\mathsf{Re}\,F^{\prime\prime}(0)=-2\pi\sum_{k=2}^{q}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda_{k,n}}.\qed

Recalling the Taylor expansion (
4.4
), the above proposition yields for
a=p\pi/q
and any
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{\pm 2\}
:

(4.9)

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda}=-\frac{\mathsf{Re}\,\alpha(\pi-a)a}{\pi}=\mathsf{Tr}\,\left(\mathsf{Re}\,A^{-1}(a,\alpha)\right),

recalling Proposition
4.3
.
Livšic criterion
4.1
then ensures that the root vectors are total. The Bessel property is independent of
\alpha
. Therefore, using Theorem
2.3
, we have managed to generalise the result of
[
3
]
also for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
:

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{\pm 2\}
,
a=p\pi/q
. Then the root vectors of
A(p\pi/q,\alpha)
form a Riesz basis in
\mathcal{H}
.

4.3.
General placement of the damping

In this section, we aim to extend the result to an arbitrary placement of the damping
a\in(0,\pi)
. From Theorem
4.6
, Proposition
4.3
and the way they are used for Theorem
4.1
, it obviously suffices to show that the sum of the series

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A(a,\alpha))}\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda}

is continuous in
a
to prove that the root vectors are total. We will make use of the following recent result of Krejčiřík and Lipovský:

Theorem 4.10
.

[
4
, Section 4]
. Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
be arbitrary and
a\in(0,\pi/2)
. Let
\lambda_{j}^{+}(a)
denote the
j
-th eigenvalue in the upper half-plane sorted in non-decreasing order according to the imaginary part. Then

\lambda_{j}^{+}(a)=\begin{cases}\mathrm{i}j+f_{j}(a),&\text{for }\alpha\neq\pm 2,\\
\frac{\mathrm{ij\pi}}{\pi-a}+f_{j}(a),&\text{for }\alpha=\pm 2,\end{cases}

where

(1)

f_{j}
are analytic in
a
with at most algebraic singularities. If for certain
a_{0}
a finite number of
\lambda_{j}(a_{0})
have the same imaginary parts, one may need to interchange their indices to get the analyticity.

(2)

|\mathsf{Re}\,f_{j}(a)|\leqslant c_{1}
, where
c_{1}>0
is independent of
j
and
a
.

(3)

|\mathsf{Im}\,f_{j}(a)|\leqslant c_{2}
, where
c_{2}>0
is independent of
j
and
a
.

Analogous statement holds for the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}^{-}(a)
in the lower half-plane.

Since

\left|\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(a)}\right|=\frac{\left|\mathsf{Re}\,\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(a)\right|}{|\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(a)|^{2}}\leqslant\frac{c_{1}}{|\lambda_{j}^{\pm}(a)|^{2}}

and the last term is eventually dominated by
\varepsilon j^{-2}
for some
\varepsilon>0
, the series converges uniformly in
a
. Consequently, the sum is continuous with respect to
a
and the root vectors remain total if and only if
\alpha\neq\pm 2
.

To admit any
a\in(0,\pi)
we only need to realise that the trace in Proposition
4.3
, Theorem
4.6
, and (
4.9
) are symmetric under the exchange of
a
and
\pi-a
.

It is left to check the Bessel property. Using Theorem
4.10
, the calculation is lengthy but straightforward and analogous to the rational case. We omit the details.

The proof of Theorem
2.4
is hence complete.

5.
Compact star graph

5.1.
Wave equation on a compact star graph

Following the footsteps and notation of Krejčiřík and Royer in
[
5
]
, we consider a metric graph
\Gamma
consisting of
n
copies of the compact interval
[0,\pi]
connected at the central vertex. We set the lengths equal to each other to avoid introducing too many new constants to the model. For
n=2
this will be, up to scaling, equivalent to the case
a=\pi/2
of the problem considered previously.

To properly construct the corresponding Hilbert space setting, we first set

L^{2}(\Gamma)\coloneqq\left(L^{2}(0,\pi)\right)^{n}

with the standard inner product extension to a Cartesian product of Hilbert spaces. Next, let

H^{k}(\Gamma^{\ast})\coloneqq\left(H^{k}(0,\pi)\right)^{n},\quad\dot{H}_{0}^{k}(\Gamma^{\ast})\coloneqq\left\{u\in H^{k}(\Gamma^{\ast})\mid(\forall j\in\{1,\dots,n\})(u_{j}(\pi)=0)\right\}.

The equality
u_{j}(\pi)=0
is to be understood in the sense of the absolutely continuous representative of the equivalence class
u_{j}\in H^{k}(0,\pi)
.

For
u\in H^{1}(\Gamma^{\ast})
, we denote
u_{j}
its components with
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
u
is said to be continuous at
0
if there exist continuous representatives
u_{j}
of the equivalence classes of each component such that
\left(\forall j,k\in\{1,\dots,n\}\right)\left(u_{j}(0)=u_{k}(0)\right)
. The common value is denoted as
u(0)
.

To obtain the continuity condition at the vertex in accordance with the previous model, we set

\dot{H}_{0}^{1}(\Gamma)\coloneqq\left\{u\in\dot{H}_{0}^{1}(\Gamma^{\ast})\mid u\text{ is continuous at }0\right\}.

Finally, we will work with the Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}\coloneqq\dot{H}_{0}^{1}(\Gamma)\times L^{2}(\Gamma).

The wave operator with Dirac damping at the central vertex of
\Gamma
is then defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{dom}\,A_{n}(\alpha)\coloneqq\left\{\psi=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}\in\left(\dot{H}_{0}^{1}(\Gamma)\cap H^{2}(\Gamma^{\ast})\right)\times\dot{H}_{0}^{1}(\Gamma)\mid\sum_{j=1}^{n}u^{\prime}_{j}(0)=\alpha v(0)\right\},

\displaystyle A_{n}(\alpha)\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}v\\
u^{\prime\prime}\end{pmatrix},

where the derivatives are understood by components.

5.2.
The characteristic function

Computing the resolvent, one can, analogously to Section
3.2
, derive the characteristic function. Solving
u^{\prime\prime}-\lambda(\lambda+\alpha\delta)u=0
with
u_{j}(\pi)=0
,
u_{j}^{\prime}(\pi)=-1
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
, we find

u_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\alpha\sinh(\lambda\pi)+(n-1)\cosh(\lambda\pi)\right)\sinh(\lambda x)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\sinh(\lambda\pi)\cosh(\lambda x).

At
x=\pi
, we have

(5.1)

S_{n}(\lambda;\alpha)\coloneqq u_{n}(\pi)=\frac{\sinh(\lambda\pi)}{\lambda}\left(n\cosh(\lambda\pi)+\alpha\sinh(\lambda\pi)\right).

It can be easily seen that for
n=2
and after scaling, this is consistent with (
2.2
) for the central damping
a=\pi/2
.

Since
A_{n}^{-1}
is again compact,
A_{n}
has purely discrete spectrum. We invoke Proposition
3.3
to state the following analogy of Theorem
2.1
.

Proposition 5.1
.

\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is an eigenvalue of
A_{n}(\alpha)
if and only if it is a root of the entire function (
5.1
). Furthermore, the index of
\lambda\in\sigma_{p}(A_{n}(\alpha))
is precisely its root multiplicity.

The situation here is significantly easier since we abandoned the parameter
a
. We simply have

(5.2)

\displaystyle\lambda S_{n}(\lambda,\alpha)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2\lambda\pi}}{4}\left(n(1-e^{-4\lambda\pi})+\alpha(1-2\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda\pi}+\mathrm{e}^{-4\lambda\pi})\right)=-\frac{\mathrm{e}^{2\lambda\pi}}{4}P_{n,\alpha}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda\pi}\right),

where we define the polynomial
P_{n,\alpha}
as

(5.3)

P_{n,\alpha}(z)=(n-\alpha)z^{2}+2\alpha z-(n+\alpha).

Here we can observe for the first time why
\alpha=\pm n
are somewhat special values.

Supposing
\alpha\neq n
, the roots are

\zeta_{1}=1,\quad\zeta_{2}=\frac{\alpha+n}{\alpha-n},

with the corresponding eigenvalues

\displaystyle\lambda_{1,k}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}k,\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},

(5.4)

\displaystyle\lambda_{2,k}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\ln\left|\frac{\alpha+n}{\alpha-n}\right|+\mathrm{i}(\theta+2\pi k)\right),\quad k\in\mathbb{Z},

now supposing also
\alpha\neq-n
and denoting
\theta
the argument of
\zeta_{2}
.

5.3.
Eigenvectors

By Proposition
5.1
it is clear that no eigenvalue has index greater than
1
. Therefore, all root vectors are in fact eigenvectors. We will once again use Theorem
2.3
to show in which cases the eigenvectors of
A(\alpha)
form a Riesz basis in
\mathcal{H}
.

As one can show analogously to Proposition
2.2
that
A_{n}^{\ast}(\alpha)=-A_{n}\left(-\overline{\alpha}\right)
, the biorthogonal sequence is provided by the eigenvectors of
A_{n}^{\ast}(\alpha)
similarly as in the prequel.

Solving the eigenvalue problem
A_{n}(\alpha)\psi=\lambda\psi
, we obtain eigenfunctions of the form

\psi=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
\lambda u\end{pmatrix},\quad u_{j}(x)\coloneqq\sinh(\lambda(\pi-x)),\,\,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

To show the Bessel property, one can again proceed similarly to Cox and Henrot in
[
3
]
and use the explicit form of the eigenvalues (
5.4
). Details are omitted.

Perhaps more interesting is the Livšic criterion which we apply to
A_{n}^{-1}
. First, we will find the trace of the real part.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
\mathsf{Tr}\,\mathsf{Re}\,A_{n}^{-1}(\alpha)=-\pi\frac{\mathsf{Re}\,\alpha}{n}
.

Proof.

Consider the equation

A_{n}(\alpha)\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}f\\
g\end{pmatrix},

yielding
v=f
,
u^{\prime\prime}=g
, and
\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}^{\prime}(0)=\alpha v(0)
. The solution is of the form

u_{j}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}(x-t)g_{j}(t)\,\textrm{d}t-\frac{x}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}(\pi-t)g_{j}(t)\,\textrm{d}t+\left(\alpha f(0)+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\Gamma}(\pi-t)g(t)\,\textrm{d}t\right)\frac{x-\pi}{n},

where
\int_{\Gamma}(\pi-t)g(t)\,\textrm{d}t\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{\pi}(\pi-t)g_{k}(t)\,\textrm{d}t
.
Similarly, for the equation

A^{\ast}_{n}(\alpha)\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}f\\
g\end{pmatrix},

we obtain
v=-f
,
u^{\prime\prime}=-g
, and
\sum_{j=1}^{n}u_{j}^{\prime}(0)=\overline{\alpha}f(0)
.
This leads to

u_{j}(x)=-\int_{0}^{x}(x-t)g_{j}(t)\,\textrm{d}t+\frac{x}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}(\pi-t)g_{j}(t)\,\textrm{d}t+\left(\overline{\alpha}f(0)-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\Gamma}(\pi-t)g(t)\,\textrm{d}t\right)\frac{x-\pi}{n}.

Overall, for any
\begin{pmatrix}f\\
g\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{H}
, we have found

\mathsf{Re}\,A^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}f\\
g\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\left(A^{-1}+(A^{-1})^{\ast}\right)\begin{pmatrix}f\\
g\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\mathsf{Re}\,\alpha}{n}f(0)\begin{pmatrix}\rho\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad\rho_{j}(x)\coloneqq x-\pi.

To find the trace, we choose an arbitrary orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}
that contains the normalized spanning vector of
\mathsf{ran}\,\mathsf{Re}\,A^{-1}
of the form
\psi_{0}\coloneqq 1/\sqrt{n\pi}(\rho,0)
.
Then we compute as follows

\mathsf{Tr}\,\mathsf{Re}\,A_{n}^{-1}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{n\pi}\left\langle{\begin{pmatrix}\rho\\
0\end{pmatrix}},{\mathsf{Re}\,A_{n}^{-1}(\alpha)\begin{pmatrix}\rho\\
0\end{pmatrix}}\right\rangle=-\frac{\mathsf{Re}\,\alpha}{n^{2}}\left|\left|\begin{pmatrix}\rho\\
0\end{pmatrix}\right|\right|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}=-\frac{\mathsf{Re}\,\alpha}{n}\pi.\qed

Let us now calculate the series

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda_{2,k}}

using (
5.4
) for
\alpha\neq\pm n
. Computing the real part, we have

\frac{1}{\lambda_{2,k}}=-2\pi\frac{\ln\left|\frac{\alpha+n}{\alpha-n}\right|-\mathrm{i}(\theta+2\pi k)}{\ln^{2}\left|\frac{\alpha+n}{\alpha-n}\right|+(\theta+2\pi k)^{2}}\implies\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda_{2,k}}=-\frac{\ln\left|\zeta_{2}\right|}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\frac{\ln^{2}\left|\zeta_{2}\right|}{4\pi^{2}}+\left(k+\frac{\theta}{2\pi}\right)^{2}}.

Invoking (
4.6
) with
\gamma\coloneqq\theta/2\pi
and
\beta\coloneqq\ln\left|\zeta_{2}\right|/2\pi
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda_{2,k}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{\sinh(\ln|\zeta_{2}|)}{\cosh^{2}\left(\frac{\ln|\zeta_{2}|}{2}\right)-\cos^{2}(\theta/2)}=\pi\frac{1-|\zeta_{2}|^{2}}{|\zeta_{2}|^{2}-2\mathsf{Re}\,\zeta_{2}+1}=\pi\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{\zeta_{2}+1}{1-\zeta_{2}}

(5.5)

\displaystyle=-\pi\frac{\mathsf{Re}\,\alpha}{n}.

On the other hand, for
\alpha=\pm n
, it follows directly from (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) that
A_{n}(\alpha)
has purely imaginary spectrum. Therefore,

(5.6)

\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A_{n}(\pm n))}\mathsf{Re}\,\frac{1}{\lambda}=0.

Finally, comparing results (
5.5
) and (
5.6
) with Proposition
5.2
, we conclude with the following generalisation of Theorem
4.7
that clarifies the appearance of the peculiar constant
\alpha=\pm 2
in the model of Bamberger, Rauch, and Taylor.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the eigenvectors of
A_{n}(\alpha)
form a Riesz basis in
\mathcal{H}
if and only if
\alpha\neq\pm n
.
