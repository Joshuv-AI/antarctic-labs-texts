Adaptive changes in behavioral and physiological traits during worker–reproductive transition in Reticulitermes chinensis (Blattodea: Rhinotermitidae)
Authors: Yalin Xiao, Huan Wang, Xin Peng, Yonghui Wang, Xinyue Li, Yanan Dong, Louis Hesler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe differentiation of workers into supplementary reproductives (ergatoids) is an important component of reproductive plasticity in termites, but the behavioral and physiological changes accompanying this transition remain incompletely understood. We used Reticulitermes chinensis (Snyder) to examine allogrooming, locomotor behavior, egg production, enzyme activity, and gene expression during the worker-to-ergatoid transition. The first ergatoids appeared within 2 days after orphaning, and female ergatoids outnumbered male ergatoids. Ergatoids that survived to the end of the observation period differentiated earlier than those that did not. Compared with workers, ergatoids received more grooming, gave less grooming, and showed lower locomotor velocity, shorter distance traveled, and reduced moving time. Total egg number was positively associated with the body mass of the largest female ergatoid and with the number of surviving workers, but not with the number of female ergatoid reproductives. Catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and acid phosphatase (ACP) activities were higher in ergatoids than in workers. Based on biological replicate means, gene-expression analyses showed higher CAT expression in both ergatoid sexes, higher SOD expression in female ergatoids, and a female-biased ACP expression pattern. These results describe behavioral and physiological changes associated with reproductive transition in laboratory groups of R. chinensis.Keywords: neoteny, eusociality, antioxidant, fecundity, caste

Introduction

Termites live in complex societies characterized by reproductive division of labor, wherein only a few fertile individuals (kings and queens) monopolize reproduction, while numerous sterile workers and soldiers forgo their own direct fitness to perform tasks such as foraging, cooperative brood care, and nest defense (Thorne 1997, Korb and Hartfelder 2008, Bignell et al. 2010). As the sole producers of offspring, these limited reproductives are essential for colony stability and perpetuation. Despite sharing the same genome, reproductives in some termite species can live substantially longer than their sterile nestmates (Jemielity et al. 2005). Certain termite lineages have retained developmental plasticity that allows workers to differentiate into apterous supplementary reproductives when primary reproductives are lost, thereby maintaining colony growth and continuity (Korb and Hartfelder 2008, Leniaud et al. 2011, Korb 2015). This reproductive plasticity gives colonies flexibility to respond to social and environmental change and contributes to their evolutionary and ecological success (Ye et al. 2019).

This reproductive transition unfolds within a social environment where reproductive opportunities can be contested. Because termites are diploid in both sexes, workers under certain colony contexts can potentially gain greater direct fitness by producing their own offspring than indirect fitness by rearing siblings (Zimmerman 1983). Consequently, the loss of primary reproductives frequently triggers intense intracolony reproductive conflict (Roisin 1994). Competition may begin before visible morphological differentiation: in Reticulitermes labralis, “elusive” workers that avoid high-conflict interactions are more likely to become replacement reproductives than highly aggressive nestmates (Bai et al. 2022, 2023). Once differentiation is initiated, cooperative policing further regulates the reproductive hierarchy. Early-differentiating individuals actively attack late-developing competitors and can recruit workers via vibratory signals to participate in cannibalistic policing, thereby establishing reproductive dominance (Sun et al. 2020, Zhou et al. 2026). Once dominance is established, ergatoids may further stabilize reproductive division of labor through sex-specific inhibition of nestmate differentiation (Sun et al. 2017). Ergatoid formation is therefore a multifaceted process shaped by the interplay of developmental potential, social competition, and behavioral regulation.

Once the reproductive hierarchy stabilizes, ergatoids and workers are expected to diverge behaviorally and functionally. In functional colonies, workers perform most allogrooming and daily maintenance and preferentially invest in reproductives and offspring, whereas reproductives reduce self-maintenance and rely more heavily on worker care (Lainé and Wright 2003, Chouvenc 2022). Similarly, workers typically traverse larger areas for foraging, nest maintenance, and social interactions, whereas reproductives remain relatively sedentary in protected nest regions. This strategy may reduce energy expenditure, injury risk, and exposure to extrinsic mortality factors (Noirot and Darlington 2000, Bourke 2007). Accordingly, we hypothesized that the worker-to-ergatoid transition would be accompanied by reduced grooming given and locomotor activity, together with increased grooming received. Reproductive output within laboratory groups may depend not only on the number of ergatoids but also on the physiological capacity of leading female reproductives and the logistical support provided by workers, as termite queens can adjust reproductive allocation according to worker support (Matsuura and Kobayashi 2010).

The behavioral transition and increased reproductive investment of ergatoids may also impose distinct physiological demands. Continuous oogenesis, nutrient mobilization, and elevated mitochondrial activity can increase reactive oxygen species production and oxidative damage if not properly regulated (Beaulieu et al. 2015, Tasaki et al. 2017). Catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) are key antioxidant enzymes involved in detoxifying hydrogen peroxide and superoxide radicals, whereas acid phosphatase (ACP) is a lysosomal enzyme associated with phagocytic immunity, intracellular degradation, and physiological maintenance (Xia et al. 2000). Therefore, measuring the activities and transcript levels of CAT, SOD, and ACP can reveal antioxidant and lysosomal/immune-related remodeling during ergatoid activation. Because females bear greater direct costs of vitellogenesis and abdominal expansion than males, these physiological responses may also be sex-specific.

Previous studies have clarified several important components of ergatoid formation. These include pre-differentiation behavioral variation (Bai et al. 2022, 2023), cooperative policing and nestmate regulation during differentiation (Sun et al. 2017, 2020, Zhou et al. 2026), social care networks surrounding established reproductives (Chouvenc 2022), and the molecular or endocrine bases of caste differentiation (Weil et al. 2007, Saiki and Maekawa 2011, Saiki et al. 2015). However, fewer studies have examined behavioral changes, reproductive output, enzyme activity, and targeted transcript responses within a single experimental framework, particularly in the subterranean termite Reticulitermes chinensis (Snyder).

To address these gaps, this study examined behavioral and physiological changes during the transition from workers to ergatoids in R. chinensis. Specifically, we (i) assessed temporal dynamics of ergatoid differentiation and changes in allogrooming and locomotor behavior; (ii) analyzed social and individual factors associated with total egg production; and (iii) compared sex-specific differences in the activities of antioxidant and immune-related enzymes and in the expression levels of their corresponding genes, including CAT, SOD, and ACP, among workers, female ergatoids, and male ergatoids. Together, these analyses describe behavioral and physiological changes associated with the worker-to-ergatoid transition in this species.

Materials and Methods

Study Termites

Six colonies of R. chinensis were collected from Guifeng Temple, Shaanxi Province, China, in 2024. Termites were extracted from the cracks of dead wood. Then, groups of 200 individuals (198 workers and 2 soldiers) were housed in plastic Petri dishes (9-cm diameter × 1.52-cm height; Guangzhou Jet Bio-Filtration Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) containing moist filter paper as a food and water source. A total of 100 laboratory groups were established and kept in a climate chamber (25 °C, 70% relative humidity, and constant darkness). After approximately 25 to 30 days, worker differentiation into ergatoids was observed. These established laboratory groups served as the source populations for the following experiments. The distribution of the 6 source colonies among the experimental units used in each assay is summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Orphaning Assay to Observe Ergatoid Number and Survival

This experimental setup was used to simulate reproductive replacement following the loss of primary reproductives. We established new laboratory groups using individuals selected from the source populations described in the Study termites section. Each experimental replicate consisted of 30 workers, 1 soldier, and 1 previously differentiated ergatoid (functioning as the active reproductive), housed in plastic Petri dishes (3.5-cm diameter × 1.26-cm height; Guangzhou Jet Bio-Filtration Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China) containing moistened paper towel substrate, with a total of 20 replicates. The reproductive individual in each laboratory group was removed after a 3-day acclimation period, and the number of differentiating ergatoids and their survival were recorded. All termites were maintained in complete darkness at 25 ± 1 °C until reproductive individuals appeared in the nest and began laying eggs, with the entire experiment lasting 50 days. Starting from the first day of isolation, the Petri dishes were inspected daily to identify the sex and count the number of ergatoids differentiated from workers. The sex of ergatoids was determined under a stereomicroscope based on abdominal sternite morphology. Female ergatoids were distinguished from male ergatoids by the enlarged seventh abdominal sternite, following criteria used in previous studies of Reticulitermes ergatoids. Ergatoids that differentiated from workers were not removed, allowing us to observe the number of remaining reproductives in each laboratory group. In the following analysis, individuals that survived until the end of the observation period were termed “survivors,” whereas individuals that disappeared or died after differentiation were termed “non-surviving ergatoids.”

Behavioral Data Collection

To track the allogrooming and locomotor behaviors of workers, female ergatoids, and male ergatoids, we established 14 replicate laboratory groups (23 individuals each: 20 workers, 1 female ergatoid, 1 male ergatoid, and 1 soldier) maintained in plastic Petri dishes (3.5-cm diam × 1.26-cm ht) covered with wet pure cellulose paper. We applied different colored dots to the thoraces of the female ergatoid, male ergatoid, and 1 worker using Street Graffiti paint (MINISO, Guangzhou, China), followed by a 7-day acclimation period in a climate-controlled chamber. After acclimation, behaviors in the 14 groups were recorded for 30 min at 12:00 PM using a Sony AX100E camera. The videos were then analyzed manually to quantify social interactions. We recorded: (i) grooming received, defined as grooming time received from other nestmates; (ii) grooming given, defined as grooming time given to other nestmates; and (iii) the number of butting occurrences, a behavior associated with reproductive dominance in which an individual repeatedly moves backward and forward while contacting another individual (Korb et al. 2009).

Locomotor Trajectory Analysis

Locomotor trajectories of workers and ergatoids were further analyzed from the same video recordings using a computerized EthoVision XT 15 tracking system (Noldus Information Technology, Beijing, China). To generate the automated behavioral measurements, the software was first provided with the test arena dimensions (3.5 cm). The target termite was selected in the trial control settings, based on the different color markings applied to the focal worker and ergatoids, and videos were captured at 5 frames per second. To keep time settings consistent across recordings, we used the video acquisition system in software to capture 30 min of the videos. Distance traveled, locomotor velocity, moving time, and resting time were calculated using the system. Motion trajectories were generated after video acquisition.

Egg Production of New Supplementary Queens

To examine how egg production was associated with the body mass of newly differentiated ergatoid reproductives and the worker population within laboratory groups, 63 laboratory groups were selected from the source colonies described in Study termites and maintained in a climate chamber (25 °C, 70% relative humidity, and constant darkness). During the experiment, sterile water and filter paper were supplemented irregularly to sustain colony conditions. After a 3-month isolation period, each laboratory group was dissected, and the following data were recorded: (i) the total number of surviving workers; (ii) the number of female and male ergatoid reproductives; (iii) the body mass of each ergatoid reproductive, measured using a precision electronic balance; and (iv) the total egg number within the laboratory group, manually counted under a stereomicroscope. Because not all variables were available for every laboratory group, the sample size differed among analyses: n = 50 for the analysis of female ergatoid reproductive number and largest female body mass, n = 56 for the number of surviving workers, and n = 45 for male ergatoid reproductive body mass.

Enzyme Activity Assays

We used assay kits (purchased from the Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute) to determine the activities of antioxidant and immune-related enzymes in 3 sample groups: workers, female ergatoids, and male ergatoids. Whole-body samples were rinsed with distilled water and disinfected with 95% ethanol before homogenization. Samples were weighed and homogenized in phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4; containing 137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, and 1.8 mM KH2PO4) at a weight-to-volume ratio of 1:9 (g: ml). The mixture was mechanically homogenized on an ice bath using a handheld tissue grinder (Coyote G50; Shanghai Logan-Tech Automation Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 6,000 rpm for 3 short pulses of 10 s each to prevent heat accumulation, with the sample volume strictly maintained under 400 μl during homogenization. The homogenate was then subjected to vortex mixing and centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant containing tissue fluid was transferred to a new tube and used as the sample for protein quantification and enzyme assays. Before the enzyme assays, the total protein (TP) concentration of each supernatant was determined using a TP Assay Kit based on the BCA method (catalog no. A045-4; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. These tissue fluid samples were preserved at −80 °C until use. All colorimetric enzyme assays and protein quantifications were performed using a multi-mode microplate reader (SuPerMax 3000FA; Shanghai Spirits Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. CAT activity was determined by measuring the decomposition of H2O2 at 240 nm. SOD activity was evaluated via the hydroxylamine method by measuring the inhibition of superoxide anion radicals at 550 nm. ACP activity was determined using disodium phenyl phosphate as the substrate and measured at 520 nm. All enzyme activities were normalized to the TP concentration. Six biological replicates were performed for each caste, each with 2 individuals.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR

We analyzed the expression of 2 antioxidant genes (CAT and SOD) and the acid phosphatase gene (ACP) in workers, female ergatoids, and male ergatoids by quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR). We designed primer pairs for each gene using Primer3, and the primers used for real-time PCR are listed in Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.5.2 (R Core Team 2025), and figures were prepared using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts, United States). Negative-binomial generalized linear models were fitted using the MASS package (Venables and Ripley 2002), and linear mixed-effects models were fitted using the nlme package. The laboratory group was the experimental unit for the orphaning, behavioral, locomotor, and egg-production analyses. The biological replicate was the experimental unit for the enzyme-activity and RT-qPCR analyses.

For the orphaning assay, the difference between survival and mortality was analyzed using a linear model, with source colony as a fixed blocking factor. Female and male ergatoid numbers were analyzed using a binomial generalized linear model with source colony as a fixed blocking factor. Differentiation time was analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model, with laboratory group nested within source colony. Behavioral count data were analyzed using negative-binomial generalized linear models with caste and laboratory group included as fixed effects. Locomotor variables were analyzed using linear models with caste and laboratory group included as fixed effects. Overall caste effects were assessed by comparing models with and without caste, and pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Holm method. Total egg number was also analyzed as count data using negative-binomial generalized linear models with the focal predictor and source colony included as fixed effects, and predictor effects were assessed using likelihood-ratio tests. Enzyme activities were analyzed using linear models with caste and source colony included as fixed effects. For RT-qPCR, technical replicates were averaged within each biological replicate, and statistical analyses were performed on log2-transformed relative expression values using linear models with caste and source colony included as fixed effects. Untransformed 2−ΔΔCt values were used for graphical presentation. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Differentiation of Supplementary Ergatoids

The first supplementary ergatoids appeared within 2 days after orphaning, with an average of 5.7 ergatoids per laboratory group (Table 2). During the 50-day observation period, mortality exceeded survival among newly differentiated ergatoids after accounting for source colony (t = −7.45; df = 14; P < 0.001; Fig. 1A), and female ergatoids outnumbered male ergatoids (z = 5.73; P < 0.001; Fig. 1B). In addition, surviving ergatoids initiated differentiation earlier than non-surviving ergatoids, with an estimated mean difference of 10.52 days (SE = 1.69; t = 6.22; df = 14; P < 0.001; Fig. 2).

Allogrooming

Grooming given differed among workers, female ergatoids, and male ergatoids (likelihood-ratio χ2 = 2999.18; df = 2; P < 0.001; Fig. 3A). Workers spent more time grooming nestmates than did female ergatoids and male ergatoids (both Holm-adjusted P < 0.001), whereas female and male ergatoids did not differ (Holm-adjusted P = 0.53). Grooming received also differed among groups (likelihood-ratio χ2 = 3688.88; df = 2; P < 0.001; Fig. 3B). Workers received less grooming than female ergatoids and male ergatoids (both Holm-adjusted P < 0.001), whereas female and male ergatoids did not differ significantly (Holm-adjusted P = 0.10). The overall caste effect on butting frequency was not significant (likelihood-ratio χ2 = 4.96; df = 2; P = 0.08; Fig. 3C). Female ergatoids had higher butting rates than workers (P = 0.046) and male ergatoids (P = 0.045), whereas workers and male ergatoids did not differ (P = 0.87).

Locomotion and Trajectory Analysis

Locomotor velocity differed among workers, female ergatoids, and male ergatoids (F = 22.21; df = 2, 26; P < 0.001; Fig. 4A). Workers moved faster than both female ergatoids and male ergatoids (both P < 0.001), whereas female and male ergatoids did not differ (P = 0.53). Distance traveled also differed among groups (F = 23.89; df = 2, 26; P < 0.001; Fig. 4B), with workers traveling farther than female ergatoids and male ergatoids (both P < 0.001), whereas female and male ergatoids did not differ (P = 0.50). Moving time differed among groups (F = 27.13; df = 2, 26; P < 0.001; Fig. 4C), and workers spent more time moving than female ergatoids and male ergatoids (both P < 0.001), whereas female and male ergatoids did not differ (P = 0.18). Resting time also differed among groups (F = 17.61; df = 2, 26; P < 0.001; Fig. 4D), with workers spending less time resting than female ergatoids and male ergatoids (both P < 0.001), whereas female and male ergatoids did not differ (P = 0.51).

Discussion

The onset of ergatoid differentiation observed in this study indicates that workers of R. chinensis possess developmental plasticity that can be mobilized shortly after the loss of primary reproductives (Huang et al. 2013, Ye et al. 2019). Our results showed that the first ergatoid appeared as early as 2 days after orphaning, and an average of 5.7 individuals per laboratory group initiated the transition from worker to reproductive status during the 50-day observation period. However, this initial differentiation was followed by considerable mortality within the newly formed reproductive cohort, likely reflecting reproductive conflict among differentiating workers (Roisin 1994, Hoffmann and Korb 2011). Notably, surviving ergatoids initiated differentiation significantly earlier than non-surviving ergatoids, suggesting that the timing of differentiation may be an important factor influencing competitive outcomes. One possible explanation is that early differentiators establish reproductive status before later competitors appear. Although direct physical conflict was not the primary focus of our observations, the observed survival advantage of early differentiators is consistent with the cooperative policing mechanism described by Sun et al. (2020): established reproductives may recruit workers to participate in cannibalistic policing of later differentiating competitors, thereby consolidating their dominance.

Furthermore, we observed a clear excess of female ergatoids relative to male ergatoids in the laboratory groups, with an average of 4.5 females but only 1.2 males per group. This female-biased pattern may reflect colony-level selection for rapid reproductive recovery through increased egg production (Matsuura and Nishida 2001), but it may also be influenced by sex-specific differentiation strategies (Kobayashi et al. 2013). For example, research on the closely related species Reticulitermes speratus has shown that many males can achieve sexual maturity without undergoing the conspicuous morphological molts typical of ergatoids, and these inconspicuous matured males are functionally reproductive while remaining outwardly indistinguishable from ordinary workers (Fujita and Watanabe 2010). Therefore, the low number of conspicuous male ergatoids recorded in this study may not fully reflect the actual extent of male reproductive participation. It is possible that a portion of the male population adopted, or had the potential to adopt, an inconspicuous reproductive strategy. Such a strategy might reduce the energetic costs of morphological remodeling and help individuals avoid social culling and cannibalism directed toward conspicuous reproductive competitors during the early stages of reproductive transition (Fujita and Watanabe 2010, Sun et al. 2020).

Compared with workers, both female and male ergatoids received more allogrooming from nestmates and spent less time grooming others, a pattern similar to care-biased interactions reported for infected queens in the same species (Dong et al. 2024). This shift suggests that ergatoids may become beneficiaries of social care after establishing reproductive status, while workers continue to perform maintenance-related social tasks. Although reproductive costs and worker care allocation may differ between male and female reproductives in termites (Fujita and Watanabe 2010, Chouvenc 2019), our grooming results did not indicate a strong sex-specific difference: female and male ergatoids did not differ significantly in grooming given, and the difference in grooming received between the sexes was not significant after multiple-comparison correction. In addition, although the overall caste effect on butting frequency was not significant, pairwise comparisons showed that female ergatoids had higher butting rates than workers and male ergatoids. This pattern is consistent with the role of butting as a signal associated with reproductive dominance (Korb 2018). In Zootermopsis nevadensis, workers increased head-butting within 6 to 12 h after the loss of primary reproductives, suggesting that butting can function as an early signal of reproductive competition (Penick et al. 2013). In the present study, observations were made after ergatoids had already established reproductive status. Therefore, butting may vary across the stages of reproductive transition, being more intense during early competition and becoming less frequent once the reproductive hierarchy is stabilized.

Regarding locomotion, workers exhibited significantly higher velocity, distance traveled, and moving time than both female and male ergatoids, whereas ergatoids allocated more time to resting. Trajectory maps further demonstrated that workers traveled extensively throughout the Petri dish, whereas both female and male ergatoids were largely restricted to corner areas. This caste-specific locomotor pattern appears to be adaptively matched to social roles: workers remain mobile to perform foraging, nest maintenance, and brood-care-related tasks, whereas ergatoids adopt a more sedentary pattern that may help conserve energy for reproduction (Shimoji et al. 2020, Bai et al. 2026). In laboratory settings, this restricted movement may also reflect a behavioral predisposition to remain in protected and stable regions within natural nest structures (Lainé et al. 2003). Such reduced movement could also limit exposure to attacks or other conflict-prone social interactions during reproductive transition (Sun et al. 2020).

The egg production assay revealed that total egg number was not significantly associated with the number of female ergatoid reproductives. Instead, total egg number was positively associated with the body mass of the largest female ergatoid and with the number of surviving workers, consistent with findings in R. speratus, where queen body weight predicted the number of eggs laid per 24 h, and total egg production was proportional to worker number (Takata et al. 2023). This indicates that the reproductive potential is not achieved simply by accumulating more female ergatoid reproductives. Rather, reproductive output is constrained by individual physiological limits and the colony’s logistical support capacity. The positive association between total egg number and the body mass of the largest female is consistent with the general expectation that body size contributes to fecundity in social insects. Larger females can store more nutritional reserves (eg fat) and possess more extensive abdominal space to accommodate developing ovaries. These traits provide the physiological foundation to sustain the high energetic demands of intensive oogenesis.

To sustain reproductive activity and cope with the physiological demands associated with reproductive activation, ergatoids showed higher activities of several maintenance-related enzymes. In this study, CAT, SOD, and ACP activities were higher in both female and male ergatoids than in workers, indicating enhanced antioxidant and lysosomal or immune-related maintenance during the worker-to-reproductive transition. Higher CAT and SOD activities are consistent with the need to manage reactive oxygen species generated by reproductive metabolism (Tasaki et al. 2017). Such physiological maintenance may help reproductives cope with the costs of egg production and may contribute to the unusual longevity of termite reproductives (Tasaki et al. 2021). Higher ACP activity may reflect enhanced lysosomal or immune-related processes in reproductive individuals (Lavine and Strand 2002). Although females are expected to bear higher reproductive costs than males because of continuous oogenesis and abdominal expansion (Tasaki et al. 2017, Nozaki and Matsuura 2019), female ergatoids showed higher CAT and SOD activities than male ergatoids, whereas ACP activity did not differ significantly between the sexes. Therefore, the enzyme activity results suggest that reproductive differentiation was accompanied by enhanced physiological maintenance in both sexes, with additional sex-specific differences in antioxidant enzyme activity.

RT-qPCR results provided further evidence that physiological regulation during reproductive transition may occur at the transcriptional level. Both female and male ergatoids showed higher CAT expression than workers, suggesting that activation of this antioxidant-related gene is associated with reproductive differentiation in both sexes. For SOD, female ergatoids showed higher expression than both workers and male ergatoids, whereas male ergatoids did not differ significantly from workers. For ACP, expression was highest in female ergatoids, intermediate in male ergatoids, and lowest in workers. These patterns suggest that sex-related regulation occurs at both the enzyme-activity and gene-expression levels, although the patterns are not entirely consistent across the 3 enzymes. The female-biased expression of SOD and ACP is consistent with the greater reproductive investment required for oogenesis and abdominal expansion, which may increase the demand for antioxidant and immune-related maintenance (Tasaki et al. 2017, Nozaki and Matsuura 2019). However, the partial mismatch between transcript abundance and enzyme activity also indicates that gene expression alone may not fully predict physiological function, because enzyme activity can be influenced by post-transcriptional regulation, protein stability, substrate availability, and the physiological state of individuals.

Together, these findings indicate that the worker-to-ergatoid transition in R. chinensis is accompanied by coordinated changes in social behavior, locomotor activity, reproductive output, enzyme activity, and gene expression. After acquiring reproductive status, ergatoids received more grooming, gave less grooming, and showed reduced locomotor activity relative to workers, suggesting a shift from worker-like tasks toward reproductive function. At the group level, total egg number was positively associated with the body mass of the largest female ergatoid and the number of surviving workers, but not with the number of female ergatoid reproductives. At the physiological level, ergatoids showed elevated antioxidant and immune-related enzyme activities compared with workers, with additional sex-specific differences in CAT and SOD activities, while RT-qPCR results suggested female-biased expression of selected genes, especially SOD and ACP. Together, these results suggest that reproductive output in laboratory groups was associated not only with the physiological capacity of reproductive individuals but also with worker support. This perspective may help explain how individual physiological plasticity and group-level social organization jointly support reproductive transition, providing insight into the evolution of reproductive division of labor in social insects.
