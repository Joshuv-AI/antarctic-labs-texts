Kidney-therapy decision-making among older adults and caregivers: treatment beliefs, prognostic information, and health priorities
Authors: Fahad Saeed, Shabir Ahmed, Rebecca J. Allen, Semra Ozdemir, Kevin A. Fiscella, Ronald M. Epstein, Paul R. Duberstein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt is critical to ensure that older patients and families understand the risks and benefits of each available treatment option when contemplating kidney therapy (KT) decisions. This study investigates older patients’ and caregivers’ beliefs regarding chronic kidney disease (CKD) curability, anticipated quality of life (QoL) with dialysis, trade-offs between longevity and QoL, frequency of prognostic discussions and views on remaining life expectancy.MethodsWe conducted secondary analyses of orally administered questionnaire-based data collected in two intervention studies aimed at improving KT decision-making for older adults with advanced CKD.ResultsWe present descriptive statistics to summarize findings. Among 118 patients (mean age 79 years) and 60 caregivers (mean age 68 years), 54% patients and 75% caregivers were women, with 75% patients and 79% caregivers identifying as White. When asked about their CKD curability, nearly 35% patients and 15% caregivers did not know or were unsure, while 10% patients and 12% caregivers expressed optimism. Participants generally lacked clarity on dialysis’s impact on QoL. Furthermore, most participants (85% patients and 92% caregivers) did not have prognostic discussions about life expectancy with their nephrologists and were either uncertain or optimistic about future 1 and 2-year survival rates. Many participants prioritized QoL—functional status (45% vs. 48%), reduced suffering (70% vs. 24%), and fewer hospitalizations (48% vs. 45%)—over choosing dialysis.ConclusionInaccurate beliefs about CKD curability, the benefits of KT options, lack of prognostic discussions and prognostic uncertainty are common among older adults and caregivers facing treatment decisions, highlighting the need for educational interventions for nephrologists, patients and families.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04682-3.

Introduction

For older people receiving maintenance dialysis, life expectancy is often short [1], and marked by decline in functional status [2, 3] and hospitalizations [4]—thus, for some older patients, conservative kidney management (CKM) is a preference concordant and viable dialysis alternative [5]. Many patients may not fully understand the effects of dialysis on quality of life (QoL) [6], and may have limited knowledge about their CKD [7–9]. To support shared decision-making about kidney therapy (KT), it is essential to understand patients’ beliefs about chronic kidney disease (CKD) and its curability, as well as their views on the harms and benefits of each KT option, their remaining life expectancy, and the trade-offs they are willing to consider. These discussions significantly influence KT decisions, and, in turn, shape the future life trajectories of both older patients and their families [5, 10, 11]. Yet, such comprehensive discussions are rare in clinical settings [12–15]. Consequently, dialysis regret [16], inadequate end-of-life (EoL) planning, and burdensome EoL experiences are common among this population [17].

In this context, treatment beliefs about CKD curability, the effects of dialysis on the future QoL, prognostic expectations, and patient priorities for treatment trade-offs emerge as critical drivers in KT decision making. While prior studies have described treatment beliefs in the context of poor CKD knowledge [7–9] and have shown that dialysis is often framed as a means to enhance QoL [19, 20], the existing literature on the treatment beliefs among older patients with CKD remains limited. Moreover, most studied on perceived life expectancy lump all adult patients together, failing to distinguish older patients undergoing maintenance dialysis as destination therapy from younger, transplant-eligible individuals [21]. Studies examining trade-offs in KT decisions making are non-US based [22], not focused on symptoms related to dialysis while weighing treatment trade-offs [23], not focused on older people [24], or are not conducted in the context of KT decision making [25]. Data on caregiver beliefs and views on all these saliant issues are generally lacking.

Consequently, this survey study advances the literature by simultaneously investigating the beliefs of older patients (aged 65 and above) and their caregivers (when available) regarding chronic kidney disease (CKD) curability, anticipated quality of life (QoL) with dialysis, remaining life expectancy and frequency of prognostic discussions and trade-offs between longevity and quality of life (QoL). This study also emphasizes the need to understand patients’ and caregivers’ perspectives—and common misconceptions—about dialysis, and to develop effective communication and educational interventions. These efforts can support shared decision-making around the tradeoffs patients and families face when selecting a kidney therapy option.

Methods

The current study is a secondary analysis of survey data collected at study entry in two pilot intervention studies aimed at improving kidney therapy decision-making for older individuals with CKD and their caregivers (if available) [26, 27]. Study 1 (the CKD-EDU study, NCT03465449, registration date: 03-07-2018) examined the feasibility and acceptability of a palliative care intervention for individuals aged 75 and older with advanced chronic kidney disease, along with their caregivers, if available [26]. Study 2 (the DIAL-SDM study, NCT04392440, registration date: 05-01-2020) explored the feasibility and acceptability of a combined communication coaching intervention for older patients aged 65 and above, along with their caregivers, to improve kidney therapy decision-making [27]. Supplementary Table 1 outlines the relevant details of each study. Notably, both studies presented CKM as a viable and valid option and cross enrollment of participants did not occur. A research coordinator orally administered the surveys separately to patients and caregivers, in office, at home or at another preferred location. We adopted the survey questions from the prior literature, and the questions underwent face validity for clarity [11, 28]. The survey primarily focused on exploring older patients’ and their caregivers’ beliefs about their chronic kidney disease curability, quality of life with dialysis, the prognostic trade-offs that patients and their families are willing to make while contemplating kidney therapy decisions, the frequency of prognostic discussions and views on remaining life expectancy. Both studies asked the same set of questions, except for those regarding life expectancy (see Table 3). Study 1 asked, “What do you believe are the chances that you will live for 1 year or more?” and “What do you believe are the chances that you will live for 2 years or more?” Response options included: (a) Do not know, (b) 0–25%, (c) 50/50, (d) 75%-100%, and (e) refused to answer. In contrast, Study 2 asked, “How long would you guess people your age with similar health conditions usually live?” Response options included: (a) I am not sure, (b) less than 2 years, b) 2–5 years, (c) 5–10 years, and (d) more than 10 years. The University of Rochester IRB approved both studies following standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsink and all the participants provided informed written consent.

Results

Table 1 presents the demographic characteristics of the study participants (a total of 178 participants: n = 118 patients, 60 caregivers). The mean eGFR in the CKD-EDU study was 19.3 ml/min with a standard deviation of 4.8, and in the DIAL-SDM study was 21.25 ml/min with standard deviation of 3.5. The mean age of the patients and caregivers was 79.20 (± 6.39) and 68.13 (± 13.22), respectively. Among the participants, 54% of patients and 75% of caregivers self-identified as women. In terms of racial and ethnic background, 74.6% of patients and 79% of caregivers self-identified as White, 22% of patients and 19% of caregivers as Black or African American, 7.6% of patients as others. Regarding annual income, 53% of patients and 40% of caregivers reported a yearly household income of $50,000 or less, while 37% of patients and 31% of caregivers had attained education at or below the high school level. Comorbid conditions were not collected for the CKD-EDU study. For the DIAL-SDM study, 90% of patients in the intervention group and 87% in the control group had three or more comorbid conditions. The study patients had been seeing a nephrologist but had not yet attended a kidney therapy education class. Their nephrologist had indicated the need for a kidney therapy decision; however, discussions about dialysis versus CKM (conservative kidney management) with the nephrologist or within social networks prior to enrollment were not captured in our study.

Discussion

In this study of an under-researched cohort of patients aged ≥ 75 years with CKD and their caregivers, we found that most were aware that CKD is incurable but substantial minorities were either uncertain or held inaccurate beliefs regarding CKD curability, their quality of life with dialysis and their life expectancy. Most reported not having had discussions regarding quality of life or life expectancy with their nephrologist. Our study also revealed that many patients would forgo dialysis if it involved trade-offs such as decline in functional status, suffering, and hospitalizations. Three aspects of our findings warrant further discussion.

First, our study sheds light on patients and caregivers’ treatment beliefs i.e. beliefs about CKD curability and anticipated QoL with dialysis. We found although a majority of older patients did not expect their CKD to be cured, a substantial proportion (35%) did not know or were uncertain about CKD curability and some (10%) were optimistic despite not being on kidney transplant list. Prior studies have shown a high prevalence of poor awareness of CKD diagnosis [18, 29] and low CKD knowledge [7–9] among people with CKD. Research has also indicated that most (but not all) patients wish to acquire more than basic CKD knowledge [30] and that improved CKD knowledge is associated with greater satisfaction with physician communication and enhanced CKD self-management [31]. Our study contributes to the literature by exploring older patients’ and caregivers’ beliefs about CKD curability, a novel finding shedding light into their very basic CKD knowledge about CKD curability. Additionally, most participants in this study, who were facing kidney therapy decisions, expressed uncertainty whether dialysis would improve (14% of patients and 15% of caregivers), worsen (22% for both), or maintain (14% of patients and 23% of caregivers) their quality of life. Our findings, in line with prior qualitative research with older adults, suggest that patients do not have in-depth knowledge of dialysis options [32] and their concerns about QoL often remain unaddressed [13, 33] despite a desire to engage in such conversations [34]. Interestingly, prior literature shows that patients generally recognize the major impact of dialysis initiation on their lives [35]. In this context, our study advances literature by highlighting the uncertainty of older patients and their caregivers regarding the QoL impact of dialysis. Future studies examining the influence of social media and the for-profit dialysis industry on shaping patients’ treatment beliefs would be informative [20, 36].

Second, 60% of patients in CKD-EDU thought they would live for up to 2 years. Indeed, 64% (37/58) were still alive by the end of year-2. At the same time, a substantial minority reported being uncertain about their life expectancy. Prognostic uncertainty can serve as a means to maintain optimism or could be epistemic in nature in the absence of high-quality prognostic data or discussions [37, 38]. It is concerning that the majority did not have prognostic discussion with their nephrologists. Prior studies have shown discordance between the prognostic beliefs of nephrologists and their patients [39] and how prognostic optimism is associated with high-intensity end-of-life care [11]. Research among patients receiving dialysis has also indicated a desire among patients for prognostic conversations [12, 13, 40] and the absence of such discussions is associated with dialysis regret [16]. Our study advances the literature by highlighting how rare prognostic discussions are for older individuals with advanced CKD and how people experience prognostic uncertainty and tend to develop prognostic optimism. Future interventions to promote prognostic discussions are needed.

Last, It is important to emphasize that many patients in our study valued QoL over survival. These findings broadly align with other studies demonstrating that older patients wish to preserve their independence and other aspects of QoL [22–25, 41–43]. Yet, our study advances the literature by examining this unique US-based cohort of older people with CKD who are asked to actively contemplate kidney therapy choices. Indeed, dialysis may potentially impair many domains of QoL, including decline in functional status, symptoms like fatigue, hospitalizations, and time spent receiving dialysis [1–4, 44–46]. However, a few studies also suggest either slowed decline in QoL with dialysis [47] or initial decline and later stabilization [48]. Future large-scale studies aimed at identifying predictors of quality-of-life domains in older patients could be valuable.

This study has several strengths, limitations, and implications. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies exploring the treatment beliefs of US-based older patients with advanced CKD and caregivers who are already receiving nephrology care, are not transplant list and are contemplating kidney therapy choices. However, we acknowledge that our sample size is limited, precluding any inferential analyses or analyses to compare differences across study sites. Our study participants were drawn from Rochester, NY, at a university hospital and two community hospitals and have less representation of Hispanic patients. Nevertheless, our study calls for efforts to ensure that decision aids clearly outline details about CKD curability and impact of dialysis on QoL. This is especially important as prior research shows how education material developed by for-profit dialysis industry can provide biased information [20]. Additionally, use of prognostic tools [38] and interventions to enhance prognostic understanding of older patients and caregivers during kidney therapy decision-making are critical. Future qualitative studies involving older adults with CKD should explore the most effective ways to deliver prognostic information, incorporating health literacy, access to care, worldviews, and influence of religion and culture on patient’s’ attitude toward death and dying [49–53]. Ultimately, the goal is to equip nephrologists with these skills [54]. Prior studies have shown that prognostic disclosure is associated with more realistic beliefs without disrupting the physician-patient alliance [55]. Training nephrology teams in primary palliative care skills, including communication, prognostic disclosure, and shared decision-making is imperative [56]. Involvement and support from palliative care specialists will be necessary in some cases. These modifications in nephrology care will require changes in nephrology culture and social norms to encourage prognostic and end-of-life discussions and healthcare financing such as financially incentivizing CKM [57, 58].

In conclusion, we showed that a substantial minority of patients and caregivers did not know that CKD is incurable, many expressed uncertainty about their own prognosis, and few reported having discussions about prognosis and treatment trade-offs with their nephrologists. Future educational interventions for nephrologists, patients and families regarding effective prognostic discussions are needed.
