1.
Introduction and statement of the results

In this paper we investigate surfaces
\Sigma
in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
which are critical points of the energy

E_{\alpha}[\Sigma]=\int_{\Sigma}|p|^{\alpha}\,d\Sigma,

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is a parameter and
|p|
denotes the modulus of a generic point
p\in\Sigma
. Critical points of this energy are called
\alpha
-stationary surfaces
and they are characterized by the Euler-Lagrange equation

(1)

H(p)=\alpha\frac{\langle N(p),p\rangle}{|p|^{2}},\quad p\in\Sigma,

where
H
and
N
are the mean curvature and the unit normal vector of
\Sigma
, respectively. Here we are assuming implicitly that the origin
0\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
does not belong to
\Sigma
. If
\alpha=0
, then
E_{0}[\Sigma]
is simply the area of
\Sigma
and
0
-stationary surfaces are minimal surfaces. From now, we will discard the case
\alpha=0
. Another interesting case is
\alpha=2
. In this case, the energy
E_{2}
represents the moment of inertia of
\Sigma
.

Stationary surfaces have been recently studied by Dierkes and Huisken in
[
5
,
7
]
as an extension in arbitrary dimensions of an old problem investigated by Euler on planar curves. In Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, Euler asked for those planar curves
\gamma
of constant density joining two given points of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with least energy
\int_{\gamma}|\gamma|^{\alpha}\,ds
: see
[
10
, p. 53]
. In the case
\alpha=2
, this energy represents the moment of inertia of
\gamma
with respect to
0
. Euler found explicit parametrizations in polar coordinates of these curves (see also
[
4
,
19
]
). Later, Mason found the minimizers for the particular case
\alpha=2
.

For
\alpha
-stationary surfaces, the energy
E_{\alpha}[\Sigma]
is the moment of inertia of
\Sigma
with respect to
0
and with exponent
\alpha
. Recently, the author has obtained a variety of results for
\alpha
-stationary surfaces, also in collaboration with Dierkes. For example, existence of the Plateau problem
[
8
]
, characterizations of the axisymmetric surfaces
[
9
]
, relationship with minimal surfaces
[
15
]
or characterizing those stationary surfaces with constant Gauss curvature
[
16
]
.

Some examples of
\alpha
-stationary surfaces are (see Fig.
1
):

(1)

Vector planes. A plane of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an
\alpha
-stationary surface if and only if the plane contains
0
. This holds for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. An affine plane passing through
0
is a vector plane.

(2)

Spheres. The only sphere that are
\alpha
-stationary surfaces are spheres centered at
0
(
\alpha=-2
) and spheres containing
0
(
\alpha=-4
).

\alpha
-stationary surfaces: vector planes (left), spheres centered at
0
(middle) and spheres crossing
0
(right).

On the other hand, the energy
E_{\alpha}
is not preserved by rigid motions of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For example, if
\Sigma
is an
\alpha
-stationary surface, the translated surface
\Sigma+\vec{v}
,
\vec{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, is not an
\alpha
-stationary surface. Only rigid motions that fix
0
, that is, linear isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, preserve the energy
E_{\alpha}
.

It is natural to classify
\alpha
-stationary surfaces in a class of surfaces of Euclidean space under some geometric properties. For example,
\alpha
-stationary surfaces of rotational type were investigated and classified in
[
8
]
. In this paper, we give two types of classification. First, we classify
\alpha
-stationary surfaces which also are ruled surfaces. Notice than among the above examples, only planes are ruled surfaces.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
\alpha\not=0
, vector planes are the only
\alpha
-stationary surfaces that are ruled surfaces.

Notice that if
\gamma
is a planar curve which it is a critical point of the energy
\int_{\gamma}|p|^{\alpha}\,ds
, then the cylindrical surface
\gamma\times\mathbb{R}\vec{v}
is not, in general, a critical point of
E_{\alpha}
, where
\vec{v}
is a unit vector orthogonal to the plane containing
\gamma
: the only cylindrical surface which it is an
\alpha
-stationary surface is any plane crossing
0
.

The second type of results concerns to the classification of
\alpha
-stationary surfaces of cyclic type. A
cyclic surface
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is surface defined as a smooth one-parameter family of circles, that is, the surface can be foliated by a family of circles. An example of cyclic surface is a surface of revolution, where all circles are coaxial with respect to a straight-line of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Rotational
\alpha
-stationary surfaces were classified in
[
9
]
. On the other hand, spheres are rotational surfaces but it can be also viewed as cyclic surfaces when we intersect a sphere for any smooth one-parameter family of planes. In such a case, the foliation of the sphere is given by circles which are not coaxial. In consequence, spheres can be parametrized as a cyclic surface without being a parametrization of a rotational surface. A first result considers the case that the planes of the foliation are parallel.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be a cyclic
\alpha
-stationary surface. If the planes of the foliation are parallel, then
\Sigma
is a surface of revolution.

If the planes of the foliation are not parallel, we distinguish cases according to
\alpha
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be a cyclic
\alpha
-stationary surface. If the planes of the foliation are not parallel, then
\alpha=-2
or
\alpha=-4
. If
\alpha=-4
, then
\Sigma
is a vector plane, a sphere containing
0
or it is the image by an inversion of a helicoid, a catenoid or a Riemann minimal example.

As consequence of Thms.
1.2
and
1.3
, we have a classification of
\alpha
-stationary surfaces when
\alpha\not=-2,-4
.

Corollary 1.4
.

Any cyclic
\alpha
-stationary surface with
\alpha\not=-2,-4
must be a surface of revolution.

The case
\alpha=-2
is particular and it has its own interest. We will prove that there are examples of non-spherical cyclic
(-2)
-stationary surfaces. See Fig.
3
in Sect.
7
.

The paper is organized as follows. Theorem
1.1
will be proved in Sects.
2
and
3
: we will distinguish if the surface is or is not of cylindrical type. In Sect.
4
we prove Thm.
1.2
. In Sect.
5
we prove Thm.
1.3
when
\alpha=-4
. Section
6
gives the proof of Thm.
1.3
when
\alpha\not=-2,-4
and finally, Section
7
is devoted to the case
\alpha=-2
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1
: part I

The proof of Thm.
1.1
is done in two steps. First, the theorem is proved for the particular case that the ruled surface is a cylindrical surface. In this case, we will prove that the surface is a vector plane. In a second step, we will consider the case of ruled non-cylindrical surfaces. In such a case, we prove that the surface cannot satisfy Eq. (
1
).

A ruled surface is a surface generated by moving a straight line in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. A ruled surface can be parametrized by

(2)

\Psi\colon I\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad X(s,t)=\gamma(s)+t\beta(s),

where
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a regular curve, called the
directrix
which we can suppose to be parametrized by arc-length. The curve
\beta
is a curve on the unit
2
-sphere
\beta\colon I\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
and it indicate the direction of the
rulings
at each point
\alpha(s)
. In case that
\beta
is a constant curve,
\beta(s)=\vec{w}
, the surface is called
cylindrical
.

According to the proof strategy, suppose that
\Sigma
is a cylindrical surface. Then the parametrization (
2
) can be written as

(3)

\Psi\colon I\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad\Psi(s,t)=\gamma(s)+t\vec{w},

where
\gamma\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a curve parametrized by arc-length,
|\vec{w}|=1
and
\gamma
is contained in a plane orthogonal to
\vec{w}
. In the following result, we classify all cylindrical
\alpha
-stationary surfaces.

Theorem 2.1
.

Vector planes are the only cylindrical
\alpha
-stationary surfaces.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma
be a cylindrical surface parametrized by (
3
). The mean curvature
H
is
\kappa
, where
\kappa
is the curvature of
\gamma
. If
\gamma
is a straight-line, then
\Sigma
is a plane and thus, the plane must be a vector plane. This prove the result in this case.

Suppose that
\gamma
is not a straight-line and we will arrive to a contradiction. Let
\{\gamma^{\prime},{\bf n},{\bf b}\}
the Frenet frame. Since
\gamma
is a planar curve, then
{\bf b}
is constant so,
{\bf b}=\pm\vec{w}
. After a change of
\vec{w}
by
-\vec{w}
if necessary, we can assume
\vec{w}=-{\bf b}
. In consequence, the unit normal
N
of
\Sigma
is
N=\Psi_{s}\times\Psi_{t}=\gamma^{\prime}\times\vec{w}={\bf n}
. Then Eq. (
1
) becomes

\kappa(s)=\alpha\frac{\langle{\bf n}(s),\Psi(s,t)\rangle}{|\Psi(s,t)|^{2}}=\alpha\frac{\langle{\bf n}(s),\gamma(s)+t\vec{w}\rangle}{|\gamma(s)|^{2}+t^{2}}=\alpha\frac{\langle{\bf n}(s),\gamma(s)\rangle}{|\gamma(s)|^{2}+t^{2}}.

This identity writes a polynomial equation, namely,

\kappa(s)t^{2}+\kappa(s)|\gamma(s)|^{2}-\alpha\langle{\bf n}(s),\gamma(s)\rangle=0.

Then
\kappa(s)=0
identically and
\gamma
would be a straight-line, which it is a contradiction.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

As we said in the Introduction, the cylindrical surface constructed with an
\alpha
-stationary planar curve is not an
\alpha
-stationary surface unless that the surface is a vector plane. This situation differs from other similar surfaces whose mean curvature satisfies a PDE similar to (
1
), and where cylindrical surfaces are not trivial. In order to show these examples, consider
(x,y,z)
canonical coordinates of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and let
N=(N_{1},N_{2},N_{3})

(1)

Translating solitons. These surfaces are critical points of the energy
\int_{\Sigma}e^{z}\,d\Sigma
. The surfaces are characterized by

.

(2)

Self-shrinkers. These surfaces are critical points of the energy
\int_{\Sigma}e^{-|p|^{2}}\,d\Sigma
. The surfaces are characterized by
H=-\langle N,p\rangle
.

(3)

Singular minimal surfaces. These surfaces are critical points of the energy
\int_{\Sigma}z^{\alpha}\,d\Sigma
. The surfaces are characterized by
H=\alpha N_{3}/z
.

In all these cases, the planar curves
\gamma
that are critical points of the one-dimensional case provide critical points in the two-dimensional case by considering the corresponding cylindrical surfaces
\gamma\times\mathbb{R}
:
[
1
,
12
,
13
]
. In the three cases, ruled surfaces that critical points of the energy must be cylindrical surfaces:
[
2
,
3
,
11
,
14
]
.

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.3
: case
\alpha=-4

We prove Thm.
1.3
in the particular case
\alpha=-4
. This case is particular because
(-4)
-stationary surfaces are related with minimal surfaces. Define the inversion
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{3}-\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}-\{0\}

\Phi(p)=\frac{p}{|p|^{2}}.

The following result was proved in
[
15
]

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a surface in Euclidean space. If
\Sigma
is an
\alpha
-stationary surface, then
\Phi(\Sigma)
is an
(\alpha+4)
-stationary surface.

The proof consists in the computation of the mean curvature of the image of a surface by the inversion
\Phi
. According to this theorem, the value
\alpha=-2
is critical in the sense that the mapping
\alpha\mapsto\alpha+4
is the symmetry about the value
-2
. For example, the inverse of a
(-2)
-stationary surface is again a
(-2)
-stationary surface. The case
\alpha=-4
is interesting because now the result says that the inverse of a
(-4)
-stationary surface is a minimal surface (
H=0
) and vice versa.

On the other hand, inversions are mappings that map the class of curves formed by straight-lines and circles into itself. In case that the circle contains
0
, then its image under
\Phi
is a straight-line crossing
0
. Any straight-line containing
0
remains invariant by the inversion but if the straight-line does not contain
0
, its image is a circle containing
0
. As a consequence, the image of a cyclic surface by the inversion
\Phi
is a surface foliated by circles and straight-lines, depending on position of the circles with respect to
0
. Similarly, the image of a ruled surface by the inversion
\Phi
is a surface foliated by circles and straight-lines.

The classification of cyclic
(-4)
-stationary surfaces is now clear. Let
\Sigma
be a cyclic
(-4)
-stationary surface. Then
\Phi(\Sigma)
is a minimal surface foliated by circles and straight-lines. It is known the only minimal surfaces foliated by circles and straight-lines, namely: planes, helicoids, catenoids or the Riemann minimal examples. In consequence, the cyclic
(-4)
-stationary surfaces are the image by the inversion
\Phi
of planes, helicoids, catenoids and the Riemann minimal examples. Notice that the inversion of a plane is a vector plane (if the initial plane contains
0
) or a sphere crossing
0
, otherwise. This proves Thm.
1.3
for the case
\alpha=-4
. See Fig.
2
. Notice that if
\Phi(\Sigma)
is a catenoid whose axis contains
0
, then
\Sigma
is a surface of revolution with the same rotation axis than the catenoid. This particular case corresponds with Thm.
1.2
for
\alpha=-4
.
