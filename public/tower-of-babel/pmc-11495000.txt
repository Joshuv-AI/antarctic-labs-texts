Combined lung and diaphragm ultrasound predicts extubation outcomes in ARDS: a prospective study
Authors: Yanfang Liu, Yinchao Zhou, Panpan Liu, Weinan Ying, Huishan Wu, Zhouzhou Dong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtubation failure is a crucial issue for acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS). Ultrasound of the lung and diaphragm is individually valuable for predicting extubation outcomes. We aimed to determine whether combined lung and diaphragmatic ultrasound could improve the accuracy of predicting the extubation of ARDS patients.MethodsThis was a prospective cohort study of ARDS patients who were ready for extubation. The lung ultrasound score (LUS), diaphragmatic displacement (DD), diaphragm thickening fraction (DTF), and diaphragmatic-rapid shallow breathing index (D-RSBI) were measured at the end of the spontaneous breathing trial. The primary outcome was extubation success. Logistic regression was used to combine these indicators, and the predictive performance of the single and combined indicators was evaluated through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, the Hosmer–Lemeshow Ĉ‐test, and the Brier score. Multivariate logistic regression was used to determine the association between combined ultrasound indicators and extubation success.ResultsThis study enrolled 132 eligible patients from January 2019 to December 2022. A total of 71% (94/132) of patients were successfully extubated from mechanical ventilation. The combination of LUS and D-RSBI had the largest area under the ROC curves, the lowest Brier score, and the greatest calibration. After formula transformation, LUS + 2.43 × D-RSBI ≤ 14.273 was significantly associated with extubation success in ARDS patients.ConclusionsIn ARDS patients receiving mechanical ventilation, the combination of LUS and D-RSBI was more accurate than a single parameter alone in predicting extubation outcomes. This combined approach could help refine extubation protocols in critical care.Clinical trial registration This study is registered online with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR), http://www.chictr.org.cn, ChiCTR1800019340 (Registration time: 2018/11/06).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-024-02103-z.Keywords: Acute respiratory distress syndrome, Extubation, Lung ultrasound, Diaphragm ultrasound, Combination model

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a series of clinical syndromes characterized by bilateral chest radiographical opacities with severe hypoxemia, which is caused by noncardiogenic pulmonary oedema [1]. The incidence of ARDS mortality is estimated to be 35–45% [2]. Mechanical ventilation is a critical lifesaving intervention for ARDS, but approximately 10–20% of patients experience difficult extubation [3, 4]. In addition, failed extubation correlates with an increased risk of mortality [5]. Therefore, accurate prediction of extubation outcomes is highly important for ARDS patients.

The pathophysiology of ARDS is complex and involves lung injury and diaphragm dysfunction [6]. For one thing, ARDS possesses the characteristics of heterogeneity and small lungs [7]. For another thing, ARDS patients are prone to diaphragmatic dysfunction due to mechanical ventilation [8], systemic inflammatory responses [9], the use of sedative drugs [10], and hypoproteinaemia [11]. Lung aeration and diaphragm function are important factors associated with extubation outcomes, and assessing a single organ may ignore potential risk factors for extubation failure. Consequently, it is essential to evaluate both lung aeration and diaphragmatic function appropriately before extubation for ARDS.

Ultrasonography is portable and free from radiation for monitoring lung recruitment and the diaphragm state [12]. Respiratory distress after extubation can be predicted through lung ultrasound because of its valuable performance in distinguishing ARDS morphology, which can be quantified with the lung ultrasound score (LUS) [13]. In addition, diaphragm ultrasound indicators, including diaphragmatic displacement (DD) [14], diaphragm thickening fraction (DTF) [15], and the diaphragmatic-rapid shallow breathing index (D-RSBI) [16], are valuable for assessing diaphragm function [17–19]. However, research on the evaluation of multiple organs by ultrasonic assessment to predict ARDS extubation outcomes is limited.

Thus, the aim of this study was to identify the role of the combination of lung ultrasound and diaphragmatic sonography in predicting the extubation outcome of mechanically ventilated ARDS patients and to evaluate the optimal cut-off thresholds of the combined indicator to provide a reference for future clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

In this study, ARDS patients who were admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) of Ningbo Medical Center LiHuiLi Hospital between January 2019 and December 2021 were enrolled. This study was approved by the ethics committee of Ningbo Medical Center LiHuiLi Hospital (No. DYLL2018050). Written informed consent was obtained from the patients or their next of kin.

We hypothesized that combined lung and diaphragm ultrasound would improve the accuracy of predicting extubation outcomes of ARDS. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) ARDS patients who underwent no less than 24 h of mechanical ventilation (MV); (b) age > 18 years; (c) patients who had successfully undergone a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) in preparation for extubation; (d) signing of the informed consent forms by all patients or their next of kin. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) complication with severe chronic organ dysfunction; (b) patients with previous diaphragmatic paralysis, diaphragmatic hernia; (c) pregnancy.

Data collection

The baseline characteristics of the study subjects, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, PaO2/FiO2 before ventilation, and primary disease of ARDS, were recorded.

The ICU physicians conducted a weaning screen for patients daily, and those who met the screening criteria underwent SBT [20]. Additional details about the methods section are available in the supplementary material. Ultrasound images of the lung and hemidiaphragm were obtained at the end of the SBT. All ultrasonic examinations were performed in the supine position. The ultrasound parameters were measured by an experienced observer using an M7 super (Mindray, Shenzhen, China) ultrasound diagnostic instrument.

Lung ultrasound

Twelve lung regions of the upper and lower parts of the left and right chest wall were examined by anterior, lateral, and posterior axillary using a 3.5–10 MHz probe. LUS was assigned on the basis of the most severe ultrasound images: 0 = normal aeration (A lines); 1 = moderate loss of lung aeration (multiple, well-defined B lines); 2 = severe loss of lung aeration (multiple coalescent B lines); 3 = lung consolidation. LUS ranges from 0 to 36 points in total [21] (Figure S1). The scores of the lung ultrasound images were obtained by two ICU physicians with professional training in ultrasound inspection. When the results were inconsistent, the images were reevaluated until agreement was reached.

Diaphragmatic ultrasound

The diaphragm was evaluated using DD, DTF, and D-RSBI. All diaphragm examinations were repeated in three respiratory cycles, and the average was calculated.

We measured the DD with a 2–4 MHz probe placed below the right costal margin in the midclavicular line. B-mode was used to identify the line referring to the right hemidiaphragm, and M-mode was applied to measure the movement of the diaphragm during a respiratory cycle [22] (Figure S2).

The morphology of the diaphragm was displayed with a 3.5–10 MHz probe placed in the position of the 8th and 10th intercostal spaces near the mid-axillary line. The diaphragmatic thickness at the end of inspiration (DTei) and the diaphragmatic thickness at the end of expiration (DTee) of the right hemidiaphragm were measured in M-mode. The DTF=DTei-DTeeDTee×100% [23] (Figure S3).

The respiratory rate (RR) was recorded when the DD was measured. The D - RSBI=RRDD [16].

Clinical outcomes

The primary outcome was extubation success, which was defined as sustained spontaneous breathing for more than 48 h following extubation without mechanical ventilation (invasive or noninvasive).

The secondary outcomes included length of ICU stay, length of hospital stay, reintubation rate, ICU mortality, and hospital mortality. Outcomes were then compared between groups according to the optimal cut-off value of the best performing combination for predicting extubation outcome.

Statistical analysis

For normally distributed, continuous variables, data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations and were compared with the t test. For non-normally distributed, continuous variables, data are presented as [M (P25, P75)] medians (interquartile ranges) and were compared using the Mann − Whitney U test. For categorical variables, data are presented as numbers (%), and the Chi-square test was used for comparisons.

Lung and diaphragm ultrasound indicators were combined via logistic regression. The predictive performance of the single and combined ultrasound indicators for predicting extubation outcomes was evaluated based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), Hosmer–Lemeshow Ĉ‐test, and Brier score. Youden's index of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to define the optimal cut‐off point and the corresponding sensitivity and specificity for each indicator to predict the extubation outcome. The DeLong test was used to compare the differences in the AUC. Fagan's nomogram was constructed to help clinicians estimate a patient's probability of successful extubation by using the best predictive indicator.

Univariate logistic regression was used to assess the relationship between the combined indicator (as dichotomous variables using the optimal cut‐off values) and extubation success. The other clinical parameters were also evaluated. We then introduced combined indicator and potential confounders (P < 0.1) into the multivariate logistic regression model to assess the relationship between the combined indicator and extubation success. The details of the multivariate analysis are shown in the supplementary material. We performed a prespecified subgroup analysis for extubation success by the aetiology of ARDS.

Statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered significant unless otherwise stated. The data were analysed with R 4.3.3 software and GraphPad Prism 9.3 software.

Results

Predictive performance of lung and diaphragm ultrasound indicators

The lung and diaphragm ultrasound indicators were combined via logistic regression. The ROC curves of single and combined ultrasound indicators for predicting extubation outcomes for ARDS patients are shown in Fig. 1. The combination of LUS and D-RSBI had the largest AUC (0.829) among the single and combined ultrasound indicators, which was significantly greater than the AUC of LUS (Z = 2.233, P = 0.026), DD (Z = 2.510, P = 0.012), DTF (Z = 4.050, P < 0.001), D-RSBI (Z = 2.763, P = 0.006), and the combination of LUS and DTF (Z = 2.118, P = 0.034) based on the DeLong test. All the ultrasound indicators except the DD showed good calibration for predicting extubation outcomes via the H–L test (P > 0.05). The combination of LUS and D-RSBI had the lowest Brier score (0.145). The optimal cut-off, Youden’s index, positive LR, and negative LR of all indicators are also shown in Table 2.

The association between the combined indicator and extubation success

In the univariate logistic regression, the value of LUS + 2.43 × D-RSBI ≤ 14.273 was associated with extubation success. The most significant combined indicators were then introduced into the multivariate logistic regression, which included the SOFA score, the PaO2/FiO2 before ventilation, and the primary disease of ARDS. The results revealed that LUS + 2.43 × D-RSBI ≤ 14.273 remained an independent predictor for successful extubation (odds ratio: 0.08, 95% CI: 0.02–0.30) (Table 3).

Subgroup analysis

A prespecified subgroup analysis by aetiology of ARDS yielded an OR of 0.08 (95% CI: 0.02 − 0.28) for extubation success in ARDS patients caused by pulmonary disease and an OR of 0.16 (95% CI: 0.03 − 0.91) in ARDS patients caused by nerve disease (Figure S5).

Discussion

This study revealed that evaluating lung aeration and diaphragm function at the end of the SBT using an ultrasonic instrument could increase the accuracy of predicting extubation outcomes in mechanically ventilated ARDS patients. The combination of LUS and D-RSBI had the largest AUC and good calibration. Our findings demonstrated that LUS + 2.43 × D-RSBI less than 14.273 was independently associated with extubation success.

Lung ultrasound has been proposed as an alternative to chest radiography in the Kigali modification to the Berlin definition of ARDS [24]. Mongodi et al. suggested that lung ultrasound is a promising tool for daily monitoring of aeration in ARDS patients [25]. The LUS was calculated by examining 12 thoracic areas, allowing quantification of lung aeration. This study indicated that the LUS is a prominent indicator for predicting the extubation outcome of ARDS patients. Similarly, Bouhemad et al. confirmed that the increase of LUS was an independent risk factor for extubation failure [26]. A high LUS corresponded to a low rate of successful extubation. In conclusion, LUS provides significant information about the loss of aeration prior to the extubation of ARDS patients and assists physicians in adjusting the treatment strategy and choosing the best extubation opportunity.

One of the main determinants of extubation failure is diaphragmatic dysfunction, which is most likely to occur in ARDS patients for various reasons [27]. Hence, the assessment of diaphragmatic function before extubation is essential. The DD reflects the movement of the diaphragm during the respiratory cycle, which is related to the tidal volume. Moreover, other studies have shown that the DD obtained by ultrasound has higher feasibility and repeatability [28]. This study confirmed that DD was valuable in predicting the outcome of extubation, but the calibration was not reliable.

The DTF, for which the principle is similar to the “ejection fraction” of the heart, reflects the change of diaphragm thickness and the ability of contractility during breathing. Zambon et al. reported that the cut-off of the DTF to predict extubation outcomes ranged between 30 and 36% during SBT, which is valuable in guiding extubation decisions [29]. However, our study demonstrated that the DTF could not accurately predict the extubation outcome of ARDS patients, which is inconsistent with previous studies. These results are likely to be related to not only this study just included patients with ARDS, but also the measurements of DTF are not always easy to perform, as mentioned in other studies[29]. First, the mean thickness of the diaphragm is approximately 1.5–2 mm. Therefore, a high-frequency probe (usually a 10 MHz “vascular” probe) is needed. The smallest measurable distance of most machines is 0.1 mm, which demonstrated corresponds to approximately 5–7% of the measurement. Therefore, small operator-dependent variations could influence the measurement. Second, technical difficulties with some patients (i.e. obese patients, postoperative patients) should be expected. In addition, patients with ARDS are more likely to present with a decrease in diaphragmatic contractility for various reasons [8–11]. Although the repeatability of bedside ultrasound for measuring DTF has been confirmed, there is still a lack of learning curves for beginners in DTF. In general, even if the DTF is valuable for extubation, more studies are needed to confirm the feasibility of DTF.

Spadaro et al. proposed D-RSBI, which is based on the rapid shallow breathing index (RSBI), to avoid situations that ignore underlying diaphragmatic dysfunction because fatigable accessory muscles contribute to ventilation during SBT [30]. This result proved that the D-RSBI was more accurate in predicting extubation outcomes. The D-RSBI reflects the relationship between respiratory load and the ability to address it during SBT [30]. Therefore, D-RSBI allows clinicians to develop a better extubation strategy.

Predicting the overall extubation outcome with only a single indicator is inappropriate since the reasons for extubation outcomes are complicated. Previous studies have demonstrated that a combination model including ultrasonic indicators of multiple organs could increase the accuracy of predicting extubation outcomes, but these studies focused on all patients receiving mechanical ventilation [18]. ARDS patients usually present with lung injury and diaphragm dysfunction. However, the lung and diaphragm work together to provide adequate respiratory power after extubation. Lung aeration is the basis for extubation, and good diaphragm function is beneficial to reduce pulmonary burden. In conclusion, the complementary role of the lung and diaphragm is essential for successful extubation. Therefore, our study combined lung and diaphragm ultrasound, which showed the combination of LUS and D-RSBI was an independent influencing indicator of extubation success and was more accurate in predicting extubation outcomes than individual indicators. The combination of lung and diaphragm ultrasound revealed the reasons associated with the extubation outcome from different perspectives, strengthened the complex connections between organs, and greatly improved the accuracy, sensitivity and specificity of prediction.

In the clinical setting, physicians could measure LUS and D-RSBI at the end of SBT and use the predictive model from our study to evaluate the rate of extubation success in ARDS patients. Our study provides a basis for clinical extubation decisions; however, mastery of basic lung and diaphragm ultrasound skills may be required. In addition, the predictive model should be validated in different patient populations in future research.

This study has several limitations. First, the different causes and oxygenation levels of ARDS in our study may limit the application of our findings. Therefore, a larger, multi-centre study is needed to further validate our results. Second, our study excluded patients with severe chronic organ dysfunction, previous diaphragmatic paralysis and diaphragmatic hernia, pregnant patients and children, and it included patients with a successful initial SBT, thus limiting the generalizability of our results to different ICU settings and patient populations. Furthermore, this was a single-centre study, which resulted in a certain selection bias due to the relatively poor representation of the included patients. In addition, the acquisition and interpretation of ultrasound images was dependent on the skills and experience of the operator, which might lead to subjectivity and differences in diagnoses.

Conclusion

Combining LUS and D-RSBI could increase the predictive accuracy of extubation outcomes, and LUS + 2.43 × D-RSBI less than 14.273 was independently associated with extubation success in ARDS patients. Our study could guide physicians in making extubation decisions and adjusting the treatment strategy in a timely manner to reduce the failure rate of extubation in ARDS patients.
