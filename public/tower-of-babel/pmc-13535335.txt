Rapidly Progressive Bilateral Infective Endocarditis Involving Prosthetic Material After Repaired Tetralogy of Fallot: A Case Report
Authors: Hiroki Ikeuchi, Masao Hirano, Masashi Kabasawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInfective endocarditis after repaired tetralogy of Fallot can rapidly progress when prosthetic material is involved. We report bilateral multivalvular endocarditis undetected by transthoracic echocardiography but identified by transesophageal echocardiography. Early diagnosis and timely surgical intervention are essential to prevent extensive intracardiac spread.Keywords: adult congenital heart disease, infective endocarditis, tetralogy of Fallot, transesophageal echocardiography

Introduction

Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) is one of the most common cyanotic congenital heart diseases. Surgical outcomes have improved markedly over recent decades, resulting in a growing population of adults with repaired TOF. However, long‐term complications remain a significant concern. Infective endocarditis (IE) represents a serious and potentially life‐threatening complication in this population, particularly in the presence of prosthetic materials. Early diagnosis using transesophageal echocardiography and appropriately timed surgical intervention is critical.

In this report, bilateral infective endocarditis refers to the involvement of both left‐ and right‐sided cardiac structures. We present a rare case of rapidly progressive bilateral IE after repaired TOF, in which infection extended from the left‐sided valves to the ventricular septal patch and right‐sided cardiac structures despite antibiotic therapy.

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria.

Case History

A 50‐year‐old woman who had undergone non‐transannular patch repair with pulmonary commissurotomy for TOF 45 years earlier was referred to our institution. One month before referral, she had been admitted to another hospital with fever and hemiplegia. Brain magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated an ischemic stroke, and blood cultures were positive for Staphylococcus aureus . Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed no evidence of IE, whereas transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) revealed vegetations on the aortic and mitral valves. Intravenous antibiotic therapy was initiated based on these findings. Despite 4 weeks of appropriate antimicrobial treatment, the patient remained bacteremic and febrile. Repeat TEE demonstrated newly developed right‐sided involvement, prompting referral to our institution for further evaluation and surgical treatment.

On admission, her blood pressure was 112/44 mmHg, heart rate 103 beats/min, and body temperature 38.0°C. She had a body mass index of 31.4 and residual hemiplegia due to cerebral embolism. Laboratory examination revealed a white blood cell count of 6020/μL and an elevated C‐reactive protein level of 1.77 mg/dL. Electrocardiography demonstrated sinus tachycardia with complete right bundle branch block.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

TTE identified a large mass at the basal interventricular septum (Figure 1A), moderate aortic regurgitation, and moderate mitral regurgitation. TEE demonstrated newly developed mobile vegetation on the tricuspid valve and a small vegetation on the mitral valve (Figure 1B). The tricuspid vegetation penetrated the septal leaflet and was continuous with the interventricular mass. The previously observed vegetation on the aortic valve was no longer detectable. In addition, a fistulous communication between the right coronary sinus of Valsalva and the right ventricle was identified and was initially suspected to represent a ruptured sinus of Valsalva (Figure 1C).

Given the persistence of bacteremia despite prolonged antibiotic therapy, previous cerebral embolism, newly developed right‐sided vegetation, and suspected extension of infection to the ventricular septal patch, emergency surgery was undertaken.

Following a right atriotomy, a 3‐cm vegetation was identified on the anterior and septal leaflets of the tricuspid valve (Figure 2A). After reopening the previous right ventriculotomy, extensive vegetation and abscess formation were found on and around the previous Teflon patch, extending to the tricuspid valve (Figure 2B). The patch was partially dehisced. Although the sinus of Valsalva was intact, a 1‐cm perforation was present in the noncoronary cusp of the aortic valve, through which the malaligned interventricular septum was visible (Figure 2C). Multiple small vegetations were also observed on both mitral valve leaflets.

Intraoperative findings. (A) Large, fragile vegetation was identified on the anterior and septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve from right atriotomy (black arrow). (B) Large vegetation and abscess on and around the previous Teflon patch extending to the tricuspid valve were found from right ventriculotomy (black arrow). (C) The malaligned interventricular septum was revealed through the perforation of the noncoronary leaflet of the aortic valve (white arrow).

All infected tissues were radically excised. The ventricular septal defect was re‐closed using a bovine pericardial patch. The mitral and aortic valves were replaced with mechanical prostheses (31‐mm and 23‐mm St. Jude Medical valves, St. Jude Medical, St. Paul, MN, USA, respectively), and the tricuspid valve was replaced with a bioprosthetic valve (29‐mm Epic; St. Jude Medical, St. Paul, MN, USA). A permanent pacemaker (Advisa MRI; Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was implanted because of complete atrioventricular block. Surgical cultures were positive for methicillin‐susceptible S. aureus .

The patient was extubated on postoperative Day 1 and discharged from the intensive care unit on postoperative Day 5. Postoperative TEE demonstrated no recurrent vegetations, valvular dysfunction, or residual ventricular septal shunt. Although she subsequently underwent rehabilitation for neurological deficits, she later died of aspiration pneumonia unrelated to recurrent IE.

Conclusion

Infective endocarditis after repaired tetralogy of Fallot can rapidly progress to extensive bilateral involvement when prosthetic material is present. Early diagnosis with transesophageal echocardiography and timely surgical intervention are essential to prevent catastrophic progression.

Discussion

Surgical outcomes for TOF have improved substantially over recent decades, resulting in a rapidly growing population of adults with repaired congenital heart disease [1]. Consequently, long‐term complications, including infective endocarditis (IE), are encountered more frequently in clinical practice. Patients with repaired TOF remain susceptible to IE because of residual structural abnormalities, prosthetic materials, and residual valvular dysfunction. Although the reported incidence of IE after repaired TOF is relatively low (3.5%), delayed diagnosis may result in devastating complications [2].

Accurate imaging plays a pivotal role in the diagnosis of IE in patients with congenital heart disease. Prosthetic materials, altered anatomy, and acoustic shadowing frequently limit the diagnostic accuracy of transthoracic echocardiography. In contrast, transesophageal echocardiography provides superior spatial resolution and allows better assessment of prosthetic material, perivalvular extension, and right‐sided cardiac structures. Current ESC guidelines also recommend TEE whenever clinical suspicion persists despite inconclusive TTE findings or when prosthetic material is present [3]. In the present case, TEE identified progressive multivalvular involvement that had not been recognized by TTE and directly influenced the treatment strategy.

Once infection reaches prosthetic material, eradication with antimicrobial therapy alone becomes increasingly difficult because bacterial biofilm formation promotes persistent infection and local tissue destruction [4]. In the present patient, infection initially appeared confined to the left‐sided valves but subsequently extended to the ventricular septal patch and right‐sided cardiac structures during prolonged antibiotic therapy. Such bilateral extension is exceedingly uncommon after repaired TOF and illustrates the aggressive nature of prosthetic‐associated IE.

Current international guidelines recommend early surgery for IE in patients with heart failure, uncontrolled infection, recurrent embolic events, or extensive perivalvular extension [5]. Our patient fulfilled several of these indications, including persistent infection despite prolonged antimicrobial therapy, previous cerebral embolism, and progressive extension to prosthetic material. Continued medical therapy alone was therefore considered unlikely to eradicate the infection, and emergency surgery was undertaken before further intracardiac destruction occurred. This case highlights that patients with repaired congenital heart disease may require earlier surgical intervention than is generally considered when prosthetic material becomes infected.

To our knowledge, bilateral IE involving multivalvular infection and a ventricular septal patch after repaired TOF has rarely been reported. This case provides two important clinical lessons. First, TEE should be performed promptly whenever IE is suspected in patients with repaired congenital heart disease, even if TTE findings are inconclusive. Second, once prosthetic material becomes involved, prolonged medical therapy alone may permit rapid intracardiac progression, and timely surgical intervention should be considered before extensive tissue destruction develops.
