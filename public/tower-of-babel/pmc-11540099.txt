TM4SF4 is a diagnostic biomarker accelerating progression of papillary thyroid cancer via AKT pathway
Authors: Lizhi Lin, Jialiang Wen, Tiansheng Xu, Yuhao Si
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe incidence of papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) has been steadily rising, though the underlying mechanism remains unclear. This study aims to elucidate the biological role of TM4SF4 in the PTC progression. Our differential expression analysis indicated that TM4SF4 was significantly upregulated in PTC, which was corroborated in both our local cohort and the data from Human Protein Atlas. Additionally, clinical characteristics analysis and receiver operating characteristic curves (ROC) demonstrated that TM4SF4 served as a significant diagnostic marker for PTC. Correlation and enrichment analysis of TM4SF4-related partners suggested that it was involved in cell junction and cohesion processes. Furthermore, immune infiltration analysis revealed a positive correlation between TM4SF4 expression and the immune activation in PTC. Importantly, in vitro experiments demonstrated that TM4SF4 downregulation suppressed the proliferation and metastasis of PTC cell lines while inducing apoptosis. We further discovered that the AKT activator SC79 was able to reverse the malignant behaviors suppression caused by TM4SF4 knockdown, suggesting that TM4SF4 may promote PTC progression via the AKT pathway. In conclusion, our study highlights the oncogenic role of TM4SF4 in PTC and identifies it as a novel biomarker for diagnosis and treatment.

Introduction

Papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) is the most common endocrine malignancy, constituting approximately 80% of all thyroid cancers.1 Over the last 30 years, PTC incidence has steadily increased; meanwhile, the advancements in diagnostic technologies such as ultrasonography and computed tomography have led to a massive detection of subclinical lesions.2,3 Although the 10-year survival rate post-surgery exceeds 90%, a substantial portion of PTC patients (35%) experience recurrence due to early lymph node metastasis.4,5 Therefore, it is urgent to unravel the molecular mechanism of PTC carcinogenesis and discover novel therapeutic targets.

Transmembrane 4 L Six family member 4(TM4SF4), a member of the transmembrane 4 superfamily, was initially identified in the human intestinal epithelium and liver as a gene associated with cell proliferation.6 Overexpression of TM4SF4 has been observed in hepatocellular carcinoma, leading to increased cell proliferation.7 Targeting TM4SF4 has shown promise as a potential treatment for inhibiting liver cancer.8,9 Furthermore, TM4SF4 has been implicated in the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) process and has been shown to promote the progression of colorectal cancer.10,11 Additional research has highlighted the potential of TM4SF4 in lung and breast cancer treatment.12 However, the functional role of TM4SF4 in PTC progression remains unexplored.

In our research, we identified a significant upregulation of TM4SF4 in PTC and analyzed its diagnostic potential. We performed correlation and enrichment analysis of TM4SF4-related partners and explored the relationship between TM4SF4 expression and immune cell infiltration. Subsequent validation of TM4SF4 function in PTC cell lines using siRNA revealed the involvement of the AKT pathway in TM4SF4-mediated progression of PTC. In summary, our study demonstrated TM4SF4 as an oncogene in PTC, potentially acting through the AKT pathway.

Methods and materials

Data acquisition and analysis

The RNAseq data from the TCGA and GTEx were obtained from the UCSC XENA database (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/) in FPKM format. The “ggplot2” package was utilized to visually represent TM4SF4 expression in various tumor tissues and their corresponding normal tissues. Additionally, mRNA expression data and clinical information for 501 PTC tissues and 58 para-cancerous tissues were retrieved from TCGA (https://tcgadata.nci.nih.gov/tcga/). The expression data for TM4SF4 in PTC was sourced from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Furthermore, Immunohistochemistry images depicting TM4SF4 protein expression in PTC tissues and normal thyroid tissues were obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) (http://www.proteinatlas.org). Gene correlation analysis was conducted using the online database GEPIA (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/).

Correlation and functional enrichment analysis

To identify the co-expression genes of TM4SF4, a Pearson correlation analysis was conducted on TM4SF4 mRNA and other mRNAs in thyroid cancer utilizing data from the TCGA database. Subsequently, the top 300 genes exhibiting the strongest positive correlation with TM4SF4 expression were selected for further enrichment analysis. We focused on gene ontology (GO) and KEGG pathways of TM4SF4 through Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). The Single Gene Set Enrichment Analysis was performed between low and high TM4SF4 expression groups using GSEA_V3.0 software.

Immune infiltration analysis

The TCGA PTC patients were stratified into two cohorts according to median TM4SF4 expression levels, and immune cell infiltration levels were compared utilizing the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Furthermore, the ssGSEA algorithm was employed to assess the presence of 24 distinct types of immune cells in PTC tissue specimens.

Clinical sample collection and cell culture

A total of 47 pairs of PTC tissues and adjacent normal tissues were obtained from patients undergoing thyroidectomy at The Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. Inclusion criteria stipulated that patients must have received a pathological diagnosis of PTC from two senior pathologists. The HTORI-3 and FTC-133 cell lines were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Shanghai Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The BCPAP, TPC-1 and KTC-1 were provided by Professor Mingzhao Xing of the Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine (Baltimore, USA). The FTC-133 cell line was cultured in DMEM (Gibco, Carlsbad, USA), while other cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). All cells were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from clinical specimens and cells using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Then, 1 μg of RNA was taken to synthesize cDNA with the reverse transcription Kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). The qRT‐PCR analysis was conducted on the ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA) employing the SYBR Premix Ex Taq II kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). The relative mRNA levels were determined using the 2 −∆∆CT method, with GAPDH serving as the internal control. The primer sequences: GADPH forward primer: 5′‐GGTCGGAGTCAACGGATTTG‐3′, GADPH reverse primer: 5′‐ATGAGCCCCAGCCTTCTCCAT‐3′.

TM4SF4 forward primer: 5′-GACAACGACCACCTTTCCCA-3′, TM4SF4 reverse primer: 5′‐GGAAGGGGTAGCCCCATGTA‐3′.

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), drugs, and antibodies

All siRNAs were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China), and the two siRNAs sequences: si-TM4SF4-1 sense: 5′-GCUGGAUACUCGUUUAUCATT-3′, si-TM4SF4-1 antisense: 5′-UGAUAAACGAGUAUCCAGCTT-3′; si-TM4SF4-2 sense: 5′-GGAAGCGAUUUGCGAUGUUTT-3′, si-TM4SF4-2 antisense: 5′-AACAUCGCAAAUCGCUUCCTT-3′. The drug concentration was 10 μM for AKT activator SC79 (MCE, Monmouth Junction, USA). For western blotting, the primary antibodies we used were β-actin (1:5000, Zenbio, Chengdu, China), BCL-2, AKT, and p-AKT (1:1000, Zenbio, Chengdu, China).

Cell proliferation assays

In the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) assay, transfected BCPAP and TPC-1 cells were seeded at a density of 1.5 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates. Following this, 10 μl of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for 3 h before measuring absorbance at 450 nm. For the colony formation assay, transfected cells were seeded in six-well plates until each cluster contained over 50 cells. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for an additional 30 min.

Cell migration, invasion, and apoptosis assays

4 × 104 cells were seeded into the upper chamber with a serum-free medium. The lower chamber was filled with a medium containing 10% FBS. After a 22-h incubation period, we fixed migrated cells using 4% paraformaldehyde and stained cells with 0.1% crystal violet. We captured the result of each well under 20× magnification of the Nikon microscope. Migration and invasion assays were conducted using Transwell chambers and Matrigel invasion chambers (Corning, NY, USA), respectively. The wound healing assay was conducted to evaluate cell migration. Scratched by 200 μl pipette tips, cells were cultured with serum-free conditions in 12-well plates for at least 24 h. Three fixed positions were captured at 5× magnification, and the results were processed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, USA). The migration rate was calculated as (original wound area – post-migration wound area)/original wound area × 100%. Cell apoptosis rate was evaluated with Annexin V- FITC and 7-AAD reagents (Becton, Dickinson, and Company, USA) on a flow cytometer (BD Accuri C6 Plus, USA).

Protein extraction and western blotting

Western blot and IP lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) with proteinase and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche) were used to extract protein from cells. Protein quantification was carried out using the BCA reagent (Pierce, Thermo, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After degeneration, 20 μg of protein per well was electrophoresed and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore, Boston, USA). Five percent skim milk or BSA was used to block membranes for an hour, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies at 4°C. Subsequently, fluorescent secondary antibodies (LI-COR Biosciences, USA) were added. Incubated for an hour, the membranes were finally scanned on the LI-COR Odyssey CLx (LI-COR Biosciences, USA). We analyzed the data using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad, San Diego, USA) and SPSS 25.0 software (IBM SPSS Inc, Chicago, USA). Correlations between gene expression and clinicopathological parameters were evaluated using the χ2 test. Two-group data were analyzed using the Student’s t-test, Mann–Whitney test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while multiple groups were analyzed by two-way analysis of variance. The ROC curve was utilized to determine the diagnostic efficacy of TM4SF4. Correlations between gene expressions were assessed using Spearman rank correlation coefficients. A significance level of p < .05 was employed.

Results

TM4SF4 is significantly upregulated in PTC

We first assessed the differential expression of TM4SF4 across various cancer types, including ACC, BLCA, BRCA, CESC, CHOL, COAD, DLBC, ESCA, GBM, HNSC, KICH, KIRC, KIRP, LAML, LGG, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, MESO, OV, PAAD, PCPG, PRAD, READ, SARC, SKCM, STAD, TGCT, THCA, THYM, UCEC, UCS, and UVM (Figure 1a). In the TCGA cohort, we found that TM4SF4 was significantly upregulated in PTC tissues compared to normal thyroid tissues (Figure 1b–c). Consistently, we verified that TM4SF4 was overexpressed in PTC compared to the normal thyroid in the GSE33630, GSE60542, and GSE129562 cohorts (Figure S1A-C). This observation was further supported by the analysis of 79 pairs of PTC tissue sequencing data (Figure 1d), as well as 47 pairs of surgical tissue samples from our local cohort (Figure 1e). Additionally, immunohistochemistry (IHC) results from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database indicated stronger TM4SF4 staining in PTC compared to normal thyroid tissue (Figure 2a). TM4SF4 expression is significantly associated with clinicopathological features of PTC

We analyzed the association between the TM4SF4 expression and the clinical characteristics of PTC patients in TCGA and our local cohort, respectively. In the TCGA cohort, TM4SF4 high expression was significantly correlated with the classical type (p < .001) and the high risk of lymph node metastasis (LNM, p < .001) (Table 1). Consistently, we found a positive association between TM4SF4 expression and LNM(p = .029) in our local cohort (Table 2). Furthermore, we performed a univariate logistic regression analysis to investigate the relationship between TM4SF4 expression and LNM. Our results demonstrated that high TM4SF4 expression (odds ratio [OR] = 2.186, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.500–3.185, p < .001), T stage III/IV (OR = 2.577, 95% CI: 1.744–3.807, p < .001), disease stage III/IV (OR = 3.590, 95% CI: 2.377–5.423, p < .001), and non-classical type (OR = 0.356, 95% CI: 0.226–0.561, p < .001) were significant factors for LNM. Subsequently, the results of multivariate logistic regression analysis indicated that TM4SF4 expression (OR = 1.786, 95% CI: 1.181–2.700, p = .006), disease stage III/IV (OR = 3.123, 95% CI: 1.911–5.102, p < .0001) and non-classical type (OR = 0.321, 95% CI: 0.192–0.537, p < .0001) were independent prediction factors of LNM (Table 3). Diagnostic value of the TM4SF4 expression in PTC

We compared TM4SF4 expression across different disease stages and LNM in PTC patients. As shown in Figure 2b–c, higher TM4SF4 expression was observed in advanced disease stages and cases with LNM, indicating a potential association between TM4SF4 expression and PTC progression. In addition, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were utilized to evaluate the diagnostic utility of TM4SF4 expression in TCGA, GSE33630, GSE60542 and validation cohorts. The results showed that TM4SF4 expression effectively distinguished between tumor and normal tissues in the TCGA dataset (AUC = 0.7952, p < .0001), GSE33630 (AUC = 0.7991, p < .0001), GSE60542 (AUC = 0.8929, p < .0001) and validated cohort (AUC = 0.8407, p < .0001) (Figure 2d). Moreover, as illustrated in Figure 2e, TM4SF4 could also determine the presence or absence of LNM in the TCGA dataset (AUC = 0.6589, p < .0001) and validated cohort (AUC = 0.7692, p = .0017). The above results indicated that TM4SF4 was a strong diagnostic marker for PTC.

Correlation and enrichment analysis of TM4SF4-related partners in PTC

To investigate the mechanism of TM4SF4, we conducted correlation analysis using the TCGA dataset and selected the top 300 related genes for further enrichment analysis. The top 35 genes were exhibited in the heatmap (Figure 3a). TM4SF4 expression was positively associated with level of C1orf116 (Chromosome 1 Open Reading Frame 116) (R = 0.684), TM4SF1 (Transmembrane 4 L Six Family Member 1) (R = 0.741), SDC4 (Syndecan 4) (R = 0.667), CST6 (Cystatin E/M) (R = 0.667), and QSOX1 (Quiescin Sulfhydryl Oxidase 1) (R = 0.667) (Figure 3b–c). The GO functional enrichment analysis results indicated a significant correlation between TM4SF4 expression and cell migration-related pathways (Figure 4a–c). Furthermore, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Reactome pathway databases. The KEGG analysis demonstrated the enrichment of cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction and cell adhesion molecules cams (Figure 4d). The Reactome analysis revealed that cell–cell communication and cell junction organization were enriched (Figure 4e). These results indicated that TM4SF4 might be involved in cell junction and cohesion, influencing cell migration and potentially promoting metastasis in PTC. Correlation between TM4SF4 expression and immune cell infiltration in PTC

As shown in Figure 5a, we compared the enrichment of immune cells between TM4SF4 high expression and low expression group. The results revealed that enrichment of Cytotoxic cells, Eosinophils, DC cells, T cells, Neutrophils cells, Mast cells, Macrophage cells, NK cells, Th1 cells, Th2 cells, and Treg cells were higher in the TM4SF4 high expression group than in the low expression group. Moreover, we analyzed the correlation between TM4SF4 expression and 24 types of immune cells using the ssGSEA algorithm. We found that majority of immune cells were positively correlated with TM4SF4 expression, including DC cells (R = 0.481), Macrophage cells (R = 0.468), Th1 cells (R = 0.445), Neutrophils cells (R = 0.44), Treg cells (R = 0.321), Th2 cells (R = 0.232), NK cells (R = 0.225), T cells (R = 0.196), and B cells (R = 0.133) (Figure 5b). Overall, these results demonstrated that TM4SF4 expression had a strong relationship with the immune-activated status of PTC. Silencing TM4SF4 inhibits PTC cell lines’ proliferation

To determine the biological role of TM4SF4 in PTC progression, we assessed TM4SF4 expression level in thyroid cell lines. The results revealed that TM4SF4 was significantly upregulated in TC cells, especially in BCPAP and TPC-1 cell lines (Figure 6a). Subsequently, si-RNA was utilized to effectively silence TM4SF4 in BCPAP and TPC-1 cells (Figure 6b). The proliferation capacity of the PTC cells was notably reduced following TM4SF4 knockdown, as evidenced by CCK-8 (Figure 6c–d) and colony formation assays (Figure 6e), suggesting a promotive role of TM4SF4 in PTC cell proliferation. Downregulated TM4SF4 reduces the metastasis ability of PTC and induces cell apoptosis

The previous enrichment analysis findings indicated that TM4SF4 was associated with cell migration. Therefore, we performed wound healing, Transwell migration, and Matrigel invasion assays to assess the cellular functions of the target gene. The results demonstrated that si-TM4SF4 hindered both cell migration and invasion (Figure 7a–b, Figure S3A-B). Since apoptosis is a common mechanism of cell death, we used flow cytometry to evaluate the apoptosis rate. The results indicated a significant increase in the apoptosis rate in the si-TM4SF4 group compared to the si-NC group (Figure 7c). The Western blot detected that protein BCL-2 was significantly decreased when TM4SF4 was downregulated (Figure 7d). These results indicated that TM4SF4 might be involved in PTC progression by promoting metastasis and inhibiting cell apoptosis. TM4SF4 regulates PTC progression via AKT pathway

The result of GSEA revealed that high TM4SF4 expression was positively correlated with the PI3K/AKT pathway (NES:1.76, NOM p-val:0.008, FDR:0.022) (Figure 8a). Consequently, we assessed the protein level of p-AKT and AKT, which were key markers of the PI3K/AKT pathway. Western blot analysis revealed that TM4SF4 downregulation did not affect total AKT expression but significantly reduced the levels of activated p-AKT (Figure 8b). However, TM4SF4 knockdown did not affect p-mTOR or N-cadherin protein levels (Figure S2A). Additionally, the mRNA levels of FOXO3, GSK3B, mTOR, CDKN1A, and MDM2 did not significantly decrease after silencing TM4SF4 in BCPAP and TPC-1 cells (Figure S2C-D). Discussions

The annual increase in the incidence of PTC has spurred further investigation into the underlying mechanisms of progression, particularly in cases where patients experience recurrence and metastasis despite a generally favorable prognosis.13 To date, the mechanism of PTC progression has never been entirely determined.

Previous findings have shown that TM4SF4 plays an important regulatory role in cancer progression. Overexpression of TM4SF4 activated cell growth, migration, and invasion in lung cancer.14 TM4SF4-triggered osteopontin secretion maintained EMT-associated CSC-like properties and metastatic capacity of lung cancer cells.15 In addition, TM4SF4 was reported to be involved in the EMT process,10 and high expression of TM4SF4 was associated with poor prognosis in CRC.16 Wang et al. explored the expression and potential mechanisms of TM4SFs in PTC, identifying an association between TM4SF4 and overall survival in PTC patients.17 However, the specific biological function of TM4SF4 in PTC remains uncovered.

In this study, we confirmed the diagnostic value of TM4SF4 and illustrated the carcinogenesis role and underlying mechanism of TM4SF4 in PTC for the first time. Analyzing our sequencing data and several public databases implied that TM4SF4 might be an oncogene in PTC. Further clinicopathological analysis demonstrated that the high level of TM4SF4 was strongly related to advanced disease stages and lymph node metastasis. Our findings also revealed that TM4SF4 was a novel diagnosis marker distinguishing tumors and determining lymph node metastasis status. Recently, the importance of immune function within the tumor microenvironment has been increasingly acknowledged.18 Numerous immune cells, including natural killer (NK) cells, macrophages, dendritic cells (DC), T cells, and B cells, have been reported to coexist with tumor cells and engage in cancer development.19–23 Our findings suggested a potential role for TM4SF4 in immune infiltration in PTC, particularly with dendritic cells and macrophages, which will probably be a new perspective for further investigation in the future.

The PI3K/AKT pathway is known for its oncogenic properties in promoting proliferation, migration, and drug resistance in various cancers.24–26 Increasing research has demonstrated that targeting the PI3K/AKT pathway paves the way for PTC treatment. For instance, Yu et al. found that oncogenic effects induced by cytokine receptor-like factor 1 (CRLF1) in PTC were suppressed by treating the cells with the AKT inhibitor MK-2206.27 Furthermore, Shu’s group reported that CircNDST1 promoted PTC progression via bonding with CSNK2A1 to activate the PI3K-Akt pathway.28 Recently, Sun et al. discovered that SHMT2 promoted PTC metastasis through epigenetic activation of AKT signaling, and blocking AKT activation could eliminate this effect.29 Our findings revealed that the downregulation of TM4SF4 led to decreased p-AKT levels in PTC cells, indicating that TM4SF4 activated the AKT pathway. Moreover, we observed that silencing TM4SF4 suppressed cell proliferation and migration and induced cell apoptosis. Mechanistically, the activation of the AKT rescued the suppressed malignant behaviors resulting from TM4SF4 knockdown, suggesting that TM4SF4 might accelerate PTC progression via the AKT pathway. Our study offers new insights into the involvement of the TM4SF4–AKT axis in developing PTC.

However, our study still has several limitations. First, the role of TM4SF4 in PTC progression requires further investigation. Second, the involvement of the TM4SF4-AKT axis needs to be validated in vivo experiments. Additionally, establishing a larger local cohort is essential to explore the clinical relevance of TM4SF4 expression.

In summary, we conclude that the TM4SF4 is upregulated in PTC and serves as a valuable diagnostic indicator. The TM4SF4 expression correlates with the immune-activated status of PTC, and its overexpression activates the AKT pathway, thereby facilitating PTC progression. Therefore, TM4SF4 is a potential therapeutic target for PTC patients.
