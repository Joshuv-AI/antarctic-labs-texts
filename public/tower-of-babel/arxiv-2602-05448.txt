BlitzRank : Principled Zero-shot Ranking Agents with Tournament Graphs
Sheshansh Agrawal, Thien Hang Nguyen, Douwe Kiela

Abstract
Selecting the top m m from n n items via expensive k k -wise comparisons is fundamental to settings ranging from LLM-based document reranking to crowdsourced evaluation and tournament design. Existing methods either rely on heuristics that fail to fully exploit the information each comparison reveals, or are inefficient when they do. We introduce a tournament graph framework that provides a principled foundation for k k -wise ranking. Our key observation is that each k k -item comparison reveals a complete tournament of ( k 2 ) \binom{k}{2} pairwise preferences; aggregating these into a global preference graph and computing its transitive closure yields many additional orderings without further oracle calls. We formalize when an item’s rank is certifiably determined and design a greedy query schedule that maximizes information gain towards identifying the top- m m items. The framework also gracefully handles non-transitive preferences – cycles induced by real-world oracles – by collapsing them into equivalence classes that yield principled tiered rankings . Applied to LLM reranking across 14 benchmarks and 5 models, our method achieves Pareto dominance over existing approaches: matching or exceeding accuracy while requiring 25–40% fewer tokens than comparable methods, and 7 × \times fewer than pairwise reranking at near-identical quality.

Introduction

Consider the classic
25 horses’ race

Tournament Graph Framework

2.1
Problem Setup

Let
V
be a set of
n

G^{*}=(V,E^{*})
be an unknown tournament representing the oracle’s latent preferences: for every pair
u\neq v
, exactly one of
(u,v)
or
(v,u)

u\succ v
or
v\succ u

We write
\deg^{-}_{G^{*}}(v):=|\{u:(u,v)\in E^{*}\}|
for the number of items that beat

– its

.
The top-
m

m

G^{*}
.

We access
G^{*}
through a
k

\mathcal{O}
.
Given any subset
S\subseteq V
with
|S|\leq k
, the oracle returns the restriction of
G^{*}
to
S
: all
\binom{|S|}{2}
directed edges among items in
S
.
The goal is to identify the top-
m
items using as few oracle calls as possible.

If there is a directed path

in

When the oracle produces cycles (
a\succ b\succ c\succ a
), no total ordering is consistent with the observations.
Rather than treating this as noise, we treat it as structure: cycles indicate that the oracle does not admit a consistent global order among those items.

We handle this via
strongly connected components
(SCCs).
Two nodes are in the same SCC if each can reach the other in
G
; this partitions
V
into equivalence classes.
The
condensation

\left[G\right]
collapses each SCC to a supernode, with edges inherited from cross-component edges in
G
.
A fundamental fact:
\left[G\right]
is always a DAG (Lemma
23
), and the condensation of any tournament is a
transitive
tournament (Proposition
24
).
This means SCCs can be totally ordered by their loss counts in the condensation.
Crucially, discovered SCCs are always subsets of true SCCs (Lemma
25
): we never incorrectly merge vertices into the same tier.
This refinement relationship induces a natural projection from discovered SCCs to true SCCs, allowing all machinery from the transitive case to lift to the general case.

For ranking, nodes within an SCC form a “tie tier” – they share the same loss count in
\left[G\right]
.
When all SCCs are singletons (the transitive case), we recover a total ordering.
Otherwise, we obtain a principled
tiered ranking
: a total order on tiers, with ties within each tier.
5
5

5

When a total ordering is required, ties within a tier may be broken arbitrarily. In retrieval applications, a natural choice is to preserve the original retrieval ranking (e.g., BM25 scores), treating the oracle as refining rather than replacing the initial ordering.

What we do (and do not) claim within tiers.

Without additional assumptions on the oracle, there may be no well-defined total order among vertices within an SCC.
Accordingly, our output in the non-transitive case is a tiered ranking: a total order over SCC tiers, with ties inside each tier.
For top-
m
selection, if the boundary tier contains more than the remaining quota, any subset of the required size from that tier is a valid top-
m
output.

The finalization criterion extends naturally.
For a node
v
, let
B(v):=\{u\in V:u\rightsquigarrow v\text{ and }v\rightsquigarrow u\}\cup\{v\}
denote the SCC containing
v

Nodes in
B(v)\setminus\{v\}
are mutually reachable with
v
, appearing in both the in-reach and out-reach sets.
The union formula for
K(v)
accounts for this overlap,

K(v)=L(v)+W(v)-(|B(v)|-1),

and finalization remains
K(v)=n-1
.

BlitzRank
(Algorithm
1
) realizes the tournament graph framework.
Each iteration: (1) computes in-reach

Why greedy scheduling works.

The algorithm queries SCCs ordered by ascending in-reach.
The key insight is the
forced-tie property
(Lemma
39
):

If two SCCs have equal in-reach in
\left[G\right]
, there is no edge between them in the condensation. Consequently, this means that there is no edge between any of their members in
G
.

Thus querying representatives from tied SCCs always reveals at least one new edge. The algorithm makes progress every round and terminates correctly (Theorem
40
).

Parallelization.

When
k
is small relative to
n

k
candidates can be queried in parallel.
The algorithm naturally supports this: non-finalized SCCs are independent, so batches of
\lfloor n/k\rfloor
queries can proceed simultaneously, reducing latency proportionally.

Correctness and termination.

BlitzRank

At this point, their loss counts are exact, and no unfinalized node can have smaller true loss count (since

Full proofs appear in Appendix

The worst-case query count is

Related Work

LLM-based document reranking.

LLM reranking methods fall into three paradigms.

Pointwise
methods score documents independently, enabling parallelism but discarding relative information; while trained cross-encoder models
(
7
)
dominate in efficiency, zero-shot LLM approaches include query likelihood
(
31
)
, which scores passages by
P(\text{query}|\text{document})
, and relevance classification with fine-grained labels
(
43
)
.

Pairwise
methods recover relative preferences:
28
introduced Pairwise Ranking Prompting with heapsort aggregation at
O(n\log n)
comparisons, noting that “pairwise comparisons are not guaranteed to be transitive.”

Listwise
methods compare multiple documents per call: RankGPT
(
33
)
and LRL
(
23
)
concurrently established the sliding-window paradigm, processing windows of 20 documents with stride 10, while subsequent work distilled this into open-source models
(
26
;
27
)
.
AcuRank
(
39
)
maintains Gaussian distributions over document relevance and performs Bayesian updates via TrueSkill, selectively reranking uncertain documents until confidence criteria are met.
These methods use fixed window sizes, which is problematic when document lengths vary or when long-context limitations – positional attention degradation
(
22
)
and effective context far below claimed capacity
(
17
)
– hurt performance; our framework accommodates variable
k
per round, adapting to both content and model constraints.

The
setwise
approach
(
44
)
bridges pairwise and listwise by prompting the LLM to select the most relevant from
k
candidates, using this primitive within heapsort.
While more efficient than pairwise, setwise extracts only the
winner
, discarding the remaining
\binom{k}{2}-(k-1)
pairwise relationships.
Our framework differs by extracting the
complete tournament
from each
k
-wise comparison and accumulating edges where transitive closure amplifies each query’s information yield.

Handling inconsistency in LLM rankings.

LLM judgments frequently violate transitivity.

41
address this via LLM-RankFusion, measuring inconsistency patterns and resolving them through rank aggregation.

34
show that shuffling and Kemeny-optimal aggregation mitigate positional biases; similarly, TourRank
(
6
)
runs multiple parallel tournaments with different random seeds and aggregates scores.
Most related to our approach, ELSPR
(
40
)
uses SCC analysis to quantify non-transitivity via structural entropy, but applies it to filter training examples for evaluator LLMs rather than for query-efficient selection.
REALM
(
38
)
takes a probabilistic approach, modeling relevance as Gaussian distributions updated via Bayesian inference.
Our framework offers a deterministic alternative that interprets cycles as meaningful structure – tiered rankings – rather than noise to be resolved.

Tournament theory and multi-wise comparisons.

Our framework builds on tournament graph theory
(
4
;
21
;
20
)
. The information-theoretic advantages of
k
-wise comparisons over pairwise feedback
(
29
;
32
)
is also relevant.
We provide a more detailed discussion of theoretical foundations in Appendix
E.5
.

Ranking quality.

Despite using fewer oracle calls, our method matches or exceeds baseline accuracy across most configurations.
With GPT-4.1,

the cost.

Pareto dominance.

The Pareto frontiers in Figure

Across all oracles, our method consistently occupies the upper-left region of the accuracy-efficiency space – achieving quality comparable to the most accurate methods at a fraction of their cost.

The efficiency-accuracy trade-off generalizes robustly across all five LLM oracles.
With Gemini-3-Flash,

BlitzRank
has a single hyperparameter: the window size
k
.
Across all 5 oracles (Table

(

.

5
Conclusion

We introduced a tournament graph framework for top-
m
selection via
k
-wise comparison oracles.
The core insight is that a
k
-wise comparison reveals a complete tournament – all
\binom{k}{2}
pairwise relationships – not just a winner, and transitive closure amplifies each query’s information yield without additional oracle calls.
The framework handles non-transitive preferences naturally: cycles indicate items that the oracle cannot distinguish, and SCC condensation produces principled tiered rankings rather than forcing a total order where none exists.
Empirically, our method achieves Pareto dominance across 14 benchmarks and 5 LLM oracles: matching or exceeding accuracy while requiring 25–40% fewer tokens than comparable methods, and 7
\times
fewer than pairwise approaches at near-identical quality.
Convergence is remarkably predictable (
\pm
2% variance in query count), enabling reliable cost estimation – a property that uncertainty-based adaptive methods lack.
The framework also accommodates variable
k
per round (any
k\geq 2
suffices for progress), enabling adaptation to heterogeneous document lengths and LLM context capacities without algorithmic modification.
Our analysis of strongly connected components confirms that cycles capture genuinely similar documents (40% lower within-SCC retrieval score variance), validating the theoretical interpretation of cycles as structure rather than noise.

Limitations.

Our framework assumes a deterministic oracle: each pairwise comparison has a fixed ground-truth outcome.
Real oracles – LLMs, crowdworkers, human experts – are noisy, and this noise interacts asymmetrically with transitive inference.
In principle, a single erroneous edge can collapse a long chain into an SCC, destroying discriminative power accumulated over many queries.
Our empirical analysis suggests such catastrophic collapse is rare with capable models on standard benchmarks, but a principled treatment remains desirable, especially for weaker oracles or more challenging domains.
Additionally, our approach uses priors only implicitly: first-stage retrieval scores inform tie-breaking within SCCs (preserving original order among indistinguishable items) but do not guide query selection or edge confidence. A more principled incorporation of priors is desirable.

Future work.
Our framework opens additional research directions.

(
Query complexity.
)
We showed
\lceil(n-1)/(k-1)\rceil
complexity for top-1 selection and conjectured a bound for general
m
(Conjecture
49
) from empirical observations.
A formal proof – or matching lower bound – remains open.
We also conjecture that transitive tournaments are worst-case: cycles collapse vertices into SCCs that finalize together, providing “free” progress that the transitive case lacks.

(
Noisy and probabilistic oracles.
)
Extending the framework to handle oracle noise is a significant open problem.
One approach models each edge as a random variable whose confidence accumulates across repeated or corroborating observations.
A key challenge is that edges differ in
structural importance
: an edge along a long chain discriminates many pairs transitively, so its corruption is catastrophic, while a leaf edge affects only one comparison.
Models that account for this asymmetry – weighting edges by transitive reach, or using soft SCCs that resist collapse from isolated errors – could yield algorithms that degrade gracefully under noise.

(
Incorporating priors.
) First-stage retrieval scores provide a natural prior over the ranking.
Algorithms that initialize edge beliefs with priors, or prioritize queries where the prior is uncertain, could improve both efficiency and robustness. This connects to to active learning formulations where queries maximize information gain relative to prior belief.

We hope the deterministic framework developed here provides a foundation for these extensions, much as noiseless sorting algorithms underpin the study of noisy comparison models.

Impact Statement

This paper develops algorithmic methods for query-efficient ranking using expensive comparison oracles. By reducing the number of oracle calls required to identify top candidates, our framework lowers computational costs when LLMs serve as oracles – contributing to more sustainable use of large-scale models. The techniques are domain-agnostic and apply broadly to ranking problems with costly comparisons (crowdsourcing, human evaluation, tournament design). We do not foresee specific negative societal consequences beyond those common to advances in information retrieval and ranking systems.

Appendix A
Baseline Comparison Rationale

Each baseline tests a specific aspect of our tournament graph framework.

Sliding Windows
(
33
)

This baseline directly tests our central hypothesis: sliding windows process overlapping document sets, yet discard comparison information from previous windows rather than accumulating it. Our framework captures these relationships via transitive closure, potentially achieving equivalent quality with fewer queries.

TourRank
(
6
)

While TourRank uses a static tournament structure to schedule matches, it does not employ tournament graphs to model comparison outcomes or exploit graph-theoretic properties such as transitivity. This comparison isolates the value of maintaining a tournament graph and propagating information via transitive closure versus using tournaments purely as a scheduling mechanism.

AcuRank
(
39
)
.

Both AcuRank and our method are adaptive – terminating when sufficiently confident about the top-
m
– but employ different certification criteria: AcuRank uses Bayesian score distributions, while our framework certifies via graph-theoretic finalization through transitive closure.

Setwise
(
44
)
.

This baseline provides the most direct contrast to our approach: both methods issue

Appendix E
Problem Statement

We consider the problem of identifying top-ranked vertices in an unknown
tournament graph through adaptive queries to an oracle.

E.1
Preliminaries

Let
G=(V,E)
be a directed graph on
n=|V|
vertices. We write

v\in G
and
e\in G
to mean
v\in V
and
e\in E
, respectively,
when the context is clear.

Tournament Graphs.

A
tournament
is a directed graph
G^{*}=(V,E^{*})
in which
every pair of distinct vertices is connected by exactly one directed
edge: for all
u\neq v\in V
, either
(u,v)\in E^{*}
or
(v,u)\in E^{*}
,
but not both. Throughout,
G^{*}
denotes an unknown tournament that
we seek to discover through oracle queries. We use the superscript
*

to indicate quantities associated with
G^{*}
.

Degrees and Reachability.

For a vertex
u
in a directed graph
G=(V,E)
, the
out-neighbors

is
N_{G}^{+}(u)=\{v\in V:(u,v)\in E\}
with
out-degree

\deg^{+}_{G}(u)=|N_{G}^{+}(u)|
,
and the
in-neighbors
is
N_{G}^{-}(u)=\{v\in V:(v,u)\in E\}

with
in-degree

\deg^{-}_{G}(u)=|N_{G}^{-}(u)|
.

We write
u\leadsto_{G}v
to denote the existence of a directed path
from
u
to
v
in
G
. The
in-reach
and
out-reach

of a vertex
v
are defined as

\displaystyle R^{-}_{G}(v)

\displaystyle:=\{u\in V\setminus\{v\}:u\leadsto_{G}v\},

\displaystyle R^{+}_{G}(v)

\displaystyle:=\{u\in V\setminus\{v\}:v\leadsto_{G}u\}.

Note that under transitive closure, in-reach and out-reach reduce
to in-neighborhood and out-neighborhood, respectively.

E.2
Oracle Model

For a set
U
with
|U|=n
and a positive integer
k
, let
\binom{U}{k}:=\{S\subseteq U:|S|=k\}

denote the collection of all
k
-subsets of
U
, and let
{U}^{\underline{k}}:=\{(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})\in U^{k}:u_{i}\neq u_{j}\text{ for all }i\neq j\}

denote the set of all ordered
k
-tuples of distinct elements from

U
.

We assume access to an oracle
O_{G^{*}}:\binom{V}{k}\to 2^{E^{*}}

that, given any
k
-subset
S\in\binom{V}{k}
, returns all directed
edges of
G^{*}
induced by
S
:

O_{G^{*}}(S):=E^{*}\cap{S}^{\underline{2}}.

E.3
Problem Statement: Ranking by In-Degree

Let
v_{*}^{(1)},v_{*}^{(2)},\ldots,v_{*}^{(n)}
be an ordering of
the vertices in
V
by non-decreasing in-degree in
G^{*}
:

r_{*}^{(1)}\leq r_{*}^{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq r_{*}^{(n)},\quad\text{where }r_{*}^{(i)}:=\deg^{-}_{G^{*}}(v_{*}^{(i)}).

In the tournament interpretation, a vertex with smaller in-degree
has fewer losses, hence higher rank. The sequence
R_{*}:=(r_{*}^{(1)},\ldots,r_{*}^{(n)})

is the
rank sequence
of
G^{*}
. When
G^{*}
is transitive,
this sequence is strictly increasing with
r_{*}^{(i)}=i-1
for each

i
.

Main Problem
: Given an unlabeled vertex set
V
of size
n
,
an oracle
O_{G^{*}}
that reveals all edges among any queried
k
-subset,
and a target count
m\leq n
, identify the top-
m
ranked vertices

v_{*}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{*}^{(m)}
using as few oracle queries as possible.

Remark 1
.

When
G^{*}
is transitive, the top-
m
vertices form a unique subset
(indeed, a unique sequence) of
V
. In the non-transitive case, ties
in in-degree may cause the top-
m
set to be non-unique. A natural
variant asks only to identify
some
set of
m
vertices achieving
the
m
smallest in-degrees. We formalize the non-transitive case
in Section
G.4
.

E.4
Algorithm Framework

We adopt an iterative approach: at each round, we query a carefully
chosen subset of vertices, update our knowledge of the graph, and
terminate once we have sufficient information to identify the top-
m

vertices (see Section
F.2
and
G.3

for the termination criterion in both transitive and non-transitive
case).

Notation.

At each round
t=1,2,\ldots,T
:

•

Q_{t}\in\binom{V}{k}
denotes the queried
k
-subset.

•

\hat{E}_{t}:=O_{G^{*}}(Q_{t})
denotes the edges revealed by the
oracle.

•

E_{t}:=E_{t-1}\cup\hat{E}_{t}
denotes the cumulative set of revealed
edges, with
E_{0}:=\emptyset
.

•

G_{t}:=(V,E_{t})
denotes the
revealed graph
at round
t
.

By construction,
\emptyset=E_{0}\subseteq E_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq E_{T}\subseteq E^{*}
.
A good algorithm should ideally produce a strictly increasing chain.

Discovered Rankings.

At round
t
, we order the vertices by non-decreasing in-reach in

G_{t}
, breaking ties arbitrarily:

v_{t}^{(1)},v_{t}^{(2)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(n)}\quad\text{with}\quad r_{t}^{(1)}\leq r_{t}^{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq r_{t}^{(n)},

where
r_{t}^{(i)}:=|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v_{t}^{(i)})|
. The sequence

R_{t}:=(r_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,r_{t}^{(n)})
is the
discovered
rank sequence
at round
t
. Initially,
R_{0}=(0,0,\ldots,0)
since
no edges have been revealed.

E.5
Theoretical Foundations and Related Work

Tournament theory and query complexity.

Tournaments are well-studied in computational social choice
(
4
;
21
)
.
Classic solutions include Copeland ranking (by outdegree), Slater ranking (minimum feedback arc set), and Kemeny ranking (minimum Kendall-tau distance); the latter two are NP-hard
(
1
;
3
)
.

14
established query complexity bounds for tournament solutions, proving that most require
\Omega(n^{2})
queries, but if the top cycle has bounded size
c
, complexity reduces to
O(nc+n\log n/\log(1-1/c))
.
The condensation of any tournament into SCCs yields a transitive tournament
(
2
)
, a fact central to our framework.

Multi-wise comparisons.

The information-theoretic advantage of
k
-wise comparisons is established.

29
prove that
full-ranking feedback
(complete ordering) improves sample complexity by factor
k\log k
over
winner feedback
for ranking recovery.

32
show similar gains under the Plackett-Luce model.

18
and
5
provide near-optimal algorithms for top-
m
selection under parametric models.
For noisy comparisons,
15
established
O(n\log n)
bounds for sorting, tightened by
16
.
Our framework operates non-parametrically, using graph structure rather than score estimation, and addresses the query-efficient top-
m
selection problem that arises when each comparison is expensive.

Appendix F
Transitive Tournaments

We now analyze Problem
E.3
for the special case of transitive
tournaments, where the underlying graph
G^{*}
satisfies: if
(u,v)\in E^{*}

and
(v,w)\in E^{*}
, then
(u,w)\in E^{*}
. We provide an algorithm
for this special case with provable guarantees.

The section is organized as follows. We first establish that discovered
in-reach provides a lower bound on true in-degree (Section
F.1
),
which enables pruning and finalization criteria (Section
F.2
).
We then present a greedy scheduling algorithm and prove its correctness
(Section
F.3
).

F.1
Transitive Closure Properties

In transitive tournaments, the transitive closure of the revealed
graph provides significant additional information. The following lemma
establishes that discovered in-reach never overestimates true in-degree.

Lemma 2

(Discovered In-Reach Lower Bounds True In-Degree)
.

Let
G^{*}
be a transitive tournament.
Then for all
v\in V
:

|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)|\leq\deg^{-}_{G^{*}}(v).

Proof.

Since
G_{t}
contains only edges revealed by the oracle, we have

E_{t}\subseteq E^{*}
, which implies
R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)\subseteq R^{-}_{G^{*}}(v)
.
By transitivity of
G^{*}
, in-reach coincides with in-neighborhood:

R^{-}_{G^{*}}(v)=N_{G^{*}}^{-}(v)
, hence
|R^{-}_{G^{*}}(v)|=\deg^{-}_{G^{*}}(v)
.
Combining these inequalities yields the result:

\left|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)\right|\leq\left|R^{-}_{G^{*}}(v)\right|=\deg^{-}_{G^{*}}(v).\qed

Lemma 3

(Discovered Ranks Underestimate True Ranks)
.

Let
G^{*}
be a transitive tournament.
Then
r_{t}^{(i)}\leq r_{*}^{(i)}
for all
i\in[n]
.

Proof.

Fix
i\in[n]
. The vertices
v_{*}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{*}^{(i)}
have
the
i
smallest true in-degrees by definition. For each
j\leq i
:

r_{*}^{(i)}\geq r_{*}^{(j)}=\deg^{-}_{G^{*}}(v_{*}^{(j)})\geq|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v_{*}^{(j)})|,

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
2
.
Thus at least
i
vertices have discovered in-reach at most
r_{*}^{(i)}
,
which implies
r_{t}^{(i)}\leq r_{*}^{(i)}
.
∎

F.2
Pruning and Finalization

The bounds from Section
F.1
enable two
key algorithmic primitives:
pruning
vertices that cannot be
in the top
m
, and
finalizing
vertices whose membership in
the top
m
is confirmed.

F.2.1
Pruning Criterion

Corollary 4

(Elimination Criterion)
.

Let
G^{*}
be a transitive tournament.
If at round
t
, a vertex
v
satisfies
|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)|\geq m
,
then
v\notin\{v_{*}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{*}^{(m)}\}
.

Proof.

In a transitive tournament on
n
vertices, the true in-degrees are
exactly
\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, so
r_{*}^{(m)}=m-1
. By Lemma
2
,

|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)|\leq\deg^{-}_{G^{*}}(v)
. Thus
|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)|\geq m

implies
\deg^{-}_{G^{*}}(v)\geq m>r_{*}^{(m)}
, so
v
cannot be
among the top
m
.
∎

F.2.2
Finalization Criterion

A vertex is
finalized
when we have sufficient information to
confirm its membership in the top-
m
set. The following proposition
characterizes when this occurs.

Proposition 5

(Top-
j
Finalization Criterion)
.

Let
G^{*}
be transitive. For

t\geq 1
, suppose there exists
j\in[n-1]
such that
r_{t}^{(1)}<r_{t}^{(2)}<\cdots<r_{t}^{(j)}<r_{t}^{(j+1)}
.
Then: (a)
r_{t}^{(i)}=i-1
for all
i\leq j
, and (b)
\{v_{t}^{(1)},v_{t}^{(2)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(j)}\}=\{v_{*}^{(1)},v_{*}^{(2)},\ldots,v_{*}^{(j)}\}
.

Proof.

We prove (a) by induction. For the base case,
r_{t}^{(1)}\geq 0

and
r_{t}^{(1)}\leq r_{*}^{(1)}=0
by Lemma
3
,
so
r_{t}^{(1)}=0
. For the inductive step, assuming
r_{t}^{(i-1)}=i-2
,
the strict inequality
r_{t}^{(i)}>r_{t}^{(i-1)}=i-2
combined with

r_{t}^{(i)}\leq r_{*}^{(i)}=i-1
from Lemma
3

forces
r_{t}^{(i)}=i-1
.

For (b), since
r_{t}^{(1)}<\cdots<r_{t}^{(j)}<r_{t}^{(j+1)}
are
strictly increasing non-negative integers, we have
r_{t}^{(j)}\geq j-1

and thus
r_{t}^{(j+1)}\geq j
. All vertices
v_{t}^{(j+1)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(n)}

have discovered in-reach at least
r_{t}^{(j+1)}\geq j
. By Corollary

4
, these cannot be among the top
j
, establishing
the set equality. The ordering within the set follows by matching
ranks.
∎

(Finalization Threshold

\text{TOP}_{t}
,

.

At round

The

\displaystyle\text{(1) }

\displaystyle r_{t}^{(i)}=i-1,\ \text{for all }i=1,\ldots,j

(4)

The

(5)

The algorithm terminates successfully when

. The following

Lemma 7

(Tied Candidates After Finalization)
.

If
0<m_{t}\leq n-2

r_{t}^{(m_{t}+1)}=r_{t}^{(m_{t}+2)}
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
r_{t}^{(m_{t}+1)}<r_{t}^{(m_{t}+2)}

We show that

, contradicting maximality of

m_{t}

Since

r_{t}^{(m_{t})}=m_{t}-1<r_{t}^{(m_{t}+1)}
.
Combined with
r_{t}^{(m_{t}+1)}\leq r_{*}^{(m_{t}+1)}=m_{t}
from

r_{t}^{(m_{t}+1)}=m_{t}
.
Thus
r_{t}^{(i)}=i-1
holds for all

, and the strict
inequality
r_{t}^{(m_{t}+1)}<r_{t}^{(m_{t}+2)}
satisfies the second

∎

Lemma 8

(No Penultimate Finalization)
.

Let
G^{*}
be a transitive tournament
on
n\geq 2
vertices. For any round

, we have
m_{t}\neq n-1
.
Consequently,
m_{t}\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-2,n\}
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
m_{t}=n-1
. By Definition

,
this requires
r_{t}^{(i)}=i-1
for all
i\in[n-1]
, and
r_{t}^{(n-1)}<r_{t}^{(n)}

(since

). The first condition gives
r_{t}^{(n-1)}=n-2

r_{t}^{(n)}\geq n-1
. By Lemma

r_{t}^{(n)}\leq r_{*}^{(n)}=n-1
,
forcing
r_{t}^{(n)}=n-1
. But then
j=n
satisfies the finalization
conditions (with

), contradicting maximality of

.
∎

(No Inconsistent Edge)
.

For any

,
if

F.3.2
Correctness

To establish correctness, we show that every non-terminal round reveals
at least one new edge, guaranteeing eventual termination.

Lemma 10

(Tied Candidates Have Unknown Edge)
.

Let
G^{*}
be a transitive
tournament. If
u,v\in V
satisfy
|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(u)|=|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)|
,
then the edge between
u
and
v
is not in
E_{t}
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that
(u,v)\in E_{t}
; without loss of
generality assume
u\to v
. For any
w\in R^{-}_{G_{t}}(u)
, there
exists a path
w\leadsto_{G_{t}}u
. Concatenating with
u\to v

gives
w\leadsto_{G_{t}}v
, so
w\in R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)
. Thus
R^{-}_{G_{t}}(u)\subseteq R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)
.
Additionally,
u\in R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)
but
u\notin R^{-}_{G_{t}}(u)
,
so
R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)\supseteq R^{-}_{G_{t}}(u)\cup\{u\}
where
the union is disjoint. This gives
|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)|\geq|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(u)|+1
,
a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 11

(Full Revelation Implies Complete Finalization)
.

Let
G^{*}
be a transitive tournament.
If
E_{t}=E^{*}
, then
m_{t}=n
.

Proof.

When
E_{t}=E^{*}
, we have
G_{t}=G^{*}
. For any vertex
v
,

|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)|=\deg^{-}_{G^{*}}(v)
. Since the in-degrees of
a transitive tournament on
n
vertices are exactly
\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
,
we have
r_{t}^{(i)}=i-1
for all
i\in[n]
. The finalization condition
is satisfied with
j=n
(since
n\in\{0,n\}
), giving
m_{t}=n
.
∎

Theorem 12
.

Algorithm
2

correctly identifies the top-
m
vertices of a transitive tournament
graph
G^{*}
and terminates in at most
\binom{n}{2}
rounds.

Proof.

At any non-terminal round
t
, we have
m_{t}<m\leq n
. By Lemma
8
,

m_{t}\neq n-1
, so
m_{t}\leq n-2
and thus
|C_{t}|=n-m_{t}\geq 2
.

We claim
r_{t}^{(m_{t}+1)}=r_{t}^{(m_{t}+2)}
. If
m_{t}>0
, this
follows from Lemma
7
. If
m_{t}=0
,
then the finalization condition
r_{t}^{(1)}<r_{t}^{(2)}
fails,
so
r_{t}^{(1)}=r_{t}^{(2)}
(since ranks are non-decreasing).

By Lemma
10
, the edge between

v_{t}^{(m_{t}+1)}
and
v_{t}^{(m_{t}+2)}
is not in
E_{t}
.
Since both vertices are in
Q_{t}
, querying the oracle reveals this
edge, ensuring
|E_{t+1}|>|E_{t}|
.

Since
|E_{t}|
strictly increases each round and
|E^{*}|=\binom{n}{2}
,
the algorithm terminates within
\binom{n}{2}
rounds. Upon termination
with
m_{t}\geq m
, Proposition
5

guarantees that
\{v_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(m)}\}=\{v_{*}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{*}^{(m)}\}
.
∎

F.4
A Stronger Finalization Criterion with Known Relationships

Proposition
13
provides a
simpler way to finalize nodes. We can simply check the known relationship
set
K_{t}(v):=R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)\cup R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v)
at time

t
to finalize nodes since nodes in
\text{TOP}_{t}
satisfy this
criterion.

Proposition 13

(Known Relationships Finalization Criterion)
.

If
v
is in
\text{TOP}_{t}
,
then
\left|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)\cup R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v)\right|=n-1
.

Proving Proposition
13
involves
establishing relationships between a node in
\text{TOP}_{t}
with
other nodes inside
\text{TOP}_{t}
as well as nodes outside
\text{TOP}_{t}
.
We first build some basic tools and analyze the interaction between
nodes inside
\text{TOP}_{t}
with nodes outside of
\text{TOP}_{t}
.

Lemma 14

(Downward Closure of In-Reach)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a DAG. If

x\in R^{-}_{G}(y)
, then
R^{-}_{G}(x)\subseteq R^{-}_{G}(y)
.

Proof.

Let
z\in R^{-}_{G}(x)
. By definition,
z\leadsto_{G}x
. Since

x\in R^{-}_{G}(y)
, we have
x\leadsto_{G}y
. Concatenating these
paths gives
z\leadsto_{G}y
, so
z\in R^{-}_{G}(y)
.
∎

Lemma 15

(Strict Decrease of In-Reach Along Edges)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a DAG. If

x\in R^{-}_{G}(y)
, then
|R^{-}_{G}(x)|<|R^{-}_{G}(y)|
.

Proof.

By Lemma
14
,
R^{-}_{G}(x)\subseteq R^{-}_{G}(y)
.
Since
x\in R^{-}_{G}(y)
but
x\notin R^{-}_{G}(x)
and
y\not\in R^{-}_{G}(x)

(due to otherwise being cyclic), the inclusion is strict.
∎

Lemma 16

(Non-Finalized Vertices Have Large In-Reach)
.

Let
G^{*}
be a transitive
tournament. If
u\notin\mathrm{TOP}_{t}
, then
|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(u)|\geq m_{t}
.

Proof.

Let
u=v_{t}^{(k)}
for some
k>m_{t}
. From the finalization criterion
(Definition
6
), we have
r_{t}^{(m_{t})}=m_{t}-1

and
r_{t}^{(m_{t})}<r_{t}^{(m_{t}+1)}
. Since the discovered rank
sequence is non-decreasing and
k>m_{t}
:

|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(u)|=r_{t}^{(k)}\geq r_{t}^{(m_{t}+1)}>r_{t}^{(m_{t})}=m_{t}-1.

Since in-reach sizes are integers,
|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(u)|\geq m_{t}
.
∎

The following Lemma connects the nodes outside of
\text{TOP}_{t}

to the nodes inside
\text{TOP}_{t}
(literally!).

Lemma 17

(Finalized Vertices Reach All Non-Finalized Vertices)
.

Let
G^{*}
be a transitive
tournament. For any
u\notin\mathrm{TOP}_{t}
and any
w\in\mathrm{TOP}_{t}
,
we have
w\leadsto_{G_{t}}u
.

Proof.

Let
u\not\in\text{TOP}_{t}
. We construct a sequence
(x_{i})_{i\geq 0}

iteratively from
u
to a node to
\text{TOP}_{t}
:

•

Set
x_{0}=u
.

•

While
R^{-}_{G_{t}}(x_{i})\cap C_{t}\neq\emptyset
, set
x_{i+1}\in R^{-}_{G_{t}}(x_{i})\cap C_{t}
.

•

We terminate the sequence when
R^{-}_{G_{t}}(x_{i})\cap C_{t}=\emptyset
.

Each
x_{i}
lies in
C_{t}=V\setminus\mathrm{TOP}_{t}
, so by Lemma

16
:

|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(x_{i})|\geq m_{t}\quad\text{for all }i.

By Lemma
15
, whenever
x_{i+1}
exists:

|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(x_{i+1})|<|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(x_{i})|.

The in-reach sizes form a strictly decreasing sequence of non-negative
integers bounded below by
m_{t}
. Therefore the sequence must terminate
at some finite index
k
. We show that at termination, the in-reach
equals
\mathrm{TOP}_{t}
.

At termination,
R^{-}_{G_{t}}(x_{k})\cap C_{t}=\emptyset
, which
means:

R^{-}_{G_{t}}(x_{k})\subseteq\mathrm{TOP}_{t}.

Since
x_{k}\in C_{t}
, Lemma
16

gives
|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(x_{k})|\geq m_{t}
. Combined with
|\mathrm{TOP}_{t}|=m_{t}

and the inclusion above:

R^{-}_{G_{t}}(x_{k})=\mathrm{TOP}_{t}.

By Lemma
14
, if
x_{i+1}\in R^{-}_{G_{t}}(x_{i})
,
then
R^{-}_{G_{t}}(x_{i+1})\subseteq R^{-}_{G_{t}}(x_{i})
.
Applying this repeatedly:

\mathrm{TOP}_{t}=R^{-}_{G_{t}}(x_{k})\subseteq R^{-}_{G_{t}}(x_{k-1})\subseteq\cdots\subseteq R^{-}_{G_{t}}(x_{0})=R^{-}_{G_{t}}(u).

Hence, for any
w\in\mathrm{TOP}_{t}
, we have
w\in R^{-}_{G_{t}}(u)
,
which by definition means
w\leadsto_{G_{t}}u
.
∎

The relationship between nodes within
\text{TOP}_{t}
are simpler
to analyze due to having abundant amount of information.

Lemma 18

(Exact In-Reach of Finalized Vertices)
.

Let
G^{*}
be a transitive
tournament. For any
v_{t}^{(j)}\in\mathrm{TOP}_{t}
, we have

R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v_{t}^{(j)})=\{v_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(j-1)}\}.

Proof.

Since
j\leq m_{t}
, the finalization criterion (Definition
6
)
gives
r_{t}^{(j)}=|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v_{t}^{(j)})|=j-1
. By Lemma

2
and transitivity of
G^{*}
:

R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v_{t}^{(j)})\subseteq R^{-}_{G^{*}}(v_{t}^{(j)})=N_{G^{*}}^{-}(v_{t}^{(j)})=\{v_{*}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{*}^{(j-1)}\}.

By Proposition
5
,
\{v_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(j-1)}\}=\{v_{*}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{*}^{(j-1)}\}
.
Since both
R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v_{t}^{(j)})
and
\{v_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(j-1)}\}

have cardinality
j-1
, equality holds.
∎

Corollary 19

(Mutual Reachability Within Finalized Set)
.

Let
G^{*}
be a transitive
tournament. For any
v_{t}^{(i)},v_{t}^{(j)}\in\mathrm{TOP}_{t}

with
i<j
, we have
v_{t}^{(i)}\leadsto_{G_{t}}v_{t}^{(j)}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
18
,
R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v_{t}^{(j)})=\{v_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(j-1)}\}
.
Since
i<j
, we have
v_{t}^{(i)}\in\{v_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(j-1)}\}=R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v_{t}^{(j)})
,
which by definition means
v_{t}^{(i)}\leadsto_{G_{t}}v_{t}^{(j)}
.
∎

We now have all the tools.

Proof of Proposition
13
.

Let
v=v_{t}^{(j)}
for some
j\leq m_{t}
. We show that every
vertex in
V\setminus\{v\}
belongs to either
R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)

or
R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v)
. First, by Lemma
18
,
we have

R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)=\{v_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(j-1)}\}.

Now, for any
v_{t}^{(i)}\in\mathrm{TOP}_{t}
with
i>j
, Corollary

19
gives
v_{t}^{(j)}\leadsto_{G_{t}}v_{t}^{(i)}
,
so:

\{v_{t}^{(j+1)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(m_{t})}\}\subseteq R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v).

Finally, by Lemma
17
, for any

u\in C_{t}=V\setminus\mathrm{TOP}_{t}
, we have
v\leadsto_{G_{t}}u
,
so:

C_{t}\subseteq R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v).

The sets
R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)
and
R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v)
are disjoint
(since
G_{t}
is a DAG and any vertex in their intersection would
create a cycle through
v
). Combining the results above:

\displaystyle R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)

\displaystyle=\{v_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(j-1)}\},

\displaystyle R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v)

\displaystyle\supseteq\{v_{t}^{(j+1)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(m_{t})}\}\cup C_{t}.

These sets partition
V\setminus\{v\}
:

V\setminus\{v\}=\{v_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(j-1)}\}\cup\{v_{t}^{(j+1)},\ldots,v_{t}^{(m_{t})}\}\cup C_{t}.

Therefore:

R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)\cup R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v)\supseteq V\setminus\{v\}.

Since trivially
R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)\cup R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v)\subseteq V\setminus\{v\}
,
we conclude:

\left|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)\cup R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v)\right|=n-1.\qed

Remark 20
.

The converse,
\left|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)\cup R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v)\right|=n-1\implies v\in\text{TOP}_{t}

is not true in general. Consider
V=\left\{a,b,c\right\}
and
E_{t}=\left\{\left(a,c\right),\left(b,c\right)\right\}

for example where
\left|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(c)\cup R^{+}_{G_{t}}(c)\right|=2=n-1

but
c
is a bottom candidate!

Appendix G
Non-Transitive Tournaments

We now extend the algorithm and correctness analysis to general (non-transitive)
tournaments. When
G^{*}
is not transitive, vertices may have equal
in-degrees (forming cycles), so the top-
m
set may not be unique.
Moreover, in-reach in
G_{t}
no longer directly bounds in-degree
in
G^{*}
. The key insight is that the condensation graph of any
tournament is itself a transitive tournament, allowing us to lift
the machinery developed in the previous section.

We begin by reviewing SCCs and showing that the condensation of any
tournament is transitive (Section
G.1
).
We then establish how SCCs in the revealed graph relate to those in
the true graph via a refinement relationship (Section
G.2
).
This enables finalization criteria analogous to Section
F

(Section
G.3
). Finally, we present
the generalized algorithm and prove correctness (Sections
G.4

and
G.5
).

G.1
Strongly Connected Components and Condensation

Definition 21

(Strongly Connected Component)
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a directed graph. A
strongly connected component

(SCC) of
G
is a maximal subset
C\subseteq V
such that for all

u,v\in C
, there exist directed paths
u\rightsquigarrow_{G}v
and
v\rightsquigarrow_{G}u
.
We define the equivalence relation
\sim_{G}
on
V
by

u\sim_{G}v\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u\text{ and }v\text{ are in the same SCC of }G.

For
u\in V
, we denote by
[u]_{G}
the equivalence class of
u

under
\sim_{G}
.

Definition 22

(Condensation Graph)
.

The
condensation graph
of
G
,
denoted
\left[G\right]
, is the directed graph with vertex set
V/{\sim_{G}}

and edge set

\left[E\right]=\left\{([u]_{G},[v]_{G})\mid\exists\,x\in[u]_{G},\,y\in[v]_{G}\text{ such that }(x,y)\in E\text{ and }[u]_{G}\neq[v]_{G}\right\}.

Lemma 23

(Condensation is a DAG)
.

For any directed graph
G
, the
condensation
\left[G\right]
is a DAG.

Proof.

Suppose
\left[G\right]
contains a cycle
[u_{1}]\to[u_{2}]\to\cdots\to[u_{\ell}]\to[u_{1}]

with
\ell\geq 2
. Then for any
x\in[u_{1}]
and
y\in[u_{2}]
,
we have paths
x\rightsquigarrow_{G}y
and
y\rightsquigarrow_{G}x
(via the cycle), implying

x\sim_{G}y
. This contradicts
[u_{1}]\neq[u_{2}]
.
∎

Proposition 24

(Condensation of Tournament is Transitive Tournament)
.

If
G^{*}
is a tournament,
then
\left[G^{*}\right]
is a transitive tournament.

Proof.

Let
C_{1},C_{2}
be distinct SCCs of
G^{*}
. Since
G^{*}
is
a tournament, for any
u\in C_{1}
and
v\in C_{2}
, either
(u,v)\in E^{*}

or
(v,u)\in E^{*}
.

We claim all edges between
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
go in the same direction.
Suppose not: let
(u_{1},v_{1}),(v_{2},u_{2})\in E^{*}
with
u_{1},u_{2}\in C_{1}

and
v_{1},v_{2}\in C_{2}
. Since
u_{1}\sim_{G^{*}}u_{2}
and
v_{1}\sim_{G^{*}}v_{2}
,
we have paths
u_{2}\rightsquigarrow_{G^{*}}u_{1}
and
v_{1}\rightsquigarrow_{G^{*}}v_{2}
.
Then

u_{1}\to v_{1}\rightsquigarrow_{G^{*}}v_{2}\to u_{2}\rightsquigarrow_{G^{*}}u_{1}

forms a cycle containing both
u_{1}
and
v_{1}
, contradicting

C_{1}\neq C_{2}
.

Thus
\left[G^{*}\right]
is a tournament. Furthermore, since every
acyclic tournament is transitive,
\left[G^{*}\right]
is a transitive
tournament.
∎

G.2
SCC Refinement and Projection

The revealed graph
G_{t}
has coarser information than
G^{*}
,
which manifests as a refinement relationship between their SCCs.

Lemma 25

(SCC Refinement)
.

For all
t\geq 0
and
u,v\in V
:

u\sim_{G_{t}}v\implies u\sim_{G^{*}}v.

Equivalently, each SCC of
G_{t}
is contained in some SCC of
G^{*}
.

Proof.

If
u\sim_{G_{t}}v
, then there exist paths
u\rightsquigarrow_{G_{t}}v
and

v\rightsquigarrow_{G_{t}}u
. Since
E_{t}\subseteq E^{*}
, these paths exist
in
G^{*}
, so
u\sim_{G^{*}}v
.
∎

Definition 26

(Projection Map)
.

The refinement induces a natural projection
\phi_{t}:\left[V_{t}\right]\to\left[V^{*}\right]

defined by

\phi_{t}([u]_{G_{t}})=[u]_{G^{*}}.

This is well-defined by Lemma
25
.

Lemma 27

(Path Preservation Under Projection)
.

For all
t\geq 0
: if
D\rightsquigarrow_{\left[G_{t}\right]}C

in
\left[G_{t}\right]
, then either
\phi_{t}(D)=\phi_{t}(C)
or

\phi_{t}(D)\rightsquigarrow_{\left[G^{*}\right]}\phi_{t}(C)
.

Proof.

Consider a path
D=D_{0}\to D_{1}\to\cdots\to D_{\ell}=C
in
\left[G_{t}\right]
.
For each edge
(D_{i},D_{i+1})\in\left[E_{t}\right]
, there exist
x_{i}\in D_{i}
,

y_{i}\in D_{i+1}
with
(x_{i},y_{i})\in E_{t}\subseteq E^{*}
.

If
\phi_{t}(D_{i})\neq\phi_{t}(D_{i+1})
, then
[x_{i}]_{G^{*}}\neq[y_{i}]_{G^{*}}
,
so
(\phi_{t}(D_{i}),\phi_{t}(D_{i+1}))\in\left[E^{*}\right]
.

If
\phi_{t}(D_{i})=\phi_{t}(D_{i+1})
, the edge collapses to a single
vertex.

Concatenating non-collapsed edges yields a (possibly empty) path from

\phi_{t}(D)
to
\phi_{t}(C)
in
\left[G^{*}\right]
.
∎

Corollary 28

(Separation Preservation)
.

For all
t\geq 0
: If
\phi_{t}(D)\neq\phi_{t}(C)

and there is no path from
\phi_{t}(D)
to
\phi_{t}(C)
in
\left[G^{*}\right]
,
then there is no path from
D
to
C
in
\left[G_{t}\right]
.

G.3
Finalization on Condensation

Let
n_{t}:=|\left[V_{t}\right]|
denote the number of SCCs in
G_{t}
,
and
n^{*}:=|\left[V^{*}\right]|
the number in
G^{*}
. Since
\left[G^{*}\right]

is transitive (Proposition
24
),
in-reach equals in-degree:

\left|R^{-}_{\left[G^{*}\right]}(C)\right|=\deg^{-}_{\left[G^{*}\right]}(C).

Definition 29

(Rank Sequences for Condensation)
.

Let
\left(C_{*}^{(1)},C_{*}^{(2)},\ldots,C_{*}^{(n^{*})}\right)

be the SCCs of
G^{*}
ordered by non-decreasing in-degree in
\left[G^{*}\right]
:

\rho_{*}^{(1)}\leq\rho_{*}^{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq\rho_{*}^{(n^{*})},\quad\text{where }\rho_{*}^{(i)}:=\deg^{-}_{\left[G^{*}\right]}(C_{*}^{(i)}).

Since
\left[G^{*}\right]
is transitive,
\rho_{*}^{(i)}=i-1
.

Similarly, let
\left(C_{t}^{(1)},C_{t}^{(2)},\ldots,C_{t}^{(n_{t})}\right)

be the SCCs of
G_{t}
ordered by non-decreasing in-reach in
\left[G_{t}\right]
:

\rho_{t}^{(1)}\leq\rho_{t}^{(2)}\leq\cdots\leq\rho_{t}^{(n_{t})},\quad\text{where }\rho_{t}^{(i)}:=R^{-}_{\left[G_{t}\right]}(C_{t}^{(i)}).

Definition 30

(Generalized Finalization Threshold and Sets)
.

At round
t
, let
n_{t}:=|\left[V_{t}\right]|
. We define:

The
finalization threshold

m_{t}
is the largest
j\geq 0
such that:

\displaystyle\text{(1) }

\displaystyle\rho_{t}^{(i)}=i-1\text{ for all }i=1,\ldots,j,

(6)

\displaystyle\text{(2) }

\displaystyle j\in\{0,n_{t}\}\text{ or }\rho_{t}^{(j)}<\rho_{t}^{(j+1)}.

The
finalized SCC set

[\mathrm{TOP}_{t}]:=\{C_{t}^{(1)},\ldots,C_{t}^{(m_{t})}\}
.

The
finalized vertex set

\mathrm{TOP}_{t}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m_{t}}C_{t}^{(i)}
.

The
candidate SCC set

\left[C_{t}\right]:=\left[V_{t}\right]\setminus[\mathrm{TOP}_{t}]
.

Note: Compare these definitions to the Definition
6
in Section
F.2
.

We now show a key Proposition.

Proposition 31

(Finalization Correctness)
.

If
m_{t}\geq 1
,
then for all
i\leq m_{t}
:

\phi_{t}(C_{t}^{(i)})=C_{*}^{(i)}.

That is, the finalized SCCs in
\left[G_{t}\right]
correctly identify
the top-ranked SCCs in
\left[G^{*}\right]
.

Proof.

We prove by strong induction on
i
.

Base case (
i=1
):
Since
\left[G_{t}\right]
is a DAG, there
exists a source and hence some SCC with in-reach
0
. This implies
that
\rho_{t}^{(1)}=0
. Since
m_{t}\geq 1
implies
\rho_{t}^{(2)}>\rho_{t}^{(1)}=0
,

C_{t}^{(1)}
must be the unique source in
\left[G_{t}\right]
. We
claim
\phi_{t}(C_{t}^{(1)})=C_{*}^{(1)}
. Let
\mathcal{F}:=\phi_{t}^{-1}(C_{*}^{(1)})=\{D\in\left[V_{t}\right]:\phi_{t}(D)=C_{*}^{(1)}\}

be the fiber over
C_{*}^{(1)}
. Since
C_{*}^{(1)}
is the unique
source (in-degree
0
) of
\left[G^{*}\right]
, no SCC outside
C_{*}^{(1)}

has a path to
C_{*}^{(1)}
in
\left[G^{*}\right]
. Hence, by Corollary

28
, no SCC outside
\mathcal{F}

has a path to any SCC in
\mathcal{F}
within
\left[G_{t}\right]
.

The induced subgraph
\left[G_{t}\right][\mathcal{F}]
is a DAG (as
a subgraph of the DAG
\left[G_{t}\right]
) with no incoming edges
from outside. Hence
\mathcal{F}
contains at least one source of

\left[G_{t}\right]
. But
C_{t}^{(1)}
is the unique SCC of
\left[G_{t}\right]

with
\rho_{t}=0
(since
\rho_{t}^{(1)}<\rho_{t}^{(2)}
by the
finalization condition when
m_{t}\geq 1
). Thus
C_{t}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{F}

i.e.,
\phi_{t}(C_{t}^{(1)})=C_{*}^{(1)}
.

Inductive step:
Assume
\phi_{t}(C_{t}^{(j)})=C_{*}^{(j)}

for all
j<i
where
i\leq m_{t}
. We show
\phi_{t}(C_{t}^{(i)})=C_{*}^{(i)}
.
Consider the fiber
\mathcal{F}_{i}:=\phi_{t}^{-1}(C_{*}^{(i)})
.
By the inductive hypothesis,
\phi_{t}\left(C_{t}^{(j)}\right)=C_{*}^{(j)}

Since

As discussed above, the only paths to

Note that

\phi_{t}(C_{t}^{(i)})=\phi_{t}(D_{0})=C_{*}^{(i)}

∎

Proposition 43

(Known Relationships Finalization Criterion)
.

If

(In-Reach and Out-Reach Intersection)

For any directed graph

Remark 46

For

\left|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)\cup R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v)\right|=\left|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)\right|+\left|R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v)\right|-\left(\left|[v]_{G}\right|-1\right).

\displaystyle\left|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)\cup R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)\right|+\left|R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v)\right|-\left|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)\cap R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|R^{-}_{G_{t}}(v)\right|+\left|R^{+}_{G_{t}}(v)\right|-\left(\left|[v]_{G}\right|-1\right).

Appendix H
Query Complexity Discussion

H.1
Setup and Notation

We measure
query complexity
as the number of calls made to the

k
-wise oracle. Formally, an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
adaptively
selects query sets
Q_{1},Q_{2},\ldots
with
Q_{t}\subseteq V
and

|Q_{t}|\leq k
, observes
\mathcal{O}(Q_{t})
, and terminates once it can
certify the desired output (top-
m
selection).

Instance-wise and worst-case complexity.

For a fixed underlying tournament
G^{*}
, let
T_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{*})

denote the (random) number of oracle calls made by
\mathcal{A}
until
termination.
6
6

6

In this paper the oracle is deterministic; randomness, if any, comes from tie-breaking or implementation choices.

We define the worst-case query complexity over a class
\mathcal{G}
as

Q_{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{G};n,k,m):=\sup_{G^{*}\in\mathcal{G}}T_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{*}).

In particular, we will distinguish the transitive class

\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\mathrm{tr}}
(total orders) from the general class

\mathcal{T}_{n}
(all tournaments).

Sequential vs. parallel depth.

The complexity above counts total oracle calls.
If multiple disjoint query sets are evaluated in parallel, a second
measure is the number of
rounds
(parallel depth). We defer a
formal treatment of parallel depth to future work.

H.2
Transitive Case

The
\binom{n}{2}
bound from Theorem
12
is a
worst-case upper bound that does not exploit the structure of greedy
selection. We now derive tighter bounds.

Proposition 48

(Top-1 Query Complexity)
.

When
m=1
, Algorithm
2

identifies the top vertex in at most
\left\lceil\frac{n-1}{k-1}\right\rceil
queries.

Proof.

In the transitive setting, the restriction of
G^{*}
to any queried
set
Q
is a total order, hence it has a unique best element (the one
with zero losses within
Q
). By transitivity, any other element of
Q

loses to this best element and therefore cannot be the global best.
Thus each query can eliminate at least
|Q|-1\geq k-1
candidates from
being the top vertex. Starting from
n
candidates, after
q
queries at
most
n-q(k-1)
candidates remain; requiring
n-q(k-1)\leq 1
yields the
bound.
∎

H.3
A Conjecture for the Transitive Case

Beyond
m=1
, a tight analysis requires tracking how the
finalization threshold
m_{t}
evolves as the revealed graph densifies.
We state a conjectural bound motivated by the structure of the algorithm
and validated empirically in Section
H.6
.

Conjecture 49

(Transitive Top-
m
Query Complexity)
.

Algorithm
2

identifies the top-
m
vertices in

O\!\left(\frac{n-1}{k-1}+\frac{m-1}{k-1}\cdot\left(1+\log_{k}m\right)\right)

queries.

Remark 50
.

The conjectured form decomposes into two terms:

•

A
candidate reduction
term
(n-1)/(k-1)
: the cost to winnow

n
candidates down to a single maximum, matching Proposition
48
.

•

A
frontier refinement
term
(m-1)/(k-1)\cdot(\log_{k}m+2)
:
the additional cost to extract elements
2,\ldots,m
from the remaining
candidates, reflecting the logarithmic depth of repeated selection.

The
O(\cdot)
notation hides a constant factor.
Empirically (Section
H.6
), we observe that
this constant is at most
1.25
across all tested configurations,
suggesting the functional form is tight up to lower-order terms.
A formal proof remains open.

H.4
Non-Transitive Case

We conjecture that transitive tournaments are worst-case instances for query complexity, so these bounds extend to the general case.

Remark 51

(Why Cycles Help)
.

The intuition is that cycles can only make top-
m
selection
easier
.
When vertices form a cycle in
G^{*}
, they belong to the same SCC and thus share a common rank in the condensation DAG.
Once the algorithm discovers such a cycle, all vertices in the SCC finalize simultaneously – they need not be distinguished from one another.
In contrast, the transitive case requires establishing pairwise orderings among all vertices: no shortcuts exist.

More precisely, for any tournament
G^{*}
, one can construct a “shadow” transitive tournament
\tilde{G}
that respects the SCC tier structure of
G^{*}
: vertices in better-ranked tiers beat vertices in worse-ranked tiers, with intra-tier orderings assigned arbitrarily.
Inter-tier edges in
\tilde{G}
are determined by the same tier structure as in
G^{*}
, so any query sequence reveals the same inter-tier relationships in both.
The difference lies within tiers: in
\tilde{G}
, all vertices are singletons requiring full resolution; in
G^{*}
, cycles merge vertices into SCCs that finalize together.
Thus the query count on
G^{*}
should never exceed that on
\tilde{G}
.
A formal proof of this reduction remains open.

H.5

We empirically measure the query complexity of Algorithm
1

on synthetic transitive instances to validate Conjecture
49
.

Instance generation.

A transitive tournament on
n
vertices corresponds to a total order,
uniquely determined by a permutation
\pi:[n]\to[n]
.
We generate instances by sampling
\pi
uniformly at random; the
underlying tournament
G^{*}_{\pi}
has edge

\pi^{-1}(u)<\pi^{-1}(v)
.
The oracle
\mathcal{O}
applied to any query set
Q\subseteq V

returns the restriction of
G^{*}_{\pi}
to
Q
– equivalently, the
total order on
Q

\pi
.

Measurement protocol.

For each parameter triple

, we execute Algorithm

with target count

and record

, the number of
oracle calls until termination.
Rather than running separate experiments for each

we exploit a key observation: a single execution with

(full sort)
reveals

Main observations.

1.

The conjectured form captures the scaling.

Across all tested
(n,k)
pairs, the empirical curves follow the
shape predicted by
B(n,k,m)
: sublinear in
k

plateau for small
m

2.

The constant is small.

The maximum observed query count satisfies

\max_{\pi}T_{\mathcal{A}}(G^{*}_{\pi})\leq 1.25\cdot B(n,k,m)

for all tested configurations.
The median typically lies below
B(n,k,m)
itself.

3.

Variance is low.

The 10th–90th percentile bands are narrow, indicating that
query complexity is stable across random permutations and
not dominated by rare worst-case instances within our sample.

Limitations.

These experiments provide evidence for, but do not prove,
Conjecture
49
. Key caveats:

•

We test only random transitive instances; adversarially
constructed permutations could yield higher query counts.

•

The parameter range is bounded (
n\leq 800
,
k\leq 50
);
extrapolation to larger scales is uncertain.

•

The
1.25

not captured by the asymptotic form, or slack in our analysis.

A formal proof of Conjecture
49
remains open
and would require a tighter analysis of how the finalization threshold
