Positions of cysteine residues reveal local clusters and hidden relationships to Sequons and Transmembrane domains in Human proteins
Authors: Manthan Desai, Bingyun Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMembrane proteins often possess critical structural features, such as transmembrane domains (TMs), N-glycosylation, and disulfide bonds (SS bonds), which are essential to their structure and function. Here, we extend the study of the motifs carrying N-glycosylation, i.e. the sequons, and the Cys residues supporting the SS bonds, to the whole human proteome with a particular focus on the Cys positions in human proteins with respect to those of sequons and TMs. As the least abundant amino acid residue in protein sequences, the positions of Cys residues in proteins are not random but rather selected through evolution. We discovered that the frequency of Cys residues in proteins is length dependent, and the frequency of CC gaps formed between adjacent Cys residues can be used as a classifier to distinguish proteins with special structures and functions, such as keratin-associated proteins (KAPs), extracellular proteins with EGF-like domains, and nuclear proteins with zinc finger C2H2 domains. Most importantly, by comparing the positions of Cys residues to those of sequons and TMs, we discovered that these structural features can form dense clusters in highly repeated and mutually exclusive modalities in protein sequences. The evolutionary advantages of such complementarity among the three structural features are discussed, particularly in light of structural dynamics in proteins that are lacking from computational predictions. The discoveries made here highlight the sequence-structure-function axis in biological organisms that can be utilized in future protein engineering toward synthetic biology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77056-8.Keywords: Posttranslational modifications, Cysteine residues, Disulfide bonds, Sequons, N-glycosylation, Transmembrane domains, Protein structure and function, Protein sequenceSubject terms: Biochemistry, Molecular biology

Introduction

Proteins are major biomolecules that perform most functions in living organisms using their special structures. Our previous studies of three structural features of membrane proteins, i.e. the transmembrane domain (TM), N-glycosylation, and disulfide bonds (SS bonds), allowed us to discover some interesting relationships among them1,2. Notably, Cys residues support the formation of SS bonds; the sequon motif, i.e. NXS/T, X can be any amino acid but not P, supports the formation of N-glycosylation; and both Cys residues and sequons are commonly present in protein sequences. Previous studies have indicated that SS bonds are closely correlated with the frequency of Cys residues in proteins3, and tight relationships between N-glycosylation and the sequon frequency of N-glycoproteins are widely used in the prediction of N-glycosylation4–6. We are therefore interested in exploring the potential relationships between Cys residues and sequons, and their positions with respect to the TMs.

Cysteine is the least abundant amino acid in human proteins7,8, and its position in protein sequences is often conserved across species. Interestingly, the utilization of Cys was found to be linked to organismal complexity and parallel to evolution8. Mutagenesis studies revealed that miscoding Cys at positions of other amino acids has relatively low toxicity9. However, surface-exposed and nonclustered Cys residues are known to be removed during evolution, potentially due to their negative effects on protein functions10. Marino et al. reported that Cys, as opposed to other amino acids, exhibited both conservation and degeneration into other amino acids in proteins10. These characteristics are reflected by the special functions carried out by Cys residues. Four general classes of functions have been frequently used to define Cys residues in proteins: structural disulfide (SS) bonds, metal-Cys clusters, regulatory and catalytic Cys residues10. Cys has a low redox potential and is typically oxidized in a physiological environment to form cystine with an SS bond joining two cysteines together. In proteins, Cys residues also form SS bonds to stabilize protein folds and participate in catalytic activities. Due to the nucleophilic nature of the sulfhydryl group, metal thiolate complexes can easily form. Cys residues are pH- and redox-sensitive and can undergo numerous other posttranslational modifications, such as S-nitrosylation, S-aceylation, S-glutathionylation and S-sulfenylation11. These PTMs are often reversible and function as regulatory switches in a number of physiological processes11. As a catalytic agent, proteins such as thiol oxidoreductases use redox Cys residues to perform SS bond isomerization and disulfide relay among glutathiones. Because SS bonds provide rigidity to protein structures, Cys is considered “order promoting”12,13. Early studies by Petersen et al3. revealed that proteins have more even than odd numbers of this residue due to the formation of SS bonds.

In addition to Cys residues, proteins also present other structural features, such as sequons and TMs. Sequon, i.e. NXS/T (X is not P), is a conserved and ancient motif in protein sequences that is known to host N-glycosylation sites for membrane and secreted proteins14. Thorough research on N-glycosylation has revealed that the oligosaccharide transferase (OST) complex prefers NXT over NXS for N-glycosylation15. Interestingly, this motif is also abundant in nucleocytosolic proteins. Typically, sequons facilitate the formation of turn structures (such as β-turns) in both nucleocytosolic proteins and membrane/secreted proteins16. We recently discovered that sequons, particularly NXS motifs, are abundant in the turn of C2H2 zinc finger domains and that the Ser residue in the sequon motif can participate in the binding of nucleic acids16. Mutations that form novel sequons in proteins are often deleterious and pathogenic17. Therefore, the presence of sequons in protein sequences is also not random but evolutionarily conserved18.

Finally, transmembrane domains (TMs) are landmarks for integral membrane proteins. This structural feature not only determines cellular localization but also defines the topology of integral membrane proteins19,20. The compartmentalization of lipid bilayers has a profound impact on protein structure and function. Most of the endo- and ecto-domains of integral membrane proteins have drastically different structures. For example, N-glycosylation occurs only in the ectodomain of membrane proteins14. Our analysis showed that the sequons residing in the ecto-domains of membrane proteins are enriched in NXT as opposed to NXS16, a bias that is also present in the sequons of secreted and nucleocytosolic proteins21compartmentalized by the plasma membrane. This bias matches the OST preference of NXT over NXS for increased efficiency of N-glycosylation15. Clearly, not only the position of the TM domains but also the protein topology is not random and have been evolutionarily selected.

With the development and maturation of human genomics22, most protein sequences in the human genome have been obtained23. Structural and functional annotations of this vast protein sequence space have been the bottleneck for unlocking the molecular fundamentals underpinning diverse biological activities. The ever-increasing power of artificial intelligence, represented by AlphaFold, has accelerated the speed and efficiency of predicting potential protein structures24,25. The obstacles preventing the effective translation of known sequences and predicted protein structures into an understanding of biological activities lie in the limited knowledge of protein structural dynamics, e.g., posttranslational modifications, including the modifications of thiol groups and the N-glycosylation of sequons. Many PTMs, such as phosphorylation, are reversible and can regulate protein interaction networks, thereby impacting biological activities. Understanding how evolution has shaped these PTMs at the protein sequence level is important for understanding their biological activities and for guiding future efforts in protein design and engineering.

We previously described complex relationships between SS bonds and N-glycosylation in the literature1and further revealed complementarity between SS bonds, N-glycans and TMs in integral membrane proteins2. Here, we broaden such studies to the entire annotated human proteome to understand the hidden relationships among protein structures and functions.

Methods

Acquisition of protein sequences and positions of Cys residues, sequons, and TMs.Human proteins reviewed in UniProt were used for the analysis. The protein canonical sequences, lengths, annotated SS bonds and N-glycosylation sites were obtained on 2023-05-13 and updated on 2024-05-10. The positions of Cys and asparagine residues in the sequons (NXS/T and X is not P) were obtained for each protein. The starting and ending positions of transmembrane domains (TMs) and the corresponding membrane topology (i.e., endo- or ectodomain with respect to the membrane) were obtained by Deep TMHMM prediction26.

Analyses of frequency, density, gaps and loops among features. Total numbers (i.e., feature frequency) of Cys residues, sequons, and TMs of each protein were obtained. Normalization of the total counts of Cys residues, sequons, and TM domains to the corresponding protein length (a description of feature density) was carried out to obtain the fragment length of each structural feature for every protein. The length of each gap or loop defined by the two adjacent features of Cys residues, SS bonds, sequons, N-glycans, and TMs in every protein was also obtained. The distributions of the frequency, density, and length of gaps/loops in the assessed features among the entire human proteome were analyzed.

Functional analysis.The corresponding functions in the discovered unique protein groups obtained from the above analyses were examined using DAVID, the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery27. Fisher’s exact test was applied for enrichment analyses of Interpro protein domains and Gene Ontologies, including “biological processes”, “cellular localization”, and “molecular functions”. Specifically, Fisher’s Exact p values were computed by summing probabilities p over a 2 × 2 contingency table including the target proteins (n) and total proteins (N) in our identified protein group and those provided from the abovementioned reference databases (d, D) (Prob ).

The statistical significance was defined as a p value below the cutoff of 0.01. Both the Bonferroni Šidák p value28and Benjamini and Hochberg linear step-up adjusted p value29 were also considered in addition to the Fisher Exact p value.

Clustering of high-C proteins by CC gap frequency. Clustering of human proteins based on the CC gaps defined by adjacent Cys residues was carried out to identify proteins with special structures defined by Cys residues, such as cystine knots, nesting/parallel cystines, Cys-metal clusters, and interchain SS networks. In detail, gaps in adjacent Cys residues in every protein were obtained and sorted (https://github.com/sfu-sun-lab/Cysteine-Analysis-in-Human-Proteins). The frequency of each gap was subsequently used as the classifier for analysis via Multiple Experiment Viewer (MeV)30. First, average linkage of Pearson correlation was used as the distance for hierarchical clustering (HCL) of the gap frequency matrix.

Both the CC gaps (x) and protein species (y) were optimized to construct hierarchical trees. To effectively decipher the complex Cys residue patterns, only proteins with more than 15 Cys residues (4565 entries, in open bar protein bins in Fig. 2 A) were used for the analysis, which was approximately 25% of the total human proteome. The evaluated CC gaps were up to 100. Principal component analysis (PCA) was also carried out to reduce the dimensionality of the considered CC gap frequencies.

Results and discussion

Cys frequency. The presence of Cys residues in human proteins is common, even though Cys is the least utilized amino acid in the human proteome. Among the 20,411 annotated human proteins from UniProt, only 615 (3.0%) did not carry any Cys residues. The length distributions of the Cys-containing proteins (C-proteins) and Cys-free (C-free) proteins are summarized in Fig. 1 A and B. Clearly, C-free proteins are shorter overall. The mean lengths of the two groups are marked in Fig. 1. Specifically, the average length of C-free proteins was 163.1, ~ 3.5-fold less than the average length of Cys-containing proteins (570.8). This length dependence is consistent with what we observed in the sequon frequency in human proteins16.

Protein length distribution of cysteine-containing proteins (A) and cysteine-free proteins (B) in the human proteome, as well as (C) the UniProt keyword enrichment analysis of the cysteine-free proteins, in which the x axis displays the -log10 transformed enrichment p value.

Cys-free proteins. Supplementary Table S1 summarizes all the human C-free proteins. It was interesting to examine why these proteins had excluded Cys from their sequences. We asked whether these proteins were too short or functionally constrained. To address this question, we analyzed the fragment length distribution in C-proteins (i.e., the average length of a protein containing one Cys residue). Figure 2A summarizes the results, in which the fragment length peaks between 30 and 40 residues and the average fragment length is 64.6. Compared with C-free proteins with an average length of 163.1, the average fragment length/Cys was more than one-fold shorter. The length constraint was unlikely to be the dominant factor for most C-free proteins.

We then studied the enriched functions in this protein group (summarized in Table S1). The enriched proteins included histones, ribosomal proteins, and mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes. The enriched PTMs of these proteins included hydroxylation, citrullination, acetylation, methylation, ribosylation, ubiquitylation, and phosphorylation as shown in Fig. 1C. For example, histones are known to undergo abundant histone hydroxylation31and citrullination32,33, as well as phosphorylation, acetylation, methylation, ubiquitylation, and ribosylation34. Because Cys is considered “order promoting”, it is not surprising that C-free proteins include histones and ribosomal proteins, which are known to host intrinsically disordered regions35,36for versatile DNA/RNA binding and regulation. The enriched PTMs in C-free proteins are also similar to those found in intrinsically disordered proteins37.

The only Cys residue in histones is in Histone 3. Nuclear glutathione is known to modify this Cys residue and cause nucleosome instability38. Interestingly, Cys residues can bind to the aromatic ring of uracil39and are close to the deoxyribose moiety to easily form crosslinking bonds under UV and regular light40, which might also explain why histones are devoid of Cys residues. The absence of Cys in histones has led to the frequent use of engineered Cys in histones for tagging purposes41.

In addition to histones, ribosomal proteins and mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes, particularly complexes I and V, are also Cys free. The scarcity of Cys residues in ribosomal proteins enabled the utility of Cys incorporation in ribosomal proteins as a marker for studying mis-translation rates42. The mitochondrial respiratory chain is responsible for oxidative phosphorylation. The presence of Cys residues that are sensitive to redox potentials, if not well regulated, jeopardizes protein function. The absence of Cys in these complexes has been reported, particularly in aerobic species, compared with anaerobic species43, suggesting functional elimination. Interestingly, a link was discovered between the absence of Cys in respiratory complexes I and V and the longevity of the species44. Furthermore, Cys is the only amino acid that is sensitive to longevity in animals45.

Another identified protein family in the C-free group was the EF-hand motif, which contains calcium-binding proteins such as calmodulin-like proteins. EF-hand domains constitute two α-helices flanking a loop that binds to calcium. In the absence of Ca2+, calmodulin exhibits a pentagonal-bipyramidal geometry, and the two α-helices are intrinsically disordered46. Upon binding to four Ca ions with four EF-hand domains, calmodulin undergoes a series of allosteric conformational changes, which result in interactions with more than 300 target proteins and peptides for Ca signaling46. The presence of Cys promotes the formation of intrachain SS bonds and increases the rigidity of the entire molecule, as exemplified by troponin C, another EF-hand calcium binding protein that shares remarkable structural similarity with calmodulin47. Introduction of such an SS bond to calmodulin decreased its affinity for calcium ions and abolished its regulatory effect on downstream effector proteins47. The SS bond in troponin C constrained its conformation and increased its desired selectivity for troponin I over other proteins.

Collectively, our analysis of C-free protein groups suggested that the absence of Cys residues in these proteins is functionally favored but not constrained by length.

C-containing proteins. The distribution of Cys in the C proteins was examined, as shown in Fig. 2B. The overall average number of Cys residues per protein was 13.2. Low C-count proteins (open bars in Fig. 2B) dominated the distribution accounting for 70% of the total proteome (Fig. 2B pie chart insert), because the number of high Cys proteins (solid bars in Fig. 2B) decreased sharply. When expanding the first three low C-count bins (< 15 Cys residues/protein), as shown in Fig. 2D, we did not observe the previously reported difference between even and odd numbers of Cys residues in proteins3. Our results in Fig. 2D show small and random fluctuations among proteins carrying even and odd numbers of Cys residues, and the distribution peaked at approximately 3–6 Cys residues/protein. Early work by Petersen et al. revealed that even Cys numbers were dominant, likely because of their small sample size, i.e., 131 nonhomologous single-chain proteins with 3D structural information3.

The frequency analysis revealed several interesting Cys-rich (C-rich) proteins, which are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. We identified more than one hundred proteins carrying more than 100 Cys residues. The functional and structural enrichment analyses shown in Table S2 revealed extracellular matrix proteins with EGF-like domains. The average number of Cys residues per protein was 171.2, which was ~ 12-fold greater than the overall average (13.2) of the human proteins obtained above. These rich Cys residues can support the formation of complex SS bonds, such as cystine knots in EGF-like domains. Indeed, the average number of annotated SS bonds per C-rich protein was 42.6, which was ~ 46-fold greater than the average (0.90) in the whole human proteome.

The protein with the highest number of Cys residues was SCO-spondin (UniProt accession: A2VEC9, NCBI gene ID: 23145, gene symbol: SSPOP), a secreted matricellular protein that functions in neuronal development48. SCO-spondin has 563 Cys residues (Table S2), which account for 10.9% of its total amino acids and are ~ 4.5-fold greater than the global C percentage of 2.5% in our dataset. SCO-spondin has a complex structure and possesses multiple C-rich domains, including the Emilin (EMI) and EGF-like domains49. The second most common C-rich protein was titin. Titin is not an extracellular matrix protein but is the longest protein in the human proteome. The identification of titin supported the correlation of protein length with Cys frequency. In addition to Cys, titin also carries the highest number of sequons in the human proteome, as we discovered previously for a similar reason16.

Not all Cys residues form SS bonds. Free Cys in proteins is often unstable and functions as an active site. We analyzed 357 SS-free Cys residues in SCO-spondin. Interestingly, these SS-free Cys residues were clustered instead of evenly spreading across the protein, as shown in Fig. 3. In total, 19 free-Cys clusters were identified and are labeled by the red box in Fig. 3. In these clusters, Cys residues positioned more densely (fragment length/Cys: 6.9) than in the SS bonds (fragment length/Cys: 30.1). This odd localization was likely attributed to the incomplete SS annotation in UniProt. Many of these clusters were annotated in Interpro and Pfam as SS-rich domains, such as the trypsin-inhibitor-like domain (IPR002919 and PF01826), which has 5 SS bonds. Therefore, the SS annotation in UniProt is incomplete. Currently, the most authentic determination of SS bonds is based on the distance measured in the crystal structure of proteins. However, the structure of SCO-spondin is unknown, and some of the apparent free Cys residues in SCO-spondin may form interchain SS bonds during protein oligomerization49.

Interpro-annotated SCO-spondin domains are shown, and the red boxes highlight the regions containing free cysteine residues. The length of each highlighted region is labeled above the corresponding box. Only 2 out of 357 free cysteine residues are outside of these boxed regions.

Because the presence of Cys residues in proteins is length dependent, we analyzed the average length of the C-rich proteins. Compared to the global average of 558.5, the average length of C-proteins was 570.8 (Fig. 1), and that of C-rich proteins was 3522.2. These long lengths suggest that C-rich proteins typically carry multiple domains, as the average domain length is approximately 100 amino acids50,51. In addition, length analysis suggested that C-rich proteins pack Cys more densely than average proteins.

Cys density. To further evaluate Cys density in the whole human proteome, we computed the fragment length/Cys for all C-proteins. The distribution of C-fragment lengths for all proteins is shown in Fig. 2A, with a bin width of 10 residues. The most abundant length bin was 30–40 residues, and the average C-fragment length was 64.7. Over 12,800 proteins, which was more than half (65%) of the human proteins, had an average C-fragment length < 60 residues (open bar in Fig. 2A). Close to 90% of the proteins with more than 15 Cys residues (open bar in Fig. 2B) were in this fraction. These results suggested that Cys residues in C-dense proteins tend to aggregate.

The proteins with the most densely packed Cys (C-dense proteins, Supplementary Table S3), to our surprise, were not secreted proteins. The most densely packed human proteins belonged to Keratin associated proteins (KAPs). In fact, 105 proteins had Cys fragment lengths less than 5.8 residues, and none of them were secreted proteins but KAPs, Metallothioneins (MTs), and Late cornified envelope proteins (LCEs). These C-dense proteins possessed some unique properties compared with C-rich proteins. First, their lengths were much shorter. The average length of C-dense proteins was only 152.2, almost 3-fold shorter than the average human protein length (558.5) and over 40-fold shorter than that of C-rich proteins (3522.2). This short average length is close to the reported average domain size, suggesting that these proteins have simple domains.

The average number of Cys residues carried by C-dense proteins was 43.3, which placed the entire group in the 9th bin in Fig. 2B; this value was much higher than the average value of 13.2 Cys residues/protein. With more than 40-fold shorter lengths than the C-rich proteins, the average Cys percentage in these C-dense proteins was 28.6%, which was one-fold higher than that in the C-rich proteins (11.4%) and over 10-fold higher than the average global average (2.5%) of the human proteome.

Given such dense Cys packing, the number of annotated intrachain SS bonds was “0”, as shown in Table S3. Instead, these Cys residues form interchain SS bonds52or Cys-metal clusters53. The exclusion of intrachain SS bonds in these proteins was tightly controlled by their cellular localization and stimulated expression. It is also possible that steric hindrance in dense Cys packing precludes the formation of intrachain SS bonds.

To further investigate how Cys was positioned in C-dense proteins, we examined their CC gaps, as shown in Fig. 4 and Table S3. The most C-dense protein was Small cys and glycine repeat-containing protein 5, or Keratin-associated protein 28 − 5 (UniProt accession A0A286YF46). The length of the protein was only 85 residues, but it contained 35 Cys residues, with C% as high as 41.2%. Figure 4A shows the distribution of CC gaps in the protein, and its computationally predicted structure is shown in Fig. 4B. The dominant gap was the vicinity C-C arrangement, accounting for 38% of all CC gaps in the protein. To verify the ubiquity of such vicinity arrangement, we further studied Metallothionein-1B, the most C-dense MTs, and the Late cornified envelope protein 2 A (i.e. Late envelope protein 9), the most C-dense LCEs. Metallothionein-1B had 61 residues, 34.4% of which were Cys, and the CC gap distribution is shown in Fig. 4C with the computationally predicted structure shown in Fig. 4D. Late envelope protein 9 had 106 residues and 23 Cys residues (C% is 21.7%), and its CC gap distribution is summarized in Fig. 4E with its predicted structure in Fig. 4F. In comparison, we also analyzed the CC gap distribution in SCO-spondin, the most C-rich protein, as shown in Fig. 4G, and the predicted structure is shown in Fig. 4H. As expected, all C-dense proteins were predicted to be unstructured, which can provide the necessary flexibility to form interchain SS and metal-thiolate bonds. This unstructured nature of C-dense proteins without the “order promoting” Cys involved bonding echoed previous studies on the “tricky” positions of Cys in the boundaries of the order and disordered regions of proteins12.

Distribution of CC gap frequency and the corresponding predicted structures for selected C-dense and C-rich proteins. A & B, Keratin-associated protein 28 − 5 (KAP); C & D, Metallothionein-1B (MT); E & F, Late cornified envelope protein 2 A (LCE); G & H, SCO-spondin.

In Fig. 4, the vicinity C-C arrangement is abundant only in C-dense proteins (KAP, MT and LCE, 20–38%) but not in the C-rich protein (SCO-spondin, 0.9%). The rarity of C-C arrangement in proteins rich of intrachain SS bonds is likely because vicinal disulfides are challenging to form54, and their presence increases the likelihood of SS bond reshuffling55. As a result, they might be eliminated through evolution. Among the C-dense proteins, the vicinity C-C arrangement was the most abundant in only KAPs (38%) and LCEs (32%). Among the MTs, even though C-C was abundant (20%), the most abundant CC gap was the C-X-C arrangement (35%). Previous studies have shown that for vicinity Cys residues, the two thiol groups typically point away from each other56and bind to different metal ions54. In KAPs and LCEs, which are abundant in cornified cells, the trans-orientation of dense thiols can ensure the robust formation of interchain as opposed to intrachain disulfides for protection barriers57. Because of the CC gap differences among the different protein types shown in Fig. 4, we asked whether it is possible to use CC gaps as a classifier to distinguish different protein groups.

Analysis of CC and SS gaps and SS loops. Due to the close relationship between CC gaps and SS bonds, we analyzed CC gaps as well as SS gaps and SS loops. The python code used for such analysis can be found in the repository of GitHub (https://github.com/sfu-sun-lab/Cysteine-Analysis-in-Human-Proteins). Previous studies have revealed with limited (> 50) protein structures that SS bridges tend to form at short distances58, favoring kinetic folding. We reanalyzed this value with 3580 human protein entries that carried 17,819 intrachain SS bridges, and we computed the length between two Cys residues in one SS bond and named it the “SS loop length” (Fig. 5A). The average loop length was 36.6, ranging from the minimum loop length of “1”, the vicinity C-C arrangement, to the maximum loop length of “1038”, belonging to a single SS-bond protein, Dual oxidase 2 (UniProt accession: Q9NRD8, and official gene symbol: DUOX2). Excitingly, our results matched the previous discovery made by Thornton, T. J58.. Thornton, T. J. discovered that 49% of SS bonds had a loop length less than 24, while our data yielded 55%. The most frequent loop length from the Thornton dataset was between 10 and 14 residues, and ours was between 9 and 15 residues. The remarkable consistency with drastically different sizes in the datasets suggested that even though the annotation of SS bonds in UniProt was incomplete, this did not prevent the derivation of insightful information.

We extended the analysis of SS loops to gaps between adjacent SS bridges, as shown in Fig. 5B and C. Interestingly, the absolute SS gap length distribution shown in Fig. 5C is similar to that of the SS loop length, except that the SS gaps can be situated inside an existing SS bond (non-consecutive SS bonds); in such cases, our gap analysis produced a negative value, as shown in Fig. 5B. Negative gaps were produced when the two adjacent SS bonds partially overlapped, as was the case for cystine knots and nesting SS bonds, in which the two adjacent SS bonds had one nesting inside the other. Among the 14,223 total gaps we obtained, 43.4% were negative. The prevalence of negative gaps suggested the complexity of the SS bonds present in most proteins. Surprisingly, the distribution of absolute SS-gap lengths (Fig. 5C) peaked at a shorter length (< 10) than did the distribution of SS-loop lengths (> 10, Fig. 5A). The dominant short lengths among SS loops and gaps as well as the abundance of negative SS gaps suggested local clustering of SS bonds.

We asked whether Cys residues were also positioned as clusters in proteins, as we discovered in SCO-spondin (Fig. 3). We analyzed the gaps between adjacent Cys residues, as summarized in Fig. 5D. As anticipated, the distribution of gap lengths between adjacent Cys residues (CC gap) was similar to the distributions of SS loops and gaps. The average CC gap length was 36.6 residues, which was comparable to the average length of SS loops and slightly shorter than the absolute length of SS gaps. In addition, the frequency of CC gaps in a given protein was approximately 10-fold greater than that of loops and gaps in SS bonds. This outcome may be attributed to the combination of under-annotated SS bonds in current human proteins and to the redundancy in protein sequences, which ensures robust PTMs.

As shown in Fig. 5D, two special CC gaps (i.e., C-X-X-C and C-X24-C) were surprisingly populated from an otherwise smooth decay in the distribution curve. Proteins carrying these two CC gaps are summarized in Supplementary Table S4. Using DAVID, we analyzed the enriched Gene Ontologies and InterPro domains among proteins harboring either of the two CC gaps. Surprisingly, both sets of proteins were highly enriched in zinc-finger C2-H2 domains (IPR013087), with Fisher exact p values and Bonferroni and Benjamini values of “0”. The exceptionally high frequency of this protein fold suggested the large size of this protein family as well as the high repeats of this fold in proteins. As a result, 25% of the proteins (704 entries) with a C-X24-C gap and 14% of the proteins (794 entries) with a C-X-X-C gap carried these Cys arrangements. Excluding this particular zinc finger domain, the two datasets were also uniquely enriched in other protein groups, as summarized in Table S4. In particular, the C-X-X-C gap proteins were also enriched in other zinc finger domains, such as the RING domain (IPR013083) and the C1-like domain in protein kinases (IPR046349), whereas the C-X24-C gap proteins were uniquely enriched in GPCR proteins, such as olfactory receptors (IPR000725) and the rhodopsin-like 7 transmembrane folds (IPR017452). Again, these domains either have high repeats or highly expanded protein families that together contributed to the discovered high frequency of these two particular CC gaps.

Clustering of Cys residues, the rare amino acid found in protein sequences, has been discussed in the past; however, our studies are unique. Early studies were limited to 2-member Cys clusters in protein sequences for interchain SS bonds59,60. Mario et al. used a distance of less than 9 Å to isolate SS bonds and Cys-metal complexes10. Our analysis is distinct from previous studies because our analysis does not rely on crystal structures and includes larger distances with multiple residues. The use of CC gaps from protein sequences eliminated not only the reliance on structural information but also the reliance on SS annotation, which allowed us to systematically examine unbiased information from the entire proteome. In the past, CC gaps were obtained as a single parameter for all the studied proteins, with a particular focus on Cys-metal interactions61. Our studies examined the CC gap frequency of every protein, which allowed us to obtain a matrix of CC gap frequencies.

Clustering of high-C proteins. Using the CC gap frequency, we attempted the unsupervised clustering of high-C proteins (the proteins in the open bars in Figs. 2B and B and 4565 entries), and the results are summarized in Fig. 6. Because most CC gaps were short in length, we only showed results from gap lengths up to 100 residues. Tight clusters were clearly visible, and functional enrichment analysis discovered many proteins with special domains and functions, such as the EGF-like domain (IPR000742), as highlighted by the white circle in the figure. According to the results obtained for 100 Gaps, most signals were condensed in the low-gap length region (< 10). We further reduced the number of CC gaps stepwise from 100 to 10, as shown in the figure, and used the EGF-like domain to benchmark the efficiency of clustering. When the number of CC gaps reduced from 100 to 50, the three clusters of EGF-like domains clasped into one tight cluster, and this cluster remained before the gap length was shorter than 15 as shown in Fig. 6A. The similarity of the circled clusters was compared against 25 gap scenario and is shown in blue in the pie chart on top of each heatmap. Higher than 90% similarity was observed, which suggested that CC gaps of 25–50 are suitable for clustering proteins with special functions supported by their Cys residues.

Principal component analysis (PCA) of CC gaps in the 25 gap scenario is shown in Fig. 6B, in which four clusters were visible. Cluster 1 is composed of Gaps 1 & 4, cluster 2 is composed by Gaps 3 & 25, cluster 3 is from Gaps 2, 5–7, and 9, and the remaining gaps form cluster 4. PCA of the proteins revealed four clusters, as shown in Fig. 6C, which matched the four clusters identified via PCA of the gap frequency, i.e., C-dense proteins (Gaps 1 and 4 high), zinc finger C2H2 proteins (Gaps 3 and 25 high), EFG-like domains (Gaps 2, 5–7, and 9 high), and the remaining proteins.

The successful clustering of unique protein families based on the frequency of CC gaps suggested that CC gaps and their corresponding frequencies can be used as quantitative structural features readily obtained from protein sequences to study the structures and functions of proteins.

Hidden relationships between Cys residues, sequons, and transmembrane (TM) domains. Having explored the unique arrangement of Cys residues in protein sequences and their contributions to protein structure and function, we further examined the relationships between Cys residues and other structural features that proteins can possess, specifically sequons and TMs. To do so, we analyzed the average fragment length of the sequon and TMs, as summarized in Fig. 7A and B, respectively. Interestingly, both Fig. 7A and B exhibited severe right-skewed distributions similar to the C-fragment length distribution shown in Fig. 2A, suggesting the existence of short clusters as well.

The height of the bar denotes the population percentage of the specified proteins relative to the total proteins analyzed. KAP, keratin-associated protein; Ig, immunoglobulin; FT III, fibronectin type III; LRR, leucine-rich repeat; MFS, major facilitator superfamily.

To verify these findings, we obtained the gap and loop distances between two adjacent sequons, N-glycans and TMs, respectively. The results are summarized in Fig. 7E and G. Indeed, similar to those of Cys residues, all the structural features we examined showed short gaps and/or loops for the majority of the obtained lengths. Specifically, for loops defined between two adjacent TMs, the length peaked at only 10 residues, and the average loop length was as short as 29.7 residues. Among the 15,992 loops we examined, 96% had a length shorter than 100 residues (domain length), and 60% had a loop length less than 20 residues (foldon length). For gaps between sequons, the length peaked at 7 residues, and the average length was 118.8 residues. For the 44,081 gaps we examined, 61% had a length shorter than 100 residues, and close to 20% of the total length was shorter than 20 residues. For gaps between two adjacent N-glycosylation sites reported in UniProt, the length also peaked at 7 residues, and the average length was 94.8 residues, which is shorter than the number of sequon gaps. Among the 11,529 N-glycan gaps we examined, 71% were shorter than 100 residues, and 25% were shorter than 20 residues. These values suggest that a closer sequon arrangement favors a greater rate of N-glycosylation.

To examine whether there was complementarity among Cys residues, sequons and TMs, as we previously discovered in the SS bonds and N-glycans of integral membrane proteins, we further analyzed the structural features of the selected bins shown in Figs. 2A and 7A and B around the distribution peak. The results are summarized in Figs. 2C and 7C and D, respectively. Among all three distributions, a decrease in fragment length was unanimously accompanied by an increase in the frequency of the target feature. Interestingly, these increases were also unanimously parallel to the decreases in the other two features and protein length. This complementary trend became clearer when the fragment length fell to the shorter side of the peak distribution in the distributions of Cys and sequons. In the TM distribution, where there was no peak and the highest frequency started from the first bin, the trend remained. The pattern we discovered clearly suggested that proteins use a short length space preferentially and densely pack only one particular structural feature among Cys residues, sequons, and TM domains. With limited protein length (shorter than average protein length), the increase in one particular structural feature is compensated for by the decrease in the other features. For the first two bins in all three distributions having the shortest fragment lengths, the complementarity of Cys residues, sequons, and TMs in protein sequences was explicitly exhibited.

For example, for the C-dense proteins in Table S3 and the 1st bin in Fig. 2A, the average number of sequons in KAPs, MTs, and LCEs was only 0.26, and no TMs were found. Further moving up the bins in Fig. 2A, we gradually discovered other protein domains. Supplementary Table S5 summarizes our analysis of the first 8 bins of proteins in Fig. 2A, including the protein list, their corresponding structural feature counts, and their functional enrichment analysis. Additional prominent protein domains/families enriched in these bins were zinc finger domains (bins 2 & 3), EGF-like domains (bin 2), peptidase folds (bins 2 & 3), GPCR families (bins 3 & 4), and Ig-like domains (bins 4 & 6).

Similar functional enrichment analyses were carried out for the first three bins of the TMs shown in Fig. 7B. These bins were enriched in ion channel, transporter, and GPCR proteins, as shown in Supplementary Table S7, which are known to have dense multipass TMs. For the first three bins of Fig. 7A, in which the sequon gaps were shorter, the most enriched Interpro domains were Ig-like domains in adhesion molecules such as CEACAM1 (UniProt accession: P13688) and LAMP proteins, as summarized in Supplementary Table S6.

The local clusters of these structural features are known to be critical to their functions and stability, and our analysis systematically identified them, as summarized in Fig. 7H and Supplementary Tables S5-S7. This success verified the potential of using the CC gap frequency as a structural feature to classify proteins of special structures and functions and confirmed the complementarity between Cys and other folding and structural features, such as sequons and TMs, in protein sequences. This discovery is supported by the experimentally identified the complementarity between TMs and N-glycans in membrane proteins62, and globally observed complementarity among TMs, N-glycans and SS bridges in integral membrane proteins2.

From a molecular mechanistic perspective, the close arrangement of protein sequences of the same structural features maximizes the efficiency and robustness of their formation and ensures future stability in a dynamic biological environment for key cellular functions. For example, in the formation of intrachain SS bonds, N-glycosylation, and TMs, enzymes of the oxidoreductase system, OST complex, and translocon complex, respectively, are needed2. Once these enzymes encounter suitable substrates, the close proximity of dense target sites ensures the highest utilization of the target sites and the maximum modification rate of the enzymes because these enzymatic systems all require direct contact for appropriate function. Certain redundancies can also be built in these repeats, and occasional site skipping may not be catastrophic for the fate of the protein or for cellular activity.

In addition, all of the abovementioned catalytic systems are located in the ER of the secretory pathway in mammalian cells2. The ER cisternae are crowded environments. Our previous review pinpointed complex relationships, including both synergy and competition, among these enzymatic reactions1,2. Our discovery of complementarity in these dense clusters in protein sequences (Fig. 6), i.e., enrichment of only one of the three structural features, can lead to an optimized arrangement through evolution to minimize potential competition among these coexisting enzymatic systems for undesired folding and PTMs. Collectively, the discovered local clusters are best suited for optimal modification and effective control of the misfolding rate.

Under nonenzymatic regulatory conditions, such as the formation of interchain SS bridges in KAPs and LEPs and the formation of Cys-metal clusters in MTs, in addition to the tight regulation of their expression and cellular localization to minimize misfolding, the special position of their Cys, i.e., the C-C arrangement in KAPs and LEPs and the C-X-C arrangement in MTs, favored the designated Cys bonds and minimized the formation of intrachain SS bonds.

Our discovered sequence patterns not only allowed us to better understand the positive feedback generated in the sequence-structure‒function axis in biological organisms by maximizing folding and modification efficiency but also revealed the inherent robustness of the resulting sequence-structure‒function axis by minimizing potential folding errors and future instability. Mounting evidence indicates that protein conformation is not static but rather dynamic63, so as are the protein‒protein interactions that directly control cellular homeostasis and physiology. In particular, both SS knots in EGF-like domains and peptidases, heavy N-glycosylation in Ig-like domains in adhesion molecules, and multipass TMs in GPCRs and transporters are known stable and protective structural features that can be maintained after protein maturation and trafficking to distant locations, such as being secreted into the extracellular milieu.

The mechanism through which the structures and functions of individual proteins, the building blocks, of a cell behave to maintain its robustness and dynamics throughout its life cycle profoundly impacts cellular activities. We believe that we discovered a likely general modality, i.e., local, mutually exclusive, and dense clusters of structural features in protein sequences for critical stability and functionality. The sizes of these tight clusters are consistent with the sizes of the foldons and domains, which are units in the kinetically controlled and nucleated folding path of regular proteins toward the complete folding conformation with tertiary and quaternary structures63.

Conclusions

Our systematic investigation of the positions of Cys residues allowed us to discover a series of special protein families, including C-free (histones, ribosomes, respiratory chain complexes and EF-hand domains), C-rich (EFG-like domains), and C-dense (KAPs, MTs, and LCEs) proteins, as shown in Supplementary Tables S1-S3. This allowed us to confirm the correlation of protein length with the occurrence of Cys residues, which has also been previously observed in SS bonds2and sequons16. Segregated Cys clusters were observed when we analyzed the free Cys in the most C-rich protein, SCO-spondin (Fig. 3). The investigation of CC gaps among C-dense and C-rich proteins (Fig. 5) revealed the potential of classifying proteins with unique functions based on their frequency of CC gaps (Supplementary Table S4), which was successfully verified in Fig. 6. These observations support the hypothesis that Cys is a structural feature and that its gap frequency (gaps of 25–50) in a protein can be used for structural and functional analyses.

Further investigation of the average fragment length per structural features of Cys (Fig. 2A), sequons, and TMs (Fig. 7), discovered a general self-clustering and mutually exclusive modality of all the investigated structural features for robust stability and function (Supplementary Tables S5-S7). The revealed short gaps among structural features coincide with the average sizes of the foldons (20 residues) and domains (100 residues) observed in nucleated protein folding63, supporting the participation of these structural features in initial foldon folding and the stabilization of the final protein conformation.

This evolutionarily selected modality under linear polypeptide constraints possesses several advantages: (1) maximized folding and modification efficiency via enhanced interaction with catalytic enzyme complexes, (2) increased folding and modification robustness by inherent redundancy, (3) the lowest folding errors achieved by minimizing competing folding and modifications, and (4) optimized the stability and protecting role in protein life cycle after structural maturation.

The discovery of such a likely general modality in protein sequences is critical in protein design and engineering to cope with the dynamic nature of protein structure and function for pharmaceutical applications and for synthetic biology. Many current protein engineering efforts have validated the discovered viewpoints. For example, to regulate the stability and/or solubility of biologics, engineering cystine-knot like scaffolds64,65, glycan adducts66,67, and keratin folds68,69into pharmaceuticals and biopolymers has been actively pursued with fruitful outcomes. Recently, the design of programmable TM receptors70,71 has also excelled in the biomedical fields for targeted drug delivery and immunotherapy. Current studies can advance these synthetic biological efforts by emphasizing the local density and mutual exclusion of structural features in their design principles.
