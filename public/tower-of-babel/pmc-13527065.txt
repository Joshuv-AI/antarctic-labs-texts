Antibiotic development and a path to access in India
Authors: Abi Manesh, Kamini Walia, Taslimarif Saiyed, Balaji Veeraraghavan, David L. Paterson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antibiotic development has historically been concentrated in high-income countries. We discuss how India is emerging as an important centre for antimicrobial development, and the implications for global health and equity of access to life-saving therapies.

The world’s most populous country is India, and without doubt the impact of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is experienced by more individuals in India than any other country. The “Critical Priority” Pathogens described by the World Health Organization (WHO) are carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB), carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) and third-generation cephalosporin-resistant Enterobacterales (3GCR-E)1. With the exception of 3GCR-E, where the treatments of choice, carbapenems, are widely available, antibiotic options for the WHO “Critical Priority” Pathogens have been extremely limited in lower-income countries across Asia. This has led to a reliance on the polymyxins, a drug class that is impaired by high rates of nephrotoxicity.

Additionally, the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR) -led national surveillance has increasingly highlighted that the resistance landscape in India is not merely an extension of broader regional and global trends but is being shaped by distinct and rapidly evolving mechanisms, including diverse NDM and OXA carbapenemases, porin loss with efflux upregulation, and emerging resistance to newer β-lactam–β-lactamase inhibitor combinations2. Data from the ICMR Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance Network reveal high burdens of carbapenem resistance across Enterobacterales, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, often reflecting multi-mechanistic resistance profiles underrepresented in high-income settings3. These realities underscore the need for antibiotic discovery in India, guided by local surveillance data, to ensure new agents are effective for Indian patients and high-resistance environments.

Historically, antibiotic discovery and development for less toxic alternatives to polymyxins has occurred via companies based in the United States, Europe, and Japan. Examples of such antibiotics have included ceftazidime-avibactam, ceftolozane-tazobactam, meropenem-vaborbactam, imipenem-cilastatin-relebactam, cefiderocol, and sulbactam-durlobactam. Each of these antibiotics was first approved globally by the United States Food and Drug Administration (US FDA) and initially marketed in the United States. Another new antibiotic, aztreonam-avibactam, received its initial approval in Europe but was quickly followed by US FDA approval.

There is no doubt that antibiotics were initially launched in the United States market for commercial reasons – historically, the bulk of drug sales has been in the United States, followed by Europe and Japan. However, sales of new antibiotics in these territories have been limited, given comparatively low rates of CRAB and CRE and the presence of strong antibiotic stewardship programs.

Since the bulk of CRAB and CRE occurs in Asia, we believe that the societal benefits of new antibiotics targeting these and other MDR pathogens will be greatest in Asia. Access to new antibiotics broadly across middle- and lower-income countries in Asia is difficult because of price and complex regulatory requirements arising from non-harmonised drug approval processes. Yet, antibiotic discovery and development are active in Asia, particularly in India and China. It appears that Asian companies are targeting AMR issues relevant to their own countries and will first launch their antibiotics in these territories before marketing elsewhere. This is particularly notable in India, and this comment focuses on molecules in the Indian antibiotic pipeline, specifically in the late pre-clinical and clinical phases, which may become available shortly. A detailed review on molecules at all stages of development and the factors catalysing it are beyond the scope of this paper, and we point the readers to works which cover that comprehensively4,5.

Antibiotic development in India

India has long been home to many of the world’s largest generic drug manufacturers. For many years these companies have also produced novel combinations of existing antibiotics. Unfortunately, these combinations have often lacked rigorous evaluation of pharmacokinetics and of clinical effectiveness. Many recent successful exceptions include the combination of cefepime and enmetazobactam, cefepime and zidebactam, and cefepime and high-dose tazobactam (WCK 4282), all of which have undergone robust evaluation as per international regulatory standards.

Discovery and development of new antimicrobial agents in India has been by generic manufacturers seeking to invest in research and development, “spin-offs” from Indian-based development programs of global companies, and smaller “start-up” companies. The cost of pre-clinical and clinical development of antibiotics in India is far lower than in the United States and Europe which has facilitated these activities. Additionally, early-stage funding and infrastructure support for antibiotic discovery and early development are provided by C-CAMP, the Center for Cellular and Molecular Platforms (C-CAMP), a bio-innovation ecosystem supported by the Government of India. Indian companies have also been the recipient of support from CARB-X which is the world’s largest public private partnership to fund AMR solutions globally, and the Global Antibiotic Research and Development Partnership (GARDP) which is a non-profit organisation created by WHO and DNDi to tackle antibiotic resistance.

Antibiotics developed and recently approved in India

Levonadifloxacin and ala-levonadifloxacin

Levonadifloxacin is the L-arginine salt of a novel benzoquinolizine fluoroquinolone with preferential targeting of DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV in gram-positive bacteria, specifically excellent MRSA activity (including quinolone-resistant)6. The oral prodrug, ala-levonadifloxacin achieves excellent oral bioavailability, mirroring the pharmacokinetic profile of the intravenous levonadifloxacin. A phase 3, open-label trial comparing levonadifloxacin IV with intravenous linezolid and oral ala-levonadifloxacin with oral linezolid showed clinical cure rates of 91.0% versus 87.8% (difference 3.2%, 95% CI −4.5 to 10.9) among 500 patients with acute bacterial skin and skin structure infections (ABSSSI). Approximately 30% of the participants had MRSA infection7. The drug was approved in India by the Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO) in 2020, indications granted are ABSSSI, diabetic foot infection and concurrent blood stream infections and is the first novel antibiotic for MRSA from India. Oral ala-levonadifloxacin offers a blend of bactericidal action, broader spectrum activity, excellent tolerability and thus is valuable for recalcitrant MRSA infections requiring longer duration treatment.

Cefepime-enmetazobactam

Enmetazobactam is a novel penicillanic acid sulphone β-lactamase inhibitor, similar to the structure of tazobactam, restoring the activity of cefepime against extended spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs). It has been evaluated in combination with cefepime in a pivotal Phase III clinical trial for treatment of complicated urinary tract infection (cUTI)8. The ALLIUM trial included 1034 patients with cUTI with piperacillin-tazobactam as a comparator. Cefepime-enmetazobactam showed superiority with clinical cure and microbiological eradication, the primary outcome (79.1% versus 58.9%, difference: 21.2% [95% CI, 14.3% to 27.9%]). Cefepime-enmetazobactam is the first invented-in-India antimicrobial that was first approved for use in the US (Feb 2024), and subsequently in Europe, the UK, and India.

Nafithromycin

Nafithromycin is a novel, semisynthetic lactone ketolide, derivative of erythromycin A. Its unique 2-pyridine-1,3,4-thiadiazole biaryl side chain allows it to bypass the common macrolide resistance mechanisms in S. pneumoniae, including erm B-mediated ribosomal modifications and mef(A/E)-mediated efflux pumps9. Beyond macrolide resistant S. pneumoniae, it shows activity against both typical and atypical respiratory pathogens like Moraxella catarrhalis, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophila, Hemophilus influenzae, Group A Streptococci and Staphylococcus aureus, positioning it as an agent for short course therapy for community acquired pneumonia. This is enhanced by its high alveolar macrophage concentrations enabling once daily dosing10. A recent double-blind, phase 3 trial across 31 centres in India, 3 day once daily course of 800 mg Nafithromycin showed non-inferiority to oral moxifloxacin for 7 days11, with early clinical response rates of 91.3% versus 89.0% at Day 4. Nafithromycin has been granted Breakthrough Medicines designation by the Saudi Arabia FDA. The molecule received approval by India’s CDSCO in January 2025. It has not yet been approved in other countries.

Antibiotics currently undergoing clinical development

Cefepime-Zidebactam

Zidebactam is a diazabicyclooctane molecule which provides antimicrobial activity via both beta-lactamase inhibition and antibiotic activity via potent binding to PBP-2. Zidebactam, which uniformly binds to PBP2 of Enterobacterales and non-fermentative gram-negative pathogens, lends a novel mechanism of β-lactam enhancer to this combination, which is highly active against MBL and OXA carbapenemases producing pathogens. This is an attribute of the β-lactam enhancer mechanism since zidebactam is not an inhibitor of MBL and OXA carbapenemases. The phase 3 trial comparing cefepime-zidebactam to meropenem for the treatment of cUTI or acute pyelonephritis has been completed (NCT04979806). Another phase 2 study conducted across 15 centres in India among patients with confirmed carbapenem resistant infections has also been completed, although results are not yet available. Cefepime-zidebactam has also been provided for compassionate use for a variety of critically ill patients with generally positive results12–15. The Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) has recently endorsed an investigational breakpoint of 64 mg/L for all the major Gram-negative organisms. Cefepime zidebactam received FDA and CDSCO approval in May 202616.

Ertapenem-zidebactam

A once-daily agent, ertapenem- zidebactam (WCK 6777) is also in phase 2/3 trials, with US FDA Fast Track designation. Earlier, the United States National Institutes of Health completed a Phase 1 study with WCK 6777 in the US, which evaluated the safety and tolerability of WCK 6777 doses up to 3g ertapenem with 3 g zidebactam. SENTRY surveillance data shows that ertapenem-zidebactam is consistently active against carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales, including MBLs17. By virtue of its once-daily dosing, ertapenem–zidebactam offers a valuable Outpatient Parenteral Antimicrobial Therapy (OPAT) option for patients in high-resistance settings or for those hospitalized solely for IV antibiotic administration.

High dose cefepime-tazobactam (WCK 4282)

This combination is being developed as a carbapenem sparing ESBL-active antibiotic and recently completed Phase 3 evaluation (NCT03630081). Its results are yet to be published. WCK 4282 has been shown to possess comprehensive activity against piperacillin/tazobactam and cefoperazone/sulbactam-resistant ESBL-producing Enterobacterales and P. aeruginosa18.

BWC0977

BWC0977 is a novel dual-target topoisomerase inhibitor, with strong inhibition against DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, at a site different from the fluoroquinolones. It has broad spectrum activity against carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales, A. baumannii and P. aeruginosa with MIC90 values from 0.03–2 µg/mL19. It is also active against ciprofloxacin-resistant Enterobacterales. Phase I studies (NCT05088421) demonstrated safety and tolerability in healthy volunteers20. Both intravenous and oral formulations are in development, supported by a collaboration between CARB-X and GARDP.

Antibiotics in advanced pre-clinical development

Klebicins

Klebicins are naturally occurring with potent bactericidal activity against MDR Klebsiella pneumoniae21. Gangagen is developing a recombinant precision version of Klebicins, leveraging their proprietary bioinformatics-driven platform, to combat carbapenem resistant K. pneumoniae causing hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP). Potential advantages with this approach include microbiome preserving effect, provision of a unique antibiotic class with less cross-resistance and species-specific targeting aiding antimicrobial stewardship activities. Currently, the molecule is in preclinical development, supported by CARB-X funding.

VRP-034 - Novel formulation of Polymyxin B

Polymyxins are commonly used agents for carbapenem-resistant infections but are associated with significant nephrotoxicity. Polymyxins are actively taken into the proximal tubular cells via megalin and cubulin receptors, triggering excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) and subsequently, cell death22. Several historical attempts have sought to modify the polymyxin structure to reduce nephrotoxicity. Venus Remedies developed VRP-034, a novel, supramolecular cationic formulation of polymyxin B, with promising preclinical results supporting its potential to reduce nephrotoxicity23.

VRP-034 primarily acts by reducing the oxidative stress within renal proximal tubular cells. The US FDA has granted Qualified Infectious Disease Product (QIDP) designation to VRP-034 for potential in treating blood stream infections.

In addition to polymyxin B, Venus Remedies has partnered with the British company, Infex Therapeutics, to develop MET-X, a novel broad-spectrum metallo-beta-lactamase inhibitor, aimed at restoring the activity of carbapenems against gram-negative pathogens.

Strengthening India’s antibiotic development ecosystem

India’s emerging position as an important centre for antibiotic development is supported by several enablers, but realising its full potential requires more coordinated support across the development pathway. Early phase development is supported by C-CAMP, Biotechnology Industry Research Assistance Council (BIRAC), and others, with many of the above-mentioned molecules receiving substantial grant support. Traditionally, India’s antibiotic development ecosystem heavily focuses on optimisation of existing antibiotic classes. First-in-class innovations emerging from basic science research in academic centres and their linkage to commercial development ecosystems need further encouragement and funding. BIRAC, BioE3 policy and Biotechnology Industry Partnership Programme (BIPP) are set up in India to address these translation gaps between academia and product development. Several global not-for-profit organizations like CARB-X and GARDP also support innovative solutions for AMR in India.

The first-in-human gap, characterised by lack of expertise in phase 1 trials is being addressed by ICMR by the establishment of 4 academic phase 1 trial centres across India in May 202424. For later phase trials, the ICMR-INTENT network provides a multistate network, capable of hosting phase 2 and phase 3 trials25. However, utilisation of the network for regulatory AMR trials is rare. The network is mainly used for trials involving drugs in the post-marketing phase.

India needs a dedicated, autonomous, public-private partnered clinical trial ecosystem for AMR research that can conduct regulatory-grade trials. Such a platform should bring together expertise in advanced clinical trial design, data management, pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic modelling, health economics, and regulatory affairs. It should be capable of advancing early-stage molecules supported by C-CAMP and others in collaboration with pharmaceutical companies, while being seamlessly integrated into domestic and global clinical trial networks26. This would accelerate development and consolidate India’s substantial but underutilised clinical trial capacity.

On the regulatory side, the New Drugs and Clinical Trials rule of 2019 offers 30-working day deemed approval provision for domestically developed agents and a recent amendment in January 2026 waives the test license requirement for non-commercial drug manufacture, saving up to 90 days27. However, the current regulatory process lacks a QIDP-like fast-tracked pathway, specifically for antibiotics addressing AMR. Finally, there are no domestic pull incentives in India; no LMIC has yet established a market entry reward (MER) pathway or subscription model28. India, with its AMR burden and emerging pipeline, is uniquely positioned to pioneer such a model.

Conclusions

There are substantial concerns around how access to antibiotics can be obtained by the patients in low- and middle-income countries who most need them. India is one of the few countries that have the potential to both develop new antibiotics and rapidly provide access to them via its own commercial market. Unfortunately, bacterial resistance to antibiotics continues to evolve, so it can never be assumed that a single antibiotic will represent a long-term solution to the problem of AMR. For this reason, a sustainable pipeline of innovative antibiotics, guided by evidence, is necessary. A public private partnered clinical trial ecosystem with diverse expertise is needed to consolidate the gains India has made in antibiotic pipeline development.
