Molecular navigation strategy enables fibrillar self-assembly for efficient as-cast organic solar cells
Authors: Hanjian Lai, Haoran Hu, Yuhui Qin, Xinyu Pu, Huanyan Jiang, Hongxiang Li, Yufei Wang, Chao Li, Yuang Fu, Xianyong Zhou, Jinbo Chen, Sha Liu, Chunguang Zhu, Qifa Zheng, Xinhui Lu, Guangye Zhang, Tao Jia, Chang Liu, Zhenye Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFibrillar networks formed during solution processing play a key role in the high performance of modern organic solar cells (OSCs). Yet their formation is intrinsically non-equilibrium and largely stochastic, leading to discontinuous domains and mismatched donor/acceptor interfaces that limit charge transport. Here, we show that the evolution of fibrillar networks can be guided through molecular design. By designing a cycloalkoxy-functionalized acceptor, O6R-4F, we create a molecular navigator that preferentially localizes at donor/acceptor interfaces, suppresses excessive self-aggregation of L8-BO-C5 acceptor, and promotes coordinated donor/acceptor aggregation and crystallization during film formation. This results in finer, more interconnected fibrillar networks with improved phase separation. As-cast D18: L8-BO-C5: O6R-4F devices achieve a power conversion efficiency of 20.9% without post-deposition treatment. The same approach also improves morphology and performance in chemically distinct donor/acceptor systems, demonstrating that controlling intermolecular interactions provides a general strategy for directing fibrillar network formation in solution-processed organic semiconductors.Subject terms: Materials for devices, Self-assembly

Introduction

Self-assembly under non-equilibrium conditions governs the morphology of a wide range of functional organic materials1–4. In organic solar cells (OSCs), rapid solvent evaporation drives donor and acceptor semiconductors to organize into nanoscale phase-separated networks during film formation. Among the various morphologies reported for high-performance OSC systems, fibrillar networks are particularly attractive because they provide continuous pathways for charge transport while maintaining sufficient donor/acceptor interfaces for efficient charge separation5–8. However, the formation of fibrillar networks is challenging to control. During solution processing, donor and acceptor molecules aggregate and crystallize on comparable timescales, and small differences in intermolecular interactions can lead to markedly different morphologies9–14. Excessive growth of acceptor-rich domains, asynchronous crystallization of donor and acceptor components, and local phase segregation often result in poorly connected fibrils and non-uniform interfaces, limiting charge transport and collection15–18.

Considerable efforts have been devoted to controlling fibrillar morphologies in high-performance OSCs. Solvent additives, mixed-solvent systems, and deposition kinetics have been widely employed to regulate aggregation behavior during film formation, enabling improvements in phase separation and molecular packing19,20. Post-deposition treatments, including thermal and solvent-vapor annealing, further provide opportunities to optimize crystallinity and molecular orientation21,22. Molecular design strategies such as side-chain engineering and molecular compatibilizers have been introduced to improve donor/acceptor miscibility and regulate domain purity23. Despite these advances, most existing strategies focus on tuning the final morphology, such as domain size, crystallinity, molecular orientation, or phase purity. Comparatively less attention has been paid to how fibrillar networks emerge during film formation24,25. Because donor and acceptor aggregation occurs under non-equilibrium conditions, the resulting morphology depends not only on thermodynamic driving forces but also on the sequence and kinetics of molecular assembly26. Whether the formation of fibrillar networks can be directed during these early stages remains largely unexplored.

In this work, we designed a cycloalkoxy-functionalized non-fullerene acceptor (NFA), O6R-4F, which acts as a molecular navigator to direct the fibrillar network during film formation of D18:L8-BO-C5 blends27. O6R-4F preferentially localizes at donor/acceptor interfaces, suppressing excessive self-aggregation of L8-BO-C5 while promoting coordinated aggregation and crystallization of donor/acceptor components during film formation. As a result, acceptor-rich domains are restrained from overgrowth, and donor/acceptor molecules are incorporated into a finer, more uniformly connected fibrillar network. The resulting morphology combines refined phase separation with enhanced network connectivity, leading to improved molecular packing, more efficient charge generation, balanced carrier transport, and reduced recombination losses. Consequently, as-cast D18: L8-BO-C5: O6R-4F devices achieve a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 20.9% without any post-deposition treatment, among the highest values reported for as-cast OSCs. Similar structural and performance improvements are observed in chemically distinct donor/acceptor systems, suggesting that employing a molecular navigator to regulate intermolecular interactions during film formation provides an effective route for controlling fibrillar network development. These results highlight the crucial role of early-stage aggregation behavior in determining the final morphology and device performance in OSC blends.

Results

Engineering intermolecular interactions for controlled aggregation

The morphology of bulk-heterojunction films is closely related to the interactions between donor and acceptor molecules during film formation. In many donor/acceptor systems, acceptor molecules tend to aggregate rapidly, while donor and acceptor components often crystallize at different rates. This can lead to excessive growth of acceptor-rich domains and poor matching between the two phases. To regulate the aggregation behavior, we designed a cycloalkoxy-functionalized NFA, O6R-4F, based on the Y6 molecular framework (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1). The conformationally rigid cyclohexyloxy side chains introduce moderate polarity and steric constraint without perturbing the conjugated backbone (Supplementary Fig. 2). This structural modification is intended to reshape the intermolecular interactions in two ways: (i) by weakening uncontrolled acceptor/acceptor lateral packing through steric hindrance, and (ii) by enhancing dispersive and electrostatic interactions with the host acceptor L8-BO-C5, thereby stabilizing hetero-associated aggregates during the earliest stages of nucleation.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to evaluate the intermolecular interactions involving O6R-4F (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Figs. 3–5). The optimized O6R-4F/L8-BO-C5 heterodimer adopts a closely associated configuration with extensive intermolecular contact. The calculated binding energy of the O6R-4F/L8-BO-C5 heterodimer (−43.81 kcal mol−1) is substantially lower (more favorable) than that of the L8-BO-C5/L8-BO-C5 homodimer (−25.64 kcal mol−1), indicating a strong interaction between O6R-4F and L8-BO-C5. Although O6R-4F also exhibits strong self-association (−55.52 kcal mol−1), the favorable heteromolecular interaction suggests that O6R-4F can readily participate in mixed aggregates with L8-BO-C5 and reduce the tendency of L8-BO-C5 to form isolated acceptor-rich clusters during the early stages of film formation.

To examine how these interactions influence molecular distribution in the blend, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were carried out for the D18:L8-BO-C5 and D18:L8-BO-C5: O6R-4F system (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 6). The simulated morphology shows that O6R-4F is predominantly located in regions where donor-rich and acceptor-rich domains meet, rather than forming a separate aggregated phase. This distribution suggests that O6R-4F can remain associated with both components within the mixed film and is preferentially enriched near donor/acceptor interfaces. To further examine the distribution of O6R-4F in the blend, the surface energies of D18, L8-BO-C5, and O6R-4F were determined from contact-angle measurements (Supplementary Fig. 7). The calculated Flory–Huggins interaction parameters reveal a markedly reduced χ value for O6R-4F/L8-BO-C5 (χ = 0.04) compared with D18/L8-BO-C5 (χ = 0.53) and D18/O6R-4F (χ = 0.12) (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, the calculated wetting coefficient of O6R-4F within the D18: L8-BO-C5 blend (ω = −0.15) indicates preferential localization at the donor/acceptor interface rather than homogeneous mixing within either phase.

The DFT calculations, MD simulations, and surface energy analysis collectively suggest that O6R-4F remains closely associated with L8-BO-C5 while preferentially occupying donor/acceptor interfacial regions. Such a distribution is expected to influence how donor and acceptor molecules organize during film formation. Rather than forming large acceptor-rich aggregates, the presence of O6R-4F may promote closer association between the two components and limit excessive lateral growth of the acceptor phase (Fig. 1e). As discussed below, this effect is accompanied by noticeable changes in aggregation kinetics and fibrillar morphology.

Aggregation and crystallization during film formation

The incorporation of O6R-4F leads to noticeable changes in the evolution of molecular aggregation during film formation. To monitor this process, in situ absorption spectroscopy was performed during spin-coating. For neat L8-BO-C5 and the binary D18:L8-BO-C5 blend, aggregation-related absorption features evolve gradually and remain broadly distributed over time (Fig. 2a–c). The progressive spectral red-shift and intensity growth indicate prolonged molecular rearrangement and continuing crystallization after solvent evaporation begins. In contrast, incorporation of O6R-4F leads to a markedly different aggregation kinetic behavior. Distinct aggregation features emerge rapidly and stabilize at earlier times (~0.41 s), and the characteristic spectral intensity reaches a steady state significantly faster than in the D18: L8-BO-C5 system (Fig. 2c, d and Supplementary Fig. 8). This behavior suggests that the major aggregation events take place at an earlier stage of film formation and that less structural rearrangement occurs afterward.

The differences observed during film formation are reflected in the final solid-state structure. Grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) reveals enhanced π–π stacking in the D18:L8-BO-C5:O6R-4F film, evidenced by an intensified and sharpened (010) diffraction peak (Fig. 2e). Quantitative analysis shows a reduced π–π stacking distance accompanied by a pronounced increase in crystalline coherence length (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 2). Notably, this increase in order occurs without domain coarsening, indicating that crystallization proceeds within geometrically constrained assemblies rather than through uncontrolled lateral thickening28. Thin-film absorption spectra also exhibit a more pronounced low-energy shoulder (Supplementary Fig. 9), indicating stronger intermolecular interactions in the aggregated state. To examine mesoscale organization, grazing-incidence small-angle X-ray scattering (GISAXS) measurements were conducted. Upon incorporation of O6R-4F, the characteristic L8-BO-C5 domain size (2Rg) decreases from 25.8 nm to 15.6 nm, while the fractal dimension (Df) increases from 2.39 to 2.79 (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Table 3). The reduction in domain size indicates suppression of excessive domain growth, whereas the higher fractal dimension suggests a more interconnected network structure. Together, these results point to a morphology composed of finer and more closely connected fibrillar domains.

To directly follow the development of crystalline order, in situ GIWAXS measurements were further carried out during spin-coating (Fig. 3)29. In the D18: L8-BO-C5 film (Fig. 3a), crystalline scattering features emerged only after ~5 s and continued to intensify throughout the observation window, indicating that crystallization proceeds over a relatively broad time range30. In contrast, the O6R-4F-containing film exhibited a markedly different evolution (Fig. 3b). Diffraction features appeared at earlier times (~4 s) and rapidly reached a quasi-steady state (~5 s), with only minimal subsequent changes in scattering intensity. The earlier appearance of diffraction features, together with the reduced evolution time, suggests that crystalline domains are established more rapidly in the presence of O6R-4F31.

These observations suggest that the presence of O6R-4F modifies how molecules associate and reorganize during film formation. Rather than undergoing prolonged structural evolution after initial aggregation, the D18:L8-BO-C5:O6R-4F system reaches a relatively stable organization more rapidly. The resulting morphology combines enhanced molecular ordering with finer phase separation, giving rise to a more continuous and interconnected fibrillar network. Such structural characteristics are expected to facilitate charge transport while maintaining sufficient donor/acceptor contact for efficient charge generation, which is consistent with the improved device performance discussed below.

Nanoscale distribution of donor, acceptor, and O6R-4F within fibrillar networks

Photo-induced force microscopy (PiFM) measurements were performed to examine the nanoscale distribution of each component within the fibrillar network. To select chemically distinct vibrational channels for PiFM imaging, photo-induced force spectra of neat D18, L8-BO-C5, and O6R-4F films were collected (Supplementary Fig. 10). D18 exhibits a characteristic band at 813 cm−1, L8-BO-C5 shows a distinct band at 1296 cm−1, and O6R-4F displays a separate absorption band at 1527 cm−1. These bands show negligible overlap among the three components and were therefore used to map the spatial distributions of D18, L8-BO-C5, and O6R-4F in the blend films. In the D18:L8-BO-C5 film, the donor exhibits pronounced, continuous fibrillar features, whereas the acceptor signal appears comparatively diffuse and weakly correlated with the donor network (Fig. 4a, e). The different distributions of donor and acceptor suggest that the two components are incorporated into the fibrillar structure to different extents during film formation. After the addition of O6R-4F, the spatial distribution of the three components changes noticeably. The O6R-4F signal is primarily observed in regions where donor-rich and acceptor-rich domains are in close proximity rather than within the center of either phase (Fig. 4c, f). In the combined PiFM images, O6R-4F follows the boundaries between donor-rich and acceptor-rich domains, indicating preferential enrichment at donor/acceptor interfaces. This distribution is consistent with the MD simulations shown in Fig. 1c and wetting-coefficient analysis shown in Fig. 1d.

The donor and acceptor networks also become more closely matched in the presence of O6R-4F. Fibrillar features observed in the donor and acceptor channels exhibit similar orientations and positions throughout the film. Line-profile analysis further shows correlated intensity variations along individual fibrils (Supplementary Figs. 11–14), suggesting a closer spatial association between the two components. In addition, both donor and acceptor fibrils become narrower after O6R-4F incorporation. Statistical analysis reveals a simultaneous reduction in the characteristic widths of the two phases (Fig. 4b, d). Together with the improved spatial correspondence between donor and acceptor domains, the reduced fibril width suggests that the growth of individual fibrils is more effectively controlled during film formation.

Photovoltaic performance and charge dynamics

Photovoltaic measurements were performed using a conventional ITO/2PACz/D18:L8-BO-C5:O6R-4F/PNDIT-F3N/Ag configuration (Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16). The as-cast D18: L8-BO-C5 device exhibits a PCE of 19.5%, with an open-circuit voltage (VOC) of 0.90 ± 0.01 V, a short-circuit current (JSC) of 27.4 ± 0.5 mA cm−2, and a fill factor (FF) of 78.5 ± 0.7%. Upon incorporating O6R-4F, the device performance improved systematically, with the optimal composition obtained at 10 wt% O6R-4F (Supplementary Table 4). The corresponding device achieved a PCE of 20.9%, with a VOC of 0.90 ± 0.01 V, a JSC of 27.9 ± 0.6 mA cm−2 and an FF of 81.6 ± 0.8% (Fig. 5a, b and Table 1). The champion device was independently certified with a PCE of 20.69% (Supplementary Fig. 17), standing for the highest values reported to date for as-cast OSCs (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Table 5). External quantum efficiency (EQE) spectra (Fig. 5d) reveal a modest yet consistent increase in photoresponse for the O6R-4F-modified devices relative to the undoped counterparts, in agreement with the increased JSC obtained from the J–V measurements.

Device stability was further evaluated under thermal aging at 60 °C, continuous LED illumination, and long-term storage in a nitrogen-filled glove box. As presented in Supplementary Fig. 18 and Supplementary Tables 6–11, devices containing O6R-4F maintained outstanding operational stability across all testing conditions, indicating that the introduction of O6R-4F does not compromise device stability. To investigate the structural basis for the stability of the D18:L8-BO-C5:O6R-4F devices, PiFM was performed on fresh as-cast D18:L8-BO-C5:O6R-4F films, as well as D18:L8-BO-C5:O6R-4F films after thermal aging, continuous illumination, and storage for 1000 h (Supplementary Figs. 19–22). The fibrillar networks remain largely intact under photo and storage aging, with donor and acceptor domains retaining their interdigitated arrangement and characteristic widths. In contrast, thermal aging induces a remarkably broadened fibril-width distribution and minor disruption of the network connectivity, indicating that the fibrillar morphology is somewhat more susceptible to thermal than to photo or storage conditions. These observations are consistent with the performance trends observed in the aged devices, where the D18: L8-BO-C5: O6R-4F system maintains high stability under illumination and storage, but shows modest degradation under prolonged thermal aging.

To elucidate the origin of improved device performance, we examined exciton and charge dynamics. The Jph−Veff characteristics indicate an increased saturated photocurrent and a higher charge generation rate in the O6R-4F doped devices, which is accompanied by an enhanced charge dissociation probability (Supplementary Fig. 23 and Supplementary Table 12). Correspondingly, photoluminescence measurements show a significant reduction in emission in the O6R-4F incorporated films, suggesting more efficient exciton quenching at the donor/acceptor interfaces (Supplementary Fig. 24). O6R-4F possesses compared lowest frontier molecular orbitals (LUMO) energy levels with that of L8-BO-C5 (Supplementary Figs. 25-26), creating a favorable energetic gradient that will not damage the VOC of the device.

Time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) results indicate a slightly longer average exciton lifetime in the D18:L8-BO-C5:O6R-4F blend (Supplementary Fig. 27), suggesting improved exciton diffusion. To further explore the influence of O6R-4F on exciton dynamics, transient absorption (TA) spectroscopy was performed on D18:L8-BO-C5 and D18:L8-BO-C5:O6R-4F films (Fig. 5e, f and Supplementary Fig. 28). Upon excitation at 700 nm, the pristine film exhibits an excitonic ground-state bleach (GSB) at ~810 nm from L8-BO-C5. The hole transfer kinetics are characterized by two components: ultrafast interfacial electron transfer (τ1) and a slower diffusion-mediated process (τ2). Notably, the D18:L8-BO-C5:O6R-4F blend shows significantly faster hole transfer kinetics, with τ1 = 6.66 ps and τ2 = 1056.84 ps, compared to τ1 = 31.49 ps and τ2 = 1764.17 ps for the D18: L8-BO-C5 film (Fig. 5g). This accelerated exciton decay, combined with the enhanced charge generation, quantitatively demonstrates that O6R-4F enhances exciton dissociation into free charges.

Space-charge-limited-current (SCLC) measurements (Supplementary Figs. 29 and 30 and Supplementary Tables 13 and 14) showed that O6R-4F slightly enhanced electron mobility (μe) in neat L8-BO-C5 film (from 1.71 × 10−3 cm2 V−1 s−1 to 1.92 × 10−3 cm2 V−1 s−1), while significantly boosted both the μe (from 0.43 × 10−3 cm2 V−1 s−1 to 1.22 × 10−3 cm2 V−1 s−1) and hole mobility (μh, from 0.48 × 10−3 cm2 V−1 s−1 to 1.25 × 10−3 cm2 V−1 s−1) of D18: L8-BO-C5 blend films, indicates that the self-assembled fiber morphology significantly improves the charge transport channel32. This suggests that the self-assembled fibrillar morphology significantly improves charge transport. Light-intensity-dependent measurements show a decrease in trap-assisted recombination (the ideality factor decreased from 1.14 kT/q to 1.07 kT/q; Fig. 5h) and a slight suppression of bimolecular recombination (the power-law exponent S slightly increased from 0.93 to 0.94; Supplementary Fig. 31) in the O6R-4F incorporated devices33. To further evaluate the contribution of trap states, the density of states (DOS) distributions of the binary and ternary devices were analyzed (Supplementary Fig. 32 and Supplementary Table 15). The extracted trap density (NG) decreases slightly from 8.19 × 1015 to 7.71 × 1015 cm−3 upon incorporation of O6R-4F, indicating a modest reduction in trap-assisted recombination pathways. Transient photovoltage (TPV) and transient photocurrent (TPC) measurements were conducted to assess charge extraction and recombination kinetics34,35. The D18:L8-BO-C5:O6R-4F device exhibited a longer lifetime (0.63 μs) compared to the D18:L8-BO-C5 device (0.39 μs), signifying reduced recombination losses (Fig. 5i)36. Similarly, the TPC-derived extraction times were 0.33 μs for the control D18:L8-BO-C5 device and 0.28 μs for the D18:L8-BO-C5:O6R-4F device (Supplementary Fig. 33), demonstrating improved charge extraction efficiency37.

The incorporation of O6R-4F alters the early-stage aggregation and crystallization of donor and acceptor molecules, leading to finer, more spatially correlated fibrillar networks. This structural evolution contributes to enhanced exciton dissociation, more balanced charge transport, reduced recombination losses, and ultimately improved PCE and FF. While conventional factors such as domain size and trap density also play a role, the combined spectroscopic and structural evidence suggests that O6R-4F-mediated fibrillar network organization is a key contributing factor to the observed device performance.

Similar assembly behavior in chemically distinct systems

To examine whether the effects of O6R-4F extend beyond the D18:L8-BO-C5 system, we introduced O6R-4F into two representative donor/acceptor blends: D18:L8-BO and PM6:Y6 (Supplementary Fig. 34). The as-cast D18:L8-BO device delivers a PCE of 19.5% (VOC = 0.92 ± 0.01 V, JSC = 26.8 ± 0.7 mA cm−2, FF = 79.1 ± 0.9%). Upon incorporation of O6R-4F, the PCE increases to 20.7%, accompanied by simultaneous enhancement of JSC and FF (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Fig. 35, and Supplementary Table 16). A similar trend is observed in the PM6: Y6 system, where introduction of O6R-4F consistently improves device JSC and FF (Supplementary Table 17), with corresponding enhancement in EQE spectra (Fig. 6b). Importantly, these improvements occur without altering VOC, indicating that electronic energetics remain largely unaffected while morphology-dependent processes are optimized.

Contact-angle measurements were performed to evaluate the interfacial properties of these blends (Supplementary Figs. 36 and 37). The calculated wetting coefficients of O6R-4F are negative in both D18:L8-BO (ω = −0.44) and PM6: Y6 (ω = −0.30) blends (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Tables 18 and 19), indicating preferential localization at donor–acceptor interfaces across distinct chemical backbones. Consistent with this result, the Flory–Huggins interaction parameters show stronger interactions between O6R-4F and the acceptor components than with the corresponding donors. The spatial distribution of O6R-4F was further examined by PiFM. Distinct vibrational modes assigned to the donor, acceptor, and O6R-4F allow the location of each component to be visualized simultaneously (Fig. 6d, h). In both blend systems, the O6R-4F signal is mainly observed in regions where donor and acceptor fibrils approach each other, rather than within the interior of either phase. The combined images show that O6R-4F follows the boundaries between donor-rich and acceptor-rich domains (Fig. 6f, g, j, k), in agreement with the interfacial preference inferred from the wetting analysis.

Changes in fibrillar organization similar to those observed in D18 are also found in these systems. After incorporation of O6R-4F, donor and acceptor fibrils become narrower and more uniformly distributed. Statistical analysis reveals a reduction in the characteristic widths of both components (Fig. 6e, i and Supplementary Figs. 38–43), indicating suppression of excessive lateral growth during film formation. At the same time, donor and acceptor fibrils display closer spatial correspondence throughout the network. Although the donor and acceptor materials differ substantially in chemical structure, the introduction of O6R-4F leads to similar changes in interfacial distribution, fibrillar organization, and device performance. The consistency of these observations across multiple systems supports the broader applicability of the molecular navigation strategy.

Discussion

This work highlights the importance of kinetic processes in determining the morphology of OSCs during solution processing. Under rapid solvent evaporation, donor and acceptor molecules assemble far from equilibrium, and the resulting fibrillar network is influenced not only by the final thermodynamic state but also by how aggregation and crystallization proceed during film formation. Uncontrolled growth and asynchronous aggregation can lead to discontinuous networks and poorly matched donor/acceptor interfaces, limiting charge transport and collection.

To address this limit, O6R-4F was introduced to modify the intermolecular interactions within the blend. The combined experimental results indicate that O6R-4F preferentially accumulates at donor/acceptor interfaces, promotes donor/acceptor coordinated aggregation and crystallization during film formation, and suppresses excessive lateral growth of acceptor-rich aggregates. As a result, donor and acceptor components form finer and more closely matched fibrillar networks, accompanied by improved phase separation and charge transport properties. Compared with conventional approaches that rely on post-deposition treatments to adjust morphology after film formation, the present strategy influences the aggregation process itself. The improvements observed in both structural characterization and device performance suggest that controlling intermolecular interactions during the early stages of assembly provides an effective route for regulating fibrillar network formation in as-cast films. The achievement of 20.9% efficiency without post-treatment further demonstrates the effectiveness of this approach.

The applicability of O6R-4F to multiple donor/acceptor systems suggests that the underlying mechanism is not restricted to a specific material combination. Instead, tuning intermolecular interactions may provide a useful means of influencing self-assembly behavior across different organic semiconductor systems. These findings offer additional insight into the relationship between molecular interactions, film formation, and fibrillar morphology, and may be relevant to the development of other solution-processed electronic materials.

Methods

Materials

Compound 1 and all the other chemicals were purchased as reagent grade from J&K, Energy, Macklin, or Sigma-Aldrich, and used without further purification. All solvents for reactions were distilled immediately prior to use.

Characterizations

UV-vis-NIR absorption along with PL spectra were obtained using an absorption–photoluminescence spectral analyzer (APLS-AS II). The J–V characteristics were measured under standard AM 1.5 G illumination provided by a 150 W AAA-rated solar simulator (Sirius-SS150A, Zolix Instruments), with a Keithley 2450 SourceMeter operated via computer control. EQE measurements were performed using a home-built setup (Zolix Instruments), calibrated against a certified silicon reference device.

TA measurements employed a Yb:KGW laser system (1030 nm, 220 fs, 100 kHz, incident pump fluence of 10 μJ cm−2). The output beam was divided into two paths: one generated ~30 fs pump pulses centered at 700 nm, while the other was directed onto a YAG crystal to produce a white-light continuum as the probe. The pump and probe beams were spatially overlapped on the sample with a crossing angle below 10°, and the transmitted probe signal was detected using a linear CCD array. Pump and probe spot sizes were approximately 2 μm, and measurements were conducted at room temperature under a nitrogen atmosphere. The TA kinetic traces were extracted at characteristic wavelengths of the blend films. After background subtraction and normalization, the traces were fitted using a double-exponential decay function: ΔA(t) = A1 exp(−t/τ1) + A2 exp(−t/τ2) + C.

TPC and TPV were recorded using a CEL-TPV2000 system. GIWAXS and GISAXS analyses were carried out on a Xeuss 2.0 platform. During the spin-coating process, in situ transmission absorption spectra were collected with a Maya2000 Pro spectrometer (Ocean Insight) using a xenon lamp as the light source. Specifically, the spin-coating speed was maintained at 6500 rpm, the spin-coating duration was 10 s, the substrate temperature was kept at 30 °C, and the measurements were carried out under an ambient temperature of 28 °C and relative humidity of 72%. The solutions were prepared using the same solvent, chloroform, and the substrates were exposed to the same ambient environment during the measurements. PiFM measurements were conducted using a VistaScope system (Molecular Vista Inc.) at the Songshan Lake Materials Laboratory. PiFM results were acquired using a VistaScope microscope from Molecular Vista Inc., from Songshan Lake Materials Laboratory. The PiFM setup utilized a pulsed quantum cascade laser (Block Engineering) with a continuously tunable narrowband range from 760 to 1950 cm−1. The spectral linewidth was approximately 2 cm−1, with a wavenumber resolution of 0.5 cm−1.

Molecular dynamics simulation

The phase in the D18:L8-BO-C5 and D18:L8-BO-C5:O6R-4F blends is obtained from the forcite molecular dynamics with the optimized structures going through the following procedures: (1) 7 D18 decamer chains and 40 L8-BO-C5 molecules (with an extra 8 O6R-4F molecules for the ternary blend) are packing in an amorphous cell with an initial density of ~0.8 g cm−3. (2) 5 annealing cycles between 300 and 1000 K (7 steps, 50 ps per step, NPT) are conducted to randomize the molecules. (3) The cells are subjected to successive dynamics of 200 ps molecular dynamics (NVT, T = 400 K) and 200 ps molecular dynamics (NVT, T = 300 K) to simulate the change in the cell. The final structures are used for further analysis.

Surface energy analysis

The contact angles (θ) of water and oil on the film surfaces were measured using a Dataphysics OCA40 Micro contact-angle system. Based on these measurements, the surface energies of the films were determined according to the following relation: (1+cosθ)γpl = 2(γsd γpld)1/2 + 2(γsp γplp)1/2. Here, γ represents the surface energy of the material under investigation, while γpl denotes that of the probing liquid. The parameters γd and γp refer to the dispersive and polar contributions to the surface energy, respectively. The angle θ corresponds to the contact angle formed by the test liquid (water or glycerol) on the film surface. The interfacial tension (γAB) between two materials was evaluated using Neumann’s equation: γAB = γA + γB –2(γAγB)1/2 e^[–β(γA –γB)2], where β is a constant equal to 0.000115 m4 m J−2. For a ternary system composed of A, B, and C, the wetting behavior of component C within the A:B blend can be described by the wetting coefficient ωC, derived from Young’s relation: ωC = (γBC − γAC)/γAB. The spatial distribution of component C depends on the value of ωC: it preferentially resides in the B phase when ωC < − 1, accumulates at the A/B interface when −1 < ωC < 1, and is located within the A phase when ωC > 1.

Fabrication of OSCs

Pre-patterned indium tin oxide (ITO) substrates were cleaned with detergent, followed by sequential ultrasonication in deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol for 15 min each. Afterward, the substrates were dried in an oven overnight. Prior to further processing, oxygen plasma treatment was applied for 4 min. A solution of (2-(9H-carbazol-9-yl)ethyl)phosphonic acid (2PACz) in ethanol (0.27 mg mL−1) was then spin-coated onto the substrates at 3000 rpm for 20 s, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 10 min. The active layers (~100 nm) were fabricated via spin coating from chloroform solutions containing different donor: acceptor systems. Specifically, D18:L8-BO-C5 (1:1.2, w/w; 14 mg mL−1; containing 50 wt% octafluoronaphene) was deposited at 6500 rpm for 30 s, D18:L8-BO (1:1.2, w/w; 14 mg mL−1; containing 50 wt% octafluoronaphene) at 6700 rpm for 30 s, and PM6:Y6 (1:1.3, w/w; 15 mg mL−1; containing 50 wt% octafluoronaphene) at 3500 rpm for 30 s. Subsequently, a PNDIT-F3N methanol solution (1 mg mL−1) was spin-coated at 3000 rpm for 20 s to serve as the cathode interlayer. Finally, a silver electrode with a thickness of 100 nm was deposited by thermal evaporation under high vacuum conditions (<1 × 10−4 Pa).

Fabrication and characterization of charge-only devices

Hole and electron transport properties were evaluated using hole-only (ITO/2PACz/active layer/Ag) and electron-only (ITO/ZnO/active layer/PNDIT-F3N/Ag) device architectures, respectively. The J–V measurements were carried out with a Keithley 2450 source meter. Carrier mobilities were derived by fitting the high-voltage region of the J–V curves using the trap-free SCLC model based on the Mott–Gurney equation: J=98ε0εrμV2d3. In this expression, J denotes the current density, μ represents the zero-field charge carrier mobility, ε0 is the permittivity of free space, εr corresponds to the dielectric constant of the active layer, and d is the thickness of the film. The effective voltage V is given by the difference between the applied voltage and the built-in potential (V = Vappl-Vbi).
