Canonical Quantization of a Memristive Leaky Integrate-and-Fire Neuron Circuit
Dean Brand, Domenica Dibenedetto, Francesco Petruccione

Abstract
We present a theoretical framework for a quantized memristive Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) neuron, uniting principles from neuromorphic engineering and open quantum systems. Starting from a classical memristive LIF circuit, we apply canonical quantization techniques to derive a quantum model grounded in circuit quantum electrodynamics. Numerical simulations demonstrate key dynamical features of the quantized memristor and LIF neuron in the weak-coupling and adiabatic regime, including memory effects and spiking behaviour. This work establishes a foundational model for quantum neuromorphic computing, offering a pathway towards biologically inspired quantum spiking neural networks and new paradigms in quantum machine learning.



Modern computing is reaching fundamental physical and architectural limits. On the physical side, continued transistor miniaturization is approaching atomic scales, where quantum tunneling induces intolerable error rates that threaten the reliability of classical processors
Waldrop 2016
. Architecturally, the von Neumann bottleneck imposes a hard constraint on performance and energy efficiency by requiring continuous data shuttling between memory and processor
Zou et al. 2021
. In contrast, biological brains seamlessly integrate memory and computation within the same substrate, achieving high parallelism and unmatched energy efficiency
Benjamin 2014
;
Merolla 2014
;
Davies 2018
.

These two limitations have catalyzed the development of alternative computational paradigms. Quantum computing addresses the physical scaling limit by harnessing superposition and entanglement to perform certain tasks with exponential speed-up, thus circumventing the need for denser circuitry. Neuromorphic computing, on the other hand, draws inspiration from biological neural systems to overcome architectural inefficiencies. By co-locating memory and processing and leveraging spiking neurons and plastic synapses, neuromorphic architectures offer low-power, massively parallel information processing. One key enabling component is the memristor, a resistor with memory, which supports localized learning and efficient connectivity
Marković et al. 2020
.

However, quantum and neuromorphic computing typically pursue separate trajectories: the former excels in computational power but lacks architectural efficiency, while the latter offers efficient structure but limited general-purpose speed-up. This complementarity has motivated a convergence into quantum neuromorphic computing, a nascent but promising field aiming to unify quantum dynamics with brain-inspired architectures
Marković and Grollier 2020
;
Sato et al. 2004
;
Pehle and Wetterich 2022
. Though the landscape is vast, encompassing hardware implementations, computational primitives, and learning models, it remains largely unexplored.

Recent contributions span quantum reservoir computing
Katumba et al. 2018
;
Ghosh et al. 2019
;
Ghosh et al. 2021
, quantum memristors
Pfeiffer et al. 2016
;
Sanz et al. 2018
;
Guo, Y. -M. et al. 2021
, and various proposals for quantum neurons
Cao et al. 2017
;
Tacchino et al. 2019
;
Kristensen et al. 2021
. In particular, a gap remains in developing biologically inspired quantum neuron models that balance computational efficiency with biological realism. In classical neuroscience and AI, neuron models range from the highly efficient yet abstract artificial neural networks to the biophysically grounded Hodgkin-Huxley model
Hodgkin and Huxley 1952
.

While both extremes have seen some level of quantization, an important intermediate model, the Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) neuron, has not yet been formally quantized. The LIF model provides a biologically plausible, yet computationally lightweight, spiking framework well-suited to time-dependent tasks and neuromorphic hardware
Yamazaki et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2020
. Although a phenomenological quantum analogue of the LIF neuron has recently been proposed
Brand and Petruccione 2024
, a formal quantum formulation remains lacking. Developing such a model is critical for establishing a theoretical foundation upon which future quantum neuromorphic architectures can be systematically built.

In this work, we present a direct quantization of a memristive LIF neuron circuit, introducing a quantum memristor model based on explicit bath mode coupling and an open quantum systems framework. Inspired by prior quantizations of biologically grounded models such as the Hodgkin-Huxley neuron
Gonzalez-Raya et al. 2019
;
Gonzalez-Raya et al. 2020
, our approach yields a fully quantum spiking neuron with intrinsic memory encoded in its circuit dynamics. We derive a microscopic Hamiltonian for an LC (inductor-capacitor) oscillator — representing the neuronal membrane — interacting with a state-dependent transmission line that captures memristive behavior. In the adiabatic and weak-coupling limit, we show analytically how this model recovers the classical LIF dynamics with a memristive leak term.

The rest of the Letter is structured as follows. We recap the classical formulation of the neuromorphic circuitry of the LIF neuron, and its memristive formulation. Following this we begin the quantization process, walking through the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formalisms for how to handle the circuit elements within rigorous circuit quantum electrodynamics principles, resulting in a fully quantized memristive LIF circuit model. Next we verify the derivations with numerical simulations of the models presented and how they match the characteristic features of the classical counterparts. Finally we summarize our findings and discuss the outlook of how this model serves as a foundation for further exploration and development of quantum neuromorphic computing.

We begin by introducing the classical models that underpin our quantum neuromorphic framework: the LIF neuron and the memristor. We show how these two components can be naturally combined to form a compact, biologically inspired circuit that serves as the classical counterpart to our quantum model.

The LIF neuron is a foundational model in computational neuroscience and neuromorphic engineering. It captures the essential behavior of biological neurons using a simple electrical circuit. In its standard form, the LIF neuron is modeled as a resistor-capacitor (RC) low-pass filter, where:
C_{m}
is the membrane capacitance,
R
is the leak resistance,
V(t)
is the membrane potential, and
I_{\mathrm{in}}(t)
is the input current. The governing equation is:

C_{m}\derivative{V(t)}{t}=-\frac{V(t)}{R}+I_{\mathrm{in}}(t).

(1)

Here, the capacitor integrates incoming current over time, while the resistor causes the potential to “leak” back toward rest. When
V(t)
reaches a threshold
V_{\mathrm{th}}
, the neuron emits a spike and the potential is reset. Despite its simplicity, this model captures core spiking behavior and serves as the basis for many hardware implementations.

To introduce adaptive behavior and memory, we use the memristor, a nonlinear, history-dependent resistor proposed by Chua in 1971
Chua 1971
;
Chua and Kang 1976
. It completes the set of fundamental passive circuit elements (resistor, capacitor, inductor, memristor) and is especially relevant for neuromorphic applications.
A memristor’s resistance evolves according to the total electric charge
q(t)
that has flowed through it, enabling it to encode memory of past activity. Its behavior can be described by a generalized Ohm’s law:

V(t)=M\left(q(t)\right)I(t),

(2)

where
M(q)
is the memristance, a charge-dependent resistance function.
When driven by a periodic signal, the current-voltage
I
-
V
curve of a memristor displays a pinched hysteresis loop, which is a key experimental signature of memristive behavior

For a more realistic description, the Strukov model considers memristors implemented with thin-film titanium dioxide (TiO
2
) devices
Strukov et al. 2008

R_{\mathrm{on}}
) and an undoped region with high resistance (
R_{\mathrm{off}}
). The boundary between these regions is defined by the width
w(t)

D

The voltage across the memristor is given by:

V(t)=\left(R_{\mathrm{on}}\frac{w(t)}{D}+R_{\mathrm{off}}\left(1-\frac{w(t)}{D}\right)\right)I(t).

(3)

Here, the overall resistance is a weighted sum of the resistances of the two regions, determined by the proportion of the device occupied by each. The memristance as a function of charge can thus be expressed as:

M(q(t))=\left(R_{\mathrm{on}}\frac{w(t)}{D}+R_{\mathrm{off}}\left(1-\frac{w(t)}{D}\right)\right).

(4)

This approach captures how the device’s resistance changes dynamically as the boundary
w(t)
shifts in response to the flow of charge, reflecting the underlying ionic drift mechanism in TiO
2
memristors.

By replacing the leak resistor in the LIF circuit with a memristor, we obtain a memristive LIF neuron. This hybrid model introduces a key improvement: the leak conductance becomes state-dependent, allowing the neuron to adapt based on past input.

Substituting the resistor with a memristor
R\to M(q(t))
in the LIF equation
Equation

1
yields:

C_{m}\derivative{V(t)}{t}=-\frac{V(t)}{M(q(t))}+I_{\mathrm{in}}(t),

(5)

while the memristor state evolves through

\derivative{q}{t}=I(t)=\frac{V(t)}{M(q(t))}.

(6)

To quantize the memristive LIF neuron, we address a key challenge: resistive elements such as memristors are inherently dissipative and thus incompatible with the unitary, reversible evolution of quantum systems. Although various quantum memristor models exist, here we pursue a physically grounded quantization within the LIF framework by modeling the memristive leak as an effective environment.

We replace the dissipative memristor with a semi-infinite, lossless transmission line (TL) composed of coupled
LC
oscillators, following the approach of the Caldeira-Leggett model
Vool and Devoret 2017
. The line acts as an absorbing bath, with its characteristic impedance
Z_{0}=\sqrt{L_{0}/C_{0}}
encoding the effective resistance. By adiabatically modulating
Z_{0}(t)
, we mimic the memristor’s history-dependent behavior, allowing us to recover classical memristive dynamics in the weak-coupling, low-frequency limit.

To couple the LIF node to this TL environment, we introduce a small coupling capacitor
C_{C}
, as shown in
Figure

3
. This ensures that the interaction remains perturbative and does not dominate the system’s dynamics.

Figure 3

\phi_{i}
in the TL has capacitance
C_{0}

L_{0}

The system is described by the Lagrangian:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}C_{m}\dot{\phi}_{0}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}C_{C}(\dot{\phi}_{0}-\dot{\phi}_{1})^{2}

(7)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left[\frac{1}{2}C_{0}\dot{\phi}_{i}^{2}-\frac{(\phi_{i+1}-\phi_{i})^{2}}{2L_{0}}\right],

where
\phi_{0}
is the flux at the membrane node and
\dot{\phi}_{0}=V_{0}
is the membrane voltage.

Taking the continuum limit with spacing
\Delta x\to 0
, we define the flux field
\phi(x,t)
, with:

C_{0}\Delta x\to\mathfrak{C},\quad\frac{L_{0}}{\Delta x}\to\mathfrak{L},

(8)

leading to the TL Lagrangian:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TL}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{C}(\partial_{t}\phi)^{2}-\frac{1}{2\mathfrak{L}}(\partial_{x}\phi)^{2}\right]\differential{x}.

(9)

The wave equation for the TL field becomes:

\mathfrak{C}\partial_{t}^{2}\phi(x,t)=\frac{1}{\mathfrak{L}}\partial_{x}^{2}\phi(x,t).

(10)

A deeper discussion of the wave formalism and its physical interpretation is provided in the Appendix. At the membrane boundary
x=0
, the Euler-Lagrange equation yields:

C_{m}\ddot{\phi}_{0}-C_{C}\left[\ddot{\phi}_{0}-\partial_{tt}\phi(0,t)\right]-\frac{\phi_{0}}{L_{m}}=I_{\mathrm{in}}(t),

(11)

where
L_{m}
is an effective inductance capturing the intrinsic membrane leak.

Under weak coupling
C_{C}\ll C_{m}
, the correction to membrane inertia is negligible, giving:

C_{m}\ddot{\phi}_{0}+\frac{1}{Z_{0}}\dot{\phi}_{0}=I_{\mathrm{in}}(t),

(12)

with
Z_{0}\equiv\sqrt{\mathfrak{L}/\mathfrak{C}}
. Recognizing
\dot{\phi}_{0}=V(t)
, we recover the classical memristive LIF equation.

We now construct a microscopic Hamiltonian by identifying the canonical momenta:

\displaystyle Q_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{\phi}_{0}}\approx C_{m}\dot{\phi}_{0},

(13)

\displaystyle\Pi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta\mathcal{L}}{\delta(\partial_{t}\phi)}=\mathfrak{C}\partial_{t}\phi(x,t).

(14)

The Hamiltonian is obtained via the Legendre transform:

H=Q_{0}\dot{\phi}_{0}+\int\Pi(x)\partial_{t}\phi(x,t)\differential{x}-\mathcal{L}.

(15)

This yields the total Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{0}^{2}}{2C_{m}}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{\Pi^{2}}{2\mathfrak{C}}+\frac{(\partial_{x}\phi)^{2}}{2\mathfrak{L}}\right]\differential{x}

(16)

\displaystyle-C_{C}\frac{Q_{0}}{C_{m}}\Pi(0)-\phi_{0}I_{\mathrm{in}}(t).

Promoting
\phi_{0}
and
Q_{0}
to operators with
[\phi_{0},Q_{0}]=i\hbar
, we obtain a fully quantum description of the circuit. The interaction term couples the membrane flux to the TL environment, enabling energy exchange and effective dissipation.

In the classical limit, Hamilton’s equations reproduce the expected dynamics:

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial H}{\partial Q_{0}}\approx\frac{Q_{0}}{C_{m}},

(17)

\displaystyle\dot{Q}_{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\phi_{0}}=I_{\mathrm{in}}(t)-\frac{V(t)}{Z_{0}}.

(18)

Combining these yields:

C_{m}\dot{V}(t)+\frac{V(t)}{Z_{0}(t)}=I_{\mathrm{in}}(t),

(19)

which identifies the effective memristive leak as
Z_{0}(t)=M(q(t))
.

We have derived a fully quantized model of the memristive LIF neuron by replacing the dissipative memristor with a semi-infinite transmission line bath. In the adiabatic, weak-coupling limit, this quantum model recovers the classical LIF neuron with a state-dependent leak conductance. This provides a rigorous microscopic foundation for future explorations of quantum neuromorphic systems.

With a now fully quantized description of the memristive LIF neuron circuit, we can verify the expected behaviours under a classical input drive,
I(t)=I_{0}\sin(\omega t)
, and investigate the validity of the outputs. This is done to obtain the characteristic feature of a memristor, as confirmation that the quantization procedure is indeed memristive. To do this, we will investigate the expectation values of the voltage and current of the quantized memristor under a period input drive, which should replicate the pinched hysteresis loop characteristic of memristors
Chua 2014
. Succeeding this we will investigate the full LIF neuron circuit, with appropriately included thresholding, firing, and reset mechanisms, to recover a consistent spiking neuron.

The characteristic feature of a memristor is the pinched hysteresis curve in the
I
-
V
plane of the memristive response under a periodic input driving current. To verify that the quantum model is truly a memristor it needs to produce this characteristic behaviour.

To produce the
I
-
V
plane of the hysteresis plot, we can extract the expectation values of voltage,
V(t)=\expectationvalue{\dot{\hat{\phi}}_0}
, and current,
I(t)=\expectationvalue{\hat{\phi}}/Z_{0}(t)
, obtained when measuring the circuit. To simulate this, we integrate a time-dependent Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad (GKSL) master equation
Breuer and Petruccione 2010
while updating the memristance
Z_{0}(t)=M(q(t))
at each of the
2\,000
time steps of the density matrix evolution
Johansson et al. 2012
.

Looking at the single-mode oscillator of the membrane node, in terms of creation/annihilation operators
a^{\dagger}
/
a
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar Z_{0}}{2}}(a+a^{\dagger}),

(20)

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2Z_{0}}}(a^{\dagger}-a).

(21)

The reduced Hamiltonian for this case is then

H(t)=\hbar\omega_{0}\left(a^{\dagger}a+\frac{1}{2}\right)-\hat{\phi}_{0}I_{0}\sin(\omega t).

(22)

We express the memristive leak in terms of a Lindbladian dissipator, in terms of annihilation operator
a
, as

\mathcal{D}[a]\rho=a\rho a^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{a^{\dagger}a,\rho\right\}.

(23)

The associated decay rate

\gamma(q)=\frac{1}{C_{m}M(q)}

(24)

is tied directly to the instantaneous memristance
M(q)
, so that the strength of the dissipative channel follows the memristive state without introducing spurious dynamics.

The GKSL master equation is then expressed as

\dot{\rho}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[H(t),\rho\right]+\gamma(t)\mathcal{D}[a]\rho,

(25)

where the collapse factor for the master equation is
\gamma(t)=1/(Z_{0}(t)C_{m})
.

The state update memristor law is adapted from the linear drift of
Equation

3
, as we now have

\displaystyle q(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\frac{\expectationvalue{\hat{\phi}_0}}{Z_{0}(\tau)}\differential{\tau},

(26)

\displaystyle Z_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=M(q(t))

(27)

\displaystyle M(q(t))

\displaystyle=R_{\mathrm{on}}\left(\frac{q}{q_{\mathrm{max}}}\right)+R_{\mathrm{off}}\left(1-\frac{q}{q_{\mathrm{max}}}\right).

(28)

To extend the quantum memristor model to the full memristive quantum leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron, we must incorporate the key characteristics of LIF dynamics. Specifically, the model must not only integrate the input driving current but also implement a thresholding mechanism that triggers an output spike and resets the membrane potential. In our approach, the thresholding is monitored classically via the expectation value of the voltage operator. In the numerical simulation, this is done through interrupting the solver and replacing the state
\rho\to\outerproduct{0}{0}
, which is the vacuum state, and continuing.

Another key feature of the LIF neuron is the refractory period — the brief interval following an output spike during which the neuron is temporarily unresponsive to further stimulation. In simulations, this is implemented by pausing both the input drive and memristor update for a duration
\tau_{\mathrm{ref}}
.

The operation of the memristive quantum leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron is depicted in
Figure

, where a sinusoidal input current drives the quantum circuit to generate output spikes via a thresholding and refractory mechanism, mirroring the behavior of a classical LIF neuron.

Depending on system parameters, the spiking neuron exhibits various behaviors. However, the example in
Figure

5
is particularly illustrative. The figure demonstrates a clear integration of the membrane potential in response to positive current, with analogous inhibitory behavior for negative current, reflecting the excitatory and inhibitory dynamics expected from biologically inspired models
Callaway 2004
;
Eshraghian et al. 2023
. Refractory periods are evident as intervals with no updates to the membrane potential or memristance.

A notable feature of this example is the asymmetry in the output spike train, arising from the interplay between the timing and amplitude of the driving current. The current’s amplitude is sometimes insufficient to reach the threshold, allowing the frequency-dependent oscillations to modulate the membrane potential while memristive dissipation damps the amplitude. Upon reversal of the current’s sign, the membrane potential again grows toward the threshold.

This example demonstrates all characteristic features of a memristive LIF neuron circuit within a fully quantum model, except for the classical input current, which remains relevant for realistic quantum device modeling.

We have presented a fully microscopic, quantized‑circuit derivation of the leaky integrate‑and‑fire neuron with a memristive leak. By coupling an
LC
membrane oscillator to a semi‑infinite transmission‑line whose characteristic impedance
Z_{0}
tracks the device’s charge history, we constructed the complete Lagrangian and Hamiltonian, performed a Legendre transform, and showed that Hamilton’s equations reduce in the weak‑coupling, adiabatic limit to
Equation

5
, recovering the classical LIF equation. This derivation provides a principled foundation for understanding how memristive plasticity and quantized bath modes shape spiking‑neuron dynamics.

We have derived a full Hamiltonian of a quantum memristive LIF neuron circuit, including all necessary handling of the dissipative circuit elements, and shown that it reduces back to the classical LIF dynamics in the appropriate limits. This led to the first-principles derivation of a quantum memristor in addition to the LIF circuit, in a novel contribution to the discussion of quantum memristors. The incorporation of this in a quantized spiking neuron circuit with demonstrable spiking behaviour provides a unified framework for building upon this model towards more elaborate networks of quantum neurons, and for including further quantum properties and a new path for investigating usable memory in quantum circuits.

Throughout this work we have shown that, under the Born-Markov and adiabatic (weak-coupling) limits, the quantized LIF-memristor faithfully reproduces the classical dynamics. However, if these limits are not maintained, for example by letting the system-bath coupling,
\gamma
, approach the core-mode frequency,
\omega_{0}
, or allowing the memristive impedance
M(q)
vary on timescales comparable to
1/\omega_{0}
, the derivation breaks down and truly quantum effects are expected to appear.

In that case we anticipate effects such as quantum superpositions of spiking trajectories, non-Markovian memory kernels, entanglement between the core oscillating mode and the memristor in a way that would complicate the memristive update law. Exploring these non-perturbative regimes opens the door to further quantum neuromorphic phenomena and how these can be harnessed for new computational models, which is an exciting research direction stemming from this work.

The unified framework derived in this work also presents each component as a self-contained novel contribution to the field of quantum neuromorphic components, which will serve to enhance other models and serve as the building blocks for other classical neuron circuits that can be quantized, or even to discover new purely quantum neurons that make fundamental use of properties like entanglement which are not available to the classical set of neurons.

While this model serves as a full formulation of a quantum memristive circuit, there are many alterations that can be made to explore interesting effects of quantum properties on this circuit beyond its classical mirroring. The first natural path of exploration is going beyond numerical simulation of the master equation governing the circuit’s behaviour into implementing the circuit in quantum hardware. The derivation in this work lends itself naturally to a superconducting quantum platform, due to its reliance on electrical circuitry and natural placing of capacitors and inductors which couple well with Josephson Junctions
Peotta and Di Ventra 2014
;
Cheng et al. 2018
;
Salmilehto et al. 2017
. Also, due to circuit QED principles used in this derivation, an exciting avenue for implementation would be photonic platforms, with the appropriate conversions of circuit elements to their photonic counterparts and building upon the exploration that has been done into photonic memristors
Sanz et al. 2018
;
Spagnolo et al. 2022
;
Zhuge et al. 2019
;
Shastri et al. 2021
.

The memristive behaviour and interaction with environmental baths can also be explored in many directions, taking the Markovian model here which is dependent on a lot of classical connection into a model which makes use of non-Markovian memristive models which use the environment itself to encode the memristive update rules and system memory
Pfeiffer et al. 2016
;
Breuer and Petruccione 2010
. Also in this direction is the interesting and accessible derivation of a model which takes in a quantized driving current as well, as in the quantized Hodgkin-Huxley example
Gonzalez-Raya et al. 2019
;
Gonzalez-Raya et al. 2020
, to investigate the properties that arise from a purely quantum model, albeit further from the realm of application.

The proposed model establishes a rigorous foundation for the emerging discipline of quantum neuromorphic computing, providing a versatile platform for subsequent theoretical and experimental advances. It is expected to enable direct applications in quantum machine learning and quantum information processing, and to serve as a modular element in computational neuroscience frameworks. Future investigations of its dynamical and scaling properties will further uncover its role in realizing neuromorphic architectures under genuinely quantum-mechanical principles.

Appendix A
Appendix: Wave Formalism

In the transmission line continuum, any solution of the wave equation,

\mathfrak{C}\,\partial_{tt}\phi(x,t)-\frac{1}{\mathfrak{L}}\,\partial_{xx}\phi(x,t)=0,\quad x>0.

(29)

can be expressed as a superposition of incoming and outgoing waves, such that at the boundary the flux operator is
\phi(0,t)=\phi_{\mathrm{in}}(t)+\phi_{\mathrm{out}}(t)
and the transmission line current into the boundary can be expanded from

I_{\mathrm{TL}}=\frac{1}{\mathfrak{L}}\partial_{x}\phi(0,t)=-\frac{1}{\mathfrak{L}v}V(t)=-\frac{1}{Z_{0}}V(t),

(30)

to

I_{\mathrm{TL}}(t)=\frac{\phi_{\mathrm{in}}(t)-\phi_{\mathrm{out}}(t)}{Z_{0}}.

(31)

Enforcing the Kirchhoff current law on the boundary coupling capacitor identifies

I_{C_{C}}(t)=C_{c}\left[\ddot{\phi}_{0}-\partial_{tt}\phi(0,t)\right]=I_{\mathrm{TL}}(t).

(32)

Taking the Fourier transform of the time domain to frequency domain gives
\partial_{tt}\to-\omega^{2}
, such that

\frac{\phi_{\mathrm{in}}(\omega)-\phi_{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)}{Z_{0}}=i\omega C_{C}\left[\phi_{0}(\omega)-\phi_{\mathrm{in}}(\omega)-\phi_{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)\right].

(33)

Defining a dimensionless coupling parameter of
\alpha(\omega)=\omega Z_{0}C_{C}
and solving for
\phi_{\mathrm{out}}
gives

\phi_{\rm out}(\omega)=\underbrace{\frac{1-i\alpha}{1+i\alpha}}_{R(\omega)}\;\phi_{\mathrm{in}}(\omega)\;-\;\underbrace{\frac{i\alpha}{1+i\alpha}}_{S(\omega)}\;\phi_{0}(\omega).

(34)

Here we identify the reflection coefficient and source-to-output transfer coefficient, respectively, as

\displaystyle R(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-i\omega Z_{0}C_{C}}{1+i\omega Z_{0}C_{C}},

(35)

\displaystyle S(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{-i\omega Z_{0}C_{C}}{1+i\omega Z_{0}C_{C}}.

(36)

Interpreting this in physical context, the weak coupling or low frequency limit,
\omega Z_{0}C_{C}\ll 1
, leads to the approximate forms of
R(\omega)\approx 1-2i\omega Z_{0}C_{C}
and
S(\omega)\approx-i\omega Z_{0}C_{C}
, where almost all incoming energy is reflected with a small phase shift, and the node drive
\phi_{0}
weakly launches waves into the transmission line.

Conversely, under strong coupling or high frequency,
\omega Z_{0}C_{C}\gg 1
, then
R(\omega)\approx-1
and
