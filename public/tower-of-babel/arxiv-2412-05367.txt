The non-stabilizerness of fermionic Gaussian states
Mario Collura, Jacopo De Nardis, Vincenzo Alba, Guglielmo Lami

Abstract
We introduce an efficient method to quantify nonstabilizerness in fermionic Gaussian states, overcoming the long-standing challenge posed by their extensive entanglement. Using a perfect sampling scheme based on an underlying determinantal point process, we compute the Stabilizer Rényi Entropies (SREs) for systems with hundreds of qubits. Benchmarking on random Gaussian states with and without particle conservation, we reveal an extensive leading behavior equal to that of Haar random states, with logarithmic subleading corrections. We support these findings with analytical calculations for a set of related quantities, the participation entropies in the computational (or Fock) basis, for which we derive an exact formula. We also investigate the time evolution of non-stabilizerness in a random unitary circuit with Gaussian gates, observing that it converges in a time that scales logarithmically with the system size. Applying the sampling algorithm to a two-dimensional free-fermionic topological model, we uncover a sharp transition in non-stabilizerness at the phase boundaries, highlighting the power of our approach in exploring different phases of quantum many-body systems, even in higher dimensions.

Preliminaries. —

We consider a quantum system of
L
qubits, with total Hilbert space dimension
D=2^{L}
. The local Pauli operators are denoted as
\hat{P}_{i}\in\{\hat{\mathbb{1}},{\hat{\text{X}}},{\hat{\text{Y}}},{\hat{\text{Z}}}\}
for
i=1,2...L
, while
\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}\in\{\hat{\mathbb{1}},{\hat{\text{X}}},{\hat{\text{Y}}},{\hat{\text{Z}}}\}^{\otimes L}
represents a generic tensor product of Pauli operators (i.e. a Pauli string). The set
\{\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}}{\sqrt{D}}\}
forms an orthonormal basis for the space of operators, since
\text{Tr}[\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\prime}\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}]=D\delta_{\boldsymbol{P}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{P}}
. Majorana operators are defined using the standard Jordan-Wigner mapping, as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{2i-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\hat{\text{Z}}}_{1}...{\hat{\text{Z}}}_{i-1}{\hat{\text{X}}}_{i}\hat{\mathbb{1}}_{i+1}...\hat{\mathbb{1}}_{L}\,,

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{2i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\hat{\text{Z}}}_{1}...{\hat{\text{Z}}}_{i-1}{\hat{\text{Y}}}_{i}\hat{\mathbb{1}}_{i+1}...\hat{\mathbb{1}}_{L}\,,

where
i=1,2...L
. The
2L
Majorana operators are Hermitian and satisfy the canonical anti-commutation relations
\{\hat{\gamma}_{\mu},\hat{\gamma}_{\nu}\}=2\delta_{\mu\nu}
, for
\mu,\nu\in\{1,2,...\,2L\}
. They can also be written in terms of the fermionic creation annihilation operators
\hat{c}_{i},\hat{c}_{i}^{{\dagger}}
, as
\hat{\gamma}_{2i-1}=(\hat{c}_{i}^{{\dagger}}+\hat{c}_{i})
,
\hat{\gamma}_{2i}=i(\hat{c}_{i}^{{\dagger}}-\hat{c}_{i})
. The computational basis is identified with the set of Fock states
\ket{\boldsymbol{z}}=(\hat{c}^{{\dagger}}_{1})^{z_{1}}...(\hat{c}^{{\dagger}}_{L})^{z_{L}}\ket{0...0}
, where
z_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is the local occupation-number and
\ket{0...0}
is the the Fermi vacuum. Majorana monomials are generic strings of Majorana operators
\hat{\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{x}}=(\hat{\gamma}_{1})^{x_{1}}(\hat{\gamma}_{2})^{x_{2}}...(\hat{\gamma}_{2L})^{x_{2L}}
, where
\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{2L}
is a binary vector whose components indicate which Majorana operators are present (or not) in the monomial. Majorana monomials are in one to one correspondence with Pauli strings, forming a complete orthogonal basis as well, since
\text{Tr}[(\hat{\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}})^{{\dagger}}\hat{\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{x}}]=D\delta_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{x}}
. A unitary operator
\hat{U}
is Gaussian if and only if it acts on the Majorana operators by rotating the vector of
\hat{\gamma}_{\mu}
as

\hat{U}^{{\dagger}}\hat{\gamma}_{\mu}\hat{U}=\sum_{\nu}O_{\mu\nu}\hat{\gamma}_{\nu}\,,

(1)

with
O\in SO(2L)
a real orthogonal matrix, corresponding to a Bogoliubov transformation
[
13
]
. The Gaussian unitary group, denoted as
{\mathcal{U}_{G}}
, is generated by the algebra of Majorana fermion pairs
\hat{\gamma}_{\mu}\hat{\gamma}_{\nu}
. Eq. (
1
) implies that all Gaussian operators commute with the parity operator
{\hat{\mathcal{P}}}=(-i)^{L}\hat{\gamma}_{1}\hat{\gamma}_{2}...\hat{\gamma}_{2L}={\hat{\text{Z}}}_{1}...{\hat{\text{Z}}}_{L}
.
The set of Gaussian states is defined as
[
15
]
:
{\mathcal{G}}_{L}=\{\hat{\rho}=\hat{U}^{{\dagger}}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{L}\left(\frac{\hat{\mathbb{1}}+\lambda_{i}{\hat{\text{Z}}}}{2}\right)\hat{U}\,,\,\,\hat{U}\in{\mathcal{U}_{G}}\ ,\lambda_{i}\in[-1,+1]\}
. It includes also non pure states, while setting all the coefficients
\lambda_{i}
to
1
restricts the ensemble to pure Gaussian states
{\mathcal{G}}_{L}^{\text{pure}}=\{\hat{U}\ket{0...0}\,,\hat{U}\in{\mathcal{U}_{G}}\}
, which are generated from the vacuum by the action of a Gaussian operator. Gaussian states are fully characterized by the real and skew-symmetric covariance matrix
[
97
]

\Gamma_{\mu\nu}(\hat{\rho})=-\frac{i}{2}\text{Tr}([\hat{\gamma}_{\mu},\hat{\gamma}_{\nu}]\hat{\rho})\,,

(2)

which contains all two-points Majorana correlation functions. When applying
\hat{U}\in{\mathcal{U}_{G}}
to a state
\hat{\rho}
, the covariance matrix rotates accordingly as
\Gamma(\hat{U}^{{\dagger}}\hat{\rho}\hat{U})=O\Gamma O^{T}
.
\Gamma
can be readily expressed in terms of the standard fermionic correlation matrix
C_{\mu\nu}(\hat{\rho})=\text{Tr}(\hat{\mathbb{c}}_{\mu}^{{\dagger}}\hat{\mathbb{c}}_{\nu}\hat{\rho})
, where
\hat{\mathbb{c}}
is defined by

\hat{\mathbb{c}}_{2i-1}=\hat{c}_{i}
,
\hat{\mathbb{c}}_{2i}=\hat{c}_{i}^{{\dagger}}
. In particular,
\Gamma_{\mu\nu}(\hat{\rho})=-i\left(2\Omega^{*}C(\hat{\rho})\Omega^{T}-\mathbb{1}\right)_{\mu\nu}
where
\Omega=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{L}\Omega^{(i)}
is the unitary matrix that transforms the
\hat{\mathbb{c}}
into
\hat{\gamma}

[
97
]
. For the vacuum state
\ket{0...0}
, the covariance matrix reads

\Gamma_{0}=\Gamma(\ket{0...0})=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{L}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\,.

The celebrated Wick’s theorem enables the calculation of any correlation function in terms of two-point correlation functions, and can be formulated as follows
[
97
]

\text{Tr}(\hat{\rho}\,\hat{\gamma}^{\boldsymbol{x}})=i^{\frac{|\boldsymbol{x}|}{2}}{\text{Pf}}[\Gamma|_{\boldsymbol{x}}]\,,

(3)

where
\Gamma|_{\boldsymbol{x}}
denotes the square sub-matrix of
\Gamma
that includes all rows and columns associated with indices equal to 1 in
\boldsymbol{x}
, and
|\boldsymbol{x}|
represents the total count of such indices. Finally, we define particle-number-preserving Gaussian transformations as those that commute with the total particle number operator
\hat{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\hat{c}^{{\dagger}}_{i}\hat{c}_{i}
. Observe that any particle number-preserving transformation must map the vacuum state
\ket{0...0}
into itself. This imposes the condition
O\Gamma_{0}O^{T}=\Gamma_{0}
, where
O
is the orthogonal transformation corresponding to
U
. In fact, it can be easily show that
O\in O(2L)
represents a number preserving transformation iff it commutes with
\Gamma_{0}
, meaning
O
is symplectic
[
67
]
. The set of pure fermionic Gaussian states with
N
particles is
{\mathcal{G}}_{L,N}^{\text{pure}}=\{\hat{U}\ket{\boldsymbol{z}}\,,\hat{U}\in{\mathcal{U}_{G}},\,[\hat{U},\hat{N}]=0,\,\sum_{i=1}^{L}z_{i}=N\}
.

Characteristic distribution and Stabilizer Rényi Entropies (SREs). —

Given an Hermitian operator
\hat{O}
and a Pauli string
\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}
, we define

\pi_{O}(\boldsymbol{P})\equiv\frac{1}{D}\frac{\text{Tr}[\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}\hat{O}]^{2}}{\text{Tr}[\hat{O}^{2}]}\,.

(4)

Over all Pauli strings,
\pi_{O}
forms a length-
D^{2}
vector with elements summing to 1. In fact, completeness of Pauli operators gives
D^{-1}\sum_{\boldsymbol{P}}(\text{Tr}[\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}O])^{2}=\text{Tr}[\hat{O}^{2}]
, where
\text{Tr}[\hat{O}^{2}]
normalizes Eq. (
4
). Thus,
\pi_{O}
is a probability distribution, often called characteristic function
[
117
,
58
]
, capturing the overlap of
O
with all Pauli operators
[
107
]
.
The evolution of this distribution under generic many-body time dynamics has been investigated in the context of operator spreading, where an initially localized operator
\hat{O}_{0}
gains overlap with Pauli strings of increasing length
[
50
,
73
,
83
]
. Marginalized versions of
\Pi
are often used, such as distributions of operator lengths or the fraction of strings ending at position
i
.

We now examine the characteristic distribution of a fixed state
\hat{\rho}
(which might be mixed). For
\alpha>0
, the
\alpha
-Stabilizer Rényi Entropy (SRE) is defined as
[
58
]

M_{\alpha}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\sum_{\boldsymbol{P}}\pi_{\rho}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{P})-\log D\,,

(5)

and, excluding an additive constant, coincides with the
\alpha
-Rényi entropy of the distribution
\pi_{\rho}
. For
\alpha\to 1
, the SRE reduces to the Shannon entropy:
M_{1}(\rho)=-\sum_{\boldsymbol{P}}\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{P})\log\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{P})-\log D
. For all
\alpha
,
the SREs are trivially upper bounded by
\log D
, which however is in general a loose bound for pure states
[
58
]
. In addition to being a natural way of characterizing the distribution
\pi_{\rho}
(
4
), SREs have also proven to have substantial relevance in the context of quantum information as measures of nonstabilizerness, aka magic. In a nutshell, nonstabilizerness is the resource required for quantum states to be unattainable through simple Clifford circuits (plus Pauli measurements). The latter are regarded as easy to implement “free” operations, while non-Clifford gates are resources. Recall that the Clifford group
\mathcal{C}_{L}
includes the unitary transformations
\hat{U}_{c}
that map Pauli strings into Pauli strings under conjugation, i.e.
\hat{U}_{c}^{{\dagger}}\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}\hat{U}_{c}=e^{i\theta\frac{\pi}{2}}\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\prime}
(
\theta\in\{0,1,2,3\}
) for all
\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}
, where
e^{i\theta\frac{\pi}{2}}
is an irrelevant phase. Pure stabilizer states, denoted as STAB, are those derived from the reference state

\ket{0...0}
through Clifford transformations, and therefore they carry no resource. SREs are widely regarded as effective measures of nonstabilizerness, particularly for
\alpha\geq 2
, where their monotonicity is rigorously established
[
57
,
45
]
. Besides, SREs provide useful bounds for other measures of magic
[
57
]
.

In Ref.
[
107
]
, it a slightly modified version of the SREs, named
Filtered Stabilizer Rényi Entropies
(filtered SREs), has been introduced in which the contribution of the identity
\hat{I}=\hat{\mathbb{1}}^{\otimes L}
is removed from the sum in Eq. (
5
). This prevents dominance of this contribution for large
L
and
\alpha>2
, enabling filtered SREs to distinguish typical from atypical states, unlike standard SREs.

A powerful method for evaluating SREs involves sampling in the Pauli basis
[
53
,
54
]
. In fact, the argument of the logarithm in Eq. (
5
) can be rewritten as
\sum_{P}\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{P})\pi_{\rho}^{\alpha-1}(\boldsymbol{P})
, which represents the expected value of
\pi_{\rho}^{\alpha-1}
. Consequently, the SREs can be estimated by sampling strings
\boldsymbol{P}
with probability
\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{P})
, and averaging
\pi_{\rho}^{\alpha-1}(\boldsymbol{P})

(
\log\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{P})
for the case
\alpha=1
)
over these samples (
a detailed description of the averaging procedure is provided in Appendix

1
).

The statistical estimator obtained in this way clearly provides an unbiased estimate of the argument inside the logarithm in Eq. (
5
) for
\alpha>1
, from which one can compute
M_{\alpha}
. For the special case
\alpha=1
, the estimator directly yields
M_{1}
. It is crucial to characterize how the fluctuations (variance) of the estimator scale with the relevant parameters. First, since the estimation is obtained through a standard sample average, the fluctuations decrease proportionally to the inverse square root of the number of samples.

Hoverer, as discussed in Ref.
[
53
]
, the number of samples required to estimate
M_{\alpha}
with a given accuracy depends on the Renyi index
\alpha
. Specifically, for
\alpha=1
, the number of required samples scales at most as
L^{2}
, demonstrating that the Shannon stabilizer entropy can be efficiently estimated through sampling. In contrast, for
\alpha>1
, the number of required samples scales exponentially with the system size
L
in the worst-case scenario. Although this exponential scaling could, in principle, pose significant limitations, in practice, growth has been observed to be relatively mild
[
53
,
54
]
. Moreover, since sampling is a highly parallelizable task, it is possible to significantly increase the number of samples using parallel computation. We refer to Ref.
[
53
]
for further discussions on the efficiency of the sampling.

While
the sampling approach is general
, it has been successfully applied to cases where the state
\ket{\psi}
is an MPS, for which an exact and efficient sampling is possible
[
53
,
54
]
. In this case, alternative methods for computing SREs, primarily based on the replica approach
[
44
,
45
,
101
]
, are also available. However, MPS have fundamental limitations, specifically in the amount of entanglement they can capture, which makes it challenging to apply these methods to study the many-body dynamics or 2D systems. Therefore, developing methods to compute the magic of a state, independent of its entanglement, is of paramount importance. In the following, we tackle this challenge by applying the sampling estimation to fermionic Gaussian states, for which we develop a novel, ad hoc sampling algorithm.

Before of detailing this sampling scheme, let us however describe a fundamental limitation of the method. Recently, it has been observed that there exists a class of states, known as
pseudo-magic states
, which possess only a limited amount of magic (for instance, scaling logarithmically with the system size) yet are indistinguishable from highly magic states (e.g. with magic scaling linearly in the system size) for any computationally bounded observer
[
42
]
. In particular, no measurement scheme implementable in polynomial time can distinguish between these two ensembles. Since the proposed sampling from
\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{P})
is such a polynomial-time procedure, it follows that this protocol cannot discriminate efficiently pseudo-magic states from genuinely magic ones. Nevertheless, the physical relevance of the notion of pseudo-magic, or more generally
of pseudo-resources
[
1
]
, remains an open
question. Indeed, many of the currently known constructions (e.g. subset-phase-states) are unstructured and do not incorporate fundamental physical
principles such as locality, finite energy, etc.

SREs for Fermionic Gaussian states. —

We consider the problem of accessing and characterizing the probability distribution
\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{P})
of a fermionic Gaussian state
\rho
, given its known covariance matrix
\Gamma
. For instance, one might be interested in computing the SREs
M_{\alpha}(\rho)
associated with the distribution. More generally, one might seek to explore the distribution
\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{P})
itself. First, we notice that since there exist a one-to-one mapping between Majorana monomials and Pauli strings, the distribution
\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})=D^{-1}|\text{Tr}[\hat{\rho}\gamma^{\boldsymbol{x}}]|^{2}/\text{Tr}[\hat{\rho}^{2}]
is perfectly equivalent to
\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{P})
.
Now, using the Wick theorem and the fact that the square of the Pfaffian equals the determinant, we obtain

\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{\det[\Gamma|_{\boldsymbol{x}}]}{\det[\mathbb{1}+\Gamma]}\,,

(6)

where the denominator is a known expression for the purity of a fermionic Gaussian state
[
97
]
. Eq. (
6
) shows that the distribution
\pi_{\rho}
defines a Determinantal Point Processes (DPP) over a system of
2L
bits, whose configurations are labeled by
\boldsymbol{x}
. DPP are a prominent class of probabilistic models, in which the probability of a given configuration is represented by the determinant of a sub-matrix —specifically, a principal minor—of a fixed kernel matrix
K

[
95
,
52
,
75
]
. DPP were initially developed to describe the distribution of fermions in thermal equilibrium
[
62
]
, and later applied across a remarkable range of fields. In particular, they find significant applications in random matrix theory and machine learning
[
52
]
. The most extensively studied case is when
K
is positive semi-definite, a setting for which well-established sampling strategies are available
[
47
,
95
]
. However, more general scenarios in which
K
is non-symmetric can also be considered, since any matrix whose principal minors are nonnegative defines a DPP
[
36
]
. In Eq. (
6
), for instance, the kernel is the real skew-symmetric matrix
\Gamma
, implying positive correlations, while standard DPPs are limited to negative correlations
[
36
]
.
In the following Section, we consider the DPP Eq. (
6
) and we introduce a novel, efficient method to sample configurations
\boldsymbol{x}
, i.e. Majorana monomials, accordingly. Our method differs from standard Monte Carlo approaches, as it does not rely on a Markov chain. It is instead a perfect sampler, meaning that our Algorithm can sample directly from Eq. (
6
), with zero autocorrelation time. Technically, our method closely resembles the iterative approach introduced in Ref.
[
56
]
for generic (non-symmetric) DPPs.

By using this sampling algorithm, one can analyze the distribution
\pi_{\rho}
and estimate the SREs as described above. For practical reasons, we adopt a filtered version of the SREs, excluding the contributions of the identity
\hat{I}=\hat{\mathbb{1}}^{\otimes L}
and parity operators
{\hat{\mathcal{P}}}=(-i)^{L}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}...\gamma_{2L}={\hat{\text{Z}}}_{1}...{\hat{\text{Z}}}_{L}
, which are trivial for pure fermionic Gaussian states (
\braket{\psi|\hat{I}|\psi}^{2}=\braket{\psi|{\hat{\mathcal{P}}}|\psi}^{2}=1
, for all
\ket{\psi}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{L}^{\text{pure}}
). In particular, we define

\tilde{M}_{\alpha}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\sum_{\boldsymbol{P}}\tilde{\pi}_{\rho}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{P})-\log(D -2)\,,

(7)

with the probability distribution
\tilde{\pi}
defined by

\tilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{P})\propto\begin{cases}0\text{ if }\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}=\hat{I},{\hat{\mathcal{P}}}\\
\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{P})\text{ otherwise}\\
\end{cases}

(8)

The filtered SREs in Eqs. (
7
) and (
8
) are motivated, as in Ref.
[
107
]
, by the need to remove trivial contributions from the operatorial entropies, which lead to anomalously large weights for typical states. The only difference with Ref.
[
107
]
is that, for pure fermionic Gaussian states, we also remove the parity operator, which is a trivial symmetry of these states.
The filtered SREs in Eq.
7
inherit the monotonicity properties of conventional SREs when restricted to parity-preserving operations (even though, in principle, they could allow for a broader set of free operations than the standard SRE).
Details on the sample estimation of the filtered SREs and the associated sources of errors are provided in the Appendix
1
.

Majorana Sampling. —

Sampling from the set of Pauli strings
\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}
, or equivalently from the set of configurations
\boldsymbol{x}
, with a size of
D^{2}=2^{2L}
, may seem exponentially difficult at first glance. To overcome this challenge, we express the full probability in terms of conditional and prior (or marginal) probabilities as follows:

\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})=\pi_{\rho}(x_{1})\pi_{\rho}(x_{2}|x_{1})\cdots\pi_{\rho}(x_{2L}|x_{1}\cdots x_{2L-1})\,,

(9)

where
\pi_{\rho}(x_{\mu}|x_{1}\cdots x_{\mu-1})
denotes the probability that bit
\mu
assumes the value
x_{\mu}
, given that the string
x_{1}\cdots x_{\mu-1}
has already occurred at positions
1\dots\mu-1
, no matter the occurrences in the rest of the system (i.e. marginalising over all possible strings for the bits
\mu+1\dots 2L
). Thanks to these observations, one can generate outcomes and their associated probabilities by sequentially iterating over each binary variable and sampling each based on its respective conditional probability. The crucial point now is to find an efficient way to calculate the conditional probability at a generic step
\mu
of the iterative process, which reads
\pi_{\rho}(x_{\mu}|x_{1}\cdots x_{\mu-1})=\frac{\pi_{\rho}(x_{1}\cdots x_{\mu})}{\pi_{\rho}(x_{1}\cdots x_{\mu-1})}
. We notice that (see (

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\pi_{\rho}(x_{1}\cdots x_{\mu})=\sum_{x_{\mu+1}^{\prime}...x_{2L}^{\prime}}\pi_{\rho}(x_{1}...x_{\mu},x_{\mu+1}^{\prime}...x_{2L}^{\prime})=\\
&=(\det[\mathbb{1}+\Gamma])^{-1}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}\delta_{x_{1}x_{1}^{\prime}}...\delta_{x_{\mu}x_{\mu}^{\prime}}\,\det[\Gamma|_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}}]\,,\end{split}

which essentially sums the determinants of all sub-matrices of
\Gamma

that have been fixed to 1. Interestingly, a known formula exists for this quantity

and is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\pi_{\rho}(x_{1}\cdots x_{\mu})=\frac{\det[(\boldsymbol{1}_{[\mu+1,2L]}\,+\,\Gamma)|_{(x_{1}\cdots x_{\mu},1...1)}]}{\det[\mathbb{1}+\Gamma]}\,,\end{split}

0

Connection with Inverse Participation
Ratios. —

For simplicity, we limit the discussion to pure states,
\hat{\rho}=\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}
, and consider the overlap between
\ket{\psi}
and a generic Fock state
\ket{\boldsymbol{z}}
. The probability
p_{\psi}(\boldsymbol{z})=|\braket{\boldsymbol{z}|\psi}|^{2}
, also known as formation probability
[
75
,
74
,
102
,
85
,
86
]
, quantifies the wave function’s spreading over the occupation-number basis, while the characteristic probability
\pi_{\psi}(\boldsymbol{z})
measures its spreading over the set of Pauli operators. Therefore, strong similarities between the two quantities are expected. Analogous to the case of operators, we can define the Participation Rényi Entropies (PREs) as

S_{\alpha}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log I_{\alpha}(\rho)\,,

(12)

where
I_{\alpha}(\rho)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}p_{\rho}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{z})
is known as Inverse Participation Ratio (IPR).
Note that, in this case, there is no need to subtract
\log D
, as in the SRE, nor to filter out any contributions. IPRs (and PREs) are powerful tools for distinguishing phases of matter, both with and without disorder, independent of system-specific observables, and have been applied across a wide range of models and settings
[
68
,
63
,
28
,
70
,
96
,
109
,
105
]
.

The PREs can provide bounds for the SREs
[
104
]
. For example, for
\alpha=2
, it is easy to show that
M_{2}(\rho)\leq 2S_{2}(\rho)
. Moreover, averaging the IPRs over all states in the same Clifford orbit, i.e. states obtained from
|\psi\rangle
via Clifford transformations, provides direct access to the SREs
[
108
]
. For instance, the quantity
F(\rho)=I_{3}(\rho)-I_{2}^{2}(\rho)
, which quantifies the flatness of the distribution
p_{\psi}(\boldsymbol{z})
, exhibits a linear scaling with
e^{-M_{2}(\rho)}
when averaged over the Clifford orbit of
|\psi\rangle

[
108
]
. In general, it is reasonable to expect qualitatively similar behavior from PREs and SREs in quantum many-body systems, as both quantify the entropy of a state’s participation over a fixed basis. However, for analytical treatment, we find it more convenient to work with the PREs. In particular, below we are able to analytically demonstrate the presence of logarithmic corrections in
L
, similar to those observed for the SREs.

In case of fermionic Gaussian states with fixed number of particles
N
, the wave function amplitudes can be written as Slater determinants. Specifically, given a set of
N
orthonormal vectors of size
L
(orbitals) stored as columns of an
L\times N
matrix
V
, one has

\braket{\boldsymbol{z}|\psi}=\det\left[V|_{\boldsymbol{z}}\right]\,,

(13)

where the multi index
\boldsymbol{z}
select the rows of
V
corresponding to occupied sites (i.e.
z_{i}=1
)
1
1

1

Note a slight abuse of notation: here, the multi-index selects only the rows of the matrix, whereas above, a similar notation was used to select both rows and columns.
. Averaging over states
\ket{\psi}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{L;N}
is thus equivalent to averaging over random isometry matrices
V
. Specifically, one has

\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim{\mathcal{G}}_{L;N}}[p_{\psi}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{z})]=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\text{Haar}}}[|\det\left[U|_{\boldsymbol{z}}]|^{2\alpha}\right]\,,

(14)

where
U|_{\boldsymbol{z}}
is a
N\times N
sub-matrix of an
L\times L
unitary (Haar) matrix
U
. The statistics of eigenvalues of sub-matrices of Haar matrices is well known in random matrix theory
[
118
,
82
,
66
]
, therefore this average can be expressed explicitly as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\text{Haar}}}[|\det\left[U|_{\boldsymbol{z}}]|^{2\alpha}\right]=\frac{1}{Z_{L,N}}\int_{|\lambda_{i}|<1}d\boldsymbol{\lambda}\,F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\\
&F(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}|\lambda_{i}|^{2\alpha}(1-|\lambda_{i}|^{2})^{L-N-1}\prod_{i<j}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}\,,\end{split}

(15)

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{N})
are the eigenvalues of
U|_{\boldsymbol{z}}
, the integral is over the unit disk in the complex plane and
Z_{L,N}
is a suitable normalization constant. This expression can also be interpreted as the partition function of a gas of particles interacting through a logarithmic potential and confined by an external field
[
71
,
10
,
17
]
. The integrals can be performed analytically by rewriting the full expression as a Vandermonde determinant
[
17
]
. The final result is the following exact expression for the average IPRs (see Appendix
2
for further details on the calculation)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim{\mathcal{G}}_{L,N}^{\text{pure}}}[I_{\alpha}(\rho)]=\binom{L}{N}\prod_{j=0}^{\alpha-1}\frac{(j+N)!(j+L-N)!}{j!(j+L)!}\,.\end{split}

(16)

The factor
\binom{L}{N}
comes from counting the number of binary strings
\boldsymbol{z}
of length
L
that contain exactly
N
occupied sites (i.e.
N
entries equal to
1
). By using the approximation
\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim{\mathcal{G}}_{L,N}^{\text{pure}}}[S_{\alpha}]\simeq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim{\mathcal{G}}_{L,N}^{\text{pure}}}[I_{\alpha}(\rho)]
and expanding the resulting expression for large
L
and fixed
n=N/L
and
\alpha
, one finds

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim{\mathcal{G}}_{L,N}^{\text{pure}}}[S_{\alpha}]\simeq&\,L\left(-n\log n-(1-n)\log(1-n)\right)+\\
&-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}\log L+c(n;\alpha)\,,\end{split}

(17)

where
c(n;\alpha)
is a constant which does not depend on
L
. The first term, which is extensive in
L
, represents the leading contribution and can also be derived using a saddle-point approximation of the integral Eq.

(similar to the approach used in Refs.
[
71

10
]
for the entanglement entropies). The second term is instead a correction which is logarithmic in the system size
L
,
exactly as observed for the SREs (see inset in Fig.
1

[
106

and has been previously observed in both the fermionic frame potential
[
106

and the PREs of critical quadratic fermion systems
[
102
]
.
The similarity between PREs and SREs is evident in Fig.

\ket{\psi}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{L,N}^{\text{pure}}
and we
plot the average 2-SRE, obtained numerically via Majorana sampling, alongside the analytically derived average 2-PRE.

Interestingly, while numerous studies have investigated IPRs in disordered systems, most have focused on the single-particle sector, with only a few addressing
I_{\alpha}
for states of many non-interacting particles
[
70

. Notably, to the best of our knowledge, the expression Eq. (

2D fermionic systems. —

Until now, the study of magic has primarily been limited to 1D systems, as existing methods for computing the SREs (e.g. those proposed in Refs.
[
53
,
99
]
) rely on tensor networks, which face significant limitations in higher dimensions. In fact, representing typical many-body wave functions using MPS requires scaling the bond dimension exponentially with the linear system size
\ell

[
100
]
offer a possible alternative, they are hindered by the approximate nature of the state representation and autocorrelation time. The Majorana sampling method we introduced overcomes these challenges, as it is unaffected by higher dimensionality or entanglement growth. As a paradigmatic example, we examine the SREs in the ground state of a topological superconductor on a 2D square lattice of size
\ell\times\ell
. In momentum space, the Hamiltonian reads
[
6
,
21

94

\Delta_{\bm{k}}^{*}&-\epsilon_{\bm{k}}\end{pmatrix}\,,

(19)

where
\hat{\Psi}^{{\dagger}}_{\bm{k}}=(\hat{c}^{{\dagger}}_{\bm{k}},\hat{c}_{-\bm{k}})
,
\bm{k}=(k_{x},k_{y})
,
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}=-(\mu-4t)-2t[\cos(k_x)+\cos(k_y)]
, and
\Delta_{\bm{k}}=2i\Delta[\sin(k_x)+i\sin(k_y)]

For
\Delta=0

M_{1}/\ell^{2}

These jumps result from the discreteness of the modes that contribute to the ground state as the chemical potential varies. At

Conclusions and Outlook. —

We presented an efficient method for computing Stabilizer Rényi Entropies (SREs) of fermionic Gaussian states, based on a novel algorithm called Majorana sampling. This algorithm, operating within the covariance matrix formalism, enables exact sampling of Majorana monomials with probabilities proportional to the square of their expectation values. Our approach serves as a powerful tool for investigating nonstabilizerness in free-fermionic states, regardless of their entanglement (which in general exhibits a volume-law scaling).
Through a detailed numerical analysis of random fermionic Gaussian states, we have demonstrated that these states exhibit nonstabilizerness features akin to those of generic Haar-random states, with only subleading corrections that scale logarithmically with the system size.
We substantiate these findings with analytical calculations for related quantities—specifically, the Inverse Participation Ratio (IPR) and the Participation Rényi Entropies (PREs) in the computational (or Fock) basis. We derive an exact formula for the average values of these quantities over random fermionic Gaussian states, showing similar logarithmic corrections.
Overall, our results demonstrate that matchgate circuits, despite carrying no resource if considered within the resource theory of Gaussian states, are highly effective at generating and propagating nonstabilizerness.
As an intriguing physical scenario, we applied our method to evaluate the magic in the ground-state of a two-dimensional non-interacting system of fermions. Here, topological properties are observed to influence the nonstabilizerness density of the system.

Looking ahead, several exciting avenues remain to be explored. One promising direction is to extend our approach to more complex systems in higher dimensions, where the interplay between entanglement and nonstabilizerness could uncover new insights into the phases of matter. Our method could also provide insights into the non-equilibrium dynamics of quantum magic in novel setups, where nonstabilizerness and quantum measurements interplay to give rise to new out-of-equilibrium phases of matter
[
81
,
35
,
9
]
.
Additionally, the Majorana sampling approach could be used to explore the distribution associated to a state over the operators in various ways, such as by analyzing the distribution of operator lengths.
In the end, our results suggest a promising way to understand the complex structure of quantum states and their complexity in many different physical systems, even when these states are just simple Gaussian fermionic states.

Details on the sample based estimation

In this section, we outline the process of estimating the Stabilizer Rényi Entropies (SREs) and filtered Stabilizer Rényi Entropies (filtered SREs) for a state
\hat{\rho}
after obtaining a batch of samples
\mathcal{X}=\{\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{{\mathcal{N}}}
via Majorana sampling (Algorithm
1
). By construction, samples are distributed according as
\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\sim\pi_{\rho}
. The algorithm also outputs the probability associated of each sample
\{\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{{\mathcal{N}}}
. To instead sample from
\tilde{\pi}_{\rho}
, it is sufficient to exclude from
\mathcal{X}
all occurrences of the identity
\hat{I}=\hat{\mathbb{1}}_{1}\dots\hat{\mathbb{1}}_{L}
and of the parity
{\hat{\mathcal{P}}}={\hat{\text{Z}}}_{1}\dots{\hat{\text{Z}}}_{L}
. Note that these Pauli strings correspond to the bit configurations
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=(0,0,\dots,0)
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}=(1,1,\dots,1)
, respectively. Note also that for large system sizes,
L\gg 1
, it is generally very unlikely to sample one of these strings, as both have exponentially small probabilities:
\pi_{\rho}(I)=\pi_{\rho}({\hat{\mathcal{P}}})=D^{-1}
. In any case, after filtering away
I
and
{\hat{\mathcal{P}}}
, one obtains a restricted batch of samples
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}=\{\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}}
, with
\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}\leq{\mathcal{N}}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)}\neq\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}
.
Now we set

M_{\alpha}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log( Q_{\alpha} )-\log D\,,\qquad\tilde{M}_{\alpha}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log( \tilde{Q}_{\alpha})-\log D\,,

(20)

which defines the quantities
Q_{\alpha},\tilde{Q}_{\alpha}
, to be estimated from the samples as
Q_{\alpha}^{\text{est}},\tilde{Q}_{\alpha}^{\text{est}}
. For the filtered SREs, we have

\tilde{M}_{\alpha}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}\tilde{\pi}_{\rho}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)-\log(D -2)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}\tilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\pi_{\rho}^{\alpha-1}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)-\log D\,,

(21)

where we used
\tilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})=\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\frac{D}{D-2}
,
\forall\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1}
.
Therefore
\tilde{Q}_{\alpha}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}\tilde{\pi}_{\rho}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x})\pi_{\rho}^{\alpha-1}(\boldsymbol{x})
, and the corresponding estimator is given by

\tilde{Q}_{\alpha}^{\text{est}}=\frac{1}{\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}}\pi_{\rho}^{\alpha-1}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)})\,.

(22)

For the original SREs instead, we have (restoring the contributions of
I
and
{\hat{\mathcal{P}}}
)

M_{\alpha}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}\pi_{\rho}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)-\log D=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\left(\frac{2}{D^{\alpha}}+\frac{D-2}{D}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}\tilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\pi_{\rho}^{\alpha-1}(\boldsymbol{x})\right)-\log D\,,

(23)

which implies
Q_{\alpha}=\frac{2}{D^{\alpha}}+\frac{D-2}{D}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}\tilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\pi_{\rho}^{\alpha-1}(\boldsymbol{x})
. In this case the estimator is therefore

Q_{\alpha}^{\text{est}}=\frac{2}{D^{\alpha}}+\frac{D-2}{D}\frac{1}{\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}}\pi_{\rho}^{\alpha-1}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)})\,.

(24)

For the particular case
\alpha=1
, in which the Rényi entropies reduce to the Shannon entropy, we have instead

\tilde{M}_{1}(\rho)=Q_{1}-\log D
,
M_{1}(\rho)=\tilde{Q}_{1}-\log D
, with

\tilde{Q}_{1}=-\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}\tilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\log\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\,,\qquad Q_{1}=-\frac{2}{D}\log\frac{1}{D}-\frac{D-2}{D}\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}}\tilde{\pi}_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\log\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x})\,.

(25)

Therefore the estimation goes as follows

\tilde{Q}_{1}^{\text{est}}=\frac{1}{\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}}\left(-\log\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)})\right)\qquad Q_{1}^{\text{est}}=\frac{D-2}{D}\frac{1}{\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}}\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{{\mathcal{N}}}}\left(-\log\pi_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{x}^{(i)})\right)+\frac{2}{D}\log D\,.

(26)

Notice that while
\tilde{Q}_{\alpha}^{\text{est}}
,
Q_{\alpha}^{\text{est}}
are always unbiased estimators for, respectively,
\tilde{Q}_{\alpha}
and
Q_{\alpha}
, when
\alpha>1
the logarithm one has to take in Eq.(
20
) to estimate possibly induces a bias in the estimation. Specifically,

M_{\alpha}^{\text{est}}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log( Q_{\alpha}^{\text{est}} )-\log D\,,\qquad\tilde{M}_{\alpha}^{\text{est}}(\rho)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log( \tilde{Q}_{\alpha}^{\text{est}})-\log D\,,

(27)

are not unbiased estimators for
M_{\alpha},\tilde{M}_{\alpha}

(this problem is not present for the case
\alpha=1
). One can correct the bias by Taylor expanding the logarithm, and obtaining the following correction

\displaystyle\begin{split}M_{\alpha}^{\text{corr.est}}(\rho)&=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(\log( Q_{\alpha}^{\text{est}} )-\frac{1}{2}\text{BIAS}_{\alpha}\right)-\log D\\
\tilde{M}_{\alpha}^{\text{corr.est}}(\rho)&=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\left(\log( Q_{\alpha}^{\text{est}} )-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{\text{BIAS}}_{\alpha}\right)-\log D\,,\end{split}

(28)

where

\text{BIAS}_{\alpha}=\frac{\text{Var}[Q_{\alpha}^{\text{est}}]}{(Q_{\alpha}^{\text{est}})^{2}}\qquad\tilde{\text{BIAS}}_{\alpha}=\frac{\text{Var}[\tilde{Q}_{\alpha}^{\text{est}}]}{(\tilde{Q}_{\alpha}^{\text{est}})^{2}}\,.

(29)

While the variance of the estimators is not known a priori, it can nevertheless be estimated from the data via the sample variance. To obtain the data presented in
Fig.
1
we indeed used the corrected estimators given in Eq.
28
. In Fig.
5
(upper panel), we plot the values of
\tilde{\text{BIAS}}_{\alpha}
for
\alpha=2,3
as a function of the system size
L
. While for
\alpha=2
the bias remains always modest, i.e. below
\sim 0.07
, for
\alpha=3
it grows much more, up to almost
0.5
for large
L
. This behavior reflects the difficulty in estimating well the exponentially small value of
\tilde{Q}_{\alpha}
with a limited number
{\mathcal{N}}
of samples.
While these values could suggest that our estimation of the nonstabilizerness is not precise, this is not the case. In fact, normalizing the bias by the leading contribution, i.e. computing
\tilde{\text{BIAS}}_{\alpha}/\log\left(\tilde{Q}_{\alpha}^{\text{est}}\right)
,
we find that this quantity remains small (
<0.01
), even for
\alpha=3
and large
L
(see lower panel in Fig.
5
). This shows that our estimation is indeed accurate. In a nutshell, although estimating the exponentially small
\tilde{Q}_{\alpha}
with a small relative error can be hard for
\alpha>1
and large
L
, this can have little impact on the
logarithm
of these quantities. Indeed, because
\tilde{Q}_{\alpha}
is already very small, even large relative errors in
\tilde{Q}_{\alpha}
result in only minor relative deviations in
\log\tilde{Q}_{\alpha}
.

Further details on the Inverse Participation Ratio calculation

Computing the average Inverse Participation Ration (IPR) for a random fermionic Gaussian state with a fixed particle number
N
reduces to evaluating
\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\text{Haar}}}[|\det(U|_{\pmb{z}})|^{2\alpha}]
, where
U|_{\boldsymbol{z}}
is an
N\times N
submatrix of a unitary Haar matrix
U
of size
L\times L
. Leveraging the known measure of the eigenvalues of a Haar submatrix, one obtains Eq.(
15
). Now, the idea is to rewrite the integrand

\prod_{i<j}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{N}|\lambda_{i}|^{2\alpha}(1-|\lambda_{i}|^{2})^{L-N-1}

(30)

as a determinant of a suitable matrix. To this purpose we first use the Vandermonde identity to obtain

\prod_{i<j}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}=\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_{i}^{*}-\lambda_{j}^{*})=\det_{1\leq i,j\leq N}[\lambda_{i}^{j-1}]\det_{1\leq i,j\leq N}[(\lambda_{i}^{*})^{j-1}]\,.

(31)

Now, we exploit the following version of the Cauchy-Binet identity
[
17
]

\int dx_{1}...dx_{N}\det_{1\leq i,j\leq N}[f_{i}(x_{j})]\det_{1\leq i,j\leq N}[g_{i}(x_{j})]\prod_{i=1}^{N}h(x_{i})=N!\det_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\big[\int dxf_{i}(x)g_{j}(x)h(x)\big]\,,

(32)

with
f_{i}(\lambda)=\lambda^{i-1}
,
g_{j}(\lambda)=(\lambda^{*})^{j-1}
and
h(\lambda)=|\lambda|^{2\alpha}(1-|\lambda|^{2})^{L-N-1}
. We thus obtain

\int_{|\lambda_{i}|<1}d\lambda_{1}...d\lambda_{N}\prod_{i<j}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{N}|\lambda_{i}|^{2\alpha}(1-|\lambda_{i}|^{2})^{L-N-1}=N!\det_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\big[\int_{|\lambda|<1}d\lambda\,\lambda^{i-1}(\lambda^{*})^{j-1}|\lambda|^{2\alpha}(1-|\lambda|^{2})^{L-N-1}\big]\,.

(33)

Switching to polar coordinates
\lambda=re^{i\theta}
, with
r\in[0,1]
, the integral becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{1}dr\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta\,r^{i+j-1}e^{i\theta(i-j)}r^{2\alpha}(1-r^{2})^{L-N-1}=2\pi\delta_{i,j}\int_{0}^{1}dr\,r^{2j-1}r^{2\alpha}(1-r^{2})^{L-N-1}\,.\end{split}

(34)

At this point, the determinant in Eq.(
33
) simplifies to a straightforward computation, as the matrix is diagonal. Moreover, the normalization constant
Z_{L,N}
is restored by evaluating Eq.(
33
) at
\alpha=0
. Combining these results, we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\text{Haar}}}[|\det\left[U|_{\boldsymbol{z}}]|^{2\alpha}\right]=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\int_{0}^{1}dr\,r^{2j-1+2\alpha}(1-r^{2})^{L-N-1}}{\int_{0}^{1}dr\,r^{2j-1}(1-r^{2})^{L-N-1}}\,.

(35)

All the integrals can be evaluated analytically, resulting in

\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\text{Haar}}}[|\det\left[U|_{\boldsymbol{z}}]|^{2\alpha}\right]=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\Gamma(j+\alpha)\Gamma(j+L-N)}{\Gamma(j)\Gamma(j+L-N+\alpha)}\,.

(36)

Expanding the product and applying successive telescopic simplifications, we arrive at the final expression

\mathbb{E}_{U\sim{\text{Haar}}}[|\det\left[U|_{\boldsymbol{z}}]|^{2\alpha}\right]=\prod_{j=1}^{\alpha}\frac{\Gamma(j+N)\Gamma(j+L-N)}{\Gamma(j)\Gamma(j+L)}\,.

(37)

Finally, we obtain Eq.
16
for the IPR
I_{\alpha}(\rho)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{z}}p_{\rho}^{\alpha}(\boldsymbol{z})
by introducing the factor
\binom{L}{N}
, which counts the number of binary strings
\boldsymbol{z}
of length
L
with
N
occupied sites. By expanding
\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim{\mathcal{G}}_{L,N}^{\text{pure}}}[S_{\alpha}]\simeq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\mathbb{E}_{\psi\sim{\mathcal{G}}_{L,N}^{\text{pure}}}[I_{\alpha}(\rho)]
for large
L
and fixed
n=N/L
and
\alpha
, we find
Eq.
17
. The constant
c(n;\alpha)
is given by

c(n;\alpha)=\frac{1}{2(\alpha-1)}\left(-\alpha\log(2 \pi)+2\log(G(\alpha+1))-\alpha^{2}\log(n (1-n))\right)

(38)

with
G
the Barnes G-function.
