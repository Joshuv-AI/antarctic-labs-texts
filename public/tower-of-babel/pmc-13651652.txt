Longitudinal radiomics of liver metastases during magnetic resonance-guided radiotherapy
Authors: Alina Paunoiu, Maksym Fritsak, Hubert S. Gabryś, Sebastian M. Christ, Matthias Guckenberger, Stephanie Tanadini-Lang, Riccardo Dal Bello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeMagnetic resonance-guided radiotherapy (MRgRT) offers high-quality soft tissue contrast and treatment monitoring, supporting longitudinal radiomics analysis. We investigated treatment-related radiomic changes during stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) for liver metastases and healthy liver.Materials and methodsFeatures were extracted from the gross tumour volume (GTV) and five 2-cm liver rings around the planning target volume (PTV) in 34 patients treated with five-fraction MRgSBRT. Features showing changes below baseline variability or high correlation (Spearman's |r| > 0.9) were excluded, with pre-treatment values averaged into a robust baseline. Linear regression slopes informed uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) followed by hierarchical density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (HDBSCAN) for patient stratification, with false discovery rate-corrected Kruskal–Wallis testing identifying representative features. Correlations with GTV volume and mean cumulative liver-ring dose were evaluated.ResultsOf 160 GTV features, 55 uncorrelated features remained. Three patient groups (n = 4, 10, and 20) with distinct longitudinal patterns were identified. Intensity-based energy showed significant differences across patient groups (adjusted p = 0.001), weakly correlated (|r| < 0.7) with volume in the two larger groups. In the innermost liver ring, 49 uncorrelated features were analysed. Two patient groups (n = 19 and 15) were identified, with intensity-based energy, mean intensity, and maximum intensity showing significant group differences, weakly correlated with mean cumulative dose.ConclusionsThis longitudinal MR-based radiomics framework identified GTV and healthy liver patient groups, with distinct treatment-related feature changes. This pipeline could be extended to integrate treatment response assessment in future studies.

Introduction

The liver is one of the most frequent sites of metastatic spread from several primary malignancies, particularly those arising from the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, breast, and lung. This is due to the liver's vascular anatomy and metabolic environment [1], [2].

For patients with a small number of liver metastases, classified as oligometastatic disease, surgical resection remains the standard of care when feasible, with reported 5-year overall survival (OS) of up to 50% in colorectal cancer [1]. However, approximately 80% of patients are not amenable to surgery due to either the location of the metastases or poor performance status. For these patients, stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) has emerged as a promising non-invasive alternative [3]. Common fractionation schemes include 45 Gy to 50 Gy delivered in three to five fractions, typically prescribed to the 65% or 80% isodose line [4].

Recent advancements in image guidance have further refined this approach, with the development of magnetic resonance-guided radiotherapy (MRgRT), enabled by the hybrid magnetic resonance linear accelerator (MR-linac) technology [5]. Such systems allow repeated MR image acquisitions, offering high-quality soft tissue contrast and real-time tracking capabilities to monitor changes over the course of treatment [2], [4], [6], [7], which can be leveraged for quantitative medical image analysis (radiomics). Radiomics has emerged as a promising approach for non-invasive cancer characterisation and refers to the extraction of a large number of quantitative features from medical images, such as computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography (PET), or MRI [1]. These features are broadly divided into morphology, intensity/histogram and texture features [8], [9], [10].

The potential of radiomics to identify prognostic imaging-based biomarkers has been demonstrated in several cancer types, including cancers of the liver, pancreas, rectum, and lung [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]. Boldrini et al. [13] evaluated delta radiomics extracted from the gross tumour volume (GTV) from patients treated with hybrid MRgRT for rectal cancer. They identified two features predictive of clinical complete response (cCR) (p = 0.001). Tomaszewski et al. [14] focused on histogram and texture features in pancreatic adenocarcinoma and showed that the ratio of histogram skewness between the first and fifth fractions strongly correlated with progression-free survival (PFS) (p = 0.005), suggesting its potential as an early response indicator. Jin et al. [15] analysed MRI-based delta-radiomic features to predict local control in liver lesions treated with MR-guided SBRT, reporting two texture features as the most predictive, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.72–0.90 depending on the biologically effective dose.

Despite the growing body of evidence, no study to date has characterised per-fraction longitudinal radiomic changes in both the GTV and the surrounding healthy liver tissue during MR-guided SBRT for liver metastases. We hypothesised that such treatment-related feature trajectories might reflect biological changes not captured by gross tumour volume alone and could be used to identify distinct patient groups with differing longitudinal response patterns. Building on this, we also aimed to evaluate whether these temporal patterns are associated with the GTV volume or the mean cumulative radiation dose to the surrounding healthy liver tissue.

Materials and methods

The workflow described below is summarised schematically in Fig. S1.

Patient cohort

Thirty-four patients with a single liver metastasis were included in the study, treated with MR-guided SBRT at the Department of Radiation Oncology of the University Hospital Zürich between December 2019 and April 2022. MR images were acquired with the 0.35 T ViewRay MRIdian system at the time of pre-treatment simulation (f0) and before each SBRT fraction was delivered (f1-f5) [20].

To account for histological heterogeneity of the primary tumour, we grouped the patients into four broader categories: ‘Breast & Gynecological’, ‘GI & Biliary’, ‘Lung & Head & Neck’, and ‘Other’. These groupings were defined with a clinical expert to ensure interpretability while preserving biological relevance. Patient characteristics are summarised in Table S1. The fractionation scheme was determined by the treating physician and ranged from 5 × 5 Gy to 5 × 9 Gy, all prescribed to the 65% isodose line. The majority of patients received 5 × 9 Gy (17/34), followed by 5 × 8 Gy (10/34), 5 × 7 Gy (5/34), 5 × 6 Gy (1/34), and 5 × 5 Gy (1/34) (Fig. S2). This retrospective analysis was approved by the cantonal ethics committee Zurich (BASEC-Nr. 2018–01794) in accordance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. All patients gave their consent for retrospective data analysis [21].

Imaging and radiomic feature extraction

In total, 204 MR scans, acquired using a True Fast Imaging with Steady State Precession (TRUFI) sequence [5], were analysed. Two scans were acquired at each fraction: the first to check and optimise patient positioning and the second to confirm correct positioning [2]. For our analysis, the first scan was used at each fraction.

All structures were manually delineated in the ViewRay treatment planning system by the treating physician, post-processed in MIM (version 7.1.6, GE HealthCare, Cleveland, USA) and exported for analysis. DICOM images were converted to NIfTI format using our software, Z-Rad (version 25.2) [22], [23].

Our analysis focused on the GTV and five three-dimensional concentric ring structures surrounding the planning target volume (PTV), representing the healthy liver tissue. These structures, hereafter rings, were generated using the NumPy, SimpleITK and SciPy packages in Python (version 3.12.2), with a width of 2 cm and cropped at the liver contour. The images were processed using bias field correction and z-score normalisation, also implemented using the SimpleITK and SciPy packages, followed by resampling from 1.5 × 1.5 × 3 mm3 to 2 × 2 × 2 mm3 isotropic voxels.

Radiomic features were extracted from the GTV and the five liver rings at all timepoints using Z-Rad. Image intensities for texture feature calculation were discretised using a fixed bin number of 32 bins. Texture features were computed in three dimensions and averaged across directions. Morphological features were excluded from the analysis of the liver rings, as the ring-shaped regions were artificially defined and do not represent actual liver morphology.

Image analysis

For the GTV-based features, we first established the absolute difference between f0 and f1 as a clinically relevant baseline, as the images at these two timepoints were acquired prior to treatment delivery. Features exhibiting smaller changes in subsequent fractions than this baseline difference were considered below baseline variability and excluded. To reduce feature redundancy, highly correlated features (Spearman's |r| ≥ 0.9) were removed using a sequential approach, in which each feature was compared against the remaining features and, once retained, any feature correlated with it above this threshold was excluded.

To obtain a robust pre-treatment reference, feature values at f0 and f1 were averaged into a single timepoint, referred to as f01. Temporal feature evolution was characterised using linear regression slopes, computed for each feature and patient.

Based on these slope values, we identified patient groups using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) followed by hierarchical density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (HDBSCAN) [24]. The optimal number of clusters was determined using the density-based cluster validation (DBCV) metric. DBCV is a validation metric that quantifies clustering quality by simultaneously assessing within-cluster density connectedness and inter-cluster separation. The score ranges from −1 to 1, with higher values indicating better-defined, separated clusters. The clustering solution with the highest DBCV score was considered optimal and used for the subsequent analyses [25].

Differences in feature values across patient groups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test with false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Adjusted p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The association between histological categories and the feature-derived patient groups was evaluated using a chi-square test.

To evaluate whether representative features were surrogates for changes in GTV volume or carried additional independent information, we calculated Spearman's |r| between each feature and the GTV volume feature across all fractions, per patient. Correlations were categorised as weak (|r| < 0.7) or strong (|r| ≥ 0.7).

For the analysis of the surrounding healthy liver tissue, the same feature processing and clustering pipeline was applied. Correlations were computed between significant features and the mean cumulative dose delivered to each liver ring, also using Spearman's |r|, to assess radiation-induced changes in healthy liver tissue.

In both cases, for each feature, we computed the number of patients per group showing strong (|r| ≥ 0.7) versus weak (|r| < 0.7) correlations with GTV volume or mean cumulative dose to the liver rings.

Discussion

By leveraging MRgRT to acquire MR images before each treatment fraction, we successfully applied a longitudinal radiomics method that identified distinct treatment-related changes in radiomic features extracted from both the GTV and the surrounding healthy liver tissue.

In the GTV analysis, HDBSCAN clustering based on temporal feature slopes identified three patient groups, with a global DBCV score of 0.58. When evaluating the two larger clusters (group 2, n = 10, and group 3, n = 20) separately, the DBCV score increased to 0.65, indicating improved cluster separation. The smallest cluster (n = 4 patients) partially overlapped with the other groups, consistent with the lower global DBCV score and suggesting greater internal variability or noise (Fig. 1). After testing multiple parameter settings, this grouping was found to be the most stable. The resulting imbalance in group sizes reflects the dominant longitudinal patterns captured by the larger patient groups.

Kruskal–Wallis testing revealed that only one GTV feature, intensity-based energy, exhibited a statistically significant difference in temporal evolution across the patient groups after FDR correction (adjusted p = 0.001, Fig. 2 and Fig. S4A). However, its biological interpretation remains challenging. Intensity-based energy is a non-specific imaging feature influenced by both signal distribution and ROI size. The group differences may reflect changes in tissue water content, vascularity, inflammation, cellular composition, or treatment-related cell injury, but these mechanisms cannot be distinguished using the non-quantitative TRUFI sequence and should therefore be presented as hypothesis-generating. Prescription dose could influence treatment-related GTV changes and thereby contribute to longitudinal radiomic trajectories. However, its influence could not be assessed due to the uneven distribution across dose regimens. The strength of the association between GTV intensity-based energy and volume was group-dependent, with predominantly strong correlations in group 1 and mostly weak correlations in groups 2 and 3 (Fig. 3A). Notably, in the two larger groups, the longitudinal behaviour of intensity-based energy is largely independent of macroscopic tumour size changes.

The same longitudinal radiomics pipeline was applied independently to each liver ring. Five concentric 2-cm-wide rings were generated at increasing distances from the PTV to assess spatially resolved treatment effects. This width balanced tissue volume and spatial separation from the target. We focused on the innermost liver ring, 2 cm from the PTV, as it is closest to steep dose gradients and most likely to exhibit radiation-related effects.

Similar to the GTV analysis, intensity-based energy emerged as the most discriminative feature between the patient groups (Fig. 5A and Figs. S6–S7) across all liver rings. Two other intensity-based statistical features, maximum intensity and mean intensity, and one texture feature, large zone high grey level emphasis, also showed significant differences between the groups. These features also exhibited weak associations with the mean cumulative dose to the liver rings (Fig. 6 and Fig. S8). This may reflect the relatively low average doses, the use of mean dose across large liver rings, and potentially nonlinear or delayed tissue responses. Technical variability from respiratory position and MRI acquisition, as well as concurrent systemic therapy or pre-existing steatosis/fibrosis, should also be acknowledged and may have influenced the findings in individual patients.

Despite increasing distance from the target volume (Fig. S1), at least one feature in each liver ring exhibited significant longitudinal changes. This suggests that treatment-related changes can be detected even in more peripheral liver tissue, capturing a broader treatment response that is not confined to high-dose regions.

This longitudinal radiomics framework could support several clinical applications. Treatment-related changes in the GTV or surrounding liver tissue warrant further investigation as potential imaging-based indicators of radiation-induced liver injury. Because feature changes in the surrounding liver were only weakly related to dose, monitoring these trajectories may capture treatment response information not explained by dosimetric data alone. In MR-guided adaptive radiotherapy, validated longitudinal radiomics trajectories could ultimately inform adaptive dose modification, for example prompting plan adaptation when healthy liver tissue shows early signs of change.

Our study has limitations. The cohort was relatively small (n = 34 patients). While it exceeds that of Jin et al. (n = 22) [15], which included primary liver cancers and metastases treated with MR-guided SBRT, our cohort consisted of only liver metastases from a broad range of primary tumour sites (Table S1 and Fig. S3), introducing further biological heterogeneity. The identified patient groups should therefore be considered exploratory, and a larger cohort would improve clustering performance and subgroup analysis. Furthermore, all images were acquired using the 0.35 T TRUFI sequence specific to the ViewRay MRIdian system [5]. Given the differences in field strength, signal-to-noise ratio, and image contrast, our findings may not directly generalise to conventional diagnostic MRI, 1.5 T or 3 T MR-Linac systems. Although preprocessing steps were implemented to reduce and account for baseline variability, the relative nature of MR signal intensities remains a limitation. Validation is therefore required before these changes can guide clinical decision-making.

Due to the absence of clinical outcome data, we were unable to assess how feature changes are associated with outcomes such as PFS or cCR, as previous radiomics studies have done [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19]. Although OS data were available, most deaths were attributable to the primary cancer, limiting the relevance of OS as an outcome in this study. In addition, as no toxicities above grade 2 were observed, correlation analyses with clinically relevant toxicity endpoints were not feasible in this cohort.

Compared with previous work [13], [14], [15], this study focused on the longitudinal evolution of features using their temporal slope values, enabling a more detailed characterisation of radiomic changes over time. We also extended our investigation to include the analysis of surrounding liver tissue, providing a more comprehensive view of radiation-induced effects beyond the GTV.

In conclusion, the identification of intensity-based energy as a driving feature in both the GTV and surrounding healthy liver analyses, with significant longitudinal differences between patient groups, suggests its relevance as a candidate imaging biomarker. Given the absence of clinical outcome or toxicity data in this study, however, this remains a hypothesis to be tested rather than a validated conclusion. This aligns with prior findings, such as those by Tomaszewski et al. [14], who also reported an intensity-based statistical feature (skewness) as predictive of PFS. Future studies incorporating clinical outcome and toxicity endpoints are needed to determine whether the longitudinal MR-based radiomics framework developed here can be incorporated into models predicting treatment response and radiation-induced toxicity.
