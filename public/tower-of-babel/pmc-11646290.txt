The role of CEACAMs in neutrophil function
Authors: Keith M Skubitz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn addition to the long‐known antibacterial actions of neutrophils, neutrophils are recognized to have a variety of other effects and are functionally diverse. Neutrophils can either stimulate or inhibit B cells and T cells, regulate NK development and activity, augment or direct the resolution of inflammation, act as myeloid‐derived suppressor cells, modulate tumour growth and metastasis and trigger autoimmune diseases. CEACAMs 1, 3, 6 and 8 are expressed on human neutrophils.MethodsA literature review was performed on the role of CEACAMs in neutrophil function.ResultsCEACAMs 1, 6 and 8 can be upregulated from intracellular stores, while CEACAM3, an opsonin‐independent phagocytic receptor, is constitutively expressed. CEACAM1 has an intracellular ITIM motif and an ITSM motif, and CEACAM3 has an ITAM‐like motif; CEACAMs 6 and 8 are glycosylphosphatidylinositol‐linked. CEACAM8 can also be released in a soluble form. These CEACAMs can interact with multiple other host CEACAMs as well as other molecules on bacteria, fungi and host cells, both transmitting and receiving signals. Known CEACAM‐binding pathogens bind the CFG face of the N domain which is also important in CEACAM‐CEACAM binding, although the ABDE face also appears to be involved in higher‐order oligomers.ConclusionsUnderstanding the exact role of each individual CEACAM in human neutrophils is complicated by the fact that the neutrophil CEACAMs can interact with multiple ligands. The data demonstrates some of the many roles of CEACAMs in neutrophil function and the extensive role of the neutrophil in human biology beyond its classical role as a short‐lived phagocyte.Keywords: CD66, CD66a, CD66b, CD66c, CD66d, CEA, CEACAM, CEACAM1, CEACAM3, CEACAM6, CEACAM8, neutrophil

Neutrophils, sometimes referred to as polymorphonuclear leukocytes, are the most prevalent circulating leukocyte. During an infection the neutrophil count in blood rises markedly. This reaction to infection was probably first identified by Thomas Hunter, a British surgeon during the battle of Belle Isle in 1761. 1 Hunter noted that when blood settled, a thin layer of material separated red cells from plasma, and in patients with active infection the layer became a thicker ‘buff‐colored inflammatory crust’, now recognized as a manifestation of neutrophilia. He also noticed that overtime blood developed a rancid smell termed ‘spoiling’, and that ‘spoilage’ could be delayed by adding the ‘inflammatory crust’ to blood, now understood to reflect neutrophil antimicrobial activity.

By 1890, it was known that injury led to neutrophil rolling along the endothelial surface, followed by attachment and subsequent transmigration across the capillary wall into the tissue where infecting organisms were engulfed by phagocytosis ( 2 and reviewed in1). During infection neutrophil production is increased, and mature neutrophils can be released into the circulation from a ‘marginating pool’, a population of neutrophils marginated largely in the pulmonary vasculature that may represent older neutrophils. 3

When a tissue infection develops, resident cells such as macrophages release cytokines that activate endothelial cells, causing circulating neutrophils to adhere to their luminal surface; thereafter, other chemoattractants result in neutrophil transmigration across the capillary wall to the site of infection. Neutrophils initially adhere to the capillary wall via selectin binding‐mediated rolling; neutrophil activation results in a conformational change of cell surface CD11/CD18, and CD11/CD18 upregulation, that then mediates tighter binding. Data suggest that ligand binding to the bent closed integrin conformation is responsible for headpiece opening to give the high‐affinity conformation. 4 Neutrophils then crawl to a site of egress either between or through endothelial cells, becoming more ‘activated’ with a heightened response to stimuli 3 (Figures 1 and 2). This process can be further regulated by factors released from both neutrophils and endothelial cells (Figure 2).

Neutrophils contain several recognized types of subcellular granules or vesicles (Figure 3). Azurophilic (primary) granules contain myeloperoxidase (MPO) and proteases such as elastase, as well as CEACAM6. Specific (secondary) granules are characterized by apolactoferrin and also CEACAM1 and CEACAM8. Gelatinase (tertiary) granules are marked by gelatinase. Secretory vesicles are marked by alkaline phosphatase. Granule release with stimulation adds granule membrane to the plasma membrane. An important role of the neutrophil is to circulate and search for invading pathogens and when encountered, to rapidly bind, phagocytize, kill and digest the pathogens. The classic mechanism of microbial killing involves assembling the components of the NADPH oxidase, provided by fusion of specific and gelatinase granules which generates superoxide and H2O2 (Figure 3). The combination of MPO and proteases, derived from the fusion of azurophilic granules, and H2O2 within the phagosome results in the potent microbicidal, HOCl. More recently a variety of other important physiologic roles of neutrophils have been identified. 3 , 5 , 6

In addition to the above described proinflammatory activities, neutrophils are now recognized to have a variety of other effects (reviewed in3) and are functionally diverse. 7 For example, neutrophils can release activating factors for B cells, inhibit CD4 T cell and B cell response to immunization, both stimulate CD4 T cells, but also inhibit CD4 T cell expansion, and upregulate PD‐L1. 8 In mice, antigen‐presenting, aged neutrophils from donor mice have been described that can augment inflammation in recipient mice. 9 A subset of CD62 low and CD11b high hyper‐segmented neutrophils form a class of myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (G‐MDSC) that can suppress T cell activation. Neutrophils can also regulate NK cell development, may be required for normal NK cell maturation, suppress NK cell tumour killing and enhance carcinoma cell dissemination in mice. 10 Neutrophils can also modulate tumour growth and metastasis; CEACAM1 associated with neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) has been reported to play an important role in this modulation. 11 , 12 Release of neutrophil IL‐26 may trigger autoinflammation in diseases such as pustular psoriasis. 13 Neutrophils may also promote the resolution of inflammation and protect epithelium by utilizing O2, producing a hypoxic environment, thus stimulating HIF1‐alpha signalling in the epithelium. Thus, neutrophils interact with many other cells as well as pathogens (Figure 4).

The CEACAMs form a family of 12 expressible genes in humans with CEACAMs 1,3,4,5,6,7,8,16,18,19,20 and 21 being expressed in various cells. CEACAM1, the primordial gene of this family, has the general structure of one IgV‐like domain and zero to three IgC‐like domains coupled to a transmembrane region with either a short or long cytoplasmic tail. CEACAMs are involved in a wide variety of physiological processes depending upon the tissue, ranging from cell adhesion, pathogen binding, regulation of insulin receptor levels, angiogenesis, immune regulation and hearing. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22

CEACAM1 exhibits homophilic adhesion and also heterophilic adhesion to CEACAMs 5, 6 and 8, and CEACAM6 exhibits homophilic adhesion and heterophilic adhesions to CEACAMs 1, 5 and 8 that appear to be mediated by the N domains. 23 , 24 CEACAM3 has no adhesion properties. 23 Reports have suggested that CEACAM5 homophilic adhesion may be via double‐reciprocal interactions of the N domain of one CEACAM with the internal A3 domain of the other. 25

Neutrophils have been shown to express 4 different CEACAMs recognized by CD66 monoclonal antibodies (mAb). CEACAM1, CEACAM3, CEACAM6 and CEACAM8 are recognized by CD66a, CD66d, CD66c and CD66b antibodies respectively; in early studies mAb to CEACAM8 were termed CD67 26 (Figure 5). CEACAM4 mRNA expression in neutrophils was more recently described. 27 The long isoform of CEACAM1 contains 2 ITIMs, more specifically 1 ITIM (V/L/IXpYXXL/V) and 1 ITSM (TXpYXXV/I), 28 , 29 and a β‐catenin binding site in the cytoplasmic tail. The cytoplasmic domains of CEACAM3 and CEACAM4 contain an ITAM‐like motif which differs slightly from the classical ITAM motif.

CEACAMs 1, 6 and 8 are activation antigens on neutrophils in that surface expression is upregulated following stimulation by several stimuli; there is very little detectable CEACAM1 expression on the surface of resting neutrophils. 26 , 30 , 31 Most of the neutrophil CEACAM1 and CEACAM8, which is attached to the membrane via a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchor, are present in the specific granules, with less on the resting plasma membrane. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 In contrast, intracellular CEACAM6 is found primarily in the azurophilic granules. 31 Specific granules, and also secretory vesicles and possibly gelatinase granules, are likely sources from which CEACAMs can be recruited to the cell surface with activation; azurophilic granules are also possible sources for recruitment, but their release requires stronger stimulation. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 Due to the fact that gelatinase granules co‐sediment with specific granules, and secretory vesicles co‐sediment with plasma membrane fractions in the typical subcellular fractionation technique, the exact CEACAM content in these compartments is not well defined. Secretory vesicles are the most readily released by stimulation. In contrast, CEACAM3 is expressed on resting neutrophils. CEACAM1 expression is increased on neutrophils in a variety of inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis. 35 Recently CEACAMs 1, 6 and 8 were found to be upregulated in patients with severe COVID‐19 infection; surprisingly, the upregulation was found in a CD16+ CEACAM1, 6, 8 + monocyte population, although the findings could represent upregulation in a ‘low density pre‐neutrophil’. 36 Given the varied and complex interactions of neutrophils with other cells and targets described above, these CEACAMs may have a multitude of different effects on neutrophil function.

One of the most extensively studied CEACAM functions is the interaction with certain pathogens. CEACAMs 1, 3, 5 and 6 bind a variety of bacteria species 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 ; adhesion to CEACAM‐expressing epithelial cells assists with colonization, via simple adhesion. One model is that certain pathogenic organisms developed the ability to bind CEACAM1 on epithelial cells as a step in adhesion and translocation to infect the host. As a compensatory mechanism, neutrophil CEACAMs evolved to counter these organisms by providing alternative binding sites neutrophils can then use to destroy the pathogens, (reviewed in.18, 22, 41, 42, 43).

While neutrophils can bind bacteria via host‐derived opsonins such as IgG or complement, some bacteria such as Neisseria can bind opsonin independently. Four CEACAMs, CEACAMs 1, 3, 5 and 6, have been shown to bind the Opa proteins of Neisseria gonorrhoeae; CEACAM8, despite its structural similarity, does not bind Opa proteins. 44 Neisseria gonorrhoeae bind epithelial cells initially via a pilus, and the Opa outer membrane adhesions then mediate tighter attachment that may assist tissue penetration. Opa binding to CEACAMs is via the CFG face on the N domain. 45 Many other bacteria also bind CEACAMs, although they have different surface proteins that interact with the CEACAM N domains including HopQ, CbpF, R28 and one that binds via a novel Ig‐fold subtype termed IgI3. 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 Most Opa variants bind CEACAM1 while only a small number bind to CEACAM3; all that bind CEACAM3 also bind CEACAM1. 44 In each case binding is to the CFG face. These observations are consistent with the hypothesis that during evolution, pathogens developed ligands to bind molecules like CEACAMs 1, 5 and 6 on host epithelial and immune cells to assist colonization and avoid the immune system, respectively, while the host evolved CEACAM3 as a defence mechanism against these pathogens (reviewed in18, 22, 41, 42, 43, 44, 52). While CEACAMs are commonly targeted as a binding site on epithelial cells by pathogens, soluble CEACAMs released by neutrophils may function as decoy receptors to interfere with epithelial binding, but could also interfere with the immune response to the pathogen via inhibitory signalling.

The role of CEACAM3 in neutrophil function is perhaps the most clearly defined among the CEACAMs expressed on neutrophils. CEACAM3 is expressed only in granulocytes, 53 and does not exhibit homophilic or heterophilic binding. 23 CEACAM3 appears to have co‐evolved with mucosa colonizing bacteria due to selective pressure and acts as a decoy receptor for bacteria that use other CEACAMs as a mechanism to adhere to and colonize epithelial surfaces. 18 , 22 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 52 In contrast to bacterial binding to other CEACAMs on mucosa, binding neutrophil CEACAM3 triggers phagocytosis and killing of the bacteria (reviewed in52 and44, 45, 54, 55, 56). CEACAM3 has one extracellular IgV‐like domain and a cytoplasmic tail containing an ITAM‐like motif that is necessary for phagocytosis. Known CEACAM3‐binding bacteria interact with neutrophils through the CFG face of the N domain; common CEACAM3 polymorphisms in humans map to the nonglycosylated CFG face, consistent with selective pressure from pathogens. 41 , 45 Upon ligation, CEACAM3 is phosphorylated in its ITAM‐like motif by SRC family kinases following receptor clustering, and this recruits SH2 domain‐containing proteins, leading to downstream signalling. CEACAM3 activation is further regulated by tyrosine phosphatase‐mediated inhibition via protein tyrosine phosphatase receptor type J (PTRPJ) and possibly others such as CD45 52 , 57 ; the balance in phosphorylation directs CEACAM3 internalization. 44 , 52 There is evidence that CEACAM3 can also mediate Opa‐dependent uptake of N. gonorrhoeae by a less efficient, tyrosine phosphorylation‐independent mechanism. 55 CEACAM3 binding results in neutrophil granule release and proinflammatory cytokine production, resulting in the recruitment of other neutrophils and superoxide production. 44 , 58 Bonsor et al. suggested that CEACAM3 does not form homodimers and exists as monomers on the cell surface. 59 Presumably, bacterial adhesion results in some clustering of CEACAM3, thus affecting cytoplasmic signalling.

CEACAM4 is structurally closely related to CEACAM3, but does not have a well‐defined function. CEACAM4 does have an ITAM‐like intracellular motif, that when fused with the extracellular domain of CEACAM3 triggers phagocytosis in a similar manner to CEACAM3. 27 While neutrophils express CEACAM4 mRNA, protein expression in neutrophils has not been well described. CEACAM4 mRNA has been found in medullary thyroid carcinoma cells. 60 Recombinant CEACAM4 has been reported to interact with PD‐L2 and VEGFR3 61 ; VEGFR3, encoded by the FLT4 gene, plays a critical role in lymphangiogenesis and is also expressed on osteoblasts and some macrophages. 62

CEACAM8 is recognized by CD66b mAb, linked to the membrane via a GPI anchor, and upregulated following stimulation. 26 In contrast to CEACAMs 1, 3 and 6, CEACAM8 does not bind known pathogens. CEACAM8 is stored in specific granules, and stimulated neutrophils secrete a soluble form of CEACAM8. 25 , 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 53 Since gelatinase granules associate with specific granules in the usual isolation technique, these and also secretory vesicles may also contain CEACAM8. CEACAM8 release can be stimulated by a variety of agents including extracellular chromatin and classic activators such as f‐met‐leu‐phe (FMLP); chromatin in particular triggers secretion but also new synthesis of soluble CEACAM8. 63 Soluble CEACAM8 has a variety of immunomodulatory activities. For example, by binding and triggering CEACAM1 on other immune cells, it can act as a co‐stimulatory factor for B cells 64 or as an inhibitor of the TLR2 response in lung epithelial cells. 65 Soluble CEACAM8 released by human neutrophils in the airway can inhibit the proinflammatory response by binding CEACAM1 on airway epithelial cells, reducing TLR2‐dependent inflammatory responses. 65 Neutrophil surface expression of CEACAM8 correlated with heart failure severity in the condition known as ‘heart failure with reduced ejection fraction’. 66

CEACAM6 is GPI‐linked, present in primary granules, and may be upregulated to the plasma membrane with stimulation where it localizes to membrane microdomains. 50 CEACAM6 is expressed in neutrophils and epithelial cells, and in inflammatory conditions CEACAM6 may be expressed on multiple cell types. For example, CEACAM6 is expressed on airway epithelial cells and this expression is increased in severe asthma. Concomitantly, neutrophils in the associated lamina propria also express CEACAM6, reflecting neutrophil activation. 67 CEACAM6 expression on tumours inhibits T cell killing and there is interest in this as an anti‐tumour target. 68 , 69 As described later, associations among CEACAMs 1, 6 and 8 may influence CEACAM1 function.

CEACAM1 is the most extensively studied CEACAM and has many roles in biology. In neutrophils, CEACAM1 is involved in several functions including regulating anti‐bacterial action, functioning as an MDSC, stimulating or inhibiting B cell and T cell function, binding PD‐1, regulating NK cell development, modulating tumour growth and metastasis, and augmenting autoimmune damage. 8 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 61 , 70 , 71 Activated neutrophils also play an important role in the biology of ischemia–reperfusion injury; in a mouse model of orthotopic liver transplantation, deletion of CEACAM1 in the recipient neutrophils augmented hepatocellular damage. 72 In a clinical trial, donor liver grafts with higher neutrophil CEACAM1‐L showed relative resistance to ischemia–reperfusion injury and improved hepatocellular function. 72

In rat neutrophils, binding of soluble CEACAM1 or antibodies to CEACAM1 causes a delay of spontaneous and Fas ligand (CD95L)‐induced apoptosis that requires tyrosine phosphorylation of CEACAM1‐L, suggesting that CEACAM1 influences the resolution of inflammation by prolonging the survival of rat granulocytes. 73 Murine CEACAM1 can at as an inhibitory receptor that inhibits granulopoiesis and lipopolysaccharide‐induced IL‐1B and ROS production in mouse neutrophils. 74 , 75 Antigen‐presenting, aged neutrophils have been described that can induce INF‐gamma release by CD4 T cells and these neutrophils can augment inflammation in recipient mice, representing another potential site of CEACAM‐mediated regulation. 9

CEACAM1 is in a dynamic equilibrium between monomeric, dimeric and higher‐order oligomers on the cell surface. 76 , 77 , 78 , 79 In the presence of other CEACAM1‐expressing cells, trans interactions also occur that can increase higher order oligomerization. 77 , 78 , 79 In human neutrophils, CEACAM1 (and also CEACAMs 6 and 8) associates with tyrosine kinase activity, including the SRC family kinases SRC, LYN and HCK, 80 , 81 and is a major phosphotyrosine‐containing protein in the neutrophil membrane. 82 CEACAM1 is activated by increased homotypic aggregation by either cis binding on the cell surface or trans binding between cells, resulting in phosphorylation in the ITIM domains of its cytoplasmic tail, which leads to downstream signal transduction. Phosphorylation of CEACAM1 Ser512 by PKA allows GSK3β to phosphorylate Ser508, and leads to binding of β‐catenin to a region spanning both CEACAM1 ITIMs. 83 β‐catenin binding required phosphorylation of both CEACAM1 and β‐catenin by PKA. Phosphorylation of Ser508/Ser512 affected the kinetics of both phosphorylation of CEACAM1 ITIMs by SRC and dephosphorylation by SHP2. 83 In rodent neutrophils, a CEACAM1 isoform with a short cytoplasmic tail lacking the ITIM motif can affect dimerization of the long tail isoforms, thus regulating CEACAM1 signalling. While the ratio of the long (CEACAM1‐L) and short (CEACAM1‐S) isoforms of CEACAM1 is important in signalling, CEACAM1‐S is not expressed in human neutrophils, in contrast to rodent cells. 84 Therefore, the potential interplay in signalling between the two isoforms present in rodents does not occur in human neutrophils. CEACAM1 has been shown to associate with CEACAM6 and CEACAM8 in human neutrophils, and it has been suggested that CEACAMs 6 and 8, which are GPI‐anchored, could play a similar role as CEACAM1‐S in neutrophils. 84

All known CEACAM1‐binding bacteria bind to the CFG face, and this face also appears to be the major site for CEACAM‐CEACAM interactions and hTim3‐CEACAM1 binding ( 38 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 55 , 56 , 57 , 76 , 77 , 85 , 86 reviewed in76). CEACAM1 is more stable in a dimeric state that stabilizes the CC′ and FG loop. 76 , 77 , 87 Studies of CEACAMs in solution revealed that CEACAM1 homodimers had a Kd of ~450 nM, CEACAM5 a Kd of ~1.3 micromolar, CEACAM6 a Kd of~60 micromolar, while CEACAM8 was a monomer in physiologic conditions; the Kd for the CEACAM6‐CEACAM8 heterodimer was ~2 micromolar. 87 Thus, the CEACAM6‐CEACAM8 heterodimer was energetically favoured compared with homodimers. In each case, binding was between the CFG faces of both CEACAMs. One caveat is that these studies involved only the N domains, and therefore it is possible that the presence of the other domains could alter binding. These findings support the suggestion that cell surface CEACAM6 and CEACAM8 could regulate both CEACAM6 and CEACAM1 activity with other CEACAMs. For example, CEACAM8 binding CEACAM6 on the neutrophil surface would reduce the availability of CEACAM6 to interact with other ligands either in cis or trans. This model is consistent with CEACAM6 homodimers and CEACAM6‐CEACAM8 heterodimers on the neutrophil surface. 87 Thus, differential upregulation of CEACAM6 vs. CEACAM8 with neutrophil activation could significantly alter the binding of both cell surface CEACAM6 and CEACAM8.

CEACAMs exhibit varying degrees of glycosylation both among cell types but also within a cell type, 84 and in addition, oligosaccharides are flexible units. 78 CEACAM1 in human neutrophils is heavily glycosylated and the major carrier of several Lewis x (Lex) structures, including lacto‐N‐fucopentaose III (LNF‐III) recognized by CD15 antibodies. 82 , 88 , 89 Carbohydrates on CEACAM1 may help protect the protein from proteases but also can interact with DC‐SIGN on dendritic cells via Lex residues, thereby potentially playing a role in immune regulation. 82 , 88 Lex was not found on CEACAM6. 88 Recent studies suggest the sites predicted to be associated with carbohydrate side‐chain modifications, as well as the amino acids on the ABED interface, are highly flexible, suggesting that dimer formation through hCEACAM1 CFG face interactions is not markedly altered by glycosylation, and that homo‐dimerization through the CFG face enables highly flexible ABED‐mediated interactions to form oligomers and higher‐order oligomers. 76 , 90 These data are consistent with the previous findings of Klaile et al. 78 , 79

In elegant studies using molecular electron tomography of soluble and liposomal membrane‐associated, glycosylated rat CEACAM1 constructs, it was shown that the CEACAM1 ectodomain is highly flexible and takes part in both anti‐parallel (trans) and parallel (cis) homophilic binding. 78 , 79 Membrane‐attached CEACAM1 ectodomains form microclusters, and this clustering involves all four Ig domains. CEACAM1 exists as a mixture of monomers, dimers and oligomers on the membrane surface, and trans‐binding of CEACAM1 N‐terminal domains increased formation of CEACAM1 cis‐dimers and changed CEACAM1 interactions within the microclusters. 78 , 79 These studies led to a model wherein trans homophilic binding between N‐terminal CEACAM1 Ig domains on different cells increases cis‐dimer formation by an allosteric mechanism transmitted by the N‐domain and changes CEACAM1 interactions within microclusters. The balance of SH2 domain protein binding is dependent on the monomer/dimer equilibrium of CEACAM1‐L; co‐expression of CEACAM1‐S modulates CEACAM1‐L dimer formation. Thus, transmembrane signalling by CEACAM1 is modulated by alteration of the monomer/dimer ratio, leading to phosphorylation of the ITIM motif and changes in the ratio of bound SHP‐1/2/SRC. Therefore, transmembrane CEACAM1 signalling is mediated by adhesion‐regulated changes of cis interactions that are then transmitted into the cell via an allosteric mechanism. 78 , 79

CEACAMs can activate neutrophils, as shown by responses to antibodies in a number of studies. The presence of multiple CEACAMs on human neutrophils complicates assessment of their individual functions. Utilizing transfection of a murine neutrophil cell line that lacks Opa‐binding CEACAMs, 44 it was shown that each of the human CEACAMs 1, 3 and 6 bind and internalize N. gonorrhoeae, but internalization via CEACAMs 1 and 6 did not result in neutrophil activation. In contrast, CEACAM3 binding initiated the oxidative burst and granule release that required phosphorylation of its cytoplasmic ITAM. 44 Interestingly, this study also showed that CEACAM1 phosphorylation in these cells did not inhibit CEACAM3‐mediated neutrophil activation. Indeed, CEACAM1 and 6 potentiated rather than inhibited CEACAM3‐dependent neutrophil activation. This finding is in contrast to the observation that CEACAM1, which contains a cytoplasmic ITIM motif, inhibits T‐cell activation but is consistent with earlier findings that antibodies to CEACAMs 1, 6 and 3 can activate human neutrophils. 91 Thus, the background biochemistry of the cell is critical to the end result of CEACAM signalling.

Candida albicans also binds the N domain of human CEACAMs 1, 3 and 6, but not 8. 92 Antibody ligation of CEACAM6, and to a lesser degree CEACAM1 but not CEACAM3, caused the immediate release of stored CXCL8 and also altered transcriptional responses of neutrophils stimulated by C. albicans. 93 In contrast, CEACAM6 ligation and CEACAM3 ligation, but not CEACAM1 ligation, increased long‐term IL‐1B and IL‐6 release by neutrophils in response to C. albicans, demonstrating differential effects of different CEACAMs in regulating the response. 93

Signalling by many cell surface receptors on neutrophils requires extracellular Ca2+, and binding of a receptor to its ligand in the absence of Ca2+ can lead to selective desensitization to that ligand while maintaining sensitivity to stimulus by a different ligand. 94 , 95 This principle was used to study the effect of mAb to CEACAMs 1, 3, 6 and 8, suggesting that each could transmit an activating stimulus. In these experiments, mAb specific to CEACAM1 (CD66a), CEACAM3 (CD66d), CEACAM6 (CD66 c) and CEACAM8 (CD66d) stimulated upregulation of CD11b/CD18 and also adhesion to resting or TNF‐alpha‐stimulated human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). 91 It should be noted that TNF upregulates CEACAM1 on HUVECs. 26 Notably, it was shown that addition of an anti‐CEACAM antibody in the absence of extracellular Ca2+ did not stimulate neutrophil adhesion to HUVECs. If mAb was added to the neutrophil in the absence of Ca2+ and Ca2+ was replaced within 20 sec of adding the mAb, adhesion was similar to that seen when Ca2+ was present the entire time; thereafter, increasing time before addition of Ca2+ led to decreasing adhesion such that addition of Ca2+ 10 min after adding CD66 antibody resulted in no increase in adhesion. This effect was due to mAb binding to the neutrophil and not the HUVEC. Using this same approach, it was found that desensitizing a neutrophil to stimulation by one of the expressed CEACAMs did not affect stimulation by mAb binding to a different CEACAM. It should be noted that extracellular Ca2+/ Mg2+ may also contribute to CEACAM‐CEACAM interactions. 78 mAb binding to CEACAMs also increased adhesion stimulated by the bacterial peptide fragment N‐formyl‐met‐leu‐phe (FMLP), thus demonstrating direct signalling to adhesion molecules, and also modulation of signalling by a different receptor, in this case a G‐protein‐coupled receptor. FMLP receptors expressed on leukocytes mediate response to N‐formylmethionine‐containing oligopeptides released by invading microorganisms. Thus, the data suggested that CEACAMs 1, 3, 6 and 8 could each trigger a transient activation signalling complex that required extracellular Ca2+ at the time of CEACAM activation and regulate the adhesive activity CD11/CD18; these CEACAMs could also modify stimulation of CD11/CD18 by FMLP which acts via a different receptor.

Similar studies demonstrated that desensitization to any two of the four CEACAMs still allowed signalling by the other two. Desensitization to CEACAMs 3, 6 and 8 still allowed stimulation by CEACAM1, but after desensitization to CEACAM1 and two other CEACAMs, the remaining CEACAM was unable to stimulate neutrophils, suggesting a unique role for CEACAM1. 96 Coupled with the known binding interactions between CEACAMs, this led to a model in which neutrophil surface CEACAMs associated in dimers on the cell surface. 96 These findings appear somewhat discordant with the fact that CEACAM3 contains an intracellular ITAM‐like motif and CEACAM1 an ITIM and ITSM motif, in addition to the lack of clear binding of CEACAM3 to CEACAM1 in soluble form. This difference could relate to different effects with different assays. In addition, CEACAMs have been reported in lipid rafts (reviewed in50). CD63 activates neutrophils and has no clear signalling motif. Most neutrophil CD63 and CD11b/CD8 are in intracellular stores not associated with each other. However, upon stimulation, CD63 and CD11b/CD18 are upregulated to the surface and associate with each other in lipid rafts. 97 It is possible that CEACAMs undergo similar changes with upregulation. The relative densities of different CEACAMs within such microdomains may also be key in altering signalling that would occur in different settings, including after upregulation. In similar experiments, synthetic peptides from the N domains of CEACAMs 1, 3, 6 and 8 were also shown to activate neutrophils, as determined by upregulation of CD11b/CD18, down regulation of CD62L, and increased adhesion to HUVECs. 98 , 99 , 100 These data are consistent with a model where neutrophil surface CEACAMs form interactions both by homo and heterodimerization and higher‐order complexes with other CEACAMs (and in the case of CEACAM1 possibly by intramolecular associations as well) which can be altered by either antibody binding or by N domain peptides of CEACAMs. Notably, peptides derived from both the CFG as well as the ABDE face can transmit activation signals to neutrophils. These data are consistent with the model that the peptides disrupt pre‐existing CEACAM‐CEACAM interactions resulting in signalling. Such a model is supported by the structural studies of soluble CEACAMs and by electron tomographic studies of CEACAM1. 78 , 79 The demonstration of the expression of CEACAM4 mRNA in human neutrophils adds further complexity to any possible model.

Thus, CEACAMs 1, 3, 6 and 8 are expressed on human neutrophils and can interact with multiple other CEACAMs as well as other molecules on bacteria, fungi and other host cells, both transmitting and receiving signals (Figure 6). Understanding the exact role of each individual CEACAM in human neutrophils is complicated by the fact that four CEACAMs are expressed that can interact with multiple ligands. The occurrence of multiple splice variants of CEACAM1, which can be co‐expressed in multiple human cells, further complicates a straightforward understanding of their biology. The varied and complex interactions of neutrophils with other cells and targets described above demonstrates some of the many roles of CEACAMs in neutrophil function and the extensive role of the neutrophil in human biology beyond its classical role as a short‐lived phagocyte.
