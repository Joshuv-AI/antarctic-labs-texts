1.
Introduction and Background

Let
\gamma_{y}(n)
(resp.
\nu_{y}(n)
) denote the product of the (resp. the number of) distinct prime factors
<y
of
n
. The count of the number of distinct positive divisors of
\gamma_{y}(n)
, that is, the number of ordered positive integer solutions
(a,b)
to the equation
ab=\gamma_{y}(n)
is
2^{\nu_{y}(n)}
. Similarly, for
k>2
,
k^{\nu_{y}(n)}
counts the number of ordered
k
-tuples,
(d_{1},d_{2},\cdots,d_{k-1},d_{k})
of positive divisors of
\gamma_{y}(n)
such that
d_{1}\cdot d_{2}\cdots d_{k-1}\cdot d_{k}=\gamma_{y}(n)
.
Hence
(-k)^{\nu_{y}(n)}
counts these tuples with a sign (positive or negative) depending on the parity (even or odd) of
\nu_{y}(n)
. In this paper, we discuss the asymptotic behavior of the sum

(1.1)

\displaystyle S_{-k}(x,y):=\sum_{n\leq x}(-k)^{\nu_{y}(n)},

uniformly for
k+2\leq y<x
.

There are striking differences in the asymptotic behavior of
S_{-k}(x,y)
depending on
k=p-1
(for some prime
p
) and
k\neq p-1
(for any prime
p
) which is what prompted this study. The lower bound
k+2
for
y
is to ensure that
all primes
p\leq k+1
are accounted for in the study of
\nu_{y}(n)
. The analysis of
S_{-k}(x,y)
involves a careful study of the asymptotic behavior separately for “small
y
” and “large
y
”. The terms “small” and “large” used in a technical sense will be made precise later. For small
y

we use the Perron integral to establish the asymptotic behavior of
S_{-k}(x,y)
, and for large
y
, we employ the Buchstab-de Bruijn iteration method to asymptotically estimate
S_{-k}(x,y)
by induction on
\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
where
\alpha=\log x/\log y
. It turns out that the two regions for small and large
y

overlap and thus we can determine the asymptotic behavior of
S_{-k}(x,y)
uniformly for
k+2\leq y\leq x
by comparing the estimates in these regions.

When
k=p-1
, the main term in the asymptotic estimate of
S_{-k}(x,y)
for small
y
vanishes and we only get an upper bound using Perron’s method in this region, but we do get a uniform asymptotic estimate of
S_{-k}(x,y)
for large
y

using the de Bruijn-Buchstab iteration. When
k\neq p-1
, the main term does not vanish for small
y
and so we obtain asymptotic estimates for
k+2\leq y\leq x
. To understand these
intricacies in the asymptotic behavior of
S_{-k}(x,y)
, we need to describe certain earlier results as a background.

1.1.
Sums of the Möbius function

The fundamental tool used to study the parity of the number of prime factors
\nu(n)
on the square-free numbers is the Möbius function
\mu(n)
which is defined as
\mu(n)=(-1)^{\nu(n)}
, when
n
is square-free, and 0 otherwise. Bounds for
M(x)
, the sum of
\mu(n)
for
1\leq n\leq x
, are closely tied to the error term in the strong form of the Prime Number Theorem (PNT), and to the Riemann Hypothesis as well (see Tenenbaum
[
13
]
). Motivated by this strong link, Alladi
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
studied the asymptotic behavior of the following two sums:

M(x,y)=\sum_{n\leq x,p(n)>y}\mu(n),

where
p(n)
is the smallest prime factor of
n
if
n>1
, and

p(1)=\infty
, and

M^{*}(x,y)=\sum_{n\leq x,P(n)<y}\mu(n),

where
P(n)
is the largest prime factor on
n
if
n>1
and
P(1)=1
. Note that
M(x,1)=M^{*}(x,x)=M(x)
. The functions
M(x,y)
and
M^{*}(x,y)
are weighted versions of the well known functions
\Phi(x,y)
- the number of uncancelled elements in the sieve of Eratosthenes, and
\Psi(x,y)
- the number of integers up to
x
free of prime factors
>y
.

In
[
1
]
it is shown that if
\alpha:=\log x/\log y>1
, is fixed, then

M(x,y)=\frac{x\cdot m(\alpha)}{\log y}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x}{\log^{2}y}}}\right),

where
m(\alpha)
satisfies a difference-differential equation and is the derivative of the famous Dickman function
\rho(\alpha)
that occurs in the asymptotic estimate of
\Psi(x,y)
. Similarly in
[
2
]
it is shown that

(1.2)

M^{*}(x,y)=\frac{x\cdot f(\beta)}{\log y}+\frac{x\cdot m^{*}(\alpha)}{\log^{2}y}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x}{\log^{3}y}}}\right),

where
\beta=x/y
, and
m^{*}(\alpha)
is the derivative of the Buchstab function
w(\alpha)
that occurs in the asymptotic estimate of
\Phi(x,y)
. It turns out that
f(\beta)\to 0
rapidly as
\beta\to\infty
, and so the striking feature about
M^{*}(x,y)
is that when
\beta>1
is “finite”, that is when
y
is like a constant times
x
,
M^{*}(x,y)
behaves like
x/\log y
, but when
\beta
is large, which happens if
\alpha>1
is fixed, then
M^{*}(x,y)
behaves like
x/\log^{2}y
. It is this type of change in asymptotic behavior, that happens for
S_{-k}(x,y)
, but in a much more intricate manner, as will be seen below. We also point out that in
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
, uniform asymptotic estimates for long ranges of
\alpha
are established for
M(x,y)
and
M^{*}(x,y)
. See de Bruijn
[
6
]
and
[
7
]
for initial results related to the uniform asymptotic estimates of
\Phi(x,y)
and
\Psi(x,y)
respectively. A comprehensive survey of subsequent results on
\Psi(x,y)
and related functions is given in Hildebrand and Tenenbaum
[
10
]
.

1.2.
A multiplicative generalization of the sieve

Before discussing
S_{-k}(x,y)
for all positive integers
k
, we will state the results in Dhavakodi’s 1992 PhD thesis
[
8
]
for
S_{-1}(x,y)
, but to motivate this, we will first discuss a multiplicative generalization of the sieve due to the first author
[
3
]
.

Let
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be a subset of the positive integers, and let
\mathscr{A}(x):=\mathscr{A}\cap[1,x]
. Let
\mathscr{P}
be a set of primes and for
y>0
, define

P_{y}:=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathscr{P}\\
p<y\end{subarray}}p.

The classical sieve problem is to estimate the quantity

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}(\mathscr{A};\mathscr{P},x,y):=\sum_{a\in\mathscr{A},a\leq x,(a,P_{y})=1}1.

To suitably rewrite (1.3) for estimation, one considers

\mathscr{A}_{d}=\mathscr{A}_{d}(x):=\{a\in\mathscr{A}:a\leq x,a\equiv 0\pmod{d}\}

so that

\mathscr{S}(\mathscr{A};\mathscr{P},x,y)=\sum_{a\in\mathscr{A},a\leq x}\sum_{d|(a,P_{y})}\mu(d)=\sum_{d|P_{y}}\mu(d)|\mathscr{A}_{d}|.

Estimates are usually obtained (see
[
9
]
) under suitable assumptions on

\mathscr{A}_{d}
, such as:

|\mathscr{A}_{d}|=\dfrac{X\omega(d)}{d}+R_{d},

where
X
is an approximation to
|\mathscr{A}(x)|
,
\omega
is multiplicative, and the remainder
R_{d}
can be controlled
in an average sense. Since the divisors of
P_{y}
are too numerous when
y
is large, the contribution due to the sum over
R_{d}
cannot be controlled. Thus
Brun had the brilliant idea to consider upper and lower “sifting functions”

\chi^{+}
and
\chi^{-}
that will satisfy

(1.4)

\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)\chi^{-}(d)\leq\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)\leq\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)\chi^{+}(d),

for all
n
. The
\chi^{+}
and
\chi^{-}
are chosen to vanish often enough to
keep the remainder term in check, and at the same time not affect the size
of the main term appreciably. Using Brun’s sieve, it can be shown that the
following estimate holds:

(1.5)

\mathscr{S}(\mathscr{A};\mathscr{P},x,y)=X\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathscr{P}\\
p<y\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\dfrac{\omega(p)}{p}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+O(e^{-\alpha})}}\right)+O_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{X}{\log^{R}X}}}\right),\quad\text{for all}\quad R>0,

where

\alpha=\dfrac{\log x}{\log y}\iff y=x^{1/\alpha}.

Thus, if
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
with
x
, then this yields an asymptotic estimate for
\mathscr{S}(\mathscr{A};\mathscr{P},x,y)
.

It was such an asymptotic estimate for large
\alpha
using Brun’s sieve that was used in the proof of the celebrated Erdős-Kac theorem. Even though sieve asymptotic estimates were employed in Probabilistic Number Theory, mainly the distribution of additive functions in the set of all positive integers
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
was considered.

In order to study the distribution of additive functions in subsets,
the first author considered the following sum
[
3
]
:

(1.6)

S_{g}(\mathscr{A}(x),y):=\sum_{n\leq x,\;n\in\mathscr{A}}g_{y}(n)

where
g(n):=\prod_{p|n}g(p)
is a strongly multiplicative function and

g_{y}(n):=\prod_{p|n,\;p<y}g(p)

is the
trunction of
g
at
y
. The sum in (
1.6
) can be viewed
as a multiplicative generalization of the sieve because the classical sieve problem is the special case when
g(p)=0
for
p\in\mathscr{P}
and
p<y
, with

g(p)=1
, otherwise. The dual
g^{*}
of
g
is the multiplicative function
defined by

g^{*}(n)=\prod_{p|n}g^{*}(p),\hskip 14.22636pt\text{where}\hskip 14.22636ptg^{*}(p)=1-g(p)

for all
n
. The function
g^{*}
occurs in the Buchstab identity for
S_{g}(\mathscr{A}(x),y)
, namely

S_{g}(\mathscr{A}(x),y)=S_{g}(\mathscr{A}(x),y_{1})+\sum_{y\leq p\ <y_{1}}g^{*}(p)S_{g}(\mathscr{A}_{p}(x),p).

The functions
g
and
g^{*}
are related via the Möbius function relation

g^{*}(n)=\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)g(d)\iff g(n)=\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)g^{*}(d).

Also

g_{y}(n)=\sum_{d|(n,P_{y})}\mu(d)g^{*}(d).

Thus

\mathscr{S}(\mathscr{A};\mathscr{P},x,y)=\sum_{n\in\mathscr{A},n\leq x}\sum_{d|(n,P_{y})}\mu(d)g^{*}(d)=\sum_{d|P_{y}}\mu(d)g^{*}(d)|\mathscr{A}_{d}|.

Of particular interest is the case
0\leq g\leq 1
which means
0\leq g^{*}\leq 1

as well and vice-versa. For such
g,\;g^{*}
, the first author noticed a
monotonicity principle
, namely that the Brun inequalities for
\chi^{+}
and
\chi^{-}

now imply that for all positive integers
n
,

(1.7)

\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)g^{*}(d)\chi^{-}(d)\leq\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)g^{*}(d)\leq\sum_{d|n}\mu(d)g^{*}(d)\chi^{+}(d).

What this means is that Brun’s sieve can be used to estimate

S_{g}(\mathscr{A}(x),y)
. More specifically, for all strongly multiplicative functions
g
satisfying
0\leq g\leq 1
, it was shown in
[
3
]
that

S_{g}(\mathscr{A}(x),y)=X\prod_{p<y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\dfrac{\omega(p)g^{*}(p)}{p}}}\right)(1+O(e^{-\alpha}))+O_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{X}{\log^{R}X}}}\right),\quad\text{for all}\quad R>0

holds uniformly. Another very unusual phenomenon was observed in
[
3
]
, namely for the
Brun pure sifting functions
,

\chi^{+}(d):=\begin{cases}1,&\nu(d)\leq 2s\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\quad\quad\quad\quad\chi^{-}(d):=\begin{cases}1,&\nu(d)\leq 2s-1\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

the
monotonicity principle
and the general inequalities (
1.7
) hold for all strongly multiplicative
g
satisfying
0\leq g^{*}\leq 2
, that is for all
-1\leq g\leq 1
. In particular, the
Brun pure sieve
can be used to estimate
S_{g}(x,y)
(
=S_{g}(\mathscr{A}(x),y)
with
\mathscr{A}=\mathbb{N}
) when

g(n)=(-1)^{\nu(n)}

in the Brun pure sieve range

\alpha>\log\log X.

As far as we know this is the first instance when the sieve has been used to estimate sums of multiplicative functions which even take negative integer values. So this motivated the first author to consider estimates for
S_{-1}(x,y)
which measures the parity of
\nu_{y}(n)
. In
[
3
]
, suitable bounds for
S_{-1}(x,y)
were established. Subsequently, these bounds were
refined by Dhavakodi
[
8
]
in his PhD thesis using analytic techniques. We now describe Dhavakodi’s results which will set the stage for our work on
S_{-k}(x,y)
.

1.3.
Estimates for
S_{-1}(x,y)

Dhavakodi
[
8
]
used the de Bruijn-Buchstab method and so his starting estimate is in the range
\sqrt{x}<y\leq x
which is

S_{-1}(x,y)=\frac{xh_{1}(\beta)}{\log y}+\frac{xh_{2}(\beta)}{\log^{2}y}+\frac{x\cdot m_{-1}(\alpha)}{\log^{3}y}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x}{\log^{4}y}}}\right),

where
\beta=x/y
and
\alpha=\log x/\log y
. The functions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}

rapidly tend to 0 as
\beta\to\infty
. Thus if
\alpha>1
is fixed,
then
S_{-1}(x,y)
is of order of magnitude
x/\log^{3}y
. Compare this with the estimate for

M^{*}(x,y)
in (
1.2
).

So with this starting estimate, he uses induction on
\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
to establish the following estimate in the range
x^{1/3}<y<\sqrt{x}
:

S_{-1}(x,y)=\frac{xh_{3}(\gamma)}{\log^{2}y}+\frac{x\cdot m_{-1}(\alpha)}{\log^{3}y}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x}{\log^{4}y}}}\right),

where
\gamma=x/y^{2}
. The main difference between
M^{*}(x,y)
and
S_{-1}(x,y)
is that for
S_{-1}(x,y)
, there are terms of size
x/\log y
, and
x/\log^{2}y

in the range
1<\alpha<2
but these will shrink as
\alpha
moves away from
1, so that the dominant term is
x/\log^{3}y
. Next for
\alpha\geq 3
,
there is no
x/\log y
term, but there is the term of size
x/\log^{2}y
.
But this shrinks to 0 as
y
moves away from
\sqrt{x}
leaving
x/\log^{3}y
as
the dominating term. For
y<x^{1/3}
, namely for
\alpha>3
, the expansion
starts with
x/\log^{3}y
which is the dominating term. It is such a phenomenon that is seen more generally
for
S_{-k}(x,y)
as our results will show.

2.
The sum
S_{z}(x,y)

For two complex valued functions
f
and
g
, the Landau notation
f(x)=O(g(x))
means that
|f(x)|<M|g(x)|
for
x\geq x_{0}
, where
M>0
is a constant. Equivalently, the Vinogradov notation
f\ll g
means that
f(x)=O(g(x))
. We will use both these notations interchangeably as is convenient.

We now discuss the sum

S_{z}(x,y)
(see (
2.1
) below for definition) for complex
z
, before we state our results for
S_{-k}(x,y)
. But first, we need to state Selberg’s classic estimate for
S_{z}(x)=S_{z}(x,x)
.

In order to estimate the number of integers below a given magnitude with a prescribed number of prime factors, Selberg
[
12
]
studied the sum

S_{z}(x)=\sum_{1\leq n\leq x}z^{\nu(n)}

for complex
z
. Since

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{z^{\nu(n)}}{n^{s}}=\prod_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\dfrac{z}{p^{s}-1}}}\right)=\zeta(s)^{z}\cdot f(s,z)

where

f(s,z):=\prod_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\dfrac{z}{p^{s}-1}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\dfrac{1}{p^{s}}}}\right)^{z}

is analytic for
\sigma=\operatorname{Re}(s)>1/2
, Selberg was able to show using the Perron integral method that

Theorem 2.1

(Selberg)
.

For
R>0
, we have uniformly for
|z|\leq R

S_{z}(x)=\dfrac{x\log^{z-1}(x)f(1,z)}{\Gamma(z)}+O_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x\log^{z-2}(x)}}\right).

Using the above theorem, Selberg obtained uniform asymptotic estimates for the number
N_{k}(x)
of integers
n\leq x
with
k
prime factors by expresssing
N_{k}(x)
as a contour integral of
\frac{S_{z}(x)}{z^{k+1}}
, and choosing the contour suitably.

Motivated by the work of the first author on
M(x,y)
and
M^{*}(x,y)
, and Selberg’s results on
S_{z}(x)
, Alladi and Molnar
[
4
]
studied the sum

(2.1)

\displaystyle S_{z}(x,y):=\sum_{1\leq n\leq x}z^{\nu_{y}(n)}

with the intent of estimating

N_{k}(x,y)=\sum_{n\leq x,\nu_{y}(n)=k}1.

Using the Perron integral method, the following result was established in
[
4
]
:

Theorem 2.2

(Alladi-Molnar)
.

Let
R>0
be fixed and
|z|\leq R
and
3\leq y\leq x
, then

\displaystyle S_{z}(x,y)=x\prod_{p<y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\dfrac{z-1}{p}}}\right)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(xe^{-\alpha}\log^{D}(x)}}\right)+O_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{x}{\log^{R+2}(x)}}}\right)

for some absolute constant
D>0
. Thus, for
\epsilon>0
, if
\alpha\geq(R+D+1+\epsilon)\log\log x
or
y\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{\log x}{(R+D+1+\epsilon)\log\log x}}}\right)
, then

\displaystyle S_{z}(x,y)=x\prod_{p<y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\dfrac{z-1}{p}}}\right)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{x}{\log^{R+1+\epsilon}(y)}}}\right)

Thus if
z\neq 1-p
for any prime
p
, we have

\displaystyle S_{z}(x,y)\asymp x\prod_{p<y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\dfrac{z-1}{p}}}\right).

Remark
.

Note that Theorem
2.2
yields an asymptotic estimate for
S_{z}(x,y)
only when

z\neq 1-p\quad\text{for any}\quad p,\quad\text{and}\quad y<x^{c/\log\log x},\quad\text{for some}\quad c>0.

Thus Theorem
2.2
yields an asymptotic estimate for
S_{z}(x,y)
only for small
y
, where “small” is given by the above bound.

Since
S_{z}(x,y)
satisfies the Buchstab identity

(2.2)

S_{z}(x,y)=S_{z}(x,y^{h})+(1-z)\sum_{y\leq p<y^{h}}S_{z}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x}{p},p}}\right),\hskip 28.45274pth>1

Alladi and Molnar
[
4
]
were able to estimate
S_{z}(x,y)
asymptotically
by induction on
\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
by the Buchstab-de Bruijn method
for
y
in a certain large range when
\operatorname{Re}(z)>0
as enunciated in the

Theorem 2.3

(Alladi-Molnar)
.

Let
\operatorname{Re}(z)>0
and
2\leq\sqrt{x}\leq y\leq x
. Let
r=\operatorname{Re}(z)
. Then we have uniformly for
|z|\leq R

\displaystyle S_{z}(x,y)=\dfrac{x\cdot m_{z}(\alpha)}{\log^{1-z}y}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{(1-z)x}{\log^{2-r}y}+\dfrac{(1-z)x}{\log x}}}\right)

where

\displaystyle m_{z}(\alpha)=\dfrac{g(1,z)}{\Gamma(z)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha^{1-z}}+\dfrac{1-z}{\alpha^{1-z}}\int_{1}^{\alpha}\dfrac{du}{u^{z}(u-1)^{1-z}}}}\right).

On the other hand, if
\operatorname{Re}(z)=r>0
and

\displaystyle S_{z}(x,y)=\dfrac{x\cdot m_{z}(\alpha)}{\log^{1-z}y}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{x\log\log x}{\log^{2-r}y}+\dfrac{x\log\log x}{\log y}}}\right)

where

\displaystyle m_{z}(\alpha)=\dfrac{2^{1-z}m_{z}(2)}{\alpha^{1-z}}+\dfrac{1-z}{\alpha^{1-z}}\int_{1}^{\alpha}\dfrac{m_{z}(u-1)\;du}{u^{z}}.

We point out that a uniform version of Theorem

valid for

\exp{((\log x)^{1-\delta})}\leq y\leq x

with a certain

is established in
[
4
]
.
It turns out that the ranges of validity of Theorem

and the uniform

overlap and so the entire interval

is covered. Using these theorems, and suitable bounds for

when

]
, the problem of the asymptotic estimation of

was resolved in
[

]
by the Selberg contour integral method. It is to be noted that the asymptotic behavior of

exhibits a certain

for complex

. Motivated by this variation of the classical theme, Tenenbaum

used the

to get uniform asymptotic estimates for

.

It is to be noted that Selberg’s theorem (Theorem

) does not yield an asymptotic estimate
for

, when

extremely interesting. Since

satisfies the Buchstab recurrence

\displaystyle S_{-k}(x,y)=S_{-k}(x,y^{h})+(k+1)\sum_{y\leq p<y^{h}}S_{-k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x}{p},p}}\right)

the upper bound for

can still be used to start the Buchstab iteration to estimate

asymptotically. Now that we have provided the necessary background, we can describe our recent results on
S_{-k}(x,y)
, which we do in the next section.

3.
Statement of results on
S_{-k}(x,y)

Here and in what follows, we shall use the following notation. We set

\displaystyle\beta=x/y,\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{\ell}=x/y^{\ell}

for an integer
\ell>1
. Also
R(t)
will denote any decreasing function of
t
that satisfies
R(t)=O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-c\sqrt{\log\,t}}}}\right)
, with some
constant
c>0
whose value does not concern us. Also,
c
need not be the
same when used in diffrent contexts. Such an
R(t)
can be used to bound
from above the relative error in the Prime Number Theorem, namely,

\frac{|\pi(t)-\text{li}(t)|}{\text{li}(t)}<R(t).

where
\pi(t)=\sum_{p\leq t}1
and
\text{li}(t)=\int_{2}^{t}\frac{du}{\log u}
.

We begin by describing the asymptotic behavior of
S_{-k}(x,y)
when

\sqrt{x}<y\leq x
.
In this region, the asymptotic
estimate for
S_{-k}(x,y)
has several terms starting with an
x/\log y
term, then an
x/\log^{2}y
term, and so on, up to an
x/\log^{k+1}y
(resp. an
x/\log^{k+2}y
) term when
k\neq p-1
(resp.
k=p-1
). Each of these terms (except the last term) is
multiplied by a function of
\beta
, where these functions of
\beta
rapidly
decay to zero, like the relative error
R(\beta)
in the PNT as
\beta\rightarrow\infty
with
x
. To be more precise, we have the following two theorems for
S_{-k}(x,y)
for the cases
k\neq p-1
, for any prime
p
, and
k=p-1
for some prime
p
:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
k+1\neq p
for any prime
p
. Then for
\sqrt{x}\leq y<x
and
\beta=x/y
, we have

\displaystyle S_{-k}(x,y)=\dfrac{(k+1)xf_{1}(\beta)}{\log y}-\dfrac{(k+1)xf_{2}(\beta)}{\log^{2}y}+\dfrac{2(k+1)xf_{3}(\beta)}{\log^{3}y}+\cdots

\displaystyle+\;(-1)^{k-1}\dfrac{(k+1)x(k-1)!f_{k}(\beta)}{\log^{k}y}+\dfrac{b_{k}(k+1)x}{\log^{k+1}x}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{xR(\beta)}{\log^{k+1}y}}}\right)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{x}{\log^{k+2}x}}}\right)

where
b_{k}
is the non-zero constant

b_{k}=k!\prod_{p}\dfrac{p^{k}(p-k-1)}{(p-1)^{k+1}}

and

(3.1)

f_{1}(\beta):=\int_{1}^{\beta}\dfrac{S_{-k}(t)}{t^{2}}dt=O(R(\beta)),\hskip 28.45274ptf_{j}(\beta):=\int_{1}^{\beta}\dfrac{f_{j-1}(t)}{t}dt=O(R(\beta)),\hskip 22.76228pt2\leq j\leq k.

The important difference in the case
k=p-1
for some
p=
prime, is illustrated in the next result:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
k+1=p
for some prime
p
. Then for
\sqrt{x}\leq y<x
and
\beta=x/y
, we have

\displaystyle S_{-k}(x,y)=\dfrac{(k+1)xf_{1}(\beta)}{\log y}-\dfrac{(k+1)xf_{2}(\beta)}{\log^{2}y}+\dfrac{2(k+1)xf_{3}(\beta)}{\log^{3}y}+\cdots

\displaystyle+\;(-1)^{k}\dfrac{(k+1)xk!f_{k+1}(\beta)}{\log^{k+1}y}+\dfrac{c_{k}(k+1)x}{\log^{k+2}x}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{xR(\beta)}{\log^{k+2}y}}}\right)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{x}{\log^{k+3}x}}}\right)

where
c_{k}
is the non-zero constant

\displaystyle c_{k}=(k+1)!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\dfrac{1}{k}}}\right)^{k+1}\log(k+1)\prod_{p\neq k+1}\dfrac{p^{k}(p-k-1)}{(p-1)^{k+1}}

and

f_{1}(\beta):=\int_{1}^{\beta}\dfrac{S_{-k}(t)}{t^{2}}dt=O(R(\beta)),\hskip 28.45274ptf_{j}(\beta):=\int_{1}^{\beta}\dfrac{f_{j-1}(t)}{t}dt=O(R(\beta)),\hskip 14.22636pt2\leq j\leq k+1.

Remark
.

In Theorem 3.2, the functions
f_{j}(\beta)
are given in the same manner, except that, we also have
f_{j}(\beta)=O(R(\beta))
when
j=k+1
. Since
R(\beta)
tends to zero rapidly as
\beta\to\infty
, what Theorem
3.1
and
3.2
say is that when
\beta
in large, as it happens when
y=x^{1/\alpha}
with
1+\delta\leq\alpha<2
, for some
\delta>0
fixed, then all terms having
the
f_{j}(\beta)
as factors become insignificant, and so the dominating term is of order of magnitude
x/\log^{k+1}y
in Theorem
3.1
and
x/\log^{k+2}y
in Theorem
3.2
. Henceforth, by
Case 1
we shall mean that
k\neq p-1
for any prime
p
, and by
Case 2
we mean the
k=p-1
for some
prime
p
.

With the asymptotic behavior of
S_{-k}(x,y)
having been precisely determined
in the interval
\sqrt{x}<y\leq x
, the Buchstab identity (
2.3
) can be used to estimate
S_{-k}(x,y)
by induction on
\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
, that is in intervals
\ell<\alpha\leq\ell+1
in succession. But what happens is that in the interval
x^{1/3}<y<x^{1/2}
, there is no
x/\log y
term and so the expansion starts with the
x/\log^{2}y
term. Next in the interval
x^{1/4}<y<x^{1/3}
, there are no terms of size
x/\log y
or
x/\log^{2}y
, thus the expansion starts with
x/\log^{3}y
, and so on until we have the stabilizing term
x/\log^{k+1}y
when
k\neq p-1
, and
x/\log^{k+2}y
, when
k=p-1
. This appealing phenomenon is described in the following theorems:

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
k+1\neq p
for any prime
p
and
\ell
be an integer in
[2,k+1]
. Then there exists functions
E_{1,\ell}^{(\ell)}(t),E_{1,\ell+1}^{(\ell)}(t),\cdots,E_{1,k}^{(\ell)}(t)
with
E_{1,j}^{(\ell)}(\beta_{\ell})=O(R(\beta_{\ell}))

(\ell\leq j\leq k)
such that in the range
\sqrt[\ell+1]{x}\leq y<\sqrt[\ell]{x}
, we have

\displaystyle S_{-k}(x,y)=\sum_{j=\ell}^{k}\dfrac{x\cdot E_{1,j}^{(\ell)}(\beta_{\ell})}{\log^{j}x}+\dfrac{x\cdot m_{1,k}(\alpha)}{\log^{k+1}y}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{xR(\beta_{\ell})}{\log^{k+1}x}}}\right)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{x}{\log^{k+2}y}}}\right)

where for non-integral
\alpha\in[1,k+1]
,
m_{1,k}(\alpha)
satisfies the
difference-differential equation

(3.2)

\displaystyle\alpha\cdot m_{1,k}^{\prime}(\alpha)+(k+1)\cdot m_{1,k}(\alpha)=(k+1)\cdot m_{1,k}(\alpha-1),\hskip 14.22636pt\alpha>1

and more precisely, for
\ell=2,3,\cdots k
and
\ell<\alpha<\ell+1
the integral equation

(3.3)

\displaystyle m_{1,k}(\alpha)=\dfrac{m_{1,k}(\ell^{-})\ell^{k+1}}{\alpha^{k+1}}+d_{1,\ell}(k)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{\ell}{\ell-1}}}\right)^{k}\dfrac{k+1}{\alpha^{k+1}}+\dfrac{k+1}{\alpha^{k+1}}\int_{\ell}^{\alpha}s^{k}m_{1,k}(s-1)\;ds,

with a certain constant
d_{1,\ell}(k)
specified below.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
k+1=p
for some prime
p
and
\ell
be an integer in
[2,k+2]
. Then there exists functions
E_{2,\ell}^{(\ell)}(t),E_{2,\ell+1}^{(\ell)}(t),\cdots,E_{2,k+1}^{(\ell)}(t)
with
E_{2,j}^{(\ell)}(\beta_{\ell})=O(R(\beta_{\ell}))

(\ell\leq j\leq k+1)
such that in the range
\sqrt[\ell+1]{x}\leq y<\sqrt[\ell]{x}
, we have

\displaystyle S_{-k}(x,y)=\sum_{j=\ell}^{k+1}\dfrac{x\cdot E_{2,j}^{(\ell)}(\beta_{\ell})}{\log^{j}x}+\dfrac{x\cdot m_{2,k}(\alpha)}{\log^{k+2}y}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{xR(\beta_{\ell})}{\log^{k+2}x}}}\right)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{x}{\log^{k+3}y}}}\right)

where for non-integral
\alpha\in[1,k+2]
,
m_{2,k}(\alpha)
satisfies the
difference-differential equation

(3.4)

\displaystyle\alpha\cdot m_{2,k}^{\prime}(\alpha)+(k+2)\cdot m_{2,k}(\alpha)=(k+1)\cdot m_{2,k}(\alpha-1),\hskip 14.22636pt\alpha>1

and more precisely, for
\ell=2,3,\cdots k+1
and
\ell<\alpha<\ell+1
the integral equation

(3.5)

\displaystyle m_{2,k}(\alpha)=\dfrac{m_{2,k}(\ell^{-})\ell^{k+2}}{\alpha^{k+2}}+d_{2,\ell}(k)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{\ell}{\ell-1}}}\right)^{k+1}\dfrac{k+1}{\alpha^{k+2}}+\dfrac{k+1}{\alpha^{k+2}}\int_{\ell}^{\alpha}s^{k+1}m_{2,k}(s-1)\;ds,

with a certain constant
d_{2,\ell}(k)
specified below.

It turns out that

\displaystyle d_{1,\ell}(k):=\int_{1}^{\infty}\dfrac{E_{1,k}^{(\ell)}(t)}{t}dt\quad\text{and}\quad d_{2,\ell}(k):=\int_{1}^{\infty}\dfrac{E_{2,k+1}^{(\ell)}(t)}{t}dt.

Since the functions
E_{j,\ell}^{(\ell)}(\beta_{\ell})
(
j=1
and
2
) in Theorems
3.3
and
3.4
tend rapidly to zero as
\beta_{\ell}\to\infty
, and in Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
the functions
f_{j}(\beta)\to 0

rapidly as
\beta\to\infty
, it follows that if
\alpha
is not an integer and fixed, then

(3.6)

\displaystyle S_{-k}(x,y)=\frac{x\cdot m_{1,k}(\alpha)}{\log^{k+1}y}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x}{\log^{k+2}y}}}\right),\quad\text{for}\quad\alpha\in(1,k+1),\quad\text{\bf{Case 1}},

and

(3.7)

\displaystyle S_{-k}(x,y)=\frac{x\cdot m_{2,k}(\alpha)}{\log^{k+2}y}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{x}{\log^{k+3}y}}}\right),\quad\text{for}\quad\alpha\in(1,k+2),\quad\text{\bf{Case 2}}.

For
Case 1
, when
\alpha>k+1
(resp. for
Case 2
, when

\alpha>k+2
), all terms involving
x/\log^{j}y
for
1\leq j\leq k

(resp.
1\leq j\leq k+1
) disappear when the Buchstab recurrence (
2.3
) is
applied to estimate
S_{-k}(x,y)
by induction on
\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
(this is due to the properties of the function
G_{k}(s)
discussed in the next section). Thus the the asymptotic behavior stabilizes. This is given by the following

Theorem 3.5
.

(i) Let
\alpha>k+1
be fixed. If
k\neq p-1
for any prime
p
, then we have

S_{-k}(x,y)=\dfrac{x\cdot m_{1,k}(\alpha)}{\log^{k+1}y}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{x}{\log^{k+2}y}}}\right),

where
m_{1,k}(\alpha)
satisfies the difference-differential equation (
3.2
).

(ii) Let
\alpha>k+2
be fixed. If
k=p-1
for some prime
p
, then we have

S_{-k}(x,y)=\dfrac{x\cdot m_{2,k}(\alpha)}{\log^{k+2}y}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{x}{\log^{k+3}y}}}\right),

where
m_{2,k}(\alpha)
satisfies the difference-differential equation (
3.4
).

Remark
.

Although Theorem
3.5
is stated for fixed
\alpha
, it is possible to use
the Buchstab formula (
2.3
) together with the integral equations satisfied by

m_{1,k}(\alpha)
and
m_{2,k}(\alpha)
, follow the method of Alladi in
[
1
]
, or of Alladi-Molnar in
[
4
]
, to show that for any
\varepsilon>0
,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|S_{-k}(x,y)-\dfrac{x\cdot m_{1,k}(\alpha)}{\log^{k+1}y}}}\right|\ll_{\varepsilon}\dfrac{x\cdot{\alpha}^{k+2}}{\log^{k+2}y},\hskip 5.69046pt\text{for}\hskip 5.69046pt\alpha\geq k+1+\varepsilon\hskip 5.69046pt\text{(\bf{Case 1})},

and

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|S_{-k}(x,y)-\dfrac{x\cdot m_{2,k}(\alpha)}{\log^{k+2}y}}}\right|\ll_{\varepsilon}\dfrac{x\cdot{\alpha}^{k+3}}{\log^{k+3}y},\hskip 5.69046pt\text{for}\hskip 5.69046pt\alpha\geq k+2+\varepsilon\hskip 5.69046pt\text{(\bf{Case 2})}.

Thus we get an asymptotic estimate for
S_{-k}(x,y)
when

y>\exp{(\log^{1-\delta}(x))}
, for some
\delta>0
. However, to fully understand the above estimates, we
need to know the asymptotic behavior of the functions
m_{1,k}(\alpha)
and

m_{2,k}(\alpha)
as
\alpha\to\infty
. For this purpose, and also to construct a continuous approximation to
S_{-k}(x,y)
, much like
\text{li}(x)
is a continuous approximation to
\pi(x)
, we need to study certain Dirichlet series, which is what we do next. These Dirichlet series also shed light on the
constants
b_{k}
and
c_{k}
in Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
respectively.

4.
Some special Dirichlet series

We start by defining a function
G_{k}(s)
given as a convergent
Dirichlet series in
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
, and which enjoys a second representation which is analytic in
Re(s)\geq 1
:

(4.1)

G_{k}(s):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-k)^{\nu(n)}}{n^{s}}=\prod_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\dfrac{k}{p^{s}-1}}}\right)=\dfrac{g_{k}(s)}{\zeta^{k}(s)}

where

g_{k}(s):=\prod_{p}\dfrac{p^{sk}(p^{s}-(k+1))}{(p^{s}-1)^{k+1}}

converges for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1/2
, and
1/\zeta^{k}(s)
is analytic in

\operatorname{Re}(s)\geq 1
. Since the Dirichlet series is absolutely convergent for
Re(s)>1
and uniformly convergent
in compact subsets of the half-plane
Re(s)>1
, we may differentiate term-by-term in that half-plane to deduce that

G^{(j)}_{k}(s)=\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\frac{(-k)^{\nu(n})(-1)^{j}log^{j}(n)}{n^{s}},\quad\operatorname{Re}(s)>1.

However, each of Dirichlet series
G^{(j)}_{k}(s)
for
j\geq 1
, converges at
s=1
. Indeed, by Steiltjes integration, we have

G_{k}(1)=G^{(0)}_{k}(1)=\sum^{N}_{n=1}\frac{(-k)^{\nu(n)}}{n}

=\int^{N}_{1^{-}}\frac{dS_{-k}(x)}{x}=\frac{S_{-k}(N)}{N}+\int^{N}_{1}\frac{S_{k}(x)dx}{x^{2}},

because
S_{-k}(1^{-})=0
. The bound
S_{-k}(x)=O(xR(x))
shows that

S_{-k}(N)/N
tends to 0 as
N\to\infty
, and that

\int^{\infty}_{1}\frac{S_{-k}(x)dx}{x^{2}}<\infty.

It follows by the same argument with a little extra detail, that each of the series

G^{(j)}_{k}(1)=\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\frac{(-k)^{\nu(n)}(-1)^{j}\log^{j}(n)}{n},

will converge for
j=1,2,\cdots
. The actual values of
G^{(j)}_{k}(1)
are given below using the second representation; these values can be established by using ideas underlying Axer’s theorem,
the Perron integral method, and the zero-free region for
\zeta(s)
in the
critical strip.

We have two cases:

Case 1
:
k+1\neq p
for any prime
p
.

In this case,
G_{k}(s)
has a zero of order
k
at
s=1
since
g_{k}(1)\neq 0
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle G_{k}(1)=G_{k}^{\prime}(1)=G_{k}^{\prime\prime}(1)=\cdots=G_{k}^{(k-1)}(1)=0\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636ptG_{k}^{(k)}(1)\neq 0.

This means

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-k)^{\nu(n)}\log^{j}n}{n}=0,\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for}\quad j=0,1,2,\cdots,k-1,\quad\text{and}\quad\neq 0,\quad\text{if}\quad j=k.

The value of
G_{k}^{(k)}(1)
equals the constant
b_{k}
in Theorem
3.1
, and we have

(4.2)

G_{k}^{(k)}(1)=b_{k}=k!\prod_{p}\dfrac{p^{k}(p-k-1)}{(p-1)^{k+1}}.

Case 2
:
k+1=p
for some prime
p
.

In this case,
G_{k}(s)
has a zero of order
k+1
at
s=1
since
g_{k}(s)
has a simple zero at
s=1
.
Thus, we have

\displaystyle G_{k}(1)=G_{k}^{\prime}(1)=G_{k}^{\prime\prime}(1)=\cdots=G_{k}^{(k)}(1)=0\hskip 14.22636pt\text{and}\hskip 14.22636ptG_{k}^{(k+1)}(1)\neq 0.

This means

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-k)^{\nu(n)}\log^{j}n}{n}=0,\hskip 17.07182pt\text{for}\quad j=0,1,2,\cdots,\quad\text{and}\quad\neq 0\quad\text{if}\quad j=k+1.

First, we consider
Case 2
which is the exceptional case. We evaluate
G_{k}^{(k+1)}(1)
in this case and show that this equals the constant
c_{k}
in Theorem
3.2
. We have

(4.3)

\displaystyle G_{k}^{(k+1)}(1):=c_{k}=(k+1)!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{1}{k}}}\right)^{k+1}\log(k+1)\prod_{p\neq k+1}\dfrac{p^{k}(p-k-1)}{(p-1)^{k+1}}.

Also, it can be shown that for any large
\beta\geq 1

(4.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq\beta}\dfrac{(-k)^{\nu(n)}\log^{k+1}n}{n}=c_{k}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(R(\beta)}}\right).

Indeed, using Steiltjes integration, (
4.1
) and (
4.3
), we have

(4.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq\beta}\dfrac{(-k)^{\nu(n)}\log^{k+1}n}{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{(-k)^{\nu(n)}\log^{k+1}n}{n}-\sum_{n>\beta}\dfrac{(-k)^{\nu(n)}\log^{k+1}n}{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{k}-\int_{\beta^{-}}^{\infty}\dfrac{\log^{k+1}x}{x}d(S_{-k}(x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{k}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\dfrac{\log^{k+1}(x)S_{-k}(x)}{x}}}\right|_{\beta^{-}}^{\infty}-\int_{\beta}^{\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{(k+1)\log^{k}x}{x^{2}}-\dfrac{\log^{k+1}x}{x^{2}}}}\right)S_{-k}(x)\;dx.

At this point, we use the estimate
S_{-k}(x)=O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x\cdot e^{-c\sqrt{\log\;x}}}}\right)
to
deduce (
4.4
). We also need the integrals
f_{j}(\beta)
defined in Section
3
, as well as the
following integrals defined using the
f_{j}(\beta)
:

\displaystyle F_{j,\ell}(\beta):=\int_{1}^{\beta}\dfrac{f_{j}(t)\log^{\ell}t}{t}dt,\quad 1\leq j\leq k+i\quad\text{and}\quad 1\leq\ell\leq k-j+i.

It turns out that for
Case 2
, we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle f_{j}(\beta)=O(R(\beta))\quad(1\leq j\leq k+1),\quad f_{k+2}(\beta)=\dfrac{(-1)^{k+1}c_{k}}{(k+1)!}+O(R(\beta)),

(4.7)

\displaystyle\text{and}\hskip 19.91684ptF_{j,\ell}(\beta)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{(-1)^{j}\ell!c_{k}}{(k+1)!}+O(R(\beta)),&\ell=k-j+1\\
\mbox{}\\
O(R(\beta)),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

To deal with
Case 1
, we consider similar integrals with
k+1
replaced by
k
.

5.
Sketch of the proof of Theorem
3.1

We only outline the proof of Theorem
3.1
. First, we use an an integral analogue of the Buchstab recurrence (
2.3
) which follows from
the strong form of the Prime Number Theorem:

(5.1)

S_{-k}(x,y)=S_{-k}(x,y^{h})+(k+1)\int_{y}^{y^{h}}S_{-k}(x/t,t)\dfrac{dt}{\log t}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x\cdot\alpha\cdot\log^{k}x\cdot R(y)}}\right).

Using (
5.1
),
S_{-k}(x,y)
can be estimated first in the range
\sqrt{x}\leq y<x
or, equivalently,
1<\alpha\leq 2
. To do so, we set
y^{h}=x
in (
5.1
) to obtain

(5.2)

S_{-k}(x,y)=S_{-k}(x,x)+(k+1)\int_{y}^{x}S_{-k}(x/t,t)\dfrac{dt}{\log t}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x\cdot\alpha\cdot\log^{k}x\cdot R(y)}}\right).

Noting that
S_{-k}(x,x)=S_{-k}(x)=O(x\cdot R(x))
, and
1\leq x/t\leq\sqrt{x}
since
\sqrt{x}\leq y\leq t\leq x
, we immediately see that
S_{-k}(x/t,t)=S_{-k}(x/t)
. Thus, (
5.2
) yields

(5.3)

S_{-k}(x,y)=(k+1)\int_{y}^{x}S_{-k}(x/t)\dfrac{dt}{\log t}+O(x\cdot R(y)).

By making the change of variable
t=x/u
, and applying integration by parts repeatedly by choosing suitable anti-derivatives determined by the functions
f_{j}(\beta)
(
1\leq j\leq k
) in Theorem
3.1
, we end up with the following:

(5.4)

\displaystyle\int_{y}^{x}S_{-k}(x/t)\dfrac{dt}{\log t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{x\cdot f_{1}(\beta)}{\log y}-\dfrac{x\cdot f_{2}(\beta)}{\log^{2}y}+\cdots+(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\dfrac{x\cdot f_{k}(\beta)}{\log^{k}y}

\displaystyle+\;x(-1)^{k}k!\int_{1}^{\beta}\dfrac{f_{k}(s)}{s\log^{k+1}(x/s)}ds.

Next, it follows by induction, and the fact that
S_{-k}(\beta)=O(\beta\cdot R(\beta))
that
f_{j}(\beta)=O(R(\beta))
for
1\leq j\leq k
. If we now set
f_{k+1}(\beta):=\int_{1}^{\beta}\dfrac{f_{k}(s)}{s}ds
, then these estimates coupled with repeated integration by parts yield

(5.5)

\displaystyle f_{k+1}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{(-1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\int_{1}^{s}\dfrac{f_{1}(t)\log^{k-1}t}{t}dt+O(R(s))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{(-1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\int_{1}^{s}f_{1}(t)\log^{k-1}td(\log t)+O(R(s))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{(-1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left.\dfrac{f_{1}(t)\log^{k}t}{k}}}\right|_{1}^{s}-\dfrac{1}{k}\int_{1}^{s}\log^{k}td(f_{1}(t))}}\right\}+O(R(s)).

The first expression inside curly braces in the right-hand side of (
5.5
) is
O(R(s))
. To evaluate the integral inside curly braces in the right-hand side of (
5.5
), we need only observe that
d(f_{1}(t))=\frac{S_{-k}(t)}{t^{2}}dt
whence changing the order of sum and integral, and repeated integration by parts yield

(5.6)

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{s}\log^{k}td(f_{1}(t))=\sum_{n\leq\beta}(-k)^{\nu(n)}\int_{n}^{s}\dfrac{\log^{k}t}{t^{2}}dt=b_{k}+O(R(s))

where
b_{k}
is as in (
4.2
). Thus, (
5.5
) and (
5.6
) yield

f_{k+1}(s)=\dfrac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}b_{k}+O(R(s)),

and this allows us to estimate the integral in the right-hand side of (
5.4
) by choosing the following anti-derivative:

(5.7)

\dfrac{f_{k}(s)ds}{s}=d\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f_{k+1}(s)-\dfrac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}b_{k}}}\right)\quad\text{where}\quad f_{k+1}(s)-\dfrac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}b_{k}\rightarrow 0,\;s\rightarrow\infty.

Using (
5.7

5.4
), and applying integration by parts for the last time yields

\displaystyle\int_{y}^{x}S_{-k}(x/t)\dfrac{dt}{\log t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{x\cdot f_{1}(\beta)}{\log y}-\dfrac{x\cdot f_{2}(\beta)}{\log^{2}y}+\cdots+(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)!\dfrac{x\cdot f_{k}(\beta)}{\log^{k}y}

\displaystyle+\;\dfrac{b_{k}\cdot x}{\log^{k+1}x}+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{x\cdot R(\beta)}{\log^{k+1}x}}}\right)+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{x}{\log^{k+2}y}}}\right)

which combines with (
5.3
) to yield the required result, and we are done.

Theorem
3.2
follows analogously, the only difference is the fact that the expansion will now have an additional main term involving
\log^{k+2}x
in the denominator. To obtain Theorems
3.3
and

For this, define

Next, define the following:

(6.1)

A_{i,k}(x,y):=\dfrac{x}{\log y}\int_{1}^{\infty}w_{k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{\log x-\log t}{\log y}}}\right)\;df_{1}(t)

7.
Asymptotic behavior of
m_{1,k}(\alpha)
and
m_{2,k}(\alpha)
as

\alpha\to\infty

For a function
f(\alpha)
which satisfies

(7.1)

f^{\prime}(\alpha)=\frac{\lambda}{\alpha}\int^{\alpha}_{\alpha-1}f^{\prime}(t)\;dt,\quad\text{for}\quad\alpha>\alpha_{0},

where
\lambda
is a constant, it follows by iteration on
\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
that

(7.2)

f^{\prime}(\alpha)=O(e^{-\alpha\log\alpha+O(\alpha)}).

See Alladi-Molnar
[
4
]
for details of the method to get the upper bound for
f^{\prime}(\alpha)
in (6.2). Since
f^{\prime}(\alpha)
tends to zero so rapidly, by writing
f(\alpha)
as an integral of
f^{\prime}
, it follows that

(7.3)

f(\alpha)=c+O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-\alpha\log\alpha+O(\alpha)}}}\right),\quad\alpha\to\infty,

where
c
is a constant.

With regard to the functions
m_{1,k}(\alpha)
, (
3.2
)
implies that if
f(\alpha)=m_{1,k}(\alpha)
, then
f^{\prime}(\alpha)=m^{\prime}_{1,k}(\alpha)
satisfies (
7.1
) with
\lambda=-(k+1)
. Thus by (
7.2
) we see that
m_{1,k}(\alpha)
tends to a limit
c
as
\alpha\to\infty
. With regard to
m_{2,k}(\alpha)
,
there is a crucial difference in the asymptotic behavior. To understand this,
consider an anti-derivative
f(\alpha)
of
m_{2,k}(\alpha)
. In this case, the difference-differential equation (
3.4
) for
m_{2,k}(\alpha)
shows that with this anti-derivative
f(\alpha)
, the function
f^{\prime}(\alpha)=m_{2,k}(\alpha)
satisfies (
7.1
) with
\lambda=-(k+1)
, that is

m_{2,k}(\alpha)=-\frac{k+1}{\alpha}\int^{\alpha}_{\alpha-1}m_{2,k}(t)dt.

Thus by (
7.2
) we see that
m_{2,k}(\alpha)\to 0
as
\alpha\to\infty
.

In order to compute the limit of
m_{1,k}(\alpha)
as
\alpha\to\infty
, let us consider the behavior of
S_{-k}(x,y)
for “small
y
”. Indeed by the Alladi-Molnar theorem (Theorem
2.2
) on
S_{z}(x,y)
for “small
y
” we have the following:

S_{-k}(x,y)\begin{cases}\sim\displaystyle x\prod_{p<y}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\frac{k+1}{p}}}\right)\asymp\frac{x}{\log^{k+1}y},&\textbf{Case 1}\\
\mbox{}\\
=O_{R}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\dfrac{x}{\log^{R}y}}}\right),&\textbf{Case 2}.\end{cases}\quad\quad\quad\text{for}\quad k+2\leq y\leq x^{c/\log\log x}

where
R
can be chosen arbitrarily large.
Notice that the large
y
interval in Theorem
6.2
overlaps with the small
y
interval
k+2\leq y\leq x^{c/\log\log x}
. So, we have all of
k+2\leq y\leq x
covered. So by comparing the two estimates in an interval common to regions for “small
y
” and “large
y
”, we see that
m_{1,k}(\alpha)
approaches a non-zero limit whereas
m_{2,k}(\alpha)
decays to zero as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
with
x
. More precisely, we have:

Case 1
:

\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow\infty}m_{1,k}(\alpha)=\ell(-k):=e^{-(k+1)\gamma}\prod_{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\dfrac{k+1}{p}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\dfrac{1}{p}}}\right)^{-k-1}\neq 0,

and so

m_{1,k}(\alpha)=\ell(-k)+O(e^{-\alpha\log\alpha+O(\alpha)})

where
\gamma
is the Euler’s constant.

Case 2
:

\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow\infty}m_{2,k}(\alpha)=0

and so

m_{2,k}(\alpha)\ll e^{-\alpha\log\alpha+O(\alpha)}.

In this case,
\ell(-k)=0
as can be seen from the product.

Remark
.

If we did not have estimates in a common region to make a comparison, then we would have to use other techniques to determine the asymptotic behavior of

as a Laplace
