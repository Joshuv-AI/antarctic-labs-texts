On the Spectral Geometry and Small Time Mass of Anderson Models on Planar Domains
Pierre Yves Gaudreau Lamarre, Yuanyuan Pan

Abstract
. We consider the Anderson Hamiltonian (AH) and the parabolic Anderson model (PAM) with white noise and Dirichlet boundary condition on a bounded planar domain D ⊂ ℝ 2 D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} . We compute the small time asymptotics of the AH’s exponential trace up to order O ⁡ ( log ⁡ t ) O(\log t) , and of the PAM’s mass up to order O ⁡ ( t ​ log ⁡ t ) O(t\log t) . Our proof is probabilistic, and relies on the asymptotics of intersection local times of Brownian motions and bridges in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . Applications of our main result include the following: (i) If the boundary ∂ D \partial D is sufficiently regular, then D D ’s area and ∂ D \partial D ’s length can both be recovered almost surely from a single observation of the AH’s eigenvalues. This extends Mouzard’s Weyl law [ 32 ] in the special case of bounded domains. (ii) If D D is simply connected and ∂ D \partial D is fractal, then ∂ D \partial D ’s Minkowski dimension (if it exists) can be recovered almost surely from the PAM’s small time asymptotics. (iii) The variance of the white noise can be recovered almost surely from a single observation of the AH’s eigenvalues.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
The Anderson Models

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be bounded, nonempty, open, and connected.
Let
\kappa\geq 0
be a nonnegative parameter, and let
\xi
be a standard Gaussian white noise on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Informally,
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is a centered Gaussian process with

\mathbf{E}[\xi(x)\xi(y)]=\delta_{0}(x-y),\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

where
\delta_{0}
is the delta Dirac distribution. In rigorous terms,
\xi
is
defined as a centered Gaussian process on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
(interpreting
\xi(f)=\int f\xi
)
with covariance

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\xi(f)\xi(g)]=\langle f,g\rangle,\qquad f,g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard inner product in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
We also use
\|\cdot\|
to denote the standard norm in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
throughout.

In this paper, we are interested in two well-studied Anderson models on
D
with noise
\kappa\xi
:
On the one hand, we consider the Anderson Hamiltonian (AH)

(1.2)

\displaystyle H_{\kappa}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta+\kappa\xi,

which acts on a dense subspace of
L^{2}(D)
that satisfies the Dirichlet boundary condition
on
\partial D
. On the other hand, we consider the parabolic Anderson model (PAM)

u_{\kappa}(t,x)=\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{\kappa}}\mathbf{1}_{D}(x)
, where
\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\}}
denotes the
indicator function. In other words,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u_{\kappa}(t,x)=\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta-\kappa\xi(x)\big)u_{\kappa}(t,x)&\qquad t>0,~x\in D,\\
u_{\kappa}(0,x)=1&\qquad x\in D,\\
u_{\kappa}(t,x)=0&\qquad t>0,~x\in\partial D.\end{cases}

When
\kappa=0
, (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) respectively reduce
to the Dirichlet Laplacian and heat equation on
D
, whose definitions are well-known
and classical. However, when
\kappa>0
, the AH and PAM are notoriously
difficult to construct rigorously due to the irregularity of
\xi
.
The standard approach to get around this obstacle is to introduce a sequence
of smooth approximations of the AH and PAM and take limits: Let

(1.4)

\displaystyle p_{t}(x)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-|x|^{2}/2t}}{2\pi t},\qquad t>0,~x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

denote the planar Gaussian kernel. For every
\varepsilon>0
, let

(1.5)

\displaystyle H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta+\kappa\xi_{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{and}\qquad u_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t,x)=\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{\kappa,\varepsilon}}\mathbf{1}_{D}(x),

where
\xi_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is a centered Gaussian
process with covariance

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\xi_{\varepsilon}(x)\xi_{\varepsilon}(y)]=p_{\varepsilon}(x-y),\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Since
\xi_{\varepsilon}
has smooth
sample paths, we can define (
1.5
)
using classical theory. Then, (
1.2
)
and (
1.3
) can be constructed as the limits

(1.7)

\displaystyle H_{\kappa}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}+\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon})\qquad\text{and}\qquad u_{\kappa}(t,x)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}u_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t,x)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}},

where
\{\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}:\varepsilon>0\}
are diverging renormalization constants that are designed to compensate
for
\xi_{\varepsilon}
’s singularity as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Constructions of this type have been carried out using regularity structures

[
29
,
31
]
and
paracontrolled calculus
[
7
,
32
]
.
See also

[
1
,
3
,
8
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
33
]

for similar constructions when
D
is replaced by a more general
two-dimensional manifold (with or without boundary) or
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
See Section
4
(more specifically, Assumption
4.9
) for more details on the
assumptions we make in this paper regarding the construction of
H_{\kappa}
and
u_{\kappa}

via (
1.7
).

1.2.
Main Result

In this paper, we are interested in the following broad problem:

Question 1.1
.

Let
\lambda_{1}(H_{\kappa})\leq\lambda_{2}(H_{\kappa})\leq\cdots
be the eigenvalues of
H_{\kappa}
,
and let
\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa})
(
n\geq 1
) be the corresponding orthonormal eigenfunctions.
How are
\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})
,
\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa}),

and the time-evolution of
u_{\kappa}(t,\cdot)
affected when one transitions
from
\kappa=0
to
\kappa>0
?

See Sections
2.1.1
and
2.1.2
for a survey of past results
concerning Question
1.1
.

The approach that we adopt to study this question in this paper is based on
the exponential trace of
H_{\kappa}
and the mass of
u_{\kappa}
, which
are respectively defined as

(1.8)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)=\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{\kappa}}\big]=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})},\qquad t>0

and

(1.9)

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)=\int_{D}u_{\kappa}(t,x)~\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})}\left\langle\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa}),\mathbf{1}_{D}\right\rangle^{2},\qquad t>0.

More specifically, we are interested in the small
t
asymptotics of these quantities, which,
in view of the series expansions on the right-hand sides of (
1.8
)
and (
1.9
), relate to the
large-
n
asymptotics of
\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})
and
\left\langle\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa}),\mathbf{1}_{D}\right\rangle^{2}
.

When
\kappa=0
, (
1.8
)
and (
1.9
) are typically called the heat trace and heat content
on
D
, respectively. The
t\to 0
asymptotics of
\mathsf{T}_{0}(t)
and
\mathsf{M}_{0}(t)
have been the subject
of considerable study in the past 60 years. In particular, it is known that these asymptotics contain a lot of information
about the geometry of
D
; see, e.g.,
[
18
]
and
[
36
, Section 1.2]
for more details and
references.
In this context, our main result quantifies the effect of
\kappa
on these quantities as follows:

Notation 1.2
.

Given a Borel measurable set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we let
\mathsf{A}(K)=\int_{K}1~\mathrm{d}x
denote
its area/Lebesgue measure.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\kappa>0
be fixed, and
suppose that
H_{\kappa}
and
u_{\kappa}
are constructed as described in
Assumption
4.9
. As
t\to 0
, it holds that

(1.10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\big[\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)\big]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{T}_{0}(t)+\tfrac{\kappa^{2}\mathsf{A}(D)}{4\pi^{2}}\log t+o(\log t)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)\big]=O(1),

(1.11)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\big[\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)\big]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{M}_{0}(t)+\tfrac{\kappa^{2}\mathsf{A}(D)}{2\pi}t\log t+o(t\log t)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)\big]=O(t^{2}).

To the best of our knowledge, there are two past results that addressed this exact question
in the setting of planar domains:
Firstly, it was shown in
[
32
]
that

(1.12)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)=\mathsf{T}_{0}(t)+o(t^{-1})=\tfrac{\mathsf{A}(D)}{2\pi}t^{-1}+o(t^{-1})\qquad\text{as }t\to 0

when
\partial D
is smooth. Then,
[
31
]
extended
(
1.12
) to the case where
\partial D
is Lipschitz.
See Section
2.1.2
for more details,
including various extensions of (
1.12
)
that exceed the setting of this paper (e.g., more general domains/manifolds
or boundary conditions).
In this context,
Theorem
1.3
can be seen as a substantial refinement
of the estimate (
1.12
)
for arbitrary planar domains. That is,
(
1.10
) and (
1.11
)
essentially state that

(1.13)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{T}_{0}(t)+\tfrac{\kappa^{2}\mathsf{A}(D)}{4\pi^{2}}\log t+o(\log t)

\displaystyle\text{as }t\to 0,

(1.14)

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{M}_{0}(t)+\tfrac{\kappa^{2}\mathsf{A}(D)}{2\pi}t\log t+o(t\log t)

\displaystyle\text{as }t\to 0,

with the important caveat that there may be some random fluctuations of
average size
O(1)
and
O(t)
in (
1.13
)
and (
1.14
) respectively.

1.3.
Outline of Applications

Among other things, the asymptotics in (
1.13
)
and (
1.14
) suggest the following informal principle:

”Any feature of
D
that can be recovered from
the
t\to 0
asymptotics of
\mathsf{T}_{0}(t)
and
\mathsf{M}_{0}(t)
up to respective orders
O(\log t)
and
O(t\log t)

can also be recovered almost surely from
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)
and
\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)
when
\kappa>0
.”

Using Theorem
1.3
, we prove
two new results that support this conclusion,
which are motivated by spectral geometry and boundary fractal geometry:

(i)

Spectral Geometry.
In
Corollary
2.6
, we show that if

\partial D
is sufficiently regular, then
D
’s area and
\partial D
’s length
can be recovered almost surely from a single observation of
H_{\kappa}
’s eigenvalues.
This improves Mouzard’s Weyl law
[
32
]

in the case of bounded domains, which showed that
D
’s area can be recovered from
\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})
’s
first order asymptotics.

(ii)

Boundary Fractal Geometry.
In
Corollary
2.8
, we show that if

D
is simply connected and

\partial D
has a well-defined Minkowski dimension
\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)\in(1,2)
, then we can recover

\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)
almost-surely from
\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)
’s small
t
asymptotics. This extends a similar result
for
\mathsf{M}_{0}(t)
due to van den Berg
[
41
]
.

See Section
2.2
for more details on the context and significance of these results.

In addition to these two applications, Theorem
1.3
identifies exactly when one ”begins to feel”
the presence of
\kappa\xi
in
H_{\kappa}
’s large eigenvalue/eigenfunction asymptotics.
We present the following application of this identification:

(iii)

Variance Recovery.
The parameter
\kappa^{2}
can be recovered almost surely from a single observation of

H_{\kappa}
’s eigenvalues; see
Corollary
2.10
for details.

This is rather surprising in light of the fact that, to the best of our knowledge,

\kappa^{2}
cannot be recovered almost surely if
\xi
is replaced by a more regular noise; see Section

3.4
for more details on this point. In fact, the
presence of logarithmic terms in
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)
’s and
\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)
’s asymptotics is quite
unusual, since adding a more regular potential to the Laplacian typically induces power-law
corrections to the heat trace; e.g.,
[
37
]
. Our method of proof shows that the
logarithmic terms emerge from the singularity of the noise
\xi
, and corresponds to the fact that the renormalization in
(
1.7
) is logarithmic in
\varepsilon
;
see Remarks
4.12
,
5.10
, and
6.4
for details.

Remark 1.4
.

In this context, it is interesting to note
that our results actually imply the presence of an infinite number of logarithmic terms
in
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)
’s and
\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)
’s asymptotics. Indeed, the expectation asymptotics
in (
1.10
) and (
1.11
)
follow from the more general statements that, as
t\to 0
, one has

\mathbf{E}\big[\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)\big]=\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}t\log t/2\pi}\big(\mathsf{T}_{0}(t)+o(1)\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{E}\big[\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)\big]=\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}(t\log t-t)/2\pi}\big(\mathsf{M}_{0}(t)+o(t)\big).

See Section
7
for more details.

1.4.
Outline of Proof

Previous results regarding Question
1.1

typically rely on the sophisticated analytic machinery used to construct
the AH’s domain and the PAM’s solution.
For instance,
[
32
]
studies
\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})
’s large
n
asymptotics using
variational methods, and
[
3
]
studies the same quantities
using
\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{\kappa}}
’s analytic properties.

In contrast to this, the approach used in this paper is entirely probabilistic.
In fact, at a purely technical level,
our results (including the presence of logarithmic terms) can be viewed as a direct manifestation of the the small-time
asymptotics of intersection local times of planar Brownian motions and bridges.
In order to explain this, we now go over a brief outline of the main steps in our proof of Theorem
1.3
.

1.4.1.
Step 1: Construction of
H_{\kappa}
and
u_{\kappa}

By referring to the constructions carried out in
[
7
,
29
,
31
,
32
]
,
in Assumption
4.9
we take the existence of
H_{\kappa}
and its
renormalized limit in (
1.7
) for granted, with one specific choice
for the renormalization constant
\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
—see (
4.4
).
We then construct
u_{\kappa}(t,\cdot)
using
H_{\kappa}
’s semigroup.
We refer to Section
4

for detailed references.

Remark 1.5
.

This step is the only potential input from regularity structures, paracontrolled calculus, or any alternative
analytical machinery in this paper.

1.4.2.
Step 2: Feynman-Kac Formulas

By combining Step 1 with some technical uniform integrability estimates, we obtain the following smooth approximations
for the expectations and covariances that appear in Theorem
1.3
:

Proposition 1.6
.

Recall (
1.5
) and (
1.7
).
For every
\kappa,\varepsilon,t>0
, denote

(1.15)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)=\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{\kappa,\varepsilon}}\big]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{M}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)=\int_{D}u_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t,x)~\mathrm{d}x.

For every
\kappa>0
,
there exists a constant
\vartheta_{\kappa}>0
such that
for every
t\in(0,\vartheta_{\kappa})
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\big[\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)\big]=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbf{E}\big[\mathsf{T}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}}\big]\quad\text{and}\quad\displaystyle\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)\big]=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathsf{T}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}}\big];

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\big[\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)\big]=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbf{E}\big[\mathsf{M}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}}\big]\quad\text{and}\quad\displaystyle\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)\big]=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathsf{M}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}}\big].

Proposition
1.6
is proved in Section
6.2
.
The significance of this result is as follows: Since
\xi_{\varepsilon}
is smooth, we can use the classical Feynman-Kac formula
(i.e., (
4.2
))
to write
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)
and
\mathsf{M}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)
in terms of simple expectations involving Brownian bridges
and motions in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Thus, Proposition
1.6
suggests that if we can somehow
make sense of the
\varepsilon\to 0
limits of the renormalized moments of these Feynman-Kac formulas, then this will provide Feynman-Kac
formulas for the moments of
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)
and
\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)
. This seems especially plausible in light of
the results in
[
20
]
, which used this exact strategy to provide Feynman-Kac formulas for the moments
of the PAM on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

In this context, the main result in the second step of our proof of Theorem
1.3

is to carry out this program. More specifically,
in Proposition
6.1
we provide Feynman-Kac formulas
for the expectations and variances of
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)
and
\mathsf{M}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)
in terms of
intersection local times (see Section
5
for definitions and references),
which quantify how often Brownian motion and bridge
paths intersect themselves or each other. The logarithmic contributions
to (
1.10
) and (
1.11
)
emerge in this step, from the expected values of approximate self-intersection local times
(see Lemma
5.9
).

1.4.3.
Step 3: Intersection Local Times Asymptotics

Once Step 2 is completed,
the proof of Theorem
1.3
reduces to analyzing
the small
t
asymptotics of the intersection local times that appear in the
Feynman-Kac formulas for
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)
’s and
\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)
’s
moments. This analysis is carried out in Section
7
.

2.
Past Results, Motivation, and Applications

2.1.
Past Results

2.1.1.
Localization/Intermittency in Small Eigenvalues/Large Time

The majority of prior works on Question
1.1
concern
the effect of
\kappa
on the small eigenvalues and eigenfunctions
of the AH, or the large-time asymptotics of the PAM. These two
problems are closely related thanks to the spectral expansion

(2.1)

\displaystyle u_{\kappa}(t,\cdot)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})}\big\langle\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa}),\mathbf{1}_{D}\big\rangle\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa}),\qquad t>0.

Indeed, the leading order contributions
to (
2.1
) as
t\to\infty
are obviously
determined by
\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})}
’s magnitude and
\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa})
’s shape for small
n
.

The main motivation for studying the effect of
\kappa
on

H_{\kappa}
’s smallest eigenvalues comes from the localization phenomenon,
first described in lattice models by Anderson

[
2
]
(see, e.g.,
[
39
]
for a survey).
That is, for small
n
, it is expected that the eigenfunction mass

|\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa})|^{2}
transitions from being delocalized when
\kappa=0

to being localized when
\kappa>0
,
with the latter effect being increasingly pronounced as
\kappa
gets larger.
We refer to
[
1
, Figures 1 and 2]
for an illustration in the case where
D

is a square.
This phenomenon is very well understood in the one-dimensional AH (on
\mathbb{R}
or an interval),
e.g.,
[
9
,
11
]
.
Related results for the two-dimensional AH include asymptotics of
H_{\kappa}
’s lowest eigenvalues

[
1
,
7
,
25
,
29
]
, and computations of
H_{\kappa}
’s
density of states
[
31
,
30
]
.

For the PAM, one is interested in the large-time asymptotics of
u_{\kappa}(t,\cdot)

due to the intermittency phenomenon, first described in lattice models by
Gärtner and Molchanov
[
15
,
16
]

(see, e.g.,
[
27
]
for a survey).
That is, as
t\to\infty
, all heat rapidly escapes
D
through the boundary, whereby
u_{0}(t,\cdot)\to 0

at rate
\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{1}(H_{0})}
.
In contrast, the PAM’s solution
u_{\kappa}(t,\cdot)
for
\kappa>0
develops
sharp and narrow peak-like formations (which correspond to
\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa})^{\prime}s

localized shapes)
that grow exponentially fast.
Prior works in this direction concerning the PAM on
\mathbb{R}^{2}

or square domains
include studies of
u_{\kappa}(t,\cdot)
’s almost-sure asymptotics

[
28
]
and of
u_{\kappa}(t,\cdot)
’s moments
[
4
,
6
,
17
]
.

2.1.2.
Delocalization in Large Eigenvalues/Small Time

Alternatively, one might ask how the large eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of

H_{\kappa}
are affected by the transition from
\kappa=0
to
\kappa>0
. In view of
(
2.1
), this is equivalent to studying

u_{\kappa}(t,\cdot)
as
t\to 0
. In sharp contrast to localization and intermittency,
the phenomenon of interest in this setting is delocalization. That is, one expects that despite the
presence of the random noise, the behavior of
\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa}),\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa})
for large
n

and
u_{\kappa}(t,x)
for small
t
is not significantly altered by the transition from
\kappa=0
to
\kappa>0
.
See
[
9
,
10
,
12
]
for a comprehensive analysis of
the transition from localization to delocalization in the one-dimensional AH,
wherein
H_{\kappa}
’s spectrum becomes increasingly similar to that of
H_{0}
at large energies
(i.e.,
[
10
, Theorem 1.7 and Remark 1]
).
Moving back to the setting of this paper, to the best of our knowledge, there are only three previous works of this nature
that apply to the AH on two- or three-dimensional spaces:

In
[
32
]
, Mouzard consructed
H_{\kappa}
on a certain class
of two-dimensional compact Riemannian manifolds
M
, which includes bounded domains with smooth
boundary as a special case. Using this construction, he then proved that for any
\kappa\geq 0
,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\#\big(\{n\geq 1:\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})\leq\lambda\}\big)=\tfrac{\mathsf{A}(M)}{2\pi}\lambda+o(\lambda)\qquad\text{as }\lambda\to\infty

almost surely, where
\#(S)
denotes the cardinality of a set
S
,
and
\mathsf{A}(M)
denotes the Riemann volume of the manifold
M
.
When
\kappa=0
, this reduces to the classical Weyl law
for the Laplacian eigenvalues
[
43
]
. In particular, (
2.2
) showed that
the leading order term in
\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})
’s asymptotics remains the same
whether
\kappa=0
or
\kappa>0
.

Remark 2.1
.

Using standard Abelian/Tauberian theorems
(e.g.,
[
14
, Theorem 2, Page 445]
), (
2.2
) is equivalent
to the trace asymptotic

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{\kappa}}\big]=\tfrac{\mathsf{A}(M)}{2\pi}t^{-1}+o(t^{-1})\qquad\text{as }t\to 0

for all
\kappa\geq 0
. If we restrict to the special case where
D
is a bounded domain,
then this implies (
1.12
).

Next, in
[
3
]
, Bailleul, Dang, and Mouzard provided
several extensions and refinements of the results in
[
32
]
in the special case of boundaryless manifolds.
Among many other things,
they proved the following:
Consider
H_{\kappa}
on some closed Riemannian compact
surface
M
. For every
\varepsilon>0
,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{\kappa}}\big]=\tfrac{\mathsf{A}(M)}{2\pi}t^{-1}+O(t^{-1/2-\varepsilon})\qquad\text{as }t\to 0.

almost surely—see the first display in
[
3
, Section 4.4]
.

Finally, in
[
31
]
, Matsuda and van Zuijlen constructed

H_{\kappa}
with general singular noise on bounded domains
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with Lipschitz boundary for
d=2,3
,
with either Dirichlet or Neumann boundary condition.
Then, on the way to calculating
H_{\kappa}
’s density of states, they extended Mouzard’s Weyl law
(i.e., (
2.2
) and (
2.3
))
for a wide class of singular noises, any
domain with Lipschitz boundary in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
and with either Dirichlet or Neumann boundary condition;
see
[
31
, Proposition 5.17]
.

Remark 2.2
.

In light of the generality of these results, it is natural to ask
if Theorem
1.3
and its corollaries (the latter of which are stated below
in Section
2.2
) are true in the same generality.
The limitations on the setting considered in this paper comes from our method
of proof. We expect that, up to overcoming some potentially challenging technical difficulties
(e.g., Open Problem
3.7
),
the proof method in this paper could be extended to some, but not all of the
settings studied in
[
3
,
31
,
30
]
.
See Section
3.3
for more details on this point.

2.2.
Motivation and Applications

The Weyl law stated in (
2.4
)
naturally leads to a tantalizing open-ended question,
which was the main motivation for our work:

Question 2.3
.

Are there more features of the Dirichlet AH/PAM on
D
(i.e., beyond the Weyl law)
that remain unchanged when going from
\kappa=0
to
\kappa>0
? More generally, when
do we begin to ”feel” the presence of
\kappa>0
in the large-
n
asymptotics of
the eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})
?

In this context, the applications of our result are inspired by some of the most important classical
developments regarding the
t\to 0
asymptotics of
\mathsf{T}_{0}(t)
and
\mathsf{M}_{0}(t)
:

2.2.1.
Spectral Geometry

Our first application is motivated by the
classical fact that if
\partial D
is sufficiently regular, then an impressive amount of information about
D
’s geometry can be
recovered from
H_{0}
’s eigenvalues alone.
For instance, it has been known since the 1950’s (and conjectured since the 1910’s)
that
H_{0}
’s eigenvalues also determine
D
’s boundary length:

Theorem 2.4

(
[
26
,
35
]
)
.

If
\partial D
is sufficiently regular (e.g., smooth), then

(2.5)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{0}(t)=\tfrac{\mathsf{A}(D)}{2\pi}t^{-1}-\tfrac{\mathsf{L}(\partial D)}{4\sqrt{2\pi}}t^{-1/2}+O(1)\qquad\text{as }t\to 0,

where
\mathsf{L}(\partial D)
is the length of the curve

\partial D
.

Remark 2.5
.

We note that (
2.5
) is not the full extent of the information about
D
that can be
recovered from
\mathsf{T}_{0}(t)
; see Section
3.1
for more details.

It is thus natural to wonder if one could improve (
2.2
)/(
2.3
) to such
an extent that
\mathsf{L}(\partial D)
or more could be recovered from
\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})
’s eigenvalues
when
\kappa>0
.
Our first application of Theorem
1.3
is to show that this is indeed the case:

Corollary 2.6
.

Suppose that the hypotheses in Theorem
1.3

hold, and that
\mathsf{T}_{0}(t)
has the asymptotic expansion in (
2.5
).
Let
t_{1}>t_{2}>\cdots>0
be a vanishing sequence of positive numbers.
The following holds for any fixed
\kappa>0
:

(1)

If
t_{n}\leq cn^{-1/2-\varepsilon}
for some fixed
c,\varepsilon>0
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}2\pi t_{n}\,\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t_{n})=\mathsf{A}(D)\qquad\text{almost surely}.

(2)

If
t_{n}\leq cn^{-1-\varepsilon}
for some fixed
c,\varepsilon>0
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}4\sqrt{2\pi t_{n}}\left(\tfrac{\mathsf{A}(D)}{2\pi}t_{n}^{-1}-\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t_{n})\right)=\mathsf{L}(\partial D)\qquad\text{almost surely}.

See Section
8.1
for a proof.

2.2.2.
Boundary Fractal Geometry

Our second application is inspired by the following seminal result
due to van den Berg:

Theorem 2.7

(
[
41
]
)
.

Suppose that
D
is simply connected and that
\partial D
is Minkowski nondegenerate with
Minkowski dimension
\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)\in(1,2)
. That is,

0<\liminf_{r\to 0}\frac{\mathsf{A}(\partial D_{r})}{r^{2-\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)}}\leq\limsup_{r\to 0}\frac{\mathsf{A}(\partial D_{r})}{r^{2-\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)}}<\infty,

where we define

\partial D_{r}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\inf_{y\in\partial D}|x-y|<r\right\}.

There exists constants
c\in(1,\infty)
and
\vartheta>0
such that

(2.6)

\displaystyle c^{-1}t^{1-\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)/2}\leq\mathsf{A}(D)-\mathsf{M}_{0}(t)\leq ct^{1-\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)/2},\qquad t\in(0,\vartheta).

Thus, the small
t
asymptotics of
\mathsf{M}_{0}(t)
uniquely identify
\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)
.
Our main result implies that this can also be done with
\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)
:

Corollary 2.8
.

Suppose that the hypotheses in Theorem
1.3

hold, and that
\mathsf{M}_{0}(t)
satisfies (
2.6
).
Let
t_{1}>t_{2}>\cdots>0
be a vanishing sequence of positive numbers.
If
t_{n}\leq cn^{-1/\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)-\varepsilon}
for some fixed
c,\varepsilon>0
,
then for every
\kappa>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}2-2\frac{\log\big(\mathsf{A}(D)-\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t_{n})\big)}{\log t_{n}}=\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)\qquad\text{almost surely}.

See Section
8.2
for a proof.

Remark 2.9
.

There exists asymptotics of
\mathsf{A}(D)-\mathsf{M}_{0}(t)
that are more precise
than (
2.6
) when
\partial D
is
a specific fractal or regular;
see
[
36
, Section 1.2]
and references therein for
a survey of results of this nature.
For instance, if
\partial D
is smooth, then

\mathsf{M}_{0}(t)=\mathsf{A}(D)-\tfrac{\sqrt{2}\mathsf{L}(\partial D)}{\sqrt{\pi}}t^{1/2}+\tfrac{\pi\chi(D)}{2}t+O(t^{3/2})\qquad\text{as }t\to 0

where
\chi(D)
denotes the Euler characteristic;
see
[
42
]
.
In this context,
our main result also implies that one can recover
\mathsf{A}(D)
and
\mathsf{L}(\partial D)

almost surely from the small
t
asymptotics of
\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)
.
Given that this is very similar to the result involving
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)
in Corollary
2.6
,
we omit this statement in the interest of brevity.

2.2.3.
Variance Recovery

Lastly, we confirm with the following result that
\kappa>0
can be recovered from
H_{\kappa}
’s eigenvalues:

Corollary 2.10
.

Suppose that the hypotheses in Theorem
1.3

hold.
Let
t_{1}>t_{2}>\cdots>0
be a vanishing sequence of positive numbers
such that
t_{n}\leq\tilde{c}\mathrm{e}^{-cn^{1/2+\varepsilon}}
for some fixed
\tilde{c},c,\varepsilon>0
.
For every
\kappa>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{4\pi^{2}}{\mathsf{A}(D)\log t_{n}}\Big(\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t_{n})-\mathsf{T}_{0}(t_{n})\Big)=\kappa^{2}\qquad\text{almost surely}.

See Section
8.1
for a proof.

Remark 2.11
.

Theorem
1.3
implies that

\kappa
can also be recovered from the
t\to 0
asymptotics of
\frac{2\pi}{\mathsf{A}(D)t\log t}\big(\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)-\mathsf{M}_{0}(t)\big)
;
we omit this statement for brevity.

3.
Open Problems

Together with the pioneering works
[
3
,
31
,
32
]

outlined in Section
2.1.2
, the
main result, applications, and method of proof in this paper naturally
lead to a large number of interesting open problems, which we hope will
lay the foundation for an active research program. In this section, we share
a sample of the problems that we find the most promising.

3.1.
Complete Expansion for
\kappa>0

It is known that if
\partial D
is sufficiently regular,
then there exists an infinite sequence of constants
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2},\ldots\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{0}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\,t^{-1+n/2}\qquad\text{as }t\to 0;

see, e.g.,
[
18
, Section 3.0]
. The constants
c_{n}
—the first
two of which were stated in Theorem

2.4
—are determined by
various features of
D
’s geometry.
For instance,
c_{2}=\frac{\chi(D)}{6}
, where
\chi(D)
denotes
D
’s
Euler characteristic;
see, e.g.,
[
38
, Page 468]
and references
therein for exact formulas for
c_{3},\ldots,c_{6}
.
In this context, our first open problem consists of understanding how the full expansion
(
3.1
) is affected by the transition
from
\kappa=0
to
\kappa>0
:

Open Problem 3.1
.

Let
\kappa>0
.
Does there exist an asymptotic expansion

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)-\mathsf{T}_{0}(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathsf{a}_{n}(\kappa,t)\qquad\text{as }t\to 0,

where
\mathsf{a}_{n}(\kappa,t)
are possibly random coefficients of decreasing size
(e.g., for every
n\geq 0
, one has
\mathsf{a}_{n+1}(\kappa,t)=o\big(\mathsf{a}_{n}(\kappa,t)\big)
as
t\to 0
)?
Can one characterize the joint distributions (over
n
and
t
) of the processes
\mathsf{a}_{n}(\kappa,t)
?

Our main result only partially address this problem, as follows:

(1)

\mathsf{a}_{0}(\kappa,t)=\tfrac{\kappa^{2}\mathsf{A}(D)}{4\pi^{2}}\log t
is deterministic.

(2)

The variance of
\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathsf{a}_{n}(\kappa,t)
is of order
O(1)
as
t\to 0
.

At best, the technique used in this paper could provide insights into the joint moments
of the
\mathsf{a}_{n}(\kappa,t)
’s. That being said, the pathwise Feynman-Kac formula for the two-dimensional
PAM on a box proved by König, Perkowski and van Zuijlen in

[
28
, Theorem 2.23]
seems to suggest that computing
the first few terms in
(
3.2
) exactly might still be possible using probabilistic arguments.

3.2.
What Can One ”Hear” About the Shape of the AH’s Domain?

The geometric information contained in the constants
c_{n}

in (
3.1
) naturally leads to
the following question:
”Does the spectrum of
H_{0}

characterize
D
?”
For several decades, this was considered
a very important open problem in analysis, which was widely
known as
”Can one hear the shape of a drum?”

due to an influential paper by Kac
[
26
]
. In the early 1990’s,
the problem was settled in the negative by Gordon, Webb, and
Wolpert
[
19
]
, who showed that
there exists non-isometric planar domains that are isospectral
(i.e., domains on which
H_{0}
has the same spectrum). One can then ask:

Open Problem 3.2
.

Are there non-isometric planar domains
on which
H_{\kappa}
’s eigenvalues have the same joint distribution when
\kappa>0
?

Corollaries
2.6
and
2.10
imply that if
\partial D

is sufficiently regular, then
H_{\kappa}
’s eigenvalue point processes
(for different
D
’s and
\kappa
’s) become mutually singular as soon as
one changes
D
’s area,
\partial D
’s length, or the strength of the noise
\kappa
. This constrains the possible
constructions that would answer Open Problem
3.2
.

However, in addition to leading to exact analogues of classical spectral geometry problems,
injecting randomness in
H_{0}
could potentially lead to entirely new phenomena.
For instance, while two domains
D
are either isospectral or not
in the deterministic problem, the spectra of
H_{\kappa}
for
\kappa>0

over different domains could conceivably interpolate between these two extremes:

Open Problem 3.3
.

Are there pairs of non-isometric domains
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
on which

H_{\kappa}
’s eigenvalue point processes are neither mutually singular nor equal in distribution?
If so, what are the possible relationships between the two laws (e.g., mutual absolute continuity, or something else)?

Corollaries
2.6
and
2.10
once again constrain the possible relationships
that such hypothetical domains might have.

3.3.
General Manifolds, Noises, and Boundary Conditions

Following-up on Remark
2.2
, it is natural to ask the following:

Open Problem 3.4
.

Do suitable analogues of Theorem
1.3
and Corollaries
2.6
,

2.8
, and
2.10
still hold if one or several of the following changes are made?

(1)

D
is replaced by a compact Riemannian manifold of any dimension.

(2)

\xi
is replaced by a more general singular noise
(e.g.,
\xi
could be Gaussian with covariance
\mathbf{E}[\xi(x)\xi(y)]=\mathfrak{K}(x,y)

for the Riesz kernel
\mathfrak{K}(x,y)=|x-y|^{-\omega}
,
\omega>0
, or the fractional
kernel
\mathfrak{K}(x,y)=\prod_{i}|x_{i}-y_{i}|^{-\omega_{i}}
,
\omega_{i}>0
).

(3)

Assuming there is a boundary, the Dirichlet boundary condition is replaced by
another boundary condition (e.g., Neumann, Robin, or mixed).

Among other things, a positive resolution of this problem would
improve every known Weyl law for the AHs (i.e., the results of
[
3
,
31
,
30
]

outlined in Section
2.1.2
).
With this out of the way, two important remarks are in order:

Remark 3.5
.

The currently-known techniques used to construct the AH and PAM
only work for a certain class of singular operators and PDEs, which are known as
subcritical (e.g.,
[
23
, Page 272]
). Among other things, this puts constraints
on the possible combinations of noises and manifolds for which
H_{\kappa}
and
u_{\kappa}
can be
defined. (For instance, the AH and PAM with white noise are not expected to
make sense on manifolds whose dimension is greater or equal to
4
.)
Modulo these kinds of limitations,
we expect that the short answer to Open Problem
3.4
is essentially positive.

Remark 3.6
.

That being said, we do not expect that the method of proof
outlined in Section
1.4
can be used to settle Open Problem
3.4
in every
case for which the AH and PAM can be defined. This is because, if the noise is too irregular,
then the moments of
\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{\kappa}}\big]
and
u_{\kappa}(t,x)
are infinite for all
t>0
,
thus rendering a statement analogous to Theorem
1.3
impossible.
(For instance, it is known that the moments of the PAM with white noise are always infinite in three dimensions;
e.g.,
[
29
, Paragraph following Theorem 2]

and
[
20
, Remark 1.7]
.) That said, we expect that our method could be used whenever
the moments of
\mathrm{Tr}\big[\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{\kappa}}\big]
and
u_{\kappa}(t,x)
are finite for all
t>0
,
or at least for all

t\in(0,\vartheta_{\kappa})
smaller than some threshold
\vartheta_{\kappa}>0
.

In this context, we point out that
the main obstacle to using the proof method
outlined in Section
1.4

with more general manifolds is as follows: To the best of our knowledge,
intersection local times have so far only been constructed for Brownian motions/bridges
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular,
following-up on Remark
2.2
, this explains why we have restricted our
attention to the AH and PAM with white noise and Dirichlet boundary condition on flat domains in this paper. That being said,
the extent of the improvements on the Weyl law in (
1.12
)
achieved in this paper motivates studying intersection
local times in curved spaces. For instance:

Open Problem 3.7
.

Let
M
be a compact Riemannian surface, with or without boundary.
Construct, and then study the small
t
asymptotics of the intersection local times
of Brownian motions and bridges (with reflection if there is a boundary) on
M
. That is, replicate all the results in Section

5
on general surfaces.

Once Open Problem
3.7
is solved,
adapting our proof of Theorem
1.3
to this more general setting
should be relatively straightforward.

3.4.
Transition of Asymptotics as
\varepsilon\to 0

One of the most striking features of Corollary
2.10

is that, at first glance, the eigenvalues of
H_{\kappa}
appear to contain more information
than the eigenvalues of
H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
.
More specifically, suppose that
\mathsf{T}_{0}(t)

satisfies (
3.1
), and let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed.
Recall the definition of
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)
in (
1.15
).
As
\xi_{\varepsilon}
is smooth, a straightforward calculation using the classical Feynman-Kac formula
(e.g., (
4.2
)) yields

(3.3)

\mathsf{T}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)=\mathsf{T}_{0}(t)-\frac{\kappa}{2\pi}\int_{D}\xi_{\varepsilon}(x)~\mathrm{d}x+o(1)\\
=\tfrac{\mathsf{A}(D)}{2\pi}t^{-1}-\tfrac{\mathsf{L}(\partial D)}{4\sqrt{2\pi}}t^{-1/2}+\left(\tfrac{\chi(D)}{6}-\frac{\kappa}{2\pi}\int_{D}\xi_{\varepsilon}(x)~\mathrm{d}x\right)+o(1)\qquad\text{as }t\to 0.

This implies that
\mathsf{A}(D)
and
\mathsf{L}(\partial D)
can
be recovered from
H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
’s eigenvalues in a way that is analogous to Corollary
2.6
-(1) and -(2):

\lim_{t\to 0}2\pi t\,\mathsf{T}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)=\mathsf{A}(D)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{t\to 0}4\sqrt{2\pi t}\left(\tfrac{\mathsf{A}(D)}{2\pi}t^{-1}-\mathsf{T}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)\right)=\mathsf{L}(\partial D).

However, there does not appear to be an analogue of Corollary
2.10
for
\varepsilon>0
.
Indeed, the dependence on
\kappa
in (
3.3
)
is contained in the constant order term, which is Gaussian with mean
\tfrac{\chi(D)}{6}
and variance

\frac{\kappa^{2}}{4\pi^{2}}\iint_{D^{2}}p_{\varepsilon}(x-y)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

Given this sharp contrast between the behavior of
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)
and
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)

as
t\to 0
, it would be interesting to better understand how these asymptotics gradually
change nature as
\varepsilon\to 0
. For this purpose, we propose the following:

Open Problem 3.8
.

Let
\mathsf{e}:(0,1)\to(0,1)
be such that
\mathsf{e}(t)\to 0
as
t\to 0
.
Calculate the small
t
asymptotics of
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa,\mathsf{e}(t)}(t)
as
t\to 0
.

We expect that there exists some critical rate of decay
\mathfrak{d}(t)
such that

(1)

if
\mathsf{e}(t)\gg\mathfrak{d}(t)
, then
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa,\mathsf{e}(t)}(t)
’s asymptotics are similar to
that of
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)
for fixed
\varepsilon
;

(2)

if
\mathsf{e}(t)\ll\mathfrak{d}(t)
, then
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa,\mathsf{e}(t)}(t)
’s asymptotics are similar to
that of
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)
.

Identifying the precise nature of this hypothetical ”phase transition” would presumably shed light on the
transition between the Gaussian constant order term in (
3.3
)
and the emergence of the deterministic term
\tfrac{\kappa^{2}\mathsf{A}(D)}{4\pi^{2}}\log t
in (
1.10
).

4.
Theorem
1.3
Step 1: Construction of
H_{\kappa}
and
u_{\kappa}

As hinted at in (
1.7
),
we assume in this paper that
H_{\kappa}
is constructed as the renormalized limit of the smooth approximations
H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
.
Given that the Feynman-Kac formula for
H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
is a fundamental aspect of our approach,
we take this opportunity to define
H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
via its semigroup:

Definition 4.1
.

We use
B
to denote a standard Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
t>0
,
we let
B^{x}=(B|B(0)=x)
denote the Brownian motion started at
x
, and let
B^{x,y}_{t}=(B|B(0)=x,~B(t)=y)

denote the Brownian bridge from
x
to
y
on the time interval
[0,t]
. Finally, when we require multiple
independent copies of Brownian motion, we use the notation
{{}_{1}}B,{{}_{2}}B,{{}_{3}}B,\ldots

(in particular, we use
{{}_{i}}B^{x_{i}}
and
{{}_{i}}B^{x_{i},y_{i}}_{t}
,
i\geq 1
, to denote independent Brownian motions
started at
x_{i}
and independent Brownian bridges from
x_{i}
to
y_{i}
on the time interval
[0,t]
).

Definition 4.2
.

For any stochastic process
Z
that takes values in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
we denote the hitting time of
D
’s complement as

\tau_{D}(Z)=\inf\big\{t>0:Z(t)\not\in D\big\},

with the convention that
\tau_{D}(Z)=\infty
if
Z
never exits
D
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
\xi:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to\mathbb{R}
be the Gaussian process with mean zero and
covariance (
1.1
). Viewing
\xi
as
a random Schwartz distribution, for every
\varepsilon>0
, we define
\xi_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

as

\xi_{\varepsilon}(x)=(\xi*p_{\varepsilon/2})(x)=\xi\big(p_{\varepsilon/2}(x-\cdot)\big),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{2},

where
*
denotes the convolution.

Remark 4.4
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
,

\xi_{\varepsilon}
is a continuous function since
p_{\varepsilon/2}
is smooth.
Moreover,
\xi_{\varepsilon}
is a Gaussian process with mean zero and covariance
(
1.6
), as can be shown with the following more general calculation: For any
\varepsilon,\tilde{\varepsilon}>0

and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

(4.1)

\mathbf{E}\big[\xi_{\varepsilon}(x)\xi_{\tilde{\varepsilon}}(y)\big]=\mathbf{E}\big[\xi\big(p_{\varepsilon/2}(x-\cdot)\big)\xi\big(p_{\tilde{\varepsilon}/2}(y-\cdot)\big)\big]\\
=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}p_{\varepsilon/2}(x-z)p_{\tilde{\varepsilon}/2}(y-z)~\mathrm{d}z=p_{(\varepsilon+\tilde{\varepsilon})/2}(x-y).

Definition 4.5
.

For any
\kappa,\varepsilon,t>0
, we let
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)
denote the integral operator on
L^{2}(D)
with kernel

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t;x,y)=p_{t}(x-y)\mathbf{E}_{B}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,y}_{t})>t\}}\exp\left(-\kappa\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{\varepsilon}\big(B^{x,y}_{t}(r)\big)~\mathrm{d}r\right)\right],\qquad x,y\in D,

where we assume that
B^{x,y}_{t}
and
\xi_{\varepsilon}
are independent, and we use
\mathbf{E}_{B}
to
denote the (conditional) expectation with respect to the randomness in
B
only.

We record the following elementary property of the
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)
’s:

Proposition 4.6
.

For every
\kappa,\varepsilon>0
,
the family
\{\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t):t>0\}
is a strongly continuous semigroup
of symmetric compact operators on
L^{2}(D)
.

By
[
40
, Chapter 1, (3.14) and (3.15)]
, it suffices to show that the function

V=\kappa\xi_{\varepsilon}
is in the Kato class; see
[
40
, Chapter 1, (2.4)]
for a definition.
Since
\xi_{\varepsilon}
is continuous and
D
is bounded,
\xi_{\varepsilon}
is bounded. Thus,

\lim_{t\to 0}\sup_{x\in D}\mathbf{E}_{B}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\big|\kappa\xi_{\varepsilon}\big(B^{x}(r)\big)\big|~\mathrm{d}r\right]\leq\lim_{t\to 0}t\kappa\|\xi_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}=0,

which concludes the proof by definition of the Kato class.
∎

We can now define
H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
and
u_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
as follows:

Definition 4.7
.

For every
\varepsilon,\kappa>0
,
we define
H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
as the the infinitesimal generator of
\{\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t):t>0\}
on
L^{2}(D)

(e.g.,
[
13
, Chapter II, Definition 1.2]
). Then, we define

u_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t,x)=\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{\kappa,\varepsilon}}\mathbf{1}_{D}(x)=\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x})>t\}}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{\varepsilon}\big(B^{x}(r)\big)~\mathrm{d}r\right)\right],\qquad t>0,~x\in D.

The following properties of
H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
are elementary:

Proposition 4.8
.

For every
\varepsilon,\kappa>0
,
the operator
H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}(D)
.
Its spectrum
\lambda_{1}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon})\leq\lambda_{2}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon})\leq\cdots
is purely discrete, bounded below, and has no accumulation point.
Moreover, the corresponding eigenfunctions
\psi_{1}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}),\psi_{2}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}),\ldots

form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(D)
. Finally, for any
f\in L^{2}(D)
,

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{\kappa,\varepsilon}}f=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon})}\big\langle\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}),f\big\rangle\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}).

See
[
40
, Chapter 1, Remark 4.14]
for the details on
H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
being self-adjoint.
See
[
40
, Chapter 3, (1.47)]
for the claims regarding
H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
’s eigenvalues
and eigenfunctions (as stated,
[
40
, Chapter 3, (1.47)]
applies to Schrödinger operators
with nonnegative potentials, but this can be trivially extended to potentials that are bounded
below—such as
\kappa\xi_{\varepsilon}
—by adding a constant to the potential).
Once the properties of
H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
’s spectrum are established, (
4.3
)
follows from the facts that the right-hand side of (
4.3
) is
clearly a semigroup that generates
H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
, and that semigroups are uniquely determined by
their generators (e.g.,
[
13
, Chapter II, Theorem 1.4]
).
∎

With this in hand, we may now state our assumption regarding the existence of
H_{\kappa}
as a renormalized limit
of
H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
, and then define
u_{\kappa}
using its semigroup:

Assumption 4.9
.

There exists a random self-adjoint operator
H_{\kappa}
on
L^{2}(D)

that satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

H_{\kappa}
’s spectrum
\lambda_{1}(H_{\kappa})\leq\lambda_{2}(H_{\kappa})\leq\cdots
is purely discrete,
bounded below, and without accumulation point. The corresponding eigenfunctions
\psi_{1}(H_{\kappa}),\psi_{2}(H_{\kappa}),\ldots

form an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(D)
.

(2)

Define the renormalization constant

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}=\tfrac{\kappa^{2}}{2\pi}\log(1/\varepsilon),\qquad\varepsilon>0.

For every
n\geq 1
,

(4.5)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\big(\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon})+\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}\big)=\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})\qquad\text{in probability.}

(3)

It is possible to choose
\{\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}):n\geq 1\}
and
\{\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa}):n\geq 1\}

in such a way that for every
n\geq 1
,

(4.6)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\|\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon})-\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa})\|^{2}=0\qquad\text{in probability}.

Finally, for every
\kappa,t>0
, we define
u_{\kappa}(t,\cdot):D\to\mathbb{R}
as the function in (
2.1
).

We end this section with three remarks, the latter two of which
comment on previous works that constructed
H_{\kappa}

in a way that is consistent with Assumption
4.9
.

Remark 4.10
.

The caveat that we must ”choose” the eigenfunctions in such a way that (
4.6
) holds is caused by the following subtlety: If we only require that the eigenfunctions of a self-adjoint operator form
an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(D)
, then there is some arbitrariness in how exactly we choose the eigenfunctions as basis elements of their respective eigenspace. As pointed out in
[
29
, Remark 1.1]
, we can reformulate (
4.6
) without making references to eigenfunction choice
as follows: Suppose that
\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa}),\ldots,\psi_{n+m}(H_{\kappa})
is an orthonormal basis of the eigenspace of some eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})

(with
m=0
if
\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})
has multiplicity one). Then, the unit ball of the subspace spanned by
\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}),\ldots,\psi_{n+m}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon})

converges in probability to the unit ball of the subspace spanned by
\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa}),\ldots,\psi_{n+m}(H_{\kappa})
with respect to the Hausdorff metric induced by the

L^{2}
-norm on
L^{2}(D)
.

Remark 4.11
.

As shown in
[
29
, Lemma 4.8]
, the convergence

H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}\to H_{\kappa}
in the sense of (
4.5
)
and (
4.6
)/Remark
4.10
can be reduced to
the convergence in norm of the resolvent operators
(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}-z)^{-1}\to(H_{\kappa}-z)^{-1}

for some
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
, together with some uniform compactness estimate.
This was proved on the square
D=(0,L)^{2}
for some
L>0
in
[
29
, Proof of Theorem 1, Page 3225]
(see
[
7
]
for a similar construction), on domains with smooth boundary in
[
32
, Proposition 2.14]
,
and on arbitrary domains in
[
31
, Theorem 5.3]
.

Remark 4.12
.

One could replace our choice of
\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}
in (
4.4
) by

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}=\tfrac{\kappa^{2}}{2\pi}\log(1/\varepsilon)+c+o(1),\qquad\varepsilon\to 0

for any arbitrary constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
and vanishing correction
o(1)

without impacting the conclusion of Assumption
4.9
. This partly explains why
the renormalization constant for the two-dimensional AH and PAM is often written as

\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\pi}\log(1/\varepsilon)+O(1)
(e.g.,
[
24
, (1.2)]
or
[
29
, Section 4.1]
).
If we were to choose (
4.7
) instead, then our main result would remain unchanged.
That being said, not all of the works cited in Remark
4.11
identify that the renormalization must be of the form (
4.7
). However, as explained in Remark
5.10
,
it is crucial for our method of proof that this specific renormalization be used.

Intersection Local Times

In this section, we collect several results on the intersection local times of planar Brownian motions
and bridges, which are essential in Steps 2 and 3 of the outline of proof presented in
Section
1.4
. The main references for the known results in this section
are the monograph
[
5
]
, which concerns Brownian motions, and Section 3 of the recent paper
[
30
]
,
which concerns Brownian bridges.

5.1.
Outline of Results

Definition 5.1
.

Recall (
1.4
).
Let
Z
and
Z_{1},Z_{2}
be stochastic processes taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
For every
\varepsilon>0
and bounded Borel measurable set
A\subset[0,\infty)^{2}
, define
the approximate self-intersection local time (SILT) of
Z
on
A
as

\beta^{\varepsilon}_{A}(Z)=\int_{A}p_{\varepsilon}\big(Z(r_{1})-Z(r_{2})\big)~\mathrm{d}r,

and the approximate mutual intersection local time (MILT) of
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
on
A
as

\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{A}(Z_{1},Z_{2})=\int_{A}p_{\varepsilon}\big(Z_{1}(r_{1})-Z_{2}(r_{2})\big)~\mathrm{d}r.

Remark 5.2
.

Given that
p_{\varepsilon}=p_{\varepsilon/2}*p_{\varepsilon/2}
(see (
4.1
)), we can equivalently write

\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{A}(Z_{1},Z_{2})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{A}p_{\varepsilon/2}\big(Z_{1}(r_{1})-y\big)p_{\varepsilon/2}\big(Z_{2}(r_{2})-y\big)~\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}y.

In particular, when
A=A_{1}\times A_{2}
, this reduces to

(5.1)

\displaystyle\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{A}(Z_{1},Z_{2})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{2}\int_{A_{i}}p_{\varepsilon/2}\big(Z_{i}(r)-y\big)\mathrm{d}r~\mathrm{d}y.

Since
p_{\varepsilon}\to\delta_{0}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the SILT and MILT approximate functionals that
count how often the path of a stochastic process
Z
intersects itself,
or how often the paths of two stochastic processes
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
intersect each other.
We now discuss the aspects of the theories of SILTs and MILTs that we need in this paper:

5.2.
Proof of Lemma

We only prove the result for
Z=B^{x,x}_{t}

Fix
\varepsilon>0

Notice that, by Brownian scaling,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\big\{B_{t}^{x,x}(r):r\in[0,t]\big\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{distr.}}}{{=}}\big\{x+\sqrt{t}\,B^{0,0}_{1}(r/t):r\in[0,t]\big\}.

Therefore,

\beta^{\varepsilon}_{A}(B^{x,x}_{t})=\int_{A}p_{\varepsilon}\big(B_{t}^{x,x}(r_{1})-B_{t}^{x,x}(r_{2})\big)~\mathrm{d}r\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{distr.}}}{{=}}\int_{A}p_{\varepsilon}\big(\sqrt{t}B_{1}^{0,0}(r_{1}/t)-\sqrt{t}B_{1}^{0,0}(r_{2}/t)\big)~\mathrm{d}r.

If we define

\beta^{\varepsilon}_{A}(B^{x,x}_{t})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{distr.}}}{{=}}\int_{A/t}t^{2}p_{\varepsilon}\big(\sqrt{t}B_{1}^{0,0}(s_{1})-\sqrt{t}B_{1}^{0,0}(s_{2})\big)~\mathrm{d}s.

By definition of the Gaussian kernel (

t^{2}p_{\varepsilon}(\sqrt{t}x)=t^{2}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-|x|^{2}/2(\varepsilon/t)}}{2\pi\varepsilon}=tp_{\varepsilon/t}(x),

hence

\beta^{\varepsilon}_{A}(B^{x,x}_{t})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{distr.}}}{{=}}t\int_{A/t}p_{\varepsilon/t}\big(B_{t}^{0,0}(s_{1})-B_{t}^{0,0}(s_{2})\big)~\mathrm{d}s=t\,\beta^{\varepsilon/t}_{A/t}(B^{0,0}_{1}).

5.3.
Proof of Theorem

t>0

For sake of readability, for the remainder of this proof we denote

Given that the

\lim_{\varepsilon,\tilde{\varepsilon}\to 0}\mathbf{E}\left[(X_{\varepsilon}-X_{\tilde{\varepsilon}})^{2}\right]=0.

Then,

(5.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m}\to 0}\mathbf{E}[X_{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots X_{\varepsilon_{m}}]=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A_{1}}(x_{1},z)\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A_{2}}(x_{2},z)~\mathrm{d}z,

, we define

(5.7)

\displaystyle\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A}(x,z)=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{m}}\int_{(A^{m})_{<}}\frac{p_{r_{1}}(z_{\sigma(1)}-x)\cdot\prod_{j=2}^{m}p_{r_{j}-r_{j-1}}(z_{\sigma(j)}-z_{\sigma(j-1)})\cdot p_{t-r_{m}}(x-z_{\sigma(m)})}{p_{t}(0)}~\mathrm{d}r.

Toward this end, we begin by calculating the moments of the form
\mathbf{E}[X_{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots X_{\varepsilon_{m}}]

for fixed
\varepsilon_{j}>0
. For this purpose, if we use (
5.1
) to write the MILT, then
the independence of
{{}_{i}}B^{x_{i},x_{i}}_{t}
for
i=1,2
and Tonelli’s theorem yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[X_{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots X_{\varepsilon_{m}}]=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{2}\int_{A_{i}^{m}}\mathbf{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{m}p_{\varepsilon_{j}/2}\big({{}_{i}}B^{x_{i},x_{i}}(r_{j})-y_{j}\big)\right]~\mathrm{d}r~\mathrm{d}y.

The expectation above is easier to calculate if the
r_{j}
’s are ordered thanks to the
following explicit calculation:
If
0<r_{1}<r_{2}<\cdots<r_{m}<t
, then for any
z\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left[B^{x,x}_{t}(r_{1})\in\mathrm{d}z_{1},\ldots,B^{x,x}_{t}(r_{m})\in\mathrm{d}z_{m}\right]=\frac{\mathbf{P}\left[B^{x}(r_{1})\in\mathrm{d}z_{1},\ldots,B^{x}_{t}(r_{m})\in\mathrm{d}z_{m},B^{x}(t)\in\mathrm{d}x\right]}{\mathbf{P}\big[B^{x}(t)\in\mathrm{d}x\big]}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{r_{1}}(z_{1}-x)\cdot\prod_{j=2}^{m}p_{r_{j}-r_{j-1}}(z_{j}-z_{j-1})\cdot p_{t-r_{m}}(x-z_{m})}{p_{t}(0)}.

Toward this end, for any permutation
\sigma\in\Sigma_{m}

and Borel measurable
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
, let

K_{<}\circ\sigma=\big\{r=(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{m})\in K:r_{\sigma(1)}<r_{\sigma(2)}<\cdots<r_{\sigma(m)}\big\}.

Note that we can write any Borel measurable
K
as

K=\left(\bigcup_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{m}}K_{<}\circ\sigma\right)\cup L,

where the union is disjoint and
L
has Lebesgue measure zero.
Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[X_{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots X_{\varepsilon_{m}}]=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{2}\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{m}}\int_{(A_{i}^{p})_{<}\circ\sigma}\mathbf{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{m}p_{\varepsilon_{\sigma(j)}/2}\big({{}_{i}}B^{x_{i},x_{i}}(r_{\sigma(j)})-y_{\sigma(j)}\big)\right]~\mathrm{d}r\right)\mathrm{d}y.

If we now apply the change of variables
r_{j}=r_{\sigma(j)}
, then we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[X_{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots X_{\varepsilon_{m}}]=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{2}\left(\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{m}}\int_{(A_{i}^{p})_{<}}\mathbf{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{m}p_{\varepsilon_{\sigma(j)}/2}\big({{}_{i}}B^{x_{i},x_{i}}(r_{j})-y_{\sigma(j)}\big)\right]~\mathrm{d}r\right)\mathrm{d}y.

With the components
r_{j}
ordered, we can now calculate

\mathbf{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{m}p_{\varepsilon_{j}/2}\big({{}_{i}}B^{x_{i},x_{i}}(r_{\sigma(j)})-y_{\sigma(j)}\big)\right]=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}p_{\varepsilon_{j}/2}(z_{j}-y_{j})\right)\\
\cdot\frac{p_{r_{1}}(z_{\sigma(1)}-x_{i})\cdot\prod_{j=2}^{m}p_{r_{j}-r_{j-1}}(z_{\sigma(j)}-z_{\sigma(j-1)})\cdot p_{t-r_{m}}(x_{i}-z_{\sigma(m)})}{p_{t}(0)}~\mathrm{d}z.

In particular, if we recall the definition of
\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A}(x,z)
in (
5.7
), then
we get

(5.8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[X_{\varepsilon_{1}}\cdots X_{\varepsilon_{m}}]=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}p_{\varepsilon_{j}/2}(z_{j}-y_{j})\right)\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A_{1}}(x_{1},z)\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A_{2}}(x_{2},z)~\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}z.

By standard results on the
L^{p}
convergence of functions convolved with a smooth mollifier
(e.g.,
[
5
, Lemma 2.2.2 and Page 32]
), (
5.6
) will follow if we show that
the function
z\mapsto\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A_{1}}(x_{1},z)\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A_{2}}(x_{2},z)
is integrable, i.e.:

(5.9)

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A_{1}}(x_{1},z)\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A_{2}}(x_{2},z)~\mathrm{d}z<\infty.

By definition of
\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A}(x,z)
in (
5.7
),
it is clear that
\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A}(x,z)\leq\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,[0,t]}(x,z)
if
A\subset[0,t]
.
Thus, by Hölder’s inequality,

(5.10)

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A_{1}}(x_{1},z)\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A_{2}}(x_{2},z)~\mathrm{d}z\leq\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,[0,t]}(x_{i},z)^{2}~\mathrm{d}z\right)^{1/2}.

Then, if we apply the change of variables
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{m})\mapsto(z_{1}+x_{i},\ldots,z_{m}+x_{i})
for
i=1,2
above, it is clear from
(
5.7
) that (
5.10
) can be reformulated into

(5.11)

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A_{1}}(x_{1},z)\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A_{2}}(x_{2},z)~\mathrm{d}z\leq\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,[0,t]}(0,z)^{2}~\mathrm{d}z.

Given any two sets
A,\tilde{A}\subset[0,t]
whose intersection has Lebesgue measure zero, it is clear from (
5.7
) that

\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A}(0,z)+\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,\tilde{A}}(0,z)=\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A\cup\tilde{A}}(0,z)
.
If we apply this with
A=[0,t/2]
and
\tilde{A}=[t/2,t]
, then we get

\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,[0,t]}(0,z)^{2}=\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,[0,t/2]}(0,z)^{2}+2\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,[0,t/2]}(0,z)\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,[t/2,t]}(0,z)+\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,[t/2,t]}(0,z)^{2}.

If we apply the change of variables
(r_{1},\ldots,r_{m})\mapsto(t-r_{1},\ldots,t-r_{m})
in (
5.7
),
then we get that
\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,[t/2,t]}(0,z)=\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,[0,t/2]}(0,z)
. Thus, we can rewrite (
5.11
)
as

(5.12)

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A_{1}}(x_{1},z)\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A_{2}}(x_{2},z)~\mathrm{d}z\leq 4\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,[0,t/2]}(0,z)^{2}~\mathrm{d}z.

If
0\leq r_{m}\leq t/2
, then

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{p_{t-r_{m}}(z)}{p_{t}(0)}\leq\frac{2\pi t}{2\pi(t/2)}=2.

If we apply this to (
5.7
),
then we get from (
5.12
) that

(5.13)

\displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A_{1}}(x_{1},z)\mathsf{g}^{m}_{t,A_{2}}(x_{2},z)~\mathrm{d}z\leq 8\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\mathsf{f}^{m}_{[0,t/2]}(z)^{2}~\mathrm{d}z.

where for any
m\geq 1
,
z\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}
, and bounded Borel set
A\subset[0,\infty)
, we define

(5.14)

\displaystyle\mathsf{f}^{m}_{A}(z)=\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{m}}\int_{(A^{m})_{<}}p_{r_{1}}(z_{\sigma(1)})\cdot\prod_{j=2}^{m}p_{r_{j}-r_{j-1}}(z_{\sigma(j)}-z_{\sigma(j-1)})~\mathrm{d}r.

With this inequality in hand, we can now prove (
5.9
)—hence
conclude the proof of Theorem
5.11
—by
noting the following: According to
[
5
, Theorem 2.2.3]
,
for any bounded Borel sets
A_{1},A_{2}\subset[0,\infty)
, one has

(5.15)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\Big[\alpha_{A_{1}\times A_{2}}\big({{}_{1}}B^{0},{{}_{2}}B^{0}\big)^{m}\Big]=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{m}}\mathsf{f}^{m}_{A_{1}}(z)\mathsf{f}^{m}_{A_{2}}(z)~\mathrm{d}z<\infty.

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{t}(Z_{1},Z_{2})\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{O}_{t,\theta}(Z_{1},Z_{2})}\qquad\text{almost surely.}

Toward this end, for every

6.
Theorem
1.3
Step 2: Feynman-Kac Formulas

6.1.
Outline

In this section, we provide the main tool that is used to calculate the expectation and variance asymptotics
in Theorem
1.3
, namely:

Proposition 6.1
.

For every
\kappa>0
,
there exists a constant
\vartheta_{\kappa}>0
such that
for every
t\in(0,\vartheta_{\kappa})
, the following holds:

(6.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbf{E}\big[\mathsf{T}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}}\big]=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}t\log t/2\pi}}{2\pi t}\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})>t\}}\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}\gamma_{t}(B^{x,x}_{t})}\Big]~\mathrm{d}x;

(6.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbf{E}\big[\mathsf{M}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}}\big]=\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}(t\log t-t)/2\pi}\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x})>t\}}\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}\gamma_{t}(B^{x})}\Big]~\mathrm{d}x;

(6.3)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathsf{T}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}}\big]=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}t\log t/\pi}}{(2\pi t)^{2}}\int_{D^{2}}\mathbf{E}\Bigg[\mathbf{1}_{\cap_{i\leq 2}\{\tau_{D}({{}_{i}}B^{x_{i},x_{i}}_{t})>t\}}\\
\cdot\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\gamma_{t}({{}_{i}}B^{x_{i},x_{i}}_{t})}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}\alpha_{t}({{}_{1}}B^{x_{1},x_{1}}_{t},{{}_{2}}B^{x_{2},x_{2}}_{t})}-1\right)\Bigg]~\mathrm{d}x;

(6.4)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathsf{M}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}}\big]=\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}(t\log t-t)/\pi}\int_{D^{2}}\mathbf{E}\Bigg[\mathbf{1}_{\cap_{i\leq 2}\{\tau_{D}({{}_{i}}B^{x_{i}})>t\}}\\
\cdot\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\gamma_{t}({{}_{i}}B^{x_{i}})}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}\alpha_{t}({{}_{1}}B^{x_{1}},{{}_{2}}B^{x_{2}})}-1\right)\Bigg]~\mathrm{d}x.

Indeed, if we combine this result with Proposition
1.6
,
then we obtain Feynman-Kac formulas for the expectation and variance of
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)

and
\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)
. The remainder of this section is organized as follows:
In Section
6.2
, we provide the proof of
Proposition
1.6
, up to two technical lemmas
(i.e., Lemmas
6.2
and
6.3

6.3
and

6.5
.

6.1
is proved in

6.4
.

\vartheta_{\kappa}>0

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t(\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon})+\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon})}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})}\qquad\text{in probability},

(6.7)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t(\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon})+\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon})}\big\langle\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}),\mathbf{1}_{D}\big\rangle^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})}\big\langle\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa}),\mathbf{1}_{D}\big\rangle^{2}\quad\text{in prob.}

Assumption
4.9
states that for every fixed
n\geq 1
,
one has

\mathrm{e}^{-t(\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon})+\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon})}\to\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})},\quad\mathrm{e}^{-t(\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon})+\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon})}\big\langle\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon}),\mathbf{1}_{D}\big\rangle^{2}\to\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})}\big\langle\psi_{n}(H),\mathbf{1}_{D}\big\rangle^{2}

as
\varepsilon\to 0
in probability. The only difficulty in establishing (
6.6
) and (
6.7
)
is therefore to justify the limit when summing over all
n\geq 1
. In order to get around this problem, we make use of the following technical result:

Lemma 6.3
.

For any

\kappa>0
, there exists
\vartheta_{\kappa}>0
such that for every
t\in(0,\vartheta_{\kappa})
,

\lim_{\varepsilon,\tilde{\varepsilon}\to 0}\mathbf{E}\left[\big\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}}-\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\tilde{\varepsilon}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\tilde{\varepsilon}}}\big\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}^{2}\right]=0.

This is proved in Section
6.5
, also using the Feynman-Kac formula and the
properties of intersection local times.

Let
\kappa>0
and
t\in(0,\vartheta_{\kappa})
be fixed, where
\vartheta_{\kappa}
is taken as in Lemma
6.3
.
By combining Assumption
4.9
with Lemma
6.3
,
every vanishing sequence of
\varepsilon
’s has a subsequence
\varepsilon_{1}>\varepsilon_{2}>\cdots>0
along which
the limits (
4.5
)
and (
4.6
) hold almost surely for all
n\geq 1
,
and, in addition,
for every
m,n\geq 1
, one has

(6.8)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\big\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}}-\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{n}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{n}}}\big\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}^{2}\right]\leq 8^{-\min\{m,n\}}.

Our aim is to show that the limits
(
6.6
) and (
6.7
)
hold almost surely along any such subsequence
\{\varepsilon_{n}:n\geq 1\}
, which will imply the desired convergence in probability.

Combining (
6.8
) with Markov’s inequality, we get that

\mathbf{P}\left[\big\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{n}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{n}}}-\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{n+1}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{n+1}}}\big\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}>2^{-n}\right]\leq\frac{8^{-n}}{4^{-n}}\leq 2^{-n}.

In particular, since
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}<\infty
, it follows from the Borel-Cantelli lemma that

\mathbf{P}\left[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{n}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{n}}}-\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{n+1}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{n+1}}}\big\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}<\infty\right]=1;

hence the sequence
\big\{\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{n}}(t):n\geq 1\big\}
is almost-surely Cauchy in
L^{2}(D^{2})
.
Given that
L^{2}(D^{2})
is complete, we conclude that there exists some Hilbert-Schmidt integral operator
\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}(t)\in L^{2}(D^{2})
such that

(6.9)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\big\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{n}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{n}}}-\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}(t)\big\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}^{2}=0\qquad\text{almost surely}.

Let us henceforth assume that we are working with a realization of
\xi

in the probability-one event where (
4.5
),
(
4.6
), and (
6.9
)
hold. We claim that, on this event,

(6.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}(t)f=\mathrm{e}^{-tH_{\kappa}}f=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})}\big\langle\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa}),f\big\rangle\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa}),\qquad f\in L^{2}(D).

For this purpose: On the one hand, for every
m,n\geq 1
, we have that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}}\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}})-\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}(t)\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}}\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}})-\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}}\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa})\|

\displaystyle\hskip 144.54pt+\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}}\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa})-\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}(t)\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}}\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}\|\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}})-\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa})\|+\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}}-\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}(t)\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})};

(
4.6
) and (
6.9
)
implies that this goes to zero as
m\to\infty
. On the other hand,
(
4.3
), (
4.5
), and
(
4.6
) implies the
L^{2}
limit

\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}}\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}})=\mathrm{e}^{-t(\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}})+\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}})}\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}})\to\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})}\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa})

as
m\to\infty
.
If we combine these two results, then we get that

\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}(t)\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa})=\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})}\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa})

for all
n\geq 1
; hence (
6.10
) holds since
\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa})
is an orthonormal basis.

With (
6.10
) in hand, we are now in a position
to prove the almost-sure versions of
(
6.6
) and (
6.7
)
along the subsequence
\varepsilon_{n}
, and thus conclude the proof of Proposition
1.6
:
On the one hand, (
4.3
),
(
6.9
) and (
6.10
) implies that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t(\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}})+\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}})}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}(t/2)\mathrm{e}^{-(t/2)\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}}\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}^{2}\\
=\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}(t/2)\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}^{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})},

which yields (
6.6
).
On the other hand, the same three results and the Cauchy-Schwarz
inequality imply that

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t(\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}})+\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}})}\big\langle\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}),\mathbf{1}_{D}\big\rangle^{2}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-t\lambda_{n}(H_{\kappa})}\big\langle\psi_{n}(H_{\kappa}),\mathbf{1}_{D}\big\rangle^{2}\right|

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\to\infty}\left|\big\langle\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}(t)\mathbf{1}_{D}-\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}(t)\mathbf{1}_{D},\mathbf{1}_{D}\big\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}(t)\mathbf{1}_{D}-\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}(t)\mathbf{1}_{D}\|\|\mathbf{1}_{D}\|

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}\|\mathcal{K}_{\kappa,\varepsilon_{m}}(t)-\mathcal{K}_{\kappa}(t)\|_{L^{2}(D^{2})}\|\mathbf{1}_{D}\|^{2}=0,

which yields (
6.7
).

By replicating the calculation we performed earlier in (

If we perform a similar calculation, but this time use the covariance property

In particular, a Gaussian moment generating function calculation in (

) implies that (

)
can be reformulated as the claim that

(6.38)

\lim_{\varepsilon,\tilde{\varepsilon}\to 0}\frac{1}{4\pi t}\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\Bigg[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{2t})>2t\}}\mathrm{e}^{(\kappa^{2}\beta^{\varepsilon}_{[0,t]^{2}_{\leq}}(B^{x,x}_{2t})-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon})+(\kappa^{2}\beta^{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{[t,2t]^{2}_{\leq}}(B^{x,x}_{2t})-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\tilde{\varepsilon}})}\\
\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}\beta^{(\varepsilon+\tilde{\varepsilon})/2}_{[0,t]\times[t,2t]}(B^{x,x}_{2t})}\Bigg]~\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}t\log(2t)/\pi}}{4\pi t}\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\Bigg[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{2t})>2t\}}\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}\gamma_{2t}(B^{x,x}_{2t})}\Bigg]~\mathrm{d}x.

Toward this end, we first note that we can write

\big(\kappa^{2}\beta^{\varepsilon}_{[0,t]^{2}_{\leq}}(B^{x,x}_{2t})-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon}\big)+\big(\kappa^{2}\beta^{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{[t,2t]^{2}_{\leq}}(B^{x,x}_{2t})-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\tilde{\varepsilon}}\big)+\kappa^{2}\beta^{(\varepsilon+\tilde{\varepsilon})/2}_{[0,t]\times[t,2t]}(B^{x,x}_{2t})

(i.e., the random variable inside the exponential on the left-hand side of (

as the sum of the following two terms:

(6.39)

\Big(\kappa^{2}\beta^{\varepsilon}_{[0,t]^{2}_{\leq}}(B^{x,x}_{2t})-\kappa^{2}\mathbf{E}\big[\beta^{\varepsilon}_{[0,t]^{2}_{\leq}}(B^{x,x}_{2t})\big]\Big)+\Big(\kappa^{2}\beta^{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{[t,2t]^{2}_{\leq}}(B^{x,x}_{2t})-\kappa^{2}\mathbf{E}\big[\beta^{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{[t,2t]^{2}_{\leq}}(B^{x,x}_{2t})\big]\Big)\\
+\Big(\kappa^{2}\beta^{(\varepsilon+\tilde{\varepsilon})/2}_{[0,t]\times[t,2t]}(B^{x,x}_{2t})-\kappa^{2}\mathbf{E}\big[\beta^{(\varepsilon+\tilde{\varepsilon})/2}_{[0,t]\times[t,2t]}(B^{x,x}_{2t})\big]\Big),

and

(6.40)

The reason why we write the terms in this particular way is that it clearly emphasizes the following
two facts: On the one hand, by Theorem
5.4
,

(6.41)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon,\tilde{\varepsilon}\to 0}\eqref{Equation: HS Expansion GOAL Averaged 1}=\gamma_{[0,t]^{2}_{\leq}}(B^{x,x}_{2t})+\gamma_{[t,2t]^{2}_{\leq}}(B^{x,x}_{2t})+\beta_{[0,t]\times[t,2t]}(B^{x,x}_{2t})-\mathbf{E}\big[\beta_{[0,t]\times[t,2t]}(B^{x,x}_{2t})\big]

in probability.
Given that
A\mapsto\beta^{\varepsilon}_{A}(Z)
is a measure on

that is zero whenever

has Lebesgue measure zero
(this is clear from Definition
5.1
),
it follows that
\beta^{\varepsilon}_{A}(Z)+\beta^{\varepsilon}_{\tilde{A}}(Z)=\beta^{\varepsilon}_{A\cup\tilde{A}}(Z)
whenever

has measure zero. By Theorem
5.4
, this same property is preserved in the limits

\gamma_{A}
and
\beta_{A}
, with an almost sure equality.
In particular, since the sets in the union

[0,t]_{\leq}^{2}\cup[t,2t]_{\leq}\cup\big([0,t]\times[t,2t]\big)=[0,2t]^{2}_{\leq}

only intersect on a set of measure zero, it follows that

(6.42)

\displaystyle\text{right-hand side of }\eqref{Equation: HS Expansion GOAL Averaged 1.1}=\gamma_{2t}(B^{x,x}_{2t})\qquad\text{almost surely.}

On the other hand, by (

) and Lemma

,

(6.43)

\lim_{\varepsilon,\tilde{\varepsilon}\to 0}\eqref{Equation: HS Expansion GOAL Averaged 2}=2\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\pi}\left(t\log(2t)+\int_{0}^{t}\log r-\log(2t-r)~\mathrm{d}r\right)\\
+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\pi}\left(\int_{t}^{2t}\log r-\log(2t-r)~\mathrm{d}r-\int_{0}^{t}\log r-\log(2t-r)~\mathrm{d}r\right)=\frac{\kappa^{2}t\log(2t)}{\pi},

where the last equality follows from the fact that the three integrals above cancel, since the map
f(r)=\log r-\log(2t-r)

satisfies
f(r)=-f(2t-r)
for
r\in[0,t]
.

Now that we have proved (

), (
6.42
),
and (
6.43
), we see that (
6.38
)
follows if we show that we can take the limits
\varepsilon,\tilde{\varepsilon}\to 0
inside the

For this purpose, if we combine the trivial bound
\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\}}\leq 1
with Hölder’s inequality,
it suffices to show that for any

(6.44)

\sup_{x\in D,~\varepsilon,\tilde{\varepsilon}>0}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{3\eta(\kappa^{2}\beta^{\varepsilon}_{[0,t]^{2}_{\leq}}(B^{x,x}_{2t})-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\varepsilon})}\right]^{1/3}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{3\eta(\kappa^{2}\beta^{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{[t,2t]^{2}_{\leq}}(B^{x,x}_{2t})-t\mathsf{c}_{\kappa,\tilde{\varepsilon}})}\right]^{1/3}\\
\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{3\eta\kappa^{2}\beta^{(\varepsilon+\tilde{\varepsilon})/2}_{[0,t]\times[t,2t]}(B^{x,x}_{2t})}\right]^{1/3}<\infty.

Indeed, this simultaneously shows that we can apply the dominated convergence theorem in the
\mathrm{d}x

integral (since
D
is bounded) and the Vitali convergence theorem in the expectation
(since (
6.44
) for
\eta>1
shows that the random variables
in question are uniformly integrable by (

)).
The finiteness of the suprema on the first line of
(

) can be proved in the
same way as the finiteness of the suprema on the first line of (
6.21
);
the finiteness of the second line of (

5.3
).

7.
Theorem
1.3
Step 3: Intersection Local Times Asymptotics

We now use the Feynman-Kac formulas in Proposition
6.1

to prove the asymptotics in Theorem
1.3
. Given that their proofs
differ slightly, we deal with the expectation
and variance asymptotics separately.

7.1.
Expectation Asymptotics

By the classical Feynman-Kac formula,

\mathsf{T}_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi t}\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})>t\}}\Big]~\mathrm{d}x\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{M}_{0}(t)=\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x})>t\}}\Big]~\mathrm{d}x.

If we combine this with Propositions
1.6
and
6.1
,
then the expectation asymptotics in (
1.10
)
and (
1.11
) can be respectively reduced to

(7.1)

\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}t\log t/2\pi}}{2\pi t}\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})>t\}}\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}\gamma_{t}(B^{x,x}_{t})}\Big]~\mathrm{d}x\\
=\frac{1}{2\pi t}\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})>t\}}\Big]~\mathrm{d}x+\tfrac{\kappa^{2}\mathsf{A}(D)}{4\pi^{2}}\log t+o(\log t)\qquad\text{as }t\to 0,

and similarly

(7.2)

\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}(t\log t-t)/2\pi}\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x})>t\}}\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}\gamma_{t}(B^{x})}\Big]~\mathrm{d}x\\
=\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x})>t\}}\Big]~\mathrm{d}x+\tfrac{\kappa^{2}\mathsf{A}(D)}{2\pi}t\log t+o(t\log t)\qquad\text{as }t\to 0.

Let us begin with (
7.1
).
By a Taylor expansion,

(7.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}t\log t/2\pi}=1+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\pi}t\log t+O(t^{2}\log^{2}t)\qquad\text{as }t\to 0.

As
t^{2}\log^{2}t=o(t\log t)
, the left-hand side of (
7.1
) can be written as

(7.4)

\frac{1}{2\pi t}\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})>t\}}\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}\gamma_{t}(B^{x,x}_{t})}\Big]~\mathrm{d}x\\
+\frac{1}{2\pi t}\left(\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\pi}t\log t+o(t\log t)\right)\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})>t\}}\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}\gamma_{t}(B^{x,x}_{t})}\Big]~\mathrm{d}x.

With this in hand, (
7.1
) is now a consequence of the claims that

(7.5)

\displaystyle\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})>t\}}\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}\gamma_{t}(B^{x,x}_{t})}\Big]~\mathrm{d}x=\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})>t\}}\Big]~\mathrm{d}x+o(t)\qquad\text{as }t\to 0,

and

(7.6)

\displaystyle\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})>t\}}\Big]~\mathrm{d}x=\mathsf{A}(D)+o(1)\qquad\text{as }t\to 0.

(More specifically, applying (
7.5
)
in the first line of (
7.4
),
and then successively applying (
7.5
)
and (
7.6
)
in the second line of (
7.4
).)

We begin with (
7.6
), since one element of its
proof is also used in (
7.5
).
Given that

(7.7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})>t\}}=1-\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})\leq t\}},

it suffices to show that for every
0<\theta\leq 1
,

(7.8)

\displaystyle\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\Big[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})\leq t\}}\Big]^{\theta}~\mathrm{d}x=o(1)\qquad\text{as }t\to 0

(we only need the result with
\theta=1
for (
7.6
),
but we will need the case of
\theta<1
in the proof of (
7.5
)).
By Brownian scaling, we can couple the Brownian bridges appearing in (
7.8
)
for different
x
’s and
t
’s in such a way that

B^{x,x}_{t}=x+\sqrt{t}\,B^{0,0}_{1},\qquad t>0,~x\in D.

Under this coupling, we observe that for every
x\in D
,

\lim_{t\to 0}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})\leq t\}}=\lim_{t\to 0}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(x+\sqrt{t}B^{0,0}_{1})\leq 1\}}=0\qquad\text{almost surely}.

Indeed, since
D
is open, for any point
x\in D
, there exists some
\varepsilon_{x}>0
small enough so that the ball

\mathcal{B}(x,\varepsilon_{x})=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|x-y|<\varepsilon\}
is contained in
D
. Since

\mathcal{S}=\sup_{s\in[0,1]}|B^{0,0}_{1}(s)|<\infty\qquad\text{almost surely},

we have that
x+\sqrt{t}B^{0,0}_{1}(s)\in\mathcal{B}(x,\varepsilon_{x})
for all
s\in[0,1]
(hence
\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(x+\sqrt{t}B^{0,0}_{1})\leq 1\}}=0
)
whenever
\sqrt{t}\mathcal{S}<\varepsilon_{x}
, which is equivalent to
t<(\varepsilon_{x}/\mathcal{S})^{2}
.
We then get (
7.8
) by dominated convergence
thanks to the trivial bound
\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\}}\leq 1
.

We now move on to (
7.5
).
By a Taylor expansion, we can write

\mathrm{e}^{z}=1+z+\mathsf{R}(z),\qquad\mathsf{|}R(z)|\leq z^{2}(1+\mathrm{e}^{z}),\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}.

If we apply this to
z=\kappa^{2}\gamma_{t}(B^{x,x}_{t})
, then
(
7.5
) can be reduced to

(7.9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{D}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})>t\}}\gamma_{t}(B^{x,x}_{t})~\mathrm{d}x\right]=o(t)\qquad\text{as }t\to 0,

(7.10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\int_{D}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})>t\}}\gamma_{t}(B^{x,x}_{t})^{2}(1+\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}\gamma_{t}(B^{x,x}_{t})})~\mathrm{d}x\right]=o(t)\qquad\text{as }t\to 0.

Using (
7.7
) and Tonelli’s theorem, we get that

\text{left-hand side of \eqref{Equation: Bridge Expectation Asymptotics 1}}=\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\left[\gamma_{t}(B^{x,x}_{t})\right]~\mathrm{d}x+\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})\leq t\}}\gamma_{t}(B^{x,x}_{t})\right]~\mathrm{d}x.

On the one hand, given that
\gamma_{t}(B^{x,x}_{t})
is constructed as the limit of random variables with expectation zero
(Theorem
5.4
-(2)) that are uniformly integrable (i.e., (
5.2
)), we get that
\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\left[\gamma_{t}(B^{x,x}_{t})\right]\mathrm{d}x=0.

On the other hand, Hölder’s inequality
and the scaling property in Lemma
5.6
implies that

\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})\leq t\}}\gamma_{t}(B^{x,x}_{t})\right]~\mathrm{d}x\leq t\cdot\mathbf{E}\left[\gamma_{1}(B^{0,0}_{1})^{2}\right]^{1/2}\int_{D}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{D}(B^{x,x}_{t})\leq t\}}\right]^{1/2}~\mathrm{d}x.

Since the moments of
\gamma_{1}(B^{0,0}_{1})
are finite (i.e., (
5.4
)),
this term is on the order of
o(t)
by (
7.8
) with
\theta=1/2
;
hence (
7.9
) holds.

We now move on to (
7.10
).
If we combine the trivial bound
\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\}}\leq 1

with Hölder’s inequality and the scaling property in Lemma
5.6
, then we get

\text{left-hand side of }\eqref{Equation: Bridge Expectation Asymptotics 2}\leq\mathsf{A}(D)\,t^{2}\,\mathbf{E}\left[\gamma_{1}(B^{0,0}_{1})^{4}\right]^{1/2}\mathbf{E}\left[\left(1+\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}t\gamma_{1}(B^{0,0}_{1})}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}.

This decays on the order of
O(t^{2})=o(t)
as
t\to 0
by (
5.2
),
thus proving (
7.10
).

With this done, we have now concluded the proof of (
7.1
),
and thus of the expectation asymptotic for
\mathbf{E}\big[\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)\big]
in Theorem
1.3
.
In order to prove the corresponding asymptotic for
\mathbf{E}\big[\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)\big]
, i.e.,
(
7.2
), we can go through exactly the same steps
with every instance of
B^{x,x}_{t}
replaced by
B^{x}
,
but with the following minor differences:

(1)

The Taylor expansion (
7.3
)
is replaced by

\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}(t\log t-t)/2\pi}=1+\frac{\kappa^{2}}{2\pi}t\log t+O(t)

due to the presence of
-\kappa^{2}t/2\pi
.

(2)

The analogue of (
7.4
) for
\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)

has no factor of the form
\frac{1}{2\pi t}
.

This concludes the proof of the expectation asymptotics in Theorem
1.3
.

7.2.
Variance Asymptotics

We begin with the variance bound for
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)
.
Since
\mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot\}}\leq 1
and
\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}t\log t/\pi}}{2\pi}=O(1)
as
t\to 0
,
an application of Hölder’s inequality in (
6.3
) (together with Proposition
1.6
) yields the
t\to 0
asymptotic

\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)\big]=O\left(t^{-2}\int_{D^{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{2\kappa^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\gamma_{t}({{}_{i}}B^{x_{i},x_{i}}_{t})}\right]^{1/2}\mathbf{E}\left[\left(\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}\alpha_{t}({{}_{1}}B^{x_{1},x_{1}}_{t},{{}_{2}}B^{x_{2},x_{2}}_{t})}-1\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\Bigg]~\mathrm{d}x\right).

By (
5.4
), this yields

\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)\big]=O\left(t^{-2}\int_{D^{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\left(\mathrm{e}^{\kappa^{2}\alpha_{t}({{}_{1}}B^{x_{1},x_{1}}_{t},{{}_{2}}B^{x_{2},x_{2}}_{t})}-1\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\Bigg]~\mathrm{d}x\right).

Given that
\mathrm{e}^{z}-1\leq|z|\mathrm{e}^{|z|}
, another application of Hölder’s inequality yields

\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)\big]=O\left(t^{-2}\int_{D^{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\alpha_{t}({{}_{1}}B^{x_{1},x_{1}}_{t},{{}_{2}}B^{x_{2},x_{2}}_{t})^{4}\right]^{1/4}\mathbf{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{4\kappa^{2}\alpha_{t}({{}_{1}}B^{x_{1},x_{1}}_{t},{{}_{2}}B^{x_{2},x_{2}}_{t})}\right]^{1/4}~\mathrm{d}x\right).

By Lemma
5.15
, the term involving an exponential above is uniformly bounded over

x
for small
t
, hence

\mathbf{Var}\big[\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)\big]=O\left(t^{-2}\int_{D^{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\alpha_{t}({{}_{1}}B^{x_{1},x_{1}}_{t},{{}_{2}}B^{x_{2},x_{2}}_{t})^{4}\right]^{1/4}~\mathrm{d}x\right).

Therefore,
we obtain the variance bound in (
1.10
)
if we show that, as
t\to 0
,

(7.11)

\displaystyle\int_{D^{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\alpha_{t}({{}_{1}}B^{x_{1},x_{1}}_{t},{{}_{2}}B^{x_{2},x_{2}}_{t})^{4}\right]^{1/4}~\mathrm{d}x=O(t^{2}).

By Lemma
5.13
,
for any
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in D^{2}
,
we can write

\mathbf{E}\left[\alpha_{t}({{}_{1}}B^{x_{1},x_{1}}_{t},{{}_{2}}B^{x_{2},x_{2}}_{t})^{4}\right]^{1/4}=\mathbf{E}\left[\alpha_{t}({{}_{1}}B^{x_{1},x_{1}}_{t},{{}_{2}}B^{x_{2},x_{2}}_{t})^{4}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{1}(t,x)\cup\mathcal{A}_{2}(t,x)}\right]^{1/4},

where we define the events

\mathcal{A}_{i}(t,x)=\left\{\sup_{r\in[0,t]}\left|{{}_{i}}B^{x_{i},x_{i}}_{t}(r)-x_{i}\right|\geq\frac{|x_{1}-x_{2}|}{3}\right\},\qquad i=1,2.

Indeed, if
\mathcal{A}_{i}(t,x)^{c}\cap\mathcal{A}_{i}(t,x)^{c}
holds, then the paths of
{{}_{i}}B^{x_{i},x_{i}}_{t}
are separated
by at least
\theta=|x_{1}-x_{2}|/3
, hence the event
\mathfrak{O}_{t,\theta}({{}_{1}}B^{x_{1},x_{1}}_{t},{{}_{2}}B^{x_{2},x_{2}}_{t})
holds.
Thus, by Hölder’s inequality, we have the estimate

\int_{D^{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\alpha_{t}({{}_{1}}B^{x_{1},x_{1}}_{t},{{}_{2}}B^{x_{2},x_{2}}_{t})^{4}\right]^{1/4}~\mathrm{d}x\\
\leq\int_{D^{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\alpha_{t}({{}_{1}}B^{x_{1},x_{1}}_{t},{{}_{2}}B^{x_{2},x_{2}}_{t})^{8}\right]^{1/8}\mathbf{P}\big[\mathcal{A}_{1}(t,x)\cup\mathcal{A}_{2}(t,x)\big]^{1/8}~\mathrm{d}x.

By Lemmas
5.14
and
5.15
,

\sup_{x\in D^{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\alpha_{t}({{}_{1}}B^{x_{1},x_{1}}_{t},{{}_{2}}B^{x_{2},x_{2}}_{t})^{8}\right]^{1/8}=O(t).

Therefore, we get that

\int_{D^{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\alpha_{t}({{}_{1}}B^{x_{1},x_{1}}_{t},{{}_{2}}B^{x_{2},x_{2}}_{t})^{4}\right]^{1/4}~\mathrm{d}x=O\left(t\int_{D^{2}}\mathbf{P}\big[\mathcal{A}_{1}(t,x)\cup\mathcal{A}_{2}(t,x)\big]^{1/8}~\mathrm{d}x\right).

If we combine a Brownian scaling with the fact that
Brownian motion and bridge suprema have Gaussian tails, then we conclude that
there exists some
constants
C,c>0
such that for
i=1,2
, one has

\mathbf{P}\big[\mathcal{A}_{i}(t,x)\big]\leq\mathbf{P}\left[\sup_{r\in[0,1]}\left|{{}_{i}}B^{0,0}_{1}(r)\right|\geq\frac{|x_{1}-x_{2}|}{3t^{1/2}}\right]\leq C\mathrm{e}^{-c|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{2}/t}.

Thus, by a union bound,

\int_{D^{2}}\mathbf{E}\left[\alpha_{t}({{}_{1}}B^{x_{1},x_{1}}_{t},{{}_{2}}B^{x_{2},x_{2}}_{t})^{4}\right]^{1/4}~\mathrm{d}x=O\left(t\int_{D^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-c|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{2}/8t}~\mathrm{d}x\right),

which yields (
7.11
) since
\int_{D^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-c|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{2}/8t}\mathrm{d}x=O(t)
.

We have now proved the variance bound for
\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t)
in Theorem
1.3
.
In order to get the corresponding asymptotic for
\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t)
, we apply exactly the
same argument with Brownian bridges replaced by Brownian motions, the only
meaningful difference being that the absence of the factor
\frac{1}{4\pi t^{2}}
in (
6.4
)
yields a variance bound on the order of
O(t^{2})
instead.
The proof of Theorem
1.3
is thus complete.

8.
Proof of Applications

8.1.
Proof of Corollaries
2.6
and
2.10

We first consider Corollary
2.6
-(1).
By (
1.10
) and (
2.5
),

\mathbf{E}\big[2\pi t_{n}\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t_{n})\big]=\mathsf{A}(D)+o(1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{Var}\big[2\pi t_{n}\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t_{n})\big]=O(t_{n}^{2})

as
n\to\infty
. Thus, by a combination of the Borel-Cantelli lemma
and Chebyshev’s inequality, it suffices to show that there exists some
\eta\in(0,2)
such that

\mathbf{P}\left[\Big|\mathbf{E}\big[2\pi t_{n}\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t_{n})-\mathbf{E}\big[2\pi t_{n}\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t_{n})\big]\Big|>t_{n}^{\eta/2}\right]=O(t_{n}^{2-\eta})

is summable in
n
. If
t_{n}\leq cn^{-1/2-\varepsilon}
for some
c,\varepsilon>0
, then we get that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}^{2-\eta}\leq c\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{(-1/2-\varepsilon)(2-\eta)}=c\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-1-2\varepsilon+\eta(1/2+\varepsilon)}.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, we can always find
\eta
small enough so that

-2\varepsilon+\eta(1/2+\varepsilon)<0
, thus making the above summable.

Corollaries
2.6
-(2) and
2.10

are proved similarly:
By (
1.10
) and (
2.5
),

\mathbf{E}\left[4\sqrt{2\pi t_{n}}\left(\tfrac{\mathsf{A}(D)}{2\pi}t_{n}^{-1}-\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t_{n})\right)\right]=\mathsf{L}(\partial D)+o(1),

\mathbf{Var}\left[4\sqrt{2\pi t_{n}}\left(\tfrac{\mathsf{A}(D)}{2\pi}t_{n}^{-1}-\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t_{n})\right)\right]=O(t_{n}),

as
n\to\infty
, and similarly by (
1.10
),

\mathbf{E}\left[\tfrac{4\pi^{2}}{\mathsf{A}(D)\log t_{n}}\big(\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t_{n})-\mathsf{T}_{0}(t_{n})\big)\right]=\kappa^{2}+o(1),

\mathbf{Var}\left[\tfrac{4\pi^{2}}{\mathsf{A}(D)\log t_{n}}\big(\mathsf{T}_{\kappa}(t_{n})-\mathsf{T}_{0}(t_{n})\big)\right]=O\big((\log t_{n})^{-2}\big).

Thus, by Borel-Cantelli and Chebyshev’s inequality, it suffices to show that there exists
\eta>0

such that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}^{1-\eta}<\infty
in the case of Corollary
2.6
-(2)
(this works if
t_{n}\leq cn^{-1-\varepsilon}
),
and similarly
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(\log t_{n})^{-2+\eta}<\infty
in the case of Corollary
2.10

(this works if
t_{n}\leq\tilde{c}\mathrm{e}^{-cn^{1/2+\varepsilon}}
). This concludes the proofs of Corollaries
2.6

and
2.10
.

8.2.
Proof of Corollary
2.8

It suffices to show that there exists random variables
\mathfrak{c},\mathfrak{N}>1
such that,
almost surely,

(8.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}^{-1}\,t_{n}^{1-\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)/2}\leq\mathsf{A}(D)-\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t_{n})\leq\mathfrak{c}\,t_{n}^{1-\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)/2}\qquad\text{for every }n\geq\mathfrak{N}.

For this purpose, let us write

\mathsf{A}(D)-\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t_{n})=\big(\mathsf{A}(D)-\mathsf{M}_{0}(t_{n})\big)+\big(\mathsf{M}_{0}(t_{n})-\mathbf{E}\big[\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t_{n})\big]\big)+\big(\mathbf{E}\big[\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t_{n})\big]-\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t_{n})\big).

Since
t\log t=o(t^{1-\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)/2})
,
thanks to the expectation asymptotic in (
1.11
) and (
2.6
),
there exists nonrandom constants
\tilde{c},\tilde{N}>1
such that

\tilde{c}^{-1}\,t_{n}^{1-\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)/2}\leq\big(\mathsf{A}(D)-\mathsf{M}_{0}(t_{n})\big)+\big(\mathsf{M}_{0}(t_{n})-\mathbf{E}\big[\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t_{n})\big]\big)\leq\tilde{c}\,t_{n}^{1-\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)/2}\qquad\text{for }n\geq\tilde{N}.

Thus, it suffices to show that for every
\eta>0
(in particular,
\eta<\tilde{c}
),

\mathbf{P}\left[\big|\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t_{n})-\mathbf{E}\big[\mathsf{M}_{\kappa}(t_{n})\big]\big|>\eta t_{n}^{1-\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)/2}\text{ infinitely often}\right]=0.

By the Borel-Cantelli lemma and Chebyshev’s inequality (using the variance bound in (
1.11
)), this follows from

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}^{2-2(1-\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)/2)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}^{\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)}<\infty.

If we choose
t_{n}\leq cn^{-1/\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)-\varepsilon}
for any fixed
c,\varepsilon>0
,
then we get

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t_{n}^{\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)}\leq c\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-1-\varepsilon\mathsf{d}_{M}(\partial D)}<\infty,

as desired.
