Syndemic effects of social isolation on mental health, health behaviours and diabetes management in older adults with diabetes: a gender-based cross-sectional study in South Korea
Authors: Ji-Su Kim, Kyehyun Kim, Hyerine Shin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo examine the associations between social isolation (SI) and mental health, health behaviours and diabetes management among older adults with diabetes in South Korea, focusing on gender-specific syndemic patterns.DesignCross-sectional secondary analysis.Setting2023 Korea Community Health Survey, a nationwide community-based survey.Participants18 924 adults aged ≥65 years with physician-diagnosed diabetes.Outcome measuresMental health (depressive symptoms, perceived stress), health behaviours (smoking, high-risk drinking, physical activity) and diabetes management (medication use, complication screening, diabetes education) were assessed. Multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate the adjusted associations between SI and study outcomes.ResultsSI was more prevalent among women (33.6%) than among men (14.1%). In men, SI was associated with depressive symptoms (aOR=1.60, 95% CI 1.18 to 2.18, p=0.003), smoking (aOR=1.51, 95% CI 1.25 to 1.82, p<0.001), high-risk drinking (aOR=1.76, 95% CI 1.36 to 2.28, p<0.001) and lower diabetes education participation (aOR=0.80, 95% CI 0.65 to 0.99, p=0.031). Among women, SI was associated with depressive symptoms (aOR=1.35, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.69, p=0.010), smoking (aOR=2.53, 95% CI 1.69 to 3.78, p<0.001), lower physical activity (aOR=0.56, 95% CI 0.44 to 0.71, p<0.001), lower medication use (aOR=0.51, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.69, p<0.001), reduced complication screening (aOR=0.86, 95% CI 0.76 to 0.97, p=0.016) and lower diabetes education participation (aOR=0.81, 95% CI 0.69 to 0.95, p=0.010). SI was not significantly associated with perceived stress in either gender.ConclusionsSI was associated with poorer mental health and unhealthy behaviours among older adults with diabetes, with distinct gender-specific patterns. The SI also showed non-uniform associations with diabetes management, indicating that its impact may differ across management domains. Addressing SI may support improvements in selected aspects of diabetes care. Further research should clarify the mechanisms linking SI and disease management.Keywords: DIABETES & ENDOCRINOLOGY, Social Interaction, MENTAL HEALTH, Aged

Background

Globally, approximately 527 million people are reported to be living with diabetes, and this number is steadily rising, estimated to surpass 780 million by 2045.1 Older adults aged 65 and above are especially vulnerable to chronic diseases such as diabetes. In 2019, 23.7% of the global older adult population was reported to have either type 1 or type 2 diabetes.2 This prevalence was even higher in South Korea, where 30.1% of older adults are affected.3

The concept of a syndemic, first introduced by Singer,4 describes the synergistic interaction of two or more diseases within a population, shaped by biological, sociocultural, economic and environmental factors, which collectively intensify health burdens. Diabetes exemplifies such a syndemic condition, as it rarely exists in isolation but rather interacts with multiple health issues, worsening patient outcomes. It significantly raises the risk of severe complications, including fractures, frailty, cardiovascular disease and cognitive impairment,5,7 emphasising the urgent need for enhanced medical and public health interventions.

Recently, increasing attention has been accorded to the syndemic nature of diabetes, especially its frequent coexistence with mental health disorders such as depression. This comorbidity further amplifies health disparities.8,10 Diabetes and depression share overlapping risk factors—including advanced age, low educational attainment, poverty, stress, physical inactivity and obesity8—and influence each other bidirectionally: diabetes elevates the risk of developing depression, while depression impairs self-care and treatment adherence in patients with diabetes, creating a self-reinforcing vicious cycle.11 Understanding the complex, multifactorial interactions driving these syndemic conditions is critical for effectively addressing the associated health inequities.

Among various factors contributing to this cycle, social isolation has emerged as a crucial but underexplored element, particularly evident during the COVID-19 pandemic. Defined as a lack of contact or interaction with others,12 social isolation not only presents direct health risks but also exacerbates related factors such as inadequate access to health information, increased physical and mental stress, inflammation and reduced physical activity.12 13 These effects are closely linked to diminished self-care capacity and physical dysfunction in older adults with diabetes.14 15 Furthermore, poor diabetes management, uncontrolled glycaemic levels, vascular complications, comorbid depression and declining daily functioning lead to decreased physical activity and self-stigmatisation.16 17 This negative self-perception, along with adverse social reactions, reinforces social isolation and perpetuates the vicious cycle. Consequently, the interplay between social isolation and health outcomes in older adults with diabetes intensifies the syndemic effect.18

Social isolation is prevalent among older adults worldwide: approximately 24% of community-dwelling adults aged 65 and older in the USA experience some level of social isolation, with 4% classified as severely isolated.12 Similar prevalence rates have been reported globally, including 29.7% in China,19 approximately 62% in Iran20 and approximately 17.8% in South Korea.21 Given its widespread occurrence, social isolation likely plays a key role in intensifying the syndemic relationship between diabetes and depression.

Although numerous studies have examined the syndemic patterns of diabetes and depression, research exploring the intersection of social isolation with these conditions remains insufficient. Moreover, previous findings indicate that the effects of depression and social isolation differ by gender,22,24 suggesting that syndemic impacts may also vary accordingly. Therefore, this study conducted a gender-based analysis to investigate the syndemic patterns of mental health and diabetes among older adults, focusing on the influence of social isolation on these interactions.

Methods

Study design

This study conducted a secondary analysis of data from the 2023 Korea Community Health Survey (KCHS),25 an annual nationwide cross-sectional survey administered by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency to assess the health status and health behaviours of adults in South Korea. The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Statement guidelines for observational studies. The 2023 survey consisted of 145 items across 17 domains and included data on sociodemographic characteristics, health status, health behaviours and chronic disease management.

To ensure national representativeness, the KCHS employs a two-stage stratified sampling design based on residential area and housing type. In the first stage, enumeration units are selected using probability proportional to size methods. In the second stage, households within each selected unit are systematically sampled and one adult aged 19 years or older from each household is invited to participate.

Data were collected from 16 May to 31 July 2023, through computer-assisted personal interviews conducted by trained interviewers who visited sampled households. A total of 231 752 adults completed the 2023 KCHS. For the present analysis, participants were included if they were aged 65 years or older and reported having diabetes mellitus diagnosed by a physician. Respondents who did not meet these criteria or had missing data for study variables were excluded. After applying these criteria, the final analytical sample consisted of 18 924 older adults with diabetes.

Research variables (measures)

General characteristics

To adjust for potential confounding factors, participants’ general characteristics were included as control variables: age, educational attainment, household income, perceived health status, unmet healthcare needs and body mass index (BMI; kg/m2). Age was categorised into three groups following prior research on older adults: 65–74 years (young-old), 75–84 years (middle-old) and ≥85 years (oldest-old).26 Educational attainment followed the KCHS response categories and was classified into four levels: not literate, elementary school, middle school and high school or higher. Household income was assessed using KCHS-defined quartiles based on annual household income distributions: Q1 (<25%, lowest), Q2 (25–49%), Q3 (50–74%) and Q4 (≥75%, highest). Perceived health status was assessed using a 5-point scale and subsequently recoded, following previous studies, into ‘fair’ (‘Very good’, ‘Good’ and ‘Fair’) and ‘poor’ (‘Poor’ and ‘Very poor’).27 Unmet healthcare needs were assessed by the question, ‘During the past year, have you been unable to receive necessary medical care?’ Responses were categorised as ‘yes’ or ‘no’. BMI was classified according to the WHO Asia-Pacific criteria:28 underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2) and obese (≥25 kg/m2).

Social isolation

Social isolation was measured using five indicators based on previous studies:29 marital status, household type, social activity participation, social network maintenance and economic activity. Each item was scored as 0 or 1, with total scores ranging from 0 to 5; higher scores indicated greater levels of isolation. Social isolation was defined as a score of 3–5. Each indicator was defined as follows.

Marital status was scored as 1 for ‘others’ (unmarried, divorced, widowed or separated) and 0 for ‘with spouse’. Household type was scored as 1 for living alone (‘Single’) and 0 for living with others (‘2 or more people’). Social activity participation was scored as 1 for ‘No’ and 0 for ‘Yes’ to engagement in religious, club, leisure or volunteer activities. Social network maintenance was scored as 1 for ‘No’ and 0 for ‘Yes’ to contact with family, neighbours or friends at least once a month. Economic activity was scored as 1 for ‘No’ and 0 for ‘Yes’ to working for pay (≥1 hour) or as unpaid family workers (≥18 hours) during the past week.

Mental health

The mental health outcomes included depression and perceived stress. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9). Although the PHQ-9 is not explicitly labelled as a standalone scale in the KCHS, all nine items constituting the PHQ-9 are included in the mental health section. The cut-off score of ≥10, originally established and validated in the PHQ-9 instrument, was formally adopted in the 2023 KCHS coding manual, which provides an official scoring algorithm to classify depressive symptoms. Accordingly, depressive symptoms were classified as ‘Yes’ for scores ≥10 and ‘No’ for scores <10 in this study.30

Perceived stress was assessed using a single KCHS item: ‘How much stress do you experience in daily life?’, and responses were recoded, in line with previous studies, into ‘Yes’ (‘Very much’ and ‘A lot’) and ‘No’ (‘A little bit’ and ‘Hardly any’).31

Health Behaviours

Health behaviours included smoking, high-risk drinking and physical activity, defined according to the KCHS guidelines aligned with international standards. Current smoking was defined as ‘Yes’ for individuals who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and currently smoked (either every day or occasionally); all others were classified as ‘Never or Former’. High-risk drinking was defined as drinking ≥2 times per week and consuming ≥7 drinks for men or ≥5 for women per occasion in the past year. Participants meeting these criteria were classified as ‘Yes’, and all others as ‘No’. Physical activity was classified as ‘Yes’ for participants who reported engaging in vigorous activity (≥20 min/day, ≥3 days/week) or moderate activity (≥30 min/day, ≥5 days/week) for more than 10 min at a time in the past week; otherwise, it was classified as ‘No’.

Diabetes management behaviours

Diabetes management behaviours included the use of diabetes medication, screening for complications, and diabetes management education. Diabetes medication use was classified as ‘Yes’ for participants currently taking oral antidiabetic drugs or insulin (including insulin pumps); all others were classified as ‘No’.32 Complication screening was classified as ‘Yes’ for those who underwent either a fundus exam for retinopathy or a urine test (excluding dipsticks) for nephropathy in the past year, consistent with previous research;33 otherwise, it was classified as ‘No’. Diabetes management education was classified as ‘Yes’ for those who had received diabetes self-management education and ‘No’ for those who had not, following the KCHS response format.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS V.26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). All results are presented as weighted values that account for the complex sampling design of the KCHS, which uses a multistage, stratified and clustered probability sampling framework. The distribution of participants by social isolation and gender was analysed using weighted descriptive statistics in accordance with KCHS guidelines. Weighted frequencies and percentages were calculated to describe the distribution of social isolation and non-isolation groups by gender. General characteristics, mental health, health behaviours and diabetes self-management behaviours were presented using descriptive statistics such as frequencies and percentages, and the associations between each variable and social isolation status were examined using χ2 tests. Logistic regression was used to assess associations between social isolation and health-related outcomes, including mental health, health behaviours and diabetes management behaviours. To facilitate interpretation, adjusted ORs (aORs) and 95% CIs were calculated. All models were adjusted for potential confounders, including age, educational attainment, household income, perceived health status, unmet healthcare needs and BMI, to minimise biased estimates. A two-sided significance level of p<0.05 was applied for all statistical tests.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Among men, depressive symptoms were 1.60 times more common in those with social isolation than in those without isolation (aOR=1.60, 95% CI 1.18 to 2.18, p=0.003). The prevalence of current smoking was 1.51 times higher (aOR=1.51, 95% CI 1.25 to 1.82, p<0.001), and high-risk drinking was 1.76 times more common (aOR=1.76, 95% CI 1.36 to 2.28, p<0.001). Furthermore, participation in diabetes education was lower, with a relative likelihood of 0.80 compared with the non-isolation group (aOR=0.80, 95% CI 0.65 to 0.99, p=0.031). No significant differences were observed in perceived stress, physical activity, diabetes medication use or complication screening.

Among women with social isolation, depressive symptoms were 1.35 times more common than in those without social isolation (aOR=1.35, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.69, p=0.010). Socially isolated women were 2.53 times more likely to be current smokers (aOR=2.53, 95% CI 1.69 to 3.78, p<0.001) and 0.56 times as likely to engage in physical activity (aOR=0.56, 95% CI 0.44 to 0.71, p<0.001). Diabetes medication use was lower at 0.51 times (aOR=0.51, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.69, p<0.001), and complication screening was also lower at 0.86 times (aOR=0.86, 95% CI 0.76 to 0.97, p=0.016). Participation in diabetes management education was lower at 0.81 times (aOR=0.81, 95% CI 0.69 to 0.95, p=0.010). No differences were found in perceived stress or high-risk drinking.

Discussion

This study examined the effect of social isolation on mental health, health behaviours and diabetes management among older adults with diabetes. We found that the prevalence of social isolation was higher among women than among men; however, previous studies have reported varying patterns.34,36 As Umberson et al37 highlighted, inconsistent definitions and measurement tools for social isolation, as well as the underestimation of the influence of marital status, may explain some of these variations. Specifically, older women are more likely to have experienced widowhood owing to their longer life expectancy compared with men; as marital status was included in the assessment of social isolation in this study, it may have influenced the higher prevalence of social isolation among women. Moreover, social isolation is influenced by socioeconomic and structural factors throughout the life course and is associated with various health outcomes,38 making it challenging to disregard sociocultural differences in the context of social isolation. More nuanced research is needed on the mechanisms by which social isolation affects each gender, as well as the development of refined measurement tools to better understand these effects.

Among both men and women, older age, lower educational attainment and lower income were significantly associated with social isolation. This aligns with existing evidence that the oldest-old are at higher risk due to increased likelihood of widowhood or divorce, declining physical function, reduced social activity and contraction of social networks.35 39 These findings emphasise the importance of community-based interventions targeting economically and educationally disadvantaged groups, as well as the oldest old, to strengthen social networks, enhance mobility and encourage social participation.

Overall, both men and women who experienced social isolation exhibited a higher prevalence of depression, consistent with previous studies reporting associations between social isolation and depression.40 41 Notably, men with social isolation were 1.60 times more likely to have depression than those without social isolation, a higher figure than that observed among women. This reflects previous findings indicating that men may be more vulnerable to the adverse effects of social isolation and loneliness than women.42 43 In Korea, traditional masculinity often aligns with hegemonic masculinity,44 framing emotional distress as weak and unmanly; this hinders help-seeking behaviours.45 Considering that social support is a critical resource for mitigating depression,46 47 it is possible that men, fearing harm to their perceived masculinity, may struggle to find appropriate ways to address moral distress, potentially resulting in higher rates of depression. Multiple studies reported that adherence to hegemonic masculinity among men is associated with poorer mental health outcomes.48,50 These findings underscore the need to develop strategies that can help both men and women effectively process the negative emotions arising from social isolation. In particular, interventions that can shift hegemonic masculinity among men and promote active help-seeking for emotional distress are needed.

Beyond mental health, social isolation was linked to adverse health behaviours in both genders. Socially isolated women were 2.53 times more likely to smoke than their non-isolated counterparts. These findings align with previous studies reporting that social isolation among older adults exacerbates poor health behaviours, including higher smoking rates.51,54 Furthermore, feelings of loneliness negatively affected health behaviours,55,57 and women tended to respond more sensitively to emotional stress.58 Thus, it is plausible that socially isolated women, being more sensitive to negative experiences such as loneliness, may exhibit a greater deterioration in health behaviours.

The influence of social isolation extends to diabetes management, presenting divergent patterns by gender. Among men, social isolation was not significantly associated with medication use or complication screening, whereas socially isolated women showed lower medication adherence, fewer regular check-ups and reduced participation in diabetes education. These findings are consistent with prior research indicating that the effects of social isolation are not uniform across diabetes management behaviours among older adults, and may not manifest differently by gender.18 Together, these gender-specific patterns suggest that social isolation may influence diabetes management through distinct psychosocial pathways for men and women, highlighting the importance of addressing these differential mechanisms in future interventions.

Overall, the findings indicate that social isolation should be explicitly considered in public health strategies for older adults with diabetes. As population ageing accelerates and social isolation remains a persistent challenge in South Korea,29 approaches that reflect the gender-specific pathways through which social isolation affects diabetes management may help strengthen community-based support. Furthermore, interventions that foster supportive networks, encourage help-seeking behaviours, and promote adaptive coping strategies may help mitigate the adverse effects of social isolation on health outcomes among older adults with diabetes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design using KCHS data precludes causal inferences between social isolation and health-related outcomes, and the timing and duration of social isolation could not be assessed, although prior studies suggest that its effects may vary by exposure period. Furthermore, the dataset used in this study was derived from a large national survey of adults in South Korea, and the findings may reflect Korea’s specific sociocultural context. Therefore, caution is warranted when generalising the results to populations in other countries. Second, social isolation was measured using a limited set of proxy indicators based on objective circumstances (eg, marital status, household type, social activity participation, social network maintenance and economic activity). While this approach captures objective aspects of social interaction, such as the presence or frequency of social contact, it may not fully reflect the qualitative or functional characteristics of social relationships. In particular, subjective perceptions of isolation, such as loneliness, were not assessed, and the findings related to social isolation should therefore be interpreted with caution. Third, all outcomes, including depressive symptoms, perceived stress, health behaviours and diabetes management, relied on self-reported data and may have been subject to recall or reporting bias. Finally, because social isolation is inextricably linked to sociocultural norms and healthcare system characteristics, the findings of this study, conducted among older adults in South Korea, may have limited generalisability beyond the Korean context.

Despite these limitations, this study has notable strengths. It used nationally representative KCHS data with sampling weights to provide generalisable estimates for older adults with diabetes in South Korea. By focusing on older adults with diabetes, it highlighted the combined impact of chronic illness and social disconnection. Finally, it examined a broad range of health domains and conducted gender-stratified analyses, emphasising the need for gender-tailored interventions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that social isolation was associated with poor mental health, unhealthy behaviours and certain aspects of diabetes management among older adults with diabetes in South Korea. The association with diabetes management varied across indicators and gender, with some indicators showing no significant association. Socially isolated women exhibited more vulnerable patterns in diabetes self-care. These findings suggest that social isolation should be considered within the broader framework of chronic disease management. Addressing social isolation may support improvements in selected aspects of diabetes care; however, further research is needed to clarify the mechanisms linking social isolation to disease management.
