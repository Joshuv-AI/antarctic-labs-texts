The usefulness of full-thickness biopsy during semi-rigid pleuroscopy for differentiating between malignant pleural mesothelioma and non-specific pleurisy
Authors: Takahiro Ueda, Toshiyuki Nakai, Sayaka Tanaka, Hiroaki Nagamine, Atsushi Miyamoto, Misako Nishimura, Yoshiya Matsumoto, Kanako Sato, Kazuhiro Yamada, Tetsuya Watanabe, Kazuhisa Asai, Yuji Matsumoto, Yu Mikami, Tomoya Kawaguchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalignant pleural mesothelioma is a rare disease with a poor prognosis; distinguishing it from non-specific pleurisy is essential for determining an appropriate treatment strategy. We aimed to evaluate the diagnostic utility and safety of full-thickness biopsy performed during semi-rigid pleuroscopy under local anaesthesia to differentiate between malignant pleural mesothelioma and non-specific pleurisy.MethodsConsecutive patients who attempted full-thickness biopsy using a cryoprobe or an insulated-tip diathermic knife during semi-rigid pleuroscopy between April 2019 and October 2023 were retrospectively enrolled. The diagnostic utility of full-thickness biopsy for distinguishing malignant pleural mesothelioma from non-specific pleurisy was assessed based on diagnostic accuracy, specimen quality, and procedural safety.ResultsAmong the 64 patients who underwent full-thickness biopsy, 28 diagnosed with malignant pleural mesothelioma or non-specific pleurisy were included in this study. With the exception of one malignant pleural mesothelioma case diagnosed by surgical biopsy, 13 malignant pleural mesothelioma and 14 non-specific pleurisy cases were histologically diagnosed through full-thickness biopsy, with clinical courses supporting these diagnoses. The median full-thickness biopsy specimen size was 18.8 mm² (range: 0.6-364.2 mm2), and in 25 cases (89.3%), full-thickness pleura was pathologically confirmed. Full-thickness biopsy demonstrated a sensitivity of 92.9%, specificity of 100%, positive predictive value of 100%, negative predictive value of 93.3%, and an overall diagnostic accuracy of 96.4%. No severe complications were reported with the procedures.ConclusionFull-thickness biopsy during semi-rigid pleuroscopy provides high diagnostic accuracy and safety for differentiating malignant pleural mesothelioma from non-specific pleurisy, while yielding high-quality pleural tissue specimens.Keywords: Semi-rigid pleuroscopy, Malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM), Full-thickness biopsy (FTB), Non-specific pleurisy (NSP)

Background

Malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM) is a rare malignancy with poor prognosis, and pleural effusions are the clinical presentation [1]. Accurate diagnosis is essential to improve treatment outcomes for patients with MPM; however, histological differentiation between MPM and non-specific pleurisy (NSP), including reactive mesothelial hyperplasia and fibrous pleurisy, remains clinically challenging due to the limited specificity of Light’s criteria for the separation of exudative pleural effusions from transudative ones [2]. Definitive differentiation between MPM and NSP involves identifying tumour cell invasion into adipose tissue morphologically using tissue specimens containing all pleural layers, along with ancillary techniques such as immunohistochemical staining and molecular testing [3]. Surgical biopsy allows for the collection of full-thickness pleural tissues; however, some patients are unsuitable for surgery due to poor general conditions resulting from advanced age and underlying diseases. Therefore, given the relatively common incidence of non-malignant NSP, developing a minimally invasive diagnostic technique capable of collecting qualitative and quantitative pleural specimens, including those from deeper layers, is required for accurate MPM diagnosis [4].

Semi-rigid pleuroscopy under local anaesthesia is a safe and effective diagnostic modality for pleural diseases associated with carcinomatous pleurisy from lung cancer or tuberculous pleurisy [5]. However, the conventional biopsy technique using flexible forceps during semi-rigid pleuroscopy is limited, as it collects only small specimens from the pleural surface, leading to clinical challenges in distinguishing MPM from NSP [6]. To overcome this limitation, advanced tools such as the cryoprobe and insulated-tip diathermic knife (IT knife) have been introduced into semi-rigid pleuroscopy, enabling less invasive full-thickness biopsy (FTB) to collect large tissue specimens encompassing all pleural layers [7–9].

To date, only a few studies have investigated the diagnostic accuracy of FTB for differentiating MPM from NSP using a combination of the cryoprobe and IT knife during semi-rigid pleuroscopy. Differentiating between MPM and NSP using a minimally invasive procedure could contribute to developing diagnostic strategies for MPM. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to assess the diagnostic utility and safety of FTB during semirigid pleuroscopy for differentiating between MPM and NSP. In addition, the quality of the specimens obtained using the FTB was evaluated.

Materials and methods

Patients and study design

We retrospectively reviewed consecutive patients who attempted FTB during semi-rigid pleuroscopy for the diagnosis of unexplained exudative effusion with pleural thickening, possibly indicative of MPM, at Osaka Metropolitan University Hospital between April 2019 and October 2023. The criteria for suspecting MPM as the cause of exudative pleural effusion were: pleural thickening on computed tomography (CT) (defined as ≥ 3 mm) [10], fluorodeoxyglucose uptake on positron emission tomography-CT, cytological findings suggestive of MPM, or a history of asbestos exposure [3]. Patients ultimately diagnosed with MPM or NSP were enrolled and analysed in this study, while those diagnosed with other specific pleurisy were excluded. Clinical data, including patient characteristics, CT findings, pleural effusion volume on CT defined in a previous report [11], procedure details, semi-rigid pleuroscopy results, and clinical courses, were prospectively compiled into the Multicentre Identical Normalised Endoscopy Registry for Valuation and Analysis (protocol no. 4364) and retrospectively reviewed for this study. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki (revised 2013) and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Osaka Metropolitan University Hospital (protocol no. 2024 − 155). Written informed consent for the procedure was obtained from all patients before undergoing semi-rigid pleuroscopy.

Semi-rigid pleuroscopy instruments and FTB procedure

A team of experts (T. U. and/or T. N.) and resident trainees performed all pleuroscopy under local anaesthesia (using a combination of fentanyl and propofol) using a semi-rigid pleuroscope (LTF-260 or LTF-H290, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The extent of pleural effusion and thickening was assessed using contrast-enhanced CT within one week prior to semi-rigid pleuroscopy. A flexible trocar (MAJ-1058, Olympus) was placed following a blunt dissection at the optimal site, identified via thoracic ultrasonography, with the patient in the lateral position. The chest cavity, including the diaphragm, parietal pleura, and visceral pleura, was carefully examined using a pleuroscope inserted through the trocar. Initially, a saline solution containing lidocaine and epinephrine was injected into the subpleural space using an injection needle (NM-9 L-1, Olympus) to raise the affected pleura. Subsequently, FTB was performed using a cryoprobe (2.4 mm diameter, Erbe Elektromedizin GmbH, Tübingen, Germany) with a 4–6 s freezing time, following previously reported methods [12]. If the pleural lesion was deemed too hard to collect using the cryoprobe, the tip of IT knife-2 (KD-611 L, Olympus) was inserted into a pinhole created in the pleura using coagulation forceps (FD-6 C-1, Olympus), and the full-thickness pleural lesion was then incised in a circular fashion [9]. After this high-frequency incision, the pleura containing the deep layer was collected in one piece using the cryoprobe or grasping forceps (FG-42 L-1; Olympus) for FTB. The decision to perform a high-frequency incision was made at the physician’s discretion based on pleural findings on pleuroscopy, the hardness of the pleural lesion, and quantity and depth of specimens collected with pleural cryobiopsy. FTB was performed to (i) endoscopically collect pleural lesions above the endothoracic fascia and (ii) to sample from two or more locations whenever possible [9]. The obtained tissue specimens were fixed in bottles containing 10% neutral-buffered formalin. Procedure time was defined as the interval between pleuroscope insertion through the trocar and its removal (excluding removal for cryobiopsy). Any procedure-related complications, including bleeding, chest pain, lung infection, and other adverse events, were recorded.

Histopathological analysis

Expert pathologists evaluated and diagnosed specimens according to the established criteria and guidelines for diagnosing MPM and its entities [1]. Additional staining and immunohistochemistry were performed for accurate classification using the following monoclonal antibodies: calretinin (clone SP13; NICHIREI BIOSCIENCES INC., Chuo, Tokyo, Japan), Wilms’ Tumour 1 protein (clone 6 F-H2; Dako, Carpinteria, California, USA), D2-40 (clone D2-40; NICHIREI BIOSCIENCES INC., Chuo, Tokyo, Japan), Ber-EP4 (clone Ber-EP4; Dako, Carpinteria, California, USA,), Anti-Thyroid Transcription Factor 1 (clone 8G7G3/1; Dako, Carpinteria, California, USA), Anti-EpCAM antibody (clone MOC-31; abcam, Cambridge Cambridgeshire, UK), Claudin-4 (clone EPRR17575; abcam, Cambridge Cambridgeshire, UK), EMA (clone E29; NICHIREI BIOSCIENCES INC., Chuo, Tokyo, Japan), Desmin (clone D33; Dako, Carpinteria, California, USA), Anti-Glucose Transporter GLUT1 antibody (clone SPM498; abcam, Cambridge Cambridgeshire, UK), Methylthioadenosine phosphorylase (clone MTAP/1813; Gene Tex, International Corporation, Irvine, California, USA), and BRCA1 associated protein 1 (clone C-4; NICHIREI BIOSCIENCES INC., Chuo, Tokyo, Japan).

Diagnosis of MPM and NSP

Semi-rigid pleuroscopy results were confirmed based on surgical findings or clinical follow-up. MPM cases were considered diagnostic when histological findings of MPM were confirmed with FTB-derived specimens and consistent clinical courses including treatment outcomes were subsequently observed. NSP, encompassing reactive mesothelial hyperplasia and fibrous pleurisy, was considered diagnostic when the following criteria were met: (i) specimens obtained by FTB showed only benign findings such as fibrosis and inflammation and (ii) the clinical course—including repeated blood tests, imaging, and physiological examinations—remained consistent with these findings during a follow-up period of at least 1 year, when feasible [13, 14]. All patients undergoing semi-rigid pleuroscopy were assessed for the need for additional surgical biopsies through a multidisciplinary conference of thoracic experts and oncologists. Clinical information not available in the registry database was obtained from electronic medical records or through interviews with patients or their primary medical institutions.

Assessment

Descriptive statistics are presented as numbers (percentages) and medians (ranges). To evaluate the utility of FTB during semi-rigid pleuroscopy in differentiating MPM from NSP, we analysed the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and diagnostic accuracy of the FTB using standard definitions for cases ultimately diagnosed with these two diseases [15]. In addition, the diagnostic yield of FTB for exudative effusions with pleural thickening, defined as the ratio of diagnosed cases to all other cases (including diseases other than MPM and NSP), was calculated.

To assess the quality of FTB-obtained specimens, an expert pathologist (S. T.) evaluated the size and tissue depth in addition to reviewing the registry database. After scanning haematoxylin and eosin (HE)-stained slides using a virtual slide scanner (Nanozoomer 2.0-HT Whole Slide Imager, Hamamatsu Photonics, Hamamatsu, Japan), the size (in mm2) of all biopsy specimens was measured by enclosing each biopsy section with NDP.view2 (version 2.6, Hamamatsu Photonics). Tissue depth was categorised from superficial to deep layers, as pleural surface, adipose tissue, and skeletal muscle. Specimens containing adipose tissue or deeper layers were defined as full-thickness tissue specimens [16], and the technique of FTB was considered successful when full-thickness tissue specimens were collected.

Results

Among the 117 patients who underwent semi-rigid pleuroscopy under local anaesthesia during the study period, 64 underwent FTB during the procedure to diagnose exudative effusion with pleural thickening. Ultimately, 36 patients were diagnosed with conditions other than MPM and NSP: 25 with lung cancer metastasis, six with non-lung cancer metastasis, three with empyema, and two with tuberculous pleurisy. Of the 14 MPM cases, FTB-derived specimens were of sufficient size and tissue depth to permit diagnosis, including histological subtype classification, in 12 cases (85.7%). Subtype misclassification occurred in one (7.1%), where the diagnosis was revised from epithelioid MPM based on FTB-derived specimens to biphasic MPM based on the surgical specimens. Of the 13 cases diagnosed as MPM based on FTB-derived specimens, 12 (92.3%) subsequently exhibited clinical courses consistent with their histological subtype classifications. One case of biphasic MPM was not diagnosed using FTB during semi-rigid pleuroscopy and was ultimately diagnosed through surgical biopsy. Treatment strategies for the 14 MPM cases included surgery, chemotherapy, and best supportive care in four, six, and four, respectively. Of the 14 NSP cases, 11 (78.6%) were diagnosed as reactive mesothelial hyperplasia, based on the absence of stromal invasion, necrosis, and atypical mitotic figures suggestive of malignancy in FTB-derived specimens. The remaining three cases (21.4%) were diagnosed as fibrous pleuritis. In all cases, the clinical courses were consistent with the biopsy findings.

The median (range) tissue specimen size obtained by FTB was 18.8 (0.6–364.2.6.2) mm2. In 25 cases (89.3%), FTB was considered technically successful, as full-thickness pleural tissue was confirmed pathologically. Among the 14 MPM cases, FTB yielded tissue specimens from adipose tissue or deeper layers in 12 (85.7%), providing sufficient evidence of tumour cell invasion into the surrounding adipose tissue layer and a definitive diagnosis. In one case of sarcomatous MPM where FTB failed to obtain full-thickness tissue, ancillary tests using FTB-derived specimens complemented the diagnosis. In the NSP cases, specimens were obtained from adipose tissue or deeper layers in 13 (92.9%). The median (range) follow-up period after semi-rigid pleuroscopy for all patients was 656 (58–1760) days, with 465 (58–765) days for MPM and 849 (121–1760) days for NSP. Although two patients with NSP died during the follow-up period of less than one year, one from aspiration pneumonia and another from heart failure, these causes of death were unrelated to MPM. Moreover, no case of pleural effusion recurrence or malignancy was observed during the follow-up period in the NSP cases.

Regarding complications, two (7.1%) experienced a prolonged air leak (both in MPM). No severe or life-threatening complications, such as bleeding, were observed.

Discussion

This study demonstrated a high diagnostic accuracy (96.4%) of FTB during semi-rigid pleuroscopy in differentiating MPM from NSP. FTB yielded large tissue specimens (median size: 18.8 mm²), including deep tissue in most cases (25/28, 89.3%), which were sufficient for differentiating MPM from NSP without serious complications [3, 7]. Thus, these findings suggest the significant potential of FTB in determining diagnostic strategies for MPM.

Concerns have been raised regarding the limited quality of tissue specimens obtained with conventional forceps, potentially leading to false-negative results for MPM diagnosis in cases such as NSP through semi-rigid pleuroscopy [1, 8, 17]. Consequently, semi-rigid pleuroscopy with conventional forceps has been associated with an undesirable risk that requires surgical biopsies in non-malignant cases and failure to diagnose MPM [18, 19]. This study indicates that integrating FTB into semi-rigid pleuroscopy could enhance the precise differentiation between MPM and NSP through a minimally invasive procedure, thereby reducing the undesirable risk that requires surgical biopsies. The high-quality tissue specimens obtained by FTB during semi-rigid pleuroscopy provide pathologists with comprehensive three-dimensional information on pleural histoarchitecture, facilitating the identification of tumour cell invasion into adipose tissue. Furthermore, this technique yields high-quality specimens of sufficient size. Therefore, even if sampling from deeper layers fails, ancillary tests could complement the differentiation MPM from NSP. Notably, all but one of the cases diagnosed as NSP by FTB followed a clinical course consistent with the pleuroscopy results, suggesting that FTB can help overcome the limited diagnostic accuracy of conventional forceps biopsy during semi-rigid pleuroscopy [20, 21]. Ideal ancillary tests for distinguishing MPM from benign lesions include confirming homozygous deletion of CDKN2A by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) [22] and loss of BAP1 [23] and MTAP [24] expression by immunohistochemistry. The latest World Health Organisation classification system emphasises the importance of recognising “mesothelioma in situ” using these ancillary tests, and their implementation in clinical practice aims to optimise patient care [25–27]. FTB-derived specimens appear to be sufficient in quality and quantity for analysing this novel early stage of MPM.

This study demonstrated that FTB achieved a high diagnostic yield (95.3%) across various cases, not limited to MPM and NSP, while maintaining an acceptable safety profile. Therefore, FTB can improve the diagnostic utility of semi-rigid pleuroscopy for pleural diseases and offer a viable alternative to surgical biopsy. Recent reports have noted challenges in obtaining tissue from dense and hard pleura using pleural cryobiopsy despite its simplicity and ease of use [8, 28]. In contrast, the IT knife allows specimen collection from such pleura [9, 29]; however, it poses the risk of leaving the deep layers of the pleura inside the thoracic cavity when retrieving the incised lesions. A key strength of this study was the flexible selection and combination of biopsy devices based on pleural hardness during the procedure, overcoming the limitations of individual devices and achieving a high success rate (89.3%) in collecting full-thickness pleura of excellent quality. However, clinicians should be aware that effective sampling during semi-rigid pleuroscopy can be challenging in cases with intrathoracic adhesions resulting from thoracic drainage or infection. This limitation was evident in one of the cases included in this study, where FTB failed to diagnose MPM. Furthermore, obtaining large specimens with FTB from lesions near the lung apex remains challenging due to the limited operating field of semi-rigid pleuroscopy. These challenges persisted despite the introduction of FTB. Therefore, clinicians should develop a suitable diagnostic strategy for suspected MPM pleural lesions, incorporating preoperative ultrasonography to assess intrathoracic adhesions and contrast-enhanced CT or 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT to identify optimal biopsy sites.

Rigid pleuroscopy has been established as a reliable and safe diagnostic modality for exudative pleural effusion, although it requires general anaesthesia [6]. Notably, compared with semi-rigid pleuroscopy using conventional flexible forceps, rigid pleuroscopy has been reported to be superior in diagnostic accuracy and in the size and depth of pleural specimens [16]. On the other hand, the size and depth of pleural specimens obtained by FTB in this study (median size: 18.4 mm2; proportion of deeper tissue: 89.3% of cases) were comparable to those reported for rigid pleuroscopy (mean size: 22.6 mm²; proportion of deeper tissue: 63% of specimens). These findings suggest that incorporating FTB into semi-rigid pleuroscopy may provide diagnostic utility and tissue sampling capability comparable to those of rigid pleuroscopy. Furthermore, FTB offers additional advantages, including lower procedural costs and avoidance of risks associated with general anaesthesia [30, 31]. The present study suggests that semi-rigid pleuroscopy with FTB is associated with relatively prolonged procedure times (median: 54.8 min). Based on these findings, rigid pleuroscopy may be more appropriate than semi-rigid pleuroscopy with FTB for diagnosing pleural effusion when minimizing procedure time is clinically desirable or when extensive intrathoracic adhesions and marked pleural thickening are suspected. Furthermore, rigid pleuroscopy offers important advantages, including the reliable management of unexpected adverse events—such as the use of hemostatic devices for bleeding or the management of accidental visceral pleural injury caused by biopsy instruments—as well as the ability to promptly convert to open thoracotomy under general anesthesia. Therefore, the choice between rigid and semi-rigid pleuroscopy should be guided by the patient’s overall condition and the anticipated intrathoracic findings.

Recently, NSP was reported to account for up to 40% of histological diagnoses in cases of unexplained exudative effusion evaluated through medical thoracoscopy. NSP presents with “non-specific” histological findings such as inflammatory and fibrotic changes, lacking specific pathological features of malignant tumours and tuberculous pleurisy [2, 4, 32]. However, the histological similarities between reactive mesothelial hyperplasia and epithelioid MPM and between fibrous pleuritis and sarcomatoid or desmoplastic MPM elevate the risk of misdiagnosis and pose significant challenges for pathologists in distinguishing NSP from MPM [1]. Considering the preference for minimally invasive NSP diagnosis in benign conditions, the poor prognosis of MPM [33], and the risk of postoperative tumour seeding with multiple surgical biopsy ports [34], FTB during semi-rigid pleuroscopy under local anaesthesia with a single port appears to be a favourable diagnostic technique for distinguishing these diseases. Notably, approximately 8–10% of cases initially diagnosed as NSP are later confirmed to be malignant, particularly MPM [35]. However, it remains unclear whether this high conversion rate results from sampling errors in biopsy techniques, misdiagnosis by the pathologist, or slow progression to malignancy due to chronic inflammation of NSP [17, 18]. Consequently, physicians should implement long-term follow-up (over 1 year), as in this study, for patients diagnosed with NSP to monitor pleural effusion recurrence and assess their overall condition [13, 36].

This study had some limitations. First, it did not compare the diagnostic utility of FTB with conventional forceps biopsy during semi-rigid pleuroscopy or rigid forceps biopsy during medical thoracoscopy. Second, cases ultimately diagnosed as benign asbestos pleurisy (BAP) were not included, as standard diagnostic criteria and methods for differentiating it from NSP are lacking. Given the histological similarities between BAP and NSP [35, 37], some NSP cases in this study might have been BAP. However, both conditions share common treatment approaches, including careful monitoring for the potential progression to MPM [17]. Therefore, it is unlikely that any significant problems related to the care and treatment of NSP cases occurred in this study. Furthermore, the lower prevalence of MPM and NSP compared to other causes of pleurisy resulted in a limited number of cases included in this study. Finally, FISH analysis was not routinely performed to distinguish MPM from NSP, as it is not covered by health insurance in our country. Notably, the limited number of cases in this study may represent a significant limitation, potentially leading to an overestimation of the utility of FTB in the histological differentiation between MPM and NSP. Further multicenter prospective studies with long-term follow-up are warranted to allow a more accurate analysis. Nevertheless, FTB yields high-quality tissue specimens, including deep pleural layers, and should be considered a valuable diagnostic technique for differentiating MPM from NSP in cases of unexplained exudative effusion.

Conclusion

FTB is a safe and effective sampling technique for accurately differentiating MPM from NSP in the diagnosis of pleural thickening and exudative pleural effusion.
