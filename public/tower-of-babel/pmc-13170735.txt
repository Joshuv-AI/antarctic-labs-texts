Heterogeneous Causes of Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome Correlate With Distinct Peripheral Polyunsaturated Fatty Acid Metabolites
Authors: Laura A Leuenberger, Joseph S Bednash, Evangeline Schott, Carmen Mikacenic, Eric D Morrell, Carson Richardson, Lynn A Fussner, Jeffrey C Horowitz, Rama K Mallampalli, Saame Raza Shaikh, Robert M Tighe, Kymberly M Gowdy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS), a heterogeneous syndrome of hypoxic respiratory failure secondary to dysregulated pulmonary inflammation, is caused by diverse insults. This heterogeneity presents challenges for mechanistic and therapeutic research, as evidenced by conflicting results from trials of n−3 polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) supplementation for ARDS. PUFAs and downstream oxylipins are important to pulmonary inflammation but are not well defined in ARDS. We hypothesized that differences in fatty acid metabolism, as measured by levels of n−3 and n−6 PUFAs and oxylipins, are associated with differences in ARDS outcomes, inflammation, and causes. To test this, PUFAs/oxylipins were measured by LC MS/MS in plasma samples from 90 patients with ARDS. Pro‐inflammatory cytokines and chemokines IL‐6 and IL‐8 were measured by ELISA. Multivariable linear regressions modeled the relationship between PUFAs/oxylipins, inflammation, and ARDS mortality, severity, and cause. Multiple n−3 and n−6 PUFA‐derived oxylipins were decreased in severe ARDS. We did not detect differences in PUFAs/oxylipins by mortality. PUFAs/oxylipins varied by cause of ARDS, especially between patients with sepsis and those with trauma. Furthermore, specific oxylipins were associated with IL‐6 and IL‐8. As we observed that oxylipins varied by disease severity and underlying cause, these metabolites may function as biomarkers and suggest dysregulated mechanisms of lung repair. Furthermore, while oxylipins are derived from PUFAs, differences in PUFAs did not directly correlate with changes in oxylipins, suggesting altered lipid metabolism as a mechanism. Further consideration of differences in lipid metabolism in ARDS could identify subgroups with differential responses to n−3 PUFA supplementation or other therapies.Keywords: acute lung injury, acute respiratory distress syndrome, inflammation, lipidomics, lipids, lipids/oxidation, omega‐3 fatty acids, oxylipins, pulmonary inflammation

Introduction

Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) is a heterogeneous syndrome of severe lung injury caused by direct injury (i.e., bacterial or viral pneumonia, aspiration) or indirect injury (i.e., sepsis, massive transfusion, pancreatitis) [1]. Heterogeneous injuries may cause distinct inflammatory mechanisms, complicating ARDS research and treatment. While hypo‐ and hyperinflammatory ARDS subphenotypes have been proposed to explain inflammation‐driven outcomes [2], these subphenotypes do not consider etiology. Therefore, understanding how disparate causes of ARDS alter the inflammatory response may help to identify molecular mechanisms and potential personalized medicine‐based treatments.

Administration of omega−3 (n−3) polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) and their metabolites improves outcomes in animal models of sepsis and ARDS, yet clinical trials in humans show inconsistent results [3]. For example, one randomized controlled trial supplementing n−3 PUFAs eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) in sepsis‐ or trauma‐related lung injury did not find differences in pulmonary or systemic inflammation [4]. Conversely, a randomized controlled trial supplementing n−3 PUFAs EPA and DHA, omega−6 (n−6) PUFA gamma‐linolenic acid (GLA), and antioxidants observed differences in inflammatory trajectories in sepsis‐related ARDS [5]. Variability in the type and dose of PUFA supplementation, differences in co‐administered vitamins or antioxidants, as well as heterogeneous cohorts of patients with ARDS [6, 7] likely contribute to inconsistent results across n−3 PUFA trials, making it challenging to compare results or establish a clear picture of how PUFAs impact ARDS outcomes. Nonetheless, the importance of PUFAs in ARDS merits careful consideration, given that PUFAs and their signaling metabolites, termed ‘oxylipins’ (i.e., oxygenated fatty acids), are known regulators of the inflammatory response [8]. Oxylipins can be synthesized from PUFAs nonenzymatically or enzymatically via lipoxygenases (LOX), cyclooxygenases (COX), and cytochromes (CYPs). Oxylipin concentrations are dynamic in inflammation, reflecting short half‐lives, and are known to both trigger inflammation and promote inflammation resolution [9]. PUFAs and oxylipins are altered in systemic circulation in infectious and inflammatory lung diseases such as asthma [10], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [11, 12], COVID‐19 [13, 14] and ARDS [15], and are known to be altered in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) samples in patients with COVID‐19 infection [16]. However, the relationships between n−3 and n−6 PUFA metabolism, ARDS etiology, and ARDS‐associated inflammation have not been determined.

We hypothesized that there are distinct patterns of n−3 and n−6 PUFAs and oxylipins that correlate with mortality, ARDS severity, ARDS etiology, and inflammation. To test this hypothesis, we measured PUFAs and oxylipins in 90 ARDS subjects and correlated them with clinical outcomes. While PUFA and oxylipin levels did not predict mortality, select oxylipins were decreased in severe ARDS. Plasma PUFAs and oxylipins significantly differed based on ARDS etiology. Additionally, PUFAs and oxylipins correlated with inflammatory cytokine interleukin (IL)‐6 and chemokine IL‐8. Collectively, our findings demonstrate a unique and previously undescribed role of PUFAs/oxylipins in ARDS.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Population

Patient samples from two separate biorepositories were used for this study. The first biorepository was The Ohio State University Intensive Care Unit Registry (IRB 2020H0175: The Ohio State University Intensive Care Unit Registry (BuckICU), 2023H0138: Critical Illness Phenotyping of Existing Biospecimens), an ongoing prospective single‐center cohort of adult patients hospitalized with ARDS or critical illness started in 2020. Patients were screened on admission to the intensive care unit, and consent was obtained within 48 h of ICU admission. Enrollment criteria included either (a) respiratory failure, defined as a need for supplemental respiratory support above baseline, including nasal cannula, heated high flow nasal cannula, non‐invasive positive pressure ventilation, or invasive mechanical ventilation to maintain an oxygen saturation greater than 92%, or (b) met sepsis criteria by Sepsis‐3 guidelines [17]. The second biorepository was the University of Washington (IRB #3412: University of Washington Human Subjects Division). Samples were collected from a prospectively enrolled cohort of critically ill adults with acute hypoxemic respiratory failure on mechanical ventilation between September 2020 and December 2023. Enrollment and initial sample collection occurred within 96 h of hospital admission. All study protocols were approved and monitored by the respective local institutional review boards (IRB) at The Ohio State University and University of Washington and abided by the Declaration of Helsinki principles.

In both cohorts, ARDS diagnosis was adjudicated by two pulmonary/critical care physicians, using the new global definition of ARDS [18]. At the time of adjudication, a clinical decision of the cause of the ARDS was assigned. The etiologies of ARDS were defined as sepsis, pneumonia, aspiration, trauma, and other. In both registries, recognizing that ARDS is frequently multifactorial, a case could be assigned up to two causes of ARDS. Therefore, ARDS etiology groups overlap in this analysis. For this study, a total of 90 patients (all intubated and mechanically ventilated) from these two registries met ARDS criteria and had available samples (59 from Ohio State and 31 from University of Washington). Given the prior collection of these samples, blinding was not possible in sample selection; however, all laboratory analyses were completed in batches without the knowledge of any clinical data and thus run in a blinded fashion. Both biorepositories of enrolled patients are reflective of the heterogeneous ICU population and did not stratify enrollment for specific sex, race, ethnicity, or cause of ARDS. For both cohorts, patient data was stored securely in REDCap [19].

Blood Collection and Processing

Peripheral blood samples were collected in citrate vacutainer tubes (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lake, New Jersey), centrifuged at 1200 × g for 10 min and then stored at −80°C.

Fatty Acid and Oxylipin Measurements

Patient plasma samples stored at −80°C from both repositories that had not undergone a prior freeze/thaw cycle were processed with 1:1 ratio of 75% ethanol for lipid extraction. Samples were again stored at −80°C until the time of processing. LC–MS/MS was performed by the Lipidomics Core Facility at Wayne State University as previously published [20]. Briefly, 200 μL of sample were spiked with 5 ng of the internal standards and then diluted to 1 mL with 15% methanol in water and purified on C18 solid‐phase extraction cartridges. The diluted sample (with standards) was applied to the cartridge with 2 mL of 15% methanol and 2 mL of hexane and dried in a vacuum for 30 s. The cartridge was eluted with 0.5 mL methanol with 0.1% formic acid into LC–MS autosample vials. The elute was dried under nitrogen then reconstituted with 25 μL of methanol. For analysis, the sample was brought to room temperature, 25 μL of 25 mM aqueous ammonium acetate was added, mixed and loaded into the autosampler at 15°C. HPLC was performed on a C18 column, as previously published [20]. Plasma concentration of each detected analyte was calculated by dividing the detected quantities (in nanograms) with their corresponding molecular masses and reported as nanomolar. This lipidomic analysis quantified n−3 and n−6 PUFAs (i.e., EPA, DHA, Docosapentaenoic Acid (DPA), Arachidonic Acid (AA), and Linoleic Acid (LA)) and targeted oxylipins (> 120 fatty acid derived metabolites). Due to technical limitations, n−3 alpha‐linolenic acid (ALA) and n−6 gamma‐linolenic acid (GLA) measurements were combined. A list of measured lipids is shown in Table S1.

Measurements of Systemic Inflammation

Plasma samples were used to measure IL‐6 and IL‐8 using the V‐PLEX Human Proinflammatory Panel 1 Kit (Catalog number K15049D, Meso Scale Discovery, Rockville, MD).

Statistical Analysis

To correlate clinical outcomes, a series of both univariable and multivariable linear regression models were used to define associations of individual metabolites with mortality and disease severity (primary outcomes). Mortality was defined by in‐hospital mortality. Disease severity was defined using the Berlin Criteria PaO2/FiO2 ratio of mild (200–300), moderate (100–200), and severe (< 100) [21]. Given the paucity of participants who met “mild” criteria (N = 6), severity and associated responses were analyzed as “Mild/Moderate” versus “Severe.” Missing data within the metabolites were imputed using the K‐nearest neighbor truncation algorithm [22] and any metabolites with > 25% missing data were excluded from analysis. The metabolite data were log2‐transformed to handle the skewed distributions across the sample. The Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate was implemented to account for multiple comparisons with a significance threshold of Q < 0.1 [23].

Multivariable linear regression was then used to describe the relationships of PUFAs/oxylipins to the secondary outcome variables of “ARDS cause” (categories defined above), comparing all causes to sepsis‐related ARDS, as this is the best described etiology in both the PUFA/oxylipin and ARDS literature. Inflammation, measured by cytokine and chemokine IL‐6 and IL‐8, was used as a secondary outcome variable. The relationships between IL‐6/IL‐8 and PUFAs/oxylipins were evaluated via separate linear regression models using the same imputation algorithm and log2‐transformed values. Given known alterations of lipid metabolism by age [24], sex [25], statin use [26], diabetes [27], and cancer [26, 28], the model was adjusted for these variables, as well as for clinical site, race, and COVID‐19 status. Adjusted and unadjusted models had similar results and thus only the unadjusted models are shown. Analysis was performed using R version 4.5.1 [29] and GraphPad Prism 10.

Peripheral PUFAs and Oxylipins Vary by Cause of ARDS

Given the relationship of RvE1 to disease severity and preclinical data regarding its impact on lung injury [30], we first investigated alterations in EPA and EPA metabolites along this pathway by ARDS cause (Figure 3A), without correcting for multiple comparisons based on our presupposed hypothesis. Plasma EPA was decreased in sepsis‐related ARDS when compared to trauma‐associated ARDS (Figure 3B). Other differences included increased EPA‐derived oxylipins 12‐hydroxyeicosapentaenoic acid (HEPE) in the sepsis group (p < 0.05) and 15‐HEPE in both sepsis (p < 0.001) and pneumonia (p < 0.001) when compared to trauma (Figure 3D,E). There were no differences in RvE1 levels by ARDS etiology (Figure 3G).

Specific PUFAs and Oxylipins in the Plasma of Patients With ARDS Correlate With Inflammation

To consider these findings within the context of ARDS driven inflammation [2], we compared the relationship of plasma IL‐6 and IL‐8 levels to PUFAs and oxylipins. Plasma levels of the n−6 PUFA AA negatively correlated with IL‐6 (Q = 0.014) (Table S5). All other n−3 and n−6 PUFAs had a negative trend with IL‐6 but did not reach significance (Figure 5). When compared to plasma IL‐8 levels, circulating PUFAs n−3 DPA (Q = 0.03) and n−6 PUFAs AA (Q = 0.013) and DGLA (Q = 0.085) were all negatively correlated (Table S5).

Associations of n−3 and n−6 PUFAs and Oxylipins as Related to Mortality, Inflammation, and ARDS Persist in a Model Without Patients With COVID‐19

To evaluate if our findings were impacted by the inclusion of COVID‐19 patients, we repeated the analyses with only non‐COVID‐19 patients (N = 57) and identified similar patterns in patients with and without COVID‐19 (Tables S7–S12). Consistent with our results in the combined model, there were no differences in PUFAs by mortality or disease severity, and oxylipins were not associated with mortality (Tables S7 and S8). The previously identified significant oxylipins associated with disease severity had similar directionality but did not reach significance when COVID‐19 patients were removed (Table S9).

Differences by cause of ARDS persisted as well in this analysis without COVID‐19 positive patients. Of note, there were no non‐COVID‐19 viral causes of ARDS (e.g., influenza, respiratory syncytial virus, parainfluenza) documented in any patients included in this study, so this analysis excluded all patients with known viral‐related ARDS. The non‐Sepsis cohorts had increases in many of the same AA‐, DGLA‐, and EPA‐derived prostaglandins seen in the complete model, as well as TXs. In this model, there were fewer AA‐derived HETEs that met significance, but 5‐, 8‐, 11‐, and 20‐HETE were also decreased in patients with trauma compared to sepsis. EPA‐derived HEPEs remained increased in trauma compared to sepsis. Differences in this cohort included RvD metabolites that were no longer significantly different and increased in non‐Sepsis groups, except for AT‐RvD6 which was increased in trauma compared to sepsis. Other DHA metabolites did remain significantly different in this model, and increased in trauma, including 11‐HDoHE, 14‐HDoHE, 16‐HDoHE, and 17‐HDoHE. Again consistent with the overall analysis, LA‐derived diHOMEs were increased compared to sepsis in 9,10‐diHOME (aspiration) and 12,13‐diHOME (aspiration and trauma). Overall, there were no major differences in directionality of metabolites observed when the cohort of COVID‐19 positive patients were excluded, but rather some lipid metabolites were no longer significant in all groups that previously reached significance.

When PUFAs/oxylipins were analyzed by inflammation, IL‐6 was negatively associated with 13,14‐dh‐PGE1 and PGE3 was positively associated with IL‐6 (Table S11). IL‐8 was negatively associated with the production of prostaglandins including PGE1,13,14‐dh‐PGE, 15‐keto‐PGE1, 11,dh‐2,3‐dinor‐TXB2, LXB4, 6‐keto‐PGE1, 2,3‐dinor‐TXB2 (Table S11). Both analyses (combined and without COVID‐19) had similar direction of changes, while the larger cohort had more oxylipins whose association with IL‐6 and IL‐8 reached statistical significance. Thus, the inclusion of patients with COVID‐19 did not impact the associations seen between inflammation and most oxylipins.

Discussion

To better understand ARDS heterogeneity, we characterized PUFAs and their oxylipins in 90 patients with ARDS from diverse causes. This study, the largest of its kind, identified select circulating n−3 and n−6 PUFAs and downstream oxylipins associated with ARDS severity and inflammation. Furthermore, we demonstrated that PUFA and oxylipin profiles are associated with etiologies of ARDS, suggesting differential roles for PUFA and oxylipins based on ARDS cause, which may be specific to the type of injury. We also found relationships between inflammation and PUFAs/oxylipins. Together, these data define PUFA metabolism as differentially regulated by various ARDS etiologies, which may predict inflammation and/or disease severity.

Our data show that plasma levels of EPA, LA, and AA‐derived oxylipins were decreased in more severe ARDS, although circulating PUFAs and oxylipins did not predict mortality. Prior studies have shown decreases in both n−3 and n−6 PUFAs with ARDS [31], but little is known about the associations between PUFAs and ARDS outcomes. There are several potential mechanisms by which oxylipin levels may influence ARDS severity. For example, EPA‐derived RvE1, which we found to be decreased in severe ARDS, is known to improve alveolar fluid clearance [32] and to decrease inflammatory cytokines in animal models of pneumonia and aspiration [30]. We speculate that RvE1 may play a similar role in mitigating ARDS severity. The LA‐derived oxylipins that were decreased in severe disease, 9,10‐diHOME and 12,13‐diHOME, are well described to be associated with exercise and cardioprotective through CaMKII inhibition and decreased endoplasmic reticular stress [33], and decreases in these metabolites are seen in COVID‐19 infections [34]. These LA‐derived oxylipins have not been explored in the context of ARDS, but similar mechanisms may confer protective effects. Finally, we identified decreased levels of the oxylipin 15d12‐14‐PGJ2 in patients with more severe ARDS, which is associated with asthma resolution [35] through its role activating the PPAR‐gamma pathway [36]. Together, our findings highlight the plausibility that specific PUFAs/oxylipins associated with ARDS severity may have a mechanistic role in ARDS pathobiology. Additional clinical and pre‐clinical studies are needed to confirm if these findings are observed in additional cohorts of patients with ARDS, to define the temporal relationships between PUFA and oxylipin levels in ARDS and to test if targeting these oxylipins with direct dietary/pharmacological supplementation in both cellular and animal models alters ARDS inflammatory pathways.

Next, we identified differences in specific oxylipins between patients with sepsis and those with multiple other causes of ARDS. Our sepsis findings are consistent with established literature, where both 12‐HEPE and 15‐HEPE are known to be increased in sepsis models [37]. While multiple prospective ARDS trials have targeted lipid pathways in sepsis‐related ARDS [38, 39], our findings illustrate that the results may not be generalizable to other causes of ARDS. Differences identified were most robust between patients with sepsis and those with trauma. This could be due to differences in the enzymatic pathways known to metabolize PUFAs. We demonstrated a general trend of increased LOX‐dependent oxylipins in sepsis‐associated ARDS, which was not seen in patients with trauma‐associated ARDS. Future studies that evaluate activation and inhibition of LOX metabolism may provide potential therapeutic and translational targets using pre‐clinical models. While we hypothesize that these differences are due to different metabolism based on the provoking cause, the differences could reflect differential time from injury, as oxylipin levels are known to be dynamic throughout inflammation and resolution [40]. For example, if trauma patients are identified earlier to be at risk for ARDS, this could bias our results toward a specific oxylipin profile based on the timeline impacting the cycle of inflammation and resolution, rather than a difference in the injury itself. We attempted to standardize this by using the first available sample, which was collected within 96 h of hospital admission or 48 h of ICU admission; however, we acknowledge that there is likely significant heterogeneity. Overall, this is the first effort, to our knowledge, to describe phenotypes of ARDS by PUFAs/oxylipin levels and adds to our understanding that differences in ARDS etiologies may drive differential inflammation.

A key novel finding of this study is the absence of an association between differences in PUFAs and their downstream oxylipins, perhaps identifying a cause for prior failed n−3 PUFA supplementation trials. Prior studies have been built on the idea that increased n−3 PUFA supplementation would increase downstream oxylipins, yet we did not see that reflected in these data. For example, while EPA‐derived oxylipins were different based on etiology, EPA itself was not different between groups in our multivariable model when adjusted for multiple comparisons. While increasing dietary n−3 PUFAs increases n−3 PUFA‐derived oxylipins in mice [41] and healthy human subjects [42], our study suggests that PUFAs may not be metabolized the same in all injury types, an area for future study. We hypothesize this may be due to differential regulation of enzymatic pathways (LOX, COX, CYP450) by ARDS etiology driving differences in downstream oxylipin concentrations. Supporting this, a prior study of COVID‐19 identified differences in oxylipins between mild and severe disease with increases in downstream metabolites specific to LOX‐derived enzymes [14]. Other potential reasons for this lack of association could be due to our measurement of the plasma, rather than the pulmonary compartment. In this study we did not analyze markers of pulmonary inflammation and PUFAs/oxylipins, and perhaps n−3 PUFA‐derived oxylipins are increased in the airspace but not in systemic circulation. Future studies will compare pulmonary versus systemic lipid metabolism in ARDS to better understand if changes in PUFAs/oxylipins in differential causes of ARDS have site specific effects.

Finally, we demonstrated that n−3 PUFA derived oxylipins were associated with pro‐inflammatory cytokines/chemokines IL‐6 and IL‐8. While our ability to directly compare the previously described hypo‐ and hyper‐inflammatory sub‐phenotypes was limited by clinical data and sample availability, using IL‐6 and IL‐8 as surrogates for hyperinflammatory subphenotypes suggests that there are associations between these markers of inflammation and oxylipin profiles. To categorize these samples as hypo‐ or hyper‐inflammatory would have required measurement of sTNFR‐1, which was not measured in this analysis, along with the measured bicarbonate and IL‐6 levels [43]. However, contrary to published subphenotypes, in this cohort, we did not see differences in mortality by IL‐6 or IL‐8 levels (data not shown), perhaps due to our smaller sample size. Because key biomarkers for ARDS subphenotype classification (e.g., protein C and sTNFR‐1) were not available, our findings cannot be placed within the previously defined hypo‐ and hyper‐inflammatory ARDS subphenotypes, which limits direct comparison with prior studies and the established inflammatory frameworks. When compared to studies that defined ARDS subphenotypes, our cohort was also different from the defining studies in that our population was more male, older, and had a higher BMI [2]. Even so, the relationship between these inflammatory markers and the n−3 PUFA‐derived oxylipins suggests a role for oxylipins as potential biomarkers in these models. Further investigation is needed to understand if specific oxylipins are more strongly related to IL‐6/IL‐8, to the ARDS subphenotypes, or are predictors of outcomes or potential therapeutic response. Our findings associated inflammation with multiple mediators described to be pro‐resolving, especially those of DHA‐derived RvDs. This suggests that pro‐resolving oxylipins may be upregulated with inflammation to initiate the process of resolution but could also be reflective of impaired resolution or impaired production of these oxylipins. As noted, oxylipins have short half‐lives and thus act quickly, and our study, which was not longitudinal, cannot evaluate the kinetic relationships of IL‐6/IL‐8 and oxylipins.

While our study identified relevant oxylipins associated with disease severity and etiology of ARDS, it has several limitations. As an exploratory analysis, this study lacked power to link ARDS mortality to lipid species. In addition, we did not distinguish between pneumonia etiologies (i.e., viral or bacterial) in our primary analysis. Given the differences in oxylipins seen in viral pneumonias (such as COVID‐19) [14] and bacterial pneumonia [44], this may have impacted differences between etiologies. Previous studies have shown differences in the lipidomics in viral and non‐viral causes of sepsis [34], and which pathogen drives pneumonia [44], and thus we acknowledge that our data does not enable us to examine this fully [14]. Further, the risk of ARDS due to COVID‐19 changed over the course of our sample collection, making it challenging to compare the effects as new viral variants emerged and vaccination and prior infections modified the host response [45]. However, our cohort of patients did not have any documented cases of viruses except for COVID‐19, and thus our analysis without COVID‐19 removed the confounding of viral ARDS. In another limitation, our study was limited to a single time point, which does not capture temporal changes. During inflammation and resolution, oxylipins are highly dynamic, presenting considerable challenges for research in this area. Despite very short half‐lives, as an example ~4 h half‐life for RvDs [46], they have significant anti‐inflammatory effects. Future studies analyzing longitudinal samples could explore how dynamic regulation of oxylipins relates to ARDS severity and outcomes. As another limitation, we measured PUFAs, oxylipins, and markers of inflammation in human plasma, reflecting systemic levels of metabolites. Prior studies have demonstrated differences between systemic and pulmonary compartments for inflammatory mediators [47], but lipid metabolites are yet to be explored. Finally, while the cohort is representative of the ICU populations at the contributing hospitals, our study is further limited by the lack of diversity within our cohort, reducing the statistical power to meaningfully explore differences by race or ethnicity. Male predominance similarly limits sex‐based comparisons.

Overall, our study clarifies the contributions of n−3 and n−6 PUFAs and oxylipins to ARDS and positions these lipid mediators as an additional data layer in understanding disease heterogeneity. The correlation of distinct oxylipins with ARDS disease severity, inflammation, and cause supports their use in defining subphenotypes in ARDS and exploring mechanisms of injury and resolution. This framework opens new areas for mechanistic research and may inform clinical trials that stratify patients by injury response for targeted interventions.
