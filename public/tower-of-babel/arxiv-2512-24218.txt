An Equivalence Result on the Order of Differentiability in Frobenius’ Theorem
Yuhki Hosoya

Abstract
This paper examines the simplest case of total differential equations that appears in the theory of foliation structures, without imposing the smoothness assumptions. This leads to a peculiar asymmetry in the differentiability of solutions. To resolve this asymmetry, this paper focuses on the differentiability of the integral manifold. When the system is locally Lipschitz, a solution is ensured to be only locally Lipschitz, but the integral manifolds must be C 1 C^{1} . When the system is C k C^{k} , we can only ensure the existence of a C k C^{k} solution, but the integral manifolds must be C k + 1 C^{k+1} . In addition, we see a counterexample in which the system is C 1 C^{1} , but there is no C 2 C^{2} solution. Moreover, we characterize a minimizer of an optimization problem whose objective function is a quasi-convex solution to a total differential equation. In this connection, we examine two necessary and sufficient conditions for the system in which any solution is quasi-convex. Keywords : Total Differential Equation, Frobenius’ Theorem, Lipschitz Functions, Quasi-Convex Functions. MSC2020 codes : 57R30, 90C25.

2.5
Convex Analysis

Recall the definition of convex functions. A nonempty set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
convex
if
(1-t)x+ty\in C
for all
x,y\in C
and
t\in[0,1]
. Suppose that
C
is a convex set, and let
f:C\to\mathbb{R}
. Then,
f
is said to be
convex
if the epigraph
E=\{(x,a)|a\geq f(x)\}
is convex. This condition is equivalent to the following: for any
x,y\in C
and
t\in[0,1]
,

f((1-t)x+ty)\leq(1-t)f(x)+tf(y).

A function
f:C\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
concave
if
-f
is convex. In other words,
f
is concave if and only if for any
x,y\in C
and
t\in[0,1]
,

f((1-t)x+ty)\geq(1-t)f(x)+tf(y).

Sometimes, a relaxed condition for convexity is useful. Suppose that
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is convex, and let
f:C\to\mathbb{R}
. Then,
f
is said to be
quasi-convex
if for any
x\in C
, the lower contour set
L(x)=\{y\in C|f(y)\leq f(x)\}
is convex. This is equivalent to the following condition: for any
x,y\in C
and
t\in[0,1]
,

f((1-t)x+ty)\leq\max\{f(x),f(y)\}.

Because
(1-t)f(x)+tf(y)\leq\max\{f(x),f(y)\}
, any convex function is quasi-convex. However, the converse is not true. For example, if
C=\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
,
f(x)=-x_{1}x_{2}
is quasi-convex but not convex.

f
is said to be
quasi-concave
if
-f
is quasi-convex. In other words,
f
is quasi-concave if and only if for any
x,y\in C
and
t\in[0,1]
,

f((1-t)x+ty)\geq\min\{f(x),f(y)\}.

Suppose that
U
is open and convex, and
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
is convex. For each
x\in U
, define

\partial f(x)=\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\forall y\in U,\ f(y)-f(x)\geq p\cdot(y-x)\}.

This set-valued function

is called the

of

are two convex functions and

\partial f(x)=\partial f_{1}(x)+\partial f_{2}(x).

See Chapter 23 of Rockafellar
[
16
]
for detailed arguments.

Suppose that
C_{1},C_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
are convex sets and
C_{1}\cap C_{2}=\emptyset
. Then, there exists
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\sup\{p\cdot x|x\in C_{1}\}\leq\inf\{p\cdot x|x\in C_{2}\}.

In particular, suppose that

is a closed convex set and

3.1
Normal Solutions to the Total Differential Equation

Recall the total differential equation (
1
):

\nabla u(x)=\lambda(x)g(x).

We assume that
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open and
g:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
is locally Lipschitz. A function
u:V\to\mathbb{R}
is called a
normal solution
to (
1
) if 1) the domain
V
of
u
is open and included in
U
, 2)
u
is locally Lipschitz, 3) any level set of
u
is an

C^{1}
manifold, 4)
g
is a normal vector field of any level set of
u
, 5)
u
has no local maximum point and local minimum point, and 6) if
u
is differentiable at
x
, then
\nabla u(x)\neq 0
. If

is a normal solution and

includes
x^{*}\in U
, then
u
is also called a
normal solution around
x^{*}
.

NOTE
: In the introduction, we treat a pair of functions
(u,\lambda)
as a solution to (
1
). In contrast, in this subsection, we define a solution as a sole function
u
, and the function
\lambda
is absent. However, the difference in these definitions is merely formal.
Actually, we can show the following result.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open and
g:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
is locally Lipschitz. If
V\subset U
is open and
u:V\to\mathbb{R},\lambda:V\to\mathbb{R}
are a pair of a
C^{1}
nondegenerate function and a continuous non-zero function that satisfies the relationship (
1
) everywhere, then
u
is a normal solution. Conversely, if
u:V\to\mathbb{R}
is a
C^{1}
normal solution, then there exists a continuous non-zero function
\lambda:V\to\mathbb{R}
such that the relationship (
1
) holds everywhere.

Proof.
Suppose that
V\subset U
is open and a pair of functions
u:V\to\mathbb{R},\lambda:V\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy all requirements in this theorem, and choose any
x\in V
. Define
X=u^{-1}(u(x))
. Because
u
is nondegenerate,
u(x)
is a regular value of
u
. By the preimage theorem,
X
is an
n-1
dimensional
C^{1}
manifold, and thus
u
is a normal solution. Let
v\in T_{x}(X)
, and choose a
C^{1}
function
c:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to X
such that
c(0)=x
and
\dot{c}(0)=v
. Then,
u(c(t))\equiv u(x)
, and thus

\nabla u(x)\cdot v=\left.\frac{d}{dt}u(c(t))\right|_{t=0}=0.

Hence,
\nabla u(x)
is orthogonal to
T_{x}(X)
, and thus
g(x)
is also orthogonal to
T_{x}(X)
. Therefore,
g(x)
is a normal vector field of
X
, as desired. It is clear that
u
has no extremum point, and thus it is a normal solution to (
1
).

Conversely, suppose that
u:V\to\mathbb{R}
is a
C^{1}
normal solution to (
1
). Then,
\nabla u(x)\neq 0
everywhere. As in the last paragraph, we can show that for
X=u^{-1}(u(x))
,
g(x)
is orthogonal to
T_{x}(X)
. Because both
g(x)
and
\nabla u(x)
are orthogonal to the
n-1
dimensional space
T_{x}(X)
,
\nabla u(x)
is proportional to
g(x)
, and thus there exists
\lambda(x)\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\nabla u(x)=\lambda(x)g(x)
. Choose any
i
such that
g_{i}(x)\neq 0
. Then,

\lambda(x)=\frac{\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}(x)}{g_{i}(x)},

where the right-hand side is continuous and non-zero around
x
. Therefore,
\lambda
is continuous and non-zero, as desired. This completes the proof.
\blacksquare

However, of course, this relaxation of the requirement reduces the available techniques. Readers may consider that the following requirement “there exists a convex normal solution to (

Hence, using the strong properties of convex functions is unnecessary, at least in this context.

Sometimes, we need a stronger assumption than quasi-convexity. Suppose that
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is convex. A function

is said to be
strictly quasi-convex
if for any

and

, if
x\neq y
, then

u((1-t)x+ty)<\max\{u(x),u(y)\}.

It is well-known that, if

is strictly quasi-convex, then a solution to (
11
) is unique. Therefore, this assumption is useful.

The following proposition presents sufficient conditions for the strict quasi-convexity.

Proposition 3
.

Suppose that
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open, and
g:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
is locally Lipschitz and satisfies Jacobi’s integrability condition. Consider the following three conditions.

(I)

for any
x^{*}\in U
, there exists an open neighborhood
V
of
x^{*}
such that for any
x,y\in V
with
x\neq y
,

g(x)\cdot y\geq g(x)\cdot x\Rightarrow g(y)\cdot x<g(y)\cdot y.

(II)

For any
x\in U
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if
g(x)\cdot v=0
and
v\neq 0
, then

\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}v\cdot g(x+tv)>0.

(III)

Any regular solution
u
to (
1
) defined on a convex domain is strictly quasi-convex.

Then, (I) is equivalent to (III), and (II) implies both (I) and (III).

Proof.
First, suppose that
u:V\to\mathbb{R}
is a regular quasi-concave solution to (
1
). Choose
x\in V
, and consider the following minimization problem:

\displaystyle\min~~~~~

\displaystyle~u(y)

subject to

\displaystyle~y\in V,

\displaystyle~g(x)\cdot y\geq g(x)\cdot x.

By the same arguments as in the proof of Theorem
3
, we have that
x
is a solution to this minimization problem. In particular, if
g(x)\cdot y\geq g(x)\cdot x
, then
u(y)\geq u(x)
. Suppose, in addition, that
u
is strictly quasi-convex. If there is another solution
y\in V
to the above problem, then for
z=(1-2^{-1})x+2^{-1}y
,
g(x)\cdot z\geq g(x)\cdot x
and
u(z)<u(x)
, which is a contradiction. Therefore,
x
is a unique solution to the above problem, and thus if
g(x)\cdot y\geq g(x)\cdot x
and

, then

.

Now, suppose that (III) holds. Choose any
x^{*}\in U
, and a regular solution
u:V\to\mathbb{R}
to (
1
) such that
V
is convex. Then,
u
is strictly quasi-convex. If
y\neq x
and

, then by the above arguments, we have

, and thus

Next, suppose that (I) holds. Choose a regular solution
u:V\to\mathbb{R}
to (
1
), where

is convex. In this case, (i) of Theorem

holds, and thus

is quasi-convex. Hence,

f((1-t)x+ty)\leq\max\{f(x),f(y)\}.

Because

(1-t)f(x)+tf(y)\leq\max\{f(x),f(y)\},

we have that any convex function is quasi-convex. Suppose that
\phi:f(U)\to\mathbb{R}
is increasing, and define
h=\phi\circ f
. If
f
is quasi-convex, then

h((1-t)x+ty)=\phi(f((1-t)x+ty))\leq\phi(\max\{f(x),f(y)\})=\max\{h(x),h(y)\},

which implies that
h
is also quasi-convex. However,
h
might be non-convex even if
f
is convex: consider
f(x)=x^{3}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\phi(x)=x^{1/4}
.

If there is a convex normal solution to (
1
) defined on some convex set
U
, then it is quasi-convex. If
U=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{++}
and
g(x)\gg 0
for any
x\in U
, then by Corollary
2
, we conclude that
any normal solution
to (
1
) defined on
U
is quasi-convex. The problem considered in this subsection is the converse of this problem. Suppose that any normal solution to (
1
) is quasi-convex. Is there a convex normal solution? The answer is negative. The following counterexample is obtained by Arrow and Enthoven
[
1
]
.

Choose
U=\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
. We consider the following function:

g_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})=1+\frac{x_{1}+1}{\sqrt{(x_{1}+1)^{2}+4x_{2}}},

g_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})=\frac{2}{\sqrt{(x_{1}+1)^{2}+4x_{2}}}.

A normal solution to (
1
) is as follows.

u(x)=(x_{1}-1)+\sqrt{(x_{1}+1)^{2}+4x_{2}}.

This function is
C^{1}
and strictly increasing. It can be easily checked that the range of
u
is
\mathbb{R}_{++}
. For any
C>0
, if
x_{1},x_{2}>0
and

(C-(x_{1}-1))^{2}=(x_{1}+1)^{2}+4x_{2},

Then

0<4x_{2}=(C-2x_{1})(C+2),

and thus,
C>2x_{1}>x_{1}-1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle u(x)=C\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle~C-(x_{1}-1)=\sqrt{(x_{1}+1)^{2}+4x_{2}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle~(C-(x_{1}-1))^{2}=(x_{1}+1)^{2}+4x_{2}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle~4x_{2}=C^{2}-2C(x_{1}-1)-4x_{1}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle~x_{2}=(-1-C/2)x_{1}+(C^{2}+2C)/4.

Hence, any level set of
u
is a straight line. Suppose that
u(x)\geq u(y)
and
u((1-t)x+ty)>u(x)
for some
t\in[0,1]
. Then,
0<t<1
. By the intermediate value theorem, there exists
t^{*}\in(t,1]
such that
u((1-t^{*})x+t^{*}y)=u(x)
. Because the level set
u^{-1}(u(x))
is a straight line, we have that
u((1-t)x+ty)=u(x)
, which is a contradiction. Therefore, we have that
u
is quasi-convex. By Corollary
2
, any normal solution to (
1
) is quasi-convex.

Next, define

\psi_{1}(C)=\frac{C}{2},\ \psi_{2}(C)=\frac{C^{2}+2C}{4}.

Then,

\displaystyle\frac{x_{1}}{\psi_{1}(C)}+\frac{x_{2}}{\psi_{2}(C)}=1\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle~\frac{2Cx_{1}+4x_{1}+4x_{2}}{C^{2}+2C}=1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle~2Cx_{1}+4x_{1}+4x_{2}=C^{2}+2C

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle~x_{2}=(-1-C/2)x_{1}+(C^{2}+2C)/4\Leftrightarrow u(x)=C.

Choose any
k>0
and define

h_{k}(C)=\sup\{x_{1}+kx_{2}|u(x)\leq C\},

h_{k}^{*}(C)=\sup\{-x_{1}-kx_{2}|u(x)\geq C\}.

Using the intermediate value theorem appropriately, we have that

\displaystyle h_{k}(C)=

\displaystyle~\sup\{x_{1}+kx_{2}|u(x)\leq C\}=\sup\{x_{1}+kx_{2}|u(x)=C\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sup\left\{x_{1}+kx_{2}\left|\frac{x_{1}}{\psi_{1}(C)}+\frac{x_{2}}{\psi_{2}(C)}=1\right.\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\max\{\psi_{1}(C),k\psi_{2}(C)\},

and

\displaystyle h_{k}^{*}(C)=

\displaystyle~\sup\{-x_{1}-kx_{2}|u(x)\geq C\}=\sup\{-x_{1}-kx_{2}|u(x)=C\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~-\inf\left\{x_{1}+kx_{2}\left|\frac{x_{1}}{\psi_{1}(C)}+\frac{x_{2}}{\psi_{2}(C)}=1\right.\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~-\min\{\psi_{1}(C),k\psi_{2}(C)\}.

Now, suppose that there exists a convex normal solution
w:U\to\mathbb{R}
to (
1
). By Corollary
2
, it is either strictly increasing or strictly decreasing, and there is a monotone function
\varphi:I\to\mathbb{R}
such that
I=w(U)
and
u=\varphi\circ w
. Because
w
is convex and the domain
U
is open, it is locally Lipschitz, and thus
I
is an interval.
14
14

14

Any convex function defined on an open and convex set is locally Lipschitz. See, for example, Rockafellar
[
16
]
.
Clearly,
\varphi(z)>0
for all
z\in I
.

We divide the proof into two cases.

Case 1
. Suppose that
\varphi
is increasing. Then,
w
is convex and strictly increasing. Choose
z_{0},z_{1}\in I
and
t\in[0,1]
, and define
z_{t}=(1-t)z_{0}+tz_{1}
. Choose any
\varepsilon>0
. Let
g_{k}=h_{k}\circ\varphi
. For
i\in\{0,1\}
, there exists
x^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
such that

g_{k}(z_{i})-\varepsilon<x^{i}_{1}+kx^{i}_{2},\ u(x^{i})\leq\varphi(z_{i}).

Hence,
w(x^{i})\leq z_{i}
, and thus

w((1-t)x^{0}+tx^{1})\leq(1-t)w(x^{0})+tw(x^{1})\leq z_{t},

which implies that
u((1-t)x^{0}+tx^{1})\leq\varphi(z_{t})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle g_{k}(z_{t})\geq

\displaystyle~[(1-t)x^{0}_{1}+tx^{1}_{1}]+k[(1-t)x^{0}_{2}+tx^{1}_{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~(1-t)(x^{0}_{1}+kx^{0}_{2})+t(x^{1}_{1}+kx^{1}_{2})

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle~(1-t)g_{k}(z_{0})+tg_{k}(z_{1})-\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary,

g_{k}(z_{t})\geq(1-t)g_{k}(z_{0})+tg_{k}(z_{1}).

Hence,
g_{k}
is concave. Letting
k\to 0
, we have that
\psi_{1}\circ\varphi
is concave. Because
\psi_{1}
is increasing and linear,
\varphi
itself is concave.

Therefore,
\varphi
is locally Lipschitz. By Rademacher’s theorem, there exists
z\in I
such that
\varphi
is differentiable at
z
. If
\varphi^{\prime}(z)=0
, then
z
is a maximum point of
\varphi
, which contradicts the monotonicity assumption of
\varphi
. Therefore,
\varphi^{\prime}(z)>0
. Define
k=\frac{2}{\varphi(z)+2}
. Then,
\psi_{1}(\varphi(z))=k\psi_{2}(\varphi(z))
. By a simple calculation,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dz^{\prime}}(\psi_{1}(\varphi(z^{\prime}))-k\psi_{2}(\varphi(z^{\prime})))\right|_{z^{\prime}=z}=

\displaystyle~\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{2}-k\psi_{2}^{\prime}(\varphi(z))\varphi^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\varphi^{\prime}(z)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\varphi(z)+1}{\varphi(z)+2}\right)<0.

Hence, there exists a neighborhood
I^{\prime}
of
z
such that if
z^{\prime}\in I^{\prime}
and
z^{\prime}>z
, then
g_{k}(z^{\prime})=k\psi_{2}(\varphi(z^{\prime}))
, and if
z^{\prime}\in I^{\prime}
and
z>z^{\prime}
, then
g_{k}(z^{\prime})=\psi_{1}(\varphi(z^{\prime}))
. Because
g_{k}
is concave, we have that

k(\psi_{2}\circ\varphi)^{\prime}(z)=D_{+}g_{k}(z)\leq D_{-}g_{k}(z)=\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{2},

and thus,

\varphi^{\prime}(z)<2k\psi_{2}^{\prime}(\varphi(z))\varphi^{\prime}(z)\leq\varphi^{\prime}(z),

which is a contradiction.

Case 2
. Suppose that
\varphi
is decreasing. Define
w^{*}=-w
and
\varphi^{*}(z)=\varphi(-z)
. Then,
w^{*}
is concave and strictly increasing,
\varphi^{*}
is increasing, and
u=\varphi^{*}\circ w^{*}
. Choose
z_{0},z_{1}\in-I
and
t\in[0,1]
, and define
z_{t}=(1-t)z_{0}+tz_{1}
. Choose any
\varepsilon>0
. Let
g_{k}^{*}=h_{k}^{*}\circ\varphi^{*}
. For
i\in\{0,1\}
, there exists
x^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
such that

g_{k}^{*}(z_{i})-\varepsilon<-x^{i}_{1}-kx^{i}_{2},\ u(x^{i})\geq\varphi^{*}(z_{i}).

Hence,
w^{*}(x^{i})\geq z_{i}
, and thus,

w^{*}((1-t)x^{0}+tx^{1})\geq(1-t)w^{*}(x^{0})+tw^{*}(x^{1})\geq z_{t},

which implies that
u((1-t)x^{0}+tx^{1})\geq\varphi^{*}(z_{t})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle g_{k}^{*}(z_{t})\geq

\displaystyle~-[(1-t)x^{0}_{1}+tx^{1}_{1}]-k[(1-t)x^{0}_{2}+tx^{1}_{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~(1-t)(-x^{0}_{1}-kx^{0}_{2})+t(-x^{1}_{1}-kx^{1}_{2})

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle~(1-t)g_{k}^{*}(z_{0})+tg_{k}^{*}(z_{1})-\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary,

g_{k}^{*}(z_{t})\geq(1-t)g_{k}^{*}(z_{0})+tg_{k}^{*}(z_{1}).

This implies that
g_{k}^{*}
is concave. Letting
k\to\infty
, we have that
-\psi_{1}\circ\varphi^{*}
is concave. Because
\psi_{1}
is increasing and linear, we have that
\varphi^{*}
is convex, and hence
\varphi
is also convex.

Hence,
\varphi
is locally Lipschitz. By Rademacher’s theorem, there exists
z\in I
such that
\varphi
is differentiable at
z
. If
\varphi^{\prime}(z)=0
, then
z
is a minimum point of
\varphi
, which contradicts the monotonicity assumption of
\varphi
. Therefore,
\varphi^{\prime}(z)<0
. Define
k=\frac{2}{\varphi(z)+2}
. Then,
\psi_{1}(\varphi(z))=k\psi_{2}(\varphi(z))
. By a simple calculation,

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dz^{\prime}}(\psi_{1}(\varphi(z^{\prime}))-k\psi_{2}(\varphi(z^{\prime})))\right|_{z^{\prime}=z}=

\displaystyle~\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{2}-k\psi_{2}^{\prime}(\varphi(z))\varphi^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\varphi^{\prime}(z)\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\varphi(z)+1}{\varphi(z)+2}\right)>0.

Hence, there exists a neighborhood
I^{\prime}
of
z
such that if
z^{\prime}\in I^{\prime}
and
z^{\prime}>z
, then
g_{k}^{*}(z^{\prime})=-k\psi_{2}(\varphi(z^{\prime}))
, and if
z^{\prime}\in I^{\prime}
and
z>z^{\prime}
, then
g_{k}^{*}(z^{\prime})=-\psi_{1}(\varphi(z^{\prime}))
. Because
g_{k}^{*}
is concave, we have that

-k(\psi_{2}\circ\varphi)^{\prime}(z)=D_{+}g_{k}^{*}(z)\leq D_{-}g_{k}^{*}(z)=-\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(z)}{2},

and thus,

\varphi^{\prime}(z)>2k\psi_{2}^{\prime}(\varphi(z))\varphi^{\prime}(z)\geq\varphi^{\prime}(z),

which is a contradiction.

Hence, in each case, we lead a contradiction. This implies that such a convex function
w
does not exist.

In this proof, the idea of using an artificial function
h_{k}
is obtained by Fenchel
[
5
]
. For more detailed arguments, see Kannai
[
11
]
or Monteiro
[
14
]
.

A.3
Katzner’s Counterexample

In this subsection, we present an example of
g
in which (III) of Proposition
3
holds but (II) is violated. This example is found by Katzner
[
12
]
.

Consider
U=\mathbb{R}^{2}_{++}
, and

g_{1}(x)=-3x_{1}^{2}x_{2}-x_{2}^{3},

g_{2}(x)=-x_{1}^{3}-3x_{1}x_{2}^{2}.

A regular solution to (
1
) is as follows.

u(x)=-(x_{1}^{3}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{2}^{3}).

It is easy to show that
u
is strictly quasi-convex. By simple calculation,

\frac{\partial g_{1}}{\partial x_{1}}(x)=-6x_{1}x_{2},\ \frac{\partial g_{1}}{\partial x_{2}}(x)=-3x_{1}^{2}-3x_{2}^{2},

\frac{\partial g_{2}}{\partial x_{1}}(x)=-3x_{1}^{2}-3x_{2}^{2},\ \frac{\partial g_{2}}{\partial x_{2}}(x)=-6x_{1}x_{2}.

Choose
v=(-1,1)
and
x=(1,1)
. Then
g(x)=(-4,-4)
, and thus
g(x)\cdot v=0
. However,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}[v\cdot g(x+tv)]=

\displaystyle~\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}[v\cdot(g(x+tv)-g(x))]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}(x)v_{i}v_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~0,

and thus
g
violates (II) of Proposition
3
.
