From simulation to stimulation: a predictive synergy-based control strategy for functional electrical stimulation cycling in a person with spinal cord injury
Authors: Alexandre B. Peres, Gabriel G. Carmona, Ana Carolina C. de Sousa, Roberto S Baptista
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFunctional electrical stimulation (FES) cycling stimulation strategies are often derived from heuristics, or able-bodied kinematics and electromyography, which may be unsafe and non-representative for people with complete spinal cord injury. Optimally controlled predictive musculoskeletal simulations with synergy implementation offer a way to generate individualized, physiologically plausible muscle trajectories without prior experimental data. Building on a previous proof of concept, this study developed and experimentally validated a simulation-derived stimulation strategy for FES cycling.MethodsA two-stage musculoskeletal predictive simulation framework was implemented using OpenSim Moco. First, a speed-tracking optimal control problem optimized the model’s kinematics and individual muscle trajectories. Second, muscle excitations were decomposed into functional synergies using non-negative matrix factorization, producing coordinated activation patterns for quadriceps and hamstrings muscle groups. Subject-specific constraints were incorporated into the model. The resulting activation profile was then processed and converted to a crank-angle lookup table for real-time control. Experimental validation was conducted in one subject with complete spinal cord injury (SCI), comparing the simulation-derived control signal (CS) with a refined empirical bang-bang (BB) protocol used in the Cybathlon 2024 edition.ResultsSimulations generated stable synergy-based muscle trajectories aligned with expected cycling dynamics. Experimentally, performance varied with trial order, with both CS and BB enabling unassisted FES cycling. BB produced higher average power and cadence, while CS delivered more symmetrical left-right balance and showed partial recovery in performance in higher stimulation intensities.DiscussionAlthough BB outperformed CS in power and cadence, the CS approach proved to be feasible and has methodological advantages. The consistent and physiologically structured behavior of the CS signal demonstrates that predictive simulation can provide a safe, reproducible baseline for individualized FES cycling control, particularly for new users or research groups.ConclusionThis was the first implementation in cycling of optimal control-driven FES combined with muscle synergies, generating feasible, physiologically grounded stimulation profiles for FES cycling in an individual with complete SCI. While not surpassing a highly refined empirical strategy, the simulation-derived signal performed reliably and represents a promising foundation for model-based personalization of FES control.Trial Registration: Universidade de Brasília Ethics Committee, 75441323.4.0000.8093.

Background

Electrical stimulation has long been recognized as a powerful rehabilitation tool capable of eliciting muscle contractions through externally applied currents [1]. When these contractions are coordinated to produce purposeful movements such as walking or cycling, the technique is known as functional electrical stimulation (FES). FES is widely used in the rehabilitation of individuals with spinal cord injury (SCI), offering well-documented benefits including improvements in muscle mass, cardiovascular fitness, and overall quality of life [1–5]. Long-term training programs have been shown to increase muscle cross-sectional area, enhance endurance, and even improve neurological outcomes [3, 5]. Remarkably, among the currently available assistive technologies, FES (in its many forms) remains the only technique capable of generating active muscle contraction in otherwise paralyzed limbs [6, 7].

Among its applications, FES-assisted cycling stands out as one of the most effective and accessible modalities in both clinical settings [8] and international competitions, such as the Cybathlon [6, 7, 9], providing a safe means to promote cardiovascular and neuromuscular conditioning while enabling repeated, symmetrical lower-limb exercise [2, 4].

In this context, FES inherently produces muscle recruitment patterns that differ from those observed during physiological voluntary contractions. Surface electrical stimulation activates multiple motor units in a non-selective manner. Another issue that arises with surface FES is limited force gradation and increased susceptibility to fatigue [10]. Because of these characteristics, stimulation strategies in FES cycling are often simplified. In FES cycling, the most commonly adopted stimulation strategy is known as bang-bang (BB), in which large muscle groups are fully activated or completely deactivated at predefined intervals. While simple and robust, this binary stimulation paradigm further departs from physiological muscle recruitment by enforcing abrupt, all-or-none contractions rather than graded activation profiles, thereby exacerbating fatigue and reducing mechanical efficiency [11, 12].

To overcome these limitations, previous works have optimized the BB stimulation signal timing and parameters using a variety of strategies, including heuristic (trial-and-error) procedures [13–15], experimentally measured torque feedback [16], or signals derived from surface electromyography (sEMG) recordings of able-bodied individuals [17–19]. The mentioned approaches can improve performance, however, heuristic procedures can be risky due to its blind-tuning nature in a participant with restricted sensory feedback, while the use of sEMG data depends on volitional muscle contraction, which are not feasible for individuals with complete SCI.

Beyond heuristic and feedforward optimization approaches, several feedback-based controllers have also been proposed for FES cycling, including proportional integral (PI) [20, 21], fuzzy logic (FL) [22], sliding-mode [23], and adaptive strategies [24]. These approaches typically regulate cadence or power by modulating the BB stimulation parameters and can compensate for non-linearities and uncertainties in the neuromusculoskeletal system. However, few studies implemented the results experimentally, and none of these implementations deviated from some form of the BB stimulation.

Musculoskeletal (MSK) modeling and simulation have emerged as powerful tools to bridge the previous strategies and physiologically grounded stimulation paradigms [25–27]. By combining optimal control theory with direct collocation methods, these simulations can estimate muscle forces and activations responsible for complex movements [28, 29], and have been successfully implemented in other applications such as walking [30]. In addition, controllers such as the previously mentioned PI or FL controllers can still be implemented in a simulation environment, as in [31]. However, most existing cycling studies have relied on experimental kinematic datasets [29, 32], and EMG data from able-bodied subjects to inform their models and subsequent simulations [33–35], limiting their application to populations with SCI.

Fully predictive simulations generate physiologically plausible activation patterns directly from the model’s dynamics and cost function definitions, without requiring experimentally measured muscle activations or kinematic recordings to guide the optimization [36]. While the framework is generic, subject-specific constraints can be incorporated when available to improve experimental feasibility. In [37], predictive simulations optimized the timings for muscle activation and deactivation. This BB stimulation was then validated in [38] with 11 people with complete SCI, achieving comparable results to other BB signals. In another simulation study [39], despite not having experimental validation, a FES cycling stimulation with four different muscle activation intensities throughout the cycle was presented, approximating it to a graded physiological muscle recruitment curve.

While predictive musculoskeletal simulations can generate detailed muscle-level activation patterns, translating these patterns to surface FES presents a fundamental challenge. Surface stimulation inherently lacks selectivity, activating multiple muscles simultaneously through paired electrodes rather than independent actuators. As a result, unconstrained optimal control solutions may produce activation patterns that are not directly implementable in our current setup. This motivates the need for a representation that both preserves physiological coordination and respects the practical limitations of surface FES.

Muscle synergies, or motor modules, are functional units of grouped muscles that work together to complete a task-specific movement [40], reducing the burden on the central nervous system (CNS) and individual muscles [41]. When this concept is applied to neuroprosthesis, especially in surface electrode FES, it provides a structured way to constrain control solutions to coordinated activation patterns that matches our physical system [42, 43]. Muscle synergies, combined with optimally controlled FES, have not yet been widely explored [44], with only one application to walking [30], and one to exoskeleton-assisted walking [45]. Prior to this study, it had not yet been implemented in a cycling setting.

Therefore, a gap remains between existing control strategies and experimentally validated stimulation paradigms that are physiologically grounded and suitable for individuals with complete SCI. Most current approaches rely on heuristic tuning and binary BB stimulation structures, which poorly reflect the graded coordination of muscles observed in physiological movement. At the same time, predictive musculoskeletal simulations can generate physiologically plausible activation patterns, but these solutions are rarely translated into experimentally implementable stimulation paradigms that respect the practical constraints of surface FES.

Recently, we introduced the first proof of concept demonstrating that predictive simulations could generate stimulation profiles capable of driving FES cycling in an individual with complete SCI [46]. However, that initial framework utilized a simplified configuration with only quadriceps activation, and did not explicitly account for the unselective FES-induced muscle contraction, system delays, or subject-specific neuromuscular limitations to high-intensity stimulation.

The present study extends the framework seen in [46]. We expanded the configuration to include the hamstrings muscle group, and addressed the introduced gap by investigating the use of predictive musculoskeletal simulations, combined with functional muscle synergies, to generate and implement feasible stimulation profiles for FES cycling while presenting an alternative to BB stimulation. Specifically, we aimed to account for the synchronous muscle contraction, system delays, and subject-specific neuromuscular properties to generate a stimulation signal that (1) more accurately reflects the muscle activation induced by surface FES, and (2) is implementable, allowing for comparison to an empirically refined BB signal in a participant with complete SCI.

Methods

The FES cycling system employed in this work is based on customized and commercial electronic equipment and a tricycle in a tadpole configuration (HP3 Trikes, Brazil). It has been the same since our team’s first Cybathlon participation in 2016 [15, 47], with upgrades to the seat and feet attachments.

The experimental setup consisted of the trike adjusted for stationary cycling with a smart trainer (Wahoo Kickr V6), and dual-sided instrumented pedals (Wahoo Powrlink Zero), as the standard setup for the Cybathlon 2024 FES Bike Race. We used the open-source software GoldenCheetah to connect the smart trainer and the instrumented pedals to measure cadence, power, and left-right balance during cycling.

Currently, the control and logging use the robotic operating system 2 (ROS2) [48] as a modular framework running in a Raspberry Pi 4 with Ubuntu 22.04. Custom ROS2 nodes were developed in Python to operate the stimulation device (Hasomed P24 Science) at the same rate as the optical encoder. A 360-degree optical encoder (E38S6G5-600B-G24N, SHARVi Electronics) interfaced with ROS2 tracked crank angle variations at a sample rate of 100 Hz. A human–machine interface (with two push-buttons and a 16x2-character LCD screen display) and batteries complete the system.

Electrical stimulation was applied using rectangular self-adhesive gel electrodes (ValuTrode, Axelgaard, USA). Two electrode pairs were positioned in each leg, one for the quadriceps (7.5cm× 13.0cm) and one for the hamstring muscle group (5.0cm× 10.0cm) (Fig. 2). Pulses were rectangular and biphasic, modulated by three main parameters: current, frequency, and pulse width. The current amplitude was ∈ [0mA,100mA] for the quadriceps muscle group and ∈ [0mA,20mA] for the hamstrings muscle group, the frequency of the signal was constant at 50 Hz, while the pulse width was automatically controlled during data collection.

The bang-bang (BB) stimulation protocol used in this study corresponds to the baseline control strategy adopted and continuously refined by our research group [15, 47] to heuristically maximize power output during the training sessions in preparation to the Cybathlon 2024 competition. Its design is the result of extensive heuristic tuning based on experimental observations, iterative adjustments, and long-term subject familiarization. As such, the BB protocol represents a well-established and practically effective reference strategy for this specific experimental setup, rather than a globally optimal solution.

Based on the long-term subject familiarization, the BB stimulation was intentionally asymmetric to improve the left-right balance. Not only the inter-leg asymmetric stimulation, but also the different stimulation intensities for different muscle groups within the same leg, are common practices in FES cycling [49], although this is rarely reported. Both adaptations can be motivated by previous subject specific neuromuscular information. Therefore, the presented methods can be complemented by prior subject neuromuscular data to improve results.

The methods include in silico steps such as the development of the musculoskeletal model, the formulation of the optimal control problems, and the post-processing of the simulated data. Model creation

The base model used was the Gait10dof18musc1, implemented in OpenSim [50], an open-source tool for modeling MSK structures, simulating, and neuromusculoskeletal analysis. This model comprises of hamstrings, biceps femoris short head, gluteus maximus, iliopsoas, rectus femoris, vasti (representing the 3 vasti in the quadriceps), gastrocnemius, soleus, and tibialis anterior in each leg, totaling 18 muscle-tendon units and 10 degrees of freedom (Fig. 1a).

Modifications to actuators and joints

Similar to our previous cycling adaptations [46, 51, 52], the model was constrained to the sagittal plane, and the ankle joints were locked to replicate the use of orthopedic boots, which are employed to ensure pilot safety during FES cycling. The muscle representation was updated from the Millard implementation [53] of the Hill-type model [54] to the De Groote-Fregly muscle model [55], improving numerical stability and realism of the activation-contraction dynamics. As in [52], a resistive crank and pedal mechanism was included to replicate the geometry and loading conditions of the experimental cycling system (Fig. 1a).

To improve convergence of the optimal control problems, the maximum isometric force of all muscles was uniformly doubled, and reserve actuators were included at the hip and knee joints, following common practice in musculoskeletal optimal control studies [32]. These modifications were introduced as numerical strategies rather than as physiological assumptions, and were not intended to represent altered muscle strength in individuals with spinal cord injury. The implications of these modeling choices were mitigated during post-processing of the simulated data prior to experimental implementation.

To simulate the FES cycling configuration, only the quadriceps (rectus femoris and vasti) and hamstrings (hamstrings and biceps femoris short head) muscles actively contributed to the motion. All other muscles were restricted to a maximum activation of 2% while preserving their passive properties. This small activation was considered negligible and was kept to improve the simulations numerical convergence.

In addition, the maximum activation of the hamstring muscles was limited to 20%. This constraint reflects the participant’s individual response to FES, as stimulation intensities above this level consistently induced involuntary spasms (Fig. 1a). By limiting the hamstrings’ activation at a modeling level, we improved the compatibility of the simulations’ results to the experimental setup, which stimulates the hamstrings at 20% of the data collection protocol’s maximum intensity. This limit was applied at the activation level within the musculoskeletal model and was not derived from a direct physiological force measurement or from the maximum output of the stimulator.

This approach explicitly constrains muscle availability and activation limits to reflect the available experimental surface-stimulation FES cycling setup and subject-specific neuromuscular characteristics.

Optimal control problem

Optimal control (OC) problems aim to identify time-varying control trajectories that minimize a specified cost function while satisfying a set of dynamical and physiological constraints. In the context of human movement, these cost functions and constraints are commonly chosen to reflect biomechanical objectives such as minimizing muscle effort or fatigue, while ensuring feasible joint kinematics and muscle activations [30].

OpenSim Moco [56] was used to formulate and solve the OC problems in this study. Moco extends the OpenSim modeling framework by enabling the solution of musculoskeletal OC problems using direct collocation. In this approach, the system’s states (e.g., joint positions and velocities) and control variables (e.g., muscle excitations) are discretized over time, and the system dynamics are enforced as algebraic constraints, resulting in a finite-dimensional nonlinear programming (NLP) problem [28].

The resulting NLP is assembled using the CasADi framework [57], which provides algorithmic differentiation for efficient gradient evaluation, and is solved using the gradient-based optimizer IPOPT [58]. Upon convergence, Moco reconstructs the continuous trajectories of states and controls, yielding dynamically consistent simulations of muscle-driven cycling motion suitable for subsequent analysis and experimental translation.

Speed-tracking simulation

The first OC problem consisted of a muscle-driven speed-tracking simulation used to generate dynamically consistent reference kinematics and muscle excitation patterns. This formulation was based on prior work [52], which investigated different objective formulations for cycling simulations. In the present study, a speed-tracking approach was adopted, in which the model completed one full crank revolution, starting with the left crank at top dead center, targeting a constant reference cadence of 40 rpm, against a constant resistive crank torque of 8 Nm (Fig. 1b).

Although cycling kinematics are periodic and symmetric, muscle recruitment strategies that produce a given movement are not unique due to muscular redundancy [55]. Simulating a complete crank cycle allowed the optimizer to explore more feasible coordination strategies compared to enforcing contralateral symmetry over a half-cycle, while avoiding the substantial increase in computational cost associated with simulating multiple consecutive cycles.

Muscle grouping

Prior to defining the synergy-based OC problem, the muscles of the model were manually grouped into six muscle groups per leg. The number of groups and the assignment of muscles to each group were determined based on the anatomical layout of the model and the set of muscles that can be synchronously activated using a single superficial electrode pair (Fig. 2). Only the quadriceps and hamstrings muscle groups actively contributed to the motion, with the other muscle groups’ contributions being considered negligible. Synergy simulation

To better represent the constraints imposed by surface functional electrical stimulation (FES), a synergy-based OC formulation was implemented. Under surface stimulation, multiple muscles are activated simultaneously and cannot be controlled independently. Because surface electrodes stimulate muscle groups rather than individual muscles, the stimulation signal should be interpreted as an approximation of the aggregate activation of the posterior thigh muscles rather than selective activation of each anatomical component. Consequently, muscles were not treated as independent actuators, but instead followed coordinated activation patterns defined by muscle synergies.

Although only two stimulation channels per leg were available experimentally, the musculoskeletal model contains multiple muscles within each stimulated group. The speed-tracking optimal control solution therefore produces distinct excitation patterns for muscles that are stimulated simultaneously in practice. The synergy decomposition was introduced to collapse these muscle-level excitations into experimentally realizable stimulation commands.

The primary motivation for using muscle synergies was to ensure that the optimized control signals were both physiologically plausible and experimentally implementable. By prescribing synergies aligned with our current FES Cycling setup, the optimization problem inherently accounted for the synchronous activation of multiple muscles during FES.

In contrast, the synergy-based simulation used 150 mesh intervals and higher convergence and constraint tolerances of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^{-1}$$\end{document}, and converged in approximately 31 s. This substantial difference in computation time reflects differences in problem formulation and numerical settings, including the use of prescribed reference kinematics and a less demanding optimization structure in the synergy-based simulation, rather than solely a reduction in the dimensionality of the control space.

All simulations were executed on a workstation equipped with an AMD Ryzen 9 5950X processor (16 cores, 32 logical processors, 4.9 GHz), running Microsoft Windows 11 Pro, with 64 GB of RAM.

Simulation-derived control signal processing

From this point onward, post-processing was performed on muscle activation trajectories rather than excitation signals. In OpenSim, muscle activation is a state variable that represents the muscle response resulting from the excitation control after activation dynamics, including temporal delays and low-pass behavior. Following the synergy-based optimization, activations were extracted at the muscle-group level, since all muscles within a group received identical excitation signals and therefore exhibited identical activation profiles. Working with group-level activations provided a physiologically meaningful and smooth representation of muscle recruitment, making them more suitable for deriving stimulation envelopes intended for experimental implementation than the corresponding excitation signals.

The activation profiles obtained for the left and right legs were not identical (Fig. 3). This occurs because the optimal control problem admits multiple feasible coordination strategies due to muscle redundancy, allowing the solver to distribute effort differently between limbs while still satisfying the kinematic objective.

For experimental implementation, the activation signals from the left and right legs were averaged to produce a single stimulation pattern. This step was adopted as a practical simplification to implement a symmetric stimulation protocol using the available hardware. While averaging may reduce some limb-specific coordination strategies identified by the optimizer, it allows a single stimulation strategy to be applied consistently during experimental trials. This operation did not introduce qualitatively different strategies but instead reduced asymmetries arising from muscle redundancy and numerical optimization.

The averaged activations were then filtered using a fifth-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 0.6 Hz and a sampling rate of 100 Hz, yielding smooth activations suitable for stimulation delivery (Fig. 3). The activation profiles were sampled at 100 Hz to match the acquisition frequency of the crank-angle encoder used to update the control signal.

Finally, to ensure effective use of the available stimulation range, the filtered activation profiles were normalized to span the interval [0, 1]. This normalization preserves the temporal structure and relative amplitude of the activation patterns while decoupling them from absolute force magnitudes. The latter is dependent on the optimization problem’s modeling assumptions that are not intended to represent subject-specific muscle strength, such as the doubling of maximum isometric muscle forces, the use of reserve actuators, and the absence of reduced recruitment efficiency associated with surface electrical stimulation in the OpenSim framework. Consequently, the normalized activations should be interpreted as dimensionless control envelopes that define the timing and relative modulation of stimulation rather than direct estimates of physiological muscle activation or force.

In the experimental implementation, stimulation intensity limits were enforced independently for each muscle group to reflect subject-specific stimulation constraints. Signal implementation

Muscle activation trajectories obtained from the synergy-based simulations were used to define the temporal structure of the electrical stimulation applied during cycling. After post-processing and normalization, the activation signals were treated as dimensionless control profiles representing the relative activation demand as a function of crank angle over the pedaling cycle, independent of absolute stimulation intensity.

The normalized activation profiles were discretized as a function of crank angle using 360 points per cycle, matching the angular resolution of the crank encoder. This resolution was sufficient to capture the temporal structure of the activation profile while avoiding unnecessary computational overhead. The discretized profiles were stored in a lookup table, with each entry corresponding to a normalized activation value associated with a specific crank angle.

The normalized activation signal was subsequently mapped to the stimulation parameters of the hardware (pulse width and current limits) based on previously established safe stimulation bounds.

A custom ROS2 node was developed to perform real-time stimulation control based on this lookup table (Fig. 1d). During cycling, the instantaneous crank angle was measured at 100 Hz and used to index the lookup table, yielding the corresponding normalized activation value and resulting current command. All stimulation parameters were constrained by software-imposed limits to ensure safe operation. To ensure a direct and fair comparison between the stimulation strategies, the same hardware and data acquisition procedures were used in both cases.

Experience in regular training sessions with the participant indicated a consistent left-right imbalanced contribution during cycling. To improve smoothness of the cycling movement, our physical setup during training sessions in preparation to Cyabthlon 2024 was set to deliver a 30% higher stimulation command to the left leg than the right leg. This compensation was kept in the experimental setup for this work. This asymmetry was implemented by scaling the commanded pulse width for the left leg by a factor of 1.3, subject to an upper limit of 480 μ s. For the right leg, the maximum pulse width was limited to 450 μ s. Once both limits were reached, the relative stimulation difference between legs remained constant. This asymmetric stimulation strategy was applied only on the BB trials (Figs. 6 and 7).

Phase shift compensation

To account for variations in crank cadence during experimental cycling, both the CS and the BB stimulation profiles were subjected to a crank-speed-dependent phase adjustment. This implementation ensured that the timing of stimulation relative to crank position remained consistent despite variations in cycling speed.

Specifically, stimulation angles were dynamically adjusted as a function of the instantaneous crank angular velocity, introducing a phase advance at higher cadences to compensate for neuromuscular activation and electromechanical delays. This phase adjustment was applied uniformly across both stimulation strategies to ensure a fair comparison between CS and BB.

Data collection protocol

Data were collected over two weeks on four non-consecutive days, following a standardized preparation and testing sequence designed to ensure participant safety and consistency across sessions. Each session began with a supervised stretching routine supervised by a physiotherapist while the participant lay on a stretcher. Stretching targeted the quadriceps, hamstrings, gluteal muscles, ankles, and lumbar region to reduce spasticity and improve comfort. This was followed by a 5-min passive warm-up, during which the participant was positioned on the recumbent trike and an investigator manually assisted the pedaling motion at approximately 40 rpm.

After the passive warm-up, surface electrodes were placed bilaterally over the quadriceps and hamstring muscle groups (Fig. 2). An active warm-up phase then followed using the bang-bang (BB) protocol, during which assisted cycling continued while pulse width intensity was gradually increased by the participant, starting from 0 μ s for all stimulation channels, until sustained unassisted pedaling was achieved. The active warm-up phase completed after 12 min of unassisted cycling and was followed by a 5-min rest period prior to data collection.

On each data collection day, both the simulation-derived Control Signal (CS) and the bang-bang (BB) stimulation protocols were tested in alternating order to minimize order effects. The protocol order was counterbalanced across sessions: day 1 (BB then CS), day 2 (CS then BB), day 3 (BB then CS), and day 4 (CS then BB). Two trials were therefore performed per session, one for each stimulation strategy (Table 1).

Each trial lasted 2 min. During the first 30 s, cycling was assisted by an investigator, following the Cybathlon 2024 race start procedure, and maintained between 55 and 60 rpm to facilitate the transition to unassisted cycling. Pulse width was progressively increased during each trial, with an increment of 25 μ s applied every 10 s, following protocol-specific initial values and saturation limits.

The two stimulation strategies differed in the temporal structure of the stimulation current and in the pulse-width modulation applied across limbs. In the CS protocol, pulse width modulation was applied symmetrically between legs, starting at 225 μ s and increasing up to a maximum of 450 μ s over the course of the trial. The stimulation current varied continuously as a function of crank angle, ranging from 0 to 100 mA for the quadriceps and from 0 to 20 mA for the hamstrings, following the normalized activation profiles described previously.

In the BB protocol, the stimulation current alternated between zero and the corresponding maximum value for each muscle group according to predefined crank-angle activation windows with linear onset and offset ramps. Pulse width modulation followed the same incremental rule but was intentionally asymmetric between legs: for the right leg, pulse width started at 225 μ s and increased up to 450 μ s, while for the left leg the pulse width command was scaled by a factor of 1.3, saturating at 480 μ s and remaining constant thereafter.

All trials were performed under constant mechanical resistance on the cycling system to match the simulated conditions. Cycling cadence, power output, and left-right balance were continuously recorded for subsequent analysis.

Experimental results

Cycling performance: power and cadence

Both stimulation strategies enabled sustained FES cycling across all sessions. However, the bang-bang (BB) protocol consistently produced higher power output and cadence during the unassisted phase compared to the simulation-derived control signal (CS) (Fig. 7). Following the initial 30-second assisted phase, CS trials exhibited an immediate reduction in power and cadence, with partial recovery as pulse width increased throughout the trial. In contrast, BB maintained higher and more stable performance over time.

Inter-limb balance

Despite differences in power and cadence, both stimulation strategies produced near-symmetrical pedaling. The CS protocol resulted in a left-right balance closer to 50% across sessions, whereas BB exhibited a consistent bias toward the right-leg (left-right balance < 50%) (Table 1).

Discussion

The proposed control strategy incorporated several subject-specific constraints to ensure experimental feasibility, including limits on hamstrings activation to prevent spasms, stimulation parameter calibration based on participant tolerance, and electrode placement corresponding to the available stimulation channels. These adjustments illustrate how the proposed simulation framework can serve as a general control design pipeline while still allowing the incorporation of subject-specific constraints when necessary for experimental feasibility.

Speed-tracking optimal control

The speed-tracking optimal control problem successfully generated dynamically consistent cycling muscle activations. As shown in Fig. 4, the crank cadence closely followed the reference value of 40 rpm throughout the simulated revolution, with repeatable oscillations occurring across the crank cycle.

The resulting muscle activation patterns support the physiological plausibility of the speed-tracking solution. The rectus femoris and vasti muscles exhibited cyclic activation profiles aligned with knee extension, while the hamstrings and biceps femoris short head activations remained constrained in their imposed limit. This behavior reflects the subject-specific activation constraint introduced to ensure experimental feasibility. Under these constraints, the optimizer exploited the admissible activation capacity almost continuously, rather than generating strongly phase-modulated profiles.

Meanwhile, the cadence oscillations are not indicative of numerical instability or poor tracking performance, but rather reflect the inherent biomechanics of cyclic pedaling. Peak deviations coincided with phases of maximal torque production, where rectus femoris and vasti activations were highest, while reduced fluctuations occurred near mechanically disadvantageous crank positions, commonly referred to as dead zones. This behavior is consistent with experimental observations of able-bodied cycling, in which power production is inherently phase-dependent rather than uniformly distributed across the crank cycle [61].

In this context, the cadence oscillations indicate that the optimizer adopted a strategy of concentrating force production in mechanically favorable regions to compensate for reduced effectiveness in the dead zones. An increased contribution from the hamstrings and biceps femoris short head could be expected to redistribute torque more evenly across the cycle, potentially reducing these oscillations through improved inter-phase coordination.

Synergy-based optimal control

The synergy-based optimal control problem successfully reproduced the cycling kinematics obtained in the speed-tracking simulation while operating under a constrained muscle control dimensionality and minimizing overall muscle effort. As shown in Fig. 5, the crank cadence closely followed the reference trajectory, preserving the oscillatory pattern observed previously. Because the reference kinematics originated from a fully muscle-driven formulation, they inherently respected muscle activation dynamics and force-length-velocity relationships, ensuring physiological feasibility of the synergy-based solution. Minor deviations in cadence relative to the speed-tracking simulation are due to the higher numerical tolerances adopted in this optimization, which were selected to improve computational tractability without altering the qualitative behavior of the solution.

The most notable differences between the speed-tracking and synergy-based solutions emerge in the muscle activation profiles (Fig. 5). In contrast to the smooth, continuously modulated activations observed in the speed-tracking simulation (Fig. 4), the synergy-based optimization converged toward sparse activation patterns characterized by brief bursts of activity sufficient to satisfy the kinematic constraints. This behavior is a direct consequence of the muscle excitation effort minimization objective, which favors temporally compact muscle contractions, reducing overall demand while preserving task performance.

Importantly, the imposed synergy structure enforced synchronized activation within muscle groups. Rectus femoris and vasti muscles followed an identical quadriceps activation profile, while the hamstrings and biceps femoris short head followed a common hamstrings profile. This grouping prevented the optimizer from selectively overloading individual muscles that could improve performance but would be inconsistent with experimentally feasible surface FES stimulation. The resulting activation patterns therefore reflect not only the task demands, but also the constraints imposed by the available experimental setup.

In the hamstrings activation profile (Fig. 6b), an apparent abrupt cutoff is observed near the 0\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ $$\end{document} and 180\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^\circ $$\end{document} crank positions, possibly affecting the experimental results. This does not indicate a discontinuity in the underlying optimized solution, and it is likely caused by the extracting and post-processing of a single steady-state cycle of a periodic movement. Averaging across multiple cycles would likely mitigate this effect without altering the qualitative structure of the activation pattern.

The synergy-based solution also exhibits features commonly observed in able-bodied cycling [61], including muscle pre-activation and periods of antagonist co-contraction. These features were not imposed explicitly through cost terms or symmetry constraints, but emerged from the interaction between musculoskeletal dynamics, task objectives, and coordination constraints. Their presence suggests that such coordination patterns are mechanically advantageous for regulating crank motion and effort-effective for force transmission across the cycle.

Interpretation of experimental results

Across all trials, both stimulation strategies enabled sustained FES cycling, confirming the experimental feasibility of the simulation-derived approach. Nevertheless, BB consistently achieved higher average power output and crank cadence, whereas CS generally produced a left-right balance closer to symmetry (Fig. 7, Table 1).

Session-level results reveal a systematic influence of protocol order. On days when BB was performed first, BB exhibited markedly higher power and cadence than CS. Conversely, when CS was tested first, its performance more closely approached that of BB within the same session, consistent with short-term carryover effects between trials (e.g., fatigue or priming). Despite this, the relative performance trends between stimulation strategies remained consistent: BB favored higher mechanical output and cadence, whereas CS favored improved inter-limb balance (Table 1).

The temporal evolution of cycling metrics provides additional insight into these differences. Following the transition from assisted to unassisted cycling at the 30-second mark, BB maintained more stable power output and cadence throughout the trial. In contrast, CS exhibited an immediate reduction in both variables, followed by gradual recovery as pulse width increased automatically over time (Fig. 7). This behavior reflects the greater robustness of BB to low initial stimulation intensities, while also illustrating the capacity of CS to improve performance as stimulation amplitude increases.

Notably, left-right balance remained comparatively stable across time for both protocols, with CS consistently closer to 50%, despite the empirical adjustment of asymmetric stimulation in BB. Balance did not exhibit the same transient drop observed in power and cadence after the assisted-to-unassisted transition (Fig. 7), suggesting that balance may be less sensitive to this transition than the mechanical output metrics. The improved balance observed during CS trials is therefore unlikely to reflect unbalanced limb strength alone, and may instead arise from differences in activation timing, coordination, and muscle responsiveness between the two control strategies.

Two primary factors likely contributed to the superior mechanical performance observed under BB. The BB signal maintains prolonged periods of near-maximal stimulation within predefined crank-angle windows, further intensified in the left leg due to the asymmetrical stimulation, both inherently favoring higher torque generation. In contrast, CS exhibits smoother, graded activation profiles that spend less time at maximal stimulation levels (Fig. 6), limiting instantaneous force output, especially at lower pulse widths (Fig. 7).

Although the empirically tuned BB protocol outperformed CS in terms of power and cadence, this result does not diminish the contribution of the proposed approach. CS was generated through a fully predictive pipeline, starting from musculoskeletal optimal control and proceeding through a structured post-processing stage to obtain experimentally implementable stimulation profiles. Beyond delay compensation and experimentally motivated activation limits, CS did not rely on manual tuning of crank-angle windows, asymmetry heuristics, or iterative shaping of stimulation behavior.

The proposed framework’s contribution therefore lies not in immediately surpassing an optimized BB protocol, but in demonstrating a reproducible and objective workflow capable of producing a stimulation strategy without exposing the participant to trial-and-error tuning or unsafe parameter exploration. This is particularly relevant for new FES cycling users, emerging research groups, or clinical contexts where extensive heuristic refinement is impractical [25].

Methodological considerations and limitations

The proposed simulation-to-stimulation framework represents a balance between physiological realism, computational tractability, and experimental feasibility. While the methodological choices adopted in this study enabled the generation and validation of a fully predictive stimulation strategy, several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the results.

Modeling considerations

At the modeling level, simplifications were introduced to reflect the experimental configuration and ensure numerical tractability. The musculoskeletal model was constrained to the sagittal plane, and only the quadriceps and hamstrings muscle groups actively contributed to the motion, consistent with the available stimulation setup. While these choices facilitated a direct translation between simulation and experiment, they necessarily limit the range of neuromuscular interactions that can be captured.

In particular, additional muscle groups such as the gluteals or lower-leg musculature, as well as three-dimensional joint dynamics, may contribute meaningfully to force redistribution and coordination in alternative FES cycling configurations. Incorporating these elements would likely require revisiting both the simulation framework and the associated synergy mapping, potentially increasing model complexity and computational cost [62].

Beyond these structural simplifications, the model was not scaled to the participant’s anthropometry and did not include individualized muscle-tendon parameters. Instead, a generic model was employed, with subject-specific constraints imposed to ensure experimental feasibility, most notably limiting hamstrings activation to prevent involuntary spasms. While further personalization may improve quantitative accuracy and alter optimal coordination strategies [63], such refinements should be considered in light of their computational cost, implementation complexity, and variable impact on outcome metrics [64, 65].

Finally, modifications to the maximum isometric forces were introduced to improve numerical convergence. While these adjustments are believed to not substantially affect the relative timing of muscle recruitment based on gait studies [64, 65] and facilitated solution stability, they decoupled simulated force magnitudes from absolute physiological strength. This highlights an inherent trade-off between numerical robustness and physiological fidelity that should be considered when extending the framework to other participants or cycling configurations.

Control design considerations

Several simplifications were introduced when translating model activations to stimulation commands. In addition to modeling assumptions, limitations arise from the control design and its interface with the stimulation hardware. The stimulation protocol treated normalized muscle activations (0 - 1) and stimulation current parameters (0 - \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{std}$$\end{document}) as a practical approximation of the neuromuscular system. However, this mapping does not fully capture the nonlinear behavior of electrically stimulated muscles, as the minimum recruitment threshold is non-zero and the maximum stimulation level does not necessarily correspond to full muscle saturation [66]. Consequently, a mismatch exists between model activations and experimentally applied stimulation parameters. Because the direct correlation between stimulation parameters and muscle force was not experimentally characterized, the normalized activation signals should be interpreted as control signals rather than direct estimates of physiological muscle activation and force. Future work should investigate the sensitivity of the resulting control strategy to these assumptions.

The muscle grouping used in the synergy formulation reflects the stimulation channels available in the experimental setup rather than the true selectivity of surface FES. In practice, surface electrodes activate overlapping muscle regions and cannot selectively recruit individual muscles such as the short head of the biceps femoris. Consequently, the simulated muscle groups should be interpreted as functional aggregates representing the region stimulated by each electrode pair rather than precise anatomical recruitment.

Because the optimal control solutions for the left and right legs can differ due to muscle redundancy, averaging the activation profiles may suppress limb-specific coordination strategies predicted by the model. A dedicated comparison between averaged and non-averaged stimulation patterns was not performed in this study. Future work should investigate whether preserving leg-specific stimulation strategies could improve cycling performance or control robustness.

Furthermore, the stimulation strategy operated in an open-loop configuration. Although phase-shift compensation helped preserve timing consistency across cadence variations, the controller did not compensate for external disturbances, time-varying fatigue, or unmodeled reflex activity. Open-loop FES control is inherently sensitive to such factors, particularly in individuals with spinal cord injury, where nonlinear neuromuscular dynamics, spasticity, and reflex reorganization are common [10]. Future work should therefore investigate closed-loop extensions, such as the studies presented in the background, of the proposed framework to regulate cadence and improve robustness during prolonged cycling.

Experimental considerations

At the experimental level, additional sources of variability should be considered. Day-to-day fluctuations in the participant’s physiological condition, and the limited sample size (one participant) all contribute to variability in the reported outcomes, limiting generalization and statistically meaningful analysis.

Moreover, the level of conditioning required for sustained FES cycling inherently restricts participant availability, constraining sample size in studies of this nature. Nonetheless, the proposed framework is not limited to high-intensity cycling protocols and could be applied to lower-intensity FES paradigms, which typically allow inclusion of a broader participant population.

Finally, it is important to emphasize that adequate muscle conditioning is a prerequisite for safe engagement in FES cycling, particularly in individuals with severe atrophy or long-term paralysis. Low-intensity isometric training may serve as a preparatory phase, enabling gradual adaptation while providing insight into subject-specific neuromuscular properties that can inform simulations and stimulation design.

Future studies should therefore extend the proposed approach to more participants and across different experimental setups and trike configurations.

Conclusion

This was the first cycling study to successfully develop and implement optimal control-driven FES combined with muscle synergy. It demonstrated an application of fully predictive musculoskeletal simulations being translated into a feasible and physiologically meaningful stimulation signal for FES cycling in an individual with complete SCI. By incorporating an additional muscle group, subject-specific muscle constraints, and functional synergies, we extended our previous proof-of-concept work toward a more realistic and individualized control strategy. Although the empirical bang-bang protocol produced higher power and cadence, consistent with its extensive refinement and long-term use by the participant, the simulation-derived signal achieved symmetrical leg contribution, stable cycling performance, and successful real-time implementation without any manual tuning. These findings highlight the potential of predictive simulations as a safe, reproducible alternative to heuristic parameter selection. Such findings are especially relevant for new FES cycling practitioners or research groups, offering a validated starting point for further refinement. This study presents a valuable tool for enhancing FES cycling control design and addressing gaps between computational neuromechanics and real-world neurorehabilitation.
